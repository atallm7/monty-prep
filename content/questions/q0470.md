::: basics
### In plain words
FR-4 is the standard material most circuit boards are made from. It is woven glass cloth soaked in epoxy resin and baked hard, with a flame retardant added so it stops burning when a flame is removed. The name does not mean four layers. The 4 is just an old grade number.

### Picture it
Think of a fiberglass boat hull. Glass cloth gives the strength and stiffness, and hardened resin glues the cloth into one solid sheet and keeps it from soaking up water. FR-4 is the same kind of sheet, thin and flat, with copper bonded to its faces. The flame retardant is an extra ingredient in the resin. The analogy breaks in one place: flame retardant means "stops burning on its own", not "fireproof".

### Words you need
- **Flame retardant (FR)**: formulated so the material self-extinguishes. UL 94 V-0 is the usual rating.
- **Epoxy**: the resin that hardens to bond the glass and insulate.
- **Glass cloth**: woven glass fiber that gives strength and stiffness ([[q0466]]).
- **G-10**: the same glass and epoxy sheet with no flame retardant.
- **Glass transition temperature ($T_g$)**: the temperature where the resin turns soft. Standard FR-4 is about 130 to 140 °C ([[q0469]]).

### The simplest example
Take a bare FR-4 sheet 100 mm by 100 mm and 1.6 mm thick. Its volume is 10 cm × 10 cm × 0.16 cm = 16 cm³. At about 1.85 g/cm³ it weighs about 30 g. A standard 0.035 mm copper foil on both faces would add only about 6 g. It is cheap, stiff and easy to drill and plate, which is why almost every ordinary board uses it. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the grade names, typical property values, why it became the default, and where it falls short, such as high-frequency loss.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know the name's meaning (**F**lame **R**etardant, grade **4**) and, more importantly, what FR-4 *is*: a woven-glass-cloth and epoxy composite laminate, rated to a flammability standard, and the default PCB substrate. The interviewer wants to see you avoid the common myths ("four layers", "four plies", "fire resistant means fireproof") and that you can state a couple of properties (Tg, $\varepsilon_r$, $\tan\delta$) and the reason it dominates (cost, strength, processing, flame rating).
:::

::: why
FR-4 is the substrate of most boards you will ever design or debug, so its properties set your limits: signal speed and loss, maximum temperature during reflow, mechanical stiffness, and how it chars when something goes wrong. The question looks trivial, but interviewers use it as a quick check that a candidate has handled real boards and read a fabricator's stack-up. It is also the gateway to the follow-ups about when FR-4 is *not* enough ([[q0469]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **FR = flame retardant**: the laminate is formulated to self-extinguish; classified by UL 94 (V-0 is the usual requirement).
- **Grade numbering**: "4" is a grade designation in the NEMA laminate grades (LI 1), not a count of anything.
- **Glass-reinforced epoxy composite**: woven E-glass cloth for strength, epoxy resin as matrix ([[q0466]]).
- **G-10**: the same glass-epoxy composite without flame retardant.
- **Glass transition temperature**: where the resin softens; characterises FR-4 grades ([[q0469]]).
- **Halogenated vs halogen-free**: the flame retardant chemistry (brominated versus phosphorus or nitrogen based).
:::

::: technical
**Name.** FR-4 = "Flame Retardant 4", a designation from the NEMA laminate grade system (NEMA LI 1). It denotes a glass-cloth/epoxy laminate meeting self-extinguishing requirements. The non-flame-retardant sibling is G-10.

**Flammability.** UL 94 vertical burn test, classification V-0: burning stops quickly after flame removal (within 10 s per application in the V-0 criteria), with no flaming drips that ignite cotton. Flame retardancy is provided by additives, historically brominated epoxy (tetrabromobisphenol A reacted into the resin), now also halogen-free systems with phosphorus or nitrogen compounds.

**Construction.** Woven E-glass fabric (styles such as 1080, 2116, 7628 set thickness and resin content) impregnated with epoxy; cured laminate for cores, partly cured for prepreg ([[q0466]]); copper-clad.

**Typical properties (grade-dependent).** $\varepsilon_r\approx4.2$ to 4.7; $\tan\delta\approx0.02$ at 1 GHz; $T_g\approx130$ to 140 °C (standard), 170 to 180 °C (high-$T_g$); $k\approx0.3$ W/mK; in-plane CTE 14 to 17 ppm/°C; z-axis CTE about 50 to 70 ppm/°C below $T_g$; moisture absorption of the order of 0.1 to 0.2% by weight.

**Classification.** IPC-4101 "slash sheets" define FR-4 variants by properties such as $T_g$ and filler, which is how fabricators specify materials.
:::

::: long
"FR-4 stands for Flame Retardant, grade 4. The FR part is the important one: the laminate is formulated so that it self-extinguishes. In the UL 94 flammability test it earns a V-0 rating, meaning that it stops burning within seconds after you remove the flame and doesn't drip burning material. That's a safety requirement for almost every commercial product. The 4 is just a grade number from the old NEMA laminate classification. People often think it means four layers or four plies, but it doesn't; a two-layer board and a twenty-layer board can both be FR-4.

What the material actually is: woven fibreglass cloth impregnated with epoxy resin. The glass gives mechanical strength and dimensional stability, the epoxy binds it and insulates, and a flame retardant is added to the resin: traditionally brominated, which is why you hear about halogen-free versions, which use phosphorus or nitrogen compounds. It's the flame-retardant version of G-10, which is the same glass-epoxy without the additive.

Its typical properties: dielectric constant about 4.2 to 4.7, loss tangent about 0.02 at a gigahertz, glass transition about 130 to 140 degrees for standard and 170 to 180 for high-Tg, thermal conductivity only about 0.3 watts per metre-kelvin, and low moisture absorption.

It became the default because it's cheap, strong, easy to drill and plate, and it meets the safety rating. Its limits are loss and variability above a few gigahertz, poor thermal conductivity, and Tg and expansion in lead-free assembly, which is when you look at high-Tg, low-loss, polyimide or ceramic alternatives."
:::

::: terms
- **FR**: flame retardant (self-extinguishing).
- **NEMA LI 1**: the National Electrical Manufacturers Association laminate specification that defines the FR-x and G-x grades.
- **UL 94 V-0**: the highest vertical-burn flammability classification for plastics.
- **G-10**: glass-epoxy laminate without flame retardant.
- **Prepreg**: partly cured FR-4-type glass-epoxy used as bonding layers ([[q0466]]).
- **High-Tg FR-4**: FR-4 with a glass transition above about 170 °C.
- **Halogen-free laminate**: flame-retardant laminate without bromine or chlorine.
- **IPC-4101**: the specification (with slash sheets) used to specify laminates.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **"FR-4 means four layers."** The 4 is a grade number; FR-4 boards exist with 1 to 30+ layers.
- **"Flame retardant means fireproof."** It means it resists ignition and self-extinguishes; it can still char and burn in a sustained fire.
- **"All FR-4 is identical."** Grades differ in $T_g$, loss, filler and resin content; "FR-4" on a drawing is under-specified.
- **Calling FR-4 a "plastic".** It is a composite: glass reinforcement plus resin.
- **Assuming FR-4 is a good thermal conductor.** At about 0.3 W/mK it is a thermal insulator relative to copper.
- **Assuming a single dielectric constant.** $\varepsilon_r$ varies with frequency, resin content, glass style and moisture ([[q0469]]).
:::

"FR" is a safety property. A laminate that ignites easily and keeps burning is a hazard in any product with power, so UL 94 classifies plastics by how they behave in standardised flame tests; V-0 is the best of the vertical-burn classes (burning stops within seconds, with no dripping flames). A PCB laminate used in a commercial product is expected to be V-0.

The "4" is a NEMA grade identifier. The NEMA LI 1 standard lists families of industrial laminates, each with a grade name such as XXXP, FR-2, FR-3, FR-4, G-10. The numbering is historical and does not encode a measurable property; do not read "grade 4" as "the fourth best". The common grades relevant to PCBs are:

## What the material is

**Glass cloth.** Woven E-glass yarns, available in a range of weave styles (for example 106, 1080, 2116, 7628, in increasing thickness), provide stiffness, dimensional stability and strength. Glass has $\varepsilon_r\approx6$ and very low thermal expansion.

**Epoxy resin.** A thermosetting polymer that bonds the fibres, gives adhesion to copper and electrically insulates. Epoxy has $\varepsilon_r\approx3.2$ and limited high-temperature capability: it softens at its $T_g$ and decomposes at its $T_d$.

**Flame retardant.** Historically a brominated epoxy (tetrabromobisphenol A reacted into the resin backbone) which releases radical-scavenging halogen on heating and quenches the flame. Concern over halogenated products (end-of-life incineration, environmental regulations and customer requirements) pushed the development of halogen-free laminates using phosphorus and nitrogen compounds and mineral fillers; these can have slightly different electrical and mechanical properties, such as higher moisture uptake.

**Composite properties.** The result is a laminate that is stiffer in-plane than out-of-plane, with a dielectric constant between those of glass and epoxy (about 4.2 to 4.7, depending on the resin fraction).

## Typical property ranges

| Property | Typical FR-4 |
|---|---|
| Dielectric constant (1 GHz) | about 4.2 to 4.7 |
| Loss tangent (1 GHz) | about 0.02 (standard); 0.004 to 0.01 (low-loss grades) |
| $T_g$ | about 130 to 140 °C standard; 170 to 180 °C high-$T_g$ |
| Thermal conductivity | about 0.3 W/mK |
| CTE (in-plane / z, below $T_g$) | 14 to 17 / 50 to 70 ppm/°C |
| Moisture absorption | about 0.1 to 0.2 wt% |
| Flammability | UL 94 V-0 |

Values are grade- and supplier-dependent: check the laminate datasheet.

## Why FR-4 became the default

1. **Cost**: glass and epoxy are cheap and the supply chain is huge.
2. **Process compatibility**: it drills, laminates, plates and routes with standard equipment.
3. **Strength and stability**: stiff, dimensionally stable through lamination and soldering.
4. **Safety**: V-0 flame rating meets most product requirements.
5. **Adequate electrical performance** for most digital and analog designs below about 1 GHz, and workable up to several GHz for short traces.

## Where FR-4 falls short

- **High-frequency loss**: $\tan\delta\approx0.02$ gives about 0.4 dB/inch at 5 GHz ([[q0469]]).
- **Variability**: $\varepsilon_r$ varies with frequency, resin content and humidity, affecting impedance control and RF tuning.
- **Glass-weave skew** at multi-gigabit rates.
- **Heat**: low thermal conductivity; $T_g$ limits for hot environments or repeated lead-free reflow.
- **Flexing**: it is rigid; flex needs polyimide ([[q0465]]).
- **Moisture absorption** can cause delamination and CAF failures.

## A quick sanity check you can do

A bare 100 mm × 100 mm × 1.6 mm FR-4 laminate has a volume of 16 cm³ and, at about 1.85 g/cm³, a mass near 30 g. Solid 1 oz copper on both faces would add $2\times35\ \mu\text{m}\times100\ \text{cm}^2\times8.96\ \text{g/cm}^3\approx6$ g. Numbers like this help when you estimate board weight for a product with a mass budget.

## Worked example: why the composite has $\varepsilon_r\approx4.2$ to 4.7

The laminate's dielectric constant is a blend of glass ($\varepsilon_r\approx6$) and epoxy ($\approx3.2$), and a blend of two materials has bounds. If the field is parallel to the layers (so both materials see the same voltage across them, like capacitors in parallel), the effective permittivity is the volume-weighted average. If the field crosses the layers (like capacitors in series), the reciprocals average. For a laminate that is half glass by volume:

$$\varepsilon_{\parallel}=0.5\times6+0.5\times3.2=4.6,\qquad \varepsilon_{\perp}=\left(\frac{0.5}{6}+\frac{0.5}{3.2}\right)^{-1}=4.17$$

Varying the resin content gives a spread: 40 % glass gives 4.3 to 3.9, 60 % glass gives 4.9 to 4.4. So the quoted range of 4.2 to 4.7 is what a mix of roughly half glass and half resin produces, and a heavier weave style (more glass per unit thickness) raises $\varepsilon_r$. The real geometry is more complicated, since the fields in a microstrip pass through the dielectric partly across and partly along the plane, but the point is that the dielectric constant is a property of the construction. A thin prepreg with high resin content has a lower $\varepsilon_r$ than a core of the same material, which is why fabs publish different values for different layers, and why an impedance calculator should use the fab's number rather than a generic 4.5.

## Worked example: dielectric loss on a long trace

The loss tangent turns the dielectric into a frequency-dependent attenuator. The dielectric attenuation of a line is, in dB per unit length,

$$\alpha_d\approx 8.686\,\frac{\pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}\tan\delta}{c}$$

Using $\varepsilon_{eff}=3.4$ for an outer-layer microstrip and $\tan\delta=0.02$: at 1 GHz this is 0.085 dB/inch, at 5 GHz 0.43 dB/inch (matching the figure quoted above), and at 12.5 GHz 1.07 dB/inch. A 10-inch trace therefore loses about 4.3 dB to the dielectric alone at 5 GHz, in addition to conductor loss. With a low-loss laminate of $\tan\delta=0.005$ it is 1.1 dB. A 4 dB loss is a 37 % reduction in amplitude, which is the difference between a clean eye and a closed one for a multi-gigabit link. That is why FR-4 is used for most digital boards and replaced, or limited to short traces, when the signal bandwidth reaches several gigahertz.

Glass weave adds a time effect on top of loss. A trace running over a glass bundle sees a slightly higher local permittivity than one over a resin-rich gap. Suppose the local effective permittivity differs by 3.2 against 3.5 between the two traces of a differential pair. The delay per inch is $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/c$, 151 ps and 158 ps, a 7 ps/inch difference. Over a 10-inch route, the skew would be 69 ps, longer than the 40 ps unit interval of a 25 Gb/s link. The numbers are an exaggerated example, since real routing averages over a few glass bundles, but it shows why high-speed designs use "spread glass" cloth styles or route at a small angle to the weave.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Local permittivity differences from the weave, and the skew they cause, depend strongly on the cloth style, the trace width and the routing angle. Treat the 69 ps as an upper-bound illustration, not a prediction for a given board.

## The board as a heat path

FR-4 insulates heat as well as current. Heat through the thickness of a 1 cm² area of 1.6 mm laminate at 0.3 W/mK faces $R_\theta = t/(kA) = 1.6\times10^{-3}/(0.3\times10^{-4}) = 53$ K/W. A 1 W part on a pad with no thermal vias would therefore run more than 50 K above the board's other face. Plated thermal vias are copper tubes. Nine vias drilled at 0.3 mm with 25 µm plating each have a copper wall area of about $0.0216$ mm² and a resistance of $1.6\text{ mm}/(385\text{ W/mK}\times0.0216\text{ mm}^2)=192$ K/W, which is 21 K/W for nine in parallel. In parallel with the laminate, the pad's thermal resistance falls to roughly 15 K/W, and 25 vias would reduce it further. That explains why thermal pads on QFN and power packages have an array of vias, and why the vias are sized and filled based on the thermal need as well as the electrical one.

## Verifying on the bench

- **Dielectric constant of a coupon:** make a capacitor from a clad laminate by etching or cutting 50 mm × 50 mm pads on both faces of a 1.6 mm piece. The expected capacitance is $\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rA/d = 8.854\times10^{-12}\times4.4\times0.0025/1.6\times10^{-3} = 61$ pF, so measuring it with an LCR meter at 1 MHz gives $\varepsilon_r$ directly. Fringing adds a few percent, so subtract it or use a guard ring if you want accuracy.
- **Moisture uptake:** weigh a bare 100 mm × 100 mm board (about 30 g), bake it for a few hours below the laminate's limit, and weigh it again. A 0.1 to 0.2 % uptake is 30 to 60 mg, within reach of a scale with 1 mg resolution.
- **Effective permittivity and delay:** measure the round-trip delay of a known-length trace with a TDR. Delay per inch gives $\varepsilon_{eff}$, which you can compare with the stack-up calculator.
- **Loss:** measure the through response of a long trace and a short trace with a VNA, and subtract. The difference divided by the length difference gives the loss per inch at each frequency, to compare with the dielectric formula plus conductor loss.
:::

::: applications
- **Computers, phones, routers and appliances**: standard FR-4 boards.
- **Automotive and industrial electronics**: high-Tg FR-4 for temperature cycling.
- **Telecom and servers**: low-loss FR-4-class epoxy-glass for multi-gigabit links.
- **Prototype boards from online fabricators**: nearly always FR-4 unless specified.
- **Mechanical parts and insulators**: G-10/FR-4 sheet for fixtures, standoffs, insulating plates.
- **Switchgear and power equipment**: flame-retardant glass-epoxy insulation components.
:::

::: assumptions
I describe FR-4 as the common glass-epoxy laminate used in rigid PCBs. The NEMA grade list and the way fabricators use the term have drifted: today "FR-4" on a fab sheet is often shorthand for a family of epoxy-glass laminates specified by $T_g$ and IPC-4101 slash sheet, and the flame retardant chemistry varies (brominated, halogen-free). Property values are typical ranges. I treat the "4" as an arbitrary grade number: sources occasionally offer alternative explanations, but the grade-designation reading is the standard one. If the interviewer asks for properties, give a range and note it depends on the grade.
:::

::: followups
### Does FR-4 mean the board is fireproof?
No. It means the laminate is flame retardant: resistant to ignition and self-extinguishing in the UL 94 test (V-0). In a sustained fire or with a strong ignition source it will char and burn, and it can release toxic fumes. Fireproof materials like ceramics don't burn at all.

### What does the "4" stand for?
It is a grade number in the NEMA laminate classification (NEMA LI 1), alongside FR-1, FR-2, FR-3, FR-5, G-10, G-11 and others. It does not mean four layers or four plies, and it doesn't rank quality.

### Why do some boards advertise "halogen-free FR-4"?
Traditional FR-4 uses a brominated flame retardant. Halogen-free variants use phosphorus or nitrogen compounds and fillers, avoiding halogens for environmental, customer or regulatory reasons (lower toxic emissions when burned). They can have somewhat different moisture absorption, processing and electrical properties, so confirm with the datasheet.

### What if my design runs at 10 GHz? Is FR-4 still acceptable?
Only for very short runs. Dielectric loss alone is about 0.8 dB per inch at 10 GHz, $\varepsilon_r$ varies, and weave effects cause skew, so most 10 GHz designs use low-loss laminates or RF material on critical layers ([[q0469]]). Standard FR-4 can still be used for the rest of the stack in a hybrid design.

### Scenario: a fab asks "which FR-4?" What do you answer?
Specify the properties you need: $T_g$ (for example 170 °C for lead-free and multiple reflows), the IPC-4101 slash sheet if you know it, halogen-free or not, copper weight, the target dielectric constant for impedance control, and the thickness. Without that, they will use their default grade, which may not meet your impedance or thermal requirements.
:::
