::: testing
Whether you can build the **efficiency expression from first principles** and then explain how each variable moves it. The key facts the interviewer wants:

1. $\eta=\dfrac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_Q)}$, which reduces to $V_{out}/V_{in}$ when $I_Q\ll I_{out}$.
2. **Output current** matters in only two places: at light load $I_Q$ dominates and efficiency collapses, and at heavy load dropout rises ($I\,R_{DS(on)}$), which forces a higher minimum $V_{in}$.
3. **Dropout** sets the *best case*: $\eta_{max}=V_{out}/(V_{out}+V_{DO})$.
4. For a fixed $V_{in}$, efficiency is flat versus load current over the middle range. Many candidates wrongly say it falls with load current.
:::

::: why
LDO efficiency is the number that decides whether an LDO is acceptable on a battery rail, and the variables are not independent: dropout depends on current, and quiescent current depends on the part. Being able to sketch the efficiency curve and explain its three regions is a standard test of power-system understanding. It also feeds the buck-vs-LDO choice ([[q0158]], [[q0222]]) and the battery-life estimate for a sensor.
:::

::: concepts
- **Efficiency**: $\eta=P_{out}/P_{in}$. See [[q0162]].
- **Quiescent current ($I_Q$)**: ground-pin current at no load. See [[q0165]].
- **Dropout voltage** $V_{DO}=I_{out}R_{DS(on)}$ for a MOSFET pass device. See [[q0277]].
- **LDO as a series resistor**: input current $=$ output current $+I_Q$. See [[q0279]].
- **Energy-weighted efficiency**: average efficiency over a duty cycle or battery discharge must be weighted by energy, not by time.
- **Energy conservation**: $P_{in}=P_{out}+P_{loss}$. See [[q0154]].
:::

::: answer
"The input current of an LDO is the output current plus the regulator's own quiescent current, and the input voltage is $V_{in}$, so efficiency is $V_{out}I_{out}$ divided by $V_{in}(I_{out}+I_Q)$. If the load current is much bigger than $I_Q$, that's just $V_{out}/V_{in}$.

So output current and dropout affect it in different ways. In the middle of the range, load current doesn't change efficiency at all, it's set by the voltage ratio. At very light load, $I_Q$ matters: when $I_{out}$ is equal to $I_Q$, efficiency is half of $V_{out}/V_{in}$. A 50 µA part with a 10 µA load is down to about 17% of the ideal.

Dropout shows up at the other end. The dropout voltage is roughly load current times $R_{DS(on)}$, so a higher current needs more headroom. If the input is set at the minimum, $V_{out}+I\,R_{DS(on)}$, the best efficiency is $V_{out}/(V_{out}+V_{DO})$: 94% for 3.3 V with 200 mV dropout, but only 80% at 2 A if the resistance is 0.4 Ω. And if the input goes below that point, the LDO is out of regulation entirely.

So to maximize LDO efficiency I'd keep $V_{in}$ as close to $V_{out}+V_{DO}$ as the worst case allows, and choose a part whose $I_Q$ is small compared with my lightest load."
:::

::: technical
**Basic expression.**
$$\eta=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_Q)}=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+I_Q/I_{out}}$$

**Regions (fixed $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$):**
- $I_{out}\ll I_Q$: $\eta\approx\dfrac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}\dfrac{I_{out}}{I_Q}$ (proportional to load).
- $I_Q\ll I_{out}$ and regulating: $\eta\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$ (flat).
- At the dropout limit: $V_{out}=V_{in}-I_{out}R_{DS(on)}$ (out of regulation), $\eta\approx1-I_{out}R_{DS(on)}/V_{in}$, falling with current.

**Headroom/dropout.** Minimum input at current $I$: $V_{in,min}(I)=V_{out}+IR_{DS(on)}$. Best-case efficiency at that input:
$$\eta_{max}(I)=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{out}+IR_{DS(on)}}$$

| $V_{DO}$ for 3.3 V out | 0.05 | 0.1 | 0.2 | 0.3 | 0.5 | 1.0 V |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\eta_{max}$ | 98.5% | 97.1% | 94.3% | 91.7% | 86.8% | 76.7% |

With $R_{DS(on)}=0.4\ \Omega$: $I=0.1$ A gives 98.8%, 0.5 A gives 94.3%, 1 A gives 89.2%, 2 A gives 80.5%.

**Light load.** 5 V to 3.3 V with $I_Q=50\ \mu$A: $\eta$ = 1.3% at 1 µA, 11% at 10 µA, 33% at 50 µA, 63% at 1 mA, 66% (limit) above about 5 mA.

**Energy-weighted average (battery).** For a constant-current load from a Li-ion cell discharging 4.2 V to 3.5 V (average about 3.85 V) into 3.3 V: $\bar\eta=\dfrac{V_{out}\,Q}{\int V_{bat}\,dq}\approx\dfrac{3.3}{3.85}=85.7\%$.
:::

::: short
"Efficiency is $V_{out}I_{out}/[V_{in}(I_{out}+I_Q)]$, essentially $V_{out}/V_{in}$ when the load is much bigger than $I_Q$, so it's flat versus load current. Output current matters at light load, where $I_Q$ dominates, and at heavy load through dropout: $V_{DO}=I\,R_{DS(on)}$ raises the minimum input, and at that minimum the best efficiency is $V_{out}/(V_{out}+V_{DO})$. E.g. 3.3 V with 200 mV dropout gives 94%, but headroom of 1.7 V gives only 66%."
:::

::: long
"Start with the input current. An LDO has no way to change current, so the input carries the load current plus the regulator's own quiescent current. That gives $\eta=V_{out}I_{out}/[V_{in}(I_{out}+I_Q)]$, which is $V_{out}/V_{in}$ whenever the load is much bigger than $I_Q$.

First effect of output current: it barely matters in the middle. At 5 V in and 3.3 V out, whether I'm at 20 mA or 500 mA, efficiency is 66%. That's the thing people get wrong, they think a heavier load lowers efficiency. It doesn't, it just raises the absolute heat.

Second, at light load, the quiescent current takes over. If $I_Q$ is 50 µA and the load is 50 µA, efficiency is half of 66%, 33%. At 10 µA, it's 11%. So for sleep-mode rails I pick an LDO with $I_Q$ well below the sleep current.

Third, dropout. The dropout voltage is roughly load current times the pass resistance. If the input is fixed and the load increases, the LDO eventually runs out of headroom. And if I want to design for the highest efficiency, I'd set $V_{in}$ at $V_{out}$ plus the dropout at the maximum load, where the best case efficiency is $V_{out}/(V_{out}+V_{DO})$. For 3.3 V with a 0.4 Ω pass device: 98.8% at 100 mA, 94.3% at 500 mA, 89% at 1 A, 80% at 2 A.

Finally, the headroom itself is the big knob. 3.5 V to 3.3 V is 94%. 5 V to 3.3 V is 66%. 12 V is 27%. For a battery, I'd weight efficiency by energy: a cell running from 4.2 V to 3.5 V into 3.3 V at constant current averages about 86%."
:::

::: terms
- **Efficiency ($\eta$)**: $P_{out}/P_{in}$.
- **Quiescent current ($I_Q$)**: current the regulator draws at no load.
- **Ground-pin current**: total current into the GND pin including bias and (for bipolar pass) base drive.
- **Dropout voltage ($V_{DO}$)**: minimum $V_{in}-V_{out}$ for regulation at a given load.
- **Headroom**: actual $V_{in}-V_{out}$.
- **Best-case efficiency**: $V_{out}/(V_{out}+V_{DO})$ at the minimum allowed input.
- **Light-load efficiency**: efficiency when $I_{out}$ is comparable to $I_Q$.
- **Energy-weighted efficiency**: total output energy divided by total input energy over a profile.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** writes $\eta\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$ and says it falls at high current.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Writes the full formula with $I_Q$ and shows *both* limits.
- States clearly that, at fixed $V_{in}$, efficiency is **independent of load current** in the middle range.
- Explains the light-load collapse and the load at which $\eta$ is half its ideal ($I_{out}=I_Q$).
- Links current to dropout: $V_{DO}=IR_{DS(on)}$, so higher current raises $V_{in,min}$ and, at the minimum input, lowers the best-case efficiency.
- Computes real numbers and defines best-case $\eta_{max}=V_{out}/(V_{out}+V_{DO})$.
- Warns that average efficiency must be energy-weighted over the real load/battery profile.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Efficiency drops as load current rises."** At fixed $V_{in}$ the ratio is constant. Power loss rises, but so does output power.
- **Ignoring $I_Q$ at light load.** For sleep currents in the microamps, $I_Q$ dominates.
- **Using the nominal dropout figure for all loads.** It scales with current and temperature.
- **Treating "efficiency at minimum $V_{in}$" as the operating efficiency.** The system has to survive the *worst-case* minimum, but at typical $V_{in}$ it may be much lower.
- **Averaging efficiency by time.** Weight by energy, since high-power periods dominate.
- **Forgetting that below $V_{out}+V_{DO}$ the LDO has no regulation**, so "efficiency" has little meaning: output follows input.
:::

::: deep
## Setting up the calculation

Let the load draw $I_{out}$. The LDO's input current is $I_{in}=I_{out}+I_Q$ (the series pass transistor carries exactly $I_{out}$, and the bias circuits, reference, and error amp draw $I_Q$ from the input, or for bipolar types some base current). Then

$$\eta=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}I_{in}}=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}\cdot\frac{I_{out}}{I_{out}+I_Q}.$$

The first factor is the **voltage ratio** (set by headroom). The second is the **current ratio** (set by $I_Q$ vs the load). That separation makes everything below easy.

## The role of dropout: best-case efficiency

The *best* an LDO can do for a given output is when $V_{in}=V_{out}+V_{DO}$:
$$\eta_{max}=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{out}+V_{DO}}.$$
Dropout depends on the load, $V_{DO}=I\,R_{DS(on)}$:

| Load | $V_{DO}$ ($R=0.4\ \Omega$) | $V_{in,min}$ | $\eta_{max}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 mA | 0.04 V | 3.34 V | 98.8% |
| 500 mA | 0.20 V | 3.50 V | 94.3% |
| 1 A | 0.40 V | 3.70 V | 89.2% |
| 2 A | 0.80 V | 4.10 V | 80.5% |

So heavier loads need more headroom *and* sacrifice more best-case efficiency. If $V_{in}$ is not under your control (battery), you simply live with $V_{out}/V_{in}$. If it is (a pre-regulator rail), choose $V_{in}$ as low as the worst case (maximum current, hot, minimum tolerances) allows, and not lower.

> [!NOTE]
> The efficiency in the table is for the **ideal** voltage ratio only. Subtract $I_Q$ effects at light load and note that $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature (roughly +0.4 to 0.7%/°C for silicon), so hot dropout is higher.

## Light-load efficiency and the battery-life angle

At 5 V in, 3.3 V out, $I_Q=50\ \mu$A:

| $I_{out}$ | $\eta$ |
|---|---|
| 1 µA | 1.3% |
| 10 µA | 11.0% |
| 50 µA | 33.0% |
| 1 mA | 62.9% |
| 100 mA | 66.0% |

An LDO with $I_Q$ of 1 µA at 3.7 V in and 3.3 V out with a 1 mA load has $\eta=3.3/3.7\times1/(1+0.001)=89.1\%$, which is excellent. So $I_Q$ is a *selection criterion* for always-on rails: a "standard" 50 µA LDO would waste 5% of a 1 mA load, and a 300 µA LDO would waste 23%.

For a sleep-dominated sensor, battery life is set by $I_Q$ plus sleep current, and the *average* efficiency is weighted by where the energy goes (usually the active bursts).

## Energy-weighted efficiency on a battery

Constant 100 mA load, Li-ion cell from 4.2 V to 3.5 V into 3.3 V. Efficiency at each instant is $3.3/V_{bat}$: 78.6% at 4.2 V, 94.3% at 3.5 V. The energy-weighted average is the ratio of total energies, $3.3\,Q/\int V_{bat}\,dq$. With a roughly linear discharge between the two voltages, the average battery voltage is 3.85 V, so $\bar\eta=3.3/3.85=85.7\%$. (A time-average of instantaneous $\eta$ would give 86.0%, similar here since the current is constant, but for a bursty load they diverge.) Compare a buck with 92% core efficiency and 20 µA quiescent, which *also* has to be weighed over the same profile.

The break-even for a buck with efficiency $\eta_b$ at a given load is $V_{in}=V_{out}/\eta_b$: for $\eta_b=0.93$ that is 3.55 V, so below about 3.55 V in the LDO is more efficient, above it the buck wins (ignoring $I_Q$ differences and noise). This is the quantitative form of [[q0158]].

## Worked example: a duty-cycled sensor on a Li-ion cell

Instantaneous efficiency is only half the story, because a battery's life is limited by charge (or energy) integrated over the load profile. Take a node that sleeps at 5 µA for 95% of the time and draws 80 mA for 5% (a burst of radio or measurement), from a 1500 mAh Li-ion cell, on a 3.3 V rail. Compare an LDO with $I_Q=2\ \mu$A to a buck with 92% efficiency in the active state, 20 µA quiescent and about 70% efficiency at the sleeping load (assumed values).

**LDO.** The battery supplies the load current plus $I_Q$, whatever the cell voltage. Average load: $0.05\times80\ \text{mA}+0.95\times5\ \mu\text{A}=4.005$ mA. With $I_Q$: 4.007 mA. Life: $1500\ \text{mAh}/4.007\ \text{mA}=374$ h (15.6 days). It does not depend on whether the cell is at 4.2 V or 3.5 V. Energy is wasted as heat, but charge is what runs out.

**Buck.** The input current scales as $1/V_{bat}$: $I_{bat,active}=3.3\times80\ \text{mA}/(0.92\,V_{bat})$, plus the sleeping load and quiescent current. Averaged over the same profile:

| Cell voltage | Buck average current | Buck life | Relative to LDO |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.2 V | 3.44 mA | 436 h | +16% |
| 3.8 V | 3.80 mA | 395 h | +5% |
| 3.5 V | 4.12 mA | 364 h | −3% |

The buck wins when the cell is full and loses at the bottom. The crossover is at $V_{bat}=V_{out}/\eta_{buck}=3.3/0.92=3.59$ V, which is the same break-even rule used earlier on this page. Averaged across a discharge from 4.2 V to 3.5 V the buck gains only about 6% (396 h versus 374 h), which is a smaller prize than most people expect. If the application does not need the cell's last 0.2 V of range or needs low noise, the LDO is a very reasonable choice.

**Now make the node sleepier.** Suppose the node is active only 0.1% of the time. The average load falls to 85 µA, so quiescent current and sleep efficiency start to dominate. LDO: $85+2=87\ \mu$A, life $1500/0.087=17,240$ h (about 2.0 years). Buck at 3.7 V: the 20 µA quiescent current plus the reflected load gives about 104 µA, life 14,400 h (about 1.6 years). The LDO wins by 20% here, despite its lower active efficiency (89% at 3.7 V against the buck's 92%), because $I_Q$ is what the sleeping node pays for. This is why the last follow-up asks about the real $I_Q$ across the load range: a number that is wrong by 10 µA decides the answer.

## Limiting cases and how the model can mislead

Two regimes look similar on a datasheet and behave differently. First, when $V_{in}-V_{out}$ is below the dropout, "efficiency $V_{out}/V_{in}$" stays high, but the output is out of spec, so the number means nothing. Second, $I_Q$ is quoted at no load and 25 °C, and for many parts it rises with load current (bipolar pass elements need base current) and with temperature. A part with a quoted 2 µA that draws 80 µA at 100 mA does not change the table above, but it does change the efficiency at moderate loads in a way the formula $I_{out}/(I_{out}+I_Q)$ would miss if you take $I_Q$ as constant. Also, batteries have capacity that depends on current and temperature, and a cell delivering 80 mA bursts has less than the rated charge, so absolute hours shift. The comparison between regulators changes much less than the absolute numbers.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The buck's 92% active efficiency, 20 µA quiescent current and 70% sleep efficiency are assumed. Real light-load efficiency varies widely between parts and between modes (a PFM buck at 5 µA load may be well under 70%). Redo the table with the actual datasheet curves for your candidates.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Measure the current profile.** Put a sense resistor or a source-measure unit in series with the battery and capture the whole sleep/active cycle with enough dynamic range. A 5 µA sleep and an 80 mA burst span four orders of magnitude, so a single meter range will fail; use a current-profiling instrument, a shunt with auto-ranging amplifier, or two ranges.
2. **Integrate.** Compute charge per cycle by integrating the captured current, then divide the cell's capacity by the average. This is the number to compare with the prediction.
3. **Sweep the cell voltage.** Repeat at several supply voltages using a bench supply set to 4.2, 3.8 and 3.5 V. The LDO's average current should stay flat. The buck's should fall as the voltage rises.
4. **Check $I_Q$ in the wild.** Measure the regulator's ground current at the sleeping load and at temperature extremes, with the load actually connected. A single-digit-microamp difference from the datasheet can dominate a sleepy design.
:::

::: applications
- **Battery rails** (Li-ion to 3.3 V/1.8 V): small headroom gives 85 to 94% efficiency with an LDO.
- **Always-on standby rails**: choose nA-class $I_Q$ LDOs so $I_Q\ll$ sleep current.
- **Post-regulation** after a buck: set the intermediate rail $V_{out}+V_{DO}+$margin to keep the LDO at about 90%.
- **Low-power sensor nodes**: energy budget analyses weight efficiency by duty cycle.
- **Power-tree design**: LDO efficiency feeds into total system efficiency as a product with upstream converters.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Constant $I_Q$** with load. Real LDOs' ground current can increase with load (especially bipolar pass devices where base current $\approx I_{out}/\beta$) and some parts reduce $I_Q$ in a light-load mode.
- **$V_{DO}=IR_{DS(on)}$** (MOSFET triode model). Bipolar LDOs have a different dropout curve.
- **Ideal reference**, no temperature dependence.
- **Efficiency numbers are ratios of average powers.** Real loads vary, and for bursty loads use energy-weighted values.
- **Buck comparison** uses assumed 92 to 93% core efficiency. Real buck efficiency depends on load, ratio and design. See [[q0222]] for the full comparison.
:::

::: followups
### An LDO has 94% efficiency at 3.5 V in. What happens if the battery drops to 3.3 V?
It is below $V_{out}+V_{DO}$, so the LDO is in dropout: the pass FET is fully on, $V_{out}\approx V_{in}-IR_{DS(on)}=3.3-0.2=3.1$ V (at 0.5 A), efficiency ($V_{out}/V_{in}$) is 94%, but the output is below spec and the load may brown out. Efficiency as a number stays high in dropout; regulation is what you lose.

### Does a bigger load current make the LDO less efficient?
At a fixed $V_{in}$ in regulation, no: efficiency is $V_{out}/V_{in}$ whatever the current (once above $I_Q$). Heat grows linearly with current. Efficiency falls with load only through dropout (if $V_{in}$ is near the minimum) or because ground current rises with load in bipolar-pass parts.

### How do you choose $I_Q$ for a sensor that sleeps at 5 µA?
Quiescent current adds directly to the sleep current: a 50 µA LDO would raise a 5 µA sleep to 55 µA, 11x more. Choose an LDO with $I_Q\le0.5$ to 1 µA, or turn the LDO off with an enable and use a load switch or a low-power always-on supply for the sleep domain.

### What is the efficiency of a 1.8 V LDO from a 3.3 V rail at 200 mA?
$\eta\approx1.8/3.3=54.5\%$ (ignoring $I_Q$). The dissipation is $(3.3-1.8)\times0.2=0.3$ W, so even though the efficiency number looks poor, the absolute heat is small. That is why LDOs for core rails (1.0 to 1.8 V) are acceptable at low current but become hot at amps.

### Scenario: your design lasts 30% less battery life than predicted. The LDO datasheet says $I_Q$ = 2 µA. What would you check?
Measure the *actual* ground current across the load range and temperature, since $I_Q$ in the datasheet is typically at no load and 25 °C and can be much higher with load or hot. Then check dropout behavior (bursts pulling the battery into the dropout region where the LDO's ground current may rise sharply), the divider current for an adjustable part, leakage through the enable network, and whether the system truly sleeps at the current you assumed. Compute the energy-weighted efficiency using the real current profile.
:::
