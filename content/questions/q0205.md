::: testing
Whether you can reason about **how loss is distributed between the two bridge devices** and what "symmetrical" gives up. The interviewer wants to hear:

1. A clear definition of the term you are answering (equal, same-type FETs in the half bridge), since the wording is ambiguous.
2. That the two FETs do not do the same job in a buck: the high side is on for $D$ and hard-switches, the low side is on for $1-D$ and switches almost for free.
3. A quantified penalty for symmetry when $D$ is far from 0.5, ideally the optimum split.
4. The real benefits of symmetry: one part number, matched drive and timing, and bidirectional operation.
:::

::: why
Real power-stage design is about spending a fixed silicon budget well. A 12 V to 1 V converter has its low side conducting 92% of the time, so giving both FETs the same $R_{DS(on)}$ wastes silicon where it is least useful. Understanding this explains why DrMOS and smart-power-stage products pair a "control" FET with a differently sized "sync" FET, and when symmetry is the right answer anyway (inverters, motor drives, bidirectional converters, buck-boost). It is a favorite way for interviewers to check whether you can move from "two switches" to "how are the losses really split".
:::

::: concepts
- **Duty cycle and time share**: the high side conducts $DT$, the low side $(1-D)T$ ([[q0180]]).
- **Conduction loss** $I^2 R\times$ time fraction ([[q0203]]).
- **Hard vs soft switching**: the high side turns on against the body diode of the low side (hard, with recovery); the low side switches after its body diode already clamps SW (nearly zero-voltage) ([[q0208]]).
- **Silicon area budget**: lower $R_{DS(on)}$ costs area, $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$ ([[q0204]]).
- **Bidirectional power flow**: a symmetrical bridge works equally as buck or boost.
:::

::: answer
"I'd read 'symmetrical' as two identical FETs, same type and same size, in the half bridge. The pros are that it's simple and flexible: one part number, the same gate-drive design on both sides, and the bridge is naturally bidirectional, so you can run it as a buck or a boost. It's the natural choice at 50% duty, and for inverters, motor drives and bidirectional chargers.

The con is that in a buck the two FETs do different jobs. The high side conducts only for $D$ and does all the hard switching; the low side conducts for $1-D$ but switches nearly for free because its body diode clamps the node first. So if $D$ is small, like 12 V down to 1.2 V, the low side runs hot with 90% of the conduction loss while the high side is mostly idle silicon. For a fixed total area the optimum has conductance split as $\sqrt{D}$ to $\sqrt{1-D}$, which for $D=0.1$ means a high side three times the resistance of the low side and 20% less conduction loss. Plus you'd want the high side optimized for low $Q_{gd}$ and the low side for low $R_{DS(on)}$.

So symmetry costs efficiency and thermal balance unless the duty is near 50%."
:::

::: technical
**Definition used.** Both bridge FETs are the same type with equal $R_{DS(on)}$, ratings and gate charge.

**Conduction loss with a fixed total conductance.** Let $G = 1/R_{DS(on)}$ and $G_H + G_L = G_{tot}$ (proportional to total die area). Ignoring ripple:
$$P_{cond} = I^2\left(\frac{D}{G_H}+\frac{1-D}{G_L}\right)$$
Minimizing with the constraint gives $G_H/G_L = \sqrt{D/(1-D)}$, and
$$\frac{P_{opt}}{P_{sym}} = \frac{\left(\sqrt{D}+\sqrt{1-D}\right)^2}{2}$$
| $D$ | $P_{opt}/P_{sym}$ | optimal $R_H/R_0$ | optimal $R_L/R_0$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.05 | 0.72 | 2.68 | 0.62 |
| 0.10 | 0.80 | 2.00 | 0.67 |
| 0.275 | 0.95 | 1.31 | 0.81 |
| 0.50 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |

Example ($D=0.1$, 10 A, symmetrical pair of 10 mΩ): conduction loss $I^2R_0=1.0$ W, split 0.1 W (high) and 0.9 W (low). Optimal pair 20 mΩ and 6.67 mΩ: 0.8 W total.

**Switching losses are asymmetric.** The high side turns on with $V_{in}$ across it while the low side's body diode recovers: overlap loss $\approx \tfrac12 V_{in}I\,(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}$ plus $Q_{rr}$ and $C_{oss}$ terms. The low side turns on with $V_{DS}\approx -V_{SD}$ (near ZVS). So the high side wants low $Q_{gd}$ ($R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{gd}$ FOM); the low side wants low $R_{DS(on)}$.

**What symmetry buys.** One part number; equal $V_{DS}$ and current ratings (relevant for bidirectional or buck-boost); simple identical drivers; symmetrical thermal stress at $D=0.5$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> "Symmetrical power bridge" is not a standard datasheet term. This page takes it as same-type, same-size FETs; other readings are listed under interpretations.
:::

::: long
"I'll take 'symmetrical' to mean the high-side and low-side FETs are the same type and the same size, same $R_{DS(on)}$, same ratings.

The pros are practical. One part number, so simpler sourcing and layout. The gate drive and dead-time design are identical on both sides. And it's bidirectional: the same bridge can buck in one direction and boost in the other, which matters for battery chargers, bidirectional supplies and motor drives. At 50% duty, which is typical for inverters and half-bridge motor drivers, it's also the optimum, because both devices carry current half the time.

The cons come from what the two devices actually do in a buck. The high side conducts for $D$ and does all the hard switching: it turns on with the full input voltage across it while the low-side body diode recovers, so it wants low gate charge. The low side conducts for $1-D$ but switches almost for free, because its body diode has already clamped the node, so it wants the lowest possible $R_{DS(on)}$.

Take 12 V to 1.2 V, so $D$ is 0.1. A symmetrical pair puts 90% of the conduction loss in the low side. If you fix the total silicon area, the optimal conductance ratio is the square root of $D$ over $1-D$, which here is a high-side resistance twice the reference and a low side two thirds of it: 20 mΩ and 6.7 mΩ instead of 10 and 10. Conduction loss drops about 20%, and the thermal load is better balanced.

At 3.3 V from 12 V, the gain is only about 5%, so it's mostly a high-ratio or high-current issue. That's why VRM stages use differently sized control and sync FETs."
:::

::: terms
- **Symmetrical bridge**: half bridge with equal, same-type switches.
- **Asymmetrical (optimized) bridge**: switches sized differently to match their duty and switching roles.
- **Conductance ($G = 1/R_{DS(on)}$)**: proportional to die area in a given technology.
- **Hard switching**: switching with voltage across the device while current flows, causing overlap loss.
- **Near-ZVS**: switching when the device's body diode already holds $V_{DS}\approx0$.
- **$Q_{gd}$ (Miller charge)**: gate-drain charge that sets voltage transition time.
- **FOM**: figure of merit, such as $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{gd}$.
- **Bidirectional converter**: a converter that can transfer power both ways.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Symmetrical is simple but not optimal for efficiency."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Defines the term and states the interpretation.
- Notes time share: $D$ vs $(1-D)$ for conduction, and hard vs near-ZVS switching.
- Derives or states the optimum $G_H/G_L=\sqrt{D/(1-D)}$ and gives a number (20% saving at $D=0.1$, about 5% at $D=0.275$).
- Separates the device-selection implication: high side low $Q_{gd}$, low side low $R_{DS(on)}$.
- Lists where symmetry is right: 50% duty, bidirectional flow, buck-boost, inverters, matched thermal stress, simplicity.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Both FETs conduct half the time."** Only at $D=0.5$. In a buck $Q_1$ conducts $D$ and $Q_2$ $(1-D)$.
- **Saying the high side should be bigger because it "does more work".** For small $D$ the low side does more conduction work; the high side does more switching work, so it should have lower $Q_{gd}$ but not necessarily lower $R_{DS(on)}$.
- **Forgetting the area constraint.** "Make both as low resistance as possible" ignores that area, $Q_g$ and cost rise with $1/R_{DS(on)}$.
- **Believing the optimum ratio is $D/(1-D)$.** For conduction-only with fixed area, it is $\sqrt{D/(1-D)}$ for conductance, not $D/(1-D)$.
- **Ignoring switching asymmetry.** Equal conduction loss does not mean equal heating; the high side adds hard-switching loss.
:::

::: deep
## Two devices, two different jobs

In a buck the high side $Q_1$ conducts the inductor current for $DT$ and the low side $Q_2$ for $(1-D)T$. If both have resistance $R_0$:

$$P_{cond} = I^2\left[D R_0 + (1-D)R_0\right] = I^2R_0$$

Independent of $D$, but the *split* depends on $D$: the fraction in $Q_2$ is exactly $(1-D)$. At $D = 0.1$ that is 90%.

## The optimum split

Hold the total conductance fixed, $G_H+G_L=G_{tot}$ (a proxy for total die area, since $R_{DS(on)}\propto 1/\text{area}$). Minimize

$$f = \frac{D}{G_H}+\frac{1-D}{G_L}.$$

Lagrange multiplier: $-D/G_H^2 = \lambda$, $-(1-D)/G_L^2=\lambda$, so $G_H/G_L = \sqrt{D/(1-D)}$. Substituting:

$$\frac{P_{opt}}{P_{sym}} = \frac{(\sqrt D+\sqrt{1-D})^2}{2}.$$

## Switching is asymmetric as well

Conduction is only half the story:

- **Turn-on of $Q_1$**: $Q_2$'s body diode is conducting, so $Q_1$ turns on against its reverse recovery, with $V_{in}$ across it while it carries the full inductor current, giving overlap loss $\approx \tfrac12 V_{in} I (t_r) f_{sw}$ plus $Q_{rr}V_{in}f_{sw}$ ([[q0209]]).
- **Turn-off of $Q_1$**: with $I_{pk}$ and rising $V_{DS}$: $\approx\tfrac12 V_{in}I\,t_f f_{sw}$.
- **Turn-on/turn-off of $Q_2$**: body diode already holds $V_{DS}\approx -0.7$ V, so there is essentially no overlap loss, only gate drive and a body-diode dead-time term.

So the best high-side device has the lowest $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{gd}$, the best low-side device has the lowest $R_{DS(on)}$ (and low $Q_{rr}$ so as not to hurt the high side). Real products reflect this: high-side parts have more $R_{DS(on)}$ but lower $Q_g$, low-side parts the reverse.

## What symmetry gets you

| Benefit | Why |
|---|---|
| One part number | Simpler BOM, supply chain, qualification, placement |
| Identical gate-drive design | Same $V_{GS}$, same dead-time shaping, same $Q_g$ |
| Natural bidirectional operation | In a bidirectional or buck-boost converter each switch sees the roles of both |
| Optimum at $D=0.5$ | Half-bridge inverters, class-D, motor legs, push-pull stages |
| Equal thermal stress at $D=0.5$ | Same heat in both devices |
| Equal voltage rating | Same $V_{DS}$ rating regardless of which side is stressed |

## What symmetry costs

| Penalty | When it bites |
|---|---|
| Up to 20 to 30% more conduction loss for the same area | $D$ below about 0.15 or above about 0.85 |
| Thermal imbalance (low side hot) | Low-$D$ bucks, e.g. 12 V to 1 V |
| Wrong FOM on the high side | The high side wants low $Q_{gd}$, not just low $R_{DS(on)}$ |
| Larger total area for the same loss | Cost |

## Practical examples

- **DrMOS / smart power stage**: separate control (high-side) and sync (low-side) FETs with different $R_{DS(on)}$/$Q_g$ in one package.
- **Multi-phase CPU VRM**: very low duty cycle (12 V to about 1 V, $D\approx0.08$), the low side is much larger.
- **Class-D amplifier / inverter**: $D$ swings around 50%; symmetrical is right.
- **Four-switch buck-boost**: all four switches are the same device.
:::

::: applications
- **CPU/GPU multiphase VRMs (12 V to about 1 V)**: asymmetrical control FET and sync FET inside DrMOS power stages.
- **Half-bridge motor drivers and class-D audio**: symmetrical, near-50% duty swings.
- **Bidirectional battery chargers/DC-DC converters**: symmetrical FETs to act as buck or boost.
- **Four-switch buck-boost converters** in USB-PD and battery systems: four matched FETs.
- **12 V to 3.3 V general point-of-load ICs**: slightly asymmetrical (high side and low side sized differently on the die) or nearly equal for simplicity.
:::

::: assumptions
- **"Symmetrical" = equal-size same-type switches.** If it means complementary N/P devices sized for equal $R_{DS(on)}$, the PFET costs 2 to 3 times the area, and the trade shifts toward the drive-simplicity argument in [[q0204]].
- **Conduction-only optimization with fixed area**; including switching losses, gate drive and $C_{oss}$ shifts the optimum toward a smaller, lower-$Q_g$ high side.
- **Negligible ripple** in the RMS formula; with ripple the optimum moves slightly because the RMS current differs from $I_{out}$ ($I_{rms}^2 = I^2+\Delta I^2/12$ in both devices).
- **Low voltage silicon** where $R_{DS(on)}$ scales inversely with area; at high voltage, $C_{oss}$ and $Q_{rr}$ matter more.
- **Bidirectional** operation changes the duty seen by each device, so a symmetric bridge may well be the right trade there.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the optimal ratio use a square root?
Because loss is conductance-inverse and area is conductance. Minimizing $D/G_H + (1-D)/G_L$ at fixed $G_H+G_L$ gives $G_H^2/G_L^2 = D/(1-D)$. A simpler guess ($G_H/G_L = D/(1-D)$) would over-skew the sizing: the marginal benefit of area diminishes as a device gets bigger.

### For $D=0.1$ and 10 A, how much does the optimum save?
Symmetrical 10 mΩ pair: $I^2R_0 = 1.0$ W. Optimal 20 mΩ / 6.67 mΩ: $100\times(0.1\times0.02 + 0.9\times0.00667) = 0.8$ W. A 20% saving for the same total area, 0.2 W less heat concentrated in the low side.

### When would you deliberately use a symmetrical bridge anyway?
For a bidirectional converter, a four-switch buck-boost, an inverter or a motor half bridge near 50% duty, and when one part number simplifies sourcing and the loss difference is only a few percent (for example $D=0.3$ to $0.7$).

### Would you also size the FETs differently for switching loss?
Yes. The high side wants low $Q_{gd}$ and $C_{oss}$, even at higher $R_{DS(on)}$ than the conduction-only optimum, because it hard-switches at $f_{sw}$. The low side wants the lowest $R_{DS(on)}$ and low $Q_{rr}$. At high $f_{sw}$ the optimal high-side size shrinks further.

### In a 48 V to 1 V converter the low side gets very hot. What do you do?
Duty is around 0.02, so the low side carries about 98% of the conduction loss. Use a much larger (or paralleled) low-side FET, a smaller fast high-side FET, consider a two-stage conversion (48 V to 12 V, then to 1 V) or an interleaved multiphase design to share the current across devices.
:::

::: interpretations
The wording "symmetrical power bridge" is unusual. The main reading here is a half bridge whose two switches are identical (same type and size). Two other possible readings: (a) a **complementary** half bridge with an NFET and a PFET sized for matched $R_{DS(on)}$ (the "symmetric" inverter-like stage; the PFET is 2 to 3 times larger), and (b) a **full (H) bridge**, which is symmetrical in structure. For (a), Q204 and Q207 give the NFET/PFET trade and shoot-through behavior; for (b), the H-bridge adds polarity reversal at the cost of two more switches. If unsure, say your assumption out loud and answer for that reading.
:::
