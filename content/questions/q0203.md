::: basics
### In plain words
Synchronous rectification means replacing the buck's freewheel diode with a second MOSFET. This MOSFET turns on when the main switch turns off, and carries the inductor current. A diode wastes a fixed voltage drop. A transistor acts like a small resistor, so it wastes much less.

### Picture it
Picture a turnstile versus an open gate. A diode is a turnstile: everyone who walks through must push against a stiff bar, which costs effort. A transistor is an open gate with a little friction. The catch is that someone must open and close the gate on time, which costs a little effort, and it must never be open at the same moment as the main switch, or current would rush straight through both.

### Words you need
- **Synchronous rectifier**: a transistor doing the job of a diode, timed by the controller.
- **Forward drop**: the voltage across a conducting diode, about 0.3 to 0.5 V for a Schottky.
- **$R_{DS(on)}$**: a transistor's small resistance when fully on.
- **Dead time**: a brief gap so the two transistors never conduct together ([[q0207]]).
- **Diode emulation**: turning the low-side transistor off when its current reaches zero, so it behaves like a diode and saves power at light load.

### The simplest example
A 12 V to 3.3 V buck delivers 2 A, and the lower device conducts 72.5 percent of the time. A Schottky with a 0.45 V drop wastes $0.45 \times 2 \times 0.725 \approx 0.65$ W. A 10 mΩ transistor wastes $2^2 \times 0.01 \times 0.725 \approx 0.03$ W. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the extra costs: a second gate driver, dead time, gate-drive loss, and light-load behavior.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can explain a design choice as a **trade-off with numbers**, not a slogan. The interviewer is checking:

1. What synchronous rectification actually is (a FET driven complementary to the high side, replacing the freewheel diode).
2. The core benefit, quantified: $I\,R_{DS(on)}$ vs $V_f$, and why the gain grows at low $V_{out}$ and high current.
3. The costs you must know without prompting: dead time and shoot-through, body-diode loss and recovery, extra gate-drive power, and especially the light-load reverse-current problem.
4. How real controllers fix it: diode emulation, PFM and FCCM modes.
:::

::: why
Sync rectification is why a modern buck reaches 90 to 97% efficiency at 1 to 5 V outputs. It also introduces a set of failure modes (shoot-through, negative current, EMI) that cause real board bring-up problems. Understanding both sides of the trade is the difference between choosing a buck IC from the datasheet headline efficiency and choosing it because you know the load profile. Questions [[q0207]], [[q0208]] and [[q0210]] in this section dig into each cost in detail, so this answer should map out the whole landscape.
:::

::: concepts
- **Freewheel path**: the low side must conduct inductor current during $(1-D)T$ ([[q0202]]).
- **Conduction loss**: $I^2R$ in a FET vs $V_f\,I$ in a diode ([[q0201]]).
- **Complementary drive with dead time**: $Q_2$ on only when $Q_1$ is off, with a gap ([[q0207]]).
- **Continuous vs discontinuous conduction**: below $I_{out} = \Delta I_L/2$ the current would go negative unless the low side is turned off ([[q0185]]).
- **Body diode and reverse recovery**: the FET's intrinsic diode carries current in dead time ([[q0208]]).
- **Gate-drive loss**: $Q_g V_{drv} f_{sw}$ per FET, independent of load.
:::

::: answer
"Synchronous rectification means replacing the buck's freewheel diode with a second MOSFET on the low side. It's driven opposite to the high-side FET: when the high side is off, the low side turns on and carries the inductor current.

The win is efficiency. A diode has a fixed forward drop, 0.3 to 0.5 V for a Schottky, so its loss is $V_f \cdot I$ times the fraction of time it conducts. A FET behaves like a resistor, so the loss is $I^2 R_{DS(on)}$. For 12 V in, 3.3 V out and 2 A, the Schottky wastes about 650 mW and a 10 mΩ FET about 30 mW. The advantage grows as the output voltage falls, because the low side conducts more of the cycle, and the converter runs cooler.

The cons are complexity and new failure modes. You need a second gate driver and dead time so both FETs never conduct together; during that gap the body diode conducts and adds loss and reverse-recovery charge. You pay gate-drive loss $Q_g V f$ for the extra FET even at no load. And at light load, a fully synchronous bridge lets the inductor current go negative, which circulates energy for no benefit. So controllers add diode emulation or PFM to turn the low side off when current reaches zero."
:::

::: technical
**Operation.** $Q_1$ on for $DT$; $Q_2$ on for about $(1-D)T$; both off for a dead time $t_d$ at each transition.

**Conduction loss.** With RMS inductor current $I_{L,rms}^2 = I_{out}^2 + \Delta I_L^2/12$:
$$P_{Q2} = (1-D)\,I_{L,rms}^2 R_{DS(on),2},\qquad P_D = (1-D)\,I_{out}V_f$$
Break-even current $I = V_f/R_{DS(on)}$: 45 A for 0.45 V and 10 mΩ.

**Numbers** ($12\to3.3$ V, 2 A): $P_D = 0.65$ W (efficiency 91% from this term). $P_{Q2} = 0.030$ W (99.6%). At 5 V to 1.8 V and 3 A, $D=0.36$: Schottky 0.96 W, 12 mΩ FET 0.069 W.

**Added costs.**
- **Gate drive**: $P_g = Q_g V_{drv} f_{sw}$. Example: 3 nC at 5 V and 500 kHz is 7.5 mW per FET.
- **Dead-time loss**: $P_{dt} \approx V_{SD}\,I\,(t_{d1}+t_{d2})\,f_{sw}$. Example: 0.75 V, 2 A, 30 ns total, 500 kHz gives 22.5 mW.
- **Reverse recovery** of the body diode: about $Q_{rr}V_{in}f_{sw}$. Example: 5 nC at 12 V is 30 mW, and rings the SW node ([[q0209]]).
- **Reverse current** when $I_{out} < \Delta I_L/2$: valley current is $I_{out}-\Delta I_L/2$. For $I_{out}=0.2$ A and $\Delta I_L=1.02$ A it is $-0.31$ A. Mitigated by zero-current detection (diode emulation) or PFM.

**Control-mode language.** FCCM (forced continuous) keeps constant frequency and low ripple at any load, at the cost of light-load efficiency. DEM (diode-emulation mode) or PFM trades that for light-load efficiency.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Dead-time, $Q_g$ and $Q_{rr}$ values vary widely by device and gate driver. The numbers above are representative for a 12 V, 2 to 3 A converter, not datasheet values.
:::

::: short
"Synchronous rectification replaces the freewheel diode with a low-side MOSFET driven complementary to the high side. It wins on efficiency: loss is $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ instead of $V_f\cdot I$, so at 3.3 V out and 2 A you cut roughly 650 mW to about 30 mW. The costs are a second gate driver, dead time to avoid shoot-through with body-diode loss during it, gate-drive power at all loads, and negative inductor current at light load unless you add diode emulation or PFM."
:::

::: long
"Synchronous rectification means replacing the buck's diode with a second MOSFET on the low side, driven opposite to the high-side FET. When the high side is on, the low side is off, and the inductor ramps up. When the high side turns off, the low side turns on and carries the inductor current down.

The benefit is efficiency. A diode's loss is its forward drop times current, fixed at about 0.3 to 0.5 V even for a Schottky. A FET is a resistor, so its loss is $I$ squared times $R_{DS(on)}$. At 12 V in, 3.3 V out and 2 A the Schottky wastes about 650 mW, about a tenth of the output. A 10 milliohm FET wastes about 30 mW. And the effect grows at low output voltage, because the low side conducts for $1-D$ of the cycle. At 1 V out, a diode caps efficiency around 70%.

The cons come in four groups. First, complexity: a second driver, a bootstrap for the high side, and dead time control. Second, dead time itself: with both FETs off, the body diode conducts, which has a higher drop than a Schottky and has reverse-recovery charge that causes ringing and loss at the next high-side turn-on. Third, fixed overhead: the second FET adds gate charge loss and output capacitance loss at every load, including light load. Fourth, the light-load problem: at low load the ripple current is bigger than twice the load current, and a synchronous FET allows the inductor current to go negative, pulling energy back from the output. That wastes power.

Controllers handle that with diode emulation, turning the low side off at zero current, or with PFM or pulse skipping at very light loads. Some applications deliberately use forced continuous mode for constant frequency and low noise."
:::

::: terms
- **Synchronous rectification**: using an actively controlled FET as the rectifying element instead of a diode.
- **Asynchronous (diode) buck**: a buck with a diode instead of the low-side FET.
- **Dead time**: gap with both FETs off to prevent shoot-through.
- **Shoot-through (cross-conduction)**: both FETs conducting, shorting $V_{in}$ to ground.
- **FCCM**: forced continuous conduction mode; the inductor current may go negative.
- **Diode emulation (DEM)**: turning the low-side FET off when the inductor current reaches zero, mimicking a diode.
- **Zero-current detection**: comparator that senses SW or $I_L$ to find the zero crossing.
- **Reverse recovery ($Q_{rr}$)**: charge the body diode must remove when it is turned off.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Sync rectification uses a FET instead of a diode, so it is more efficient but needs more control."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Compares $V_f\,I$ with $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ with numbers and finds the crossover (about 45 A for 0.45 V and 10 mΩ).
- Says the benefit grows as $V_{out}$ falls because the low side conducts for $(1-D)$.
- Lists costs by mechanism: gate drive ($Q_gV f$), dead-time body-diode loss, $Q_{rr}$, $C_{oss}$, and shoot-through risk.
- Explains the light-load reverse current with valley current $I_{out} - \Delta I/2$ and names diode emulation, PFM and FCCM as the cure and trade.
- Notes that Schottky-assisted sync (a parallel Schottky) and GaN/zero-$Q_{rr}$ devices attack the body-diode part.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Sync is always more efficient."** At very light load, fixed gate-drive and $C_{oss}$ losses and negative-current circulation can make it worse than a diode or a PFM mode.
- **"The low-side FET is on whenever the high side is off."** There is a dead time in which both are off; ignoring it hides the body-diode loss and shoot-through discussion.
- **Quoting the diode loss without the $(1-D)$ factor.** The diode conducts for $(1-D)T$, so low-$V_{out}$ designs lose most.
- **Calling shoot-through "just a small spike".** With 12 V across two milliohm-class FETs, the peak current is limited by device saturation and wiring to tens of amps or more ([[q0207]]).
- **Assuming sync removes reverse recovery.** The body diode of the low-side FET still has $Q_{rr}$, and it conducts every cycle in dead time.
:::

## Conduction loss: the main reason

The low side conducts for $(1-D)$ of the period. Include the ripple current in the RMS value:

$$I_{L,rms}^2 = I_{out}^2 + \frac{\Delta I_L^2}{12}$$

| Case ($V_{in}=12$ V, 2 A) | $D$ | Diode (0.45 V) | Sync (10 mΩ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $V_{out}=5$ V | 0.417 | 0.52 W | 0.024 W |
| $V_{out}=3.3$ V | 0.275 | 0.65 W | 0.030 W |
| $V_{out}=1.8$ V | 0.15 | 0.77 W | 0.034 W |
| $V_{out}=1.0$ V | 0.083 | 0.83 W | 0.037 W |

As the output voltage falls the diode loss rises, while the output power falls, so the efficiency penalty explodes: about 71% at 1 V.

## The costs, one by one

**1. Gate drive.** Each FET needs $Q_g V_{drv}$ per cycle. With $Q_g = 3$ nC at 5 V: 15 nJ per cycle, 7.5 mW at 500 kHz per FET. This is independent of load. A diode has no gate. At light load that overhead dominates.

**2. Dead time.** Both FETs must never conduct together ([[q0207]]). In the gap the body diode of $Q_2$ carries $I_L$ at a silicon $V_{SD}\approx0.7$ to 1 V:

$$P_{dt} = V_{SD}\,I_L\,(t_{d,fall}+t_{d,rise})\,f_{sw}$$

For 0.75 V, 2 A, 30 ns total: 22.5 mW. Small, but it grows linearly with $f_{sw}$ ([[q0216]]).

**3. Reverse recovery.** When $Q_1$ turns on, the body diode of $Q_2$, which was conducting, must be swept out. The stored charge $Q_{rr}$ flows through $Q_1$ from $V_{in}$ as a current spike, causing loss $\approx Q_{rr}V_{in}f_{sw}$ and SW ringing. For 5 nC: 30 mW. A Schottky has essentially none ([[q0209]]).

**4. Output capacitance.** $Q_2$ is a bigger device than a diode junction; $C_{oss}$ is charged and discharged every cycle.

## The light-load problem

In a diode converter the current stops at zero: below $I_{out} = \Delta I_L/2$ the converter enters DCM automatically, because the diode blocks reverse current. A synchronous FET does not block, so the valley current goes negative:

$$I_{valley} = I_{out} - \frac{\Delta I_L}{2}$$

At $I_{out} = 0.2$ A and $\Delta I_L = 1.02$ A the current swings from $-0.31$ A to $+0.71$ A.

That is why PFM modes reduce switching frequency at light load.

## Mode names you will see in datasheets

| Mode | Behavior | Use |
|---|---|---|
| FCCM / "forced PWM" | Constant $f_{sw}$, negative current allowed | Low noise, constant spectrum, fast load response |
| DEM / "auto" | $Q_2$ off at zero current, still PWM | Better light-load efficiency |
| PFM / "power-save" | Skips pulses or lowers frequency at low load | Best light-load efficiency, spectrum varies |

## When it is not worth it

- Very high $V_{out}$ (say 24 V and above at low current) where $V_f/V_{out}$ is already small.
- Cost- or size-limited designs where a diode is cheaper than a second FET and driver.
- Ultra-low-current always-on rails where gate-drive loss exceeds the diode's loss.

## Worked example: where the fixed losses go, and what they do to efficiency

Take the 12 V to 3.3 V, 500 kHz converter above and add up the load-independent losses of a forced-CCM synchronous bridge. The per-cycle items from the previous section are gate drive of two FETs (15 mW), dead-time body-diode conduction (22.5 mW) and reverse recovery (30 mW). Add $C_{oss}$ charging (about 11 mW) and switching overlap (about 60 mW at 2 A) and the sum is about 140 mW at full load. At light load in forced CCM the overlap, dead-time and recovery terms shrink, because the current at the switching edges falls to about ±0.5 A, so the model below uses a round 110 mW, inside the 100 to 150 mW range quoted above, plus a few milliwatts of controller quiescent current. Treat it as an assumed constant, not a derived one. The ripple-current conduction loss does not vanish at zero load either. Using an assumed total series resistance of 70 mΩ (two FETs plus inductor winding resistance), $\Delta I^2/12\times R=0.0864\times0.07=6$ mW, which matches the figure quoted earlier.

With those assumptions, efficiency in forced CCM is

$$\eta(I)=\frac{3.3\,I}{3.3\,I+0.11+(I^2+0.0864)(0.07)}$$

At 2 A the denominator is $6.6+0.11+0.29=7.0$ W, so $\eta=94.3\%$. At 0.2 A it is $0.66/(0.66+0.11+0.006+0.003)=84.7\%$, and at 50 mA it collapses to $0.165/(0.165+0.116)=59\%$. At 10 mA it is 22%: the converter burns 0.11 W to deliver 33 mW. A pulse-skipping or PFM mode scales the fixed loss down with load because it switches less often. As a rough model take the fixed loss as $3\text{ mW}+107\text{ mW}\times\min(1,I/0.5\text{ A})$, and the same 50 mA load comes in near 92%.

The practical lessons are that sync rectification is a high-load win and a light-load risk, and that "FCCM" is a choice you make for noise, not efficiency.

## Limiting cases and where the simple model breaks

At very high $V_{out}$ the low-side duty $(1-D)$ shrinks and the diode's $V_f$ is small relative to $V_{out}$, so sync loses its main argument. At very low $f_{sw}$ the dead-time and gate terms vanish and sync is essentially free, which is why low-frequency high-current converters use it even at modest ratios. At very high $f_{sw}$ the opposite holds: dead time of 30 ns at 2 MHz is 12% of the period, and the body diode conducts for a large fraction of $Q_2$'s on-time, so the 10 mΩ figure of merit stops mattering. In that regime GaN devices help through their much lower gate charge and zero reverse-recovery charge. They have no body diode, but they still conduct in reverse during dead time, with a third-quadrant drop of roughly 2 V or more, so their dead time must be kept very short or that term gets worse, not better.

The conduction-loss formula also assumes $R_{DS(on)}$ is constant. It rises with junction temperature, by roughly 50% to 70% from 25 °C to 125 °C for common silicon MOSFETs (check the datasheet's normalized curve). A design that meets its efficiency target on a cold bench can miss it in a hot enclosure.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The temperature coefficient of $R_{DS(on)}$ depends on the device technology and voltage class. The 50% to 70% range is typical of low-voltage silicon power MOSFETs, not a universal rule.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Efficiency sweep.** Measure $V_{in}I_{in}$ and $V_{out}I_{out}$ at 10 mA, 50 mA, 200 mA, 1 A and 2 A, ideally with a source-measure unit or two precision meters. Plot efficiency against the log of load current and compare to the shape above. A part with a mode pin should show the red curve when forced and the green curve in auto or power-save.
2. **Negative inductor current.** At 50 mA in forced mode, put a current probe on the inductor. The waveform should cross zero every cycle, valley near $-0.46$ A. In diode-emulation mode the current should sit at zero for part of each cycle.
3. **Dead time.** Probe SW at a short ground and look for the roughly $-0.7$ V shelves at each edge. Measure their widths and compare with $t_d$. Adaptive dead-time parts will shift them with load.
4. **Temperature.** Compare $V_{DS}$ of $Q_2$ at the same current cold and after warm-up. The ratio gives you the $R_{DS(on)}$ temperature coefficient directly.

::: applications
- **Point-of-load converters for FPGA/MCU rails (0.9 to 3.3 V)**: synchronous buck ICs where efficiency at low $V_{out}$ is the point.
- **CPU/GPU VRMs**: multiphase synchronous bucks delivering over 100 A at about 1 V; a diode there would burn tens of watts.
- **Battery-powered devices**: PFM/auto mode with diode emulation at light load for standby efficiency.
- **Bidirectional converters**: FCCM synchronous bridges allow current in both directions, for example in battery chargers or regenerative systems.
- **Audio or RF-sensitive supplies**: FCCM so the switching frequency stays fixed and out of the sensitive band.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM with a constant on-resistance** and a Schottky $V_f$ of 0.45 V; real $V_f$ varies with current and temperature, and $R_{DS(on)}$ rises about 50 to 100% from 25 to 125 °C.
- **Loss terms are approximations** (first order); real $Q_{rr}$ and dead time depend on di/dt and layout.
- **Silicon MOSFETs**; GaN devices have no $Q_{rr}$ but a higher reverse conduction drop in dead time.
- If the question is asked of a **non-buck** converter (boost, buck-boost), the same trade holds with the rectifier on the other half-cycle.
- For very low output currents, **PFM and diode emulation** make a synchronous buck as efficient as an asynchronous one or better.
:::

::: followups
### At what output voltage does sync rectification help the most?
The lowest. The low side conducts for $(1-D)$ of the cycle, and the diode drop is a fixed voltage compared with a shrinking $V_{out}$. At 1 V out and 2 A with a 0.45 V Schottky the diode alone caps efficiency near 71%. At 12 V out the same diode loss is under 1% of the output.

### What is diode emulation and how does it work?
A comparator watches the voltage across the low-side FET (or a current sense) and turns $Q_2$ off when the current reaches zero, mimicking a diode's block. The delay of the comparator can let a small negative current flow, so designers trim the threshold, and at zero current the SW node rings against the inductor and capacitances until the next cycle.

### Why do some sync bucks still put a Schottky in parallel with the low-side FET?
During dead time the body diode drops 0.7 to 1 V and has $Q_{rr}$; a Schottky in parallel conducts instead at about 0.4 V with no recovery. It only works if the connection inductance is small, since $L\,di/dt$ across a few nH of lead can exceed the difference; hence many ICs integrate it ([[q0210]]).

### What if the dead time is much too long? Too short?
Too long: the body diode carries current longer, adding $V_{SD}\,I\,t_d\,f_{sw}$ loss, and heats up. Too short or negative: both FETs overlap, shoot-through, huge current spikes through $V_{in}$. Adaptive dead time senses the SW node or the gate voltage to set the gap as small as is safe.

### A sync buck's efficiency at 50 mA load is poor despite a good datasheet peak. Why?
Datasheet peak efficiency is at moderate load in a specific mode. At 50 mA in FCCM, fixed losses (gate drive, $C_{oss}$, switching, quiescent) dominate and the ripple current circulates. Check whether the part is in auto/PFM mode, the quiescent current, and whether the output current is below $\Delta I_L/2$ so the inductor goes negative.
:::

::: interpretations
"Pros/cons" is open-ended, so a good answer groups them: efficiency and thermal gains on one side; complexity, dead time, body diode, gate drive and light-load behavior on the other. The section note says many of these questions assume synchronous rectification, so be ready to give the comparison with the diode as the baseline. Q201 asks which bridge is common; Q203 asks to explain the technique and its trade-offs.
:::

::: references
- Erickson & Maksimovic, *Fundamentals of Power Electronics*: synchronous rectifiers and discontinuous conduction mode.
- Texas Instruments, *Basic Calculation of a Buck Converter's Power Stage* (SLVA477): conduction, switching and dead-time loss terms.
- Mohan, Undeland & Robbins, *Power Electronics*: MOSFET switching and synchronous rectification.
- Datasheets of synchronous buck regulators from major vendors: "forced PWM", "auto/PFM" and "diode emulation" mode descriptions.
:::

::: rubric
- Defines it: low-side FET replaces the diode, driven complementary with dead time :: Without the definition, the pros and cons float free.
- Explains the conduction-loss benefit with $I R_{DS(on)}$ vs $V_f$ and a number :: The core reason for the technique.
- States the benefit is largest at low $V_{out}$ and high current :: Shows you see the dependence, not just the headline.
- Lists dead time, body-diode conduction and shoot-through risk :: The reliability and loss costs most often asked as follow-ups.
- Mentions extra gate-drive loss and added complexity or cost :: The overhead that matters at light load.
- Explains the light-load negative-current issue and fixes (diode emulation, PFM) :: Differentiates textbook from practical understanding.
- Mentions reverse recovery or EMI effects :: Shows awareness of second-order side effects.
:::
