::: basics
### In plain words
A ground plane shields because it is a good conductor, and a good conductor does three things to nearby fields. It stops electric field lines, so they do not reach the other side. It carries the return current, whose magnetic field cancels most of the trace's own. And at higher frequencies, above a few MHz, currents induced in the copper soak up what is left. A solid plane between two layers cuts the coupling between them a lot, as long as it has no slots or holes.

### Picture it
Picture a thick wall between two noisy rooms. Voices hit the wall instead of passing through, so the neighbor hears much less. A hole or gap in the wall lets sound leak straight through, and a thin wall does little against deep rumbles. A ground plane is that wall. Slots and holes leak, and a thin copper plane does almost nothing against slow magnetic fields, such as 50 Hz hum from mains wiring.

### Words you need
- **Electric field**: the push a voltage exerts across space. Its field lines end on a conductor ([[q0022]]).
- **Return current**: the equal and opposite current in the plane under a trace. Its field cancels the trace's field.
- **Mutual capacitance**: the small capacitance between two traces that couples a signal from one to the other ([[q0565]]).
- **Aggressor and victim**: the trace that makes the noise, and the trace that picks it up.
- **Aperture**: a slot or hole in the shield through which fields leak.

### The simplest example
An aggressor trace swings 3.3 V and couples through 0.5 pF to a victim input that has 5 pF to ground. The two capacitances split the voltage like a divider, so the victim sees $3.3 \times 0.5/(0.5+5) = 0.30$ V, a big glitch. Put a grounded plane between them and the direct coupling falls to about 0.05 pF (the page assumes a tenth). Now the victim sees $3.3 \times 0.05/(0.05+5) \approx 0.033$ V, about nine times less. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the three mechanisms and their limits: leaks through holes, board edges, slow magnetic fields and noise shared through the plane.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can explain shielding **from physics**, separating three mechanisms: electric-field termination (a conductor is an equipotential, so field lines end on it and don't reach the other side), return-current/image cancellation (the plane carries an equal and opposite current under the trace, so the net magnetic field falls off quickly), and eddy-current absorption at higher frequency (skin depth shorter than the copper thickness). The interviewer also wants the limits: apertures, edges, low-frequency magnetic fields, and plane noise.
:::

::: why
"Put a ground plane between those layers" is the standard fix for crosstalk and emissions, but it's only reliable if you know why it works and when it doesn't. Without that, people cut the shield with slots, route across it, or expect it to block 50 Hz hum. This question also ties to EMI shields ([[q0569]]–[[q0573]]) and to crosstalk control ([[q0565]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Electric-field boundary condition**: tangential $E=0$ at a good conductor, so field lines terminate on it. See [[q0022]].
- **Capacitive coupling**: $C$ between traces falls when a grounded conductor intercepts the field. See [[q0565]].
- **Image currents**: a conductor plane makes a trace's current look like a pair of opposing currents. See [[q0489]].
- **Skin effect**: current and fields penetrate only $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu)}$.
- **Shielding effectiveness**: reflection plus absorption loss in dB.
- **Apertures and discontinuities**: slots and holes leak. See [[q0487]].
:::

::: answer
"A ground plane shields because it's a good conductor, and a good conductor does three things to the fields around it.

First, electric fields: a conductor is an equipotential, so the electric field lines from a trace end on the plane instead of passing through. If I put a plane between two signal layers, the aggressor's field terminates on the plane and never reaches the victim, so the capacitive coupling between them is mostly gone. That works at any frequency, as long as the plane is solid and grounded.

Second, magnetic fields: the return current flows in the plane right under the signal, equal and opposite to the signal current. The two fields cancel as you move away, so the net field is much smaller than the field of a signal with a far-away return. That's the same as saying the plane makes an image of the trace with opposite current.

Third, at higher frequency the plane absorbs fields directly: eddy currents in the copper oppose the incident magnetic field, and once the skin depth is smaller than the thickness, which for one ounce copper is above a few megahertz, absorption grows quickly, about 47 dB at 100 MHz.

The limits are that it only shields if it's continuous: slots, holes and the board edge leak, and a thin plane does almost nothing against low-frequency magnetic fields like 50 Hz hum."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanisms**

1. **E-field termination.** A grounded plane interposed between two traces cuts their mutual capacitance to a small fringe/edge value. Coupling proxy for traces over a plane: $k\approx1/(1+(s/h)^2)$. With the plane between layers, the direct broadside coupling is replaced by the (small) coupling through gaps.
2. **Image currents/return current cancellation.** Over a plane at height $h$, the field at distance $r\gg h$ scales like $h/r^2$ relative to a single current (dipole of separation $2h$). Moving $h$ from 1.6 mm to 0.2 mm cuts the far field by $\approx8\times$.
3. **Eddy-current (absorption) shielding.** $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu_0)}$; absorption loss $A=8.686\,t/\delta$ dB:

| $f$ | $\delta$ (Cu) | $A$ for 35 µm |
|---|---|---|
| 10 kHz | 652 µm | 0.5 dB |
| 1 MHz | 65 µm | 4.7 dB |
| 10 MHz | 20.6 µm | 14.7 dB |
| 100 MHz | 6.5 µm | 46.6 dB |
| 1 GHz | 2.1 µm | 147 dB |

Reflection loss adds for electric fields (large) and is small for low-impedance magnetic fields.

**Limits.** Apertures: a slot of length $\ell$ leaks at about $SE\approx20\log(\lambda_0/2\ell)$ dB; vias' anti-pads, board edges, and cutouts all count. Low-frequency magnetic fields (e.g. 50 Hz) pass through thin copper. And the plane itself carries return currents, so noise currents flowing in it can couple to circuits that share the conductor.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The $8.686\,t/\delta$ absorption formula assumes a plane wave and a thick-enough conductor model; real near-field shielding (especially magnetic, close to a source) differs. The aperture formula is a rule of thumb. Use these as scaling guides.
:::

::: long
"There are really three mechanisms, and it helps to keep them separate.

The first is for electric fields. A conductor is an equipotential, so any electric field line coming from a trace has to end on the plane. If I put a ground plane between two signal layers, the aggressor's field lines land on the plane instead of reaching the victim trace, so the capacitive coupling between layers is mostly eliminated. That works at any frequency as long as the plane is solid and grounded.

The second is the return current. A trace over a plane has its return current in the plane directly underneath, equal and opposite. Currents in opposite directions make opposing magnetic fields, so they cancel at a distance. Equivalent picture: the plane creates an image of the trace with opposite current, and the pair behaves like a tiny dipole. The closer the plane, the smaller the dipole and the less field. Moving from 1.6 millimetres to 0.2 millimetres cuts the far field by about eight times.

The third is absorption. Eddy currents in the copper oppose an incident magnetic field. Skin depth in copper is 65 microns at 1 MHz and 6.5 microns at 100 MHz. Once it's shorter than the 35 microns of one ounce copper, absorption loss is 8.7 dB per thickness: about 15 dB at 10 MHz and 47 dB at 100 MHz. At 1 kHz the skin depth is 2 millimetres, so a thin plane does nothing against 50 Hz hum.

And the limits: the plane has to be continuous. Slots, rows of overlapping anti-pads, and the board edge are apertures, and leakage goes as 20 log of the wavelength over twice the slot length. So I keep the plane unbroken, stitch the edges, and put a ground plane, not just a pour, between sensitive layers."
:::

::: terms
- **Shielding effectiveness (SE)**: ratio of field without and with the shield, in dB.
- **Image current**: the equivalent opposite current that replaces a conducting plane in field calculations.
- **Eddy current**: circulating current induced in a conductor by a changing magnetic field.
- **Skin depth**: $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu)}$; the penetration depth.
- **Absorption loss**: attenuation inside the shield, $8.686\,t/\delta$ dB.
- **Reflection loss**: attenuation from impedance mismatch at the shield surface.
- **Aperture**: opening in a shield that leaks fields.
- **Mutual capacitance**: capacitance between two conductors that carries capacitive coupling.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer separates electric, return-current and absorption mechanisms, notes which work at which frequency (electric: all; image/cancellation: all, because it's the field of the pair; absorption: above the skin-depth threshold), and gives numbers (47 dB at 100 MHz, 0.5 dB at 10 kHz). It lists the leaks: slots, via fields, edges and the signal vias passing through, and notes that the plane is itself a conductor that can carry noise. It also distinguishes between a shield plane in the stackup and an enclosure/shield can.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "ground is zero volts so fields can't pass".** The mechanism is conductor boundary conditions and eddy currents, not "0 V".
- **Expecting a thin plane to block 50/60 Hz magnetic hum.** It doesn't; skin depth is millimetres.
- **A floating (ungrounded) plane.** An unconnected conductor still intercepts some field, but it couples capacitively to both sides; it should be tied to ground.
- **Forgetting apertures:** a slot or a via row with overlapping anti-pads leaks.
- **Relying on a ground pour with traces through it** as a shield.
- **Believing the plane stops crosstalk between traces on the same layer.** For those, spacing, guard traces and plane distance matter.
:::

## Mechanism 1: electric field termination

At the surface of a perfect conductor the tangential electric field is zero and the field is normal to the surface. Field lines from a charge near the conductor end on induced surface charge. The conductor does not need to be at 0 V; it needs to be an equipotential that doesn't carry the aggressor's potential to the victim. (A *floating* conductor would pick up potential from the aggressor and couple to the victim through capacitance, an *electrostatic* divider. A grounded plane holds its potential, so it drains the coupling.)

For capacitive coupling: aggressor-to-victim mutual capacitance $C_m$ and victim impedance $Z_v$ give a coupled voltage $V_v\approx V_a\,\frac{j\omega C_mZ_v}{1+j\omega C_m Z_v}\approx j\omega C_mZ_vV_a$. Add a grounded shield in between and $C_m$ is replaced by the tiny capacitance through the gaps, so $V_v$ falls by the ratio.

## Mechanism 2: the return current and the image

The signal current $I$ on a trace and the return current $-I$ in the plane form a loop whose magnetic fields oppose. At distance $r\gg h$, the combined field is that of a dipole of separation $\approx2h$ (image theory), so it falls like $h/r^2$ times the single-wire field. For a given $r$, the field with $h=0.2$ mm is $0.2/1.6=1/8$ of the field with $h=1.6$ mm. In decibels that's 18 dB less.

For crosstalk, the same effect reduces coupling: the return current of the aggressor is concentrated within about $\pm3h$ of the trace; neighbours beyond that see far less. A rough proxy $k=1/(1+(s/h)^2)$ gives:

## Mechanism 3: eddy currents and absorption

When a time-varying magnetic field hits a conductor, eddy currents flow in a way that opposes the field (Lenz's law), reducing the field inside. The field decays as $e^{-x/\delta}$. Absorption loss through a thickness $t$ is $20\log_{10}e^{t/\delta}=8.686\,t/\delta$ dB.

## The limits

**Apertures.** A slot or hole of length $\ell$ leaks. The estimate is $SE\approx20\log_{10}(\lambda_0/2\ell)$ dB (see [[q0487]]). At 1 GHz a 10 mm slot gives about 23.5 dB; a 30 mm slot about 14 dB. A row of vias with overlapping anti-pads in an inner plane forms such a slot. A signal via passing through the plane has its anti-pad too.

**Edges.** The board edge is a boundary where fields wrap around. Stitching vias near the edge cut that leakage.

**Low frequency.** A 35 µm plane has a skin depth that is thin compared with the penetration depth at kHz frequencies. The plane does reduce *loop-area* pickup (by hosting the return current), but doesn't "absorb" field.

**Noise in the plane.** If noise currents flow through the plane near a victim, they create voltage gradients: the plane shields fields, not conducted currents. Keep return currents away from sensitive circuits.

**Floating or pour.** A pour with traces cut through it and few stitching vias is a poor shield.

## Using the shield properly

1. Put a solid ground plane between sensitive layers (or between a noisy layer and a sensitive one).
2. Keep the plane unbroken in the shielded region; don't route across slots.
3. Stitch to other ground layers, especially near the edge and around openings.
4. Keep sensitive traces close to the plane (small $h$).
5. For stronger shielding, add a can or enclosure; see [[q0569]]–[[q0573]].

> [!INTERVIEW]
> The strongest short answer: "Electric fields end on the conductor, the return current cancels the magnetic field, and above a few MHz eddy currents absorb it. But a slot or an edge leaks, and it won't stop 50 Hz hum."

## Worked example: capacitive coupling with and without the shield

Put numbers on mechanism 1. An aggressor trace swings 3.3 V with a 1 ns edge and lies broadside over a victim trace on the next layer with no plane between them, with a mutual capacitance of $C_m=0.5$ pF. The victim is a high-impedance CMOS input (a floating-ish node) whose total capacitance to ground is $C_v=5$ pF. For a high-impedance victim the edge couples through a capacitive divider:

$$V_v\approx V_a\frac{C_m}{C_m+C_v}=3.3\times\frac{0.5}{5.5}=0.30\ \text{V}$$

That is a glitch of nearly a tenth of the supply, enough to trip a threshold in a noisy system. Now insert a grounded plane between the layers. The direct mutual capacitance drops to the fringe leakage around the plane's openings, say a tenth of the original (0.05 pF; the real ratio depends on the openings), while the victim's capacitance to ground rises slightly because the plane is now adjacent:

$$V_v\approx 3.3\times\frac{0.05}{0.05+5}=0.033\ \text{V}$$

Ten times less, 20 dB, and the glitch is below typical noise margins. For a low-impedance victim (a 50 Ω terminated line), the coupling is instead $V_v\approx Z_v C_m\,dV/dt = 50\times0.5\times10^{-12}\times3.3/10^{-9}=83$ mV without the plane and about a tenth of that with it. The scaling is the same: the coupled voltage goes down in proportion to $C_m$. Inductive (magnetic) crosstalk is handled by the return-current mechanism, not by this capacitance argument.

## Worked example: how long can a slot be?

Use the aperture rule $SE\approx20\log_{10}(\lambda_0/2\ell)$ in reverse to set a design limit. If an enclosure-level requirement means the plane region must shield by at least 40 dB at 1 GHz (free-space wavelength 300 mm), the slot must satisfy

$$\ell\le\frac{\lambda_0}{2\times10^{40/20}}=\frac{300\ \text{mm}}{200}=1.5\ \text{mm}$$

A single 1.5 mm gap is fine. But a row of ten through-vias at a 1 mm pitch with 0.8 mm anti-pads leaves only 0.2 mm necks between them, and if the necks are lost (anti-pad clearances larger than the pitch) the row is one 10 mm slot, which gives only $20\log(300/20)=23.5$ dB. That is why via rows under connectors and BGAs show up as emission hot spots after the first scan: the rule of thumb is that anything longer than a small fraction of the shortest wavelength of concern is an antenna.

## Worked example: why a thin plane cannot stop the buck's field

The page's scenario uses 400 kHz. Compute the absorption a 35 µm plane provides. The skin depth is $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu_0)}=103\ \mu\text{m}$ at 400 kHz, so

$$A=8.686\frac{t}{\delta}=8.686\times\frac{35}{103}=2.9\ \text{dB}$$

Almost nothing. To reach 40 dB of pure absorption at that frequency, the copper would have to be $t=\frac{40}{8.686}\delta\approx0.48$ mm thick, over 13 times thicker than 1 oz. So the plane's role at this frequency is not absorption. It cuts coupling by terminating the electric field and, more importantly, by carrying the return current so that the loop area (and thus both radiated and received magnetic coupling) shrinks. If the real problem is magnetic pickup of a buck's hot loop, the best fixes are: shrink the hot loop, increase separation (the $1/d^3$ falloff), and keep the victim's own loop small, rather than relying on shielding by copper. At mains frequencies (50/60 Hz hum) the skin depth is 8.5 to 9.3 mm and copper shielding is hopeless for the same reason, so the same loop-area and distance fixes apply.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **Image-plane effects need the plane to be continuous under both loops.** A plane with a split between aggressor and victim does not provide the return-current cancellation, even though it still blocks electric field in the area it covers.
- **Via anti-pads act as apertures.** Each signal via through a plane is a small hole; many are fine, a dense field is not. Stitching vias near the signal vias restore the shielding.
- **Board edges.** Fringing fields wrap around a plane's edge; a ring of stitching vias and a continuous copper edge reduce radiation from the layer sandwich. Fast traces within a few $h$ of the edge radiate noticeably.
- **Shield cans.** When copper layers are not enough, a soldered can over the noisy or sensitive circuit adds absorption and a closed Faraday surface. Reserve footprints for cans on the first spin, since adding them costs one board revision.

## Bench verification

Near-field probes make shielding visible. With a small E-field probe and an H-field loop probe on a spectrum analyzer, scan the board's surface and the back while the aggressor is running: the field should be strong at the aggressor and weak behind a continuous plane. A simple A/B test is to compare the same victim trace's pickup with a sheet of grounded copper tape (or adhesive foil) taped over the aggressor, which emulates the plane or can. For apertures, scan along a via row or plane edge and look for peaks at frequencies where the slot length approaches $\lambda/2$ ($f=c/(2\ell)$, for example 15 GHz for a 10 mm slot in air but lower in the dielectric). Document the spectrum before and after each fix so you can tell which change did something.

::: applications
- **Stackups that put a ground plane between two signal layers** (for example S/G/S/P/G/S), preventing broadside coupling.
- **Mixed-signal boards**: a solid ground under the analog front end shields it from the digital area's fields.
- **RF**: ground plane under the RF line plus via fences for lateral shielding.
- **Switching converters**: a ground plane directly under the power stage, reducing its radiated field and coupling to sense traces.
- **Cable entry**: stitched ground at the connector reduces fields coupling into the board.
:::

::: assumptions
- Solid, grounded copper plane of 1 oz (35 µm); thinner or perforated planes shield less.
- Near-field and far-field behave differently; the absorption formula is a plane-wave estimate.
- Rule-of-thumb apertures: the real leakage depends on slot geometry, thickness and the field orientation.
- Frequencies from kHz to GHz: electric-field shielding at all; magnetic field absorption above a few MHz.
- For magnetic shielding at power-line frequencies, high-permeability materials (mu-metal) are needed, which isn't a PCB-plane function.
:::

::: followups
### Does a floating metal plane shield?
Partially. It intercepts field lines and reduces direct coupling, but because it's floating it picks up the aggressor's potential and couples to the victim through its own capacitances, forming a capacitive divider. Grounding it fixes that by holding its potential at 0 V, so the coupling current is drained away.

### Why doesn't a ground plane stop 50 Hz hum from a nearby transformer?
At 50 Hz the skin depth in copper is about 9 mm, so a 35 µm plane absorbs essentially nothing. The hum is magnetic, so the fixes are to reduce the loop area picking it up (keep signal and return close), move away from the source, or use high-permeability shielding.

### How does a ground plane reduce crosstalk between two traces on the same layer?
It reduces it by confining each trace's field to the dielectric between the trace and the plane and concentrating the return current under the trace, which shrinks the overlap of their fields. The coupling falls like $1/(1+(s/h)^2)$ in the proxy, so a plane at $h$ and spacing $s=3h$ gives about 10%, and bringing the plane closer improves it further. A plane does not stop all crosstalk: spacing and guard traces still matter.

### What reduces the shielding of a plane in practice?
Slots and overlapping anti-pads, the board edge, connector cutouts, and signal vias passing through. Also if the plane isn't well connected to ground (floating), or is thin or perforated. Fix with stitching vias, closer via spacing, avoiding via rows in the plane, and keeping a ring of ground at the edge.

### Scenario: a sensitive analog trace on layer 3 picks up the 400 kHz switching of a buck converter whose power stage is on layer 1 directly above it, with no plane between. What do you change?
Insert a solid ground plane between them in the stackup (L2), move the analog trace to a layer beneath the plane, or move it laterally away from the power stage. At 400 kHz the skin depth is about 100 µm, so a thin plane's benefit is mainly the electric-field termination and return-current cancellation, not eddy-current absorption. Also reduce the buck's hot-loop area so it radiates less in the first place.
:::
