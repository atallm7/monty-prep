::: testing
Whether you can explain the two phases of the standard Li-ion charge **as control problems**: what is regulated, what floats, why the order is CC then CV, how the transition is detected and how termination works. Good answers derive the CV current decay ($I = (V_{set} - V_{OCV})/R$ and its time constant) and show why a bare voltage source would be dangerous at the start, as it would need to supply $(4.2 - 3.0)/R_{int}$, a huge current. The follow-up is usually how a charger IC implements it (two loops, the lower command wins) and what changes in a multi-cell pack.
:::

::: why
CC-CV is the algorithm inside every Li-ion charger, from earbuds to EVs. If you can explain it from first principles you can configure a charger IC, debug a charge that never terminates, predict charge time, or design the control loops. It also underlies the questions on charge curves ([[q0316]]), fast charge ([[q0317]]) and the charger design question ([[q0323]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Current regulation**: a loop senses current through a shunt and adjusts the pass element or switching duty to hold $I = I_{cc}$ ([[q0307]]).
- **Voltage regulation**: a loop senses battery voltage through a divider and holds $V = 4.20$ V.
- **Cell model**: $V_t = V_{OCV}(SoC) + I(R_0 + R_1)$ during charge ([[q0316]]).
- **Termination criteria**: end of charge when current falls below a threshold or time expires ([[q0320]]).
- **Min-selector of loops**: whichever loop demands less current is active.
- **Safety limits**: 4.20 V ±1 %, temperature window ([[q0319]]).
:::

::: answer
"Constant current, constant voltage is the standard Li-ion charge algorithm, and it's really two regulators sharing one power stage.

In the constant-current phase the charger regulates the current, for example 1C, and lets the voltage do what it wants. The cell's voltage rises as it charges: terminal voltage equals the open-circuit voltage plus $I$ times the internal resistance. That continues until the terminal voltage reaches 4.2 volts. For a 2 amp-hour cell at 1C that's about 50 minutes and about 85 percent charged in my model.

Then it switches to constant voltage. Now the charger regulates 4.2 volts, within about a percent, and the current becomes whatever the cell takes: $(4.2 - V_{OCV})/R$. As the cell fills, its open-circuit voltage rises toward 4.2, so the difference shrinks and the current decays roughly exponentially, with a time constant of about $RQ$ divided by the OCV slope; for 100 milliohms and 2 amp-hours that's about 10 minutes. When the current falls to about C over 20 to C over 10, the charger terminates; a timer is the backup.

Why that order? If I applied 4.2 volts to a flat cell at 3.0 volts, the current would be 1.2 volts over 50 milliohms, 24 amps, so I need the current limit first. And why not stay in CC? Because the terminal voltage would go above 4.2 volts, and the only way to fill the last 15 percent without overvoltage is to hold the voltage and let the current taper."
:::

::: technical
**Plant model during charge.** Terminal voltage $V_t = V_{OCV}(s) + I(R_0 + R_1(1 - e^{-t/\tau}))$, $\dot s = I/(3600\,Q)$. In steady state, $V_t \approx V_{OCV} + I R$ with $R = R_0 + R_1$.

**CC phase.** The loop regulates $I = I_{cc}$. With a sense resistor $R_s$ and a reference $V_{ref,I}$ (for example 100 mV), $I_{cc} = V_{ref,I}/R_s$; 100 mV / 50 mΩ = 2 A. $V_t$ rises monotonically until it reaches $V_{set} = 4.20$ V at SoC where $V_{OCV}(s_{CV}) = V_{set} - I_{cc}R$. For $R = 0.08$ Ω (model total) and $I_{cc} = 2$ A: $V_{OCV}(s_{CV}) = 4.04$ V, which is about 82 to 85 % SoC.

**CV phase.** The loop regulates $V_t = 4.20$ V, giving

$$I(t) = \frac{V_{set} - V_{OCV}(s(t))}{R}$$

and with $\dot s = I/Q$ and $V_{OCV}$ linearised near full with slope $k = dV_{OCV}/ds$:

$$\dot I = -\frac{k}{R Q}\,I \;\Rightarrow\; I(t) = I_0 e^{-t/\tau_{CV}},\quad \tau_{CV} = \frac{RQ}{k}$$

For $R = 0.1$ Ω, $Q = 2$ Ah = 7200 C, $k \approx 1.2$ V: $\tau_{CV} = 600$ s. Time to decay from $I_0 = I_{cc}$ to $I_{term}$: $t = \tau\,\ln(I_0/I_{term})$; from 2 A to 0.1 A (C/20): $600 \ln 20 = 1800$ s (30 min); model: about 26 min (the effective $\tau$ is slightly smaller).

**Why CC first.** With CV-only from 3.0 V: $I_{inrush} = (4.2 - 3.0)/0.05 = 24$ A (12C). The cell would be damaged; and the source would need to deliver that. CC enforces the limit.

**Termination.** $I < I_{term}$ with $I_{term} \approx C/20$ to $C/10$ ($0.1$ to $0.2$ A for 2 Ah), or a timer, or temperature. At the end the cell is at about 98 to 99 % of full capacity in the model.

**Charger implementation.** Two error amplifiers (current and voltage) both drive the pass element or PWM controller; the one demanding the lower output current wins ("min-selector"). Equivalent to a diode-OR of error signals. Charge accuracy: CV ±0.5 to 1 %, CC ±5 to 10 %.
:::

::: long
"CC-CV is two regulation modes of the same charger.

Constant current: the charger measures the current with a shunt and adjusts its power stage to hold, for example, 1C. The cell's terminal voltage is the open-circuit voltage plus $I$ times the internal resistance, so as the cell charges and its OCV rises, the terminal voltage ramps up. The charger doesn't regulate the voltage here; it follows. When it reaches 4.2 volts, I'm at a state of charge where $V_{OCV}$ is 4.2 minus $I R$; with 2 amps and about 80 milliohms that's about 4.04 volts, around 85 percent charged. In my model that's about 50 minutes at 1C.

Constant voltage: now the charger regulates the voltage at 4.2 volts, within about one percent. The current is whatever the cell accepts: $(4.2 - V_{OCV})/R$. As charge goes in, $V_{OCV}$ rises, the difference shrinks, and the current decays. Because the rate of rise of OCV is proportional to the current, you get an exponential decay with a time constant $\tau = R Q / (dV_{OCV}/dSoC)$. With 100 milliohms, 2 amp-hours and about 1.2 volts per unit SoC, that's 600 seconds. The charger terminates when the current drops to somewhere between C over 20 and C over 10, so 2 amps to 0.1 amps takes about thirty minutes in the formula, about 26 in the simulation.

The order matters. If I applied 4.2 volts straight to a cell at 3.0 volts with 50 milliohms, the inrush would be 24 amps, about 12C, so the current limit is first. And CV is needed because staying in CC beyond 4.2 volts would overcharge it; holding the voltage and letting the current taper is how you fill the last 15 percent safely.

In the chip, it's two error amplifiers, one for current and one for voltage, and the lower command wins. For a series pack the voltage loop watches the highest cell, or the pack total, with balancing during CV."
:::

::: terms
- **CC (constant current)**: regulating charge current; voltage floats upward.
- **CV (constant voltage)**: regulating voltage at 4.20 V per cell; current decays.
- **Transition point**: where terminal voltage first reaches the CV set-point.
- **Termination current**: current threshold that ends charge.
- **Time constant $\tau_{CV}$**: $RQ/(dV_{OCV}/dSoC)$, the decay time of the CV current.
- **Sense resistor**: shunt used to measure charge current.
- **Error amplifier / min-selector**: control scheme where the lower of the current-loop and voltage-loop commands drives the power stage.
- **Inrush current**: initial current into an empty cell if limited only by resistance.
:::

::: exceptional

- Gives the **inrush argument** for CC first (24 A into an empty cell).
- Describes the **charger implementation** (shunt, error amplifiers, min-selector) and accuracy targets.
- Mentions **termination options** (C/20 to C/10, timer) and the **multi-cell** nuance (highest cell, balancing during CV).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"CV means constant current at 4.2 V."** In CV the *voltage* is constant and the current falls.
- **Terminating when the voltage reaches 4.2 V.** That only ends CC; the cell is about 85 % full at 1C.
- **Ignoring $IR$ in the transition.** The point where CC ends depends on current and resistance; higher current or an aged cell switches to CV earlier ([[q0317]]).
- **Believing CV time is fixed.** It depends on $R$, $Q$, and the termination threshold ($t = \tau\ln(I_0/I_{term})$).
- **Using a lab supply in "CC/CV mode" without termination logic.** Supplies keep regulating 4.2 V forever; the cell is held at full and nothing stops it.
- **Setting the CV level by the pack's no-load voltage.** Regulate under charge, with the sense at the cell terminals (Kelvin) to avoid including lead drops.
:::

::: deep
## Phase 1: Constant current

Let $V_t = V_{OCV}(s) + I R$ with $R = R_0 + R_1$. If the charger holds $I = I_{cc}$, $V_t$ is just the OCV curve shifted up by the constant $I_{cc}R$. As $s$ increases the OCV rises along the curve of [[q0305]]; there is no control action on the voltage. When $V_t = V_{set}$, the transition occurs at

$$V_{OCV}(s_{CV}) = V_{set} - I_{cc} R$$

## Phase 2: Constant voltage

In CV the output of the voltage loop clamps $V_t$ at $V_{set}$. Current follows the cell:

$$I(t) = \frac{V_{set} - V_{OCV}(s(t))}{R}, \qquad \dot s = \frac{I}{3600\,Q}$$

Near full $V_{OCV}$ is approximately linear in $s$ with slope $k$ (the OCV table gives about 1.2 V per unit SoC for 95 to 100 %, [[q0316]]):

$$V_{OCV}(s) = V_{set} - R I_0 + k(s - s_0)\ \Rightarrow\ \dot I = -\frac{k}{RQ'}\,I,\quad \tau = \frac{R Q'}{k}$$

where $Q'$ is the capacity in coulombs (7200 C for 2 Ah). For $R = 0.1$ Ω, $\tau = 0.1 \times 7200/1.2 = 600$ s. The time to reach termination from the initial CV current $I_0$:

$$t_{CV} = \tau \ln\frac{I_0}{I_{term}}$$

| $I_0$ → $I_{term}$ | $t_{CV}$ with $\tau = 600$ s |
|---|---|
| 2 A → 0.2 A (C/10) | 23 min |
| 2 A → 0.1 A (C/20) | 30 min |
| 1 A → 0.1 A | 23 min |

The simulator's CV phase at 1C lasts 26 min (50.7 to 76.9 min) because the tail is not purely exponential: the OCV slope and resistance vary and $I_0$ at the transition is slightly below $I_{cc}$.

## Why this order, and what else could be done

- **CV only** would draw $(4.2 - 3.0)/0.05 = 24$ A into an empty cell: unsafe.
- **CC only**: the voltage would exceed 4.2 V: overcharge ([[q0319]]).
- **CC then CV** gives bounded current and bounded voltage, and a natural taper. 

Alternatives for faster or gentler charging: multi-stage CC (current steps down with voltage), pulse charging, or terminating CV early (4.1 V) for life. They are variations of this scheme ([[q0317]], [[q0318]]).

## Detecting the transition and termination

Detection is natural in the two-loop architecture: when the voltage loop's command becomes lower than the current loop's, the charger is in CV. Termination uses a comparator on the sense voltage: $V_s < I_{term} R_s$, plus timers and temperature checks.

## Multi-cell packs

For $N$ series cells the voltage loop targets $4.20 N$ V (or the highest cell reaches 4.20 V in per-cell-aware chargers). During CV, balancing bleeds the high cells, so the others continue charging while the current tapers ([[q0314]]). Termination waits for balance.

> [!NOTE]
> Charger ICs often trim the termination to a fraction of the programmed current (for example 10 % of $I_{cc}$) so the same charger works for different capacities.
:::

::: applications
- **Single-cell charger ICs** (earbuds, wearables): CC set by a resistor, CV fixed at 4.2 V, termination about 10 % of $I_{cc}$.
- **Hobby balance chargers**: CC-CV with per-cell monitoring and balancing during CV.
- **EV and charging stations**: CC (power-limited) then CV with the BMS commanding voltage and current limits.
- **Battery test equipment**: cyclers implement CC-CV charge steps with programmable termination.
- **Bench power supplies**: CC/CV operating modes (a supply can implement the profile, but needs external termination logic).
:::

::: assumptions
- The model is the **equivalent-circuit cell** with $R = R_0 + R_1$ constant over the phase; the numbers are for a 2 Ah, 50 to 100 mΩ cell.
- The exponential CV result **linearises $V_{OCV}$** near full; the real decay deviates, especially early in CV.
- Termination at **C/20 to C/10** and 4.20 V are typical; specific cells differ.
- **Temperature** is constant at 25 °C; cold or hot cells change $R$ and the allowed current.
- For **LiFePO$_4$** the same CC-CV principle applies with 3.65 V and a much flatter OCV, so the CV phase is shorter.
:::

::: followups
### What is the voltage in the CC phase, and who sets it?
The cell does. $V_t = V_{OCV}(SoC) + I_{cc}R$; the charger regulates only the current and its output rises to whatever is needed, up to a compliance limit. If the cell can't take $I_{cc}$ at an acceptable voltage, the charger would hit its voltage limit and transition to CV.

### How long does the CV phase last for a 3 Ah cell, R = 80 mΩ, from 3 A down to C/20?
$\tau = R Q/k = 0.08 \times 10800/1.2 = 720$ s (with $Q = 3$ Ah = 10800 C). $t = \tau\ln(3/0.15) = 720 \times 3.0 = 2157$ s, about 36 min. Larger capacity with the same resistance makes the tail longer; but larger cells typically have lower $R$ ($R \propto 1/Q$), so $\tau$ is roughly independent of capacity.

### What happens if the CV set-point is 1 % too high?
$V_{set} = 4.242$ V instead of 4.2 V. The cell is held 42 mV higher, charging an extra 3 to 4 % SoC (the OCV slope near full is about 1.2 V per unit SoC) but accelerating aging and getting closer to the protection IC's trip threshold. That is why charger CV accuracy is specified at ±0.5 to 1 %.

### Why does the charge never terminate on a cell with an internal leakage path?
If the cell has a parasitic leakage (an internal soft short) of, say, 50 mA, the CV phase can't fall below that current: the charger keeps supplying it. If $I_{term}$ is below the leakage, termination never occurs; the safety timer stops the charge, and the cell should be flagged. This is a good diagnostic: a current floor in CV indicates self-discharge.

### Could you do CV first and CC second?
No. CV first would produce a huge inrush on an empty cell. And after CV the current is falling; switching to CC would raise the voltage above 4.2 V. The order CC then CV is determined by the cell physics: the voltage limit is hit first at moderate current, and the current only decays after.
:::
