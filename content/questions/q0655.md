::: testing
Whether you can move from "wires are thin" to a quantitative current-handling argument. The interviewer is looking for $I^2R$ heating, voltage drop, fusing and electromigration limits, and then a menu of fixes: **parallel wires**, shorter and thicker wire (aluminum heavy wire, ribbon), copper clips, flip-chip bumps, and using the die's backside for the main current path. Hidden sub-skills: parallel resistance, current sharing, thermal reasoning, and recognizing why you don't simply use fat wire everywhere (pad size, pitch, cost, stress).
:::

::: why
Every power IC (regulators, motor drivers, MOSFETs, PMICs) has to push amps through a package that was built for hair-thin wires. If you size or choose them wrong the part runs hot, loses efficiency to package resistance, or fails by fusing a wire. The same ideas explain why datasheets list "maximum continuous current" per pin, why power pins are doubled up, and why high-current packages look so different from signal packages ([[q0654]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Joule heating**: power in the wire is $P = I^2R$, so heat rises with the *square* of current.
- **Parallel resistances**: $n$ identical wires have $R/n$ and each carries $I/n$. See [[q0001]].
- **Cross-section scaling**: resistance falls as $1/d^2$, so a 300 µm wire has the copper area of 144 wires of 25 µm.
- **Fusing**: when heat generation exceeds what the wire can shed, it melts (the wire acts as a fuse).
- **Electromigration**: sustained current density moves metal atoms; it limits pads and bond interfaces.
- **Current sharing and mutual inductance**: parallel wires do not share equally at high frequency or with unequal lengths.
:::

::: answer
"You're right that a bond wire is thin, around 25 micrometres, and a single one can only handle something like an amp or so before it heats badly or fuses; the exact limit depends on length, material and the package. The way you reconcile it is mostly with parallelism and geometry.

First, use many wires in parallel. Six 25 micron gold wires of 3 millimetres each have about 25 milliohms in parallel instead of 150, and each only carries a sixth of the current, so the heating per wire drops with the square. Power and ground pins are usually bonded with several wires to several pads. Second, shorten the wires and put the die close to the lead. Third, use bigger conductors where needed: copper or aluminum wire, fat aluminum wire of hundreds of micrometres on power modules, ribbons, or a solid copper clip instead of wires on power MOSFETs. And for the highest current and best thermals, flip-chip bumps or copper pillars spread the current over an area array. I'd also mention that on a vertical power MOSFET, the drain current goes through the die backside, so only the source needs these big connections.

What I'd watch for is current sharing, because wires of different length and layout don't share equally, and if one lifts, the others pick up the current."
:::

::: technical
**Heating.** A wire's resistance $R = \rho\ell/A$ and dissipation $P = I^2R$. For a 25 µm, 3 mm gold wire, $R = 0.149\ \Omega$: at 1 A, $P = 0.149$ W and the drop is 149 mV. Heat flows into the mould compound, the die, and the leads; temperature rise depends on those paths, so the continuous current rating is package-dependent.

**Fusing.** For short pulses the process is nearly adiabatic: energy $\approx I^2R\,t$ must raise the wire to its melting point and supply latent heat. For 25 µm gold, 3 mm long, the energy to melt from 25 °C is about 5.6 mJ. With temperature-dependent resistivity, a numerical estimate gives $I^2t \approx 13.6\ \text{A}^2\text{ms}$: about 3.4 ms at 2 A, 0.54 ms at 5 A and 0.14 ms at 10 A. (This ignores heat loss, so it is a lower bound on survival time and valid for pulses shorter than about a millisecond or so.)

**Sizing by parallel wires.** For a total current $I$ and a per-wire design limit $I_w$ (from vendor data, with margin), $n \ge I/I_w$. With $I_w = 0.5$ A and $I = 3$ A, use 6 wires: $R = 0.149/6 = 24.8\ \text{m}\Omega$, loss $= 3^2\times0.0248 = 0.22$ W total.

**Thicker wire.** A 300 µm aluminum wire, 5 mm long: $R = 2.65\times10^{-8}\times5\times10^{-3}/(\pi\,(150\ \mu\text{m})^2) = 1.87\ \text{m}\Omega$, which has the cross-section of 144 wires of 25 µm. At 20 A the loss is 0.75 W.

**Current sharing.** Resistive sharing is stable because metal has a positive temperature coefficient: a hotter wire has higher $R$ and sheds current. But at fast edges, inductance matters: wires with longer loops have more inductance and carry less transient current, and mutual coupling between neighbouring wires raises the net inductance, so $n$ parallel wires have less than a $1/n$ reduction in $L$.

**Other limits.** Bond pad and under-pad metal electromigration, voltage drop budget, and interface IMC growth. Hence big pads and multiple bonds per pad.
:::

::: long
"The numbers do look scary at first. A typical gold bond wire is 25 micrometres in diameter. At three millimetres long it's about 0.15 ohms, so at one amp you're dissipating 0.15 watts in a wire thinner than a hair. That wire is really a tiny fuse, and its safe continuous current depends on its length, the mould compound and so on, but it's roughly an amp. So you don't push the whole current through one wire.

The main trick is parallelism. If I need 3 amps and design for half an amp per wire, I use six wires. The resistance in parallel is about 25 milliohms, and each wire dissipates only about 37 milliwatts since heating goes as I squared. In real packages, power and ground are bonded with many wires to many pads, and a high-current regulator often has several supply and ground pins in parallel.

The second lever is the conductor itself. Copper has about 30 percent lower resistivity than gold. For power modules and MOSFETs people use aluminum wire hundreds of micrometres thick, or ribbon, or a copper clip bonded straight from the die to the lead, which gives milliohm or sub-milliohm connections. A 300 micrometre aluminum wire has the cross-section of about 144 thin wires.

Third, change the geometry: flip-chip bumps and copper pillars are short, wide and spread over an area, so they handle high current and lower inductance. On vertical power devices, the drain is the die backside soldered to the lead frame, so only the source needs wires.

What I'd verify is current sharing. Wires with different lengths don't share equally, at high di/dt the inductance dominates, and if one wire lifts off the others see more current. So you add margin and check thermal and reliability limits."
:::

::: terms
- **Fusing current**: current at which a wire melts; depends on pulse duration and cooling.
- **Parallel bonding**: several wires from one pad or one pin to share current.
- **Heavy wire**: aluminum bond wire of roughly 100–500 µm diameter for power devices.
- **Ribbon bond**: a flat aluminum or gold strip with larger cross-section than a round wire.
- **Copper clip**: a solid stamped copper strap soldered between the die and lead, replacing wires.
- **Electromigration**: transport of metal atoms by current; sets current-density limits in pads and thin metal.
- **Down bond**: a wire from die pad to a ground ring or exposed paddle.
- **Current sharing**: how total current divides among parallel paths.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "use multiple wires." An exceptional answer:
- Computes the single-wire resistance, power and fusing time, and shows why $I^2R$ drives the limit.
- Notes that $n$ wires give $R/n$ and $P/n$ in total, but per wire $P_w=(I/n)^2R$, a $1/n^2$ reduction.
- Lists options in order: parallel wires, shorter/thicker/copper, Al heavy wire or ribbon, copper clip, flip chip.
- Raises current sharing, mutual inductance and failure cascade when one wire lifts.
- Mentions that vertical power devices use the backside as the main current path and that pad electromigration is also a limit.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Thin wires just can't carry current."** They can, within limits; the real answer is power dissipation and fusing, and the limit is raised by parallelism and geometry.
- **Assuming parallel wires always share equally.** Different lengths and mutual inductance cause uneven sharing, particularly at high di/dt.
- **Forgetting the pad.** A 5 A bond wire array on one small pad can hit pad electromigration or lift-off.
- **Thinking inductance falls by 1/n for parallel wires.** Mutual inductance between neighbours means it falls less than that.
- **Giving a single "magic" fusing current.** The rating depends on material, length, mould compound, ambient and pulse width. Cite the datasheet or the vendor.
:::

::: deep
## The core physics: $I^2R$ in a tiny volume

A wire's heat generation is $P = I^2R$ and its heat capacity is tiny. For a 25 µm gold wire 3 mm long:

- cross-section $A = \pi(12.5\ \mu\text{m})^2 = 4.91\times10^{-10}\ \text{m}^2$
- resistance $R = \rho\ell/A = 2.44\times10^{-8}\times3\times10^{-3}/4.91\times10^{-10} = 0.149\ \Omega$
- volume $= A\ell = 1.47\times10^{-12}\ \text{m}^3$, mass $\approx 28\ \mu\text{g}$ (density 19,300 kg/m³)

So at 1 A the wire dissipates 149 mW, a power density of $10^{11}$ W/m³. The wire's own heat capacity is about $129\ \text{J/kg·K}\times 2.8\times10^{-8}\ \text{kg} = 3.7\ \mu\text{J/K}$, so adiabatic heating at 1 A would be about $149\ \text{mW}/3.7\ \mu\text{J/K} = 40{,}000$ K/s. The wire only survives continuous current because heat conducts into the mould compound and the two ends (which are cooled by the die and lead frame).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The continuous current rating of a given bond wire is not a universal constant: published fusing and "maximum continuous current" values vary with wire length, diameter, mould compound and ambient, and between suppliers. For design, use the package vendor's number. Treat "about an amp for 25 µm gold" as a rough order of magnitude only.

## Fusing time vs current (adiabatic estimate)

Assume the wire heats without cooling. Let $\rho(T)=\rho_0[1+\alpha(T-20)]$, $\alpha = 0.0034/\text{K}$ for gold. Heating to melting ($1064\ ^\circ$C) takes about 5.6 mJ for this wire (including 63 kJ/kg latent heat). A numerical integration gives roughly constant $I^2t \approx 13.6\ \text{A}^2\text{ms}$.

| n (25 µm Au, 3 mm) | R total | I per wire at 3 A | Loss per wire | Total loss |

| 1 | 149 mΩ | 3 A | 1.34 W | 1.34 W |

The per-wire heating, which governs fusing, falls as $1/n^2$.

## Why sharing is not perfect

1. **Unequal lengths.** Wires at the pad corner are longer, so $R$ differs; a 3.5 mm wire has 1.4× the resistance of a 2.5 mm one and carries correspondingly less current.
2. **Positive TCR.** If one wire runs hotter, its resistance rises and it sheds current, a stabilizing effect (the opposite of what happens with parallel BJTs).
3. **Mutual inductance.** Closely spaced parallel wires couple. If each has self-inductance $L$ and coupling $k$, $n$ identical wires have an effective inductance $L(1+(n-1)k)/n$. For $L = 3.25$ nH, $n=4$, $k = 0.5$ that is 2.03 nH, not $L/4 = 0.81$ nH; for $k=0.7$ it is 2.5 nH. So parallel wires help resistance a lot more than inductance. (The coupling values are illustrative and depend on spacing.)
4. **One wire lifts.** The survivors carry more current, heat more, and a cascade can follow.

## Moving beyond round wire

- **Heavy Al wire (125–500 µm)** and **ribbon**: used in power modules and discrete power devices; 300 µm × 5 mm Al has 1.87 mΩ.
- **Copper clip**: a stamped copper strap soldered from die to lead; milliohm-class connection and good thermal path (used in power packages of the PowerPAK or LFPAK type).
- **Flip chip / copper pillars** ([[q0656]]): current spreads over an area array of bumps; inductance is lower.
- **Die backside connection**: in a vertical MOSFET the drain contact is the die backside soldered to the lead frame, so the bond wire/clip only carries the *source* current.
- **Down bonds and multiple pads**: spread current across the die pad ring to also reduce on-die IR drop and electromigration at any single pad.

## Real design checklist

1. Estimate the total current and per-pin share.
2. Choose wire diameter, material and count from the vendor's current rating with margin.
3. Compute the voltage drop budget: for a 100 mΩ budget at 3 A (300 mV) you can afford few wires, but if you need 10 mΩ, you need dozens or a clip.
4. Check the thermal path and pad electromigration.
5. For high di/dt, check inductance and ground bounce ([[q0657]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Power MOSFETs (SO-8, DPAK, PowerPAK, LFPAK)**: source connection by multiple Al wires or a copper clip; drain through the lead-frame paddle.
- **IGBT and SiC power modules**: arrays of heavy aluminum wire bonds or ribbon bonds, sometimes now replaced with copper clips or sintered interconnects.
- **PMICs and buck regulators**: several parallel bond wires on VIN, SW and GND pins, often with multiple pins per rail in the datasheet.
- **LED drivers and motor drivers**: current-carrying pins are doubled or tripled, with thermal pad connections.
- **High-current flip-chip regulators**: copper pillars or solder bumps share multi-amp currents with low resistance.
:::

::: assumptions
- Gold, 25 µm, 3 mm is a representative thin wire, not a universal one. Heavier wire, copper or shorter lengths change the numbers proportionally.
- Fusing estimates use bulk gold properties and an adiabatic model. Real fusing depends on cooling, ambient and the mould compound.
- Parallel wires are treated as resistive with equal lengths. Real packages have different lengths and mutual coupling.
- The 1/n² heating claim holds for equal resistances. If sharing is unequal, the worst wire sets the limit.
- If asked about PCB traces, the same $I^2R$ and parallel-conductor reasoning applies but with much larger cross-sections and plane-based cooling.
:::

::: followups
### How much current can a 25 µm gold bond wire carry?
There's no single number. Published values depend on length, mould compound and ambient, and are usually on the order of an ampere for a few millimetres of wire. A few-ms pulse can be much higher: using $I^2t \approx 13.6\ \text{A}^2\text{ms}$ for this wire, a 10 A pulse lasts about 0.14 ms before melting in the adiabatic limit. For a design number, use the package vendor's rating.

### How many wires would you use for 5 A?
Take a conservative 0.5 A per wire, so 10 wires, giving $R = 14.9$ mΩ and a loss of $25\times0.0149 = 0.37$ W. I'd split them across several pads and spread them out to limit mutual heating. If 5 A is continuous and the loss or thermals matter, I'd consider a copper clip.

### Why not just use thicker wire everywhere?
Pad size and pitch: a 300 µm wire needs a pad and bond area far bigger than the 50–100 µm pads in a signal IC, and thick wire needs wedge bonding with large footprint. It also costs more, is stiffer and can crack under-pad structures. So thick wire is used on power devices with big metal pads.

### What happens if one of several parallel bond wires lifts off?
Its share of current moves to the others. For 6 wires at 3 A, each carries 0.5 A; if one lifts, the other five carry 0.6 A each, a 20% increase and a 44% increase in per-wire heating. If margin is thin, a second wire can fail, and so on. Lift-off is a known failure mode in power cycling.

### Scenario: a regulator's output measures 40 mV low at 4 A and the board is fine. Could the bond wires explain it?
Yes. A 40 mV drop at 4 A means 10 mΩ in the path. If the regulator uses a few thin gold bond wires on the output pin (a 6-wire set is about 25 mΩ, 12 wires about 12 mΩ), that is plausible. I'd measure with a Kelvin sense at the pin versus the pad if possible and check the datasheet for package resistance.
:::
