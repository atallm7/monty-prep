::: testing
Whether you can state the actual potential (a small *negative* number, not "zero" and not "$V_{out}$") and explain what sets it: **the inductor current forces the switch node down until the freewheel element conducts**, so the node sits at $-V_f$ (diode), $-I_L R_{ds(on)}$ (synchronous channel) or $-V_{f,body}$ (during dead time). Strong answers add the exceptions (DCM, where the node floats and rings around $V_{out}$) and the practical consequences (bootstrap refresh, ringing, measurement).
:::

::: why
The off-state switch-node level is what you see on every scope probe on a buck, it sets the inductor's down-slope and the duty cycle, and it determines the voltage stress on the high-side FET ($V_{in}-V_{SW}$). It also decides whether the bootstrap capacitor gets refreshed, and it is the cleanest way to tell diode conduction from channel conduction and CCM from DCM just by looking at the plateau.
:::

::: concepts
- **Freewheeling**: the diode or low-side FET carries the inductor current in the off state. See [[q0197]].
- **Diode drop**: $V_f$ for a Schottky about 0.3 to 0.5 V. See [[q0038]].
- **Channel drop**: $V=I\,R_{ds(on)}$ with the sign of the current. See [[q0203]].
- **Inductor continuity**: forces the node to whatever potential makes the current flow. See [[q0043]].
- **Body diode**: conducts during dead time at about 0.6 to 0.8 V. See [[q0208]].
:::

::: answer
"In the off state the switch node sits slightly below ground. The high-side FET is open, but the inductor current has to keep flowing, so the inductor drives the node down until something conducts. With an asynchronous buck that's the diode, so the node is at minus the diode's forward drop, about minus 0.4 volts for a Schottky. With a synchronous buck it's the low-side FET's channel, so it's minus the current times $R_{ds(on)}$, for 2 amps and 10 milliohms that's about minus 20 millivolts, essentially zero. During dead time, when both FETs are off, the body diode conducts and the node briefly goes to about minus 0.7 volts.

That's in continuous conduction. At light load in an asynchronous or diode-emulation buck, the inductor current reaches zero, the diode turns off, and the node is no longer clamped: it floats and rings around the output voltage, 3.3 V in my example, before the next cycle.

The important consequences: the voltage across the inductor is $V_{out}$ plus that small drop, the high-side FET sees the full input voltage plus that drop across it, and for the bootstrap capacitor, a low node is what lets it recharge."
:::

::: technical
**CCM, asynchronous.** $V_{SW,off}=-V_f(I_L)$; Schottky $V_f\approx0.4$ V at 1.5 to 2.5 A (device dependent, rises with current, falls with temperature). Inductor voltage $-(V_{out}+V_f)$.

**CCM, synchronous.** Channel on: $V_{SW,off}=-I_LR_{ds(on),LS}$; with 10 mΩ and $I_L$ from 2.51 A (just after turn-off) to 1.49 A (end): $-25$ mV to $-15$ mV, a ramp following the inductor current. Dead time $t_d$ (10 to 50 ns typical): $-V_{f,body}\approx-0.7$ V (body diodes of power MOSFETs are slower and have higher drop than Schottky; check the datasheet).

**DCM.** $i_L$ reaches 0 at $t=(D+D_2)T$. The diode (or diode-emulating FET) turns off. SW rings at the resonance of $L$ with the node capacitance, around $V_{out}$, decaying with the loop resistance: $V_{SW}\approx V_{out}+(\text{initial offset})e^{-t/\tau}\cos\omega t$. In forced-CCM synchronous mode there is no ringing; the node stays near 0 V and the current goes negative.

**High-side FET stress.** $V_{DS,Q1(off)}=V_{in}-V_{SW}=12+0.4=12.4$ V (async), plus ringing at the edge.

**Relationship to the average.** $\langle V_{SW}\rangle=D(V_{in}-V_{on})-(1-D)V_{drop}=V_{out}+I_LR_{DCR}$. Check: $0.275\times12-0.725\times0.4=3.01$ V, so the duty cycle must be larger to reach 3.3 V ($D=0.298$).

**Bootstrap.** In a bootstrapped high-side driver the capacitor charges from $V_{CC}$ through a diode when SW is low: $V_{boot}\approx V_{CC}-V_{f,boot}-V_{SW}$. With $V_{CC}=5$ V and matching diode drops: 5.0 V when SW is at $-0.4$ V. If SW sits at $V_{out}=3.3$ V (DCM idle) the capacitor reaches only $5-0.4-3.3=1.3$ V, so controllers force periodic refresh pulses.
:::

::: short
"Just below ground: the inductor current keeps flowing, so the switch node is pulled down until the freewheel path conducts. With a Schottky that's about minus 0.4 V; with a synchronous low-side FET it's minus $I\,R_{ds(on)}$, tens of millivolts, with brief dips to about minus 0.7 V in dead time on the body diode. The one exception is DCM: after the inductor current hits zero the node floats and rings around $V_{out}$."
:::

::: long
"In the off state the high-side FET is open, so the switch node is no longer tied to the input. What sets its voltage is the inductor: its current is still flowing from the switch node to the output, and it won't stop. The inductor will drive the node down to whatever potential makes that current flow through the available path.

In an asynchronous buck the path is the diode from ground, so the node goes to about minus the diode's forward drop, minus 0.3 to 0.5 volts for a Schottky, depending on current. With a synchronous buck, the low-side FET is on after dead time and the node is minus the current times its on-resistance, for 2 amps and 10 milliohms that's about minus 20 millivolts, close enough to zero. In the dead time before and after, both FETs are off, so the low-side body diode conducts and the node sits at about minus 0.7 volts for a few tens of nanoseconds.

That all assumes continuous conduction. At light load in an asynchronous or diode-emulation buck, the inductor current reaches zero before the cycle ends, the diode shuts off, and the node isn't clamped by anything anymore. It floats near the output voltage and rings at the resonance of the inductor with the node capacitance, then settles at $V_{out}$.

Why it matters: the voltage across the inductor during the off state is $V_{out}$ plus this small drop, which is what sets the duty cycle correction and the ripple; the high-side FET's off-state stress is $V_{in}$ minus this node voltage; and the bootstrap capacitor charges when this node is low, so in DCM controllers have to refresh it."
:::

::: terms
- **Switch node (SW, LX, PHASE)**: the junction of the high-side FET, the low-side element and the inductor.
- **Freewheel clamp**: the conduction of diode/low-side FET that sets the off-state node voltage.
- **$V_f$**: diode forward drop.
- **$R_{ds(on)}$ drop**: $I\times R_{ds(on)}$ across a conducting FET channel.
- **Dead time**: both FETs off; body diode conducts.
- **DCM ringing**: oscillation of the floating node around $V_{out}$.
- **Bootstrap refresh**: recharging the high-side supply capacitor when SW is low.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Zero volts, since the diode or low-side FET is on."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the sign and level: slightly negative, $-V_f$ or $-IR_{ds}$, with numbers.
- Explains why with current continuity ($v=L\,di/dt$).
- Covers dead time ($-0.7$ V body diode) and DCM (floating, ringing around $V_{out}$).
- Mentions the consequences: inductor voltage $V_{out}+V_f$, $V_{DS}$ stress on $Q_1$, bootstrap refresh.
- Notes that measured plateaus depend on probe grounding (inductive pick-up looks like ringing).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"It's 0 V."** It's slightly negative; that negativity is the whole mechanism and the loss.
- **"It's $V_{out}$."** That's the *output* end of the inductor. The SW end is at $V_{out}$ only in DCM idle.
- **Quoting the diode drop as 0.7 V for a Schottky.** 0.7 V is silicon or a MOSFET body diode; Schottkys are 0.3 to 0.5 V at these currents.
- **Ignoring that the synchronous plateau is a ramp.** $-I_LR_{ds}$ follows the inductor current, from $-25$ mV to $-15$ mV in the example.
- **Forgetting that DCM changes the answer.** After the inductor current reaches zero the node floats.
- **Reading a ringing scope trace as the true level.** Long ground leads add apparent overshoot; use a spring ground tip.
:::

::: deep
## The inductor decides

When $Q_1$ opens, the inductor current $I_L\approx2.5$ A is still flowing from SW to the output. The inductor equation says that to keep that current, its voltage must be whatever the circuit demands. Whatever conducting path exists between SW and ground determines that: the node drops until that path conducts the current.

## The three plateau levels

| Situation | $V_{SW}$ | Typical value |
|---|---|---|
| Async, diode conducting | $-V_f$ | −0.3 to −0.5 V (Schottky) |
| Sync, channel on | $-I_LR_{ds(on)}$ | −15 to −25 mV (10 mΩ, 1.5 to 2.5 A) |
| Dead time, body diode | $-V_{f,body}$ | about −0.7 V, tens of ns |
| DCM, inductor empty | floats, rings around $V_{out}$ | about +3.3 V |
| CCM on state (for contrast) | $V_{in}-I_LR_{ds,HS}$ | about 11.97 V |

### Why the level is negative at all

The node only *needs* to be low enough that the freewheel path conducts. The diode needs $V_f$ of forward voltage to carry 2 A; so SW = $-V_f$. The FET channel needs only $IR_{ds}$. In both cases a negative node is the sign of power dissipated in the freewheel element: $P=V\times I$.

## The effect on the converter

1. **Inductor down-slope**: $-(V_{out}+|V_{SW}|)/L$: 0.79 vs 0.70 A/µs (diode vs ideal). See [[q0195]].
2. **Duty cycle**: $D\approx\frac{V_{out}+|V_{SW,off}|}{V_{in}+|V_{SW,off}|}$.
3. **High-side stress**: off-state $V_{DS}=V_{in}-V_{SW}=12.4$ V for the diode case.
4. **Efficiency**: loss $=|V_{SW,off}|\times(1-D)I_{out}$ = 0.725 W with a diode drop of 0.5 V; ≈ 30 mW with the channel.

## DCM: the node is no longer clamped

When $I_{out}<\Delta I_L/2$ the inductor current reaches zero before the end of the period. The diode turns off (it cannot conduct in reverse), and nothing clamps SW.

> [!NOTE]
> A synchronous buck in forced-CCM mode never shows this: the low-side FET stays on and the current goes negative, so the node stays near 0 V. In diode-emulation or PFM modes the low-side FET turns off at zero current and the node behaves as in the plot.

## A hidden consequence: the bootstrap capacitor

A bootstrapped high-side NFET driver stores charge in a capacitor between BOOT and SW. It charges when SW is low:

$$V_{boot}\approx V_{CC}-V_{f,boot}-V_{SW}.$$

With $V_{CC}=5$ V and SW at $-0.4$ V (diode off state): $\approx5.0$ V (assuming equal diode drops). In DCM idle with SW at 3.3 V: $5-0.4-3.3=1.3$ V. Too low to enhance $Q_1$, so controllers force a periodic refresh (turn on the low-side FET briefly) or limit the minimum duty cycle in DCM. This is also why 100% duty cycle operation of bootstrapped converters requires a charge pump or a refresh pulse.

## Measuring it

- Use a short ground spring on the probe. A 15 cm ground lead adds inductance and shows ringing and overshoot that are not on the board.
- Set a vertical scale like 2 V/div with 0 V in the middle to see both the 12 V on-plateau and the small negative plateau, then zoom to 100 mV/div to measure the off plateau. The scope's offset range may saturate the amplifier; check that the zoomed trace is not clipping.
- The difference between the diode and synchronous cases is visible: a 0.4 V step below ground vs essentially at 0 V with 0.7 V dead-time spikes.
:::

::: applications
- **Debugging**: reading CCM/DCM, diode vs channel conduction and dead time from the SW plateau.
- **Loss estimation**: $|V_{SW,off}|(1-D)I_{out}$ gives the freewheel loss directly from a scope measurement.
- **FET selection**: the high-side voltage rating must exceed $V_{in}-V_{SW}$ plus ringing margin.
- **Bootstrap design**: determining the refresh strategy in light-load modes and 100% duty operation.
- **Negative-voltage tolerance of ICs**: controller SW pins specify allowable negative transients (for example hundreds of mV for a few ns); too-negative ringing can latch up an IC.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM** unless explicitly noted; DCM behaviour is described separately.
- **Schottky $V_f$ 0.4 V and body diode 0.7 V** are typical numbers; check datasheets. Both vary with current and temperature.
- **Ideal parasitic-free node** for the plateau values; real nodes ring at the edges.
- **Forced-CCM synchronous**: SW stays near 0 V even at no load (current negative).
- **Dead time** in the 10 to 50 ns range is typical; controllers adapt it.
:::

::: followups
### What is the voltage across the inductor in the off state?
$V_{SW}-V_{out}$: about $-3.3-0.4=-3.7$ V with a Schottky, $-3.32$ V with a synchronous channel. Magnitudes are slightly larger than $V_{out}$ because the node is below ground.

### What's the stress on the high-side FET in the off state?
$V_{DS}=V_{in}-V_{SW}=12.4$ V (diode) plus ringing at the edge. It must be rated for the worst-case input, and ringing can double it, so a 20 or 30 V part is typical for a 12 V rail.

### How can you tell from a scope whether a buck is in DCM?
The off-state plateau at $-V_f$ ends before the next edge and the node rises to about $V_{out}$ and rings, with a visible idle interval. In forced-CCM synchronous mode you never see that, but you may see the inductor current going negative.

### Does the node voltage depend on the load current?
Slightly: the diode drop rises with current (about 0.3 V at 0.5 A to 0.5 V at 3 A for a typical Schottky) and the channel drop is proportional to $I$. In DCM it depends strongly on whether the diode is conducting.

### Harder: why does the switch node briefly go to about +0.7 V above the input during turn-off on some boards?
Not above the input in the plateau, but at the edge: parasitic loop inductance carrying the sudden change in current produces an overshoot ($L_{loop}\,di/dt$), and with a PFET or a high-side body diode this can forward-bias the high-side body diode when SW exceeds $V_{in}$. Layout and gate-resistor/snubber measures reduce it.
:::

::: interpretations
"Switch node voltage potential in the off state" is read as the voltage level of the SW node relative to ground while the high-side FET is off and (in CCM) the freewheel element conducts. The duplicate-style question [[q0208]] asks the same about the body-diode state in the Power Bridge section, and [[q0191]] asks about the inductor's polarity (the SW node level is what causes it).
