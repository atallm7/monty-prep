::: basics
### In plain words
A logic gate is a tiny circuit with one or two inputs, each 0 or 1, and one output that follows a fixed rule. Each rule gets its own drawn symbol, so an engineer can read a circuit diagram at a glance. This question asks you to sketch the standard symbols: NOT, AND, OR, NAND, NOR, XOR and XNOR.

### Picture it
Think of road signs. You don't read a sign, you recognize its shape. Gate symbols work the same way. A small circle on a symbol, called a bubble, means "flip the answer", a bit like a "not" stamp. So most of the alphabet is a few base shapes plus an optional bubble, and learning the pattern beats memorizing seven pictures.

### Words you need
- **NOT (inverter)**: the output is the opposite of the input. It is a triangle with a bubble.
- **AND**: the output is 1 only if all inputs are 1. It is drawn as a flat-backed "D" shape.
- **OR**: the output is 1 if at least one input is 1. It has a curved back and a pointed front.
- **Bubble**: the small circle that inverts a signal. Adding one to AND gives NAND, and adding one to OR gives NOR.
- **XOR**: the output is 1 when the two inputs differ. It is an OR with one extra curved line behind it.

### The simplest example
Draw the AND gate: a flat back, a round front, two input wires on the left and one output on the right. Feed it A = 1 and B = 1, and the output is 1. Feed it A = 1 and B = 0, and the output is 0. Now add a bubble on the output tip. That is a NAND, and it gives the opposite answers: 0 and 1.

That is the whole idea: a few shapes plus a bubble cover the whole family. The rest of this page shows every symbol, a whiteboard drawing order, the rule that lets you redraw a gate with bubbles on its inputs, the rectangular European style, and real chip part numbers.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can **draw the standard gate shapes from memory, quickly and correctly**, and know what each shape means rather than just copying a picture. The checks hiding inside are: AND has the flat back and the round front, OR has the curved back and the pointed front, a small circle (the "bubble") means inversion, XOR is OR plus one extra curve at the input side, a triangle alone is a buffer. Stronger candidates also give the Boolean expression next to each symbol, mention the alternative rectangular IEC symbols, and explain that the symbol says nothing about how the gate is built (that is [[q0890]]).
:::

::: why
Schematics and block diagrams are how hardware engineers talk. If you cannot sketch an AND or a NAND without hesitation, you will struggle to whiteboard anything harder, like a mux, a latch or an adder. Symbols also carry meaning during debugging: seeing a bubble on a pin tells you that signal is **active-low** (a chip-select called CS_N, a reset called RST_N). This question is often the warm-up, and a fluent, tidy answer, with names, expressions and a note about bubbles, sets the tone for the rest of the digital part of an interview.
:::

::: concepts
- **Boolean operations**: NOT, AND, OR and their combinations; each symbol is one operation. Truth tables are in [[q0891]].
- **The inversion bubble**: a small circle at a pin means the signal is inverted at that point. NAND = AND + bubble, NOR = OR + bubble.
- **Gate shapes**: flat-back D for AND, curved-back shield for OR, triangle for buffer, extra back-curve for XOR.
- **Active-low signals**: names ending in N or with an overbar (like $\overline{CS}$) and bubbles on the pin mean the signal is asserted when low.
- **De Morgan's laws**: $\overline{AB}=\bar A+\bar B$ and $\overline{A+B}=\bar A\bar B$. These give the alternate symbols (a NAND can be drawn as an OR with input bubbles).
- **Abstraction**: the symbol is the function; the transistor circuit that realizes it is separate ([[q0890]]).
:::

::: answer
"Sure. I'll draw them left to right, inputs on the left, output on the right.

A NOT gate, an inverter, is a triangle pointing right with a small circle on the tip. The triangle is a buffer, which just repeats the input, and the circle is the inversion. An AND gate is a D shape: flat back where the inputs enter, rounded front, output on the curve, and the output is high only if both inputs are high. An OR gate has a concave curved back and a pointed, curved front, like a shield, and its output is high if either input is high.

Then everything else is those plus a bubble. Put a bubble on the output of the AND shape and you get NAND. Put it on the output of the OR and you get NOR. XOR is the OR shape with one extra curved line behind the back, and its output is high when exactly one input is high, so when the inputs differ. XNOR is XOR with a bubble.

I'd label each with its expression: Y equals A-bar, A AND B, A plus B, and so on. There's also the rectangular IEC style, with an ampersand in the box for AND and greater-or-equal-to-one for OR, but the distinctive shapes are what most US schematics use. And I should say that a symbol only describes the logic function. Whether it's built from CMOS transistors, relays or a lookup table is a separate question."
:::

::: technical
**Distinctive-shape (ANSI/IEEE) symbols and functions**

| Gate | Shape | Expression ($Y$) | Output is 1 when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buffer | triangle | $Y=A$ | input is 1 |
| NOT | triangle + bubble | $Y=\bar A$ | input is 0 |
| AND | flat back, round front | $Y=AB$ | all inputs are 1 |
| NAND | AND + bubble | $Y=\overline{AB}$ | any input is 0 |
| OR | curved back, pointed front | $Y=A+B$ | any input is 1 |
| NOR | OR + bubble | $Y=\overline{A+B}$ | all inputs are 0 |
| XOR | OR + extra back curve | $Y=A\oplus B=A\bar B+\bar A B$ | inputs differ (odd number of 1s) |
| XNOR | XOR + bubble | $Y=\overline{A\oplus B}$ | inputs are equal |

**Drawing rules.** Inputs enter on the left and outputs leave on the right. A bubble is placed exactly at the pin it inverts, either at an input or an output. Multi-input gates (3, 4 inputs) widen the back; the output still has one pin. The bubble counts as a full inversion: a NAND is electrically one gate, not an AND followed by a separate inverter (see [[q0890]]).

**Bubble pushing and De Morgan.** $\overline{AB} = \bar A + \bar B$ means a NAND can also be drawn as an OR shape with bubbles on both inputs, and a NOR as an AND shape with input bubbles. Engineers choose whichever form reads best: a signal that is active-low at a gate's input is shown as a bubbled input, so that "bubble meets bubble" cancels along a wire.

**IEC 60617 rectangular symbols** use a rectangle with a qualifying label: $\&$ for AND, $\geq1$ for OR, $=1$ for XOR, $1$ for buffer, and a small negation marker for inversion. These are standard in much European documentation and in datasheets of logic ICs.

**Extras.** A buffer with an extra enable pin is a tri-state buffer (output can be high-impedance). A gate symbol with a small hysteresis loop inside is a Schmitt-trigger input. Standard 74-series logic ICs map these directly: 7404 hex inverter, 7400 quad 2-input NAND, 7402 quad NOR, 7408 AND, 7432 OR, 7486 XOR.
:::

::: short
"I draw inputs on the left, output on the right. A triangle is a buffer, and a triangle with a bubble is NOT. AND is a D shape, OR is the curved-back shield shape. Add a bubble on the output of those and you get NAND and NOR. XOR is OR with an extra curved line at the back, XNOR adds a bubble. The bubble always means inversion. I'd write the expression next to each one: A·B, A+B, A⊕B. And the symbol only tells you the function, not how it's built."
:::

::: long
"I'll draw from left to right with inputs on the left and one output on the right.

Start with the single-input ones. A buffer is a triangle pointing right: output equals input. Add a small circle at the tip and you've got a NOT gate, output is A-bar. That small circle is the key idea, the bubble means inversion at that pin.

For two inputs, AND is the D shape: flat on the input side, rounded on the output side, and the output is 1 only when both inputs are 1. OR looks like a shield: the back is curved inward and the front comes to a rounded point, and the output is 1 when either input is 1. NAND is the AND shape with a bubble at the output, so it's 0 only when both inputs are 1. NOR is the OR shape with a bubble. XOR is the OR shape with a second curved line just behind the back, and its output is 1 when exactly one input is 1; XNOR is that with a bubble on the output, so it tells you when two bits are equal.

I'd write the expression beside each symbol, A AND B, A OR B, and so on, so there's no ambiguity.

A couple of extras. By De Morgan's law a NAND can also be drawn as an OR shape with bubbles on the inputs, which is useful when your signals are active-low, because then the bubbles line up with the signal polarity. There's also the rectangular IEC form, a box with an ampersand for AND, and a greater-than-or-equal-to-1 for OR. And I'd stress that these symbols only define behavior. In CMOS, an AND is actually a NAND followed by an inverter, six transistors, while a NAND is only four."
:::

::: terms
- **Inverter / NOT**: output is the logical complement of the input.
- **Buffer**: output equals input; used to restore drive strength or add delay.
- **Bubble (inversion circle)**: a small circle on a symbol's pin that marks logical inversion.
- **Active-low**: a signal that is asserted at logic 0, marked by a bubble, an overbar or an N/B suffix on its name.
- **NAND / NOR**: AND/OR followed by inversion; each is a universal gate ([[q0892]]).
- **XOR / XNOR**: exclusive OR (true when inputs differ) and its complement (true when equal).
- **De Morgan's laws**: $\overline{AB}=\bar A+\bar B$, $\overline{A+B}=\bar A\bar B$; they give the alternate symbols.
- **IEC 60617 symbol**: the rectangular style with labels such as & (AND), ≥1 (OR), =1 (XOR).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws an AND and an OR shape correctly, perhaps mixes up NOR and XOR, and moves on.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws every gate in under a minute, with a consistent size, inputs on the left, and the bubble placed *at the pin*, not floating nearby.
- States the function next to each shape (AND = $AB$, XOR = exactly one high) and explains the bubble as inversion.
- Uses **De Morgan** to show the alternate NAND/NOR forms and says why one would draw them that way (active-low signals).
- Distinguishes the symbol from the implementation, e.g. "an AND is 6 transistors in CMOS because it's a NAND plus an inverter".
- Mentions the IEC rectangular style and tri-state/Schmitt variants only briefly, without drifting off topic.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Mixing up OR and XOR.** XOR has *two* curves at the back (the OR back plus one extra, separated by a small gap). A single curved back is OR.
- **Putting the bubble in the wrong place.** For NAND/NOR the bubble is on the output. A bubble on an input means only that input is inverted, a different gate.
- **Drawing an AND with a curved back, or an OR with a flat back.** AND is the flat-backed D; OR is the curved-back shield.
- **Treating the symbol as the transistor circuit.** A NAND symbol is one gate; a 2-input AND in CMOS needs an extra inverter stage.
- **Forgetting the output of an XOR is 1 for an *odd* number of 1s.** With three inputs, 1,1,1 gives 1 (not 0).
- **Ignoring active-low conventions.** Seeing a bubble pin named CS_N and still describing it as "high to enable" is a correctness error, not a style one.
:::

::: deep
## Why the symbols look the way they do

The first thing to understand is that these are a visual **alphabet** with only a handful of ideas. If you learn the ideas, you can regenerate every symbol instead of memorizing them.

1. **The triangle** means "amplify / repeat the signal in one direction". It is the buffer.
2. **The bubble** (a small circle) means "invert here". Triangle + bubble = NOT.
3. **The D shape** means AND: a flat back where all the inputs line up, as if every input has to be present at the wall before anything gets through.
4. **The shield shape** means OR (the concave back lets either input reach the output).
5. **One extra back curve** means "exclusive".

Everything else is a combination: NAND = D + bubble, NOR = shield + bubble, XNOR = shield + extra curve + bubble.

## What the bubble means, and De Morgan

The bubble is not decoration: it is a logical inverter drawn compactly. Two equivalent ways to draw the same NAND follow from De Morgan:

$$\overline{AB}=\bar A+\bar B\qquad\overline{A+B}=\bar A\,\bar B$$

So a NAND (AND shape with output bubble) is the same function as an OR shape with bubbles on *both inputs*. Why draw it that way? When your inputs are **active-low**, the second form reads naturally. Say a memory has two chip-select pins that are active-low, and you want to know when *either* is asserted. Drawing an OR-shape with bubbled inputs shows "output goes high when A or B is low", which is how you think about it, instead of making the reader do De Morgan in their head.

> [!TIP]
> "Bubble pushing" is the whiteboard technique: slide a bubble through a gate and change the gate shape (AND to OR or OR to AND) at the same time. Two bubbles on one wire (output of one gate into the input of the next) cancel.

Worked check: take $Y = \overline{A\cdot B}$ with $A=1$, $B=0$. AND gives 0; the bubble inverts to 1. The OR-with-input-bubbles version gets $\bar A=0$, $\bar B=1$; the OR gives 1. Same answer.

## Beyond two inputs

A 3-input AND just has a wider back with three input leads, with expression $Y=ABC$. 4-input gates are the same. In real CMOS libraries, NAND3/NAND4 and NOR3 exist but large fan-in gates get slow, so wide functions are built as trees of smaller gates. The symbol does not hint at this because it only specifies function.

For XOR with more than two inputs, the output is the **parity** of the inputs: 1 if an odd number are 1. The "exactly one" description only works for two inputs, which is a classic trap.

## Symbol versus implementation

An AND gate symbol is one box in a schematic. In CMOS a plain AND2 is really a NAND (4 transistors) plus an inverter (2 transistors), so 6 transistors, whereas a NAND is a single 4-transistor stage ([[q0890]]). Likewise XOR has no simple one-stage CMOS form: it is built from several gates or from transmission gates. A schematic symbol hides all that. So when an interviewer asks "draw the symbol" and then follows with "now draw it from transistors", they are testing whether you know the difference. A fact worth saying aloud: inverting gates (NOT, NAND, NOR) are the *natural* CMOS gates, and the non-inverting ones are inverting gates plus a buffer stage.

## Real parts

The classic 74-series packages have the symbols printed on the datasheet's pinout diagram: **7404** (hex inverter, six NOT gates in one 14-pin package), **7400** (quad NAND2), **7402** (quad NOR2), **7408** (quad AND2), **7432** (quad OR2) and **7486** (quad XOR2). Modern equivalents appear as 74HC/74HCT/74LVC families, and single-gate parts (for example "little logic" 74LVC1G08, one AND gate in a tiny package) exist for fixing one gate on a board.

::: followups
### Draw an XOR gate and tell me what its output is for three inputs 1, 1, 1.
Draw the OR shape with an extra curve behind its back. For two inputs, XOR is 1 when exactly one input is 1. A cascaded 3-input XOR gives the parity of the inputs: 1 if an odd number are 1. With $1\oplus1\oplus1$: $(1\oplus1)=0$, then $0\oplus1=1$. So the output is 1, not 0, which is where "exactly one" breaks.

### Why is there a bubble on a NAND and what does it mean electrically?
Logically it is an inversion after the AND. Electrically it signifies that the output is the complement, which in CMOS happens to be the natural result: a NAND pulls its output *low* only when all inputs are high, so the gate is a single inverting stage. It is not a separate inverter attached to an AND.

### Draw the alternate symbol for a NOR gate and say when you'd use it.
By De Morgan, $\overline{A+B}=\bar A\,\bar B$, so a NOR can be drawn as an AND shape with a bubble on both inputs. I'd use it when the inputs are active-low and I want the schematic to read "output goes high when both are asserted (low)".

### How would you spot an active-low signal on a schematic?
A bubble at the pin, an overbar on the net name or a suffix like _N, #, or B. All mean "asserted at logic 0". For example, a chip-select pin shown $\overline{CS}$ enables the chip when it is pulled low.

### Harder: draw Y = (A+B)·C' using only NOR gates.
Use De Morgan: $(A+B)\bar C=\overline{\overline{A+B}+C}$. That is a NOR whose inputs are $\text{NOR}(A,B)$ and $C$, so two NOR2 gates: first NOR(A,B), then NOR of that with C. Check A=1, B=0, C=0: NOR(1,0)=0, then NOR(0,0)=1, and directly $(1+0)\cdot1=1$. Matches. A second check, C=1: the final NOR has a 1 on one input so Y=0, as $\bar C=0$ demands.

### What is the symbol for a tri-state buffer and why would you use one?
A buffer triangle with an extra enable pin on its side or top (a bubble on it if active-low). When disabled the output is high-impedance, as if disconnected, which lets several drivers share one bus as long as only one is enabled at a time.
