::: basics
### In plain words
An active probe has a tiny amplifier at its tip. The amplifier barely loads the circuit, so it adds very little capacitance to the node. A standard passive 10x probe adds about 10 to 15 pF. An active probe adds about 1 pF or less.

### Picture it
Capacitance is like a small bucket that must fill before the voltage on a wire can rise. A passive probe adds a bigger bucket to the node. A weak driver needs longer to fill it, so the edge slows. An active probe adds only a thimble. The limit: its amplifier handles only a few volts and breaks more easily.

### Words you need
- **Capacitance**: how much charge a part must take in for its voltage to rise. A pF is a trillionth of a farad.
- **Passive probe**: a plain probe of wire and resistors with no amplifier. A 10x passive probe has about 10 MΩ in parallel with 10 to 15 pF. See [[q0997]].
- **Source impedance**: the resistance a node drives through. A high one is easy to disturb.
- **Rise time**: the time an edge takes to go from 10% to 90% of its final value.

### The simplest example
A node is driven through 500 Ω and has a fast, clean 1 ns edge. Add a passive probe with 12 pF. The 500 Ω and the probe capacitance slow the edge, which now takes about $2.2 \times 500 \times 12\ \text{pF} = 13$ ns. The probe has changed what you are measuring.

Now use an active probe with 1 pF. It adds only about $2.2 \times 500 \times 1\ \text{pF} = 1.1$ ns of its own, so the edge stays near 1.5 ns.

At 100 MHz the 12 pF probe acts like a 133 Ω load, heavy on a 500 Ω node. The 1 pF probe acts like 1.6 kΩ.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds when a passive probe is better, such as a 12 V rail.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand **probe loading in the frequency domain**: a probe is a resistor in parallel with a capacitor, and at high frequency the capacitor is what matters. A good answer says what an active probe is (a tiny high-impedance amplifier at the tip), why it has far lower input capacitance than a passive probe, what you pay for that (cost, limited input range, fragility, offset, noise) and when it is the right tool: fast edges, high-impedance or sensitive nodes, GHz bandwidth. It also separates active single-ended probes from differential ones ([[q0995]]).
:::

::: why
A signal's rise time of a nanosecond or less has content far into the hundreds of MHz. A typical 10x passive probe adds 10-15 pF to the node, whose reactance at 100 MHz is only about 130 Ω. On a 500 Ω source that probe visibly slows the edge and shifts the very signal you are trying to look at. An active probe cuts that capacitance by roughly 10x. It is the standard tool for digital bring-up, clocks, memory buses and RF-ish signals, and knowing why is a common interview check.
:::

::: concepts
- **Probe as an RC load**: a passive probe is about $R_{in}\parallel C_{in}$; at high frequency $|Z|\approx1/(2\pi fC_{in})$. See [[q0999]].
- **10x passive probe**: a compensated divider with ~10 MΩ and 10-15 pF. See [[q0997]].
- **Source impedance**: loading error depends on probe impedance relative to the source's.
- **Bandwidth and rise time**: $t_r\approx0.35/BW$, and probe plus scope bandwidth combine. See [[q0983]] and [[q0980]].
- **Buffer / amplifier at the tip**: a unity-gain high-impedance, low-capacitance amplifier driving a 50 Ω cable.
:::

The amplifier has very high input resistance and very low input capacitance, typically around a picofarad or less, and then drives the cable and the scope's 50 ohm input. So it loads the circuit far less than a passive probe at high frequency.

The reason that matters is that a passive probe is basically a resistor in parallel with a capacitor of 10 to 15 picofarads. At low frequency it looks like 10 megaohms, great. But at 100 MHz that capacitance is only about 130 ohms. If I probe a 500 ohm node, the probe is a real load and slows the edge. With an active probe at 1 pF the reactance at 100 MHz is 1.6 kilohms, so the same node barely notices.

So I'd use it for fast digital edges, clocks, high-speed buses, high-impedance nodes and anywhere bandwidth over a few hundred MHz matters. The price is real: it's more expensive, has a limited input voltage range and DC offset range, adds a bit of noise, needs power from the scope, and is easy to damage with ESD or overvoltage. ::: technical
**Structure.** An active probe puts a unity-gain (or slightly attenuating) buffer, typically a low-capacitance FET or a small RF IC, in the tip assembly. The input is high resistance (about 1 MΩ, sometimes more) in parallel with a very small capacitance (on the order of 1 pF, lower on multi-GHz probes). The buffer drives a 50 Ω cable terminated at the scope input (50 Ω path), which gives wide bandwidth and low loading of the signal. Power comes from the scope's probe interface.

**Why capacitance is the figure of merit.** With $C_{in}$ in parallel with $R_{in}$, $|Z_{in}|=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1/R_{in}^2+(2\pi fC_{in})^2}}$. Above about $f=1/(2\pi R_{in}C_{in})$ it is $\approx 1/(2\pi fC_{in})$.

| Probe | $C_{in}$ | $|Z|$ at 100 MHz | $|Z|$ at 1 GHz |
|---|---|---|---|
| Passive 10x (12 pF) | 12 pF | 133 Ω | 13 Ω |
| Active (1 pF) | 1 pF | 1.6 kΩ | 160 Ω |
| Multi-GHz active (0.3 pF) | 0.3 pF | 5.3 kΩ | 530 Ω |

**Loading effect.** A source resistance $R_s$ with probe capacitance $C$ creates a pole at $f=1/(2\pi R_sC)$ and a 10-90% rise time $t_r\approx2.2R_sC$. For $R_s=500$ Ω: 12 pF gives pole 26.5 MHz and $t_r\approx13$ ns; 1 pF gives pole 318 MHz and $t_r\approx1.1$ ns.

**Costs and limits.** Limited linear input range (often several volts, so a 12 V rail may be out of range without an attenuator tip); limited DC offset range; the amplifier adds noise (higher than a passive 10x referred to the input at the same sensitivity, in some cases); ESD and overvoltage can destroy the input FET; it needs scope-supplied power (or a battery) and is much more expensive.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The input range, capacitance and noise vary a lot between product families (from <1 pF multi-GHz probes to ~1-2 pF general-purpose ones). Quote typical figures only as ranges, and check the datasheet for the specific probe.
:::

That means much less loading at high frequency, so I use it for fast edges, clocks, high-speed buses and sensitive high-impedance nodes. ::: long
"A passive probe is a resistor in parallel with a capacitor. A normal 10x probe is about 10 megaohms with around 10 to 15 picofarads. At DC that is a light load, but the capacitor dominates at high frequency: at 100 MHz, 12 picofarads is only about 130 ohms. If I probe a node with a few hundred ohms of source impedance, the probe forms an RC low-pass with it, rounding the edges and sometimes even changing circuit behavior. A 500 ohm source with 12 picofarads has a pole near 26 MHz, so a 1 ns edge would look about 13 ns long.

An active probe fixes that by putting a tiny high-impedance buffer right at the tip. The amplifier typically has 1 megaohm in parallel with about a picofarad or less, and it drives the cable with low impedance into the scope's 50 ohm input. So at 100 MHz it looks like roughly 1.6 kilohms, and that same 500 ohm node would have a pole near 300 MHz instead.

So I reach for an active probe for digital edges of a nanosecond or less, clock and memory buses, high-speed serial lines, and high-impedance nodes where any capacitance would change the circuit. The trade-offs: it costs several times more than a passive probe, the input range is limited to a few volts and the offset is limited too, so I cannot just put it on a 12 volt rail, it adds noise, it is easy to damage with ESD or overvoltage, and it needs the scope's probe interface to power it. For power rails, mains-side stuff or anything with hundreds of volts, I use passive or differential probes."
:::

::: terms
- **Active probe**: a probe with an amplifier (buffer) at or near the tip.
- **Input capacitance ($C_{in}$)**: the capacitance the probe adds to the node; the main driver of high-frequency loading.
- **Input resistance ($R_{in}$)**: DC loading; 1 MΩ typical for active probes, 10 MΩ for passive 10x.
- **Probe bandwidth**: frequency at which the probe's response is −3 dB; combines with the scope's.
- **Dynamic range**: the linear voltage range at the input of an active probe.
- **Offset range**: the DC offset the probe can accept and cancel.
- **Rise-time degradation**: measured $t_r=\sqrt{t_{r,sig}^2+t_{r,probe}^2+t_{r,scope}^2}$.
- **Probe interface**: the connector that supplies power and identifies the probe to the scope.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it has an amplifier, so it has less loading."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains that the thing that matters is **input capacitance**, and shows the reactance math: 12 pF → 133 Ω at 100 MHz vs 1 pF → 1.6 kΩ.
- Shows the loading effect on a given source, e.g. 500 Ω × 12 pF = pole at 26.5 MHz.
- Lists when to use: sub-ns edges, high-speed buses, high-Z nodes, GHz bandwidth.
- Lists the costs: range, offset, noise, fragility, power, cost.
- Separates "active single-ended" from "differential" probes, and mentions that a passive 10x is still the right default for general work.
- Notes that scope bandwidth needs to match too: a GHz probe on a 200 MHz scope is no help.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking active probes have infinite impedance.** They have about 1 MΩ and ~1 pF (typically), so they still load a node at high frequency, just a lot less.
- **Using one on a rail above its input range.** The buffer saturates or is destroyed. Use an attenuating tip or a passive probe.
- **Assuming an active probe is the same as a differential probe.** Active probes are usually single-ended; differential probes also have an amplifier but reject common-mode ([[q0995]]).
- **Forgetting the scope's bandwidth.** The measured rise time combines the signal, probe and scope contributions.
- **Dropping or ESD-zapping it.** The input FET is fragile; ground yourself and use the supplied tips.
- **Believing a larger bandwidth always means a better measurement.** More bandwidth also lets in more noise, and a 1 ns edge does not need a 10 GHz probe; see the bandwidth rule $BW\approx0.35/t_r$ with a margin of 3-5x.
:::

::: deep
## A passive probe is an RC load

Take a standard 10x passive probe on a node. Its input looks (at its simplest) like

$$Z_{in}(f)=R_{in}\parallel\frac{1}{j2\pi fC_{in}}$$

with $R_{in}\approx10$ MΩ and $C_{in}\approx$10-15 pF. Plot the magnitude and you see it is flat at 10 MΩ up to a corner of $f_c=1/(2\pi R_{in}C_{in})$, for 12 pF about 1.3 kHz, and then falls at 20 dB/decade. By 100 MHz it is 133 Ω; by 1 GHz about 13 Ω. The probe is not "10 MΩ" at the frequencies a fast edge cares about; it is a 100-ish ohm capacitor.

## Loading changes the signal, not just the amplitude

Consider a driver with output resistance $R_s=500$ Ω making a clean 1 ns edge. Adding a probe with capacitance $C$ creates an RC low-pass:

$$f_{3dB}=\frac{1}{2\pi R_sC},\qquad t_r\approx2.2\,R_sC$$

- Passive, 12 pF: $f_{3dB}=26.5$ MHz, $t_r\approx13$ ns. The edge is 13x slower than the real one.
- Active, 1 pF: $f_{3dB}=318$ MHz, $t_r\approx1.1$ ns.

Both numbers show how the measurement can change the circuit. The passive probe not only slows the displayed edge but also actually slows the circuit's edge, since the capacitance is physically on the node. That might even make a failing circuit pass, or vice versa. Note this effect applies to the *source impedance at that node*. A 50 Ω driver with a 12 pF probe has a pole at 265 MHz, much less harmful, which is why many fast digital outputs with low drive impedance are fine with passives for modest speeds.

## What is inside

The tip contains a buffer, often a source follower or a small RF amplifier. It must offer:

1. Very low input capacitance (sub-pF to a couple of pF).
2. High enough input resistance for DC accuracy (~1 MΩ).
3. A low output impedance to drive a 50 Ω cable, which reduces cable capacitance issues and allows GHz bandwidth.
4. Flat gain and phase over bandwidth, with small ringing.

The amplifier limits things: its supply rails limit the linear input swing to several volts; its offset and bias limit DC accuracy; and it adds noise. Many active probes have a DC offset knob to extend the effective range around a bias point, e.g. looking at a 2 V signal on a 5 V level.

## Rise time budget

For a measurement with bandwidths adding as rise times,

$$t_{r,meas}=\sqrt{t_{r,sig}^2+t_{r,probe}^2+t_{r,scope}^2}$$

For a 1 GHz probe ($t_r\approx0.35$ ns) and 1 GHz scope, the system rise time is about $0.35\sqrt2=0.5$ ns, adequate for a signal with 1.5 ns rise: $\sqrt{1.5^2+0.5^2}=1.58$ ns (5% error). If you used a 200 MHz scope ($t_r\approx1.75$ ns) with a 1 GHz probe, the combined time is $\sqrt{0.35^2+1.75^2}=1.78$ ns, so the probe's bandwidth is wasted. This is why the right instrument chain, not a single component, determines the quality ([[q0983]]).

## How to check whether the probe is lying to you

Loading is easy to miss because the waveform on the screen still looks plausible. Three quick checks:

1. **Swap probes.** Measure the same node with a passive 10x and with the active probe. If the edge time or amplitude changes noticeably, at least one probe is loading the circuit; the one with lower capacitance is closer to the truth.
2. **Look for behavior change.** If the circuit itself starts misbehaving when you touch the probe (a clock changes frequency, a bus starts failing, an oscillator stops), the probe capacitance is shifting something physical. That is no measurement error: the circuit really is different with the probe attached.
3. **Estimate it.** Take the node's source resistance (driver output impedance or the parallel of pull-up and pull-down), multiply by the probe capacitance and compare $2.2RC$ with the edge time you are trying to see. If $2.2RC$ is more than about a third of the rise time, do not trust the edge.

For example, a 4.7 kΩ pull-up on an open-drain line with 12 pF gives $2.2\times4700\times12\text{ pF}=124$ ns added rise time. That matters on a fast-mode I²C bus (300 ns allowed), and an active probe at 1 pF brings it to about 10 ns. This is also a good argument for probing at the driver pin instead of the far end of a long trace: the trace's own capacitance is already there, and you want to add as little as possible.

## Choosing between probes

| Situation | Probe |
|---|---|
| Power rail ripple, 12 V, tens of MHz | Passive 10x (range and robustness) |
| 1 ns edge on a 100-500 Ω CMOS output | Active |
| High-impedance node (e.g. oscillator input) | Active (low C), or a high-Z FET probe |
| Floating high-side signal | Differential or isolated probe ([[q0995]]) |
| 50 Ω transmission line | 50 Ω input/Z0 probe ([[q1000]]) |
| DC to a few MHz, mains-side | Passive with correct rating, or differential |

## Practical care

- Use the **shortest ground** with the right tip: the spring ground or a socketed tip, not the long alligator lead, because the ground inductance rings with the probe capacitance (see the "ground" view in the simulator).
- Do **auto-zero/offset** at the right power state, and use the **offset knob** to center signals that sit on a DC level.
- **Never exceed** the specified input voltage: look up both the dynamic range and the maximum non-destructive voltage.
- Store it in its case; the tip assembly is delicate.
:::

::: applications
- **DDR and other memory bus signals** at hundreds of MHz to GHz, where passive probes distort the edges.
- **Clock and PLL nodes**: low capacitance avoids shifting the oscillator or pulling a high-impedance clock line.
- **Switching regulator gate drive** low-voltage section: fast edges on 5 V gate drivers.
- **High-speed serial (USB 2.0/3.0, SATA)** setup debugging with active or differential active probes.
- **RF front-end test points** and oscillator nodes, where a passive probe's capacitance would detune it.
- **Sensor front ends** with megaohm source impedance.
:::

::: assumptions
- The probe's input capacitance is the main loading mechanism; inductance of the ground lead is assumed small (short ground).
- The signal is within the probe's input range and the scope has enough bandwidth.
- Values (1 pF, 1 MΩ, 12 pF) are typical; actual numbers are model-dependent.
- If the source impedance is very low (a few ohms), a passive probe is also fine, and may even be better because of its range and ruggedness.

Alternative valid answers: a Z0 (low-impedance resistive divider) probe can also give very low capacitance at low cost ([[q1000]]); for high voltages you need passive or differential probes, whatever the capacitance.
:::

::: followups
### A 10x passive probe shows a 5 ns edge on my clock; the active probe shows 1.5 ns. Which one is right?
Probably the active one, since the passive one loaded the node and also slows what it displays. To check, put both on a low-impedance signal and compare. If they agree there, the difference at the clock node is loading. Then try the active probe on a 50 Ω-source reference to verify its own rise time.

### Can I use an active probe to measure a 12 V power rail?
Usually not directly. Its linear input range is often a few volts (check the datasheet), so a 12 V DC rail will saturate it unless it has an attenuator tip, or you use the AC-coupled path. A passive 10x is the standard choice for rails.

### How does the 1 pF input capacitance of an active probe affect an 8 MHz crystal oscillator?
A crystal circuit has load capacitances on the order of 10-20 pF. Adding 1 pF shifts the frequency by a small amount (parts per million to tens of ppm depending on the circuit), while a passive probe's 12 pF can stop it oscillating. For crystals, an active probe or probing a buffered output is the right approach.

### What bandwidth probe do I need for a 1 ns rise-time signal?
Using $BW\approx0.35/t_r$, the signal bandwidth is 350 MHz. To measure with under 5% error use about 3-5x that, so around 1-1.5 GHz for the probe and scope. Probe plus scope can be combined with the root-sum-square rule for rise time.

### What would you do if an active probe's reading changes when you touch the ground lead?
The ground lead is too long, forming a loop with inductance that resonates with the tip capacitance, or the lead is picking up fields. Shorten it, use a spring ground, and verify with the probe tip on the ground pin: the "zero" trace must stay quiet.
:::
