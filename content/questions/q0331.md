::: testing
Whether you can combine **Ohm's law with the cell's internal resistance** to predict what a short really does, then connect the electrical result to heat and protection. The hidden sub-skills:

1. You can compute short-circuit current $I = V/(R_{int} + R_{ext})$ and know it is limited by tiny resistances (milliohms), so it is hundreds of amps for a hobby pack.
2. You see where the power goes ($I^2R$ in the cell, the wiring and the contact) and why the cell can be heated within seconds.
3. You can name the protections and their limits: fuse, protector IC short-circuit detection, cell PTC/CID, wire and connector choices, and note why timing (microseconds) matters.
:::

::: why
A battery is a very low-impedance source by design, which is exactly what makes it dangerous when something conductive bridges its terminals. Short-circuit current sets the fuse rating, the wire gauge, the MOSFET safe-operating area, and the connector choice in every battery product. It is also behind real accidents: loose packs in bags, a dropped screwdriver in a battery bay, a wiring mistake on a bench. Interviewers like it because it is a short calculation that leads straight to design judgement.
:::

::: concepts
- **Internal resistance**: the cell's equivalent series resistance, typically milliohms to tens of milliohms. See [[q0303]].
- **Ohm's law and series resistance**: current is the source voltage divided by the total loop resistance. See [[q0001]].
- **Joule heating**: $P = I^2R$ in the cell, the wires and any contact. See [[q0331]] and [[q0328]].
- **C rating**: continuous discharge limit as a multiple of capacity. See [[q0315]].
- **Protection**: fuse, protector IC short-circuit detection and cell-level devices. See [[q0325]].
:::

::: answer
"A LiPo has very low internal resistance, just milliohms, so if you short the terminals the only thing limiting current is that internal resistance plus the resistance of whatever you used to short it. For one cell at 4.2 volts and 50 milliohms, that's about 84 amps. For a hobby 4S pack with 5 milliohms per cell, so 20 milliohms total, at 16.8 volts, that's around 840 amps.

All that current goes through the cell's own internal resistance and the wiring. Power is I squared R: about 350 watts inside that single cell, or over 14 kilowatts in the 4S pack. That heats the cell by several degrees per second, and as the temperature climbs past around 100 to 130 degrees you get separator damage, venting and thermal runaway. The wiring and connectors also heat or weld, or arc.

So the cell is unforgiving, and protection has to be fast: a fuse sized for the pack voltage and interrupting current, a protection IC that detects the short through a sense resistor or the FET voltage and opens within a few hundred microseconds, and PTC or CID devices inside some cells. Also wire and connectors, like XT60, are chosen to survive or fail safely. And as always, the best protection is mechanical: keep terminals insulated."
:::

::: technical
**Short-circuit current.** For a source of open-circuit voltage $V_{oc}$, internal resistance $R_{int}$ and external short resistance $R_{ext}$:

$$I_{sc} = \frac{V_{oc}}{R_{int} + R_{ext}}$$

- 1S, 2000 mAh, $R_{int} = 50\ \text{m}\Omega$, dead short ($R_{ext}\approx 0$): $I = 4.2/0.05 = 84$ A (42 C).
- 4S, 5000 mAh high-discharge hobby pack, $R_{int} = 4\times 5\ \text{m}\Omega = 20\ \text{m}\Omega$: $I = 16.8/0.02 = 840$ A.
- With a 10 mΩ external short on the 4S pack: $I = 16.8/(0.02+0.01) = 560$ A.

**Where the power goes.** In the cell: $P_{cell} = I^2 R_{int}$. For the 4S pack, $840^2 \times 0.02 = 14.1$ kW. In 1 m of 12 AWG copper (about 5.2 mΩ/m): $840^2 \times 0.0052 = 3.7$ kW/m. Both exceed anything the parts are rated for, so something melts, arcs, or vents within seconds.

**Cell heating.** For the 1S cell ($m \approx 40$ g, $c \approx 1$ kJ/(kg·K)): $dT/dt \approx 353/(0.04\times1000) = 8.8$ K/s. From 25 °C to about 130 °C takes roughly 12 s if all heat stays in the cell. Real heat spreads unevenly and is hotter at the tabs.

**Timing.** The initial rise is limited by loop inductance: with $L = 1\ \mu\text{H}$, $di/dt = V/L = 16.8\ \text{A/}\mu\text{s}$ and the time constant is $L/R = 1\ \mu\text{H}/20\ \text{m}\Omega = 50\ \mu\text{s}$. So the short reaches its full value within about 100 to 200 µs. A protector IC's short-circuit delay (typically a few hundred µs, datasheet-dependent) is comparable, so the *peak current* before cutoff can be hundreds of amps.

**Positive feedback.** Cell resistance falls as temperature rises (ionic conductivity increases), so for a fixed voltage the short current *rises* as the cell heats, which raises the heating. Separator shutdown and cell PTC devices act against this, but not reliably.

**Protection (layered).**

- **Fuse** in the pack path with a DC voltage rating above the pack voltage and an interrupt rating above the prospective short current.
- **Protector IC** with short-circuit threshold (via sense resistor or FET $R_{DS(on)}$), a hardware delay of microseconds to hundreds of microseconds, and latching shutdown.
- **FET safe operating area**: the opening switch must withstand the inductive spike and the current at turn-off.
- **Cell-level**: PTC or CID in cylindrical cells, tab design.
- **Wiring**: wire gauge, insulation, connectors (polarized, with shrouds) so shorts are unlikely.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Internal resistance, protector delays and cell shutdown behaviour differ widely between cells and ICs. The numbers are typical hobby/consumer values, not a specific part.
:::

::: short
"A LiPo has milliohm-level internal resistance, so a short is limited almost only by that: one 4.2 V cell at 50 milliohms gives about 84 amps, and a 4S hobby pack with 20 milliohms gives around 840. That puts hundreds of watts to kilowatts of $I^2R$ into the cell and wiring, heating it by many degrees per second toward venting and thermal runaway. So you need layered protection: a properly rated fuse, a fast short-circuit-detecting protector IC, cell-level devices, and wiring and connectors that prevent shorts."
:::

::: long
"A battery is designed to be a stiff voltage source, so its internal resistance is small. For a LiPo that's a few milliohms for a high-discharge hobby cell up to tens of milliohms for a small consumer cell. If you short the terminals, Ohm's law says the current is the cell voltage divided by that internal resistance plus whatever resistance the short has. One 2 amp-hour cell at 4.2 volts and 50 milliohms gives 84 amps. A 4S hobby pack, 16.8 volts across about 20 milliohms, gives 840 amps.

Now where does that power go? I squared R. In the single cell that's about 350 watts, in the 4S pack over 14 kilowatts, plus a few kilowatts per metre in 12-gauge wire. The cell is only tens of grams, so it's heating at something like 9 degrees C per second or more. In about ten seconds it's near the temperature where the separator fails and thermal runaway starts, and the wires and connectors get hot enough to melt or arc. And because the cell resistance drops as it warms, the current actually increases, which is positive feedback.

So we protect in layers. A fuse rated for the pack voltage and interrupting current, which is the last resort. A protection IC that detects over-current across a sense resistor or the FET and opens within a few hundred microseconds. Cell-level safety devices like a PTC or current-interrupt device. And mechanical design: insulated, polarized connectors, wire gauge that matches the fuse, and handling rules to keep terminals covered. I'd also point out an internal short, like a puncture, is much worse because none of these external devices can see it."
:::

::: terms
- **Short-circuit current**: current through a near-zero-resistance connection across the source, limited by $R_{int}$.
- **Internal resistance**: equivalent series resistance of the cell that limits current and causes $I^2R$ heating.
- **C rating**: continuous (or burst) discharge capability expressed as a multiple of capacity.
- **Prospective fault current**: the current a source *could* deliver into a short; the fuse and wiring must be chosen for it.
- **Interrupt rating (breaking capacity)**: the maximum current at a given voltage a fuse can safely open.
- **PTC (polymeric positive temperature coefficient)**: resettable device whose resistance rises sharply when hot.
- **CID**: pressure-triggered current interrupt device in cylindrical cells.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "very high current, it heats up and can catch fire".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Does the **calculation**: $I = V/(R_{int}+R_{ext})$ with numbers, and expresses it in C-rate (84 A is 42 C on a 2 Ah cell).
- Calculates **power and heating rate** and compares with the thermal limit (about 12 s to 130 °C in the example).
- Notes the **positive feedback** (resistance falls with temperature) and the **inductance limit** on the initial rise.
- Explains protection **timing and ratings**: DC fuse voltage/interrupt ratings, protector delay, FET SOA.
- Distinguishes **external vs internal** shorts ([[q0329]]).
- Mentions practical safeguards: polarised connectors, shrouds, wire gauge, fusing at the battery, not at the load.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Short-circuit current is the rated C-rate."** The short is far above the rated continuous current, often 2 to 10 times.
- **Using an AC fuse or a fuse with too low DC voltage rating.** DC arcs do not self-extinguish; the fuse must be rated for the pack's DC voltage and fault current.
- **Putting the fuse far from the battery.** Wiring between the cell and the fuse is unprotected. Fuse at the battery terminal.
- **Counting on the protector IC alone.** It can fail short, take hundreds of µs to act, and cannot see internal shorts.
- **Thinking low state of charge makes a short harmless.** The current depends on voltage and resistance, not remaining capacity.
- **Ignoring wiring heating.** The wire or connector often fails before the cell does; size them for the fuse rating.
:::

For the 4S pack at 14.1 kW on roughly 0.5 kg of cells, the heating rate is $14\,112/(0.5\times1000) \approx 28$ K/s, so the same temperature is reached in about 4 s. Energy in the pack is $5\ \text{Ah}\times14.8\ \text{V}=74$ Wh (266 kJ), so at this rate it would dump all its energy in 19 s. In practice something fails or vents long before.

## How fast does the current rise?

The loop has inductance (wires, busbars, connectors). For $L = 1\ \mu\text{H}$ and $V = 16.8$ V, the initial slope is $di/dt = 16.8\ \text{A/}\mu\text{s}$ and the time constant is $\tau = L/R = 1\ \mu\text{H}/20\ \text{m}\Omega = 50\ \mu\text{s}$. So the current reaches about 63% of its final value in 50 µs and basically the final value within 200 µs.

The takeaway for protection: a short-circuit detector with a 200 to 500 µs delay lets the current reach nearly its full prospective value before opening. The opening switch then faces this current *and* an inductive voltage spike (see [[q0340]]).

## Protection design checklist

| Protection | What it must withstand or sense | Common pitfall |
|---|---|---|
| Fuse | DC voltage rating above pack voltage; interrupt rating above prospective current (840 A in this example) | Using an AC-rated or low-interrupt fuse; fusing at the load end |
| Protector IC + FETs | SC detect threshold, delay, FET SOA at turn-off current | FET $R_{DS(on)}$ threshold drifts with temperature |
| Wire/connectors | Fuse current rating plus margin; insulation | Connectors that can touch when unplugged; unprotected wire between cell and fuse |
| Cell PTC/CID | Shutdown on heating or pressure | Not resettable after CID; PTC slow compared with short |
| Mechanical | Insulated terminals, keyed connectors | Loose cells with metal objects |

## What the cell sees: sag and C-rate

During a short the terminal voltage collapses toward zero because the internal resistance drops almost all the EMF. The same physics appears in normal high-current discharge: at $I$ amps the terminal voltage is $V_{oc} - I R_{int}$ and the cell wastes $I^2R_{int}$ as heat. A 5C discharge of a 2 Ah cell is 10 A: $P = 100\times0.05 = 5$ W, which is why hobby packs quote a C rating and why high-rate use warms the pack ([[q0332]]).

## Related: why this is worse than a short of a power supply

A bench supply, a wall adapter and a regulator all have current limiting. A battery does not: its limit is chemistry and geometry, which is why battery packs need external limiting (fuse, protector) and why battery-powered products use polarized, finger-safe connectors. See also the internal-short case in [[q0329]].
:::

::: applications
- **Hobby/RC and drones**: XT60/XT90 connectors with shrouds, inline fuses and ESCs with current limiting to survive crash shorts.
- **Laptop and phone packs**: protector IC with short-circuit threshold plus a fuse or thermal cutoff in the pack.
- **Power tools and e-bikes**: contactor or MOSFET arrays with fast short-circuit trip, rated fuses, and busbar design.
- **Energy storage systems**: DC fuses and breakers with high interrupt ratings and arc-flash analysis.
- **Bench practice**: current-limited supplies when testing; insulated tools near batteries.
:::

::: assumptions
- Internal resistances (50 mΩ per small cell, 5 mΩ per high-discharge hobby cell) are **illustrative**; real values change with SoC, temperature and age ([[q0303]]).
- Simplified linear Thevenin model: real cells sag nonlinearly at very high C-rates and heating changes resistance.
- Adiabatic estimates ignore heat conduction and thermal mass of tabs/wires; they give an upper bound on heating rate.
- External-short assumption: the terminals are bridged. An *internal* short is analyzed in [[q0329]].
- A question that gives *only* a C rating (say, 20C on 2 Ah) expects you to note the short exceeds it: 40 A continuous versus a short of 84 A.
:::

::: followups
### A 3S 2200 mAh pack has R_int = 30 mΩ total. What current flows in a dead short at 12.6 V?
$I = 12.6/0.03 = 420$ A, which is about 190 C. Cell heating is $I^2R = 420^2 \times 0.03 = 5.3$ kW. That is far beyond the pack's rating, so a fuse or protector must open it within milliseconds.

### Why does a fuse need a DC voltage rating?
In DC there is no zero crossing to extinguish the arc when the fuse element melts. A fuse rated only for 32 V DC can sustain an arc on a 50 V pack and fail to interrupt. Pack fuses are chosen for at least the maximum pack voltage and an interrupt rating above the prospective current.

### How does a protector IC detect a short circuit?
It measures the voltage across a sense resistor (or across the series FETs, $I\times 2R_{DS(on)}$) and compares it with a threshold. If the threshold is exceeded for longer than a delay (often a few hundred µs, to avoid false trips), it turns the FETs off and latches until the load is removed. FET-based sensing saves a resistor but varies with temperature.

### What if the short comes through a long thin wire?
The wire resistance limits current and becomes the heating element. 1 m of 26 AWG wire is about 0.13 Ω, so a 4S pack would drive only about $16.8/0.15 = 112$ A and the wire would dissipate about 1.6 kW, glowing or melting in a second or two. That is why you fuse the wire at the source and why thin sense lines on balance leads are dangerous if they short.

### Scenario: your product's pack fuse sometimes opens when a motor starts. What do you check?
Compare the motor inrush current (stall current $V/R$) and its duration with the fuse's time-current curve and the cell's C-rate. It may be a legitimate overload (undersized fuse or cell), or a pack-level voltage sag pulling the protector's threshold. Fix with soft start, a bigger-rated fuse (only if wiring and cells allow), or a higher-C cell. Do not simply put a bigger fuse in.

### Which is worse: a short at the battery or at the load end of a long cable?
At the battery end, current is limited only by $R_{int}$, so it is highest, but a fuse at the battery protects the wire. At the load end through a long cable, the cable's resistance reduces current, but the unprotected cable heats along its whole length if the fuse is at the load. So fuse at the source and size the wire to the fuse.
:::

::: interpretations
"Short circuit a LiPo" is taken as an *external* short between the pack terminals. The puncture/internal short is treated in [[q0329]]; hazards in general are in [[q0327]]. The C rating context is [[q0315]]. For a calculation-style follow-up, interviewers often expect you to ask for $R_{int}$ and $V_{oc}$, then apply $I = V/R$.
