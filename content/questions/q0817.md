::: basics
### In plain words
In an ADC, the sample time is how long the input is connected to the sampling capacitor so it can charge up. Too short, and the capacitor does not reach the input voltage, so the reading is wrong. Too long, and you waste time and get fewer readings per second. You pick the shortest time that is accurate enough.

### Picture it
Picture filling a small bucket through a thin hose. If you stop too soon, the bucket is not full and you under-measure. The thinner the hose, the longer you must wait. Waiting far longer than needed is safe but slow. The hose is the sensor's resistance, called source impedance, and the bucket is the sampling capacitor.

### Words you need
- **Sampling (acquisition) time**: how long the input stays connected to the sampling capacitor.
- **Conversion time**: how long the ADC then takes to turn the held voltage into a code.
- **Time constant**: resistance times capacitance, which says how quickly the capacitor charges. About 9 are needed for a 12-bit result.
- **Source impedance**: the resistance of whatever drives the ADC pin, such as a sensor.
- **Throughput**: complete readings per second, which is 1 / (sampling time + conversion time).

### The simplest example
A sensor plus the ADC's internal switch gives 11 kΩ of resistance, and the sampling capacitor is 8 pF. The time constant is 11 kΩ × 8 pF = 88 ns. For 12 bits you need about 9 of them, so about 0.8 µs of sampling. Give it only 100 ns and the capacitor reaches only part of the way, so the reading is low and depends on the sensor's resistance. A longer window also lowers throughput, so you pick the shortest setting that is accurate enough.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows what under-settling looks like in real data, the cost in readings per second, and ways out such as a buffer or a capacitor on the pin.
:::

::: testing
Whether you see sample time as a **budget between two failure modes**. Too short and the sampling capacitor does not settle (gain error, memory of the previous channel, distortion); too long and you lose throughput, let droop and leakage accumulate (for the hold side), and in some cases lose time resolution. The interviewer wants the quantitative core, $t_{acq}\ge\tau\ln2^{N+1}$ with $\tau=(R_s+R_{on})C$, the consequences for a microcontroller (the sampling-time register, high-impedance sensors, multiplexed channels) and the design levers (smaller capacitor, stronger driver, buffer, bucket capacitor) with their own costs.
:::

::: why
This is the question behind every "why does my ADC reading depend on which channel I read last?" bug and every "how fast can I scan 8 channels?" planning exercise. A wrong sampling-time setting passes a bench test with a low-impedance source and fails in the field with a thermistor or a divider. It also frames the trade-off at the heart of S/H design ([[q0816]]) and ADC front ends ([[q0818]]): speed, accuracy, noise and power can't all be maximised at once.
:::

::: concepts
- **RC settling**: $v(t)=V_{in}(1-e^{-t/\tau})$; after $n$ time constants the residual error is $e^{-n}$. See [[q0814]].
- **Required time constants for $N$ bits**: $\ln2^{N+1}$ ($=9.0$ for 12 bits, 11.8 for 16).
- **Source impedance**: the sensor's resistance adds to the switch resistance in $\tau$.
- **Droop** during hold: $I/C$ ([[q0815]]).
- **Throughput**: $f_s=1/(t_{sample}+t_{conv})$.
- **Channel memory**: charge left on the capacitor from the previous input.
:::

::: answer
"The sampling window is a trade-off between accuracy and speed. During it, the sampling capacitor charges through the source resistance and the switch, which is an RC, and I need about nine time constants to get within half an LSB at 12 bits.

If the sample time is too short, the capacitor doesn't reach the input. The reading is pulled toward whatever was on the capacitor before, so you get a gain error that depends on source impedance and a crosstalk-like 'memory' from the last channel. As a number: with 11 kΩ and 8 pF the time constant is 88 ns, so I need about 0.8 µs. If I give it only 100 ns, it's still about a third of the step away.

If it's too long, I'm just wasting time. Throughput drops, and since the conversion rate is one over sample plus conversion time, a 240-cycle sample window on a 14 MHz ADC limits me to about 55 kS/s, against 1 MS/s at the minimum. A long window can also let more noise through because I'm tracking a noisy source, although that's partly offset by averaging.

On the hold side it's the opposite way round: too short and the ADC can't finish; too long and leakage droops the sample. In practice I compute the required time from the source impedance and pick the shortest setting that satisfies it with margin. If I need faster, I lower the source impedance with a buffer or put a capacitor on the pin."
:::

::: technical
**Track-phase settling.** Total resistance $R_{tot}=R_s+R_{on}$, sampling capacitance $C_S$, $\tau=R_{tot}C_S$. For a full-scale step the residual error as a fraction of full scale is $e^{-t/\tau}$. Half-LSB accuracy at $N$ bits:

$$t_{acq}\ge\tau\ln2^{N+1}=0.693(N+1)\,\tau$$

| $N$ | 8 | 10 | 12 | 14 | 16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\ln2^{N+1}$ (time constants) | 6.2 | 7.6 | 9.0 | 10.4 | 11.8 |

**Example (12-bit, $C_S=8$ pF, $R_{on}=1$ kΩ).**

| Source $R_s$ | $\tau$ | $t_{acq}$ (9.01 τ) | In 14 MHz ADC cycles |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 kΩ | 16 ns | 144 ns | 2.0 |
| 10 kΩ | 88 ns | 0.79 µs | 11.1 |
| 100 kΩ | 808 ns | 7.3 µs | 102 |

**Cost of too short.** The residual is $\epsilon=e^{-t/\tau}$. With $\tau=88$ ns and a window of 1.5 cycles at 14 MHz (107 ns), $\epsilon=30\%$, nearly 1 V on a 3.3 V step. The error depends on the previous sample (channel memory) and on $R_s$, so it looks like gain error, crosstalk between multiplexed channels and distortion on varying signals.

**Cost of too long.**
- *Throughput*: $f_s=f_{ADC}/(n_{samp}+n_{conv})$. With $n_{conv}=12.5$ cycles at 14 MHz: 1.5-cycle sample gives 1 MS/s, 13.5 cycles gives 538 kS/s, 239.5 cycles gives 55.6 kS/s.
- *Bandwidth/aperture*: the input is effectively averaged over the window, so a very long window attenuates fast signals (the track bandwidth $1/(2\pi\tau)$ is a property of the circuit, but the window sets how much of the input's history is integrated).
- *Noise*: a longer window lets more broadband noise from a noisy high-impedance source onto $C_S$, though the RC limits its bandwidth to $1/(2\pi\tau)$.

**Hold phase.** Too short: not enough time for the $N$ comparisons of a SAR. Too long: droop $I_{leak}t/C$ ([[q0815]]) and, in a multi-channel scan, stale data.

**Design levers and their price.**

| Lever | Helps | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Smaller $C_S$ or $R_{on}$ | Faster settling | More $kT/C$ noise, more injected charge per capacitance, bigger switch loads source |
| Lower source impedance (buffer) | Faster settling | Buffer noise, power, must drive switched-capacitor kickback |
| External bucket capacitor $C_{ext}\gg C_S$ | Source only has to recharge the bucket slowly; kickback absorbed | Slows the sensor's response, needs a source that can drive $C_{ext}$ |
| Longer sampling window | Better accuracy | Lower throughput |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The sampling-time options (for example 1.5 to a few hundred cycles), minimum ADC clocks and equivalent input circuits (sampling capacitance, switch resistance) differ by MCU family. Use your part's reference manual and its source-impedance table.
:::

::: long
"The trade-off is accuracy against speed, and it comes from one equation: the sampling capacitor charges through the source resistance plus the switch resistance, so it settles like an RC.

To get within half an LSB at 12 bits I need about nine time constants. Take an 8 pF sampling capacitor, a 1 kΩ switch and a 10 kΩ sensor. Tau is 88 nanoseconds, so I need about 0.8 microseconds. If the source is 100 kΩ, that's 7 microseconds.

What happens if I make the window too short? The capacitor never reaches the input. Say I only give it 107 nanoseconds in that example: it's still about 30% short, almost a volt on a 3.3 V step. And because it's a step from whatever was there before, the error depends on the previous channel, so channels leak into each other. It looks like gain error that changes with source impedance, and it's easy to miss on the bench with a low-impedance supply.

Too long: I lose throughput. On a 14 MHz ADC with 12.5 cycles of conversion, a 1.5-cycle sample gives 1 million samples a second, while a 240-cycle sample gives about 55,000. Also the longer I track a noisy source, the more noise I can pick up, although the RC limits the bandwidth.

On the hold side it's reversed: too short and the ADC can't complete its comparisons, too long and leakage droops the sample.

So the design process is: compute tau, multiply by nine, add margin, pick the shortest sampling time that covers it. If I need to go faster than the source allows, I lower the source impedance with an op-amp buffer, or I put a capacitor at the pin that's much bigger than the sampling capacitor, so it supplies the charge and the sensor only has to top it up."
:::

::: terms
- **Sampling (acquisition) time**: how long the switch stays closed to charge $C_S$.
- **Conversion time**: time for the quantizer to produce the code after the hold.
- **Time constant $\tau$**: $R_{tot}C_S$.
- **Settling error**: remaining fraction $e^{-t/\tau}$.
- **Channel memory / crosstalk**: error from charge left from the previous channel.
- **Source impedance limit**: the maximum $R_s$ the datasheet allows at a given sampling time.
- **Bucket (charge reservoir) capacitor**: external capacitor at the ADC pin that supplies the sampling charge.
- **Throughput**: complete conversions per second, $1/(t_{samp}+t_{conv})$.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **Choosing the shortest sampling time "for speed" with a sensor of unknown impedance.** It passes with a bench supply and fails with the real sensor.
- **Setting the ADC clock faster without increasing the number of sampling cycles.** The window is cycles divided by clock, so it shrinks in absolute time.
- **Assuming the error is random.** Under-settling is systematic and repeatable, which is why calibration can mask it for one channel and break it for another.
- **Putting a large capacitor on the pin of a high-impedance sensor without checking the sensor's own bandwidth.** $R_s\cdot C_{ext}$ may be milliseconds.
- **Believing a longer window is always "safer".** It lowers throughput and, when scanning channels, makes timing between channels worse.
- **Forgetting the hold side.** A very low ADC clock lengthens the conversion and the droop ([[q0815]]).
:::

::: deep
## Two windows, two failure modes

A conversion has two phases. During the **sample (track)** window the switch is closed and $C_S$ charges toward $V_{in}$. During the **hold + conversion** window the switch is open and the quantizer works. The question asks "super fast/slow sample and hold durations", which covers both, so keep them separate.

| | Too short | Too long |
|---|---|---|
| **Sample window** | $C_S$ doesn't settle: gain error, channel memory, distortion | Throughput falls; noisy sources integrate more noise; scan time grows |
| **Hold window** | Quantizer can't finish: wrong or truncated code | Droop and leakage corrupt the held value; stale data in multi-channel scans |

## The settling physics

During track, $v_C(t)=V_{in}-(V_{in}-V_0)e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau=(R_s+R_{on})C_S$. The worst case is a full-scale step from the previous sample, so the residual error is $\epsilon=V_{FS}e^{-t/\tau}$.

## What under-settling looks like in real data

Suppose a 14 MHz ADC scans two channels: channel A at 0 V and channel B at 3.3 V, 10 kΩ source impedance and the minimum 1.5-cycle (107 ns) sampling time. With $\tau=88$ ns the capacitor reaches only $1-e^{-1.22}=70\%$ of the step each time. B reads about $0.70\cdot3.3+0.30\cdot V_{previous}$: after A (0 V) it reads 2.32 V; on repeated B conversions it creeps up geometrically (2.32, 3.01, 3.21 V, ...) and only approaches 3.3 V after several conversions. So the same input reads differently depending on scan order, which is channel crosstalk caused by memory.

Three signatures that a reading is under-settled:
1. The error changes with **source impedance** (swap in a 1 kΩ source and it improves).
2. The error changes with the **sampling-time setting** (double it and the error shrinks).
3. The error depends on the **previous channel** in the scan.

## Ways out of the trade-off

1. **Lower the source impedance with a buffer.** An op-amp follower with a few ohms of output resistance turns the 100 kΩ problem into a 1 kΩ one, a 50× faster settling window, at the cost of buffer power, noise and offset. It must also be stable driving the ADC's switched-capacitor load, which is why datasheets recommend a small series resistor.
2. **Use an external bucket capacitor.** Place $C_{ext}\gg C_S$ (nanofarads) at the pin. At the moment the switch closes, $C_S$ takes charge from $C_{ext}$ and not from the far-away sensor, causing a small droop $\Delta V=V\cdot C_S/(C_{ext}+C_S)$, e.g. 8 pF vs 10 nF gives 0.08% (about 3 LSB at 12 bits), and then the sensor recharges $C_{ext}$ slowly between samples. Increase $C_{ext}$ to $2^N C_S$ (about 33 nF for 12 bits at 8 pF) to push the droop below an LSB. The cost is a slow input RC: 100 kΩ with 10 nF has $\tau=1$ ms, so it filters and slows the signal.
3. **Reduce $C_S$ and $R_{on}$** (an IC design choice): faster, but more kT/C noise and charge-injection error.
4. **Accept a lower rate**, which is fine when the signal is slow (battery voltage, temperature), as the extra throughput is unused anyway.

## Noise and bandwidth

Because the settling RC has a bandwidth $f=1/(2\pi\tau)$, a longer window doesn't change the circuit's bandwidth; it only gives the capacitor more time to reach its final value. What the sampling window *does* do is set how much of the source's noise and signal history is integrated before the hold. A high-impedance source has thermal noise $\sqrt{4kTR_s\Delta f}$; with $R_s=100$ kΩ and $\Delta f=1/(4\tau)=310$ kHz the noise on the capacitor is about 22 µV rms, the same as $\sqrt{kT/C_S}$, as it must be, since the capacitor ends up sampling the thermal noise of the whole RC network.

## A firmware recipe

1. Find the maximum source resistance of the signal (including any filter resistor).
2. Read the ADC's equivalent input circuit from the datasheet ($C_S$, $R_{on}$) or its table of $R_{AIN}$ versus sampling time.
3. Compute $t_{acq}=(R_s+R_{on})C_S\ln2^{N+1}$.
4. Convert to ADC cycles and select the next higher setting.
5. Validate with a known DC source at both ends of the range and with the channel order swapped.
:::

::: applications
- **Multiplexed sensor scans** in an MCU: temperature, battery voltage and current on one ADC, each needing its own sampling time.
- **High-impedance sensors** such as thermistors in large-value dividers, pH electrodes or photodiodes, where a buffer or bucket capacitor is required.
- **Motor control**: current samples taken at the PWM centre with a short, known window, using a low-impedance current-sense amplifier ([[q0449]]).
- **Audio and data acquisition**: choosing between high rate and settling accuracy.
- **Low-power sensor nodes**: lengthening the window to allow a cheap high-impedance divider reduces standing current in the divider.
- **Calibration routines**: using the internal reference channel, which typically needs a long sampling time because of its high source impedance.
:::

::: assumptions
- A single-pole RC settling model and a full-scale step as the worst case. Real input structures have parasitic capacitances and may settle with multiple time constants.
- **$C_S$ and $R_{on}$ are constant**. Real switch resistance varies with the input voltage.
- A **SAR ADC with an on-chip sampling capacitor**. The same ideas apply to external S/H amplifiers, with the amplifier's settling replacing the source RC.
- The numbers use a hypothetical 14 MHz ADC clock and a 12.5-cycle conversion; real parts differ.
- Throughput numbers ignore any additional overhead cycles (trigger latency, DMA).
:::

::: followups
### My ADC reads fine with the bench supply but too low with the real sensor. Why?
The sensor's source impedance is much larger than the supply's, so $\tau=R_sC_S$ is larger and the sampling window no longer covers $9\tau$. The capacitor falls short of the true voltage. Increase the sampling time, buffer the sensor, or add a bucket capacitor.

### How large should the bucket capacitor be?
Large enough that the voltage dip when $C_S$ connects is below half an LSB: $C_{ext}\ge C_S(2^{N+1}-1)$, about 66 nF for 12 bits and 8 pF. A 10 nF capacitor leaves about 3 LSB of dip but the sensor re-charges it between samples, so the steady-state error is much smaller; 100 nF is a common practical choice if the sensor can drive it.

### What if I scan 8 channels? How does that change the sampling time?
Each switch to a new channel starts from the previous channel's voltage, so each sample must settle through a worst-case full-scale step, and the total scan time is $8(t_{samp}+t_{conv})$. If one channel is high-impedance, give it a long window and the others short windows (many MCUs allow per-channel sampling times).

### Does a longer sampling window average out noise?
Not really. The RC filter means the capacitor tracks the noise within bandwidth $1/(2\pi\tau)$ and the final value includes noise at the instant the switch opens. Averaging happens only if you take several conversions. A long window just ensures settling.

### Harder: how would you verify the sampling time is adequate on real hardware?
Apply a DC level through a source resistor equal to the worst case, alternate channels at 0 V and full scale, and compare readings with the same level on a single channel. Then repeat with double the sampling time: if the codes change by more than about one LSB, the original setting was under-settled. Repeat at temperature.
