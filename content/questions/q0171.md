::: basics
### In plain words
Power sequencing means turning a system's supply voltages (its "rails") on and off in a controlled order and with controlled timing. Many chips have several rails, such as a low-voltage core and a higher-voltage I/O ring. Powering them in the wrong order can push current through paths that were never meant to carry it. Sequencing keeps the chip safe and starting in a known state.

### Picture it
It is like getting dressed: socks go on before shoes. You could force it the other way, but it works badly and you might damage something. The difference is that you can see the right order for clothes. For chips, the datasheet tells you the order, and common sense can mislead you.

### Words you need
- **Rail**: one supply voltage in the system, such as 1.0 V or 3.3 V.
- **Power domain**: a part of a chip fed by its own rail, such as the core or the I/O.
- **ESD diode**: a protection diode on each pin that guards against static shocks. It can also conduct if the wrong rail is up first.
- **Power-good (PG)**: a signal that says a rail has reached a valid level.
- **Reset**: a signal that holds a chip in a known state until all rails are stable ([[q0166]]).

### The simplest example
A chip has a 1.0 V core and a 3.3 V I/O rail. Its datasheet says the I/O voltage must not exceed the core voltage by more than 0.3 V. If 3.3 V comes up first, the I/O sits 3.3 V above a core that is still at 0 V. That breaks the rule, and current can flow through the ESD diodes into the core. So the core comes up first. Once its power-good signal says it is valid, the I/O rail starts. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows what goes wrong, how to read the order from datasheets, and how to choose the timing.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand that multi-rail ICs have *conditions* on the order, ramp and timing of their supplies, and *why* (ESD diodes, substrate parasitics, undefined logic states, back-powering, latch-up, contention), and whether you know where the answer comes from (the device datasheets, not intuition). The timing part checks that you say "it depends on the part, usually threshold-based rather than a fixed delay" and can describe what limits the delay (settling past a threshold, power-good, reset hold times) with real numbers.
:::

::: why
A wrong power-up order can cause excess current, a stuck or never-booting processor, a latch-up that destroys the part later, or a field failure that appears only when the supply rises slowly. FPGAs, SoCs, DDR memory, ADCs/DACs and mixed-voltage systems all have sequencing requirements, so every hardware engineer meets them. Interviewers ask because it tests whether you read datasheets for conditions, know the physics behind them, and can design the control circuitry ([[q0172]]). The same topic reappears in the power-integrity section as [[q0706]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Multiple supply domains**: core, I/O, analog, memory, PLL, each at its own voltage ([[q0166]]).
- **ESD and substrate diodes**: parasitic paths that conduct when one domain is powered and another is not.
- **Power-good (PG) and reset**: signals telling downstream circuits when a rail is valid.
- **Ramp rate and monotonicity**: how fast and how cleanly a rail rises.
- **Soft-start and inrush**: $I=C\,dV/dt$ into output capacitors.
- **Brownout and power-down order**: what happens when a rail falls or when power is removed.
:::

::: answer
"Power sequencing is controlling the order and timing in which the supply rails of a system turn on and off.

It's needed because many chips have several supply domains, like a 1 volt core, 1.8 volt auxiliary and 3.3 volt I/O, and they have internal structures that don't like the wrong order. If the I/O is powered while the core is off, the signals can drive current through ESD diodes into the unpowered core rail, back-powering it, which can cause latch-up or leave the chip in an undefined state, with output pins fighting each other on a bus. With the core on and I/O off, the logic can be in unknown states. DDR memory, FPGAs, SoCs and ADCs often spell out requirements.

How do I determine the sequence? From the datasheets: the power-up sequence section and the absolute maximum ratings, like 'I/O voltage must not exceed VDD by more than 0.3 volts', which tells me whether I need core first, I/O first or tracking. Then I make a dependency table across all chips, including the interfaces between them.

How much time? There isn't a universal number. Some parts allow any order if rails are monotonic, others give ramp windows, like a few hundred microseconds to tens of milliseconds, or require one rail to reach a threshold before the next starts. So I'd do it threshold-based using power-good rather than fixed delays, add a small settling delay, and hold reset until all rails are stable. Power-down is usually the reverse order."
:::

::: technical
**Why order matters (physics).** An IC's pins connect through ESD diodes to the supply rails and the substrate. If an input pad is driven above an unpowered rail by more than $\approx0.6$ V, the diode conducts and current flows into that rail (back-power). Example: a 3.3 V driver with 50 Ω output resistance into a pad whose clamp diode feeds a 330 Ω core load: $V_{DD}\approx2.26$ V and $I\approx6.8$ mA flows, enough to put the chip into an undefined partial-power state; CMOS parts can also latch up (a parasitic SCR turns on) when I/O pins swing beyond the supplies. With I/O off and core on, output buffers have undefined states and can fight; with the wrong order in a DDR system, termination can drive contention.

**What sets the order.**
1. *Datasheet power-up/-down sequence sections* (explicit order, ramp-time window, monotonicity, hold times).
2. *Absolute maximum ratings* like $V_{IO}\le V_{DD}+0.3$ V: this implies $V_{DD}$ must be up first or tracking.
3. *Internal dependencies*: PLL/analog references before logic; I/O bank supplies vs core; configuration voltages.
4. *Board-level interfaces*: a signal from a device powered at 3.3 V into a pin on a powered-down 1.8 V domain.
5. *Power-down*: typically the reverse; with brownout (loss of supply) the order is not controlled, so parts must be robust or supervised.

**Timing.**
- Often **threshold-based**: rail B may start when rail A is above X% of nominal (PG asserted).
- Sometimes **ramp-window based**: each rail must rise monotonically within $t_{min}$ and $t_{max}$ (for example hundreds of µs to tens of ms is typical for large FPGAs/SoCs, but this is part-specific).
- Sometimes **voltage-difference based**: $V_{IO}-V_{core}\le0.5$ V at all times.
- **Reset hold:** reset remains asserted for a datasheet time ($10$–$200$ ms typical for many MCUs/SoCs) after the last rail is valid.
- **Delays between rails** are set to be longer than rail settling (soft-start, load), including tolerance, and short enough for boot-time targets: usually from hundreds of µs to a few ms or tens of ms.

**Soft-start and inrush.** Output charge current $I=C\,dV/dt$: $470\ \mu$F ramping to 3.3 V in 1 ms draws 1.55 A, in 5 ms 0.31 A. A typical soft-start timer is $t_{ss}=C_{ss}V_{ref}/I_{ss}$; with $C_{ss}=10$ nF, $V_{ref}=0.8$ V, $I_{ss}=5\ \mu$A, $t_{ss}=1.6$ ms (typical form, check the part).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Specific ramp-time windows, allowed voltage differences and reset-hold times are device-specific and revision-specific. This page gives the method; always take the numbers from the datasheet or the vendor's power-sequencing guide for the exact part.
:::

::: long
"Sequencing is deliberately controlling the order and timing in which the rails of a system come up and go down.

Why is it needed? Because modern chips have more than one supply domain, a low-voltage core, an auxiliary or PLL rail, an I/O rail, and each has structures between domains: ESD clamp diodes, substrate diodes, level shifters. If an I/O pin is driven while the chip's own supply is off, current flows through the clamp diode into the unpowered rail, which can partially power the chip, leaving it in an undefined state, or trigger latch-up, which can destroy it. If the core is up and the I/O isn't, the output buffers can be in unknown states and fight on shared buses. DDR memory has its own tracking requirements, and FPGAs and SoCs have documented sequences.

How do I determine the order? From the datasheets. I read the power-up sequence section and the absolute maximum table. If it says the I/O voltage must not exceed the core by more than 0.3 volts, that tells me the core has to rise first or the rails must track. Then I look at the system: what drives what, and I build a dependency table covering all the devices and the interfaces between them. Power-down is usually the reverse, and I think about what happens in an uncontrolled brownout too.

How much time between rails? There's no universal figure. Some parts only need every rail monotonic and some have ramp-time windows, like 200 microseconds to 50 milliseconds, some need one rail past a threshold before the next. I prefer threshold-based sequencing using power-good signals, since rail rise time varies with load, capacitance and temperature, plus a small settling delay of about a millisecond. Then I hold reset, typically tens to hundreds of milliseconds, until all rails and clocks are stable. I also watch inrush: charging 470 microfarads in one millisecond takes 1.5 amps, so soft-start matters."
:::

::: terms
- **Power sequencing**: controlled order and timing of rail turn-on and turn-off.
- **Power good (PG)**: output flag that a rail is within its valid window.
- **Ramp rate / ramp time**: how quickly a rail rises, specified as a window.
- **Monotonic**: rising without dips or non-monotonic steps.
- **Tracking**: rails rising together, ratiometrically or simultaneously.
- **Back-powering**: current flowing from an active I/O into an unpowered supply through ESD diodes.
- **Latch-up**: parasitic thyristor in CMOS that shorts supply to ground and can destroy the part.
- **Supervisor / reset IC**: monitors rails and holds reset until valid.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Assuming a rail order from a diagram.** The order is in the datasheet and absolute max ratings; do not guess.
- **Using fixed delays only.** If the rail slows (cold, big load capacitor), the next rail may start before it is valid; use PG.
- **Ignoring power-down.** Rails removed in the wrong order, or back-fed by I/O, cause the same damage in reverse.
- **Forgetting interface pins.** A device in the sequence can drive another device that is still off.
- **Releasing reset too early.** The processor or FPGA must see all rails and its clock valid before reset ends.
- **No consideration of inrush.** Simultaneous soft-start into large capacitances can brown out the source.
:::

::: deep
## What is a power sequence?

A *power sequence* is the set of rules that govern when each supply rail is allowed to start (and stop) relative to the others. The simplest system has none: a single 3.3 V rail. A typical FPGA or SoC board has a core rail (0.8–1.2 V), an auxiliary rail (1.8 V), I/O banks (1.8 V, 2.5 V, 3.3 V), transceiver analog rails, DDR rails (VDDQ, VTT) and a configuration flash, each from its own regulator ([[q0166]]).

## Why: the physical reasons

### Back-powering through ESD structures

Every digital pin has diodes to the supply rails to clamp ESD. If a pin is driven high while the chip's supply is off, the upper diode becomes forward-biased and conducts into the supply rail:

With $R_{out}=50\ \Omega$ and a 330 Ω equivalent core load, solving $(3.3-0.7-V_{DD})/50=V_{DD}/330$ gives $V_{DD}=2.26$ V and $I_{back}=6.8$ mA. At 2.26 V the part is partially alive: logic in undefined states, outputs possibly enabled, current drawn through paths that were never meant to carry it, and the supply rail may be pulled up so the regulator's start-up looks different from what was designed (a non-monotonic or pre-biased start). Back-powered rails can also prevent a regulator's UVLO from resetting.

### Latch-up

CMOS has parasitic PNPN structures. If an I/O pin goes above the I/O rail by a diode drop or more (or below ground) while the supply rails are in a transient condition, enough current can trigger a parasitic thyristor, shorting the supply to ground until power is removed, with potential for permanent damage.

### Undefined state and contention

With the core off and I/O on, the logic is not guaranteed to be in any state. Pins configured as outputs may drive randomly onto buses, causing contention. With the core on and I/O off, a chip can attempt to drive pins that are not powered.

### Analog and memory-specific constraints

- References and PLLs may need to be up *before* digital logic starts to avoid mis-calibration.
- DDR memory commonly requires VDD and VDDQ to come up together (tracking) with the termination rail VTT after them, plus a reset hold; each generation has a defined standard.
- FPGAs often require ramps within a window and sometimes a specific order for configuration banks.

## Where the sequence comes from

1. **Datasheet "power-up sequence" and "power-down sequence"** sections. Treat these as requirements.
2. **Absolute maximum ratings** that couple rails, e.g. "$V_{IN(IO)}\le V_{DD}+0.3$ V". This implies that, at any time, the I/O voltage can exceed $V_{DD}$ by no more than 0.3 V, so $V_{DD}$ must be present first or the rails must track.
3. **Interfaces between devices** (a powered device's output into an unpowered device's input).
4. **System requirements**: reset timing, clock availability, boot-time targets, minimum inrush, safety (e.g. motor driver off until logic is up).

Build a table (rows: rails; columns: constraints like "after VCORE > 90%", "monotonic, 0.2–20 ms", "reset hold 20 ms"), then derive the order. This is a partial order; the board needs one consistent sequence satisfying all devices.

## How much time between rails?

There is no universal figure, and the best designs avoid depending on a fixed number.

| Method | How it works | Pros / cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Threshold (PG-based)** | next rail enabled when previous PG asserts | adapts to load/temperature; needs PG routing; usually add settle delay |
| **Fixed delay (RC, timer)** | next EN after $t_d$ | simple; fails if rails vary; must exceed the *worst-case* settling time |
| **Ratiometric tracking** | rails ramp together proportionally | satisfies $\Delta V$ limits automatically |
| **Simultaneous tracking** | rails rise at the same rate, same time | good for core/I/O differences |
| **Sequencer IC / MCU / PMIC** | programmable thresholds and delays | flexible, higher cost, more design |

**What the delay must cover.**
- *Rail settling:* soft-start time $t_{ss}=C_{ss}V_{ref}/I_{ss}$ (e.g. $10\ \text{nF}\times0.8\ \text{V}/5\ \mu\text{A}=1.6$ ms) plus the load-dependent charge time for the output capacitance. A 470 µF output ramped over 1 ms draws 1.55 A; over 5 ms, 0.31 A.
- *Tolerances:* the PG threshold (e.g. 90–95% of nominal), comparator hysteresis and component tolerance.
- *Datasheet windows:* some require $t_{rise}$ between a minimum and maximum; very slow ramps can also be a problem (e.g., sitting in an undefined region).
- *Reset hold:* often 10–200 ms after rails valid so oscillators and PLLs stabilise.
- *Boot-time target:* total start-up must still meet the product requirement.

Typical orders of magnitude: soft-start 0.5–10 ms, inter-rail settle delay 1–10 ms, reset hold 10–200 ms. These are guidance only.

## Power-down and faults

- **Power-down order** is usually the reverse of power-up. To follow it with a fast-falling input, stored energy (bulk capacitance) must keep the later rails up while earlier rails are removed; or use controlled discharge circuits so rails do not float and linger at a harmful level.
- **Brownouts and fast power loss** are not under control; the system relies on part robustness, supervisors to assert reset when a rail drops below a threshold, and bulk hold-up (energy $\tfrac12C(V_1^2-V_2^2)$, e.g., 1000 µF from 12 V to 9 V stores 31.5 mJ).
- **Fault conditions:** if one rail fails (PG deasserts), the system should shut down dependent rails in order or hold the processor in reset.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> After you explain the mechanism, say how you would *verify* it: put a scope on every rail, PG and RESET together, trigger on the first enable, capture power-up and power-down at hot, cold, fast and slow input ramps, and check each datasheet condition against the capture.
:::

::: applications
- **FPGA and SoC boards:** core, auxiliary, I/O and transceiver rails sequenced via PG chaining or a PMIC.
- **DDR memory systems:** VDD/VDDQ tracking, VTT after, reset hold.
- **Mixed-voltage microcontroller systems** with 5 V sensors and 3.3 V logic, where outputs must not drive unpowered parts.
- **Precision ADC/DAC boards:** analog reference up before the digital interface is enabled.
- **Motor and power stages:** logic up and validated before the gate-driver supply is enabled.
- **Hot-swap and backplane cards:** controlled inrush and ordered turn-on when inserted.
:::

::: assumptions
- DC supplies with multiple domains, where the datasheets specify (or imply through absolute maximum ratings) ordering constraints; for a single-rail microcontroller no sequencing is needed.
- Example numbers (ramp times, delays, 330 Ω core load, 0.3 V rule) are illustrative; real values come from the specific datasheets.
- The ESD-diode model is simplified: real input structures vary (some pins are 'fail-safe' or '5 V tolerant' and do not back-power).
- Rails are assumed to be supervised by PG outputs; some regulators have no PG, in which case the delay-based approach or an external supervisor is used.
- For parts that state "no sequencing required", I would still check monotonic ramp, and check interfaces between devices, because the ordering problem may live in the other chip.
:::

::: followups
### Core before I/O or I/O before core?
It depends on the part. Many designs require the core (lowest voltage) first or at least tracking, because an I/O rail above the core by more than the specified margin causes current through the substrate/ESD paths. Some parts specify the opposite or allow any order if rails are monotonic. The datasheet's absolute maximum rating between rails and its power-up section decide; I would never assume.

### How would you check that your sequence is correct on the first board?
Probe all rails, PG signals, EN signals and RESET on a multi-channel scope, trigger on the input power or the first enable, and capture power-up, power-down and a brownout at room temperature, hot and cold (and with slow and fast input ramps). Check each datasheet condition against the traces: order, ramp window, voltage difference limits, reset timing.

### What if the rail ordering you need conflicts between two chips?
Look for a compromise that satisfies both (for instance tracking within the allowed voltage difference), or isolate the interface: use level translators with output-enable, series resistors or load switches so that a device powered earlier cannot drive an unpowered one, or use a sequencer/PMIC with programmable thresholds per rail, and ask the vendor for guidance.

### Why use power-good instead of a fixed delay?
Rail rise time depends on load, capacitance, temperature and input ramp rate, so a fixed delay either wastes time or is too short in the worst case. PG says the previous rail is actually valid. I'd still add a small settling delay after PG for margin and use a timer-based reset hold at the end.

### Scenario: your board boots with a cold start but fails when power is cycled quickly. What's going on?
Rails and capacitors have not discharged: a rail still holds charge, so the next power-up is not from zero and the ramp is not monotonic or the sequence conditions are violated (for example, a rail not below a threshold for a minimum time before re-powering). Check the power-off discharge, the supervisors' reset thresholds and hold times, and add bleed resistors or a controlled discharge and a minimum off-time.
:::
