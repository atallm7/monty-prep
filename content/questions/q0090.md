::: basics
### In plain words
A MOSFET has four terminals: gate, drain, source and body. The gate is the control, and a voltage on it decides whether current can flow from drain to source. It does that by creating or removing the channel, a thin conducting path between drain and source. The body is the silicon the channel forms in. On discrete parts the body is wired to the source inside the package, which is why you usually see only three pins.

### Picture it
Think of a garden hose with a pinch valve. Water enters at the source, leaves at the drain, and the gate is a hand squeezing the hose from the outside through a thin wall. The hand never touches the water, and it takes no flow to hold the squeeze. The body is the ground the hose lies on. The analogy breaks down on one point: the hand here is an electric field, not a push.

### Words you need
- **Gate**: the control terminal, insulated by a thin oxide, so it draws almost no DC current.
- **Source**: where the charge carriers enter the channel. Voltages are measured from it.
- **Drain**: where the carriers leave the channel.
- **Body (bulk, substrate)**: the silicon under the channel. See [[q0091]].
- **Threshold voltage ($V_t$)**: the gate-to-source voltage where the channel starts to form.

### The simplest example
Take an NFET with its source on ground and an LED and resistor from the supply to its drain. Say its threshold is about 2 V. With the gate at 0 V, no channel forms and the LED is off. Raise the gate to 5 V, the channel forms, current flows from drain to source, and the LED lights. The gate itself carries almost no current.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers each terminal's role in more depth, the body connection, and how real packages are wired.
:::

::: testing
On the surface it's "name the pins." What the interviewer actually wants:

1. You name **four** terminals, not three: gate, drain, source **and body**. Mentioning the body unprompted shows you know what's under the hood of a 3-pin part.
2. You can say what each terminal *does*: the gate controls through an electric field (insulated, no DC current), the source supplies the carriers, the drain collects them, the body is the silicon the channel forms in.
3. You know voltages are **referenced to the source** ($V_{GS}$, $V_{DS}$), which is why the source placement matters.
4. Bonus depth: how the terminals look on a real package (tab = drain, Kelvin source) and the capacitances between them.
:::

::: why
Every MOSFET datasheet, gate-drive calculation and layout decision is phrased in terms of these terminals. Gate drive is $V_{GS}$, not "gate voltage," so a high-side NFET needs a gate voltage *above the supply*. The drain is usually the package tab, so it's the node you heatsink and also the noisy switch node you keep away from sensitive traces. Source inductance shared between the power path and gate loop slows switching. And in ICs the body terminal changes threshold voltage. Knowing the terminals precisely is the foundation for [[q0091]], [[q0101]] and every power-electronics question later on.
:::

::: concepts
- **MOSFET structure**: a gate electrode over a thin oxide on a doped body, with source and drain regions of the opposite doping. See [[q0096]].
- **Symbol and orientation**: how the four terminals appear on NFET/PFET symbols. See [[q0089]].
- **Carrier flow**: electrons (NMOS) or holes (PMOS) leave the source and arrive at the drain. See [[q0069]].
- **PN junctions**: source-body and drain-body are diodes that must stay reverse-biased. See [[q0063]].
- **Body tie**: why discrete parts connect body to source. See [[q0091]].
- **Gate as a capacitor**: the gate draws current only while charging or discharging. See [[q0022]].
:::

::: answer
"A MOSFET has four terminals: gate, drain, source and body, which is also called the bulk or substrate.

The **gate** is the control terminal. It sits on a thin oxide, so it's insulated from everything else, and at DC it draws basically no current. It acts like a capacitor: the voltage on it creates an electric field that forms or removes the channel.

The **source** is where the charge carriers come from: electrons for an NFET, holes for a PFET. Everything is referenced to it, so when we say the FET turns on, we mean $V_{GS}$ crossed the threshold.

The **drain** is where the carriers are collected. In a switch, the load usually hangs off the drain.

The **body** is the piece of silicon the channel forms in. On discrete MOSFETs it's tied to the source inside the package, which is why you only see three pins, and that tie is what creates the body diode. On an IC, the body is a separate node, usually ground for NFETs and the supply for PFETs, and its voltage relative to the source shifts the threshold.

On a power package like a TO-220 or D2PAK, the metal tab is typically the drain, since that's the side of the die that's bonded to the tab."
:::

::: technical
| Terminal | Physical region (NMOS) | Role | DC current |
|---|---|---|---|
| Gate (G) | Poly-Si or metal over $\text{SiO}_2$ (or high-k) | Field control of channel charge, $Q_{ch} \approx C_{ox}WL(V_{GS}-V_t)$ | ≈ 0 (pA–nA leakage). Transient $i_G = C\,dv/dt$ |
| Source (S) | N+ region | Supplies carriers. Reference for $V_{GS}$, $V_{DS}$ | $I_S = I_D$ (with $I_G\approx 0$) |
| Drain (D) | N+ region (N− drift + N+ substrate in power FETs) | Collects carriers. Carries load current | $I_D$ |
| Body (B) | P-type substrate or P-well | Hosts channel. Sets $V_t$ via $V_{SB}$ | ≈ 0 if junctions are reverse-biased |

**Reference rules.** Control and operating region are defined by $V_{GS}$ and $V_{DS}$ (NMOS) or $V_{SG}$, $V_{SD}$ (PMOS). Body effect: $V_t = V_{t0} + \gamma\left(\sqrt{2\phi_F + V_{SB}} - \sqrt{2\phi_F}\right)$.

**Junction constraint.** Source-body and drain-body PN junctions must stay reverse-biased (or at zero), so the NMOS body must be the most negative node it touches and the PMOS body the most positive.

**Symmetry.** In a lateral IC MOSFET, source and drain are physically identical. The source is defined as the terminal at lower potential (NMOS). In a vertical power MOSFET the source and body are shorted on the top surface and the drain is the back of the die, so the terminals are fixed and a body diode exists from S to D (NMOS).

**Inter-terminal capacitances** (datasheet form): $C_{iss} = C_{GS} + C_{GD}$, $C_{oss} = C_{DS} + C_{GD}$, $C_{rss} = C_{GD}$ (the Miller capacitance). Charging $C_{iss}$ = 2 nF by 10 V in 20 ns needs $i = C\,\Delta V/\Delta t = 1$ A peak, so "zero gate current" is a DC statement only.

**Packages.** Power packages (TO-220, D2PAK/TO-263, DPAK, many SMD power packages) usually use the tab/exposed pad as the drain. TO-247-4 and some SMD packages add a **Kelvin source** pin for the gate return.
:::

::: short
"Four terminals: gate, drain, source and body. The gate is insulated by the oxide, draws no DC current, and its voltage relative to the source creates or removes the channel. The source supplies the carriers and is the reference for $V_{GS}$ and $V_{DS}$. The drain collects them and usually connects to the load. The body is the silicon the channel lives in. Discrete parts tie it to the source internally, which is why they have three pins and a body diode."
:::

::: long
"A MOSFET has four terminals, even though most discrete parts only show three pins.

First the gate. Physically it's a conductor sitting on a very thin insulating oxide over the silicon. Because of that insulation, it draws essentially zero DC current: it's a capacitor plate. When I raise the gate voltage above the source by more than the threshold, the field pulls carriers to the surface and forms a channel. The flip side is that the gate does draw current during switching, because I'm charging and discharging that capacitance. Charging two nanofarads by ten volts in twenty nanoseconds is an amp of peak current, which is why we use gate drivers.

Second, the source. That's where the carriers originate, electrons for an NFET and holes for a PFET. All the device voltages are measured from it, $V_{GS}$ and $V_{DS}$. That's why an NFET on the high side is hard to drive: its source is up at the output voltage, so the gate has to go above the supply.

Third, the drain, which collects the carriers. In a low-side switch the load connects between the supply and the drain. On power packages like a D2PAK, the tab is usually the drain because the drain is the back of the die. That makes it the thermal path, and in a switching converter it's also the noisy switch node.

Fourth, the body or substrate, which is the doped silicon the channel forms in. In discrete MOSFETs, the body is shorted to the source inside the package. That prevents the body-source junction from forward-biasing and stops the threshold from shifting, but it also creates the body diode from source to drain. In ICs, the body is a separate node, typically ground for NFETs and the supply for PFETs, and the source-to-body voltage raises the threshold, which is the body effect.

So the short version: gate controls, source supplies, drain collects, body hosts the channel."
:::

::: terms
- **Gate (G)**: insulated control electrode. Its field sets the channel charge.
- **Source (S)**: the terminal carriers come from. The reference for $V_{GS}$ and $V_{DS}$.
- **Drain (D)**: the terminal carriers flow to. Usually tied to the load in a switch.
- **Body / bulk / substrate (B)**: the doped semiconductor the channel forms in.
- **Kelvin source**: a separate source pin used only as the gate-drive return, so load current doesn't flow through the gate loop.
- **Body effect**: threshold voltage increases as the source rises above the body ($V_{SB} > 0$ for NMOS).
- **$C_{iss}$, $C_{oss}$, $C_{rss}$**: datasheet input, output and reverse-transfer capacitances built from $C_{GS}$, $C_{GD}$, $C_{DS}$.
- **Tab / exposed pad**: the metal back of a power package, usually electrically the drain.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "gate, drain, source."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names the **body** as the fourth terminal and explains why discrete parts only have three pins (internal source-body short) and what that creates (the body diode).
- Describes each terminal's *job* in terms of carriers: the source supplies them, the drain collects them, the gate controls via field, not current.
- Says everything is referenced to the **source**, and uses that to explain a practical consequence (high-side NFET needs a gate above the rail).
- Clarifies that "no gate current" is DC only: switching needs amps of gate current, $i = C\,dv/dt$.
- Mentions a real-hardware detail: the tab is the drain, or Kelvin source pins.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Forgetting the body.** "Three terminals" is incomplete. The body is always there; discrete parts just tie it to the source.
- **Saying the gate "draws current to turn on."** That's BJT thinking. The gate is a capacitor: zero DC current, but large transient current while switching.
- **Referencing gate voltage to ground.** The device responds to $V_{GS}$. A 10 V gate on a high-side NFET whose source sits at 12 V is $V_{GS} = -2$ V: off.
- **Assuming drain and source are interchangeable on a discrete part.** True for a symmetric IC MOSFET, false for a power MOSFET with an internal body-source short and a body diode.
- **Assuming the tab is always the drain.** Usual for power MOSFETs, but always check the datasheet pinout. Some packages differ.
- **Thinking carriers flow drain → source in an NMOS.** Electrons flow source → drain. Conventional current flows drain → source, which is the opposite direction because electrons are negative.
:::

Each terminal maps to a region:

- **Gate**: a conductor (doped polysilicon in older processes, metal in modern high-k/metal-gate processes) on top of the oxide. The oxide is the dielectric of a capacitor whose other plate is the channel. Gate charge sets channel charge: per unit area, $Q_{ch} \approx -C_{ox}(V_{GS}-V_t)$ with $C_{ox} = \varepsilon_{ox}/t_{ox}$. For a 10 nm oxide, $C_{ox} = 3.9\cdot 8.854\times10^{-12}/10^{-8} \approx 3.45\ \text{mF/m}^2 = 3.45\ \text{fF/µm}^2$.
- **Source**: the N+ island the electrons enter the channel from. Because the barrier between source and channel is what the gate modulates, $V_{GS}$ is the controlling voltage.
- **Drain**: the N+ island that collects electrons. A positive $V_{DS}$ creates the lateral field that drives them across.
- **Body**: the P-type silicon. It forms two PN junctions, one with the source and one with the drain. Both must stay reverse-biased.

> [!NOTE]
> Source and drain in this lateral device are physically identical. The names are assigned by voltage: for NMOS, the source is the terminal at the **lower** potential, because that's where electrons come from. In a transmission gate or pass transistor, the source and drain swap as the signal swings.

## Voltages are referenced to the source

The gate doesn't care about its absolute voltage. It cares about the field across the oxide, which depends on the gate's voltage relative to the channel, and the channel's potential at its source end is the source voltage. So every MOSFET equation is written in $V_{GS}$ and $V_{DS}$:

$$I_D = \tfrac{1}{2}k_n'\tfrac{W}{L}(V_{GS}-V_t)^2 \quad\text{(saturation, } V_{DS}\ge V_{GS}-V_t\text{)}$$

Practical consequence: in a **low-side** switch the source is at ground, so a 5 V logic signal gives $V_{GS} = 5$ V. In a **high-side** NFET the source rises to nearly the supply when the device turns on. To keep $V_{GS} \approx 10$ V with a 12 V supply, the gate must reach about 22 V, which is why high-side NFETs need bootstrap or charge-pump drivers ([[q0097]], [[q0107]]).

## The body terminal and why you usually don't see it

Discrete MOSFETs are built **vertically**: source and body on the top surface, drain on the back of the die. The manufacturer shorts body to source with the source metal. That has three effects:

1. The body-source junction can never forward-bias.
2. $V_{SB} = 0$ always, so there's **no body effect** and $V_t$ is stable.
3. The body-drain junction is now a diode from source to drain: the **body diode** ([[q0101]]).

In an IC, the NMOS body is the shared P-substrate, normally tied to ground. An NMOS whose source sits above ground has $V_{SB} > 0$ and a higher threshold. That's why IC designers draw the fourth terminal. See [[q0091]] for the body-effect math.

Figure: Three capacitances connect the terminals. The gate sees $C_{GS}$ plus $C_{GD}$ (the Miller capacitance, which gets amplified as the drain swings). Datasheets report combinations: $C_{iss} = C_{GS}+C_{GD}$, $C_{oss} = C_{DS}+C_{GD}$, $C_{rss} = C_{GD}$.

The gate's DC current is picoamps to nanoamps (oxide leakage, plus any ESD Zener). But to switch, you must move charge onto the gate. A power MOSFET with $C_{iss} = 2$ nF driven from 0 to 10 V in 20 ns needs

$$i_G = C\frac{\Delta V}{\Delta t} = 2\,\text{nF}\cdot\frac{10\ \text{V}}{20\ \text{ns}} = 1\ \text{A}$$

of peak gate current. In practice you use datasheet gate charge $Q_g$ instead of $C_{iss}$ because $C_{GD}$ changes with voltage and the Miller plateau needs extra charge. Average gate-drive current is $Q_g f_{sw}$: 30 nC at 200 kHz is 6 mA.

## How the terminals show up on real packages

| Package | Typical pinout | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| SOT-23 (small-signal, e.g. 2N7002) | 3 pins: G, S, D | Pin numbering varies by vendor. Always check |
| TO-220 / D2PAK / DPAK | G, D, S leads, tab = D | Tab is the drain because the die's back side is the drain |
| Power SMD (e.g. 5×6 mm "SO-8 FL" style) | Several S pins, one G, exposed pad = D | Multiple source pins reduce resistance and inductance |
| TO-247-4 / some SMD | G, D, power S, **Kelvin S** | Separate source return for the gate driver |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> "Tab = drain" holds for the vast majority of power MOSFETs, and the G-D-S lead order is very common for TO-220. But pinouts are vendor- and package-specific (especially for SOT-23 and newer SMD packages). Treat these as typical, and always confirm against the datasheet's pin diagram.

## Why a Kelvin source exists: worked example

The source lead and bond wires have inductance $L_S$ of a few nH. If both the load current and the gate-drive return flow through it, the voltage $L_S\,di_D/dt$ subtracts from the gate drive during turn-on. With $L_S = 5$ nH and the drain current rising 20 A in 20 ns:

$$V_{L_S} = L_S\frac{di}{dt} = 5\ \text{nH}\cdot\frac{20\ \text{A}}{20\ \text{ns}} = 5\ \text{V}$$

That's 5 V stolen from a 10 V gate drive, exactly when you want the device to turn on fast. A Kelvin source pin gives the driver its own return directly from the die's source, so load current doesn't flow in the gate loop. It's a nice example of why "the source" in the equations means the *die's* source, not the PCB pad.

> If they ask "what are the terminals" and you've got 10 seconds, say "gate, drain, source, plus the body, which is tied to the source in discrete parts." That one clause about the body sets up their next question perfectly ([[q0091]]).

::: applications
- **Low-side switch for an LED strip or relay**: load between +12 V and drain, source to ground, MCU GPIO to gate through a resistor, plus a gate pulldown so the floating gate can't drift on.
- **High-side PFET load switch**: source at the battery, drain to the load, gate pulled low to turn on. All defined relative to the source.
- **Synchronous buck converter**: the high-side FET's source and the low-side FET's drain form the switch node. The drain tab of the low-side FET is a big, noisy copper area.
- **Kelvin-source SiC/GaN and silicon power MOSFETs** in TO-247-4 packages for fast switching in server PSUs and EV chargers.
- **IC current mirrors and source followers**: the body terminal (tied to substrate) makes the source follower's gain less than 1 because of the body effect.
:::

::: assumptions
- I described an **N-channel enhancement** device. For PMOS swap every doping type and voltage polarity: holes come from the source, which sits at the most positive potential, and the body is N-type.
- "Body tied to source" is assumed for **discrete** parts. Some small-signal MOSFETs and analog switches expose a separate body pin.
- Gate current "≈ 0" is a **DC** statement. During switching, gate current is set by $Q_g$ and the drive resistance.
- Package pinouts are **typical**, not universal.
- A valid alternative answer for a board-level role is "three terminals: gate, drain, source" as long as you then add that the body exists and is tied to the source.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the gate draw no current?
It's separated from the channel by an insulating oxide, so there's no conduction path at DC. It behaves like a capacitor plate. Leakage is pA to nA for a plain oxide, up to µA if there's an integrated ESD Zener. During switching, though, it draws $i = C\,dv/dt$, which can be amps.

### What's the difference between the source and the drain if the device is symmetric?
Only the voltage. For an NMOS, the source is whichever end is at lower potential, since electrons enter the channel there. For a PMOS it's the higher one. Discrete power MOSFETs aren't symmetric, because of the vertical structure and the internal body-source short, so their source and drain are fixed.

### Why is the drain usually the tab on a power package?
In a vertical power MOSFET, current flows from the top-side source through the die to the back-side drain, so the die's backside, which is soldered to the tab, is the drain. That conveniently puts the main heat-conducting surface on the drain. The catch is that in a switching circuit the tab is also the switch node, so it radiates noise.

### What happens if the body of an NMOS is accidentally connected above the source?
The body-source PN junction forward-biases at around 0.6–0.7 V, so current flows from body into source, the parasitic NPN (source-body-drain) can turn on, and in a CMOS IC this is the start of latch-up. Below that voltage, a positive $V_{BS}$ lowers the threshold (forward body bias), which is used deliberately in some low-voltage designs.

### What does a Kelvin source pin do?
It gives the gate driver a dedicated return straight from the die, so the high $di/dt$ load current doesn't flow through the gate loop's inductance. With 5 nH and 1 A/ns, that inductance would otherwise subtract 5 V from $V_{GS}$ and slow switching. See the worked example above.
:::
