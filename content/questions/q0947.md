::: basics
### In plain words
Supply voltage is the electrical push that powers a chip's transistors. A higher voltage makes transistors switch faster, so the chip can run at a higher clock speed. But it costs a lot of power, and that power turns into heat. Power grows with the square of the voltage, so a small cut saves a lot. Go too low, though, and the chip becomes too slow, and its memory can stop working.

### Picture it
Imagine filling a tall barrel. The higher the level you fill to, the more water the barrel holds. Each liter also has to be lifted higher. Both effects grow with the level, so the energy grows with the level squared. A higher water level also pushes water through the pipes faster, which is why a higher voltage gives a faster chip. But the barrel only models the cost side. It says nothing about the wear that high voltage causes over the years.

### Words you need
- **Supply voltage ($V$)**: the electrical push that powers the transistors.
- **Threshold voltage ($V_t$)**: the voltage a transistor needs before it turns on.
- **Dynamic power**: switching power, $\alpha C V^2 f$.
- **Leakage**: current that flows through transistors that are supposed to be off.
- **DVFS**: changing voltage and frequency together to match the load.
- **Guardband**: extra voltage added on top of the minimum, to cover droop, aging and variation.

### The simplest example
A chip runs at 0.9 V and 2 GHz and uses 32.4 W. Cut the voltage 20%, to 0.72 V. At the same frequency, power falls to $0.8^2=0.64$ of the original, a 36% saving. But with a threshold of 0.35 V, the transistors are now slower, and the top clock drops to about 1.49 GHz. At that clock, power is about 15.5 W, roughly half. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the speed formula, how leakage changes, and the voltage window where a chip works reliably.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand voltage as the **most powerful and most dangerous knob** in digital design, and can explain all three effects with the right physics. The interviewer wants:

1. Power: dynamic power goes with $V^2$, leakage grows steeply with $V$ (and temperature).
2. Performance: higher $V$ gives more drive current, so shorter delay and higher $f_{max}$, with diminishing returns far above $V_t$ and a steep loss near $V_t$.
3. Thermals: more power is more heat; leakage-temperature feedback.
4. The trade-off made explicit: energy per operation $\propto V^2$ versus speed, the basis of DVFS.
5. Limits: $V_{min}$ (SRAM stability, noise margin, process variation) and $V_{max}$ (oxide reliability, aging, electromigration).
:::

::: why
Supply voltage is the first thing a low-power architect reaches for, because of the square law: a 20% voltage cut is a 36% dynamic power cut. It is also what DVFS governors, boost modes, overclockers and chip qualification engineers manipulate every day, and what voltage droop and IR drop silently change on real boards. An engineer who can say how $V$ moves power, speed and heat, and where it stops working, can reason about almost every power-performance trade-off on the list. It pairs directly with the clock-frequency question ([[q0945]]), and leads into multiple supply rails ([[q0948]]) and PVT ([[q0949]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Dynamic power**: $P_{dyn} = \alpha C V^2 f$; energy per operation $\alpha C V^2$. See [[q0945]].
- **Alpha-power delay law**: gate delay $\propto V/(V-V_t)^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha \approx 1.3$ for short-channel devices.
- **Leakage**: subthreshold current rises exponentially as $V_t$ falls or $V_{DS}$ rises (DIBL); gate-oxide leakage rises steeply with $V$.
- **Overdrive**: $V_{GS}-V_t$ sets drive current. See [[q0949]].
- **$V_{min}$ and $V_{max}$**: the safe voltage window of a design.
- **Guardband**: voltage margin added for droop, aging and variation.
:::

::: answer
"Supply voltage is the biggest lever on all three. For power, dynamic power is proportional to V squared, so dropping the voltage 20% cuts dynamic power by 36%. Leakage depends strongly on voltage too, since higher voltage raises subthreshold current through drain-induced barrier lowering and increases gate leakage, so leakage drops faster than linearly as I lower V.

For performance, the transistor's drive current depends on the overdrive, V minus the threshold voltage, so higher voltage means faster switching and a higher maximum clock frequency. Delay goes roughly like V over (V minus Vt) to a power around 1.3, so the gains flatten at high voltage, and near the threshold the circuit slows down dramatically. Example: with a 0.35 V threshold, dropping from 0.9 to 0.72 V reduces the achievable frequency by about 25%, but energy per operation drops 36%, and dynamic power drops about 52% once the clock comes down to what 0.72 V supports.

Thermals follow from power: less power, less heat, junction temperature lower, and leakage drops further because of its temperature dependence. So reducing voltage is the most effective way to reduce power and heat, as long as timing still closes.

The limits are on both sides: too low and SRAM becomes unstable and logic can't meet timing, too high and I risk oxide breakdown, aging like NBTI, electromigration, and a thermal blow-up. That's why chips ship with voltage-frequency tables and guardbands."
:::

::: technical
**Dynamic power.** $P_{dyn} = \alpha C V_{DD}^2 f$. At fixed frequency, $P_{dyn}\propto V^2$. Energy per operation $E = \alpha C V^2$ is independent of frequency, so lowering $V$ always reduces energy per operation if the circuit still works.

**Delay (alpha-power law).** Saturation drive current $I_D \approx k\,(V_{GS}-V_t)^{\alpha}$, and the gate delay is $t_d \approx \dfrac{C_L V_{DD}}{I_D} \propto \dfrac{V_{DD}}{(V_{DD}-V_t)^{\alpha}}$, $\alpha \approx 1.3$ in velocity-saturated short-channel devices (approaching 2 in long-channel, square-law devices). Hence

$$f_{max}(V) \propto \frac{(V-V_t)^{\alpha}}{V}$$

Sensitivity: $\dfrac{d\ln f}{d\ln V} = \dfrac{\alpha V}{V - V_t} - 1$. At $V = 0.9$ V, $V_t=0.35$ V, $\alpha=1.3$: $\frac{1.3\times0.9}{0.55} - 1 = 1.13$, so a 1% voltage change is about a 1.1% frequency change near this point, but a 2% dynamic-power change at fixed frequency (about 3% if the frequency follows). At $V=0.6$ V the sensitivity is $\frac{1.3\times0.6}{0.25}-1 = 2.1$, so speed is much more voltage-sensitive near threshold.

**Leakage.** Subthreshold leakage $I_{sub}\propto \exp\!\big(\frac{V_{GS}-V_t + \eta V_{DS}}{n\,kT/q}\big)$ where $\eta$ is the DIBL coefficient: higher $V_{DS}=V_{DD}$ lowers the effective barrier and increases leakage exponentially. Gate tunneling leakage rises even faster with the oxide field (older nodes; high-k metal gate reduces it). So $P_{leak} = V\,I_{leak}(V,T)$ drops faster than $V^2$ when scaling down.

**Thermals.** $T_j = T_a + P_{tot}\theta_{JA}$; lower $V$ lowers $P_{dyn}$ and $P_{leak}$, lowering $T_j$, which reduces leakage further (the positive feedback loop working in the beneficial direction).

**Worked example** ($V_t=0.35$ V, $\alpha=1.3$): $0.9\to0.72$ V gives $f_{max}$ ratio 0.747 (2 GHz to 1.49 GHz), dynamic power at *fixed* $f$ ratio $(0.72/0.9)^2=0.64$, at the *new* $f$ ratio $0.64\times0.747=0.478$, energy per operation ratio 0.64.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The exponent $\alpha$ and $V_t$ differ per process, device flavor (HVT/SVT/LVT) and temperature. The values here are textbook-style placeholders for the shape of the curve; a real DVFS table is measured and fused per part.
:::

::: short
"Dynamic power goes as V squared, so a 20% voltage cut saves 36% of dynamic power, and leakage falls even faster. Performance also depends on voltage: drive current scales with V minus Vt, so delay shrinks and maximum frequency rises with voltage, with diminishing returns at high V and a steep slowdown near threshold. Less power means less heat. So voltage is the most effective power knob, limited below by SRAM and timing and above by reliability, which is why DVFS pairs voltage with frequency."
:::

::: long
"Voltage hits all three outputs, so let me take them in order.

Power. Switching power is $\alpha C V^2 f$, so it's quadratic in voltage. Taking 1.0 V down to 0.8 V cuts dynamic power by 36% at the same frequency. Energy per operation is $\alpha C V^2$, so energy per task falls by the same 36%. Leakage is also voltage dependent: higher drain voltage lowers the barrier in short-channel devices, a DIBL effect, and gate tunneling leakage rises quickly with the oxide field. So leakage drops faster than linearly with voltage too.

Performance. A transistor's drive current depends on the overdrive, $V_{GS}$ minus $V_t$. Higher supply gives more current, charges the load faster, and gate delay goes roughly as $V/(V-V_t)^{1.3}$. So $f_{max}$ rises with voltage, but the gains flatten at high voltage and collapse near threshold. With a 0.35 V threshold, dropping from 0.9 V to 0.72 V costs about 25% in max frequency. Which means I can't have a free lunch: power falls quadratically but speed only falls roughly linearly, so energy per operation improves.

Thermals. Power becomes heat, so lowering voltage lowers junction temperature, which further reduces leakage. Conversely a high-voltage boost mode is limited by thermals and is only sustainable for short periods.

The limits matter. On the low side, SRAM cells need a minimum voltage for stable read and write, logic with process variation fails timing near threshold, and noise margins shrink. On the high side, oxides and interconnects wear out faster, through TDDB, NBTI and electromigration, and the power wall hits. So every chip has a voltage-frequency table with guardband for supply droop and aging, and DVFS moves along it."
:::

::: terms
- **Overdrive voltage**: $V_{GS} - V_t$, which sets drive current.
- **Alpha-power law**: delay $\propto V/(V-V_t)^\alpha$.
- **DIBL**: drain-induced barrier lowering; higher $V_{DS}$ reduces effective $V_t$ and raises leakage.
- **$V_{min}$**: the minimum voltage at which the circuit (often the SRAM) works reliably at a given frequency.
- **Guardband**: voltage margin for droop, aging and variation.
- **DVFS**: dynamic voltage and frequency scaling.
- **IR drop / droop**: voltage lost between regulator and transistor.
- **NBTI / TDDB / EM**: aging mechanisms that accelerate with voltage and temperature.
- **Near-threshold computing**: operating close to $V_t$ for minimum energy at a large speed penalty.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "higher voltage is faster but uses more power and generates more heat."

**An exceptional answer**:
- States $P \propto V^2$ and also that **leakage rises faster than linearly** with $V$ (DIBL, gate leakage).
- Writes the **alpha-power delay law** and works an example: the same voltage drop buys 36% less dynamic power while costing less in frequency.
- Notes diminishing returns at high $V$ and the steep slowdown near $V_t$.
- Names **$V_{min}$ (SRAM, variation) and $V_{max}$ (TDDB, NBTI, EM)** as the limits of the window, plus guardband for droop.
- Closes the loop with temperature: lower voltage gives lower $T_j$, lowering leakage further, and ties it to DVFS and boost.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Power is linear in voltage."** Dynamic power is quadratic; dynamic current is roughly linear in $V$ (for fixed $f$), but power is $V\times I$.
- **"Lower voltage always saves energy."** Near threshold, delay explodes so leakage energy per operation rises; there is a minimum-energy voltage.
- **Ignoring leakage when lowering $V$.** Leakage does drop with $V$, but if $V_t$ is lowered to keep speed (a common design choice), leakage can increase exponentially.
- **Assuming performance is linear in voltage.** The relation is sub-linear at high $V$ (velocity saturation) and super-linear near threshold.
- **Forgetting the board.** Regulator accuracy, IR drop and transient droop mean the die sees less than the setpoint; margin must cover it.
- **Thinking higher voltage is safe if you cool the chip.** Reliability mechanisms (TDDB, HCI, EM) depend on voltage as well as temperature.
:::

::: deep
## Power: why the square law

Charge stored on a node capacitance $C$ at $V$ is $Q = CV$; energy drawn from the supply to charge it is $QV = CV^2$ (half stored, half burned in the PMOS). Each full cycle of an active node therefore costs $CV^2$ regardless of how fast it happens, so

$$E_{op} = \alpha C V^2, \qquad P_{dyn} = E_{op} f.$$

The square comes from two sources: the charge moved is proportional to $V$, and each unit of charge is moved through $V$ volts. That is why voltage is far more effective than frequency for power: scaling frequency by $k$ scales power by $k$; scaling voltage by $k$ scales it by $k^2$.

## Performance: why $V$ helps speed

A gate delay is the time to move charge $C_L V_{DD}$ with current $I_D$:

$$t_d \approx \frac{C_L V_{DD}}{I_D}, \qquad I_D \propto (V_{DD}-V_t)^\alpha$$

In an old long-channel device $\alpha=2$ (square-law); in modern short-channel devices carriers hit velocity saturation, so $\alpha\approx1.1$ to $1.5$. Plugging in:

$$f_{max}(V)\propto \frac{(V-V_t)^{\alpha}}{V}$$

## Leakage: the extra effect

Subthreshold leakage of an off transistor:

$$I_{sub} \propto \exp\!\left(\frac{V_{GS}-V_t+\eta V_{DS}}{n\,V_T}\right),\quad V_T = kT/q \approx 26\text{ mV at 300 K}$$

With $V_{GS}=0$ and $V_{DS}=V_{DD}$, the DIBL term $\eta V_{DD}$ means leakage grows exponentially with supply voltage. A DIBL coefficient of 0.1 and $n=1.4$: a 0.2 V rise in $V_{DD}$ lowers the barrier by 20 mV, i.e. $\exp(20/(1.4\times26)) = 1.7\times$ leakage; power adds another 1.2x factor from $V\cdot I$. Gate leakage and GIDL add to this. Net: leakage power typically falls faster than $V^2$ when voltage is reduced, but exponential temperature dependence ([[q0949]]) can still dominate in hot silicon.

**Trade-off with $V_t$.** To get speed at low $V$, designers lower $V_t$; but subthreshold swing of about 80 to 100 mV/decade means each 60 mV $V_t$ reduction raises leakage by roughly 4 to 6x ($10^{60/100}$ to $10^{60/80}$). That is why chips use multiple threshold flavors (HVT, SVT, LVT): LVT only on critical paths.

## Thermals

Lower voltage reduces both $P_{dyn}$ and $P_{leak}$, so $T_j = T_a + P\theta_{JA}$ drops, which further reduces leakage. In boost mode the loop goes the other way. This coupling is why firmware controls voltage and frequency together: e.g. throttling to a lower V-f point cuts heat much faster than lowering frequency alone ([[q0953]]).

## The voltage window

**Lower limit $V_{min}$.** Set by:
- *SRAM stability*: read static noise margin and write margin shrink with $V$ and with local $V_t$ mismatch, so memories usually fail first; many designs put SRAM on a separate higher rail ([[q0948]]).
- *Timing under variation*: slow-corner silicon and supply droop at low $V$ make critical paths miss.
- *Noise margins* and level-shifting limits.

**Upper limit $V_{max}$.** Set by reliability: time-dependent dielectric breakdown (TDDB) of the gate oxide, bias-temperature instability (NBTI/PBTI) that slowly raises $V_t$, hot-carrier injection, and electromigration (current density), all accelerating with voltage and temperature. Overvoltage "boost" is therefore limited in duration.

**Guardband.** A shipped chip's voltage setpoint is the minimum for the part plus margin for IR drop, transient droop and aging. Adaptive voltage scaling and on-die droop detectors trim this margin.

## Worked example: DVFS step

Assume $\alpha C=20$ nF. Point A: 0.9 V at 2 GHz: $P = 20\text{ nF}\times0.81\times2\text{ GHz}=32.4$ W. Point B: 0.72 V, $f_{max}$ ratio 0.747, so $f=1.49$ GHz: $P = 20\text{ nF}\times0.5184\times1.493\text{ GHz} = 15.5$ W. Power dropped 52% for a 25% speed loss; energy per operation fell 36%. A workload needing 2 GHz would miss; a workload needing 1.4 GHz would use B and save half the power.

## Near-threshold and subthreshold

Pushing $V$ close to $V_t$ minimizes energy per operation (dynamic energy keeps falling as $V^2$) but delay grows exponentially and leakage energy per operation rises because each operation takes longer. There is a **minimum-energy point**, typically near or just above $V_t$, used for ultra-low-power sensor nodes. Variation becomes severe, so such designs need special circuits.
:::

::: applications
- **Phone and laptop SoCs**: voltage-frequency tables per core cluster, adjusted dozens of times per second by DVFS.
- **Server CPUs**: voltage regulators with fast response (VRMs) and droop detection so the chip can run a lower guardband.
- **Overclocking and boost**: raising $V$ to get higher $f_{max}$ at a steep power cost and a reliability risk.
- **Ultra-low-power sensors and wearables**: near-threshold or low-voltage operation for energy efficiency.
- **Memory design**: SRAM $V_{min}$ and assist circuits; DRAM voltage steps between DDR generations (1.5 V DDR3, 1.2 V DDR4, 1.1 V DDR5) reduce I/O energy.
- **Characterization labs**: shmoo plots of frequency vs voltage to find a part's operating window.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CMOS digital logic** with the alpha-power delay model; $\alpha$ and $V_t$ are placeholders (0.35 V, 1.3).
- **Fixed temperature** in the delay model. In reality delay depends on temperature, and at low $V$ and advanced nodes the slowest case can be at cold temperature (temperature inversion, see [[q0949]]).
- **Dynamic power model** neglects short-circuit power and treats $C$ and $\alpha$ as constants.
- **Leakage numbers** are illustrative; the sensitivity depends strongly on node, $V_t$ flavor and gate-oxide technology.
- If the interviewer is asking about **analog or I/O circuits**, voltage also sets headroom, gain and signal swing rather than just speed and power.
:::

::: followups
### Why does lowering the voltage reduce power more than lowering the frequency?
Power is quadratic in $V$ and linear in $f$, and lowering $V$ also reduces leakage. Lowering $f$ alone cuts power proportionally but leaves the energy per operation unchanged. Lowering $V$ cuts energy per operation by $V^2$. That is why the usual strategy is to scale both: the lowest voltage that still meets timing at the target frequency.

### Why can't I lower the voltage indefinitely?
Three limits: at low overdrive the gate delay grows steeply and then exponentially in subthreshold, so you can't meet the target frequency; SRAM cells lose read/write noise margin ($V_{min}$) and fail first; and variation makes some transistors much slower than others. There's also a minimum-energy point below which leakage energy per operation increases.

### What does raising the voltage do to chip reliability?
It accelerates wear-out mechanisms: gate-oxide breakdown (TDDB), bias temperature instability (threshold drift), hot-carrier damage and electromigration. They depend on both voltage and temperature, so a chip run at high $V$ and high $T$ ages much faster. Vendors specify a maximum voltage and may limit the time spent in boost.

### Does leakage go up or down when I lower the supply voltage?
Down, thanks to DIBL and lower gate-oxide field, and also because $P=VI$. However, if you lowered $V_t$ to keep speed, or the temperature rises, leakage can go up. And at the minimum-energy point, a longer runtime means more leakage energy per task.

### Scenario: you have a thermal-limited device and a performance target 10% below the current peak. What do you do?
Move to the lowest V-f point that meets the 10% lower target. With $f$ down 10%, the alpha-power model ($V_t$ = 0.35 V, $\alpha$ = 1.3) lets $V$ drop by about 8.5% (0.9 V to roughly 0.82 V), so dynamic power falls by roughly 25% (about $0.9\times0.915^2 \approx 0.75$ of the original). Temperature falls too, which trims leakage further. Compare with lowering only the clock, which would save just 10%.
:::

"Supply voltage" can be asked about a **processor core** (the main answer here), or about an **I/O interface** (where voltage affects signal swing, speed and noise) or **analog circuits** (headroom). In the power/performance/thermals cluster the intended meaning is the digital core supply and DVFS. If the follow-up is "why multiple supply voltages?" see [[q0948]].
