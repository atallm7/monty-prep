::: testing
Whether you can draw SPI correctly and **without the classic naming trap**. The interviewer wants to see:

1. Four signals connected function to function: SCLK to SCLK, MOSI (master out) to the slave's data **in**, MISO (master in) from the slave's data **out**, and CS from a master GPIO.
2. Ground and supply, with a decoupling capacitor at each chip.
3. The small details that make it work: CS pull-up so the slave is deselected at boot, optional series resistors for fast clocks, shared lines for multiple slaves with one CS each.
4. Unlike I2C, no pull-ups on the data lines, because the drivers are push-pull.
:::

::: why
SPI wiring looks trivial, yet miswired MOSI/MISO (or SDI/SDO labels read the wrong way) is probably the most common day-one SPI failure. Drawing it correctly, and mentioning CS idle state, damping and decoupling, shows practical experience. It's the SPI twin of [[q0754]] for I2C.
:::

::: concepts
- **Signal roles**: SCLK and CS and MOSI are driven by the master; MISO is driven by the selected slave. See [[q0764]].
- **Push-pull outputs**: no pull-ups on the data lines. See [[q0761]].
- **Chip select per slave**: separate CS lines, shared data lines. See [[q0765]].
- **Common ground and decoupling**: see [[q0032]] and [[q0001]].
- **Series termination**: a 22 to 47 ohm resistor at the source damps ringing on fast edges. See [[q0716]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd draw the master on the left and the slave on the right with four wires between them. SCLK goes from the master's clock pin to the slave's clock pin. MOSI, master out slave in, goes from the master's output to the slave's data input. MISO goes the other way, from the slave's data output back to the master's input. And chip select is a GPIO from the master to the slave's CS pin, active low. So it's all function to function, and note that a slave's pin might be labelled SDI or SDO, which means data in and data out from the slave's point of view, so the master's output goes to the slave's SDI.

Then I add the real-world bits. Both chips share a ground and each gets its own supply with a 100 nF decoupling cap. There are no pull-ups on the data lines, because SPI outputs are push-pull, but I'd add a pull-up on CS so the slave stays deselected while the MCU is in reset or booting. For fast clocks I might put a 33 ohm series resistor near the source on SCLK and MOSI to damp ringing.

For more slaves, SCLK, MOSI and MISO are shared and each slave gets its own CS from another GPIO."
:::

::: technical
**Connections (single slave).**

| Master pin | Wire | Slave pin | Driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| SCLK / SCK | SCLK | SCLK / SCK | master |
| MOSI (SDO, COPI) | MOSI | SDI / MOSI / COPI | master |
| MISO (SDI, CIPO) | MISO | SDO / MISO / CIPO | slave (tri-state when deselected) |
| GPIO (or hardware CS) | CS | CS / SS (active low) | master |
| GND | GND | GND | n/a |

Naming rule: connect by function. The master's data **output** goes to the slave's data **input**; if the slave labels pins SDI/SDO, MOSI goes to SDI and MISO comes from SDO.

**Supplies.** Each chip has its own VDD pin with a 100 nF ceramic capacitor close to it; grounds common. If the slave runs at a different voltage, add a level translator ([[q0769]]).

**Pull-ups and defaults.** No pull-ups on SCLK/MOSI/MISO in normal use. Add a pull-up (about 10 kΩ) on each CS to hold the slave deselected during MCU reset, and optionally a pull-down or pull-up on SCLK to match the idle level (CPOL). On a shared MISO with several slaves, a weak pull-up/down keeps the line defined when nobody drives it.

**Signal integrity.** For clocks of tens of MHz: keep traces short, route SCLK with a ground reference, keep MISO stubs short, and add 22 to 47 Ω series resistors at the *source* of SCLK and MOSI (and at the slave for MISO) if edges ring. Series resistance with pin capacitance forms an RC: 33 Ω with 10 pF is 0.33 ns, negligible against a 10 ns half-period at 50 MHz.

**Multiple slaves.** Share SCLK, MOSI, MISO; one CS per slave ([[q0765]]). Daisy-chain wiring is different ([[q0768]]).
:::

::: long
"I'd draw the master on the left and the slave on the right and connect four wires. SCLK from the master clock output to the slave clock input. MOSI, which stands for master out, slave in, from the master's data output to the slave's data input. MISO from the slave's data output back to the master's data input. And chip select, usually active low, from a master GPIO to the slave's CS pin.

The one trap is names. Datasheets for slaves often call their pins SDI and SDO, meaning data in and data out from the slave's point of view. So the master's MOSI goes to the slave's SDI, and the slave's SDO goes to the master's MISO. I always wire by function: outputs to inputs, never output to output.

Then I add what makes the board work. A common ground between the chips and a separate 100 nF decoupling capacitor at each chip's supply pin. Unlike I2C, I don't add pull-ups on the data lines, because SPI uses push-pull drivers. But I do add a pull-up, around 10 kΩ, on chip select. When the MCU is in reset or booting, its GPIO is floating, and without the pull-up the slave could see noise as a command. If the clock is fast, say tens of MHz, I put a small series resistor, 22 to 47 ohms, close to the driver on SCLK and MOSI to tame ringing, and keep the traces short with a ground plane under them.

For more slaves, the three shared lines go to every slave and each slave gets its own CS from another GPIO; MISO has to be tri-stated by whichever slaves aren't selected. If the slave is at a different voltage from the master, I put a push-pull level translator in the path, with the right direction for each line, since MISO runs the opposite way to the others."
:::

::: terms
- **SCLK / SCK**: clock from master.
- **MOSI / COPI / SDO (master)**: master data out.
- **MISO / CIPO / SDI (master)**: master data in.
- **SDI / SDO (slave)**: slave's data in and out, from its own viewpoint.
- **CS / SS / CSn**: active-low chip select.
- **Series (source) termination**: resistor at the driver to damp reflections.
- **Decoupling capacitor**: local supply bypass, about 100 nF. See [[q0032]].
- **Level translator**: push-pull voltage-domain interface. See [[q0877]].
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists four names and says "connect them".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Wires by function and explicitly resolves the SDI/SDO naming confusion.
- Notes that there are no data-line pull-ups (push-pull) but CS needs one for the reset/boot state.
- Includes ground, decoupling and supply compatibility without being asked.
- Mentions series damping and short stubs for fast clocks, with a number.
- Extends to multiple slaves: shared lines, per-slave CS, MISO tri-state; and to daisy chain as a different wiring.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Connecting MOSI to the slave's SDO** (output to output). Wire master out to slave in.
- **Crossing like UART TX/RX by habit.** SPI names are from the master's viewpoint, so MOSI-to-MOSI is straight; only if the slave's labels are SDI/SDO do you read them as in/out.
- **Adding I2C-style pull-ups** to MOSI/MISO/SCLK. Not needed, and they only waste power.
- **Leaving CS floating at reset**, so the slave sees spurious clocks.
- **No common ground** between boards connected by only signal wires.
- **Long unterminated MISO stubs** to several slaves at high clock rates.
:::

## Wire by function, not by label

Master signals are named from the master's viewpoint. Slave pin labels vary:

| Master | Slave labels you might see |
|---|---|
| MOSI / SDO / COPI (output) | MOSI, SDI, DIN, SI, COPI |
| MISO / SDI / CIPO (input) | MISO, SDO, DOUT, SO, CIPO |
| SCLK | SCK, CLK, SCLK |
| CS | CS, SS, CE, NSS (active low) |

Rule: the master's output always goes to a slave's input pin (SDI, DIN, SI), and the master's input comes from a slave's output pin (SDO, DOUT, SO). When the slave datasheet uses MOSI/MISO the labels are from the *master's* point of view; when it uses SDI/SDO they're from the slave's. If in doubt, find the pin that is described as "output" in the pin table.

## No pull-ups on data lines, but yes on CS

SPI outputs are push-pull ([[q0761]]), so they drive both levels; resistors to $V_{DD}$ would only waste current when low. The exception is CS. When the MCU resets, its GPIOs float until firmware configures them. A floating CS can fall into the slave's active range and let SCLK noise be interpreted as commands (or worse, a flash write/erase sequence). A 10 kΩ pull-up costs $3.3/10\text{ kΩ}=0.33$ mA only while CS is low, and keeps every slave deselected by default. SCLK similarly benefits from a weak resistor to its idle level on boards where the master's pin is high-Z at boot.

## Series damping for fast clocks

A fast GPIO driving a trace sees the trace as a transmission line. If the driver impedance is lower than the line impedance, the edge reflects and rings. A series resistor at the source brings driver plus resistor close to $Z_0$:

$$R_{series}\approx Z_0-R_{driver}$$

For a 50 Ω trace and a 20 Ω driver, about 30 Ω, which is why 22 to 47 Ω values are common. See [[q0716]] for the series versus parallel trade. The cost is a slightly slower edge: with 10 pF of load a 33 Ω resistor adds an RC of 0.33 ns, which is irrelevant at 50 MHz (half period 10 ns) but worth counting on a 133 MHz link. MISO comes from the slave, so a damping resistor belongs at the slave's SDO if the slave edges ring.

## Worked example: ringing and the series resistor

Take a 3.3 V GPIO with a 20 Ω output impedance driving 10 cm of 50 Ω trace into a slave pin (about 5 pF). The trace delay is roughly $0.1\text{ m}\times6.7\text{ ns/m}=0.67$ ns one way, so the round trip is about 1.3 ns. If the driver's edge is 1 ns, the edge is faster than the round trip and the line must be treated as a transmission line. With no resistor the source reflection coefficient is $(20-50)/(20+50)=-0.43$ and the load is nearly open (+1), so the signal overshoots, rings back, and settles over several round trips (a few ns). With a 33 Ω resistor the total source impedance is 53 Ω, matching the line: the launched step is half the swing, doubles at the near-open load to the full swing, and the returning reflection is absorbed at the matched source. At a 10 MHz clock the half-period is 50 ns and none of this matters; at 50 MHz (10 ns half-period) the ringing must have died before the sampling edge.

## Supply and ground

- **Common ground:** SPI is single-ended, so the receiver measures the signal relative to its own ground. Boards connected by a ribbon cable need ground wires interleaved with the signals (a common practice is a ground between every one or two signals).
- **Decoupling:** a 100 nF ceramic at each VDD pin ([[q0032]]); bulk capacitance nearby for devices with bursty current such as flash during programming.
- **Voltage compatibility:** if master and slave have different supplies, put a push-pull translator in line, with three lines going one way and MISO the other ([[q0769]]). A single-FET shifter ([[q0879]]) is designed for open-drain buses and is a poor fit for push-pull SPI at speed.

## Multi-slave wiring (short version)

SCLK, MOSI, MISO shared; one CS per slave (3+N lines). MISO needs every unselected slave to go high-Z; add a weak pull-up/pull-down on MISO so it doesn't float if all are deselected. Keep the shared lines as a bus with short stubs, not a star. See [[q0765]] for the options and [[q0768]] for daisy-chain wiring where MOSI → slave 1 → slave 2 → MISO.

## Pre-flight checklist

1. Master output pins go to slave *input* pins and vice versa.
2. One common ground; decoupling at each device.
3. CS (every one) has a pull-up and idles high.
4. Mode and clock agree with the slave datasheet ([[q0766]]).
5. Voltage levels compatible, or a translator is fitted ([[q0769]]).
6. Fast clock: series resistors, short stubs, ground reference under the traces ([[q0770]]).

::: applications
- **MCU to SPI flash** on a development board: four wires, CS pull-up, 33 Ω series resistor on SCLK.
- **Sensor breakout boards** (IMUs, pressure sensors) wired to a dev board with jumper wires: common ground and short leads matter.
- **TFT displays**: SCLK and MOSI at tens of MHz, plus separate D/C and reset lines.
- **SD card sockets** in SPI mode: pull-ups on MISO/CS as the card spec recommends.
- **Daughter-board connectors**: SCLK/MOSI/MISO/CS with ground pins between signals.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Single master, one slave**, 3.3 V push-pull, mode and speed per the slave datasheet.
- **Short on-board traces** where a series resistor is optional; long cables need more care.
- **Active-low CS.** A few devices use active-high chip enable; follow the datasheet.
- **Voltage-compatible parts.** Otherwise a translator is required.

If the question comes with a particular slave in mind (a flash, say), the same diagram applies with additional WP#/HOLD# pins tied high.
:::

::: followups
### What if the slave's pins are labelled SDI and SDO?
Those labels are from the slave's viewpoint: SDI is its data input, so it connects to the master's MOSI; SDO is its data output, so it connects to the master's MISO. Always connect outputs to inputs.

### Why do you pull CS up, but not the other lines?
CS must be defined whenever the master's GPIO is high-Z (reset, boot), or the slave could see a random select. SCLK/MOSI/MISO are push-pull driven by whoever owns them, and the slave ignores them while CS is high, so they need no pull-ups. A weak pull on MISO is only useful if all slaves can be high-Z.

### Do I need to add termination to SPI?
Not at a few MHz on a short board. At tens of MHz with longer traces, a 22 to 47 Ω series resistor at the source is the usual cheap fix. Parallel or Thevenin termination wastes power and is rarely used on SPI.

### How do you wire two slaves?
Share SCLK, MOSI and MISO between both, and give each slave its own CS from a GPIO. Make sure each slave tri-states MISO when deselected, and that CS lines idle high.

### The slave doesn't respond. What do you check on the wiring first?
That MOSI isn't tied to SDO (swapped data lines), that ground is common, that the slave's supply is up, and that CS actually goes low (scope it). Then mode and clock.
:::
