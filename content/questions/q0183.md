::: testing
Whether you give the **qualified yes** rather than a reflex formula. The question "given $D$ and $V_{in}$, can you find $V_{out}$?" has a one-line answer ($V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$) and three traps behind it. The interviewer is checking that you:

1. State the answer *and its assumptions*: ideal switches, continuous conduction, steady state.
2. Know what to add when they are violated: conduction drops (a few percent), and discontinuous mode (a big change, and it needs the load, $L$ and $f_{sw}$).
3. Understand that in a **closed-loop** regulator $D$ is not an independent input: $V_{out}$ is set by the reference and divider, and $D$ is the result ([[q0178]]).
:::

::: why
Reading a duty cycle off a scope and predicting the output (or the reverse, using $V_{out}$ to infer $D$) is one of the first diagnostics you do on a switching converter. If the prediction is badly off, that tells you something: a lossy path, DCM at light load, or a loop that isn't doing what you think. Interviewers also use this question to see whether a candidate blindly applies $D\,V_{in}$ at no load, where an open-loop buck's output climbs toward $V_{in}$. The calculation shows up everywhere from sizing a startup ramp to debugging a rail that is 20% high at light load.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ideal buck ratio**: $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$ in CCM ([[q0174]], [[q0178]]).
- **Conduction drops**: $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}-I[\,D R_{hs}+(1-D)R_{ls}+R_{DCR}]$ for a synchronous buck ([[q0180]]).
- **Diode drop**: $V_f$ enters in the off-state for an asynchronous buck.
- **DCM relation**: depends on $K=2L/(RT)$; the output exceeds $D\,V_{in}$ ([[q0182]], [[q0229]]).
- **Open vs closed loop**: with feedback, the loop chooses $D$ to hit $V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$ ([[q0223]]).
- **Power-stage gain**: $dV_{out}/dD=V_{in}$ (CCM), so each 1% of duty is $0.01V_{in}$ of output.
:::

::: answer
"Yes, with a condition. If the converter is in continuous conduction, the output is just the duty cycle times the input: $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$. So 27.5 percent of 12 V is 3.3 V. That follows from the inductor having zero average voltage in steady state.

The caveats matter, though. With real switches there are voltage drops, so the output is a bit lower: for a synchronous buck $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}-I[D R_{hs}+(1-D)R_{ls}+R_{DCR}]$, which for 2 amps and a few tens of milliohms takes off about 55 mV. For a diode buck, it's the diode drop in the off-time, so more like 0.4 V lower.

The bigger caveat is light load. If the inductor current reaches zero each cycle, the converter is in discontinuous mode, and for the same duty cycle the output is higher. At 27.5 percent from 12 V with a 33 ohm load, 4.7 µH and 500 kHz, it's about 6.1 V, not 3.3. In that case I need the load, the inductance and the frequency, not just $D$ and $V_{in}$. With no load at all an open-loop buck's output rises toward $V_{in}$.

And finally in a regulated converter $D$ isn't an input: the feedback loop picks it, so the output is whatever the reference and divider say, and $D$ is a consequence."
:::

::: technical
**Ideal CCM:** $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$.

**CCM with drops.**
- Synchronous: $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}-I\big[D R_{hs}+(1-D)R_{ls}+R_{DCR}\big]$.
- Asynchronous: $V_{out}=D(V_{in}-IR_{hs})-(1-D)V_f-IR_{DCR}$.
- At $D=0.275$, 2 A, 20/10/15 mΩ, $V_f=0.5$ V: sync $3.2445$ V, async $2.8965$ V, ideal $3.3$ V.

**DCM (current load $I$).**
$$V_{out}=\frac{V_{in}}{1+\dfrac{2Lf_{sw}I}{V_{in}D^2}}$$
**DCM (resistive load).** With $K=2L/(RT)$, $M=\dfrac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4K/D^2}}$ for $K<1-D$; otherwise $M=D$.

**Boundary.** CCM if $I_{out}>I_{crit}=\dfrac{V_{in}D(1-D)}{2Lf_{sw}}$ (0.509 A here).

**Open-loop numbers** ($V_{in}=12$ V, $D=0.275$, $L=4.7$ µH, 500 kHz):

| Load | Mode | $V_{out}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 2 A, current load | CCM | 3.24 V (with drops) |
| 33 Ω resistor | DCM | 6.12 V |
| 100 mA | DCM | 7.91 V |
| 50 mA | DCM | 9.53 V |
| no load | DCM | rises toward 12 V |

**Closed loop.** $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$; $D$ is whatever makes that true.
:::

::: long
"Yes, but I'd state the conditions. In continuous conduction mode, ideal components, steady state, the answer is $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$. For 12 volts at 27.5 percent duty, 3.3 volts. That's volt-second balance: the inductor's average voltage is zero, so the output equals the average of the switch node.

Next, real hardware. The output is slightly lower because the switches and inductor drop voltage. For a synchronous buck I'd write $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}-I[\,D R_{hs}+(1-D)R_{ls}+R_{DCR}]$. With 20, 10 and 15 milliohms at 2 amps, that's about 55 millivolts below the ideal, so 3.24 V. For a diode buck the off-time drop is the diode's forward voltage, so the same duty gives about 2.9 V.

The case that really changes the answer is light load. If the load current is below half the inductor ripple, the inductor current hits zero every cycle: discontinuous mode. Then the average switch-node voltage during the zero-current interval is pulled up towards $V_{out}$, and the output is higher than $D\,V_{in}$. I'd need the load, $L$ and frequency. With a constant load current, $V_{out}=V_{in}/(1+2Lf_{sw}I/(V_{in}D^2))$. For the same 27.5 percent duty, 12 volts in, 4.7 microhenries and 500 kHz, a 33 ohm resistor gives 6.1 volts, and 50 milliamps gives 9.5 volts. With no load, an open-loop buck's output keeps rising toward the input.

Finally, in a real regulator the loop closes around all this. $D$ isn't a given; the feedback adjusts it until the feedback pin matches the reference, so $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$ independent of the details above. The formulas are still useful: from the duty cycle on the scope I can predict the output and detect losses or a mode change."
:::

::: terms
- **Power-stage gain ($dV_{out}/dD$)**: equals $V_{in}$ in CCM; the sensitivity of the output to duty.
- **Conduction drop**: voltage lost across $R_{DS(on)}$, DCR or a diode.
- **Critical current ($I_{crit}$)**: the load current at the CCM/DCM boundary, $\Delta I_L/2$.
- **DCM (discontinuous conduction mode)**: inductor current is zero for part of each period.
- **Open-loop / closed-loop**: fixed duty versus duty adjusted by feedback.
- **Line regulation / load regulation**: how $V_{out}$ responds to $V_{in}$ / $I_{out}$ changes.
- **$K$**: the dimensionless parameter $2L/(RT)$ comparing inductor slew with load.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "$V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$, yes."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Answers yes, then immediately lists the conditions: ideal, CCM, steady state.
- Quantifies the corrections: about 55 mV for a synchronous buck at 2 A, about 0.4 V for a Schottky diode, with the formulas.
- Gives the DCM formula and a number (6.1 V at 33 Ω, or 9.5 V at 50 mA) showing how badly the simple formula fails at light load; notes the open-loop no-load runaway toward $V_{in}$.
- Points out that in closed loop $D$ is an output of the controller, so you'd *read* $D$ to learn about the converter, not set it.
- Offers the CCM/DCM boundary check, $I_{crit}=V_{in}D(1-D)/(2Lf_{sw})$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Stopping at $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$** when the question includes load current, a diode or light load.
- **Applying the CCM formula in DCM.** The output is *higher* than $D\,V_{in}$, sometimes by a factor of two or more.
- **Treating $D$ as a free design input in a regulator.** The loop sets it; forcing $D$ means opening the loop.
- **Believing an open-loop buck is safe at no load.** With a diode or diode emulation, the output creeps up toward $V_{in}$ and can overvoltage the load.
- **Forgetting the sign of the drop terms.** Drops *reduce* $V_{out}$ for a given $D$ (equivalently, they raise the $D$ needed).
- **Mixing up $D$ and $1-D$** when including the freewheel drop. The diode or low-side FET conducts for $(1-D)T$.
:::

::: deep
## The base case

In CCM, volt-second balance on the inductor ([[q0174]]) gives

$$V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$$

It's a straight line through the origin, $V_{in}$ is the slope, so every 1% of duty is $0.01\,V_{in}$. For 12 V, that's 120 mV per 1% of duty. This is the power-stage "gain" that the feedback loop has to work with, and it is also the reason PWM resolution matters: a 10-bit modulator moves $V_{out}$ in 11.7 mV steps ([[q0179]]).

Reading the equation both ways:

| Given | Find | Formula | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| $D=0.275$, $V_{in}=12$ V | $V_{out}$ | $D\,V_{in}$ | 3.3 V |
| $D=0.4$, $V_{in}=12$ V | $V_{out}$ | $D\,V_{in}$ | 4.8 V |
| $D=0.6$, $V_{in}=5$ V | $V_{out}$ | $D\,V_{in}$ | 3.0 V |
| $V_{out}=1.8$ V, $V_{in}=3.7$ V | $D$ | $V_{out}/V_{in}$ | 0.486 ([[q0182]]) |

## Correction 1: conduction drops

The inductor's volt-second balance holds for the *inductor* voltage, so you have to evaluate the switch-node average with real devices ([[q0180]]). For a synchronous buck carrying load current $I$:

$$V_{out}=D(V_{in}-IR_{hs})-(1-D)IR_{ls}-IR_{DCR}=D\,V_{in}-I\left[D R_{hs}+(1-D)R_{ls}+R_{DCR}\right]$$

For the usual numbers ($R_{hs}=20$ mΩ, $R_{ls}=10$ mΩ, $R_{DCR}=15$ mΩ, 2 A, $D=0.275$): bracket $=0.275\times0.02+0.725\times0.01+0.015=27.75$ mΩ, drop $=55.5$ mV, so $V_{out}=3.2445$ V.

For an asynchronous buck with a 0.5 V Schottky:

$$V_{out}=D(V_{in}-IR_{hs})-(1-D)V_f-IR_{DCR}=0.275(11.96)-0.725(0.5)-0.03=2.8965\text{ V}$$

That is 0.40 V below the ideal 3.3 V: a **12%** error at the output, versus 1.7% for the synchronous version. This is the quantitative reason synchronous rectification wins at low output voltage ([[q0203]]).

## Correction 2: discontinuous conduction

Now the big one. A buck in CCM has $i_L>0$ for the whole period, with ripple $\Delta I_L$, so DCM occurs when the load current falls below $\Delta I_L/2$:

$$I_{crit}=\frac{V_{in}D(1-D)}{2Lf_{sw}}=\frac{12\times0.275\times0.725}{2\times4.7\text{ µH}\times500\text{ kHz}}=0.509\text{ A}$$

Below $I_{crit}$ the current ramps up for $DT$, falls to zero in $D_2T$, and stays at zero. During the zero-current interval, a diode (or emulation) blocks reverse current, SW floats at about $V_{out}$, and the inductor doesn't pull the average down. The balance becomes $(V_{in}-V_{out})D=V_{out}D_2$ with $D_2<1-D$. Working it through for a constant-current load gives

$$V_{out}=\frac{V_{in}}{1+\dfrac{2Lf_{sw}I}{V_{in}D^2}}\qquad(\text{DCM},\ I<I_{crit})$$

(and for a resistive load, $M=2/(1+\sqrt{1+4K/D^2})$ with $K=2L/(RT)$; both are derived in [[q0182]]). The two forms agree because $R=V_{out}/I$.

The no-load limit makes sense physically: each cycle the inductor delivers charge to $C_{out}$ and nothing takes it out, so the output creeps up until the diode (or low-side) can no longer conduct, with $V_{out}\to V_{in}$ and the inductor current going to zero. An open-loop buck with no load is a classic overvoltage hazard.

## Why the formula changes: a physical picture

In CCM, SW is either $V_{in}$ or 0 for the entire period, so its average is $D\,V_{in}$. In DCM, SW is $V_{in}$ for $D$, 0 for $D_2$, and (for the diode or emulated case) floats near $V_{out}$ for the remainder. The average of SW is therefore *larger* than $D\,V_{in}$: the third interval adds $V_{out}(1-D-D_2)$. The inductor still has zero average voltage, so $V_{out}$ must rise until the balance is restored. A numerical cycle-by-cycle simulation (ideal switches, a 33 Ω load, $D=0.275$) settles at 6.12 V, matching the formula.

## Closed loop: D is not an input

Everything above is open-loop: you choose $D$ and the circuit responds. In a regulator, feedback chooses $D$:

$$V_{out}=V_{ref}\left(1+\frac{R_1}{R_2}\right),\qquad D=f(V_{in},I_{out},\ldots)\ \text{is the result.}$$

Read the formulas the other way: the controller *must* use $D=0.275$ for 3.3 V at 2 A, but only $D=0.122$ at 100 mA ([[q0182]]). If you measured $D=0.275$ on the scope at 100 mA and the output were 3.3 V, something is wrong: in DCM the loop would be using a lower duty. If you read $D$ and $V_{in}$ off a scope, $D\,V_{in}$ is a *prediction*, and the difference between it and the measured $V_{out}$ is diagnostic: a larger-than-expected gap points to resistive losses; a negative gap (output higher than $D\,V_{in}$) points to DCM.

## How to answer in an interview

1. Say yes and give $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$ with the reason (volt-second balance).
2. State "assuming CCM and ideal components".
3. Offer the corrections with a number: about 55 mV for a synchronous buck at 2 A, 0.4 V for a Schottky.
4. Mention the light-load behaviour and what you'd need to know ($L$, $f_{sw}$, load).
5. Add the closed-loop note.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If they say "you're given only $D$ and $V_{in}$", reply "then I'd assume CCM and ideal components and answer $D\,V_{in}$, and I'd flag that if the converter might be in DCM I'd need the load, inductance and frequency." That's the answer they want to hear.

::: applications
- **Scope diagnostics**: read $D$ and $V_{in}$, predict $V_{out}$, and compare. A gap tells you about drops, DCM or dead time.
- **Open-loop test fixtures and start-up**: when commanding a fixed duty to characterise the power stage (e.g. before closing the loop), you need the DCM relation to avoid overvoltage at light load ([[q0267]]).
- **Soft-start design**: ramping $D$ to ramp $V_{out}$ ($0.01\,V_{in}$ per 1% duty).
- **Digital power control and feed-forward**: using $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$ as a feed-forward term, with the loop trimming the rest.
- **Design verification**: predicting $V_{out}$ in the lab for a fixed PWM from a microcontroller driving an open-loop buck, e.g. a DC motor or LED driver.
- **Understanding PFM/pulse-skipping**: the DCM relation is the reason light-load modes exist ([[q0231]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Steady state, fixed-frequency PWM.**
- **CCM for the base formula.** The DCM relations apply for $I<I_{crit}$ with diode (or emulated-diode) behaviour.
- **Constant resistances** for $R_{hs}$, $R_{ls}$ and DCR, and a constant $V_f$. Real values vary with temperature and current.
- **Large output capacitor**, so $V_{out}$ is essentially constant within a cycle.
- **Open loop** for the duty-to-output relations. A closed loop overrides them by changing $D$.
- **Forced-PWM synchronous converters** do not enter DCM (the inductor current reverses instead), so $V_{out}\approx D\,V_{in}$ holds down to no load, at the cost of circulating current and light-load efficiency ([[q0229]]).
:::

::: followups
### What output do you expect for $D=0.4$ from a 12 V input?
Ideal CCM: $0.4\times12=4.8$ V. With a synchronous stage at 1 A and the same resistances, the bracket is $0.4\times0.02+0.6\times0.01+0.015=0.029$ Ω, so the drop is 29 mV and $V_{out}\approx4.77$ V. If the load is below the critical current, $V_{in}D(1-D)/(2Lf_{sw})=12\times0.24/4.7=0.613$ A, the output would be higher (for example 9.64 V at 100 mA, open loop).

### Why does an open-loop buck's output rise at light load?
In DCM the inductor current is zero for part of each cycle, so nothing pulls the switch node down during that time and the SW average rises above $D\,V_{in}$. Since the inductor's average voltage must be zero, $V_{out}$ rises to compensate. At no load the output climbs toward $V_{in}$, which is why a closed loop is essential for light-load regulation.

### Does this change for a synchronous buck with forced PWM?
Yes. With forced PWM the low-side FET stays on after the current reaches zero, letting the inductor current go negative. The converter never enters DCM and $V_{out}\approx D\,V_{in}$ holds to zero load. The cost is a circulating current that keeps the switching losses at light load, which is why many parts offer a mode input to choose between FPWM and PFM/diode-emulation ([[q0229]], [[q0231]]).

### With a closed loop holding 3.3 V, the duty cycle on the scope reads 12% at 12 V in. What does that tell you?
$D\,V_{in}=1.44$ V, far below 3.3 V, so the CCM relation clearly doesn't apply: the converter must be in DCM or a skipping mode at light load. The DCM relation (resistive load) says $D=M\sqrt{K/(1-M)}$, and $D=0.122$ corresponds to about 100 mA for 4.7 µH at 500 kHz ([[q0182]]).

### Scenario: you drive a buck from a microcontroller PWM at a fixed 30% with a 12 V supply and no feedback. The output reads 3.55 V at 2 A but 11.5 V with the load removed. Explain.
At 2 A the converter is in CCM and $V_{out}=0.3\times12-2\times(0.3\times0.02+0.7\times0.01+0.015)=3.544$ V, matching the reading. With the load removed, it enters DCM and, being open loop with no load, the output charges toward $V_{in}$. The 11.5 V reading is the output nearly reaching 12 V. You need feedback, a minimum load (bleeder), or overvoltage protection.
:::

::: interpretations
"Given duty cycle and $V_{in}$, can you find $V_{out}$?" is a yes/no question with an explanation attached. The yes comes with the assumption of CCM and ideal components. If the interviewer is hinting at "what else do you need to know", the answer is: the load (or the CCM/DCM status), the drops, and whether the loop is open or closed. Compare [[q0182]] (given a ratio, find $D$) and [[q0180]] (general $D$ calculation).
