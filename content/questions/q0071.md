::: testing
Whether you can derive the answer from the conductivity equation rather than quote "electrons are faster". A strong answer has three beats:

1. $\sigma=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$ and, at **equal doping**, the majority density is the same, so the difference is purely mobility: $\rho_p/\rho_n\approx\mu_n/\mu_p\approx2.8$ in lightly doped silicon.
2. The "typically" qualifier: the claim is for equal doping and is a statement about a trend, not a law. It narrows at heavy doping and can reverse if the P-type is doped more.
3. Practical consequences: N-channel power MOSFETs, N-well resistors, NPN transistors, N+ contacts.
:::

::: why
The same fact explains why power designers prefer N-channel MOSFETs (about 2-3x lower $R_{DS(on)}$ per area than P-channel), why high-side switching often needs a charge pump so an NMOS can be used, why integrated resistors and wells are laid out with N and P regions differently, and why NPN is the default transistor. It's an easy question that tests whether you connect a microscopic quantity (mobility) with a number on a datasheet (resistance).
:::

::: concepts
- **Conductivity and resistivity**: $\sigma=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$, $\rho=1/\sigma$. See [[q0068]].
- **Majority carrier density**: set by doping, $n\approx N_D$ or $p\approx N_A$. See [[q0069]].
- **Mobility**: electrons about 1350, holes about 480 cm²/V·s in lightly doped Si. See [[q0070]].
- **Sheet resistance**: $R_s=\rho/t$ in Ω/square, used for IC resistors.
- **Ionized-impurity scattering**: reduces mobility at high doping, narrowing the N/P gap.
:::

::: answer
"Because at the same doping level the number of majority carriers is about the same, but electrons are more mobile than holes. Conductivity is $q\,n\,\mu_n$ for N-type and $q\,p\,\mu_p$ for P-type. With $n=p=10^{16}$ per cm³, the only difference is mobility. Electrons in silicon have about 1350 cm²/V·s and holes about 480, so N-type conducts around 2.8 times better and its resistivity is about 2.8 times lower.

As numbers, $10^{16}$ per cm³ doping gives about 0.5 ohm-centimetres for N-type and about 1.5 for P-type. The same ratio shows up in transistors: an N-channel MOSFET has lower on-resistance than a P-channel of the same size, which is why power designers prefer NMOS, and why CMOS needs the PMOS to be wider.

Two caveats. It's at equal doping: if the P-type is doped ten times heavier it will be the better conductor. And at very heavy doping the gap narrows because impurity scattering dominates both mobilities, to maybe 1.6x at $10^{20}$."
:::

::: technical
**Conductivity.**
$$\sigma=q\,(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$$
N-type ($n\approx N_D\gg p$): $\sigma_n\approx qN_D\mu_n$. P-type ($p\approx N_A\gg n$): $\sigma_p\approx qN_A\mu_p$. At equal doping $N_D=N_A=N$:
$$\frac{\rho_p}{\rho_n}=\frac{\mu_n(N)}{\mu_p(N)}$$

**Numbers (Si, 300 K).** Using the Caughey-Thomas/Arora mobility fit:

| $N$ (cm⁻³) | $\rho_n$ (Ω·cm) | $\rho_p$ (Ω·cm) | $\rho_p/\rho_n$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $10^{14}$ | 47 | 135 | 2.9 |
| $10^{16}$ | 0.51 | 1.43 | 2.8 |
| $10^{17}$ | 0.080 | 0.19 | 2.4 |
| $10^{18}$ | 0.024 | 0.044 | 1.8 |
| $10^{20}$ | 0.00068 | 0.0011 | 1.6 |

**Sheet resistance example.** A 1 µm thick layer at $10^{17}$ cm⁻³: $R_s=\rho/t$ gives 803 Ω/sq (N) versus 1890 Ω/sq (P). A 10-square resistor is 8.0 kΩ versus 18.9 kΩ.

**Power MOSFETs.** The channel and drift region resistance scale with $1/\mu$, so P-channel devices need about $\mu_n/\mu_p$ times the area for equal $R_{DS(on)}$: roughly 2.5-3x in older silicon, less in modern strained designs.

**Caveat on intrinsic and temperature effects.** Near the intrinsic limit both carriers contribute; the ratio is not governed by the formula above. Mobility falls with temperature, so both resistivities rise with $T$ in the extrinsic range, roughly in step.
:::

So N-type conducts about 2.8 times better. At $10^{16}$ per cm³ that's about 0.5 Ω·cm versus 1.5 Ω·cm. It narrows at heavy doping, and it only holds at equal doping."

::: long
"It comes down to the conductivity equation. Conductivity is $\sigma=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$, and resistivity is its inverse. In N-type material the electron term dominates, so $\sigma\approx qN_D\mu_n$. In P-type material the hole term dominates, so $\sigma\approx qN_A\mu_p$.

If I dope them equally, say $10^{16}$ per cm³, then the density of majority carriers is the same, because essentially all the dopants are ionized. What differs is the mobility. Electrons in silicon have about 1350 cm²/V·s, holes about 480, so electrons move about 2.8 times more easily under the same field. That makes the conductivity of N-type about 2.8 times higher and the resistivity 2.8 times lower. With numbers: about 0.5 Ω·cm for N-type and about 1.5 for P-type at $10^{16}$.

Where does that show up? In integrated circuits, a 1 µm thick N-layer at $10^{17}$ has about 800 ohms per square, while the equivalent P-layer has about 1900. In power electronics, N-channel MOSFETs have roughly 2 to 3 times lower on-resistance than P-channel devices of the same area, so for high-current switches you use NMOS and add a gate-drive trick for the high side. And in CMOS you widen the PMOS to compensate.

A few caveats are worth saying. This holds at equal doping. P-type can have lower resistance than N-type if it's more heavily doped. At very high doping, ionized impurity scattering dominates both mobilities, so the ratio shrinks, maybe 1.6 at $10^{20}$. Dopant solubility and ionization also differ, so a real process can shift it. And in other materials the ratio changes, such as GaN where p-type is much more resistive, partly because magnesium acceptors are deep and poorly ionized."
:::

::: terms
- **Resistivity ($\rho$)**: material property in Ω·cm; $1/\sigma$.
- **Conductivity ($\sigma$)**: $q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$, in S/cm.
- **Sheet resistance ($R_s$)**: $\rho/t$, in Ω/square, for thin layers.
- **Majority carrier density**: $n\approx N_D$ (N-type) or $p\approx N_A$ (P-type).
- **Mobility**: drift velocity per unit field; higher for electrons.
- **$R_{DS(on)}$**: on-resistance of a MOSFET, which scales with channel and drift-region resistivity.
- **Ionized-impurity scattering**: mobility-limiting mechanism at high doping.
- **N-well / P-well**: lightly doped regions in CMOS used for transistors and resistors.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"N-type has more carriers."** At equal doping it doesn't. The difference is mobility. (N-type wafers may be doped differently in practice.)
- **"N-type always has lower resistance."** Only at the same doping. A P-type region doped 10x higher can have lower resistance.
- **"Electrons are lighter, so they conduct better" without the equation.** True but incomplete; the statement needs $\sigma=qn\mu$.
- **Quoting the ratio as exactly 3 everywhere.** It falls with doping (about 1.6 at $10^{20}$) and varies with temperature and the mobility fit.
- **Forgetting that resistance ≠ resistivity.** Resistance also depends on geometry; compare $\rho$ or sheet resistance, not raw resistors of different size.
- **Applying it to metals.** In metals there is no N/P distinction; this is a semiconductor statement.
:::

::: deep
## Starting from the conductivity equation

Current density in a semiconductor with both carrier types in a field $E$:
$$J=q\,(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)\,E=\sigma E$$
so $\sigma=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$. Because the minority density is tiny, the sum reduces to a single term.

N-type, donor density $N_D=10^{16}$ cm⁻³, $\mu_n(N)\approx1220$ cm²/V·s:
$$\rho_n=\frac{1}{qN_D\mu_n}=\frac{1}{1.602\times10^{-19}\times10^{16}\times1220}=0.51\ \Omega\cdot\text{cm}$$

P-type, $N_A=10^{16}$ cm⁻³, $\mu_p(N)\approx437$ cm²/V·s:
$$\rho_p=\frac{1}{qN_A\mu_p}=1.43\ \Omega\cdot\text{cm}$$

Ratio $=1.43/0.51=2.8$. Everything in the ratio is mobility. (See [[q0066]] for why doping sets the carrier numbers, and [[q0068]] for the mobility model behind these numbers.)

## Resistors on a chip

IC resistors are layers or wells with a defined sheet resistance. For a layer of thickness $t$:
$$R_s=\frac{\rho}{t}\ \ [\Omega/\text{sq}],\qquad R=R_s\frac{L}{W}$$
Take $t=1\ \mu$m $=10^{-4}$ cm at $N=10^{17}$ cm⁻³:
- N-type: $R_s=0.0803/10^{-4}=803\ \Omega/\text{sq}$.
- P-type: $R_s=0.189/10^{-4}=1890\ \Omega/\text{sq}$.

A resistor of 10 squares (e.g. $L/W=10$) is therefore 8.0 kΩ in an N-layer and 18.9 kΩ in a P-layer. A designer wanting a given resistance with a small footprint will choose the higher-$R_s$ layer (P in this example); wanting low resistance (a low-resistance path or guard ring) chooses N.

## Power MOSFETs: why NMOS wins

The on-resistance of a vertical power MOSFET is the sum of channel and drift-region contributions. The drift region is lightly doped and carries the voltage. For a unipolar device, the specific on-resistance scales as
$$R_{on,sp}\propto\frac{V_{BR}^{2}}{\varepsilon\,\mu\,E_c^3}$$
(see the Baliga figure of merit), so $R_{on,sp}\propto1/\mu$. A P-channel device with hole mobility 2.8x lower needs 2.8x the area for the same resistance, so more silicon, more capacitance, more cost. Designers therefore use N-channel devices wherever they can, and for high-side switching either use an NMOS with a bootstrap or charge-pump gate drive or accept a larger PMOS where simplicity matters (for example a load switch or a reverse-polarity protection FET).

## Why are electrons more mobile? The effective-mass picture

Mobility is $\mu = q\tau/m^*$, where $\tau$ is the mean time between scattering events and $m^*$ is the effective mass that describes how the carrier accelerates inside the crystal's periodic potential rather than in free space. Commonly quoted conductivity effective masses for silicon are about $0.26\,m_0$ for electrons and $0.37\,m_0$ for holes (reference values differ slightly between textbooks). The mass ratio is $0.37/0.26=1.4$, which is only half of the observed mobility ratio of 2.8. The rest comes from the scattering time. Working backward from the lightly doped mobilities:

$$\tau_n=\frac{\mu_n m^*_n}{q}=\frac{0.135\ \text{m}^2/\text{Vs}\times0.26\times9.11\times10^{-31}}{1.602\times10^{-19}}\approx0.20\ \text{ps},\qquad \tau_p\approx0.10\ \text{ps}$$

Holes scatter about twice as often. The valence band has two degenerate bands at its top (heavy and light holes), so a hole has more final states to scatter into, including transitions between the two bands. The conduction-band minima for electrons offer fewer such shortcuts. In other words, the answer "electrons are lighter" is correct but only explains part of the effect; the rest is that holes are scattered more efficiently.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The split between effective-mass and scattering-time contributions depends on which effective mass is used (conductivity, density-of-states, or band-edge masses all differ) and on the temperature. Treat the 1.4 times 2 decomposition as a physical picture, not a precise breakdown.

## Temperature: both resistivities rise together

In the extrinsic range with full ionization, the carrier density is pinned at the doping level, so the temperature dependence of resistivity is the temperature dependence of mobility. Lattice scattering gives $\mu\propto T^{-\alpha}$ with $\alpha$ around 2.2 to 2.4 for lightly doped silicon (values vary by source). Taking 2.4 for electrons, heating the 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ N-type sample from 300 K to 400 K multiplies the resistivity by $(400/300)^{2.4}=1.99$, from 0.51 to about 1.0 Ω·cm. Since holes have a similar exponent, the P/N ratio stays close to 2.8 and both curves rise in step.

This positive temperature coefficient is why a power MOSFET's on-resistance rises with temperature, about 0.8% per kelvin at 300 K in the lightly doped drift region by this estimate ($\alpha/T=2.4/300$). Because it rises, paralleled MOSFETs share current well: the one that runs hotter carries less. Complete devices also have a channel component whose temperature behavior differs, so datasheet curves show less than 0.8% per kelvin for low-voltage parts. Use the datasheet's normalized $R_{DS(on)}$ versus temperature plot, not this estimate.

## Worked example: area penalty for P-channel

Suppose an N-channel design with $R_{DS(on)}=10\ \text{m}\Omega$ on die area $A$. Resistance of a unipolar device scales as $1/(\mu\cdot\text{area})$, so the same die area in P-channel gives about $10\times2.8=28\ \text{m}\Omega$, and reaching 10 mΩ needs about 2.8 times the area. The gate charge and output capacitance both scale with area, so the P-channel part is slower to switch and costs more silicon. A designer comparing two datasheets should compare **resistance times area** (or resistance times gate charge as a figure of merit), not the bare resistance. In standard-cell CMOS the same effect is why the PMOS gets drawn about 2 to 3 times wider than the NMOS for equal rise and fall times, with the ratio shrinking in strained-silicon processes where PMOS mobility has been boosted (the exact ratio is process-dependent).

## Where the simple statement breaks

1. **Unequal doping.** If $N_A=10N_D$ the P-type material can be more conductive. Real wells and substrates aren't doped equally: a P-type substrate at $10^{15}$ is far more resistive than an N+ source at $10^{20}$ regardless of mobility.
2. **Dopant solubility and activation.** Arsenic and phosphorus can be activated above $10^{20}$ cm⁻³ in silicon, while boron saturates a bit lower, so the best-case N+ layer is more conductive than the best-case P+ layer.
3. **Other materials.** In GaAs the electron mobility is about 20x the hole mobility, so N-type is vastly more conductive. In GaN, p-type is limited both by low hole mobility and by Mg being a deep acceptor with only a few percent ionized, so p-GaN is highly resistive.
4. **Strain and quantum confinement.** In advanced MOSFETs, strained SiGe PMOS channels have hole mobility much closer to the electron mobility.
5. **Compensation.** Compensated material has lower mobility and higher resistivity for the same net doping, either type.

## How to verify it on the bench or in the fab

1. **Four-point probe.** Four collinear probes touch the wafer; current is forced through the outer pair and voltage is measured across the inner pair. For a thin layer much larger than the probe spacing, $R_s=\dfrac{\pi}{\ln 2}\dfrac{V}{I}=4.532\,\dfrac{V}{I}$. A reading of $V/I=177\ \Omega$ gives $R_s=803\ \Omega/\text{sq}$, the 1 µm N layer at $10^{17}$ cm⁻³ in the worked example. A $V/I$ of 417 Ω would be the P layer at 1890 Ω/sq. Multiplying by the layer thickness gives $\rho=R_st$.
2. **Hall measurement.** A van der Pauw structure in a magnetic field gives the carrier type and density from the Hall voltage, $R_H=1/(qn)$ for electrons, and the mobility from $\mu_H=R_H/\rho$. The Hall mobility differs from the drift mobility by a factor near one (the Hall factor), so cross-checks against published fits agree to within tens of percent rather than exactly.
3. **Compare matched test structures.** On a test die, measure an N-well and a P-well resistor of identical drawn geometry. The sheet-resistance ratio shows the process-specific P/N ratio, including doping profile and thickness, which the bulk-mobility estimate cannot predict.
4. **Temperature sweep.** Repeat the measurement at 25 °C and 100 °C. The resistance should rise roughly as $T^{2.4}$ in the extrinsic range; a much smaller slope suggests contact or probe artifacts, and a fall suggests approaching intrinsic conduction.

::: applications
- **Power MOSFET selection**: N-channel devices for the same $R_{DS(on)}$ are smaller and cheaper than P-channel ones, so most half-bridge and motor-drive designs use N-channel high- and low-side FETs.
- **Load switches and reverse-polarity protection**: a P-channel FET is used for convenience (no charge pump), accepting higher resistance or size.
- **CMOS standard cells**: PMOS drawn wider than NMOS to equalize drive.
- **IC resistors and wells**: choose N-well or P-well resistor by the sheet resistance needed and by matching/parasitic considerations.
- **Substrate resistivity**: epitaxial CMOS wafers use a heavily doped substrate under a lightly doped epi layer to give low-resistance substrate and good latch-up immunity.
- **Solar cells and sensors**: contact grids and emitter layers are chosen for sheet resistance, often N+ emitter on a P base.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Equal doping, silicon, 300 K, fully ionized dopants.** The statement "N-type has lower resistance" is conditional on these.
- **Lightly to moderately doped, extrinsic material** where one carrier dominates. Near intrinsic, both carriers contribute.
- **Same geometry and temperature.** Resistance compares only after normalising for shape; the question speaks of material behaviour.
- **Mobility model**: Arora-type empirical fit; real values vary with dopant species, compensation and measurement.
- **Alternative reading**: some interviewers mean "N-channel MOSFETs have lower on-resistance". Same physics, with the extra factor that inversion-layer mobility, not bulk, applies.
:::

::: followups
### Does N-type always have lower resistance than P-type?
No. It's true at equal doping because of the mobility difference. If the P-type is doped 10x heavier than the N-type, it can be more conductive. Real structures also differ in thickness, compensation and dopant activation. The correct claim is "at equal doping and geometry, N-type conducts about 2-3x better in silicon".

### Compute the resistivity of N- and P-type silicon doped at 10^16 cm⁻³.
Using $\mu_n\approx1220$ and $\mu_p\approx437$ cm²/V·s at this doping: $\rho_n=1/(1.602\times10^{-19}\times10^{16}\times1220)=0.51\ \Omega\cdot$cm and $\rho_p=1/(1.602\times10^{-19}\times10^{16}\times437)=1.43\ \Omega\cdot$cm. The ratio is 2.8. If you use the lightly doped textbook mobilities (1350, 480) you get 0.46 and 1.30 Ω·cm, with ratio 2.8.

### Why do power designers prefer N-channel MOSFETs?
For a given die area N-channel has about 2-3x lower $R_{DS(on)}$ because electrons are more mobile, so it also has lower gate charge and output capacitance at equal resistance. The price is that a high-side NMOS needs a gate voltage above the source, supplied by a bootstrap or charge pump, while a high-side PMOS can be driven by simple level-shifting. Where simplicity or very low frequency matters a PMOS is used. See [[q0083]].

### What would you expect the resistivity ratio to be at 10^19 cm⁻³ doping, and why is it different?
Smaller, about 1.7 in the Arora fit. At that doping ionized-impurity scattering limits both carriers, so electron mobility falls from ~1340 to ~114 cm²/V·s and hole mobility from ~460 to ~69; the ratio is 1.7. Both mobilities are heading toward their (similar) minimum values, so the N advantage shrinks.

### In a CMOS process, you need a 10 kΩ resistor in a small area. Do you pick an N-well or P-well resistor?
I'd start from sheet resistance: pick the layer with the higher $R_s$ so fewer squares are needed, then weigh other issues (voltage coefficient, parasitic capacitance to the substrate, matching, available layers). Usually N-well resistors have higher $R_s$ (thin, light doping) but suffer a large voltage coefficient from well depletion; polysilicon or thin-film resistors are often better for precision. The mobility argument tells you the sign of the trade but the process data decide.

### Scenario: a P-channel high-side switch has twice the resistance you can tolerate, and you can't spend more area. What are your options?
Switch to an N-channel device with bootstrap/charge-pump drive (about 2-3x lower resistance at the same area), put two PMOS in parallel (halves resistance but doubles area and cost, so not the case here), increase gate drive $|V_{GS}|$ to enhance the channel, or move to a newer-generation PMOS with better figure of merit. Also check whether thermal derating (resistance rises about 0.5%/K) is part of the problem.
:::
