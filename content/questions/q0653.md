::: basics
### In plain words
A 3D chip stacks several thin silicon dies on top of each other in one package and wires them together vertically. Signals then travel tens of micrometers up instead of centimeters sideways. That gives huge bandwidth and uses less energy per bit. The price is heat that is hard to remove, and more difficult testing.

### Picture it
Compare a sprawling town with a skyscraper. In the town, getting from one building to another means a long road. In the skyscraper, an elevator makes the trip in seconds. Stacking dies is the skyscraper. The catch is heat: the lower floors must push their heat up through all the floors above, so the ground floor gets the warmest.

### Words you need
- **Die**: one chip's worth of silicon. See [[q0645]].
- **TSV (through-silicon via)**: a metal-filled vertical hole through a thinned die that carries signals and power to the die below or above.
- **Hybrid bonding**: joining two dies face to face by bonding their copper pads directly, with no solder bumps.
- **Bandwidth**: how much data moves per second.
- **HBM (high bandwidth memory)**: a stack of memory dies with a very wide connection, placed next to a processor.

### The simplest example
Take one HBM stack. It has 1024 separate vertical connections, and each carries 6.4 Gb/s. The total is 1024 × 6.4 = 6,554 Gb/s, which is about 819 GB/s (divide by 8 bits per byte). To get that over a normal memory interface you would need dozens of channels and many more pins. Wires matter too: crossing a die takes about 10 mm of wire, while a vertical hop is only about 0.05 mm. Short wires carry less capacitance, so moving a bit costs far less energy.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers why heat is the hard part, how hybrid bonding raises connection density, and how this differs from 2.5D interposers, 3D NAND flash and 3D transistors.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can separate the several things people mean by "3D" and explain the real engineering benefit and cost of each. A good candidate says that 3D chips stack active silicon vertically and connect layers with through-silicon vias (TSVs) or hybrid bonds, then immediately names the payoffs (shorter wires, huge bandwidth, smaller footprint) and the big problems (heat, power delivery, yield and test). Hidden sub-skills: wire-length intuition, thermal resistance, and awareness that 3D NAND and 3D transistors (FinFET) are different ideas.
:::

::: why
Memory bandwidth and wire energy limit modern processors and accelerators. Stacking is how HBM feeds GPUs, how some CPUs get a large extra cache, and how phone chips get memory close by. If you work with boards or packaging you will see these parts and need to understand their power, thermal and reliability behaviour. It also builds on chiplets ([[q0652]]), PoP ([[q0647]]) and flip-chip bumps ([[q0656]]), and interviewers like it because the answer touches physics, manufacturing and system design together.
:::

::: concepts
- **Die stacking**: placing two or more dies on top of each other, in one package, as opposed to side by side. See [[q0652]].
- **Through-silicon via (TSV)**: a metal-filled vertical hole through a thinned die that carries signals and power between layers.
- **Hybrid bonding**: direct copper-to-copper and oxide-to-oxide bonding with no solder bump, giving very fine pitch.
- **Microbump**: a small solder or copper-pillar bump (tens of µm pitch) used for die-to-die connection. See [[q0656]] for the larger flip-chip version.
- **Wire length and energy**: wire RC delay scales with length squared, and energy per bit scales with capacitance, so shorter wires win.
- **Heat flow**: heat from buried dies must travel up through other silicon and bond layers, so thermal resistance grows.
:::

::: answer
"By 3D chips I mean ICs where active silicon is stacked vertically, instead of everything sitting on one flat die. You thin the dies, drill through-silicon vias or use hybrid bonding, and connect the layers directly so signals travel tens of microns up instead of centimetres sideways.

Why do it? Shorter wires. A vertical connection is a few tens of microns, while crossing a die is around a centimetre, so you get far lower delay and energy per bit and you can put thousands of connections in a square millimetre. That's how HBM works, with DRAM dies stacked on a base die and a very wide interface, and how some CPUs put a big cache die on top of the compute die. It also saves footprint, which matters in phones.

The downsides are mostly heat and cost. The die at the bottom has to push its heat through the dies above it, or you put the hot die on top near the heat sink. Power delivery to each layer is harder, you need known-good-die testing before you stack, and if you stack three dies and one is bad you lose the lot.

I'd also mention that people say '3D' for other things like 3D NAND, which is memory cells built vertically on one die, and 3D transistors like FinFETs, which is a different idea."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** A 3D IC stacks two or more active dies (or layers) vertically with vertical interconnects. Common technologies:

- **TSV + microbumps**: dies are thinned (tens of µm), TSVs of a few µm diameter run through them, and microbumps (pitch typically ~25-50 µm) join dies. This is what HBM uses; HBM3 has a 1024-bit interface per stack at about 6.4 Gb/s per pin, giving $1024 \times 6.4/8 = 819$ GB/s per stack.
- **Hybrid bonding (Cu-Cu)**: no solder; pitch can be ≲10 µm. Density scales as $1/p^2$, so going from 40 µm to 10 µm pitch gives $(40/10)^2 = 16\times$ more connections per unit area.
- **Monolithic 3D**: building second device layers on top of the first on the *same* wafer (hard because of thermal budget). 3D NAND is the commercial example: hundreds of layers of memory cells etched as vertical strings.

**Benefits.**
- Wire length drops from ~10 mm to tens of µm. At ~0.2 fF/µm, 10 mm of wire is ~2 pF versus ~10 fF for 50 µm plus a TSV's few tens of fF. Energy per bit $\tfrac12 CV^2$ at 1 V goes from ~1 pJ to well under 0.1 pJ.
- Bandwidth density rises since you can put thousands of connections per mm².
- Heterogeneous layers (DRAM, logic, sensors) can be stacked.

**Costs.**
- **Thermal**: a layer of thickness $t$, conductivity $k$ and area $A$ has $R_\theta = t/(kA)$. Silicon has $k\approx 150$ W/m·K but underfill or bond layers can be ~1 W/m·K or lower.
- **Power delivery**: current must flow up through TSVs; IR drop and electromigration limit it.
- **Mechanical**: thinned dies are fragile, and thermal expansion mismatch stresses bumps.
- **Test/yield**: each stacked die must be known-good, and stack yield multiplies.
:::

That gives much higher bandwidth and lower energy per bit, which is how HBM and stacked cache work. The trade-offs are heat, since lower dies must push heat through the others, plus harder power delivery, test and yield. 3D NAND is a related but different idea."

::: long
"When people say 3D chips they usually mean 3D integration: you stack silicon dies on top of each other in one package and wire them together vertically. Ordinary chips are 2D, meaning one layer of transistors and all signals travel across the die or across the board. In a 3D chip you thin the dies and connect them with through-silicon vias, which are metal-filled holes through the silicon, or with hybrid bonding, which fuses copper pads directly without solder.

The big win is wire length. Crossing a die is about a centimetre of wire with a couple of picofarads of capacitance. A vertical connection is tens of microns with maybe tens of femtofarads. Shorter wire means lower delay, lower energy per bit, and the connections are tiny, so you can have thousands per square millimetre. That gives you enormous bandwidth. HBM is the classic example: a stack of DRAM dies on a base die with a 1024-bit bus per stack, which gives hundreds of gigabytes per second per stack. Another example is a processor with an extra cache die bonded on top.

The costs are mostly thermal and economic. Heat has to flow out through the stack, and bond layers have poor conductivity compared with silicon, so the lower die can run hot. Power delivery to each layer goes through TSVs, which limits current. Testing is harder because the interior dies are not probe-accessible after stacking, so you need known-good-die testing. And yield multiplies: one bad die kills the stack.

It's worth noting that 'three-D' is also used for 3D NAND, which is memory cells built vertically on one die, and for 3D transistors like FinFETs. Those are different things from stacking dies, so I'd clarify which one the interviewer means."
:::

::: terms
- **3D IC**: an integrated circuit with two or more active layers stacked and connected vertically.
- **TSV (through-silicon via)**: a vertical conductive via through a thinned die.
- **Microbump**: a small solder bump or copper pillar (tens of µm pitch) between stacked dies.
- **Hybrid bonding**: direct Cu-Cu plus dielectric-dielectric bonding, with pitch of about 10 µm or less.
- **HBM (high bandwidth memory)**: stacked DRAM with a very wide interface placed near a processor.
- **2.5D**: dies side by side on an interposer; contrast with true 3D stacking.
- **Face-to-face / face-to-back**: bonding orientation of the stacked dies' active sides.
- **3D NAND**: flash memory cells built in many vertical layers on one die.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "stacked chips connected with vias." An exceptional answer:
- Separates 3D stacking from 2.5D interposers, 3D NAND and FinFET-style 3D transistors.
- Gives a wire-length/energy argument with numbers (centimetre vs tens of microns).
- Computes a real bandwidth, for example HBM at 1024 bits × 6.4 Gb/s = 819 GB/s.
- Explains the thermal problem with $R = t/(kA)$ and says which die you put near the heat sink.
- Mentions hybrid bonding pitch scaling ($1/p^2$) and known-good-die test.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying 3D chips are the same as 3D transistors.** FinFET and gate-all-around transistors are 3D *device* geometries; 3D ICs stack *dies*.
- **Ignoring heat.** Stacking raises power density and adds thermal resistance. A "free" bandwidth gain can force lower clocks.
- **Calling every multi-die package 3D.** Side-by-side dies on an interposer are 2.5D; PoP ([[q0647]]) is stacked *packages*, not stacked dies with TSVs.
- **Assuming TSVs are tiny and free.** TSVs consume silicon area, create stress zones ("keep-out zones") and add capacitance.
- **Forgetting test.** Interior dies can't be probed, so you need pre-stack test and design-for-test access.
:::

## Why vertical wires win

On-chip wire resistance and capacitance both grow with length, so Elmore RC delay goes as $L^2$. For a wire with resistance per length $r$ and capacitance per length $c$, $\tau \approx \tfrac12 rcL^2$. A wire that is 10 mm long becomes about 0.005 mm of vertical connection, i.e. $(0.005/10)^2 = 2.5\times10^{-7}$ of the RC delay, though in practice drivers and TSV parasitics dominate, so real gains are smaller.

Energy per transition is $E = \tfrac12 CV^2$. Taking ~0.2 fF/µm:

- 10 mm planar wire: $C \approx 2\ \text{pF}$, $E \approx 1\ \text{pJ}$ at 1 V.
- 50 µm vertical route (about 10 fF) plus a TSV (tens of fF, assumed 30 fF): $C \approx 40$ fF, so $E \approx 0.02$ pJ.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Wire and TSV capacitances vary with technology. The values above (0.2 fF/µm, ~30 fF per TSV) are typical order-of-magnitude figures, not data for a specific process.

## The thermal problem with numbers

A 1D conduction resistance is $R_\theta = t/(kA)$. Suppose a stack footprint of 100 mm² ($10^{-4}$ m²):

- 50 µm of silicon ($k = 150$ W/m·K): $R = 50\times10^{-6}/(150\times10^{-4}) = 0.0033$ K/W.
- 20 µm of underfill-filled microbump layer ($k \approx 1$ W/m·K): $R = 20\times10^{-6}/(1\times10^{-4}) = 0.2$ K/W.
- 1 µm of oxide hybrid-bond layer ($k \approx 1.4$ W/m·K): $R \approx 0.007$ K/W.

At 50 W through one microbump layer, $\Delta T = 50\times 0.2 = 10$ K. The *interface layers* dominate, not the silicon, and this is a reason hybrid bonding runs cooler than microbump stacks. HBM stacks add several interfaces in series, so the bottom die can be noticeably hotter than the top.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Effective conductivity of bump layers depends on bump density, copper fill fraction and underfill. The 1 W/m·K figure is a rough illustration; check vendor or paper data for real structures.

Design responses: put the hottest die closest to the heat sink, add dummy thermal TSVs/copper, reduce activity in vertical hotspots, and throttle based on stack temperature.

## Power delivery and reliability

Every layer needs VDD and ground through the TSVs. A copper TSV 5 µm in diameter and 50 µm long has $R = \rho L/A = 1.7\times10^{-8}\times 50\times10^{-6}/(\pi (2.5\times10^{-6})^2) \approx 43$ mΩ, so 100 mA through it drops about 4 mV, and many TSVs in parallel are needed to feed amps. High current density in micro-bumps risks electromigration. And thermal expansion mismatch between thinned silicon, bumps and underfill stresses interconnects over temperature cycling.

## Yield and test

If a stack has $n$ dies, each with known-good probability $y$, stack yield is $y^n$ times assembly yield. Pre-stack testing matters: with $y=0.98$ and four dies, $0.98^4 = 92\%$, before assembly losses. Designs include design-for-test access (JTAG-like through TSVs) because inner dies are unreachable after bonding.

## 3D NAND: the largest 3D chip business

The most commercially successful "3D" memory is not die stacking at all. 3D NAND builds the storage cells vertically on a single wafer: alternating layers of oxide and nitride (or polysilicon) are deposited to a stack of hundreds of layers, then very deep holes (the channel holes) are etched through the entire stack and lined, by ALD and CVD, with charge-trap dielectric and a thin polysilicon channel. Each layer crossing a hole becomes one memory cell, so a string of, say, 100–200+ cells is a vertical NAND string. The wordlines at each layer are reached through a staircase edge. Capacity scales with the number of layers rather than with lateral shrink, which is why lithography pressure is lower than for planar flash, but the etch pressure is huge: aspect ratios of several tens to one over micrometre-deep stacks ([[q0662]]). Some designs bond the CMOS control circuitry wafer face-to-face to the memory wafer with hybrid bonds, which combines both meanings of 3D in one product.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Layer counts and string heights change every generation and differ between vendors; "hundreds of layers" is the current order of magnitude, not a specific product claim.

## A bandwidth estimate for a stacked-memory interface

HBM shows the bandwidth-density benefit. A 1024-bit interface that would need 1024 signal pins on a conventional package is made of 1024 microbump connections that fit in a strip a few millimetres wide on the base die. At 6.4 Gb/s per pin and 819 GB/s per stack, four stacks supply about 3.3 TB/s. Delivering the same with a conventional 64-bit DDR channel at, say, 6.4 Gb/s would need $3.3\times10^{12}\times8/(64\times6.4\times10^{9}) \approx 64$ channels, far more pins and board area than any package could offer. The short vertical and interposer connections are what allow a wide, slow, low-energy interface instead of a narrow, fast, power-hungry one.

## Putting it together

3D stacking is a bandwidth and energy-per-bit play. It pays off where a lot of data must move between dies, such as DRAM-to-processor or cache-to-core, and where the thermal budget allows. For power-hungry compute-on-compute stacking, thermals usually limit gains.
:::

::: applications
- **HBM** next to GPUs and AI accelerators: stacks of DRAM dies on a base die, joined by TSVs, connected to the processor via an interposer.
- **Stacked cache on CPUs**: an SRAM die hybrid-bonded on top of a CPU die adds cache without a larger 2D footprint.
- **Image sensors**: pixel array die stacked on a logic die (and sometimes DRAM) for fast readout.
- **3D NAND flash**: hundreds of layers of storage cells built vertically for density.
- **Phone SoCs with DRAM**: mostly PoP ([[q0647]]), but with some 3D integration in advanced parts.
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between 2.5D and 3D packaging?
In 2.5D, dies sit side by side on a silicon interposer or bridge that wires them together. In 3D, dies are stacked on top of each other with TSVs or hybrid bonds. GPUs with HBM are often both: the HBM stacks are 3D, and the stack-to-GPU connection is 2.5D.

### Why is heat the main problem in 3D chips?
Power density goes up because more active silicon shares one footprint, and heat from lower dies must conduct through upper dies and bonding layers, which have low conductivity. For example, 50 W through a 20 µm underfill-like layer at ~1 W/m·K over 100 mm² gives a 10 K rise in that layer alone.

### What is a TSV and what are its side effects?
A metal-filled hole through a thinned silicon die, typically a few µm in diameter. It adds capacitance (tens of fF), takes area, and creates mechanical stress that shifts nearby transistor characteristics, so designs use keep-out zones.

### How does hybrid bonding differ from microbumps?
Microbumps are solder or copper-pillar bumps with underfill, around 25-50 µm pitch. Hybrid bonding fuses copper pads and oxide directly, with pitch of about 10 µm or less. It gives 10× or more connection density, lower resistance and better thermal conduction, but needs very flat, clean surfaces and tight particle control.

### Scenario: a stacked memory-on-logic part throttles after 30 seconds of load. Where do you look?
Check which die has the hottest sensor and the thermal path from it. If the logic die is on the bottom and the memory above it, heat crosses the memory and its interface layers. Remedies include moving high-activity logic toward the heat sink side, adding thermal TSVs, improving the thermal interface material and lid, and tuning the throttle thresholds. Verify with thermal simulation and a transient thermal camera or sensor readings.
:::

The wording "3D chips" is ambiguous. Say which one you are answering and mention the others briefly. For chiplets arranged in 2.5D, see [[q0652]].
