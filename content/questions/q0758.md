::: testing
Whether you can **turn the rise-time equation into levers**, and notice that the question is ambiguous. "Increase rise time" literally means making the edge *slower*, but in context it almost certainly means "improve" (speed up) a rise time that is too slow. A strong answer says so in one sentence, then covers both directions:

1. The model: $t_r=0.8473R_pC_b$, so the knobs are $R_p$, $C_b$, and the driver.
2. The constraints on each knob (sink current, device count, EMI).
3. Beyond resistors: active pull-ups, buffers, current sources.
4. When you'd actually want a *slower* edge (ringing, EMI).
:::

::: why
Rise-time problems are the number-one I2C physical-layer failure, and the fix is almost never "use a smaller resistor and hope". Each knob has a cost, and a good engineer picks the cheapest one that meets the spec with margin. The question also tests communication: do you clarify wording before diving in?
:::

::: concepts
- **RC rise**: $t_r=0.8473R_pC_b$ (30–70%). See [[q0755]].
- **Bus capacitance**: what you're charging. See [[q0756]] and [[q0757]].
- **Sink-current limit**: how small $R_p$ may be. See [[q0755]] and [[q0760]].
- **Open-drain asymmetry**: falling edge is driven, rising edge is passive. See [[q0761]].
- **Active pull-up / current source**: a switched source that supplies charging current during the rise.
- **Slew control and EMI**: sometimes you slow edges deliberately. See [[q0011]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd first check which direction you mean, because literally 'increase the rise time' is making it slower. I'll assume you mean the common case: the rising edge is too slow and I want to speed it up, meaning shorten the rise time. And I'll mention how to do the opposite at the end.

The rise time is set by the pull-up charging the bus capacitance, about 0.85 times $R_p$ times $C_b$. So I have three levers. One, lower the pull-up resistance: halving it halves the rise time, but I'm limited by how much current the weakest device can sink, so $R_p$ can't go below $(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$, about 970 Ω at 3.3 V. Two, reduce the bus capacitance: shorten traces and cables, remove devices or low-capacitance protection parts, or put branches behind a mux. Three, if the resistor window is already closed, change the architecture: use a bus buffer with a rise-time accelerator, a current-source pull-up, or drop to a slower speed mode that allows a longer rise time.

If I actually wanted a slower edge, for example to cut ringing or EMI on a short, lightly loaded bus, I'd do the opposite: a bigger resistor, or a small series resistor and capacitor at the driver."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** The 30–70% rise through a pull-up resistor is $t_r=R_pC_b\ln(7/3)=0.8473R_pC_b$. Limits: 1000 ns (Standard), 300 ns (Fast), 120 ns (Fast-mode Plus).

**To make the edge faster (shorter $t_r$):**

| Lever | Effect | Limit / cost |
|---|---|---|
| Lower $R_p$ | $t_r\propto R_p$ | $R_p\ge(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$: about 967 Ω at 3.3 V, 3 mA; low-state current and power rise |
| Reduce $C_b$ | $t_r\propto C_b$ | Layout, fewer devices, low-cap ESD, shorter cable |
| Split the bus | each side sees its own $C_b$ | Mux, switch, or buffer adds a part |
| Active pull-up / rise-time accelerator | high current only during the edge: $t\approx C\Delta V/I$ | Extra IC; must not fight the pull-down |
| Current-source pull-up (HS-mode style) | linear ramp, e.g. 3 mA into 100 pF: 44 ns for the 30–70% swing | Needs a compatible master/buffer |
| Higher speed-grade devices (Fm+) | 20 mA sink allows $R_p\approx145$ Ω | Parts must support it |
| Lower $V_{DD}$ | lowers $R_{p,min}$ (e.g. 1.8 V: 480 Ω) | System constraint |
| Slower mode | allowed $t_r$ goes 120 → 300 → 1000 ns | Throughput |

**To make the edge slower (longer $t_r$)**, e.g. EMI or ringing on Fm+ stubs: raise $R_p$ within the window; add a small series resistor (22–100 Ω) near the driver; add a few pF with care; or use a device with edge-rate control. Don't exceed the spec's maximum $t_r$, and note that I2C also specifies a *minimum* fall-time for the output stage, so the drivers' own slew control is already there.

**Quick numbers (3.3 V, 100 pF).** 10 kΩ gives 847 ns; 4.7 kΩ gives 398 ns; 2.2 kΩ gives 186 ns; 1 kΩ gives 85 ns. At 400 pF a plain resistor can't meet 300 ns (needs 885 Ω, below the 967 Ω floor), but a 3 mA current source gives $400\text{ pF}\times1.32\text{ V}/3\text{ mA}=176$ ns.
:::

::: long
"First, the wording: 'increase the rise time' literally means slower, but in an I2C context people mean the edge is too slow and they want to improve it. So I'll answer that, and then say how to go the other way.

The edge on I2C is a passive RC charge. The pull-up resistor charges the bus capacitance, and the 30-to-70% rise time comes out to 0.85 times $R_p$ times $C_b$. So there are two direct knobs. First, lower the resistor. Halving it halves the rise time. The limit is the sink current of the weakest device: when it pulls the line low, the resistor current flows into it, so $R_p$ has to stay above $(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$, which is about 970 Ω at 3.3 V and 3 mA. Second, lower the capacitance: shorter traces, remove connectors or stubs, use low-capacitance ESD parts, and put heavy branches behind an I2C mux or buffer so the master drives less.

If those run out, so the minimum resistor still gives too slow an edge, I change the architecture. A bus buffer with a rise-time accelerator gives high current during the edge but not while the line is low. A current-source pull-up charges the line linearly: 3 mA into 400 pF would take only 176 ns for the 30-to-70% swing. Or I accept a slower speed mode, since Standard mode allows a microsecond.

Going the other way, for EMI or ringing on a short bus, I'd use a larger pull-up if the window allows, or a small series resistor at the driver. But I would be careful: I2C has a maximum rise time too, so I don't overdo it."
:::

::: terms
- **Rise time ($t_r$)**: 30%–70% of $V_{DD}$ in I2C. 1000/300/120 ns for Sm/Fm/Fm+.
- **$R_{p,min}$**: smallest pull-up allowed by sink current.
- **Rise-time accelerator**: circuit that adds a strong pull-up only during the rising edge.
- **Current-source pull-up**: constant-current charging; linear ramp, used in HS-mode masters.
- **I2C buffer / repeater**: splits a bus into segments and can add edge acceleration.
- **I2C switch/mux**: connects one downstream branch at a time.
- **Edge rate control**: driver slew limiting to reduce EMI/ringing.
- **Fast-mode Plus**: 1 MHz mode with 20 mA drivers and 120 ns rise limit.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use a smaller pull-up resistor".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Points out the wording ambiguity and covers both directions.
- Gives the equation and ranks levers by cost: layout and capacitance first, resistor next, active parts last.
- Quantifies the floor on $R_p$ and shows when no resistor works (885 Ω needed vs 967 Ω floor at 400 pF).
- Mentions accelerators, current sources, buffers and Fm+ drivers, and what they cost.
- Mentions that you wouldn't speed edges beyond what EMI allows, and that falls have a *minimum* time.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Just shrinking $R_p$ without checking $V_{OL}$.** The low level rises and you trade a rise-time bug for a logic-low bug. See [[q0760]].
- **Using internal pull-ups** as the "fix". They're far too weak.
- **Ignoring capacitance.** Often the cheapest improvement is removing a connector or a stub.
- **Forgetting that 10–90% ≠ 30–70%.** Scope measurements from the wrong thresholds look 2.6 times worse.
- **Adding accelerators on every segment.** Two accelerators on the same net can fight or oscillate; follow the buffer vendor's guidance.
- **Making the edge too fast** and getting ringing or EMI; I2C edges don't need to be sharp.
:::

::: deep
## Setting up the problem

The line voltage during a release is

$$v(t)=V_{DD}\left(1-e^{-t/(R_pC_b)}\right),\qquad t_r=0.8473R_pC_b$$

So $t_r$ is linear in both $R_p$ and $C_b$. Anything that scales either one scales the rise time by the same factor, which makes this one of the easiest design problems to reason about with ratios: halve $C_b$ and you've bought exactly what halving $R_p$ would, without the power cost.

## Lever 1: lower $R_p$ (cheap, but bounded)

Lowering $R_p$ speeds the edge proportionally but increases the current that a device must sink when pulling low: $I=(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/R_p$. With $R_p=1$ kΩ at 3.3 V that's 2.9 mA, right at the standard 3 mA limit. Below that, $V_{OL}$ rises above 0.4 V and eventually above $V_{IL}$ ([[q0760]]). Also, power burned while low goes up: $3.3\times2.9\text{ mA}=9.6$ mW per line.

So the usable range is bounded below by 967 Ω (3.3 V, 3 mA). If you're using Fast-mode Plus parts with a 20 mA sink, the floor is 145 Ω.

## Lever 2: reduce $C_b$ (the best lever when you can)

Capacitance is the cause; resistance is the compensation. If you can remove 50 pF from a 150 pF bus you gain a third of the rise time at zero power cost.

- Shorten traces and cables; every cm is about 1 pF ([[q0757]]).
- Remove stubs, unused footprints and test points on the bus.
- Replace high-capacitance protection with a 1 pF I2C-rated TVS.
- Put distant or optional branches behind a mux/switch so the master sees them only when needed.

## Lever 3: change the architecture

When $R_{p,min}>R_{p,max}$, no resistor works. At 3.3 V and 400 pF, Fast mode needs 885 Ω but the floor is 967 Ω. Options:

**Bus buffer / repeater.** The master sees $C_{b1}$, the far side sees $C_{b2}$, each with its own pull-up and each meeting the spec. Many buffers also add rise-time acceleration.

**Rise-time accelerator.** A comparator senses the line crossing a threshold (e.g. about 0.3$V_{DD}$) and switches on a stronger pull-up, which turns off near the top. The DC pull-up can then be large (low static power), while the edge sees a large current.

**Current-source pull-up.** Charging a capacitor with constant current $I$ gives a linear ramp: $t_{30\to70}=C_b(0.4V_{DD})/I$. With 400 pF, 3.3 V and 3 mA:

$$t=\frac{400\text{ pF}\times1.32\text{ V}}{3\text{ mA}}=176\text{ ns}$$

A resistor at the 967 Ω floor would give 328 ns on the same bus, i.e. 1.9 times slower. That's the principle of HS-mode (3.4 Mbit/s), where the master switches in a current-source pull-up during the high-speed phase.

**Slower mode.** If the system can tolerate throughput loss, Standard mode lets you use bigger resistors and a larger capacitance with a wide margin.

## Lever 4: run at a lower supply

$R_{p,min}=(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$ falls with $V_{DD}$ (480 Ω at 1.8 V with $V_{OL}=0.2V_{DD}$). The time for 30–70% doesn't depend on $V_{DD}$ itself, but the *allowed* $R_p$ gets lower, so the window widens on low-voltage buses.

## The opposite: slowing the edge on purpose

Sometimes you want slower edges: a very short bus with strong drivers can ring on Fast-mode Plus, or you're fighting EMI from an I2C line that runs near an antenna. Options:

- Use a bigger $R_p$ if it still meets the rise-time limit.
- Add a small series resistor (22–100 Ω) close to the driver: it forms an RC with the pin capacitance (for 100 Ω and 10 pF that's about 1 ns, so it only damps ringing, not the 100 ns edge).
- Add a small shunt capacitor (10–50 pF) with care: it's part of $C_b$ and eats into the same budget.
- Choose parts with slew-rate-limited outputs (the spec already requires a minimum fall time for Fm).

Note that none of these should push $t_r$ past the spec's maximum.

## A quick decision procedure

1. Measure $t_r$ at the far device (30–70%).
2. Compute $C_b=t_r/(0.8473R_p)$ and compare with expectation ([[q0756]]).
3. If $C_b$ is much bigger than expected, hunt for the extra picofarads.
4. If $C_b$ is as expected, compute $R_{p,max}$ and $R_{p,min}$; if the window is open, choose a lower $R_p$ with margin.
5. If the window is closed, split the bus, use an accelerator or current-source, or slow down.

> Lead with the clarification, then give the ranked list: capacitance first, resistor second, active parts third. It shows you prioritise by cost and don't reach for a component first.

::: applications
- **Fast-mode (400 kHz) buses with 3.3 V sensors and a connector**: 2.2 kΩ pull-ups and short traces.
- **Fast-mode Plus (1 MHz) on-board buses**: Fm+ rated parts with 20 mA sinks and 0.5 to 1 kΩ pull-ups.
- **Backplane or daughter-card links**: buffers with accelerators on each segment.
- **Hot-swap or long cable**: I2C buffer with rise-time acceleration and the ability to isolate capacitance.
- **EMI-sensitive designs**: slower edges via larger $R_p$ or series resistors where the spec allows.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Resistive pull-up, lumped $C_b$** for the basic equations.
- **Spec limits**: 1000/300/120 ns rise times (30–70%); check the specific devices.
- **$V_{OL}$ and $I_{OL}$** per the standard (0.4 V at 3 mA, 20 mA for Fm+).
- **Short bus.** Long cables add inductance and delay, and the lumped model starts to fail.

If the question is intentionally literal ("how do I make the edge slower?"), the answer is the "opposite" section: bigger $R_p$, series R, shunt C, slew-limited drivers, all within the spec maximum.

::: followups
### Why not just use a 500 Ω pull-up everywhere?
Because the device has to sink $(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/R_p$ when it pulls low. At 3.3 V and 500 Ω that's 5.8 mA, above the 3 mA that standard devices guarantee, so $V_{OL}$ would rise above 0.4 V. It also wastes power and can push the low level past $V_{IL}$. Fm+ parts with 20 mA drivers can take it.

### What does a rise-time accelerator do, and what could go wrong?
It senses the rising edge and switches on an extra pull-up current until the line is high. This beats the resistor trade-off because the DC pull-up stays large. Risks: two accelerators on one segment interact, or an accelerator fighting a slave that is still pulling low (the buffer has to detect that), so use parts designed for it and follow their layout notes.

### Why is the current-source pull-up faster for the same current?
A resistor delivers $(V_{DD}-v)/R_p$, which falls as the line rises (an exponential). A current source holds the current constant, so the average current over the edge is higher and the ramp is linear. For 400 pF, 3 mA gives 176 ns where a 967 Ω resistor gives 328 ns.

### If the bus is too slow only on SDA, what do you check?
SDA and SCL carry the same devices, so a difference points at something on one net only: a longer route, an extra ESD or level-shifter channel, different pull-up values (or a module that pulls up only one of them), or a probe on only one line. Compare $C_b$ computed from each net's measured $t_r$ and $R_p$ and look for the extra picofarads.

### How would you slow down the edge on a high-speed I2C line that's radiating?
First check whether the rise time is already close to its maximum; if there's room, increase $R_p$ slightly. Next add a 33–100 Ω series resistor at the driver. Last, reduce the bus length or add a ferrite. Slow edges lower the high-frequency content: the knee frequency is about $0.35/t_r$.
:::
