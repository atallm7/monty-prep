::: basics
### In plain words
A boost converter works in two phases that repeat every cycle. First the switch is on, and the inductor charges up from the input. Then the switch is off, and the inductor pushes its current through the diode into the output capacitor and load. A controller adjusts how long the switch stays on to hold the output steady.

### Picture it
Imagine a heavy flywheel connected to a pump. In phase one you spin the flywheel up, and meanwhile a storage tank feeds the customers. In phase two you release the flywheel, and it drives water into the tank, topping it up. Each cycle the flywheel gains and gives back the same amount of energy. The picture does not show the tank's small droop, which is the output ripple.

### Words you need
- **On phase**: the switch is closed, and the inductor current ramps up.
- **Off phase**: the switch is open, and the inductor current ramps down through the diode ([[q0270]]).
- **Continuous mode (CCM)**: the inductor current never reaches zero.
- **Discontinuous mode (DCM)**: the inductor current falls to zero before the cycle ends, usually at light load.
- **Ripple**: the small up-and-down swing in current or voltage.

### The simplest example
Take $V_{in}=5$ V, $V_{out}=10$ V, $L=10$ µH and 500 kHz, so $T=2$ µs. At $D=0.5$ the switch is on for 1 µs. The inductor sees 5 V, so the current rises at $5/10=0.5$ A/µs, a gain of 0.5 A. In the off phase it sees $5-10=-5$ V, so the current falls at 0.5 A/µs for 1 µs, a loss of 0.5 A. The gain and the loss match, so the cycle repeats.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the ripple formulas, the average currents in each part, and the boundary where the converter drops into discontinuous mode.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can narrate a switching cycle in the right order and tie each step to a physical law, not just recite "the inductor charges then discharges". The interviewer is checking:

1. You track **which element carries current in each phase** (inductor, switch, diode, capacitor).
2. You use $v_L = L\,di/dt$ correctly, including the *sign flip* in the off-state.
3. You know the output capacitor supplies the load during the on-time, and what that implies for ripple.
4. You can say what changes in CCM vs DCM and how the loop adjusts $D$ to regulate.

It is the boost counterpart of [[q0174]], so expect the interviewer to compare it with the buck.
:::

::: why
You will be asked to draw or debug switching waveforms in nearly any power-electronics interview or lab. If you can explain the boost cycle phase by phase, you can predict where to put the scope probes (SW node, inductor current, output ripple) and what each trace should look like when the converter is healthy. It also exposes real boost quirks that don't exist in a buck: the *output* current is pulsed (only flows in the off-time), so the output capacitor sees much harsher ripple current, and the control loop has a right-half-plane zero because raising the duty cycle first *reduces* the delivered current before it raises it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor equation** $v_L = L\,di_L/dt$: positive voltage ramps current up, negative ramps it down. See [[q0041]].
- **Continuity** of inductor current: the current at the end of the on-time is the starting value of the off-time. See [[q0043]].
- **Capacitor as charge reservoir**: the output capacitor supplies the load whenever the diode is off. See [[q0025]].
- **Volt-second balance**: net inductor volt-seconds over a period must be zero in steady state. See [[q0270]].
- **Diode behaviour**: conducts only when forward-biased, so it acts as a self-timed switch. See [[q0202]] for the buck analog.
- **CCM vs DCM**: whether $i_L$ stays above zero throughout the cycle. See [[q0229]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd split it into two phases. In the first phase the low-side switch is on. That connects the far end of the inductor to ground, so the whole input voltage sits across the inductor and its current ramps up at $V_{in}/L$. The diode is reverse-biased because the output is higher than the switch node, so the output capacitor alone is feeding the load and its voltage droops a little.

In the second phase the switch turns off. The inductor current can't stop, so the switch node rises until the diode conducts. Now the inductor current flows through the diode into the output capacitor and the load. The inductor voltage is $V_{in}-V_{out}$, which is negative, so the current ramps down. The capacitor gets recharged, and its ripple is set by that pulsed current.

At steady state the current ramps up and down by the same amount each cycle, which gives $V_{out}=V_{in}/(1-D)$. The controller senses the output through a feedback divider and nudges the duty cycle up or down to hold it. At light load the inductor current can reach zero before the cycle ends, which is discontinuous mode, and the switch node starts to ring."
:::

::: technical
Take a non-synchronous boost (switch $Q$, diode $D$), CCM, steady state, ideal parts.

**State 1: $Q$ on, $0<t<DT$.**
- $V_{SW}\approx0$ (neglecting $I R_{DS(on)}$). $v_L=V_{in}$.
- $\dfrac{di_L}{dt}=\dfrac{V_{in}}{L}$, so $\Delta i_{L,on}=\dfrac{V_{in}DT}{L}$.
- $D$ blocks with $V_{out}$ across it. Load current comes from $C_{out}$: $\Delta V_{out,on}\approx -\dfrac{I_{out}DT}{C_{out}}$.
- Switch current equals inductor current.

**State 2: $Q$ off, $DT<t<T$.**
- $V_{SW}=V_{out}+V_F$, $v_L=V_{in}-V_{out}-V_F$ (negative).
- $\dfrac{di_L}{dt}=-\dfrac{V_{out}+V_F-V_{in}}{L}$.
- Diode current $=i_L$. $C_{out}$ receives $i_L-I_{out}$, so it charges.

**Steady state.** $\Delta i_{L,on}=|\Delta i_{L,off}|$ gives $V_{out}=\dfrac{V_{in}}{1-D}$ (ideal). Average currents: $\bar i_L=I_{in}=\dfrac{I_{out}}{1-D}$, $\bar i_D=I_{out}$, $\bar i_Q=\dfrac{D\,I_{out}}{1-D}$.

**Ripple.**
$$\Delta I_L=\frac{V_{in}D}{Lf_{sw}},\qquad \Delta V_{out}\approx\frac{I_{out}D}{C_{out}f_{sw}}\ (+\ \text{ESR step } I_{pk}R_{ESR})$$

**CCM/DCM boundary.** $i_L$ just touches zero when $\bar i_L=\Delta I_L/2$, i.e. $I_{out,crit}=\dfrac{V_{in}D(1-D)}{2Lf_{sw}}$. Below this load current the converter runs in DCM, the SW node rings during the dead time, and the gain becomes load dependent, so the loop must change $D$ to hold $V_{out}$.

**Regulation.** A feedback divider scales $V_{out}$ to the reference ($V_{ref}\approx$ 0.6 to 1.25 V on most parts). The error amplifier moves $D$ to hold it. Because the output current is delivered only during $(1-D)T$, increasing $D$ first starves the output for one more moment before the larger inductor current arrives, a non-minimum-phase response (right-half-plane zero, [[q0274]]).
:::

::: short
"With the switch on, the inductor sits across $V_{in}$, current ramps up at $V_{in}/L$, the diode blocks, and the output capacitor feeds the load. When the switch turns off, the inductor current has to continue, so the switch node rises, the diode conducts, and the inductor delivers current into the output, now ramping down because the voltage across it is $V_{in}-V_{out}$. In steady state the up and down ramps match, giving $V_{out}=V_{in}/(1-D)$, and the controller trims $D$ using the feedback divider."
:::

::: long
"I'll walk through one cycle of a standard non-synchronous boost: an inductor from the input to the switch node, a low-side MOSFET from the switch node to ground, a diode from the switch node to the output, and the output capacitor.

Phase one, switch on. The switch node is pulled to ground, so the inductor has the full $V_{in}$ across it. $v=L\,di/dt$ means the current ramps up linearly with slope $V_{in}/L$. The diode is reverse biased by $V_{out}$, so nothing flows to the output, and the load is fed entirely by the output capacitor, which droops slightly.

Phase two, switch off. The inductor current can't change instantly, so the switch node flies up until the diode turns on, at roughly $V_{out}$ plus a diode drop. Now the inductor is connected between $V_{in}$ and $V_{out}$, which means $v_L=V_{in}-V_{out}$, negative. The current ramps *down*, and that current goes into the output capacitor and the load. The capacitor recharges.

In steady state the current has to come back to where it started, so the up-slope times $DT$ equals the down-slope times $(1-D)T$, and that gives $V_{out}=V_{in}/(1-D)$. Average inductor current equals the input current, which is $I_{out}/(1-D)$.

Things I'd point out: the diode current is pulsed, so the output cap sees a nasty ripple current with a sharp edge, and ESR matters. At light load the inductor current reaches zero, that's DCM, and the node rings. The controller closes the loop by sensing $V_{out}$ through a divider and adjusting $D$, but boosts have a right-half-plane zero, so the loop has to be slower than a comparable buck."
:::

::: terms
- **On-time / off-time**: $DT$ and $(1-D)T$ for the low-side switch.
- **Ramp slopes**: $+V_{in}/L$ on, $-(V_{out}-V_{in})/L$ off (ideal).
- **Pulsed output current**: diode (output) current flows only in the off-time.
- **Ripple current / ripple voltage**: $\Delta I_L$ in the inductor and $\Delta V_{out}$ on the output.
- **Critical conduction**: the boundary where $i_L$ just reaches zero at the end of the off-time.
- **DCM ringing**: the SW node oscillates at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{L C_{sw}})$ once the diode current hits zero.
- **RHP zero**: a zero in the right half-plane in the control-to-output transfer function that limits loop bandwidth.
- **Feedback divider**: scales $V_{out}$ to $V_{ref}$: $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "inductor charges, then discharges into the load".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names *which* element supplies the load in each phase (capacitor during on, inductor via diode during off).
- Gives the slopes $V_{in}/L$ and $-(V_{out}-V_{in})/L$ and uses them to derive the gain.
- Spots that output current is **pulsed** and what that does to ripple and capacitor choice (ESR, RMS ripple rating).
- Describes both CCM and DCM and gives the critical load current.
- Mentions the RHP zero and what it means for loop bandwidth.
- Puts a number on it: 3.7 V to 5 V at 1 A, $D=0.26$, $\Delta I_L=0.41$ A, $I_{in}=1.35$ A.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying the load is fed by the inductor in both phases.** Only in the off-time. In the on-time the diode is off and the capacitor does it all.
- **Drawing the inductor voltage as positive in the off-state.** It is $V_{in}-V_{out}$, negative, and that is why the current falls.
- **Ignoring that the diode current is pulsed.** Average is $I_{out}$ but the peak is $I_{pk}\approx I_{out}/(1-D)+\Delta I_L/2$. Size the diode's repetitive peak current and the capacitor's ripple rating for it.
- **Assuming CCM always.** At light load the converter drops into DCM (or PFM/burst) and the equations change.
- **Mixing up the buck and boost output ripple.** Buck output current is continuous (inductor in series with the output), so ripple is small and set by $\Delta I_L$. Boost output current is discontinuous, so ripple is larger for the same $L$, $C$.
- **Expecting the output to start at 0 V.** Before the switch ever runs, $C_{out}$ is charged to roughly $V_{in}-V_F$ through the inductor and diode, so the output starts at about $V_{in}$ minus a drop, and an inrush current flows ([[q0276]]).
:::

## Output ripple and why a boost is harder on its capacitor

In a buck the inductor feeds the output continuously, so the capacitor only sees the small inductor ripple. In a boost the capacitor has to deliver the **entire** load current during the on-time with *nothing* coming in:
$$\Delta V_{C}\approx\frac{I_{out}DT}{C_{out}}=\frac{1\times0.52\ \mu}{22\ \mu}=23.6\text{ mV}$$
Add the ESR step at turn-off. The capacitor current jumps by roughly $i_{L,pk}=1.56$ A, so a 10 mΩ ESR adds about $1.56\times0.010\approx16$ mV of step. With a 2 A load the capacitive part doubles. The capacitor's RMS ripple current is large too: $I_{C,rms}\approx I_{out}\sqrt{D/(1-D)}=1\times\sqrt{0.26/0.74}=0.59$ A for this example (ignoring inductor ripple), and it grows rapidly with $D$. At $D=0.75$ it is $1.73\times I_{out}$.

## Continuous vs discontinuous conduction

If the load current falls below $I_{out,crit}=\dfrac{V_{in}D(1-D)}{2Lf_{sw}}=\dfrac{3.7\times0.26\times0.74}{2\times4.7\mu\times500k}=0.15$ A, the inductor current reaches zero before the end of the period. In a non-synchronous converter the diode then blocks, so the inductor is disconnected and the SW node rings at $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_{sw}})$ with the parasitic capacitance of the switch, diode and layout. The ringing is not a fault but it is a noise source. Also, in DCM the gain is $V_{out}/V_{in}=\tfrac12\left(1+\sqrt{1+\tfrac{4D^2}{K}}\right)$ with $K=2L f_{sw}/R_{load}$ (standard result, e.g. Erickson & Maksimovic), so output voltage rises with $D$ *and* falls with load. A voltage-mode controller must reduce $D$ at light load or the output runs away upward, which is why many controllers go to pulse-skipping or burst mode there.

## Regulation and the right-half-plane zero

When the load steps up, the controller increases $D$. What happens in the very next cycle? The diode is conducting for a *shorter* time, so less charge is delivered to the output, and $V_{out}$ initially *dips further* before the larger inductor current takes hold. The transfer function from duty cycle to output has a right-half-plane zero at $\omega_{RHPZ}=\dfrac{R_{load}(1-D)^2}{L}$. For our example with $R_{load}=5\ \Omega$: $f_{RHPZ}=\dfrac{5\times0.74^2}{2\pi\times4.7\ \mu}=92.7$ kHz. The usual rule of thumb is to set the loop crossover below roughly one third to one fifth of that, so about 20 to 30 kHz for this converter. That's far lower than a buck at the same switching frequency, and the reason boost loops respond more slowly. Peak-current-mode control, which regulates inductor current directly, helps but does not remove the RHPZ.

## Startup

Before the controller switches at all, $C_{out}$ charges through $L$ and $D$ up to about $V_{in}-V_F$, producing an inrush spike limited only by the inductor, the diode and the source impedance. Parts add soft-start and input current limit to control the rest. A boost with no load switch cannot isolate the output: a short on $V_{out}$ is also a short across the battery through $L$ and $D$. Details are in [[q0276]].
:::

::: applications
- **Single-cell Li-ion to 5 V** (phones, speakers, handheld): the cycle above, with synchronous rectification to cut diode loss.
- **White-LED strings**: boost holds the string current constant while the supply sags.
- **PFC front ends**: continuous-conduction or critical-conduction boosts shape the input current into a sine in phase with the line voltage.
- **Solar and fuel-cell interfaces**: boost keeps the DC bus up when the source voltage swings.
- **IC supply rails**: small boost ICs make 5 V, 12 V or 15 V rails from a battery for sensors and analog front ends.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Non-synchronous, CCM, ideal** components. A synchronous boost replaces the diode with a MOSFET and the rectifier conduction loss drops, but needs dead-time and anti-reverse-current logic at light load.
- **Constant frequency PWM**. Variable-frequency or hysteretic parts (like many small boost ICs) change the details but not the two-phase story.
- **Output capacitor ESR** assumed small. A bigger ESR adds a step at the transitions.
- **Voltage-mode versus current-mode control** changes how the loop is closed, not the power-stage waveforms.
- The figures use the *ideal* gain. Real converters need slightly more duty cycle because of $V_F$, $R_{DS(on)}$ and DCR.
:::

::: followups
### What does the inductor current look like at light load?
It falls until the minimum touches zero, which happens at $I_{out,crit}=V_{in}D(1-D)/(2Lf_{sw})\approx0.15$ A in our example. Below that the current is a triangle that returns to zero partway through the off-time, then stays at zero, and the SW node rings. Efficiency at light load suffers from fixed losses, so many controllers drop to PFM or burst mode.

### Which element carries what average current?
Inductor: $I_{in}=I_{out}/(1-D)=1.35$ A. Diode: $I_{out}=1$ A (so a Schottky with $V_F=0.4$ V loses 0.4 W). Switch: $DI_{out}/(1-D)=0.35$ A average, but with RMS about $I_{in}\sqrt D=0.69$ A for conduction loss. Output capacitor: zero average, 0.59 A RMS.

### Why does the output ripple get worse as duty cycle increases?
The capacitor has to supply $I_{out}$ for the on-time $DT$ with no input. $\Delta V=I_{out}DT/C$ grows linearly with $D$, and the RMS current grows as $\sqrt{D/(1-D)}$. At $D=0.75$ the on-time ripple is about 2.9 times the $D=0.26$ value for the same $I_{out}$, $C$ and $f$.

### What would happen if the inductor value were too small?
The ripple $\Delta I_L=V_{in}D/(Lf_{sw})$ gets large, the peak current may exceed the switch's current limit or saturate the inductor ([[q0046]]), the boundary load for CCM moves up (more time in DCM), and RMS losses rise. A tiny $L$ with a high $D$ can even trip cycle-by-cycle limit and cause the output to fall out of regulation.

### Scenario: on the scope the SW node shows 5 V pulses but the output sits at 3.3 V and the input current is huge. Diagnose.
The switch node is behaving but the output isn't building. Suspects: the inductor is saturating (current runaway, check peak current and $I_{sat}$), a shorted or very leaky output capacitor or load pulling the output down, a diode that is open (then SW would fly far above 5 V at turn-off, into the switch's avalanche, not sit at a clean 5 V) or installed backwards (it conducts from the output into SW whenever the switch is on, dumping $C_{out}$ through the switch and drawing large current), or the controller current limit holding duty down. Measure the inductor current directly (low-value sense resistor or current probe) and compare against $I_{sat}$.
