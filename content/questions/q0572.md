::: testing
Whether you can place shielding where it pays off, with an engineer's view of the whole board:

1. **Over the right thing**: the strongest or most localised source, or the most sensitive victim (and which one to choose).
2. **With the right boundaries**: ground ring and via fence, with the PCB planes forming the floor and inner walls.
3. **Handling the crossings**: signals and supplies that cross the shield wall.
4. **Avoiding side effects**: antennas, inductors, crystals, heat, test access and cavity resonances.
:::

::: why
Shields are expensive in board area and cost, and they only work if they surround the right thing and are built as a closed cage. A can placed over the wrong block, or with an unfiltered trace running through it, does nothing. Layout reviews routinely catch these.
:::

::: concepts
- **Source vs victim shielding**: contain the source, protect the victim. See [[q0569]].
- **Cage = can + ground plane + via fence**. See [[q0571]].
- **Partitioning**: noisy and quiet zones on the board. See [[q0483]].
- **Crossings must be filtered or routed through the floor**.
- **Cavity resonance**: a can is a small metal cavity with resonant modes. See [[q0570]].
- **Keep-outs for antennas and inductors**.
:::

::: answer
"I place a shield over whichever block is cheapest to isolate and that matters most: usually the RF section (transmitter or receiver) and any localised noisy source that I can't fix at its origin, like a switching regulator, a processor and memory cluster or a crystal oscillator and PLL. If one noisy source affects many victims I shield the source; if one sensitive circuit sees many aggressors I shield the victim.

Layout-wise, the can goes over a closed ring of ground pads, stitched to the ground plane, and I extend the wall down through the PCB with a via fence on every ground layer so the planes and the vias act as the walls and floor of a cage. Signals entering or leaving go on an inner layer under the wall, with a filter, like a ferrite or feed-through capacitor, or I use feed-through pads. Nothing unfiltered crosses.

I keep the antenna and its keep-out outside any can, keep tall parts and inductors clear of the lid and walls, leave room for pick-and-place, and put test points outside or use a frame and lid. I check the can height against the tallest part and consider heat. And one noisy source per can, or divide the can with an internal wall, so a compartment doesn't couple one source into another."
:::

::: technical
**Where.**
1. *Intentional radiators and sensitive receivers*: RF front end, PA, LNA, VCO/PLL and crystal; shield them and keep the antenna outside.
2. *Localised noise sources*: switching regulator (and its inductor, if unshielded), processor/DDR cluster, display bridge, high-speed transceivers.
3. *Sensitive analog*: µV sensors, references, high-impedance amplifiers.
4. *Between* noise and victim when they can't be separated: a wall or divider (frame with partitions).

**How (cage construction).**
- Closed ground ring under the wall, vias into the ground plane. Via spacing $\le\lambda/20$ (6.25 mm at 2.4 GHz; 3 mm at 5 GHz).
- Extend the wall through the stack with via fences on inner ground layers where signals run, so fields between planes can't leak under the wall.
- Solid ground plane beneath the shielded area, no slots.
- Clearance between components and the wall (vendor-specific, commonly of the order of 0.5 to 1 mm or more) and under the lid; check the tallest part.

**Crossings.** Route entering/exiting signals on an inner layer *under* the wall, add a ferrite bead, RC/LC filter or feed-through capacitor at the boundary; supplies get a pi filter. Every unfiltered conductor crossing a wall carries noise across.

**Don't shield / avoid.**
- The antenna and its matching network (shielding detunes it).
- Close to inductors: a shield near an inductor reduces $L$ and adds eddy-current loss.
- A shield over a crystal changes its load capacitance slightly.
- Heat-generating parts without a thermal path.

**Cavity resonance.** A can behaves as a resonant cavity: lowest rectangular mode $f_{101}=\tfrac{c}{2}\sqrt{1/a^2+1/b^2}$ (air-filled, empty): 10.6 GHz for 20×20 mm, 5.3 GHz for 40×40 mm. Components lower it. A cavity resonance near a radio's band can amplify noise inside; use smaller compartments or absorber.

**Placement logistics.** Pick-and-place pad, test points outside, rework access (frame + lid), airflow.
:::

::: short
"I put the shield over the block that's most localised and matters most: the RF section, or a noisy source like a switching regulator or processor and memory, and sometimes sensitive analog. It sits on a closed ground ring, stitched to the plane with a via fence so the planes form the floor and walls of a cage. Anything crossing the wall gets filtered or goes through an inner layer. I keep the antenna outside, watch clearance to parts and inductors, put test points outside, and keep compartments small so cavity resonances stay out of the band."
:::

::: long
"First I decide what to shield. If one noisy source affects several victims, I shield the source: a switching regulator with its inductor, a processor and memory cluster, a clock generator. If one sensitive circuit is hit by several aggressors, like a GPS front end or a microvolt sensor amplifier, I shield the victim. For RF circuits I usually do both, one can over the transmitter, a separate one over the receiver. And I keep one noisy source per compartment, or put a wall between them.

Second, how I build it. The can sits on a closed ring of ground copper, with ground vias into the plane with spacing under a twentieth of a wavelength, 6 millimetres at 2.4 GHz. Under the can the ground plane is solid. I carry the wall through the board with a via fence on the inner ground layers where signals are routed, so the can plus the planes plus the fence is really a closed cage.

Third, the crossings. Every wire that enters or leaves is a hole in the cage. Signals route on an inner layer, pass under the wall and get filtered at the boundary with a ferrite, an RC or a feed-through capacitor; supplies get a pi filter.

Fourth, what not to shield and what to leave clear. The antenna stays outside the can with its keep-out, because a shield detunes it. Inductors need clearance because a nearby conductor lowers the inductance and adds loss. Tall parts have to clear the lid, I check the thermal path, and I leave test points outside or use a frame and lid for rework.

And I keep an eye on cavity resonance: a 40 by 40 millimetre can resonates near 5.3 GHz, which could be right in a Wi-Fi band, so I might split it with a partition."
:::

::: terms
- **Shield footprint**: ground pad ring and keep-out on the PCB for a can.
- **Via fence**: row of ground vias forming a wall through the PCB.
- **Compartment / partition**: internal wall of a shield frame separating zones.
- **Feed-through capacitor**: capacitor that passes a conductor through the shield wall and shunts noise to the shield.
- **Cavity resonance**: resonant mode of the shield's enclosed volume.
- **Keep-out**: area where no components or copper may be placed (for example antenna).
- **Frame and lid**: two-piece shield allowing access.
- **Aperture**: opening that lets fields escape.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "over the noisy or sensitive circuit".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives a rule for source vs victim shielding and says to use one source per compartment.
- Builds the cage properly: ground ring, via fence through inner layers, solid plane, $\lambda/20$ spacing.
- Treats crossings as the main weakness and describes how to filter them.
- Knows what to keep out of the can (antenna) and what to keep clear (inductors, crystals, hot parts).
- Mentions cavity resonance and a practical formula; plans test access and rework.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the antenna or its matching network under the can.** It is detuned and shielded from the world.
- **Running unfiltered traces across the wall** on the top layer, defeating the shield.
- **Fence on the surface only**, with inner-layer signals able to leak out through the planar gap under the wall.
- **Putting two unrelated noise sources and a sensitive circuit in one can.** The can couples them via cavity modes.
- **Inadequate clearance to tall parts or inductors**, causing shorts or detuned coils.
- **No test access**, so tuning or debug requires removing the can.
:::

::: deep
## What and where: a decision rule

The question to ask first is "if this circuit is the problem, is it the source or the victim, and which is easier to isolate?"

| Situation | Shield | Why |
|---|---|---|
| One strong localised source, many victims | The source | One can protects everything |
| One sensitive circuit, many aggressors | The victim | One can protects it from all |
| Source and victim must be adjacent | Both or a partition wall | Wall between them reduces coupling |
| Noise is on a cable | Not a can; filter and bond the cable ([[q0571]]) | The cable is the antenna |
| Antenna | Never inside | Shields detune and block it |

## Construct it as a cage

A can alone is a lid. The cage is: lid + ground ring + via fence + solid plane.

1. **Ground ring and vias.** Closed ring of exposed copper under the wall (soldered 360°), vias to the plane spaced $\le\lambda/20$ ($\lambda$ in the PCB dielectric is shorter, so use some margin).
2. **Inner-layer fence.** If signals are routed in inner layers that run under or near the wall, the plane-to-plane gaps would leak. Extend the via fence so every ground plane connects around the perimeter.
3. **Plane under the can** solid, no slots; keep signals referenced to it.
4. **Clearances.** Keep parts at least the vendor's minimum from the wall (a pick-and-place and solder-fillet tolerance; typically of the order of a millimetre), and clear of the lid by enough to avoid shorting. Check tolerance stack and tallest component.

## Handle the crossings

Signals and supplies need to cross. Options, in order of preference:

- **Inner layer under the wall, filtered at the boundary**: a ferrite bead and a small capacitor, or a pi filter for supplies; the filter sits at the wall, with its ground connection to the fence.
- **Feed-through capacitor** or filtered connector pin through a metal wall.
- **Optical or isolated link** for extreme cases.
- **Slow signals and DC** can pass through simple RC filters; RF lines through a microstrip that exits under the wall (it still radiates a little), or via a coax-like feed.

An unfiltered trace crossing the wall carries noise out and acts as an antenna on the outside.

## Don'ts and side effects

- **Antenna**: out of the can, with its keep-out; the shield next to a patch or PCB antenna changes tuning, so tune the antenna with the can in place.
- **Inductors**: a conductive lid or wall close to an inductor reduces its inductance and adds loss. Keep clear by a few times the inductor height, or use a shielded inductor.
- **Crystals**: the lid adds a little capacitance; verify frequency/load.
- **Heat**: a can traps heat over regulators and processors. Add a thermal pad to the lid or vents (small holes) and recheck the junction temperature.
- **Test and rework**: test points outside, or frame + lid.

## Cavity resonance

A metal can with the PCB floor is a rectangular cavity with modes at

$$f_{mnp}=\frac{c}{2}\sqrt{\left(\frac{m}{a}\right)^2+\left(\frac{n}{b}\right)^2+\left(\frac{p}{d}\right)^2}.$$

For the lowest mode in a thin can ($m$ = n = 1, $p$ = 0) with $a = b$: 20 mm: 10.6 GHz; 30 mm: 7.1 GHz; 40 mm: 5.3 GHz (empty, air-filled). Components and dielectric lower these. A resonance near 5 GHz can amplify noise inside the can or raise coupling between circuits; fix by splitting into compartments, adding absorber on the lid, or changing the dimensions.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The cavity formula assumes an empty, perfectly conducting rectangular box. A populated can has lower, shifted and damped resonances. Treat the numbers as an order-of-magnitude guide or simulate for critical designs.

## Worked example: Wi-Fi + PMIC board

A 4-layer wearable has a Wi-Fi/BT module, an application processor with LPDDR and a buck PMIC. Pre-compliance shows 2.4 GHz desense with the processor active. Plan: a can over the processor/DDR (source), the module's own can left in place (victim, with its antenna keep-out outside), a ferrite and capacitor on the display flex at the board edge, a via fence of 3 mm pitch around the processor can, inner-layer routing under the wall for the few external signals with ferrites, and test points outside. Resulting isolation: can 20–30 dB plus flex filtering 10 dB, enough to bring I/N below −10 dB in this example (illustrative; verify by measurement).
:::

::: applications
- **Phones, tablets, wearables**: cans over processors, PMICs, RF front ends and charger ICs, with compartment walls.
- **Wi-Fi/BT/GNSS modules**: integrated can with an antenna keep-out outside.
- **Switch-mode power modules**: shielded section for converter and inductor.
- **Test and measurement instruments**: partitioned compartments for analog front ends and clocks.
- **Automotive telematics**: cans for the modem and GNSS, filtered feed-throughs for harness lines.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Shield effectiveness is limited by seams and crossings**; the placement advice assumes they are handled.
- **λ/20 spacing** is computed in free space; in FR-4 dielectric near the surface the effective wavelength is shorter, so use margin.
- **Cavity resonance values** are for empty ideal cavities.
- **Vendor clearances** vary; check the shield maker's design guide for pad width, keep-out and height tolerances.
- **Alternative placement**: shielding at enclosure level (conductive coating or metal case) when many blocks need protection, or absorber sheets for local cavity damping.
:::

::: followups
### Should I shield the source or the victim?
Whichever is cheaper to isolate, and whichever has fewer partners. A single noisy regulator with several victims is shielded at the source. A single GPS front end with many aggressors is shielded at the victim. For RF circuits, I use both: the transmitter and receiver each get a compartment so they don't couple to each other or to the digital area.

### How do you route a signal into a shielded area?
On an inner layer, under the wall, over a solid reference plane, with a filter at the boundary: a ferrite bead and a small capacitor to the fence, or a feed-through capacitor, or a pi filter for supplies. For RF signals the line needs controlled impedance and might use a short microstrip under the wall; keep the opening in the wall as small as possible and surrounded by vias.

### Why not put the antenna inside the shield?
The shield would block it and detune it. Antennas need a clear space (keep-out) with no metal nearby, and a ground layout that matches the antenna design. The can covers the circuits; the antenna sits beside it, connected through a controlled-impedance feed that crosses the wall at one place.

### How big can a shield can be before cavity resonances matter?
Rule of thumb: the lowest mode is about $(c/2)\sqrt{1/a^2+1/b^2}$. A 40×40 mm can resonates around 5.3 GHz, within the Wi-Fi band, and 20×20 mm around 10.6 GHz. Partition large cans or add absorber if a mode falls within a radio's band; components reduce it, so simulate if it matters.

### Scenario: you added a can over the buck regulator and its efficiency dropped and inductor ran hot. What happened?
The lid was too close to the inductor: eddy currents in the metal added loss and lowered the inductance, increasing ripple current and saturation risk. Fix with clearance (taller can or a recess), a shielded inductor, or shielding the converter IC only and leaving the inductor outside with its own shielded construction; also check the thermal path of the can.
:::
