::: testing
The interviewer wants the answer **"source and body"** and, more importantly, the *why*. The hidden sub-skills:

1. You know the MOSFET is really a **4-terminal** device and that the body forms PN junctions with source and drain that must never forward-bias.
2. You can explain the **body effect**: if the source rises above the body, the threshold voltage goes up.
3. You know the tie is a **design choice with consequences**: in discrete parts it creates the body diode (so a single MOSFET blocks only one direction), and in ICs you often *can't* tie them because NMOS devices share a substrate.
4. Bonus: you recognise the other common "two terminals tied together" pattern, **gate-drain** (diode-connected), and can explain it too.
:::

::: why
This question links device physics to everyday design. The source-body tie is why a power MOSFET has a body diode, which drives reverse-polarity protection, battery-disconnect design with back-to-back FETs, dead-time losses in converters and level-shifter behaviour. In IC design, *not* being able to tie source and body is why stacked transistors, source followers and pass gates lose headroom through the body effect, and why designers pay for isolated wells or triple-well processes. Answering well shows you understand the transistor below the 3-pin abstraction.
:::

::: concepts
- **Four terminals**: gate, drain, source, body. The body is the silicon the channel forms in. See [[q0090]].
- **PN junctions inside the MOSFET**: body-source and body-drain are diodes. Forward-biasing either one breaks normal operation. See [[q0063]].
- **Threshold voltage**: the gate voltage needed to invert the surface. It depends on how much depletion charge the gate must first support. See [[q0096]].
- **Body effect**: $V_t$ rises with source-to-body reverse bias $V_{SB}$.
- **Body diode**: the drain-body junction appears between drain and source once the body is tied to the source. See [[q0101]].
- **Diode-connected transistor**: gate tied to drain, the building block of current mirrors. See [[q0104]].
:::

::: answer
"Usually it's the source and the body. On basically every discrete MOSFET they're shorted together inside the package, which is why you only see three pins.

There are two reasons. First, the body forms a PN junction with the source and another with the drain, and those have to stay reverse-biased or at zero. Tying the body to the source guarantees the source-body junction can never forward-bias. Second, it kills the body effect. The threshold voltage depends on the source-to-body voltage: if the source rises above the body, the depletion region under the gate widens, the gate has to support more charge, and $V_t$ goes up. Tie them together, $V_{SB}$ is zero, and $V_t$ stays at its nominal value.

The side effect is the body diode. Once the body is on the source, the body-drain junction becomes a diode from source to drain in an NFET, so a single MOSFET only blocks voltage in one direction. That's why battery protection circuits use two FETs back to back.

In ICs it's different. All the NMOS transistors usually share the P-substrate tied to ground, so a stacked NFET can't have its body on its source, and you just design around the body effect.

The other pair people tie is gate and drain, which makes a diode-connected transistor for current mirrors."
:::

::: technical
**Source-body tie (the standard answer).**

The body forms two PN junctions: body-source and body-drain. For NMOS (P body), they stay off if $V_B \le V_S$ and $V_B \le V_D$. Tying $B$ to $S$:

1. Guarantees $V_{BS} = 0$, so the body-source junction can't forward-bias (no injection, no parasitic NPN turn-on, no latch-up trigger from that junction).
2. Removes the **body effect**:
$$V_t = V_{t0} + \gamma\left(\sqrt{2\phi_F + V_{SB}} - \sqrt{2\phi_F}\right),\qquad \gamma = \frac{\sqrt{2q\varepsilon_{Si}N_A}}{C_{ox}}$$
With $\gamma = 0.4\ \sqrt{\text{V}}$ and $2\phi_F = 0.7$ V: $\Delta V_t$ = 0.19 V at $V_{SB} = 1$ V and 0.32 V at 2 V.
3. Removes the back-gate transconductance $g_{mb} = \eta\, g_m$, $\eta = \frac{\gamma}{2\sqrt{2\phi_F+V_{SB}}}$ (typically 0.1–0.3), which otherwise reduces source-follower gain to $\approx \frac{1}{1+\eta}$.

**Consequence: body diode.** With $B = S$, the remaining body-drain junction is a diode S→D (NMOS) or D→S (PMOS). A single MOSFET therefore blocks only $V_{DS} > 0$ (NMOS). Bidirectional blocking requires two devices in series, common-source or common-drain ("back-to-back").

**Why it's automatic in discrete parts.** Vertical power MOSFETs short the P-body to the N+ source with the source metal so that the parasitic NPN (N+ source / P body / N drift) has its base-emitter shorted and can't turn on during fast $dV/dt$ or avalanche.

**In ICs.** NMOS bodies share the P-substrate (at the lowest supply), PMOS bodies sit in N-wells (at $V_{DD}$, or tied to their source if the well is separate). Stacked NMOS, source followers and NMOS pass gates see $V_{SB} > 0$. Triple-well or SOI processes allow per-device body ties at extra cost.

**Alternative pair: gate-drain (diode-connected).** With $V_{GS} = V_{DS}$, $V_{DS} > V_{GS} - V_t$ always holds, so the device is always in saturation when on: $I_D = \tfrac{1}{2}k_n(V_{GS} - V_t)^2$. Small-signal it looks like a resistor $\approx 1/g_m$. This is the reference side of a current mirror.
:::

::: short
"Source and body. Discrete MOSFETs short them internally. It keeps the source-body PN junction from ever forward-biasing, and it removes the body effect: if the source sits above the body, the threshold voltage goes up, so with $V_{SB} = 0$ the threshold stays put. The cost is the body diode from source to drain, so one FET only blocks one direction. In ICs, NMOS bodies usually share the grounded substrate, so you often can't tie them and have to live with the body effect."
:::

::: long
"The two terminals that are usually connected are the source and the body. On a discrete MOSFET it's done inside the package, which is why the part has three pins instead of four.

To see why, picture an N-channel device. The body is P-type silicon, and the source and drain are N-type regions sitting in it. So there are two PN junctions in there, body-to-source and body-to-drain, and for the transistor to work, both have to be reverse-biased or at zero. If the body ever goes above the source by a diode drop, you inject current, you can turn on the parasitic NPN formed by source, body and drain, and in an IC you can trigger latch-up. Tying the body to the source makes that junction permanently zero-biased.

The second reason is the body effect. The threshold voltage is the gate voltage needed to invert the surface, and part of what the gate has to fight is the depletion charge in the body. If the source rises above the body, that depletion region gets wider, there's more charge to overcome, and the threshold goes up. With a typical body-effect coefficient, a one-volt source-to-body voltage adds about two hundred millivolts to $V_t$. With source and body tied, $V_{SB}$ is zero and the threshold is fixed.

Now the catch. Once the body is on the source, the body-drain junction becomes a diode from source to drain. That's the body diode on every power MOSFET datasheet. It means a single MOSFET can only block in one direction, which is why battery packs use two FETs back-to-back for charge and discharge control.

In integrated circuits the picture changes. All the NMOS transistors usually share one P-substrate at ground, so a transistor stacked on top of another can't have its body on its source. You see the body effect in source followers and pass gates, and you design around it or use a triple-well process.

And if they meant something else, gate and drain are also commonly tied. That's a diode-connected transistor, always in saturation, and it's the reference half of a current mirror."
:::

::: terms
- **Body (bulk, substrate, back gate)**: the semiconductor region the channel forms in. The fourth terminal.
- **Body effect**: threshold voltage increase with source-to-body reverse bias, $\Delta V_t = \gamma(\sqrt{2\phi_F+V_{SB}}-\sqrt{2\phi_F})$.
- **Body-effect coefficient $\gamma$**: sets how strongly $V_{SB}$ shifts $V_t$. Typically a few tenths of $\sqrt{\text{V}}$.
- **Back-gate transconductance $g_{mb}$**: the body acting as a second, weaker gate.
- **Body diode**: the body-drain junction, appearing between drain and source once body and source are tied.
- **Parasitic BJT**: the N+ source / P body / N drain structure of an NMOS. Shorting body to source keeps it off.
- **Latch-up**: a parasitic thyristor in CMOS triggered by forward-biased body junctions.
- **Diode-connected**: gate shorted to drain, forcing saturation. Used in current mirrors.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "source and body, because that's how they come."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives **both** physical reasons: keeps the source-body junction from forward-biasing, and eliminates the body effect (with the equation or a number like "~0.2 V per volt of $V_{SB}$").
- Names the **consequence**: the body diode, unidirectional blocking, back-to-back FETs for bidirectional switches.
- Mentions the parasitic NPN in power MOSFETs, and that the source-body short is what keeps it off during fast transients.
- Explains why ICs often **can't** tie them (shared substrate) and where that bites: source followers, NMOS pass gates, stacked devices.
- Notes the alternative reading (gate-drain, diode-connected) and handles it in a sentence.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "drain and source are tied."** That would short the channel and make the transistor useless. It's source and body.
- **Thinking the body tie is cosmetic.** It sets $V_t$ and creates the body diode, which decides which direction the device blocks.
- **Assuming every IC transistor has body = source.** In a standard bulk CMOS process, NMOS bodies share the substrate at ground. Only the bottom NMOS in a stack gets $V_{SB} = 0$ for free.
- **Getting the body-effect sign wrong.** For NMOS, raising the source above the body (reverse bias) *increases* $V_t$. Forward body bias lowers it.
- **Forgetting the body diode when designing a switch.** A single NFET with its drain at a battery and source at a load conducts through its body diode if the "load side" is ever higher, e.g. when a charger is connected.
- **Confusing the body diode with a separately added protection diode.** It's intrinsic, created by the source-body tie, not an extra part.
:::

## The MOSFET has four terminals and two hidden diodes

Draw an NMOS in cross-section and you find two PN junctions buried in it: N+ source to P body, and N+ drain to P body. The transistor action only works if neither junction conducts.

Shorting S to B leaves the body-source diode permanently at 0 V. The body-drain diode remains and becomes the "body diode" between S and D.

Tying body to source handles the first junction for good. The second, body-drain, is reverse-biased whenever $V_D > V_S$, which is normal NMOS operation. If $V_D$ drops below $V_S$ by ~0.7 V, it conducts. That's the body diode ([[q0101]]).

## Reason 1: keep the junctions off

If the P body of an NMOS rises above its N+ source by about 0.6–0.7 V, the source-body diode forward-biases. Then:

- current flows into the body instead of through the channel,
- the N+ source (emitter), P body (base) and N drain (collector) form a **parasitic NPN** that can turn on and conduct uncontrollably,
- in CMOS, this NPN plus a PNP from the neighbouring PMOS form a thyristor: **latch-up**.

Power MOSFET designers short the body to the source across the entire die precisely to hold the parasitic NPN's base-emitter at zero. During a fast $dV/dt$ on the drain, displacement current through the drain-body capacitance flows laterally under the source. Any resistance in that path develops a voltage that could forward-bias the base-emitter junction. The source metal shorting the body keeps that resistance tiny.

## Reason 2: kill the body effect

The threshold voltage is the gate voltage that creates an inversion layer. Before the gate can attract mobile electrons to the surface, it has to support the **depletion charge** of ionized acceptors in the body. If the source (and therefore the channel) is raised above the body by $V_{SB}$, the channel-body junction is more reverse-biased, the depletion region widens, and there's more depletion charge to balance:

$$Q_{dep} = \sqrt{2q\varepsilon_{Si}N_A(2\phi_F + V_{SB})}$$

Dividing by $C_{ox}$ to convert charge to gate voltage gives the body-effect equation:

$$V_t = V_{t0} + \gamma\left(\sqrt{2\phi_F + V_{SB}} - \sqrt{2\phi_F}\right),\qquad \gamma = \frac{\sqrt{2q\varepsilon_{Si}N_A}}{C_{ox}}$$

## Worked example: a source follower that can't tie its body

In bulk CMOS, an NMOS source follower has its body at ground (shared substrate) and its source at the output. Take $V_{in} = 3$ V, $V_{t0} = 0.7$ V, $k_n = 1$ mA/V², bias current 100 µA.

- Overdrive: $V_{ov} = \sqrt{2I_D/k_n} = \sqrt{2\cdot100\,\mu/1\,\text{m}} = 0.447$ V.
- Without body effect: $V_{out} = 3 - 0.7 - 0.447 = 1.85$ V.
- With body effect, $V_t$ depends on $V_{out} = V_{SB}$, so iterate $V_{out} = V_{in} - V_t(V_{out}) - V_{ov}$. It converges to $V_{out} \approx 1.58$ V, with $V_t \approx 0.97$ V.

That's 0.27 V of headroom lost to the body effect alone. Small-signal, the body acts as a second gate with $g_{mb} = \eta g_m$, where $\eta = \gamma/(2\sqrt{2\phi_F + V_{SB}}) \approx 0.13$ here, so the follower gain drops from 1 to about $1/(1+\eta) \approx 0.88$ even with an ideal current-source load. Tie body to source (possible for a PMOS in its own N-well, or with a triple-well NMOS) and both problems vanish.

Common battery-protection circuits use exactly this common-source pair (often sold as a dual NFET in one package) so the protection IC can independently stop charge or discharge.

## In integrated circuits: when you can't tie them

In a standard **bulk CMOS** process:

- All NMOS devices sit in the same P-substrate, tied to the lowest supply. Only an NMOS whose source is at ground gets $V_{SB} = 0$.
- PMOS devices sit in N-wells. Each well *can* be tied to its own source, at the cost of area (wells need spacing) and extra well capacitance.
- **Triple-well** processes add a deep N-well that isolates a P-well, so NMOS devices can have their own body. **SOI** processes isolate each device with buried oxide.

Where the body effect bites: NMOS pass transistors (a pass gate passing a "1" only reaches $V_{DD} - V_t$, and the $V_t$ is body-effect-boosted), stacked NAND pull-downs, cascodes and source followers.

Some designs **use** the body on purpose: forward body bias to lower $V_t$ for speed, or reverse body bias to raise $V_t$ and cut leakage in standby.

## The other pair: gate tied to drain

If the interviewer meant something else, the other common tie is **gate to drain**, the diode-connected transistor. With $V_{GS} = V_{DS}$, the condition for saturation, $V_{DS} \ge V_{GS} - V_t$, is automatically satisfied. So the device is always saturated when on, and

$$I_D = \tfrac{1}{2}k_n(V_{GS} - V_t)^2 \;\Rightarrow\; V_{GS} = V_t + \sqrt{2I_D/k_n}$$

Force a reference current into it and it generates exactly the $V_{GS}$ that a matched transistor needs to carry that same current: the basis of the **current mirror** ([[q0104]]).

::: applications
- **Discrete power MOSFETs** (every TO-220, D2PAK, SOT-23 FET): internal source-body short, giving the datasheet body diode.
- **Li-ion battery protection**: two NFETs in common-source configuration (back-to-back body diodes) controlled separately for charge and discharge.
- **Reverse-polarity protection with a PFET**: relies on the body diode's direction (from the source-body tie) to start conducting, then the channel turns on ([[q0101]]).
- **CMOS analog design**: PMOS input pairs placed in their own N-well with body tied to source to avoid body effect and improve matching.
- **Current mirrors and bias circuits**: diode-connected (gate-drain tied) reference transistors ([[q0104]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- I assumed an **N-channel** device. For PMOS, the body is N-type, should sit at the *highest* potential, and the body effect raises $|V_t|$ when the source drops below the body.
- The body-effect numbers assume **bulk** CMOS with a long-channel model. FinFET and FD-SOI devices behave differently (weaker body coupling in FinFETs, strong back-gate control in FD-SOI).
- "Discrete parts always tie S and B" holds for 3-pin power MOSFETs. A few small-signal and analog-switch MOSFETs bring the body out separately.
- If the interviewer meant **gate and drain**, the answer is the diode-connected transistor (see the last section). If they meant two terminals of *different* transistors, e.g. the joined sources of a back-to-back pair, that's also covered above.
:::

::: followups
### What is the body effect and how big is it?
It's the increase in threshold voltage when the source is reverse-biased relative to the body: $\Delta V_t = \gamma(\sqrt{2\phi_F + V_{SB}} - \sqrt{2\phi_F})$. With $\gamma = 0.4\ \sqrt{\text{V}}$ and $2\phi_F = 0.7$ V, 1 V of $V_{SB}$ adds about 0.19 V. Physically, the wider depletion region holds more charge that the gate must balance before inversion.

### If source and body are tied in a discrete MOSFET, how do you make a switch that blocks both directions?
Put two MOSFETs in series with their body diodes opposed, either common-source (sources joined, the usual choice since one gate driver referenced to the common source can drive both) or common-drain. Whatever the polarity, one body diode is reverse-biased. Battery packs and USB-PD power paths use this.

### Why can't you tie source to body for every NMOS on a chip?
In bulk CMOS all NMOS transistors share the P-substrate, which must sit at the lowest potential. A transistor whose source isn't at ground therefore has $V_{SB} > 0$. Isolating each body needs a triple-well or SOI process, which costs area and money.

### What happens if the body of an NMOS floats?
Its potential is set by leakage and capacitive coupling, so $V_t$ wanders, and fast drain transients can push the body high enough to forward-bias the source junction and turn on the parasitic NPN. SOI devices with floating bodies show the "kink effect" and history-dependent $V_t$ for exactly this reason.

### Harder: an NMOS pass gate passes a logic 1 from a 1.8 V supply. Why is the output well below 1.8 V − 0.5 V even though V_t0 = 0.5 V?
The output (the source) rises toward $V_{DD}$, so $V_{SB}$ grows with it while the body stays at ground. The threshold increases along the way, so the device shuts off when $V_{out} = V_{DD} - V_t(V_{out})$. With $\gamma = 0.4$ and $2\phi_F = 0.7$, solving gives $V_{out} \approx 1.1$ V rather than 1.3 V. That's why CMOS uses transmission gates (NMOS plus PMOS) for passing full logic levels.
:::

::: interpretations
The expected answer is **source and body**: this question follows "What are the terminals?" in the MOSFET section, and it's the classic "why do discrete MOSFETs have three pins" point. A reasonable alternative reading is **gate and drain** (diode-connected transistor), which shows up in current mirrors and is covered in the deep dive. Interviewers sometimes also have in mind the joined sources of back-to-back MOSFETs. Lead with source-body and offer the others briefly.
:::

- Explains it keeps the body-source PN junction from forward-biasing :: Shows you know the MOSFET contains junctions that must stay off.
- Explains the body effect: $V_t$ rises with $V_{SB}$, tying them removes it :: The main electrical reason, and the one IC interviewers probe.
- Names the consequence: the body diode, so one FET blocks only one direction :: Links device physics to real board-level design decisions.
- Notes that in bulk CMOS ICs, NMOS bodies share the substrate so the tie isn't always possible :: Shows awareness that discrete and IC worlds differ.
- Mentions back-to-back FETs or the parasitic NPN :: Demonstrates practical or deeper device knowledge.
- Acknowledges the gate-drain (diode-connected) alternative :: Covers the other common reading without derailing.
:::
