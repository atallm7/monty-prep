::: testing
Whether you can discuss integration as an **engineering trade-off at the die and system level**, not a slogan. The interviewer is looking for:

1. Benefits: energy, bandwidth, latency, size, cost, reliability.
2. Costs on silicon: **yield falls with die area**, process compromise for analog/RF/memory, noise coupling, thermal hot spots, power-domain complexity.
3. Costs on engineering: verification complexity, NRE, schedule, IP integration, test, respin risk.
4. A quantitative feel (yield model, cost per good die) and the modern answer: chiplets and advanced packaging.
:::

::: why
Deciding what goes on which die is one of the most consequential choices in chip design, and the same reasoning applies at board and system level. Yield and thermal limits are why very large monolithic dies stop making economic sense, and noise coupling is why a precision ADC or an RF front end is often kept off a big digital die. It connects to yield considerations ([[q0650]]) and chiplets ([[q0652]]), and it is the die-level companion to the system-level comparison in [[q0929]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Yield and die area**: bigger dies catch more defects. See [[q0650]].
- **Process matching**: logic, analog, RF, DRAM and flash prefer different processes.
- **Interference**: digital switching noise coupling through supply and substrate into sensitive circuits.
- **Power density and thermal coupling**: hot spots and temperature-dependent leakage.
- **Verification complexity**: the cross-product of blocks, modes and power states.
- **Chiplets and advanced packaging**: partitioning integration across dies. See [[q0652]].
- **Energy/latency benefits of proximity**: see [[q0929]].
:::

::: answer
"The benefits of integration are that things on the same die are close, so you get lower energy per bit, higher bandwidth, lower latency, smaller area and fewer parts and solder joints. The costs show up in several places.

First, yield. Defects land randomly on the wafer, so the bigger the die, the more likely it has one. Yield falls roughly exponentially with area, and the number of dies per wafer falls too, so cost per good die rises faster than linearly. Second, process compromise: you can't put DRAM, flash, high-voltage power stages and RF on the same leading-edge logic process without penalties, so you either give up performance on the odd blocks or don't integrate them. Third, interference: digital switching noise couples through the supply and substrate into PLLs, ADCs and RF, so a sensitive analog block next to a big digital block gets harder to design. Fourth, thermal: you concentrate heat in one place, which creates hot spots and raises leakage. Fifth, complexity: verifying dozens of blocks, power modes and clock domains is hard, and a bug means a respin, and that's expensive and slow.

The modern answer is to integrate selectively with chiplets: split a big design into smaller dies, each in its best process, and connect them with advanced packaging, so you get yield and flexibility while keeping short, wide links."
:::

::: technical
**Benefits.** Lower energy per bit moved, high on-die bandwidth, low latency, smaller area/package count, lower BOM and assembly cost at volume, fewer interconnect failure points. See [[q0929]].

**Costs and risks.**
1. **Yield and cost per good die.** With defect density $D_0$ (defects/cm²) and die area $A$ (cm²), the Poisson model gives $Y=e^{-AD_0}$. Dies per 300 mm wafer is roughly $\pi(d/2)^2/A-\pi d/\sqrt{2A}$. Cost per good die $=C_{wafer}/(N_{die}\,Y)$. Example ($C_{wafer}=10{,}000$ USD, $D_0=0.1$/cm²): 100 mm² gives 640 dies and $Y=0.905$ for 17 USD per good die; 600 mm² gives 91 dies and $Y=0.549$ for 201 USD, i.e. 6x the area costs 11.6x as much.
2. **Process compromise.** DRAM (capacitor processes), flash, RF/analog and power devices have different optimal processes.
3. **Analog does not scale like digital.** Analog blocks occupy almost the same area at a new node, but cost more per mm².
4. **Noise coupling.** Supply bounce, substrate noise and clock coupling into PLLs, ADCs, RF.
5. **Thermal.** Power density and hot spots; leakage rises with temperature.
6. **Verification and test.** More blocks, power states and clock-domain crossings; access to test ports and BIST.
7. **NRE, schedule, IP licensing, respin risk.**
8. **Single point of failure and supply concentration.**

**Chiplet response.** Split the 600 mm² design into four 150 mm² dies with about 10% extra area for die-to-die interfaces (165 mm² each): 376 dies per wafer, $Y=0.848$, about 31 USD per good chiplet, so roughly 125 USD of silicon for four versus 201 USD monolithic (before packaging and test overhead), assuming known-good-die testing.
:::

::: short
"Integration gives lower energy, higher bandwidth, lower latency and smaller, cheaper systems. The costs: yield drops with die area so cost per good die rises faster than linearly; one process can't suit logic, analog, RF and memory; digital noise couples into sensitive blocks; heat concentrates; and verification, NRE and respin risk grow. Chiplets are the modern compromise: smaller dies in the best process, joined by advanced packaging."
:::

::: long
"I'd split it into what you gain and what it costs.

The gain is proximity. On-die wires are short, so moving a bit costs less energy, links can be very wide, latency is a few cycles, and you have fewer packages, fewer board traces and fewer solder joints. At volume, that's a smaller, cheaper, more reliable product.

On the cost side, the first thing is yield. Defects land randomly, so the chance a die is good falls roughly as e to the minus area times defect density. And a bigger die also means fewer dies per wafer, with more wasted at the edge. With a defect density of 0.1 per square centimeter, a 100 square millimeter die yields about 90 percent and costs maybe 17 dollars per good die on a 10,000 dollar wafer, while a 600 square millimeter die yields 55 percent and costs about 200 dollars. Six times the area, more than eleven times the cost.

Second, process. DRAM, flash, RF and high-voltage power want different processes from leading-edge logic, so integrating them means either compromising them or using expensive options. Analog also doesn't shrink like digital.

Third, noise. Digital switching puts noise on the supply and substrate, which couples into PLLs, ADCs and RF. That's why a precision front end is often a separate die.

Fourth, heat. One die concentrates power, so hot spots and leakage rise with temperature.

And finally complexity. More blocks and power modes means more to verify, and a silicon bug costs weeks and millions in a respin.

That's why the industry moved to chiplets: split a big design into smaller dies, each in its best process, so yield goes up and you can mix technologies, and join them with advanced packaging. You give up some bandwidth and energy efficiency compared with a single die but keep a lot of the benefit."
:::

::: terms
- **Defect density ($D_0$)**: killer defects per unit area on the wafer.
- **Die yield**: fraction of dies without a killer defect; $Y=e^{-AD_0}$ in the Poisson model.
- **Dies per wafer**: number of whole dies that fit on the wafer.
- **Known good die (KGD)**: a die tested good before assembly into a multi-die package.
- **Chiplet**: a small die designed to be combined with others in a package.
- **Substrate noise**: noise coupled through the silicon bulk.
- **Hot spot**: local temperature peak due to high power density.
- **Reticle limit**: the maximum die size that a lithography exposure can print.
- **Respin**: a new mask revision to fix a silicon bug.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "integration saves power and cost but is less flexible".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Computes **cost per good die** from yield and dies per wafer and shows the superlinear growth.
- Explains the *physical* reasons for process compromise and noise coupling, not only lists them.
- Includes thermal and leakage feedback and power-domain complexity.
- Includes the engineering side: verification, NRE, respin, IP licensing, test.
- Gives the chiplet/advanced-packaging answer with its costs (die-to-die interface area and energy, KGD test, packaging cost).
- Notes the reticle limit and that integration is a spectrum (die, package, module, board).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying integration always reduces cost.** Cost per good die explodes for large dies.
- **Using a linear yield model.** Yield is roughly exponential in area under the Poisson model.
- **Forgetting that analog does not shrink.** A bigger share of the die goes to blocks that gain little from the new node.
- **Ignoring noise coupling.** Many sensitive blocks are placed on a different die for this reason.
- **Treating chiplets as free.** They cost die-to-die interface area, energy and packaging complexity.
- **Believing a single-die design is more robust.** One failed block can scrap the entire die.
:::

::: deep
## Yield: the area tax

Wafers have random killer defects. If defects are scattered with density $D_0$ per cm² and a die has area $A$, the number of defects per die is Poisson-distributed with mean $\lambda=AD_0$, and the probability of zero defects is

$$Y=e^{-AD_0}$$

(Real fabs use refined models, such as Murphy or negative-binomial, that account for defect clustering. They are less pessimistic for large dies, but the trend is the same.)

**Dies per wafer.** A 300 mm wafer has area $\pi(150)^2=70{,}686$ mm². Dividing by die area overcounts, because dies at the circular edge are lost. A common estimate is

$$N_{die}\approx\frac{\pi (d/2)^2}{A}-\frac{\pi d}{\sqrt{2A}}$$

**Cost per good die** $=C_{wafer}/(N_{die}Y)$. With $C_{wafer}=10{,}000$ USD and $D_0=0.1$ /cm²:

| Die area (mm²) | Dies/wafer | Yield | Good dies | Cost per good die (USD) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 1319 | 95.1% | 1255 | 8 |
| 100 | 640 | 90.5% | 579 | 17 |
| 150 | 417 | 86.1% | 359 | 28 |
| 300 | 197 | 74.1% | 146 | 69 |
| 600 | 91 | 54.9% | 50 | 201 |
| 800 | 65 | 44.9% | 29 | 344 |

> Wafer cost, defect density and the yield model are illustrative. Real advanced-node wafer costs and defect densities are confidential and vary by foundry and process maturity. Use the shape of the curve, not the dollar values.

There is also the **reticle limit**: a lithography exposure field limits a monolithic die to a bit over 800 mm² on current scanners (approximate). Beyond that, you need multiple dies.

## Chiplets: buying back yield

Consider a 600 mm² design. Split it into four 150 mm² chiplets. Each needs die-to-die interface circuits; assume 10% more area (165 mm²). Then (D0 = 0.1):

- Monolithic: $N=91$, $Y=0.549$, 201 USD per good die.
- Chiplet: $N=376$ (for 165 mm²), $Y=0.848$, about 31 USD per good chiplet; four give about 125 USD of silicon, before the extra package, substrate, assembly and test.

If defects are worse (D0 = 0.3): monolithic yield is 16.5%, cost about 668 USD; four chiplets total about 174 USD. The worse the process, the more partitioning helps. Assembly requires **known good die** testing so that bad chiplets are not packaged, and the packaging (silicon interposer, organic substrate with fine pitch, or 3D stacking) adds cost, risk and thermal complexity. Each die-to-die link also uses area and energy per bit (more than on-die wires, less than off-package SerDes), and latency rises.

Chiplets also allow **process mixing**: a leading-edge compute die with a mature-node I/O die, or logic stacked with DRAM. Industry interconnect standards exist for this, for example UCIe (Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express).

## Process compromise

| Function | Preferred process | Why it hurts on a leading-edge logic die |
|---|---|---|
| Logic | Smallest node | The reference |
| SRAM | Logic node, but scaling slows | Bit cells stop shrinking as fast as logic at advanced nodes |
| DRAM | Special capacitor and low-leakage process | Needs deep trench or stacked capacitors; not logic-compatible |
| Flash | Floating-gate/charge-trap process | High-voltage programming; embedded flash is hard below certain nodes |
| Analog/RF | Mature node with higher supply voltage | Lower supply headroom, matching and noise suffer, area barely shrinks |
| Power devices | High-voltage BCD process | Thin-oxide logic devices cannot take voltage |

The trade is not binary: SoCs integrate SRAM, small regulators and low-power radios, but leave bulk DRAM, power conversion and RF power amplifiers off-die.

## Noise coupling and mixed-signal integration

Digital logic draws current in sharp edges. With package and bond-wire inductance $L$, a current step $\Delta I$ over time $\Delta t$ causes a supply bounce $V=L\,\Delta I/\Delta t$. For 2 nH and a 0.2 A step in 1 ns (16 outputs at about 12 mA each), $V=2\text{ nH}\times0.2\text{ A}/1\text{ ns}=0.4$ V. On-die decoupling reduces the current the package sees, but even a few hundred millivolts of bounce couples into any analog circuit that shares the rail or substrate.

Figure: With 16 outputs, 2 nH of ground inductance and 0.5 ns edges, the on-chip ground jumps about 1.4 V, enough to falsely trigger a quiet line whose threshold is 0.3·V_DD. Reduce the number of outputs, slow the edges or add parallel ground pins and watch it shrink. The simulated bounce is well below the open-loop $N\,L\,di/dt$ estimate, because the bounce itself reduces the drivers' current. The same mechanism, applied inside a SoC, is why PLLs and ADCs get dedicated supplies, guard rings and careful floorplan separation, and why very sensitive blocks are often on a separate die.

Mitigations on a mixed-signal die: separate supplies and grounds for analog, guard rings, deep n-well isolation, differential signaling, clock-frequency planning to avoid tones in the signal band, and physical separation. These cost area and design effort and are never perfect.

## Thermal and power-state complexity

Concentrating blocks raises power density. 5 W in a 100 mm² die is 5 W/cm². Hot spots (a CPU core at full load) can be several times the average, and subthreshold leakage roughly doubles for every 10 to 20 °C rise in many technologies, so a hot spot raises leakage which raises heat (thermal runaway risk if unmanaged). Integrated SoCs therefore use temperature sensors, DVFS, power gating and thermal throttling ([[q0933]]). Each power domain adds level shifters, isolation cells, retention and sequencing logic. With $n$ independent power states per block across $m$ blocks, the state space is $n^m$, and the verification of transitions grows fast.

## Verification, test and schedule

Many blocks on one chip mean many interfaces and clock domains. Bugs found after tape-out require a respin: weeks to months and a new set of masks, which in advanced nodes can cost millions of USD (uncertain, vendor-specific). Verification effort typically rivals or exceeds design effort. Test must reach every block: scan chains, memory BIST, boundary scan and a limited number of pins constrain access. Late changes in one block can disturb timing in others.

## Reliability and supply

A single die has one failure domain: a defect in any block (if not repairable) kills the part. Mitigations include redundancy (spare rows and columns in SRAM, spare cores that can be fused off) and binning (selling dies with defective cores as lower SKUs), which recover yield. A single-source, single-node part is also a supply-chain risk, whereas a board of discrete parts can substitute pieces.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A crisp closing line: "Integration is a bet that proximity is worth more than flexibility; yield, process mismatch, noise and heat are where the bet loses, and chiplets are how the industry hedges it."
:::

::: applications
- **Phone SoCs**: highly integrated monolithic dies because area and power dominate and yield is acceptable at moderate die sizes.
- **Server CPUs and GPUs**: chiplet-based designs to exceed practical monolithic yield limits.
- **RF and mixed-signal products**: RF transceivers on different processes combined in SiPs or on separate dies.
- **Memory-on-logic**: high-bandwidth memory stacked next to logic dies on interposers.
- **Automotive and safety parts**: separation of safety and application logic, redundancy and diagnostics.
- **Redundancy and binning**: SRAM repair and fused-off cores to recover yield.
:::

::: assumptions
- The yield model is Poisson, which overstates losses for large dies compared with clustered-defect models. Conclusions on trend are robust, absolute values are not.
- Wafer cost, defect density and die-to-die overhead are illustrative; real values are foundry- and node-dependent and not public.
- Chiplet comparison ignores packaging, test, KGD escape rate and extra design effort, all of which reduce the savings; for small dies chiplets lose.
- Supply bounce example ignores on-die decoupling and uses a simple $L\,di/dt$ estimate.
- Subthreshold leakage temperature dependence varies by process; "doubling per 10 to 20 °C" is a rule of thumb.
:::

::: followups
### Why does cost per good die rise faster than die area?
Two effects multiply. Fewer dies fit on a wafer (and the circular edge wastes proportionally more of larger dies), and each die is more likely to contain a killer defect, so the yield falls exponentially with area. Cost per good die is wafer cost over (dies per wafer times yield), so it rises faster than linearly.

### Why not put the RF front end and the application processor on one die?
Different process needs, and noise. RF circuits want high-$f_T$, low-noise devices and good passives; the digital die creates substrate and supply noise that degrades receiver sensitivity. Integration also forces a re-spin of the whole die when only the radio changes. Many designs use separate dies in one package or a separate radio chip.

### What are the downsides of chiplets?
Die-to-die interface area and energy, extra latency, more complex and costly packaging (interposers, bumps, thermal handling), known-good-die test requirements, and design complexity from partitioning and standardizing interfaces. For small designs they don't pay; they win when yield or process mixing gains outweigh these costs.

### A design has 30% of its die area as analog that barely shrinks. What do you consider?
Moving the analog to a separate die in a mature, cheaper process and connecting by a SiP or chiplet interface. The digital die then shrinks and yields better; the analog avoids expensive advanced-node area and gets cleaner supplies. The cost is packaging and interface complexity, so check the numbers for the volume.

### Binning: how can a partly defective die still be sold?
If the defect falls in a repairable or redundant block, the die is fixed with spare rows or columns (SRAM repair) or a core is fused off and the part sold as a lower-core-count SKU. This recovers yield above the zero-defect probability, which is why vendors design for redundancy and multiple SKUs from one die.
:::
