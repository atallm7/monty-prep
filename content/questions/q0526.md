::: basics
### In plain words
A reflection happens wherever a wave hits a change in impedance. Part of the signal keeps going and part bounces back. On a board, impedance changes at the driver and receiver ends, at stubs and branches, vias, pads, connectors, width changes and gaps in the plane. You fix them by removing the change, making it small, or adding a termination that absorbs the bounce.

### Picture it
Imagine a rope with a heavy knot in the middle. Send a pulse down the rope and part of it reflects back at the knot. A smooth, uniform rope has no reflection. Now tie the far end to a wall, and the pulse bounces fully back. The knot is a bad via or a stub. The wall is an unterminated receiver. A damper at the end soaks up the bounce.

### Words you need
- **Reflection coefficient ($\Gamma$)**: $(Z_2 - Z_1)/(Z_2 + Z_1)$, the fraction that bounces back ([[q0711]]).
- **Stub**: a dead-end branch off a line ([[q0713]]).
- **Termination**: a resistor that matches the line so the wave is absorbed ([[q0532]]).
- **Ringing**: oscillation from repeated reflections between mismatched ends.
- **TDR**: a measurement that shows impedance along a line using a fast step.

### The simplest example
A wave on a 50 Ω line hits an open receiver. With $Z_L \to \infty$, $\Gamma = +1$, so the voltage at the end doubles. Now a T-junction splits the line into two 50 Ω lines. The wave sees $50 \parallel 50 = 25$ Ω, so $\Gamma = (25 - 50)/(25 + 50) = -1/3$. A third of the wave bounces back. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page lists each source with its size, shows how repeated bounces cause ringing, and gives the cure ladder: remove, shrink, terminate, slow, verify.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can list the **actual impedance discontinuities on a board** (source/load mismatch, stubs and branches, vias, pads, connectors, width and layer changes, plane gaps, package) rather than just "mismatch", and for each give the cause and the cure. The unifying idea is $\Gamma=(Z_2-Z_1)/(Z_2+Z_1)$ at every place the local impedance changes, and the cure is either to remove the change, make it electrically small compared with the edge, or terminate so reflections are absorbed.
:::

::: why
Ringing, overshoot, false clock edges and eye closure are reflection problems. Locating them means walking the signal path in your head and asking "where does the impedance change?" Layout reviewers do exactly that. The question also leads into termination ([[q0529]]–[[q0533]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Reflection coefficient** $\Gamma=\dfrac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0}$. See [[q0711]], [[q0713]].
- **Reactive discontinuities**: small L or C reflect in proportion to $L/t_r$ or $C/t_r$. See [[q0515]].
- **Stubs and T-branches**: change the impedance seen by the wave. See [[q0526]].
- **Source and load mismatch**: driver impedance and receiver termination. See [[q0714]].
- **Return path discontinuities**: plane gaps and layer changes. See [[q0486]].
- **Termination**: absorbs the wave at the source or load. See [[q0532]].
:::

::: answer
"A reflection happens anywhere the impedance the wave sees changes, with a coefficient of load minus line over load plus line. So I walk down the path and list the changes.

At the ends: the driver, whose output impedance might be 10 or 20 ohms against a 50 ohm line, and the receiver, which is usually high impedance, near an open, so it reflects almost everything. In the middle: stubs and T-junctions, because where a line splits in two the wave sees half the impedance; vias, which add some inductance and capacitance; pads and test points that add capacitance; width changes and neck-downs; layer changes where the reference changes; connectors; and any gap or slot in the reference plane, where capacitance drops and inductance rises. Even bends and package bond wires add a little.

To minimise them I first avoid the discontinuity: point-to-point routing, no stubs, fewer vias, constant width, solid plane. Where I can't avoid it, I make it small compared with the edge: small pads, anti-pads under big pads, ground vias, shorter stubs. And I terminate the line: a series resistor at the source so the driver plus resistor equals Z0, or a parallel terminator at the load, so whatever reflection remains gets absorbed instead of bouncing back and forth. Then I check by simulation or TDR."
:::

::: technical
**At a boundary** between impedances $Z_1\to Z_2$: $\Gamma=\dfrac{Z_2-Z_1}{Z_2+Z_1}$, transmission $\tau=1+\Gamma$.

**Sources and sizes (50 Ω line).**
| Source | Equivalent | Reflection |
|---|---|---|
| Open receiver | $Z_L\to\infty$ | $\Gamma=+1$ |
| Low-Z driver (10 Ω) | $Z_s=10$ | $\Gamma_s=-0.67$ at the source end |
| T-junction of two equal lines | $Z_0\parallel Z_0=25$ Ω seen | $\Gamma=-1/3$ |
| Width/layer change 50→65 Ω | step | $\Gamma=+0.13$ |
| Plane gap under trace | local $Z_0$ rises | $\Gamma>0$, plus EMI |
| Pad / test point (0.3 pF) | shunt C | $\approx Z_0C/(2t_r)$: 3.8% at $t_r=200$ ps |
| Via (1 nH, 0.4 pF) | L + C | 1% at 1 ns, 10% at 100 ps |
| Connector | L/C section | few % to tens of % at GHz |
| Package bond wire | L ≈ 1–3 nH | large for fast edges |

**Multiple reflections** bounce between mismatched ends. For source $\Gamma_s$ and load $\Gamma_L$, a step of the line wave $V_0=V_s Z_0/(Z_s+Z_0)$ arrives, the load returns $\Gamma_LV_0$, the source returns $\Gamma_s\Gamma_LV_0$, and so on, every $2t_d$; ringing results when $\Gamma_s\Gamma_L<0$ (e.g. $\Gamma_L=+1$, $\Gamma_s<0$).

**Minimising.** (1) Remove: route point-to-point, no stubs, avoid layer changes, keep the plane solid. (2) Make it electrically short: when $t_{discontinuity}\ll t_r$ it behaves lumped and small ($\Gamma\approx Z_0C/2t_r$): small pads, plane cut-outs, 0402 parts. (3) Compensate: anti-pads under pads, ground vias, tuning. (4) Terminate: series $R_s=Z_0-R_{out}$ at the source, or $R=Z_0$ parallel/Thevenin at the load. (5) Slow the edge: reflections from small reactive discontinuities scale as $1/t_r$. (6) Verify: TDR, simulation.
:::

::: long
"A reflection is just what happens when a wave hits a change in impedance: part of it goes forward and part comes back, with a coefficient of load minus line over load plus line. So to find reflections on a PCB I walk the signal path and ask where the impedance changes.

Start with the two ends. The driver might have 10 or 20 ohms of output impedance on a 50 ohm line, and a typical CMOS receiver is nearly an open, so it reflects almost the whole wave, which then returns to the driver and reflects again with the opposite sign: that's the classic ringing.

Along the line, the biggest offenders are stubs and branches. At a T the wave sees two lines in parallel, so half the impedance, and reflects a third of its amplitude. Vias add a nanohenry or so and a few tenths of a picofarad. Pads and test points add shunt capacitance. A width change or neck-down changes the impedance. A layer change that changes the reference, or a gap in the reference plane, makes the impedance jump and breaks the return path. Connectors and the IC package add their own inductance and capacitance.

To fix them, in order: remove the discontinuity, with point-to-point routing, no stubs, as few vias as possible and a solid plane. If I can't, I make it small compared with the rise time, using small pads, cut-outs under big pads and ground vias, because a small L or C reflects in proportion to its size over the edge time. Then terminate the line. A series resistor at the driver makes driver plus resistor equal Z0 so the returning reflection is absorbed, or a parallel termination at the receiver stops the first reflection. Last, I slow the edges if I can, and I verify with TDR or a simulation."
:::

::: terms
- **Reflection coefficient ($\Gamma$)**: ratio of reflected to incident wave amplitude at a discontinuity.
- **Transmission coefficient ($\tau$)**: $1+\Gamma$ for voltage.
- **Stub**: dead-end branch off a line.
- **T-junction / branch**: place where one line splits into two.
- **Return loss**: $-20\log|\Gamma|$ (dB).
- **Ringing**: oscillation caused by repeated reflections between mismatched ends.
- **Lattice (bounce) diagram**: time-ordered chart of reflections between source and load.
- **TDR**: reflectometry; plots impedance vs distance by launching a step and watching reflections.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "impedance mismatch causes reflections; use termination." An exceptional answer:
- Lists **a complete set of board-level sources** (ends, stubs/T, vias, pads, width/layer changes, plane gaps, connectors, package) with **sizes** ($\Gamma$ numbers).
- Distinguishes **resistive steps** from **small reactive** ones ($\propto L/t_r$, $C/t_r$) and uses the edge rate to judge importance.
- Explains **multiple bounces and ringing** (sign of $\Gamma_s\Gamma_L$).
- Gives the cure ladder: **eliminate → shrink → compensate → terminate → slow → verify**.
- Mentions TDR as the diagnosis tool and the return path as a source.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Only blaming the receiver end.** Stubs, vias and pads cause reflections along the way.
- **Treating all discontinuities as equal.** A 0.1 pF pad is invisible at 2 ns edges and matters at 50 ps.
- **Terminating to cure a stub.** A terminator at the end doesn't cancel the reflection from a mid-line stub.
- **Ignoring the source end.** A low-impedance driver re-reflects the returning wave unless a series resistor matches it.
- **Using star topology on a multi-drop net.** Each branch is a stub; use daisy chain or fly-by.
- **Forgetting that reflections need electrical length.** If $t_d<t_r/6$ the line is lumped and reflections merge into the edge.
:::

::: deep
## What happens at one discontinuity

A wave of amplitude $V^+$ travelling on a line of impedance $Z_1$ meets $Z_2$. KVL/KCL at the junction give
$$\Gamma=\frac{Z_2-Z_1}{Z_2+Z_1},\qquad \tau=1+\Gamma=\frac{2Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}$$
Open: $\Gamma=+1$ (the voltage doubles). Short: $-1$. Matched: 0. At a T-junction where a line splits into two identical lines, $Z_2=Z_0/2$: $\Gamma=-1/3$, $\tau=2/3$ into each branch.

## The bounce picture

The first wave launched into the line is $V_0=V_sZ_0/(Z_s+Z_0)$. With $Z_s=10\ \Omega$, $\Gamma_s=-0.667$, and an open load $\Gamma_L=+1$, and $V_s=3.3$ V: $V_0=2.75$ V. The load voltage is then 5.5 V at $t_d$ (a large overshoot, which input ESD diodes would clamp), 1.83 V at $3t_d$, 4.28 V at $5t_d$, 2.65 V at $7t_d$, 3.73 V at $9t_d$, converging on 3.3 V. Each round trip ($2t_d$) multiplies the ringing by $\Gamma_s\Gamma_L=-0.667$, so the period is $4t_d$.

## Catalogue of PCB sources, with cures

1. **Driver and receiver ends.** CMOS drivers are 10–50 Ω, receivers are high impedance. *Cure:* series termination at the driver ($R_s=Z_0-R_{out}$) or parallel termination at the receiver ([[q0533]]).
2. **Stubs and branches** (star, test-point pads, unused pads, via stubs). A stub of delay $t_s$ looks like a shunt capacitance when $t_s\ll t_r$ and a resonator at $f=1/(4t_s)$. *Cure:* route daisy-chain/fly-by, remove stubs, back-drill via stubs.
3. **Vias.** ≈1 nH, 0.4 pF. *Cure:* fewer vias, ground vias beside them, anti-pad tuning ([[q0515]]).
4. **Pads, test points, 0201/0402 landing pads.** 0.1–0.5 pF. *Cure:* small pads, plane cut-out under big pads.
5. **Width and layer changes.** $\Gamma=\Delta Z/(2Z_0+\Delta Z)$. *Cure:* constant width, tapers, same layers ([[q0516]]).
6. **Reference plane changes**: gaps, slots, layer swaps from ground to power reference. *Cure:* stitching vias/caps, keep the plane solid.
7. **Connectors and cables.** Pin fields have L/C and coupling; *cure:* choose connectors rated for the speed, optimise footprint and ground pin placement.
8. **Package and die.** Bond wire ≈ 1 nH/mm and pin capacitance 1–5 pF at the receiver. *Cure:* on-die termination (ODT), buffering, using the vendor IBIS model in simulation.
9. **Load capacitance** of multiple receivers: each adds shunt C that lowers the line impedance locally. *Cure:* fewer loads, fly-by topology with spacing of receivers.

## Size depends on the edge

For a small shunt $C$ the peak reflection is $\rho\approx Z_0C/(2t_r)$ and for a series $L$, $\rho\approx L/(2Z_0t_r)$. Example: a 0.3 pF test pad at $t_r=200$ ps gives 3.75%; at $t_r=1$ ns, 0.75%; at 50 ps, 15%. When the discontinuity delay is greater than about $t_r/6$ it's no longer lumped; use the full $\Gamma$ formula.

## Interaction between discontinuities

Reflections from two discontinuities spaced by $t_s$ add. If they are of opposite sign and spaced by about $t_r$ they may cancel (a via's L then C does this by design). If they're spaced by an integer multiple of the half-wavelength they can resonate, like two pads separated by a tuned spacing. Therefore decreasing the number of discontinuities usually beats optimising each.

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Over-/undershoot** that exceeds the receiver's absolute-maximum rating is clamped by ESD diodes, injecting current into the supply.
- **Non-monotonic edges** from reflections at a mid-line receiver can double-clock a flip-flop.
- **Radiated EMI** worsens with ringing since the energy is at the ring frequency.

## Worked example: a T-junction clock, step by step

The table says a T-junction reflects $-1/3$, but the consequence on a real clock is easy to miss until you compute the receiver waveform. Take a 3.3 V clock driver with 22 Ω output impedance and a 28 Ω series resistor (so the source is matched at 50 Ω). A 50 Ω line runs to a T-junction, which splits into two equal 50 Ω branches, each ending in an open CMOS receiver. The first wave launched into the line is $3.3\times50/(50+50)=1.65$ V.

At the junction, three equal lines meet, so the reflection coefficient is $-1/3$ and the transmission into each of the other two lines is $+2/3$:

1. Each branch receives $\tfrac23(1.65)=1.10$ V; a wave of $-0.55$ V returns toward the source and is absorbed by the matched source.
2. Each receiver is open, so the wave doubles: the receiver sees **2.20 V**, which is 67 % of the supply.
3. The reflected 1.10 V wave returns to the junction on both branches at once. With equal waves incident from both branches, each branch gets back $-\tfrac13(1.10)+\tfrac23(1.10)=+0.37$ V, and the source line gets $\tfrac23(1.10)+\tfrac23(1.10)=1.47$ V, which is absorbed at the source.
4. The 0.37 V waves double again at the receivers: the voltage climbs to $2.20+0.73=2.93$ V one round-trip on a branch later, then 3.18 V, 3.26 V, 3.29 V, converging on 3.3 V.

The receiver therefore sees a staircase: 2.20, 2.93, 3.18, 3.26, ... each step $2T_b$ apart, where $T_b$ is the branch delay. If the receiver's high threshold is $0.7V_{DD}=2.31$ V (a common CMOS value; use the datasheet's number), the first plateau at 2.20 V is below it, so the clock edge is not recognized until the second step, a branch round trip later. With 50 mm branches ($T_b=295$ ps) that is a 0.6 ns delay-and-slow-edge penalty; with 150 mm branches it grows to 1.8 ns, and the edge can look like a stalled or double-clocked transition. A point-to-point line with the same source gives 3.3 V at the receiver immediately, with no staircase. This is the quantitative reason clock distribution uses point-to-point or buffer-per-load topologies rather than a passive T, and it is why a "fly-by" or daisy-chain with short stubs, not a star, is used on memory buses.

## Worked example: series versus parallel termination

Compare two ways to terminate the same 3.3 V driver with 22 Ω output impedance into a 50 Ω line.

**Series (source) termination.** Add $R_s=Z_0-R_{out}=28$ Ω at the driver. The launch is 1.65 V, the open far end doubles it to 3.3 V after $t_d$, and the reflection returns to the matched source and is absorbed after $2t_d$. Static current is zero (the load is open), so the only power is the dynamic $CV^2f$ charging of the line and the receiver. The cost is that the signal at the middle of the line is a half-height plateau for a time of $t_d$: any receiver on the line except at the very end sees a staircase, so series termination only suits point-to-point nets. For a 170 mm line ($t_d\approx1$ ns), a receiver at mid-line sees 1.65 V from 0.5 ns to 1.5 ns after the driver edge.

**Parallel (end) termination to ground.** Add $R_t=50$ Ω at the receiver. The wave arrives at the receiver, is fully absorbed (no reflection), and the voltage settles at $3.3\times50/(22+50)=2.29$ V. The high level has dropped to 69 % of the supply, close to a typical logic threshold, so the noise margin is poor, and the static current while the line is high is $3.3/72=45.8$ mA, with $I^2R_t=105$ mW in the terminator and 151 mW total (about half that on average for a 50 % duty clock). That static power is the price of mid-line receivers seeing a clean edge. A Thevenin termination (two resistors to VDD and ground) sets the DC level back to mid-rail at the cost of similar static power, and AC termination with a series capacitor passes only the edge and blocks DC.

| | Series | Parallel to GND |
|---|---|---|
| Receivers allowed | at the end only | along the line |
| High level at receiver | 3.3 V | 2.29 V |
| Static power | 0 | 151 mW per net while high |
| Parts at | driver | receiver |

The right choice follows from the topology, the power budget, and the receiver's threshold, which is the "decision" an interviewer wants to hear.

## Interaction of two small discontinuities

Two small discontinuities do not simply add: their reflections arrive at different times, and what matters is their spacing against the edge. A shunt pad (a dip) followed by a series via inductance (a peak) at a separation $t_s$ gives two reflections of opposite sign. If $t_s\approx t_r/2$, their sum is a short pulse with the wiggle of an inductor-capacitor pair; if $t_s\ll t_r$ they cancel partially, forming a short low-pass T-section whose response is smoother than either alone, which is the idea behind optimizing a via with the right anti-pad size. If $t_s$ is long (several $t_r$) the reflections are separate, and a TDR sees two bumps. The rule of thumb is that when a pair of discontinuities is closer than about $t_r/6$ in delay you can lump them (add the $C$ and the $L$), and when they are farther apart you treat them separately and, in the worst case, add their peaks.

## Limiting cases and when the lumped formulas fail

- **Large discontinuities.** The approximations $\rho\approx Z_0C/(2t_r)$ and $L/(2Z_0t_r)$ assume $\rho\ll1$. For a long stub or a large pad (delay greater than $t_r/6$), use the transmission-line formula directly; the reflection can reach tens of percent.
- **Slow edges.** For $t_r$ much larger than the round-trip delay of the whole net, the net is a lumped capacitor and the driver charges it through its output resistance (an $RC$ edge); reflections never appear, and termination is unnecessary.
- **Lossy lines.** A very lossy trace attenuates reflections as well as the signal, which can mask discontinuities: a 10 dB-loss line reduces a round-trip reflection by 20 dB. This helps ringing and hurts the usefulness of TDR at the far end.
- **Nonlinear drivers and receivers.** CMOS drivers have output impedance that varies during the edge and receivers have clamp diodes, so the linear bounce picture is an approximation. Use IBIS models in simulation for sign-off.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **Series resistor value.** Driver output impedance varies with process, voltage and temperature (often by ±20 to 30 %), so the nominal $Z_0-R_{out}$ is a starting value; choose $R_s$ from the IBIS data and check the extremes.
- **Placement of the series resistor.** It must be close to the driver (a few millimeters); a long trace between driver and resistor is an unterminated stub.
- **Test points are stubs.** A probe pad on the end of a 5 mm stub is a visible reflection at 100 ps edges. Put test points on the net, or use a series resistor and probe after it.
- **Via stubs.** On thick boards a through-via with an unused section is a quarter-wave stub; back-drilling or using the layers closest to the surface avoids it.
- **ESD clamp diodes.** An overshoot that exceeds the supply by a diode drop injects current into the rail; this is a reliability and noise issue, not just a signal-integrity one.

## Bench verification

Use a TDR (or a scope with a fast step and a splitter) to see the impedance profile and locate each feature by time (1 ns round-trip is about 85 mm of microstrip). Confirm the T-junction prediction directly: probe a receiver pin with a short-spring ground and a high-bandwidth probe, drive a clean edge, and look for the staircase of 2.2, 2.9 and 3.2 V steps spaced by the branch round trip. For termination, measure the received waveform with and without the series resistor, then change its value 10 to 20 % either way and see the overshoot or ringing change; the best value is where the first plateau is flat and the final level is reached cleanly. A VNA's time-domain transform of $S_{11}$ gives a calibrated alternative to TDR for finding discontinuities.

::: applications
- **Clock distribution**: series-terminated point-to-point lines to avoid ringing and false clocking.
- **DDR fly-by routing**: stubs minimised so reflections from each DRAM are small.
- **High-speed serial links**: connector/via optimisation using S-parameters and TDR.
- **Debugging a ringing GPIO or SPI line** with a scope: look for overshoot and the period $4t_d$.
- **RF boards**: matching networks and mitred bends to hold the return loss low.

::: assumptions
- Reflections are analysed for a **linear line with real impedances**; real drivers are nonlinear and their impedance varies with voltage.
- The small-discontinuity formulas hold when $Z_0C/2$ or $L/2Z_0$ is much less than $t_r$.
- The example profile in the TDR plot is schematic; real TDR traces are smoothed by the instrument's rise time.
- Slow edges on short lines ($t_d<t_r/6$) don't show reflections as separate events.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a CMOS receiver at the end of a line reflect almost everything?
Its input is a few pF with essentially infinite resistance, so at the edge's frequencies it looks like an open, $\Gamma\approx+1$. The wave doubles at the receiver and returns. Unless the source end is matched, it re-reflects and rings.

### How would you find an unknown reflection source on a built board?
Use a TDR (or a fast scope with a step) to see the impedance profile and locate bumps by time (1 ns round trip is about 85 mm of FR-4 microstrip). Each bump's sign tells whether it's capacitive (dip) or inductive (peak); I match them to features in the layout.

### Does a series resistor at the source remove all reflections?
It removes the *re-reflection* at the source: the first wave launched is half amplitude, reflects at the open load to full amplitude, returns and is absorbed by the matched source. Mid-line discontinuities still reflect, but any reflected energy is absorbed at the source instead of bouncing. See [[q0533]].

### What if the stub is 3 mm long and the edge is 2 ns?
Stub delay is about 18 ps, far below $t_r/6=333$ ps, so it's a small capacitance (a few tenths pF); the reflection is below 1%. It's fine. At 100 ps edges the same stub is a visible disturbance.

### Can reflections ever be useful?
Occasionally, for example in "reflected-wave switching" (the first incident wave only reaches half-level, the reflection completes the swing), used in some bus designs; and TDR relies on them. For normal digital interconnect they are noise.
