::: basics
### In plain words
Capacitance tells you how much charge a device stores for each volt across it: $C = Q/V$. It is measured in farads. A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator. Put a voltage across it and charge collects on the two sides. Capacitors are used to store energy briefly and to smooth out voltage changes.

### Picture it
Think of a bucket. The water level is the voltage, the amount of water is the charge, and the width of the bucket is the capacitance. A wide bucket takes a lot of water to raise the level by one centimeter. A narrow one takes little. Likewise, a large capacitance takes a lot of charge to change its voltage. The ideal bucket never overflows, but a real capacitor has a maximum voltage rating.

### Words you need
- **Charge (Q)**: the amount of electric charge stored, measured in coulombs.
- **Farad (F)**: the unit of capacitance. One farad is one coulomb per volt. Real parts are pF to mF.
- **Plates**: the two conductors that hold the opposite charges.
- **Dielectric**: the insulator between the plates. It sets how much capacitance you get.
- **Current into a capacitor**: $i = C\,dv/dt$, so changing the voltage takes current and time.

### The simplest example
Charge a 1 µF capacitor to 5 V. It holds $Q = CV = 1\ \mu\text{F} \times 5\ \text{V} = 5\ \mu\text{C}$. To charge it, push 1 mA into it. That raises the voltage by 1 V every millisecond, so it takes 5 ms to reach 5 V. The voltage cannot jump, because charge takes time to arrive. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers what sets the capacitance, where the stored energy sits, and why $i = C\,dv/dt$ matters in real circuits.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand capacitance as a **physical property** rather than as "the thing measured in farads". The interviewer wants to hear:

1. The definition $C = Q/V$: charge stored per volt, and that the farad is a coulomb per volt.
2. What sets it: geometry and material, $C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/d$ for parallel plates, and *not* the applied voltage (for an ideal capacitor).
3. That it stores **energy in an electric field**, $E = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2$.
4. The consequence for circuits: $i = C\,dv/dt$, so capacitance resists changes in voltage. Strong candidates also note that capacitance exists everywhere, wanted or not (parasitics).
:::

::: why
Every circuit you'll ever build has capacitance: decoupling capacitors holding up a supply rail, timing capacitors in oscillators, the gate capacitance a MOSFET driver has to charge, the trace-to-plane capacitance that slows edges, the sample-and-hold capacitor in an ADC. Understanding *what* capacitance is lets you reason about all of them with one idea: stored charge per volt, and the current needed to change that voltage. It's also the foundation for the rest of this section: impedance ([[q0024]]), the differential equation ([[q0025]]), continuity ([[q0037]]), and the real-world model ([[q0030]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Charge and voltage**: voltage is energy per unit charge; separating charge across a gap creates a voltage. See [[q0001]] for the basic circuit quantities.
- **Electric field in an insulator**: the field between the plates is what actually stores the energy.
- **Dielectric and permittivity**: an insulating material that polarizes and lets the plates hold more charge per volt. See [[q0023]].
- **Geometry dependence**: larger plate area and smaller spacing mean more capacitance.
- **Rate of change**: the current into a capacitor is proportional to how fast its voltage changes, $i = C\,dv/dt$ ([[q0025]]).
- **Symbol and physical structure**: two plates separated by a gap ([[q0021]]).
:::

::: answer
"Capacitance is a measure of how much charge a device stores per volt across it. If I have two conductors separated by an insulator and I put a voltage $V$ across them, a charge $+Q$ collects on one and $-Q$ on the other, and the ratio is the capacitance: $C = Q/V$. The unit is the farad, which is one coulomb per volt, and that's a huge amount, so in practice we work in picofarads up to millifarads.

It's set by geometry and material, not by the voltage. For a parallel-plate capacitor it's $C = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r A/d$: more plate area or a thinner gap gives more capacitance, and a dielectric with higher permittivity multiplies it.

Physically, the energy is stored in the electric field between the plates, and it's $\tfrac{1}{2}CV^2$.

The circuit consequence is the important part. Since $Q = CV$, differentiating gives $i = C\,dv/dt$. So to change a capacitor's voltage you have to move charge, which takes current and time. That's why capacitors smooth out supply rails and why every node in a circuit, with its stray capacitance, can't change voltage instantly."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** For two conductors carrying equal and opposite charge $\pm Q$ with potential difference $V$:
$$C = \frac{Q}{V}\qquad [\text{F} = \text{C/V} = \text{A·s/V}]$$

**Parallel-plate capacitor** (plate area $A$, gap $d$, fringing neglected): Gauss's law gives a uniform field $E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon A}$, and $V = Ed$, so
$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A}{d},\qquad \varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}$$
Example: 1 cm × 1 cm plates, 0.1 mm air gap gives **8.85 pF**. The same plates with FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r\approx4.3$) give about **38 pF**, which is roughly the capacitance of 1 cm² of a power/ground plane pair separated by 0.1 mm.

**Energy.** Moving charge $dq$ across voltage $v = q/C$ takes $dW = v\,dq$:
$$W = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2$$
100 µF at 12 V stores 7.2 mJ; a 1 F, 2.7 V supercapacitor stores 3.6 J.

**Circuit law.** With constant $C$, $q = Cv \Rightarrow i = C\frac{dv}{dt}$. A 1 mA constant current into 1 µF ramps at 1 V/ms. Consequently capacitor voltage cannot change instantaneously without infinite current (the continuity condition, [[q0037]]).

**Combinations.** Parallel capacitors add ($C = C_1 + C_2$, area adds); series capacitors combine reciprocally ($1/C = 1/C_1 + 1/C_2$, gap adds).

**Real-world deviations.** Class II ceramics (X5R/X7R) lose capacitance with DC bias, temperature and age, so "$C$ is constant" is an idealization. Real parts add ESR, ESL and leakage ([[q0030]]).
:::

::: long
"Capacitance describes the ability of two conductors separated by an insulator to store charge. When I put a voltage across them, electrons are pulled off one conductor and pushed onto the other, so one side ends up with $+Q$ and the other with $-Q$. The capacitance is just the ratio, $C = Q/V$, in farads, which are coulombs per volt.

What sets it is geometry and the material in the gap. For the classic parallel-plate case, $C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/d$. More area means more room for charge, a thinner gap means the opposite charges attract each other more strongly so more charge accumulates for the same voltage, and a dielectric with relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r$ multiplies the whole thing. To give a sense of scale, two 1 cm square plates 0.1 mm apart in air are only about 9 pF. That's why practical capacitors either use enormous effective area, like the etched foil in an electrolytic, or very high permittivity and very thin layers, like a multilayer ceramic with hundreds of stacked layers.

The energy is stored in the electric field between the plates, and it works out to $\tfrac{1}{2}CV^2$, so doubling the voltage quadruples the energy.

For circuits, the most useful form is the derivative. Since $Q = CV$, the current is $i = C\,dv/dt$. That tells me a capacitor resists changes in voltage: to move its voltage quickly, I need a lot of current. A 1 µF capacitor charged with 1 mA ramps at 1 volt per millisecond. That one equation explains decoupling, RC timing, slew limits on gate drivers, and why at DC steady state a capacitor carries no current.

Finally, capacitance isn't only in capacitors. Any two conductors near each other have it: traces over a ground plane, a MOSFET's gate, a scope probe. A lot of real debugging is finding the capacitance you didn't put there on purpose."
:::

::: terms
- **Capacitance ($C$)**: charge stored per unit voltage, $C = Q/V$.
- **Farad (F)**: SI unit of capacitance, one coulomb per volt. Practical values range from pF to mF, with supercapacitors reaching kilofarads.
- **Permittivity ($\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$)**: how strongly a material supports an electric field by polarizing; $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854$ pF/m.
- **Relative permittivity / dielectric constant ($\varepsilon_r$)**: permittivity relative to vacuum (air ≈ 1, FR-4 ≈ 4, Class II ceramics in the thousands).
- **Electric field energy**: the stored energy $\tfrac12 CV^2$, located in the field between the conductors.
- **Parasitic (stray) capacitance**: unintended capacitance between any two conductors, such as a trace and a plane.
- **Displacement current**: the changing electric field between the plates, which keeps current continuous through the circuit even though no charge crosses the dielectric.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "capacitance is the ability to store charge, measured in farads."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the ratio definition $C = Q/V$ and the unit as C/V.
- Explains what *determines* capacitance, $\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/d$, with intuition for each term, and notes it doesn't depend on voltage for an ideal part.
- States where the energy lives (the field) and $\tfrac12CV^2$.
- Connects to circuits with $i = C\,dv/dt$ and a quick number (1 mA into 1 µF = 1 V/ms).
- Mentions that real Class II ceramics violate "constant C" under DC bias, and that parasitic capacitance is everywhere.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"A capacitor stores charge."** Net charge on a capacitor is zero ($+Q$ and $-Q$). It stores *separated* charge, and really it stores **energy** in the electric field.
- **Thinking capacitance depends on the voltage applied.** For an ideal capacitor, $C$ is geometry and material only; $Q$ scales with $V$. (Class II ceramics are a real-world exception due to their nonlinear dielectric.)
- **Getting series/parallel backwards.** Capacitors in parallel *add* (like resistors in series). In series they combine like resistors in parallel.
- **Believing current flows through the dielectric.** No conduction current crosses an ideal dielectric; the circuit current is continued by displacement current $\varepsilon\,\partial E/\partial t$ as charge piles up on the plates.
- **Underestimating how big a farad is.** A 1 F air-gap parallel-plate capacitor with a 0.1 mm gap would need about 11 km² of plate area.
:::

::: deep
## Starting from charge and fields

Take two flat metal plates, area $A$, separated by a gap $d$, and connect them to a battery. The battery pulls electrons off the plate attached to its positive terminal and pushes the same number onto the other plate. Current flows in the wires only until the plates' voltage equals the battery's. At that point one plate holds $+Q$, the other $-Q$, and an electric field $E$ points across the gap.

Capacitance is the proportionality constant between that charge and voltage:

$$Q = CV$$

Why is $Q$ proportional to $V$? Because the field is proportional to the charge (Gauss's law), and the voltage is the field integrated across the gap. Double the charge, double the field, double the voltage. The constant of proportionality depends only on the shape of the conductors and the material between them.

### Deriving the parallel-plate formula

Gauss's law for a sheet of charge density $\sigma = Q/A$, with the field confined between the plates, gives:

$$E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon} = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon A}$$

The voltage is the field times the distance (uniform field):

$$V = Ed = \frac{Qd}{\varepsilon A}$$

So:

$$\boxed{C = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A}{d}}$$

Each term has intuition:
- **Area $A$**: more plate means more room for charge at the same surface density.
- **Gap $d$**: a thinner gap means a given voltage produces a stronger field, which means more charge per volt.
- **Permittivity $\varepsilon_r$**: a dielectric polarizes, partially cancelling the field from the free charge, so the plates can hold more free charge before reaching the same voltage ([[q0023]]).

## A sense of scale

The farad is enormous. Let me put numbers on that:

| Structure | Calculation | Capacitance |
|---|---|---|
| 1 cm² plates, 0.1 mm air gap | $8.854\text{p}\times10^{-4}/10^{-4}$ | 8.85 pF |
| 1 cm² of plane pair, 0.1 mm FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r$≈4.3) | ×4.3 | ≈ 38 pF |
| Air-gap plates for 1 F at 0.1 mm | $A = Cd/\varepsilon_0$ | ≈ 11 km² of plate |
| Isolated sphere the size of Earth | $4\pi\varepsilon_0 R$ | ≈ 709 µF |

Real capacitors get from picofarads to microfarads and beyond by attacking every term:

- **MLCCs** stack hundreds of thin layers (each a few µm or less) of a barium-titanate ceramic with $\varepsilon_r$ in the thousands, all connected in parallel. Illustrative example: 100 layers × 0.25 mm² overlap × $\varepsilon_r = 2000$ at a 2 µm spacing gives about 0.22 µF in a tiny chip.
- **Aluminium electrolytics** etch the foil to multiply its surface area many times and use an anodised oxide only nanometres thick per volt of rating.
- **Supercapacitors** (EDLCs) use activated carbon with enormous internal surface area and a charge separation of molecular dimensions, reaching farads to kilofarads at a few volts.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The MLCC layer count, thickness and permittivity above are illustrative, not from a specific datasheet. Real values vary widely by manufacturer, case size and voltage rating.

## Where the energy goes

Charging a capacitor means moving each additional bit of charge $dq$ "uphill" against the voltage already built up, $v = q/C$:

$$W = \int_0^Q v\,dq = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\,dq = \frac{Q^2}{2C} = \frac12 CV^2$$

That energy is stored in the electric field. The energy density in a dielectric is $\tfrac12\varepsilon E^2$, and integrating it over the volume $A d$ gives exactly $\tfrac12 CV^2$. Discharge the capacitor and the field collapses, delivering the energy back to the circuit.

Numbers: a 100 µF capacitor at 12 V stores $\tfrac12(100\,\mu)(12^2) = 7.2$ mJ. A 1 F supercap at 2.7 V stores 3.6 J. Notice the $V^2$: that's why high-voltage capacitors in camera flashes and power supplies can be dangerous even at modest capacitance.

## From Q = CV to circuit behaviour

The single most useful consequence for circuits comes from differentiating $q = Cv$ with respect to time:

$$i = \frac{dq}{dt} = C\frac{dv}{dt}$$

This says the current into a capacitor is proportional to how fast its voltage is changing ([[q0025]]). Two practical readings:

1. **Constant current makes a ramp.** 1 mA into 1 µF: $dv/dt = I/C = 1$ V/ms. Charging it to 3.3 V takes 3.3 ms. This is how integrators, ramp generators and soft-start circuits work.
2. **Fast voltage changes demand big currents.** Changing a 1 nF node by 5 V in 5 ns needs $I = C\,\Delta V/\Delta t = 1$ A. That's why gate drivers need peak current ratings in amps and why every node's stray capacitance limits edge speed.

## Displacement current: how "current flows through" a capacitor

No electrons cross an ideal dielectric. Yet if you put an ammeter in series with a capacitor driven by AC, it reads a steady current. The resolution, due to Maxwell, is that a changing electric field acts as a current, the **displacement current** $\varepsilon\,\partial E/\partial t$ per unit area. As the field between the plates grows, charge piles up on one plate and leaves the other, so the wire current on both sides is the same. That's why KCL still works for a capacitor treated as a two-terminal element.

## Capacitance you didn't ask for

Since any two conductors have capacitance, it shows up everywhere:

- **PCB traces** over a ground plane: a few pF per inch to tens of pF, depending on geometry.
- **MOSFET gates**: $C_{iss}$ from hundreds of pF to several nF in power devices; the gate driver must supply $Q_g$ each switching edge.
- **IC input pins**: typically a few pF, which together with a high-impedance source forms an RC low-pass.
- **Inductor windings and resistors**: parallel parasitic capacitance that limits their high-frequency behaviour ([[q0011]]).

## When "C" isn't constant

The ratio $C = Q/V$ is a constant only if the dielectric is linear. Class II ceramics (X5R, X7R) use ferroelectric barium titanate whose permittivity drops as the DC field rises, so a 10 µF, 6.3 V X5R in 0603 can deliver well under half its rated capacitance at its rated voltage. Varactor diodes deliberately exploit voltage-dependent capacitance. For these, engineers use the **small-signal capacitance** $C(V) = dQ/dV$ around the operating point.
:::

::: applications
- **Decoupling and bulk storage**: a 100 nF ceramic next to an IC supply pin supplies charge for fast current spikes, using $i = C\,dv/dt$ to limit the rail's dv/dt ([[q0032]]).
- **Timing**: an RC with $\tau = RC$ sets delays in reset circuits, debouncers and the 555 timer.
- **Energy storage**: camera flash capacitors, supercapacitors for RTC backup or holding up a microcontroller long enough to write to flash on power loss.
- **Sensing**: capacitive touch sensors detect the extra capacitance a finger adds to an electrode; MEMS accelerometers measure capacitance change as a proof mass moves.
- **Sample-and-hold**: an ADC's internal capacitor holds the input voltage during conversion.
- **Parasitic budgeting**: estimating trace and pin capacitance to predict edge rates and I²C rise times.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear, ideal dielectric**: $C$ independent of voltage, frequency and temperature. Real Class II ceramics, electrolytics and varactors deviate.
- **Parallel-plate formula ignores fringing**: valid when the gap is much smaller than the plate dimensions; otherwise fringing fields add capacitance.
- **Quasi-static**: lumped capacitance is valid when the structure is small compared with a wavelength. At very high frequencies, structures like PCB planes behave as transmission lines or cavities.
- **No leakage, ESR or ESL**: all real capacitors have them ([[q0030]]).

An alternative definition you might hear is the incremental one, $C = dQ/dV$, which is equivalent for linear capacitors and the correct one for nonlinear devices.
:::

::: followups
### How much charge is stored on a 22 µF capacitor at 5 V, and how long could it supply 1 mA before dropping 100 mV?
$Q = CV = 22\,\mu\text{F}\times5 = 110$ µC. For the droop: $\Delta t = C\,\Delta V/I = 22\,\mu\times0.1/1\,\text{m} = 2.2$ ms. This is the kind of hold-up calculation used for bulk capacitors.

### Why do capacitors in parallel add?
In parallel they share the same voltage, so the total charge is $Q_1 + Q_2 = (C_1 + C_2)V$. Physically, you've just increased the plate area. In series, the same charge sits on each, and the voltages add: $V = Q/C_1 + Q/C_2$, so $1/C = 1/C_1 + 1/C_2$, which is like increasing the gap.

### If you double the voltage across a capacitor, what happens to the charge and the energy?
Charge doubles ($Q = CV$) and energy quadruples ($\tfrac12CV^2$). That's why high-voltage capacitors are rated and handled carefully: a 470 µF capacitor at 400 V stores about 37.6 J, enough to injure.

### What is the capacitance between a trace and a ground plane, roughly?
Approximate the trace as a parallel plate: width × length over the dielectric height, times $\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$, plus fringing. A 0.2 mm wide, 25 mm long trace 0.1 mm above a plane in FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r$≈4.3): $C \approx 8.854\,\text{pF/m}\times4.3\times\frac{(0.2\,\text{mm})(25\,\text{mm})}{0.1\,\text{mm}} = 8.854\,\text{pF/m}\times4.3\times0.05\,\text{m} \approx 1.9$ pF, and fringing typically adds a sizeable fraction on top. Real tools use field solvers.

### What if a capacitor's capacitance depends on voltage? How do you define C then?
Use the incremental capacitance $C(V) = dQ/dV$ at the operating point. For a Class II MLCC with DC bias, the small-signal capacitance an AC ripple "sees" is the slope of the Q–V curve at that bias, which can be far less than the nameplate value. For large-signal energy you integrate: $W = \int V\,dQ$, which no longer equals $\tfrac12CV^2$ with a single C.
