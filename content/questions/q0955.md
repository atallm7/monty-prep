::: testing
Whether you understand that chip design is **constrained optimization**: you can never improve all three of power, performance and area at once, and every design decision moves at least two of them. The interviewer is looking for:

1. The correct definitions, with performance as a *rate* (frequency, throughput, latency) and area as a *cost* proxy (die size, yield, dollars).
2. Concrete knobs and what each one trades: pipelining, voltage, transistor sizing and threshold flavors, caches, parallelism.
3. Quantitative intuition: $P\propto CV^2f$, $f$ versus $V$, the cost of area through yield.
4. An engineer's awareness that the weights depend on the product: a phone, a server, an IoT sensor and a GPU all pick different corners of the triangle.
:::

::: why
PPA is the vocabulary of every architecture review and synthesis report. Whenever a team says "this adds 3% area for 8% speed" or "we can win back power with a high-threshold library", they are working in PPA. For an intern it is also a quick way to show you can connect circuit-level facts (switching energy, delay, wire cost) to product decisions, and that you know there are no free lunches. Cost matters as much as technical metrics: area drives die cost super-linearly through yield, so a "small" area increase is rarely small.
:::

::: concepts
- **Power**: dynamic $\alpha C V^2 f$ plus static leakage. See [[q0947]] for voltage and [[q0945]] for frequency.
- **Performance**: how fast the chip finishes useful work: clock frequency times instructions per cycle, or throughput and latency at the system level. See [[q0909]] for pipelining.
- **Area**: silicon die area in mm$^2$, which sets cost via dies per wafer and yield.
- **Operating point**: voltage, frequency and temperature; moving along the V-f curve trades power against speed. See [[q0949]].
- **Design knobs**: pipeline depth, parallel units, cache size, logic sizing, threshold-voltage flavors, library choice, utilization.
- **Figures of merit**: energy per operation, energy-delay product, performance per watt, performance per dollar.
:::

::: answer
"PPA is power, performance and area, the three numbers you trade against each other in chip design.

Performance is how fast the thing does its job, so clock speed, instructions per cycle, throughput. Power is how much energy it burns, which is the switching power, alpha C V squared f, plus leakage. Area is how much silicon it uses, which really means cost, because bigger dies yield worse and fit fewer per wafer.

The trade-offs are everywhere. If I want more speed I can raise voltage, but power goes up with V squared on top of the frequency, so you pay roughly cubically. I can deepen the pipeline to raise the clock, which costs flop area and clock power. I can add parallel units or a bigger cache to raise throughput, which costs area and leakage. I can use low-threshold-voltage cells on critical paths, which are faster but leak more. And I can go the other way, relaxing the clock target so synthesis uses smaller, slower, lower-power cells.

Which corner you pick depends on the product. A phone SoC cares about power and area. A server CPU pays area and power for performance. A tiny IoT sensor wants minimum area and almost no power, and it doesn't care how slow it is."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.**
- **Power**: $P = \alpha C V_{DD}^2 f + V_{DD} I_{leak}$. Dynamic power scales with activity, switched capacitance, voltage squared and frequency. Leakage grows roughly exponentially with temperature and with lower threshold voltage.
- **Performance**: for a processor, $\text{time} = \frac{\text{instructions}\times \text{CPI}}{f}$. For a block it can be maximum clock frequency, latency, or throughput.
- **Area**: die area $A$. Cost per good die is wafer cost divided by (dies per wafer $\times$ yield). A common yield model is Poisson, $Y = e^{-D_0 A}$, and dies per wafer fall roughly as $1/A$ with edge losses.

**Core relationships.**
- Frequency rises with voltage (alpha-power law $f\propto (V - V_t)^\alpha / V$), so DVFS moves power superlinearly: roughly $P\propto f^3$ in the regime where $f\propto V$.
- Pipelining shortens the cycle to $T = T_{logic}/N + T_{overhead}$ with diminishing returns, while flops and clock-tree power grow with $N$.
- Parallelism raises throughput linearly in area; because it allows a lower voltage and frequency for the same throughput, it can also reduce energy per operation.
- Threshold-voltage flavors: low-$V_t$ is faster and leakier, high-$V_t$ slower and lower leakage; synthesis mixes them to meet timing at minimum leakage.
- Cache and memory: more cache raises IPC but costs area and leakage with diminishing hit-rate gains.

**Typical flow.** Fix a target (for example "2 GHz in 5 mm$^2$ under 1 W"), synthesize, read the report for slack, area and power, then iterate. The final design is a point on a Pareto surface rather than a single optimum.

**Related metrics.** Energy per operation (J/op), energy-delay product $E\cdot D$, performance per watt, and performance per dollar. The metric chosen encodes the product's priorities.
:::

Area is silicon cost. Raising voltage or deepening the pipeline buys speed but costs power or area. Relaxing timing saves power and area. Which trade you make depends on the product: a phone wants low power, a server wants performance, an IoT chip wants small area."

::: long
"PPA stands for power, performance and area. Those are the three things you optimize when you design a chip, and they pull against each other.

Performance is how quickly the chip finishes work: clock frequency, instructions per cycle, throughput. Power has two parts: dynamic power, alpha C V squared f, from switching capacitance, and static leakage that depends on threshold voltage and temperature. Area is the silicon footprint, and it matters mostly because it's cost: a bigger die means fewer dies per wafer and a lower yield, so cost grows faster than area.

Here's how the knobs trade. Voltage: frequency rises roughly linearly with voltage, but power rises with V squared times f, so a ten percent speed gain can cost a third more power. Pipelining: more stages shorten the clock period, but the flops add area and clock power, and returns diminish because each stage has fixed overhead. Parallelism: duplicate a unit and you double throughput for double area, but because you can run each copy slower at lower voltage, you may even save energy per task. Sizing and threshold flavors: low-threshold cells on critical paths close timing but leak, high-threshold cells everywhere else save leakage. Caches: more cache raises performance until the hit rate saturates, and costs area and leakage.

So the practical answer is: decide what the product needs, set the targets, synthesize, and walk along the Pareto curve. A phone cares about energy and area, a server about performance per watt, an IoT chip about area and microwatts."
:::

::: terms
- **PPA**: power, performance, area, the three principal design metrics. Sometimes extended to PPAC with cost.
- **Dynamic power**: $\alpha C V^2 f$, the energy used by charging and discharging capacitance.
- **Leakage (static) power**: current that flows even when nothing switches; grows with temperature and lower $V_t$.
- **Pareto frontier**: the set of designs where improving one metric necessarily worsens another.
- **Timing slack**: the margin between the required and achieved cycle time; negative slack means failing timing.
- **Yield**: the fraction of manufactured dies that work.
- **Utilization**: fraction of the floorplan area occupied by cells; high values hurt routability.
- **Energy-delay product (EDP)**: energy per operation times delay, which balances efficiency and speed.
- **Multi-$V_t$ library**: cell variants with different thresholds trading speed against leakage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** spells out the acronym and says "they trade off against each other".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the formulas ($P=\alpha CV^2f$ plus leakage, execution time $=\text{IC}\times\text{CPI}/f$, yield-based cost) rather than slogans.
- Shows one worked trade with numbers, such as pipelining returns or the V-f-P curve.
- Explains that area is cost through yield, super-linear in die size.
- Notes the surprise: parallelism plus lower voltage can improve power *and* performance per area.
- Frames the target by product (phone, server, IoT) and mentions the metrics that capture it (perf/W, EDP).
- Mentions that the answer is a Pareto surface and that PVT corners complicate it.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating the three axes as independent.** Almost every knob moves at least two of them; list the cost of each gain.
- **Equating performance with clock frequency.** Instructions per cycle, memory stalls and throughput matter; a lower-clocked wide design can win.
- **Thinking power scales linearly with speed.** With voltage scaling it is closer to cubic, so the last 10% of frequency is very expensive.
- **Treating area as only silicon size.** Area drives cost through yield and dies per wafer, so the cost increase exceeds the area increase.
- **Ignoring leakage.** In advanced nodes and at high temperature, leakage can dominate; power is not just $\alpha CV^2f$.
- **Forgetting PVT.** A design must meet timing at the slow corner and power limits at the fast, hot corner. See [[q0949]].
:::

::: deep
## The three axes, one at a time

**Performance** is the one with the most meanings. For a CPU core, execution time is
$$T_{exec} = \frac{N_{instr}\times \text{CPI}}{f}$$
so you can win through higher $f$ (circuit and pipeline), lower CPI (microarchitecture: caches, branch prediction, wider issue), or fewer instructions (ISA, compiler). A GPU or accelerator cares about throughput: operations per second regardless of the latency of any one.

**Power** splits into dynamic and static:
$$P = \underbrace{\alpha\, C\, V_{DD}^2\, f}_{\text{switching}} + \underbrace{V_{DD} I_{leak}}_{\text{static}}$$
Each term responds to different knobs. Dynamic power falls with lower activity (clock gating), lower capacitance (smaller cells, shorter wires), lower voltage and lower frequency. Leakage falls with higher thresholds, power gating, lower temperature and lower voltage.

**Area** is not the objective in itself; **cost** is. Dies per 300 mm wafer is approximately
$$\text{DPW}\approx \frac{\pi (d/2)^2}{A} - \frac{\pi d}{\sqrt{2A}}$$
where $d$ is the wafer diameter, and with a Poisson yield model $Y = e^{-D_0 A}$ the cost per good die is $C_w/(\text{DPW}\cdot Y)$. Using an illustrative wafer cost of USD 17,000 and a defect density of 0.1 per cm$^2$:

| Die area | Dies per wafer | Yield | Good dies | Cost per good die |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 mm$^2$ | 640 | 90.5% | 579 | USD 29 |
| 150 mm$^2$ | 417 | 86.1% | 359 | USD 47 |
| 200 mm$^2$ | 306 | 81.9% | 251 | USD 68 |

A 50% larger die costs 61% more; a doubling of area costs 2.3 times as much. Those are made-up but plausible numbers (see the uncertainty note) and the shape is the point: area is super-linearly expensive.

## Trade 1: voltage buys speed at a superlinear power price

With the alpha-power-law model, $f\propto (V-V_t)^{\alpha}/V$. Taking $V_t = 0.3$ V, $\alpha = 1.3$ and normalizing to 0.9 V:

| $V_{DD}$ | relative $f$ | relative dynamic power ($V^2 f$) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.6 V | 0.61 | 0.27 |
| 0.7 V | 0.76 | 0.46 |
| 0.8 V | 0.89 | 0.70 |
| 0.9 V | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 1.0 V | 1.10 | 1.36 |
| 1.1 V | 1.19 | 1.78 |

Going from 0.9 V to 1.0 V buys 10% frequency for 36% more dynamic power. Going from 0.9 V down to 0.7 V loses 24% of the speed but saves 54% of the dynamic power, a 1.65 times improvement in energy per operation ($0.81/0.49$). This is the physics behind DVFS and why "race to idle" is not always best.

## Trade 2: pipelining and diminishing returns

Suppose a function has 1000 ps of logic and each pipeline stage costs 60 ps of flop overhead (clock-to-Q plus setup, ignoring skew). With $N$ stages the cycle is $T = 1000/N + 60$ ps:

| Stages $N$ | Cycle (ps) | Frequency (MHz) | Speed-up vs $N=1$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1060 | 943 | 1.00 |
| 2 | 560 | 1786 | 1.89 |
| 4 | 310 | 3226 | 3.42 |
| 8 | 185 | 5405 | 5.73 |
| 16 | 122.5 | 8163 | 8.65 |

Sixteen times as many pipeline registers gives only 8.65 times the clock, because the fixed overhead dominates. Meanwhile the flops grow in area, and the clock tree and flop switching grow in power, since flops toggle their clock every cycle. Real pipelines also suffer longer branch-mispredict penalties, so CPI worsens. The sweet spot is typically at a moderate depth, which is why high-end CPUs have settled around 10 to 20 stages rather than going deeper.

## Trade 3: parallelism and the energy view

Duplicating a unit doubles area, but two units at half the frequency deliver the same throughput. Half the frequency permits a lower voltage. In the model above, a relative speed of 0.5 per unit needs only about 0.54 V. At that voltage dynamic power per unit is about 0.18 of nominal, so two units draw about 0.36 of the original dynamic power, with the same throughput and twice the area. Real designs cannot go this low: minimum-voltage limits, SRAM stability and variation put a floor on $V_{DD}$, and leakage from the doubled area eats into the gain, so treat 0.36 as an upper bound on the benefit. That is "spend area to buy power" and it is the argument for multicore, SIMD and GPUs. Leakage grows with area, which limits the gain, and parallel work must exist in the software.

## Trade 4: sizing, thresholds, and library choice

Synthesis and place-and-route do this automatically. On a critical path, low-$V_t$ (or "ultra-low-$V_t$") cells and larger drive strengths cut delay at the price of leakage and area. On the 80% of paths with slack, high-$V_t$ and minimum-sized cells save leakage and area. Relaxing the clock target by even 10% often lets the tool shrink area and power noticeably, because the tool stops upsizing and buffering, which is the "timing wall" effect: results are flat for relaxed targets and degrade sharply as you approach the technology limit. Hence the practice of sweeping frequency targets and plotting power and area against them.

## Trade 5: memory and specialization

Caches buy CPI at area and leakage cost: doubling cache capacity typically gives smaller and smaller hit-rate gains. Specialized accelerators trade flexibility for orders of magnitude better energy per operation on their target workload, at the price of area that is idle when the function is not used (dark silicon). Choosing the memory hierarchy and what to accelerate is usually the largest PPA lever, bigger than any circuit trick.

## Reading it as a Pareto surface

No design is "best". You get a frontier of points, and the product requirement picks the one. A useful way to compare is a single figure of merit that matches the product: performance per watt for data centers, energy per operation for battery devices, performance per dollar for consumer parts. Teams usually fix two as constraints and optimize the third: "must hit 2 GHz and 1 W, minimize area."

## Process, voltage and temperature interplay

Timing sign-off is done at the slow corner (low voltage, high temperature for older nodes), while worst-case power is evaluated at the fast corner and high temperature for leakage. The design has to close both, so PPA is quoted for a specified corner. See [[q0949]] and [[q0950]].
:::

::: applications
- **Smartphone application processors**: big.LITTLE-style clusters of fast, large cores and slow, small, efficient cores trade performance against power and area at the architecture level.
- **Server CPUs**: spend area on large caches and wide cores to maximize per-socket performance within a power envelope.
- **GPUs and AI accelerators**: many simple parallel units at modest clocks, buying throughput with area and keeping energy per operation low.
- **Microcontrollers for IoT**: minimum-area cores at low frequency and voltage with aggressive power gating, because cost and microwatts matter more than speed.
- **Standard-cell library selection**: choosing between high-density, high-performance and low-power libraries at synthesis time.
- **Chiplet-based products**: splitting a big die into smaller dies improves yield and therefore cost, paying with packaging area and interconnect power.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CMOS logic dominated by dynamic power.** At advanced nodes and high temperature, leakage can be a large fraction; at deep sub-threshold or ultra-low-power operation it dominates.
- **Alpha-power delay model** with $V_t = 0.3$ V, $\alpha=1.3$. These are illustrative; real processes have different parameters and the model fails near $V_t$.
- **Poisson yield and the dies-per-wafer approximation** are textbook simplifications; foundries use more detailed negative-binomial models and have systematic yield loss.
- **Pipelining overhead** of 60 ps and 1000 ps of logic are example numbers, not a real design.
- **Product context.** If the interviewer says "battery device", weight power; "server", performance per watt; "cost-sensitive consumer part", area.

Another valid reading is PPAC, adding cost or "C" for time-to-market and schedule; mention it if the interviewer hints at business constraints.
:::

::: followups
### Give me one concrete knob that improves performance and say what it costs in power and area.
Deepening the pipeline from 4 to 8 stages in the example takes the clock from 3.2 GHz to 5.4 GHz (1.68 times), but doubles the number of pipeline registers (area), raises clock-tree and flop switching power, and makes the branch-misprediction penalty longer, so IPC drops. Net performance gains are less than the clock ratio, and the power cost is above it.

### Why might using more area actually reduce power?
Because parallelism lets each unit run slower at lower voltage. With dynamic power $\propto V^2 f$, in the alpha-power model two units at about 0.54 V and half the frequency each use roughly 0.36 of the dynamic power of one unit at 0.9 V for the same throughput, at twice the area. Real savings are smaller, bounded by minimum-voltage limits, leakage growth and whether the workload parallelizes.

### How does area translate to cost?
Through dies per wafer and yield. Bigger dies mean fewer candidates per wafer, and the chance a die has a killer defect rises as $1-e^{-D_0A}$. In the table above, 100 mm$^2$ costs about USD 29 per good die and 200 mm$^2$ about USD 68, 2.3 times as much for 2 times the area.

### What do you do if synthesis misses timing by 5% on a block that is already too big?
First check whether the tool is fighting the constraint (look at the worst paths). Options: restructure the critical logic (retime or add a pipeline stage), use faster threshold cells on just those paths, fix constraints or false paths, relax the target if the product allows, or move work across stages. Blindly upsizing everything blows area and leakage.

### Scenario: you need to hit a 25% performance gain on an existing core without increasing power. What are your options?
Voltage is out because the cost is cubic. Look for CPI improvements that cost little power: better branch prediction, larger or better-organized caches, prefetching, wider issue only where the code has parallelism. Reduce power elsewhere to fund frequency: clock gating, multi-$V_t$ swaps, better physical design (shorter wires). Finally consider a process or library change. Each has an area price, so I would model all of them and pick the cheapest in PPA terms.

### What if the interviewer asks which of the three matters most?
It depends on the product, so ask what the product is. A battery device cares about energy per task, a server about throughput per watt and per dollar, and an embedded controller about cost. Say that out loud and then justify the choice for the case given.
:::
