::: basics
### In plain words
The insulating material around a trace is not perfect. When a fast signal's electric field flips back and forth, the material's tiny charges shift to follow it, and they lag a little. That lag turns a small part of the signal's energy into heat. This is dielectric loss. The faster the signal, the more flips per second, so the more energy is lost on each inch of board.

### Picture it
Squeeze a rubber ball over and over. Each squeeze wastes a little energy, and the ball warms up. Squeeze twice as fast and you waste twice as much per second, because the ball loses the same small fraction on every squeeze. The insulator behaves like that ball, and each field flip is a squeeze. The ball is a loose picture: the real lag comes from tiny charges shifting, not from rubbing.

### Words you need
- **Dielectric**: the insulating material between conductors, such as the fiberglass-epoxy of a circuit board ([[q0023]]).
- **Polarization**: tiny charges in the material shifting to follow the electric field.
- **Loss tangent ($\tan\delta$)**: the fraction of stored energy lost as heat on each cycle. Smaller is better.
- **Attenuation**: how much the signal shrinks per unit length, here in dB per inch.
- **FR-4**: the standard, cheap circuit-board material. Its $\tan\delta$ is about 0.02.

### The simplest example
FR-4 loses about 0.1 dB per inch from this effect at 1 GHz. At 5 GHz it loses about 0.5 dB per inch, five times more, because the loss grows in step with frequency. A 10-inch trace at 5 GHz loses about 5 dB to the dielectric alone. A low-loss board material with $\tan\delta$ of 0.004 is five times better than FR-4. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page explains where the lag comes from, shows how this loss compares with copper loss, and shows how moisture and temperature change it.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand dielectric loss as a **physical process** (the polarization of the material lagging the field), not only as "$\tan\delta$ is bad". The interviewer wants you to:

1. Define loss tangent as $\tan\delta=\varepsilon''/\varepsilon'$ and say it's the ratio of energy dissipated to energy stored per cycle.
2. Give origins: dipole (orientational) relaxation, ionic motion, interfacial effects, and a little DC conductivity.
3. Give the frequency behaviour: dielectric attenuation grows about linearly with $f$, where conductor loss grows as $\sqrt f$, so dielectric dominates at high frequency in FR-4. And mention that $\tan\delta$ itself is not perfectly flat.
:::

::: why
At multi-gigabit rates, the dielectric is usually the biggest term in the loss budget, and it's the thing you pay for when you upgrade the laminate. Knowing where it comes from lets you reason about materials (FR-4 vs PTFE), temperature and moisture effects, and why capacitors have ESR. It also separates "I memorized the loss tangent" from "I know why $\tan\delta$ makes a trace lossy".
:::

::: concepts
- **Dielectric polarization**: bound charges shift under an electric field, storing energy. See [[q0022]].
- **Complex permittivity** $\varepsilon=\varepsilon'-j\varepsilon''$: real part stores energy, imaginary part dissipates it.
- **Loss tangent** $\tan\delta=\varepsilon''/\varepsilon'$, also called dissipation factor $D_f$.
- **Shunt conductance** $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$ in the transmission-line model. See [[q0718]].
- **Relaxation (Debye) behaviour**: polarization lags the field, giving loss that peaks near the relaxation frequency.
- **Conductor vs dielectric loss**: different frequency scaling. See [[q0722]].
:::

::: answer
"Dielectric loss is energy turned into heat inside the insulating material of the line. Physically the dielectric has bound charges and molecular dipoles. When the field alternates they try to follow it, but they can't respond instantly, so the polarization lags the field by a small angle. Because of that lag, some of the energy put into polarizing the material each cycle isn't returned; it's dissipated as heat. Mathematically that's the imaginary part of the permittivity, and the ratio of imaginary to real parts is the loss tangent, $\tan\delta$.

In a transmission line it shows up as a shunt conductance, $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$, so the attenuation from the dielectric is about $\alpha_d\approx\pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\tan\delta/c$. That's linear in frequency, which is different from conductor loss, which goes as the square root.

So for FR-4, with $\tan\delta$ around 0.02, it's about 0.1 dB per inch at 1 GHz and about 0.5 dB per inch at 5 GHz, and it dominates the loss above a GHz or so. Low-loss laminates with $\tan\delta$ around 0.004 or less cut that by a factor of five. $\tan\delta$ itself changes slowly with frequency, temperature and moisture, which is why datasheets list the frequency they measured at."
:::

::: technical
**Complex permittivity.** For an applied field $E e^{j\omega t}$ the displacement lags: $\varepsilon^*=\varepsilon'-j\varepsilon''$. Power dissipated per unit volume: $p=\tfrac12\omega\varepsilon''|E|^2$. Loss tangent $\tan\delta=\varepsilon''/\varepsilon'$ (plus $\sigma/\omega\varepsilon'$ if there's a bulk conductivity $\sigma$).

**Debye relaxation** (single time constant $\tau$):
$$\varepsilon^*(\omega)=\varepsilon_\infty+\frac{\varepsilon_s-\varepsilon_\infty}{1+j\omega\tau},\qquad \tan\delta=\frac{(\varepsilon_s-\varepsilon_\infty)\,\omega\tau}{\varepsilon_s+\varepsilon_\infty(\omega\tau)^2}$$
Loss peaks near $\omega\tau\approx\sqrt{\varepsilon_s/\varepsilon_\infty}$ and $\varepsilon'$ falls from $\varepsilon_s$ to $\varepsilon_\infty$ across the relaxation. Real laminates (epoxy-glass) have a broad distribution of relaxation times, so $\tan\delta$ is fairly flat over decades and $\varepsilon_r$ falls slowly with frequency.

**Transmission-line attenuation.** For a uniform TEM-like line (stripline):
$$\alpha_d=\frac{\pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\tan\delta}{c}\ \text{Np/m}\ (\times8.686\text{ for dB/m})$$
For microstrip multiply by the filling factor: $\alpha_d=\frac{\pi f}{c}\frac{\varepsilon_r}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}}\frac{\varepsilon_{eff}-1}{\varepsilon_r-1}\tan\delta$.

**Numbers** ($\varepsilon_r=4.3$, $\tan\delta=0.02$, stripline formula): 1 GHz → 3.8 dB/m = 0.096 dB/in; 5 GHz → 0.48 dB/in; 10 GHz → 0.96 dB/in. Microstrip with the filling factor ($\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3.26$) is about 20% lower: about 0.075 dB/in at 1 GHz.

**Typical $\tan\delta$ (datasheet-level orders of magnitude):** FR-4 about 0.015–0.025; mid-loss laminates about 0.005–0.01; RF laminates about 0.001–0.004; PTFE-based about 0.0005–0.002; air 0.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $\tan\delta$ and $\varepsilon_r$ depend on resin content, glass weave, temperature, humidity and the test method and frequency of the datasheet. Treat datasheet values as typical, and use measured or vendor-modelled values for design.

**Link to capacitors.** A capacitor with dielectric loss has equivalent series resistance $\text{ESR}=\tan\delta/(\omega C)$. For a 100 nF ceramic with $\tan\delta=0.025$ at 100 kHz, $\text{ESR}\approx0.4$ Ω.
:::

::: long
"A dielectric is an insulator, but it isn't lossless. When you apply an alternating field, the bound charges in the material shift, and many materials have permanent molecular dipoles that rotate to align with the field. That's polarization, and it's what stores energy and gives you a capacitance proportional to $\varepsilon_r$. The catch is that the dipoles can't respond instantly. They lag the field by a small phase, and that lag means some of the energy you put in during each cycle is converted to heat, not returned. We describe it with a complex permittivity: the real part is the energy-storing part, the imaginary part is the loss, and their ratio is the loss tangent, $\tan\delta$.

There are several mechanisms. Dipole or orientational polarization, like water or polar resin groups, has a relaxation time and the loss peaks near it. Ionic polarization and interfacial effects, like moisture or ions at the glass-resin boundary, add loss. And a small DC leakage conductivity matters only at low frequency.

On a line, the dielectric appears as a conductance across the capacitance, $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$. The attenuation is about $\pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\tan\delta/c$, so for constant $\tan\delta$ it's linear in frequency. Compare that to the conductor loss, which scales with the square root, so at high frequency the dielectric takes over. For FR-4 at $\tan\delta\approx0.02$ you get about 0.1 dB per inch at 1 GHz and half a dB per inch at 5 GHz. A low-loss laminate with 0.004 is five times better.

In practice $\tan\delta$ isn't perfectly flat: it drifts with frequency, temperature, and humidity, since absorbed water is very lossy. So I'd read the datasheet at the frequency I care about, and consider moisture and temperature when I budget loss."
:::

::: terms
- **Loss tangent** $\tan\delta$ (dissipation factor $D_f$): $\varepsilon''/\varepsilon'$, the ratio of lost to stored energy per cycle.
- **Complex permittivity** $\varepsilon^*=\varepsilon'-j\varepsilon''$.
- **Dielectric constant** $D_k$ or $\varepsilon_r$: real relative permittivity.
- **Polarization**: displacement of charge or alignment of dipoles under a field.
- **Relaxation time** $\tau$: how fast polarization follows a change in field.
- **Dielectric absorption**: slow polarization components that give capacitors a memory effect.
- **Shunt conductance** $G'$: the transmission-line representation of dielectric loss.
- **ESR**: effective series resistance of a capacitor, partly from dielectric loss.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "dielectric loss is heat in the insulator; FR-4 has high loss tangent".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the **mechanism**: lagging polarization, dipole relaxation, ions and interfaces, with the complex permittivity.
- Defines $\tan\delta=\varepsilon''/\varepsilon'$ and links it to $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$.
- Gives the scaling: $\alpha_d\propto f\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\tan\delta$, linear in $f$, vs $\sqrt f$ for conductors.
- Gives numbers: about 0.1 dB/in at 1 GHz and 0.5 dB/in at 5 GHz for FR-4 at $\tan\delta=0.02$.
- Adds caveats: $\tan\delta$ varies with frequency, temperature and moisture; $\varepsilon_r$ is also frequency dependent (dispersion).
- Links to the capacitor ESR picture and to material selection.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Confusing dielectric constant with loss.** $\varepsilon_r$ sets $C'$ and velocity; $\tan\delta$ sets loss. A material can have high $\varepsilon_r$ and low loss.
- **Calling it leakage current.** Dielectric loss is mostly polarization lag, which exists even in a perfect insulator with zero DC conductivity.
- **Saying it grows as $\sqrt f$.** That's the conductor term. The dielectric term is linear in frequency for constant $\tan\delta$.
- **Using one datasheet number blindly.** $\tan\delta$ is quoted at a specific frequency, temperature and humidity, and varies.
- **Forgetting the moisture effect.** Absorbed water raises loss and $\varepsilon_r$.
- **Thinking a lower $\varepsilon_r$ always means lower loss.** The attenuation depends on the product $\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\tan\delta$ and the field fraction in the material.
:::

::: deep
## What a dielectric does in a field

A dielectric between two plates raises capacitance by $\varepsilon_r$ because its internal charges rearrange to partly cancel the applied field. That rearrangement is **polarization**, and it comes in several flavours, each with a different speed:

- **Electronic**: the electron cloud of each atom shifts relative to its nucleus. Extremely fast (optical frequencies). Nearly lossless at RF.
- **Ionic**: ions in a lattice shift. Fast (infrared). Lossless at RF in good crystals.
- **Orientational (dipolar)**: molecules with permanent dipoles, like water or polar groups in epoxy, rotate toward the field. Slower, with relaxation times that put the loss peak somewhere between MHz and tens of GHz. This is the main source of loss in resins.
- **Interfacial (Maxwell-Wagner)**: charges pile up at boundaries between materials, such as glass fibre and resin, or at contamination. Contributes a broad low-frequency loss.

If the field alternates faster than the slowest dipoles can follow, they lag. The polarization vector is then partly in phase with $E$ (storing energy, the $\varepsilon'$ part) and partly 90° out of phase (dissipating energy, the $\varepsilon''$ part). The displacement current has a component in phase with the voltage, exactly like a resistor in parallel with the ideal capacitor. That's the shunt conductance $G'$ of the line model.

## Dielectric loss on the line

With the shunt conductance $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$ and the low-loss formula $\alpha_d=G'Z_0/2$, and using $C'=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/(cZ_0)$:
$$\alpha_d=\frac{\omega\tan\delta\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}}{2c}=\frac{\pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}\tan\delta}{c}\ \text{Np/m}$$
This is independent of $Z_0$ and geometry, which is why dielectric loss is a *material* property. Conductor loss depends on trace width. For stripline $\varepsilon_{eff}=\varepsilon_r$.

## Worked example

A 12 in stripline in FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r=4.3$, $\tan\delta=0.02$) carries 10 Gb/s NRZ (fundamental at 5 GHz).
1. $\alpha_d=\pi\times5\times10^9\times\sqrt{4.3}\times0.02/(3\times10^8)=2.17$ Np/m $=18.9$ dB/m $=0.48$ dB/in.
2. Over 12 in: $0.479\times12=5.7$ dB from the dielectric alone.
3. Adding conductor loss (about 0.11 dB/in at 5 GHz, ignoring roughness) brings the line to about 7 dB.
4. Swap to $\tan\delta=0.004$: dielectric falls to 1.15 dB, total about 2.5 dB. A 4.5 dB improvement for a material change alone.

## What changes $\tan\delta$ in real hardware

- **Moisture**: water is highly polar and lossy in the GHz range. Boards stored in humid conditions can have noticeably higher loss and higher $\varepsilon_r$. Baking before assembly addresses the delamination side of moisture; the electrical effect reverses as the board dries.
- **Temperature**: relaxation times shorten at higher temperature, shifting the loss curve; many laminates show a small increase of $\tan\delta$ with temperature.
- **Glass weave and resin content**: the effective $\tan\delta$ is a blend of glass (low loss) and resin (higher loss).
- **Frequency**: usually slowly increasing or flat $\tan\delta$ and decreasing $\varepsilon_r$ across the GHz range for epoxy systems.

## The capacitor connection

In a ceramic capacitor the same loss appears as dissipation factor, and it's one component of ESR: $\text{ESR}_d=\tan\delta/(\omega C)$. For Class 2 ceramics like X7R the dissipation factor can be a few percent at 1 kHz, and at 100 kHz a 100 nF part with $\tan\delta=0.025$ has about 0.4 Ω from dielectric loss alone. In power decoupling this is usually dominated by the other ESR contributions, but in RF, filters and high-Q resonators the dielectric $Q=1/\tan\delta$ is the limit.

## Where dielectric loss overtakes conductor loss

Write the two terms as $\alpha_c=k_c\sqrt f$ and $\alpha_d=k_d f$. They are equal at
$$f_x=\left(\frac{k_c}{k_d}\right)^2$$
Using the first-order numbers for a thin 50 Ω FR-4 microstrip, $k_c\approx0.051$ dB/in per $\sqrt{\text{GHz}}$ and $k_d\approx0.083$ dB/in per GHz, so $f_x\approx(0.051/0.083)^2\approx0.38$ GHz. Three things move that crossover:

- **Rough copper** multiplies $k_c$ by $K\approx1.65$ at 5 GHz, which raises $f_x$ by $K^2\approx2.7$, to about 1 GHz. That matches the usual rule of thumb that FR-4 is dielectric-dominated above about 1 GHz.
- **Low-loss laminate** with $\tan\delta=0.004$ cuts $k_d$ to 0.0166, so $f_x\approx(0.051/0.0166)^2\approx9.4$ GHz: conductor loss now dominates through the whole 10 Gb/s band.
- **Wider traces** lower $k_c$ (lower $R'$) but leave $k_d$ untouched, so they pull $f_x$ down. You can't buy your way out of dielectric loss with copper.

That is the practical use of the question: it tells you whether to spend on smoother foil (conductor-dominated) or a better laminate (dielectric-dominated).

::: applications
- **Laminate selection** for PCIe, SAS, DDR5 and 25G+ Ethernet boards: mid-loss and low-loss materials chosen specifically for $D_f$.
- **RF and microwave PCBs**: PTFE-based or ceramic-filled laminates with $\tan\delta$ near 0.001–0.004 for low insertion loss.
- **Coax cable dielectrics**: solid PE vs foamed PE vs PTFE; foamed has lower $\tan\delta$ effective (more air).
- **Capacitor selection**: C0G/NP0 (very low loss) for filters and RF vs X7R for bulk decoupling.
- **Microwave heating**: the same polarization lag in water converts microwave energy into heat.
- **Cable and connector insulation** for high-voltage, high-frequency use where dielectric heating limits power.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear, isotropic dielectric**; real laminates are anisotropic (glass weave), and effective $\varepsilon_r$ varies with direction and weave position.
- **Constant $\tan\delta$** over the band is a simplification; real values drift.
- **Low-loss line**: $\alpha_d=G'Z_0/2$ requires $G'\ll\omega C'$.
- **Uniform filling**: stripline has the field fully in the dielectric; microstrip has part in air, so use the filling factor.
- **Temperature and humidity** assumed constant (room, dry). They shift the numbers noticeably.
- **The answer changes** below about 1 MHz, where bulk conductivity (leakage) and interfacial polarization dominate, and in very high field, where nonlinear effects appear.
:::

::: followups
### What's the difference between dielectric constant and loss tangent?
Dielectric constant $\varepsilon_r=\varepsilon'/\varepsilon_0$ is the energy-storing part of the permittivity, setting capacitance and velocity. The loss tangent $\tan\delta=\varepsilon''/\varepsilon'$ is the ratio of the dissipating part to the storing part. You can have a high-$\varepsilon_r$, low-loss material (like C0G ceramic) or a low-$\varepsilon_r$, lossy one.

### Why is dielectric loss linear in frequency but conductor loss isn't?
The dielectric loss power per cycle is a fixed fraction ($\tan\delta$) of the stored energy; more cycles per second means more total loss, so loss per length rises linearly. Conductor loss depends on resistance, which grows as $\sqrt f$ due to the skin effect, so it grows more slowly. See [[q0725]].

### At what frequency do conductor and dielectric loss cross over on FR-4?
It depends on trace geometry and roughness. In the model I used, they cross near 0.4 GHz for a thin 50 Ω microstrip. With wider traces or low-loss material it's higher. A common rule of thumb is that FR-4 is dielectric-dominated above about 1 GHz.

### How would you measure $\tan\delta$ of a laminate?
Resonator methods such as a split-post dielectric resonator or stripline resonators measure $D_k$ and $D_f$ at specific frequencies from the shift and Q of a resonance. You can also extract it from measured insertion loss of long and short coupons after removing the conductor part. Different methods give different numbers, so compare like for like.

### Why does humidity change loss?
Absorbed water adds orientational polarization with a large loss in the GHz range, so $\tan\delta$ and $\varepsilon_r$ both rise. The effect varies by laminate (resin systems absorb different amounts) and reverses as the board dries.

### Harder: does a lossy dielectric always cause dispersion?
Yes, via causality. The Kramers–Kronig relations tie $\varepsilon''(\omega)$ to $\varepsilon'(\omega)$, so any frequency-dependent loss comes with a frequency-dependent real permittivity, hence velocity changes with frequency. For a constant $\tan\delta$ over a wide band, $\varepsilon'$ falls slowly with the log of frequency. This is what the models for broadband dielectric loss (e.g. Djordjevic–Sarkar) capture.
:::

::: interpretations
"Where do they come from and how does it vary with frequency?" has two answers: the *physical origin* (polarization lag, dipoles, ions, interfaces) and the *dependence* of attenuation on frequency (linear in $f$ for constant $\tan\delta$, plus the weak frequency dependence of $\tan\delta$ itself). I give both. The overall loss breakdown is in [[q0722]], skin effect in [[q0725]].
