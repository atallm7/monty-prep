::: testing
Whether you can **compute and compare** output ripple rather than say "PFM is noisier". The key insight interviewers want:

1. Ripple on the output capacitor is set by the charge in the surplus part of each inductor-current pulse divided by $C_{out}$, plus an ESR term.
2. In PWM-CCM that charge is set by the ripple current and is independent of load; in PWM-DCM it shrinks with load; in PFM it is fixed by the peak current and does not shrink.
3. So PFM ripple is comparable to full-load PWM ripple in amplitude, many times larger than PWM at the same light load, and at a much lower, variable frequency.
:::

::: why
A processor's rail spec, an ADC reference or an RF supply has a ripple budget, and the idle state is where PFM is active. The same converter can be quiet at full load and noticeably noisier (and audible) at idle. Knowing how ripple scales with $I_{pk}$ and $C_{out}$ lets you pick the capacitor and peak-current setting, and tells you when to force PWM. It builds on the capacitor-ripple derivation in [[q0196]] and the PFM mechanics in [[q0231]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor ripple from charge**: the capacitor charges while $i_L>I_{load}$, so $\Delta V_C=\frac{Q_{excess}}{C}$ (peak minus trough). See [[q0196]].
- **ESR contribution**: adds about $ESR\cdot i_C$ in phase with the capacitor current. 
- **CCM ripple**: $\Delta V\approx\frac{\Delta I_L}{8C f_{sw}}$.
- **DCM ripple**: tied to the pulse height, which is set by the duty cycle for the current load. See [[q0229]].
- **PFM ripple**: tied to the fixed peak current. See [[q0231]].
- **Ripple frequency**: $f_{sw}$ in PWM; the pulse rate $I_{out}/Q$ in PFM.
:::

::: answer
"In PWM, the ripple is a small triangle-ish wave at the switching frequency. At heavy load in CCM it is the inductor ripple current integrated on the output capacitor, $\Delta I_L/(8Cf_{sw})$, plus the ESR drop, about 12 mV for my 22 µF example. At light load in DCM it gets smaller still, because the converter shortens its pulses, so a 20 mA load gives under 2 mV.

PFM behaves differently. Each pulse is a fixed-size packet, a fixed peak current, so every pulse bumps the output up by about the charge per pulse over the capacitance, and then the output droops until the next pulse. For a 0.6 A peak and 22 µF that's about 15 millivolts. And it doesn't shrink as the load falls, it just happens less often. So compared with PWM at the same light load, PFM ripple is something like ten times bigger, and compared with PWM at full load it's comparable in amplitude but at a much lower, varying frequency, from tens of kilohertz down to a few kilohertz.

Practically that means more ripple, harder to filter, possibly audible, and the average output sits slightly high. To reduce it you'd lower the peak current, increase the output capacitance, or force PWM mode."
:::

::: technical
**Unified formula.** The capacitor charges while $i_L>I_{load}$, which lasts $(I_{pk}-I_{load})\left(\frac{L}{V_{in}-V_{out}}+\frac{L}{V_{out}}\right)$, delivering a surplus charge
$$Q_{ex}=\frac{(I_{pk}-I_{load})^2}{2}\cdot\frac{LV_{in}}{(V_{in}-V_{out})V_{out}},\qquad \Delta V_C=\frac{Q_{ex}}{C_{out}}$$
(plus an ESR term $\approx ESR\,(I_{pk}-I_{load})$ at the peak, partly in phase). For 12 V to 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 22 µF this is $\Delta V_C=44.6\text{ mV/A}^2\cdot(I_{pk}-I_{load})^2$.

- **PWM CCM**: $I_{pk}-I_{load}=\Delta I_L/2=0.509$ A, so $\Delta V_C=11.6$ mV, independent of load. Simulation with 5 mΩ ESR: 12.3 mV p-p. At 500 kHz.
- **PWM DCM**: $I_{pk}=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})}{L}\frac{D}{f_{sw}}$ with $D$ reduced for the load: at 0.2 A, 9.3 mV; at 20 mA, 1.8 mV; at 10 mA, 1.0 mV (simulated with ESR).
- **PFM**: $I_{pk}=0.6$ A: at 20 mA, 15.3 mV; at 10 mA, 15.8 mV; at 100 mA, 11.7 mV. Frequency 57 kHz at 20 mA, 28 kHz at 10 mA.

**Scaling.** $\Delta V\propto I_{pk}^2/C$ in PFM: 0.3 A gives 3.8 mV, 0.6 A gives 15.3 mV, 1.0 A gives 43 mV (22 µF). With $C=47$ µF: 7.5 mV; 100 µF: 4.3 mV.

**ESR check.** At 0.6 A and 5 mΩ the ESR step is only 3 mV beside the 15 mV capacitive term. With a 50 mΩ polymer or electrolytic capacitor it would be 30 mV and dominate, so the formula changes character with capacitor technology. Layout adds ESL spikes on the edges.
:::

::: short
"PWM ripple is small and at the switching frequency: in CCM about $\Delta I_L/(8Cf)$ plus ESR, and it shrinks further in DCM as the pulses get shorter. PFM ripple comes from a fixed-size charge packet each pulse, about $Q/C$, which doesn't shrink with load; it just occurs less often. So at light load PFM ripple is roughly ten times PWM's, similar to full-load PWM in amplitude, but at a low and variable frequency that can be audible and is harder to filter."
:::

::: long
"Let me use the same converter throughout: 12 V to 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 22 µF with 5 milliohms of ESR.

The output ripple comes from the inductor current pulses. The capacitor charges whenever the inductor current is above the load current and discharges when it's below. So the peak-to-peak capacitor ripple is the surplus charge, the area of the inductor current above the load, divided by the capacitance. For a triangle, that charge is $\frac12(I_{pk}-I_{load})^2$ times $L\,V_{in}/((V_{in}-V_{out})V_{out})$.

In PWM at heavy load, CCM, the peak minus the load is half the ripple current, 0.51 A, and that gives 11.6 mV, or about 12 mV with ESR, at 500 kHz. That's independent of load. At light load in DCM, the converter shortens the duty cycle so the pulse peak is lower: at 20 mA the peak is only 0.2 A, and the ripple is under 2 mV, and it's still at 500 kHz.

In PFM, the pulse peak is a fixed design number, 0.6 A, so peak minus load is about 0.58 A at 20 mA and the ripple is about 15 mV. It's essentially independent of load: 15.8 mV at 10 mA, 15.3 mV at 20 mA. What changes is the frequency: 28 kHz at 10 mA, 57 kHz at 20 mA. At 100 mA the pulses nearly run back to back and the ripple drops to 12 mV, similar to the PWM value.

So the comparison: at the same light load PFM ripple is roughly ten times PWM's, at much lower frequency. Compared with full-load PWM it's similar amplitude. It scales as peak current squared over capacitance, so halving the peak cuts ripple four-fold, and doubling the capacitor halves it. The other costs are that the spectrum is low-frequency and variable, maybe audible, and the output sits half a ripple above the threshold. If the load can't tolerate that, I force PWM."
:::

::: terms
- **Peak-to-peak ripple**: maximum minus minimum output voltage over a period.
- **Surplus charge ($Q_{ex}$)**: charge delivered when $i_L>I_{load}$, which charges $C_{out}$.
- **ESR**: equivalent series resistance of the output capacitor; adds a step proportional to capacitor current.
- **Peak current ($I_{pk}$)**: the inductor current at the end of the on-time (fixed in PFM).
- **Ripple frequency**: $f_{sw}$ in PWM; $I_{out}/Q$ in PFM.
- **Hysteresis window**: the voltage band between start and stop thresholds, also contributing to PFM ripple.
- **Overshoot**: extra charge from comparator and driver delay, $t_d$ times the slope.
- **Audible band**: 20 Hz to 20 kHz; ripple here can excite ceramic capacitors and inductors.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "PFM has more ripple than PWM".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives ripple from **surplus charge over $C_{out}$** and states how $I_{pk}$, $C$ and load enter.
- Distinguishes **CCM, DCM and PFM** ripple: constant, shrinking with load, constant with fixed peak.
- Gives numbers for one converter (12 mV, 1.8 mV, 15 mV), including ESR.
- Notes the **frequency difference** (500 kHz vs 28-57 kHz) and why it matters for filtering and audible noise.
- Lists the mitigations: lower $I_{pk}$, larger $C$, forced PWM, ultrasonic floor, and mentions the DC offset.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying PFM ripple is "always much larger than PWM".** At full load, PWM ripple (12 mV) is comparable to PFM (15 mV). The big ratio only appears against PWM at the same *light* load.
- **Believing PFM ripple shrinks at lighter load.** It stays fixed (set by $I_{pk}$ and $C$); only its repetition rate drops.
- **Using the CCM formula $\Delta I_L/(8Cf)$ for PFM.** The PFM waveform is a sawtooth, not triangular ripple at $f_{sw}$.
- **Forgetting ESR or comparator delay.** ESR adds up to $ESR\cdot I_{pk}$ (3 mV at 0.6 A and 5 mΩ); delay overshoot adds a few percent to the peak.
- **Ignoring the frequency.** A 15 mV ripple at 500 kHz is easily filtered; the same at 3 kHz passes straight through an LC filter and may hit audio circuits.
- **Treating the DC output as nominal.** In PFM the average is raised by about half the ripple.
:::

::: deep
## Where output ripple comes from

The output capacitor sees the difference between the inductor current and the load current: $i_C=i_L-I_{load}$. When $i_C>0$ the capacitor charges, when $i_C<0$ it discharges. Over one repeating period the net charge is zero, so the voltage ripple (ignoring ESR) is the area of the positive part of $i_C$ divided by $C$:
$$\Delta V_C=\frac{1}{C}\int_{i_L>I_{load}}(i_L-I_{load})\,dt$$

For a triangular peak above the load, rising at $s_1=\frac{V_{in}-V_{out}}{L}$ and falling at $s_2=\frac{V_{out}}{L}$, the part above the load is a triangle of height $I_{pk}-I_{load}$ and width $(I_{pk}-I_{load})(1/s_1+1/s_2)$:
$$Q_{ex}=\frac{(I_{pk}-I_{load})^2}{2}\left(\frac1{s_1}+\frac1{s_2}\right)=\frac{(I_{pk}-I_{load})^2}{2}\frac{LV_{in}}{(V_{in}-V_{out})V_{out}}$$

With $L=4.7$ µH, 12 V and 3.3 V: $\frac{LV_{in}}{(V_{in}-V_{out})V_{out}}=1.96\text{ µs}$, so $Q_{ex}=0.98\text{ µs}\cdot(I_{pk}-I_{load})^2$ and with $C=22$ µF:
$$\Delta V_C=44.6\text{ mV/A}^2\times(I_{pk}-I_{load})^2$$

Everything depends on how far the pulse peak sticks out above the load: $(I_{pk}-I_{load})$.

| Mode | $I_{pk}-I_{load}$ | Ripple (cap term) | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| PWM, CCM | $\Delta I_L/2=0.509$ A (any load above 0.51 A) | 11.6 mV | 500 kHz |
| PWM, DCM at 0.2 A | 0.438 A | 8.6 mV | 500 kHz |
| PWM, DCM at 20 mA | 0.18 A | 1.5 mV | 500 kHz |
| PFM at 20 mA ($I_{pk}=0.6$ A) | 0.58 A | 15.0 mV | 57 kHz |
| PFM at 100 mA | 0.5 A | 11.2 mV | 283 kHz |

Including 5 mΩ of ESR, a time-domain integration gives 12.3, 9.3, 1.8, 15.3 and 11.7 mV p-p respectively. The ESR adds only a fraction since the ESR voltage peaks in phase with a capacitor-current peak.

## What sets PFM ripple, and how to reduce it

From $\Delta V\approx44.6\text{ mV/A}^2\,(I_{pk}-I_{load})^2$ at $C=22$ µF:

| Knob | Change | Ripple |
|---|---|---|
| Peak current | 1.0 A | 43 mV |
| | 0.6 A | 15 mV |
| | 0.3 A | 3.8 mV |
| Output capacitance (0.6 A peak) | 22 µF | 15.3 mV |
| | 47 µF | 7.5 mV |
| | 100 µF | 4.3 mV |

- **Lower $I_{pk}$** cuts ripple quadratically but multiplies the pulse rate, and the per-pulse overhead: 0.3 A gives $Q=0.088$ µC per pulse, so the rate for a given load is four times higher and efficiency drops a few points ([[q0230]]).
- **Larger $C_{out}$** cuts ripple in proportion but costs area and affects load-step behaviour (a larger capacitor lowers the undershoot in PFM too).
- **Force PWM** below a noise threshold, at the cost of efficiency.
- **Ultrasonic floor** keeps the rate above 20 kHz so the ripple is not audible.
- **Hysteresis and delay** matter in real parts: the comparator's window and its propagation delay add overshoot of $t_d(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ to $I_{pk}$ (0.09 A for 50 ns), which raises $(I_{pk}-I_{load})^2$ by about 30% at 0.6 A.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Datasheets typically quote PFM output ripple as a typical value for a specific inductor and capacitor, and the peak-current setting, hysteresis and delay are part dependent. The numbers here come from an ideal pulse model and a simple time-domain integration; use them for scaling, not as predictions of a specific part.

## Frequency content and why it matters

The PWM ripple is concentrated at 500 kHz, where a small ferrite bead, an LC post-filter or the load's own PSRR attenuate it well. In PFM the ripple fundamental sits at the pulse rate: 28 kHz at 10 mA, 2.8 kHz at 1 mA. At those frequencies, an op-amp or an LDO has good PSRR, but a second-stage LC filter has little attenuation, and a ripple amplitude of 15 mV in the audio band can show up in a microphone preamp or an ADC. For an ADC reference or a VCO supply, PFM is usually forbidden.

## Worked check: from ripple budget to capacitor

Suppose the rail tolerates 20 mV peak to peak in PFM, with a 0.6 A peak, the 4.7 µH inductor and a 20 mA load. The surplus charge per pulse is 0.330 µC, so the effective capacitance must be at least $0.330\text{ µC}/20\text{ mV}=16.5$ µF. A 22 µF MLCC looks sufficient, but at 3.3 V of DC bias a small-case 22 µF part often keeps only 40 to 70% of its nominal value, which could be 9 to 15 µF effective and 22 to 37 mV of ripple. Two 22 µF parts (or one larger case size) bring the effective value back above 20 µF and the ripple to about 15 mV. The lesson is to read the capacitance-versus-bias curve of the actual part before trusting the formula.

::: applications
- **Smartphone/IoT rails**: PFM ripple of 10 to 30 mV is accepted on digital core rails; analog and RF rails are forced to PWM or post-filtered by an LDO.
- **Audio/codec supplies**: forced PWM or an ultrasonic floor to avoid audible whine from ceramic capacitors.
- **ADC/PLL references**: PWM only, plus an LDO or a ferrite-and-capacitor filter.
- **Sensor nodes**: PFM everywhere, with a bigger output capacitor to bring ripple down, since the loads tolerate it.
- **Battery chargers** in idle: burst mode is accepted since the battery is a huge capacitor.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Peak-current PFM** with ideal zero-current detection and no comparator delay; delay adds overshoot.
- **Output capacitor** is 22 µF with 5 mΩ ESR, no ESL, no DC-bias derating. A real 22 µF 0805 MLCC at 3.3 V may lose 30 to 60% of its capacitance, which would raise ripple by 1.4× to 2.5×. Ripple numbers here are therefore optimistic.
- **Load current constant** during the period; at very light load the load is a steady DC current.
- **Triangle approximation** for the surplus charge; a time-domain integration was used to include ESR. Numbers were produced with a small simulation of ideal pulses, not a SPICE model of a real IC.
- **Hysteretic PFM variants** with burst grouping have larger ripple (the hysteresis window is added).
:::

::: followups
### Why doesn't PFM ripple shrink when the load decreases?
Because each pulse is a fixed-size packet of charge, independent of the load. A lighter load only means the pulses are spaced further apart, and the output droops for longer between them, but the step per pulse ($Q/C\approx16$ mV here) is the same. In PWM-DCM, the pulse itself gets smaller at lighter load.

### How would you halve the PFM ripple?
Either double the output capacitance (ripple $\propto1/C$) or reduce the peak current by a factor of $\sqrt2$ (ripple $\propto I_{pk}^2$ at light load), which costs more pulses and a little efficiency. Alternatively add a post-filter or switch to forced PWM for sensitive loads.

### Does a larger inductor reduce PFM ripple?
Not directly. With the peak current fixed, the surplus charge $\propto L\,I_{pk}^2$, so a *larger* $L$ increases the charge per pulse and thus the ripple (10 µH roughly doubles it) while halving the pulse rate. Ripple falls only if you also reduce the peak current (for the same pulse energy, $LI_{pk}^2$ constant).

### Why might the real PFM ripple be worse than the calculation?
Comparator hysteresis and delay (extra overshoot of $t_d(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ on the peak current), MLCC capacitance loss with DC bias, ESR at the pulse peak ($ESR\cdot I_{pk}=3$ mV here), and ESL spikes at the pulse edges. All increase the observed ripple, especially at high $V_{in}$.

### Is the output voltage regulation as good in PFM?
No. The output is held within the hysteresis window plus the pulse step, so the regulation error is about the ripple amplitude (about ±0.3% for 15 mV on 3.3 V), and the average sits above nominal by roughly half the ripple. For loads with tight DC tolerances (for example ±1%) this consumes much of the budget. See [[q0232]].
:::

::: interpretations
"How does the ripple compare between PFM and PWM?" has different answers by load. I treat PWM in both of its regimes (CCM at heavy load, DCM at light load) and compare each with PFM, since the honest answer depends on which PWM point you pick: PFM is comparable to full-load PWM, and several times worse than PWM at the same light load.
:::
