::: testing
Whether you can say what makes a battery *good* in a way that is tied to a use case, not a wish list. Every desirable property has a cost: high energy density usually costs safety margin or cycle life; high power usually costs energy; low cost usually costs everything else. The interviewer is looking for a structured list (energy, power, voltage behaviour, life, safety, temperature range, cost, form factor) and for you to say which ones dominate for a given product. The hidden skill is naming the **trade-offs** and the quantity that links them: internal resistance.
:::

::: why
Choosing or specifying a battery is a core system decision, and the properties you prioritise determine the chemistry ([[q0290]]), the protection circuitry ([[q0304]]) and the thermal design. A drone needs specific power; a pacemaker needs decade-long reliability; a phone needs volumetric energy density; a forklift needs cycle life and cost per kWh. Being able to say "desirable depends on the product, and here is how I weigh it" is what separates an engineer from someone reading a marketing sheet.
:::

::: concepts
- **Energy density** (Wh/kg, Wh/L): how much runtime per unit mass or volume. See [[q0297]].
- **Power density / specific power** (W/kg): how hard you can pull current; limited by internal resistance ([[q0303]]).
- **Flat, predictable discharge curve**: easier regulator design, but harder state-of-charge estimation ([[q0308]]).
- **Cycle life and calendar life**: how long until 80% capacity ([[q0301]]).
- **Self-discharge and shelf life**: charge lost when idle.
- **Safety and abuse tolerance**: behaviour under overcharge, short circuit and puncture ([[q0327]]).
- **Operating temperature range** and **cost**.
:::

::: answer
"I'd group them. First, how much it stores: high energy density, so watt-hours per kilogram and per litre, because that sets how big and heavy the battery has to be for a given runtime. Second, how hard it can work: low internal resistance, so it can deliver high current without the voltage collapsing or the cell heating up. Third, behaviour over time: long cycle life, low self-discharge, and ideally a flat voltage curve so the electronics see a steady supply.

Then the practical ones: safe, meaning it tolerates abuse and doesn't catch fire easily; wide temperature range, because capacity and resistance both get worse in the cold; fast charging; low cost; and the right form factor, like a thin pouch for a phone or a rugged cylinder for a tool.

You can't have everything, though. Chasing energy density tends to eat cycle life and safety margin. Chasing power means a thicker, lower-resistance cell with less energy. So which ones are 'desirable' depends on the product: a drone wants specific power, an IoT sensor wants low self-discharge, a phone wants volumetric energy density, a power tool wants high current and robustness."
:::

::: technical
**Quantitative definitions**

- Energy density: $E/m$ in Wh/kg, $E/V$ in Wh/L, with $E=Q\bar V$ (capacity times average discharge voltage). Typical Li-ion cells are 150-270 Wh/kg; NiMH 60-120; lead-acid 30-50 (order of magnitude; vendor-dependent).
- Specific power: maximum sustainable power per kg. Matched-load peak power is $P_{max}=V_{oc}^2/(4R_{int})$, but usable power is limited by thermal rise and a minimum voltage.
- Efficiency at current $I$: $\eta\approx 1-\dfrac{I R_{int}}{V_{oc}}$ (voltage efficiency), plus coulombic losses. Heat is $I^2R_{int}$.
- Self-discharge: percent of capacity lost per month; Li-ion typically low single-digit %/month, NiMH higher, primary lithium well below 1%/year (typical, varies).
- Cycle life: cycles to 80% capacity at stated depth of discharge, rate and temperature.

**Why internal resistance links everything.** Low $R_{int}$ means high power capability, low heat, high efficiency, small voltage sag and fast charging. But low $R_{int}$ requires thicker current collectors, more electrode area and thinner active layers, which reduces energy per kg. That is the fundamental **energy vs power** trade-off between "energy cells" and "power cells".

**Safety and life are connected.** Operating at high SoC, high temperature and high rate each accelerate aging ([[q0301]]). A cell with a stable cathode (LiFePO4) gives up energy density to gain thermal runaway margin and cycle life.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Quoted energy densities, cycle lives and self-discharge numbers differ by cell format, manufacturer, test conditions and year. Use them as ranges, not specs.
:::

::: short
"Desirable: high energy density, so it's small and light for the runtime; low internal resistance, so it can deliver high current without sagging or heating; a flat, stable voltage; long cycle life and low self-discharge; wide temperature range; fast charging; safe under abuse; and low cost with the right form factor. They trade off: more energy usually means less power, life or safety, so which ones matter depends on the product."
:::

::: long
"I'd split desirable properties into five buckets.

One, storage: high energy density, in watt-hours per kilogram and per litre. That sets how big and heavy the pack is for a given runtime. Lithium-ion is far ahead of lead-acid and nickel cells here.

Two, delivery: low internal resistance, which gives high power density. It means the voltage doesn't sag under a pulse, less energy is wasted as heat, and it can charge fast. A drone or a power tool cares a lot about this.

Three, voltage behaviour: a flat, predictable discharge curve is nice for the electronics because the supply stays nearly constant. The downside is that a flat curve makes it hard to guess the state of charge from voltage, so there's a trade-off there too.

Four, life and reliability: long cycle life, long calendar life, low self-discharge, and a wide operating temperature range. A battery that loses forty percent of its capacity in the cold isn't much use outdoors.

Five, practical and safety: it should tolerate abuse like overcharge and short circuit without a fire, it should be affordable, and it should fit the mechanical shape you have, like a thin pouch.

The thing I'd emphasise is that they conflict. Thick electrodes give energy but high resistance. Thin electrodes give power but less energy. A stable cathode gives safety and life but lower voltage and energy. So the answer is: pick the two or three properties that dominate for the product and accept compromises on the rest."
:::

::: terms
- **Energy density**: Wh per kg (gravimetric) or per litre (volumetric).
- **Specific power**: W per kg a cell can deliver.
- **Internal resistance ($R_{int}$)**: lumped resistance that causes voltage sag and heating.
- **Discharge curve**: terminal voltage vs capacity at a given rate.
- **Cycle life**: cycles before capacity drops to a threshold, normally 80%.
- **Self-discharge**: capacity loss at rest.
- **Depth of discharge (DoD)**: fraction of capacity used per cycle.
- **Thermal runaway**: self-accelerating heating that can end in fire.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "long life, high capacity, cheap, safe".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies with units (Wh/kg, W/kg, mΩ, cycles to 80%).
- Separates **energy** from **power** and ties power to internal resistance, with the formula $P_{max}=V^2/4R$ and efficiency $1-IR/V$.
- Names the trade-offs explicitly (energy vs power, energy vs safety/life, flat curve vs SoC estimation).
- Makes it product-specific: "for a wearable I'd weigh volumetric density and safety; for a drone, specific power and low Rint; for a utility meter, self-discharge and shelf life".
- Mentions thermal behaviour and charge rate, which people forget.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Highest capacity is best."** mAh at a low rate says nothing about how the cell behaves at your load, and a larger capacity cell may have a higher resistance and lower cycle life.
- **Confusing energy with power.** A supercapacitor has high power and low energy; a Li-ion energy cell is the opposite. They are different axes.
- **Wanting a flat discharge curve and easy SoC estimation.** They conflict; LiFePO4's flat curve makes voltage-based gauging poor.
- **Ignoring temperature.** A cell that is fine at 25 °C may not deliver the current at -10 °C.
- **Treating cost as only up-front.** Cost per cycle or per delivered kWh over life is what matters in a heavily-cycled product.
- **Equating "safe" with "never fails".** Safety is about tolerance to abuse and good protection circuits, not magic.
:::

::: deep
## Energy and power are different axes

Energy is the area under the discharge curve: capacity times average voltage, $E=Q\,\bar V$. Power is how fast you can take it out: $P=VI$. A cell's *terminal* voltage under load is

$$V = V_{oc} - I R_{int}$$

so the delivered power is $P=I(V_{oc}-IR_{int})$, which rises with $I$ and peaks at $I=V_{oc}/(2R_{int})$ giving $P_{max}=V_{oc}^2/(4R_{int})$. But this is the *matched-load* point where half of the electrochemical power is wasted in $R_{int}$; real systems operate far below it. For a cell with $V_{oc}=3.7$ V and $R_{int}=50$ mΩ, $P_{max}=3.7^2/(4\times0.05)=68$ W: far more than the cell can survive thermally (it would be dissipating 68 W inside itself). So specification of a "max continuous current" comes from heating and degradation limits, not from the matched-power peak.

The efficiency of delivering current $I$ is

$$\eta_V \approx 1-\frac{I R_{int}}{V_{oc}}$$

## Why energy cells and power cells differ

To lower resistance you increase electrode area, shorten ion paths with thin electrode coatings, use more conductive additive, and use thicker current collectors and more tabs. All of that adds inactive mass and volume per unit of stored charge. To raise energy you do the opposite: thicker, more densely-packed active layers and less foil. So there is a physical trade-off: an "energy" cell (e.g. 3-3.5 Ah in an 18650) has higher resistance and is rated for a few amps, while a "power" cell (e.g. 1.5-2.5 Ah in the same can) is rated for 20-30 A continuous. These example ratings are typical of the class, not a spec of any particular part.

## The rest of the list, with the reason behind each

**Flat discharge curve.** It keeps the supply near constant, which simplifies regulators and lets a load run until the cell is nearly empty. But the OCV-SoC slope is tiny, so a 10 mV measurement error is a big SoC error ([[q0308]]).

**Cycle life.** Each cycle grows the solid-electrolyte interphase and cracks electrodes a little ([[q0301]]). Lower depth of discharge, moderate temperatures and moderate charge voltage extend life. Cycle life is quoted at a stated DoD and rate; a cell rated 500 cycles at 100% DoD may reach several times that at 50% DoD.

**Self-discharge and shelf life.** For a sensor that sits for years, self-discharge dominates the energy budget. At 1.5% per month a rechargeable cell loses 16% per year, eating the entire margin for a 1 µA design.

**Safe by design.** Thermal runaway starts when internal heating exceeds heat removal. Stable cathodes (LiFePO4), ceramic-coated separators and robust venting improve margins. The electronic protection layer is a separate matter: see [[q0295]] and [[q0304]].

**Temperature range.** In the cold, ion diffusion slows and resistance rises (typically doubling or tripling at around 0 °C compared with room temperature; vendor-dependent), so both usable capacity and peak power drop. In the heat, aging accelerates. See [[q0303]].

**Fast charging.** Limited by resistance and by lithium plating at the graphite anode at high charge rate and low temperature. A desirable cell accepts a high charge current without plating.

**Cost.** Dollar per kWh and cost per cycle. A cell with half the cycle life must be half the price to break even in a heavily cycled use.

## Choosing which properties to weight

| Product | Dominant priorities |
|---|---|
| Phone / earbuds | volumetric energy density, safety, thin pouch |
| Quadcopter / RC | specific power, low $R_{int}$, weight |
| Utility meter / tag | self-discharge, temperature range, 10-20 yr life |
| Power tool | continuous current, robustness, thermal |
| Stationary storage | cycle life, cost per kWh, safety |

::: applications
- **Smartphones and wearables**: LiPo pouch cells chosen for volumetric energy density and thin form.
- **FPV drones and RC cars**: high-C LiPo packs chosen for low resistance and specific power.
- **Cordless power tools**: high-drain 18650/21700 cells chosen for continuous current and thermal tolerance.
- **Remote sensors and utility meters**: lithium primary cells for self-discharge and temperature range.
- **Home or grid storage**: LiFePO4 for cycle life, safety and cost per cycle.
:::

::: assumptions
The answer assumes a general electronics application and takes lithium-type rechargeable cells as the reference. For primary cells the same list applies but cycle life is replaced by shelf life, and for large stationary systems cost per kWh and lifetime dominate. "Desirable" is always relative to the requirement.

The formulas use a simple resistive model; real cells have a time-dependent resistance ([[q0291]]), so power capability depends on pulse length. All density, resistance and life numbers are typical ranges.
:::

::: followups
### If you could only pick two properties for a drone battery, which would you choose?
Specific power (low internal resistance, high C rating) and energy density (flight time). A drone needs tens of amps at lift-off and needs to carry as little mass as possible. I'd check the cell's continuous current at the pack's max temperature, because a pack that sags to 3 V under load triggers undervoltage cut-off in flight.

### Why is a flat discharge curve both good and bad?
Good: the load sees a steady voltage, so a regulator can be simpler and the device runs at full performance until the cell is nearly empty. Bad: voltage barely changes with SoC, so a voltage-based fuel gauge is imprecise, and the sudden knee at the end gives little warning. A coulomb counter is needed.

### How does low internal resistance help beyond just "more current"?
It also reduces I²R heating, so the cell stays cooler and ages more slowly; it increases efficiency; it reduces voltage sag so the device stays above brown-out; and it allows faster charging. For 10 A through 50 mΩ, heat is 5 W; through 20 mΩ it is 2 W.

### Is a cell with twice the cycle life always worth twice the price?
Break-even is on cost per delivered kWh over life, so yes if it will actually be cycled to the end of life. For a product that is cycled lightly and replaced for other reasons before wear-out, calendar life and up-front cost matter more and extra cycle life is wasted.

### What if the cell must run at -20 °C?
Look for chemistry and electrolyte designed for it and check low-temperature discharge curves. Expect capacity to drop significantly and resistance to rise several times. Never charge standard Li-ion below about 0 °C. Self-heating from I²R, insulation, or a heater can help. This is a vendor-dependent area, so verify with cold-chamber testing.
:::

::: interpretations
"Desirable" is open-ended, so the grader is looking for a framework and trade-offs rather than one correct list. The sibling question [[q0293]] asks the buyer's version (which datasheet specs to read), and [[q0291]] asks how to measure these properties. Keep this answer at the "what makes a battery good, and why" level and let those questions handle spec-sheet details.

- Names energy density (Wh/kg, Wh/L) and explains it sets size/weight for a runtime :: This is the headline battery metric.

- Mentions cycle life, self-discharge and temperature range :: Real products live for years in a range of environments.
- Mentions safety/abuse tolerance :: Lithium cells are a safety-critical component.
- Includes flat discharge curve and its trade-off with SoC estimation :: Shows system-level thinking.
