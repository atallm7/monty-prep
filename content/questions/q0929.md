::: basics
### In plain words
You can build a product's electronics as one integrated chip (a SoC, [[q0926]]) or as several separate chips on a board. One chip is smaller, uses less energy to move data, and is cheaper per unit when you make many. The price is a big one-time design cost and less freedom to change parts later. So the right choice depends mostly on how many units you will make.

### Picture it
Think of moving boxes inside one house versus between buildings across town. Inside the house, you carry a box a few steps. Across town, you load a truck, drive, and unload, which takes more time and fuel. A SoC is the house: data moves short distances. Separate chips are the buildings. The picture misses one thing: designing a custom house costs a lot up front, while renting existing buildings costs almost nothing to start.

### Words you need
- **Energy per bit**: the energy needed to send one 0 or 1 across a link. Shorter wires cost less.
- **NRE (non-recurring engineering)**: the one-time cost of designing, verifying and making the chip.
- **Unit cost**: what it costs to make each extra chip once the design exists.
- **Break-even volume**: the number of units where two options cost the same in total.
- **Respin**: a new version of the chip made to fix a bug, which costs time and money.

### The simplest example
Compare a custom SoC with a board of separate chips. The SoC has an NRE of 5,000,000 USD and costs 18 USD per unit. The board has an NRE of 150,000 USD and costs 45 USD per unit. The SoC saves 27 USD per unit but costs 4,850,000 USD more up front. Break-even is $4{,}850{,}000/27 \approx 180{,}000$ units. Below that, the board is cheaper. Above it, the SoC wins. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page explains why on-chip data is cheaper to move, and what else you give up when you integrate.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can weigh a **system-level** decision, not just recite "SoC is smaller". The interviewer wants:

1. Benefits of integration: size, power (energy per bit moved), performance/latency, cost at volume, reliability, BOM and assembly.
2. Costs: NRE and time, loss of flexibility, process compromises, risk of a respin, supplier lock-in, thermal concentration.
3. A decision rule based on **volume, constraints and schedule**, ideally with a break-even calculation.
4. The two readings of the question: buying a catalog SoC versus several chips, and designing a custom SoC versus a board of chips.
:::

::: why
Make-versus-buy and integrate-versus-partition decisions are daily work in hardware: choose a SoC or a chip-set, a custom ASIC or an FPGA plus off-the-shelf parts, a module or a chip-down design. Interviewers use this question to see if you think in terms of constraints and cost curves rather than slogans. The die-level view of the same trade-off (yield, noise, thermals) is in [[q0932]]; the module alternative is [[q0931]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Energy of data movement**: on-die wires versus pins and PCB traces. See [[q0926]].
- **NRE and unit cost**: one-time engineering/mask cost versus per-unit cost; break-even volume.
- **Yield**: bigger dies yield worse, which raises cost per good die. See [[q0932]].
- **Latency and bandwidth**: on-chip fabric versus board-level links.
- **Flexibility**: swapping a part, upgrading memory, second sourcing.
- **Time to market and risk**: respins, verification, supply chain.
:::

::: answer
"The short version is that integration wins on size, power, performance and per-unit cost at volume, and loses on flexibility, upfront cost and risk.

Power and speed come from keeping signals on the die. A wire on chip is short and low capacitance, so moving a bit costs a fraction of what it costs to drive it through package pins and across a board, and the bus can be much wider and lower latency. Size and BOM are obvious: one package instead of several, fewer decoupling caps, fewer layers and connectors, simpler assembly and often better reliability because there are fewer solder joints.

The costs: if you design the SoC yourself, the NRE is huge, masks, IP licenses, verification and a possible respin, so it only makes sense above a break-even volume. A board of off-the-shelf parts is quick to build, and you can swap one chip, upgrade the memory, or second-source. Also in a SoC everything shares one process, which isn't optimal for analog, RF or DRAM, and everything shares one heat spot.

So my rule: for high volume and tight power or size limits, integrate; for low volume, fast schedule or uncertain requirements, use catalog parts or a module. Most teams don't design the SoC, they buy one."
:::

::: technical
**Quantitative advantages of integration.**
- *Energy*: moving data off-chip costs roughly an order of magnitude or more per bit than moving it a few mm on-die. Illustrative: 10 pJ/bit off-chip (PHY, pins, trace, receiver) versus about 1 pJ/bit on-die over several millimeters. At 10 GB/s (80 Gb/s) that is 0.8 W versus 0.08 W.
- *Bandwidth per wire*: on-die fabrics can be hundreds of bits wide at a GHz; off-chip links are pin-limited.
- *Latency*: an on-die hop is a few cycles (nanoseconds); a chip-to-chip hop through a SerDes link or external bus is typically tens of nanoseconds to microseconds depending on protocol.
- *BOM/area*: fewer packages, decoupling networks and routing layers.

**Break-even.** Total cost $C(V)=NRE+V\cdot c_u$. A custom SoC (NRE 5 MUSD, unit cost 18 USD) versus a discrete board (NRE 0.15 MUSD, unit cost 45 USD) breaks even at $V^*=(5{,}000{,}000-150{,}000)/(45-18)\approx1.8\times10^5$ units. Below that, discrete wins; above, the SoC does. (Numbers illustrative.)

**Costs and risks of integration.** NRE, long schedule, respins, harder verification, single process for all blocks, shared power/thermal budget, IP license royalties, and a single point of failure in supply.

**Trade-off summary.**

| Factor | SoC (integrated) | Separate components |
|---|---|---|
| Size / BOM | Smaller, fewer parts | Larger, more parts |
| Power | Lower (on-die links) | Higher (I/O power) |
| Performance | Wider/faster links | Link-limited |
| Unit cost at volume | Lower | Higher |
| NRE / schedule | High / long | Low / short |
| Flexibility, second sourcing | Low | High |
| Process optimization | Compromise | Each chip in its best process |
| Thermal | Concentrated | Spread out |
:::

::: short
"Integration wins on size, power, speed and unit cost at volume: on-die wires are shorter and cheaper to drive than package pins and traces, and you cut packages, passives and board area. It loses on upfront NRE, schedule, flexibility and process compromises, and heat is concentrated. So integrate when volume and size or power limits justify the NRE, and use discrete parts or a module for low volume or uncertain requirements."
:::

::: long
"I'd frame it as a trade between per-unit efficiency and upfront cost plus flexibility.

On the SoC side, the physics is that signals on a die are cheap. A wire of a few millimeters has tiny capacitance, so a bit costs on the order of a picojoule to move, while sending it through a PHY, package, board trace and a receiver costs perhaps ten times more. At 10 GB/s that's the difference between roughly a tenth of a watt and most of a watt, which is a huge deal in a phone. On-chip links can also be hundreds of bits wide and have nanosecond latency, whereas pins are limited and board links add tens of nanoseconds or more. Then the board gets smaller and cheaper: fewer packages, fewer decoupling caps and fewer layers, and fewer solder joints to fail.

The costs are mostly upfront. A custom SoC needs design, IP licenses, masks and verification, millions of dollars and a long schedule, and if there's a bug, a respin. With, say, 5 million NRE and 18 dollars a unit versus a 45 dollar board of chips with 150 thousand NRE, the break-even is about 180,000 units. Below that, discrete wins. You also lose flexibility: you can't upgrade the memory or swap a part, and the SoC has to compromise on one process for logic, analog and I/O. Heat is concentrated in one die too.

So for high volume with tight power and size, integrate. For low volume, tight schedules or changing requirements, use off-the-shelf parts or a system on module. In practice most companies buy a catalog SoC, which gives them most of the integration benefit with none of the NRE."
:::

::: terms
- **NRE**: non-recurring engineering cost; design, masks, IP, verification.
- **Unit cost**: per-chip manufacturing, test and packaging cost.
- **Break-even volume**: where total costs of two options are equal.
- **PHY / I/O power**: energy spent driving signals off-die.
- **Respin**: a new silicon revision to fix bugs; costs weeks to months and mask cost.
- **BOM**: bill of materials.
- **Chip-down**: placing the SoC and its support parts directly on the product PCB.
- **Second source**: alternative supplier for a part.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "smaller, cheaper, lower power, but less flexible".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the **physical reason** (on-die versus off-chip energy and bandwidth) and quantifies it.
- Does a **break-even** calculation with NRE and unit cost, and notes it depends on volume.
- Distinguishes designing your own SoC from buying a catalog SoC.
- Raises process compromises (analog/RF/DRAM), thermal concentration, respin risk, IP and supply lock-in.
- Gives a decision rule tied to volume, schedule, power and uncertainty.
- Mentions middle options: SoM ([[q0931]]), SiP, chiplets ([[q0932]]), FPGA.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"SoC is always cheaper."** Not below the break-even volume, and not if NRE is ignored.
- **Ignoring the cost of respins and verification.** Bugs in silicon are expensive.
- **Forgetting that a SoC still needs external parts** (DRAM, power, clock). See [[q0930]].
- **Saying separate chips are "more flexible" without saying how**: swappable memory, second source, independent upgrades.
- **Quoting power savings with no mechanism.** The mechanism is lower capacitance and shorter distances.
- **Neglecting thermal limits.** Concentrating everything in one die can create hot spots and throttling.
:::

::: deep
## The physics of why on-die is cheaper

Switching a wire costs $E=\tfrac12 CV^2$ per transition (or $CV^2$ for charge drawn from the supply per full 0-1-0 cycle). On-die wire capacitance is of order 0.1 to 0.2 fF per micrometer, so 5 mm is about 0.5 to 1 pF. At 0.8 V, $\tfrac12CV^2$ is $0.5\times1\text{ pF}\times0.64=0.32$ pJ per transition, so roughly 0.3 pJ for a few millimeters. Going off-chip adds pad and ESD capacitance (hundreds of fF), package capacitance and inductance, a PCB trace of several pF (about 1 to 2 pF per cm), the receiver input and termination, plus the PHY's serialization, calibration and clocking circuitry. Total energy per bit for an LPDDR-class interface is typically a few pJ per bit at the controller side and higher with termination and the DRAM end; DDR-class interfaces cost more.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Published pJ/bit numbers depend on process, link speed, termination and what is included (PHY only, PHY plus DRAM I/O, or a whole memory access). The 10 pJ/bit versus 1 pJ/bit pair above is an order-of-magnitude illustration.

**Worked example: a GPU-to-memory link.** Suppose the GPU needs 50 GB/s from memory. Off-chip at 10 pJ/bit: $50\times10^9\times8\times10\times10^{-12}=4$ W for the link alone. Moving the same traffic through an on-die cache at 1 pJ/bit would cost 0.4 W. If a system-level cache serves even half the traffic on-die, the interface power drops by a few watts, a substantial saving in a 5 W phone. This is why SoCs bury caches and keep accelerators on the same die ([[q0933]]).

## Bandwidth, latency and pins

Bandwidth off-chip scales with *pins times data rate*. A 64-bit LPDDR5-6400 interface delivers $64\times6.4/8=51.2$ GB/s and needs on the order of a hundred signal balls plus power. An on-die fabric of 512 bits at 1 GHz gives 64 GB/s with no balls at all; widening it is just more metal. For latency, an on-die transfer between a CPU and a cache or accelerator is a few to tens of cycles. A chip-to-chip hop adds serialization, link-layer and clock-domain crossing latency, typically tens of nanoseconds at best (higher for PCIe-style links). Those costs matter for fine-grained interactions such as cache coherency between CPU and GPU or sharing small data structures.

## Cost: NRE versus unit cost

Compare two ways to build a product: a custom SoC integrating CPU, GPU and I/O onto one die, versus a board with separate chips.

- Custom SoC: NRE 5,000,000 USD (design team, IP, masks, verification, package and qualification), unit cost 18 USD (die, package, test).
- Discrete board: NRE 150,000 USD (schematic, layout, bring-up), unit cost 45 USD (several chips, more PCB area, passives, assembly).

Average cost per unit: $c(V)=NRE/V+c_u$. Break-even: $V^*=\dfrac{NRE_{SoC}-NRE_{disc}}{c_{disc}-c_{SoC}}=\dfrac{4{,}850{,}000}{27}\approx179{,}600$ units.

A second source of cost is **die size**: unit cost scales superlinearly with area because yield falls as area rises ([[q0932]]). A very large SoC may cost more per good die than splitting into dies or chiplets.

## When the catalog SoC changes the question

Most teams do not design the SoC. They buy one, so NRE is zero and the choice is between (a) one catalog SoC that includes the CPU, GPU and peripherals you need and (b) building from separate processor, GPU/FPGA and interface chips. Here the SoC nearly always wins on BOM, power and board area, and the real trade-offs are different: vendor lock-in and roadmap, software support, longevity, lack of exactly the peripheral mix you need, and thermal limits. If one function is missing, the usual answer is to add the missing function externally (for example an Ethernet PHY or a CAN transceiver, [[q0930]]) rather than abandon the SoC.

## Flexibility, risk and process compromise

- **Process compromise.** Logic wants a fine-pitch digital node; analog and RF perform better in older, higher-voltage nodes; DRAM needs special capacitor processes; flash needs its own. A monolithic SoC picks one node, so the analog or RF parts may be larger and noisier than they would be in a dedicated process. Embedded flash is often unavailable below certain nodes, which is why advanced-node SoCs use external flash.
- **Thermal concentration.** 5 W in a 100 mm² die is 5 W/cm². Spreading across chips lowers local density.
- **Risk.** A bug in a custom SoC means a respin: weeks to months of schedule and a new mask set. A board can be fixed with a revision of a PCB, typically in days or weeks.
- **Supply.** One highly integrated, sole-source part is a single point of failure for the product; a discrete design might have alternatives for individual parts.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> State the decision rule explicitly: "I'd integrate when volume, power or size justify the NRE and the requirements are stable; otherwise I'd use a catalog SoC, a module, or discrete parts." Then give the break-even formula.
:::

::: applications
- **Smartphones**: custom or semi-custom application processors justified by hundreds of millions of units.
- **Low-volume industrial controllers**: off-the-shelf SoC on a SoM ([[q0931]]) or discrete MCU plus peripherals.
- **FPGA prototypes**: discrete FPGA plus off-the-shelf parts used to prove a design before committing to an ASIC.
- **Cost-optimized consumer products**: highly integrated catalog SoCs with PMIC and DRAM as the only major external parts.
- **High-performance computing**: chiplet-based packages, partitioning integration across several dies ([[q0932]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- NRE and unit-cost numbers are illustrative and vary widely by process node, design complexity and IP.
- Energy-per-bit figures are order-of-magnitude and depend on interface, process and what is counted.
- The analysis treats volumes as known; in reality demand uncertainty adds risk to the NRE-heavy option.
- Assumes functionality is equal between the options; a SoC might not integrate a needed function.
- Software cost is not included; a custom SoC adds driver, board-support and tool development, a catalog SoC may include a software ecosystem.
:::

::: followups
### Why is a signal on-die cheaper to send than off-chip?
Capacitance. Switching energy is $\tfrac12CV^2$. On-die wires are a fraction of a picofarad per millimeter-scale length, while off-chip signals drive pad, package, board trace and receiver capacitance, plus termination, so energy per bit is typically about an order of magnitude higher. The PHY also adds serialization and clock-recovery circuits.

### How would you find the break-even volume between a custom ASIC and an FPGA-based design?
Write total cost for each as NRE plus volume times unit cost and set them equal: $V^*=(NRE_A-NRE_F)/(c_F-c_A)$. The FPGA has low NRE but high unit cost; the ASIC has high NRE and low unit cost. Also include schedule: if the FPGA gets you to market six months earlier, that revenue counts. Non-recurring risk such as respin probability should be priced in.

### Name a case where separate components beat a SoC.
Low volume specialist products; designs needing a process the SoC doesn't offer, such as high-voltage drivers, RF power or high-resolution precision ADCs; designs where one function must be upgraded independently; or where thermal density from one die is a problem. A precision analog front end is usually best as a separate chip because digital switching noise on the SoC would degrade it.

### Does integrating more always lower power?
No. Integration reduces I/O power, but it adds leakage from more transistors, requires larger shared clock and power distribution, and can cause the whole die to run hot, raising leakage. If a block is unused it must be power-gated, or it wastes static power. Net savings depend on what moves on-die and how well power is managed ([[q0933]]).

### A startup has 5,000 units/year and wants the best-in-class power. SoC or discrete?
Not a custom SoC, because 5,000 units cannot pay back millions of NRE. Choose a catalog low-power SoC (or MCU) that meets the power goal and add external parts as needed, or a SoM ([[q0931]]) to cut hardware risk. Revisit a custom chip only if volumes reach hundreds of thousands per year and the differentiation requires it.
:::

::: interpretations
Two readings coexist. "SoC vs all separate components" can mean *buying* a catalog SoC versus several chips, or *designing* an integrated chip versus a board of chips. This page treats both: the physics and board-level benefits apply to either; the break-even cost analysis applies mainly to custom design. The integration trade-off at die level (yield, noise, thermals, chiplets) is in [[q0932]].
:::
