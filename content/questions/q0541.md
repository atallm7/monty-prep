::: testing
Whether you decide on a heat sink by calculation rather than by feel. The interviewer wants the condition (the board alone cannot keep $T_j$ below its derated limit at worst-case power and ambient), the thermal-resistance chain with a worked number, and the alternatives you try first (less dissipation, more copper, airflow, spreading into the enclosure). Bonus points for mechanical and electrical details: isolation pads, mounting force, height, and floating metal near switching nodes.
:::

::: why
Heat sinks add cost, height, weight, assembly steps and sometimes EMC problems, so you want one only when you need it, and you want to specify it correctly when you do. Choosing too small a sink gives field failures; choosing too big wastes money and space. The calculation is a ten-line exercise that interviewers like because it shows you can go from a datasheet to a decision.
:::

::: concepts
- **Thermal resistance chain**: $T_j = T_a + P(\theta_{JC} + \theta_{CS} + \theta_{SA})$. See [[q0540]].
- **Derating**: designing to a $T_j$ well below the absolute maximum (often 10 to 25 °C below).
- **Convection**: natural vs forced air; a fan lowers $\theta_{SA}$ by a factor of several.
- **Thermal interface materials**: paste, pads and insulators that fill microscopic gaps.
- **Efficiency**: first try to dissipate less. See [[q0157]].
- **Why heat matters**: [[q0332]].
:::

::: answer
"I use a heat sink when the board alone can't keep the junction below its limit at the worst-case ambient and power. The test is a calculation: junction temperature is ambient plus power times the total thermal resistance. Suppose a TO-220 FET dissipates 3 W in a 50 °C enclosure and I want to keep the junction at 110 °C, which gives margin under a 150 °C limit. That allows 60 degrees of rise, so the total resistance has to be 20 kelvin per watt or less. A TO-220 in free air is more like 60. With junction-to-case at 1.5 and an insulating pad at 0.5, the heat sink needs to be 18 K/W or better, which a modest extruded sink can do, and a fan would help further.

Before I add one I look at the cheaper options: dissipate less, for example with a buck instead of an LDO; add copper, thermal vias and airflow; or bolt the part to the enclosure. A heat sink makes sense when the dissipation is a few watts or more in a package that can't spread it on the board, when the enclosure is hot, or when the part has a tab designed to be mounted to one.

Then the details: thermal paste or pad, an insulator if the tab is at a voltage, mounting pressure, and keeping a floating metal sink away from switching nodes, because it acts like an antenna."
:::

::: technical
**Criterion.** A heat sink is warranted if, with the best reasonable board (copper, vias, airflow) and the worst-case ambient and power, $T_j = T_a + P\,\theta_{JA,board} > T_{j,design}$, where $T_{j,design}$ is the derated limit (typically 10 to 25 °C below $T_{j,max}$).

**With a heat sink.**
$$T_j = T_a + P\,(\theta_{JC} + \theta_{CS} + \theta_{SA})$$
$\theta_{JC}$ is the junction-to-case value from the datasheet, $\theta_{CS}$ is the case-to-sink interface (about 0.1 to 0.5 K/W with paste, 1 to 3 K/W with an electrical insulator, depending on the package), and $\theta_{SA}$ is the sink-to-air value from its datasheet (depends on airflow and orientation). The required sink is
$$\theta_{SA,req} = \frac{T_{j,design}-T_a}{P} - \theta_{JC} - \theta_{CS}$$
Example: $P=3$ W, $T_a=50$ °C, $T_{j,design}=110$ °C, $\theta_{JC}=1.5$, $\theta_{CS}=0.5$: $\theta_{SA,req}=60/3 - 2 = 18$ K/W. The sink temperature will then be $50+3\times18 = 104$ °C and the case $105.5$ °C.

**Typical triggers.**
- Dissipation above roughly 1 W for small surface-mount packages, a few watts for TO-220/TO-247, unless the board spreads heat well.
- High ambient (enclosure at 60 to 85 °C), low derating margin, or sealed enclosure.
- Parts specified to be mounted on a sink (power transistors, rectifiers, power modules).

**Alternatives first.** Cut $P$ (higher efficiency, lower $V_{in}$), more copper and thermal vias, forced air, mounting to a metal chassis or enclosure, or a pad to the case.

**Practical details.** Thermal paste or a phase-change pad; insulating washers or pads when the tab is not at ground; correct torque; keep the mounting from stressing a PCB or BGA; check height and weight; ground floating sinks or keep them away from the switch node.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Heat sink thermal resistances in datasheets are measured under specified airflow and orientation. Real values in your enclosure can be 1.5 to 2x worse. Interface resistances vary widely with pressure, flatness and material.
:::

::: long
"I use a heat sink when the board can't do the job by itself, and I decide that with a number, not a feeling.

The model is a chain of thermal resistances. Junction temperature equals ambient plus power times the sum of junction-to-case, case-to-sink, and sink-to-air. Say I have a TO-220 FET dissipating 3 W in a 50 °C enclosure, and I want to keep the junction at 110 °C so there's margin to a 150 °C limit. That gives 60 degrees of rise, so the total has to be 20 kelvin per watt or lower. Free-air TO-220 is typically around 60, so the board alone fails. Junction-to-case on the datasheet is maybe 1.5, an insulating pad adds about 0.5, so the heat sink must be 18 K/W or better. That's a modest extruded sink in some airflow, and a fan would give more margin.

But first I'd see whether I can avoid it. I'd look at reducing the dissipation, for example a buck instead of an LDO. I'd add copper, thermal vias, and airflow. I'd see whether the part can bolt to the enclosure, which is just a big heat sink I already have.

If I still need one, details matter: thermal paste or a pad to fill the gaps, an insulator if the tab is at a voltage, the right mounting torque, height and weight, and I'd ground a floating metal sink or keep it away from the switch node, because it can act like an antenna or couple noise. And I'd verify with a thermocouple or a camera at worst-case load."
:::

::: terms
- **Heat sink**: a metal body with large surface area that conducts and convects heat away from a component.
- **$\theta_{SA}$**: sink-to-ambient thermal resistance (K/W); depends on airflow and orientation.
- **$\theta_{CS}$**: case-to-sink interface resistance.
- **Thermal interface material (TIM)**: paste, pad, or phase-change material filling surface gaps.
- **Derating**: limiting $T_j$ below its absolute maximum for reliability.
- **Forced convection**: airflow from a fan; lowers $\theta_{SA}$ several-fold.
- **Insulating pad / washer**: electrically isolates a tab from the sink, with added thermal resistance.
- **Thermal runaway**: a positive feedback where rising temperature raises dissipation.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "when the part gets too hot."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames it as a calculation: required $\theta_{SA}$ from $T_j$, $T_a$, $P$, $\theta_{JC}$, $\theta_{CS}$, with numbers.
- Distinguishes derated target from absolute max, and worst-case ambient from room temperature.
- Lists what to try before a sink: lower dissipation, board copper/vias, airflow, chassis mounting.
- Mentions interfaces and insulators (they add K/W), torque, and mechanical limits.
- Notes EMC side effects of floating metal and how to handle them.
- Knows when a sink is the wrong answer (small power, tight height, vibration) and when forced air is better.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Choosing a sink at room temperature.** Use the worst-case ambient inside the enclosure, which can be 20 to 40 °C higher.
- **Ignoring the interface.** An insulating pad can add 1 to 3 K/W, which for a 10 K/W system is a big fraction.
- **Using the sink's datasheet value without checking airflow and orientation.** A sink rated in forced air will be several times worse in still air.
- **Forgetting that the case is at voltage.** TO-220 tabs are usually connected to the drain or collector; a grounded sink shorts the tab unless you insulate.
- **Leaving a floating metal sink next to a switching node.** It couples noise; ground it or move it. See [[q0547]].
- **Adding a sink and forgetting mechanical stress.** A heavy sink hanging on a through-hole tab can crack solder joints in vibration.
:::

::: deep
## The decision: board or heat sink?

Start with the simplest model: $T_j = T_a + P\,\theta_{JA}$. If the best board you can reasonably build gives $T_j$ below the derated limit with margin, you do not need a sink. If not, the heat must leave through a lower-resistance path: a heat sink or the enclosure.

Maximum power for each path: $P_{max}=(110-50)/\theta$ = 1.0 W (60 K/W), 1.7 W (35 K/W), 3.0 W (20 K/W). That is a handy rule: a small SMD package on an ordinary board reaches its limit around 1 W at 50 °C ambient. Above that, either reduce the power or step up the thermal path.

Figure: The thermal network is Ohm's law with temperature as voltage and heat flow as current. With 3 W flowing, the drops are 4.5 K across the junction-to-case, 1.5 K across the interface and 54 K across the sink: $T_j = 50 + 3\times20 = 110$ °C.

Required sink resistance:

$$\theta_{SA,req}=\frac{T_{j,design}-T_a}{P}-\theta_{JC}-\theta_{CS}=\frac{110-50}{3}-1.5-0.5 = 18\ \text{K/W}$$

Check the temperatures along the chain: case $=50+3(18+0.5) = 105.5$ °C, sink $=104$ °C, junction $=110$ °C. The part is rated 150 °C, so there is 40 °C of margin to the absolute maximum, which matters for lifetime and for load or ambient excursions.

Insulating pads: a typical TO-220 electrical insulator is 1 to 3 K/W (ceramic or silicone-based pads differ); with paste but no insulator, $\theta_{CS}$ is about 0.1 to 0.5 K/W. If the tab is not at ground and you need isolation, that term can be a third of the budget.

## Sizing the sink

Heat sink data:

- Natural convection: small extruded or clip-on sinks for a TO-220 are in the range of 10 to 25 K/W; bigger finned sinks reach 3 to 8 K/W; bare flat plates and enclosure walls are tens of K/W and depend strongly on area.
- Forced air: a fan commonly reduces $\theta_{SA}$ by a factor of 2 to 5 depending on velocity.
- Orientation: vertical fins convect better than horizontal ones.
- Adjacent heat: heat sinks next to other hot parts see higher local ambient; use the air temperature at the sink, not the room.

If the required $\theta_{SA}$ is below about 5 K/W in natural convection, the sink is getting large; forced air or a different approach (spread into the chassis, reduce power) may be better.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Heat sink ranges above are typical orders of magnitude, not guarantees; use the manufacturer's curve for the specific part, airflow and orientation, and expect real enclosures to be worse than the datasheet.

## What to try before adding a sink

1. **Reduce $P$**. Efficiency is the first resort ([[q0157]], [[q0211]]): swap an LDO for a buck, lower the input voltage, parallel FETs.
2. **Improve the board path** ([[q0540]]): exposed pad, thermal vias, copper area and weight, planes.
3. **Airflow**: orient the board along the flow, add a fan, or open vents.
4. **Mount to the enclosure**: an aluminum chassis is an excellent spreader; use an insulating thermal pad if needed.
5. **Spread across parts**: use several smaller devices instead of one hot one, or multiphase ([[q0242]]).

## Mechanical and electrical details

- **Tab potential.** TO-220/TO-247 tabs are normally the drain, collector or cathode. A grounded sink needs an insulator, an insulating bushing for the screw, and creepage care.
- **Mounting force and flatness.** Thermal paste needs thin bond lines (tens of µm) and moderate pressure; over-torquing can crack a die or bow a PCB.
- **PCB stress.** For BGAs and large QFNs, glued or clipped sinks must not load the solder balls; a spring clip or backing plate is common.
- **Vibration and shock.** Heavy sinks need support, not just a through-hole lead.
- **EMC.** A floating metal sink near a switching node can pick up and radiate noise (it is a capacitively coupled patch). Ground it, or keep clear of the switch node. See [[q0547]].
- **Assembly and cost.** Paste or pad application, screws or clips, tolerance, and rework access.
- **Airflow.** Keep fins aligned with the flow; do not shadow the sink with tall parts.

## Transient and thermal capacity

For short bursts, a heat sink's thermal mass (specific heat × mass) absorbs energy, so a smaller sink can handle pulses. A 50 g aluminium sink ($c\approx0.9$ J/g·K) holds 45 J/K; a 10 W burst for 2 s raises it by only $20/45 = 0.44$ K. Datasheet transient thermal impedance ($Z_{th}$) curves quantify this for the package. For continuous loads steady-state values apply.

## When a sink is the wrong answer

- Low power (a few hundred mW): copper and vias suffice.
- Strict height or weight limits: use the enclosure or a different topology.
- High vibration or shock: a bonded or spring-clipped spreader may be safer than a large sink.
- Sealed enclosures: the sink only moves heat to the inside air; you need a path to the outside (a metal case or conduction to a chassis).
:::

::: applications
- **Linear regulators and pass transistors** in bench supplies and audio amplifiers (TO-220 with finned sinks).
- **Motor drivers and H-bridges** at several amps, where the module or MOSFET tab is bolted to a sink or the chassis.
- **Switch-mode power supplies**: rectifier diodes and primary FETs on an aluminium sink, often shared and grounded for EMC.
- **LED lighting**: metal-core PCB bonded to a finned sink.
- **BGA processors and FPGAs**: stick-on or clipped sinks with fans.
- **Battery chargers and inverters**: chassis-mounted power stages using the case as the sink.
:::

::: assumptions
The calculation assumes steady-state dissipation, a single hot component, and a lumped thermal-resistance chain, which ignores heat flowing out through the PCB in parallel with the sink path. In reality both paths operate: a part on a sink also loses a little heat to the board, which gives extra margin. Datasheet $\theta_{JC}$ is defined for a specified case reference and mounting method. Sink ratings assume stated airflow and ambient; real enclosures are hotter. The derated $T_j$ target (110 °C here) is a design choice, set by reliability and product requirements, not a universal number. An alternative to a sink in some designs is to design the circuit so the part dissipates less, or to mount the part to an existing chassis.
:::

::: followups
### How do you compute the required sink resistance?
$\theta_{SA} = (T_{j,design} - T_a)/P - \theta_{JC} - \theta_{CS}$. With 3 W, 50 °C ambient and a 110 °C junction target, the total allowed is 20 K/W; subtract 1.5 (junction to case) and 0.5 (paste and pad) to get 18 K/W. I would pick a sink with a margin, say 12 to 14 K/W at my airflow.

### When would you use forced air instead of a bigger sink?
When the required $\theta_{SA}$ gets so low that the sink is too big or heavy, or when the enclosure can't afford the volume. A fan reduces $\theta_{SA}$ by 2 to 5x, at the cost of power, noise, reliability (fans wear out), dust and an airflow path. If the product needs to be silent or sealed, I'd rather reduce the dissipation or spread heat into the chassis.

### Does a heat sink help if the part is surface-mount?
Yes, but differently. For QFN or BGA packages, heat mainly goes through the bottom into the PCB, so a stick-on sink on top helps a little for BGAs but much less than board copper. For power SMD parts, the best route is usually copper and vias, plus the board bolted to the chassis with a thermal pad. A top sink is for parts that dissipate through the top, like some BGAs and power modules.

### What goes wrong if the tab is not insulated from a grounded sink?
The tab connects to the drain or collector, which is not at ground, so the sink shorts it; at best the circuit won't work, at worst there is a destructive short or a shock hazard. Use an insulating pad and shoulder washer, and check creepage. Then add the pad's thermal resistance (1 to 3 K/W) to the budget.

### Why might you ground a heat sink?
An isolated metal body near a switching node couples noise capacitively and can radiate like a patch antenna. Connecting it to a quiet ground reference (and keeping it away from the switch node) damps this. Some designs instead tie the sink to the switch node deliberately; then it needs creepage and is not touchable, so check safety and EMC.
:::

"When should heat sinks be used?" is a design-decision question: the answer is the criterion plus the alternatives. Siblings: board-level heat reduction ([[q0540]]) and thermal relief ([[q0538]]). A few interviewers also mean "which components" (power transistors, regulators, processors), which the applications list covers.
