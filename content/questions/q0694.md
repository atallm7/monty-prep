::: testing
Whether you understand crosstalk as **unwanted coupling between neighbouring conductors through mutual capacitance and mutual inductance**, not "noise from nearby traces" in the abstract. The interviewer expects: aggressor and victim, the two coupling paths, near-end versus far-end crosstalk, the main knobs (spacing, height above the plane, coupled length, edge rate, termination) and the layout fixes (3W rule, ground plane, guard traces with stitching, orthogonal routing, differential signalling). Quantitative instincts ("crosstalk is about a few percent of the aggressor swing and grows with $dV/dt$") are a plus.
:::

::: why
Crosstalk is a top cause of "mystery" glitches on boards: a quiet reset or enable line pulses when a bus next to it switches, an ADC input picks up SPI clock harmonics, a memory bus fails at high speed. It scales with edge rate, so every new, faster device makes it worse, and it's set at layout time: you can't fix it in firmware. It also drives routing rules you will hear in any PCB discussion ([[q0567]], [[q0537]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Mutual capacitance ($C_m$)**: the electric field between adjacent traces injects a current $C_m\,dV/dt$ into the neighbour. See [[q0022]].
- **Mutual inductance ($L_m$)**: the magnetic flux of one trace's current links the neighbour's loop and induces $L_m\,dI/dt$. See [[q0041]].
- **Transmission-line behaviour**: coupled lines carry waves, so crosstalk shows up as a near-end pulse and a far-end pulse. See [[q0711]].
- **Edge rate**: both coupling terms scale with $dV/dt$ and $dI/dt$. See [[q0689]].
- **Return path**: the ground plane under the trace sets how tightly each line couples to ground versus to its neighbour.
:::

::: answer
"Crosstalk is unwanted coupling from one signal line, the aggressor, onto a neighbouring line, the victim. It happens through two mechanisms. Capacitive: the electric field between the traces acts like a small capacitor, so a changing voltage on the aggressor injects a current $C_m\,dV/dt$ into the victim. Inductive: the changing current on the aggressor, and its return in the plane, produces magnetic flux that links the victim's loop and induces a voltage $L_m\,dI/dt$.

On a real board with transmission lines, it shows up as two pulses on the victim: near-end crosstalk, which appears at the end closest to the driver and lasts about twice the coupled delay, and far-end crosstalk, which arrives at the far end with the aggressor's edge and is narrow. Both scale with the edge rate and the coupled length, and get stronger the closer the traces are relative to their height above the ground plane.

To reduce it: increase spacing, for example the 3W rule, get the traces closer to a solid ground plane, shorten parallel runs, route adjacent layers orthogonally, slow the edge where possible, and use differential signalling for the sensitive nets."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism and coupled model.** Two parallel traces over a plane have self capacitance $C$ and inductance $L$ per unit length, and mutual $C_m$ and $L_m$. An aggressor edge of amplitude $V$ and rise time $t_r$ couples onto a victim terminated at both ends:

- **Near-end (backward) crosstalk**, measured at the end of the victim nearest the aggressor's driver:
$$V_{NEXT} = K_b\,V,\qquad K_b = \frac14\left(\frac{C_m}{C} + \frac{L_m}{L}\right)$$
The pulse lasts $2t_d$ (twice the coupled one-way delay) and reaches full amplitude once the coupled length exceeds the *saturation length* $\ell_{sat} = \dfrac{t_r}{2\,t_{pd}}$; shorter lengths give a proportionally smaller pulse.

- **Far-end (forward) crosstalk**:
$$V_{FEXT} = K_f\,\frac{t_d}{t_r}\,V,\qquad K_f = \frac12\left(\frac{C_m}{C} - \frac{L_m}{L}\right)$$
A pulse of width $\approx t_r$, growing linearly with coupled length and with $1/t_r$. In a homogeneous dielectric (stripline) $C_m/C = L_m/L$ so far-end crosstalk is ideally zero; in microstrip the inductive term typically dominates and FEXT is negative.

**Illustrative numbers.** For $C_m/C = 0.05$, $L_m/L = 0.07$, a 3.3 V aggressor: $K_b = 0.03$, so NEXT $= 99$ mV; $K_f = -0.01$, so a 6-inch coupled run ($t_d \approx 1.02$ ns) with $t_r = 1$ ns gives FEXT $\approx -34$ mV. With FR4 at about 170 ps/inch, $\ell_{sat} = 1\text{ ns}/(2\cdot 170\text{ ps/in}) = 2.9$ in for $t_r = 1$ ns, and 0.29 in for 100 ps.

**Geometry.** Coupling falls rapidly with spacing $s$ relative to height $h$ above the plane; a common approximation is proportional to $1/(1+(s/h)^2)$. So $s = h$ gives half, $s = 2h$ gives 0.2, $s = 3h$ gives 0.1 of the reference.

**Mitigation.** Spacing (3W rule: centre-to-centre at least $3\times$ the trace width, a rule of thumb), thinner dielectric (smaller $h$), shorter parallel runs, orthogonal routing between adjacent layers, stripline for sensitive nets, guard traces stitched to ground with vias, differential pairs, series termination or slower edges, and well-terminated victims (an open-ended victim doubles the voltage at the open end).
:::

::: long
"Crosstalk is unwanted signal coupling between neighbouring traces. The noisy one is the aggressor and the one picking it up is the victim.

There are two coupling mechanisms. First, capacitive coupling: there's a small mutual capacitance between adjacent traces, so a changing voltage on the aggressor pushes a current $C_m\,dV/dt$ into the victim. Second, inductive coupling: the aggressor's current loop, which includes its return path in the ground plane, makes magnetic flux that links the victim's loop and induces $L_m\,dI/dt$.

On a PCB with traces long enough to act like transmission lines, these two combine into two pulses on the victim. Near-end crosstalk appears at the end close to the aggressor's driver; both mechanisms add there, it lasts about twice the coupled delay, and its amplitude is roughly a quarter of the sum of $C_m/C$ and $L_m/L$ times the aggressor swing. With about 5 percent capacitive and 7 percent inductive coupling that's 3 percent, around 100 millivolts on a 3.3 volt swing. Far-end crosstalk arrives at the other end, as a narrow pulse the width of the edge; in stripline it largely cancels, but in microstrip it's usually a negative pulse that grows with length and with faster edges.

The knobs are spacing relative to height above the plane, parallel length, edge rate, and termination. So I'd route traces further apart, keeping the 3W rule as a minimum, use a thin dielectric to the reference plane, shorten parallel runs, route adjacent layers at right angles, use stripline or differential pairs for sensitive signals, add a stitched guard trace if needed, and slow edges that don't need to be fast."
:::

::: terms
- **Aggressor / victim**: the line that causes the coupling / the line that receives it.
- **Mutual capacitance ($C_m$) and inductance ($L_m$)**: the coupling elements between the two lines.
- **NEXT (near-end crosstalk, backward)**: coupled noise at the victim end near the aggressor's source.
- **FEXT (far-end crosstalk, forward)**: coupled noise at the end far from the aggressor's source.
- **Saturation length**: coupled length beyond which NEXT stops growing, $t_r/(2\,t_{pd})$.
- **3W rule**: rule of thumb of spacing traces at least three trace widths centre-to-centre.
- **Guard trace**: a grounded trace between aggressor and victim, effective only if stitched to ground frequently.
- **Mode conversion**: differential-to-common-mode coupling caused by asymmetry; a related SI effect ([[q0697]]).
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "coupling between nearby traces causing noise". An exceptional answer names both mechanisms with their formulas ($C_m\,dV/dt$ and $L_m\,dI/dt$), distinguishes **NEXT** from **FEXT** and states how they scale with length and edge rate (saturating at $t_r/2t_{pd}$, versus growing linearly), knows that stripline cancels FEXT ideally while microstrip does not, gives a number (e.g. 3% of 3.3 V is about 100 mV), and offers layout mitigations in order of effect: spacing/height ratio, shorter runs, orthogonal routing, reference plane. It also warns that an unstitched guard trace can make things worse and that a victim's termination changes the measured amplitude.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Crosstalk is just capacitive."** Inductive coupling contributes equally at high speed and it is what dominates far-end crosstalk in microstrip.
- **Trusting guard traces blindly.** A floating or sparsely-stitched guard trace can resonate and increase coupling; it must be tied to ground with frequent vias.
- **Thinking slower clocks fix it.** Crosstalk depends on the edge rate, not the clock frequency. A 1 MHz signal with a 500 ps edge couples like a GHz one.
- **Using a thick dielectric to the plane.** A larger $h$ means the trace couples more to neighbours relative to the plane. A thin dielectric is better.
- **Ignoring return paths.** If the return current has to detour (plane split, via transitions), the loop grows and so does $L_m$.
- **Mixing up "near-end" and "far-end".** Near-end is at the aggressor driver's end of the victim.
:::

::: deep
## What's physically happening

Two traces side by side over a ground plane form a four-conductor structure (two signal conductors plus the plane). Each trace has a self capacitance to the plane and its own loop inductance. Because they are close, there's also a mutual capacitance (the electric field linking trace to trace) and a mutual inductance (magnetic flux from one loop passing through the other).

Figure: Cross-section of two microstrip traces. The purple arc is the electric-field coupling (C_m); the green dashed arc is the magnetic flux shared by the two signal-return loops (L_m). Each trace also couples to the plane (C_g). The closer the plane (smaller h) relative to the spacing s, the more of each trace's field terminates on the plane, and the less couples to the neighbour.

**Capacitive injection.** The current into the victim is $i = C_m\,\dfrac{d(V_a - V_v)}{dt}$ where $V_a$ is the aggressor voltage. A 3.3 V swing in 1 ns has $dV/dt = 3.3$ V/ns; with $C_m = 0.5$ pF over the coupled length, $i = 0.5\text{ pF}\cdot 3.3\text{ V/ns} = 1.65$ mA. This current splits towards both ends of the victim.

**Inductive induction.** The voltage induced in the victim loop is $v = L_m\,\dfrac{dI_a}{dt}$. For a 50 Ω line carrying $3.3/100 = 33$ mA edges in 1 ns and $L_m = 5$ nH, $v = 5\text{ nH}\cdot 33\text{ mA/ns} = 165$ mV in the loop (a lumped upper bound for a short, lumped section).

**Directions.** The two contributions *add* at the near end and *partially cancel* at the far end. That is why NEXT reflects the sum $C_m/C + L_m/L$ and FEXT the difference.

Figure: Blue: the near-end pulse is a plateau of about +99 mV that lasts roughly twice the coupled delay (2.04 ns here) after the edge. Red: the far-end pulse is a narrower dip of about −34 mV, appearing one coupled delay after the edge and as wide as the aggressor's rise time. Make the edge faster and FEXT grows (it scales as 1/t_r) while NEXT only saturates.

**Saturation length.** The NEXT pulse is a plateau only if the coupled length is long enough for the near-end pulse to reach full height before the reflected return arrives: length $\ge t_r/(2\,t_{pd})$. With $t_r = 1$ ns and 170 ps/inch that's 2.9 in. Short coupled runs give a smaller, triangular NEXT.

**Terminations matter.** The numbers assume the victim is terminated at both ends. An open victim end reflects the pulse, doubling it at that end and creating more ringing. Unterminated high-impedance inputs are the most vulnerable because they integrate the capacitively injected current.

## Geometry rules, with numbers

Reducing coupling is mostly geometry. A useful approximation of how coupling falls with spacing $s$ relative to height $h$:

$$\frac{\text{crosstalk}}{\text{crosstalk at } s\to 0\text{ reference}} \approx \frac{1}{1+(s/h)^2}$$

| $s/h$ | relative coupling |
|---|---|
| 0.5 | 0.80 |
| 1 | 0.50 |
| 2 | 0.20 |
| 3 | 0.10 |
| 4 | 0.059 |

Going from $s = h$ to $s = 3h$ cuts crosstalk by 5x (14 dB). The **3W rule** (centre-to-centre spacing of $3W$ for trace width $W$) is a quick version; for a typical 50 Ω microstrip with $W \approx 2h$ it corresponds to an edge-to-edge spacing of $2W = 4h$, i.e. around 0.06 relative coupling in this approximation.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The $1/(1+(s/h)^2)$ form is an approximation commonly quoted for weakly coupled microstrip and stripline; the real value depends on the stack-up, solder mask and exact geometry. For design-critical nets use a 2D field solver or the vendor's SI tool.

## Other mitigation, ranked

1. **Increase spacing / reduce $h$**: the strongest, free-at-layout-time lever.
2. **Shorten parallel runs**; break parallelism with a jog or route aggressors on a different layer.
3. **Orthogonal routing on adjacent layers**: coupling between crossing traces is tiny.
4. **Stripline** for sensitive or fast nets: FEXT ideally cancels.
5. **Guard trace with via stitching** at less than $\lambda/20$ intervals of the highest frequency of concern: gives modest improvement, and only when properly stitched.
6. **Slower edges or series termination** on aggressors that don't need speed ([[q0689]]).
7. **Differential signalling** for the sensitive victim: coupled noise appears as common-mode and is rejected ([[q0697]]).

## Debug approach

Observe the victim with the aggressor idle and with it toggling; the glitch should align with aggressor edges. Check polarity: a positive near-end pulse and negative far-end pulse is the classic microstrip signature. Try changing the aggressor's drive strength; the glitch amplitude should scale with $dV/dt$. If probing, use a short ground spring; a long ground lead couples the probe loop to nearby fields and fakes crosstalk.
:::

::: applications
- **Memory buses (DDR address/data)**: spacing rules (for example 3W or more) and length matching with crosstalk simulation as part of layout guidelines.
- **High-speed clock routing**: clocks routed with extra spacing and kept away from sensitive analog inputs.
- **Mixed-signal boards**: SPI or PWM lines kept away from ADC inputs and reference traces.
- **Ribbon cables**: alternate signal and ground wires to reduce coupling between adjacent conductors.
- **Flex and connector design**: ground pins interleaved with signals to limit coupling in the connector.
- **Differential pairs for USB, PCIe, Ethernet**: tightly coupled pairs reject common-mode crosstalk from neighbours.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Weak coupling, lossless, uniform lines**: the $K_b$ and $K_f$ formulas apply to weakly coupled lines with matched terminations; strong coupling (very close traces) requires full even/odd mode analysis.
- **Both ends of the victim terminated at $Z_0$**, so no reflections of the crosstalk pulse.
- **Linear superposition** of the aggressor's edge; real drivers are nonlinear and the victim's driver impedance matters.
- **Coupling ratios** used in the numbers ($C_m/C = 0.05$, $L_m/L = 0.07$) are illustrative, not derived from a specific stack-up.
- **Microstrip sign convention**: FEXT is typically negative when inductive coupling dominates; some references flip the sign convention.
:::

::: followups
### What's the difference between near-end and far-end crosstalk and which is usually bigger?
NEXT is measured at the victim end next to the aggressor's source and lasts $2t_d$; both coupling terms add. FEXT is at the other end, narrow ($\approx t_r$), and depends on the difference of capacitive and inductive coupling. For typical microstrip, NEXT is larger unless the run is very long and edges are fast; for stripline FEXT is near zero.

### Why does crosstalk get worse when I speed up the edges, even if the clock frequency stays the same?
Both injection mechanisms scale with the slew rate ($C_m\,dV/dt$ and $L_m\,dI/dt$), and FEXT scales as $1/t_r$ for a fixed coupled delay. The clock frequency itself isn't what matters; the edge is. Halving $t_r$ doubles FEXT and increases the length over which NEXT is not yet saturated.

### Does a guard trace help? When can it hurt?
A guard trace tied to ground with frequent vias shields the victim to some extent (a few dB to maybe 10 dB, depending on geometry) by diverting field lines to ground. If it's floating or stitched only at the ends, it can resonate and couple more. It's often more effective to just increase the spacing the same amount and skip the guard.

### You see noise on a quiet reset line that correlates with an SPI clock next to it. How would you confirm it's crosstalk and fix it?
Scope the reset line with the SPI active; the noise should line up with the SPI clock edges and scale with its drive strength. I'd also check by changing the SPI clock edge (drive strength) to see the amplitude change. Fixes: move the reset trace or add spacing, route on another layer or orthogonally, add a small RC filter at the reset input (reset isn't fast), and slow the SPI edges with a series resistor.

### If I double the dielectric thickness between the trace and the plane, what happens to crosstalk and why?
It goes up. The trace is farther from its reference, so more of its field lands on the neighbour and the loop inductance (and $L_m$) grows, while the trace width needed for 50 Ω also grows. Using the $1/(1+(s/h)^2)$ approximation: with $s$ fixed, doubling $h$ halves $s/h$ and increases coupling (for $s/h$ from 2 to 1, from 0.2 to 0.5 relative).
:::
