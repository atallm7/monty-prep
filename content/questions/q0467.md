::: testing
Whether you can choose a conductor by **reasoning about requirements**, not just answering "copper". The interviewer wants the criteria (resistivity, cost, processability by etching and plating, solderability, ductility, thermal conductivity, availability) and a comparison with silver, gold and aluminium that shows why copper wins for traces while others win for specific roles (finishes, contacts, bond wires, heat-spreader bases). Numbers help: copper resistivity, foil thicknesses, current capacity, temperature coefficient.
:::

::: why
The conductor sets resistance, current capacity, heating, high-frequency loss and cost for every board. Knowing how wide a trace must be for a given current, or why a gold trace would be silly, is basic layout judgement. The question also leads naturally to [[q0468]] (gold versus copper) and into reliability topics like copper roughness, plating and thermal expansion.
:::

::: concepts
- **Resistivity**: $R=\rho l/A$, and why low $\rho$ matters ([[q0014]], [[q0015]]).
- **Copper weight**: 0.5, 1 and 2 oz/ft² foil correspond to 17.5, 35 and 70 µm.
- **Current capacity**: the IPC-2221 relation $I=k\Delta T^{0.44}A^{0.725}$ for trace heating.
- **Temperature coefficient**: copper's resistance rises about 0.39% per °C ([[q0019]]).
- **Skin effect and roughness**: high-frequency current uses only the surface ([[q0056]]).
- **Process compatibility**: etching, electroplating and soldering must all work on the metal.
:::

::: answer
"Copper, for almost everything. It has the second-lowest resistivity of any practical metal, about 1.7 times 10 to the minus 8 ohm-metres, only slightly worse than silver, but it's hundreds of times cheaper. It's ductile, so it laminates and bends well; it's easy to etch and to electroplate, so you can build up thickness and plate the vias; it solders very well; and it conducts heat well, about 400 W/mK, so it spreads heat as well as current. Standard foil is 35 microns, one ounce, with 17.5 and 70 microns also common.

Silver is slightly better electrically but costs far more, tarnishes and has migration problems. Gold is a worse conductor and extremely expensive, though it's used thinly as a corrosion-proof finish and on contacts. Aluminium is a worse conductor, forms an oxide that's hard to solder, and plates poorly, so it's used as a heat-sink base in metal-core boards rather than for traces.

The trace sizing follows from copper's properties: roughly a 10 mil, 0.25 mm, trace of 1 ounce copper carries about 0.9 A for a 10 degree rise, and I'd go to 2 ounce or wider traces for power. And remember copper's resistance rises about 0.4% per degree, so hot traces are more resistive."
:::

::: technical
**Resistivity at 20 °C ($10^{-8}\ \Omega$m):** silver 1.59, copper 1.68 (annealed standard 1.724 defines 100% IACS, 58 MS/m), gold 2.44, aluminium 2.65, nickel 6.99, tin about 11.5. Copper's resistance temperature coefficient is about $+0.39\%$/°C: +50 °C gives +19.6%.

**Foil.** 0.5 oz (17.5 µm), 1 oz (35 µm), 2 oz (70 µm), up to 10 oz heavy copper. Sheet resistance 1 oz: $\rho/t=0.49$ mΩ/sq, so a 1 mm wide trace is 0.49 mΩ per mm.

**Current capacity (IPC-2221):** $I=k\,\Delta T^{0.44}A^{0.725}$ with $A$ in mil² (width × thickness; 1 oz = 1.378 mil), $k=0.048$ external and $0.024$ internal, $\Delta T$ in °C. For $\Delta T=10$ °C, 1 oz external: 10 mil gives 0.89 A, 20 mil 1.46 A, 50 mil 2.84 A, 100 mil 4.70 A. For 3 A the trace needs about 1.37 mm (54 mil) in 1 oz or 0.68 mm in 2 oz, external. IPC-2221's internal-layer value is conservative; IPC-2152 gives more accurate data that account for planes and board thickness.

**Why copper wins:** high conductivity per unit cost, ductility, easy photo-etching and electroplating, excellent solderability, good thermal conductivity (about 400 W/mK).
:::

::: short
"Copper, almost always. It has the second-lowest resistivity of any practical metal, about 1.7 times ten to the minus eight ohm-metres, close to silver but vastly cheaper. It's ductile, easy to etch and to electroplate, solders well and conducts heat. Standard foil is 35 microns, one ounce. Silver is costly and tarnishes, gold is worse and very expensive so it's only a thin finish, and aluminium is hard to solder and plate. So copper for traces, with the width set by the current."

::: long
"For the conductive layers I'd use copper, and I can give the reasons.

Electrically it's close to the best: resistivity of about 1.7 times ten to the minus eight ohm-metres, only slightly worse than silver at 1.6. Silver is not practical: it's roughly a hundred times more expensive per kilogram, tarnishes, and silver ions migrate under humidity and bias, which grows conductive filaments between traces. Gold is a worse conductor, around 2.4, and orders of magnitude more expensive, so it's only used as a thin finish. Aluminium, 2.65, is cheap and light but its oxide makes soldering and plating difficult, so it's used as a metal-core base or for wire bonds, not for traces.

Beyond conductivity, copper is ductile, so it survives lamination and thermal cycling; it can be photo-etched into fine patterns; it can be electroplated to build thickness and plate vias; it solders reliably; and it conducts heat, about 400 W/mK, so copper areas spread heat.

The standard foil is 35 microns, one ounce, with half-ounce inside and two-ounce or more for power. Current capacity follows IPC-2221: a 0.25 millimetre trace of one ounce copper carries about 0.9 amps for a ten degree rise, and 1.27 millimetres carries about 2.8 amps. Copper's resistance rises about 0.4% per degree, so hot traces are more resistive. At high frequency, skin effect and foil roughness matter, so RF boards use smooth foil.

So: copper for the traces, with finishes like gold, tin or nickel on top for the pads."
:::

::: terms
- **IACS**: International Annealed Copper Standard; 100% IACS is 58 MS/m (1.724×10⁻⁸ Ω·m).
- **Foil weight (oz)**: mass of copper per square foot; 1 oz is about 35 µm.
- **ED / RA copper**: electrodeposited and rolled-annealed foil ([[q0465]]).
- **Sheet resistance**: $\rho/t$, in ohms per square.
- **Temperature coefficient of resistance**: fractional change of resistance per °C (about 0.39%/°C for copper).
- **Skin depth**: depth to which AC current penetrates ([[q0056]]).
- **Electromigration / ion migration**: movement of metal under current or bias, a reliability concern for silver and for fine copper lines.
- **Heavy copper**: foil of 3 oz or more.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "copper because it's a good conductor".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the **criteria** (resistivity, cost, processability, solderability, ductility, thermal) and compares **silver, gold and aluminium** with numbers.
- Knows the **foil weights and thicknesses** and the **current-capacity relation**, with a worked example.
- Mentions **temperature coefficient** and **skin effect/roughness** as second-order issues.
- Knows where other metals **do** appear: gold finishes and contacts, nickel barrier, tin/solder, aluminium bases, silver inks, NiCr resistive films.
- Notes that the **thermal expansion mismatch** between copper and the laminate (z-axis) affects via reliability.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Silver is better, so use it."** Cost, tarnish and migration make it impractical for general traces.
- **Believing a wider trace never matters.** Current capacity scales as area to the 0.725 power; sizing needs the formula, not a guess.
- **Using the DC resistance at high frequency.** Skin effect and roughness raise AC resistance ([[q0463]]).
- **Ignoring temperature.** At +50 °C, copper's resistance is about 20% higher; sense resistors and traces shift.
- **Treating inner-layer traces like outer-layer traces.** Inner layers cool worse; IPC-2221 halves the constant (0.024 versus 0.048).
- **Confusing the conductor with the finish.** The finish (ENIG, HASL, OSP) is on the pads; the conductor is copper.
:::

::: deep
## What do we need from a conductor layer?

A PCB conductor has to do five things at once:

1. **Conduct** well (low $\rho$) so voltage drop and heating are small.
2. **Be manufacturable** in thin patterned layers: laminate to a dielectric, photo-etch to 0.1 mm features, electroplate for vias.
3. **Solder reliably**, or be coated with something that does.
4. **Survive mechanical and thermal cycling** (ductile, adhesion, expansion near the dielectric's).
5. **Be affordable** at square-metre quantities.

Copper meets all five. Almost no other material does.

## Copper foil

- **Weights**: 0.5 oz (17.5 µm), 1 oz (35 µm), 2 oz (70 µm), up to 10 oz (350 µm) for heavy copper. 1 oz/ft² is 305 g/m², thickness $305/8960\approx34$ µm.
- **Types**: ED (electrodeposited) is standard; RA (rolled-annealed) is used for flex ([[q0465]]). ED foil has a rough "tooth" side for adhesion. At GHz frequencies roughness lengthens the current path and raises loss, so low-profile (VLP, HVLP) foils are used for fast signals.
- **Plated copper**: via barrels and outer layers are electroplated; the plated copper has different grain structure and is slightly less ductile than foil.

## How much current can a trace carry?

IPC-2221's empirical relation (derived from old chart data) is

$$I = k\,\Delta T^{0.44}A^{0.725}$$

where $A$ is the cross-section in mil² (width in mil × thickness in mil; 1 oz is 1.378 mil), $\Delta T$ is the allowed temperature rise in °C, and $k=0.048$ for external layers or $0.024$ for internal layers.

Figure: Current capacity grows slower than width (exponent 0.725), so doubling the width does not double the current. Doubling the copper weight has the same effect as doubling the width. Internal layers carry about half the current for the same rise in this (conservative) formula.

**Worked example.** What width for 3 A in 1 oz copper with a 10 °C rise? Solve for area: $A=\left(\frac{I}{k\Delta T^{0.44}}\right)^{1/0.725}=\left(\frac{3}{0.048\times10^{0.44}}\right)^{1.379}=74.2$ mil², so $w=74.2/1.378=53.8$ mil = 1.37 mm. In 2 oz copper the width halves to 0.68 mm; for an inner layer the formula asks for about 3.6 mm in 1 oz.

> [!NOTE]
> IPC-2221's chart data are old and conservative for inner layers in boards with planes. IPC-2152 (based on more recent measurements) shows that internal traces in multilayer boards with copper planes can run cooler than IPC-2221 predicts. For critical designs (power, high ambient), use IPC-2152 or thermal simulation, and remember that vias, connectors and neighbouring heat sources change the picture.

## Temperature and resistance

$R(T)=R_{20}[1+\alpha(T-20)]$ with $\alpha\approx0.0039$/°C. A trace at +50 °C above 20 °C has 19.6% more resistance; for current sensing with a copper trace as shunt, that is a large error, which is why shunt resistors use alloys with near-zero temperature coefficient (manganin and similar) while PCB-trace sensing needs temperature compensation. It also makes the I²R heating self-reinforcing: higher temperature raises R, which raises heat.

## Frequency effects

Skin depth in copper is 66 µm at 1 MHz and 2.1 µm at 1 GHz ([[q0463]]), so at RF only the outer few microns carry current, the foil thickness stops mattering and **roughness and plating finish** matter: a nickel layer (poor conductor, magnetic) under gold on the surface adds loss at GHz because the skin depth reaches into it ([[q0468]]).

## Thermal and mechanical

Copper conducts heat at about 400 W/mK, vastly better than FR-4 (about 0.3 W/mK), so copper pours, planes and thermal vias are the main heat spreaders on a board. Its expansion (about 17 ppm/°C) matches the in-plane expansion of FR-4 reasonably, but FR-4's z-axis expansion is several times larger, which stresses via barrels in thermal cycling (a key reliability issue; thicker plating and high-Tg laminates help).

## Other conductive materials on boards

- **NiCr or other resistive foils** for embedded resistors.
- **Silver or carbon ink** on flexible and printed electronics (higher resistivity; low-temperature processes).
- **Aluminium** in metal-core bases and sometimes as wire-bond material.
- **Copper alloys and beryllium copper** in connectors and spring contacts (not PCB traces).
:::

::: applications
- **Standard traces and planes**: 0.5 to 1 oz copper on almost every board.
- **Power supplies and motor drives**: 2 to 4 oz or heavy copper for high current and heat spreading.
- **RF boards**: smooth low-loss copper foil to reduce skin-effect loss.
- **Flex circuits**: rolled-annealed copper ([[q0465]]).
- **Printed electronics**: silver or carbon ink on PET.
- **Embedded resistors**: NiCr films for terminations and pull-ups in dense boards.
:::

::: assumptions
I assumed rigid, subtractively etched copper-on-laminate boards. The IPC-2221 current formula is empirical and conservative (and the internal-layer constant is not accurate for boards with planes); real current capacity depends on board thickness, nearby copper, airflow, vias and ambient temperature. Prices are market-dependent. For printed electronics, additive processes and different conductors dominate. For superconducting or cryogenic boards, material choice is a different problem. If the intended answer is just "copper", the explanation of why other metals fail is the added value.
:::

::: followups
### How wide a trace do you need for 5 A on an outer 1 oz layer with a 20 °C rise?
Solve $A=(I/(k\Delta T^{0.44}))^{1/0.725}$: $\Delta T^{0.44}=20^{0.44}=3.74$, so $I/(0.048\times3.74)=27.9$, raised to 1.379 gives $A=98.8$ mil², so $w=98.8/1.378=71.7$ mil = 1.82 mm. In practice you'd also check the voltage drop ($R=0.49$ mΩ/mm per mm of width, so a 1.82 mm trace is 0.27 mΩ/mm: 100 mm drops 0.13 V at 5 A) and connector and via limits.

### Why does copper resistance rise with temperature, and does it matter for a PCB trace?
Metals scatter electrons more from lattice vibrations at higher temperature, so $\rho$ rises (about 0.39%/°C for copper). It matters for precision current sensing with trace resistance, for long power traces that heat up, and in thermal runaway-like feedback (more heat, more resistance, more heat). For ordinary signal traces it is negligible.

### Why not use aluminium traces since it's cheaper and lighter?
Aluminium's native oxide is hard, insulating and re-forms instantly, so soldering and electroplating are difficult; its resistivity is about 1.6 times copper's; and it has poorer fatigue and creep behaviour in joints. It's used where those do not matter: heat sink bases (MCPCB), wire bonds, and in IC metallisation (with barrier layers) historically.

### What if you need very high frequency (30 GHz) performance? Does copper still win?
Copper still does, but its surface matters: use very smooth foil, avoid thick magnetic nickel under the finish (or use ENEPIG, immersion silver or direct-gold alternatives with thin nickel), and low-loss laminate. At 30 GHz the skin depth is only about 0.38 µm, so the surface treatment and roughness (on the order of µm) can double the loss.

### Scenario: a 50 mm long, 0.25 mm wide 1 oz trace feeds a load of 1 A. How hot and how much voltage drop?
Resistance is about 0.098 Ω, so drop is 98 mV and dissipation is 98 mW. IPC-2221 predicts about 13 °C rise at 1 A for this width (0.89 A gives 10 °C and the rise scales as $I^{1/0.44}$). So 1 A is slightly above a 10 °C design point; for a 5 V rail the 2% drop might also matter. Widen to 0.5 mm or use 2 oz copper to halve both the drop and the heating.
:::
