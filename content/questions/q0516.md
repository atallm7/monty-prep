::: testing
Whether you know the "no 90° corners" rule **and** can explain what physically drives it (extra copper at the corner = extra capacitance = a small impedance dip) **and** how big the effect really is. A strong candidate gives the practical shape rules (constant width, 45° mitres or smooth arcs, no stubs or acute angles, gradual tapers, symmetric bends on pairs), then shows judgement: right angles are mostly a tolerable sin below a few GHz, but the discipline protects return paths, manufacturing and the fastest nets.
:::

::: why
Layout reviews are full of this question. Engineers who recite "90° corners radiate" are repeating a myth; engineers who say "they add a few tens of femtofarads, which matters at tens of ps edges, and I route 45° anyway because it costs nothing" are showing real understanding. The same thinking also covers pad transitions, tapers and teardrops, where the real impedance discontinuities hide. See also the routing overview [[q0514]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Impedance depends on geometry**: $Z_0$ changes with trace width and height; any local width change is a small discontinuity. See [[q0524]].
- **Reflection from a small shunt capacitance**: peak $\approx Z_0C/(2t_r)$. See [[q0711]].
- **Mitred and curved bends**: remove the excess copper at a corner so the local width stays constant.
- **Return current follows the trace**: shape matters less than keeping the plane intact beneath. See [[q0486]].
- **Stubs and T-junctions**: shape features that reflect strongly. See [[q0526]].
- **Manufacturability**: acute angles and thin necks can cause etching and yield issues.
:::

::: answer
"I want them straight where I can and, when they have to turn, 45 degree mitred corners or smooth curves, never a sharp 90 and never an acute angle. Constant width, no stubs, no T-junctions, and any width change as a gradual taper.

The reason is that the impedance of a trace depends on its width over the plane. At a square 90 degree corner, the copper momentarily gets about 40% wider along the diagonal, so you get a bit of extra capacitance, so the impedance dips for a few picoseconds and part of the edge reflects. I actually worked the number: for a 50 ohm line that's on the order of ten femtofarads, which is a reflection of well under 1% with a 100 ps edge. So at modest speeds a right angle is not what kills your board. But it only costs me a mouse click to route 45 degrees, it's honest good practice at multi-gigabit speeds where edges are 20 or 30 ps, and it avoids acute angles that trap etchant at the fab.

The shapes that really hurt are stubs, wide pads on a narrow trace, and tapers that are too abrupt, so I pay more attention to those: teardrops where a trace meets a pad, symmetric bends on differential pairs, and a smooth fan-out."
:::

::: technical
**Constant geometry gives constant $Z_0$.** For microstrip, $Z_0$ is a function of $w/h$, $t/h$ and $\varepsilon_r$; a 10% width change at $w/h\approx 1.8$ moves $Z_0$ by about 5%. Keeping one width along the net keeps $\Gamma\approx 0$.

**90° corner.** At a square bend the copper area at the junction is larger than a straight run of the same length. The excess area is roughly $w^2/2$, giving an excess capacitance $\Delta C\approx\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r (w^2/2)/h$ (parallel-plate estimate, ignoring fringing). For $w=0.36$ mm, $h=0.2$ mm, $\varepsilon_r=4.3$ this is about 12 fF. A shunt capacitor produces a peak reflection of about $\rho\approx Z_0\Delta C/(2t_r)$: 0.3% for $t_r=100$ ps, 1% for 30 ps. Published measurements and field simulations agree that this is a small, high-frequency effect (typically tens of fF); it becomes relevant above several GHz or in tightly toleranced RF designs. The corner also contributes a small series inductance and slightly shortens the current path.

**Fixes.** (1) *45° mitre:* chamfer the outer corner. The optimum chamfer depends on $w/h$; the figure usually quoted from the microwave literature for a typical 50 Ω FR-4 line ($w/h\approx2$) is a little over half of the corner diagonal, rising for narrower traces. CAD defaults are close enough for digital work. (2) *Arc or "rounded" bend:* radius of at least about 3 trace widths keeps the local width constant. (3) *Two 45° bends* (what most CAD tools do by default).

**Other shape rules.**
- No **acute angles** (< 90° between segments): acid traps at the fab, extra coupling between nearby segments, and a larger local capacitance.
- No **T-junctions or stubs**; branch with a series resistor or topology change ([[q0526]]).
- Changes in width (pad entry, BGA breakout) should be **tapered** or minimised; for a wide pad on a thin trace, remove plane copper under the pad (anti-pad) to restore impedance. **Teardrops** reinforce pad–trace joints.
- On **differential pairs** make bends symmetric and compensate the outer length ([[q0521]]).
- Keep bends **away from vias and pads** so reflections don't add.
:::

::: short
"Straight where possible, and 45° mitres or curves when they have to turn, with constant width, no stubs and no acute angles. A 90° corner puts extra copper over the plane, which is a little capacitance and a small impedance dip. For a 50 Ω line that's around ten femtofarads, so well under a percent of reflection for a 100 ps edge, mostly negligible below several GHz. It costs nothing to route it right, though, and it matters on multi-gigabit nets. The bigger offenders are stubs, abrupt width changes and wide pads."
:::

::: long
"For routing shape, I use a few simple rules. Keep the trace a single constant width, because the impedance is set by width over the plane and any change is a discontinuity. Go straight where I can. When the trace has to turn, I use 45 degree mitres or a smooth arc, never a square 90 and never an acute angle. No stubs and no T-junctions, because those reflect much more than any corner. And where the width has to change, like entering a pad, I taper it and I may cut the plane away below a large pad so the impedance doesn't dip.

Why the 90 degree rule? At a square corner, the copper is momentarily wider on the diagonal, which is extra area over the plane, so extra capacitance and a local impedance dip. I estimated it for a 0.36 millimetre trace on a 0.2 millimetre dielectric: the excess is about half a trace width squared, roughly 12 femtofarads. For a shunt capacitor, the reflection is about Z0 times C over two times the rise time, so with a 100 picosecond edge that is about 0.3%, and with a 30 picosecond edge about 1%.

So honestly, the 90 degree corner is a small effect at moderate speeds. The old claim that it causes serious radiation or ringing is mostly folklore. But the cost to avoid it is zero, it becomes measurable in multi-gigabit and RF designs, and acute angles also create manufacturing problems. On a differential pair I make both bends symmetric and add length compensation right after the bend. I spend my real attention on the return path under the trace, vias and the pad transitions."
:::

::: terms
- **Mitred (chamfered) bend**: a corner with the outer vertex cut at 45° to keep local width constant.
- **Excess corner capacitance**: extra shunt capacitance from the additional copper area at a right-angle corner.
- **Acute angle / acid trap**: a sharp trace angle < 90° where etchant can pool, over-etching copper.
- **Taper**: a gradual change of width over a distance of several widths to avoid a step.
- **Teardrop**: widened trace-to-pad or trace-to-via transition for mechanical strength and smoother impedance.
- **Neck-down**: locally narrowing a trace (e.g. between BGA pads); unavoidable but short.
- **Anti-pad (plane cut-out under pad)**: removing plane copper under a large pad to reduce its capacitance.
- **Stub**: dead-end branch; a strong reflector and resonator.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "no 90° corners because of reflections/EMI". An exceptional answer:
- Explains the **mechanism** (excess copper = excess shunt C) and **quantifies** it (≈ 10 fF; <1% at 100 ps).
- States **honestly that the effect is small** and why people still do it (zero cost, matters at ≥ 10 Gb/s and RF, DFM).
- Lists the **bigger offenders** (stubs, pads, vias, plane gaps) and what to do about them.
- Knows the alternatives: **45° mitre, arc, teardrops, tapers**, and says to make bends symmetric on differential pairs.
- Distinguishes **plan shape** (this question) from **cross-section** (trapezoid from etch factor) when the question is ambiguous.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"90° corners radiate."** Measured and simulated radiation from a single right-angle bend is negligible; the real radiation comes from broken return paths and common-mode currents.
- **Using a mitre that is too deep or too shallow.** A tiny chamfer still has extra copper; a very deep one looks inductive. Use the CAD tool's recommended or typical ~50–60% cut.
- **Using acute angles (< 90°)** when fanning out. They cause etching defects and increase coupling.
- **Fixing the corners but leaving a stub** (a T for a test point or a branch). The stub is a larger discontinuity.
- **Abrupt pad transitions.** A 0.3 mm trace into a 1 mm pad is a far larger capacitance than any bend.
- **Asymmetric bends on a differential pair.** The outer trace is longer; without compensation you create skew.
:::

::: deep
## Plan shape: what a bend does electrically

The impedance of a microstrip is set by the field distribution between the trace and the plane, and so by $w/h$. Along a straight run the field is uniform. At a bend the geometry changes for a short distance; two effects appear.

1. **Extra capacitance.** At a square corner, the electrical path turns 90°, but the copper has a corner "patch" with additional area, roughly $w^2/2$ beyond what a unit of straight line would have. The capacitance to the plane under that extra patch is
$$\Delta C \approx \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\frac{w^2/2}{h}$$
For $w=0.36$ mm, $h=0.2$ mm and $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$ (a 50 Ω line), $\Delta C\approx 12$ fF.

2. **Slightly shortened inductive path** around the inside of the corner, which partly compensates by adding a little less series inductance.

A small shunt capacitance in a line produces a reflection whose peak is, to first order,
$$\rho\approx \frac{Z_0\,\Delta C}{2\,t_r}$$

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Quantitative statements about corner capacitance vary by source and geometry. The parallel-plate estimate above ignores fringing and is deliberately rough; published field-solver and measurement studies report tens of fF for typical FR-4 microstrip corners and an optimum mitre cut of a little over half the diagonal for $w/h\approx2$ (more for narrower lines). The exact optimum depends on substrate, $w/h$ and frequency, so treat these as orders of magnitude.

## The shapes that do hurt

| Feature | Typical effect | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Stub of length $\ell$ | Reflection + quarter-wave notch at $1/(4t_s)$ | Eliminate, use daisy chain / fly-by |
| Wide pad on a thin trace | 0.2–0.5 pF shunt C | Teardrop, plane cut-out, smaller pad |
| Abrupt width change | Step in $Z_0$, $\Gamma\approx\Delta Z/(2Z_0)$ | Taper over ≥ several widths |
| Acute angle | Local cap + etch defects + coupling | Use ≥ 90° (45° bends) |
| Meander too tight | Self-coupling and speed-up | Spacing ≥ 3–4 $w$ ([[q0517]]) |
| Bend directly at a via | Compounded reflections | Separate by a few widths |

**Taper example.** Going from a 0.36 mm trace to a 0.20 mm neck-down under a BGA changes $Z_0$ from 50 Ω to about 67 Ω (with $h=0.2$ mm); $\Gamma = 0.15$ for a long enough section, but if the neck-down is shorter than a sixth of the rise time it behaves as a small series L and reflects only a few percent. Keep necks short.

## Differential pairs and bends

In a pair, the outer trace of a bend is longer than the inner trace by about $s_{c}\cdot\theta$ (centre spacing times angle in radians); for a 90° bend of centre spacing 0.4 mm it is $0.4\times\pi/2\approx0.63$ mm = 3.7 ps in microstrip. If left uncorrected, it accumulates at every bend. Insert a small compensating jog on the *inner* trace *right after* the bend ([[q0521]]), keep both traces coupled, and keep the bend symmetric.

## Cross-section "shape" (trapezoid)

Etched traces are trapezoidal: the top is narrower than the bottom by about the copper thickness ($\sim$ 0.5–1 × $t$ total, vendor-dependent). The fab accounts for this when calculating the controlled-impedance width. It is the reason you specify the target impedance and the fab adjusts the trace width.

## Real hardware behaviour

At 25–56 Gb/s SerDes speeds (edges ~10–20 ps), every discontinuity counts: designers use smooth arcs, keep bends short and away from vias, and may run the 3D-EM model of the whole breakout. At 100 MHz CMOS, nobody will ever measure the difference between a 90° corner and a 45° one; the board will work or not based on return paths, termination and layer stack.

## Worked example: ranking discontinuities by the reflection they cause

The useful skill is not to remember that a corner is bad, but to compute how bad each feature is for a given edge so you spend effort in the right place. Take a 50 Ω microstrip ($h=0.2$ mm, $\varepsilon_r=4.3$, about 6 ps/mm) driven with a $t_r=100$ ps edge, and compare three discontinuities.

**1. A square 90° corner.** From above, $\Delta C\approx12$ fF, so the peak reflection is $Z_0\Delta C/(2t_r)=50\times12\times10^{-15}/(2\times10^{-10})=0.3\ \%$.

**2. A 1 mm round pad on the trace.** The parallel-plate capacitance of a 1 mm diameter pad over the plane at $h=0.2$ mm is
$$C=\frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A}{h}=\frac{(8.854\times10^{-12})(4.3)\,\pi(0.5\times10^{-3})^2}{0.2\times10^{-3}}=0.15\ \text{pF}$$
The peak reflection is $50\times0.15\times10^{-12}/(2\times10^{-10})=3.7\ \%$, about twelve times worse than the corner, and it scales the same way with rise time: 0.37 % at 1 ns. Fringing fields make the real value somewhat higher. This is why the page recommends cutting the plane under big pads: removing the reference under a pad drops its capacitance to the next plane's much smaller value.

**3. A 3 mm neck-down to 0.2 mm width.** The narrow section has about 67 Ω impedance and a delay of $3\times6=18$ ps, much shorter than the edge, so it acts as a small series inductor of $\Delta L\approx(Z_n-Z_0)\,T_d=(67-50)\times18\ \text{ps}=0.31$ nH. A series inductor reflects about $\Delta L/(2Z_0t_r)=0.31\times10^{-9}/(2\times50\times10^{-10})=3.1\ \%$ for the 100 ps edge. The neck-down is again about ten times worse than the corner.

| Feature | Peak reflection at 100 ps | At 1 ns |
|---|---|---|
| 90° corner | 0.3 % | 0.03 % |
| 1 mm pad over plane | 3.7 % | 0.37 % |
| 3 mm neck-down to 0.2 mm | 3.1 % | 0.31 % |

So at 100 ps edges the pad and the neck-down deserve far more attention than a corner. The ordering is general: features that add many tens or hundreds of fF or sub-nH inductance matter first, and a corner at the tens-of-fF level comes last. At 1 ns edges none of these would be a problem on their own, although several together add.

## Why the neck-down formula only holds for short necks

The series-inductor model is only valid while the neck's delay is small compared to the edge. A neck of length $\ell$ has delay $T_d=\ell\cdot6$ ps/mm, and when $T_d$ approaches $t_r/2$ or more the reflection is no longer the small ripple above but approaches the full step $\Gamma=(67-50)/(67+50)=0.145$ at its leading edge, with a matching negative step at the end. For a 100 ps edge the crossover is at about $\ell\approx8$ mm. So a 3 mm neck is in the inductor regime and a 20 mm neck is in the transmission-line regime where it should simply be impedance-matched, for example by widening the neck's dielectric or accepting the new impedance for the whole length.

## Stub resonance as a limit case

A stub is the shape that most clearly breaks the "small effect" assumption. A 3 mm open stub has a one-way delay of 18 ps and its quarter-wave resonance is at

$$f=\frac{1}{4T_d}=\frac{1}{4(18\ \text{ps})}=13.9\ \text{GHz}$$

where it presents a short circuit to the line. For a signal with $t_r=100$ ps (knee 3.5 GHz) the stub is far below its resonance and appears as a lumped capacitance of about $T_d/Z_0=18\ \text{ps}/50\ \Omega=0.36$ pF, giving a reflection of $Z_0C/(2t_r)=9\ \%$. That is three times worse than the pad, and it is why stubs are not on the "free insurance" list: a test point or a pull-up branch is a much larger discontinuity than a bend.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **Copper roughness and etch factor.** Fabs etch a trapezoidal trace, and the 0.2 mm neck is a size at which etch tolerance (a few mils either way) shifts the width by 10 to 20 %, which shifts the impedance by 5 to 10 % by itself. Do not size a neck-down by calculation alone; check with the fab's controlled-impedance rules.
- **Soldermask.** Mask over a trace adds capacitance and lowers $Z_0$ by a couple of ohms; a bend that is bare copper versus under mask differs slightly. Fabs' impedance calculators include a mask thickness parameter.
- **Plane under a bend.** A bend over a slot or a plane edge is much worse than a bend over a solid plane. Check the return path first and the shape second.
- **Acute angles in the fab.** Minimum feature rules and etch behavior in sharp internal angles are fab-specific; run the fab's DRC rather than assuming.
- **CAD defaults.** Most layout tools route 45° bends by default. The common error is manual 90° jogs inserted near pads when cleaning up, and a copper pour shaped by hand around a trace.

## Bench verification

A TDR is the right tool: a step with 35 to 70 ps edge sent into a test coupon with the feature under test shows a bump whose height and polarity tell you what the feature is. A capacitive feature (pad, corner) gives a dip in the reflected impedance trace, and an inductive one (neck, narrow jog) a bump upward; the area of the reflected bump divided by the step height is about $Z_0\Delta C/2$ for a capacitor and $\Delta L/(2Z_0)$ for an inductor, so integrating the trace gives you the lumped value directly. Build a coupon with several corners in series and compare it with a straight line of the same length: if the individual corners are invisible but ten of them are visible, you have your per-corner capacitance. For production, the fab's impedance coupon plus a pass/fail impedance tolerance is the standard check; per-feature verification is a design-development step.

::: applications
- **RF microstrip bends**: mitred bends are standard in RF design and in PCB filter layouts, where even small mismatches matter.
- **High-speed SerDes breakout**: smooth curved routing out of BGA fields on PCIe, USB, SATA and Ethernet.
- **DDR byte lanes**: bends done in 45° steps, with symmetric treatment of data pairs and strobes.
- **Antenna feeds and 50 Ω launches**: tapered transitions from a connector footprint to a controlled-impedance trace.
- **Digital controller boards**: 45° routing used as a CAD default for DFM and neatness.
:::

::: assumptions
- Corner numbers assume **FR-4 microstrip, 50 Ω, 1 oz copper**, with a parallel-plate estimate of the extra area. Stripline, different $w/h$ and different laminates give different (but similar-order) values.
- The reflection formula $Z_0C/(2t_r)$ is valid for **small $C$ relative to the edge** ($Z_0C/2\ll t_r$).
- "Shape" is read as the **plan view** of the route. If the interviewer means the **cross-section** (rectangular vs trapezoid, rounded), see the trapezoid note.
- For **slow signals** (≥ 5 ns edges), the corner type has no electrical significance at all.
:::

::: followups
### Do 90° corners really cause EMI problems?
Not by themselves. The capacitance of a corner is tiny (tens of fF), and the field-structure change does not make the bend an efficient antenna. EMI usually comes from return-path discontinuities, common-mode currents and unterminated fast edges. I'd still route 45° because it's free, but I wouldn't blame a corner for a failed EMC test without looking at the plane under the trace first.

### How would you taper a 0.3 mm trace into a 1.0 mm pad?
First I'd ask if the pad needs to be that wide; a narrower pad or a smaller package may be possible. If it must be, I'd taper over a length of at least a few widths, and remove the plane under the pad, or push the reference to the layer below, to cut its capacitance so the impedance stays near 50 Ω. A teardrop gives mechanical strength too.

### What do you do at a bend on a differential pair?
Keep both traces at constant spacing through the bend, use symmetric 45° or arc bends, and add the length compensation immediately after the bend on the shorter (inner) trace. Never fix the skew at the end of the route: the common-mode noise is generated where the skew is, so correcting it locally keeps it from propagating.

### Why are acute angles bad even if the electrical effect is small?
At the fab, etchant can pool in an acute internal angle ("acid trap") and over-etch it, causing a thinner or broken trace; they also place two conductors close together over a short length, increasing local coupling and capacitance. Neither is catastrophic, but both are avoidable.

### At what edge rate would you start worrying about a single right-angle bend?
Using $\rho\approx Z_0\Delta C/(2t_r)$ with 12 fF and 50 Ω, a 1% reflection needs $t_r\approx 30$ ps, which is a ~10 GHz signal. So for ≥ 10 Gb/s links, I'd use arcs and, ideally, check with a 3D solver. Below 1 Gb/s with edges of 300 ps or more, it's ≤ 0.1%.
:::

::: interpretations
"What shape" is mainly the **plan-view route shape** (bends, corners, tapers); the standard answer is "45° or curved, no 90°, no stubs". It could also be read as **cross-section** (the trace is a trapezoid due to etching), or as a "serpentine/meander" shape for delay matching, which is covered in [[q0517]] and [[q0521]]. I address the plan-view shape in depth and mention the other two.
