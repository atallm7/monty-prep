::: basics
### In plain words
Back-EMF is the voltage a motor generates simply because it is spinning. A motor and a generator are the same device. When the shaft turns, the coil moving through a magnetic field makes a voltage. In a motor, that voltage pushes against the supply, which is why it is called "back". It is what keeps a spinning motor from drawing huge current.

### Picture it
Imagine pushing a playground merry-go-round with a friend who runs along the rim. At the start, the merry-go-round is still, so you push hard. As it speeds up, it moves away from your hand, and you can push less and less. The faster it goes, the less your push matters. Back-EMF works the same way: speed reduces the net push of the supply. The analogy has one gap: the motor creates this opposition itself.

### Words you need
- **EMF**: electromotive force, a voltage produced by a source. Here the source is the spinning coil.
- **Back-EMF**: the voltage a spinning motor generates, opposing the supply. It equals $K_e\omega$.
- **$\omega$ (omega)**: shaft speed in radians per second.
- **Stall**: shaft held still, so back-EMF is zero.
- **Flyback voltage**: a different effect, the spike when coil current is interrupted. See [[q0340]].

### The simplest example
Take a 12 V motor with a 1 Ω winding. Suppose $K_e = 0.02$ V per rad/s. Stalled, back-EMF is 0 V, so the current is 12 V / 1 Ω = 12 A. At 300 rad/s, back-EMF is 0.02 × 300 = 6 V. The net voltage is 12 − 6 = 6 V, so the current is **6 A**. At 575 rad/s, back-EMF is 11.5 V, so the current is only 0.5 A.

That is the whole idea: faster spin means more back-EMF and less current. The rest of this page shows why $K_e$ equals the torque constant, and how back-EMF is used for sensorless speed measurement and regenerative braking.
:::

1. A conductor moving in a magnetic field generates a voltage, and in a motor it **opposes** the supply (Lenz's law).
2. You know the relation $e = K_e\omega$, that $K_e = K_t$ in SI, and how back-EMF limits current and speed.
3. You can use it: it explains why current falls with speed, sets the no-load speed, provides sensorless speed measurement and regenerative braking, and is **not the same as flyback voltage**.
:::

::: why
Back-EMF is why a motor is not a resistor. It determines the speed-current-torque relationships, the supply voltage needed for a target speed, the efficiency, how sensorless BLDC controllers know where the rotor is, and how braking recovers energy. In an interview it is the word that separates people who have used motors from people who have read about them. It also pairs with flyback voltage ([[q0340]]), the other "inductive voltage", and mixing the two up is a classic mistake.
:::

::: concepts
- **Faraday's law**: induced voltage = rate of change of flux linkage. See [[q0334]].
- **Lenz's law**: the induced voltage opposes the change that caused it, so back-EMF opposes the supply.
- **Motor equations**: $V = IR + L\,di/dt + K_e\omega$, $\tau = K_tI$. See [[q0336]].
- **Power balance**: $e\,I = \tau\omega$ (electrical to mechanical).
- **Generator mode**: if $e > V$ the motor delivers power. See [[q0337]].
:::

::: answer
"Back-EMF is the voltage a motor generates because it's spinning. A motor and a generator are the same device: if you spin a coil in a magnetic field, Faraday's law says it induces a voltage, proportional to the speed. In a motor that voltage opposes the supply voltage, by Lenz's law, which is why it's called 'back'. The relation is e equals K_e times omega, with K_e equal to the torque constant K_t in SI units.

It matters because it limits the motor. The current is the supply voltage minus the back-EMF over the winding resistance. At stall the back-EMF is zero, so the current is big. As the motor speeds up, the back-EMF rises, the current falls, until the torque just balances the load, or until the back-EMF is almost equal to the supply, which sets the no-load speed. So voltage sets speed and current sets torque.

And you can use it: you measure the back-EMF during the PWM off-time to estimate speed without an encoder, and BLDC controllers detect it to know the rotor position. If the shaft is driven faster than the back-EMF equals the supply, the current reverses and the motor brakes, returning energy. It's not the same as flyback voltage, which is the spike from interrupting the coil current."
:::

::: technical
**Origin.** Flux linkage $\lambda(\theta)$ through the rotor winding changes as it rotates; Faraday's law gives $e = d\lambda/dt = \dfrac{d\lambda}{d\theta}\,\omega$. For a PM DC motor the average over a commutated winding is $e = K_e\omega$. Units: $K_e$ in V·s/rad (= V/(rad/s)); commonly quoted in V/krpm: $K_e[\text{V/krpm}] = K_e[\text{V·s/rad}]\times1000\times2\pi/60 = 104.7\,K_e$. Example: $K_e = 0.02$ V·s/rad is $2.09$ V/krpm, i.e. 477 rpm per volt. A BLDC motor rated 1000 rpm/V ($K_v$) has $K_e = 1/(1000\times2\pi/60) = 0.00955$ V·s/rad (phase-to-phase conventions vary).

**Circuit.** $V = IR + L\,\dot I + K_e\omega$. In steady state $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$ and $\omega = (V - IR)/K_e$.

**Energy.** $P_{elec\to mech} = eI = K_e\omega I = \tau\omega$, hence $K_e = K_t$ (SI). The $IR$ drop is copper loss. At $\omega = V/K_e$ the motor draws zero current (ideal no-load speed).

**Numbers.** 12 V, $R = 1\ \Omega$, $K_e = 0.02$: at 350 rad/s (3340 rpm), $e = 7$ V and $I = 5$ A; at 575 rad/s, $e = 11.5$ V and $I = 0.5$ A. Spun by hand at 3000 rpm (314 rad/s) as a generator, it produces $0.02\times314 = 6.3$ V open circuit.

**Uses.** Speed estimate $\omega \approx (V_{term} - IR)/K_e$ or measured directly at the terminals when $I = 0$ (during PWM off with the bridge floating); BLDC zero-crossing sensing; regenerative and dynamic braking (short or resistor across the winding gives torque $\propto \omega$).

**Solenoid.** There is a small motional back-EMF while the plunger moves: $e = i\,\dfrac{dL}{dx}\,v$, which is why the current dips during the stroke ([[q0336]]).

**Not flyback.** Back-EMF is a continuous voltage proportional to speed, present whenever the shaft turns. Flyback is a transient spike $L\,di/dt$ when current is interrupted.
:::

It also gives free speed information for sensorless control and is what makes regenerative braking possible. ::: long
"A motor and a generator are physically the same machine. If I turn the shaft of a motor, the coil moves through the magnetic field, the flux linkage changes, and Faraday's law says a voltage appears across the winding, proportional to the speed: e equals K_e times omega. When the motor is being driven by a supply, that voltage is always present, and by Lenz's law it opposes the applied voltage. That's the back-EMF.

It's what makes a motor behave differently from a resistor. KVL says V equals I R plus L di dt plus K_e omega. In steady state, the current is V minus K_e omega, over R. At stall there's no back-EMF so the current is the full V over R. As the motor accelerates, the back-EMF grows, the voltage left across the winding falls, and the current drops until the torque, K_t times I, matches the load plus friction. If there's no load the speed rises until the back-EMF almost equals the supply. For example, 12 volts with K_e of 0.02 gives an ideal top speed of 600 radians per second, about 5700 rpm.

Power-wise, the electrical power converted to mechanical is e times I, which equals torque times speed, and that's why K_e and K_t are equal in SI units.

In practice, you use it: measure the motor terminal voltage during the off-time of the PWM, when the current is zero, and you read K_e omega directly, so you get speed without an encoder. BLDC controllers detect the back-EMF zero-crossings to find the rotor position. If the load drives the motor faster than the supply speed, the back-EMF exceeds the supply and the current reverses, braking the load and regenerating. And it's not flyback: back-EMF is continuous and speed-proportional; flyback is a spike when you interrupt the coil current."
:::

::: terms
- **Back-EMF (counter-EMF)**: voltage generated by a rotating winding that opposes the applied voltage.
- **Back-EMF constant $K_e$**: V per rad/s; equal to $K_t$ in SI units.
- **Faraday's law**: induced voltage $= d\lambda/dt$.
- **Lenz's law**: induced effects oppose their cause.
- **$K_v$ rating**: rpm per volt of a BLDC motor, the inverse of $K_e$ (in rpm units).
- **Zero-crossing sensing**: BLDC position detection using the back-EMF of the floating phase.
- **Regenerative braking**: using the motor as a generator to return energy.
- **Dynamic braking**: shorting or loading the winding so back-EMF drives a braking current.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **Confusing back-EMF with flyback voltage.** Back-EMF is speed-proportional and continuous; flyback is $L\,di/dt$ at current interruption.
- **"Back-EMF only appears when braking."** It exists whenever the rotor turns, in motoring mode too.
- **"Back-EMF wastes energy."** It is the electromechanical conversion: $eI$ is the mechanical power; only $I^2R$ is loss.
- **Mixing units.** $K_e$ in V/krpm differs from V·s/rad by a factor of about 104.7; $K_e = K_t$ only in SI.
- **Expecting a speed signal at standstill.** Back-EMF is zero at zero speed, so sensorless methods fail at start and low speed.
- **Forgetting that a high-speed no-load motor can exceed a supply's tolerance in regeneration**, raising the bus voltage.
:::

::: deep
## Faraday in a rotating coil

A rotor winding with $N$ turns of area $A$ turning in uniform field $B$ at angular speed $\omega$ links flux $\lambda = NBA\cos\theta$, so

$$e(t) = -\frac{d\lambda}{dt} = NBA\,\omega\sin\theta$$

A commutator picks off the winding at the right instant for each coil, rectifying these sinusoids into a nearly constant DC voltage proportional to $\omega$: $e = K_e\omega$ with $K_e = NBA$ (effective). Compare the torque on the same loop with the same angle convention, $\tau = NBAI\sin\theta$, commutated to $K_tI$: the same geometric constant, so $K_e = K_t$ in SI units. The sign in Faraday's law is Lenz's law: the induced voltage opposes the change in flux, i.e. opposes the current that is driving the rotor.

## The voltage budget of a running motor

KVL gives the picture: the supply voltage is split between the back-EMF (the part that does mechanical work) and the resistive drop (the part that makes heat).

$I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$ then gives $\omega = (V - IR)/K_e$, which says: for a given current (torque) the speed is proportional to the supply voltage minus the $IR$ drop. That is why PWM duty cycle controls speed.

Check by hand: spin the shaft at about 3000 rpm and an open-circuit voltmeter shows about 6.3 V. That's the quickest bench test of $K_e$ (with a drill and a scope, or a known-speed source).

## Back-EMF as a sensor

When the drive bridge floats the winding (current zero, for example during the off-time of PWM or the unpowered phase of a BLDC), the terminal voltage equals the back-EMF. Sample it with an ADC, maybe through a divider and RC filter, and $\omega = V_{emf}/K_e$ gives speed without an encoder. With current flowing, $V_{term} = K_e\omega + IR + L\,di/dt$, so you must know $I$ and $R$ (and $R$ changes with temperature, +0.39%/K).

## Braking and regeneration

If the load pushes the shaft above $V/K_e$, $e > V$ and $I < 0$: the motor is a generator ([[q0337]]). Shorting the winding (dynamic braking) gives $I = K_e\omega/R$ and braking torque $\tau = K_t K_e\omega/R$, proportional to speed; at 350 rad/s on the example motor that's $0.02\times0.02\times350/1 = 0.14$ N·m of braking torque and 49 W dissipated in the winding (7 A through 1 Ω). Regeneration into a battery recovers part of the kinetic energy.

## Back-EMF vs flyback voltage

| | Back-EMF | Flyback voltage |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | Rotation in a field ($d\lambda/dt$ from motion) | Interrupted current ($L\,di/dt$) |
| Time behaviour | Continuous, proportional to speed | Short spike at turn-off |
| Size | At most about the supply (normal) | Can be hundreds of volts unclamped |
| Effect | Limits current and speed; enables sensing | Damages switches; needs clamp |
| Present at standstill? | No | Yes (if current is interrupted) |

See [[q0340]] for flyback. During a PWM-off interval you actually see both: a brief flyback transient as the current decays through the diode, followed by the back-EMF plateau.

## Worked example: how wrong can a current-compensated speed estimate be?

Suppose you estimate speed while the bridge is driving, using $\omega \approx (V_{term} - IR)/K_e$. The firmware stores the cold resistance $R = 1.00\ \Omega$. It reads $V_{term} = 12.0$ V and $I = 5$ A, so it computes $E = 12 - 5 = 7$ V and reports 350 rad/s. But the winding has warmed by 60 K, and copper resistance rises by about 0.39% per kelvin, so the real $R$ is $1.236\ \Omega$. The true drop is $5\times1.236 = 6.18$ V, the true back-EMF is $5.82$ V, and the true speed is $5.82/0.02 = 291$ rad/s. The estimate is 20% too high, from resistance drift alone. Even a 0.2 Ω error in $R$ at 5 A shifts the estimate by 1 V, about 14% of a 7 V back-EMF. This is the main reason sensorless speed measurement is done during an off-time with zero winding current: the $IR$ term vanishes and the temperature dependence goes with it. The price is that the measurement window must be long enough for the flyback current to decay (a few $L/R$) and for the ADC to settle, so you give up a slice of the maximum duty cycle.

## Reading $K_e$ for a brushless motor

A BLDC motor is quoted in $K_v$, rpm per volt. A motor rated at $K_v = 1000$ rpm/V has $K_e = 1/(1000\times2\pi/60) = 0.00955$ V·s/rad. That value is only meaningful once you know what is being measured. If the vendor means line-to-line peak, a wye-connected motor has a phase-to-neutral peak of $K_e/\sqrt{3} = 0.00551$ V·s/rad; if they mean RMS, the numbers shift again. At 10,000 rpm (1047 rad/s) the line-to-line peak is 10 V. With 7 pole pairs, the electrical frequency is $7\times10{,}000/60 = 1{,}167$ Hz, which is the frequency a zero-crossing detector has to track. Because the voltage is a ripple-free trapezoid only in an ideal motor, real back-EMF waveforms also contain harmonics that shift the apparent crossing point.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Whether a published $K_v$ or $K_e$ is line-to-line, phase, peak, or RMS is a vendor convention. Verify it by spinning the motor and measuring on a scope before you put it into a control loop.

## Conditioning the signal for the ADC

A 12 V rail cannot go straight into a 3.3 V converter. A divider of 27 kΩ and 10 kΩ scales 11.5 V (the no-load back-EMF) to 3.1 V, leaving room before the rail, and its Thevenin resistance of 7.3 kΩ pairs with the converter's input capacitance and any filter capacitor. During a PWM drive the terminal sits at either the rail or ground while the bridge conducts, so the signal you want exists only in the off-window, and the filter time constant must be short compared with that window yet long enough to suppress commutation noise. Brush noise on a brushed motor is broadband and spiky, so oversample and take a median rather than a mean.

## Limiting cases and where the model drifts

- **Brush drop.** A brushed motor loses a roughly constant voltage across the brush contact, which acts as a small offset in the speed estimate and shows up as a non-zero intercept when you plot terminal voltage against speed at light load.
- **Magnet temperature.** Neodymium magnets lose flux as they heat, so $K_e$ falls slightly with temperature (vendor-dependent) and the same terminal voltage gives a higher speed.
- **Armature reaction.** At high current the winding's own field distorts the main field, lowering the effective $K_e$ and $K_t$ a little under heavy load.
- **Very low speed.** The back-EMF is smaller than comparator offsets and ADC noise, which is why sensorless starts blind.

## Verifying on the bench

Do the open-circuit spin test first. Couple the shaft to a drill, an encoder or a second motor and record terminal voltage against speed at three speeds. A straight line through the origin with slope $K_e$ confirms the model; a line with an offset indicates brush drop or a bad ground. Then compare $K_e$ to $K_t$ measured with a torque arm and a known current: they must agree within a few percent in SI. Finally, check the ripple on the terminal voltage during off-time with a scope and compare the sampled value with the average. A voltage that does not settle to a flat plateau means your wait time after turn-off is too short.

::: followups
### How can you measure K_e of an unknown motor?
Spin it at a known speed (with a drill or another motor and a tachometer or encoder) with the terminals open and measure the voltage: $K_e = V/\omega$. Alternatively run it at no load on a known voltage and measure speed: $K_e \approx (V - I_{nl}R)/\omega$.

### Why does the back-EMF zero out the current at no load?
As $\omega$ rises, $K_e\omega$ approaches $V$, so the voltage across $R$ and $L$ goes to zero and so does the current. Friction means the speed settles slightly below $V/K_e$ where just enough current flows to balance it.

### How do BLDC controllers use back-EMF?
In a six-step drive, one phase is left floating at any time. Its voltage crosses the midpoint (zero-crossing) at a known rotor angle; the controller times the next commutation from that. It gives position without Hall sensors but needs enough speed to see a signal, so the motor starts open-loop.

### Why does a 24 V motor draw less current than a 12 V version for the same power?
$P = VI$, so for the same power the 24 V motor draws half the current. It achieves that with more turns of thinner wire (higher $R$ and higher $K_e$, so the same speed needs 24 V of back-EMF).

### Scenario: your motor drives a vehicle down a hill and the supply voltage spikes. Why, and what do you do?
The vehicle drives the motor above $V/K_e$, so $e > V$ and the motor regenerates into the supply. If the supply can't absorb the current (a diode-fed rail, a fully charged battery or one with a BMS that blocks charging), the bus voltage rises. Fix: allow regeneration into a battery that can accept it, use a brake chopper/dump resistor, or limit regen.

### Does a solenoid have back-EMF?
Only a small motional one: while the plunger moves, $e = i\,(dL/dx)\,v$, which causes the current dip during the stroke. There is no speed-proportional back-EMF in the motor sense, since the plunger doesn't rotate continuously.
:::
