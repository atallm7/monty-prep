::: testing
Whether you understand that a regulator **has to measure what it is regulating**, and how that measurement is done in practice. The interviewer is looking for:

1. A resistor divider from $V_{out}$ to a feedback (FB) pin, compared with an internal reference by an error amplifier.
2. The equation $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$ and *why* the divider is needed (the reference is much lower than $V_{out}$, and the divider sets the output voltage).
3. Awareness of the extras: a feed-forward capacitor across $R_1$, compensation around the error amplifier, and where to take the sense point (at the load, not at the IC).
:::

::: why
Every closed-loop regulator needs an accurate, low-noise measurement of its output. The divider ratio sets the output voltage and its tolerance sets your rail accuracy, the FB node is the most noise-sensitive node on the board, and its impedance level interacts with the loop stability ([[q0224]]). Getting a rail from 3.3 V to 3.0 V on a prototype is a two-minute resistor swap, so you will be asked about this on day one of a real job.
:::

::: concepts
- **Voltage divider**: $V_{FB}=V_{out}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$, scaled down to the reference level. See [[q0008]].
- **Error amplifier**: compares $V_{FB}$ with $V_{ref}$ and produces a control voltage $V_c$ that sets duty cycle (or the current-mode peak setpoint). See [[q0227]].
- **Reference voltage**: a bandgap (typically 0.5 to 1.25 V on many ICs) that sets the accuracy.
- **Negative feedback**: the loop forces $V_{FB}\to V_{ref}$, which pins $V_{out}$. See [[q0176]] for where the switch fits in.
- **Feed-forward capacitor**: $C_{ff}$ across $R_1$ adds a zero-pole pair for phase boost. See [[q0250]].
- **Remote (Kelvin) sensing**: sensing at the load so that PCB resistance does not shift the rail.
:::

::: answer
"The standard way is a resistor divider from the output to the feedback pin of the controller. The IC has an internal reference, say 0.6 V, and an error amplifier that compares the feedback pin to that reference. The loop adjusts the duty cycle until the feedback pin sits at exactly the reference, so the output ends up at $V_{ref}\cdot(1+R_1/R_2)$, where $R_1$ is the top resistor and $R_2$ the bottom.

The divider is needed for two reasons. First, the reference is low, around 0.6 to 1 V, and the output is usually higher, so I have to scale it down to compare. Second, it's how you program the output voltage with two resistors, rather than needing a custom IC for every rail.

In a real design there's more around it. There's usually a small feed-forward capacitor across the top resistor to add phase boost and speed up the loop. The error amplifier has its own compensation network, either internal or external on a COMP pin. And I'd connect the top of the divider at the load or right at the output capacitor with a Kelvin trace, away from the switch node, so it senses the real output and doesn't pick up noise."
:::

::: technical
**Sensing network.** $V_{out}$ → $R_1$ → FB node → $R_2$ → GND. The error amplifier (usually a transconductance or voltage amp) compares $V_{FB}$ to $V_{ref}$. In steady state $V_{FB}=V_{ref}$, so
$$V_{out}=V_{ref}\left(1+\frac{R_1}{R_2}\right),\qquad R_1=R_2\left(\frac{V_{out}}{V_{ref}}-1\right)$$
Example: $V_{ref}=0.6$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $R_2=10$ kΩ gives $R_1=45$ kΩ. With the nearest E96 value (45.3 kΩ) $V_{out}=3.318$ V (+0.5%).

**Accuracy.** Output tolerance ≈ reference tolerance plus a resistor term: with 1% resistors and a 1% reference, the worst case is about +3.2%/-2.1% for this example. Use 0.1% resistors if you need tight accuracy.

**Loop gain.** The divider contributes a gain of $\beta=V_{ref}/V_{out}=0.18$ (about -15 dB) to the loop. Compensation is designed around that.

**Feed-forward capacitor.** $C_{ff}$ across $R_1$ gives $\frac{V_{FB}}{V_{out}}=\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\cdot\frac{1+sR_1C_{ff}}{1+s(R_1\parallel R_2)C_{ff}}$: a zero at $\frac{1}{2\pi R_1C_{ff}}$ and a pole at $\frac{1}{2\pi(R_1\parallel R_2)C_{ff}}$, a factor $(1+R_1/R_2)$ higher.

**Layout.** The FB node is high impedance (kΩ) and must stay away from the switch node and inductor.
:::

::: short
"The output voltage is sensed with a resistor divider into the feedback pin. The error amp compares that pin to an internal reference, usually around 0.6 to 1 V, and the loop adjusts the duty cycle until they match, so $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$. The divider is needed because the reference is lower than the output and because it lets you program the rail with two resistors. There's usually a feed-forward cap across the top resistor and compensation on the error amp for stability."
:::

::: long
"A buck is a closed-loop system, so it has to measure its output. The measuring element is almost always a resistor divider, $R_1$ from the output to the feedback pin and $R_2$ from the pin to ground. Inside the IC there's a reference, commonly 0.6 or 0.8 V, and an error amplifier that compares the feedback pin to it. If the output is too low, the error amp increases the duty cycle, and if it's too high it reduces it, until the feedback pin equals the reference. At that point $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$.

Why the divider? The reference is a bandgap-type voltage that can't scale with the output, and the error amp inputs have a limited common-mode range, so I have to bring the output down into that range. The nice side effect is that two resistors program any output voltage above the reference. For 3.3 V with a 0.6 V reference the ratio $R_1/R_2$ is 4.5, so 45.3 k over 10 k gives 3.318 V.

There's also an AC role. The divider attenuates the loop gain by $V_{ref}/V_{out}$, and a small capacitor across $R_1$, the feed-forward cap, gives a zero and a pole that add phase boost around crossover. On top of that, the error amplifier has a compensation network, a resistor and capacitor to ground or around the amp, to shape the loop.

In practice I'd choose resistors of about 10 k to 100 k at the FB node, 0.1 or 1 percent depending on accuracy, route the sense trace as a Kelvin connection to the point of load, and keep it away from the inductor and switch node."
:::

::: terms
- **Feedback (FB) pin**: the controller input where the divider's midpoint connects. It sits at $V_{ref}$ in regulation.
- **Reference voltage ($V_{ref}$)**: the internal precision voltage (bandgap) that sets accuracy and temperature drift.
- **Error amplifier**: amplifies $V_{ref}-V_{FB}$ to create the control signal.
- **Feed-forward capacitor ($C_{ff}$)**: capacitor across the top divider resistor that provides phase lead.
- **Compensation network**: resistors and capacitors around the error amplifier that set loop shape (Type II/III). See [[q0227]].
- **Kelvin / remote sensing**: sensing the voltage at the load with separate traces to avoid IR drop errors.
- **Loop gain attenuation ($\beta$)**: $R_2/(R_1+R_2)$, how much the divider scales the output in the loop.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a voltage divider" and gives the formula.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws the divider, FB node, reference and error amp, and shows $V_{FB}=V_{ref}$ in regulation.
- Derives $R_1$ for a target (3.3 V from 0.6 V) and snaps to an E96 value, quoting the resulting error.
- Distinguishes the DC role (program $V_{out}$) from the AC role (loop gain attenuation, feed-forward zero).
- Mentions Kelvin sensing and keeping the FB trace away from the switch node.
- Brings up the resistor magnitude trade-off ([[q0224]]) before being asked.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Swapping $R_1$ and $R_2$** in $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$. $R_1$ is the resistor from output to FB, and it is in the numerator.
- **Thinking the divider is "for the load".** It carries only microamps; it is a measurement path, not a power path.
- **Ignoring tolerance.** 1% resistors plus a 1% reference can give about ±2 to 3% on the output, which breaks a 3.3 V ±3% rail.
- **Routing the FB trace next to the inductor or the switch node.** Noise couples into a high-impedance node and shows as jitter or ripple on the output.
- **Forgetting the feedforward capacitor is part of the loop.** Adding $C_{ff}$ moves crossover and phase margin.
- **Sensing at the IC instead of the load** so the rail at the load is lower by the IR drop in the trace.
:::

::: deep
## What is being measured and why it's scaled

The buck's power stage produces $V_{out}\approx D V_{in}$. For a fixed $D$, any change in $V_{in}$, load, or component values shifts the output. A feedback loop fixes this: measure $V_{out}$, compare to a reference, and adjust $D$ until the error is zero.

A reference is a stable, low voltage, typically a bandgap at 0.5 to 1.25 V (values vary by IC; check the datasheet). The error amplifier's inputs work best near that value. A 3.3 V or 12 V output cannot be connected straight to it, so we scale it with a divider. If the reference is 0.6 V and we want 3.3 V:

$$\frac{R_1}{R_2}=\frac{3.3}{0.6}-1=4.5$$

## Choosing resistor values

Start with $R_2$ (the bottom resistor) at a convenient value, such as 10 kΩ, then solve $R_1$:

- $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $V_{ref}=0.6$ V, $R_2=10$ kΩ → $R_1=45.0$ kΩ. The nearest E96 value 45.3 kΩ gives 3.318 V, +0.55%.
- With 1% resistors the divider alone contributes about +1.65% / -1.62% (worst case, R1 and R2 at opposite extremes); add a 1% reference and worst-case is about +3.2% / -2.1% from 3.3 V.
- Divider current is $3.3/(55.3\text{ k})=60$ µA, about 0.2 mW. Negligible at heavy load but meaningful at light load ([[q0224]], [[q0230]]).

Most datasheets give a recommended $R_2$ or a maximum for $R_1\parallel R_2$ ("keep the FB divider impedance below ..."). Check, since the FB pin has input bias current and capacitance.

## The AC picture: divider as part of the loop

The divider scales the output into the loop with a gain $\beta=V_{ref}/V_{out}=0.18$ (-14.8 dB here). The total loop gain is $T(s)=\beta\cdot G_c(s)\cdot G_{vd}(s)\cdot\frac{1}{V_{ramp}}$ (voltage mode) or the equivalent in current mode. Because $\beta$ shrinks as $V_{out}$ grows (a 0.6 V reference gives $\beta=0.5$ at 1.2 V but only 0.05 at 12 V), the same compensation gives different crossover frequencies at different output voltages, which is why datasheets specify compensation values per output voltage.

**Feed-forward capacitor.** With $C_{ff}$ across $R_1$:
$$\frac{V_{FB}}{V_{out}}=\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\cdot\frac{1+sR_1C_{ff}}{1+s\,(R_1\parallel R_2)\,C_{ff}}$$
For $C_{ff}=22$ pF with $R_1=45.3$ kΩ, $R_2=10$ kΩ:

- zero: $1/(2\pi\cdot45.3\text{ k}\cdot22\text{ pF})=160$ kHz
- pole: $1/(2\pi\cdot8.19\text{ k}\cdot22\text{ pF})=883$ kHz

Between the zero and pole the divider has a rising gain with positive phase, up to a maximum lead near $\sqrt{f_zf_p}\approx376$ kHz of $\arcsin\frac{R_1/R_2}{R_1/R_2+2}$. In plain terms, the maximum phase lead is $\arcsin\frac{N-1}{N+1}$ with $N=f_p/f_z=5.53$, about 44°. If your crossover is near 100 to 400 kHz, this boosts phase margin. ([[q0250]] goes deeper.)

## What the sense point should be

The sense line connects to the output capacitor or, better, at the load. With 2 A flowing through 10 mΩ of PCB trace between the IC and the load, the load sits 20 mV below the IC pin; at 3.3 V that's 0.6%. Kelvin-sensing at the load closes the loop around that drop. The trade-off: a long sense line is a long high-impedance antenna, so route it as a quiet pair, and put a small RC or a decoupling cap near the load. Also **never leave the sense open** (a disconnected remote sense makes the regulator drive the output up to its limit). Many ICs add a pull-up or internal tie for that case.

## Other ways to sense the output

- **Direct FB for adjustable-only parts, internal divider for fixed-output parts.** Fixed-output ICs integrate a precision divider, saving two resistors and giving matched tempco.
- **Digital controllers** sense with an ADC and a similar divider.
- **Isolated supplies** sense on the secondary and send the error through an optocoupler or a transformer. The divider is still on the output side.

::: applications
- **Adjustable buck ICs** (for example a 0.6 V reference part with a two-resistor FB divider): program 1.0 to 5 V rails from the same part.
- **Fixed-output regulators** with an internal divider and a "SENSE" pin tied to the load for remote sense.
- **FPGA/CPU core supplies** with remote differential sense (VSENSE+ and VSENSE-) at the die to cancel PCB drop.
- **Dynamic voltage scaling**: an MCU adjusts the output by driving a DAC into the FB node, or switching extra resistors into the divider.
- **LED drivers**: the "feedback" node senses current rather than voltage, with the same comparator structure.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Adjustable-output IC with a single feedback pin.** Fixed-output ICs hide the divider inside, and the answer then focuses on the sense pin.
- **Resistive sensing.** Capacitive dividers or digital sensing exist for special cases.
- **Ideal reference and error amplifier**, no bias current. Real FB-pin current adds an error $I_{FB}R_1$ ([[q0224]]).
- **Voltage-mode or current-mode** control both use this same output sensing path; only what the error amp drives differs ([[q0227]]).
- Reference values quoted (0.6 V, 0.8 V) are common but vary by part.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Typical reference voltage ranges (0.5 to 1.25 V) are a general observation from many datasheets, not a standard. Always use the actual part's value.
:::

::: followups
### Given $V_{ref}=0.8$ V, how would you set a 5 V output?
$R_1/R_2=5/0.8-1=5.25$. With $R_2=10$ kΩ, $R_1=52.5$ kΩ, so the nearest E96 value is 52.3 kΩ, giving $0.8(1+5.23)=4.98$ V (-0.3%). I would also check that $R_1\parallel R_2\approx8.4$ kΩ is within the datasheet's recommended range.

### Why is there a capacitor across the top resistor?
It adds a zero at $1/(2\pi R_1C_{ff})$ and a pole $(1+R_1/R_2)$ higher. Between them, the signal path from output to FB has phase lead, which raises the phase margin and improves the transient response. Too large a $C_{ff}$ moves the zero below crossover and may reduce noise margin or cause peaking at the FB pin.

### What happens if the top resistor $R_1$ opens?
The FB pin is pulled to ground by $R_2$ and sees 0 V, which is below the reference. The error amp thinks the output is far too low and drives the duty cycle to maximum, so $V_{out}$ rises toward $V_{in}$. This is a real failure mode; good designs add overvoltage protection (OVP) on the FB pin or on a separate sense.

### If I need to change the output from 3.3 V to 3.0 V on a prototype, what do I do?
Keep $R_2$ and recompute $R_1=R_2(3.0/0.6-1)=40$ kΩ (standard 40.2 kΩ gives 3.01 V). Then re-check loop stability, since $\beta$ changed from 0.18 to 0.2, and the load/inductor ripple changes with the lower $V_{out}$.

### Your output reads 3.3 V at the IC but 3.2 V at the FPGA. What would you do?
There is a 100 mV drop in the path from the IC to the load, for example 2 A through 50 mΩ. Move the sense point to the FPGA with a Kelvin trace so the loop regulates the voltage at the load, or reduce the path resistance with wider copper/more vias. Remote sense also compensates connector resistance.
:::

::: interpretations
"What network is typically used" most directly means the resistor divider. Some interviewers also mean the **compensation network** around the error amplifier (a resistor and capacitors, Type II or III) which shapes loop gain. I cover the divider as the sensing network and describe $C_{ff}$ and the compensation network as the AC part of the same feedback path; [[q0227]] and [[q0250]] go into detail.
:::
