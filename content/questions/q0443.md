::: basics
### In plain words
A sense resistor is a small, precise resistor placed in series with the load, so all the load current flows through it. By Ohm's law the voltage across it is the current times its resistance. Because you know the resistance, measuring that voltage tells you the current. The voltage is tiny, so an amplifier boosts it and an ADC turns it into a number the firmware can use.

### Picture it
Picture a short, slightly narrow section in a long water pipe. All the water has to pass through it, and the pressure drops across it in proportion to the flow. Read the pressure drop and you know the flow. The narrow section must stay only slightly narrow, or it would choke the pipe and waste energy. The analogy leaves out one trap: you must read the pressure right at the two ends of the narrow section, not somewhere further along the pipe.

### Words you need
- **Shunt (sense resistor)**: a low-value resistor in series with the load used to turn current into voltage. See [[q0001]].
- **Sense voltage**: the voltage across the shunt, $V_s = I R_s$.
- **Current sense amplifier**: an amplifier that boosts the small difference across the shunt. See [[q0449]].
- **Kelvin connection**: attaching the sense wires right at the resistor's pads so only the resistor is measured. See [[q0557]].
- **ADC**: the converter that turns the amplified voltage into a digital number.

### The simplest example
Let 5 A flow through a 10 mΩ resistor. The voltage across it is $5 \times 0.01 = 50$ mV, and it wastes $5^2 \times 0.01 = 0.25$ W. A current sense amplifier with a gain of 50 turns that into 2.5 V, which fits a 3.3 V ADC. Firmware reverses the steps: divide by 50, then by 0.01, to get back 5 A. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers why the resistor must be small, how high-side and low-side placement differ, and the real-world errors: temperature drift, inductance, offset and layout.
:::

::: why
The sense resistor ("shunt") is the default current sensor on most boards: battery gauges, motor drivers, buck controllers, hot-swap circuits, USB power monitors. Understanding exactly how it works, including what you measure, where, and what it costs, is the base for everything else in the section: sizing ([[q0445]], [[q0446]]), high vs low side ([[q0447]]), amplifiers ([[q0449]]), and layout ([[q0557]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Ohm's law**: $V = IR$. A known resistance converts current to voltage. See [[q0001]].
- **Series current**: the same current flows through every element in a series path, so a resistor in the load path carries the full load current. See [[q0004]].
- **Power dissipation**: $P = I^2R$ heats the resistor and wastes energy.
- **Differential measurement**: the signal is the *difference* between the two resistor terminals, which may both sit at a high voltage. See [[q0115]].
- **Signal conditioning**: the millivolt signal is amplified and offset to fit an ADC. See [[q0449]].
- **Kelvin (4-wire) sensing**: sense wires attach at the resistor's own pads so lead and trace resistance aren't included. See [[q0557]].
:::

::: technical
**Principle.** For a series resistor $R_s$ in the load path, $V_s = V_+ - V_- = I R_s$ exactly (if $R_s$ is the only resistance between the sense points). Then
$$I = \frac{V_s}{R_s}$$

**Chain.** $I \rightarrow V_s = IR_s \rightarrow$ CSA with gain $G$: $V_{out} = G I R_s (+ V_{ref}) \rightarrow$ ADC code $= \frac{V_{out}}{V_{FS}}2^N$. Inverting: $I = \frac{V_{out}}{G R_s}$.

**Numbers.** $I = 5$ A, $R_s = 10$ mΩ: $V_s = 50$ mV, $P = 0.25$ W. With $G = 50$: $V_{out} = 2.5$ V on a 3.3 V 12-bit ADC (LSB 0.806 mV), so each count is $0.806\,\text{mV}/(50\times 0.01) = 1.6$ mA.

**Error budget.** Total current error is roughly: resistor tolerance (0.5 to 1 percent) + resistor tempco × ΔT (50 ppm/°C × 80 °C ≈ 0.4 percent) + CSA gain error (0.1 to 1 percent) + offset $V_{os}/R_s$ (100 µV/10 mΩ = 10 mA) + ADC errors.

**Heating feedback.** Dissipation raises the resistor's temperature, which shifts $R_s$ by its TCR; a 1 W dissipation in a part with 50 K/W rise adds 50 °C, so at 50 ppm/°C that's 0.25 percent.

**Connection.** A 4-terminal (Kelvin) shunt or Kelvin pad routing makes the sensed voltage exclude the high-current solder joints and traces, whose resistance (0.1 to a few mΩ) can be a significant fraction of a 5 mΩ shunt.
:::

It's small to limit loss, a few milliohms, and I connect the sense lines directly to its pads, Kelvin style, so only the resistor is in the measurement."

::: long
"A sense resistor, or shunt, is just Ohm's law used on purpose. I put a small, accurate resistor in series with the load so the entire load current flows through it. Then the voltage across it is the current times the resistance, and since I know the resistance, measuring the voltage gives me the current.

The resistor has to be small, because it adds a voltage drop and wastes power. For example, with 5 amps through 10 milliohms I get 50 millivolts and $I^2R$ is a quarter of a watt. That's a good compromise, but 50 millivolts is far too small for a typical ADC, whose range is volts, and its resolution would be poor. So a current sense amplifier takes the difference between the two ends of the resistor, multiplies it by a gain, say 50, and presents a ground-referenced voltage, maybe offset by a reference for bidirectional current. The ADC converts, and in firmware I divide by gain and by resistance.

The device can go on the low side, between the load and ground, or on the high side, between supply and load. High side needs an amplifier that handles a common-mode voltage near the supply.

Practical points: use a proper low-TCR metal-strip resistor with adequate power rating, route the sense lines with a Kelvin connection to the resistor pads so the solder joints and high-current trace aren't part of the measurement, and remember amplifier offset puts a floor on the smallest current I can read, equal to offset voltage divided by resistance."
:::

::: terms
- **Shunt / sense resistor**: low-value resistor in series with the load used to convert current to voltage.
- **Sense voltage ($V_s$)**: the differential voltage $IR_s$ across the shunt.
- **Burden voltage**: the voltage the sensing element steals from the load.
- **Kelvin connection**: separate force (high current) and sense paths, so the sense lines see only the resistor.
- **TCR**: temperature coefficient of resistance (ppm/°C); metal-strip parts are 20 to 100 ppm/°C.
- **Current sense amplifier (CSA)**: amplifier designed for small differential signals on a large common-mode voltage.
- **Full-scale current**: the maximum current mapped to the ADC's top code.
- **LSB**: one ADC step; smallest voltage (and thus current) change resolved.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it's a resistor in series, you measure the voltage across it, $V=IR$".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Walks the whole chain: $I \to V_s \to$ CSA gain $\to$ ADC code and back, with real numbers.
- Explains *why it must be small* (drop and $I^2R$) and *why that makes the signal tiny* (millivolts, hence amplifier, offset limits).
- Mentions Kelvin sensing and tempco/self-heating as accuracy limits.
- Distinguishes high-side and low-side placement and the common-mode consequence.
- States the low-current floor $I_{min} \approx V_{os}/R_s$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Measuring across the wrong points.** Probing the resistor's far-end traces includes trace resistance and gives a wrong reading. Tap at the resistor pads.
- **Choosing a large resistor "for a bigger signal".** It wastes power and drops the load voltage. A bigger signal is the amplifier's job.
- **Ignoring power rating.** A 0.1 W 0603 resistor cannot carry 5 A at 10 mΩ (0.25 W) reliably.
- **Treating it as a perfect resistor.** TCR, self-heating, ESL at high $dI/dt$ and thermal EMFs all matter at the percent level.
- **Putting an ordinary op-amp in a high-side measurement** without checking its common-mode range.
- **Forgetting the sign/direction.** A single-ended output can't show negative current unless offset with a reference.
:::

::: deep
## First principles

Current is invisible to a voltmeter, but a resistor converts it. For two terminals of a resistor $R_s$ with current $I$ flowing from $a$ to $b$:

$$V_a - V_b = I R_s$$

Because the shunt is in *series* with the load, KCL ([[q0004]]) guarantees the load current equals the shunt current. Nothing else is needed: the shunt is an ammeter with a very small internal resistance (the bench ammeter works the same way: shunt plus meter).

## Size matters: signal versus loss

Pick $R_s$ for a 5 A full-scale load:

| $R_s$ | $V_s$ at 5 A | $P$ at 5 A | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 mΩ | 5 mV | 25 mW | tiny signal, offset dominates |
| 10 mΩ | 50 mV | 250 mW | typical balance |
| 100 mΩ | 500 mV | 2.5 W | needs a heavy part; drops 0.5 V from the load |

A larger resistor gives more signal and less relative offset error but costs power and headroom; a smaller one does the opposite. Choosing is covered in [[q0445]] and [[q0446]].

## What is the amplifier doing?

The sense voltage rides on whatever voltage the load sits at. On the low side the bottom of the shunt is ground (or within millivolts of it), so a simple op-amp can read it, as long as its input range includes ground. On the high side both terminals sit near the supply, so you need a difference amplifier with a common-mode range up to the bus ([[q0447]], [[q0449]]).

The output of a unidirectional CSA is $V_{out} = G\,I\,R_s$; for bidirectional current a reference voltage is added, $V_{out} = V_{ref} + G\,I\,R_s$, so zero current sits at mid-scale.

## Seeing the burden

An ammeter uses the same principle and it has the same side effect: the voltage the meter drops from the circuit is its *burden voltage*. A shunt in your design behaves the same way, so the load sees $V_{bat} - I R_s$.

## Real hardware behavior

- **Self-heating and TCR.** $R(T) = R_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]$. A shunt at 1 W with 60 K/W rise heats 60 °C; with 50 ppm/°C that's 0.3 percent gain error.
- **Inductance.** A 5 nH ESL has impedance equal to 10 mΩ at 318 kHz, so fast current edges add a $L\,dI/dt$ spike ([[q0444]]).
- **Thermal EMF.** Dissimilar metals at the shunt's solder joints create µV-level voltages when the two ends are at different temperatures, which matters at low currents.
- **Kelvin routing.** Two traces from the resistor pads' inner edges to the amplifier, routed as a tight differential pair. Details in [[q0557]].
- **Trace resistance.** 1 oz copper is about 0.5 mΩ per square; 20 mm of 1 mm trace is 10 mΩ, the same size as the shunt itself. That's why you never tap the sense line from the load trace.

> [!TIP]
> In an interview, say the chain out loud: "current to millivolts to amplified volts to ADC code, and back." It shows you see the system, not just the resistor.

## Worked example: a complete 10 A shunt chain and its error budget

Design a measurement of 0 to 10 A on a motor supply with a 3.3 V, 12-bit ADC. Choose $R_s = 5$ mΩ. Then:

- Full-scale sense voltage: $10\times0.005 = 50$ mV.
- Dissipation: $10^2\times0.005 = 0.5$ W, and the voltage lost to the load is 50 mV.
- Gain 50 maps 50 mV to 2.5 V, leaving 0.8 V of headroom on the 3.3 V converter.
- One ADC LSB is $3.3/4096 = 0.806$ mV, which corresponds to $0.806\text{ mV}/(50\times0.005) = 3.2$ mA of current.

Now put real-world errors into amps. The amplifier's 100 µV input offset corresponds to $100\ \mu\text{V}/5\text{ m}\Omega = 20$ mA, independent of the current. For gain: assume a 1 % resistor, a 0.5 % amplifier gain error, and a 75 ppm/K temperature coefficient. The shunt dissipates 0.5 W and, with an assumed 40 K/W from the pad to ambient, heats by 20 K, so the TCR contributes $20\times75\ \text{ppm} = 0.15\ \%$. Added in the worst case, gain errors are $1+0.15+0.5 = 1.65\ \%$. At the top of the range that is 165 mA, and the 20 mA offset is minor. Down at 0.2 A the picture reverses: the gain error is only 3.3 mA while the offset is still 20 mA, so the reading is uncertain by about 23 mA, or roughly 12 % of the reading. The same shunt is a good 2 % meter at 10 A and a poor 12 % meter at 200 mA. A two-point calibration (zero current plus one known current) removes the resistor tolerance and most of the offset, leaving temperature drift and noise.

## Dynamic behavior: the shunt is an inductor too

A real power shunt has a few nanohenries of series inductance. Model it as $R_s$ in series with $L = 5$ nH. The resulting time constant is $L/R_s = 5\text{ nH}/5\text{ m}\Omega = 1\ \mu$s, equivalent to a 160 kHz corner. During a fast current step, the voltage across the shunt is $IR_s + L\,dI/dt$. A 10 A step in 1 µs (as a switching edge in a motor bridge can easily do) adds $5\text{ nH}\times10\text{ A}/1\ \mu\text{s} = 50$ mV on top of the 50 mV of resistive drop: the sense voltage overshoots to double its steady-state value for an instant. This is why current-sense signals are blanked during switching edges, sampled in the flat part of the waveform, or low-pass filtered. It also shows that physically larger or thinner shunts are not interchangeable: metal-strip and reverse-geometry parts are sold with very low inductance, and the datasheet usually quotes it.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Inductance and thermal resistance depend on the exact package and land pattern. The 5 nH and 40 K/W figures are typical-order values used to show the method; use the datasheet and your layout for design.

## Limiting cases of the model

- **$R_s \to 0$:** signal vanishes into offset and noise. You can't make the burden arbitrarily small, because the amplifier offset divided by $R_s$ becomes the smallest resolvable current.
- **$R_s$ large:** the load's supply sags by $IR_s$, the part overheats, and in a low-side arrangement the load's ground lifts above the system ground, shifting logic thresholds.
- **High frequency:** the L/R behavior above, plus skin effect in thick copper strips, make the shunt a frequency-dependent impedance rather than a pure resistance.
- **Very low current:** thermal EMFs of a few microvolts at solder joints become comparable to the signal.

## Verifying on the bench

- **Check the Kelvin connection:** pass a known DC current (from a supply in current-limit mode, monitored with a trusted meter) and compare the voltage at the amplifier pins to the voltage across the shunt's pads, measured with a DMM. If the former is larger, the sense traces share copper with the high-current path and are measuring trace drop too.
- **Gain and offset:** record the amplifier output at zero current and at two or three known currents. A straight-line fit gives gain and offset directly.
- **Temperature drift:** take the same reading while the shunt is at 25 °C and after warming it with a heat gun, keeping the amplifier cool. A change of 0.15 % per 20 K is the TCR; a larger shift points to thermal EMF or amplifier drift.
- **Step response:** apply a fast current edge (a switched resistive load) and compare the amplifier output with a current probe on the same wire. The L/R overshoot appears as a spike at each edge.

- **Battery fuel gauges**: 5 to 20 mΩ shunt with a coulomb counter.
- **Brushless motor drives**: phase or low-leg shunts of 1 to 10 mΩ for torque control.
- **USB power monitors and PD controllers**: high-side shunt with a digital power monitor.
- **Buck/boost controllers**: external sense resistor for accurate current limiting.
- **Bench power supplies**: output current readback through a precision shunt.
- **Hot-swap controllers**: milliohm shunt with a fast comparator to limit inrush.

::: followups
### Why not use a large resistor to get a big signal?
Because the drop $IR_s$ comes out of the load's supply and the dissipation $I^2R_s$ is wasted and must be dissipated. At 5 A, a 1 Ω resistor would drop 5 V and burn 25 W. Use a small resistor and amplify.

### Where do you connect the sense lines and why?
Directly on the resistor's pads (inner side, Kelvin), not on the high-current trace. Otherwise the sense voltage includes the resistance of the copper and solder between the point you tapped and the resistor, which can be several mΩ and temperature-dependent.

### How small a current can you measure with a 10 mΩ shunt and a CSA with 100 µV offset?
The offset is equivalent to $V_{os}/R_s = 10$ mA. You can measure currents below that only after calibrating the zero. Above it you get percent-level accuracy from about 100 mA upward.

### Scenario: your measured current is 3 percent higher than a bench meter at full load but correct at light load. What would you check?
Gain-type error that grows with power: shunt self-heating (TCR), a thermal gradient, or amplifier gain error. A shunt dissipating 1 W with 100 ppm/°C and 80 °C rise gives 0.8 percent, not 3 percent. So also check that the sense traces are attached at the resistor pads and not partly on the high-current path, where copper's +0.39 percent per °C tempco adds error.

### What if the load current is AC or very fast?
Then the resistor's inductance, the amplifier's bandwidth and the layout loop area matter. Use a low-inductance (reverse-geometry or metal-strip) resistor, a wide-bandwidth amplifier, and keep the sense loop small and twisted to avoid picking up $L\,dI/dt$ and magnetic coupling.
