::: testing
Whether you can **compare two spectra** and say precisely what changes when the edges are slowed: the fundamental and low harmonics barely move, the high harmonics drop dramatically, the roll-off steepens from $-20$ to $-40$ dB/decade above a knee near $1/(\pi t_r)$, and nulls appear at multiples of $1/t_r$. A good answer also shows the time-domain / frequency-domain duality: smooth edges mean less high-frequency content, and the cost is longer transition time and less timing margin. The interviewer wants numbers and the underlying math (sinc factor), not just "slow edges have fewer harmonics".
:::

::: why
This is the practical heart of EMI and bandwidth planning. If you can state how much quieter a slower edge is at a given frequency, you can decide whether a 22 Ω series resistor will fix an emissions failure, or whether a driver's slew-rate setting is worth the timing hit. It is also how you sanity-check a spectrum on an analyzer: where the knee is, whether the slope past it is $-40$ dB/decade, whether nulls fall where $1/t_r$ predicts.
:::

::: concepts
- **Trapezoid spectrum** $=$ ideal spectrum $\times\,\text{sinc}(ft_r)$ ([[q0673]]).
- **Knee frequency** $\approx1/(\pi t_r)$ (linear ramp), or $0.5/t_{r,10\text{-}90}$ as a conservative marker ([[q0676]]).
- **Roll-off slopes**: $-20$ dB/decade ($1/n$) for ideal and below the knee, $-40$ dB/decade ($1/n^2$) above.
- **Time/frequency duality**: smoother in time (continuous derivatives) means faster decay in frequency.
- **dB arithmetic**: 20 dB per factor of 10 in amplitude, 6 dB per factor of 2.
- **Gibbs ringing** appears when the sharp wave is band-limited ([[q0671]]).
:::

::: answer
"Both have the same fundamental and the same low harmonics. What changes is the high end. A sharp square wave has odd harmonics falling as one over n, so 20 dB per decade, and that continues out to very high frequency. A wave with finite, slower edges follows the same line up to a knee frequency of roughly one over pi times the rise time, then falls at 40 dB per decade, because the edge multiplies the spectrum by a sinc of the rise time.

In numbers, take a 100 MHz clock. With a 100 ps edge the knee is about 3 GHz; with a 1 ns edge it's about 300 MHz, and at 2 GHz the slower edge's harmonic is roughly 15 dB lower (approaching 20 dB further up, once both are past their knees). Slowing the edge ten times cuts high-frequency content by ten times, 20 dB, with almost no effect on the first few harmonics. If the edge takes up half the period, the wave becomes a triangle and the harmonics go as one over n squared right from the start.

The trade-off is that a slower edge has less timing margin and is more affected by noise at the threshold. And if the edge is slowed by a real filter, the shape is smoother with no overshoot, which is also visible as the absence of Gibbs-type ringing."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** A trapezoid of period $T$, 50% duty, equal rise/fall $t_r$ (0-100% linear). Odd harmonic amplitude for a 0-to-$V$ wave:
$$A_n=\frac{2V}{n\pi}\left|\frac{\sin(n\pi t_r/T)}{n\pi t_r/T}\right|.$$
The first factor is the ideal square-wave amplitude; the second is the edge sinc, $\text{sinc}(nf_0t_r)$.

**Comparison**
| | Sharp ($t_r\to0$) | Slow edge ($t_r>0$) |
|---|---|---|
| Fundamental | $2V/\pi$ | slightly lower ($\text{sinc}(f_0t_r)\le1$) |
| Harmonics below $1/(\pi t_r)$ | $\propto1/n$ | essentially the same |
| Harmonics above knee | $\propto1/n$ ($-20$ dB/dec) | $\propto1/n^2$ ($-40$ dB/dec) |
| Nulls | none from edge | at $f=k/t_r$ |
| Time-domain overshoot (band-limited) | Gibbs ringing | smoother, little ringing |

**Numerical examples** (relative to ideal, $20\log_{10}|\text{sinc}(nt_r/T)|$):
- $t_r/T=0.02$: harmonic 15 is $-1.3$ dB, harmonic 31 $-6.4$ dB.
- $t_r/T=0.1$: harmonic 3 is $-1.3$ dB, harmonic 5 $-3.9$ dB, harmonic 15 $-13.5$ dB.
- $t_r/T=0.25$: harmonic 3 is $-10.5$ dB, harmonic 9 $-20$ dB.
- $t_r/T=0.5$ (triangle): harmonic 1 is $-3.9$ dB, harmonic 3 $-13.5$ dB, 9th $-23$ dB.

**Above both knees**: amplitude $\approx\dfrac{2V}{\pi^2n^2(t_r/T)}$, inversely proportional to $t_r$: 10$\times$ slower is $-20$ dB, 2$\times$ slower is $-6$ dB.

**Triangle limit** ($t_r=T/2$): odd harmonics at $4V/(\pi^2n^2)$ peak for a 0-to-$V$ wave (relative to the fundamental, the 3rd is $1/9$). Even harmonics remain zero because of half-wave symmetry.

> [!NOTE]
> Using 10-90% rise times, $t_{lin}=t_{10\text{-}90}/0.8$. The formulas above use the 0-100% linear edge; convert before comparing with datasheet values.
:::

::: short
"Sharp and slow-edged square waves share the same fundamental and low harmonics. The sharp one falls at 20 dB per decade, one over n, all the way up. The slow-edged one follows that until a knee near one over pi times the rise time, then falls at 40 dB per decade because of the extra sinc factor, and it has nulls at multiples of one over the rise time. So high-frequency energy scales inversely with rise time: a 10 times slower edge is 20 dB quieter up high, at the cost of timing margin."
:::

::: long
"Start with what's the same. Both waves are periodic with the same period, so both have harmonics at multiples of the fundamental. The low harmonics are essentially identical: the fundamental and the third and fifth barely notice whether the edges are 50 picoseconds or 500 picoseconds, as long as the edge is short compared to the period.

What changes is how far the energy extends. An ideal sharp square wave has odd harmonics with amplitude proportional to one over n, so 20 dB per decade forever. A real wave with a linear edge of rise time tr has a spectrum equal to the ideal one multiplied by a sinc function of the rise time. Below a knee at about one over pi times tr the sinc is close to one, so the slow wave looks like the sharp one. Above the knee the sinc itself falls like one over frequency, so the total is one over f squared, 40 dB per decade, and the sinc also puts nulls at multiples of one over tr.

An example: a 100 MHz clock. With 1 ns edges the knee is about 300 MHz and the first null is at 1 GHz. With 100 ps edges the knee is about 3 GHz. At 2 GHz the faster wave is roughly 15 dB higher, approaching 20 dB further up. In general the high-frequency amplitude is proportional to one over tr, so ten times slower is 20 dB quieter.

At the extreme, if the edge fills half the period the wave is a triangle, and the harmonics fall as one over n squared from the start. In the time domain smooth edges also remove the Gibbs-like ringing you'd get from band-limiting a sharp edge. The cost is timing margin and sensitivity to noise at the threshold, which is the trade-off I'd weigh against EMI benefits."
:::

::: terms
- **Sharp edge**: transition fast relative to the observation bandwidth; effectively infinite spectrum in the model.
- **Rise time $t_r$**: time for the edge; here 0-100% linear unless noted.
- **Edge sinc**: $\sin(\pi ft_r)/(\pi ft_r)$, the factor a linear edge multiplies onto the spectrum.
- **Knee frequency**: where the envelope steepens, $1/(\pi t_r)$.
- **Roll-off slope**: $-20$ dB/dec for $1/f$, $-40$ dB/dec for $1/f^2$.
- **Null**: frequency at which the sinc equals zero, $f=k/t_r$.
- **Triangle wave**: trapezoid limit with $t_r=T/2$; harmonics $\propto1/n^2$.
- **Gibbs ringing**: overshoot from truncating a sharp edge's spectrum.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "sharp edges have more high-frequency harmonics".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the mechanism: edge $=$ convolution with a ramp, so spectrum $\times$ sinc.
- Gives the three regimes and slopes ($-20$, then $-40$ dB/decade past $1/(\pi t_r)$) and the null positions $k/t_r$.
- Quantifies with a concrete example (100 MHz, 1 ns vs 100 ps) and the "$10\times$ slower is $20$ dB quieter" rule.
- States what does not change (the fundamental and low harmonics) and what the cost is (timing margin, noise sensitivity).
- Mentions the triangle limit and the time-domain view (no Gibbs ringing, smoother derivative).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying slow edges change the fundamental.** They barely do unless the edge is a large fraction of the period.
- **Saying the harmonics "disappear".** They are attenuated, with a steeper slope, not removed.
- **Claiming a slope of $-40$ dB/decade for the whole spectrum.** Below the knee it is still $-20$.
- **Mixing 10-90% and 0-100% rise times** when locating the knee.
- **Confusing rise time and repetition rate.** A slow clock with sharp edges still has high-frequency content.
- **Assuming nulls are deep in practice.** Real edges are not linear ramps; the nulls are shallow dips.
:::

## Physical interpretation

A sharp edge is a sudden event; any sudden event is made of many frequencies in phase. A ramp spread over $t_r$ is like averaging the sudden event over a window of length $t_r$: components whose period is much shorter than $t_r$ average away (that is the sinc), and components with period longer than $t_r$ pass. The window-averaging interpretation also explains nulls: a sinusoid with period exactly $t_r/k$ averages to zero over the window.

The derivative viewpoint is cleaner still. An ideal edge has an impulse for a derivative, whose spectrum is flat. Dividing by $j\omega$ (integrating) gives the $1/f$ spectrum of the step. A ramp edge has a *rectangular* derivative of width $t_r$, with a sinc spectrum that dies away above $1/t_r$; after dividing by $j\omega$ you get $\text{sinc}(ft_r)/f$. A smoother edge, with a derivative that itself is continuous, falls faster still.

## When the edge is part of the story: filters

Real "slow" edges often come from an RC (a series resistor and load capacitance) rather than a linear ramp. The edge is an exponential, $t_{10\text{-}90}=2.2RC$, and the spectrum is the ideal one times the RC's low-pass response $1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}$ with $f_c=0.35/t_{10\text{-}90}$. That response is a clean $-20$ dB/decade above $f_c$, so the combined slope past $f_c$ is $-40$ dB/decade with no nulls. Same trend, different shape: first-order rounded edges have a smooth knee with no deep notches; linear ramps have notches.

## Worked example: is the series resistor worth it?

A 50 MHz clock drives 15 pF with a 10 Ω output impedance, producing about $t_{10\text{-}90}=2.2\times10\times15\text{ pF}=0.33$ ns. Adding 33 Ω: $2.2\times43\times15\text{ pF}=1.4$ ns. The RC corners are $0.35/0.33\text{ ns}=1.06$ GHz and $0.35/1.42\text{ ns}=247$ MHz. At the 9th harmonic (450 MHz), the first-order filters give $1/\sqrt{1+(0.45/1.06)^2}=0.92$ ($-0.7$ dB) and $1/\sqrt{1+(0.45/0.247)^2}=0.48$ ($-6.4$ dB); at the 21st harmonic (1.05 GHz), $0.71$ ($-3$ dB) vs $0.23$ ($-12.8$ dB). The resistor buys about 6 to 10 dB across the radiating harmonics for the cost of about 1 ns more edge time, which must fit the timing budget.

::: applications
- **Choosing driver strength or series resistor** to pass an emissions limit without breaking timing.
- **Reading a spectrum analyzer plot** of a clock: spot the knee and the $-40$ dB/dec region to infer the edge speed.
- **Specifying a scope bandwidth** from the spectrum of a given edge ([[q0676]]).
- **PWM and switching converters**: gate resistors slow the switch node, steepening the spectrum above the knee and trimming conducted/radiated EMI.
- **Audio and instrumentation**: filtered (slow-edge) digital clocks near analog signal paths to avoid spurs.
- **Simulation setup**: set the maximum simulated frequency to a few times the knee based on edge time ([[q0675]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear ramp edges** with equal rise and fall time; RC edges have a similar but smoother high-frequency behaviour.
- **50% duty** for the harmonic list; other duties shift which harmonics exist but not the effect of the edge.
- **Rise time convention**: 0-100% linear in the formulas, 10-90% elsewhere. $t_{10\text{-}90}=0.8\,t_{lin}$ for a linear ramp.
- **Same amplitude** for both waves; slowing the edge does not change swing.
- **Linear, ideal measurement**: instruments with limited bandwidth show a slower edge than reality.

Alternative framing: some texts compare "sharp" and "rounded" using a Gaussian edge, which gives a spectrum falling as $\exp(-f^2)$ with no nulls and no knee, only a smooth rolloff.
:::

::: followups
### A 10 MHz clock has a 5 ns edge and a second one has a 0.5 ns edge. How different are their spectra near 500 MHz?
Take $t_r$ as the 0-100% ramp. At about 500 MHz the slow edge has $ft_r=2.5$, so $|\text{sinc}|=1/(2.5\pi)=0.127$ ($-17.9$ dB); the fast edge has $ft_r=0.25$, $|\text{sinc}|=0.90$ ($-0.9$ dB). The difference is about 17 dB. Well beyond both knees it approaches the full 20 dB ratio of the rise times. At 100 MHz the difference is only about 4 dB (slow edge $-3.9$ dB, fast edge about 0 dB), because the slow edge's knee is at $1/(\pi\cdot5\text{ ns})=64$ MHz.

### How does an RC-rounded edge differ from a linear-ramp edge in the spectrum?
Both fall at $-40$ dB/dec above their knee. The linear ramp has nulls at $k/t_r$; the RC edge has none because its response is a smooth first-order roll-off. Real drivers lie in between.

### What does the triangle wave tell you?
When the rise time equals half the period, harmonic amplitudes go as $1/n^2$ from the start, and the 3rd harmonic is $1/9$ of the fundamental ($-19.1$ dB) instead of $1/3$ ($-9.5$ dB). It is the continuous-with-corners limit; a sine, with all derivatives continuous, has a single line.

### Why do real clocks show strong emissions at a few specific harmonics rather than a smooth roll-off?
Because the envelope is smooth but real structures are resonant: a cable or slot antenna radiates best at its resonant frequencies, parasitic LC circuits ring and add energy there, and the sinc nulls and lobes create peaks and dips. The emission spectrum is the signal spectrum multiplied by the radiating structure's transfer function.

### If slowing the edge by 10$\times$ is 20 dB quieter, why not always do it?
Because it costs timing: the edge consumes more of the unit interval, setup/hold margin shrinks, and noise at the threshold produces more jitter ($v_n t_r/\Delta V$). For power switches it increases switching loss ($\propto t_r$). Choose the slowest edge that meets timing ([[q0677]]).
:::
