::: basics
### In plain words
An open-drain output can only pull a wire down to ground or let go of it. It can never push the wire up. A resistor to the supply, called a pull-up, brings the wire back up when everyone lets go. This sounds like a limit, but it is what lets many chips share one wire safely. If two chips disagree, "low" simply wins and nothing is harmed.

### Picture it
Picture a cord held up by a spring, with several people each holding a handle. Anyone can pull the cord down. Nobody ever pushes it up. When all of them let go, the spring lifts it. Two people pulling down never fight each other. The analogy shows the cost too: a spring lifts slowly, while pulling down is quick. That is why this kind of wire is slow to go high.

### Words you need
- **Open-drain**: an output with only a pull-down switch. It pulls low or lets go. See [[q0785]].
- **Push-pull**: an output with two switches, one that drives high and one that drives low.
- **Pull-up resistor**: the resistor that lifts the wire high when nobody pulls it low. See [[q0755]].
- **Contention**: two outputs driving opposite levels at once, so current flows straight through both.
- **Wired-AND**: a shared wire that is high only if every chip lets go.

### The simplest example
Two chips share a wire on a 3.3 V supply. Suppose both were push-pull, and one drives high while the other drives low. Current flows from supply to ground through both switches. If those switches have about 25 Ω and 20 Ω, that is $3.3/45 \approx 73$ mA. That can damage the chips. Now make both open-drain with a 2.2 kΩ pull-up. The worst case is the pull-up current through a chip pulling low: $3.3/2200 = 1.5$ mA. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows how I2C uses this for its protocol, and what it costs in speed and power.
:::

2. The big benefits: no contention damage, multi-master arbitration, clock stretching and ACK on a shared wire, easy voltage-level mixing, hot-plug and power-off tolerance.
3. The honest costs: slow rising edge, static power, a required resistor.

::: why
Open-drain outputs show up everywhere: I2C, SMBus, interrupt and reset lines, wired-OR alerts, 1-Wire, level shifters. Understanding why they exist lets you choose between push-pull and open-drain in your own designs, debug shared-line problems, and explain the speed limits of I2C in one sentence. It connects MOSFET basics to protocol design.
:::

- **Open-drain vs push-pull**: one NMOS to ground, versus a PMOS/NMOS pair. See [[q0785]] and [[q0780]].
- **Pull-up resistor**: supplies the high. See [[q0755]] and [[q0020]].
- **Wired-AND**: the line is high only if every device releases it.

- **Level translation**: each side pulls up to its own rail. See [[q0877]].

::: answer
"An open-drain output has just one transistor, an NMOS from the pin to ground. It can pull the line low, or it can let go, but it can never drive it high; a pull-up resistor does that. That sounds like a limitation, but it's what makes a shared bus work.

First, there's no contention. If two push-pull drivers disagree, one PMOS and one NMOS are both on, and you get a low-resistance path from supply to ground: tens of milliamps and possibly damage. With open-drain, if two devices disagree, one pulls low and the other lets go, and low just wins. The bus acts as a wired-AND.

That wired-AND is also how I2C does its protocol tricks: multi-master arbitration, where a master that sees a 0 when it sent a 1 knows it lost; clock stretching, where a slave holds SCL low to slow the master; and the ACK, where the slave pulls SDA low during the ninth clock.

It also gives easy voltage mixing, because the high level is set by whatever rail the pull-up goes to, so two voltage domains can share the line with the right shifter. And parts designed for it tolerate hot-plugging and being powered off on a live bus, because there's no PMOS driving high and, if the datasheet says so, no clamp from the pin to their supply.

The cost is speed and power: the rising edge is only as fast as $R_pC_b$, and the pull-up burns current whenever the line is low."
:::

::: technical
**Structure.** Open-drain: NMOS from output to ground, drain at the pin, no PMOS. Logic 0 = NMOS on (low impedance to ground); logic 1 = NMOS off (high-impedance), and the external pull-up supplies the high.

**Wired-AND.** With several open-drain outputs on one net, the line is high only if all outputs are off:

$$V_{line}=\text{HIGH} \iff \prod_i \overline{\text{pull-down}_i}=1 \quad\text{(AND of the "released" states)}$$

**Advantages.**
1. **Contention-safe.** Worst-case fault current is $V_{DD}/R_p$ (e.g. 1.5 mA at 3.3 V and 2.2 kΩ) versus $V_{DD}/(R_{on,P}+R_{on,N})$ for two push-pull drivers (e.g. 3.3/45 Ω = 73 mA).
2. **Multi-driver bus on one wire**: any device can talk, arbitration is "free" from wired-AND, ACK/NACK, clock stretching.
3. **Level flexibility.** The high level is the pull-up rail, so mixed-voltage buses need only a pull-up per domain and a pass-FET or translator ([[q0877]]).
4. **Hot-plug / power-off tolerance.** No PMOS body diode or clamp to $V_{DD}$ at the output (design dependent), so an unpowered device doesn't clamp a live line (check "Ioff" specs).
5. **Wired-OR for interrupts**: many open-drain active-low INT outputs share one line; any can assert it.
6. **Simple, small output stage**: one transistor.

**Costs.**
- Rising edge is passive: $t_r=0.8473R_pC_b$. Falls are fast, rises slow, so speed is limited.
- DC power while low: $V_{DD}(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/R_p$ (about 4 mW per line at 3.3 V, 2.2 kΩ).
- Needs an external resistor, whose value trades speed for sink-current ([[q0755]]).
- Asymmetric edges, so duty cycle and eye are asymmetric at high speed.
- Pull-up current through the driver while low sets $V_{OL}$ ([[q0760]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Whether a given open-drain pin has a clamp diode to $V_{DD}$ is device-dependent. Tolerance of a live bus with the device unpowered (Ioff, "partial power-down") is a datasheet specification, not a property of open-drain in general.
:::

::: short
"Open-drain outputs can only pull low; a pull-up makes the high. That gives a wired-AND, so devices can share a wire without contention: if two disagree, low simply wins, instead of a shoot-through current between a PMOS and an NMOS. It's what enables I2C's arbitration, clock stretching and ACK. It also makes level mixing easy and tolerates hot-plug. The cost is a slow rising edge set by $R_pC_b$ and static current when the line is low."
:::

::: long
"An open-drain output is a single NMOS from the pin to ground. It can pull the line low or it can release it, and when it releases, an external pull-up resistor makes the line high. Compared with push-pull, where there's a PMOS to drive high and an NMOS to drive low, that gives some really useful properties for a shared bus.

The first is safety. On a shared wire, two push-pull drivers can disagree. Then one device's PMOS is on and the other's NMOS is on, so there's a path from supply to ground through two on-resistances, easily tens of milliamps, and it can damage the parts. With open-drain, nobody ever drives high, so the worst case is two devices pulling low together, which is harmless. The most you can pull from the supply is $V_{DD}/R_p$.

The second is that the bus becomes a wired-AND: the line is high only if everybody lets go. That's the mechanism behind a lot of I2C's features. Arbitration works because a master that sends a 1 but reads back a 0 knows another master is pulling low, so it backs off. Clock stretching works because a slave can hold SCL low. The ACK is just the slave pulling SDA low on the ninth clock.

Third, voltage domains. Because the high level is whatever the pull-up goes to, you can mix 1.8 V and 3.3 V sides with a simple shifter, each side with its own pull-up. And it's friendly to hot-plug, since there's no output stage driving high into a device that isn't powered.

The downsides are speed and power. Falls are fast because a transistor discharges the line, but rises are an RC charge through the pull-up, so the edge time is about 0.85 times $R_p$ times $C_b$. And when the line is low, the pull-up burns current. That's why I2C tops out at modest speeds compared with push-pull buses like SPI."
:::

::: terms
- **Open-drain**: output stage with only a pull-down transistor (open-collector for BJTs).
- **Push-pull**: output with both pull-up and pull-down transistors.
- **Wired-AND / wired-OR**: logic created by connecting open-drain outputs to one net.
- **Contention (bus fight)**: two drivers driving opposite levels, causing high current.
- **Arbitration**: multi-master conflict resolution by comparing the driven and the observed level.
- **Clock stretching**: a slave holding SCL low to delay the master.
- **Ioff / partial power-down**: spec that an unpowered device doesn't load or clamp the bus.
- **Pull-up resistor**: sets the high level and the rise time.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Open-drain means it outputs high-Z when high."** Correct physically, but say that the *resistor* supplies the high, and the line's high level equals the pull-up rail.
- **Thinking it's just a cost-saving trick.** The benefits are architectural (shared wires, arbitration).
- **Forgetting the pull-up.** An open-drain line with no pull-up never goes high.
- **Saying open-drain is fast.** It has fast falls and slow rises; that asymmetry is the limit.
- **Assuming any open-drain pin is hot-plug safe.** Some have a body or clamp diode to $V_{DD}$; check the datasheet.
- **Using open-drain for point-to-point high-speed links.** Push-pull is better where only one device drives each wire.
:::

::: deep
## What an open-drain output is

A push-pull output has two transistors: PMOS to $V_{DD}$, NMOS to ground. Exactly one is on at a time, so the output is driven to a rail either way. An open-drain output keeps only the NMOS. The pin is its drain; hence the name.

- NMOS **on**: pin pulled to ground through $R_{on}$ (tens of ohms).
- NMOS **off**: pin floats (high impedance). An external resistor to a supply defines what the line does.

See [[q0785]] for the transistor-level comparison and [[q0780]] for GPIO modes.

## Wired-AND, concretely

Connect two open-drain outputs and a pull-up to one net. The truth table is:

| A pulls low | B pulls low | Line |
|---|---|---|
| no | no | 1 (pull-up) |
| yes | no | 0 |
| no | yes | 0 |
| yes | yes | 0 |

That's an AND of the "released" states: the line is 1 only if both release.

## The contention argument, with numbers

Two push-pull outputs on one wire at 3.3 V where one drives high (PMOS $R_{on}\approx25$ Ω) and the other low (NMOS $R_{on}\approx20$ Ω):

$$I=\frac{3.3\text{ V}}{25+20\ \Omega}=73\text{ mA},\quad P=I^2R_{total}=242\text{ mW}$$

That's beyond the absolute-maximum pin current of a typical GPIO (rated for tens of mA at most) and heats the driver. Real output FETs saturate at high current, so the actual number is somewhat lower than this linear estimate, but still well over the rating. With open-drain, the worst case is *both* pulling low, which draws $V_{DD}/R_p=3.3/2200=1.5$ mA total from the single pull-up, and a device pulling low against a resistor is what it's designed for.

## What wired-AND buys the protocol

**ACK.** At the ninth clock the master releases SDA and the slave pulls it low. Since the master isn't driving high, there's no conflict at the handover, which would not be safe with push-pull SDA without a tri-state turnaround.

**Clock stretching.** A slave can hold SCL low while it processes; the master releases SCL and checks whether it actually rises before continuing ([[q0752]]).

**Multi-master arbitration.** Two masters start simultaneously. Each drives SDA while watching it. The first master that releases (1) where the other pulls (0) sees a mismatch and loses; the winner's data is intact because wired-AND is non-destructive ([[q0748]]).

**Shared interrupts.** Many open-drain INT outputs on one line form a wired-OR (active-low): any device asserts it, and the master polls to find out which.

## Voltage flexibility

The high level is the pull-up rail. Two voltage domains (1.8 V and 3.3 V) can talk across a pass transistor with a pull-up on each side, because both sides only ever pull low, and the transistor passes the low in both directions. That is the principle of the single-FET shifter ([[q0879]]) and of dedicated auto-direction I2C translators ([[q0877]]).

It also allows outputs rated for higher voltage than the core. An open-drain output with a 5 V-tolerant drain can sit on a 5 V pull-up while the MCU core runs at 1.8 V. Check the pin's absolute maximum and "5 V tolerant" spec.

## Hot-plug and power-off behaviour

A push-pull output has a PMOS whose body diode (to $V_{DD}$) conducts when the pin is above $V_{DD}$. If $V_{DD}$ is off, the line gets clamped to a diode drop above ground through it. A well-designed open-drain output can omit that diode path, so an unpowered device stays invisible on a live bus. But many practical devices still include an ESD diode to $V_{DD}$, so this is a feature to check, not a guarantee.

## The costs

**Slow rising edge.** The falling edge is driven by the transistor in nanoseconds. The rising edge is an RC charge:

$$t_r=0.8473R_pC_b$$

At 100 pF and 2.2 kΩ the 30–70% rise is 186 ns (483 ns measured 10–90%). A push-pull driver with $R_{on}\approx30$ Ω charges the same capacitance in $t_r\approx2.2\times30\times100\text{ pF}=6.6$ ns (10–90%), about 70 times faster. That gap is a big part of why I2C is so much slower than SPI.

**DC power.** While the line is low, $R_p$ conducts: $3.3\times2.9/2200=4.4$ mW per line. A push-pull line has no DC path. For I2C, the clock is low about half the time: roughly 2.2 mW average for SCL, and a battery-powered design may choose bigger pull-ups for that reason ([[q0755]]).

**Edge asymmetry.** Fast fall, slow rise give an asymmetric waveform and a duty-cycle shift at high speed.

**Resistor required, and sized to a window** (minimum from sink current, maximum from rise time).

## When to use which

| Use open-drain when | Use push-pull when |
|---|---|
| Several devices may drive the same wire | One driver per wire (SPI MOSI, SCLK) |
| You need wired-AND/OR (interrupts, arbitration) | You need fast edges and clean duty cycle |
| Mixing voltage domains on one wire | A single supply domain |
| Hot-plug tolerance matters | Power consumption of pull-ups matters |
| Speed is modest (≤ 1 MHz class) | Tens of MHz or more |

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Frame it as a trade: "I give up speed and a bit of power to get a bus that can never short itself and that can arbitrate." One sentence with both sides is what they want.
:::

::: applications
- **I2C and SMBus**: SDA and SCL are open-drain.
- **Shared interrupt lines**: several sensors' INT pins wired together on one MCU input.
- **Reset lines**: open-drain supervisors let a manual push-button and several ICs pull RESET low.
- **1-Wire bus** (Dallas/Maxim): single-wire open-drain with a pull-up.
- **I2C level translation**: pass-FET and auto-direction translators rely on open-drain behaviour.
- **Fault/status flags** (PGOOD, FAULT#): open-drain flags from regulators combine as wired-OR/AND.
:::

::: assumptions
- **NMOS open-drain with an external resistor**, the standard I2C implementation. Open-collector (BJT) behaves similarly with a saturation voltage.
- **Short bus**: lumped $C_b$.
- **Slow-to-moderate speeds**: at tens of MHz the asymmetry of open-drain is a dominant limit and other bus styles are used.
- **Typical on-resistances** (20 to 40 Ω for push-pull GPIO) vary widely with the part and supply.

If the interviewer says "ignore speed", the first-order advantages are the safety and shared-wire features; if they say "speed matters", open-drain is the wrong choice for point-to-point.
:::

::: followups
### Why doesn't SPI use open-drain?
Each SPI line has exactly one driver (MOSI, SCLK, CS are driven by the master; MISO by the selected slave), so there's no contention to avoid, and speed matters. Push-pull gives fast, symmetric edges and no pull-up power. The one case where MISO needs care is a shared MISO line between multiple slaves, where non-selected slaves must tri-state ([[q0766]]).

### What's the difference between open-drain and tri-state?
Tri-state has a push-pull output with a third, disabled (high-Z) state. It can drive high *or* low when enabled, so two enabled drivers can still fight. Open-drain has only "pull low" or "off". The line's high level in open-drain comes from a resistor rather than a driver, and an open-drain line can't be driven high by the device.

### How does arbitration work and why does it depend on open-drain?
Both masters drive SDA while monitoring it. If a master sends a 1 (releases) but reads 0, another master is pulling low, so it knows it lost and stops. This relies on the line being the AND of all drivers; with push-pull the loser's 1 would fight the winner's 0 and corrupt the data.

### Could you make an open-drain bus faster?
Reduce $R_pC_b$, use an active/current-source pull-up or rise-time accelerator, or use Fast-mode Plus devices with 20 mA drive. There's still a limit; at some point you use a push-pull protocol such as SPI instead. See [[q0758]].

### What happens if one device on an I2C bus is unpowered?
If its pins have clamp diodes to $V_{DD}$, the live bus can forward-bias them and the device (or the bus) is dragged down. Devices with Ioff protection or a bus switch isolating that branch avoid it. Verify in the datasheet before relying on partial-power-down behaviour.
:::
