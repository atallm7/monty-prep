::: testing
Whether you understand a feedforward capacitor as a **lead compensator hidden in the feedback divider**, not a magic fix. The interviewer wants:

1. Where it goes: in parallel with the upper feedback resistor $R_1$.
2. What it does: a zero at $1/(2\pi R_1C_{ff})$ and a pole at $1/(2\pi(R_1\parallel R_2)C_{ff})$, adding phase lead between them and a high-frequency path from $V_{out}$ to the feedback node.
3. When it is needed: ceramic-only output capacitors with no ESR zero, low phase margin, slow transient response, or when the datasheet calls for it.
4. The costs: a high-frequency gain increase of $V_{out}/V_{ref}$, noise and ripple coupling, and sensitivity in ripple-based controllers.
:::

::: why
A 200 pF capacitor can be the difference between a buck that rings and one that is well damped, and it is often the cheapest fix on a board. It is also easy to misuse. Interviewers ask about it because it combines the voltage divider ([[q0008]]) with a control-loop idea (lead compensation) in a few components, and because "just add a cap across the top resistor" is the sort of folk advice that you should be able to justify with equations.
:::

::: concepts
- **Feedback divider**: $V_{FB}=V_{out}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ and $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$ ([[q0223]], [[q0008]]).
- **Lead compensator**: a zero below a pole gives phase lead between them.
- **Impedance of a parallel RC**: $R_1\parallel(1/sC_{ff})$ gives the zero.
- **Crossover frequency and phase margin** ([[q0244]]).
- **ESR zero** that ceramics lack ([[q0247]]).
- **High-frequency coupling**: at high frequency the divider becomes capacitive and passes ripple to the FB pin.
:::

::: answer
"A feedforward capacitor is a small capacitor placed across the upper resistor of the output feedback divider, from V out to the feedback pin. At DC it does nothing, because the capacitor is open and the divider ratio sets the output voltage. At higher frequency it shorts out R1, so the feedback signal follows V out more directly, with no divider attenuation.

That makes a lead network: a zero at 1 over 2 pi R1 Cff, and a pole at 1 over 2 pi times R1 in parallel with R2 times Cff. Between them the phase advances, with a peak lead that depends on the ratio of the pole to zero, which is V out over V ref. So it gives me extra phase margin right around the crossover frequency, if I choose Cff so that the zero is below and the pole above crossover. It also lets the loop see output changes faster, so the transient response improves.

I'd use it when the output has only ceramic capacitors, which have no ESR zero to give lead, when the phase margin is low or the part's datasheet recommends it, or to speed up a slow response.

The trade-off is that at high frequency the feedback gain goes up by V out over V ref, so switching ripple and noise get coupled directly to the FB pin, which can add jitter. Too large a Cff makes this worse, and it only helps if the output voltage is much higher than the reference. For a 3.3 V output from a 0.8 V reference that's a 12 dB boost."
:::

::: technical
**Network.** $V_{out}\to R_1\parallel C_{ff}\to V_{FB}\to R_2\to$ ground. With $Z_1=R_1/(1+sR_1C_{ff})$:

$$\frac{V_{FB}}{V_{out}}=\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\cdot\frac{1+sR_1C_{ff}}{1+s\,(R_1\parallel R_2)\,C_{ff}}$$

- Zero: $f_z=\dfrac{1}{2\pi R_1C_{ff}}$.
- Pole: $f_p=\dfrac{1}{2\pi (R_1\parallel R_2) C_{ff}}$.
- Ratio: $f_p/f_z=R_1/(R_1\parallel R_2)=(R_1+R_2)/R_2=V_{out}/V_{ref}\equiv a$.
- HF gain boost relative to DC: $a$ (that is $20\log a$ dB).
- Maximum phase lead: $\phi_{max}=\arcsin\dfrac{a-1}{a+1}$ at $f_{max}=\sqrt{f_zf_p}$.

**Design to center the lead at crossover $f_c$:** $f_z=f_c/\sqrt a$, $f_p=f_c\sqrt a$, so $C_{ff}=\dfrac{\sqrt a}{2\pi R_1f_c}$.

**Worked example.** $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $V_{ref}=0.8$ V, $R_2=10$ kΩ so $R_1=R_2(a-1)=31.25$ kΩ, $a=4.125$, crossover 40 kHz.

- $f_z=40/\sqrt{4.125}=19.7$ kHz, $f_p=40\cdot\sqrt{4.125}=81.2$ kHz.
- $C_{ff}=1/(2\pi\cdot31.25\text{ k}\Omega\cdot19.7\text{ kHz})=259$ pF (nearest standard 270 pF).
- Check: $R_1\parallel R_2=7.58$ kΩ, $f_p=1/(2\pi\cdot7.58\text{ k}\Omega\cdot259\text{ pF})=81.2$ kHz ✓.
- Maximum lead: $\arcsin(3.125/5.125)=37.6^\circ$. HF gain boost: 12.3 dB.

**Effect on the loop.** It adds up to about 38° of phase at 40 kHz and 12 dB of gain boost above 81 kHz, which also raises the loop gain at high frequency and can move the crossover up if the compensator isn't re-tuned.

**When the output is near the reference.** For $V_{out}=1.2$ V and $V_{ref}=0.8$ V, $a=1.5$: lead is only $\arcsin(0.5/2.5)=11.5^\circ$, so the capacitor does very little.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Recommended $C_{ff}$ values depend on the controller. Fixed-compensation ICs and constant-on-time controllers often give a recommended range or formula in the datasheet; follow it rather than a generic centring rule.
:::

::: long
"A feedforward capacitor is a small capacitor, typically tens to hundreds of picofarads, connected across the upper resistor of the feedback divider, so from V out to the FB pin. It looks innocent, but it's a lead compensator.

At DC the capacitor is open, so the divider ratio, R2 over R1 plus R2, sets the output voltage as usual. As frequency goes up the capacitor's impedance falls and eventually shorts R1, so the feedback node follows V out directly with no attenuation.

In transfer function terms the divider gets a zero at 1 over 2 pi R1 Cff and a pole at 1 over 2 pi times the parallel of R1 and R2 times Cff. The pole is higher than the zero by exactly V out over V ref. Between them the phase leads, with maximum lead at the geometric mean, and the maximum lead is arcsine of a minus 1 over a plus 1, where a is V out over V ref.

A worked example: 3.3 V out, 0.8 V reference, R2 of 10 k, so R1 is 31.25 k and a is 4.125. If I want the lead centered on a 40 kHz crossover, the zero should be at 40 over root a, about 19.7 kHz, and the pole at 81 kHz. That gives Cff around 260 picofarads, with a maximum phase boost of about 38 degrees and 12 dB of high-frequency gain.

When do I need it? With ceramic output capacitors there's no ESR zero, so the loop can have poor phase margin near crossover. A feedforward cap provides the phase lead for free, and it also sends output changes to the error amplifier faster, improving the load transient. I'd also use it when the datasheet recommends a value for an internally compensated part.

The downsides: that high-frequency gain also passes switching ripple and noise to the FB pin, so too big a cap increases jitter, and in ripple-based converters it changes the ripple injection. It does little when the output is close to the reference voltage. And it modifies the loop gain, so I'd re-check the Bode plot."
:::

::: terms
- **Feedforward capacitor ($C_{ff}$)**: capacitor across the upper feedback resistor.
- **Lead compensation**: a zero below a pole that adds phase advance.
- **Feedback divider**: $R_1/R_2$ network setting $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$.
- **Zero / pole frequency**: $1/(2\pi R_1C_{ff})$ and $1/(2\pi(R_1\parallel R_2)C_{ff})$.
- **Maximum phase lead**: $\arcsin\frac{a-1}{a+1}$, $a=V_{out}/V_{ref}$.
- **Reference voltage ($V_{ref}$, $V_{FB}$)**: the regulation point of the error amplifier.
- **Ripple injection**: deliberately coupling ripple to FB (in COT converters).
- **ESR zero**: lead from the output capacitor's ESR.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the cap across $R_2$** instead of $R_1$. Across $R_2$ it creates a lag, not lead.
- **Claiming it changes the DC output voltage.** At DC the capacitor is open.
- **Using a large value "to be safe".** It pushes the zero too low, raises high-frequency gain and couples more switching noise.
- **Ignoring the pole.** The lead exists only between the zero and the pole, so the crossover should be in that band.
- **Using it with $V_{out}$ close to $V_{ref}$.** There is almost no ratio to exploit.
- **Forgetting to re-check the loop.** The added HF gain moves the crossover and may excite the sampling effects near $f_{sw}/2$.
:::

## Why it helps stability

In a voltage-mode loop with ceramic output capacitors the plant has two poles and almost no ESR zero up to MHz, so near crossover the phase is close to $-180^\circ$. The compensator zeros must provide the lead, and an internally compensated controller may not have enough. $C_{ff}$ adds a zero-pole pair in the feedback path that supplies up to about 38° in the example, which is the difference between 20° and 58° of margin in a marginal design. Because it is in the feedback path it needs no extra pins on the IC.

It also helps the transient: at high frequency the error amplifier sees $V_{out}$ directly, so a droop is detected sooner and with $a$ times more signal. That shortens the reaction time, the second stage in [[q0248]].

## Choosing the value

1. Determine the crossover $f_c$ from the loop design or the datasheet.
2. Compute $a=V_{out}/V_{ref}$ and $R_1$, $R_2$.
3. Choose $C_{ff}=\sqrt a/(2\pi R_1f_c)$ for centered lead, then pick a standard value.
4. Verify with a loop-gain measurement. Check phase margin and the crossover shift caused by the HF gain.
5. Check noise: look at the FB pin ripple and the switch-node jitter.

For a 1 V output from 0.8 V ($a=1.25$) the maximum lead is only $\arcsin(0.25/2.25)=6.4^\circ$, so use compensation or ESR instead.

## Where it hurts

- **Noise and ripple**: the FB pin sees the output ripple multiplied by up to $a$, so with large $C_{ff}$ ripple coupling can cause cycle-to-cycle jitter or subharmonic-like behavior in some controllers.
- **High-frequency gain**: the crossover can move up if the loop gain is not re-tuned.
- **Start-up and soft-start**: the capacitor couples the output ramp to FB, which usually is harmless but can change soft-start behavior slightly.
- **Leakage and tolerance**: very high-value resistors with small capacitors are sensitive to PCB parasitics (a few pF of stray capacitance at the FB node), so layout of the FB node matters.

## In ripple-based (COT) controllers

In constant-on-time or other ripple-based controllers, $C_{ff}$ often carries the ripple from $V_{out}$ to the comparator input, so it sets the ripple the controller sees. There it is mandatory in some designs, with a value specified by the datasheet. This is a different use of the same component.

## Deriving the maximum lead

The lead formula quoted above is worth deriving, because it shows why the lead depends only on $a=V_{out}/V_{ref}$ and not on $C_{ff}$ itself. Let $x=f/f_z$ and recall $f_p=a f_z$. The phase of $(1+jx)/(1+jx/a)$ is $\phi=\arctan x-\arctan(x/a)$, so

$$\tan\phi=\frac{x-x/a}{1+x^2/a}=\frac{(a-1)\,x}{a+x^2}.$$

Setting the derivative with respect to $x$ to zero gives $a+x^2=2x^2$, so $x^2=a$ and $f_{max}=f_z\sqrt a=\sqrt{f_zf_p}$. At that point $\tan\phi_{max}=(a-1)/(2\sqrt a)$, which converts to $\sin\phi_{max}=(a-1)/(a+1)$. Two consequences. First, $C_{ff}$ only slides the lead *left or right* on the frequency axis. It never changes how much lead there is. Second, the only way to get more lead is a larger $a$, and $a$ is fixed by the output voltage you chose. A 1.8 V rail from 0.6 V gives $a=3$ and 30°, a 5 V rail from 0.8 V gives $a=6.25$ and 46.4°.

## What a tolerance does to the lead

Because $\phi_{max}$ is independent of $C_{ff}$, the design is forgiving. Using the 3.3 V example ($R_1=31.25$ kΩ, $R_2=10$ kΩ, 40 kHz crossover) and evaluating the phase at exactly 40 kHz:

| $C_{ff}$ | $f_z$ | $f_p$ | lead at 40 kHz | gain at 40 kHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 207 pF (−20%) | 24.6 kHz | 101 kHz | 36.9° | +5.0 dB |
| 259 pF (centered) | 19.7 kHz | 81.1 kHz | 37.6° | +6.2 dB |
| 311 pF (+20%) | 16.4 kHz | 67.6 kHz | 37.1° | +7.1 dB |
| 270 pF (standard value) | 18.9 kHz | 77.8 kHz | 37.5° | +6.4 dB |
| 1 nF (4x too big) | 5.1 kHz | 21.0 kHz | 20.5° | +11.3 dB |

A ±20% capacitor spread moves the lead at crossover by under 1°, which is why the standard 270 pF is as good as the computed 259 pF. A 4x oversize capacitor loses 17° of lead at crossover *and* adds 5 dB of extra gain there, so it both hurts the margin and drags the crossover up. This table is also the answer to "why not use a bigger one to be safe": the lead is flat-topped, but the gain keeps climbing.

## The cost in noise, with numbers

Suppose the output has 20 mV of peak-to-peak switching ripple at $f_{sw}=500$ kHz, well above the 81 kHz pole. Without $C_{ff}$ the FB pin sees the divider's attenuated copy: $20\ \text{mV}\times0.2424=4.8$ mV. With $C_{ff}$ in place the divider is bypassed at that frequency and the FB pin sees nearly the whole 20 mV, about four times more ($a=4.125$). On a 0.8 V reference that is 2.5% of the reference riding on the error-amplifier input. For a voltage-mode controller whose error amplifier has a bandwidth of a few MHz or less, the ripple is attenuated further by the amplifier. For a controller whose comparator or modulator is fast, it becomes duty-cycle jitter. How much ripple the part tolerates at the FB pin is controller-specific, so check the datasheet's guidance and measure the switch node with a persistence display.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The FB-pin ripple tolerance and the controller's input filtering vary by part. The 20 mV ripple and 500 kHz switching frequency here are illustrative values, not a specification of any device.

## Parasitics and layout around the FB node

- **Stray capacitance at the pin.** With a low-impedance divider (10 kΩ-ish) a few pF of pin and pad capacitance sits at a pole above 4 MHz and does nothing. Low quiescent-current designs use a megaohm-range divider: with $R_1=1$ MΩ, $R_2=320$ kΩ and 5 pF at the FB pin, the parasitic pole is at 131 kHz, close to a typical crossover, and 0.2 pF across $R_1$ (from a nearby trace) creates a zero at 796 kHz. In those designs the "feedforward capacitor" may already exist as layout, and a deliberate $C_{ff}$ must be sized against it.
- **Dielectric.** The capacitor is only tens to hundreds of pF. Use a C0G/NP0 part for a predictable value over temperature and voltage. A class-II part (X7R) is cheaper but its value drifts with temperature and bias, and the 2.5 V that sits across it in the example is not small. Whether the drift matters depends on how close the design sits to its margin limit.
- **Keep the node small.** Place $R_1$, $R_2$ and $C_{ff}$ right at the FB pin, with the ground return of $R_2$ going to the controller's analog ground, not under the inductor or the switch node.

## Bench verification

Place the loop-injection resistor (10 Ω to 50 Ω, tiny compared with $R_1$) between the output node and the top of the $R_1\parallel C_{ff}$ network, so $C_{ff}$ is inside the measured loop. Sweep with $C_{ff}$ not populated, then with 100 pF, 220 pF and 470 pF. A correct design shows phase margin rising to a maximum near the centered value, a crossover that moves up modestly, and a gain-margin figure that does not collapse. If the margin keeps rising with bigger values, the real problem is somewhere else (inductor or output-capacitor model), not the feedforward network. Finally check the transient response at a few load levels, because a lead network helps the response most when the loop crosses in the zero-to-pole band at the operating point.

::: applications
- **Internally compensated buck ICs with ceramic outputs**, where the datasheet gives a $C_{ff}$ table versus $V_{out}$ and $L$.
- **LDOs**: a bypass capacitor across the upper divider resistor reduces output noise and improves transient response in adjustable LDOs.
- **Constant-on-time buck converters** where $C_{ff}$ is part of the ripple-injection network.
- **Boost and buck-boost controllers** with similar divider-based lead networks.
- **Debugging**: a small pad for $C_{ff}$ is commonly left on a first board for tuning.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Voltage-mode or similar loop** where the feedback network is in the loop gain. Peak-current-mode converters benefit less, but it can still help with ceramics.
- **Ideal capacitor and resistors**, no stray capacitance at the FB node (a few pF can matter for small $C_{ff}$).
- **Centred-lead design rule** is a starting point. The best value is found by measuring the loop.
- **DC behavior** unaffected, unless leakage is significant.
- For a converter with an internal type II/III compensator, the interaction with the compensator's own poles and zeros needs checking.
:::

::: followups
### Where is the maximum phase lead and what is it?
At the geometric mean of the zero and pole, $f_{max}=\sqrt{f_zf_p}=f_z\sqrt a$, and its value is $\arcsin\frac{a-1}{a+1}$. For $a=4.125$, $f_{max}=40$ kHz and the lead is 37.6°.

### What is the difference between a feedforward capacitor and a larger output capacitor?
The output capacitor sets the plant (LC corner, droop, ripple); the feedforward capacitor is part of the feedback network and shapes the loop gain. They affect stability differently: more output capacitance lowers $f_0$ and reduces droop, while $C_{ff}$ adds phase lead near crossover and speeds detection.

### Why does $C_{ff}$ raise the crossover?
Above the pole frequency the feedback gain is $a$ times higher than at DC, so the loop gain at high frequency rises by up to 12.3 dB in the example. If the loop gain was crossing at 40 kHz on a −20 dB/decade slope, a 12 dB increase pushes the crossover up by roughly a factor of 4 if the whole region were above the pole. In practice the effect is partial, but you must re-check.

### What if the output voltage is 1.0 V and the reference 0.8 V?
$a=1.25$, so the lead is at most 6.4° and the HF gain increase is just 1.9 dB. The capacitor does very little. You would need the compensator, ESR or a different approach to get phase margin.

### Scenario: a converter is stable on the bench but jitters visibly on the switch node when $C_{ff}$ is 1 nF. Why?
$C_{ff}$ is too large: the zero is far below crossover, and the HF gain boost couples output ripple (and noise) at full strength into FB. The comparator or error amplifier sees extra ripple that modulates the on-time cycle by cycle. Reduce $C_{ff}$ toward the centered value (260 pF in the example), shorten and shield the FB trace, and re-measure.
:::
