::: basics
### In plain words
An inductor wastes some power as heat, and this page asks where that waste comes from. There are two main sources. Winding loss is the heat from current pushing through the copper wire. Core loss is the heat from the magnetic material being pushed back and forth every cycle. Knowing both tells you why a real inductor runs warm.

### Picture it
Think of rubbing your hands together. Two things make them warm. Your skin drags against itself, which is like current dragging through the wire's resistance. And if you flex a stiff rubber band back and forth, it warms up too, which is like the core being magnetized one way and then the other. Faster rubbing or flexing makes more heat. The analogy is rough, because real core loss has several separate causes.

### Words you need
- **Winding loss**: heat in the copper wire, equal to current squared times the wire's resistance ($I^2R$). The wire's resistance to steady current is its DCR. See [[q0047]].
- **Core loss**: heat in the magnetic core from hysteresis and eddy currents. See [[q0048]].
- **Hysteresis**: energy lost each time the core's magnetism is flipped to point the other way.
- **Eddy currents**: small circulating currents induced in the core by the changing flux.
- **Skin and proximity effects**: at high frequency, current crowds toward the wire's surface and away from neighboring turns, so less copper carries it and resistance rises.

### The simplest example
Run 3 A of DC through a winding with 20 mΩ of resistance. The loss is $I^2R = 3 \times 3 \times 0.02 = 0.18$ W. That is copper loss alone. Now add ripple current at a high switching frequency. Skin and proximity effects raise the wire's AC resistance, and the core also loses energy each cycle. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers how AC resistance and the Steinmetz equation put numbers on these, and how a converter design balances copper loss against core loss.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can separate inductor loss into its **physical mechanisms**, say where each comes from, and estimate which dominates in a given application. The interviewer expects:

1. **Winding (copper) loss**: DC resistance ($I_{dc}^2R_{dc}$) and AC resistance from skin and proximity effects.
2. **Core loss**: hysteresis, eddy current (and residual/relaxation) losses, summarized by the Steinmetz equation $P_v=kf^\alpha B^\beta$.
3. **Other losses**: air-gap fringing flux in nearby copper, dielectric/capacitive losses at high frequency, and (in transformers) leakage effects.
4. How they scale with frequency, flux swing and current, and what you'd change to reduce them.
:::

::: why
Inductor loss sets the efficiency and temperature rise of almost every power converter, and often the Q of an RF or filter inductor. Knowing which mechanism dominates tells you what to change: a thicker wire fixes DC loss but does nothing for a core that's hot from high flux swing; a bigger core cuts flux density but may increase copper loss. "Why is my inductor hot?" is a classic debugging question.
:::

::: concepts
- **Joule heating in wire**: $P=I^2R$ ([[q0006]]); resistance rises with temperature (0.39 %/K for copper).
- **Skin and proximity effects** ([[q0056]], [[q0051]]): AC current crowds the conductor, raising resistance.
- **B–H loop and hysteresis**: the loop area is the energy lost per cycle per volume.
- **Faraday's law and eddy currents**: a changing flux induces currents in conductive core material.
- **Steinmetz equation**: empirical $P_v=k f^\alpha B_{pk}^\beta$ for sinusoidal flux.
- **Ripple current and flux swing** ([[q0048]]): $B_{pk}=L\,\Delta I/(2NA_e)$ in a converter inductor.
:::

::: answer
"There are two big buckets: winding loss and core loss.

Winding loss is the copper resistance. The DC part is the current squared times the DC resistance, so a 3 amp load through 20 milliohms is 0.18 watts. On top of that, AC currents, like the ripple in a buck converter, see a higher resistance because of skin effect, where the current crowds toward the surface of the wire, and proximity effect, where neighboring turns' fields push current around. That AC resistance can be several times the DC value at hundreds of kilohertz.

Core loss comes from the magnetic material being driven around its B-H loop every cycle. Hysteresis loss is the energy per cycle, proportional to the loop area, so it scales with frequency. Eddy currents are circulating currents induced in the core by the changing flux, which scale roughly as frequency squared times flux squared, and are suppressed by using an insulating ferrite, laminations or powder. We summarize it with the Steinmetz equation: P equals k f to the alpha B to the beta, with alpha around 1 to 2 and beta around 2 to 3. So flux swing matters a lot: halving it cuts loss by around 5 times.

There are also smaller ones: fringing flux around an air gap induces eddy currents in nearby windings, and the winding capacitance causes dielectric loss and resonance at high frequency.

In a converter, DC copper loss usually dominates at low ripple, and core loss and AC copper loss grow with ripple and frequency, so you balance them in the design."
:::

::: technical
**Total:** $P_{loss}=P_{cu,dc}+P_{cu,ac}+P_{core}+P_{other}$.

**Copper:** $P_{cu,dc}=I_{dc}^2R_{dc}$ with $R_{dc}=\rho\ell/A$. AC: $P_{cu,ac}=\sum_k I_{k,rms}^2R_{ac}(f_k)$, $R_{ac}=F_R(f)R_{dc}$ where $F_R=1+\ldots$ accounts for skin depth $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu_0)}$ and proximity ([[q0051]]). Copper: $\delta=66\ \mu$m at 1 MHz, 92 µm at 500 kHz, 209 µm at 100 kHz.

**Core:**
- Hysteresis: $P_h=f\oint H\,dB\cdot V_e\ \propto\ f\,B^{\beta}$ ($\beta\approx2$–3).
- Eddy: $P_e\propto f^2B^2d^2/\rho$ (thin lamination of thickness $d$, resistivity $\rho$).
- Residual/relaxation: appears at high frequency in ferrites (resonance of domain wall motion).
- **Steinmetz:** $P_v=k\,f^\alpha B_{pk}^\beta$ (mW/cm³); for non-sinusoidal flux use iGSE (improved generalized Steinmetz). Coefficients from vendor curves; $\alpha\approx1.1$–2, $\beta\approx2$–3 depending on material and band.

**Converter flux:** $B_{pk}=\dfrac{L\,\Delta I_{pp}}{2NA_e}$. For fixed $L$ and voltage, $\Delta I\propto1/f$, so $B\propto1/f$ and $P_v\propto f^{\alpha-\beta}$: core loss *falls* with frequency in a fixed-$L$ converter design if $\beta>\alpha$.

**Worked example** (buck 12 V → 3.3 V, $I_{dc}=3$ A, $L=4.7\ \mu$H, $f=500$ kHz, DCR 20 mΩ): $D=0.275$, $\Delta I_{pp}=(V_{in}-V_o)D/(Lf)=1.018$ A, $I_{ac,rms}=\Delta I/(2\sqrt3)=0.294$ A. $P_{dc}=3^2\cdot0.020=0.18$ W. With an assumed $R_{ac}=3R_{dc}$, $P_{ac}=0.294^2\cdot0.06=5.2$ mW. For $N=10$ and $A_e=10$ mm², $B_{pk}=L\Delta I/(2NA_e)=4.7\ \mu\text{H}\cdot1.018/(2\cdot10\cdot10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2)=24$ mT. The core loss at 24 mT and 500 kHz must be read from the vendor curve; for typical ferrite or powder cores it is of the order of tens of mW to about 0.1 W.

:::

::: long
"Inductor loss splits into winding loss and core loss, with a few smaller extras.

Winding loss is the copper. At DC, it's the current squared times the winding resistance: a 3 amp load through 20 milliohms dissipates 0.18 watts, and it heats the wire, which raises its resistance by 0.4 percent per kelvin. At AC it's worse. Skin effect pushes current to the surface of the wire, with a skin depth of about 66 micrometers in copper at 1 megahertz, so a thick wire is not fully used. Proximity effect, in a multi-layer winding, makes the fields of neighboring turns induce circulating currents that crowd the current further. So the AC resistance can be several times the DC value. For ripple current that matters; for DC load current it doesn't.

Core loss is the magnetic material being driven around the B-H loop. Hysteresis loss is the loop area times frequency, so it goes up in direct proportion to the frequency and strongly with flux swing. Eddy-current loss comes from the changing flux inducing currents in the core, which scales as f squared, B squared, and thickness squared over resistivity; that's why cores are ferrite, laminated or powdered. In practice we use the Steinmetz equation with vendor coefficients: P equals k f to the alpha B to the beta, alpha 1 to 2, beta 2 to 3. If I halve the flux swing, I cut core loss by about 5 times.

Others: an air gap sprays fringing flux into nearby copper, inducing eddy currents, and the winding capacitance gives dielectric loss and resonance at high frequencies.

In a converter, the DC copper loss is usually the biggest unless the ripple is large. Increasing frequency at fixed inductance lowers ripple and flux so core loss goes down, but the AC resistance, switching losses in the FETs and the skin effect go up, so I pick the frequency and core to balance them and check the temperature rise with total loss times thermal resistance."
:::

::: terms
- **Copper (winding) loss**: $I^2R$ in the wire, DC plus AC.
- **Skin effect / proximity effect**: AC current crowding in a conductor due to its own and neighbors' fields.
- **Hysteresis loss**: energy dissipated per cycle equal to the B–H loop area.
- **Eddy-current loss**: $I^2R$ in circulating currents induced in the core.
- **Steinmetz equation**: $P_v=kf^\alpha B^\beta$.
- **Fringing loss**: eddy loss in nearby conductors from the flux spreading around an air gap.
- **Flux swing $\Delta B$**: peak-to-peak flux density change per cycle.
- **Thermal resistance $\theta$**: temperature rise per watt.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Quoting only DCR.** At switching frequencies the AC resistance and core loss can exceed the DC loss.
- **Assuming core loss depends on DC current.** It depends on the *AC flux swing*; the DC bias mostly affects saturation and (a bit) permeability.
- **Ignoring flux swing.** $\beta\approx2$–3 means a modest increase in ripple causes a large rise in core loss.
- **Using sinusoidal Steinmetz data for a triangular or PWM waveform** without correction (use the iGSE/composite waveform hypothesis).
- **Confusing saturation with loss.** Saturation is a nonlinearity (L collapses), not a dissipation mechanism, though it can lead to overheating from the resulting large current.
- **Thinking hysteresis loss is "friction".** It's the energy dissipated in irreversible domain-wall motion; its magnitude is the B–H loop area per cycle.
:::

::: deep
## 1. Winding loss

**DC.** $R_{dc}=\rho\ell/A$ with copper $\rho=1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega$m at 20 °C, rising 0.39 %/K. Example: a 20 mΩ winding at 3 A dissipates 0.18 W; at a winding temperature 60 K above 20 °C, $R$ rises to $0.02\cdot(1+0.0039\cdot60)=24.7$ mΩ and the loss to 0.22 W, so thermal and electrical designs are coupled.

**AC.** For a round wire of radius $r$ in isolation the skin depth $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu_0)}$ determines current crowding: at 500 kHz, $\delta=92\ \mu$m, so a 0.5 mm-diameter wire ($r=250\ \mu$m) is beyond 2δ and its AC resistance is clearly above DC. In a multi-layer winding the **proximity effect**, caused by neighbors' fields inducing eddy currents (opposite in sign to the main current), is usually worse than skin effect alone: Dowell's analysis shows the AC resistance factor $F_R$ grows rapidly when the layer thickness exceeds δ, and the optimal layer thickness is of the order of δ. Mitigations: foil or Litz wire, fewer layers, interleaving of primary/secondary, and wide copper in planar magnetics ([[q0051]]).

## 2. Core loss

**Hysteresis.** In a magnetic material, applied H aligns domains. Domain-wall motion is irreversible, so the B–H curve is a loop. The enclosed area $\oint H\,dB$ is the energy per unit volume per cycle, so $P_h=f\cdot\text{(area)}\cdot V_e$. The area grows with flux swing as roughly $B^{\beta}$, $\beta\approx2$–3.

**Eddy currents.** Faraday's law: a time-varying B in a conducting core induces circulating E fields and currents. The loss density scales as $\sigma(\omega B d)^2$, i.e. $\propto f^2B^2d^2/\rho$. Suppressed by high resistivity (ferrite), thin laminations $d$ (steel, amorphous), or insulated particles (powder).

**Residual/relaxation loss** (mostly ferrites at high frequency): resonance-like losses from domain-wall and spin dynamics near the material's relaxation frequency; limits the frequency range of each ferrite grade.

**Steinmetz.** For sinusoidal flux: $P_v=kf^\alpha B_{pk}^\beta$. $\alpha$ is between hysteresis-like (1) and eddy-like (2) behaviour; $\beta$ typically 2–3. Typical power-ferrite values: $\alpha\approx1.3$–1.8, $\beta\approx2.3$–2.8 (grade and band dependent). Consequences:

- Double the frequency at the same $B$: loss × $2^\alpha\approx$ 2.5–3.5.
- Double the flux swing at the same $f$: loss × $2^\beta\approx$ 5–7.

For PWM (triangular) flux the iGSE gives a small correction by using the actual $dB/dt$; and the composite waveform hypothesis combines segments. Core-loss vendors also provide curves (mW/cm³ vs B at several frequencies and temperatures; the temperature of minimum loss is designed around 80–100 °C in power ferrites).

## 3. Other loss mechanisms

- **Fringing loss:** at an air gap, flux fans out; it induces eddy currents in the adjacent winding turns, causing local hot spots. Keep windings away from the gap, or use a distributed gap (powder core).
- **Dielectric/capacitive loss:** the winding capacitance and insulation lose energy at high frequency; modelled by the parallel resistance in the equivalent circuit ([[q0047]]).
- **Radiated/leakage loss:** an unshielded core radiates; not a heating effect but an EMI loss.
- **Saturation-induced loss:** not a mechanism by itself but a regime change: when $L$ collapses the current rises, and $I^2R$ loss rises.
- **Temperature coupling:** both copper resistance and core loss depend on temperature; core loss has a minimum near a design temperature in power ferrites, and thermal runaway is possible if core loss rises faster than cooling.

## A worked converter example

Buck, 12 V → 3.3 V, load 3 A, $L=4.7$ µH, $f=500$ kHz, DCR 20 mΩ:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Duty cycle | $D=3.3/12=0.275$ |
| Ripple $\Delta I_{pp}$ | $(12-3.3)\cdot0.275/(4.7\ \mu\text{H}\cdot500\ \text{kHz})=1.02$ A |
| Ripple rms | $1.02/(2\sqrt3)=0.294$ A |
| DC copper | $3^2\cdot0.020=0.180$ W |
| AC copper ($R_{ac}\approx3R_{dc}$, assumption) | $0.294^2\cdot0.060=5.2$ mW |
| $B_{pk}$ ($N=10$, $A_e=10$ mm²) | $L\,\Delta I/(2NA_e)=24$ mT |
| Core loss (assumed, from a vendor curve at 24 mT, 500 kHz) | order of 0.05–0.15 W |
| Total | ≈ 0.25–0.35 W |

Temperature rise with $\theta=40$ K/W: 10–14 K. Reducing flux by 2× (more turns on a bigger core or bigger $L$) cuts core loss by about 5×, but adds copper.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The core loss number depends entirely on the material grade, geometry and temperature; it is an assumed order-of-magnitude in this example. In a real design read $P_v$ from the manufacturer's curve at the actual $B_{pk}$, $f$ and temperature, and verify by measurement.

## What to change to reduce loss

1. **Copper DC:** thicker wire/foil, fewer turns (higher-μ/gap trade), shorter mean turn length, better thermal path.
2. **Copper AC:** Litz wire, foil or planar with layer thickness near δ, fewer layers, interleaving.
3. **Core:** lower flux swing (larger core, more turns, higher L, higher frequency in a fixed-L design), better material (lower loss at the frequency), distributed gap to reduce fringing.
4. **System:** reduce ripple current (multiphase, higher L), and verify the thermal design (cores run best at 80–100 °C for ferrites).

::: followups
### Why does core loss depend on the AC flux swing and not on the DC current?
Loss comes from traversing the B–H loop; a DC bias just shifts the center of a minor loop. The area of that minor loop depends on the swing $\Delta B$, which comes from the AC (ripple) current. DC bias affects permeability and saturation, and slightly modifies loss, but isn't the primary driver.

### If you double the switching frequency with the same inductor in a buck converter, what happens to core loss?
Ripple current halves, so $B_{pk}$ halves; $P_v\propto f^\alpha B^\beta$ becomes $2^{\alpha}\cdot2^{-\beta}=2^{\alpha-\beta}$. With α = 1.4, β = 2.5, that's 0.47: core loss approximately halves. DC copper is unchanged and AC copper changes with $R_{ac}$; FET switching loss doubles.

### How would you reduce AC winding loss in a 1 MHz inductor?
Use Litz wire (many insulated strands each thinner than δ), or foil/planar traces with thickness around δ, reduce the number of layers, and interleave windings to reduce proximity effect. Keep the winding away from the gap and use a distributed-gap core to limit fringing.

### What if your inductor is running hot but the DC current is small?
Look at ripple: high-frequency ripple flux produces core loss and AC copper loss regardless of DC. Check $\Delta I$, $B_{pk}$, and the core-loss curve at that frequency and temperature, and consider whether the core grade is wrong for the frequency (e.g. a low-frequency grade at 1 MHz).

### Why do powder cores have more core loss than ferrite at high frequency but are still used?
Metal particles have lower resistivity and higher hysteresis than ferrite, so loss per volume is higher at hundreds of kHz. But their high $B_{sat}$ and soft saturation let you build a smaller inductor for the same DC bias, so the total loss in the smaller part can still be acceptable. It's a trade-off ([[q0049]]).
