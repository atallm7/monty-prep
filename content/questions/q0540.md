::: testing
Whether you can organise heat-reduction ideas into a sensible chain: generate less heat, give it a low-resistance path out of the package, spread it, and get it into the air or enclosure, plus protect the neighbours. The interviewer wants board-level techniques (exposed pad, thermal vias, copper area, planes, copper weight, component spacing, airflow) and the quantitative backbone, $T_j = T_a + P\,\theta_{JA}$. Good candidates also say that the first lever is often the circuit (a buck instead of an LDO), not the layout.
:::

::: why
Temperature sets lifetime, drift and sometimes the maximum current a part can deliver (many regulators and drivers reduce their current limit or shut down when hot). A layout that cuts $\theta_{JA}$ from 60 to 35 K/W can be worth more than a bigger heat sink, and costs nothing. In an interview this question separates people who know the data-sheet words from people who have actually laid out a thermal pad. It leads into heat sinks ([[q0541]]) and the broader "why is heat a concern" question ([[q0332]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Junction temperature**: $T_j = T_a + P\,\theta_{JA}$, where $\theta_{JA}$ depends strongly on the board. See [[q0332]].
- **Thermal resistance in series**: junction to case, case to board, board spreading, board to air.
- **Copper spreads heat**: conduction in copper is about 385 W/m·K versus 0.3 W/m·K through FR-4. See [[q0535]].
- **Thermal vias**: plated holes carrying heat between layers. See [[q0539]].
- **Efficiency**: the cheapest watt to remove is the one you did not dissipate. See [[q0157]].
- **Convection and radiation**: board area to air is the final bottleneck.
:::

::: answer
"I think of it as a chain. First, dissipate less: if an LDO is burning 0.85 W dropping 5 V to 3.3 V at half an amp, a buck converter at 90% efficiency burns about 0.18 W, so the best thermal fix is sometimes a different part.

Then I give the heat a good way out. For a package with an exposed pad, I solder the pad to a copper area on the top layer with a solid connection, not a thermal relief, and add an array of thermal vias, say 9 to 12 small ones, to inner planes and the bottom layer. I use as much copper area as I can, on both sides, and heavier copper if needed, because copper spreads heat about a thousand times better than FR-4. It all ends up in the air, so board area and airflow set the final temperature: roughly 20 K/W for a 50 by 50 mm board in still air.

Also placement: spread the hot parts apart, put them near the board edge or in the airflow, keep them away from temperature-sensitive parts like references, crystals, electrolytic capacitors and sensors, and don't stack them. And if the board alone isn't enough, I'll add a heat sink or spread into the enclosure. Then I check with a thermal camera."
:::

::: technical
**Governing relation.** $T_j = T_a + P\,\theta_{JA}$, with $\theta_{JA} = \theta_{JC} + \theta_{CB} + \theta_{spread} + \theta_{conv}$ (junction to case or pad, to board, spreading in copper, convection and radiation to ambient). Datasheet $\theta_{JA}$ values are measured on a standard JEDEC board and are not your board's value.

**Reduce dissipated power.** Linear regulator: $P = (V_{in}-V_{out})I$: 0.85 W for 5 V → 3.3 V at 0.5 A. A 90%-efficient buck: $P = V_{out}I(1/\eta - 1) = 0.18$ W. Reduce $R_{ds(on)}$, parallel devices, reduce switching frequency or gate charge, lower the input voltage.

**Heat path out of the package.** Solder the exposed pad to a top-layer copper pad; connect directly (no relief). Vias under the pad: a 0.3 mm via with 25 µm plating and 1.6 mm board is $L/(kA) = 176$ K/W; 12 vias in parallel give 14.7 K/W (copper-filled vias about 4.9 K/W). Keep vias open or filled/capped as the assembler requires (solder wicking).

**Spreading.** Radial spreading resistance from a 3 mm radius pad to 15 mm radius in 1 oz copper: $\ln 5/(2\pi kt) = 19$ K/W; 2 oz gives 9.5 K/W. Two or more copper layers in parallel divide it.

**Convection.** Board to air: $\theta \approx 1/(hA)$. With $h\approx10$ W/m²·K (natural convection plus radiation) and 50 × 50 mm on both sides, $\theta \approx 20$ K/W; with 25 × 25 mm it is about 80 K/W. Forced air raises $h$ several-fold.

**Placement and secondary effects.** Space hot parts, keep sensitive parts away, orient along airflow; each 10 °C rise roughly halves electrolytic capacitor life (Arrhenius rule of thumb).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $\theta_{JA}$ values and $h$ depend heavily on the part, board, orientation and enclosure. The numbers above are illustrative planning figures; use the package's $\theta_{JB}$/$\Psi_{JT}$ data and verify with a thermal camera or thermocouple.
:::

::: short
"First dissipate less, for example a buck instead of an LDO. Then give heat a path: solder the exposed pad to a big top-layer copper area with a solid connection and an array of thermal vias into inner planes and the bottom side, use as much copper as possible, and heavier copper if needed. Spread hot parts apart, keep them near airflow and away from sensitive parts, and finally check with a thermal camera. If the board can't do it, add a heat sink."
:::

::: long
"I treat it as a chain from the die to the air and look at each link.

The first link is the heat itself. If a linear regulator drops 5 V to 3.3 V at half an amp it dissipates 0.85 watts, and that's 51 degrees of rise at 60 kelvin per watt. A buck at 90% efficiency dissipates about 0.18 W. So the best fix may be a different part, or a lower input voltage, or splitting the loss across several devices.

Second is getting heat out of the package. For parts with an exposed pad, I solder the pad to a top copper area with a direct connection, not a thermal relief, because I want heat to flow. Under it I put an array of thermal vias, around 0.3 mm each, nine to twelve of them. Each plated via is roughly 176 kelvin per watt, so twelve are about 15, and filled vias are lower. They take the heat to inner planes and the bottom layer.

Third is spreading. Copper conducts heat about a thousand times better than the board material, so I use all the copper area I can on both sides, and 2 oz if it's tight. Spreading from a 3 mm pad out to 15 mm is about 19 kelvin per watt in 1 oz copper.

Fourth is the air. In still air a board loses heat at roughly 10 watts per square metre per kelvin, so a 50 by 50 mm board with copper on both sides is about 20 kelvin per watt. A 25 by 25 mm board is about 80. So for a couple of watts you need real board area or airflow.

Finally placement: spread hot parts out, put them near the edge or in the airflow, and keep them away from references, crystals, electrolytics and sensors. And I verify with a thermal camera."
:::

::: terms
- **$\theta_{JA}$**: junction-to-ambient thermal resistance (K/W); depends on board and airflow.
- **$\theta_{JC}$, $\theta_{JB}$**: junction to case (or exposed pad) and junction to board resistances; more portable than $\theta_{JA}$.
- **Exposed (thermal) pad**: a metal pad on the package bottom that conducts die heat to the board.
- **Thermal via**: a plated via under a pad that conducts heat to other layers.
- **Thermal spreading**: lateral heat flow in a copper plane or pour.
- **Heat sink**: a finned or large-area thermal mass for convection. See [[q0541]].
- **Derating**: reducing power or current at high temperature.
- **Thermal camera / thermocouple**: tools for measuring actual board temperatures.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "add copper and thermal vias."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts with *reducing the dissipation* (LDO vs buck, $R_{ds(on)}$, load sharing) and quantifies it.
- Gives the chain $T_j = T_a + P\theta_{JA}$ and points out that datasheet $\theta_{JA}$ is board-dependent.
- Explains the exposed pad, solid connection (no relief), via count and what a via is worth in K/W.
- Mentions that the final bottleneck is board area to air, with a number.
- Considers neighbours: spacing, temperature-sensitive parts, airflow, enclosure.
- Closes with verification (thermal camera, $\Psi_{JT}$ measurement) and when to escalate to a heat sink.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using a thermal relief on the exposed pad.** It adds series thermal resistance exactly where you want none. See [[q0538]].
- **Trusting the datasheet $\theta_{JA}$.** It was measured on a standard board; yours may be better or much worse.
- **Putting thermal vias outside the pad footprint** or too few of them. Vias must be under the exposed pad to shorten the path.
- **Forgetting that heat has to reach air.** A perfect spreading plane that is only 20 × 20 mm still has about 100 K/W to ambient.
- **Placing hot parts next to references, crystals, batteries or electrolytic capacitors.** Drift and life suffer.
- **Cutting the plane under the part** with slots or antipads that choke spreading.
:::

::: deep
## Start with the watts

$T_j = T_a + P\,\theta_{JA}$, so the two levers are $P$ and $\theta_{JA}$. Reducing $P$ gives the biggest returns because it also reduces the load on every other link.

For a linear regulator, $P = (V_{in}-V_{out})I$. 5 V → 3.3 V at 0.5 A is 0.85 W. With $\theta_{JA}=60$ K/W the rise is 51 °C; on a better board with 35 K/W it is 30 °C. A buck at 90%: $P = 3.3\times0.5\times(1/0.9-1) = 0.18$ W, giving 11 °C rise at 60 K/W. See [[q0157]], [[q0211]].

The LDO-vs-buck comparison in [[q0542]] shows the other lever: generating less heat in the first place.

Other ways to cut $P$: choose lower $R_{ds(on)}$ FETs or parallel two, use synchronous rectification, lower the input voltage with a pre-regulator, lower switching frequency if switching loss dominates, or share the load among phases ([[q0236]]). Series resistors in dropping paths (a resistor from the input to a linear regulator) move heat away from the IC; that is a real trick.

## The thermal path, link by link

Heat flows from the junction through the package to the PCB, along copper, and finally to air. Each link adds resistance:

| Link | Typical value | What you control |
|---|---|---|
| Junction → exposed pad ($\theta_{JC,bot}$) | 1 to 10 K/W | Part choice |
| Pad → top copper (solder) | about 0.5 to 2 K/W | Solder coverage, voiding |
| Vias to inner/bottom copper | 5 to 30 K/W | Via count, diameter, fill |
| Spreading in copper | 10 to 30 K/W | Copper area and weight |
| Board → air | 10 to 80 K/W | Board area, airflow |

The last one dominates in still air. A common mistake is to optimise the first three and ignore the fourth.

### Thermal vias

A plated barrel's thermal resistance is $R = h/(k\,\pi d\,t_p)$: for $d = 0.3$ mm, $t_p = 25$ µm and $h = 1.6$ mm, $A = 2.36\times10^{-8}$ m² and $R = 176$ K/W each. Twelve in parallel give 14.7 K/W and 25 give 7.1 K/W. If filled with copper or conductive epoxy the area becomes $\pi d^2/4 = 7.1\times10^{-8}$ m², about 59 K/W each, or 4.9 K/W for twelve. Typical guidance is a grid at 1.0 to 1.5 mm pitch under the exposed pad, 0.3 mm drill. Open vias can wick solder away from the pad, so many assemblers want them tented, plugged or filled, or placed with a smaller drill; check your assembler's rules.

### Spreading

From the pad out, copper behaves like the plane in [[q0535]]: a radial resistance $\ln(r_2/r_1)/(2\pi kt)$. For $r_1 = 3$ mm, $r_2 = 15$ mm this is 19 K/W in 1 oz and 9.5 K/W in 2 oz. Two or three layers in parallel lower it further, which is why vias to inner planes help twice: they shorten the vertical path and add spreading layers.

### The air side

Natural convection plus radiation gives roughly $h \approx 10$ W/(m²·K) for a board in still air. Then:

$$\theta_{board\to air} = \frac{1}{hA}$$

For 50 mm × 50 mm with copper (and surface) on both sides, $A = 5\times10^{-3}$ m², so 20 K/W; for 25 mm × 25 mm it is 80 K/W; at $h = 20$ it halves. So a 2 W part on a 25 × 25 mm board would rise about 160 °C above ambient in still air, regardless of its via array. Forced air, a bigger board, a metal enclosure used as a spreader, or a heat sink are the remedies ([[q0541]]).

## A worked example

Part: QFN regulator, dissipating 1.5 W, ambient 50 °C, $T_{j,max}=125$ °C, so the maximum allowed rise is 75 K, i.e. $\theta_{JA}\le50$ K/W.

1. Datasheet minimum-copper board: $\theta_{JA}=70$ K/W → $T_j = 50 + 105 = 155$ °C: fail.
2. Solid connection of the pad to a 15 × 15 mm top pour, 12 vias (14.7 K/W) to an inner ground plane and a 30 × 30 mm bottom pour: perhaps $\theta_{JA}\approx 40$ K/W → $T_j = 110$ °C: pass with 15 °C margin.
3. Change copper weight to 2 oz on outer layers: spreading halves, maybe $\theta_{JA}\approx35$ K/W: $T_j=102$ °C.
4. If still not enough, reduce the dissipation (higher efficiency, lower input) or add a heat sink.

(Planning numbers only; the $\theta_{JA}$ values are illustrative. Measure the real board.)

## Placement and the neighbours

- **Spread hot parts apart.** Two 1 W parts 5 mm apart share a thermal footprint; moved apart, each gets its own copper and airflow.
- **Use the edges and the airflow.** Parts at the board edge radiate and convect on three sides and are in the enclosure's flow.
- **Keep sensitive parts cool.** Voltage references and crystals drift with temperature; electrolytic capacitor life halves for roughly every 10 °C. Batteries and sensors need protection from conducted heat.
- **Do not pack heat sources in a row.** Heat from the upstream part warms the downstream part.
- **Keep copper under hot parts continuous.** Do not cut the plane with slots or antipads beneath the thermal pad.
- **Use both sides.** A bottom-side pour under the part, with vias, doubles the radiating area.
- **Use the enclosure.** A thermal pad to the metal case turns the whole case into a heat sink.

## Verification

Measure, don't trust: put a thermocouple on the package top, or use the datasheet's $\Psi_{JT}$ (junction-to-top characterisation) to infer $T_j$; run at the worst-case ambient and load; use a thermal camera for hot spots and for neighbours. Note that the camera reads surface emissivity; paint or tape improves accuracy.
:::

::: applications
- **Buck and LDO regulators in QFN or DFN packages** with exposed pads soldered to top pours and thermal via arrays.
- **Power MOSFETs and motor drivers**, using large pours on the drain tab and sometimes the bottom side, with extra copper weight.
- **LED drivers and LED metal-core PCBs**, where heat removal sets lifetime and brightness.
- **Processors and FPGAs**, which use inner planes, thermal vias and an enclosure heat spreader.
- **Battery chargers** (linear charge paths dissipate $(V_{in}-V_{bat})I$) where thermal regulation reduces charge current as the board heats.
- **Power amplifiers** in RF and audio boards, with vias and large pours under the transistor.
:::

::: assumptions
The numbers assume FR-4, 1.6 mm board thickness, 1 oz outer copper, 0.3 mm vias with 25 µm plating, an equivalent convective coefficient near 10 W/m²·K and still air. All of these vary: $\theta_{JA}$ differs by part and board; filled and capped vias differ; forced air changes $h$ by an order of magnitude; metal-core or ceramic boards change the entire picture. I also assumed steady state. For short bursts, the thermal mass of the copper and the board lowers the temperature rise, and a transient thermal-impedance curve ($Z_{th}$) from the datasheet applies. An alternative to optimising the PCB is to move the problem elsewhere: a different topology, lower input voltage, a bigger package, or a heat sink.
:::

::: followups
### How many thermal vias do you need under an exposed pad?
Enough that the via array's thermal resistance is a small fraction of the whole chain. With 0.3 mm vias at roughly 176 K/W each, 12 vias give about 15 K/W; going from 12 to 25 saves 7 K/W which may or may not matter. I would start with the datasheet recommendation (often a 3 × 3 or 4 × 4 grid at 1.0 to 1.2 mm pitch), then check the simulation or temperature. More than the pad area allows is not useful.

### Why not just use a thermal relief on the exposed pad so the part is easy to solder?
Because the relief puts several tens of K/W of series resistance exactly where you want almost none, which raises $T_j$. The pad is solidly connected and assembly is handled by the solder paste stencil and reflow profile. See [[q0538]].

### How can you cool a part that has no exposed pad?
Heat leaves mainly through its leads and package body. Connect the ground and power pins with wide copper and several vias, use a larger pour on pins that carry the heat (like a TO-252's tab), put a small heat sink or thermal pad on top, and use airflow. Choose a package with a pad if dissipation is significant.

### The camera shows the board hot but the IC is cool. Is that a problem?
Not necessarily. It means the board is spreading heat well and the junction-to-board path is not the bottleneck. But a hot board raises the temperature of neighbours and shifts $T_a$ for them, so check temperature-sensitive parts nearby and that the board's total power fits within its natural-convection capacity.

### If you could only change one thing on a hot LDO board, what would it be?
Reduce the dropout voltage if possible, because dissipation scales directly with $V_{in}-V_{out}$ and that benefits every link. If the supply is fixed, I'd swap to a buck or add a series dropping resistor or a pre-regulator. If the part has to stay, more copper plus a via array is the next best option.
:::

::: interpretations
"Reduce heating of a component/IC" can mean either reduce the heat the component *makes* or reduce its *temperature* by moving heat away. I cover both, and I treat the board as the main route. Related: heat sinks ([[q0541]]), thermal relief ([[q0538]]), the broader "why heat matters" ([[q0332]]) and heat density in multiphase ([[q0242]]).
:::
