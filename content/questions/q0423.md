::: testing
Whether you can compare two power stages on engineering axes rather than recite "one has two transistors and the other has four". The interviewer wants a structured trade-off: what each can do (voltage polarity, braking, regeneration), what it costs (parts, drivers, conduction loss, board area), and when you pick which. The hidden sub-skills are:

1. Seeing that the difference is the **load reference**: a half-bridge drives a load against a fixed rail, an H-bridge floats the load between two legs.
2. Quantifying: peak load voltage, power delivered, conduction loss.
3. Knowing the escape hatches (split rail, three legs for BLDC) so you don't claim a half-bridge "can't" do something it can with extra parts.
:::

::: why
Choosing between a half-bridge and an H-bridge is a real design decision: a fan, a heater, a solenoid or a buck converter needs one leg; a reversing motor, a voice coil, a class-D BTL amplifier or a transformer drive needs two; a BLDC needs three. Choosing wrong costs board area and money (too many FETs) or capability (no reverse, no brake). Interviewers like this question because there is no single right answer; they want to see the reasoning.
:::

::: concepts
- **Half-bridge**: two switches, one output node; the load returns to a fixed rail ([[q0421]]).
- **H-bridge**: two half-bridges; the load floats between the outputs ([[q0420]]).
- **Bipolar voltage from one supply**: only a floating load across two legs gets $\pm V_M$ ([[q0422]]).
- **Conduction loss**: $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ per FET in the current path ([[q0093]]).
- **Braking and regeneration**: how each topology can short or reverse the motor ([[q0424]]).
- **Gate-drive count**: each leg needs a high-side and low-side drive with dead time ([[q0206]]).
:::

::: answer
"The half-bridge is one leg: two FETs and a single output node. It can drive a load between that node and a fixed rail, so a motor on a half-bridge only sees zero to the supply voltage and only spins one way. It's cheaper, has one FET in the current path, and it's all you need for a fan, a heater, a solenoid or a buck converter.

The H-bridge is two legs, four FETs, and the motor floats between the two outputs. That gives me plus or minus the full supply from a single rail, so I get both directions, active braking and regeneration back to the supply. The price is twice the parts, twice the gate drivers, two FETs in series in the current path so double the conduction loss, and more ways to shoot through.

You can get a bipolar output from a half-bridge by returning the load to a mid-point made of two capacitors, but then the load only sees half the supply and the capacitors carry the full load current, so it's not free. My rule: unidirectional load, use a half-bridge; need reverse or full-voltage bipolar drive, use an H-bridge; three-phase motor, three half-bridges."
:::

::: technical
**Load voltage range** ($V_M$ supply):

| Configuration | $v_{load}$ | Peak power into resistive load $R$ |
|---|---|---|
| Half-bridge, load to ground | $0\ldots V_M$ | $V_M^2/R$ |
| Half-bridge, load to $V_M/2$ (split caps) | $-V_M/2\ldots+V_M/2$ | $V_M^2/(4R)$ |
| H-bridge | $-V_M\ldots+V_M$ | $V_M^2/R$ |

Per unit supply voltage, the H-bridge delivers 4 times the peak power of a split-rail half-bridge, and the same peak power as a ground-referenced half-bridge but in both polarities.

**Conduction loss at current $I$.** Half-bridge: one FET carries the current at a time: $P=I^2R_{DS(on)}$. H-bridge: two FETs in series: $P=2I^2R_{DS(on)}$. For 5 A and 20 mΩ: 0.5 W vs 1.0 W. If the H-bridge must deliver the same load voltage from the same supply, it needs larger FETs or lower $R_{DS(on)}$ to match.

**Switching events.** A half-bridge with PWM has two transitions per period. An H-bridge in sign-magnitude drive has the same number (one leg switching); in lock-anti-phase both legs switch, doubling switching loss and ripple.

**Parts.** Half-bridge: 2 FETs, 2 gate-drive channels (one floating), 1 bootstrap. H-bridge: 4 FETs, 4 channels, 2 bootstraps (or a charge pump), 4 body diodes, a sense resistor and more layout area. Integrated motor drivers hide this but the die and cost scale similarly.

**Braking and regeneration.** Half-bridge with ground-referenced load: turning on the low side shorts the motor (brake) but the motor can't be driven in reverse or plugged. H-bridge: brake, coast, reverse, plug and regenerate ([[q0424]]).

**Failure modes.** Shoot-through can occur in either leg: the H-bridge has two legs and four commutation edges to protect.

**Supply stress.** In a half-bridge with a mid-rail return, the capacitors carry the load's AC current, and regenerated energy raises one cap and lowers the other (balance problem).
:::

::: short
"A half-bridge is one leg of two FETs; it drives a load between its output and a fixed rail, so the load sees zero to the supply and a motor only turns one way. An H-bridge is two legs with the load floating between them, which gives plus or minus the full supply, so reverse, brake and regeneration, but it needs twice the FETs and drivers, and has twice the conduction loss. Use a half-bridge for fans, solenoids and bucks, an H-bridge for reversing motors and bipolar drive."
:::

::: long
"The way I think about it is: what does the load connect to? In a half-bridge there's one output node, so the load has to return to something fixed, a rail or a mid-point. In an H-bridge there are two output nodes, so the load floats between them and I control both ends.

Capability first. With a half-bridge and the load to ground, the load only sees zero to the supply voltage, so a motor turns one way. I can brake it by turning on the low side, but I can't reverse it. If I want both directions I either add a second leg, or I make a mid-rail with two capacitors, in which case I only get plus or minus half the supply and the capacitors carry the motor current, which is a bad deal at high current. The H-bridge gives me the full plus or minus supply, so I get forward, reverse, brake, coast, and regeneration, with four-quadrant operation.

Cost next. The half-bridge has two FETs and one floating gate driver. The H-bridge has four FETs, two half-bridge drivers and more layout. In the current path there's one FET for the half-bridge and two for the H-bridge, so at 5 amps with 20 milliohm FETs that's half a watt versus a watt of conduction loss, which matters on a small board.

Then control complexity and risk. The H-bridge has more states, and more ways to shoot through. In lock-anti-phase drive both legs switch, so switching loss and ripple go up.

So my selection: unidirectional loads like fans, heaters, solenoids and any buck converter use one half-bridge. Reversing brushed motors, bipolar stepper windings, voice coils and class-D speakers use an H-bridge. A BLDC uses three half-bridges. And if the power level is high enough that FET losses matter, I'd pick a driver that makes synchronous rectification easy."
:::

::: terms
- **Half-bridge**: two FETs, one output node; the load returns to a rail.
- **H-bridge (full-bridge)**: two half-bridges; floating load between outputs.
- **Four-quadrant operation**: positive and negative speed with positive and negative torque (drive and brake in both directions).
- **BTL (bridge-tied load)**: a load across two outputs of an amplifier, an H-bridge in audio.
- **Split rail**: two capacitors creating a mid-point reference at $V_M/2$.
- **Plugging**: reversing the drive voltage while the motor is still turning ([[q0424]]).
- **Conduction loss**: $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ in the FETs in the current path.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "H-bridge has four transistors, can reverse; half-bridge has two, can't."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts from the **load reference**, which explains everything else.
- Gives numbers: $\pm V_M$ vs $0\ldots V_M$, 0.5 W vs 1.0 W conduction loss, 4$\times$ power vs split rail.
- Names the escape hatches (split rail, three legs for BLDC) and their costs.
- Compares braking, regeneration and shoot-through exposure.
- Ends with a decision rule tied to real loads.
- Notes that integrated drivers make the parts-count argument weaker and the thermal and package argument stronger.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"A half-bridge can't brake."** With the load tied to ground, turning on the low side shorts the motor; it just can't reverse or plug.
- **"The H-bridge has the same loss as the half-bridge."** It has two FETs in series in the current path.
- **Ignoring the split-rail option.** It exists, but halves the load voltage and loads the capacitors.
- **Equating "more FETs" with "more power".** Power capability is set by the supply voltage, FET current rating and cooling, not the leg count.
- **Forgetting that three half-bridges (BLDC inverter) are not "three H-bridges".** Each motor phase shares the other legs.
:::

::: deep
## The core difference: how many ends of the load you control

A half-bridge fixes one end of the load (to a rail) and controls the other with PWM. An H-bridge controls both ends. That single difference determines polarity, braking and cost.

## What you get from each

| Capability | Half-bridge (load to rail) | Half-bridge (split rail) | H-bridge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Forward drive | yes | yes | yes |
| Reverse drive | no | yes (half voltage) | yes (full voltage) |
| Short-circuit brake | yes (one rail) | no (either switch applies $\pm V_M/2$) | yes |
| Plug (reverse-voltage brake) | no | yes (half voltage) | yes |
| Regenerative return to supply | one direction of rotation only (synchronous switching) | via capacitors | yes |
| FET count | 2 | 2 + 2 caps | 4 |
| FETs in current path | 1 | 1 + cap | 2 |
| Typical use | fan, heater, solenoid, buck | audio (class-D half-bridge), simple drive | brushed motor, stepper coil, voice coil, BTL, transformer |

## Cost and loss numbers

Suppose a 12 V, 5 A load with 20 mΩ FETs.

- **Half-bridge**: conduction $=25\times0.02=0.5$ W per conducting FET. Over the cycle, high side runs $D$ and low side $1-D$; the total is 0.5 W.
- **H-bridge**: two FETs conduct in series: $25\times0.04=1.0$ W.
- To match the half-bridge's loss, the H-bridge needs FETs with half the $R_{DS(on)}$ (10 mΩ), which is roughly twice the die area per FET and four FETs: four times the silicon.

That is why, for a unidirectional load, an H-bridge would be wasteful. For the motor that needs reverse, nothing else gives full voltage.

## What a split rail really costs

The split rail makes a virtual ground from two capacitors. The load current flows into or out of the capacitor mid-node. With 5 A of PWM-modulated current in each direction, the capacitors must handle a large ripple current $I_{RMS}$ and their value sets the mid-point ripple. For an audio amplifier, a single half-bridge with a split-rail supply (a real ±V supply) is common; with capacitors only, the DC component of the load current charges one capacitor and discharges the other, so a motor that holds torque in one direction drifts the mid-point away from $V_M/2$ unless something balances it. That is why a "split capacitor" is fine for AC loads and poor for DC motors.

## Where the half-bridge actually wins

- **Synchronous buck converter**: a half-bridge with an LC ([[q0201]]). The output is only ever positive.
- **Unidirectional motor or fan**: one leg and a freewheel path.
- **Solenoid, heater, lamp, LED driver**: all are fine with ground-referenced drive.
- **Three-phase loads**: three half-bridges. A BLDC or PMSM inverter ([[q0363]]) is not three H-bridges because each leg serves two phases.

## Where the H-bridge wins

- **Reversible brushed DC motor**: both directions and four-quadrant braking.
- **Bipolar stepper winding**: each coil needs both current directions.
- **Voice-coil and linear actuator**: force direction follows current polarity.
- **Class-D BTL amplifier**: doubles the effective output swing on a single rail.
- **Transformer and wireless-power drive**: needs symmetric AC from a DC rail (volt-seconds balance).

## Design process in an interview

Say the questions you'd ask: does the load need to reverse? Does it need active braking or regeneration? What are the supply voltage and current? What is the cost and board-area budget? Is a motor-driver IC available at this power level? Then give the recommendation, plus what changes it: "if the supply is a battery and the motor decelerates a lot I'd also want regenerative braking capability, which is the H-bridge, and I'd check the battery's charge-acceptance." That shows you reasoning on constraints.

## Integrated drivers blur the line

A modern brushed-motor driver IC includes the H-bridge FETs, gate drive, charge pump, current sense and protection, with two to four logic inputs. A half-bridge IC usually breaks out the output so you can use it as a unidirectional driver, or combine two in parallel for current. At low power the "parts count" argument vanishes and the argument becomes die area, package thermal limits and the number of pins on the connector. Many such ICs also let you tie the two half-bridges in parallel to double the current, which gives you a half-bridge with twice the current rating.
:::

::: applications
- **Half-bridge**: synchronous buck converters, fan and pump drivers, solenoid/valve drivers, class-D audio from a split rail, LLC converter primary.
- **H-bridge**: robot and toy drive motors, power windows, steering assist actuators, bipolar stepper coils.
- **Full-bridge audio (BTL)**: higher output swing from a single supply.
- **Three half-bridges**: drone and appliance BLDC inverters.
- **Four-switch buck-boost**: two half-bridges around an inductor; the same hardware as an H-bridge, used for DC-DC conversion.
:::

::: assumptions
- Same supply, same FET technology, same switching frequency in the comparison.
- "Half-bridge" means a leg driving a load to a fixed rail; "H-bridge" means a full bridge with a floating load.
- The motor is brushed DC unless stated; for BLDC, three half-bridges are the standard answer.
- Cost is dominated by FETs and drivers; at low power an integrated IC makes the cost similar.

If the interviewer says the load is a **solenoid** or **heater**, the answer is clearly a half-bridge (or even a single low-side switch). If they say **bidirectional with full voltage and regeneration**, it's an H-bridge. If they say **"cheapest possible"** and the motor only turns one way, a single low-side FET with a flyback diode is cheaper still.
:::

::: followups
### Can you build an H-bridge from two half-bridge ICs?
Yes, that's exactly what an H-bridge is. Use two half-bridge drivers (or two phase legs of a three-phase driver), tie their supplies and grounds, and put the motor between the outputs. The control logic provides complementary signals for each leg and dead time.

### What's the cheapest way to drive a motor one way with PWM and braking?
For PWM alone, one low-side NFET with a flyback diode across the motor. That circuit cannot actively brake, though: with the FET off the diode only lets the current freewheel, and the motor coasts. To brake, replace the diode with a second FET across the motor (or drive the motor from a half-bridge), so you can short the winding on command. That is a half-bridge with the motor returned to the supply, and it costs one extra FET and a gate drive.

### How do you get both polarities with a half-bridge?
Two ways: a bipolar supply (±V) with the load to ground, which needs two rails, or a split capacitor rail with the load to the mid-point, which halves the swing and loads the capacitors. Otherwise add a second leg.

### The H-bridge runs hotter than expected at the same current as a previous half-bridge design. Why?
Two FETs in series carry the current, so conduction loss doubles for the same $R_{DS(on)}$; in lock-anti-phase drive both legs also switch, doubling switching loss. Reduce $R_{DS(on)}$, use sign-magnitude drive, or reduce $f_{sw}$.

### What if the supply is a bench supply with no sink capability? Which topology is riskier?
Both are at risk when the motor regenerates, but the H-bridge is more likely to be commanded to brake or plug, which returns more energy. You need a bulk capacitor or a clamp on the supply, plus a deceleration profile that respects what the bus can absorb.
:::
