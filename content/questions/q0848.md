::: testing
Whether you can contrast the two sensors on **what they measure, how errors behave, and in what frequency band each is trustworthy**, and then turn that into the fusion argument. The interviewer is not asking for definitions only; they want the reasoning "gyro is good at high frequency, bad at low; accelerometer is the opposite; combine them". Strong answers include the complementary-filter idea with a time constant, the failure modes (linear acceleration corrupts tilt, bias integrates into drift) and the yaw observability limit.
:::

::: why
This is the most common IMU question in interviews because it tests sensor physics, error modelling and basic filter design in one go. In practice every orientation estimator, whether in a phone, a drone or a VR headset, is built on this complementarity. If you can explain it on a whiteboard with one equation and one number, you show that you understand why IMUs are packaged together and what a hardware engineer should demand from the sensors (low gyro bias instability, good accelerometer noise, synchronised sampling).
:::

::: concepts
- **Accelerometer**: measures specific force; with the device static it gives the gravity direction. See [[q0846]].
- **Gyroscope**: measures angular rate; integrate for angle. See [[q0846]].
- **Integration drift**: bias times time for angle, and bias times $t^2/2$ for position. See [[q0849]].
- **Low-pass and high-pass filters**: how to split the frequency bands. See [[q0129]].
- **Complementary filter / Kalman filter**: the fusion machinery. See [[q0845]].
- **Observability**: yaw cannot be recovered from gravity.
:::

::: answer
"They measure different things and fail in opposite ways, which is exactly why they are used together.

An accelerometer measures specific force, so at rest it reads gravity, and that gives me an absolute tilt reference that never drifts. But it also reads any real acceleration. If the device is a car accelerating at 0.3 g, the accelerometer thinks it is tilted by about 17 degrees. It is also noisy sample to sample. So it is trustworthy at low frequencies, on average over seconds, and not trustworthy during motion.

A gyroscope measures rotation rate. It is smooth, has low noise, and responds instantly, so for short times integrating it gives an excellent angle. But every gyro has a bias, and integrating a constant bias gives an error that grows linearly forever. Half a degree per second is 30 degrees after a minute. So it is trustworthy at high frequencies and not at low ones.

Put them together with a complementary filter: take the gyro for fast changes, and let the accelerometer slowly pull the estimate back to gravity. That is angle equals alpha times, the previous angle plus gyro times dt, plus one minus alpha times the accelerometer angle. With alpha 0.98 at 100 hertz the time constant is half a second.

One more difference: neither can measure yaw drift when level, because gravity does not change when you rotate about the vertical. For heading you add a magnetometer or GPS."
:::

::: technical
**Comparison.**

| | Accelerometer | Gyroscope |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | specific force (m/s² or g) | angular rate (°/s) |
| Gives orientation by | direct (gravity vector) | integration |
| Dominant error | noise, linear acceleration, vibration | bias and bias instability (drift) |
| Good at | low frequency, long term | high frequency, short term |
| Bad at | during manoeuvres | long-term (unbounded) |
| Yaw | cannot observe | can measure rate, but drifts |
| Typical noise | 100 to 300 µg/√Hz (consumer) | 0.005 to 0.02 °/s/√Hz (consumer) |

**Errors in numbers.**
- Accelerometer tilt noise: 150 µg/√Hz over a 50 Hz bandwidth is $150\,\mu g\cdot\sqrt{50}=1.06$ mg rms, i.e. $1.06\times10^{-3}$ rad $=0.061°$ of tilt noise per sample.
- Linear acceleration error: forward acceleration $a_x$ appears as pitch error $\arctan(a_x/g)$; 0.3 g gives 16.7°.
- Gyro angle random walk: 0.01 °/s/√Hz gives $\sigma_\theta=0.01\sqrt{t}$, 0.077° after 60 s (small).
- Gyro bias: 0.5 °/s gives $0.5t$: 30° after 60 s (dominant). Bias, not noise, is the issue.

**Complementary filter.**

$$\hat\theta_k=\alpha(\hat\theta_{k-1}+\omega_k\Delta t)+(1-\alpha)\theta_{a,k},\quad\tau=\frac{\alpha\Delta t}{1-\alpha},\quad f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}.$$

$\alpha=0.98$, $\Delta t=10$ ms: $\tau=0.49$ s, $f_c=0.32$ Hz. Steady-state error from gyro bias $b$: $b\tau=0.245°$ for 0.5 °/s. Accelerometer noise is attenuated by about $\sqrt{(1-\alpha)/(1+\alpha)}$ in standard deviation terms $=0.1$ (first-order smoothing), so 0.061° becomes about 0.006°.

**Why both are needed:** accelerometer alone fails when moving (0.3 g gives 16.7° error); gyro alone fails after seconds to minutes (drift); together they give a drift-free, smooth angle for pitch and roll. For yaw add a magnetometer, GPS heading or vision.
:::

::: short
"An accelerometer measures specific force, so at rest it gives an absolute gravity reference for tilt with no drift, but it is noisy and corrupted by real acceleration. A gyroscope measures rotation rate, so integrating it gives a smooth, responsive angle, but bias integrates into unbounded drift. Their errors are complementary, so a complementary or Kalman filter trusts the gyro at high frequency and the accelerometer at low frequency. Neither can fix yaw when level, so a magnetometer is added."
:::

::: long
"They measure different physical things and fail in opposite ways.

The accelerometer measures specific force. At rest that is gravity, so it gives me an absolute reference for pitch and roll that never drifts. But it measures all acceleration, not just gravity. If a car accelerates at 0.3 g, the accelerometer reads as if the car is tilted by about 17 degrees. Vibration and sample noise also show up. So it is good on average over long times and bad during motion, a low-frequency sensor.

The gyroscope measures rotation rate. It is smooth, low-noise and instantaneous, so integrating it gives an excellent angle over short times. But all gyros have a bias, and integrating a constant gives a ramp: half a degree per second is 30 degrees in a minute. So it is good at high frequency and bad at low frequency.

Because the errors live in opposite bands, I can fuse them. The simplest scheme is a complementary filter. Each cycle, I propagate the angle with the gyro, then nudge it toward the accelerometer angle: theta equals alpha times theta plus omega dt, plus one minus alpha times theta accelerometer. With alpha 0.98 at 100 hertz, the time constant is about half a second, so changes faster than about a third of a hertz come from the gyro and slower ones from gravity. A gyro bias of half a degree per second then gives a steady error of only about a quarter of a degree, instead of unbounded drift. A Kalman filter does the same thing with optimal weights and can estimate the bias.

The limitation is yaw. Rotating about the vertical axis does not change the gravity vector, so the accelerometer cannot correct yaw drift, and I need a magnetometer, GPS or vision.

On the hardware side, I want synchronised sampling, low gyro bias instability, and mounting and filtering that keep vibration out."
:::

::: terms
- **Specific force**: what an accelerometer measures: $\vec a-\vec g$.
- **Angular rate**: what a gyro measures: rotation speed about an axis.
- **Bias / bias instability**: constant offset in the output and its slow wander.
- **Angle random walk (ARW)**: gyro white-noise integrated into angle error growing as $\sqrt t$.
- **Complementary filter**: high-pass on gyro, low-pass on accelerometer, summing to 1.
- **Crossover frequency**: $f_c=1/2\pi\tau$, where trust passes from accelerometer to gyro.
- **Observability**: whether a state (e.g. yaw) can be recovered from the measurements.
- **Magnetometer**: sensor of magnetic field direction used as a heading reference.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Accelerometer measures acceleration, gyro measures rotation; together they track orientation better."

**An exceptional answer**:
- States **what each measures** (specific force vs rate) and the **dominant error** of each (motion corruption, bias drift).
- Frames it in **frequency**: accelerometer good below the crossover, gyro above it.
- Quantifies with **numbers**: 0.3 g gives 16.7° error; 0.5 °/s gives 30° per minute; $\tau$ from $\alpha$ and $\Delta t$.
- Writes the **complementary filter** and states the steady-state bias error $b\tau$, then mentions the Kalman improvement.
- Points out the **yaw observability** problem and the magnetometer fix, and what the hardware should provide (sync, low bias, vibration isolation).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The gyro measures angle"**. It measures rate; angle is the integral and drifts.
- **"Accelerometers measure gravity"**. They measure specific force; gravity is only part of it at rest.
- **Claiming the gyro noise is the drift problem**. The main issue is bias: noise integrates as $\sqrt t$, bias as $t$.
- **Expecting the accelerometer to fix yaw**. It cannot, because rotating about gravity changes nothing.
- **Setting alpha without reasoning about $\tau$**. Alpha is meaningless without the sample period; compute $\tau$ and compare it with disturbance duration and bias tolerance.
:::

::: deep
## Two sensors, two error spectra

Think of each sensor's error as a function of frequency. For tilt:

- The **accelerometer** angle estimate has noise that is roughly white in the sensor bandwidth, plus large motion-induced error at frequencies where the device accelerates (body motion, vibration). Its *low-frequency* mean is correct.
- The **gyro-integrated** angle has noise that grows slowly ($\sqrt t$, angle random walk) and bias error that grows as $t$. In the frequency domain, the integral turns bias into a $1/f$-like error that dominates at low frequency; at high frequency the gyro is excellent.

## Why integration turns a harmless offset into a disaster

An angular rate $\omega(t)=\omega_{true}(t)+b$ integrates to

$$\theta(t)=\theta_{true}(t)+bt.$$

Contrast with noise $n(t)$ of density $N$ (°/s/√Hz): $\theta_n(t)$ has standard deviation $N\sqrt t$. For $N=0.01$ °/s/√Hz and $b=0.5$ °/s, at $t=60$ s: noise 0.077°, bias 30°, a factor of 390. Even a well-calibrated consumer gyro with residual bias of 0.05 °/s gives 3° per minute. Hence gyro *zero-rate calibration* at startup (average the output for a second while stationary) is standard practice.

## Why the accelerometer cannot be trusted during motion

The accelerometer reads $\vec f=\vec a_{lin}-\vec g$. The tilt estimate assumes $\vec a_{lin}=0$. Real devices accelerate: walking, braking, turning, vibration. The pitch error for forward acceleration $a_x$ is $\arctan(a_x/g)$:

| $a_x$ | error |
|---|---|
| 0.05 g | 2.9° |
| 0.1 g | 5.7° |
| 0.3 g | 16.7° |
| 1 g | 45° |

A turning aircraft or car has a sustained centripetal acceleration that tilts the apparent gravity vector, so the accelerometer-only "horizon" leans into the turn. This is the classic reason pilots cannot rely on their inner ear and why attitude indicators use gyros.

## The complementary filter as a frequency split

In the Laplace domain, with $\Theta_g=\Omega/s$ (integrated gyro) and $\Theta_a$ (accelerometer angle):

$$\hat\Theta=\underbrace{\frac{\tau s}{\tau s+1}}_{\text{high-pass}}\Theta_g+\underbrace{\frac{1}{\tau s+1}}_{\text{low-pass}}\Theta_a.$$

Figure: The two paths cross at the filter corner (0.32 Hz for tau = 0.49 s). Below it the output follows the accelerometer's angle; above it, the integrated gyro. The two responses add to exactly 1, so a motion at any frequency is reproduced without distortion if neither sensor has error.

**Choosing tau.** Increase $\tau$ to reject more accelerometer disturbance (longer motions are ignored) at the price of more gyro bias error ($b\tau$) and slower convergence after a startup error. A useful rule: $\tau$ should exceed the duration of typical sustained accelerations (a second for a car, tenths for hand motion) and satisfy $b_{res}\tau<$ the error budget.

## Smarter than a fixed alpha

Real implementations adapt:

1. **Gating**: if $\big|\,|\vec a|-1\,g\,\big|>$ threshold (say 0.1 g), the accelerometer is clearly measuring more than gravity; reduce its weight or skip the correction.
2. **Bias estimation**: while stationary (low gyro variance), average the gyro and subtract; a Kalman filter or Mahony filter integral term estimates bias continuously.
3. **Temperature compensation**: gyro bias moves with temperature; model it or recalibrate.
4. **Magnetometer for yaw** with outlier rejection against magnetic disturbances; GPS or visual heading for vehicles.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked "what would you ask from the hardware to make fusion better?", answer: low gyro bias instability and temperature coefficient, low accelerometer noise density, simultaneous sampling of both (or a timestamp), a stiff, vibration-isolated mount near the centre of mass, and a documented axis alignment so that cross-axis error is small.

## Yaw: the unobservable axis

With the device level, gravity points along $z$ and $\vec g$ is unchanged by rotation about $z$. The accelerometer therefore carries no information about yaw. The gyro's $z$ rate is integrated and drifts; nothing in a 6-axis IMU can correct it. A 9-axis IMU adds a magnetometer measuring Earth's field (about 25 to 65 µT depending on location), whose horizontal component points to magnetic north. Disturbances from nearby motors, steel and currents corrupt it (hard-iron and soft-iron effects), so it needs calibration. In a car, GPS course and wheel odometry are better references.

## Worked example: drone attitude

A quadcopter flight controller samples gyro and accelerometer at 1 kHz. Take $\Delta t=1$ ms. Choose a crossover $f_c=0.5$ Hz: $\tau=0.318$ s, $\alpha=\tau/(\tau+\Delta t)=0.99687$. With a residual gyro bias of 0.1 °/s the steady error is $0.1\times0.318=0.032°$. During a punch-out manoeuvre of 1 g sideways acceleration for 0.5 s, the accelerometer error would be 45°, but the filter's response is $\approx45°\times(1-e^{-0.5/0.318})=35.6°$ if the correction were applied unchecked. That is huge, which is why real firmware gates the accelerometer correction when $|a|\neq1$ g and relies on the gyro during manoeuvres.

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Gyro g-sensitivity and vibration rectification** add bias in vibrating airframes.
- **Cross-axis sensitivity**: a gyro axis slightly responds to other axes' rotation, so axis misalignment matters.
- **Latency mismatch**: if gyro and accelerometer filters have different group delays, the fused angle shows glitches during fast rotations; use the same ODR and filter, or timestamp.
- **Temperature**: both biases move with temperature; the board's heating profile after power-up creates a drifting zero.

::: applications
- **Smartphone orientation** (portrait/landscape, AR overlays): gyro plus accelerometer plus magnetometer fusion.
- **Quadcopter attitude control**: 1 to 8 kHz gyro with accelerometer correction for level hold.
- **Self-balancing robots**: complementary filter on pitch drives the motor controller to stay upright.
- **VR/AR headsets**: gyro for low-latency rotation; accelerometer and cameras to remove drift.
- **Camera gimbals**: gyro for fast stabilisation, accelerometer to define "level".
- **Aircraft attitude and heading reference systems (AHRS)**: IMU plus magnetometer, fused with a Kalman filter.
:::

::: assumptions
- Numbers (150 µg/√Hz, 0.01 °/s/√Hz, 0.5 °/s bias) are representative of consumer MEMS parts or worked-example choices; actual noise and bias depend on the device and temperature.
- The complementary filter is first-order and single-axis, small-angle; full 3-D orientation needs quaternion methods but the same logic.
- Assumes accelerometer and gyro are co-located and time-aligned; separation adds centripetal and latency errors.
- The 16.7° figure treats acceleration as horizontal and perfectly aligned with a body axis; the exact error depends on geometry.
- For higher-grade sensors (fibre-optic gyros, tactical-grade MEMS), the bias is orders of magnitude smaller and the time before accelerometer correction is needed is correspondingly longer.
:::

::: followups
### Why not just use a better gyro and skip the accelerometer?
Better gyros (tactical or navigation grade) cost hundreds to tens of thousands of dollars and still drift. Consumer MEMS gyros drift by degrees per minute. An accelerometer is nearly free (it is in the same package), has no drift on the gravity reference, and costs milliwatts. Even high-end systems use gravity or other aiding sensors to bound error.

### How do you calibrate the gyro bias?
Hold the device still, average the gyro output for one to several seconds and subtract it. Repeat at several temperatures if the temperature coefficient matters. Continuous methods detect stationarity (low variance of the gyro and accelerometer magnitude near 1 g) and update the bias estimate. Kalman or Mahony filters estimate it as part of the state.

### What does the accelerometer give you that the gyro cannot, and vice versa?
The accelerometer gives an absolute gravity reference and thus absolute pitch and roll, and detects shocks and linear acceleration. The gyro gives rotation independently of gravity: fast, smooth, and valid during manoeuvres, and it is the only one of the two with any information about yaw rate.

### What if you can only sample the gyro at 100 Hz but the device rotates at 2000 °/s in bursts?
Two problems: range (a ±250 °/s part clips; choose ±2000 °/s) and sampling (at 100 Hz, a 2000 °/s rotation turns 20° per sample, and integration with rectangular rule loses accuracy). I would raise ODR to kHz rates with the on-chip filter, integrate quaternions with proper higher-order or exact rotation updates instead of small-angle approximations, and use the FIFO to avoid missing samples.

### Scenario: your self-balancing robot slowly leans and then falls over after about a minute, though it balances fine at first. What is going on?
A slowly growing tilt estimate error: either gyro bias is not removed and the filter's accelerometer correction is too weak (alpha too close to 1), or the accelerometer is mounted off axis and sees motor-induced acceleration. Check the zero-rate offset, reduce $\tau$ or estimate bias, verify the "level" calibration, and check the accelerometer filter for vibration from the motors.
:::
