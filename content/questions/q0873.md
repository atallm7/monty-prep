::: basics
### In plain words
Every measurement is wrong by some amount. Characterizing the error means measuring a known reference many times and splitting the mistakes into two kinds. Bias is the average offset, the same every time. Random error is the scatter around that average. The two kinds respond to different fixes, and combining several error sources takes a special rule.

### Picture it
Imagine measuring a table with a tape measure that has stretched a little, using shaky hands. The stretch makes every reading wrong by the same proportion, which is bias. The shaky hands make each reading a bit different, which is random error. Measuring ten times and averaging calms the shaking, but the stretched tape still gives the wrong answer. Only checking the tape against a known ruler, which is calibration, fixes that.

### Words you need
- **Bias**: the average error, repeatable and correctable by calibration.
- **Random error (noise)**: scatter around the average, which averaging reduces.
- **Standard deviation**: the typical size of the scatter of single readings.
- **Standard error of the mean**: the standard deviation divided by the square root of the number of readings. It tells how well you know the average.
- **Root-sum-square (RSS)**: combining independent errors by squaring each, adding, and taking the square root.

### The simplest example
A sensor measures a 5.000 V reference 100 times. The mean is 5.012 V and the standard deviation is 20 mV. The bias is plus 12 mV. The standard error is 20 divided by the square root of 100, which is 2 mV. So the bias is about 12 plus or minus 4 mV, clearly not zero. A single reading still scatters by 20 mV. For combining, independent errors of 3 mV and 4 mV give 5 mV by RSS, not 7 mV, since independent errors partly cancel. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers error budgets, where averaging stops helping, and uses such as guard bands and filter noise settings.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can separate **systematic error (bias)** from **random error (noise)**, quantify each with the right statistic (mean, standard deviation, standard error, confidence interval), combine independent error sources correctly (root-sum-square, not a straight sum), and then say what the numbers are *for*: calibration, filtering, setting spec limits and guard bands, choosing averaging, and feeding noise values into an estimator. The hidden skill is knowing which errors average away and which do not.
:::

::: why
A measurement without an uncertainty is a guess with units. Every downstream decision depends on knowing how wrong the reading can be: whether a part passes production test, whether a controller will be stable, how much margin a battery gauge needs, how a Kalman filter should weight a sensor against a model. In interviews, error characterization is the quickest way to show you think statistically about hardware instead of trusting a single reading.
:::

::: concepts
- **Error definition**: error = measured minus true (or reference). Sign matters; define it once.
- **Bias vs random error**: the mean error is the bias; the spread around the mean is the random part. See [[q0871]] for removing bias.
- **Gaussian statistics**: about 68% of readings fall within ±1σ, 95% within ±2σ, 99.7% within ±3σ if the noise is Gaussian.
- **Standard error of the mean**: $\sigma/\sqrt N$, the uncertainty of the *average*, not of one reading.
- **Combining independent errors**: add in quadrature (RSS); correlated errors add directly.
- **Units and scaling**: keep error in the sensor's output units or as % of reading/full scale, and say which. See [[q0001]] for the basic voltage and current relationships behind most sensor outputs.
- **What to validate**: [[q0872]] lists the properties whose errors you characterize.
:::

::: answer
"I'd start with a reference, feed it to the sensor, record a few hundred readings and compute the error for each one: reading minus truth. The mean of that is the bias, the systematic part, and the standard deviation is the random part. I'd plot a histogram to see whether it's roughly Gaussian or has outliers or a second peak, and plot error against time to check for drift. Then I'd repeat that across the range, temperature and supply, because the bias and noise usually aren't constant.

Once I have those numbers I can do real things. Bias I can calibrate out, so the offset goes in firmware. Random error I can reduce by averaging, with the standard error going down as one over root N, until I hit a floor from drift or 1/f noise. I can combine the independent error sources, reference, gain, offset, temperature and noise, in quadrature to get a total uncertainty and quote it with a coverage factor, say k=2 for about 95%. And I can use it to set the spec, test limits with a guard band, and the measurement noise covariance in a Kalman filter."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.** Error for sample $i$: $e_i = y_i - x_{ref}$. Over $N$ samples at fixed input:

$$\text{bias } \bar e = \frac1N\sum e_i,\qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum (e_i-\bar e)^2},\qquad \text{SEM} = \frac{s}{\sqrt N}.$$

The 95% confidence interval on the bias is about $\bar e \pm t\cdot s/\sqrt N$ with $t\approx2$ for large $N$ (1.98 at $N=100$). For one *new* reading, about 95% of errors fall within $\bar e \pm 1.96\,s$ if the noise is Gaussian.

**Combining.** For independent standard uncertainties $u_i$: $u_c = \sqrt{\sum u_i^2}$; expanded uncertainty $U = k\,u_c$ (usually $k=2$). For fully correlated contributions, add linearly. Worst-case (sum of absolute values) is used for safety-critical limits where distribution is unknown.

**Over range.** Specify error as a combination such as $\pm(a\% \text{ of reading} + b\% \text{ of full scale})$, or characterize each as offset, gain, nonlinearity, noise, tempco. Check that the error is independent of input (homoscedastic) or scale it accordingly.

**Beyond white noise.** Use the Allan deviation to see where averaging stops helping; use a power spectral density to see 1/f and interference peaks; use a histogram or Q-Q plot to check for outliers.

**Uses.** (1) Bias → calibration. (2) Noise → averaging and filter bandwidth choice. (3) Total uncertainty → pass/fail with guard bands. (4) Noise variance $R = s^2$ → Kalman filter. (5) Error budget → which source to attack first.
:::

::: long
"I'd split the error into two kinds, because they behave differently. Systematic error, or bias, is the same every time: offset, gain error, nonlinearity. Random error is different every time: noise.

To measure them I'd use a reference that is much better than the sensor, hold it fixed, and take a few hundred readings. For each one I compute error as reading minus truth. The mean error is the bias. The standard deviation is the noise. The standard error, sigma over root N, tells me how well I know that bias: if sigma is 20 millivolts and I took 100 readings, my bias estimate is good to about 2 millivolts. Then I look at the picture: a histogram to check it's bell-shaped without outliers, and error versus time for drift.

Then I'd do it again across the input range, temperature and supply, because the numbers often change. A sensor can have a small bias at 25 degrees and a large one at 85.

What I do with it: bias goes into calibration, so I subtract it. Noise I can reduce with averaging or a filter, trading it against bandwidth, until the Allan deviation shows a floor from drift. Independent error sources, say reference, gain, offset, temperature and noise, combine as the square root of the sum of squares, and I quote a total with a coverage factor of two for roughly 95 percent. That total sets the spec, the production test limits with a guard band so I don't ship marginal parts, and the measurement noise covariance for any estimator that fuses this sensor with others."
:::

::: terms
- **Bias (systematic error)**: mean error; repeatable and correctable.
- **Random error (noise)**: zero-mean scatter; reduced by averaging.
- **Standard deviation ($\sigma$ or $s$)**: spread of individual readings.
- **Standard error of the mean (SEM)**: $s/\sqrt N$, uncertainty of the average.
- **Confidence interval**: range expected to contain the true value with a stated probability.
- **Root-sum-square (RSS)**: combining independent uncertainties as $\sqrt{\sum u_i^2}$.
- **Coverage factor (k)**: multiplier that turns a standard uncertainty into an expanded one (k=2 is about 95%).
- **Guard band**: tightening the test limit by the measurement uncertainty so passing parts really pass.
- **Allan deviation**: noise versus averaging time; reveals the bias-instability floor.
- **Error budget**: a table of contributing error sources and their sizes.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "take lots of measurements and compute the average and standard deviation".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Defines error with a sign and separates bias from noise explicitly.
- Distinguishes the standard deviation of a *reading* from the standard error of the *average*.
- Combines sources in quadrature and states a coverage factor.
- Checks Gaussianity and time behavior (histogram, error vs time) and knows white noise averages but drift does not.
- Gives concrete uses: calibrate the bias, set averaging, guard-band the test limits, set Kalman R.
- Puts numbers on all of it.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating the standard deviation as the accuracy.** It measures precision. A big bias with a tiny sigma is still inaccurate.
- **Adding independent errors linearly.** That overestimates; use RSS for independent terms (but add correlated ones directly).
- **Quoting SEM as the single-reading error.** SEM is for the mean; a single reading still has the full $\sigma$.
- **Assuming Gaussian without checking.** Outliers, quantization steps and bimodal noise break the 68-95-99.7 rule.
- **Averaging past the floor.** After the 1/f/drift floor, more samples do nothing.
- **Characterizing at one input, one temperature.** Error often depends on both.
- **Ignoring the reference's own uncertainty.** It sits inside every number you report.
:::

::: deep
## What "error" is

Define error as $e = y_{measured} - x_{true}$. In practice, $x_{true}$ is the reading of a reference with its own uncertainty. If you flip the sign convention between people and spreadsheets, you will correct the offset in the wrong direction, so write it down.

Each reading's error splits into two pieces:

$$e_i = \underbrace{b}_{\text{bias (repeatable)}} + \underbrace{n_i}_{\text{noise (random, zero mean)}}.$$

Bias is anything that is the same every time under the same conditions: offset, gain error, nonlinearity, a temperature shift. Noise is anything that differs from reading to reading. The distinction matters because they respond to different treatments: calibration removes $b$, averaging reduces $n_i$.

## A worked measurement

A voltage sensor reads a 5.000 V reference. You take 100 readings and get a mean of 5.012 V and a standard deviation of 20 mV.

- Bias: $+12$ mV.
- Random error: $s = 20$ mV.
- Standard error of the mean: $20/\sqrt{100} = 2$ mV.
- 95% confidence interval on the bias: $12 \pm 1.98\times2 = 12\pm3.97$ mV, so bias is between about 8 and 16 mV. It is clearly not zero.
- For an individual reading, 95% fall within $12 \pm 1.96\times20 = 12\pm39.2$ mV.

> Always plot it. A mean and a standard deviation can describe a clean bell, a bell with an outlier tail, or two separate peaks (for example a sensor that switches between two ranges). The numbers look the same; the physics is not.

## Combining error sources

A real sensor has several independent contributions. Suppose, after calibration at 25 °C, the leftover standard uncertainties are:

| Source | Standard uncertainty |
|---|---|
| Reference | 2 mV |
| Gain error (residual) | 5 mV |
| Offset (residual) | 3 mV |
| Temperature effect | 4 mV |
| Noise, single reading | 20 mV |

Systematic residuals only (including the reference's 2 mV, which doesn't average away): $\sqrt{2^2+5^2+3^2+4^2} = 7.35$ mV. With single-reading noise: $\sqrt{7.35^2+20^2} = 21.3$ mV. With 100 averaged readings, noise drops to 2 mV and the total becomes $\sqrt{7.35^2+2^2}=7.62$ mV, expanded to $U = 2\times7.6 = 15.2$ mV at about 95%.

Notice two things. First, quadrature means the biggest term dominates: noise at 20 mV makes the 3 mV offset irrelevant until you average. Second, once you've averaged the noise down, the next-biggest term (gain residual, 5 mV) sets the floor, so you work on that next. That ranking is the whole value of an error budget.

## When errors are not simple

**Error that scales.** Many sensors have errors that grow with the reading (gain-type) plus a fixed part (offset-type), so datasheets say "±(0.5% of reading + 2 mV)". Characterize at several input levels and see which form fits.

**Colored noise and drift.** If the noise is not white, successive readings are correlated, and $\sigma/\sqrt N$ is too optimistic. The Allan deviation plot is the right tool: the slope $-1/2$ region is where averaging works; the flat bottom is bias instability; a rising region is drift.

**Quantization.** If the noise is smaller than one ADC LSB, the error is dominated by the quantizer's staircase, and averaging does not help without dither. A 12-bit, 3.3 V ADC has an LSB of 0.806 mV; a signal with 0.1 mV noise just reads a constant, wrong by up to 0.4 mV.

**Hysteresis and history.** Error that depends on direction cannot be summarized by one mean and sigma. Characterize it as a loop.

**Outliers.** One glitch every 10,000 samples can dominate the peak error. Use robust statistics (median, MAD) for diagnosis, but keep the raw data and understand the cause.

## What you can do with the numbers

1. **Calibrate.** Subtract the bias, or fit offset, gain and curvature. See [[q0871]].
2. **Choose averaging and filtering.** Pick $N$ or a bandwidth so noise falls below the other budget terms, and stop there.
3. **Set spec and test limits.** If the production test limit is ±50 mV and the measurement uncertainty is 15.2 mV (k=2), a guard-banded pass limit is $50-15.2=34.8$ mV, so a unit that passes is truly within spec with about 95% confidence.
4. **Feed an estimator.** In a Kalman filter the measurement noise variance is $R = s^2 = (20\text{ mV})^2 = 4\times10^{-4}\ \text{V}^2$. Too small an $R$ makes the filter trust a noisy sensor; too large makes it sluggish.
5. **Decide what to improve.** The budget shows the largest contributor. Don't polish a 1 mV term when a 5 mV term exists.
6. **Detect faults.** A reading more than, say, 5σ from prediction is probably a fault, not noise.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Coverage factor and confidence level conventions differ between fields. k=2 is common and about 95% for Gaussian errors, but some industries use k=3 or require a specific statistical method (for example, per the GUM). Use the convention of the standard or customer you work with.
:::

::: applications
- **Factory test limits**: guard-banding production tests by the measurement uncertainty of the tester.
- **Kalman filters on drones, robots and phones**: sensor noise variance from bench characterization sets the filter gains.
- **Battery fuel gauges**: current-sense offset and gain error accumulate in coulomb counting, so characterizing them sets recalibration strategy.
- **Camera and LiDAR range accuracy**: range bias is calibrated per channel; range noise sets precision, and the two are specified separately (see [[q0870]]).
- **ADC and instrument specs**: effective number of bits, SNR and total unadjusted error all come from the same bias-plus-noise analysis.
- **Thermal and process control**: knowing the noise level lets you set alarm thresholds that do not nuisance-trip.
:::

::: assumptions
I assumed errors that can be modeled as a fixed bias plus zero-mean random noise, with independent contributions combined in quadrature, and approximately Gaussian noise for the 68-95-99.7 numbers. If errors are correlated, add them linearly or use a covariance matrix. If noise is heavy-tailed or has outliers, use robust statistics or report percentiles (for example the 99.9th percentile of absolute error). If the sensor is nonlinear, the bias is a function of input and should be characterized across the range. In safety-critical settings you may be asked for worst-case rather than statistical bounds. A valid alternative answer frames the same material around a standard such as the GUM (type A and type B uncertainty evaluation); the content is the same.
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between standard deviation and standard error?
Standard deviation $s$ describes the scatter of individual readings. Standard error $s/\sqrt N$ describes how well you know their mean. With $s = 20$ mV, one reading is uncertain by 20 mV but the mean of 100 readings by 2 mV. Mixing them up makes a sensor look 10 times better, or worse, than it is.

### Why do independent errors add in quadrature?
Their variances add, $\sigma_{tot}^2 = \sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2$, because independent deviations sometimes cancel. Two 3 mV sources give 4.24 mV, not 6 mV. If two errors share a cause (both scale with temperature, say), they are correlated and you add them directly, up to $\sigma_1+\sigma_2$.

### Your averaging stopped helping at N = 400. Why?
The noise is not white. 1/f noise, drift or a slowly moving reference limits the average, and the Allan deviation will show a flat bottom. Beyond that point, more samples average in the drift. To go lower you need a lock-in or chopping approach, better stability (temperature control), or a differential/ratiometric measurement that cancels the common drift.

### How would you set the measurement noise covariance for a Kalman filter?
Record the sensor at rest in the real system and compute the variance of the output about its mean over the bandwidth the filter will use. For a sensor with 20 mV σ, $R = 4\times10^{-4}$ V². Then tune with real data, since correlated noise and model errors make the effective R larger than the bench number.

### A 50 mV spec, a tester with 15 mV expanded uncertainty. Where do you set the pass limit?
With a guard band the limit is $50 - 15 = 35$ mV, so devices that pass are within spec with high confidence. The cost is yield: good devices between 35 and 50 mV are rejected. A better tester allows a wider limit; that is the economic trade.

### The error is 2 mV at the bottom of the range and 20 mV at the top. How do you characterize it?
That is a gain-type error, scaling with the reading, so I would characterize it as a percent of reading plus a fixed offset term: for example, $\pm(1\%\text{ of reading} + 1\text{ mV})$ fits 2 mV at 100 mV and 20 mV at 1.9 V roughly. Take data at several levels and fit the form.
:::

"Characterize measurement error" can mean the statistical description of the error (what I focused on) or the specification of a sensor's error terms (offset, gain, nonlinearity). ::: rubric
- Defines error as measured minus reference and separates bias from random error :: These two respond to different fixes (calibration vs averaging).
- Computes mean, standard deviation and SEM from repeated readings :: These are the basic statistics that quantify error.
- Distinguishes SEM from single-reading sigma :: Mixing them up misjudges sensor quality by a factor of $\sqrt N$.
- Combines independent contributions in quadrature with a coverage factor :: This is how an error budget is built honestly.
- Checks the distribution and time behavior (histogram, drift, Allan) :: The numbers only mean what they appear to if the noise is well behaved.
- Notes errors vary with input, temperature and supply :: A single number at one condition is not a characterization.
- States what to do with it: calibrate, average, guard-band, estimator R :: The question asks for uses, not only statistics.
