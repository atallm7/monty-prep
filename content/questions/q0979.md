::: testing
Two things at once. First, **honest depth**: do you know what these instruments are, what they are for, what they can't do, and how you would actually use them? Second, **how you talk about experience**. "How familiar are you with…" is a behavioral question: the interviewer wants a calibrated self-assessment, a concrete example for the tools you've used, and a sensible plan for the ones you haven't. Overclaiming on a VNA you've never touched is punished by the first follow-up; saying "I haven't used one, but here's what it measures and how I'd learn it" scores well.
:::

::: why
Hardware roles list instruments as skills, and interviews probe them directly. You usually don't need to be an expert on all four. DMMs and scopes are expected of any intern; VNAs matter for RF roles; SMUs and multi-quadrant supplies matter for power, battery, semiconductor characterization and test roles. What matters is that you can describe each instrument's *principle, limits and a real use*, and that you can reason about an instrument you haven't used by analogy. This page gives you the facts per instrument, a structure for answering honestly, and the follow-up knowledge that backs it up. The generic overview is [[q0978]]; the later pages ([[q0984]] VNA, [[q0986]] DMM, [[q0990]] scope) go deeper.
:::

::: concepts
- **DMM basics**: accurate DC/AC voltage, current and resistance, high input impedance, slow, averaged. See [[q0986]].
- **Scope basics**: voltage versus time, bandwidth, sample rate, triggering, probes. See [[q0990]], [[q0983]].
- **S-parameters and VNAs**: ratios of traveling waves at the ports of a network; need calibration. See [[q0984]].
- **Power supply modes**: constant voltage (CV) vs constant current (CC), and how a supply crosses between them.
- **Quadrants**: whether an instrument can source and sink, at positive and negative voltage and current.
- **Honest self-assessment**: a structure for describing experience without overclaiming. See [[q0964]] for the same framing applied to coding experience.
:::

::: technical
**DMM.** Measures DC voltage, AC voltage (true-RMS or average-responding), resistance (2-wire, sometimes 4-wire), current (shunt in series), continuity, diode drop, often capacitance, frequency and temperature. Input impedance typically 10 MΩ on voltage ranges. Accuracy is quoted as ±(% of reading + % of range or counts). Reading rate typically a few per second for handhelds, up to thousands per second at reduced resolution on bench models. See [[q0986]] to [[q0988]].

**Oscilloscope.** Samples the input with an ADC (commonly 8 bit, 10 to 12 bit on newer models), stores in memory, displays voltage vs time. Specs that matter: analog bandwidth, sample rate, memory depth, vertical resolution, noise floor, trigger capabilities. Rule of thumb: bandwidth ≈ 5× the highest frequency you care about, or $BW\approx 0.35/t_r\times$ 3 to 5 for edges ([[q0980]]). Input: 1 MΩ || ~13 pF, or 50 Ω for RF/high-speed. Probes: passive 10× (about 10 MΩ || ~10 pF at the tip), active, differential, current ([[q0990]]).

**VNA.** Sources a swept or stepped sine and measures incident, reflected and transmitted waves with coherent receivers, giving complex S-parameters (magnitude and phase). Needs calibration (SOLT, TRL, or electronic cal) to move the reference plane to the DUT. Return loss $RL=-20\log_{10}|S_{11}|$ and $VSWR=\frac{1+|S_{11}|}{1-|S_{11}|}$. Typical use: filters, amplifiers, antennas, cables, PCB traces, impedance (via $Z=Z_0\frac{1+\Gamma}{1-\Gamma}$). See [[q0984]].

**Power supplies.** A basic bench supply has CV and CC modes: it regulates voltage until the load current hits the set limit, then regulates current instead. Programmable supplies add remote control, sequencing, OVP/OCP and readback. **Electronic loads** sink current (CC, CV, CR, CP modes). **Multi-quadrant** supplies or amplifiers can source and sink at both polarities (e.g. battery emulators, motor drive testers).

**SMU.** Sources voltage *or* current and simultaneously measures the other with high resolution (µV, pA ranges on precision models), usually four-quadrant, with 4-wire (Kelvin) sensing and fast sweeps. Used for I–V curves of diodes, transistors, LEDs, solar cells, batteries and ICs.
:::

::: terms
- **True-RMS**: a meter that computes the real root-mean-square of the waveform, correct for non-sinusoidal signals.
- **Burden voltage**: voltage dropped across an ammeter's shunt in the circuit.
- **Four-wire (Kelvin) sensing**: separate force and sense leads so lead resistance doesn't affect the measurement.
- **S-parameters**: $S_{11}$ reflection at port 1, $S_{21}$ transmission from port 1 to port 2, and so on.
- **Calibration (SOLT)**: short-open-load-through procedure that removes systematic VNA and cable errors.
- **CV / CC mode**: a supply regulating voltage, or regulating current when its limit is reached.
- **Quadrant**: a region of the V–I plane (sign of voltage and sign of current).
- **SMU**: source-measure unit, a precision source and meter in one.
- **Sink**: absorbing power (current flows into the instrument's output).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "I've used all of them" or "I've used DMMs and scopes, not the rest".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives a **calibrated** self-rating per tool with **one concrete example** each (a ripple measurement, a four-wire resistance, a filter S21).
- States each tool's **limits** (DMM loading and averaging, scope accuracy and bandwidth, VNA calibration, SMU compliance).
- For unfamiliar tools, explains the **principle** and a **learning plan** instead of bluffing.
- Understands **quadrants**: knows a normal supply only sources, an e-load only sinks, a four-quadrant source can do both.
- Shows **practical habits**: probe compensation, ground spring, Kelvin connections, calibration before VNA measurements, current-limit before connecting a DUT.
- Uses an experience framework: situation, instrument, what you did, what you learned.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Overclaiming.** Saying you're an expert with VNAs and then not knowing what calibration is. Be honest about depth.
- **Giving only a feature list.** The interviewer wants what you *did* and what you learned.
- **Treating a bench supply as a perfect source.** It has a current limit, output capacitance, remote sense and sometimes overshoot at turn-on; set the limit *before* connecting a DUT.
- **Calling an SMU "just a power supply".** An SMU forces one quantity and measures the other with precision, and a source of either polarity.
- **Using the scope ground clip on a floating or mains-referenced node.** It's earth-grounded; it can short the circuit.
- **Forgetting VNA calibration.** Uncalibrated S-parameters include cable and connector errors.
:::

::: deep
## How to answer "how familiar are you"

Interviewers hear this question from people with very different experience. A structure that works whatever your level:

1. **Rate yourself plainly**: "daily user", "have used on a project", "understand the concept, not hands-on".
2. **Give one concrete example** for each tool you've used: what you measured, what you saw, what you learned.
3. **Show the principle** for each: one or two sentences on how it works.
4. **Name limits** and a typical pitfall.
5. **For gaps, give a plan**: how you'd get competent in a day (read the manual, measure a known reference, calibrate, compare with another instrument).

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Never invent a project. If you haven't used a VNA, say so and show you understand what it measures; that earns more credit than a bluff that collapses at the first follow-up. If you used it only once, say that, and describe exactly what you did.

## DMMs: more than a voltmeter

A digital multimeter is an ADC with input conditioning: a divider for high voltage ranges, a shunt for current, a current source for resistance, and an AC converter for RMS. The result is a number with an accuracy specification of the form ±(% reading + % range). On a 5 V reading in a 10 V range, ±(0.003 % + 0.0005 %) works out to ±0.0002 V; a handheld at ±(0.5 % + 2 counts) on the same reading is ±25 mV or so. That range of 100× is why bench DMMs cost what they do.

What the DMM cannot do: see anything fast (it integrates for tens of milliseconds to remove mains noise), see waveform shape, or measure at high frequency (AC bandwidth is typically kHz to hundreds of kHz). The DMM simulation below shows two practical limits you should be able to explain: loading and burden.

The rule: the shunt must be much smaller than the circuit resistance; on a low-voltage rail that burden changes the very circuit you are measuring, so use a higher range or a shunt and a voltmeter.

## Scopes: what you can claim

A comfortable scope answer covers: bandwidth and rise time (the scope's own rise time is about $0.35/BW$), sample rate and memory depth, triggering, probes (10× passive, ground spring), coupling (AC vs DC, 1 MΩ vs 50 Ω), averaging and bandwidth limit for noisy signals, and measurements and math (FFT). A good example has this shape (use it only if you really did something like it, with your own numbers): "I measured switch-node ringing on a buck converter. A 20 MHz bandwidth limit hid it; with full bandwidth and a spring ground the ring was about 150 MHz and 3 V peak overshoot."

## VNAs: a clean conceptual answer

A VNA is a network analyzer with *vector* (magnitude and phase) receivers. For a two-port device under test with reference impedance $Z_0$ (usually 50 Ω):

- **S11** is the ratio of the wave reflected at port 1 to the wave incident on port 1: it measures input reflection (return loss, VSWR, impedance).
- **S21** is the wave arriving at port 2 relative to the incident wave at port 1: it measures gain or insertion loss.
- S12 and S22 are the reverse-direction equivalents.

Worked example: a 75 Ω load on a 50 Ω system has $\Gamma=\frac{75-50}{75+50}=0.2$, return loss $=-20\log_{10}0.2=13.98$ dB, $VSWR=\frac{1.2}{0.8}=1.5$, and 4 % of the incident power is reflected (mismatch loss 0.18 dB). That is the kind of number you can quote to show you understand what the instrument is telling you. More in [[q0984]].

The calibration step matters: before measuring you connect known standards (short, open, load, and a through) at the cable ends so the instrument can compute the cables' and connectors' error terms and remove them. Without calibration the reference plane is at the instrument's port, not at your device.

## Power supplies: CV, CC and the crossover

A bench supply is a regulated source with two control loops. In constant-voltage mode it holds the set voltage while the current is below the limit. When the load tries to draw more than the limit, the current loop takes over and the voltage falls: constant-current mode.

Practical points: use remote (4-wire) sense to remove lead drop, check overshoot at turn-on, and know that the supply's output capacitor stores energy. A basic supply can only **source**: it cannot pull the voltage down on a charged load. An **electronic load** only **sinks**.

A basic supply sources in quadrant I. A bidirectional supply or SMU can also sink current (quadrant IV, positive voltage with current flowing in), which lets it charge or discharge a battery or hold a node. SMUs add:

- **Precision measurement** of the quantity not being forced (µV and pA range resolution on the better ones).
- **Compliance limits**: when forcing voltage you set a current compliance, and vice versa, like a CV/CC limit.
- **4-wire sensing** so lead resistance doesn't corrupt forced voltage.
- **Sweeps and pulsed modes** for I–V curves while keeping the DUT cool.

Typical uses: LED and diode I–V curves, MOSFET transfer curves, solar-cell characterization, battery charge/discharge, leakage measurements, and IC parametric test.

## Quick comparison

| Tool | Forces | Measures | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DMM | nothing (small test current for R) | V, I, R | Accuracy, simplicity | Slow, averages |
| Scope | nothing | Waveform | Shape, timing | Accuracy, resolution |
| VNA | RF stimulus | S-parameters | Phase and magnitude, dynamic range | Needs calibration, cost |
| Bench supply | V (limit I) | Often V and I | Power for DUT | Single quadrant, limited accuracy |
| SMU | V or I | I or V | Four-quadrant, precision | Cost, lower power |

::: applications
- **Bring-up**: DMM for rail checks, scope for resets and clocks, supply with current limit to protect a first power-up.
- **Filter design**: VNA S21 sweeps of an LC or SAW filter; compare to simulation.
- **Antenna and cable matching**: VNA S11, return loss and VSWR, Smith chart.
- **Battery and charger testing**: SMU or bidirectional supply to charge, discharge and measure ([[q0291]]).
- **Device characterization**: SMU sweeps of diode, LED and transistor I–V curves ([[q0074]]).
- **Production test**: programmable supplies and DMMs on automated fixtures.

::: assumptions
- This answer assumes you have **some** hands-on experience with the DMM and scope; adapt the examples to what you actually did. Never claim use that didn't happen.
- Quadrant naming uses **V horizontal, I vertical** and "source" when $P=VI>0$ for the instrument's output; some manuals define the sign of current differently, so always check the convention.
- Accuracy and bandwidth numbers are **typical**; real models vary widely.
- "Power supplies, maybe with more functions like multiple quadrants or SMUs" is treated as a family from simple bench supplies to precision four-quadrant instruments.
- For an **RF-focused role**, spend more time on the VNA and calibration; for a **power or battery role**, on supplies, e-loads and SMUs.

::: followups
### What is the difference between a bench power supply and an SMU?
A bench supply is a regulated voltage source with a current limit and basic readback, usually quadrant I only. An SMU forces either voltage or current and measures the other with much higher resolution, can usually sink as well as source (four quadrants), has compliance limits and 4-wire sensing, and supports fast sweeps. Use a bench supply to power a board; use an SMU to characterize a device.

### Why do you calibrate a VNA, and what do the standards do?
Cables, connectors and the instrument's own directivity, source match and tracking errors add to the reading. SOLT calibration measures short, open, load and through standards whose characteristics are known, so the VNA can solve for those error terms and remove them mathematically, setting the reference plane at the cable ends. Without it, S11 of a 50 Ω load might look like a poor match due to the cable.

### Your DMM's current range reads 0 mA but you know the circuit draws current. What could be wrong?
The meter's fuse may be blown (common after measuring current across a voltage source), the leads may be in the voltage jack, or the burden voltage may be so large the circuit can't operate (low-voltage supply browning out). Check the fuse and lead position, and switch to a smaller-burden range or use a shunt plus a voltmeter.

### What is constant-current mode in a bench supply good for?
Protecting the DUT and the supply: a short or unexpected load draws only the set current. Also useful for charging batteries (constant-current phase), driving LEDs and stress testing. The crossover is where the supply stops regulating voltage and starts regulating current.

### Scenario: you must measure the leakage of a capacitor at 5 V, expected around 1 nA. What would you use?
A DMM with a current range typically can't resolve nA with acceptable burden, so use an SMU or picoammeter (source 5 V, measure current), with guarding or shielding and a settling time because dielectric absorption makes leakage decay for seconds to minutes. Use triaxial or shielded cables and keep the fixture clean and dry.
:::
