::: basics
### In plain words
At DC, current spreads through the whole cross-section of a wire. As the frequency rises, the current crowds into a thin layer near the surface, and the middle carries almost none. This is the skin effect. A thinner usable layer means less metal carrying current, so the wire's resistance goes up with frequency. It is a main reason copper loss on a circuit-board trace grows at gigahertz speeds.

### Picture it
Picture a long hallway with a crowd moving along it. When everyone walks slowly, people fill the whole width. When the crowd rushes, everyone hugs the walls and the middle of the hallway stays empty. The hallway is just as wide, but only the edges get used. In a wire the cause is not crowding. A changing current builds a changing magnetic field, which pushes current out of the middle toward the surface.

### Words you need
- **Skin depth ($\delta$)**: how deep into the metal most of the current flows. It shrinks as frequency rises.
- **Eddy currents**: small circling currents the changing magnetic field creates inside the metal. They cancel current in the middle.
- **AC resistance**: a wire's effective resistance at high frequency, higher than its DC value.
- **Conductor loss**: signal energy lost as heat in the copper ([[q0722]]).
- **Surface roughness**: tiny bumps on the copper that lengthen the path current takes, so loss rises further.

### The simplest example
For copper, the skin depth is about $66\ \mu\text{m}/\sqrt{f/\text{MHz}}$. That is 66 µm at 1 MHz, about 6.6 µm at 100 MHz, and about 2.1 µm at 1 GHz. A trace on a circuit board is 35 µm thick. Above about 14 MHz the current uses only a thin skin of it. From 100 MHz to 1 GHz the skin gets about three times thinner, so the resistance rises about three times. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page derives the depth formula, shows why a thicker trace doesn't help but a wider one does, and covers roughness and wire choices.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can explain the skin effect from physics, not just say "current flows on the surface". The interviewer is looking for:

1. The mechanism: changing current makes changing magnetic flux, which induces eddy currents that oppose current in the interior and reinforce it at the surface.
2. The formula $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu)}$ and a number: 2.1 µm for copper at 1 GHz.
3. The consequence for lines: $R'\propto\sqrt f$, loss rising with frequency, and current crowding in a PCB trace and its return plane.
4. Honest awareness of where it shows up: transmission lines, inductors, wires, busbars, and roughness.
:::

::: why
Skin effect is the reason copper loss on a PCB isn't just $\rho L/A$ at gigabit speeds, why a thick trace isn't a fix for HF loss, and why coax center conductors are often silver-plated. It also appears in inductor and transformer winding design, in busbars at tens of kHz, and in EMC. A candidate who can derive the scaling and put a number on it shows they understand loss, not just names it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Faraday's law and Lenz's law**: changing flux induces a voltage that opposes the change. See [[q0022]].
- **Eddy currents**: circulating currents induced inside a conductor by changing flux.
- **Skin depth** $\delta$: the depth where current density has fallen to $1/e$ (37%) of its surface value.
- **AC resistance** $R_{ac}$ vs $R_{dc}$: the effective resistance when current uses only a surface layer.
- **Transmission-line $R'(f)$**: conductor loss $\alpha_c=R'/2Z_0\propto\sqrt f$. See [[q0718]], [[q0722]].
- **Proximity effect** and **surface roughness**: related effects that raise loss further.
:::

::: answer
"The skin effect is the tendency of alternating current to crowd near the surface of a conductor as frequency goes up. The reason is electromagnetic induction: a changing current produces a changing magnetic field, and inside the conductor that field induces eddy currents which oppose the current in the center and add to it near the surface. So at high frequency the current only flows in a thin layer, the skin depth, $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu)}$. For copper that's about 66 µm at 1 MHz, 6.5 µm at 100 MHz and 2.1 µm at 1 GHz.

The effect is that the usable cross-section shrinks, so the resistance goes up as the square root of frequency, since $R\propto1/\delta\propto\sqrt f$.

In transmission lines it's the main conductor loss. A PCB trace is 35 µm thick with the skin depth falling to 17 µm at about 14 MHz, and after that the series resistance per length grows as $\sqrt f$. The current also crowds along the surfaces facing the return plane and at the trace edges, which is why making a trace thicker barely helps at GHz, and why rough copper makes things worse. It also matters for coax center conductors, inductor windings and busbars, which is why people use Litz wire, plated conductors, and smooth copper."
:::

::: technical
**Skin depth.** For a good conductor with conductivity $\sigma=1/\rho$ and permeability $\mu$:
$$\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}}=\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\pi f\mu}}$$
Current density falls as $J(z)=J_se^{-z/\delta}$ (with a phase shift $e^{-jz/\delta}$) from the surface.

**Copper** ($\rho=1.68\times10^{-8}$ Ω·m, $\mu\approx\mu_0$): $\delta\approx66\,\text{µm}/\sqrt{f/\text{MHz}}$.

| $f$ | 60 Hz | 100 kHz | 1 MHz | 10 MHz | 100 MHz | 1 GHz | 10 GHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\delta_{Cu}$ | 8.4 mm | 206 µm | 65 µm | 21 µm | 6.5 µm | 2.1 µm | 0.65 µm |

**Surface resistance.** $R_s=\rho/\delta=\sqrt{\pi f\mu\rho}$ (Ω per square), 8.1 mΩ/□ at 1 GHz for copper.

**Wire.** For radius $r>\delta$, the current occupies an annulus of area $\pi(2r\delta-\delta^2)$, so $R_{ac}/R_{dc}=r^2/(2r\delta-\delta^2)\approx r/2\delta$. For 24 AWG ($r=0.255$ mm, $R_{dc}\approx0.082$ Ω/m): ratio 2.2 at 1 MHz, 6.4 at 10 MHz, 20 at 100 MHz.

**Transmission line.** Above the skin corner, $R'\approx\rho/(w_{eff}\delta)\propto\sqrt f$, so $\alpha_c=R'/2Z_0\propto\sqrt f$. For a 35 µm trace the corner (where $\delta=t/2$) is $f\approx14$ MHz; below it current uses the whole thickness.

**Current distribution under a microstrip.** With a wide plane, return-current density under the trace follows $J(x)\propto1/[1+(x/h)^2]$, so about 80% of return current lies within $\pm3h$ of the trace, and the *plane* has a skin depth too, which is why loss isn't fixed by thick planes.

**Roughness.** When rms roughness $\Delta$ is comparable to $\delta$, current follows the surface contour and $R'$ rises by $K\le2$ (Hammerstad–Jensen model, $K=1+\frac2\pi\arctan[1.4(\Delta/\delta)^2]$).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Immersion-gold-over-nickel (ENIG) surface finishes can raise loss at RF because nickel is magnetic and has a short skin depth. The size of the effect depends on finish thickness and trace geometry; treat it as qualitative unless you have vendor data.
:::

::: long
"Start with induction. If you push an alternating current through a wire, it makes an alternating magnetic field, and the field inside the wire is strongest at the center. By Faraday's law that changing flux induces eddy currents in the copper, and by Lenz's law those eddy currents oppose the change, which means they subtract from the current at the center and add to it near the surface. So the net current gets pushed out toward the surface.

The result is an exponential decay of current density into the conductor, with a characteristic depth, the skin depth, $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/\pi f\mu}$. For copper that's about 8 mm at 60 Hz, 66 µm at 1 MHz, 6.5 µm at 100 MHz, 2 µm at 1 GHz. Once the conductor is thicker than a couple of skin depths, only the outer layer carries current, so the effective cross-sectional area is roughly perimeter times $\delta$, and the resistance rises as one over $\delta$, which is $\sqrt f$.

Where it shows up in transmission lines: it's the dominant conductor loss. The series resistance per length $R'$ is $\rho/(w\delta)$ so attenuation, $R'/2Z_0$, goes as $\sqrt f$. On a PCB, 1 oz copper is 35 µm thick, so skin effect sets in around 14 MHz. The current in a microstrip is also concentrated on the bottom of the trace facing the plane and near its edges, and the return current in the plane is spread under the trace. Making the trace thicker does almost nothing at GHz, whereas widening it helps. Rough copper makes it worse, up to about twice the loss when roughness is comparable to the skin depth.

Outside lines, the same effect shapes inductor and transformer windings, where you'd use Litz wire, and busbars, where it's the reason for laminated or hollow conductors."
:::

::: terms
- **Skin depth** $\delta$: depth at which current density falls to $1/e$ of its surface value.
- **Surface resistance** $R_s$: resistance per square for a conductor much thicker than $\delta$, $\rho/\delta$.
- **Eddy currents**: currents induced in a conductor by a changing magnetic field.
- **Proximity effect**: current redistribution due to the magnetic field of nearby conductors.
- **Litz wire**: many insulated, individually thin strands woven so each strand carries equal current.
- **AC resistance** $R_{ac}$: effective resistance at frequency $f$.
- **Surface roughness**: microscopic copper tooth that lengthens the current path.
- **Skin corner frequency**: frequency at which $\delta$ equals half the conductor thickness.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "high-frequency current flows on the surface, so resistance increases".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the cause (induced eddy currents, Lenz) and gives $J(z)=J_se^{-z/\delta}$ with $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/\pi f\mu}$.
- Puts numbers on it: 66 µm at 1 MHz, 2.1 µm at 1 GHz for copper.
- Derives $R\propto\sqrt f$ and applies it to a trace: corner about 14 MHz for 35 µm copper, $\alpha_c\propto\sqrt f$.
- Notes current crowding at the trace's underside and edges and the return plane's skin effect, and that thickness doesn't fix it but width does.
- Mentions roughness, proximity effect, plating, and non-TL uses (windings, Litz, busbars).
- Contrasts with dielectric loss scaling ($\propto f$).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Current flows only on the very surface."** It decays exponentially; 63% of current flows within $\delta$ and 86% within $2\delta$.
- **Saying it's due to charge repelling itself.** The cause is induction (eddy currents), not electrostatic repulsion.
- **Believing thicker copper fixes high-frequency loss.** Once thickness exceeds $2\delta$ nothing changes. Only perimeter (width) and smoothness matter.
- **Applying skin effect to DC or power-line frequencies in thin conductors.** At 60 Hz, $\delta=8.4$ mm, so it matters only for conductors thicker than about a centimetre.
- **Ignoring the plane.** The return plane has the same skin depth and the same current crowding.
- **Mixing up the scaling with dielectric loss.** Skin effect: $\sqrt f$. Dielectric: $f$.
:::

::: deep
## Why current moves to the surface

Consider a round wire carrying AC. The current produces a magnetic field circling the axis. The field inside the wire is a bit like that inside a solenoid's core: it grows from zero at the center to a maximum at the surface. When the current rises, that field changes with time, and Faraday's law says an electric field is induced, $\nabla\times E=-\partial B/\partial t$. Lenz's law tells us the induced field drives currents that oppose the *change*: along the axis it points against the applied current, and it's strongest where the flux linkage is greatest, near the center. Eddy-current loops are drawn: against the current in the middle, with the return paths along the surface adding to it.

Mathematically, combining Maxwell's equations with Ohm's law inside a good conductor ($J=\sigma E$, displacement current negligible) gives a diffusion equation:
$$\frac{d^2J}{dz^2}=j\omega\mu\sigma J\ \Rightarrow\ J(z)=J_se^{-(1+j)z/\delta},\qquad\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}}$$
So current decays exponentially *and* rotates in phase as it goes in: by a depth of $\delta$ it's down to 37% and has shifted by one radian. That phase lag means the interior current partly cancels out too, which is why the resistance is $\rho/(w\delta)$, not simply that of a layer.

## The same effect on a PCB trace

A 0.39 mm × 35 µm trace has $R'_{dc}=\rho/(wt)=1.23$ Ω/m. At 1 GHz the skin depth is 2.1 µm, far less than the thickness, so the current uses a thin surface layer along the underside (facing the plane) and the edges. If I assume it occupies a skin layer along the bottom width only, $R'\approx\rho/(w\delta)=1.68\times10^{-8}/(0.39\text{ mm}\times2.06\text{ µm})\approx21$ Ω/m, 17 times the DC value. In reality the current also crowds at the edges and spreads over the plane, so numbers from field solvers are roughly 1.5–3× different; use the estimate for scaling only.

The conductor attenuation $\alpha_c=R'/2Z_0=21/100=0.21$ Np/m $=1.8$ dB/m $=0.046$ dB/in at 1 GHz, consistent with the roughly 0.05 dB/in used in [[q0722]].

The return plane's current: the field of a trace above a plane makes the plane current follow $J(x)\propto1/[1+(x/h)^2]$. About 50% of it flows within $\pm h$ of the trace, 80% within $\pm3h$. That current also has to live inside a skin depth of the plane, so the plane contributes loss comparable to the trace unless its width under the trace is large. Moving the reference plane closer (smaller $h$) squeezes the return current, which *raises* its resistance a bit while lowering inductance, an engineering trade-off.

## Worked example: a 500 kHz inductor winding

Say the winding needs the copper area of 20 AWG (0.81 mm diameter, 0.52 mm², $R_{dc}=\rho/A=0.0325$ Ω/m). At 500 kHz, $\delta=\sqrt{1.68\times10^{-8}/(\pi\cdot5\times10^5\cdot4\pi\times10^{-7})}=93$ µm.

1. Solid 20 AWG: radius 0.405 mm is more than four skin depths, so current uses an annulus: $R_{ac}/R_{dc}=r^2/(2r\delta-\delta^2)=2.45$. The winding resistance per meter is 0.080 Ω instead of 0.0325 Ω, and the copper in the middle of the wire carries almost nothing.
2. Litz of 40 strands of 36 AWG (0.127 mm diameter, radius 63.5 µm): strand radius is smaller than $\delta$, so each strand's current is nearly uniform and $R_{ac}/R_{dc}\approx1$ from skin effect alone. Total copper area $40\times\pi(0.0635)^2=0.51$ mm², matching 20 AWG. The Litz bundle has about 2.4 times lower resistance at 500 kHz than the solid wire.

Real Litz has proximity-effect and strand-twisting penalties and costs more, so it only pays off when the skin factor is well above 1.5 or so. That is the engineering point: compute $\delta$, compare it with the conductor radius, and only then decide whether it's worth changing the wire.

## Where else the skin effect appears

- **Inductors and transformers at hundreds of kHz+**: winding resistance rises and Litz wire or foil windings are used. Proximity effect, where adjacent turns push current to the facing sides, often matters more than pure skin effect. See [[q0722]] for the transmission-line viewpoint.
- **Busbars and power cabling**: a copper conductor of 20 mm diameter at 60 Hz has $\delta=8.4$ mm, a modest rise. At 20 kHz switching harmonics it matters.
- **Coaxial cables**: the inner conductor's loss dominates, hence silver plating on RF coax center conductors.
- **Shielding**: a shield thicker than a few skin depths blocks high-frequency fields. That's why thin foil shields work at MHz but not for low-frequency magnetic fields.
- **Waveguides and RF cavities**: wall losses are set by $R_s$, which is why they're silver- or gold-plated.
:::

::: applications
- **PCB loss budgeting**: $\alpha_c\propto\sqrt f$ plus roughness factor in channel models for PCIe, Ethernet and DDR.
- **Litz wire and foil windings** in high-frequency switching transformers (100 kHz–MHz).
- **RF coax**: silver-plated or copper-clad-steel inner conductors (the steel core carries nothing at RF, only the copper skin).
- **Antenna and RF waveguide plating**.
- **EMC shielding**: foil or plated enclosures work because only a few skin depths are needed at high frequency.
- **Induction heating**: deliberately uses skin depth to heat surfaces (case hardening) and select depth by frequency.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Good conductor**: $\sigma\gg\omega\varepsilon$, true for metals well beyond optical frequencies.
- **Linear, nonmagnetic material** ($\mu\approx\mu_0$). Iron and nickel have much larger $\mu$ and much smaller $\delta$.
- **Thickness $\gg\delta$** for $R_s=\rho/\delta$. For thickness comparable to $\delta$, the current uses the whole cross-section partly.
- **Smooth surface**. Roughness changes the effective $R_s$.
- **Skin depth** doesn't capture edge crowding and proximity effects fully in real geometries. Use a field solver for accuracy.
- **Temperature** raises $\rho$ (about 0.4%/K for copper), raising $R_s$ by half that percentage.
:::

::: followups
### What's the skin depth of copper at 1 GHz and at 100 kHz?
$\delta=66\ \text{µm}/\sqrt{f/\text{MHz}}$: 2.1 µm at 1 GHz, 206 µm at 100 kHz. Check: at 100 kHz $f=0.1$ MHz, $66/\sqrt{0.1}=209$ µm, consistent with the exact 206 µm.

### Why does a thicker trace not reduce high-frequency loss?
Above the skin corner the current occupies a layer $\delta$ thick regardless of how thick the conductor is, so extra thickness carries no current. Resistance is set by perimeter times $\delta$. Widening the trace helps because it increases the surface carrying current.

### How does skin effect affect the choice of wire for a 500 kHz inductor?
At 500 kHz, $\delta=66/\sqrt{0.5}=93$ µm. A solid wire thicker than about 0.2 mm wastes copper in the core. So use Litz wire with strands under about 0.1 mm diameter, or foil thinner than $2\delta\approx0.19$ mm. Also watch proximity effect, which grows with winding layers.

### Does the skin effect cause dispersion?
Yes. $R'\propto\sqrt f$ and the matching internal inductance $\omega L_i=R'$ (surface impedance has equal real and imaginary parts) make both attenuation and phase delay vary with frequency, which adds dispersion to the edge response. The effect is small compared to dielectric dispersion on PCBs but present.

### What's the effect of surface roughness on loss at 10 GHz?
At 10 GHz $\delta=0.65$ µm, below typical copper roughness (0.5–2 µm rms for standard foil). The current follows the rough surface, so the roughness factor $K$ approaches its maximum of about 2, doubling conductor loss. Smoother foil (VLP, HVLP) is chosen for that reason.

### Harder: why do some RF engineers worry about ENIG plating, and what's the physics?
Nickel is ferromagnetic ($\mu_r$ of order 100), so its skin depth is about 10× smaller than copper's, and a thin gold layer over nickel at GHz means the current flows partly in lossy nickel when gold is thinner than a few skin depths. Result: higher conductor loss than bare copper or immersion silver. The effect depends on finish thickness, so check the fab's process and your frequency band.
