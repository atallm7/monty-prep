::: basics
### In plain words
A metal wire is full of free electrons that can move. A voltage pushes them along, but they keep bumping into the atoms of the metal, so they never speed up forever. They settle at a steady average drift speed. A bigger push means a faster drift and more current, and resistance is how much the material gets in the way.

### Picture it
Picture a marble rolling down a tilted board covered in pegs. It bounces off pegs all the way down, so it travels at a steady average speed instead of accelerating. Tilt the board twice as steeply and it moves about twice as fast. The tilt is the voltage, the pegs are the atoms, and the marble flow is the current. Real electrons are far more numerous and the drift is very slow, but the logic is the same.

### Words you need
- **Free electron**: an electron in a metal that is not stuck to one atom and can carry current.
- **Electric field**: the push on each charge, caused by the voltage across the material.
- **Collision**: a bump between an electron and an atom or impurity that wastes some of its energy.
- **Drift speed**: the slow average speed at which electrons move along the wire.
- **Resistance**: how hard the material makes it for electrons to move. See [[q0001]].

### The simplest example
Put 1 V across a wire and 1 mA flows. Put 2 V across it. The push is twice as big, so the drift speed is twice as big, and 2 mA flows. The extra energy lost in the collisions becomes heat, which is why resistors get warm. If the wire heats up, its atoms jiggle harder, collisions become more frequent, and the resistance rises. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page puts numbers on the collision model and shows how slow the drift really is.
:::

::: testing
Q1 asked *what* Ohm's law is. This one asks *why* it's true, and the interviewer wants to see a physical model in your head rather than a memorised equation:

1. Can you explain current as charge flowing because a voltage (electric field) pushes it, and resistance as whatever impedes that flow?
2. Can you give a good analogy (water in a pipe) **and** say where the analogy breaks?
3. Do you know the microscopic reason for linearity: electrons drift at a speed proportional to the field because they keep colliding with the lattice?
4. Can you connect the picture to consequences: why $R$ scales with length and area, why resistors get hot, why $R$ rises with temperature.
:::

::: why
Intuition is what lets you debug without a calculator. If you picture current as a flow pushed by voltage through a restriction, you immediately see why a long thin trace drops more voltage, why a hot motor winding draws less current, and why a resistor turns electrical energy into heat. Interviewers ask "conceptually" to separate students who can only plug numbers into $V = IR$ from those who understand what's physically going on, which predicts how you'll handle unfamiliar problems later (device physics, IR drop, thermal design).
:::

::: concepts
- **Voltage as push**: a potential difference sets up an electric field $E$ in the conductor that exerts a force $qE$ on each charge. See [[q0059]].
- **Current as flow rate**: charge per second crossing a cross-section, $I = nqv_dA$.
- **Drift vs random motion**: electrons zoom around randomly at ~10⁶ m/s, but the field adds a tiny net drift (~10⁻⁴ m/s) that *is* the current.
- **Scattering (collisions)**: electrons repeatedly bounce off lattice vibrations and impurities, which caps their average speed and makes it proportional to $E$.
- **Mobility and conductivity**: $v_d = \mu E$ and $\sigma = nq\mu$; resistance follows from geometry, $R = L/(\sigma A)$. See [[q0015]], [[q0068]].
- **Energy loss**: every collision dumps kinetic energy into the lattice, which is the $I^2R$ heat. See [[q0006]].
:::

::: answer
"Conceptually, voltage is the push and current is the flow. When I put a voltage across a resistor, it creates an electric field inside it, and that field pushes on the free electrons. Resistance is how hard the material makes it for them to move.

The reason the relationship is linear is collisions. Electrons don't just accelerate forever; they keep bumping into vibrating atoms and impurities, so they reach an average drift speed that's proportional to the field. Twice the voltage means twice the field, twice the drift speed, and twice the current. That's Ohm's law.

The water analogy captures it nicely: voltage is pressure, current is flow rate, and the resistor is a narrow section of pipe. A longer or narrower pipe restricts flow more, the same way a longer, thinner wire has more resistance.

Two consequences fall out of this picture. Those collisions turn electrical energy into heat, which is why resistors get warm, $I^2R$. And if the material gets hotter, atoms vibrate more and electrons collide more often, so a metal's resistance goes up with temperature."
:::

::: technical
**Microscopic model (Drude).** A field $E$ accelerates each conduction electron at $a = qE/m$. Between collisions (mean free time $\tau$), it gains extra velocity $qE\tau/m$ on average, then scatters and "forgets" its direction. The average **drift velocity** is

$$v_d = \frac{q\tau}{m}E = \mu E$$

where $\mu = q\tau/m$ is the mobility.

**From drift to Ohm's law.** With $n$ free carriers per m³, current density is $J = nqv_d = \frac{nq^2\tau}{m}E = \sigma E$. For a uniform bar of length $L$ and area $A$: $E = V/L$ and $I = JA$, so

$$I = \sigma\frac{A}{L}V\quad\Rightarrow\quad V = I\underbrace{\frac{L}{\sigma A}}_{R} = I\,\frac{\rho L}{A}$$

**Why it's linear.** $\tau$ is set by thermal motion and lattice vibrations, not by the applied field, because the drift velocity is minuscule next to the electrons' intrinsic speed. In copper at 1 A/mm², $v_d \approx 7\times10^{-5}$ m/s, while the Fermi velocity is ≈1.6×10⁶ m/s: a ratio of ~10⁻¹¹. The field is a tiny perturbation, so the response is linear.

**Copper numbers.** $n \approx 8.5\times10^{28}$ m⁻³, $\sigma \approx 5.96\times10^{7}$ S/m gives $\tau \approx 2.5\times10^{-14}$ s, $\mu \approx 44$ cm²/V·s, mean free path ≈40 nm.

**Energy.** Each electron gains energy from the field and loses it to the lattice in collisions. The power per volume is $J\cdot E = \sigma E^2$; integrated over the bar, $P = VI = I^2R$.

**When the picture breaks:** high fields in semiconductors (velocity saturation near 10⁵ m/s in Si), temperature changes ($\tau$ shrinks as the lattice vibrates harder), and devices where carrier *number* depends on voltage (diodes, transistors).
:::

::: short
"Voltage is the push, current is the flow, and resistance is how much the material fights it. A voltage creates an electric field that pushes electrons, but they keep colliding with the atoms in the lattice, so they settle at an average drift speed proportional to the field. Double the voltage, double the drift speed, double the current. That's why $V = IR$ is linear. The collisions are also where the $I^2R$ heat comes from, and why a hotter metal has higher resistance."
:::

::: long
"I'd explain it with the water analogy first and then say what's actually happening physically.

In the analogy, voltage is like water pressure, current is the flow rate, and a resistor is a narrow, rough section of pipe. More pressure gives proportionally more flow through the same pipe. A longer pipe or a narrower one restricts the flow more. That maps directly onto resistance increasing with length and decreasing with cross-sectional area.

Physically, a metal has a huge number of free electrons that are already moving around randomly at about a million metres per second, but in random directions, so there's no net current. When I apply a voltage, it sets up an electric field along the conductor that nudges every electron in the same direction. The electron accelerates, then collides with a vibrating atom or an impurity and loses that extra velocity, then accelerates again. The net effect is a small average drift speed that's proportional to the field. In copper carrying an amp through a square millimetre, that drift is about 0.07 millimetres per second, which surprises people.

Because current is just the number of electrons times their charge times their drift speed, current ends up proportional to field, and the field is proportional to voltage. That's the linearity in Ohm's law.

That picture explains a lot. The collisions transfer energy to the lattice, which is the heat a resistor dissipates. At higher temperature the atoms vibrate more, collisions happen more often, and a metal's resistance rises. And it tells me when Ohm's law won't hold: in a diode the number of carriers changes with voltage, and in silicon at very high fields the drift speed saturates.

The analogy's limit is that a signal doesn't have to wait for electrons to travel. The field propagates at a good fraction of the speed of light, so the light turns on instantly even though each electron barely moves."
:::

::: terms
- **Electric field ($E$)**: force per unit charge inside the conductor; for a uniform bar, $E = V/L$.
- **Drift velocity ($v_d$)**: the small net average velocity of carriers along the field, superimposed on fast random motion.
- **Mean free time ($\tau$)**: the average time between scattering events; ≈2.5×10⁻¹⁴ s in copper at room temperature.
- **Mobility ($\mu$)**: drift velocity per unit field, $\mu = v_d/E = q\tau/m$.
- **Conductivity / resistivity ($\sigma$, $\rho = 1/\sigma$)**: material properties, $\sigma = nq\mu$; resistance adds geometry, $R = \rho L/A$.
- **Current density ($J$)**: current per unit area, $J = nqv_d = \sigma E$ (the point form of Ohm's law).
- **Scattering**: collisions with lattice vibrations (phonons), impurities and defects that randomise electron momentum.
- **Joule heating**: the conversion of electrical energy to heat through scattering, $P = I^2R$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** gives the water analogy and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the analogy *and* the real physics: field → force on electrons → collisions → constant drift speed ∝ field.
- Explains **why** it's linear: the collision rate doesn't depend on the field because drift is tiny compared to random thermal motion.
- Derives $R = \rho L/A$ from the same picture in one breath (longer = field spread thinner; wider = more parallel paths).
- Uses the picture to predict things: Joule heating, positive temperature coefficient in metals, why diodes aren't ohmic.
- Mentions the "slow electrons, fast signal" surprise, with a number (≈0.07 mm/s drift at 1 A/mm² in copper).
- Says where the water analogy fails rather than over-trusting it.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Electrons travel at nearly the speed of light in a wire."** The *signal* (the field) propagates near light speed; electrons drift at fractions of a millimetre per second.
- **"Current gets used up in the resistor."** Charge is conserved: the same current leaves as enters. What's "used up" is energy (voltage drop × charge), which becomes heat.
- **"Voltage flows."** Voltage is a difference between two points; current is what flows. Keep the analogy straight: pressure difference vs flow.
- **"Resistance is friction that slows each electron continuously."** It's better modelled as repeated scattering events that reset the electron's extra velocity; the average result looks like friction, which is why the water analogy works at all.
- **Over-extending the water analogy**: a pipe's flow isn't linear in pressure for turbulent flow, and water has no equivalent of a magnetic field or of capacitance between separate pipes. Use it for intuition only.
- **Saying resistance always rises with temperature.** True for metals; in semiconductors and NTC thermistors, more carriers are freed at higher temperature and resistance *falls*.
:::

::: deep
## Start with the analogy, then fix it

The classic picture: a pump (battery) creates a pressure difference; water flows through pipes; a narrow section restricts flow.

| Electrical | Water | Holds well? |
|---|---|---|
| Voltage $V$ | Pressure difference | Yes |
| Current $I$ | Volume flow rate | Yes |
| Resistance $R$ | Narrow/long/rough pipe | Yes, for slow (laminar) flow |
| Power $VI$ | Pressure × flow | Yes |
| Wire | Wide, short pipe | Yes |
| Field propagating at ~c | Pressure wave in a full pipe | Roughly |
| Capacitor, inductor | Elastic membrane, heavy turbine | Only loosely |

It's a good starting point because laminar pipe flow really *is* linear in pressure (Poiseuille's law), and a long, narrow pipe really does restrict more, just like $R = \rho L/A$. But it says nothing about *why* a metal behaves that way. For that, we need a picture of electrons.

## What electrons are actually doing

A copper atom contributes about one free electron to the "sea" of conduction electrons, giving $n \approx 8.5\times10^{28}$ electrons per m³. Even with no voltage applied, they move fast (≈1.6×10⁶ m/s, the Fermi velocity) but in random directions, so the net flow through any cross-section is zero.

Apply a voltage $V$ across a wire of length $L$. Inside the wire a field $E = V/L$ appears, and every electron feels a force $qE$. Between collisions it accelerates; after a collision with a vibrating ion or an impurity, its direction is randomised and the extra velocity it picked up is lost. Then it accelerates again.

## Deriving Ohm's law from collisions

Average over many electrons. If the mean time between collisions is $\tau$, the average extra velocity gained from the field is

$$v_d = \frac{qE}{m}\tau = \mu E,\qquad \mu \equiv \frac{q\tau}{m}$$

Current is how much charge crosses an area per second. In time $dt$, all carriers within $v_d\,dt$ of a cross-section cross it, so

$$I = nqv_dA\quad\Rightarrow\quad J = \frac{I}{A} = nq\mu E = \sigma E$$

That's Ohm's law in **point form**. For a uniform bar, substitute $E = V/L$:

$$I = \sigma\frac{A}{L}V \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{V = I\cdot\frac{L}{\sigma A} = IR}$$

The geometry falls out naturally:
- **Longer bar** → the same voltage is spread over more length → weaker field → slower drift → less current. $R \propto L$.
- **Fatter bar** → more electrons in parallel, each drifting at the same speed → more current. $R \propto 1/A$.
- **Better material** → more carriers ($n$) or longer time between collisions ($\tau$) → higher $\sigma$, lower $R$. See [[q0015]].

## Why it's linear: the crucial step

The derivation only gives a *constant* $R$ if $\tau$ doesn't depend on $E$. Why should it not? Because collisions are governed by the electron's total speed, which is dominated by its random motion, not by the drift. Let's put numbers on copper:

- Conductivity $\sigma = 5.96\times10^{7}$ S/m, so $\tau = \sigma m/(nq^2) \approx 2.5\times10^{-14}$ s.
- Mobility $\mu = \sigma/(nq) \approx 4.4\times10^{-3}$ m²/V·s (44 cm²/V·s).
- Mean free path (Fermi velocity × τ) ≈ 40 nm, roughly 150 atomic spacings.
- Drift at 1 A in 1 mm²: $v_d = I/(nqA) \approx 7.4\times10^{-5}$ m/s ≈ 0.26 m per hour.

The drift is ~10⁻¹¹ of the random speed. The field barely changes how electrons move, so their collision rate is unchanged and the response is perfectly linear. That's why copper obeys Ohm's law from microamps to thousands of amps per square millimetre (until it melts).

> [!NOTE]
> The field needed is tiny too: 1 A/mm² in copper requires only $E = J/\sigma \approx 17$ mV/m. A 1 m length of 1 mm² wire has $R \approx 17$ mΩ, consistent with $R = \rho L/A$ and $\rho_{Cu} \approx 1.68\times10^{-8}$ Ω·m.

## Where the heat comes from

Each electron gains kinetic energy from the field during a free flight and dumps it into the lattice at the next collision. The lattice vibrates more: that's heat. Power delivered per unit volume is $J\cdot E = \sigma E^2$. Multiply by the volume $AL$:

$$P = \sigma E^2 AL = \left(\sigma\frac{A}{L}V\right)V = IV = I^2R$$

So Joule heating isn't a separate law, it's the same collisions that cause resistance. See [[q0006]] and [[q0007]] for why that makes current, not voltage, the expensive quantity.

## Temperature: the picture predicts it

Heat a metal and its ions vibrate harder, so electrons scatter more often: $\tau$ shrinks, $\mu$ drops, $\sigma$ drops, $R$ rises. For copper the rise is ≈0.39% per °C. A motor winding at 100 °C has about 30% more resistance than at room temperature.

Semiconductors are the interesting counterexample. Heating a lightly doped semiconductor *frees more carriers* ($n$ rises sharply), which can outweigh the mobility drop, so resistance falls. That's how an NTC thermistor works. Same equation $\sigma = nq\mu$, different term dominating.

## Where the linear picture breaks

The linear relation needs $\tau$ constant and $n$ constant. Break either and Ohm's law fails:

Once the drift becomes comparable to the carriers' thermal speed, they shed energy faster to the lattice and the velocity saturates near 10⁵ m/s. Short-channel MOSFETs live in this regime, which is why their current isn't simply proportional to drain voltage. (Simple empirical model; real curves vary with doping and temperature.)

- **High fields** (semiconductors, nanometre transistors): velocity saturation, as above.
- **Carrier number changes with voltage**: diodes and transistors, where the voltage moves the edge of a depletion region or injects minority carriers ([[q0063]], [[q0072]]).
- **Self-heating**: a lamp filament's $R$ rises as it heats, so its I–V curve bends.
- **Very high frequency**: current crowds to the surface (skin effect, [[q0056]]), which changes the effective $A$, not the law itself.

## The "slow electrons, fast lights" puzzle

If electrons drift at 0.26 m per hour, why does a light turn on instantly? Because the wire is already full of electrons. Closing the switch sends an electromagnetic disturbance along the wire at a large fraction of the speed of light, which establishes the field everywhere almost at once. Every electron in the circuit starts drifting nearly simultaneously, just as water comes out of a full hose the instant you open the tap even though the water molecules from the tap take a while to arrive. The analogy works here too, for the same reason.

::: applications
- **Trace and wire sizing**: $R = \rho L/A$ from the drift picture tells you a 2× longer trace has 2× the IR drop and a 2× wider one has half. IPC-2221/IPC-2152 current-capacity charts are built on this plus heating.
- **Thermistors and RTDs**: a platinum RTD (PT100, ≈0.385%/°C) uses the metal's rising resistance with temperature; an NTC thermistor uses the semiconductor's falling resistance.
- **Heaters and fuses**: nichrome heaters and fuse elements are deliberate applications of Joule heating from collisions.
- **Strain gauges**: stretching a thin metal foil increases $L$ and decreases $A$, so $R$ rises; a Wheatstone bridge measures the tiny change.
- **Semiconductor design**: mobility and velocity saturation set MOSFET drive current, which is why electrons (higher mobility) make faster transistors than holes ([[q0070]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Classical (Drude) model**: treats electrons as classical particles bouncing off ions. It gets conductivity and the linearity right but needs quantum mechanics (Fermi velocity, band structure) to get some details correct, e.g. which electrons participate and the real scattering mechanism. For an interview, Drude with the Fermi-velocity correction is the right level.
- **Uniform conductor, uniform field**: for odd shapes, use $J = \sigma E$ locally.
- **DC or low frequency**: at high frequency the current distribution changes (skin effect).
- **Fixed temperature**: $\tau$ depends on temperature through lattice vibrations.

An equally valid conceptual answer is the **energy view**: voltage is energy per charge; a resistor converts that energy to heat at a rate proportional to the flow. Interviewers accept either, but the collision picture explains linearity best.
:::

::: followups
### If electrons drift so slowly, why does a lamp turn on instantly?
The conductor is already full of free electrons. Closing the switch launches an electromagnetic wave along the wires that sets up the field everywhere within nanoseconds (the signal travels at roughly 50–100% of c depending on the insulation around the conductors). All electrons start drifting at once, so the current is established almost instantly. It's like a full hose: water exits as soon as you open the tap.

### Why does a metal's resistance increase with temperature, but a semiconductor's decrease?
In a metal, $n$ is fixed (about one free electron per atom), so the only effect of heat is more lattice vibration, more scattering, shorter $\tau$, lower mobility, higher $R$. In a lightly doped or intrinsic semiconductor, heat frees many more carriers across the band gap, and that increase in $n$ outweighs the mobility drop, so $R$ falls. Heavily doped silicon can actually behave more like a metal.

### Why do resistors get hot?
Electrons gain kinetic energy from the field between collisions and give it to the lattice at each collision, increasing its vibration, i.e. temperature. The total rate is $VI = I^2R$. A 1 kΩ resistor with 10 V across it dissipates 0.1 W; in an 0603 package that's at its rating and it will run noticeably warm.

### Estimate the drift velocity for 10 A in a 2.5 mm² copper wire.
$v_d = I/(nqA) = 10/(8.5\times10^{28}\times1.6\times10^{-19}\times2.5\times10^{-6}) \approx 2.9\times10^{-4}$ m/s, about 0.3 mm/s. Still glacial.

### What if you made a wire so short that it's shorter than the electron mean free path? (harder probe)
Then electrons cross it without scattering (ballistic transport) and the Drude picture fails. Resistance no longer scales as $L/A$; instead there's a minimum quantised contact resistance per conduction channel (on the order of 12.9 kΩ per spin-degenerate channel). This matters in nanoscale devices and nanowires, not in normal PCB-scale conductors, where the ~40 nm mean free path in copper is tiny compared with any trace.
:::

"Conceptually explain" means the interviewer doesn't want only the formula (that's [[q0001]]). If they push further toward materials ("what does resistance depend on?"), that's [[q0015]]; if they push toward "what is electricity", that's [[q0059]].
