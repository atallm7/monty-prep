::: testing
Whether you can go from the capacitor equation to the answer *for the right current*: the slope of the output voltage is $i_C/C$, and in a buck $i_C$ is the inductor current minus the load current, not the load current or the switch current. Good answers say what moves the slope (ripple current, $C$, load steps, startup current), what does not (the switch state per se), and bring in the real-world terms: ESR steps, ESL spikes and derated capacitance.
:::

::: why
The capacitor slope tells you how fast the output moves in each part of a cycle, how much it droops in a load step before the loop reacts, how fast the output rises at start-up (inrush current $=C\,dV/dt$), and what slew a downstream load experiences. Interviewers use it to check whether you can use $i=C\,dv/dt$ in a live circuit and identify which current it is.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor equation** $dv_C/dt=i_C/C$. See [[q0025]].
- **Output-node KCL**: $i_C=i_L-i_{load}$. See [[q0187]].
- **ESR** adds $i_CR_{ESR}$ to the terminal voltage, so the *terminal* slope has extra terms. See [[q0030]].
- **Capacitance derating**: effective $C$ is smaller under DC bias and temperature.
- **Continuity**: $v_C$ is continuous; its slope changes where $i_C$ changes. See [[q0037]].
:::

::: answer
"The slope of the capacitor voltage is the capacitor current divided by the capacitance: $dv/dt = i_C/C$. In the buck the capacitor current isn't the load current; it's the inductor current minus the load current, because at the output node the inductor current splits between the load and the capacitor.

In steady state that's a triangle of plus and minus half the ripple current, so the capacitor voltage slope swings linearly from about minus 23 millivolts per microsecond to plus 23 in my example, 0.51 A over 22 µF. The slope is zero at the voltage extremes, where the inductor current equals the load, and the steepest slope is half the ripple current over $C$. So what sets it is the ripple current and the capacitance: more inductance or frequency means less ripple current, so a lower slope; more capacitance means a lower slope.

In a load step the picture changes: if the load jumps by 1 A before the inductor catches up, the capacitor supplies it, so the slope is about 1 A over 22 µF, 45 millivolts per microsecond. And at start-up the slope is set by the soft-start: charging a 22 µF cap to 3.3 V in 1 ms means 3.3 V per millisecond, which needs about 73 mA of inrush."
:::

::: technical
**Ideal capacitor.** $\dfrac{dv_C}{dt}=\dfrac{i_C}{C}=\dfrac{i_L-i_{load}}{C}$.

**Steady state (CCM).** $i_C=i_L-I_{out}$, a triangle with peaks $\pm\Delta I/2$. So the slope varies linearly between $-\frac{\Delta I}{2C}$ and $+\frac{\Delta I}{2C}$: $\pm0.509\ \text{A}/22\ \mu\text{F}=\pm23.1$ mV/µs. The rate of change of the slope is $\frac{1}{C}\frac{di_L}{dt}=\frac{1.85\ \text{A}/\mu s}{22\ \mu F}=84$ mV/µs² in the on state and $-32$ mV/µs² ($0.70/22$) in the off state.

**Terminal voltage with ESR/ESL.** $v_{term}=v_C+R_{ESR}i_C+L_{ESL}\,di_C/dt$. So the terminal slope $=\frac{i_C}{C}+R_{ESR}\frac{di_C}{dt}+L_{ESL}\frac{d^2i_C}{dt^2}$. The ESR term is a step in slope at each switching edge: $R_{ESR}\times(1.85+0.70)\ \text{A}/\mu s=12.8$ mV/µs for 5 mΩ. The ESL term gives a voltage step $L_{ESL}\,\Delta(di/dt)$: 1 nH × 2.55 A/µs = 2.6 mV step at the edge.

**Load step $\Delta I$.** Before the inductor responds, $i_C\approx-\Delta I$: slope $-\Delta I/C$ (45 mV/µs for 1 A and 22 µF), reducing linearly to zero as the inductor current ramps up at $V/L$. The resulting droop is about $\frac12\Delta I\,t_{rise}/C$ with $t_{rise}=\Delta I\,L/(V_{in}-V_{out})$: for $\Delta I=1$ A, $t=0.54\ \mu s$, droop $\approx12$ mV (ideal, no ESR, with the loop already maximising the duty cycle).

**Start-up.** $i_C=C\,dV/dt$; a soft-start ramp of 3.3 V in 1 ms into 22 µF: 73 mA.

**Real capacitance.** If DC-bias derating leaves 12 µF effective, the slope is $0.509/12\ \mu F=42$ mV/µs, 1.8 times larger.
:::

::: long
"The capacitor equation says the slope of the voltage is the capacitor current over the capacitance. So the question is which current. In the buck, at the output node the inductor current has to go somewhere: part goes to the load, and whatever is left goes into the capacitor. The load current is approximately the DC average of the inductor current, so the capacitor current is just the AC part of the inductor current: a triangle going from minus half the ripple to plus half the ripple.

That means the capacitor voltage slope is zero at the top and the bottom of the ripple, where the inductor current equals the load current, and is steepest when the inductor current is at its peak or valley. In my example, ripple of about 1 amp and 22 µF, the maximum slope is 0.51 amps over 22 microfarads, about 23 millivolts per microsecond. The slope itself changes linearly with time, so the voltage is made of parabolas.

So the parameters are the ripple current, which is set by the input and output voltage, the inductor and the switching frequency, and the capacitance, including its derating: if the effective capacitance drops to 12 microfarads the slope nearly doubles.

There are two other regimes. In a load step, the inductor can't follow, so the capacitor takes the whole step and the slope is the step current over C, 45 millivolts per microsecond for an amp, until the inductor catches up. And at start-up, the slope is what the soft-start imposes, and the capacitor current is just C times that, about 70 milliamps for a 1 ms ramp. Finally, in real hardware the ESR adds a step in the terminal voltage's slope at each switching edge."
:::

::: terms
- **Slew rate of $V_{out}$**: $dv/dt$ of the output; here up to about 23 mV/µs in steady state.
- **Capacitor current $i_C$**: $i_L-i_{load}$.
- **Inrush current**: $C\,dV/dt$ at start-up.
- **Soft-start**: controlled ramp of reference/output to limit inrush.
- **ESR step**: instantaneous voltage step $R_{ESR}\Delta i$ at a switching edge.
- **Effective capacitance**: capacitance after DC-bias, temperature and ageing derating.
- **Load-step droop**: voltage drop while the inductor catches up.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "$dv/dt=i/C$, so a bigger capacitor gives a smaller slope."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Identifies the right current ($i_L-i_{load}$) and gives the slope range from the ripple current.
- Notes the slope is zero at the extremes of the voltage and *linearly varying*, hence parabolas.
- Covers the three regimes (steady-state ripple, load step, start-up) with the appropriate current in each.
- Adds ESR/ESL steps and derated capacitance.
- Gives a quick use: inrush $=C\,dV/dt$, droop estimate $\tfrac12\Delta I\,t/C$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using the load current in $dv/dt=I/C$ in steady state.** The load's DC current is supplied by the inductor; only the *ripple* current charges the capacitor.
- **Saying the slope is constant in the on state.** $i_C$ changes through the on state, so the slope changes linearly.
- **Forgetting derating.** At 3.3 V a 22 µF ceramic may be 12 to 16 µF effective.
- **Ignoring ESR when comparing with a scope.** The terminal waveform contains ESR steps, so it is not the pure capacitor slope.
- **Treating inrush as negligible.** With a large output capacitor and a fast ramp it can trip current limits: $C\,dV/dt$ adds to the load current.
:::

## The slope is the current over $C$

$$\frac{dv_C}{dt}=\frac{i_C}{C}$$
At the output node, $i_L=i_C+i_{load}$, so
$$\frac{dv_C}{dt}=\frac{i_L-i_{load}}{C}.$$

## What changes the slope

| Parameter | Effect on steady-state slope |
|---|---|
| $\Delta I_L$ ($L\uparrow$, $f\uparrow$) | proportionally lower |
| $C$ | inversely proportional (use *effective* $C$) |
| $V_{in}$ (fixed $V_{out}$) | rises, as ripple current rises |
| Load current | no effect in CCM (shifts $i_L$ and $I_{out}$ together) |
| ESR | adds a step $R_{ESR}\,\Delta(di_C/dt)$ at the terminal |

Quick check with derating: the 22 µF ceramic at 3.3 V might provide only 12 µF, so $|dv/dt|_{max}=0.509/12\ \mu F=42$ mV/µs.

## Terminal voltage includes ESR and ESL

$$v_{term}=v_C+R_{ESR}\,i_C+L_{ESL}\frac{di_C}{dt}.$$

- $R_{ESR}i_C$ is a triangle (5 mΩ × 0.51 A = 2.5 mV peak).
- $L_{ESL}\,di_C/dt$ is a *rectangle* in the ideal buck since $di_C/dt=di_L/dt$ is $+1.85$ A/µs on and $-0.70$ A/µs off: with 1 nH it is $+1.85$ mV and $-0.70$ mV. It steps at each switching edge. In real boards with 3 to 5 nH loop inductance it can dominate the high-frequency content of the ripple.

## Load step: the constant-current regime

When the load jumps by $\Delta I$ the inductor current takes $t_r=\Delta I\,L/(V_{in}-V_{out})$ to follow (at best, with the duty cycle at 100%). In the meantime the capacitor current is $-(\Delta I-\text{ramp})$.

For $\Delta I=1$ A: $t_r=1\times4.7\ \mu H/8.7\ V=0.54\ \mu s$. The initial slope is $-\Delta I/C=-45$ mV/µs, falling linearly to zero. The droop is the triangle area: $\tfrac12\times1\ A\times0.54\ \mu s/22\ \mu F=12.3$ mV, plus the ESR step $\Delta I\cdot ESR=5$ mV. A real controller needs a few loop delays to reach 100% duty, so the observed droop is larger.

## Start-up: the slope you impose

A soft-start ramp sets $dV_{out}/dt$ and thus the capacitor current $C\,dV/dt$:

| Soft-start time | $dV/dt$ for 3.3 V | Inrush into 22 µF |
|---|---|---|
| 100 µs | 33 V/ms | 726 mA |
| 1 ms | 3.3 V/ms | 73 mA |
| 10 ms | 0.33 V/ms | 7.3 mA |

With a large bulk capacitor (say 1000 µF) and 1 ms soft-start, the inrush is 3.3 A, which can trip the current limit. Controllers adapt by lengthening soft-start or letting the user set it with a capacitor on an SS pin.

## The slope as a speed limit on the output: dynamic voltage scaling

Processors and FPGAs often ask their supply to move to a new voltage on the fly. The output capacitor's slope limit, $dv/dt=i_C/C$, sets how quickly that can happen. Take a 100 µF output capacitance and a converter with a 6 A current limit feeding a 2 A load, and ask the rail to rise from 0.9 V to 1.1 V.

- **Rising.** The most current available to charge the capacitor is the limit minus the load: $6-2=4$ A. The maximum slope is $4/100\ \mu\text{F}=40$ mV/µs, so the 0.2 V step takes at least 5 µs. In practice the controller's reference ramp, not the capacitor, usually limits it, but the capacitor sets the floor.
- **Falling.** A diode-rectified (or DCM) buck cannot pull current out of the output. The only discharge path is the load, so the slope is $-I_{load}/C$. At the 2 A load that is $-20$ mV/µs, so 0.2 V takes 10 µs. At a 10 mA load it is only $-0.1$ mV/µs, and the same step takes 2 ms.

That last number is a real surprise on a bench: a rail overshoots or is commanded down and then sits high for milliseconds at light load. A forced-CCM synchronous buck can sink current (it has a negative inductor current), so it falls at a rate set by its reverse current limit instead, which is why DVS-capable rails are usually specified in forced-CCM.

## Reading the capacitance from the slope

The relation $C=i_C/(dv/dt)$ is also a measurement. During the first fraction of a microsecond after a load step, the inductor current has not moved yet, so the entire step current goes into the capacitor. If a 1 A step produces an initial slope of 45 mV/µs, $C=1/0.045=22$ µF, as labeled. If the scope shows 83 mV/µs, then $C_{eff}=12$ µF, which is the derated value at the actual bias and temperature, and it is the right value to use in the ripple formula. This is a quick in-circuit test that avoids the pitfalls of an LCR meter measuring at low bias (see [[q0143]]).

## Why the capacitive ripple is spectrally clean

The ESR term in the ripple is a triangle, which has a corner at each switching edge and whose harmonics fall off as $1/n^2$. The capacitive term is the integral of that triangle, so it has no corners at all: the voltage and its slope are both continuous, and only the second derivative jumps. Its harmonics fall off faster ($1/n^3$). The consequence is that a capacitance-dominated ripple (all-ceramic output) has less high-frequency content than an ESR-dominated one, even at the same peak-to-peak value, and the extra high-frequency content that appears in practice comes from ESL and the switch node coupling and not from the capacitor's charging.

## Limiting cases

- $C\to\infty$: the slope goes to zero and the ripple vanishes, but the startup inrush $C\,dV/dt$ and the loop's phase margin (a lower-frequency output pole) become the issue.
- $C\to0$: the slope is limited only by the parasitic capacitance of the board and the load, and the "ripple" becomes the inductor ripple times the load impedance.
- ESR-dominated: the slope seen at the terminal is $R_{ESR}\,di_C/dt$ and the terminal voltage looks like a copy of the inductor current.

## Bench: confirming the slope

1. Capture the output after a 1 A load step with a fast edge (1 A/µs or faster). Zoom into the first 200 ns after the step and measure the initial slope with cursors. It should equal $\Delta I/C_{eff}$.
2. Measure the capacitor's true terminal voltage at the pads, not at the load connector, since a long ground wire adds a $L\,di/dt$ error of similar size to the ESR step.
3. For start-up inrush, use a current probe on the output inductor and compare the plateau current against $C\,dV/dt$ for the programmed soft-start time. A plateau higher than predicted means the load is drawing current while the rail ramps.

- **Load-transient droop estimates** for processor and FPGA rails.
- **Soft-start and inrush design** (SS capacitor, current-limit sizing).
- **Choosing output capacitors** by effective capacitance and ESR.
- **Predicting scope waveforms** of ripple, including ESR/ESL steps.
- **Understanding downstream slew limits**: a load sensitive to $dV/dt$ (some ADC references, PLL supplies) needs the ripple slope kept below its spec.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM, steady state** for the ripple slope; load steps and start-up are separate regimes.
- **Ideal capacitor** for the slope; ESR and ESL change the terminal waveform.
- **Constant load current**; resistive loads add a minor term $v/R$.
- **Capacitance is constant**; derating changes it by tens of percent for ceramics, and again with temperature.
- **Load-step droop estimate** assumes the controller drives the duty cycle to its maximum immediately; real loops take longer.
:::

::: followups
### What is the maximum slope of the output ripple in my example?
$|dv/dt|_{max}=\frac{\Delta I/2}{C}=\frac{0.509\ A}{22\ \mu F}=23.1$ mV/µs, at the peak and valley of the inductor current, i.e. at the switching edges for the capacitive part.

### Why is the slope zero at the voltage peak?
At the peak of $v_C$, $dv_C/dt=0\Rightarrow i_C=0\Rightarrow i_L=I_{out}$. That is where the inductor current crosses the load current (on the falling ramp).

### How does the slope change if I add more capacitance but keep the same inductor?
The ripple current is unchanged, so the capacitor current waveform is unchanged and the slope falls in proportion: 47 µF instead of 22 µF reduces 23.1 to 10.8 mV/µs. If the ESR is unchanged the ESR step does not shrink.

### How would you calculate the inrush current at start-up?
$I_{inrush}=C_{total}\,dV/dt+I_{load}$ where $dV/dt$ is set by the soft-start (reference ramp). For 220 µF total and a 2 ms ramp to 3.3 V: $220\ \mu F\times1650\ V/s=0.36$ A, on top of any load. If the converter starts into a pre-biased output the behaviour depends on the controller.

### Harder: why does a high-ESR capacitor make the measured slope look wrong?
The terminal voltage is $v_C+R_{ESR}i_C$, so with 100 mΩ the ESR term (±51 mV) swamps the 5.8 mV capacitive term, and the slope you see at the terminal is $R_{ESR}\,di_C/dt$ steps rather than $i_C/C$. For an electrolytic the ripple looks triangular and its slope follows $di_L/dt$, not the capacitor's integral. You would then need an internal capacitor model to talk about $v_C$.
:::

"Capacitor voltage slope" is taken as $dv_C/dt$ of the output capacitor voltage, mainly in steady-state ripple, with the load-step and start-up regimes as extensions. Compare [[q0189]] (shape: parabolic) and [[q0190]] (when it charges and discharges); this page asks what *sets* the slope.
