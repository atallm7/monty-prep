::: testing
Whether you can trace a component choice into the loop gain. A strong answer:

1. Says $L$ and $C$ set the resonance $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, the characteristic impedance $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$ and (with ESR) the zero, and shows which way each moves.
2. Relates $f_0$ and the ESR zero to the crossover frequency and the compensator's zeros.
3. Notes that ceramic derating and tolerance move these values in the field.
4. Distinguishes voltage mode from current mode, where $L$ also sets the slope-compensation requirement ([[q0255]]).
:::

::: why
Inductor and capacitor selection are the first things changed in any buck design. Swapping a 47 µF electrolytic for ceramics, or doubling the inductor to cut ripple, can silently destroy phase margin on a design that worked. Every datasheet lists allowed $L$/$C$ ranges for exactly this reason. This question checks whether you know how much to expect, in what direction, and why.
:::

::: concepts
- **Resonant frequency** $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ and characteristic impedance $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$ ([[q0246]]).
- **Quality factor** $1/Q\approx Z_0/R+(r+R_L)/Z_0$: load damps by $Z_0/R$, series resistance by $(r+R_L)/Z_0$.
- **ESR zero** $f_z=1/(2\pi rC)$.
- **Crossover and compensation**: type II/III zeros are placed around $f_0$ ([[q0244]]).
- **Capacitor derating**: ceramic capacitance drops with DC bias, temperature and aging.
- **Slope compensation vs $L$**: in peak current mode the required ramp scales with $V_{out}/L$ ([[q0255]]).
:::

::: answer
"The inductor and capacitor set the LC resonance, f zero equals 1 over 2 pi root LC, and the Q of that resonance. In voltage mode the compensator is designed around where that double pole is. Bigger L or C lowers f zero; if the crossover is fixed and the compensator's zeros stay where they were, the double pole is no longer where they cancel it, and the phase margin drops. Smaller L or C raises f zero closer to the crossover, which can put the high-Q peak right at the 0 dB crossing and cost gain margin.

The capacitor matters in a second way, through its ESR. The ESR creates a zero at 1 over 2 pi ESR C, which adds up to 90 degrees of phase lead. An electrolytic with 100 milliohms puts that zero around 30 kHz and helps; a ceramic puts it at megahertz and gives no help, so the loop needs the compensator to supply the lead. That's why changing capacitor type often breaks stability.

The values also change with tolerance and operating point: a ceramic might lose half its capacitance under DC bias, an inductor drifts by 20 percent and drops toward saturation, so I check the loop at the extremes. In current-mode control the plant is first order so L and C are much less critical, but L sets the inductor slope, so the slope compensation needs to be matched, and the output capacitor sets the load pole.

That's why datasheets give a range of allowed L and C: the internal compensation is only good in that window."
:::

::: technical
**Plant parameters** (voltage mode, from [[q0246]]): $f_0=\dfrac1{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$, $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$, $\dfrac1Q\approx\dfrac{Z_0}{R}+\dfrac{r+R_L}{Z_0}$, $f_z=\dfrac1{2\pi rC}$.

**Sensitivities.**

- $C\uparrow$: $f_0\downarrow$ as $C^{-1/2}$; $Z_0\downarrow$; $f_z\downarrow$ (more phase lead at crossover for a given ESR).
- $L\uparrow$: $f_0\downarrow$ as $L^{-1/2}$; $Z_0\uparrow$; $Q$ from the load term falls ($Z_0/R$ rises); $f_z$ unchanged.
- Ripple $\Delta I=V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf_{sw})$ scales as $1/L$ and output ripple voltage as $1/(LC)$.
- **Crossover constraint**: $f_c\le f_{sw}/5$ to $f_{sw}/10$, so $f_0$ should be well below it: more LC product, more room.

**Worked table.** $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, 1 A load (3.3 Ω), $f_{sw}=500$ kHz, $R_L=20$ mΩ, ESR 10 mΩ:

| Case | $f_0$ | $Z_0$ | $Q$ | ESR zero | ripple $\Delta I$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4.7 µH, 47 µF (base) | 10.7 kHz | 0.316 Ω | 5.2 | 339 kHz | 1.02 A |
| 4.7 µH, 200 µF | 5.2 kHz | 0.153 Ω | 4.1 | 80 kHz | 1.02 A |
| 22 µH, 47 µF | 4.95 kHz | 0.68 Ω | 4.0 | 339 kHz | 0.22 A |
| 4.7 µH, 23.5 µF (derated 50%) | 15.1 kHz | 0.447 Ω | 4.9 | 677 kHz | 1.02 A |
| 1 µH, 47 µF | 23.2 kHz | 0.146 Ω | 4.0 | 339 kHz | 4.8 A |

With the compensation zeros placed at 10.7 kHz (base design), the 200 µF and 22 µH cases have the resonance at about half that frequency, so the zeros no longer cancel it: the result is a drop in phase margin of tens of degrees at a fixed crossover. The derated-capacitor case shifts the resonance up by $\sqrt2$.

**Current mode.** The plant becomes first order, with the load pole $1/(2\pi RC)$ (1.0 kHz for 3.3 Ω and 47 µF; 241 Hz for 200 µF), so crossover can sit well above it with a simple type II compensator, and $L$ matters mostly through slope compensation: $m_2=V_{out}/L$ (0.70 A/µs for 4.7 µH, 3.3 A/µs for 1 µH).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The $Q$ numbers use the simple load-plus-series-loss approximation. Real values depend on the actual ESR versus frequency, inductor AC resistance and load type.
:::

::: long
"Start with what L and C do to the power stage. They form a second-order low-pass with a resonant frequency of 1 over two pi root LC, a characteristic impedance root of L over C, and a Q that depends on the load and the resistances. Take my example: 4.7 microhenries and 47 microfarads gives about 10.7 kHz, Q of about 5 at 1 amp.

In voltage mode the compensator is built around that resonance: two zeros near f zero to cancel the double pole's phase drop, and the crossover set some multiple above it. If I increase C to 200 microfarads, f zero falls to about 5 kHz. The compensator zeros are still at 10.7 kHz, so they no longer cancel the double pole and the phase margin falls. If I decrease L or C, f zero rises toward the crossover and the high-Q peak can approach the 0 dB line, which hurts gain margin.

The ESR is the second effect. It gives a zero at 1 over two pi ESR C. A 100 milliohm electrolytic puts it around 30 kHz, which helps the phase near crossover. A ceramic with 5 milliohms puts it in the megahertz and gives nothing, so I lose up to 90 degrees of phase and the compensator has to provide it, or I add a feedforward cap.

Then the real-world part: inductance is plus or minus 20 percent and falls as the inductor approaches saturation, and ceramic capacitance can drop by half with DC bias. These shift f zero by around 40 percent, so I check the loop at the corners. This is why datasheets give an allowed range for L and C.

In current mode the story changes. The inner loop controls the inductor current, so the plant looks first order with a pole at 1 over two pi R C, and L and C are much less critical. But L still matters because the slope compensation needs to be at least half the inductor's down-slope, V out over L, so too small an inductor with fixed internal compensation leaves the loop prone to subharmonic oscillation."
:::

::: terms
- **Resonant (LC) frequency $f_0$**: corner of the double pole.
- **Characteristic impedance $Z_0$**: $\sqrt{L/C}$, sets how much the load and ESR damp the resonance.
- **ESR zero**: zero from the capacitor's ESR.
- **Capacitance derating**: loss of effective capacitance with DC bias, temperature, aging.
- **Internal compensation**: fixed on-chip compensation valid for a window of $L$ and $C$.
- **Load pole**: $1/(2\pi RC)$ in current-mode control.
- **Slope compensation**: ramp added to the current signal to prevent subharmonic oscillation.
- **Corner analysis**: checking min/max component values, temperature and load.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Bigger is always more stable."** A bigger $LC$ lowers $f_0$ and, with fixed compensator zeros, can reduce margin. Bigger also means slower transient and worse transient droop in the large-signal sense.
- **Using nominal capacitance.** A 47 µF/16 V ceramic at 12 V bias may offer 20 µF or less.
- **Ignoring ESR.** Swapping capacitor types changes the loop even at equal capacitance.
- **Forgetting that Q falls with load.** The worst case for margin is often light load, where less damping gives a sharper resonance.
- **Treating inductor value as fixed.** Tolerance (±20% typical) and saturation reduce inductance at high current.
- **Ignoring the current-mode link.** In peak current mode a small $L$ steepens the down-slope and demands more slope compensation.
:::

Figure: Resonant frequency against capacitance for three inductors. The dashed line is $f_{sw}/10=50$ kHz, a typical upper limit for the crossover. A design wants $f_0$ comfortably under the crossover, so that the compensator has a clean region to work in. **Increase $C$** (same $L$): $f_0\downarrow$, $Z_0\downarrow$, and the ESR zero moves down (more lead in the crossover region). The load-step droop falls (more charge), and the output ripple voltage falls. Cost: size, a slower start-up, and detuned compensator zeros. The inductor and the load are barely affected.

**Increase $L$** (same $C$): $f_0\downarrow$, $Z_0\uparrow$, ripple current falls as $1/L$, the inductor slew is slower. The ESR zero doesn't move. In a loop with fixed zeros, the resonance moves below them.

## Detuning the compensator

A type III compensator for the base case places its zeros at 10.7 kHz. When the plant changes, the cancellation is off:

Figure: Plant gain and phase for $L=4.7$ µH/$C=47$ µF, $C=200$ µF, and $L=22$ µH. The corner moves from 10.7 kHz down to about 5 kHz. Compare the magnitude near 40 kHz (the crossover region): larger $LC$ lowers the plant gain there by $(f_0/f)^2$, so the crossover moves down for a given compensator, and the type III zeros at 10.7 kHz now sit above the resonance, leaving less phase boost where it is needed.

Rules of thumb: for fixed compensator zeros, keep $LC$ within about ±30 to 40% of nominal (a factor of about 1.4 in $f_0$) unless the datasheet says otherwise. Auto-tuning digital controllers can re-design the zeros.

## Where ESR enters

For the same capacitance the ESR zero decides whether the plant looks like a $-180^\circ$ double pole or a $-90^\circ$ single pole at crossover. For the 47 µF base case with ESR of 10 mΩ the zero is at 339 kHz, above any reasonable crossover (with $f_c$ = 40 kHz), so the full 180° of lag is present. With 100 mΩ the zero is at 34 kHz and gives roughly 50° of lead at 40 kHz. This is why ceramic-only designs need type III compensation, or a feedforward capacitor ([[q0250]]), while older electrolytic designs got by with type II.

## Tolerances and operating point

- **Ceramic DC-bias derating**: a 47 µF X5R at 50% bias might retain only 40 to 60% of its capacitance. That moves $f_0$ up by $\sqrt{1/0.5}=1.41$ and $f_z$ up by 2.
- **Inductance**: ±20% tolerance plus saturation roll-off, so $f_0$ varies by about ±10% from tolerance alone and more at high current.
- **Temperature**: ESR of electrolytics rises sharply when cold, moving the zero.
- **Aging**: electrolytic capacitance falls and ESR rises.

Check the loop at the corners: minimum and maximum $LC$, maximum and minimum ESR, light and full load, minimum and maximum $V_{in}$.

## Current mode and slope compensation

With peak-current-mode control the plant is a single pole at $1/(2\pi RC)$ (about 1 kHz at 3.3 Ω and 47 µF) plus a sampling double pole near $f_{sw}/2$, so $L$ and $C$ no longer cause the big resonance. But:

- The slope-compensation ramp must be at least about half the inductor's falling slope, $m_2=V_{out}/L$. Halving $L$ doubles $m_2$ (0.70 A/µs at 4.7 µH, 3.3 A/µs at 1 µH). Datasheets state a minimum $L$ for this reason ([[q0255]]).
- The load pole moves with $C$ and $R$, and crossover must stay below the sampling pole.

## Worked example: phase margin as the capacitor changes

Everything above says "detuning costs phase margin". Here is a calculation that puts numbers on how fast. I built a small model of a voltage-mode buck: $V_{in}=12$ V, $L=4.7$ µH, a 3.3 Ω load (1 A), 30 mΩ of series resistance (switch plus inductor DCR) and 10 mΩ of capacitor ESR. The power stage is

$$G_{vd}(s)=\frac{V_{in}(1+sC\,ESR)}{(1+R_s/R)+s(L/R+R_sC)+s^2LC}.$$

The compensator is a type III with an integrator, a double zero placed at the nominal resonance of the 47 µF design (10.7 kHz), poles at 300 kHz and 600 kHz, and a gain set for a 50 kHz crossover. I also added a 1 µs delay for the modulator and sampling, which is an assumption (it is on the order of half a switching period at 500 kHz). The plant's quality factor is the first thing to compute: $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}=0.316\ \Omega$ gives $Q_{load}=R/Z_0=10.4$ from the load alone and $Z_0/R_s=10.5$ from the series resistance, so the combined $Q=1/(Z_0/R+R_s/Z_0)=5.2$, a 14 dB peak. Plugging in other capacitor values without touching the compensator gives:

| Effective $C$ | $f_0$ | Crossover | Phase margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| 15 µF | 19.0 kHz | 133 kHz | 5° |
| 24 µF | 15.0 kHz | 90 kHz | 29° |
| 47 µF (design) | 10.7 kHz | 50 kHz | 44° |
| 80 µF | 8.2 kHz | 32 kHz | 45° |
| 150 µF | 6.0 kHz | 20 kHz | 36° |
| 300 µF | 4.2 kHz | 13 kHz | 24° |

Figure: The peak is flat around the design value and falls off both ways. Losing half the capacitance (a ceramic derated by DC bias) drops the margin from 44° to about 29°, and doubling or tripling it also costs margin, because the crossover slides down while the fixed zeros stay at 10.7 kHz. The two ends fail for different reasons: too little capacitance pushes the crossover up toward the delay, and too much pushes the plant's double pole below the compensator zeros. These are results of this specific model, not a general rule, but the shape is typical.

Two lessons stand out. First, the margin is asymmetric in what it costs you. A 50% capacitance loss is far more dangerous than a 50% gain, so the DC-bias curve of the real part belongs in the stability analysis, not just the ripple analysis. Second, a 45° design on paper is already marginal once you notice that the delay alone took 18° off at the nominal crossover (the margin was 62° without it). When an interviewer asks "what if the cap derates?", this is the table you describe.

## Why Q matters, and what damps it

The peak at $f_0$ has height $Q$. In a load-damped plant the load is the main damping, so a light load ($R$ large) raises $Q$. At 100 mA instead of 1 A the load resistance is 33 Ω and $Q_{load}$ becomes 104, with the series resistance now limiting the total to $Q\approx9.5$. The plant gain peak and the phase drop through $-180^\circ$ become sharper, which is why light-load stability is checked separately from full-load stability. A voltage-mode loop that is fine at 1 A can ring at 100 mA, because the phase falls through $-180^\circ$ within a narrow band around $f_0$ instead of gradually.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Measure the loop.** Inject a small AC signal (10 to 50 mV) in series with the feedback resistor through a 50 Ω resistor or a transformer, using a network analyzer or a scope with a frequency-response analyzer. Plot the crossover frequency and phase margin at the corners: minimum and maximum $V_{in}$, light and full load, minimum and maximum capacitance.
2. **Check the capacitor.** Measure the real capacitance at bias, or look up the DC-bias curve for the exact part number. Compare the crossover you measure with the one the model predicts for that value of $C$.
3. **Do a load step.** Apply a step of about 25 to 75% of full load with a fast electronic load. Count the ringing cycles after the step. Roughly, 45° of phase margin rings about two to three cycles, while under 30° rings for many more.
4. **Swap components.** Change $C$ or $L$ by one value up and down and watch the crossover move in the direction the model predicts.

- **Point-of-load converters with large ceramic banks**, where derating at bias matters.

::: assumptions
- **Voltage-mode CCM** analysis with the averaged model, unless stated otherwise.
- **Fixed compensator**: stability conclusions assume the compensator is not re-tuned.
- **1 A, 3.3 V load** for the numeric table; $Q$ changes with load.
- **ESR constant** with frequency and temperature (it is not in practice).
- For hysteretic/COT converters the relevant parameter is the ESR ripple (the output capacitor's ESR must be large enough), a different stability story.

::: followups
### If I double the output capacitance, does stability always improve?
Not necessarily. $f_0$ falls by $\sqrt2$, which can move the resonance below the compensator zeros and cost phase margin, while the extra capacitance also lowers the plant gain at crossover (so the actual crossover moves lower). Transient response improves in terms of droop but the loop might become slower. Check the margin; datasheets give a maximum $C$ for this reason, and large $C$ also raises start-up inrush.

### Why do ceramics make some designs unstable that worked with electrolytics?
The ESR zero. With a 100 mΩ electrolytic the zero is near 34 kHz and gives phase lead around crossover. A ceramic with 5 mΩ puts the zero in the MHz range, so the plant has the full $-180^\circ$ lag at crossover. The compensator (type III) or a feedforward cap must replace the lead.

### How does inductor value affect transient response vs stability?
A smaller $L$ gives faster current slew, higher ripple, and a higher $f_0$, so the loop can be faster. A larger $L$ gives lower ripple and less loss but slower slew, so the large-signal response is limited by the inductor even if the loop is fast. Stability-wise both are manageable if the compensator is designed for them.

### What happens to margin if the inductor saturates at peak load?
Inductance falls (say to 50%), so $f_0$ rises by 1.4×, ripple and peak current rise further, and in peak-current mode the slopes change. The loop sees a different plant exactly at the highest load, which is why designers choose $I_{sat}$ with margin ([[q0259]]).

### Scenario: you replace a 47 µF/10 V ceramic with a same-value 0603 part and the converter now oscillates at 15 kHz. What's the likely cause?
The new part has much lower effective capacitance at the DC bias (maybe 15 µF), so $f_0$ moved from about 10.7 kHz to about 19 kHz and the double pole moved above the compensator zeros. With ceramics there is no ESR zero to help. Measure the actual capacitance at bias, re-check the loop gain with injection, and either restore the capacitance (more or larger-voltage parts) or adjust the compensation.
