::: testing
Whether you can **rank** loss sources and, more importantly, say how the ranking changes with operating point. The interviewer is looking for:

1. A defensible top three at nominal load (the two power FETs and the inductor), with reasons.
2. The load dependence: conduction scales as $I^2$ and dominates at heavy load; fixed and frequency-proportional terms dominate at light load.
3. The $V_{in}$ and $f_{sw}$ dependence: switching, recovery and quiescent terms grow with $V_{in}$ and frequency.
4. What is *not* a big loss: capacitor ESR, diode/inductor "magic", the feedback network.
:::

::: why
Efficiency work is triage. If you can say "at full load it is the low-side FET and the inductor DCR; at 100 mA it is gate drive, recovery and quiescent current; at 48 V in it is switching", you know what to optimize and what to leave alone ([[q0214]]). This question is the ranking companion to [[q0213]]'s itemization, and it feeds measurement-based debugging: a thermal camera tells you which part is hot, and the ranking tells you whether that is expected.
:::

::: concepts
- **Conduction loss**: $I_{rms}^2R$ in FETs and inductor DCR, scales as $I^2$ ([[q0213]]).
- **Switching-related loss**: overlap, $C_{oss}$, dead time and recovery; scales with $f$ and $V_{in}$ ([[q0203]]).
- **Fixed loss**: gate drive, quiescent current, core loss (weakly load dependent).
- **Light-load vs full-load regimes**: different dominant terms ([[q0214]]).
- **Duty cycle**: sets how conduction is split between high and low side ([[q0205]]).
:::

::: answer
"At full load in a typical 12 V to 3.3 V buck, the biggest losses are the two FETs and the inductor, in that order, and together they are 90 to 95% of the total. The low-side FET is usually the single largest term because it conducts for $1-D$ of the cycle, 72% here, so it dissipates $I^2R$ for most of the period. Then the high-side FET, which has less conduction loss but all the hard switching loss. Then the inductor: its DCR loss $I^2R$ plus core loss. In my 2 A example: low side about 180 milliwatts including dead time and recovery, high side about 140, inductor about 120, out of 457, so efficiency is 93.5%.

The capacitors are negligible: ESR loss is a few milliwatts. The feedback divider and controller bias are tens of microwatts to a few milliwatts.

But the ranking depends on the operating point. At light load, the $I^2R$ terms vanish and what's left is fixed: gate drive, $C_{oss}$, recovery, core and quiescent current, about 100 milliwatts here, which is why efficiency falls to 74% at 100 milliamps. At high input voltage, switching and recovery grow with $V_{in}$: at 48 V switching is over half the total. At high frequency, switching dominates. And at high current, conduction in the FETs and inductor takes over; at 5 A it's 85% of the loss."
:::

::: technical
**Reference design** (12 V to 3.3 V, $f=500$ kHz, $L=4.7$ µH, FETs 60/40 mΩ, DCR 20 mΩ; model of [[q0213]]). Loss groups in mW:

| Operating point | FET conduction | Inductor (DCR+core) | Switching, dead time, recovery | Gate + $I_q$ | Caps | Total | $\eta$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 A | 4 | 42 | 41 | 27 | 0.4 | 115 | 74.1% |
| 0.5 A | 15 | 47 | 58 | 27 | 0.6 | 147 | 91.8% |
| **2 A** | **186** | **122** | **120** | **27** | **2.8** | **457** | **93.5%** |
| 5 A | 1141 | 542 | 243 | 27 | 15 | 1969 | 89.3% |

Shares at 2 A: FETs 41% conduction plus part of the 26% switching group; inductor 27%; switching/recovery 26%; gate and $I_q$ 6%; caps 0.6%.

**Scaling laws.**
- Conduction: $\propto I_{rms}^2$; FET part $\propto D R_H+(1-D)R_L$.
- Overlap: $\tfrac12V_{in}I\,t\,f$; $C_{oss}$, gate, recovery: $\propto V_{in}^{(0\text{ to }2)}f$.
- Quiescent: $I_qV_{in}$.
- Core: $\propto f^\alpha\Delta B^\beta$ (assumed 40 mW at the reference).

**Input-voltage dependence** (same $V_{out}$, $I_{out}$, $f$, $L$): $V_{in}=5$ V: 387 mW, 94.5%, conduction 55% of loss; 12 V: 457 mW, 93.5%; 24 V: 588 mW, 91.8%, switching 39%; 48 V: 882 mW, 88.2%, switching 56%.

**Frequency dependence**: 200 kHz: 438 mW (conduction and inductor 84%); 2 MHz: 833 mW (switching 57%).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The numbers are from a representative loss model, with an assumed core loss and generic FET parameters. They show the pattern, which agrees with typical datasheet efficiency curves, but not exact values for a given part. At 5 A the 4.7 µH inductor in the model would likely be near saturation; real designs would change it.
:::

::: short
"At nominal load: the low-side FET (conducts $1-D$ of the cycle, so the biggest $I^2R$), then the high-side FET (conduction plus all the hard switching loss), then the inductor (DCR plus core). Capacitors and feedback are negligible. In a 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A example, about 180, 140 and 120 mW of 457 mW. The ranking shifts: at light load fixed losses (gate drive, recovery, quiescent) dominate; at high $V_{in}$ or high frequency switching loss dominates; at high current conduction dominates."
:::

::: long
"For a typical buck at its rated load, the answer is the two power FETs and the inductor. They typically account for 90% or more of the loss.

Take my 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, 500 kilohertz example. The total is about 457 milliwatts, 93.5% efficient. The biggest item is the low-side FET, about 180 milliwatts, because it conducts $1-D$, 72% of the cycle, so its $I^2R$ runs most of the time, and it also has dead-time diode conduction and reverse recovery. Next the high-side FET, about 140, which conducts only 27% of the time but does all the hard switching: overlap loss is $\tfrac12 V I\,t\,f$, about 60 milliwatts. Then the inductor, about 120: DCR loss at 20 milliohms is 80 and core loss maybe 40. Everything else is small: gate drive and quiescent current about 30, and the capacitors' ESR just a few milliwatts.

But the ranking is not fixed. If I drop the load to 100 milliamps, conduction vanishes, because it goes as $I^2$, and I'm left with the fixed loss: gate charge, $C_{oss}$, recovery, core and quiescent, about 110 milliwatts, so efficiency collapses to 74%. If I raise the input voltage to 48 V, switching and recovery losses scale with $V_{in}$ and become over half the loss. If I raise the frequency to 2 MHz, switching terms again take over. And at 5 amps, conduction in the FETs and inductor is 85% of the loss, and the inductor is probably near saturation.

So my rule: heavy load, low $V_{in}$, low $f$: look at $R_{DS(on)}$ and DCR. Light load: look at fixed losses and mode. High $V_{in}$ or high $f$: look at switching, $Q_{rr}$, and dead time."
:::

::: terms
- **Conduction loss**: $I^2R$ in on-state resistance and winding resistance.
- **Switching loss**: overlap loss plus $C_{oss}$, gate and recovery losses.
- **Fixed loss**: load-independent loss (gate drive, quiescent, parts of core loss).
- **Load-dependent efficiency curve**: rises, plateaus, falls with $I^2$ loss.
- **Light-load efficiency**: dominated by fixed loss.
- **Loss budget**: the itemized breakdown ([[q0213]]).
- **Thermal imaging**: finding the hot component to locate loss.
- **PFM**: pulse-frequency mode, reducing switching loss at light load.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "FETs and the inductor."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the ranking with numbers and the physical reason for each (duty-weighted conduction, hard switching on the high side, DCR and core).
- Says clearly what is negligible (capacitor ESR, feedback divider) and why.
- Shows how the ranking moves with load, $V_{in}$ and $f_{sw}$ and explains with scaling laws ($I^2$, $V_{in}f$).
- Mentions the diagnostic use: infer from thermal image and efficiency curve shape which regime you are in.
- Notes that datasheet efficiency curves show the rise at light load, plateau and fall at heavy load, and why.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The MOSFETs are the biggest loss" without the inductor.** The inductor is routinely 20 to 30% of the loss and sometimes the largest.
- **Giving one ranking for all conditions.** At 100 mA the fixed terms dominate; at 48 V in, switching does.
- **Blaming the output capacitor.** ESR loss is a few milliwatts; the capacitor sets ripple and transients.
- **Ignoring dead time and recovery.** They can be 10% of the loss at higher $V_{in}$ and frequency ([[q0209]]).
- **Forgetting temperature.** At 100 °C $R_{DS(on)}$ and DCR rise 30 to 40%, increasing conduction share.
:::

::: deep
## The ranking at nominal load

For the reference design ([[q0213]]), by component:

| Rank | Component | Loss | Share | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Low-side FET | 182 mW | 40% | conducts $(1-D)=72.5\%$ of the time: 119 mW; plus dead time 22 mW and recovery 30 mW |
| 2 | High-side FET | 138 mW | 30% | 67 mW conduction plus 60 mW of overlap loss |
| 3 | Inductor | 122 mW | 27% | 82 mW DCR plus about 40 mW core |
| 4 | Controller | 12 mW | 3% | quiescent |
| 5 | Capacitors | 3 mW | 0.6% | ESR |

The first three are 97% of the total. The reason the low side leads is $D=0.275$: if the output were 9 V from 12 V ($D=0.75$) the high side would carry most of the conduction ([[q0205]]).

Figure: On log-log axes, the $I^2$ terms are steep lines; the fixed terms are flat. Below about 0.5 A the flat and linear groups (switching/recovery, inductor core, gate and $I_q$) dominate and total 100 to 150 mW regardless of load; above about 1 A the FET conduction takes over and at 5 A it is 58% of the loss. Efficiency is the ratio of output power to output plus these curves, hence the familiar hill-shaped efficiency curve.

Reading the efficiency curve ([[q0214]]):

- **Rising part (light load)**: fixed loss divided by a small $P_{out}$.
- **Peak** (here about 1 A, 93.8%): conduction loss equals the fixed loss.
- **Falling part (heavy load)**: $I^2R$ grows faster than $P_{out}$.

## How the ranking moves with $V_{in}$

Same $V_{out}=3.3$ V, 2 A, 500 kHz, 4.7 µH:

| $V_{in}$ | FET conduction | Inductor | Switching group | Gate+$I_q$ | Total | $\eta$ | Dominant |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 V | 214 mW (55%) | 89 | 61 | 20 | 387 | 94.5% | conduction |
| 12 V | 186 (41%) | 122 | 120 | 27 | 457 | 93.5% | conduction, balanced |
| 24 V | 176 (30%) | 139 | 231 (39%) | 39 | 588 | 91.8% | switching |
| 48 V | 171 (19%) | 149 | 498 (56%) | 63 | 882 | 88.2% | switching |

(The model reuses the 12 V FET parameters; a real 48 V design uses 60 to 100 V devices with higher $R_{DS(on)}$ and charge.) Switching loss grows with $V_{in}$ because the overlap loss ($\propto V_{in}$), $C_{oss}$ ($\propto V_{in}^2$) and recovery ($\propto V_{in}$) terms all do. The quiescent term $I_qV_{in}$ also scales. Meanwhile conduction falls slightly because the duty cycle falls, which shifts conduction toward the low-side FET and lowers the RMS current ripple... but the inductor ripple grows, adding RMS current and core loss.

## How the ranking moves with $f_{sw}$

At 200 kHz (same $L$): total 438 mW, of which conduction and inductor are 84%: a "conduction-limited" design. At 2 MHz: 833 mW, switching group 57%: a "switching-limited" design. See [[q0216]] for the full efficiency-vs-frequency curve.

## What is *not* a major loss

- **Capacitors**: ESR loss in the output capacitor is $\Delta I^2/12\cdot ESR=0.4$ mW; in the input capacitor $I_{rms}^2ESR=2.4$ mW. They do affect ripple and EMI, not efficiency.
- **Feedback divider**: $V_{out}/(R_1+R_2)$, microwatts to a milliwatt.
- **Bootstrap diode/capacitor**: gate charge is already counted in the driver loss.
- **PCB copper**: 10 mΩ extra costs $4.09\times0.01=41$ mW — not negligible if the layout is poor (2 A, thin traces), so keep the power path short and wide.

## Diagnosing a real board

1. Measure efficiency vs load and compare to the hill shape. A low plateau means conduction or switching problems; a poor light-load end means fixed losses or mode.
2. Use a thermal camera at full load: the low-side FET, high-side FET and inductor should be the warmest; if the controller is the hottest, check gate drive or quiescent current.
3. Vary $f_{sw}$ (if the controller allows): loss that moves linearly with $f$ is switching-related.
4. Vary $V_{in}$: loss growing with $V_{in}$ points to switching, recovery and quiescent terms.
5. Compare with the calculation: a gap of more than 20% points to a missed term (core loss, copper, bias supply).

::: applications
- **Selecting FETs for a given operating point**: low-$R_{DS(on)}$ for high-current POL; low-$Q_g$/low-$Q_{rr}$ for high-$V_{in}$ and high-$f$ designs.
- **Reading datasheet efficiency curves**: the shape tells which loss dominates in each current range.
- **Battery-powered products**: attacking fixed loss at light load.
- **Thermal design**: allocating watts to the low side, high side and inductor locations on the PCB.
- **Debug of unexpected heat**: unusual hot spots indicate a mismatch with the expected ranking.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Representative loss model** for a 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, 500 kHz silicon synchronous buck; real shares change with part selection.
- **Core loss** assumed at 40 mW and independent of load; in reality it depends on ripple current, which is load-independent in CCM, but changes in PFM/DCM modes.
- **CCM, forced-PWM** at light load; in PFM the fixed loss scales down with load.
- **$V_{in}$ sweep** keeps the same FETs; real high-voltage designs use different devices.
- **Temperature** at 25 °C; hot parts shift weight toward conduction.
- If the converter is **asynchronous**, the diode becomes the largest single loss at low $V_{out}$ ([[q0201]]).
:::

::: followups
### Why is the low-side FET usually the biggest loss, not the high-side?
Because $D<0.5$ in most step-down converters, so the low side conducts for $(1-D)$ of the cycle: 72.5% vs 27.5% in the example. With equal resistances it has $(1-D)/D=2.6$ times the conduction loss. The high side has the hard-switching loss to make up, but at 500 kHz that is roughly equal to its conduction loss. At $D>0.5$ it reverses ([[q0205]]).

### What dominates efficiency at 100 mA, and what do you do about it?
Fixed losses: gate drive, $C_{oss}$, reverse recovery, core loss and quiescent current, about 110 mW against 330 mW of output power, hence 74% (forced PWM). Use PFM or diode-emulation modes, choose a controller with low $I_q$, and lower the frequency at light load.

### Your buck is 4 points less efficient at 24 V in than at 12 V in with the same output. Which losses would you suspect?
Switching-related: overlap loss grows with $V_{in}$, $C_{oss}$ loss with $V_{in}^2$, recovery with $V_{in}$, and quiescent current times $V_{in}$. The model gives 93.5% to 91.8% (1.7 points); a 4-point drop suggests an additional term such as high $Q_{rr}$ or slow edges, or a FET with a higher $R_{DS(on)}$ because it was chosen for the higher voltage rating.

### How would you tell from a thermal image whether conduction or switching loss dominates?
Compare which devices heat with load and frequency. If the low-side FET and inductor are hottest and temperature scales with $I^2$, it is conduction. If the high-side FET and the low-side FET's recovery are hot and temperature scales mainly with frequency and $V_{in}$, it is switching. Change $f_{sw}$ or load and watch which parts respond.

### What if the converter were a diode (asynchronous) buck?
The diode becomes the largest loss: $(1-D)I_{out}V_f=0.65$ W at 0.45 V in this example, more than the three largest items of the synchronous design combined (about 400 mW). The ranking would be: diode, then inductor, then the single high-side FET ([[q0201]]).
:::
