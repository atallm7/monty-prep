::: basics
### In plain words
A sense resistor produces a tiny voltage, often only tens of millivolts. If the amplifier that reads it is far away, that faint signal must cross a noisy board and picks up more noise the farther it goes. The best fix is to amplify the signal right at the resistor, so the long trip carries a strong signal.

### Picture it
Think of a whisper across a loud party. The farther the listener stands, the more the party noise drowns the whisper. Handing the speaker a megaphone right where they stand fixes it, because the party noise stays the same but the voice is far louder.

### Words you need
- **Sense signal**: the small voltage across the sense resistor, proportional to current. See [[q0554]].
- **Current sense amplifier**: a chip that multiplies that small voltage to a larger one. See [[q0449]].
- **Gain**: how many times an amplifier multiplies a signal.
- **Noise pickup**: unwanted voltage a long wire collects from nearby switching. It depends on the wire, not on the signal. See [[q0555]].
- **Ground offset**: two "ground" points on a board differ slightly in voltage when current flows through the copper between them.

### The simplest example
A 5 mΩ resistor carries 8 A, so the signal is 8 × 0.005 = 40 mV. Say the long wire picks up 2.4 mV of noise. That is 2.4 / 40 = 6% of the signal. Now put an amplifier with a gain of 50 at the resistor. The signal sent down the wire is 50 × 40 mV = 2 V. The wire still picks up the same 2.4 mV, which is now only 0.12% of the signal. The signal-to-noise ratio improved 50 times.

That is the whole idea: make the signal big before it travels. The rest of this page covers other options and ground offset errors.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can reason about a layout constraint instead of reciting a rule. "Short traces" is the easy answer; the interviewer wants to hear what you do when you *can't* have them. The real skills:

1. Recognising that the weak point is a millivolt-level, high-impedance signal travelling across a noisy board.
2. Knowing the standard fixes: move the amplifier to the resistor, send an amplified or digital signal, or protect the raw pair.
3. Remembering the ground offset between the resistor's local ground and the receiver's ground.
:::

::: why
In real products the resistor sits where the current is (at the battery connector, on a motor phase, at the bus input) and the MCU or ADC sits where the board has room. The two are rarely side by side. Engineers who treat "far" as a design problem with a solution, rather than as a layout flaw, ship products that read current accurately. This also connects layout to system thinking: gain placement, signal levels, grounds and where digitisation happens.
:::

::: concepts
- **Signal-to-interference ratio**: injected noise on a long line is roughly independent of the signal, so a bigger signal is proportionally cleaner. See [[q0555]].
- **Current-sense amplifier (CSA)**: gain stage that converts the sense voltage to a larger, ground-referenced voltage. See [[q0449]].
- **Kelvin routing** at the resistor end. See [[q0554]], [[q0557]].
- **Ground offset**: the voltage between two "ground" points when current flows in the copper between them. See [[q0497]].
- **Differential transmission**: a pair that carries the signal and rejects common-mode noise. See [[q0565]].
- **Current-output and digital sensors**: alternatives that are tolerant of long runs.
:::

::: technical
**Interpretation.** "Far from your" is most likely "amplifier / ADC" (sense chain), but see also "load" or "source" (power-path length) in the interpretations.

**What breaks as the distance grows.**
- *Magnetic pickup* $v = A\,dB/dt$ with $A = g\cdot L$ grows with length. 50 mm × 0.2 mm near a 5 A, 20 ns edge gives about 50 mV ([[q0555]]).
- *Capacitive pickup* $i = C_m\,dv/dt$ grows with length. 0.1 pF from a 2.4 V/ns node is 0.24 mA; across a 10 Ω filter resistor it is 2.4 mV, which is 6 % of a 40 mV signal.
- *Ground offset*: the receiving ADC's ground differs from the resistor's by $I_{gnd}R_{gnd}$. 5 A in 5 mΩ is 25 mV, i.e. 0.76 % of a 3.3 V full scale.
- *Source impedance and filtering*: a long high-impedance run plus the filter capacitor and any leakage adds error and slows the signal.

**Fixes, in order of preference.**
1. **Amplify at the resistor.** A CSA of gain $G$ at the Kelvin tap turns a $V_s$ into $GV_s$ with a low output impedance (ohms). Injected noise on the long run is unchanged in volts, so the signal-to-interference ratio improves by $G$ (34 dB for $G = 50$). Drive the line from a low impedance and filter at the ADC.
2. **Digitise at the resistor.** A power-monitor IC with an I²C/SPI interface (for example, the INA219 / INA226 family) sends bits, which tolerate noise far better than millivolts. Keep the digital edges slow.
3. **Current-output CSA.** Some high-side monitors output a current proportional to the sense voltage; a resistor at the *receiver* converts it to a voltage. Current signals are insensitive to the line's resistance and to capacitively coupled voltage noise on a low-impedance node.
4. **Protect the raw pair** if it must travel: matched, tight, short stubs, over a plane, flanked by ground vias, away from aggressors, with the filter at the amplifier end.
5. **Isolate** if the grounds really differ (high-side with a large voltage, safety isolation).

**Ground reference.** Run the amplifier's reference/ground to the resistor's low side with its own wire/trace, and bring the output *and its reference* to the ADC (differential ADC input, or a difference stage at the receiver).
:::

::: terms
- **Signal-to-interference ratio (SIR)**: signal amplitude divided by injected noise amplitude; amplifying before the noise is added improves it.
- **Current-output CSA**: a current-sense amplifier that outputs a current proportional to the sense voltage, converted to voltage at the receiver.
- **Power monitor (digital)**: an IC that measures sense voltage and bus voltage and reports numbers over I²C/SPI.
- **Ground offset**: the difference in "ground" potential between two points caused by current in the copper between them.
- **Differential transmission**: sending a signal as the difference between two lines, so common-mode noise cancels.
- **Isolation amplifier**: an amplifier with galvanic isolation between input and output, tolerant of ground differences.
- **Remote ground sensing**: using a separate sense line to reference the receiver to the source's ground.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "keep traces short; if far, use twisted pair or shielding".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Reframes the problem: noise is injected at a fixed absolute level, so raising the signal level *before* the run improves SIR by the gain (34 dB at G = 50).
- Offers an ordered set of options: amplify locally, digitise locally, current-output, protected raw pair, isolation.
- Brings up the ground offset and quantifies it (25 mV from 5 A × 5 mΩ).
- Knows that filtering belongs at the receiving end and the filter resistors should stay low.
- Can say what they'd measure to prove it: scope the pair at the amplifier with the load switching.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Adding more filter instead of moving the amplifier.** Filtering at the amplifier end cannot remove noise that is within the signal band, and heavy filtering slows the current-limit response.
- **Sending a raw sense voltage through a connector and a cable** because the schematic has the resistor "on the left page" and the amplifier "on the right page".
- **Forgetting the ground offset.** The output is referenced to the amplifier's ground, the ADC to its own. Run the reference with the signal.
- **Putting a high-impedance output on a long run.** A long line driven from kΩ levels picks up capacitive noise easily. Drive it from ohms.
- **Routing the long run past the switch node because it is the shortest path.** The right-of-way matters as much as the length.
- **Assuming a twisted pair solves everything.** It cancels magnetic pickup well, but not capacitive coupling to one wire, and it does nothing about ground offset.
:::

::: deep
## Why "far" is a different problem from "close"

Close, the sense pair is a short, tight loop and the layout rules ([[q0554]], [[q0555]]) are enough. Far, three things scale against you:

1. **Pickup grows with length.** Magnetic: $v = g L\,\dot B$. Capacitive: $i = C_m'\,L\,dv/dt$.
2. **The ground the signal is referenced to drifts.** The resistor's low pad and the ADC's ground are separated by copper that carries other currents.
3. **Source impedance and leakage matter more.** A filtered sense line is tens of ohms and passes through vias and fills that may have flux, moisture and leakage.

Noise injected onto a line is set by the aggressor and the geometry, not by the victim's signal. That observation drives the main solution.

## The cascade argument: amplify first

If a long line collects an interference voltage $v_n$ whatever the signal, then

$$\text{SIR} = \frac{V_{signal}}{v_n}.$$

Put a gain $G$ in front of the line and SIR becomes $G V_s / v_n$. So the improvement is $20\log_{10}G$ dB: 34 dB for $G = 50$, 40 dB for $G = 100$. The line's low impedance helps too, since capacitively injected *current* produces $v = i Z$ and $Z$ at an amplifier output is ohms instead of the tens-of-ohms-plus of a filtered sense line.

## Worked example: why gain at the source also fixes the ADC resolution

A 5 mΩ shunt with 8 A full scale gives 40 mV. A 12-bit ADC with a 3.3 V reference has an LSB of 0.806 mV, so the *raw* signal would span only about 50 codes. With $G = 50$ the full-scale output is 2 V = 2482 codes. So amplification at the source solves both the transport problem and the quantisation problem ([[q0453]] for the gain selection). The CSA's own offset and drift are then what limit low-current accuracy.

## When you can't move the amplifier

Sometimes the resistor is on a daughter board, a motor cable or a connector, and the amplifier is on the main board.

- **Twisted pair or shielded twisted pair** from the resistor's Kelvin pads: cancels magnetic pickup by the twist, and a shield handles electric coupling. Connect the shield at the receiving end (or per [[q0571]] for HF) and filter at the receiver.
- **On the PCB**: tight pair, same layer, same length, continuous reference plane below, ground vias flanking it every few millimetres (a "guard" pair), no stubs, no vias unless paired. Keep a distance of several times the trace-to-plane height from aggressors ([[q0566]]).
- **Low-value filter resistors, large differential C**, placed at the receiving end only.
- **Differential receive**: use a CSA or instrumentation amplifier with high CMRR at the receiver.

## Ground reference at the far end

An amplifier output is referenced to the amplifier's ground. If the ADC's ground is 25 mV different (5 A through 5 mΩ of ground trace), the reading shifts by 25 mV, 0.76 % of a 3.3 V range, and it moves when the load current moves, which is exactly the thing being measured. Fixes: tie the amplifier ground to the resistor's low side with a quiet connection; bring the amplifier's reference out to the ADC as a differential pair; or let the ADC be a differential input referenced to that pair. See also [[q0496]] and [[q0497]].

## Beyond the board

For a resistor out on a motor cable, a battery or a different assembly:

- **Digital monitor at the resistor**, powered locally, data over a short serial link.
- **Isolated amplifier or isolated modulator** when the shunt sits at high common-mode voltage (motor phase on an inverter, high-side of a battery bus).
- **Current-loop (4–20 mA) transmitter** in industrial settings.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Which specific current-sense IC (current-output vs voltage-output vs digital) suits a given board depends on bandwidth, common-mode range and cost; the part families named above are examples of each type, so check current datasheets before choosing.

## Worked example: what the receiving end must do

Take the amplified architecture above: 5 mΩ shunt, $G=50$, 2 V full scale on a long run, 12-bit ADC with a 3.3 V reference. Three numbers decide whether it works.

**Ground offset with a single-ended receive.** If 5 A returns through 5 mΩ of ground copper between the amplifier and the ADC, the reading shifts by 25 mV, which is $25/0.806=31$ LSB, or 0.76% of the 3.3 V range. Worse, it is proportional to the load current, so it acts like a gain error: at 5 A the true reading is 1.25 V (full scale 2 V is 8 A), and 25 mV is a 2% error. With a differential receive that senses the amplifier's own reference, the same 25 mV is a common-mode voltage. A receiver with 60 dB CMRR reduces it to 25 µV, which is 0.03 LSB, below the noise floor.

**Settling through the receiver filter.** With $R_F=100\ \Omega$ and $C_F=10$ nF, $\tau=1\ \mu$s and the corner is 159 kHz. To settle within one LSB of a full-scale 12-bit step takes $\ln(4096)\,\tau=8.3\ \mu$s (half an LSB: $\ln(8192)\,\tau=9.0\ \mu$s), so the sampling rate of a signal that must settle between samples is limited to roughly 110 to 120 kHz. Choose the filter against the ADC's sample-and-hold requirement too; a larger $C_F$ than the ADC's sampling capacitance (often tens of pF) provides charge for the sampling kick.

**Amplifier drive of the line.** A voltage-output current-sense amplifier drives the cable capacitance (about 100 pF per meter for a typical twisted pair, order of magnitude only) through its output resistance. A 30 cm run is 30 pF, harmless; a 3 m cable is 300 pF and can turn a tens-of-ohm output resistance into a visible pole or even an unstable amplifier. Put the isolating $R_F$ at the amplifier end in that case, or select an amplifier rated for capacitive loads.

## Limiting cases

- **$G\to1$:** the long run carries a raw 40 mV signal and every analysis from [[q0555]] applies at full strength; the line effectively is the sensor's weak point.
- **$G$ very large:** the amplifier saturates on the common-mode ripple or on a fault current (a short-circuit current ten times full scale drives the output into the rail). Either choose a gain with headroom or use a CSA with a defined overload recovery time.
- **Bandwidth:** gain-bandwidth limits $G$: a CSA with 500 kHz bandwidth at $G=50$ is a poor fit for a 100 kHz current loop, because phase lag near the crossover reduces the control loop's phase margin.
- **Digital transport at the resistor:** removes the analog noise problem completely but adds latency from conversion and serial transfer, which matters in fast current loops.

## Bench verification

1. **Compare both ends of the long run** with the same scope, at full load and at zero load: the signal at the amplifier output and at the ADC pin should be identical in amplitude, with the ADC pin showing any added pickup. Subtract to see the noise the run added.
2. **Check the ground offset directly**: measure the DC voltage between the amplifier's ground pin and the ADC's ground pin with a millivolt meter at 0 A and at full load. Any change is a ground-return error that single-ended reception will inherit.
3. **Step the load** (or inject a known current with a bench supply) and confirm gain linearity across the range, before and after the board has heated, to separate layout error from drift.
4. **Disconnect the long run and substitute a short run:** if the noise disappears, the run is the problem, not the amplifier.

::: applications
- **Battery packs**: the shunt is at the pack terminal, and a gauge IC sits beside it, reporting over I²C or a one-wire interface to the main MCU.
- **Three-phase motor drives**: shunts at the bottom of each inverter leg with amplifiers or isolated modulators next to them, sending signals to a distant controller.
- **Server and telecom power boards**: digital power monitors at each rail's sense resistor feeding a board management controller.
- **Industrial 4–20 mA loops**: current signalling that tolerates long cables and resistance.
- **EV and power-tool battery management**: high-side shunts at high common-mode voltage using isolated amplifiers.
:::

::: assumptions
- **"Far" means the order of centimetres to tens of centimetres on a board**, with noisy converters in between. Over a metre of cable, treat it as a cabling problem.
- **The noise injected is roughly independent of the signal.** That holds for coupling from external aggressors; amplifier noise that scales with gain is a different story.
- **The ground return is shared.** If the amplifier and ADC are on a single solid plane with little current, the ground offset may be negligible.
- **Alternatives**: the question is truncated; "far from your load/source" is covered in the interpretations. If it means "far from the controller", the same remedies apply.
- **Part examples** are illustrative families, not recommendations.
:::

::: followups
### Why is amplifying before the long run better than amplifying after?
The noise voltage that couples onto the run is set by the geometry and the aggressor, not by the signal. If the signal is 50 times larger when it travels, the same noise is 50 times smaller relative to it (34 dB). Amplifying after the run just amplifies signal and noise together.

### The amplifier must be on the main board, 10 cm from the resistor. What do you do?
Route the sense pair as a tightly coupled, equal-length pair on one layer over a continuous ground plane with ground vias beside it. Keep it out of the neighbourhood of switch nodes and inductors. Put the filter at the amplifier. If the signal is still noisy, change the resistor to a larger value (more signal, within power limits) or move the amplifier to a small daughter or flex piece at the resistor.

### How do you handle a sense resistor that's on the other side of a connector?
Use a twisted pair, or better, move the amplifier or a digital monitor to the resistor side. Filter at the receiving end, ground the shield at the receiving end (or at both ends if RF matters), and use a differential receiver. Also add ESD protection at the connector.

### What if the ground at the ADC is 100 mV away from the ground at the resistor?
Then a single-ended reading is off by up to 100 mV, which is more than the signal. Use a differential read of the amplifier's output and its reference, tie the amplifier's reference to the resistor side, fix the ground path so the current doesn't flow in the measurement, or isolate the measurement.

### Scenario: the over-current trip fires randomly when the motor starts. The sense resistor is 8 cm from the comparator. How do you debug it?
Scope the sense pair differentially at the comparator, with the probe ground spring close, at motor start. If the glitches coincide with switch edges and show up common-mode on each line, improve the filter and CMRR. If they are differential, look at geometry: gap, length, neighbours, plane splits. Then improve: move the comparator next to the resistor or use an amplifier at the resistor, add blanking time in firmware or hardware for the start-up transient, and check the ground path.
:::

::: interpretations
"What if your sense resistor is far from your…" is cut off in the source. The most natural endings are "…amplifier" or "…ADC" (the sense chain), which is what this page assumes. Other plausible endings: "…load" or "…source" (meaning the resistor is far from where the current is used or comes from; the answer is that the extra copper in the path adds resistance and inductance, which matters for the load but not for the measurement if Kelvin routing is used) and "…controller". The remedies overlap: shorten, isolate, amplify early. Neighbours: [[q0554]] (routing recipe), [[q0555]] (why short) and [[q0557]] (Kelvin).
