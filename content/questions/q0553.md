::: testing
Whether you know that a capacitor on a board is a capacitor plus its mounting inductance, and that routing decides which of the two you get. The interviewer wants the rules for the small decoupling ceramics (closest to the pin, short wide connections, vias right at the pads, ground via straight to the plane, smallest value closest) and for bulk capacitors (near the regulator and the power entry or load cluster, fed through wide copper, with some ESR), plus the reasons: loop inductance, self-resonance, and anti-resonance peaks between capacitor values.
:::

::: why
Decoupling is where most power-integrity budgets are won or lost. A beautifully chosen 100 nF capacitor wired through 5 nH of trace and via is a 6 MHz resonator that does nothing at 100 MHz, where the chip is actually drawing its fast current. Reviewers check capacitor placement before almost anything else, because it is cheap to fix at layout time and impossible afterwards. This page covers the "how to route" side; the "why so many" and "what if far" siblings are in the decoupling section ([[q0503]] to [[q0510]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor model**: $C$ in series with ESR and ESL; self-resonance $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{ESL\cdot C})$. See [[q0509]].
- **Mounting inductance**: pads, traces and vias add to the capacitor's own ESL. See [[q0510]].
- **Loop area**: pin → capacitor → return plane → ground pin; small area, small $L$.
- **Hierarchy**: small and close for high frequency, bigger and farther for low frequency. See [[q0507]].
- **Anti-resonance**: parallel peaks between capacitor groups. See [[q0506]].
- **Bypass vs bulk**: [[q0032]].
:::

::: answer
"For decoupling capacitors I think in loops. The capacitor only helps if its loop to the pin is tiny, so I put the smallest, lowest-ESL ceramic, say 0.1 µF in 0402, right at the IC's power pin, on the same side as the IC, with the pin connected to the capacitor pad by a short wide trace, and the capacitor's ground pad connected by a via right next to it straight down to the ground plane, ideally on the plane layer closest to the top. If it's a power plane, the power via also sits right at the capacitor. I avoid long thin stubs and I don't share one via between several capacitors. For BGAs I put capacitors on the back, directly under the pins, or use via-in-pad.

The numbers show why: a 100 nF capacitor with 1.5 nH of mounting inductance self-resonates around 13 MHz and is 0.94 ohm at 100 MHz; with 6 nH from sloppy routing it's about 6.5 MHz and 3.8 ohms at 100 MHz.

For bulk capacitors, the electrolytic or polymer ones, the placement is less critical but still: near the regulator output and where power enters or at the load cluster, connected with wide copper or a plane and several vias, and I use their ESR to damp the anti-resonance with the ceramics. I use several values, small closest, and I check the impedance against a target with the PDN tool."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** $Z(f) = \sqrt{ESR^2 + (2\pi f L_{tot} - 1/(2\pi f C))^2}$, $L_{tot} = ESL + L_{mount}$ (pads, traces, vias, plane spreading). Self-resonant frequency $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{tot}C})$.

| 100 nF, $L_{tot}$ | $f_0$ | $|Z|$ at 100 MHz |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 nH | 22.5 MHz | 0.31 Ω |
| 1.5 nH | 13.0 MHz | 0.94 Ω |
| 3 nH | 9.2 MHz | 1.9 Ω |
| 6 nH | 6.5 MHz | 3.8 Ω |
| 20 nH (long trace) | 3.6 MHz | 12.6 Ω |

**Via inductance** (Johnson): $L \approx 5.08h[\ln(4h/d)+1]$ nH, $h,d$ in inches: about 1.3 nH for a 0.3 mm via in 1.6 mm. Two vias per pad halve it; vias placed beside or in the pad avoid trace inductance.

**Decoupling routing rules.**
1. One capacitor per power pin (or per pin pair as the datasheet says); smallest value nearest.
2. Capacitor on the IC's side (BGA: back side directly under, via-in-pad if the process allows).
3. Short, wide pin-to-pad connection; capacitor between the pin and the via, or the via right at the pad; no stub.
4. Ground: via adjacent to the ground pad, directly to a solid plane; reference plane layer as close to the top as stack-up allows (thin dielectric reduces plane spreading inductance).
5. Do not share vias; do not daisy-chain capacitors through thin traces.
6. Use adjacent via pairs (power and ground) with opposite current directions: mutual coupling reduces loop inductance.

**Bulk routing rules.** Near the regulator's output and the connector, or at the load cluster; connection by wide copper or plane with several vias; low-ESR ceramics plus polymer/electrolytic for damping: choose ESR near $\sqrt{L/C}$ of the resonance it must damp; check the impedance vs frequency against a target $Z_{target}=\Delta V/\Delta I$.

**Verification.** Impedance vs frequency (PDN simulation or measurement with a VNA), and the rail's transient and noise with a short-ground scope probe.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Mounting inductance depends on stack-up (dielectric thickness to the first plane), via geometry and capacitor package; the values in the table are typical planning figures. Capacitance of ceramics also derates with DC bias and temperature.
:::

::: long
"I think of every capacitor as a loop: power pin, capacitor, ground via, plane, back to the ground pin. The capacitor only helps at a frequency if that loop's inductance is small, so the whole job is minimising loop area.

For the small decoupling ceramics, the 0.1 microfarad kind, I put the smallest package, 0402, right at the IC's power pin on the same side, connect the pin to the pad with a short wide trace, and put the ground via right beside the ground pad, going straight down to a solid plane, ideally the layer closest to the top because the thin dielectric keeps the loop small. If power comes through a plane, the power via sits at the capacitor too. No long stubs, no sharing a via between capacitors, and for a BGA I put them on the back directly under the pins or use via-in-pad.

Why it matters: a 100 nanofarad capacitor with 1.5 nanohenries total has a self-resonance near 13 megahertz and about 0.94 ohms at 100 megahertz. With 6 nanohenries from sloppy routing it's about 6.5 megahertz and 3.8 ohms at 100 megahertz, so at the frequencies where the chip draws its fast current, it's not decoupling anything.

Bulk capacitors, the polymer or electrolytic ones, handle low frequencies and the slower load steps, so they sit near the regulator output and where power enters, on wide copper or a plane with several vias. Their ESR is a feature: it damps the anti-resonance peak between the bulk and the ceramics. I use a few values, smaller closer, and I check the impedance against the target for the rail."
:::

::: terms
- **Decoupling (bypass) capacitor**: a local capacitor that supplies fast current to a pin. See [[q0032]].
- **Bulk capacitor**: a large capacitor for low-frequency charge and damping.
- **ESL / ESR**: equivalent series inductance and resistance.
- **Mounting inductance**: inductance of pads, traces and vias connecting the capacitor.
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: where the capacitor's reactance cancels its inductance.
- **Anti-resonance**: a parallel resonance between capacitor groups, appearing as an impedance peak.
- **Target impedance**: $Z_{target}=\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I_{step}$.
- **Via-in-pad**: via placed in the capacitor's pad to remove trace inductance.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "put small capacitors close to the pins and big ones farther away."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames it as loop inductance and gives $f_0$ and impedance numbers for good and bad routing.
- Specifies the geometry: same side, short wide pin connection, via at the pad to a nearby plane, no shared vias, via-in-pad or backside for BGAs.
- Notes the plane layer proximity and via pairs' mutual-inductance benefit.
- Describes bulk placement, connection, and the role of ESR in damping anti-resonance.
- Mentions the target-impedance check and DC-bias derating.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Placing the capacitor close but routing through long thin traces and one shared via.** The mounting inductance cancels the benefit.
- **Putting the via on the far side of the capacitor** so the current path runs around it. The via goes at the pad, on the pin-side route.
- **Using the biggest capacitor closest.** Larger packages have more ESL; small goes first.
- **Same value everywhere,** leaving a gap at some frequencies and a sharp anti-resonance. See [[q0506]].
- **Forgetting the plane layer.** If the first plane is 1 mm below the top layer, the loop is ten times larger than with 0.1 mm.
- **Assuming nominal capacitance.** DC bias and temperature can cut ceramic capacitance by half.
- **Placing bulk capacitors far from the regulator with thin traces.** They become inductive and useless for the load steps they exist to supply.
:::

::: deep
## A capacitor is a loop

A decoupling capacitor delivers charge to a pin in the time before the regulator can respond. The current takes a loop: from the capacitor's positive pad to the IC power pin, through the IC, out of the ground pin, to the plane, and back up the ground via to the capacitor's other pad. The inductance of this loop is what limits how fast the capacitor can deliver:

$$Z(f)=\sqrt{ESR^2+\left(2\pi f L_{tot}-\frac{1}{2\pi f C}\right)^2}$$

with $L_{tot} = ESL + L_{pad} + L_{trace} + L_{via} + L_{plane\ spreading}$. For a 0402 100 nF the ESL is 0.4 to 0.7 nH; the rest is layout, often larger than the capacitor itself.

## Routing the small decoupling capacitors

**Order of connection.** From the power pin: pin → short trace → capacitor pad (and the power via beside it, if the supply is on another layer) → capacitor → ground pad → ground via → plane. The via should be as close to the pad as possible (touching or at the side facing the load). A trace stub between the capacitor and the via, or the via on the far end, adds inductance.

**Via placement in detail.** The inductance of a via pair is lower the closer power and ground vias are (the opposing currents' fields cancel). Place the power and ground vias of a capacitor side by side. Two ground vias per pad halve the inductance. Via-in-pad (filled and capped) removes the stub; many assemblers require filled vias for pads under 0402.

**Layer and plane.** The shorter the vertical path and the thinner the dielectric to the plane, the lower the loop inductance, so put the key reference plane on layer 2 with a thin prepreg (0.1 mm or less on a 4-layer power board).

**Top vs bottom.** For BGAs the pins are under the part; fan-out vias drop to the back where capacitors sit directly beneath. The capacitor then is only a board thickness (1.6 mm) away, but via pairs keep inductance near 1 to 1.5 nH.

**Number and hierarchy.** One capacitor per power pin or pair; mix a few values (for example 10 nF, 100 nF, 1 µF, 10 µF) so that SRFs spread across the band, with the smallest closest ([[q0507]]). Same-value capacitors in parallel also lower inductance ($L/n$). Avoid large numbers of identical capacitors without damping: they leave a parallel resonance with the next group ([[q0506]]).

**Don'ts.** No shared vias; no daisy-chaining capacitor to capacitor through thin traces; no capacitor placed across a plane split; no capacitor whose ground return crosses a noisy region.

## Routing the bulk capacitors

Bulk capacitors (10 µF to 1000 µF; ceramic, polymer or electrolytic) supply the slow part of load steps and stabilise the regulator. Their ESL is 1 to 10 nH plus mounting, so they act as capacitors only below a few MHz.

- **Where:** near the regulator's output (it is part of the regulator's loop), near the power entry (to buffer the cable), and at each load cluster that has big steps (memory banks, FPGAs, motors).
- **How:** wide copper or plane; several vias to each plane. Their placement tolerance is looser than the small ones but the distance still adds inductance (about 1 nH/mm).
- **ESR:** a polymer or electrolytic's 5 to 100 mΩ ESR damps the parallel resonance with the ceramics: the peak impedance at the anti-resonance is about $\sqrt{L/C}/Q$, and choosing ESR near $\sqrt{L/C}$ of the interacting pair flattens it.
- **Ceramic-only bulk:** low ESR gives a sharp peak; add a lossy element (a polymer in parallel, or a small series resistance) if the regulator or load is sensitive.

## Target impedance

For a rail with a ±3% tolerance on 1.0 V (±30 mV) and a 0.6 A load step, $Z_{target} = 30\ \text{mV}/0.6\ \text{A} = 50$ mΩ up to the frequency where the on-die and package capacitance take over. The capacitor network must keep $|Z|\le Z_{target}$ across the band. Count the capacitors needed at each decade, then verify with a PDN tool or a VNA measurement.

## A layout recipe

1. For each IC power pin, place the smallest ceramic as close as the courtyard allows, on the IC side.
2. Route pin → capacitor by a 0.2 to 0.3 mm wide trace shorter than 1.5 mm, or direct pad-to-pad.
3. Put ground and power vias adjacent to the capacitor pads (or in-pad), connecting to the planes on layers 2/3.
4. Add the next value nearby, further out; share the plane but not the vias.
5. Place bulk near the regulator and entry with wide copper and several vias.
6. Run a PDN check against $Z_{target}$.
7. Check on the board: ripple and droop at the pin with a short-ground probe, under the worst step.
:::

::: applications
- **FPGA and processor BGAs** with banks of small ceramics under the package and larger ones around it.
- **Microcontroller boards** with a 0.1 µF capacitor on every power pin and a bulk capacitor at the regulator.
- **DDR memory** with per-pin or per-group decoupling and bulk near the DIMM.
- **Mixed-signal ADC/DAC boards** where the analog supply pins have close, quiet decoupling.
- **RF modules** with decoupling at each stage's supply pin and ferrite isolation.
- **Motor drivers** with bulk electrolytic capacitors on the bus and small ceramics at the gate drivers.
:::

::: assumptions
The impedance numbers assume 0402/0603 X7R ceramics with 0.4 to 1 nH of ESL, 0.1 mm to 0.2 mm prepreg to the first plane, 0.3 mm vias and a single capacitor per branch; real values depend on package, via structure, and stack-up. Capacitance values assume no derating; at DC bias, effective capacitance can fall by half or more, which shifts the SRF up and the impedance up at low frequency. At low frequencies the capacitor placement is less critical and the regulator's own response dominates; this page concerns routing for frequencies above a few MHz. For two-layer boards without planes, the "plane" is a ground pour and loop inductance is higher. Some ICs specify exact capacitor placement; follow the datasheet.
:::

::: followups
### Where exactly should the via go relative to the capacitor pad?
At the pad edge, on the side that shortens the current path, or in the pad if the process permits. The rule is minimal trace between the pad and the via and minimal distance between the power-via and ground-via, so their opposing currents' magnetic fields cancel. A via on the far end of the capacitor makes the current go around it, adding loop area.

### Why do several capacitor values have anti-resonance peaks, and how does routing affect them?
Above its SRF a capacitor is inductive; in parallel with a smaller capacitor that is still capacitive at the same frequency, the two form a parallel LC and the impedance peaks between their SRFs. Higher mounting inductance moves the peak lower and widens the gap. ESR damps it, which is why bulk capacitors with ESR help, and why minimising $L_{mount}$ (and using several equal capacitors in parallel) lowers the peak.

### Do you need a decoupling capacitor on every power pin?
Usually yes, or per pin pair as the datasheet states, since each pin feeds a different block with its own current. Where several pins are adjacent and fed from one plane, one capacitor per pair can work if it is within a couple of millimetres. For FPGAs, the vendor's guide specifies counts and values per rail.

### How would you decide the number of 100 nF capacitors for a rail?
From the target impedance: each 100 nF capacitor with 1.5 nH mounting has about 1 Ω near 100 MHz; $n$ in parallel give $1/n$ of that (ignoring mutual coupling). To meet 50 mΩ at 100 MHz would need 20 capacitors, so I'd use smaller package, better vias (lower $L$), plane capacitance and on-die/package capacitance, then confirm with a PDN simulation.

### How do you check that the decoupling works on a real board?
Measure the rail at the IC pin with a short-ground-spring probe (or a coax tip) while the load steps, looking at droop and ringing, and use a VNA or impedance analyser to measure the PDN impedance from the pin. Compare against the target. A spectrum-analyser/near-field scan can also reveal resonances.
:::
