::: testing
Whether you understand that **layers aren't interchangeable**: outer layers are exposed to air, probes and components, inner layers are buried between planes. The interviewer wants a rule set for what goes where, with reasons grounded in physics (field containment, return paths, propagation speed, ESD/EMI) and manufacturing (access, rework, vias). They also listen for the trade-off: inner routing is quieter but costs vias, and outer routing is simple but radiates and picks up more.
:::

::: why
Every layout has this decision on every net: top, bottom or inside? Bad answers cause real problems: a clock on the top layer radiating at an EMC test, an ADC input on a layer next to a switching node, or a differential pair that changes layer type mid-route and picks up skew. It also determines whether you can debug the board: you can probe a top trace, but an inner trace is invisible without a via or test point.
:::

::: concepts
- **Microstrip vs. stripline**: outer traces have one plane and air above; inner traces sit between two planes. See [[q0479]].
- **Return current path**: every signal needs an adjacent plane; routing layer choice follows from this. See [[q0478]], [[q0486]].
- **Crosstalk and orthogonal routing**: adjacent signal layers should cross at right angles. See [[q0490]].
- **EMI/ESD**: shielded inner traces radiate and couple less. See [[q0489]].
- **Vias**: layer changes cost inductance, impedance discontinuities and area. See [[q0515]].
- **Assembly access**: parts, test points and rework happen on the outer layers.
:::

::: answer
"My rule of thumb is that the outer layers are for components and short connections, and the inner layers are for the signals I want protected.

On the top and bottom I put everything that has to touch a part: pad fanouts, short point-to-point links, power pours, and things I want to be able to probe or tune, like test points and RF matching networks. Anything that carries a lot of current gets wide copper on the outer layers where the heat can leave.

On the inner signal layers, between two planes, I put long runs and fast or noisy signals, like clocks and DDR lines. Because they're a stripline with a plane on each side, the field stays inside the board, so there's less radiation and less pickup from outside, plus better ESD immunity. The cost is vias to get there, and I can't probe them.

I also keep adjacent signal layers orthogonal to each other, one horizontal and one vertical, to reduce broadside crosstalk. And I keep a whole bus or a differential pair on the same layer, because the propagation speed is different on outer and inner layers, and I'd rather not create skew by changing layer types."
:::

::: technical
**Typical assignment**

| Layer type | Put here | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Top/Bottom (microstrip) | Component fanout, short links, RF with tuning, test points, high-current pours, sensitive analog near its IC | Access, probing, rework; thermal path to air; shorter via stubs |
| Inner signal (stripline) | Long fast buses, clocks, DDR, high-speed pairs between BGA and far parts, noisy signals | Field contained between planes; lower emissions; ESD/EMI immunity |
| Planes | Ground (and power) | Return path and shielding. Avoid cutting them with signals |

**Propagation speed.** A microstrip's effective dielectric constant is lower ($\varepsilon_{eff}\approx 3.2$ for 0.35 mm over 0.2 mm, $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$) because part of the field is in air; a stripline sees the full $\varepsilon_r$. Delay: $t_{pd}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/c$:

- microstrip: $\sqrt{3.24}/c \approx 6.0$ ps/mm;
- stripline: $\sqrt{4.3}/c \approx 6.9$ ps/mm.

So 100 mm of trace is 600 ps outside vs 690 ps inside, a 90 ps difference. For a DDR pair or differential pair routed partly on each type, that's skew.

**Impedance.** For the same dielectric height, stripline has lower impedance for a given width, so inner traces are usually narrower for 50 Ω: about 0.37 mm for microstrip over 0.2 mm; about 0.15 mm for stripline between planes 0.4 mm apart ($\varepsilon_r=4.3$, copper 35/17.5 µm; Hammerstad–Jensen and Wheeler formulas, within a few percent of a field solver).

**Rules**
- Adjacent signal layers: orthogonal.
- Don't route over plane splits or gaps. See [[q0486]].
- Layer changes: move the reference with the signal, and put a ground via within a few mm of the signal via.
- Keep sensitive analog nets away from noisy layers, and ideally on the layer with a clean ground reference.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Some engineers prefer routing the very fastest (multi-GHz) signals on outer layers to avoid via stubs and use the lower-loss mixed dielectric; others always go stripline for EMC. Which is better depends on frequency, via technology and the compliance risk.
:::

::: short
"Outer layers are for component access: fanouts, short links, test points, RF tuning and power pours. Inner signal layers, between planes, are for long runs and fast or noisy signals, because the field stays inside the board, so there's less emission and pickup. I route adjacent signal layers orthogonally to cut crosstalk, keep each bus or differential pair on one layer type to avoid skew from the different propagation speeds, and always give the signal a continuous plane next to it."
:::

::: long
"I think about each layer in terms of what it's exposed to and what it's near.

The outer layers are where the parts live. Every net starts and ends there, so that's where short connections and fanouts go. They're also where I can see and probe things, so test points, programming headers, and RF matching networks that might need tuning stay outside. And outer copper is the best place for high-current pours because heat can leave to the air.

The inner signal layers sit between planes, which makes them striplines. The field is contained, so there's less radiation, less pickup from the outside, and better ESD immunity. So long runs, clocks, memory buses, and high-speed pairs go inside when I can. The cost is vias, which add inductance and capacitance, and the fact that I can't probe them.

There are a few details. Adjacent signal layers should be orthogonal so crosstalk between them averages out. A stripline is slower than a microstrip: about 6.9 versus 6 picoseconds per millimetre in FR-4, so I keep a bus or a differential pair on the same layer type or I account for the skew. And whichever layer I use, the signal needs a continuous plane next to it, and when it changes layers I put a ground stitching via nearby to carry the return current.

For sensitive analog, I keep it on whichever layer has the cleanest ground right beside it and away from noisy digital routing, rather than burying it in the middle of a digital bus."
:::

::: terms
- **Microstrip**: outer-layer trace over one reference plane with air above.
- **Stripline**: inner-layer trace between two reference planes.
- **Fanout**: short stub connecting a pad to a via or a trace.
- **Broadside coupling**: coupling between traces on adjacent layers, stacked directly above each other.
- **Orthogonal routing**: routing adjacent layers in perpendicular directions to minimize overlap length.
- **Via stub**: unused portion of a through via beyond the layer where the signal exits.
- **Skew**: timing difference between signals of a bus or pair.
- **Effective dielectric constant ($\varepsilon_{eff}$)**: the weighted $\varepsilon$ the field sees; lower for microstrip.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer separates *what the layer is* (exposed vs shielded) from *what the net needs*, and quantifies at least one thing: the 6.0 vs 6.9 ps/mm delay difference, or the narrower stripline width. It also flags the exceptions: very high-speed signals sometimes stay outside to avoid via stubs; RF is routed outside for tuning; analog may go where it is quiet rather than where it is shielded. And it ties layer change to return path (ground stitching via), which most candidates forget.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Inner layers are always better for high speed."** They are quieter, but vias add discontinuities and stubs. For multi-GHz signals the via can be worse than the exposure.
- **Burying a signal between a plane and another signal layer** with no second plane, and still calling it stripline. It needs a plane on each side to be shielded.
- **Changing layers in the middle of a diff pair or bus** without accounting for the propagation difference.
- **Routing parallel traces on adjacent signal layers.** Cross them at right angles.
- **Cutting the plane with a row of vias or a trench** of signal traces, which splits the return current.
- **Burying all test points.** Debugging is much harder; keep key signals accessible.
:::

::: deep
## The question behind the question

Every net sees two things: *where its return current flows* and *what the field around it touches*. A top layer trace has air above, so some of its field extends into free space. It can couple to other traces, cables, probes, and an enclosure. A stripline has copper on both sides, so the field terminates on planes. That is the whole physical reason inner routing is "quieter."

## Microstrip vs. stripline numbers

For a trace with height $h$ over a plane (microstrip), IPC-2141 gives

$$Z_0 \approx \frac{87}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r+1.41}}\ln\frac{5.98h}{0.8w+t}$$

For symmetric stripline with plane spacing $b$:

$$Z_0 \approx \frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\frac{4b}{0.67\pi(0.8w+t)}$$

These IPC-2141 forms are handy for quick arithmetic but rough. On thin dielectrics like these the microstrip form underestimates the 50 Ω width by 5–20% (it gives 0.335 mm here instead of about 0.37 mm). The plot therefore uses the Hammerstad–Jensen microstrip and Wheeler stripline formulas, which stay within a few percent of a field solver over the whole slider range. For a real board, use the fabricator's field-solved stackup.

## Propagation delay and why it matters

Effective permittivity of a microstrip ($w=0.35$, $h=0.2$ mm, $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$):

$$\varepsilon_{eff} \approx \frac{\varepsilon_r+1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon_r-1}{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+12h/w}} \approx 3.24$$

giving $t_{pd}=\sqrt{3.24}/c = 6.0$ ps/mm. For stripline, $t_{pd}=\sqrt{4.3}/c=6.9$ ps/mm. Over 100 mm that's 600 ps vs 690 ps. Suppose one leg of a pair spends 20 mm on an inner layer and its partner spends the same 20 mm outside: the mismatch is $20\times0.9 = 18$ ps. That is negligible at 1 Gb/s (a 1000 ps bit) but 18% of a bit at 10 Gb/s, where a unit interval is 100 ps. The rule: route every member of a group on the same layers.

## Return paths across layer changes

When a signal changes layer from L1 (reference L2 ground) to L3 (between L2 ground and L4 power), it moves its return current from the L2 plane to the L2/L4 pair. If the signal references the *same* plane on both layers (here L2 for L1, and L2 as the upper plane for L3), the L2 share of the return current simply stays on L2 and passes the via without a detour. A stripline on L3 also returns part of its current on L4, though, so that portion still has to transfer from L2 to L4 at the via, and a decoupling capacitor (or, for a ground-ground pair, a stitching via) close to the transition still helps. If it changes to a layer whose reference is a different plane, the return current must hop between planes. A nearby stitching via, or decoupling capacitor in a power-ground transition, carries that. See [[q0487]].

## Crosstalk between adjacent signal layers

Broadside coupling between two overlapping traces is set by the dielectric between them. Two parallel traces, one above the other with 0.2 mm of prepreg between them, will couple strongly along their overlap. If they cross at 90°, the overlap is only one trace width, so the coupled length is tiny. That's why adjacent signal layers go orthogonal, and why the "horizontal layer / vertical layer" convention exists in autorouters.

## Exposure: EMI, ESD and pickup

A clock edge on an outer layer microstrip radiates more than on stripline because the fields extend into air and the board edge. For a product that has to pass radiated emissions, the highest-energy nets (clock, switching node, fast buses) go inside where possible. Likewise, ESD arcs at connectors couple into exposed traces; sensitive nets inside are better protected. The trade-off is the via at each end.

## What stays outside

- **RF matching networks and filters**: need tuning, probing, and low parasitics from vias.
- **Switching power stage**: high di/dt loops should be tiny and on one layer with no vias; the input capacitor, switch and inductor all on top, with ground plane directly below (L2). See [[q0489]].
- **Test points and debug headers**: accessible.
- **Thermal copper**: heat sinks to air and enclosure.

## A worked layer assignment

An application-processor + DDR3 board (for example an STM32MP1-class part): DDR address/command and data lanes escape on the top layer, drop to inner stripline layers that have solid ground on one side and power on the other, and come back up near the memory chip. The vendor stackup specifies the exact layers. Everything else (I²C, GPIO to a connector) stays on the top and bottom layers. Power rails are pours on L4 with a P/G pair under them. A USB pair stays on the top layer from connector to MCU if short (<50 mm), over unbroken ground.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Don't just say "high-speed goes inside." Say which signals, why (field containment, ESD), and the cost (vias and no probing), then the exception (multi-GHz via stubs, RF tuning). That's the answer an interviewer remembers.
:::

::: applications
- **DDR memory interfaces**: byte lanes on inner stripline layers with ground on both sides, matched lengths on the same layer.
- **USB 2.0/Ethernet**: short differential pairs left on the top layer next to the connector, with an unbroken ground plane under.
- **RF front end**: antenna feed, matching and filters on the top layer over a solid ground, with tuning footprints.
- **Switch-mode supplies**: power loop on a single outer layer, ground plane directly below, sense traces routed away.
- **Industrial I/O with ESD risk**: lines from the connector go immediately to TVS parts on top, then inside.
:::

::: assumptions
- 4–8 layer rigid FR-4 board with solid reference planes.
- The 6.0 / 6.9 ps/mm figures assume $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$ and are approximate. Real boards vary with glass weave, copper roughness and mask.
- At frequencies above several GHz, the loss tangent, via stubs and glass weave matter more than the microstrip/stripline distinction; some designers choose outer-layer routing with back-drilled or blind vias.
- On a 2-layer board "inner layers" don't exist, so the rules reduce to: route on the layer closest to ground, keep return paths short.
- RF boards may route RF only on the top layer over ground and use inner layers only for DC.
:::

::: followups
### Why is a stripline slower than a microstrip?
In a microstrip some of the field is in air ($\varepsilon=1$) and some in FR-4, so the effective permittivity is about 3.2 and the signal travels faster. A stripline is embedded entirely in dielectric ($\varepsilon_{eff}=\varepsilon_r\approx4.3$). Delay per mm is $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/c$: ≈6.0 ps/mm vs 6.9 ps/mm, around 15% slower on the inner layer.

### Should a differential pair ever change layers?
Avoid it where possible. If you must, change both signals together, keep via spacing the same, add ground vias nearby, and compensate for the delay. For a 10 Gb/s pair, even 10 ps of skew is 10% of a unit interval.

### Where would you route a sensitive analog input and a noisy switching node on the same board?
Analog inputs: on the layer with a clean ground reference right below, kept short and far from digital, ideally guarded by ground pour and not across planes' splits. The switching node: as short as possible on a single outer layer with no vias, then not routed anywhere else. Neither is buried near the other.

### What if the board is 2-layer: what's "inner" then?
There is none. The answer shifts to: put the ground on one layer as a continuous pour, route signals on the other with ground close, and keep critical traces short and over unbroken ground. If a sensitive net needs shielding, flank it with ground traces stitched with vias.

### Scenario: a 400 MHz clock on the top layer fails radiated emissions at its harmonics. What do you try?
Move the trace to an inner stripline layer between two grounds if possible; if not, shorten it, add a series resistor near the driver to slow the edge, make sure ground directly beneath is solid, stitch the ground around it, and check that the trace isn't near the board edge or a cable connector. I'd measure with near-field probes before and after to confirm which change helped.
:::

- Outer layers: components, fanouts, short links, test points, RF tuning, power pours :: Shows you know why the outside is special (access, heat, probing).
