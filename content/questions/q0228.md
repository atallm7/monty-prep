::: testing
Whether you can name the modes **and** explain what separates them. "CCM and DCM" is the minimum; a strong answer organises the modes along two independent axes:

1. *Inductor current:* does it stay above zero the whole cycle (continuous), touch zero (discontinuous), or go negative (forced continuous)?
2. *Modulation:* is the switching frequency fixed and the pulse width varied (PWM), or are pulses skipped or their rate varied (PFM, burst, pulse-skipping, constant on-time)?

Then the interviewer wants the "why": each mode exists to win efficiency or ripple in a particular load range.
:::

::: why
A converter that is superb at 3 A can be mediocre at 3 mA, and a phone, a sensor node and a laptop all spend most of their time at light load. Mode behaviour determines battery life, the noise you see on an audio or RF rail, and whether the control loop is stable at every load. It also underlies the rest of this section ([[q0229]], [[q0230]], [[q0231]], [[q0232]]). Datasheets name these modes constantly: "FCCM", "auto PFM/PWM", "PSM", "burst mode", "eco-mode".
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor current waveform**: triangle with average $I_{out}$ and ripple $\Delta I_L=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{Lf_{sw}}$. See [[q0188]], [[q0195]].
- **CCM / DCM boundary**: the inductor current just touches zero when $I_{out}=\Delta I_L/2$.
- **Synchronous rectification**: the low-side FET can conduct current in either direction, so the inductor current can go negative. See [[q0203]].
- **PWM**: fixed frequency, duty cycle adjusts. See [[q0179]].
- **PFM / burst**: output is regulated by *when* pulses occur, with fixed pulse size. See [[q0231]].
- **Duty-cycle limits**: 100% duty ("dropout") mode when $V_{in}\approx V_{out}$. See [[q0181]].
:::

::: answer
"I think about the modes along two axes. The first is what the inductor current does. In continuous conduction mode, CCM, the current never reaches zero. That's the heavy-load mode: low RMS current per amp, simple small-signal behaviour. As the load drops, the average falls until the bottom of the ripple touches zero, and below that the converter goes discontinuous, DCM, where the current ramps up, ramps down to zero, and sits at zero for part of each cycle. With a synchronous bridge you can also force CCM, where the low-side stays on and the current goes negative at light load.

The second axis is how the output is regulated. In PWM the frequency is fixed and the duty cycle changes. In pulse-frequency modulation, PFM, or burst or pulse-skipping mode, each pulse is a fixed size and the controller regulates by changing how often it fires, so the frequency drops with the load and there are quiet gaps between pulses. That cuts switching losses at light load.

Most modern ICs combine them: PWM in CCM at high load, DCM with diode emulation in the middle, then PFM at light load, switching automatically. There are also special cases like 100 percent duty mode when the input is barely above the output, and constant on-time or hysteretic control."
:::

::: technical
**Boundary.** In CCM, $\Delta I_L=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{Lf_{sw}}$. The inductor current just reaches zero when $I_{out,crit}=\Delta I_L/2=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{2Lf_{sw}}$. For 12 V to 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz: $\Delta I_L=1.02$ A and $I_{crit}=0.51$ A.

**Modes by inductor current.**
- *CCM*: $i_L>0$ all cycle. $V_{out}=DV_{in}$ (ideal), independent of load.
- *DCM*: $i_L=0$ for part of the cycle; three switching states. $V_{out}/V_{in}=\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4K/D^2}}$ with $K=2L/(RT)$, so the gain depends on load.
- *BCM/CrCM (critical)*: $i_L$ reaches zero exactly at the end of the cycle; frequency varies with load.
- *FCCM (forced CCM)*: synchronous low-side FET stays on, $i_L$ goes negative below $I_{crit}$; behaves like CCM at all loads, but with no load-dependent loss reduction.

**Modes by modulation.**
- *PWM*: constant $f_{sw}$, variable $D$.
- *PFM / burst / pulse-skipping*: constant peak current or constant on-time, variable pulse rate. 
- *COT / hysteretic*: fixed $t_{on}$ (often $V_{out}/(V_{in}f)$), output ripple triggers the next pulse; frequency depends on load in DCM.
- *100% duty (dropout) mode*: high-side FET stays on.
- *Protection modes*: hiccup, foldback, soft-start.

**Typical automatic sequence with load**: PFM (below roughly $I_{crit}/3$ to $I_{crit}/2$, vendor dependent) → DCM-PWM → CCM-PWM.

In every mode the average inductor current equals the load current; only the shape and timing of the ripple change.
:::

::: short
"There are two axes. Inductor current: continuous (CCM), where it never hits zero; discontinuous (DCM), where it returns to zero and rests; and forced CCM, where a synchronous low side lets it go negative. Modulation: PWM, fixed frequency with varying duty, and PFM or burst mode, fixed-size pulses at a rate set by the load. Real converters switch between them automatically: CCM-PWM at heavy load, DCM in the middle, PFM at light load for efficiency."
:::

::: long
"I find it easiest to split modes along two axes: what the inductor current does and how pulses are generated.

On the inductor side, the heavy-load mode is continuous conduction, CCM. The current is a triangle sitting on top of the load current and never reaches zero. As load drops, the average current falls but the ripple stays the same, so at $I_{out}=\Delta I_L/2$ the bottom of the triangle touches zero. That's the critical, or boundary, mode. Below it, the converter is in discontinuous mode, DCM: the current ramps up, ramps back down to zero, and stays there until the next cycle. For my usual example, 12 V to 3.3 V with 4.7 µH at 500 kHz, that boundary is about half an amp. With a synchronous bridge there's also forced CCM, where the low-side FET stays on after zero current and the inductor current goes negative, so the converter acts continuous at any load. It's simple and has constant frequency, but you waste the ripple-current losses at light load.

On the modulation side, PWM runs at a fixed frequency and varies the duty cycle. PFM, also called burst or pulse-skipping mode, delivers fixed-size pulses, say a peak current of 0.6 A, and regulates by changing how often they come. At light load the frequency drops to tens of kilohertz, so gate-drive and switching losses fall in proportion to load.

Real ICs chain these together. At heavy load: PWM in CCM. In the middle: PWM in DCM with diode emulation. Below some threshold: PFM. There are also constant on-time and hysteretic converters, and the 100 percent duty mode when the input is barely above the output."
:::

::: terms
- **CCM (continuous conduction mode)**: inductor current stays above zero for the whole switching period.
- **DCM (discontinuous conduction mode)**: inductor current reaches zero and stays there for part of the period.
- **BCM / CrCM**: boundary or critical conduction, where the current just reaches zero at the end of each cycle.
- **FCCM**: forced continuous conduction, the low side stays on so $i_L$ can go negative.
- **Diode emulation**: turning the low-side FET off at zero current so it behaves like an ideal diode.
- **PWM**: pulse-width modulation, constant frequency, variable duty.
- **PFM / burst / pulse skipping**: regulation by changing the pulse rate or skipping cycles, at light load.
- **COT**: constant on-time control, where $t_{on}$ is fixed and the off-time is set by the feedback comparator.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "CCM and DCM" and gives a definition of each.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates the *current-shape* axis (CCM/BCM/DCM/FCCM) from the *modulation* axis (PWM/PFM/COT).
- Computes the boundary: $I_{crit}=\Delta I_L/2$ and gives a number (0.51 A for the standard example).
- States that the gain is load-independent in CCM and load-dependent in DCM.
- Explains that **forced CCM costs efficiency** at light load but gives constant frequency, and why audio/RF designs choose it.
- Describes the typical automatic hand-off with load (CCM-PWM, DCM-PWM, PFM) and mentions 100% duty and COT.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating PFM as a separate topology.** It is a modulation scheme of the same buck power stage.
- **Equating DCM with PFM.** DCM is a property of the inductor current; a converter can be in DCM at a fixed frequency (PWM-DCM) without skipping anything.
- **Believing the ripple current shrinks at light load.** In CCM and FCCM, $\Delta I_L$ is independent of load; only the average changes.
- **Saying "asynchronous converters never reach CCM".** They do; a Schottky diode just forces DCM at light load because it cannot conduct backwards.
- **Forgetting that mode changes the small-signal behaviour.** CCM has the LC double pole; DCM behaves more like a single-pole system, so the same compensation can be too slow or too aggressive.
:::

::: deep
## Axis 1: what the inductor current does

During the on-time the inductor current rises at $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$, and during the off-time it falls at $V_{out}/L$. The ripple $\Delta I_L$ is fixed by $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$, $L$ and $f_{sw}$. The load only sets the *average*. So the valley current is $I_{out}-\Delta I_L/2$, and it hits zero when $I_{out}=\Delta I_L/2$.

For $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $L=4.7$ µH, $f_{sw}=500$ kHz: $\Delta I_L=1.018$ A, so $I_{crit}=0.509$ A. Three load points show the three behaviours:

At 0.2 A, which is below the 0.51 A boundary, the controller shortens the on-time to 345 ns, the current ramps to 0.64 A and back to zero, and then idles for about 750 ns (DCM). The DCM numbers come from inverting the DCM gain for the duty needed to hold $V_{out}=3.3$ V: $D=M\sqrt{K/(1-M)}$ with $M=V_{out}/V_{in}$ and $K=2L/(RT)$. For 0.2 A, $R=16.5$ Ω, $K=0.285$, $M=0.275$ gives $D=0.172$ (see [[q0229]]).

| Property | CCM | DCM | FCCM |
|---|---|---|---|
| Valley current | >0 | = 0 | <0 at light load |
| $V_{out}/V_{in}$ | $D$ (load independent) | depends on $D$, $L$, $f$, $R$ | $\approx D$ |
| Peak current at given load | lowest | highest | medium |
| Inductor ripple | $\Delta I_L$ | larger (relative to load) | same $\Delta I_L$ at all loads |
| Switch-node ringing | none | rings when current is zero | none |
| Light-load efficiency | poor | better | worst |

## Axis 2: how the pulses are generated

**PWM** keeps $f_{sw}$ constant. The loop changes $D$. The spectrum is clean: a fundamental and harmonics at known frequencies, so filtering and EMI are predictable. The cost is that every cycle burns gate charge, $C_{oss}$ and (in FCCM) ripple loss regardless of load.

**PFM** (pulse-frequency modulation, also called burst, pulse-skipping or PSM) holds the pulse size constant: the high-side turns on until a peak current or a fixed on-time, then the inductor empties into the output, then the converter idles until $V_{out}$ droops below a threshold. The rate of pulses is proportional to the load: with 0.6 A peak pulses of 0.354 µC each, 20 mA needs 56 kHz and 2 mA needs 5.7 kHz. Per-cycle losses scale with *pulse rate*, so efficiency stays flat down to very light loads ([[q0230]], [[q0231]]).

**COT / hysteretic**: fixed on-time (often $t_{on}=V_{out}/(V_{in}f)=550$ ns here), off-time set by a comparator on $V_{out}$. In CCM the frequency is nearly constant; in DCM it falls with load, giving natural PFM-like behaviour with very fast transient response.

**100% duty ("dropout") mode**: the high-side FET stays on when $V_{in}$ is just above $V_{out}$ ([[q0222]]).

## How ICs combine them

Most synchronous buck ICs offer:
1. **Auto mode** (PFM/PWM, "eco", "PSM", "burst"): PWM-CCM at high load, PWM-DCM or PFM below a threshold.
2. **Forced-PWM / FCCM**: constant frequency at all loads, for noise-sensitive applications.
3. **Ultrasonic PFM**: PFM with a minimum frequency of about 20 to 25 kHz so bursts stay out of the audible band.

## Choosing a mode in practice

Start from the requirements. If the product spends most of its life near idle and runs on a battery, pick auto PFM/PWM and make sure the datasheet's PFM quiescent current is in the µA range. If a sensitive analog or RF block hangs off the rail, force PWM, put $f_{sw}$ away from the sensitive band, and accept the idle loss (or follow the converter with an LDO). If the load steps hard, as on a processor core rail, look at constant on-time or current-mode converters that stay in PWM and shed phases at light load instead of dropping into a burst mode.

Finally, check the mode transitions on the bench. Sweep the load slowly up and down with a programmable electronic load and watch the output and the switch node. A good converter changes mode with hysteresis and a small output glitch; a poor one chatters between modes at one load and shows a visible low-frequency wobble. The same sweep also finds the knee of the efficiency curve that a datasheet graph tends to hide.

::: applications
- **Smartphones and wearables**: auto PFM/PWM bucks, since the device sits at tens of µA to a few mA most of the time.
- **Audio, RF and precision analog rails**: forced-PWM mode to keep switching noise at a fixed, filterable frequency.
- **IoT sensor nodes on coin cells**: nano-amp quiescent-current PFM/burst converters.
- **Processor core rails**: CCM at high load with phase shedding at light load.
- **Automotive and industrial**: COT/hysteretic bucks for fast transient response, with spread spectrum in PWM for EMI.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Single-phase synchronous buck** with a fixed inductor. A diode-rectified buck enters DCM automatically at $I_{crit}$ and has no FCCM.
- **Ideal-ish waveforms**: the numbers ($I_{crit}$, DCM duty, pulse rate) assume lossless components and fixed $V_{in}$.
- **Vendor terminology varies**: "PSM", "eco-mode", "burst", "skip", "light-load mode" may or may not mean true PFM; some skip PWM cycles at a fixed peak, others use COT. Check the datasheet's description.
- **Mode names differ**: some textbooks call BCM "critical conduction mode" and treat it as a distinct control strategy (fixed on-time, variable frequency).

An alternative valid answer is the simple three-bullet one: CCM, DCM and PFM/burst. The two-axis framing above shows why those three are not parallel categories.
:::

::: followups
### At what load does a buck go from CCM to DCM?
When the average inductor current falls to half the ripple: $I_{crit}=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{2Lf_{sw}}$. For 12 V to 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz, that is 0.51 A. Raise $L$ or $f_{sw}$ and the boundary moves down; a smaller inductor moves it up.

### Why would anyone use forced CCM if it wastes power at light load?
Constant switching frequency (clean spectrum for EMI and audio/RF), load-independent gain and small-signal behaviour that make compensation simple, and bidirectional current capability, which allows a regulator to sink current and pull the output down quickly after a load drop.

### What's the difference between PFM and DCM?
DCM describes the *inductor current shape* (it returns to zero each cycle); PFM describes *when pulses happen* (rate varies with load). A converter in DCM-PWM still switches at a fixed frequency with a shorter duty. PFM converters are almost always in DCM (each pulse empties the inductor), but not all DCM converters are PFM.

### How would you pick the PFM-to-PWM threshold?
Where the two efficiency curves cross, with hysteresis. In the model above they cross near 0.19 A. Too low a threshold wastes efficiency in the middle; too high makes the output ripple and frequency spectrum worse in a range where PWM would have been fine. Datasheets give the threshold as a function of $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$ and $L$.

### What if the load suddenly jumps from 1 mA to 1 A in PFM?
The output sags until the comparator triggers; pulses then become continuous. The response is delayed by the sleep interval (tens of µs in deep sleep), and the first few pulses are fixed-size, so the converter needs several pulses before the average current catches up. Typical remedies are a larger output capacitor, a faster wake-up comparator, or a mode that jumps to PWM when the output drops below a threshold. See [[q0233]].
