::: basics
### In plain words
When you measure with an oscilloscope (scope) probe, you connect two things: the tip to the signal and the ground lead to a ground point. Together with the board's own return copper, these form a closed loop. That loop is part of the measurement. It can ring after a fast edge and pick up noise, so what you see may not be what is really on the board.

### Picture it
Imagine holding a hula hoop up in the rain. The bigger the hoop, the more rain it catches. A measuring loop catches stray magnetic fields from nearby switching wires in the same way, and bigger loops catch more. Now picture the loop as a long, springy rope. A sudden jolt keeps it swinging for a while. A long ground lead behaves like that rope, ringing after each fast edge.

### Words you need
- **Loop**: a closed path that current flows around, here made by the tip, the ground lead and the board return.
- **Return path**: the route a signal's current takes back to where it started.
- **Inductance**: a wire's resistance to fast changes in current. A longer wire has more.
- **Probe capacitance**: the probe tip's small ability to store charge, around 10 pF (picofarads) for a typical probe.
- **Ringing**: a signal wobbling back and forth after a fast edge.

### The simplest example
A 15 cm clip lead has roughly 150 nH. With 10 pF of probe capacitance, the loop rings at about 130 MHz, and a fast edge shows overshoot (the signal swinging past its true level) that is not on the board. A short spring tip of roughly 10 nH rings near 500 MHz, far above most of the signal. The phrase has a second meaning: a mains ground loop, which adds 50 or 60 Hz hum when the scope and the board both connect to earth through the wall-socket wiring. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the ringing math, magnetic pickup, and shared-copper errors.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can define the term precisely and see **both** meanings in the same physics. In probing, "the ground loop" most often means the **loop formed by the probe tip, the signal path, the return path and the probe's ground lead**: the area enclosed is what picks up magnetic fields, and its inductance rings with the probe capacitance. The other common meaning is the **mains ground loop**: multiple earth connections (scope earth, DUT earth, other instruments) forming a loop that carries current and injects hum or noise. A strong answer defines the first (the one the question is about), mentions the second, and ties both to "current flowing in a return path that you didn't intend".
:::

::: why
Most of what looks like "noise" or "ringing" on a bench measurement is the measurement loop itself. Knowing what the ground loop is lets you make the right fixes: shrink the loop (spring tip, nearby ground pad) or break an unwanted mains-earth loop (isolated or battery measurement, differential probe). It's also the same loop concept that underlies EMI and PCB return-path design, so a clear mental model pays off across hardware work. Follow-ups are [[q0630]] (why small) and [[q0631]] (how to reduce).
:::

::: concepts
- **Loop**: any closed conductive path; current flows round it, flux threads through it.
- **Return path**: every signal current comes back to its source; the probe's ground lead is part of the return of the *measurement*.
- **Loop inductance** $L$: grows with loop area/length (rule of thumb 1 nH/mm for wire).
- **Faraday induction**: $v = -d\Phi/dt$, proportional to loop area. See [[q0630]].
- **Resonance with probe capacitance**: $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$.
- **Mains ground loops**: multiple safety-earth paths; hum at 50/60 Hz.
- **Ground pads for probing**: see [[q0618]].
:::

::: answer
"When you measure a signal with a scope probe you connect two things: the probe tip to the signal and the probe's ground lead to a ground point. The ground loop is the closed loop those make together: from the signal node, through the probe tip and cable, back along the ground lead to the ground reference, and then back through the board's return path to where the signal started.

That loop matters for two reasons. It has inductance, about a nanohenry per millimetre of lead, so a 15 cm clip lead is roughly 150 nanohenries, and with the probe's 10 or so picofarads it forms a resonant circuit near 130 MHz that rings on fast edges. And it has area, so any changing magnetic field from a nearby switch node or trace induces a voltage in it, which you see as noise that's not really on the circuit.

So the ground loop isn't a property of the board alone; it's the measurement setup plus the board. There's also a different use of the phrase, a mains ground loop, when the scope and the device under test are both connected to earth and to each other, and current from ground-potential differences flows around that loop, which shows up as 50 or 60 hertz hum or noise. Both are about current in a return path you didn't intend to have, and both are fixed by making the path small or breaking it."
:::

::: technical
**Definition (probe ground loop).** The loop consists of: signal source node → probe tip → probe body/cable → ground lead → ground reference point on the board → board return path (plane) → back to the source. Its area $A$ is the area between the tip contact and the ground contact (plus the board return path); its inductance is

$$L_{loop} \approx L_{lead} + L_{pad\text{-}to\text{-}pad}$$

with the ground lead dominating. A long clip lead is about 100–200 nH; a spring tip about 5–15 nH; the exact value depends on geometry (self-inductance grows with length and with the loop's size).

**Equivalent circuit.** The lead inductance $L$ is in series with the probe input capacitance $C_{in}$ (and the tip resistance). The source (with its resistance $R_s$) charges $C_{in}$ through $L$:

$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC_{in}}},\quad Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C_{in}},\quad \zeta = \frac{R_s}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C_{in}}{L}}$$

For $L = 150$ nH, $C = 10$ pF: $f_0 = 130$ MHz, $Z_0 = 122$ Ω; $\zeta = 0.2$ for $R_s = 50$ Ω, so overshoot ≈ 52%. Critical damping needs $R \approx 2Z_0 = 245$ Ω. For a spring tip, $L = 10$ nH: $f_0 = 503$ MHz, $Z_0 = 32$ Ω.

**Magnetic pickup.** An external changing flux induces $v = A\,dB/dt$ (for a uniform field). It's proportional to the loop area $A$.

**Common impedance coupling.** If the probe's ground connects to the board at a point carrying large return current (for example, a regulator's power ground), the voltage drop in that copper ($I R$ or $L\,dI/dt$) appears in the measurement because the "ground" you referenced isn't a single potential.

**Mains ground loop (the other sense).** The scope's BNC shield is tied to safety earth. If the DUT is also earthed (via its supply), the two earth paths plus the probe ground form a loop. Small voltage differences between earth points (mV, from currents in the building ground) drive current in the loop, adding 50/60 Hz (and harmonics) to the measurement. For example 100 mA through 50 mΩ of shield and wiring is 5 mV of hum.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Inductance per millimetre and probe capacitance values are typical rules of thumb; real values depend on the lead's geometry and the specific probe. The question's context ("when measuring a signal") points to the probe ground loop; the mains-loop meaning is the alternate reading.
:::

"When I measure a signal, I connect two things: the probe tip to the signal, and the probe's ground lead to a ground point. The ground loop is the closed loop those make: from the signal node through the tip and cable, back through the ground lead to the ground reference, and then back through the board's return path to the source.

Second, the loop has area, and any changing magnetic field passing through it induces a voltage, proportional to area. So the loop picks up noise from nearby switching nodes and shows it on the scope, even though the circuit isn't carrying it.

Third, if the ground point I attach to carries large return current, the voltage drop along that copper adds to my reading.

So the ground loop is the measurement setup plus the board. The same words are used for a different thing, a mains ground loop, where the scope's earth and the circuit's earth form a loop that carries current and injects 50 or 60 hertz hum. Both come down to current taking a return path I didn't intend, and the fix is either to shrink the loop or to break the unwanted one."

::: terms
- **Ground loop (probe)**: closed loop of tip, signal path, ground lead and board return.
- **Loop inductance**: self-inductance of that loop, about 1 nH/mm for wires.
- **Loop area**: area enclosed; sets magnetic pickup.
- **Return path**: path current takes back to its source.
- **Common-impedance coupling**: error from sharing a return conductor with another current.
- **Mains ground loop**: multiple earth connections forming a loop that carries stray current (hum).
- **Spring ground tip**: short probe ground attachment for minimal loop.
- **Series resonance**: LC resonance at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$.
- **Differential probe**: measurement not referenced to the scope's ground.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the ground loop is the loop made by the probe ground lead".

**An exceptional answer**:

- Defines the whole loop (tip, signal, return, ground lead, board return) and says it is *part of the circuit being measured*.
- Gives both consequences: inductance (with numbers: 150 nH → 130 MHz ring) and area (pickup, ∝ area).
- Mentions common-impedance error from shared return copper.
- Distinguishes the mains ground loop and shows how it differs (hum from earth currents, fixed by isolation) while seeing the shared principle.
- Mentions how to test for it: shorten the lead, short the tip to ground and look at the noise floor.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking ground is a perfect zero-volt point.** Return conductors have inductance and resistance.
- **Believing a loop only matters at high speed.** Hum from a mains loop is at 50/60 Hz.
- **Removing the scope's earth pin to "break the ground loop".** It's unsafe and does not fix the probe loop.
- **Confusing the board's ground plane with the probe's ground path.** They're connected in the measurement but separate objects.
- **Treating the ringing as circuit behaviour.** It may be the probe loop's LC.
- **Measuring with the ground lead clipped to a far-away ground.** The loop includes the whole board path.
:::

## Why an ordinary wire has inductance

A current-carrying wire creates a magnetic field. Storing energy in that field means inductance: $E = \tfrac12 L I^2$. For a straight round wire of length $\ell$ and radius $a$, $L \approx 2\ell\left(\ln\frac{2\ell}{a} - 1\right)$ nH with $\ell$ in cm (approximate formula for $\ell \gg a$), which for 15 cm and radius 0.25 mm gives about 180 nH; the "1 nH/mm" rule of thumb follows. The return path matters too: the inductance of a loop depends on the area enclosed between the current and its return, so the closer the return, the lower the loop inductance.

Compute with node: for $\ell = 15$ cm, $a = 0.25$ mm: $2\cdot15(\ln(2\cdot150/0.25)-1) = 30(\ln 1200 - 1) = 30(7.09-1) = 183$ nH (the loop adds the return, order 100–200 nH). So the 1 nH/mm rule is a sound estimate.

### 2. Picks up fields

Flux through the loop changes as nearby currents switch. By Faraday, $v = A\,dB/dt$. See [[q0630]] for numbers.

### 3. Shares return copper with other currents

If you attach the probe's ground to a pad that carries a large pulsed return current (say, a buck converter's power ground), the voltage drop $L\,dI/dt + IR$ in that copper adds to your signal. A "ground" is not an equipotential. Pick a quiet ground near the signal's own return. See [[q0618]].

## The other ground loop: mains earth

The scope's BNC shell connects through the instrument to mains earth. A bench power supply for the DUT may also be earth-connected, as may a laptop on USB and other instruments. Two earth connections joined by the probe ground form a loop:

- Building earth conductors have small impedance and carry leakage and fault-return currents; the potential difference between two outlets can be millivolts to volts.
- That voltage drives current through the loop (including the probe's ground lead and the DUT's ground), creating a voltage drop that appears in the measurement as 50/60 Hz hum and harmonics.

Estimate: 100 mA circulating in 50 mΩ of shared return gives 5 mV of hum, which is visible when looking at mV-level signals.

Fixes: use a differential or isolated probe, power the DUT from an isolated or battery supply, use a single earth point ("star"), ferrite/common-mode choke on the probe cable at high frequencies, and never remove the scope's safety earth. See [[q0631]].

## How to see your own ground loop

1. **Short the tip to its ground at the ground pad** (tip-and-barrel on a ground pad): the displayed noise is the setup's noise floor. A big value means pickup.
2. **Change the lead length**: if the waveform changes, it was the loop.
3. **Move the probe lead near the suspect noise source** (inductor, switch node): if the displayed noise changes, the loop is picking up the field.
4. **Float the DUT** from a battery: if the 50 Hz hum disappears, it was a mains loop.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> One memorable line: "The ground loop is the probe's contribution to the circuit: it's an inductor in series with the probe capacitance, and an antenna." Then give the 150 nH/130 MHz example.

## Worked example: the mains loop in numbers, and why a differential probe fixes it

The technical section estimated 5 mV of 50/60 Hz hum from 100 mA circulating through 50 mΩ of shared return. Take the next steps. Suppose you are looking at a 20 mV sensor signal. The hum is $5/20 = 25\%$ of the signal, which is plainly visible. Now swap the single-ended probe for a differential probe whose common-mode rejection at 50 Hz is 60 dB. The hum is a common-mode voltage between the DUT ground and the scope ground, so it is attenuated by a factor of 1,000: $5\text{ mV}\times10^{-60/20} = 5\ \mu$V, or 0.025% of the signal (computed). The loop still exists, since the currents still flow, but the probe no longer measures the voltage that drives them as part of the signal.

Rejection is not constant. Differential probes and isolated probes lose common-mode rejection as frequency rises, often by tens of dB between line frequency and hundreds of MHz (the figures are model-specific; see the probe's datasheet). A fast switching node in a half-bridge is a large common-mode step, which is exactly the case where rejection is worst. That is the reason a plain differential probe is fine for the hum problem and an isolated probe with a high-frequency CMRR specification is used for floating gate-drive measurements.

## Worked example: magnetic hum is tiny, conducted hum is large

It is tempting to blame mains hum on magnetic pickup. Check. A stray 50 Hz field of 1 µT is on the high side for a bench away from a transformer. The induced voltage is $v = 2\pi f B A$. For the 75 cm² clip-lead loop used earlier, that is $2\pi\times50\times10^{-6}\times75\times10^{-4} = 2.4\ \mu$V, and for a 0.3 cm² spring-ground loop it is 0.009 µV (computed). Both are negligible next to the 5 mV from the conductive loop. So hum on a bench measurement is overwhelmingly a conducted ground-loop problem, and shrinking the loop area does very little for it. The fix is to break the loop (isolate, float or use a differential probe), not to shorten the lead. The opposite is true for the fast, nanosecond-scale pickup in the next question: there the field changes millions of times faster and the area dominates. The same word, "ground loop", names two different problems, and the right tool depends on which one you have.

## The two-channel trap

A subtle case is measuring a floating voltage with two single-ended probes and the scope's A−B math function, for example the voltage across a high-side sense resistor. Each probe's ground clip ties to the scope's chassis ground, so connecting both grounds to different nodes of the DUT shorts those two nodes together through the scope. With the DUT also earthed, the result can be a destroyed trace or a tripped supply. Even if nothing breaks, the connection redefines the circuit. The correct approaches are to connect both grounds to the same node (and accept the A−B common-mode error), to use a true differential probe, or to use an isolated oscilloscope channel. Never defeat the scope's safety earth to "float" it.

## Where the picture is incomplete

**Series resonance of the ground lead is only one of several.** At high frequency the probe cable's shield also carries common-mode current, so the cable itself acts as an antenna and as a path into the DUT. A ferrite on the cable near the probe head reduces this, which is the reason probes for fast switching measurements are often shipped with a clamp-on choke.

**The scope's input capacitance is not always 10 pF.** A 1× probe loads a node with the cable's capacitance, often 100 pF or more, making all the ringing numbers worse (much lower $f_0$) and the loading much heavier. A 10× probe is typical for fast work, and an active probe with well under 1 pF is better.

**A "quiet" ground may not exist.** On a dense board with a switching converter, every ground pad carries some noise. The best you can do is pick the pad nearest to the signal's own return and verify with the tip-to-ground test.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The stray-field magnitude (1 µT), the CMRR figures and the typical probe capacitances are order-of-magnitude values that vary with location, equipment and probe model.

## Verifying on the bench

1. **Tip-to-ground test.** Touch the probe tip to its own ground connection while the DUT runs. Any waveform you see is the measurement system's own pickup and ground-bounce floor. Record it and compare against the signal you want to read.
2. **Lead-length test.** Repeat a measurement with a clip lead and with a spring tip. Whatever changes was the loop, as in the 52%-versus-1.7% overshoot comparison above.
3. **Float or battery test.** Power the DUT from a battery or an isolated supply. If hum disappears, it was the conducted mains loop, and you can then decide between keeping the DUT isolated or using a differential probe.
4. **Amplitude-versus-frequency test.** Look at the hum on a spectrum or FFT. Lines at 50 or 60 Hz and its harmonics point to the mains loop. Broadband noise or spikes synchronous with a switching edge point to the probe's high-frequency loop.

::: followups
### What is the ground loop made of when you probe with a 15 cm clip lead?
The tip contact, the signal's path to the ground reference, the clip lead (~150 nH), the board's return path between the clip point and the source. The lead dominates the inductance; the area enclosed (centimetres by centimetres) dominates the magnetic pickup.

### Where would you attach the ground clip on a regulator board?
At a quiet ground near the signal's own return, such as the output capacitor's ground pad, not at the power-stage ground where large switching currents flow, because that copper carries voltage from $L\,dI/dt$ and would add to the reading.

### How do you tell a ground-loop artifact from real ringing?
Change the ground lead: shortening it should change an artifact. Short the probe tip to ground and see if noise persists (pickup). Use a different probe or the tip-and-barrel; if the ring frequency moves with lead inductance ($f \propto 1/\sqrt{L}$), it's the probe.

### What if you see 60 Hz hum on a µV-level measurement?
Suspect a mains ground loop: multiple earth paths between instruments and DUT. Float the DUT on batteries, use a differential/isolated probe or instrument, connect grounds at one point, or use an isolation amplifier. Don't lift the safety earth.

### Scenario: the same ringing appears with every probe lead you try, even short ones. What now?
Then it's probably real: the circuit has an LC resonance (gate loop, trace inductance with load capacitance). Confirm with a different measurement method, such as a differential probe or measuring at another point, and check that the ring frequency matches a circuit L and C (for example $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ with your estimated L and C).
