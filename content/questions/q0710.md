::: testing
Whether you can explain $Z_0$ as more than "50 ohms". Interviewers look for the physical picture: it is the ratio of voltage to current in a *single traveling wave*, set by the line's inductance and capacitance per unit length, $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$. They also check the classic traps: you cannot measure it with an ohmmeter, it does not depend on length, it is not a dissipative resistance (a lossless line has a real $Z_0$ and no loss), and it is what the source "sees" until the first reflection returns. Candidates who derive it from charging capacitance per unit length stand out.
:::

::: why
Every decision about a high-speed trace starts with $Z_0$: the width you route, the stack-up you choose, the value of a series or parallel termination, the match at a connector and the divider formed with the driver at launch time. If the source, line and load impedances don't agree you get reflections ([[q0711]]). It is also the quantity a TDR reports, so reading $Z_0$ off a TDR trace is how you find a bad via, a neck-down or a connector. In RF it sets the system impedance (50 or 75 Ω). Interview-wise, this is the quick test of whether you understand transmission lines or have just memorized the numbers.
:::

::: concepts
- **Per-unit-length inductance and capacitance** ($L'$, $C'$) set by the conductor geometry and dielectric. See [[q0039]] and [[q0022]].
- **Traveling wave**: a forward-moving disturbance with voltage $V^+$ and current $I^+$ related by a fixed ratio.
- **Propagation velocity** $v=1/\sqrt{L'C'}$, which ties $Z_0$ to delay ([[q0708]]).
- **Energy storage**: the wave front charges $C'$ and builds magnetic field in $L'$ equally. Nothing is dissipated in a lossless line.
- **Reflection coefficient** $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$, defined relative to $Z_0$ ([[q0711]]).
- **Series-parallel voltage division at the source**: launch amplitude $V_s Z_0/(R_s+Z_0)$.
:::

::: answer
"Characteristic impedance is the ratio of voltage to current for a wave traveling along a line. If I put a step on the line, the wave front moves down at some speed, and as it goes it has to charge up the capacitance of each new bit of line and build up current through its inductance. The current that has to flow to do that is set by how fast the front moves and how much capacitance there is per unit length. Work through it and you get $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$, in ohms, with $L'$ and $C'$ the inductance and capacitance per unit length.

The key points: it depends only on the geometry and the dielectric, not on the length. A 1 m and a 10 m piece of the same coax are both 50 ohms. It isn't a resistance in the sense that it doesn't dissipate power: a lossless line still has a $Z_0$, and an ohmmeter can't measure it. What it means is that until the wave comes back from the far end, the source sees the line as a resistor of value $Z_0$. That's why a driver launches $V_s Z_0/(R_s+Z_0)$, and why a load equal to $Z_0$ absorbs the wave with no reflection. On a PCB I set it with the trace width, the dielectric thickness and the dielectric constant, and typically target 50 ohms single-ended or 100 differential."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** For a forward wave, $Z_0=V^+/I^+$. For the general (lossy) line with per-unit-length $R',L',G',C'$:
$$Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{R'+j\omega L'}{G'+j\omega C'}}$$
At high enough frequency ($\omega L'\gg R'$, $\omega C'\gg G'$) it reduces to the real constant $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$. At low frequency, where $R'$ dominates, $Z_0\approx\sqrt{R'/(j\omega C')}$ is complex and larger.

**Physical derivation (lossless).** A step $V$ travels at $v=1/\sqrt{L'C'}$. In time $dt$ the front covers $dz=v\,dt$ and must charge the capacitance of that stretch: $dQ=C'\,dz\,V$. The current into the line is $I=dQ/dt=C'vV$. Hence
$$Z_0=\frac{V}{I}=\frac{1}{C'v}=\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}$$
The same follows from energy: electric energy per length $\tfrac12C'V^2$ equals magnetic energy per length $\tfrac12L'I^2$ in a traveling wave, so $V/I=\sqrt{L'/C'}$.

**Interpretations.**
- Input impedance of an infinitely long line is $Z_0$. For a finite line it equals $Z_0$ until the first reflection returns, and for all time if the load is $Z_0$.
- A line terminated in $Z_0$ looks like $Z_0$ at its input for any length.
- $Z_0$ is set by geometry and dielectric: $Z_0\propto1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$ for a given geometry.

**Typical formulae.** Coax: $Z_0\approx\dfrac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\dfrac{D}{d}$. Microstrip (IPC-2141 approximation, $0.1<w/h<2$): $Z_0\approx\dfrac{87}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r+1.41}}\ln\dfrac{5.98h}{0.8w+t}$.

**Launch amplitude.** With source resistance $R_s$ driving the line, the first wave has amplitude $V_1=V_s\dfrac{Z_0}{R_s+Z_0}$.
:::

::: short
"Characteristic impedance is the ratio of voltage to current in a single wave traveling down the line. It comes from the charge and magnetic field the wave front has to build per unit length, so $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$. It depends only on geometry and dielectric, not length, and it isn't a loss: a lossless line has a real $Z_0$. Until the first reflection comes back, the source sees the line as a resistor of value $Z_0$, and a load of $Z_0$ absorbs the wave with no reflection."
:::

::: long
"Think of what happens when I connect a step source to the start of a long line. The voltage can't appear everywhere at once, so a front moves down at a finite speed. Behind the front the line is charged to V, ahead of it it's still at zero. To advance one meter, the front has to charge the capacitance of that meter, and that means charge must flow in. The current is the charge per unit length, $C'V$, times the speed, $v$. So the current drawn from the source is $I=C'vV$ and the ratio $V/I$ is $1/(C'v)$. Using $v=1/\sqrt{L'C'}$ that becomes the familiar $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$. You can check it with energy too: the front stores equal electric and magnetic energy per unit length.

A few things follow. First, $Z_0$ is set by geometry and dielectric only: for a PCB, trace width, dielectric thickness, copper thickness and permittivity. Wider trace means more capacitance and lower inductance, so lower $Z_0$. Second, length doesn't matter: a meter and a kilometre of the same cable have the same $Z_0$. Third, it isn't a dissipative resistance and you can't measure it with a DMM, because at DC the line is just an open or short at the far end. Fourth, it is what the source sees until the reflection returns. So the launch voltage is a divider between the source resistance and $Z_0$, and a load equal to $Z_0$ absorbs everything with no reflection.

In real lines with loss, $Z_0$ becomes frequency-dependent and slightly complex, but above a few MHz on a typical PCB trace it's essentially real and constant. Typical targets are 50 ohms single-ended and 90 to 100 ohms differential."
:::

::: terms
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: ratio $V^+/I^+$ for a traveling wave; $\sqrt{L'/C'}$ for a lossless line.
- **Per-unit-length parameters ($R'$, $L'$, $G'$, $C'$)**: series resistance, series inductance, shunt conductance and shunt capacitance per meter.
- **Phase velocity ($v_p$)**: $1/\sqrt{L'C'}$, equal to $c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$ in a TEM line.
- **Launch (incident) voltage**: the first wave's amplitude, $V_sZ_0/(R_s+Z_0)$.
- **Return loss**: $-20\log_{10}|\Gamma|$ dB, how well a load is matched to $Z_0$.
- **VSWR**: $(1+|\Gamma|)/(1-|\Gamma|)$, the standing-wave ratio caused by a mismatch.
- **TDR (time-domain reflectometry)**: launching a fast step and reading $Z(t)$ from the reflections.
- **Controlled impedance**: a PCB trace fabricated to a specified $Z_0$ (for example 50 Ω ±10%).
- **Characteristic admittance** ($Y_0=1/Z_0$): sometimes used in analysis of parallel structures.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "$Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$ and it's 50 Ω".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives it from charging capacitance per unit length (or energy equality), not just quoting it.
- States that it is **independent of length**, can't be read with an ohmmeter, and is not a loss mechanism.
- Explains the consequence "the source sees $Z_0$ until the reflection returns" and uses it for the launch voltage.
- Shows how geometry sets it (wider trace: lower $Z_0$; thicker dielectric: higher; higher $\varepsilon_r$: lower).
- Mentions that real lines have a frequency-dependent complex $Z_0$ and where it becomes constant.
- Knows why 50 Ω is conventional and the common values (75 Ω video, 90 to 100 Ω differential) ([[q0720]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"It's the resistance of the trace."** DC resistance of a trace is milliohms; $Z_0$ is tens of ohms. They are unrelated quantities.
- **"Longer lines have higher impedance."** Impedance seen from a finite line depends on the load and the electrical length, but $Z_0$ itself is length-independent.
- **"A 50 Ω line dissipates power in $Z_0$."** The energy goes to the load (or is reflected). A lossless line stores and carries energy without dissipating it.
- **Thinking you can measure it with a DMM.** At DC the far end is an open or a short and the meter reads that. Use a TDR or VNA.
- **Forgetting the return path.** $Z_0$ is defined for the signal-and-return pair. Change the reference plane distance and you change $Z_0$.
- **Confusing $Z_0$ with the driver's output impedance.** The driver's $R_s$ and the line's $Z_0$ form the launch divider; they are different things, and matching means making them agree.
:::

::: deep
## A wave on a line: the picture

Connect a source with open-circuit voltage $V_s$ and series resistance $R_s$ to a long line at $t=0$. Nothing downstream is charged. The first bit of line acts like a capacitor to the return plane, with an inductor in series feeding it. As the capacitor charges it lets the next section charge, and so on. A front moves down the line at speed $v=1/\sqrt{L'C'}$. Behind it, voltage is $V$ and a current $I$ flows. Ahead of it, nothing has happened yet.

The current drawn from the source is the charge per second needed to bring new line up to voltage:

$$I=\frac{dQ}{dt}=C'\,V\,\frac{dz}{dt}=C'vV$$

so the source sees a resistance $V/I=1/(C'v)=\sqrt{L'/C'}\equiv Z_0$. That is *all* $Z_0$ is: how much current the line demands per volt, while the front is moving through uncharged territory. A numerical example for a typical 50 Ω FR-4 microstrip: $v=1.73\times10^8$ m/s, $C'=115.5$ pF/m, so $I/V=C'v=1/(50.0\ \Omega)$.

Energy provides an independent check. With $V=2.75$ V, $I=V/Z_0=55$ mA. Per meter of line: electric energy $\tfrac12C'V^2=\tfrac12(115.5\text{ pF})(2.75)^2=0.437$ nJ and magnetic energy $\tfrac12L'I^2=\tfrac12(289\text{ nH})(0.055)^2=0.437$ nJ. They are equal, as they must be for a traveling wave.

## What sets $Z_0$ physically

$Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and $v=1/\sqrt{L'C'}$, so you can write $Z_0=1/(vC')$ or $Z_0=L'v$. For a fixed dielectric the product $L'C'=\varepsilon\mu$ is fixed (it is the wave speed), and the geometry only decides *how that product is shared* between $L'$ and $C'$:

- **Wider trace** (or closer plane): more area facing the plane, so larger $C'$ and smaller loop inductance $L'$, so lower $Z_0$.
- **Thicker dielectric** (plane farther away): lower $C'$, larger loop area and $L'$, so higher $Z_0$.
- **Higher $\varepsilon_r$**: $C'$ up, $L'$ unchanged, so $Z_0$ down as $1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$ (and the delay goes up as $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$).
- **Thicker copper**: slightly lower $Z_0$.

IPC-2141 microstrip example: $w=0.3$ mm, $h=0.2$ mm, $t=0.035$ mm, $\varepsilon_r=4.3$ gives $Z_0=87/\sqrt{5.71}\cdot\ln(1.196/0.275)=53.5\ \Omega$. Widen to $w=0.4$ mm and it falls to 44.2 Ω; thicken the dielectric to $h=0.3$ mm (keeping $w=0.3$) and it rises to 68.3 Ω. [[q0715]] and [[q0719]] go through the effects in detail.

Coax is cleaner: $Z_0=\frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln(D/d)$. For polyethylene ($\varepsilon_r=2.25$), 50 Ω needs $\ln(D/d)=1.25$, so $D/d=3.49$; 75 Ω needs $D/d=6.52$. With these numbers $L'=\frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln(D/d)=250$ nH/m and $C'=\frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}{\ln(D/d)}=100$ pF/m for the 50 Ω case, so $\sqrt{L'/C'}=50\ \Omega$ and $v=2.0\times10^8$ m/s (0.67c).

## Real lines: complex and frequency dependent

With loss, $Z_0=\sqrt{(R'+j\omega L')/(G'+j\omega C')}$. At high frequency this tends to $\sqrt{L'/C'}$ but at low frequency series resistance dominates. Take a 50 Ω line with the $L'=289$ nH/m and $C'=115.5$ pF/m used above, built as a fine 0.15 mm wide, 35 µm thick microstrip (which needs a thin dielectric, about 0.08 mm, to be 50 Ω). Its copper has $R'\approx3.3\ \Omega$/m at DC. The corner $f=R'/(2\pi L')=3.28/(2\pi\cdot289\text{ nH})\approx1.8$ MHz. Below that, $|Z_0|$ climbs, reaching about 670 Ω at 10 kHz.

Figure: At the default R′, above a couple of MHz the impedance is the flat 50 Ω of the lossless formula. Below the corner the resistive term takes over and $|Z_0|$ rises (it is off the top of this scale at 10 kHz, where it is about 670 Ω). Skin effect ($R'$ rising as $\sqrt f$) and dielectric loss make a smaller, real effect at GHz ([[q0722]]). This is why we say "50 Ω" without mentioning frequency: for any logic edge faster than a microsecond or so, the line is in the flat region.

## Why length doesn't matter, and why a DMM can't see it

Any length of lossless line terminated in $Z_L=Z_0$ has $Z_{in}=Z_0$ at every frequency. Only the *reflections* from the far end carry length information (they take $2t_d$ to return). A DMM applies DC for a long time, so the line has settled: the DMM sees only the load (open, short or whatever is connected) plus the DC resistance of the conductors. That is why $Z_0$ must be measured with a TDR/VNA (fast edge, early time) or computed from geometry.

## Mismatch quantified

Joining a 50 Ω line to a 75 Ω line gives $\Gamma=(75-50)/(75+50)=0.2$. Power reflected is $\Gamma^2=4\%$, return loss is $-20\log_{10}0.2=14$ dB, and VSWR is $(1+0.2)/(1-0.2)=1.5$. On a TDR with a 5 V launch step, a 60 Ω section would show a reflection of $5\cdot(60-50)/(110)=0.45$ V when it begins. That is how a TDR reveals a neck-down or a connector.

## Real hardware

- A PCB fab's "controlled impedance" service tolerance is usually quoted as ±10% (some offer ±5%); the number comes from etch tolerance, dielectric thickness and $\varepsilon_r$ variation. The fab often adjusts the trace width to hit the target based on their stack-up data.
- Impedance varies along a real trace: pad entries, vias, connector pins and package escapes all have their own local $L'$ and $C'$. Designers try to keep each below the TDR resolution (roughly $t_r/2$ in time).
- Cables: RG-58 (50 Ω), RG-59 (75 Ω); USB, HDMI and Ethernet pairs are specified in differential impedance.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A great one-liner: "$Z_0$ is what the line looks like while the signal hasn't yet found out what's at the far end." It shows you understand it as a transient concept, not a resistance.
:::

::: applications
- **Choosing PCB trace geometry** for 50 Ω single-ended or 90/100 Ω differential interfaces (USB, Ethernet, HDMI, PCIe, DDR).
- **Coaxial cables** for lab and RF: 50 Ω for instruments and RF, 75 Ω for video and cable TV.
- **Source/series termination**: choosing a damping resistor $R_s=Z_0-R_{drv}$.
- **TDR fault location**: an open or short in a cable reflects with $\Gamma=\pm1$, and the round-trip time gives the distance.
- **Antenna and RF front ends**: matching networks transform an antenna impedance to the system $Z_0$ for maximum power transfer.
- **Probing**: a 50 Ω coax probe or a Z0-probe uses a resistor equal to the line impedance to present a light, well-behaved load at GHz.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Lossless, uniform line** for the main formula. Real lines have $R'$ and $G'$, which make $Z_0$ frequency-dependent and slightly complex (loss and dispersion in [[q0722]] and [[q0723]]).
- **Single-mode, TEM/quasi-TEM** propagation. The ratio $V/I$ is well defined for the forward wave only.
- **Frequency above the RL corner**, a few MHz for fine PCB traces, so $Z_0$ is flat and real.
- **Microstrip $Z_0$ values come from approximate formulae** (IPC-2141 style) valid within a limited $w/h$ range, with a few percent error. Fabs and field solvers give better numbers.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Formulae for microstrip and stripline impedance differ between sources (IPC-2141, Hammerstad-Jensen, Wheeler) by a few ohms, and the dielectric constant of FR-4 varies by laminate, glass weave and frequency. Always take the final number from the fabricator's stack-up or a field solver.

Alternative views: in RF texts $Z_0$ is introduced from the telegrapher's equations in the frequency domain; in digital texts via the step-response picture used here. They give the same result.
:::

::: followups
### Why is $Z_0$ independent of length?
Because it is a *per-unit-length* ratio: both $L'$ and $C'$ scale with length, so their ratio doesn't, and the "current drawn per volt" by the advancing front is the same wherever it is. Length only enters through the delay $t_d=\ell/v$.

### How would you measure the characteristic impedance of a cable?
With a TDR: launch a fast step from a known source impedance, record the voltage at the launch plane. The plateau tells you $Z_0$ by $V_1=V_sZ_0/(R_s+Z_0)$. Or with a VNA, measure the open and short input impedances $Z_{oc}$ and $Z_{sc}$: $Z_0=\sqrt{Z_{oc}Z_{sc}}$. A DMM cannot do it.

### If I widen the trace, what happens to $Z_0$ and delay?
$Z_0$ drops (more capacitance to the plane and less loop inductance). For the earlier example, going from $w=0.3$ mm to $w=0.4$ mm drops $Z_0$ from 53.5 Ω to 44.2 Ω. The delay per length changes only slightly (it depends on $\varepsilon_{eff}$, which creeps up a little as more field is in the dielectric).

### What does a shorted quarter-wave line look like at its input?
Quarter-wave transformation: $Z_{in}=Z_0^2/Z_L$. With a short ($Z_L=0$) it is infinite, so the line looks like an open circuit at its input at that one frequency. That is how quarter-wave stubs and traps work, and why a short stub on a clock line can have a nasty resonance.

### Harder: two coax cables, 50 Ω and 75 Ω, are joined with a BNC adapter. What do you see on a TDR and in the data?
At the junction $\Gamma=0.2$: 20% of the incident voltage reflects with positive sign (going from low to high impedance), 4% of power, 14 dB return loss. For slow data it's invisible; for fast edges with a 1 ns round-trip, it adds a visible echo of 20% amplitude and eye closure for a high-speed link. The fix is an impedance-matching pad or transformer, or just using matching cable and connectors.
