::: basics
### In plain words
A switching drive is limited by whichever of several things runs out first. The main limit is heat: each switching transition wastes a little energy, so losses grow in step with frequency. Fixed delays, like dead time and the minimum pulse width, eat a bigger share of each shorter period. The gate driver, the timer, and EMI also set ceilings. There is a lower limit too, because slow switching gives large ripple and audible whine.

### Picture it
Think of stirring a pot of soup. Stir faster and the soup stays smoother, which is lower ripple, but your arm tires faster, which is heat. Each stroke also has fixed pauses at each end while you reverse. When the strokes are long, the pauses barely matter. When you stir very fast, the pauses become most of each stroke.

### Words you need
- **Switching loss**: energy wasted during each transition, while the transistor has both voltage and current. It is $\tfrac12 V I (t_r + t_f) f$.
- **Dead time**: a deliberate gap between complementary switch transitions.
- **Minimum on-time**: the shortest pulse the driver, sense blanking, and gate network can make.
- **Current ripple**: the variation in load current caused by the PWM. It falls as $1/f$.
- **Gate charge ($Q_g$)**: the charge needed to switch the FET. Gate-drive power is $Q_g V_g f$.

### The simplest example
A 12 V, 5 A stage with 100 ns edges, a 20 nC FET, and 16 mΩ of hot resistance loses about 0.47 W at 20 kHz, and about 2.15 W at 500 kHz. With a 1.3 W thermal budget, the ceiling is near 250 kHz. Fixed times bite sooner: 200 ns of dead time on each of two edges is 0.8% of a 20 kHz period, but 40% of a 1 MHz period.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the gate-driver and timer limits, EMI, and why 20 to 50 kHz is typical.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can name *all* the limiters of a switching drive and rank them for the situation: switching and gate-drive losses (heat), device speed (rise/fall, gate drive current), timing limits (dead time, minimum on-time, current-sense blanking, bootstrap refresh), controller limits (timer resolution, CPU load), load limits (core losses, ripple, audible noise) and EMI. Strong candidates give a loss equation with numbers, say which limit bites first in a motor or solenoid drive, and remember that the frequency also has a *lower* bound (ripple, audible noise).
:::

::: why
PWM frequency is a free parameter that interacts with everything: current ripple, motor heating, audible whine, EMI, efficiency, MCU load and component size. Choosing it badly makes an inaudible, efficient, quiet drive into a hot, buzzing or noisy one. The question tests whether you can reason about trade-offs rather than quote "20 kHz".
:::

::: concepts
- **Switching loss**: $P_{sw} = \tfrac12VI(t_r + t_f)f$, proportional to frequency. See [[q0417]].
- **Gate-drive loss and capability**: $P_g = Q_gV_gf$; driver peak current sets edge times. See [[q0431]].
- **Dead time and minimum pulse width**: fixed times that become a large fraction of the period at high $f$. See [[q0411]].
- **Current ripple**: $\Delta I = VD(1-D)/(Lf)$; sets the *lower* limit on $f$.
- **EMI**: edge rate and repetition rate set harmonic content.
- **Thermal limit**: total loss times $R_\theta$ against $T_{j,max}$. See [[q0416]].
:::

::: answer
"Switching frequency is limited by whichever of several things runs out first.

The big one is loss. Every transition has a period where the FET has both voltage and current, so switching loss is about half V times I times the rise-plus-fall time times frequency, and it scales linearly with frequency. Add the gate-drive power, Q_g times V_g times f, the output-capacitance loss and the body-diode reverse recovery. All proportional to f. The heat from those, on top of the conduction loss, sets a thermal ceiling. For a 12 volt, 5 amp example that's about 0.5 watts at 20 kilohertz but about 2 watts at 500 kilohertz, so with 1.3 watts of thermal budget the limit is around 250 kilohertz.

Second, speed and timing limits: the gate driver can only deliver so much current, so the edges can only be so fast; dead time of a couple hundred nanoseconds eats 40% of a 1 megahertz period; there's a minimum on-time for the driver and the current-sense blanking, so at high frequency I can't reach low duty cycles; and the bootstrap capacitor needs refresh time.

Third, the controller: a timer has finite resolution, so a 72 megahertz clock with 12-bit duty resolution gives only 17.6 kilohertz.

Fourth, EMI: faster, more frequent edges mean more harmonics.

And there's a lower limit: too low and the current ripple gets large and the motor whines, below 20 kilohertz it's audible. So I'd pick something like 20 to 50 kilohertz for a motor, higher for small inductors."
:::

::: technical
**Loss model** (12 V, 5 A, hot $R_{DS(on)} = 16$ mΩ, $t_r + t_f = 100$ ns, $Q_g = 20$ nC at 5 V, $C_{oss} = 500$ pF, $Q_{rr} = 30$ nC):

| Loss | Formula | Value at 20 kHz | Value at 500 kHz |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conduction | $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ | 0.40 W | 0.40 W |
| Overlap | $\tfrac12VI(t_r+t_f)f$ | 0.06 W | 1.50 W |
| Gate drive | $Q_gV_gf$ | 2 mW | 50 mW |
| $C_{oss}$ | $\tfrac12C_{oss}V^2f$ | 0.7 mW | 18 mW |
| Reverse recovery | $Q_{rr}Vf$ | 7 mW | 180 mW |
| **Total** | | **0.47 W** | **2.15 W** |

With a thermal budget $\Delta T = 125 - 60 = 65$ °C and $R_{\theta JA} = 50$ °C/W, $P_{max} = 1.3$ W, so $f_{max} \approx (1.3 - 0.4)/(3.5\times10^{-6}\text{ W/Hz}) = 257$ kHz (ignoring the $R_{DS(on)}$ rise with temperature, so the real limit is lower).

**Timing limits.**
- **Dead time** $t_d$ per edge: fraction of period lost $= 2t_df$: 200 ns gives 0.8% at 20 kHz, 40% at 1 MHz.
- **Minimum on-time** $t_{min}$ (driver propagation, current-sense blanking, bootstrap): minimum duty $= t_{min}f$; 300 ns at 100 kHz is 3%, at 1 MHz 30%.
- **Edge time** $t \approx Q_{gd}/I_g$: when $t_r + t_f$ approaches a sizable fraction of $T$, the waveform is no longer a clean square wave.

**Controller limits.** PWM timer resolution $N$ bits at clock $f_{clk}$: $f_{PWM} = f_{clk}/2^N$. 72 MHz and 12 bits: 17.6 kHz; 10 bits: 70.3 kHz. Some timers offer higher resolution modes. Control-loop ISR rate at $f$ (CPU load).

**Load and magnetics.** Ripple $\Delta I = V D(1-D)/(Lf)$: for $L = 1$ mH, 12 V, $D = 0.5$: 3 A at 1 kHz, 0.15 A at 20 kHz, 0.03 A at 100 kHz. Core (iron) loss in the motor increases with ripple frequency but falls with ripple amplitude; at 20 to 50 kHz it is usually modest in brushed motors (vendor-dependent). Audible noise: below about 20 kHz the motor and ceramic capacitors can whine.

**EMI.** Harmonics of $f$ extend to $\approx 1/(\pi t_r)$; each doubling of $f$ moves the fundamental and harmonics into different bands. Higher $f$ increases the number of harmonics in a regulated band (e.g. conducted emissions starting at 150 kHz), so the design often keeps $f$ below that or filters accordingly.

**Gate driver capability.** Average gate current $I_{g,avg} = Q_gf$: 20 nC at 500 kHz is 10 mA, at the limit for some GPIO-like drivers; peak current sets the edge time.
:::

::: long
"There are several limits, and which one bites first depends on the drive.

The main one is loss. In each transition the FET has voltage and current at once, so switching loss is about half V times I times rise-plus-fall time times frequency. It's linear in frequency, as are the gate-drive power, Q_g times V_g times f, the loss in the output capacitance, and the reverse recovery of the body diode. For a 12 volt, 5 amp example with 100 nanosecond edges, total loss goes from about 0.47 watts at 20 kilohertz to 2.2 watts at 500 kilohertz. If the part can only shed about 1.3 watts, the ceiling is around 250 kilohertz, less in practice because on-resistance rises with temperature.

Second, there are fixed times that don't scale. Dead time of 200 nanoseconds per edge is under 1% of a 20 kilohertz period but 40% of a 1 megahertz period. The driver and current-sense blanking set a minimum on-time, 300 nanoseconds is 3% duty at 100 kilohertz but 30% at 1 megahertz, so I lose the low end of duty cycle. And a high-side bootstrap needs refresh time.

Third, the gate driver: the average gate current is Q_g times f, and its peak current sets how fast the edges can be.

Fourth, the controller: a timer with 12-bit resolution at 72 megahertz tops out around 17 kilohertz.

Fifth, EMI. More edges per second and faster edges raise harmonics, and I may have to stay below the conducted-emission band that begins at 150 kilohertz.

On the other side, there's a lower limit: ripple current goes as one over frequency, and below about 20 kilohertz the motor sings. So for a motor drive I'd choose 20 to 50 kilohertz, where all of this is comfortable."
:::

::: terms
- **Switching loss**: energy lost during the voltage-current overlap in each transition.
- **Gate charge $Q_g$**: charge needed to turn the FET on; sets gate-drive current and power.
- **Dead time**: deliberate gap between complementary switch transitions.
- **Minimum on-time**: shortest pulse the driver, sense blanking and gate network can produce.
- **Reverse recovery ($Q_{rr}$)**: charge swept out of a diode when it turns off, causing loss and spikes.
- **Current ripple**: peak-to-peak variation in load current from PWM.
- **Thermal limit**: maximum power for the allowed junction temperature.
- **Harmonics**: multiples of the switching frequency in the EMI spectrum.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "switching losses". An exceptional answer:

- Writes the **loss equations** (overlap, gate, $C_{oss}$, $Q_{rr}$) and computes a frequency ceiling from a **thermal budget**.
- Includes **fixed-time limits** (dead time, minimum on-time, blanking, bootstrap) as fractions of the period.
- Mentions the **gate driver** current and the **controller timer** resolution.
- Covers **EMI** and the **load/magnetics** side.
- States the **lower bound** (ripple, audible noise) and picks a sensible range with a reason.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking switching frequency is only limited by the MOSFET's datasheet "speed".** Drivers, timing, thermal, EMI and the controller matter as much.
- **Forgetting that dead time and minimum on-time are fixed.** They become large fractions of the period at high $f$.
- **Ignoring the $R_{DS(on)}$ temperature rise** when computing the thermal ceiling.
- **Believing higher $f$ is always better.** It costs efficiency and EMI; the gain is smaller ripple, smaller magnetics and silence.
- **Treating the timer clock as free.** Resolution times frequency is constrained by $f_{clk}$.
- **Confusing mechanical and electrical limits for solenoids.** The PWM frequency of a hold chopper is electrical; the cycle rate of the plunger is mechanical ([[q0415]]).
:::

::: deep
## Where frequency comes from

In a PWM drive the load current ripples around its average. Ripple amplitude falls as $1/f$ and the loss rises as $f$. The best frequency sits between: high enough that the ripple, noise and magnetics are small, low enough that heat, EMI and timing limits are met. This page concentrates on the *upper* limit; [[q0419]] covers how to push it up.

## Loss budget

Per switching event a hard-switched MOSFET with an inductive load sees, during the rise (turn-on) and fall (turn-off) times, voltage and current together. Approximating linear transitions, the energy per transition is $\tfrac12 V I t_{sw}$ and so

$$P_{sw} = \tfrac12 V I\,(t_r + t_f)\,f$$

Other frequency-proportional losses:

- **Gate charge**: $P_g = Q_gV_gf$ dissipated in the driver and gate resistor.
- **Output capacitance**: $P_{Coss} = \tfrac12 C_{oss}V^2f$ (energy stored and dumped each cycle).
- **Body-diode reverse recovery**: $P_{rr} = Q_{rr}Vf$ (more when the opposite FET turns on into a recovering diode).
- **Dead-time diode conduction**: $V_{SD}\,I\,2t_df$.

## Timing limits that do not scale

Some times are fixed by silicon, drivers and sensing, so they take a larger share of the period as $f$ rises.

| Limit | Value | Share of period at 20 kHz | 100 kHz | 1 MHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dead time (2 edges, 200 ns each) | 400 ns | 0.8% | 4% | 40% |
| Min on-time (300 ns) | 300 ns | duty min 0.6% | 3% | 30% |
| Edge times (100 ns total) | 100 ns | 0.2% | 1% | 10% |

At 1 MHz the dead time alone eats 40% of the period; the controllable duty range collapses and the nonlinearity gets severe. Current sensing adds a blanking window after each turn-on to ignore the switching spike (typically 100 to 500 ns); the minimum pulse width includes it. The high-side bootstrap needs the low-side to conduct for at least enough time to recharge the bootstrap capacitor at high duty ([[q0427]]).

## Gate drive capability

Average gate current is $I_{g,avg} = Q_g f$: for 20 nC at 500 kHz, 10 mA, comfortable for a gate driver but at the limit of a GPIO. Peak current sets the edge time: $t \approx Q_{gd}/I_g$. A weak driver makes edges slow, increasing $P_{sw}$ in proportion; the driver loss $Q_gV_gf$ is dissipated in the driver and gate resistor, so a tiny driver package can itself overheat at high $f$.

## Controller and software limits

PWM timers divide a clock into $2^N$ steps per period: $f_{PWM} = f_{clk}/2^N$ for an up-counting timer. Examples: 72 MHz, 12-bit: 17.6 kHz; 10-bit: 70 kHz; 168 MHz, 12-bit: 41 kHz. Fewer bits mean coarser duty steps (1% steps with 100 counts). Control loops running once per PWM period need CPU time at that rate; at 100 kHz an ISR of 5 µs consumes 50% of the CPU. Some MCUs have high-resolution timer extensions (sub-nanosecond edge placement) to get both.

## Load, magnetics and acoustics: the other direction

The load sets the *lower* bound and some upper effects.

- **Ripple**: $\Delta I = V D(1-D)/(Lf)$. For a 1 mH motor at 12 V and 50% duty: 3 A pk-pk at 1 kHz (the formula overstates it: the current would simply swing between near zero and a large peak each cycle), 0.15 A at 20 kHz, 0.03 A at 100 kHz.
- **Audible noise**: the motor windings and capacitors vibrate at the PWM frequency; below about 20 kHz many people hear it, so drive above that or use random/spread-spectrum modulation.
- **Iron and copper loss in the motor**: ripple current adds $I_{ripple,rms}^2R$ and core loss. Skin and proximity effects in the winding increase at high frequency. For a typical brushed motor these remain small at 20 to 50 kHz.
- **Motor bandwidth**: the electrical time constant $L/R$ (1 ms for the example) means anything above a few hundred hertz is filtered; the motor doesn't care about frequencies far above that except through losses and noise.

## EMI

The harmonic content of a trapezoidal wave extends to about $f_{knee} = 1/(\pi t_r)$; for $t_r = 50$ ns that is 6.4 MHz. Frequency sets the number of harmonics inside each regulated band, and conducted-emission limits typically start at 150 kHz; a 20 kHz fundamental has its 8th harmonic at 160 kHz. Spread spectrum and slew-rate control reduce peaks. See the EMI topics in the PCB section.

## So, how to choose

1. Pick the minimum frequency from ripple and audibility (about 20 kHz for motors, lower for solenoid hold if acoustics allow).
2. Compute the loss budget at that frequency; check the thermal limit with margin.
3. Check dead-time/min-on-time fractions and duty range.
4. Check timer resolution and CPU load.
5. Check EMI plan.
6. If you need to go higher, see [[q0419]].
:::

::: applications
- **Brushed motor and fan drives**: 20 to 50 kHz PWM, a balance of silence and loss.
- **Solenoid hold choppers**: 20 kHz or higher, chosen for current ripple and acoustic silence.
- **Brushless motor drives for drones**: 24 to 48 kHz typical, with some at 96 kHz or higher for low-inductance motors.
- **Buck converters and point-of-load supplies**: 500 kHz to several MHz with fast low-$Q_g$ FETs, where all limits above apply.
- **Class-D amplifiers**: hundreds of kHz with careful dead-time management.
- **Servo drives**: 8 to 20 kHz IGBT drives (tail current, limited switching frequency).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Hard-switched** half-bridge or low-side stage with an **inductive load**; soft-switched converters (ZVS) escape the overlap loss.
- A **silicon MOSFET** with illustrative parameters; IGBTs have tail loss and typically lower limits (20 to 50 kHz class, vendor-dependent), GaN/SiC can switch much faster.
- Thermal limit from a **simple $R_\theta$ model**; real designs include transient thermal impedance and the temperature dependence of $R_{DS(on)}$.
- The **load** is a DC motor or solenoid with $L/R$ in the millisecond range.
- EMI limits depend on the standard and product; frequencies quoted are typical, not universal.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the motor not care about switching at 100 kHz instead of 20 kHz?
Because its electrical time constant ($L/R$, around a millisecond) filters the PWM. Both give essentially the same average current, with smaller ripple at 100 kHz. The motor itself sees a tiny benefit (less ripple heating); the cost is more loss and EMI in the drive.

### Which loss dominates at high frequency for a hard-switched MOSFET?
Usually the overlap (voltage-current) loss grows fastest in absolute terms, followed by $C_{oss}$ and reverse-recovery loss in bridges. Gate-drive loss is small for logic-level power FETs but rises for large FETs at MHz. Reducing $t_r + t_f$, $C_{oss}$ and $Q_{rr}$ is what GaN and good layout do.

### Your PWM at 500 kHz can't reach 5% duty. Why?
The minimum pulse width: driver propagation delay, current-sense blanking and gate rise/fall time together may need 300 ns or more, and at 500 kHz the period is 2 µs so the minimum duty is 15%. Lower the frequency, shorten the blanking, or use pulse-skipping at light load.

### What's the limit for IGBTs?
Turn-off tail current: stored charge decays slowly, adding switching loss that grows with $f$; typical hard-switched IGBT drives run at about 8 to 20 kHz and up to 50 kHz for fast types (vendor-dependent). Above that MOSFETs, SiC or GaN take over.

### The drive is fine at 20 kHz but the EMI test fails at 150 kHz to 300 kHz. What are the options?
Reduce the harmonics: slow the edges (more $R_g$, a snubber), add input filtering, use spread-spectrum modulation, or move $f$ so that no harmonic falls on a limit-line corner. Alternatively lower $f$ (so the strong harmonics move below 150 kHz) or use better layout to reduce loop area.
:::

::: interpretations
"Switching frequency" refers to the **PWM/switching frequency of a drive** (motor, solenoid, converter). For solenoids it could also mean the mechanical on/off rate; I treat the electrical PWM here and refer to [[q0415]] for the mechanical cycle rate. The inverse question, how to increase it, is [[q0419]].
