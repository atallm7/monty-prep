::: basics
### In plain words
An LDO is simple and quiet, but it wastes the whole voltage difference as heat. A buck converter is efficient, but needs an inductor and makes switching ripple and noise. So the choice depends on headroom and current. With a small headroom or a small load, or when the rail feeds sensitive analog parts, use an LDO. With a big headroom and more than a few hundred milliamps, use a buck.

### Picture it
Slowing a car by braking turns all the extra motion into heat, like an LDO. Slowing it with regenerative braking gets some energy back, like a buck. Plain brakes are simple and smooth, but they waste energy and get hot. Regenerative braking saves energy but adds machinery and some noise. The analogy skips the fact that you can combine them: a buck first, then an LDO to clean up.

### Words you need
- **Efficiency**: output power divided by input power ([[q0277]]).
- **Headroom**: the difference between input and output voltage.
- **Ripple and EMI**: the switching noise a buck makes and an LDO does not.
- **PSRR**: how well a regulator blocks noise from its input.
- **Quiescent current**: the current a regulator uses just to stay on.

### The simplest example
Take 12 V in, 3.3 V out, and a 1 A load. The LDO wastes $(12-3.3)\times1=8.7$ W, an efficiency of about 27%. A 90% buck wastes only about $3.3/0.9-3.3\approx0.37$ W. Now take 3.5 V in instead. The LDO wastes just $(3.5-3.3)\times1=0.2$ W, and it needs no inductor, so the LDO wins.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page puts LDO, buck, and charge pump side by side in a table, and shows how to combine them.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can give a **structured, quantified comparison** instead of "LDOs are simple but inefficient". The interviewer wants the full set of axes (efficiency, heat, noise, PSRR, transient speed, size, cost, EMI, quiescent current, input/output range, parts count) and the judgement of which axis dominates in which situation. They are also listening for the *combination* trick (buck followed by an LDO) and for numbers. This overlaps with [[q0157]] and [[q0158]], but here the comparison is against **step-down regulators in general** (buck, charge pump, and passive dropping), not just a buck.
:::

::: why
Rail design is almost always a choice between these regulator types, and the answer is rarely "always buck". On a real board you will put an LDO on the noisy-sensitive analog rail, a buck on the 12 V to 3.3 V main rail and a buck-then-LDO combination where both efficiency and noise matter ([[q0169]]). Interviewers use this question to see if you pick the tool based on requirements: current, headroom, noise budget, thermal budget, board area and cost.
:::

::: concepts
- **Efficiency**: LDO $\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$, buck about 85 to 97% depending on load and design. See [[q0162]].
- **Heat**: LDO dissipates $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$. See [[q0282]].
- **Output noise and ripple**: LDOs have no switching ripple. Bucks have ripple and switching spikes. See [[q0233]].
- **PSRR**: LDO rejects input ripple (limited by loop bandwidth). See [[q0278]].
- **Quiescent current**: LDOs can be microamps. See [[q0165]].
- **Step-down alternatives**: buck, charge pump (switched-capacitor), dropper resistor, zener shunt.
:::

::: answer
"The main trade-off is efficiency versus simplicity and noise. An LDO is just a pass transistor, so its efficiency is about $V_{out}/V_{in}$ and all the difference is burned as heat. From 12 V to 3.3 V at 1 A that's 8.7 W, which won't work, while a buck would be about 90% and lose under a watt. So at large headroom or high current, the buck wins and the LDO is out.

But the LDO wins on everything else. It has no switching, so no inductor, no ripple, and no EMI. It's quiet, it has high power-supply rejection at low frequency, and it has a fast response to load steps. It needs only a couple of capacitors, so it's tiny and cheap. It has very low quiescent current options for battery standby. And it works when the input is close to the output.

The usual way I'd resolve it: if headroom is small, or the load is tiny, or the rail feeds sensitive analog or RF, use an LDO. If headroom is big and the current is more than a few hundred milliamps, use a buck. And when you need both, buck down to just above the target, then an LDO to clean it up. A charge pump sits in between: no inductor, but fixed ratios and limited current."
:::

::: technical
**Comparison table** (typical ranges, design-dependent):

| Property | LDO | Buck | Charge pump (step-down) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Efficiency | $\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$ | 85 to 97% | $\approx V_{out}/(V_{in}/N)$ at fixed ratio $N$ |
| Heat | $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$ | $I_{out}V_{out}(1/\eta-1)$ | $\approx$ similar to LDO between ratio points |
| Output noise | very low (about 10 to 100 $\mu V_{rms}$ low-noise parts) | ripple mV plus spikes | ripple mV, spikes |
| PSRR | high at low frequency (60 to 80 dB), falls above loop bandwidth | not applicable (ripple is the issue) | low |
| EMI | none | significant, needs layout and filtering | moderate |
| Parts | 2 capacitors (+ feedback divider) | inductor, 2 to 3 capacitors, maybe diode/FET | flying and output capacitors |
| Max practical current | limited by thermals, about 1 to 3 A | tens of amps | up to a few hundred mA |
| Quiescent current | sub-$\mu$A to tens of $\mu$A available | typically about 10 to 100 $\mu$A (light-load mode) | 10 to 100 $\mu$A |
| Min headroom | 0.05 to 0.5 V (dropout) | any (buck can have 100% duty) | needs ratio headroom |
| Transient response | fast (loop bandwidth up to MHz) | slower (LC filter, loop bandwidth ~ $f_{sw}/10$) | fixed-ratio, load-dependent |
| Cost / area | lowest | higher | between |

**Thermal limit** (key practical constraint): $P_{max}=\dfrac{T_{J,max}-T_A}{\theta_{JA}}$. With $T_J=125$ °C, $T_A=70$ °C, $\theta_{JA}=60$ °C/W: 0.92 W, so $I_{max}=0.92/(V_{in}-V_{out})$: 0.54 A at 1.7 V of headroom (5 to 3.3 V) and 4.6 A at 0.2 V.

**Combined architecture**: buck from 12 V to about 3.6 V (92%) then LDO to 3.3 V (91.7%): 84% overall with LDO-grade noise, versus 27.5% for an LDO alone from 12 V.
:::

::: short
"LDOs are simple, quiet and small, with fast transient response and no EMI, but their efficiency is $V_{out}/V_{in}$ and the lost power is heat, so they only work at small headroom or low current. Buck converters are efficient at large step-downs and high currents but need an inductor, make switching noise and EMI, and cost more area. So: small headroom or sensitive analog, LDO; big headroom or high current, buck; and if you need both, buck followed by an LDO."
:::

::: long
"I'd compare along efficiency, noise, size and cost, transient behavior and current range.

Efficiency is the big one. An LDO's efficiency is just $V_{out}/V_{in}$, because input current equals output current. From 5 V to 3.3 V it's 66%, and from 12 V it's 27%. A buck is typically 85 to 95% regardless of ratio. That turns into heat: at 12 V to 3.3 V and 1 A the LDO burns 8.7 W, which needs a heatsink, while the buck loses under a watt. So the LDO has a practical current limit set by thermals, typically one to a few amps, while a buck can supply tens of amps.

But for noise, size and simplicity, the LDO wins. No inductor, no switching ripple, no EMI, two capacitors. Output noise can be tens of microvolts, and PSRR can be 60 to 80 dB at low frequency, which is what you want for an ADC reference supply or a PLL. It also responds to load steps within microseconds, and you can get quiescent currents in the nanoamp to microamp range for battery standby.

A third point is headroom. A buck can regulate from any input above the output, including down to 100% duty cycle, but an LDO needs its dropout voltage. And a charge pump is a middle option: no inductor, but fixed ratios and typically under a few hundred milliamps.

In practice I'd pick by requirement: small headroom or very sensitive rail, an LDO; large headroom or high current, a buck; both, then a buck to a few hundred millivolts above the target followed by an LDO, which gets maybe 84% efficiency from 12 V to 3.3 V with clean output."
:::

::: terms
- **Headroom**: $V_{in}-V_{out}$ available to the regulator.
- **Thermal limit**: maximum dissipation allowed by $T_{J,max}$ and $\theta_{JA}$.
- **PSRR**: rejection of input ripple at the output, in dB.
- **Output noise ($\mu V_{rms}$)**: integrated noise density over a band, for example 10 Hz to 100 kHz.
- **EMI**: radiated and conducted interference from switching.
- **Post-regulation**: an LDO after a switching regulator to clean the rail.
- **Charge pump**: inductorless switched-capacitor converter with fixed ratios ([[q0284]]).
- **Quiescent current**: current drawn by the regulator at no load ([[q0165]]).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "LDOs are less efficient but simpler and less noisy".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies efficiency and heat with a real example (12 V to 3.3 V at 1 A: 27% and 8.7 W vs about 90% and under 1 W).
- Calculates the thermal current limit from $\theta_{JA}$ and $T_J$.
- Lists specific LDO advantages: no EMI, low noise, high PSRR, fast transients, tiny BOM, sub-microamp $I_Q$ options.
- Notes the **dropout** and **headroom** constraints and that bucks have no such floor.
- Gives the combined **buck + LDO** answer and its efficiency, and mentions where charge pumps fit.
- Points out that at tiny loads or very small headroom the LDO can be *more* efficient overall ([[q0158]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"LDOs are always worse."** At small headroom or very light load the LDO can match or beat a buck in efficiency, with less noise.
- **"A buck is noise-free if filtered."** Switching spikes and ripple still reach sensitive analog circuits. An LDO or a careful LC filter after the buck is the standard fix.
- **Forgetting thermals.** The LDO's current limit is thermal, not electrical. SOT-23 packages can only dissipate a few hundred milliwatts.
- **Quoting PSRR at one frequency.** PSRR falls above the loop bandwidth, so a 70 dB spec at 1 kHz may be 20 dB at 1 MHz, exactly where the buck's switching ripple lives.
- **Ignoring dropout and temperature.** The LDO needs more headroom hot and at high current.
- **Assuming a charge pump replaces a buck.** Charge pumps have fixed ratios and high output resistance, with limited current.
:::

::: deep
## Efficiency and heat: the deciding factor at large headroom

For an LDO, $\eta\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$ and $P_{loss}=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$. The buck's loss is a small fraction of output power. Three operating points, all at 3.3 V:

| Case | LDO loss | LDO $\eta$ | Buck (assume 90%) loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 V, 1 A | 8.70 W | 27.5% | 0.37 W |
| 5 V, 0.2 A | 0.34 W | 66% | 0.07 W |
| 3.7 V, 0.1 A | 0.04 W | 89% | 0.04 W (at best, 90%) |

At the last point the LDO ties the buck: with a typical buck quiescent current of tens of microamps and light-load efficiency near 85 to 90% for the best parts, the LDO is as good, and it is quieter. That's the "LDO can win" regime from [[q0158]].

### The thermal ceiling

The practical limit on an LDO is not the transistor's current rating but the heat:
$$I_{max}=\frac{(T_{J,max}-T_A)/\theta_{JA}}{V_{in}-V_{out}}$$

## Noise, PSRR and EMI: where LDOs are better

A buck's output has switching ripple and sharp spikes at $f_{sw}$ and harmonics (see [[q0233]]). Typical numbers: 10 to 50 mV of ripple and spikes of 50 to 200 mV with fast edges at the SW node. Sensitive circuits (ADCs, PLLs, VCOs, RF, microphones) cannot tolerate that directly.

An LDO has no switching, and its noise is the reference and error amplifier noise, often a few to tens of $\mu V_{rms}$ in low-noise parts. Its PSRR is high at low frequency (loop gain) but falls above the loop bandwidth. So the combination is: buck ripple at 500 kHz to 2 MHz lands where the LDO's PSRR is poor (maybe 30 to 50 dB, vendor- and headroom-dependent), and the output capacitor and an optional ferrite bead take over.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Output-noise and PSRR values quoted are typical ranges for low-noise parts. Check the datasheet's PSRR *vs frequency and headroom* curves for the actual numbers: PSRR often degrades sharply when headroom approaches dropout.

## Transient response

An LDO loop can have a bandwidth of 100 kHz to several MHz, so it can recover from a load step in a few microseconds with modest droop ($\Delta V\approx\Delta I/(2\pi f_cC_{out})$). A buck's loop is typically limited to roughly $f_{sw}/10$ to $f_{sw}/5$ by the LC filter and the sampled nature of the converter, and its output inductor limits slew rate ($di/dt=(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$). In practice bucks often need more output capacitance. See [[q0248]].

## Size, cost and parts count

LDO: IC plus input and output capacitors, adjustable parts add two resistors. Buck: IC plus inductor (the big and costly part), input and output capacitors, maybe a diode, bootstrap capacitor and compensation network, and a more careful layout with a small high-$di/dt$ loop. For a 100 mA rail from a battery the LDO may use about 4 mm² and the buck 15 to 30 mm².

## The standard hybrid: buck then LDO

Efficiency of a cascade is the product. From 12 V to 3.3 V at 0.5 A:

- LDO only: $\eta=27.5\%$, 4.35 W lost.
- Buck only (90%): 0.18 W lost, but ripple on the 3.3 V rail.
- Buck to 5 V (92%) then LDO to 3.3 V: $0.92\times0.66=61\%$. Poor, headroom too large.
- Buck to 3.6 V (92%) then LDO to 3.3 V: $0.92\times0.917=84\%$, and the LDO cleans the ripple. The extra 0.3 V headroom is chosen to exceed the LDO dropout plus ripple and tolerance, but stay small ([[q0169]]).

The headroom selected for the post-LDO is a three-way trade-off between efficiency (small), PSRR (needs enough headroom to keep the pass device in saturation), and dropout at worst-case load, temperature and tolerance.

## Other step-down options

- **Charge pump (step-down)**: switched-capacitor divide by 2 or 3 gives efficiency near the ideal $V_{out}/(V_{in}/N)$ when the output is near $V_{in}/N$, no inductor, but the output voltage is not regulated by ratio (some are regulated with an LDO stage or by modulating the switch resistance). Limited current ([[q0284]]).
- **Zener/shunt reference with resistor**: only for tiny, constant loads. It wastes power continuously and gives poor load regulation.
- **Series dropper resistor**: for constant load only, like an LED. It cannot regulate against input or load changes ([[q0279]]).
:::

::: applications
- **ADC, DAC and sensor analog rails**: a low-noise LDO after the digital buck.
- **PLL and RF supplies**: high-PSRR LDOs to keep switching spurs off the VCO.
- **Battery-powered microcontroller with tiny headroom**: single LDO from a Li-ion cell to 3.3 V or 1.8 V.
- **Large step-down, high current rails** (12 V to 1 V at 10 A, CPU/FPGA core): multiphase buck, no LDO.
- **Hybrid rails**: buck-LDO for audio, instrumentation, and radio modules.
- **Always-on standby**: nanoamp-$I_Q$ LDO to keep a real-time clock or wake-up circuit alive.
:::

::: assumptions
- Numbers are **typical** and part-dependent. Buck efficiency spans a wide range (50% at tiny loads to 97% at best) and LDO noise and PSRR vary by orders of magnitude between part families.
- The comparison is for **non-isolated DC-DC step-down at low to moderate power** (up to tens of watts).
- Thermal limits assume a single package at given $\theta_{JA}$. A large copper pour or heatsink can raise the LDO's usable dissipation to several watts.
- **Quiescent current** matters only in standby-dominated designs. In an active 1 A rail it is irrelevant.
- An alternative valid answer lists *cost and design time* as a major axis: an LDO takes minutes to lay out, a buck takes careful thought.
:::

::: followups
### When is an LDO more efficient than a buck?
When headroom is tiny (efficiency $V_{out}/V_{in}$ approaches 90 to 97%), or when the load is so light that the buck's quiescent and fixed switching losses dominate. For example 3.7 V in, 3.3 V out, a few mA: the LDO gives 89% with no switching, while a buck's light-load efficiency is typically in the 80s at best. See [[q0158]].

### Why not just filter a buck's output with an LC filter instead of using an LDO?
An LC filter attenuates high-frequency ripple but does nothing for low-frequency noise and load regulation, adds DCR drop, and can resonate with the buck's loop. An LDO regulates, rejects noise broadband up to its bandwidth and gives a precise voltage. For ripple at the switching frequency a small ferrite or LC is sometimes enough. For wideband noise, an LDO.

### What is the maximum current you would put through an LDO?
It depends on headroom and package: use $I=P_{max}/(V_{in}-V_{out})$. With a power-dissipation budget of about 1 W (a typical SOT-223 or small DFN on a decent board) and 1 V of headroom, about 1 A. With 0.2 V of headroom (very low dropout) several amps are possible. I would not put an LDO at more than a couple of watts without a good thermal design or a pre-regulator.

### How do you improve the thermal situation of an LDO without changing the topology?
Lower the headroom with a pre-regulator (buck) or tap the input from a lower rail, use a series resistor to move part of the dissipation off the chip, spread the dissipation (two LDOs in parallel with ballast, if the part allows), use a package with an exposed pad and thermal vias, and increase the copper area and airflow.

### Scenario: your boss wants to power a 5 V, 100 mA sensor from a 24 V rail. LDO or buck?
Compute: LDO loss is $19\times0.1=1.9$ W, efficiency 21%, and the part needs a power package and a big copper pad. A buck would lose roughly 0.1 W at 85 to 90% efficiency. If the sensor is noise-sensitive, I'd use a buck to about 6 V and an LDO to 5 V, which gets $\eta\approx0.9\times5/6=75\%$ with a 0.1 W LDO loss. If the budget is tight and 100 mA is truly the maximum, a good thermal design could allow the LDO, but I'd flag it as a risk.
