::: testing
Whether you know the **common numbers** and, more importantly, the **reasons** behind them. The interviewer is checking that you:

1. Know the headline values: 50 Ω (RF, scopes, PCB single-ended), 75 Ω (video), 90/100 Ω differential (USB, Ethernet, HDMI, LVDS), 120 Ω (CAN, RS-485).
2. Can explain why 50 Ω is the compromise between lowest loss and highest power handling in a coax, instead of reciting "it's just standard".
3. Understand that a standard impedance is a **system agreement**: everything in the chain must share it or you get reflections.
:::

::: why
When you design a board you don't pick $Z_0$ freely. The connector, cable, driver and receiver of the interface all assume a number, and your PCB trace must hit it. Knowing the values lets you set up the stackup correctly, choose a termination, and spot a mistake immediately (a 75 Ω cable plugged into a 50 Ω scope input, a 90 Ω USB pair routed at 100 Ω). It is also a common warm-up question because it's a quick check on whether you've done real high-speed work.
:::

::: concepts
- **Characteristic impedance** $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and what it means for a wave. See [[q0710]].
- **Matching**: termination equal to $Z_0$ gives no reflection, $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$. See [[q0714]].
- **Single-ended vs differential impedance**: a pair has $Z_{diff}\approx2Z_0$ if uncoupled. See [[q0719]].
- **Coax trade-offs**: conductor loss, power handling and voltage breakdown depend on the ratio $D/d$.
- **Termination styles**: parallel, Thevenin, series, differential. See [[q0716]].
- **Return loss and VSWR**: how mismatch is quantified for RF.
:::

::: answer
"The big one is **50 Ω**: RF gear, test equipment, scope inputs and most single-ended PCB traces. It's a compromise in air-filled coax, since lowest loss is near 77 Ω and highest power handling is near 30 Ω, so 50 Ω gives good loss and good power. **75 Ω** is for video and cable TV because it's near the minimum loss point and loss mattered over long runs.

For differential pairs it's usually **100 Ω**: Ethernet over twisted pair, HDMI, DisplayPort, LVDS, and PCIe is a bit lower at about 85 Ω. **USB** is 90 Ω differential. **CAN and RS-485** use **120 Ω** twisted pair, with a 120 Ω terminator at each end of the bus. For memory like DDR, single-ended traces are typically 40 to 50 Ω and the controller's on-die termination is selectable, often 40, 60 or 120 Ω.

Termination values follow from those: a parallel termination equals $Z_0$, a series termination is $Z_0$ minus the driver's output impedance, and a differential termination is across the pair at the pair's differential impedance. And the real rule is: whatever the standard says is what the cable, connector and PCB trace all have to match, because any step in $Z_0$ makes a reflection."
:::

::: technical
**Common single-ended values**

| $Z_0$ | Where | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 50 Ω | RF, test gear, microwave, most PCB single-ended, coax like RG-58 | Compromise between loss and power handling; convenient trace widths |
| 75 Ω | Video, CATV, coax like RG-6, some broadcast and digital audio | Near minimum conductor loss for air-like coax |
| 93 Ω | RG-62 style coax, old networking | Low-capacitance cable |
| 300 Ω | Twin-lead antenna feed | Balanced line, low loss |
| 600 Ω | Legacy telephone and audio line interfaces | Historic convention |
| 40–50 Ω | DDR/LPDDR single-ended PCB traces | Set by the memory spec and driver strength |

**Common differential values**

| $Z_{diff}$ | Where |
|---|---|
| 90 Ω | USB 2.0 and 3.x |
| 100 Ω | Ethernet (twisted pair), HDMI, DisplayPort, LVDS, SATA, MIPI D-PHY |
| 85 Ω | PCIe (many generations; check the spec) |
| 120 Ω | CAN, RS-485/RS-422 bus |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact targets and tolerances differ between interface generations and board classes (for example PCIe has used both 100 Ω and 85 Ω targets across versions and connector types, and DDR targets vary by generation and vendor guide). Always take the number from the interface spec or the chip vendor's layout guide.

**Why 50 Ω.** For an air-dielectric coax $Z_0=60\ln(D/d)$. At fixed outer diameter $D$:
- Minimum conductor loss at $D/d=3.59$ → $Z_0\approx77$ Ω.
- Maximum power handling (limited by breakdown at the inner conductor) at $D/d=\sqrt e=1.65$ → $Z_0=30$ Ω.
- 50 Ω gives about 10% more loss than the minimum and about 86% of the maximum power capacity. It's the geometric-style midpoint between 30 and 77 Ω ($\sqrt{30\times77}\approx48$ Ω).

**Mismatch cost.** A 75 Ω system on a 50 Ω input: $\Gamma=(75-50)/(75+50)=0.2$, return loss $=-20\log_{10}0.2=14$ dB, VSWR $=1.5$. Fine for casual video, wrong for an RF test.

**Termination values.** Parallel: $R=Z_0$. Thevenin: $R_1\parallel R_2=Z_0$ (e.g. 100 Ω/100 Ω for 50 Ω). Series at source: $R_s=Z_0-R_{drv}$. Differential: $R_T=Z_{diff}$ across the pair (or two $Z_{diff}/2$ resistors with a cap to ground in the middle for common-mode).
:::

::: long
"The most common is 50 Ω. It's what RF equipment, scope inputs and most single-ended PCB traces use. The reason is a compromise in coax: if you only care about loss at a fixed outer diameter, the best impedance is about 77 Ω, and if you only care about power handling it's about 30 Ω. 50 Ω is roughly the middle, about 10% more loss than the best case and most of the power capability. It also makes PCB trace widths practical.

75 Ω is the other big single-ended one. Video, cable TV and digital audio use it because those links run long and loss matters, and 75 Ω is close to the 77 Ω loss minimum of an air-dielectric coax.

For differential signals, what matters is the impedance between the two wires. 100 Ω is the usual number: Ethernet over twisted pair, HDMI, DisplayPort, LVDS, SATA. USB is 90 Ω. PCIe uses about 85 Ω in many versions, which is why you can't assume 100. CAN and RS-485 buses use 120 Ω twisted pair with a 120 Ω terminator at each end. For memory, DDR traces are typically 40 to 50 Ω single-ended and the chips have selectable on-die termination.

The practical rule is that the standard is a system contract: connector, cable, PCB trace, driver and receiver all assume the same number. If the cable is 75 Ω and the scope input is 50 Ω the reflection coefficient is 0.2, which is a 14 dB return loss. And I'd take the exact target from the interface spec or the vendor's layout guide instead of memory, because tolerances differ."
:::

::: terms
- **Characteristic impedance** $Z_0$: the ratio of voltage to current for a travelling wave.
- **Differential impedance** $Z_{diff}$: the impedance between the two conductors of a pair for the differential-mode signal.
- **Return loss**: $-20\log_{10}|\Gamma|$, how far below the incident signal the reflection is.
- **VSWR**: $(1+|\Gamma|)/(1-|\Gamma|)$, voltage standing wave ratio.
- **Parallel / Thevenin / series termination**: ways to terminate a line to $Z_0$.
- **ODT (on-die termination)**: termination resistors built into the receiver or memory chip.
- **Characteristic impedance tolerance**: usually ±10% on PCBs, tighter for RF.
- **Balanced line**: a line carrying equal and opposite signals on two conductors.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **Claiming 50 Ω is a law of nature.** It's a convention chosen as a loss/power compromise. Other values are just as valid inside a closed system.
- **Mixing single-ended and differential numbers.** A 100 Ω differential pair is two roughly 50 Ω traces, not a 100 Ω trace.
- **Using 100 Ω everywhere for differential.** USB is 90 Ω and PCIe about 85 Ω. Using 100 Ω gives about 5–8% reflections, which is small but eats margin.
- **Mating 50 Ω and 75 Ω components** (cable, connector, adapter) without a matching pad.
- **Thinking a termination "sets" the line's $Z_0$.** It only matches it. The trace geometry sets $Z_0$.
- **Forgetting the driver's own output impedance.** A series termination should subtract it: with a 20 Ω driver on a 50 Ω line add about 30 Ω.
:::

::: deep
## Where the numbers come from

Standard impedances are partly physics, partly history, and mostly agreement. Three sets of constraints push the design:

1. **Loss.** Conductor loss in coax is dominated by the inner conductor, where the current density is highest.
2. **Power and voltage handling.** Smaller inner conductor means higher field at its surface, so breakdown happens sooner.
3. **Practicality.** Cable and trace sizes, connector ecosystems, and the existing installed base.

### The 50 Ω compromise

For a coax with air dielectric, $Z_0=60\ln(D/d)$. Hold the outer diameter $D$ fixed and vary the inner diameter $d$, with $x=D/d$:

- Conductor attenuation $\alpha_c\propto\frac{1}{Z_0}\left(\frac1d+\frac1D\right)\propto\frac{1+x}{\ln x}$. Minimizing gives $x=3.59$, and so $Z_0=60\ln3.59\approx77$ Ω.
- Peak power at the breakdown field $E_{max}$ at the inner conductor: $E_{max}=V/[(d/2)\ln x]$, so $V_{max}\propto d\ln x=D\ln x/x$ and $P=V^2/Z_0\propto\ln x/x^2$. Maximizing gives $x=\sqrt e=1.65$ and $Z_0=30$ Ω.

The commonly told history is that 50 Ω emerged as a compromise between these two goals and was then fixed by use (radar, military, then test equipment). I'd avoid quoting dates or specific origin stories as facts; the engineering rationale is what matters.

### 75 Ω video and 300 Ω twin-lead

75 Ω sits near the conductor-loss minimum of air-dielectric coax (77 Ω), which is what you want for long TV and video runs where power handling doesn't matter. With a solid dielectric the optimum ratio $D/d=3.59$ is unchanged but $Z_0$ scales by $1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$, so for solid polyethylene the true loss minimum is about 51 Ω and for foam dielectrics about 60 to 65 Ω. 75 Ω video cable is therefore a convention that is close to, not exactly at, the loss optimum; it also gives low capacitance per metre and is close to the roughly 73 Ω of a half-wave dipole. The 300 Ω twin-lead used for older TV antennas is a balanced line with air between wires, with low loss and a conveniently high impedance matched to folded dipole antennas (about 300 Ω).

### Differential values

A pair of uncoupled 50 Ω traces gives $Z_{diff}=2\times50=100$ Ω. Real pairs are coupled, which reduces $Z_{diff}$ (see [[q0719]]), so designers widen or narrow the traces to hit the target. 100 Ω became common for twisted pair (Cat5/6 Ethernet cabling naturally lands at 100 Ω) and for LVDS, HDMI and DisplayPort. USB chose 90 Ω; PCIe specs use about 85 Ω in many cases. CAN and RS-485 use 120 Ω because the twisted-pair cable used for industrial buses has about that impedance, and a 120 Ω resistor at each end of the bus matches it.

## What a mismatch costs

Reflection coefficient $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$:

| Mismatch | $\Gamma$ | Return loss |
|---|---|---|
| 50 Ω line into 75 Ω | 0.20 | 14 dB |
| 100 Ω pair into 90 Ω receiver | −0.053 | 25.6 dB |
| 100 Ω pair into 85 Ω receiver | −0.081 | 21.8 dB |
| 50 Ω ±10% (45 Ω or 55 Ω) | ±0.05 | 26 dB |

A $\Gamma$ of 5% on a 1 V edge is 50 mV of reflection. Fine for most digital, but eats noise margin on a low-swing LVDS link.

## Where adapters and mixed systems bite

Real setups often mix standards. A 75 Ω BNC cable into a 50 Ω scope gives the 14 dB return loss computed above. A minimum-loss pad (an L-pad) can match 75 to 50 Ω with a fixed insertion loss: the series resistor is $\sqrt{Z_h(Z_h-Z_l)}=\sqrt{75\times25}=43.3$ Ω on the 75 Ω side and the shunt resistor is $Z_lZ_h/43.3=86.6$ Ω, giving an insertion loss of $20\log_{10}\big(\sqrt{Z_h/Z_l}+\sqrt{Z_h/Z_l-1}\big)=5.7$ dB. That loss is the price of presenting a match to both sides; it is acceptable for measurement and not for signal-power-critical links.

In a PCB design, a mix shows up when one interface is 90 Ω (USB) and another 100 Ω (Ethernet) share a board: each pair needs its own width and spacing rule in the layout tool, not one global differential rule. Similarly a 50 Ω RF feed to an antenna passes through connectors and pads that must each hold 50 Ω, which is why RF footprints are validated with TDR or a VNA rather than assumed.

## How standard values turn into terminations

| Interface | Termination |
|---|---|
| 50 Ω single-ended, parallel | 50 Ω to $V_{TT}$ or ground at the receiver |
| 50 Ω, Thevenin | 100 Ω to VCC and 100 Ω to GND, giving 50 Ω with DC bias at mid-rail |
| Source series with 20 Ω driver | about 30 Ω in series at the source |
| 100 Ω differential (LVDS) | 100 Ω across the receiver inputs |
| CAN / RS-485 | 120 Ω at each end of the bus only, not at the stubs |

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If they ask "why 50 Ω?", say it out loud as a compromise between 30 Ω (power) and 77 Ω (loss). It's a tiny argument but it shows you can reason about design trade-offs and not just quote constants.
:::

::: applications
- **Oscilloscope inputs and RF test gear**: 50 Ω inputs (and 1 MΩ selectable on scopes). Mismatch shows up as ripples and ringing in measurements.
- **Video and cable TV**: 75 Ω coax (RG-6) and BNC connectors.
- **USB, HDMI, Ethernet**: PCB differential routing at 90/100 Ω, plus connector footprint tuning.
- **CAN bus**: a 120 Ω resistor at each end; measuring about 60 Ω across CANH/CANL on a powered-off bus confirms two terminators are present.
- **DDR memory**: 40–50 Ω single-ended traces and ODT settings in the controller firmware.
- **RF front ends**: 50 Ω matching networks around antennas, filters and amplifiers.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Values are conventions** for specific interfaces and may vary by generation, vendor, or board class. Check the spec.
- **Tolerances** (commonly ±10% on PCB traces, tighter in RF) mean the real impedance sits in a band.
- **Frequency**: $Z_0$ is treated as constant over the band of interest. A lossy line's $Z_0$ drifts at low frequency (see [[q0718]]).
- **Differential values** assume balanced, coupled pairs. For loosely coupled pairs, $Z_{diff}\approx2Z_0$.
- **Loss versus power** reasoning for 50 Ω uses air-dielectric coax. With a solid dielectric the optimum $D/d$ ratios are the same, but every $Z_0$ is divided by $\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$ (minimum loss at about 51 Ω for solid polyethylene), so the 30/77 Ω argument is really an air-line argument.
:::

::: followups
### Why is 75 Ω used for video instead of 50 Ω?
Video runs are long and low power, so conductor loss dominates and power handling is irrelevant. For air-like (foam or air-spaced) coax the loss minimum is near 77 Ω, so 75 Ω keeps attenuation per metre close to the minimum. (For solid-polyethylene coax the strict minimum is lower, about 51 Ω, so part of the choice is simply convention.)

### What happens if I connect a 75 Ω cable to a 50 Ω scope input?
$\Gamma=(50-75)/(50+75)=-0.2$, so 20% of the incident wave reflects with inverted polarity at the scope input. That's a 14 dB return loss. On a short cable with slow video the error is small, but with a long cable and fast edges you see a reflected step coming back from the source end and the amplitude reading is off by roughly the divider ratio of the mismatch.

### Why is the CAN bus terminated at only the two ends?
The bus is a single 120 Ω line with short stubs. Terminations at both ends absorb the wave and prevent reflections. Adding more terminators in the middle would load the bus: with two 120 Ω resistors in parallel you measure 60 Ω.

### How would you terminate a 50 Ω line driven by a driver with 20 Ω output impedance?
Series termination at the source: add $R_s=50-20=30$ Ω. The initial divider with the line gives $V\times50/(20+30+50)=V/2$, a half-amplitude wave that doubles at the open receiver. If you have a receiver with high input impedance, this uses no DC power.

### Harder: why is PCIe 85 Ω and not 100 Ω?
I'm not certain of the history. What I can say is the spec defines the target, and the lower impedance means wider or closer-coupled traces and a slightly different loss/crosstalk trade-off. In practice you follow whatever your board stackup tool and the PCIe layout guidelines specify, and you don't mix 85 and 100.

### Scenario: your 100 Ω differential board shows a slight reflection on a USB link. What do you check?
USB is 90 Ω, so the pair you designed for 100 Ω has $\Gamma\approx(100-90)/(100+90)=0.053$ against the connector. That's not enough to fail by itself, but it eats margin. Also check the connector pad region, since pad capacitance dips the impedance locally.
:::
