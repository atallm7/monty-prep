::: testing
This is the **mechanism** question: why does the voltage go up when there is no inductor? The answer the interviewer wants is series stacking of a pre-charged capacitor on top of the source, with charge conservation and energy accounting showing that current falls (input current is a multiple of output current). Stronger answers extend to N stages (Dickson) and quantify the cost, and contrast with the inductive boost in [[q0270]].
:::

::: why
"Where does the extra voltage come from?" is a classic trap: a candidate who says "the capacitor amplifies" fails the energy-conservation test. Understanding the stacking picture lets you predict ratio, input current, loss and the multi-stage behavior of on-chip high-voltage generators (flash programming, LCD drivers).
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor as a transferable battery**: a charged capacitor holds a voltage; moving its plates changes the reference. See [[q0022]].
- **Series voltages add**: KVL. See [[q0004]].
- **Charge conservation**: $\Delta Q=C\Delta V$, charge leaving one capacitor arrives at another.
- **Energy conservation**: $V_{in}I_{in}\ge V_{out}I_{out}$. See [[q0154]].
- **Diode as a one-way valve** for passive pump stages. See [[q0101]].
- **Switch phases**: see [[q0284]].
:::

::: answer
"The voltage goes up because a charged capacitor gets stacked on top of the input, like putting two batteries in series.

In phase one I charge a capacitor from the input, so it holds $V_{in}$. In phase two I disconnect it from ground and connect its negative plate to $V_{in}$. A capacitor's voltage can't change instantly, so its positive plate is now at $V_{in}+V_{in}=2V_{in}$ relative to ground. That plate is connected to the output, so the output sits at $2V_{in}$ and charge flows into the output capacitor.

The energy is paid for by current. Each cycle, the input supplies a packet of charge to charge the flying capacitor and then the same amount again while the capacitor is in series, but only one packet goes to the output. So input current is twice output current: voltage doubled, current halved, power conserved, minus losses.

For bigger multiples you chain stages. In a diode-capacitor Dickson pump, each stage lifts the node by another clock swing, so $N$ stages give about $(N+1)V_{in}$ minus diode drops and droop. Each added stage adds output resistance, which is why high multiples only work at tiny currents."
:::

::: technical
**Stacking.** After phase 1: $V_C=V_{in}$. Phase 2: bottom plate to $V_{in}$ so $V_{top}=V_{in}+V_C=2V_{in}$ (ideal).

**Charge accounting per cycle.** Output charge $Q_{out}=I_{out}/f$. Input charge in phase 1 equals $Q_{out}$ (recharging $C_{fly}$); in phase 2 the same charge passes from $V_{in}$ through $C_{fly}$ to the output. So $Q_{in}=2Q_{out}$:
$$P_{in}=V_{in}\cdot2I_{out},\quad P_{out}=2V_{in}I_{out}\;\Rightarrow\;\eta_{ideal}=100\%\ (\text{at }V_{out}=2V_{in}).$$
Under load $V_{out}=2V_{in}-I_{out}R_{out}$ and $\eta=V_{out}/(2V_{in})$.

**Dickson pump** ($N$ stages, equal capacitors $C$, clock swing $V_\phi$, diode drop $V_D$):
$$V_{out}\approx V_{in}+NV_\phi-(N+1)V_D-\frac{N\,I_{out}}{fC}$$
Example: $N=3$, $V_{in}=V_\phi=3.3$ V, $V_D=0.3$ V, $I_{out}=10$ mA, $f=500$ kHz, $C=1\ \mu$F: $13.2-1.2-0.06=11.94$ V (ideal 13.2 V).

**Rough scaling.** Droop grows with $N$; for fixed total area the optimum number of stages trades ratio against $R_{out}$.
:::

::: short
"It's series stacking. Charge a capacitor to $V_{in}$, then in the next phase put its bottom plate on $V_{in}$, so its top plate is at $2V_{in}$, and connect that to the output. Energy is conserved because the input supplies charge twice per cycle but the output only gets it once: input current is twice output current. More stages stack more clock swings, about $(N+1)V_{in}$, at the price of more droop and diode drops."
:::

::: long
"The intuition is two batteries in series. A capacitor charged to $V_{in}$ is a battery of $V_{in}$. If I put it in series with the supply, the total is $2V_{in}$.

The switches make that happen. Phase one: connect the flying capacitor across the supply. It charges to $V_{in}$. Phase two: I lift its bottom plate from ground up to $V_{in}$ and connect its top plate to the output. The capacitor's voltage can't jump, so the top plate follows the bottom up by $V_{in}$, landing at $2V_{in}$. Charge flows from that plate into the output capacitor and load, and the capacitor droops a bit.

So where's the catch? Energy. Per cycle, the input gives one packet of charge in phase one to refill the flying capacitor, and in phase two the same current path runs through the input again. The output gets only one packet. So input current is twice the output current, and power in roughly equals power out: double voltage, half current.

The loss shows up as the flying capacitor's droop, $I/(fC)$, the switch resistance, and overhead, so under load the output is $2V_{in}-I_{out}R_{out}$.

To go higher you chain stages. In a Dickson pump, a clock pushes alternate nodes up by the clock amplitude and diodes pass the charge forward, so you get about $(N+1)V_{in}$ minus a diode drop per stage and a droop of $N\,I/(fC)$. With three stages from 3.3 V that's 13.2 V ideal, about 11.9 V at 10 mA. Beyond that the output resistance becomes the problem, which is why those pumps appear on chips making programming voltages at microamps."
:::

::: terms
- **Series stacking**: placing a charged capacitor in series with a source to add voltages.
- **Flying capacitor**: the capacitor whose plates move between circuit nodes.
- **Clock swing ($V_\phi$)**: amplitude of the driving square wave in a Dickson pump.
- **Dickson multiplier**: diode-capacitor chain with alternating clock phases.
- **Charge packet**: $Q=I/f$ transferred per cycle.
- **Droop**: voltage lost per cycle, $\Delta V=Q/C$.
- **Cockcroft-Walton**: a similar multiplier driven by AC.
- **Conversion ratio**: $V_{out}/V_{in}$ fixed by topology.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "capacitors are charged in parallel and discharged in series".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains with plates: the capacitor voltage is fixed, so the reference move of the bottom plate carries the top plate up.
- Does the energy/charge accounting and gets $I_{in}=2I_{out}$.
- Gives the loaded equation $2V_{in}-I_{out}R_{out}$.
- Describes the $N$-stage extension with a formula and a number, with the penalty.
- Distinguishes charge-pump stacking from inductor flyback ([[q0270]]): no magnetic storage, fixed ratio.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The capacitor creates extra energy."** It only moves it.
- **Assuming the output is exactly $2V_{in}$** under load.
- **Ignoring diode drops in passive pumps**: three Schottky drops of 0.3 V cost 1.2 V in the Dickson example.
- **Putting caps in series for charging**: the standard form charges in parallel (across the supply) and discharges in series.
- **Forgetting that clock amplitude sets the per-stage step** in Dickson pumps.
- **Treating more stages as free**: $R_{out}$ and parasitic capacitance grow with $N$.
:::

## KVL in phase 2

$V_{out}=V_{in}+V_{C}$, where $V_C$ is the capacitor voltage at that instant. Unloaded, $V_C=V_{in}$ so $V_{out}=2V_{in}$. Loaded, $V_C$ has fallen by $\Delta V$ and the output sits there. Since $V_C$ cannot change instantly, KVL gives the output instantly at the switching edge: no current source, no inductor, no magnetic storage is involved.

## Energy and charge: nothing for free

Example: $V_{in}=3.3$ V, 20 mA out at 6.435 V.
- Output power: $6.435\times0.02=0.1287$ W.
- Input current: $2\times0.02=0.04$ A (plus overhead), so input power $3.3\times0.04=0.132$ W.
- Ideal efficiency here is $6.435/6.6=97.5\%$: the missing 2.5% is the droop and switch drops ($I^2R_{out}=0.02^2\times8.25=3.3$ mW).

A boost with $D=0.5$ also halves the current: both are voltage-for-current trades, differing in the storage element (magnetic vs electric) and the ratio flexibility (continuous vs fixed).

## Contrast with the inductive boost

| | Boost ([[q0270]]) | Charge pump |
|---|---|---|
| Storage | inductor (magnetic field) | capacitors (electric field) |
| Ratio | continuous, set by $D$ | fixed (2, 1.5, $N+1$) |
| Regulation | duty cycle | burst / linear / ratio hop |
| Output | stiff | $R_{out}$ droop |
| EMI | high $di/dt$ | edges but no magnetics |
| Current | amps | up to a few hundred mA |

## Where the energy goes: the $\tfrac12C\,\Delta V^2$ rule

The stacking story says the output is $2V_{in}$ and the efficiency is $V_{out}/(2V_{in})$. The loss mechanism is worth deriving once, because it applies to every switched-capacitor circuit. When a capacitor $C$ is connected through *any* resistance to a source that differs from its voltage by $\Delta V$, it ends up at the source voltage, and the energy dissipated in the resistance is

$$E_{loss}=\tfrac12C\,\Delta V^2,$$

independent of the resistance. (Integrate $i^2R$ with $i=(\Delta V/R)e^{-t/RC}$ and the $R$ cancels.) In steady state under load the flying capacitor reconnects twice per cycle, each time with a mismatch of $\Delta V=Q/C_{fly}=I_{out}/(fC_{fly})$. For the doubler at 50 mA, 500 kHz and 1 µF: $Q=100$ nC and $\Delta V=0.1$ V, so each event dissipates $\tfrac12(1\ \mu\text{F})(0.1\ \text{V})^2=5$ nJ, and two events per cycle at 500 kHz is $10\text{ nJ}\times500\text{ kHz}=5$ mW. That is exactly $I^2R_{SSL}=0.05^2\times2\ \Omega=5$ mW, so the output resistance in the slow-switching regime *is* this charge-sharing loss, written as a resistance. It is also why a bigger switch cannot fix it: you only reduce $\Delta V$ by increasing $C$ or $f$. This is the energy-conservation counterpart of the mechanism, and it is why a pump that mixes capacitors at very different voltages is inherently lossy.

## A Dickson chain, node by node

The Dickson formula $V_{out}\approx V_{in}+NV_\phi-(N+1)V_D$ can be built node by node. Take three stages, $V_{in}=V_\phi=3.3$ V, $V_D=0.3$ V, ideal capacitors and no load. Each node swings by $V_\phi$ because its capacitor is driven by one phase of the clock. A node settles at its *low* value when the diode behind it conducts, so it sits one diode below the previous node's *high* value:

| Node | Low value | High value | Diode that charges it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $V_{in}-V_D=3.0$ V | 6.3 V | $D_1$ from the input |
| 2 | $6.3-0.3=6.0$ V | 9.3 V | $D_2$ from node 1 (high) |
| 3 | $9.3-0.3=9.0$ V | 12.3 V | $D_3$ from node 2 (high) |
| Output | $12.3-0.3=12.0$ V | | $D_4$ from node 3 (high) |

The output is $3.3+3\times3.3-4\times0.3=12.0$ V, matching the formula, and the 12.0 V is one diode below the highest node. Two things the table reveals that the formula does not. First, the *capacitor voltages* grow along the chain: stage capacitors see about 3, 6 and 9 V, and the output capacitor sees 12 V, so later stages need higher voltage ratings and suffer more DC-bias derating than the first. Second, each stage lifts the node by a fixed clock swing, so the supply current also grows: the input must supply charge for every stage, $I_{in}=(N+1)I_{out}$, which at 10 mA out is 40 mA in. With the loaded output of 11.94 V, $\eta=11.94/(4\times3.3)=90.5\%$, the ideal $(N+1)$ multiple being the denominator. The cost of multiplying is current: ratio $N+1$ means $N+1$ times the input current.

## Parasitic capacitance: why on-chip pumps are worse than the formula

On a chip, each pumped node also has capacitance to the substrate. The clock swing seen at the node is a capacitive divider, $V_\phi\,C/(C+C_s)$, so the per-stage gain is $V_\phi C/(C+C_s)-V_D$ instead of $V_\phi-V_D$. With an illustrative 10 pF stage capacitor and 2 pF of stray capacitance, the factor is 0.833, the per-stage swing is 2.75 V rather than 3.3 V, and the three-stage no-load output falls from 12.0 V to $3.3+3\times(2.75-0.3)-0.3=10.35$ V, a 14% shortfall before any load current. This is a design trade-off: bigger stage capacitors reduce the shortfall but use die area, and bottom-plate parasitics from the integrated capacitor are why the layout orientation of the plates matters.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The stray-to-stage capacitance ratio depends on the process, the capacitor type and the layout. The 20% figure here is for illustration, not for any real technology.

## Diode drops versus switches

A diode-based chain loses $V_D$ per stage regardless of load; with 0.3 V Schottky diodes and a 3.3 V clock that is a tenth of every stage. Replacing the diodes with clocked switches (as in the doubler of [[q0284]]) removes the fixed drop and leaves only the $I R_{on}$ drop, which is small at light load. For MOS pumps the body effect raises the threshold voltage of the diode-connected devices as the node voltage rises along the chain, so later stages lose more than earlier ones. Whether the diode form or the switch form wins depends on the current: at microamp loads the fixed diode drop dominates the loss, at higher currents the switch resistance and the clock-driver current matter more.

## What you can verify on the bench

- **Node voltages stage by stage.** Probe each node with a high-impedance, low-capacitance probe: a 10 pF to 15 pF probe on a 10 pF stage node is not a small perturbation, so for on-chip pumps, probe only the accessible external nodes or compare against a simulation.
- **Ripple and droop versus current.** At a fixed clock, measure the output at two loads. The slope is $R_{out}$ and should be roughly $N\times$ the single-stage value in the slow-switching regime; a larger slope points at stray capacitance or a diode drop that rises with current.
- **Input current ratio.** Measure $I_{in}/I_{out}$: it should approach $N+1$ for a Dickson pump and 2 for a doubler, plus the clock-driver overhead. A much larger ratio means the clock driver or leakage dominates.
- **Capacitor ratings and bias.** The last stages sit near the output voltage: check the voltage rating and the effective capacitance at the working voltage, because ceramic derating there lowers $C$ and raises the droop term $NI/(fC)$.

- **Gate-drive rails** for high-side N-FETs and load switches: a pump makes $V_{in}+$ gate voltage.
- **Flash/EEPROM programming** voltages generated on-die by Dickson-style pumps.
- **Negative supplies** for sensors and audio from a single rail.
- **LED drivers** using 1x/1.5x/2x modes to keep LEDs lit as the battery falls.

::: assumptions
- Ideal switches and capacitors for the stacking argument; $R_{out}$ added afterward.
- 50% duty, non-overlapping clocks.
- Dickson formula assumes equal capacitors, clock swing equal to $V_{in}$ (or stated), negligible parasitic capacitance.
- Other topologies (series-parallel, ladder, Fibonacci) reach other ratios with fewer capacitors, which this page doesn't cover.

::: followups
### Why can't a charge pump make arbitrary voltages?
The ratio is set by how the capacitors are re-wired. Intermediate ratios need extra capacitors/states (1.5x, 3/2) or a linear regulator, which wastes the excess.

### What happens to the capacitor voltage in phase 2?
It droops by $Q/C_{fly}$ as it feeds the output. Its voltage cannot jump at the switching edge, which is why the stacked top plate lands at $2V_{in}$.

### Why do passive diode pumps lose voltage?
Each diode drops $V_D$, and there are $N+1$ of them. Three 0.3 V drops cost 1.2 V. Synchronous (switch-based) pumps replace diodes by FETs to remove it.

### Does a charge pump boost power?
No. Power out is power in minus losses. Voltage doubles and current halves.

### Scenario: you need 12 V at 5 mA from a 3.3 V rail and have no room for an inductor. Is a charge pump plausible?
Plausible, but not with three stages. Three Dickson stages give 13.2 V ideal and only about 11.97 V at 5 mA ($13.2-1.2-0.03$), just short of 12 V and with no headroom for regulation. Four stages give about 14.96 V at 5 mA ($16.5-1.5-0.04$), and a small regulator or a feedback-controlled clock trims it to 12 V. Check the stage capacitors' DC-bias derating (the last stages sit near 12 to 15 V) and the input current, about $(N+1)I_{out}=5\times5$ mA = 25 mA plus clock-driver overhead.
