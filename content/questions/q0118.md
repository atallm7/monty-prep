::: basics
### In plain words
A fast slew rate means the voltage changes quickly, and a slow one means it changes gently. Fast edges give clean timing and less loss in power switches. Slow edges are quieter and cause less electrical noise. Most designs pick the slowest edge that still meets timing.

### Picture it
Think of a car stopping at a light. A hard stop gets you there sooner but throws the passengers forward and screeches the tires. A gentle stop is smooth but takes more road. Fast electrical edges are the hard stop: quick, but with jolts such as ringing, spikes and radiated noise.

### Words you need
- **Slew rate**: how fast a voltage changes, in V/ns or V/µs. See [[q0117]].
- **EMI**: electromagnetic interference, unwanted radio-frequency noise.
- **Ringing and overshoot**: oscillation and spikes after a fast edge.
- **Ground bounce**: a brief ground voltage shift caused by a quickly changing current.
- **Crosstalk**: a signal leaking into a neighboring trace.

### The simplest example
Say an output drives a 10 pF load (a small capacitance that must be charged) with a 3.3 V swing. If the edge takes 1 ns, the slew rate is 3.3 V/ns and the charging current is $C\,dV/dt=10\text{ pF}\times3.3\text{ V/ns}=33$ mA. If the edge takes 10 ns, the current is 3.3 mA. The fast edge gives 10 times the current spike, and with it more noise and ringing. The slow edge costs timing margin.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page lists the benefits and costs of each, and how to choose with a series resistor or a drive-strength setting.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can argue **both sides** and pick based on the application, rather than saying "faster is better". A good answer lists the benefits and costs in a structured way (timing, signal integrity, EMI, power/loss, noise, cost) and then uses a rule of thumb: **make the edge only as fast as the system needs**. The interviewer is also listening for numbers (spectral knee $\approx1/(\pi t_r)$, switching loss $\propto t_r$, bounce $\propto di/dt$) and for the contexts: digital I/O, power switches, and amplifiers.
:::

::: why
Edge rate is the single knob that trades speed for noise. Chip vendors give selectable output slew for exactly this reason, power-supply designers adjust gate resistors to trade efficiency against EMI, and every EMC test failure at a high-frequency harmonic gets partly fixed by slowing an edge. Knowing the pros and cons lets you pick sensible defaults and diagnose problems from the symptoms.
:::

::: concepts
- **Edge rate and spectrum**: a trapezoid's harmonics roll off beyond about $1/(\pi t_r)$; faster edges extend high-frequency content. See [[q0117]].
- **$di/dt$ and $dv/dt$**: inductive and capacitive coupling scale with them. See [[q0022]].
- **Transmission-line effects**: edges shorter than a few times the line delay need termination.
- **Switching loss**: in a power switch the overlap of $V$ and $I$ during the edge scales with $t_r$.
- **Timing budget**: edges consume part of the bit period.
- **Drive strength / slew control**: how edge rate is adjusted. See [[q0111]].
:::

::: answer
"Fast slew rates buy you speed and cleaner timing. Edges that traverse the logic threshold quickly leave more of the bit period for setup and hold, there's less jitter and delay variation caused by noise on a slow edge, and in a power switch a fast edge spends less time with high voltage and high current overlapping, so switching loss is lower. In an amplifier a fast slew rate means less distortion and a wider full-power bandwidth.

The cost is everything that grows with $dV/dt$ and $dI/dt$. A faster edge has more high-frequency content, so more EMI and higher harmonics where cables and traces radiate well. It causes larger current spikes and ground bounce, since $L\,di/dt$ goes up. It increases crosstalk and makes ringing and overshoot worse because it excites the parasitic L and C. It needs termination on shorter traces, since reflections matter once the edge is shorter than twice the line delay. And it may stress the part: overshoot above the supply, or $dv/dt$ turning on a neighboring MOSFET.

Slow edges are the mirror image: quiet and EMI-friendly, but they eat timing margin, increase switching loss in power stages, can create slow-edge sensitivity to noise and cause a lot of short-circuit current in the next stage. So I'd choose the slowest edge that meets timing, tune with a series resistor or drive-strength setting, and verify on the scope and in EMC."
:::

::: technical
**Rule-of-thumb relations**
- Spectral knee frequency: $f_{knee}\approx\dfrac{1}{\pi t_r}$ (about $\dfrac{0.35}{t_r}$ is the $-3$ dB bandwidth needed to pass the edge). A 1 ns edge: 318 MHz knee; 10 ns: 32 MHz. Harmonic amplitude falls 20 dB/decade above the first corner ($1/\pi\tau_{pulse}$) and 40 dB/decade above the knee.
- Capacitive coupling current: $i=C_m\,dV/dt$; inductive coupled voltage $v=M\,di/dt$. Both scale as $1/t_r$ (crosstalk) and the supply-bounce voltage scales roughly as $1/t_r^2$ for fixed $L$ because the current *and* its rate both scale: $V\approx L\,di/dt\propto L\,(C\Delta V/t_r)/t_r$.
- Transmission lines: reflections matter when $t_r\lesssim2t_d$. On FR-4 with about 170 ps/inch, a 1 ns edge makes lines longer than about 3 in "long"; a 10 ns edge only above about 29 in.
- Power switch loss: $P_{sw}\approx\tfrac12V I(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}$. For 12 V, 5 A, 500 kHz: 5 ns edges give 0.15 W; 20 ns gives 0.6 W; 100 ns gives 3 W.
- Amplifier: slew-limited distortion if $2\pi fV_p>SR$ ([[q0117]]).

**Numbers: ground bounce.** Eight outputs, 15 pF each, 3.3 V, 2 nH of shared inductance. Charge current about $NC\Delta V/t_r=0.40$ A for $t_r=1$ ns; ramping this in about $t_r/2$ gives $V\approx L\,\Delta I/(t_r/2)\approx1.6$ V (order of magnitude, a large fraction of the supply). At $t_r=4$ ns: about 0.10 V: a 16x reduction for a 4x slower edge.

**Summary table.**

| Property | Fast slew | Slow slew |
|---|---|---|
| Timing margin / max data rate | better | worse |
| Power-switch loss | lower | higher |
| Jitter from noise on edge | lower | higher |
| EMI / harmonics | worse | better |
| Ground bounce, supply droop | worse | better |
| Crosstalk | worse | better |
| Ringing / overshoot | worse | better |
| Termination needs | more | less |
| Amplifier distortion at high $f$/amplitude | lower | higher |
| Power in driver (current spikes) | higher peak | lower peak |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The bounce and crosstalk figures are order-of-magnitude estimates: real values depend on package inductance, return paths and routing. Use them to compare options, not as predictions.
:::

::: short
"Fast slew rates give better timing margin, less jitter, lower switching loss in power stages and less distortion in amplifiers. The cost is more high-frequency content, so more EMI, ground bounce, crosstalk, ringing and overshoot, and you start to need termination. Slow slew rates are quiet and easy on EMC but eat timing margin and raise switching loss. So I choose the slowest edge that meets timing and tune with drive strength or a series resistor."
:::

::: long
"I think about it as speed versus noise, and the right answer depends on the application.

The benefit of fast edges is time. In a digital link the edge uses up part of the bit period; a 1 ns edge on a 10 ns bit leaves most of the window clean, while a 5 ns edge eats half of it and noise on a slow edge turns into more timing jitter. In a power converter a fast edge means the switch spends less time with voltage and current overlapping, so the switching loss, which goes as half $V\times I\times(t_r+t_f)\times f$, is lower: with 12 V and 5 A at 500 kHz, 5 ns edges lose about 0.15 W versus 3 W with 100 ns edges. In an amplifier, a high slew rate means large high-frequency signals aren't distorted into triangles.

The price is all the things that grow with dV/dt and dI/dt. The spectrum extends to about $1/(\pi t_r)$, 318 MHz for 1 ns, where traces and cables are great antennas, so EMI harmonics go up. Fast current edges across package and board inductance cause ground bounce and supply droop. Crosstalk to neighbours scales with dV/dt. Fast edges excite parasitic L and C, giving ringing and overshoot that can overstress inputs or turn on a neighbouring MOSFET through its Miller capacitance. And once the edge is shorter than about twice the line delay, you must terminate. For a 1 ns edge on FR-4 that's anything over about 3 inches.

Slow edges reverse every item: quiet and easy for EMC, but you give up timing, you lose efficiency in switches, and slow edges linger in the input's linear region, which increases short-circuit current in the receiver and makes it more sensitive to noise.

So in practice: choose the slowest edge that meets timing, use drive-strength or slew-rate control registers, a series resistor, a gate resistor in power stages, and then test on the scope and in EMC."
:::

::: terms
- **Slew rate / edge rate**: how fast a signal changes, $dV/dt$.
- **Knee frequency**: the frequency above which the edge's spectrum rolls off faster, about $1/(\pi t_r)$.
- **Ground bounce**: transient shift in the local ground/supply due to $L\,di/dt$ from simultaneous switching.
- **Crosstalk**: unwanted coupling to neighbouring signals through mutual capacitance and inductance.
- **Ringing / overshoot**: oscillation at an edge from parasitic L and C.
- **Switching loss**: energy dissipated in a power switch during the voltage/current overlap of an edge.
- **Termination**: matching resistor to suppress reflections.
- **Jitter**: timing uncertainty at the threshold crossing.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer lists "fast: speed; slow: less EMI". An exceptional answer:

- Organizes by **context** (digital I/O, power switching, amplifiers) because the trade-off differs, and says that the *same* knob appears in all three.
- Gives **quantitative** rules: knee frequency $1/(\pi t_r)$, $P_{sw}\propto t_r$, bounce $\propto1/t_r^2$, critical line length $\approx t_r/(2\times$ delay per inch$)$.
- Notes the **less obvious costs of fast edges** (turning on a neighbouring FET via Miller coupling, overshoot beyond ratings) and **less obvious costs of slow edges** (short-circuit current in receivers, noise sensitivity and jitter).
- Describes the **engineering approach**: choose the slowest edge that meets timing, tune with series R/gate R/drive strength, verify with a scope and EMC pre-scan.
- Mentions mitigation of fast edges (termination, decoupling, spread spectrum, filtering).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Faster is always better."** Speed beyond what timing needs costs EMI, bounce and parts.
- **Treating EMI as a function of clock frequency only.** Radiation depends strongly on edge rate: a 10 MHz clock with 0.5 ns edges has about as much 500 MHz content as a 100 MHz clock with 5 ns edges (their harmonic envelopes there are within about 2 dB), despite running 10x slower.
- **Believing slow edges are free.** They hurt timing margins, increase switching loss, can cause multiple threshold crossings with noise and increase receiver shoot-through current.
- **Slowing the edge at the wrong place.** A series resistor at the load end doesn't help; put it at the source.
- **Ignoring the power-switch side of the trade-off.** Slowing gate edges to cure EMI raises MOSFET switching loss and heat.
- **Forgetting amplifier slew distortion** because "bandwidth is high enough" ([[q0117]]).
:::

::: deep
## What an edge does to the spectrum

A trapezoidal pulse with amplitude $A$, width $\tau$, and rise time $t_r$ has an envelope with two break points: flat out to $1/(\pi\tau)$, falling at 20 dB/decade up to $1/(\pi t_r)$, then 40 dB/decade beyond. Slowing the edge moves that second corner down and cuts high-frequency harmonics dramatically.

## Digital I/O

**Pros of fast edges.** Timing margin: with a 100 MHz bus (10 ns period) a 1 ns edge uses 10% of the period, a 5 ns edge uses 50%. Noise-induced jitter: the time error from noise $v_n$ on an edge is $v_n/(dV/dt)$, so 50 mV of noise on a 3.3 V/1 ns edge gives about 15 ps but on a 3.3 V/5 ns edge about 76 ps.

**Cons of fast edges.** Ground bounce, crosstalk, ringing, EMI, termination.

**Transmission lines.** A line is "electrically long" if the edge is shorter than about twice the one-way delay: $l_{crit}\approx t_r/(2t_{pd})$. With $t_{pd}\approx170$ ps/in: 1 ns gives about 2.9 in, 4 ns about 12 in, 10 ns about 29 in. Fast edges on long lines need series or parallel termination ([[q0113]] for the lumped-RC case).

**Slow-edge dangers.** The input of a CMOS receiver spends longer near its threshold; noise can cause multiple transitions (chatter), and both its transistors conduct longer (short-circuit current, [[q0110]]). Some standards specify a *maximum* rise time for this reason, and Schmitt-trigger inputs are used for slow signals.

## Power switching

For a hard-switched MOSFET in a converter at 12 V, 5 A, 500 kHz with equal rise and fall times $t$:

| Edge time | $P_{sw}=\tfrac12VI(2t)f$ | $dv/dt$ (12 V swing) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 ns | 0.15 W | 2.4 V/ns |
| 20 ns | 0.60 W | 0.6 V/ns |
| 100 ns | 3.0 W | 0.12 V/ns |

Slower edges reduce ringing, EMI, and the spurious turn-on through $C_{gd}$ ([[q0102]]), but the loss grows linearly. The designer chooses the gate resistor to balance efficiency and EMI: start with a fast edge, add resistance until the EMI and overshoot limits are met, and filter or snub rather than slow down further.

## Amplifiers

- **Fast slew**: handles large high-frequency signals with low distortion (full-power bandwidth $SR/(2\pi V_p)$), needs more internal bias current (power), can be less stable with capacitive loads and noisier (wider noise bandwidth).
- **Slow slew**: low power and cheap, enough for audio and sensors at small amplitude, but distorts large fast signals (turns sine into triangle, [[q0117]]).

## The engineering process

1. Determine the required timing (bit period, setup/hold, switching loss budget).
2. Choose the slowest edge that meets it, with margin for process/temperature spread.
3. Provide control: series resistor, drive-strength register, gate resistor, or a slew-limited driver (RS-485/CAN transceivers have slew control for long cables).
4. Verify on the scope with a good probe (a poor ground lead creates false ringing) and with a pre-compliance EMC scan.
5. If EMI is still a problem: reduce $di/dt$ loop areas, add decoupling, filter, spread-spectrum clocking.

## Real hardware

- **MCU GPIO**: slew-rate bits (e.g. "slow", "medium", "fast", "very fast") that change edge times by several times.
- **RS-485 transceivers**: slew-limited versions for long cables at 250 kbps versus fast ones for 20 Mbps.
- **Class-D amplifiers and motor drivers**: gate resistors and slew control chosen to meet EMI limits at the cost of efficiency.
- **Audio op-amps**: slew rates of a few V/µs are enough for 20 kHz at several volts.

## Worked example: picking an edge rate for a 20 MHz SPI clock

Let us make "slowest edge that meets timing" concrete. A 20 MHz SPI clock has a 50 ns period. A common budget is to let each edge consume no more than about 10% of the period, so $t_r\le5$ ns. The pin drives a trace plus input of $C_L=20$ pF, and the driver's output resistance is about 25 Ω (assumed; use your part's datasheet value).

1. Total series resistance for a 10-90% edge of 5 ns: $R=t_r/(2.2C_L)=5\text{ ns}/(2.2\times20\text{ pF})=114\ \Omega$.
2. Subtract the driver: $114-25\approx89\ \Omega$, so a standard 91 Ω series resistor at the source gives $t_r=2.2\times116\ \Omega\times20\text{ pF}=5.1$ ns.
3. The knee drops from $1/(\pi\cdot1\text{ ns})=318$ MHz (a fast pin with no resistor) to $1/(\pi\cdot5.1\text{ ns})=62$ MHz.
4. The 15th harmonic at 300 MHz sits above the new knee, so its envelope falls by $1/(\pi\cdot300\text{ MHz}\cdot5\text{ ns})=0.21$, about 13.5 dB, relative to the unresistored case. If your radiated-emissions scan fails near 300 MHz, that is a free margin gain.

The cost is timing: each 25 ns half-cycle at 20 MHz still has about 20 ns of settled level after a 5 ns edge, so the bus works, but at 100 MHz the same resistor would leave almost nothing. That is why slew control is a per-bus decision, not a global one.

## Limiting cases of the trade-off

- **Edge slower than the bit.** If $t_r$ approaches the bit period the signal never reaches the logic levels, inter-symbol interference closes the eye, and you are in the regime where a faster edge is not a choice but a requirement.
- **Edge faster than the board can use.** If the receiver, the package or the scope cannot pass the content above $0.35/t_r$, extra speed buys nothing and still radiates. A 100 ps edge into a trace with 1 GHz of usable bandwidth is just EMI.
- **Edge shape, not just edge time.** A series resistor makes an RC edge: steepest at the start, with a slow exponential tail that adds timing uncertainty near the upper threshold. A current-limited driver gives a linear ramp. Both have an abrupt change of slope (the RC edge at its start, the ramp at both ends), so both roll off at 40 dB/decade above the knee; for the same 10-90% time the ramp's envelope is actually slightly lower ($0.8/\pi\approx0.25$ vs $2.2/2\pi\approx0.35$, times $1/(f\,t_r)$). Edges with no slope discontinuity (S-shaped, raised-cosine-like) roll off faster still, one reason slew-controlled transceivers shape their edges rather than just slowing them.
- **Power stages with two-step drive.** Some gate drivers switch quickly through the plateau region where the loss occurs but slow only the part of the edge responsible for ringing, so the loss and EMI trade-off is better than a single resistor allows.

## Verifying edge rate on the bench

Measuring an edge is itself a rise-time problem. The scope and probe add their own rise time in quadrature, $t_{meas}=\sqrt{t_{sig}^2+t_{scope}^2}$, with $t_{scope}\approx0.35/BW$. For the 5.1 ns SPI edge, a 200 MHz scope ($t_{scope}=1.75$ ns) reads 5.4 ns, a 6% error, which is acceptable. For a 1 ns edge the same scope reads 2.0 ns, twice the truth; you need 1 GHz ($t_{scope}=0.35$ ns, reading 1.06 ns) to be within 6%. Rule of thumb: pick a scope whose rise time is at most one-third of the edge you are measuring.

Probing matters just as much. A passive probe's ground lead of a few centimeters has tens of nanohenries (a common rule of thumb is about 1 nH per millimeter), which rings with the probe tip capacitance and invents overshoot that is not on the board. Use the spring-tip ground adapter, measure at the receiver rather than the driver when you care about what the receiver sees, and compare edge times for a few pin settings. To confirm that slowing the edge helped emissions, use a near-field probe and a spectrum analyzer before and after: the amplitude at harmonics above the new knee should drop at the rate the envelope predicts. If it does not, the radiation is coming from a different loop or cable and the edge was not the cause.

::: applications
- **GPIO and bus drivers**: selecting output slew or drive strength per pin to meet timing and EMC.
- **Switch-mode power supplies**: gate resistor and snubber tuning to balance efficiency and EMI.
- **Long-cable interfaces (RS-485, CAN, motor encoder lines)**: slew-limited transceivers.
- **Clock distribution**: edges just fast enough for the downstream flip-flops, with spread spectrum.
- **High-speed ADC/DAC and amplifier chains**: choosing slew rate for the full-power bandwidth needed.
- **EMC debugging**: slowing a few critical edges to remove harmonics from a failing spectrum.

::: followups
### Why does slowing the edge reduce EMI if the clock frequency is unchanged?
The harmonic amplitudes above $1/(\pi t_r)$ fall at 40 dB/decade, and the knee moves down in proportion to $1/t_r$. A 10x slower edge cuts content around the old knee by 20 dB or more, while the fundamental and low harmonics remain. EMC limits are set in the 30 MHz-1 GHz band, where the high harmonics matter.

### What's the downside of slowing a gate drive edge in a power converter?
More overlap of voltage and current, so higher switching loss and heat: loss scales linearly with the transition time. For 12 V, 5 A, 500 kHz it goes from 0.15 W at 5 ns to 3 W at 100 ns. It can also lengthen dead time requirements.

### How do you quickly estimate whether a trace needs termination?
Compare the edge time with twice the one-way delay: unterminated reflections matter when $t_r<2t_d$. With 170 ps/in and a 1 ns edge, $l>1\text{ ns}/(2\times0.17\text{ ns/in})\approx2.9$ in. Shorter than that, lumped analysis is fine.

### Fast edges on one pin make another pin glitch. What's the mechanism and fix?
Crosstalk (capacitive $C_m\,dV/dt$ and inductive $M\,di/dt$) or shared ground/supply bounce. Fix by slowing the aggressor, increasing spacing, adding a ground return, and decoupling. Check using the ground-bounce simulation: more simultaneously switching outputs or faster edges increase it.

### Harder: why can a slow edge increase power consumption in a CMOS receiver?
During a slow transition the input spends time between the thresholds where both NMOS and PMOS conduct, giving short-circuit current (about linear in the transition time, 11 µW at 1 ns to 229 µW at 20 ns in the example of [[q0110]]). Also noise on a slow edge can cause repeated threshold crossings.

### Scenario: your product fails radiated emissions at 420 MHz. You can slow one 8 MHz clock edge or add a filter. How do you decide?
Identify the source with a near-field probe. A 0.5 ns edge has a knee near 640 MHz, so at 420 MHz its envelope is still on the 20 dB/decade segment set by the pulse width and the edge has not yet helped; slowing it to 2 ns moves the knee to 160 MHz and cuts the envelope at 420 MHz by $1/(\pi\cdot420\text{ MHz}\cdot2\text{ ns})=0.38$, about 8 dB. The improvement grows with frequency up to the old 640 MHz knee and then levels off at the rise-time ratio, $20\log_{10}4=12$ dB, so this is a modest fix: confirm the 8 dB actually covers the margin you are missing. Check timing margin is still adequate (2 ns on a 125 ns period is fine). If the source is not a clock but a data bus, slow only the noisiest pins, and add a series resistor at the driver.
