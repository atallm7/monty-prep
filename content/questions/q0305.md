::: testing
Whether you know the four numbers every LiPo engineer carries in their head (4.2 V full, about 3.7 V nominal, about 3.0 V empty, about 3.8 V storage) **and** can say why those limits exist instead of reciting them. The "why" is electrochemistry: cell voltage is the difference between two electrode potentials, and going outside the window destroys one of the electrodes or the electrolyte. Strong candidates also separate *open-circuit* voltage from *terminal* voltage under load, and know that a series pack just multiplies the numbers (a 3S is 12.6 V full).
:::

::: why
Almost every battery product you will touch has a charger, a fuel gauge or a protection circuit whose thresholds come straight from these voltages. Set the charge limit 100 mV too high and you get swelling and fire. Set the cutoff too low and you slowly kill the cell. In an interview this question is a quick filter: people who have only read a spec sheet quote "3.7 V" and stop, people who have built a battery product tell you about 4.2 V, the cutoff under load, and what happens outside the window.
:::

::: concepts
- **Cell voltage = cathode potential minus anode potential.** Each electrode's potential depends on how much lithium it holds, so voltage tracks state of charge. See [[q0290]].
- **Open-circuit voltage (OCV) vs terminal voltage.** OCV is the rested value; under load the terminal voltage sags by $I R_{int}$ (and a polarization term). See [[q0303]].
- **Voltage window.** The safe operating range is bounded above by electrolyte and cathode stability and below by anode current-collector stability.
- **Nominal voltage.** A convention: roughly the average voltage over a discharge. It is a label for energy math ($Wh = Ah \times V_{nom}$), not a voltage you will measure at a given moment.
- **Series and parallel packs.** Series cells add voltage (1S, 2S, 3S...), parallel cells add capacity. Each series cell must stay inside its own window ([[q0314]]).
:::

::: answer
"A single LiPo cell is about 3.7 volts nominal, but that's just an average label. The real range is 4.2 volts when it's fully charged down to roughly 3.0 volts when it's empty. In practice I'd treat 4.20 V as a hard ceiling, within about 50 millivolts, and I'd stop discharging around 3.0 to 3.3 volts depending on the load, because the voltage sags under current.

The reason for the limits is chemistry. The cell voltage is the cathode potential minus the anode potential. At the top, if you push past about 4.2 volts you start pulling too much lithium out of the cathode and you begin oxidizing the electrolyte. That makes gas, which is why pouches swell, and it can plate metallic lithium on the anode, which is how you get internal shorts. At the bottom, if you drain it below about 2.5 to 3 volts, the anode potential rises far enough that the copper current collector can start dissolving, and then it plates back as copper dendrites on the next charge.

So the useful window is about 3.0 to 4.2 volts, and for long life you avoid living at either extreme. For storage it's about 3.8 volts, and for packs you just multiply: a 3S is 12.6 V full and 11.1 V nominal."
:::

::: technical
**Numbers (single cell, graphite anode with LCO/NMC-type cathode):**

| Quantity | Typical value | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Full-charge (CV set-point) | 4.20 V ± ~0.05 V | Some "high-voltage" cells are specified to 4.35 V or 4.4 V. Always use the datasheet. |
| Nominal | 3.6 to 3.7 V | Average over a low-rate discharge. |
| Discharge cutoff | 2.75 to 3.0 V per datasheet | Hobby practice is 3.2 to 3.3 V under load so the *rested* voltage stays above about 3.5 V. |
| Absolute minimum | about 2.5 V | Below this, copper dissolution begins. Protection ICs typically trip in the 2.3 to 2.8 V region. |
| Storage | 3.80 to 3.85 V | About 50 to 60 % SoC. See [[q0324]]. |
| Overcharge protection trip | about 4.25 to 4.35 V | Backup layer, not an operating limit. |

**Why 4.2 V.** The cell voltage is $V_{cell} = E_{cathode} - E_{anode}$, both versus Li/Li$^+$. A fully lithiated graphite anode sits at about 0.1 V and a delithiated LCO or NMC cathode at about 4.3 V, so the full cell is about 4.2 V. Pushing further needs the cathode above roughly 4.3 to 4.4 V versus Li/Li$^+$, where the carbonate electrolyte oxidizes and LCO undergoes structural change and cobalt dissolution. Meanwhile the anode is saturated, so extra charge current plates lithium metal on its surface.

**Why about 3.0 V.** As the anode runs out of lithium, its potential climbs. At about 3.1 to 3.5 V versus Li/Li$^+$ the copper foil dissolves. Cell voltage when this happens is roughly 2.5 V. You also have almost no energy left: the OCV curve falls off a cliff below 10 % SoC ([[q0316]]).

**Terminal vs open-circuit.** Under load, $V_t = V_{OCV}(SoC) - I R_{int}$ (plus a slower polarization term). A cell at 10 % SoC with $V_{OCV} = 3.5$ V and $R_{int} = 100$ mΩ delivering 3 A reads $3.5 - 0.3 = 3.2$ V. That is why a 3.2 V cutoff "under load" corresponds to a healthy rested voltage.

**Series packs.** $V_{pack} = N \times V_{cell}$. A 3S pack: 12.6 V full, 11.1 V nominal, 9.0 V at 3.0 V/cell. A 3S charger must regulate 12.6 V total, but without balancing each cell can drift ([[q0314]]).

**Energy.** $E \approx Q \times V_{nom}$. A 2 Ah cell stores about $2 \times 3.7 = 7.4$ Wh.
:::

::: long
"The headline number is 3.7 volts nominal, but that's an average. What actually matters is the window: 4.2 volts is full, and around 3.0 volts is empty. I'd also say that the 4.2 has a tight tolerance, about 50 millivolts, whereas the low end is softer and depends on the load.

The reason for the window is electrochemistry. The cell voltage is the difference between the cathode and anode potentials. When the cell is full, the graphite anode is loaded with lithium and sits near zero volts versus lithium metal, and the cathode, which has given up most of its lithium, sits around 4.3 volts. If I charge beyond that, two bad things happen. The cathode becomes unstable and starts oxidizing the electrolyte, producing gas, so the pouch swells. And since the anode is already full, extra lithium plates as metal on its surface, which can grow dendrites and short the cell.

At the empty end, the anode runs low on lithium and its potential rises. Around 2.5 to 3 volts of cell voltage it hits the point where the copper current collector dissolves. That copper comes back as dendrites on the next charge. So I'd never let a cell sit below about 3 volts, and I'd use something like 3.2 under load so the rested voltage is still reasonable.

Two practical numbers to add: storage voltage is about 3.8 volts per cell, and series packs scale linearly, so a 3S is 12.6 volts full and 11.1 nominal. And remember the terminal voltage sags under current, so you can't read state of charge from voltage while a heavy load is on."
:::

::: terms
- **Nominal voltage**: the labelled average voltage (3.6 to 3.7 V for LiPo/Li-ion); used for energy calculations, not a setpoint.
- **Charge voltage / float limit**: 4.20 V per cell, the constant-voltage setpoint of the charger.
- **Cutoff voltage (end-of-discharge)**: the lowest voltage at which the load is disconnected; 2.75 to 3.0 V per datasheet, higher in practice.
- **OCV (open-circuit voltage)**: rested cell voltage with no current, a function of SoC.
- **Terminal voltage**: the voltage at the pins, $V_{OCV} - I R_{int}$ under discharge.
- **Voltage window**: the range between cutoff and charge voltage within which the cell is safe.
- **Plating**: deposition of metallic lithium on the anode surface, usually from overcharge, cold charging or very fast charging.
- **S/P notation**: "3S2P" means three cells in series, two in parallel.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "3.7 V nominal, 4.2 V max, 3.0 V min" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Distinguishes nominal, full, empty, storage and absolute-minimum voltages, and gives each a number.
- Explains the limits by electrochemistry: cathode and electrolyte stability on top, copper dissolution on the bottom, and lithium plating when overcharged.
- Notes that the 4.2 V ceiling is tight (±50 mV) while the bottom is a soft limit that depends on current, because of $IR$ drop.
- Mentions that high-voltage cells (4.35 V, 4.4 V) exist, so you always read the cell's datasheet rather than assume.
- Extends to packs ($N \times$) and says why per-cell monitoring matters.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"A LiPo is always 3.7 V."** That is the nominal label. A fresh-off-charger cell is 4.2 V and a nearly empty one is near 3.3 V. If your 3.3 V regulator assumes 3.7 V, it will brown out.
- **Treating 4.2 V as a soft guideline.** An overcharge of 100 to 200 mV costs life quickly and begins to risk safety. The charger's CV accuracy is typically specified at about ±0.5 % to ±1 %.
- **Judging empty by terminal voltage under load.** A cell reading 3.2 V at 20 A may be 40 % full once it rests. Conversely, a rested 3.0 V cell is basically dead.
- **Discharging "to zero".** Unlike lead-acid, a Li cell below about 2.5 V is permanently damaged and may be unsafe to recharge.
- **Assuming a series pack is one big cell.** The pack voltage can be perfectly normal while one cell is already over 4.2 V ([[q0314]]).
:::

::: deep
## Where the 4.2 V comes from

A lithium-ion cell stores energy as lithium sitting at two different "heights" (chemical potentials). While charging, lithium ions leave the cathode, cross the electrolyte, and tuck themselves between graphite layers in the anode. On discharge they go back.

The cell voltage is just the potential difference between the two electrodes, both referenced to lithium metal:

$$V_{cell} = E_{cathode}(x) - E_{anode}(y)$$

where $x$ and $y$ describe how much lithium each electrode holds. As the cell charges, $E_{cathode}$ rises (it is losing lithium) and $E_{anode}$ falls toward about 0.1 V (it is gaining lithium), so the difference grows. That is why the open-circuit voltage climbs monotonically with state of charge.

Graphite goes through several "stages" as it fills, which produces the fairly flat middle of the curve. The cathode curve is sloped, so the combined curve slopes gently through the middle and steepens at the ends.

## Why you cannot go above 4.2 V

Past the upper limit, three things start to go wrong at once:

1. **Cathode instability.** In LCO, removing more than roughly half the lithium shifts the crystal structure and releases oxygen at high temperature. Cobalt and nickel dissolve into the electrolyte. This is permanent capacity loss and, in the extreme, fuel for thermal runaway.
2. **Electrolyte oxidation.** Carbonate solvents decompose above about 4.3 to 4.5 V versus lithium at the cathode surface, producing CO$_2$, CO and other gases. In a pouch cell, that is the swelling you see after an overcharge.
3. **Lithium plating.** The graphite can only hold so much lithium. If current keeps coming, the surplus lithium deposits as metal on the anode. Metal plating is not fully reversible, and it grows needles that can pierce the separator.

Because the cell voltage drifts upward very gently near the top ($d V/ d SoC \approx 1$ V per unit SoC), the charger needs a tight voltage reference. A 1 % error on 4.2 V is 42 mV, which is a few percent of SoC but also meaningful degradation over many cycles. That is why single-cell charger ICs specify regulation accuracy around ±0.5 % to ±1 %.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 4.2 V limit applies to the common graphite/LCO and graphite/NMC cells that "LiPo" almost always means. Silicon-doped anodes and high-voltage cathode chemistries are specified at 4.3 to 4.45 V, and LiFePO$_4$ cells are 3.6 to 3.65 V full with a 3.2 V nominal. Use the datasheet of the exact cell.

## Why you cannot go below about 3.0 V

At the bottom the problem is the anode, not the cathode. When the graphite is almost empty, its potential versus lithium shoots up. If it climbs beyond roughly 3 V versus Li/Li$^+$, the copper foil that carries the anode current begins to dissolve into the electrolyte. The cell voltage at that moment is around 2.5 V. When you recharge, the dissolved copper ions plate out on the anode as dendrites. Repeat that and you build a short circuit path. This is why a cell that has been over-discharged is treated as damaged and is not recharged by quality chargers ([[q0330]]).

There is also a practical reason to stop early: the voltage curve is falling so fast below 3.3 V under load that there is almost no extra energy to gain. Let us put numbers on it using the model in the simulation below. A 2 Ah cell at 1C (2 A) delivers about 1.95 Ah to a 3.0 V cutoff. Cutting off at 3.25 V loses only about 0.1 Ah (5 %) but keeps the cell away from the damaging region.

## Terminal voltage under load

The OCV is the voltage you see after the cell rests. Under load the terminal voltage is lower:

$$V_t = V_{OCV}(SoC) - I R_0 - V_{RC}$$

$R_0$ is the instantaneous ohmic resistance (electrolyte, contacts, foils) and $V_{RC}$ is a slower polarization term (charge transfer and diffusion, time constant tens of seconds). For a 2 Ah cell with $R_0 = 50$ mΩ at 1C (2 A), the instantaneous step is $2 \times 0.05 = 0.1$ V. At 5C it is 0.5 V. The same cell at the same SoC will read very different voltages depending on current, which is why you should state both when you quote a voltage.

## Nominal voltage and energy

Because the average voltage over a discharge is about 3.6 to 3.7 V, energy is $E = Q \times V_{nom}$. Our 2 Ah cell is $2 \times 3.7 = 7.4$ Wh. The simulator reports 7.46 Wh at C/5 and 7.08 Wh at 1C, because more energy is lost to $I^2R$ heat at higher rates. This is why "nominal" is fine for first-order sizing but not for precise runtime ([[q0311]]).

## Packs

For N cells in series:

| Pack | Full | Nominal | Empty (3.0 V/cell) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1S | 4.2 V | 3.7 V | 3.0 V |
| 2S | 8.4 V | 7.4 V | 6.0 V |
| 3S | 12.6 V | 11.1 V | 9.0 V |
| 4S | 16.8 V | 14.8 V | 12.0 V |

The pack voltage hides cell-to-cell differences. A 3S pack reading 11.5 V could be three cells at 3.83 V or one at 4.25 V and two at 3.62 V. The charger sees only the sum, which is why multi-cell packs need a balance connector and per-cell monitoring.

## What this means for circuit design

- A "3.3 V rail from a 1S LiPo" needs a buck-boost or a boost, or you accept a drop-out around 3.5 V and lose the last chunk of capacity ([[q0323]]).
- Your ADC divider for pack voltage must be scaled for 4.2 V per cell plus margin, not for 3.7 V.
- A fuel gauge based on voltage alone is unreliable in the flat middle of the curve ([[q0308]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Smartphone and laptop batteries**: single-cell or multi-cell pack with 4.2 V (or 4.35 V/4.4 V high-voltage) charge limits enforced by the charger IC and a second protection layer.
- **RC and drone packs**: 3S to 6S LiPo packs where a low-voltage alarm at about 3.5 V/cell loaded protects the cell and the aircraft.
- **Wearables and Bluetooth devices**: small 1S pouch cells with a linear charger such as the Microchip MCP73831 family (4.2 V option) and a protection IC.
- **Power-bank design**: boost converters take the 3.0 to 4.2 V range to 5 V, and the cutoff is chosen to protect the cell.
- **Battery-powered sensors**: the 3.0 to 4.2 V swing sets the minimum LDO or buck-boost choice for a 3.3 V logic rail.
:::

::: assumptions
- The numbers are for **graphite-anode cells with LCO/NMC/NCA-type cathodes at room temperature**. LiFePO$_4$ is 3.65 V max and 2.5 V min. Lithium titanate (LTO) is 2.8 V max. Silicon-rich and high-voltage cells reach 4.35 to 4.5 V.
- The cutoff of 3.0 V is a compromise. Datasheets quote 2.75 V or 3.0 V at low rates; hobby and drone practice is higher (about 3.3 V under load) because high current plus voltage sag means a large part of capacity is gone before the loaded voltage reaches 3.0 V.
- "LiPo" in the hobby sense is a pouch Li-ion cell with gelled or polymer-assisted electrolyte. The voltage window is the same as ordinary Li-ion.
- The answer changes when the question is about **temperature**: cold cells sag more and may hit cutoff early, and charging below 0 °C requires a reduced limit or is forbidden ([[q0306]]).
:::

::: followups
### Why is 3.7 V called "nominal" if the cell is rarely at 3.7 V?
It is the average of the discharge voltage curve, roughly where the cell sits in the middle of its capacity (about 40 to 50 % SoC). Manufacturers use it so that $Wh = Ah \times 3.7$ gives a sensible energy figure. You almost never need a regulator that is *designed* for 3.7 V; design for the full 3.0 to 4.2 V range.

### What is the voltage of a 4S pack and what would a charger need to supply?
Full is $4 \times 4.2 = 16.8$ V, nominal is $4 \times 3.7 = 14.8$ V, empty is $4 \times 3.0 = 12.0$ V. The charger needs an output limited to 16.8 V (with ±1 % accuracy, so ±168 mV across the pack), and an input at least a volt or two higher if it is a buck type. A balance connector lets the BMS or charger watch each cell.

### Why not charge to 4.3 V to get more capacity?
You do get more: the last 100 mV is worth maybe 5 to 8 % of capacity. But the cost is accelerated cycle-life loss, more gas generation, and a smaller safety margin before the protection trips at around 4.25 to 4.35 V. Cells designed for 4.35 V have a modified cathode and electrolyte to tolerate it. Without that, you are trading life and safety for a few percent.

### A LiPo reads 3.0 V on a meter after sitting on a shelf. What do you do?
Treat it as suspect. Check for swelling and temperature. Quality chargers will refuse to charge a cell below about 2.5 to 3.0 V, or will pre-charge at 0.1C. If the cell dropped below about 2.5 V, copper dissolution may have occurred and the cell could short internally on recharge, so many labs discard it. If it was only briefly at 3.0 V and is not swollen, a gentle pre-charge at 0.05C to 0.1C while watching temperature is the usual recovery, with the understanding that its life is shortened.

### A 3S pack reads 11.1 V total. Can you tell that all cells are fine?
No. 11.1 V is the average of 3.7 V per cell, but one cell could be at 4.1 V while another is at 3.3 V. You need per-cell voltages from the balance lead. A spread more than about 50 to 100 mV on a resting pack is a sign that balancing or a weak cell needs attention.
:::

::: interpretations
"Typical cell voltage" is ambiguous between **nominal** (3.7 V), **maximum** (4.2 V), and the **working range**. The answer above gives all three, and the "ranges and why" part is the electrochemistry of the upper and lower limits. If the interviewer is thinking of a specific product (for example a 1S wearable), tie the answer back to that device's charger and cutoff.
