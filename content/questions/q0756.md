::: testing
Whether you understand that the "wires" of a digital bus are analog, and that every pin and every centimetre of copper is a small capacitor to ground. The interviewer wants:

1. A clean definition: total capacitance from SDA (or SCL) to ground, summing everything attached.
2. Where it comes from: device pins and ESD structures, PCB traces, connectors, cables, test points.
3. The *consequence*: it's what the pull-up has to charge, so it sets rise time and the 400 pF limit.
4. How you'd estimate or measure it.
:::

::: why
Bus capacitance is the reason I2C has a maximum bus size and a speed that depends on layout. Nearly every I2C signal-integrity problem (slow edges, rounded clock, failed ACKs at higher speed) comes back to $C_b$ being larger than expected. It also illustrates a general idea, that parasitics set the speed of any node, which carries over to GPIO, SPI lines and gate drive.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitance** ($C=Q/V$): a line stores charge; changing its voltage means moving charge. See [[q0022]].
- **RC charging**: the pull-up moves that charge, so $t_r\propto R_pC_b$. See [[q0028]] and [[q0755]].
- **Parasitic capacitance**: unintended capacitance from pins, traces, packages. See [[q0030]].
- **Open-drain bus**: only the resistor can charge the line, which makes the rising edge sensitive to $C_b$. See [[q0761]].
- **Dielectric / geometry**: trace capacitance scales with width, length and $\varepsilon_r$, and inversely with the distance to the reference plane. See [[q0023]].
:::

::: answer
"Bus capacitance is the total capacitance between a bus line, SDA or SCL, and ground. It's the sum of everything hanging on the wire: each device's pin and its ESD protection, the PCB trace, connectors, any cable, test points, and so on.

It matters because the pull-up resistor has to charge it every time the line goes high. The rising edge is an RC charge, so the rise time is about 0.85 times $R_p$ times $C_b$. The more capacitance, the slower the edge. The I2C spec caps it at 400 pF for Standard and Fast mode. Fast-mode Plus allows a bit more, but only because its drivers sink 20 mA, so you can use a much stronger pull-up.

A typical budget is about 5 to 10 pF per device pin, roughly 1 pF per centimetre of trace on a normal board, and tens of pF per metre of cable. So a short on-board bus with six devices might be 80 to 100 pF, and a bus with a long cable can blow through 400 pF fast.

To check, I'd estimate from the datasheets, then measure the actual rise time on a scope and back-calculate $C_b=t_r/(0.8473R_p)$."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** $C_b$ is the total capacitance from the line (SDA or SCL) to ground (and to the supply, which is AC ground) as seen at any point on the bus. Each line has its own $C_b$.

**Contributors.**

| Source | Typical value (varies) |
|---|---|
| Device I/O pin (including ESD structure, package, bond wire) | 2 to 10 pF, check the datasheet "input capacitance $C_i$" |
| PCB trace over a plane | about 1 to 1.3 pF/cm for 50 Ω-class geometry; less for narrow traces |
| Connector | 1 to 5 pF each |
| Cable | tens of pF per metre, strongly dependent on type |
| Series ESD/TVS diode | 0.5 to 15 pF |
| MCU pad | 5 to 10 pF |

**Effect.** With pull-up $R_p$ the 30–70% rise time is

$$t_r = 0.8473\,R_pC_b$$

and the spec limits $C_b$ to 400 pF for Standard and Fast mode, 550 pF for Fast-mode Plus (thanks to its 20 mA drivers) and 100 pF for Hs-mode at 3.4 MHz (check the table in the spec revision you use).

**Limits as a consequence.** $R_{p,min}$ is fixed by the sink current, so rising $C_b$ eventually leaves no valid $R_p$ (the "empty window"). At 3.3 V Fast mode that is about 366 pF. See [[q0755]].

**Measurement.** Measure $t_r$ (30–70%) on the scope with a low-capacitance probe (the probe itself adds 8–15 pF); then $C_b = t_r/(0.8473R_p)$. Or power the bus off and measure with an LCR meter at 100 kHz: but power-off ESD diodes may behave differently, so scope-based estimates on the real bus are more representative.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Per-pin and per-length numbers are typical, not guaranteed. Pin capacitance depends on device and bias; trace capacitance depends on stackup. Always check the datasheet and your own board.
:::

::: long
"Bus capacitance is the total capacitance between one of the bus lines, SDA or SCL, and ground. Every line is basically a small capacitor, and each thing you connect adds to it: every device pin and its ESD protection, every centimetre of trace over the ground plane, every connector, any cable.

Why it matters: I2C is open-drain, so the only thing that can charge the line is the pull-up resistor. A falling edge is quick, because a transistor discharges the capacitance. A rising edge is an RC charge through the resistor, and the I2C spec measures it from 30% to 70% of the supply, which comes out to 0.85 times R times C. So as capacitance grows, the rising edge gets slower for a given resistor, and eventually it's too slow to meet the limit: 1 µs for standard mode, 300 ns for fast.

The spec puts a ceiling of 400 pF on the bus capacitance for Standard and Fast mode. The ceiling isn't magic, it's what you get when the pull-up that meets the rise time hits the minimum resistor that the devices can drive low.

For a rough budget I use a few pF to maybe ten per device pin, around 1 pF per centimetre of trace on a normal board, and a few pF per connector. Cable is the killer: tens of pF per metre. So an on-board bus with a handful of parts is typically under 100 pF, and a bus with a metre of cable and a few modules can approach the limit.

In the lab, I'd put a scope on the line, read the 30 to 70% rise time, and compute $C_b$ from $t_r/(0.8473R_p)$."
:::

::: terms
- **Bus capacitance ($C_b$)**: total capacitance of one bus line to ground, specified at 400 pF max for Standard/Fast mode.
- **Parasitic capacitance**: unintended capacitance from structures like pins, traces and packages. See [[q0030]].
- **Input capacitance ($C_i$)**: a device's datasheet value for its I/O pin.
- **Rise time ($t_r$)**: 30–70% of $V_{DD}$ in I2C.
- **Time constant ($\tau$)**: $R_pC_b$.
- **Fringing capacitance**: field lines at trace edges that add to the parallel-plate estimate.
- **ESD structure**: diodes at a pin that add junction capacitance.
- **Lumped vs distributed**: modelling the bus as one capacitor versus a transmission line; valid when the line is short compared with an edge's spatial extent.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "capacitance on the bus that slows edges".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Defines it per line and lists contributors with numbers (pins, trace, connector, cable).
- Ties it to the **specific** equation $t_r=0.8473R_pC_b$ and the 400 pF limit, explaining where the limit comes from.
- Notes the asymmetry: falling edges are fast (transistor), rising edges slow (resistor), so capacitance only hurts the high-going edge noticeably.
- Gives a practical way to measure it (scope rise time, probe capacitance caveat) and the remedies.
- Distinguishes lumped $C_b$ from transmission-line behaviour for long cables.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Calling it "stray capacitance" without numbers.** Give typical values; show you can budget.
- **Forgetting the probe.** A 10× probe adds roughly 8–15 pF, a big error on a 50 pF bus.
- **Adding the capacitance of SDA and SCL together.** Each line has its own $C_b$; they don't sum.
- **Thinking fall time is affected much.** A transistor discharges quickly; the rise suffers. (The spec actually requires a *minimum* fall time to limit ringing.)
- **Assuming device count is the limit.** It's total capacitance; a long cable with two parts can fail where a compact board with ten works.
- **Treating 400 pF as always achievable.** At 3.3 V Fast mode, a resistor can't meet 300 ns at 400 pF with a 3 mA sink limit. See [[q0755]].
:::

::: deep
## What is it, physically?

A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator. On an I2C line, one conductor is the net itself (pin pads, copper trace, connector pin). The other is "ground": the reference plane under the trace, the neighbouring ground pour, and the supply rail (which is AC ground thanks to decoupling). Whenever the line's voltage changes by $\Delta V$, charge $Q=C_b\Delta V$ has to move onto or off that capacitor.

The falling edge is easy: the device's NMOS turns on and dumps the charge through its on-resistance (tens of ohms). The rising edge is hard: nothing drives; the pull-up resistor feeds in charge through $R_p$, which is hundreds to thousands of ohms. So the rising edge's time constant is $\tau=R_pC_b$.

## Where the picofarads come from

**1. Device pins.** Each I/O includes a bond wire/pad, an ESD clamp (junction capacitance, a few pF), and the input transistor gate. Datasheets list $C_i$ for the pin, commonly 2 to 10 pF. Some parts (muxes, level shifters, ESD diodes) have larger pin capacitance because of switch/clamp junctions.

**2. PCB trace.** A trace over a reference plane is a capacitor with a fringing correction. For a trace of width $w$ over a dielectric of height $h$ with $\varepsilon_r\approx4.3$, a 50 Ω microstrip needs $w/h\approx1.9$ and has a capacitance per length of

$$C'=\frac{1}{Z_0\,v}=\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}}{c\,Z_0}\approx\frac{\sqrt{3.3}}{3\times10^8\times50}=121\text{ pF/m}\approx1.2\text{ pF/cm}$$

(A pure parallel-plate estimate for $w=0.38$ mm, $h=0.2$ mm over 10 cm gives 7.2 pF; the fringing field raises that toward 12 pF.) Narrower traces over thicker dielectric have a higher $Z_0$ and lower $C'$: a 75 Ω line would be about 0.8 pF/cm. If there's *no* plane below the trace (two-layer boards with a distant return), the capacitance per cm is lower still, but the loop inductance and noise pickup are worse.

**3. Connectors and sockets.** A few pF each, more for ribbon connectors and ones with ground pins close by.

**4. Cables.** Flat ribbon and twisted pair are in the tens of pF per metre; shielded cable more. A one metre cable can easily add 50 to 100 pF. *Treat these as ballpark; check the cable datasheet.*

**5. Protection and switches.** TVS arrays and series ESD diodes: 0.5 pF (low-cap parts) to 15 pF or more. Analog switches and muxes: their "on" capacitance adds to whichever branch is connected.

## A worked budget

Six devices, one connector, 12 cm of trace per line:

| Item | Capacitance |
|---|---|
| MCU pad | 7 pF |
| 5 slaves × 6 pF | 30 pF |
| Connector | 3 pF |
| 12 cm trace × 1.2 pF/cm | 14 pF |
| Test points / pads | 2 pF |
| **Total** | **≈ 56 pF** |

With $R_p=4.7$ kΩ: $t_r=0.8473\times4700\times56\text{ pF}=223$ ns, fine for Standard (1000 ns) and Fast (300 ns). Add a 1 m cable at ~100 pF/m: $C_b\approx156$ pF, $t_r=621$ ns: now Fast mode fails, and you'd go to 2.2 kΩ ($t_r=291$ ns, marginal) or slow down.

## Watching it on a scope

The RC charge shape is visible on the real bus. The rising edge is an exponential, not a straight ramp, and the falling edge is nearly vertical. Read the 30–70% time and compute

$$C_b=\frac{t_r}{0.8473\,R_p}$$

For example, a 2.2 kΩ bus with a 250 ns measured rise gives 134 pF. If your scope probe adds 12 pF, subtract it: about 122 pF is the real bus.

## Lumped or distributed?

Treating the line as one capacitor works when it is electrically short. Rule of thumb: if the line's one-way delay is much less than the edge's rise time (say less than $t_r/6$), a lumped model is fine. Typical I2C edges are 100 ns to 1 µs; a 30 cm board trace has about 2 ns of delay: perfectly lumped. A 10 m cable has 50 ns of delay, still fine against a 1 µs edge but increasingly awkward at Fast-mode Plus. For long cables the inductance and resistance matter too, and that's when you move to a differential extender or another bus.

## Capacitance and speed modes

Because of $C_b$, the speed modes each demand something different:
- Standard (100 kHz): 1000 ns rise, 400 pF.
- Fast (400 kHz): 300 ns rise, 400 pF.
- Fast-mode Plus (1 MHz): 120 ns rise, with stronger drivers (20 mA) to allow smaller $R_p$. The capacitance limit is higher, 550 pF, because those drivers can sink more current.
- High-speed mode (3.4 MHz): uses a current-source pull-up in the master during the high-speed phase and allows far less capacitance: 100 pF at 3.4 MHz, or 400 pF if you drop to 1.7 MHz.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The Fm+ and Hs-mode capacitance limits above are from recent revisions of UM10204. Check the electrical-characteristics table of the revision you design to.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Say the formula and the budget: "about 5–10 pF per part, 1 pF per cm, and 0.85·R·C for the rise." Numbers make this answer stick.
:::

::: applications
- **Sizing pull-ups at design time**: estimate $C_b$ first, then choose $R_p$ within its window ([[q0755]]).
- **Choosing connectors/cables for an inter-board I2C link**: cable capacitance can dominate $C_b$.
- **I2C muxes and buffers**: each downstream branch sees only its own capacitance, which is why they're used to "fix" large systems.
- **Low-cap ESD protection**: choosing TVS arrays with 1 pF rather than 15 pF on I2C lines.
- **Debugging a failing bus**: back-calculating $C_b$ from a scope rise time to find that a connector or cable was the culprit.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Lumped-capacitor model.** Valid for short buses; long cables behave as transmission lines.
- **Linear capacitance.** Real pin capacitance depends on bias voltage (junction capacitance varies), so treat datasheet values as typical.
- **Resistive pull-up.** With a current-source or active pull-up the charging is linear, not exponential.
- **Numbers in the budget are ballparks.** Per-length figures depend on stackup; check your own.

If the interviewer says "the bus is 5 cm with two devices", $C_b$ is maybe 30 pF and almost any pull-up works; the question mostly bites at long cables or many devices.
:::

::: followups
### Does bus capacitance affect the falling edge?
Much less. A device's NMOS discharges the line through its on-resistance (tens of ohms) so the fall is fast. If anything, the spec wants a *minimum* fall time, so output stages are slew-limited to reduce ringing. The slow edge is always the rise.

### How would you measure bus capacitance?
Scope: measure the 30–70% rise with a known $R_p$ and compute $t_r/(0.8473R_p)$, subtracting the probe's capacitance. LCR meter: with the bus unpowered, measure line-to-ground, remembering that unpowered ESD diodes have different capacitance than powered. Datasheet sums are a third, approximate method.

### Why is there a 400 pF limit?
Because at that size the pull-up needed to meet the rise-time limit gets so small that devices can't sink the resulting current. It isn't a physical wall, it's where the resistor window closes for the standard devices (at 3.3 V Fast mode the closing point is about 366 pF).

### Our bus is at 380 pF and failing at 400 kHz. What do you do?
Lower $R_p$ is limited; slow to 100 kHz where the rise limit is 1 µs and the window is wide open; or split the bus with a mux/buffer; or shorten traces/remove unnecessary devices. If we need 400 kHz, I'd use a buffer with rise-time accelerators.

### The bus works until I attach a scope probe. What does that tell you?
That the bus is marginal. A 10x probe adds roughly 8 to 15 pF (plus the ground-lead inductance), and on a 100 pF bus that's 10% more capacitance; if that tips it over, you were already near the rise-time limit. Re-measure with a low-capacitance active probe, and fix the margin (smaller $R_p$, less $C_b$) rather than leaving the probe on.
:::

"What is bus capacitance?" is about the electrical load on an I2C line. It's different from the *capacitance of a capacitor* ([[q0022]]) and from "bus load" in CAN. The follow-on question [[q0757]] asks how it changes and what impacts it; this page defines it and shows where it comes from.
