::: basics
### In plain words
Flyback voltage is the voltage spike you get when you suddenly cut the current through a coil, like a motor or relay. A coil stores energy while current flows. If the switch opens, that energy must go somewhere, so the coil pushes its voltage as high as needed to keep the current going. The fix is to give the current a safe path.

### Picture it
Imagine a long, heavy pipe full of moving water with a valve at the end. Slam the valve shut and the water's momentum cannot just vanish. Pressure spikes at the valve, and it can burst. A bypass pipe lets the water keep circulating safely. In the circuit, the coil current is the moving water, the switch is the valve, and a diode is the bypass. The analogy has a gap: the coil's spike goes in the opposite direction to the supply.

### Words you need
- **Inductor**: a coil whose current cannot change instantly. Its voltage is $v = L\,di/dt$.
- **Stored energy**: $\tfrac12 L I^2$, the energy held in the coil's magnetic field.
- **Flyback (freewheel) diode**: a diode across the coil that gives the current a loop to circulate in. See [[q0339]].
- **Snubber / TVS**: parts that absorb or clamp a spike to a safe level.

### The simplest example
A relay coil carries 0.1 A from a 12 V supply. You open the switch. Without a diode, the current has nowhere to go, so the voltage across the switch shoots up until something breaks down. With a diode across the coil, the current flows through the diode instead. The switch sees only about 12 V plus 0.7 V for the diode.

That is the whole idea: interrupted coil current makes a spike, and a diode gives it a path. The rest of this page covers the energy and voltage math, the downside of slow current decay, and faster clamps like a Zener or TVS.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand **why interrupting an inductor current makes a voltage spike**, and can size and choose the protection. The hidden sub-skills:

1. The physics: $v = L\,di/dt$; the inductor forces its current to continue, so the voltage is whatever it must be (the stored energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ has to go somewhere).
2. The consequence: a switch can see a voltage far above the supply, limited only by parasitic capacitance or breakdown.
3. The fixes and their trade-offs: flyback (freewheel) diode (slow decay), diode plus Zener or TVS (faster), RC snubber, active clamp; and the disambiguation from a *flyback converter*.
:::

::: why
Unclamped inductive switching kills MOSFETs, BJTs and microcontroller pins, makes relays arc, and radiates EMI. It is the number one gotcha when students first drive a motor or solenoid, which is why it comes up in nearly every interview that touches motor drives. The same physics, used on purpose, is how boost converters and ignition coils make high voltage. A strong answer shows the equation, a number, and a protection choice with its consequence (decay time and switch stress).
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor law**: $v = L\,di/dt$; current cannot change instantly. See [[q0022]].
- **Energy storage**: $W = \tfrac12LI^2$. See [[q0335]].
- **Parasitic capacitance**: the switch's output capacitance and the winding capacitance limit the spike. 
- **Diode clamping**: forward conduction gives the current a path. See [[q0339]].
- **Avalanche energy**: how much a MOSFET can absorb in breakdown.
:::

::: answer
"Flyback voltage, or inductive kick, is the voltage spike you get when you suddenly interrupt the current through an inductor, like a motor or solenoid coil. An inductor's voltage is L times di dt. While the switch is on, the coil has current I and stores half L I squared of energy in its magnetic field. When you open the switch, the current wants to keep flowing, so the coil generates whatever voltage it needs, with the polarity reversed, to push the current through. With nothing to take the current, that voltage shoots up until something conducts: the switch avalanches, an arc forms across relay contacts, or the parasitic capacitance charges up to hundreds or thousands of volts.

The fix is to give the current a path. The standard solution is a flyback or freewheel diode across the coil, reverse-biased in normal operation. When the switch opens, the diode conducts and the current recirculates through the coil and diode, so the switch only sees the supply plus a diode drop. The downside is that the current decays slowly, since the only voltage opposing it is the diode drop. If you need fast release, you add a Zener or TVS in series with the diode, a snubber, or an active clamp, at the price of a higher switch voltage.

I'd also mention it's not the same as a flyback converter, which uses the same effect on purpose in a transformer-based supply."
:::

::: technical
**Origin.** For an inductor, $v_L = L\,\dfrac{di}{dt}$. When a switch carrying current $I_0$ opens, $di/dt$ becomes large and negative, so $v_L$ reverses and becomes large: the inductor acts as a current source of value $I_0$ that pushes into whatever impedance it finds.

**Unclamped peak.** Energy conservation into the parasitic capacitance $C_p$ (switch output capacitance plus winding capacitance): $\tfrac12LI_0^2 = \tfrac12C_pV_{pk}^2$, so

$$V_{pk} = I_0\sqrt{\frac{L}{C_p}}$$

Example: $L = 10$ mH, $I_0 = 1$ A, $C_p = 100$ pF gives $10$ kV (ideal). In practice the switch avalanches or an arc strikes first, so the number shows *why* unclamped inductive switching is destructive, not what you will measure.

**Flyback diode clamp.** With the diode (drop $V_d$) across a load $R + L$, the switch voltage is clamped at $V_s + V_d$. The loop current obeys $L\,di/dt = -(V_d + iR)$:

$$i(t) = \left(I_0 + \frac{V_d}{R}\right)e^{-t/\tau} - \frac{V_d}{R},\quad \tau = \frac{L}{R}$$

and reaches zero at $t_{off} = \tau\ln\!\left(1 + \dfrac{I_0R}{V_d}\right)$. Example $L = 10$ mH, $R = 12\ \Omega$, $I_0 = 1$ A, $V_d = 0.7$ V: $\tau = 0.83$ ms and $t_{off} = 2.4$ ms.

**Higher clamp voltage = faster release.** Diode plus 24 V Zener ($V_d \approx 24.7$ V): $t_{off} = 0.33$ ms but the switch now sees $12 + 24.7 = 36.7$ V, so use a MOSFET rated well above that.

**Energy.** $\tfrac12LI_0^2 = 5$ mJ in the example, dissipated mostly in the clamp (diode, Zener or TVS) and the coil resistance.

**Disambiguation.** A *flyback converter* is a switching supply topology (coupled inductor/transformer that stores energy during on-time and delivers it during off-time). The flyback voltage here is the same physical effect, unwanted rather than used.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The ideal $I_0\sqrt{L/C_p}$ spike is an upper bound; real peaks depend on the switch's avalanche voltage and the parasitic inductance and resistance in the loop.
:::

::: long
"Flyback voltage is also called inductive kick. It's what you get when you suddenly break the current through an inductor, like a relay coil, solenoid or motor winding. The inductor law is v equals L di dt, and the energy stored while current flows is half L I squared. If I open the switch, the current can't drop to zero instantly, because that would require infinite voltage, so the inductor flips polarity and generates whatever voltage is needed to keep pushing the current. If there's no path, that voltage keeps rising until something gives, like the transistor avalanching, an arc across the contacts, or the parasitic capacitance charging. Equating the energy to half C V squared gives a peak of I times root L over C, which for 10 millihenries, 1 amp and 100 picofarads is theoretically 10 kilovolts. In reality the switch breaks down well before that, and often not survivably.

The standard protection is a flyback or freewheel diode across the load, cathode to the positive side. Normally it's reverse-biased. At turn-off it conducts, the current recirculates in the coil, and the switch sees the supply plus a diode drop. The current then decays with L over R, so a 10 millihenry, 12 ohm coil takes a couple of milliseconds. That slowness is the trade-off: it slows solenoid release and motor braking. If you need faster decay you put a Zener or TVS in series with the diode, accept a higher switch voltage, say 37 volts instead of 12.7, and get release in a third of a millisecond. Other options are an RC snubber, a varistor, or an active clamp where the MOSFET itself is turned on partially.

And just to avoid confusion: a flyback converter is a power supply topology that uses the same effect deliberately."
:::

::: terms
- **Flyback voltage / inductive kick**: reverse voltage spike when inductor current is interrupted.
- **Freewheel (flyback) diode**: diode across an inductive load that conducts at turn-off.
- **Clamp voltage**: maximum voltage the protection allows across the switch.
- **TVS (transient voltage suppressor)**: avalanche diode that clamps spikes.
- **Snubber**: RC (or RCD) network that absorbs energy and slows $dv/dt$.
- **Active clamp**: MOSFET gate controlled to turn on at a set drain voltage, absorbing the energy.
- **Avalanche energy ($E_{AS}$)**: energy a MOSFET can absorb in breakdown without damage.
- **Flyback converter**: isolated switching supply that stores energy in a coupled inductor, a deliberate use of the same effect.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a voltage spike when you turn off a motor, so use a diode".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives the spike from $v = L\,di/dt$ and the **energy** $\tfrac12LI^2$, and gives the peak formula with a number.
- Explains **diode polarity** (cathode toward the supply) and the clamp voltage $V_s + V_d$.
- States the **trade-off**: the diode makes the current decay slowly ($\tau = L/R$, $t_{off} = \tau\ln(1 + I_0R/V_d)$); fast release needs a higher clamp (Zener/TVS/active clamp) and a switch rated for it.
- Mentions **MOSFET body diodes in an H-bridge**, avalanche ratings, and layout (clamp close to the switch, short loop to limit EMI).
- Distinguishes flyback voltage from a flyback converter.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the diode in forward polarity.** It then shorts the supply; the cathode goes to the positive rail side of the coil.
- **Believing the diode makes the energy disappear.** It dissipates it in the diode and coil resistance over milliseconds.
- **Using a slow diode (1N4001) for fast PWM.** Use fast/Schottky diodes at PWM frequencies; a slow diode's reverse recovery adds loss and noise.
- **Ignoring the release time with a diode.** Relays and solenoids drop out noticeably later with a plain diode (often several times slower).
- **Placing the diode far from the load or switch.** Loop inductance in the wiring adds spikes and EMI; put the clamp at the coil.
- **Calling a flyback converter "the same circuit".** Same physics, different purpose.
:::

::: deep
## The physics in one picture

While the switch is closed, $di/dt = (V - iR)/L$ builds the current to $I_0$ and the coil stores $W = \tfrac12LI_0^2$ of energy in its field. At the instant the switch opens, the current through the coil is still $I_0$ (continuity) but the path is gone. The coil voltage must satisfy $v = L\,di/dt$ and whatever circuit remains determines $di/dt$: if the circuit is only a small capacitance $C_p$, it charges at $dv/dt = I_0/C_p$, an enormous slew rate, and the voltage rings up as an LC oscillation with amplitude $I_0\sqrt{L/C_p}$.

## Decay with different clamps

With the loop closed by a clamp of voltage $V_c$ (diode drop, or diode plus Zener), the current decays as $i(t) = (I_0 + V_c/R)e^{-t/\tau} - V_c/R$ until it reaches zero. For the example ($L = 10$ mH, $R = 12\ \Omega$, $I_0 = 1$ A):

| Clamp | $V_c$ | Switch sees | Decay time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diode only | 0.7 V | 12.7 V | 2.4 ms |
| Diode + 24 V Zener | 24.7 V | 36.7 V | 0.33 ms |
| TVS/Zener 100 V | about 100 V | about 112 V | 0.094 ms |
| None | set by breakdown | breakdown voltage or arc | microseconds, destructive |

## Energy accounting

All of $\tfrac12LI_0^2 = 5$ mJ must be dissipated. With a diode clamp the diode absorbs part and the coil resistance the rest; with a Zener the Zener takes the bulk. A TVS rated for 600 W peak for 1 ms handles 0.6 J, far above 5 mJ. For a MOSFET relying on avalanche (no clamp), check the single-pulse avalanche rating $E_{AS}$ and the repetitive rating: 5 mJ per cycle at 1 kHz is 5 W average into the die, which would overheat a small MOSFET.

## Design checklist

1. **Find $I_0$ and $L$**: stall current (not running current) and the worst-case inductance.
2. **Pick the clamp** to meet the release-time requirement within the switch voltage rating (with 20 to 30% margin).
3. **Use the right diode**: Schottky or fast-recovery for PWM; rated for peak current $I_0$ and the average current during recirculation.
4. **Place it at the coil** (or right at the switch) with a tight loop.
5. **Check the H-bridge**: in a bridge, the body diodes of the opposite MOSFETs provide freewheel paths, but check their ratings and shoot-through (see [[q0422]]).
6. **Consider an active clamp or TVS** for automotive or high-reliability designs where load dump and inductive transients exist.
7. **Verify on a scope** with a short ground lead: observe the drain voltage at turn-off.

## Beyond the simple diode

- **Diode + Zener/TVS**: fast release at a controlled voltage.
- **RC snubber across the switch**: damps ringing, slows $dv/dt$; sized as $C \gg C_p$ with $R \approx \sqrt{L_p/C_p}$.
- **Varistor (MOV)**: clamps both polarities; common on AC coils.
- **Active clamp**: gate-drive circuit that partially turns on the MOSFET at $V_{clamp}$, using the switch itself to absorb the energy (needs SOA).
- **Synchronous recirculation**: in a half- or H-bridge, turn the opposite MOSFET on during the off interval for lower loss than the body diode (slow decay mode).

## Where flyback is a feature

The same physics is the basis of the flyback converter, the boost converter and automotive ignition coils: stored inductive energy is deliberately released at a higher voltage. In a boost converter the switch opens, the inductor current continues through a diode into the output capacitor, and the voltage rises to $V_{out} > V_{in}$. The difference between a *bug* and a *feature* is whether the energy has a controlled path.
:::

::: applications
- **Relay and solenoid coil drivers**: a diode or diode-Zener across the coil.
- **H-bridge motor drives**: body diodes or synchronous freewheeling in slow-decay mode.
- **Automotive ECU outputs**: active clamps and TVS for injector and solenoid drivers.
- **Boost converters and ignition coils**: deliberate use of inductive kick to make a higher voltage.
- **Industrial AC coils**: RC snubbers and varistors across contactor coils.
:::

::: assumptions
- DC inductor driven by a low-side switch, with ideal $L$ and $R$; real windings have capacitance, saturation and core losses.
- Ideal clamp (constant $V_c$); real diodes have forward-voltage increase with current and reverse recovery.
- Parasitic capacitance (100 pF) is a typical order of magnitude; actual values vary.
- Question is about flyback *voltage*, not a flyback converter; the converter is discussed only to disambiguate.
:::

::: followups
### Which way does the flyback diode face?
Cathode to the positive supply side of the coil, anode to the switch node. In normal operation it is reverse-biased by the supply. When the switch opens, the coil's node goes above the supply, the diode forward-biases and the current recirculates.

### Why does the diode make relays drop out more slowly?
The coil current decays with only the diode drop opposing it, so it falls with time constant $L/R$ instead of quickly. The contacts release when the current falls below the drop-out threshold, which can be several times later than without a diode. A Zener in series with the diode speeds it up at the cost of a higher clamp voltage.

### How do you size the flyback diode?
Reverse voltage rating above the supply (with margin), peak forward current rating at least $I_0$ (the coil current at turn-off), and fast recovery for PWM use. A Schottky diode has low forward drop but check reverse leakage at temperature; a standard rectifier diode works for slow, on/off loads.

### What does the H-bridge do for flyback protection?
Each MOSFET's body diode provides the freewheel path to the supply or ground; during decay, the current flows through the diode or, in synchronous mode, through the turned-on opposite MOSFET. You still must check the current rating and avoid shoot-through during the transition.

### Scenario: you add a flyback diode to a solenoid driver and now the plunger releases too slowly, causing a missed cycle. What are your options?
Add a Zener or TVS in series with the diode so the clamp is, say, 30 to 40 V instead of 0.7 V (ensuring the MOSFET is rated well above it), or use an active clamp. Alternatively reverse-drive briefly with a bridge to force the current down faster. When the clamp voltage is large compared with $I_0R$, the release time is roughly $L I_0/V_c$, so doubling the clamp voltage about halves it.

### What would you see on a scope at the switch node when the diode is missing?
A tall narrow spike or ring after turn-off, with amplitude set by the MOSFET avalanche or the LC resonance, typically tens to hundreds of volts, ringing at the frequency set by the leakage inductance and parasitic capacitance. Add the diode and the spike disappears, replaced by a plateau at $V_s + V_d$.
:::

::: interpretations
"Flyback voltage" here means the **inductive kick** from switching an inductor, as in the DC inductive-load section. It is *not* the output of a flyback converter, though the physics is the same. The related ideas of the motor's own generated voltage are in [[q0341]] (back-EMF), a different effect: back-EMF exists while the motor is spinning; flyback exists at the moment of interrupting current. See [[q0345]] for why inrush still happens despite inductance.
