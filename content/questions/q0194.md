::: testing
Whether you can *define* ripple as a circuit quantity (the peak-to-peak variation of a state variable inside one switching cycle) and *derive* it, rather than waving at "noise on the output". The two ripples are different things: **current ripple** $\Delta I_L$ is the inductor's triangular excursion, set by volt-seconds across $L$; **voltage ripple** $\Delta V_{out}$ is what the capacitor does with that current (plus ESR). A strong answer derives both in a few lines, gives numbers, and notes what changes with $L$, $C$, $f_{sw}$, $V_{in}$ and ESR.
:::

::: why
Ripple drives component choice and system behaviour: inductor ripple sets peak current, core loss and the CCM/DCM boundary; output ripple decides whether noise-sensitive loads (ADCs, PLLs, RF, sensors) work; input ripple drives capacitor rating and EMI. Almost every question in the "Efficiency, Ripple, and Switching Frequency" section is the same derivation turned around. The "from a circuit analysis perspective" qualifier asks for the waveform-based derivation, not a rule of thumb.
:::

::: concepts
- **State variables**: inductor current and capacitor voltage are the quantities that ripple; they cannot jump. See [[q0043]].
- **Inductor equation** $\Delta I=\frac{v\,\Delta t}{L}$: ripple current from volt-seconds. See [[q0188]].
- **Capacitor equation** $\Delta V=\frac{\Delta Q}{C}$: ripple voltage from charge. See [[q0189]].
- **Steady state**: ripple repeats every period; averages are constant.
- **ESR contribution**: $\Delta I\cdot R_{ESR}$. See [[q0030]].
- **Ripple ratio** $r=\Delta I_L/I_{out}$, a design parameter, typically 0.2 to 0.4.
:::

::: answer
"Ripple is the AC variation, peak to peak, of a quantity that's supposed to be DC, here the inductor current and the output voltage, caused by the switching.

For the inductor current: during the on time the inductor has $V_{in}-V_{out}$ across it for $DT$, so the current rises by $\Delta I=(V_{in}-V_{out})DT/L$. Using $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$ that's $V_{out}(1-D)/(L f_{sw})$. In my 12 V to 3.3 V, 500 kHz, 4.7 µH example that's about 1 A peak to peak, around 50% of the 2 A load, so the current runs from 1.5 to 2.5 A. Typical designs target 20 to 40%.

For the output voltage: the capacitor absorbs that triangular current ripple minus the DC. The capacitor only charges during the half period when the inductor current is above the load, so the charge is $\Delta I\,T/8$ and the voltage ripple is $\Delta I/(8 f C)$, about 11.6 mV for 22 µF, plus the ESR term, $\Delta I$ times the ESR, which is 5 mV for 5 milliohms. Since the two are 90 degrees apart they combine to about 12 mV, not 17.

So the current ripple goes down with a bigger inductor and a higher frequency, and the voltage ripple goes down with a bigger capacitor, lower ESR and everything that reduces the current ripple. Ripple current is worst at maximum input voltage."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.** $\Delta I_L=I_{L,max}-I_{L,min}$; $\Delta V_{out}=V_{out,max}-V_{out,min}$; both per switching period in steady state.

**Inductor current ripple (CCM).**
$$\Delta I_L=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})\,D\,T}{L}=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{L\,f_{sw}}=\frac{V_{out}}{L f_{sw}}\Big(1-\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}\Big).$$
Increases with $V_{in}$ (for a fixed $V_{out}$); worst at $V_{in,max}$. Example: $3.3\times0.725/(4.7\ \mu H\times500\ kHz)=1.018$ A. Ripple ratio $r=\Delta I/I_{out}=0.51$; $I_{pk}=2.51$ A, $I_{val}=1.49$ A; rms $\sqrt{I_{out}^2+\Delta I^2/12}=2.02$ A.

**Output voltage ripple.**
$$\Delta V_{out}\approx\underbrace{\frac{\Delta I_L}{8f_{sw}C}}_{\text{capacitive}}\ +\ \underbrace{\Delta I_L R_{ESR}}_{\text{ESR}}\quad(\text{upper bound; true value }\le\text{sum, they are in quadrature}),$$
$11.6+5.1=16.7$ mV bound; numerical integration gives 12.3 mV. Add ESL spikes $L_{ESL}\,di/dt$ at the edges.

**Capacitor current ripple.** $i_C=i_L-I_{out}$: $I_{C,rms}=\Delta I/\sqrt{12}=0.29$ A.

**Input ripple.** Input current pulses $0\to i_L$; capacitor rms $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$; voltage ripple $I_{out}D(1-D)/(fC_{in})$. See [[q0193]].

**Sizing from a spec.** $L=\frac{V_{out}(1-D_{min})}{r\,I_{out}f}$ evaluated at max $V_{in}$; $C\ge\frac{\Delta I_L}{8f(\Delta V_{spec}-\Delta I_LR_{ESR})}$.

**Scaling laws.** $\Delta I\propto\frac{1}{Lf}$; $\Delta V_C\propto\frac{1}{LCf^2}$. Doubling $f$ halves current ripple and quarters the capacitive voltage ripple.
:::

::: short
"Ripple is the peak-to-peak switching-frequency variation of the inductor current and the output voltage. The inductor current ramps by $V\cdot t/L$ each state, so $\Delta I=V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf)$, about 1 A in my example. The capacitor sees that as a triangle with zero average, so the capacitive voltage ripple is $\Delta I/(8fC)$, about 11.6 mV, plus $\Delta I\cdot ESR$. Bigger $L$, higher $f$, bigger $C$ and lower ESR all reduce it, and current ripple is worst at the highest $V_{in}$."
:::

::: long
"From a circuit perspective, ripple is just what the state variables do inside one switching cycle. There are two state variables that matter in a buck: the inductor current and the capacitor voltage.

Starting with the inductor, during the on time it has $V_{in}$ minus $V_{out}$ across it for $DT$, so its current rises by that voltage times time over $L$. In steady state it falls back by the same amount in the off time. Using $D = V_{out}/V_{in}$ I can write that as $V_{out}$ times $1-D$ over $L f$. With 12 volts to 3.3, 4.7 microhenries and 500 kilohertz it's about 1.02 amps peak to peak, about half the 2 amp load. A typical design target is 20 to 40 percent of the load, so I'd probably increase the inductor in a real design. Because it depends on $1-D$, it gets worse as the input voltage goes up, so I size it at the maximum input voltage.

The voltage ripple comes from the capacitor handling the inductor ripple. The capacitor current is the inductor current minus the load, a triangle with zero average. The capacitor charges for half the period, when the triangle is positive, and the area of that half is the ripple current times T over 8, so the voltage ripple is $\Delta I/(8fC)$, about 11.6 millivolts for 22 microfarads. On top of that, the ESR turns the same triangle current into a voltage, $\Delta I$ times ESR, about 5 millivolts. The two peak at different times, so it's about 12 millivolts total, though the plain sum is a good conservative estimate.

Scaling: current ripple goes as one over $L$ times $f$, capacitive voltage ripple as one over $L C f$ squared. So doubling the switching frequency cuts the voltage ripple by four. Ripple on the input side is a separate calculation, set by the pulsed input current and the input capacitor."
:::

::: terms
- **Ripple current $\Delta I_L$**: peak-to-peak inductor current variation per cycle.
- **Ripple voltage $\Delta V_{out}$**: peak-to-peak output variation at the switching frequency.
- **Ripple ratio $r$**: $\Delta I_L/I_{out}$, usually 0.2 to 0.4.
- **ESR ripple**: the part of voltage ripple equal to $\Delta I\cdot R_{ESR}$.
- **Volt-second product**: $V\cdot t$ across the inductor, sets $\Delta I$.
- **Charge ($\Delta Q$)**: the area under the positive lobe of the capacitor current, $\Delta I\,T/8$.
- **Switching spikes**: high-frequency edges from ESL and layout, distinct from ripple.
- **Steady state**: waveforms repeat each period.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Ripple is the small AC voltage on the output; you reduce it with a bigger capacitor."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates inductor current ripple from output voltage ripple and gives the formula for each with a derivation.
- Gives numbers and the ripple ratio design rule.
- Explains dependence on $V_{in}$ (worst at max), $L$, $f$, $C$, and ESR, including the scaling laws.
- Says the capacitive and ESR terms are in quadrature (the sum is an upper bound).
- Distinguishes ripple from high-frequency switching spikes and from low-frequency noise/loop effects.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using the load current in the ripple formula.** In CCM $\Delta I_L$ does not depend on $I_{out}$.
- **Sizing the inductor at nominal $V_{in}$ rather than maximum.** Ripple current peaks at $V_{in,max}$.
- **Forgetting ESR** and the result that with electrolytics ESR dominates the ripple.
- **Using $\Delta I/(4fC)$ or $\Delta I/(2fC)$.** The correct triangle-area factor is 8.
- **Treating spikes as ripple.** Edge spikes are from ESL and loop inductance and are not helped by a larger capacitance.
- **Believing a larger inductor always helps.** It lowers ripple but slows transients and increases DCR and size.
:::

::: deep
## Ripple as the AC part of a state variable

A switching converter is a periodic circuit. In steady state every state variable returns to its initial value after a period $T$. A variable's *ripple* is the peak-to-peak size of what is left when you remove its average.

| Quantity | Average | Ripple |
|---|---|---|
| $i_L$ | $I_{out}=2$ A | $\Delta I_L=1.02$ A |
| $v_{out}$ | $V_{out}=3.3$ V | $\Delta V_{out}\approx12$ mV |
| $i_C$ | 0 | $\Delta I_L$ p-p, 0.29 A rms |
| $i_{in}$ at the FET | $DI_{out}=0.55$ A | pulses 1.49 to 2.51 A |

## Step 1: inductor current ripple

Applying $v_L=L\,di/dt$ over the on interval:

$$\Delta I_L=\int_0^{DT}\frac{V_{in}-V_{out}}{L}dt=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})DT}{L}.$$

From volt-second balance $(V_{in}-V_{out})D=V_{out}(1-D)$, so

$$\boxed{\Delta I_L=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{L\,f_{sw}}}$$

### Dependence on $V_{in}$ (fixed $V_{out}=3.3$ V, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz)

| $V_{in}$ | $D$ | $\Delta I_L$ |
|---|---|---|
| 5 V | 0.660 | 0.48 A |
| 6 V | 0.550 | 0.63 A |
| 12 V | 0.275 | 1.02 A |
| 24 V | 0.137 | 1.21 A |
| 36 V | 0.092 | 1.28 A |

Ripple saturates toward $V_{out}/(Lf)=1.40$ A as $V_{in}\to\infty$. This is why the worst case for the inductor (peak current, saturation, core loss) is at maximum input voltage.

### Choosing $L$

Pick $r=0.3$ and compute at $V_{in,max}=24$ V: $L=\frac{3.3(1-0.1375)}{0.3\times2\times500\ kHz}=9.5\ \mu H$. A standard 10 µH part then gives $\Delta I=3.3\times0.8625/(10\ \mu H\times500\ kHz)=0.57$ A, $r=0.28$.

## Step 2: output voltage ripple

KCL at the output: $i_C=i_L-I_{out}$. Charge delivered during the positive lobe:

$$\Delta Q=\frac12\cdot\frac T2\cdot\frac{\Delta I_L}{2}=\frac{\Delta I_L T}{8}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\Delta V_C=\frac{\Delta I_L}{8f_{sw}C}.$$

A conservative rule: $\Delta V_{out}\le\Delta I_L\left(\frac{1}{8fC}+R_{ESR}\right)$. For the 22 µF ceramic, $\frac{1}{8fC}=11.4$ mΩ, so the capacitive and ESR terms are comparable at 5 mΩ ESR. For an electrolytic with $R_{ESR}=100$ mΩ, ESR dominates by a factor of nine.

## Step 3: design from a ripple spec

Given $\Delta V_{spec}=20$ mV and $\Delta I_L=1.018$ A at 500 kHz:
- ESR budget: $R_{ESR}<20\ \text{mV}/1.018\ \text{A}=19.6$ mΩ (using the bound).
- Capacitance: if ESR is negligible, $C\ge1.018/(8\times5\times10^5\times0.02)=12.7\ \mu F$ effective. Allow for DC-bias derating on ceramics (nominal maybe 22 µF).

## Scaling

| Change | $\Delta I_L$ | $\Delta V_{C}$ | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| $L\times2$ | /2 | /2 | slower transient |
| $f\times2$ | /2 | /4 | more switching loss |
| $C\times2$ | same | /2 | cost/size, ESR may dominate |
| $V_{in}\times2$ (fixed $V_{out}$) | up to ×1.2 | up | worst case at max $V_{in}$ |
| ESR ×2 | same | ESR term ×2 | |

Example: doubling $f$ to 1 MHz with the same $L$ and $C$: $\Delta I=0.509$ A and $\Delta V_C=0.509/(8\times10^6\times22\times10^{-6})=2.9$ mV, a factor of four lower than 11.6 mV.

## Beyond the idealised formula

- **Spikes**: at the switch edges $L_{ESL}\,di/dt$ and the ringing of the SW node couple into $V_{out}$. These are not ripple at $f_{sw}$; they are high-frequency noise with edge-rate-dependent amplitude. Measure with a short ground spring.
- **Load-dependent mode**: in DCM the current ripple is set by the peak ($\Delta I=I_{pk}$) and voltage ripple behaves differently with pulse skipping.
- **Low-frequency "ripple"**: loop jitter or subharmonic oscillation shows up as modulation below $f_{sw}$, which is not captured by these formulas.
- **Resistive load** adds a ripple current $\Delta V/R$ in the load; negligible for a few mV.
- **Ceramic derating**: at 3.3 V, a 22 µF 0805 X5R may be 15 µF effective, so the capacitive ripple is 17 mV, not 11.6 mV.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> When asked "what is ripple?" lead with the definition (AC part of the state variable), then derive $\Delta I$ in two lines, then $\Delta V$ in two lines. Finish with one scaling law ("current ripple $\propto1/(Lf)$, voltage ripple $\propto1/(LCf^2)$ so frequency is the strongest lever") to show you can think in trade-offs.

## The ripple ratio is a design choice, not a constant

Engineers parametrize the inductor by the ripple ratio $r=\Delta I_L/I_{out}$. For the 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, 500 kHz example, with a 15 µF effective output capacitance, the trade-off looks like this:

| $r$ | $L$ | Peak current | $\Delta V_C$ | Peak stored energy $\tfrac12LI_{pk}^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | 12.0 µH | 2.20 A | 6.7 mV | 29 µJ |
| 0.3 | 8.0 µH | 2.30 A | 10.0 mV | 21 µJ |
| 0.4 | 6.0 µH | 2.40 A | 13.3 mV | 17 µJ |
| 0.6 | 4.0 µH | 2.60 A | 20.0 mV | 13 µJ |

A larger inductor lowers the ripple and the peak current, but the part is physically bigger (energy storage tracks size) and its transient response is slower. A smaller inductor shrinks the part and speeds up the loop, but needs more output capacitance for the same ripple and pushes the peak current up. The common choice of $r$ between 0.2 and 0.4 sits at the knee of this curve, and the RMS current barely moves (2.003 A to 2.013 A across that range), so conduction loss is not what decides it.

## Worst-case ripple: a tolerance stack

The nominal 12 mV from the example is the number you hand an interviewer, but the number you have to guarantee is much larger. Stack the realistic tolerances:

- Inductance: ±20% is typical, so take 3.76 µH as the worst case (assumed tolerance; check the part).
- Switching frequency: ±10% for an internal oscillator, so take 450 kHz.
- Input voltage: the highest $V_{in}$ gives the largest ripple. Use 24 V for a wide-range design.
- Capacitance: 15 µF effective at 3.3 V bias, minus a further 20% for tolerance and temperature, so 12 µF.

Then at 12 V in, $\Delta I_L=3.3\times0.725/(3.76\ \mu\text{H}\times450\ \text{kHz})=1.41$ A, so the peak is 2.71 A, not the nominal 2.51 A. At 24 V in, $\Delta I_L=1.68$ A and the peak is 2.84 A. The capacitive ripple at 24 V is $1.68/(8\times450\times10^3\times12\times10^{-6})=38.9$ mV, and the ESR term adds $1.68\times0.005=8.4$ mV, so the arithmetic bound is about 47 mV. That is four times the 12 mV nominal figure, and it shows why a ripple spec is verified at corners and not at the typical condition. It also sets the inductor's saturation rating (2.84 A plus margin) and the current-limit threshold, which must sit above the worst-case peak or the converter will current-limit in normal operation.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The ±20% inductance, ±10% frequency and ±20% capacitance figures are plausible representative tolerances. The datasheets for the specific inductor, controller and capacitor give the real limits.

## Where else the ripple shows up

The current and voltage ripple are the same event seen at different nodes, and each has a consequence: current ripple drives the inductor's core loss and peak rating, voltage ripple drives the load's supply noise and the ADC or RF sensitivity, and the input-side pulses drive EMI ([[q0193]]). A ripple that is acceptable to a digital rail can be unusable for an ADC reference, which is why precision rails follow the buck with an LDO or a filter.

## Verifying the ripple numbers on the bench

- Put a current probe on the inductor and read the peak-to-peak directly, then repeat at minimum and maximum input voltage. Check that the ripple rises with $V_{in}$ and does not change with load in CCM.
- Measure the voltage ripple across the capacitor pads with a tip-and-barrel probe and a 20 MHz bandwidth limit, AC-coupled. Report the figure with the bandwidth stated, since an unlimited bandwidth view includes edge spikes that are not ripple.
- If the measured ripple is twice the calculation, check the usual suspects: capacitor derating at the actual bias, a different effective $L$ near saturation, a lower switching frequency than assumed, or a probe loop picking up the SW field.
:::

::: applications
- **Inductor selection** by ripple ratio and peak current ($I_{out}+\Delta I/2<I_{sat}$).
- **Output capacitor selection** for rails feeding ADCs, PLLs and RF (tens of µV to mV budgets).
- **Efficiency trade-offs**: higher $f$ means lower ripple but more switching loss.
- **Core supplies** where ripple must stay below a percentage of $V_{out}$ (tens of mV on 1 V rails).
- **EMC planning**: the input ripple current and the SW edges define the emissions to be filtered.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM, steady state**, constant duty cycle.
- **Small-ripple approximation** ($V_{out}$ treated as constant in computing $\Delta I$).
- **Ideal $L$ and $C$** apart from ESR: no DC-bias derating, saturation or ESL.
- **Constant load current**; a resistive load or a dynamic load changes the picture slightly.
- **In DCM** the formulas for $\Delta I$ change ($\Delta I$ becomes the peak current) and $D$ is no longer $V_{out}/V_{in}$.
- Ripple can mean RMS or peak-to-peak; here peak-to-peak unless stated.
:::

::: followups
### What is a good ripple ratio and why?
About 0.2 to 0.4. Lower means a larger, costlier inductor and slow transient response; higher means higher peak current, more core and AC copper loss, higher output ripple and a risk of DCM at moderate load. A ratio around 0.3 balances those. Very high ratios push the DCM boundary up: at $r=1$ the converter already enters DCM below half load, since the boundary is $I_{out}=\Delta I_L/2$.

### How would you reduce output ripple without changing the capacitor?
Reduce $\Delta I_L$ (larger $L$ or higher $f_{sw}$), lower the ESR (parallel capacitors), add an LC post-filter, or interleave phases (ripple cancellation, see multiphase buck). Raising $f_{sw}$ is the most powerful because $\Delta V_C\propto1/f^2$, at the cost of switching loss.

### Why is ripple current worst at max input voltage?
$\Delta I=V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf)$ and $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$, so a larger $V_{in}$ means smaller $D$, longer off time, and more time for the current to fall at $V_{out}/L$ and then to be restored. The off time lengthens as $V_{in}$ rises, so peak-to-peak increases. Size $L$ and $I_{sat}$ at $V_{in,max}$.

### The measured ripple is 40 mV where I calculated 12 mV. What could be wrong?
DC-bias derating of the ceramic (less capacitance), high ESR, ESL/loop spikes from the probing or layout, smaller inductance than assumed (tolerance, saturation), a lower switching frequency than you assumed, or measuring with a long ground lead. Re-measure with a spring tip right at the capacitor.

### Harder: how does ripple behave at light load in a PFM or DCM buck?
In DCM the inductor ramps to a peak set by the on time, so $\Delta I=I_{pk}$ and each burst delivers a fixed charge. Output ripple is dominated by the pulse (charge $/C$) and, in PFM, by the lower-frequency envelope between bursts, which can be several times the CCM ripple; at very light load the burst rate can even fall into the audible range.
