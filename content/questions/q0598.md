::: basics
### In plain words
Parts reach a board in two basic ways. Through-hole parts have wire legs that pass through drilled holes and are soldered on the other side. Surface-mount parts sit flat on pads and are soldered on the same side. Surface-mount is smaller, cheaper at volume and better at high frequency. Through-hole is stronger against pulling and bending.

### Picture it
Think of two ways to fix something to a wall. A sticker lies flat, goes on fast, and takes almost no room. A nail goes right through and holds much harder when someone yanks on it. Small electronic parts are fine as stickers. A plug that people shove and pull needs the nail. The analogy has one limit: the wire legs of a through-hole part also add electrical side effects, which a sticker-style part mostly avoids.

### Words you need
- **Surface-mount (SMT)**: parts soldered flat onto pads on the board surface.
- **Through-hole (THT)**: parts with legs that go through holes in the board.
- **Pad**: the exposed copper patch where a part is soldered.
- **Lead**: the metal leg or terminal of a part.
- **Inductance**: a wire's resistance to fast changes in current. A longer wire has more, and it matters at high frequency.

### The simplest example
A straight 10 mm lead has about 7 nH of inductance, and a small surface-mount capacitor has about 1 nH. At 100 MHz, 7 nH opposes current by about 4.4 Ω, while 1 nH opposes it by only about 0.6 Ω. So a decoupling capacitor on long legs helps much less at high frequency. But a connector that gets plugged and unplugged all day is better held by through-hole legs, because small surface pads can tear off. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page compares the two on size, cost, strength, heat and repair, and shows when to mix them.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can compare two technologies on *several axes* instead of saying "SMD is smaller." The interviewer wants size and density, cost and automation, parasitics at high frequency, mechanical strength, thermal behaviour, repairability and prototyping, and when you would still choose through-hole (connectors, high mechanical stress, high-power parts). A good answer ends with a recommendation tied to a scenario.
:::

::: why
Every part you pick has a package, and the package choice decides how the board is built, how much it costs, how it behaves above 100 MHz and whether it survives a cable being yanked. Interns pick between a 0402 resistor and an axial one, or between an SMD and a through-hole connector, all the time. The answer also links to assembly steps ([[q0599]], [[q0602]], [[q0603]]) and to DFM ([[q0613]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Through-hole technology (THT)**: leads pass through drilled, plated holes and are soldered on the opposite side, usually by wave or selective soldering ([[q0603]]).
- **Surface-mount technology (SMT)**: components sit on pads on the surface and are soldered by reflow ([[q0602]]).
- **Parasitic inductance and capacitance**: package leads and loops add L and C; see decoupling and ESL.
- **Mechanical strength**: through-hole leads anchor into the board; SMD joints bear load only through pad adhesion and solder.
- **Thermal mass and power**: through-hole leads and bodies can shed heat; many SMD power packages rely on copper area.
- **Mixed technology**: boards that combine both. See [[q0599]].
:::

::: answer
"Through-hole parts have leads that go through holes in the board and get soldered on the other side. Surface-mount parts sit flat on pads and are soldered on the same side by reflow.

SMT wins on size and density, because parts are much smaller, can sit on both sides of the board, and don't need drilled holes under every pin. It also wins on cost at volume because pick-and-place machines are very fast, and on high-frequency performance, because there are no long leads: a 10 mm straight lead adds roughly 7 nH of inductance, while an SMD part has something like one nanohenry, which matters in decoupling and RF.

Through-hole wins on mechanical strength, so you still see it on connectors, switches, large electrolytic capacitors, transformers and heavy inductors, anything that gets plugged, pulled or vibrated. It's also easier to prototype and rework by hand, and it can handle high power with big leads.

So for a normal modern design I'd go SMT for everything electrical, and through-hole only for parts that take mechanical abuse or carry large current, and I'd try to keep that to one side of the board so only one soldering step is needed."
:::

::: technical
**Comparison.**

| Attribute | SMT | Through-hole |
|---|---|---|
| Size / density | Small (0402 about 1.0 x 0.5 mm, 0201 about 0.6 x 0.3 mm); both sides usable | Large; holes block routing on every layer |
| Assembly | Print, place, reflow; fully automatic, very fast | Insertion (auto or manual) then wave / selective / hand solder |
| Cost at volume | Lower per part and per placement | Higher; drilling, insertion, extra solder step |
| High-frequency behaviour | Low lead inductance (~0.5-1 nH for small chips) | Long leads, ~7 nH for a 10 mm lead (computed from the straight-wire formula) |
| Mechanical strength | Joint is pad + fillet; weak under stress or vibration | Lead anchored in plated hole; strong |
| Power / thermal | Needs copper area; limited by package | Large leads and heatsinks easy to attach |
| Rework | Needs hot air or hot tweezers, steady hands for small parts | Soldering iron and desoldering braid are enough |
| Prototyping | Harder by hand (0402 and smaller), breadboard-incompatible | Easy, breadboard-friendly |
| Inspection | AOI; hidden joints (BGA, QFN) need X-ray | Joints visible; AOI straightforward |

**Why leads cost inductance.** A straight round wire of length $\ell$ and radius $r$ has approximately $L \approx 2\times10^{-7}\,\ell\,[\ln(2\ell/r) - 0.75]$ H (SI units). For $\ell = 10$ mm, $r = 0.25$ mm this gives about 7.3 nH, so at 100 MHz its reactance is $2\pi f L \approx 4.6\ \Omega$, comparable to the whole impedance budget of a power-delivery network. A small chip capacitor has an equivalent series inductance (ESL) of under 1 nH.

**Self-resonance example.** A 100 nF capacitor with 1 nH ESL self-resonates at $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC}) = 15.9$ MHz; with 7 nH of lead inductance it resonates at about 6 MHz. Above that frequency the part is an inductor, not a capacitor.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> ESL depends on package size, mounting (pad shape, via placement) and manufacturer. 0.5-1 nH for small chip parts and about 1 nH/mm of lead are rules of thumb, not guarantees; check the part's impedance curve or S-parameters in the datasheet.
:::

::: short
"Through-hole parts have leads that go through the board and are soldered on the back; surface-mount parts sit on pads on the surface and are reflow soldered. SMT is smaller, denser, cheaper at volume because it's fully automated, and better at high frequency because it has almost no lead inductance. Through-hole is mechanically stronger and easier to prototype and rework, so I'd still use it for connectors, large power parts and anything under mechanical stress. A modern board is mostly SMT with a few through-hole parts."
:::

::: long
"The difference is how a part attaches to the board. Through-hole parts, like an axial resistor or a pin header, have leads that go through plated holes and get soldered on the far side. Surface-mount parts sit flat on copper pads and are soldered in place, normally by reflow.

On the SMT side, the advantages come from size and automation. A chip resistor is a millimetre long instead of a centimetre, so you get far more parts per area, you can use both sides of the board, and you don't lose routing space to holes on every layer. The pick-and-place machine can lay down tens of thousands of parts per hour, and everything goes through the oven in one pass, so cost at volume is low. It's also better electrically: a long through-hole lead adds roughly a nanohenry per millimetre, so a 10 mm lead is about 7 nH, which moves a decoupling capacitor's self-resonance from around 16 MHz down to around 5 or 6 MHz. Short SMD terminations keep that inductance low.

Through-hole's advantage is mechanical and practical. The lead is anchored in the plated barrel, so connectors, switches and big electrolytic caps survive being plugged, pushed and shaken, where an SMD pad might tear off the board. High-current or high-voltage parts with heavy leads are easier too. And it's easy to prototype and rework with an iron, and breadboard-friendly.

The trade-offs are: SMT needs better assembly tools and inspection; through-hole costs board area, a wave or selective soldering step and often hand work. So I'd go SMT by default and keep through-hole for the parts that need strength or power."
:::

::: terms
- **THT**: through-hole technology.
- **SMT / SMD**: surface-mount technology / device.
- **Footprint / land pattern**: copper pad layout for a part.
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance; the parasitic inductance of a capacitor, including leads.
- **Pitch**: centre-to-centre spacing of leads or pads.
- **Wave / selective soldering**: methods for soldering through-hole leads ([[q0603]]).
- **Reflow**: melting printed solder paste in an oven ([[q0602]]).
- **Mixed assembly**: boards with both SMT and THT parts.
- **Pin-in-paste**: reflow soldering of through-hole parts using paste printed over the holes.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "SMD is smaller and through-hole is bigger."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Compares along several axes: density, cost and automation, high-frequency parasitics, mechanical strength, power handling, rework and inspection.
- Gives a number for the electrical difference (e.g. about 7 nH for a 10 mm lead vs about 1 nH) and what it does (self-resonance of a decoupling cap).
- Names when through-hole still wins: connectors, high-strength joints, large power parts, prototypes and education.
- Mentions mixed assembly and how it affects process steps and cost ([[q0599]]).

:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying through-hole is obsolete.** Connectors, transformers, electrolytics and power components still use it.
- **Claiming SMT is always cheaper.** For a handful of prototypes with no stencil or placer, through-hole by hand can be cheaper and faster.
- **Ignoring parasitics.** A decoupling capacitor on long leads or long traces can be useless above a few MHz.
- **Thinking SMT can't handle power.** Power SMD packages with thermal pads work well if there is copper to spread heat.
- **Forgetting mechanical stress.** A big SMD connector with only small pads can rip off; add through-hole anchor tabs or mounting posts.
- **Mixing up soldering methods**: wave for through-hole and glued SMT; reflow for SMT.
:::

::: deep
## Why the two technologies differ at a fundamental level

A solder joint has two jobs: **electrical continuity** and **mechanical attachment**. In through-hole, those are separate. The lead gives mechanical locking (shaped like a nail in a hole), and the solder fills the barrel for electrical and additional mechanical contact. In SMT, solder alone does both jobs on a thin, flat interface. That explains nearly every difference in the table.

### Mechanical strength

A through-hole pin soldered in a plated barrel resists pull and shear through the whole barrel, with the lead and barrel acting as a pin. An SMD joint relies on fillet area. Mechanical load is resisted by pad adhesion to the laminate and the solder shear strength, and when a connector gets yanked the failure is usually pad cratering or pad lift. That's why a USB-C receptacle or a heavy header uses through-hole shell tabs even though its signal pins are SMD.

### Electrical behaviour

Every straight conductor has inductance. For a round wire of length $\ell$ and radius $r$:

$$L \approx 2\times10^{-7}\,\ell\left[\ln\frac{2\ell}{r}-0.75\right]\ \text{H}\quad(\ell, r \text{ in metres})$$

Using $r=0.25$ mm: a 5 mm lead is about 2.9 nH, 10 mm is about 7.3 nH (computed). The reactance at 100 MHz of 10 nH is $X_L = 2\pi \cdot 10^{8}\cdot 10^{-8} = 6.3\ \Omega$, while for 1 nH it is 0.63 Ω. Compare with a target power-rail impedance of tens of mΩ and you see why lead inductance matters.

### Density and cost

A 0402 resistor's land pattern is on the order of 1 mm², an axial resistor with a 10 mm lead pitch consumes more like 25 mm², over 20x more (rough estimates, not a standard). In addition, through-hole parts block routing on *every* layer because the hole goes all the way through. For a 1,000-part board, that is a huge amount of board area, and area is cost.

On the assembly line, SMT parts are placed at tens of thousands per hour ([[q0600]]) and soldered in one oven pass ([[q0602]]). Through-hole requires insertion (auto-inserters for axial/radial parts, or manual) and then a soldering step ([[q0603]]) plus often manual touch-up. Labour is the cost driver.

### Thermal

SMD parts transfer heat to the board mainly through pads and thermal vias; so the board copper must be designed as the heatsink. Through-hole power parts (TO-220, TO-247) can clamp to a heatsink and use the big leads as thermal paths. SMT power packages (D2PAK, DPAK, PowerPAK) work well but need copper area and thermal vias.

### Process interactions

- SMT parts on the **bottom** side that pass through a wave need glue and must be wave-compatible ([[q0603]], [[q0599]]).
- Through-hole parts on the top side need to survive the reflow heat if they sit there during SMT reflow, or be placed after.
- Hybrid solutions: **pin-in-paste** (print paste into holes, insert the lead, reflow) avoids the wave step, and **selective soldering** nozzles solder individual through-hole parts.

## Choosing: a quick rubric

1. Electrical signal or passive: SMT, unless an unusual voltage/current/size forces otherwise.
2. User-facing or mechanically stressed connector: through-hole (or SMT with through-hole anchors).
3. High-current, high-power or high-voltage with big leads: through-hole or heavy SMT with copper.
4. Hand-assembled prototype or education kit: through-hole or large SMD (0805 and up).
5. Product at volume: all-SMT and one reflow pass per side; any through-hole should be minimised and placed on one side.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> When asked "any tradeoffs?", tie your answer to a scenario: "For a 5 mm x 5 mm wearable sensor board I'd use 0402 parts and SMT everywhere, including the connector, because space and automated assembly dominate. For a bench power supply with a 5 A terminal block and a big input capacitor I'd use through-hole for those two."

## Worked example: one load step, two mounting styles

Take a 1.8 V core rail that must stay within ±5% (±90 mV) when the load steps by 1 A in 2 ns. That is $dI/dt = 0.5$ A/ns. The first thing the load sees is whatever sits in the loop between it and the nearest capacitor, and that loop is mostly inductance. The voltage across an inductor is $v = L\,dI/dt$, so:

- **Short SMD path, $L = 1$ nH** (small chip cap plus pad and via): $v = 1\text{ nH} \times 0.5\text{ A/ns} = 0.5$ V. That is already far outside the 90 mV budget, which tells you one capacitor is not enough and the loop needs to be even smaller or the current needs to be shared among several parts.
- **10 mm axial or radial leads, $L \approx 7.3$ nH** (the formula above with $r = 0.25$ mm): $v = 3.65$ V. That is larger than the rail itself, so the model has stopped being physical, but the message is right: the decoupling is useless for that edge.

The same budget in the frequency domain: a target impedance of $Z_t = 90\text{ mV}/1\text{ A} = 90$ mΩ. An inductor reaches 90 mΩ at $f = Z_t/(2\pi L)$, which is 14.3 MHz for 1 nH and 1.96 MHz for 7.3 nH. Above those frequencies the capacitor's own inductance, not its capacitance, is what the load sees. Mounting style moves the usable bandwidth of a decoupling capacitor by a factor of about seven, and nothing you do with capacitance value can recover it.

## Where the simple comparison breaks

**"SMD has lower inductance" is really "SMD has a smaller loop".** A chip capacitor placed 10 mm from the IC with long traces to a distant via can be worse than a short-lead part pressed right against the pins. What matters is the area enclosed by the current loop through the capacitor, the IC and the plane. SMT makes a small loop easy, because you can put the part within a few millimeters and drop vias beside its pads. It does not make it automatic.

**Thermal cycling is harder on SMT.** A leaded joint can flex because the lead bends. A leadless chip resistor or ceramic capacitor is bonded rigidly between two pads, and FR-4 expands much more with temperature than alumina or ceramic dielectric does. Over many temperature cycles the solder takes the strain and fatigues (the Coffin-Manson picture: cycles to failure fall as strain range rises). Large ceramic chips and leadless packages are the usual victims, which is why automotive designs derate package size and add stress-relief features. Through-hole joints are not immune, but the barrel and lead share the stress.

**Ceramic parts crack from board flex.** A large MLCC near a board edge or screw hole can crack when the board is depanelized or bent. The crack can be a hidden short that shows up only later. Through-hole parts do not have this failure, one reason power-input filters and bulk capacitors near connectors are sometimes still leaded.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Whether a given product needs SMT-specific stress mitigation (flexible terminations, orientation relative to the board edge, package derating) depends on the customer's reliability class and the capacitor vendor's guidance. Treat the points above as the mechanism, and read the part's application notes for the numbers.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Measure the part, not the schematic.** Use a VNA with a two-port shunt-through measurement (the standard low-impedance method), or an impedance analyzer, on the capacitor soldered to a test board. Read the self-resonant frequency off the notch and back out ESL with $L = 1/((2\pi f_0)^2 C)$. For a 100 nF part with a notch at 15.9 MHz that returns 1.0 nH.
2. **Measure the mounting.** Compare the same capacitor with short vias against a version with the vias moved a few millimeters away. The notch moves down in frequency as $1/\sqrt{L}$; back out $L$ from each notch, and the difference is the extra inductance of the longer loop, which tells you how much of the total is the part and how much is your layout.
3. **Look at the real edge.** Probe the rail at the IC with a short ground spring rather than a long clip lead (see [[q0618]]). The ringing frequency on the load step is set by loop inductance and local capacitance, and it moves when you change the mounting.
4. **Mechanical checks.** For connector joints, a pull or push test on a few boards at the target force is a legitimate pass/fail check. For solder fatigue, thermal cycling a few boards while monitoring continuity is how assemblers qualify a design.

::: applications
- **Smartphone and wearable boards**: all-SMT, both sides, 0201 passives, BGAs.
- **Power supplies**: SMT control section; through-hole bulk capacitors, transformers and terminals.
- **Arduino-style hobby boards**: mixes SMT MCU with through-hole headers for easy access.
- **Automotive connectors**: SMD signal pins with through-hole shell for strain relief.
- **Education kits**: through-hole to make soldering feasible.
- **RF boards**: SMT for low parasitics; through-hole used rarely (e.g. SMA connectors with through-hole center pins).
:::

::: assumptions
- Generic numbers (ESL, lead inductance, area ratio) are rules of thumb; exact values depend on part geometry and layout.
- Assumes lead-free soldering and standard parts; exotic packages (press-fit, compliant pins) have their own trade-offs.
- Assumes volume production. For one-off builds, the cost and automation comparisons flip.
- Press-fit and hybrid connectors blur the line: they are through-hole mechanically but need no solder.
:::

::: followups
### Why does lead inductance matter for decoupling?
A decoupling capacitor supplies current for fast edges. Above its self-resonant frequency (set by ESL and C) it looks inductive, so it can't supply the fast current. Long leads add nanohenries and move the resonance down, for example a 100 nF part goes from about 16 MHz at 1 nH to about 6 MHz at 7 nH. Place SMD capacitors close to the pins with short vias.

### Name three parts you'd keep through-hole in a modern design and why.
Connectors that get plugged repeatedly (mechanical strength), large aluminium electrolytic or film capacitors and transformers (size, weight, leads), and high-current terminal blocks or TO-247 power devices that bolt to a heatsink.

### A board has SMT on both sides and a few through-hole connectors. How do you solder it?
Reflow both SMT sides first (heavier or fragile parts on the second pass or glued), then solder the connectors by selective soldering or by hand, or use pin-in-paste so they go through reflow with the SMT parts, because wave soldering would need the bottom SMT parts glued and wave-compatible. See [[q0599]].

### Why are BGAs and QFNs harder than through-hole parts to inspect?
The joints are hidden under the package, so AOI can't see them. You use X-ray inspection and electrical tests, such as boundary scan, for coverage. See [[q0608]].

### Your board fails a vibration test and the SMD connector detaches. What do you change?
Add mechanical retention: through-hole mounting tabs or screws, larger anchor pads, strain relief on the cable, and underfill or adhesive for heavy parts. It is a classic place to use hybrid SMD signal pins with through-hole anchors.
:::
