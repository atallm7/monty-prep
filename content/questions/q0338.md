::: testing
Whether you can describe what is actually **happening physically** at the two extremes of steady state, not just quote the currents. The hidden sub-skills:

1. In DC steady state $dI/dt = 0$, so the inductor drops no voltage: the coil is just $R$ (plus back-EMF for a motor).
2. Maximum current is **stall**: speed zero, no back-EMF, torque at maximum, **all input power becomes heat** and the winding temperature climbs. Minimum is **no load**: high speed, back-EMF almost equal to the supply, small current, power goes to friction.
3. You can attach numbers (current, torque, speed, power, heating rate) and the consequences (burnout time, efficiency, stored magnetic energy).
:::

::: why
These two operating points bound everything: the driver and supply are sized for the maximum, the idle power and battery life are set by the minimum, and thermal protection exists to handle the stalled state. Interviewers phrase it as "what's happening at min and max current" to see if you can describe the energy flows (electrical in, mechanical out, heat), not only the numbers. The same picture applies to a solenoid, where the "maximum" is simply the holding steady state.
:::

::: concepts
- **DC steady state of an inductor**: $v_L = L\,di/dt = 0$, so it behaves like a wire. See [[q0010]].
- **Motor equation**: $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$; torque $K_tI$. See [[q0336]].
- **Power flow**: $VI = I^2R + K_e\omega I$ (+ friction). See [[q0334]].
- **Copper loss and heating**: $I^2R$ into the winding's thermal mass.
- **Stored magnetic energy**: $\tfrac12LI^2$. See [[q0335]].
:::

::: answer
"At steady state the current isn't changing, so the inductor drops no voltage and the winding is basically its resistance, plus the back-EMF for a motor. There are two extremes.

Maximum current is the stalled case: the shaft isn't turning, so there's no back-EMF, the current is V over R, and the torque is at its maximum. Nothing moves, so no mechanical power is produced and all the electrical power, V squared over R, goes into heating the winding. With 12 V and 1 ohm that's 12 amps and 144 watts into a few tens of grams of copper, which heats at tens of degrees per second, so it burns out in seconds if you don't limit it. The magnetic field is at its strongest too, storing half L I squared.

Minimum current is free-running with no load. The motor is at its highest speed, the back-EMF is nearly equal to the supply, and the current is just enough to overcome friction, maybe half an amp. Almost all the input power goes to friction and windage, not useful work, so efficiency is zero there too. Efficiency peaks somewhere in between.

For a solenoid it's the same logic: steady state is just V over R, held current, with all the power as heat, unless you reduce the voltage after pull-in."
:::

::: technical
**Steady-state equations.** With $di/dt = 0$ and $d\omega/dt = 0$:

$$V = IR + K_e\omega,\qquad K_tI = \tau_L + \tau_f$$

**Maximum current (stall, $\omega = 0$).**

- $I = V/R = 12$ A, $\tau_{stall} = K_tV/R = 0.24$ N·m, back-EMF $= 0$.
- Mechanical output $= 0$. Input $P = V^2/R = 144$ W, all heat in the winding.
- Heating rate for ~20 g of copper ($c = 385$ J/kg·K): $144/(0.02\times385) \approx 18.7$ K/s, so 105 K in about 5.6 s (adiabatic; real motors have more thermal mass and some conduction, so a few seconds to a minute).
- Stored magnetic energy $\tfrac12LI^2 = \tfrac12\times10^{-3}\times144 = 72$ mJ.
- Hot resistance rises (about 0.39%/K), so current falls a little as it heats.

**Minimum current (no load, free-running).**

- $I_{nl} = \tau_f/K_t = 0.5$ A, $\omega_{nl} = (V - I_{nl}R)/K_e = 575$ rad/s, back-EMF $= 11.5$ V.
- Input $= 6$ W: copper $0.25$ W, friction and windage $5.75$ W, useful output zero.
- Stored energy $= \tfrac12\times10^{-3}\times0.25 = 0.125$ mJ.

**Between the two.** The loaded motor runs with $I = (\tau_L+\tau_f)/K_t$; mechanical power $P_{mech} = K_e\omega I$ is maximum at half the stall current and half the no-load speed ($\omega = V/(2K_e) = 300$ rad/s): $P_{max} = V^2/(4R) = 36$ W, efficiency 50% there (ignoring friction). Peak *efficiency* occurs at a lower current.

**Solenoid.** Maximum: $I = V/R$ with the plunger seated, $dI/dt = 0$, coil power $V^2/R$ continuously (12 W for 24 V and 48 Ω), stored energy $\tfrac12L_{closed}I^2$. Minimum: off, or the hold current after pull-in (PWM/economizer).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Real thermal time-to-failure depends on the motor's thermal mass, insulation class (often 130 °C to 180 °C) and airflow. The 5.6 s figure is an adiabatic estimate for 20 g of copper only.
:::

::: long
"First, what steady state means: all currents and speeds are constant. For the winding that means di/dt is zero, so the inductor drops zero volts and behaves like a plain wire. All that's left is the winding resistance and, for a motor, the back-EMF. So V equals I R plus K_e omega.

At maximum current the motor is stalled. Speed zero, so no back-EMF, the current is V over R: 12 amps for 12 volts and 1 ohm. Torque is K_t times that, the maximum the motor can produce, 0.24 newton-metres here. But since nothing moves, the mechanical output power is zero. All 144 watts of input power goes into heating the copper. A 20 gram winding heats at about 19 degrees per second, so it reaches insulation limits in a few seconds. The magnetic field is also at its strongest, storing half L I squared, 72 millijoules in this example.

At minimum current the motor is running free at its top speed. The back-EMF is about 11.5 volts against a 12 volt supply, so only half a volt is across the resistor and the current is about 0.5 amps, just enough torque to overcome friction and windage. The 6 watts of input power goes mostly into friction and a quarter-watt into copper. There's no useful output, so efficiency is zero here as well. In the middle, maximum mechanical power is at half the stall current and half the free speed, and peak efficiency sits at a lower current than that.

A solenoid has no speed term, so its steady state is V over R with the plunger closed and all the power dissipated as heat. The way to get a minimum is to reduce the average voltage after it has pulled in."
:::

::: terms
- **Steady state**: all currents and speeds constant, so $di/dt = 0$.
- **Stall**: shaft stationary under power, $\omega = 0$.
- **No-load speed**: free-running speed with zero external load torque.
- **Locked-rotor torque (stall torque)**: $K_tV/R$.
- **Copper loss**: $I^2R$ heat in the winding.
- **Mechanical power**: $\tau\omega = K_e\omega I$.
- **Stored magnetic energy**: $\tfrac12LI^2$.
- **Insulation class**: maximum winding temperature rating (for example 130 °C or 155 °C).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "max at stall, min at no load" with a sentence on each.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Begins with the **steady-state definition** and notes the inductor is a short at DC.
- Gives **both operating points with the full energy picture** (input, copper, mechanical, friction) and numbers.
- Explains that stall is **100% heat** and computes a **heating rate or burnout time**.
- Notes that **no load is also 0% efficient**, that maximum power is at half stall current, and peak efficiency is lower.
- Mentions the **stored magnetic energy** at each extreme, linking to flyback ([[q0340]]).
- Extends to the solenoid (no speed term, always $V/R$ at DC) and design mitigation (PWM hold, current limit, thermal cutoff).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"At stall the motor has maximum power output."** Output is zero at stall; maximum *mechanical* power is at half speed.
- **"No-load means no loss."** Friction, windage and copper still dissipate the input.
- **"Inductance limits the steady-state current."** At DC steady state the inductor has no effect; resistance and back-EMF decide.
- **Forgetting temperature.** A hot winding has higher $R$ and lower stall current; the cold stall current is the worst case for the supply.
- **Equating high current with high power output.** At stall almost all of it is heat.
- **Believing a stall for "just a moment" is harmless.** Seconds can be enough to cook small windings.
:::

## The two operating points, side by side

| Quantity | Stall (max current) | No load (min current) |
|---|---|---|
| Speed $\omega$ | 0 | 575 rad/s (5490 rpm) |
| Back-EMF $K_e\omega$ | 0 V | 11.5 V |
| Current $I$ | 12 A | 0.5 A |
| Torque | 0.24 N·m | 0.01 N·m (friction only) |
| Input power $VI$ | 144 W | 6 W |
| Copper loss $I^2R$ | 144 W | 0.25 W |
| Mechanical output | 0 W | 0 W (useful) |
| Stored energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ | 72 mJ | 0.125 mJ |
| Efficiency | 0% | 0% |

## Where the power goes along the way

Ignoring friction, the three power terms along the speed axis are

$$P_{in} = V\frac{V - K_e\omega}{R},\quad P_{cu} = \frac{(V - K_e\omega)^2}{R},\quad P_{mech} = K_e\omega\,\frac{V - K_e\omega}{R}$$

## What the stalled coil is doing

At stall nothing converts electrical energy to mechanical: the rotor does not move, no work is done, and $E\cdot I = 0$. The supply pushes a steady current through a resistance:

$$P = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}$$

and the winding temperature follows $mc\,\dot T = P - P_{loss}$. For 20 g of copper with no cooling the rise is 18.7 K/s; for a real motor the steel and housing add thermal mass (slower), but heat is concentrated in the winding, so hot spots appear within seconds. Insulation class limits (130 to 180 °C) set a stall time; hobby motors can often only tolerate a few seconds, and stalled servo motors get hot enough to smell.

## Steady state means the inductor disappears

In the steady state the equation $V = IR + L\,dI/dt + K_e\omega$ loses its derivative term. This is why DC current at stall is just $V/R$ and why inductance affects only *how fast* you get there. L still matters for what happens when you switch off (the stored energy is $72$ mJ at stall), where the current cannot stop instantly and you get flyback ([[q0340]]).

## The solenoid's two extremes

For a DC solenoid there is no motion-dependent term, so:

- **Maximum steady current:** $I = V/R$ (plunger seated), power $V^2/R$ continuous. For 24 V and 48 Ω: 0.5 A, 12 W, stored energy $\tfrac12L_{closed}I^2$ (with $L_{closed} = 0.15$ H, 18.8 mJ).
- **Minimum:** zero when off; when held, whatever the average hold current is. Since the closed magnetic circuit makes force large for small current, you can hold with perhaps 20 to 40% of the pull-in current (design-specific), cutting power to a few percent.

The inductance when the plunger is closed is larger, so the time constant is longer: $\tau = L_{closed}/R = 3.1$ ms for $0.15$ H and 48 Ω, which sets how fast the current decays in the off state too.

## Using the steady-state view in design

- **Supply and driver:** size for the maximum (cold stall) current, or enforce a current limit and size for that.
- **Thermal:** if stall can last seconds, add a thermal cutoff or current limit with time-out; derive stall-time limit from $mc\,\Delta T/P$.
- **Idle power:** the minimum current sets battery life when running unloaded; reduce friction, voltage or duty cycle.
- **Sensing:** use current to infer load: near the minimum means unloaded; near the maximum means stalled or jammed.
:::

::: applications
- **Stall detection** in robots and window lifts: monitor current rise toward $V/R$ and stop the motor.
- **Servo and gearmotor protection**: thermal cutoffs and stall timers.
- **Solenoid hold optimization**: PWM economizer after pull-in to reduce heat and power.
- **Battery-life estimation**: idle current of motors and fans at no load.
- **Motor specification**: reading the datasheet's no-load current/speed and stall current/torque, which are exactly these two operating points.
:::

- The supply is ideal and can source the stall current without sagging.
- AC motors/solenoids differ (impedance, power factor); this is DC steady state.

::: followups
### What's the mechanical output power at stall, and why?
Zero: $P_{mech} = \tau\omega$ and $\omega = 0$. The motor produces torque (maximum) but no motion, so no work is done and the input power becomes heat.

### At what speed is mechanical output maximum, and what's the efficiency there?
At half the ideal speed ($V/(2K_e) = 300$ rad/s), where the current is half of stall (6 A). The output is $V^2/(4R) = 36$ W and the efficiency (ignoring friction) is 50%: half the input power goes to copper loss.

### How long can the motor stay stalled?
Estimate with $t = mc\,\Delta T/P$ for the winding: 20 g copper, 105 K rise, 144 W gives about 5.6 s (adiabatic). Real answers come from the motor's rated stall time or its thermal model; a controller should limit stall current, and a thermal switch should back it up.

### Why does the inductor not matter in steady state but matters at turn-off?
In steady state the current is constant so $L\,di/dt = 0$. At turn-off the current wants to fall quickly, $L\,di/dt$ is large and negative, producing a voltage spike; the energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ (72 mJ at stall) must be dissipated somewhere ([[q0340]]).

### Scenario: a servo is commanded to a position it cannot reach and starts to smell hot. What's happening and what do you fix?
The motor is stalled against the stop, drawing near $V/R$, and all that power is heat. Fixes: add a current limit or torque limit in the controller, a stall timer that cuts power, mechanical end stops that match software limits, and a thermal sensor for protection.

### How does temperature change the stall current?
Copper resistance rises 0.39% per K, so at 100 °C (75 K above 25 °C) $R$ is 29% higher and stall current drops from 12 A to about 9.3 A. Specify the supply for the *cold* stall current.
:::
