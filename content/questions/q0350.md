::: basics
### In plain words
A linear motor makes force in a straight line instead of torque on a shaft. It is like a normal motor that has been cut open and rolled out flat. A stationary track holds magnets, and a sliding part carries coils. Switching the coils in order pushes the slider along the track with no gears or screws in between.

### Picture it
Imagine slicing a cardboard tube lengthwise and unrolling it into a flat sheet. The curved wall of the tube becomes a straight strip. A linear motor does the same to a rotary motor: the circular stator becomes a straight track, and the rotor becomes a slider. The analogy has one catch: the flattened version needs a position sensor along the track, since there is no shaft to put an encoder on.

### Words you need
- **Forcer (slider)**: the moving part that carries the coils.
- **Track**: the stationary strip of alternating magnets.
- **Lorentz force**: the force on a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field, $F = BIL$.
- **Direct drive**: no gearbox, belt or screw between motor and load.
- **Linear encoder**: a sensor that reports position along the track.

### The simplest example
Suppose a wire 0.2 m long sits in a 0.5 T field and carries 10 A. The force is $F = BIL$ = 0.5 × 10 × 0.2 = **1 N**. Double the current to 20 A and the force doubles to 2 N. That straight-line push is what moves the slider, and the force is proportional to current just like torque in a rotary motor.

That is the whole idea: force from current in a magnetic field, laid out in a line. The rest of this page covers the benefits (no backlash, high speed), the drawbacks (heat, cost, strong magnet attraction) and examples like hard-drive voice coils.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can generalise from rotary machines to linear ones: a linear motor is an electric motor "cut open and unrolled", producing force and motion in a line with no gearbox, screw or belt. The interviewer wants the picture (forcer and magnet track), the equations ($F = K_fI$, $E = K_ev$), the trade-offs against rotary + lead screw (speed, accuracy, no backlash vs cost, heat, force limits, magnet attraction), and a few real examples (voice coil, maglev, wafer stages).
:::

::: why
Precision and high-speed motion systems (semiconductor equipment, pick-and-place machines, hard-drive heads, camera autofocus, lab automation) often use linear motors because converting rotation to linear motion adds backlash, compliance, friction and wear. A hardware engineer needs to know when direct drive is worth its cost, how to drive it (same drivers as rotary motors) and what new problems it brings (cooling, cable management, strong magnetic attraction, end stops).
:::

::: concepts
- **Rotary motor torque**: $T = K_tI$ from Lorentz force. See [[q0348]] and [[q0353]].
- **Lorentz force**: force on a conductor, $F = BIL$, is already linear; a rotary motor turns it into torque by geometry.
- **Back-EMF**: $E = K_ev$ in a linear motor, same as $K_e\omega$ in a rotary one. See [[q0341]].
- **Commutation**: a multi-phase linear motor needs a drive that switches coils with position, just like a BLDC ([[q0363]]).
- **Solenoid and voice coil**: the short-stroke linear actuators ([[q0347]]).
- **Mechanical transmission**: lead screw, belt and rack and pinion, which linear motors replace.
:::

::: answer
"A linear motor is basically a rotary motor that's been cut open and rolled out flat, so instead of torque on a shaft you get force along a line. There's a stationary part, usually a track of alternating magnets, and a moving part, the forcer or slider, which carries the coils. When you energise the coils in the right sequence, the interaction between the coil currents and the magnet field pushes the slider along the track. It's the same Lorentz force, $F = BIL$, so force is proportional to current and the moving coils generate a back-EMF proportional to speed, just like a rotary motor.

The big advantage is direct drive: no gearbox, lead screw or belt, so no backlash, much less friction and wear, and very high speed and acceleration with good positioning accuracy. The downsides are cost, heat (no gear reduction means you need high current for force), limited continuous force, strong attraction between iron-core forcers and magnets, and the need for a position sensor like a linear encoder.

Examples: the voice coil that moves a hard drive head, wafer stages in chip manufacturing, high-end pick-and-place machines, and maglev trains."

::: technical
**Construction.** Take a rotary PM synchronous machine, cut along an axial line, and flatten it. The stator becomes a flat **forcer** (coil assembly, often with an iron core) or, in "tubular" motors, a cylindrical coil stack; the rotor becomes a **magnet track** (alternating N-S permanent magnets) or a rod of magnets. Either part may move. Variants:
- **Linear synchronous / PM (brushless) motor**: magnet track and 3-phase forcer; most common in precision motion.
- **Linear induction motor (LIM)**: a forcer with 3-phase coils and a conductive plate (aluminium or copper over iron) as the "rotor"; no magnets. Used where long, cheap reaction rails are needed.
- **Voice coil actuator**: a single coil moving in a magnet gap, limited stroke.
- **Tubular linear motor**: coils around a magnetic rod; compact, no external magnetic field.

**Equations** (single-phase equivalent, brushless/voice-coil):
$$F = K_fI,\qquad E = K_ev,\qquad V = IR + L\frac{dI}{dt} + K_ev,\quad K_f = K_e\ (\text{N/A} = \text{V·s/m}).$$
Force-velocity: $F = K_f(V - K_ev)/R$. Stall force $K_fV/R$, no-load speed $V/K_e$, peak mechanical power $\tfrac14 F_{stall}v_{max}$.

**Example** ($K_f = 10$ N/A, $R = 4\ \Omega$, $V = 24$ V): $I_{stall} = 6$ A, $F_{stall} = 60$ N, $v_{max} = 2.4$ m/s, peak power 36 W. At 0.5 m/s, back-EMF = 5 V. A 2 A current gives 20 N.

**Rotary + lead screw equivalent**: $F = 2\pi\eta T/\ell$. With lead $\ell = 2$ mm, efficiency 40%, $T = 0.1$ N·m: $F = 126$ N. The screw gives *force multiplication* for free; a direct-drive motor must make that force electromagnetically, so it needs much more current and copper for the same force.

**Thermal and magnetic issues.** Force-per-watt is lower than a geared rotary system; continuous force is limited by winding temperature (water-cooling in high-end stages). Iron-core forcers have strong attractive force to the magnet track (often several times the rated thrust), plus cogging ripple; ironless forcers avoid both at the cost of lower force density.
:::

::: long
"I think of a linear motor as a rotary motor that's been cut along its length and unrolled. In a brushless rotary motor you have a stator with three-phase coils and a rotor with magnets. In the linear version you have a flat track of alternating north and south magnets, and a forcer, which carries the three-phase coils and slides along the track. Either one can be the moving part, but usually the coils move or the magnets are fixed.

The physics is the same Lorentz force, $F = BIL$. In a rotary motor the geometry turns it into torque; here it's already a straight force. So the force is proportional to current, $F = K_fI$, with $K_f$ in newtons per amp, and the moving coil generates a back-EMF proportional to velocity, $E = K_ev$. It even has the same voltage equation: $V = IR + L\,di/dt + K_ev$. For a typical example with 10 newtons per amp, 4 ohms and 24 volts, the stall force is 60 newtons and the top speed 2.4 metres per second.

The advantage is direct drive. Normally, to make linear motion from a rotary motor, you add a lead screw or belt, which brings backlash, friction, compliance and wear. A linear motor removes all that, so you can get high speed, high acceleration and very good accuracy with a linear encoder. The disadvantages are that you've lost the mechanical gear advantage, so you need a lot of current and heat management for a given force, the cost is high, iron-core versions have large magnetic attraction to the track, and you have to deal with cables to a moving part.

Where is it used? Semiconductor wafer stages, high-end pick-and-place, hard-drive voice coils, lab automation, and in the bigger picture, linear induction motors in maglev and roller coasters."
:::

::: terms
- **Forcer (slider)**: the part of a linear motor carrying the coils.
- **Magnet track (way)**: the row of alternating-polarity permanent magnets.
- **Force constant $K_f$**: N/A, equal to the back-EMF constant in V·s/m.
- **Direct drive**: load coupled to the motor without a transmission.
- **Voice coil actuator**: a coil in a magnet gap with limited stroke and a linear force-current relationship.
- **Linear induction motor (LIM)**: flattened induction motor; the reaction plate replaces the rotor.
- **Pole pitch**: the distance between adjacent magnets of opposite polarity.
- **Cogging / detent force**: ripple force from the interaction of iron teeth with magnets.
- **Linear encoder**: sensor that measures position along a scale.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "a motor that moves in a straight line". An exceptional answer:

- Describes the unrolled-rotary picture and the forcer/track structure, and names the types (PM synchronous, induction, voice coil, tubular).
- Gives $F = K_fI$, $E = K_ev$ and the stall-force / top-speed numbers, showing it is the rotary-motor model transplanted.
- Compares with rotary + lead screw using $F = 2\pi\eta T/\ell$ to show what you give up (mechanical advantage) and gain (no backlash, stiffness, speed).
- Mentions thermal limits, cooling, cogging, magnet attraction and the need for a linear encoder and a three-phase driver.
- Names real applications and a case where a linear motor is the wrong choice (holding a vertical load without power, large force at low cost).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking a solenoid is a linear motor.** A solenoid has short stroke and a force that varies strongly with position ([[q0347]]); a true linear motor has roughly constant force along its travel.
- **Forgetting the force constant equals the back-EMF constant.** $K_f$ in N/A equals $K_e$ in V·s/m, by energy conservation.
- **Ignoring cooling.** Without gearing, high force means high $I^2R$; continuous force is much less than peak force.
- **Claiming "no friction".** Linear bearings and guides still have friction and are needed to hold the air gap against magnetic attraction.
- **Forgetting position feedback.** Commutation and closed-loop control need a linear encoder or Hall sensors.
- **Using a linear motor to hold a vertical load with the power off.** It has no self-locking, unlike a worm gear or lead screw; gravity compensation or a brake is needed.
:::

## Cutting a rotary motor open

Consider a brushless rotary motor with a stator of three-phase coils and a rotor carrying alternating N-S magnets. Cut the stator along an axial line and flatten it: you get a row of coils (the forcer). Do the same to the rotor: a row of alternating magnets (the track). Because the stator's rotating magnetic field now becomes a **travelling wave**, the forcer and track interact exactly as before, but the force is along the line instead of around a circle.

The equivalent rotary-to-linear translation:

| Rotary | Linear |
|---|---|
| Torque $T$ (N·m) | Force $F$ (N) |
| Angular speed $\omega$ (rad/s) | Velocity $v$ (m/s) |
| $T = K_tI$ | $F = K_fI$ |
| $E = K_e\omega$ | $E = K_ev$ |
| Inertia $J$ (kg·m²) | Mass $m$ (kg) |
| Stall torque $K_tV/R$ | Stall force $K_fV/R$ |
| Pole pitch → synchronous speed $n_s = 120f/p$ | Synchronous velocity $v_s = 2\tau_pf$ (pole pitch $\tau_p$) |

For an induction-type LIM, $v_s = 2\tau_pf$. With a pole pitch of 0.1 m at 50 Hz, $v_s = 10$ m/s, and the LIM moves slightly slower because of slip.

## Force-velocity behaviour

Combine $V = IR + K_ev$ with $F = K_fI$:
$$F = \frac{K_f}{R}\left(V - K_ev\right).$$

## Comparison with rotary + lead screw

A lead screw of lead $\ell$ converts torque to force: $F = 2\pi\eta T/\ell$. With $\ell = 2$ mm, $\eta = 40\%$ and $T = 0.1$ N·m, $F = 126$ N, much more than our 60 N direct-drive motor's stall force from the same electrical power class. The screw is a mechanical gear of ratio $2\pi/\ell \approx 3140$ rad/m. That's why a lead screw plus a cheap motor wins for slow, high-force, cost-sensitive jobs and can hold a load unpowered (self-locking). The linear motor wins when:

- **Speed/acceleration**: no rotating screw inertia and no whip at critical speed; several m/s and accelerations of many g are practical (figures vary widely by design).
- **Accuracy and stiffness**: no backlash, windup or pitch error; accuracy is that of the linear encoder (down to nanometres with a good scale and loop).
- **Life**: no contact wear in the drive train (the guide bearings still wear).
- **Smoothness**: low vibration, no screw ripple.

## The practical problems

1. **Heat.** $P = I^2R$ for a given force $F$: $P = (F/K_f)^2R$. Delivering 60 N with $K_f = 10$ N/A needs 6 A and 144 W in 4 Ω! Per-force efficiency is poor at low speeds. High-end stages use liquid cooling.
2. **Magnetic attraction.** An iron-core forcer is attracted to the track with a normal force often several times the thrust; linear bearings must hold the gap. Ironless forcers avoid this and cogging, with lower force.
3. **Cogging and ripple.** Iron teeth interacting with magnets create position-dependent detent force; skewing or ironless design reduces it.
4. **Cabling.** Moving forcers need flex cables (cable chains) with fatigue limits; moving-magnet versions avoid this at the cost of mass.
5. **No self-lock.** Vertical axes need a counterbalance or brake or they fall when power is lost.
6. **End stops and crash protection.** Because the motor can accelerate fast, you need hard stops with dampers and limit switches, plus current limits.

## Driving it

Electronically it is a rotary motor. A three-phase forcer uses the same inverter/servo drive as a BLDC, with commutation angle taken from a linear encoder or Hall sensors; a voice coil uses an H-bridge or linear amplifier in current mode. Current control loops use the forcer resistance and inductance as usual; the position/velocity loops sit on top. See [[q0364]] for BLDC control.

## Worked example: a 0.2 m move on the example motor

Put numbers on the actuator from above ($K_f = K_e = 10$, $R = 4\ \Omega$, $V = 24$ V) moving a 2 kg payload (forcer plus load, no gravity, a horizontal axis). The job is a 0.2 m move, done as a symmetric accelerate-then-decelerate profile at $a = 10$ m/s² (about 1 g), repeated every 0.5 s.

**Kinematics.** Each half of the move covers 0.1 m, so $t = \sqrt{s/a} = \sqrt{0.2/10} = 0.141$ s and the full move takes $0.283$ s. The peak velocity is $at = 1.41$ m/s, well below the 2.4 m/s no-load limit.

**Force and current.** $F = ma = 20$ N, so $I = F/K_f = 2$ A, positive during acceleration and negative during deceleration.

**Voltage check.** At peak speed the drive must supply $V = IR + K_ev$, which at the end of the acceleration phase is $2\times4 + 10\times1.41 = 22.1$ V. That is less than 24 V, but only just: 92% of the bus. A tougher profile (higher peak speed or acceleration) would run out of voltage before running out of current, and the force would fall. Voltage headroom, not stall force, is often what limits a linear motor's top speed at a given force. A higher bus or a lower-$K_e$ winding fixes it, at the cost of more current.

**Heating.** Current has magnitude 2 A during the 0.283 s of motion and is near zero when stationary (a horizontal axis needs almost no holding force), so the RMS current over the 0.5 s cycle is $2\sqrt{0.283/0.5} = 1.50$ A. The winding dissipates $1.5^2\times4 = 9.05$ W. With a thermal resistance to ambient of 2 K/W (illustrative), the temperature rise is $18$ K, comfortable. The motor constant $K_m = K_f/\sqrt{R} = 10/2 = 5$ N/√W summarizes this: the force per root-watt is the same at every operating point of the winding, so you can predict that 20 N continuous would need $(20/5)^2 = 16$ W. Peak force is limited by the drive's current and voltage; continuous force by $K_m$ and the cooling.

## Direct drive versus screw: the inertia argument

The headline claim for linear motors is speed and acceleration, and a number shows why. A lead screw of lead $\ell = 2$ mm reflects rotor inertia $J$ into an equivalent linear mass of $m_{eq} = J(2\pi/\ell)^2$. For a rotor and screw totalling $J = 2\times10^{-6}$ kg·m² (a modest figure, illustrative), $m_{eq} = 2\times10^{-6}\times(3141.6)^2 = 19.7$ kg. The load is 2 kg, so the drive train multiplies the effective moving mass by about eleven, and the motor must also accelerate that equivalent mass. Direct drive removes that term entirely: the only moving mass is the forcer and the payload. This is the physical reason linear stages achieve accelerations that screws cannot, and it is a good point to make in an interview. The screw's advantages remain real: to push the same 20 N, a screw needs a motor torque of only $F\ell/(2\pi\eta) = 20\times0.002/(2\pi\times0.4) = 16$ mN·m, a tiny motor, versus the 144 W-class forcer for 60 N above.

## Where the simple model breaks

- **Position-dependent $K_f$.** The force constant varies with forcer position (end effects, magnet pitch, slot ripple). Cogging in an iron-core design adds a position-dependent force that a feed-forward map or a higher-bandwidth loop must remove; ironless motors avoid it.
- **Thermal drift.** Neodymium magnets lose flux with temperature, so $K_f$ falls as the track warms, and winding resistance rises about 0.39% per kelvin. A stage that is accurate cold may need loop-gain scheduling hot.
- **Back-EMF and bus limits.** As the example shows, the voltage budget caps velocity under load.
- **Forcer wiring and mass.** A moving forcer drags a cable chain whose stiffness, friction and mass load the motor; a moving-magnet design avoids the cable but adds track mass.

## Bench checks

Measure $K_e$ by dragging the forcer by hand or with another stage at a known speed and reading the open-circuit voltage; compare with $K_f$ from a force gauge pushing at a known current, and they should agree. Check the encoder: a scale with a 20 µm signal period gives 5 µm resolution with 4x quadrature decoding, and interpolating the analog sine/cosine by 4096 reaches a few nanometres in principle, though real accuracy is set by signal quality and mounting, not interpolation depth. Verify commutation by locking the forcer and rotating the commutation angle: force should vary as the cosine of the electrical angle error. Finally, run the representative move cycle for half an hour while logging winding temperature (or resistance rise) to confirm the thermal model before trusting the continuous rating.

::: applications
- **Hard-drive voice coil actuators**: swing arm or linear voice coil positions the read/write head with nm-scale accuracy at kHz bandwidth.
- **Semiconductor lithography and inspection stages**: ironless or air-bearing linear motors for nanometre-level precision.
- **Pick-and-place and SMT assembly machines**: high acceleration, fast cycle times.
- **Maglev trains and some roller coasters**: linear synchronous or induction motors on the guideway (long-stator or short-stator designs).
- **Camera autofocus and optical image stabilisation**: small voice-coil actuators (VCMs) moving the lens.
- **Machine tools, lab automation, loudspeakers**: a loudspeaker driver is a voice coil.
- **Tubular linear motors in packaging and industrial automation**: compact linear actuators with long life.

::: assumptions
- **PM linear synchronous motor / voice coil** as the default picture; LIMs follow similar thrust-velocity ideas with slip.
- **Single-phase equivalent model**: $F = K_fI$, $E = K_ev$, with constant $R$, $L$, $K_f$. Real motors have end effects, cogging, saturation and position-dependent $K_f$ variation.
- **Constant supply voltage and ideal driver**; real systems are current-limited and thermally limited, so continuous force is far below stall force.
- **Linear-motor force levels** (tens to thousands of newtons) vary a lot with size and cooling; the 60 N example is illustrative.
- A valid alternative answer defines linear motors more broadly as any actuator that makes linear motion directly from electromagnetic force, including voice coils and (arguably) solenoids; the narrower definition treats the solenoid as a separate device.
:::

::: followups
### Why not just use a rotary motor with a lead screw everywhere?
Lead screws are cheaper, self-locking and give mechanical advantage, so for slow, high-force or cost-driven jobs they win. But they have backlash, wear, friction and a critical speed limit (whip), which limits speed and accuracy. Direct drive wins at high speed and acceleration or when stiffness and positioning accuracy matter.

### How do you commutate a linear motor?
Like a BLDC: you need to know where the forcer is relative to the magnets. Use Hall sensors on the forcer or a linear encoder, then the drive applies three-phase currents with the right phase angle ($F$ is maximised with the current vector in quadrature with the magnet flux, i.e. field-oriented control). At power-up an incremental encoder needs a phase-finding routine (a small controlled move or a current pulse).

### What does the force constant tell you, and how do you measure it?
$K_f$ (N/A) relates force to current, equal to $K_e$ (V·s/m). Measure $K_e$ by moving the forcer by hand or with another actuator at a known speed and reading the open-circuit voltage; e.g. 5 V at 0.5 m/s gives $K_e = 10$ V·s/m, so $K_f = 10$ N/A.

### What limits the continuous force?
Winding temperature: $P = (F/K_f)^2R$ must be removed by conduction, convection or liquid cooling. For example, at $K_f = 10$ N/A, $R = 4\ \Omega$ and a thermal resistance of 2 K/W, 20 N needs 2 A and 16 W, giving a 32 K rise. The motor constant $K_m = K_f/\sqrt{R}$ (N/√W) is the figure of merit: force per root-watt.

### A linear stage drifts down when power is removed. Why, and what is the fix?
No mechanical self-locking, so gravity wins. Add a spring or pneumatic counterbalance, or a brake/clamp, and design the controller to fail safe (for example a drop-prevention brake that engages without power).

### Is a loudspeaker a linear motor?
Basically yes: a voice coil in a radial magnet gap with force $BlI$, moving a cone with a spring suspension, and it generates a back-EMF $Blv$ that shapes its impedance (the motional impedance peak at resonance). It's a limited-stroke linear actuator.
:::

::: interpretations
"What are linear motors?" is short and open. A good answer defines it (unrolled rotary motor, direct-drive linear force), names the forcer-track structure and a couple of types, and moves on to the trade-offs against rotary + screw. Some interviewers use "linear motor" for any linear actuator (including solenoids and voice coils); mention that and clarify. Related pages: [[q0347]] (solenoid vs motor), [[q0349]] (types of motors), [[q0376]] (steppers, which can also be built linear).
:::
