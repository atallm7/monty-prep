::: basics
### In plain words
A motor or solenoid drive must survive faults, not just work on the bench. You protect it in layers. A flyback diode or clamp handles the voltage spike when the switch opens. Resistors on the MOSFET's gate (its control pin) keep the switch under control. A current limit and a fuse handle stalls. Thermal shutdown handles overheating. Supply protection handles reverse polarity, surges, and regeneration. A safe default state handles processor crashes.

### Picture it
Think of a car's safety systems. The crumple zone, seat belts, and airbags each handle a different part of a crash, and no single one is enough. A drive works the same way. Each part answers one specific fault, and the layers overlap, so one failure does not destroy the drive.

### Words you need
- **Flyback diode**: a diode across the coil that gives its current a safe path when the switch opens.
- **Zener / TVS diode**: a diode that blocks until a set voltage, then conducts, clamping spikes to that voltage.
- **Slow-blow fuse**: a fuse that tolerates a brief inrush but opens on a sustained overload.
- **Regeneration**: a slowing motor acting as a generator and pushing its supply voltage up.

### The simplest example
A 10 mH coil on 12 V carries 1 A (so it is 12 Ω) and stores $\tfrac12 \times 0.01 \times 1^2 = 5$ mJ. With a plain diode clamp, the current falls to 0.1 A in about 1.58 ms. Add a 36 V Zener in series with the diode and that drops to about 0.21 ms, but the MOSFET now sees the supply plus the clamp (36 V plus the diode's 0.7 V): $12 + 36.7 = 48.7$ V, so choose a 60 V part. Separately, a 12 V, 1 Ω motor that stalls draws 12 A and dissipates 144 W, so it needs a current limit and a timeout.

That is the whole idea: one layer per fault, each with a number. The rest of this page adds gate protection, fuse sizing, thermal checks, supply protection, and bridge-specific measures.
:::

::: testing
Whether you think about a drive as a circuit that must survive faults, not just work on the bench. The expected list: flyback/clamp for the inductive kick, gate protection (series resistor, pull-down), over-current protection (fuse, current limit, stall handling), thermal protection, supply protection (reverse polarity, surge, bus pumping, undervoltage), shoot-through prevention in bridges, and noise suppression. Strong candidates attach a *reason and a number* to each and know the trade-off of the clamp (voltage versus release time).
:::

::: why
Motors and solenoids are the parts of a product that fail most violently: they stall, jam, spike, kick and sometimes short. A drive without protection works until the first stall or the first hot-plug. Interviewers ask this to see whether you design for fault conditions, one of the habits that separates a working prototype from a product.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductive kick**: turn-off spike $v = L\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$ and the clamp options. See [[q0340]].
- **Stall and inrush current**: $V/R$ at zero speed. See [[q0343]].
- **Gate protection**: series resistor, pull-down, gate-source Zener. See [[q0430]] and [[q0431]].
- **Over-current / over-temperature protection**: fuse, current limiting, thermal shutdown, derating.
- **Supply-side transients**: reverse polarity, surge, regeneration. See [[q0412]].
- **Shoot-through**: both switches of a leg on at once. See [[q0428]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd think in layers, from the load outward.

First, the inductive kick: a flyback diode across the load for a motor or solenoid, or a diode in series with a Zener or a TVS if I need fast release, and sometimes an RC snubber for ringing. Without that, turn-off spikes kill the FET.

Second, the gate: a series resistor to limit gate current and damp ringing, a pull-down so the FET is off while the MCU boots, and sometimes a Zener at the gate to stop transients going over the gate-source rating.

Third, over-current. Motors stall and draw $V/R$, so I want a current sense and limit, with a fuse or PTC as the last resort, and sized so inrush doesn't blow it. Fourth, thermal: thermal shutdown in a driver IC, or a sensor on the board or the motor and derating.

Fifth, the supply: reverse-polarity protection with a diode or a P-FET, a TVS for surges or load dump, bulk capacitance, and a plan for regenerated energy pumping the bus. Undervoltage lockout so the FET isn't driven half-on. For bridges, dead time and shoot-through lockout. And for noise, a capacitor across the motor terminals to tame brush arcing.

Finally the system level: stall and jam detection, and a safe default state, outputs off when the MCU resets or crashes."
:::

::: technical
**1. Inductive kick.** Energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ must go somewhere. Options:

| Clamp | Coil voltage at turn-off | FET $V_{DS}$ peak | Decay time ($L = 10$ mH, $R = 12\ \Omega$, $I_0=1$ A to 0.1 A) |
|---|---|---|---|
| none | set by avalanche/parasitics | breakdown | n/a (destructive) |
| diode | $-0.7$ V | $V_S + 0.7$ | 1.58 ms |
| diode + Zener (36 V) | $-36.7$ V | $V_S + 36.7$ | 0.21 ms |
| TVS (60 V) | $-60$ V | $V_S + 60$ | 0.14 ms |

Decay time: $t = \frac{L}{R}\ln\frac{V_c + I_0R}{V_c + 0.1I_0R}$ with clamp voltage $V_c$. The FET must be rated above $V_S + V_c$ with margin. An RC snubber across the switch adds damping for ringing from parasitic $L$ and $C$ but doesn't give a hard clamp.

**2. Gate.** $R_g$ about 10 to 100 Ω, $R_{pd}$ about 10 to 100 kΩ; Zener or TVS from gate to source if the gate drive can exceed $V_{GS(max)}$ (typically ±20 V).

**3. Over-current.** Stall: $I = V/R$ (12 A for 12 V/1 Ω). Use a sense resistor and comparator/amplifier ([[q0395]]), cycle-by-cycle limit in the PWM, a fuse (slow-blow, $I^2t$ above the inrush) or PTC as backup. Time-limited stall protection: motor winding heats at $I^2R$; limit the time at high current.

**4. Thermal.** FET $T_j = T_a + P R_{\theta JA}$; driver ICs have thermal shutdown; sense motor temperature by NTC ([[q0396]]) or by winding resistance rise.

**5. Supply.** Reverse polarity: series Schottky (about 0.5 V drop; at 5 A that is 2.5 W) or a P-FET in the supply path (10 mΩ at 5 A is 0.25 W). Surge: TVS with stand-off above the maximum operating voltage and clamping below the FET rating; bulk electrolytic or polymer for inrush, regen and ripple. UVLO so the gate driver doesn't leave the FET partly on.

**6. Bridge-specific.** Dead time, shoot-through lockout, bootstrap capacitor refresh, desaturation detection (VDS monitoring) in gate drivers.

**7. EMI / noise.** Brushed motors arc: a 100 nF ceramic across the terminals, plus 10 to 47 nF from each terminal to the case (typical practice), snubbers and ferrites on the leads.

**8. System.** Stall detection by current and speed ([[q0402]]), watchdog and safe default (outputs off at reset), end stops, and mechanical hard stops with torque limits.
:::

For over-current, a sense resistor with current limit, plus a fuse; motors stall at V over R. Thermal shutdown. On the supply side, reverse-polarity protection, a TVS for surges and bulk capacitance for regeneration. In bridges, dead time. Plus a capacitor across brushes for noise and a safe default off."
:::

::: long
"I'd go layer by layer.

Starting at the load, the big one is the inductive kick. A flyback diode across the motor or solenoid gives the current a path. The catch is that a plain diode makes the current decay slowly, so if release time matters I'd put a Zener or TVS in series to raise the clamp voltage, for example a 36 volt Zener lets a 10 millihenry coil at 1 amp drop to a tenth in 0.2 milliseconds instead of 1.6, as long as the FET is rated above the supply plus the clamp. An RC snubber can help with ringing.

At the gate, a series resistor limits current and damps ringing, a pull-down keeps the FET off while the MCU boots, and a gate Zener protects the gate-source rating.

For over-current, motors and solenoids draw V over R when stalled or starting, so I'd have a sense resistor and a current limit in the PWM, and a fuse or PTC as last resort, picking one that tolerates inrush. For thermal, driver ICs have thermal shutdown, and I'd monitor motor or board temperature for derating.

On the supply: reverse polarity protection, either a diode or better a P-FET to avoid the loss, a TVS for surge, and bulk capacitance to absorb regenerated energy. Undervoltage lockout so the gate drive doesn't leave the FET in the linear region. In an H-bridge, dead time and shoot-through lockout.

Finally noise and system protection: a ceramic capacitor across the brushes, stall detection, a watchdog and a safe default so outputs are off if the processor resets."
:::

::: terms
- **Flyback / freewheel diode**: gives inductor current a path at turn-off.
- **Clamp (Zener/TVS)**: limits the voltage across the switch by conducting above a set voltage.
- **Snubber (RC)**: a resistor-capacitor network that damps ringing.
- **Slow-blow fuse**: tolerates short inrush ($I^2t$) but opens on sustained overload.
- **TVS diode**: transient voltage suppressor for surges.
- **UVLO**: undervoltage lockout.
- **Thermal shutdown**: automatic disable at excessive die temperature.
- **Desaturation detection**: monitoring $V_{DS}$ to catch short-circuit conditions.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "flyback diode and a fuse". An exceptional answer:

- Organises protection by **layers** (kick, gate, over-current, thermal, supply, bridge, noise, system).
- Explains the **clamp trade-off** with numbers (diode versus Zener/TVS, decay time versus FET voltage).
- Mentions **inrush/stall** and sizes protection around it.
- Compares **reverse-polarity** options by loss.
- Includes **safe default** and **stall detection**, i.e. protection at the system level.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Only adding a flyback diode.** It doesn't protect against stall, reverse polarity, surges or gate transients.
- **Fuse sized at the running current.** Inrush blows it. Use a slow-blow type and check $I^2t$.
- **Clamp voltage above the FET's breakdown.** A 60 V clamp with a 40 V FET (and 12 V supply) destroys the FET.
- **Ignoring bus pumping.** Regenerated energy raises the supply. See [[q0412]].
- **No pull-down on the gate.** The FET may conduct during reset.
- **Diode reverse-polarity protection on a high-current rail.** It dissipates $V_f I$ continuously. Use a P-FET or ideal-diode controller.
- **Relying on firmware alone for protection.** A hung processor does not protect anything; use hardware limits too.
:::

## Layer 1: the inductive kick

Without a clamp the switch takes the energy $\tfrac12 LI^2$ in avalanche. A plain diode clamps at $V_S + V_f$ but gives a slow decay; adding a Zener or TVS raises the coil's reverse voltage and speeds the decay:

Energy check: $\tfrac12\times0.01\times1^2 = 5$ mJ. In a clamp it is dissipated as $V_c\cdot I$ over the decay time; a TVS rated for kW-level pulses (microseconds to milliseconds) absorbs it easily. In the unclamped case a small FET's avalanche rating (single pulse, often milli-joules, vendor-dependent) is the limit.

## Layer 2: the gate

A MOSFET gate is a capacitor (several nC to tens of nC) with a threshold. Three failures:

1. **Floating gate** at reset: pull-down (10 to 100 kΩ).
2. **Overshoot** (ringing from lead inductance and gate capacitance): series resistor, and a Zener or TVS from gate to source rated below $V_{GS(max)}$ if the drive can overshoot.
3. **Miller-induced turn-on**: fast $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}t$ on the drain couples through $C_{gd}$ into the gate; a low-impedance pull-down (or a driver with strong sink) keeps it off. See [[q0431]].

## Layer 3: over-current and stall

A stalled motor draws $V/R$, a solenoid that doesn't close draws full current, and a short in the cable draws far more. Protection tiers:

- **Fast (µs to ms)**: current limit or desaturation shutdown in the driver, cycle-by-cycle in the PWM.
- **Medium (ms to s)**: firmware limits current-time (an $I^2t$ model of winding heating).
- **Slow, last resort**: fuse, PTC or circuit breaker.

Example: a 12 V, 1 Ω, 3 A-rated motor stalls at 12 A, and the winding dissipates 144 W; at, say, a 20 J/K thermal mass that rises 7 K per second. A 2 second stall gives about 14 K, a 20 second stall melts insulation. So: current limit at 4 to 5 A and stall detection within 1 s.

Sizing a fuse: choose a slow-blow type rated above the continuous current (with derating margin, vendor guidance often 1.25x to 2x) such that the motor's inrush $I^2t$ does not open it, yet it opens on a hard short. Use the fuse manufacturer's time-current curve.

## Layer 4: thermal

$T_j = T_a + P\,R_{\theta JA}$. At 5 A through a 16 mΩ hot FET, $P = 0.4$ W; at a stall limit of 12 A, $P = 2.3$ W and with $R_{\theta JA} = 50$ °C/W the junction rises 115 °C: a protection limit or heat-sinking is needed. Driver ICs shut down at about 150 to 175 °C (vendor-dependent) and restart or latch.

## Layer 5: the supply side

- **Reverse polarity**: a series Schottky costs $V_f I$ (at 5 A and 0.5 V: 2.5 W); a P-FET in the supply path costs $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ (at 10 mΩ: 0.25 W). An ideal-diode controller with an N-FET is another option.
- **Surge**: automotive load-dump or hot-plug inductance on long cables. A TVS clamps; the FET and driver must be rated above the clamp. Series inductance in the supply cable plus a hot-plug can ring to twice the supply: bulk capacitor ESR and a TVS tame it.
- **Regeneration**: bulk capacitance, TVS or brake chopper, see [[q0412]].
- **Undervoltage**: UVLO in the gate driver stops it from driving the FET with an insufficient gate voltage.

## Layer 6: bridges

Everything above, plus: dead time and cross-conduction lockout ([[q0411]]), bootstrap capacitor refresh at 0% and 100% duty, desaturation detection on each high-side/low-side FET, and fault-latched outputs.

## Layer 7: noise and EMI

Brushed motor commutation creates broadband noise. Standard practice: a 100 nF ceramic across the motor terminals and 10 to 47 nF ceramics from each terminal to the motor case (when the case is grounded), ferrite beads or a common-mode choke on long leads, short loops for the switching current. This is also protection for the logic: brush noise can crash an MCU or corrupt an ADC reading.

## Layer 8: system

- **Stall/jam detection** by current rise with zero speed ([[q0402]]).
- **Safe default**: outputs low at reset; watchdog; fail-safe brakes for load-bearing motors.
- **Isolation** between logic and the motor supply when noise or safety demands ([[q0432]]).
- **Mechanical protection**: hard stops with clutches or current-limited torque.
:::

::: applications
- **Automotive actuators (window lifts, pumps, valves)**: low-side or half-bridge drivers with integrated clamps, diagnostics, reverse-battery protection and load-dump TVS.
- **Industrial solenoid valves**: Zener/TVS clamps for fast release plus fuses and surge protection.
- **Battery-powered robots**: current-limited bridges, UVLO, bulk capacitance and regeneration handling.
- **Appliances**: thermal fuses on motors, snubbers across relays and triacs.
- **3D printer fans/pumps**: simple diode plus gate network, thermal fuse on the supply.
- **Hobby ESCs**: current limiting, thermal derating and low-voltage cutoff.
:::

::: assumptions
- A **low-voltage DC** drive (12 to 48 V class) of a brushed motor or solenoid; mains-powered and high-voltage drives add isolation, creepage and safety-standard requirements not covered here.
- Component values (clamp voltages, resistors) are **representative**; parts must be selected from datasheets with margin.
- Thermal and avalanche numbers are **vendor-dependent**; the examples are illustrative.
- The decay-time formula assumes a **constant** $L$, $R$ and an ideal clamp; real inductors saturate and real diodes have series resistance.
- **PCB layout** (loop area, ground return) affects spike height but is covered in the PCB section.
:::

::: followups
### Why not always use a big TVS so the coil releases fast?
Because the FET must withstand $V_S + V_c$. A larger clamp needs a higher-voltage FET with higher $R_{DS(on)}$ and cost. Also higher clamp voltages raise EMI and dissipation in the clamp device per event, and may exceed the driver IC's rating. Choose the clamp for the required release time, then the FET.

### What protects against a motor that stalls for a long time?
Current limiting plus timeout: the controller limits current in hardware, and firmware monitors time at high current, reducing or cutting drive after, say, 1 s. A thermal sensor on the motor, or an $I^2t$ estimator, is more precise. A thermal fuse in the winding is the physical last resort.

### How would you pick a fuse for a motor drive?
Take the continuous current, apply the manufacturer's derating, then check the time-current curve against the start-up inrush and stall limit: the fuse must not open on normal inrush ($I^2t$ above it), must open on faults before wiring or the PCB is damaged, and its voltage and breaking-capacity ratings must suit the supply. Slow-blow types suit motors.

### Is a body diode enough as the flyback path in an H-bridge?
Often yes: each FET's body diode freewheels the motor current. But body diodes have slow reverse recovery and a high drop (0.7 to 1 V), which costs efficiency and causes shoot-through spikes; high-frequency bridges add Schottky diodes or use synchronous rectification ([[q0411]], [[q0419]]).

### What happens if the battery is connected backwards and you have no protection?
The FET body diodes, driver IC ESD diodes and the electrolytic capacitors become forward or reverse biased. Current flows through body diodes into the motor, or the capacitors (polarised) are reverse-charged and can vent. Add a P-FET reverse-polarity switch (cheap and low loss) or at least a series diode.
:::
