::: basics
### In plain words
A servo's big advantage is that it measures where it is. Because of that, it reaches its target under any load, it pushes back when disturbed, and it knows when it has failed to arrive. A stepper only counts steps and trusts that it got there. A plain motor has no idea where it is. The price of the servo's advantage is cost, extra parts, and tuning.

### Picture it
Imagine walking across a dark room by counting exactly 20 paces. That is a stepper. If you slip once, you never find out, and you end up in the wrong spot. A servo is the same walk with the lights on: you look, see you are short, and take another step. The analogy breaks in one place: the lights cost money. The sensor, the drive and the tuning all add expense, so for easy jobs counting paces is fine.

### Words you need
- **Open loop / closed loop**: running without feedback, or with a measured result fed back to correct it.
- **Following error**: the gap between commanded and actual position. A big one raises a fault.
- **Stepper**: a motor that moves a fixed step per pulse and never checks where it ended up. See [[q0376]].
- **Phase (winding)**: one of a motor's coils of wire. Current through it makes heat.

### The simplest example
Compare two motors sitting still, holding a position. A stepper with 1.5 A in each of two phases, 2 ohms each, burns $2 \times 1.5^2 \times 2 = 9$ W in its windings, even with no load. A servo holding a balanced load draws almost nothing, because its current follows the load. Now overload both: the stepper silently loses steps, and the servo shows a following-error fault.

That is the whole idea: feedback buys accuracy, fault detection and efficient current use. The rest of this page adds the torque-versus-speed comparison, a worked axis example, and when a stepper is the better pick.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can compare actuators honestly: not "servos are better" but "servos win on these axes and lose on those". The interviewer wants specific properties (position accuracy from feedback, torque available across speed, efficiency, fault detection, dynamic response), the matching weaknesses (cost, tuning, complexity, noise), and a sense of when a stepper or plain motor is the better engineering choice.
:::

::: why
Actuator selection is one of the first design decisions in any mechatronic product and the wrong choice is expensive to fix. A candidate who can place servo, stepper, brushed DC and open-loop BLDC on a small table of trade-offs shows decision-making maturity. It also exposes whether you understand torque-speed behaviour, which the later spec question ([[q0388]]) relies on.
:::

::: concepts
- **Closed-loop vs open-loop**: servos measure and correct; steppers and plain motors do not. See [[q0383]].
- **Torque-speed curve**: how much torque is available at each speed. See [[q0341]].
- **Holding torque and holding current**: stepper holds at fixed current, servo holds with current matching load. See [[q0382]].
- **Efficiency**: electrical-to-mechanical conversion; related to current drawn at a given load.
- **Bandwidth**: how fast the loop can follow a command.
- **Cost and complexity**: sensor, drive, tuning.
:::

::: answer
"The big advantage of a servo is feedback. Because it measures where the shaft is, it's accurate regardless of the load and it knows when it hasn't reached the target. A stepper just counts steps and trusts that it got there, so it can lose steps without telling you. A plain DC or brushless motor doesn't know its position at all.

Second, torque holds up with speed. A stepper's torque falls off fast as it speeds up, whereas a servo can deliver its rated torque out to its rated speed, plus short bursts of two to three times that for acceleration.

Third, efficiency. A stepper runs at full current whether or not it's doing anything, so it gets hot just sitting there. A servo draws current in proportion to the load; at rest with no load it draws very little.

Fourth, dynamics: servos accelerate quickly, run smoothly and quietly without a stepper's step resonance, and they can reject disturbances.

The downsides are cost and complexity. You need a sensor, a driver and a tuned control loop, and a badly tuned servo can oscillate. For light, predictable loads at low speed a stepper is simpler and cheaper, and for constant-speed jobs a plain motor with a speed controller is plenty."
:::

::: technical
**Comparison.**

| | Brushed DC (open loop) | Stepper (open loop) | Brushless, trapezoidal (speed control) | Servo (brushless + encoder) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Position known | No | Assumed from step count | Coarse (Hall) | Yes, from encoder |
| Lost-position detection | None | None unless encoder added | None | Following-error fault |
| Torque vs speed | Falls linearly with speed | Falls quickly (inductance, back-EMF) | Roughly constant to base speed | Constant to rated speed, peak 2–3× briefly |
| Holding at rest | None | Yes, at full current | None | Yes, at load-proportional current |
| Efficiency | Moderate | Low (constant current) | High | High |
| Complexity / cost | Lowest | Low | Medium | Highest |
| Noise / vibration | Brush noise | Step resonance, audible | Low | Low, smooth |

**Torque.** For a DC-type motor, $\tau = K_t I$ and current is bounded by drive and heat. Steppers have torque limited by winding inductance: current reaches its setpoint only if $L\,di/dt$ allows it, so at step rate $f_s$ the available current drops and torque falls roughly as
$$\tau(f) \approx \frac{\tau_{hold}}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_c)^2}}\ \text{(first-order guide only)}$$
where $f_c$ is set by the winding time constant and driver voltage.

**Heat at rest.** A two-phase stepper with 1.5 A per phase and 2 Ω per winding dissipates $P = 2 I^2 R = 2\times1.5^2\times2 = 9$ W just holding. A servo with no external load needs little more than friction and gravity compensation current.

**Where steppers or plain motors win.** Low cost, simple drive, good holding at standstill without tuning, no feedback hardware, known loads, low speed, and microstepping giving fine resolution open loop. Brushed DC motors win on pure cost and simplicity. Brushless open-loop wins for fans and pumps.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The "2–3× peak torque" figure is a typical industrial servo claim; the actual ratio is motor and drive specific and set by the drive's current limit and the motor's thermal and magnetic limits.
:::

::: short
"A servo's advantage is feedback: it knows where it is, so it's accurate under any load, holds position by pushing back and reports when it fails to arrive. Its torque stays flat out to rated speed with brief higher peaks, whereas a stepper's torque falls with speed. It's efficient because current follows load, while a stepper burns full current at rest. Disadvantages are cost, complexity and tuning, so I'd pick a stepper for simple light low-speed jobs."
:::

::: long
"I'd compare it against the three obvious alternatives: plain DC motor, stepper, and open-loop brushless.

Against a plain DC motor, the servo wins on position. An ordinary motor has no idea where it is and its speed changes with load. Add a sensor and a controller and you get a servo, and now it goes to a defined place.

Against a stepper, it wins on a few things. First, torque at speed: a stepper loses torque quickly as step rate rises because the winding inductance limits how fast current can build, whereas a servo gives close to rated torque all the way to rated speed and extra torque in short bursts for acceleration. Second, efficiency and heat: a stepper holds with full current whether or not the load needs it, so a typical one dissipates a few watts to ten watts just sitting still, while a servo draws roughly what the load demands. Third, it detects problems: a stepper silently loses steps if overloaded, a servo shows a following-error fault. Fourth, it's smooth, with no step resonance and no audible stepping.

Against open-loop brushless, a servo adds precise position and torque control, because the encoder allows the drive to commutate and close position loops.

Where does the servo lose? Cost and complexity. It needs a sensor, a driver and tuning. A poorly tuned servo can oscillate or hunt. For a 3D printer extruder or a small indexing table with a known light load, I'd happily use a stepper. For a robot arm or a machine tool, I'd use servos because losing position there is not acceptable."
:::

::: terms
- **Open loop / closed loop**: control without or with measured feedback.
- **Following error**: difference between commanded and actual position, used as a fault trigger.
- **Holding torque**: torque a motor can exert at standstill.
- **Pull-out torque**: stepper torque beyond which it loses synchronism.
- **Peak torque**: short-duration torque limited by drive current and heating.
- **Continuous torque**: torque sustainable indefinitely within thermal limits.
- **Microstepping**: driving a stepper with sine/cosine currents for finer position.
- **Bandwidth**: highest command frequency the servo can follow.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer shows both sides with numbers (for example 9 W holding dissipation for a typical stepper), mentions torque-speed curve shape, brings up fault detection through following error, and says when it would choose a stepper instead. It also mentions closed-loop steppers as the middle option and the subtlety that "better" depends on speed, load, cost and the need for diagnostics.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Servos are always more accurate."** Accuracy comes from the sensor; resolution is not accuracy.
- **"Steppers can't be precise."** Microstepping and gearing give fine resolution; the problem is not knowing if a step was lost.
- **Ignoring cost and tuning.** Presenting servos as free upgrades is naive.
- **Comparing stall torque numbers across types.** Compare continuous torque at the operating speed.
- **Forgetting open-loop BLDC.** For fans and pumps it is the better choice than a full servo.
:::

::: deep
## What "advantage" means: position, torque, efficiency, diagnostics

### Position and disturbance rejection

An open-loop actuator's output depends on its model: how many steps, how much voltage, how long. A closed loop's output depends on the measurement. With proportional control the steady-state error under torque load $\tau_L$ is $e = \tau_L R/(K_tK_p)$ (see [[q0383]]), shrinking with gain, and with an integral term it goes to zero in steady state. A stepper's equivalent behaviour is a spring-like restoring torque that rises with displacement up to a limit and then drops: past a step or so, the rotor slips to the next stable position without any indication.

### Efficiency and heat at rest

A stepper holds a position by sitting at a detent with winding current flowing. For a motor rated 1.5 A per phase and 2 Ω per phase (a plausible NEMA 17 class motor), the dissipation is $2\times1.5^2\times2 = 9$ W continuously, even with zero load. Drivers often reduce current at standstill (idle current reduction), which saves heat at the cost of holding torque. A servo's holding current is whatever the load requires: zero for a balanced load, and the drive can sit near zero when the shaft is stationary.

### Diagnostics

A drive that monitors following error, motor current, encoder health and temperature gives you diagnostics the stepper cannot: "my axis couldn't reach the target." In a machine that is the difference between a scrapped part noticed immediately and one discovered at inspection.

### Dynamics and smoothness

Steppers have a resonance, typically in the low hundreds of hertz in step rate, where the rotor rings; microstepping and damping mitigate it. Servos run smooth sinusoidal current, accelerate quickly because of high peak torque and low rotor inertia, and respond to loop commands at bandwidths of hundreds of hertz or more.

## Costs of the advantages

1. **Sensor**: pot, magnetic or optical encoder, plus wiring and sometimes a shield.
2. **Drive**: current loop, protection and communications.
3. **Tuning**: gains, filters and limits per axis; poor tuning gives overshoot, noise or oscillation.
4. **Failure of feedback**: a lost encoder signal can send a servo into runaway unless the drive faults on it. Industrial drives treat this as a safety fault.

## Decision examples

- **3D printer axis, 0.5 kg moving mass, known load, 100 mm/s**: stepper. Cheap, adequate, open loop is fine.
- **CNC router axis, cutting forces vary, 20 m/min rapids**: servo, to keep tracking under cutting load and detect stall.
- **Pump or fan**: open-loop BLDC with speed control; position irrelevant.
- **Cheap prototype with 90 degrees of travel**: RC servo.

## When "servo" and "stepper" converge

Closed-loop steppers add an encoder to a stepper and fall back to servo-like behaviour: they use current only as needed and detect lost steps. They sit between the two on cost and performance, and are a good interview mention.

## Worked example: one axis, two answers

Numbers decide the stepper-versus-servo question better than adjectives. Consider a belt-driven axis moving a 3 kg carriage 0.4 m in 0.4 s, then dwelling for the rest of a 1 s cycle. The pulley radius is 12.7 mm. Use a trapezoidal profile with equal thirds of accelerate, cruise and decelerate (illustrative requirements).

**Kinematics.** Each phase lasts 0.133 s. The peak speed is $v = d/(t\cdot2/3) = 0.4/(0.4\times2/3) = 1.5$ m/s, the acceleration is $v/0.133 = 11.25$ m/s², and the distance works out: two ramp phases give $0.1 + 0.1$ m, the cruise gives $1.5\times0.133 = 0.2$ m, total $0.4$ m.

**Load.** The force during acceleration is $F = ma = 3\times11.25 = 33.75$ N, which at the pulley is $T = Fr = 33.75\times0.0127 = 0.43$ N·m. The pulley speed at the end of acceleration is $v/r = 118$ rad/s, which is 1,128 rpm. The peak mechanical power is $T\omega = 0.43\times118 = 50.6$ W. This ignores rotor and pulley inertia, which add to the acceleration torque, and friction, here taken as 0.05 N·m during cruise.

**Stepper check.** Use the illustrative stepper curve from this page, $\tau(\text{rpm}) = \tau_{hold}/\sqrt{1 + (\text{rpm}/700)^2}$. A NEMA 17 with 0.5 N·m of holding torque delivers only $0.5/\sqrt{1 + 1.61^2} = 0.26$ N·m at 1,128 rpm, well short of 0.43 N·m. To get a 50% torque margin at that speed you would need a holding torque near $0.43\times1.5\times\sqrt{1+1.61^2} = 1.2$ N·m, which means a larger frame (NEMA 23 class), more weight on the carriage and more heat at standstill. A gear reduction barely helps: it divides the required motor torque by $N$ but multiplies the motor speed by $N$, and above the corner this curve's torque falls roughly as 1/speed, so the two cancel. On this curve no ratio gets more than about 72% of the needed torque, and a real curve, which droops faster than 1/speed at high speed, makes gearing worse. The stepper can work, but only with a larger motor, a higher supply voltage (which raises the corner frequency, and so is a valid fix to try first) and acceleration limits.

**Servo check.** The servo has to supply 0.43 N·m peak for two 0.133 s intervals. The RMS torque over the cycle is $\sqrt{(0.43^2\times0.133\times2 + 0.05^2\times0.133)/1.0} = 0.22$ N·m, so a servo with about 0.3 N·m continuous and 0.9 N·m peak (illustrative) covers it, with room for the inertia that was ignored. At the speed of 1,128 rpm the servo is still in its flat-torque region. During the dwell it draws only the current to counter friction or gravity, and in a horizontal axis nothing. A stepper at the same time would dissipate its full holding power.

So the stepper solution costs a bigger motor and a bigger driver but no sensor; the servo solution costs a sensor and a drive but the motor stays small. Which is cheaper depends on production volume. What the numbers show is that the winner changes when the speed (here 1,128 rpm) moves past the stepper's corner frequency. Slow the move to 1.2 s and the peak speed falls to a third, the acceleration to a ninth, and a plain NEMA 17 handles it easily.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The stepper curve above is a one-pole guide with a 700 rpm corner, not data for any motor. Real corner speeds depend on winding inductance and driver voltage; use the manufacturer's torque-speed curve at your supply voltage.

## Limiting cases and where the comparison shifts

- **Very low speed, high holding.** Positioning a light load slowly and holding it is the stepper's best case; the servo has to hunt around a small error to hold, and quantization noise from the encoder can make it jitter.
- **Very high speed.** A stepper simply cannot follow; a servo is the only choice of the two.
- **Continuous duty.** A servo's continuous torque is thermally limited just like a stepper's; the difference is that the stepper burns it at rest.
- **Closed-loop steppers.** Adding an encoder gives the stepper fault detection and lets the drive reduce current at light load, closing much of the efficiency gap while keeping the stepper's low-speed torque and price.
- **Resolution versus accuracy.** A servo's accuracy is set by its sensor and mechanics, not its resolution; a 20-bit encoder on a loose belt is still a loose belt.

## Verifying the choice with a prototype

Don't trust the catalog torque. Measure the real friction and the moving mass, then run the real profile on each candidate. With a stepper, raise acceleration until the first lost step, using a home-switch return to detect it, and halve it for the operating margin. With a servo, log the following error and the motor current through the move: the peak current tells you the true peak torque ($I\times K_t$), and the RMS current over the cycle checks the thermal rating. In both cases measure motor temperature at steady duty after half an hour. Put a scale or spring gauge on the carriage to confirm the force, and a scope on the supply to see the peak power draw. The prototype data replaces the approximations in this page.

- **3D printers**: steppers on axes where cost and open loop suffice; servos on high-end machines.
- **CNC machine tools**: AC servo axes for tracking under varying cutting force.
- **Robot arms**: servos or servo-like brushless actuators for torque and fault detection.
- **Camera gimbals**: servo loops for smooth, quiet, fast stabilisation.
- **RC models**: hobby servos where cost and simplicity matter most.

::: assumptions
The comparison assumes typical hobby or industrial parts, not extreme designs. Steppers with high-voltage chopper drives, low-inductance windings and microstepping do much better at speed than the simple model; high-end closed-loop steppers narrow the gap. Torque-speed numbers differ greatly by motor, drive and supply voltage. The efficiency argument is for holding and low-load operation; at high load the gap shrinks. "Servo" includes both hobby RC servos and industrial drives, and many statements, like 2–3× peak torque, apply only to the latter.

::: followups
### Why does stepper torque drop at high speed?
Each step requires current to build in an inductive winding: $di/dt = (V - IR - e_{bemf})/L$. At high step rate there is not enough time for the current to reach its setpoint, and back-EMF rises with speed. So current and therefore torque fall. A higher driver voltage helps, as do chopper drives.

### Can a servo run open loop?
No. Without feedback it is just a motor and driver. But brushless servo drives sometimes run in a sensorless or Hall-only mode as a fallback, and a stepper can be run closed loop by adding an encoder.

### Why do servos have peak torque higher than continuous?
Heating goes as $I^2R$ and takes time to build, so you can exceed rated current briefly while the winding temperature rises slowly. The peak is limited by the drive and the magnets. This is how a servo accelerates a load quickly then settles to a lower continuous torque.

### Which is better for a robot gripper holding force?
For holding with a fixed force a servo in torque (current) mode, because current sets torque and it adapts to the object. A stepper holds too, but at constant current and heat. Many hobby servos in a gripper overheat since position-mode servo keeps pushing against a stalled object.

### Harder: your stepper design misses steps at 600 rpm but not 100 rpm. Would you switch to a servo?
First check cheaper fixes: raise the driver voltage, lower the acceleration, use microstepping, or add current boost. If torque at 600 rpm still falls short, a closed-loop stepper or a small servo is justified. The answer depends on torque needed at speed, cost target and whether detecting a lost step matters.
:::
