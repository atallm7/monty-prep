::: basics
### In plain words
A differential probe measures the voltage between two points, instead of one point compared with the scope's ground. It has two inputs, plus and minus, and an amplifier that passes on only their difference. It ignores any voltage both inputs share. You need one when neither point is at ground.

### Picture it
Two people ride an elevator. You want to know how much taller one is than the other. The floor does not matter, only their difference. The elevator's floor is the shared "common-mode" voltage. A grounded probe is like measuring each person's height above street level, then subtracting, so small errors in the two numbers wreck the answer.

### Words you need
- **Ordinary probe**: measures one point compared with the scope's ground, which is tied to earth.
- **Differential voltage**: the voltage between the probe's plus and minus inputs.
- **Common-mode voltage**: the voltage both inputs share relative to ground. The probe should ignore it.
- **CMRR**: common-mode rejection ratio, how strongly the probe ignores the shared voltage. It is given in dB (decibels): 60 dB means 1000 to 1. Higher is better, and it gets worse at high frequency.
- **Floating signal**: a signal where neither point is at ground.

### The simplest example
In a motor-drive circuit, two transistors (electronic switches) are stacked, and the point between them jumps between 0 and 400 V every cycle. The top transistor's control input, its gate, sits about 12 V above that point, and you want to see this 12 V. Neither point is at ground.

An ordinary probe's ground clip on the swinging point would short it to earth through the scope. A differential probe connects plus to the gate and minus to that point. It outputs the 12 V difference and rejects the 400 V swing.

With 60 dB of CMRR, the leftover error from the 400 V swing is $400/1000 = 0.4$ V.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds how CMRR falls with frequency and the alternatives.

::: testing
Whether you understand **why a normal scope probe cannot measure a floating voltage** and what a differential probe does about it. The interviewer is looking for: ground-referenced vs differential measurement, common-mode vs differential voltage, CMRR (and that it falls with frequency), the high-side measurement use cases (half-bridge gate drive, high-side shunt, differential buses), and the alternatives (isolated probes, two probes with math, floating the scope, which is dangerous). Bonus: you know the trade-offs (attenuation, noise, cost, limited common-mode range).
:::

::: why
Power electronics and high-speed serial links are full of signals that have no ground connection: a high-side MOSFET's gate-source voltage rides on a switch node that jumps by hundreds of volts, a shunt on a 48 V rail has 10 mV across it, a LVDS or CAN pair carries information in the difference of two wires. A grounded probe either shorts the circuit or reads nonsense. Knowing the right probe, and its limits, is the difference between a clean waveform and a blown board.
:::

::: concepts
- **Single-ended measurement**: the scope measures a node relative to its own ground, which is tied to earth. See [[q0990]].
- **Differential voltage and common-mode voltage**: $V_d=V_+-V_-$ and $V_{cm}=(V_++V_-)/2$. See [[q0683]] and [[q0684]].
- **CMRR** (common-mode rejection ratio): how much the probe attenuates common-mode compared with differential, in dB.
- **Ground loop**: connecting a probe ground to a node at a different potential makes current flow. See [[q0629]].
- **Attenuation and offset range**: differential probes divide the signal (10x, 50x, 500x...) and have a maximum differential and common-mode input range.
:::

::: answer
"A differential probe measures the voltage *between* two points, not a point relative to ground. It has two inputs, plus and minus, and an amplifier that outputs the difference to the scope, while rejecting whatever voltage they both share. That shared part is the common-mode voltage, and how well it is rejected is the CMRR.

I'd need one any time neither side of what I'm measuring is at ground. The classic example is the gate drive of a high-side MOSFET in a half-bridge: the source is the switch node, jumping from 0 to, say, 400 V every cycle. If I clipped a normal probe ground there I'd short the switch node to earth through the scope. Other cases are a shunt on the high side of a rail, measuring across a floating sensor, or looking at the two lines of a differential pair directly. Also anywhere I'm worried about a ground loop corrupting a small signal.

The cautions: CMRR drops with frequency, so a probe that has 80 dB at DC may give only 30 or 40 dB at 100 MHz. It's more expensive and noisier than a passive probe and has a limited input range. For very high voltage or fast edges, like GaN or SiC, I'd look at an optically isolated probe."
:::

::: technical
**Principle.** The probe contains a differential amplifier (often with input attenuators) whose output is $v_{out}=A_d(V_+-V_-)+A_{cm}\tfrac{V_++V_-}{2}$. $\text{CMRR}=20\log_{10}(A_d/A_{cm})$. The output is single-ended into a scope channel, referenced to the scope's ground, so the scope ground never touches the DUT.

**When needed.**
- Neither measurement node is at ground potential: floating high-side signals, bridge outputs, shunt on a positive rail.
- Need to avoid ground loops, e.g. across a long cable or between two powered systems.
- Differential signaling (LVDS, CAN, RS-485, USB, SerDes) when you want the differential waveform without common-mode distortion.

**Error from finite CMRR.** A residual common-mode error $V_{err}=V_{cm}/10^{\text{CMRR}/20}$. Example: 400 V swing with 60 dB (1000:1) gives 0.4 V error; with 40 dB, 4 V; with 80 dB, 40 mV. CMRR typically drops with frequency as the input capacitances and gain mismatch grow, so check the CMRR vs frequency curve at the edge rate that matters, not the DC spec.

**Other specs.** Differential input range (set by the attenuation switch; higher attenuation gives a larger range but more output-referred noise); maximum common-mode (and "safe" absolute) input voltage; bandwidth (tens of MHz to GHz); input impedance and capacitance per leg; noise (higher than passive at the same scale because of the attenuator and amplifier); offset.

**Alternatives.** (a) Two single-ended probes subtracted by math (poor CMRR, but fine for slow signals with small common-mode). (b) Isolated or optically isolated probes for high common-mode swing and high bandwidth. (c) Floating the scope by removing its earth: **never**, a safety hazard because the chassis and the BNC shells become live.
:::

::: long
"A normal scope probe measures one node relative to the scope's ground, which is hard-wired to earth. A differential probe has two inputs and an amplifier that outputs only the difference between them, so it can measure a voltage between two nodes where neither is at ground.

Where do I actually need it? Number one is power electronics. In a half-bridge, the high-side gate-source voltage rides on the switch node, which jumps by the full bus voltage every cycle. A grounded probe clip on that node would short it to earth. Number two is high-side current sensing: a shunt on a 48 volt rail has maybe 10 millivolts across it, but both ends sit near 48 volts. Number three is differential signals like LVDS, CAN or USB, where I want to see what the receiver sees, the difference, without the common-mode.

The quality metric is CMRR, the ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain. If I have a 400 volt swing and 60 dB of CMRR, that leaks 400 millivolts of error onto my signal. And CMRR is much worse at high frequency, so I check the datasheet curve at the edge rate I care about.

Other trade-offs: differential probes have attenuation settings, so the signal might be 1/50 of its size and noise goes up; they have a limited common-mode range; and they cost a lot more than passives. For very fast edges with big swings, like SiC or GaN gate drives, I'd step up to an isolated probe, which has much better CMRR at high frequency. And I would never defeat the scope's earth to 'float' it. That's a shock hazard."
:::

::: terms
- **Differential voltage**: $V_+-V_-$, the quantity you want.
- **Common-mode voltage**: the average of the two inputs relative to ground, which should be rejected.
- **CMRR**: $20\log_{10}(A_d/A_{cm})$ in dB; higher is better, and it degrades with frequency.
- **Floating measurement**: a measurement where neither node is the scope's ground.
- **Isolated probe**: a probe that transmits the signal optically or magnetically with galvanic isolation; very high CMRR.
- **Attenuation setting**: the divide ratio (e.g. 50x, 500x) that sets maximum range and noise.
- **Common-mode range**: the maximum voltage of either input relative to ground that the probe can handle.
- **Ground loop**: unwanted current path created by connecting two ground references; a normal probe on a floating node creates a short.
:::

::: exceptional

::: mistakes
- **Floating the scope by cutting off the earth pin.** This puts mains-referenced voltage on the chassis and BNC shells. The correct fix is a differential or isolated probe.
- **Thinking a differential probe has infinite CMRR.** It might be 60-80 dB at low frequency and under 40 dB at hundreds of MHz.
- **Ignoring the attenuation setting's range.** A 20 mV signal on the 50x range is mostly noise, while a 600 V signal can exceed the differential range of the lower-attenuation setting. Pick the range for the signal.
- **Subtracting two passive probes for a hundred-volt common-mode** and trusting the result. Gain mismatch between the probes alone produces volts of error.
- **Exceeding the probe's common-mode or absolute rating.** A differential probe is not a high-voltage probe unless its datasheet says so.
- **Using a differential probe to "fix" noise** that is really a long ground lead or loop area. A differential probe has its own lead inductance and must be kept short too.
:::

::: deep
## Why single-ended probes fail on floating nodes

A scope channel's BNC shell is connected to the scope's chassis, which is connected through the power cord to earth. A standard probe's ground clip is therefore at earth potential. If you clip it onto a node that is not at earth potential, a current flows through the scope's ground until something gives. Think of a bench supply powering a board off an isolated wall adapter with its negative at 0 V relative to the board but not earth; fine. But in a half-bridge connected to rectified mains, a "ground" may be 170 V off earth. Clipping to it conducts through the earth connection and trips breakers or destroys the probe or the board.

## Differential and common-mode in numbers

Let the probe inputs be $V_+$ and $V_-$. Define

$$V_d=V_+-V_-,\qquad V_{cm}=\frac{V_++V_-}{2}$$

For the high-side Vgs: $V_+=V_{SW}+V_{gs}$, $V_-=V_{SW}$. If $V_{SW}$ swings 0 → 400 V and $V_{gs}$ is 12 V, then $V_d=12$ V and $V_{cm}\approx 400$ V (plus half the gate drive). The probe output is $V_{out}=\tfrac{1}{k}\left(V_d + V_{cm}/\text{CMRR}\right)$ with attenuation $k$. The second term is the error. With CMRR in dB:

| CMRR | ratio | error from 400 V common-mode swing |
|---|---|---|
| 40 dB | 100:1 | 4 V |
| 60 dB | 1,000:1 | 0.4 V |
| 80 dB | 10,000:1 | 40 mV |
| 100 dB | 100,000:1 | 4 mV |

A 12 V gate drive with 0.4 V error is a 3% error, fine for checking Vgs levels. But **the error is not just a gain error**: it is a copy of the switch-node waveform leaking in, so it looks like ringing on the gate. You can verify by shorting the probe's + and − leads together and putting them on the SW node: whatever you see is the probe's CM leakage.

> Real probes have different CMRR curves; the figures above show the shape only. Take the numbers from the actual probe's datasheet at the frequency of your edge.

## Using two single-ended probes with math

Probes A and B on the two nodes, scope computes A−B. This is cheap and sometimes adequate when the common-mode is small. The residual error is

$$V_{err}\approx V_{cm}\,|g_A-g_B|/g$$

where $g_A,g_B$ are the gains. With 12 V common-mode and 1% mismatch that is 120 mV (compare [[q0993]]); and with different cable lengths there is also skew, which turns a fast common-mode edge into a spike in the difference. For a 10 mV shunt signal this fails. For a 5 V logic signal with 50 mV tolerances, it is fine.

## When a differential probe is still not enough: isolated probes

Fast wide-bandgap devices have 50 V/ns or faster edges. At 100+ V/ns, parasitic capacitance of a differential probe's inputs to ground turns the common-mode dv/dt into a current that appears as error. Optically isolated probes (a transmitter at the DUT, light over fiber to a receiver at the scope) have a much smaller common-mode capacitance and keep CMRR high into the 100 MHz range. They are bulkier and more expensive, with limited input range at the highest sensitivity. For GaN/SiC gate-drive and switching-loss measurements they are often the correct instrument. Check the datasheet.

## Worked example: is the gate drive overshooting?

A 400 V half-bridge drives a high-side FET from a 12 V gate driver. The FET's absolute maximum Vgs is ±20 V, so you want to see whether the gate overshoots above, say, 16 V at turn-on. You attach a differential probe set to 50x attenuation (the output is 1/50 of the input, so a 12 V signal reaches the scope as 240 mV; set the channel's probe factor to 50x, or let probe auto-recognition do it, so the screen reads in real volts, then pick a scale such as 2 V/div, which is 40 mV/div at the scope input).

1. Short the probe's inputs together, connect them to the switch node and trigger on the SW rising edge. Suppose you see 0.5 V of apparent spike at the edge. That is the probe's CM leakage; subtract it mentally (or choose a better probe).
2. Connect + to the gate and - to the source (directly at the FET pins, with the shortest leads). Capture the turn-on edge. Suppose the display shows a peak of 16.8 V against a plateau of 12 V.
3. Correct for the leakage seen in step 1: real overshoot is at most 16.8 - 0.5 = 16.3 V, below the 20 V limit but with a thin margin. You decide to raise the turn-on gate resistor, then repeat.

The point of the first step is an **honest baseline**: before trusting a differential probe on a violent node, measure what it shows when it should show zero. If the artifact is a significant fraction of the number you care about, the measurement is not valid at that edge rate.

## Practical tips

- Keep the input leads short and twisted or use the supplied probe tips/spring contacts; the leads form a loop whose inductance picks up magnetic fields and rings with the input capacitance.
- Choose the **lowest attenuation range** that fits the signal, because attenuation multiplies the amplifier's noise at the output.
- Do an **auto-zero/offset null** with the inputs shorted.
- Use the scope's probe-recognition (many diff probes auto-set the scale) or set the attenuation manually so the displayed volts/div is correct.
- Respect the **absolute common-mode and differential limits**. These are on the data sheet.

::: applications
- **Half-bridge and full-bridge gate drive** (Vgs of the high-side switch; floating bootstrap supply).
- **High-side current sensing** on a motor or battery rail via the voltage across a shunt ([[q0993]]).
- **Differential signals**: LVDS clocks, CAN/RS-485 buses, Ethernet, USB: look at the differential waveform that the receiver sees.
- **Mains-referenced circuits** (off-line SMPS primary side) where an isolated or differential probe protects the scope and the user.
- **Bridge sensors**: strain gauge or thermocouple bridge outputs.
- **Battery pack cell voltage** measurement between two cells in a series stack.
:::

::: assumptions
- The signal is within the probe's differential and common-mode range, and the probe bandwidth covers the signal.
- CMRR is taken at the frequency of interest. For fast edges that is far above DC, where CMRR is much lower than the headline spec.
- The signal is genuinely floating. For ground-referenced signals a normal passive probe ([[q0997]]) or a single-ended active probe ([[q0996]]) is the better choice.

Alternative answer: for small common-mode and slow signals, "two probes and A−B math" is acceptable. For safety-critical mains work, an isolated scope or isolated probe is the standard answer.
:::

::: followups
### Why can't I just float the scope instead?
Floating the scope by lifting the earth pin makes the chassis and every BNC shell sit at whatever voltage the circuit has, so touching the scope can be lethal on a mains-referenced circuit. It also raises the common-mode capacitance and it is against safety rules. Use a differential or isolated probe, or an isolation transformer on the DUT only if you know what you are doing.

### What CMRR do I need to measure the high-side Vgs of a 400 V GaN half-bridge with 1 V accuracy?
Need $400/\text{CMRR}\le 0.1$ V (to leave margin), so CMRR $\ge 4000$ = 72 dB at the relevant frequency. With edges at say 5 ns, the relevant frequency is about $0.35/5\text{ ns}=70$ MHz. Few ordinary differential probes have that at 70 MHz; an optically isolated probe is the usual choice.

### My differential probe shows ringing at the SW node edge. Is it real?
Test: short the probe's inputs together, connect them to the switch node and see if the ringing appears. If yes it is common-mode leakage or lead pickup, not real. Real Vgs ringing stays when you measure Vgs directly and responds to gate-resistor changes.

### Difference between a differential probe and a differential amplifier on the board?
The probe amplifier sits outside the DUT, adds loading and bandwidth limits and takes a standard connection to the scope. A board-level instrumentation or current-sense amplifier is part of the design, with its own CMRR and range, and is used for permanent monitoring. For debug, I use the probe.

### How is a differential probe different from just a high-voltage probe?
A high-voltage passive probe (e.g. 100:1, 1000:1) is still ground-referenced: it divides a signal relative to ground. A differential probe may have a high-voltage rating, but its defining feature is the two-input difference measurement with common-mode rejection.
