::: testing
A one-word answer ("asynchronous") is the easy part. The interviewer wants the **definition** of what that means (no clock *signal* shared between the devices), what replaces the clock (agreed baud rate plus start-bit re-synchronisation), and what the trade-off is against SPI or I2C. They also check whether you know about the USART exception.

Hidden sub-skills: clock-domain thinking, tolerance budgets, and being able to say why "asynchronous" does not mean "no timing".
:::

::: why
The sync/async distinction decides everything about how a bus behaves: how many wires it needs, how fast it can go, how sensitive it is to clock accuracy, and how receivers recover data. It is also the setup for the next question, "since there's no clock, how is data read?" ([[q0778]]). Candidates who just say "async, no clock" and stop leave the obvious follow-up hanging; the strong answer volunteers the mechanism.
:::

::: concepts
- **Synchronous link**: a clock signal travels with the data (SPI SCLK, I2C SCL); the receiver samples on clock edges. See [[q0764]].
- **Asynchronous link**: no shared clock; each side has its own oscillator and they agree on a rate in advance.
- **Re-synchronisation**: the receiver realigns its timing on each start edge, so errors cannot accumulate beyond one frame.
- **Clock tolerance**: allowed mismatch of about 3 to 4% in practice. See [[q0775]].
- **Framing overhead**: the price of async: start and stop bits on every byte.
- **USART**: Universal *Synchronous/*Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter, which can optionally drive a clock pin.
:::

::: answer
"UART is asynchronous. There is no clock wire between the two devices. Each one has its own oscillator, and they just agree beforehand on a baud rate, say 115200, so they both know how long a bit lasts.

The catch is that two independent clocks are never exactly equal and have no phase relationship, so the receiver has no idea where bits start. That's why every byte is framed with a start bit and a stop bit. The start bit is a falling edge that tells the receiver 'now', and from that edge it counts its own bit times and samples each bit in the middle. At the next byte it re-syncs from the next start edge, so errors don't pile up.

Contrast that with SPI or I2C, which are synchronous: a clock wire runs alongside the data, and the receiver samples exactly on the clock edges, so the two sides can't drift relative to each other, and the speed is just whatever the clock is.

The trade is that async saves a wire and tolerates unrelated clocks, but costs framing overhead, 20% at 8N1, and needs the two baud rates to match within a few percent. One footnote: many chips are USARTs, which can also run in a synchronous mode with a clock pin, but plain UART use is asynchronous."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** A link is synchronous if transmitter and receiver share a timing reference carried on the link (a clock wire, or a clock embedded and recovered from the data). UART carries neither: it is **asynchronous** at the bit level.

**What stands in for the clock.**
1. A pre-agreed nominal bit time $T_b = 1/\text{baud}$.
2. A known idle level (high), so the start bit is a guaranteed falling edge.
3. A per-frame re-alignment: the receiver measures time from the start edge with its own clock and samples at $T_b(n+\tfrac12)$.

**Tolerance.** Let the relative frequency error between the two ends be $\varepsilon$. For 8N1 the sampling point for the stop bit is at $9.5\,T_b$; it must stay within $\pm 0.5\,T_b$ of the true bit centre, so $9.5\,\varepsilon < 0.5 \Rightarrow \varepsilon < 5.3\%$. After allowing for sampling granularity and edge jitter, designs budget about 3 to 4%.

**Compared with typical oscillators.** A crystal at ±50 ppm is 0.005%, about 1000 times better than needed. An on-chip RC oscillator at ±1% (typical order; vendor-dependent) uses a quarter or so of the budget per end. A wrong divider can consume the rest ([[q0775]]).

**Overhead.** Each 8-bit byte needs 10 line bits, so 80% efficiency; synchronous buses have no per-byte framing but need a clock wire (SPI) or a clock plus ack bits (I2C).

**USART.** Many MCUs implement a USART, adding a CK pin to run synchronously (master drives the clock, data valid on a defined clock edge). That mode is closer to SPI and drops the start/stop bits. Standard "UART" use is asynchronous.
:::

::: short
"Asynchronous. There's no clock wire. Both sides pick the same baud rate and each uses its own oscillator. To make that work, each byte is framed by a start bit and a stop bit, so the receiver can lock onto the falling start edge and then sample each bit in the middle using its own clock. Compare that with SPI or I2C, where the clock is on a wire and the receiver samples on its edges. Async saves a wire and tolerates unrelated clocks but costs about 20% overhead and needs the rates to match within a few percent."
:::

::: long
"UART is asynchronous, which means there's no clock signal shared between the two devices. Each chip has its own oscillator, and what they share is an agreement on the baud rate, say 115200 bits per second, so they both know a bit lasts about 8.7 microseconds.

The question that follows is how the receiver knows where the bits are, since two free-running clocks have no phase relationship. The answer is the frame. The line idles high. A byte starts with a low start bit, which the receiver sees as a falling edge, and that's its one synchronisation point. It counts off its own bit times from there, samples each bit in the middle, and finishes with a stop bit that returns the line high. On the next byte it re-syncs again, so any frequency error between the two clocks only accumulates over ten bits, not forever.

That is also why the tolerance is what it is. The last sample, the stop bit, is 9.5 bit-times after the edge, and it must still land inside its bit. So the two clocks can differ by about 5% in theory, and in practice I'd budget three or four percent. A crystal is a thousand times better than that. An internal RC oscillator might use a good part of the budget, and a bad baud divider can use the rest.

Compare it with a synchronous bus like SPI, where a clock wire travels with the data. The receiver samples on the clock edges, so there's no framing overhead and the data rate is just the clock rate, but you need that extra wire and the clock has to be distributed. Async trades 20% overhead for one less wire and independent clocks.

One caveat: many MCUs have a USART, which can run in synchronous mode with a clock pin. But UART as normally used is asynchronous."
:::

::: terms
- **Asynchronous**: no clock signal is transmitted; timing comes from a pre-agreed rate and edge re-synchronisation.
- **Synchronous**: a clock signal accompanies the data and defines when to sample.
- **Baud rate**: the agreed bit rate both sides count to.
- **Re-synchronisation**: realigning the receiver's timing at each start edge.
- **Clock tolerance / mismatch**: the allowed frequency difference between the two ends.
- **Framing overhead**: extra start/stop bits not carrying payload.
- **USART**: universal synchronous/asynchronous receiver/transmitter, which supports an optional clock output.
- **Plesiochronous**: nearly the same frequency but not locked; a precise term for what two crystals are.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "asynchronous, no clock".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Says *what replaces the clock*: agreed baud rate, start-bit edge, mid-bit sampling, re-sync every frame.
- Quantifies tolerance (9.5 bit-times to the stop-bit sample gives a 5.3% theoretical limit, 3 to 4% in practice) and compares with real oscillators (crystal ppm, RC percent).
- States the trade: one fewer wire and independent clocks vs 20% framing overhead and a speed ceiling.
- Mentions USART synchronous mode as a nuance.
- Connects to a design consequence: use a crystal or calibrated clock for UART, and check the baud divider.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Asynchronous means the timing doesn't matter."** It matters more: the receiver must hold the bit timing within a few percent for the whole frame.
- **"The start bit is a clock."** It is a single edge used once per frame; there is no recurring clock to track.
- **"UART has no timing information."** The baud rate and framing are the timing information.
- **Confusing asynchronous with 'unreliable'.** UART is very reliable when rates match; the failure mode is mismatch.
- **Believing a USART is always synchronous.** Synchronous mode is optional and rarely used.
- **Thinking I2C is asynchronous because it has only two wires.** I2C has a clock (SCL); it is synchronous.
:::

::: deep
## Two clocks, no relationship

Imagine two musicians told "play at 120 beats per minute" but with no conductor. If both are accurate, they stay close for a long time, but they never start on the same beat unless somebody gives a cue. A UART frame provides the cue (the start bit) and gives a short piece of music (10 bits) so drift cannot matter. After the piece, they stop and wait for the next cue.

## The tolerance derivation

Let the transmitter's bit time be $T$ and the receiver's be $T(1+\varepsilon)$. The receiver aligns on the falling start edge at $t=0$ and samples bit $k$ at its own time $t_k = (k + 0.5)\,T(1+\varepsilon)$ where $k=0$ is the start bit and $k=9$ is the stop bit in 8N1. The true centre of bit $k$ is $(k+0.5)T$. The error at bit $k$ is

$$\Delta_k = (k+0.5)\,T\,\varepsilon$$

The sample stays inside the bit as long as $|\Delta_k| < 0.5\,T$. The worst case is the stop bit, $k = 9$:

$$9.5\,\varepsilon < 0.5\ \Rightarrow\ \varepsilon < \frac{0.5}{9.5} = 5.26\%$$

Sampling granularity (with 16x oversampling the start edge is detected to within 1/16 bit) and edge slew/jitter eat some more: $(0.5 - 1/16)/9.5 = 4.6\%$. Practical designs budget **3 to 4% total** across both devices.

With parity enabled (8E1) the stop bit is at 10.5 T; $0.5/10.5 = 4.76\%$. With 9 data bits it tightens further. This is a nice illustration of why more bits per frame means less tolerance: the further you go after the edge, the more drift builds up.

## Real clocks

| Clock source | Typical tolerance (order of magnitude) | Fraction of 3 to 4% budget |
|---|---|---|
| Quartz crystal | ±10 to ±50 ppm = 0.001 to 0.005% | negligible |
| Ceramic resonator | about ±0.5% | small |
| Internal RC (factory trimmed) | about ±1 to ±2% over temperature | large |
| Internal RC (untrimmed) | ±10% or worse | too much |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Internal oscillator accuracy varies by part, trim, supply and temperature; many MCUs quote ±1% or better after calibration over a limited range and several percent over the full range. Check the datasheet for your part, and the baud divider error ([[q0775]]) adds on top.

## Synchronous mode on a USART

```sim
{"id":"uart-frame","char":"U","baud":115200,"parity":"none","stop":1,"mismatch":5}
```
Figure: At +5% mismatch the frame still decodes, but the stop-bit sample is about 42% of a bit off centre. The sim quotes a tolerance of about −3.8% / +4.9% rather than ±5.3%, because it models the real receiver: the start edge is detected up to 1/16 bit late and the three-sample majority vote spans ±1/16 bit, which costs margin, and the late detection partly cancels drift for a fast receiver but adds to it for a slow one. Push the slider further and watch the last bits slide out of their windows and framing errors appear. A synchronous link has no equivalent failure, because the clock is transmitted.

A USART adds a clock pin (CK or SCLK): the master drives it and data changes on one edge and is sampled on the other, exactly as in SPI. There are no start/stop bits in this mode (or they can be dropped), so there is no framing overhead, but you give up the point of using UART: independence from a clock wire. The mode is mostly used for smart-card (ISO 7816) interfaces and for hooking a USART up as an SPI-like port.

## What breaks an asynchronous link in practice

In the field the failures are rarely about the theory and nearly always about the clocks. A product ships with an internal RC oscillator because it saves a crystal, passes at room temperature, and then returns from the field with garbled serial output in a hot enclosure, because the RC drifted by 3% and the baud divider error was already 2%. Another classic: a bootloader that runs from the internal oscillator at power-up and switches to the PLL later, so the console works for the first line and then prints garbage because the baud divisor was not recomputed after the clock change. A third: two boards whose firmware says "115200" but where one's clock tree gives 111,111 baud and the other's gives 117,647, which is a 5.9% relative mismatch that no amount of staring at the code will reveal until you put a scope on the TX line and time one bit. The habit to build is simple: when an async link misbehaves, measure the real bit time on both ends and compute the relative error before touching anything else.

## Pros and cons at a glance

| | Asynchronous (UART) | Synchronous (SPI) |
|---|---|---|
| Wires per direction | 1 data | 1 data + shared clock |
| Clock accuracy | within ~3 to 4% | no matching required |
| Overhead | 20% (8N1) | none |
| Max speed | limited by oversampling and line | limited by clock and line |
| Sampling margin | shrinks with frame length and mismatch | fixed by setup/hold at the clock edge |
| Multi-device | no (point-to-point) | yes (chip selects) |

The pattern generalises: a faster, longer or more precise link wants a clock. Gigabit serial links (PCIe, USB, Ethernet) are "asynchronous" in the same sense as UART, since they have no clock wire, but they embed the clock in the data with line coding and use a PLL (clock and data recovery) at the receiver to track it continuously. UART's start-bit re-sync is the cheap, low-speed cousin of that idea.
:::

::: applications
- **Debug consoles and module links** where simplicity matters more than speed.
- **GPS, Bluetooth and Wi-Fi modules** which run their own oscillators.
- **Modbus RTU over RS-485**: async framing across long cables, nodes with separate clocks.
- **ISO 7816 smart cards** use a USART's synchronous/asynchronous character mode.
- **Contrast case**: SPI flash or displays use a synchronous clock for tens of MHz.
:::

::: assumptions
- "Synchronous" means a clock signal accompanies the data. By this definition I2C and SPI are synchronous, and so are links that embed the clock but recover it with a PLL.
- The tolerance numbers assume 8N1 with 16x oversampling; other frame lengths and oversampling modes shift them slightly.
- Oscillator tolerances are order-of-magnitude figures; check the specific device.
- Some authors call UART "asynchronous serial" and reserve "synchronous" for the clocked USART mode. I follow that usage.
:::

::: followups
### How can an asynchronous link work if the clocks differ?
Because it only has to stay aligned for one frame at a time. The start edge re-synchronises it for every byte, and the sampling points stay within the bit as long as the combined frequency error is below about 5% (3 to 4% budgeted).

### What is the maximum clock mismatch for 8N1?
$0.5/9.5 = 5.3\%$ in theory for the stop-bit sample, around 4.6% with 16x oversampling, and 3 to 4% in practice to leave room for edge jitter and noise.

### Why did SPI and I2C choose to be synchronous?
They are designed for fast on-board links and multiple devices. A clock wire removes the need for matching rates, enables much higher speeds and removes framing overhead; I2C also uses the clock for clock stretching and ack timing. The cost is an extra wire and (for SPI) chip selects.

### What is a USART and when would you use the synchronous mode?
A UART that can optionally output or accept a clock on a CK pin. Synchronous mode drops the start/stop bits and behaves like SPI. It is used for smart cards or when a spare SPI-like port is needed, but dedicated SPI is more common.

### Harder: a link between two MCUs works at 9600 but fails at 115200 using the internal RC oscillators. Why?
At 9600 the integer divider error is small (0.16% at 16 MHz) and RC drift of 1 to 2% fits in the budget. At 115200 the divider error alone is 3.55% (integer divisor on a 16 MHz clock), and the two RC oscillators can differ by another few percent, which together exceed about 5%. Use a crystal, calibrate the RC, or use a fractional baud divider.
:::
