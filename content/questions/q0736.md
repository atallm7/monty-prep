::: basics
### In plain words
A reset signal forces a chip into a known starting state and then lets it begin running from a fixed place. It exists because a chip's internal memory cells power up in random states, and the supply voltage takes time to rise. The chip must wait until power and clock are good. Reset also gives a way to recover if the program hangs or the supply dips too low.

### Picture it
Think of a race. The runners are held behind the starting line until everyone is ready and the track is clear. Then the starting gun fires, and all of them start from the same line. A reset holds the processor still while the supply and clock settle, then fires the gun. The same signal can also recall the runners mid-race if something goes wrong.

### Words you need
- **Reset**: forcing the chip's settings and built-in hardware blocks to their defaults, then restarting the processor.
- **Active-low**: "on" when the pin is pulled to 0 V. The reset pin, often called NRST, is usually active-low.
- **Power-on reset**: circuitry inside the chip that holds it in reset until the supply has risen high enough.
- **Brown-out**: the supply sagging below a safe level while the chip runs, which can corrupt its work.
- **Watchdog**: a timer that resets the chip if the program stops checking in.
- **Reset vector**: the stored address where the processor starts running after a reset.

### The simplest example
Suppose the supply ramps from 0 to 3.3 V over 10 ms. A bare resistor and capacitor on the reset pin (10 kΩ and 100 nF) would release reset after about 3 ms. At that moment the supply is only about 1.1 V, too low for reliable running. A supervisor (a small chip that watches the supply), or the MCU's built-in power-on reset, waits until the supply is high enough. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers all the reset sources, what gets reset, and the circuit design.
:::

::: why
A processor that starts in an unknown state, or keeps running while its supply sags, will misbehave in ways that are impossible to debug. Reset is the first thing checked when a board "doesn't boot", and poor reset design causes intermittent start-up failures that only show on slow supply ramps or cold mornings. It is a small circuit with a big effect on reliability.
:::

::: concepts
- **Sequential logic state**: flip-flops power up randomly; reset forces a defined value. See [[q0732]].
- **Active-low signal**: reset pins are usually named NRST or RESET# and asserted by pulling low.
- **Power-on reset (POR) and brown-out detection (BOR)**: on-chip supply monitors.
- **Reset vector**: the address the CPU fetches its first instructions from.
- **Watchdog**: timer that triggers reset when firmware hangs. See [[q0834]].
- **Supervisor IC**: external chip that holds reset until the supply is valid.
:::

::: technical
**Reset sources (typical MCU):** power-on reset (POR), brown-out reset (BOR, programmable threshold), external NRST pin, independent/window watchdog, software reset (for Cortex-M a write to the system reset request bit), lockup or fault escalation, debugger-initiated reset, and in some parts low-power-mode exit resets. A reset-cause status register records which one fired.

**Effect.** Core halted; core and peripheral registers set to reset values; GPIO usually input floating or default state; clocks at the default internal oscillator; flash and (usually) RAM retained but not guaranteed valid. On Cortex-M, after reset the core reads the initial main stack pointer from address 0x0000_0000 and the reset handler address from 0x0000_0004, then runs.

**Pin electrical.** NRST is active low with an internal pull-up (tens of kΩ is common) and often an internal filter; many parts also drive it low themselves during internal resets (open-drain), so it can't be hard-driven high from outside.

**Simple RC reset.** A resistor from NRST to VDD and capacitor to ground. Time to reach a threshold of 0.7×VDD after power-up with an instantaneous supply: $t=-RC\ln(0.3)=1.2\,RC$. For 10 kΩ and 100 nF, $RC=1$ ms and $t=1.2$ ms.

**Why the RC alone fails on slow ramps.** If VDD ramps linearly to 3.3 V over 10 ms, the capacitor voltage lags VDD by about $a\tau$ with slope $a=0.33$ V/ms and $\tau=1$ ms, so about 0.33 V. NRST crosses 0.7×VDD when VDD is only about 1.06 V (3.2 ms in this model), long before the supply is in spec. A supervisor that releases reset only once VDD exceeds, for example, 2.93 V and then waits a fixed delay fixes this.

**Typical numbers.** Minimum NRST pulse width to guarantee a reset is on the order of hundreds of ns to a few µs (datasheet), POR thresholds around 1.5–2 V, and programmable BOR thresholds in the 2–2.9 V range. Supervisor reset timeouts commonly range from a few ms to hundreds of ms.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> All thresholds, delays and pin behaviours are part-specific. Use the datasheet's electrical characteristics for NRST pulse width, POR/BOR levels and filter.
:::

::: long
"Reset is the mechanism that puts a digital system into a known state. When power is applied, all the flip-flops come up randomly, and the supply takes milliseconds to ramp, so the processor needs to be held still until things are valid.

While reset is asserted, the core is halted, its registers and all the peripherals go to their default values, GPIOs go to a safe state, usually inputs, and the clock falls back to the internal oscillator. When it's released, a Cortex-M reads the initial stack pointer and the reset vector from the start of flash, and starts running my startup code, which initializes variables and clocks and calls main.

There are several sources. Power-on reset is built into the chip and holds it until the supply passes a threshold. Brown-out reset watches for the supply dropping below a level during operation, which matters because executing, and especially writing flash, at too low a voltage can misbehave or corrupt memory. The reset pin, active low, normally has a pull-up and connects to a pushbutton, the debugger, and possibly an external supervisor chip. The watchdog fires if the firmware stops servicing it. And software can request a reset, for example after a firmware update.

Circuit-wise, the simple version is an RC on the reset pin: 10 kΩ and 100 nF give about 1.2 ms to reach the threshold. But that only works if the supply comes up faster than the RC. On a slow ramp the capacitor tracks the supply too closely, and the reset releases at about a volt, which is far below the minimum operating voltage. So for robust designs I rely on the chip's POR and BOR, or add a supervisor IC that releases reset only above a set threshold plus a delay.

Why it's useful: deterministic start-up, recovery from hangs, protection from bad supplies, and manual restart. I'd also check the reset-cause register on boot so I can log whether a restart was a power-on, brown-out or watchdog event."
:::

::: terms
- **Reset vector**: address (or table entry) the CPU fetches at reset to find its first instruction.
- **POR**: power-on reset, generated while VDD rises through a threshold.
- **BOR / brown-out**: reset or interrupt when VDD falls below a threshold.
- **NRST / RESET#**: active-low external reset pin.
- **Supervisor (voltage monitor)**: IC that asserts reset when VDD is out of range and for a delay after.
- **Watchdog**: timer that resets the system if not serviced.
- **Cold vs warm reset**: after power loss vs a reset with power maintained (RAM contents may survive).
- **Reset-cause flag**: status bits recording the last reset source.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Leaving NRST floating.** Noise can reset the chip randomly; use the pull-up and a small capacitor if the datasheet allows.
- **Relying on a bare RC for slow supply ramps.** It releases reset at a low voltage.
- **Driving NRST high with a push-pull output.** Internal resets pull it low; use open-drain.
- **Confusing reset with interrupt.** Reset restarts everything; an interrupt preserves context.
- **Assuming RAM is cleared on reset.** Often it keeps old contents; startup code must initialize it.
- **Ignoring brown-out when writing flash or EEPROM.** Low voltage during a write corrupts data.
- **Not logging the reset cause.** Field watchdog resets then go unnoticed.
:::

::: deep
## Why a digital system needs reset

A synchronous digital circuit is a set of flip-flops whose next state depends on the current state and inputs. At the instant power is applied, each flip-flop settles to an essentially random value set by tiny asymmetries in its transistors. No amount of correct logic can tell what the machine is doing from an unknown state, so a reset forces every state element that matters to a defined value. The CPU's program counter must start somewhere known, the peripherals must be off so they don't drive pins unexpectedly, and the clock must be something that works before software configures a PLL.

Two physical facts make this hard. First, the supply doesn't jump to its final voltage: it ramps over microseconds to tens of milliseconds, passing through a range where transistors partly conduct and logic is unreliable. Second, oscillators take time to start, particularly crystals that need hundreds of microseconds to milliseconds. So reset must stay asserted until the supply is above the minimum operating voltage and the clock is stable, and then release cleanly.

## What happens at reset

1. **Core stops and registers reset.** Program counter, status registers and interrupt state return to defaults, interrupts are masked.
2. **Peripherals reset.** Timers stop, serial ports are disabled, and clock gates are closed on many MCUs, which is why peripheral registers don't respond until you enable their clocks (see [[q0732]]).
3. **Pins go to a safe default**, usually high-impedance inputs, so external circuits aren't driven. Pull-ups on boot-strap pins may select the boot mode at the reset release.
4. **Clock falls back** to the internal oscillator.
5. **At release**, a Cortex-M reads the initial stack pointer from 0x0000_0000 and the reset handler from 0x0000_0004. The reset handler copies initialized data from flash to RAM, zeroes uninitialized data, configures clocks and calls `main`.

## Reset sources and what each is for

| Source | Trigger | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Power-on reset | VDD rising through about 1.5–2 V | Start cleanly |
| Brown-out reset | VDD falling below a programmed threshold | Never run (or write flash) on a bad supply |
| External pin | Pin pulled low: button, supervisor, debugger | Manual or system-level restart |
| Watchdog | Timer expires unserviced | Recover from software hang |
| Software | Core system-reset request | Apply update, controlled restart |
| Lockup / fault | Unrecoverable exception | Escape a dead state |

A well-behaved product records the cause at boot (a flag register) and logs it. A watchdog reset in the field is a bug report you would otherwise never get.

Many MCUs already have an internal pull-up, so the external resistor is optional, but the capacitor and button are common. A debugger connects to the same net through an open-drain driver.

**RC power-on delay.** With a fast supply, the capacitor charges through R and NRST crosses the input-high threshold (about 0.7 VDD) at $t=-RC\ln(1-0.7)=1.2RC$. For 10 kΩ and 100 nF that's 1.2 ms. That is fine if the supply is up in well under a millisecond and the oscillator starts quickly.

**Why a bare RC fails on slow ramps.** Suppose VDD ramps linearly to 3.3 V in 10 ms, with slope $a=0.33$ V/ms, and $\tau=RC=1$ ms. The capacitor voltage lags the supply by about $a\tau=0.33$ V once settled. The reset pin crosses 0.7 VDD as soon as $0.33<0.3\,VDD$, i.e. at VDD of about 1.1 V.

Therefore: use the MCU's built-in POR and BOR (properly enabled), or an external supervisor IC for margin, for sequencing several chips together, or when the supply is slow or noisy. Supervisors also provide a hold time after release, often combined with a watchdog input.

## Brown-out and flash

Digital logic needs a minimum VDD to meet timing at its clock frequency. As VDD sags (a battery under load, a motor starting), the CPU may mis-execute without crashing outright, and if it is in the middle of writing flash the cell can be programmed incorrectly. A brown-out detector compares VDD with a threshold (programmable on many parts, often with hysteresis) and resets the chip, then holds it until VDD recovers. For battery products, choose the threshold above the voltage at which the clock speed in use stops being safe, but below the normal minimum battery voltage so it doesn't reset in normal use.

## Reset in the system

- **Multiple chips**: tie reset lines together (open-drain wired-AND) so a single event restarts the whole board in a known order, or sequence them: processor after its memory and PHY are powered.
- **Debugging**: the debugger can assert NRST, which is why the SWD header includes it; "connect under reset" is the way to reach a part whose firmware disables the debug pins or sleeps immediately.
- **Boot configuration pins** are sampled at the reset release, so their pull-ups/pull-downs must be stable by then.
- **Watchdog interplay**: a window watchdog catches code that runs too fast as well as too slow.
- **Software resets** preserve some state: warm reset keeps RAM, so retained variables survive but must be validated.

::: followups
### What is the difference between a POR and a BOR?
POR acts as VDD rises from zero through its threshold at start-up, holding the chip until the supply is valid. BOR watches VDD during operation and triggers when it falls below a (usually higher, programmable) threshold, so the chip doesn't run on a sagging supply. Many chips combine them with hysteresis.

### Why is reset usually active low?
Historically, open-collector/open-drain outputs can only pull low, so many devices can share one line by wired-AND, with a pull-up providing the released state. Also, if the pin is shorted to ground or disconnected, behaviour is defined (low means reset). Active-low also tolerates a pulled-up default and noise on supply rails more gracefully.

### How long must a reset pulse be?
Long enough to meet the datasheet minimum, typically hundreds of ns to a few µs for the NRST pin, and longer for the full chip to stabilize at power-up: until the supply is valid and the oscillator is running. A supervisor typically adds a timeout of tens to hundreds of ms.

### The board resets randomly when a motor starts. What do you check?
Supply sag or noise on VDD or NRST. Check VDD at the MCU pins with a scope during motor start: if it dips below the BOR threshold, add bulk capacitance, a separate regulator or lower the load step. If NRST glitches, add a capacitor close to the pin, improve grounding and routing, and keep NRST away from noisy traces.

### Does reset clear RAM and flash?
Flash is non-volatile and unaffected. RAM normally keeps its contents through a warm reset (it is not guaranteed after power loss or brown-out), so startup code zeroes `.bss` and copies initialized data. This also allows deliberately retained data across software resets, but it has to be validated.

### Scenario: your product fails to start about 1 in 50 cold starts, usually in the cold. What do you suspect?
A reset or power-up timing problem: cold slows the supply ramp or oscillator start-up, and a bare RC releases NRST while VDD is still low. Probe VDD, NRST and the clock together on a scope during failed starts, check reset release voltage and delay against the datasheet minimums, then add a supervisor or enable the BOR with proper threshold, and verify across temperature and ramp-rate corners.
