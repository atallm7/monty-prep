::: testing
Whether you can **turn a loss budget into an action list** and rank the actions by gain and cost. The interviewer is looking for:

1. A framework: find the dominant loss first, then pick the fix that matches it (conduction vs switching vs fixed loss).
2. A wide enough list: FET selection, synchronous rectification, inductor choice, switching frequency, dead time, edge rates, light-load mode, multiphase, input voltage and thermal/layout.
3. The trade-off attached to each (gate charge, size, ripple, EMI, cost).
4. Numbers: how many points of efficiency each change is worth for a concrete converter, and that the right answer depends on the load profile.
:::

::: why
"Make it more efficient" is a daily request: battery life, thermal limits, regulatory efficiency standards. The wrong move is to start swapping parts; the right move is to measure, attribute the loss and then change the biggest item. Interviewers like this question because it sorts people who know the topic as a list from people who know how to prioritize. It builds on the loss budget in [[q0213]] and sets up the "what limits it" discussion in [[q0217]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Loss budget**: conduction ($I^2R$), switching ($VIt f$, $C_{oss}$, $Q_{rr}$), gate drive, inductor DCR and core, quiescent ([[q0213]]).
- **Load dependence**: conduction scales with $I^2$; fixed and switching losses dominate at light load ([[q0217]]).
- **Frequency trade-off**: lower $f$ cuts switching loss but needs bigger $L$ and $C$ ([[q0216]]).
- **Synchronous rectification** and dead-time management ([[q0203]], [[q0210]]).
- **Modes**: forced PWM vs diode emulation and PFM at light load.
:::

::: answer
"I'd start by measuring efficiency across the load range and working out where the loss is, because the fix depends on that. At heavy load the loss is mostly conduction, $I^2R$ in the FETs and the inductor. At light load it's fixed and switching loss: gate drive, $C_{oss}$, quiescent current.

For heavy load: lower $R_{DS(on)}$ FETs, especially on the low side, which conducts most of the time, and an inductor with lower DCR, which in practice means a bigger part. If the current is high enough, go multiphase so each phase carries less and conduction scales as $I^2/N$. Keep the layout copper short and thick.

For switching loss: reduce the frequency and compensate with a bigger inductor, use FETs with low $Q_g$ and $Q_{rr}$, speed up the edges if EMI allows, and tune dead time with adaptive control.

For light load: use a PFM or pulse-skipping mode so the effective frequency falls with load, use diode emulation, and pick a part with low quiescent current.

At the system level, lower the input voltage if you can: switching loss scales with $V_{in}$. In my 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A example, halving the low-side resistance gains about 0.5 to 0.8 points, dropping the frequency to 300 kHz with a bigger inductor gains about a point, and PFM gives 25 points at 50 mA."
:::

::: technical
**Reference** (12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, 500 kHz, $L=4.7$ µH; budget 457 mW, 93.5%; see [[q0213]]). Sensitivities from the loss model (illustrative):

| Change | $\Delta\eta$ (points) | Cost / catch |
|---|---|---|
| Low-side FET 40 to 20 mΩ | +0.8 (ideal), +0.4 with higher $Q_g$, $C_{oss}$, $Q_{rr}$ | cost, area |
| High-side FET 60 to 40 mΩ | +0.3 | $Q_{gd}$ up |
| Inductor DCR 20 to 10 mΩ | +0.5 (+0.3 if core loss rises 50%) | bigger or costlier part |
| Frequency 500 to 300 kHz, $L$ to 7.8 µH | +1.0 | larger $L$, slower transient |
| Frequency 500 kHz to 1 MHz, $L$ 2.35 µH | $-2.6$ | smaller parts |
| Adaptive dead time 30 to 10 ns, $Q_{rr}$ halved | +0.4 | controller feature |
| Edge times 5 to 2.5 ns | +0.4 | EMI, ringing |
| Edge times 5 to 10 ns | $-0.8$ | lower EMI |
| $V_{in}$ 12 to 5 V | +0.9 | needs a lower rail |
| $V_{in}$ 12 to 24 V | $-1.7$ | |
| Quiescent 1 mA to 0.2 mA | +0.1 | light-load benefit is larger |

**Conduction vs frequency loss structure.** $P_{cond}\propto I^2R$; $P_{sw}\propto V f I$; $P_{fixed}\propto f$ (gate, $C_{oss}$, $Q_{rr}$, core); $P_{iq}$ constant.

**Light load.** With PFM the effective frequency scales with load below a threshold: for the example at 50 mA, efficiency rises from 59% (forced PWM) to 86% (PFM model, effective frequency 10% of nominal).

**Multiphase.** $N$ phases: conduction $\propto P_{c1}/N$, fixed loss $\propto N$, so $N^*=\sqrt{P_{c1}/P_{f}}$. Example (12 V to 1.2 V, 20 A, $P_{c1}=2.0$ W, $P_f=0.15$ W per phase): total loss 2.15 W (1 phase), 1.30 W (2), 1.12 W (3), 1.10 W (4), 1.23 W (6).

**Thermal.** $R_{DS(on)}$ rises about 0.4%/°C, so 100 °C junction means about 30% more conduction loss than at 25 °C; copper, airflow and layout feed back into efficiency.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The percentages come from an assumed loss model and generic part parameters, so treat them as relative sizes of the options, not predictions for a specific design.

::: long
"My approach is to measure first. I'd get an efficiency curve across the load range and, if I can, estimate the loss budget, because the fix depends on which loss dominates.

At heavy load the budget is mostly conduction loss, $I^2R$. In a 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A buck the low-side FET is the biggest single item, then the inductor's DCR, then the high-side FET. So: lower on-resistance FETs, particularly on the low side because it conducts 72% of the time; an inductor with lower DCR, which usually means a physically larger part; and for tens of amps, multiphase, because conduction loss falls as $I^2/N$. I'd pick the number of phases where the added fixed losses balance the savings, which in a 20 A, 1.2 V example is about four. I'd also look at copper resistance and thermal design, because $R_{DS(on)}$ rises about 0.4% per degree.

For switching loss, the main knob is frequency: dropping from 500 to 300 kHz and raising $L$ to keep the same ripple gives me about a point of efficiency in this example. Beyond that, I'd choose FETs with a good $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_g$ and low $Q_{rr}$, use adaptive dead time to shrink body-diode loss, and speed up the edges if EMI allows: 5 ns to 2.5 ns is worth 0.4 points.

For light load, the fixed losses dominate. Use a PFM or pulse-skipping mode so frequency drops with load, diode emulation to avoid reverse current, and a low-quiescent-current controller. That's worth 25 points at 50 milliamps.

At the system level, lower $V_{in}$ when you can, since switching loss goes with $V_{in}$, and consider an intermediate bus or direct conversion.

I'd also mention that each fix has a trade: lower frequency needs a bigger inductor and is slower; faster edges bring EMI; better FETs cost more."
:::

::: terms
- **Loss budget**: itemized power loss by mechanism and component.
- **Conduction loss**: $I_{rms}^2R$.
- **Switching loss**: overlap, $C_{oss}$ and recovery losses that scale with $f_{sw}$.
- **Fixed (load-independent) loss**: gate drive, quiescent current, parts of core loss.
- **PFM / pulse skipping**: lowering the switching rate at light load.
- **Diode emulation (DEM)**: turning off the low-side FET at zero current.
- **Multiphase**: several interleaved phases sharing the load current ([[q0215]]).
- **$R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_g$ FOM**: trade-off between conduction and switching loss in a FET.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Use better FETs and a better inductor, lower the frequency, use sync rectification."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts with measurement and attribution, and ties each fix to a loss term and to the load range where it matters.
- Quantifies a few fixes for a concrete converter and ranks them (frequency and low-side FET and PFM on top).
- Names the trade each one creates and the interactions ($R_{DS(on)}$ vs $Q_g$; $f$ vs $L$ and $C$; edge rate vs EMI).
- Mentions system-level options: multiphase with an optimum $N$, lower $V_{in}$ or intermediate bus, GaN, thermal/layout, topology.
- Notes that efficiency should be optimized for the actual load profile, not the peak number on the datasheet.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Optimizing for the wrong load point.** A converter that idles at 50 mA all day needs PFM and low $I_q$ more than a lower $R_{DS(on)}$.
- **"Lower the frequency" with no other change.** Fixed $L$ at lower $f$ increases ripple current and RMS loss; you must raise $L$ (and check saturation).
- **Chasing $R_{DS(on)}$ alone.** A lower-resistance FET adds gate charge and capacitance; at high $f$ it can lose.
- **Ignoring temperature.** Hot FETs have 30% higher resistance; the datasheet at 25 °C is optimistic.
- **Forgetting layout.** Milliohms of copper and the loop inductance cost real efficiency and add ringing.
:::

::: deep
## Step 0: find the dominant loss

Efficiency is $\eta=P_{out}/(P_{out}+P_{loss})$, and $P_{loss}$ is a sum of terms with different load dependence ([[q0213]]):

| Term | Scales with | Dominates at |
|---|---|---|
| Conduction ($I_{rms}^2R$) in FETs, DCR | $I^2$ | heavy load |
| Overlap switching ($\tfrac12VI\,t\,f$) | $V_{in}\,I\,f$ | moderate/heavy load and high $V_{in}$ |
| $C_{oss}$, gate drive, $Q_{rr}$, core | $f$ (and $V_{in}$) | light load, high frequency |
| Quiescent current | constant | standby |

## Fixes for conduction loss (heavy load)

1. **Lower $R_{DS(on)}$**, especially the low side: conduction $(1-D)I^2R_L$ is the largest item for small $D$ ([[q0205]]). Cutting 40 to 20 mΩ saves about 60 mW ($+0.8$ points) before counting extra gate charge; with a 50% increase in $Q_g$, $C_{oss}$ and $Q_{rr}$ the net is $+0.4$.
2. **Lower DCR inductor**: $I_{rms}^2\,DCR$ is 82 mW. Halving DCR saves about 41 mW but costs board area or a thicker winding; core loss may rise with a different core or a smaller turn count.
3. **Multiphase / paralleling**: each phase carries $I/N$; total conduction $\propto I^2/N$. Fixed losses scale with $N$: the optimum $N^*=\sqrt{P_{c1}/P_f}$. For 20 A, 1.2 V ($P_{c1}=2.0$ W, $P_f=0.15$ W per phase):

| Phases $N$ | Conduction | Fixed | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2.00 W | 0.15 W | 2.15 W |
| 2 | 1.00 W | 0.30 W | 1.30 W |
| 3 | 0.67 W | 0.45 W | 1.12 W |
| 4 | 0.50 W | 0.60 W | 1.10 W |
| 6 | 0.33 W | 0.90 W | 1.23 W |

4. **Layout and copper**: 10 mΩ extra path resistance at 2 A costs 40 mW. Use wide planes, multiple vias, short high-current paths.
5. **Thermal management**: $R_{DS(on)}$ rises about 0.4%/°C ($+30\%$ at 100 °C). Better copper and airflow lower temperature and conduction loss.
6. **Gate drive voltage**: higher $V_{GS}$ (within limits) lowers $R_{DS(on)}$, but costs gate-drive power $Q_gV_{drv}f$; typical optimum 5 to 10 V.

## Fixes for switching and fixed loss

1. **Lower the switching frequency**, with $L$ increased to hold the same ripple current: in the example, 300 kHz with 7.8 µH is $+1.0$ point. Keep $L$ fixed and the ripple rises and the gain shrinks to $+0.4$ ([[q0216]]).
2. **Pick FETs by $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_g$ and $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{oss}$** for the high side (hard switching) and by $R_{DS(on)}$ and $Q_{rr}$ for the low side.
3. **Dead-time management**: adaptive dead time and a Schottky or low-$Q_{rr}$ FET ([[q0210]]): 30 to 10 ns is $+0.4$.
4. **Faster edges** (stronger driver, lower gate resistance, low inductance layout): 5 ns to 2.5 ns is $+0.4$, but ringing and EMI worsen; slowing to 10 ns costs $-0.8$.
5. **Lower $V_{in}$**: switching loss scales with $V_{in}$. 12 V to 5 V is worth about $+0.9$ (and $-1.7$ if you go up to 24 V). An intermediate bus or two-stage architecture may help, but the first stage has its own loss.
6. **Soft switching / GaN**: ZVS techniques and wide-bandgap devices (low $Q_g$, $Q_{oss}$, no $Q_{rr}$) allow megahertz operation without proportional loss.

## Fixes for light-load efficiency

- **PFM / pulse skipping / burst mode**: below a threshold current, the controller skips cycles, so effective frequency drops with load and fixed losses scale down.
- **Diode emulation**: stops the inductor current at zero, avoiding circulating negative current ([[q0203]]).
- **Low-quiescent-current controller**: many parts give a few µA to tens of µA; matters for standby.
- **Phase shedding** in multiphase: turn off phases as load drops.
- **Bias and driver supply from the output** (rather than a lossy linear regulator from $V_{in}$) to save the drop and quiescent power.

## Ranking by return for this converter

| Rank | Option | Gain (points) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lower frequency with bigger $L$ (if size allows) | about 1.0 |
| 2 | Low-side FET resistance | 0.4 to 0.8 |
| 3 | Lower $V_{in}$ | 0.9 |
| 4 | Lower-DCR inductor | 0.3 to 0.5 |
| 5 | Adaptive dead time / low-$Q_{rr}$ | 0.4 |
| 6 | Faster edges (EMI cost) | 0.4 |
| 7 | High-side FET resistance | 0.3 |
| 8 | Lower quiescent current | 0.1 at full load; large at standby |

At 50 mA, PFM is worth about 26 points; no other change comes close.

## Verification

Measure efficiency at several loads and temperatures with $V_{in}$ and $V_{out}$ measured at the board (not at the supply), including the controller's quiescent supply. A thermal camera quickly shows which part is hot, giving the loss attribution directly.

::: applications
- **Battery-powered and IoT devices**: PFM modes and nanoamp quiescent-current regulators for standby efficiency.
- **Server/CPU VRMs**: multiphase with phase shedding, tens of amps at about 1 V.
- **USB-PD chargers and adapters**: high-efficiency standards; GaN and soft switching at higher frequency.
- **Automotive**: efficiency vs thermal limit in a hot environment; lower frequency to cut switching losses at high $V_{in}$.
- **Point-of-load converters in networking gear**: choosing FET and inductor combinations to meet efficiency-at-load-profile targets.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Reference converter** is the 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, 500 kHz, 4.7 µH synchronous buck of [[q0213]] with generic FET and inductor parameters.
- **PFM model** is idealized: effective switching frequency scales with load below 0.5 A, other terms constant.
- **Constant-ripple** assumption when changing frequency ($L\propto1/f$); DCR and size scale as stated.
- **Percentages** are modeled and illustrative; a real part's data may differ in sign for small changes.
- **Different operating points** (higher $V_{in}$, lower $V_{out}$, bigger current) shift the ranking, for example low-side conduction dominates more at small $D$.

::: followups
### Which single change gives the most efficiency at 2 A? At 50 mA?
At 2 A in this example, lower switching frequency with a larger inductor (about +1 point) and a lower-resistance low-side FET (+0.4 to +0.8). At 50 mA, switching to a PFM mode or diode emulation (about +25 points), because fixed losses dominate and scale with frequency.

### Why can lowering the frequency hurt if I don't change the inductor?
$\Delta I_L\propto1/(Lf)$, so ripple current rises as $f$ falls. RMS current increases ($I^2+\Delta I^2/12$) and core loss rises with flux swing; the peak current may approach the inductor's saturation. So lower $f$ only pays when $L$ rises proportionally.

### When does multiphase make sense for efficiency, and how many phases?
When conduction loss at full load is much larger than the fixed loss of a phase. $N^*=\sqrt{P_{c1}/P_f}$: with 2.0 W single-phase conduction and 0.15 W fixed per phase, about 4 phases (1.10 W total vs 2.15 W). Phase shedding keeps efficiency high at lower load.

### Would a GaN FET improve efficiency here?
At 500 kHz and 12 V to 3.3 V it would help a bit (lower $Q_g$, $Q_{oss}$ and no $Q_{rr}$ save maybe 50 mW), but conduction would not change much. It pays off at frequencies of several MHz or higher input voltages where silicon's switching loss dominates, and with the shorter dead time its reverse conduction is small ([[q0210]]).

### The converter is 94% at 2 A but only 72% at 100 mA. What do you do?
Light-load fixed losses are the issue (about 100 to 130 mW). Check the mode: if forced PWM, switch to diode emulation or PFM; reduce quiescent current; lower the gate-drive supply; consider a smaller FET for light load or phase shedding. If the load profile is mostly 100 mA, optimize for that, not 2 A.
