::: basics
### In plain words
External EMI is interference that comes from outside your board: radio transmitters, nearby motors, mains noise and static zaps. Every loop of copper and every cable acts as an antenna that picks it up. The effects are unwanted voltage added to weak signals, DC shifts from rectification, resets and glitches in digital chips, and sometimes damage. Connectors and inputs are the entry points.

### Picture it
Imagine rain falling on a yard full of open buckets. A bigger opening catches more rain. A long gutter, like a cable, funnels water in from far away. Loops and cables on a board collect radio energy in the same way. The doors of the house are the connectors.

### Words you need
- **Immunity**: how well a circuit keeps working when outside interference arrives.
- **Induced voltage**: the unwanted voltage a field creates in a loop or wire.
- **Loop area**: the area enclosed by a wire and its return path. The induced voltage is proportional to it.
- **Rectification**: a diode-like junction passes current one way, so fast radio-frequency (RF) energy turns into a steady DC shift.
- **ESD**: electrostatic discharge, a static zap with a very fast edge.

### The simplest example
Suppose a strong radio field of 10 volts per meter at 100 MHz hits a 10 cm² loop on a board. The induced voltage is about 21 mV. If the loop carries a 20 mV sensor signal, the interference is as big as the signal. The induced voltage is proportional to loop area, so shrinking the loop to 1 cm² cuts it to about 2 mV. Even if the amplifier cannot respond at 100 MHz, its input junctions can rectify the RF, so the reading shifts when a phone is nearby.

That is the whole idea: loops and cables collect interference, and junctions turn it into errors. The rest of this page covers sources, entry paths, and failure modes in more detail.
:::

::: why
Real products live next to phones, motors, welders and static-charged people. A board that is quiet on the bench can misread a sensor when a phone rings next to it, or reset when someone touches a connector. Standards (IEC 61000-4-x) test this, and customers return products that fail it in the field. Understanding the mechanisms is what lets you protect a design without guessing.
:::

- **RF rectification**: nonlinear junctions demodulate RF into DC and low-frequency error.
- **ESD and surge**: fast, high-energy transients injected at connectors.
- **Immunity testing**: IEC 61000-4-2/-3/-4/-5/-6 families. See [[q0559]].

::: technical
**Sources.** Intentional transmitters (broadcast, cellular, Wi-Fi, radar), industrial equipment (motors, welders, switching supplies), mains transients, ESD (human body/air discharge), lightning-induced surges, other boards in the same enclosure.

**Entry paths.**
- *Radiated into loops*: $v = A\,dB/dt$. For a plane wave, $B = E/c$, so for a sinusoid $v_{pk} = 2\pi f\,E\,A/c$. At 10 V/m, 100 MHz, 10 cm²: 21 mV. At 3 V/m: 6.3 mV.
- *Radiated into traces/cables*: an electric field induces a voltage on a conductor of effective height $h_e$: $v \approx E h_e$; a 1 m cable at 10 V/m can see volts of common-mode voltage near resonance.
- *Conducted*: noise on power and signal lines; cable common-mode currents.
- *Apertures and seams*: fields leak through enclosure openings.
- *Ground*: transient current in shared ground raises the local reference.

**Effects.**
- *Linear pickup*: added signal on high-impedance or low-level nodes.
- *Rectification (EMI-induced offset)*: for an input stage with an exponential characteristic, RF amplitude $V_{rf}$ gives a DC shift on the order of $V_{rf}^2/(4V_T)$ for a junction (illustrative); even RF above the amplifier's bandwidth causes offsets because the input junctions still respond. Amplifier datasheets often specify EMI rejection ratio (EMIRR) for this reason.
- *Digital upsets*: glitches on resets, clocks and interrupts; metastability.
- *Damage*: ESD, surge, latch-up.

**Mitigation overview** ([[q0564]]): small loops, differential signalling, RC/LC filtering at inputs, ferrite beads and common-mode chokes, TVS/ESD protection with low-inductance return, solid planes, shielding, chassis bonding.
:::

By reciprocity, it gets in the same way my board would radiate. Every loop of copper is a magnetic antenna, every long trace and cable is an electric antenna, and cables also carry conducted noise and common-mode current in from outside.

What it does is circuit-dependent. On analog inputs it adds directly, but there's a subtler effect: rectification. Transistor junctions in op-amp inputs and ESD diodes are nonlinear, so they demodulate RF. A 900 MHz signal that the amplifier can't even amplify shifts its DC offset or leaves an audible buzz, because the input junctions still rectify it. That's why amplifier datasheets quote EMI rejection ratio. Digital circuits see glitches on reset, interrupt and clock lines, and ESD can reset or latch up a part, or destroy it.

So the protection strategy is at the entry points: small loops, differential signalling, RC or ferrite filters at connectors, TVS diodes with a short return, solid ground planes, and short traces on high-impedance nodes."
:::

::: terms
- **Immunity / susceptibility**: how well a circuit tolerates external interference.
- **Induced voltage**: $v = d\Phi/dt$ in a loop exposed to a changing field.
- **RF rectification**: demodulation of RF by a nonlinear junction into DC or low-frequency error.
- **EMIRR**: EMI rejection ratio, a measure of an amplifier's immunity to RF at its inputs.
- **ESD**: electrostatic discharge, a nanosecond-scale transient of amps to tens of amps.
- **Surge**: high-energy transient on power or signal lines (lightning, load switching).
- **Field strength (V/m)**: the electric-field amplitude used in radiated immunity tests (for example 3 V/m or 10 V/m levels).
- **Aperture**: opening in a shield or enclosure that lets fields leak.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"RF above the bandwidth is harmless."** The input junctions still rectify it; amplifiers have EMIRR specs for that reason.
- **Treating only digital lines as vulnerable.** Analog front ends with high impedance and low-level signals are often worse.
- **Ignoring cable length.** A cable is the best antenna on the product and carries interference to the connector.
- **Assuming a metal enclosure solves everything.** Seams, openings and cable penetrations let energy in.
- **Putting the protection far from the connector.** The noise radiates onto the board before reaching the protection.
- **Confusing immunity with emissions.** They share physics but are separate tests and different failure types.
:::

::: deep
## What "external" means

External EMI includes:

| Source | Typical spectrum | Example effect |
|---|---|---|
| Broadcast, cellular, Wi-Fi, radar | 30 MHz to several GHz, narrowband, often AM-modulated | Rectified audio buzz on analog inputs; offset shifts |
| Industrial equipment (motors, welders, SMPS) | Broadband to hundreds of MHz | Glitches, burst noise on cables |
| Mains and switching transients | kHz to tens of MHz | Conducted noise on supplies |
| ESD (human/air) | Sub-ns edges, GHz content | Resets, latch-up, damage |
| Surge (lightning, load dump) | µs scale, high energy | Component failure |

## How much gets in: loop pickup

For a plane wave in free space, $H = E/\eta_0$ with $\eta_0 = 377$ Ω, and $B = \mu_0H = E/c$. A loop of area $A$ perpendicular to $B$ has induced peak voltage $v = \omega B A = 2\pi f\,E\,A/c$.

## What it does: rectification

A diode or transistor junction has $i = I_s(e^{v/V_T}-1)$. An RF voltage $V_{rf}\cos\omega t$ superimposed on a bias gives an average current shift proportional to $V_{rf}^2$ (second-order term), so the junction produces a DC component $\Delta V\approx V_{rf}^2/(4V_T)$ for small amplitude (with $V_T$ = 26 mV: for 20 mV RF, 3.8 mV of DC shift). If the RF is amplitude-modulated at audio, the rectified output follows the modulation, which is the familiar GSM "buzz" in speakers. The rectified result lands at DC or low frequency, where the circuit *does* have gain, even though the RF itself is well beyond the bandwidth.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The $V_{rf}^2/(4V_T)$ expression is a first-order illustration for an ideal junction. The real shift in an op-amp depends on topology, source impedance and frequency. Manufacturers characterise it as EMIRR on datasheets; use those figures.

Defences at the input: a series resistor plus capacitor to ground before the amplifier (an RC low-pass with corner far below the RF), keeping the input source impedance low and *balanced* so rectified errors cancel, and short leads.

## Digital circuits and ESD

- **Reset/interrupt/clock** pins with long traces pick up spikes and glitch. Use series resistors, small capacitors, Schmitt inputs and debounce in firmware.
- **ESD**: a human-body discharge has a rise time of about a nanosecond and peak currents of several amps to tens of amps. Without a low-inductance path to chassis, it injects into the board ground. Typical results: reset, corrupted registers, latch-up or damage.
- **Surge** on power lines requires TVS or MOV protection and series impedance.

## Cables and apertures

A cable tens of centimetres long is a better antenna than any trace and exposes the board to both fields and conducted common-mode. Fields also leak through gaps in the enclosure (see [[q0569]], [[q0572]]), so the effective environment inside is not zero.

## What it means for design

1. **Define the environment**: immunity requirements (IEC 61000-4 levels), cable exposure, and consequences of failure.
2. **Protect at the boundary**: connector filters, TVS, chokes ([[q0564]]).
3. **Reduce pickup geometry**: small loops, short high-impedance nodes, differential signalling.
4. **Harden the victim**: low-impedance, filtered inputs; robust firmware for transients (watchdog, retry, checksums).
5. **Test**: pre-compliance with a handheld transmitter or ESD gun, near-field injection, and failure analysis.

## Worked example: from field strength to a DC error, and the filter that fixes it

Take a 10 V/m, 100 MHz field on a 10 cm² input loop feeding an op-amp whose input junction rectifies, as in the plots above. The peak induced voltage is 21 mV. Using the first-order rectification estimate with $V_T=26$ mV:

$$\Delta V\approx\frac{V_{rf}^2}{4V_T}=\frac{(0.0209)^2}{4\times0.026}=4.2\ \text{mV}$$

A 4.2 mV DC offset on a sensor chain that was supposed to resolve 20 mV signals is a 21% error, with no obvious sign on a scope with 20 MHz bandwidth, since the RF itself is far outside the instrument's bandwidth: an error you only see as a "mystery offset".

Now add the standard defence: a 1 kΩ series resistor and a 1 nF capacitor to ground at the input pin. The corner is $1/(2\pi RC)=159$ kHz, and at 100 MHz the ideal attenuation is $1/(2\pi\times10^8\times10^3\times10^{-9})=1/628$, which is $-56$ dB. The RF at the junction becomes $20.9\ \text{mV}/628=33\ \mu$V, and since rectification goes as the square of amplitude the DC shift becomes $(33\times10^{-6})^2/(4\times0.026)\approx10$ nV: negligible. The squared law is the real payoff: reducing the RF amplitude by 56 dB reduces the rectified error by 112 dB.

The cost is that 1 kΩ with the op-amp's input bias current produces an offset ($I_b R$), and the 159 kHz corner limits signal bandwidth. And a real 1 nF capacitor is not ideal: a 0402 part with about 0.7 nH of parasitic inductance self-resonates near 190 MHz and above that it looks like an inductor, so attenuation at 500 MHz to 1 GHz is much less than the ideal calculation predicts. The usual remedy is two capacitors of different value (for example 1 nF and 10 pF) or a ferrite bead in series, and keeping the filter right at the pin so no unfiltered trace remains between the filter and the junction.

Also notice what shrinking the loop does: cutting the area from 10 cm² to 0.5 cm² reduces the induced voltage from 21 mV to about 1 mV, a 20-fold reduction, and the rectified error falls by 400 (the square). Geometry and filtering are complementary, and the geometry is free.

## ESD as a pickup problem

An IEC 61000-4-2 style 8 kV contact discharge has a first current peak of about 30 A with a rise time under 1 ns (commonly 0.7 to 1 ns). With a representative $di/dt=30\ \text{A}/0.8\ \text{ns}=3.75\times10^{10}$ A/s, each nanohenry of path inductance develops $L\,di/dt=37.5$ V, and 10 nH of ground trace between the TVS diode and the chip ground gives 375 V. A TVS diode clamping at 15 V with a leaky 10 nH path therefore does nothing for a 3 V CMOS input. Placement is the entire answer: the protection device goes at the connector with a very short, wide path to chassis or ground, and the series resistor or ferrite between it and the IC makes the surviving stress small.

## Limiting cases

- **Loop below the wavelength limit:** the $2\pi f E A/c$ formula holds while the loop's largest dimension is under about $\lambda/10$ (30 cm at 100 MHz, 3 cm at 1 GHz). Above that, current is not uniform and real pickup saturates.
- **Balanced inputs:** if the pickup is identical on both inputs of a differential amplifier it is common-mode and CMRR rejects it, but the rejection at 100 MHz is often only 20 to 40 dB because CMRR falls with frequency and any source-impedance imbalance converts common-mode to differential.
- **Narrowband vs broadband:** a single 10 V/m carrier is a rectification and offset problem; broadband impulsive interference (ESD, relay arcs) is a glitch, reset and latch-up problem. They need different defenses.

## Bench verification

1. **Radiated immunity pre-test:** hold a handheld transmitter at increasing distances from the board and cables while a scope monitors the victim output; the distance at which the output moves is a rough sensitivity index.
2. **Conducted injection:** inject RF onto a cable with a bulk-current-injection probe at a few dBm and sweep 1 to 400 MHz. The frequency where the output shifts most points at the resonant path.
3. **ESD gun walkthrough** at low voltage (500 V steps) at connectors, cases and nearby points, watching for resets and corrupted registers. Increase only after the weakest spot is found and fixed.
4. **Verify the fix by repeating the same test**, so that the dB improvement is real and not caused by changed setup.

::: applications
- **Medical sensors (ECG, EEG)**: microvolt signals exposed to mains pickup and RF; instrumentation amplifiers with input RC filters.
- **Automotive modules**: tested for radiated and conducted immunity and transients; heavily filtered I/O.
- **Audio equipment**: GSM-style buzz from rectification at amplifier inputs; fixed by RF filters on inputs.
- **Industrial controllers**: connector protection for ESD, EFT and surge.
- **Smartphones and wearables**: ESD events on exposed connectors and buttons.

::: assumptions
- **Plane-wave, far-field exposure** with the loop perpendicular to the field gives the worst-case value used here; real fields may be near-field, polarised differently or partially shielded by an enclosure.
- **Small-loop approximation** requires dimensions well below $\lambda/10$; above that the induced voltage saturates and has resonances.
- **Rectification estimates are illustrative**; use datasheet EMIRR where available.
- **Test levels** such as 3 V/m or 10 V/m are typical immunity levels; product standards define the real ones.
- **Alternative answer**: for mains-connected products, conducted transients (surge, EFT) usually dominate over radiated fields.
:::

::: followups
### How do you estimate the voltage a loop picks up?
$v = A\,dB/dt$; for a plane wave $B = E/c$, so $v_{pk} = 2\pi f E A/c$. At 100 MHz, 10 V/m and 10 cm²: $2\pi\cdot10^8\cdot10\cdot10^{-3}/(3\times10^8) = 21$ mV. It scales linearly with area, frequency and field.

### Why would RF at 900 MHz affect an op-amp that only has 1 MHz of bandwidth?
The input transistors respond at 900 MHz even if the amplifier can't amplify it. Their nonlinearity rectifies the RF into a DC shift (and, if modulated, low-frequency noise) which the amplifier *can* amplify. Defences are an RC filter at the input, low and balanced source impedance, and parts with high EMIRR.

### What's the difference between ESD and EFT/surge?
ESD is a very fast (sub-ns to ns rise), low-energy discharge from a person or object, injected at touch points. EFT is bursts of fast pulses from switching inductive loads on mains or signal cables. Surge is a slower (µs to ms), high-energy pulse from lightning or large load switching. Each uses different protection: TVS and layout for ESD, filters for EFT, MOV/TVS and series impedance for surge.

### A sensor reading drifts when a cellphone is nearby. How do you debug?
Reproduce with a transmitting phone and see if the effect is at the modulation (audio) rate, suggesting rectification. Probe the input with a scope. Try an RC filter at the amplifier pins, shorten leads, or ground the cable shield at the connector. If the effect disappears with a metal shield over the front end, entry is radiated; if it vanishes with the cable unplugged, entry is via the cable.

### Scenario: board resets when a person touches the USB connector shell. What do you check?
ESD path. The shell must be bonded to chassis (or connected to ground with low inductance), and the discharge current should be shunted by a TVS or a capacitor to chassis near the connector, not through the logic ground. Check the reset line: add a series resistor and a small capacitor, and make sure the watchdog or brown-out threshold isn't tripped by ground bounce.
:::
