::: testing
Whether you can explain a switched-capacitor converter with **charge, not magic**: capacitors are charged in one phase and re-wired in the next, so energy moves in packets of charge. The interviewer wants the two-phase picture, the role of the flying and output capacitors, the fact that no inductor is involved, and the consequences (fixed ratios, output resistance, ripple). Bonus: you distinguish a power charge pump from the PLL "charge pump" and you know the input current is a multiple of the output current.
:::

::: why
Charge pumps are the standard way to make a rail without an inductor: a negative bias from a positive supply, a gate-drive rail above the supply, an RS-232 level, a small LED backlight supply. They are small, quiet in EMI terms and cheap, but they behave very differently from inductive converters: output resistance instead of duty-cycle regulation. Knowing how they work tells you what they can and cannot deliver ([[q0287]], [[q0288]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor charge and voltage**: $Q=CV$; voltage across a capacitor cannot jump. See [[q0022]] and [[q0037]].
- **Switches as phases**: two non-overlapping clock phases $\phi_1,\phi_2$ re-wire the capacitors.
- **Series stacking**: voltages add when sources are in series.
- **Charge conservation**: charge leaving the flying capacitor equals charge delivered to the output per cycle.
- **Conversion ratio**: ideal $V_{out}/V_{in}$ is fixed by topology (2x, -1x, 1.5x).
- **Contrast with the boost**: an inductor stores energy in a field ([[q0270]]); a charge pump stores it in electric fields of capacitors.
:::

::: answer
"A charge pump moves charge with switches and capacitors to make a voltage that's a multiple of the input, with no inductor.

Take a doubler. In phase one, two switches connect the flying capacitor across the input, so it charges up to $V_{in}$. In phase two, the switches re-wire it so its bottom plate goes to $V_{in}$ and its top plate goes to the output. Now the capacitor is stacked on top of the input source, so the output node sits at $V_{in}+V_{in}=2V_{in}$ and the capacitor delivers its charge to the output capacitor and the load. Then it flips back and recharges. The output capacitor holds the output steady between pulses.

The thing to remember is that it isn't free energy. Each cycle the flying capacitor takes a packet of charge from the input in phase one and again in phase two, so the input current is about twice the output current. And the output isn't stiff: the more current you draw, the more the flying capacitor droops and the switch resistance drops voltage, so the output looks like $2V_{in}$ minus $I_{out}R_{out}$. That's why charge pumps suit low currents, tens to a few hundred milliamps."
:::

::: technical
**Doubler, ideal switches.** Phase 1: $C_{fly}$ across $V_{in}$, charges to $V_C=V_{in}$; $C_{out}$ supplies the load. Phase 2: $C_{fly}$ in series with $V_{in}$ onto the output: $V_{out}=V_{in}+V_C=2V_{in}$ (ideal, no load).

**Under load.** Charge per cycle delivered to the output: $Q=I_{out}/f_{sw}$. The flying capacitor loses $\Delta V=Q/C_{fly}$, so
$$V_{out}=2V_{in}-\frac{I_{out}}{f_{sw}C_{fly}}-I_{out}R_{sw,eff}\;\Rightarrow\;V_{out}=2V_{in}-I_{out}R_{out}$$
with $R_{out}\approx\sqrt{R_{SSL}^2+R_{FSL}^2}$, $R_{SSL}=\dfrac{1}{f_{sw}C_{fly}}$ (slow-switching limit) and $R_{FSL}=8R_{sw}$ (fast-switching limit, four switches of resistance $R_{sw}$, two in series per phase; add $4R_{ESR}$). The root-sum-square is an approximation.

**Currents.** $I_{in}=2I_{out}$ (plus quiescent), so ideal efficiency is $V_{out}/(2V_{in})$.

**Example.** $V_{in}=3.3$ V, $f=500$ kHz, $C_{fly}=1\ \mu$F, $R_{sw}=1\ \Omega$: $R_{SSL}=2\ \Omega$, $R_{FSL}=8\ \Omega$, $R_{out}=8.25\ \Omega$. At 20 mA: $V_{out}=6.6-0.165=6.435$ V.

**Startup (no load).** Charge sharing gives $V_{out}[n]=2V_{in}(1-r^n)$ with $r=C_{out}/(C_{fly}+C_{out})$.
:::

::: short
"A charge pump uses switches and capacitors, no inductor. In phase one a flying capacitor is charged to $V_{in}$. In phase two it's re-wired in series with the input and dumps its charge into the output capacitor, so the output is about $2V_{in}$. Input current is roughly twice the output current, and the output droops with load like $2V_{in}-I_{out}R_{out}$, where $R_{out}$ depends on $1/(fC)$ and the switch resistance."
:::

::: long
"A charge pump is a switched-capacitor converter: capacitors and switches, no inductor, making a voltage that's a fixed multiple of the input.

Here's the voltage doubler. There's a flying capacitor, an output capacitor and four switches driven by a two-phase clock. In phase one, the flying capacitor is connected across the input, so it charges to $V_{in}$. In phase two, the switches disconnect it from ground and connect its bottom plate to $V_{in}$ and its top plate to the output. Now the capacitor is in series with the input, like stacking a second battery on top, so the output tries to go to $2V_{in}$ and the capacitor passes its charge to the output capacitor and load. The output capacitor holds the voltage during phase one, when the flying capacitor is away.

Energy-wise, nothing is created. Each cycle the input supplies one packet of charge in phase one and one in phase two, so the input current is about twice the output current, and the efficiency tops out at $V_{out}/(2V_{in})$.

The output isn't regulated by duty cycle. Under load the flying capacitor loses voltage, $\Delta V=I/(fC)$, and the switches drop voltage, so the output is $2V_{in}-I_{out}R_{out}$. With 3.3 V in, 500 kHz, 1 µF and 1 Ω switches, $R_{out}$ is about 8 Ω, so 20 mA costs 165 mV. Regulated parts add a feedback comparator that skips cycles or an LDO stage, and some switch between 1x, 1.5x and 2x ratios.

That's why they're used for small currents: no inductor, little EMI, but a few hundred milliamps at most."
:::

::: terms
- **Flying capacitor ($C_{fly}$)**: the capacitor that is moved between configurations to carry charge.
- **Reservoir / output capacitor ($C_{out}$)**: holds the output between pulses.
- **Two-phase clock**: non-overlapping $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ that control the switches.
- **Conversion ratio**: ideal $V_{out}/V_{in}$ (2, 1.5, -1, ...).
- **Output resistance ($R_{out}$)**: slope of $V_{out}$ vs load current.
- **SSL / FSL**: slow- and fast-switching limits of $R_{out}$.
- **Dickson pump**: diode-capacitor chain version ([[q0286]]).
- **Charge sharing**: redistribution when two capacitors at different voltages are connected.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "capacitors are charged then switched in series to double the voltage".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names the two phases and which switches close in each, and says where each plate goes.
- States input current $=2\times$ output current, so efficiency is bounded by $V_{out}/(2V_{in})$.
- Gives $R_{out}$ with its slow/fast limits and a number.
- Explains that regulation requires extra control (burst, linear, mode switching), unlike a buck's duty cycle.
- Notes the inverting variant and that the same idea scales to $N$ stages.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The capacitor amplifies the voltage."** It is a series battery, not an amplifier: current drops by the same factor the voltage rises.
- **Ignoring the load.** The unloaded $2V_{in}$ is never reached under load.
- **Confusing it with the PLL charge pump**, a current-steering circuit.
- **Forgetting the output capacitor.** The flying cap only connects to the output in one phase.
- **Saying efficiency can reach 100% at any load.** It is bounded by the ratio and degraded by $R_{out}$ and overhead.
- **Overlapping switches.** Both phases closed shorts the supply; non-overlap time is essential.
:::

## Why it doubles

At the end of phase 1: $V_{top}-V_{bottom}=V_{in}$. In phase 2 the bottom is forced to $V_{in}$ (relative to ground), and capacitor voltage cannot change instantly, so the top plate goes to $V_{in}+V_{in}=2V_{in}$. If $V_{out}$ is below that, $C_{fly}$ pushes charge into it until the two settle. Charge conservation applied to both capacitors gives the whole behavior.

## Startup: charge sharing

Each cycle the flying capacitor (at $V_{in}$) shares charge with $C_{out}$ (at $V_o$):
$$V_o'=\frac{C_{fly}(2V_{in})+C_{out}V_o}{C_{fly}+C_{out}}\;\Rightarrow\;V_o'-2V_{in}=r\,(V_o-2V_{in}),\quad r=\frac{C_{out}}{C_{fly}+C_{out}}$$

## Under load: where $R_{out}$ comes from

Per cycle the output draws $Q=I_{out}/f$. In phase 2, $C_{fly}$ gives up that charge, so its voltage drops by $Q/C_{fly}$ and the output sits that much below $2V_{in}$:
$$\Delta V_{SSL}=\frac{I_{out}}{fC_{fly}}\;\Rightarrow\;R_{SSL}=\frac{1}{fC_{fly}}$$
Resistive limit: in each phase the charge $Q$ flows in $T/2$ so the current is $2I_{out}$ through two switches: loss $=(2I_{out})^2(2R_{sw})\cdot\tfrac12$ per phase, so $R_{FSL}=8R_{sw}$. Both are modeled by $R_{out}\approx\sqrt{R_{SSL}^2+R_{FSL}^2}$. Raising the frequency lowers $R_{SSL}$ until the switches limit it. Crossover at $f=1/(8R_{sw}C_{fly})=125$ kHz for 1 µF and 1 Ω.

Output at 20 mA: $6.6-0.02\times8.25=6.435$ V; at 100 mA: $6.6-0.825=5.775$ V. Charge pumps are stiff only if the load is small.

## Ripple

$C_{out}$ supplies the load alone in phase 1: $\Delta V\approx I_{out}/(2fC_{out})=0.02/(2\times5\times10^5\times10\ \mu)=2$ mV, plus an ESR step. This ripple is at $f_{sw}$ and has sharp edges, so charge pumps are cleaner than inductor converters at the same current but are not noise-free.

## Variants

- **Inverter:** phase 1 charges $C_{fly}$ across $V_{in}$; phase 2 puts its top plate at ground, so the bottom plate (the output) sits at $-V_{in}$.
- **Fractional (1.5x, 1/2x):** two capacitors, charged in series or parallel and re-stacked, giving ratios between 1 and 2.
- **Multi-stage / Dickson:** $N$ stages give about $(N+1)V_{in}$ ([[q0285]]).
- **Regulated:** burst (skip cycles when above threshold), linear post-regulation or ratio selection ([[q0288]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Negative bias from a single positive rail**: op-amp and ADC front ends, LCD contrast.
- **RS-232 transceivers**: +-10 V from 5 V or 3.3 V (the classic MAX232-style parts).
- **White-LED backlights and camera-flash LED drivers** using 1x/1.5x/2x modes.
- **Gate-drive and high-side supplies** in small switches and load switches.
- **On-chip high-voltage generation** for flash/EEPROM programming and DRAM word lines.
:::

::: assumptions
- Ideal capacitors and equal switch resistance $R_{sw}$; $R_{out}$ is a root-sum-square approximation.
- Clock frequency $f$ is the full cycle frequency (two phases per cycle). Datasheets sometimes quote an oscillator at twice this, so check.
- Doubler, 50% duty, non-overlapping phases, $C_{out}\gg C_{fly}$ for the ripple formula.
- Parts with internal regulation behave differently from the open-loop model here.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the input current twice the output current in a doubler?
In each cycle, $C_{fly}$ receives charge $Q$ from the input in phase 1 and the input again supplies $Q$ through the series path in phase 2, while only $Q$ reaches the output. So $I_{in}=2I_{out}$ and $\eta_{max}=V_{out}/(2V_{in})$.

### What limits how much current a charge pump can supply?
$R_{out}$: at 100 mA with 8.25 Ω the output drops 0.8 V, and switch and capacitor current ratings and thermal limits are reached. Lowering $R_{out}$ needs larger switches, higher $f$ and larger $C_{fly}$, which raise cost and switching loss.

### How do you regulate the output?
Options: burst mode (turn the pump off when $V_{out}$ exceeds a threshold), a linear regulator after the pump (efficiency drops to $V_{reg}/(nV_{in})$), or variable switch resistance. Fractional ratios select the smallest multiplier that works.

### What happens if both phases overlap?
Switches S1/S2 and S3/S4 together short $V_{in}$ to ground through $C_{fly}$ and the switches, giving a large shoot-through current. Break-before-make dead time is mandatory.

### Scenario: your charge pump's output is 5.2 V instead of the 6.4 V you computed. What do you check?
Measure the load current, the actual clock frequency, and the real capacitance (ceramic DC-bias derating can halve $C_{fly}$ and raise $R_{SSL}$). Check $R_{sw}$ at the actual gate drive, ESR, and whether the part is in a lower-ratio or burst mode. Look at $V_{out}$ ripple and the $C_{fly}$ waveform on a scope.
:::
