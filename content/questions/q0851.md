::: basics
### In plain words
Measuring light usually means finding out how much light power lands on a surface. A small silicon part called a photodiode does this by turning light into a tiny electric current. The more light hits it, the more current flows. A second circuit then turns that tiny current into a voltage you can read.

### Picture it
Picture a rain gauge. Light arrives as a stream of tiny packets, like raindrops. Each packet that lands knocks loose one unit of charge. A heavier shower gives more charge per second, so the current rises in step with the brightness. Unlike rain, light packets come in different colors, and a silicon part responds differently to each color. Human eyes also respond differently, so there are two scales: one counts raw light power, the other weights it by how bright the eye sees it.

### Words you need
- **Photon**: one tiny packet of light energy.
- **Photodiode**: a small silicon part that produces a current when light hits it. See [[q0853]].
- **Responsivity**: how many amps of current you get per watt of light, in A/W.
- **Lux**: a brightness unit weighted to match the human eye, not raw power.
- **Transimpedance amplifier**: a circuit that turns a tiny current into a voltage, using an op-amp and a feedback resistor.

### The simplest example
Say 1 µW of near-infrared light falls on a silicon photodiode with a responsivity of 0.5 A/W. The current is 0.5 A/W times 1 µW, which is 0.5 µA. That is far too small to feed straight to a converter. Feed it into a transimpedance amplifier with a 100 kΩ feedback resistor. The output voltage is 0.5 µA times 100 kΩ, which is 0.05 V. Double the light and the voltage doubles. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the lux scale, the two ways to run a photodiode, noise, and how to handle a huge range from dim rooms to sunlight.
:::

::: why
Light sensing appears in ambient-light and proximity sensors in every phone, pulse oximeters, smoke detectors, barcode scanners, optical encoders, LiDAR and cameras, and in the optical receiver at the end of every fibre link. You need to know how to convert photons to a usable voltage, how much signal to expect (nanoamps to milliamps), and where the noise comes from. It is also a clean example of the general sensor chain: transduction, conditioning, ADC ([[q0841]]).
:::

- **Radiometry vs photometry**: watts per m² (irradiance) vs lux (illuminance, eye-weighted).
- **Photodiode**: a PN junction that makes current proportional to light power; responsivity in A/W. See [[q0853]].
- **Transimpedance amplifier**: an op-amp converting photocurrent to voltage. See [[q0121]].
- **Photoresistor (LDR)**: resistance falls with light; slow and nonlinear. See [[q0853]].
- **Image sensor**: array of photodiodes with readout. See [[q0856]].

::: answer
"Measuring light usually means measuring how much optical power is falling on a surface, and sometimes also its wavelength, direction or timing. There are two ways to describe the amount. Radiometric units are physical: watts per square metre. Photometric units, lumens and lux, weight the power by how bright the human eye perceives it, peaking at 555 nanometres green, where one watt is 683 lumens. So when a phone says 500 lux, it is reporting what the eye would call brightness, not total energy.

To measure it I convert photons into an electrical signal. The workhorse is the photodiode: a photon with enough energy creates an electron-hole pair in the junction, and the resulting current is proportional to the light power, about 0.5 amps per watt for silicon in the near infrared. The current is tiny, nanoamps to microamps, so I read it with a transimpedance amplifier: an op-amp with a feedback resistor that turns the current into a voltage, V equals I times Rf. Cheaper options are a phototransistor, which has gain but is slower and less linear, and a photoresistor, which changes resistance with light but is slow and nonlinear. For pictures, I use an image sensor, a 2D array of photodiodes. For very faint light, a photomultiplier or an avalanche diode.

Then I'd choose based on speed, linearity, spectral response, dynamic range and cost."

::: technical
**What is measured.**
- *Radiant power* $\Phi$ (W), *irradiance* $E_e$ (W/m²).
- *Luminous flux* (lm), *illuminance* $E_v$ (lux = lm/m²): $E_v=683\int E_e(\lambda)V(\lambda)\,d\lambda$, with $V(\lambda)$ the eye's photopic response, normalised to 1 at 555 nm. At 555 nm, 1 lux $=1/683=1.46$ mW/m².

**Typical levels.** Moonlit night about 0.1 to 1 lux, living room 50 to 200 lux, office about 300 to 500 lux, overcast day about 1,000 to 10,000 lux, direct sun about 100,000 lux. That is a 10^5 to 10^6 dynamic range.

**Photodiode.** A photon of energy $E=hc/\lambda=1.24\,\text{eV}\cdot\mu\text{m}/\lambda$ is absorbed if $E>E_g$. Silicon ($E_g=1.12$ eV) responds below 1.1 µm. Quantum efficiency $\eta$ gives responsivity

$$\mathcal R=\frac{\eta q\lambda}{hc}=\eta\,\frac{\lambda[\mu\text{m}]}{1.24}\ \text{A/W}.$$

At 850 nm with $\eta=0.8$: $\mathcal R=0.55$ A/W. At 450 nm with $\eta=0.7$: 0.25 A/W.

**Operating modes.** *Photovoltaic* (zero bias): lowest dark current and noise, best for precision at low frequency. *Photoconductive* (reverse bias): lower junction capacitance, faster, but more dark current and shot noise.

**Transimpedance amplifier.** $V_{out}=I_{ph}R_f$. With a feedback capacitor $C_f$ the bandwidth is about $1/(2\pi R_fC_f)$ and $C_f$ is chosen for stability against the photodiode capacitance $C_d$: $C_f\approx\sqrt{C_{in}/(2\pi R_f\,GBW)}$.

**Sizing example.** 500 lux of white LED light is roughly $500/300=1.7$ W/m² (assuming about 300 lm/W luminous efficacy of radiation, a typical figure for white LEDs). On a 5 mm² photodiode: 8.3 µW, and with an effective responsivity of 0.4 A/W: 3.3 µA, or 6.7 nA/lux. With $R_f=100$ kΩ: 0.33 V at 500 lux, 0.67 mV at 1 lux, but 67 V "wanted" at 100,000 lux, so the TIA saturates at about 5,000 lux with a 3.3 V rail. This is why wide-range sensors switch gains or use logarithmic or integrating readouts.
:::

::: long
"First, what does it even mean to measure light? There are a few properties: how much power is arriving, at which wavelengths, from which direction, with what timing, and with what polarisation. The most common one is the amount. You can express it radiometrically, as watts per square metre, or photometrically, in lux, which weights each wavelength by the sensitivity of the human eye. The weighting peaks at 555 nanometres where one watt is 683 lumens, so 500 lux is what the eye perceives as an office level of brightness, but the same lux value from different light sources has different physical power.

To measure it, I need a transducer that converts photons into an electrical signal. The standard one is a photodiode. A photon with enough energy, more than the bandgap, so below about 1.1 micrometres in silicon, generates an electron-hole pair. The junction field sweeps them out, giving a photocurrent proportional to light power. The responsivity is eta times lambda over 1.24, so around 0.5 to 0.6 amps per watt at 850 nanometres.

The current is small, a few microamps for an office-level light on a 5 square millimetre diode, so I put it into a transimpedance amplifier: an op-amp with the photodiode current into the inverting input and a feedback resistor, giving Vout equals I times Rf. Rf sets the gain, a feedback capacitor sets the bandwidth and stability. For a wide range of light levels, I might switch the gain, or use a log or integrating converter.

Other choices: a phototransistor gives built-in gain but is slower and less linear; a photoresistor is cheap but slow and nonlinear; an image sensor is an array of photodiodes; and for single photons a photomultiplier or SPAD. I'd pick by speed, linearity, spectral range, dynamic range and cost."
:::

::: terms
- **Irradiance**: optical power per unit area (W/m²).
- **Illuminance (lux)**: eye-weighted irradiance, lumens per m².
- **Responsivity**: photocurrent per watt (A/W).
- **Quantum efficiency**: fraction of photons giving collected carriers.
- **Photovoltaic / photoconductive mode**: zero-bias vs reverse-biased photodiode.
- **Dark current**: current with no light (leakage), a noise and offset source.
- **Transimpedance amplifier (TIA)**: current-to-voltage converter, $V=IR_f$.
- **Dynamic range**: ratio of largest to smallest measurable signal.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating lux as a physical power unit**. Lux is eye-weighted; different spectra give different power for the same lux.
- **Connecting a photodiode directly to an ADC**. The current is nA to µA; a resistor gives a small, nonlinear voltage and the source impedance is huge. Use a TIA.
- **Ignoring dynamic range**. A fixed-gain TIA that works at 500 lux saturates in sunlight and drowns in the dark.
- **Forgetting the spectral response**. A silicon photodiode is sensitive to IR that the eye cannot see, so lux readings can be wrong under IR sources without a filter.
- **Forgetting ambient/stray light and dark current**. They add offset and noise, especially at high temperature.
:::

::: deep
## Two languages: radiometry and photometry

Light is electromagnetic radiation; a sensor that just counts absorbed photons or energy is **radiometric**. Human vision, though, is selective: most sensitive in green (555 nm in bright light), almost blind at 700 nm in the deep red or at 450 nm in violet-blue. **Photometric** units weight the optical power by the eye's photopic curve $V(\lambda)$.

$$E_v=683\ \tfrac{\text{lm}}{\text{W}}\int_{380}^{780}E_e(\lambda)\,V(\lambda)\,d\lambda.$$

Consequences:
- At 555 nm one watt is 683 lm; at 650 nm ($V\approx0.107$) one watt is only 73 lm; at 850 nm, effectively 0 lm (the eye cannot see it, but the photodiode can).
- 500 lux of green light (555 nm) is $500/683=0.73$ W/m². 500 lux of broad white LED light is more power per lux, about $500/300\approx1.7$ W/m² (assuming roughly 300 lm/W luminous efficacy of radiation for a white LED spectrum).
- Therefore, a bare silicon photodiode (broadband, extends into IR) does not read "lux" correctly unless an optical filter shapes its response to $V(\lambda)$, which is what ambient-light sensor ICs do.

|---|---|
| Moonlit night | 0.1 to 1 lux |
| Dim living room | 50 lux |
| Office | 300 to 500 lux |
| Overcast day | 1,000 to 10,000 lux |
| Direct sunlight | about 100,000 lux |

> Typical lux levels are approximate and vary with source, time and location; the luminous efficacy of radiation of white LEDs depends on colour temperature and phosphor, 250 to 350 lm/W is a plausible range.

## From photon to electron

A photon of wavelength $\lambda$ carries $E=hc/\lambda$. For 850 nm, $E=1.46$ eV; for 450 nm, 2.76 eV. Silicon's bandgap is 1.12 eV, which gives a cutoff at $1.24/1.12=1.1$ µm. At 1 µW of 850 nm light, the photon rate is $P/E=4.3\times10^{12}$ photons/s. If $\eta=0.8$ of them create collected carriers, the current is $0.8\times4.3\times10^{12}\times1.6\times10^{-19}=0.55$ µA, i.e. $\mathcal R=0.55$ A/W.

## Photodiode modes

A photodiode is a PN junction where absorbed light adds a current $I_{ph}$ in the reverse direction: $I=I_s(e^{V/V_T}-1)-I_{ph}$.

- **Photovoltaic (zero bias)**: $V=0$, so only $I_{ph}$ flows. Dark current is negligible; no shot noise from leakage; excellent linearity (many decades) and best low-frequency noise. Slower because junction capacitance is large when there is no depletion widening.
- **Photoconductive (reverse bias, e.g. 5 V)**: depletion widens, capacitance drops (so bandwidth rises), and carriers are collected faster; dark current and its shot noise increase.
- **Open-circuit voltage** (photovoltaic, unloaded) grows only logarithmically with light, $V_{oc}\approx V_T\ln(I_{ph}/I_s)$, so a log response is possible but temperature-dependent.

Figure: The photodiode's cathode feeds the inverting input (a virtual ground); its anode is grounded (photovoltaic mode). The op-amp forces the diode to see about 0 V, supplies the photocurrent through R_f, and V_out = +I_ph·R_f. C_f keeps the loop stable against the diode capacitance and sets the bandwidth near 1/(2πR_fC_f).

**Why a TIA and not a resistor to ground?** A resistor $R$ from the diode to ground gives $V=I_{ph}R$ but makes the diode see a signal-dependent voltage (non-zero bias), adds the diode capacitance as a pole at $1/(2\pi RC_d)$ (with $R=1$ MΩ, $C_d=50$ pF: 3.2 kHz) and exposes you to nonlinearity. The TIA keeps the diode at virtual ground and pushes the pole out by the loop gain: bandwidth about $\sqrt{GBW/(2\pi R_fC_{in})}$. With GBW 10 MHz, $R_f=1$ MΩ, $C_{in}=53$ pF: 173 kHz, with optimum $C_f\approx0.9$ pF.

**Sizing.** Take a 5 mm² diode and an office-level 500 lux of white LED: 8.3 µW, effective $\mathcal R\approx0.4$ A/W, $I_{ph}=3.3$ µA. With $R_f=100$ kΩ the output is 0.33 V. At 1 lux: 0.67 mV; at 100,000 lux: 0.67 mA of photocurrent, so $R_f$ would have to be about 5 kΩ to stay within 3.3 V. The span 1 lux to 100,000 lux is 10^5, or 16.6 bits, which is more than a single linear 12-bit ADC and a single gain provide. Hence gain switching.

## Noise: where the floor comes from

- **Shot noise** of the photocurrent plus dark current: $i_n=\sqrt{2q(I_{ph}+I_d)B}$. At 0.5 µA and 10 kHz bandwidth: $\sqrt{2\times1.6\times10^{-19}\times5\times10^{-7}\times10^4}=40$ pA.
- **Thermal noise** of $R_f$: $i_n=\sqrt{4kT B/R_f}$; at 1 MΩ and 10 kHz, 0.13 pA/√Hz scaled: $1.3\times10^{-13}\times\sqrt{10^4}=13$ pA. Larger $R_f$ lowers this current noise, which is why low-light TIAs use large resistors (100 MΩ) at low bandwidth.
- **Amplifier voltage noise** amplified by the noise gain, which rises at high frequency due to the diode capacitance.
- **Ambient light**: DC background adds offset and shot noise; modulate the source and demodulate (lock-in) to reject it.

Thus the signal to noise at 0.5 µA over 10 kHz: $0.5\,\mu\text{A}/40\,\text{pA}=12{,}500$ (82 dB). Photodiode readout is typically shot-noise-limited at higher light levels and thermal- or amplifier-limited at very low ones.

| Photoresistor (LDR) | photoconductivity | tens to hundreds of ms | poor (power law) | cheap, see [[q0853]] |
| Image sensor | photodiode array + readout | frame rate | good | see [[q0856]] |

## Worked example: ambient light for screen brightness

Requirement: map 1 to 100,000 lux to backlight level; 16-bit-equivalent range. Approach: a sensor IC with a filtered photodiode (matched to $V(\lambda)$) and an integrating converter whose integration time is auto-ranged: long in the dark (accumulate charge for 100s of ms), short in sun. Firmware then applies a log-like mapping because perceived brightness is roughly logarithmic in luminance.

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Cover glass**: the sensor behind dark glass sees an attenuated, spectrally shifted signal; calibrate with a correction factor.
- **IR contamination**: tungsten or sun have lots of IR; filtered or dual-diode sensors subtract it.
- **Flicker**: LED lighting modulated by PWM at 100 to 400 Hz appears as fluctuations; either integrate over a period or detect flicker intentionally.
- **Temperature**: dark current roughly doubles every 8 to 10 °C for silicon; at 85 °C a nA-level dark current becomes tens to hundreds of nA.
:::

::: applications
- **Ambient light sensors**: phone auto-brightness, display and keyboard backlight control.
- **Proximity sensors**: IR LED plus photodiode measuring reflection to detect a face near the screen.
- **Pulse oximeters and heart-rate sensors**: red and infrared LEDs through tissue into a photodiode.
- **Optical receivers**: fibre and IR remote photodiodes with TIAs.
- **Smoke detectors**: photodiode senses LED light scattered by smoke particles.
- **Cameras**: image sensors, photodiode arrays with per-pixel readout.
:::

::: followups
### Why use a transimpedance amplifier instead of a resistor?
A resistor makes the diode voltage track the signal, puts the diode capacitance and the resistor into a slow RC pole, and compromises linearity. A TIA holds the diode at a virtual ground, so the photocurrent flows through $R_f$ linearly, and the closed-loop bandwidth is far higher than $1/(2\pi R C_d)$.

### What is the difference between photovoltaic and photoconductive mode?
Photovoltaic means zero bias: the lowest dark current and noise, great for precision at low frequency, but higher capacitance and so lower speed. Photoconductive uses reverse bias: lower capacitance and faster response but more dark current and shot noise. High-speed receivers use photoconductive mode, precision light meters use photovoltaic.

### How do you handle 1 lux to 100,000 lux?
No single linear stage covers $10^5$. Options: switchable $R_f$ or gain stages, a logarithmic amplifier, an integrating converter with auto-ranged integration time, or a sensor IC that does this internally. I would verify the transitions between ranges for continuity and hysteresis.

### How do you reject ambient light when you only want your own LED's signal?
Modulate the LED at a known frequency, AC-couple the photodiode signal and synchronously demodulate (lock-in). Alternatively, take a reading with the LED off and subtract it from the LED-on reading, which cancels the slowly varying ambient component. Proximity sensors do exactly this.

### Scenario: your light sensor reading rises in the dark at high temperature. What is going on?
Dark current is the likely culprit: it roughly doubles every 8 to 10 °C, and at high temperature through a large $R_f$ it adds an output offset. Check the output with the sensor covered at different temperatures, use zero-bias (photovoltaic) mode, reduce $R_f$ or subtract a measured dark reading, and keep the diode away from heat sources.
:::
