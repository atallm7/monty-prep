::: testing
Whether you can define PWM precisely and then go beyond the definition: **duty cycle, frequency, amplitude, average and RMS values, resolution, harmonics, and why it is efficient**. The interviewer is checking that you know the average is $D\cdot V_{pk}$ but the RMS (and therefore heating) is $V_{pk}\sqrt D$, that the load or a filter must average the pulses, and that frequency is a trade-off (switching loss, EMI, ripple, audible noise, resolution). Applications (buck converters, motors, LEDs, class D, servos) show real exposure.
:::

::: why
PWM is the workhorse of embedded and power electronics. It lets a digital pin or a switch produce an analogue-like output with almost no wasted power: a transistor that is either fully on or fully off dissipates little, unlike a linear regulator or resistor that burns the difference. Understanding duty cycle, frequency and filtering explains how a buck converter regulates, how a motor is speed-controlled, how an LED is dimmed without colour shift, and why a PWM-controlled load can still be noisy or hot. This page is the power-electronics framing; [[q0672]] asks the same thing in the embedded/microcontroller section.
:::

::: concepts
- **Square wave and duty cycle**: $D=t_{on}/T$, with $0\le D\le1$. See [[q0179]].
- **Average and RMS**: $V_{avg}=DV_{pk}$ and $V_{rms}=V_{pk}\sqrt D$. See [[q0006]] for power.
- **Filtering**: an RC or LC low-pass passes the DC component and attenuates the switching harmonics. See [[q0010]].
- **Fourier content**: a PWM wave has DC plus harmonics at $nf_{sw}$. See [[q0024]] for reactance.
- **Timer resolution**: a timer counting at $f_{clk}$ with period $N$ counts gives $f_{PWM}=f_{clk}/N$ and $\log_2N$ bits.
- **Switch losses**: a MOSFET switching on and off dissipates conduction and switching power only, not linear power.
:::

::: answer
"PWM, pulse-width modulation, is a way of controlling average power or voltage by switching a signal fully on and off at a fixed frequency and varying the fraction of the time it's on. That fraction is the duty cycle, $D=t_{on}/T$. If I switch a 5 volt signal at 30% duty, the average value is 1.5 volts, and if the load or a low-pass filter averages it, the load acts as if it were driven by 1.5 volts.

The key characteristics are the frequency, the duty cycle, the amplitude, and the resolution, which is how finely I can set the duty cycle. The average voltage is $D\cdot V$, but the RMS is $V\sqrt D$, which is what matters for heating a resistive load. For a 5 volt 30% wave the average is 1.5 volts and the RMS is 2.74.

The big advantage is efficiency. The switch is either fully on, with almost no voltage across it, or fully off, with no current, so very little power is lost, unlike a linear regulator that burns the difference. That's why buck converters, motor drivers, LED dimmers and class D amplifiers use it.

The trade-offs are in the frequency. Higher frequency means smaller filters and less ripple, but more switching loss and EMI. Lower means audible noise, visible flicker or a lot of ripple. And the output has harmonics at multiples of the switching frequency, so you need a filter or a load that is slow enough to average."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** A periodic rectangular wave with period $T=1/f_{sw}$, high for $t_{on}=DT$ and low for the remainder. Amplitude $V_{pk}$ (low level taken as 0).

**Average and RMS.**
$$V_{avg}=\frac1T\int_0^TV\,dt=DV_{pk},\qquad V_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac1T\int_0^TV^2dt}=V_{pk}\sqrt D$$
For a resistor, $P=V_{rms}^2/R=DV_{pk}^2/R$. Example: 5 V, $D=0.3$: $V_{avg}=1.5$ V, $V_{rms}=2.74$ V.

**Spectrum.** $v(t)=DV_{pk}+\sum_{n\ge1}\dfrac{2V_{pk}}{n\pi}\sin(n\pi D)\cos(2\pi nf_{sw}t)$. The first harmonic amplitude at $D=0.3$ is $2\cdot5\sin(0.3\pi)/\pi=2.57$ V. Harmonic $n$ vanishes when $nD$ is an integer.

**Filtering.** An RC low-pass with $\tau\gg T$ gives an almost-DC output with peak-to-peak ripple approximately $V_{pk}D(1-D)T/\tau$. For $V_{pk}=5$ V, $D=0.3$, $f=10$ kHz, $\tau=1$ ms: ripple $\approx0.105$ V (exact calculation 0.105 V).

**Resolution.** A timer clocked at $f_{clk}$ counting to $N$ gives $f_{PWM}=f_{clk}/N$ and $\log_2N$ bits of duty resolution, so 16 MHz and 8 bits is 62.5 kHz; 10 bits is 15.6 kHz. Frequency and resolution trade against each other.

**Other characteristics.** Rise/fall times (finite edges add switching loss and EMI), dead time in bridges, phase/centre-aligned versus edge-aligned modes, minimum on/off pulse width, jitter, and for converters the relation $V_{out}=DV_{in}$ (buck, CCM).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Typical PWM frequencies depend heavily on the application and part: for example a few hundred Hz to a few kHz for LED dimming and heaters, tens of kHz for motor drives (often above audible range), and hundreds of kHz to MHz for switching regulators. Treat these ranges as typical, not rules.
:::

::: short

The main benefit is efficiency, since the switch is either on or off. Frequency is the trade-off: higher means smaller filters but more switching loss and EMI."
:::

::: long
"PWM is pulse-width modulation. Instead of producing an intermediate voltage with a linear device that burns the difference as heat, I switch a signal fully on and fully off at a fixed frequency and control the fraction of time it's on. That fraction is the duty cycle, on-time over period. The average value is duty times amplitude, so a 5 volt signal at 30% duty averages 1.5 volts. If the load is slow, like a motor winding, an LED seen by the eye, or an LC filter, it responds to the average.

The characteristics I'd list are frequency, duty cycle and amplitude, plus resolution, how many distinct duty values a timer can produce. A timer running at 16 megahertz with 8 bits gives 62.5 kilohertz, and with 10 bits, 15.6 kilohertz, so frequency and resolution trade off.

One subtlety is that average and RMS differ. For a resistive load, power is $D V^2/R$, which uses RMS, $V\sqrt D$. Average voltage is what sets motor speed or what a filter outputs, while RMS is what heats a resistor.

Spectrally, a PWM wave is a DC term equal to the average, plus harmonics at multiples of the switching frequency. The filter's job is to remove those, and a first-order RC with tau much bigger than the period gives ripple of about $V D(1-D) T/\tau$.

The reason it's everywhere is efficiency. The switch is either on with almost no voltage across it, or off with no current, so conduction and switching losses are small. That's how buck converters work, where $V_{out}=D V_{in}$ in continuous mode, and how motors, LED drivers and class D amplifiers are driven. The trade-off is frequency. Higher frequency shrinks the filter and ripple, but increases switching loss and EMI. Lower frequency can be audible or flicker, and gives larger ripple."
:::

::: terms
- **Duty cycle ($D$)**: fraction of the period the signal is high, $t_{on}/T$.
- **Switching frequency ($f_{sw}$)**: repetition rate of the PWM cycle.
- **Average value**: $DV_{pk}$, the DC component a filter or slow load responds to.
- **RMS value**: $V_{pk}\sqrt D$, which determines resistive heating.
- **Resolution**: number of distinct duty values, $\log_2$ of the timer's period count.
- **Harmonics**: spectral components at $nf_{sw}$ that a filter must remove.
- **Dead time**: deliberate gap between complementary switches to prevent shoot-through.
- **Edge-aligned vs centre-aligned PWM**: whether pulses start together or are symmetric about the period midpoint.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "PWM varies the duty cycle to control average voltage" and gives a duty-cycle formula.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Distinguishes **average ($DV$)** and **RMS ($V\sqrt D$)** and says which matters for what (motor speed, filter output vs resistive heating).
- Explains *why* it is efficient: the switch is either saturated or off, so there is no linear dissipation.
- Quantifies resolution: $f_{PWM}=f_{clk}/N$, bits $=\log_2N$ and the trade-off with frequency.
- Describes the spectrum (DC plus harmonics at $nf_{sw}$) and filter ripple $\approx VD(1-D)T/\tau$.
- Names the **frequency trade-offs** (switching loss, EMI, ripple, audible noise, flicker) and ties PWM to $V_{out}=DV_{in}$ in a buck converter.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating the average as the only number.** For heating, use RMS: a 30% duty 5 V wave heats a resistor like 2.74 V DC, not 1.5 V.
- **"Duty cycle changes the frequency."** It does not. Frequency is fixed; pulse width changes.
- **Using PWM without any averaging** where the load responds fast (e.g. an op-amp input or a logic input): the load sees the pulses.
- **Choosing a low frequency for a motor or buzzer.** It can be audible or cause torque ripple, so pick frequency above hearing or choose consciously.
- **Assuming 100% resolution at any frequency.** Resolution drops as frequency rises on a fixed timer clock.
- **Forgetting dead time** in half bridges, which causes shoot-through and a duty-cycle nonlinearity near 0% and 100%.
:::

::: deep
## What PWM is and the idea behind it

Suppose you have a 5 V supply and want to run a load at 1.5 V. A linear approach (a resistor divider or a linear regulator) drops 3.5 V across a device that carries the load current, burning $3.5\,I$ watts. PWM avoids this: a switch connects the load to 5 V for 30% of each period and to 0 V for the remaining 70%. When the switch is on, almost 5 V appears across the load and almost nothing across the switch; when it is off, the load voltage is zero and the switch carries no current. In both states the switch dissipates very little.

## Average, RMS and power

$$V_{avg}=DV_{pk},\qquad V_{rms}=V_{pk}\sqrt D,\qquad P_R=\frac{DV_{pk}^2}{R}$$

The difference is crucial. For a 10 Ω resistor driven by 5 V at $D=0.3$: $P=0.3\cdot25/10=0.75$ W, the same as DC at $V=\sqrt{0.75\cdot10}=2.74$ V (not 1.5 V). For a motor, speed follows the *average* voltage (back-EMF balances it), and the winding inductance smooths the current. For an LED, brightness tracks the average *current*. For a filter output, the DC level is the average.

## From pulses to a smooth voltage: the filter

Putting an RC low-pass after the PWM output extracts the average. A first-order RC attenuates the $n$-th harmonic by about $1/(2\pi nf_{sw}RC)$ for frequencies well above the corner, so a corner frequency far below $f_{sw}$ is required.

For $\tau=RC\gg T$ the steady-state ripple (peak to peak) is approximately

$$\Delta V\approx V_{pk}\,\frac{D(1-D)}{f_{sw}\,RC}$$

With $V_{pk}=5$ V, $D=0.3$, $RC=1$ ms: at 1 kHz, $\Delta V\approx1.05$ V; at 10 kHz, $\Delta V\approx0.105$ V.

## Spectrum

A rectangular pulse train has a Fourier series: a DC term $DV_{pk}$ and harmonics of amplitude $\dfrac{2V_{pk}}{n\pi}|\sin(n\pi D)|$ at $nf_{sw}$. For $V_{pk}=5$ V and $D=0.3$:

| $n$ | Amplitude (V) |
|---|---|
| 1 | 2.57 |
| 2 | 1.51 |
| 3 | 0.33 |
| 4 | 0.47 |
| 5 | 0.64 |

(The fundamental is large, and harmonics fall slowly, as $1/n$, because of the sharp edges. Finite rise time reduces the high harmonics. That's why EMI is a problem and why slower edges are used when possible.) $D=0.5$ gives only odd harmonics.

## Frequency and resolution on a microcontroller

A timer counts from 0 to $N-1$ at $f_{clk}$, so $f_{PWM}=f_{clk}/N$, and the duty is set in steps of $1/N$. With a 16 MHz clock: $N=256$ (8 bits) gives 62.5 kHz; $N=1024$ (10 bits) gives 15.6 kHz; $N=4096$ (12 bits) gives 3.9 kHz. To get 20 kHz from 72 MHz you have about $\log_2(72\text{ MHz}/20\text{ kHz})=11.8$ bits. See [[q0672]] for the MCU-side view.

## Choosing frequency

| Higher frequency | Lower frequency |
|---|---|
| Smaller filter L and C, lower ripple | Larger filters |
| More switching loss ($f\cdot CV^2$, gate drive) | Lower switching loss |
| More EMI at higher harmonics, shorter wiring tolerance | Easier EMC |
| Fewer bits of resolution on a fixed clock | More resolution |
| Above hearing (>20 kHz) for motors and coils | Audible whine or flicker below ~100 Hz-1 kHz |

For LEDs, flicker is a human-perception and camera issue, so a few hundred Hz to kHz is common. For motors, tens of kHz avoids audible noise, but the driver's switching loss rises. For a buck converter, hundreds of kHz to MHz are typical.

## Why PWM is efficient

A MOSFET in the on state has only $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ loss, and in the off state it has no current. The losses occur during transitions. To dissipate 1 W while delivering 5 W is a 83% efficient linear stage, while a well-designed PWM stage can exceed 90-95%. In a buck converter ([[q0183]]), the ideal $V_{out}=DV_{in}$ and the inductor and capacitor are the "filter" that averages the PWM node. For the converter-specific duty relations see [[q0179]] and [[q0180]].

## What PWM is not good at

- **Loads that respond fast**: logic inputs, sensitive analogue circuits and audio need a filter or a different technique.
- **Noise-sensitive systems**: harmonics can couple into ADCs or radios, so frequency choices can matter (e.g. avoid a harmonic landing in an IF band).
- **Very small or very large duty**: minimum on-time and dead time limit the range.
- **Comparisons**: for a PFM or pulse-skipping alternative see [[q0232]] and [[q0233]].
:::

::: applications
- **Buck converters**: the switch node is a PWM waveform and the LC averages it to $V_{out}=DV_{in}$.
- **Brushed and brushless motor drives**: speed control via duty; BLDC drives use PWM on the half-bridges.
- **LED dimming**: brightness is controlled by average current; PWM avoids colour shift that analogue dimming can cause.
- **Class D audio amplifiers**: a high-frequency PWM is modulated by the audio and filtered by an LC at the speaker.
- **RC servos and hobby ESCs**: a 1-2 ms pulse in a roughly 20 ms frame encodes position or throttle (pulse width carries the information).
- **Heaters and TECs**: duty sets average power with a slow thermal load.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Fixed-frequency, fixed-amplitude PWM** with a low level of 0 V. A bipolar PWM (H-bridge) has average $(2D-1)V$.
- **Ideal switches** with zero rise/fall time and zero on-voltage. Real edges, dead time and $R_{DS(on)}$ shift the average.
- **Load or filter is slow compared with $T$.** If not, ripple dominates.
- **Typical frequency ranges** are application-dependent (see the uncertainty note).
- **Alternative valid answers.** Some interviewers want PWM described as a *modulation* technique (the duty cycle encodes a signal, as in class D or servo control), others as a *power control* technique. Cover both, but start with the one that matches the section (here: power electronics).
:::

::: followups
### What's the difference between average and RMS for a PWM signal?
Average is $DV_{pk}$ and is what a filter output, motor speed or LED average current follow. RMS is $V_{pk}\sqrt D$ and is what sets heating in a resistor. At $D=0.25$ and 12 V: average 3 V, RMS 6 V. A 12 Ω heater dissipates $0.25\cdot144/12=3$ W, as if driven by 6 V DC.

### How would you get a smooth DC voltage from a 3.3 V PWM pin?
Use a low-pass: an RC with a corner well below the PWM frequency, e.g. 1 kΩ and 1 µF has $f_c=159$ Hz, so at 10 kHz it attenuates the carrier by about 63×, with ripple $\approx3.3\cdot D(1-D)/(f\,RC)$ (max about 82 mV at $D=0.5$ and 10 kHz). Output impedance is 1 kΩ, so buffer it if the load draws current.

### How does PWM relate to a buck converter's output voltage?
The switch node is a PWM waveform and the inductor-capacitor filter extracts the average, so $V_{out}=DV_{in}$ in continuous conduction mode. The duty is varied by a feedback loop to hold $V_{out}$ constant. See [[q0183]].

### What limits how high you can raise the PWM frequency?
Switching loss ($\approx\tfrac12VI(t_r+t_f)f$ plus gate charge and $C_{oss}$ losses), gate-driver capability, minimum on-time and dead time, EMI, and timer clock/resolution. For motors, current ripple falls with frequency, but losses rise.

### What happens near 0% and 100% duty?
Minimum on/off times and dead time create a dead zone: pulses too narrow are skipped or distorted, so effective duty doesn't reach exactly 0 or 1 linearly. Bootstrapped high-side drivers need refresh pulses, so 100% duty may be limited. See [[q0181]].

### Scenario: you PWM a 12 V DC motor at 500 Hz and hear a loud whine. What do you change?
The frequency is in the audible range and the motor windings and magnetics vibrate at it. Raise it above about 20 kHz, check that the driver's switching loss and edge-rate EMI are acceptable, and ensure the timer still gives enough resolution. If the whine persists, check for a subharmonic caused by the control loop.
:::
