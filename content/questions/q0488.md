::: basics
### In plain words
There are two ways to distribute ground on a board. A ground plane is a solid copper layer that every ground connection drops into through a via. Ground traces are routed connections, such as a chain, a star or a grid. The plane has far lower resistance and inductance and keeps each signal's return loop small. Traces make sense on cheap one- or two-layer boards, or where you want a deliberately separate return.

### Picture it
Imagine a neighborhood where everyone must walk home. With a plane, it is an open field, and each person walks a short, direct route home. With ground traces, it is one narrow lane that all the neighbors share, so the last house on the lane has everyone else's foot traffic in the way. The analogy is loose: a fast return current in a plane follows the path right under its own signal, not just any straight line.

### Words you need
- **Daisy chain**: loads connected in a row on one ground trace, so each later load sees the earlier loads' currents ([[q0497]]).
- **Star grounding**: a separate trace from each load to one common point ([[q0495]]).
- **Ground grid**: ground traces in both directions tied together with vias, a middle option.
- **Loop area**: the area between a signal and its return. Radiation grows with it.
- **Common-impedance coupling**: voltage made in a shared conductor that appears in another circuit's reference ([[q0484]]).

### The simplest example
Put three 100 mA loads on a daisy-chained ground trace, with 50 mΩ per segment. The first segment carries all 300 mA, so it drops 15 mV. The second carries 200 mA and adds 10 mV, so the second load's ground sits 25 mV up. The third adds 5 mV, so it sits 30 mV up. Any change in the first load's current moves the other two. On a plane the same currents spread out and the drops are tens of times smaller. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page compares impedance, loop area and cost, and shows when traces are still the right choice.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can **compare two ways of distributing ground** with numbers: a dedicated plane (or large pour) versus routed ground traces (daisy-chain, star or grid). The interviewer wants resistance and inductance, shared-impedance coupling, loop area/EMI, routing space and cost, and the honest cases where traces are right (1–2 layer boards, deliberate single-point connections, Kelvin sensing). The best answers show that traces force *you* to design every return path, while a plane lets the physics pick the best one.
:::

::: why
Many real products, especially cost-sensitive ones, are 1- or 2-layer boards where ground is a mix of traces and pours. Engineers have to know what they give up compared with a plane and how to claw it back (grids, wide traces, local pours). It also shows why the planes in [[q0486]] are the default for anything fast: not preference, but a factor of ten or more in inductance.
:::

::: concepts
- **Sheet resistance and trace resistance**: $R=\rho\ell/(wt)$. See [[q0484]].
- **Partial and loop inductance**: an isolated trace is about 1.4 nH/mm; over a plane the loop is about 0.3 nH/mm. See [[q0478]].
- **Common-impedance coupling**: daisy-chained grounds make later loads see earlier loads' currents. See [[q0497]].
- **Star grounding**: separate trace per load to a single point. See [[q0495]].
- **Loop area**: ground traces away from the signal enlarge loops. See [[q0489]].
- **Ground grid**: orthogonal traces on two layers tied with vias, a compromise.
:::

::: answer
"A ground plane is a solid layer that all ground connections drop into with a via. Ground traces are routed connections: a daisy chain, a star or a grid.

The plane wins on impedance. One ounce copper is about half a milliohm per square, so a plane between two points is under a milliohm, while a 100 millimetre, quarter-millimetre ground trace is 190 milliohms and around 140 nanohenries, about 90 ohms at 100 MHz. The plane also gives each signal a return path right underneath it, so loops are small; with traces the return is wherever you routed it, usually far from the signal, so loops are big, which means more radiation and pickup. And with a plane there's no shared-impedance coupling to speak of, while a daisy chain adds up every upstream load's current in the downstream segments.

The costs of the plane are layers and money, plus capacitance and the other things we covered. Traces make sense on one or two-layer boards where a plane isn't affordable, and where you want a deliberately separated ground, like a star point for a shunt measurement or for separating a motor return. Even then I use wide traces or a ground pour, a grid if I can, and keep the signal and its return together. So: plane by default, traces when the cost or the topology demands it."
:::

::: technical
**Numbers (1 oz copper, 100 mm run, $\varepsilon_r=4.3$):**

| Connection | R (DC) | L | $|Z|$ at 10 MHz | $|Z|$ at 100 MHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ground trace 0.25 mm wide | 192 mΩ | ≈141 nH | 8.9 Ω | 88.6 Ω |
| Ground trace 2 mm wide | 24 mΩ | ≈102 nH* | 6.4 Ω | 64 Ω |
| Plane, current spread over 50 mm width (low-frequency picture) | ≈1 mΩ | ≈0.5 nH | 0.03 Ω | 0.31 Ω |
| Plane, return concentrated under a signal ($\pm3h$, $h=0.2$ mm) | ≈40 mΩ | ≈21 nH | 1.3 Ω | 13.2 Ω |

\*Grover approximation for a wide strip; inductance falls only logarithmically with width.

The last row is the *loop inductance of that one signal's return*. It is the same order as the trace-over-plane figure ($0.3$ nH/mm × 100 mm = 30 nH loop), and it is *private* to that signal: other circuits share only the small part of the plane where their currents overlap.

**Daisy-chain accumulation.** Three 100 mA loads on a chain with 50 mΩ per segment: the ground at G1 sits 15 mV above the source ground, G2 is 25 mV, G3 is 30 mV. In a plane the same currents spread, so the rises are typically tens of times smaller.

**Loop area.** Radiation from a small loop scales as $f^2IA$. A ground trace routed 20 mm away from a 50 mm signal gives 1000 mm² of loop; a plane 0.2 mm below gives 10 mm², 100× less area.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Inductance values are first-order estimates (Grover-type formula for the isolated strip, current-concentration model for the plane) and depend on geometry. The ratio, not the absolute number, is the lesson.
:::

::: short
"A ground plane gives much lower impedance than ground traces: about 0.5 mΩ per square and sub-nanohenry spreading, versus roughly 190 mΩ and 140 nH for a 100 mm thin trace. It also puts the return directly under each signal, so loops are small, and it avoids the common-impedance buildup of a daisy chain. Traces cost less and let you force a deliberate single-point return, so they're right for one- or two-layer boards and for things like a shunt measurement. Otherwise I use a plane, or a ground grid as a compromise."
:::

::: long
"Compare them by what they do to the return current.

Impedance first. Copper is about half a milliohm per square at one ounce, so a plane is under a milliohm between two points. A ground trace a hundred millimetres long and a quarter millimetre wide is about 190 milliohms and 140 nanohenries, which is around 9 ohms at 10 MHz and almost 90 ohms at 100 MHz. Even a 2 millimetre wide trace is still tens of ohms at 100 MHz, because inductance only improves logarithmically with width. The plane fixes that.

Second, loop area. At high frequency, the return follows the signal. Over a plane it's directly beneath it, so the loop is a fraction of a square millimetre per millimetre of trace. With a ground trace the return is where you routed it, which is usually somewhere else, so the loop is large, and radiation and pickup scale with loop area.

Third, coupling. With a daisy-chained ground, each downstream device sees the current of every upstream one flowing through the shared segments. For three 100 milliamp loads on 50 milliohm segments, the last one is 30 millivolts above the source ground. A plane spreads that out, and the coupling basically goes away.

Where traces make sense: two-layer or one-layer boards where a plane isn't affordable, where I deliberately want a separate return like a star point at a shunt, or a Kelvin connection. Even then I make them wide, keep them short and next to the signal, or use a ground grid, with orthogonal ground traces on the two layers tied together with vias. On anything fast or sensitive I go with a plane, and if I have to cut it, I cut it carefully."
:::

::: terms
- **Ground trace**: a routed copper connection used as ground return.
- **Daisy chain**: loads connected in series along one ground trace.
- **Star ground**: each load has its own ground trace to a single point.
- **Ground grid / mesh**: orthogonal ground traces on two layers joined by vias.
- **Sheet resistance**: $\rho/t$ in Ω/□.
- **Partial inductance**: inductance of a conductor segment, apart from its return.
- **Loop inductance / loop area**: properties of the signal-plus-return circuit.
- **Kelvin connection**: separate sense and force paths so sense lines carry no load current.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer *quantifies the gap* (about two orders of magnitude between a thin trace and a plane at 100 MHz), explains why width alone doesn't fix inductance (log dependence), and distinguishes the shared-impedance problem from the loop-area problem. It also gives a fair account of when traces are right (cost, star/Kelvin), and what to do to make them better: wide traces, grids, ground pours, short and adjacent to signals. It might note that a plane can also be cut into a "mostly plane with deliberate necks" for controlled separation of returns, which is the plane's version of a star point.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking a wide trace is as good as a plane.** Width helps resistance a lot, inductance very little.
- **Using a daisy chain for mixed loads.** A noisy load upstream disturbs everything downstream.
- **Assuming the star ground works at high frequency.** The long star legs have inductance, and the return currents don't follow your star.
- **Ignoring loop area** when routing ground traces: routing the ground "out of the way" and the signal somewhere else.
- **Calling a ground pour "a plane".** A pour interrupted by traces and not stitched can be as bad as a trace.
- **Saying planes are always better.** They cost layers and add capacitance; for slow, high-impedance or DC-precision circuits, a deliberate trace topology can be better.
:::

## Two different pictures of "ground"

**Plane**: a 2-D conductor. The return current finds its own path, and at high frequency it chooses the one under the signal, minimizing loop inductance.

**Traces**: 1-D conductors. The return current has no choice: it flows in whatever you routed. If your ground trace is a long snake, the loop is long.

## The loop-area argument

Radiated emissions from a small loop: $E\propto f^2 I A$. With a ground trace routed 20 mm from the signal along 50 mm, the loop is $50\times20=1000$ mm². The same signal over a plane 0.2 mm away has $50\times0.2=10$ mm². That's 100× less area, 40 dB less field, ignoring other details. This is the main reason fast boards need planes and not just fat ground traces. See [[q0489]].

## When ground traces are the right answer

1. **1- and 2-layer cost-driven boards.** Use wide ground traces and a pour on the second layer. Keep ground next to signals ("run the return alongside"). Tie pours with vias.
2. **Star points for DC/low-frequency measurements.** For a current-sense shunt, the high current returns through the power-ground conductor, and the sense lines (Kelvin) run on their own traces to the amplifier's ground, joined to the plane at one point.
3. **Intentional isolation of a noisy return.** A motor or relay current is routed on its own ground trace back to the supply input, not under the controller.
4. **Very low-frequency analog** (audio, thermocouples): where loop areas are small and $\omega L$ negligible, topology (star vs daisy) matters more than plane.

## Making traces better: the ground grid

A grid puts ground traces in both directions (horizontal on top, vertical on bottom) and joins them with vias at crossings, with a pitch of 5–10 mm. The inductance of a mesh falls roughly in proportion to the number of parallel paths, and every signal has some nearby ground. It's cheaper than a plane and much better than a daisy chain; it's not as good as a plane for return-current confinement or controlled impedance.

Rules for ground traces:
- Make them as **wide** as space allows for resistance.
- **Run them next to** the signals they serve for small loops.
- **Avoid daisy chains** of mixed loads; use a star or tree from the source.
- **Don't make loops** with them accidentally (ground loops).
- **Add pours** to fill unused space and tie them with vias.

## Making planes better

If you use a plane, remember the rules in [[q0485]]: no slots, vias at pads, deliberate separation by placement. See [[q0493]] and [[q0496]] for separating.

## Putting it together: a decision table

| Situation | Choose |
|---|---|
| Edges faster than a few ns, USB, clocks, SMPS | Plane |
| High-resolution measurement with a high-current shunt | Plane + deliberate single connection (star/Kelvin) |
| 2-layer slow control board | Ground pour/grid, wide traces, short loops |
| Motor/relay return that must not touch logic | Separate trace or plane region, joined at one point |
| Audio and low-frequency analog | Star topology on a plane or grid |

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Give the ratio: "A plane is easily 100× better than a thin trace at 100 MHz, and it's the loop area, not the resistance, that decides EMI." Then give the honest case for traces: "On a two-layer board I'd use a grid and pours, and for a shunt I'd still use a star point."

## Worked example: sizing the shared segment behind a 20 mV error

The scenario in the follow-ups can be turned into a measurement you could actually make. A 350 mA LED driver shares a ground segment with a sensor's reference, and the sensor reading shifts by 20 mV when the LED turns on. Common-impedance coupling means the shift is just Ohm's law across the shared segment:

$$R_{shared}=\frac{\Delta V}{I}=\frac{20\ \text{mV}}{0.35\ \text{A}}=57\ \text{m}\Omega$$

With 1 oz copper at $0.49$ mΩ per square, that is $57/0.49\approx116$ squares. A 0.25 mm trace would need to be $116\times0.25=29$ mm long to produce it; a 0.5 mm trace, 58 mm. So the story is believable on a two-layer board where the LED's return and the sensor's reference both pass through a thin ground neck near the connector. The same current spreading through a plane region of, say, 3 squares between the two points would drop $3\times0.49\ \text{m}\Omega\times0.35\ \text{A}=0.5$ mV, about 40 times less. And a star connection that gives the sensor its own thin return to the supply's ground pin carries only microamps, so the sensor sees nearly zero millivolts regardless of the trace length. The lesson: the *shared* resistance is what matters, and you can find it by measuring the voltage between the two reference points with the LED on and off.

## Worked example: how good is a ground grid?

A ground grid is a compromise, and its quality is easy to estimate. Build the grid from 0.5 mm traces at a 5 mm pitch in both directions on a two-layer board, with a via at every crossing. Current flowing along one direction uses only the traces running that way, each 0.5 mm wide on a 5 mm pitch, so the effective sheet resistance is approximately

$$R_{\square,grid}\approx R_\square\,\frac{p}{w}=0.49\ \text{m}\Omega\times\frac{5}{0.5}=4.9\ \text{m}\Omega/\square$$

about ten times the resistance of solid copper, in return for leaving 90 % of each layer free for routing. The cross-links from the vias help current take diagonal paths, so the real value is somewhat lower, but a factor of several versus a solid plane is the right expectation. The inductance story is similar: parallel paths divide the loop inductance, but the return is no longer confined under each trace, so loops near fast signals are larger than with a solid plane. For slow boards that is fine; for 100 MHz harmonics it is not.

A second consequence is the maximum current. A 0.5 mm, 1 oz outer-layer trace carries roughly 1.4 A for a 10 °C rise by the IPC-2221 chart fit (about half that on an inner layer; the allowed rise and the newer IPC-2152 data change the number, so check). A 5 A return thus needs four or more grid traces sharing it, more with margin, or a wider trunk at the point it enters the grid, and the current only shares evenly if the entry point feeds several traces at once.

## When the simple impedance table breaks down

The table above uses one number per conductor. Several effects change it:

- **Frequency-dependent resistance.** For 1 oz copper, the skin depth equals the 35 µm thickness near 3.6 MHz. Above that, a trace's resistance rises as $\sqrt f$, and in a plane the return current stays on the surface facing the signal. The 192 mΩ DC figure for a thin trace is a lower bound at 10 and 100 MHz, but because $\omega L$ is already 8.9 Ω at 10 MHz, the inductive term still dominates.
- **Mutual inductance.** The 141 nH figure is for an isolated trace. A return trace running right next to the signal sees mutual inductance that cancels part of the loop's flux, which is why running the return alongside its signal (the first rule for ground traces) cuts loop inductance substantially.
- **Meshed returns.** Ground traces that are joined in several places form loops. Currents divide according to impedance, not according to your intent, so a "tree" on paper can behave as a mesh at high frequency.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **Pour connectivity is by vias, not by intent.** A ground pour on a two-layer board is only as good as the vias that tie its two sides together and the necks between its fragments. Look for narrow bridges the CAD tool left between zone islands; each is a series resistor and inductor.
- **Current rating of ground traces.** A 0.25 mm, 1 oz outer trace is good for a bit under 1 A at a 10 °C rise (IPC-2221 chart fit), and less on an inner layer. A ground trace that also carries an LED or motor current should be sized like a power trace.
- **Creepage and clearance.** On boards that handle mains or high voltage, the choice between a plane and traces is constrained by isolation distances, which can force cutouts.
- **Assembly.** A big ground pour makes through-hole soldering harder; use thermal reliefs on pins where heat sinking is a problem and direct connections elsewhere.

## Bench verification

Verify a ground path by measuring it. For DC and low frequency, drive a known current (a bench supply in current-limit mode at 1 A is fine) through the ground path and measure the millivolts between two points with a four-wire (Kelvin) technique or a sensitive DMM; $R = \Delta V/I$ gives the shared resistance directly. For the high-frequency picture, an impedance analyzer or a VNA with a small shorted-loop fixture gives the inductance. A simpler functional test: toggle the aggressor (LED, motor, clock) and probe the victim's ground reference relative to the supply's ground pin with a short-spring probe; a visible step or spike on the order of the prediction ($I\,R$ for slow, $L\,di/dt$ for fast) confirms common-impedance coupling.

::: applications
- **Two-layer consumer electronics**: ground grid and pours with stitching vias to approximate a plane.
- **Motor drives**: separate power-ground trace or region for the stage current, joined to logic ground at one point.
- **Shunt current sensing**: Kelvin sense traces to the amplifier, high current on wide traces or planes.
- **Audio**: star ground from the power input to each stage.
- **Multilayer digital boards**: full ground planes under all signal layers.
:::

- 1 oz copper, FR-4, 100 mm path length, as an illustration.

::: followups
### Why doesn't widening a ground trace fix the inductance?
Inductance depends on the ratio of length to width logarithmically: $L\approx 2\ell[\ln(2\ell/(w+t))+0.5+\ldots]$. Going from 0.25 mm to 2 mm wide (8×) on a 100 mm trace reduces $L$ from about 141 nH to about 102 nH (28%), while resistance falls by 8×. Meanwhile a plane brings the loop inductance down by bringing the return *close*, not by making the conductor wide.

### When is a star ground better than a plane?
At DC and low frequency, when you must keep a large current out of a sensitive reference: a current shunt, a precision sensor, a bridge, audio gain stages. A plane lets the current spread under everything, whereas a star forces large currents down their own paths. In many designs you combine them: a plane for HF, plus a deliberate single connection between power ground and sense ground.

### Does a plane solve ground loops?
No. A plane reduces impedance and loop area on the board, but a ground loop forms when two grounded systems are connected by two or more paths (for example, a cable shield and a ground wire). The loop through a cable can still pick up magnetic fields or carry noise. Break the loop with isolation, a common-mode choke or a single connection.

### How would you improve a 2-layer board's ground?
Make the bottom a near-solid pour, route fast signals on top directly over it, avoid cutting it with long routes, add stitching vias between top pours and the bottom every few millimetres, and put ground vias beside decoupling-capacitor pads. If a section must remain separate, use a deliberate single connection point.

### Scenario: your analog sensor reading shifts by 20 mV whenever a nearby LED driver turns on. The board has ground traces, not a plane. What do you suspect and change?
Common-impedance coupling: the LED current shares a ground segment with the sensor's reference. I'd measure the voltage along the ground trace with the LED on, then re-route so the LED current returns directly to the supply (its own trace or a local pour), with the sensor tied to the supply ground at a single point near the source. If I can spare a layer, I'd add a plane.
