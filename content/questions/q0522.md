::: testing
Whether you can name the **physical parts** that make a PCB trace a transmission line (signal conductor, reference plane, dielectric, plus the lumped features along the way) *and* the **distributed circuit model** that describes it (series R and L, shunt G and C per unit length). A strong answer ties the two together: geometry sets $L'$ and $C'$, so geometry sets $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and the delay; loss comes from $R'$ and $G'$; and the "components" that break the line are vias, pads, connectors and terminations.
:::

::: why
Every high-speed PCB question (impedance, reflections, termination, loss, crosstalk) is a question about this structure. If you can sketch a microstrip cross-section, write the RLGC ladder and say what each element physically is, you can answer most follow-ups from first principles instead of memorised rules. It is also the vocabulary the fab uses when you request controlled impedance. The general theory is in [[q0708]] and [[q0718]]; this page is the PCB-specific view.
:::

::: concepts
- **Signal conductor and return conductor**: a transmission line always has two conductors; on a PCB the return is usually a plane. See [[q0486]].
- **Dielectric**: FR-4 core/prepreg between trace and plane sets $\varepsilon_r$ (about 4.2–4.5) and the loss tangent. See [[q0474]].
- **Per-unit-length model (RLGC)**: $R'$, $L'$, $G'$, $C'$ in $\Omega$/m, H/m, S/m, F/m. See [[q0721]].
- **Characteristic impedance and delay**: $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ (lossless), $t_{pd}=\sqrt{L'C'}$. See [[q0710]].
- **Structures**: microstrip, stripline, coplanar waveguide. See [[q0476]].
- **Lumped discontinuities**: vias, pads, connectors, terminations. See [[q0526]].
:::

::: answer
"A PCB transmission line has two conductors and a dielectric. The signal trace is one conductor, the reference plane under it, usually ground, is the return conductor, and the FR-4 or prepreg between them is the dielectric. Depending on where the trace sits you call it microstrip if it's on an outer layer with air above, stripline if it's buried between two planes, or coplanar waveguide if there are ground pours on the same layer beside it.

To model it, I chop the line into short segments and each segment has a series resistance and inductance, from the copper and the magnetic field around the loop, and a shunt capacitance and conductance, from the electric field in the dielectric between trace and plane. In per-length terms that's R prime, L prime, C prime and G prime. For a low-loss line the impedance is square root of L over C, and the delay per length is square root of L times C. For a 50 ohm FR-4 microstrip that works out around 0.3 nanohenries and 0.12 picofarads per millimetre, about 6 picoseconds per millimetre.

On top of the line itself there are lumped components in the path: the driver and receiver pins, vias, pads, AC-coupling caps, connectors, and termination resistors. Each of those is where the impedance deviates and signals reflect, which is why they matter as much as the trace."
:::

::: technical
**Physical components.**
- *Signal trace*: copper, typically 17–35 µm thick, width $w$ set by the impedance target.
- *Reference plane(s)*: the return path; return current at high frequency concentrates directly under the trace.
- *Dielectric*: core/prepreg with $\varepsilon_r\approx4.3$ and loss tangent $\tan\delta\approx0.015$–0.025 for FR-4 (frequency dependent; see [[q0525]]); thickness $h$.
- *Solder mask / air above microstrip*: lowers effective $\varepsilon_r$; $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3.1$ for the example below, which gives faster propagation than stripline.
- *Lumped/discrete items on the path*: pads, vias, AC caps, series resistors, connectors, IC package.

**Distributed model.** For an increment $\Delta z$: series $R'\Delta z$ and $L'\Delta z$, shunt $C'\Delta z$ and $G'\Delta z$.
$$Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{R'+j\omega L'}{G'+j\omega C'}}\;\xrightarrow{\;\text{lossless}\;}\;\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}},\qquad v=\frac{1}{\sqrt{L'C'}}=\frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}}$$
For FR-4 microstrip with $h=0.2$ mm, $w=0.36$ mm, 1 oz copper: $Z_0\approx50\ \Omega$, $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3.1$, $L'\approx0.30$ nH/mm, $C'\approx0.12$ pF/mm, $t_{pd}\approx5.9$ ps/mm.

**Loss.** Conductor loss $\alpha_c\approx R'/(2Z_0)$ grows as $\sqrt f$ (skin effect); dielectric loss $\alpha_d\approx\pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}\tan\delta/c$ grows linearly with $f$. At 1 GHz, roughly 0.08 dB/inch each for typical FR-4 microstrip; dielectric dominates above a few GHz.

**Structures.**
| Type | Description | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Microstrip | trace on outer layer over a plane | simple, probeable; radiates; $\varepsilon_{eff}<\varepsilon_r$ |
| Stripline | trace between two planes | shielded; $\varepsilon_{eff}=\varepsilon_r$; needs vias |
| Coplanar waveguide (CPW/GCPW) | trace with ground pour either side (and plane below) | RF; tighter fields; via fences |
| Differential variants | edge- or broadside-coupled pairs | $Z_{diff}=2Z_{odd}$ |
:::

::: short
"A PCB transmission line is a signal trace over a reference plane with FR-4 dielectric between them. It's microstrip if it's on an outer layer, stripline if buried between two planes. Electrically it's a ladder of series R and L and shunt C and G per unit length; $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and delay is $\sqrt{L'C'}$, about 50 Ω and 6 ps/mm for a typical trace. Along the path you also have lumped pieces, vias, pads, connectors and terminations, which are the places that cause reflections."
:::

::: long
"I like to describe a PCB transmission line in two ways: the physical parts and the electrical model.

Physically it's three things. The signal trace, the copper on one layer. The return conductor, which on a modern board is a solid ground plane on an adjacent layer, and at high frequency the return current flows right under the trace. And the dielectric between them, FR-4 with a relative permittivity around 4.3 and a loss tangent around 0.02. Depending on geometry it's microstrip when the trace is on an outer layer with air or solder mask above, stripline when it's buried between two planes, and coplanar waveguide when there are ground pours alongside, which is common for RF.

Electrically I treat it as a chain of tiny segments. Each segment has a series resistance for the copper and a series inductance for the loop formed by the trace and its return, plus a shunt capacitance between trace and plane and a shunt conductance for the dielectric loss. Per unit length, R prime, L prime, G prime and C prime. In the lossless case the characteristic impedance is square root of L prime over C prime and the velocity is one over square root of L prime C prime. For a 50 ohm microstrip on 0.2 millimetre FR-4 you get about 0.3 nanohenries and 0.12 picofarads per millimetre, about 6 picoseconds per millimetre.

The R and G are why signals lose amplitude: conductor loss goes up with the square root of frequency from skin effect, dielectric loss goes up linearly with frequency.

Finally, there are the things on the line that aren't line: pads, vias, AC-coupling caps, connectors, the package. They're lumped or short non-uniform sections, and each one is where the impedance deviates and some of the signal reflects. Good layout is mostly about keeping the line uniform and making those lumped bits as invisible as possible."
:::

::: terms
- **Signal conductor / reference conductor**: the two conductors of the line; the plane is the return.
- **Dielectric / $\varepsilon_r$ / $\tan\delta$**: insulating material, its relative permittivity and its loss tangent.
- **$\varepsilon_{eff}$**: effective permittivity seen by the fields; between 1 and $\varepsilon_r$ for microstrip.
- **Per-unit-length parameters ($R',L',G',C'$)**: distributed series resistance/inductance and shunt conductance/capacitance.
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: ratio of voltage to current for a single travelling wave.
- **Propagation delay ($t_{pd}$)**: time per unit length, $\sqrt{L'C'}$.
- **Microstrip / stripline / CPW**: outer-layer, buried and coplanar line geometries.
- **Discontinuity**: any place along the path where geometry or impedance changes (via, pad, connector, stub).
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer lists "trace, ground plane, dielectric." An exceptional answer:
- Draws or describes the **cross-section** with $w$, $h$, $t$ and $\varepsilon_r$ and distinguishes microstrip, stripline and CPW with their $\varepsilon_{eff}$ consequences.
- Gives the **RLGC model**, maps each element to physics (loop inductance, plate capacitance, skin effect, dielectric loss), and states $Z_0$ and $t_{pd}$ formulas.
- Provides **typical numbers**: 50 Ω, 5.9 ps/mm, $L'\approx0.3$ nH/mm, $C'\approx0.12$ pF/mm, loss $\approx0.08$ dB/in per mechanism at 1 GHz.
- Names the **lumped elements** (vias, pads, connectors, termination) as the practical sources of discontinuity.
- Notes that return current flows in the **plane under the trace**, not in a separate wire.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Forgetting the return conductor.** A transmission line is a pair of conductors; the plane is half the line.
- **Using $\varepsilon_r$ instead of $\varepsilon_{eff}$ for microstrip delay.** Some field is in air, so the velocity is faster than $c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$.
- **Saying inductance and capacitance are "parasitic" and negligible.** They *are* the line; $Z_0$ and delay come from them.
- **Treating R as the cause of impedance.** In a low-loss line $Z_0$ is set by $L'/C'$; R and G only add loss.
- **Believing $Z_0$ depends on trace length.** It depends only on cross-section geometry and materials.
- **Ignoring the lumped parts** (vias, connectors, pads) when asked about "components".
:::

Letting $\Delta z\to0$ gives the telegrapher's equations, and the single-wave solution has impedance and propagation constant
$$Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{R'+j\omega L'}{G'+j\omega C'}},\qquad \gamma=\alpha+j\beta=\sqrt{(R'+j\omega L')(G'+j\omega C')}$$
At GHz frequencies $R'\ll\omega L'$ and $G'\ll\omega C'$ so $Z_0\to\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and $v=1/\sqrt{L'C'}$.

## Numbers for a typical trace

Take FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r=4.3$), $h=0.2$ mm, $t=35$ µm, $w=0.36$ mm: the field-solver-like model gives $Z_0=50$ Ω and $\varepsilon_{eff}=3.12$. Then $t_{pd}=\sqrt{3.12}/c=5.9$ ps/mm, $L'=Z_0t_{pd}=0.295$ nH/mm and $C'=t_{pd}/Z_0=0.118$ pF/mm. Check: $\sqrt{0.295\text{ nH}/0.118\text{ pF}}=50.0\ \Omega$ ✓.

## Where the resistive and conductance terms come from

- **$R'$**: DC resistance $\rho/(wt)\approx1.4\ \Omega$/m for this trace, but skin effect confines current to a depth $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu_0)}=2.1\ \mu$m at 1 GHz, so $R'$ rises as $\sqrt f$ (about 20–35 Ω/m at 1 GHz including the return plane and edge crowding; roughness raises it more). See [[q0725]].
- **$G'$**: dielectric polarisation lag, $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$. For $\tan\delta=0.02$ at 1 GHz, $G'\approx0.015$ S/m. See [[q0723]].

> Loss values are approximate and depend on copper roughness, laminate (loss tangent varies 0.015–0.025 for FR-4 and with frequency), trace width and the exact geometry. Treat 0.08 dB/in per mechanism at 1 GHz as an order-of-magnitude estimate, then use the fab's data or a solver.

## Components in the signal path

A real net looks like: driver output impedance → package → pad → via (or none) → trace → AC-coupling cap → via → trace → connector → ... → receiver with termination. Each non-uniform item has its own small equivalent circuit:

| Item | Equivalent | Typical size | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Via | series L, shunt C | ~1 nH, ~0.4 pF | reflection (see [[q0515]]) |
| Pad / test point | shunt C | 0.1–0.5 pF | impedance dip |
| AC-coupling cap | series C + ESL | 100 nF, ~0.5 nH | step response; pad cap |
| Connector | L, C, coupled | 1–3 nH | discontinuity, crosstalk |
| Termination resistor | R + ESL | 0.4–1 nH | match at the end |
| IC input | shunt C | 1–5 pF | capacitive load |

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Impedance tolerance.** Fabs hold ±10% (±5% with extra cost), set by thickness and etch variation.
- **Skin and proximity effect**: current crowds at trace edges and in the plane directly below; this is why plane quality matters ([[q0725]]).
- **Return path**: with the plane intact the line is uniform; with a slot it's non-uniform and the model breaks ([[q0514]]).

## Worked example: where the 0.08 dB per inch comes from

The loss figures in the technical section can be derived from the RLGC model, which is a good way to check that you understand each term. For small loss, the attenuation constant is the sum of a conductor part and a dielectric part:

$$\alpha_c\approx\frac{R'}{2Z_0},\qquad \alpha_d\approx\frac{G'Z_0}{2}=\frac{\omega C'\tan\delta\,Z_0}{2}=\frac{\pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}\tan\delta}{c}$$

(using $C'Z_0=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/c$). With $\tan\delta=0.02$, $\varepsilon_{eff}=3.12$ and $f=1$ GHz:

$$\alpha_d=\frac{\pi(10^{9})(1.766)(0.02)}{3\times10^{8}}=0.37\ \text{Np/m}=0.37\times8.686=3.2\ \text{dB/m}=0.082\ \text{dB/in}$$

The dielectric term is independent of the trace geometry: it depends only on $f$, $\varepsilon_{eff}$ and $\tan\delta$, so no layout can reduce it except by the choice of material. For the conductor term at 1 GHz take $R'\approx25\ \Omega$/m including the plane, edge crowding and some roughness (an assumption, between the 1.4 Ω/m DC value and the roughness-increased values): $\alpha_c=25/(2\times50)=0.25$ Np/m, or 0.055 dB/in, in the same range as the 0.076 used in the plot (rougher copper raises it). The totals show the crossover: conductor loss varies as $\sqrt f$ and dielectric loss as $f$, so the two are equal near 1 GHz in this example and dielectric loss is about 2.2 times larger at 4 GHz.

## Worked example: a loss budget for a 10-inch, 8 Gb/s channel

An 8 Gb/s NRZ signal has its fundamental (Nyquist) frequency at 4 GHz. Using the plot's model, total loss at 4 GHz is $0.076\sqrt4+0.082\times4=0.152+0.328=0.48$ dB/in, so a 10-inch trace loses about $4.8$ dB at Nyquist: the receiver sees 57 % of the launched amplitude at that frequency ($10^{-4.8/20}=0.575$), while low-frequency content is barely attenuated. The result is a closed eye from inter-symbol interference unless the link equalizes: a transmit de-emphasis or receiver equalizer that boosts high frequencies by a few dB. Compare the same trace in a low-loss laminate with $\tan\delta=0.005$ (an assumed illustrative value; check the laminate datasheet): the dielectric term falls by 4 times to about 0.08 dB/in at 4 GHz, and the total to about 0.23 dB/in, or 2.3 dB over 10 inches. This is the basic reason board materials are selected from the channel's insertion-loss budget, not from the dielectric constant alone.

## Worked example: when is the trace a transmission line at all?

A 150 mm trace has a delay of $150\times5.9\ \text{ps/mm}=0.885$ ns. Using the rule that a line is electrically long when the one-way delay exceeds about $t_r/6$, this trace must be treated as a transmission line for any edge faster than about 5.3 ns, which includes most digital I/O on modern parts, and not for a 100 kHz sine wave whose period is 10 µs. Even at low frequency, though, the formula $Z_0=\sqrt{(R'+j\omega L')/(G'+j\omega C')}$ keeps its meaning only for a line long enough to see a travelling wave. Evaluating the full expression with an illustrative $R'=3$ Ω/m, $L'=295$ nH/m, $C'=118$ pF/m and $\tan\delta=0.02$:

| Frequency | $|Z_0|$ | Phase |
|---|---|---|
| 100 kHz | 201 Ω | −43° |
| 1 MHz | 69 Ω | −29° |
| 10 MHz | 50.3 Ω | −4° |
| 100 MHz | 50.0 Ω | 0° |

Below a few megahertz the characteristic impedance rises and becomes complex because the series resistance dominates; above about 10 MHz it is 50 Ω to within a percent. That is why a 50 Ω scope input or VNA measurement is only valid above the frequency where $R'\ll\omega L'$, and why the textbook shortcut $\sqrt{L'/C'}$ is the right model for digital edges.

## Limiting cases: when the model changes

- **Short line (electrically small).** If the trace delay is below $t_r/6$, treat it as a lumped capacitor and inductor; the telegrapher's equations still hold but reflections are not visible.
- **Very high frequency.** Above about 10 GHz, surface roughness raises conductor loss by up to a factor of 1.5 to 2, $\varepsilon_r$ falls slightly with frequency (dispersion), and modes other than the quasi-TEM mode (surface waves, radiation) appear in microstrip. The simple RLGC model needs frequency-dependent parameters.
- **Stripline versus microstrip.** For the same $Z_0$, stripline has about 17 % higher delay (6.9 versus 5.9 ps/mm), but is shielded, so crosstalk and radiation are lower; the extra delay matters for length matching between layers.
- **Coupled lines.** Two adjacent lines share fields, so each has an even-mode and an odd-mode impedance, and their velocities in microstrip differ, which is why a differential pair in microstrip can have mode conversion.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **Dielectric constant is not a single number.** Glass weave and resin content vary within a board, and $\varepsilon_r$ falls with frequency; vendors quote 3.9 to 4.7 for FR-4 depending on test method and frequency. Use the fab's stackup values for the specific laminate.
- **Solder mask over the trace.** Mask over a microstrip raises $\varepsilon_{eff}$ and lowers $Z_0$ by about 1 to 3 Ω. Fabs' calculators include a parameter for it; a line that is bare on one section and masked on another has a step in impedance.
- **Etch factor and thickness.** The trace is a trapezoid, and the plated copper on outer layers is thicker than inner foil, so the same drawn width gives slightly different impedances on different layers.
- **Surface finish.** ENIG's nickel layer adds loss at GHz frequencies for traces that are bare (for example RF pads), because current flows in the nickel. For digital traces under mask this is negligible.

## Bench verification

Characterize your own board line. A TDR on a coupon gives $Z_0$ directly (the flat part of the trace after the connector) and, from the round-trip time, the delay per length: a 150 mm trace should show a round-trip of about 1.77 ns. A VNA measurement of $S_{21}$ on a long (say 10 inch) and a short (1 inch) trace, subtracted, gives the insertion loss per inch versus frequency: compare the slope with the $\sqrt f$ and $f$ model to separate conductor and dielectric contributions, and back out $\tan\delta$ from the high-frequency slope. Whatever the fab reports for coupon impedance, check it against your own TDR on one prototype; a persistent 5 to 10 % offset points to a thickness or $\varepsilon_r$ assumption you can correct in the next stackup.

::: applications
- **Controlled-impedance traces** for DDR, PCIe, USB, Ethernet and HDMI specified to the fab as 50/90/100 Ω.
- **RF front-ends and antennas**: microstrip or GCPW feed lines on Rogers-class laminates.
- **Clock distribution**: 50 Ω microstrip/stripline runs with series termination.
- **Backplane and connector design**: line + connector + via modelled as a cascade of transmission-line sections.
- **TDR measurement of a coupon**: a test strip on the panel verifies the line's impedance.

::: assumptions
- Numbers assume **FR-4** with $\varepsilon_r\approx4.3$, $\tan\delta\approx0.02$ and 1 oz copper; Rogers-class RF laminates have lower $\varepsilon_r$ and $\tan\delta$ (about 0.002–0.004).
- The lossless $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ holds for $\omega L'\gg R'$ (roughly above 1–10 MHz for PCB traces); at low frequency the line is resistive-capacitive ([[q0525]]).
- The *uniform line* model assumes constant cross-section and an unbroken plane.
- "Components" is read as both **physical constituents** and **model elements**, plus lumped parts on the path; see interpretations.

::: followups
### How do $L'$ and $C'$ relate to $Z_0$ and delay?
$Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and $t_{pd}=\sqrt{L'C'}$. So $L'=Z_0t_{pd}$ and $C'=t_{pd}/Z_0$. For 50 Ω and 5.9 ps/mm: $L'=0.295$ nH/mm, $C'=0.118$ pF/mm. Moving the plane closer raises $C'$ and lowers $L'$, lowering $Z_0$ without changing the delay much.

### Why is stripline slower than microstrip?
In stripline the field is entirely in the dielectric, so $\varepsilon_{eff}=\varepsilon_r=4.3$ and $t_{pd}=6.9$ ps/mm. In microstrip part of the field is in air ($\varepsilon=1$), so $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3.1$ and $t_{pd}\approx5.9$ ps/mm: about 15% faster.

### What is the role of $G'$?
It models dielectric loss: polarisation lags the field, dissipating energy. $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$, so dielectric loss grows linearly with frequency. It does not affect $Z_0$ to first order, but it attenuates and disperses the signal.

### Is the return current really in the plane directly below the trace?
At high frequency, yes: it follows the path of least inductance, within a few $h$ of the trace. At DC it spreads over the lowest-resistance path. This is the reason a plane slot under a fast trace is harmful.

### What if the trace crosses a gap in the plane?
The line is no longer uniform: $C'$ drops and $L'$ rises, so $Z_0$ jumps up, and the return current detours around the gap, creating inductive loop and radiation. Add a stitching capacitor or reroute ([[q0514]]).
:::

::: interpretations
"Transmission line components on a PCB" can be read three ways: (1) the **physical parts** (trace, plane, dielectric), (2) the **distributed circuit elements** (R', L', G', C'), and (3) the **lumped components** along the net (vias, pads, connectors, terminators). I cover all three, with emphasis on how (1) produces (2) and how (3) disturbs it.
:::
