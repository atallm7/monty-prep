::: basics
### In plain words
A diode acts differently depending on the voltage across it, and the three cases are called operating regions. Forward bias means it conducts. Reverse bias means it blocks. Breakdown means a large reverse voltage makes current flow again. Knowing which region a diode is in tells you which simple model to use.

### Picture it
Picture a gate in a fence. Walk toward it from the front, and it swings open once you push hard enough. Walk toward it from the back, and it stays shut. Push from the back with a bulldozer, and the gate breaks and lets everything through. Those are forward bias, reverse bias and breakdown. The analogy fails in one way: a real diode's breakdown is reversible if you keep the current low.

### Words you need
- **Forward bias**: the anode is more positive than the cathode, so the diode conducts. See [[q0072]].
- **Reverse bias**: the cathode is more positive, so the diode blocks and passes only leakage.
- **Breakdown**: a strong reverse field causes sudden current.
- **Operating region**: which of the three cases the diode is in right now.
- **Zener diode**: a diode designed to work in breakdown.

### The simplest example
Look at three voltages across a silicon diode, anode minus cathode. At +0.8 V, it is forward biased and on, with about 0.7 V across it for hand analysis. At minus 12 V on a 100 V diode, it is reverse biased and off, like an open switch. At minus 8 V across a 5.1 V Zener, it is in breakdown. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers each region in more detail, and the method of guessing a region, solving the circuit, and checking the guess.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can name the diode's operating regions, **state the condition that puts the diode in each one**, and describe what the device physically does and how you model it there. The three standard regions are:

1. **Forward bias** (conducting),
2. **Reverse bias** (blocking, leakage only),
3. **Reverse breakdown** (conducting in reverse at ≈ $V_{BR}$).

Strong answers add nuance: the forward region has a weak-conduction stretch below the knee and a resistive stretch at high current; transitions between regions take time (capacitance, reverse recovery); and some diodes are *designed* to live in a particular region (Zener and TVS in breakdown, photodiodes and varactors in reverse bias).
:::

::: why
Every diode circuit analysis starts with "which region is it in?". You assume a region, solve, then check the assumption, the same workflow you'll use for BJTs (cutoff/active/saturation, [[q0087]]) and MOSFETs (cutoff/triode/saturation, [[q0099]]). Getting the regions right is also how you pick parts: a rectifier must never reach breakdown, a Zener must never leave it, and a photodiode must stay reverse biased. Interviewers ask this to see whether you connect the curve's shape to circuit behaviour and design rules.
:::

::: concepts
- **I–V curve**: forward exponential, reverse leakage, breakdown. The regions are just labelled pieces of it. See [[q0074]].
- **Barrier height**: forward bias lowers the junction barrier, reverse bias raises it. See [[q0072]].
- **Breakdown mechanisms**: avalanche (impact ionization) and Zener (tunnelling).
- **Dynamic resistance**: the slope of the curve in each region ($r_d = nV_T/I_D$ forward, $r_z$ in breakdown).
- **Junction capacitance and stored charge**: depletion capacitance in reverse bias, diffusion capacitance in forward bias, which make region changes non-instantaneous.
:::

::: answer
"A diode has three main operating regions, and which one you're in depends on the voltage across it, anode minus cathode.

Forward bias is when the anode is more positive than the cathode. The junction barrier is lowered and current rises exponentially. Below about half a volt it's only microamps, but past the knee, roughly 0.6 to 0.7 volts for silicon, it conducts properly and the voltage stays nearly constant. So for hand analysis I treat it as a 0.7 volt drop.

Reverse bias is when the cathode is more positive, but by less than the breakdown voltage. The barrier is higher, the depletion region gets wider, and only a tiny leakage current flows, nanoamps, so I treat it as open. The depletion region also acts as a capacitor here, which is how varactors work, and it's where photodiodes operate.

Breakdown is when the reverse voltage reaches the breakdown voltage. The field is strong enough for avalanche multiplication or tunnelling, and the current increases sharply while the voltage stays roughly at $V_{BR}$. For a rectifier that's a failure condition, but Zener and TVS diodes are designed to operate there as references and clamps.

The general method is: guess the region, solve the circuit, and check the guess is consistent."
:::

::: technical
| Region | Condition ($V_D = V_A - V_K$) | Physics | Current | Hand model |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Forward bias | $V_D > 0$; "on" once $V_D \gtrsim 0.5$–0.7 V (Si) | Barrier lowered to $V_{bi}-V_D$; minority-carrier injection | $I_S(e^{V_D/nV_T}-1)$, µA–A | Short + $V_{D0}$ (+ $r_D$) |
| Reverse bias | $-V_{BR} < V_D < 0$ | Barrier raised to $V_{bi}+V_R$; depletion widens; only thermal generation | $\approx -I_S$ − generation, pA–µA | Open (+ $C_j$) |
| Breakdown | $V_D \le -V_{BR}$ | Avalanche (impact ionization) or Zener tunnelling | Large, set by external circuit | Source $V_Z$ + $r_z$ (reverse) |
| (Zero bias) | $V_D = 0$ | Equilibrium: drift = diffusion | 0 | Photovoltaic mode for solar cells |

**Sub-regions of forward bias.**
- *Weak conduction* ($0 < V_D < \sim0.5$ V Si): exponential but tiny current; $n \to 2$ from depletion-region recombination. Matters for leakage paths and low-current biasing.
- *Normal conduction*: ideal exponential, ~60 mV/decade, $r_d = nV_T/I_D$.
- *High-current / ohmic*: $V_D = V_j + I_DR_S$; high-level injection and self-heating.

**Capacitance by region.** Reverse: depletion capacitance $C_j = C_{j0}(1+V_R/V_{bi})^{-m}$ ($m \approx 0.33$–0.5). Forward: diffusion capacitance $C_d = \tau_T I_D/(nV_T)$, from stored minority charge $Q = \tau_T I_D$.

**Transitions.** Forward → reverse: the stored charge must be removed, so current flows in reverse for $t_{rr}$ (ns for 1N4148, µs for 1N4007, essentially none for Schottky). Reverse → forward: forward-recovery overshoot as conductivity modulation builds up.

**Breakdown details.** Zener mechanism dominates below ~5 V (negative tempco); avalanche above ~6 V (positive tempco). Breakdown is non-destructive if $P = V_{BR}I$ keeps $T_J$ within limits.
:::

::: long
"The diode has three operating regions, and they correspond to three pieces of its I–V curve. What decides the region is the voltage from anode to cathode.

First, forward bias, where the anode is more positive. Physically, the applied voltage lowers the potential barrier at the junction, so carriers diffuse across and the current rises exponentially, about ten times every 60 millivolts. I'd actually split this region in two. Below about half a volt for silicon, it is technically forward biased but the current is only micro- or nanoamps. Past the knee, around 0.6 to 0.7 volts, it conducts milliamps to amps with almost no change in voltage, and that's when I say the diode is 'on' and model it as a 0.7 volt source, sometimes with a small series resistance. At very high current, series resistance takes over and the curve straightens out.

Second, reverse bias, where the cathode is more positive but we haven't reached breakdown. The barrier gets taller and the depletion region wider. Only minority carriers and thermally generated carriers cross, so the current is a tiny leakage, picoamps to nanoamps for silicon at room temperature. I model it as an open circuit, but with a capacitance, because the depletion region is basically a capacitor whose plate spacing depends on voltage. Varactors use that, and photodiodes operate here too.

Third, breakdown. Once the reverse voltage reaches $V_{BR}$, the field is high enough for avalanche multiplication, or tunnelling in heavily doped junctions, and the reverse current shoots up while the voltage stays roughly constant. For a rectifier that's a failure mode, so you choose a reverse rating with margin. Zener diodes and TVS diodes are designed to operate there, as voltage references and clamps.

One more thing: switching between regions isn't instant. Going from forward to reverse, the stored charge has to be removed, which is reverse recovery, and that matters a lot in switching power supplies."
:::

::: terms
- **Forward bias**: $V_A > V_K$; barrier lowered, exponential conduction.
- **Reverse bias**: $V_K > V_A$ but $|V_D| < V_{BR}$; barrier raised, leakage only.
- **Breakdown region**: $|V_D| \ge V_{BR}$ in reverse; steep reverse conduction.
- **Cut-in (knee) voltage**: the forward voltage where current becomes significant for the circuit, ~0.5–0.7 V for silicon.
- **Avalanche breakdown**: carriers accelerated by the field ionize lattice atoms, multiplying current.
- **Zener breakdown**: quantum tunnelling across a very thin, heavily doped depletion region.
- **Depletion (junction) capacitance $C_j$**: capacitance of the depletion region, dominant in reverse bias.
- **Diffusion capacitance**: capacitance from stored minority charge, dominant in forward bias.
- **Reverse recovery**: the brief reverse current when a conducting diode is switched to reverse bias.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "on and off" or lists forward, reverse and breakdown without conditions.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the **condition** for each region in terms of $V_D$ and $V_{BR}$, and the current magnitude in each (mA, nA, externally limited).
- Explains the **physics** briefly: barrier lowered, barrier raised, avalanche or tunnelling.
- Gives the **model** used in each region for hand analysis.
- Notes which **devices are designed for which region** (rectifier/forward-reverse, Zener/TVS/breakdown, photodiode/varactor/reverse, solar cell/zero-bias-to-forward).
- Mentions that **transitions take time** (reverse recovery) and that the "on" threshold is an engineering approximation.
- Describes the **assume–solve–verify** method for finding the region ([[q0076]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "the diode is off below 0.7 V".** It's forward biased and conducting exponentially smaller currents. Whether that current matters depends on the circuit (e.g. µA leakage paths in a sleep-mode circuit).
- **Calling breakdown "damage".** Breakdown itself is reversible. Damage comes from excessive power or current. Zeners operate in breakdown continuously.
- **Confusing "reverse bias" with "reverse breakdown".** Reverse bias is the blocking region; breakdown starts only at $V_{BR}$.
- **Mixing up which terminal is positive.** Forward bias means anode positive relative to cathode. The cathode is the band on the package and the bar on the symbol.
- **Assuming instant switching between regions.** A PN diode switched from forward to reverse conducts backwards for $t_{rr}$; this causes losses and EMI in converters.
- **Forgetting that a Zener in forward bias is just a normal diode.** It drops ~0.7 V forward, not $V_Z$.
:::

## The three regions on the curve

The operating regions are three labelled stretches of the same I–V curve ([[q0074]]). What decides which stretch you're on is the voltage $V_D = V_{anode} - V_{cathode}$, and through it, what the junction barrier is doing.

## Forward bias: conducting

**Condition:** $V_D > 0$. In practice we say the diode is "on" when the current is large enough to matter to the circuit, which for silicon at mA levels means $V_D \approx 0.6$–0.7 V.

**Physics:** the applied voltage subtracts from the built-in barrier. Majority carriers diffuse across and are injected as minority carriers into the opposite side, where they recombine. Current follows $I_D = I_S(e^{V_D/nV_T}-1)$.

The forward region has three useful sub-regions:

| Sub-region | Roughly (silicon) | What's happening | What matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weak conduction | 0 to ~0.5 V | Exponential, but nA–µA; recombination current, $n \to 2$ | Sneak paths, sleep current, precision circuits |
| Normal conduction | ~0.5 to ~0.8 V | Ideal diffusion current, ~60 mV/decade | Most circuit design |
| High current | above ~0.8 V | $V_D = V_j + I_DR_S$, high injection, self-heating | Power rectifiers, surge |

**Model:** constant drop $V_{D0} \approx 0.7$ V, optionally plus $r_D$. For small signals around a bias point, $r_d = nV_T/I_{DQ}$.

**Capacitance:** stored minority charge $Q = \tau_T I_D$ gives diffusion capacitance $C_d = \tau_T I_D/(nV_T)$, which can be nF in a power rectifier. That charge is why the diode can't turn off instantly.

## Reverse bias: blocking

**Condition:** $-V_{BR} < V_D < 0$.

**Physics:** the barrier rises to $V_{bi} + V_R$ and the depletion region widens as $\sqrt{V_{bi}+V_R}$. Majority carriers are stopped. The only current is minority carriers drifting across plus thermal generation in the depletion region: pA–nA for small-signal silicon at 25 °C, µA or more for Schottkys and hot or large-area junctions.

**Model:** open circuit, plus a voltage-dependent capacitance

$$C_j = \frac{C_{j0}}{\left(1+V_R/V_{bi}\right)^m},\qquad m\approx 0.33\text{–}0.5$$

A 1N4148 has a few pF at zero bias, dropping as reverse voltage increases.

**Devices designed for this region:** photodiodes in photoconductive mode (reverse bias widens the depletion region, which speeds them up and lowers capacitance), varactors (the voltage-tuned $C_j$), and every rectifier during its blocking half-cycle.

## Breakdown: controlled or catastrophic

**Condition:** $V_D \le -V_{BR}$.

**Physics:** two mechanisms, both driven by the very high field in the depletion region:

- **Avalanche**: a carrier gains enough energy between collisions to knock loose another electron-hole pair. The new carriers do the same, multiplying the current. Dominant for $V_{BR}$ above about 6 V. Positive temperature coefficient (more phonon scattering means carriers need a stronger field).
- **Zener (tunnelling)**: in very heavily doped junctions, the depletion region is only a few nanometres thick and electrons tunnel straight from the P-side valence band into the N-side conduction band. Dominant below about 5 V. Negative temperature coefficient (the bandgap shrinks with temperature).

Near 5–6 V both contribute and their tempcos roughly cancel, which is why ~5.6 V Zeners are the most temperature-stable.

**Model:** a voltage source $V_Z$ (pointing so the cathode is positive) in series with dynamic resistance $r_z$, typically a few Ω to tens of Ω.

> [!WARNING]
> Breakdown is reversible; overheating is not. A 1N4007 that avalanches at ~1.2 kV with even 10 mA is dissipating 12 W and will die. A Zener operates there permanently because the circuit limits its current and its power is within rating.

Figure: The Zener's cathode faces the positive node, so it is reverse biased. As long as enough current flows through R_S to feed both the load and the Zener, the Zener sits in breakdown and holds V_out ≈ 5.1 V.

**With $R_L = 1$ kΩ:** assume breakdown, so $V_{out} = 5.1$ V.
- $I_S = (12 - 5.1)/330 = 20.9$ mA.
- $I_L = 5.1/1000 = 5.1$ mA.
- $I_Z = 20.9 - 5.1 = 15.8$ mA > 0. ✔ Assumption holds. Zener dissipation $= 5.1 \times 15.8 \approx 81$ mW.

**With $R_L = 220$ Ω:** assume breakdown again: $I_L = 23.2$ mA, but $I_S$ is still only 20.9 mA, so $I_Z$ would be **negative**. ✘ That's impossible in breakdown. The Zener must be in the **reverse-bias (blocking)** region, so remove it and solve the divider: $V_{out} = 12 \times 220/(330+220) = 4.8$ V. Check: 4.8 V < 5.1 V, consistent with blocking.

The boundary is $R_L = 5.1\text{ V}/20.9\text{ mA} \approx 244$ Ω. Below that, the "regulator" falls out of regulation. This assume-solve-check routine is exactly what [[q0076]] asks for.

## Moving between regions takes time

Region changes aren't instantaneous, because charge has to move.

**Forward → reverse (turn-off).** In forward conduction the diode stores minority charge $Q \approx \tau_T I_F$ in its neutral regions. When the circuit tries to reverse-bias it, that charge flows back out first: the diode conducts *in reverse* for the reverse-recovery time $t_{rr}$ before it blocks.

Figure: The PN diode keeps conducting after its current crosses zero, now in reverse, until the stored charge is swept out. Only then does it enter the blocking region. A Schottky (majority-carrier device) has essentially no stored charge, so only a small capacitive blip. In a buck converter this reverse current flows through the high-side switch and costs energy every cycle. Shape and timescale are illustrative.

**Reverse → forward (turn-on).** Less dramatic: a brief forward-voltage overshoot (forward recovery) while conductivity modulation builds up, noticeable in high-voltage rectifiers at fast $di/dt$.

**Reverse → breakdown.** Avalanche is fast (sub-ns), which is why TVS diodes can clamp ESD events with rise times of about 1 ns.

## Which devices live where

| Device | Normal operating region(s) | Must avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Rectifier / signal diode | Forward and reverse, alternating | Breakdown |
| Flyback / catch diode | Forward (when switch is off), reverse otherwise | Breakdown, slow recovery |
| Zener | Breakdown | Excess power; leaving breakdown (under-current) |
| TVS | Reverse (standby), breakdown (during surge) | Exceeding peak pulse power |
| LED | Forward | Reverse > ~5 V (many LEDs have low reverse ratings) |
| Photodiode | Reverse (photoconductive) or zero bias (photovoltaic) | Forward bias |
| Varactor | Reverse | Forward bias (Q collapses) |
| Solar cell | Fourth quadrant: forward voltage, reverse current (it's a source) | Reverse bias from shaded cells (hence bypass diodes) |

::: applications
- **Half-wave rectifier**: the diode alternates between forward (positive half-cycle, conducting) and reverse (negative half-cycle, blocking up to the peak input voltage, so $V_{RRM}$ must exceed $V_{peak}$ with margin).
- **Zener shunt regulator / clamp**: biased into breakdown with a series resistor; e.g. a 3.3 V Zener protecting an ADC input.
- **TVS diode on USB or CAN lines**: reverse biased (nA–µA leakage) during normal operation, briefly in breakdown during an ESD strike.
- **Photodiode TIA front end**: reverse biased to reduce capacitance and increase bandwidth, current proportional to light.
- **VCO tuning varactor**: reverse-biased junction capacitance varied by a control voltage in an RF PLL.
- **Solar panel bypass diodes**: forward biased only when a cell is shaded, so the shaded cell isn't forced into reverse breakdown and hot-spot heating.
:::

::: assumptions
- Silicon PN junction at room temperature; the "on" voltage of ~0.6–0.7 V is current-dependent and differs for Schottky (~0.2–0.45 V), germanium and LEDs. See [[q0078]].
- The three-region view describes **DC** behaviour. With fast switching, capacitance and stored charge add transient behaviour between regions.
- Breakdown voltage is device-specific: from ~2 V (low-voltage Zener) to kV (HV rectifiers). Some datasheets give a minimum $V_{RRM}$ rather than the actual breakdown voltage.
- Some textbooks give only two regions (on/off) for the ideal diode model; that's a valid simplified answer if the context is digital or large-signal. Some also treat zero bias separately because photovoltaic devices operate there.
:::

::: followups
### How do you decide which region a diode is in when analysing a circuit?
Assume a region, replace the diode with that region's model, solve, then check consistency. If you assumed "on" (0.7 V source), the current through it must come out positive (anode → cathode). If you assumed "off" (open), the voltage across it must come out below ~0.7 V. For Zeners, if you assumed breakdown, the reverse current must be positive. If the check fails, switch assumption and resolve. See [[q0076]].

### Why does a Zener have a near-zero temperature coefficient around 5.6 V?
Two breakdown mechanisms with opposite tempcos overlap there. Tunnelling (Zener) breakdown, dominant at low voltages, gets easier as temperature rises because the bandgap shrinks: negative tempco. Avalanche breakdown, dominant at higher voltages, gets harder as temperature rises because carriers scatter more and need a stronger field: positive tempco. Around 5–6 V the two contribute similarly and roughly cancel.

### What happens if a rectifier diode is pushed into breakdown?
It conducts large reverse current at a high voltage, so $P = V_{BR}I$ is large. Unless it's a controlled-avalanche part rated for avalanche energy, it overheats quickly and usually fails short. That's why rectifiers are chosen with $V_{RRM}$ well above the maximum reverse voltage, e.g. 1N4007 (1000 V) on 230 V AC mains where the peak is about 325 V.

### Why does reverse recovery matter in a switch-mode power supply?
In an asynchronous buck, the catch diode carries the inductor current during the off-time. When the high-side switch turns back on, it must sweep out the diode's stored charge, so for $t_{rr}$ the switch sees inductor current plus reverse-recovery current, with nearly full input voltage across it. That's a loss of roughly $Q_{rr}V_{in}$ per cycle plus a ringing, EMI-generating current spike. Schottky diodes (no minority storage) or synchronous MOSFETs avoid this. See [[q0197]].

### Can a diode be forward biased and still be effectively "off"?
Yes. At 0.3 V a silicon diode with $I_S = 10^{-14}$ A carries about 1 nA. That's forward bias by definition, but for most circuits it's negligible, so you'd treat it as off. In a µA-level battery circuit, though, a few such leakage paths add up. The point is that "on" and "off" are engineering judgments about the current level, while "forward" and "reverse" are defined by polarity.
