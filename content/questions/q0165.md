::: testing
Whether you know what the datasheet number $I_q$ actually is (the regulator's own supply current with no load, i.e. input current minus output current), that it is *not* the same as load current or shutdown current, and why it matters: it sets standby battery life and light-load efficiency. A strong answer also mentions that $I_q$ varies with load and mode (LDO ground current, buck switching vs non-switching $I_q$) and gives a battery-life calculation.
:::

::: why
In battery products, the device spends most of its life asleep, and the regulator that sits in front of a microamp MCU can draw more than the MCU. Choosing a 50 µA-$I_q$ LDO for a rail that idles at 2 µA reduces battery life by an order of magnitude. $I_q$ is also the lever behind "when is an LDO more efficient than a buck" ([[q0158]]) and one of the first numbers to check when a datasheet says "ultra-low power".
:::

::: concepts
- **Quiescent current ($I_q$)**: current drawn from the input by the regulator itself when the output is unloaded and regulating.
- **Ground current ($I_{GND}$)**: $I_{in}-I_{out}$ at any load; equals $I_q$ at no load.
- **Shutdown current ($I_{SD}$)**: input current when the part is disabled (typically far lower than $I_q$).
- **Efficiency at light load**: $\eta=\dfrac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_q)}$ ([[q0162]]).
- **Battery capacity**: runtime $=Q/I_{avg}$.
- **Switching vs non-switching $I_q$**: a buck's bias current when idling vs its much larger current while actively switching.
:::

::: answer
"Quiescent current is the current a regulator draws from its input just to stay alive and regulate, with nothing on the output. It's the bias current for the reference, the error amplifier, the feedback divider and the control logic. On an LDO it flows out of the ground pin, so it's also called ground current, and it's just input current minus output current.

It matters because it never goes away. At a 1 amp load a 50 microamp Iq is irrelevant, but if my device sleeps at a few microamps, the regulator can be most of the sleep current. A 220 milliamp-hour coin cell with a 52 microamp sleep current lasts about 176 days, but with 3 microamps it could last years, so Iq is what determines standby life. It also kills light-load efficiency: at 10 microamps of load, a 50 microamp Iq means under 20 percent efficiency.

So for always-on rails I'd look for nanoamp-class Iq parts, and I'd check whether Iq changes with load or mode: some LDOs draw more ground current at heavy load, and switching regulators have a low Iq when idling but a much higher current while actually switching."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** For a regulator with input current $I_{in}$ and output current $I_{out}$:
$$I_{GND}=I_{in}-I_{out},\qquad I_q\equiv I_{GND}\big|_{I_{out}=0}\ \text{(output regulating, enabled)}.$$
$I_q$ includes the bandgap reference, error amplifier/comparator, feedback divider current, protection and housekeeping circuits. For a switcher, the datasheet may distinguish a non-switching $I_q$ (device biased, output above regulation, not switching) from the average input current in light-load PFM/burst mode.

**Related currents.**
- *Shutdown current $I_{SD}$:* input current with EN low; typically nanoamps to a few µA, much lower than $I_q$.
- *Ground current vs load:* a PNP-pass LDO's ground current grows with load (base drive $\approx I_{out}/\beta$), while a MOSFET-pass LDO's stays nearly constant.
- *Feedback divider current:* an adjustable regulator with $R_1+R_2=100$ kΩ across a 3.3 V output adds 33 µA of load current that is not part of the datasheet $I_q$.

**Effect on efficiency.** Power wasted by $I_q$ is $V_{in}I_q$ (e.g. 50 µA at 3.6 V is 180 µW), so
$$\eta=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_q)}.$$

**Effect on battery life.** For capacity $Q$ and average current $I_{avg}$, runtime $t=Q/I_{avg}$ (ignoring self-discharge and derating). For a 220 mAh cell: 52 µA (50 µA regulator + 2 µA MCU) gives $0.22/52\mu\text{A}=4231$ h (176 days); 3 µA (1 µA regulator + 2 µA MCU) gives about 8.4 years, limited in practice by self-discharge.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Datasheets define $I_q$ at specified conditions (temperature, $V_{in}$, enabled, no load), and vendors sometimes quote typical rather than maximum values. Check the test conditions and the worst-case column, and note that $I_q$ often rises with temperature and with input voltage.
:::

::: long
"Quiescent current is the current the regulator itself consumes, separate from the current it delivers to the load. Think of it as the cost of staying ready. The reference, the error amplifier, the feedback divider, the control logic and protection circuits all need bias current. In an LDO that current exits through the ground pin, so I can find it as input current minus output current. The datasheet number is at no load, but it's not always constant: LDOs with a bipolar pass element draw more ground current as the load rises, while FET-based ones stay about flat. For a switching regulator you'd see a low Iq in a non-switching standby state, and a much higher average current as soon as it starts switching.

Why do I care? Two reasons. One is battery life. Say a sensor node sleeps at 2 microamps and the LDO has 50 microamps of Iq. The regulator is 96 percent of the sleep current. On a 220 milliamp-hour coin cell that's about 176 days, compared to years with a 1 microamp part. The other is light-load efficiency. Efficiency is Vout Iout over Vin times Iout plus Iq, so at 10 microamps of load with 50 microamps of Iq, I'm at about 15 percent, regardless of how small the dropout is. The effect disappears at amps of load, which is why Iq is a spec that mostly matters on rails that idle.

In design I'd pick the regulator whose Iq matches the idle budget, use enable pins or load switches to turn off rails I don't need, and check Iq over temperature and input voltage. I'd also remember that the external feedback divider adds its own current on top."
:::

::: terms
- **Quiescent current ($I_q$)**: regulator supply current at no load, output regulating.
- **Ground current ($I_{GND}$)**: current flowing out of the regulator's ground pin, $I_{in}-I_{out}$.
- **Shutdown current ($I_{SD}$)**: input current when the regulator is disabled.
- **Light-load efficiency**: efficiency at a small fraction of rated load, dominated by fixed losses such as $I_q$.
- **PFM / burst mode**: switching mode that lowers average $I_q$ at light load by skipping cycles.
- **Standby / sleep current**: total system current in low-power state.
- **Nano-power regulator**: regulator with $I_q$ in the nA to low-µA range.
- **Bias current**: current needed by internal circuits to operate.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the current a regulator draws with no load."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Defines $I_q$ as $I_{in}-I_{out}$ at no load and links it to the ground pin and the reference/error-amp/divider bias.
- Distinguishes $I_q$ from shutdown current and from ground current at load, and notes that switchers have switching vs non-switching values.
- Shows the efficiency formula and a number (50 µA at 10 µA load gives about 15%).
- Gives a battery-life calculation (176 days vs 8 years on a 220 mAh cell).
- Mentions the external divider current and checking temperature/$V_{in}$ dependence, and the practical remedies (nano-power parts, enable pins, load switches).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating $I_q$ as negligible.** It is negligible only when the load is mA or more; on sleeping devices it often dominates.
- **Confusing $I_q$ with shutdown current.** $I_{SD}$ applies when the device is disabled and is usually orders of magnitude smaller.
- **Assuming a buck is lower than an LDO in $I_q$.** Many bucks have tens of µA; nano-power LDOs can be below 1 µA ([[q0158]]).
- **Forgetting the external divider.** An adjustable output with a low-value feedback divider can draw more than the IC's quoted $I_q$.
- **Using the typical, 25 °C number for the worst case.** $I_q$ rises with temperature and may vary with $V_{in}$.
- **Mixing up with the transistor "Q-point".** Quiescent current in amplifier bias is a related word (the DC bias current with no signal), but for regulators it is the supply overhead.
:::

## What the current is made of

Consider the block structure of an LDO.

Figure: The input current splits into two paths: $I_{out}$ flows through the pass element to the load, and the remaining $I_{GND}$ flows through the regulator's internal circuits to ground. At no load, $I_{GND}=I_q$.

Inside the regulator box: a bandgap reference ($\approx1.2$ V) and its bias; an error amplifier comparing the feedback voltage to the reference; the pass-element driver (which must charge the pass device's gate or supply base current); the internal feedback divider (often 100 kΩ–1 MΩ total, which is a 3.3–33 µA path at 3.3 V on a fixed-output part); and protection (current limit, thermal shutdown, UVLO). Each draws a bias current. Lowering $I_q$ is a design trade: lower bias means slower response, lower bandwidth, lower PSRR and a higher noise floor, which is why ultra-low-$I_q$ parts have limited transient performance unless they adaptively boost bias when the load steps.

**Ground current vs load.** In an NMOS or PMOS pass LDO, the gate takes no static current, so $I_{GND}$ is almost constant vs $I_{out}$. In a PNP-pass regulator, base current is $I_B=I_{out}/\beta$, so $I_{GND}$ rises with load (for a pass-device $\beta$ of 10–20 it could be 5–10% of $I_{out}$; this is part-specific, and a datasheet graph of "ground current vs. load" shows it).

## Efficiency at light load

$$\eta=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_q)}.$$

For $V_{in}=3.6$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V and $I_q=50\ \mu$A:

| $I_{out}$ | $\eta$ |
|---|---|
| 10 µA | 15% |
| 100 µA | 61% |
| 1 mA | 87% |
| 10 mA | 91% |
| 100 mA | 92% |

The peak of 91.7% is the $V_{out}/V_{in}$ limit; $I_q$ only bites at light load. A part with $I_q=1$ µA would give 83% at 10 µA.

## Battery life: the real reason it matters

Runtime is capacity over average current. Take a 220 mAh coin cell, a sensor MCU that sleeps at 10 µA overall and wakes rarely, and compare regulators by their $I_q$.

Figure: Doubling the sleep current halves the life, so a 100 µA regulator next to a 10 µA system costs nearly 90% of the runtime compared with a 1 µA part. (Ideal capacity; real coin cells suffer from self-discharge, pulse-current capacity loss and temperature, so use the cell datasheet for real numbers.)

Real systems have a *duty cycle*, so use the weighted average. If a node spends 99% of the time asleep at (2 µA + $I_q$) and 1% active at 5 mA (plus $I_q$), $I_{avg}=0.99(2\ \mu A+I_q)+0.01(5\ \text{mA}+I_q)=52\ \mu A+I_q$ (roughly), so an $I_q$ of 50 µA doubles the average current. Anything that makes the regulator run all the time, in every mode, is paid for all the time.

## Switching regulators

A buck has several different "quiescent" numbers and the datasheet will be explicit about the conditions:

- **Non-switching $I_q$ (or sleep):** the bias current when the output is above its regulation threshold and the part is not switching, in a PFM or burst mode. This can be a few µA for ultra-low-power parts, but 25–100 µA for general-purpose ones.
- **Switching $I_q$:** the current while switching, including gate drive: much larger and proportional to $f_{sw}$.
- **Effective light-load input current:** in burst mode the average input current includes bursts. A rail that delivers 10 µA at 3.3 V (33 µW) with a 20 µA $I_q$ at 12 V in already burns 240 µW before any conversion loss.
- **Fixed overhead** regardless of load: reference, UVLO, bias, which dominates light-load efficiency.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked to choose a regulator for a coin-cell product, lead with $I_q$ and sleep current, not efficiency at full load. "Ninety percent at 1 A" is irrelevant for a device that lives at 10 µA.

## How to measure $I_q$

1. Enable the regulator, apply the specified $V_{in}$, leave the output unloaded (also disconnect any external feedback divider if you want the IC-only number, or include it and note it).
2. Measure the input current with a source-measure unit or µA-range meter. Mind the burden voltage of a series meter, since it reduces the regulator's input voltage.
3. Wait for settling (output capacitor charge, soft-start, leakage in the input capacitor).
4. Repeat over temperature and $V_{in}$.

Watch for leakage from capacitors (electrolytics leak tens of µA), flux residue, and parasitic paths such as a scope probe or DMM input resistance (10 MΩ at 3.3 V is 0.33 µA).

## Reducing the cost of $I_q$

- Select a nano-power LDO or a buck with a low-power mode for always-on rails.
- Use **enable pins or load switches** to turn off rails not needed during sleep (shutdown current is usually far smaller).
- Power the always-on part of the system from a small LDO, and the large load from a main converter that is switched off in sleep.
- Choose a high-value feedback divider (or an internal one) so it does not exceed the IC's $I_q$.
- Match the part to the profile: one part with excellent full-load efficiency and another for standby, with a handoff.
:::

::: applications
- **Coin-cell and energy-harvesting sensor nodes:** nano-power LDOs with $I_q$ under 1 µA.
- **Always-on real-time clock or wake-up logic** powered through a separate low-$I_q$ regulator.
- **Wearables and smartwatches:** buck converters with µA-class PFM modes.
- **Automotive always-on rails:** low-$I_q$ regulators that stay connected to the car battery (the "key-off" current budget is tens of µA).
- **Smart meters and trackers** that need years of standby.
- **Power-management ICs:** separate low-power and high-power regulators for standby vs active.
:::

::: assumptions
- $I_q$ is defined at no load, output regulating, enabled, at a stated temperature and $V_{in}$; other definitions (e.g. including internal feedback divider, or measured in shutdown) are used by different vendors.
- Battery-life calculations use ideal capacity and constant current; real cells have self-discharge, capacity dependence on load profile, and cutoff voltage limits.
- The PNP-pass ground-current statement is a general trend; actual ground current vs load is on the part's datasheet graph.
- For switching regulators, quiescent current and no-load input current can differ depending on mode and conditions.
- "Quiescent current" in the context of an amplifier output stage means the bias current at zero signal; this page deals with the regulator meaning, which is the standard one for power supplies.
:::

::: followups
### How does quiescent current differ from shutdown current?
Quiescent current is drawn while the regulator is enabled and regulating with no load; shutdown current is drawn when the enable pin disables the part. Shutdown is typically nanoamps to a few µA, lower than $I_q$ by one to two orders of magnitude, which is why disconnecting unused rails with the enable pin matters for standby.

### An LDO has $I_q=50$ µA and your MCU sleeps at 2 µA. What would you change?
The regulator is 96% of sleep current. Replace it with a nano-power LDO ($I_q\le1$ µA), or power the MCU's sleep domain from a low-$I_q$ rail and use the 50 µA LDO only for the active domain with its enable pin controlled by the MCU. Also check the divider and any pull-ups. For a 220 mAh cell that changes runtime from 176 days to a few years.

### Why does a lower $I_q$ LDO often have worse transient response?
$I_q$ biases the error amplifier and drive stage. Lower bias current means lower bandwidth and slew rate, so the pass device responds more slowly to a load step and the output dips further. Some LDOs use adaptive biasing: low $I_q$ at light load, but boosting bias when the output sags, to balance both.

### What's the quiescent current of a buck converter in light-load mode, and how is it different from an LDO's?
It depends on the mode. In PFM/burst mode the device sleeps between pulses with a non-switching $I_q$ (µA range for good parts), but the average input current also includes the energy of the bursts. In forced-PWM mode it switches continuously and draws mA at no load. A useful comparison metric is no-load input current at the real $V_{in}$ and $V_{out}$.

### Scenario: your product's standby current is 120 µA but the MCU datasheet says 5 µA. Where do you look?
Measure rail by rail. Typical culprits: regulator $I_q$ (and its feedback divider), LED or pull-up leakage, sensor standby currents, level-shifter and USB-chip leakage, a backlight or an always-on analog block, and electrolytic leakage. Cut rails with enable pins or load switches one at a time and watch the total current drop, and measure with a µA-capable source-measure unit.
:::

::: interpretations
In power supplies, "quiescent current" means the regulator's own no-load supply current, which is the meaning used here. In amplifier design the same words describe the DC bias current of an output stage with no signal (class AB), and for CMOS logic "quiescent" means static (leakage) current; if the interviewer is in an analog or digital context, adjust accordingly and say which meaning you are using. Compare with the LDO-efficiency discussions in [[q0277]] and [[q0158]], and the treatment of external parts in [[q0281]].
:::
