::: testing
Whether you can explain a very specific manufacturing term correctly and connect it to its purpose. A mousebite is not a defect or a bug: it is a row of small drilled holes along a tab that makes the tab easy to snap. The interviewer checks that you know where mousebites appear (tab-routed panels), why they're perforated (controlled breakage), what the trade-offs vs V-score are (shape freedom vs stub and burrs) and what you must design around (keep-outs, tab placement, stress on parts).
:::

::: why
Anyone who has ever broken a small board out of a panel has seen the little rough bumps on the edge: those are leftover mousebites. They matter because the stubs can stop a board from sliding into an enclosure, a rail slot or a card guide, and because snapping them stresses nearby parts. Getting tab width, hole size and placement right is a standard DFM item, and a good intern spots these issues in a review ([[q0613]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Tab routing**: the board outline is routed out, but not completely; small bridges (tabs) keep the board attached to the panel. See [[q0594]].
- **Perforation**: a row of small holes through the tab so it breaks along a defined line.
- **Breakout stress**: bending or twisting breaks the tab and flexes the board.
- **Tab stub / burr**: the fragment left on the board edge after the snap.
- **Plated vs non-plated holes**: mousebite holes are normally non-plated to avoid copper burrs and shorts.
- **Keep-outs**: areas around tabs where no copper or components should be.
:::

::: answer
"Mousebites are the little rows of holes you see on the edge of a panelized PCB. When a board has an irregular outline or gaps between boards, the fab routes the outline but leaves a few narrow tabs holding the board to the panel. Those tabs would be hard to break cleanly, so instead of leaving them solid, we drill a line of small holes through each tab, usually around half a millimetre in diameter and spaced very close together. That works like a perforated sheet of paper: when you flex the board, the tab breaks along the line of holes and not wherever it likes.

The name comes from the way the leftover edge looks, like a mouse took little bites out of it. Design-wise I'd put tabs where there's no copper or components nearby, keep ceramic caps and connectors out of the breakout area, and make sure the stub left on the edge doesn't protrude past the outline the enclosure needs. If the edge has to be perfectly smooth, I'd ask for a depaneling router or ask the fab to leave the stub recessed into a notch."
:::

::: technical
**What they are.** Mousebites (also called *perforated tabs* or *drilled breakaway tabs*) are a line of small holes, typically 3-5 holes of about 0.5-0.8 mm diameter on roughly 0.8-1.0 mm centres, drilled through the narrow bridge (tab) that holds a routed board to its panel. The holes weaken the tab so it fractures along their line.

**Geometry (typical, vendor dependent).**
- Tab width about 2-3 mm; a 3 mm tab with 0.5 mm holes at 0.8 mm pitch holds $\lfloor(3 - 0.5)/0.8\rfloor + 1 = 4$ holes (computed).
- Hole pattern is centred on the board outline so the break line sits at the board edge. Holes are **non-plated**, and copper is kept clear (a typical rule is 0.3-0.5 mm of copper keep-out around the holes) to avoid burrs and shorts.
- Tabs are placed in pairs on opposite sides of each board, or three to four per board for larger ones, away from corners and from sensitive parts.

**Why not just solid tabs?** A solid tab would need a router or heavy bending to remove, and the break would be ragged and could delaminate the edge or tear copper. Perforation concentrates stress in the holes.

**Stress.** The strain delivered by breaking a tab is local to the tab but adds up for several tabs broken together. Keep MLCCs, BGAs and brittle parts at least several millimetres from tabs and orient MLCCs parallel to the tab line.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Hole diameters, pitch and tab widths in common DFM guides differ (e.g. some recommend 0.5 mm holes at 0.8 mm pitch, others 0.8 mm at 1.0 mm). Use the guide of the fab/assembly house you are actually ordering from.
:::

::: short
"Mousebites are rows of small, non-plated drilled holes through the narrow tabs that hold a routed board in a panel. They perforate the tab so it snaps cleanly where I want it when I break the board out. They're used for odd shapes where V-score can't work. The trade-off is a small stub on the edge, so I keep parts and copper clear of the tabs and make sure the stub doesn't interfere with the enclosure."
:::

::: long
"Mousebites are what you get when a board is held into its panel by routed tabs. If you route the full outline, the board falls out of the panel and you lose alignment for assembly, so the fab leaves a few narrow bridges to hold it. The problem is that a solid bridge is hard to break cleanly and tends to tear the board edge. So the fab drills a line of small holes across each bridge, typically half a millimetre or so and tightly spaced. That's the mousebite: a perforation, like a tear line on paper.

When I snap the board, the tab breaks along the holes. What's left on the edge is a small stub with scalloped bumps that look like something nibbled the edge, which is where the name comes from.

Why use them instead of V-score? V-score only works with straight lines and boards that touch, with nothing overhanging. If my board is round, has a connector sticking out, or I want a gap between boards for the router bit, tab routing with mousebites is the answer.

The design considerations are keep-outs and stress. The holes should be non-plated and have copper clear of them, otherwise you get burrs and possible shorts. Breaking the tab flexes the board, so I keep ceramic capacitors, connectors and BGAs away and orient capacitors parallel to the edge. And I check that the stub doesn't stick out past the outline my enclosure or card guide needs; if it does, I recess the tab into a small notch in the board edge."
:::

::: terms
- **Mousebite**: perforation holes in a breakaway tab.
- **Tab / bridge**: the narrow piece of board material connecting a routed board to the panel.
- **Breakaway tab**: a tab designed to snap off.
- **Non-plated through hole (NPTH)**: drilled hole with no copper barrel.
- **Stub / nub**: the leftover piece on the edge after breaking a tab.
- **Router (depaneling)**: a milling machine that cuts tabs cleanly instead of snapping them.
- **Edge keep-out**: area near the outline where copper or components are not allowed.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "mousebites are holes on the edge of a PCB so you can break it off."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains that they're perforations in routed tabs and contrasts that with V-score (straight line, boards edge-to-edge).
- Quotes typical geometry with a "vendor-dependent" caveat: about 0.5 mm holes, ~0.8 mm pitch, 2-3 mm tabs.
- Mentions non-plated holes and copper keep-out to avoid burrs and shorts.
- Raises mechanical stress and MLCC cracking and tab placement away from sensitive parts.
- Notes the stub left on the board edge and how to deal with it (notch/recess, router depaneling, sanding).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Calling mousebites a defect.** They're intentional. A defect would be a ragged or torn tab break.
- **Making the holes plated.** Plated holes leave copper burrs that can short to adjacent traces or chassis.
- **Placing tabs at corners or beside connectors.** Corner stresses are high and stubs interfere with mating parts.
- **Putting copper pours up to the board edge at the tab.** The break tears copper and can lift it; add a clearance.
- **Using too few holes or too thick a tab.** The tab refuses to break cleanly or breaks off a chunk of board.
- **Ignoring the stub.** An edge connector or slide-in board edge can jam on a 0.3 mm stub.
:::

::: deep
## The mechanism

A solid tab of width $w$ and thickness $t$ resists bending with a bending stiffness proportional to $w t^3$ and a cross-section for fracture proportional to $w t$. Drilling holes of diameter $d$ at pitch $p$ replaces the continuous cross-section with *ligaments* between holes of width $p - d$. With $d = 0.5$ mm and $p = 0.8$ mm, each ligament is 0.3 mm wide; the effective load-bearing width is $(p-d)/p = 37.5\%$ of a solid tab. The holes also create stress concentrations (about 3x the nominal stress at a circular hole edge in a plate under tension) so a crack starts at the holes and runs along the line.

## Typical numbers

| Item | Typical range |
|---|---|
| Hole diameter | 0.5-0.8 mm |
| Hole pitch (centre-centre) | 0.8-1.0 mm |
| Number of holes per tab | 3-5 |
| Tab width | 2-3 mm (sometimes up to ~5 mm for heavy boards) |
| Copper keep-out around holes | 0.3-0.5 mm |

Check the arithmetic for a 3 mm tab with 0.5 mm holes at 0.8 mm pitch: 4 holes fit, giving a perforation length of 3.1 mm of line with 3 ligaments of 0.3 mm. That is why the tab breaks with modest force.

## Placement rules

1. **Two to four tabs per board.** For a rectangular board, one near each end of the long sides; for a heavy board, add one more so it doesn't sag.
2. **Away from corners and from edge-mounted parts.** A corner break rotates and tears; connectors need clean edges.
3. **Away from sensitive components**: several millimetres from MLCCs, crystals, BGAs and QFNs, and align capacitor axes parallel to the break line.
4. **Recess the stub.** Make a notch in the board outline where the tab attaches so the broken stub stays inside the nominal outline, as in the figure above if the tab sits in a recess.
5. **No copper across the tab**, except maybe a thin ground ring that's cleared, or the copper tears and can short.

## Cleaning up the edge

After breaking, stubs have rough edges. Options: leave them (if nothing mates to that edge), sand or file them, use a depaneling router to cut the tab cleanly, or request a *pocketed tab* design with a notch so the stub never extends beyond the outline. The edge quality also matters if the board slides into card guides or mates with an enclosure rib.

## Mousebites vs V-score vs other

| | Mousebites | V-score | Router/laser |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Any | Straight, edge-to-edge | Any |
| Edge | Stubs | Smooth | Smoothest |
| Material | Wastes a routing channel | None wasted | Channel wasted |
| Stress | Local, moderate | Moderate to high | Low |
| Cost | Low | Low | Higher |

See [[q0594]] for the full panelization context, and [[q0596]] for the fiducials that sit on the rails around these tabs.

> [!TIP]
> If you want to sound like you've handled boards: mention that the hole row is on the *board* outline, not the panel, so the leftover bump sits flush. And that you'd always view the fab's panel drawing before approving, because tab location is usually something the fab places by default.
:::

::: applications
- **Round or odd-shaped boards** (wearables, sensors, LED pucks) that can't be V-scored.
- **Boards with connectors or edge fingers that overhang** the outline.
- **Prototype panels** where one tab pair is enough to hold a board for assembly.
- **Thin boards** (below about 0.8 mm), where V-score leaves too little web.
- **Panels with mixed board designs** where some boards are tab-routed and others V-scored.
:::

::: assumptions
- Describes rigid-board **mechanical** tab breakout. Laser or router depaneling avoids the snap step.
- Hole size and pitch are typical and vary by shop; some shops use a continuous slot pattern or "perforated" slits.
- Assumes non-plated holes. Plated holes are only used when the edge itself must be conductive (e.g. castellated half-holes), which is a different feature.
- If the board is very small (under ~10 mm), tabs may be unnecessary if the panel uses a carrier.
:::

::: followups
### Why are mousebite holes non-plated?
Plated holes would leave copper barrels that smear, burr and tear when the tab breaks, and the burrs can short to nearby traces or the enclosure. Non-plated holes leave bare laminate, which breaks cleanly. The copper clearance around them is for the same reason.

### How many tabs would you put on a 100 mm x 60 mm board?
Typically 3 or 4. Two on one long side and one or two on the other, spaced away from corners and sensitive parts. More tabs hold the board better during reflow but add more breaking force and more stubs; fewer tabs mean it may sag or flex under paste printing.

### The enclosure doesn't close because of a bump on the board edge. What do you do?
The bump is the leftover tab stub from the mousebite break. Short term, file or sand it. Long term, redesign the outline with a notch at each tab location so the stub remains inside the nominal outline, or use router depaneling. Add the maximum protrusion to the mechanical tolerance analysis.

### What if the board has a ceramic capacitor right near the tab?
It can crack when the board flexes during breakout, causing latent shorts. Move it, or orient it with its long axis parallel to the tab line, or switch to router/laser depaneling. A flex-termination capacitor is another mitigation (vendor dependent).

### You see shorts on boards from the edge after depaneling. Suspect mousebites?
Yes. Burrs of copper from copper near the holes or from plated holes can short to nearby traces or ground pours. Cross-section or inspect under a microscope, add copper keep-out around the perforation, and make sure the holes are NPTH.
:::

::: interpretations
"Mousebites" could be misheard as a *defect* (like "mouse bites" in solder mask or copper), but in PCB manufacturing it means perforated breakaway tabs. A separate meaning in some circles is *scalloped edge nibbles* left on a board after breaking tabs; this is the same thing viewed after the fact. Compare [[q0594]] for the overall panel.
:::
