::: basics
### In plain words
The output inductor and capacitor act together like a weight on a spring: they have a natural frequency where they resonate. The control loop (the circuit that keeps correcting the output) struggles near and above that frequency, because the filter delays signals so much that a correction can arrive too late and push the wrong way. That eats into the safety margin against oscillation. So L and C decide where that frequency sits and how the loop must be tuned.

### Picture it
Picture a weight hanging on a spring. The inductor is the heavy weight, since it resists change in current. The capacitor is the spring. A heavier weight or a softer spring bounces more slowly. Light load is like removing friction, so the bounce rings harder and is harder to control. The analogy leaves out the capacitor's own small resistance (ESR), which adds some damping.

### Words you need
- **Double pole**: the resonant frequency of L and C, $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$. Above it the filter's output falls off steeply and lags far behind ([[q0246]]).
- **Phase margin**: how much extra delay the loop could take before its corrections arrive late enough to cause oscillation. More is safer ([[q0244]]).
- **Crossover frequency**: the frequency where the loop stops amplifying its corrections (the loop gain equals 1).
- **ESR**: the small series resistance inside a capacitor. It adds a "zero", which partly cancels the lag.
- **Compensation**: the extra resistors and capacitors around the error amplifier that reshape the loop to keep it stable.

### The simplest example
Take $L=10$ µH and $C=100$ µF. Then $\sqrt{LC}=31.6$ µs, so $f_0\approx5$ kHz. If you raise C four times to 400 µF, the square root doubles and $f_0$ falls to about 2.5 kHz. The loop must then cross over lower, or the compensation must add more phase lead.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the transfer function, why light load is the worst case, and how current mode removes one of the poles.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can connect component values to loop dynamics: the LC filter is a **second-order plant** with a double pole at $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, a peak whose height depends on load and ESR, and a 180° phase drop the compensator must handle. The interviewer wants to see that you know *how* L, C, ESR and load shift that pole, peak and zero, what that does to phase margin, and what you do about it (crossover placement, Type II/III compensation, ESR zero, current mode). Naming the difference between voltage-mode and current-mode plants is the sign of a strong answer.
:::

::: why
A buck is a feedback system. The output LC isn't just a ripple filter: it sits inside the loop, and mismatching it to the compensation is the most common reason a freshly built supply rings, whines or oscillates, especially after someone swaps a capacitor or the capacitor ages. Understanding the plant lets you choose output capacitors, predict what changes with load, and explain why datasheets restrict $L$ and $C$ to a window ([[q0260]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Feedback stability**: phase margin and gain margin of the loop gain $T(s)$ ([[q0244]], [[q0245]]).
- **LC double pole**: $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, with quality factor set by load and losses ([[q0246]]).
- **ESR zero**: $f_z = 1/(2\pi\,ESR\,C)$ adds phase lead.
- **Voltage-mode vs current-mode**: voltage-mode keeps the double pole; current-mode (peak) turns the inductor into a controlled current source, leaving a single pole at $1/(2\pi R_L C)$ ([[q0252]]).
- **Compensation**: Type II or III network shapes $T(s)$ to cross at a safe frequency with margin ([[q0250]]).
- **Crossover frequency**: typically $f_{SW}/5$ to $f_{SW}/10$.
- **Load dependence**: light load raises $Q$ and reduces damping.
:::

::: answer
"The output LC is a second-order low-pass in the loop, with a double pole at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$. Above that frequency the gain falls at 40 dB per decade and the phase drops by up to 180°, so it eats most of the phase margin unless the compensation provides lead. In voltage mode the compensator typically has a Type III network with two zeros placed near the double pole to give phase boost.

L and C set where that pole sits and ESR sets a zero at $1/(2\pi\,ESR\,C)$. A bigger L or C moves the pole down, so the crossover has to come down or the compensation has to be retuned. How sharp the peak is depends on the damping from load and resistance: at light load the resonance is much sharper, so it's the worst case for stability. Ceramic capacitors have tiny ESR, so their zero is far above crossover and gives no phase boost; electrolytics with higher ESR give a free zero.

In current-mode control the inductor current is regulated inside the loop, which removes the second pole, leaving a first-order plant with a pole at $1/(2\pi R_L C)$, so it's much easier to compensate. But then the compensator gain, C and the slope compensation interact. Practically, I stay in the datasheet's L and C window and verify with a loop measurement."
:::

::: technical
**Voltage-mode power stage.** With series loss $r_L$ (DCR plus switch resistance), load $R$, and capacitor ESR $r_C$, the control-to-output transfer function (CCM) is approximately
$$G_{vd}(s) \approx V_{IN}\,\frac{1 + s\,r_C C}{1 + s\left(\frac{L}{R} + (r_L + r_C)C\right) + s^2 LC}$$
Double pole $f_0 = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$, quality factor $Q \approx \dfrac{\sqrt{LC}}{L/R + (r_L+r_C)C} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{L/C}/R + (r_L + r_C)/\sqrt{L/C}}$, ESR zero $f_z = 1/(2\pi r_C C)$. Phase falls by 180° around $f_0$ (a steeper fall for higher $Q$); gain beyond $f_0$ falls $-40$ dB/dec until $f_z$.

**Consequences.**

- Crossover $f_c$ above $f_0$ leaves the plant at about $-180^\circ$, so the compensator must provide lead ($>45^\circ$ margin needs a Type III with two zeros near $f_0$, or the ESR zero must lie below or near $f_c$).
- Increasing L or C lowers $f_0$, which changes the needed zero locations. Decreasing C raises $f_0$ and raises ESR-zero frequency.
- $Q$ rises as $R$ increases (lighter load): the damping from the load ($\sqrt{L/C}/R$ term) falls, leaving only $r_L + r_C$. Light load is the stability corner for voltage mode.
- Crossover must stay below about $f_{SW}/5$ to $f_{SW}/10$ because of the PWM sampling/delay.

**Peak-current mode.** The inner current loop gives $G_{vc}(s) \approx \dfrac{R}{R_i}\dfrac{1+s r_C C}{1+sRC}\,H_e(s)$ with a sampling double pole at $f_{SW}/2$ with $Q_p = 1/[\pi(m_c D' - 0.5)]$. Here $L$ barely appears in the low-frequency plant, which is why current-mode is easier to compensate; $L$ influences the sampling-pole damping through the slope compensation ([[q0255]]).
:::

Bigger L or C moves the pole down and forces a lower crossover or different compensation. ESR adds a zero at $1/(2\pi\,ESR\,C)$ that gives phase lead, but ceramics have almost none. Light load raises the Q of the peak, so it's the worst case. Current-mode control removes the second pole, leaving a single pole at $1/(2\pi R_LC)$ that's easier to compensate."

::: long
"I look at the buck as a feedback loop with a plant, a compensator and a modulator. The output inductor and capacitor are part of the plant. In voltage-mode control the control-to-output transfer function is a second-order low-pass: a double pole at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, flat gain of $V_{IN}$ below it, minus 40 dB per decade above, and the phase drops by close to 180° through the corner. How sharply it drops, and how big the resonant peak is, depends on the Q, which is set by the load and the series resistance: heavy load damps it, light load leaves almost nothing.

So the effects of the components are, first, L and C set the corner. Bigger L or C lower the double pole, so for a given crossover frequency the plant has more phase lag there and the compensator must supply more lead, or I lower the crossover and accept a slower response. Second, the ESR creates a zero at $1/(2\pi\,ESR\,C)$: with electrolytics, that zero sits at tens of kHz, close to crossover, and gives phase lead for free. With ceramics, ESR is a few milliohms and the zero is up in MHz, so I need a Type III compensator with two zeros to cancel the double pole, and a loop that is sensitive to the actual capacitance after DC-bias derating.

In current-mode control the inductor current is being regulated, so the plant looks like a controlled current source charging the capacitor: just one pole at $1/(2\pi R_LC)$. That's why current-mode is so popular, and why internally compensated parts have a window of allowed L and C.

In practice I check the loop with a network analyzer at light and heavy load and at the worst-case capacitance, and look for at least 45° phase margin and 10 dB gain margin."
:::

::: terms
- **Double pole / LC corner**: $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, where the output filter's gain starts falling at 40 dB/dec.
- **Quality factor ($Q$)**: sharpness of the resonance; high $Q$ means little damping.
- **ESR zero**: $1/(2\pi\,ESR\,C)$; lead from capacitor series resistance.
- **Phase margin**: $180^\circ + \angle T(f_c)$; margin against oscillation.
- **Crossover frequency ($f_c$)**: where $|T| = 1$ (0 dB).
- **Type II / Type III compensation**: error-amp networks with one or two zeros for lead.
- **Control-to-output ($G_{vd}$)**: plant transfer function from duty cycle to output.
- **Sampling pole**: current-mode modulator's double pole at $f_{SW}/2$.
:::

**An exceptional answer**:

- Explains **load dependence of $Q$** and that light load is the worst case in voltage mode.
- Contrasts voltage-mode (second-order, needs Type III) with current-mode (first-order $1/(2\pi R_LC)$ pole plus sampling pole).
- Notes ceramic capacitors' lack of ESR zero and the need to use **effective** capacitance.
- Quotes practical targets: crossover about $f_{SW}/5$ to $f_{SW}/10$, PM > 45°, GM > 10 dB, and tests at multiple loads.

:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying C only affects ripple.** C moves the pole, the zero and the crossover in current mode.
- **Thinking more C always improves stability.** It lowers $f_0$ and, in current mode, lowers the crossover; with ceramics it can leave the ESR zero absent and push you out of the compensation's window.
- **Testing stability only at full load.** In voltage mode the least damped condition is light load (high $Q$).
- **Ignoring ceramic DC-bias derating.** The effective $C$ may be half, shifting $f_0$ by $\sqrt2$ and the crossover in current mode by $2\times$.
- **Assuming ESR is always bad.** Moderate ESR provides damping and a lead zero; zero ESR makes compensation harder.
- **Confusing the converter's own switching delay and sampling with the LC plant.** They limit the maximum crossover to a fraction of $f_{SW}$.
:::

::: deep
## The plant seen by the controller

Open the loop at the feedback divider and ask: what does a small change of duty cycle do to $V_{OUT}$? During each cycle the switch node is a square wave of amplitude $V_{IN}$ and duty $D$; averaged, it is $D\,V_{IN}$. The inductor and output capacitor, with the load, form a low-pass filter that responds to this average. For small perturbations,
$$G_{vd}(s) = \frac{\hat v_{out}}{\hat d} \approx V_{IN}\,\frac{1+s r_C C}{1 + s\left(\tfrac{L}{R}+(r_L+r_C)C\right) + s^2LC}$$
In words: flat gain $V_{IN}$ at low frequency (for $r_L \ll R$), a second-order low-pass with resonance at $f_0$, and a zero from the ESR.

**Why the phase drops by 180°.** Each pole contributes up to $-90^\circ$ lag. A double pole has two. How steeply that happens is governed by $Q$: a high-$Q$ system swings from $0^\circ$ to $-180^\circ$ within a narrow frequency range around $f_0$; a low-$Q$ one spreads it over a decade or more.

## Numbers for the running example

12 V in, 3.3 V out, $L = 6.8$ µH, $C = 55$ µF effective, $r_L = 50$ mΩ (DCR plus switch), load 3 A ($R = 1.1$ Ω):

- $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{6.8\mu\times55\mu}) = 8.2$ kHz.
- $Q \approx 2.1$ at full load; at 0.3 A ($R = 11$ Ω) $Q \approx 5.5$.
- ESR zero: 3 mΩ ceramic gives 0.96 MHz (useless; above $f_{SW}$); 100 mΩ electrolytic gives 29 kHz.

Take a crossover at $f_c = 50$ kHz ($f_{SW}/10$). With ceramics the plant magnitude there is about $-9.5$ dB (so the compensator gain at 50 kHz must be +9.5 dB to cross) and its phase is about $-175^\circ$. A compensator that adds, say, a 90° of lead from a Type III double-zero would leave phase margin of about $-175+180+90 \approx 90$° at best before delay; subtracting the modulator and sampling delay (at 50 kHz with $T_s = 2$ µs, a 1.5-cycle delay costs about $360\times50\text{k}\times3\mu = 54^\circ$) leaves about 36° to 45°; hence the need for careful design and measurement.

## How L and C each move things

| Change | $f_0$ | $Q$ (full load) | Effect on loop |
|---|---|---|---|
| L doubled | $\downarrow\ 1/\sqrt2$ | lower ($\sqrt{L/C}$ up so load damping term $\sqrt{L/C}/R$ rises) | plant lags earlier; compensator zeros must move down; slower slew |
| C doubled | $\downarrow\ 1/\sqrt2$ | higher (less load damping) | lower crossover in current mode, lower ESR zero frequency |
| C halved (derated) | $\uparrow\ \sqrt2$ | | current-mode crossover doubles; may approach $f_{SW}/2$ sampling pole |
| ESR increased | same | lower (more damping) | zero at lower frequency, phase lead, more ripple |
| Load lightened | same | higher | resonance sharper; voltage-mode phase falls abruptly |

## Voltage mode: what compensation do you need?

The goal is a loop gain $T(s) = G_c(s)\,G_{vd}(s)\,/\,V_{ramp}\times H$ with a $-20$ dB/dec slope through crossover and PM > 45°. Since $G_{vd}$ is $-40$ dB/dec past $f_0$, the compensator should supply about $+20$ dB/dec there: that means an integrator with two zeros (**Type III**) placed near $f_0$ (to cancel the double pole's phase drop), and two poles above crossover to roll off high-frequency gain (one cancelling the ESR zero if there is one). With an electrolytic and a low ESR zero near $f_c$, a **Type II** compensator (one zero) can be enough because the ESR zero gives the rest. This is the reason older designs liked electrolytic capacitors and modern ceramic-based designs need Type III or current mode.

## Current mode: the first-order plant

In peak-current mode the inner loop makes the inductor current follow the control voltage $v_c$, $i_L \approx v_c/R_i$. The remaining plant is a current source into $C \parallel R$:
$$G_{vc}(s) \approx \frac{R}{R_i}\,\frac{1 + s r_C C}{1 + s R C}$$
Only one low-frequency pole at $f_p = 1/(2\pi RC)$: 2.6 kHz at 3 A load, and 263 Hz at 0.3 A for 55 µF. The pole moves with load, but its *phase* contribution is at most $-90^\circ$, and combined with Type II compensation gives straightforward phase margin. Crossover scales as $f_c \propto g_m/C$ for a given error-amp transconductance, so changing C moves the crossover, which is what the "recommended C range" protects against.

At the top of the range, the modulator has a sampling double pole at $f_{SW}/2$ with
$$Q_p = \frac{1}{\pi\,(m_c D' - 0.5)}, \quad m_c = 1 + \frac{S_e}{S_n},\ D'=1-D$$
Slope compensation lowers $Q_p$ (here $S_n$ is the on-time slope of the sensed current), which damps the peaking at half the switching frequency. With $m_c = 1$ at $D = 0.275$: $Q_p = 1/[\pi(0.725-0.5)] = 1.41$; at $D = 0.6$ with $m_c = 1$ the bracket is negative (unstable) so compensation is needed ([[q0254]], [[q0255]]).

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Capacitor tolerance and bias.** Ceramic capacitance can be half the nominal and varies ±20% with tolerance and temperature; the loop must be stable at both extremes.
- **Load dependence.** Check at minimum and maximum load, and at DCM/CCM boundaries where the plant changes ([[q0229]]). In DCM, the double pole disappears (the inductor current is zero at the start of each cycle) and the plant approaches first-order.
- **Inductor saturation.** Reduced L at high current shifts $f_0$ upward and changes the slew ([[q0259]]).
- **Feed-forward capacitor.** A small capacitor across the upper feedback resistor adds a zero-pole pair that boosts phase around crossover ([[q0250]]).
- **Measuring.** Inject a small signal in series with the feedback divider (a 10 to 50 Ω resistor in the divider's top leg), measure the loop gain with a network analyzer, and confirm phase margin and gain margin at several loads and input voltages ([[q0267]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The simplified $G_{vd}$ uses the standard averaged model with lumped $r_L$; real $Q$ and the shape near $f_0$ also depend on $R_{DS(on)}$ vs duty cycle, inductor core losses and the load type (resistive vs constant-power). Treat the numbers as illustrations and always measure.
:::

::: applications
- **Internally compensated bucks**: datasheet tables of allowed L and effective $C_{OUT}$ keep the plant inside the fixed compensation's window.
- **Voltage-mode controllers for CPU rails**: Type III compensation with ceramic capacitors and careful placement of the zeros around $f_0$.
- **Electrolytic or polymer-based power supplies**: use ESR zero deliberately for stability.
- **Load-point converters with large load changes**: designed so the worst-case $Q$ (light load) still has 45° or more phase margin.
- **Production test**: bode measurements across corners to certify stability margin.
:::

::: assumptions
- Small-signal averaged CCM model; DCM and PFM are different plants ([[q0229]]).
- The lumped $r_L$ includes DCR and switch resistance; ignoring ESR in $Q$ overestimates the peaking.
- Voltage-mode formulas are for a resistive load; a regulated constant-power load adds a negative-resistance term that reduces damping.
- Delay and sampling cost phase proportional to crossover frequency; the numbers shown are approximations.
- "Stable" here means a measured phase margin > 45° and gain margin > 10 dB (conventional guidance, not a law); some applications demand more.
:::

::: followups
### Why is light load the worst case for stability in voltage mode?
The load resistance $R$ damps the LC resonance: $Q \approx 1/[\sqrt{L/C}/R + (r_L+r_C)/\sqrt{L/C}]$. As the load lightens, $R$ grows and the first term shrinks toward zero, leaving only series resistances. In the example $Q$ rises from 2.1 at 3 A to 5.5 at 0.3 A, so the phase drops faster and the resonance peak is taller, eating phase margin near the crossover.

### What does the ESR zero do for the loop, and why do ceramics change things?
It adds a zero at $1/(2\pi\,ESR\,C)$, tilting gain from $-40$ to $-20$ dB/dec and returning up to 90° of phase. With an electrolytic ESR of 100 mΩ and 55 µF, the zero is at 29 kHz: near crossover, giving lead. With ceramics, ESR is 3 mΩ, so the zero is at about 1 MHz, beyond $f_{SW}$ and useless; the compensator has to provide that lead, as a Type III.

### Why does current-mode control make L and C less critical?
The inner loop regulates inductor current, replacing the inductor and its pole with a controlled current source. The plant is just that source charging $C\parallel R$, one pole at $1/(2\pi RC)$. Compensation can be a simple Type II, and the converter tolerates a broad range of L. C still sets the crossover ($f_c\propto1/C$) so it needs a window, and slope compensation must match L.

### Scenario: your buck oscillates after you replaced a 22 µF ceramic with a 10 µF one in the same footprint. Why?
Effective capacitance fell (and possibly derated further at bias), so the current-mode crossover rose and the plant's pole moved up; with less C the compensation's gain is now too high, crossover approaches the sampling pole at $f_{SW}/2$ and phase margin collapses. Verify with a loop measurement; fix by restoring C, lowering compensator gain (smaller $R_c$ or gm) or lowering the crossover.

### How would you stabilise an unstable voltage-mode buck with all-ceramic output without changing parts?
Retune the compensator: place the two zeros near $f_0$ to cancel the double pole (e.g. 0.75 $f_0$ to $f_0$) and the two poles near the ESR-zero frequency or $f_{SW}/2$, lower the mid-band gain to put crossover around $f_{SW}/10$, and add a feed-forward capacitor across the upper feedback resistor to bring phase near crossover. Then measure loop gain at light and heavy load.
:::
