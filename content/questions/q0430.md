::: testing
Whether you understand that a MOSFET gate is a **high-impedance capacitor**, not a "pin that is 0 when you aren't driving it". The interviewer wants:

1. The failure scenario: the MCU is in reset, booting, or has a pin set as an input, so the gate floats and the FET can partially turn on.
2. The mechanism: leakage, coupling and stored charge move a floating node; the FET sees $V_{GS}$ near threshold and conducts in its linear region.
3. The consequence for an inductive power stage: a motor or solenoid fires at power-up, or the FET runs hot in a half-on state.
4. The trade-offs in choosing the value: pull-down speed ($R\,C_{iss}$), static current, and what it can and cannot do about dv/dt coupling.
:::

::: why
The floating-gate bug is one of the classic embedded-hardware surprises: the board works on the bench with the debugger attached, then a motor twitches or a heater runs when the product is powered without a program, or after a watchdog reset. A pulldown costs a fraction of a cent and prevents it. It is also a gentle way to test whether you understand impedance: a "high-Z" pin is not "off", it is "not driven", and the node moves wherever its leakage and coupling push it. Related: pull-ups and pull-downs on logic lines ([[q0020]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **MOSFET gate as capacitor**: $C_{iss}$ stores charge; DC input resistance is giga-ohms ([[q0096]], [[q0090]]).
- **High-impedance (tri-state) pin**: an MCU pin in reset or input mode does not drive the net ([[q0020]]).
- **Threshold voltage**: the FET starts to conduct at $V_{GS}\approx V_{th}$, and the transition is gradual ([[q0099]]).
- **Leakage and coupling**: gate leakage (nA), board leakage and capacitive pickup move a floating node.
- **Linear-region dissipation**: a half-on FET drops voltage while carrying current, so $P=V_{DS}I$ can be large.
- **RC discharge**: pull-down speed is $R\,C_{iss}$ ([[q0429]]).
:::

::: answer
"A MOSFET gate is basically a capacitor with an extremely high DC resistance. If nothing is driving it, it just sits wherever leakage, noise or stored charge put it. And there are plenty of times when nothing is driving it: the microcontroller is in reset, it's booting, the pin is configured as an input, or it's powered down with the supply on the load still present.

If that floating gate drifts up toward the threshold voltage, the FET starts to conduct partway. With a motor or solenoid on the other side, that means it can twitch or fire at power-up. Worse, a half-on FET drops a good part of the supply voltage while carrying current, so it can get very hot very quickly. With 12 volts and a 24 ohm load, if the FET lets 0.2 amps through, it's dropping over 7 volts, about 1.4 watts, in a small package.

A pulldown resistor from gate to source gives the gate a defined path to ground, so whenever the pin isn't driving, the gate discharges to zero and the FET stays off. I'd use something between 10 and 100 kilohms. Small enough to beat leakage and noise and discharge the gate in a reasonable time, large enough that the GPIO can still drive the gate high without wasting current: 3.3 volts across 100 k is only 33 microamps."
:::

::: technical
**Gate as an open-circuit node.** $I_{GSS}$ (gate leakage) is nA to tens of nA at rated $V_{GS}$; PCB surface leakage and flux can add nA to µA. A node with $C_{iss}=1$ nF charged by 100 nA drifts at $dV/dt=I/C=0.1$ V/ms. Capacitive coupling from adjacent traces or from the drain through $C_{gd}$ adds more.

**Behaviour at reset.** After power-up many MCUs leave pins as high-impedance inputs, some with weak default pull-ups (often tens of kΩ; device-specific). A weak pull-up to 3.3 V on a FET gate *turns the FET on*. A pull-down must be strong enough to beat it: a 10 kΩ pull-down against a 40 kΩ internal pull-up gives $3.3\times10/50=0.66$ V, below threshold.

**Half-on dissipation.** For a 12 V, 24 Ω coil in series with the FET and a FET current of $I$, $V_{DS}=12-24I$ and $P=I(12-24I)$, with a maximum of 1.5 W at $I=0.25$ A, where $V_{DS}=6$ V. A SOT-23 FET (about 0.3 to 0.5 W capability on typical boards) fails quickly. *Illustrative; depends on part and layout.*

**Discharge speed.** $\tau=R_{pd}C_{iss}$: for $C_{iss}=1$ nF, $R_{pd}=100$ kΩ gives 100 µs; $10$ kΩ gives 10 µs. In normal operation the pin drives the gate through the series resistor and the pull-down also loads the pin: $I=V_{OH}/R_{pd}$ ($33$ µA at 100 kΩ, 330 µA at 10 kΩ). The gate voltage is reduced by the divider $R_{pd}/(R_{pd}+R_g+R_{out})$ (a negligible 0.2% error for 100 kΩ vs 220 Ω).

**Choosing the value.** 10 to 100 kΩ typical for general loads; 1 to 10 kΩ where the gate may be disturbed by dv/dt coupling (bridge legs), at the cost of static current. For dv/dt immunity, a larger $R_{pd}$ cannot help: the induced voltage is set by $R\,C_{gd}\,dv/dt$ or the capacitive divider limit ([[q0207]]).

**PFET equivalent.** A high-side PFET needs a **pull-up** from gate to source (to the supply) to hold it off. Same idea, mirrored ([[q0438]]).
:::

::: short
"A MOSFET gate is a capacitor with a huge DC resistance, so if the driving pin is in reset, booting or set as an input, the gate floats and drifts with leakage and noise, and can drift above threshold. Then the FET half-turns on and either fires the load at power-up or sits in its linear region burning power. A 10 to 100 kΩ pulldown from gate to source gives the gate a defined path to ground so it defaults off, and costs only microamps when the pin drives it high."
:::

::: long
"The gate of a MOSFET is insulated, so electrically it's a capacitor with a DC resistance of gigaohms or more. That means if nothing drives it, it doesn't go to zero, it stays at whatever voltage it last had, and then it drifts: gate leakage of tens of nanoamps, surface leakage on the board, flux, humidity, and capacitive coupling from neighbouring traces or from the drain.

When is nothing driving it? More often than you'd like. When the microcontroller powers up, its pins are high impedance until the firmware configures them. During reset, or in a bootloader, or when the pin is set to input, it's the same. Some MCUs even have weak internal pull-ups on by default, which would turn an N-channel FET *on*.

If the gate floats up to somewhere around the threshold, the FET conducts partially. In a motor or solenoid circuit that can be a twitch, a click or a full on-state at power-up, which can be a safety problem. Even if the load doesn't move, a FET that's partially on drops a lot of voltage with real current through it. With a 12 volt supply and a 24 ohm load the worst case is about 1.5 watts in the FET, far more than a small package can take.

The fix is a pulldown from gate to source, typically 10 to 100 kilohms. It's tiny compared with the gate's impedance, so any leakage current creates only a few millivolts instead of volts, and it discharges the gate in R times C, 10 to 100 microseconds for a 1 nanofarad gate. It costs 33 microamps when the pin drives the gate high with 100 k at 3.3 volts. I'd choose the value based on how noisy the environment is and how fast the gate has to discharge, and it doesn't replace dead time or a stiff gate driver in a bridge, because pulldowns that big won't stop a dv/dt-induced turn-on."
:::

::: terms
- **Pulldown resistor**: resistor from gate (or pin) to ground that defines the default low state.
- **High-impedance (Hi-Z) pin**: an output that is neither driving high nor low.
- **Floating node**: a node with no DC path; its voltage is set by leakage and coupling.
- **Gate leakage ($I_{GSS}$)**: DC current through the gate oxide, nA scale.
- **Threshold voltage ($V_{th}$)**: gate-source voltage where conduction starts.
- **Linear (triode) region**: the FET behaves as a variable resistor and dissipates $V_{DS}I$.
- **Default state**: the state of the load when the controller is not driving it.
- **Pull-up**: the complementary resistor to a supply, used with PFETs and open-drain lines.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "so the gate doesn't float".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names **when** it floats: reset, boot, input mode, power sequencing, and MCU default pulls.
- Explains the mechanism (leakage, coupling, charge storage) and quantifies a drift rate.
- Shows the consequence with numbers: partial turn-on and 1.5 W in a half-on FET.
- Gives the value trade-off: 10 to 100 kΩ, $R C_{iss}$ discharge, static current $V/R$.
- States the limits: it doesn't fix dv/dt-induced turn-on, and a PFET needs a pull-up instead.
- Adds belt-and-braces: fail-safe defaults in the driver, enable pins, or hardware interlocks.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The MOSFET is off when the gate is 0, so a floating gate is 0."** A floating gate is undefined, not zero.
- **Too large a pulldown (1 MΩ+).** Leakage and noise can still move the gate; discharge is slow ($1$ MΩ $\times$ 1 nF = 1 ms).
- **Too small (100 Ω).** Wastes drive current and reduces the available gate voltage.
- **Putting the pulldown on the wrong side of the series gate resistor.** It should go at the gate side or at the pin; both work, but placing it at the gate guarantees the FET sees it even if the series resistor opens.
- **Believing a pulldown solves Miller turn-on.** It limits the duration but not the peak induced voltage; the divider and dv/dt set that.
:::

A MOSFET gate input impedance is huge but not infinite: oxide leakage is typically nA, and PCB contamination or flux residue can add much more. Consider a 1 nF gate and 100 nA of leakage current into it with no resistor: the gate voltage slews at $I/C=100$ V/s. In 20 ms it moves 2 V, right into the threshold region. In practice leakage can be positive or negative and rarely this orderly, but it makes the point: a floating gate has no stable value.

## Where the gate actually goes at reset

| MCU state | Pin | Gate without $R_{pd}$ | Gate with $R_{pd}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Supply off | 0 V or Hi-Z, ESD diodes | Floating | 0 V |
| Reset asserted | Hi-Z | Floating | 0 V |
| Bootloader, pin default input | Hi-Z (some have weak pull-up) | Floating or pulled high | 0 V (if $R_{pd}$ beats the pull-up) |
| Firmware sets output low | Driven low | 0 V | 0 V |
| Firmware sets output high | Driven high | $V_{OH}$ | $V_{OH}\times R_{pd}/(R_{pd}+R_g+R_{out})$ |

If the MCU has a weak internal pull-up of, say, 40 kΩ enabled by default (device-dependent; check the datasheet), a 100 kΩ pull-down gives $3.3\times100/140=2.4$ V on the gate: enough to turn the FET on. Choose $R_{pd}\ll R_{pullup}$, for example 10 kΩ, which gives $3.3\times10/50=0.66$ V.

## What a half-on FET does

A FET with $V_{GS}$ just above threshold conducts in saturation with a large $V_{DS}$. With a 12 V, 24 Ω load: $I=(12-V_{DS})/24$, and $P=V_{DS}I$.

## Choosing the value

Three effects set $R_{pd}$:

1. **Defeat leakage and pull-ups**: $R_{pd}\ll V_{th}/I_{leak}$ and $\ll$ any MCU default pull-up. 10 to 100 kΩ satisfies this.
2. **Gate discharge time**: $\tau=R_{pd}C_{iss}$, with $C_{iss}=1$ nF: 100 kΩ gives 100 µs, 10 kΩ gives 10 µs, 1 kΩ gives 1 µs.
3. **Static load on the pin**: $I=V_{OH}/R_{pd}$ when the pin is high: 33 µA, 330 µA or 3.3 mA. And the divider: with $R_g=220$ Ω and $R_{out}=25$ Ω the gate reaches 99.8% of $V_{OH}$ at 100 kΩ.

## What a pulldown does *not* do

- **Dv/dt-induced turn-on** in a bridge: the induced voltage is $\Delta V\approx R\,C_{gd}\,dv/dt$ up to the capacitive-divider limit $V\,C_{gd}/(C_{gd}+C_{gs})$ ([[q0207]]). A large pull-down does not change the limit and a small one only reduces the duration. Use a stiff driver and negative bias for that.
- **Overvoltage on the gate**: use a Zener or TVS from gate to source in harsh environments ([[q0102]]).
- **Protect against a driver stuck high**: add a hardware enable or an interlock if safety requires it.

## The PFET mirror image

A high-side PFET needs a **pull-up** from gate to source (the supply). With the gate floating, it can be partially on just as well. The default state of the load, off, drives the choice: NFET low-side gets a pull-down, PFET high-side gets a pull-up, and both get a series gate resistor ([[q0431]], [[q0438]]).

## Fail-safe thinking

The pulldown sets a **default off** state. Ask for each load: what do I want when the processor is dead? For a heater or motor it's off; for a fail-safe valve (a solenoid that must close on failure) off may mean "de-energized" and the mechanical design handles it; for a cooling fan it may be *on*, in which case you'd use a pull-up (or a PFET) so the load defaults to running. The resistor placement encodes that policy.
:::

::: applications
- **Motor, solenoid and relay drivers** on microcontroller boards: default-off at reset.
- **Load switches and power-path FETs** controlled by an I/O expander that powers up Hi-Z.
- **Gate drivers for bridges**: pull-downs on each gate (or driver output stages with low off-impedance) to hold gates off.
- **Hot-swap and enable circuits**: a pulldown on the enable pin defines "off" before the controller is ready.
- **LED drivers and heaters**: prevent a flash or heat at power-up.
- **Automotive modules**: default-off outputs while the MCU is held in reset by a watchdog.
:::

::: assumptions
- Low-side NFET switched by an MCU GPIO that may be Hi-Z during reset or boot.
- $I_{GSS}$ in the nA range; board leakage can be larger in contaminated or humid conditions.
- MCU pin defaults vary: some pins boot Hi-Z, some with weak pull-ups, some driven low. Check the datasheet's reset-state table.
- Gate capacitance about 1 nF for the discharge calculation; scale $\tau=RC_{iss}$ for your part.
- PWM frequency low compared with $1/(R_{pd}C_{iss})$.

For a **gate driver IC** with an internal active pull-down (UVLO or low-impedance off state), an external resistor is still good practice because it holds the gate even if the driver is unpowered or the connection is open.
:::

::: followups
### Why not just rely on the MCU to drive the pin low at startup?
Because there is a period before the firmware runs, and during reset or a brown-out, when the pin is Hi-Z or in an arbitrary state. Hardware must set the default; firmware can only set it after it is running.

### What's the downside of a 1 kΩ pulldown?
Static current when the pin is high: 3.3 mA from 3.3 V, which can exceed the pin's budget when many pins do the same, and it forms a divider with the gate series resistor: with $R_g=220$ Ω, $R_{out}=25$ Ω the gate only gets $1000/1245=80\%$ of the pin voltage. In exchange it discharges the gate faster (1 µs) and resists noise.

### What if the MCU has a weak internal pull-up that turns on at reset?
Choose $R_{pd}$ much smaller than the pull-up resistance so the divider output stays below threshold. For a 40 kΩ pull-up and $V_{th,min}=1$ V, $R_{pd}\le V_{th}R_{pu}/(V_{DD}-V_{th})=1\times40/2.3=17$ kΩ, so use 10 kΩ. Also check whether the pin is 5 V tolerant and its default state.

### How do you test that the default-off behaviour works?
Hold the MCU in reset (or remove the program) and power the board up with the load connected; measure the drain and gate voltages. Also pulse the supply slowly, with a ramp slower than 1 V/ms, and briefly brown out the MCU with the load supply still on.

### Does an N-channel high-side switch with a bootstrap driver need a pulldown?
Yes: a gate-source resistor holds the FET off if the bootstrap capacitor is empty or the driver has no power, so the gate doesn't float up with the switch node's dv/dt ([[q0427]]).
:::
