::: basics
### In plain words
An edge can be too slow or too fast, and each fails in its own way. A slow edge lingers near the receiver's switching point, so noise can shift or even repeat the switching moment. A fast edge causes ringing, crosstalk, ground bounce and radio interference. The goal is the slowest edge that still meets your timing.

### Picture it
Think of a door. A person who hovers in the doorway is neither in nor out. One small nudge decides which, so you cannot tell exactly when they crossed. That is a slow edge near the threshold. Slamming the door shut settles the question instantly, but it rattles the whole house. That is a fast edge. A door closed at a firm, steady pace avoids both problems.

### Words you need
- **Rise time**: how long an edge takes to go from 10% to 90% of its swing.
- **Threshold**: the voltage at which the receiver switches, usually about halfway.
- **Slew rate**: how fast the voltage changes, in volts per ns. The edge's slope.
- **Jitter**: edges arriving earlier or later than they should ([[q0695]]).
- **Ringing**: wobbles after an edge, like a struck bell still humming ([[q0696]]).

### The simplest example
A 3.3 V signal has a 1 ns rise time. Its slope is about 2.64 V/ns (80% of 3.3 V in 1 ns). Add 20 mV of noise right at the threshold. The edge crossing moves by 20 mV / 2.64 V/ns, which is about 7.6 ps.

Slow the edge to 10 ns. The slope falls to 0.264 V/ns, and the same 20 mV of noise moves the crossing by about 76 ps, ten times more. That is the cost of a slow edge.

That is the whole idea: noise turns into timing error faster when the slope is shallow. The rest of this page covers the other side, why fast edges make long traces misbehave, and how to choose the edge rate.
:::

::: testing
Whether you see edge rate as a **trade-off with two failure modes**, not "faster is better". Too slow hurts timing margin and noise immunity: the signal lingers near the threshold, the setup budget shrinks, noise turns into jitter, and CMOS inputs can chatter or burn shoot-through current. Too fast hurts signal integrity and EMI: reflections, ringing, crosstalk, ground bounce and radiated emissions all scale with $dV/dt$ or $dI/dt$. The interviewer wants the cause and a number or rule of thumb for each side, and the practical knob (slew control, series resistor, termination).
:::

::: why
Edge rate is one of the few parameters you choose directly. On a real board you tune it with a drive-strength setting or a 33 Ω series resistor and the whole noise picture shifts. Too slow shows up as marginal timing and I²C-style bus failures ([[q0759]]); too fast shows up as ringing, EMC test failures and neighbouring signals glitching. Understanding both sides is what lets you pick the *slowest edge that still meets timing*, which is the rule layout and EMC engineers actually follow.
:::

::: concepts
- **Rise time and bandwidth**: $BW \approx 0.35/t_r$; faster edges contain higher frequencies. See [[q0132]].
- **Electrically long lines**: when the trace delay is a significant fraction of $t_r$, reflections matter. See [[q0711]].
- **Slew rate and noise-to-jitter conversion**: $\sigma_t = \sigma_v/SR$. See [[q0695]].
- **Setup/hold budget**: a slow edge delays the threshold crossing and tightens setup. See [[q0693]].
- **Coupling scales with edge rate**: crosstalk ($\propto dV/dt$, [[q0694]]) and ground bounce ($\propto dI/dt$, [[q0688]]).
- **Ringing**: [[q0696]].
:::

::: answer
"Both extremes cause trouble, so I try to use the slowest edge that still meets timing.

If the edge is too slow, the signal spends a long time near the receiver's threshold. Any noise on the line moves the crossing time by noise divided by slew rate, so slow edges mean more jitter and, with enough noise, multiple crossings and glitches. It also eats the setup budget because the crossing happens later. On CMOS inputs it makes both transistors conduct while the input is mid-supply, so you get extra shoot-through current and sometimes oscillation on the output. I've seen that on slow RC-filtered inputs without a Schmitt trigger.

If the edge is too fast, the line starts acting like a transmission line, so any impedance mismatch makes reflections and ringing. The fast edge also couples into neighbours as crosstalk, makes large current spikes that cause ground bounce, and puts more energy at high frequency, which means EMI. The usual fix is a series resistor, slew-rate control or termination."
:::

::: technical
**Too slow.**
- *Noise-to-jitter conversion*: $\sigma_t = \sigma_v/SR$. A 3.3 V edge with 1 ns 10–90% rise has $SR = 2.64$ V/ns; 20 mV rms noise gives 7.6 ps rms. At 10 ns the same noise gives 76 ps rms.
- *Setup budget*: a slow edge crosses the threshold later. For an edge ramping 0 to $V_{DD}$ over $t_{ramp}$ and a threshold at $V_{DD}/2$, the arrival is delayed by $t_{ramp}/2$ relative to an ideal step.
- *Multiple crossings*: noise larger than $SR \cdot \Delta t$ near the threshold produces chatter. A Schmitt-trigger input ([[q0692]]) or hysteresis cures it.
- *Shoot-through (crowbar) current*: CMOS inputs drawing current while both devices conduct; the time in the transition region scales with edge time. Datasheets specify a maximum input transition time for this reason (the value varies by family; check the part).
- *Bus performance*: I²C rise time too slow means the bus can't meet the spec and data setup fails ([[q0758]], [[q0759]]).

**Too fast.**
- *Reflections/ringing*: when the one-way delay $t_d$ is more than roughly $t_r/6$ (rules of thumb from $t_r/3$ to $t_r/10$ are quoted), the line must be treated as a transmission line. With FR4 microstrip at about 170 ps/inch, $t_r = 1$ ns gives about 1 inch; $t_r = 100$ ps gives about 0.1 inch.
- *Crosstalk*: coupled voltage scales with $dV/dt$ for the capacitive part and $dI/dt$ for the inductive part ([[q0694]]).
- *Ground bounce / supply noise*: $V = N L\,di/dt$ ([[q0688]]).
- *EMI*: the spectrum envelope of a trapezoid falls at 20 dB/decade beyond $1/(\pi\tau)$ and 40 dB/decade beyond $1/(\pi t_r)$, where $\tau$ is the pulse width; a faster edge pushes the second corner up and raises high-frequency emissions.
- *Overshoot / clamp stress*: unterminated fast edges can exceed absolute-maximum ratings.

**Choosing.** Required rise time is set by timing budget ($t_r \lesssim$ 10–20% of the period for a clock is a common target). Everything faster only adds noise.
:::

::: short
"Too slow, and the edge sits near the threshold: noise turns into timing jitter or even multiple crossings, setup margin shrinks, and CMOS inputs draw shoot-through current. Too fast, and the line behaves like a transmission line so you get reflections and ringing, more crosstalk, ground bounce from the current spikes, and more EMI. So I pick the slowest edge that still meets timing, using drive strength, a series resistor or termination."
:::

::: long
"Edge rate is a trade-off, so I think about each end.

A slow edge has three problems. First, noise sensitivity. The time the signal crosses the threshold moves by noise divided by slew rate, so the same 20 millivolts of noise gives you about 8 picoseconds of jitter on a one-nanosecond edge, but 76 picoseconds on a ten-nanosecond edge. Second, it eats timing margin, because the threshold crossing is late and the signal spends longer in the undefined region, so setup margin shrinks. And if noise is larger than the slope over that window you get multiple crossings, which is why you'd want a Schmitt trigger on slow inputs. Third, standard CMOS inputs conduct both transistors while the input is mid-supply, so a slow edge means extra supply current and sometimes oscillation.

A fast edge has the opposite problem. Once the trace is electrically long compared with the edge, say longer than about a sixth of the rise time in delay, it's a transmission line, and any impedance mismatch reflects and rings. Fast edges also couple into neighbouring traces as crosstalk, and the current spikes produce ground bounce and supply noise. The spectrum extends higher, so EMI gets worse and you might fail radiated emissions.

So in practice I'd choose the slowest edge that meets timing. I'd set the driver's slew or drive strength, add a series resistor near the driver, and terminate if the line is long, then check the receiver waveform on a scope."
:::

::: terms
- **Slew rate ($SR$)**: $dV/dt$ of the edge; in V/ns.
- **Rise time ($t_r$)**: time to go between two percentage points of the swing; 10–90% is typical.
- **Electrically long line**: a trace whose delay is a meaningful fraction of the rise time, so reflections cannot be ignored.
- **Shoot-through (crowbar) current**: supply current while a CMOS gate has both transistors partly on.
- **Slew-rate control**: driver setting that limits $dV/dt$.
- **Knee frequency**: roughly $0.5/t_r$, the frequency above which an edge's spectrum drops quickly.
- **Noise-to-jitter conversion**: amplitude noise at the crossing turning into time uncertainty, $\sigma_t = \sigma_v/SR$.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "slow edges are bad for timing, fast edges cause ringing and EMI". An exceptional answer gives the **mechanisms and numbers**: noise-to-jitter $\sigma_t = \sigma_v/SR$ with a worked example, the electrically-long rule of thumb ($t_d > t_r/6$, giving about 1 inch at 1 ns on FR4), and the scaling of crosstalk and ground bounce with $dV/dt$ and $dI/dt$. It names the *tools*: series termination, slew control, Schmitt inputs. It also frames the goal ("slowest edge that meets timing") and mentions that slow edges can *also* be a problem because a slow, clean-looking signal into a noisy environment can still glitch or chatter.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Faster edges are always better."** They reduce jitter conversion but cause reflections, crosstalk, ground bounce and EMI. Pick what the timing budget needs.
- **"Slow edges are always safe."** A slow edge into an input without hysteresis can produce multiple transitions and shoot-through current; and a slow clock edge adds jitter and kills setup margin.
- **Quoting rise time without saying 10–90% or 20–80%.** The definition changes the numbers by around 1.6x.
- **Ignoring that the driver's *output* edge differs from the *receiver's* edge.** Loading and RC filtering slow it, reflections reshape it.
- **Using the clock frequency to judge edge rate.** A 1 MHz clock with a 500 ps edge still produces GHz content and needs controlled impedance.
- **Thinking only the driver chooses edge rate.** Layout capacitance, series resistance and cables all slow edges, and probing adds its own limit.
:::

::: deep
## Two failure modes of one parameter

An edge is characterised by its rise time $t_r$ (or slew rate $SR = \Delta V / t_r$). Everything about it that matters falls into one of two buckets:

- things that get worse as $t_r$ **grows** (timing margin, noise immunity, supply current), and
- things that get worse as $t_r$ **shrinks** (reflections, coupling, EMI).

The design goal is to sit between the two with margin on both sides.

## Slow edges: noise becomes jitter and chatter

Near the threshold, a small voltage error $\Delta v$ equals a time error $\Delta t = \Delta v / SR$:

$$\sigma_t = \frac{\sigma_v}{SR}$$

With $SR = 0.8\cdot 3.3/1\text{ ns} = 2.64$ V/ns (1 ns, 10–90%), 20 mV rms noise gives 7.6 ps. With a 10 ns edge: 76 ps. A 100 MHz clock has a 10 ns period, so 76 ps is only 0.76% of a period, which sounds small, but sets the budget for high-speed capture ([[q0693]]).

**Chatter.** If noise peak-to-peak is larger than the voltage the edge moves in a noise period, the signal can cross the threshold more than once. A Schmitt trigger ([[q0692]]) adds a window so a single decision is made. A slow edge into a plain CMOS input also keeps both transistors conducting, giving supply current spikes on every transition. For slow sensor or button inputs the right fix is a Schmitt input, not a faster edge.

**Setup budget.** A linear edge crossing at 50% is $t_{ramp}/2$ late relative to an instantaneous edge. With $t_{ramp} = 1.25$ ns (for a 1 ns 10–90% edge) that's about 0.6 ns, which is large for a 100 MHz timing budget but included in standard timing analysis only if the datasheet gives you the right $t_{su}$ vs input-slew curve.

> [!NOTE]
> Datasheet setup and hold times are given for a specified input slew. A slower edge gives a *longer* effective setup time; many tools derate it for you, but handwritten budgets often forget.

## Fast edges: when does a trace become a transmission line?

The signal takes $t_d$ to travel the trace. If the edge is much slower than the round trip the line is "lumped" and reflections blur into the edge. Common rules of thumb call a line electrically long when

$$t_d > \frac{t_r}{6}\quad(\text{some references use } t_r/3 \text{ to } t_r/10)$$

With FR4 microstrip at about 170 ps/inch (a typical figure; it varies with the dielectric and geometry):

| $t_r$ | $t_r/6$ | Critical length |
|---|---|---|
| 10 ns | 1.67 ns | ≈ 9.8 in |
| 1 ns | 167 ps | ≈ 0.98 in |
| 100 ps | 16.7 ps | ≈ 0.098 in |

Past that length you need controlled impedance and termination. Without it, a driver with source resistance $R_s$ into an open line launches $V_{DD}Z_0/(R_s+Z_0)$ and reflections bounce between source and load with coefficients $\Gamma_s = (R_s - Z_0)/(R_s+Z_0)$ and $\Gamma_L$; a low-impedance driver ($\Gamma_s < 0$) into an open end rings, as the table of a 10 Ω driver on a 50 Ω line shows: receiver voltages of 5.5 V, 1.83 V, 4.28 V, 2.65 V and so on, settling to 3.3 V ([[q0696]]).

## Fast edges: coupling and EMI

- **Crosstalk**: capacitive coupling current is $C_m\,dV/dt$ and inductive coupling is $L_m\,dI/dt$; both scale with edge rate ([[q0694]]).
- **Ground bounce**: $V = N\,L\,di/dt$ ([[q0688]]); halving the edge time doubles the bounce at the same current.
- **EMI**: the amplitude spectrum of a trapezoidal pulse falls at 20 dB/decade above $f_1 = 1/(\pi\tau_w)$ and at 40 dB/decade above $f_2 = 1/(\pi t_r)$. A 1 ns edge puts the second corner at 318 MHz; a 100 ps edge puts it at 3.18 GHz, so emissions rise in the range where cables and slots radiate efficiently.

## Choosing the edge rate

1. Pick the timing requirement: edge rate as slow as the setup/hold budget and the maximum clock rate allow. A rule of thumb for clocks: $t_r \approx 10\%$–$20\%$ of the period.
2. Use the driver's slew or drive setting first, then a series resistor near the driver (typically 22–33 Ω, but size it to match $Z_0 - R_{drv}$).
3. For lines that are electrically long at that edge, terminate.
4. Verify at the receiver with a scope of sufficient bandwidth ([[q0687]]).

::: applications
- **Clock distribution**: series resistors at clock drivers keep the edge fast enough for timing but slow enough to avoid ringing and EMI.
- **I²C bus**: pull-up and bus capacitance set the rise time; too slow breaks the spec, the I²C pull-up sizing sim shows it ([[q0759]]).
- **SPI to a flash across a cable**: slow the SPI edges with drive-strength settings to reduce overshoot and radiated noise.
- **EMC fixes**: slew-rate-limiting or ferrite beads on fast GPIOs and LED PWM outputs to pass radiated emissions.
- **Mechanical switches and slow sensors**: feed them through Schmitt-trigger inputs because their edges are slow and noisy.
- **High-speed memory interfaces**: calibrated drive strength and on-die termination choose the edge rate for the channel.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Single-ended CMOS receiver** with mid-supply threshold; differential receivers cross at the common point of the two signals, where noise-to-jitter conversion differs ([[q0697]]).
- **Linear ramp edge** for the jitter calculation. Real edges are curved, and the slope at the *threshold crossing* is what matters.
- **FR4 propagation delay of about 170 ps/inch** is typical but varies with stack-up. Critical length rules of thumb are approximate (some sources use $t_r/6$, others $t_r/4$ or $t_r/10$).
- **Datasheet slew and threshold limits** vary by family; this answer gives mechanisms, not guaranteed spec values.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Maximum allowed input transition times differ widely by logic family (from tens of ns/V for fast CMOS to hundreds of ns for older HC parts). Check the specific datasheet before relying on a slow edge into a non-Schmitt input.
:::

::: followups
### How would you quantify the maximum trace length that does not need termination?
Use the electrical-length rule. With $t_r = 1$ ns and 170 ps/in, $t_r/6 = 167$ ps, so about 1 inch. Some designers use a more conservative $t_r/10$ (about 0.6 inch) when overshoot margin is tight. For an actual design I'd simulate with the IBIS model because the driver's real edge and the receiver clamp matter.

### What happens if a slow, noisy signal goes into a standard CMOS inverter, and how do you fix it?
The input spends a long time near threshold, so noise causes multiple output transitions and both transistors conduct, drawing current. I'd use a Schmitt-trigger input, such as a 74HC14-type buffer, or add hysteresis with positive feedback ([[q0692]]).

### Why does making a clock edge faster raise EMI even if the clock frequency stays the same?
EMI depends on the spectrum, and the spectrum above $1/(\pi t_r)$ falls at 40 dB/decade. Cutting $t_r$ from 1 ns to 100 ps moves that corner from about 318 MHz to 3.18 GHz, so harmonics in the radiating range rise by up to 20 dB.

### A design passes timing in simulation but fails on hardware only at low temperature, with slower-looking edges on the scope. Could slow edges be involved?
Possibly. If drive strength or slew changes with temperature or supply, or capacitance on the net is higher than modelled, the edge reaches the threshold later and setup margin shrinks. I'd measure the actual edge at the receiver across temperature and compare against the capture flop's setup time at that slew.

### Where is the break-even if the same net must be both low-EMI and meet a tight setup?
Use a faster edge only where timing needs it: tune drive strength per pin, terminate properly so a fast edge doesn't ring, and move timing-critical signals to shorter routes so you need less edge rate. For EMC, spread-spectrum clocking or shielding can supplement slower edges.
:::
