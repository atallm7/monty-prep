::: basics
### In plain words
Serpentining a differential pair means adding a small zig-zag of extra length to the shorter trace so both traces arrive at the same time. You add it right where the difference appears, such as just after a bend. If the whole pair needs to be longer, you meander both traces together so they stay equal.

### Picture it
Imagine two people walking side by side around a curve. The person on the outside walks farther. To arrive together, the inside person takes a small detour right after the curve. If they waited until the end, they would have been out of step the whole way. And the detour must stay close, so they remain a pair.

### Words you need
- **Intra-pair skew**: the arrival-time difference between the two traces of one pair ([[q0520]]).
- **Jog**: a small zig-zag added to the shorter trace to give it extra length.
- **Pitch**: the center-to-center distance between the two traces, equal to width plus gap.
- **Coupled meander**: a serpentine in which the whole pair winds together, so both traces gain equal length.
- **Speed-up**: tight legs couple, so a meander adds less delay than its length suggests ([[q0517]]).

### The simplest example
Take 0.24 mm traces with a 0.2 mm gap, so the pitch is 0.44 mm. A 90 degree bend makes the outer trace longer by $0.44 \times \pi/2 \approx 0.69$ mm. At 5.9 ps per millimeter, that is about 4.1 ps of skew. A U-shaped jog of height $H$ adds about $2H$ of length, so $H \approx 0.345$ mm on the inner trace fixes it. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers where to place the jog, leg spacing rules, the impedance effect, and meandering a whole pair.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know that serpentining a **differential pair** is a different job from meandering a bus line ([[q0517]]): the goal is to cancel *intra-pair* skew to a few picoseconds, it must be done **where the asymmetry is created** (bends, breakouts, vias), and the way you do it must not break the coupling or the differential impedance. Good answers distinguish the small local jog on the shorter trace from a coupled meander of the whole pair, give geometry rules and reasons, and quantify the length needed.
:::

::: why
At multi-gigabit speeds a bend can introduce several ps of skew, and a misplaced or badly shaped meander can add reflections, crosstalk and mode conversion that undo the benefit. Layout reviewers look at tuning structures first because they betray whether the designer understands why skew matters ([[q0520]]). The question is a natural follow-up to "how should a pair be routed" ([[q0519]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Intra-pair skew** and its conversion into common-mode. See [[q0520]].
- **Length to delay**: $\Delta t=\Delta\ell\,t_{pd}$, with $t_{pd}\approx5.9$ ps/mm in microstrip and 6.9 ps/mm in stripline.
- **Bend asymmetry**: the outer trace of a bend is longer by about $(w+s)\theta$. See [[q0519]].
- **Coupling and differential impedance**: moving traces apart raises $Z_{diff}$; meander legs also couple to themselves. See [[q0524]].
- **Speed-up in tight meanders**: coupling between legs makes them look shorter than their length. See [[q0517]].
- **Compensate where it is created**: skew generates common-mode along the section where it exists.
:::

::: answer
"Serpentining a diff pair means adding a little extra length to the shorter trace so that the two halves arrive together. It's important because any skew within the pair turns some of the differential signal into common-mode, which hurts the eye and radiates. At 6 picoseconds per millimetre, a single 90 degree bend with typical spacing makes the outer trace about 0.7 millimetres longer, and that is already around 4 picoseconds, a few percent of a bit at 10 gigabit.

So the technique is: right after each bend, or wherever the traces diverge, I add a small jog or accordion on the shorter, inner trace to give it back that length. I keep it local because the common-mode noise is generated along the section where the lengths differ, so fixing it at the far end doesn't fix the middle. The jog is small with 45 degree or curved corners, and I keep the legs apart by at least three or four trace widths so they don't couple with each other, and I try not to pull the trace far from its partner, since that raises the differential impedance.

If the whole pair needs more length, to match another pair or a clock, I meander the pair together, as a coupled pair with symmetric bends, so both traces gain the same length instead of adding it to just one."
:::

::: technical
**Two different jobs.**
1. *Intra-pair skew fix* (P vs N): add length to the **shorter** trace of the pair, locally. Skew budget is typically a few ps or ≲ 5% of UI.
2. *Pair-to-pair (inter-pair) length match* (e.g. to a clock or another lane): lengthen **both** traces of the pair equally with a coupled meander, then trim the pair's intra-pair skew separately.

**Quantities.** $\Delta t=\Delta\ell\,t_{pd}$ with $t_{pd}=5.9$ ps/mm (microstrip). The imbalance of a bend of angle $\theta$ and pitch $s_c=w+s$ is $\Delta\ell\approx s_c\theta$ (outer longer). Example: $w=0.24$ mm, $s=0.2$ mm, 90° bend: $0.44\times\pi/2=0.69$ mm = 4.1 ps. To restore 0.69 mm, a U-shaped jog of height $H$ adds about $2H$, so $H\approx0.35$ mm. Alternating left and right bends cancel each other, so a route with balanced bends needs little compensation.

**Geometry rules (typical).**
- Add the jog **immediately after** the bend or discontinuity that caused the skew; keep the extension close to the partner trace so they remain coupled.
- Use 45° or curved corners; keep jog height small (a few trace widths) and the **spacing between legs ≥ 3–4 W** so the legs don't couple to each other (speed-up, crosstalk).
- Avoid opening the pair spacing locally: for $w=0.242$ mm, $h=0.2$ mm, $Z_{diff}$ rises from 100 Ω at $s=0.2$ mm to about 109 Ω at 0.3 mm and 114 Ω at 0.4 mm (IPC-style model).
- Don't place jogs near vias, pads or connectors; keep them on a solid reference plane.
- Use *several small jogs* rather than one large to keep the structure close to the pair; it also reduces its local effect on impedance.
- Verify with a simulation and, for high-speed links, with a TDR/mode-conversion measurement.
:::

::: long
"Serpentining a differential pair is adding a bit of zig-zag length to the shorter trace so the two halves have equal delay. The reason is that any intra-pair skew turns some of the differential signal into common-mode: the eye gets smaller, you get jitter, and the common-mode current radiates. At around 6 picoseconds per millimetre, small amounts of length matter. For example, a 90 degree bend in a pair with 0.24 millimetre traces and 0.2 millimetre spacing leaves the outer trace about 0.7 millimetres longer, around four picoseconds. At 10 gigabit per second that's 4% of a bit.

The most important thing is where I put the compensation. Skew generates common-mode wherever the lengths differ, so I add the length right after the bend or the breakout that caused the difference, not at the end. If I leave a long skewed section, I've left a long section producing common-mode.

How I draw it: a small jog or accordion on the inner, shorter trace, with 45 degree or rounded corners. Each U adds about twice its height, so for 0.7 millimetres I'd use a bump of about 0.35 millimetres. I keep the legs apart by at least three or four trace widths, otherwise they couple to each other, the delay is less than the length suggests and crosstalk goes up. I keep the jog close to the partner trace, because pulling a trace away raises the differential impedance: with this stackup, going from 0.2 to 0.4 millimetres of spacing takes the impedance from 100 to about 114 ohms. And I keep it off vias and over a solid plane.

If instead the whole pair needs to be longer, to match another pair or a clock, I meander the pair together with symmetric bends, so both traces gain equal length. Then I check the intra-pair skew separately."
:::

::: terms
- **Compensation jog / tuning bump**: a small local detour added to the shorter trace of a pair.
- **Accordion / trombone**: serpentine shapes with parallel legs; the legs of a trombone are open U-turns.
- **Pitch ($s_c$)**: centre-to-centre distance of the two pair traces, $w+s$.
- **Intra-pair skew**: arrival-time difference between the pair's traces.
- **Inter-pair skew**: arrival difference between different pairs (lanes or clock vs data).
- **Speed-up**: reduction in effective delay of tightly coupled meander legs.
- **Coupled meander**: a pair routed as a unit through a serpentine, keeping spacing constant.
- **Mode conversion**: conversion of differential to common-mode due to asymmetry.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "add length to the shorter trace." An exceptional answer:
- Separates **intra-pair** (shorter trace only, local) from **inter-pair** (whole pair, coupled meander).
- Says **where** (right after the asymmetry) and **why** (common-mode is generated along the skewed section).
- Quantifies **how much**: $(w+s)\theta$ per bend, ≈ 4 ps for a 90° bend; ps ↔ mm via 5.9 ps/mm.
- Gives geometry rules (45°/arc, leg spacing ≥ 3–4 W, small bumps, near the partner, over solid plane) and **explains** the effect on $Z_{diff}$ and coupling.
- Mentions **balanced bends cancelling**, fibre weave, and verification.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Adding the compensation at the far end.** The skewed section already generated common-mode; place the jog right after the bend.
- **Making a large detour with one trace.** Pulling one trace far away raises $Z_{diff}$ and breaks coupling. Use small jogs.
- **Tight accordions.** Legs closer than ~3 W couple and speed up, so the added delay is less than predicted.
- **Meandering one trace when the whole pair is too short.** That adds skew; lengthen both equally.
- **Compensating a via mismatch with a meander.** Different via parasitics can't be cancelled by length; use identical vias.
- **Putting jogs over plane gaps or next to vias/pads.** The return path and impedance suffer.
- **Counting length and ignoring that bends cancel** when alternating directions, leading to over-compensation.
:::

::: deep
## Where the skew comes from, and how much length fixes it

When a pair turns, the traces run on concentric arcs. For a bend angle $\theta$ and centre-to-centre pitch $s_c$, the outer trace is longer than the inner by $\Delta\ell=s_c\theta$. A pair with $w=0.24$ mm and $s=0.2$ mm has $s_c=0.44$ mm:

| Bend | Extra length (outer) | Skew (microstrip, 5.9 ps/mm) |
|---|---|---|
| 45° | 0.35 mm | 2.0 ps |
| 90° | 0.69 mm | 4.1 ps |
| 180° (U-turn) | 1.38 mm | 8.2 ps |

If a route has a left turn followed by a right turn, the outer trace swaps and the imbalances cancel. So bends in alternating directions need no compensation; successive bends in the same direction (a long curve or a "C" shape) accumulate.

## Why local compensation matters

Let the skewed section have length $L_s$ with a constant skew $\Delta t$. In that section the common-mode content produced is the same as for the whole link, per edge ([[q0520]]); it continues to travel down the pair. Moving the compensation later does not remove the content already generated; it only adds a later, opposite-phase generation. So the length-matching rule for pairs is: *compensate immediately after the cause, in the same neighbourhood*. The same logic applies to via transitions and connector breakouts.

## Meander geometry (legs, spacing, amplitude)

A meander has legs of height $H$ and a gap $G$ between neighbouring legs of the *same* trace. When $G$ is small the legs couple: energy crosses the gap and part of the signal takes a shortcut (speed-up), and the structure radiates and picks up crosstalk. Guidelines:

- $G\ge3$–$4W$ edge to edge; more for a clock or multi-Gb/s lane.
- $H\gtrsim 3W$ so that the legs act as proper lengthened paths (very short bumps add little length per corner).
- 45°/arc corners; no acute angles.
- Do not run legs parallel to the partner trace at a different spacing than the pair spacing for long distances.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Quantitative meander rules (3 W vs 4 W vs 5 W, minimum amplitude, maximum number of turns) vary between textbooks, vendor layout guides and the speed of the interface. Treat the numbers as starting points and follow the guide for your standard; for the highest speeds verify with a 3D/field-solver simulation.

## Pair-level meander (for inter-pair matching)

When a whole pair must be longer to match a clock or another lane, route it as a unit through the meander: constant spacing, symmetric bends. Because bends alternate left and right, the outer/inner roles swap and the first-order skew cancels, leaving a small residual. Then trim the residual with local jogs. The coupled meander legs should be separated from each other by ≥ 3–4 times the *pair pitch* in many guides (the legs are now pairs, not single traces).

## Worked example

A 10 Gb/s pair (UI = 100 ps) has a 90° bend and then two more 45° bends in the same direction. The total imbalance is $0.44\times(\pi/2+\pi/4+\pi/4)=0.44\times2.356=1.04$ mm → 6.1 ps. The budget is 5% of UI = 5 ps, so the pair is out of tolerance by 1.1 ps even before vias.

1. Required correction ≈ 1.04 mm. With $H=0.52$ mm per U, one jog adds 1.04 mm.
2. Better: split it into two small rounded jogs of 0.26 mm height (each adds about 0.52 mm): one right after the 90° bend and one after the following 45° bends. Each is a short bump next to the partner trace rather than a coupled meander, so the leg-gap rule is not what limits it.
3. Check the local spacing: keep the jog adjacent to the partner so spacing stays near 0.2 mm; otherwise adjust the spacing/width to hold 100 Ω.
4. Verify: compute residual skew ($<$ 1 ps), run a simulation, and plan to measure $S_{cd21}$ on the first article.

## Real hardware behaviour

- At ≥ 25 Gb/s, designers avoid jogs and bends altogether or shape them with EM simulation; the tuning bumps themselves are radiating structures.
- **Fibre weave** often dominates the skew budget. Zig-zag routing across the weave averages the effect, but it also adds length; account for it in the delay constraints.
- Constraint managers in modern EDA tools compute **delay-based** matching including vias, layers and package delays, making hand arithmetic only a check.
:::

::: applications
- **PCIe/USB/SATA/DisplayPort lanes**: post-bend jogs, via compensation, and clock vs data lane pair meanders.
- **DDR3/DDR4/LPDDR differential strobes and clocks**: matched pairs with local compensation.
- **JESD204/high-speed ADC/DAC lanes**: matched pairs with symmetrical breakout.
- **Gigabit/automotive Ethernet PHY to magnetics or connector**: balanced routing to pass emissions.
- **MIPI D-PHY/CSI lanes**: intra-pair skew control plus lane-to-lane matching.
:::

::: assumptions
- Numbers use **edge-coupled microstrip on FR-4** ($t_{pd}=5.9$ ps/mm) with a $Z_{diff}$ approximation (IPC-2141 style); stripline is 6.9 ps/mm.
- The skew budget (5% of UI) is a heuristic; each standard defines its own limit.
- Assumes the bends and vias are the only skew sources; fibre-weave and driver/connector skew are added separately.
- For **very low-speed** pairs (≤ 100 Mb/s), skew of 100s of ps is harmless and no tuning is needed.
:::

::: followups
### Which trace of the pair do you lengthen, and where?
The shorter one, which is the inner trace of a bend. I put the jog right after the bend (or at the breakout, near the source of the difference), not at the end of the pair, because common-mode is generated wherever the two lengths differ.

### Can you meander both traces?
Yes, as a coupled meander, when the whole pair needs more length (e.g. to match a clock or another lane). Both traces then gain the same length. It doesn't fix intra-pair skew; that is trimmed with a local jog afterward.

### What if there's no room near the bend for a jog?
Then reduce the cause: re-route to use fewer same-direction bends, alternate bend directions, or rearrange components. If a jog still has to go elsewhere, put it as close as possible, still on a solid plane, away from vias. A far-away fix leaves a skewed run, so I'd simulate to confirm the penalty is acceptable.

### How would you verify the compensation worked?
Check delays in the layout tool (including via and layer delays), then in a post-layout simulation look at the differential-to-common-mode conversion, and on hardware use a TDR/TDT to compare arrival times or a VNA to measure $S_{cd21}$. Eye diagram and common-mode measurement on the link confirm it.

### Should I meander a trace at multi-gigabit rates at all?
Preferably as little as possible. Each bump is a small discontinuity and a radiating structure; at ≥ 16 Gb/s, I'd first try placement and routing changes, use alternate bends, and use simulation to optimise the few tuning structures that remain. Where it's unavoidable, a rounded, small-amplitude tune beats a big accordion.
:::
