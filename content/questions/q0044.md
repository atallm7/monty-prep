::: testing
Whether you can predict an inductor's behaviour at the two extremes of a transient without solving a differential equation:

1. **Initially excited** (a step applied to a de-energized inductor): it acts like an **open circuit**, because its current is zero and can't jump. More generally, at $t = 0^+$ it's a **current source** equal to its previous current.
2. **DC steady state**: it acts like a **short circuit** (a wire, or its small DCR in a real part), because constant current means $v = L\,di/dt = 0$.
3. Can you combine the two with $\tau = L/R_{th}$ to sketch the full response, and do you know the real-world caveats (DCR, saturation, inrush)?
:::

::: why
These two shortcuts let you analyse most switching circuits in seconds: what a relay coil or motor does at turn-on, the inrush current through a power-entry choke, the startup of a buck converter, or what node voltages look like right after a switch flips. In debugging, "the inductor looks like an open at the edge and a short at DC" explains why a node spikes at power-up and then settles. It's the inductor half of a pair with the capacitor version ([[q0028]]), and interviewers expect you to give both instantly and explain why.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor law**: $v_L = L\,di_L/dt$ ([[q0041]]).
- **Continuity**: inductor current can't change instantly, $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$ ([[q0043]]).
- **DC steady state**: all derivatives are zero, so $v_L = 0$.
- **Thevenin resistance seen by the inductor**: sets the time constant $\tau = L/R_{th}$.
- **First-order response**: everything moves exponentially from the $0^+$ value to the final value, $x(t) = x_\infty + (x_0 - x_\infty)e^{-t/\tau}$.
- **Frequency-domain view**: a step's edge is high-frequency (inductor ≈ open), the flat top is DC (inductor ≈ short) ([[q0045]]).
:::

::: answer
"When an inductor is first excited, say I suddenly apply a voltage to one that has no current in it, it behaves like an open circuit. Its current is zero before the step, and since inductor current can't change instantly, it's still zero right after. So no current flows through it at that instant, and the full applied voltage appears across it.

At DC steady state it's the opposite: it acts like a short. Once the current stops changing, di/dt is zero, so $v = L\,di/dt$ is zero. An ideal inductor drops no voltage, so it's just a wire. In a real one you'd see its DC resistance, which is usually milliohms to ohms.

Between the two, the current rises exponentially with a time constant L over R, where R is the resistance the inductor sees. After about five time constants you're at steady state.

One refinement: 'open circuit at t = 0' is only exactly true if it started with zero current. If the inductor was already carrying current, then at the switching instant it acts like a current source holding that current. And it's the exact dual of the capacitor, which is a short when first charged and an open at DC."
:::

::: technical
**At the switching instant ($t = 0^+$).** Continuity gives $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$.
- De-energized ($i_L(0^-) = 0$): the inductor is an **open circuit**. All other currents in the circuit flow elsewhere; $v_L(0^+)$ is whatever KVL demands (often the full step voltage).
- Pre-energized ($i_L(0^-) = I_0$): the inductor is a **current source** of $I_0$, same direction.

**At DC steady state ($t \to \infty$).** $di_L/dt = 0 \Rightarrow v_L = 0$: an ideal inductor is a **short circuit**. A real inductor is its DCR, so $v_L = I\cdot R_{DC}$.

**In between.** With $R_{th}$ the Thevenin resistance seen by the inductor:
$$i_L(t) = i_L(\infty) + \left[i_L(0^+) - i_L(\infty)\right]e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R_{th}}$$
After $1\tau$: 63.2% of the change; $3\tau$: 95.0%; $5\tau$: 99.3%.

**Example.** $V_s = 10$ V, $R_1 = 100$ Ω in series to a node, with $L = 10$ mH and $R_2 = 100$ Ω both from that node to ground. At $0^+$ the inductor is open: $v_L = 10\cdot\frac{100}{200} = 5$ V, $i_{R1} = 50$ mA, $i_L = 0$. At $\infty$ the inductor is a short: $v_L = 0$, $i_L = 100$ mA, $i_{R2} = 0$. $R_{th} = R_1\parallel R_2 = 50$ Ω, $\tau = 200\ \mu$s.

**Frequency-domain consistency.** $Z_L = j\omega L$: zero at DC (short) and large at high frequency (open). A step's sharp edge is "all high frequency", its flat top is DC. The time-domain shortcuts are the same statement ([[q0045]]).

**Dual (capacitor):** short at $0^+$ if uncharged (voltage source if charged), open at DC steady state ([[q0028]]).

**Real-part caveats.** Winding capacitance means the very first nanoseconds of an edge see a capacitive spike current; DCR sets the steady-state current of an inductor directly across a supply ($V/R_{DC}$, often enormous); and if steady-state current exceeds $I_{sat}$ the inductance collapses on the way up ([[q0046]]).
:::

::: short
"When first excited from zero current, an inductor acts like an open circuit: its current can't jump, so it's still zero right after the step and the full voltage appears across it. At DC steady state it's a short, because constant current means $L\,di/dt = 0$; a real one shows just its DC resistance. In between, the current rises exponentially with $\tau = L/R$. If it was already carrying current, at the switch instant it acts as a current source. It's the dual of a capacitor."
:::

::: long
"There are two snapshots I'd describe: the instant the inductor is first excited, and DC steady state long afterward.

At the instant of excitation, the key is continuity. Inductor current can't change instantaneously, because that would need infinite voltage. So if the inductor had zero current before I applied the step, it still has zero current right after. Something carrying zero current regardless of the voltage across it is an open circuit. That's how I'd model it at t equals zero-plus: open, with the full available voltage across it.

If instead it was already carrying some current, it acts like a current source of that value at the switching instant. The open-circuit case is just the special case of a zero-amp current source.

At DC steady state, nothing is changing anymore, so di/dt is zero and the voltage $L\,di/dt$ is zero. An inductor with zero voltage across it and any current through it is a short circuit. In real life it's a short with a small resistance, the DCR, typically milliohms for a power inductor.

To connect the two, the current moves exponentially from its initial value to its final value with a time constant L over the Thevenin resistance seen by the inductor. As a quick example: 10 volts through 100 ohms into a node with a 10 millihenry inductor and another 100 ohm resistor to ground. At t equals zero-plus the inductor is open, so the node sits at 5 volts. At steady state it's a short, so the node is at zero and all 100 milliamps go through the inductor. The time constant is 10 millihenries over 50 ohms, so 200 microseconds.

It lines up with the frequency picture too: a sharp step is high frequency, where $j\omega L$ is large, and DC is zero frequency, where it's zero. And it's exactly opposite to a capacitor, which is a short at the instant of charging and an open at DC."
:::

::: terms
- **$t = 0^+$**: the instant just after a switching event; the inductor current equals its value at $0^-$.
- **Open-circuit approximation**: a de-energized inductor at the instant a step is applied carries zero current.
- **Current-source model**: an energized inductor at $0^+$ behaves as a source of its prior current.
- **DC steady state**: the condition where all currents and voltages are constant, so inductor voltage is zero.
- **Short-circuit approximation**: an ideal inductor at DC steady state.
- **DCR**: DC resistance of the winding; what a real inductor looks like at DC.
- **Time constant (τ)**: $L/R_{th}$; sets the speed of the transition.
- **Inrush current**: the large current that can flow at turn-on when the steady-state limit is only the circuit resistance.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "open at first, short at DC".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains **why** each holds: continuity ($i_L$ can't jump) for the first, $v = L\,di/dt = 0$ for the second.
- Refines "open" into "current source of $i_L(0^-)$", noting the open circuit is the zero-current special case.
- Ties the two together with $\tau = L/R_{th}$ and the exponential, and does a quick numerical example.
- Connects it to the frequency picture ($j\omega L$: high frequency ≈ open, DC ≈ short).
- Adds real-part caveats: DCR at DC, winding capacitance in the first nanoseconds, saturation if steady-state current is too high, and the danger of an inductor directly across a supply.
- Mentions the capacitor dual unprompted.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Swapping with the capacitor.** Capacitor: short at first, open at DC. Inductor: open at first, short at DC. Re-derive from continuity if in doubt.
- **Saying the voltage across the inductor is zero at $t = 0^+$.** It's the current that's zero (if de-energized). The voltage is typically at its maximum then.
- **Treating "initially" as always "open".** If the inductor already carries current, it's a current source at $0^+$, and an open-circuit model gives wrong answers.
- **Thinking a "short at DC" means it's harmless.** An inductor directly across a supply has current limited only by DCR. A 10 µH, 20 mΩ part across 5 V would try to reach 250 A and saturate long before.
- **Using the wrong R in τ.** It's the Thevenin resistance seen by the inductor with sources zeroed, not the resistor that happens to be in series.
:::

::: deep
## The two snapshots, from first principles

Everything follows from $v_L = L\,\dfrac{di_L}{dt}$ and its consequence, current continuity ([[q0043]]).

**Snapshot 1: the instant of excitation.** Suppose the inductor has been sitting with no current, and at $t = 0$ a switch applies a voltage. Before the switch, $i_L(0^-) = 0$. Continuity says $i_L(0^+) = 0$. So at the first instant, the inductor carries zero current *no matter what voltage is across it*. An element that carries zero current at any voltage is, by definition, an **open circuit**.

If the inductor was already carrying $I_0$, the same logic says it carries $I_0$ at $0^+$ regardless of voltage: it's a **current source** of $I_0$. The open circuit is just the $I_0 = 0$ case.

**Snapshot 2: DC steady state.** After a long time with constant sources, nothing changes: $di_L/dt = 0$. So $v_L = 0$ for any current. An element that drops zero volts at any current is a **short circuit**.

| | De-energized at $0^+$ | Energized at $0^+$ | DC steady state |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inductor** | Open circuit | Current source $I_0$ | Short circuit |
| **Capacitor** | Short circuit | Voltage source $V_0$ | Open circuit |

**At $t = 0^+$:** inductor open. The circuit is a plain divider: $v_x = 10\cdot\frac{100}{100+100} = 5$ V. Current from the source is 50 mA, all through $R_2$. Inductor current is 0, but its voltage is 5 V, so it's ramping at $di/dt = 5/0.01 = 500$ A/s.

**At $t \to \infty$:** inductor short. It shorts out $R_2$, so $v_x = 0$, and the full $10/100 = 100$ mA flows through $R_1$ and the inductor. $R_2$ carries nothing.

**Time constant:** zero the source (short it) and look into the inductor's terminals: $R_1 \parallel R_2 = 50$ Ω. So $\tau = 10\text{ mH}/50\ \Omega = 200\ \mu$s.

$$i_L(t) = 100\text{ mA}\left(1 - e^{-t/200\,\mu\text{s}}\right),\qquad v_x(t) = 5\text{ V}\cdot e^{-t/200\,\mu\text{s}}$$

## The frequency-domain view says the same thing

The impedance of an inductor is $Z_L = j\omega L$ ([[q0040]]):

- **DC** ($\omega = 0$): $Z_L = 0$, a short.
- **Very high frequency**: $|Z_L| \to \infty$, an open.

A step input has an instantaneous edge, which contains energy at arbitrarily high frequencies, then a flat top, which is DC. So the inductor looks open to the edge and shorted to the plateau. The time-domain and frequency-domain stories are consistent, which is a nice way to sanity-check either one ([[q0045]]).

## What changes with a real inductor

**DCR at steady state.** A real inductor is a short only to the extent its winding resistance is small. A 10 µH SMD power inductor might have 15–50 mΩ; a 10 mH audio coil might have a few ohms. The steady-state voltage is $I\cdot R_{DC}$, and that's also the $I^2R$ heating ([[q0050]]).

**Inductor directly across a source.** Ideal analysis says the current ramps forever. Real analysis says it heads toward $V/R_{DC}$ with $\tau = L/R_{DC}$, which is usually long and huge: 5 V across 10 µH with 20 mΩ would aim for 250 A with $\tau = 0.5$ ms. Long before that the core saturates ([[q0046]]), $L$ collapses, and current rises even faster. Never connect an inductor across a supply without something limiting the current.

**The first nanoseconds.** The "open circuit at $0^+$" picture ignores winding capacitance. A fast edge actually sees $C_p$ first, so there's a brief capacitive current spike before the inductive behaviour takes over. This is why an inductor doesn't block a 1 ns edge perfectly, and why common-mode chokes have limited high-frequency performance ([[q0047]]).

**Motors and solenoids.** A DC motor's winding inductance (say 1 mH, 1 Ω) makes $\tau = 1$ ms, so inrush current isn't instantaneous, but it reaches near stall current $V/R$ within a few milliseconds because there's no back-EMF yet. See [[q0345]].

::: applications
- **Inrush through EMI chokes**: at power-up an input choke initially blocks the current step, then becomes nearly a short at DC.
- **Relay and solenoid pull-in time**: the coil current, and therefore the magnetic force, rises with $\tau = L/R$; drivers sometimes overdrive the coil briefly to pull in faster.
- **Buck converter soft-start**: the inductor current can't jump, so the controller ramps the output to avoid large inrush into output capacitors.
- **Transient analysis of switching nodes**: at each switch edge, model the inductor as a current source to find node voltages (e.g. switch-node swing in a buck).
- **Bias tees**: an inductor feeds DC to an RF amplifier or antenna (short at DC) while appearing open to the RF signal.
- **DC motor startup**: winding inductance shapes the first milliseconds of inrush current.
:::

::: assumptions
- **"Initially excited" means a step applied to a de-energized inductor.** If it already carries current, model it as a current source at $0^+$.
- **Ideal inductor**: no DCR, no winding capacitance, no saturation. Real DCR makes the DC "short" slightly resistive; real $C_p$ makes the first nanoseconds capacitive.
- **DC sources and enough time**: "steady state" assumes constant excitation for many time constants. With periodic switching (PWM), the inductor never reaches DC steady state within a cycle; instead you get periodic steady state with zero *average* voltage ([[q0041]]).
- **Linear circuit**: the single-exponential solution assumes a first-order circuit. With capacitors present the response can ring ([[q0009]]), but the $0^+$ and $\infty$ snapshots still hold.
:::

::: followups
### What does a capacitor do in the same two situations?
The dual: an uncharged capacitor at $t = 0^+$ is a short (its voltage can't jump, so it stays at 0 V), and at DC steady state it's an open (constant voltage means $i = C\,dv/dt = 0$). A pre-charged capacitor at $0^+$ is a voltage source. See [[q0028]].

### A 24 V supply drives a 2 mH solenoid with 12 Ω of winding resistance. What's the current at 0⁺ and at steady state, and how long does it take to settle?
At $0^+$: 0 A (open). At steady state: $24/12 = 2$ A (inductor is a short, only the winding resistance limits current). $\tau = 2\text{ mH}/12\ \Omega \approx 167\ \mu$s, so it's within 1% after about $5\tau \approx 0.83$ ms.

### In the circuit above, what's the current through R₂ at 0⁺ and at steady state?
At $0^+$ the inductor is open, so all 50 mA from $R_1$ goes through $R_2$ (5 V across 100 Ω). At steady state the inductor shorts $R_2$, so $R_2$ carries 0 A. The current through $R_2$ decays as $50\,e^{-t/200\,\mu s}$ mA.

### Why can't I just use an inductor alone to limit inrush current into a large capacitor at power-up?
An LC with little resistance forms a resonant circuit. At turn-on the inductor does slow the current rise, but then energy sloshes between L and C and the capacitor voltage can overshoot to nearly twice the input. You need damping (a resistor, NTC thermistor, or an active soft-start/hot-swap controller).

### What if the switch closes, then opens again after only 0.5τ? What does the inductor act like at that second instant?
It's carrying $i_L = I_\infty(1 - e^{-0.5}) \approx 0.39\,I_\infty$, so at the opening instant it acts like a current source of that value. Whatever path remains must carry that current, and if there's only a high resistance, you get a large inductive voltage spike ([[q0043]]).
:::

::: interpretations
"Initially excited" most naturally means "the instant a voltage step is first applied to an inductor with no stored energy", so the expected answer is **open circuit**. A stricter reading (an inductor that's already carrying current when something changes) gives **current source**; mentioning both covers you. This is the inductor twin of [[q0028]] for capacitors.
:::
