::: testing
Whether you see the clock as an **abstraction that tames time**, not just "something that makes the chip run". A strong answer explains that combinational logic has delays and glitches, so you need a moment at which values are guaranteed settled and captured; that the clock orders operations, enables pipelining and gives a shared notion of "next state". It also names the costs (power, skew, jitter, EMI) and the asynchronous alternative.
:::

::: why
Every processor, FPGA design and microcontroller peripheral is a clocked system, and most hardware bugs at the digital boundary are timing bugs: setup/hold violations, clock-domain crossings, glitches. Knowing *why* the clock exists makes the rest of the digital block ([[q0880]], [[q0885]], [[q0887]], [[q0946]]) fit together. Interviewers also use it to see whether you can discuss trade-offs rather than recite definitions.
:::

::: concepts
- **Combinational delay and glitches**: outputs of logic settle after a delay and may flicker on the way ([[q0889]], [[q0892]]).
- **State elements**: flip-flops capture values on an edge ([[q0881]], [[q0882]]).
- **Synchronous design**: all state changes on a shared clock edge; logic between flops must settle within a period ([[q0880]]).
- **Pipelining**: splitting logic with registers to raise throughput ([[q0886]]).
- **Dynamic power** $P=\alpha CV^2f$, with the clock net (which toggles every cycle) a major contributor.
- **Asynchronous / multi-clock designs** and clock-domain crossing ([[q0946]], [[q0887]]).
:::

::: answer
"A clock gives a digital system a shared sense of time. Logic gates take time to settle, and on the way to the right answer their outputs can glitch. If everything updated whenever its inputs changed, you'd have races, glitches being mistaken for real data, and no way to say when a result is valid.

The clock fixes that by dividing time into cycles. Flip-flops only look at their inputs at the clock edge. Between edges, the combinational logic is free to settle and glitch, because nobody is looking. As long as the logic finishes inside one period, setup and hold are met, and at the next edge every flip-flop captures a clean value at the same time. So I only have to reason about 'state now' and 'state next cycle', which is what lets us design huge systems with tools.

It also gives us pipelining, where I put registers between stages so each stage has a short path and the clock can be faster, and a way to sequence operations in a CPU or state machine. And it's the time base for timers and communication protocols.

The costs are real though: the clock network can burn a large part of dynamic power, skew and jitter eat into timing margin, and fast edges cause EMI. The alternative is asynchronous design with handshakes, which avoids the clock but is harder to design and verify, so most real systems use clocks, sometimes with several domains."
:::

::: technical
**Problem the clock solves.** A combinational output $Y=f(X)$ is only valid after $t_{pd}$, and may show **hazards** (glitches) before then: a static-1 hazard in $F=AB+\bar AC$ when $B=C=1$ and A switches. Feedback or multiple paths make the order of events depend on delays (races).

**Synchronous discipline.** All state elements update on one edge. Between edges: $T_{clk}\ge t_{cq}+t_{logic,max}+t_{su}+t_{skew}$ ([[q0880]]). Then outputs are sampled only when stable, which turns a continuous-time circuit into a discrete-time one: $S_{n+1}=F(S_n,X_n)$.

**Benefits.** (1) Hides glitches. (2) Gives a defined order of operations. (3) Enables pipelining: with $k$ stages and logic delay $t_L$ split evenly, $T\approx t_L/k+t_{ovh}$. (4) Permits automated design: STA and synthesis assume synchronous timing. (5) A time base for protocols, timers, ADC sampling.

**Costs.** Clock power: for $N_{ff}$ flops each loading $C_{ff}$ on the clock, $P_{clk}\approx N_{ff}C_{ff}V^2f$ (the clock toggles every cycle). Example: $10^6$ flops $\times2$ fF at 0.8 V and 1 GHz: $C=2$ nF, $P=1.28$ W. Plus skew/jitter margin, clock-tree buffers, EMI from edges at the clock fundamental and harmonics.

**Alternatives.** Asynchronous (handshake, dual-rail) logic removes the global clock but needs completion detection and is hard to verify; GALS (globally asynchronous, locally synchronous) uses many clock domains with synchronizers ([[q0887]]); clock gating saves power by stopping the clock to idle blocks.
:::

::: short
"Logic takes time to settle and glitches on the way, so we need a point at which values are guaranteed valid. The clock provides it: flip-flops capture on the edge, and between edges the logic can glitch freely. That makes the design a simple state-to-next-state machine, lets us pipeline for speed, orders operations, and gives timers and protocols a time base. The costs are clock power, skew and jitter, and EMI; the alternative is asynchronous design, which is harder."
:::

::: long
"The core reason is that real logic takes time. A gate has a propagation delay, different paths have different delays, and so when inputs change the outputs of a block of logic wander through wrong or glitchy values before settling. If everything updated whenever its inputs changed, those glitches would be treated as data, and signals racing along different paths would give unpredictable results.

A clock fixes this by defining when values matter. Flip-flops sample their inputs only at the clock edge. Between edges, the logic can do whatever it wants, as long as it has settled by the next edge, minus setup time. So the rule is clock-to-Q plus the longest logic delay plus setup must fit in one period. If it does, every flip-flop captures a clean value at the same instant, and I can think of the whole machine as 'state at cycle n goes to state at cycle n plus one', which is a mathematical model we can simulate, synthesize and time automatically.

The clock also lets me pipeline. If one big block of logic takes 4 ns, I can split it into four 1 ns pieces with registers between, and run at about 1.5 ns per cycle including overhead. Latency is longer but throughput is much higher. It orders operations in a processor, and it's the time base for timers, UARTs and ADC sampling.

The costs: the clock toggles every cycle on every flop, so the clock network can be a large share of dynamic power; a million flops at 2 femtofarads, 0.8 volts and 1 GHz is over a watt just in the clock. There's skew and jitter, which eat into the timing budget, and sharp edges cause EMI. Asynchronous design avoids the clock using handshakes, but it's hard to verify, so most designs are synchronous, often with several clock domains and synchronizers between them."
:::

::: terms
- **Clock**: periodic signal whose edges define when state elements update.
- **Synchronous design**: all registers update on a common clock edge.
- **Glitch / hazard**: temporary wrong output from unequal path delays.
- **Race condition**: outcome depends on which of two signals arrives first.
- **Pipelining**: inserting registers to shorten each stage and raise throughput.
- **Clock skew / jitter**: spatial and temporal variation of clock edges.
- **Clock tree**: buffered distribution network delivering the clock to all flops.
- **Asynchronous design**: logic that coordinates with handshakes instead of a global clock.
- **Clock domain**: group of registers on the same clock; crossing needs synchronizers.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the clock synchronizes the circuit so everything happens at the same time".

**An exceptional answer**:

- Gives the timing rule ($T\ge t_{cq}+t_{logic}+t_{su}+t_{skew}$) and the discrete-time state-machine abstraction it enables.
- Describes pipelining with numbers, and mentions timers and protocols.
- Quantifies the costs (clock power $CV^2f$ example, skew, jitter, EMI).
- Names the alternatives (asynchronous, GALS, clock gating) and why most designs still use clocks.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The clock makes things faster."** The clock does not speed the logic; it adds overhead per stage. Speed comes from short paths and pipelining; the clock makes them *orderable*.
- **"Everything happens instantly at the edge."** Outputs change after $t_{cq}$ and logic needs the rest of the period to settle.
- **Ignoring power.** Clock distribution often consumes a large fraction of a chip's dynamic power; clock gating exists for this reason.
- **Saying asynchronous circuits are impossible or always slower.** They exist and can be fast and low-power, but are harder to design and verify.
- **Mixing up clock frequency with performance.** Throughput depends on cycles per task and cycle time together.
:::

::: deep
## The problem: logic has delay, and the delay is not the same on every path

Imagine a block computing $F=A\cdot B+\overline A\cdot C$, a 2:1 multiplexer ([[q0888]]). With $B=C=1$, $F$ should stay at 1 regardless of $A$. But when $A$ falls, the $A\cdot B$ term drops immediately, while $\overline A$ needs an extra inverter delay to rise. For a moment both product terms are 0, and $F$ dips to 0.

Q only samples F at the edges: just before the glitch and again after F has settled back to 1 (the setup margin is what guarantees it has settled). The glitch itself is never captured. The clock decides *when* you look.

If this signal drives an edge-sensitive input (a counter clock, a latch enable, an interrupt line), a 100 ps glitch can look like a real event. That is the entire argument for synchronous design: let logic make a mess, but only sample when it has finished.

## The discrete-time abstraction

With a clock, a circuit becomes a state machine: $S_{n+1}=F(S_n, X_n)$, where $n$ counts clock cycles and $F$ is pure combinational logic. All that matters about $F$ is: *did it settle in time?* That is one inequality per register-to-register path ([[q0880]]):

$$T_{clk}\ge t_{cq}+t_{logic,max}+t_{su}+t_{skew}.$$

This abstraction is what allows a design of $10^9$ transistors: synthesis tools optimize $F$ for timing, static timing analysis checks every path, and simulators evaluate one cycle at a time. Without it, you would need analog-style simulation of every glitch.

## Pipelining: trading latency for throughput

Suppose a computation needs 4.0 ns of logic. With register overhead ($t_{cq}+t_{su}$) of 0.5 ns:

| Stages | Logic per stage | Period | Max throughput | Latency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 (unpipelined) | 4.0 ns | 4.5 ns | 222 MHz | 4.5 ns |
| 2 | 2.0 ns | 2.5 ns | 400 MHz | 5.0 ns |
| 4 | 1.0 ns | 1.5 ns | 667 MHz | 6.0 ns |

Throughput triples while latency grows from 4.5 to 6 ns. Without a clock and registers there is no stage boundary to put the pipeline on. The overhead $t_{cq}+t_{su}$ is the price of every stage, which is why deeper pipelines have diminishing returns.

## The clock as time base

Beyond logic correctness, clocks give meaning to time itself: a UART's baud rate, an ADC's sample rate, a PWM period, an SPI bit rate are all derived from a clock by dividers and PLLs.

## What the clock costs

**Power.** The clock net toggles every cycle (activity factor 1, two transitions), so $P=C_{clk}V^2f$ where $C_{clk}$ is the clock-tree wire plus flop clock-pin capacitance. The worked example: $10^6$ flops $\times2$ fF $=2$ nF; at 0.8 V and 1 GHz, $P=2\times10^{-9}\cdot0.64\cdot10^9=1.28$ W. Data signals toggle with $\alpha<1$ (often 0.1), so the clock can be a large fraction of dynamic power, in many designs on the order of a quarter to a half. Hence **clock gating**: stop the clock to idle blocks, so $f_{eff}\to0$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The clock's share of total power varies a lot with design style and workload. Treat "a quarter to a half" as a rough range for synchronous logic-heavy designs, not a measured value for a specific chip.

**Skew and jitter.** Different flops receive the clock at slightly different times (skew), and the edge position varies cycle to cycle (jitter). Both subtract from the timing budget and force the clock tree to be carefully balanced.

**EMI.** A periodic edge train concentrates energy at the clock frequency and harmonics. Spread-spectrum clocking and slew-rate control reduce peak emissions.

**Distribution of a single clock** becomes hard at GHz rates and large die sizes, which is why large chips use multiple clock domains.

## Alternatives

- **Asynchronous (self-timed) circuits**: each stage signals completion with request/acknowledge handshakes. No clock power, average-case rather than worst-case timing, but they need completion detection logic and are hard to verify with standard tools.
- **GALS**: locally synchronous islands with their own clocks communicating through asynchronous FIFOs or synchronizers ([[q0946]], [[q0887]]).
- **Event-driven designs**: sensors that output only on change, for power saving, still usually drive a clocked receiver.

## Real hardware view

In a microcontroller, one crystal feeds a PLL that produces the core clock and, through dividers, bus and peripheral clocks. Disabling a peripheral's clock gate (a register bit in the clock control unit) stops its dynamic power entirely, which is the first thing you do in a low-power design. In an FPGA, the clock tree is a dedicated global network because ordinary routing has too much skew.

> [!TIP]
> If asked "do you always need a clock?", answer: "No: purely combinational logic doesn't, and asynchronous designs exist. But anything with state that needs a defined order and to be timing-verified is almost always clocked."
:::

::: applications
- **Processors and SoCs**: the CPU, buses and memory controllers are synchronous to derived clocks.
- **FPGAs**: every design in HDL is mostly clocked processes with global clock networks.
- **Microcontroller peripherals**: timers, UART, SPI and ADC sampling derive their timing from the system clock.
- **Low-power design**: clock gating and dynamic frequency scaling reduce $CV^2f$.
- **Synchronous communication**: SPI, I²S, DDR use a clock to define when data is valid.
:::

::: assumptions
- Single-clock synchronous design with edge-triggered flip-flops as the baseline; latch-based and asynchronous styles are alternatives.
- The power example assumes the clock toggles the whole capacitance every cycle with no gating and uses assumed per-flop capacitance.
- Pipelining table assumes perfectly balanced stages and fixed 0.5 ns register overhead; real stages are imbalanced.
- "Clock" refers to a periodic square wave; some systems use pulsed or multi-phase clocks.
:::

::: followups
### Can you build a state machine without a clock?
Yes, with asynchronous sequential logic (feedback loops with careful hazard-free design, or handshake circuits). It is difficult: hazards and races determine the behavior, and it needs special design rules (fundamental mode operation). That is why almost everything uses a clock.

### What are the downsides of just running the clock faster?
Setup slack shrinks (the period must still cover $t_{cq}+t_{logic}+t_{su}$), dynamic power rises linearly with $f$, the clock edges radiate more, and above some frequency the critical path fails ([[q0885]]). You'd pipeline to go faster, which adds registers and clock load.

### Why do chips have many clock domains?
Different blocks need different speeds (CPU 2 GHz, a low-speed peripheral 10 MHz), power gating wants independent control, I/O interfaces run at standard rates, and distributing one clock across a large die is hard. The price is clock-domain crossings, which need synchronizers ([[q0887]], [[q0946]]).

### What does clock gating do, and what can go wrong?
It disables the clock to registers that don't need to change, cutting dynamic power. If done with a bare AND gate and an enable that changes while the clock is high, it makes runt pulses; use a latch-based gating cell that holds the enable stable during clock-high ([[q0881]]).

### Your design works at 100 MHz but randomly fails at 200 MHz. What clock-related causes do you check?
Setup violations on the critical path (period halved from 10 ns to 5 ns), clock skew and jitter, slow edges from a weak clock buffer, a PLL not yet locked or noisy supply modulating the delay. Start from the STA report for negative slack and the scope on the clock at the failing block.
:::

For why you'd run multiple clocks, see [[q0946]].
