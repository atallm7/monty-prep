::: testing
Whether you know that a battery is not a "voltage source with a capacity" but a nonlinear, temperature- and history-dependent electrochemical device that you have to *measure* to model. The interviewer wants to hear the list of parameters (capacity, voltage curve, internal resistance, rate capability, temperature behaviour, self-discharge, cycle life) **and** how you would actually test each one on a bench with a programmable load, a source-measure unit and a temperature chamber. The hidden skill is test design: constant conditions, rest periods, and knowing which numbers are conditions-dependent.
:::

::: why
Datasheets quote capacity at one convenient condition. Your product will run at other currents and temperatures, and if you do not characterise the cell you will mis-size the battery, mis-set the fuel gauge, or miss that your radio burst browns out the MCU at 5 °C. Battery model parameters are also what simulators and BMS firmware ([[q0304]]) use. Real teams do exactly this: pulse tests, capacity tests, and cycling.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacity (Ah, Wh)**: charge or energy delivered between defined voltage limits at a defined rate and temperature. See [[q0311]].
- **Open-circuit voltage (OCV) vs SoC**: the voltage when the cell has rested and no current flows; a fingerprint of the chemistry ([[q0308]]).
- **Internal resistance**: the voltage drop per amp, measured by pulses (DC) or impedance spectroscopy (AC). See [[q0303]].
- **C-rate**: current normalised to capacity; 1C for a 2 Ah cell is 2 A ([[q0315]]).
- **Coulombic and energy efficiency**: charge out / charge in, energy out / energy in.
- **Self-discharge and cycle life**: capacity lost while idle, and how many cycles before the cell falls to 80% ([[q0312]]).
:::

::: answer
"I'd treat the battery like any device under test and measure the things my design depends on. First capacity: charge it fully with the manufacturer's CC/CV procedure, let it rest, then discharge at a fixed current, say C over 5 or the datasheet rate, down to the cutoff voltage, and integrate current over time. That gives amp-hours, and multiplying by voltage and integrating gives watt-hours. I'd repeat it at several C-rates and temperatures, because capacity drops at high rate and in the cold.

Second, the voltage curve: terminal voltage versus state of charge during a slow discharge, and the rested open-circuit voltage at steps of say 5 or 10% SoC. That gives me the OCV curve for a fuel gauge.

Third, internal resistance. I'd apply a current pulse, like 1 A for 10 or 30 seconds, and look at the instantaneous voltage step, which is the ohmic part, and the further sag as the diffusion and charge-transfer parts kick in. Resistance changes with SoC, temperature and age, so I'd map it. If I have an impedance analyser I can do impedance spectroscopy too.

Then the long-term stuff: self-discharge, by resting and measuring OCV or capacity after weeks, and cycle life, by cycling and tracking capacity and resistance fade. And throughout I'd log temperature, because the cell heats up."
:::

::: technical
**Parameters and how each is measured**

| Parameter | Typical test | Output |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity $Q$ | CC/CV charge to full, rest 30-60 min, CC discharge to cutoff; $Q=\int I\,dt$ | Ah at given C-rate and temperature |
| Energy $E$ | $E=\int V I\,dt$ over the same discharge | Wh |
| OCV vs SoC | Discharge in 5-10% steps, rest 1-2 h at each; read rested voltage | OCV(SoC) table |
| DC internal resistance | Current step $\Delta I$, $R=\Delta V/\Delta I$ at fixed time (e.g. 10 ms, 1 s or 10 s after step) | $R_{DC}(SoC,T,I)$ |
| AC impedance | EIS, small sine sweep (e.g. mHz-kHz); or a 1 kHz meter reading | Nyquist/Bode, ESR |
| Rate capability | Capacity at 0.2C, 0.5C, 1C, 2C, ... | Q vs C-rate (Peukert-like) |
| Temperature | Repeat above in a chamber (e.g. -20, 0, 25, 45 °C) | Q(T), R(T) |
| Self-discharge | Charge, rest N weeks, measure remaining capacity | %/month |
| Cycle life | Repeated charge/discharge; check Q and R every N cycles | Cycles to 80% |
| Efficiency | $\eta_{coulomb}=Q_{out}/Q_{in}$, $\eta_E=E_{out}/E_{in}$ | % (typically >99% coulombic for Li-ion at moderate rates) |

**Pulse analysis.** With a current step $I$ at $t=0$ the voltage is approximately $V(t)=V_{oc}-I\left[R_0+R_1(1-e^{-t/\tau_1})\right]$. The instantaneous drop gives $R_0$; the slower relaxation gives $R_1$ and $\tau_1=R_1C_1$. Quoting "internal resistance" without the timescale is meaningless.

**Conditions matter.** State the charge protocol, rest time, cutoff voltages, rate and temperature with every number. A "2000 mAh" cell may deliver 1700 mAh at 2C or at 0 °C.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Test protocols (rest times, cutoff voltages, pulse lengths) differ by manufacturer and by standard. Use the cell datasheet's own specification conditions for comparison to the vendor's numbers.
:::

::: short
"I'd measure it like a device under test. Capacity: charge with CC/CV, rest, then constant-current discharge to cutoff and integrate current. Open-circuit voltage versus state of charge, with rests, for the fuel gauge. Internal resistance from a current pulse: the instant voltage step over the current. Then repeat across C-rates and temperatures, and measure self-discharge over weeks and capacity and resistance fade over cycling. And I'd always record conditions, because every one of these numbers depends on rate and temperature."
:::

::: long
"Think of a battery as having a handful of parameters that my design depends on, and I'd characterise each one with a specific test.

Capacity first. Fully charge it with the datasheet's constant-current, constant-voltage profile, let it rest, then discharge at a constant current to the cutoff voltage, logging time, voltage and current. Integrating current gives amp-hours; integrating voltage times current gives watt-hours. I'd run that at several rates, like 0.2C, 0.5C, 1C and 2C, and at several temperatures in a chamber, because capacity falls at high rate and in the cold.

Second, the voltage-versus-SoC relationship. I'd step the cell down in 5 or 10 percent steps, rest an hour or two each time, and record the rested open-circuit voltage. That table is what a fuel gauge or a SoC estimator uses.

Third, internal resistance. Apply a current pulse, say 1 A, and look at the voltage trace. The immediate step is the ohmic resistance; the slower sag over seconds is charge transfer and diffusion. So I'd quote DC resistance at a stated pulse length, and map it against SoC and temperature. If I have an impedance analyser, I'd do impedance spectroscopy and fit an equivalent circuit.

Then the slow stuff. Self-discharge: charge it, leave it for weeks, and measure what's left. Cycle life: repeat charge and discharge hundreds of times and track capacity and resistance until capacity hits 80 percent. During all of this I'd measure temperature on the cell surface, because self-heating changes everything. And I'd write down the conditions with every number, because a capacity without a rate and temperature is not a spec."
:::

::: terms
- **Nominal capacity**: manufacturer's rated capacity at a reference rate, voltage window and temperature.
- **OCV**: open-circuit voltage after the cell has rested (relaxed) with no current.
- **DCIR / ACIR**: DC internal resistance from a pulse; AC internal resistance from an impedance measurement (often 1 kHz).
- **EIS**: electrochemical impedance spectroscopy; small-signal frequency sweep.
- **Rate capability**: how much capacity/voltage you keep as C-rate rises.
- **Coulombic efficiency**: $Q_{out}/Q_{in}$ over a cycle.
- **Cutoff voltage**: the voltage at which discharge or charge is stopped.
- **Capacity fade / resistance growth**: the two aging signatures ([[q0301]]).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "voltage, capacity and maybe internal resistance".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives a *test method* for each: CC discharge and integrate, rested OCV steps, current-pulse resistance, chamber for temperature.
- Says the numbers are meaningless without conditions (rate, temperature, cutoffs, rest time).
- Distinguishes **DC pulse resistance** (timescale-dependent) from **1 kHz AC resistance**.
- Mentions the instrument setup: programmable electronic load or SMU, 4-wire (Kelvin) sensing so contact resistance does not corrupt a milliohm measurement, thermocouple on the cell, and safe limits.
- Connects the measurements to what they feed: fuel gauge OCV table, regulator minimum voltage, thermal design (I²R).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Measuring voltage under load and calling it the OCV.** Under current it is $V_{oc}-IR$, and recently-charged cells need time to relax. Rest first.
- **Measuring resistance with a 2-wire DMM.** Lead and contact resistance (tens of milliohms) are as large as the cell. Use 4-wire sensing.
- **Quoting capacity without rate or temperature.** Always state them.
- **Using only a 1 kHz meter for resistance.** A 1 kHz reading is typically lower than the DC pulse value your regulator sees on a 100 ms load.
- **Not logging temperature.** At 3C a pouch cell can self-heat by many degrees, and that changes both capacity and resistance.
- **Cycling to cell limits with no safety.** Use voltage/current/temperature limits on the test equipment ([[q0295]]).
:::

::: deep
## Start from what you need to know

A designer wants to answer: how long will it run? What voltage will my circuit see? How hot will it get? How long will it last? Each question maps to a test.

| Question | Parameter | Measurement |
|---|---|---|
| How long? | Capacity vs rate and temperature | CC discharge, integrate |
| What voltage under load? | OCV(SoC) and R(SoC,T) | rested steps + pulses |
| How hot? | $R$ and heat $I^2R$, plus entropic heat | pulse + thermocouple |
| How long will it last? | Cycle and calendar aging | long-term cycling |
| How much does it lose idle? | Self-discharge | rest and re-measure |

## Capacity: integrate the current

Run a constant current $I$ from full to the cutoff voltage $V_{cut}$ and record the time $t_{end}$. For constant current the capacity is just $Q=I\,t_{end}$; for variable current $Q=\int_0^{t_{end}} I\,dt$. Energy is $E=\int V I\,dt$. Because the voltage sags more at high current, **delivered capacity falls with C-rate** and *energy* falls even faster, since both $Q$ and the average voltage are lower. A 2 Ah cell with 50 mΩ delivers about $1-\tfrac{IR}{V}$ of its voltage as useful: at 2 A (1C) that's a 0.1 V drop of 3.7 V, 2.7%; at 10 A (5C) it is 0.5 V, 13.5% (computed: $0.5/3.7 = 13.5\%$ ).

## OCV curve: rest, then read

When you stop current the terminal voltage does not jump to OCV; it relaxes with time constants of seconds to hours as concentration gradients equalise. So an OCV table is built from long rests (typically 1-2 h, or until $dV/dt$ is below a threshold). The shape is chemistry-dependent: steep for NMC/LCO and very flat for LFP ([[q0300]]).

## Internal resistance from a pulse

Apply a step of current and look at the voltage: instant drop, then a slower sag, then (when the pulse ends) an instant recovery and a slow relaxation.

Figure: At t = 10 s the voltage steps down by I·R0 = 60 mV instantly (ohmic path: electrolyte, contacts, current collectors). It then keeps sagging as the charge-transfer/diffusion branch R1C1 charges, reaching about 83 mV at the end of the 30 s pulse. When the pulse ends it jumps back 60 mV and creeps up over tens of seconds.

From this: $R_{0}=\Delta V_{inst}/I=0.060/1=60$ mΩ, and $R_{DC,30s}=(4.000-3.917)/1\approx 83$ mΩ. Both are "the internal resistance"; they differ by the timescale. That is why you must state the pulse length. See [[q0303]] for temperature, aging and frequency dependence.

## Temperature, rate and self-discharge

Repeat capacity and pulse tests in a thermal chamber. Expect resistance to rise sharply and capacity to fall as temperature falls. Self-discharge is measured by rest: charge, wait a defined period (weeks), measure OCV or run a discharge, and compare. Li-ion is typically a few percent per month, NiMH is much higher, and primary lithium cells can be well below 1% per year. These values vary by manufacturer and temperature.

## Cycle life

Cycle life testing runs repeated charge and discharge at a stated rate, depth of discharge and temperature, with periodic reference tests (a slow capacity test and a pulse test). The result is two fade curves, capacity and resistance, versus cycle count. "End of life" is usually defined at 80% of initial capacity, but a power-limited application can fail earlier because resistance grows. See [[q0301]].

## Instrumentation notes

- Use a programmable electronic load or battery cycler with accurate current measurement (the integral is only as good as the current sensor offset and gain; compare coulomb counting error in [[q0308]]).
- Use **4-wire (Kelvin) sensing** for voltage so the connector and lead resistance is not part of the measurement.
- Put a thermocouple or NTC on the cell body, not on the lead.
- Set hard voltage, current and temperature limits on the instrument and test in a ventilated, fire-safe location.

::: applications
- **Fuel-gauge ICs** (e.g. impedance-tracking and OCV-lookup gauges) ship with a per-cell profile derived from exactly these tests.
- **Cell selection** for a product: comparing three vendor cells by capacity at your actual load and temperature.
- **Thermal design**: DCIR × current² gives heat to remove from a pack.
- **Warranty and quality control**: incoming inspection by capacity and ACIR on samples.
- **Battery simulation models** (Thevenin equivalents) are fitted from the pulse and OCV data.
:::

::: assumptions
The answer assumes a single secondary lithium-type cell in a lab test. For primary cells the key curves are discharge versus load and shelf life; for lead-acid add float behaviour and sulfation.

A test protocol should follow whatever standard the customer or vendor uses (rest times and cutoffs differ). Pulse resistance depends on pulse length, SoC, current and temperature, so a single number is always shorthand.

For packs rather than cells, add cell-to-cell variation and interconnect resistance to the list of things to measure.
:::

::: followups
### How do you measure the internal resistance of a battery?
Apply a known current step and measure the voltage step: $R=\Delta V/\Delta I$. For a 1 A step that produces a 60 mV instantaneous drop, $R_0=60$ mΩ. Use 4-wire sensing, state the time at which you read $\Delta V$ (10 ms vs 10 s give different answers), and note the SoC and temperature. An LCR meter at 1 kHz gives an AC resistance that is usually lower than the DC pulse value.

### Why do you let the battery rest before reading OCV?
After a charge or discharge, concentration gradients inside the electrodes and electrolyte take seconds to hours to relax, so the terminal voltage is still moving. A 1-2 hour rest (or a settling criterion on dV/dt) gets close to the true equilibrium. In the cold, relaxation is slower and takes longer.

### Two cells show the same 2000 mAh on the label. How do you decide which is better for a 3C load?
Compare capacity at 3C and at the operating temperature, plus pulse resistance. A high-energy cell may deliver only 1600 mAh at 3C with large heating, while a high-power cell delivers 1900 mAh with half the I²R. Also check rated continuous current, cycle life at that rate, and cost.

### Your capacity test gives 2.1 Ah, but the label says 2.0 Ah. Should you trust it?
Not blindly. Check current-sensor offset and gain error, cutoff voltage, rate and temperature; a 1% gain error is 20 mAh, and a low cutoff voltage makes the cell appear larger. Label capacity is often a minimum or typical value at a specific condition. Repeat on several cells and compare with the datasheet's test conditions.

### How would you do this quickly if you only have a multimeter and a resistor?
Measure OCV with the DMM, connect a known resistor (e.g. 10 Ω), measure the loaded voltage, and solve $R_{int}=R_L(V_{oc}/V_{load}-1)$. E.g. $V_{oc}=4.00$ V, $V_{load}=3.94$ V with $R_L=10$ Ω gives $R_{int}=10(4.00/3.94-1)=0.152$ Ω. Record time too, because the reading will drift. It's crude (2-wire, long pulse) but good for a sanity check.
:::

::: rubric
- Lists capacity, voltage/OCV curve, internal resistance, rate and temperature behaviour, self-discharge, cycle life :: These are the standard parameters; missing several shows shallow knowledge.
- Describes capacity test as CC discharge to cutoff and integrating current :: Shows you can turn the definition into a procedure.
- Mentions resting before OCV measurement :: Voltage under or just after load is not OCV.
- Measures internal resistance from a current step ($\Delta V/\Delta I$) and notes dependence on timescale :: A single number without a timescale is misleading.
- Repeats across C-rate and temperature :: Real products operate off the datasheet conditions.
- States conditions with every number :: Capacity and resistance are conditions-dependent.
- Mentions 4-wire sensing and temperature logging, and safety limits :: Distinguishes someone who has actually run a battery test.
