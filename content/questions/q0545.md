::: testing
Whether you have a systematic layout method for a switching supply instead of a list of tips. The interviewer wants you to separate the circuit into the high-current/high-di/dt power stage, the high-dV/dt switch node, the quiet control and feedback side, and the thermal and mechanical needs, then place and route in priority order: hot loop first, then the inductor and switch node, then output capacitors, then feedback and control. Mentioning the ground plane, vias and thermal copper is expected.
:::

::: why
In a switching regulator the schematic is only half the design: the same parts with a bad layout will ring, radiate, run hot, jitter and sometimes fail or oscillate. Datasheet layout sections exist because the first-pass layout of most engineers is wrong. This question tells the interviewer whether you can turn a schematic into a board that works the first time, which is one of the most valued practical skills in power electronics.
:::

::: concepts
- **Buck power stage**: input capacitor, high-side and low-side switches, inductor, output capacitor. See [[q0175]].
- **Hot loop**: the loop of input capacitor and both switches carries pulsed current. See [[q0548]].
- **Switch node**: high dV/dt and a noise source. See [[q0550]].
- **Feedback network**: a high-impedance, noise-sensitive node. See [[q0552]].
- **Ground plane and return paths**: the plane under the power stage carries the return currents. See [[q0486]].
- **Thermal management**: exposed pad, copper and vias. See [[q0540]].
:::

::: answer
"I lay out a switching supply in priority order, following the current. First, the hot loop: the input capacitor, the high-side switch and the low-side switch form a loop with fast pulsed current, so those go as close together as possible, with the input capacitor right at the VIN and ground pins, so the loop is a few millimetres. Second, the switch node: the short connection from the switch pins to the inductor, as small an area as the current allows, on one layer, with no vias, and nothing sensitive routed near it. Third, the inductor and the output capacitors, with the capacitors close to the inductor and the load, and wide copper for the output path.

Then the quiet side: the feedback divider close to the feedback pin, its trace short, on the opposite side of the IC from the switch node and the inductor, with its ground going to the quiet analog ground and the sense taken from the output at the right place. The compensation, soft-start and bootstrap parts also sit tight against their pins.

Underneath everything is a solid ground plane directly below the power stage, with the power ground tied in with plenty of vias. And I handle heat: exposed pad soldered to copper with thermal vias, enough copper area, and the inductor kept clear. Then I check against the datasheet's layout example, because it is usually the proven answer."
:::

::: technical
**Priority order.**
1. **Hot loop** ($C_{in}$, high-side FET, low-side FET/diode): minimise loop area and inductance (target a few nH, loop of a few mm). Input capacitor (small ceramic, 0402 to 0805) directly at VIN/PGND pins. Use layer 2 as a solid ground plane right below (a thin dielectric lowers loop inductance: roughly $\mu_0 h$ per square).
2. **Switch node (SW)**: short, direct from the SW pin(s) to the inductor pad; wide enough for the inductor current but minimum area; one layer; no vias or other nets underneath on the adjacent layers where avoidable; bootstrap capacitor tight between BOOT and SW.
3. **Inductor**: next to SW; its other pad feeds the output capacitors; orient to keep stray field away from sensitive nodes.
4. **Output capacitors**: ceramic close to the inductor/PGND and to the load; ground connection with vias to the plane.
5. **Feedback, compensation, enable, soft-start**: small parts at the IC's pins on the quiet side; feedback trace short and away from SW/inductor; sense the output at the load side of the output capacitor (or remote sense).
6. **Ground**: PGND (power) and AGND (signal); connect at one point near the IC (often the thermal pad); no switching current through AGND.
7. **Thermal**: exposed pad soldered to a top pour, thermal vias, copper on several layers; keep the inductor DCR heat and the IC's heat from stacking.
8. **Mechanical/test**: test points, keep tall parts clear, probe access for SW with short ground spring.

**Numbers for context.** 12 V → 3.3 V at 5 A, $f_{sw} = 500$ kHz: $D = 0.275$, input capacitor RMS current $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)} = 2.2$ A; with 5 A pulses in 3 ns, a hot loop of 3 nH gives $L\,di/dt\approx$ 5 V, 10 nH about 17 V of ring potential (peak overshoot is usually lower owing to damping).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Loop inductance depends on geometry; "a few nH" is a typical target for good layouts. The overshoot numbers are first-order estimates, since real edge times and damping vary by part.
:::

::: long
"I follow the current and rank things by how fast and how big the current or voltage edge is.

First is the hot loop. In a buck, the input capacitor, the high-side switch and the low-side switch form a loop that carries pulses of the full load current with edges of a few nanoseconds. Any inductance there makes overshoot and ringing, V equals L di by dt, and it radiates. So the input capacitor goes right at the VIN and ground pins, and I make the loop a few millimetres. A solid ground plane on the next layer, close to the top, shrinks the loop further.

Second is the switch node, which swings from the input voltage to ground in a few nanoseconds. It must be wide enough for the inductor current but as small in area as possible, short from the pins to the inductor, on one layer, no vias, nothing sensitive nearby. The bootstrap capacitor goes tight between the boot and switch pins.

Third are the inductor and output capacitors: the inductor right next to the switch node, the ceramics close by with good vias to the ground plane, and wide copper to the load.

Fourth is the quiet side. The feedback divider sits close to the feedback pin, with a short trace on the side of the chip away from the switch node and inductor, and a clean ground. Compensation, soft-start and enable parts also hug their pins.

Throughout, I keep a solid plane under it all, tie power ground and analog ground at one place, and give the exposed pad copper and thermal vias. Finally I compare with the datasheet's recommended layout, because the manufacturer has already debugged it."
:::

::: terms
- **Hot loop**: the high-di/dt loop through input capacitor and switches. See [[q0548]].
- **Switch node (SW)**: the node connecting the switches and inductor; high dV/dt.
- **PGND / AGND**: power and analog (signal) grounds.
- **Bootstrap (BOOT) capacitor**: the capacitor that powers the high-side gate drive.
- **Feedback (FB) node**: high-impedance node from the output divider.
- **Compensation**: the RC network that sets the control loop; a sensitive node.
- **Exposed pad**: the thermal and often ground connection of the package.
- **Ground plane**: the solid return layer under the power stage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "short traces, close capacitors, good ground."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Orders the work by current slope and voltage slope: hot loop, switch node, inductor/output, then feedback.
- Explains *why* each rule exists (overshoot $L\,di/dt$, displacement current from SW, noise pickup on FB).
- Quantifies the loop (a few nH, a few mm) and the effects.
- Describes grounding: plane under the power stage, PGND/AGND join, no switching current in AGND.
- Covers heat and mechanical aspects, and refers to the datasheet layout.
- Knows where the vias go and where they must not.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Placing the input capacitor "near" instead of at the pins.** Millimetres matter: see [[q0549]].
- **Routing the feedback trace beside the inductor or switch node.** It picks up noise. See [[q0552]].
- **Making the switch node a big pour "for current".** Area equals noise coupling. See [[q0550]].
- **Splitting the ground plane under the power stage.** It forces return currents on long detours.
- **Putting vias in the hot loop that move current to a distant layer.** Each adds inductance.
- **Ignoring the thermal pad and heat.** An overheating regulator derates and shortens life.
- **Ignoring the datasheet layout example.** It is the safest starting point.
:::

::: deep
## Think in loops and slopes

Every conductor in a buck falls into one of three classes:

| Class | What flows | Example | Layout rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| High di/dt | Pulsed current, ns edges | Hot loop ($C_{in}$, $Q_H$, $Q_L$) | Minimum loop area; no vias |
| High dV/dt | Voltage swings, ns edges | Switch node, bootstrap | Minimum area; shield with plane; keep sensitive nodes away |
| Quiet and sensitive | Small current, high impedance | FB, COMP, EN, SS, reference | Short, away from the above, own clean ground |
| Steady current | Inductor and output current | $L$, $C_{out}$, load path | Wide copper, low resistance |

## Priority 1: the hot loop

When the high-side FET turns on, current flows $C_{in} \to Q_H \to L$. When it turns off, the inductor current freewheels through $Q_L$ and the input capacitor briefly sees the current commutate. The loop $C_{in}\to Q_H\to Q_L\to C_{in}$ therefore carries a trapezoid of amplitude $\approx I_{out}$ with edges of 1 to 5 ns. Any inductance $L_{loop}$ produces $L_{loop}\,di/dt$, and resonates with the FET output capacitance ([[q0549]]). Numbers for 5 A in 3 ns ($1.7\times10^9$ A/s): 1 nH → 1.7 V, 3 nH → 5 V, 10 nH → 17 V of potential overshoot (damping reduces it, but it stresses the FET's $V_{DS}$ rating and radiates).

How to get a few nH:
- Put the input ceramic at the IC's VIN and PGND pins with copper directly under the pins; no vias between the capacitor and the pins on the loop path.
- Use layer 2 as a solid ground plane directly below (loop inductance roughly $\mu_0 h\,l/w$: a 5 mm × 5 mm loop at $h = 0.1$ mm is about 0.13 nH per square).
- Use several small ceramics in parallel rather than one big one.
- In controller + external FET designs, put $Q_H$ and $Q_L$ and the capacitor in a tight triangle, with the ground vias at the source of $Q_L$ and the capacitor.

## Priority 2: switch node

Short, compact, one layer. See [[q0550]]. A rule of thumb: the SW copper should look like a short wide link, not a pour. Keep the plane beneath it solid (it provides a return and shields), but keep other nets away on both sides.

## Priority 3: inductor and output

The inductor current is smooth (ripple only), so its connection is about resistance and heat: wide copper. Place it adjacent to the SW pins, orient it so its winding terminals face the IC, and keep its stray field away from sensitive nodes ([[q0551]]). Output ceramics sit within a few mm of the inductor's output pad with ground vias to the plane. The path from the output capacitors to the load should be wide and short ([[q0543]]).

## Priority 4: the quiet side

The feedback divider's resistors are typically tens of kΩ; a few fF of coupling from SW injects a measurable error ([[q0552]]). Place the divider next to the FB pin; send its sense line to the output after the last capacitor; keep it on the side of the IC opposite to SW; use the analog ground. Compensation parts hug the COMP pin. Enable, soft-start and power-good lines are slow but should avoid SW. 

## Grounds

Use a solid plane under the IC and the whole power stage. Power ground (input capacitor, low-side source, output capacitor returns) and analog ground (feedback divider bottom, compensation, reference) join at one point, commonly the exposed pad. Do not run power-stage return current through the analog area. Stitch the plane to others with vias away from the hot loop ([[q0539]]).

## Heat and mechanics

The IC's exposed pad needs copper and thermal vias ([[q0540]]); the inductor's DCR heats its pads, so give them copper too, but remember large pours on SW are bad ([[q0537]]). Keep the inductor and the IC from being thermal neighbours of sensitive parts. Allow probe access to SW and the output (short ground spring), and test points for FB and enable.

## Compare with the datasheet

Most controller datasheets include a layout example with the intended placement and routing and a list of "do and don't" notes. Treat it as the starting point and deviate only with a reason; if your layout differs (different package orientation, different layer count), recheck the hot loop and the feedback path.

::: applications
- **Integrated buck regulators** (QFN with internal FETs): layout following the datasheet example, input capacitor on pins, thermal pad vias.
- **Controller plus external MOSFET designs**, where the power loop includes the FETs, gate drivers and bootstrap.
- **Point-of-load converters for FPGAs** with tight space and high current.
- **Automotive and industrial buck converters** with EMI-critical layouts and input filters.
- **Battery-powered designs** where efficiency (loss from ringing and copper resistance) matters.
:::

::: assumptions
The answer assumes a buck converter with an integrated or external FET pair on a 4-layer board with a ground plane on layer 2, running at hundreds of kHz to a few MHz. Boost, buck-boost, flyback and isolated converters have different hot loops (for a boost, the loop is output capacitor, switch and diode) but the principle is the same: identify the pulsed loops, the high-dV/dt node and the sensitive nodes. On a two-layer board the ground plane may be a bottom pour, and the loop inductance is higher. For very high currents, multiple phases and bus bars change the layout. If the datasheet gives a specific layout, it overrides generic rules.
:::

::: followups
### Which part of the buck layout is the most critical, and why?
The hot loop. Its inductance sets overshoot and ringing on the switch node, EMI, and stress on the FETs, and it is the only part that gets worse with every extra millimetre. Everything else is a refinement. That's why the input capacitor goes right at the pins.

### Where do you connect the feedback divider's bottom resistor?
To analog ground, a quiet point, joined to power ground near the IC's exposed pad. If it connected to the output capacitor's ground through a noisy return, switching currents would offset the reference seen by the loop. The top resistor connects to the output at the load side of the output capacitor.

### Can you put an inductor on the opposite side of the board from the IC?
You can, but the switch node then needs vias, which adds inductance, area on two layers and a noise path. If you must, use several vias and keep the plane intact, but usually the inductor goes on the same side as the IC, next to the SW pins.

### What changes on a two-layer board?
With no inner plane, the return path is a bottom pour. The hot loop on the top layer closes through the bottom via a via at each capacitor and the IC, so loop area is larger. I would keep the loop on one layer where possible, put the input capacitor between the VIN and PGND pins on the top layer, and add more ground vias.

### How would you test that your layout is good?
Probe the switch node with a short ground spring on a high-bandwidth scope and check for overshoot and ringing. Look at conducted and radiated emissions in pre-compliance. Measure output ripple with a tip-and-barrel probe at the output capacitor. Check thermal rise and the feedback-pin noise. Compare with the datasheet's expected waveforms.
:::

::: rubric
- Identifies the hot loop and puts it first :: It is the highest-priority layout item in any switcher.
- Treats the switch node as high dV/dt, small and short :: Directly tied to noise and radiation.
- Places the inductor and output capacitors with wide copper and short returns :: Completes the power stage.
- Routes feedback and compensation short, quiet and away from SW :: Prevents noise-induced regulation errors.
- Uses a solid ground plane, with PGND/AGND handled deliberately :: The foundation for the rest.
- Addresses thermal (exposed pad, copper, vias) and test access :: Practical completeness.
- References the datasheet layout :: Shows engineering pragmatism.
