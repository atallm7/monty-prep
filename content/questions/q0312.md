::: testing
Whether you know that "health" has several measurable dimensions and that you can name the practical tests: **capacity fade** (Ah now versus new), **internal resistance rise** (DC pulse or AC 1 kHz), **self-discharge**, **cycle count and history**, plus **physical signs** such as swelling. The interviewer also wants the context: end-of-life is usually defined at about 80 % of rated capacity, resistance matters for power capability and heating, and a cell can pass one test and fail another. A strong answer describes how a BMS tracks it in-system without a lab.
:::

::: why
Batteries age, and the consequences range from short runtime to fires. Users want a "battery health" figure, manufacturers want to decide whether to replace or reuse a pack (second-life storage), and safety engineers want to spot a cell that is about to fail. Because both capacity and resistance drive product behaviour (runtime and peak power), a good engineer measures both. This question connects test design ([[q0311]]) with the physics of aging ([[q0301]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacity fade**: loss of usable Ah from lithium inventory loss and active-material loss ([[q0311]]).
- **Internal resistance growth**: SEI thickening, contact loss and electrolyte degradation raise $R$ ([[q0303]]).
- **State of health (SoH)**: a normalized indicator, commonly $Q_{now}/Q_{rated}$ or $R_{EOL}/R_{now}$-style measures.
- **End of life (EOL)**: a defined threshold, often 80 % capacity (or a doubling of resistance), set by the application.
- **Self-discharge**: unusually high self-discharge flags internal micro-shorts.
- **Impedance spectroscopy**: frequency-dependent impedance that separates ohmic, interface and diffusion contributions.
- **Swelling and gas**: a physical sign of electrolyte decomposition ([[q0319]]).
:::

::: answer
"Health has a few dimensions, and I'd check them in this order.

First, capacity. I do a full charge, rest, and a controlled discharge at a low rate to the cutoff, integrating current, and compare to the rated value. State of health is capacity now over rated capacity, and a common end-of-life line is about 80 percent.

Second, internal resistance. A fresh 2 amp-hour cell might be 50 milliohms, and as it ages it climbs. I measure it with a DC pulse: take the rested voltage, apply a known current step, and the voltage change over the current is the resistance. A quick AC check at 1 kilohertz with an IR meter also works, though it captures mostly the ohmic part. Resistance matters because it limits peak power and creates heat, and it can age out of spec even when capacity still looks fine.

Third, I check self-discharge and imbalance: a cell that loses voltage faster than its siblings may have an internal short. In a pack, compare the cells' voltage and resistance, since the weakest one decides.

Fourth, I look at physical signs: swelling, warm spots, odd smell, voltage that sags under a normal load. A swollen cell is retired regardless of its numbers.

In a product, the fuel gauge does this continuously by tracking learned capacity and resistance, plus cycle count and temperature history, and reports state of health."
:::

::: technical
**Capacity-based SoH.** $SoH_Q = Q_{now}/Q_{rated}$ measured under standard conditions ([[q0311]]). EOL is frequently defined at 80 % (some applications use 70 %).

**Resistance-based SoH.** $R_{now}$ via a DC pulse: from rest at $V_0$, apply current $I$ for time $t_p$ and measure $V_1$:

$$R_{DC}(t_p) = \frac{V_0 - V_1}{I}$$

The result depends on $t_p$: the instantaneous drop (around 10 to 100 ms) gives mostly ohmic resistance $R_0$, and 10 to 30 s includes charge transfer and diffusion (adding $R_1$). Report the pulse time, SoC and temperature along with the value. The AC internal resistance at 1 kHz (IR meter) typically reads close to the ohmic part. A common EOL criterion is $R_{now} \approx 2\times R_{new}$, but it depends on the application's power demands.

**Example.** A 2 Ah cell, rested at 3.95 V, with a 4 A pulse. In a first-order model with $R_0 = 50$ mΩ, $R_1 = 30$ mΩ, $\tau = 40$ s, the voltage is 3.75 V immediately and 3.72 V after 10 s, which is $R_{DC,10\,s} = R_0 + R_1(1 - e^{-10/40}) = 56.6$ mΩ (a drop of 0.227 V from 3.95 V).

**Self-discharge test.** Charge to a set voltage, rest at constant temperature for days, and measure the voltage drop or the capacity loss. Abnormally fast loss means an internal micro-short.

**Pack-level.** Compare cell voltages at rest and under load, spread in resistance and capacity; the weakest cell sets the pack's usable capacity and safe limits ([[q0314]]).

**In-system estimation.** Gauges track $Q_{full}$ (from rests) and $R$ (from voltage response to load steps), and count cycles and time at high temperature/high SoC.

**Physical checks.** Thickness (swelling), temperature rise under standard load, damaged pouch, odor/electrolyte leakage, visible deformation. A swollen cell has gas from electrolyte decomposition and should be taken out of service.
:::

::: short
"I'd check three things. Capacity: charge fully, discharge slowly to cutoff, and compare to rated; state of health is the ratio, and about 80 percent is a typical end-of-life line. Internal resistance: apply a current pulse and take the voltage drop over the current, or use a 1 kHz meter; it rises with age and limits power. Then self-discharge, cell-to-cell mismatch and physical signs like swelling. In a product, a fuel gauge tracks learned capacity and resistance and reports state of health."
:::

::: long
"State of health is really two numbers, capacity and resistance, plus a few safety indicators.

For capacity, I'd run a controlled test: fully charge with constant current and constant voltage, rest, then discharge at a low rate like C over 5 to the cutoff voltage, integrating current. Dividing by the rated capacity gives the state of health. The usual end-of-life definition is 80 percent, but it depends on the application. Doing this properly takes hours, so in a product the fuel gauge estimates it between rested voltage points as the cell is used.

For internal resistance, I'd do a DC pulse. From a rested state, apply a known current, say 4 amps, and measure the voltage drop. If the voltage falls from 3.95 to 3.72 volts after ten seconds, the resistance is about 57 milliohms. I'd report the pulse length, SoC and temperature, because resistance depends on all of them. A quick check with a 1 kilohertz AC meter works too, though it mainly sees the ohmic part. A doubling of resistance is a common end-of-life sign, and it matters because high resistance means voltage sag, heating and loss of peak power, even if the Ah still looks fine.

Third, self-discharge: a cell that loses voltage faster than its neighbors might have an internal short, which is a safety issue. In a pack I compare cell voltages and resistances, since the weakest cell limits everything.

And finally physical inspection: swelling, hot spots, leaking electrolyte or a deformed pouch means retire the cell immediately, regardless of the numbers. In the product, the gauge combines learned capacity, resistance and usage history to display a battery health number."
:::

::: terms
- **State of health (SoH)**: normalized measure of a cell's condition versus new (capacity and/or resistance).
- **End of life (EOL)**: the threshold (commonly 80 % capacity or 2× resistance) at which the cell is retired from its application.
- **Capacity fade**: reduction of deliverable charge with cycles and time.
- **DCIR (DC internal resistance)**: resistance from a current-pulse voltage response.
- **ACIR (AC internal resistance)**: resistance measured by a small AC current at a fixed frequency, typically 1 kHz.
- **EIS (electrochemical impedance spectroscopy)**: impedance versus frequency.
- **Self-discharge**: loss of charge at rest.
- **Cycle count / throughput**: number of equivalent full cycles or total Ah delivered.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "measure capacity and compare to rated".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names **both capacity and resistance** and explains why each matters (runtime vs. power and heat).
- Gives a **pulse-test method** with numbers, and stresses reporting pulse length, SoC and temperature.
- Notes that **ACIR at 1 kHz** and DCIR measure different things.
- Mentions **self-discharge and cell mismatch** as safety indicators, and **physical inspection** as an immediate disqualifier.
- Explains how a **BMS estimates SoH in-system** without a lab, and the interplay of SoH with SoC accuracy ([[q0309]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Judging health by voltage.** A rested voltage of 4.2 V says the cell charges to the limit, not that it has capacity.
- **Using only capacity.** A cell can have 90 % capacity yet double resistance, and fail in a high-power application.
- **Quoting resistance without conditions.** DCIR changes with SoC, temperature and pulse length; compare like with like.
- **Testing a cold or freshly charged cell.** Thermal and polarization transients bias both capacity and resistance.
- **Re-using a swollen or dented cell** because "it still reads 3.7 V". Gas or internal damage is a safety problem.
- **Assuming 80 % is universal.** The EOL threshold comes from the application.
:::

::: deep
## What ages in a LiPo

Aging has two broad modes, which show up as two different measurements:

- **Loss of lithium inventory (LLI):** lithium consumed in side reactions (SEI growth on the anode, plating) is no longer available to shuttle, reducing capacity.
- **Loss of active material (LAM):** electrode particles crack, lose contact or dissolve, also reducing capacity and increasing resistance.
- **Impedance growth:** thicker SEI, lost contacts and electrolyte decomposition raise $R$.

Temperature, high SoC (high voltage), high current and deep cycling all accelerate these ([[q0301]], [[q0306]]). Cycle life figures such as "500 cycles to 80 %" depend on conditions and should be read from the datasheet.

## Capacity test

The test procedure is the same as in [[q0311]] and the SoH is the ratio:

$$SoH_Q = \frac{Q_{measured}}{Q_{rated}}\times 100\%$$

For a 2 Ah cell measured at 1.62 Ah at 0.2C: $SoH_Q = 81$ %, just above a typical 80 % line.

## Resistance test: the pulse

Resistance matters for power: the maximum deliverable current before the cutoff is reached is $I_{max} = (V_{OCV} - V_{cut})/R$. If $R$ doubles from 50 to 100 mΩ at $V_{OCV} = 3.7$ V and $V_{cut} = 3.0$ V, $I_{max}$ halves from 14 A to 7 A. Dissipated heat at a given current doubles.

A pulse test records the voltage response, which has a fast step (ohmic) and a slower relaxation (charge transfer, diffusion):

**Procedure:**
1. Rest the cell to a known SoC (say 50 %) and temperature (25 °C), record $V_0$.
2. Apply a constant-current pulse ($\le 1\text{C}$ to $2\text{C}$) for 10 s, logging at kHz rates.
3. Calculate $R_{0.1 s}$, $R_{10 s}$ from $(V_0 - V(t))/I$.
4. Compare to the baseline at the same SoC and temperature. Also take a rest recovery trace.

**AC method.** A 1 kHz meter drives a small AC current and reads the in-phase voltage; it is quick and good for sorting cells in production but sees mostly $R_0$.

## Self-discharge and internal shorts

A healthy Li-ion cell self-discharges a few percent per month at room temperature (varies by chemistry and temperature). If a cell's open-circuit voltage falls markedly faster than its siblings over days, suspect an internal micro-short, which is a safety defect. In production this "K-value" test (voltage drop over time) screens cells.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Quoted self-discharge rates (a few percent per month) are typical for new Li-ion cells at about 20 to 25 °C. They are higher with temperature and age, and specific limits come from the cell maker.

## Pack-level health

Series packs: compare each cell's resting voltage, resistance and capacity. A cell that drifts from the others in voltage under charge or discharge, or heats more, is the weak link. Imbalance that returns quickly after balancing implies a weak or high-self-discharge cell ([[q0314]]).

## In-system estimation

A fuel gauge estimates SoH continuously:

- **Capacity**: updated from rest-to-rest charge counting ([[q0311]]); reported as $Q_{full}/Q_{design}$.
- **Resistance**: estimated from the voltage response to known current steps (the same math as the pulse).
- **History**: cycle count, time spent hot or at 100 % SoC, deep discharges.

The result is shown as "battery health" or used to switch the device to a lower power mode when the battery's resistance is too high to sustain peak loads.

## Decision: retire or keep?

| Observation | Action |
|---|---|
| Capacity ≥ 80 %, $R$ < 1.5× new, no swelling | Healthy |
| Capacity 70 to 80 %, $R$ about 2× new | End of life for demanding use; may serve low-power, second-life use |
| Swelling, heat, leakage, or fast self-discharge | Retire and dispose safely ([[q0328]]) |
| One weak cell in a pack | Replace cell or pack; do not mix aged and new cells |
:::

::: applications
- **Smartphone and laptop "battery health" indicators** derived from the fuel gauge's learned capacity.
- **EV battery warranty and resale**: capacity retention (for example 70 % after N years) is checked with BMS data or a diagnostic cycle.
- **Drone and RC pilots**: IR meters and balance-lead checks to retire packs with high or uneven resistance.
- **Second-life storage**: used EV packs graded by capacity and resistance for stationary storage.
- **Production screening**: ACIR, OCV and self-discharge tests on incoming cells.
:::

::: assumptions
- The 80 % capacity and 2× resistance thresholds are **common conventions**. Each application and each manufacturer's warranty sets its own.
- Resistance values are quoted for a stated **SoC, temperature and pulse time**. Changing any of these changes the number.
- The pulse-response numbers use a **first-order equivalent-circuit model** with representative parameters; real cells show additional time constants.
- The answer is for **rechargeable Li-ion/LiPo**. NiMH and lead-acid health tests differ.
- If a cell shows **any physical damage**, the electrical numbers no longer matter: it should be removed.
:::

::: followups
### A 2 Ah cell reads 3.95 V at rest, then 3.72 V after 10 s at 4 A. What is its DCIR and is it healthy?
$R = (3.95 - 3.72)/4 = 57.5$ mΩ. Whether this is healthy depends on the new-cell baseline at the same SoC and temperature: if new is 50 mΩ, it is about 1.15× and fine; if new is 25 mΩ, it has already doubled and the cell is near end of life for power-demanding applications.

### Why can a cell have 90 % capacity and still be unsuitable?
Because its resistance may have doubled. At high current, the voltage sags to the cutoff early, heating increases ($I^2R$) and the peak power falls, so a drone or power tool may trip its low-voltage limit mid-maneuver even though a slow capacity test looks fine. Health needs both numbers.

### How do you tell a bad cell from the others in a series pack?
Charge/discharge the pack with balance monitoring: the weak cell usually hits the voltage limits first, shows a larger voltage sag under load, may heat more, and drifts from the others after balancing. Measure each cell's resistance (ACIR) and capacity if possible. If one cell is consistently the outlier, replace or retire the pack.

### What if the cell is swollen but passes the capacity test?
Retire it. Swelling is gas from electrolyte decomposition and indicates internal damage, such as overcharge, overheating, or aging. It can short or vent. Do not charge it. Dispose through proper channels ([[q0328]]).

### How would a BMS track SoH during use?
It tracks full-charge capacity by coulomb counting between rested OCV points and updates the stored value; it estimates resistance from the voltage response to current changes, and it logs cycles and temperature exposure. From those it reports an SoH percentage and may adjust power limits and SoC calculations as the cell ages.
:::

::: interpretations
"Health" is not a single standard quantity. Here it means **state of health (SoH)**: how the cell compares with new in capacity and in power capability (resistance), plus safety indicators. If the interviewer is thinking of **consumer devices** they likely want "capacity vs. rated and cycle count". If they mean **pack engineering**, add resistance, mismatch and self-discharge. Related: [[q0301]] (aging), [[q0303]] (internal resistance), [[q0311]] (capacity).
:::
