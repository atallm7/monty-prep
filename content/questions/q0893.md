::: basics
### In plain words
A number base is how many different digits a counting system uses. Decimal uses 10 digits, binary uses 2 (0 and 1), and hexadecimal (hex) uses 16 (0 to 9, then A to F). Converting between bases changes how a number is written, not how big it is. Hardware stores everything in binary, but people find hex shorter to read.

### Picture it
Suppose you must pay 13 cents. With coins worth 1, 10 and 100 you pay one 10 and three 1s, which is the decimal "13". With coins worth 1, 2, 4 and 8 you pay one 8, one 4 and one 1, which is binary 1101. The amount is the same, and only the coin set changes.

### Words you need
- **Base (radix)**: how many different digits the system has, such as 2, 10 or 16.
- **Place value**: the worth of a digit's position. In binary the places are 1, 2, 4, 8, and so on.
- **Bit and nibble**: a bit is one binary digit. A nibble is 4 bits, which is exactly one hex digit.
- **Hex digits A to F**: stand for 10 to 15, so a single character holds a value up to 15.

### The simplest example
Convert 175 to binary. Pick the largest place values that fit: 128 + 32 + 8 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 175. That puts a 1 in those places and a 0 in the others, which gives 1010 1111.

For hex, split the bits into groups of four from the right. 1010 is 10, written A, and 1111 is 15, written F. So 175 is 0xAF, and as a check, $10 \times 16 + 15 = 175$.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows conversion by repeated division, octal, fractions, how many bits a value needs, and negative numbers.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can **convert between binary, octal, decimal and hex fluently and by method**, out loud and without a calculator. The interviewer wants to see that you know positional notation (every digit is a coefficient times a power of the base), that binary to hex and binary to octal are just *grouping* (4 bits or 3 bits), that decimal to another base uses repeated division, and that you check your answer. Fast, calm, structured work is the real signal: a hardware engineer reads hex addresses, register values and bit masks all day.
:::

::: why
Every hardware interaction uses these bases: register maps and memory addresses are written in hex, bit fields are checked in binary, Unix permissions use octal, and sensors/ADCs report decimal counts that you convert to volts. Being able to turn 0xA5 into 1010 0101 in your head makes datasheet bit tables, logic analyzer traces and bit masking easy. It is also a calibration question: it is simple enough that interviewers use it to judge whether your fundamentals are automatic. Related topics that often follow are endianness ([[q0875]]) and MSB/LSB ([[q0876]]), plus two's complement for negative values.
:::

::: concepts
- **Positional notation**: a number $d_{n-1}\ldots d_1d_0$ in base $b$ has value $\sum d_i\,b^i$. See also bit significance in [[q0876]].
- **Binary, octal, decimal, hex**: bases 2, 8, 10 and 16 with digits 0-1, 0-7, 0-9 and 0-9 plus A-F.
- **Powers of 2**: $2^4=16$ and $2^3=8$ are why hex and octal map cleanly onto binary: one hex digit is exactly 4 bits, one octal digit exactly 3 bits.
- **Repeated division**: decimal to base $b$: divide by $b$, collect remainders (least significant first).
- **Repeated multiplication**: for fractions: multiply the fractional part by $b$ and collect the integer parts.
- **Prefixes**: `0b` binary, `0x` hex, and a leading `0` (or `0o`) for octal in many languages; subscripts such as $101_2$ in writing.
:::

::: answer
"Sure, give me a number. Let me say how I'd approach it in general, and then do an example.

Every base is positional: each digit is multiplied by a power of the base. To go to decimal I just add digit times power. For example hex 0xAF is 10 times 16 plus 15, which is 175.

To go from decimal to another base I divide repeatedly by the target base and read the remainders from bottom to top. 175 divided by 16 is 10 remainder 15, so that's A and F, 0xAF. For binary I'd divide by 2 repeatedly, or more quickly, write 175 as 128 plus 32 plus 8 plus 4 plus 2 plus 1, which is 1010 1111.

The shortcut between binary, hex and octal is grouping. Hex is four bits per digit, so I group the binary into nibbles from the right: 1010 is A, 1111 is F. Octal is three bits per digit, so I group by three from the right: 10 101 111 is 2, 5, 7, so 0o257.

So 175 decimal is 0xAF, binary 10101111, octal 257. I'd check by converting back: 2 times 64 plus 5 times 8 plus 7 is 128, 168, 175. Good.

Two things I always watch: pad the leftmost group with zeros, and don't mix up the group direction, it always starts from the binary point."
:::

::: technical
**Value of a numeral.** For base $b$ and digits $d_i$: $N=\sum_{i} d_i b^i$ (for fractional digits $i<0$). Example: $0xBEEF=11\cdot16^3+14\cdot16^2+14\cdot16+15=45056+3584+224+15=48879$.

**Any base to decimal**: expand the positional sum (Horner's form is quickest mentally: $((11\cdot16+14)\cdot16+14)\cdot16+15$).

**Decimal to base $b$ (integers)**: repeated division. Divide by $b$, record the remainder, repeat with the quotient until it is 0; read the remainders last to first.

Example $217_{10}\to$ binary: 217/2=108 r1; 108/2=54 r0; 54/2=27 r0; 27/2=13 r1; 13/2=6 r1; 6/2=3 r0; 3/2=1 r1; 1/2=0 r1. Reading upward: $11011001_2$. Check: $128+64+16+8+1=217$.

**Binary ↔ hex**: group in 4 bits from the binary point (pad with leading zeros). $1101\,1001=D9$.
**Binary ↔ octal**: group in 3 bits. $11\,011\,001=331_8$.
**Hex ↔ octal**: go through binary.

| Decimal | Binary | Octal | Hex |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0000 | 0 | 0 |
| 5 | 0101 | 5 | 5 |
| 9 | 1001 | 11 | 9 |
| 10 | 1010 | 12 | A |
| 15 | 1111 | 17 | F |
| 16 | 1 0000 | 20 | 10 |
| 175 | 1010 1111 | 257 | AF |
| 255 | 1111 1111 | 377 | FF |

**Fractions**: multiply the fractional part by the base and take the integer part each time. $0.6875\to.1011_2$ (it terminates because $0.6875=11/16$). A decimal fraction like $0.1$ does not terminate in binary: $0.1_{10}=0.000110011\overline{0011}\ldots_2$, which is why floating-point sums like $0.1+0.2$ are not exactly $0.3$.

**Bits needed**: $n$ bits represent $0\ldots2^n-1$; to store a value $V$ you need $\lceil\log_2(V+1)\rceil$ bits. $1000$ needs 10 bits ($2^{10}=1024$).

**Signed values**: in $n$-bit two's complement the range is $-2^{n-1}\ldots2^{n-1}-1$; the pattern for $-5$ in 8 bits is $1111\,1011$ (invert 0000 0101 and add 1).
:::

::: short
"Every base is positional, so to get to decimal I add digit times power of the base: 0xAF is 10 times 16 plus 15, which is 175. Going from decimal, I divide repeatedly by the target base and read the remainders bottom to top. For binary to hex or octal I just group the bits, 4 per hex digit and 3 per octal digit, starting from the right: 1010 1111 is AF, and 10 101 111 is 257 in octal. Then I check by converting back."
:::

::: long
"Whatever number I'm given, the framework is the same. All of these systems are positional: each digit is worth digit times base to the power of its position. So converting any base into decimal is a sum. Binary 1101 is 8 plus 4 plus 0 plus 1, 13. Hex 0xBEEF is 11 times 4096, plus 14 times 256, plus 14 times 16, plus 15, which gives 48879.

For converting from decimal, I use repeated division by the target base, collecting remainders and reading them from the last one up. To convert 217 to binary, I divide by 2 over and over and get 11011001. For hex I can divide by 16 instead: 217 over 16 is 13 remainder 9, so D9. Or, since I know my powers of two, I can subtract: 217 is 128 plus 64 plus 16 plus 8 plus 1.

The big trick is that binary, octal and hex are related by powers of two, so converting between them requires no arithmetic. A hex digit is exactly four bits and an octal digit exactly three. So to go from binary to hex I split the bits into groups of four from the right, padding the left with zeros, and write each group as a digit: 1101 1001 is D9. For octal I group by three: 11 011 001 is 3, 3, 1, so 331 octal. To go from hex to octal, I go through binary.

For fractions, I multiply the fractional part by 2 repeatedly and take the integer bit each time: 0.6875 gives .1011. Some decimals, like 0.1, repeat forever in binary, which is why floating-point has rounding error.

And I always sanity check: convert back, or check the parity of the last bit, and the number of bits needed, 2 to the n minus 1 is the max value for n bits. In hardware, I use hex for addresses and registers, binary for individual bit fields, and octal for things like Unix permissions."
:::

::: terms
- **Radix / base**: the number of distinct digits in a positional system (2, 8, 10, 16).
- **Positional notation**: the value of a digit depends on its position, as digit times base to the power of the position.
- **Bit / nibble / byte**: 1 binary digit; 4 bits (one hex digit); 8 bits (two hex digits).
- **MSB / LSB**: most/least significant bit; the leftmost and rightmost digit positions ([[q0876]]).
- **Remainder method**: repeated division by the target base; remainders give the digits least-significant first.
- **Grouping method**: converting binary to hex/octal by splitting into 4-bit/3-bit groups.
- **Two's complement**: the usual signed integer encoding; negate by inverting bits and adding 1.
- **Hex prefix 0x / binary prefix 0b**: language notations that mark the base of a literal.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** converts the number given, perhaps using a calculator-like approach, and gets there slowly.

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the **method before the arithmetic**: positional sum, repeated division, 4-bit/3-bit grouping.
- Uses **binary as the pivot** between hex and octal and never does decimal arithmetic more than needed.
- Knows the small table (0-F in binary) and powers of two (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 1024) cold.
- **Checks the result** by converting back or by a quick parity/range check.
- Handles **fractions and negative numbers** if asked, and mentions practical uses (register maps, permissions, color codes) and the 0.1 non-terminating case.
- Notes whether the number is signed and how many bits are in play, and writes leading zeros to show the width.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Grouping from the left.** Binary-to-hex and binary-to-octal grouping always starts at the binary point (the right for integers). Group from the left and the digits come out wrong: 10 1111 is 0x2F, not 0xBC.
- **Reading remainders in the wrong order.** Repeated division gives the *least* significant digit first; reverse the list.
- **Forgetting that hex letters are 10-15.** A=10, B=11, C=12, D=13, E=14, F=15; mixing up B and 8 or D and 0 on paper is common.
- **Treating a leading zero as decimal.** In C, `010` is octal 8, not 10. Use `0x` or `0b` (a compiler extension before C23, standard in C23 and C++14) deliberately.
- **Dropping leading zeros when the width matters.** 0b101 stored in 8 bits is 0000 0101. Width matters for sign extension and for bit-field alignment.
- **Assuming every decimal fraction terminates in binary.** Only fractions whose denominator is a power of two do (0.5, 0.25, 0.6875). 0.1 does not.
:::

::: deep
## The idea: positional notation

Write the decimal number 4,205. It means $4\cdot10^3+2\cdot10^2+0\cdot10^1+5\cdot10^0$. Every digit is a coefficient, every position is a power of the base, and "10" is just the base. Change the base and everything else stays the same:

$$N=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} d_i\,b^i,\qquad 0\le d_i<b$$

- Binary ($b=2$): digits 0, 1. $1011_2=8+0+2+1=11$.
- Octal ($b=8$): digits 0-7. $257_8=2\cdot64+5\cdot8+7=175$.
- Decimal ($b=10$).
- Hex ($b=16$): digits 0-9, A-F. $0xAF=10\cdot16+15=175$.

All four describe the *same quantity*; only the notation differs. That is why the rules are so mechanical.

## Any base to decimal

Expand and add. Horner's rule is the quickest mental way: start with the leading digit and repeat "multiply by the base, add the next digit".

Example: $0xBEEF$. B=11, E=14, E=14, F=15.

$$((11\cdot16+14)\cdot16+14)\cdot16+15 = ((190)\cdot16+14)\cdot16+15=(3054)\cdot16+15=48879$$

Powers of 2 are worth knowing cold: $2^4=16$, $2^8=256$, $2^{10}=1024$, $2^{12}=4096$, $2^{16}=65{,}536$, $2^{20}=1{,}048{,}576$, $2^{32}=4{,}294{,}967{,}296$.

## Decimal to any base: repeated division

To write $N$ in base $b$: divide by $b$, the remainder is the lowest digit; divide the quotient again; stop at a quotient of 0.

Example, $217_{10}$ to binary:

| Division | Quotient | Remainder (digit) |
|---|---|---|
| 217 / 2 | 108 | 1 (LSB) |
| 108 / 2 | 54 | 0 |
| 54 / 2 | 27 | 0 |
| 27 / 2 | 13 | 1 |
| 13 / 2 | 6 | 1 |
| 6 / 2 | 3 | 0 |
| 3 / 2 | 1 | 1 |
| 1 / 2 | 0 | 1 (MSB) |

Read the remainders from the bottom up: $11011001_2$. Check: $128+64+16+8+1=217$. 

For hex, the same method with 16: $217/16=13$ remainder $9$, then $13/16=0$ remainder $13$, so digits D, 9: $0xD9$. For octal: $217/8=27$ r1, $27/8=3$ r3, $3/8=0$ r3, so $331_8$.

**A faster mental method for binary: subtract powers of two.** For 175: the biggest power of 2 not exceeding it is 128, remainder 47; then 32, remainder 15; then 8, 4, 2, 1. So the set bits are 128, 32, 8, 4, 2, 1, which is $1010\,1111$. Putting a 1 in positions 7, 5, 3, 2, 1, 0 and 0 elsewhere.

## The pivot: binary, hex and octal are one family

Because $16=2^4$ and $8=2^3$, one hex digit corresponds to exactly four bits and one octal digit to exactly three. No arithmetic is needed: **group and look up**.

## Fractions

For the fractional part, **multiply by the base** and peel off the integer part each time.

$0.6875\to$ ×2 = 1.375 (digit 1), 0.375×2 = 0.75 (0), 0.75×2 = 1.5 (1), 0.5×2 = 1.0 (1): digits $1011$, so $0.6875=0.1011_2$. Check: $1/2+1/8+1/16=0.6875$ ✓.

Now try $0.1$: ×2 = 0.2 (0), 0.4 (0), 0.8 (0), 1.6 (1), then 0.6×2 = 1.2 (1), 0.2×2 = 0.4 (0), 0.8 (0), 1.6 (1) ... and the pattern 0011 repeats forever: $0.1_{10}=0.0\overline{0011}_2$. A computer must round it, which is why `0.1 + 0.2` is `0.30000000000000004` in double-precision floating point. In general a fraction terminates in base $b$ only if its denominator's prime factors divide $b$; $1/10$ has a factor of 5, which does not divide 2.

## How many bits? And negative numbers

An $n$-bit unsigned field holds $0$ to $2^n-1$: 8 bits go to 255, 16 bits to 65,535. For a given value $V$ you need $\lceil\log_2(V+1)\rceil$ bits: 1000 needs 10 bits, and 1,000,000 needs 20. For hex, each digit adds 4 bits, so a 32-bit register is 8 hex digits.

For signed numbers the standard is **two's complement**: to negate, invert all bits and add 1. $-5$ in 8 bits: $0000\,0101\to1111\,1010\to1111\,1011=0xFB$. The range is $-2^{n-1}$ to $2^{n-1}-1$, i.e. $-128\ldots127$ for 8 bits. The same bit pattern $0xFB$ is 251 unsigned or $-5$ signed, so you must always know which interpretation is in use.

## Common-sense checks

1. **Last digit parity**: a binary number is odd iff its last bit is 1; a hex number is odd iff its last digit is odd.
2. **Range**: $n$ bits cannot hold more than $2^n-1$. If a conversion needs more bits than the field has, you have an error (or overflow).
3. **Round trip**: convert back and compare.
4. **Digit validity**: octal has no 8 or 9; binary only 0 and 1; hex goes to F.

## Where it shows up

- **Register maps and addresses** are quoted in hex because they pack 4 bits per digit; `0x4002 0000` is easier to read than 1073872896.
- **Bit masks**: `0x0F` selects the low nibble, `0x80` the MSB of a byte ([[q0876]]).
- **Colors**: `#FF8800` is three bytes, 255, 136, 0.
- **Unix permissions**: octal 644, 755.
- **Memory layout and endianness**: the byte order of the 0xBEEF in memory depends on the machine ([[q0875]]).
- **ADC codes**: a 12-bit code of 0xFFF is 4095; with a 3.3 V reference, the code 0x800 (2048) is 1.65 V.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Say the method as you go: "I'll go to binary first, then regroup." Interviewers care that your process is reliable, and a slow but correct answer with a check beats a fast wrong one. If you are asked a number you can't do instantly, pivot through binary.
:::

::: applications
- **Reading datasheet register maps**: converting 0x2C into the bit pattern to see which enable bits are set.
- **Embedded debugging**: interpreting memory dumps, stack addresses and bitfields from a debugger or logic analyzer in hex.
- **Setting bit masks and flags** in C: `REG |= 0x40;` sets bit 6.
- **ADC/DAC code conversion**: turning a raw 12-bit code into volts ($V=\text{code}\cdot V_{ref}/4096$).
- **Networking and colors**: MAC addresses and IPv6 are hex; HTML/CSS colors are 3 hex bytes.
- **File permissions** on Linux: `chmod 755` is octal for rwxr-xr-x.
:::

::: assumptions
- I treated the numbers as **non-negative integers** unless stated; for signed values the interpretation (two's complement vs sign-magnitude) changes what a bit pattern means.
- I assumed **common notation**: 0x for hex, 0b for binary, a leading 0 or 0o for octal. Languages differ; check the one you use.
- "Some binary/hex/oct/decimal number" is open: I picked 175, 217 and 0xBEEF as examples. The method does not depend on the number.
- **Fractions** assume a fixed-point notation, not IEEE 754 floating point; the floating-point layout (sign, exponent, mantissa) is a separate topic.
- Mental conversions assume you know powers of two to $2^{10}$; if you do not, use repeated division, which always works.
:::

::: followups
### Convert 0xDEAD to decimal.
$13\cdot4096+14\cdot256+10\cdot16+13=53248+3584+160+13=57005$. Using Horner: $((13\cdot16+14)\cdot16+10)\cdot16+13=57005$. In binary: $1101\,1110\,1010\,1101$.

### Convert 100 decimal to binary, octal and hex.
Binary: 100 = 64+32+4 = $0110\,0100$. Hex: $100/16=6$ r4, so $0x64$. Octal: $100/8=12$ r4, $12/8=1$ r4, $1/8=0$ r1, so $144_8$. Check: $64+32+4=100$ and $1\cdot64+4\cdot8+4=100$.

### How many bits do you need to represent 0 to 1000? How many hex digits?
$\lceil\log_2 1001\rceil=10$ bits ($2^{10}=1024$). As hex, 1000 = 0x3E8, which is 3 hex digits (12 bits, with the top two unused). In practice you would use a 16-bit variable.

### Convert $0.8125$ to binary.
×2 = 1.625 (1); 0.625×2 = 1.25 (1); 0.25×2 = 0.5 (0); 0.5×2 = 1.0 (1). So $0.1101_2$. Check: $1/2+1/4+1/16=0.8125$. It terminates because $0.8125=13/16$.

### A register reads 0xF4240. What is that in decimal and how many bits?
$15\cdot65536+4\cdot4096+2\cdot256+4\cdot16+0=983040+16384+512+64=1{,}000{,}000$. So 0xF4240 = one million, needing 20 bits (5 hex digits). Interviewers sometimes use round decimal values like this to see if you can spot them.

### Harder: why does 0.1 + 0.2 not equal 0.3 on a computer, in terms of number bases?
$0.1$ and $0.2$ have no finite binary expansion (their denominators contain the factor 5), so a binary floating-point format stores the nearest representable values, each with a small rounding error. Their sum is slightly off from the stored value of 0.3, giving 0.30000000000000004 in double precision. Fixes: compare with a tolerance, or use integers (for example work in millivolts or cents) or decimal arithmetic.
:::

::: interpretations
The question is open-ended ("some number to a different base"), so the interviewer will pick the number. Expect: decimal to binary/hex, hex to binary, or binary to octal; possibly a fraction or a negative number. I provided the full method for each and worked examples. If the interviewer asks "convert it to base 3" or another unusual base, the same repeated-division method applies (divide by 3). The related questions are [[q0875]] (byte order of a multi-byte value) and [[q0876]] (which bit is most/least significant).
