::: basics
### In plain words
PVT stands for process, voltage, and temperature. The same buck converter behaves differently from one unit to the next, across its input and load range, and from cold to hot. This page is about finding the worst case in each of these and making sure the design still works there. You design with margins, then check the corners on the bench.

### Picture it
Imagine a bridge that must stay safe in summer heat, winter cold, and rush-hour traffic, and also if the steel is a bit weaker than the spec. You do not check only the average case. You check the worst combinations. The bridge picture does not show that for a converter, the worst case differs by quantity: ripple is worst at high input, and heat is worst at high input, full load, and hot.

### Words you need
- **Process**: unit-to-unit variation in parts, such as ±1% on a reference or ±20% on an inductor.
- **Voltage**: the range of input voltage and load that the converter sees.
- **Temperature**: the operating range, which changes resistance and ratings.
- **Corner**: an extreme combination of these.
- **Tolerance stack-up**: adding up the worst-case errors from several parts.

### The simplest example
Take an inductor with a DCR of 50 mΩ at 25 °C. Copper resistance rises about 0.39% per °C, so at 105 °C it rises by $80\times0.39\%\approx31\%$, to about 66 mΩ. At 3 A, the loss goes from $3^2\times0.05=0.45$ W to about 0.59 W. So the hot corner has more loss, which makes it even hotter.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page goes through process, voltage, and temperature effects in turn, and shows how they combine into the worst cases for accuracy, loop stability, and heat.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can take the chip-design idea of **process, voltage and temperature corners** and apply it to a power converter: which parameters spread with process (reference, oscillator, current limit, FET resistance, passive tolerances), what changes with voltage (input, output, load: duty, ripple, on-time, losses) and what changes with temperature (copper, silicon, magnetics, ceramics). The interviewer wants direction and rough size of each effect, the worst corner for each metric (efficiency, accuracy, ripple, stability, thermal), and how you verify and design margin.
:::

::: why
A buck that works on a nominal bench unit can fail in production or in the field at a corner: reference and divider tolerance push the rail out of spec, a hot inductor saturates, a slow oscillator raises ripple, cold electrolytics change the loop. PVT thinking turns "it works" into "it works across everything it will see", and it is the same language digital and IC teams use ([[q0267]] is the matching test plan).
:::

::: concepts
- **Process**: unit-to-unit variation of IC parameters (reference, oscillator, FET $R_{DS(on)}$, limits) and tolerance of external parts.
- **Voltage**: $V_{IN}$ range, $V_{OUT}$, load current; duty, ripple, on-time, switching loss all depend on them ([[q0222]], [[q0257]]).
- **Temperature**: copper +0.393%/°C, silicon $R_{DS(on)}$ rising with temperature, magnetics, ceramic and electrolytic capacitor changes ([[q0259]], [[q0268]]).
- **Corner analysis**: evaluate worst-case combinations, not just typical.
- **Accuracy stack-up**: reference + divider + load/line regulation ([[q0262]]).
- **Stability across corners** ([[q0261]]).
- **Thermal feedback**: losses heat the part, which raises losses.
:::

::: answer
"Process is the unit-to-unit spread: the IC's reference might be ±1% or so, the oscillator ±10%, the current limit ±20%, FET resistance varies, and the inductor and capacitors have their own tolerances, say ±20% on L and a lot more on a derated ceramic. Voltage is the input range, the output and the load. A wide input changes the duty cycle: at maximum input the duty is smallest, ripple current is largest, switching loss is highest and the on-time is shortest, which can hit the minimum on-time. Temperature raises resistance: copper DCR goes up 0.39% per °C and FET $R_{DS(on)}$ rises about half a percent per °C, so efficiency drops when hot, and that heats the part further. Hot ferrite inductors saturate at lower current, ceramics change capacitance, cold electrolytics have much higher ESR.

For the output accuracy the stack-up is reference, divider tolerance and regulation, giving around ±2.5% in my 1% example. For the loop, the worst case is usually low effective capacitance at light load, and for thermal it's maximum input, full load, hot. I'd verify at corners on the bench and design margins: current limit above the load including tolerance, saturation above the maximum limit hot, ripple within spec at maximum input with the oscillator and inductor at their tolerance extremes."
:::

::: technical
**Process (unit-to-unit).** IC: $V_{REF}$ (±0.5 to ±2%), $f_{SW}$ (±10 to 15%), current limit (±15 to 30%), $R_{DS(on)}$ (±20 to 30%), UVLO/EN thresholds (±3 to 5%), soft-start time. External: L (±20 to 30%), C (±10 to 20% plus DC-bias derating), resistors (±1%), sense DCR.

**Voltage.** $D = V_{OUT}/V_{IN}$; $\Delta I = V_{OUT}(1-D)/(Lf_{SW})$ max at $V_{IN(max)}$; $t_{on} = D/f_{SW}$ min at $V_{IN(max)}$; switching loss $\propto V_{IN} f_{SW}$; conduction loss $\propto I^2R$ (max load); minimum $V_{IN}$ sets maximum duty/dropout and UVLO; also light load changes mode (CCM/DCM/PFM).

**Temperature.** $DCR(T) = DCR_{25}[1 + 0.00393(T-25)]$; $R_{DS(on)}$ rises (order of +0.4 to +0.7%/°C for silicon, device-dependent); $V_{REF}$ drifts tens to hundreds of ppm/°C; $f_{SW}$ drifts; ferrite $I_{sat}$ falls hot; MLCC capacitance moves with temperature (class II) and electrolytic ESR rises greatly cold; leakage and quiescent current rise hot; thermal shutdown ~150 °C class.

**Worst corners by metric.**

| Metric | Worst corner |
|---|---|
| Output accuracy | Reference and divider at opposite extremes, plus load/line regulation |
| Ripple | Max $V_{IN}$, low $f_{SW}$, low L, low effective C, (cold electrolytic ESR) |
| Efficiency / heat | Max load, hot, slow/high-$R_{DS(on)}$ silicon; max $V_{IN}$ for switching loss |
| Min on-time / duty | Max $V_{IN}$, fast oscillator; min $V_{IN}$ for max duty |
| Saturation / current limit | Hot, high limit tolerance, low L |
| Loop stability | Light load (high $Q$), low effective C, extremes of crossover |
| Start-up | Cold, max C, min current limit |
:::

::: short
"Process is part-to-part spread: reference, oscillator, current limit, FET resistance, and L and C tolerance. Voltage is input range, output and load: at high input, duty is smallest, ripple and switching loss are highest and on-time is shortest. Temperature raises DCR and $R_{DS(on)}$ so efficiency falls when hot, ferrite saturates earlier, ceramic capacitance and electrolytic ESR shift. I'd find the worst corner for each metric, stack up tolerances for accuracy, and verify and margin across corners."
:::

::: long
"I'll take the three letters in turn for a 12 V to 3.3 V, 3 A buck.

Process: the IC's reference is maybe ±1%, switching frequency ±10%, current limit ±20 to 30%, and the FET resistances vary. My external parts have tolerances too: inductor ±20%, capacitors with derating and tolerance, resistors ±1%. These combine. For accuracy, the reference and divider at opposite extremes give about ±2.5% on the output in my example, before regulation error.

Voltage: with the input swinging from 9 to 18 V the duty goes from 37% to 18%. At 18 V, ripple current is largest, switching loss is highest, and on-time is shortest, which might approach the minimum on-time. At 9 V, I'm closer to maximum duty and UVLO. Load changes the mode: light load goes DCM or PFM and efficiency, ripple and the loop plant all change.

Temperature: copper resistance climbs 0.39% per degree, so inductor DCR is about 31% higher at 105 °C; FET resistance rises maybe 40% over the same swing; so efficiency falls, and that dissipation raises temperature further. A hot ferrite saturates at lower current, ceramic capacitance changes, electrolytic ESR gets large when cold, thermal shutdown is the limit.

So I look for the worst corner per metric: ripple at maximum input and low inductance, heat at hot and full load, stability at light load and low effective capacitance, start-up cold with maximum capacitance. I'd verify those at the bench with tolerance variants and leave margin."
:::

::: terms
- **PVT**: process, voltage, temperature variations used to define corners.
- **Corner**: combination of extremes (e.g. slow/hot/low-$V_{IN}$).
- **Tolerance stack-up**: worst-case combination of independent tolerances.
- **Temperature coefficient (tempco)**: fractional change per °C (e.g. copper +0.393%/°C).
- **Thermal feedback**: loss raises temperature, which raises resistance and loss.
- **Line / load regulation**: output change vs input / load.
- **Derating**: reduced capacitance with bias; also safe-operating derating.
- **Guardband**: margin kept between measured worst case and spec.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "efficiency drops at high temperature and the output varies with input".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates **process** (unit spread and tolerances), **voltage** (input, output, load) and **temperature** and gives direction and magnitude for each.
- Identifies **worst corners per metric** rather than a single "worst case".
- Computes a **stack-up** (output accuracy ±2.5% for 1% parts) and a **temperature efficiency** swing.
- Notes **coupled effects**: thermal feedback, hot ferrite saturation, cold electrolytic ESR, load-dependent loop $Q$.
- Mentions verifying with **corner tests and multiple units** and leaving guardband.
- Flags that tempco numbers vary by device.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating PVT as only temperature.** Process spread and voltage range change duty, ripple and stability as much.
- **Assuming the worst corner is the same for every metric.** Ripple is worst at high $V_{IN}$; heat at hot and full load; stability at light load.
- **Using nominal L and C.** ±20% inductance and derated capacitance move ripple and loop crossover.
- **Ignoring thermal feedback.** Hot parts lose more, which makes them hotter.
- **Assuming 1% parts give a 1% output.** Reference and divider errors add.
- **Forgetting cold.** Electrolytic ESR, start-up current and reference drift are worse cold.
:::

::: deep
## Process: spread you cannot trim away

*Inside the IC* the reference is trimmed but still carries a tolerance (a ±1% class reference is common; ±0.5% parts exist). The oscillator or on-timer varies by ±10% or more, the current limit by ±20% or more because it depends on $R_{DS(on)}$, sense ratio and comparator offset, and the FET resistances vary by tens of percent. These parameters are rarely independent, and the datasheet's min/max columns already cover the process and temperature range, which is why you design to the *min/max* column, not the typical.

*Outside the IC* the inductor value ($\pm20$% typical, with further drop towards $I_{sat}$), capacitors (nominal tolerance, DC-bias derating, temperature, aging) and resistors (1%) add their own spread.

**Output accuracy stack-up.** With $V_{OUT} = V_{REF}(1+R_1/R_2)$, $V_{REF} = 0.8$ V ±1%, $R_1 = 31.6$ kΩ and $R_2 = 10$ kΩ both ±1%:
- High corner: $0.8(1.01)\,(1 + 31.6(1.01)/(10(0.99))) = 3.413$ V.
- Low corner: $0.8(0.99)\,(1 + 31.6(0.99)/(10(1.01))) = 3.245$ V.
Nominal 3.328 V, so about ±2.5% around it (+3.4%/−1.7% relative to exactly 3.3 V because the 1% resistor series gives 3.328, not 3.300). If the load tolerance is ±5%, load/line regulation (0.1 to 0.4%), temperature drift and ripple (±1%) must also fit: it works but uses most of the budget.

**Frequency spread.** At $f_{SW}$ = 500 kHz ±10% and L = 6.8 µH ±20%, worst-case ripple at 18 V (low frequency, low L): $3.3\times0.817/(450\text{k}\times5.44\mu) = 1.10$ A, versus the nominal 0.79 A, that's 40% more, and peak current rises from 3.4 to 3.55 A ([[q0258]]). At the other extreme (fast, high L) ripple is smaller than nominal, which can push light-load DCM boundaries around. A fast oscillator (550 kHz) at 18 V gives $t_{on} = 0.183/550\text{k} = 333$ ns, which must exceed the minimum on-time with margin ([[q0257]]).

- **Duty cycle** from 0.37 (9 V) to 0.18 (18 V) sets minimum on-time and maximum duty margins ([[q0222]]).
- **Switching loss** scales with $V_{IN}$ and $f_{SW}$: in the example it's about 0.09 W at 12 V, 0.135 W at 18 V.
- **Output voltage** changes the duty and the loop plant (and $V_{FB}$ divider ratio) for adjustable parts.
- **Load:** conduction loss goes as $I^2$; light load changes mode (CCM to DCM, PFM) so ripple, frequency and plant all change; the loop $Q$ rises at light load ([[q0261]]).

## Temperature: what moves and how far

| Parameter | Tempco / behaviour | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Copper DCR | +0.393%/°C | 15 mΩ → 19.7 mΩ at 105 °C |
| FET $R_{DS(on)}$ | roughly +0.4 to +0.7%/°C (device dependent) | +40% at 105 °C using 0.5%/°C |
| $V_{REF}$ | tens to ~100 ppm/°C typical class | ~0.5 to 1% over a −40 to 125 °C range |
| $f_{SW}$ | a few %, vendor dependent | ripple, on-time |
| Ferrite $I_{sat}$ | falls hot | saturation margin shrinks |
| MLCC | class II: capacitance moves ±15% (X7R) to −22% (X5R) over temperature ranges (class definition) | loop crossover, ripple |
| Electrolytic ESR | several times higher at −40 °C | ripple, loop zero |
| Leakage/quiescent | rise hot | light-load efficiency |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $R_{DS(on)}$ and reference tempco values are device-specific. The 0.5%/°C figure is a mid-range illustration; read the normalized $R_{DS(on)}$-vs-temperature curve and the reference's drift specification for the real part. The MLCC class limits quoted (X7R ±15%, X5R ±22% over rated range) are from the EIA classification and exclude DC-bias effects.

Figure: Efficiency falls by about 3 points from −40 to 125 °C, because resistive losses grow while switching losses stay roughly constant. The effect is small in efficiency terms but about 0.33 W more heat between the extremes, which raises temperature in turn: thermal feedback. The FET term uses the duty-weighted average $0.275\times40 + 0.725\times30 = 32.75$ mΩ and a fixed 0.17 W for switching, gate, dead-time and quiescent loss (the [[q0263]] budget).

Thermal feedback loop: losses 0.72 W at 85 °C with $\theta_{JA}$ of 40 °C/W on the IC's share (about 0.45 W) gives a junction rise of about 18 °C; if the rise raises the IC's loss by 6%, the extra loss gives about 1 °C more, which converges. With poor $\theta_{JA}$ (say 100 °C/W) the loop gain is larger and can run away to thermal shutdown.

## Stability across PVT

Crossover and phase margin move with: effective $C_{OUT}$ (DC bias, temperature, tolerance, aging), L tolerance and saturation, load ($Q$ and the current-mode pole $1/(2\pi R_LC)$), $V_{IN}$ (voltage-mode modulator gain $V_{IN}/V_p$ changes loop gain), and the IC's error-amp transconductance and oscillator (process). Typical validation: measure loop gain at (min C, max load), (min C, light load), (max C, light load) and at both $V_{IN}$ extremes, hot and cold ([[q0261]], [[q0267]]).

## Making the design robust

- **Design to datasheet min/max**, not typical: current limit minimum above the load; limit maximum below inductor $I_{sat}$ (hot).
- **Margin:** ripple, on-time, accuracy, thermal and phase margin all with tolerance and derating; leave guardband (e.g. 20%).
- **Reduce sensitivity:** input-voltage feed-forward in voltage mode, accurate references, 0.5% divider resistors if accuracy matters.
- **Validate:** corner matrix and multiple units ([[q0267]]), end-of-life values ([[q0268]]).

::: assumptions
- Example numbers are illustrative: ±1% reference, ±10% oscillator, ±20% inductor, +0.5%/°C $R_{DS(on)}$.
- Process variation in the IC is treated as the datasheet min/max; designers can't see inside the distribution.
- Corners are independent bounding cases; a statistical (Monte Carlo) analysis is less pessimistic but needs distributions.
- A synchronous, current-mode, integrated-FET converter is assumed; other control modes change the sensitive parameters.
- Temperature is part temperature (junction or component), not ambient; self-heating links them.

::: followups
### Which corner gives the highest inductor peak current?
Maximum $V_{IN}$ (largest ripple), minimum L and minimum $f_{SW}$ (largest ripple), maximum load, and maximum current-limit tolerance in a fault. In the example the peak is $3 + 1.10/2 = 3.55$ A under normal operation, and up to the IC's maximum limit in faults, which is what $I_{sat}$ at hot must exceed ([[q0259]]).

### How much does output accuracy degrade with temperature?
The reference drifts (maybe 0.5 to 1% over −40 to 125 °C for a typical buck reference; datasheet specifies), resistors drift (25 to 100 ppm/°C for 1% parts adds about 0.1 to 0.3% over the span), and load regulation changes slightly. Add those to the ±2.5% initial stack-up: about ±3 to 3.5% total. If that exceeds the load's tolerance, use a tighter reference and 0.1 to 0.5% resistors.

### Why can a converter be stable on the bench and unstable in the field?
The bench is nominal room temperature with fresh parts. In the field: low effective capacitance (DC bias, cold or aged), light loads (higher $Q$), $V_{IN}$ extremes (modulator gain), and temperature changes in the error amp. Validate loop gain across corners.

### Scenario: efficiency measured at 25 °C is 94.3% but the product overheats at 70 °C ambient. What do you check?
Hotter parts have higher resistance: at 105 °C the model gives about 92.8% (0.76 W). Check actual $\theta_{JA}$ in the enclosure, whether thermal feedback is converging (measure IC temperature vs time), the inductor's temperature and $I_{sat}$, and the capacitor temperatures. Improve copper/airflow, lower $f_{SW}$ or choose lower-$R_{DS(on)}$ parts.

### How do you decide between worst-case and statistical (Monte Carlo) analysis?
Worst-case guarantees every unit but may be over-conservative (all tolerances simultaneously at their extreme is improbable). Statistical analysis estimates the yield for a given spec. For safety-critical or large-volume products use both: worst-case for hard limits (ratings, saturation, voltage), statistical for yield-driven parameters like accuracy.
:::

::: interpretations
PVT is chip-design vocabulary; for a buck I mapped "process" to part-to-part (IC and component) variation, "voltage" to input, output and load conditions, and "temperature" to ambient and self-heating. If the interviewer is thinking of the IC's silicon process corners specifically (slow/typical/fast), that corresponds to the IC parameters in the process section.
