::: basics
### In plain words
Doping means adding a tiny, controlled amount of another element to a semiconductor on purpose. Pure silicon barely conducts. A few impurity atoms give it many more free carriers, and they set the carrier type: electrons for N-type or holes for P-type. Doping is how chip makers build every diode, transistor and resistor on a chip.

### Picture it
Think of a nearly empty dance floor with only a handful of dancers. Adding a few more dancers makes it far livelier, and a small number of extra dancers has a big effect. Dopant atoms are those extra dancers. You do not change the room, only invite a few guests. The analogy is loose: the dopants also set whether the new carriers are electrons or holes.

### Words you need
- **Dopant**: the impurity atom added on purpose. See [[q0067]].
- **Carrier**: a free electron or hole that can move and carry current. See [[q0061]].
- **N-type and P-type**: silicon with extra electrons, or extra holes. See [[q0062]].
- **Intrinsic**: pure silicon with no doping.
- **Ion implantation**: firing electrically charged dopant atoms into the silicon slice (wafer), then heating it to repair the damage.

### The simplest example
Silicon has about $5\times10^{22}$ atoms per cubic centimeter (cm³). Add just one phosphorus atom per million silicon atoms. That is $5\times10^{16}$ dopant atoms per cm³, each giving one free electron. Pure silicon has only about $10^{10}$ free carriers per cm³, so doping raises the number about five million times. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the range of doping levels, the resistivity they give, and how implantation and diffusion put the atoms in.
:::

::: why
Every transistor, diode and resistor on an IC is made by doping silicon in precise patterns. Doping is how a flat wafer becomes N-wells, P-wells, sources, drains, bases and emitters. Understanding the idea explains threshold-voltage adjustment, why a MOSFET has N+ source and drain regions, why resistors on a chip are made with doped regions and why leakage and breakdown depend on doping profile. It's an essential first question for any semiconductor interview, and it sets up [[q0066]] (why doping helps) and [[q0067]] (which elements).
:::

::: concepts
- **Intrinsic vs extrinsic semiconductor**: pure silicon has few carriers ($\approx10^{10}$ cm⁻³); doped silicon has a carrier density you choose. See [[q0062]].
- **Donors and acceptors**: Group V atoms donate electrons (N-type), Group III atoms accept them to make holes (P-type). See [[q0067]].
- **Doping concentration**: atoms of dopant per cm³, typically $10^{14}$ to $10^{20}$ cm⁻³; silicon has $5\times10^{22}$ atoms/cm³.
- **Conductivity**: $\sigma=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$, so carrier density and mobility both matter. See [[q0068]].
- **Ion implantation and diffusion**: the two standard ways to introduce dopants into a wafer.
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** Doping is the intentional introduction of impurity atoms into a semiconductor crystal to control carrier type ($n$ or $p$) and density.

**Mechanism.** A substitutional Group V atom (P, As, Sb) in silicon has one extra valence electron. Its binding energy to the ion is about 45 meV for phosphorus, so at 300 K it is nearly fully ionized and adds a free electron: $n\approx N_D$. A substitutional Group III atom (B) accepts a bond electron from the lattice, ionizing at about 45 meV and leaving a free hole: $p\approx N_A$.

**Carrier density and conductivity.** In N-type, $n\approx N_D$ and $p=n_i^2/n$. Conductivity $\sigma=q\mu_nn$ and resistivity $\rho=1/\sigma$. For $N_D=10^{16}$ cm⁻³ with $\mu_n\approx1140$ cm²/V·s: $\rho\approx0.55\ \Omega\cdot$cm, versus about $3.4\times10^{5}\ \Omega\cdot$cm for intrinsic silicon, roughly six orders of magnitude lower.

**Compensation.** With both dopants present the net carrier density is $|N_D-N_A|$.

**Methods.**
- **Ion implantation:** dopant ions accelerated to keV-MeV energies are fired into the wafer. Dose (ions/cm²) is set by integrated beam current, depth by energy. Gives precise, repeatable profiles; it causes crystal damage that needs an anneal.
- **Thermal diffusion:** the wafer sits at about 900-1100 °C in a dopant-containing atmosphere; dopants diffuse in with $x\sim\sqrt{Dt}$.
- **In-situ doping during epitaxy:** the dopant is added to the gas while a crystal layer grows.
- **Anneal:** a high-temperature step (rapid thermal anneal or furnace) that repairs lattice damage and moves dopants onto substitutional sites, where they are electrically active.

**Orders of magnitude.** Light (substrate, drift) $10^{14}$-$10^{16}$; moderate (wells, channel) $10^{16}$-$10^{18}$; heavy ("N+" / "P+": source, drain, emitter, contacts) $10^{19}$-$10^{21}$ cm⁻³.
:::

::: long
"Pure, intrinsic silicon is a poor conductor. At room temperature it has about $10^{10}$ free carriers per cm³ among $5\times10^{22}$ atoms, and that number is set by temperature, not by anything I can control. Doping fixes that by adding a small, known amount of a different element.

If the dopant has five valence electrons, like phosphorus or arsenic, four go into the bonds and the fifth is nearly free, so each atom adds a mobile electron. That's an N-type donor. If it has three valence electrons, like boron, it's one electron short, takes one from the lattice, and leaves a mobile hole. That's a P-type acceptor. The concentration of dopant sets the carrier concentration almost one-for-one, because at room temperature they're essentially all ionized. So I can pick the carrier density anywhere from about $10^{14}$ to $10^{20}$ per cm³.

The numbers are tiny relative to the crystal. $10^{16}$ per cm³ is about one dopant atom per five million silicon atoms, yet it cuts the resistivity from hundreds of kilohm-centimetres to about half an ohm-centimetre.

How do we do it? The dominant method is ion implantation: ionize the dopant, accelerate it, and shoot it into the wafer through a mask. Dose is controlled by the beam current and depth by the energy. It damages the crystal, so we anneal at high temperature to repair it and put the atoms on lattice sites where they're active. The older method is diffusion, where dopants diffuse into the wafer at about a thousand degrees from a gas or a film. We can also dope while growing a crystal layer, which is epitaxy.

Why do we do it? Because a PN junction needs N and P regions side by side, a MOSFET needs heavily doped source and drain wells, and resistors and contacts need controlled conductivity. Doping is the thing that turns a bare wafer into circuits."
:::

::: terms
- **Dopant**: an impurity element intentionally added to a semiconductor (P, As, B, ...).
- **Donor / acceptor**: dopant that contributes an electron / a hole.
- **Doping concentration ($N_D$, $N_A$)**: dopant atoms per cm³.
- **N+ / P+**: heavily doped regions (above about $10^{19}$ cm⁻³), used for contacts, sources, drains and emitters.
- **Ion implantation**: shooting accelerated dopant ions into the wafer; controlled dose and depth.
- **Diffusion**: dopant atoms migrating from high to low concentration at high temperature.
- **Activation / anneal**: heating to heal crystal damage and place dopants on substitutional lattice sites.
- **Compensation**: having both donors and acceptors, so net doping is their difference.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Doping means mixing in a different material to make an alloy."** Doping is a tiny substitution (ppm to ppb mostly) that leaves the crystal basically silicon. Alloys such as SiGe have percent-level compositions and are a different concept.
- **"Doping adds charge."** It adds carriers, not net charge: each free electron comes with a fixed positive ion.
- **"More doping always means more conductivity in proportion."** Mobility falls with doping (ionized-impurity scattering), so the conductivity grows a bit slower than linearly, and at very high doping solubility and dopant clustering limit active carriers.
- **Mixing up donor and acceptor.** Group V = donor = N-type; Group III = acceptor = P-type.
- **Forgetting the anneal.** Implanted dopants sit in damaged crystal, mostly electrically inactive, until annealed.
- **Thinking doping only matters for transistors.** Resistors, diodes, solar cells, sensor diaphragms and contact layers also depend on it.
:::

::: deep
## The problem doping solves

Intrinsic silicon has $n=p=n_i\approx10^{10}$ cm⁻³ at 300 K. Its conductivity is $\sigma=qn_i(\mu_n+\mu_p)$ where $\mu_n\approx1350$ and $\mu_p\approx480$ cm²/V·s, so

$$\rho_{intrinsic}=\frac{1}{1.602\times10^{-19}\times10^{10}\times1830}\approx3.4\times10^{5}\ \Omega\cdot\text{cm}$$

That is a poor conductor and, worse, it's uncontrollable: $n_i$ roughly doubles for every 10 K of temperature rise near room temperature. Doping makes the carrier density independent of temperature (over the "extrinsic" range) and sets it by design.

## What one dopant atom does

The silicon crystal has each atom bonded to four neighbours using four shared valence electrons. Replace one silicon atom with phosphorus (five valence electrons):

- Four electrons form the same bonds.
- The fifth electron is bound to the $P^+$ ion by a hydrogen-like attraction **screened by silicon's dielectric constant** ($\varepsilon_r=11.7$). The effective binding energy drops from the 13.6 eV of hydrogen to about 45 meV, and the orbit expands to a few nanometres. Thermal energy $kT=26$ meV at 300 K easily frees it.

So each phosphorus atom contributes one free electron and leaves a fixed $+q$ ion. With $N_D=10^{16}$ cm⁻³, $n\approx10^{16}$ cm⁻³. Boron is the mirror image: it needs a fourth bond electron, takes one from a neighbouring bond, becomes $B^-$, and the vacancy is a mobile hole.

## How resistivity depends on doping

Because $n\approx N_D$, resistivity is $\rho=1/(qN\mu(N))$. Mobility decreases with doping because ionized impurities scatter carriers. The empirical Caughey-Thomas form is
$$\mu(N)=\mu_{min}+\frac{\mu_{max}-\mu_{min}}{1+(N/N_{ref})^{\alpha}}$$
with silicon electron parameters of roughly $\mu_{min}=88$, $\mu_{max}=1252$ cm²/V·s, $N_{ref}=1.26\times10^{17}$ cm⁻³, $\alpha=0.88$, and for holes $\mu_{min}=54$, $\mu_{max}=407$, $N_{ref}=2.35\times10^{17}$, $\alpha=0.88$ (Arora et al. fit).

Worked example: a bar of 1 cm length and 1 mm² cross-section ($0.01$ cm²) of $10^{16}$ N-type silicon has $R=\rho L/A=0.55\times1/0.01=55\ \Omega$. The same bar of intrinsic silicon would be 34 MΩ, a factor of about 600,000.

> Mobility versus doping depends on dopant species, compensation and the fit used. The parameters above are one widely used set; others differ by 10-20%. The point of the figure is the shape, not the third digit.

## How dopants get in

**Ion implantation** is the workhorse. A source ionizes the dopant (e.g. $B^+$, $P^+$, $As^+$), a magnet selects the species, and the ions are accelerated to 1-1000 keV or more. The ions penetrate to an approximately Gaussian depth profile (mean range increasing with energy), losing energy by collisions that displace silicon atoms. The dose (ions/cm²) is measured by integrating beam current. Typical source/drain doses are $10^{15}$ cm⁻². After implantation the dopants are mostly not on lattice sites and the crystal is damaged or even amorphized, so an anneal is mandatory.

**Diffusion** was the original method. At 900-1100 °C dopants move through the lattice, with a diffusion length of order $\sqrt{Dt}$. A predeposition step provides a surface source and a drive-in step spreads it deeper. It is still used for deep wells and for some solar cells.

**Epitaxial doping** adds dopant gases (e.g. phosphine or diborane) during crystal growth, giving a uniform, abrupt layer.

Note that every high-temperature step after the dopant is placed causes it to diffuse more. Advanced CMOS uses millisecond or laser anneals to activate dopants while limiting diffusion.

## What doping is used for

- **Junctions:** adjacent N and P regions ([[q0063]]).
- **MOSFETs:** heavily doped N+ source/drain in a P body (or the reverse), with the channel doping setting threshold voltage.
- **BJTs:** heavily doped emitter, thin moderately doped base, lightly doped collector.
- **Resistors:** diffusion or implant resistors with sheet resistance set by dose; polysilicon resistors too.
- **Ohmic contacts:** heavy doping ($>10^{19}$) thins the Schottky barrier so carriers tunnel through and the contact is non-rectifying.
- **Substrates:** wafer resistivity (e.g. 1-20 Ω·cm for P-type CMOS wafers) is a doping choice.

## Worked example: from implant recipe to sheet resistance

Suppose you implant a source/drain region with a dose of $10^{15}$ ions/cm² and, after the anneal, the dopant has spread to a depth $x_j=0.2\ \mu\text{m}=2\times10^{-5}$ cm. Treat the profile as a box (a rough but useful first pass, since real profiles are Gaussian-ish with a tail). The average concentration is

$$\bar N=\frac{\text{dose}}{x_j}=\frac{10^{15}}{2\times10^{-5}}=5\times10^{19}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$$

At that doping the Caughey-Thomas fit above gives an electron mobility of about 94 cm²/V·s. Then

$$\rho=\frac{1}{q\bar N\mu}=\frac{1}{1.602\times10^{-19}\times5\times10^{19}\times94}\approx1.3\ \text{m}\Omega\cdot\text{cm},\qquad R_s=\frac{\rho}{x_j}\approx66\ \Omega/\square$$

Sheet resistance is what a layout engineer actually uses: a drawn strip of length $L$ and width $W$ has $R=R_s\,L/W$, so a 10 µm long, 1 µm wide strip of this region is 660 Ω. Notice the shortcut: $R_s\approx 1/(q\,\mu\,\text{dose})$ whenever the dopants are fully active, which is why process engineers quote dose and sheet resistance almost interchangeably.

The same dose tells you how long the implanter must run. Charge per unit area is dose $\times q=10^{15}\times1.602\times10^{-19}=1.6\times10^{-4}$ C/cm². A 300 mm wafer has an area of about 707 cm², so it needs 0.113 C of singly charged ions. At a 1 mA beam current that is 113 s per wafer. Doubly charged ions count twice toward current, which is why current integrators must know the charge state.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Real activated concentration is capped by solid solubility and by clustering, and the anneal also moves the junction, so the true sheet resistance is usually somewhat higher than this idealized number. Treat 66 Ω/sq as the lower bound that a fully active box profile would give.

## The thermal budget: how far do dopants move?

Diffusion is thermally activated, $D=D_0e^{-E_a/kT}$. Using representative textbook values for boron in silicon ($D_0\approx0.76$ cm²/s, $E_a\approx3.46$ eV), the numbers at two anneal temperatures for 30 minutes are:

| Anneal | $D$ (cm²/s) | $\sqrt{Dt}$ for 30 min |
|---|---|---|
| 1000 °C | $1.5\times10^{-14}$ | 0.05 µm |
| 1100 °C | $1.5\times10^{-13}$ | 0.17 µm |

A 100 °C increase raises $D$ tenfold and $\sqrt{Dt}$ about 3.2 times. That exponential sensitivity is the reason modern flows use rapid thermal and laser anneals: a few seconds or milliseconds at high temperature activates the dopant but leaves it nearly where the implant put it.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Diffusivities depend on dopant, on whether the silicon is full of implant-generated point defects (transient enhanced diffusion makes boron move much more during the first seconds of an anneal), and on concentration. Use these only as order-of-magnitude figures.

## Where the simple picture breaks

- **Freeze-out at low temperature.** The 45 meV donor level is only about 1.7 $kT$ at 300 K, yet most donors are still ionized at moderate doping. Cool the wafer far enough and carriers fall back onto their dopant atoms, so conductivity drops steeply. Cryogenic electronics must account for this.
- **Intrinsic takeover at high temperature.** $n_i$ rises exponentially with temperature. Once $n_i$ approaches the net doping, the material behaves as intrinsic and junctions stop working. Lightly doped regions fail first, which limits ordinary silicon devices to roughly 150 to 200 °C junction temperatures in practice.
- **Degenerate doping.** Above about $10^{19}$ cm⁻³ the material behaves like a poor metal. That is exactly what you want for contacts and source/drain, but the simple equations in the earlier sections no longer apply.

## How you verify doping on the bench or in the fab

You never see the dopants directly, so you infer them from electrical and physical measurements:

- **Hot-point probe** (a heated tip plus a cold tip and a millivolt meter): the sign of the thermoelectric voltage tells N from P in seconds. It is the quick check that you didn't mix up wafers.
- **Four-point probe:** force a current $I$ through the outer two probes and measure $V$ across the inner two. For a layer much wider than the probe spacing, $R_s=4.532\,V/I$. For the 66 Ω/sq example above, 1 mA gives about 0.30 V. Using four probes removes contact resistance from the measurement.
- **Capacitance-voltage (C-V) profiling:** the depletion capacitance of a junction or MOS capacitor versus bias gives $N(x)$ near the surface.
- **SIMS** (secondary ion mass spectrometry) gives the chemical profile versus depth, including inactive dopant. Comparing SIMS to an electrical profile shows how much of the dopant is activated.
- **Hall measurement** gives carrier type, density and mobility directly from $V_H=IB/(qnt)$.

A discrepancy between the chemical dose from SIMS and the electrical sheet resistance is the classic sign of an under-annealed or over-solubility implant.

::: applications
- **CMOS source/drain and wells**: N+ and P+ implants form transistors on every digital chip; well implants set threshold and isolation.
- **Zener and TVS diodes**: breakdown voltage is set by the doping on each side of the junction (heavy doping gives low-voltage Zener tunnelling).
- **Solar cells**: a shallow N+ emitter diffused into P-type silicon makes the junction that separates photo-generated carriers.
- **Integrated resistors and sensors**: piezoresistive pressure sensors use boron-implanted resistors on a silicon diaphragm.
- **Power MOSFETs and diodes**: a lightly doped epitaxial drift layer sets breakdown voltage; heavy doping elsewhere keeps resistance low.
- **Ohmic contacts**: any metal-to-silicon contact on a chip uses a heavily doped layer under it.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Silicon as the host.** Doping works in any semiconductor, but the dopant elements differ (see [[q0067]]), as does the activation efficiency.
- **Room temperature, fully ionized dopants.** At very low temperature carriers freeze out onto the dopants; at very high temperature intrinsic carriers swamp the doping.
- **Non-degenerate doping** for the simple relations. Above about $10^{18}$-$10^{19}$ cm⁻³ the Fermi level reaches the band edge and the simple $n\approx N_D$ and mass-action relations fail.
- **Substitutional dopants only.** Interstitial or clustered dopants don't contribute carriers; the active concentration is capped by solid solubility (about $10^{20}$-$10^{21}$ cm⁻³ for As/P/B in silicon).
- **Valid alternatives:** "doping" can also mean the *process* (implantation, diffusion) or the *profile* (how concentration varies with depth). Tailor the emphasis to the interviewer's follow-up.
:::

::: followups
### Why do we need such small amounts of dopant?
Intrinsic silicon has only $10^{10}$ carriers per cm³ against $5\times10^{22}$ atoms, so even $10^{15}$ dopant atoms per cm³ (20 parts per billion) raises the carrier density 100,000-fold. A small amount is enough to dominate conductivity. More would reduce mobility through scattering and degrade crystal quality without proportionate benefit.

### Compare ion implantation and diffusion. Which is used today and why?
Implantation is used for nearly all dopant placement today. It gives precise control of dose (via beam current) and depth (via energy), works at low temperature with masking (resist), and allows shallow, repeatable profiles. Diffusion is simpler and gentler on the crystal but its profile depends on time, temperature and surface conditions, and it diffuses laterally under masks. The price of implantation is crystal damage that has to be annealed.

### What is the difference between N+ and N, and why do we use both in a MOSFET?
N+ is heavily doped (above about $10^{19}$ cm⁻³), giving low resistance and good ohmic contacts. Source and drain are N+ for low series resistance. The channel/body is lightly doped P so the depletion layer is wide and threshold voltage is controlled. Some MOSFETs add a lightly doped drain (LDD) extension to reduce the peak field and hot-carrier damage.

### What happens if you dope a region with both boron and phosphorus?
They compensate. The net doping is $|N_A-N_D|$ and the type is set by the larger. With $N_A=3\times10^{16}$ and $N_D=1\times10^{16}$ the region is P-type with $p\approx2\times10^{16}$. Mobility is reduced since both types of ions scatter carriers. Process engineers use this deliberately to counter-dope a region (e.g. making a P-well inside an N-type layer).

### Why does very heavy doping stop helping?
Three reasons: mobility keeps falling because of ionized-impurity scattering; dopants hit their solid-solubility limit so extra atoms don't become electrically active; and the Fermi level moves into the band (degenerate), so the simple $n=N_D$ relation and some device behaviours (like bandgap narrowing) change. For silicon, active concentration saturates around $10^{20}$-$10^{21}$ cm⁻³.

### Scenario: after a high-temperature process step your shallow junction has become deeper and the transistor leaks. What happened?
Diffusion. Dopant atoms keep moving during every hot step, with $x\sim\sqrt{Dt}$ and $D$ rising exponentially with temperature. A shallow junction at a thermal budget it wasn't designed for will push deeper and also spread laterally, which shortens the effective channel and increases punch-through and leakage. Fixes are a lower-temperature or shorter anneal (rapid thermal or laser), lower-diffusivity dopants (arsenic in place of phosphorus for shallow N+), or reordering process steps.
