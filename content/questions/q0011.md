::: testing
Whether you know that **no real component is the ideal element on the schematic**. The interviewer wants to hear, for each of R, C and L, what extra element hides inside it, where it physically comes from, and at what frequency it starts to matter. Hidden sub-skills:

1. Physical intuition: any conductor has inductance, any two conductors near each other have capacitance, any conductor has resistance.
2. Being able to put a number on it: self-resonant frequency $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$.
3. Knowing the *consequence*: a "decoupling capacitor" that is an inductor above 20 MHz, a "resistor" that is a capacitor at 100 MHz.

This question is the umbrella for [[q0016]] (resistor), [[q0030]] (capacitor) and [[q0047]] (inductor).
:::

::: why
Most hardware bugs that "shouldn't happen" at high frequency trace back to a parasitic: a rail that rings because the decoupling cap has too much ESL, an ADC front end that peaks because a feedback resistor has 0.2 pF across it, a filter that stops filtering at 50 MHz because the inductor became a capacitor. Schematic capture tools and simulators use ideal parts by default, so the parasitics are what the *board* does that the *simulation* did not. Interviewers use this question to separate people who have only drawn circuits from people who have measured them.
:::

::: concepts
- **Every conductor has resistance, inductance and capacitance.** A wire is $R = \rho \ell / A$ in series with about 1 nH/mm of loop inductance, and it has capacitance to anything nearby. See [[q0014]] and [[q0039]].
- **Impedance of the ideal parts**: $Z_R = R$, $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)$, $Z_L = j\omega L$. Parasitics are simply the *other* two terms added to each part. See [[q0024]] and [[q0040]].
- **Series vs. parallel combination**: a series parasitic (ESL, ESR, lead inductance) matters at *high* frequency for a capacitor; a parallel parasitic (winding capacitance, leakage) matters for an inductor or high-value resistor.
- **Self-resonance**: when the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel, $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$. Above it the part behaves as the "wrong" element. See [[q0031]] and [[q0045]].
- **Frequency vs. size**: parasitics are set by geometry (package, leads, vias), so they are roughly fixed numbers: tenths of a nH, tenths of a pF.
:::

::: answer
"Every real passive has the other two elements hiding inside it, and they come from geometry and materials. A resistor is a conductor, so it has some lead and trace inductance in series, and its two end caps and the body have a bit of capacitance across it, a fraction of a picofarad. A capacitor has resistance in its plates and terminals, that's the ESR, and it has inductance from the current loop through the plates and leads, the ESL. It also leaks a tiny bit of DC current through the dielectric. An inductor has the resistance of the copper winding, that's DCR, and capacitance between adjacent turns that sits across the whole coil, plus a loss term from the core.

The result is that every part is only the ideal part over a band. A capacitor looks like a capacitor up to its self-resonant frequency, where its ESL cancels the capacitance. Above that it's an inductor. An inductor does the opposite: it's an inductor up to its resonance, then a capacitor. For example a 100 nF 0402 with about 0.5 nH of ESL resonates near 22 MHz, so above that it's not decoupling anything the way you'd draw it.

So in practice I pick parts and package sizes by looking at the impedance curve, not the nominal value, and I keep the loop area small because mounting inductance often dominates the part's own ESL."
:::

::: technical
Each ideal element gets a **series** and **parallel** companion:

| Part | Dominant parasitics | Topology | Typical size |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resistor | lead/body inductance $L_s$, terminal capacitance $C_p$ | $(R + sL_s)\,\|\,C_p$ | 0402: $L_s\approx$ 0.4 nH, $C_p\approx$ 0.05–0.2 pF |
| Capacitor | ESR, ESL, insulation resistance, dielectric absorption | $R_{esr} + sL_{esl} + 1/(sC)$ | 0402 MLCC: ESR 5–50 mΩ, ESL 0.4–0.7 nH |
| Inductor | DCR/ACR, winding capacitance $C_w$, core loss | $(R_s + sL)\,\|\,C_w\,\|\,R_{core}$ | 10 µH power inductor: DCR 10–50 mΩ, $C_w$ several pF |

Self-resonance for the capacitor (series $L$–$C$) and the inductor (parallel $L$–$C$) both occur at

$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$

Below $f_0$ the capacitor has $|Z| \approx 1/(\omega C)$; above it $|Z|\approx \omega L_{esl}$. At resonance, a capacitor's impedance bottoms out at ESR, while an inductor's impedance peaks (to roughly $Q\,\omega L$).

Numbers: 100 nF with $L_{esl} = 0.7$ nH gives $f_0 = 19$ MHz; a 10 µH inductor with $C_w = 5$ pF gives $f_0 = 22.5$ MHz; a 10 kΩ resistor with 0.2 pF across it has a pole at $1/(2\pi RC) = 80$ MHz.

Other non-ideal behaviors that are not "extra elements" but belong in the answer: resistor **TCR** and voltage coefficient, **thermal noise** $\sqrt{4kTR}$, capacitor **DC-bias derating** and dielectric absorption, inductor **saturation** and core-loss dependence on $f$ and $B$.
:::

::: short
"Every real passive has the other two elements in it. A resistor has a little series inductance and a little shunt capacitance. A capacitor has ESR and ESL, so above its self-resonant frequency, around 20 MHz for a small 100 nF part, it acts like an inductor. An inductor has winding resistance and winding capacitance, so above its self-resonance it acts like a capacitor. They come from geometry: lead length, plate loops, and turn-to-turn spacing. So I always check the impedance curve, not just the nominal value."
:::

::: long
"The way I think about it is that any physical conductor carries current, so it has resistance, it makes a magnetic field, so it has inductance, and it has an electric field to its neighbors, so it has capacitance. Ideal components are just the case where one of those dominates.

Starting with the resistor: the main parasitics are a small series inductance, from the leads or the spiral trimming cut on some film resistors, and a small parallel capacitance between the end terminals. For a 0603 that's maybe half a nanohenry and a tenth of a picofarad. That doesn't matter at audio, but a 10 kilohm resistor with 0.2 pF forms a pole around 80 MHz, which can wreck a feedback network on a fast op-amp.

The capacitor has ESR, which is plate and terminal resistance, ESL, which is the inductance of the current loop, and a very large parallel leakage resistance. Capacitance and ESL resonate at the self-resonant frequency, around 20 MHz for a 100 nF 0402. Beyond that it's an inductor, so I use several values and short vias.

The inductor has DCR from the winding, a parallel capacitance between turns, and core loss. It's an inductor up to its self-resonance, then it's a capacitor, which is why a big choke doesn't filter 200 MHz noise.

So the practical rule is: choose parts by their impedance versus frequency curve, keep loops small because mounting inductance adds to ESL, and simulate with a model, not an ideal symbol."
:::

::: terms
- **Parasitic element**: an unintended R, L or C that comes from the physical construction of a part or layout.
- **ESR (equivalent series resistance)**: the lumped resistance in series with a capacitor, from plates, terminals and dielectric loss.
- **ESL (equivalent series inductance)**: the inductance of the capacitor's internal current path plus its mounting.
- **DCR / ACR**: the DC and AC series resistance of an inductor winding; ACR rises with frequency (skin and proximity effects, see [[q0051]]).
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: where the part's own inductive and capacitive reactances cancel; the part stops being its nominal self.
- **Winding / interwinding capacitance**: capacitance between turns that appears in parallel with an inductor.
- **Insulation resistance / leakage**: a large parallel resistance (MΩ to GΩ) that sets DC leakage current of a capacitor.
- **Q factor**: ratio of reactance to resistance, $Q = \omega L / R$ for an inductor; a measure of how "ideal" the part is.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "capacitors have ESR and inductors have DCR" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives all three parts with *both* the series and parallel parasitics and says which one matters for which part.
- Ties each parasitic to its physical origin: the spiral trim cut in a film resistor, the plate loop in a capacitor, the proximity of adjacent turns in a winding.
- Computes an SRF with real numbers and says what the part *becomes* above it.
- Distinguishes the part's own parasitics from **layout** parasitics (pads, vias, traces), noting that mounting inductance can be larger than ESL.
- Mentions non-ideal behaviors that are not parasitic elements: capacitor DC-bias derating, inductor saturation, resistor TCR.
- Describes what they do about it: choose package, parallel several values, use models from the manufacturer, and look at impedance plots.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Only capacitors have ESR."** All three parts have loss. An ideal resistor with zero inductance doesn't exist either; at high enough frequency every resistor is partly reactive.
- **Assuming a bigger capacitor is always better for high frequency.** A bigger value has a *lower* SRF (more $C$ in $\sqrt{LC}$, and bigger packages have more ESL). A 10 µF part is an inductor at a few MHz, where a 10 nF part is still capacitive.
- **Forgetting layout parasitics.** A 100 nF cap with 0.5 nH ESL on a pad connected through a 5 mm via/trace stub has 3–5 nH of effective inductance. The layout wins.
- **Thinking parasitics matter only at RF.** A 10 MΩ resistor with 1 pF is already a 16 kHz pole, so high-impedance sensor nodes see parasitics in the kHz range.
- **Treating the SRF as a hard cutoff.** The capacitor doesn't "stop working" at SRF; impedance is at its minimum there. It is the region far above it where it fails to decouple.
:::

::: deep
## The unifying idea

A physical device is a piece of geometry. Maxwell's equations don't know that you called it a resistor. Whenever current $I$ flows, a magnetic flux $\Phi = L I$ links the loop (inductance), whenever voltage $V$ exists between conductors there is stored charge $Q = CV$ (capacitance), and whenever charge moves through a real material there is dissipation (resistance). The "ideal component" is a model where only one of these three is kept. The others are small, but they are not zero, and they have a different *frequency dependence*:

- Resistance: constant (to first order).
- Inductive impedance: $\omega L$, grows with $f$.
- Capacitive impedance: $1/(\omega C)$, shrinks with $f$.

So there is always a frequency at which the small element catches up with the intended one. That frequency is the part's usable bandwidth.

For a **10 kΩ** resistor, $C_p = 0.2$ pF gives a pole at $f_p = 1/(2\pi R C_p) = 80$ MHz. For a **10 Ω** resistor, the inductive corner $f = R/(2\pi L_s) = 10/(2\pi\cdot 0.5\text{ nH}) = 3.2$ GHz. The rule: **high-value resistors go capacitive, low-value resistors go inductive.** The crossover is near $R \approx \sqrt{L_s/C_p} = \sqrt{0.5\text{ nH}/0.2\text{ pF}} = 50$ Ω, which looks like a characteristic impedance, and it is.

Wire-wound resistors are inductive because they are literally coils. Film resistors with a laser spiral trim also have some inductance. Carbon composition and thick-film chip resistors are the best RF behavers. More on this in [[q0016]].

## Capacitor

The capacitor is a series chain $R_{esr}$, $L_{esl}$, $C$ (and a very large parallel leakage resistance).

$$Z(\omega) = R_{esr} + j\omega L_{esl} + \frac{1}{j\omega C}$$

The magnitude has a V-shaped "bathtub" on log-log axes: falling at $-20$ dB/dec, flat at ESR, rising at $+20$ dB/dec. The minimum is at $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{esl}C})$. A 100 nF, 0.7 nH, 20 mΩ cap resonates at 19 MHz and has $|Z| = 20$ mΩ there. At 100 MHz, the ESL reactance is $2\pi\cdot 100\text{ MHz}\cdot 0.7\text{ nH} = 0.44$ Ω, 22 times the ESR.

## Inductor

An inductor is a series $R_s + j\omega L$ branch shunted by a capacitance $C_w$ (and an equivalent core-loss resistance). Its impedance rises with frequency until the SRF, where it peaks, then falls like $1/(\omega C_w)$.

$$|Z|\;\approx\;\frac{\sqrt{R_s^2+\omega^2L^2}}{\sqrt{(1-\omega^2 L C_w)^2+(\omega R_s C_w)^2}}$$

For 10 µH and 5 pF: $f_0 = 22.5$ MHz. At 100 MHz the reactance of $C_w$ is 318 Ω, versus 6.3 kΩ for the "inductor" at that frequency if it had been ideal. That choke you chose to kill 100 MHz noise passes it.

## The layout adds its own parasitics

A pad, a via and a short trace add inductance on the order of 0.5–1 nH per via-and-pad pair, usually *more* than the capacitor's own ESL for small packages. So the effective inductance in the decoupling case is $L_{esl} + L_{mount}$, and the SRF is lower than the datasheet implies. See [[q0032]] and the decoupling-network simulation there. Likewise, probing a high-impedance node with a 10 MΩ, 10 pF scope probe adds its own pole.

> [!TIP]
> When you quote a parasitic, quote a *package-level* number and a *context*: "about half a nanohenry for an 0402, plus another nanohenry or two for the via to the plane."

## What to do about it

1. **Pick the part for the band.** For high-frequency decoupling use small packages (0402, 0201) and low-ESL reverse-geometry or 3-terminal types.
2. **Parallel different values** to cover more bandwidth, accepting anti-resonance peaks between them ([[q0032]]).
3. **Use manufacturer S-parameter or SPICE models** and view the impedance curve.
4. **For RF resistors**, pick thin-film chip types, short physical length, and avoid wire-wound parts.
5. **For filters**, remember the stopband floor is set by parasitics, not by the ideal roll-off: an LC filter stops attenuating at the frequency where the inductor's $C_w$ and the capacitor's ESL create a bypass path.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> ESL, ESR and $C_p$ values vary by manufacturer, dielectric, package and construction (for example reverse-geometry MLCCs have much lower ESL). The numbers here are order-of-magnitude typical values; use the vendor's models for design.
:::

::: applications
- **Decoupling a microcontroller**: a 100 nF 0402 right at each VDD pin, with the impedance flat to ~50 MHz; larger parts carry the lower frequencies ([[q0032]]).
- **EMI chokes and ferrite beads**: the bead is purposely lossy; its impedance is mostly resistive above a few MHz, and its SRF is the upper limit of usefulness.
- **High-speed op-amp feedback**: a 10 kΩ feedback resistor plus input capacitance forms a pole that can cause peaking, fixed with a small feedback capacitor.
- **RF matching networks**: 0201 or 0402 parts chosen so the SRF is well above the operating frequency (e.g. 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi), and the layout parasitics are included in the design.
- **Current-sense resistors**: the low-value (mΩ) sense resistor has inductance that adds an $L\,di/dt$ error in a switching supply; sensors specify low-inductance metal-strip parts.
- **Scope probe tip models**: the compensated probe is an RC divider designed to cancel the parasitic capacitance seen by the input.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Lumped-element model.** I treat each part as a small network. When the part is a significant fraction of a wavelength (above a few GHz for chip parts), you need a distributed or S-parameter model.
- **Linear small-signal.** DC-bias capacitance loss, inductor saturation and resistor self-heating are nonlinear effects that I mention but do not include in the $Z(f)$ curves.
- **Typical values.** Parasitic numbers depend on package and vendor; ranges are given for 0402-0805 SMD parts.
- **Room temperature.** ESR and DCR vary with temperature (copper about +0.39 %/K; electrolytics get much worse when cold).
- If the interviewer asks only about *one* component class, go deep on that class and mention the others in a sentence.
:::

::: followups
### What is the SRF of a 1 µF capacitor with 1 nH of total inductance, and what does that mean for decoupling?
$f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{1\text{ nH}\cdot 1\text{ µF}}) = 5.0$ MHz. Above 5 MHz the part looks inductive, so for high-frequency noise you need smaller values in smaller packages or lower-inductance placement. The 1 µF part is still great for the 100 kHz–2 MHz range.

### Why does a high-value resistor go capacitive while a low-value one goes inductive?
Because the parallel capacitance shunts a *high* impedance easily (it only has to be lower than $R$), while the series inductance only matters compared with a *low* resistance. The boundary is $R = \sqrt{L_s/C_p}$, about 50 Ω for the numbers above.

### How would you model these parasitics in simulation?
Use the vendor's SPICE or S-parameter model if available (many MLCC and inductor makers publish them). Otherwise build the lumped model: add $R_{esr}$ and $L_{esl}$ in series with every cap, $C_w$ across every inductor, and $L_s$/$C_p$ for critical resistors. Then compare the $|Z|$ curves against the datasheet.

### Why can adding a capacitor make noise worse?
Two capacitors in parallel with different SRFs have an **anti-resonance** between them where the inductive branch of one resonates with the capacitive branch of the other. The combined impedance has a *peak* there. A little ESR (or a deliberately lossy bead) damps it. [[q0032]] has the simulation.

### What if your measurement shows the real SRF is much lower than the datasheet?
The measurement fixture or board adds inductance. A 0.5 nH part measured through a 5 mm trace to a plane can show 5 nH, giving $f_0$ about three times lower. I'd de-embed the fixture, or measure with short pads/vias. In a real board, that effective number is what matters.
:::

::: interpretations
"Passive components' parasitics" is very broad. It could mean (a) the standard R/C/L lumped-model parasitics, (b) the effects of layout, or (c) a specific component the interviewer follows with. I answer (a) briefly for all three parts, then mention (b), then ask which one to go deeper on. Dedicated pages: [[q0016]] (resistor), [[q0030]] (capacitor), [[q0047]] (inductor).
:::

::: rubric
- Names the extra elements for all three parts (R: $L_s$, $C_p$; C: ESR, ESL, leakage; L: DCR, $C_w$, core loss) :: A complete model shows you know the parts are not ideal in any of the three cases.
- Ties each parasitic to a physical origin (geometry, leads, turns, plates, dielectric) :: Explaining why they exist shows understanding, not recall.
- Explains self-resonance and computes it with $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ :: The SRF is the number that tells you the usable band.
- States what each part becomes above SRF (C → L, L → C) :: This is the consequence that leads to wrong designs.
- Mentions layout/mounting inductance :: Real designs are limited by layout as much as the part.
- Gives a practical mitigation (package, value mixing, vendor models, curves) :: Shows you can act on the knowledge.
- Notes non-parasitic non-idealities (DC bias, saturation, TCR) :: Demonstrates awareness beyond the lumped three-element model.
:::
