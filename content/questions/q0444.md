::: basics
### In plain words
A sense resistor is simple, cheap, tiny and very linear: its voltage is exactly proportional to the current. It works from DC up to high frequencies and it shrugs off overloads. Its drawbacks are side effects: it wastes power and drops voltage in the supply path, its small signal is easily swamped by amplifier offset, its resistance drifts with heat, and a fast current edge makes its tiny inductance add a false spike. It also gives no electrical isolation.

### Picture it
Think of a speed bump on a road. It is cheap, it always works and it responds to every car the same way. But every car has to slow down, which wastes energy, and a very busy highway would turn into a traffic jam. And the bump itself warms up and changes its behavior.

### Words you need
- **Insertion loss**: the voltage and power the sensor takes from the circuit. See [[q0443]].
- **TCR**: how much the resistance changes with temperature, in parts per million per °C.
- **ESL**: the tiny series inductance every real resistor has, a few nanohenries.
- **Offset floor**: the smallest current you can tell from zero, $V_{os} / R_s$. See [[q0445]].
- **Kelvin sensing**: taking the sense voltage at the resistor's own pads. See [[q0557]].

### The simplest example
A 5 mΩ shunt at 30 A wastes $30^2 \times 0.005 = 4.5$ W, so it does not scale to high current. Now the small-signal side: a 100 µV amplifier offset on a 10 mΩ shunt is $0.0001 / 0.01 = 10$ mA, so you cannot trust readings near that value. Finally, a fast edge: with 5 nH of inductance and a 10 A step in 100 ns, the extra spike is $5\text{ nH} \times 10\text{ A} / 100\text{ ns} = 0.5$ V, five times the 0.1 V real signal from 10 A in 10 mΩ. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows each effect and its fix.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can give a balanced, quantified assessment of the shunt rather than "simple but lossy". The interviewer wants to hear the real second-order effects (tempco and self-heating, parasitic inductance on fast edges, offset limiting low-current accuracy, thermal EMF, common-mode and no isolation) and a sense of when each con actually bites.

"Its" refers to the sense resistor of [[q0443]], so the answer should be framed relative to alternatives such as Hall sensors and Rds(on) sensing.
:::

::: why
Every current-sensing design starts by asking whether a shunt is good enough. Knowing its strengths (accuracy, linearity, DC to high frequency, tiny size, cost) and exactly where it fails (high current, high $dI/dt$, isolation, tiny currents) tells you when to use it and how to design around its limits. These are also the failure modes that show up in the lab: readings that drift as the board warms, spikes that don't match the real current, or a 48 V common-mode that kills an amplifier.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ohm's law and power**: $V = IR$, $P = I^2R$. See [[q0443]].
- **Temperature coefficient (TCR)**: $R(T) = R_0[1+\alpha\Delta T]$; with self-heating the resistance depends on the current itself.
- **Parasitic inductance (ESL)**: any real resistor is a resistor in series with a few nH; $V_L = L\,dI/dt$ matters in switching circuits. See [[q0463]].
- **Offset and signal level**: low-current accuracy is limited by $V_{os}/R_s$. See [[q0445]].
- **Common-mode voltage**: a high-side shunt rides on the bus; the amplifier must cope. See [[q0447]].
- **Thermoelectric (Seebeck) EMF**: junctions of dissimilar metals generate µV per °C of gradient.
:::

::: answer
"The big pros of a sense resistor are that it's simple, cheap, small, and very linear. It works from DC up to hundreds of kilohertz, there's no saturation or hysteresis, and with a good metal-strip part and a decent amplifier I can get well under one percent accuracy. It also survives big overloads for short periods because it's just metal.

The cons are mostly about the side effects. It dissipates $I^2R$, and it drops voltage in the supply path, so it's a bad fit above roughly 50 to 100 amps. The signal is tiny, so amplifier offset sets how small a current I can resolve. The resistance changes with temperature and self-heating, so accuracy drifts. At fast switching edges its parasitic inductance adds an $L\,dI/dt$ spike that corrupts the reading. It has no isolation, and on the high side it sits at the bus voltage, so I need a CSA with enough common-mode range. And layout matters: if I don't use a Kelvin connection, trace resistance becomes part of the measurement.

So I'd choose it for low to moderate currents without an isolation requirement, and go magnetic for isolation or very high current."
:::

::: technical
**Pros.**
- Accuracy: 0.1 to 1 percent resistor tolerance, 20 to 100 ppm/°C TCR for metal-strip parts, plus amplifier gain error (typically 0.1 to 1 percent).
- Linearity: purely resistive up to its power limit; no hysteresis or remanence.
- DC to wide bandwidth: limited by the resistor's ESL and the amplifier; the resistor corner $f = R_s/(2\pi\,\text{ESL})$ is about 318 kHz for 10 mΩ with 5 nH.
- Size/cost: a few cents to a dollar; 2512 or smaller packages up to about 3 W.
- Overload robustness: metal element tolerates short surges many times rating (pulse ratings are in datasheets).

**Cons, with numbers.**
- *Loss and drop*: 5 A through 10 mΩ is 0.25 W and 50 mV; 100 A through 2 mΩ is 20 W.
- *Small signal vs offset*: $I_{err,min} = V_{os}/R_s$; 100 µV on 10 mΩ is 10 mA.
- *Tempco and self-heating*: at 100 ppm/°C and 100 °C rise, a 1 percent gain error ($100\times10^{-6}\times100$).
- *ESL*: 5 nH with a 10 A step in 100 ns gives $L\,dI/dt = 5\text{ nH}\times 10^8 \text{ A/s} = 0.5$ V, five times the 0.1 V resistive signal.
- *Thermal EMF*: copper-to-alloy junctions give a few µV/°C, which matters when the signal is a few hundred µV.
- *No isolation, common-mode*: needs CSA with the bus in its input range, e.g. 48 V; CMRR error is signal × (CM/CMRR).
- *Layout*: 1 mΩ of trace between pad and sense tap is 10 percent of a 10 mΩ shunt ([[q0557]]).
:::

::: short
"Sense resistors are cheap, small, linear and accurate, they work from DC to high frequency, and they handle overloads. The downsides are that they dissipate $I^2R$ and drop voltage so they don't scale to very high current, their tiny signal means amplifier offset limits low-current accuracy, resistance drifts with temperature, parasitic inductance corrupts fast edges, and there's no isolation. They also need Kelvin routing and a CSA that handles the common-mode voltage on the high side."
:::

::: long
"I'd split it into what's good and what bites you.

The good: a shunt is the simplest sensor there is. It's a resistor, so it's cheap and tiny, it's very linear, there's no saturation or hysteresis, and it works from DC out to hundreds of kilohertz. A good metal-strip part is 0.5 to 1 percent with 50 ppm per degree, and with a decent amplifier the whole chain can hit under a percent. And since it's just metal, it handles overload pulses well.

The bad, in order of how often I'd hit them. First, power and drop: it's $I^2R$ and $IR$ in the main path. A 10 milliohm shunt at 5 amps is a quarter watt; a 2 milliohm one at 100 amps is 20 watts, which is why shunts don't scale.

Second, the signal is small, so amplifier offset sets the floor on the smallest current I can measure: 100 microvolts of offset on 10 milliohms is 10 milliamps. To improve it I either increase the resistor, with more loss, or use a zero-drift amplifier.

Third, temperature and self-heating change the resistance, so gain drifts a fraction of a percent.

Fourth, parasitic inductance: in a switching motor driver, 5 nanohenries and a fast edge produce half a volt, bigger than the actual signal, so I filter or blank those edges and use low-ESL parts.

Fifth, no isolation, and on the high side the amplifier needs common-mode range and CMRR. And finally layout: without Kelvin connections, trace resistance becomes part of the shunt.

So I use shunts at low to moderate current without isolation requirements, and switch to magnetic sensors otherwise."
:::

::: terms
- **Insertion loss**: voltage drop and power added to the measured circuit by the sensor.
- **TCR**: temperature coefficient of resistance, in ppm/°C.
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance of a resistor, from its geometry (typically 0.5 to 5 nH for chip/metal-strip parts).
- **Offset current floor**: $V_{os}/R_s$, the smallest current distinguishable from amplifier offset.
- **Seebeck/thermal EMF**: voltage generated at junctions of dissimilar metals with a temperature difference.
- **Kelvin sensing**: sense connections made at the resistor pads so only the resistor is measured.
- **Pulse rating**: the surge energy or current a resistor tolerates for a short time, higher than its continuous rating.
- **CMRR**: common-mode rejection ratio, the amplifier's ability to ignore the bus voltage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "simple and cheap; but dissipates power and has no isolation".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies each con (0.25 W at 5 A, 20 W at 100 A; 10 mA offset floor; 0.5 V ESL spike; 1 percent per 100 °C).
- Includes the *non-obvious* cons: ESL on fast edges, thermal EMF, Kelvin layout, and CMRR on the high side.
- Gives mitigations: low-TCR alloy, low-ESL reverse-geometry parts, zero-drift CSA, RC filter or blanking, Kelvin pads, calibration.
- Frames the answer relative to alternatives and says when to switch (isolation, hundreds of amps).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying shunts are "lossless".** Loss is $I^2R_s$; it's small only at low currents.
- **Treating a shunt as an ideal resistor at any frequency.** ESL makes it an R-L network, and fast currents produce $L\,dI/dt$ errors.
- **Forgetting self-heating.** The resistance depends on the dissipated power, so accuracy changes with load.
- **Assuming "more gain" fixes small signals.** Amplifier offset and noise are amplified too, and offset sets the floor.
- **Using a large resistor to get more signal** without counting the wasted power and the voltage drop in the load path.
- **Believing a shunt is isolated.** It is conductively connected, and a faulty ground return can put the bus voltage on the amplifier.
:::

::: deep
## What makes it attractive

A sense resistor is a *physical standard of current*: a piece of alloy whose resistance is known and (with the right alloy) barely moves. Manganin and similar alloys have TCR of order 10 to 50 ppm/°C, so over a 100 °C swing the resistance changes 0.1 to 0.5 percent. There's no magnetic material to saturate, so the response is linear from DC to wherever parasitics bite.

The result is why shunts dominate in battery gauges, buck converters, motor drives and power monitors: the whole chain from shunt to ADC can be accurate to about 1 percent without any calibration, and 0.1 percent with it.

## Loss: the $I^2R$ wall

For a given sense voltage $V_s$ (chosen to beat offset and fit the amplifier), $R_s = V_s/I$, so

$$P = V_s I$$

The loss rises linearly with current at constant sense voltage. Holding the signal at 100 mV: 1 A costs 0.1 W, 10 A costs 1 W, 100 A costs 10 W. With a smaller signal (10 mV) the same currents cost 10 mW, 100 mW, 1 W, which is why high-current designs use small shunts with low-offset amplifiers or move to magnetic sensing.

## The small-signal problem

The sense voltage $V_s = IR_s$ is in millivolts. Amplifier offset $V_{os}$ and noise add directly, so the smallest *meaningful* current is about $V_{os}/R_s$.

| $R_s$ | Equivalent error from 100 µV offset | Loss at 10 A |
|---|---|---|
| 1 mΩ | 100 mA | 0.1 W |
| 5 mΩ | 20 mA | 0.5 W |
| 20 mΩ | 5 mA | 2 W |
| 100 mΩ | 1 mA | 10 W |

Trade-off: halving the offset floor costs doubling the loss. Zero-drift (chopper) CSAs lower $V_{os}$ to a few µV and let you use a smaller resistor.

## Temperature and self-heating

$R(T) = R_0[1+\alpha(T-T_0)]$. A resistor rated 1 W at 70 °C ambient can reach 125 °C or more at full power; with TCR 100 ppm/°C, a 100 °C rise gives 1 percent gain change. Strategies: choose a 20 to 50 ppm/°C alloy, derate power (use at 30 to 50 percent of rating), give it copper area for heat spreading, or measure board temperature and compensate in firmware.

Also mind **copper traces**: copper has about 3900 ppm/°C. If the sense path includes trace length, those tens of percent per 100 °C swamp the shunt's tempco, one more reason to Kelvin-connect.

## Inductance and fast edges

A real shunt is $R_s$ in series with ESL. The impedance is $Z = R_s + j\omega L$, so the corner frequency is $f_c = R_s/(2\pi L)$.

Figure: Above about 318 kHz the inductive term overtakes the resistance. A PWM edge with 10 A in 100 ns has frequency content far above that, so the measured voltage is dominated by $L\,dI/dt$, not $IR$. Drag the ESL down to 0.5 nH, as in a low-inductance metal-strip part, and the corner moves out by a decade; raise R and the corner moves up too, at the cost of more $I^2R$ heat in the shunt.

Numerically: $L\,dI/dt = 5\text{ nH}\times 10\text{ A}/100\text{ ns} = 0.5$ V, against a resistive signal of only 0.1 V. The spike lasts about as long as the edge. Fixes: sample after the edge settles (blanking or center-of-PWM sampling), add an RC filter (watching its delay), use low-ESL reverse-geometry or metal-strip parts, and keep the sense loop small.

## Layout-driven errors

Kelvin connection ([[q0557]]): bring the sense lines from the inner edge of each pad. Without it, the sensed resistance includes solder joints and trace (a few tenths of mΩ to several mΩ). At 5 mΩ, an extra 1 mΩ is 20 percent error and drifts with copper's tempco.

## Common-mode and isolation

A high-side shunt sits at the bus voltage: 24 or 48 V is typical, hundreds of volts on a drive. The amplifier must withstand it, and its CMRR determines how much of the bus (and its ripple or PWM edges) leaks into the reading: for a 10 mV signal on 48 V, 80 dB CMRR gives $48/10^4 = 4.8$ mV equivalent error, half the signal ([[q0447]]).

::: applications
- **Battery gauges and chargers**: 5 to 20 mΩ shunts where accuracy and DC response matter.
- **Motor drives**: low-leg shunts of 1 to 10 mΩ, sampled mid-PWM to avoid ESL spikes.
- **Server and PC power monitors**: high-side shunts with digital monitor ICs, 1 to 5 mΩ.
- **Bench supplies and source-measure units**: precision low-TCR shunts and zero-drift amplifiers.
- **Automotive 12/48 V systems**: busbar and strip shunts handling hundreds of amps with isolated amplifiers.
- **USB-C power delivery**: 5 to 10 mΩ high-side shunts monitoring VBUS current.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes a proper low-TCR metal-strip or metal-film current-sense resistor, not a generic thick-film chip, which has several hundred ppm/°C and may have poor pulse capability.
- Numbers like 5 nH ESL, 100 µV offset and 50 ppm/°C are typical; check parts. Reverse-geometry and four-terminal parts can have well under 1 nH.
- "Cons" depend on the application: ESL doesn't matter in a slow battery gauge; loss doesn't matter at 100 mA.
- At very high currents (hundreds of amps) busbar shunts are used in the 50 to 200 µΩ range, so the discussion is about voltage drop of tens of mV, not milliohms.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> ESL values vary widely with package and construction (sub-nH to several nH). Treat the 5 nH example as illustrative.
:::

::: followups
### How do you reduce a shunt's power dissipation without losing accuracy?
Lower $R_s$ and use a zero-drift CSA with lower offset and higher gain, so the equivalent offset current $V_{os}/R_s$ stays acceptable. Or calibrate the zero point. If the loss still isn't acceptable, switch to a magnetic sensor or a lossless method.

### What does parasitic inductance do in a motor driver's shunt?
Each PWM edge makes a large $dI/dt$, and $L\,dI/dt$ adds a spike to the sense voltage. With 5 nH and 10 A in 100 ns that's 0.5 V, bigger than the 100 mV signal. You sample after the edge (center of the PWM period), use a low-ESL part, and blank or filter the spike.

### How does self-heating cause measurement error and what can you do?
Dissipation raises the resistor's temperature, changing its resistance by TCR × ΔT. At 100 ppm/°C and 80 °C rise, that's 0.8 percent. Use a lower-TCR alloy, derate power, add copper area, or compensate in firmware using a temperature measurement.

### Scenario: your shunt reads 5 mA with no load. Is that a bug?
It is almost certainly amplifier offset (for example 50 µV on 10 mΩ), thermal EMF, or bias current through input resistors. Measure with zero current and subtract that in firmware (and recheck over temperature), or use a zero-drift CSA. If it changes when you heat the board, thermal EMF is likely.

### What if you need to sense a 200 A line with a shunt?
Use a busbar shunt of roughly 100 to 250 µΩ, which gives 20 to 50 mV and burns 4 to 10 W at full current, with Kelvin sense taps and a low-offset, ideally isolated, amplifier. Consider a Hall sensor if isolation is needed or the heat is unacceptable.
