::: testing
Whether you can turn the impedance $1/(j\omega C)$ into a physical statement about **which signals** go through and which don't, and name the filter. The interviewer is checking:

1. The headline: a capacitor **blocks DC and low frequencies, passes high frequencies**, so in series it is a **high-pass** filter.
2. The nuance: whether it behaves as high-pass or low-pass depends on *where* it sits: in series with the signal (high-pass) or shunted to ground (low-pass).
3. The time-domain meaning: it passes *changes* ($i=C\,dv/dt$): edges, spikes, noise, AC; it blocks constant levels.
4. A number: the corner frequency $1/(2\pi RC)$, and that "blocks" really means attenuates at $-20$ dB/dec.
:::

::: why
Every time you AC-couple a signal, filter noise off a supply, bypass a rail, or debug why a square wave looks like spikes after a capacitor, you're using this. It's also the quick way to analyze a circuit by inspection: look at each capacitor and ask "what frequency am I looking at relative to $1/(2\pi RC)$?" It ties together impedance, filters, time-domain response and Fourier thinking.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor impedance** ([[q0024]]): $|Z_C|=1/(2\pi fC)$, falling with frequency.
- **Differential equation** ([[q0025]]): $i=C\,dv/dt$, the current is proportional to the rate of change of voltage.
- **Voltage divider with impedances**: $V_{out}=V_{in}\,Z_2/(Z_1+Z_2)$ ([[q0008]]).
- **First-order RC filters** ([[q0010]]): corner $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$, $\pm20$ dB/dec roll-off.
- **Fourier view**: a square wave is a sum of sinusoids; each harmonic is attenuated or passed according to its frequency.
- **Displacement current**: changing electric field in the dielectric acts like current, though no charge crosses the gap.
:::

::: answer
"A capacitor passes signals that change and blocks signals that don't. Its impedance is one over j omega C, so at DC the impedance is infinite, and DC is blocked. At high frequency the impedance gets small, and the signal passes. In the time domain that's i equals C dv/dt: current flows only when the voltage is changing, so a constant level gives no current, while fast edges, spikes and noise give a lot.

What filter that makes depends on where you put it. In series with the signal, with a resistor to ground after it, it's a high-pass filter: low frequencies are dropped, and the corner is one over two pi R C. In shunt, from the signal to ground, with a resistor in series, the capacitor shorts high frequencies to ground and the output is a low-pass filter, same corner.

It's not a brick wall: the roll-off is 20 dB per decade, so a signal one decade below the corner is attenuated by 20 dB, not removed. For example with 1 microfarad and 10 kilohms, the corner is about 16 hertz; a 1 hertz signal comes through at about minus 24 dB, 100 hertz is essentially unchanged.

And a square wave through a series capacitor comes out differentiated: the flat portions decay and the edges pass as spikes, because the edges contain the high frequencies."
:::

::: technical
**Impedance:** $Z_C=\dfrac{1}{j\omega C}$; $|Z_C|\to\infty$ as $f\to0$; $\to0$ as $f\to\infty$.

**Series C, shunt R (high-pass):** $H(j\omega)=\dfrac{j\omega RC}{1+j\omega RC}$, $|H|=\dfrac{f}{\sqrt{f^2+f_c^2}}$, phase $=90^\circ-\arctan(f/f_c)$ (leads). Passband above $f_c$; slope $+20$ dB/dec below.

**Series R, shunt C (low-pass):** $H=\dfrac{1}{1+j\omega RC}$, $|H|=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}}$, phase lags up to $-90^\circ$.

**Corner:** $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$ (−3 dB). Example 1 µF, 10 kΩ: 15.9 Hz.

**HPF gain at selected frequencies** ($f_c=15.9$ Hz): 1 Hz: $-24.0$ dB; 5 Hz: $-10.6$ dB; 15.9 Hz: $-3.0$ dB; 50 Hz: $-0.4$ dB; 100 Hz: $-0.11$ dB.

**Time domain:** series-C HPF with pulse width $T$ and $\tau=RC$:
- $\tau\gg T$: the pulse passes with a small droop ($\approx T/\tau$) (AC coupling).
- $\tau\ll T$: the output is a pair of narrow spikes at the edges (differentiator).

**Passes / blocks (series C):** passes AC, edges, spikes, ripple, noise above $f_c$; blocks DC, slow drifts, and very-low-frequency content.
:::

::: short
"A capacitor blocks DC and low frequencies and passes high frequencies: impedance one over j omega C is infinite at DC and small at high frequency. In series with a resistor to ground it's a high-pass filter; across the signal to ground it's a low-pass. The corner is one over two pi R C, with a gentle 20 dB per decade roll-off. In the time domain it passes changes, edges and spikes, and blocks constant levels."
:::

::: long
"A capacitor's impedance is one over j omega C. At zero frequency that's infinite, so it blocks DC completely. As the frequency rises, the impedance falls, so it passes AC more and more easily. In the time domain, that's i equals C dv/dt: current only flows when the voltage is changing, so constant levels give zero current, and fast edges, spikes and noise give large currents.

So the capacitor passes high frequencies and blocks low ones, which is a high-pass behavior, when it's in series with the signal and followed by a resistor to ground. The output is the divider R over R plus 1 over j omega C, which gives a corner at one over two pi R C. A 1 microfarad capacitor into 10 kilohms has its corner at about 16 hertz: a 100 hertz signal is nearly untouched, 16 hertz is down 3 dB, and 1 hertz is down about 24 dB.

If I instead put the capacitor across the signal to ground, with a resistor in series, the capacitor shorts high frequencies away and passes low ones: a low-pass filter with the same corner formula. So, same part, opposite filter, depending on topology.

Two points of detail. First, it's not a brick wall; first-order roll-off is 20 dB per decade, so 'blocking' is a matter of degree. Second, the time-domain effect: a square wave through a series capacitor is differentiated. If the time constant is long compared to the pulse, the pulse passes with a slight droop, that's AC coupling. If it's short, you get narrow spikes at the edges. In Fourier terms the edges contain the high-frequency harmonics, which pass, and the flat tops are low-frequency content, which doesn't."
:::

::: terms
- **High-pass filter**: passes frequencies above a corner and attenuates those below.
- **Low-pass filter**: passes frequencies below a corner and attenuates those above.
- **Corner / cutoff frequency**: the −3 dB point, $1/(2\pi RC)$.
- **Roll-off**: attenuation slope, 20 dB/decade for first order.
- **Displacement current**: $\varepsilon\,dE/dt$ through the dielectric that completes the circuit.
- **Differentiator**: circuit whose output is proportional to the derivative of the input (series C with small $\tau$).
- **Droop/tilt**: decay of a flat pulse top through a high-pass.
- **Blocking capacitor**: series capacitor used to remove DC.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a capacitor blocks DC and passes AC, so it's a high-pass filter".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives the behaviour from $Z_C=1/(j\omega C)$ and from $i=C\,dv/dt$, and links the two.
- States the topology dependence: series ⇒ high-pass, shunt ⇒ low-pass, with the same corner.
- Gives the corner formula with a numeric example and the attenuation at a few frequencies.
- Explains time-domain consequences: AC coupling droop, differentiated square waves, edges/spikes passing, and relates to Fourier content.
- Mentions real-world limits: SRF/ESL for very high frequencies, leakage at DC, and that "blocks" is −20 dB/dec not infinite.
- Notes where each is used: AC coupling, bypassing, filtering, snubbers, differentiators.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Capacitors pass AC because charge crosses the dielectric."** No charge crosses an ideal dielectric; the changing field gives displacement current.
- **Saying a capacitor is always a high-pass filter.** Shunted to ground, it forms a low-pass.
- **Thinking blocking is absolute.** A first-order filter only attenuates 20 dB per decade; a 1 Hz signal through a 16 Hz corner still shows up at −24 dB.
- **Forgetting the resistor.** A capacitor alone has no corner; the resistance (source, load or bias) sets it.
- **Ignoring real parasitics.** Above its SRF a real capacitor stops passing signals as an ideal one would (becomes inductive).
- **Assuming the output of a high-pass to a square wave looks like a square wave.** It is tilted or differentiated depending on $\tau$ vs. pulse width.
:::

::: deep
## Two views of the same fact

**Frequency domain.** The capacitor's impedance magnitude is $1/(2\pi fC)$. For $C=1\ \mu$F: 159 kΩ at 1 Hz, 159 Ω at 1 kHz, 1.6 Ω at 100 kHz, 0.16 Ω at 1 MHz. When compared with a resistance $R$ in a divider, the capacitor "wins" (has the larger drop) at low frequencies and "loses" at high frequencies. The crossover is where $|Z_C|=R$: $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$.

**Time domain.** $i=C\,dv/dt$. If $v$ is constant, $i=0$ (blocked). If $v$ steps by $\Delta V$ in a time $t_r$, then $i\approx C\,\Delta V/t_r$. A 5 V edge in 5 ns is $10^9$ V/s, so a 1 nF capacitor carries $i=C\,dv/dt=10^{-9}\cdot10^9=1$ A for those 5 ns. That is why capacitively coupled edges are so effective at injecting crosstalk and noise, and why the inductive mirror image ($v=L\,di/dt$, [[q0052]]) produces large voltage spikes.

## What a pulse looks like

For a unit pulse of width $T$ through the series-C high-pass, $v_{out}(t)=e^{-t/\tau}-e^{-(t-T)/\tau}u(t-T)$.

With τ much shorter, only the edges survive as spikes of opposite sign. This is the capacitor "passing change and blocking the constant level".

The average (DC) value of any signal through a series capacitor is zero in steady state, so the area above and below zero must match: that's why unipolar waveforms shift and the baseline wanders as duty cycle changes ([[q0033]]).

## Fourier picture

A 1 kHz square wave contains harmonics at 1, 3, 5, 7 kHz... plus a DC component (mean). A series-C high-pass with $f_c=16$ Hz removes the mean and attenuates only content near and below 16 Hz, so the waveform keeps its shape (tilt represents the effect on the lowest harmonics). A high-pass with $f_c=10$ kHz would remove the fundamental (1 kHz) almost completely and leave only the higher harmonics, so edges and ringing remain but the "flat" part is gone.

## Beyond first order and ideal parts

- **Higher order:** cascade stages (or use RLC) for a steeper slope: 40 dB/dec for a second-order filter.
- **Real capacitors:** a series coupling capacitor has an SRF; a shunt bypass capacitor has ESR/ESL limiting its high-frequency effectiveness ([[q0030]], [[q0031]]).
- **Source and load impedances** change the effective R and thus the corner.
- **Leakage and bias:** an electrolytic coupling cap with leakage current modifies the DC bias after it.
- **Capacitive coupling as an unwanted path:** stray capacitance between traces couples edges (crosstalk) in the same high-pass manner: $v_{coupled}\approx R\,C_m\,dv/dt$ for small $R C_m$.
:::

::: applications
- **AC coupling** between amplifier stages and into ADCs ([[q0033]]).
- **Power supply bypass** (shunt capacitors form a low-pass for the noise on the rail).
- **RC low-pass filters** for anti-aliasing and debouncing; **RC high-pass** for edge detection.
- **Snubbers and differentiators** in analog computation and pulse shaping.
- **Touch sensing**: a finger changes capacitance, and the circuit responds to edge/charge changes.
- **Crosstalk and EMI analysis**: parasitic capacitance couples fast edges between traces.
:::

::: assumptions
- Ideal capacitor and first-order RC filter with a single resistor; real systems have source/load impedances and parasitics.
- Linear, time-invariant circuit and sinusoidal analysis.
- "Blocks" means "attenuates strongly relative to the passband"; for DC it is exact for an ideal capacitor (real parts leak).
- A series capacitor yields high-pass only when followed by a resistive path to a reference; without a DC path the node floats.
- Valid alternative: describe it purely in the time domain ($i=C\,dv/dt$).
:::

::: followups
### What kind of filter is a capacitor shunted to ground with a series resistor, and what is its corner?
A low-pass filter: the capacitor shorts high frequencies to ground. The corner is $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$, and above it the output falls at −20 dB/dec. For example 10 kΩ and 100 nF gives 159 Hz.

### Why does a square wave come out of a series capacitor as spikes?
The edges are fast changes with large $dv/dt$, which produce large currents through the capacitor, while the flat parts have $dv/dt=0$ and produce none (the output decays toward zero). If $\tau\ll T$, the output is basically $RC\,dv_{in}/dt$.

### How much attenuation does a 100 nF capacitor into 1 kΩ give at 100 Hz?
$f_c=1/(2\pi\cdot1000\cdot10^{-7})=1.59$ kHz. At 100 Hz, $|H|=100/\sqrt{100^2+1590^2}=0.0628$, or −24 dB. So the capacitor is a poor coupler there; use a larger C or a higher-impedance input.

### What if you need to pass 10 Hz but block a DC offset and 50 Hz mains hum?
Those requirements conflict for a simple capacitor: a high-pass can remove DC but 50 Hz is above 10 Hz so it passes. Use a notch filter at 50 Hz (e.g. twin-T or a digital notch), plus the AC coupling for DC. A capacitor alone can't separate two frequencies in the passband.

### Why does a capacitor "conduct" AC if there's an insulator between the plates?
Because the current in the circuit is the charging/discharging of the plates; the changing electric field in the gap is the displacement current, which has the same magnitude as the conduction current in the wires, so the total current is continuous ([[q0025]]).
