::: testing
Whether you know that a sense resistor is a *measurement* as well as a power component, and that the PCB can quietly ruin that measurement. The interviewer wants to hear:

1. **Kelvin routing**: the sense lines tap the resistor's own pads, not somewhere along the high-current copper.
2. **Noise discipline**: the two sense lines run as a tight pair, short, over a plane, away from switching nodes.
3. **Component placement**: the filter and amplifier sit right next to the sense lines' end, and the resistor sits in the right spot in the power path.

Strong candidates put numbers on it, for example "10 mm of thin copper can be larger than my 5 mΩ resistor".
:::

::: why
Current-sense resistors are small on purpose. At 5 to 50 mΩ you are measuring tens of millivolts, and the copper around the part has resistance in the same range. A layout that looks harmless can double the effective resistance, make it drift with temperature, and pick up switching noise as large as the signal. This is also a classic "you have to have done it" topic: people who have only simulated circuits tend to route the sense lines wherever the autorouter put them. It shows up in motor drives, battery packs, buck converters with current-mode control and every power monitor.
:::

::: concepts
- **Sense resistor**: a low-value precision resistor whose voltage drop $V = IR_s$ is proportional to the current. See [[q0443]] and [[q0445]].
- **Kelvin (four-wire) connection**: separate "force" paths that carry the current and "sense" paths that only measure the voltage. See [[q0557]].
- **Trace resistance**: $R = \rho L/(wt)$. Copper is about 17 to 18 nΩ·m and has a large temperature coefficient (roughly 0.39 %/°C).
- **Loop area and mutual coupling**: a sense pair picks up noise in proportion to the area it encloses. See [[q0555]].
- **High-side vs low-side sensing**: sets the common-mode voltage and where the ground reference sits. See [[q0447]].
- **Input filtering of a current-sense amplifier**: a small differential RC at the amplifier pins. See [[q0449]].
:::

::: answer
"I treat the sense resistor as a four-terminal device even if the part only has two pads. The load current goes straight through the resistor's pads on wide copper, a pour or a short fat trace. The measurement is made separately: two thin sense traces start at the inside edge of each pad, that is, right where the resistive element begins, and they go off to the amplifier. That way the sense lines see only the drop across the resistor, not the drop across the power copper feeding it.

Then I treat the sense lines like a small differential signal. They run together as a tight pair, as short as possible, over a solid ground plane, away from the switch node, the inductor and any gate drive. If I need an input filter I put the small RC right at the amplifier pins, and the amplifier right by the end of the pair. The pair doesn't cross a plane split or run alongside a noisy trace.

Last thing, I connect the amplifier's ground to the resistor's ground side with a clean path, so the load return current isn't flowing through the reference I'm measuring against. I'd also check power and temperature: P = I²R in the resistor, with enough copper to spread the heat."
:::

::: technical
**Kelvin tap geometry.** Make the sense pickoff on the *inner* edge of each pad, as close as possible to the resistive element, and connect it with a trace that leaves the pad perpendicular to the current direction, away from the power path. The force current enters and leaves through the outer edge of the pads or from a pour. Any copper between the force entry and the sense pickoff is in the current path but *not* in the measurement.

**Why it matters.** A 1 oz (35 µm) trace has $R = \rho L/(wt)$. With $\rho = 1.72\times10^{-8}$ Ω·m, 10 mm × 0.25 mm gives 19.7 mΩ; 10 mm × 1 mm gives 4.9 mΩ. Against a 5 mΩ sense resistor, that is a 100 % error if it ends up in the sense path, plus copper's 3900 ppm/°C drift against roughly 50 to 100 ppm/°C for alloy resistors.

**Sense pair.** Route both lines on the same layer (or the same path) with equal length, equal width and the same neighbours, so noise couples *equally* and the amplifier's common-mode rejection can remove it. Keep a ground plane directly beneath. Keep them away from the switch node, inductor and gate-drive nets: crosstalk from an edge of several V/ns onto a mV-level pair is easily the same size as the signal.

**Filter and amplifier.** A differential RC (a few ohms to tens of ohms in each leg, 1 nF to 100 nF across, optional smaller capacitors to ground) goes at the amplifier pins. Keep the resistors small: bias current and the amplifier's internal input resistors turn them into offset and gain error (see the datasheet).

**Ground and power path.** For low-side sensing, tie the amplifier and ADC ground to the low side of the resistor at one spot so the sensed current does not flow through shared reference copper. For high-side sensing, check the amplifier's common-mode range.

**Power and thermal.** Dissipation $I^2R_s$ must be within the resistor's rating with derating, and the pads and pour spread the heat. Heat that raises the resistor's temperature changes its value slightly, so also note the TCR in the datasheet.
:::

::: short
"I route it as a four-wire measurement. The load current runs through wide copper into the resistor pads, and two thin sense traces tap the inside edge of each pad, so the measurement only sees the drop across the resistor. Those two traces go to the amplifier as a short, tight differential pair over a ground plane, away from the switch node and inductor, with the small input filter right at the amplifier pins. Skip any of that and the copper in the path can easily be as large as the resistor itself."
:::

::: long
"Think of the resistor as a four-terminal part. Two terminals carry the current, which is the big wide copper in and out of the pads. Two terminals are for sensing, which are thin traces, and I connect them at the inner edges of the two pads, right where the resistive element begins. The reason is simple. A low-value sense resistor is something like 5 milliohms, and 10 millimetres of 1 millimetre wide 1 ounce copper is already about 5 milliohms. If my sense lines tap the power trace out past that copper, I'm measuring the resistor plus the trace, and the copper has a much bigger temperature coefficient, so my reading drifts as the board warms up.

Second, the sense lines carry almost no current, but they carry a signal of tens of millivolts, so they're noise-sensitive. I route them as a pair: same layer, same length, tight spacing, short, over a solid ground plane. That makes any noise they pick up common-mode, which the amplifier rejects. And I keep that pair away from the switch node, the inductor, the gate drive, anything with fast edges.

Third, the support parts. The small differential filter goes right at the amplifier pins, with low-value resistors so I don't create gain error or offset. The amplifier sits at the end of the pair, so if the signal has to travel, it travels after amplification.

Finally the ground. The sensed current has to return somewhere. I connect the amplifier's ground to the low side of the resistor at one clean point, not through the power return. And I check P equals I squared R and make sure there's enough copper under and around the resistor to get the heat out."
:::

::: terms
- **Kelvin connection**: a four-wire connection where the force (current) path and sense (voltage) path are separate, so the sense lines see only the element's drop.
- **Force / sense**: the high-current terminals and the high-impedance measurement terminals of a four-terminal resistor.
- **Sense pair**: the two sense traces routed together as a differential signal.
- **Common-mode rejection (CMRR)**: the amplifier's ability to ignore noise that appears equally on both inputs. Mismatched routing turns common-mode noise into differential error.
- **TCR (temperature coefficient of resistance)**: the fractional change of resistance per °C, about 3900 ppm/°C for copper and tens of ppm/°C for sense alloys.
- **Loop area**: the area enclosed by a signal and its return; noise pickup is proportional to it.
- **Input filter**: the small RC at the amplifier inputs that removes switching noise before it is amplified.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use short traces and a Kelvin connection" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains *where* the sense lines connect (inner pad edge, away from the current path) and puts a number on the error if they don't, like the 10 mm of 1 mm copper that equals 4.9 mΩ.
- Mentions the copper TCR problem: it is not only an offset, it is a drift.
- Treats the sense pair as a differential signal: matched, tight, over a plane, away from the switch node, and explains that matching converts noise to common-mode.
- Places the filter and amplifier at the end of the pair and keeps the filter resistors small for a reason (gain error and bias current).
- Remembers the ground/return path and the power rating, and says the layout should be checked on the bench with a scope, not just trusted.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Tapping the sense line from the power trace** some distance from the pad. The copper in between is in the measurement. Tap at the resistor's own pads, inner edge.
- **Routing the sense lines as two separate, unmatched traces** on different layers or around the board. Noise appears differential instead of common-mode. Route them as a pair.
- **Running the sense pair next to the switch node or under the inductor.** The coupling is as large as the signal. Move it or add ground between.
- **Putting the filter far from the amplifier**, so the long unfiltered stretch picks up noise after the filter. The filter goes at the amplifier.
- **Using large filter resistors** (hundreds of ohms) on an amplifier with input bias current and internal input resistors. It costs offset and gain accuracy.
- **Sharing the ground return with the sensed current.** The ground drop appears directly in the reading. Reference to the low side of the resistor at one point.
:::

::: deep
## What the layout has to protect

A sense resistor turns current into a voltage, $V_s = I R_s$. The measurement is only as good as the answer to "is the voltage the amplifier sees really the voltage across the resistor element?". The PCB can break that in three ways:

1. **Extra series resistance in the measurement path** (copper in the sense loop).
2. **Noise added to the small signal** (coupling from switching nodes, ground bounce, EMI).
3. **Voltage offsets** (ground drops, thermal EMFs, amplifier bias current times filter resistance).

The routing rules are all just ways of preventing one of those three.

Figure: Orange is the high-current path, blue is the sense pair. The taps leave the pad at its inside edge, so any voltage drop in the wide copper on the left and right is not in the measurement. The two blue traces stay together and end at the amplifier with the filter beside it.

Where the tap lands matters because current spreads out inside a pad: the pad nearest the resistor body has the "cleanest" potential of the element's end. A tap on the far side of the pad includes a bit of pad copper and solder, which adds a small error. How small depends on the part and pad geometry. For resistors above roughly 50 mΩ, nobody cares. Below 5 mΩ, it can dominate. See [[q0557]] for the full Kelvin discussion and how four-terminal parts remove the ambiguity.

### Worked example: what goes wrong without it

A 5 mΩ resistor carries 8 A. The signal is 40 mV and the resistor dissipates 0.32 W. Now suppose the sense lines are connected on the power trace 5 mm outside each pad, and the trace is 1 mm wide, 1 oz. Each 5 mm piece is 2.46 mΩ, so the two pieces add 4.9 mΩ. The amplifier now sees $I(R_s + 4.9\text{ m}\Omega)$, a gain error of +98 % for the same current. That alone is calibratable, but the copper's temperature coefficient of about 0.39 %/°C isn't. If the board warms by 60 °C, the copper part grows by about 23 %, and since it is half the measurement the reading drifts by about 11.6 %. The resistor itself, at 75 ppm/°C, contributes 0.45 %. So the layout, not the resistor, determines the accuracy.

## The sense pair as a differential signal

The sense lines carry negligible *current*, so their own resistance doesn't matter much (except through bias currents and filter resistors). What matters is the *voltage noise* that couples in:

- **Capacitive coupling** from a switching node: $i = C_m\,dv/dt$. A 12 V node with 5 ns edges has about 2.4 V/ns. With only 0.1 pF of mutual capacitance, that is 0.24 mA, and across a few tens of ohms that is millivolts.
- **Inductive coupling** from the power loop: $v = M\,di/dt$. With 5 A changing in 20 ns (0.25 A/ns), a mutual inductance of just 0.2 nH produces 50 mV, the size of the whole signal.

Noise that hits both lines equally is common-mode and gets rejected. Noise that hits them differently (because one line is longer, or on a different layer, or closer to the aggressor) becomes differential, and no amplifier can separate it from the signal. So routing as an equal, tight pair is not tidiness, it is what converts noise from differential to common-mode. The ground plane underneath keeps the loop area between the pair and its return small and gives the noise a low-impedance path.

The differential corner is $f_c = 1/(2\pi\,(2R_f)\,C_d)$. For $R_f = 10$ Ω and $C_d = 100$ nF, that is 79.6 kHz. It must be well above the bandwidth of the current you want to measure (a current-mode control loop might need several hundred kHz, in which case use a smaller C). It also needs to be well below the ringing frequency of the switch node, which is why switching-converter layouts rely on layout first and filtering second.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact limits for filter resistors depend on the amplifier. Many current-sense amplifiers have internal input resistors in the hundreds of ohms to a few kΩ range, so each ohm of external series resistance changes the gain by about one part in several hundred. Always check the datasheet's "input filtering" or "layout" section for your part; the 10 Ω figure above is only illustrative.

## Ground and return path

A low-side sense resistor sits between the load and ground, so the *entire load current* flows through it and then into the main ground plane. The amplifier output is referenced to its own ground. If the amplifier's ground and the resistor's low pad are separated by some copper that also carries load current, the IR drop in that copper appears directly in the reading. A 5 mΩ ground connection carrying 5 A adds 25 mV, which on a 3.3 V ADC is 0.76 % of full scale for free. The fix is to connect the amplifier ground (and the ADC reference ground) to the low side of the resistor at one clean point, and let the load current go on from there.

For a high-side sense resistor the same discipline applies to the supply side, and you additionally need an amplifier whose common-mode input range includes the bus voltage (see [[q0447]]).

## Placement and thermal

- Put the resistor where the current is *already* flowing in one path, such as at the input connector or between the battery and the converter, so the full current goes through it and nothing bypasses it (bypass paths make the reading smaller than the real current).
- Give it copper: wide traces or pours at both ends spread the heat. But remember pad copper is also a heat conductor, so a large pour on one side and a thin trace on the other creates a temperature gradient across the part and a small thermal EMF. Symmetric copper helps.
- Keep temperature-sensitive parts (voltage references, the amplifier) off the hottest area.

::: applications
- **Motor drives (low-side shunt in each leg)**: a 1 to 10 mΩ shunt per phase feeding an amplifier close to the shunt, with the gate drive on the other side of the plane.
- **Battery packs and fuel gauges**: a 1 to 5 mΩ low-side shunt where the amplifier or gauge IC sits at the end of a short Kelvin pair to count coulombs. Accuracy requirements make four-terminal parts common.
- **Buck converters with current-mode control or current limit**: sense resistor in series with the inductor or switch, with the sense lines kept away from the switch node.
- **Power monitors on boards** (high-side current/power monitor ICs on 3.3 V, 12 V and 48 V rails): the datasheets show the Kelvin routing in their layout sections.
- **Hot-swap and eFuse circuits**: the sense resistor drops a few tens of millivolts at the trip current and the comparator inside the controller must not false-trip from noise.
:::

::: assumptions
- **The amplifier input draws negligible current** (or a known bias current), so the sense lines' own resistance only matters through the filter and bias. Some current-sense amplifiers (current-output types) do put current through the input resistors; then external resistance matters more.
- **The numbers use 1 oz (35 µm) copper and 1.72×10⁻⁸ Ω·m.** Resistivity rises with temperature; inner layers and 0.5 oz copper differ.
- **DC to a few hundred kHz measurement bandwidth.** At higher frequencies the resistor's own inductance (ESL) and the loop inductance of the force path matter, and low-ohm shunts for fast current-mode control are designed differently.
- **The resistor is a standard SMD part.** A four-terminal part or an integrated shunt changes the footprint, but the principle (force vs sense) is the same.
- **Alternative answers**: a current-sense amplifier integrated at the resistor, a Hall or magnetic sensor ([[q0441]]), or a MOSFET $R_{DS(on)}$ measurement ([[q0448]]) change the routing but not the principle that the measurement must be isolated from the power path.
:::

::: followups
### Why not just connect the sense lines to the nearest power trace?
Because the power trace carries the full current, so it drops voltage along its length. Whatever length lies between the connection point and the resistor body adds its resistance to the measurement. With 1 mm wide, 1 oz copper that is about 0.5 mΩ per millimetre, and copper also drifts with temperature at about 0.39 %/°C. The tap should be at the resistor's own pad, inner edge.

### Where does the filter go and how big should it be?
At the amplifier input pins, with a differential capacitor and small series resistors (typically a few ohms to tens of ohms). Larger resistors add offset (bias current × R) and gain error with the amplifier's internal input resistors. The capacitor sets the corner: $f_c = 1/(2\pi\cdot 2R_f C_d)$. For 10 Ω legs and 100 nF it is about 80 kHz. Pick it above the signal bandwidth you need.

### My sense reading jumps whenever the switch node transitions. What do you check?
Look at the sense pair with a differential probe or two matched probes close to the amplifier. If the noise is common-mode, add filtering and improve CMRR matching. If it's differential, it was coupled unequally: look for the pair passing near the switch node, inductor or gate drive, different lengths or layers, or a large loop. Fix the geometry, add a ground plane or shield, and then tune the filter.

### Do you ever not use Kelvin routing?
When the resistor is large enough (hundreds of mΩ and up) that the copper is negligible, or the accuracy target is loose (overcurrent trip only). Even then I'd keep the sense lines routed as a pair, because noise coupling doesn't depend on the resistance value. The Kelvin matters most below about 10 mΩ.

### The resistor is on a 12-layer board with the amplifier on the other side of the board. How do you route the sense lines?
Keep them as a pair on one layer as long as possible. If a layer change is needed, use two vias placed next to each other (same size) and a return via close by, or place the amplifier on the same layer by moving it. Over a continuous reference plane the whole way. If distance is the problem, see [[q0556]].
:::
