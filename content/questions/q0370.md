::: testing
Whether you understand that the ESC isn't an optional accessory: **in a brushless motor the commutator has been deleted, so something must do its job**. The interviewer is testing:

1. The causal chain: no brushes, so no mechanical switching, so electronic switching is mandatory.
2. The physics of why plain DC doesn't work (a static field produces a locked rotor, not rotation).
3. The extra reasons beyond commutation: speed control, current limiting, startup, protection.
4. A careful reading of the word "often": there are cases where the ESC is built in or where a different drive (mains-fed, line-start) is used.
:::

::: why
A frequent beginner mistake is wiring a brushless motor to a battery and expecting it to spin. Understanding why it can't is the same understanding that lets you debug a stuttering drone motor, choose between a hobby ESC and an integrated driver, and talk about the drive electronics as part of the motor system rather than an accessory. It also makes the brushed vs brushless trade-off ([[q0366]]) concrete: brushless gets rid of the brushes but pays with this electronics.
:::

::: concepts
- **Commutator function**: in a brushed motor, brushes and commutator switch coil current at the right rotor angle. See [[q0357]].
- **Torque vs angle**: torque depends on the angle between rotor field and stator field. See [[q0363]].
- **Three-phase inverter**: the electronic replacement for the commutator.
- **Position sensing**: Hall sensors or back-EMF so the electronics know when to switch.
- **PWM speed control**: the same electronics set the voltage.
- **What an ESC is**: see [[q0369]].
:::

::: answer
"Because a brushless motor has no commutator. In a brushed motor the brushes and commutator automatically switch the coil current as the rotor turns, so the torque keeps the same sign. In a brushless motor the magnets are on the rotor and the coils are fixed, so there's nothing mechanical to do that switching. Something electronic has to energise the right windings at the right moment, and that's what the ESC does.

If I just put DC on two of the three wires, the stator makes a fixed magnetic field. The rotor magnet swings to line up with it, overshoots a bit, and then stops, locked in place, drawing current through a pair of windings with no back-EMF to limit it. The motor only rotates if the stator field keeps moving ahead of the rotor, which needs the controller to know where the rotor is and step the phases in sequence.

The ESC also takes care of other things you need anyway. It sets the speed by PWMing the voltage, it limits current so the windings and the battery are protected, it handles startup, since a sensorless motor can't see its position at standstill, and it can brake or protect against undervoltage.

The word 'often' is fair: some motors, like PC fans and hard-drive spindles, have the commutation electronics built in, so it looks like a DC motor from outside. But the function is still there. And in industrial drives the same job is done by an inverter."
:::

::: technical
**Torque requires a rotating field.** The torque on the rotor is $T = T_{max}\sin\delta$ where $\delta$ is the angle between the stator MMF and the rotor field (in electrical degrees). With a fixed DC phase current the stator MMF is fixed, so as the rotor turns toward alignment $\delta\to0$ and $T\to0$; beyond alignment the torque reverses. The static equilibrium at $\delta=0$ is stable, so the rotor settles there and oscillates damped by friction. Continuous rotation requires $\delta$ to stay around 90°, which means moving the stator MMF at the rotor's electrical speed.

**Stall current without commutation.** With no motion there is no back-EMF, so a DC voltage across two phases gives $I = V/R_{LL}$. For example 12 V across 0.1 Ω is 120 A, which burns the winding and the supply in seconds. The ESC's current limit and PWM prevent this.

**What the ESC adds beyond commutation.**
- Voltage/speed control: $V_{avg} = D\,V_{bus}$, so $\omega_{no-load}\approx DV_{bus}/K_e$.
- Current limiting at startup and stall: $I\le I_{lim}$ by cycle-by-cycle chopping.
- Startup: alignment pulse, then open-loop ramp until back-EMF is visible.
- Protection: undervoltage, overvoltage, over-temperature, short-circuit.
- Interface: translates throttle commands to duty/torque.

**Exceptions.** Fans, disk spindles and small appliances integrate the commutation IC in the motor housing, presenting a DC-like interface. Industrial PMSM/BLDC drives are inverters or servo drives (the same function, a different name). A permanent-magnet motor with a squirrel-cage damper can line-start on 50/60 Hz mains, but then it is a synchronous motor at fixed speed, not the controllable BLDC.
:::

::: short
"Because a brushless motor has no commutator. In a brushed motor the brushes switch the coil current automatically as the rotor turns. With permanent magnets on the rotor and fixed coils, something electronic has to do that switching, and that's the ESC. Plain DC just creates a fixed field, so the rotor locks to it and draws huge current. The ESC reads rotor position, steps the phase currents, sets speed with PWM, limits current and protects the battery and motor."
:::

::: long
"The short version: a brushless motor has no commutator, so it needs electronics to do the commutation, and the ESC is that electronics.

Let me explain why. Torque comes from the angle between the stator's magnetic field and the rotor's magnet. In a brushed motor, the brushes slide over the commutator segments and flip the current in each coil at the right angle, so the torque never changes sign. In a brushless motor the magnets are on the rotor and the coils are fixed, so there's no sliding contact at all. If I connect a battery across two windings, the stator makes a field in a fixed direction, the rotor turns to align with it, and stops. Worse, with no rotation there's no back-EMF, so the current is limited only by the winding resistance, which might be a fraction of an ohm, so it's a near short circuit.

To get continuous rotation I have to move the stator field ahead of the rotor, in six steps per electrical revolution, which means energising different pairs of phases in sequence. To know when to step, I need the rotor position: Hall sensors, or in most hobby motors the back-EMF of the floating phase. So I need a three-phase bridge, a microcontroller and sensing. That's an ESC.

Once you have that electronics, it also does the other jobs: PWM sets the voltage and so the speed; current limiting protects the motor and battery, especially at startup and stall; undervoltage cutoff protects LiPo cells; and it handles safe arming and startup.

It's not always a separate box: a PC fan has the commutation chip built into the motor, and an industrial servo uses a drive or inverter, but those are all the same function. So I'd say: the 'ESC' is required in the sense that the motor can't work without a commutating drive."
:::

::: terms
- **Commutation**: switching the phase currents according to rotor position.
- **Stator field / MMF**: the magnetic field produced by the winding currents.
- **Torque angle ($\delta$)**: angle between stator and rotor fields; torque $\propto\sin\delta$.
- **Back-EMF**: voltage generated by the spinning magnets; zero at standstill.
- **Stall current**: $V/R$ with no back-EMF.
- **Open-loop startup**: forced commutation to get the rotor moving before position can be sensed.
- **Integrated driver**: commutation electronics built into the motor.
- **Inverter**: general term for a DC-to-AC (three-phase) power stage.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "because brushless motors need electronic commutation". An exceptional answer:

- Starts from the commutator's job and shows what replaces it.
- Explains the physics of DC locking the rotor ($T\propto\sin\delta$, stable equilibrium at $\delta=0$).
- Mentions the stall-current consequence: no back-EMF, current $=V/R$.
- Lists the other things the ESC provides (PWM speed control, current limit, startup, protection).
- Reads "often" carefully: integrated drivers in fans and servo drives, so the function is always present even if the box isn't.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Brushless motors are DC motors, so they run from a battery."** They're three-phase synchronous machines fed by an electronic inverter.
- **"The ESC just varies the voltage."** It also sequences the phases; voltage control alone gives no rotation.
- **Believing the Hall sensors can replace the ESC.** They report position only; switching the current still needs a power stage.
- **"You could use a mechanical switch."** In principle a mechanical commutator can be built (that is the brushed motor); the brushes are the thing the brushless design removes.
- **Forgetting that the rotor locks and the current spikes.** Connecting a battery directly to a BLDC can destroy windings or the supply within seconds.
- **Assuming a 'DC' fan has no electronics.** It does, inside the housing.
:::

::: deep
## The job the commutator did

In a brushed motor, as the rotor turns the commutator segments pass under the brushes and swap which end of each coil is connected to the supply. The effect: the current in the coil under a given pole always flows in the direction that produces torque in the same sense. The brush-commutator is a rotary switch driven by the shaft itself ([[q0357]], [[q0362]]).

Remove it and nothing is left to flip the current. So the flipping must be driven by information about the rotor angle and carried out by switches, the ESC.

## What plain DC does

Consider the torque between a fixed stator field and a rotor magnet at angle $\delta$ ahead of the stator field:

So DC applied to two phases = the rotor aligns and locks. Attempt to turn it by hand and it springs back.

## Why current explodes

At standstill $E = K_e\omega = 0$. The only impedance is the winding resistance. A typical drone motor has $R_{LL}$ of 30 to 100 mΩ. A 4S battery at 14.8 V gives $I = 14.8/0.05 \approx 300$ A, limited only by the battery and the wires: a short circuit. A running motor is protected by its own back-EMF, but a stalled one is not. Even a spinning motor, if commanded to full voltage, draws $(V - E)/R$. This is why the ESC chops (PWMs) and limits current.

## Position information

To step at the right time, the ESC has to know the rotor angle. Two methods ([[q0364]]):

- **Hall sensors**: three digital outputs, 120° apart. Simple, works at standstill.
- **Back-EMF**: the floating phase's voltage shows the magnet position; the ESC detects the zero-crossing. No extra sensors or wires, cheaper and more rugged, but doesn't work at zero speed, so the ESC does an open-loop startup.

## The other jobs the electronics do

| Job | Why it's needed |
|---|---|
| PWM voltage control | A battery gives a fixed voltage; speed needs a variable average voltage |
| Current limiting | Inrush and stall currents are tens to hundreds of amps |
| Startup | Sensorless motors can't see position at standstill |
| Protection | Battery undervoltage, over-temperature, short circuits |
| Command interface | Convert throttle or serial commands into duty or torque |
| Braking / regeneration | Control what happens when the command drops |

## "Often" required, not always

1. **Built-in drivers.** A PC fan or a small appliance motor includes a driver chip and Hall sensor in the motor housing. Externally it takes DC, but inside the commutation still happens.
2. **Industrial drives.** A servo drive or VFD is the same electronics under a different name.
3. **Line-start PM motors.** With a damper cage they start on mains and run synchronously at a fixed speed. You don't get variable speed, and they aren't "BLDC" in the usual sense.

::: applications
- **Drone and RC motors**: a separate ESC per motor, as the motor itself has no electronics.
- **PC fans and HDD spindles**: commutation chip in the motor housing.
- **E-bike hub motors**: a controller with throttle and Hall inputs does the ESC's job.
- **Industrial servo drives and VFD inverters**: the same three-phase bridge with advanced control.
- **Robotic joints**: ESC-like driver boards with encoders.
:::

::: assumptions
- "Brushless motor" refers to a three-phase permanent-magnet machine; other brushless types (switched reluctance, induction) also need electronic drives, for similar reasons.
- I assume a battery or DC supply as the source. A motor fed with three-phase AC from a variable-frequency drive is the same idea one level up.
- The numbers for winding resistance and stall current are illustrative; check the actual motor.
- Some very small BLDCs with integrated electronics are sold as "DC motors", and for them there is no external ESC.
:::

::: followups
### Could a mechanical switch do the commutation instead?
Yes, that is exactly the brushed motor. The brushes and commutator are a shaft-driven switch. The price is wear, arcing and noise, which brushless designs avoid by using transistors instead.

### What happens if you hold DC on two phases of a BLDC?
The rotor aligns with the resulting field, and stops. Current is limited only by resistance (no back-EMF), so a large current flows and may overheat the winding or the supply. A holding current can be useful, as in a stepper-like hold, but only at a controlled value.

### Why do sensorless ESCs make a noise or twitch at startup?
They have no position information at standstill, so they apply an alignment pulse, which moves the rotor to a known position, then ramp the commutation open-loop. The twitch and beeps are that alignment and the ramp.

### Is the ESC needed if you have Hall sensors?
Yes. The Hall sensors only tell you the rotor sector; they don't carry motor current. You still need a power stage that switches the phase currents. Some motors plus a simple Hall-driven driver chip work like a small ESC.

### Scenario: you connected a 12 V battery across two wires of a BLDC motor for a test and it got warm quickly and the battery sagged. Why?
With the rotor locked there is no back-EMF, so current $\approx V/R_{LL}$, tens to hundreds of amps for a low-resistance motor. The battery's internal resistance and wiring limited it, but the winding dissipates $I^2R$ and warms in seconds. A current-limited supply or a PWM-limited ESC avoids this.
:::
