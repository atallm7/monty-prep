::: testing
Whether you can walk through one switching cycle **in the right physical order** and connect it to the equation. The interviewer is listening for:

1. The two states (on and off) and which way current flows in each.
2. *Why* the inductor current ramps: $v_L = L\,di/dt$ with a constant voltage across it.
3. The steady-state argument: the current ramps up and down by the same amount, so $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$.
4. The role of the output capacitor and of the feedback loop that adjusts $D$.

The question is a gentle version of the "Circuit Analysis" block ([[q0185]] to [[q0200]]), so answering it cleanly sets you up for those.
:::

::: why
Every other buck question depends on this one: ripple, efficiency, stability, DCM, synchronous rectification, layout. Interviewers use "how does it work?" to find out whether you see a buck as a *sequence of events* (inductor charges, inductor discharges, capacitor smooths) or as a black box with an equation. People who can narrate the on-state and off-state and say where the inductor current comes from in each state usually handle the follow-ups easily. Those who recite "it steps down voltage with PWM" usually stall on the first follow-up.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor law** $v_L = L\,\frac{di_L}{dt}$: a fixed voltage across $L$ gives a constant slope. See [[q0041]].
- **Continuity of inductor current**: the current can't jump, so when the switch opens the current continues through the freewheel path. See [[q0043]].
- **Volt-second balance**: in steady state, average inductor voltage over a cycle is zero.
- **Charge balance (capacitor)**: in steady state, average capacitor current is zero, so average inductor current equals the load current.
- **Capacitor law** $i_C = C\,dv/dt$: the capacitor absorbs the AC part of the inductor current. See [[q0025]].
- **Feedback regulation**: the duty cycle is adjusted by the loop to hold $V_{out}$.
:::

::: answer
"I think of it as two states per cycle. In the on-state, the high-side switch is closed, so the switch node is at $V_{in}$. The inductor now has $V_{in}-V_{out}$ across it, so its current ramps up, and that current goes to the load and the output capacitor. The inductor is storing energy in its magnetic field.

In the off-state, the switch opens. The inductor current can't change instantly, so the switch node swings down until the diode, or the low-side FET in a synchronous buck, takes over. Now the inductor has $-V_{out}$ across it, so the current ramps down, still flowing into the load. The inductor is giving back its energy.

The output capacitor smooths all that. It supplies the load during the dips and absorbs the extra during the peaks.

In steady state the current has to end each cycle where it started. The up-ramp is $(V_{in}-V_{out})DT/L$ and the down-ramp is $V_{out}(1-D)T/L$. Set them equal and you get $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$.

The last piece is the feedback loop. The controller compares a scaled copy of $V_{out}$ to a reference and nudges $D$ up or down, so the output holds when the input or load changes."
:::

::: technical
**On-state** ($0<t<DT$): $Q_1$ on, freewheel element off. SW $\approx V_{in}$. Inductor voltage $v_L = V_{in}-V_{out}$ (positive), so
$$\frac{di_L}{dt}=\frac{V_{in}-V_{out}}{L},\qquad \Delta I_{L,on}=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})\,DT}{L}$$

**Off-state** ($DT<t<T$): $Q_1$ off, diode/$Q_2$ on. SW $\approx 0$. $v_L=-V_{out}$, so
$$\frac{di_L}{dt}=-\frac{V_{out}}{L},\qquad \Delta I_{L,off}=-\frac{V_{out}\,(1-D)T}{L}$$

**Steady state (periodicity):** $\Delta I_{L,on}+\Delta I_{L,off}=0 \Rightarrow V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$ (CCM).

**Peak-to-peak ripple:** $\Delta I_L=\dfrac{(V_{in}-V_{out})D}{L f_{sw}}=\dfrac{V_{out}(1-D)}{L f_{sw}}$.

**Capacitor:** average capacitor current is zero, so $\bar I_L=I_{out}$. The capacitor carries $i_C=i_L-I_{out}$, a triangle of $\pm\Delta I_L/2$, giving
$$\Delta V_{out}\approx\frac{\Delta I_L}{8\,f_{sw}\,C_{out}}\ \text{(plus }\Delta I_L\cdot \text{ESR)}$$

**Conduction mode:** CCM holds when $I_{out} > \Delta I_L/2$. Below that the current reaches zero before the cycle ends (DCM, [[q0229]]).

**Worked numbers** ($V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I_{out}=2$ A, 500 kHz, $L=4.7$ µH, $C=22$ µF): $D=0.275$, up-slope $=1.85$ A/µs for 550 ns, down-slope $=0.70$ A/µs for 1450 ns, $\Delta I_L=1.02$ A, $I_{L}$ swings 1.49 A to 2.51 A, and $\Delta V\approx 11.6$ mV from the capacitance.
:::

::: short
"A buck has two states. When the high-side switch is on, the inductor sees $V_{in}-V_{out}$ and its current ramps up while feeding the load. When the switch turns off, the inductor current keeps flowing through the diode or low-side FET, the inductor sees $-V_{out}$, and the current ramps down. The output capacitor smooths the ripple. In steady state the up and down ramps balance, so $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$. A feedback loop adjusts the duty cycle to hold the output."
:::

::: long
"Let me walk through one switching cycle. At the start, the controller turns the high-side switch on. The switch node jumps up to $V_{in}$, and since the output is held near $V_{out}$ by the capacitor, the inductor suddenly has $V_{in}-V_{out}$ across it. $v = L\,di/dt$ tells me the current ramps linearly upward. That current is feeding the load and topping up the capacitor, and energy is accumulating in the inductor's magnetic field.

After a time $DT$, the switch turns off. The inductor current can't stop instantly, so the switch node is forced down until a second path conducts. In a diode buck that's the freewheel diode, which turns on at about $-0.5$ V. In a synchronous buck it's the low-side FET, after a short dead time. Now the inductor has $-V_{out}$ across it, so the current ramps down, still flowing into the load. The inductor is returning the energy it stored.

Throughout, the output capacitor is the shock absorber. Whenever the inductor current is above the load current, the extra goes into the capacitor, and whenever it's below, the capacitor supplies the shortfall. That's what keeps the output voltage nearly flat, with a few millivolts of ripple.

For steady state, the current must return to where it started each cycle. The rise is $(V_{in}-V_{out})DT/L$, the fall is $V_{out}(1-D)T/L$. Setting them equal gives $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$.

With 12 V in, 3.3 V out and 2 A, the duty cycle is 27.5 percent, so the switch is on 550 nanoseconds out of every 2 microseconds, and the inductor current ripples about 1 amp peak-to-peak around the 2 amp load current.

The final piece is feedback: a divider senses $V_{out}$, an error amplifier compares it to a reference, and the loop adjusts $D$ until they match, so it holds regulation as the load and input change."
:::

::: terms
- **On-state**: high-side switch on, SW near $V_{in}$, inductor current rising.
- **Off-state**: high-side switch off, freewheel path on, SW near ground, inductor current falling.
- **Volt-second balance**: in steady state $\int_0^T v_L\,dt = 0$, which fixes the DC conversion ratio.
- **Charge balance**: in steady state the capacitor's average current is zero.
- **Inductor current ripple ($\Delta I_L$)**: peak-to-peak variation of inductor current in a cycle.
- **Continuous conduction mode (CCM)**: inductor current stays above zero throughout the cycle.
- **Freewheeling**: the inductor's current continuing through the diode or low-side FET during the off-state.
- **Error amplifier**: compares the feedback voltage to the reference and drives the PWM stage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the switch turns on and off, the inductor stores energy and releases it, and the capacitor smooths it."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names the voltage across the inductor in each state ($V_{in}-V_{out}$, then $-V_{out}$) and gets the slopes from $v=L\,di/dt$.
- Says *where the load current comes from in each state*: the input through the switch, then the inductor's stored energy through the freewheel path.
- Derives $V_{out}=DV_{in}$ from equal up and down ramps, and mentions the capacitor's charge balance ($\bar I_L = I_{out}$).
- Gives numbers: 550 ns on, 1.02 A ripple, and notes ripple is a *design choice* via $L$ and $f_{sw}$.
- Mentions the feedback loop and that real converters add losses and may enter DCM at light load.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The inductor steps down the voltage."** The inductor doesn't scale voltage; the switching and the volt-second balance do. In steady state the inductor has zero average voltage.
- **Saying the capacitor supplies the load in the off-state.** The inductor current supplies the load during *both* states; the capacitor only handles the difference between $i_L$ and $I_{out}$.
- **Believing current flows through the freewheel diode "from ground" as new energy.** It is the inductor's stored current continuing on a different path. No new charge is created.
- **Mixing up the slopes.** On: $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ (steeper when $V_{out}$ is small). Off: $V_{out}/L$. Their ratio is $D$ to $(1-D)$ inverted.
- **Forgetting that the output current is the *average* inductor current**, not the peak. The peak is $I_{out}+\Delta I_L/2$, which matters for saturation.
- **Treating $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$ as always true.** In DCM the output is higher for the same $D$.
:::

## The setup in one picture

Everything below is about the circuit from [[q0175]]: a high-side switch, a freewheel path, an inductor $L$, an output capacitor $C_{out}$ and a load. For the analysis, assume $C_{out}$ is large enough that $V_{out}$ is nearly constant within one cycle. This makes the inductor voltage a clean rectangular wave.

## Off-state: discharging

At $t=DT$ the controller turns $Q_1$ off. The inductor current of ~2.5 A cannot vanish instantly (an inductor "resists change in current", [[q0043]]), so it forces SW downward until the freewheel path conducts. With a Schottky diode, SW goes to about $-0.4$ to $-0.5$ V. With a synchronous FET, SW goes to about $-I\,R_{DS(on)}$ (a few tens of mV) after the dead time.

## Why the current ramps *linearly*

A linear ramp is a consequence of the output being nearly constant. If $V_{out}$ is constant, then $v_L$ is a constant ($V_{in}-V_{out}$ or $-V_{out}$) during each state, and $i_L=\frac{1}{L}\int v_L\,dt$ is a straight line. That is also a design assumption: it breaks down if $C_{out}$ is tiny (ripple becomes big) or if the inductor saturates (the slope steepens, [[q0188]]).

## Steady state: two balances

**Volt-second balance on the inductor.** For the current to repeat each period, $\int_0^T v_L dt=0$. The area of the on-state rectangle equals the area of the off-state rectangle:

$$(V_{in}-V_{out})\,DT = V_{out}(1-D)T\ \Rightarrow\ V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$$

**Charge balance on the capacitor.** For $V_{out}$ to repeat, $\int_0^T i_C dt=0$, so the average inductor current equals the load current: $\bar I_L=I_{out}$. The inductor current's DC level is set by the load, and its AC part by $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$, $L$ and $f_{sw}$.

## Output ripple

Take the capacitor ripple current: a triangle with amplitude $\pm\Delta I_L/2$. It is positive for half the period (charging), and the charge added in that half is the area of a triangle, $\frac12\cdot\frac{T}{2}\cdot\frac{\Delta I_L}{2}=\frac{T\Delta I_L}{8}$. So

$$\Delta V_{C}=\frac{T\,\Delta I_L}{8C}=\frac{\Delta I_L}{8 f_{sw} C}$$

For our numbers: $1.018/(8\times500\text{ k}\times22\text{ µ})=11.6$ mV. A real capacitor's ESR adds $\Delta I_L\cdot ESR$ (about 5 mV for 5 mΩ), at a different phase. Ripple is covered in [[q0212]].

## How feedback holds the output

Everything above assumed a given $D$. A real converter doesn't have a fixed $D$; the controller finds it:

1. A resistor divider scales $V_{out}$ to $V_{FB}$.
2. An error amplifier compares $V_{FB}$ to $V_{ref}$ and produces a control voltage.
3. A PWM comparator turns that into a duty cycle.
4. If the load increases and $V_{out}$ sags, the error pushes $D$ up, which raises the average inductor current until the output recovers.

So $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$ is set by the divider and reference, and $D$ is whatever value makes this true. See [[q0178]].

## What changes in real hardware

- **Dead time and body-diode conduction** in a synchronous buck: for a few ns the body diode carries the current and SW goes about 0.7 V below ground ([[q0208]]).
- **Parasitic ringing** on SW when the high-side turns on (the hot loop's inductance rings with FET capacitance).
- **Losses shift the duty cycle** slightly: $D$ must be a bit higher than $V_{out}/V_{in}$ to cover the drops ([[q0180]]).
- **Light load:** below $I_{out}=\Delta I_L/2$ (about 0.51 A here) the current wants to go negative. A diode (or diode-emulating low-side FET) blocks it, giving DCM ([[q0229]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Any point-of-load rail**: the cycle described here runs in the 5 V to 1 V rails of nearly every embedded board.
- **Understanding scope captures**: the SW waveform is a rectangle with amplitude $V_{in}$ and duty $V_{out}/V_{in}$. You can estimate $D$ from the scope and check it against $V_{out}/V_{in}$ ([[q0180]]).
- **Inductor sizing**: the ramp slopes tell you the ripple current, which sets the saturation current rating ([[q0259]]).
- **Debugging**: if the inductor current doesn't ramp as expected, check $V_{in}-V_{out}$ across $L$. A wrong slope implies a wrong inductance or a saturating core.
- **Current-mode control**: the up-ramp of the inductor current is what a peak-current-mode controller senses and compares ([[q0252]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal components** (zero switch resistance, no diode drop). With losses, $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}-$(drops).
- **CCM** (inductor current positive for the whole cycle). In DCM the off-state is followed by an idle interval, and the formula changes ([[q0183]]).
- **Constant $V_{out}$ within a cycle**, which holds when $\Delta V_{out}\ll V_{out}$. Otherwise the ramps curve slightly.
- **Steady state.** After a load step the average inductor current changes over several cycles (transient behaviour, [[q0248]]).
- **Synchronous or diode freewheel**: the analysis is the same; only the off-state SW voltage ($\approx 0$ vs. $-V_f$) differs.
:::

::: followups
### What happens to the inductor ripple if you double the switching frequency?
It halves, since $\Delta I_L=(V_{in}-V_{out})D/(L f_{sw})$. At 1 MHz with the same 4.7 µH, the ripple would be 0.51 A instead of 1.02 A, and the capacitive output ripple would fall by 4 (because both $\Delta I_L$ and $1/f_{sw}$ shrink). The cost is higher switching loss and a shorter minimum on-time margin ([[q0216]]).

### Where does the load current come from during the off-state?
From the inductor, via the freewheel path. The inductor's magnetic energy converts back into current, which flows from ground through the diode or low-side FET into SW, through $L$ and into the load. The input supply provides nothing during this time.

### Why does the converter average to $D\,V_{in}$ and not $D\,V_{in}$ minus something because of the inductor?
Because in steady state the inductor has zero average voltage across it: it can't have a DC drop, since a DC voltage would make the current ramp forever. Any DC difference between the SW average and $V_{out}$ would be absorbed by a (tiny) drift of the average inductor current until the DC drop across the inductor's resistance accounts for it.

### What if the inductor were half the value? What changes besides ripple?
Ripple doubles (2.04 A p-p at $I_{out}=2$ A), the valley current drops to about 0.98 A, and the peak rises to about 3.0 A, so the saturation current rating needs to be higher. At light load the converter enters DCM sooner: the CCM/DCM boundary moves up to $\Delta I_L/2 \approx 1.0$ A. Transient response improves because a smaller $L$ slews current faster ([[q0248]]).

### Scenario: you measure SW and see a 4 V-high pulse train at 500 kHz on a converter that should make 3.3 V from 12 V. What's wrong?
SW should swing to about $V_{in}=12$ V. A 4 V amplitude suggests the supply isn't what you think (maybe a sagging input due to a missing $C_{in}$ or a current-limited source), or the probe/measurement is off. If $V_{in}$ is truly 4 V then the output can't be 3.3 V from 12 V. Measure $V_{in}$ at the converter's input pins first, then check duty cycle against $V_{out}/V_{in}$.
:::
