::: basics
### In plain words
An ideal capacitor has only capacitance. A real one also has a small resistance in series (ESR), a small inductance in series (ESL), and a very large resistance in parallel that lets a tiny leakage current through. The result is that a real capacitor only acts like a capacitor up to a certain frequency. Above that, the hidden inductance takes over and it acts like a coil.

### Picture it
Picture a bucket that you fill through a hose. The bucket is the capacitance. A narrow hose adds friction, like ESR. A long, heavy hose makes the flow slow to start or stop, like ESL. A tiny hole in the bottom of the bucket drains it slowly, like leakage. At gentle changes, only the bucket matters. At very fast changes, the hose dominates what you see. The analogy cannot show the exact way these parts add up in AC.

### Words you need
- **ESR**: equivalent series resistance, from the plates, terminals and dielectric losses.
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance, from the internal current loop and leads.
- **Leakage**: the tiny current through the imperfect insulator, shown as a large parallel resistor.
- **Self-resonant frequency**: where ESL and the capacitance cancel, so only ESR is left.
- **Parasitic**: an unwanted hidden property. See [[q0011]].

### The simplest example
Take a 100 nF ceramic capacitor with about 1 nH of ESL. The ESL and capacitance cancel at $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, which is about 16 MHz. Below 16 MHz, the impedance falls as frequency rises, like a capacitor. At 16 MHz, only the ESR is left, so the impedance is at its lowest. Above 16 MHz, the impedance rises like an inductor. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page draws the model, explains where each element comes from, and covers losses and DC-bias effects. See also [[q0024]].
:::

::: testing
Whether you can draw the standard capacitor equivalent circuit and, more importantly, say where each element **physically comes from** and when it matters. The interviewer wants:

1. The three main elements in series with the ideal $C$: **ESR** and **ESL**, with a large **parallel leakage** resistance. Bonus: dielectric absorption.
2. Origins: ESR from plates, terminals and dielectric loss; ESL from the current loop through the plates and leads (plus mounting); leakage from imperfect dielectric.
3. The consequence: the impedance curve is a bathtub with a self-resonant frequency, and above it the part is an inductor.
4. Type differences (ceramic, electrolytic, film, tantalum) and non-element effects such as DC-bias derating and temperature.
:::

::: why
Capacitors are the most-used parts on a board and the least ideal. Decoupling performance, regulator stability (output-capacitor ESR sets a zero in the loop), ripple heating in bulk capacitors, audio distortion in coupling caps, and the leakage of a sample-and-hold capacitor all come from parasitics. A designer who only uses $1/(j\omega C)$ will be surprised that the 10 µF capacitor next to the processor does nothing at 100 MHz, or that the "10 µF" ceramic is 3 µF at 5 V. This question tests whether you know the real part.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitance and dielectric** ([[q0022]], [[q0023]]): $C=\varepsilon A/d$; the dielectric material sets $\varepsilon$, loss and leakage.
- **Impedance of the ideal part** ([[q0024]]): $Z=1/(j\omega C)$.
- **Series parasitics**: ESR (loss) and ESL (inductance) in series with $C$.
- **Parallel parasitics**: insulation resistance (leakage) and dielectric absorption branches.
- **Self-resonance** ([[q0031]]): ESL and $C$ resonate at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{esl}C})$.
- **Non-ideal capacitance**: DC bias, temperature, aging and frequency alter $C$ itself, particularly in class-II ceramics.
:::

::: answer
"I draw the ideal capacitor C, with a series resistance, the ESR, and a series inductance, the ESL, in line with it, and then a large resistor in parallel with C for leakage. If I want to be thorough I add a small series R–C branch in parallel for dielectric absorption.

The ESR comes from the resistance of the plates and terminals, plus losses in the dielectric as it polarizes back and forth. The ESL comes from the current loop: current flows in through one terminal, across the plates, and out the other, which makes a small loop, and the leads and mounting add to it. For a small ceramic chip that's about half a nanohenry; for a leaded or electrolytic part it's several nanohenries. The leakage resistor represents the dielectric not being a perfect insulator, so a charged capacitor slowly discharges. It's typically gigaohms for ceramics and film.

The effect is that a capacitor is only a capacitor below its self-resonant frequency, where its impedance falls with frequency. Around that frequency, the impedance bottoms out at the ESR. Above it, the ESL dominates and the part acts like an inductor. A 100 nF ceramic with 1 nH of ESL resonates near 16 MHz. And besides these elements, real capacitors also change value: a class-II ceramic can lose half its capacitance at rated DC bias."
:::

::: technical
**Series model** (impedance): 
$$Z(\omega)=R_{esr}+j\omega L_{esl}+\frac{1}{j\omega C},\quad |Z|=\sqrt{R_{esr}^2+\left(\omega L_{esl}-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}$$
Minimum $|Z|=R_{esr}$ at $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{esl}C})$. Below $f_0$: capacitive ($-90^\circ$); above: inductive ($+90^\circ$).

**Loss measures:** $\tan\delta=\text{DF}=R_{esr}/X_C=\omega CR_{esr}$ (dissipation factor); $Q=1/\tan\delta$.

**Leakage:** parallel $R_{ins}$; time constant $R_{ins}C$ for self-discharge (typical datasheet spec for ceramics: $R_{ins}\cdot C\ge$ several hundred to thousands of MΩ·µF, or a cap in GΩ; film capacitors are orders higher; electrolytics have a leakage current $\sim0.01\,CV$ or 3 µA, whichever is larger).

**Dielectric absorption (DA):** a charged capacitor that is briefly shorted then released "recovers" some voltage; modelled by series R–C branches across $C$. Typical DA: C0G/film ≲0.1 %, X7R a fraction of a percent to a few percent, electrolytic ~10 % or more.

| Type | ESR | ESL | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| MLCC (0402) | 5–50 mΩ | 0.4–0.7 nH | low ESR/ESL; class-II loses C with bias |
| Aluminum electrolytic | 0.05–several Ω | 5–20 nH | high C, ESR rises when cold, finite life |
| Tantalum / polymer | 10 mΩ–1 Ω | 1–3 nH | polymers have very low ESR |
| Film | 1–50 mΩ | 5–20 nH (leaded) | very stable, low DA |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> All numbers are typical ranges; ESR and ESL depend on package, voltage rating, value and frequency. For design use the manufacturer's impedance curves or SPICE/S-parameter models.
:::

::: short
"The realistic model is the ideal capacitance in series with ESR, the resistance of plates, terminals and dielectric loss, and ESL, the inductance of the current loop, with a large leakage resistance in parallel. Because of ESL, the part is only a capacitor below its self-resonant frequency, where impedance bottoms out at the ESR; above it, it's an inductor. A 100 nF ceramic with 1 nH is around 16 MHz. Real capacitors also lose capacitance with DC bias and temperature."
:::

::: long
"If I draw a realistic capacitor, I start with the ideal C. In series with it, I put the ESR, which is the effective series resistance, and the ESL, which is the series inductance. Across the capacitor I put a large leakage resistance. And if the application is sample-and-hold or an integrator, I'd also add a series R-C branch in parallel to represent dielectric absorption.

Where they come from: the ESR is the resistance of the electrodes and terminals plus dielectric losses, which is why it also depends on frequency and temperature. The ESL comes from geometry: current has to run in one end, through the plates and out the other, forming a loop with some nanohenries of inductance, plus the pads and vias. The leakage resistance is the imperfect dielectric, gigaohms for ceramics and film, and it's the reason a charged capacitor loses its voltage slowly. Dielectric absorption is the dielectric slowly relaxing, so a cap that you discharge and release will creep back to a small percentage of its voltage.

The impedance curve is a V or bathtub: it falls at minus 20 dB per decade like 1 over omega C, reaches a minimum equal to ESR at the self-resonant frequency, then rises with ESL. A 100 nF 0402 with 1 nH is about 16 MHz; a 100 microfarad electrolytic with 10 nH and 0.3 ohms has a resonance around 160 kHz with a flat 0.3 ohm floor.

On top of the elements, I'd mention that the capacitance itself isn't constant, particularly for class-2 ceramics like X7R: it falls with DC bias, sometimes by half or more, and changes with temperature and age. So I'd derate it, or use C0G where stability matters, and check the manufacturer's curves."
:::

::: terms
- **ESR**: equivalent series resistance, lumping all loss.
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance of the capacitor and its connection.
- **Insulation resistance / leakage**: high parallel resistance giving DC leakage current.
- **Dissipation factor (tan δ)**: $\omega CR_{esr}$, the ratio of loss to stored energy.
- **Dielectric absorption**: voltage recovery after discharge because of slow polarization.
- **Self-resonant frequency**: $1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{esl}C})$, where $|Z|$ is minimum.
- **DC-bias derating**: loss of effective capacitance in class-II ceramics under applied voltage.
- **Class I (C0G/NP0) / class II (X7R, X5R)**: stable, low-$\varepsilon$ ceramics vs. high-$\varepsilon$, voltage- and temperature-dependent ceramics.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws C with ESR and says ESL "somewhere".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws the series ESR–ESL–C with parallel leakage (and optionally the DA branch) and gives origins for each.
- States the impedance function, the bathtub shape, and that the minimum equals ESR at the SRF.
- Gives numbers per capacitor type (ceramic vs electrolytic vs film) and a worked SRF.
- Notes that the capacitance itself varies (DC bias, temperature, aging) and what to use instead (C0G, film).
- Connects to design: decoupling, regulator loop stability (ESR zero), ripple current heating ($I^2R_{esr}$), sample-and-hold DA.
- Mentions mounting inductance (pads, vias) as part of the effective ESL.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting ESR in parallel.** ESR is series; leakage is the parallel one.
- **Forgetting ESL entirely.** That's the part that turns the capacitor into an inductor and limits decoupling.
- **Treating the nominal capacitance as constant.** A 10 µF X5R at its rated voltage can fall to a small fraction of nominal.
- **"Bigger capacitance always lowers high-frequency impedance."** Above the SRF only the ESL matters.
- **Ignoring ripple heating.** $P=I_{rms}^2R_{esr}$ in an electrolytic is what dries it out.
- **Assuming leakage is zero** for hold capacitors and long-time-constant filters; electrolytics leak microamps.
:::

::: deep
## Where each element comes from

A parallel-plate capacitor stores charge on two plates separated by a dielectric, $C=\varepsilon A/d$ ([[q0022]]). In an MLCC, interleaved electrodes are stacked and connected at two end terminations. Current has to flow along the electrodes to reach the charge on each plate, and across the termination, and that finite conductor sets the resistance. The path also encloses magnetic flux (current in on one side, out on the other): inductance. The dielectric isn't perfect: it polarizes with a lag (loss) and conducts a trace of DC (leakage).

## Impedance: the bathtub

For $R_{esr}=20$ mΩ and $L_{esl}=1$ nH, the 100 nF ceramic has $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{10^{-9}\cdot10^{-7}})=15.9$ MHz and a minimum $|Z|=20$ mΩ. An electrolytic 100 µF with ESR 0.3 Ω and ESL 10 nH has $f_0=159$ kHz and a floor at 0.3 Ω, far higher than the ceramic's.

Dissipation factor: $\tan\delta=\omega CR_{esr}$. For the ceramic at 1 MHz, $2\pi\cdot10^6\cdot10^{-7}\cdot0.02=0.0126$ (1.3 %), assuming the ESR is still 20 mΩ at 1 MHz (real ESR varies with frequency).

## Q of the series resonance: why a ceramic has a notch and an electrolytic doesn't

The series $R$–$L$–$C$ path has a characteristic impedance $Z_0=\sqrt{L_{esl}/C}$ and a quality factor $Q=Z_0/R_{esr}$. For the 100 nF MLCC, $Z_0=\sqrt{10^{-9}/10^{-7}}=0.1\ \Omega$ and $Q=0.1/0.02=5$, so there is a clear notch down to the ESR floor at 15.9 MHz. For the 100 µF electrolytic, $Z_0=\sqrt{10^{-8}/10^{-4}}=0.01\ \Omega$ and $Q=0.01/0.3=0.033$: the resonance is so heavily damped that the curve just slides from the capacitive slope onto a flat 0.3 Ω floor and then up the inductive slope. The notch exists only when $R_{esr}<Z_0$, and its depth is exactly $R_{esr}$.

This is also why very-low-ESR parts are not an unconditional win. In a board with two paralleled capacitors the high-Q notches leave sharp anti-resonant peaks between them, as the last follow-up describes. For a 10 µF capacitor with 2 nH ESL in parallel with the 100 nF part above (1 nH ESL), the anti-resonance falls where $Z_1+Z_2=0$:

$$f_{ar}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(L_1+L_2)\dfrac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2}}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{3\ \text{nH}\times 99.0\ \text{nF}}}=9.2\ \text{MHz}$$

with the SRFs at 1.1 MHz and 15.9 MHz on either side. Damping (a bit of ESR, or a third value that fills the gap) lowers the peak.

## Mounting inductance often dominates ESL

Of the three numbers in the model, the datasheet ESL of a small MLCC (about 0.4 to 0.7 nH) is often less than what the board adds. The pad-to-via loop is part of the effective ESL. A common estimate for a single via through a board of thickness $h$ and drill diameter $d$ (both in inches) is $L\approx5.08\,h\,[\ln(4h/d)+1]$ nH. For a 62 mil board and a 12 mil via that is about 1.27 nH per via, which is more than double the capacitor's own ESL.

Add that to the 1 nH part: with 2.3 nH total the self-resonance moves from 15.9 MHz to 10.5 MHz, and at 100 MHz the inductive reactance is 1.45 Ω instead of 0.63 Ω. That is why layout guidance says to put the via right at the pad (or in it), use two vias per pad on power-delivery paths, and prefer a smaller footprint when frequency matters.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The via-inductance expression is an empirical approximation and the real value depends on the return path (the nearest plane and other vias). Treat it as an estimate; use a field solver or measured data for a final design.

## Leakage and dielectric absorption in numbers

Take a 1 nF C0G hold capacitor on a precision sample-and-hold, with an insulation resistance of 10 GΩ. Its self-discharge time constant is $R_{ins}C=10\ \text{s}$. With 1 V stored, the leakage current is $1/10^{10}=100$ pA and the droop rate is $I/C=100\ \text{pA}/1\ \text{nF}=0.1$ V/s, about 100 µV in a millisecond. That is tolerable for a short hold. If the capacitor were an electrolytic with a leakage of microamps, the same hold would collapse in milliseconds.

Dielectric absorption is a slower, separate effect. Suppose a hold capacitor is charged to 4 V, then discharged briefly and left open. If it has a DA of 0.1% (an upper-end figure for good film or C0G parts), the voltage creeps back by $0.001\times4=4$ mV. In a 12-bit converter with a 4.096 V reference the LSB is 1 mV, so the memory of the previous sample is four LSBs, which is why high-resolution sample-and-hold stages specify low-DA dielectrics such as C0G, PTFE or polypropylene film.

## Worked example: DC-bias derating on a rail

An LDO output requires 22 µF of effective capacitance at 3.3 V. If the chosen 10 V X5R part keeps only 40% of its nominal value at 3.3 V (a plausible but part-specific number, see the DC-bias note below), you need $22/0.4=55$ µF nominal, for example three 22 µF parts (66 µF nominal, about 26 µF effective), instead of one 22 µF part. The same 40% factor is also why a "10 µF" part can appear as 4 µF on a pulsed load test. The honest procedure is to read the vendor's capacitance-versus-bias curve for the exact part number, use the value at the operating voltage, and add a margin for temperature and aging.

## What isn't an "element": the capacitance itself changes

- **DC bias (class II ceramics):** the permittivity of X5R/X7R falls as the electric field in the dielectric rises. A typical small-package, high-value X5R/X7R at its rated voltage may retain only a fraction (sometimes 30–50 %) of its nominal capacitance. Smaller packages and higher capacitance per volume are worse. C0G/NP0 shows essentially none.
- **Temperature:** X7R is ±15 % over −55 to +125 °C; C0G is ±30 ppm/K; Y5V can swing by −80 %.
- **Aging:** class-II ceramics lose a few percent per decade-hour (log time) after being heated above the Curie temperature; reflow resets it.
- **Frequency:** $\varepsilon$ and ESR vary with frequency; electrolytics lose capacitance above tens of kHz.
- **Piezoelectric effect:** class II ceramics can generate voltage from vibration or "sing" under AC ripple.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The amount of DC-bias derating depends strongly on dielectric, case size, rated voltage and manufacturer. Always read the vendor's capacitance-vs-bias curve (e.g. from the vendor's simulation tool) for your exact part.

## Consequences in real designs

1. **Decoupling:** choose package/value to put the SRF and bathtub where the noise is; reduce mounting inductance with short, wide connections and vias close to the pad ([[q0032]], [[q0031]]).
2. **LDO and regulator stability:** the output capacitor's ESR creates a zero at $1/(2\pi R_{esr}C)$ that some regulators require; a ceramic with ESR 5 mΩ can destabilize an old LDO that expected 0.3–5 Ω.
3. **Ripple current:** $P=I_{rms}^2R_{esr}$ heats the electrolytic; life roughly halves per +10 °C ([[q0036]]).
4. **Precision timing/sample-and-hold:** use C0G or film for low DA and leakage.
5. **Audio coupling:** class-II ceramic's voltage dependence causes distortion; use film or C0G where signal-level linearity matters.

## Verifying capacitor behavior on the bench

1. **Capacitance versus bias.** A standard LCR meter at 1 kHz and 0.5 to 1 V RMS shows the small-signal value near zero bias. Use a meter or impedance analyzer with a DC-bias option and step the bias from 0 to the rated voltage; plot $C$ against bias and compare with the vendor's curve.
2. **ESR and SRF.** An impedance analyzer or VNA (shunt-through S21 is the usual choice for milliohm-level impedances) shows the notch. The minimum is the ESR and the frequency is the SRF; check that the fit gives $L_{esl}$ in the expected range and then repeat on your own PCB footprint to find the mounting inductance.
3. **Leakage.** Charge the capacitor for a long time at its working voltage and measure the settled current with a picoammeter or an electrometer. Electrolytics need minutes to settle. A leakage reading that never settles points to a damaged or reformed part.
4. **Ripple heating.** Run the real ripple current and watch the case temperature with a thermocouple or thermal camera. The temperature rise divided by $I_{rms}^2R_{esr}$ gives the part's effective thermal resistance in your layout.
5. **Dielectric absorption.** Charge for a long time, short the capacitor through a small resistor for a short fixed interval, release it, and monitor the open-circuit voltage with a high-impedance meter. The recovered voltage as a percentage of the charge voltage is the DA.

::: applications
- **Decoupling and bypassing** on digital ICs, with ESL-optimized MLCCs and mixed values.
- **DC-link and bulk capacitors** in converters, sized by ripple current and ESR.
- **LDO/regulator output capacitors**, where ESR and ESL set the transient and stability behavior.
- **Sample-and-hold, integrators and timing**: C0G/film chosen for low leakage and DA.
- **EMI filters** (X/Y safety capacitors and feedthrough capacitors with ultra-low ESL).
- **RF blocking and matching**: capacitors chosen for SRF above the operating frequency.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Lumped linear model**; valid until the part is a sizeable fraction of a wavelength.
- ESR and ESL are treated as **constants**; in reality ESR depends on frequency and temperature (ceramic ESR has a minimum near the SRF; electrolytic ESR rises sharply when cold).
- DA and leakage are included only when relevant (precision, long time constants).
- Numerical parasitic values are typical for the named type; vendor data wins.
- The same series model with different values covers all capacitor families, but some parts (feedthrough, 3-terminal, reverse geometry) have different topologies that reduce ESL.
:::

::: followups
### What is the self-resonant frequency of a 10 µF electrolytic with 15 nH of ESL?
$f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{15\times10^{-9}\cdot10^{-5}})=411$ kHz. Above it the part is an inductor, so it cannot decouple MHz noise; pair it with ceramics.

### Why does capacitor ESR matter for LDO stability?
In some LDOs the output capacitor's ESR creates a zero that adds phase lead to compensate the load pole. Too-low ESR (a modern ceramic) removes that zero and can cause oscillation; modern LDOs are designed for ceramics, older ones specify a minimum ESR. Check the datasheet's "stable ESR range".

### How much heat does a capacitor with 50 mΩ ESR dissipate with 3 A rms ripple?
$P=I^2R=9\cdot0.05=0.45$ W. For an electrolytic, a few tenths of a watt is a substantial self-heating contribution to its life calculation; compare with the datasheet's ripple-current rating.

### What if you measure much lower capacitance than the label?
Check the measurement conditions (frequency and test voltage, typically 1 kHz/1 V for class II) and the bias; at the actual operating voltage the class-II ceramic may have lost 50 % or more. Also check aging time since reflow and temperature. If it's electrolytic, check frequency, ESR and age (dry-out).

### Why do two paralleled capacitors of different values create a peak at some frequency?
Below its SRF the larger capacitor is capacitive and above it inductive, while the small one is still capacitive. Where the inductance of the large part resonates with the capacitance of the small one, you get a parallel (anti-)resonance with high impedance, limited by ESR. Damping with ESR or a third value reduces it ([[q0032]]).
