::: testing
Whether you know thermal reliefs exist for *manufacturing*, not for electrical performance, and that they trade assembly ease against current capacity and heat flow. The interviewer wants the definition (a pad connected to a plane or pour through narrow spokes instead of a solid connection), the reason (the plane would otherwise sink soldering heat and cause cold joints, tombstoning or painful rework), the cost (higher electrical and thermal resistance), and when you should switch it off (high-current pads, exposed thermal pads, RF grounds).
:::

::: why
This is one of the most common reasons a prototype board is hard to hand-assemble or a power pad overheats. Reworking a through-hole connector tied solidly to a ground plane can take minutes and wreck the board, whereas a four-spoke relief makes it easy. On the other side, a relief on a power MOSFET's source pad quietly doubles its resistance and limits its current. Knowing when to apply it is a real layout skill, and it leads directly into reworkability questions ([[q0637]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Heat sinking by copper**: a large pour conducts soldering heat away from a pad so the joint does not reach solder temperature. See [[q0535]].
- **Thermal and electrical resistance of a narrow link**: $R = L/(kA)$ for heat, $R = \rho L/A$ for current.
- **Wetting**: solder flows only when pad and lead reach the liquidus temperature together.
- **Tombstoning / cold joint**: failures caused by uneven heating of the pads. See [[q0537]].
- **Current capacity of the spokes**: their combined width sets the current limit. See [[q0534]].
:::

::: answer
"A thermal relief is the way a pad connects to a copper plane or pour using a few narrow spokes with an air gap around the rest of the pad, instead of fusing the pad solidly into the copper. You see it as a little wheel or cross around a through-hole pin on a ground plane.

The reason is soldering. A plane is a huge heat sink, so if the pad is solidly tied to it, the iron or the reflow oven has a hard time getting that pad up to solder temperature. You end up with cold joints, or the part tombstones because one pad melts first, and reworking it is miserable. The spokes limit how fast heat can leave the pad, so it heats up like an isolated pad but is still electrically connected.

The cost is that the spokes are a bottleneck: they have higher resistance and lower current capacity than a solid connection, and they add a bit of inductance. So I use reliefs for ordinary through-hole pins and small pads on pours, and I turn them off for high-current power pads, exposed thermal pads where I want heat to flow out, and anything where inductance matters, like some high-frequency ground connections. Or I make the spokes wider."
:::

::: technical
**Geometry.** A thermal relief pad has a copper pad, an annular clearance gap, and typically four spokes (at 0° or 45°) bridging the gap to the surrounding pour or plane. Typical CAD defaults: spoke width 0.25 to 0.5 mm, gap 0.2 to 0.3 mm. Tool rules usually let you set spoke count, width and angle per net or per pad class.

**Why it works.** Heat flow into a plane from a pad of radius $r_1$ spreads radially: $R_{th,plane} = \dfrac{\ln(r_2/r_1)}{2\pi kt}$. With 1 oz copper ($t = 35$ µm, $k \approx 385$ W/m·K), $r_1 = 1.2$ mm, $r_2 = 15$ mm gives about 30 K/W. Four spokes of 0.25 mm × 0.25 mm add $L/(kA) = 74$ K/W each, or 18.5 K/W in parallel, about 1.6x. The effect is larger on multilayer boards where a through-hole barrel is tied to several planes, and the narrow link limits flow *while the joint is heating*.

**Electrical cost.** Four 0.25 mm × 0.25 mm spokes are four squares in parallel: 0.12 mΩ and about 1 mm of total width, roughly 3.2 A at a 20 °C rise (IPC-2221, outer layer); 0.3 mm spokes give about 3.7 A and 0.5 mm spokes about 5.4 A. That is fine for logic and low-power rails, not for 10 A.

**Inductance.** The spokes add a little partial inductance in the return path, which matters for decoupling and RF ground connections.

**When to avoid.** High-current power pads, power-IC thermal pads, high-frequency grounds, and any pad where heat extraction is the goal ([[q0540]]). For those use a direct connection and manage assembly with more heat, preheat or a suitable profile.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The thermal ratio between solid and relief connections depends heavily on pad size, plane layers, barrel plating and the heat source. The 1.6x figure is a single-layer estimate. Manufacturer and assembler design rules (spoke width and gap) vary; follow your fabricator's guidance.
:::

::: long
"A thermal relief is how a pad connects to a copper plane or pour using a few narrow spokes, usually four, with a clearance gap around the rest of the pad, instead of a solid connection. On a layout it looks like a small wheel around a through-hole pin sitting in a ground plane.

The problem it solves is heat. During hand soldering, wave or reflow, the joint has to reach solder temperature. A big plane next to a pad is an excellent heat sink: it carries the heat away as fast as the iron supplies it, so the solder doesn't flow properly, and you get a cold joint. On small surface-mount parts, if one pad is tied to a pour and the other isn't, they melt at different times and the part can tombstone. And when you need to rework a connector pin that is solidly tied to a plane, you can spend minutes with the iron and damage the board.

With a relief, the narrow spokes limit the heat flow. As a rough number, in one layer, four 0.25 millimetre spokes add around 18 kelvin per watt in series with a plane that is itself around 30. The electrical connection is still there, so the net is still connected.

The price is that the spokes are a bottleneck. Four spokes of 0.25 mm have the cross-section of a trace one millimetre wide, so roughly 3 amps, and they add resistance and inductance. So on high-current pads I disable it or widen the spokes, on thermal pads where I want heat to leave I use a direct connection with vias, and on RF grounds I avoid it for inductance. For normal through-hole pins, reliefs are the default."
:::

::: terms
- **Thermal relief**: a connection to a plane through spokes with a gap, to limit heat flow during soldering.
- **Spoke**: one of the narrow copper bridges in a thermal relief.
- **Anti-pad / clearance**: copper-free gap around a pad or via.
- **Direct (solid) connect**: pad flooded directly into the copper area, with no gap.
- **Cold joint**: a solder joint that never reached full wetting temperature; dull, weak and unreliable.
- **Tombstoning**: a chip component standing on one end after reflow.
- **Exposed (thermal) pad**: a bottom pad on a package to conduct heat to the board.
- **Rework**: removing and replacing a part after assembly.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it connects a pad to a plane with spokes so it's easier to solder."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the physical reason: plane as heat sink, solder wetting, cold joints, tombstoning, rework.
- Quantifies the cost: spoke resistance, current limit (about 3 to 5 A for common spoke sizes), thermal resistance.
- Knows the typical parameters (0.25 to 0.5 mm spokes, 0.2 to 0.3 mm gap, four spokes).
- Says when to disable it: high-current pads, thermal pads, RF/low-inductance grounds.
- Mentions the trade between assembly and electrical performance, and alternatives (wider spokes, more spokes, preheat).
- Notes that the benefit is smaller than people assume and depends on the stack.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking reliefs are for electrical or EMC reasons.** They are for manufacturing and rework; electrically they are a penalty.
- **Leaving default reliefs on a power pad.** Four default spokes can limit a 10 A source pin to a few amps and run hot.
- **Using reliefs on an exposed thermal pad.** That defeats the purpose of conducting heat away. Connect directly and add vias ([[q0540]]).
- **Making the spokes too wide, then expecting an assembly benefit.** If the spokes are as wide as the pad, it is effectively a solid connection.
- **Forgetting that inner planes need reliefs too.** The barrel of a through-hole pin touches each plane layer it crosses; relieve those layers, not just the outer layer.
- **Assuming reliefs eliminate rework difficulty.** They reduce it; preheating the board still helps for heavy ground planes.
:::

::: deep
## The problem: copper is a very good heat sink

Solder melts around 183 °C (eutectic tin-lead) or 217 to 220 °C (SAC lead-free), and the joint has to be hot for a few seconds with both pad and lead above that. A tiny pad isolated by traces heats quickly. A pad flooded into a 1 oz plane is in thermal contact with a huge heat reservoir. Copper's thermal conductivity is about 385 W/(m·K), roughly 1,300 times FR-4's through-plane value (0.3 W/m·K), so heat travels easily along the copper.

A plane's resistance to a spreading heat source follows the same logarithmic law as electrical resistance:

$$R_{th} = \frac{1}{2\pi k t}\ln\frac{r_2}{r_1}$$

For 1 oz copper, $2\pi kt = 0.0847$ W/K, so each unit of $\ln(r_2/r_1)$ is 11.8 K/W. From a 1.2 mm radius pad to a 15 mm radius gives $\ln 12.5 = 2.53$, about 30 K/W. A 25 mm × 25 mm pour also stores heat: $0.196\ \text{g}\times0.385\ \text{J/(g·K)} \approx 75$ mJ/K, so it takes substantial energy to bring it up in temperature. When a pin is tied to *several* plane layers, each one acts in parallel and the effective resistance to the soldering heat can be 5 to 10 K/W, so a 40 W iron can't lift the joint above solder temperature.

## The fix: put a thermal resistor in series

A thermal relief replaces the direct copper contact with a ring gap plus spokes, so heat must pass through them. A spoke of width $w$, length $l$ and thickness $t$ has

$$R_{th,spoke} = \frac{l}{k\,w\,t}$$

For $w = l = 0.25$ mm: $0.25\times10^{-3}/(385\cdot0.25\times10^{-3}\cdot35\times10^{-6}) = 74$ K/W; four in parallel give 18.6 K/W; with 0.5 mm spokes it is 9.3 K/W. The electrical resistance of those four spokes is only $0.48\ \text{mΩ}/4 = 0.12$ mΩ, and the thermal numbers are what matters: the isolated pad's heat path now includes an extra 10 to 20 K/W in series with the plane's own. That is a modest ratio per layer (about 1.6x for a single layer in this model), but it adds up when applied across all layers the barrel touches, and the most important contribution is that the narrow bridges throttle heat flow *during the first seconds* when the joint is rising to temperature.

## What it costs electrically

The spokes are short narrow traces in the supply or return path:

- Current: the IPC-2221 outer-layer chart gives about 3.2 A for a total spoke width of 1 mm (four 0.25 mm spokes), 3.7 A for four 0.3 mm spokes and 5.4 A for four 0.5 mm spokes (20 °C rise). The numbers are conservative because spokes are short and pinned between copper masses, but they are still a good planning limit.
- Resistance: 0.12 mΩ for four 0.25 mm spokes in this geometry, negligible for signals but significant for a 20 A source pad in a MOSFET (whose entire Rds(on) might be 2 mΩ).
- Inductance: tens of picohenries, small but not zero; at hundreds of MHz this matters for decoupling grounds ([[q0553]]).
- Thermal conduction out: the same bottleneck that helps assembly hurts cooling. A power pad that needs to dump 3 W into a plane should be connected directly ([[q0540]]).

## Rules of thumb

| Pad type | Relief? | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Through-hole signal/ground pin on a plane | Yes (4 spokes, 0.25 to 0.3 mm) | Easy hand soldering and rework |
| Small SMD passive on a pour | Yes, or a short neck ([[q0537]]) | Avoid tombstoning |
| High-current power pad (source, shunt, connector) | Wider spokes or solid | Current capacity and Rds loss |
| Exposed thermal pad of a power IC | Solid, plus thermal vias | Heat must flow out ([[q0540]]) |
| RF ground vias / decoupling returns | Solid | Minimise inductance |
| Inner plane layers that a barrel crosses | Yes, same as outer | Barrel touches every plane |

A practical compromise for a high-current through-hole pin (say a 10 A terminal block) is to give each plane connection wide (0.5 to 1 mm) spokes or a direct connection on only the layers that carry the current, and to rely on a hotter iron and board preheat for assembly. Wave soldering and selective soldering add heat through a larger area than hand soldering, which is why many assemblers specify minimum spoke widths in their DFM rules.

## Reflow and small parts

For SMD components, the problem is differential heating. If pad A is tied to a ground pour and pad B is on a thin signal trace, B melts first, and the surface tension of that melt can rotate the component onto end (the Manhattan or tombstone effect). Reliefs, or just a short narrow neck, make the thermal mass of both ends similar. Modern solder pastes and ovens tolerate more asymmetry, but it is still good practice.

## Rework

Hot-air rework and iron rework both fight the plane. Larger parts tied solidly to inner planes can need pre-heating from below, higher tip temperatures, and longer dwell, all of which risk lifting pads or delaminating the board. Reliefs reduce this risk, which is why designers care about them for connectors and heavy pins ([[q0637]]).

::: applications
- **Through-hole connectors and headers** on a ground plane, where hand soldering is common.
- **Pull-up and bypass capacitors** (0402/0603) on ground pours, where a short narrow neck prevents tombstoning.
- **Power inductor and bulk capacitor terminals**: reliefs with wide spokes or solid connection, depending on current.
- **Power MOSFET source and exposed pads**: solid connection and thermal vias to a pour.
- **Mixed-technology boards** where wave or selective soldering of through-hole parts uses a plane-heavy stack-up.
- **Test points and jumpers** that may be reworked often.
:::

::: assumptions
The answer assumes conventional lead-free or leaded solder processes on FR-4 with 1 oz copper, and that "thermal relief" means the standard spoked connection to a pour or plane (also called a "thermal" in Altium or Eagle, and "thermal relief" in KiCad). Alternatives: some fabs and assemblers recommend solid connections on small SMD pads if the paste and profile are tuned, and many modern designs use solid connections everywhere with a preheating assembly process. The relative benefit depends on pad size, number of planes the barrel touches, and whether the process is hand, wave, selective or reflow. Where a design needs both good thermal/electrical connection and easy assembly, wider spokes or more spokes are the usual compromise.
:::

::: followups
### Why would you not use a thermal relief on the exposed pad of a power IC?
That pad's job is to carry heat from the die to the board. A relief would add a series thermal resistance (tens of K/W in my estimate for narrow spokes), raising the junction temperature. I would connect it directly to a copper pour on the top layer and add an array of thermal vias to inner or bottom copper. Assembly is handled by the reflow profile, not by the layout.

### How much current can a four-spoke relief carry?
It depends on spoke width. For four 0.25 mm spokes the combined width is 1 mm, which is roughly 3.2 A at a 20 °C rise on an outer layer by IPC-2221. With 0.5 mm spokes it is about 5.4 A. Short spokes between large copper masses can run somewhat hotter than that estimate allows, so for more than a few amps I'd widen the spokes or use direct connection.

### Do thermal reliefs affect EMC or decoupling?
Slightly. The spokes add tens of picohenries to the ground return, which matters for high-frequency decoupling capacitors and RF ground vias. For those I use a solid connection and let the assembler deal with heat, or place several vias per pad to shorten the path.

### What happens if you leave off reliefs on a heavy ground connector?
The pad cannot reach solder temperature at normal iron settings. You get dull, cold joints with poor wetting, or you raise the iron temperature and risk burning the board or lifting the pad. Wave solder might give incomplete hole fill. Reworking that pin later is hard. That is the classic reason for turning reliefs on for connectors.

### A board has a 15 A through-hole terminal connected to a plane. Relief or solid?
For 15 A, reliefs with default spokes are inadequate, so I would use solid connections on the layers that carry the current, and several wide spokes (0.5 to 1 mm) on the others if assembly demands it. I would also tell the assembler it is a heavy thermal mass pin and ask for preheat or selective soldering.
:::

::: interpretations
"Thermal relief pad" can also be heard as the thermal pad (exposed pad) of a package; that is a different thing, the opposite in intent: the package's thermal pad conducts heat out, and the relief is the *spoked* connection that limits it. Related questions: board-level heat reduction ([[q0540]]), rework ([[q0637]]), and via stitching ([[q0539]]).
:::
