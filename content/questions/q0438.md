::: testing
Whether you can turn the challenges of [[q0437]] into a working circuit with numbers. The interviewer expects a requirements list, a clean schematic, the sizing of each part, and a statement of where the design stops working. They are checking:

1. You derive the gate swing from the supply: off at $V_{in}$, on at $V_{in}-V_{GS,on}$.
2. You use an NFET level shifter rated for the bus, with a pull-up to the supply and a Zener to clamp $V_{GS}$.
3. You size $R_c$, $R_{gs}$ and $V_Z$ from dissipation, speed and tolerance, and check the extremes of supply voltage.
4. You know the limits (PWM frequency, switching loss) and the upgrade path (push-pull stage, gate-driver IC, or an NFET with bootstrap).
:::

::: why
A PFET high-side switch with a level-shifting NFET is the standard way to switch a load or a slow PWM on a 24 to 60 V rail from a 3.3 V MCU, without a bootstrap or charge pump. It is also the circuit most often copied with the wrong parts: a 60 V shifter on a 48 V bus, no clamp, or a PWM at 20 kHz. Working through the numbers shows what the circuit can and cannot do, and why the page [[q0437]] lists the challenges it does.
:::

::: concepts
- **PFET gate swing**: off at $V_G=V_{in}$, on at $V_G\approx V_{in}-10$ to $12$ V ([[q0097]], [[q0437]]).
- **Level shifter**: an NFET with its source at ground pulls the gate node down through a resistor ([[q0429]]).
- **Zener clamp**: limits $|V_{GS}|$ independently of the supply voltage.
- **Pull-up to the source**: holds the PFET off when the shifter is off or unpowered ([[q0430]]).
- **Gate time constant**: $RC_{iss}$ and the Miller plateau set edge time and loss ([[q0431]]).
- **Low-side NFET drive**: the shifter's gate network is the same as in [[q0429]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd design it for a 48 volt bus, a 5 amp motor and a 3.3 volt microcontroller. The PFET has its source on the bus and its drain to the motor. It needs to be off with the gate at the bus voltage and on with the gate about 12 volts below it.

I'll use a small N-channel FET as the level shifter. Its source is at ground, so the GPIO can drive its gate through 220 ohms with a 100 k pulldown, like any low-side switch. Its drain connects through a 10 kilohm resistor to the PFET's gate node. A second resistor, also 10 k, pulls the gate up to the bus, which holds the PFET off whenever the shifter is off or unpowered. And a 12 volt Zener from gate to source clamps V_GS so the gate can never be pulled more than 12 volts below the source, even at 60 volts.

When the GPIO goes high, the shifter turns on and pulls the gate node toward ground through the 10 k, but the Zener stops it at the bus minus 12 volts, so V_GS is minus 12 and the PFET is fully on. The current in that path is about 3.6 milliamps at 48 volts, about 130 milliwatts in the resistor, so I'd use a half-watt part. The shifter needs to be rated above the bus: a 100 volt part. When the GPIO goes low the pull-up recharges the gate.

The limit is speed: 10 k with a 2 nanofarad gate is a 20 microsecond time constant, with a plateau of a few microseconds at 5 amps and 48 volts, so about 0.5 millijoule per edge: fine at 100 hertz to 1 kilohertz, a problem above that. For faster PWM I'd add a push-pull stage, or switch to an NFET with a bootstrap driver."
:::

::: technical
**Requirements.** $V_{in}=48$ V nominal (range 24 to 60 V), $I_L=5$ A, MCU 3.3 V ($V_{OH}\ge3.0$ V), PWM up to about 1 kHz (resistor drive), load inductive (motor), 100% duty required.

**Q1 (PFET).** $V_{DS}\ge100$ V (bus 60 V plus flyback and margin), $V_{GS}$ rating $\pm20$ V, $V_{GS(th)}$ about −2 to −4 V, $R_{DS(on)}\le50$ mΩ at $V_{GS}=-10$ V, $I_D\ge10$ A. Conduction loss at 5 A: $25\times0.05=1.25$ W.

**Gate clamp $D_Z$.** $V_Z=12$ V (tolerance 5%: 11.4 to 12.6 V), cathode to source, anode to gate. $V_{GS,on}=-V_Z$; $|V_{GS}|\le12.6$ V$<20$ V. Power rating 500 mW or more.

**Pull-up $R_{gs}$.** Gate to source, 10 kΩ, in parallel with $D_Z$. Holds $V_{GS}=0$ when the shifter is off or unpowered. Current when on: $V_Z/R_{gs}=1.2$ mA.

**Level shifter $Q_2$ (NFET), $R_c$.** $Q_2$: $V_{DS}\ge100$ V, logic-level (gate threshold below about 1.5 V, $R_{DS(on)}$ spec at 3.3 V), small package; $R_c=10$ kΩ from $Q_2$'s drain to the PFET gate. On-state current $I_c=(V_{in}-V_Z-V_{ds})/R_c$: 3.6 mA at 48 V; 4.8 mA at 60 V. Power in $R_c$: 130 mW at 48 V, 230 mW at 60 V, so a 0.5 W (or 1 W) resistor rated above 60 V. Zener current at 48 V: $3.6-1.2=2.4$ mA, 29 mW.

**$Q_2$ gate network.** $R_{g2}=220$ Ω series, $R_{pd2}=100$ kΩ to ground: default off ([[q0430]], [[q0431]]).

**Supply tolerance.** With $R_{gs}=R_c$ the gate voltage is $V_{GS,on}=-\min(V_Z,\,V_{in}/2)$. At $V_{in}=24$ V: $-12$ V (just at the knee); at 16 V: $-8$ V (still on, higher $R_{DS(on)}$); at 12 V: $-6$ V. Below about 16 V the on-resistance degrades; for wide-range supplies, lower $R_{gs}$ relative to $R_c$ or use a lower $V_Z$ (10 V).

**Switching.** $\tau_{on}=R_cC_{iss}=20$ µs; $\tau_{off}=R_{gs}C_{iss}=20$ µs. Plateau: $I_g\approx(V_{in}-V_{plat})/R_c=4.3$ mA, $t_{pl}=Q_{gd}/I_g=20\text{ nC}/4.3\text{ mA}=4.7$ µs, $E\approx\tfrac12V_{in}I_Lt_{pl}=0.56$ mJ per edge: 1.1 W at 1 kHz (two edges per period). Bridge or PWM above a few kHz needs the upgrade below.

**Protection.** TVS on the bus (standoff above 48 V, clamp below the PFET and shifter ratings); flyback diode across the motor from ground to the PFET drain; optional gate-source TVS.

**Upgrade (faster).** A complementary emitter-follower pair, powered between $V_{in}$ and $V_{in}-12$ V (the Zener node, decoupled by a capacitor), driven by the same shifter, gives 0.3 A or more of gate drive: $t_{pl}=20\text{ nC}/0.3\text{ A}=67$ ns, and switching loss at 20 kHz about 0.32 W. Or use a gate-driver IC or a load-switch controller with an internal charge pump driving an N-channel FET.
:::

::: long
"Let me set the requirements. A 48 volt bus, up to 60 with transients, a 5 amp motor, a 3.3 volt microcontroller, and I want full duty and a slow PWM, maybe up to a kilohertz. The PFET has its source on the bus and its drain feeding the motor, with a flyback diode from ground to the drain.

The gate needs to be at the bus voltage to be off, and about 12 volts below the bus to be on. The microcontroller can't produce either, so I need a level shifter. I'll use a small logic-level N-channel FET, source at ground, rated 100 volts since it'll see the whole bus when it's off. The GPIO drives its gate through 220 ohms, with a 100 k pulldown so the default is off, exactly the low-side circuit I'd use for any load.

The shifter's drain goes through a 10 kilohm resistor, R_c, to the PFET's gate. From the gate to the source I put two parts in parallel: a 10 k pull-up resistor and a 12 volt Zener, cathode to the bus. When the shifter is off, the gate sits at the bus through the 10 k and V_GS is zero: the PFET is off, and that's also the state if the MCU or shifter loses power. When the GPIO goes high, the shifter pulls the gate node toward ground through R_c, and the Zener stops it when V_GS reaches minus 12 volts. So V_GS is minus 12 at 48 volts, at 60 volts, whatever the bus, protecting the oxide, and the on-resistance is fully enhanced.

Numbers: standing current at 48 volts is about 3.6 milliamps, 130 milliwatts in R_c, 230 at 60 volts, so I'd pick a half-watt resistor rated for the voltage. The Zener carries 2.4 milliamps, 29 milliwatts. At low bus voltages, with the two resistors equal, V_GS is half the bus, so it's still minus 8 at 16 volts but I'd check the minimum supply.

Speed. The gate has about 2 nanofarads, so with 10 k each way the time constant is 20 microseconds, and the Miller plateau at 5 amps, 48 volts lasts about 5 microseconds, 0.56 millijoule per edge. That's a watt at 1 kilohertz, so this is a slow-PWM or load-switch design. For faster I'd add a complementary push-pull stage on the gate, which is 70 times faster, or use a charge-pump driver and an N-channel FET."
:::

::: terms
- **Level shifter**: small NFET that converts the 0 to 3.3 V command to a gate pull-down referenced to the bus.
- **$R_c$**: resistor from the shifter's drain to the PFET gate; sets turn-on current and standing dissipation.
- **$R_{gs}$**: gate-to-source pull-up; holds the PFET off and recharges the gate on turn-off.
- **Gate Zener ($D_Z$)**: clamps $V_{GS}$ at $-V_Z$; cathode at the source.
- **Standing current**: current that flows continuously in the on state, $(V_{in}-V_Z)/R_c$.
- **Miller plateau time ($t_{pl}$)**: duration of the $V_{DS}$ transition during which most switching loss occurs.
- **Flyback diode**: freewheel diode across the inductive load.
- **Bus TVS**: transient suppressor on the supply line.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws an NFET pulling down the PFET gate with a pull-up resistor.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts from requirements and writes the on/off gate voltages relative to the bus.
- Includes the **Zener clamp** and says why a plain divider fails over a wide supply range (−24 V at 48 V).
- Rates the level-shifter, resistors and Zener for the **bus maximum** plus margin, and checks standing power at 60 V.
- Checks the minimum supply behaviour ($V_{GS,on}=-\min(V_Z,V_{in}/2)$).
- Quantifies speed and switching loss and states the PWM frequency ceiling; gives the push-pull or NFET-driver upgrade.
- Adds the flyback diode, bus TVS and default-off analysis.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using a divider instead of a Zener** on a variable bus: $V_{GS}$ swings with the supply and exceeds the rating at high bus voltage.
- **A 60 V shifter on a 48 V bus** with no margin for transients.
- **Standard 0.125 W or 0.25 W resistors** for $R_c$ at the high-voltage corner; 230 mW at 60 V is too much.
- **Forgetting the pull-up** ($R_{gs}$). Without it the gate floats when the shifter is off and the FET may stay on.
- **Running 20 kHz PWM through this circuit.** The plateau loss is more than 20 W in the example.
:::

::: deep
## Requirements and strategy

| Item | Value | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Bus | 48 V nominal, 24 to 60 V with transients | Parts rated above 60 V; clamp $V_{GS}$ |
| Load | 5 A inductive motor | Q1 sized for 5 A, flyback diode needed |
| Control | 3.3 V GPIO | NFET level shifter, ground-referenced |
| Duty | 0 to 100%, slow PWM up to 1 kHz | Resistor drive is acceptable; PFET gives 100% duty |
| Default | Off at reset or unpowered | Pull-up to source, shifter default off |

The strategy: let a ground-referenced NFET set *whether* the gate is pulled down, but let a Zener decide *how far* ($V_Z$ below the source).

## How it works, step by step

**Off.** GPIO low, Q2 off. No current flows in $R_c$. The gate node is held at $V_{in}$ by $R_{gs}$: $V_{GS}=0$. Q1 is off. The same is true if the MCU is in reset, the shifter fails open, or the 3.3 V rail is absent.

**On.** GPIO high, Q2 on (drain near 0 V). Current $I_c=(V_{G}-0)/R_c$ flows from the gate node to ground; the gate node falls until $V_{in}-V_G=V_Z$ and the Zener conducts, holding $V_G=V_{in}-12$ V. Q1: $V_{GS}=-12$ V, fully on, $R_{DS(on)}$ per the datasheet at $-10$ V or $-12$ V.

**Turn-on and off times.** Turn-on discharges $C_{iss}$ through $R_c$; turn-off recharges it through $R_{gs}$; both have $\tau=10\text{ k}\times2\text{ nF}=20$ µs.

Parts: $R_c$ at least 0.5 W with a working-voltage rating above 60 V (many 0805 resistors are rated 150 V; check), $D_Z$ 0.5 W, Q2 100 V.

**Choosing $R_c$ and $R_{gs}$.** Lower resistors give faster edges and a stiffer gate hold, but more standing current and dissipation ($\propto1/R$). 10 kΩ is a compromise at 48 V; at 12 V you could use 2.2 kΩ.

**Q1 selection.** $V_{DS}\ge100$ V, $|V_{GS}|\le20$ V, $|V_{GS(th)}|$ less than 4 V, $R_{DS(on)}\le50$ mΩ at $-10$ V, SOA for the motor's inrush and any stall. Check $Q_{gd}$ and $C_{iss}$ for the speed estimate.

Set $V_{DD}$ to 48 V to see per-edge energies in the millijoule range, then reduce $R_g$ to about 10 Ω, the push-pull case, and the edges shrink to tens of ns.

Plateau current $I_g\approx(V_{in}-V_{plat})/R_c$, with a 5 V offset: $(48-5)/10\text{ k}=4.3$ mA. With $Q_{gd}=20$ nC, $t_{pl}=4.7$ µs. Loss per edge $\tfrac12V_{in}I_Lt_{pl}=0.56$ mJ. At 100 Hz: 0.11 W; at 1 kHz: 1.1 W; at 20 kHz: 22 W.

For PWM above a kHz, upgrade:

1. **Push-pull stage**: an NPN/PNP emitter-follower pair on the gate, powered from $V_{in}$ and from the $V_{in}-12$ V Zener node (with a capacitor), driven from the shifter. The gate gets 0.3 A or more: $t_{pl}=67$ ns and 0.32 W at 20 kHz.
2. **Driver IC**: a high-side gate driver or load-switch controller with an internal charge pump, driving an **N-channel** FET (lower $R_{DS(on)}$, more parts to choose from, [[q0427]]).
3. **Lower $R_c$ and $R_{gs}$** with a heatsinked resistor, if the standing power can be afforded: 1 kΩ gives 10× faster, 10× the standing power (1.3 W at 48 V).

## Protection and default behaviour

- **Flyback diode** $D_{fw}$ (ground to the motor/PFET drain node): rating at least the motor current and 60 V. If the motor can regenerate and the bus cannot absorb it, add a TVS or brake resistor ([[q0424]]).
- **Bus TVS**: standoff above the nominal bus, clamp below the lowest rated component (Q1, Q2 at 100 V). Add input capacitance for hot-plug.
- **Default off**: shifter off, pull-up holds $V_{GS}=0$; MCU reset, shifter gate pulldown holds Q2 off.
- **Supply loss**: if the bus is absent, nothing conducts; if only the MCU rail is absent, the Q2 pulldown holds the PFET off.
- **Fault on the shifter**: if Q2 fails short, the PFET stays on: a fuse or current limit on the bus is the backstop.

## Verification

Bench: measure $V_{GS}$ on the PFET at the extremes of bus voltage (24 V and 60 V), confirm the Zener clamps at about 12 V; measure turn-on and turn-off waveforms of the drain with a scope; check temperatures of $R_c$ and Q1 at 60 V and full load; power-cycle the MCU and the bus to confirm the load never fires; hot-plug the bus with a TVS in place.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A good closing line: "This is a slow, simple, 100%-duty-capable high-side switch. If I need real PWM I'd move to an N-channel FET with a bootstrap or charge-pump driver, since the PFET's resistor drive costs about a watt per kilohertz here."

::: applications
- **Load switches and power-path control** on 24 to 48 V rails.
- **Slow-PWM heaters, solenoid valves and fans** on industrial buses.
- **Battery-pack output switches** with a MCU enable.
- **Automotive 12 V high-side switches** (with a TVS for load dump).
- **Hot-swap and soft-start** arrangements using a PFET with an RC on the gate.
- **Reverse-polarity-tolerant** power inputs (a PFET orientation with the body diode blocking).
:::

::: assumptions
- Bus 48 V (24 to 60 V), load 5 A, 3.3 V MCU, PFET with $C_{iss}\approx2$ nF and $Q_{gd}\approx20$ nC (illustrative).
- Slow PWM up to about 1 kHz or static on/off; resistor-driven gate.
- $V_Z=12$ V Zener with ±5% tolerance; $|V_{GS}|$ rating of Q1 is ±20 V.
- Standard through-hole or SMD parts with the voltage and power ratings stated; check the actual part ratings (resistor working voltage, Zener power).
- $R_c=R_{gs}$ for the supply-range plot; they can be chosen independently.

If the bus is only 12 V, the level shifter can be a simple NPN or NFET with a smaller resistor and no Zener is needed if $|V_{GS}|\le20$ V. If the bus is 100 V or more, the shifter needs a high-voltage NFET and the resistor dissipation scales as $V^2$; a driver IC or a different topology is better.
:::

::: followups
### What changes if the bus is 12 V instead of 48 V?
The Zener is optional: pulling the gate to ground gives $V_{GS}=-12$ V, within the rating. $R_c$ can be small (1 to 2.2 kΩ) with low dissipation ($12^2/2.2\text{ k}=65$ mW), so edges are 5 to 10 times faster. A small NPN or NFET shifter works with ordinary ratings. This is the easy case.

### How would you make it switch at 20 kHz?
Add a complementary emitter-follower stage on the gate, supplied between $V_{in}$ and $V_{in}-12$ V, driven from the shifter. 0.3 A peak gives a 67 ns plateau and 0.3 W of switching loss at 48 V, 5 A. Or use a gate-driver IC and an NFET with bootstrap/charge pump.

### What happens if the bus drops to 10 V while the MCU still commands on?
With equal resistors the gate sees $V_{in}/2=5$ V of $V_{GS}$, which is barely above threshold, so $R_{DS(on)}$ rises and Q1 dissipates more. Add an undervoltage lockout (a comparator or the MCU measuring the bus) or lower $R_{gs}$ relative to $R_c$ so $V_{GS}$ is closer to $V_{in}$ at low bus voltage.

### What if Q2 fails short?
Then the gate is held low and Q1 stays on, an unsafe default. Mitigations: a current limit or fuse on the bus, a second independent enable (a series NFET or supervisor), and monitoring the load voltage from the MCU.

### Why not use the same resistor for $R_c$ and $R_{gs}$?
You can choose them independently: $R_c$ sets turn-on speed and standing current, $R_{gs}$ sets turn-off speed and the pull-up. A smaller $R_{gs}$ gives faster turn-off, helps hold the gate against Miller coupling, but adds $V_Z/R_{gs}$ of standing current through the Zener branch; making $R_c$ smaller than $R_{gs}$ raises $V_{GS}$ at low bus voltage but increases dissipation.
:::

::: interpretations
"Design a circuit to properly drive a high-side PFET. Why is this needed?" The "why" is the level-shifting and clamping problem of [[q0437]]: the gate is referenced to the supply, which is far from logic levels. "Properly" means respecting the $V_{GS}$ rating over the whole supply range, and having a defined default-off state. The design is for a 48 V bus as an example; for other voltages the same topology applies with different component values. For a fast bridge the usual answer is an NFET with a bootstrap or charge-pump driver ([[q0427]]).
:::
