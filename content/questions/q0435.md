::: basics
### In plain words
An opto-isolated gate drive lets a microcontroller switch a power transistor with no electrical connection between them. The pin lights an LED. A phototransistor on the other side sees the light, and a small buffer stage then pushes current into the power FET's gate. You design it from the numbers: the LED resistor, the output stage sized for an aged part, and a buffer fast enough to charge the gate. You also choose the polarity so that a reset leaves the FET off.

### Picture it
Picture a person flashing a small lamp to tell a crane operator across a yard to lift. The lamp signal is weak and the crane needs real power. So the operator uses the signal to work a big lever, a power amplifier. In the circuit, the phototransistor's weak output is the lamp signal, and the buffer transistors are the big lever. The analogy leaves out one detail: the far side needs its own power source, an isolated supply, or the crane cannot run at all.

### Words you need
- **LED series resistor ($R_{LED}$)**: sets the LED current, $(V_{pin} - V_F) / I_F$. See [[q0434]].
- **End-of-life CTR**: the lowest transfer ratio the part will have after aging and heat; the value to design with.
- **Emitter follower**: a transistor stage whose output copies its input voltage and can supply more current.
- **Isolated supply**: a power source for the output side that shares no conductor with the microcontroller side.
- **Gate pulldown**: a resistor that holds the FET off if nothing drives the gate. See [[q0430]].

### The simplest example
A 3.3 V pin drives the LED at 10 mA, so $R_{LED} = (3.3 - 1.2) / 10\text{ mA} = 210$ Ω, and you use 200 Ω. At 20% end-of-life CTR the phototransistor passes $0.2 \times 10.5 = 2.1$ mA. A plain pull-up of 5.6 kΩ feeding a 2 nF gate has a time constant of $5.6\text{ k} \times 2\text{ nF} = 11$ µs, far too slow, and it is also inverting. So use an emitter-follower output, which is non-inverting and off by default, then add a buffer stage. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page walks through each stage with worst-case numbers and a verification checklist.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can **design** an optocoupler interface from numbers, and not just draw an LED and a transistor. The interviewer expects the sequence below, with worst-case reasoning:

1. Input side: choose $I_F$ and $R_{LED}$ from $V_{OH}$, $V_F$ and the pin's current limit.
2. Output side: choose the output configuration and size it from CTR at end of life, with a saturation or voltage check.
3. Speed: show you know a pull-up into a MOSFET gate is far too slow and add a buffer.
4. System defaults: reset behaviour, the isolated supply, the gate resistor and pulldown.
5. Verification: scope the delays, check CTR margin over temperature, check CMTI.
:::

::: why
A GPIO-to-optocoupler-to-MOSFET chain is the textbook way to drive a power stage from a microcontroller across a barrier. The failure modes are classic: the FET switches in tens of microseconds because a pull-up resistor is charging the gate, the output no longer saturates after a year in the field, or the default state at reset turns the load on. Being able to design this with numbers shows that you understand each of those.
:::

::: concepts
- **LED biasing**: $R_{LED}=(V_{OH}-V_F)/I_F$ ([[q0434]], [[q0072]]).
- **CTR and worst-case design**: end-of-life, hot, minimum bin ([[q0434]]).
- **Saturation and pull-up sizing**: $R_{pull}\ge(V_{CC}-V_{CE(sat)})/(CTR\cdot I_F)$ ([[q0434]]).
- **Gate charge and RC speed**: a pull-up charging $C_{iss}$ is slow ([[q0431]], [[q0429]]).
- **Emitter-follower (totem-pole) buffer**: gives current gain to drive a gate ([[q0086]]).
- **Default-off states**: pulldowns and polarity choice ([[q0430]]).
:::

::: answer
"I'll walk through it from the microcontroller to the gate.

On the input side the LED needs about 10 milliamps to get good CTR and speed. A 3.3 volt GPIO, an LED drop of about 1.2 volts, so the resistor is 2.1 volts over 10 milliamps, 210 ohms, and I'd use 200. I drive it active-high so that when the pin is low or floating, the LED is off.

On the output side the phototransistor sits on an isolated 12 volt supply. If I just put a pull-up on the collector and wire that to the gate, two things go wrong. First, the output is inverted, so a high at the microcontroller would give a low at the gate, and the default at reset would turn the FET on. Second, a pull-up of several kilohms into a gate capacitance of a nanofarad or two gives a time constant of microseconds, which is far too slow. So I use the phototransistor as an emitter follower: collector to the 12 volts, emitter to a pulldown of 6.8 kilohms, and I take the voltage across that resistor. With the LED on, the phototransistor passes at least 2 milliamps at end of life, so that node goes to about 11 volts, and with the LED off it's pulled to ground. That's non-inverting and defaults to off.

That node is high impedance, so I follow it with an NPN and PNP emitter-follower pair that supplies and sinks the gate current, with a 10 ohm gate resistor and a 100 k gate-source pulldown. Then I check the numbers at worst case: CTR at end of life and hot, the delays on a scope, and that the barrier's CMTI is higher than my dv/dt."
:::

::: technical
**Given.** MCU GPIO $V_{OH}=3.3$ V, pin limit 20 mA. Isolated gate supply $V_{CC2}=12$ V. Motor FET: $Q_g=40$ nC, $C_{iss}\approx2$ nF, wants $V_{GS}\approx10$ V. Optocoupler: phototransistor type, nominal $V_F=1.2$ V, CTR 50% at 5 mA nominal; assume worst-case CTR 20% at end of life (illustrative).

**Input.** Choose $I_F\approx10$ mA (inside the CTR-specified range, well within the pin limit): $R_{LED}=(3.3-1.2)/10\text{ mA}=210\ \Omega\to200\ \Omega$ (E24). $I_F=10.5$ mA; resistor dissipation $I^2R=22$ mW. Active-high drive: GPIO high lights the LED; Hi-Z or low turns it off.

**Output option A (inverting, pull-up).** $R_{pull}\ge(12-0.3)/(0.2\times10.5\text{ mA})=5.6$ kΩ for saturation at end of life. Direct to a 2 nF gate: $\tau=5.6\text{ k}\times2\text{ nF}=11$ µs, so about 25 µs to rise: unacceptable for PWM. Also inverting (LED on gives gate low), so a reset (LED off) turns the FET on: the wrong default.

**Output option B (emitter follower, used here).** Collector to $V_{CC2}$, emitter to $R_{pd}$ (6.8 kΩ) to isolated ground. When the LED is on, $I_C=CTR\cdot I_F$ and $V_E=\min(I_CR_{pd},\,V_{CC2}-V_{CE(sat)})$. At end of life: $0.2\times10.5=2.1$ mA gives $14.3$ V, clamped at about 11.7 V: the transistor goes to saturation and $V_E\approx11.7$ V. At the best case CTR (100% or more): still clamps at 11.7 V. Off: $V_E\approx I_{dark}R_{pd}\approx0$.

**Buffer.** NPN/PNP complementary emitter followers on node $V_E$: output $\approx V_E-0.7$ V when high, so about 11 V, within the 20 V gate rating; sinks and sources the gate current with gain $\beta$ (about 100, small-signal part). Peak gate current $\approx(11-V_{gate})/R_g$: with $R_g=10$ Ω from 11 V to 5 V about 0.6 A, and the buffer base current needed is $I_B=I_E/\beta=6$ mA, more than the phototransistor's 2.1 mA at end of life, so the transition is limited by base current. Part of the photocurrent also goes into $R_{pd}$ (0.7 to 1.6 mA as $V_E$ rises from 5 to 11 V), so the NPN gets roughly 0.5 to 1.4 mA and the gate current is about $\beta I_B\approx50$ to 140 mA. Charging $Q_g=40$ nC at about 0.1 A takes roughly 0.4 µs (increase $I_F$, use a higher-beta or Darlington stage or a gate driver IC if faster). On turn-off the PNP's base current is sunk by $R_{pd}$ (about 1.6 mA at 11 V), giving a similar gate discharge current, and the PNP stops conducting about one $V_{BE}$ above ground, so $R_{gs}$ finishes pulling the gate from about 0.6 V to 0 V (well below $V_{th}$).

**End-to-end delay.** The buffer is not the bottleneck: the phototransistor itself takes microseconds, and because it saturates in this circuit its turn-off (storage) delay is longer than its turn-on. Expect several µs of delay and µs of mismatch, a few percent of duty distortion at 20 kHz. That is acceptable for a single low-side switch or on/off control, not for a bridge leg that needs tight dead time.

**Gate network.** $R_g=10$ Ω (damping and dv/dt, [[q0431]]); $R_{gs}=100$ kΩ from gate to source ([[q0430]]).

**Power.** Gate drive power at 20 kHz: $Q_gV_{G}f=40\text{ nC}\times11\text{ V}\times20\text{ kHz}=8.8$ mW. Supply for $V_{CC2}$: an isolated DC-DC or a transformer winding sized for tens of mA.

**Verification.** CTR over temperature and life; scope $V_{GS}$ and the input; propagation delay and its mismatch (on vs off) versus dead time; check false triggers at maximum $dv/dt$ (CMTI); check $V_{GS}$ peak (no overshoot above 20 V).
:::

::: long
"Let me design it from the MCU out.

Input side. The LED wants a few to 10 milliamps; I'll pick about 10 for good CTR and speed, well within a 20 milliamp pin. A 3.3 volt GPIO and about 1.2 volts across the LED leaves 2.1 volts, over 10 milliamps gives 210 ohms, so I'd use the standard 200 ohm value. I'll drive it active-high, so that when the pin is low, floating, or the MCU is in reset, there's no current and the LED is off. That sets the default of the whole chain to off.

Output side. The first idea is a pull-up resistor on the phototransistor's collector, like in a logic interface. For a 12 volt supply I'd have to size it for saturation at end-of-life CTR, say 20 percent of 10 milliamps, which is 2 milliamps, so at least 5.6 kilohms. But then the signal is inverted, so my reset default would turn the FET on, and with 5.6 kilohms feeding a 2 nanofarad gate, the time constant is 11 microseconds. Too slow, too much time in the linear region, and a wrong default.

So I use the phototransistor as an emitter follower. Collector to the isolated 12 volts, emitter to a 6.8 kilohm resistor to the isolated ground. With the LED on, the transistor passes at least 2 milliamps even at end of life, which would want 14 volts across the resistor, so it goes into saturation and the emitter sits at about 11.7 volts. With the LED off, only leakage flows and the node is at about zero. Non-inverting, and the default is off.

That node can't drive a gate, so I add an NPN and PNP emitter-follower pair, base to base, emitters to a 10 ohm gate resistor and then the gate. It gives current gain of the transistors' beta, so the couple of milliamps of photocurrent, minus what the pulldown takes, becomes on the order of 100 milliamps of gate current, and the gate charges in a few hundred nanoseconds. The optocoupler itself still adds a few microseconds of delay, which is fine for a single low-side switch at 20 kilohertz but not for a bridge with tight dead time. A 100 k resistor from gate to source holds it off if anything is unpowered. Gate-drive power is only about 9 milliwatts.

Finally I check: CTR over temperature and end of life, which is why I used 20 percent; the delays on both edges against my dead time; the CMTI of the optocoupler against my dv/dt, and if the bridge is fast I'd switch to an isolated gate driver instead, which does all this in one package."
:::

::: terms
- **$R_{LED}$**: series resistor setting the LED forward current.
- **Active-high drive**: LED on when the GPIO is high; off when low or Hi-Z.
- **Emitter-follower output**: phototransistor with collector at the supply and the signal taken at the emitter.
- **Totem-pole buffer**: complementary NPN/PNP emitter followers providing current gain.
- **CTR (end of life)**: lowest CTR the design must still work with.
- **Isolated supply ($V_{CC2}$)**: the far side's power, from an isolated DC-DC or transformer winding.
- **Gate-source pulldown ($R_{gs}$)**: holds the FET off when undriven.
- **Propagation mismatch**: difference between turn-on and turn-off delays through the isolator.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws an LED, a transistor and a pull-up.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Computes $R_{LED}$ from $V_{OH}$, $V_F$ and $I_F$ and checks pin current and power.
- Designs the output at **end-of-life CTR**, not nominal.
- Spots the two traps of the simple pull-up (inversion and RC speed) and fixes both.
- Chooses the polarity so that the **default state is off**.
- Adds a buffer, gate resistor and gate-source pulldown, and an isolated supply.
- Gives a verification plan (delays, CTR over temperature, CMTI) and says when an isolated gate driver IC is the better choice.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Designing with nominal CTR.** At end of life the output may not reach its level.
- **Pull-up straight into the gate.** The RC time constant is microseconds: heavy linear-region dissipation and slow PWM.
- **Inverted default.** If LED-off means FET-on, every reset or brown-out fires the load.
- **No isolated supply on the output side.** If both sides share a rail the barrier is bypassed.
- **Forgetting that the LED current comes from a pin.** Check the GPIO's current and the sum of all pins; $I_F=10$ mA for several channels adds up.
:::

## Step 2: why not a pull-up into the gate?

The usual logic-style output: collector pulled up by $R_{pull}$ to $V_{CC2}=12$ V, output at the collector.

- **Saturation (end of life):** need $(12-0.3)/R_{pull}\le CTR\cdot I_F=0.2\times10.5=2.1$ mA, so $R_{pull}\ge5.6$ kΩ.
- **Speed:** the gate is $C_{iss}\approx2$ nF, so $\tau=R_{pull}C_{iss}=11$ µs; the 10 to 90% rise takes 2.2τ $=25$ µs, and a PWM at 20 kHz has a period of 50 µs. The FET spends microseconds in its linear region: massive switching loss.
- **Polarity:** LED on pulls the output *low*: inverting, so the default (LED off) is gate *high*: FET on at reset.

Each of these alone rules it out. A buffer after the pull-up fixes speed but not polarity; the emitter-follower arrangement fixes polarity and makes buffering natural.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The phototransistor's $V_{CE(sat)}$, the NPN/PNP base-emitter drops and CTR vs $I_F$ and temperature vary by part. Use the datasheet curves at your operating point and verify on the bench.

**Buffer current.** The photocurrent (2.1 mA at end of life) is the base drive for the totem-pole pair, minus what the 6.8 kΩ pulldown takes (up to about 1.6 mA near the top of the swing), so the gate current is $\beta$ times what is left: with $\beta\approx100$, roughly 50 to 150 mA; $40\text{ nC}/0.1\text{ A}=0.4$ µs for the gate charge, so the gate edges are a few hundred ns. The optocoupler's own response (several µs, longer at turn-off because it saturates) dominates the total delay. If you need faster, raise $I_F$, use a Darlington or MOSFET-output buffer, or move to an isolated gate driver IC (4 A class).

## Step 4: gate network and the load

- **$R_g=10$ Ω**: damps the gate loop ([[q0431]]): $2\sqrt{L/C}$ for 20 nH and 2 nF is 6.3 Ω. Pick 10 Ω, with a faster off-path if needed.
- **$R_{gs}=100$ kΩ**: default off if the buffer or isolated supply is lost ([[q0430]]).
- **$D_{fw}$ across the load**: flyback path ([[q0429]]).
- **Isolated 12 V supply**: from an isolated DC-DC converter or a small transformer winding; it needs about $Q_g\cdot V\cdot f+$ quiescent current $\approx9$ mW + (opto and buffer currents of a few mA) — tens of mW.

## Step 5: check the worst cases

| Check | Condition | Result |
|---|---|---|
| LED current | $V_{OH}$ from 3.0 to 3.3 V, $V_F$ 1.1 to 1.3 V | 8.5 to 11 mA, within limits |
| Gate voltage high | CTR 15% (aged, hot) | node at least 10.7 V; gate about 10 V |
| Gate voltage low | LED off (dark current at temperature, say 1 µA) | Node $\approx7$ mV; the PNP pulls the gate to about 0.6 V, then $R_{gs}$ takes it to 0 |
| Speed | Propagation delay on both edges | Measure; mismatch must be ≪ dead time |
| CMTI | System $dv/dt$ vs part rating | Part must exceed with margin |
| Default state | MCU in reset, isolated supply up | LED off, gate low, FET off |
| Isolated supply loss | 12 V gone | $R_{gs}$ holds the gate low |

If the delay mismatch of a phototransistor optocoupler (often µs) is larger than the dead time you need in a bridge, this circuit is not appropriate: use a digital isolator and a proper isolated gate driver ([[q0433]]).

## A direct alternative: gate-drive optocoupler or isolated gate driver

An optocoupler designed for gate drive integrates a photodiode, an amplifier and a totem-pole output stage with 0.5 to 2.5 A peak current and UVLO. You add $R_{LED}$, a decoupling capacitor, a gate resistor and the isolated supply. It replaces Steps 2 and 3 with one part, at higher cost, and with datasheet CMTI and delays suitable for IGBT and MOSFET bridges. A digital-isolator-based gate driver does the same with faster, more consistent timing and no LED.
:::

::: applications
- **Low-side motor, solenoid and heater drives** where the load supply is separate from the MCU supply.
- **Solid-state relay-style outputs**: opto plus FET pair, driven from an isolated rail.
- **AC phase-control and zero-cross circuits** with a photo-triac coupler.
- **Industrial digital outputs (24 V PLC outputs)**: opto input stage and power-FET output.
- **IGBT drivers in older VFDs and inverters**: gate-drive optocouplers with isolated supplies.
- **Medical and instrument outputs** where patient or user isolation is mandated.
:::

::: assumptions
- Phototransistor-type optocoupler with CTR 50% nominal and an assumed 20% worst case at end of life and temperature; replace with vendor data.
- 3.3 V GPIO with a 20 mA pin limit; 12 V isolated supply available on the output side.
- Low-side NFET with $Q_g=40$ nC, $C_{iss}\approx2$ nF; PWM at 20 kHz or below.
- Small-signal NPN/PNP pair with $\beta\approx100$; real current gain falls at high current and temperature.
- No bridge dead-time constraint; otherwise the optocoupler's delay mismatch must be checked.

If speed or timing matters (a half-bridge at 100 kHz), use an isolated gate driver instead; if only a slow on/off is needed, a simple opto and a logic-level FET with a pull-up is enough.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the emitter-follower better than a pull-up here?
It is non-inverting and defaults to off with the LED off, and the node is low-impedance enough to drive a buffer. The pull-up version is inverting, so reset would turn the FET on, and into a gate it is microseconds slow.

### What if the CTR at end of life is only 10%?
The node reaches $0.1\times10.5\text{ mA}\times6.8\text{ k}=7.1$ V, which gives about 6.4 V at the gate, so higher on-resistance but not off. Options: raise $I_F$ (the LED ages faster), raise $R_{pd}$ to 10 kΩ (then the turn-off is slower: $\tau=R_{pd}C_b$), or choose a better part or a gate-drive optocoupler.

### How would you speed up the turn-off?
Reduce $R_{pd}$ (a lower pulldown discharges the base node faster, at the cost of more LED current for the same voltage), add a Schottky from gate to the buffer to bypass $R_g$ on turn-off ([[q0428]]), or use a PNP buffer with a stronger base drive. The phototransistor's own turn-off is the slowest part, so a high-speed opto or digital isolator is the real fix.

### What if the bridge has dv/dt of 20 V/ns?
A basic phototransistor optocoupler with perhaps 1 to 10 kV/µs of CMTI is too weak (20 V/ns is 20 kV/µs). Use a rated high-CMTI gate-drive optocoupler or a digital-isolator gate driver with 50 to 100+ kV/µs ([[q0432]]).

### Where does the 12 V isolated supply come from?
From an isolated DC-DC converter module (a few hundred mW), a flyback or push-pull with a small transformer, or a winding on the main supply's transformer. It must have the same isolation rating as the signal path and its interwinding capacitance counts toward the common-mode path.
:::
