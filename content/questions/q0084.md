::: testing
Whether you can **sketch the BJT's characteristic curves from memory and label them correctly**, and explain what each region of the sketch means. The interviewer is checking:

1. The output family: $I_C$ vs $V_{CE}$ for stepped $I_B$, with a steep saturation region near the origin, a nearly flat forward-active region with a slight upward slope (Early effect), and breakdown at high $V_{CE}$.
2. The input curve: $I_B$ vs $V_{BE}$ looks like a diode with a knee near 0.6-0.7 V.
3. The transfer curve $I_C$ vs $V_{BE}$: exponential, a decade of current per about 60 mV.
4. That you can read the curves: $\beta=I_C/I_B$, $r_o=V_A/I_C$, the load line and operating point.
:::

::: why
These curves are how you read a datasheet, choose a bias point, find where a transistor leaves the active region and see why a common-emitter stage clips. Interviewers ask for the curve because it forces you to connect the physics (diode input, controlled current-source output) with circuit behaviour (load line, saturation, gain). It's also the BJT counterpart of the MOSFET curve question ([[q0095]]) and the diode I-V curve ([[q0074]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Diode I-V**: the base-emitter junction follows $I\propto e^{V_{BE}/V_T}$. See [[q0074]].
- **Regions**: cutoff, forward-active, saturation, breakdown. See [[q0087]].
- **Current gain**: $I_C=\beta I_B$ in the active region. See [[q0088]].
- **Early effect**: the collector current rises slightly with $V_{CE}$ because the base narrows; $r_o=V_A/I_C$.
- **Load line**: $I_C=(V_{CC}-V_{CE})/R_C$; the operating point is where it crosses a device curve.
:::

::: answer
"For a BJT there are really three curves, and the one people usually mean is the output characteristic: collector current on the vertical axis versus collector-emitter voltage on the horizontal axis, with a family of curves for different base currents.

Each curve starts at the origin, rises steeply over the first couple of tenths of a volt, which is the saturation region, and then flattens out into the forward-active region. There, the collector current is roughly $\beta$ times the base current, almost independent of $V_{CE}$, with a small upward slope from the Early effect. Higher base current gives a higher curve, evenly spaced if beta is constant. At large $V_{CE}$ the curves shoot upward at breakdown. Below the $I_B=0$ curve is cutoff, where there's only a tiny leakage current.

The input curve, base current versus $V_{BE}$, is just a diode: nothing until about 0.5 V, then an exponential knee around 0.65 to 0.7 V. And the transfer curve, $I_C$ versus $V_{BE}$, is the exponential $I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$: roughly a factor of 10 for every 60 mV.

I'd also add a load line to the output plot: from $V_{CC}$ on the voltage axis down to $V_{CC}/R_C$ on the current axis. The intersection with the base-current curve gives the operating point."
:::

::: technical
**Output characteristic** ($I_C$ vs $V_{CE}$ at fixed $I_B$). Forward-active ($V_{CE}>V_{CE(sat)}\approx0.2$ V):
$$I_C=\beta I_B\left(1+\frac{V_{CE}}{V_A}\right)$$
with Early voltage $V_A\approx50$-$200$ V for small-signal NPNs, output resistance $r_o=\frac{V_A+V_{CE}}{I_C}\approx\frac{V_A}{I_C}$. Saturation ($V_{CE}<\approx0.2$ V): both junctions forward biased; $I_C$ falls steeply toward 0 at $V_{CE}=0$ and is less than $\beta I_B$ (forced $\beta<\beta_{F}$). Breakdown at $V_{CE}\approx BV_{CEO}$ (about 40 V for a 2N3904).

**Input characteristic** ($I_B$ vs $V_{BE}$): $I_B=\frac{I_S}{\beta}e^{V_{BE}/V_T}$, with the knee near 0.65 V for mA-level collector current. At $I_S=10^{-14}$ A, $\beta=100$: $I_B=57.5\ \mu$A at 0.70 V, and 1.2 µA at 0.60 V.

**Transfer characteristic** ($I_C$ vs $V_{BE}$): $I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$: 0.83 mA at 0.65 V, 5.75 mA at 0.70 V, 39.8 mA at 0.75 V. One decade per $V_T\ln10=59.5$ mV. $V_{BE}$ falls about 2 mV/K at fixed current.

**Load line** for $V_{CC}$, $R_C$: $I_C=(V_{CC}-V_{CE})/R_C$. With $V_{CC}=10$ V and $R_C=1$ kΩ it runs from (10 V, 0) to (0, 10 mA). For $I_B=40\ \mu$A and $\beta=100$ the operating point is $I_C\approx4$ mA, $V_{CE}\approx6$ V (about 5.8 V with Early effect).

**Reading the curves.** $\beta_{DC}=I_C/I_B$ at a point; $\beta_{AC}=\Delta I_C/\Delta I_B$ between neighbouring curves; $r_o=1/\text{slope}$ of the active-region lines; $V_A$ from extrapolating the lines back to $I_C=0$ (they meet near $V_{CE}=-V_A$).
:::

::: short
"The BJT output curve plots collector current against collector-emitter voltage for stepped base currents. Each curve rises steeply near the origin in saturation, then goes nearly flat in the forward-active region at $I_C\approx\beta I_B$ with a slight Early-effect slope, and breaks down at high $V_{CE}$. The input curve is a diode, with a knee near 0.65 V, and the transfer curve is exponential, a decade per 60 mV. A load line across the output curves gives the operating point."
:::

::: long
"A BJT has a few characteristics, and I'd start with the one they usually mean, the output family. I draw $V_{CE}$ along the horizontal axis and $I_C$ along the vertical, and for each value of base current, say 20, 40, 60 µA and so on, I draw a curve.

Each curve starts at the origin. For the first two tenths of a volt or so it rises steeply: that's saturation, where both junctions are forward biased and the transistor behaves like a closed switch with a small resistance. Then it bends over and flattens into the forward-active region. There the collector current is about $\beta$ times the base current. With a beta of 100, 40 µA gives about 4 mA. The curves are nearly horizontal but tilt upward slightly because of the Early effect, where a higher collector voltage widens the collector-base depletion region, narrows the base and raises the gain a bit. The slope gives an output resistance $r_o=V_A/I_C$, which is 25 kΩ for a 100 V Early voltage and 4 mA. If I keep raising $V_{CE}$ the current eventually turns sharply upward at breakdown, about 40 V for a general-purpose part. Below the curve for zero base current is cutoff, where only leakage flows.

Then the input curve, base current against $V_{BE}$. It's a diode: nothing until about 0.5 V, and then an exponential rise, 58 µA at 0.7 V in my example. And the transfer curve, collector current against $V_{BE}$, is the exponential $I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$, a factor of ten for every 60 millivolts, and shifting about 2 mV per kelvin.

Finally, to use the picture in a circuit I draw the load line: from the supply voltage on the horizontal axis up to $V_{CC}/R_C$ on the vertical axis. Where that line crosses the curve for my base current is the operating point, here about 4 mA and 6 V for a 10 V supply and 1 kΩ. It also shows me the limits: cutoff at the right end and saturation near the left."
:::

::: terms
- **Output characteristic**: $I_C$ vs $V_{CE}$ at constant $I_B$ (the "family of curves").
- **Input characteristic**: $I_B$ vs $V_{BE}$ at constant $V_{CE}$; diode-like.
- **Transfer characteristic**: $I_C$ vs $V_{BE}$; exponential.
- **Early voltage ($V_A$)**: the (negative) $V_{CE}$ intercept of the extrapolated active-region lines; larger means flatter curves.
- **Output resistance ($r_o$)**: $V_A/I_C$; slope of the curves in the active region.
- **Load line**: the circuit's constraint $I_C=(V_{CC}-V_{CE})/R_C$, drawn over the device curves.
- **Q-point**: quiescent operating point at the intersection of the load line and a device curve.
- **Breakdown ($BV_{CEO}$)**: collector-emitter voltage at which current rises sharply with the base open.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** sketches a family of curves with a flat region and says "this is where it amplifies."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Labels both axes, shows evenly spaced curves for $I_B$ steps and identifies cutoff, saturation, active and breakdown regions.
- Includes the slight Early-effect slope and gives $r_o=V_A/I_C$ with a number.
- Sketches (or describes) the input and transfer curves too, with numbers (0.65-0.7 V knee, 60 mV/decade, -2 mV/K).
- Adds a load line and finds the operating point with arithmetic.
- Explains why spacing between curves is uneven if $\beta$ varies with $I_C$ (rolls off at high current, lower at low current).
- Notes the curves shift with temperature and that datasheet curves show $h_{FE}$ versus $I_C$ and $V_{CE(sat)}$ versus $I_C$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing the curves starting flat from the origin.** There's a steep saturation region at small $V_{CE}$, where $I_C$ rises quickly from zero.
- **Perfectly flat active-region curves.** Real ones slope upward due to the Early effect; this slope sets $r_o$ and limits gain.
- **Swapping the axes** (the collector current is the dependent variable on the vertical axis).
- **Labelling the curves with $V_{BE}$ instead of $I_B$.** The family is parameterised by base current (or sometimes $V_{BE}$, but be explicit).
- **Treating saturation as $V_{CE}=0$.** $V_{CE(sat)}$ is typically 0.1-0.3 V.
- **Forgetting that equal base-current steps give unequal collector steps** when $\beta$ varies with current, and that the curves cross over with temperature.
:::

**Regions.**
- **Cutoff** ($I_B\approx0$, $V_{BE}<0.5$ V): $I_C\approx$ leakage ($I_{CEO}$, nA).
- **Forward-active** ($V_{BE}\approx0.7$ V, $V_{CE}>0.3$ V): $I_C\approx\beta I_B(1+V_{CE}/V_A)$.
- **Saturation** ($V_{CE}<V_{BE}$, collector-base forward biased): $I_C<\beta I_B$; the ratio $I_C/I_B$ is the "forced beta."
- **Breakdown**: at $V_{CE}\approx BV_{CEO}$ avalanche multiplication in the collector-base junction is amplified by the transistor's own gain.

## The Early effect

The base-collector depletion region widens as $V_{CB}$ rises, eating into the neutral base. A narrower base means less recombination and a steeper minority-carrier gradient, so the collector current increases:
$$I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}\left(1+\frac{V_{CE}}{V_A}\right)$$
The straight lines extrapolate back to meet the voltage axis at $-V_A$. For $V_A=100$ V and $I_C=4$ mA, $r_o=V_A/I_C=25$ kΩ. A common-emitter stage's maximum (intrinsic) voltage gain is $g_mr_o=V_A/V_T=100/0.02585=3868$, independent of current, set by $V_A$.

**Worked operating point with Early effect.** Load line $V_{CE}=10-1000I_C$. With $I_C=\beta I_B(1+V_{CE}/V_A)=4\text{ mA}\times(1+V_{CE}/100)$, solve: $I_C=4\text{ mA}\times(1.1-10I_C)\Rightarrow I_C(1+0.04)=4.4\text{ mA}$, so $I_C=4.23$ mA and $V_{CE}=5.77$ V. The Early effect moved the answer by about 6% from the ideal 4 mA.

## What the datasheet shows

Datasheets give variations of these curves in more useful forms:
- **$h_{FE}$ vs $I_C$** at several temperatures. $\beta$ peaks in the mid-range and rolls off at both low current (recombination in the depletion region) and high current (high-level injection, Kirk effect).
- **$V_{CE(sat)}$ vs $I_C$** (at forced $\beta=10$): typically 0.1-0.3 V.
- **$V_{BE(on)}$ and $V_{BE(sat)}$ vs $I_C$**.
- **Safe operating area**: current and voltage limits imposed by thermal and second breakdown.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The numbers here (β = 100, V_A = 100 V, I_S = 1e-14 A) are representative, not those of a specific part. Real small-signal NPNs vary: β from under 50 to over 400, V_A from tens to hundreds of volts, and I_S from 1e-15 to 1e-13 A. A 2N3904's specified h_FE at 10 mA, 1 V is 100-300, for example.

## Drawing it in an interview: a checklist

1. Axes: $V_{CE}$ horizontal, $I_C$ vertical.
2. Draw 4-5 curves stepped by $I_B$, steep at the origin then flat with a slight slope.
3. Label regions: saturation (left, steep), forward-active (flat), cutoff (bottom, $I_B=0$), breakdown (far right).
4. Add the load line and the Q-point.
5. State $I_C=\beta I_B$, and $r_o=V_A/I_C$.
6. Mention the input (diode) and transfer (exponential) curves in a sentence.

## Worked example: reading $\beta$, $V_A$ and the small-signal parameters off the curves

Everything the small-signal model needs can be read from the output family. Take the 40 µA curve of the plot ($\beta=100$, $V_A=100$ V). At $V_{CE}=2$ V it reads $I_C=4\text{ mA}\times(1+2/100)=4.08$ mA, and at 10 V it reads $4.40$ mA. Two points on a straight line are enough to extrapolate back to the voltage axis, where the line crosses at $-V_A$:
$$V_A=\frac{I_{C1}V_{CE2}-I_{C2}V_{CE1}}{I_{C2}-I_{C1}}=\frac{4.08\times10-4.40\times2}{4.40-4.08}=\frac{32.0}{0.32}=100\ \text{V}$$
which recovers the value the curves were drawn with. The slope itself is $\Delta I_C/\Delta V_{CE}=0.32\text{ mA}/8\text{ V}=40\ \mu$S, the reciprocal of $r_o=25$ kΩ.

At the operating point found earlier ($I_C=4.23$ mA, $V_{CE}=5.77$ V) the other parameters follow:

- $g_m=I_C/V_T=4.23\text{ mA}/25.85\text{ mV}=164$ mS
- $r_\pi=\beta/g_m=100/0.164=611\ \Omega$
- $r_o=V_A/I_C=100/4.23\text{ mA}=23.6$ kΩ (using $I_C$ at the Q-point; the $V_A+V_{CE}$ correction is small)
- intrinsic gain $g_mr_o=164\text{ mS}\times23.6\text{ k}\Omega=3870\approx V_A/V_T$, as predicted.

A subtle point: the **DC** beta, $I_C/I_B$ at a point, differs from the **AC** (incremental) beta, $\Delta I_C/\Delta I_B$ between two adjacent curves. In the ideal flat-curve picture they match, but a real $\beta$ varies with current, so datasheets list both $h_{FE}$ (DC) and $h_{fe}$ (small-signal). Small-signal gain uses $h_{fe}$.

## Worked example: the saturation edge and what "forced beta" means

The load line in the plot runs from $(0\text{ V},\ 10\text{ mA})$ to $(10\text{ V},\ 0)$. The 100 µA curve gives $\beta I_B=10$ mA, exactly the saturation current, so a base current of 100 µA just reaches the edge: the collector is already pulled almost to the knee. Any base current above 100 µA cannot raise $I_C$ (the resistor limits it to $V_{CC}/R_C$), so the transistor goes into saturation and the extra base current is wasted charge.

Define the **forced beta** $\beta_{forced}=I_C/I_B$. At 10 mA collector current:

| $I_B$ needed for $I_C=10$ mA | $\beta_{forced}=I_C/I_B$ | Region |
|---|---|---|
| 100 µA | 100 | edge of saturation (the transistor's full gain is just used up) |
| 500 µA | 20 | saturated, moderate margin |
| 1 mA | 10 | hard saturation, the usual switch design rule |

Note what does not appear in the table: the 40 µA curve. At 40 µA the transistor does not carry 10 mA; it carries 4.23 mA, because that is where the load line crosses the curve and the device is still in the active region. "Forced beta" only has meaning once the circuit demands more collector current than $\beta I_B$ can deliver. Designers use $\beta_{forced}=10$ (a tenth of a typical beta, leaving margin for the minimum datasheet beta) to guarantee $V_{CE(sat)}$ stays at its specified value across parts, temperature and aging. Overdriving costs base-drive power and the storage-time delay at turn-off ([[q0082]]).

## Temperature moves the whole picture

The exponential transfer curve is strongly temperature dependent because the saturation current rises with temperature as $I_S\propto T^{3}e^{-E_g/kT}$ (the exponent 3 is a common model value, called XTI in SPICE models). Using that with $I_S=10^{-14}$ A at 300 K:

- At a **fixed $V_{BE}=0.65$ V**, going from 300 K to 310 K raises $I_C$ from 0.83 mA to 1.65 mA, a factor of **1.98**: the current doubles for a 10 K rise.
- At a **fixed $I_C=1$ mA**, $V_{BE}$ falls from 0.655 V to 0.637 V over those 10 K, about **−1.8 mV/K**, or −110 mV between 25 °C and 85 °C.

The first is the dangerous one. Biasing a BJT from a stiff voltage source sets the current with an exponential temperature dependence, so the current rises with temperature, which raises power dissipation $V_{CE}I_C$, which raises the junction temperature further. That positive feedback is **thermal runaway**, and it is the reason power-BJT stages use emitter ballast resistors and why paralleled BJTs without them do not share current. The cure is to bias with a current (or add emitter degeneration) rather than a voltage. The 2 mV/K coefficient is also useful: it is the basis of simple BJT thermometers and of the CTAT half of a bandgap reference.

## Real curves vs. the idealized plot

- **Knee shape.** The drawing uses a smooth `tanh` knee near 0.1 to 0.3 V. In a real device the knee depends on collector series resistance and on how hard the base is driven, so $V_{CE(sat)}$ rises with collector current: a datasheet will show perhaps 0.1 V at low current and several tenths of a volt at the rated current for a power device.
- **Quasi-saturation.** In a transistor with a lightly doped collector the lightly doped region can be modulated by injected charge before the junction is fully forward biased; the curve bends over gradually rather than sharply.
- **Breakdown.** At high $V_{CE}$ the curves turn up steeply near $BV_{CEO}$. In power parts, second breakdown (localized thermal collapse) can destroy the device at voltages and currents below the dissipation limit, which is why the safe operating area boundary is not simply a hyperbola of constant power.
- **Self-heating.** Measured with long pulses, the curves tilt downward at high power because $\beta$ and mobility change with the heated junction. Curve tracers therefore use short pulses.

## How to measure the curves yourself

A curve tracer is the real instrument. Without one, a benchtop version is: a source-measure unit (or a bench supply with a current meter) on the collector sweeping $V_{CE}$ from 0 to 10 V in 0.1 V steps, and a second source stepping $I_B$ through 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100 µA. At each step record $I_C$ and plot the family. Use short pulses or a low duty cycle at high power to keep self-heating out of the data. Before the sweep, set a current limit on the collector supply to avoid destroying the part if you cross into breakdown. Check that your extracted $\beta$ and $V_A$ match the datasheet's $h_{FE}$ range, and extract $V_A$ from the slope as in the worked example. Finally, repeat one curve with the part on a hot plate to see the temperature shift directly.
:::

::: applications
- **Choosing a bias point**: place the Q-point mid-way along the load line for maximum symmetric swing.
- **Checking a switch**: ensure the load line's high-current end falls in saturation for the chosen base drive.
- **Amplifier output swing**: from near saturation (about 0.2 V) up to $V_{CC}$; clipping shows when the signal hits either end.
- **Current-source output resistance**: $r_o=V_A/I_C$ determines mirror accuracy and amplifier gain.
- **Reading datasheets and curve tracers**: the same family appears on a curve tracer for characterising parts.
- **SPICE modelling**: $I_S$, $\beta_F$, $V_{AF}$ (the Gummel-Poon parameters) are fitted to these curves.
:::

::: assumptions
- **NPN, small-signal, silicon, room temperature**; PNP curves are the same with all voltages and currents sign-reversed (so they are drawn in the third quadrant or with $V_{EC}$ and $I_C$ as positive quantities).
- **Constant $\beta$** for evenly spaced curves; in reality $\beta$ varies with $I_C$, $V_{CE}$ and temperature, so spacing is uneven.
- **Simple Early model** (linear tilt) and a smooth saturation knee; real saturation depends on collector resistance and base drive.
- **Static (DC) curves**; at high frequency capacitances cause hysteresis-like loops on a curve tracer.
- **Alternative reading**: the question may mean only the output characteristic; mention the input and transfer curves in a sentence.
:::

::: followups
### How would you get beta from the output curves?
Pick a point in the active region, divide $I_C$ by $I_B$ for that curve: $\beta_{DC}=I_C/I_B$. For small-signal gain take two adjacent curves at the same $V_{CE}$: $\beta_{AC}=\Delta I_C/\Delta I_B$. For example, the 40 µA and 60 µA curves at $V_{CE}=5$ V might be 4.2 mA and 6.3 mA, so $\beta_{AC}=2.1/0.02=105$.

### Why do the curves slope upward in the active region?
The Early effect. Higher $V_{CE}$ means higher reverse bias on the collector-base junction, widening its depletion region and thinning the neutral base. A thinner base shortens the diffusion path, increasing the collector current and lowering recombination. Extrapolating the lines back they intersect the $V_{CE}$ axis at $-V_A$; $r_o=V_A/I_C$. A larger $V_A$ means flatter curves and a better current source.

### What does the saturation region look like and why is it steep?
When $V_{CE}$ falls below about 0.3 V the collector-base junction becomes forward biased, so the collector stops collecting all injected carriers and even injects some back. $I_C$ falls rapidly toward 0 as $V_{CE}\to0$. In this region $I_C<\beta I_B$ and the transistor acts like a low resistance (a few ohms for a small-signal part), i.e. a closed switch with $V_{CE(sat)}\approx0.1$-$0.3$ V.

### Where is the operating point for a transistor with $V_{CC}=12$ V, $R_C=2$ kΩ and $I_B=20$ µA, $\beta=100$?
Active-region estimate: $I_C=2$ mA, $V_{CE}=12-2\text{ mA}\times2\text{ k}\Omega=8$ V. That's well above saturation, so the active-region assumption holds. For $I_B=60$ µA the ideal $I_C=6$ mA would require $V_{CE}=0$, so the transistor saturates instead with $I_C\approx(12-0.2)/2\text{ k}=5.9$ mA.

### How do the curves change with temperature?
At fixed $V_{BE}$ the collector current rises with temperature ($V_{BE}$ falls 2 mV/K, so the curve shifts left by about 2 mV/K on the transfer plot). At fixed $I_B$, $\beta$ increases with temperature (about +0.5 %/K for many parts), so the output curves move up and the spacing grows. This is why a fixed-$V_{BE}$ bias drifts badly and why emitter degeneration is used.

### Scenario: a curve tracer shows the active-region lines are much steeper than expected on one sample. What might be going on?
Low Early voltage, perhaps a part with a very thin base or low $V_A$ (high-frequency devices often have $V_A$ of 20-50 V), or self-heating raising $\beta$ with power so the curve rises with $V_{CE}\cdot I_C$ (thermal effects look like extra slope). A tracer using a long pulse heats the device; short pulses separate thermal from electrical effects. Also a leaky or counterfeit part can show quasi-saturation or premature breakdown.
:::

::: interpretations
"Draw IV curve." under BJTs most likely means the **output characteristic** (the $I_C$-$V_{CE}$ family), which is the standard BJT plot; the input and transfer curves are mentioned for completeness. The same wording appears for diodes ([[q0074]]) and for MOSFETs ([[q0095]]). In MOSFET questions the corresponding axes are $I_D$ vs $V_{DS}$ with $V_{GS}$ as the parameter, while for BJTs the parameter is $I_B$.
