::: testing
Whether you can **specify ripple from the load's needs** instead of minimizing it blindly. The interviewer is looking for:

1. A clear criterion: ripple matters in proportion to (ripple amplitude) times (how well the load rejects it) compared with the load's tolerance.
2. Concrete cases where it matters little: wide-tolerance digital rails, loads behind a post-regulator or filter, resistive/thermal/inductive loads that average it away, loads whose bandwidth is far below $f_{sw}$.
3. The payoff: tolerating more ripple lets you shrink $L$ and $C$, lower cost and raise efficiency or transient speed.
4. The caveats that remain: peak current, EMI, and the CCM/DCM boundary.
:::

::: why
Over-specifying ripple is a common and expensive mistake: extra capacitors, big inductors, filters. Knowing when ripple is harmless is how you hit cost, size and efficiency targets. It is also the flip side of [[q0218]], and a quick test of engineering judgment: can you name the load and the reason?
:::

::: concepts
- **Ripple budget**: allowed ripple = supply window minus other errors ([[q0218]]).
- **PSRR and filtering**: downstream rejection reduces effective ripple.
- **Load bandwidth**: loads that respond slowly average out fast ripple (thermal, mechanical, inductive).
- **Ripple ratio trade-off**: larger $\Delta I_L/I_{out}$ means smaller $L$ ([[q0212]]).
- **Remaining costs of ripple**: peak current, core loss, EMI ([[q0215]]).
:::

::: answer
"Ripple matters in proportion to how much the load notices it, so it matters least when the load has a wide tolerance, rejects it, or averages it.

Wide-tolerance loads first: a 3.3 V digital rail that allows plus or minus 10% has a 330 mV window, so 17 mV of ripple is 5% of the window, and I could tolerate 50 or 100 mV. Second, loads behind another stage: if the buck feeds an LDO with 30 dB of PSRR at the switching frequency, 17 mV becomes 0.5 mV, so the buck ripple only needs to keep the LDO from running out of headroom. Third, loads that average: a heater has a thermal time constant of seconds, a motor winding is an inductor that filters the pulses, an LED driven by current only changes average brightness, and 500 kHz is invisible. Fourth, loads that are only measuring DC.

So in those cases I'd take a bigger ripple ratio, 40 or 50% instead of 20 to 30, which allows a smaller inductor and fewer capacitors, lower cost and faster transients.

What I'd still check is peak current against saturation, the EMI, and that the larger ripple doesn't push the converter into DCM too early."
:::

::: technical
**Criterion.** Effective error at the load $=\Delta V_{ripple}\cdot10^{-PSRR(f_{sw})/20}$ (for active loads), compared against the load's allowance; for averaging loads compare the load's cutoff $f_{load}$ to $f_{sw}$: attenuation $\approx f_{load}/f_{sw}$ for a first-order response.

**Examples (12 V to 3.3 V, ripple 17 mV pp):**
- Digital 3.3 V $\pm10\%$ rail: window 330 mV; ripple uses 8.5 mV per side, 2.6%.
- Followed by LDO, PSRR 30 dB at 500 kHz: 17 mV becomes 0.54 mV.
- Resistive heater, thermal time constant 10 s: first-order cutoff 0.016 Hz; attenuation of 500 kHz ripple by about $3\times10^{-8}$.
- LED at constant current: average current is what sets brightness; flicker at 500 kHz is far above perception (a few hundred Hz). Ripple current only matters through the LED's nonlinearity (efficacy) and its allowed peak current.
- Motor winding: an $L/R$ filter; with $L=1$ mH, $R=1$ Ω, corner 160 Hz, attenuation at 20 kHz PWM about 125 times (about 42 dB).

**Savings from relaxed ripple.** Required $L=V_{out}(1-D)/(f\Delta I)$: going from 30% to 60% ripple ratio at 2 A halves $L$ (7.97 µH to 3.99 µH at 500 kHz) and the output capacitor for a 100 mV spec is $\Delta I/(8f\cdot0.1)=1.2/(8\times5\times10^5\times0.1)=3$ µF.

**What still matters.** $I_{pk}=I_{out}+\Delta I/2$ (at 60%: 2.6 A) against $I_{sat}$ and current limit; core loss $\propto\Delta I^\beta$; CCM/DCM boundary at $I_{out}=\Delta I/2$ (0.6 A for 60%); conducted EMI.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Load tolerances, PSRR and the thermal/winding constants are examples; use the specific load's datasheet. Some battery chargers and LED lighting have specified ripple-current limits (for life or flicker standards).
:::

::: short
"Ripple matters less when the load tolerates it or doesn't see it: a digital rail with plus or minus 10% tolerance, a load behind an LDO or filter with good rejection at $f_{sw}$, or loads that average it, such as heaters, motor windings, and LEDs driven at average current. Then I'd raise the ripple ratio, using a smaller inductor and capacitor for lower cost and faster transients, while still checking peak current, EMI and the DCM boundary."
:::

::: long
"The rule I use is that ripple matters in proportion to how much the load notices it, so I compare the ripple, after any rejection, with what the load can tolerate.

The first case is a load with a wide tolerance. A digital 3.3 volt rail that allows plus or minus 10% has a 330 millivolt window per side. A 17 millivolt ripple uses about 2.6% of it. I could let it go to 50 or 100 millivolts and still be fine.

The second case is a load behind another stage. If the buck feeds an LDO, the LDO's PSRR at the switching frequency divides the ripple. With 30 dB at 500 kilohertz, 17 millivolts becomes half a millivolt. So the buck only needs to keep enough headroom for the LDO.

The third case is loads that average. A heater has a thermal time constant of seconds, so 500 kilohertz ripple is attenuated by seven or eight orders of magnitude. A motor winding is a 1 millihenry inductor, which filters PWM by 40 dB or so. An LED driven by a constant current responds to the average, and flicker at hundreds of kilohertz is imperceptible.

When ripple matters less, I use a larger ripple ratio: 40 to 60% rather than 20 to 30. At 2 amps that halves the inductor from 8 to 4 microhenries at 500 kilohertz, shrinks the capacitor, lowers cost and speeds up the transient response.

The checks that remain: peak current is the load plus half the ripple, so make sure the saturation current and current limit have margin; the converter enters DCM below half the ripple current, so light-load behavior changes; core loss rises; and conducted EMI at the switching frequency still has to pass."
:::

::: terms
- **Ripple ratio**: $\Delta I_L/I_{out}$.
- **Ripple budget**: allowed ripple after other errors.
- **PSRR**: supply rejection of the load, in dB.
- **Thermal time constant**: how slowly a heated body responds.
- **Averaging load**: a load whose response bandwidth is far below $f_{sw}$.
- **DCM boundary**: load below which current reaches zero, $I_{out}=\Delta I/2$.
- **Post-regulator**: LDO or filter after the converter.
- **Headroom**: margin between supply and the minimum the load needs.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "When the load isn't noise-sensitive, like digital circuits or motors."

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the criterion: ripple after rejection vs the load's allowance, with numbers.
- Distinguishes three mechanisms: wide tolerance, downstream rejection, load averaging.
- Quantifies the benefit of relaxing ripple (half the inductor, smaller capacitor).
- Keeps the remaining constraints in view: peak current and saturation, DCM boundary, core loss, EMI.
- Notes exceptions (digital timing/jitter, LED flicker standards, charger ripple-current limits).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Digital doesn't care about ripple."** High-speed digital cares about supply-induced jitter and a minimum-voltage dip; the window is wide, not infinite.
- **Ignoring headroom for a post-LDO.** The LDO's dropout must hold at the ripple's minimum.
- **Assuming a motor or LED never cares.** Motor ripple current heats the winding and LED ripple current affects peak current and life limits.
- **Relaxing ripple without checking peak current.** Saturation is the usual surprise.
- **Forgetting EMI.** Big ripple current still radiates and conducts.
:::

::: deep
## A decision framework

Define the **effective ripple at the point of use**:

$$V_{eff}=\Delta V_{ripple}\times H_{rej}(f_{sw}),$$

where $H_{rej}$ is the PSRR (active circuits), the filter attenuation, or the load's low-pass response. Compare $V_{eff}$ with the load's allowance. Ripple matters less when $V_{eff}\ll$ allowance.

| Load | Mechanism | Typical allowance or rejection |
|---|---|---|
| Digital core/IO rail ($\pm5$ to $\pm10\%$) | wide window | tens to hundreds of mV |
| MCU/FPGA behind decoupling | local capacitors filter | tens of mV |
| Rail followed by an LDO | LDO PSRR | 20 to 50 dB at hundreds of kHz (check) |
| Resistive heater | thermal time constant (seconds) | effectively unlimited |
| Motor winding | inductance filters PWM | tens of dB at tens of kHz |
| LED, constant current | average current sets output | ripple ±20 to 30% of current often accepted (flicker standards aside) |
| Battery charging (CC/CV) | average current/voltage | datasheet or cell limit on ripple current |
| ADC reference, PLL, RF | high PSRR needed | the ripple does matter ([[q0218]]) |

## The value of relaxing ripple

A larger ripple ratio $r=\Delta I_L/I_{out}$ gives a smaller $L$ ($L\propto1/r$):

Figure: Doubling the ripple ratio halves the inductor. Going from 30% to 60% saves roughly 4 µH of inductance (physically about half the volume, with lower DCR possible), at the cost of a higher peak current (2.6 A vs 2.3 A).

Output capacitor for a given ripple spec $\Delta V$: $C\approx\Delta I/(8f\Delta V)$. For $\Delta V=100$ mV: 3 µF at 60% ripple. A tolerant load can use one or two small ceramic capacitors (subject to transient needs, [[q0212]]).

Other benefits: faster current slew ($di/dt=(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ doubles), so the transient droop and the needed output capacitance shrink; lower cost and board area.

## What you cannot ignore

| Item | Effect of larger ripple | Check |
|---|---|---|
| Peak current | $I_{out}+\Delta I/2$ rises | inductor $I_{sat}$, FET and current-limit ratings |
| RMS current | $+\Delta I^2/12$ | at 60% ripple: 4.12 vs 4.09 A² (negligible) |
| Core loss | $\propto\Delta I^\beta$ | inductor core data |
| DCM boundary | $I_{out,crit}=\Delta I/2$ rises to 0.6 A | light-load mode and efficiency |
| EMI | more ripple current at $f_{sw}$ | filter, layout |
| Capacitor heating | $\Delta I^2/12\cdot ESR$ | capacitor rating |

## Example: where an engineer would deliberately allow high ripple

A 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A rail that feeds (i) a microcontroller and DRAM (3.3 V $\pm5\%$ = 165 mV), (ii) an LDO that produces 1.8 V for an ADC (PSRR 30 dB at 500 kHz). With $r=0.6$ ($L=4$ µH) and 22 µF (derated 11 µF): $\Delta V\approx1.2/(8\times5\times10^5\times11\times10^{-6})+1.2\times0.005=27+6=33$ mV, 10% of the 165 mV window. The LDO sees 33 mV, outputs about 1 mV of ripple. The ADC (a separate quiet rail) is fine, and the inductor is half the size.

## When to resist relaxing

- High-speed SerDes/DDR with tight jitter budgets: supply-induced jitter.
- Rails feeding references or VCOs directly.
- Products with EMI limits near $f_{sw}$.
- LED lighting with flicker or ripple-current specs, and batteries with ripple-current limits.
:::

::: applications
- **FPGA/MCU digital rails**: moderate ripple allowed; use small inductors and few capacitors.
- **Bucks feeding LDOs**: ripple limited by LDO headroom and PSRR.
- **LED drivers**: ripple current allowed, with flicker standards at low frequency.
- **Motor drives and heaters**: PWM current ripple absorbed by load inductance and thermal mass.
- **Cost-driven consumer products**: tolerate 50 to 100 mV ripple to cut parts.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Load requirements** are example values; real loads specify tolerance and PSRR.
- **First-order approximations** for thermal and winding responses.
- **CCM operation** at the stated load; at lighter loads with larger ripple the converter enters DCM.
- **Ripple at the switching frequency** only; low-frequency ripple (burst modes) is a different problem.
- **Safety and standards** (flicker, EMC) may impose limits independent of the load's tolerance.
:::

::: followups
### A buck feeds an LDO. How much ripple can the buck have?
Enough that the LDO still has headroom at the lowest point and its output ripple (buck ripple minus PSRR) meets the load. With 30 dB PSRR, 30 mV on the buck becomes about 1 mV. Allow the dropout voltage plus half the ripple: the buck output should be about 0.3 to 0.5 V above the LDO's target.

### Why doesn't a motor care about PWM ripple?
The winding is an inductor in series with resistance: $L/R$ sets a corner far below the PWM frequency (160 Hz for 1 mH and 1 Ω), so the current ripple is $V/(L\cdot f_{pwm})$-limited. Remaining effects are heating ($I^2R$ from ripple) and audible noise, not shaft torque ripple at that frequency.

### What would go wrong if I set the ripple ratio to 100%?
The peak is $1.5\,I_{out}$ and the valley $0.5\,I_{out}$: the inductor must be rated for much higher peak current, core loss is high, the CCM/DCM boundary is at half load so the converter enters DCM below $I_{out}/2$, and EMI and capacitor stress rise. In practice 40 to 60% is a limit.

### Does a digital load really not care?
It cares about the minimum voltage during the ripple valley (timing, brown-out), the supply-induced jitter on clocks and links, and the combination with load transients. If the tolerance is $\pm5\%$ and the other errors use most of it, ripple can still be critical.

### How would you decide between 30% and 50% ripple for a 2 A rail?
Compare: 50% needs 4.8 µH at 500 kHz instead of 8 µH, the peak is 2.5 A instead of 2.3 A. If the load tolerates the resulting 20 to 40 mV, the smaller inductor saves cost and area; check $I_{sat}$ and the light-load mode, then choose.
:::
