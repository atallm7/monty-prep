::: testing
Whether you understand **why winding wire into many turns works**, and what that does to the electrical behaviour. The hidden sub-skills:

1. Magnetic field adds up turn by turn: $B$ (or flux) scales with ampere-turns $NI$, and a coil concentrates it inside.
2. You know the consequence: a coil is an **inductor**, with $L \propto N^2$, so it stores energy and resists current changes.
3. You can reason about the design trade-off: for fixed ampere-turns, more turns means less current and more voltage, and thin wire; copper loss stays the same. That is how you match a motor or solenoid to its supply.
:::

::: why
One turn of wire carrying one amp makes a field you can barely detect. Real actuators need newtons of force from volts and amps, and the coil is how they get it. The same coil is also the reason motors and solenoids have inrush, flyback spikes, and time constants that a hardware engineer has to deal with. In interviews the answer links the mechanical function (strong field) with the electrical consequence (inductance), showing you can move between the two worlds.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ampère's law**: field is set by enclosed current; $N$ turns carrying $I$ give $NI$ ampere-turns. See [[q0334]].
- **Superposition**: the field of each turn adds, in the same direction inside the coil.
- **Inductance**: $L = N^2/\mathcal{R}$, energy $\tfrac12LI^2$. See [[q0022]].
- **Ferromagnetic cores**: iron raises flux for the same $NI$, at the cost of saturation.
- **Copper loss**: $I^2R$, set by wire cross-section and length.
:::

::: answer
"Coils are used because they multiply the magnetic effect of the current. The field from a single loop is tiny, but if you wind N turns, each turn carries the same current and the fields from all the turns add up in the same direction inside the coil. So the field is proportional to N times I, ampere-turns, which is what actually creates the force or torque. That lets a small current from a modest supply produce a useful field. The coil also concentrates the field in the middle, and if you put iron there, the field gets hundreds or thousands of times stronger.

What's special is that the same configuration is an inductor: the inductance goes as N squared, so more turns gives a stronger field but also more stored energy and a slower current rise. That's why motors and solenoids behave like inductive loads.

There's a design trade-off: for a given amount of copper and a given number of ampere-turns, you can use a lot of thin turns with low current at a higher voltage, or a few thick turns with high current at a lower voltage. The copper loss is about the same either way, so you pick the winding that matches your supply voltage. That's why a 12 V solenoid and a 24 V solenoid of the same size have different wire."
:::

::: technical
**Field of a coil.** For a long solenoid (length $\ell$, $N$ turns, current $I$) in air: $B = \mu_0 NI/\ell$. For a single circular loop of radius $r$: $B_{centre} = \mu_0 I/(2r)$; e.g. $r = 1$ cm, $I = 1$ A gives $63\ \mu\text{T}$, and 200 turns of the same loop give $12.6$ mT. With a magnetic core, $B \approx \mu_0\mu_r NI/\ell$ until saturation (about 1.5 to 2 T for silicon iron, less for ferrite).

**Magnetic circuit.** $NI = \Phi\mathcal{R}$, where $\mathcal{R}$ is the reluctance. Force or torque depends on flux, so the *ampere-turns* are what matter, not $N$ or $I$ individually.

**Inductance and energy.** $L = N^2/\mathcal{R}$. Example: $N = 1000$, $A = 1\ \text{cm}^2$, $g = 2$ mm gap (single-gap approximation): $L = \mu_0N^2A/g = 63$ mH. At $I = 0.5$ A the stored energy is $\tfrac12LI^2 = 7.9$ mJ. Electrical time constant $\tau = L/R$.

**Turns vs current at fixed ampere-turns.** Let the winding window have area $A_w$ filled with copper at fill factor $k$. Wire cross-section $a = kA_w/N$, mean turn length $\ell_t$, resistivity $\rho$:

$$R = \frac{\rho N\ell_t}{a} = \frac{\rho\,\ell_t\,N^2}{k A_w},\qquad I = \frac{(NI)}{N}$$

$$P = I^2R = \frac{(NI)^2\rho\,\ell_t}{kA_w}\quad\text{(independent of } N\text{)}$$

so copper loss and current density ($J = NI/(kA_w)$) are fixed by the ampere-turns and the window; $N$ only sets the voltage needed, $V = IR \propto N$ (and wire diameter $\propto 1/\sqrt{N}$).

**Why a coil and not a straight wire?** Same current in a straight wire gives a field falling as $1/r$ and an enormous current is needed for useful force; a coil uses the *same* current $N$ times, reuses the field path inside, and packs it compactly.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Single-gap formulas ignore fringing and leakage flux; real inductances typically differ by tens of percent. Fill factors for round wire are usually 0.4 to 0.6.
:::

::: short
"A coil multiplies the magnetic effect of the current: each turn carries the same current and their fields add in the same direction inside the coil, so field and force scale with ampere-turns NI, and iron inside makes it far stronger. The same configuration is an inductor with L proportional to N squared, so these loads store energy and resist current changes. For fixed copper and ampere-turns, more turns means less current and more voltage with similar loss, so you pick turns to match the supply."
:::

::: long
"A single loop of wire carrying an amp produces a tiny field, tens of microtesla. To get useful force you'd need enormous current. A coil gets around that by passing the same current through the field region N times. Each turn adds its own field in the same direction inside the coil, so the field and the resulting force depend on the product N times I, the ampere-turns. Put an iron core in it and the field increases by the relative permeability, hundreds or thousands of times, until the iron saturates.

That configuration has a second consequence: it's an inductor. Inductance goes as N squared over the reluctance, so doubling the turns quadruples the inductance. The coil stores energy half L I squared in its field, it resists changes in current, and when you switch it off the energy has to go somewhere, which is the flyback spike.

The third point is a design trade-off. Suppose I need a given number of ampere-turns, and I have a fixed winding window. If I double the turns, the wire gets thinner by root two in diameter, the resistance goes up four times, but I only need half the current, so I squared R stays the same. The loss is the same, but the voltage needed doubles. So I choose turns to match my supply. A 12 volt and a 24 volt solenoid of the same size differ only in turns and wire gauge.

Finally, in a motor the coils are on the rotor so each conductor sees the field, and the many turns also raise K_t and K_e together, which is why a high-turn motor is slower but with more torque per amp."
:::

::: terms
- **Ampere-turns ($NI$)**: the magnetomotive force driving magnetic flux.
- **Inductance ($L$)**: flux linkage per amp; scales as $N^2$.
- **Reluctance ($\mathcal{R}$)**: magnetic "resistance" of the flux path.
- **Fill factor ($k$)**: fraction of the winding window occupied by copper.
- **Relative permeability ($\mu_r$)**: factor by which a core multiplies flux.
- **Saturation**: core's flux limit, beyond which extra ampere-turns add little field.
- **Copper loss**: $I^2R$ heating in the winding.
- **Magnet wire**: enamel-insulated copper wire used for windings.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "more turns give a stronger magnetic field".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies with $B = \mu_0NI/\ell$ and says that **ampere-turns** are what matter, not $N$ alone.
- Notes the **electrical consequence** ($L\propto N^2$, stored energy, flyback).
- Does the **turns-versus-current trade-off** and shows copper loss is independent of $N$ at fixed $NI$ and window, so $N$ matches the supply voltage.
- Mentions **cores** (iron/ferrite), saturation and the reason motors are wound on laminated cores.
- Connects to motor constants: $K_t$ and $K_e$ rise together with turns, so more turns means lower speed and higher torque per amp.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"More turns always means more force."** At fixed voltage, more turns means higher resistance and *lower* current, so $NI$ may not go up (and copper heating changes).
- **"The coil stores charge."** It stores energy in the magnetic field, not charge.
- **Ignoring saturation.** Beyond about 1.5 to 2 T in iron, extra ampere-turns just heat the coil.
- **Thinking thinner wire only costs resistance.** It also limits current capacity (current density) and heating.
- **Believing the coil's inductance is fixed.** In a solenoid it changes as the plunger moves; in a saturating core it falls at high current ([[q0345]]).
- **Forgetting the winding's insulation and fill factor.** Real windings waste a large part of the window.
:::

::: deep
## Why one turn is not enough

The field of a long straight wire is $B = \mu_0 I/(2\pi r)$, which for $I = 1$ A at 1 cm is $20\ \mu\text{T}$, less than half Earth's field. For $B = 0.1$ T at 1 cm you would need $5\times10^{3}$ A. A coil changes the geometry: the loops stack so that the fields from every turn pass through the same central region and add. In a long solenoid the field outside nearly cancels and inside is nearly uniform:

$$B = \mu_0\,\frac{N}{\ell}\,I = \mu_0\,n\,I$$

## Why the same coil is an inductor

The flux through the coil links all $N$ turns, so the flux linkage is $\lambda = N\Phi = N\cdot\dfrac{NI}{\mathcal{R}} = \dfrac{N^2}{\mathcal{R}}I$, hence $L = N^2/\mathcal{R}$. Faraday's law gives the voltage:

$$v = \frac{d\lambda}{dt} = L\frac{di}{dt}$$

so current cannot change instantly. Energy stored: $W = \tfrac12 LI^2$. For the example above (1000 turns, 1 cm², 2 mm gap), $L = 63$ mH and at $0.5$ A $W = 7.9$ mJ. Switching off a coil at 0.5 A dumps that into whatever path it finds ([[q0340]]).

## Choosing turns: same loss, different voltage

Consider a winding window $A_w = 100\ \text{mm}^2$, fill factor 0.5, mean turn length 60 mm, copper ($\rho = 1.72\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\text{·m}$), and a need for 500 ampere-turns:

| Turns $N$ | Wire diameter | $R$ | $I = 500/N$ | $P = I^2R$ | $V = IR$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 | 0.80 mm | 0.21 Ω | 5.0 A | 5.2 W | 1.0 V |
| 500 | 0.36 mm | 5.2 Ω | 1.0 A | 5.2 W | 5.2 V |
| 1000 | 0.25 mm | 20.6 Ω | 0.5 A | 5.2 W | 10.3 V |
| 1163 | 0.23 mm | 27.9 Ω | 0.43 A | 5.2 W | 12.0 V |
| 2000 | 0.18 mm | 82.6 Ω | 0.25 A | 5.2 W | 20.6 V |

Same force (same $NI$), same copper loss, very different voltage. With a 12 V supply you choose about 1160 turns of 0.23 mm (roughly AWG 31) wire; with 24 V about 2300. The current density is the same in every row: $J = 500/50\ \text{mm}^2 = 10$ A/mm², which is a lot for continuous use (typically 2 to 5 A/mm² is a comfortable continuous design), so that coil is a pulsed one. For continuous duty you would increase the window or reduce the ampere-turns.

## The special thing about the configuration

1. **Field addition:** every turn contributes to the same flux path.
2. **Concentration with a core:** the iron path carries flux with little loss of $NI$ except across the air gap, where the force is produced.
3. **Large conductor length in a small volume:** in a motor, each rotor conductor length contributes to $BI\ell$ force; a coil of $N$ turns multiplies the effective length by $N$, so $K_t = NBA$.
4. **Inductive energy storage:** a bonus in a boost converter or relay driver, a hazard in a driver switching a motor.

## Limits and trade-offs

- **Saturation:** at high $NI$ the core saturates; adding turns or current stops increasing force and inductance collapses, so current rises quickly ([[q0345]]).

- **Heat:** $P = I^2R$ is fixed by ampere-turns and window; continuous duty needs $J$ below a few A/mm² or cooling, and copper's resistance rises about 0.39% per K, so a hot coil draws less current at fixed voltage.
- **Insulation:** enamel wire limits temperature (insulation class), and turn-to-turn voltage during flyback stresses thin windings.
- **Parasitic capacitance:** many turns give inter-turn capacitance, creating a self-resonant frequency above which the coil looks capacitive, relevant for fast PWM edges.
- **Winding technique:** layer winding, fill factor, wire gauge and bobbin geometry set both performance and cost.
:::

::: applications
- **Solenoid valves and locks**: 12 V and 24 V versions differ mainly in turns and wire diameter.
- **Brushed motor rotors**: many coils on a laminated iron core multiply torque per amp.
- **Voice-coil actuators**: a light coil in a magnet gap for proportional force (speakers, hard-drive heads).
- **Relays**: a coil of thousands of turns driving a contact from tens of milliamps.
- **Transformers and inductors**: the same coil-and-core idea used to store or transfer energy.
:::

::: assumptions
- Windings assumed uniform, with fill factor 0.5 and copper at room temperature; hot copper is about 25 to 30% more resistive at 75 K rise.
- Field formulas assume an ideal long solenoid or a thin coil at its centre; real devices have fringing and leakage.
- The turns-vs-voltage argument holds with a **fixed winding window and fixed ampere-turns**; if you change the window or the force requirement the numbers change.
- Core materials are assumed linear until saturation; hysteresis and eddy-current losses (important for AC) are ignored.
:::

::: followups
### If I double the turns on a solenoid at the same supply voltage, what happens to the force?
The resistance goes up about four times (twice the length, half the area), so the current falls to about a quarter at a fixed voltage; $NI$ becomes $2N\times I/4 = NI/2$ and force falls to about a quarter. To benefit from more turns, you must raise the voltage in proportion.

### Why do the 12 V and 24 V versions of the same solenoid exist?
Same housing and ampere-turns, different turns: the 24 V version has about twice the turns of thinner wire (about $\sqrt2$ times thinner in diameter), so it draws half the current at the same force and loss. You pick the one that matches the supply to avoid a bigger driver.

### What happens to the field if the iron core saturates?
Flux stops increasing with $NI$ (about 1.5 to 2 T for steel), so force plateaus and the inductance falls, making the current rise much faster. In a drive circuit the sudden drop in $L$ can cause a current spike and overheating ([[q0345]]).

### Why do motors use laminated cores?
Eddy currents induced in a solid iron core waste power as heat and oppose flux changes; thin insulated laminations break the eddy-current paths, which matters because the rotor field alternates as it turns.

### Scenario: you need 3x the force from a solenoid in the same housing at 12 V. Options?
Force $\propto (NI)^2$, so you need $\sqrt3 \approx 1.73$ times the ampere-turns. At 12 V and fixed window the loss budget limits $NI$; $P = (NI)^2 \rho\ell_t/(kA_w)$ so 3x the ampere-turns squared means 3x the power. Options: accept 3x heating with pulsed duty (pull-in boost then reduce to hold), use a larger window or better fill, a more permeable core with a smaller air gap, or a bigger solenoid. Duty cycle and thermal limits decide.

### Why does a coil resist sudden changes in current?
Faraday's law: changing current changes flux linking the turns, which induces a voltage $L\,di/dt$ opposing the change (Lenz's law). A sudden current drop would need infinite voltage, so the voltage rises to whatever is needed to keep the current flowing a little longer, that's the flyback voltage.
:::
