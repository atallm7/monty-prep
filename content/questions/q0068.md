::: basics
### In plain words
Mobility tells you how fast a charge carrier moves for a given push from the electric field. A higher mobility means carriers move faster, so the material conducts better and devices can switch faster. Carriers keep bumping into vibrating atoms and impurities along the way, and those collisions limit the speed.

### Picture it
Picture walking across a room while pushed by a steady breeze. In an empty room you reach a good steady speed. In a crowded room you keep bumping into people, and you move more slowly with the same breeze. The breeze is the electric field, you are the carrier, and the crowd is the vibrating atoms and impurities. The analogy fails at very high fields, where the speed stops increasing at all.

### Words you need
- **Drift velocity ($v_d$)**: the average speed of carriers along the field.
- **Electric field ($E$)**: the push on charge, in volts per cm. See [[q0059]].
- **Mobility ($\mu$)**: speed per unit field, $v_d = \mu E$, in cm²/V·s.
- **Scattering**: a carrier bouncing off a vibrating atom or an impurity.
- **Doping**: adding impurities, which scatter carriers. See [[q0065]].

### The simplest example
In lightly doped silicon, electron mobility is about 1350 cm²/V·s. Apply a field of 100 V/cm. The drift velocity is $1350 \times 100 = 1.35\times10^{5}$ cm/s. Holes in silicon have a mobility of about 480, so at the same field they drift at $4.8\times10^{4}$ cm/s, about three times slower. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page explains where mobility comes from, why it falls with heat and with doping, and what happens at very high fields.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand mobility as **how easily a carrier moves under a field**, and whether you can connect that single number to what limits it in a real crystal. The interviewer is looking for:

1. The definition $v_d=\mu E$ and the units (cm²/V·s).
2. The microscopic picture: carriers accelerate, scatter, and settle to an average drift velocity; $\mu=q\tau/m^*$.
3. What reduces mobility: lattice (phonon) scattering at high temperature and ionized-impurity scattering at high doping.
4. How it connects to conductivity $\sigma=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$, device speed and why electrons beat holes.
5. Ideally, that at high field velocity saturates and the linear formula stops working.
:::

::: why
Mobility sets the resistivity of doped silicon, the on-resistance of a MOSFET channel, the transit time of a transistor, the speed of a gate and, via the Einstein relation, the diffusion constant. It explains why NMOS beats PMOS for the same size, why GaAs and GaN are used for RF, why strained silicon and SiGe exist, why transistors slow down when hot and why a heavily doped region isn't as conductive as a naive carrier count suggests. It's the "second number" in conductivity after carrier density.
:::

::: concepts
- **Drift velocity**: average carrier velocity under a field, $v_d=\mu E$ at low field. See [[q0001]] for current and Ohm's law.
- **Scattering and relaxation time ($\tau$)**: carriers collide with lattice vibrations and impurities every ~0.1-1 ps on average.
- **Effective mass ($m^*$)**: how heavy a carrier "feels" in the crystal band structure; lighter means higher mobility.
- **Conductivity**: $\sigma=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$, the product of density and mobility. See [[q0062]].
- **Einstein relation**: $D=\mu\,kT/q$ links mobility to diffusion. See [[q0063]].
:::

::: answer
"Mobility is how fast a charge carrier drifts per unit electric field. Formally $v_d=\mu E$, with units of cm² per volt-second. A high mobility means carriers move quickly for a given field, so you get high conductivity and fast devices.

Physically, a carrier in a crystal is accelerated by the field, but every so often it scatters off a vibrating lattice atom or an ionized dopant, and loses its direction. The average time between collisions is $\tau$, and the drift velocity averages out to $\mu E$ with $\mu=q\tau/m^*$, where $m^*$ is the effective mass. So lighter effective mass and longer scattering time give higher mobility.

In silicon at room temperature, electron mobility is about 1350 cm²/V·s in lightly doped material and hole mobility about 480. Mobility drops as temperature rises because the lattice vibrates more, and drops as doping rises because ionized impurities scatter carriers. Conductivity is carrier density times mobility times charge, so mobility matters as much as doping. At very high fields the drift velocity saturates around $10^7$ cm/s, and that's what really limits transistor speed."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** $v_d=\mu E$, so $\mu=v_d/E$ in cm²/V·s (or m²/V·s; 1 m²/V·s = $10^4$ cm²/V·s). Electrons and holes have separate mobilities $\mu_n$, $\mu_p$.

**Drude picture.** Between collisions a carrier accelerates at $qE/m^*$. With average free time $\tau$:
$$v_d=\frac{q\tau}{m^*}E\ \Rightarrow\ \mu=\frac{q\tau}{m^*}$$
For $\mu_n=1350$ cm²/V·s and $m^*\approx0.26\,m_0$ (conductivity effective mass for electrons in silicon), $\tau\approx0.2$ ps.

**Scattering mechanisms** (rates add, so mobilities combine by Matthiessen's rule):
$$\frac{1}{\mu}=\frac{1}{\mu_{lattice}}+\frac{1}{\mu_{impurity}}+\cdots$$
- Lattice (phonon): $\mu_L\propto T^{-3/2}$ roughly, dominates in lightly doped material at room temperature and above.
- Ionized impurity: $\mu_I\propto T^{3/2}/N_I$, dominates at high doping and low temperature.

**Typical silicon values at 300 K** (lightly doped): $\mu_n\approx1350$, $\mu_p\approx480$ cm²/V·s. At $10^{17}$ cm⁻³: roughly 730 and 290; at $10^{19}$: roughly 110 and 70 (fit-dependent).

**Conductivity and resistivity.** $\sigma=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$. For N-type with $n=10^{16}$ and $\mu_n=1140$: $\rho=0.55\ \Omega\cdot$cm.

**Einstein relation.** $D=\mu\frac{kT}{q}$: $D_n\approx35$ cm²/s and $D_p\approx12.4$ cm²/s at 300 K for $\mu_n=1350$, $\mu_p=480$.

**High-field saturation.** Above a critical field of order $10^4$ V/cm, optical-phonon emission caps velocity at $v_{sat}\approx10^7$ cm/s for electrons in silicon (somewhat lower for holes). A common model: $v=\frac{\mu E}{1+\mu E/v_{sat}}$.
:::

::: long
"Mobility tells you how fast a charge carrier moves in response to an electric field. At low field the drift velocity is proportional to the field, $v_d=\mu E$, so $\mu$ has units of cm² per volt-second. A big mobility means carriers respond strongly, which gives low resistance and fast transistors.

The microscopic picture is simple. A carrier is accelerated by the field, but it keeps scattering. It collides with lattice atoms that vibrate with heat, which we call phonon scattering, and with ionized dopant atoms. Between collisions it picks up velocity, and at each collision it loses its direction. The average time between collisions is $\tau$, and the net drift is $v_d=(q\tau/m^*)E$. So $\mu=q\tau/m^*$: longer free time and lighter effective mass both help. In silicon $\tau$ is on the order of 0.2 picoseconds.

Two scattering mechanisms matter. Phonon scattering gets worse as temperature rises, so mobility falls roughly as $T^{-3/2}$. That's why transistor drive current and on-resistance get worse when a chip is hot. Impurity scattering gets worse as doping rises, so mobility falls from about 1350 for electrons at light doping to around 100 at $10^{19}$. The rates add up, so the total mobility is dominated by the stronger scatterer.

Why does it matter? Conductivity is $q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$, so a material with the same carrier count and more mobility is more conductive. Electrons in silicon are about three times faster than holes, which is why NMOS is stronger than PMOS and why N-type has lower resistivity than P-type at the same doping. In GaAs the electron mobility is around 8500, which is why it's used at microwave frequencies.

One more caveat: the linear relationship holds only at moderate fields. At very high fields the velocity saturates, around $10^7$ cm/s in silicon, which is what limits the speed of modern short-channel transistors."
:::

::: terms
- **Mobility ($\mu$)**: drift velocity per unit field, cm²/V·s.
- **Drift velocity ($v_d$)**: average velocity of carriers due to an applied field.
- **Relaxation (scattering) time ($\tau$)**: mean time between scattering events.
- **Effective mass ($m^*$)**: the mass a carrier appears to have in the crystal, which depends on the band curvature.
- **Phonon scattering**: collisions with lattice vibrations; increases with temperature.
- **Ionized-impurity scattering**: collisions with charged dopant ions; increases with doping.
- **Matthiessen's rule**: scattering rates (inverse mobilities) add.
- **Velocity saturation**: at high field drift velocity stops growing, reaching about $10^7$ cm/s in silicon.
:::

- Links mobility directly to conductivity, resistivity and a worked number.
- Mentions the Einstein relation $D=\mu kT/q$ as the bridge to diffusion.

:::

::: mistakes
- **"Mobility is the same as drift velocity."** Mobility is velocity *per unit field*. Velocity depends on the field applied.
- **"Higher doping increases mobility because there are more carriers."** More carriers increases conductivity, but mobility decreases because of ionized-impurity scattering.
- **"Mobility increases with temperature because carriers have more energy."** In normal operation lattice scattering dominates so mobility falls with temperature. (Impurity-limited mobility does rise with $T$, which is why very low temperature behaviour is different.)
- **Mixing units.** 1350 cm²/V·s equals 0.135 m²/V·s. Using SI and CGS in one formula gives errors of $10^4$.
- **Assuming the linear law holds at all fields.** Velocity saturates near $10^7$ cm/s; in deep submicron transistors this dominates.
- **Confusing mobility with carrier speed.** Thermal speed is about $10^7$ cm/s *with no field and random direction*; drift is the small net bias on top of it.
:::

::: deep
## From Ohm's law to mobility

Ohm's law in its microscopic form is $J=\sigma E$. The current density is carrier density times charge times drift velocity: $J=nqv_d$. Equating:
$$\sigma=nq\frac{v_d}{E}=nq\mu$$
so mobility is the missing "how fast does each carrier go per unit field" factor. Both carrier species contribute, so $\sigma=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$.

## The Drude model: a free run and a collision

Consider an electron of effective mass $m^*$ in a field $E$. Between collisions it accelerates: $a=qE/m^*$. After an average time $\tau$ it scatters, and its velocity is randomized. The average velocity gained is $\langle v\rangle=a\tau=\frac{q\tau}{m^*}E$, which is the drift velocity. Therefore:
$$\mu=\frac{q\tau}{m^*}$$

Worked numbers for electrons in silicon: $\mu=1350$ cm²/V·s $=0.135$ m²/V·s, $m^*=0.26\times9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg.
$$\tau=\frac{\mu m^*}{q}=\frac{0.135\times2.37\times10^{-31}}{1.602\times10^{-19}}\approx2.0\times10^{-13}\ \text{s}=0.2\ \text{ps}$$
With a thermal speed of order $2\times10^7$ cm/s the mean free path is of order tens of nanometres (about 45 nm with these numbers). Short-channel MOSFETs are shorter than that, which is why they operate quasi-ballistically and why simple drift-diffusion estimates get less accurate.

At a field of 1000 V/cm, $v_d=1350\times1000=1.35\times10^6$ cm/s, about 14 times smaller than the random thermal speed. Drift is a small bias on top of fast random motion.

## What limits mobility

**Lattice (phonon) scattering.** Atoms vibrate with an amplitude that grows with $T$, scattering carriers more often. In the simplest model $\mu_L\propto T^{-3/2}$. From 300 K to 400 K that predicts a drop to $0.65\times$ the 300 K value. In real silicon the exponent is nearer to 2 for electrons and 2.2 for holes (fit-dependent), but the message is the same: hot silicon is slower.

**Ionized-impurity scattering.** Each charged dopant ion deflects passing carriers like a Coulomb scatterer. More ions means more scattering, $\mu_I\propto T^{3/2}/N_I$, so it falls with doping and *rises* with temperature (fast carriers are deflected less).

**Matthiessen's rule.** Scattering rates add, so the slowest mechanism dominates:
$$\frac1\mu=\frac1{\mu_L}+\frac1{\mu_I}$$
Example: if $\mu_L=1450$ and $\mu_I=2000$ cm²/V·s then $\mu=1/(1/1450+1/2000)=840$ cm²/V·s.

Other mechanisms: neutral-impurity scattering, carrier-carrier scattering at very high density, surface roughness and Coulomb scattering in a MOSFET inversion layer (where mobility is only ~200-600 cm²/V·s for electrons, much lower than bulk).

| Material (300 K, lightly doped) | $\mu_n$ (cm²/V·s) | $\mu_p$ (cm²/V·s) |
|---|---|---|
| Si | ~1350 | ~480 |
| Ge | ~3900 | ~1900 |
| GaAs | ~8500 | ~400 |
| 4H-SiC | ~900-1000 | ~100 |
| GaN (bulk) | ~1000-1500 | ~30 |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Mobilities vary strongly with doping, crystal quality, orientation, measurement method (Hall vs drift) and for SiC/GaN with polytype and layer type. The values here are representative numbers for comparison; use device datasheets or process design kits for actual design.

## Mobility and the Einstein relation

Diffusion and drift share the same scattering physics, so their coefficients are linked:
$$D=\mu\frac{kT}{q}$$
At 300 K $kT/q=25.85$ mV, giving $D_n=35$ cm²/s and $D_p=12.4$ cm²/s. The diffusion length over a minority-carrier lifetime of 1 µs is $L=\sqrt{D\tau}$: $\sqrt{35\times10^{-6}}\approx59\ \mu$m. This is used for BJT and diode charge-storage estimates.

## High field: velocity saturation

At low field $v=\mu E$. When carriers gain energy faster than they can shed it to optical phonons, the velocity flattens at $v_{sat}$.

## Worked example: a doped bar

An N-type silicon bar, $N_D=10^{16}$ cm⁻³, $\mu_n=1140$ cm²/V·s, length 100 µm, cross-section 10 µm × 1 µm, with 1 V applied.
- $\rho=1/(qn\mu_n)=0.55\ \Omega\cdot$cm
- $R=\rho L/A=0.55\times10^{-2}/(10^{-7})=5.5\times10^{4}\ \Omega$ (here $A=10^{-3}\text{cm}\times10^{-4}\text{cm}=10^{-7}$ cm²)
- $I=1/55{,}000=18$ µA
- Field $E=1\ \text{V}/10^{-2}\text{cm}=100$ V/cm, $v_d=1140\times100=1.14\times10^5$ cm/s, transit time $L/v_d=10^{-2}/1.14\times10^{5}=88$ ns.

The bar's field is far below saturation, so the linear model is valid.

## Real hardware consequences

- **CMOS sizing:** PMOS is made 2-3x wider than NMOS to equalise drive, since $\mu_p\approx\mu_n/3$ at low field.
- **Temperature:** MOSFET on-resistance rises with temperature, about +0.4 to +0.7 %/K for silicon power MOSFETs, largely from mobility decreasing.
- **Strained silicon and SiGe:** engineered strain raises hole and electron mobility by tens of percent without changing the material.
- **HEMTs:** GaN/AlGaN 2DEGs place carriers in undoped material so there is no ionized-impurity scattering, giving high mobility (about 2000 cm²/V·s).
:::

::: applications
- **Sizing PMOS vs NMOS in CMOS logic**: widen PMOS to compensate for lower hole mobility.
- **Power MOSFET $R_{DS(on)}$ rise with temperature**: mobility falls as the die heats, which gives a positive temperature coefficient that helps parallel devices share current.
- **RF and microwave amplifiers in GaAs, InP and GaN HEMTs**: high electron mobility and saturation velocity give high cutoff frequency.
- **Strained-silicon transistors (SiGe source/drain)**: engineered strain increases carrier mobility in advanced CMOS.
- **Hall-effect sensors**: Hall mobility and carrier density come from the same measurement used to characterise wafers.
- **Four-point-probe wafer characterisation**: sheet resistance combined with doping gives mobility and process checks.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Low-field, steady-state drift** unless stated. At high field use $v_{sat}$ models; in nanoscale devices transport can be quasi-ballistic and mobility is not well defined.
- **Bulk silicon at 300 K.** In inversion layers and thin films surface scattering lowers mobility considerably (tens of percent to a factor of 3).
- **Parabolic bands and a single effective mass** for the Drude estimate; actual silicon has anisotropic valleys and heavy/light holes, so $m^*$ is a conductivity average.
- **Hall mobility vs drift mobility**: they differ by a scattering factor of order 1 (often 1.1-1.2 for holes).
- **Valid alternatives**: some interviewers want just "mobility relates drift velocity to electric field; electrons are more mobile than holes"; give that first, then expand.
:::

::: followups
### Why is electron mobility higher than hole mobility in silicon?
Electrons in the conduction band have a smaller conductivity effective mass (about 0.26 $m_0$) than holes in the valence band, which has heavy and light hole bands that mix and a larger effective mass (about 0.4 or more). Since $\mu=q\tau/m^*$, the lighter carrier is faster; also hole bands allow more scattering channels. The ratio is about 2.8 in lightly doped silicon (1350/480). See [[q0070]].

### How does mobility change with temperature, and why?
In lightly doped material, lattice scattering dominates and $\mu\propto T^{-3/2}$ or steeper: from 300 K to 400 K it falls by about 35 % or more. In heavily doped material, ionized-impurity scattering matters more and the temperature dependence is weaker. At low temperatures mobility can rise with $T$ for impurity-limited transport. This is why a hot MOSFET has higher on-resistance.

### What is the drift velocity of an electron in silicon at 1000 V/cm, and is the model still valid?
$v_d=\mu E=1350\times1000=1.35\times10^6$ cm/s (using the linear formula). With saturation, $v=1.35\times10^6/(1+0.135)=1.19\times10^6$ cm/s, about 12 % lower. So the linear model is acceptable here but it starts to fail above a few kV/cm, and by $10^4$ V/cm the true velocity is about 5.7e6 cm/s, less than half the linear prediction.

### How do mobility and diffusion constant relate?
The Einstein relation $D=\mu kT/q$. Both come from the same scattering physics. At 300 K, $kT/q=25.85$ mV, so $D_n=1350\times0.02585=35$ cm²/s. It lets you convert drift-based data into diffusion-based quantities such as the diffusion length $L=\sqrt{D\tau}$ used for diodes and BJTs.

### Why is the mobility in a MOSFET channel lower than the bulk value?
In the inversion layer the carriers are squeezed within a few nanometres of the oxide interface by the vertical gate field. They scatter off the rough Si/SiO₂ interface, off charged traps and with extra phonon modes. Typical channel electron mobility is a few hundred cm²/V·s. It falls further as gate overdrive rises ("universal mobility curve"), which is why modern drive current does not increase quadratically with $V_{GS}-V_T$.

### Scenario: your design uses a long resistor made from lightly doped N-type silicon and the resistance rises 20 % between 25 °C and 100 °C. Is this consistent with mobility?
Yes. A 75 K rise with $\mu\propto T^{-3/2}$ from 298 K to 373 K gives $\mu_{373}/\mu_{298}=(373/298)^{-1.5}=0.71$, so resistance rises about 40 % for pure lattice scattering. With real exponents around 1.5-2.4 and dopant effects, a 20-60 % change is expected. Carrier density is constant in the extrinsic range, so mobility accounts for the change.
:::

::: interpretations
"Charge mobility" is read here as **carrier mobility** in a semiconductor ($\mu_n$, $\mu_p$), not "ion mobility" in electrolytes (used in battery chemistry) or "electrophoretic mobility" in liquids; those share the idea of drift velocity per unit field but have different physics. The related questions are [[q0070]] (holes versus electrons) and [[q0071]] (why N-type has lower resistance), which build directly on this.
