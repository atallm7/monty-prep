::: basics
### In plain words
You can cut coupling (noise leaking from one trace to another) with layout choices, and most are free. Keep each trace close to its ground plane so its fields stay trapped under it. Space noisy traces from sensitive ones, avoid long parallel runs, and cross at 90 degrees. Give noisy and quiet circuits separate return paths. If a victim is very sensitive, add a grounded guard trace.

### Picture it
Hold a flashlight close to the floor and its pool of light stays small. Lift it higher and the pool spreads to neighbors. A trace's electric field behaves the same way. The closer a trace is to the ground plane, the more its field ends right underneath it, and the less reaches the next trace. So moving a trace nearer its plane often beats moving traces apart.

### Words you need
- **Reference plane**: a solid copper layer, usually ground, that a trace's field and return current use.
- **Dielectric height ($h$)**: the thickness of insulation between the trace and its plane.
- **3W rule**: keep trace centers at least three trace widths apart. It is a quick guideline, not a guarantee.
- **Guard trace**: a grounded trace between aggressor and victim, tied down with vias. A floating guard can make coupling worse.
- **Return path**: the route current takes back to its source.

### The simplest example
A rough empirical rule says the coupled amount is about $1/(1+(s/h)^2)$, where $s$ is the spacing between traces. Take traces 0.3 mm apart. Over a plane 0.1 mm below ($s = 3h$), coupling is about 0.1, a tenth. Over a plane 0.4 mm below ($s/h = 0.75$), it is about 0.64. So at the same spacing, a four times thinner dielectric cuts coupling about six times. Check critical nets with field-solver software.

That is the whole idea: keep traces near the plane first, then separate, then guard. The rest of this page lists the techniques in order of cost.

Every one of these techniques is nearly free at layout time and expensive afterwards, so being able to rank them and apply them quickly is a senior-intern skill. It also shows whether you know that distance to the *reference plane* matters as much as distance to the neighbouring trace.

::: technical
**Spacing and height.** For microstrip over a plane, coupling falls steeply once spacing $s$ exceeds the dielectric height $h$. A common empirical approximation for relative coupled amplitude is $\approx 1/(1+(s/h)^2)$: 0.5 at $s=h$, 0.2 at $2h$, 0.1 at $3h$ (−20 dB), 0.04 at $5h$ (−28 dB).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> This formula is a rough empirical approximation (sometimes cited for crosstalk coefficient scaling). Real coupling depends on trace width, stackup, termination and coupled length; use a field solver or the vendor's tool for critical nets. The "3W rule" is a guideline, not a guarantee.

**Techniques.**
1. *Reduce $h$* (thin dielectric to the reference plane): both $C_m/C$ and $L_m/L$ fall. Stripline between two planes confines fields even more and makes far-end crosstalk nearly zero in a homogeneous dielectric.
2. *Increase $s$*; ≥ 3W or ≥ 3h–5h for sensitive pairs. 
3. *Shorten parallel length*; coupled length sets far-end crosstalk and the duration of near-end.
4. *Orthogonal routing* on adjacent layers: overlap area collapses ([[q0567]]).
5. *Guard trace or pour*, grounded at both ends and via-stitched at spacing below $\lambda/20$ of the fastest edge content; a floating guard can act as a resonant coupler.
6. *Separate returns* for noisy and quiet circuits; plane over wires; star points ([[q0495]]).
7. *Edge rate*: crosstalk amplitude ∝ $dv/dt$; slow non-critical edges.
8. *Victim impedance*: lower impedance reduces capacitive pickup; terminate lines; buffer sensitive nodes near the source.
9. *Differential signalling and twisted pairs* reject common coupling.
10. *Partition and placement*: aggressors, victims and connectors in separate zones; no sensitive net in the switcher's neighbourhood.
11. *Filtering and decoupling* at the victim.
:::

::: long
"I'd start with the free stuff, which is geometry. The fields from a trace are mostly confined within about the height of the dielectric above its reference plane, so I keep traces close to the plane, and I separate aggressors and victims by several times that height. As a quick rule I use 3W, spacing of three trace widths centre to centre, but what matters is spacing relative to height. Coupling falls off roughly as one over one plus spacing over height squared, so at three times the height it's about 20 dB down. I also shorten parallel runs, because crosstalk accumulates with coupled length, and when two signals have to cross, I cross them at 90 degrees, especially on adjacent layers.

Second, return paths. Coupling through a shared return is the sneaky kind, so I give noisy and quiet circuits separate returns, I keep the planes solid with no slots under signals, and I put ground vias next to every layer change so return current doesn't wander.

For a really sensitive net, I add a guard: a ground trace or pour on both sides, stitched to the ground plane with vias every few millimetres. The important thing is that it's grounded; a floating guard can make things worse.

Then the electrical levers: slow the aggressor with a series resistor, lower the victim's impedance with a buffer or termination, run long signals differentially, and filter at the victim.

And finally placement, keeping the switching converter, clocks and connectors away from the sensitive analog."
:::

::: terms
- **Reference plane**: the adjacent plane that carries the return current of a signal.
- **3W rule**: centre-to-centre spacing of at least three trace widths to reduce coupling.
- **Guard trace**: a grounded trace between aggressor and victim.
- **Via stitching**: vias that tie a guard or plane region to ground at regular intervals.
- **Stripline / microstrip**: traces between two planes / on the outer layer over one plane.
- **Orthogonal routing**: routing adjacent layers in perpendicular directions to minimise overlap.
- **Near-end / far-end crosstalk**: coupling seen at the aggressor's near end vs the far end.
- **Partitioning**: arranging circuit blocks so noisy and sensitive parts are separated.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "space the traces apart, use a ground plane and guard traces".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames spacing relative to height over the plane (not absolute), and gives the scaling with numbers.
- Distinguishes capacitive, inductive and common-impedance coupling and picks a technique for each.
- Notes the guard-trace caveat (must be grounded and stitched; floating can be worse; helps less than spacing).
- Uses orthogonal routing and stripline to kill overlap and far-end crosstalk.
- Includes electrical levers (edge rate, impedance, differential) and placement/partitioning.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using a floating guard trace.** It becomes a coupled resonator; ground it at both ends and stitch it.
- **Spacing without considering height.** 0.2 mm spacing over a 0.1 mm dielectric is far better than the same spacing over 0.4 mm.
- **Routing parallel on adjacent layers** with no plane between them; broadside coupling is large ([[q0567]]).
- **Splitting ground planes** to "isolate" circuits, then routing signals across the split.
- **Guard traces with no via stitching**, which cannot hold ground potential at high frequency.
- **Believing 3W is a guarantee.** It's a rule of thumb; check critical nets with simulation or measurement.
:::

## Coupling depends on geometry in a predictable way

Coupling between neighbouring traces over a plane is a competition between the trace-to-plane capacitance (which is *good*, since it terminates fields on the plane) and the trace-to-trace capacitance. If the dielectric is thin, most field lines end on the plane right under each trace and few reach the neighbour.

## The techniques, in order of cost

### 1. Free: geometry

- **Reference plane close, continuous.** Thin prepreg between signal and plane. Every high-speed or sensitive signal has an adjacent solid plane ([[q0478]], [[q0479]]).
- **Spacing ≥ 3W, and ≥ 3 to 5 × h** for sensitive nets.
- **Short parallel runs.** Where a bus must run parallel to a clock, shorten the overlap or route around.
- **Orthogonal routing** on adjacent layers, so overlap is a point rather than a length ([[q0567]]).
- **Stripline** for the noisiest signals: fields confined between two planes.

### 2. Free: returns

- **Don't share returns** between noisy and quiet circuits; give each its own path back to the source (star where appropriate, [[q0495]]).
- **No slots under signals**; stitch vias next to layer changes.
- **Decoupling** close to the source so current loops stay small and local.

### 3. Cheap: guards and shields

A guard trace or pour between aggressor and victim helps *if it's grounded*: connect it to the ground plane with vias every few millimetres (spacing below $\lambda/20$ at the highest frequency of interest; in air that is 15 mm at 1 GHz and 3 mm at 5 GHz, and on FR-4 microstrip with $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3$ it is closer to 8.7 mm and 1.7 mm). A floating guard couples both lines and can resonate. A guard also costs board area that could instead be used for extra spacing, and its benefit is often a few dB to about 10 dB compared with plain spacing, so use it where space is limited. A coplanar ground pour with stitching vias is the usual form.

### 4. Cheap: electrical levers

- **Slow the aggressor.** Crosstalk is proportional to $dv/dt$ and $di/dt$. A series resistor at the driver is the simplest knob.
- **Lower the victim's impedance.** A terminated or buffered line sees much less capacitive pickup ([[q0565]]).
- **Differential pairs** reject coupling that hits both lines equally; keep the pair tightly coupled with each other (a pair coupled to each other much more than to anything else is less sensitive).
- **Filtering** at the victim, particularly RC filters on high-impedance analog nodes.

### 5. Layout-level: partition and placement

Group by noise class: switching converters, clocks and high-speed digital in one area; analog front end and references in another; connectors at the edge with filtering. Keep the boundary between areas short and crossed only by filtered or low-speed signals. Return paths must respect the partitions (no split planes crossed by fast signals).

## A worked estimate

Two 0.15 mm traces over a 0.1 mm dielectric (stripline-like, microstrip for the purpose of the estimate). At 0.15 mm edge-to-edge spacing, $s/h$ = 1.5, the approximate relative coupling is $1/(1+2.25) = 0.31$ (−10 dB). Increase the edge-to-edge spacing to 0.45 mm (0.6 mm centre-to-centre, 4W): $s/h$ = 4.5, coupling $1/(1+20.25) = 0.047$ (−26 dB). A 16 dB improvement, for an extra 0.3 mm of space. Adding a stitched guard between them (width 0.15 mm, vias every 3 mm) can add a few more dB but only if the spacing allows it.

## Verify

Measure with a scope or VNA between two nets with the board running, or simulate a representative stackup with a field solver. For sensitive analog, check both noise coupled from the digital neighbours and the sensitivity to nearby plane or via changes.

## Worked example: spend the budget on spacing or on stack-up?

Using the empirical shape $K(s/h)=1/(1+(s/h)^2)$, compare ways to reduce coupling between two 0.15 mm traces that currently sit with 0.15 mm edge-to-edge spacing over a 0.1 mm dielectric. The baseline is $s/h=1.5$, $K=0.31$ or $-10.2$ dB.

| Option | $s$ | $h$ | $s/h$ | $K$ (dB) | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline | 0.15 mm | 0.10 mm | 1.5 | −10.2 | none |
| Triple the spacing | 0.45 mm | 0.10 mm | 4.5 | −26.5 | 0.3 mm more board width per pair |
| Halve the dielectric | 0.15 mm | 0.05 mm | 3.0 | −20.0 | thinner prepreg, narrower trace for 50 Ω, tighter fab tolerance |
| Halve the dielectric and double the spacing | 0.30 mm | 0.05 mm | 6.0 | −31.4 | both |

Halving $h$ buys almost as much as tripling the spacing (9.8 dB vs 16.3 dB) at no board-area cost, but it also changes the impedance of the trace: for the same 50 Ω the trace width must shrink roughly in proportion to $h$, so the narrower, tighter geometry then changes $s/h$ again, and a thinner dielectric makes manufacturing tolerance a larger fraction of $h$. If the stack-up is already fixed (as it almost always is late in a project), spacing is the only free variable, which is why the stack-up decision belongs in the first week of layout, not the last ([[q0478]]).

Now the bus case. As an upper bound, eight equally coupled aggressors switching in the same direction would deliver the sum of eight pulses: $20\log_{10}8=+18$ dB over a single aggressor. A spacing that gave an acceptable $-26$ dB for one neighbor leaves only $-8$ dB for the worst-case pattern. Budget for the worst data pattern, and note that only the two nearest neighbors dominate in practice (coupling falls steeply with distance), so the realistic worst case is the two adjacent traces plus a smaller contribution from the next ring.

## Guard traces: the failure that needs numbers

A guard trace grounded only at its ends is a transmission line stub. A 50 mm guard on FR-4 ($\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3$, velocity $c/\sqrt3$) is a half wave at

$$f=\frac{c}{2L\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}}=\frac{3\times10^8}{2\times0.05\times1.73}=1.7\ \text{GHz}$$

and at that frequency a guard grounded at both ends *amplifies* the coupling between the two lines. Via stitching every 5 mm or so pushes this resonance far above the signal's spectral content. That is the quantitative reason for the "vias every few millimeters" rule, and why an unconnected or end-grounded guard can be worse than none near its resonance.

## Limiting cases and when the rules of thumb break

- **Very thick dielectric** ($h$ comparable to the spacing): a plane no longer confines the field, and the crosstalk behaves like two traces in free space; the $s/h$ rule saturates and spacing is the only lever.
- **Differential and tightly coupled pairs:** the 3W rule applies between *pairs*, not within them. Coupling to a neighbor is largely common-mode and cancels at a differential receiver, so a pair is less sensitive than a single trace at the same spacing, until the imbalance of skew or length mismatch converts it back.
- **Slow edges:** if the edge is slow compared with the coupled length's delay, far-end crosstalk almost disappears and spacing can be relaxed. This is why a slow GPIO next to a clock rarely needs the same spacing as a clock next to a clock.
- **Via fields and connector breakouts:** the plane is interrupted there, so the $s/h$ estimate does not apply. These are the usual places where coupling is worst and where field-solver results are needed.

## Verification on the bench

Excite one net with a signal generator or the real driver and use a spectrum analyzer or a VNA ($S_{21}$ between the two nets, others terminated in 50 Ω) to read the coupling in dB across frequency. A good result is a smooth upward slope of 20 dB per decade (capacitive and inductive coupling both rise with frequency) up to the first resonance; a resonance peak points at a floating guard, an unstitched plane gap or a stub. Repeat after a layout change to confirm the dB improvement predicted by the table above, rather than relying on the rule of thumb alone.

::: applications
- **DDR and parallel memory buses**: trace spacing to height ratios (for example ≥ 2h to 3h) with length matching, to hold crosstalk-induced timing skew down.
- **Mixed-signal boards**: analog front end routed on a separate side, with guarded high-impedance nodes and clean ground returns.
- **Clock distribution**: clocks in stripline, with keep-out zones around them.
- **Switching converters**: feedback and sense traces away from the switch node, with ground between.
- **Audio and sensor boards**: guard rings around high-impedance op-amp inputs and photodiode nodes.
:::

::: assumptions
- **The empirical formula** $1/(1+(s/h)^2)$ is a rough guide for weakly coupled microstrip; it ignores trace width, termination and coupled length.
- **Spacing rules** assume a reference plane exists; without one, coupling is much larger and the rules don't apply.
- **Guard traces** are assumed grounded and stitched; effectiveness depends on frequency and via spacing.
- **Frequency**: lumped via-spacing rule ($\lambda/20$) assumes the dominant frequency content is known; use the spectrum from $1/(\pi t_r)$.
- **Alternative approaches**: for the highest-sensitivity nets, spend board area on isolation: separate layer, shield can, or physical distance, over clever routing.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the distance to the reference plane so important for crosstalk?
Because the fields from a trace terminate mainly on the nearest conductor. A thin dielectric means most of the trace's capacitance is to the plane and most of its magnetic flux is cancelled by plane return currents, so little reaches the neighbour. Halving $h$ with the same spacing is like doubling $s/h$.

### Do guard traces always help?
No. A grounded, via-stitched guard helps, typically by several dB. A floating or sparsely stitched guard can couple to both lines and resonate, making crosstalk worse. And the area it uses could often give more benefit as plain extra spacing. I use guards where space is tight or where a sensitive node needs shielding from specific aggressors.

### What's the benefit of orthogonal routing on adjacent layers?
Broadside coupling depends on overlap area. Two parallel traces on adjacent layers overlap along their whole length; crossing at 90° reduces overlap to width × width. For 0.2 mm traces and 0.2 mm dielectric, a 50 mm overlap is about 1.9 pF vs 7 fF for a single crossing, a 250:1 reduction.

### How do you reduce crosstalk on a bus you can't space out?
Shorten the parallel length (route in groups), reduce edge rates with series resistors, thin the dielectric to the plane, use stripline layers, add ground traces between groups, and terminate lines to lower their impedance. And check timing margin, since crosstalk adds jitter and skew.

### Scenario: an analog input picks up noise from a nearby SPI bus on the same layer. What do you change?
First, spacing and plane: confirm a continuous plane under both, increase spacing (≥ 3 to 5 × h), and shorten the parallel run. If the board is fixed, add series resistors on the SPI lines to slow edges, add an RC filter on the analog node close to the ADC, and a grounded, stitched guard between them. If the analog source is high impedance, buffer it near the source.
