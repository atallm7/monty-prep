::: testing
Whether you understand that **a component on a board is not an isolated component**. The strong answer says how each type is checked and, more importantly, what in-circuit measurement actually reports:

1. Parallel paths corrupt resistor, capacitor and inductor readings.
2. Remedies: power-off measurement with awareness, lifting a pin, guarding (ICT), or measuring a *powered* circuit's voltage/current instead.
3. Type-specific checks: diode drops, transistor junctions, crystals (probe loading!), regulators under load, connectors, fuses.
4. Low-value parts need 4-wire (Kelvin) measurement.
:::

::: why
The first instinct on seeing a wrong value is to put a meter across a resistor. If you don't know it reads the whole network, you'll condemn good parts and miss bad ones. Production ICT solves this with guarding, bench engineers solve it with judgment.
:::

::: concepts
- **Parallel paths**: other components connected across the part under test.
- **Guarding**: driving other nodes to the same potential so they carry no current.
- **4-wire (Kelvin)**: separate force and sense leads. See [[q0627]].
- **Diode-mode test**: forward-drop check of junctions.
- **Probe loading**: probe affects sensitive circuits (crystals, high-Z).
- **Powered-circuit inference**: voltage/current checks instead of component values.
:::

::: answer
"The key point is that on a board, a meter measures everything connected across the part, not just the part. A 10 kΩ resistor with another 10 kΩ path across it reads 5 kΩ. So for resistors I either measure unpowered knowing what's in parallel, lift one leg, or better, infer from voltages and currents on the powered circuit. Production ICT uses guarding to cancel the parallel paths.

For capacitors I look for shorts with a meter, and I use an LCR meter for value and ESR, but with the same parallel-path caveat; in-circuit I often check by ripple and decoupling behaviour on a scope. Diodes and LEDs I check in diode mode or by lighting them: about 0.6 V for silicon and 1.8 to 3 V for LEDs, with the polarity right. Transistors have junction checks, but better is to check the bias voltages when powered.

Crystals are special: a normal probe adds capacitance and can stop the oscillation, so I use a high-impedance probe or look at the buffered clock output. Regulators I test under load, connectors for continuity and polarity, fuses for resistance, and low-value shunts with a 4-wire measurement, because lead resistance would swamp a milliohm."
:::

::: technical
**In-circuit resistor.** $R_{meas}=R_x\parallel R_{path}$. $R_x=10$ kΩ with a 10 kΩ path: 5 kΩ. A reading *higher* than nominal means a bad part or open; *lower* means either a bad part or a parallel path, so ambiguity. Lift one leg or check powered voltages.

**Capacitors.** Short: ~0 Ω steady (a good cap shows a charging transient). Value/ESR: LCR meter (test frequency e.g. 1 kHz or 100 kHz). In-circuit, other paths and the meter's test signal confound readings. Ceramic X7R loses capacitance with DC bias, so a powered rail's actual capacitance can be much lower than nominal.

**Inductors/ferrite beads.** DC resistance (DCR) with a milliohm-capable meter, 4-wire; inductance with LCR (in-circuit unreliable).

**Diodes/LEDs.** Diode mode: silicon about 0.5-0.7 V forward, Schottky about 0.15-0.4 V, LED about 1.8-3.3 V depending on colour; reverse reads OL. In-circuit parallel paths can mask.

**Transistors/FETs.** Unpowered junction checks (BJT: two diodes; MOSFET: body diode and gate leakage open). Powered: $V_{BE}\approx0.65$ V, $V_{GS}$ vs threshold, drain voltage and gate drive waveform.

**Crystals.** A 10x probe (about 10 pF) can detune or stop a 16 MHz crystal with 12 pF load capacitors; observe via the buffered clock pin or a low-C probe.

**Regulators.** Output voltage unloaded and loaded, dropout, ripple, thermal.

**Connectors/fuses/switches.** Continuity, pin mapping, polarity; fuse about 0.01-0.5 Ω cold.

**Low-ohm parts.** 4-wire: a 10 mΩ shunt measured 2-wire through 0.2 Ω of leads reads 0.21 Ω (21x error).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Meter test currents and voltages differ (diode mode open-circuit voltage often around 3 V, test current about 1 mA); consult your meter. Passive tolerances and DC-bias effects depend on the part.
:::

::: long
"The thing to remember is that on a board the meter measures everything connected across the part. If I put a meter across a 10 kilohm resistor and there's another 10 kilohm path in parallel, I read 5 kilohms. A reading that's higher than nominal means the part is bad or open, but a lower reading is ambiguous. So for resistors I lift one leg, or better, I power the board and infer from the voltages and currents. In production, ICT uses guarding to drive other nodes to the same potential so they don't carry current.

For capacitors I look for shorts, and use an LCR meter for value and ESR, with the same caveat. In-circuit I often check decoupling by looking at ripple on a scope. Ceramic capacitors also lose capacitance under DC bias, so the nominal value isn't what a powered rail sees.

Diodes and LEDs I check in diode mode: about 0.6 volts for silicon, 0.2 to 0.4 for Schottky, 1.8 to 3 for LEDs, and open in reverse. Transistors have junction checks unpowered, but the real test is the bias voltages when powered.

Crystals are special: a normal scope probe adds about 10 picofarads and can stop the oscillation, so I measure the buffered clock output or use a low-capacitance probe. Regulators I check unloaded and under load for voltage, ripple and heat. Connectors get continuity and polarity checks.

And for low-value shunts and traces I use 4-wire, because a few tenths of an ohm of lead resistance would swamp milliohms."
:::

::: terms
- **In-circuit measurement**: measuring a part while installed.
- **Guarding**: driving neighbours to cancel parallel paths.
- **4-wire / Kelvin**: force/sense measurement.
- **DCR**: DC resistance of an inductor.
- **ESR**: equivalent series resistance of a capacitor.
- **Diode mode**: meter function showing forward drop.
- **DC-bias derating**: capacitance loss under voltage.
- **Lifting a leg**: isolating a part by unsoldering one terminal.
:::

::: exceptional
**Average**: "Use a multimeter on each component."

**Exceptional**:
- Explains the parallel-path error with numbers.
- Offers remedies: lift leg, guard, powered inference.
- Gives type-specific checks and expected values.
- Knows probe loading on crystals and 4-wire for low ohms.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Trusting low in-circuit resistance readings.** A parallel path makes a good part read low. Lift a leg or check the powered node voltage.
- **Probing a crystal with a standard probe.** Its capacitance pulls or stops the oscillator. Use the buffered clock output or a low-C probe.
- **Using 2-wire on milliohm parts.** Lead and contact resistance swamps the part. Use 4-wire (Kelvin).
- **Ignoring capacitor DC-bias derating.** A ceramic cap can lose much of its value at its working voltage. Check the datasheet curve.
- **Measuring a powered board with the DMM in resistance mode.** The reading is meaningless and the meter or part can be damaged. Power down first.
:::

## Type by type

| Part | Quick check | Better check | Pitfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resistor | R unpowered | lift leg / powered V and I | parallel paths |
| Capacitor | short check, LCR | ripple on scope | DC-bias derating |
| Inductor/bead | DCR 4-wire | saturation by current ramp | tiny DCR |
| Diode/LED | diode mode, lights | forward V at operating current | parallel paths |
| BJT/MOSFET | junction check | bias voltages powered | gate damage by ESD |
| Crystal | oscillation at buffered pin | start-up time, frequency | probe stops oscillation |
| Regulator | Vout unloaded | under load, ripple, thermal | dropout/thermal shutdown |
| Fuse/connector | continuity | 4-wire resistance, pin map | mis-mapped pins |

## Crystal probing

A 16 MHz crystal with 12 pF caps on each pin sees the two caps in series (6 pF) plus a few pF of stray, roughly 9–10 pF of load. A 10x probe adds about 10 pF to one side, so that side becomes about 22 pF and the series combination rises to about 7.8 pF plus stray, a roughly 20 % change in load capacitance. That pulls the frequency by something like tens of ppm (the exact figure depends on the crystal's motional capacitance), and a marginal oscillator may stop altogether. Probe the buffered clock output instead, or use a low-capacitance active probe.

## Powered inference

Often the best "component test" is voltage and current: a resistor in a divider is verified by the node voltage; a capacitor by ripple; a diode by drop at known current. Compare against the schematic expectation. See [[q0587]].

::: followups
### How does ICT avoid the parallel-path problem?
It drives guard nodes to the same potential as the measurement node's neighbours so no current flows in parallel paths.

### Why is a capacitor's in-circuit value different from its label?
Parallel paths, DC bias derating, temperature, tolerance (±10-20 % for X7R).

### Why 4-wire for a 10 mΩ shunt?
Lead and contact resistance (0.1-0.5 Ω) is far larger; separate sense leads measure only the part.

### How to check an LED on a board without power?
Diode mode lights many LEDs faintly at about 1 mA, with the anode on the meter's positive lead. It only works if the meter's open-circuit voltage exceeds the LED's forward drop, so blue and white LEDs (about 3 V) may not light on meters that only supply about 2 V.

### Scenario: board reads 3.2 kΩ across a 10 kΩ pull-up. Is it bad?
Not necessarily. Likely a parallel path (3.2 kΩ ≈ 10 kΩ ∥ 4.7 kΩ); lift a leg or check the node voltage powered.
