::: testing
Whether you can justify EMI control as an engineering and business decision, not just a rule. Strong answers give several distinct reasons and quantify at least one:

1. **Regulation**: emissions limits decide whether a product can be sold.
2. **Interference with other systems**, including your own radios.
3. **Reciprocity**: a structure that radiates well also receives well, so emitters are susceptible.
4. **Diagnostic value**: a radiating loop is a bad signal-integrity design.
5. **Cost and schedule**: late-stage fixes are expensive.

Bonus: you know that tiny currents (microamps on a cable) can fail a test.
:::

::: why
Radiated emissions are the usual reason a hardware product misses its launch date. Compliance testing happens late, in a chamber, with the hardware essentially finished, so a failure means rework on a schedule that's already tight. Interviewers ask this to see whether you've connected the "layout rules" to their purpose. If you can say "this loop is 10 cm², so it's 20 dB worse than a 1 cm² loop", you sound like someone who has done it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Radiated emission limits**: field-strength limits at a specified distance, in dBµV/m. See [[q0559]].
- **Loop radiation**: $E \approx 1.316\times10^{-14}\,f^2AI/r$. See [[q0560]].
- **Common-mode cable radiation**: $E \approx 1.26\times10^{-6}\,fLI/r$ over a ground plane.
- **Antenna reciprocity**: an antenna has the same pattern and efficiency transmitting and receiving.
- **Desense (self-interference)**: noise raising a receiver's floor. See [[q0568]].
- **Immunity**: the other side of EMC. See [[q0563]].
:::

::: answer
"There are a few reasons. The first is that it's regulated: in most markets a product has to meet radiated emission limits, like FCC Part 15 or CISPR 32, before it can be sold. A failure late in development means a respin or a pile of ferrites and shields.

The second is that it interferes with other things. That can be the neighbour's radio or TV, an aircraft or medical device, or, most often, my own wireless: noise from my processor landing in the Wi-Fi, Bluetooth or GPS band desensitises my own receiver, and range drops.

The third is reciprocity. A structure that radiates efficiently also receives efficiently. A big loop that radiates my clock is also a big loop that picks up someone else's transmitter, so a board that's loud is typically also susceptible.

The fourth is that radiation is a symptom. It tells me I have large current loops or cable common-mode current, and those same features cause ringing, crosstalk and ground bounce. And it's sensitive: for a 30 cm cable at 100 MHz, about 12 microamps of common-mode current reaches the FCC Class B limit at 3 metres, and a 10 cm² loop carrying 10 milliamps at 100 MHz is about 9 dB over."
:::

::: technical
**1. Regulation.** Radiated limits are set in dBµV/m at a fixed distance. For example, FCC Part 15 Class B (3 m): about 40 dBµV/m for 30–88 MHz, 43.5 for 88–216 MHz, 46 for 216–960 MHz and 54 above 960 MHz. CISPR 32 Class B uses a 10 m distance with limits of roughly 30 dBµV/m (30–230 MHz) and 37 dBµV/m (230 MHz to 1 GHz).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Limit values and measurement distances depend on region, product class and the edition of the standard. Use the applicable current standard for real work; the values above are typical.

**2. Magnitudes.** Small loop: $E = 1.316\times10^{-14} f^2 A I / r$. At 100 MHz, 10 mA, 3 m: 1 cm² gives 32.8 dBµV/m (10.7 dB under the 43.5 limit); 10 cm² gives 52.8 dBµV/m (9.3 dB over). Area ×10 = +20 dB. CM on a cable: $E \approx 1.26\times10^{-6} f L I/r$ (with ground reflection; the free-space value is half). Solving for $I$ at the limit: $I = E\,r/(1.26\times10^{-6} fL)$ = about 12 µA for $L$ = 0.3 m at 100 MHz (valid while $L\lesssim\lambda/10$).

**3. Reciprocity.** A given antenna geometry has the same gain and effective area in transmit and receive, so the loops and cables that radiate your clock will also couple external fields in ([[q0563]]).

**4. Self-interference.** Receiver sensitivity degrades as $10\log(1+I/N)$ dB when in-band interference $I$ equals a fraction of the thermal noise $N$ ([[q0568]]).

**5. Symptom of poor SI/PI.** Large loops mean high inductance, which means ringing, ground bounce, crosstalk and signal degradation as well as radiation.

**6. Cost.** Late fixes (shield cans, ferrites, filters, respin) cost money on the BOM and in schedule; layout-level fixes cost close to nothing at the start.

**7. Security.** Unintended emissions can leak information about internal signals (TEMPEST-type concerns) in some products.
:::

::: short
"Mainly four reasons. It's regulated: you can't sell a product that fails FCC or CISPR limits. It interferes with other radios, including your own, because noise in the Wi-Fi or GPS band desensitises your receiver. By reciprocity, anything that radiates well also picks up interference well. And it's a symptom of bad layout: big loops and cable common-mode current also cause ringing and crosstalk. It's very sensitive too: about 12 microamps of common-mode current on a 30 centimetre cable can reach the limit at 100 MHz."
:::

::: long
"First, it's the law. Every market has limits on radiated emissions, FCC Part 15 in the US, CISPR 32 and the EU rules elsewhere, and a product that fails can't be shipped. Because testing happens late, a failure means a scramble: ferrites on cables, shield cans, filters, or a board respin.

Second, interfering with others. Radiated noise can upset radios, TVs, medical devices, avionics. But the more common victim is my own product's wireless. If the processor's harmonics land in the Wi-Fi, Bluetooth or GPS band, they raise the receiver's noise floor. If the interference is equal to the thermal noise it costs 3 dB of sensitivity, which is a big range hit. That's desense.

Third, reciprocity. An antenna's pattern and efficiency are the same transmitting and receiving. So a loop or cable that radiates my 100 MHz harmonic well is also good at picking up someone else's signal at that frequency. A loud board is almost always also a susceptible board.

Fourth, radiation is a symptom. The radiators on a PCB are current loops and cables with common-mode current. A big loop means high inductance, which means ringing, ground bounce and crosstalk, so cleaning up the radiation cleans up the signal integrity.

And the numbers are sobering. A small loop radiates proportional to frequency squared times area times current, so ten times the area is 20 dB. At 100 MHz and 10 milliamps, a one square centimetre loop is about 11 dB under the Class B limit and a 10 square centimetre loop is about 9 dB over. On the cable side, about 12 microamps of common-mode current on a 30 cm cable is enough to hit the limit."
:::

::: terms
- **Radiated emissions**: electromagnetic fields emitted by a product, measured as field strength (dBµV/m) at a distance.
- **FCC Part 15 / CISPR 32**: emission standards for unintentional radiators / multimedia equipment.
- **Class A / Class B**: commercial/industrial vs residential limits; Class B is stricter.
- **Reciprocity**: an antenna's transmit and receive patterns and efficiencies are the same.
- **Desense**: loss of receiver sensitivity from in-band noise.
- **Far field**: region beyond about $\lambda/2\pi$ where the simple formulas used here apply.
- **Common-mode choke / ferrite**: components used to suppress CM current on a cable.
- **Pre-compliance testing**: in-house near-field and conducted scans before the formal test.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "to meet regulations and not interfere with other devices".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives multiple reasons (regulation, interference with others and with your own radios, reciprocity, symptom of bad SI, cost) and ranks them.
- Quantifies sensitivity: $f^2A$ scaling (10× area is +20 dB), and microamps of CM current on a cable at the limit.
- Mentions desense with the $10\log(1+I/N)$ relationship.
- Mentions that compliance testing is late and fixes then are costly, so you design for it.
- Knows limit values are standard- and class-dependent, and says so.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"EMI only matters for the regulators."** It also hurts your own wireless performance, and it is a sign of weak signal integrity.
- **Thinking of radiation as the only issue.** Conducted emissions are tested too and often fail first on switching supplies.
- **Assuming small currents are harmless.** Microamps of CM current can fail a radiated test.
- **Believing a shield can always fix it afterwards.** Shield cans, ferrites and filters add cost, don't fix cable common mode on their own, and leak at seams and openings.
- **Forgetting reciprocity.** Reducing emissions by fixing loop area also reduces susceptibility; people often test them separately and miss the connection.
- **Quoting limits as universal.** They vary by region, product class and standard edition.
:::

::: deep
## 1. You can't ship it

Radiated emission limits are field strengths at a defined distance, usually in a certified chamber or open-area test site. A typical set (FCC Class B, 3 m): 40, 43.5, 46 and 54 dBµV/m for the bands 30–88, 88–216, 216–960 and above 960 MHz. In dB, "over by 3 dB" means you need to reduce the field by a factor of 1.4. Over by 20 dB means a factor of 10.

Because testing needs a finished product and a booked chamber, a failure costs weeks. Fixing EMI at layout costs almost nothing; at the end it costs a respin or cans and ferrites on every unit.

## 2. How sensitive is the margin? Two numbers to carry

### A loop

For a small loop (dimensions ≪ λ), the maximum far-field electric field is

$$E = \frac{120\pi^2}{c^2}\frac{f^2AI}{r} = 1.316\times10^{-14}\,\frac{f^2AI}{r}.$$

At 100 MHz, 10 mA and $r$ = 3 m: 1 cm² gives 44 µV/m (32.8 dBµV/m); 3 cm² gives 42.4 dBµV/m; 10 cm² gives 52.8 dBµV/m. Against 43.5 dBµV/m: 10.7 dB under, 1.1 dB under, 9.3 dB over.

### A cable

A cable of length $L \ll \lambda$ carrying common-mode current $I$ is a short monopole. Over a ground plane: $E \approx 1.26\times10^{-6}\,fLI/r$. Solving for the current that just meets the limit $E_{lim}$ at distance $r$:

$$I_{max} = \frac{E_{lim}\,r}{1.26\times10^{-6}\,fL}.$$

For $L$ = 0.3 m: 26.5 µA at 30 MHz (limit 100 µV/m), and 11.9 µA at 100 MHz (limit 150 µV/m).

## 3. It hurts your own product

Wireless receivers are designed for a thermal-noise-limited floor. In-band interference raises the effective floor, reducing sensitivity by $10\log_{10}(1+I/N)$ dB, where $I$ is the in-band interference power and $N$ the receiver's noise power. Interference equal to the noise costs 3 dB; at −10 dB relative to the noise, 0.4 dB. See [[q0568]] for how this is called desense and how to prevent it. Many radiated EMI peaks are actually found *because* the product's radio performance dropped.

## 4. Reciprocity: loud means susceptible

Antenna theory: for a given structure, the radiation pattern and the receiving pattern are the same, and the effective area is related to gain. So the geometry that makes your 100 MHz harmonic radiate (a big loop, a long cable) also couples an external 100 MHz field into your circuit. If you pass a radiated *immunity* test with margin, it is often because you also pass emissions. This is why good layout reduces both ([[q0563]], [[q0564]]).

## 5. A symptom of poor signal integrity

A large current loop has high inductance; the same loop area that radiates also rings, bounces and crosstalks. Radiated EMI is a measurable proxy for these problems:

| Layout fault | EMI effect | Signal-integrity effect |
|---|---|---|
| Big hot loop | Radiation ∝ area | Voltage overshoot ∝ $L\,di/dt$ |
| Return path broken by a slot | Radiating loop, CM current | Reflections, crosstalk |
| Poor decoupling | Supply ripple coupled to cables | Ground bounce, jitter |
| Unfiltered cable | CM antenna | Noise injected into analog front ends |

## 6. What the fixes cost

Layout fixes (small loops, solid planes, edge rate control) are essentially free if done at the start. Late fixes: ferrites on cables (cents to dollars per unit, plus assembly), shield cans (tens of cents to a few dollars, plus assembly and rework difficulty), extra layers, or a respin (weeks and a prototype run). The cheapest EMI fix is the one you never need.

## 7. Information leakage

Emissions correlated with internal signals can leak information (for example, keyboard or display emissions in "TEMPEST"-type analyses). It matters only for security-critical products, but is another reason to control radiation.
:::

::: applications
- **Consumer electronics (phones, laptops, routers)**: passing FCC/CE emissions and keeping radio sensitivity high.
- **Automotive**: radiated emissions are limited to protect on-board AM/FM/GNSS receivers.
- **Medical devices**: emissions limits protect other patient-connected equipment.
- **Aviation and military equipment**: strict limits (and stricter immunity) because of safety-critical receivers.
- **IoT and wireless modules**: layouts that protect the receiver from digital noise.
- **Switching power supplies**: designed with small hot loops and input filters to meet conducted and radiated limits.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Far-field, small-antenna formulas** for loop and short monopole in free space or with an idealised ground plane. Real chamber results vary by several dB because of reflections, cable layout and enclosure.
- **The loop example uses 10 mA at every frequency** for illustration. Real harmonic currents fall with frequency according to the spectral envelope ([[q0559]]).
- **Limits are quoted for FCC Part 15 Class B at 3 m and CISPR 32 Class B**; confirm against current standards and your product category.
- **Reciprocity assumes linear, passive antenna structures.** It holds for the geometry; the "source" and "receiver" circuits have different impedances so the actual coupling depends on both.
- **Alternative answer**: for a product with no radios and a low-speed clock, "regulatory compliance" may be the only reason and the cheapest fixes are enough.
:::

::: followups
### How does reciprocity relate to EMI?
For a passive antenna structure the transmit and receive patterns and efficiency are identical. A loop or cable that radiates at 100 MHz couples in an external field at 100 MHz just as well. Good emissions design (small loops, filtered, bonded cables) therefore also improves immunity, although the two are tested separately.

### Why is cable common-mode current so important to emissions?
The cable is long compared to the board's traces and sticks out of the product, so it's an efficient antenna, and the return path for CM current is large and uncontrolled. $E\propto fLI$. About 12 µA at 100 MHz on a 0.3 m cable can reach the FCC Class B limit at 3 m (idealised), which is far less current than any signal in the cable carries.

### What does 3 dB over the limit mean in practice?
3 dB is a factor of 1.41 in field strength (a factor of 2 in power). To pass you need to reduce the emission by at least that amount, plus margin (typically 3 to 6 dB for unit-to-unit variation). A 10 dB reduction means a factor of 3.2: halving the loop area gives only 6 dB, so combine it with a slower edge or a ferrite.

### Can radiated EMI fail even if every trace is perfectly routed?
Yes. Cable common-mode current can come from ground noise at the connector, from a poorly terminated shield, from imbalanced pairs, or from a switching supply's noise coupling into a ground wire. The traces can be ideal and the cable still radiates. Also the enclosure matters: a metal enclosure with seams can reduce or amplify emissions.

### Scenario: your product passes radiated emissions but Wi-Fi throughput drops when the display is on. How do you connect those?
The display interface (MIPI or LVDS) clocks and harmonics may fall in the 2.4 or 5 GHz band at levels below the regulatory limit but above the Wi-Fi receiver's thermal floor, which is tens of dB below. That's desense. I'd measure the receiver's noise floor with the display on and off, locate the source with a near-field probe, and fix it by filtering, shielding or changing the clock to move its harmonics.
:::

::: interpretations
"Why do I want to avoid radiated EMI?" can mean (a) why it matters at all (the reasons above) or (b) why *radiated* specifically rather than conducted. Conducted emissions matter too, on power and signal cables, but radiated emissions are harder to localise and fix, and they hit both the regulatory limits and nearby receivers. Neighbours: [[q0559]] (what EMI is), [[q0560]] (sources), [[q0562]] (how to minimise it).
:::
