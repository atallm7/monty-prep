::: testing
Whether you can apply the subharmonic analysis to **disturbances**, noise and load perturbations, and describe both regimes concretely. The interviewer wants:

1. $D<0.5$: a disturbance's effect on the inductor current alternates in sign and **shrinks** each cycle by $D/(1-D)$. Noise gets filtered, the converter returns to its steady state.
2. $D>0.5$: the same disturbance **grows** each cycle by $D/(1-D)>1$, so pulse widths alternate with growing amplitude until nonlinear limits clamp it.
3. What counts as a disturbance: a noise spike that trips the comparator early, a load step, a line step, a change of command.
4. What you see and what you do about it.
:::

::: why
Real converters never sit in a perfect steady state. A noise spike on the current-sense, a load step, or an input-voltage dip happens every few milliseconds. Whether the converter shrugs these off or breaks into a period-2 oscillation depends on which side of 50% duty it is on. This question tests whether you can turn an equation into predicted behavior in the lab.
:::

::: concepts
- **Per-cycle error multiplier** $\alpha=-D/(1-D)$ for uncompensated peak current mode ([[q0253]]).
- **Disturbance as an initial condition**: a noise event or step shifts the current at some clock edge by $\delta$.
- **Geometric decay/growth**: $\delta_n=\alpha^n\delta_0$.
- **Noise gain**: for random per-cycle disturbances the rms current error is amplified by $1/\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}$.
- **Nonlinear limits**: minimum/maximum on-time and current limit.
- **Slope compensation** modifies $\alpha$ ([[q0255]]).
:::

::: answer
"In peak current mode, any disturbance, say a noise spike that makes the comparator trip early, or a load step, shows up as a small error in the inductor current at the start of a cycle. The question is what that error does over the following cycles. Each cycle it flips sign and gets multiplied by D over one minus D.

Below 50 percent duty that factor is less than one. At 30 percent it's 0.43, at 40 percent 0.67, so the error shrinks quickly: a 50 milliamp kick shrinks to 1 percent of its initial size within about five to eleven cycles, and the converter looks stable. Noise just produces a little extra jitter, and the closer you get to 50 percent, the more that jitter is amplified: at 45 percent it's about 1.7 times the noise rms.

Above 50 percent, the factor is more than one. At 60 percent it's 1.5, at 70 percent 2.3, so the same 50 milliamp kick doubles roughly every two cycles and the pulse widths alternate long, short, long, short with growing amplitude. In a few cycles it reaches the limits, the converter skips pulses or hits maximum duty, and you see a large period-two pattern at half the switching frequency.

So noise or load perturbations are harmless below 50 percent and are an oscillation trigger above it. With slope compensation the factor becomes minus m2 minus m a over m1 plus m a, which stays below one at any duty."
:::

::: technical
**Setup.** Start-of-cycle current error $\delta_n$; $\delta_{n+1}=\alpha\,\delta_n$ with $\alpha=-m_2/m_1=-D/(1-D)$ (no compensation).

**$D<0.5$.** $|\alpha|<1$: $\delta_n=\alpha^n\delta_0$, alternating but shrinking. Cycles to reach 1% of $\delta_0$: $n=\ln0.01/\ln|\alpha|$.

**$D>0.5$.** $|\alpha|>1$: alternating and growing as $|\alpha|^n$. Cycles for a 50 mA kick to reach 1 A: $n=\ln(1/0.05)/\ln|\alpha|$.

| $D$ | $\alpha$ | Behavior | Cycles |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | $-0.43$ | decays | 5.4 to 1% |
| 0.40 | $-0.67$ | decays | 11.4 to 1% |
| 0.45 | $-0.82$ | decays slowly | 22.9 to 1% |
| 0.50 | $-1$ | persists | none |
| 0.60 | $-1.5$ | grows | 7.4 to reach 1 A from 50 mA |
| 0.70 | $-2.33$ | grows fast | 3.5 to reach 1 A from 50 mA |

**Random noise.** If every cycle receives an independent disturbance of rms $\sigma$ (comparator noise, switching-edge noise), the stationary rms error for $|\alpha|<1$ is $\sigma/\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}$: gain 1.11 at $D=0.3$, 1.34 at 0.4, 1.74 at 0.45, 3.6 at 0.49, unbounded at $D\ge0.5$.

**Load and line perturbations.** A load step changes the command $V_c$ and so the current target; the first cycle sees an error equal to the change, which then evolves with the same multiplier. For $D<0.5$ the inner loop settles in a few cycles and the outer loop takes over. For $D>0.5$ the inner loop itself oscillates. A line step at fixed $V_c$ changes $m_1$, $m_2$ and $D$; if it pushes $D$ across 0.5 the converter changes regime.

**End state for $D>0.5$.** The on-time is limited between 0 and $T$ (and the blanking minimum): the waveform locks to a bounded period-2 pattern, for example a long pulse followed by a very short or skipped one, and the output carries a large $f_{sw}/2$ ripple.

**With slope compensation $m_a$:** $\alpha=-(m_2-m_a)/(m_1+m_a)$, so for $m_a\ge m_2/2$ the converter is stable for any $D<1$.
:::

::: short
"In peak current mode a disturbance, like noise tripping the comparator early or a load step, shows up as a current error that flips sign each cycle and is multiplied by D over one minus D. Below 50 percent that's less than one, so the error dies out and noise only adds a bit of jitter. Above 50 percent it's greater than one, so the error grows every cycle, giving alternating long and short pulses at half the switching frequency, until the duty limits clamp it. Slope compensation fixes it."
:::

::: long
"Let me take a concrete disturbance: a noise spike on the current-sense signal makes the comparator trip early in one cycle, so the inductor current at the start of the next cycle is off by, say, 50 milliamps. Same thing for a small load step. What matters is what happens to that error over the next cycles, and in peak current mode with no slope compensation the rule is: it flips sign and is multiplied by D over one minus D each cycle.

Below 50 percent duty, that multiplier is under one. At 30 percent duty it's 0.43, so 50 milliamps becomes 21, then 9, then 4. After five cycles it's below 1 percent. At 40 percent it's 0.67, so it takes about eleven cycles. At 45 percent it's 0.82 and takes over twenty. So the converter is stable, but the nearer to 50 percent, the more sluggishly it forgets disturbances, and if noise keeps arriving every cycle, the rms jitter on the inductor current is amplified: about 1.3 times at 40 percent, 1.7 times at 45, and more than 3 at 49.

Above 50 percent, the multiplier is greater than one. At 60 percent it's 1.5: the 50 milliamp error becomes 75, 112, 169 and so on, alternating sign. About seven cycles later it's an amp. At 70 percent, the multiplier is 2.3 and it takes about three and a half cycles. The pulse widths alternate: long, short, long, short. That continues until a nonlinear limit stops it, like the on-time reaching zero so a pulse is skipped, or hitting the maximum duty or the current limit, and the converter stays in a large period-two pattern at half the switching frequency.

Exactly 50 percent is marginal: the error never decays.

So below 50 percent, noise and load perturbations are absorbed. Above, they are amplified, and any noise event triggers oscillation. The standard fix is slope compensation, which changes the multiplier to minus m2 minus m a over m1 plus m a. With a ramp at least half the down-slope, it stays below one at any duty cycle, so I get the same benign behavior above and below 50 percent."
:::

::: terms
- **Per-cycle multiplier $\alpha$**: ratio of successive start-of-cycle current errors.
- **Alternating error**: sign flips each cycle because $\alpha<0$.
- **Noise gain**: $1/\sqrt{1-\alpha^2}$ for independent per-cycle disturbances.
- **Perturbation / disturbance**: noise, load step, line step or command change.
- **Pulse skipping**: on-time reaching zero because the current is already above the threshold.
- **Limit cycle**: bounded steady oscillation set by nonlinear limits.
- **Leading-edge blanking**: masks turn-on noise but sets minimum on-time.
- **Slope compensation**: ramp that reduces $|\alpha|$ ([[q0255]]).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "stable below 50%, unstable above".

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the per-cycle multiplier $-D/(1-D)$ and what it implies for sign, size and period.
- Tabulates decay and growth cycles for several duty cycles and gives the noise gain near 50%.
- Treats noise, load step and line step separately, noting that a line step can cross 50%.
- Describes the end state above 50% (skipped/maximum pulses, large $f_{sw}/2$ ripple).
- Adds that slope compensation changes the multiplier and what ramp is needed.
- Mentions blanking and comparator noise as realistic disturbance sources.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying below 50% nothing happens.** The error decays but the noise gain rises steeply near 50%.
- **Saying above 50% the converter "oscillates immediately".** It grows geometrically from the disturbance: a smaller disturbance takes more cycles.
- **Thinking load steps are different.** A load step enters as an initial condition on the same map.
- **Assuming the outer voltage loop will fix it.** The inner loop instability is at $f_{sw}/2$, above the voltage loop's bandwidth.
- **Ignoring that line changes move $D$.** A converter stable at 12 V in can cross 50% at 5.5 V in.
- **Neglecting the nonlinear end state.** Real oscillation amplitude is limited by clamps, not exponential.
:::

On the switch node this appears as pulses of alternating width.

## The noise amplification story below 50%

With independent disturbances each cycle, the stationary variance is $\sigma^2/(1-\alpha^2)$. That means:

Figure: Rms amplification of cycle-to-cycle noise in start-of-cycle current for uncompensated peak current mode. The gain is close to 1 at low duty, rises toward 2 at $D\approx0.45$ and is unbounded at 0.5. This is the reason the first signs of trouble, such as pulse-width jitter and an $f_{sw}/2$ spur, appear at duties of 40 to 49%, before true oscillation.

## Disturbance types

| Disturbance | Enters as | What happens for $D<0.5$ | $D>0.5$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comparator/sense noise spike | early or late turn-off | one-cycle current error, decays | grows |
| Load step | change of $V_c$ command, new target | inner loop settles in a few cycles | inner loop oscillates, outer loop can't help |
| Line step | change of $m_1$, $m_2$, $D$ | slope change, then decay | grows (and may cross 0.5) |
| Reference or command step | change of $V_c$ | like a load step | like a load step |

## Why the voltage loop can't rescue it

The outer loop's crossover is typically $f_{sw}/10$ or lower, while the instability is at $f_{sw}/2$ and grows within a few switching cycles. The voltage loop sees only the average of the alternating pattern. Only changing the inner loop (slope compensation, or lowering $D$) helps.

## Practical symptoms

- Switch-node pulse widths alternate, spectrum shows a line at $f_{sw}/2$.
- Output ripple has an $f_{sw}/2$ component; at low $f_{sw}$ it may be audible.
- Appears at low $V_{in}$ or high $V_{out}$ (large $D$), heavy load, or when the inductor is smaller than recommended.
- Intermittent: a quiet converter that starts oscillating after a load step or noise event is the signature of $D$ just above 0.5.

## Where the noise goes: a spectral peak at $f_{sw}/2$

The rms formula hides something useful. Write $a=|\alpha|$ so the map is $\delta_{n+1}=-a\,\delta_n+w_n$ with white per-cycle noise $w_n$. The power spectrum of the error is

$$S_\delta(f)=\frac{\sigma^2}{\left|1+a\,e^{-j2\pi f/f_{sw}}\right|^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{1+a^2+2a\cos(2\pi f/f_{sw})}.$$

At DC the denominator is $(1+a)^2$ and the noise is *suppressed*. At $f=f_{sw}/2$ the cosine is $-1$ and the denominator is $(1-a)^2$, so the amplitude gain there is $1/(1-a)$. That is a resonance at exactly half the switching frequency whose height depends only on the duty cycle:

| $D$ | $a=D/(1-D)$ | Amplitude gain at $f_{sw}/2$ | Amplitude gain at DC | rms gain (integrated) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | 0.43 | 1.75 | 0.70 | 1.11 |
| 0.40 | 0.67 | 3.0 | 0.60 | 1.34 |
| 0.45 | 0.82 | 5.5 | 0.55 | 1.74 |
| 0.49 | 0.96 | 25.5 | 0.51 | 3.6 |

The two gain columns tell the whole story. The converter is not noisier at every frequency as $D\to0.5$. It is *quieter* at DC and *much louder* in a narrow band at $f_{sw}/2$, and the integrated rms (last column) understates how a spectrum analyzer sees it: a 25x spur at $D=0.49$ while the total rms has grown only 3.6x. This is why an $f_{sw}/2$ line in the output or switch-node spectrum is the early warning, visible well before any alternating pulse widths can be seen on a scope.

## From milliamps to nanoseconds

The thing you actually see on the scope is pulse-width jitter, not current error. A current error $\delta$ at the start of a cycle shortens or lengthens the on-time by $\delta/m_1$. With $V_{in}=12$ V and $L=10$ µH, a 50 mA error at $D=0.4$ ($V_{out}=4.8$ V, $m_1=0.72$ A/µs) moves the turn-off edge by 69 ns. At $D=0.45$ it is 76 ns, and at $D=0.49$ it is 82 ns. Against a 2 µs on-time that is about 3.5% — small enough that you would not notice a single event, but a persistent alternation of 70 to 80 ns edge-to-edge is easy to catch on a scope in persistence mode. If the noise gain at $f_{sw}/2$ is 5.5x, expect the alternating component of the pulse width to be several hundred nanoseconds from nominal noise of tens of milliamps.

## How a load step plays through the inner loop

A load step appears as a jump in the control voltage $V_c$ and therefore a jump in the peak-current command. The first cycle after the jump starts with an error equal to the change in current target, then evolves with the same multiplier. The time for the inner loop alone to settle to within 5% is $n=\ln0.05/\ln a$ cycles. At 200 kHz (5 µs per cycle):

| $D$ | cycles to 5% | time |
|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | 3.5 | 18 µs |
| 0.40 | 7.4 | 37 µs |
| 0.45 | 14.9 | 75 µs |
| 0.49 | 75 | 374 µs |

This is the *inner-loop* memory. Because the error alternates in sign, the cycle-averaged inductor current error is much smaller than the valley error: over two cycles the errors $\delta$ and $-a\delta$ sum to $(1-a)\delta$, only 0.04 of $\delta$ at $D=0.49$. The output capacitor responds to the average, so the output voltage may look fine while the valleys visibly alternate. This explains a common observation: a converter near 50% duty can pass a load-step test on the output, yet show a high-frequency alternation on the inductor-current probe, which the output test would not catch.

## Line transients that cross 50%

A line step or a battery sag changes the duty cycle, and for a 3.3 V output the 50% boundary is at $V_{in}=6.6$ V. The error grows by $|\alpha|^n$ while the input is below that, with $n=f_{sw}\times t_{dwell}$. At 200 kHz and a 50 mA initial error:

| Dip | Dwell | $|\alpha|$ | Growth | Error after dwell |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| to 6.5 V | 1 ms (200 cycles) | 1.03 | 470x | about 23 A (saturates) |
| to 6.0 V | 100 µs (20 cycles) | 1.22 | 55x | 2.8 A (saturates) |
| to 6.0 V | 1 ms | 1.22 | $2.7\times10^{17}$ | saturates in a few dozen cycles |
| to 5.0 V | 100 µs | 1.94 | $5.8\times10^{5}$ | saturates in about 5 cycles |

The takeaway is that even a very shallow, brief excursion past the 50% line can ring up to the nonlinear limit, because the growth is exponential in the number of cycles, not in the amplitude of the excursion. An input filter that sags for a millisecond during a motor start or a hot-plug event is more than long enough. This is why designs verify the transient condition, not only the DC corners.

## Real hardware that changes the story

- **Blanking and propagation delay.** The comparator cannot act earlier than the blanking time, and its delay adds $m_1t_d$ of overshoot (a 50 ns delay at $m_1=0.72$ A/µs is 36 mA). A fixed overshoot is only an offset, but a delay that varies cycle to cycle acts like added noise $\sigma$.
- **Sense-signal quality.** Ringing from the switch-node edge couples into the sense node, and a noise amplitude of tens of millivolts at 0.1 V/A is hundreds of milliamps of apparent error. Good layout of the sense trace (Kelvin connection, RC filter with a time constant shorter than blanking) lowers $\sigma$ directly.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Numerical noise levels, blanking times and comparator delay are part-specific; the numbers here are illustrative and should be replaced with datasheet or measured values for a particular controller.

## Bench procedure

1. Set full load and the lowest input voltage in the specification, and capture the switch node with persistence on. Look for two pulse widths and for a spectral line at exactly $f_{sw}/2$.
2. Step the input voltage across the 50% boundary ($V_{in}=V_{out}/0.5$) with a programmable supply, with slew and dwell chosen from the table above, and watch the inductor current with a current probe (the output voltage can mask the problem).
3. Compare the spectrum at $f_{sw}/2$ at 0.5 V steps of $V_{in}$ approaching the boundary: the height should increase as $1/(1-a)$ from the table. A peak that grows much faster than predicted points to extra delay, noise coupling or a smaller-than-expected inductance.
4. Repeat with the minimum inductance and at temperature extremes, then apply the slope-compensation change you intend to ship and confirm the peak at $f_{sw}/2$ disappears.
:::

::: applications
- **Battery-powered converters** whose input falls toward the output as the cell discharges, crossing 50% duty.
- **Automotive bucks** during cold crank, where $V_{in}$ dips and duty cycle increases sharply.
- **Wide-input industrial supplies** designed across a range where $D$ spans well below to well above 0.5.
- **Validation sweeps**: stepping $V_{in}$ and load while watching for alternating pulse widths.
- **Noise debugging**: if a converter jitters mostly at duties around 40 to 50%, suspect high noise gain even before oscillation.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Trailing-edge peak current mode, fixed frequency, CCM,** no slope compensation unless stated.
- **Linear map** for small disturbances; large events are limited by nonlinear clamps.
- **Independent, identically distributed noise** for the rms formula.
- **Ideal slopes** (constant $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$ over the cycle); in real converters the output ripple and finite loop bandwidth modify them slightly.
- The same analysis applies to valley current mode with $D\to1-D$.
:::

::: followups
### What happens at exactly 50% duty?
$\alpha=-1$: the error never decays and never grows (marginal). Any noise changes the phase and amplitude randomly, so the converter shows persistent alternating-pulse jitter. In practice small nonidealities push it toward one side; with noise the closed-loop spectrum has a large $f_{sw}/2$ peak.

### Does the output capacitor filter out the alternating pattern?
Partly. The alternation is at $f_{sw}/2$, so the output filter attenuates it by a lot, but the inductor current and switch node carry it fully, so EMI is worse, the inductor and FETs see higher peak currents, and in large cases the output ripple has an $f_{sw}/2$ component.

### A converter works at 12 V in (D=0.275) and fails at 5 V in with 3.3 V out. Quantify.
At 5 V, $D=0.66$, $\alpha=-1.94$: a 50 mA error reaches 1 A in $\ln20/\ln1.94=4.5$ cycles. To fix: need $m_a>(m_2-m_1)/2$; with $L=4.7$ µH, $m_2=0.70$ A/µs and $m_1=(5-3.3)/4.7\text{ µH}=0.36$ A/µs, so $m_a>0.17$ A/µs at minimum, and $m_a\approx m_2$ for a damped design.

### How do you tell sense noise from a genuine subharmonic problem?
Subharmonic: alternating pulse widths in a regular period-2 pattern that appears systematically above a duty threshold and moves with $V_{in}$ and $L$. Sense noise: random jitter that doesn't have a clean period-2 spectral line, improves with blanking/filtering, and doesn't change with a duty threshold.

### Scenario: a converter is rock solid on the bench with a 12 V supply but shows an intermittent $f_{sw}/2$ spur in the field with a battery that sags to 6 V. What's your analysis?
At 6 V in and 3.3 V out, $D=0.55$, $\alpha=-1.22$, slightly unstable, which would show up intermittently when battery sag and transients meet. Verify on the bench at 6 V with a scope on the switch node; fix by increasing the ramp or inductance, or by choosing a controller with higher internal slope compensation. Test with margin: $D$ up to 0.8 at minimum $V_{in}$.
