::: testing
Whether you can **design** the floating gate drive, not just name "bootstrap". The interviewer expects you to state requirements, draw the circuit with every part justified, and size the parts with numbers. You are also showing that you know why it is needed at all. The hidden checks:

1. Requirement: $V_G\ge V_{SW}+V_{GS(on)}$, with $V_{SW}$ swinging to the rail.
2. A working topology: driver, bootstrap diode and capacitor, gate resistors, gate-source resistor, dead time.
3. Sizing: $C_{boot}$, diode rating and recovery, gate resistor from the desired edge rate, droop at long on-times.
4. The limits and the fix for them: max duty and start-up (precharge or charge pump), undershoot, dv/dt, layout.
:::

::: why
Almost every N-channel half-bridge ends up with a circuit like this one, usually inside a gate-driver IC, but the engineer still picks $C_{boot}$, the diode, $R_g$ and the layout, and still debugs the failures: a motor bridge that cuts out at 100% duty, a driver that latches at start-up, a high side that fails after a hard brake because the switch node undershoots. Being able to design it from scratch is the cleanest proof you understand it. The buck-converter version of this question is [[q0206]]; this page designs it for a motor bridge, where 100% duty matters.
:::

::: concepts
- **Gate drive referenced to the source**: $V_{GS}=V_G-V_{SW}$ ([[q0426]]).
- **Bootstrap supply**: $C_{boot}$ charged through $D_{boot}$ when SW is low, then floated up ([[q0206]]).
- **Gate resistor and edge rate**: $R_g$ sets turn-on current and dv/dt ([[q0431]], [[q0111]]).
- **Gate-source pull-down**: holds the FET off when the driver is unpowered ([[q0430]]).
- **Dead time and shoot-through**: a high side that turns on late or off late shorts the supply ([[q0428]]).
- **Charge pump**: a continuous supply above the rail for 100% duty ([[q0285]]).
:::

::: answer
"The requirement is that the gate has to be about ten volts above the source. The source is the switch node, which goes up to the motor supply. So I need a gate supply that moves with the switch node.

I'd use a half-bridge driver with a bootstrap. There's a diode from the 10 volt driver supply to a boot pin, and a capacitor between the boot pin and the switch node. When the low-side FET is on, the switch node is near ground and the capacitor charges to about 9.6 volts through the diode. When the high side turns on, the switch node rises to the supply, the capacitor holds its 9.6 volts, so the boot pin sits 9.6 volts above the switch node, and the driver's high-side output uses that to push the gate.

Sizing: say the gate charge is 40 nanocoulombs. To keep the droop under half a volt I need at least 80 nanofarads, so I'd pick about half a microfarad. The diode has to block the supply plus the driver voltage, so a 60 volt fast Schottky for a 24 volt bridge. I'd add a gate resistor of about 10 ohms with a small diode for fast turn-off, a 100 k resistor from gate to source so it stays off if the driver loses power, and dead time in the driver.

The limitation is that a bootstrap only refreshes when the low side is on, so for 100% duty I'd use a driver with an internal charge pump, or limit duty to about 98%."
:::

::: technical
**Requirements (example).** $V_M=24$ V, $I_{max}=10$ A, $f_{sw}=20$ kHz, NFET $Q_g=40$ nC at 10 V, $Q_{gd}=12$ nC, $V_{th}\approx3$ V, gate rating $\pm20$ V. Driver supply $V_{DRV}=10$ V.

**Gate voltage needed.** $V_G=V_M+V_{GS(on)}=24+10=34$ V. $V_{GS}$ never exceeds 10 V.

**Bootstrap charge.** $V_{boot}\approx V_{DRV}-V_f-V_{SW,low}=10-0.4+0.0\approx9.6$ V (a Schottky drop of 0.4 V, a low-side drop near $-I R_{DS(on)}$).

**Sizing $C_{boot}$.** Charge drawn per cycle: $Q_g+I_Q t_{on}$. With $\Delta V\le0.5$ V: $C\ge Q_g/\Delta V=80$ nF. Use $\ge10\times$: 470 nF X7R, 25 V (derates to about 60% at 10 V DC bias, so effective 280 nF still gives $\Delta V=40/280=0.14$ V).

**Diode.** $V_R\ge V_M+V_{DRV}=34$ V plus ringing, so 60 V; fast recovery or Schottky; average current $Q_gf_{sw}=40\text{ nC}\times20\text{ kHz}=0.8$ mA; peak charging current limited by a series resistor $R_{boot}$ of 2 to 5 Ω.

**Gate resistor.** Turn-on current during the Miller plateau ($V_{plateau}\approx5$ V): $I_g=\dfrac{V_{DRV}-V_{plat}}{R_g+R_{drv}}=\dfrac{10-5}{10+2}=0.42$ A, so $t_{r}\approx Q_{gd}/I_g=12\text{ nC}/0.42\text{ A}=29$ ns and $dv/dt\approx24\text{ V}/29\text{ ns}=0.83$ V/ns. Turn-off through a parallel diode with $R\approx2$ Ω is faster, which gives dead-time margin and Miller immunity.

**Gate-source resistor.** 100 kΩ: holds $V_{GS}=0$ when the driver is off or the bootstrap is empty; its current ($10/100\text{ k}=0.1$ mA) is negligible.

**Droop at long on-time.** $\Delta V=I_Qt/C$; with $I_Q=100$ µA and $C=470$ nF: 0.21 V/ms. Gate stays well above threshold for about 20 ms; beyond that use a charge pump.

**Max duty.** Bootstrap refresh needs $t_{off,min}\approx1$ µs: $D_{max}=1-1\ \mu\text{s}\times20\text{ kHz}=98\%$.

**Dead time.** 100 to 200 ns, longer than the driver's propagation mismatch plus the FET turn-off delay.
:::

::: short
"A high-side NFET needs its gate about 10 V above its source, and the source is the switch node, so I need a gate supply that floats with it. I'd use a half-bridge driver with a bootstrap: a fast diode from the 10 V supply to a capacitor that sits between the boot pin and the switch node. It charges when the low side is on and lifts the gate drive when the high side turns on. Size it as gate charge over allowed droop, about 0.5 µF for 40 nC, add a gate resistor and a gate-source pull-down, and use a charge pump if I need 100% duty."
:::

::: long
"Start with the requirement. For an N-channel FET to be on, the gate needs to be roughly ten volts above the source. In a high-side position the source is the switch node, which goes up to the motor supply, say 24 volts. So when the FET is on the gate has to be at about 34 volts, which I don't have anywhere. I need a gate supply that rides on the switch node.

The standard way is a bootstrap. I use a half-bridge driver: its high-side section runs between a boot pin and the switch node. A fast diode connects my 10 volt driver supply to the boot pin, and a capacitor connects the boot pin to the switch node. While the low-side FET is on, the switch node is at about zero, so the capacitor charges through the diode to about 9.6 volts. When the high-side FET turns on, the switch node jumps to 24 volts, the capacitor holds its 9.6 volts, so the boot pin goes to about 33.6, and the diode is reverse-biased so the charge can't leak back. The driver now has a floating 9.6 volt supply to push the gate against.

Sizing. A 40 nanocoulomb gate needs a capacitor of at least charge over allowed droop, 40 nano over half a volt, so 80 nanofarads. Ceramics lose capacitance with DC bias, so I'd go to about 470 nanofarads. The diode needs to block 24 plus 10 volts with margin, so a 60 volt Schottky or fast-recovery diode, and a few ohms in series limits the charging spike. A gate resistor around 10 ohms sets the edge rate, I'd put a diode across it so turn-off is fast, and a 100 k from gate to source so the FET stays off if the driver is unpowered.

Then the limits. The capacitor only gets refreshed when the low side is on, so I have a max duty of around 98 percent at 20 kilohertz with a 1 microsecond minimum off-time. If the motor needs 100 percent duty, the capacitor droops over milliseconds, so I need a driver with a charge pump. And at start-up the capacitor is empty, so the first thing the controller does is turn on the low side briefly. I'd also watch the switch node undershoot and keep the loop between the capacitor, driver and FET very small."
:::

::: terms
- **Bootstrap capacitor ($C_{boot}$)**: floating storage capacitor between the boot pin and SW that supplies the high-side gate drive.
- **Bootstrap diode ($D_{boot}$)**: charges $C_{boot}$ and blocks when SW rises.
- **Boot pin (VB, BST)**: the high-side supply pin of the driver; sits at $V_{SW}+V_{boot}$.
- **Switch node (SW, VS)**: the junction of high-side source and low-side drain.
- **Gate resistor ($R_g$)**: sets turn-on current and therefore edge rate.
- **UVLO**: under-voltage lockout; keeps the FET off until the drive supply is high enough.
- **Charge pump**: generates a supply above $V_M$; allows 100% duty.
- **Minimum off-time**: the interval the low side must be on to recharge $C_{boot}$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use a bootstrap capacitor and diode".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives the requirement ($V_G=V_M+V_{GS}=34$ V) and shows why a ground-referenced supply fails.
- Draws the full circuit with the gate resistor, off-diode, gate-source resistor, and low-side drive.
- Sizes $C_{boot}$, diode, $R_g$ with numbers and mentions DC-bias derating.
- Identifies the max-duty and start-up limits, and the 100% duty fix.
- Calls out SW undershoot, dv/dt and layout (tight bootstrap loop).
- Explains the alternatives (charge pump, isolated supply, PFET) and when each wins.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Powering the high-side driver from the ground-referenced 10 V rail.** The gate would only reach 10 V while the source is at 24 V.
- **Choosing $C_{boot}$ equal to the FET gate capacitance.** It needs to be 10 to 20 times larger so that the transferred charge does not droop the supply.
- **Using a slow rectifier as the bootstrap diode.** Reverse recovery dumps charge back and spikes; use a fast Schottky or fast-recovery part.
- **No gate-source resistor.** At power-up or if the driver browns out, a floating gate can leak on.
- **Forgetting 100% duty or start-up.** A bootstrapped high side cannot stay on indefinitely and is dead until the low side has run.
:::

::: deep
## Define the problem

A 24 V motor bridge, 10 A, 20 kHz PWM. High-side NFET: $Q_g=40$ nC at 10 V, $Q_{gd}=12$ nC, $V_{th}\approx3$ V, $R_{DS(on)}=10$ mΩ, gate rating $\pm20$ V. We have a 10 V gate-driver supply referenced to ground.

**Why drive the high side differently from the low side.** The low-side source is ground: a 10 V driver gives $V_{GS}=10$ V. The high-side source rises to 24 V when the FET is on. With the same ground-referenced 10 V drive, $V_{GS}=10-24=-14$ V while the FET should be on: it stays off, and then the body diode clamps the node. The gate must reach 34 V, and since the gate supply is only 10 V, the supply must float with SW.

When $Q_H$ turns on, SW and the bottom plate of $C_{boot}$ rise to $V_M$, VB goes to about $V_M+9.6$ V and $D_{boot}$ is reverse-biased. $R_g$ sets the turn-on edge and $R_{gs}$ keeps $Q_H$ off if the driver is unpowered. The turn-off path: add a Schottky diode in antiparallel with $R_g$ (anode at the gate, cathode at HO) so the gate discharges through a few ohms while turn-on is still limited by $R_g$ — asymmetric drive that gives dead-time margin and Miller immunity ([[q0428]]).

## Sizing, with numbers

```sim

```
Figure: Try $C_{boot}=47$ nF and then 470 nF, and move the duty cycle toward 1: the high-side $V_{GS}$ droop during on-time and the maximum duty limit appear.

1. **$C_{boot}$**: $C\ge Q_g/\Delta V=40\text{ nC}/0.5\text{ V}=80$ nF. Add the quiescent term $I_{Q}t_{on}$ (100 µA × 50 µs = 5 nC, negligible). A ceramic at 25 V rating in 0603 may retain only 50 to 60% of its capacitance at 10 V bias, so use 470 nF to get about 250 nF effective, droop $\approx40/250=0.16$ V.
2. **$D_{boot}$**: blocking voltage $V_M+V_{DRV}=34$ V plus ringing margin, so 60 V. Low forward drop (Schottky, 0.3 to 0.5 V) and low junction capacitance. Average current $Q_gf_{sw}=0.8$ mA. With a 3 Ω series resistor, initial charging current $(10-0.4)/3=3.2$ A pulse for microseconds: choose a diode with adequate surge rating.
3. **$R_g$**: pick edge rate. With 10 Ω plus driver $R\approx2$ Ω: plateau current 0.42 A, $t_r=Q_{gd}/I_g=29$ ns, $dv/dt\approx0.83$ V/ns. A faster edge is lower switching loss but more ringing and EMI. Switching loss estimate: $\tfrac12V_MI(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}=0.5\times24\times10\times58\text{ ns}\times20\text{ kHz}=0.14$ W per FET.
4. **$R_{gs}$**: 100 kΩ to 10 kΩ.
5. **Dead time**: in the driver, 100 to 200 ns.

Figure: At 100 µA of drain the gate drive falls by 0.21 V/ms. After about 22 ms it is at 5 V and the FET's on-resistance rises, and the FET is lost at about 31 ms. A motor held at 100% duty for seconds needs a charge pump.

| Limit | Numbers | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum duty | $D_{max}=1-t_{off,min}f_{sw}=1-1\ \mu\text{s}\times20\text{ kHz}=98\%$ | Charge pump driver for 100% duty |
| Start-up | $C_{boot}$ is empty; the high side can't turn on | Turn on the low side first (a precharge pulse of at least $C\Delta V/I$) |
| SW undershoot | SW at $-5$ V charges $C_{boot}$ to about $V_{DRV}+5-V_f=14.6$ V: fine for a $\pm20$ V gate, not for a lower-rated or GaN gate | Driver with negative-$V_{SW}$ rating; small $R_{boot}$; tight layout |
| dv/dt | 0.83 V/ns here; GaN or fast SiC edges 50 V/ns or more | High-CMTI driver, slower $R_g$ |
| Pre-charge time | $t=C\Delta V/I$: $470\text{ nF}\times9.6\text{ V}/(3.2\text{ A})\approx1.4$ µs per cycle at the start | Fine for most drivers |

**Charge-pump option.** A small oscillator drives a capacitor and diode stack from $V_{DRV}$ and $V_M$ to produce $V_M+V_{DRV}$ continuously. Motor-driver ICs integrate it for 100% duty and hold-on at standstill; you add a pump capacitor, usually 10 to 100 nF.

**Isolated supply.** For high-voltage or high-power bridges, a tiny isolated DC-DC per high-side gives a fully floating supply and isolated gate-drive signal ([[q0433]]); no duty-cycle limit, at higher cost.

## Layout checklist

- The loop $C_{boot}\to$ driver $\to$ gate $\to$ source $\to C_{boot}$ must be as small as possible; place $C_{boot}$ and $D_{boot}$ right at the driver.
- Keep SW a short, wide copper pour; avoid long traces to the driver's VS pin.
- Use a Kelvin connection from VS to the FET source if the driver allows.
- Add a small resistor or ferrite in the VB path if SW undershoots.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> When asked to "design" this, state the requirement first ("gate must be 10 V above the source, which moves 0 to 24 V"), draw the bootstrap, size $C_{boot}$ from $Q_g$ and droop, then say what breaks it (100% duty, start-up, undershoot). That ordering is the answer interviewers are listening for.

::: applications
- **Brushed and BLDC motor drivers** with integrated charge pump for 100% duty.
- **Half-bridge gate-driver ICs** (bootstrap diode on chip, external capacitor).
- **Synchronous buck converters** with a bootstrap between BST and SW.
- **Class-D amplifiers** with bootstrapped high-side FETs.
- **Server power stages and DrMOS modules** where the driver, FETs and bootstrap diode are integrated.
- **High-side load switches** using a charge pump to turn on an NFET above the rail.
:::

::: assumptions
- Single-supply motor bridge, $V_M=24$ V, 10 V gate drive from a ground-referenced rail.
- Silicon NFET with $\pm20$ V gate rating; GaN gate rating (about $\pm6$ V) needs a regulated or clamped bootstrap.
- The low-side FET conducts (or its body diode does) for at least $t_{off,min}$ every cycle to recharge the cap.
- Leakage plus driver quiescent current of 100 µA is a typical figure for the droop example; check the driver datasheet.
- Ceramic capacitor derating of 40 to 50% at DC bias is typical for X7R in small packages.

If the driver is integrated (most are), these calculations become the choice of external $C_{boot}$, $R_g$, and the bootstrap diode if not integrated. The first thing to read in the driver datasheet is the maximum duty cycle, the negative-$V_{SW}$ rating and the minimum $C_{boot}$ recommendation.
:::

::: followups
### Why can't the bootstrap hold the FET on at 100% duty?
Because the capacitor is only recharged when SW is low. During a long on-time, the driver's quiescent current and leakage discharge it: $\Delta V=I_Qt/C$. At 100 µA and 470 nF that is 0.21 V/ms, so after tens of milliseconds the gate drive collapses. A charge pump removes this by continuously regenerating the supply.

### What happens at start-up if the motor is already spinning and the low side hasn't run?
$C_{boot}$ is empty and $V_{SW}$ may be at an arbitrary voltage because of back-EMF through the high-side body diode. The high side cannot turn on until the cap is precharged. Drivers include UVLO that keeps the high side off until $V_{boot}$ is high enough, and the controller runs a short low-side pulse first (or a precharge routine) to charge it.

### How would you protect the gate if SW undershoots by 8 V?
The bootstrap cap charges to $V_{DRV}+8-V_f=17.6$ V, close to the 20 V rating. Put a 2 to 5 Ω resistor in series with $D_{boot}$, add a 12 to 15 V Zener across the cap or driver supply pins, reduce loop inductance (layout), slow the turn-off edge with a larger low-side $R_g$, and pick a driver rated for negative $V_{SW}$.

### What if you replace the NFET with a PFET to avoid all this?
You would need a level shifter instead of a bootstrap, and a larger, more resistive part (2 to 3 times the area for the same $R_{DS(on)}$). It works for low-voltage and low-current bridges and 100% duty; see [[q0438]]. At 24 V and 10 A the NFET with a charge-pump driver is the better engineering choice.

### Why do you put a diode across the gate resistor?
To make turn-off faster than turn-on. A slow turn-on ($R_g$ about 10 Ω) limits dv/dt and EMI, while a fast turn-off (about 2 Ω) reduces the dead time needed and holds the gate more firmly against Miller-induced turn-on caused by the other FET's dv/dt.
:::

::: interpretations
"Why it is needed" asks for the reason a special circuit exists: the high-side NFET's source moves, so a ground-referenced gate supply cannot give a positive $V_{GS}$. This page designs it for a **motor bridge** (24 V, 10 A, 20 kHz, 100% duty considerations); [[q0206]] designs the same circuit for a **buck converter**, where the low side conducts each cycle and a minimum off-time is acceptable. If the interviewer wants a no-IC answer, the same bootstrap can be built from a discrete level shifter, but the driver IC is the realistic choice.
