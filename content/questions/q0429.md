::: testing
Whether you can turn a vague "drive a solenoid from a microcontroller" into a complete, protected circuit with numbers. The interviewer wants to see the design process, not just a transistor symbol:

1. Requirements first: load voltage and current, $L$ and $R$, switching speed, MCU logic level and pin current limit.
2. Switch choice with a reason: a logic-level low-side NFET whose $R_{DS(on)}$ is specified at the MCU's gate voltage.
3. The four supporting parts: flyback diode, gate series resistor, gate pull-down, and decoupling.
4. What can go wrong: floating gate at reset, inductive spike, gate ringing, ground bounce, isolation needs ([[q0432]]).
:::

::: why
Every embedded product that moves, clicks or heats something uses this circuit: relays, solenoids, valves, small motors, fans, buzzers. A 3.3 V GPIO can source only a few to a few tens of milliamps, an inductive load wants hundreds of milliamps at a higher voltage, and the load produces a voltage spike every time it turns off. Getting the four little parts right is the difference between a prototype that works on the bench and a product that survives the field, which is why the next questions ([[q0430]], [[q0431]], [[q0432]]) drill into them one at a time.
:::

::: concepts
- **Logic-level MOSFET**: fully enhanced at 2.5 to 4.5 V; read $R_{DS(on)}$ at your $V_{GS}$ ([[q0096]], [[q0099]]).
- **Inductive kick and flyback diode**: current in $L$ cannot stop instantly ([[q0144]], [[q0043]]).
- **GPIO limits**: source/sink current, output resistance, and high-Z at reset ([[q0020]]).
- **Gate pull-down**: defines the gate when the pin floats ([[q0430]]).
- **Gate series resistor**: limits GPIO current and damps ringing ([[q0431]]).
- **Low-side switching**: the load connects to the supply and the FET to ground, so the gate is referenced to ground ([[q0107]]).
:::

::: answer
"I'd start with the numbers. Say it's a 12 volt solenoid that draws half an amp and has about 50 millihenries, driven from a 3.3 volt GPIO. The simplest robust circuit is a low-side logic-level N-channel MOSFET: the load goes from 12 volts to the drain, the source goes to ground, and the GPIO drives the gate.

I choose a MOSFET with a drain rating of at least 30 volts and an on-resistance that is specified at 2.5 or 3.3 volts of gate drive, so that at half an amp it dissipates milliwatts. Then four little parts. A flyback diode across the load, cathode to 12 volts, because when I switch off, the inductor current has to go somewhere and without the diode the drain would spike to hundreds of volts and kill the FET. A 100 k pull-down from gate to ground so the FET is off when the MCU is in reset and its pins are floating. A series gate resistor of a couple hundred ohms to limit the GPIO current and damp ringing with the wiring inductance. And a bulk capacitor on the supply.

If the load can't tolerate a slow decay through the diode, or I need fast turn-off, I add a Zener in series with the diode. If the load is on a separate, noisy or high voltage supply, I'd add isolation, like an optocoupler or a digital isolator, between the MCU and the gate."
:::

::: technical
**Requirements (example).** $V_S=12$ V, coil $R=24\ \Omega$ ($I=0.5$ A), $L=50$ mH, switching at up to 1 kHz (on/off) or 20 kHz PWM, MCU $V_{OH}=3.3$ V, pin limit about 10 to 20 mA.

**Switch.** Low-side NFET with: $V_{DS,max}\ge2V_S$ (30 V); $V_{GS(th),max}\le1.5$ V; $R_{DS(on)}$ specified at $V_{GS}=2.5$ V or 3.3 V; $I_D\ge2I$. Conduction loss: $I^2R_{DS(on)}=0.25\times0.05=12.5$ mW for 50 mΩ.

**Flyback diode.** Across the load, cathode to $V_S$. Rating $\ge I$ and $V_R\ge V_S$. With the diode alone the current decays as $i=I_0e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau=L/R=2.08$ ms; time to 10%: $\tau\ln10=4.8$ ms. The drain peaks at $V_S+V_F=12.5$ V. Energy stored: $\tfrac12LI^2=6.25$ mJ, dissipated in the coil resistance and diode.

**Fast turn-off option (Zener or TVS in series with the diode).** The drain clamps at $V_S+V_Z+V_F$; with $V_Z=18$ V, the drain sees 30.7 V (so a 40 V FET) and the current falls in about $LI_0/(V_Z+V_F)=1.34$ ms (an upper bound that ignores the coil resistance; about 1.0 ms with it). Faster but higher $V_{DS}$ and more energy in the clamp.

**Gate resistor.** $R_g\ge V_{OH}/I_{pin}-R_{out}$; for 3.3 V, 20 mA limit, 25 Ω output: 140 Ω. Choose 220 Ω: peak 13.5 mA. With $C_{iss}=400$ pF: $\tau=(220+25)\times400\text{ pF}=98$ ns, so $t_r\approx2.2\tau=215$ ns; fine up to tens of kHz.

**Gate pull-down.** $R_{pd}=10$ to 100 kΩ; static current $3.3/100\text{ k}=33$ µA when on.

**Supply.** Bulk capacitor (47 to 220 µF) plus 100 nF near the load; TVS on the supply if it is exposed (vehicle, long cable).

**Alternatives.** Logic-level NFET (best), NPN Darlington or BJT with base resistor (needs $I_B\approx I_C/10$, so a larger GPIO current), PFET high side with level shifter ([[q0438]]), load-switch or driver ICs with built-in flyback clamp.
:::

::: long
"I'd start by pinning down the load: the supply voltage, the current, the inductance and how fast I need to switch it. Let's say a 12 volt solenoid at half an amp, about 50 millihenries, driven from a 3.3 volt microcontroller pin, which can supply maybe 10 to 20 milliamps.

The pin can't drive the load directly, so I need a switch. The simplest is a low-side N-channel MOSFET. The load connects from the supply to the drain and the source goes to ground, so the gate sees a ground-referenced signal that the GPIO can drive. I need a logic-level part: a low enough threshold and an on-resistance that's actually specified at 2.5 or 3.3 volts of gate drive. A 30 volt part with 50 milliohms gives half an amp times half an amp times 50 milliohms, about 12 milliwatts, so no heat sink.

Then the protection and interface parts. First, the flyback diode across the load, cathode to the supply. When the FET turns off the inductor insists on keeping its current, so the diode gives it a path. Without it the drain would shoot up until the FET avalanches. With it the drain goes to 12.5 volts and the current decays over a few milliseconds. If I need faster release, I add a Zener in series with the diode and accept a higher drain voltage.

Second, a pull-down resistor, 10 to 100 k, from the gate to ground, so the FET is off when the microcontroller is in reset or the pin is floating. Third, a series gate resistor around 100 to 220 ohms to limit the pin's current into the gate capacitance and to damp ringing between the wire inductance and the gate. Fourth, a bulk capacitor and a small ceramic on the supply near the load.

And for the system: if the load is on a separate or noisy supply, or high voltage, I'd put isolation, like an optocoupler or digital isolator, between the microcontroller and the gate driver, and for fast PWM I'd use a proper gate driver instead of the GPIO."
:::

::: terms
- **Low-side switch**: a switch between the load and ground.
- **Logic-level MOSFET**: a MOSFET with $R_{DS(on)}$ specified at low gate voltage (2.5 to 4.5 V).
- **Flyback (freewheel) diode**: diode across an inductive load that conducts when the switch opens.
- **Gate pull-down**: resistor from gate to source that defines the off state.
- **Gate series resistor**: resistor between the driver and gate that limits current and damps ringing.
- **Inductive kick**: the voltage $L\,di/dt$ produced when the current is interrupted.
- **Clamp (Zener/TVS)**: a voltage limiter to speed up release or protect the FET.
- **GPIO**: general-purpose input/output pin of a microcontroller.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use a MOSFET and a flyback diode".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts from the load numbers and the MCU's limits and builds up from them.
- Picks the FET by $V_{DS}$, $V_{th}$ and $R_{DS(on)}$ **at the available gate voltage**, then computes dissipation.
- Includes all the little parts and states what each does: diode, pull-down, gate resistor, decoupling.
- Quantifies the flyback: peak drain voltage, decay time, and the trade-off with a Zener clamp.
- Raises reset-state behaviour, ground bounce and isolation, and says when a driver IC replaces the GPIO.
- Sketches the verification: scope the drain at turn-off, the gate at turn-on, the supply droop.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using a standard-level FET (10 V gate) with a 3.3 V GPIO.** It may only be partially on, with high $R_{DS(on)}$ and heat. Check the datasheet curve at 3.3 V.
- **Forgetting the flyback diode** or installing it backwards. Cathode goes to the positive supply side of the load.
- **No gate pull-down.** At reset the pin floats and the FET can partly turn on, firing the load ([[q0430]]).
- **Driving the gate directly with no series resistor.** Peak pin current can exceed ratings, and the pin ringing with trace inductance can overshoot ([[q0431]]).
- **Sharing the MCU ground with a noisy motor return.** Load current in the ground path lifts the MCU's ground; use a star or separate returns ([[q0432]]).
:::

## Step 1: write down the requirements

| Item | Example value | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Supply voltage $V_S$ | 12 V | Sets FET $V_{DS}$ rating and diode rating |
| Load current $I$ | 0.5 A (24 Ω coil) | FET current rating, dissipation |
| Load inductance $L$ | 50 mH | Flyback energy, decay time |
| Switching | on/off at up to 1 kHz, or PWM at 20 kHz | Gate-drive speed, switching loss |
| MCU logic | 3.3 V, 10 to 20 mA pin | Gate-drive voltage and current |
| Ground and supply | shared or separate? | Isolation or star grounding |

## Step 2: choose the switch

A **low-side N-channel MOSFET** is the standard answer: the gate is referenced to ground, so the GPIO can drive it, and NFETs have the best $R_{DS(on)}$ ([[q0426]]). Choose by:

- $V_{DS(max)}\ge2\times V_S$ (24 V, so a 30 V part), or higher if you use a clamp (see below).
- $V_{GS(th)}$ low (max below about 1.5 V), and **$R_{DS(on)}$ specified at the gate voltage you will have** (2.5 V or 3.3 V): a "10 V" part with 20 mΩ at $V_{GS}=10$ V may have 10× the resistance at 3.3 V, or not turn on at all.
- $I_D\ge2I$ for margin.
- Small $C_{iss}$ and $Q_g$ so a GPIO can drive it.

Dissipation: $I^2R_{DS(on)}=0.25\times0.05=12.5$ mW, so a SOT-23 part is enough for DC. For PWM at 20 kHz with $Q_g=5$ nC the gate-drive power is $5\text{ nC}\times3.3\text{ V}\times20\text{ kHz}=0.33$ mW, negligible.

## Step 3: handle the inductive kick

When $Q_1$ turns off, the load current $I_0=0.5$ A is still flowing. Without a path the inductor forces $v=L\,di/dt$ across the FET; the drain voltage climbs until the device avalanches or parasitic capacitance limits it ([[q0144]], [[q0043]]). Add the diode:

- **Diode only.** The drain is clamped at $V_S+V_F=12.5$ V. The current decays through the coil and diode: $i(t)=I_0e^{-t/\tau}$, $\tau=L/R=50\text{ mH}/24\ \Omega=2.08$ ms; to 10% takes $4.8$ ms (about 4 ms if you include the 0.7 V diode drop, which also ends the current at about 6 ms instead of an endless tail). Energy $\tfrac12LI_0^2=6.25$ mJ goes into the coil resistance and diode. Simple, slow release (a relay or solenoid releases late).
- **Diode plus Zener.** The clamp voltage is $V_Z+V_F$, so the drain reaches $V_S+V_Z+V_F$; for $V_Z=18$ V: 30.7 V (a 40 V FET). Ignoring the coil resistance, the current ramps down linearly in $LI_0/(V_Z+V_F)=0.05\times0.5/18.7=1.34$ ms; the coil's own 24 Ω helps, so the exact figure is $\tau\ln(1+RI_0/(V_Z+V_F))\approx1.0$ ms. Faster release, more stress, more energy in the Zener (about 6 mJ per event).
- **TVS across the FET.** Similar, bidirectional or unidirectional parts available for fixed clamp voltage.

## Step 4: drive the gate properly

**Series resistor.** The GPIO has output resistance (about 25 Ω) and a pin-current limit; the FET gate is a capacitor. At the instant of turn-on the current is $V_{OH}/(R_{out}+R_g)$: $3.3/(25+220)=13.5$ mA. The time constant with $C_{iss}=400$ pF is $245\ \Omega\times400\text{ pF}=98$ ns, so the gate settles in about 0.2 µs. The resistor also damps the series-$LC$ formed by the trace or bond-wire inductance and $C_{iss}$ ([[q0431]]).

**Pull-down.** 100 kΩ holds the gate low. If the MCU pins float at reset the FET can leak on; with $R_{pd}$ the gate discharges and the FET stays off ([[q0430]]).

**Gate voltage rating.** A 3.3 V drive is far below the $\pm20$ V gate rating, but a 5 V MCU with a long wire can ring above; add a 12 V Zener or a small TVS from gate to source in a harsh environment.

## Step 5: supply and layout

- **Bulk and decoupling**: 47 to 220 µF electrolytic or polymer plus 100 nF ceramic near the load and FET. The switch current steps from 0 to 0.5 A and the coil's turn-off puts current back.
- **Ground**: return the load current to the supply, not through the MCU's analog ground. A shared ground wire of 10 nH carrying 0.5 A stepping at 1 A/µs gives $L\,di/dt=10$ mV of bounce, harmless here, but at 10 A and 100 A/µs it is 1 V ([[q0432]]).
- **Diode placement**: right at the load terminals, to keep the flyback loop small.
- **Cables**: a long cable to a remote solenoid adds inductance and picks up noise; put the diode at the solenoid end or use a TVS at the supply.

## What if the requirements change?

| Change | What I'd do |
|---|---|
| Load needs 24 V or higher and the MCU is 3.3 V | Same circuit; check $V_{DS}$ rating and diode $V_R$. Add a TVS at the supply |
| Load current grows to 5 A | Larger FET, check $I^2R$ and the gate-charge power; a gate-driver IC for PWM |
| Fast PWM (100 kHz) | Gate driver to charge $Q_g$ quickly; GPIO current limits $dv/dt$ |
| High-side load (the load must be grounded) | PFET or NFET with bootstrap/charge pump ([[q0426]], [[q0438]]) |
| MCU and load on separate or hazardous supplies | Optocoupler or digital isolator ([[q0432]], [[q0435]]) |
| Motor in both directions | H-bridge ([[q0420]]) with driver |
| Cheap and low current (50 mA) | NPN or small FET (2N7002 class) |

## Verification

Check on the bench, not just in your head: scope the drain at turn-off to see the clamp voltage and the decay; scope $V_{GS}$ at turn-on for ringing; measure the FET temperature at steady state; power-cycle the MCU and hold it in reset to confirm the load does not fire; and test brown-out (supply sagging slowly) for gate-threshold behaviour.

::: applications
- **Relay coils**, **solenoid valves** and **door latches** driven from a microcontroller.
- **Small brushed DC motors** and **fans** (one direction, PWM speed control).
- **Buzzers and haptic motors** with a flyback diode.
- **Stepper-motor coil** drivers (one switch per coil end in unipolar drivers).
- **Heating elements** and **LED strips** on a low-side FET (resistive, no diode needed).
- **Valve drivers in appliances and automotive modules**, with a TVS for load dump.
:::

::: assumptions
- 3.3 V logic and a low-side NFET; resistive pull-down and no external gate driver IC.
- Switching up to a few kHz; at higher PWM frequencies the GPIO current limits the edge rate and a gate driver is warranted.
- Pin current limit of 10 to 20 mA and output resistance of 25 Ω are typical but device-specific; check the MCU datasheet.
- The load is inductive and not exposed to fast external transients; automotive or industrial supplies need a TVS and filtering.
- The FET gate leakage and the pull-down are small compared with the GPIO's drive.

If the load is purely resistive (a heater), omit the diode but keep the pull-down and the gate resistor. If the load is a motor with a mechanical load that back-drives, add the freewheel path and consider regeneration ([[q0424]]).
:::

::: followups
### Why does the diode go cathode-to-supply?
At turn-off the load current flows in the same direction as before (from the supply, through the load, toward the FET). The inductor pushes the drain node *up* above the supply. A diode with its cathode at the supply conducts when the drain exceeds the supply voltage, returning the current to the supply side through the coil. Reverse it and the diode conducts while the load is on, shorting the supply.

### What if my FET does not turn on with 3.3 V?
Check the $R_{DS(on)}$ versus $V_{GS}$ curve in the datasheet; a standard-gate part may need 10 V. Use a logic-level FET (specified at 2.5 or 3.3 V), add a gate-driver IC, or boost the gate drive voltage with a small level shifter (an NPN or a driver on a 5 or 12 V supply).

### How do you handle a 100 kHz PWM?
The GPIO's output resistance and current limit make the edges slow: with 245 Ω and 1 nF it is about 540 ns of rise time, a large fraction of a 10 µs period. Use a gate driver IC with 1 to 4 A peak current, and drop $R_g$ to a few ohms with a pulled-down gate and good layout.

### The load fires briefly at power-up. Why?
At reset the GPIO is high impedance; with no pull-down the gate floats and may be pulled up by leakage or noise, or the MCU outputs a default state for a few milliseconds. A 10 to 100 kΩ pull-down, a power-on-reset-safe driver, or a gate driver with an enable input prevents it ([[q0430]]).

### What if the supply is 48 V instead of 12 V?
The FET and diode ratings go up ($V_{DS}\ge100$ V for 48 V with margin and a clamp), $V_{GS}$ is unchanged, the flyback voltage rating of the diode must exceed 48 V, and the energy and spike are larger. Add a TVS at the supply for hot-plug and load-dump events, and consider isolation.
:::

::: interpretations
"An inductive load" here means a solenoid, relay coil or small motor. The question sits under *Control from Embedded Device*, so the emphasis is on the interface: logic-level MOSFET, protection and the gate network. A purely **digital** way to read it is to ask how to turn a GPIO into a PWM-controlled load; the same circuit applies, with the gate resistor and the FET's switching speed becoming more important. The 'design' framing means the answer should include requirements, component choices and numbers, not only the schematic. Isolation ([[q0432]]) and the PFET high-side alternative ([[q0437]], [[q0438]]) are treated as separate questions.
