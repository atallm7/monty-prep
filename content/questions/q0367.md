::: testing
Whether you know what a "pole" actually is (a magnet pole on the rotor, not a terminal) and can connect pole count to **electrical frequency, torque density, speed, cogging and control**. The interviewer is checking:

1. Correct definitions: poles vs pole pairs; electrical vs mechanical angle; and the difference between poles and slots.
2. The relationship $f_e = p\cdot\text{rpm}/60$ and what it implies for the controller.
3. Real trade-offs: more poles gives more torque density and finer position steps but a higher electrical frequency, higher iron and switching loss, and a harder control job.
4. That you can state this for BLDC and, ideally, note how stepper motors (many poles) follow the same logic.
:::

::: why
Pole count shows up directly in the datasheet and in the controller configuration: you have to enter the number of pole pairs to convert electrical speed to rpm. Choosing a 2-pole versus 14-pole motor changes how fast the inverter must commutate, how much torque you get for the same size, and how smooth the motor feels at low speed. Gimbal, drone and robot-joint motors are all high pole count for a reason, while high-speed spindles are low pole count for another. Interviewers use this as a quick test of whether you have actually configured a motor controller.
:::

::: concepts
- **Poles and pole pairs**: a pole is one magnet face of fixed polarity on the rotor; they come in N-S pairs. $p$ = pairs. See [[q0363]].
- **Electrical vs mechanical angle**: $\theta_e = p\theta_m$; the electrical cycle repeats every $360^\circ/p$ of shaft rotation.
- **Torque and back-EMF constants**: $K_t$ and $K_e$ determine speed and torque for a given voltage and current. See [[q0352]], [[q0353]].
- **Slots and cogging**: the stator has teeth (slots); the slot/pole combination sets cogging torque.
- **Iron loss and switching loss**: both scale with electrical frequency.
- **Stepper motors**: the same idea taken to the extreme with 50 rotor teeth. See [[q0377]].
:::

::: answer
"The poles are the magnetic poles on the rotor: the permanent magnets arranged north, south, north, south around the rim. Magnets come in pairs, so a 14-pole motor has seven pole pairs. This matters because the electrical cycle is tied to pole pairs: one full mechanical rotation produces $p$ electrical cycles, so electrical frequency is $p$ times rpm over 60.

How it affects performance: more poles means the stator field only has to rotate a fraction of a turn for each electrical cycle, so for the same motor size you get more torque at lower speed, because the magnetic path through the back-iron can be thinner and the winding end-turns are shorter. That's why gimbal motors and drone outrunners have lots of poles, around 12 to 14 or more. You also get finer position resolution, since the Hall code changes every $60^\circ/p$ of shaft angle.

The cost is a higher electrical frequency for the same rpm. A 14-pole motor at 30 000 rpm is at 3.5 kHz electrical, so the inverter has to commutate at 21 kHz in six-step, iron loss and switching loss go up, and the controller loop needs enough bandwidth. A 2-pole motor can spin very fast with a low electrical frequency, which is why high-speed spindles and turbo-machinery often use few poles. Pole count also interacts with the slot count to set cogging, so I'd choose a good combination, like 12 slots and 14 poles."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.** $2p$ poles on the rotor. Mechanical speed $\omega_m$ (rad/s); electrical speed $\omega_e = p\,\omega_m$; $f_e = p\cdot\text{rpm}/60$. Six-step commutation events per mechanical revolution: $6p$.

**Torque.** Electromagnetic torque of a PM machine: $T = \tfrac{3}{2}\,p\,\lambda_m I_q$ (FOC form, peak-referred). For a fixed $I$ and fixed physical size, $T$ depends on $p\lambda_m$. The flux per pole $\Phi_p \propto B\cdot(\text{rotor area}/2p)$ falls as $1/p$, but the winding links $p$ times as many electrical cycles per revolution, so the product $p\lambda_m$ is roughly constant for a given *winding*; the real gains of high pole count come from (i) thinner stator yoke and rotor back-iron (less mass) because each pole carries less flux, and (ii) shorter end-turns with concentrated windings (less copper resistance for the same active length).

**Frequency-dependent losses.** Core loss scales roughly as $f_e^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha$ between 1.5 and 2 (hysteresis + eddy), so going from 2 to 7 pole pairs at constant rpm multiplies electrical frequency by 3.5 and core loss by order 6 to 12, unless lamination thickness or material is improved. Inverter switching losses rise with PWM frequency, which must scale with $f_e$.

**Cogging.** Cogging torque has a fundamental period equal to $360^\circ / \text{LCM}(N_s, 2p)$ where $N_s$ = number of slots. Larger LCM gives more, smaller, cogging cycles per revolution and lower amplitude.

| Slots/poles | LCM | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| 12 / 8 | 24 | high cogging |
| 12 / 10 | 60 | good |
| 12 / 14 | 84 | very good, common for outrunners and gimbals |
| 9 / 12 | 36 | moderate |
| 24 / 22 | 264 | very low cogging |

**Position resolution.** Hall sensors change every $60^\circ/p$ of shaft rotation: 14 poles gives 42 states per revolution (8.6° each); 2 poles gives 6 (60° each).
:::

::: short
"Poles are the permanent-magnet poles on the rotor; $p$ pole pairs means $p$ electrical cycles per revolution, so $f_e = p\cdot\text{rpm}/60$. More poles gives more torque at lower speed for the same size, thinner back-iron, finer Hall resolution and, with the right slot count, less cogging. The cost is a higher electrical frequency for the same rpm, which raises iron and switching losses and demands a faster controller. Few poles suit very high speed; many poles suit gimbals, drones and direct drive."
:::

::: long
"A pole here means a magnetic pole on the rotor, one of the permanent magnet faces, and they come in north-south pairs. A motor spec'd as 14 poles has 7 pole pairs, and the number that matters for electronics is the pole pairs, because it relates mechanical to electrical angle: one mechanical turn is $p$ electrical cycles. So electrical frequency is $p$ times rpm over 60.

Why that matters for performance. With more poles, each pole carries less flux, so you can use thinner back-iron and the concentrated windings have shorter end-turns, which means less copper resistance for the same active length. The result is that a high pole count motor gives high torque at low speed for its size, which is why gimbal motors, robot joints and drone outrunners use 12 to 14 poles or more and can be direct-drive without a gearbox. It also gives finer resolution: with Hall sensors the code changes every $60/p$ degrees of shaft rotation.

The flip side is frequency. At 30 000 rpm a 14-pole motor is at 3.5 kHz electrical, so a six-step drive commutates every 48 microseconds and I only get a handful of PWM cycles per electrical period. Core losses and inverter switching losses scale with that frequency, and the control loops have to be fast enough. So for very high-speed motors, like spindles or turbo-machinery, designers use two or four poles to keep the electrical frequency down.

Pole count also interacts with the slot count to set cogging torque. The more the least common multiple of slots and poles, the smoother the motor at low speed; 12 slots with 14 poles is a popular combination for that reason.

So the rule of thumb: many poles for torque and smoothness at low speed, few poles for high speed."
:::

::: terms
- **Pole / pole pair**: a magnet face on the rotor / an N-S pair. Datasheets list poles; formulas use pairs.
- **Electrical frequency $f_e$**: frequency of the phase currents, $p\cdot\text{rpm}/60$.
- **Electrical degrees**: angle measured in the electrical cycle, $\theta_e = p\theta_m$.
- **Slot**: gap between stator teeth that holds the winding; slot count $N_s$.
- **Cogging torque**: torque ripple from magnet-to-tooth attraction at zero current.
- **Concentrated winding**: coil wrapped around a single tooth, short end-turns.
- **Torque density**: torque per unit volume or mass.
- **Core (iron) loss**: hysteresis and eddy loss in the laminations, rising with $f_e$.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "poles are the magnets; more poles gives more torque and less speed". An exceptional answer:

- States $f_e = p\cdot\text{rpm}/60$ and computes the electrical frequency for a real case (e.g. 3.5 kHz at 30 000 rpm for 14 poles).
- Separates poles (rotor) from slots (stator) and mentions the LCM rule for cogging.
- Explains *why* torque density improves (thinner back-iron, shorter end-turns) instead of repeating "more poles more torque".
- Names the cost: core and switching losses, PWM cycles per electrical period, controller bandwidth.
- Connects it to application choices (gimbal vs spindle) and to steppers as an extreme case.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Confusing poles with phases or terminals.** A BLDC typically has three phases regardless of whether it has 2 or 14 poles.
- **Using poles instead of pole pairs in the formula.** $f_e = (\text{poles}/2)\cdot\text{rpm}/60$. Mixing them up gives a factor of 2 error.
- **"More poles always means more torque."** At the same current, size and winding, torque is not simply proportional to poles. The gains come from structure and are limited by saturation, leakage and frequency losses.
- **Ignoring the slot count.** Cogging and winding factor depend on the slot/pole combination, not poles alone.
- **Forgetting the controller.** A motor with many poles may look great on paper and be impossible to run at high rpm with a small MCU and 20 kHz PWM.
- **Assuming rpm and electrical rpm are the same on a controller display.** Some tools show eRPM (electrical rpm) = rpm × pole pairs. Always check.
:::

::: deep
## What a pole is

The rotor of a permanent-magnet motor has magnets glued on or buried in the rotor core, alternating north and south around the circumference. Each alternating face is a pole. The field in the air gap goes N, S, N, S as you walk around the rim. A stator winding "sees" one sine-like (or trapezoid-like) cycle of flux each time a pair of magnets passes it, so one pole pair = one electrical cycle.

## Why more poles gives more torque for the size

Consider an outer-rotor motor of fixed diameter and axial length. The magnet area per pole shrinks as $1/2p$, so the flux per pole $\Phi_p$ shrinks as $1/2p$ (the air-gap flux density is fixed by the magnet and gap). That has two effects:

1. **Thinner yoke.** The back-iron only needs to carry $\Phi_p/2$, so it can be thinner, freeing space for copper or reducing mass.
2. **Shorter end-turns.** With a concentrated winding the coil is wrapped around one tooth; the tooth pitch shrinks as the pole count rises, so the copper that does no useful work at the ends shrinks. Less non-working copper means lower $R$ and $I^2R$ for the same torque.

Torque per amp increases because the winding can fill the slot with active length better, and torque per kg of copper rises. This is the reason a 14-pole gimbal motor can produce large torque at very low rpm with small, almost no, gear reduction.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> There is no single formula "torque ∝ poles". Whether $K_t$ rises or falls with pole count at fixed size depends on turns per phase, winding factor, magnet and saturation. State the *reasons* (yoke thickness, end-turn length, winding factor), not a proportionality.

## Why more poles gets harder to drive

Everything in the electronics is tied to $f_e$:

- **Inverter PWM ratio.** To approximate a sine at $f_e$ you want at least 10 to 20 PWM cycles per electrical period. With $f_{sw}=24$ kHz and $f_e=3.5$ kHz you have 6.9, which is marginal; you either raise $f_{sw}$ (switching loss) or accept a distorted current.
- **Current-loop bandwidth.** The loop must track an AC reference at $f_e$. A rule of thumb is a current-loop bandwidth of at least 5 to 10 times $f_e$, which at 3.5 kHz is demanding.
- **Inductive impedance.** $X_L = 2\pi f_eL$ rises, so at high speed the available bus voltage has to overcome both back-EMF and the $\omega_eL$ drop. Back-EMF in volts depends on rpm, not poles ($E = K_e\omega_m$), but the reactive drop rises with $f_e$, which reduces the usable torque at high speed.
- **Core loss** (hysteresis ∝ $f_e$, eddy ∝ $f_e^2$): going from 1 to 7 pole pairs at constant rpm increases $f_e$ 7×.
- **Sensorless detection** relies on the back-EMF zero-crossing timing, whose resolution in time becomes tighter at high $f_e$.

## Slots, poles and cogging

The stator has $N_s$ slots. The magnets sit near a stable (minimum-reluctance) position relative to each tooth, giving cogging torque even with zero current. The cogging cycle repeats $\text{LCM}(N_s, 2p)$ times per revolution; larger LCM spreads the cogging into more, smaller bumps that partly cancel. Common combinations: 12N/14P (LCM 84), 12N/10P (60), 9N/12P (36); 12N/8P (24) is poor. Skewing the magnets or stator also reduces cogging.

## Steppers as the limit case

A hybrid stepper has about 50 rotor teeth ($p=50$ in this sense) and two phases. One electrical cycle of the phase currents (4 full steps) moves the rotor 7.2°, so 200 steps per revolution. The same logic applies: a very high pole count gives very high torque at low speed and fine steps but very high electrical frequency at moderate rpm, which is why steppers lose torque quickly with speed ([[q0377]]).

## Choosing pole count: heuristics

| Need | Lean towards |
|---|---|
| Very high rpm, small inverter | 2 to 4 poles |
| Direct drive, high torque at low rpm | 12 to 24+ poles |
| Smooth low-speed, low cogging | 12N/14P or fractional-slot with high LCM |
| Fine Hall resolution without encoder | More poles |
| Minimum core loss at high speed | Fewer poles, thin laminations |
:::

::: applications
- **Drone outrunners (e.g. 12N14P)**: many poles, direct-drive propellers with high torque at modest rpm.
- **Gimbal motors**: 12N14P or similar, very high pole count for smooth, strong low-speed torque with no gearbox.
- **High-speed spindles and compressors**: 2 to 4 poles for 50 000+ rpm with practical inverter frequencies.
- **Hub motors in e-bikes**: 20 to 46 poles, direct-drive at 150 to 400 rpm.
- **Hard drive spindles and fans**: often 8 to 12 poles, with simple Hall-based commutation.
- **Stepper motors**: 50 rotor teeth for fine steps.
:::

::: assumptions
- I treat "poles" as rotor magnetic poles in a permanent-magnet machine, as the BLDC section implies. In AC induction machines, poles refer to stator winding poles; the same $f_e$ relationship holds for synchronous speed.
- Some vendors list pole *pairs* as "poles", and some controllers ask for "motor poles" meaning pairs. Always verify.
- Torque and loss statements assume the same outer size, magnet grade, current density and cooling; changing these changes the comparison.
- Slot/pole examples assume fractional-slot concentrated windings, the most common in small BLDCs.
:::

::: followups
### What is the electrical frequency of a 14-pole motor at 6000 rpm?
Pole pairs $p = 7$, so $f_e = 7\cdot6000/60 = 700$ Hz. In six-step that is 4200 commutations per second.

### Why do high-speed motors use fewer poles?
The inverter and core see electrical frequency. At 100 000 rpm a 2-pole motor is at 1.67 kHz; a 14-pole motor would be at 11.7 kHz, which makes PWM, current control and core losses very difficult. Fewer poles keeps $f_e$ manageable.

### How does pole count relate to Kv?
$K_v$ (rpm per volt) is set by the back-EMF constant, which depends on turns per phase, flux and winding factor, not directly on pole count. In practice, high-pole-count outrunners are often wound for low $K_v$ because they are built for torque at low rpm, but that is a design choice, not a law. The relationship is through the whole winding design, not a single formula.

### What's the difference between poles and slots?
Poles are on the rotor (magnets). Slots are gaps between stator teeth where the windings sit. A motor "12N14P" has 12 slots and 14 poles. Their ratio and LCM determine winding factor, cogging and noise.

### Scenario: your FOC firmware shows the motor spinning at 7000 "rpm" but a tachometer reads 1000 rpm. What happened?
The firmware is displaying electrical rpm (eRPM), $= \text{rpm}\times p$. With $p = 7$ the mechanical speed is $7000/7 = 1000$ rpm. Set the pole-pair parameter correctly; otherwise speed loops, current-loop feedforward terms and the back-EMF constant all use the wrong scale.
:::

::: rubric
- Defines poles as rotor magnet poles and pole pairs as pairs :: Everything else depends on getting this definition right.
- Gives $f_e = p\cdot\text{rpm}/60$ with a number :: The key link between motor and electronics.
- States more poles gives torque density and low-speed torque, with a reason :: Shows you understand the physics, not just the claim.
- States the cost: higher $f_e$, iron and switching losses, controller bandwidth :: Balanced trade-off is what interviewers want.
- Mentions slots and cogging (LCM idea) :: Shows awareness that poles alone don't set smoothness.
- Mentions Hall/position resolution :: A practical effect of pole count.
- Gives an application example (gimbal vs spindle) :: Connects theory to design choice.
