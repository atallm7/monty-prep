::: testing
Two things at once: **why** the bus needs pull-ups at all (open-drain drivers can't drive high), and whether you can **size** them with real constraints rather than "4.7 kΩ because everyone uses 4.7 kΩ". The interviewer is listening for:

1. A lower bound from the sink-current limit: $R_{p,min} = (V_{DD}-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$.
2. An upper bound from the rise-time requirement: $R_{p,max} = t_r/(0.8473\,C_b)$.
3. The idea that these form a **window**, and that bus capacitance and speed mode move it.
4. Secondary effects: power burned while low, parallel pull-ups from breakout boards, and weak MCU internal pull-ups.
:::

::: why
Pull-ups are the only "analog" part of an otherwise digital protocol, and they're where I2C buses fail in real hardware: slow rounded edges, a low level that doesn't get low enough, a bus that works on the bench and not in the product. Sizing them is a short, clean calculation that shows whether you can turn a datasheet into a design decision. It's also a bridge between RC circuits, MOSFET output drivers, and digital timing.
:::

::: concepts
- **Open-drain output**: a driver can pull low or release, but never drives high. Something must supply the high. See [[q0761]] and [[q0785]].
- **RC charging**: the line rises exponentially through $R_p$ into $C_b$ with $\tau = R_pC_b$. See [[q0022]] and [[q0028]].
- **Rise-time definition**: I2C measures $t_r$ from 30% to 70% of $V_{DD}$, so $t_r = \tau\ln(0.7/0.3) = 0.8473\tau$.
- **Sink current / $V_{OL}$**: when a device pulls low, $R_p$ becomes a current source into its output. See [[q0760]].
- **Bus capacitance**: the load that the resistor has to charge. See [[q0756]].
- **Pull-up/pull-down basics**: [[q0020]] and [[q0786]].
:::

::: answer
"Pull-ups are needed because I2C devices are open-drain. A device can pull the line to ground, or let go, but nothing in the bus actually drives it high. So the resistor is what makes the high level and what restores the line after a device releases it. It also means a device can never short the bus by disagreeing with another one.

To spec it, I treat it as a window. The lower limit comes from the weakest device's sink current: when a device pulls low, it has to hold the line below its $V_{IL}$ against the current through the resistor, so $R_{min}$ equals $(V_{DD} - V_{OL})$ over $I_{OL}$. At 3.3 V with the standard 3 mA and 0.4 V that's about 970 Ω. The upper limit is the rise time. The line is an RC charge, and the I2C rise time from 30% to 70% is 0.85 times R times C, so $R_{max}$ is the allowed rise time over $0.85\,C_b$. For Fast mode, 300 ns, and 100 pF of bus capacitance, that's about 3.5 kΩ.

So I'd pick something in the window, usually a bit toward the low side for margin, like 2.2 kΩ for 400 kHz or 4.7 kΩ for 100 kHz, and I'd check the power burned while the line is low and whether breakout boards add extra pull-ups in parallel."
:::

::: technical
**Why pull-ups.** I2C outputs are open-drain (or open-collector). The line's high state is only produced by a resistor (or current source) to $V_{DD}$. Pull-ups also set the idle bus state and enable wired-AND, arbitration and clock stretching.

**Lower bound (low-level):** when a device sinks current $I_{OL}$ at output voltage $V_{OL}$,

$$R_{p,min} = \frac{V_{DD}-V_{OL}(\max)}{I_{OL}}$$

With the NXP spec values $V_{OL}=0.4$ V, $I_{OL}=3$ mA (Standard/Fast; 20 mA for Fast-mode Plus): 3.3 V gives 967 Ω; 5 V gives 1533 Ω; 1.8 V (using $V_{OL} = 0.2V_{DD}$ at lower voltages) gives about 480 Ω at 3 mA, or about 720 Ω if you size for the 2 mA that the spec associates with that low-voltage case. Use the *weakest* device's datasheet figure.

**Upper bound (rise time):** the node charges toward $V_{DD}$ as $v(t) = V_{DD}(1-e^{-t/R_pC_b})$. The 30% point is at $t_1=-R_pC_b\ln 0.7$ and 70% at $t_2=-R_pC_b\ln 0.3$, so

$$t_r = t_2 - t_1 = R_pC_b\ln\frac{0.7}{0.3} = 0.8473\,R_pC_b \;\Rightarrow\; R_{p,max}=\frac{t_r}{0.8473\,C_b}$$

Limits: 1000 ns (Standard, 100 kHz), 300 ns (Fast, 400 kHz), 120 ns (Fast-mode Plus, 1 MHz). Max $C_b$ is 400 pF for Standard and Fast mode.

**Feasibility.** The window closes when $R_{p,min}>R_{p,max}$, i.e. when $C_b > t_r/(0.8473R_{p,min})$. At 3.3 V Fast mode: 366 pF. At 5 V Fast mode: 231 pF. At 3.3 V Standard mode: 1.2 nF, so it never limits.

**Second-order checks.**
- Power while low: $P\approx V_{DD}\,(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/R_p$; 2.2 kΩ at 3.3 V burns about 4.4 mW per line *while that line is low*.
- Parallel pull-ups: $R_{eq} = 1/\sum(1/R_i)$ across all boards.
- 10–90% measured rise time is longer: $t_{10-90}=2.197R_pC_b$, 2.6 times the spec's 30–70%.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact $V_{OL}$ and $I_{OL}$ limits (including the $0.2V_{DD}$ rule below 2 V) depend on the specification revision and the device mode. Use your parts' datasheets and the NXP UM10204 table for your mode.
:::

::: long
"The pull-ups exist because I2C devices are open-drain. Each device has a transistor that can pull the line to ground, but there's no transistor to drive it high. So a resistor to the supply is what creates the logic high, and it's also what pulls the line back up whenever everyone has let go. That's useful because multiple devices can share the line, and nobody can fight anybody: if two devices disagree, low just wins.

To spec it I think about two constraints that pull in opposite directions. The first is the low level. When a device pulls the line down, the resistor is pushing current into it, $(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/R_p$. That has to stay under what the device can sink, normally 3 mA at 0.4 V. So the resistance has a minimum. At 3.3 V it's about 970 Ω, at 5 V about 1.5 kΩ.

The second is the rise time. When the line is released, the pull-up has to charge the bus capacitance. It's an RC charge, and the I2C spec measures the rise from 30% to 70%, which works out to 0.85 times R times C. Standard mode allows 1 µs, Fast 300 ns, Fast-mode Plus 120 ns. So the resistance has a maximum: rise time divided by 0.85 times the capacitance. For 300 ns and 100 pF that's about 3.5 kΩ.

So I pick a value inside the window. If the window is empty, because the capacitance is too big for the speed and voltage, the answer isn't a clever resistor; it's reduce the capacitance, slow down, or use a buffer or current-source pull-up.

Then the sanity checks: how much power burns when the line is low, which is a few milliwatts per line; whether breakout boards have added their own pull-ups in parallel; and whether I'm relying on the MCU's internal pull-ups, which are typically tens of kΩ and way too weak for anything but a very slow bus."
:::

::: terms
- **Open-drain**: output transistor to ground only; high requires an external pull-up.
- **$R_{p,min}$**: lower resistance limit from sink current: $(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$.
- **$R_{p,max}$**: upper resistance limit from rise time: $t_r/(0.8473C_b)$.
- **Rise time ($t_r$)**: time from 30% to 70% of $V_{DD}$ in I2C.
- **$V_{OL}$, $I_{OL}$**: output low voltage at a specified sink current (0.4 V at 3 mA standard).
- **$V_{IL}$, $V_{IH}$**: input thresholds, 0.3$V_{DD}$ and 0.7$V_{DD}$.
- **Bus capacitance ($C_b$)**: total capacitance on the line. See [[q0756]].
- **Wired-AND**: the bus is high only if every device releases it.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "pull-ups pull the line high; use 4.7 kΩ".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives *why* the bus can't work without them in one sentence (open-drain).
- Gives both bounds with their physical origins and puts numbers on them ($\approx$ 970 Ω and 3.5 kΩ).
- Notes the window can close and says what to do then (reduce $C_b$, slow down, buffer, current-source pull-up).
- Mentions the 30–70% definition and that a scope's 10–90% reading is about 2.6 times longer.
- Checks static power and parallel breakout-board resistors, and says MCU internal pull-ups usually don't qualify.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Believing one value fits all buses.** 4.7 kΩ is great at 100 kHz with 100 pF and hopeless at 400 kHz with 300 pF.
- **Using the 10–90% rise time in the formula.** The spec uses 30–70%, so the constant is 0.8473, not 2.2.
- **Forgetting the lower bound.** Making $R_p$ tiny for speed pushes $V_{OL}$ up and eventually above $V_{IL}$. See [[q0760]].
- **Ignoring the weakest device.** $I_{OL}$ is set by the device that sinks least, not the MCU.
- **Relying on internal pull-ups.** Typical 20–50 kΩ gives a rise time of microseconds. Fine only at tens of kHz with a few pF.
- **Adding pull-ups at every module.** They're in parallel, so the sum may violate $R_{p,min}$.
:::

::: deep
## Why the bus can't work without pull-ups

Take the line from [[q0754]]: each device has an NMOS from the line to ground and nothing to $V_{DD}$. With every NMOS off, the line has no path to any rail, so it floats at an undefined level. The resistor is what defines the "released" state as logic 1.

That is also why it's safe. In a push-pull bus, two drivers that disagree create a path from $V_{DD}$ to ground through two transistors: contention and heat ([[q0785]]). In open-drain, the worst case is two NMOS pulling low together, which is harmless. See [[q0761]] for the full advantage list.

## The two edges are not symmetric

- **Falling edge**: the device's NMOS turns on and discharges $C_b$ through its on-resistance, which is tens to a couple hundred ohms. Fast, and the spec restricts how fast it may be (a *minimum* fall time) to limit ringing and EMI.
- **Rising edge**: nothing is driving; the resistor charges $C_b$. Slow, determined by $R_pC_b$.

So the rise time is the thing you control with $R_p$, and it's the thing that fails first as speed goes up.

## Deriving the window

**Upper bound.** Charging from 0 toward $V_{DD}$:

$$v(t)=V_{DD}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\quad \tau=R_pC_b$$

30% is at $t_1 = -\tau\ln(0.7)=0.357\tau$; 70% at $t_2 = -\tau\ln(0.3)=1.204\tau$. The difference is $0.8473\tau$.

Figure: τ = 220 ns. The line crosses 30% at 78 ns and 70% at 265 ns, giving $t_r$ = 186 ns, which is $0.8473\tau$. A 10–90% reading would be $2.197\tau$ = 483 ns, so don't compare a scope's 10–90% number directly with the spec. Drag C_b up to 400 pF with the 2.2 kΩ pull-up: t_r grows to about 746 ns, far past the 300 ns Fast-mode limit; dropping R_p to 500 Ω brings it back to about 170 ns.

Rearranged: $R_{p,max}=t_r/(0.8473C_b)$.

**Lower bound.** When a slave turns on its NMOS, a DC current flows from $V_{DD}$ through $R_p$ into the device. The device's output voltage rises to $V_{OL}=V_{DD}-I\,R_p$, so $I=(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/R_p$. The device is guaranteed to hold $V_{OL}\le 0.4$ V only while $I\le I_{OL}$ (3 mA standard). So

$$R_{p,min}=\frac{V_{DD}-V_{OL}}{I_{OL}}$$

## Worked example

3.3 V, Fast mode, sensors at 3 mA and an estimated 120 pF.

- $R_{p,min}=2.9/0.003=967$ Ω
- $R_{p,max}=300\text{ ns}/(0.8473\times120\text{ pF})=2.95$ kΩ
- Pick 2.2 kΩ (a standard value, 2.3 times the minimum and 25% below the maximum).
- Check: $t_r=0.8473\times2200\times120\text{ pF}=224$ ns. Under 300 ns, with 25% margin for tolerance on $R$ and $C$.
- Low current: $2.9/2200=1.32$ mA, under 3 mA.
- Power: about 4.3 mW per line while low. With 100 kHz SCL at 50% duty that's about 2 mW average; SDA less.

Now suppose the sensor board adds 100 pF and two other modules each bring a 10 kΩ pull-up: the effective $R_p$ is $2.2\parallel10\parallel10=1.53$ kΩ, and $C_b$ is now 220 pF so $R_{p,max}=1.61$ kΩ. Still inside the window, but barely. A third such module gives 1.33 kΩ with another 100 pF of load, and now the bus fails the rise-time check ($R_{p,max}$ at 320 pF is 1.1 kΩ, so 1.33 kΩ is too weak) while the low current climbs; add a device that sinks only 1 mA and the low level breaks too.

## When the window is empty

If $R_{p,min} > R_{p,max}$ no resistor works. Options, in order:
1. **Reduce $C_b$**: shorter traces, fewer devices, a switch that disconnects unused branches.
2. **Slow the clock** to a mode with a longer allowed rise time. 
3. **Use a bus buffer** that splits capacitance into segments.
4. **Use a current-source or active pull-up** ("rise-time accelerator"): high current during the edge, small DC current while low. This breaks the trade-off that a plain resistor imposes.
5. **Increase $I_{OL}$ capability** by choosing Fast-mode Plus devices (20 mA) so $R_{p,min}$ can drop to about 145 Ω.

## Internal pull-ups

MCU weak pull-ups are typically in the 10s of kΩ (varies by part; check the datasheet). With 40 kΩ and 50 pF, $t_r = 0.8473\times40\text{ k}\times50\text{ pF}=1.7$ µs: too slow even for Standard mode's 1 µs limit. It can work at a very slow clock (tens of kHz) with a tiny bus. Don't design that in.

## Worst-case version of the same example

A paper design that only works at nominal values will fail on someone's bench. Redo the example at the corners: resistor at -5% (2.09 kΩ) for the low-current check, resistor at +5% (2.31 kΩ) and capacitance at +30% (156 pF) for the rise-time check.

- Low current at the corner: $2.9/2090=1.39$ mA, fine against 3 mA.
- Rise time at the corner: $0.8473\times2310\times156\text{ pF}=305$ ns. Just over 300 ns.

So the nominal 2.2 kΩ passes with 25% margin but fails the corner by a hair. The fix is cheap: drop to 1.8 kΩ, which gives $t_r=0.8473\times1890\times156\text{ pF}=250$ ns at the +5%/+30% corner, and still leaves $2.9/1710=1.7$ mA at the low corner. Say this in an interview: "I pick values that survive the corners, not the nominal."

## Real-hardware notes

- **Tolerance:** use the minimum $R_p$ with -1% to -5% in the lower bound, the maximum with +5% in the upper bound, and use worst-case $C_b$.
- **Temperature and voltage:** pin capacitance and $V_{OL}$ vary a bit, so keep 20–30% margin.
- **Measure:** check $t_r$ on the scope at the *far* device and $V_{OL}$ at the weakest device.
:::

::: applications
- **Standard 3.3 V sensor bus**: 4.7 kΩ at 100 kHz or 2.2 kΩ at 400 kHz are common starting values for short boards.
- **Fast-mode Plus at 1 MHz**: devices that sink 20 mA let you go down to about 150 Ω to 1 kΩ pull-ups to hit the 120 ns rise-time limit.
- **Battery-powered products**: a larger resistor (such as 10 kΩ) at 100 kHz with small capacitance cuts the current drawn every time a line is held low (the bus idles high, so the pull-ups only cost energy during traffic).
- **Rise-time accelerators**: devices like I2C buffers with built-in boost improve edges on high-capacitance buses (check the specific buffer datasheet).
- **Camera/display modules**: the module's own pull-ups plus the host's can sum to well under the recommended value; one of the first things to check on integration.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Resistor pull-up, linear RC rise.** A current-source pull-up gives a linear ramp and different numbers.
- **Single dominant capacitance $C_b$ lumped at the line.** Long lines behave as transmission lines and need different treatment ([[q0711]]).
- **Spec values** ($V_{OL}$ 0.4 V, $I_{OL}$ 3 mA, 30/70% thresholds, limits 1000/300/120 ns) are for the NXP I2C-bus spec; individual parts may specify more (or less, in non-conforming SMBus/PMBus systems).
- **$V_{DD}$ constant and equal for pull-up and device.** If the pull-up goes to a different rail, use that rail in both formulas.

If the interviewer says "assume a typical 4.7 kΩ", then check the bus the other direction: what $C_b$ and speed does that value allow? $4.7$ kΩ gives $t_r=1$ µs at $C_b=251$ pF, so it's a Standard-mode value.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the I2C rise time defined 30% to 70% and not 10% to 90%?
The thresholds are $V_{IL}=0.3V_{DD}$ and $V_{IH}=0.7V_{DD}$, so 30–70% is exactly the time the signal spends in the *undefined* region between valid low and valid high, which is the only part that matters to the receiver. The constant is $\ln(0.7/0.3)=0.8473$; the 10–90% constant would be 2.197.

### What happens if the pull-up is too strong (too small)?
$V_{OL}$ rises because the device has to sink more current than it's rated for. When the low level approaches $V_{IL}$, noise margin disappears and you get unreliable ACKs. Also you waste power and, on very small values, violate the device's absolute maximum output current.

### What if the pull-up is too weak (too large)?
The edge is slow: the line spends too long between thresholds, setup times are violated at the receiver, and at high clock rates the line never reaches a valid high. The classic symptom is a bus that works at 10 kHz and fails at 400 kHz.

### Can you use the MCU's internal pull-up?
Only on a very slow, very short bus. Internal pull-ups are tens of kΩ; with 50 pF that's about 1.7 µs of rise time, past the Standard-mode limit. Many MCUs also don't guarantee them as I2C-valid. Use external resistors for anything that has to work reliably.

### How would you pick a pull-up for a bus that you can't measure yet?
Estimate $C_b$ from the datasheets (pins ≈ 5–10 pF each, trace about 1 pF/cm, connector few pF), compute both bounds, choose about the geometric mean of $R_{min}$ and $R_{max}$ or lean low, then put footprints for 0 Ω/resistors so you can tune after measuring on the first board.
:::
