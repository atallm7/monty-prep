::: basics
### In plain words
In a synchronous buck, both transistors are switched off for a few nanoseconds at every hand-over, so they never short the input. During those gaps the inductor current flows through the bottom transistor's built-in diode instead. That diode has three side effects you must design around. It wastes some power, it stores charge that causes a spike when it is forced off, and it pulls the switch node below ground.

### Picture it
Picture a crowd forced through a narrow side door while the main door is shut. Every second spent there costs effort. When the main door reopens, people still in the side door must back out, and that rush causes a brief jam. A shorter wait and a better side door both help.

### Words you need
- **Body diode**: the diode built into every power MOSFET, which conducts during dead time ([[q0208]]).
- **Dead time**: the short gap when both transistors are off ([[q0207]]).
- **Switch node**: the point between the two transistors that feeds the inductor.
- **Reverse-recovery charge ($Q_{rr}$)**: charge stored in the diode that must be swept out before it stops conducting.
- **Conduction loss**: heat from the diode's voltage drop times the current through it.
- **Undershoot**: the dip of the switch node below ground.

### The simplest example
Take a 2 A buck switching 500,000 times a second, with a 0.75 V body-diode drop. Each cycle has two hand-overs, with 25 ns of dead time at each, so the diode conducts 50 ns per cycle. That is $50\ \text{ns} \times 500\ \text{kHz} = 2.5$ percent of the time. The loss is $0.75 \times 2 \times 0.025 \approx 0.04$ W. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the other consequences, including dead-time setting, pin ratings and ringing, and the reverse-recovery loss, which is often bigger (about 0.12 W here).
:::

::: testing
Whether you can turn a device-level behavior (the body diode conducts in dead time and has to recover) into **a list of concrete design actions**. The interviewer wants:

1. The causal chain: dead-time conduction at $V_{SD}\approx0.7$ to 1 V, stored charge, reverse recovery at the next high-side turn-on.
2. The consequences, each with a mechanism: loss, ringing/EMI, negative SW voltage at the controller pins, current and thermal stress on the diode, and the shoot-through trade-off in setting dead time.
3. Prioritization and numbers rather than a bare list: which effect is biggest at a given $V_{in}$, current and frequency.
4. A pointer to the cures without confusing the question with Q210 (which asks for them).
:::

::: why
Every synchronous buck, motor bridge and inverter leg has a body diode in its power path, and many mysterious problems trace back to it: a few percent of efficiency missing at high $V_{in}$ or high frequency, a spike on SW that violates a pin rating, EMI failing in the 100 to 300 MHz band, a controller that latches up or resets only at heavy load. Knowing the checklist lets you ask the right bench questions. This page is the "what must I account for"; [[q0210]] is the "what can I do about it".
:::

::: concepts
- **Body-diode conduction in dead time**: $V_{SD}\approx0.7$ to 1 V at the load current ([[q0208]]).
- **Reverse recovery**: stored charge $Q_{rr}$ flows backward when the diode is forced off, through the high-side FET.
- **Dead-time trade**: too short risks shoot-through, too long wastes energy ([[q0207]]).
- **Negative SW voltage**: the controller's SW pin sees $-V_{SD}$ plus undershoot.
- **Loop inductance and ringing**: $Q_{rr}$ current snapping off in nH of inductance creates fast spikes.
- **FOM**: $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{rr}$ and $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{oss}$ for device selection.
:::

::: answer
"The body diode conducts during the dead time at about 0.7 to 1 V, and that has five consequences I'd design around.

One, loss: $V_{SD}\cdot I\cdot t_{dead}\cdot f$, plus reverse recovery. At 12 V in, 2 A and 500 kHz with 50 ns of total dead time per cycle, 25 at each of the two edges, that's about 40 milliwatts of conduction, but 20 nC of recovery charge is another 120 milliwatts, so recovery usually matters more as $V_{in}$ and frequency go up.

Two, dead-time setting: it has to be long enough to avoid shoot-through across delay mismatch and temperature, and no longer, because every extra nanosecond costs diode loss and charge. That pushes toward adaptive dead time.

Three, the negative voltage on SW. The controller's SW or phase pin must tolerate about minus one diode drop plus undershoot, or it can latch up through its substrate. I'd check the absolute maximum rating.

Four, EMI and ringing. When the high side turns on and the diode snaps off, the recovery current flows in loop inductance and rings, producing overshoot on SW and noise at 100 megahertz or more. I'd think about layout, gate resistance or a snubber.

Five, the diode's current and thermal rating, and temperature drift: $V_{SD}$ drops with temperature, and the diode must carry the peak inductor current. And for device selection I'd pick a FET with low $Q_{rr}$."
:::

::: technical
**Dead-time conduction.** $P_{dt}=V_{SD}\,I\,(t_{d1}+t_{d2})\,f_{sw}$. Example (2 A, 0.75 V, $t_{d1}+t_{d2}=50$ ns total, 500 kHz): 37.5 mW. $V_{SD}$ has a negative temperature coefficient (about $-2$ mV/°C), so hot parts drop less.

**Recovery.** $P_{rr}\approx Q_{rr}\,V_{in}\,f_{sw}$ (energy dissipated mostly in the high-side FET during overlap of recovery current and $V_{DS}$). 20 nC, 12 V, 500 kHz: 120 mW. $Q_{rr}$ rises with forward current, $di/dt$ and the time the diode has conducted, and with temperature.

**Trade-off in $t_d$.** Total loss vs dead time has a minimum: below it shoot-through grows sharply, above it body-diode terms grow roughly linearly. In the illustrative model on this page the optimum is near 20 ns; real devices differ.

**Negative SW.** $V_{SW,min}\approx-V_{SD}-\Delta V_{undershoot}$. Compare with the pin rating (many controllers list a transient allowance for the SW pin of order $-1$ V to $-5$ V for a few ns, and a smaller DC value; read the datasheet). Substrate injection can latch up integrated converters.

**Recovery spike / EMI.** Peak recovery current $I_{RM}$ and its decay slope $di/dt$ produce $V=L_{loop}\,di/dt$. Ringing frequency $f_{ring}=1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{loop}C_{oss}})$ is typically tens to hundreds of MHz.

**Stress.** The diode must carry $I_{L,pk}$ for the dead time: peak power $V_{SD}I_{pk}$ (1.9 W at 2.5 A), average power low, but datasheet source current ($I_S$) and pulsed ratings should exceed $I_{pk}$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $V_{SD}$, $Q_{rr}$, pin ratings and temperature coefficients are device and vendor specific. Use the datasheet of the actual FET and controller.
:::

::: short
"The body diode conducts in dead time at about 0.7 to 1 V and then must be forced off by the high-side turn-on. So I'd budget its conduction loss $V_{SD}It_df$ and its recovery loss $Q_{rr}V_{in}f$, set dead time as short as is safe, probably adaptive, check the negative SW voltage against the controller's rating, watch the recovery spike and ringing for EMI and overshoot, check the diode's current and thermal rating, and choose a low-$Q_{rr}$ FET."
:::

::: long
"The body diode in the low-side FET is not a feature I chose, so I treat it as a parasitic that I have to budget around. It conducts in the dead time at about 0.7 to 1 V, and when the high side turns on, it has to be forced off. That creates five things I'd design for.

First, loss. The conduction loss is the diode drop times the current times the dead time times the switching frequency. For 0.75 V, 2 A, 50 ns of total dead time per cycle, which is two 25 ns gaps, and 500 kHz, that's about 40 milliwatts. The reverse-recovery loss is the stored charge times the input voltage times frequency. With 20 nC at 12 V and 500 kHz that's 120 milliwatts, so recovery is usually the bigger term, and it scales with $V_{in}$ and frequency.

Second, dead-time setting. Too short and I risk shoot-through; too long and I pay both of those losses. There's an optimum, which is why good controllers use adaptive dead time, and I'd verify it hot.

Third, negative voltage on SW. During the shelf, SW is about minus 0.75 V and can undershoot. The controller's SW pin has an absolute-maximum rating and in a monolithic part, pulling the substrate negative can cause latch-up.

Fourth, EMI and overshoot. The diode snaps off when the high side turns on, and the recovery current changes quickly in the loop inductance, which rings the SW node at tens to hundreds of megahertz. That drives my layout, gate resistor, snubber and maybe a boot resistor.

Fifth, the diode itself needs enough current rating and thermal margin, and I'd compare FETs on $Q_{rr}$ and $R_{DS(on)}$, not just on-resistance. If those losses are too large, I'd consider a Schottky or a controller with better dead-time control, which is what [[q0210]] asks."
:::

::: terms
- **$V_{SD}$**: the body-diode forward voltage of a MOSFET.
- **$Q_{rr}$ / $t_{rr}$ / $I_{RM}$**: reverse-recovery charge, time and peak reverse current.
- **Soft vs snappy recovery**: how gently the reverse current decays; snappy recovery rings more.
- **Dead-time optimum**: the dead time minimizing total loss.
- **Latch-up**: parasitic thyristor action triggered by minority-carrier injection into the substrate.
- **Absolute maximum rating**: the voltage or current beyond which damage can occur.
- **Snubber**: RC network that damps SW ringing.
- **FOM $R_{DS(on)}Q_{rr}$**: figure of merit combining conduction and recovery.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Dead time, the voltage drop and the reverse recovery."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Organizes the answer: loss, dead time, negative voltage, EMI/overshoot, device stress, device selection.
- Quantifies conduction and recovery with a worked example and identifies which one dominates and how it scales with $V_{in}$ and $f_{sw}$.
- Frames dead time as a minimum of a U-shaped loss curve with shoot-through on one side and diode loss on the other.
- Knows the IC-level hazard: negative SW voltage, substrate injection, latch-up, pin ratings.
- Mentions temperature (lower $V_{SD}$ hot, higher $Q_{rr}$ hot), light-load/DCM interplay, and testing (scope SW with a good probe).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Only discussing conduction loss.** At 12 V and above, $Q_{rr}V_{in}f$ is typically larger than the dead-time conduction.
- **"Make dead time as long as possible to be safe."** It increases loss and stored charge; efficiency has an optimum.
- **Ignoring the controller's pin rating.** Undershoot at SW can violate a datasheet spec and shorten life or cause latch-up.
- **Assuming a Schottky in parallel always helps.** It needs very low loop inductance or the body diode conducts first ([[q0210]]).
- **Using one temperature.** $V_{SD}$ falls and $Q_{rr}$ rises with heat; shoot-through margin also changes.
:::

::: deep
## The chain of events

1. $Q_1$ turns off. $I_L$ commutates into $Q_2$'s body diode. SW falls to $-V_{SD}$ ([[q0208]]).
2. $Q_2$ turns on after the dead time; the channel takes over.
3. $Q_2$ turns off again, back into the body diode for the second dead time.
4. $Q_1$ turns on. SW rises; the diode is forced off. A reverse current $I_{RM}$ (set by $Q_{rr}$ and $di/dt$) flows through $Q_1$, then decays; SW overshoots and rings.

Each step creates a design consideration.

## 1. Conduction loss in the dead time

$$P_{dt} = V_{SD}\,I\,(t_{d1}+t_{d2})\,f_{sw}$$

For $V_{SD}=0.75$ V, $I=2$ A, total 50 ns, 500 kHz: 37.5 mW. It scales linearly with $f_{sw}$ and with dead time: at 2 MHz and 50 ns, 150 mW (2.3% of a 6.6 W output). Because the diode only conducts 2.5% of the period here, the *average* power is small, but at high frequency (several MHz) the dead-time fraction becomes significant.

## 2. Recovery loss and the spike

$$P_{rr}\approx Q_{rr}\,V_{in}\,f_{sw}$$

20 nC, 12 V, 500 kHz: 120 mW. It scales with $V_{in}$ and $f_{sw}$, and $Q_{rr}$ itself rises with the forward current, with $di/dt$, with temperature and with how long the diode conducted (up to a saturation set by the carrier lifetime). That is why a long dead time hurts twice.

The recovery current flows through $Q_1$ while its $V_{DS}$ is still high; the energy ends up in $Q_1$ (and in the ringing). The peak recovery current adds to the inductor current at the instant of turn-on, so the high-side FET sees $I_L + I_{RM}$.

## 4. Negative voltage at the controller

At the dead-time shelf SW is $-V_{SD}\approx-0.75$ V; undershoot and ringing add more. Check:

- **Pin rating**: SW/phase pin absolute maximum, DC and transient (read the datasheet footnote on time and magnitude).
- **Substrate injection (monolithic converters)**: pulling SW more than about 0.3 to 0.5 V below ground can forward-bias the drain-substrate junction, injecting electrons that disturb neighboring circuits (comparators, bandgap) or trigger latch-up. Datasheets with negative-SW tolerance have guard structures.
- **Bootstrap cap overcharge**: negative SW adds to the cap voltage ([[q0206]]).
- **Sensing circuits**: zero-current detectors and current-sense comparators that look at SW or $V_{DS}$ must tolerate it or blank during dead time.

## 5. Ringing, overshoot, EMI

The recovery current stops abruptly at the end of recovery (a "snappy" diode). In a loop inductance $L_{loop}$ this makes a spike $V=L_{loop}\,di/dt$ and rings with $C_{oss}$:

$$f_{ring}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{loop}C_{oss}}}$$

For $L_{loop}=2$ nH and $C_{oss}=300$ pF: $f_{ring}\approx205$ MHz. The overshoot can exceed the FET's $V_{DS}$ rating margin, couples to the output as a spike and radiates in the VHF band. Considerations: loop layout (input cap right at the half bridge), gate resistance on $Q_1$ turn-on to slow $di/dt$, an RC snubber, and a soft-recovery FET.

## 6. The diode's own ratings

- **Source current rating $I_S$** (continuous) and pulsed rating: must exceed $I_{L,pk}$; for 30 A phases, $V_{SD}I_{pk}=22$ W instantaneous for the dead time.
- **Thermal**: average power is tiny (about 0.5 W at 30 A, 50 ns, 500 kHz), but the junction is small; the thermal time constant is short, so the pulse is not a problem unless dead time is long.
- **Temperature**: $V_{SD}$ drops about 2 mV/°C (a hot diode conducts at about 0.55 V); $Q_{rr}$ rises with temperature; shoot-through margin falls as $V_{th}$ falls.

## 7. Operating-mode interactions

- **Light load / DCM**: diode emulation turns $Q_2$ off at zero current, so the body diode conducts only briefly; $Q_{rr}$ is small. In forced CCM it conducts every cycle ([[q0203]]).
- **Reverse current**: if the inductor current is negative, the body diode of $Q_1$ may conduct during the other dead time into $V_{in}$ (bidirectional operation).
- **Frequency scaling**: both $P_{dt}$ and $P_{rr}$ are proportional to $f_{sw}$. At 1 to 3 MHz these dominate unless dead time is adaptive and $Q_{rr}$ is small.

## 8. Choosing the FET

Look at $Q_{rr}$ (at the actual $di/dt$ and current) next to $R_{DS(on)}$, $Q_g$ and $Q_{oss}$. Modern shielded-gate trench devices reduce $Q_{rr}$ and soften recovery; monolithically integrated Schottky devices (the body diode is replaced by a Schottky-like path) are offered for synchronous rectifiers. Compare with the datasheet *conditions*: $Q_{rr}$ at $I_F=20$ A and 300 A/µs is not what your 2 A, 0.5 A/ns operating point sees.

::: applications
- **Selecting FETs for a VRM or point-of-load stage**: comparing $Q_{rr}$, $Q_{oss}$ and $R_{DS(on)}$ along with the controller's dead-time scheme.
- **Choosing a buck IC for high $V_{in}$ and high $f_{sw}$**: efficiency datasheet curves already include these effects; understanding them guides when the loss budget is tight.
- **EMI/ringing fixes on the SW node**: RC snubbers or boot resistors tuned for the recovery spike.
- **Motor and inverter legs**: body-diode recovery stress on the opposite device; dead-time compensation to cut distortion.
- **Wide-bandgap migration**: GaN eliminates $Q_{rr}$ but its reverse conduction drop and dead-time sensitivity changes the considerations.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Silicon MOSFETs** in a synchronous buck with a positive inductor current; GaN/SiC devices alter several items.
- **Representative values**: $V_{SD}=0.75$ V, $Q_{rr}=20$ to 30 nC, dead time 25 ns per edge; use device data for real work.
- **First-order loss formulas**; recovery energy depends on how $di/dt$ and loop inductance shape the current/voltage overlap in $Q_1$.
- **Constant frequency**; in PFM or skip modes the effects scale with the actual switching rate.

::: followups
### Why does $Q_{rr}$ loss grow with $V_{in}$ but conduction loss does not?
Conduction loss depends on the diode drop (about 0.75 V) and current, independent of $V_{in}$. Recovery energy is dissipated while the high-side FET has most of $V_{in}$ across it, so it scales as $Q_{rr}V_{in}$. At 48 V in, $Q_{rr}V_{in}f$ would be four times the 12 V value for the same $Q_{rr}$, which is why high-voltage converters are sensitive to it.

### How would you measure body-diode loss on the bench?
Measure efficiency versus dead time (programmable controllers make this easy) at fixed load and temperature: the curve shows the optimum and the slope on the long side is the diode contribution. Scope SW with a short-ground probe and look at the width and depth of the shelf and the recovery spike; a current probe in the low-side source shows $I_{RM}$.

### What if the controller's SW pin is rated only to $-0.3$ V?
Then a bare silicon body-diode shelf at $-0.75$ V violates it, so the part is intended for a design with a clamp: a Schottky in parallel at very low inductance, or a controller feature that avoids the shelf (very small dead time). Otherwise choose a part with negative-SW tolerance. Always check whether the spec is DC or transient.

### How does temperature change the picture?
$V_{SD}$ falls (about $-2$ mV/°C), reducing conduction loss, but $Q_{rr}$ and recovery time rise, increasing recovery loss and spike, and $V_{th}$ falls so shoot-through margin shrinks. Test dead time at the hot corner, not at room temperature.

### The converter is fine at 1 A but the high-side FET gets hot at 5 A. Body diode?
Possibly: $Q_{rr}$ rises with forward current, and a long dead time increases it further, so the high-side FET sees more recovery energy at high current. Compare high-side temperature rise against the $Q_{rr}V_{in}f$ estimate, vary dead time, and look at SW overshoot and the high-side $V_{DS}$ on a scope.
:::
