::: basics
### In plain words
A solenoid's moving part, the plunger, travels a few millimeters in a few milliseconds. You cannot use a spinning-speed sensor (tachometer) on it, because nothing spins. Instead you time the plunger between two known points, or track its position and see how fast it changes, or read its motion from the coil's own current. The coil trick works because a moving plunger generates a small voltage that opposes the supply.

### Picture it
Think of timing a sprinter with two cones and a stopwatch. Start the watch at the first cone, stop it at the second, and divide the distance by the time. Two sensors along the stroke do the same job. The analogy breaks in one place: the plunger speeds up all the way to the end stop, so the average speed over the whole stroke is far lower than its speed at impact.

### Words you need
- **Plunger (armature)**: the moving iron part of the solenoid.
- **Stroke**: how far the plunger travels.
- **Transit time**: the time to cross the stroke or a set segment.
- **Motional EMF**: the voltage the moving plunger generates in the coil. It grows with speed and opposes the supply.
- **Current dip**: the dip in coil current during pull-in, caused by that voltage. The dip ends at arrival.

### The simplest example
A plunger crosses a 10 mm stroke in 8 ms. Its average speed is $0.010 / 0.008 = 1.25$ m/s. Now put two gates 5 mm apart and read a 2.5 ms gap between them. The speed there is $0.005 / 0.0025 = 2$ m/s. With no added sensor at all, drive the coil with a steady voltage and watch the current. It rises, dips while the plunger moves fast, and climbs again once the plunger stops.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the coil equation, why turning position into speed amplifies noise, how to use the dip, and calibration.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can think about a *fast, short-stroke, linear* actuator the way you would a motor, but with different tools. A solenoid does not spin, so encoders and tachometers do not apply directly, and its whole motion lasts milliseconds. The interviewer wants to hear transit-time timing with two sensors, differentiating a position signal, and the elegant trick of reading plunger motion from the coil current. A strong answer also knows what the motional back-EMF is and why it shows up as a dip in the current waveform.
:::

::: why
Plunger velocity decides impact noise, wear, bounce and valve timing. Fuel injectors, door locks, relays and hydraulic valves are all tuned by watching how fast the plunger arrives. In a product you rarely have room for a sensor, so being able to extract velocity information from the drive electronics is valuable. It is also a clean test of whether you understand the electromechanical coupling of a solenoid (back-EMF is not only for motors).
:::

::: concepts
- **Solenoid dynamics**: electrical equation plus mechanical equation coupled through $\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x$. See [[q0334]].
- **Motional back-EMF**: voltage induced by the plunger moving in the changing inductance, $e = i\,\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}x}\,v$. See [[q0341]] and [[q0408]].
- **Transit time**: time between leaving and reaching the end of stroke; average velocity is stroke over time.
- **Differentiating position**: $v = \mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t$, which amplifies noise.
- **Current signature**: the shape of the coil-current waveform contains plunger arrival information. See [[q0395]] for current measurement.
- **Sensor options**: Hall, optical gates, LVDT, linear encoder strips. See [[q0407]].
:::

"A solenoid moves in a few milliseconds over a few millimetres, so I think in terms of transit time and short-range linear sensors rather than rpm.

The simplest way is timing: put two sensors a known distance apart along the stroke, say optical gates or Hall sensors, and time the plunger between them. Velocity is the distance over the time, and a microcontroller timer gives microsecond resolution. If I have a continuous position sensor, like a linear Hall sensor with a magnet on the plunger or an LVDT, I can differentiate it, but I'd filter because differentiation amplifies noise.

And if I don't want any sensor, the coil itself works. The coil equation has a term, current times dL/dx times velocity, which is the motional back-EMF. When the plunger accelerates, it pulls current down, so under constant voltage drive you see the current rise, dip and rise again. The depth and timing of the dip are tied to velocity and the arrival time. That's the same idea as measuring motor speed from back-EMF.

In the lab I'd confirm with a high-speed camera or a laser displacement sensor."
:::

::: technical
**Coil equations.** The terminal voltage and the mechanical force are

$$v_c = iR + \frac{\mathrm{d}(Li)}{\mathrm{d}t} = iR + L\frac{\mathrm{d}i}{\mathrm{d}t} + i\,\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}x}\,v,\qquad F_{mag} = \tfrac12 i^2\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}x}$$

with $x$ the plunger travel and $v = \mathrm{d}x/\mathrm{d}t$. The last term of the voltage equation is the **motional EMF** $e_m = i\,(\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x)\,v$. It opposes the supply when the plunger moves toward the closed position and $\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x > 0$.

**Solving for velocity.** If you measure $v_c$ and $i$ and know $R$, $L(x)$, $\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x$:
$$v = \frac{v_c - iR - L\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t}{i\,\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x}$$
In practice this is awkward (needs $\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x$ vs position and noise-free $\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$), so most products use the *shape* of the current: a local minimum (the "dip") marks arrival, and the dip depth correlates with impact velocity after calibration.

**Transit timing.** Two gates a distance $d$ apart give $v = d/\Delta t$. With $d = 5$ mm and $\Delta t = 2.5$ ms, $v = 2$ m/s. A 1 µs timer resolution gives 0.04% timing error here, so sensor position tolerance and edge jitter dominate.

**Differentiating a position sensor.** If the position signal has noise $\sigma_x$ and you differentiate over $\Delta t$, velocity noise is about $\sqrt2\,\sigma_x/\Delta t$. A noise of 10 µm on a 100 µs sample gives 0.14 m/s of velocity noise, which is 7% of a 2 m/s signal. Filter, or fit a line over a few samples.

**Other methods.** A **pickup (search) coil** or a fixed coil with a moving magnet on the plunger produces $e = B\ell v$-type voltage proportional to velocity directly; **accelerometers** on the body (integrate or detect impact); **laser Doppler vibrometer**, **high-speed camera** and **laser triangulation** for lab characterisation.
:::

::: terms
- **Plunger (armature)**: the moving ferromagnetic part of the solenoid.
- **Stroke**: the travel distance of the plunger.
- **Transit time**: time to travel the stroke or a defined segment.
- **Motional EMF**: $i\,\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x\,v$, the voltage due to plunger motion in a changing inductance.
- **Current signature (dip)**: the characteristic shape of coil current during pull-in.
- **LVDT**: linear variable differential transformer, a contactless linear position sensor ([[q0407]]).
- **Impact velocity**: speed at the end stop; sets noise and wear.
- **Search (pickup) coil**: an extra winding that senses flux change.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "use a position sensor and differentiate". An exceptional answer:

- Gives **two or three distinct methods** (transit timing, differentiation, coil-current signature) with their trade-offs.
- Writes the **coil equation** and identifies the motional EMF term.
- **Quantifies** with a number (stroke over transit time; noise amplification when differentiating).
- Knows that average velocity is not **impact velocity**.
- Explains **why the current dips** and how that is used for arrival detection, and notes calibration needs.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using a rotary encoder or tach on a solenoid.** There is no rotating shaft. Use linear position sensors or timing.
- **Differentiating raw position at high sample rates.** Noise dominates. Filter or fit.
- **Treating average velocity as impact velocity.** The plunger accelerates; the end-of-stroke speed can be several times the average (in the example below about 2.7 m/s versus 0.7 m/s).
- **Expecting the current dip to appear with any drive.** With a hard current-source drive there is no dip, the voltage rises instead. The signature appears under voltage drive.
- **Ignoring that $\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x$ changes with position and temperature.** Calibrate the signature for each solenoid, supply voltage and temperature.
- **Forgetting eddy currents.** In solid steel plungers eddy currents slow flux and distort the signature.
:::

::: deep
## What is physically moving

A solenoid is an electromagnet with a movable ferromagnetic plunger. When current flows, flux pulls the plunger in to reduce the air gap. The inductance rises as the gap closes, because the magnetic path gets better. That is why the current flowing into a solenoid is not a simple RL charge curve: the inductance changes during the stroke.

Energy view: with flux linkage $\lambda = L(x)\,i$,
$$v_c = iR + \frac{\mathrm{d}\lambda}{\mathrm{d}t} = iR + L\frac{\mathrm{d}i}{\mathrm{d}t} + i\frac{\mathrm{d}L}{\mathrm{d}x}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}$$
The first term is resistive, the second is the usual inductor term and the third is **speed voltage**, the same physics as motor back-EMF but for a linear actuator.

## Method 1: transit timing

Place two optical interrupters or Hall switches a distance $d$ apart near the end of stroke. A timer in input-capture mode records both edges:
$$v = \frac{d}{t_2 - t_1}$$
Example: gates 2 mm apart at the end of the stroke see $\Delta t \approx 0.87$ ms in the simulation, giving $v \approx 2.3$ m/s. That is the average over the last 2 mm, still about 15% below the 2.7 m/s impact speed because the plunger is accelerating hard right up to the stop. A 1 µs timer gives 0.1% resolution; sensor position tolerance (say ±0.1 mm on a 2 mm baseline, 5%) is the real error source, so longer baselines are better when the plunger velocity does not change much over the baseline.

The cheapest version has a single end-of-stroke sensor: *transit time from the command edge to the arrival edge* gives a figure of merit. A rising transit time warns of wear, dirt or low supply voltage.

## Method 2: differentiate a position sensor

A linear Hall sensor reading a magnet on the plunger, an LVDT or a linear optical encoder strip gives $x(t)$. Differentiating gives $v$. With a sample interval $\Delta t$ and position noise $\sigma_x$, the derived velocity noise is about
$$\sigma_v \approx \frac{\sqrt2\,\sigma_x}{\Delta t}$$
For $\sigma_x = 10\ \mu$m and $\Delta t = 100\ \mu$s: 0.14 m/s. Use a least-squares fit over 5 to 10 samples, or a Kalman/observer using the force model.

## Method 3: the coil as the sensor

Because the voltage equation contains the speed voltage, a measurement of $i(t)$ (with a shunt, see [[q0395]]) under constant-voltage drive contains the motion. Signal processing options:

- **Detect the dip**: find the local minimum of $i(t)$ or the zero crossing of $\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$ after the peak. Its time is the arrival time. Missing dip means the plunger did not move or stalled.
- **Dip depth / area**: correlates with impact velocity once calibrated.
- **Observer**: using a model of $L(x)$ and the force law, estimate $x$ and $v$ from $v_c$ and $i$ (self-sensing). Requires a calibrated model.

Constraints: you need enough current to see the dip (a low supply voltage gives a slow plunger and a shallow dip); PWM drive makes the current sampling harder (sample at a fixed point in the PWM cycle); and the shape changes with temperature (coil resistance rises about 0.39% per kelvin for copper) and supply voltage.

At arrival the current rate of change flips sign: it is the same phenomenon exploited by "plunger arrival detection" features in some solenoid-valve and relay drivers (check specific driver datasheets for how they implement it).

## Method 4: additional ways, mostly lab

- **Pickup coil**: a few turns around the stroke path with a magnet on the plunger produce $e \propto v$.
- **Accelerometer** on the body: the impact shows up as a spike; integrating acceleration drifts, so it is only used for event timing.
- **Laser displacement or Doppler vibrometer**; **high-speed camera** (at 10,000 fps the frame interval is 100 µs, giving a position sample every 0.2 mm at 2 m/s).

## Which method to choose

| Need | Method | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Average speed, pass/fail | transit time via end-of-stroke sensor | very low |
| Impact velocity | two gates near end, or position sensor + fit | low to medium |
| Continuous profile | LVDT / linear Hall + filter | medium |
| No added sensor | coil current signature | firmware only; calibration |
| Lab characterisation | high-speed camera, laser | high |

::: applications
- **Fuel injectors and gas valves**: current-signature detection of the opening and closing events to adjust timing.
- **Door locks and latches**: confirm bolt travel with the current dip or a Hall switch.
- **Industrial pneumatic and hydraulic valves**: Hall or inductive sensors on the spool to monitor response times.
- **Relays and contactors (a solenoid-like actuator)**: coil-current signature to monitor wear.
- **Pinball and vending actuators**: end-of-stroke sensing for timing and jam detection.
- **Test benches**: high-speed camera and laser displacement for characterising designs.

::: assumptions
- The question says "speed of a solenoid": I interpret it as **plunger (armature) velocity**, since a solenoid has no rotation. A *rate-of-actuation* (cycles per second) reading is covered in [[q0415]].
- The current-dip method assumes **voltage drive** and a coil whose inductance changes with position. For a **current-regulated** drive the controller changes the voltage instead; the voltage signal carries the information.
- The simulated numbers are from an illustrative lumped model; do not read them as typical.
- **Saturation and eddy currents** change the waveform; calibrate on the real part.
- Differentiation noise estimates assume white noise on the position sensor.

::: followups
### Why does the coil current dip when the plunger moves?
Because moving the plunger changes the flux linkage. The term $i\,\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x\,v$ is a voltage that opposes the supply, so the voltage left to raise the current ($v_c - iR - e_m$) shrinks and $\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$ goes negative. Once the plunger stops, $e_m$ disappears and the current climbs again to $V/R$.

### How would you measure impact velocity without any extra sensor?
Calibrate the current signature: record current waveforms while measuring the true impact velocity with a camera or laser, and fit the dip depth or the post-dip slope against velocity. In the product, measure the current and map the feature to velocity. Re-calibrate across supply voltage and temperature.

### You use two Hall sensors 4 mm apart and see a transit time of 2 ms. What's the velocity, and what limits accuracy?
$v = 4\text{ mm}/2\text{ ms} = 2$ m/s. Accuracy is limited by the sensors' switching-point tolerance and temperature drift (a ±0.2 mm error on 4 mm is 5%), by the plunger's acceleration over the baseline (you get an average) and by timer resolution (negligible at 1 µs).

### The current dip disappears. What could be wrong?
Either the plunger isn't moving (jam, too little supply voltage, too much load, a stuck spool) or it moved too slowly for the speed voltage to show, or the drive changed (a current-limited supply flattens the signature). Check supply, mechanical freedom, and whether PWM is distorting your sampling.

### Can you tell from the current whether the solenoid is hot?
Yes, partly: the final current $V/R$ falls as the coil warms (copper's resistance rises about 0.39% per kelvin). A coil that rises from 25 °C to 105 °C has 31% more resistance, so a 2 A final current becomes 1.5 A. That also lowers force, so hot solenoids pull in more slowly; compensate with voltage or PWM.
:::
