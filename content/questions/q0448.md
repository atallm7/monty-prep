::: basics
### In plain words
A MOSFET that is fully on behaves like a small resistor, called $R_{DS(on)}$. So if you measure the voltage across its two power pins (drain and source) while it conducts, you can compute the current, $I = V_{DS} / R_{DS(on)}$. This adds no extra resistor, no extra loss and no extra parts, which is why it is popular for overcurrent protection. It is not very accurate, because $R_{DS(on)}$ changes a lot with temperature.

### Picture it
Imagine a pipe that already has a valve in it, and the valve is fully open but still slightly narrow. You can read the small pressure drop across that valve to estimate the flow, with no new part in the pipe. The estimate is rough: when the valve warms up it swells and narrows, so the same pressure drop now means less flow. Also, just after the valve opens the water sloshes, so you wait a moment before trusting the reading.

### Words you need
- **$R_{DS(on)}$**: the resistance between drain and source when the FET is fully on. See [[q0095]].
- **Triode region**: the operating region where the FET acts like a resistor.
- **Blanking time**: a short delay after turn-on during which the reading is ignored.
- **Body diode**: the diode inside the FET, which can carry current while the FET is off.
- **Comparator**: a circuit that flips its output when a voltage crosses a set threshold.

### The simplest example
A FET has $R_{DS(on)} = 5$ mΩ at 25 °C. At 30 A the drain-source voltage is $30 \times 0.005 = 150$ mV. Set a comparator at 150 mV and it trips at 30 A when cold. At 125 °C the resistance rises by about 1.68 times, to 8.4 mΩ, so the same 150 mV is reached at only $0.15 / 0.0084 = 17.8$ A. The threshold slides with temperature. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the other limits (part spread, gate drive, blanking, small signal against comparator offset), setting a threshold against the spread, and fixes such as temperature compensation.
:::

Whether you can treat a MOSFET as a **resistor with a known but imperfect value** and reason through what that implies: $V_{DS} = I_D R_{DS(on)}$ only while the FET is fully on, $R_{DS(on)}$ moves with temperature and gate drive, and part-to-part spread makes absolute accuracy poor.

The interviewer wants the equation, the use case (lossless overcurrent protection), and a balanced list of pros and cons with numbers, plus practical details like blanking time and what happens when the FET is off or the body diode conducts.
:::

::: why
Lossless current sensing is attractive in every power stage that already has a MOSFET in the current path: buck converters, motor drivers, load switches, eFuses. Many gate-driver and controller ICs include a "$V_{DS}$ monitor" or "desaturation" comparator precisely because it adds no loss, no part, and reacts in hundreds of nanoseconds. Knowing its limits (±20 to 40 percent accuracy, temperature dependence) tells you when it's the right tool, protection, and when it isn't, metering. It's the "free" row in the comparison of [[q0442]].
:::

::: concepts
- **MOSFET triode region**: with $V_{GS}$ well above threshold and small $V_{DS}$, the FET behaves like a resistor, $I_D = V_{DS}/R_{DS(on)}$. See [[q0095]].
- **$R_{DS(on)}$ temperature dependence**: channel mobility falls with temperature so $R_{DS(on)}$ rises, roughly 40 to 100 percent between 25 °C and 125 °C depending on device.
- **Gate drive level**: $R_{DS(on)}$ depends on $V_{GS}$; a weakly enhanced FET has higher resistance.
- **Blanking**: switching transitions and ringing corrupt $V_{DS}$; sense only after settling.
- **Body diode**: when the channel is off or current is reversed, $V_{DS}$ follows the diode, not $R_{DS(on)}$.
- **Comparator thresholds**: protection compares $V_{DS}$ against a set voltage. See [[q0455]].
:::

"The idea is that a MOSFET that's fully on is basically a small resistor, $R_{DS(on)}$. So if I measure the drain-source voltage while it's conducting, $I = V_{DS}/R_{DS(on)}$. There's nothing to add to the circuit and no extra loss, because the FET is already in the current path.

The cons are accuracy and conditions. $R_{DS(on)}$ rises a lot with temperature, perhaps 50 percent or more at 125 degrees, and varies part to part, so my threshold might correspond to 20 amps cold and 14 amps hot. It only works when the FET is fully on, so I have to blank the switching edges, and during the off-time or when the body diode conducts the reading is meaningless. So it's great for protection, where plus or minus 30 percent is acceptable, and not for precise measurement unless I calibrate and compensate for temperature."

::: technical
**Principle.** In the triode (ohmic) region, for small $V_{DS}$:
$$I_D \approx \frac{V_{DS}}{R_{DS(on)}(T, V_{GS})}$$
so the sensed current is $\hat I = V_{DS}/R_{nom}$, with an error equal to the fractional deviation of the real $R_{DS(on)}$ from the assumed $R_{nom}$.

**Temperature.** $R_{DS(on)}(T)\approx R_{25}\,(T_K/298.15)^{n}$ with $n\approx1.5$ to $2.3$ for silicon power MOSFETs (an illustrative model; the datasheet gives a normalized curve). With $n = 1.8$: factor 1.39 at 85 °C, 1.68 at 125 °C, 1.88 at 150 °C.

**Example.** $R_{25} = 5$ mΩ, $I = 30$ A: $V_{DS} = 150$ mV at 25 °C, but 252 mV at 125 °C. A fixed 150 mV trip threshold therefore trips at 30 A cold and only about **17.8 A** at 125 °C.

**Part-to-part.** Datasheets give typical and maximum $R_{DS(on)}$, often max ≈ 1.2 to 1.5× typical, so the same threshold trips from 23 A (max-spec part) to 30 A (typical part) at 25 °C.

**Conditions.** Valid only if (1) the FET is fully enhanced ($V_{GS}$ at its rated drive level), (2) $V_{DS}$ is sensed after switching transients settle (blanking, typically 100 ns to a few µs), (3) current flows in the forward direction through the channel.

**Common-mode (high-side).** For a high-side FET the drain sits at the bus, so the sense comparator needs a high-voltage clamp or level shifter; for low-side it is ground-referenced.
:::

::: long
"A power MOSFET that's fully turned on acts like a small resistor, its $R_{DS(on)}$, which for a power part might be a few milliohms. So if I measure the drain-source voltage while it conducts, I can compute the current as $V_{DS}$ divided by $R_{DS(on)}$. In circuit terms, I just put a comparator across the FET with a threshold, and when $V_{DS}$ exceeds the threshold I declare overcurrent and shut off. That's exactly how desaturation or $V_{DS}$ monitoring works in many gate drivers and motor driver ICs.

The advantages are significant. There's no sense resistor, so no added loss, no voltage drop, no board space, and no cost. Response is fast, within a few hundred nanoseconds to a microsecond after blanking. And it senses the FET's own current, so it protects the device that actually matters.

The disadvantages are all about accuracy and conditions. $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature, around 50 to 70 percent by 125 degrees for many parts, so a fixed threshold corresponds to a lower current when hot. With a 5 milliohm FET and a 150 millivolt threshold, I trip at 30 amps cold but about 18 amps hot. It also varies unit to unit by 20 to 30 percent, and depends on gate drive voltage. It only works when the FET is on, so I need blanking after each turn-on to ignore ringing, and I get nothing during the off-time. With a low $R_{DS(on)}$ the signal is small, so comparator offset becomes an error. And on the high-side, the comparator must handle the bus voltage.

So I'd use it for overcurrent protection and cycle-by-cycle limiting where ±30 percent is fine, and use a shunt, with temperature compensation or calibration, whenever I need an actual measurement."
:::

::: terms
- **$R_{DS(on)}$**: drain-source resistance when the MOSFET is fully enhanced.
- **Triode (linear/ohmic) region**: operating region where $I_D$ is roughly proportional to $V_{DS}$.
- **$V_{DS}$ monitoring / desaturation detection**: comparing drain-source voltage against a threshold to detect overcurrent.
- **Blanking time**: interval after turn-on during which the sense output is ignored to avoid switching transients.
- **Body diode**: the intrinsic diode from source to drain; conducts in reverse, clamping $V_{DS}$ near $-0.7$ V.
- **Temperature coefficient of $R_{DS(on)}$**: positive for silicon MOSFETs, about 0.4 to 0.8 percent per °C.
- **SenseFET**: a MOSFET with a small mirrored section for scaled current sensing ([[q0439]]).
- **Leading-edge blanking**: blanking window at the start of each switching cycle.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating $R_{DS(on)}$ as a fixed number.** It depends on temperature, $V_{GS}$ and the part's tolerance.
- **Sensing during switching transitions.** $V_{DS}$ rings and overshoots; without blanking you get false trips.
- **Using it in the off state or with body-diode conduction.** $V_{DS}$ then reflects the diode drop (about 0.7 V), not current in the channel.
- **Ignoring the comparator's offset.** A 5 mV offset on a 5 mΩ FET is a 1 A error.
- **Calling it "lossless sensing" and stopping there.** The FET's own conduction loss is the same with or without sensing; what's saved is the extra resistor.
- **Applying it to a FET operating in saturation.** If $V_{DS}$ is large (desaturation in a short circuit), the relation is no longer $I = V/R$, which is in fact what desaturation detection exploits as a *fault* indicator, not a measurement.
:::

::: deep
## The model

A MOSFET with enough gate drive operates in its **triode region** at low $V_{DS}$:

$$I_D = k\left[(V_{GS}-V_{th})V_{DS} - \tfrac12V_{DS}^2\right]\approx k(V_{GS}-V_{th})V_{DS}$$

for $V_{DS}\ll V_{GS}-V_{th}$. This is a resistor with

$$R_{DS(on)} = \frac{1}{k(V_{GS}-V_{th})}$$

For power MOSFETs, the datasheet gives $R_{DS(on)}$ (typical and maximum) at specified $V_{GS}$ (such as 4.5 V and 10 V) and a plot of normalized $R_{DS(on)}$ versus junction temperature.

## When it works and when it doesn't

1. **Only while on.** During the off time $V_{DS}\approx V_{bus}$ (no information about current through the channel). After turn-on, $V_{DS}$ takes some time to settle: it starts at $V_{bus}$ and falls to $I R_{DS(on)}$ with ringing from parasitic inductance. So the sense output is **blanked** for typically 100 ns to a few µs.
2. **Gate drive must be fully on.** At low $V_{GS}$ the resistance is higher. If $V_{GS}$ sags (bootstrap droop, weak driver), the reading is biased high.
3. **Forward conduction only.** In a synchronous buck's low-side FET after the switch node falls, the current may flow in reverse (during light-load or negative-current operation): then $V_{DS}$ is negative and the reading corresponds to $-I R_{DS(on)}$ if the FET is on, or the body diode drop if off.
4. **Small signal.** A 2 mΩ FET at 20 A is only 40 mV. A comparator with 5 mV offset is a 2.5 A error (12 percent). Hence protection thresholds are set loosely.

## Desaturation and short-circuit protection

For very high short-circuit currents the FET leaves the triode region and enters saturation, where $V_{DS}$ rises toward the bus voltage even though the channel is fully on. Gate drivers exploit this: a $V_{DS}$ comparator set a few volts above the normal on-state drop trips when the FET "desaturates", signalling a short without needing a precise current number. This is the same circuit as the one above with a larger threshold, used for IGBTs (as $V_{CE}$) and MOSFETs.

## Compensation and calibration

Ways to improve accuracy:

- **Thermistor-based threshold shift**: raise $V_{th}$ with temperature to follow $R_{DS(on)}$.
- **Digital correction**: measure $V_{DS}$ with an ADC and the die/board temperature, compute $I = V_{DS}/R(T)$.
- **End-of-line calibration**: apply a known current and trim the threshold to cancel part spread.
- **Use a SenseFET or shunt** when accuracy is needed.

## Where else it appears

Peak and valley current mode controllers in buck converters use $V_{DS}$ of the low-side or high-side FET (see [[q0225]]), accepting ±15 to 25 percent limit accuracy. Load switches and eFuses report current through the same principle, often with a built-in $R_{DS(on)}$ calibration.

## Worked example: setting the threshold against the spread

Suppose the system can legitimately run at 15 A, and the protection must not trip below 20 % above that, so the lowest acceptable trip current anywhere in the spread is 18 A. The FET is the 5 mΩ part from the table. The worst case for a too-low trip is the hottest, highest-resistance device. At 125 °C the normalized factor is $(398.15/298.15)^{1.8} = 1.68$, so the typical resistance is 8.4 mΩ, and with the 1.3× maximum-to-typical spread it is 10.9 mΩ. The threshold needed is

$$V_{th} = 18\ \text{A}\times10.9\ \text{m}\Omega = 197\ \text{mV}$$

Now look at the other end. A cold, typical part at 5 mΩ trips at $0.197/0.005 = 39$ A. So the window between "worst-case low" and "worst-case high" is a factor of 2.2 (18 A to 39 A), purely from temperature and process spread. That number must be checked against the thing the protection is protecting. If the wiring, connector or FET safe operating area can't survive 39 A for the trip delay, then $R_{DS(on)}$ sensing alone isn't good enough and you need a shunt or a SenseFET, or temperature compensation. A comparator offset of 5 mV adds ±1 A to the cold threshold and about ±0.46 A to the hot one, minor next to the spread but not zero, and a reason to set the threshold at a larger voltage than the offset.

## Worked example: how long to blank after turn-on

The comparator must ignore the transition after the gate turns on. Suppose the driver supplies 10 V through a 10 Ω gate resistor, the Miller plateau is at 4.5 V and the gate-drain charge $Q_{gd}$ is 10 nC. During the plateau the gate current is $(10-4.5)/10 = 0.55$ A, and the drain voltage falls over $t = Q_{gd}/I_g = 10\text{ nC}/0.55\text{ A} = 18$ ns. The turn-on delay and the charge needed to reach the plateau come before that, and the drain node typically rings for a few more tens of nanoseconds in parasitic inductance. A blanking time of 100 to 300 ns, a few times this, is reasonable, but it also sets the minimum on-time at which the protection works: a pulse shorter than the blanking window is never checked. Raising the gate resistor slows the edge, reduces ringing and lengthens the blanking time, which is the usual trade-off with switching losses ([[q0225]]). For desaturation protection the same logic applies, but the blanking time is longer, often microseconds, because the threshold is a few volts.

## Self-heating closes the loop

The temperature in the table is the junction temperature, which is not the same as the board temperature. A 20 A load in 5 mΩ dissipates 2 W at 25 °C, more as it heats. With a junction-to-ambient resistance of 30 K/W and a 0.7 %/K resistance tempco, the junction settles about 100 K above ambient, as in the thermal-iteration example in [[q0397]]. So at 20 A in a 25 °C room the part is already near 125 °C, and the trip current is already near the hot column of the table. The right design temperature for the threshold is the junction temperature at the highest current the system will actually carry.

## Verifying on the bench

- **Measure $R_{DS(on)}$ in circuit:** with the gate fully driven, apply a few known DC currents through the FET (briefly, with a 4-wire probe of the drain and source pins) and compute $V_{DS}/I$. Compare with the datasheet. Repeat after a thermal soak, measuring case temperature, to build your own curve.
- **Trip threshold:** ramp the load current slowly with a programmable electronic load and record the current at which the fault line asserts. Do this at cold, room and hot conditions, and on several boards, to see the actual spread.
- **Blanking and false trips:** with the load disconnected, run the gate drive at the minimum on-time and check the fault output stays quiet. Then probe $V_{DS}$ at the FET pins with a short ground spring and confirm the ringing has died before the blanking window ends.
- **Short-circuit response:** with a current-limited supply and a sacrificial board, apply a hard short and measure the time from the short to the gate turning off. This is the figure that decides whether the FET survives.

::: applications
- **Gate drivers with $V_{DS}$/desaturation monitoring**: motor and inverter drivers trip on short circuits within a microsecond or two.
- **Synchronous buck converters**: valley current limit via low-side $R_{DS(on)}$ sensing.
- **Load switches and eFuses**: integrated FET with built-in current limit based on $R_{DS(on)}$ or a SenseFET mirror.
- **Battery protection ICs**: charge/discharge FET $V_{DS}$ used for overcurrent detection.
- **Automotive smart high-side switches**: current sense output based on the FET's own resistance, factory-calibrated.
- **Hot-swap controllers** using the pass FET's drop as a coarse current monitor.

::: followups
### Why does a $V_{DS}$ monitor need blanking time?
At turn-on, $V_{DS}$ starts at the bus voltage and falls through the transition, with ringing from parasitic inductance and the Miller effect. Without blanking the comparator sees a large voltage and falsely trips. Blanking (about 100 ns to a few µs, set by the controller or a comparator filter) ignores that period, at the cost of delaying protection for a short moment.

### How can you improve Rds(on)-sensing accuracy?
Compensate for temperature (a thermistor to adjust the threshold, or a temperature measurement and digital correction), calibrate at test, keep gate drive at the full specified level, and choose an FET whose $R_{DS(on)}$ is high enough to give a usable signal. If accuracy still isn't enough, use a SenseFET or shunt.

### What happens to the reading if the FET's gate drive droops?
$R_{DS(on)}$ rises because $V_{GS}-V_{th}$ falls, so for a given current $V_{DS}$ increases and the monitor reads *high*, tripping early. This is a safe failure direction for protection, but a bias for any measurement.

### Scenario: a motor driver trips falsely at start-up in the cold but never at room temperature. Why might this be?
Cold $R_{DS(on)}$ is lower, so the threshold is *less* sensitive when cold, not more, which doesn't explain the trips. More likely candidates are a cold weak gate-drive or bootstrap supply (low $V_{GS}$, higher $R_{DS(on)}$), a slower turn-on edge that outlasts the blanking time, or inrush/stall current that really is higher because the motor winding resistance is lower when cold ($I = V/R$). Check the gate waveform and the actual current with a scope probe.

### What if you need both protection and accurate metering from the same FET?
Use $V_{DS}$ sensing for the fast protection comparator, and a separate precise path (a small shunt, SenseFET, or a calibrated ADC reading of $V_{DS}$ with temperature compensation) for metering. That is the common two-path design.
