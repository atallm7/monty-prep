::: basics
### In plain words
I2C, SPI and UART are three ways for chips on a board to pass bytes to each other. All three send one bit at a time, which saves wires. They differ in how the receiver knows when to read each bit, and that choice sets their wire count, speed and how many devices they can serve. UART links two devices with no shared clock. SPI is fast but needs a select wire per device. I2C shares two wires among many devices.

### Picture it
Picture three ways of passing notes in a classroom. In UART, two friends agree beforehand on how fast to talk, and each keeps their own watch. In SPI, a teacher claps a steady beat that everyone follows, and each student has a private line to the teacher. In I2C, the whole class shares one party line, and each student answers to their own name. The analogy skips electrical details: I2C also needs pull-up resistors on its two shared wires.

### Words you need
- **Serial**: sending bits one after another on a single wire.
- **Clock**: a steady tick on its own wire that tells the receiver when to read each bit.
- **Baud rate**: how many bits per second both sides agreed to use. UART needs it because it has no clock wire.
- **Chip select**: a wire that wakes up one SPI device.
- **Address**: a number that picks one device on a shared I2C bus.

### The simplest example
Send one byte. UART adds a start bit and a stop bit, so it is 10 bits. At 115,200 baud, that is $10/115{,}200 \approx 86.8$ µs. SPI at 8 MHz sends the 8 bits in 8 clock ticks, which is 1 µs. I2C at 100 kHz takes 9 ticks per byte, 8 data bits plus an acknowledge, so 90 µs, plus another byte for the address. So SPI is fast, I2C is slower but uses only two wires. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows the wires and timing of each bus, with advice on which to choose.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can describe each of the three board-level serial buses **in terms of what is physically on the wires**, not just recite acronyms. A strong answer covers, for each protocol: how many wires, whether there is a shared clock, how a device is selected, which directions data can flow, and roughly how fast it goes. The interviewer is also listening for whether you know *why* three buses exist at all: each one is a different trade-off between pin count, speed and complexity. If you can say "UART is two devices with no clock, SPI is one wire per device but fast, I2C is two wires for many devices but slower", you have already separated yourself from people who only memorized the names.
:::

::: why
Almost every embedded board talks to something over one of these three: a temperature sensor on I2C, a flash chip or display on SPI, a GPS module or debug console on UART. Choosing the wrong one costs pins, speed or reliability, and debugging a dead bus is a daily task for a hardware engineer. Interviewers ask this as a warm-up because the answer reveals whether you have actually wired up a bus, or probed one with a scope or logic analyzer. It is also the entry point to deeper follow-ups (pull-ups, clock modes, baud error, arbitration), so a clean overview sets up the rest of the conversation.
:::

::: concepts
- **Serial vs parallel**: one bit at a time on each wire saves pins and avoids skew between wires, at the cost of needing a higher bit clock. See [[q0740]].
- **Clocking**: either a shared clock wire (synchronous: SPI, I2C) or each side keeping its own timer (asynchronous: UART). See [[q0741]].
- **Logic levels**: both ends must agree on what voltage means 1 and 0. See [[q0742]].
- **Push-pull vs open-drain outputs**: SPI and UART drive both high and low; I2C only pulls low and relies on a resistor to go high. See [[q0761]].
- **Addressing vs chip-select**: how one controller picks which device to talk to ([[q0746]], [[q0766]]).
- **Full vs half duplex**: whether data can flow in both directions at the same time.
:::

::: answer
"The three I'd name are UART, SPI and I2C. All of them are serial, so a byte goes out one bit at a time.

UART is the simplest. It's two wires plus ground, TX and RX, and it's point to point. There's no clock. Both sides agree on a baud rate ahead of time, the line idles high, a start bit pulls it low to wake the receiver, then you send the data bits, least significant first, and a stop bit puts it back high. The receiver samples in the middle of each bit using its own clock.

SPI is synchronous. The controller drives a clock, SCLK, and sends data out on MOSI while the device sends data back on MISO at the same time, so it's full duplex. Each device has its own chip-select line, and you pull it low to talk to that device. It's fast, tens of MHz is normal, but you pay one pin per extra device.

I2C uses just two wires, clock and data, shared by every device. Each device has a 7-bit address, the controller sends a start condition, the address, and a read or write bit, the addressed device acknowledges, then data bytes follow, each acknowledged. The lines are open-drain with pull-up resistors, so it's slower, typically 100 or 400 kHz, but you can hang lots of devices on two pins."
:::

::: technical
**UART (Universal Asynchronous Receiver/Transmitter).** Two signal wires (TX, RX) plus a common ground, point to point, full duplex. The line idles high. A frame is 1 start bit (low), 5 to 9 data bits (usually 8, LSB first), an optional parity bit, and 1 or 2 stop bits (high). For 8N1 that is 10 bit times per byte, so the maximum payload rate is 80% of the baud rate. At 115200 baud: 11,520 bytes/s, 86.8 µs per byte. There is no clock wire: the receiver detects the falling edge of the start bit, then samples each bit near its centre, typically using a 16× (or 8×) internal oversampling clock. Transmitter and receiver clocks must match to within a few percent over one frame.

**SPI (Serial Peripheral Interface).** Four signals: SCLK, MOSI, MISO, and one chip-select (CS, active low) per target. The controller generates SCLK; each clock cycle shifts one bit out of the controller and one bit in from the target, so the two shift registers form a ring and every transfer is full duplex. CPOL sets the idle level of SCLK and CPHA picks which edge samples vs shifts, giving four modes (0 to 3) that both sides must match. Outputs are push-pull, so edges are fast and clock rates are limited by the chip and trace, commonly 1 to 50 MHz, sometimes more. No addressing and no acknowledge: if nothing is connected, MISO just reads back garbage or all ones.

**I2C (Inter-Integrated Circuit).** Two wires, SCL and SDA, both open-drain with pull-up resistors, shared by all devices. Transactions begin with a START (SDA falls while SCL is high), then a 7-bit address and a R/W bit, then a 9th clock where the receiver ACKs by pulling SDA low. Data bytes follow, each followed by an ACK, until the controller issues STOP (SDA rises while SCL is high). Standard speeds: 100 kHz (Standard), 400 kHz (Fast), 1 MHz (Fast-mode Plus), 3.4 MHz (High-speed). Half duplex: only one device drives SDA at a time.

| | UART | SPI | I2C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Signal wires | 2 (TX, RX) | 3 + 1 CS per target | 2 (shared) |
| Clock | none (async) | controller drives SCLK | controller drives SCL |
| Selection | point to point | chip-select pin | 7- or 10-bit address |
| Duplex | full | full | half |
| Typical rate | 9.6k to ~1M+ baud | 1 to 50+ MHz | 100k, 400k, 1 MHz |
| Output type | push-pull | push-pull | open-drain |
:::

::: short
"UART is two wires and no clock: start bit, data LSB first, stop bit, and both sides just agree on a baud rate. SPI is synchronous: the controller drives a clock, data goes out on MOSI and comes back on MISO at the same time, and each device gets its own chip-select, so it's fast but uses more pins. I2C is two shared open-drain wires, SCL and SDA, where each device has a 7-bit address and acknowledges every byte, so it scales to many devices but runs slower, typically 100 to 400 kHz."
:::

::: long
"They're the three workhorse serial buses on a board, and I'd explain them by what's on the wires.

Start with UART. It's two signal wires, TX and RX, plus ground, and it connects exactly two devices. There's no clock line, so both ends agree on a baud rate in advance. The line sits high when idle. To send a byte, the transmitter pulls the line low for one bit time, the start bit, then sends eight data bits, least significant bit first, and finishes with a stop bit that returns the line high. The receiver sees the falling edge, waits half a bit, and then samples once per bit using its own clock, usually oversampled sixteen times. That's why the two baud rates have to match within a couple of percent.

SPI adds a clock wire. The controller toggles SCLK, and on every clock it shifts one bit out on MOSI and one bit in on MISO, so it's full duplex by construction. A device only listens when its chip-select is pulled low. Because the outputs are push-pull, edges are fast and you can run at tens of megahertz, which is why flash memory, displays and ADCs use it. The cost is one extra pin per device, and there's no acknowledge, so you can't tell if anyone's there.

I2C goes the other way: two wires, SCL and SDA, shared by everybody. Every device has an address. The controller sends START, a 7-bit address plus a read-write bit, and the device that recognises its address pulls SDA low for the ninth clock as an ACK. Then data bytes follow, each ACKed, and a STOP ends it. The lines are open-drain with pull-up resistors, which allows multiple devices on one bus safely but limits speed to about 100 kHz to 1 MHz. So: UART for point to point links, SPI for speed, I2C for lots of slow devices on few pins."
:::

::: terms
- **Serial**: bits sent one after another on one wire, as opposed to a parallel bus with one wire per bit.
- **Baud rate**: symbols per second on the line. For UART with two-level signaling it equals bits per second.
- **Controller / target**: modern names for master / slave. Older datasheets and interview lists still say master and slave, and the two are identical in meaning.
- **SCLK / SCL**: the serial clock, driven by the controller in SPI and I2C.
- **MOSI / MISO**: controller-out-target-in and controller-in-target-out data lines in SPI.
- **Chip select (CS / SS)**: active-low pin that enables one SPI target.
- **Start / stop bit**: UART's framing bits (low then high), or I2C's START and STOP conditions (SDA edges while SCL is high). They are different things with similar names.
- **Open-drain**: an output that can only pull the line low; a pull-up resistor makes it high. Used by I2C.
- **ACK**: the receiver's "I got that" bit, SDA pulled low on the 9th clock in I2C.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists the pin names: "UART has TX and RX, SPI has MOSI, MISO, SCK and CS, I2C has SDA and SCL."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains each bus in terms of **what the receiver uses to know when to sample**: UART its own oversampled clock after a start edge, SPI and I2C the clock wire.
- States the **scaling story**: UART is point to point, SPI needs one CS per target, I2C needs only two wires but needs unique addresses.
- Mentions **open-drain plus pull-ups** for I2C and why that sets its speed limit.
- Gives **real numbers**: 8N1 is 10 bits per byte so 115200 baud is about 11.5 kB/s, and I2C needs 9 clocks per byte.
- Ends with **when to choose which**, for example "flash and display on SPI, a pile of sensors and EEPROM on I2C, GPS or console on UART".
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying UART is "slow"**. It is only limited by the baud rate the two sides agree on; 1 to 3 Mbaud is common on modern parts. What it lacks is multi-device addressing, not speed.
- **Calling a UART's start/stop bits "the same" as I2C's START/STOP**. UART frames every byte with a start and stop bit; I2C has one START and one STOP around a whole multi-byte transaction.
- **Forgetting SPI is full duplex by construction.** Every clock shifts a bit in *and* out; "read-only" transfers are really "write dummy bytes, keep the reply".
- **Claiming I2C has chip-selects.** It has addresses carried in-band on the same two wires.
- **Saying SPI has an ACK.** It does not, so a missing device looks like a floating or all-ones MISO. See [[q0738]].
- **Mixing up TX/RX wiring.** TX connects to the *other* device's RX; wiring TX to TX is the classic first-bring-up bug.
:::

::: deep
## Three answers to one problem

All three buses solve the same problem: move bytes between chips using as few pins as possible. They differ in *how the receiver knows when each bit is valid*, and everything else (speed, pin count, wiring rules) follows from that choice.

| Method | How the receiver finds the bit | Used by |
|---|---|---|
| Separate clock wire | Sample on a clock edge supplied by the controller | SPI, I2C |
| Agreed timing + start edge | Detect start edge, then use local timer | UART |

```timing
{"signals":[{"name":"TX","wave":"101000001011"},{"name":"field","wave":"=3========4=","data":["idle","start","D0","D1","D2","D3","D4","D5","D6","D7","stop","idle"]}],"hscale":1.4}
```
Figure: The byte 0x41 ("A", binary 01000001) sent LSB first at 8N1. The data bits read 1,0,0,0,0,0,1,0 left to right, which is the value reversed. The falling edge of the start bit is the only timing reference the receiver gets.

The receiver sees the line fall, waits half a bit time to land in the middle of the start bit, confirms it is still low (rejecting glitches), then samples every bit time after that. Because the sampling instants are set by the receiver's own oscillator, any rate error accumulates across the frame. The stop bit, the last bit the receiver checks, is sampled 9.5 bit times after the start edge, and the sample point must stay within ±0.5 bit of the true centre, so the *ideal* total mismatch limit is $0.5/9.5 \approx 5.3\%$. Real receivers also have edge jitter and sampling granularity, so budgets of about ±2% to ±3% in total are the usual design target. More on this in [[q0778]] and [[q0779]].

```timing
{"signals":[{"name":"CS","wave":"100000000000000001"},{"name":"SCLK","wave":"001010101010101010"},{"name":"MOSI","wave":"z1.0.1.0.0.1.0.1.z"},{"name":"MISO","wave":"z0.0.1.1.1.1.0.0.z"}],"hscale":0.9}
```
Figure: SPI mode 0, controller sends 0xA5 (10100101) while the target returns 0x3C (00111100), both MSB first. Data changes while SCLK is low and is sampled on the rising edge, so setup and hold are built into the clock shape. Both bytes move in the same 8 clocks.

Inside each chip, the data path is just a shift register. The controller's register and the target's register are connected in a ring (MOSI one way, MISO the other), and each clock rotates the ring by one bit. After 8 clocks the two bytes have swapped places. That is why SPI has no concept of "read" vs "write" at the wire level: a read is a write of dummy bytes where you care about the reply.

## Putting numbers on it

Moving 6 bytes of sensor data (read from a register on a target):

- **I2C at 400 kHz**: address+W, register, repeated START, address+R, 6 data bytes is 9 bytes at 9 clocks each = 81 clocks, about 202 µs (not counting START/STOP). At 100 kHz it is 810 µs.
- **SPI at 8 MHz**: 1 command byte + 6 data bytes = 56 clocks = 7 µs.
- **UART at 115200 8N1**: 6 bytes = 60 bit times = 521 µs, but there's no register read: you would need a protocol on top.

> [!TIP]
> Always compute throughput from clocks-per-byte, not from the headline clock rate. I2C spends 9 clocks per byte plus address overhead; UART spends 10 bit times per byte; SPI spends exactly 8 clocks per byte.

## When to pick which

- **UART**: console/debug, GPS, Bluetooth and cellular modules, anything point to point that already speaks a text or packet protocol. Few pins, long-ish cables with RS-232/RS-485 transceivers.
- **SPI**: anything bandwidth-hungry or latency-sensitive: flash, SD cards, ADCs/DACs, displays, IMUs at high sample rates.
- **I2C**: low-speed housekeeping with many chips: temperature sensors, EEPROMs, PMICs, RTCs, GPIO expanders, battery gauges.

The follow-on question is usually "how do you actually connect them?" which is covered per-bus in [[q0767]], [[q0774]] and [[q0754]].
:::

::: applications
- **Debug console**: a UART at 115200 baud from an MCU to a USB-serial bridge prints boot logs.
- **GPS/GNSS module**: typically streams NMEA text over UART at 9600 baud or faster.
- **Serial flash and SD cards**: SPI (often quad SPI for execute-in-place) for fast block reads.
- **Environmental sensors** such as temperature/humidity parts: commonly I2C so a handful share two MCU pins.
- **EEPROMs and RTCs**: typical I2C parts; the 24-series EEPROMs are the textbook I2C slave.
- **TFT/OLED displays**: small OLEDs often use I2C or SPI; larger TFTs use SPI or parallel for refresh rate.
:::

::: assumptions
- The question is about **board-level, single-ended serial buses** found on MCUs. Other valid "three common protocols" lists exist (for example CAN, USB, Ethernet), but the parenthetical names I2C, SPI and UART settle the intent.
- Speeds quoted are **typical**; actual limits come from the chip's datasheet, the trace length and, for I2C, the bus capacitance and pull-up value ([[q0756]]).
- "UART" here means the logic-level peripheral. RS-232, RS-485 and RS-422 are *electrical* standards that carry the same UART frames with different voltage levels, covered in [[q0743]].
- Some parts support 3-wire SPI (shared MOSI/MISO), dual or quad SPI, or open-drain I2C-like modes. The basic four-wire SPI description still holds.
- I2C is described with 7-bit addressing; 10-bit addressing exists ([[q0749]]).
:::

::: followups
### Which one would you pick for a board with eight sensors and a very tight pin budget?
I2C. Two MCU pins serve every sensor as long as addresses are unique. I'd check that the sensors' addresses don't collide (or use address-select pins), that the total bus capacitance stays under 400 pF, and that the pull-ups are sized for the speed I need. If two sensors have the same fixed address I'd use an I2C multiplexer. See [[q0747]] and [[q0750]].

### Why can SPI run so much faster than I2C?
SPI uses push-pull drivers that actively drive both rising and falling edges. I2C uses a resistor to pull the line up, so its rise time is an RC charge (about $0.85\,R_pC_b$ from 30% to 70%) and that sets the clock ceiling. With 4.7 kΩ and 100 pF the rise is about 400 ns, which already fails the 300 ns Fast-mode limit. See [[q0739]].

### How do you add a fifth SPI device when the controller only has four chip-select pins?
Use a spare GPIO as an extra chip-select (CS is just a GPIO the software toggles), or a decoder like a 74HC138 to turn 3 pins into 8 selects, or daisy-chain compatible parts so they share one CS. See [[q0768]].

### The UART link works at 9600 but garbles at 115200. What do you check?
Baud mismatch first: with a 16 MHz clock and integer dividers, the achievable 115200 rate may be off by 2 to 3%, which is borderline. Then edge quality (long wire or high capacitance rounding the edges), common ground, and logic-level compatibility. Switching to a crystal-based clock or fractional baud generator usually fixes the first one.

### What if two I2C devices on the bus have the same address and you cannot change either?
Put them on different channels of an I2C multiplexer (for example a 1-to-8 switch part), or use two separate I2C peripherals on the MCU. You can't get two devices with the same address to coexist on one segment, because both would ACK and drive data simultaneously.
:::
