::: testing
Whether you can design from requirements to component values and defend each choice. The interviewer is looking for the three-part core (input buffer, switch, hold capacitor with output buffer), then **sizing with numbers**: $C_H$ from $kT/C$ noise, droop and charge injection; switch resistance from the settling requirement $t_{acq}\ge\tau\ln2^{N+1}$; amplifier requirements (bandwidth, slew, bias current); and clock timing and jitter. A good answer also names the traps (charge injection, signal-dependent $R_{on}$, feedthrough) and the standard fixes (dummy switch, bootstrapping, transmission gate), and ends with how you would test it.
:::

::: why
S/H design is where the analog theory of an ADC meets real component limits. Even if you never design one on silicon, you will choose the buffer and the RC filter in front of a SAR ADC, which is exactly the same problem: size the capacitor and resistor so that settling, noise and droop all meet a spec ([[q0818]]). It also tests whether you can run a design calculation under interview pressure, which is the pattern behind most "design X" questions.
:::

::: concepts
- **Track/hold operation and errors**: [[q0814]], [[q0815]].
- **Settling**: $t_{acq}=\tau\ln2^{N+1}$ with $\tau=R_{tot}C_H$; see [[q0817]] for the trade-offs.
- **$kT/C$ noise**: sampled thermal noise $\sqrt{kT/C_H}$.
- **MOSFET as a switch**: $R_{on}=1/[\mu C_{ox}(W/L)(V_{GS}-V_t)]$, and the channel charge it injects.
- **Op-amp buffer**: unity-gain follower, rules of an ideal op-amp ([[q0124]], [[q0125]]).
- **Aperture jitter**: sampling-clock timing error converts to amplitude error.
:::

::: answer
"I'd start with the spec. Say it's a 12-bit S/H for a 0 to 3.3 V signal up to 100 kHz, sampling at 1 MS/s, so I've got a microsecond per sample: about 0.4 µs of tracking and 0.6 µs of hold for the ADC.

Architecture: an input buffer to drive the capacitor, an NMOS or transmission-gate switch, a hold capacitor to ground, and an output buffer with a MOSFET input so it doesn't load the cap.

Hold capacitor: kT/C noise should be well below the 233 µV of quantization noise, say 20 µV, which needs about 10 pF; that also keeps droop at 1 nA to 0.06 mV over the hold. Bigger is better for noise and droop, but slower to charge.

Switch: I need to settle to half an LSB, which is about 9 time constants. In 0.4 µs that gives a time constant under 44 ns, so with 10 pF the total resistance can be up to 4 kΩ. I'd make the switch about 500 Ω to 1 kΩ, so settling takes about 45 to 90 ns with lots of margin. The switch resistance varies with input voltage, which causes distortion, so I'd use a transmission gate or a bootstrapped switch that keeps the gate drive constant.

Then the errors: charge injection, which I'd cancel with a half-size dummy switch, feedthrough, and clock jitter: for 100 kHz at 74 dB SNR I need under about 0.3 ns of jitter. I'd simulate it with a ramp and sine inputs, then check settling, droop, and linearity."
:::

::: technical
**Spec.** $N=12$, $V_{FS}=3.3$ V, $f_{in,max}=100$ kHz, $f_s=1$ MS/s (period 1 µs; track 0.4 µs, hold 0.6 µs).

**1. Hold capacitor.**
- Noise: target $\sqrt{kT/C_H}\le0.1\times233\text{ µV}=23$ µV (so it adds under 1% to the total noise power). $C_H\ge kT/v_n^2=4.14\times10^{-21}/(23\text{ µV})^2=7.8$ pF. Choose $C_H=10$ pF: $v_n=20.4$ µV rms.
- Droop: $I_{leak}=1$ nA, 0.6 µs hold: $\Delta V=1\text{ nA}\cdot0.6\text{ µs}/10\text{ pF}=0.06$ mV, 0.07 LSB. OK. At 85 °C with leakage about 64 times larger, 3.8 mV, 4.8 LSB, so a low-leakage (guarded) switch matters if the hot spec is real.
- Charge injection: $\Delta V=Q_{inj}/C_H$; 3 fC gives 0.3 mV (0.37 LSB). Cancel with a dummy switch.

**2. Switch and settling.** Required: $e^{-t_{acq}/\tau}\le2^{-13}$, i.e. $t_{acq}\ge9.01\tau$. With $t_{acq}=0.4$ µs, $\tau\le44$ ns, so $R_{tot}\le4.4$ kΩ at 10 pF. Take $R_{on}=1$ kΩ and a buffer output resistance of tens of ohms: $\tau\approx10$ ns, $t_{acq}=90$ ns, a 4× margin. For an NMOS with $\mu C_{ox}=200$ µA/V², $W/L=20$ and $V_{GS}-V_t=1.5$ V: $R_{on}=1/(200\text{ µ}\cdot20\cdot1.5)=167$ Ω (illustrative). For rail-to-rail inputs use a transmission gate (NMOS and PMOS in parallel) or a bootstrapped NMOS.

**3. Track bandwidth and distortion.** The track-mode low-pass has $f_{-3dB}=1/(2\pi\tau)=15.9$ MHz at $\tau=10$ ns, 160× the 100 kHz signal, so amplitude error is tiny ($\approx(f/f_{3dB})^2/2=2\times10^{-5}$) but if $R_{on}$ varies with the input then $\tau$ and so the phase/gain vary with the signal, creating harmonics. A bootstrapped switch holds $V_{GS}$ constant so that $R_{on}$ stays near-constant.

**4. Buffers.** Input buffer A1 must charge $C_H$ and settle within the track phase: peak current $\approx C_HV_{step}/t$; for a 3.3 V step on 10 pF in 40 ns that's 0.8 mA, and its gain-bandwidth should be at least $\sim5/(2\pi\cdot t_{settle})$ for several time constants. Output buffer A2: MOS/JFET input, bias current $\ll$ 1 nA, offset small (or cancel), able to drive the ADC input.

**5. Clock and jitter.** The sample instant is the opening edge. Max slope at 100 kHz, 1.65 V amplitude: $2\pi\cdot10^5\cdot1.65=1.04$ V/µs. One LSB of error needs $\Delta t=0.806\text{ mV}/1.04\text{ V/µs}=0.78$ ns. For an SNR of 74 dB from jitter alone, $\sigma_t=10^{-74/20}/(2\pi\cdot10^5)=0.32$ ns rms. Use a clean, buffered clock, non-overlapping phases if there are two switches.

**6. Verification.** Simulate step settling (to ½ LSB), droop at 85 °C, pedestal versus input, and FFT with a 99 kHz sine; measure THD and SNR; do Monte-Carlo for mismatch of the dummy switch.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Switch $R_{on}$, channel charge and leakage values depend on the process and device sizing. The numbers above illustrate the method; a real design sizes the switch from the foundry's model.
:::

::: long
"For a design question I'd begin with the spec, so say it's a 12-bit sample-and-hold for a 0 to 3.3 V signal up to 100 kHz at 1 megasample per second. That gives me a microsecond per sample, which I'd split into about 0.4 µs tracking and 0.6 µs hold for the ADC to convert.

The architecture is the standard one: an input buffer that drives the switch so the source isn't loaded, an MOS switch, a hold capacitor to ground, and an output buffer with a MOSFET input so it won't discharge the capacitor.

Let me size it. The capacitor first: kT/C noise has to be small compared with the 233 microvolts of quantization noise. If I want about 20 microvolts, I need around 10 picofarads. At 1 nanoamp of leakage, the droop over 0.6 microseconds is 0.06 millivolts, under a tenth of an LSB, so that works too.

Now the switch. In 12 bits I need to settle to half an LSB, which is about nine time constants. In 0.4 microseconds that means a time constant under 44 nanoseconds, so total resistance under about 4 kilohms with 10 picofarads. I'd aim for about 1 kilohm, which settles in 90 nanoseconds. The catch is that a MOSFET's on-resistance depends on the input voltage, which creates distortion, so I'd use a transmission gate or, better, a bootstrapped switch that holds the gate-source voltage constant.

Then the non-idealities: charge injection when the switch opens, which I'd cancel with a half-size dummy switch driven by the opposite clock, and feedthrough, which I'd cut with a series-shunt arrangement. For timing, a 100 kHz full-scale sine at 12 bits can tolerate only about 0.3 nanoseconds of clock jitter, so the clock must be clean.

Finally I'd verify with a step to check settling, a hot corner for droop, and an FFT with a near-Nyquist sine to measure distortion."
:::

::: terms
- **Bootstrapped switch**: a switch whose gate is driven to $V_{in}+V_{DD}$, holding $V_{GS}$ constant so $R_{on}$ doesn't vary with the signal.
- **Transmission gate**: parallel NMOS and PMOS; conducts rail-to-rail with flatter $R_{on}$.
- **Dummy switch**: half-size switch with opposite clock that absorbs the main switch's channel charge.
- **Channel charge**: charge in the MOS inversion layer, $WLC_{ox}(V_{GS}-V_t)$, released when the switch turns off.
- **Clock feedthrough**: gate-drain capacitance coupling the clock edge onto the hold node.
- **Settling time**: time for the track-phase output to reach within the error band.
- **Acquisition time**: same, specified for a full-scale step.
- **Aperture jitter**: random variation in the sampling instant.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws a switch, a capacitor and an op-amp.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Begins with **requirements** and a **timing budget** (track versus hold).
- **Sizes $C_H$** from three constraints (noise, droop, charge injection) with numbers, and **sizes $R_{on}$** from the settling relation.
- Identifies **signal-dependent $R_{on}$** as the distortion source and offers a transmission gate or bootstrapping.
- Cancels **charge injection** with a dummy switch or a differential design, and explains bottom-plate sampling.
- Specifies the **amplifiers** (bias current, bandwidth, slew) from the requirement, not just "an op-amp".
- Derives the **jitter budget** and lists a test plan.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Choosing $C_H$ by noise only** and forgetting that a larger cap slows tracking and loads the driver.
- **Using a single NMOS for a full-range input.** It stops conducting when $V_{in}>V_{DD}-V_t$ (or at least its $R_{on}$ rises steeply near the top), so use a transmission gate or bootstrap.
- **Forgetting the source impedance.** If the input isn't buffered, $R_s$ is added to $R_{on}$ in $\tau$.
- **Ignoring charge injection** because "it's just an offset". It depends on the signal level, so it becomes distortion.
- **A slow or ordinary op-amp for the output buffer** with nA-level bias current, which discharges $C_H$ ([[q0815]]).
- **No test plan.** Settling, droop, linearity and jitter all need to be checked.
:::

## Step 2: size $C_H$ against three constraints

**Noise.** $v_n=\sqrt{kT/C_H}$ is independent of $R_{on}$. Quantization noise for 12 bits is $V_{LSB}/\sqrt{12}=233$ µV.

| $C_H$ | $kT/C$ noise | Added to quantization noise in quadrature | Droop (1 nA, 0.6 µs) | Pedestal (3 fC) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 pF | 64 µV | 242 µV (+4%) | 0.6 mV | 3.0 mV |
| 4 pF | 32 µV | 235 µV (+1%) | 0.15 mV | 0.75 mV |
| 10 pF | 20 µV | 233.9 µV (+0.4%) | 0.06 mV | 0.30 mV |
| 20 pF | 14 µV | 233.4 µV (+0.2%) | 0.03 mV | 0.15 mV |

Noise alone says 4 pF is plenty, but droop and charge injection push toward 10 pF. Settling pushes the other way. The compromise is a design choice, and 10 pF is reasonable.

Only the pedestal and droop at small $C$ approach half an LSB, so they set the lower bound on $C_H$ (noise alone would allow a smaller cap). The upper bound comes from the settling time ($\tau=RC$) and the driver current.

## Step 3: size the switch from the settling requirement

$$e^{-t_{acq}/\tau}\le\frac{1}{2^{N+1}}\;\Rightarrow\;t_{acq}\ge\tau\ln2^{13}=9.01\tau$$

With $t_{acq}=0.4$ µs, $\tau_{max}=44$ ns. For $C_H=10$ pF, $R_{tot,max}=4.4$ kΩ, and since the A1 output is low-impedance, $R_{on}\le4$ kΩ. Design for margin (4× is typical because the real input also has to settle through A1): $R_{on}=1$ kΩ, $\tau=10$ ns, $t_{acq}=90$ ns.

The MOSFET sets $R_{on}$ by size:

$$R_{on}=\frac{1}{\mu_nC_{ox}\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS}-V_t)}$$

and $V_{GS}-V_t$ falls as $V_{in}$ rises toward the gate voltage. With the gate at 3.3 V, $V_t=0.6$ V and $V_{in}=2.7$ V, $V_{GS}-V_t\to0$ and $R_{on}\to\infty$. That's why a single NMOS can't pass the top of the range and why the design uses a transmission gate or bootstrapping.

## Step 4: kill charge injection

When M1 turns off, its channel charge $Q_{ch}=WLC_{ox}(V_{GS}-V_t)$ splits between the two terminals. With a source-side buffer, roughly half goes to $C_H$. A dummy switch M2 at the same node, half the size of M1 and driven with the *opposite* clock, absorbs that charge: it turns on as M1 turns off, soaking up the injected charge. Better: **bottom-plate sampling** (open the switch connected to ground-referenced plate first so its charge is signal-independent) or a **differential** S/H in which injection becomes common-mode.

## Step 5: amplifier requirements

- **A1 (input buffer).** Must supply a charging current of $C_H\,dV/dt$; in 40 ns for a 3.3 V step on 10 pF that is about 0.8 mA peak. Closed-loop bandwidth of about 100 MHz or more gives settling in the track window. Unity-gain stable with a capacitive load through $R_{on}$ (see [[q0125]] for the follower).
- **A2 (output buffer).** Input bias current of a few pA or less; low offset (or cancel with auto-zero); drive for the ADC input. Its settling matters in hold only for the step when the switch opens.

## Step 6: clock and jitter

The error from timing uncertainty at the top input frequency is $2\pi fA\,\Delta t$. At 100 kHz and 1.65 V amplitude the slope is 1.04 V/µs, so 1 ns of jitter is 1.04 mV, 1.3 LSB. To keep jitter noise below the 12-bit quantization noise (SNR 74 dB) requires $\sigma_t<0.32$ ns rms. A buffered crystal-derived clock does that easily; a software-controlled GPIO may not.

## Step 7: verification checklist

1. Step response: settle to ½ LSB within $t_{acq}$ for a 0 to 3.3 V step, across process, voltage and temperature.
2. Hold droop at 85 °C.
3. Pedestal versus input level (sweep DC).
4. FFT with a sine at 99 kHz: SNR, SFDR, THD.
5. Jitter sensitivity: add random clock jitter in simulation.
6. Monte-Carlo for dummy switch mismatch and amplifier offset.

::: applications
- **ADC front ends**: the S/H in pipeline ADCs (called the SHA) and the input stage of precision SAR converters.
- **Multi-channel simultaneous-sampling data acquisition** with one S/H per channel.
- **Switched-capacitor circuits**: every switched-capacitor filter and integrator uses this same switch and capacitor structure.
- **Analog sample delays and peak holds**: audio effects and measurement instruments.
- **Board-level equivalents**: the RC and buffer you place before an MCU's ADC pin follow this same design logic ([[q0818]]).
- **Oscilloscope track-and-hold**: wideband front ends with bootstrapped switches.
:::

::: assumptions
- The spec is an assumed example (12 bits, 3.3 V, 100 kHz, 1 MS/s). A different spec changes the numbers but not the method.
- The switch is a CMOS device in a generic process: leakage, injected charge and $R_{on}$ values are illustrative.
- A1 and A2 are ideal followers apart from the properties discussed.
- Single-ended design. A fully differential S/H would cancel charge injection and even-order distortion at the price of area and power.
- If the interviewer's context is board-level (an MCU ADC and discrete parts), treat $C_H$ and the switch as fixed inside the chip and design the external RC and buffer by the same equations ([[q0818]]).
:::

::: followups
### Why not just use a larger hold capacitor to be safe?
It reduces noise, droop and pedestal, but raises $\tau=RC$, so the acquisition time grows, and it needs a driver with more current. Doubling $C_H$ doubles $t_{acq}$ and can force you to lower the sample rate or increase buffer power.

### How does a transmission gate help?
An NMOS passes low voltages well and a PMOS passes high voltages well. In parallel, one of them is always strongly on, so $R_{on}$ is much flatter across the input range. The charge injection of the two partly cancels. Bootstrapping is better for linearity (constant $V_{GS}$) but needs a charge-pump-like circuit.

### What limits the maximum input frequency of this S/H?
Three things: track bandwidth $1/(2\pi\tau)$ (here 16 MHz), aperture jitter ($\text{SNR}=-20\log_{10}(2\pi f\sigma_t)$), and signal-dependent $R_{on}$ producing distortion that grows with frequency. For the 12-bit target the jitter limit is the binding one at 100 kHz (0.3 ns).

### How would you reduce kT/C noise without increasing $C_H$?
You can't change $\sqrt{kT/C}$ for a given $C$, but you can average several samples (oversampling), cool the circuit, or increase $C$. Correlated double sampling removes offset and low-frequency noise, and in image sensors it cancels the *reset* $kT/C$ noise because the same frozen noise appears in both samples. It cannot remove the $kT/C$ noise frozen onto the signal sample in an S/H like this one.

### Harder: the S/H meets spec in simulation but the measured THD is 10 dB worse at 100 kHz. What do you check?
Look for signal-dependent charge injection or $R_{on}$ that the model didn't capture, A1 slew-limiting on the largest steps, clock edge-rate sensitivity (a slow gate edge makes the sampling instant depend on the input level), and supply or reference coupling from the digital clock. A sweep of the clock rise time and a test with half the input amplitude help isolate slew versus switch non-linearity.
