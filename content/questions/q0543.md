::: testing
Whether you can explain, with physics and numbers, what distance does to a supply: DC drop ($IR$), transient droop ($L\,di/dt$), loss of regulation because the regulator senses its own output rather than the load, a worse control loop, and extra noise radiation and pickup. The interviewer is looking for at least two mechanisms, a quantified example, and an awareness of the exception (noise and heat can push you the other way, and remote sense can help).
:::

::: why
At 1 V and tens of amps, a few milliohms is a few percent of the rail, and a few nanohenries is volts during a load step. Many "mystery" resets and ADC errors trace back to a regulator that was routed far from its load because the schematic didn't say otherwise. The principle also explains why decoupling capacitors sit at the pins ([[q0504]]), why point-of-load converters exist, and why remote sense is on every serious bench supply.
:::

::: concepts
- **Resistance of copper**: $R = R_\square l/w$; 0.48 mΩ/sq for 1 oz. See [[q0534]].
- **Inductance of traces**: about 1 nH/mm without a close plane; $V = L\,di/dt$. See [[q0536]].
- **Regulation loop**: a regulator only controls the voltage where its feedback pin senses it.
- **Local charge reservoir**: capacitors at the load supply fast current. See [[q0553]].
- **Constant-power loads**: converters downstream draw more current as their voltage falls.
- **Noise and EMI**: long conductors are better antennas. See [[q0562]].
:::

::: answer
"Because every millimetre between the supply and the load is a resistor and an inductor in series with it. The resistor gives you DC drop: one ounce copper is about half a milliohm per square, so with a 1 V rail at 10 A, 20 mm of 1 mm trace each way costs roughly 190 millivolts, about a fifth of the rail. The inductor gives you transient droop: a few nanohenries of trace and a load that steps 5 A in 100 ns makes V equals L di by dt, so 10 nH gives half a volt.

There's a control point too. The regulator's feedback senses its own output capacitor, not the load, so it regulates perfectly at the wrong place and can't fix the drop in between. The extra inductance also slows and can destabilise the loop's response to load steps.

And a long trace is a bigger antenna: it radiates the supply's switching noise and picks up noise from elsewhere.

So I put the regulator and its output capacitors as close to the load as the heat and noise constraints allow. If I can't, I widen the path into planes, put capacitance at the load, use remote sense, or use a point-of-load regulator fed from a higher-voltage bus."
:::

::: technical
**DC drop.** $\Delta V = I(R_{supply}+R_{return})$, with $R = R_\square l/w$. Example: 1 V, 10 A, each path 20 squares → 9.6 mΩ, total 19.2 mΩ → 0.19 V (19%). With 5 mm wide traces it is about 77 mV; with a plane return it is closer to 50 mV.

**Transient droop.** $\Delta V = L\,di/dt$. A 10 nH path with a 5 A step in 100 ns gives 0.5 V; 2 nH gives 0.1 V. The local capacitor must supply the step: $C \approx \Delta Q/\Delta V = (\tfrac12 I\,t_r)/\Delta V$, i.e. about 5 µF for 50 mV with the numbers above.

**Regulation.** A regulator with local feedback keeps $V_{FB}$ at its set point at its own output capacitor. Any drop downstream of that point is uncorrected, so the load sees $V_{set} - I R_{path}$. Remote sense moves the sense point to the load and makes the regulator raise its output to compensate (within its headroom).

**Loop effects.** The extra series inductance between the output capacitor and the load changes the plant: the output impedance seen by the load rises at high frequency, and the loop's response time is now limited by $L$ and the load capacitor. A remote sense connection can add delay and ringing if the sense point is far from the output capacitor.

**Constant-power loads.** If the load is a converter drawing $P$, then $I = P/V$; as the drop lowers $V$, $I$ rises, increasing the drop. Solving $V = V_s - (P/V)R$ gives $V = \tfrac12\left(V_s + \sqrt{V_s^2 - 4RP}\right)$, which has no solution beyond $P_{max} = V_s^2/4R$ (collapse). See [[q0544]].

**Noise.** A longer supply path radiates and receives more; the loop area and antenna efficiency increase with length.

**Heat.** Drop becomes heat: $I^2R = 1.9$ W in the example.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The inductance per mm depends on the geometry and the return path. Values from 0.3 to 1 nH/mm are typical, with planes giving the lower values. Treat the numbers as estimates and measure.
:::

::: short
"Distance adds resistance and inductance to the supply path. At 1 V and 10 A, 20 mm of 1 mm trace each way drops about 190 mV, and 10 nH with a 5 A step in 100 ns droops half a volt. The regulator can't compensate, because its feedback senses its own output, not the load, and the extra inductance hurts the loop. Long traces also radiate and pick up noise. So I place the regulator and output capacitors next to the load, or use remote sense and a point-of-load regulator."
:::

::: long
"Every millimetre between the supply and the load is a resistor and an inductor in series with the rail, and they cost you in four ways.

First, DC drop. One ounce copper is around half a milliohm per square. For a 1 volt rail at 10 amps, 20 millimetres of 1 millimetre trace is 20 squares, 9.6 milliohms, and the return path costs the same, so you lose about 190 millivolts, almost a fifth of the rail. It's also heat, nearly 2 watts.

Second, transient droop. A trace is roughly a nanohenry per millimetre, so 10 nanohenries is a short run. When the processor steps 5 amps in 100 nanoseconds, V equals L di by dt gives half a volt, even though the DC drop looked fine. The local capacitors have to supply that step, which is why they sit at the pins.

Third, regulation. The regulator's feedback senses its own output capacitor, so it holds the voltage perfectly there and can't see what is lost between that point and the load. Adding inductance also changes the output impedance the load sees and can slow or destabilise the response.

Fourth, noise. A long path is a bigger antenna, so it radiates the regulator's switching noise and picks up other noise.

There's a nasty case too: if the load is itself a converter drawing constant power, a lower voltage means more current, which means more drop, and past a limit it collapses.

So I place the regulator and its output capacitors as close as I can. If I can't, I use planes for the path, put capacitance at the load, use remote sense, or put a point-of-load converter next to it."
:::

::: terms
- **IR drop**: the voltage lost in the resistance of the supply and return conductors.
- **Droop**: a temporary dip in the load voltage during a current step.
- **Remote sense (Kelvin sense)**: extra sense lines that let the regulator measure the voltage at the load.
- **Constant-power load**: a load whose current rises as its voltage falls, like a downstream converter.
- **Point-of-load (POL) regulator**: a regulator placed beside its load.
- **Output impedance**: the impedance the load sees looking back into the supply network.
- **Loop inductance**: the inductance of the supply-plus-return path.
- **Local decoupling**: capacitors at the load pins that supply fast transients. See [[q0553]].
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "to reduce drop and noise."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates DC drop ($IR$), transient droop ($L\,di/dt$) and control-loop effects.
- Gives numbers for each (190 mV, 0.5 V, 1.9 W).
- Points out that local-feedback regulators cannot correct drop downstream of their sense point, and offers remote sense as the fix.
- Mentions constant-power loads and collapse.
- Notes the exceptions (noise and heat can push the regulator away) and how to compensate (planes, local capacitors, POL).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Counting only the supply trace.** The return path doubles the drop and sets the loop inductance.
- **Saying "the regulator will compensate."** Not unless remote sense moves the feedback point to the load.
- **Forgetting transients.** DC drop may be fine while $L\,di/dt$ ruins the transient.
- **Using remote sense with a long, thin sense pair and no care.** It can add ringing and delay; keep the sense pair short, twisted or tightly routed, with the regulator's recommended filtering.
- **Assuming local capacitance replaces closeness.** Capacitors must be reachable with low inductance too; a capacitor across a long trace doesn't help the load at high frequency. See [[q0549]].
- **Treating all loads as constant-resistance.** Switching converters downstream behave as constant-power.
:::

## Transient droop

The load changes faster than the regulator loop can respond, typically on a 100 ns to 10 µs time scale. During that time the local capacitor and the path inductance determine the dip. The voltage across $L_p$ is $L_p\,di/dt$:

- 10 nH, 5 A in 100 ns: 0.5 V
- 2 nH (e.g. a short run over a plane): 0.1 V

The charge needed from the capacitor during a triangular current step of $I$ over $t_r$ is about $\tfrac12 I t_r$ = 0.25 µC for 5 A over 100 ns. To keep droop to 50 mV, $C \approx 0.25\,\mu\text{C}/50\,\text{mV} = 5$ µF *at the load, with low inductance*. If the capacitor is far away, its own path inductance is in series and it cannot help at the speeds that matter ([[q0549]], [[q0553]]).

Hence the standard recipe: regulator close, local ceramics at the pins, bulk nearby, and as little loop as possible.

## Regulation: what the feedback actually sees

Most regulators connect the feedback divider to the output capacitor. The loop forces that point to $V_{set}$. The load, farther away, sees $V_{set} - IR_p$, and the regulator has no knowledge of it. **Remote sense** reroutes the feedback to the load: the regulator raises its output until the sensed voltage is correct, compensating for drop up to a limit (a few hundred mV for a bench supply, less for on-board converters).

Remote sense adds a small loop in the feedback path and any ringing or delay caused by the long sense line can destabilise the loop. Good practice: route the sense pair as a differential pair, keep it away from noisy nodes, use the regulator's recommended compensation and filtering, and limit the span.

## Control-loop effects

A switching regulator's output stage is designed around its output capacitor. Extra inductance between that capacitor and the load changes the load impedance seen by the loop and can create a resonance with the load's capacitance at $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_pC})$. For 50 nH and 10 µF it is 225 kHz, right in the range of many control-loop crossover frequencies, so it can cause peaking or oscillation. See [[q0245]].

## Constant-power loads

A downstream converter delivers a roughly constant power $P$ to its own load, so $I = P/V$. The rail voltage then satisfies

$$V = V_s - \frac{P}{V}R_p \;\Rightarrow\; V = \frac{V_s + \sqrt{V_s^2 - 4R_pP}}{2}$$

which exists only if $P \le V_s^2/(4R_p)$. For $V_s = 3.3$ V and $R_p = 100$ mΩ: $P_{max} = 27$ W, and the load voltage at 20 W is 2.5 V (not 3.3 − 2 × 0.1 × 6 = 2.1, so it's the more self-consistent value that matters). The point is not the exact numbers but that drop makes things worse, not just a linear sag. See [[q0544]].

## Noise and EMI

A longer path means a larger loop and a better antenna, both for radiating the regulator's noise and for picking up external noise. Short paths over a close plane keep the loop small and the coupling low.

## When "close" isn't free

Closeness has costs: noise from the switch node and inductor near sensitive circuits, heat concentrated near the load, board area. The usual compromises:

- A regulator next to the load with its noisy stage shielded or partitioned ([[q0547]]).
- A POL converter fed from a higher-voltage, lower-current bus (the current on the long run falls in proportion to the voltage ratio, and drop with its square-scaled impact).
- An LDO after a switcher to clean the rail at the sensitive load ([[q0158]]).
- Remote sense, wide pours, and capacitance at the load when distance is unavoidable.

A quick feel for the bus voltage: to deliver 12 W, a 1 V rail needs 12 A, while a 12 V bus needs 1 A; the same wiring then drops 12x less voltage and, as a fraction of the rail, 144x less.

::: applications
- **Processor and FPGA cores** use POL regulators placed adjacent to the BGA, with the output capacitors under it.
- **Memory (DDR) rails** with regulators and bulk capacitors placed at the DIMM or memory chip.
- **Precision analog and ADC rails** use local LDOs next to the converter.
- **Bench supplies and test equipment** use four-wire remote sense to regulate at the device under test.
- **Automotive and industrial boards** run a higher-voltage bus and convert near each load.
:::

::: assumptions
The analysis assumes a switching or linear regulator with local feedback, a low-voltage, high-current load, and 1 oz copper on FR-4. The numbers change with copper weight, width, planes (which reduce both $R$ and $L$ substantially) and regulator type. For a few-mA load with a long run, none of this matters. The loop effects depend on the compensation of the specific regulator; remote-sense limits are vendor-specific. In some systems the regulator is deliberately distant (thermal, noise or mechanical reasons) and the design relies on planes, local capacitance and remote sense; this is a valid alternative to "close to the load".
:::

::: followups
### What is remote sense and when do you use it?
It routes the regulator's feedback to the load pins, so the regulator corrects for the drop in the supply and return conductors. I use it when the load is far, the current is high, or the rail is tight (for example 1 V cores). It only compensates DC and slow drop; it does not fix inductive droop, which still needs local capacitors. And the sense pair needs careful routing to avoid noise and stability issues.

### How does a long trace affect the response to a load step?
The inductance limits how fast current can arrive from the regulator, so the voltage dips until the local capacitor supplies it. With 20 nH and a 3 A step in 50 ns, $L\,di/dt = 1.2$ V across the path, so almost all the charge has to come from capacitors at the load, and the regulator only sees the smoothed result.

### Could bigger decoupling at the regulator compensate for distance?
Not for fast transients. A capacitor at the regulator end of the path has the path's inductance between it and the load, so the load can't draw from it quickly. The capacitance has to be near the load. Capacitance at the regulator helps the regulator, not the load.

### Why can a long trace cause oscillation?
The trace inductance forms an LC resonance with the output and load capacitance; with 50 nH and 10 µF it is at 225 kHz, which may be near the loop crossover. The regulator's loop was compensated for a particular load impedance, and an unexpected resonance reduces phase margin, causing ringing or oscillation.

### Why is a high-voltage bus with POL regulation better than low-voltage distribution?
For the same power the current is lower in proportion to the voltage, so $I^2R$ loss falls with the square and the voltage drop becomes a smaller fraction of the rail. A 12 V bus carrying 1 A for a 12 W load has 144 times less wiring loss than a 1 V rail carrying 12 A over the same copper.
:::

::: interpretations
"Close to the load" here means the *regulated output and its capacitors*, not only the regulator IC; the hot loop at the input must also stay tight ([[q0548]]). The question is a conceptual "why"; the "what if it isn't" version is [[q0544]], and the placement trade-offs are in [[q0542]].
