::: basics
### In plain words
Before raising the switching frequency, ask whether you need to, because every step costs efficiency and EMI. If you do, attack the limits one at a time. Cut the loss per switching cycle with faster gate drive, better transistors, tight layout, and synchronous rectification. Shrink the fixed delays. Or trick the load into seeing a higher frequency without making the devices switch faster, using a unipolar bridge or interleaving.

### Picture it
Think of two rowers sharing a boat. Each one pulls at a modest stroke rate, so each tires slowly. But because they alternate, the boat feels a stroke twice as often, and the ride is smoother. That is interleaving, or a three-level bridge: the load sees double the frequency, and each device still switches at the original rate, so the loss does not double.

### Words you need
- **Loss per cycle ($k$)**: the frequency-proportional loss, in watts per hertz. Raising the ceiling means shrinking $k$.
- **Figure of merit (FOM)**: a product like $R_{DS(on)} \times Q_g$ that compares transistors for switching use.
- **Synchronous rectification**: replacing the freewheel diode with a switched FET, to remove diode recovery and drop.
- **Unipolar (3-level) PWM**: an H-bridge scheme where the load sees pulses at twice the leg frequency.

### The simplest example
Edge time is $Q_{gd}/I_g$. With a 5 V drive, a 3 V plateau, and 5 nC, a 47 Ω gate resistor gives 117 ns, and 10 Ω gives 25 ns. Faster edges cut the loss per hertz, so in the 12 V, 5 A example the thermal ceiling can rise from about 257 kHz to about 624 kHz. The cost is ringing: 5 A changing in 10 ns across 10 nH overshoots by 5 V. Separately, a unipolar bridge cuts ripple from 0.225 A to 0.075 A with no extra device switching.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers each lever, the controller timer, and the EMI and thermal checks.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can turn the list of limiters from [[q0418]] into a list of *remedies* and rank them by payoff and cost. The expected themes: cut switching loss (faster, stronger gate drive, lower-charge devices, better layout, synchronous rectification, wide-bandgap devices, soft switching), shrink the fixed times (dead time, blanking, bootstrap), raise the *effective* ripple frequency without raising the device frequency (3-level unipolar bridge, interleaving), give the controller more timing resolution, and manage the EMI and thermal consequences. Strong answers also ask "do you need it?".
:::

::: why
Higher switching frequency means smaller filters and inductors, lower current ripple, faster current loops and inaudible operation, but only if the losses, EMI and timing can be tamed. This is the standard design trade in every converter and motor drive, and interviewers use it to see if you can fix the right limit rather than just "use a faster MOSFET".
:::

::: concepts
- **Loss terms that scale with $f$**: overlap, gate, $C_{oss}$, $Q_{rr}$. See [[q0418]].
- **Gate-drive speed**: $t \approx Q_{gd}/I_g$, set by driver current and $R_g$. See [[q0431]].
- **Figures of merit**: $R_{DS(on)}Q_g$, $R_{DS(on)}Q_{gd}$, $R_{DS(on)}C_{oss}$. See [[q0417]].
- **Synchronous rectification**: use a FET instead of a diode for freewheeling to remove diode recovery and drop.
- **Effective ripple frequency**: unipolar H-bridge or interleaved phases make the load see a multiple of the switching frequency. See [[q0411]].
- **Wide-bandgap devices**: GaN and SiC FETs have very low $Q_g$, $C_{oss}$ and no body-diode recovery (GaN).
:::

::: answer
"First I'd ask whether I need it, because every doubling costs efficiency and EMI. Assuming I do, I attack the limiters.

Switching loss: make the transitions shorter. A stronger gate driver and a lower gate resistor shorten the Miller plateau: with a 5 volt drive, a 10 ohm resistor gives about 25 nanoseconds for a 5 nanocoulomb gate-drain charge; 47 ohms gives 117. Pick a MOSFET with a better figure of merit, lower gate charge times on-resistance, lower output capacitance. Reduce the parasitic inductance in the power loop and the gate loop with layout, so I can run fast edges without ringing. Use synchronous rectification so the freewheel diode's reverse recovery doesn't add loss. Go to GaN or SiC if I need hundreds of kilohertz to megahertz. For specialised cases, soft-switching.

Fixed times: use a driver with short, matched propagation delay and adaptive or smaller dead time, shorten the current-sense blanking with good layout.

A cheaper trick for a motor: make the *load* see a higher frequency without switching the devices faster. A three-level unipolar H-bridge doubles the effective ripple frequency, and interleaved phases multiply it, so the ripple drops without more loss.

Then support it: a timer with enough resolution, thermal design, and EMI measures like slew-rate control, filters and layout."
:::

::: technical
**Remedy for each loss term** (using the 12 V, 5 A example of [[q0418]]; baseline $t_r + t_f = 100$ ns, $k_0 = 3.5\ \mu$W/Hz of frequency-proportional loss):

| Loss | Lever | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Overlap $\tfrac12VI(t_r+t_f)f$ | stronger driver, lower $R_g$, lower $Q_{gd}$ FET | linear in edge time: 100 ns to 40 ns cuts it by 2.5x |
| Gate $Q_gV_gf$ | smaller $Q_g$ (FOM), lower drive voltage | proportional |
| $C_{oss}$ $\tfrac12C_{oss}V^2f$ | low-$C_{oss}$ device, lower voltage, ZVS | proportional; ZVS recovers it |
| Reverse recovery $Q_{rr}Vf$ | synchronous rectification with low-$Q_{rr}$ FET, GaN, Schottky/SiC diode | removes the term |
| Dead-time diode conduction | shorter or adaptive dead time | $\propto 2t_df$ |

**Edge time vs gate resistor** (5 V drive, 3 V plateau, $Q_{gd} = 5$ nC): $I_g = (5-3)/R_g$ and $t_{Miller} = Q_{gd}/I_g$: $R_g = 47\ \Omega$: 117 ns; 22 Ω: 55 ns; 10 Ω: 25 ns; 4.7 Ω: 12 ns. Faster edges raise $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}t$ and ringing: the voltage overshoot from loop inductance is $L_{loop}\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$: with $L = 10$ nH and the 5 A current commutating in 10 ns, 5 V; at 25 ns, 2 V.

**Example payoff.** If better gate drive and layout reduce the proportional loss from 3.5 to 1.44 $\mu$W/Hz (40 ns edges, smaller $C_{oss}$ and $Q_{rr}$) the 1.3 W thermal ceiling moves from 257 kHz to about 624 kHz. A GaN-like device with 20 ns edges, 5 nC $Q_g$ and no $Q_{rr}$ gives 0.63 $\mu$W/Hz and about 1.4 MHz (illustrative).

**Raise the effective ripple frequency.** For 12 V, $L = 1$ mH, 20 kHz, average 6 V: lock-antiphase bipolar PWM gives 0.225 A pk-pk; sign-magnitude (one leg) 0.15 A; three-level unipolar (two legs, 180° apart) 0.075 A at an effective $2f$ with no increase in device switching frequency (each leg still switches at $f$). Interleaving $N$ phases multiplies the ripple frequency by $N$ and (in converters) cancels ripple.

**Fixed-time reductions.** Dead time: use a driver with matched propagation delay and adaptive dead-time sensing the switch node; blanking: lower parasitic ringing so a shorter blank suffices; min on-time: faster comparators and drivers.

**Controller.** Higher clock, high-resolution timer modes, dithering for fractional duty steps, and running the control loop at a fraction of the PWM rate.
:::

::: short
"I'd cut each limiter: shorten the switching edges with a stronger gate driver, lower $R_g$ and low-$Q_{gd}$ FETs, tighten the layout to keep ringing down, use synchronous rectification to kill diode recovery, and go to GaN or SiC if I need MHz. Reduce dead time and blanking, give the timer resolution, and manage EMI and heat. For a motor, I might not raise the device frequency at all: a unipolar bridge or interleaving doubles or multiplies the ripple frequency the load sees."
:::

::: long
"I'd start by asking whether I really need a higher frequency, since each step costs efficiency and EMI. If I do, I take the limiters one by one.

Loss first. Overlap loss is proportional to the edge time, so I make the edges faster: a stronger gate driver and a smaller gate resistor. With a 5 volt drive and a 5 nanocoulomb gate-drain charge, 47 ohms gives a 117 nanosecond Miller plateau and 10 ohms gives 25. Then the device: lower gate charge and output capacitance for the same on-resistance. Layout matters, because fast edges ring on parasitic inductance, so the power loop and the gate loop need to be small. Synchronous rectification replaces the freewheel diode's recovery loss, and for high frequencies I'd consider GaN or SiC. In an example, tightening the edges from 100 to 40 nanoseconds takes the thermal ceiling from about 250 kilohertz to about 600.

Second, the fixed times: choose a driver with short and matched delays, adaptive dead time and a shorter current-sense blanking, so the minimum duty doesn't collapse.

Third, a cheaper route for a motor: make the load see a higher frequency without making the devices switch faster. A three-level unipolar H-bridge, with the legs 180 degrees apart, doubles the effective ripple frequency and cuts the ripple from about 0.15 amps to 0.075 amps in the example, and interleaving phases multiplies it further.

Fourth, support it: a timer with enough resolution or a high-resolution mode, thermal design, and EMI measures like slew-rate control, filtering and spread spectrum."
:::

::: terms
- **Figure of merit (FOM)**: product like $R_{DS(on)}\times Q_g$ comparing devices for switching applications.
- **Miller plateau**: gate-voltage plateau during the drain voltage transition.
- **Synchronous rectification**: replacing the freewheel diode with a controlled FET.
- **ZVS**: zero-voltage switching; turning on when $V_{DS}$ is already near zero.
- **Unipolar (3-level) PWM**: H-bridge scheme where the load sees $+V$, 0, $-V$ at twice the leg frequency.
- **Interleaving**: phase-shifting parallel switching legs to multiply the ripple frequency.
- **Adaptive dead time**: dead time set by sensing the switch node.
- **Wide-bandgap (GaN, SiC)**: semiconductors with lower switching charge and, for GaN, no reverse recovery.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "use faster transistors". An exceptional answer:

- Maps **each limiter to a remedy** with numbers (edge time vs $R_g$, FOM, loss and ceiling shift).
- Knows the **cost of faster edges**: $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}t$, ringing and $L\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$ overshoot, and how layout reduces it.
- Offers **architectural tricks** (unipolar H-bridge, interleaving, synchronous rectification, soft switching) that raise the *effective* frequency or cut loss without brute force.
- Handles the **fixed-time and controller** limits.
- Opens with **"do you need it?"** and closes with **EMI and thermal verification**.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Cutting $R_g$ without regard to ringing and EMI.** Faster edges are only usable with a low-inductance layout and sometimes a snubber.
- **Choosing the lowest-$R_{DS(on)}$ FET.** Larger die means larger $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$; at high frequency pick by FOM.
- **Keeping a slow body diode for freewheeling.** Its $Q_{rr}$ becomes a major loss; use synchronous rectification or low-$Q_{rr}$ parts.
- **Forgetting dead time and blanking.** They don't shrink with frequency and take over the period.
- **Assuming higher always means better.** Efficiency, EMI and cost get worse; stop at what the load needs.
- **Ignoring the controller.** A timer with too few bits can't generate the frequency with usable duty resolution.
- **Raising the frequency but not checking thermal and EMI.** Verify with measurements.
:::

::: deep
## Think in terms of loss per hertz

For a hard-switched stage the frequency-dependent power is

$$P_f = f\left[\tfrac12VI(t_r+t_f) + Q_gV_g + \tfrac12C_{oss}V^2 + Q_{rr}V + \dots\right] = k\,f$$

The constant $k$ (W/Hz, or equivalently J per cycle) is what you want to shrink. The thermal ceiling is $f_{max} = (P_{max} - P_{cond})/k$. Each remedy attacks one term of $k$.

## Lever 1: faster, cleaner edges

The Miller-plateau time is $t_{Miller} = Q_{gd}/I_g$ with $I_g = (V_{drive} - V_{plateau})/R_{g,total}$. For $V_{drive} = 5$ V, $V_{plateau} = 3$ V, $Q_{gd} = 5$ nC:

| $R_g$ | $I_g$ | $t_{Miller}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 47 Ω | 43 mA | 117 ns |
| 22 Ω | 91 mA | 55 ns |
| 10 Ω | 200 mA | 25 ns |
| 4.7 Ω | 426 mA | 12 ns |

Remember $R_{g,total}$ includes the driver's output resistance and the FET's internal gate resistance. Raise the drive voltage (to the FET's rated gate voltage) for more plateau current. Use a stronger driver (peak current of an amp or more) and separate turn-on and turn-off resistances.

```sim

```
Figure: Compare $R_g$ = 47 Ω with 4.7 Ω: the Miller plateau shortens and the switching loss falls, but ringing appears when the gate-loop inductance (10 nH here) is large. Increase `lg` to see the penalty of a bad layout: faster edges are only useful if the layout can carry them.

**Cost of speed.** Faster $\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$ across loop inductance $L_{loop}$ produces an overshoot $L_{loop}\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$ (5 A in 10 ns across 10 nH is 5 V), and fast $\mathrm{d}v/\mathrm{d}t$ couples through $C_{gd}$ into the opposite gate (Miller turn-on) and into the environment (EMI). Remedies: minimise the power loop area (decoupling capacitors right at the half-bridge), Kelvin source connection, separate turn-off path, a small RC snubber or a ferrite/resistor in the gate for damping. Layout rules are in the PCB section.

## Lever 2: better devices

Pick on the figure of merit for the target frequency:

- Low $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{gd}$ for hard-switching overlap loss.
- Low $R_{DS(on)}\cdot C_{oss}$ for capacitive and ZVS losses.
- Low $Q_{rr}$ body diode (or add a Schottky) for bridges.
- Lower-voltage devices have lower $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$ per amp; don't over-margin voltage.

**GaN HEMTs** have very small $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$ and no reverse recovery (the "diode" is a reverse conduction channel with a voltage drop of 1.5 to 3 V during dead time, so dead time must be short); **SiC MOSFETs** are used above a few hundred volts. Their gate drive is more demanding (tight gate-voltage limits, careful layout).

## Lever 3: remove the diode loss (synchronous rectification)

A freewheel diode drops $V_f$ and, if it is a PN diode, has reverse recovery; a Schottky has no recovery but leaks and has a lower voltage rating. Replace it with a FET switched on during freewheeling (the other bridge FET): conduction loss becomes $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ instead of $V_fI$, and the body diode carries current only during dead time. At 5 A and $V_f = 0.5$ V the diode dissipates 2.5 W while it conducts; a 10 mΩ FET in its place dissipates 0.25 W. In an H-bridge this is just the complementary PWM of the opposite switch (with dead time).

## Lever 4: reduce the fixed times

- **Dead time**: use a driver with matched propagation delay and gate-drive current so the margin can be small; adaptive dead-time sensing the switch node; GaN needs very short dead time to limit body-diode-like conduction.
- **Blanking**: a clean current-sense signal needs less blanking: tight layout, differential sensing, careful filter.
- **Bootstrap**: a charge pump or isolated supply for 100% duty and short low-side times.

## Lever 5: soft switching

If the stage turns on when $V_{DS}$ is already zero (ZVS), the $C_{oss}$ and overlap losses disappear. Resonant and quasi-resonant topologies, or a triangular-current-mode half-bridge (the inductor current reverses before turn-on so the switch node swings naturally), achieve it. For motor drives this is rare, but it is how MHz converters reach high efficiency.

## Lever 6: raise the effective frequency, not the device frequency

For a motor, what matters for ripple and acoustic noise is the frequency of the *voltage steps the load sees*, not the frequency at which each device toggles.

## Lever 7: the controller

- Run the timer at a higher clock or use the high-resolution mode (sub-nanosecond edge placement, found in some motor-control MCUs).
- Use dithering to obtain fractional duty steps (average resolution above the counter bits).
- Run the control loop slower than the PWM if the plant is slow (motor electrical time constant 1 ms needs a loop rate of 10 to 20 kHz, not 200 kHz).
- Hardware PWM and DMA/ADC triggering to keep CPU load low.

## EMI and thermal consequences

Every lever above that speeds edges or raises $f$ pushes harmonics into higher bands:

- Slew-rate control or gate resistors, as needed.
- A small RC snubber across the switch node; ferrites.
- Input filter and bulk/ceramic capacitor placement.
- Spread-spectrum modulation to spread peaks.
- Thermal: more copper, thermal vias, heat sink; check the junction temperature at the worst case.

Always measure: a conducted-emission pre-scan and a thermal image at maximum load.

::: applications
- **Point-of-load buck converters**: MHz switching with low-$Q_g$ FETs, sync rectification and multiphase interleaving.
- **Drone and robot BLDC drives (48 to 96 kHz)**: low-inductance motors need higher $f$ for acceptable ripple; fast FETs and careful layout.
- **Class-D audio and servo amplifiers**: unipolar/3-level modulation to double the effective ripple frequency.
- **Wireless power and resonant converters**: ZVS GaN stages at hundreds of kHz to MHz.
- **High-density EV chargers and DC-DC**: SiC and GaN for frequency and size reduction.
- **Silent household appliances**: 20 kHz+ PWM chosen just above audibility.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Hard-switched half-bridge or bridge** with an inductive load as the starting point; soft-switching is mentioned as an option.
- The three loss models are **illustrative**; real devices have more complicated scaling (e.g. $C_{oss}$ nonlinearity, temperature dependence).
- **Layout** and **EMI** consequences are treated qualitatively here; the PCB section covers layout details.
- A **silicon MOSFET** baseline; GaN, SiC and IGBTs have different driver and layout needs.
- The "payoff" numbers assume the thermal budget and conduction loss remain fixed.

::: followups
### Why does halving $R_g$ not halve switching loss?
Edge time is $Q_{gd}/I_g$ with $I_g$ set by the whole gate path: driver output resistance, internal gate resistance, plus $R_g$. Halving only the external $R_g$ helps less than 2x if the other resistances are significant. Also at low $R_g$ the gate-loop inductance limits $\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$, and ringing forces a damping resistor back. And the current-commutation phase isn't controlled by the Miller plateau alone.

### What is the main advantage of GaN for switching frequency?
Very low $Q_g$, $C_{oss}$ and $Q_{rr}$ (none) at a given $R_{DS(on)}$, so $k$ falls by roughly 3 to 5 times compared with silicon. The trade-offs: tighter gate voltage limits (about 6 V typical max), the reverse conduction drop in dead time, more demanding layout, and cost.

### How does a 3-level unipolar H-bridge help without raising the switching frequency?
The motor sees pulses at $2f$ with half the width, so the ripple current falls (0.075 A against 0.225 A for lock-antiphase in the example) and the audible/EMI fundamental sits at $2f$. Each device still switches at $f$, so switching loss is unchanged.

### Does a higher frequency always let you use a smaller inductor?
Yes for an external inductor in a converter: $L \propto 1/f$ for the same ripple. For a motor the winding inductance is fixed, so higher $f$ just lowers ripple; the benefit is smaller ripple heating, quieter operation and faster current loops, not smaller parts.

### You raised the PWM frequency to 100 kHz and the driver IC gets hot. What happened?
Gate-drive loss $Q_gV_gf$ and switching loss scale with $f$ and appear in the driver IC (internal FETs) or gate-drive stage. Also dead-time diode conduction and reverse recovery. Compute the losses, check the datasheet's maximum PWM frequency, reduce slew or frequency, or choose a part rated for higher frequency.
:::
