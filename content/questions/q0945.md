::: basics
### In plain words
Clock frequency is how many steps per second a chip takes. A faster clock gets more work done per second, but it uses more power, and that power turns into heat. Faster clocks also need a higher supply voltage, so power grows faster than speed does. Heat then limits how fast the chip can run for long, so chips slow themselves down when they get too hot.

### Picture it
Picture an assembly line. Speeding up the belt means more items per minute, and each item costs the same energy, so power rises in step with speed. But past a certain speed, workers need overtime pay to keep up, and that makes every item cost more. Doubling the speed then costs much more than double the power. The factory also warms up, and a hot factory wastes more energy, which warms it further.

### Words you need
- **Clock frequency**: the number of ticks per second, in hertz. The chip takes one step per tick.
- **Dynamic power**: power spent on switching, equal to $\alpha C V^2 f$ (how much capacitance toggles each tick, times voltage squared, times frequency).
- **Leakage**: power that flows even when nothing switches. It rises as the chip heats up.
- **Thermal resistance ($\theta$)**: how many degrees the chip warms per watt.
- **Throttling**: lowering the clock so the chip stays cool.
- **DVFS**: changing voltage and frequency together to match the load.

### The simplest example
Take a chip whose switching capacitance term $\alpha C$ is 20 nF. At 2 GHz and 0.9 V, power is $20\times10^{-9}\times0.9^2\times2\times10^9 = 32.4$ W. At 4 GHz it needs 1.1 V, so power is $20\times10^{-9}\times1.1^2\times4\times10^9 = 96.8$ W. Twice the frequency costs about 3 times the power. With $\theta=0.5$ K/W and a 45 °C surrounding, the chip runs at about 61 °C and then about 93 °C. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers why speedup is less than 2 times, why voltage must rise, and how heat feeds back.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can connect **one knob (clock frequency)** to the three coupled outputs (performance, power, temperature) with equations, and then show that the real story involves voltage. The interviewer wants:

1. Dynamic power $P = \alpha C V^2 f$: linear in $f$ at fixed voltage.
2. Performance scales with $f$ only to the extent the workload is not limited by memory or I/O.
3. The catch: higher $f$ needs higher $V$ to meet timing, so power grows faster than linearly along the DVFS curve (toward $f^3$ in the idealized limit $V \propto f$).
4. Thermals: power becomes heat, $T_j = T_a + P\,\theta_{JA}$, and leakage rises with temperature (feedback, even thermal runaway).
5. Why this makes frequency the main tool for power and thermal management (DVFS, throttling).
:::

::: why
Frequency is the most visible spec of a chip and the easiest dial to turn, which is why every product from phones to servers advertises or limits it. But frequency is also a thermal and battery issue, so system architects constantly trade it. Knowing why "2x clock" does not mean "2x speed" and does mean "more than 2x power" is basic literacy for anyone doing silicon, boards or firmware. This question opens the power-performance-thermal cluster: the supply voltage side is in [[q0947]], PVT in [[q0949]], and the knobs in [[q0952]] and [[q0953]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Dynamic (switching) power**: $P_{dyn} = \alpha C V_{DD}^2 f$, where $\alpha$ is the activity factor and $C$ the switched capacitance.
- **Static (leakage) power**: $P_{leak} = V_{DD} I_{leak}$, independent of $f$ but strongly dependent on temperature and $V_t$.
- **Maximum frequency**: set by the critical path delay, $f_{max} \approx 1/t_{path}$, and $t_{path}$ shrinks as $V_{DD}$ rises. See [[q0947]].
- **Thermal resistance**: $T_j = T_a + P\,\theta_{JA}$; power in watts becomes degrees.
- **Amdahl-style scaling**: only the clock-limited fraction of runtime speeds up with $f$. See [[q0936]].
- **DVFS**: dynamic voltage and frequency scaling pairs $f$ with the lowest safe $V$.
:::

::: answer
"Clock frequency directly sets how often the circuit switches, so dynamic power is $\alpha C V^2 f$. At a fixed voltage, double the frequency and you double the dynamic power. Performance goes up with frequency too, but not perfectly: only the part of the work that is limited by the core clock speeds up. If the program spends time waiting on DRAM, which doesn't get faster when I raise the CPU clock, I get less than the full gain. For example, if 30% of the time is memory-bound, 2x clock gives about 1.54x speedup.

The real cost is that you can't raise frequency for free. Faster clocks mean shorter cycle times, so the logic needs a higher supply voltage to meet timing, and power goes with the square of the voltage. So along a DVFS curve power grows much faster than frequency; if voltage scaled in proportion it would be frequency cubed, and in practice it lands in between. For instance, 2 GHz at 0.9 V to 4 GHz at 1.1 V is 2x the frequency but about 3x the power.

That power turns into heat. Junction temperature is ambient plus power times thermal resistance, and leakage grows exponentially with temperature, so a hot chip burns more power, which makes it hotter. That feedback can cause throttling or even runaway. So the system uses frequency as the main control knob: boost when there is thermal headroom, throttle down when it's hot, and drop frequency and voltage together when load is light to save energy."
:::

::: technical
**Dynamic power.** Each clock cycle a fraction $\alpha$ of the total switched capacitance $C$ charges and discharges through $V_{DD}$, drawing $C V_{DD}^2$ from the supply per full charge-discharge cycle (half is dissipated in the pull-up while charging, the other half is stored and then dissipated in the pull-down while discharging):

$$P_{dyn} = \alpha\, C\, V_{DD}^2\, f$$

At fixed $V_{DD}$ it is linear in $f$. Energy per operation $\alpha C V_{DD}^2$ does not depend on $f$ at all, so at fixed voltage, running faster only finishes sooner but uses the same dynamic energy for the same work.

**Performance.** Execution time $T = N_{inst}\cdot CPI/f$. For a workload where a fraction $m$ of the time is frequency-independent (memory latency, I/O), $\text{speedup}(f_2/f_1 = k) = \dfrac{1}{m + (1-m)/k}$. With $m = 0.3$ and $k = 2$ the speedup is 1.54.

**Frequency needs voltage.** Gate delay roughly follows the alpha-power law, $t_d \propto \dfrac{V_{DD}}{(V_{DD}-V_t)^{\alpha}}$ where this $\alpha$ is the velocity-saturation index (not the activity factor): about 2 for long-channel devices and roughly 1.2 to 1.5 for short-channel ones. So $f_{max}$ rises with $V_{DD}$, roughly linearly over the upper part of the DVFS range. In the idealized limit $V \propto f$, $P \propto f^3$; because real V-f curves have an offset (set by $V_t$), the practical exponent is lower, e.g. 2x frequency for about 3x power (an exponent near 1.6) in the example below.

**Thermals.** $T_j = T_a + P_{tot}\,\theta_{JA}$ (or through a chain $\theta_{JC} + \theta_{CS} + \theta_{SA}$). Leakage current grows roughly exponentially with temperature (commonly cited as doubling every 10 to 20 °C, varying by process), forming a positive feedback loop $P \to T \to P_{leak}$.

**Example numbers** (illustrative chip with $\alpha C = 20$ nF effective): at 2 GHz and 0.9 V, $P_{dyn} = 20\text{ nF}\times0.81\times2\text{ GHz} = 32.4$ W; at 4 GHz and 1.1 V, $P_{dyn} = 96.8$ W, a 2.99x power increase for 2x frequency. With $\theta_{JA} = 0.5$ K/W and a 45 °C local ambient, $T_j$ rises from 61 °C to 93 °C.
:::

::: short
"Dynamic power is $\alpha C V^2 f$, so at fixed voltage it's linear in frequency. Performance also rises with frequency, but only for the part of the workload not waiting on memory. The catch is that higher frequency needs higher voltage to meet timing, so along a DVFS curve power grows much faster than linearly, about 3x for 2x frequency in a typical example. All that power becomes heat, $T_j = T_a + P\theta$, and leakage rises with temperature, which can feed back into throttling. That's why frequency is the main knob for managing power and thermals."
:::

::: long
"Start with the circuit. A CMOS node dissipates energy each time it switches, about $C V^2$ per charge-discharge cycle. If a fraction $\alpha$ of the capacitance switches each clock, then dynamic power is $\alpha C V^2 f$. So at a fixed voltage, power is linear in frequency. Notice that energy per operation doesn't change, so running faster at the same voltage just finishes the work sooner.

Performance is execution time $= $ instructions times CPI divided by frequency. So frequency helps linearly, but only for the part of the runtime that scales with the clock. DRAM latency, flash and network wait times don't. If 30% of time is memory-bound, then doubling the clock gives 1.54x, not 2x.

Now the coupling that makes this interesting: timing. The cycle time must exceed the critical path delay, and gate delay depends on supply voltage, going roughly like $V/(V - V_t)^{\alpha}$. To run faster you raise $V$. Power goes with $V^2$, and $V$ rises with $f$, so along the DVFS curve power grows much faster than frequency, toward $f^3$ if voltage scaled in proportion. In numbers: 2 GHz at 0.9 V versus 4 GHz at 1.1 V is 2x frequency and about 3x power. That's why phones don't run at peak frequency for long.

Finally thermals. Power becomes heat; junction temperature is ambient plus power times the thermal resistance to ambient. Higher temperature increases leakage, which adds power, a positive feedback. If the cooling can't handle it, the chip must throttle: reduce frequency, and ideally voltage too, until temperature stabilizes. And on the other side, when there's little work, I drop frequency and voltage together to save energy, which is DVFS."
:::

::: terms
- **Dynamic power**: switching power, $\alpha C V^2 f$.
- **Activity factor ($\alpha$)**: the average fraction of nodes that toggle per cycle.
- **Static / leakage power**: power drawn with no switching, mostly subthreshold and gate leakage.
- **Critical path**: the slowest register-to-register path, which sets $f_{max}$.
- **DVFS**: dynamic voltage and frequency scaling.
- **Thermal resistance ($\theta_{JA}$)**: junction-to-ambient temperature rise per watt (K/W).
- **Throttling**: reducing frequency or activity to stay inside thermal or power limits.
- **Thermal runaway**: leakage-temperature positive feedback that diverges without intervention.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "higher frequency means more power, more heat and more performance."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Writes $P = \alpha C V^2 f$ and states that *energy per operation* is independent of $f$ at fixed $V$.
- Explains that performance gains are sub-linear (memory-bound fraction) with a number.
- Brings in the **voltage coupling** and quantifies the super-linear power growth (bounded by $f^3$) with a concrete example (2 GHz / 0.9 V vs 4 GHz / 1.1 V).
- Chains it to temperature, $T_j = T_a + P\theta$, and mentions the **leakage-temperature feedback**.
- Names the system response: DVFS, boost and thermal throttling, and race-to-idle versus slow-and-steady trade-offs.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Power is proportional to frequency" (full stop).** True only at fixed voltage. In practice $V$ changes with $f$ and power rises faster.
- **"Higher clock always means proportionally faster."** Memory latency, I/O and IPC limits mean the speedup is usually less.
- **Forgetting static power.** Leakage does not scale with frequency; at low $f$ it can dominate, which is why simply slowing the clock does not always save energy.
- **Confusing power and energy.** Halving $f$ at fixed $V$ halves power but doubles runtime, so the energy per task is unchanged (plus leakage energy goes *up* with a longer run).
- **Ignoring temperature feedback.** Leakage increases with $T$, so a hot chip is not at the power you measured cold.
- **Treating the thermal limit as instant.** Heat capacity gives a time constant of seconds, so short boosts above the sustained power limit are allowed.
:::

::: deep
## Where frequency power comes from

Each CMOS gate output drives a load capacitance $C_L$ (wire plus the next gates' inputs). Charging it from 0 to $V_{DD}$ draws charge $Q = C_L V_{DD}$ from the supply, i.e. energy $C_L V_{DD}^2$ from the supply, of which half is stored in the capacitor and half is dissipated in the PMOS. Discharging dissipates the stored half in the NMOS. So per full cycle of one node: $E = C_L V_{DD}^2$, and summed over the chip with activity factor $\alpha$:

$$P_{dyn} = \alpha\, C_{tot}\, V_{DD}^2\, f$$

The clock itself is the busiest signal in the chip: $\alpha = 1$ on the clock network (it toggles every cycle, twice), and clock distribution plus flip-flops commonly account for a large fraction of dynamic power (often cited as 20% to 40%, design dependent), which is why clock gating pays off ([[q0952]]).

There is also **short-circuit power** (both transistors briefly on during an edge, a few percent to roughly 10% of dynamic) and **leakage**, which does not depend on $f$.

## The performance side

Execution time is $T = \dfrac{N \cdot CPI}{f}$. In a real workload some stalls are measured in **nanoseconds, not cycles**: a DRAM access of ~80 ns costs 160 cycles at 2 GHz and 320 cycles at 4 GHz. So CPI itself rises with $f$ for memory-bound code. If a fraction $m$ of the runtime is frequency-independent, the speedup from scaling $f$ by $k$ is:

$$S(k) = \frac{1}{m + (1-m)/k}$$

## Why higher frequency needs higher voltage

The clock period must satisfy $T_{clk} \ge t_{clk\to q} + t_{logic} + t_{setup} + t_{skew}$. Logic delay is set by how fast transistors charge capacitances, and drive current $I_{on}\propto (V_{DD}-V_t)^{\alpha}$, so $t_d \propto C V_{DD}/I_{on} = \dfrac{C\,V_{DD}}{k\,(V_{DD}-V_t)^\alpha}$. To make $t_d$ smaller you raise $V_{DD}$. A typical DVFS table might look like this (illustrative, $\alpha C = 20$ nF):

| Operating point | Voltage | Frequency | $P_{dyn}$ | $P / f$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 0.8 V | 1 GHz | 12.8 W | 12.8 nJ/cycle |
| Mid | 0.9 V | 2 GHz | 32.4 W | 16.2 nJ/cycle |
| High | 1.0 V | 3 GHz | 60 W | 20 nJ/cycle |
| Boost | 1.1 V | 4 GHz | 96.8 W | 24.2 nJ/cycle |

The last column is *energy per cycle* and it rises with voltage squared: the boost point costs 1.9x the energy per operation of the low point, for 4x the speed. So you "pay" in battery life for speed.

## Thermals

All dissipated power becomes heat. The steady-state junction temperature is

$$T_j = T_a + P_{tot}\,\theta_{JA}$$

With $\theta_{JA}=0.5$ K/W and 45 °C local ambient: 32.4 W gives $T_j \approx 61$ °C; 96.8 W gives $T_j \approx 93$ °C. The heat capacity of die, spreader and heatsink gives a **thermal time constant** of seconds to tens of seconds, so a chip can exceed its sustainable power for a short burst (turbo) before the temperature climbs to the limit.

**Leakage feedback.** $P_{leak}$ rises roughly exponentially with $T_j$. The loop is: higher $f$ gives more $P$, which gives higher $T_j$, which gives more leakage and more $P$. If $\theta$ is too high the loop does not converge, which is thermal runaway; in practice the thermal management firmware intervenes first ([[q0953]]).

## What does and doesn't save energy

Reducing only the clock at fixed voltage reduces power but not energy per task: the task takes longer at the same $\alpha C V^2$ per operation, and leakage energy ($P_{leak}\times$ runtime) *increases*. To save energy you must reduce **voltage** along with frequency ([[q0947]]). Conversely, **race-to-idle** (run fast, then power-gate or sleep) wins when leakage and fixed platform power dominate.

## Multiple clocks, one chip

Not every block needs the top clock. A GPU, a CPU and a memory controller run at different frequencies, each with its own DVFS, which is why chips have multiple clock domains ([[q0946]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Smartphone SoCs**: big and little cores each have a table of voltage-frequency points; the governor picks the lowest point that meets the load.
- **Laptop and desktop CPUs**: turbo/boost frequencies above the sustained rating, limited by power and temperature budgets.
- **Data-center processors**: power capping and frequency limits to fit rack power budgets.
- **Microcontrollers**: run at low clock (e.g. a few MHz) most of the time for µA-class average current, then burst up for compute.
- **GPUs and AI accelerators**: high activity factor, so power and thermal limits set the sustained clock.
- **Overclocking**: raises $f$ and $V$ together, accepting a steeply super-linear power cost and needing better cooling.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CMOS digital logic** with the simple $\alpha C V^2 f$ model, ignoring short-circuit current and treating $C$ as constant.
- **Voltage scaling follows an illustrative linear V-f relation** ($V = 0.7 + 0.1f$); real silicon has its own measured curve, flattens at the low end ($V_{min}$) and may have steeper rise at the top.
- **Thermal model is a single resistance** at steady state; real packages and coolers have multiple RC stages and transient behavior.
- **At very low voltages** power no longer scales as $V^2 f$ in the same way (leakage dominates), and there is a minimum-energy voltage near or below threshold.
- If the interviewer is thinking of a **microcontroller**, the same principles hold but flash wait states can make performance scale even less with frequency.
:::

::: followups
### If I double the clock frequency, does power double?
Only if the voltage stays the same and the activity factor is unchanged, in which case dynamic power doubles. Typically doubling $f$ requires more voltage, so power rises by 2x times $(V_2/V_1)^2$, e.g. 3x for 0.9 V to 1.1 V. Leakage power stays the same at a given temperature but rises when the chip heats up.

### Why is it more efficient to run at a lower frequency and voltage?
Energy per operation is $\alpha C V^2$, so lowering $V$ cuts energy quadratically. Going from 1.1 V to 0.9 V reduces energy per operation to $(0.9/1.1)^2 = 67\%$, a 33% saving, at the cost of speed. That is the entire premise of DVFS and of big.LITTLE-style designs.

### Can reducing frequency alone save battery?
It reduces power but not energy per task. The work takes longer, and leakage plus fixed platform power (display, radios, DRAM refresh) are drawn for longer, so total energy may actually go up. It helps thermals (lower instantaneous power), but to save energy you need to lower the voltage, or finish fast and sleep (race-to-idle).

### Why does a hot chip run slower and burn more power?
Higher temperature reduces carrier mobility, which increases gate delay in many regimes (and in modern low-voltage nodes there can be temperature inversion where cold is slower, see [[q0949]]), and it increases leakage exponentially. Controllers therefore reduce the frequency when the temperature sensor crosses a threshold.

### Scenario: a laptop hits 4 GHz for a few seconds then drops to 2.5 GHz and stays there. Explain.
That's boost behavior against a thermal and power budget. The chip may exceed the sustained power rating while thermal mass is absorbing heat, then once the junction or skin temperature reaches the limit (or a power-limit timer expires), firmware lowers frequency and voltage until power equals what the cooling can remove. Better cooling, a higher power limit, or a lighter workload would hold boost longer.
:::

::: interpretations
"Impact power, performance, and thermals" is a three-part question: a good answer addresses **all three and how they interact** (frequency gives performance, performance costs power, power gives heat, heat limits frequency). Interviewers often follow with voltage ([[q0947]]), so state the voltage coupling early. If the interviewer is in a microcontroller context, mention that wait states and peripheral clocks complicate the scaling.
:::
