::: testing
Whether you understand the CMOS inverter as two transistors that **both conduct for part of every transition**, not as two ideal switches. The interviewer wants:

1. The mechanism: while the input is between $V_{tn}$ and $V_{DD} - |V_{tp}|$, both FETs are on and a "crowbar" current flows straight from $V_{DD}$ to ground.
2. The consequences: wasted power (short-circuit power), supply current spikes and ground bounce, and in power stages, destroyed FETs.
3. The prevention toolbox at two levels: logic gates (fast input edges, Schmitt triggers for slow signals, no floating inputs, balanced sizing) and drivers/power stages (separate gate drives with break-before-make dead time).
:::

::: why
Shoot-through is a real-world power and reliability problem. In logic it's the reason a floating GPIO input raises sleep current, the reason slow RC-filtered signals need a Schmitt-trigger input, and one of the terms in every chip's power budget ([[q0110]]). In power electronics it's how half-bridges blow up: if the high-side and low-side FETs of a buck or motor driver overlap, the supply is shorted through two milliohm switches ([[q0207]]). Interviewers ask it because it connects device physics (thresholds) with timing, power and layout.
:::

::: concepts
- **Threshold voltages**: the NMOS conducts once $V_{in} > V_{tn}$; the PMOS conducts while $V_{in} < V_{DD} - |V_{tp}|$. See [[q0099]].
- **Overlap window**: when $V_{tn} < V_{in} < V_{DD} - |V_{tp}|$, both conduct. This window exists whenever $V_{DD} > V_{tn} + |V_{tp}|$.
- **Square-law saturation current**: $I_D = \frac{k}{2}(V_{GS} - V_t)^2$, which sets the peak crowbar current at the switching threshold.
- **Short-circuit power**: energy lost per transition, proportional to the input transition time.
- **Dead time / break-before-make**: turning one switch fully off before turning the other on, used when the two gates can be driven separately.
- **Schmitt trigger**: an input with hysteresis that turns a slow edge into a fast one.
:::

::: answer
"Shoot-through, or crowbar current, is when both the PMOS and NMOS in a CMOS stage are on at the same time, so current flows straight from $V_{DD}$ to ground instead of into the load.

It happens on every transition. The NMOS turns on once the input passes $V_{tn}$, but the PMOS doesn't turn off until the input gets within $|V_{tp}|$ of $V_{DD}$. Between those, both are conducting, and the current peaks around the switching threshold, near $V_{DD}/2$. On a normal fast edge that's a short spike and a modest power cost. It gets bad when the input edge is slow, or when an input is left floating and sits near mid-rail, because then the crowbar current flows for a long time or continuously.

To prevent it in logic: keep input edges fast, use Schmitt-trigger inputs for slow or noisy signals so the internal edge is fast, never leave CMOS inputs floating, and size stages so input and output transition times are similar. For output drivers and power stages, where the two FETs can have separate gate signals, you use break-before-make: turn the PMOS fully off, wait a dead time, then turn the NMOS on, and vice versa. That's exactly what half-bridge gate drivers do, because there a shoot-through event can short the supply through two power FETs and destroy them."
:::

::: technical
**Condition.** For a static CMOS inverter with $V_{DD} > V_{tn} + |V_{tp}|$, both devices conduct for
$$V_{tn} < V_{in} < V_{DD} - |V_{tp}|.$$

**Peak current.** For a symmetric inverter ($k_n = k_p = \beta$, $V_{tn} = |V_{tp}| = V_t$) with no load, the current is limited by whichever device is in saturation:
$$I_{sc}(V_{in}) = \frac{\beta}{2}\min\!\left[(V_{in}-V_t)^2,\ (V_{DD}-V_{in}-V_t)^2\right],\qquad I_{peak} = \frac{\beta}{2}\left(\frac{V_{DD}}{2}-V_t\right)^2.$$
$\beta = 1$ mA/V², $V_{DD} = 3.3$ V, $V_t = 0.7$ V gives $I_{peak} \approx 0.45$ mA.

**Short-circuit power (Veendrick, unloaded, linear input ramps of duration $\tau$):**
$$P_{sc} = \frac{\beta}{12}\,(V_{DD}-2V_t)^3\,\tau\,f.$$
With the numbers above, $\tau = 1$ ns, $f = 100$ MHz: $P_{sc} \approx 57$ µW. At $\tau = 10$ ns: 570 µW. It scales linearly with input transition time. A load capacitance reduces it because the output lags and one device stays near $V_{DS} \approx 0$ longer.

**Floating input.** An input stuck at $V_{DD}/2$ draws $I_{peak}$ continuously: 0.45 mA × 3.3 V ≈ 1.5 mW for this one gate.

**Prevention.**
- Logic: fast input edges; Schmitt-trigger inputs for slow signals; tie or pull unused inputs; match input and output slopes (keeps $P_{sc}$ to ~10–20% of dynamic power); at very low supply, $V_{DD} < V_{tn} + |V_{tp}|$ eliminates the overlap altogether, at a large speed cost.
- Drivers/power stages: separate PMOS/NMOS (or high/low-side) gate signals with **non-overlapping** timing (break-before-make), adaptive dead-time, and gate pull-downs so a floating driver can't half-enhance a FET. Also guard against $dV/dt$-induced turn-on via $C_{GD}$.
:::

::: long
"Shoot-through, sometimes called crowbar current, is the moment in a CMOS stage when both the pull-up PMOS and the pull-down NMOS are conducting, so there's a low-resistance path from $V_{DD}$ to ground that bypasses the load.

The reason it happens is the thresholds. Say $V_{DD}$ is 3.3 volts and both thresholds are about 0.7. As the input rises, the NMOS starts conducting at 0.7 volts, but the PMOS doesn't shut off until the input reaches 3.3 minus 0.7, which is 2.6. So the whole window from 0.7 to 2.6 volts has both devices on. The current peaks around the switching point, near mid-rail, and for a small gate it's maybe a few hundred microamps.

Is that a problem? On a fast edge it's a brief spike, and it shows up as a small extra term in the power budget, called short-circuit power. It's proportional to how long the input spends in that window, so it scales with the input rise time. Where it really hurts is slow or floating inputs. A slow RC-filtered signal sits in the window for microseconds, and a floating input can sit there forever, which is why an unconnected CMOS pin raises your sleep current and can even oscillate.

So, prevention. For logic gates, keep input edges fast; use Schmitt-trigger inputs when the signal is slow or noisy, because the hysteresis makes the internal transition fast and clean; never leave inputs floating; and size the stages so the input and output transitions are about equally fast.

For output drivers and power stages it's more serious, because the 'both on' path is two power FETs across the supply, which can mean tens of amps. There, you don't tie the gates together. You drive the high-side and low-side gates separately with break-before-make timing: turn one fully off, wait a dead time, then turn the other on. Every half-bridge gate driver has that built in, and I'd also put pull-downs on the gates so nothing half-turns-on during power-up."
:::

::: terms
- **Shoot-through / crowbar current**: current flowing directly from $V_{DD}$ to ground through simultaneously conducting pull-up and pull-down devices.
- **Short-circuit power**: the average power wasted by shoot-through, $\propto \tau f$.
- **Overlap window**: the input range $V_{tn} < V_{in} < V_{DD} - |V_{tp}|$ where both FETs conduct.
- **Dead time**: a deliberate interval where both switches are off between one turning off and the other turning on.
- **Break-before-make**: switching sequence that guarantees the dead time.
- **Non-overlapping clocks/gate signals**: two control signals that are never active simultaneously.
- **Schmitt trigger**: input stage with hysteresis that switches cleanly on slow or noisy inputs.
- **Ground bounce**: supply/ground voltage noise from fast current spikes through package inductance.
:::

::: exceptional
**Average answer:** "Both transistors are on and you get a short. Use dead time."

**Exceptional answer:**
- Gives the exact window ($V_{tn} < V_{in} < V_{DD} - |V_{tp}|$) and where the current peaks ($V_M$).
- Quantifies it: peak current from the square law, and that short-circuit power scales with input transition time (Veendrick's $\frac{\beta}{12}(V_{DD}-2V_t)^3\tau f$).
- Distinguishes **logic gates** (gates tied together, so no dead time possible: fix with fast edges, Schmitt inputs, sizing, no floating pins) from **drivers/power stages** (separate gate drives with break-before-make).
- Mentions the floating-input case as a practical source of excess current.
- Mentions second-order effects: supply spikes, ground bounce, $dV/dt$-induced turn-on in half-bridges.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Add dead time to a CMOS inverter."** In a simple gate both gates are the same wire; you can't insert dead time. Dead time needs separately driven gates.
- **Thinking shoot-through only happens in faults.** In ordinary CMOS logic it happens briefly on every transition; it's a normal power term, kept small by fast edges.
- **Confusing shoot-through with bus contention.** Contention is two *different* outputs fighting on a shared net ([[q0788]]). Shoot-through is inside one stage.
- **Ignoring floating inputs.** An unused CMOS input left open can sit mid-rail and draw shoot-through current continuously.
- **Making dead time "as long as possible".** In a half-bridge, excessive dead time makes the body diode conduct longer, adding loss and reverse-recovery problems. It's a trade-off.
:::

::: deep
## Where the overlap comes from

Put the inverter's two "on" conditions side by side, with the input as the only variable:

- NMOS on: $V_{GS,N} = V_{in} > V_{tn}$
- PMOS on: $V_{SG,P} = V_{DD} - V_{in} > |V_{tp}|$, i.e. $V_{in} < V_{DD} - |V_{tp}|$

Both are true for $V_{tn} < V_{in} < V_{DD} - |V_{tp}|$. With $V_{DD} = 3.3$ V and $V_t = 0.7$ V that's 0.7–2.6 V, nearly 60% of the swing. The window only disappears if $V_{DD} < V_{tn} + |V_{tp}|$, which is a deliberate choice in some ultra-low-power subthreshold designs, at a large speed cost.

## How big is the current?

With no load (worst case), the output moves almost instantly, so whichever device is in saturation limits the current. For a symmetric inverter ($k_n = k_p = \beta$):
$$I_{sc}(V_{in}) = \frac{\beta}{2}\min\!\left[(V_{in}-V_t)^2,\ (V_{DD}-V_{in}-V_t)^2\right]$$
peaking at $V_{in} = V_{DD}/2$:
$$I_{peak} = \frac{\beta}{2}\left(\frac{V_{DD}}{2}-V_t\right)^2 = \frac{1\text{ mA/V}^2}{2}(1.65-0.7)^2 \approx 0.45\text{ mA}$$

## Short-circuit energy and power

If the input ramps linearly over a time $\tau$ (0 to $V_{DD}$), then $dV_{in} = (V_{DD}/\tau)\,dt$. The charge passed per edge is twice the integral from $V_t$ to $V_{DD}/2$:
$$Q_{edge} = 2\int_{V_t}^{V_{DD}/2}\frac{\beta}{2}(v-V_t)^2\,\frac{\tau}{V_{DD}}\,dv = \frac{\beta\,\tau}{24\,V_{DD}}(V_{DD}-2V_t)^3$$
Two edges per period, drawn from $V_{DD}$:
$$\boxed{P_{sc} = \frac{\beta}{12}(V_{DD}-2V_t)^3\,\tau\,f}$$
This is Veendrick's classic result. Three things to read off it:

1. **Linear in input transition time $\tau$.** Halve the edge time, halve the waste.
2. **Cubic in $(V_{DD}-2V_t)$.** Lowering $V_{DD}$ shrinks it fast; at $V_{DD} = 2V_t$ it vanishes.
3. **Proportional to $\beta$.** Bigger (stronger) devices crowbar harder, so a big output stage driven by a slow predriver is the worst case.

**Worked example.** $\beta = 1$ mA/V², $V_{DD} = 3.3$ V, $V_t = 0.7$ V, $f = 100$ MHz:

| Input edge $\tau$ | $P_{sc}$ | vs. $CV^2f$ for $C = 100$ fF (109 µW) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 ns | 57 µW | about half |
| 10 ns | 572 µW | about 5× larger |

The formula is the *unloaded* worst case. With a load capacitance the output lags the input, so the device that's turning on sees little $V_{DS}$ for much of the overlap and the real crowbar current is smaller. Designers aim to make each stage's input and output slopes comparable, which keeps short-circuit power to roughly 10–20% of dynamic power.

## The pathological cases: slow and floating inputs

- **Slow inputs.** An RC-debounced button, a long cable, or a sensor output with a 10 µs edge keeps the input inside the overlap window 10,000× longer than a 1 ns edge. Besides the wasted energy, noise on a slow edge can make the output toggle several times. The fix is a **Schmitt-trigger** input: its hysteresis makes the first stage snap, so downstream stages see a fast edge.
- **Floating inputs.** An unconnected CMOS input is a few pF of gate capacitance with nothing defining its voltage. It drifts, often near mid-rail, and the gate then draws close to $I_{peak}$ forever: for our example gate about $0.45\text{ mA}\times3.3\text{ V} \approx 1.5$ mW. On an MCU, a handful of floating pins can multiply sleep current. Fix: internal pull-ups/pull-downs, tie unused inputs, or configure unused GPIOs as outputs or analog.

## Prevention in drivers and power stages: break-before-make

In a simple gate the PMOS and NMOS gates are one wire, so there is no way to insert a delay between them. In an **output driver** (an I/O pad, a gate driver, a half-bridge), you can split the gates and drive them with separate, non-overlapping signals.

During the dead time both devices are off and the output briefly floats (in a half-bridge with an inductive load, a body diode carries the current).

The same principle, scaled up, is **dead time in half-bridges** ([[q0207]]). In a buck converter or motor driver the "both on" path is two power MOSFETs with a few mΩ each across a stiff supply, so shoot-through current is limited only by $R_{DS(on)}$ and stray inductance: easily tens or hundreds of amps for nanoseconds. Gate drivers therefore provide fixed or adaptive dead time (adaptive drivers watch the switch-node or gate voltage to decide when it's safe to turn the other FET on). Too little dead time risks shoot-through; too much makes the body diode conduct longer, adding loss and reverse-recovery current.

A subtler half-bridge failure is **$dV/dt$-induced turn-on**: when the switch node slews fast, current through the off FET's $C_{GD}$ into its gate resistance can lift its $V_{GS}$ above threshold. Strong gate pull-down (low-impedance off-state drive, negative gate drive in high-power designs) prevents it.

## Side effects beyond power

Each shoot-through spike is a fast current pulse through the supply pins. With package inductance $L$, many outputs switching together create ground bounce $V = L\,di/dt$, which is why decoupling capacitors sit right at every supply pin.
:::

::: applications
- **MCU low-power design**: configuring unused GPIOs (analog mode, or output-low) to avoid floating-input crowbar current in sleep.
- **Schmitt-trigger buffers** (e.g. 74LVC1G17, 74HC14) for RC-debounced buttons, slow sensor outputs and signals arriving over long cables.
- **I/O pad drivers**: separate predrivers for the output PMOS and NMOS so large output devices don't crowbar.
- **Half-bridge gate drivers** in buck converters and motor drivers with fixed or adaptive dead time. See [[q0207]].
- **Class-D audio output stages**: dead time trades shoot-through risk against distortion.
- **Clock buffers**: sharp edges into each stage keep short-circuit power low in high-fanout clock trees.
:::

::: assumptions
- The equations use the long-channel square-law model and a symmetric inverter ($k_n = k_p$, $V_{tn} = |V_{tp}|$). Short-channel devices follow a different current law, but the window and the dependence on transition time remain.
- Veendrick's formula assumes no output load (worst case) and linear input ramps. Real loaded gates waste less.
- I'm assuming $V_{DD} > V_{tn} + |V_{tp}|$. Below that (subthreshold logic), there is no strong-inversion overlap, though small subthreshold currents still flow.
- For a logic gate, "prevention" means minimizing; it can't be eliminated with tied gates. For drivers with independent gate signals it can be eliminated with dead time, subject to the diode-conduction trade-off.
:::

::: followups
### Can you add dead time to a standard CMOS inverter?
Not without changing its structure. The two gates are the same node, so they always see the same voltage. You'd have to split the gates and drive each through its own path, for example a fast-off/slow-on predriver for each device, which is what output buffers and gate drivers do.

### Why does a slow input edge increase power?
Short-circuit energy per edge is $\frac{\beta}{24}(V_{DD}-2V_t)^3\tau$: linear in the input transition time. A 10 ns edge wastes 10× the energy of a 1 ns edge. Very slow edges also risk multiple output transitions from noise, each with its own $CV^2$ cost.

### What's the difference between shoot-through and bus contention?
Shoot-through is inside one gate: its own PMOS and NMOS overlap during a transition. Contention is two separate push-pull outputs connected to one net, one driving high and the other low, so current flows from one's PMOS through the other's NMOS. Contention can last indefinitely and is a design bug; see [[q0788]].

### How does lowering $V_{DD}$ affect shoot-through?
The overlap window is $V_{DD} - V_{tn} - |V_{tp}|$ wide and $P_{sc} \propto (V_{DD}-2V_t)^3$. Going from 3.3 V to 1.8 V with 0.7 V thresholds shrinks $(V_{DD}-2V_t)^3$ from $1.9^3 = 6.86$ to $0.4^3 = 0.064$, about 100× less. Below $V_{tn}+|V_{tp}|$ the window disappears entirely.

### In a half-bridge, what happens if the dead time is too long?
During dead time the inductor current has to go somewhere, so it flows through a body diode. That diode conducts with roughly 0.7–1 V of drop instead of a few mV across the FET channel, adding conduction loss, and when the opposite FET turns on, the diode's reverse-recovery charge causes a current spike and switching loss. So dead time is set just long enough to cover gate-driver and FET delay spread over temperature.
