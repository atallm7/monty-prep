::: testing
Whether you can explain subharmonic oscillation as a **discrete-time (cycle-by-cycle) instability** of fixed-frequency peak-current-mode control, and not just recite "it happens above 50% duty". The interviewer wants:

1. What it looks like: alternating wide and narrow pulses, a period-2 pattern at $f_{sw}/2$.
2. Why it happens: a perturbation in the inductor current changes the on-time, and the changed on-time changes the next cycle's starting current by a factor $-m_2/m_1=-D/(1-D)$.
3. The condition: $|\alpha|<1$, that is $D<0.5$, without compensation.
4. The cure: slope compensation (next page [[q0255]]) and the contrast with loop instability.
:::

::: why
This is a classic "have you actually debugged a peak-current-mode buck" question. A converter that is perfectly stable at 12 V input can start squealing or showing pulse jitter at 5 V input, because the duty cycle crossed 50%. Knowing the mechanism lets you diagnose it quickly on a scope, and choose inductor and slope compensation values that avoid it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Peak current-mode control**: switch on at clock, off when $i_L$ reaches the command ([[q0252]]).
- **Inductor slopes**: $m_1=(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ rising, $m_2=V_{out}/L$ falling ([[q0188]]).
- **Duty cycle**: $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$, so $m_2/m_1=D/(1-D)$.
- **Discrete-time map**: the current at the start of cycle $n+1$ as a function of cycle $n$.
- **Eigenvalue/multiplier $\alpha$**: stable if $|\alpha|<1$.
- **Period doubling**: instability at $f_{sw}/2$.
:::

::: answer
"Subharmonic oscillation is an instability of fixed-frequency peak current-mode control where the inductor current and duty cycle alternate cycle by cycle, so you get a pattern of a long pulse, a short pulse, a long pulse, repeating at half the switching frequency.

Here's why. In peak current mode the high side turns on at each clock edge and turns off when the inductor current hits the control level. Suppose in one cycle the starting current is a little higher than normal by delta. It reaches the threshold sooner, by delta over m1 where m1 is the rising slope, so the on-time is shorter, and the off-time is longer by that same amount. During the longer off-time the current falls at m2, the falling slope, so it falls an extra m2 times delta over m1. So at the start of the next cycle the error is minus m2 over m1 times delta: the sign flips, and the size is multiplied by m2 over m1.

With the slopes in terms of duty cycle, m2 over m1 is D over one minus D. If D is below 50 percent, that factor is less than one, so the error shrinks every cycle and the converter is stable. At exactly 50 percent it neither grows nor shrinks, and above 50 percent it grows, so the pulses alternate wider and narrower with growing amplitude until something nonlinear limits it, like a skipped pulse or the clamp.

The fix is slope compensation: add an artificial ramp to the sensed current, or subtract it from the command, so the threshold falls during the on-time. That changes the factor to minus m2 minus m a over m1 plus m a, which can be made less than one for any duty cycle."
:::

::: technical
**Map derivation.** Let $I_n$ be the inductor current at the start of cycle $n$ (a clock edge), $I_c$ the peak threshold, $m_1$ the on-slope and $m_2$ the (positive) off-slope magnitude, $T$ the period, no compensation. Turn-off occurs when $I_n+m_1t_{on,n}=I_c$, so $t_{on,n}=(I_c-I_n)/m_1$. The next start current is

$$I_{n+1}=I_c-m_2(T-t_{on,n})=I_c-m_2T+\frac{m_2}{m_1}(I_c-I_n)$$

Perturb: $\Delta I_{n+1}=-\dfrac{m_2}{m_1}\Delta I_n$. So $\alpha=-\dfrac{m_2}{m_1}=-\dfrac{D}{1-D}$.

With a compensation ramp of slope $m_a$ added to the sensed current (threshold falls as $I_c-m_at$): $t_{on,n}=(I_c-I_n)/(m_1+m_a)$ and

$$\alpha=-\frac{m_2-m_a}{m_1+m_a}$$

**Condition.** $|\alpha|<1\iff m_a>\dfrac{m_2-m_1}{2}$. With $m_a=0$ this is $m_2<m_1$, i.e. $D<0.5$.

**Numbers.** $V_{in}=12$ V, $L=10$ µH, $f_{sw}=200$ kHz, 2 A average:

| $D$ | $\alpha$ | cycles to 1% (decay) or growth of 20× |
|---|---|---|
| 0.30 | $-0.43$ | 5.4 cycles to 1% |
| 0.40 | $-0.67$ | 11.4 cycles |
| 0.45 | $-0.82$ | 22.9 cycles |
| 0.50 | $-1.00$ | marginal |
| 0.60 | $-1.50$ | grows 20× in 7.4 cycles |
| 0.70 | $-2.33$ | grows 20× in 3.5 cycles |

At $D=0.6$: $m_1=0.48$ A/µs, $m_2=0.72$ A/µs. With $m_a=0.5m_2=0.36$ A/µs, $\alpha=-(0.72-0.36)/(0.48+0.36)=-0.43$. With $m_a=m_2$, $\alpha=0$ (dead-beat).

**Equivalent frequency-domain statement (Ridley's sampled model).** A double pole at $f_{sw}/2$ with $Q_p=1/[\pi(m_cD'-0.5)]$, $m_c=1+m_a/m_1$, $D'=1-D$. With $m_a=0$, $Q_p\to\infty$ as $D\to0.5$.

**Nonlinear outcome.** The map is only linear for small errors. Once the error grows, the on-time hits its limits (zero, or a full period), so the converter settles into a large, bounded period-2 pattern of long and very short (or skipped) pulses.
:::

::: long
"Subharmonic oscillation is what happens when a fixed-frequency peak current-mode buck becomes unstable in a cycle-to-cycle way. On the scope you see the switch node pulse widths alternate: long, short, long, short, so the pattern repeats at half the switching frequency. That's why it's called subharmonic.

The mechanism is simple once you write the inductor current for two consecutive cycles. In peak current mode the clock turns the high side on, the inductor current rises at m1, which is V in minus V out over L, and when it reaches the command level the switch turns off and the current falls at m2, which is V out over L, until the next clock.

Suppose the current at the start of one cycle is higher than normal by a small amount delta. It reaches the threshold earlier by delta over m1. So the on-time is shorter and the off-time is longer by the same amount, and over the longer off-time the current falls by an extra m2 times delta over m1. At the start of the next cycle, the starting current is lower than normal by m2 over m1 times delta. So the error flips sign every cycle and is multiplied by minus m2 over m1 each time.

In terms of duty cycle m2 over m1 is D over one minus D. For D under 50 percent that's less than one, so the error decays: at 40 percent it's 0.67 per cycle, and a perturbation is gone in about 11 cycles. At exactly 50 percent it never decays. Above 50 percent, say 60 percent, the factor is 1.5, so it grows 50 percent each cycle until nonlinear limits clamp it, usually pulses that skip or hit maximum width.

It's different from loop instability, which is at a few kHz to tens of kHz and is about phase margin. Subharmonic oscillation is at half the switching frequency and lives in the inner current loop. You can see it even if the averaged loop looks great on the Bode plot.

The cure is slope compensation: add a ramp to the sensed current, so the effective threshold falls during the on-time. That changes the multiplier to minus m2 minus m a over m1 plus m a. A ramp of at least half the down-slope makes it stable at any duty cycle, and a ramp equal to m2 gives a dead-beat response where a disturbance is gone in one cycle."
:::

::: terms
- **Subharmonic oscillation**: oscillation at $f_{sw}/2$ (period doubling) in a fixed-frequency converter.
- **Peak current-mode control**: switch turns off when inductor current reaches the command.
- **$m_1$, $m_2$**: inductor rise and fall slopes.
- **$m_a$ (slope compensation)**: ramp slope added to the current sense signal.
- **Perturbation multiplier $\alpha$**: per-cycle ratio of error amplitudes (negative: alternates).
- **Dead-beat response**: $\alpha=0$, a perturbation vanishes in one cycle.
- **Period doubling**: a bifurcation where a period-1 waveform becomes period-2.
- **Sampled-data model**: model that treats the converter as updating once per cycle.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "above 50 percent duty cycle current mode is unstable and needs slope compensation".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives $\alpha=-m_2/m_1=-D/(1-D)$ from the two-cycle current picture.
- Says the oscillation is at $f_{sw}/2$ with alternating pulses and how it looks on the scope.
- Gives the condition with slope compensation, $|\alpha|<1\iff m_a>(m_2-m_1)/2$, and the dead-beat case.
- Distinguishes it from loop instability and notes it can coexist with a good averaged loop.
- Mentions the nonlinear end state (skipped or max-width pulses).
- Notes valley mode mirrors it ($D<0.5$) and that DCM, voltage mode and COT don't have it.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Calling it "ripple" or "jitter" without the $f_{sw}/2$ pattern.** The alternating pulse pattern is the signature.
- **Believing it's an LC resonance.** It's a property of the discrete current loop, independent of $C$ and the LC double pole.
- **Saying duty cycle exactly 50% is the safe boundary.** Practical designs need margin, since noise and tolerances change the effective slopes.
- **Fixing it with a compensator change.** The voltage-loop compensation does not cure it; you need slope compensation (or lower duty).
- **Believing it only happens at high $V_{out}/V_{in}$.** It happens whenever $D>0.5$, including a 12 V to 7 V converter and any converter in dropout.
- **Expecting small-signal Bode plots to show it.** Averaged models miss it unless the sampling double pole is included.
:::

Figure: The inductor current $i_L$ ramps up at $m_1=(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ while the high side is on and down at $m_2=V_{out}/L$ while the low side is on. Peak current mode compares $i_L$ with a command; the clock sets the next on-time.

Take the steady state: a rise of $m_1DT$ and a fall of $m_2(1-D)T$ that cancel. Now the start-of-cycle current is a bit higher by $\delta$. The sketch in words:

1. The ramp starts higher, so it meets the threshold earlier by $\delta/m_1$: **on-time shorter**.
2. The clock still arrives at the same time, so the off-time is longer by $\delta/m_1$.
3. During that extra off-time the current drops by an extra $m_2\delta/m_1$.
4. The next cycle therefore starts with an error of $-(m_2/m_1)\delta$ relative to nominal: opposite sign, scaled by $m_2/m_1$.

So the error alternates sign and scales by $m_2/m_1=D/(1-D)$.

## The same thing in the frequency domain

The sampled current loop contributes a double pole at $f_{sw}/2$ to the control-to-output transfer function. Its quality factor (Ridley) is

$$Q_p=\frac{1}{\pi(m_cD'-0.5)},\quad m_c=1+\frac{m_a}{m_1},\ D'=1-D$$

Without compensation ($m_c=1$): $Q_p=1/(\pi(0.5-D))$, which is 3.2 at $D=0.4$ and 6.4 at $D=0.45$ and infinite at 0.5. A high $Q_p$ gives a sharp peak at $f_{sw}/2$ that sits near the crossover limit and shows up as peaking and ringing even below 50% duty. With $m_a=m_2$, $Q_p=2/\pi=0.64$ for every duty cycle. This is why practical designs use more than the minimum ramp: it damps the sampling pole, not just prevent growth.

## What it looks like and how to find it

- **Scope**: switch-node pulse widths alternate every cycle, a spectrum line at $f_{sw}/2$, the inductor current showing alternating peaks. Audible hiss/whine if $f_{sw}/2$ is not too high, or with beat patterns.
- **Conditions**: low $V_{in}$ (high $D$), dropout, large $V_{out}$, small $L$ (larger $m_2$) or lower internal ramp.
- **Not to confuse**: loop oscillation at $f_0$ to $f_{sw}/10$ and burst behavior at light load.

## Where it doesn't occur

Voltage-mode PWM (no inner current loop); constant on-time and hysteretic control (no clock, no fixed instants); and DCM, where the current returns to zero each cycle and the memory of the previous cycle is lost. Valley current mode has a mirror problem at $D<0.5$.

::: applications
- **Debugging a buck that squeals at low input voltage**, where duty cycle crosses 50%.
- **Choosing internal ramp and minimum inductance** for current-mode IC datasheets.
- **Battery-powered converters** whose input drops near the output (dropout operation).
- **High-duty boost and buck-boost** converters, where the same analysis applies with their slopes.
- **Validation tests**: sweeping $V_{in}$ to the minimum with full load and checking the switch-node pulse train.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Fixed-frequency, trailing-edge peak current mode**, CCM.
- **Ideal comparator and no blanking**; real sense noise and delays alter the effective slopes.
- **Linear map** for small perturbations; the large-signal end state depends on limits (min on-time, max duty, current limit).
- **$m_2$ taken as positive magnitude**; the formulas use $m_a$ with the same sign convention as a falling threshold.
- Valley current mode and other schemes: by symmetry, replace $D$ with $1-D$.
:::

::: followups
### Why does subharmonic oscillation occur at exactly $f_{sw}/2$?
The error flips sign every cycle ($\alpha<0$), so it repeats every two cycles: period doubling. A positive $\alpha$ would give a slow monotonic drift. The negative sign comes from the fact that a higher start means a shorter on-time, and therefore a longer off-time and a lower next start.

### Does a bigger inductor help?
It reduces both $m_1$ and $m_2$ proportionally, so the ratio $m_2/m_1$ and $\alpha$ don't change. What changes is that a fixed internal ramp becomes relatively larger (since $m_2=V_{out}/L$ falls), so the compensation is more effective. That's why datasheets specify a minimum $L$ for a given internal ramp.

### What if $D=0.5$ exactly and there's no slope compensation?
$\alpha=-1$: marginal. Any perturbation persists at the same amplitude, alternating. In practice noise and tolerance push it either way, and the sampled double pole has $Q_p\to\infty$, so the loop is very peaky near $f_{sw}/2$.

### How does leading-edge blanking interact with this?
Blanking sets a minimum on-time. When the converter is asked for a shorter on-time, the comparator can't trip until blanking ends, so the effective $\alpha$ near that limit changes, and pulses may skip or jitter. This looks like subharmonic-like behavior at very low duty cycles, but its cause is different.

### Scenario: a 5 V to 3.3 V peak current-mode buck oscillates at half the switching frequency only when the battery drops below 5.5 V. Diagnosis and fix?
At $V_{in}=5.5$ V, $D=0.6$ and $\alpha=-1.5$ if the ramp is too small. Confirm with a scope: alternating pulse widths at $f_{sw}/2$. Fix: more slope compensation (a stronger internal ramp or a part with higher ramp), a larger inductor so the fixed ramp is relatively larger, or limit the minimum input voltage / use a part with 100% mode and reduced frequency. Re-verify across $V_{in}$ and load.
