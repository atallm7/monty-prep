::: testing
Whether you understand that capacitors in parallel are **not** just "added capacitance": every real capacitor has series inductance, so what matters at high frequency is inductance, spacing and the number of current paths. The interviewer wants:

1. The ESL argument: above SRF a capacitor is an inductor, and bigger values resonate lower.
2. The parallel argument: $N$ capacitors divide ESL, ESR and mounting inductance by $N$.
3. The *geometry* argument: one capacitor can only be close to one place; a chip with many pins needs charge near each.
4. The role of different values (bandwidth coverage) and the anti-resonance risk that comes with mixing.
:::

::: why
This is a classic, because the naive answer ("they're in parallel, so just add them up") is exactly what a good hardware engineer knows is wrong. The question separates people who treat capacitors as ideal components from those who understand impedance versus frequency. In the PCB context (where this question sits), it ties directly to layout: where the capacitors go, how many, and why a pile of 0402s under a BGA beats one 100 µF part at the edge of the board. The same question appears in power-rail form at [[q0142]]; here the emphasis is layout and package.
:::

::: concepts
- **Real capacitor model**: $C$ in series with ESR and ESL ([[q0030]]).
- **Self-resonance**: above $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{ESL\,C})$ the part is an inductor ([[q0031]]).
- **Parallel combination**: equal capacitors in parallel give $NC$, $ESR/N$, $ESL/N$.
- **Target impedance**: the supply network must stay below $Z_{target}=\Delta V/\Delta I$ across the band ([[q0501]]).
- **Distribution**: capacitors are needed near each pin group, not in one spot.
- **Anti-resonance**: parallel capacitors with different resonances produce an impedance peak between them.
:::

::: answer
"Because capacitors in parallel only add up like ideal capacitors at low frequency. A real capacitor has series inductance, so above its self-resonant frequency it's an inductor, and a bigger capacitor in a bigger package has a lower self-resonance, not a higher one. A single 100 microfarad part might stop being capacitive at a few hundred kilohertz, and above that its impedance climbs at 2 pi f times its ESL. At the frequencies where fast digital current lives, tens to hundreds of megahertz, it's basically a small inductor.

Using many smaller capacitors fixes that in two ways. Electrically, N capacitors in parallel divide the ESL and the mounting inductance by N, so ten 10 microfarad capacitors have a tenth of the inductance of one, with the same total capacitance. Physically, the current has to be supplied near every power pin, and one capacitor can only be near one place; a chip with dozens of supply pins needs charge close to each.

And using different values covers different bands: big ones for lower frequencies, small ones with the highest self-resonance for the top end. The catch is that mixing values makes anti-resonance peaks between them, so I check the combined impedance against a target and damp the peaks with ESR if needed."
:::

::: technical
**One cap vs $N$ in parallel.** For $N$ identical capacitors each with $(C,ESR,ESL_{tot})$ where $ESL_{tot}$ includes mounting inductance (vias, pads), ignoring mutual coupling:

$$C_{eq}=NC,\quad ESR_{eq}=\frac{ESR}{N},\quad L_{eq}=\frac{L_{tot}}{N}$$

The self-resonant frequency $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{eq}C_{eq}})$ is *unchanged* ($N$ cancels), but the impedance at resonance and above scales by $1/N$: above $f_0$, $|Z|\approx 2\pi f L_{tot}/N$.

**Example.** Compare one 100 µF capacitor (ESL 1.5 nH + 1 nH mounting = 2.5 nH, ESR 5 mΩ) with ten 10 µF capacitors (ESL 1 nH + 0.5 nH mounting = 1.5 nH each, ESR 4 mΩ), both 100 µF total:

| f | single 100 µF | ten 10 µF |
|---|---|---|
| 1 MHz | 15 mΩ | 0.8 mΩ |
| 10 MHz | 0.16 Ω | 9 mΩ |
| 50 MHz | 0.79 Ω | 47 mΩ |
| 100 MHz | 1.6 Ω | 94 mΩ |

Same total capacitance; 16× lower high-frequency impedance with ten smaller parts.

**Bands.** Different values cover different bands: $f_0$ of 100 nF is about 20 MHz, 10 nF about 70 MHz, 1 µF about 6 MHz (with ~0.5–0.7 nH). Mixing values extends the range where $|Z|<Z_{target}$.

**Geometry.** A chip with $M$ supply pins distributed over a package has current demand across the whole footprint. Plane spreading inductance (nH-scale over centimetres) means distant capacitors cannot supply local current at high frequency. Capacitors should be distributed by pin group.

**Anti-resonance.** Two capacitors with different resonances, $C_1$ with $L_1$ and $C_2$ with $L_2$ ($C_1 > C_2$), have an impedance peak between their self-resonances where the inductive $C_1$ resonates with the capacitive $C_2$. Peak height depends on the ESR and mounting inductances; damping (ESR, ferrite, polymer bulk) lowers it.

**Practical limits.** Cost, area, DC-bias derating, and assembly count; also, adding capacitors beyond a point only helps if the mounting inductance per capacitor is low.
:::

::: long
"The key point is that capacitors in parallel only behave like ideal capacitors at low frequency. Every real capacitor has series inductance, ESL, so its impedance falls to a minimum at its self-resonant frequency and then rises as it becomes an inductor. Bigger value in a bigger package means lower self-resonance, so a 100 microfarad part can go inductive at a few hundred kilohertz, and then at 100 megahertz it looks like a couple of nanohenries, over an ohm. A single 100 microfarad capacitor is the wrong tool for high-frequency current even though the capacitance is huge.

Using many smaller capacitors helps in a few ways. First, parallel inductances divide: N identical capacitors give you one N-th of the ESL and one N-th of the mounting inductance, so ten 10 microfarad capacitors have about a tenth of the high-frequency impedance of one 100 microfarad part at the same total capacitance. In my numbers, about 0.09 ohms versus 1.6 at 100 megahertz.

Second, geometry. The chip draws current at many pins spread across the package, and a capacitor can only be close to one spot. The connection from a distant capacitor adds nanohenries through plane spreading and vias, so I need capacitors near each group of pins.

Third, different values. A 10 microfarad covers up to a couple of megahertz, 100 nanofarad up to twenty, 10 nanofarad to seventy, so a mix extends the band where the impedance is below target.

The cost of mixing values is anti-resonance: between two capacitors' self-resonances there's an impedance peak, where one is inductive and the other capacitive. So I check the whole network against a target impedance, with simulation or measurement, and damp it with ESR or a polymer bulk capacitor if it peaks. And the answer isn't unlimited either, because of cost, area, and DC-bias derating."
:::

::: terms
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance of the capacitor, including leads/termination.
- **Mounting inductance**: inductance of pads, traces and vias connecting the capacitor.
- **SRF**: self-resonant frequency.
- **Anti-resonance (parallel resonance)**: impedance peak between the SRFs of two capacitors in parallel.
- **Target impedance**: $\Delta V/\Delta I$ the PDN must stay below.
- **PDN**: power distribution network, including regulator, planes, capacitors, package and die.
- **Plane spreading inductance**: inductance from current spreading in a plane between two connection points.
- **Parallel combination**: $N$ identical parts give $NC$, $ESR/N$, $ESL/N$.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "different values cover different frequencies". An exceptional one:

- Starts with $|Z|\approx 2\pi f\,ESL$ above SRF, so "one massive" has high-frequency impedance set by inductance, not capacitance.
- Shows parallel **divides** ESL and mounting inductance by $N$, with a numeric comparison (16× lower |Z| at 100 MHz).
- Adds the **geometry** argument (charge must be near each pin group; plane spreading inductance).
- Notes the cost of mixing (anti-resonance peaks) and how to handle it (ESR damping, simulation against $Z_{target}$).
- Mentions limits: area, cost, DC-bias, and diminishing returns once mounting inductance dominates.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Parallel capacitors just add."** Capacitance adds, but the ESL divides and resonances shift; the behaviour above SRF is what changes.
- **Believing "more capacitors always helps".** Unmanaged mixes of values can create anti-resonance peaks that make noise worse at one frequency.
- **Ignoring mounting inductance.** If each capacitor shares one via pair, the vias dominate and the parallel benefit disappears.
- **Thinking the SRF changes when you add identical parallel capacitors.** It doesn't: both $L$ and $C$ scale, so $f_0$ is the same, only the impedance scales down.
- **Using only 100 µF-class capacitors at the chip.** They are bulk parts, placed for low-frequency load steps.
:::

::: deep
## What "in parallel" really does

Treat one real capacitor as a series RLC branch with $C$, $ESR$ and $L$ (the sum of ESL and mounting). Its impedance is

$$Z(\omega)=ESR + j\omega L + \frac{1}{j\omega C}$$

Put $N$ identical branches in parallel and the combined impedance is just $Z/N$:

$$Z_N(\omega)=\frac{ESR}{N}+j\omega\frac{L}{N}+\frac{1}{j\omega\,NC}$$

So $N$ capacitors behave as one with $NC$, $ESR/N$ and $L/N$. The resonant frequency $1/(2\pi\sqrt{(L/N)(NC)})$ is unchanged, but everything scales down. This is the **same total capacitance** as one big capacitor $C_{big}=NC$, and the same low-frequency behaviour. What changes is *which* capacitor has the lower $L$: the one big capacitor typically has *more* ESL than a small one, because bigger values come in bigger packages with longer terminations and larger loops.

## Three reasons for many capacitors

### 1. Inductance divides

The only way to lower the high-frequency impedance of a decoupling network is to lower the effective inductance. Each additional identical capacitor with its own vias adds a parallel path. Eight 100 nF capacitors with 0.8 nH each (ESL plus mounting) give 0.1 nH, which has $|Z|=0.063\ \Omega$ at 100 MHz instead of 0.5 Ω. This is why FPGA and processor boards under the BGA look like a field of small capacitors.

The benefit holds only if each capacitor has its *own* connection. Sharing a single via pair among many capacitors leaves the via inductance as the bottleneck. And capacitors that sit close together have mutual inductance, which reduces the benefit slightly: parallel inductors with coupling $k$ have $L_{eq}\approx L(1+k)/N$.

### 2. Geometry: charge has to be near the pins

A chip draws current across its footprint. Plane spreading inductance and vias mean a capacitor 20 mm away can't supply a pin at high frequency ([[q0504]]). A 0402 capacitor beside each pin group puts charge where it's needed. A single large capacitor can only be near one of them, and the rest of the pins must reach it through the planes' spreading inductance.

### 3. Different values, different bands

Smaller capacitors have higher SRF and lower ESL, so they take over where larger ones go inductive:

| Value | Typical ESL | $f_0$ |
|---|---|---|
| 10 µF | ~1 nH | ~1.6 MHz |
| 1 µF | ~0.7 nH | ~6 MHz |
| 100 nF | ~0.5 nH | ~22 MHz |
| 10 nF | ~0.4 nH | ~80 MHz |

A network of several values has low impedance across a wider band than any one capacitor, which is why you see 10 µF, 1 µF, 100 nF and 10 nF near high-performance parts.

## The anti-resonance trap

When two capacitors of different value are in parallel, their impedances have opposite signs between their resonances: the larger one is inductive and the smaller one capacitive. Those two reactances resonate (parallel resonance) and the *combined* impedance has a peak. The peak height is limited by the ESR of the two branches and the mounting inductance.

Remedies for peaks:

- Choose capacitors with some ESR (or add a small series resistor).
- Use polymer or tantalum bulk for its ~10–50 mΩ ESR, which damps the lower peaks.
- Avoid gaps of more than about a decade between values, and use several of each mid value, so the peak is shallow.
- Include the planes: closely spaced power/ground planes provide distributed capacitance in the 100 MHz+ region.
- Verify with measurement or simulation against $Z_{target}$.

## Putting numbers on it

Take a processor core rail with 1.0 V ±5 % (50 mV) and a 5 A step: $Z_{target}=10$ mΩ, from DC up to where on-die and package capacitance take over (maybe 50–100 MHz). One 100 µF at 2.5 nH misses it above about 0.6 MHz ($2\pi f\times2.5\text{ nH}=10$ mΩ at 0.64 MHz). Ten of the 10 µF (0.15 nH combined) hold 10 mΩ out to only about 10.6 MHz, so more small capacitors and smaller mounting inductance are needed up to the die: reaching 10 mΩ at 100 MHz requires $L_{eq}\le 16$ pH, equal to tens of parallel capacitors with minimal vias, plus on-package and plane capacitance. This is why processor boards have dozens or hundreds of capacitors.

## Why not 1000 capacitors

- **Mounting inductance floor**: vias and plane spreading limit gains once $L_{eq}$ approaches the plane's own inductance.
- **Cost and area**: each part costs space, assembly and reliability exposure (MLCCs can crack and short).
- **DC-bias derating**: the effective capacitance may be half of nominal at operating voltage.
- **Diminishing returns**: above the frequency where on-die and package capacitance dominates, board capacitors are no longer the limiting element.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> ESL, ESR and mounting inductance values are typical estimates for common MLCC sizes; real values vary by vendor, series, footprint, and via geometry.
:::

::: applications
- **FPGA/processor core rails**: dozens of capacitors in several values under the BGA, sized from a target impedance.
- **DDR memory interfaces**: many small capacitors per power group around the device.
- **Switching-regulator output**: parallel ceramic capacitors lower ESR and ESL for fast load response.
- **Power amplifier supplies**: banks of capacitors to supply burst current.
- **Backplane or board entry**: bank of bulk capacitors for low-frequency load steps.
:::

::: assumptions
- Typical MLCC characteristics with ESL set largely by package and mounting rather than capacitance.
- Mutual inductance between adjacent capacitors is neglected or small.
- Each capacitor in a parallel bank has its own via pair; shared vias reduce the benefit.
- The chip's current demand is spread across many pins (large digital devices).
- For a small, low-current, slow-edge part, one 100 nF per pin is sufficient and extra capacitors add nothing.
- Another valid answer is in the time domain: many small capacitors supply charge near the pins, a large one supplies the slower demand ([[q0142]], [[q0143]]).
:::

::: followups
### If capacitors in parallel just add, why isn't one 100 µF capacitor equivalent to ten 10 µF capacitors?
At low frequency it is: same total capacitance. At high frequency the impedance is set by inductance, and ten parts in parallel have one tenth of the inductance of one comparable part, and smaller packages have lower ESL anyway. In my numbers, 94 mΩ vs 1.6 Ω at 100 MHz.

### Doesn't adding capacitors change the self-resonant frequency?
For identical capacitors, no: $L/N$ and $NC$ cancel so $f_0$ is the same, only $|Z|$ scales by $1/N$. Using *different* values moves the resonances apart and gives broader coverage but adds anti-resonance peaks.

### What causes the impedance peak when you mix values?
Between the two self-resonances the larger capacitor is inductive and the smaller is capacitive, and they resonate with each other in parallel. The peak is limited by the loop resistance (ESR plus trace). Higher ESR or a small series resistor flattens it.

### How do you decide how many capacitors a chip needs?
From the target impedance: $Z_{target}=\Delta V/\Delta I$. Then pick values and counts so that $|Z|$ stays below it up to the frequency where package and die capacitance take over, including mounting inductance. Vendors often give a recommended network, and a PDN simulation confirms it.

### Scenario: you replace ten 100 nF capacitors with one 1 µF. What changes?
Capacitance is the same, but now the 1 µF capacitor's single connection has all the mounting inductance (maybe 1.2 nH), whereas ten had about 0.1 nH combined. Above a few MHz the impedance is 10× higher, and only one pin group has a nearby source. The ripple at the pins will rise at tens of MHz, even if the DC and low-frequency behaviour look identical.
:::
