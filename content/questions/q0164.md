::: basics
### In plain words
Power that a converter takes in but does not deliver does not vanish. It turns into heat inside the transistors, the inductor, the copper traces (the board's wiring), and the controller. That heat raises the temperature of the parts, which hurts reliability, performance, and battery life, and forces you to add heatsinks or fans.

### Picture it
Rub your hands together hard. The friction wastes some effort and your hands warm up. The harder you rub, the hotter they get. A converter's losses are the friction. And just as your hands can only lose heat to the air so fast, a chip's temperature rises until enough heat escapes. If it cannot lose enough, it overheats.

### Words you need
- **Power loss**: input power minus output power, turned into heat.
- **Thermal resistance**: how many degrees a part warms per watt of heat, in °C/W.
- **Junction temperature**: the temperature of the silicon inside the package.
- **Thermal shutdown**: the chip turns itself off to avoid damage.
- **Efficiency**: see [[q0162]].

### The simplest example
A regulator loses 1.5 W, and its package has a thermal resistance of 40 °C/W. The temperature rise is $1.5 \times 40 = 60$ °C. In a 70 °C enclosure, the silicon reaches 130 °C, close to the 125 to 150 °C limit that many chips have. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds where the loss comes from in each component, and the other costs: shorter life, drifting performance, wasted battery, and extra cooling parts.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can follow the energy: conservation says lost electrical power does not vanish, it becomes heat in the converter, and you can then say precisely *why heat is a problem*: junction temperature limits, thermal shutdown and derating, shortened life, drifting parameters, wasted battery, and cost and size of cooling. Good answers include the thermal calculation $T_J=T_A+P\theta$ and a number.
:::

::: why
Power loss is the reason regulators get hot, enclosures need fans, batteries die early and parts fail in the field. Thermal margin is one of the first things a hardware reviewer asks about a power design. Being able to go from "10 W out at 85%" to "1.8 W of loss and a 60 °C temperature rise" is the daily work of power engineers, and it ties directly to the LDO versus buck decision ([[q0157]]) and the energy-conservation fundamentals in [[q0154]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Energy conservation**: input energy equals output energy plus losses; the losses become thermal energy ([[q0154]]).
- **Loss mechanisms**: $I^2R$, switching overlap, core and dielectric loss, quiescent current ([[q0162]]).
- **Thermal resistance**: $\theta=\Delta T/P$ in °C/W, in series from junction to case to board to ambient.
- **Junction temperature limit**: typically about 125–150 °C for silicon ICs, with parts-specific datasheet limits.
- **Thermal shutdown and derating**: protection that cuts output or reduces current when hot.
- **Reliability scaling**: failure rates increase roughly exponentially with temperature (Arrhenius).
:::

::: answer
"The lost power doesn't disappear, it becomes heat. Whatever the converter takes in and doesn't deliver is dissipated in the resistance of the FETs, inductor and traces, switching transitions, the core of the inductor, the controller's own bias current. All of that ends up as thermal energy in the components, flows into the PCB and package, and out to the air.

It's bad for several reasons. First, temperature: the junction temperature rises by the loss times the thermal resistance, and every chip has a limit, often 125 to 150 degrees. A 1.5 watt loss in a package with 40 degrees per watt is 60 degrees above ambient, so in a 70 degree enclosure you're at 130 and may hit thermal shutdown. Second, reliability: failure rates roughly double every ten degrees or so, and electrolytic capacitor life drops similarly. Third, performance drifts: FET resistance rises with temperature, so it gets worse as it heats up. Fourth, it's wasted energy, so battery runtime suffers. And fifth, dealing with the heat costs area, heatsinks, fans and money.

So I'd design to the loss: compute it from efficiency, check the junction temperature at the worst-case ambient, and keep margin."
:::

::: technical
**Energy flow.** $P_{in}=P_{out}+P_{loss}$. In steady state, $P_{loss}$ is converted entirely into heat inside the converter: resistive heating in conductors and switches ($I^2R_{DS(on)}$, inductor DCR), switching loss ($\approx\tfrac12V I(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}$, $C_{oss}$ and gate charge), magnetic core loss (hysteresis, eddy currents), capacitor ESR loss, diode drops, and controller bias current. A negligible fraction leaves as radiated EMI or acoustic noise.

**Heat removal.** Conduction through the package to the PCB copper, then spreading and convection/radiation to air:
$$T_J=T_A+P_{loss}\,\theta_{JA},\qquad \theta_{JA}=\theta_{JC}+\theta_{CA}\ (\text{series}).$$
Example: 1.5 W in a part with $\theta_{JA}=40$ °C/W at $T_A=70$ °C gives $T_J=70+60=130$ °C, which is above or near a typical 125 °C limit. The maximum allowed loss at that ambient is $(125-70)/40=1.4$ W.

**Why heat is bad.**
1. *Thermal limits.* Exceeding $T_{J(max)}$ risks damage; ICs have thermal shutdown (often with hysteresis) so the output cycles on/off.
2. *Derating.* Datasheets reduce allowed current with temperature.
3. *Reliability.* Arrhenius acceleration $AF=\exp\left[\frac{E_a}{k}\left(\frac1{T_1}-\frac1{T_2}\right)\right]$. With an assumed $E_a=0.7$ eV, raising 55 °C to 65 °C gives $AF\approx2.1$, and 85 °C to 125 °C about $9.8$.
4. *Parameter drift.* $R_{DS(on)}$ increases with temperature (a few tenths of a percent per °C), inductors lose saturation current, references drift, and leakage grows.
5. *Neighbours.* Heat raises the temperature of nearby components (capacitors, sensors, batteries).
6. *Energy and cost.* Battery run time falls; cooling hardware costs area and money.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The activation energy $E_a$ and the "doubling every 10 °C" rule of thumb depend on the failure mechanism and component technology. They are useful for intuition, but use manufacturer life models (e.g. for electrolytic capacitors) for real predictions.
:::

::: short
"Lost power becomes heat. The energy not delivered to the load is dissipated in FET and inductor resistance, switching transitions, magnetic cores and the controller's bias. That raises junction temperature by loss times thermal resistance, which risks thermal shutdown and shortens component life, shifts parameters like on-resistance, wastes battery energy, and forces larger heatsinks and more cost. So I compute the loss and check junction temperature at worst-case ambient."
:::

::: long
"The lost power doesn't vanish, it turns into heat, because of conservation of energy: what comes in either goes out to the load or stays in the converter as thermal energy. Where does it come from? Conduction loss, which is current squared times resistance in the FET channels, inductor winding, and copper traces; switching loss when voltage and current overlap in a transistor and from charging the output capacitance and gate; core loss in the inductor; ESR loss in capacitors; and the controller's own bias current.

That heat flows from the junction through the package into the PCB and then to air, like a series of resistors. The temperature rise is the loss times the thermal resistance. As a number: if I deliver 3 watts at 85 percent, I lose 0.53 watts; at 70 percent, 1.29 watts. With a 40 degree-per-watt package, that's 21 degrees versus 51 degrees above ambient. Put that in a 70 degree enclosure and one design is fine at 91 degrees, the other is at 121 and about out of margin.

Why is that bad? First, hard limits: silicon is usually rated to 125 or 150 degrees at the junction, and parts have thermal shutdown which causes the output to drop out or oscillate. Second, reliability: lifetime falls rapidly with temperature, roughly an exponential, which is often summarised as about a factor of two per ten degrees, and electrolytic capacitors in particular suffer. Third, parameters drift: FET on-resistance rises with temperature, so losses grow in a feedback loop, and references and sensors drift. Fourth, energy: it's wasted battery. And finally cost: heatsinks, copper area, fans, and airflow.

So in a design review I'd budget the loss for each regulator, compute junction temperature at worst-case ambient, and add margin."
:::

::: terms
- **Power loss**: $P_{in}-P_{out}$, dissipated as heat.
- **Junction temperature ($T_J$)**: temperature of the silicon die.
- **Thermal resistance ($\theta$)**: °C of rise per watt dissipated; $\theta_{JA}$ junction-to-ambient, $\theta_{JC}$ junction-to-case.
- **Thermal shutdown**: on-chip protection that turns the output off above a threshold.
- **Derating**: reducing allowed current or power as temperature rises.
- **Arrhenius model**: failure-rate dependence $\propto\exp(-E_a/kT)$.
- **Thermal runaway**: loss increases with temperature, which raises temperature further.
- **Heat spreading**: using copper area or planes to reduce thermal resistance.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it turns into heat, which is bad because it can overheat."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names the loss mechanisms (conduction, switching, core, ESR, quiescent) and says all become heat by conservation of energy.
- Computes the temperature rise: $\Delta T=P\theta$ with a real number and compares it with the datasheet limit at worst-case ambient.
- Lists several distinct consequences: shutdown/derating, reliability (Arrhenius), parameter drift/thermal runaway, wasted battery, cooling cost.
- Mentions the series thermal resistance chain ($\theta_{JC}$, board, $\theta_{CA}$) and what you can change (copper area, airflow, lower loss).
- Ties it back to the design decision (LDO vs buck, efficiency target).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying the lost power "is wasted" without saying where it goes.** It becomes heat inside the converter.
- **Using the efficiency number instead of the loss.** A 90% converter at 100 W loses 11 W, not "10%" of something small.
- **Ignoring ambient.** $T_J=T_A+P\theta$; a design with 30 °C of rise is fine at 25 °C ambient and fails at 85 °C.
- **Treating $\theta_{JA}$ as a constant.** It depends on PCB copper, layers, airflow and neighbours; datasheet values are for a specified test board.
- **Forgetting positive feedback.** Hot FETs have higher $R_{DS(on)}$, increasing loss; for BJTs and some LDOs the opposite can cause runaway.
- **Claiming "10 °C halves life" as a law.** It's a rule of thumb that depends on the mechanism and part.
:::

::: deep
## Energy goes somewhere

Conservation of energy makes this unavoidable. In steady state the converter's internal stored energy is constant, so the input power must equal the output power plus the power leaving by other routes. The only significant "other route" is heat, with tiny amounts radiated as EMI or sound.

Where it comes from, for a buck delivering 3 W (0.91 A at 3.3 V) from 12 V:

| Mechanism | How it is produced | Scales with |
|---|---|---|
| FET conduction | $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ | load current squared |
| Inductor DCR | $I^2\,\mathrm{DCR}$ | load current squared |
| Switching overlap | $\tfrac12V_{in}I(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}$ | $V_{in}$, $I$, $f_{sw}$ |
| Gate drive | $Q_gV_{drv}f_{sw}$ | $f_{sw}$ |
| Core loss | hysteresis/eddy in the inductor | ripple flux, $f_{sw}$ |
| Capacitor ESR | $I_{rms}^2\,\mathrm{ESR}$ | ripple current |
| Controller $I_q$ | $V_{in}I_q$ | fixed |
| (LDO) pass element | $(V_{in}-V_{out})I$ | headroom and load |

Each is a place where electrical energy is converted into random motion of atoms: heat.

## From watts to degrees

## Why heat is a problem: the full list

**1. Hard thermal limits.** Silicon devices have a maximum junction temperature, commonly 125 °C or 150 °C (check the datasheet). Above it you risk permanent damage and the device may violate its specification long before. Regulators usually include thermal shutdown (e.g. at about 150–170 °C, part-specific) with hysteresis; in practice the output switches off, cools, restarts, and chatters.

**2. Derating.** Datasheets give maximum current versus ambient or board temperature. If you do not meet it you cannot claim the rated current.

**3. Reliability.** Many wear-out and failure mechanisms are thermally activated. The Arrhenius acceleration factor between temperatures $T_1<T_2$ (in kelvin) for activation energy $E_a$ is
$$AF=\exp\left[\frac{E_a}{k}\left(\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac{1}{T_2}\right)\right],\quad k=8.617\times10^{-5}\ \text{eV/K}.$$
For an assumed $E_a=0.7$ eV: 55 °C to 65 °C gives $AF=2.1$; 55 °C to 85 °C gives 8.0; 85 °C to 125 °C gives 9.8. That is the origin of "a 10 °C rise roughly doubles the failure rate" near room-to-warm temperatures. Electrolytic capacitor life is commonly modelled with a similar factor of about 2 per 10 °C (the exact rule depends on the series; use the datasheet).

**4. Parameter drift and feedback.** MOSFET $R_{DS(on)}$ increases with temperature (the exact coefficient depends on the device; roughly 0.4–0.7% per °C is typical for silicon power MOSFETs, so a 100 °C rise can raise it by 40–70%). Hotter FET, more conduction loss, hotter FET: a positive loop that settles unless the thermal resistance is high. For devices with negative resistance coefficients (BJT $V_{BE}$ falling with temperature at fixed current) the loop can run away and destroy the device (thermal runaway), which is why paralleling BJTs needs emitter resistors.

**5. Collateral damage.** Heat conducts along copper to nearby parts: batteries age faster when warm, ADC references drift, oscillators shift frequency, and capacitors near an inductor run hotter.

**6. Energy and cost.** The 10% lost at 90% efficiency is battery energy: 3.7 Wh at 1 W load lasts 3.33 h at 90% but 2.96 h at 80%. Dealing with heat costs copper area, heatsinks, fans (which add noise, power and a failure point), thicker enclosures, or throttling that reduces performance.

## What you can do about it

| Lever | Effect |
|---|---|
| Raise efficiency (buck instead of LDO, synchronous rectification, lower $R_{DS(on)}$) | reduces $P_{loss}$ at the source |
| Reduce input-to-output headroom for linear stages | reduces $P_{loss}$ |
| More copper, thermal vias under exposed pads, planes | lowers $\theta_{CA}$ |
| Heatsink, airflow, thermal interface | lowers $\theta$ |
| Spread the loss across parts (multiphase, parallel) | lowers temperature of each |
| Derate current or frequency, throttle | lowers $P_{loss}$ at the worst case |
| Move the hot part away from sensitive ones | reduces collateral heating |

> After you say "it becomes heat", immediately give the one-line thermal calculation. "At 1.5 W and 40 °C/W that's a 60 °C rise, which at 70 °C ambient puts me at 130 °C. That's why I'd rather lower the loss or add copper." The calculation shows you apply the idea instead of reciting it.

::: applications
- **LDO sizing:** compute $P=(V_{in}-V_{out})I$ and check $T_J$ before choosing the package ([[q0157]]).
- **Buck converter layout:** thermal vias and large copper pours under the exposed pad of QFN power ICs.
- **Laptop and phone thermal management:** throttling and fan control based on temperature sensors, using the loss budget of each rail.
- **Battery packs and chargers:** thermal limits on charge current to avoid damage.
- **LED drivers:** efficiency determines LED junction temperature and thus lumen output and life.
- **Data-centre power:** every watt of conversion loss is also a watt of cooling load.
:::

::: assumptions
- Steady-state operation: heat generated equals heat removed. During short transients, thermal capacitance delays the temperature rise ($\tau=R_\theta C_\theta$).
- $\theta_{JA}$ values are board- and airflow-dependent and quoted for a standard test board; real designs differ.
- The 125–150 °C junction limits are typical for silicon ICs; wide-bandgap devices, automotive parts and special processes have different limits.
- The Arrhenius numbers use an assumed $E_a=0.7$ eV; real mechanisms vary from about 0.3 to 1.2 eV or more.
- "Lost power becomes heat" neglects the tiny radiated, acoustic and magnetic-field energy leaving the converter, and for LEDs or motors, the part of "loss" that becomes useful light or mechanical work depends on how you define the output.
:::

::: followups
### Your buck converter's IC is at 128 °C in the lab at 25 °C ambient with a 1.2 W loss. What now?
Compute $\theta_{JA}=(128-25)/1.2=86$ °C/W, which is high. First check the board: exposed pad soldered with thermal vias into a plane? Add copper area and vias to cut $\theta$. Then reduce the loss: lower $f_{sw}$ (less switching loss), pick a lower-DCR inductor, or lower $R_{DS(on)}$. Check ambient worst case: at 70 °C ambient it would be 173 °C, which fails.

### Why does an LDO get hotter than a buck delivering the same power?
The LDO dissipates $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$ in the pass device; a buck dissipates only its small conversion losses. For 12 V to 3.3 V at 1 A, that is 8.7 W against about 0.3 W. See [[q0157]].

### What is thermal runaway, and where can it happen in a power supply?
It is a positive feedback in which rising temperature increases dissipation and further raises temperature without bound. BJTs at constant base-emitter bias (negative $V_{BE}$ tempco), paralleled devices where one hogs current, and ceramic-capacitor failure modes can run away. MOSFETs generally share current well because $R_{DS(on)}$ increases with temperature. Thermal shutdown and current limiting are the protections.

### How does the heat move when a part has an exposed pad?
Most heat conducts from the die into the exposed pad (a low $\theta_{JC(bottom)}$), through solder into copper, down thermal vias into internal planes, and spreads laterally before leaving by convection and radiation from the board surface and the other side. Thermal vias (0.3 mm drill, filled or tented, in an array under the pad) greatly reduce $\theta_{CA}$.

### If lost power is only 1 W, why does the battery matter?
A 1 W loss is 1 W of battery power as well. On a device with a 3.7 Wh battery that powers a 2 W load, the loss is a third of the total input power, and cuts runtime by the same factor. Small losses add up over many converters, and in sleep states even µW-level losses (quiescent current) dominate ([[q0165]]).
:::
