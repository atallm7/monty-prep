::: basics
### In plain words
The critical nodes of a switching supply are the spots where the layout decides whether it works well. Each has its own risk. The input loop carries fast current pulses, so any inductance there causes voltage spikes. The switch node swings the full input voltage in nanoseconds, so it pushes noise into nearby copper. The feedback node is sensitive, so it picks up that noise. Ground carries both noisy and quiet currents.

### Picture it
Think of a house with a workshop and a nursery. The power saw and the hammer (input loop and switch node) are loud and sudden. The sleeping baby (the feedback pin) wakes at the smallest sound. The floor (ground) carries vibration from every room. So you put the workshop at one end, the nursery at the other, and keep the workshop's shaking out from under the crib.

### Words you need
- **High di/dt**: current that changes very fast, as in the input (hot) loop. Through inductance it makes spikes ([[q0548]]).
- **High dV/dt**: voltage that changes very fast, as at the switch node. Through stray capacitance it pushes current into neighbors ([[q0550]]).
- **Feedback pin**: where the chip compares a scaled copy of the output with an internal reference. It is high impedance: a tiny current makes a big voltage.
- **PGND and AGND**: the power ground (noisy) and the analog ground (quiet) ([[q0495]]).

### The simplest example
The switch node swings 12 V in 3 ns. Say just 0.1 pF of stray capacitance couples it to the feedback pin, which has 5 pF to ground. The two capacitances split the step like a divider: $12 \times 0.1/(0.1 + 5) \approx 0.235$ V. Against a reference near 0.8 V, that is a big error. So the feedback trace stays short and far from the switch node. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page sorts every node by its class, gives the equation for each, and notes where the rules conflict.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can look at a switcher schematic and sort each node into a class (high di/dt, high dV/dt, steady power, sensitive high-impedance) and say why that class dictates the layout. The interviewer wants named nodes (VIN and its capacitor loop, switch node, bootstrap, ground, feedback, compensation, output) and, for each, the physical reason: inductance times di/dt, capacitance times dV/dt, noise pickup on a high-impedance pin, or return-current sharing on a ground.
:::

::: why
Layout rules for switchers are not arbitrary: each one protects a specific node from a specific mechanism. If you know the mechanism, you can adapt to a new topology, package or datasheet without a recipe, and you can debug a board that misbehaves by asking which node was compromised. That is what turns "I followed the layout guide" into "I understand the layout".
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductance and di/dt**: $V = L\,di/dt$ makes fast current loops the source of overshoot and EMI. See [[q0548]].
- **Capacitance and dV/dt**: $i = C\,dv/dt$ makes fast voltage nodes the source of coupled noise. See [[q0550]].
- **High-impedance nodes** pick up noise: feedback, compensation. See [[q0552]].
- **Return currents and grounds**: where current flows decides where the ground bounces. See [[q0495]].
- **Buck operation**: [[q0174]], [[q0175]].
- **Ripple current**: the inductor's triangular current and the capacitors that smooth it. See [[q0186]].
:::

::: answer
"I sort the nodes by what they do electrically. The input hot loop, the input capacitor and the two switches, carries pulses of the full load current with nanosecond edges, so its inductance makes overshoot and EMI; that's the number one node to get right. The switch node is the one that swings the full input voltage in a few nanoseconds, so it radiates and couples through capacitance, which is why it should be small and short. The bootstrap capacitor between BOOT and the switch node powers the high-side drive and needs to be tight.

Ground matters because there are really two kinds of current in it: the noisy power return and the quiet reference. The feedback node is the opposite of the switch node: it's high impedance, tens of kilohms, so a few femtofarads of coupling from the switch node produces a visible error, and it should be short and far from the inductor. Compensation and enable pins are similar.

The output side, the inductor and output capacitors, carries smooth ripple current, so there it's about resistance and keeping the sense point honest. I remember it as: high di/dt, high dV/dt, quiet and sensitive, and steady current, and each gets its own rule."
:::

::: technical
**Node classes.**

| Node | Signal type | Mechanism | Layout consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| VIN and input hot loop ($C_{in}$, $Q_H$, $Q_L$) | Pulsed current, ns edges, amplitude $\approx I_{out}$ | $V = L\,di/dt$, radiation from loop area | Minimum loop area, $C_{in}$ at the pins, no vias |
| SW (switch node) | Full-swing voltage, ns edges ($12\ \text{V}/3\ \text{ns} = 4\times10^9$ V/s) | $i = C\,dv/dt$, ringing with parasitics | Short, small area, one layer, plane below, keep clear |
| BOOT / gate-drive loop | 1 A-scale pulses from gate charge ($Q_g/t$) | Loop inductance slows/rings the gate | Cap tight to BOOT and SW |
| PGND / return | Pulsed and ripple current | Shared impedance, ground bounce | Solid plane, single join to AGND |
| AGND / reference | Quiet | Corrupted by shared return | Separate from PGND, join at one point |
| FB | High-Z (tens of kΩ), small signal | Capacitive/magnetic pickup | Short, away from SW and $L$, own ground |
| COMP, SS, EN | High-Z or slow | Pickup, shifts loop | Close to pin, quiet side |
| Inductor and $V_{OUT}$ | Smooth triangle ($\Delta I \approx$ 1 A p-p for 12→3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz) | $I^2R$, stray magnetic field | Wide copper, keep field away from FB |
| Output sense point | DC | Drop between sense and load | Sense at the load end of $C_{out}$ or remote |

**Numbers.** For $V_{in} = 12$ V, $V_{out} = 3.3$ V, $I_{out} = 5$ A, $f_{sw}= 500$ kHz, $L = 4.7$ µH: $D=0.275$, $\Delta I_L = (V_{in}-V_{out})D/(Lf_{sw}) = 1.02$ A, input capacitor RMS current $= 2.2$ A. A 0.1 pF coupling from SW onto an FB node with 5 pF to ground gives a 0.24 V step on a 12 V edge ($12\times0.1/5.1$).

**Why classes matter.** They determine which rule beats which: for example, the SW trace wants to be wide (inductor current) but small (dV/dt); the compromise is "short and just wide enough".

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Coupling capacitance and parasitic values depend on geometry and stack-up; the 0.1 pF example is illustrative. Always check the specific datasheet's guidance.
:::

"I sort nodes by mechanism. The input hot loop (input cap and both switches) carries nanosecond pulses of the full load current, so its inductance causes overshoot and EMI. The switch node swings the full input voltage, so it couples through capacitance and radiates; keep it short and small. Ground is shared between noisy power return and quiet reference, so I separate them, and the output path is about resistance."
:::

::: long
"I classify nodes by what the current or voltage is doing.

The most important is the input hot loop: the input capacitor and the two switches. It carries pulses of roughly the full load current with edges of a few nanoseconds, so any inductance in it gives overshoot, V equals L di by dt, and the loop radiates. That's why the input capacitor sits right at the pins.

Next is the switch node. It swings the full input voltage, say 12 volts in 3 nanoseconds, so four billion volts per second. Any copper area on that node injects displacement current into neighbours, I equals C dV by dt, and radiates. So it's short and small, one layer, with a plane beneath it. The bootstrap capacitor between BOOT and the switch node powers the high-side gate drive, and its loop carries about an amp of gate charge, so it needs to be tight too.

The ground has two jobs. The power return carries those pulses; the analog reference must stay quiet. A solid plane carries the first, and I connect the analog ground at one point near the chip.

The feedback node is the opposite: high impedance, maybe 10 kilohms, with millivolt precision requirements. Just 0.1 picofarad from the switch node onto a few picofarads at the pin gives about a quarter of a volt per edge, so it's short, close to the pin, and far from the inductor and switch node. Compensation and soft-start are similar.

Finally the inductor and output capacitors carry smooth ripple current, about an amp peak to peak in a typical design, so it's resistance and heat there, plus choosing the sense point carefully."
:::

::: terms
- **Hot loop**: the loop of input capacitor, high-side and low-side switch; high di/dt.
- **Switch node (SW)**: the node joining the switches and the inductor; high dV/dt.
- **BOOT / bootstrap**: the capacitor from BOOT to SW that supplies the high-side gate drive.
- **PGND / AGND**: power and analog ground references.
- **FB (feedback)**: the divider tap that the error amplifier compares with the reference.
- **COMP**: the compensation node of the error amplifier.
- **Displacement current**: $i = C\,dv/dt$ through a parasitic capacitance.
- **Ground bounce**: voltage developed across ground impedance by return current.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** names "the switch node and the feedback" without a reason.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Classifies nodes by mechanism (high di/dt, high dV/dt, high-Z, steady) and gives the equation for each.
- Includes the hot loop, switch node, bootstrap/gate loop, ground, feedback, compensation and output sense point.
- Gives numbers: $4\times10^9$ V/s, about 1 A of ripple, 0.24 V coupled spike.
- Explains that rules can conflict (SW wide for current but small for noise) and how to compromise.
- Notes the single-point ground join and the sense-point choice.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating "the switch node" as the only noisy node.** The input hot loop is just as important and is a different mechanism.
- **Calling every high-current trace noisy.** The inductor and output paths carry smooth current; they need low resistance, not low inductance.
- **Calling the feedback pin "just a DC node".** It is high impedance and responds to fast coupling.
- **Joining PGND and AGND at many places** so switching current flows through the quiet reference.
- **Forgetting the bootstrap/gate loop,** which is a small but fast current loop.
- **Sensing the output at the inductor side of the capacitors,** which feeds ripple into the loop and mismeasures the load voltage.
:::

::: deep
## Why sort nodes at all

A buck has about ten nodes, and they differ by orders of magnitude in current, voltage slew, and impedance. Any single rule ("keep it short") is true everywhere and useful nowhere. The useful rule is: *identify the physical quantity that is changing fast on that node and the physical quantity it can disturb*.

## Input and the hot loop

During the on-time the high-side FET carries the inductor current from $C_{in}$; during the off-time $Q_L$ (or the diode) carries it. At each transition the full inductor current (about $I_{out}$) is commutated between two branches in nanoseconds. The capacitor $C_{in}$ must supply this current locally, since the source cannot respond that fast. The path is $C_{in}\to Q_H\to Q_L\to C_{in}$, and its inductance $L_{loop}$ gives overshoot $L_{loop}\,di/dt$ and rings with the FET's output capacitance at $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{loop}C_{oss}})$ (about 168 MHz for 3 nH and 300 pF). That frequency is where EMI emissions peak. Ripple current in $C_{in}$ is $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$ (2.2 A here), so it also heats the capacitor.

## The switch node

The SW node (and BOOT, which rides about 5 V above it) is the only node that sees the full input-to-ground swing at nanosecond edges: $12/3\text{ ns} = 4\times10^9$ V/s. Any capacitance $C$ from SW to another conductor sources $i = C\,dv/dt$: 1 pF gives 4 mA. In a layout, "capacitance" means copper area facing other copper (planes below, nearby traces) plus the inductor winding itself. SW is therefore short and compact, with a plane below to terminate field lines in a harmless place, and no sensitive trace within a few mm. The same node rings after each edge with the parasitic L and C of the hot loop, which is why a snubber or a gate resistor can be used to damp it (and why probing it needs a short ground spring, [[q0549]]).

## Bootstrap and gate-drive loop

The bootstrap capacitor (typically 100 nF) between BOOT and SW holds roughly 5 V and supplies the high-side gate drive. When $Q_H$ turns on the gate draws $Q_g$ in the switching time: 10 nC in 10 ns is a 1 A pulse. The loop BOOT → capacitor → SW → gate driver is small, but its inductance slows turn-on and rings the gate. Keep the capacitor right at the BOOT and SW pins.

## Grounds

There are two currents in ground. The power return is pulsed ($I_{out}$ amplitude with ns edges) and returns to $C_{in}$ and $C_{out}$. The signal reference is a quiet few mV. If both share a conductor with impedance $Z$, the pulsed current drops $Z\cdot i$ on the reference. Hence a solid plane for the power return, and a single connection from AGND to PGND near the IC's exposed pad, so the reference is taken from a point carrying almost no switching current ([[q0495]]).

## The feedback and compensation nodes

The FB node sees a Thevenin impedance of $R_1\parallel R_2$ (for example 31.6 kΩ ‖ 10 kΩ = 7.6 kΩ) in series with the error amplifier input. A coupling capacitor $C_c$ from SW to FB and pin capacitance $C_p$ form a divider; the voltage step on FB for a 12 V SW edge is $12\,C_c/(C_c+C_p)$:

| $C_c$ | Step on FB ($C_p$ = 5 pF) |
|---|---|
| 20 fF | 48 mV |
| 100 fF | 0.24 V |
| 500 fF | 1.09 V |

For a regulator with an 0.8 V reference, a 0.24 V glitch on every edge is huge (though it is brief, a few ns to tens of ns, and filtered by the amplifier's bandwidth, it can alias into jitter or modulator errors). Keep the line short and away from SW. See [[q0552]]. COMP, SS, EN and PG are similar but slower; keep them close to their pins.

## Inductor and output

The inductor carries a triangular current: here $\Delta I_L = 1.02$ A peak-to-peak on a 5 A DC. The loop with $C_{out}$ is large in current but smooth, so inductance is irrelevant and resistance and DCR heat matter. The inductor's stray magnetic field couples into nearby loops (especially FB) and the output capacitors' ESR sets the output ripple (1.02 A × 5 mΩ ≈ 5 mV). The sense point for FB should be at the load side of the output capacitors, so the regulator sees what the load sees and the inductor ripple doesn't enter the loop.

## A checklist by node

1. $C_{in}$ at VIN/PGND pins, tiny loop, no vias in the loop.
2. SW short and small, plane below, nothing near.
3. BOOT capacitor adjacent to BOOT and SW.
4. PGND plane; AGND tied once near the IC.
5. FB divider at the pin; trace short; away from SW/L; sense at $C_{out}$ or load.
6. COMP/SS/EN parts at their pins.
7. $L$ and $C_{out}$ in wide copper; shielded inductor if space is tight.

::: applications
- **Integrated buck regulators** where the datasheet's layout figure highlights VIN, SW and FB placement.
- **Controller plus FET designs** (gate-drive loop and bootstrap included in the critical set).
- **Automotive and industrial converters** passing EMC, where the hot loop and SW area are optimised first.
- **Low-noise supplies** for sensors and ADCs, where FB coupling and inductor field are managed carefully.
- **Multiphase regulators** where each phase has its own hot loop and SW node.
:::

::: assumptions
The classification is for a synchronous or non-synchronous buck with an integrated or external FET pair, switching hundreds of kHz to a few MHz. Boost converters have a different hot loop (output capacitor, switch, diode) and flybacks add a transformer with leakage-inductance spikes; the same node-class thinking applies. Edge speed matters: slower edges (a gate resistor) reduce di/dt and dV/dt effects at the cost of switching loss. Values like 3 nH, 300 pF, and 0.1 pF are typical orders of magnitude for illustration, and actual parts differ.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the feedback node more sensitive than the output node?
Impedance and signal size. The output node is low impedance (the output capacitors) and carries volts, so a small injected current barely moves it. The FB node is tens of kΩ and compared against a 0.6 to 1.2 V reference with a precision of a percent or so, so microamps or femtofarads of coupling show up as errors. High impedance means high sensitivity.

### Which node causes most of the conducted and radiated EMI in a buck?
Usually the hot loop (magnetic, from di/dt) dominates the high-frequency emissions, and the switch node (electric, from dV/dt) adds to it, especially through its capacitance to heatsinks or planes. The relative size depends on layout. That is why EMI fixes start with shrinking the hot loop and reducing the SW area, then edge-rate control or snubbers.

### Why is the bootstrap capacitor placed so close to the IC?
Because its loop carries the gate-charge pulses (about 1 A for 10 ns for a 10 nC gate), and any inductance slows and rings the high-side gate drive, increasing switching loss and noise. It is a small loop but fast, so it gets the same treatment as the hot loop at a smaller scale.

### How would you make the SW node quieter without changing the layout?
Slow the edges: a small resistor in series with the bootstrap capacitor or the gate (or a gate-drive strength setting on the IC) reduces dV/dt and ringing at the cost of switching loss. An RC snubber across the low-side FET damps the ring. Each of those trades efficiency for noise.

### What if your FB trace has to pass near the inductor?
I'd shield it: route it on an inner layer or with a ground plane between it and the inductor, keep it perpendicular to the inductor's winding axis, use a shielded inductor, and increase distance. If possible I'd also lower the divider impedance (smaller resistors) to reduce noise sensitivity, accepting a bit more quiescent current.
:::
