::: basics
### In plain words
An ideal diode conducts forward with zero voltage drop and never conducts backward. Compared with a real diode, the biggest change is efficiency, because a real diode wastes power equal to its forward drop times its current. The ideal version would also remove reverse-recovery ringing.

### Picture it
Picture a one-way turnstile. A real diode is a turnstile that is a bit stiff, so each person pays an effort to push through. An ideal diode is a turnstile with no friction at all. It still only turns one way, so it does not change the one-way rule, only the effort wasted.

### Words you need
- **Forward voltage drop**: the voltage across a diode while it conducts, about 0.5 V for a Schottky.
- **Conduction loss**: the power wasted as heat, $V_f \times I$.
- **Reverse recovery**: the brief backward current when a diode switches off.
- **Efficiency**: output power divided by input power.
- **Discontinuous mode**: the inductor current falls to zero for part of each cycle.

### The simplest example
A 12 V to 3.3 V buck delivers 2 A, which is 6.6 W. The diode conducts for 72.5 percent of each cycle, so its average current is $0.725 \times 2 = 1.45$ A. With a 0.5 V drop, it wastes $0.5 \times 1.45 = 0.725$ W, so efficiency is about $6.6/(6.6+0.725) = 90$ percent. An ideal diode removes that loss. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the knock-on effects, and what an ideal diode still cannot do at light load.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can separate what a diode's *non-idealities* cost you from what its *topology* limits. An ideal diode has zero forward drop, infinite reverse resistance, no stored charge and no capacitance. The question is a prompt to go through the consequences one by one (efficiency, duty cycle, ripple, switch-node waveform, EMI) and, crucially, to say what an ideal diode does **not** fix: it still cannot conduct in reverse, so the converter still enters DCM at light load, and it is still a passive device with no control over timing.
:::

::: why
This is the bridge between "why is there a diode" and "why use synchronous rectification". The loss in a real diode is $V_f\,(1-D)I_{out}$: at low output voltage it is the dominant loss. Quantifying what an ideal diode would buy, and what it would not, is how you justify low-side FETs, and also how you decide when a Schottky is good enough. It also tests whether you can calculate the knock-on effects (duty cycle and ripple shift) rather than only quoting efficiency.
:::

::: concepts
- **Diode loss** $P=V_f\,I_{avg}$, with $I_{avg}=(1-D)I_{out}$. See [[q0197]].
- **Volt-second balance with drops**: $D\approx\frac{V_{out}+V_f}{V_{in}+V_f}$. See [[q0191]].
- **Reverse recovery** and junction capacitance: charge that causes switching loss and ringing in real diodes.
- **DCM**: blocked reverse current gives discontinuous operation at light load. See [[q0185]].
- **Synchronous rectification**: a FET that approximates an ideal diode. See [[q0203]].
:::

::: answer
"An ideal diode has zero forward drop and no reverse recovery, so the biggest effect is efficiency. In my 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A example with a Schottky at 0.5 V, the diode averages 1.45 A for 0.725 W, about 11% of the 6.6 W output, so with a perfect diode I'd recover that: the diode-limited efficiency goes from about 90% to 100%. At a 1 V output it's much worse: the same diode would waste about 0.9 W per 2 W delivered, so the ideal diode is the difference between 69% and near 100%.

There are knock-on effects. The off-state voltage across the inductor is exactly $V_{out}$ rather than $V_{out}$ plus 0.5 V, so the duty cycle is exactly $V_{out}/V_{in}$, 0.275 instead of about 0.30, and the inductor ripple is a bit lower, 1.02 A instead of 1.10. The switch node sits at exactly 0 V in the off state, and there's no reverse recovery spike or capacitance, so less ringing and EMI.

But an ideal diode still doesn't conduct backwards, so at light load the inductor current goes to zero and the converter still enters discontinuous mode, the node still rings at $V_{out}$, and you can't get forced-CCM behaviour. The practical way to get close to an ideal diode is a MOSFET with a controller, which is synchronous rectification, with its own dead time and gate-drive cost."
:::

::: technical
**Real diode baseline** (Schottky, $V_f=0.5$ V): $P_D=V_f(1-D)I_{out}=0.5\times0.725\times2=0.725$ W; $P_{out}=6.6$ W; loss-only efficiency $\eta=P_{out}/(P_{out}+P_D)=90.1\%$.

**Ideal diode:** $P_D=0$.

**Duty cycle.** Volt-second balance with drops: $(V_{in}-V_{out})D=(V_{out}+V_f)(1-D)\Rightarrow D=\frac{V_{out}+V_f}{V_{in}+V_f}$ (ignoring the high-side drop). Real: $\frac{3.3+0.5}{12.5}=0.304$; with $V_f=0.4$: 0.298. Ideal: $0.275$.

**Inductor ripple.** $\Delta I=\frac{(V_{out}+V_f)(1-D)}{Lf}$: $V_f=0.4$ V: $\frac{3.7\times0.702}{2.35}=1.10$ A; ideal $1.018$ A (8% lower).

**Low $V_{out}$ penalty.** At $V_{out}=1$ V, 2 A, 12 V in: $D\approx0.083$, $P_D=0.5\times0.917\times2=0.92$ W vs $P_{out}=2$ W; $\eta_{diode}=69\%$. Ideal: loss gone.

**What stays:** (1) blocking of reverse current: $i_L\ge0$, so DCM appears when $I_{out}<\Delta I/2$ (0.51 A here, for the ideal diode as well); (2) the diode remains passive: its turn-on is dictated by the circuit, not timing; (3) other losses: high-side conduction ($I_{rms}^2R_{ds}D$), switching loss in $Q_1$, inductor DCR/core, gate drive, etc. (4) a truly ideal diode would also remove $Q_{rr}$, $C_j$: the high-side turn-on loss from diode reverse recovery goes away.
:::

::: short
"An ideal diode removes the forward-drop loss: for 12 V to 3.3 V at 2 A with a 0.5 V Schottky that's 0.73 W of 6.6 W, so efficiency goes from about 90% toward 100%, and the gain is bigger at lower output voltages. It also makes the off-state switch node exactly 0 V, the duty cycle exactly $V_{out}/V_{in}$, and removes reverse-recovery spikes. What it doesn't change is that it can't conduct backwards, so you still get DCM at light load."
:::

::: long
"An ideal diode means zero forward voltage, zero reverse current, no stored charge and no capacitance. What does that buy?

The first, biggest thing is efficiency. A real Schottky at 0.5 volts carrying the inductor current for 72.5 percent of the cycle, average 1.45 amps in my 12 volt to 3.3 volt, 2 amp example, dissipates 0.73 watts, about 11 percent of the 6.6 watts delivered. With an ideal diode that loss is gone. It's worse at low output voltage: at 1 volt out, the same diode would dissipate nearly 0.9 watts for 2 watts delivered, so the ideal diode matters a lot more for low-voltage rails.

The second is behavioural. The inductor voltage in the off state becomes exactly minus $V_{out}$, so the duty cycle is exactly the voltage ratio, 0.275 rather than about 0.30, and the ripple current is about 8 percent lower. The switch node is exactly zero in the off state, with no reverse-recovery current spike when the high-side FET turns on, no junction capacitance to ring with, so less EMI and less turn-on loss in the high-side FET.

But some limits remain. An ideal diode still blocks reverse current, so at light load, when the average current drops below half the ripple, the inductor current stops at zero and the converter goes discontinuous: the switch node rings at $V_{out}$, and the loop sees a different plant. That's the thing a synchronous FET can change, by letting current go negative. Also the other losses stay: high-side FET conduction and switching, inductor loss, gate drive. And in practice the nearest thing to an ideal diode is a MOSFET plus a controller, which brings its own dead-time and shoot-through problems."
:::

::: terms
- **Ideal diode**: zero $V_f$, zero reverse current, no reverse recovery, no capacitance, instant switching.
- **Forward drop $V_f$**: voltage across a conducting diode.
- **Conduction loss**: $V_f\times I_{avg}$.
- **Reverse recovery ($Q_{rr}$)**: charge removed when a diode turns off, causing a current spike.
- **Junction capacitance $C_j$**: capacitance of the diode, resonates with loop inductance.
- **DCM**: discontinuous conduction mode at light load.
- **Synchronous rectifier**: FET used as a controlled, near-ideal diode.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "No voltage drop, so more efficient."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies the loss recovered ($V_f(1-D)I_{out}$, 0.73 W, 11%) and notes it grows as $V_{out}$ falls.
- Lists the secondary effects: exact duty cycle, lower ripple, cleaner switch node, no reverse recovery or $C_j$ effects.
- Says what an ideal diode *doesn't* fix: DCM at light load (reverse blocking), other losses, and passivity.
- Connects to synchronous rectification as the practical approximation and its costs (dead time, gate drive, shoot-through).
- Mentions that an "ideal diode" controller (FET plus comparator) is exactly such a synchronous element.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"An ideal diode makes the converter 100% efficient."** Only the diode loss goes; high-side conduction/switching, inductor, gate and quiescent losses stay.
- **Ignoring the dependence on $V_{out}$.** The diode loss is $V_f(1-D)I_{out}$; relative to output power it is $\frac{V_f}{V_{out}}(1-D)$, so it matters more at low $V_{out}$.
- **Believing the converter would no longer go DCM.** The reverse-blocking property of the ideal diode is still there.
- **Forgetting the effect on duty cycle and ripple.** The ideal diode changes $D$ slightly, and the ripple.
- **Equating synchronous rectification with an ideal diode.** A synchronous FET conducts in reverse as well, which an ideal diode never does (unless the controller blocks it).
:::

::: deep
## What an ideal diode is

| Property | Real Schottky | Ideal |
|---|---|---|
| Forward drop | 0.3 to 0.5 V at amps | 0 |
| Reverse current | µA to mA (rises with T) | 0 |
| Reverse recovery charge | small (Schottky) to large (PN) | 0 |
| Junction capacitance | tens to hundreds of pF | 0 |
| Conducts in reverse? | no | no |
| Turn-on/off timing | set by circuit | instant, set by circuit |

The last two lines are the important ones for the "what does not change" part.

## 1. Efficiency

The diode conducts $i_L$ for $(1-D)T$. Average current $(1-D)I_{out}=1.45$ A.

## 2. Duty cycle and ripple shift

With a real diode the inductor sees $V_{out}+V_f$ in the off state. Volt-second balance:

$$(V_{in}-V_{out})D=(V_{out}+V_f)(1-D)\ \Rightarrow\ D=\frac{V_{out}+V_f}{V_{in}+V_f}.$$

With $V_f=0.4$ V: $D=3.7/12.4=0.298$. Ideal: $0.275$. The converter's controller adjusts automatically, so you only notice it if you compare measured to calculated duty cycle.

The ripple changes too: $\Delta I=\frac{(V_{out}+V_f)(1-D)}{Lf}=\frac{3.7\times0.702}{2.35}=1.10$ A (real) vs 1.02 A (ideal).

Figure: With the real diode the off-state level is $-V_f$ (about −0.4 V). With an ideal diode the node would sit at exactly 0 V and have no ringing contribution from the diode's reverse recovery or capacitance.

Real diode non-idealities that disappear:
- **Reverse recovery.** When $Q_1$ turns on, the diode must be turned off: stored charge $Q_{rr}$ is swept out as a current spike through $Q_1$. For $Q_{rr}=20$ nC at 12 V and 500 kHz, the loss is about $Q_{rr}V_{in}f=0.12$ W, and the spike rings with the loop inductance, causing EMI. (Schottky diodes have almost no $Q_{rr}$ but do have capacitive charge $Q_C=C_jV$ with similar effects at higher voltage.)
- **Junction capacitance** $C_j$ adds to the switch-node capacitance, increasing the ringing energy and the turn-on loss.
- **Leakage current** in hot Schottkys wastes power in the off time.

These are the "secondary" benefits; for a Schottky they are smaller than the forward-drop loss, but they dominate EMI behaviour in fast converters.

## 4. What an ideal diode cannot do

### Reverse conduction

A diode, ideal or not, blocks current from cathode to anode. The inductor current cannot go negative. At light load ($I_{out}<\Delta I_L/2=0.51$ A here) the current reaches zero before the end of the off time and the node floats:

Figure: DCM operation of an asynchronous buck: after the inductor current hits zero, the ideal diode turns off and the node rings at $V_{out}$ (damped by the real loop resistance in practice). The same would occur with an ideal diode.

DCM is not necessarily bad (it improves light-load efficiency by lowering switching frequency in PFM or skipping), but it changes the control dynamics and the output ripple. A synchronous FET that conducts in reverse yields forced-CCM instead.

### Other losses

High-side conduction and switching, inductor DCR/core loss, gate-drive power and controller quiescent current are all unchanged.

### Timing control

A diode switches whenever the circuit says so. A synchronous FET needs a gate signal, dead time and anti-shoot-through control. "An ideal diode in practice" means a MOSFET with a comparator across it (an ideal-diode controller), which senses the polarity of the drain-source voltage and turns the FET on only when current would flow forward. That device is exactly a synchronous rectifier with *diode-emulation* behaviour: it blocks reverse current like a diode, with a tiny $R_{ds(on)}I$ drop instead of $V_f$.

> [!TIP]
> If asked "how would you build an ideal diode?", answer: "MOSFET plus a comparator that turns it on when the drain-source voltage shows forward conduction and off when current tries to reverse." That is also the diode-emulation mode of many synchronous buck controllers.

## Putting numbers on the improvement

For the 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A example, with a 0.5 V Schottky versus a synchronous FET (10 mΩ) as an approximation:

| | Schottky | Sync FET (10 mΩ, ideal timing) |
|---|---|---|
| Rectifier loss | 0.725 W | $I_{rms,LS}^2R_{ds}=1.72^2\times0.01=0.030$ W |
| Diode-limited efficiency | 90.1% | 99.5% |

The remaining loss comes from dead-time body-diode conduction ($2\times30\ \text{ns}\times0.7\ \text{V}\times2\ \text{A}\times500\ \text{kHz}=42$ mW), gate charge and other terms.
:::

::: applications
- **Low-voltage, high-current rails** (1.0 to 1.8 V cores) where diode loss dominates: synchronous (diode-emulating) rectification is mandatory.
- **ORing and reverse-battery protection** with ideal-diode controllers and MOSFETs.
- **Light-load efficiency modes** (diode-emulation/DCM or PFM) in synchronous bucks.
- **EMI-sensitive designs**: eliminating reverse recovery by using a FET instead of a PN diode.
- **Simulation**: replacing the diode by an ideal switch/diode element to estimate the best-case efficiency and spot other losses.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM example** at 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, 500 kHz, 4.7 µH.
- **Schottky $V_f$ of 0.4 to 0.5 V** at 1.5 to 2.5 A; the real value depends on part and temperature.
- **"Ideal" means electrically ideal** including no reverse recovery and no capacitance, but *still blocking reverse current*.
- **Alternative reading**: if "ideal diode" means a controlled switch that can conduct either way, then you have a synchronous rectifier and the DCM limitation disappears.
- **Other losses unchanged**; the efficiency numbers refer only to the diode's share.
:::

::: followups
### How much efficiency does an ideal diode give at 12 V to 5 V, 2 A?
$D=0.417$, diode average current $1.17$ A, loss $0.5\times1.17=0.58$ W against 10 W out, so about 5.5% points. The gain is smaller at higher $V_{out}$ because the fixed $V_f$ is a smaller fraction.

### Does the ideal diode change the output voltage?
No: the controller adjusts the duty cycle to hold $V_{out}$. The duty cycle shifts from about 0.30 to 0.275.

### Does an ideal diode eliminate the need for dead time?
There is no dead time in an asynchronous buck anyway. The dead time exists only when a controlled FET replaces the diode. An ideal diode would remove the *body-diode conduction* issue because there would be no low-side FET.

### Would the converter still enter DCM?
Yes. An ideal diode blocks reverse current, so below $I_{out}=\Delta I_L/2$ (0.51 A in the example) the inductor current is clamped at zero. Only a device that conducts in reverse (a synchronous FET in forced-CCM) avoids it.

### Harder: why might a *real* synchronous FET perform worse than an ideal diode at very light load?
Gate charge and dead-time losses are fixed per cycle, independent of the load, and in forced-CCM the circulating ripple current causes $I^2R$ and core losses even at zero load. An ideal diode has no such losses, and in DCM the switching frequency drops. This is why synchronous controllers offer diode-emulation or PFM modes at light load.
:::

::: interpretations
"Replaced with an ideal diode" is read as keeping the circuit asynchronous: only the diode's imperfections (forward drop, reverse recovery, capacitance, leakage) are removed. If the interviewer means an ideal *switch* (synchronous rectifier), the DCM behaviour changes as noted. Related pages: [[q0197]] (why the diode), [[q0203]] (synchronous rectification pros/cons) and [[q0202]].
