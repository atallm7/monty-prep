::: testing
Whether you can build the CMOS power budget from first principles and know which knobs move it. The interviewer is checking for:

1. The three components: **dynamic** ($\alpha C V_{DD}^2 f$), **short-circuit** (both FETs briefly on during slow edges) and **static/leakage**.
2. The **why** behind $CV^2$: charging a capacitor from a fixed supply always burns $\tfrac12CV^2$ in the resistance, independent of the resistance value.
3. The dependencies the question names: power is linear in frequency, quadratic in supply and swing, and rise/fall time only affects the short-circuit term (and, indirectly, the peak current and EMI).
:::

::: why
Battery life, thermal limits, supply sizing and decoupling all start from this budget. It's why chips run at lower and lower $V_{DD}$, why clock gating is everywhere, why I/O power can rival core power, and why "just run it faster" has a thermal price. Once you can estimate the power of a bus of 32 pins at 100 MHz in your head, you can size a regulator or judge whether a part needs a heatsink.
:::

::: concepts
- **Energy in a capacitor**: $E=\tfrac12CV^2$; charging from a source of fixed voltage takes $QV=CV^2$. See [[q0022]].
- **CMOS inverter operation**: PMOS charges the load, NMOS discharges it, almost no static path. See [[q0105]].
- **Shoot-through (short-circuit) current**: both devices conduct during the input transition. See [[q0109]].
- **Activity factor ($\alpha$)**: the fraction of cycles in which the node makes a 0 to 1 transition.
- **Leakage**: subthreshold and gate leakage that flows with no switching. See [[q0099]].
- **Slew rate**: edge speed, set by drive current over load capacitance. See [[q0111]].
:::

::: answer
"CMOS is efficient because in steady state there's no DC path from supply to ground: one transistor is always off. So power is only drawn when it switches. The dominant term is dynamic power: each time the output goes low to high, the supply delivers $C V_{DD}^2$ of energy, half of it is stored on the load capacitor and half is burned in the PMOS. On the way back down the stored half is burned in the NMOS. So per full cycle you lose $C V_{DD}^2$ whatever the transistor resistance, and average power is $\alpha C V_{DD}^2 f$.

So frequency matters linearly, and supply voltage and logic swing matter quadratically, which is why lowering $V_{DD}$ is so powerful. If I halve the voltage I cut dynamic power by four. Rise and fall time don't change that dynamic term. What they change is the short-circuit current: with slow input edges both the NMOS and PMOS conduct for a while and you get a through-current. That's typically a few percent of the dynamic power if edges are fast and can reach 20% or more for sluggish edges. Then there's leakage, which doesn't depend on frequency but grows with temperature and low thresholds.

For example, a 10 pF load at 3.3 V toggling at 10 MHz burns about 1.1 mW, and at 1.8 V about 0.32 mW."
:::

::: technical
**Power components**
$$P=\underbrace{\alpha\,C_L V_{DD}V_{sw}\,f}_{\text{dynamic}}+\underbrace{V_{DD}I_{sc}}_{\text{short-circuit}}+\underbrace{V_{DD}I_{leak}}_{\text{static}}$$
with full-swing $V_{sw}=V_{DD}$ giving $\alpha C_LV_{DD}^2f$. Here $\alpha$ is the number of $0\to1$ transitions per clock cycle ($\alpha=1$ for a node that charges and discharges once per cycle, e.g. a clock net; 0.1-0.25 typical for data) and $f$ the clock frequency. If instead $f$ is defined as the toggle (charge/discharge pair) rate, set $\alpha=1$.

**Why $CV^2$.** Charging $C$ through any resistance $R$ (including the PMOS $R_{on}$) from supply $V$: the supply delivers $Q=CV$ at voltage $V$, so $E_{supply}=CV^2$. The capacitor stores $\tfrac12CV^2$, so $\tfrac12CV^2$ is lost in $R$. Discharging dissipates the stored $\tfrac12CV^2$ in the NMOS. The loss is independent of $R$, so a stronger driver does not make the edge cheaper, only faster. Efficiency of charging from a fixed supply is 50%.

**Effect of swing.** If the output swings only $V_{sw}<V_{DD}$ (a reduced-swing driver) charge still comes from $V_{DD}$: $P=C V_{DD}V_{sw}f$. A 1.8 V swing from a 3.3 V supply: $10\text{ pF}\times3.3\times1.8\times10\text{ MHz}=0.594$ mW vs 1.089 mW full swing. Powering the driver from the lower rail does better still.

**Short-circuit power** (Veendrick-style estimate for a symmetric inverter, input ramp time $\tau$ with the output transition fast relative to input):
$$P_{sc}\approx\frac{\beta}{12}(V_{DD}-2V_t)^3\,\tau\,f$$
with $\beta=\mu C_{ox}W/L$ of each device. For $\beta=2$ mA/V², $V_{DD}=3.3$ V, $V_t=0.7$ V, $f=10$ MHz: $\tau=1$ ns gives 11 µW; 5 ns gives 57 µW; 20 ns gives 229 µW (21% of the 1.09 mW dynamic term). If $V_{DD}<2V_t$ there is no short-circuit path at all. It grows linearly with $\tau$.

**Numbers** ($C_L=10$ pF, $f=10$ MHz, full swing): 1.2 V: 0.144 mW; 1.8 V: 0.324 mW; 3.3 V: 1.089 mW; 5 V: 2.5 mW. Energy per cycle: 14, 32, 109 and 250 pJ.

**Adiabatic limit.** If the supply ramps over time $T\gg RC$ instead of stepping, the loss falls to about $\dfrac{2RC}{T}CV^2$. With $R=1$ kΩ, $C=10$ pF, $T=1$ µs it is 2% of $CV^2$. This is how resonant/adiabatic logic and clock drivers save energy, at the cost of complexity.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The short-circuit formula is a first-order result for long-channel devices and a small output load. Short-channel devices, velocity saturation and large loads change the coefficient; use a circuit simulator for real numbers. Leakage varies by orders of magnitude with process, threshold and temperature.
:::

::: short
"CMOS has no DC path in steady state, so power is mostly dynamic: every low-to-high transition draws $CV_{DD}^2$ from the supply, half stored on the load and half burned in the PMOS, and the discharge burns the stored half in the NMOS. Average power is $\alpha CV_{DD}^2f$: linear in frequency, quadratic in supply and swing, independent of transistor resistance. Rise and fall time only matter through short-circuit current, which grows with slow edges, plus there's leakage that doesn't depend on frequency. Halving the supply cuts dynamic power by four."
:::

::: long
"Start with why CMOS is efficient at all: in steady state either the NMOS or the PMOS is off, so there's no DC current from supply to ground. Power is drawn only when the output changes, in three pieces.

First and biggest: dynamic power. When the output rises, the supply delivers charge $Q=CV_{DD}$ at $V_{DD}$, so it provides $CV_{DD}^2$. The load capacitor ends up storing half of that, and the other half is dissipated in the PMOS channel. On the falling edge, the stored half is dissipated in the NMOS. So each full cycle costs $CV_{DD}^2$ no matter how big or small the transistor resistance is. Multiply by the activity factor and frequency and you get $\alpha CV_{DD}^2f$. So it's linear in frequency, and quadratic in supply voltage and in swing. If the swing is reduced but the driver still draws from $V_{DD}$, it's $CV_{DD}V_{sw}f$. For a 10 pF load at 3.3 V and 10 MHz that's about 1.1 mW; at 1.8 V about 0.32 mW.

Second: short-circuit power. During an input transition both transistors conduct for a moment, giving a current pulse from supply to ground. It's proportional to the input edge time and roughly $(V_{DD}-2V_t)^3$, so slow edges make it worse and it vanishes when $V_{DD}<2V_t$. Typically it's a few percent of the dynamic power with fast edges, over 20% with sluggish ones.

Third: leakage, which flows all the time and rises with temperature and with lower thresholds.

So efficiency levers: lower the supply, lower the capacitance, lower the activity with clock gating, lower the swing, keep edges crisp but not so fast that you cause EMI and ground bounce, and use high-$V_t$ devices where speed isn't needed."
:::

::: terms
- **Dynamic power**: $\alpha CV_{DD}^2f$, from charging and discharging capacitances.
- **Activity factor ($\alpha$)**: probability of a $0\to1$ transition per clock cycle.
- **Short-circuit (crowbar) power**: power from the through-current when both FETs conduct during an edge.
- **Leakage / static power**: $V_{DD}I_{leak}$, flowing without switching.
- **Swing ($V_{sw}$)**: the voltage difference between logic levels.
- **Energy per transition**: $\tfrac12CV^2$ lost per edge, $CV^2$ per cycle.
- **Clock gating**: stopping the clock to idle blocks to set $\alpha\approx0$.
- **Adiabatic charging**: ramping the supply slowly so less energy is lost in resistance.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer states $P=CV^2f$. An exceptional answer:

- Derives the $\tfrac12CV^2$ per edge loss and shows it is **independent of resistance**, so a stronger driver is faster not cheaper.
- Separates dynamic, short-circuit and leakage power and gives numbers for each.
- States each dependence the question names: **frequency** (linear), **swing/logic level** (quadratic, or $V_{DD}V_{sw}$), and **rise/fall time** (only short-circuit; also peak current/EMI).
- Mentions the practical knobs: voltage scaling, clock gating, lowering $C$, reduced-swing signalling, multi-$V_t$.
- Notes the efficiency of the **supply** too: powering a 1.0 V core from 3.3 V through an LDO wastes 70%.
- Notes the validity limits of the short-circuit formula.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Faster edges burn more power."** The switching energy $CV^2$ is independent of edge rate. Faster edges *reduce* short-circuit loss but increase peak current, EMI and ground bounce.
- **Forgetting that half the energy is burned on charge, not discharge only.** Both transitions dissipate, in different transistors.
- **Using $CV^2f$ without $\alpha$.** Not every node toggles every cycle (and a clock toggles twice per cycle).
- **Ignoring leakage** in "idle" estimates; in a modern process it can dominate when the clock stops.
- **Using the wrong swing.** The energy drawn from the supply is $CV_{DD}V_{sw}$, not $CV_{sw}^2$.
- **Treating $R_{on}$ as a loss knob.** A smaller $R_{on}$ gives a faster edge, not a lower $CV^2$ loss.
:::

::: deep
## The energy bookkeeping

Take an inverter driving a load capacitance $C$ (wire, next gate inputs, pin) with a step in the input. The output is pulled up through the PMOS (resistance $R_p$) or down through the NMOS.

**Rising output.** The capacitor voltage rises as $v(t)=V(1-e^{-t/R_pC})$ and the current is $i=\dfrac{V}{R_p}e^{-t/R_pC}$. Energy from the supply:
$$E_{supply}=\int_0^\infty V\,i\,dt=V\cdot CV=CV^2$$
Energy stored on the capacitor: $\tfrac12CV^2$. Dissipated in the PMOS:
$$E_{R}=\int_0^\infty i^2R_p\,dt=\tfrac12CV^2$$
The $R_p$ cancels. A bigger or smaller transistor changes only the time constant.

**Falling output.** The capacitor's stored energy $\tfrac12CV^2$ is dissipated in the NMOS. No energy is drawn from the supply (apart from short-circuit current). Per cycle: $CV^2$ drawn, all of it ultimately converted to heat.

## Frequency and activity

Average power is linear in $f$. For a bus of $N$ pins each driving 10 pF at 3.3 V toggling at an average activity of 0.5 at 100 MHz:
$$P=N\cdot0.5\cdot10\text{ pF}\cdot3.3^2\cdot100\text{ MHz}=N\times5.4\text{ mW}$$
so a 32-bit bus burns 174 mW. Compare 8 pins at 1.8 V, 100 MHz, 5 pF: $8\times0.5\times5\text{ pF}\times3.24\times10^8=6.5$ mW. This is the argument for low-voltage, low-capacitance, narrower or serialized interfaces.

Frequency also interacts with $V_{DD}$: the maximum frequency scales roughly with $V_{DD}-V_t$ over $V_{DD}$-ish, so dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS) reduces $V$ along with $f$ and gets a nearly cubic power reduction.

The practical rule: keep edges to a fraction of the period, and keep the output edge no slower than the input edge (a weak driver on a heavily loaded node makes the *next* gate's input slow).

In the formula the output transition must be fast relative to the input; when the output lags (large load), the PMOS and NMOS never both see a large $V_{DS}$, and the short-circuit current is much smaller. The worst case for the *next* stage is a slow edge from a heavily loaded node.

## Logic levels and swing

Lower $V_{DD}$ reduces dynamic power quadratically, but delay increases as the overdrive $V_{DD}-V_t$ shrinks. Below $2V_t$ the short-circuit path disappears but the circuit slows down sharply and noise margin shrinks. Subthreshold-supply logic gets enormous energy savings at kilohertz-to-megahertz speeds. In the other direction, 5 V logic costs $(5/3.3)^2=2.3\times$ the power of 3.3 V logic at the same $C$ and $f$, which is one reason older 5 V families gave way to 3.3 V, 1.8 V and below. Comparing families: see [[q0108]].

**Reduced-swing signalling** (e.g. 0.4-1.0 V swings on long on-chip or off-chip lines) reduces $P=CV_{DD}V_{sw}f$ when the driver is supplied from a matching low rail; terminated differential schemes trade a static current for much faster, quieter edges.

## Leakage

When the clock stops, $P_{leak}=V_{DD}I_{leak}$. For $10^6$ gates leaking 1 nA each at 3.3 V: 3.3 mW. Subthreshold leakage rises about a decade per 80-100 mV reduction of $V_t$ and doubles roughly every 10-20 °C ([[q0099]]), so power gating, high-$V_t$ cells on non-critical paths, and body biasing are standard fixes.

## Efficiency including the supply

"CMOS efficiency" can also mean energy delivered per useful operation, including the power supply. A 1.0 V core fed from 3.3 V by an LDO wastes $1-1/3.3=70\%$ before the first gate toggles; a buck converter at 90% efficiency wastes 10%. A system view therefore includes the regulator type, decoupling and I/O voltage.

## Real hardware

- **Power measurement**: put a small series resistor or use a current probe on the supply; clocks and burst activity make averages misleading, so measure with realistic traffic.
- **Decoupling**: dynamic current arrives in sharp pulses of height $\sim CV/t_r$; size bypass capacitors for the transient, not the average.
- **I/O dominated power**: pad capacitances (5-15 pF) switching at the pin voltage often exceed core power in small devices.
- **Thermal**: dynamic power is dissipated in the driver FETs and the load's resistance; with $\theta_{JA}$ this sets temperature ([[q0111]] treats edge rate trade-offs).

## Worked example: a block-level power budget

Put numbers on a small digital block so the pieces of the formula $P=\alpha C V^2f+P_{sc}+P_{leak}$ can be compared. All values are illustrative round numbers, chosen to show proportions rather than describe a real chip.

- 2 million gates, each presenting an average of 3 fF of switched capacitance (gate input plus its share of wiring and drain junctions).
- Average activity $\alpha=0.1$ (10 % of nodes toggle per cycle, a typical order of magnitude for random logic).
- $V_{DD}=1.0$ V, $f=500$ MHz.
- Clock network total capacitance 0.3 nF (flip-flop clock pins plus buffer tree and wiring), with activity 1 because the clock charges and discharges every cycle.
- Leakage of 1 nA per gate at 25 °C.

**Logic dynamic power.** $P=\alpha NCV^2f=0.1\times2\times10^6\times3\times10^{-15}\times1^2\times5\times10^8=0.30$ W.

**Clock power.** $P_{clk}=C_{clk}V^2f=0.3\times10^{-9}\times1\times5\times10^8=0.15$ W. Even though the clock net is a tiny fraction of the circuit, activity 1 versus 0.1 makes it half as large as the entire logic; in real designs the clock network is commonly a large fraction of total dynamic power, which is why clock gating (disabling the clock to idle blocks) is the first power-saving technique applied.

**Leakage.** $I_{leak}=2\times10^6\times1\ \text{nA}=2$ mA, so $P_{leak}=2$ mW at 25 °C: negligible next to 450 mW. Subthreshold leakage grows roughly twofold per 10 to 20 K; taking a doubling every 15 K, at 100 °C the factor is $2^{75/15}=32$, giving 64 mW, now about 14 % of dynamic power and rising. This is why the same chip is "dynamic-power-limited" when cool and "leakage-aware" at high temperature, and why burn-in and worst-case thermal tests are done hot.

**Energy per operation.** One gate switching at 1 V costs $CV^2=3$ fJ from the supply (half stored, then dissipated, as derived above). A 1 pF wire, by comparison, costs 1 pJ per transition at 1 V, which is why moving data (long wires, off-chip pins) costs far more energy than computing on it.

## Worked example: dynamic voltage and frequency scaling

Now lower the supply from 1.0 V to 0.8 V while the threshold is 0.35 V. The maximum clock frequency does not scale with voltage squared as power does. A widely used model for short-channel gates (the alpha-power law) takes the delay as proportional to $V/(V-V_t)^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha\approx1.3$, so $f_{max}\propto(V-V_t)^{1.3}/V$:
$$\frac{f(0.8)}{f(1.0)}=\frac{(0.45)^{1.3}/0.8}{(0.65)^{1.3}/1.0}=\frac{0.442}{0.571}=0.775$$
Running at 78 % of the frequency, the dynamic power is
$$\frac{P(0.8)}{P(1.0)}=\left(\frac{0.8}{1.0}\right)^2\times0.775=0.64\times0.775=0.50$$
So a 22 % loss in speed halves the power, and the energy per cycle ($CV^2$) falls 36 %. That is the reason for DVFS: when the workload is light, lower the voltage and clock together and the quadratic voltage dependence delivers energy savings far larger than the loss of speed. The limits are the threshold (as $V\to V_t$ the delay rises steeply and noise margin shrinks), leakage (which doesn't fall with frequency, so running slowly for a long time eventually costs more leakage energy than it saves, a "race to idle" effect) and the regulator's transition time.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The alpha-power exponent and threshold are process dependent and the model is approximate at low voltage. In a real design the voltage/frequency pairs come from silicon characterization, not from a formula.

## Short-circuit and slew: a number to remember

The short-circuit term, $P_{sc}\approx(\beta/12)(V_{DD}-2V_t)^3\tau f$, has a useful consequence for the supply choice: it vanishes when $V_{DD}\le2V_t$. At $V_{DD}=1.0$ V and $V_t=0.35$ V, $V_{DD}-2V_t=0.3$ V and the cubic factor is 0.027 V³, nearly 250 times smaller than at 3.3 V and $V_t=0.7$ V (6.9 V³), so in modern low-voltage logic the short-circuit term is a few percent of the total and the dynamic term dominates. On a long unbuffered wire or a heavily loaded net with a slow edge, however, the term can be significant for the next gate, which is the case the formula warns about.

## Where the power goes after the chip: the supply chain

Following the earlier supply-efficiency argument, put the 450 mW block behind a converter. From a 3.3 V rail through an LDO to 1.0 V, the input power is $0.45\ \text{A}\times3.3\ \text{V}=1.49$ W, so 1.04 W is dissipated in the regulator: 70 % lost, and a 20 °C/W package with that dissipation heats by 21 °C. Through a buck converter at 88 % efficiency, the input power is 0.51 W. That 1 W difference is more than the block itself uses. The practical lesson from the chip designer's side is that every milliwatt saved in the core is multiplied by the inverse of the regulator efficiency, and that battery life depends on the sum of both.

## Verifying power on the bench

- **Measure the supply current properly.** Insert a small sense resistor in the core supply (for 450 mA, a 0.1 Ω resistor drops 45 mV and wastes 20 mW) and read its voltage with an oscilloscope or differential probe. A DMM will show only the average and may mislead; the oscilloscope will show the current pulses at the clock rate.
- **Separate dynamic and leakage power.** Run the block at several clock frequencies (say 100, 200, 300 and 400 MHz at fixed voltage) and fit $I=I_0+bf$. The slope is dynamic, the intercept is leakage plus any static current. Check that the intercept is small, and then repeat with the part warmed by a heat gun to see the leakage rise.
- **Check the $V^2$ law.** Hold the frequency fixed and lower the supply in steps; the current should fall roughly linearly with $V$ (power quadratically). A significant departure means short-circuit current or a leakage component with a different dependence.
- **Measure the energy per operation.** Divide the average power by the operations per second (or the dynamic power by $\alpha f$) for different data patterns; random data should cost more than constant data, which is a direct test of the activity factor.

- **Battery-powered devices**: voltage scaling, clock gating and sleep modes to extend life.
- **Microcontroller power estimates**: $\mu$A/MHz specifications are essentially $\alpha CV^2$.
- **Bus and I/O design**: choosing 1.8 V I/O and lower pin capacitance to cut interface power.
- **Processor DVFS**: lowering both voltage and frequency for cubic power savings.
- **Regulator sizing**: dynamic current requirement and supply decoupling for FPGA or SoC rails.
- **Thermal design**: converting a switching-power budget into a junction temperature.

::: assumptions
- **Full-swing CMOS** with an ideal step unless stated; reduced-swing drivers use $CV_{DD}V_{sw}f$.
- **Capacitive load dominates**; resistive terminations or pull-ups add static power.
- **Short-circuit formula** is a first-order, long-channel estimate with a fast output edge.
- **Activity factor** is workload-dependent; $\alpha=1$ means a charge/discharge pair per cycle (clock nets toggle both edges within one period).
- **Leakage figures** are illustrative; real values depend on process, $V_t$ flavour and temperature.
- Power loss in the **driver's output stage and pad ring** is included in "$C$" only if its capacitance is lumped.

::: followups
### What is the power of a 5 V CMOS output driving 20 pF at 1 MHz?
$P=CV^2f=20\text{ pF}\times25\times10^6=0.5$ mW (with $\alpha=1$). Cutting to 3.3 V gives 0.22 mW. Check by energy: $CV^2=500$ pJ per cycle.

### Why doesn't a larger transistor make the switching energy smaller?
Because the energy dissipated in charging through resistance $R$ is $\tfrac12CV^2$, independent of $R$. A bigger transistor reduces $R$ and thus the edge time but also adds its own capacitance (drain junction, gate for the previous stage), so total energy rises.

### How does slow rise time hurt power?
It increases the time both FETs conduct, so short-circuit power grows linearly with $\tau$ (11 µW at 1 ns to 229 µW at 20 ns in the example). The $CV^2$ term is unchanged. In practice slow edges also move the next stage's switching threshold into noise sensitivity.

### What's the benefit of halving $V_{DD}$ and $f$ together?
Dynamic power scales as $V^2f$, so halving both gives $1/8$. Computation per second halves, so energy per operation falls by $1/4$. This is DVFS. The limit is the minimum voltage for correct timing.

### Harder: a clock net toggles twice per cycle. How do you account for it in $\alpha CV^2f$?
Per cycle there is one charge and one discharge, so energy per cycle is $CV^2$, and with $f$ the clock frequency the activity factor is 1 (not 2) if you use the formula with $\alpha$ = $0\to1$ transitions per cycle. The error to avoid is counting both edges and doubling the result.

### Scenario: your MCU's measured current is twice the datasheet's µA/MHz figure when many GPIOs toggle. What do you check?
The pin loads. Datasheet numbers assume little I/O activity. Each pin drives board capacitance plus input pin capacitance (say 15 pF) at 3.3 V; at 10 MHz that is 1.6 mW per pin. Also check for contention or pull-up current and slow edge rates that raise short-circuit current.
:::
