::: testing
Whether you know reflow as a *thermal process*, not just "the oven." The interviewer wants the four zones (preheat, soak, reflow, cooling), why each exists (flux activation, avoiding thermal shock, wetting, grain structure), the key numbers (liquidus, peak temperature, time above liquidus, ramp rates), and the defects that follow from a bad profile (tombstoning, voids, cold joints, solder balls, component damage). Strong candidates mention thermocouple profiling and the part temperature ratings.
:::

::: why
Reflow is the step where a placed board becomes an electrically and mechanically sound product. The process window is narrow: lead-free solder melts around 217 °C while many components are rated to about 260 °C, so there is perhaps 30-40 °C of room. Hardware engineers need to know it because layout (thermal mass, symmetry), part selection (moisture sensitivity, temperature ratings) and defects like tombstoning ([[q0615]]) are all reflow topics.
:::

::: concepts
- **Solder paste**: solder powder suspended in flux; printed before placement ([[q0597]]).
- **Liquidus / solidus**: temperatures above which the alloy is fully molten / below which it is fully solid. SAC305 melts at roughly 217-220 °C; eutectic Sn63Pb37 at 183 °C.
- **Reflow profile**: temperature versus time seen by the board; measured with thermocouples.
- **Time above liquidus (TAL)**: how long the joint is molten; enough to wet, not so long that intermetallics grow thick.
- **Flux activation**: flux removes oxides at roughly 150-200 °C.
- **Wetting and surface tension**: molten solder pulls parts into alignment (self-alignment).
- **Moisture sensitivity level (MSL)**: bake requirements for parts that can crack ("popcorning") in reflow.
:::

::: answer
"Reflow is how surface-mount parts get soldered. Solder paste, which is tiny solder balls in flux, is printed on the pads and parts are placed on it. The whole board then rides a conveyor through an oven that follows a temperature profile, which melts the paste so the solder wets the pads and leads, and then cools it so the joints freeze.

The profile has four phases. Preheat ramps the board gently, around one to three degrees per second, so nothing cracks from thermal shock. The soak holds it around 150 to 180 °C for a minute or so to let temperature even out across the board and activate the flux, which strips oxide. Then the reflow zone ramps above the solder's melting point, about 217 °C for common lead-free alloys, to a peak of roughly 240 to 250 °C, and holds above melting for something like 45 to 90 seconds so the solder wets and flows. Finally controlled cooling freezes the joints into a fine grain structure.

The tricky bit is the window: the solder needs to be hot enough to wet but the parts have a maximum temperature, so you profile real boards with thermocouples and adjust the oven zones. A bad profile gives cold joints, voids, solder balls or tombstoned parts."
:::

::: technical
**Oven.** A multi-zone conveyor oven (typically 7-12 heated zones) with convection heating, a nitrogen option, and a cooling section. Conveyor speed and zone setpoints define the profile; typical belt speeds are of order 0.5-1.2 m/min. A 2.4 m heated length at 0.8 m/min means 3 min inside the heated zones (computed).

**Typical lead-free (SAC305) profile (paste datasheet governs).**

| Phase | Typical target |
|---|---|
| Preheat ramp | up to about 1-3 °C/s from ambient to about 150 °C |
| Soak | about 150-180 °C for 60-120 s |
| Ramp to peak | about 1-3 °C/s |
| Peak | about 235-250 °C (must stay below the lowest component rating) |
| Time above liquidus (217 °C) | about 45-90 s |
| Cooling | about 2-4 °C/s down (too fast risks thermal stress; too slow coarsens grains) |

**Process window.** With a 245 °C peak against 217 °C liquidus the margin is 28 °C. Large parts (BGAs, big connectors, ground planes) lag small ones by many degrees (the "delta T" across the board), so the cold spot must reach above liquidus long enough while the hot spot stays below component limits. Reducing delta T by longer soak, better convection and thermally balanced layout widens the window.

**Defect mechanisms.**
- **Tombstoning**: one end of a chip part wets first, and the surface-tension moment lifts the other end; caused by unbalanced pad thermal mass or paste ([[q0615]]).
- **Solder balls and beading**: paste slumps or oxidises, or heats too fast so flux splatters.
- **Voids**: trapped flux gas, worse under QFN thermal pads and BGAs; mitigated by profile and paste choice, and vacuum reflow.
- **Cold or dull joints**: insufficient peak or TAL.
- **Head-in-pillow**: BGA ball and paste fail to merge because of warpage or oxidation.
- **Component damage**: exceeding MSL or peak ratings; popcorning from absorbed moisture.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Profile numbers are typical industry ranges. The solder paste datasheet and each component's rating (often 260 °C peak per the J-STD-020 classification) set the real limits; always follow the paste vendor's recommended profile.
:::

::: long
"Reflow soldering is how nearly all surface-mount boards get their joints. The sequence is: print solder paste on the pads, place the components, and send the board through a reflow oven on a conveyor. Paste is small solder spheres in flux, tacky enough to hold parts until it melts.

The oven is a series of heated zones, and the temperature the board sees over time is called the reflow profile. It has four stages. Preheat brings the board up at a gentle one to three degrees a second so the parts and laminate don't suffer thermal shock. Next is the soak, around 150 to 180 °C for a minute or two, which lets big and small parts come to the same temperature and lets the flux activate to clean oxide off the metal. Then the reflow stage: temperature goes above the alloy's melting point, about 217 °C for the common lead-free SAC alloys, up to a peak of roughly 240 to 250 °C, and stays above melting for about 45 to 90 seconds. That's where the solder melts, wets the pads and leads, and surface tension even pulls small parts into alignment. Finally the board cools at a controlled rate, which freezes fine-grained joints.

The art is the process window. There's only about 30 degrees between the solder's melting point and the peak, and parts have maximum temperatures, while big BGAs and ground planes heat slower than little resistors. So you profile with thermocouples on the real board, then tune zone temperatures and belt speed. Bad profiles show up as tombstoned parts, solder balls, voids and cold joints, and I'd design with that in mind: symmetric pads, balanced copper and parts with the right moisture rating."
:::

::: terms
- **Reflow profile**: temperature versus time experienced by the board.
- **Liquidus**: temperature above which solder is fully liquid.
- **TAL (time above liquidus)**: seconds the joint stays molten.
- **Soak (thermal equalisation)**: plateau where the board temperature evens out.
- **Peak temperature**: highest temperature on the board during reflow.
- **Ramp rate**: heating or cooling rate, °C/s.
- **Tombstoning**: chip part standing on one end after reflow.
- **Voiding**: gas pockets in a solder joint.
- **MSL**: moisture sensitivity level of a package.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "reflow melts the solder paste in an oven."

**An exceptional answer**:

- Explains the narrow window between solder melting and component ratings, and delta T across the board.
- Names defects and mechanisms (tombstoning from imbalance, voids, head-in-pillow, popcorning).
- Mentions thermocouple profiling and links layout choices (thermal balance, symmetric pads) to reflow.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking the oven just "bakes" at 250 °C.** The time-temperature profile matters; sitting at peak too long damages parts and grows intermetallics.
- **Quoting one universal profile.** Each paste and board has its own; leaded and lead-free differ by about 35 °C.
- **Ignoring delta T.** A BGA in the middle of a big board can lag the edge parts by many degrees.
- **Forgetting moisture.** Parts that sat open can crack in reflow; MSL handling and baking exist for this reason.
- **Confusing reflow with wave.** Reflow melts pre-printed paste; wave passes the board over molten solder ([[q0603]]).
- **Cooling too fast or too slow.** Fast cooling stresses parts; slow cooling coarsens the grain and weakens the joint.
:::

::: deep
## What has to happen to a joint

Each solder joint goes through a sequence: (1) flux in the paste dries off solvent and activates, removing oxides from the pad, lead and solder powder; (2) the powder particles melt (above liquidus) and coalesce; (3) the molten solder wets the clean metal surfaces, spreading and forming a fillet driven by surface energy; (4) a thin intermetallic compound layer (Cu₆Sn₅ on copper) forms at the interface, which is what makes the bond; (5) the joint cools and solidifies. If the metal is not hot long enough, steps 2-4 are incomplete (cold joint); if it is too hot or too long, the intermetallic grows thick and brittle and parts get damaged.

## Why each zone exists

**Preheat.** Heats the board gently. A fast ramp gives thermal shock, cracked ceramic capacitors, and rapid solvent boil-off that spits solder balls off the pads. A slow ramp wastes time and lets paste slump.

**Soak.** Heavy parts and large copper areas lag small parts by tens of degrees; the soak lets everything approach one temperature before the final spike, reducing delta T and with it tombstoning and uneven wetting. Flux is activated here and consumed progressively; if soak is too long, flux is exhausted before the solder melts and oxide wins.

**Reflow (spike).** Takes every joint above liquidus. Wetting needs a bit of time above liquidus, commonly tens of seconds. Surface tension of the molten solder aligns small parts onto pads.

**Cooling.** The cooling rate sets the microstructure; moderate, controlled cooling gives a fine, strong grain. Exiting too hot also stresses parts.

## Profiling

Thermocouples are attached to the coldest and hottest locations (the biggest BGA, a heavy connector, a small passive, a bare edge) using high-temperature solder or tape, and the instrumented board rides through the oven with a data logger. Software extracts ramp rate, soak time, peak and TAL for each location and compares them with the paste datasheet and component ratings. Oven zone temperatures and belt speed are adjusted until every location is inside the window. The more thermal mass difference, the narrower the window becomes.

## Atmosphere

Air reflow works for most boards. **Nitrogen** reduces oxidation, improving wetting on fine pitch and OSP finishes, at a cost. **Vacuum reflow** (for example power modules) lowers void rates dramatically. **Vapor-phase** reflow heats by condensing a fluid at a fixed boiling point, giving a perfectly bounded peak temperature.

## Design for reflow

| Concern | Layout response |
|---|---|
| Tombstoning ([[q0615]]) | Symmetric pads, equal copper connected to each pad, thermal reliefs on one-sided connections |
| Delta T | Spread heavy parts, avoid big copper islands, keep large BGAs from edge-to-edge shadowing |
| Voids | Segmented thermal-pad paste apertures, via-in-pad filled or plugged |
| Moisture | Respect MSL; bake parts if floor life is exceeded |
| Double-sided | Heavy parts on the last side ([[q0599]]) |
| Warpage | Symmetric stack-up, rails and supports ([[q0594]]) |

::: applications
- **All SMT assembly lines**: 7-12 zone convection ovens.
- **Prototype boards**: small benchtop ovens, toaster-oven conversions with controllers, or hot-plate reflow.
- **Power electronics**: vacuum or vapour-phase reflow to reduce voiding under power packages.
- **BGA rework**: localised hot-air reflow with a profile.
- **Pin-in-paste through-hole**: reflowed with SMT ([[q0599]]).
:::

::: followups
### Why is there a soak zone?
To equalise temperature across parts of different thermal mass before the final ramp, and to activate the flux so oxides are removed before the solder melts. It reduces delta T, which reduces tombstoning and cold joints.

### What happens if the peak temperature is too low? Too high?
Too low: solder doesn't fully melt or wet, producing cold or dull joints and poor wetting, especially on large parts. Too high or too long: parts exceed ratings, flux burns off, intermetallics grow brittle, laminate can delaminate and discolour.

### How do you measure the profile?
Attach thermocouples to representative locations (large BGA, small passive, board edge) and run the instrumented board through the oven with a data logger. Compare ramp, soak, peak and TAL with paste and component specs, then tune.

### Why do some boards need nitrogen?
It reduces oxidation during the hot phase, improving wetting on difficult finishes or fine-pitch parts and reducing solder balling. It costs money, so it is used when defects justify it.

### A BGA shows head-in-pillow defects. What do you investigate?
Warpage during reflow lifting the ball from the paste, oxidised balls or paste, too little paste volume, a profile that lets flux exhaust before reflow, or too-fast ramp. Fixes include a flatter board support, a lower-oxidation soak, nitrogen, a larger paste aperture or a profile change.
:::
