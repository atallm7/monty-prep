::: basics
### In plain words
Etching removes material from the wafer in a chosen pattern. After lithography (printing with light) leaves a stencil of resist, etching eats away the thin layer (film) in the openings, so the pattern becomes part of the chip itself. Wet etching uses a liquid chemical. Dry etching uses a plasma, which is an electrically charged gas. Modern chips need dry etching because it cuts straight down.

### Picture it
Picture sandblasting through a stencil. The sand flies straight down, so the cut matches the stencil's openings with straight walls. That is dry etching. Now picture dipping the same stencil in a bath of acid. The liquid also creeps sideways under the stencil's edges. That is wet etching. That creep is harmless for big shapes but ruins tiny lines.

### Words you need
- **Mask**: the protective layer, such as resist, that covers areas you want to keep.
- **Isotropic**: etching equally in all directions, so it eats sideways as fast as down.
- **Anisotropic**: etching mainly in one direction, usually straight down.
- **Undercut**: sideways etching beneath the edge of the mask.
- **Selectivity**: how many times faster the etch removes the target film than the mask or layers beneath.
- **Plasma**: a gas energized so it contains charged particles and reactive fragments.

### The simplest example
Say you etch a 100 nm film isotropically under a mask line 1 µm (1000 nm) wide. The undercut is about 100 nm on each side, so the line shrinks to 1000 − 200 = 800 nm. A 50 nm line would vanish. A directional plasma etch keeps the width near its drawn size.

Selectivity sets how thick the mask must be. To etch 500 nm of oxide (glass) with a 5:1 selectivity to the resist, plus 20% extra etch time to clear leftovers, you need at least 1.2 × 500 ÷ 5 = 120 nm of resist.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers how plasma etching gets vertical walls, deep etches such as the Bosch process, and problems with very narrow, deep holes.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand etching as the pattern-transfer step that follows lithography, and can compare wet and dry etching using the right vocabulary: **isotropic vs anisotropic**, **selectivity**, etch rate, undercut and endpoint. The interviewer wants a clear picture of plasma (reactive-ion) etching, why modern chips need vertical walls, and a few real chemistries. Hidden sub-skills: reasoning about masks (resist thickness vs selectivity), recognising why undercut kills fine features, and linking etching to TSVs, FinFETs and 3D NAND.
:::

::: why
Every patterned layer in a chip passes through an etch, and the quality of the etch (profile, uniformity, damage, selectivity) directly sets transistor dimensions, interconnect shape and yield. Etch limits explain design rules, why some structures (deep trenches, high-aspect-ratio holes) are hard, and where variation comes from. It connects lithography ([[q0661]]) to the finished structure and to deposition ([[q0663]]), and shows up in TSV, DRAM and 3D NAND discussions ([[q0653]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Pattern transfer**: lithography makes a resist pattern; etching removes the unprotected film so the pattern becomes permanent. See [[q0661]].
- **Wet vs dry etching**: liquid chemistry vs plasma (gas-phase) chemistry.
- **Isotropic vs anisotropic**: etching equally in all directions vs mainly vertically.
- **Selectivity**: ratio of etch rate of the target film to that of the mask or underlying layer.
- **Reactive-ion etching (RIE)**: plasma etching where ion bombardment and chemical reactions work together.
- **Endpoint and overetch**: detecting when the film has cleared and etching a little longer to remove residue.
:::

::: answer
"Etching is the process of selectively removing material from the wafer. After lithography has put a patterned layer of photoresist on top, etching removes the film in the openings and leaves the rest protected, so the pattern is transferred into the actual device layer.

There are two families. Wet etching uses liquid chemicals, like hydrofluoric acid to etch silicon dioxide. It's cheap and very selective, but usually isotropic, meaning it etches sideways as fast as down, so it undercuts the mask. That's fine for large features but not for nanometre ones. Dry etching uses a plasma: a gas like a fluorine or chlorine compound is ionized in an RF field, and reactive radicals chemically attack the film while ions accelerated by an electric field bombard the surface. That bombardment mostly goes straight down, so the etch is anisotropic, giving vertical sidewalls. That's reactive-ion etching, and it's what's used for modern transistors and interconnect.

The key metrics are etch rate, anisotropy, and selectivity: how fast it removes the target film compared to the mask and the layer underneath. If selectivity is low you burn through the resist or punch through the layer below. Fab engineers tune the gas chemistry, pressure and RF power to balance profile, selectivity and damage."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** Etching removes material selectively through openings in a mask (photoresist or a hard mask such as SiO₂ or SiN) to transfer a pattern into a film or the substrate.

**Wet etching.** Chemical dissolution in a liquid. Examples: buffered HF for SiO₂; hot phosphoric acid (~160 °C) for Si₃N₄; KOH or TMAH for silicon, which etches anisotropically along crystal planes (on (100) silicon KOH leaves {111} sidewalls at $\arctan\sqrt2 = 54.74^\circ$). Mostly isotropic for amorphous films, with undercut about equal to the etch depth.

**Dry etching.** Plasma etching in a low-pressure RF chamber (13.56 MHz is a common frequency). Ions are accelerated across the plasma sheath into the wafer (energies from tens to hundreds of eV). Chemistries: fluorocarbons (CF₄, CHF₃, C₄F₈) for SiO₂ and SiN; SF₆ for silicon; Cl₂ or HBr for silicon and poly; various halogens for metals. Mechanism: chemical etching plus ion-assisted damage and sidewall passivation (a polymer film that protects sidewalls), giving vertical profiles.

**Metrics.**
- Etch rate $R = \Delta h/\Delta t$.
- Selectivity $S = R_{film}/R_{mask}$. To etch a film of thickness $t$ with 20% overetch needs mask thickness $t_{mask}\ge 1.2\,t/S$. Example: 500 nm SiO₂ with resist selectivity 5:1 needs ≥120 nm of resist.
- Anisotropy $A = 1 - R_{lateral}/R_{vertical}$ (0 isotropic, 1 perfectly vertical).
- Aspect ratio = depth/width. A 10 µm wide, 100 µm deep TSV is 10:1; 3D NAND channel holes reach aspect ratios of several tens to one.
- Uniformity and loading effects: narrower openings etch slower (aspect-ratio-dependent etching).

**Endpoint detection.** Optical emission spectroscopy watches a plasma species whose signal changes when the film clears; the etch continues for a controlled overetch.

**Deep silicon (Bosch) process.** Alternates an SF₆ etch step with a C₄F₈ passivation step, giving high-aspect-ratio vertical features with small "scallops" on the sidewalls (used for TSVs and MEMS).

**Atomic layer etching** removes one monolayer per cycle by alternating surface modification and removal, the mirror image of ALD ([[q0663]]).
:::

:::

::: long
"Etching is the step after lithography that actually removes material. Lithography leaves a patterned layer of photoresist, and etching removes the film in the openings so the pattern is carved into the layer below.

There are two main kinds. Wet etching dips the wafer in a liquid chemical, for example buffered hydrofluoric acid dissolves silicon dioxide. It's cheap and can be extremely selective, meaning it attacks the target film and leaves the others alone. But for amorphous films it's isotropic: it etches sideways as fast as downward, so it undercuts the mask by as much as the depth. For a 100 nanometre film that's 100 nanometres on each side, which would eat a 50 nanometre wide line completely. So wet etching is used for large features, cleaning, and special cases like KOH etching of silicon along crystal planes.

Modern chips use dry etching, which is a plasma. Gas such as a fluorocarbon or chlorine is ionized by RF power in a low-pressure chamber. The radicals chemically react with the surface, and the ions are accelerated by an electric field straight down into the wafer, and that bombardment makes the etch anisotropic. Often a thin polymer passivates the sidewalls, so you get steep, vertical walls even for deep, narrow trenches. This is reactive-ion etching. A special version, the Bosch process, alternates etch and passivation steps to etch very deep holes for through-silicon vias.

The main numbers are etch rate, anisotropy and selectivity. Selectivity is the ratio of the etch rate of the film to the mask or underlayer: if I'm etching 500 nanometres of oxide with a 5 to 1 selectivity to resist, I need about 120 nanometres of resist to allow for overetch. Process engineers tune gases, pressure and power to balance profile, selectivity, uniformity and plasma damage, and they use optical emission to detect when the film has cleared."
:::

::: terms
- **Etch rate**: film thickness removed per unit time.
- **Isotropic etch**: removes material equally in all directions, causing undercut.
- **Anisotropic etch**: removes material mainly in one direction (vertical).
- **Selectivity**: ratio of etch rates of two materials, e.g. film vs mask.
- **RIE (reactive-ion etching)**: plasma etching with ion bombardment plus chemical reactions.
- **Undercut**: lateral etching beneath the mask edge.
- **Overetch**: extra etch time after endpoint to clear the film everywhere.
- **Hard mask**: an oxide or nitride mask used when resist is not robust enough.
- **Aspect ratio**: depth divided by width of an etched feature.
- **Bosch process**: alternating etch/passivation process for deep silicon etching.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "etching removes material, wet or dry." An exceptional answer:
- Places etching right after lithography as the pattern-transfer step.
- Explains isotropic vs anisotropic with the undercut argument (undercut ≈ depth for isotropic) and why it forces dry etch below micron scale.
- Defines selectivity and works a mask-thickness example ($1.2\,t/S$).
- Describes plasma etching physically (radicals plus directional ions, sidewall passivation) and names real chemistries.
- Mentions high-aspect-ratio etching (Bosch, TSVs, 3D NAND), loading effects and endpoint detection.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying wet etching is "bad".** It is excellent for selectivity, cleaning and large features; and KOH gives well-defined anisotropic silicon profiles.
- **Ignoring selectivity.** A fast etch is useless if it also removes the mask or the underlying layer.
- **Equating dry etching with "no chemistry".** Plasma etching is chemical plus physical; pure sputtering has poor selectivity.
- **Forgetting undercut.** In isotropic etching lines get narrower than drawn by roughly twice the etch depth.
- **Confusing etching with developing.** Developing removes resist; etching removes the device film.
:::

## Why undercut matters

For a film of thickness $t$ and a line drawn width $w$, an isotropic etch leaves a line of width $w - 2t$ (undercut $t$ per side). With $t = 100$ nm and $w = 50$ nm the line is gone. With $t = 100$ nm and $w = 500$ nm it is 300 nm wide, a 40% error. Dry etching is the answer once feature sizes approach the film thickness.

## Wet etching

- **Buffered oxide etch (BOE, HF-based)** dissolves SiO₂, with an enormous selectivity over silicon. Typical rates are on the order of 100 nm/min depending on mix and temperature.
- **Hot phosphoric acid** strips silicon nitride, selectively over oxide.
- **KOH/TMAH** etch silicon with strong crystal-plane dependence: {111} planes etch much more slowly than {100}. On a (100) wafer this makes V-grooves and pyramidal pits with sidewall angle $\arctan\sqrt2 = 54.74^\circ$, used in MEMS.
- Advantages: simple, cheap, batch processing, excellent selectivity, no plasma damage. Disadvantages: isotropic for most films, hard to control for small features, chemical waste, and capillary and surface-tension problems for fragile nanostructures.

## Dry (plasma) etching

A low-pressure gas (typically a few mTorr to hundreds of mTorr) is energized by RF power (13.56 MHz is common) into a plasma containing ions, electrons and neutral reactive radicals. The wafer sits on an electrode that develops a negative self-bias, so positive ions accelerate across the sheath and strike the wafer at nearly normal incidence with energy tens to hundreds of eV.

Two mechanisms act together:

1. **Chemical**: radicals (e.g. F from CF₄ or SF₆) react with the surface to form volatile products (e.g. SiF₄) that are pumped away. Alone this would be isotropic.
2. **Physical/ion-assisted**: ion bombardment breaks surface bonds and removes passivation on horizontal surfaces, speeding up *vertical* etching, while sidewalls, shielded from ions, stay protected by a thin polymer or oxide formed by the gas chemistry.

This synergy is why the combined etch rate is much greater than either chemical or physical sputtering alone. Typical chemistries: CF₄/CHF₃/C₄F₈ for SiO₂ and SiN; SF₆ or Cl₂/HBr for silicon and polysilicon; chlorine- and fluorine-based mixtures for metals.

## Selectivity and mask thickness

Mask erosion during etch is the central constraint. If the film has thickness $t$, etch includes an overetch fraction $f$ (e.g., 20%), and selectivity to the mask is $S$:

$$t_{mask,min}=\frac{(1+f)\,t}{S}$$

## Aspect ratio and deep etching

Etching a narrow opening deeply is hard: reactive species and ions have a harder time reaching the bottom and etch by-products have difficulty escaping, so the rate falls as aspect ratio rises (**aspect-ratio-dependent etching**, "RIE lag"). Ion scattering in tall trenches can cause bowing or tapering.

The **Bosch process** alternates (a) an SF₆ plasma step that etches silicon isotropically for a short time and (b) a C₄F₈ step that deposits a fluorocarbon polymer on all surfaces. The next etch step removes polymer from the bottom first (by ions), but sidewalls stay protected, so the trench deepens vertically with tiny "scallops" on the sidewall. It's used for TSVs ([[q0653]]), MEMS and deep-trench structures.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Achievable aspect ratios and etch rates depend on tool, chemistry and feature size; values like "tens-to-one" for memory holes or "several µm per minute" for Bosch etching are typical ranges quoted in the literature, not specifications.

## Atomic layer etching

At the smallest scales even RIE removes too many atoms per second. **Atomic layer etching (ALE)** alternates a self-limiting surface-modification step with a gentle removal step, so each cycle removes about one monolayer, the opposite of atomic layer deposition ([[q0663]]). It gives sub-nm thickness control and low damage, at lower throughput.

## Damage and cleaning

Ion bombardment creates damage in the top few nm of silicon, and plasma can charge structures (plasma-induced damage to thin gate oxides, mitigated by protection diodes in layout). After etching, residues are removed in a clean (resist strip by oxygen plasma "ashing", wet clean). The resist is stripped and the loop repeats ([[q0658]]).

## Where etching shows up

- **Transistor fins and gates**: tall silicon fins and polysilicon or metal gates are defined by anisotropic etches.
- **Copper interconnects**: trenches and vias etched in low-k dielectric (dual damascene) and then filled.
- **Memory**: DRAM capacitor holes and 3D NAND channel holes are among the highest-aspect-ratio etches in the industry.
- **Spacers**: a blanket deposit followed by an anisotropic etch leaves material only on sidewalls, which is the basis of spacer-based multiple patterning ([[q0664]]).
:::

::: applications
- **FinFET and gate-all-around transistors**: etching fins, gate stacks and nanosheet release.
- **Copper damascene wiring**: etching trenches and vias in low-k dielectrics.
- **3D NAND and DRAM**: very high-aspect-ratio hole and trench etching.
- **MEMS sensors and TSVs**: Bosch deep silicon etch and KOH etching of cavities.
- **PCB manufacturing**: the same idea in wet form: ferric chloride or persulfate etchants remove unwanted copper, and undercut is a design consideration for trace width.
:::

::: assumptions
- Numbers and chemistries are representative of silicon CMOS processing; metals, III-V and MEMS processes use different etchants.
- Selectivity and rates depend on temperature, pressure, power and gas mix, so quoted numbers (such as 5:1 or ~100 nm/min) are illustrative.
- The undercut equals depth rule applies to isotropic etching of a uniform amorphous film; crystalline silicon in KOH is a special anisotropic case.
- Overetch of 20% is a typical illustration; real recipes depend on film non-uniformity and selectivity to the underlayer.
- Alternative valid answer: mention chemical-mechanical polishing as a related removal process, but it planarizes rather than patterns.
:::

::: followups
### What's the difference between isotropic and anisotropic etching, and why does it matter?
Isotropic etching removes material equally in all directions, so it undercuts the mask by about the etch depth on each side. Anisotropic etching mainly removes material vertically, so feature width is preserved. For features close to the film thickness, isotropic etching destroys the pattern, so fine features need anisotropic (plasma) etching.

### What is selectivity and how much do you need?
It's the ratio of etch rates, e.g. film to mask or film to underlying layer. If I etch 500 nm of oxide with 5:1 selectivity to resist and want 20% overetch, I need at least $1.2\times500/5=120$ nm of resist. If the underlying layer is a 2 nm gate oxide, I need a very high selectivity to it (tens to one or better) or the etch punches through.

### How does a plasma etch achieve vertical walls?
Ions accelerate vertically through the sheath and hit horizontal surfaces, enhancing the etch there, while the vertical sidewalls see few ions and are covered by a protective polymer or oxide formed from the plasma chemistry. So the bottom etches and the sidewall doesn't.

### Why is etching high-aspect-ratio holes difficult?
Reactants and ions struggle to get down narrow openings and by-products struggle to leave, so etch rate falls with aspect ratio and the profile can bow, twist or taper. Charging effects can deflect ions. Techniques include pulsed plasmas, cryogenic temperatures, and process tuning with hard masks.

### Scenario: after etch, the gate lines are narrower at the top than the resist pattern and the underlying oxide is thinned. What changed?
Narrower lines mean more lateral etch (reduced sidewall passivation, higher pressure or lower bias) or mask erosion causing faceting. Thinned gate oxide means insufficient selectivity or too long an overetch. I'd check the polymer-forming gas flow, the bias power, the endpoint detection, and adjust overetch time and chemistry, then verify with a cross-section.
:::
