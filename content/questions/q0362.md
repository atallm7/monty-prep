::: testing
Whether you understand that brush contact is a **mechanical** design problem: springs push the brush onto the commutator with a controlled force, a holder guides it, and a flexible pigtail carries the current so the spring does not have to. The deeper test is the force trade-off (too low causes bounce, arcing and noisy contact; too high causes wear and friction), how the force changes as the brush wears (coil spring vs constant-force spring), and how small motors replace all of this with a spring-metal finger.
:::

::: why
Contact pressure sets brush life, contact drop, noise and sparking. A weak or fatigued spring is a classic cause of intermittent motors; a too-strong one burns through brushes. Whether you are designing a motor, picking a replacement brush assembly ([[q0361]]) or debugging a noisy motor ([[q0355]]), knowing how contact is maintained and what the pressure should be is practical, quantifiable engineering.
:::

::: concepts
- **Contact pressure**: force per unit area on the brush face; typical carbon brushes work at low pressures (about 1.5–2.5 psi, roughly 10–17 kPa; check the grade). See [[q0358]].
- **Spring force vs wear**: as a brush wears, the spring extends and its force may change.
- **Brush bounce**: contact loss when inertial force exceeds spring force. See [[q0359]].
- **Friction power**: $\mu F_N v$ lost at the interface. See [[q0360]].
- **Pigtail (shunt)**: flexible copper braid carrying the current from the brush to the holder.
- **Spring types**: compression coil, constant-force (rolled-strip) spring, torsion spring on a pivoting arm, leaf spring finger.
:::

::: answer
"Springs. Each brush sits in a holder, a small box or guide that lets it slide freely in and out, and a spring pushes it against the commutator with a controlled force. As the brush wears, the spring pushes it further forward so contact is maintained.

There are a few typical arrangements. In larger motors and power tools, you have a brush block in a rectangular holder with a coil compression spring behind it, or a spring clip. In industrial machines, they often use a constant-force spring, a coiled strip that gives nearly the same force however much the brush has worn, or a pivoting arm with a torsion spring. The current doesn't go through the spring; a flexible copper braid called a pigtail goes from the brush to the terminal, so the spring isn't heated and the brush can move freely.

In small, cheap motors, like toy motors, the brush is just a thin springy metal finger, often with a precious-metal contact tip, bent so its own elasticity presses it on the commutator. So the spring and the conductor are the same part.

The key is getting the right force: typically something like 1.5 to 2.5 pounds per square inch on the brush face for a carbon brush. Too low and the brush bounces, so you get sparking and noise. Too high and you wear out the brushes and commutator and waste power in friction."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism.**
1. **Holder** (box or channel): guides the brush radially or at an angle, with small clearance (enough to slide freely at temperature, little enough to avoid chatter/tilting). The holder's tilt angle relative to the commutator radius (radial, reaction or trailing) affects chatter and rotation-direction sensitivity.
2. **Spring**: applies normal force $F_N$ along the brush axis.
3. **Pigtail**: flexible braided copper from the brush body to the holder or terminal, carrying the current so that it doesn't flow through the spring (which would heat and anneal it) or through sliding holder contacts.

**Spring types and behaviour.**
- *Compression coil spring* with rate $k$: $F = k(x_0 - w)$ where $w$ is brush wear. Force falls as the brush wears. Example: $k = 60$ N/m, 8 mm preload compression gives 0.48 N; after 4 mm of wear, 0.24 N (halved).
- *Constant-force (clock-type, rolled strip) spring*: the force is nearly independent of extension, so the pressure stays at its set value as the brush wears (until the end of travel).
- *Torsion spring on a pivoted arm*: compact, moderate force variation, common in larger machines.
- *Leaf/finger spring brush* (small motors): the metal finger's elasticity gives force and conducts current; the finger is bent so its tip bears on the commutator. Force drops with wear/deflection and the finger must be sized to avoid fatigue.

**Pressure.** Typical design pressure for carbon-graphite brushes on a copper commutator is of order 1.5–2.5 psi (about 10–17 kPa), and for metal-graphite or high-speed brushes can be different; grade datasheets give the range. For a 40 mm² (0.4 cm²) brush, 1.5–2.5 psi corresponds to $F_N \approx 0.41$–0.69 N.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Recommended brush pressures depend on the grade, speed and application, and are published by brush manufacturers. The values here are typical round figures for general-purpose carbon grades, not a specification.

**Force limits.**
- *Lower*: the brush must follow runout and vibration, requiring $F_N \gtrsim m e\omega^2$ (bounce condition), plus margin ([[q0359]]); and enough pressure for a stable contact drop.
- *Upper*: wear $\propto F_N$ ([[q0360]]); friction power $P = \mu F_Nv$ per brush (e.g. $0.2\times0.5\times3.77 = 0.38$ W); heating; commutator wear.
So there is an optimum window, and constant-force springs keep the brush within it over life.
:::

::: long
"Brush contact is basically a spring-loaded sliding block.

Each brush sits in a holder, a small box or channel that guides it so it can slide in and out as it wears and as the commutator runs slightly out of round. A spring behind the brush pushes it against the commutator, and the current doesn't go through that spring: there's a flexible braided copper lead, a pigtail, from the brush to the terminal, so the brush can move freely and the spring doesn't get hot.

Springs come in a few types. A simple coil compression spring is common in power tools and smaller industrial brush holders. Its force falls as the brush wears and the spring extends: with a 60 newton per metre spring at 8 millimetres of preload, you start at 0.48 newtons and after 4 millimetres of wear you're down to 0.24, so contact gets worse as it wears. A constant-force spring, which is a coiled strip, gives almost the same force through the whole wear range, which is why you see it in industrial brush gear. Some larger machines use a pivoted arm with a torsion spring. And in small motors, like toy motors, the brush is just a bent, springy metal finger that is both the contact and the spring.

The force is the design trade-off. Typical carbon brushes want something like 1.5 to 2.5 psi on the face, roughly half a newton for a 40 square millimetre brush. Too low, and the brush can't follow the commutator's runout at speed, because the inertial force $m e \omega^2$ exceeds the spring force, so it bounces, arcs and makes noise. Too high, and wear goes up in proportion and you waste power in friction, around 0.4 watts per brush in my example.

So I'd pick the lowest force that keeps contact over the speed range, use a pigtail and a holder with the right clearance, and use constant-force springs where long life matters."
:::

::: terms
- **Brush holder (box)**: guide that constrains the brush and carries the spring.
- **Contact pressure**: normal force divided by brush face area (psi or kPa).
- **Constant-force spring**: rolled strip providing nearly constant force over its extension.
- **Pigtail / shunt**: flexible braided lead carrying current from brush to holder.
- **Brush bounce**: loss of contact when inertia exceeds spring force.
- **Reaction/trailing/radial holder**: brush axis orientation relative to the commutator radius and rotation.
- **Spring rate $k$**: N/m, force per unit compression.
- **Preload**: force with a new brush installed.
- **Leaf-spring (finger) brush**: a conductive spring-metal finger acting as both spring and brush.
- **Runout (TIR)**: out-of-roundness the brush must follow.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "springs press them on". An exceptional answer:

- Describes the whole contact system: holder guidance, spring, pigtail, and why current avoids the spring.
- Names spring types and the difference in force-vs-wear behaviour, with numbers (coil: force halves after 4 mm, constant force flat).
- Gives the pressure window with a number (≈1.5–2.5 psi, ≈0.4–0.7 N for 40 mm²) and notes it's grade dependent.
- Explains both limits: bounce condition $F_N > m e\omega^2$ and wear/friction $\propto F_N$.
- Mentions small-motor finger brushes and wear indicators/auto-stop features.
- Mentions holder clearance and orientation (radial, trailing/reaction), and checking spring fatigue in maintenance.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying gravity or the brush's own weight keeps contact.** Motors work in any orientation; springs provide the force.
- **Sending the current through the spring.** It would heat and lose temper; a pigtail carries current.
- **Using as much force as possible "to be safe".** Wear and friction rise with force; there's an optimum.
- **Assuming the force stays constant as the brush wears** with a coil spring: it falls, so contact degrades late in life.
- **Mixing spring types/forces across brushes** of one commutator: uneven current sharing and wear.
- **Ignoring holder clearance.** Too tight and the brush sticks (loses contact), too loose and it chatters or tilts.
:::

## The force on the brush

The normal force sets the contact conditions:
- **Pressure** $p = F_N/A$. With a brush face of 5 × 8 mm = 40 mm² (0.4 cm²) at $F_N = 0.48$ N: $p = 12$ kPa = 1.74 psi, in the typical window.
- **Friction force** $F_f = \mu F_N$; power loss $\mu F_N v$: $0.2\times0.48\times3.77 = 0.36$ W per brush at 6000 rpm on a 12 mm commutator.
- **Contact drop and noise** improve with pressure up to a point, because the real contact area grows; beyond it, extra force just wears.
- **Bounce** is avoided when $F_N$ exceeds the inertial force from runout, $m e\omega^2$. With $m = 2$ g and $e = 20$ µm: 0.016 N at 6000 rpm; 0.39 N at 30,000 rpm ([[q0359]]). So the required minimum rises with the square of speed, and high-speed motors need stiffer springs or lighter brushes.

Therefore: minimum force = max(bounce requirement with margin, contact stability) and maximum force = wear/friction/heat limit. Design inside the window.

## Spring behaviour over the brush's life

A coil spring has force $F = k(x_0 - w)$, with $w$ the wear. The brush is worn away at the contact end while the spring extends, and force falls linearly. A constant-force spring maintains $F\approx F_0$.

*Lower spring rate means less force change with wear*: halve $k$ and double the preload deflection for the same $F_0$, and the force drop with a given wear is halved. That is why long, soft springs are used in brush boxes with long travel.

## Spring types in practice

| Type | Force vs wear | Typical use | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compression coil | Falls with wear (rate $k$) | Power tools, small industrial | Cheap; choose low rate and long length |
| Constant-force (rolled strip) | Nearly constant | Industrial DC machines, slip rings | Keeps pressure over the whole brush length |
| Torsion spring on pivot arm | Moderate change | Larger machines, adjustable | Pressure adjustable; hinge needs to move freely |
| Leaf/finger spring (metal brush) | Falls with deflection | Small PM motors, toys, miniature | Spring and conductor in one; precious-metal tip |
| Tension/leaf with pigtail | Varies | Compact motors | Space-saving |

## Holder design details

- **Clearance**: small, uniform clearance in the box; the brush must slide freely with thermal expansion and dust, but not tilt or chatter. Typical clearances are a few tenths of a millimetre, or less, in the width direction (follow the maker's tolerance).
- **Angle**: *radial* holders put the brush axis along the radius (works in both rotation directions); *reaction* and *trailing* holders tilt the brush so friction pulls it against the holder (reduces chatter, but usually one direction only; reversing motors use radial brushes).
- **Height above commutator**: a small gap (about 2 mm, check spec) between holder and commutator prevents the holder from touching it and lets dust escape.
- **Pigtail**: braided copper sized for the current; attached by crimp or swaged into the brush body and terminated on the holder or terminal. It must be flexible and long enough not to restrain the brush; it is one of the checks in brush replacement.
- **Spring finger limit**: wear indicators and auto-stop brushes (a spring that pushes a contact or a clip when the brush wears to a limit) protect the commutator from running on worn-out brushes.

## Small motors: the brush is the spring

In a toy or micro motor, each brush is a metal strip (copper alloy such as phosphor bronze or beryllium copper, or a multi-finger precious-metal "paliney" style contact). It is bent so that when the commutator is in place, the strip is deflected by $\delta$ and exerts $F = k\delta$, with a few tens of millinewtons for micro motors. It also carries the current, so the spring is subject to heating and must stay elastic (a thin strip carrying amps heats, so the current is limited). Force is set by geometry, not adjustable, and drops with wear or deflection.

## How it all links to the rest of the brush questions

Spring force sets friction and wear ([[q0360]]), bounce and speed limit and arcing ([[q0359]]), contact drop ([[q0357]]), and the grade requirement ([[q0358]]). When replacing brushes ([[q0361]]), always check spring condition, because a weak or fatigued spring shortens the new brushes' life and a worn commutator could be only a symptom.

::: applications
- **Power tools**: coil spring brush holders in plastic or metal boxes with removable end caps.
- **Industrial DC motors and generators**: brush boxes with constant-force or torsion springs, adjustable pressure, wear monitoring.
- **Slip rings in wind turbines**: multiple spring-loaded brushes on continuous rings.
- **Automotive starters and alternators**: spring-loaded carbon blocks with pigtails in a brush plate.
- **Toy, camera and pager motors**: bent metal finger brushes acting as spring and contact.
- **Auto-stop brush gear**: spring-loaded wear indicators that stop the motor before damage.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Carbon-graphite brushes on a copper commutator** with typical pressures; other grades differ.
- **Single brush with linear spring** for the calculations; real springs have nonlinearity, friction in the holder and pivot geometry effects.
- **Rigid, round commutator** with small runout; real surfaces vary.
- **Orientation-independent**: spring force dominates gravity (brush mass of grams).
- **Numbers are illustrative**; the manufacturer's recommended pressure and holder tolerances should be used for design.
- In small motors the finger brush has no separate pigtail, so current passes through the spring, which limits current capability.

::: followups
### Why not just use a really strong spring so the brush never bounces?
Wear rate and friction increase in proportion to force (Archard, $P = \mu F_Nv$), so life shortens and the motor loses power to friction, and the commutator wears faster. The right choice is the lowest force that tracks the surface with margin at the maximum speed, which is typically a modest pressure of around 1.5–2.5 psi for carbon grades.

### How does a constant-force spring work, and why is it used?
It is a pre-stressed strip that coils on itself; unrolling it takes nearly constant force because the strip's bending stress state at the coil radius stays the same. So the brush pressure doesn't drop as the brush wears, which keeps the contact consistent over the brush's life and reduces end-of-life sparking.

### Why a pigtail instead of passing the current through the spring?
The spring would carry amps through a thin, high-resistance, low-thermal-mass element: it would heat, lose temper (lose force) and could fuse. The sliding contact between brush and holder wall is also a poor, noisy conductor. A pigtail provides a dedicated, flexible low-resistance path.

### A brush sticks in its holder and the motor runs intermittently. What do you check?
Dust and carbon build-up or a swollen/warped brush; holder clearance too tight or bent; a tilted brush; a bent or fatigued spring; rust or varnish in the box; and a pigtail that is too short and pulls the brush. Clean, check dimensions and replace worn parts as a set ([[q0361]]).

### How would you measure the brush force?
Use a spring scale or a small force gauge pulling the brush off the commutator (or pressing the brush against a scale) at the position of installation; compare with the specification and between brushes, which should be within about 10% of each other. Measure with a new brush and with a worn one to see the change with wear.

### Does the optimal force depend on speed?
Yes. The bounce limit rises with $\omega^2$, so high-speed motors need more force or lighter brushes, while for low speeds a lower force is enough and wears less. This is why universal motors turning 20,000 rpm use stiffer springs than slow geared DC motors.
:::
