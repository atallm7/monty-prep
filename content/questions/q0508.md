::: testing
Whether you can predict and recognise the **consequences** of badly placed decoupling, not just state the rule. The interviewer wants:

1. The mechanism: extra loop inductance gives $L\,di/dt$ spikes and moves a PDN anti-resonance into the chip's active band.
2. The symptoms in real hardware: resets, logic errors, ADC noise, jitter, EMC failures, margin-test failures.
3. A numeric feel (a 1 nH vs 10 nH comparison).
4. How you would diagnose it and what you would change.
:::

::: why
Far-placed capacitors are the classic "looks fine in the schematic, fails in the lab" defect. The capacitor is on the board, the value is right, and the rail averages 3.3 V, but the board resets when a bus switches. Knowing the symptom-to-cause chain lets you debug it in an afternoon instead of a week, and in an interview it shows you have seen a real power-integrity failure.
:::

::: concepts
- **Loop inductance**: grows with distance, about 0.3-1 nH/mm of connection plus vias ([[q0504]]).
- **$L\,di/dt$ droop**: the immediate voltage spike when current steps ([[q0501]]).
- **Anti-resonance with die capacitance**: parallel resonance of loop L with the chip's own capacitance.
- **Return/loop area**: bigger loop means more radiation and coupling ([[q0503]]).
- **Shared-rail coupling**: noise at one pin appears on the rail other chips use ([[q0142]]).
- **Short-lead probing**: how to see the problem on the bench.
:::

::: answer
"The capacitor still holds its charge, but it can't deliver it fast enough. The connection between the capacitor and the chip is inductance, so when the chip's current steps in a nanosecond, the pin voltage dips by L times di/dt before the capacitor can respond. A 100 milliamp step in a nanosecond across 1 nanohenry is 100 millivolts; across 10 nanohenries, which is what a centimetre or so of trace and vias gives you, it's a full volt. That shows up as supply glitches right at the pin.

What you see in hardware: random resets or brown-out triggers, logic errors or corrupted bits when many outputs switch together, noise or offset on ADC readings, jitter on PLLs and clocks, and it fails margining, like working at 1.8 V but not at 1.7. The longer loop also resonates with the chip's own capacitance at a lower frequency with a higher peak, and it radiates more, so you can fail EMC scans with spurs at those frequencies. The noise also leaks onto the shared rail, so other chips suffer.

To confirm, I'd probe the supply at the pin with a spring-tip ground, not at the capacitor, and compare. The fix is to move the capacitor closer, add smaller capacitors against the pins, put them on the back under the part with short vias, add parallel capacitors to divide the inductance, and slow the edges if I can."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism.** With loop inductance $L$ between capacitor and pin, a load step $\Delta I$ with edge time $t_r$ gives an immediate drop $L\,\Delta I/t_r$. After the edge, the capacitor discharges: $\Delta V\approx \Delta I\,t/C + R\,\Delta I$.

**Numbers.** For $\Delta I=100$ mA, $t_r=1$ ns, 100 nF, $R=50$ mΩ: with $L=1$ nH the edge spike is $0.1$ V (plus 5 mV from $R$); with $L=10$ nH it is 1.0 V. After the edge both settle to about $-6.5$ mV at 2 ns and $-9.5$ mV at 5 ns; the capacitor is fine, the inductance is the problem.

**Anti-resonance.** The loop inductance resonates with the die and package capacitance $C_d$: $f_{ar}\approx 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L C_d})$, peak impedance $\approx L/(R\,C_d)$. For $C_d=10$ nF and $R=0.1\ \Omega$: 1 nH gives 50 MHz and about 1 Ω; 10 nH gives 16 MHz and about 10 Ω. Far placement lowers the frequency and raises the peak.

**Symptoms by mechanism.**
| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| Resets / brown-out flags at specific activity | spike below the brown-out or logic threshold |
| Corrupted data when many I/Os toggle | ground and supply bounce, SSN |
| ADC noise correlated with digital activity | supply noise through PSRR |
| PLL/clock jitter | noise on VCO/PLL supply |
| Fails at low voltage corner only | reduced headroom absorbs the spike |
| EMC spurs near $f_{ar}$ and clock harmonics | larger loop and PDN peak |

**Diagnosis.** Measure at the pin (spring-tip ground), at the capacitor, and at the regulator. A large difference between pin and capacitor voltages is the connection inductance. Compare with the capacitor removed or moved; use a current probe or a pulsed-load test.
:::

::: short
"If the capacitors are too far, the connection inductance limits how fast they can supply current, so the pin sees L di/dt spikes regardless of capacitance: 100 mA in a nanosecond through 10 nH is a volt. You see resets, corrupted data when outputs switch together, ADC and PLL noise, and failures at low-voltage corners. The bigger loop also shifts the PDN anti-resonance lower and radiates more, so EMC suffers. I'd confirm at the pin with a spring-tip probe and fix it by moving smaller capacitors closer with short vias."
:::

::: long
"The capacitor itself still works. What fails is the path between it and the chip. That path is inductance: roughly half to one nanohenry per millimetre of trace, plus about a nanohenry for each pair of vias through a thick board. When the chip's current steps, the pin voltage drops by L times di/dt before the capacitor can respond. With 100 milliamps in a nanosecond, 1 nanohenry gives 100 millivolts and 10 nanohenries gives a full volt. The capacitor then holds the rail within a few millivolts afterwards, which is why a slow multimeter or a long ground-lead probe at the capacitor looks fine.

The symptoms depend on what the spike hits. Brown-out detectors or reset circuits trip, so the board resets randomly. Logic sees a glitch, so you get corrupted bits when a lot of outputs switch together. Analog parts convert supply noise to output noise, so an ADC picks up noise tied to digital activity, and PLLs get jitter. Margin tests fail first at the low-voltage corner. And the longer loop does two more things: it resonates with the chip's own capacitance, lower in frequency and higher in impedance, so there's a peak where the chip has energy, and it radiates more, so you see spurs in an EMC scan. Noise also leaks onto the rail that other chips share.

To diagnose, I'd probe at the pin with a spring-tip ground, not at the capacitor, and compare. I'd trigger on the activity that causes it. Fixes: put small capacitors against the pins, same side or on the back under the part with short vias adjacent to the pads, paralleling more capacitors to divide inductance, thinning the dielectric to the planes, and slowing the edges or adding series resistors if the design allows."
:::

::: terms
- **$L\,di/dt$ spike**: instantaneous voltage across loop inductance when current steps.
- **Anti-resonance**: parallel resonance between loop inductance and die/package capacitance.
- **Brown-out**: supply dip below the threshold at which logic or memory misbehaves.
- **SSN**: simultaneous switching noise.
- **Voltage margining**: testing at supply corners (low and high).
- **Spring-tip probe**: short-ground probe tip that avoids ground-lead inductance.
- **PSRR**: supply rejection of an analog part.
- **Loop area**: area enclosed by the current loop.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "more noise and resets". An exceptional one:

- Gives the **mechanism and a number** (1 V for 100 mA/ns through 10 nH) and explains why the capacitor's own voltage looks fine.
- Maps **symptoms to causes** (reset vs data corruption vs ADC noise vs jitter vs low-voltage failures).
- Includes the **anti-resonance shift** and the EMC effect.
- Describes a **diagnosis method** (probe at the pin vs at the capacitor, spring tip, correlation with activity).
- Lists fixes in order of effect (shorter loop, parallel parts, plane spacing, edge rate).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Probing at the capacitor** and concluding the rail is clean. The spike exists at the pin, across the connection.
- **Adding more capacitance** on the same far connection. Capacitance doesn't reduce inductance.
- **Blaming firmware** for resets that follow supply spikes.
- **Using a long ground lead** on the scope, which adds its own inductive spikes and makes the measurement meaningless ([[q0492]]).
- **Assuming "too far" means centimetres.** For fast edges a few millimetres of unreferenced trace already matter.
:::

::: deep
## What the pin sees

Model the loop as inductance $L$ and resistance $R$ between the capacitor $C$ and the chip pin. A load current ramps from 0 to $\Delta I$ in time $t_r$, then holds. The pin voltage (relative to the capacitor's initial voltage, ignoring the slow regulator) is

$$v(t) = -\left[L\frac{di}{dt}+R\,i(t)+\frac{1}{C}\int i\,dt\right]$$

During the ramp the $L\,di/dt$ term is constant at $L\,\Delta I/t_r$; after the ramp it vanishes and only the slow capacitor discharge and $R\,\Delta I$ remain.

## Why it resets, corrupts and jitters

A spike is only a problem if the circuit can see it.

- **Reset and brown-out circuits** respond to a threshold crossing. A 1 V dip on a 3.3 V rail for 1 ns is usually too short for a comparator with filtering but not for a fast supervisor or for the logic itself; many parts have no deglitch.
- **Logic**: a supply dip shifts thresholds and slows gates (propagation delay rises as $V_{DD}$ falls), so setup/hold margin shrinks and bits flip. The same spike appears as ground bounce on the return side ([[q0497]]).
- **Analog circuits**: PSRR is finite and falls with frequency, so a 100 mV, 10 MHz ripple with 40 dB PSRR gives 1 mV at the output, more than 1 LSB for a 12-bit ADC.
- **PLLs and clocks**: supply noise modulates the VCO, creating jitter and spurs at the ripple frequency.
- **Margin**: the failure appears first when headroom is smallest (low voltage, hot, fast corner).

## The anti-resonance moves into the active band

The board capacitor looks like a short at high frequency, so the loop inductance is effectively in parallel-resonance with the chip's own capacitance $C_d$ (on-die plus package):

$$f_{ar}\approx\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L\,C_d}},\qquad |Z|_{peak}\approx\frac{L}{R\,C_d}$$

| Loop $L$ | $f_{ar}$ ($C_d=10$ nF) | $|Z|_{peak}$ ($R=0.1\ \Omega$) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 nH | 50 MHz | 1 Ω |
| 3 nH | 29 MHz | 3 Ω |
| 10 nH | 16 MHz | 10 Ω |

Further placement lowers the frequency and raises the peak. If the chip has clock or bus activity near $f_{ar}$, the rail rings at that frequency. Try the network model:

## Radiation and coupling

The loop area is bigger, so magnetic coupling and radiation rise. Radiated field from a small loop scales with area and $f^2$, so a 10x bigger loop is roughly 20 dB worse at the same current. EMC scans show peaks at clock harmonics near $f_{ar}$, and the noise couples into neighbouring traces and cables.

## Debugging recipe

1. **Probe at the pin** with a spring-tip ground (or a coax tip-and-barrel) on the supply and ground pads next to the pin. Never use the long ground clip ([[q0492]]).
2. **Trigger on the activity**: GPIO burst, memory access, radio transmit.
3. **Compare** the pin to the capacitor pad a few mm away. A difference of hundreds of mV with nearly identical low-frequency level shows connection inductance.
4. **Experiment**: bridge a small capacitor directly across the pin and ground pads (a dead-bug 0402 or 0201) and watch the spike shrink. This quick test proves the diagnosis.
5. **Correlate with symptoms**: if resets or errors disappear with the dead-bug capacitor, layout is the cause.

## Fixes, in rough order of effect

1. Put a small ceramic (100 nF or less) directly at the pins, with vias adjacent to the pads ([[q0503]]).
2. Place parts on the back under the package; use via-in-pad.
3. Parallel more capacitors with their own vias ($L/N$) ([[q0507]]).
4. Move power and ground planes closer to the surface; thin the plane-to-plane dielectric.
5. Reduce $di/dt$: slower edge rates, series resistors, staggered switching.
6. Add damping: a little ESR or a polymer capacitor to flatten the anti-resonance.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Mention the dead-bug test: "I'd solder a 0402 straight across the pin and ground pads; if the problem vanishes, it's placement."

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The anti-resonance peak formula is a simple lumped model; real dies include distributed damping and the actual peak is usually lower. The 0.5-1 nH/mm figure depends on how close the return plane is.
:::

::: applications
- **FPGA and processor boards**: resets or training failures traced to distant decoupling.
- **MCU boards with fast GPIO or SPI**: corrupted bits when many outputs toggle.
- **Mixed-signal boards**: ADC noise correlating with digital bursts from the same rail.
- **Wireless modules**: transmit bursts droop the rail if capacitors are far from the PA supply.
- **EMC debugging**: tracing a spur back to a PDN resonance and a loop that is too large.
:::

::: assumptions
- Fast-edge digital or burst loads; for slow loads, distance matters much less.
- Connection inductance of 0.3-1 nH/mm plus about 0.1-1.3 nH per via depending on stackup.
- Load edge of 1 ns and 100 mA are illustrative values.
- The lumped RLC model ignores distributed plane effects and on-die damping, so peaks are upper bounds.
- Another valid answer: the capacitors still help at low frequency, so a far capacitor is not useless, only ineffective above a few MHz.
:::

::: followups
### Does a far capacitor do anything at all?
Yes, for slow events. At 100 kHz, 10 nH is only 6 mΩ, so a capacitor several centimetres away still supplies microsecond-scale current. It fails for nanosecond events, where the same 10 nH presents about 6 Ω at 100 MHz.

### How would you prove that placement is the problem?
Probe at the pin and at the capacitor; then solder a small capacitor across the pin and ground pads. If the spike and the symptom shrink, the loop inductance was the cause. Simulation of the PDN with the extracted mounting inductance gives the same answer before layout.

### Why can the capacitor voltage look clean while the pin is noisy?
The inductance sits between them. The capacitor voltage changes only by $I\,t/C$, a few mV, while the voltage across the connection is $L\,di/dt$, hundreds of mV. Measure at the pin.

### What if you can't move the capacitors because the board is already built?
Rework: add 0201 or 0402 capacitors on the back directly under the pins, or dead-bug capacitors across the pins to prove the fix. Longer term: slower edge rates in firmware (drive strength), series resistors, and layout correction in the next spin.

### Scenario: board resets only when SPI runs at 50 MHz and the supply is 3.0 V. What do you suspect?
A supply spike from simultaneous switching, with the low-voltage corner reducing headroom to the brown-out threshold. I'd probe the MCU supply pin with a spring tip during SPI bursts, check capacitor distance and via length, add a capacitor at the pin, and reduce the SPI pin drive strength.
:::
