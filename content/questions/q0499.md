::: basics
### In plain words
To keep ground voltage differences small, you attack from two sides. Make the ground path low impedance, with a solid plane close to the signals, no slots or necks, and plenty of vias. And make the currents in shared ground paths small and local, with decoupling capacitors at the pins, tiny switching loops and slower edges. Then keep noisy and quiet circuits in different areas, and verify with probe points.

### Picture it
Think of quieting a noisy street. You can widen the road so traffic flows easily, which is lowering the impedance. You can cut the number of cars and the speed they go, which is lowering the current and its rate of change. You can route trucks along a different street from the school, which is partitioning. And you can place a sound meter outside the school to check, which is probe points. The analogy is loose: ground has no "street" of its own, only the copper you give it.

### Words you need
- **Return path impedance**: the resistance and inductance of the ground path ([[q0486]]).
- **Stitching vias**: vias that tie ground layers together near signal layer changes and at edges ([[q0487]]).
- **Decoupling capacitor**: a nearby capacitor that supplies fast current so it does not flow through the plane ([[q0503]]).
- **Ground bounce**: a ground shift when many outputs switch together, $V = N L\,di/dt$.
- **Error budget**: the allowed ground shift, from which you work backward to milliohms and nanohenries.

### The simplest example
A 12-bit, 3.3 V ADC has an LSB of about 0.81 mV, so half an LSB is about 0.4 mV. If 100 mA of return current crosses the shared path, the resistance must be under 0.4 mV / 0.1 A = 4 mΩ, about 8 squares of 1 oz copper. For ground bounce, 8 outputs switching 20 mA in 1 ns each (2 × 10⁷ A/s) with a 100 mV target need $L \le 0.1/(8 \times 2\times10^{7}) = 0.625$ nH. Budgets like these turn rules into numbers. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the stackup, decoupling, connectors, edge rates and how to verify.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can turn the *causes* of ground variation ([[q0498]]) into concrete layout and design rules, and justify each with a number. The hidden sub-skills:

1. Lowering the impedance (R and L) of the return path: planes, stackup, vias, copper weight.
2. Reducing the *current* that creates the drop: decoupling, loop area, edge rates, simultaneous switching.
3. Keeping noisy returns away from quiet circuits (placement, partitioning).
4. Budgeting: knowing how many millivolts you can afford and working backwards to milliohms and nanohenries.
:::

::: why
This is the question that distinguishes someone who can *name* ground problems from someone who can lay out a board that avoids them. Every rule here is cheap on paper and expensive to retrofit: the stackup, connector pinout and placement are fixed early. Real products that skip these rules fail EMC, drift in precision, or reset randomly, and a respin costs weeks.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ground plane as low-impedance conductor**: very low $R$ (≈0.5 mΩ/sq) and, when close to the signal, low $L$ ([[q0486]]).
- **Return path continuity**: keep an unbroken reference under every fast signal; stitch layers near transitions ([[q0487]]).
- **Source reduction**: lower $di/dt$ (decoupling, slower edges, fewer simultaneous switches).
- **Partitioning by placement**: noisy and quiet circuits in separate regions of the plane ([[q0494]], [[q0495]]).
- **Impedance budgeting**: from an allowed ground shift $\Delta V$ and the current $I$ to a maximum $R$ and $L$.
- **Measurement**: ground probe points to check the result ([[q0492]]).
:::

::: answer
"I attack it from both sides: make the ground impedance low, and make the currents flowing through it small and local.

For impedance, I use a solid, unbroken ground plane on the layer right next to the signal layers, with a thin dielectric so the loop inductance is tiny. I avoid slots, splits and neck-downs, I don't let via fields or connector keep-outs cut the plane, and I stitch layers with ground vias wherever signals change layers and around the edges. Connectors get lots of ground pins interleaved with signals, because each pin is about ten nanohenries.

For the currents, I put decoupling capacitors right at the pins so the fast current loops close locally and don't spread through the plane. I keep switching regulators' hot loops tiny. I limit simultaneous switching with slower edges, series resistors or staggered timing, and use enough supply and ground pins.

Then I partition by placement: power stage and digital on one side, sensitive analog on the other, with the ADC at the boundary, so noisy return currents never cross quiet circuits. And I put ground probe points on the board to verify it. I usually budget it numerically: if I want under half an LSB of error, I work back to how many milliohms and nanohenries I can afford."
:::

::: technical
**Two levers.** Ground variation is $\Delta V = I R + L\,di/dt$. Reduce $R$ and $L$, and reduce $I$ and $di/dt$ in the paths shared with sensitive circuits.

**Lower impedance.**
- *Plane per signal layer*: put a continuous ground plane adjacent to each routing layer. Plane inductance per square is $L_{sq}\approx\mu_0 h$; at $h=0.1$ mm that is 0.13 nH and at 0.2 mm 0.25 nH.
- *No slots or necks*: keep copper between mounting holes, connectors and via fields; if a neck is unavoidable, widen it and parallel it on another layer.
- *Copper weight*: 2 oz halves the sheet resistance (0.25 mΩ/sq) where DC current is large.
- *Stitching vias*: ground vias next to every signal layer change and at plane edges, spacing $\lesssim \lambda/20$ at the highest frequency of concern.
- *Connectors*: several ground pins, interleaved (e.g. 1 ground per 2 signals or better for fast signals); each pin ~5–15 nH, so parallel pins divide the inductance.

**Reduce the current.**
- Local decoupling (100 nF at each supply pin plus smaller/larger values as needed, [[q0503]]).
- Compact switching-regulator hot loop (< 2–5 nH).
- Edge-rate control: series resistors, slew-rate-limited drivers; $V=N L\,di/dt$ falls at least in proportion to edge slowing (roughly as $1/t_r^2$ for capacitive loads, since the peak current also drops).
- Fewer simultaneously switching outputs; spread across banks and ground pins.

**Segregate.** Place high-current and fast circuits near the power entry; place sensitive analog away from their return paths; keep analog return under analog.

**Budget example.** A 12-bit ADC (0.81 mV LSB) should see less than 0.5 LSB = 0.4 mV ground shift. With 100 mA of DC return current through the shared path, $R\le 0.4\text{ mV}/0.1\text{ A}=4$ mΩ, about 8 squares of 1 oz copper. For ground bounce with 8 outputs each switching 20 mA in 1 ns, keeping the bounce to 100 mV needs $L\le 0.1/(8\times 2\times10^{7}) = 0.625$ nH.
:::

::: short
"I keep ground variation small by lowering the impedance and shrinking the currents. Impedance: a solid unbroken plane right under the signal layers, no slots or neck-downs, stitching vias at layer changes, and many ground pins on connectors. Currents: tight decoupling at every power pin, compact switching loops, slower edges and fewer simultaneous switches. :::

::: long
"I treat ground like any other conductor with an impedance, so the voltage variation is I times R plus L di/dt. That gives me two levers: lower the impedance, and lower the currents in shared paths.

On impedance, the biggest single thing is a solid ground plane directly next to every routing layer, with a thin dielectric. Plane inductance per square is roughly mu-zero times the spacing, so 0.2 millimetres gives about a quarter of a nanohenry. Then I protect that plane: no slots under fast signals, no neck-downs between mounting holes and connectors, and I watch via fields and connector keep-outs that chop the copper. I put stitching vias wherever a signal changes layers or near the edge, so the return can change layers with it. For connectors I spend pins on ground and interleave them, because a pin is around ten nanohenries and a one-amp edge in ten nanoseconds across it is a volt. If there's a big DC current I'll use thicker copper.

On the current side, I decouple at every power pin so fast current loops close locally instead of running through the plane. I keep switching regulator input loops small, and I limit simultaneous switching with slower edges, series resistors or staggering, because ground bounce scales as N times L times di/dt.

Then I partition by placement: the power stage and digital near the entry, the analog in its own area, the ADC at the boundary, so return currents don't cross sensitive regions.

Finally I check it numerically and on the bench. For a 12-bit ADC I want under half an LSB, about 0.4 millivolts, so with 100 milliamps of return current that's 4 milliohms, around eight squares of one-ounce copper. And I add a ground probe point near the ADC to measure the real thing with a short spring-tip."
:::

::: terms
- **Plane inductance per square**: $L_{sq}\approx\mu_0 h$, with $h$ the distance to the signal layer.
- **Stitching via**: a via that ties ground layers together near a signal layer transition or edge.
- **Hot loop**: the high-$di/dt$ current loop in a switching converter (input cap, high-side and low-side switches).
- **Edge-rate control**: slowing rise/fall to reduce $di/dt$.
- **Slew-rate-limited driver**: an output buffer whose edge speed is limited internally.
- **Return path continuity**: unbroken reference plane beneath a signal.
- **Neck-down**: a narrow region of copper that concentrates current.
- **Ground probe point**: a test point that allows a low-inductance measurement of ground noise.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "use a ground plane and decoupling capacitors". An exceptional one:

- Frames it as two levers (lower $Z$, lower $I$ and $di/dt$ in shared paths) and sorts each rule under one of them.
- Gives *numbers*: 0.25 nH/sq at 0.2 mm; 4 mΩ budget from 0.4 mV and 100 mA; 0.6 nH for a 100 mV bounce target.
- Names the less obvious killers: via fields, connector pinouts, neck-downs, return path crossing a slot.
- Includes **placement and partitioning** and **edge-rate control**, not just copper.
- Closes the loop with measurement: ground probe points and short-lead scope technique.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Add more decoupling capacitors" as the only answer.** It reduces $di/dt$ in the plane, but a slotted or necked plane still develops large offsets.
- **Using a ground trace instead of a plane on a mixed-signal board** to "keep ground clean". It has far higher impedance.
- **One ground pin on a connector.** Fast edges across ten nH give volts; use many.
- **Splitting the plane and crossing it with fast signals.** See [[q0496]].
- **Putting the ADC next to the switching regulator** and relying on layout tricks later. Placement is the first-order fix.
:::

::: deep
## Where to start: the budget

Good layout is easier when you know what you are aiming for. Pick the quantity that matters and work backwards.

**Analog budget.** An ADC with $N$ bits and full-scale $V_{FS}$ has $LSB = V_{FS}/2^N$. A reasonable target is under 0.5 LSB of ground-induced error:

| ADC | LSB | 0.5 LSB budget | Max R at 100 mA | Squares of 1 oz Cu |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 bit, 3.3 V | 3.2 mV | 1.6 mV | 16 mΩ | ≈33 |
| 12 bit, 3.3 V | 0.81 mV | 0.40 mV | 4.0 mΩ | ≈8 |
| 16 bit, 3.3 V | 50 µV | 25 µV | 0.25 mΩ | ≈0.5 |

A 16-bit system therefore needs essentially zero shared copper with 100 mA of return current: either the current must go elsewhere, or the sensitive circuit must be on a separate region with its own return.

**Digital budget.** Most logic wants ground bounce below roughly 10–20 % of $V_{DD}$ (about 300–600 mV on 3.3 V), minus margin for other noise. With $N$ outputs, per-output $di/dt$ and shared inductance $L$:

$$L \le \frac{V_{bounce}}{N\,(di/dt)}$$

For 8 outputs at 20 mA/ns each and a 100 mV target, $L\le 0.625$ nH, which tells you to use many ground pins, short vias and a tight decoupling loop.

### Protect the plane from damage

- **Slots and splits**: don't route fast signals over them; remove unneeded splits.
- **Necks**: where the plane narrows (mounting holes, connectors), add copper on another ground layer or widen it.
- **Via fields**: leave solid copper between rows of vias; avoid long continuous antipad rows ("perforation").
- **Stitching vias**: place at layer transitions within a few mm of the signal via, around edges, and at spacing small compared with the shortest wavelength of concern.

### Connectors and cables

A mating pair of pins has about 5–15 nH (vendor-dependent). Interleave ground pins, e.g. one ground for every two or fewer signals on fast links, and use a ground-rich pinout for any connector carrying edges faster than a few ns. For cables, add dedicated ground conductors, twisted pairs or coax.

## Lowering the current in shared paths

### Decoupling

A capacitor at every IC supply pin provides the fast current locally, so the loop (supply pin → capacitor → ground pin) closes within a few millimetres rather than through the whole plane. The key is *low loop inductance*, not just value, so mount close and use short wide connections ([[q0503]], [[q0504]]).

### Switching regulators

The input capacitor – high-side switch – low-side switch loop carries amps at nanosecond edges. Make it a tight loop on the top layer with ground return under it on layer 2. Keep it away from the analog area.

### Edge rates and simultaneous switching

$V_{bounce}=N L\,di/dt$. For a fixed current step, slowing the edge by 2× halves $di/dt$; with capacitive loads the peak current also falls, so the benefit is closer to 4×. Series resistors (22–47 Ω) or slew-limited I/O settings, staggered output timing, and spreading outputs across ground pins all help. Try it in the simulation.

## Keep the noisy and quiet apart

- Put the power stage, motor drivers and clock generators near the power entry.
- Put the ADC and reference in a quiet region, with its input traces over solid plane and its decoupling directly at the pins.
- Route return currents on purpose: a high-current load's return should have a short, wide path directly to the supply, not through the sensitive region.
- If two grounds are truly necessary (isolation, motor), join them deliberately at one point ([[q0496]]).

## Verify it

Add ground probe points (a pad and a nearby ground via) near the ADC, the MCU, and the regulator. Measure ground-to-ground with a scope using a spring tip, not a long clip lead ([[q0492]]). Compare against the budget.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> When asked "how do you avoid it?", structure the answer as "lower the impedance, lower the current, separate the circuits, then verify". The structure is itself a signal of engineering habit.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The stitching-via spacing rule (≲ λ/20) and the per-pin connector inductance are common guidelines. Actual values depend on the highest significant frequency content, the connector family and the stackup.
:::

::: applications
- **Four-layer MCU/FPGA boards**: signal/GND/PWR/signal with 0.1–0.2 mm prepreg and stitched edges.
- **Precision DAQ cards**: ADC at the analog/digital boundary on a solid plane, with ground probe points to verify noise.
- **Motor drivers**: heavy copper, compact bridge loops, short returns to the bulk capacitor.
- **High-speed connectors (USB, HDMI, board-to-board)**: ground pins interleaved with signals.
- **Switching-regulator modules**: tiny hot loops, vias directly at the input capacitor ground pad.
:::

::: assumptions
- 1 oz (35 µm) copper and FR-4 dielectric unless stated; thicker copper reduces R but not L.
- $L_{sq}\approx\mu_0 h$ is a simple estimate for a wide plane close to the signal; real values depend on the current distribution.
- Targets (0.5 LSB, 10–20 % bounce) are design conventions, not standards; tighten them for precision systems or relax for noisy logic.
- Where isolation or very high currents are involved (motors, mains), a deliberate separation of grounds is justified ([[q0496]]).
- Another valid answer is simulation-led: use a field solver or PDN tool to check the plane impedance before layout.
:::

::: followups
### What's the first thing you'd do on a new mixed-signal board to keep ground clean?
Pick the stackup: signal/GND/PWR/signal with a thin dielectric to ground, then place the circuits so noisy ones are far from the quiet ones before routing anything. Those two decisions set most of the outcome; the rest is protecting the plane from slots and necks.

### How do you decide the number of ground pins on a connector?
From the return current and edge rate. A ground pin of 10 nH with 1 A in 10 ns gives 1 V, so for fast edges I'd interleave grounds so that every signal has a ground pin next to it and use enough to bring the total shared inductance down. The connector datasheet or the standard's pinout recommendation guides this.

### Why do stitching vias matter?
When a signal changes layers its return must change reference planes too. If there's no nearby ground via the return must travel around to find one, which creates a large loop and a ground voltage at that point. A stitching via within a few mm gives the return a short path.

### What if you can't afford a 4-layer board?
I'd go to a 2-layer board with a nearly solid ground pour on the bottom, route signals on top with a short ground return directly below them, avoid cutting the pour, place the sensitive analog away from the noisy logic, and use more decoupling and slower edges. The inductance per square is higher (about 2 nH at 1.6 mm), so I'd budget more conservatively.

### Scenario: your 16-bit ADC shows 6 LSB of noise correlated with an LED driver. How do you reduce it?
First measure: ground-to-ground at the ADC with a spring tip while the LED toggles. If I see a pulse, I'd reroute the LED's return to join the plane near the supply entry rather than through the ADC region, add a small resistor or slew control on the driver, put decoupling next to the LED driver, and check the ADC's own reference and analog ground connection. 6 LSB at 16 bit is only 0.3 mV, so tiny shared impedances matter.
:::

::: interpretations
"Avoid too much variation" means keeping the voltage difference between different points on the ground small enough for the circuits' tolerances. It's about *layout and design practice*; the system-level strategy of tolerating the variation that remains (differential links, isolation, ratiometric measurement) is in [[q0500]].
:::

::: rubric
- Plane next to the signal layers with no slots or neck-downs :: This is the foundation of low ground impedance.
- Stitching vias at layer transitions and plane edges :: They keep return paths short and planes at the same potential.
- Connector ground-pin strategy (many, interleaved) :: Connectors are often the highest-impedance link in the return.
- Local decoupling and compact switching loops to reduce $di/dt$ in shared paths :: Reducing the source is as important as reducing impedance.
- Edge-rate control and limiting simultaneous switching :: Ground bounce scales with $N L\,di/dt$.
- Partitioning by placement and verifying with ground probe points :: Shows the whole design-measure cycle.
- Gives a numeric budget (mV → mΩ, nH) :: Turns rules into engineering decisions.
