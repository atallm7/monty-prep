::: basics
### In plain words
A rotary encoder turns shaft rotation into electrical signals. The common type is an incremental quadrature encoder. It outputs two square waves, A and B, that are a quarter of a cycle apart. Counting the edges gives how far the shaft turned, and which wave comes first gives the direction. Dividing the count change by the elapsed time gives speed. An absolute encoder outputs a unique code at each angle instead.

### Picture it
Picture a striped belt sliding past two eyes that are placed a quarter of a stripe apart. As the belt moves one way, the first eye sees each stripe edge first. Move the belt the other way and the second eye sees it first. So the order tells you direction. Every change at either eye is an edge you can count, so one stripe gives four counts.

### Words you need
- **Lines (PPR)**: how many cycles of channel A occur per revolution.
- **Quadrature**: two signals 90° apart. Their order gives the direction.
- **CPR**: counts per revolution after decoding every edge, which is 4 times the lines.
- **Index pulse (Z)**: one pulse per revolution, used for homing.
- **Hardware quadrature counter**: a timer mode that counts up and down without CPU interrupts.

### The simplest example
Take a 1024-line encoder decoded on all edges, so CPR is $4 \times 1024 = 4096$. At 3000 rpm, that is 50 rev/s, or 204,800 counts per second. Read the counter every 1 ms and you get about 205 counts. One count is $60/(4096 \times 0.001) = 14.65$ rpm, which is 0.49% at this speed. At 30 rpm the same window holds only about 2 counts, so the speed estimate hops in steps.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the decoder state machine, the M/T hybrid method, signal conditioning, line drivers, absolute types, and homing.
:::

::: why
Encoders are the default feedback device in servos, robots, printers and any drive that needs good speed or position. Misusing one is common: decoding only one edge, forgetting direction, picking a speed estimator that jitters, or exceeding the count rate an MCU can handle. In interviews this is a compact test of sensors, digital timing and control thinking.

::: technical
**Incremental quadrature encoder.** Lines per rev $P$ give channel A and B each with $P$ cycles per revolution, 90° apart. Decoding modes:
- 1x: count one edge of A (P counts/rev); 2x: both edges of A; 4x: all four edges, $\text{CPR} = 4P$.

**Direction.** Forward sequence $(A,B)$: 00 → 10 → 11 → 01 → 00 (A leads B); reverse is the opposite order. A decoder is a 4-state machine; invalid transitions (00 → 11) indicate a missed edge or noise.

**Speed from counts.** Let $\Delta N$ be counts in time $\Delta t$:
$$\omega = \frac{2\pi\,\Delta N}{\text{CPR}\,\Delta t}\ \text{rad/s},\qquad n = \frac{60\,\Delta N}{\text{CPR}\,\Delta t}\ \text{rpm}$$
Resolution of one count: $\Delta n = 60/(\text{CPR}\,\Delta t)$. For $P = 1024$, CPR = 4,096 and $\Delta t = 1$ ms: $\Delta n = 14.6$ rpm. At 3,000 rpm that is 0.49%; at 30 rpm it is 49%.

**Speed from edge timing.** Between two successive counts the angle step is $2\pi/\text{CPR}$; if the timer measures $T_e$ seconds, $\omega = 2\pi/(\text{CPR}\,T_e)$. At 10 rpm with CPR = 4,096 the edge interval is 1.46 ms, so a 1 MHz timer has 1,465 ticks (0.07% error). At 3,000 rpm the interval is 4.9 µs, only about 5 ticks at 1 MHz, so use a faster clock (e.g. 72 MHz gives about 350 ticks) or switch to counting.

**Count rate.** At 3,000 rpm with 1,024 lines: channel A runs at 51.2 kHz and decoded edges arrive at 204.8 kHz. Polling or a simple pin-change interrupt per edge can miss counts, so use the MCU's hardware quadrature interface or a decoder IC. The encoder itself has a maximum output frequency (vendor-dependent; often in the 100 kHz range for common optical encoders, check the datasheet).

**Output types.** Open-collector or push-pull (needs pull-ups and filtering), or differential line-driver (RS-422 style, A and /A, much better noise immunity on long cables). **Absolute** types output parallel Gray code, SSI or other serial protocols.

**Mechanical/panel encoders** (rotary knobs with detents) are the same idea with contacts, so they bounce and need debouncing and have only a few counts per revolution, so they are not used for motor feedback.
:::

"An encoder is a shaft sensor that outputs pulses or codes representing angle. The common motor one is an optical incremental quadrature encoder: a disc with slots, an LED on one side and photodetectors on the other. Two detectors are offset by a quarter of a slot pitch, so I get two square waves A and B, 90 degrees apart. When A leads B the shaft is going one way, and when B leads A it's going the other. If I count rising and falling edges on both channels, I get four counts per line, so a 1,024-line encoder is 4,096 counts per rev. Most also provide an index pulse once per revolution, which I use to home. Absolute encoders replace that with a code that tells me the angle immediately.

To measure speed, I take the change in count over time. In the simplest version, every control period, say 1 millisecond, I read the counter, subtract the last value and convert: rpm equals 60 times delta counts over counts per rev times the period. With 4,096 counts per rev and 1 ms, one count is about 15 rpm, which is fine at 3,000 rpm but terrible at 30. At low speed I'd instead time the interval between edges with a hardware timer, which gives me fine resolution but only updates when an edge arrives. Real motor drives often use a hybrid: count edges over a window and also timestamp the last edge, so the resolution stays good at all speeds.

On the hardware side I'd use the MCU's quadrature decoder peripheral, so no CPU interrupts per edge, and condition the signals with pull-ups and a small RC or Schmitt trigger, or use differential line-driver encoders for long cables."
:::

::: terms
- **Incremental encoder**: outputs pulses relative to the starting position; needs homing ([[q0404]]).
- **Absolute encoder**: outputs a unique code at each angle; no homing.
- **Quadrature**: two signals 90° apart; order gives direction.
- **PPR / lines per revolution**: cycles of channel A per revolution.
- **CPR**: counts per revolution after decoding (4x PPR in 4x mode).
- **Index pulse (Z)**: once-per-revolution marker.
- **Line driver**: differential output (A, /A) for noise immunity over long cables.
- **M/T method**: combined count-and-time speed measurement ([[q0403]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using only channel A.** You lose direction and 3/4 of the resolution. Use both channels and both edges.
- **Confusing PPR with CPR.** The 4x mode multiplies by four. Getting this wrong yields a 4x speed error.
- **Differentiating counts over a short window at low speed.** A single count dominates, producing a staircase speed signal. Lengthen the window, time the edges or add a filter.
- **Handling every edge in a software interrupt at high speed.** At 200 kHz edge rates the CPU burns cycles and misses counts. Use a hardware decoder.
- **Ignoring noise and bounce.** A glitch on one channel makes a spurious count that accumulates as position error. Filter, use differential signalling and check for illegal state transitions.
- **Forgetting the index when homing.** The index only identifies one angle per turn, not which turn.
:::

::: deep
## What the encoder actually is

An **optical incremental encoder** is a disc with a regular pattern of opaque and transparent slots (or a reflective pattern) between an LED and a pair of photodetectors. As the disc turns, each detector sees light alternately pass and be blocked, giving a square wave after a comparator. The number of dark/light pairs per turn is the **lines per revolution** (LPR or PPR). A **magnetic** encoder does the same with a multipole ring magnet and Hall or magnetoresistive sensors: more rugged, less precise.

Two detectors are placed so their outputs are offset by a quarter of a cycle, which is the **quadrature** relationship. That makes the two signals 90° out of phase, and every full cycle splits into four distinct states.

```timing
{"signals":[{"name":"A","wave":"0.1.0.1.0.1.0.1.0.","color":"orange"},{"name":"B","wave":"00.1.0.1.0.1.0.1.0","color":"accent"}],"hscale":1.0}
```
Figure: Forward rotation: A rises first, then B rises a quarter cycle later. Each of the four edges per cycle is a count, so one line equals four counts. If the shaft reverses, B leads A and the same state machine counts down.

**State machine.** With state $(A,B)$ and A leading as in the figure, forward goes 00 → 10 → 11 → 01 → 00 (which physical direction counts as "forward" depends on wiring). Any transition that skips a state (00 → 11) is invalid and signals an error or a missed edge. Hardware decoders count up or down accordingly; software decoders use a 16-entry lookup indexed by the previous and current $(A,B)$ pairs.

| prev AB | new AB | action |
|---|---|---|
| 00 | 10 | +1 |
| 10 | 11 | +1 |
| 11 | 01 | +1 |
| 01 | 00 | +1 |
| 10 | 00 | −1 |
| 11 | 10 | −1 |
| 01 | 11 | −1 |
| 00 | 01 | −1 |
| same | same | 0 |
| 00↔11, 01↔10 | | error (missed edge) |

## From counts to speed

Take $P = 1024$ lines, 4x decoding, so CPR = 4,096. The motor runs at 3,000 rpm = 50 rev/s.

- Channel A frequency: $50\times1024 = 51.2$ kHz.
- Decoded counts: $50\times4096 = 204{,}800$ counts/s.
- In a 1 ms control window: 204.8 counts; resolution 1 count = $60/(4096\times0.001) = 14.65$ rpm (0.49% of 3,000 rpm).

At 30 rpm the same window sees only 2.05 counts, so the measured speed hops among 0, 14.6, 29.3, 43.9 rpm. That is the staircase problem. Two fixes:

1. **Longer window** (e.g. 10 ms): 1.46 rpm per count, but 10 ms of delay inside the loop.
2. **Period measurement**: at 30 rpm the edge interval is $60/(30\times4096) = 0.488$ ms. A 1 MHz timer gives 488 ticks, so 0.2% resolution. The speed estimate $\omega = 2\pi/(\text{CPR}\,T_e)$ updates at each edge (every 0.49 ms), which is faster than the 10 ms window.

Figure: Count-based speed resolution is inversely proportional to the window. Fast loops (short windows) pay in resolution, which is why precise low-speed control needs edge timing or a high-CPR encoder.

**M/T method in practice.** Latch both the encoder count and a high-speed timer value at the same instant each control period (hardware does this with a capture register). Speed = $\Delta\text{counts}$ divided by $\Delta\text{timestamp}$ of the *last edge in each window*. The window effectively lasts a whole number of counts, so resolution is about one timer tick regardless of speed. See [[q0403]].

Keep the RC corner well above the maximum signal frequency, but low enough to reject noise. The cut-off frequency is $1/(2\pi R_sC_f) = 159$ kHz.

For cables longer than tens of centimetres in noisy environments, use a **differential line driver** (A, /A, B, /B) and a differential receiver; common-mode noise cancels. Add an illegal-transition counter in firmware as a health check.

**Hardware decoders.** Most motor-control MCUs have a timer that can be configured as a quadrature counter: it does the 4x state machine in hardware, up/down counts and, if you wish, resets on the index pulse. The software only needs to read the counter each control period. Do not rely on a pin-change interrupt per edge for fast shafts.

## Index pulse and homing

The index (Z) pulse is one pulse per revolution at a fixed angle. At start-up, rotate slowly until Z fires, then zero the counter. For a multi-turn mechanism you also need a limit switch to identify the turn. Absolute encoders replace this procedure with a code.

## Absolute encoders and resolution choice

An absolute encoder has several concentric tracks (Gray code) or a serial protocol (SSI, BiSS and others) giving a unique word per angle, typically 12 to 20+ bits. **Multi-turn** versions add gear-driven discs or battery-backed turn counters. They cost more, but remove homing and are not fooled by power loss during motion.

How many lines do you need? For speed control, the encoder should give at least a few counts per control period at the lowest speed you care about. For position, resolution should be several times finer than the accuracy target. Increasing lines also raises the maximum count rate, so verify that the MCU and the encoder's output stage can handle it.

::: assumptions
- The discussion is about **incremental quadrature** encoders, the usual meaning in a motor-control interview, with absolute types noted.
- Count and rate numbers assume **ideal pulses**: real edges have jitter, duty-cycle error, and line-pattern errors, which show up as speed ripple at the line frequency.
- Maximum frequency limits differ by encoder and MCU. I flag those as vendor-dependent.
- The speed formulas assume the encoder is **rigidly coupled to the shaft** whose speed you want; compliance or a belt in between means the sensor sees something slightly different from the load.

::: followups
### How does quadrature give direction?
The A and B tracks are offset by a quarter of a cycle, so their edges occur in a fixed order. Going one way, A changes before B; going the other way, B changes before A. A state machine (or hardware counter) checks the previous and new $(A,B)$ and counts up or down. Without the second channel you only know that the shaft moved, not which way.

### An encoder has 1,000 lines. What's the speed resolution with a 2 ms window?
CPR = 4,000. One count over 2 ms is $60/(4000\times0.002) = 7.5$ rpm. At 1,500 rpm that is 0.5%. At 75 rpm it would be 10%, so use period measurement at low speed.

### You see occasional position jumps of ±1 count that accumulate. What's wrong?
Likely noise or glitches on A or B creating false counts, or a missed edge because the decoder isn't keeping up. Check the wiring, shielding and grounding, add a filter or Schmitt trigger, use a line-driver encoder, and verify the decoder is hardware-based. Add an invalid-transition counter to confirm.

### What is the difference between incremental and absolute encoders, and when do you need absolute?
Incremental encoders give relative counts and need homing; absolute encoders output a unique code per angle. Use absolute when the system can't home safely (a robot arm that cannot swing blind), when power may be lost mid-motion, or when downtime for homing is unacceptable. Multi-turn absolute adds a turn counter.

### What happens to your speed estimate if the encoder is on the motor but the load connects through a belt that stretches?
The estimate reports motor speed, not load speed. Compliance introduces a dynamic difference: during acceleration the load lags, and the loop sees the motor, so it may overshoot or oscillate at the belt resonance. If load precision matters, add a second encoder on the load and use a dual-loop scheme.
:::
