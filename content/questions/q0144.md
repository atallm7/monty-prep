::: basics
### In plain words
Inductive flyback is a voltage spike that appears when you suddenly cut the current through a coil. An inductor resists changes in its current. If you open a switch, the coil pushes a huge voltage in whatever direction keeps the current going. In a relay driver it destroys the transistor, so you add a diode to give the current a safe path. In a boost converter the same effect is used on purpose.

### Picture it
Picture a long pipe full of fast-moving water. If you slam a valve shut, the moving water has nowhere to go and produces a huge pressure spike, called water hammer. The inductor's current is the moving water, and the switch is the valve. A bypass pipe next to the valve gives the water somewhere to go, like the flyback diode.

### Words you need
- **Inductance**: how strongly a coil resists changes in current. See [[q0039]].
- **$L\,di/dt$**: the voltage across an inductor. Faster current change means bigger voltage. See [[q0041]].
- **Flyback (freewheel) diode**: a diode across the coil that gives current a path when the switch opens.
- **Relay coil**: an inductor that pulls a switch closed when current flows.
- **TVS / snubber**: parts that clamp or absorb a voltage spike.

### The simplest example
A relay coil has $L = 1$ mH and carries 0.1 A. A transistor cuts the current in 1 µs. The spike is $L\,di/dt = 10^{-3} \times 0.1 / 10^{-6} = 100$ V, from a circuit that might run on 12 V. With a diode across the coil, the current circulates through the diode and the voltage stays near 0.7 V. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds faster-release clamps and why boost and flyback converters use the same effect on purpose.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand why an inductor produces a voltage spike when its current is interrupted, and whether you can reason about it from $v=L\,di/dt$ and energy rather than memorized "put a diode across it". The interviewer wants a balanced answer to all four parts: **what** it is, **when it hurts** (relays, motors, solenoids, switch FETs), **when it is useful** (boost, flyback converters, ignition, drivers) and **how to protect** (freewheel diode, diode plus Zener, snubber, TVS), including the trade-off that a simple diode slows the release of a relay.
:::

::: why
Every board that drives a relay, motor, solenoid, valve or transformer has to deal with inductive kick, and a missing flyback diode is one of the classic ways to destroy a driver transistor or MCU pin. At the same time, the same effect is the working principle of boost, buck-boost and flyback converters. Being able to say "same physics, harmful in one place, useful in another" is exactly the understanding interviewers look for. It also feeds directly into motor driving, the switch-node waveforms of a buck ([[q0208]]) and EMI.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor law**: $v_L=L\,di/dt$. An inductor resists *changes* in current. See [[q0039]] and [[q0041]].
- **Current continuity**: inductor current cannot change instantly, so if the switch opens the inductor forces current by raising its voltage. See [[q0043]].
- **Energy storage**: $E=\tfrac12LI^2$, the energy that has to go somewhere when the switch opens.
- **Diode behaviour**: forward-biased diode clamps voltage to $V_f$, giving the current a path. See [[q0072]].
- **Parasitic capacitance**: with no path, the energy rings into the switch and wiring capacitance, setting the spike height.
- **Avalanche / breakdown**: a FET or BJT that sees more than its $V_{DS}$ rating may avalanche and be damaged.
:::

::: answer
"Inductive flyback, or inductive kick, is the voltage spike you get when you suddenly interrupt current through an inductor. An inductor's voltage is $L\,di/dt$, so if I open a switch and the current tries to drop to zero in nanoseconds, the inductor generates a big voltage, with whatever polarity it needs, to keep the current flowing. The energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ has to go somewhere. If there's no path, it charges the stray capacitance of the switch and wiring, and the voltage can reach kilovolts, which blows up a transistor or arcs a relay contact.

It's unwanted when I'm driving a relay, a motor or a solenoid from a transistor or microcontroller pin. The usual fix is a flyback or freewheel diode across the coil, reverse-biased in normal operation, so when the switch opens the current circulates through the diode and decays safely. If I need faster release, I use a diode in series with a Zener or a TVS, which allows a higher clamp voltage and a faster decay, or an RC snubber.

It's wanted in switching converters. A boost converter and a flyback converter deliberately interrupt inductor current so that the inductor's voltage adds to the input and steps the voltage up. Ignition coils and camera flash circuits work the same way. So it's the same physics: in one case I clamp it, in the other I use it."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism.** $v_L=L\,\dfrac{di}{dt}$. If a switch interrupts $I_0$ in time $t_f$, the induced voltage magnitude is about $L I_0/t_f$, opposite in polarity to the applied voltage (for a low-side switch the node goes *above* the supply).

**Energy.** Stored energy $E=\tfrac12LI_0^2$. With no clamp, this energy rings into the total parasitic capacitance $C_p$ at the node. Equating $\tfrac12LI_0^2=\tfrac12C_pV_{pk}^2$ gives
$$V_{pk}=I_0\sqrt{L/C_p}$$
Example: $L=10$ mH, $I_0=1$ A, $C_p=100$ pF gives $V_{pk}=10$ kV (in practice limited by arcing, avalanche, or the dielectric breakdown of the switch).

**Protection options** (low-side switch, coil from supply $V_S$ to switch node):

| Method | Clamp / behaviour | Decay time | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Freewheel diode across coil | Coil voltage $\approx -V_f$, switch sees $V_S+V_f$ | Slowest | Simple, cheap; relay release is delayed |
| Diode + Zener in series | Coil voltage $-(V_Z+V_f)$ | Faster | Switch sees $V_S+V_Z+V_f$; choose below switch rating |
| TVS across switch | Clamps switch to $V_{clamp}$ | Fast | Bidirectional or unidirectional; check peak pulse power |
| RC snubber | Absorbs energy in R, limits $dv/dt$ | Fast | Sized for ringing; dissipates about $CV^2f$ in repetitive use |
| Resistor across coil | $V=I_0R$ | $\tau=L/R$ | Wastes power continuously |
| Active clamp / synchronous | MOSFET clamp, or body diode path | Controlled | Used in drivers and motor bridges |

**Decay time.** For a coil with series resistance $R_c$ and a clamp of constant voltage $V_c$ opposing the current, $t=\dfrac{L}{R_c}\ln\!\left(1+\dfrac{I_0R_c}{V_c}\right)$. For $L=10$ mH, $R_c=12$ Ω, $I_0=1$ A: $\tau=0.83$ ms, diode ($V_c=0.7$ V) gives 2.4 ms, a 24 V Zener plus diode ($V_c=24.7$ V) gives 0.33 ms.

**Where it is wanted.** In boost, buck-boost and flyback converters, the inductor current is interrupted every cycle and the inductor's voltage is used to transfer energy to the output; a flyback converter stores $\tfrac12L_pI_{pk}^2$ per cycle in its coupled inductor, giving $P=\tfrac12L_pI_{pk}^2f_{sw}$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Real spike height depends on parasitic capacitance, wiring inductance, how fast the switch opens, and avalanche behaviour of the switch, so the kV figure for the unprotected case is a bound, not a prediction. Test with a scope and a proper probe.
:::

::: long
"Inductive flyback is what happens when you interrupt the current through an inductor. The inductor voltage is $L\,di/dt$, so if a switch tries to cut a current of one amp in a few nanoseconds, the inductor produces whatever voltage is necessary to keep that current flowing, with the polarity reversed relative to what it saw while charging. The stored energy is half L I squared, and it has to go somewhere. With no path, it rings into the stray capacitance of the switch and wiring, and the peak is $I\sqrt{L/C}$. For a 10 millihenry coil at 1 amp with 100 picofarads that's a theoretical 10 kilovolts, so in reality something breaks down: the FET avalanches, a relay contact arcs.

It's unwanted when I'm driving a relay, solenoid, motor or any coil from a transistor or MCU pin. The standard protection is a freewheel diode across the coil, reverse-biased in normal operation. When the switch opens, the current finds a path through the diode and decays through the coil's own resistance. The switch only sees the supply plus a diode drop.

There's a catch: with a plain diode the coil voltage is stuck at about minus 0.7 volts, so the current decays slowly and a relay may release late. If I need faster release I use a Zener in series with the diode, a TVS across the switch, or an RC snubber, and pick the clamp voltage below the switch's rating with margin.

It's wanted in switch-mode converters. In a boost converter, I charge the inductor, then open the switch, and the inductor voltage adds to the input to push current into a higher-voltage output. A flyback converter stores energy in a coupled inductor and releases it on the secondary. Ignition coils and camera flashes work the same way. So the physics is identical. It's about whether I give the energy a controlled place to go."
:::

::: terms
- **Flyback (inductive kick)**: the voltage spike generated when inductor current is interrupted.
- **Freewheel (flyback) diode**: a diode across an inductive load that conducts the current after the switch opens.
- **Continuity condition**: an inductor's current cannot change instantaneously. See [[q0043]].
- **Clamp voltage**: the voltage at which a protection device limits the spike (diode $V_f$, Zener $V_Z$, TVS $V_{clamp}$).
- **Snubber**: an RC (or RCD) network that absorbs energy or slows a voltage edge.
- **TVS**: transient voltage suppressor, a diode designed to absorb short high-energy pulses.
- **Avalanche rating**: the energy a MOSFET can absorb in breakdown without damage ($E_{AS}$).
- **Flyback converter**: an isolated converter that stores energy in a coupled inductor and delivers it when the switch turns off.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a voltage spike when you turn off an inductor, so you put a diode across it."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives it from $v=L\,di/dt$ and energy $\tfrac12LI^2$, and estimates the unclamped peak as $I\sqrt{L/C_p}$.
- States the **polarity** (the node swings above the supply for a low-side switch) and where the current flows after the diode conducts.
- Gives the diode's **drawback** (slow release, the coil sees only $-V_f$) and the Zener/TVS/snubber alternatives with the clamp rule: $V_S+V_{clamp}$ below the switch rating.
- Names the **useful** side: boost, flyback converters, ignition and flash circuits.
- Mentions practical details: diode current rating at least the coil current, fast recovery in switching use, diode placed physically at the coil, and PWM motor drives where the diode carries current every cycle.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the diode in the wrong direction.** It must be reverse-biased during normal operation (cathode to the positive supply side for a low-side switch). Wrong orientation is a short circuit.
- **Believing a diode eliminates the spike energy.** The energy is still there. It is dissipated in the coil's resistance and the diode as the current decays.
- **Forgetting the release delay.** With a plain diode a relay holds its contacts for several milliseconds longer. Add a Zener or resistor in series if release timing matters.
- **Undersizing the clamp.** A Zener clamp of 24 V on a 12 V supply puts $12+24+0.7=36.7$ V on the switch; if it is a 30 V FET it will avalanche.
- **Saying "back EMF" and "flyback" are identical.** Back-EMF in a motor is generated by rotation; flyback is from interrupted current. Both involve inductance and motors have both ([[q0341]]).
- **Ignoring the body diode in a half-bridge.** In a motor driver, the other FET's body diode, or an external one, provides the freewheel path; the spike is clamped there.
:::

::: deep
## Why the voltage spikes

The inductor equation $v_L=L\,di/dt$ says the voltage across the coil is whatever it needs to be to make the current change at the rate the circuit demands. While the switch is on and the current is steady (or ramping slowly), $v_L$ is small. When the switch opens, the circuit demands $di/dt$ of $-I_0/t_{off}$: huge if $t_{off}$ is nanoseconds. The inductor produces a correspondingly large voltage with polarity opposing the *decrease* of current, so the end of the coil connected to the switch swings *positive* relative to the supply for a low-side switch.

## The energy view and the unclamped spike

Energy stored: $E=\tfrac12LI_0^2$. For a relay coil with $L=0.5$ H and $I_0=30$ mA, $E=0.225$ mJ. For a motor or solenoid with $L=10$ mH and $I_0=1$ A, $E=5$ mJ. The switch node also has capacitance $C_p$ (FET $C_{oss}$, wiring, the coil's own winding capacitance). If nothing else absorbs the energy, it oscillates between $L$ and $C_p$, and $\tfrac12LI_0^2=\tfrac12C_pV_{pk}^2$ gives

$$V_{pk}=I_0\sqrt{\frac{L}{C_p}}$$

With $I_0=1$ A, $L=10$ mH and $C_p=100$ pF, $V_{pk}=10$ kV. Something has to give: the transistor avalanches (above its $V_{DS}$ rating it conducts and dissipates the energy, which may be fine within its avalanche energy rating $E_{AS}$, or destructive), the wiring arcs, or a relay contact sparks. The 10 kV is a bound, not a prediction.

## Protection and trade-offs

**Freewheel diode.** With the diode across the coil, the coil voltage is clamped to about $-0.7$ V. The current decays as $i(t)$ with a time constant $L/R_c$ set by the coil's own resistance, plus the small diode drop. The switch only sees $V_S+0.7$ V. Cheap and robust. Downside: slow decay. A relay with only a diode releases noticeably later than without one, which matters for timing and contact wear.

**Diode + Zener.** A Zener in series with the diode (back-to-back) allows the coil voltage to rise to $V_Z+V_f$ before conducting, so the current decays faster. The switch sees $V_S+V_Z+V_f$.

**TVS across the switch.** A TVS diode (or an MOV in AC) clamps the node to its clamping voltage without a series diode. It also protects against supply-side transients. Check peak pulse power: energy $\tfrac12LI_0^2$ over the clamp time.

**RC snubber.** A resistor and capacitor in series across the switch (or coil) slows the $dv/dt$ and damps ringing. In repetitive switching, the snubber resistor dissipates roughly $CV^2f$ (the capacitor is charged and discharged every cycle).

**Active clamp / bridge.** In an H-bridge or half-bridge, the body diodes (or synchronous FETs) provide the freewheel path automatically. That is why motor drivers have diodes: see [[q0208]] and the H-bridge pages.

## When flyback is wanted

The same physics is how most step-up converters work. In a **boost converter** the switch charges the inductor from $V_{in}$; when the switch opens, the inductor current must continue, so its voltage reverses and adds to $V_{in}$ until the diode conducts into the output capacitor. The output can therefore exceed the input. In a **flyback converter** the coupled inductor stores energy on the primary while the switch is on and releases it through the secondary when the switch turns off, so power per cycle is $\tfrac12L_pI_{pk}^2$ and average power $P=\tfrac12L_pI_{pk}^2f_{sw}$. For $L_p=100$ µH, $I_{pk}=1$ A, $f_{sw}=100$ kHz this is 5 W. Ignition coils, camera flash chargers, piezo drivers and Nixie/LCD backlight supplies use the same idea. See [[q0276]] for what happens to the boost inductor's flyback voltage.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> When asked "when is it wanted?", name a boost or flyback converter and say the key sentence: "The inductor is deliberately forced to dump its energy into a higher-voltage node." That signals you see the energy transfer and not only the hazard.

## Real-hardware details

- **Diode choice:** current rating at least the coil current (it conducts the full $I_0$ at the start), reverse voltage above the supply plus margin. For repetitive PWM drives use a fast or Schottky diode.
- **Placement:** put the diode directly across the coil terminals; a long loop adds inductance and lets the fast edge radiate or spike before the diode takes over.
- **MOSFET body diode:** it protects against *negative* excursions, not against the positive spike at the drain of a low-side FET.
- **Avalanche energy:** a MOSFET specified for single-pulse avalanche energy $E_{AS}$ can absorb a one-off spike, but it is not a design choice for repetitive operation.
- **AC loads:** relays and contactors on AC use RC snubbers or MOVs, since a diode would conduct on the wrong half cycle.
:::

::: applications
- **Relay and solenoid drivers**: a flyback diode (or diode plus Zener) across every coil, as on typical relay driver ICs and ULN2003-type arrays that include clamp diodes.
- **Brushed motor H-bridges**: the body diodes of the half-bridge FETs provide the freewheel path during PWM and braking.
- **Boost converters and LED backlight drivers**: deliberately interrupt inductor current to raise the voltage.
- **Flyback power supplies**: phone chargers and small isolated AC-DC supplies use the stored energy of a coupled inductor, plus an RCD or TVS clamp for leakage-inductance spikes.
- **Ignition systems and camera flashes**: a coil is switched to produce a high-voltage pulse.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Low-side switch with the coil to the supply** is the example. For a high-side switch, the node swings *below* ground and the diode goes from ground to the node.
- **Ideal inductor with linear $L$.** If the core saturates, inductance falls and the effective $di/dt$ and spike change.
- **Single event.** For repetitive switching (PWM) the average power in clamps and snubbers matters, not only the peak.
- **Parasitic capacitance values are estimates**, so the unclamped peak is an order-of-magnitude bound.
- **Alternative valid answers.** Many people call this "inductive kick", "back-EMF spike" or "flyback voltage". The diode is called freewheeling, flyback, catch or snubber diode depending on context; they mean the same part in this application.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the voltage spike rise above the supply, not below?
For a low-side switch the coil's current flows from the supply, through the coil, into the switch. When the switch opens, the current must keep flowing in the same direction, so the coil end connected to the switch has to become more positive than the end connected to the supply to push current onward, hence the node rises above $V_S$. For a high-side switch, the node swings below ground.

### Why does a flyback diode make a relay release slower, and how do you fix it?
With the diode, the coil voltage is clamped near $-0.7$ V, so the current decays with $\tau=L/R_c$, taking milliseconds. A relay releases only when the coil current falls below the drop-out value. Adding a Zener in series with the diode (say 24 V) allows a larger reverse voltage across the coil so the current falls faster, as long as the switch can handle $V_S+V_Z+V_f$.

### Size the clamp for a 12 V relay coil of 400 Ω driven by a transistor rated at 40 V.
Coil current is $12/400=30$ mA. The switch must stay below 40 V with margin, so a 24 V Zener would put $12+24+0.7=36.7$ V on the transistor, which leaves little margin. An 18 V Zener gives $12+18+0.7=30.7$ V, a safer margin, and still releases much faster than a plain diode.

### In a buck converter, where is the "flyback" diode?
It is the freewheel (catch) diode, or the synchronous low-side FET, that carries the inductor current when the high-side switch turns off. The inductor current continues, the switch node goes to about $-V_f$, and that is the off state. See [[q0197]] and [[q0208]].

### What if the diode is rated for much less current than the coil current?
It will see the full $I_0$ at the instant of switch-off and may overheat or fail open or short. It needs a repetitive/peak forward current rating of at least $I_0$, and for repetitive PWM a power dissipation check: $P\approx V_fI_{avg}$.

### Scenario: a MOSFET driving a solenoid fails after a few thousand operations even though there is a diode. What do you check?
Check diode orientation, peak current rating and placement (long wires add inductance in the loop); confirm with a scope at the drain. A TVS could also be needed for supply-borne spikes. Also check whether the FET's $V_{DS}$ margin is enough once the supply tolerance and clamp voltage are included, and whether the gate is driven fast enough to avoid linear-mode dissipation.
:::
