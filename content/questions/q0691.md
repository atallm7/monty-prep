::: testing
Whether you can connect a waveform detail to a **system-level failure**. The interviewer wants to hear that a receiver treats each crossing of its threshold as an event, so a non-monotonic edge on a clock, reset, enable, interrupt or counter input becomes extra events: double-clocking, spurious counts, false resets, metastability. They also want the nuance: data lines sampled after they settle tolerate ringing, edge-triggered inputs do not. Mentioning hysteresis as a mitigation, and the cost in timing margin, shows depth.
:::

::: why
Non-monotonic edges are an unusually nasty class of bug: the logic is correct, the supply is fine, and yet a counter occasionally increments twice or a state machine jumps. The failure depends on temperature, trace length, driver strength and even probe position, so it appears "random". Knowing *why* it breaks lets you recognise the symptoms and also decide where ringing can be tolerated, which saves board area and cost on lines where termination isn't needed.
:::

::: concepts
- **Edge-triggered inputs**: flip-flops, counters, interrupt lines and clocks respond to *each* threshold crossing. See [[q0887]] and [[q0693]].
- **Monotonic edge**: single directional pass through the threshold window. See [[q0690]].
- **Hysteresis**: a Schmitt input ignores ringback smaller than its window. See [[q0692]].
- **Reflections and ringing**: the main source of non-monotonic edges. See [[q0696]] and [[q0711]].
- **Setup/hold**: an edge that changes late can violate a downstream flop's window. See [[q0693]].
:::

::: answer
"It matters because digital inputs react to threshold crossings, and a non-monotonic edge crosses the threshold more than once. On a clock or any edge-triggered input, that's extra edges: a counter counts twice, a flip-flop captures at the wrong time, a state machine advances when it shouldn't. On reset or interrupt pins it can cause spurious resets or interrupts. These bugs are painful because they only show up at certain temperatures, voltages or trace lengths, and the logic itself looks perfect.

Even if the edge doesn't actually double-trigger, a ringback that dips into the threshold window moves the crossing time around, so you lose setup and hold margin. And receivers don't guarantee anything between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$, so different chips might disagree.

Data lines that are sampled after they've settled can tolerate some ringing, but clocks, resets and strobes can't. I'd fix it at the source with termination and clean routing, and where I can't, I'd use a Schmitt-trigger input to give the receiver some hysteresis."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism.** A receiver input stage (or the first inverter of a flip-flop's clock path) is a high-gain comparator with a threshold $V_{TH}$ near $V_{DD}/2$ for CMOS, or an $V_{IL}$/$V_{IH}$ window for a guaranteed level. Each time $v_{in}(t)$ crosses $V_{TH}$, the internal node flips. A clock edge that rises, falls back below $V_{TH}$ and rises again produces three internal transitions: a rising edge, a falling edge, and a *second* rising edge. Edge-triggered logic registers the second rising edge as a new clock event.

**Consequences.**
1. *Double clocking*: counters, shift registers, FIFOs and state machines advance twice.
2. *Spurious resets/interrupts*: asynchronous inputs react immediately to any crossing; a 1 ns glitch can reset a microcontroller.
3. *Timing margin loss*: the crossing time can move by the ringback duration (nanoseconds), consuming setup/hold budget ([[q0693]]).
4. *Metastability*: a ringback crossing that coincides with another flop's capture window can leave it in an undefined state.
5. *Undefined-window behaviour*: between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ different devices (and the same device at different temperatures) read different values.

**Where it is tolerable.** Data signals sampled by a clock after they settle only need to be monotonic-free *near the sampling instant* (setup/hold window), so ringing that has decayed by then is fine. That is why termination is judged by clock, strobe, reset and control lines first.

**Quantifying margin.** The ringback must not re-enter the threshold. Without hysteresis, the margin is $V_{ringback,min} - V_{TH}$ for a rising edge; with a Schmitt input of lower threshold $V_{T-}$ it is $V_{ringback,min} - V_{T-}$. In the example below, a ring-back minimum of 1.55 V gives $-0.10$ V margin against a 1.65 V threshold (fails) but $+0.25$ V against $V_{T-} = 1.3$ V (passes).
:::

::: short
"Receivers react to every threshold crossing, so a non-monotonic edge shows up as extra edges. On a clock or counter input that means double-clocking; on reset or interrupt lines, spurious events. It also moves the crossing time and eats setup and hold margin, and in the threshold window different parts may disagree. Data that's sampled after it settles can tolerate it, but clocks and control lines can't. I fix it with termination and layout, or with a Schmitt input."
:::

::: long
"Monotonicity matters because digital logic doesn't look at a waveform, it looks at threshold crossings. If an edge rises, rings back below the threshold, and rises again, then the receiver sees three transitions instead of one: up, down, up.

For an edge-triggered input, like a clock, a counter input, or an interrupt, the second rising edge is a brand new event. A counter counts twice, a shift register shifts twice, a state machine moves two states. If the ringing is on a reset or interrupt line, you get a spurious reset or interrupt. The nasty part is that the logic is right, and the failure depends on trace length, driver strength and temperature. It looks random.

Even without a real double edge, the ringback that dips toward the threshold shifts the time the receiver switches. That uses up setup and hold margin. And between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ the receiver's decision isn't guaranteed, so two chips on the same net can disagree about the value.

Where is it tolerable? On a data line that's only sampled after it has settled, you can accept some ringing because only the signal near the sampling window matters. That's why the first nets I clean up are clocks, strobes, resets and enables.

The fixes go in this order. First, remove the cause with proper source termination and short stubs. Second, if I can't, use a receiver with hysteresis such as a Schmitt trigger, so the ringback has to be bigger than the hysteresis window before it counts. Slowing the edge is the last resort because it costs timing."
:::

::: terms
- **Double clocking**: a single intended clock edge registered as two because the edge crossed the threshold twice.
- **Ringback**: voltage returning toward the starting level after overshoot; the part that threatens the threshold.
- **Edge-triggered input**: an input that acts on a transition rather than a level (clock, interrupt, counter).
- **Hysteresis**: separation between the rising and falling thresholds of a Schmitt-trigger input.
- **Threshold window ($V_{IL}$–$V_{IH}$)**: the range where logic level is not guaranteed.
- **Runt pulse**: a pulse that doesn't reach a valid logic level, often created by ringing or crosstalk.
- **Metastability**: an undefined flip-flop output after a setup/hold violation.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "non-monotonic edges can cause false triggering". An exceptional answer explains **why** (receivers act on every crossing) and **where it hurts** (clock, reset, interrupt, strobe; edge-triggered, asynchronous inputs) versus **where it doesn't** (data sampled after settling). It quantifies **ringback margin** against the threshold (with and without hysteresis), mentions timing-margin loss and the undefined window between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$, lists mitigations in the right order (remove the cause with termination, then hysteresis, then slow the edge), and notes the debug pattern: intermittent, temperature- and pattern-dependent.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Ringing only matters if it exceeds the supply."** Overshoot is a reliability issue; ringback toward the threshold is the functional one.
- **Assuming every net needs perfect monotonicity.** Settled data lines don't; clocks and async controls do. Over-terminating everything wastes power and area.
- **Looking only at the settled level.** The final value can be perfect while the edge was non-monotonic.
- **Believing the receiver has one exact threshold.** Real thresholds shift with temperature, supply and process; the safe design keeps ringback outside $V_{IL}$–$V_{IH}$.
- **Applying hysteresis as a cure-all.** It tolerates ringback smaller than the window and adds delay variation; it can't fix a ring larger than its width.
- **Debugging with a probe that adds ringing.** Check the effect on the *function* (counter value, captured data) not just the scope picture.
:::

::: deep
## What the receiver actually sees

Take a clock net driven by a strong output into an unterminated line. The waveform at the receiver is a damped ring around the final value. Model it as the step response of an underdamped second-order system with damping ratio $\zeta = 0.1$ and a 250 MHz ring frequency; with $V_{DD} = 3.3$ V:

$$v(t) = V_{DD}\left[1 - e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\left(\cos\omega_d t + \frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\sin\omega_d t\right)\right]$$

The extremes of this ring alternate above and below $V_{DD}$ by $V_{DD}\cdot OS\cdot e^{-\pi k\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$, where $OS = e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} = 0.729$ for $\zeta = 0.1$. The first dip below the final value is $3.3\cdot 0.729^2 = 1.75$ V below it: the signal dips to $3.3 - 1.75 = 1.55$ V.

## Cost to timing, even without a double clock

Suppose the first ringback dips to just *above* the threshold. There's no extra edge, but the signal spends time near $V_{TH}$ at slow slope: the crossing is uncertain by the noise-over-slope relation, $\sigma_t = \sigma_v/SR$ ([[q0689]]), and the effective setup/hold windows widen. A second subtlety is that the *first* crossing might be at the fast part of the edge while a later crossing is at the slow part; changing temperature or driver strength shifts which one the receiver latches.

## Why guaranteed thresholds matter

A receiver guarantees "1" only above $V_{IH}$ and "0" only below $V_{IL}$. Suppose a driver's ringback dips to 1.83 V on a LVTTL line. That's above the 1.65 V mid-supply point where a typical CMOS gate switches, so one chip may remain high; another with a slightly higher threshold (still within its $V_{IL}$–$V_{IH}$ spec of 0.8–2.0 V) sees a low. The net is fine on the bench and fails on the second batch of chips. That is why standards specify ringback relative to $V_{IH}$/$V_{IL}$, not the typical threshold.

## The other fix: filter first, then Schmitt

Sometimes you cannot change the source, for example a rotary encoder on a cable or a button wired to a microcontroller. The practical fix is an RC low-pass followed by a Schmitt-trigger input. With $R = 1$ kΩ and $C = 10$ nF the time constant is 10 µs and the corner is 15.9 kHz. A ring at 250 MHz is attenuated by about $1/(2\pi f RC) = 6.4\times10^{-5}$, so a 5 V ring becomes 0.3 mV at the Schmitt input, far below any hysteresis window. The cost is delay: the filtered edge reaches a threshold near 0.69 RC = 6.9 µs after the true edge, so this only suits signals that are slow compared with 10 µs, such as buttons, switches and low-speed encoders. For a 1 MHz clock the same filter would erase the signal, which is why clocks are fixed at the source with termination instead.

## What the timing analysis does not see

Static timing analysis assumes each net transitions once per cycle. It therefore cannot detect a ringback that produces an extra edge. The only places this is caught are a signal-integrity simulation and a bench measurement of the receiver pin. That is why SI checklists include a "ringback below threshold" test on every clock, strobe, reset and enable net, and why the debug recipe above begins with measuring at the receiver rather than reading the timing report.

## Where to apply what

| Net type | Tolerates non-monotonic edges? | Typical action |
|---|---|---|
| Clock, strobe, DQS | No | terminate, controlled impedance, simulate ringback |
| Reset, interrupt, enable | No | filter + Schmitt input or termination |
| Counter / encoder / button inputs | No | RC filter + Schmitt trigger, debounce |
| Synchronous data lines sampled mid-bit | Yes, if settled before setup | check setup/hold, light termination |
| Slow analog-to-digital outputs | Usually | none |

## Debug recipe

1. Reproduce the failure and record the count or captured data.
2. Probe the receiver pin (short ground spring) with the edge triggered on the *clock* and zoom into the threshold window.
3. Compare ringback minimum against $V_{TH}$ and against $V_{IL}$/$V_{IH}$, at the worst corner you can create (warm, low supply).
4. Fix the source: series resistor at the driver, shorten stubs. Re-run the count test over temperature.
5. If a layout change isn't possible, add hysteresis or an RC filter into a Schmitt input, but re-check timing.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked "have you seen this?", a good honest structure is: symptom (counter off by one at hot), measurement (zoomed scope at the receiver showing the dip), cause (source impedance versus line), fix (33 Ω series), verification (count test, temperature sweep). Use your own project's numbers.
:::

::: applications
- **Quadrature encoder and tachometer inputs**: extra edges give wrong counts; encoder lines use Schmitt inputs and filtering ([[q0690]] sets the criterion).
- **Memory strobes (DQS/CK) in DDR**: ringback into the threshold causes capture errors; ringback limits are in the spec.
- **Mechanical switches and relays**: bounce and ringing are made monotonic by RC and Schmitt-trigger debounce.
- **Reset supervisors**: a clean monotonic reset release prevents a microcontroller from booting in an undefined state.
- **Clock gating and PLL reference inputs**: glitches on the reference cause cycle slips.
- **Long ribbon cables**: series termination at the driver cleans up the clock edge for the receiving board.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Receiver with a single static threshold** (typical CMOS) for the main example; Schmitt receivers have two thresholds and different margins.
- **Idealised second-order ringing** for the numbers; a real ring has more modes (reflections, parasitics) and clamp diodes that cut the overshoot.
- **Ringing near the threshold only matters at edge-triggered or asynchronous inputs**; for data sampled after settling the relevant constraint is setup/hold.
- **Thresholds are typical LVTTL numbers** (0.8 V and 2.0 V); other families differ. Check the datasheet.
- The word "monotonic" here refers to edge shape; DAC/ADC monotonicity is a separate converter property.
:::

::: followups
### A counter on a rotary encoder over-counts only when the cable is long. What do you suspect and test?
Ringing or crosstalk on the encoder lines at the receiver producing extra threshold crossings. I'd scope the receiver pin, zoom on the threshold window, and look for ringback or a notch. Fixes: series resistor at the encoder driver or RC filter into a Schmitt-trigger input, and shielded or twisted pair cable.

### Why does a Schmitt trigger help, and what does it cost?
The output only flips once the input crosses the upper threshold on the way up, and does not flip back until it crosses the lower threshold, so ringback smaller than the window is ignored. Cost: switching thresholds are less precise and the delay varies with the edge; and it doesn't help if the ringing is larger than the window ([[q0692]]).

### Can a perfectly monotonic edge still fail setup/hold?
Yes. Monotonic only says the receiver crosses the threshold once. If the edge is slow, late or has a long ledge near the threshold the crossing time can still miss the capture window. Monotonicity is necessary for clean edge-triggered operation but doesn't guarantee timing ([[q0693]]).

### What if the ring-back lands in the threshold window only at low temperature, never at room temperature?
Driver output impedance falls at low temperature (stronger driver), so $\Gamma_s$ becomes more negative and the ring gets bigger. This is a classic temperature-dependent failure; verify ringback margin over PVT corners, not just at 25°C.

### How would you decide whether a data line needs termination?
Check the settling time: if the reflections and ringing decay to within noise margin before the setup window of the capturing flop, it's fine. Otherwise, add series termination. I'd simulate with IBIS models or probe at the receiver across temperature. For clocks I'd always terminate.
:::

::: interpretations
In the converter world, "monotonicity" is a DAC or ADC specification (output doesn't reverse as the input code increases), important for control loops (a non-monotonic DAC can make a feedback loop hunt). In this signal-integrity section, the question is about **edge shape on a digital line**, so that's what I've answered. If asked, I'd mention the converter version as a one-line contrast.
:::
