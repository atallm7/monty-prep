::: testing
Whether you understand **what actually limits the current of one buck stage** and how splitting the load removes those limits. The interviewer wants:

1. The summation: $I_{out}=\sum I_{L,k}$, each phase carrying about $I_{out}/N$.
2. The limits being relieved: FET conduction and thermal, inductor saturation and heating, package and copper.
3. Honest derating: imbalance, ripple on top of DC, temperature and the loss of a phase.
4. A calculation: how many amps you really get from $N$ phases.
:::

::: why
"Multiphase gives N times the current" is a half-truth that gets challenged in design reviews. A single stage is limited by several separate things, and the usable total is less than $N\times$ the datasheet rating. Understanding the limits is what lets you size inductors (saturation current includes ripple), choose power stages, and predict which phase will fail first. It is also a direct link to inductor saturation questions such as [[q0259]].
:::

::: concepts
- **KCL at the output node**: the inductor currents add, $I_{out}=\sum_k I_{L,k}$.
- **Inductor current rating**: saturation current $I_{sat}$ and heating (RMS) current $I_{rms}$ ([[q0259]]).
- **Peak inductor current**: $I_{pk}=I_{ph}+\Delta I_{ph}/2$, which must stay below $I_{sat}$.
- **FET and power stage limits**: conduction loss $I^2R$ and junction temperature.
- **Current imbalance**: the highest phase sets the limit ([[q0238]]).
- **Derating**: usable current is lower at high ambient temperature.
:::

::: answer
"Each phase has its own inductor and FETs, and all the inductor currents flow into the same output node, so by KCL the output current is the sum of the phase currents. A single stage can only carry so much, limited by the FETs' conduction loss and temperature, by the inductor's saturation and heating current, and by the package and the copper. With four phases each stage only has to carry a quarter of the load, so a 60 amp load becomes four 15 amp stages, which are parts that are easy to buy and cool.

In practice you don't get exactly N times the single-stage rating. The currents are never perfectly equal, so the most heavily loaded phase hits its limit first. If the sense error and mismatch are 5 percent, the usable total is about N times the phase limit divided by 1.05. You also have to look at the peak current: the DC current plus half the ripple has to stay below the inductor's saturation current. And you leave margin for temperature, and for what happens if one phase fails and the others pick up its share.

So a 4-phase design built from 25 amp derated stages is good for about 95 amps, and I would rate the system for somewhat less than that."
:::

::: technical
**Summation.** KCL at the output node gives $I_{out}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}I_{L,k}$. With sharing error $\varepsilon$ (largest phase is $(1+\varepsilon)$ times the average), the highest phase carries $(1+\varepsilon)I_{out}/N$, so

$$I_{out,max}=\frac{N\,I_{ph,lim}}{1+\varepsilon}$$

where $I_{ph,lim}$ is the lowest of the power-stage, inductor and thermal limits, each derated.

**Inductor limits.** The peak current is $I_{pk}=I_{ph}+\Delta I_{ph}/2$, with $\Delta I_{ph}=V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf_{sw})$. It must be below $I_{sat}$ (with a margin and over temperature, since saturation current falls when hot). The RMS current heats the winding: $P_{Cu}=I_{rms}^2R_{DCR}$, and $I_{rms}\approx\sqrt{I_{ph}^2+\Delta I_{ph}^2/12}$.

**FET and power stage.** Conduction loss $I_{ph}^2R_{eff}$ with $R_{eff}=D R_{HS}+(1-D)R_{LS}$. Losses divide among $N$ stages, so each runs cooler for a given total current.

**Worked example.** 12 V to 1 V, 300 nH per phase, 500 kHz, power stage rated 25 A derated, inductor $I_{sat}=30$ A, sharing error 5%.

- Ripple per phase 6.11 A p-p.
- Four phases: $I_{out,max}=4\cdot25/1.05=95.2$ A.
- At $I_{out}=80$ A: $I_{ph}=20$ A, worst phase 21 A, $I_{pk}=21+3.06=24.1$ A < 30 A. OK.
- If one phase fails: the other three carry $80/3=26.7$ A, over the 25 A derated limit. So full 80 A cannot be sustained with one phase lost. The design needs $N+1$ redundancy or a current-limit foldback.

**Why not just parallel FETs?** Parallel FETs share conduction loss but one inductor still carries the whole current, and its $I_{sat}$, DCR loss and size grow, with no gain in ripple frequency or slew.
:::

::: short

Each stage only needs FETs, an inductor and a package rated for its own share, so the single-stage limits go away. In practice the total is N times the derated phase limit divided by one plus the sharing error, and I'd check inductor peak current against saturation and think about what happens if one phase fails."
:::

::: long
"The mechanism is just KCL. All the phase inductors land on the same output node, so the output current is the sum of the phase currents. If I have four phases and the load is 60 amps, each phase carries around 15 amps.

Why that matters: a single buck stage has three separate limits. The FETs: conduction loss grows with I squared, and the junction temperature caps the current. The inductor: it has a saturation current, above which the inductance collapses, and a heating current set by its DC resistance. And the package and board copper. Splitting the current across N stages brings each of those down by a factor of N, so I can use standard parts.

But the total isn't simply N times the datasheet number. The currents aren't exactly equal, so whichever phase carries the most hits its limit first. If the sense and mismatch error is 5 percent, the usable total is N times the phase limit divided by 1.05. With four 25 amp derated stages, that's about 95 amps.

I'd also check the inductor peak current, which is the DC current plus half the ripple, against its saturation current, including the drop with temperature. And I'd check what happens if one phase fails: the remaining ones carry N over N minus 1 times more. In my example three phases would carry 26.7 amps each at 80 amps load, which is over the limit, so I'd either add a spare phase or have the controller fold back the current.

So multiphase increases output current by letting me build it from several moderate stages, with margin for imbalance, ripple and failure."
:::

::: terms
- **KCL (Kirchhoff's current law)**: currents entering a node sum to zero, so the phase currents add at the output.
- **Saturation current ($I_{sat}$)**: the inductor current at which inductance falls by a stated percentage (often 20 to 30%, vendor-defined).
- **Heating (RMS) current**: the current that raises the inductor temperature by a stated amount.
- **DCR**: the inductor's winding DC resistance.
- **Derating**: reducing the rated current for temperature and margin.
- **Sharing error**: how much above average the worst phase runs.
- **N+1 redundancy**: one more phase than needed so that a failure is survivable.
- **Foldback**: reducing the current limit under a fault condition.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "each phase supplies part of the current so you get N times more".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Cites KCL at the output node and names the three limits that disappear: FETs, inductor and package/thermal.
- Gives the derated formula $N I_{ph,lim}/(1+\varepsilon)$ and a number.
- Calculates the inductor peak current (DC + half ripple) against saturation.
- Discusses what happens if a phase fails.
- Contrasts it with paralleling FETs.
- Notes that the controller's per-phase current limit protects the weakest phase.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"N phases = N times the rating."** Ignores imbalance, derating and ripple.
- **Comparing average current to $I_{sat}$.** Saturation is about the *peak*: average plus half the ripple.
- **Ignoring temperature.** Saturation current decreases and DCR increases when hot.
- **Forgetting the failure case.** Losing one of four phases raises the others by 33%.
- **Thinking the input side doesn't matter.** Input capacitors, connectors and PCB copper also carry the total current.
- **Using the datasheet's 25 °C power-stage rating.** The usable current at the real board temperature is lower.
:::

::: deep
## The three single-stage limits

Consider what stops one buck stage at 60 A and 1 V:

1. **FET conduction and temperature.** With $R_{eff}\approx1.4$ mΩ the conduction loss is $60^2\cdot1.4\text{ mΩ}=5.0$ W in one small package. At a thermal resistance of tens of °C/W that is a very hot junction.
2. **Inductor.** It must not saturate at $I_{pk}$, and its $I^2R_{DCR}$ heating limits RMS current. A 60 A inductor with low DCR and low inductance is physically big.
3. **Board and package.** Copper traces and vias that carry 60 A have noticeable $I^2R$ drops and temperature rise.

Splitting the load by $N$ divides current in each of these.

## Peak current and saturation

The inductor current is a triangle. Its peak is $I_{ph}+\Delta I_{ph}/2$. For 12 V to 1 V, 300 nH, 500 kHz: $\Delta I_{ph}=6.11$ A. At 80 A load with 4 phases the average per phase is 20 A and with 5% imbalance the worst is 21 A, so

$$I_{pk}=21+3.06=24.1\text{ A}$$

An inductor with $I_{sat}=30$ A gives 25% headroom. Choose lower inductance and you get more ripple, a higher peak and a faster slew. So the ripple/slew trade-off feeds back on current capability. Saturation current is also lower at high temperature, which is why datasheets give it at 25 °C and 100 °C. See the sim:

## When a phase is lost

Losing one of $N$ phases means the others carry $N/(N-1)$ times as much: 1.33x for four phases, 1.5x for three. Options are N+1 redundancy (design the surviving $N-1$ for the full load), a foldback current limit, or accepting a reduced-performance mode.

## Why splitting helps more than a factor of $N$ for each device

The single-stage limit is thermal, and heat goes as current squared. Keep the stage the same (same $R_{eff}=1.4$ mΩ and the same thermal resistance) and compare one stage carrying 60 A with three carrying 20 A each:

| | Current per stage | Loss per stage | Total loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 stage | 60 A | $60^2\times1.4\text{ mΩ}=5.04$ W | 5.04 W |
| 3 stages | 20 A | $20^2\times1.4\text{ mΩ}=0.56$ W | 1.68 W |

The total falls by $N$ (a factor of 3) but the loss *per device* falls by $N^2$ (a factor of 9), because each device carries $1/N$ of the current and loss goes as $I^2$. With a hypothetical thermal resistance of 15 °C/W to the board, the single stage would rise 76 °C over the board and each of three stages 8.4 °C. This is the real reason a stage that cannot handle 60 A is perfectly comfortable at 20 A: the margin is quadratic, not linear. (The 15 °C/W figure is only illustrative; real values depend heavily on package, copper area and airflow.) The table counts conduction loss only. Switching loss per device falls only as $1/N$, so once it is included the per-device benefit lies between $N$ and $N^2$: with the full loss model of [[q0241]], one stage carrying 60 A dissipates 8.75 W against 1.27 W per stage for four phases, a factor of 6.9 rather than 16.

## A tolerance stack for the inductor

The earlier worked example used the nominal 300 nH. Real parts are worse in two ways: the inductance has a manufacturing tolerance (±20% is common, but check the datasheet), and the inductance falls as DC current biases the core. Suppose the worst case is 20% low from tolerance and a further 20% low from bias at the operating current, so $L_{eff}=300\times0.8\times0.8=192$ nH. Recomputing the phase ripple at 12 V to 1 V and 500 kHz:

| Case | $L_{eff}$ | $\Delta I_{ph}$ | $I_{pk}$ (21 A worst phase) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nominal | 300 nH | 6.11 A | 24.1 A |
| −20% tolerance | 240 nH | 7.64 A | 24.8 A |
| −20% tolerance and −20% bias | 192 nH | 9.55 A | 25.8 A |

Still below a 30 A $I_{sat}$, with 14% margin. Notice how little the peak moves: because ripple is half-added to a large DC current, a 36% loss in inductance only moves the peak by 1.7 A. At light load the margin is huge; at full load it is the DC term that eats the headroom. If you used the inductor's *heating* current instead, the RMS is $\sqrt{21^2+6.11^2/12}=21.07$ A: ripple adds only 0.7% to the I²R heating, so the heating limit is almost entirely about the DC current.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The percentage drops for tolerance and bias are example values. Vendors define $I_{sat}$ at a specific inductance drop (commonly 10 to 30%) and at a specific temperature, so compare datasheets on the same basis.

## The rest of the current path

The phases remove the stage limit but not the plumbing limit. All 80 A still has to get from the inductors to the load and, on the input side, from the source to the stages.

- **Output copper.** Sheet resistance of 1 oz copper (35 µm) is $1.72\times10^{-8}/35\times10^{-6}=0.49$ mΩ per square at room temperature. If the 80 A flows through just two squares of a single 1 oz layer, that is about 0.98 mΩ: a drop of 78 mV on a 1 V rail (about 8%) and 6.3 W of heating in the copper itself. This is why processor boards use many layers of heavy copper and place each phase's output near the load, so that each stage's 20 A only travels a short distance before joining the plane.
- **Input capacitors.** Using the interleaved RMS formula (flat-top phase currents of 20 A, $ND=1/3$), the input capacitor RMS current is $20\sqrt{(1/3)(2/3)}=9.4$ A for four phases, versus $80\sqrt{(1/12)(11/12)}=22.1$ A for one phase delivering the same total. The input side needs fewer capacitors, but still has to meet its RMS rating.
- **Average input current** is only about $80/12/0.92=7.2$ A at 12 V, so the supply cable is easy, but it arrives as pulses that the bulk capacitors smooth.

## Limits that do not scale with $N$

1. **Ripple per phase stays the same.** Each inductor's own ripple is $6.11$ A regardless of $N$. Only the sum changes. So the inductor's peak-current and core-loss limits never relax by adding phases; they relax only because $I_{ph}$ falls.
2. **Fixed losses scale up with $N$.** Each additional phase brings its own gate drive and driver current, so total light-load loss rises with the phase count (see [[q0241]]).
3. **Controller and sense limits.** A controller supports a fixed maximum phase count; per-phase current sensing resolution (mV on a few-mΩ or sub-mΩ sense) gets worse as the phase current falls.
4. **Failure case.** Redundancy means oversizing: to survive losing one phase of four at 80 A, the three remaining must carry 26.7 A each. If the derated limit is 25 A, the system needs either a fifth phase or a lower guaranteed current.

## What to measure on the bench

1. **Per-phase currents at several loads** (25, 50, 75, 100%): fit the worst-to-average ratio to get your real $\varepsilon$, then compare with the 5% assumption.
2. **Inductor peak current** with a current probe on the inductor (or its sense node) at full load and at the hottest ambient: confirm the peak is below $I_{sat}$ with the margin you computed, and that the waveform is a clean triangle rather than a ramp that steepens at the top (a sign of saturation).
3. **Thermal map** at full load with a thermal camera: compare each power stage and inductor. A stage more than a few degrees above its neighbors usually carries more current or has worse cooling.
4. **N-1 test.** Disable one phase (many controllers allow this) and confirm that the controller either holds the load with the remaining phases within their limits or folds back gracefully rather than tripping into a hiccup loop.

::: applications
- **Processor core rails** (60 to 300 A) built from 6 to 16 phases of 20 to 50 A stages.
- **FPGA/ASIC rails** around 40 to 80 A from three to four phases.
- **High-current POL modules** that stack phases for 50 to 100 A.
- **Telecom/industrial 48 V to 12 V intermediate bus converters** where multiple phases share a high current at the output.
- **Redundant N+1 server VRMs**, which can lose a phase and keep running.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Equal, CCM operation** in each phase, and that the sharing error is bounded by $\varepsilon$.
- **Power-stage limit is thermal** and derated to the actual board temperature.
- **Inductor saturation is defined by the vendor** (for example the current at which inductance falls by 20 or 30%). Definitions vary.
- **Peak current computed with the nominal $L$.** Tolerance (±20% is common) and saturation roll-off change the real ripple.
- If input capacitors, connectors and copper are not scaled too, the input side may limit the system before the phases do.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the peak, not the average, current matter for the inductor?
Saturation depends on the instantaneous flux, which tracks the instantaneous current. Once the peak current exceeds $I_{sat}$ the inductance drops, the ripple grows and the current rises faster. In the example the average is 21 A but the peak 24.1 A, so the margin to 30 A is smaller than it looks.

### What is the peak-to-peak ripple if you change L to 150 nH?
$\Delta I_{ph}=1\cdot(11/12)/(150\text{ nH}\cdot500\text{ kHz})=12.2$ A. The peak at 21 A average becomes 27.1 A, close to the 30 A limit, and the RMS current is also higher. So lower inductance gives faster slew but eats saturation headroom.

### How would you protect the weakest phase?
Per-phase current limits using the sensed current, a thermal sensor in each power stage, and a balance loop. If a phase exceeds its limit, the controller can reduce its duty, or shut it down and reduce the total current limit.

### How does the input side scale?
The input current is the output current times $D$, in pulses. With interleaving the RMS current in the input capacitors falls, but connectors, the 12 V rail and copper still carry about $I_{out}D/\eta$. For 80 A at 1 V and 92% efficiency the input is about $80\cdot(1/12)/0.92=7.2$ A average.

### Scenario: your four-phase design is rated 95 A but trips overcurrent at 80 A. Why?
First suspect one phase carrying much more than its share: check per-phase currents. A 25% imbalance would put one phase at 25 A at 80 A total. Then check the current-limit threshold and its temperature dependence, DCR sensing drift, and saturating inductors in a hot spot. Finally check whether one phase is not firing (open gate drive).
