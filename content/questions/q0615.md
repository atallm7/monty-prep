::: basics
### In plain words
Tombstoning is when a tiny two-ended part, such as a small chip resistor or capacitor, stands up on one end during soldering, like a gravestone. One end ends up unsoldered, so the connection is open. It happens when the solder at the two ends melts at different moments or pulls with different strength.

### Picture it
Picture a domino lying flat. Someone grabs its left end face and pulls it down and outward. If nothing holds the right end, the domino pivots on its left edge and stands up. Molten solder does the grabbing: it climbs the end of the part and pulls. If both ends melt at the same moment, the two pulls balance and the part stays flat.

### Words you need
- **Reflow**: the oven step that melts solder paste. See [[q0602]].
- **Surface tension**: the force that makes molten solder pull itself, and the part, toward the pad.
- **Wetting**: molten solder spreading and sticking to a metal surface.
- **Thermal mass**: how much heat a pad's attached copper absorbs, slowing its warm-up.
- **Land pattern**: the size and shape of the pads. See [[q0613]].

### The simplest example
A small 0402 chip part (1.0 mm by 0.5 mm) weighs about 1.2 mg, so its weight is about 12 µN, where a µN is a millionth of a newton, the unit of force. A wetting pull of 0.5 N/m across a 0.5 mm wide end is about 250 µN, roughly twenty times the weight. So even a modest timing difference between the two ends can lift one up. The fix is to make both ends melt together: equal pads and copper connections, equal paste, and a gentle oven ramp. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the causes in detail and the prevention steps in order of effect.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand a **physical failure mechanism**, not just its name. Tombstoning (also called the Manhattan effect or drawbridging) is a small-passive defect where one end of a chip component lifts off the pad during reflow. The interviewer checks that you can:

1. Define it and say which parts are susceptible (small, light, two-terminal chip parts like 0402 and smaller).
2. Explain the cause as **unequal wetting force**: one pad's solder melts first and its surface tension pulls the part upright.
3. List the root causes across design (pad size, thermal mass), process (paste, placement, profile) and materials.
4. Give prevention in the right order: layout first, then stencil, then profile.
:::

::: why
Tombstoned parts are open circuits that AOI usually catches, but each one needs rework, and a design with a systematic imbalance can produce them at percent levels on thousands of boards. It's a classic example of how a layout choice (a pad tied to a plane, a trace that leaves one side) shows up as a factory yield problem. Interviewers like it because the cause is physical, quantitative, and fixable at design time.
:::

::: concepts
- **Reflow soldering**: paste melts above the solder's liquidus temperature (about 217 °C for SAC305) and wets the pads. See [[q0602]].
- **Surface tension and wetting force**: molten solder pulls the part toward the pad; each end generates a force.
- **Thermal mass**: copper area connected to a pad slows its heating.
- **Torque balance**: the net moment about the opposite end decides whether the part stays flat.
- **Stencil/paste volume**: unequal paste on two pads means unequal wetting force.
- **Land pattern**: pad size and spacing set the geometry of the wetting forces. See [[q0613]].
:::

::: answer
"Tombstoning is when a small two-terminal chip part, like an 0402 resistor or capacitor, stands up on one end during reflow, like a tombstone, leaving one pad unsoldered, so it's an open.

It happens because the two ends don't melt at the same moment. Whichever pad's solder melts first starts wetting the terminal, and molten solder's surface tension pulls on the component. If the other end is still solid paste, there's nothing to balance it, so the pulling torque lifts the far end off its pad. The part is tiny and light, about a milligram for an 0402, while that surface tension force is dozens of times its weight, so it doesn't take much imbalance.

The causes are anything that makes the two ends heat or wet unequally: one pad tied to a big copper plane and the other to a thin trace, different pad sizes, uneven paste volume, a part placed off-centre, a hot spot or fast ramp in the oven, or oxidised terminations.

To prevent it I'd first fix the layout, equal pads, symmetric thermal connections with thermal reliefs on the plane side, the right pad size. Then the stencil, to get equal paste. Then the profile: a soak so both ends start the final ramp at the same temperature and reach melting close together, and a moderate ramp so the temperature difference between them stays small. And I'd check placement accuracy."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism.** Let the two pads be A and B. The component rests on both with paste. When pad A's paste first exceeds the liquidus (about 217 °C for SAC305, around 183 °C for tin-lead), the solder wets terminal A and exerts a surface-tension force on it, of order $F \sim \gamma\,w$, where $\gamma$ is the surface tension of molten solder (about 0.4–0.5 N/m, alloy and flux dependent) and $w$ the terminal width. That force acts on the upper part of the terminal and tries to rotate the body about the wetted end A, lifting the B end. Two things resist: the body's weight moment, about $W L/2$ for length $L$, and any wetting force at B. While B's paste is still solid, B contributes nothing. If

$$M_{wet,A} > W\,\frac{L}{2} + M_{B}$$

the part tips up onto end A. Once B melts it is too late, because B's terminal is now off the pad and the standing part's own geometry holds it there. Equalising melt timing makes $M_B$ grow in step with $M_{wet,A}$ so the part settles flat.

For an 0402 (about 1.0 × 0.5 × 0.5 mm body), mass ≈ 1.2 mg (assuming a density near 5 g/cm³, ceramic-ish) so weight ≈ 12 µN, while a wetting force of 0.5 N/m × 0.5 mm ≈ 250 µN, roughly twenty times larger. So a small imbalance is enough; that's why smaller parts (0201, 01005) are worse. Weight scales with volume (L³), wetting force with width (L), so the ratio of force to weight grows as parts shrink.

**Root causes.**

| Category | Cause | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Layout | Pad tied to plane or large copper on one end only | That end heats slowly; the other end melts first |
| Layout | Unequal pad sizes or pad-to-body spacing | Different wetting areas and forces |
| Layout | Via-in-pad on one pad | Heat sink and solder wicking |
| Paste | Unequal paste volume or offset print | Unequal wetting force |
| Placement | Component off-centre or rotated | One terminal sits on more paste |
| Profile | Fast ramp rate, large ΔT across board | Ends melt at different times |
| Materials | Oxidised terminals, poor wetting, unequal flux activity | One side wets earlier |
| Process | Hot-air convection with directional airflow; component orientation to oven direction | Heating asymmetry |

**Prevention (in order of leverage).**

1. **Layout:** same copper connection on both pads; use thermal reliefs/spokes on the plane side; equal-width traces leaving each pad; follow IPC-7351 land patterns with pad lengths that match the component and avoid oversize pads; no vias in only one pad.
2. **Stencil/paste:** equal apertures, reduced aperture (for example, 10% smaller or "home-plate" shapes) to lower paste volume on the pads; type 3 or 4 paste for small parts; good print registration.
3. **Placement:** accurate centring (fiducials); correct placement force to embed the part in paste.
4. **Profile:** slower ramp (about 1–2 °C/s), a soak that equalises temperature, and a short time above liquidus; nitrogen can reduce oxidation but also changes wetting speed, so check.
5. **Materials:** fresh components, good termination plating, no-clean flux with proper activity.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Typical numbers (ramp 1–3 °C/s, aperture reduction ~10%, surface tension 0.4–0.5 N/m) vary by alloy, flux and process. Treat as typical ranges; the paste datasheet and assembler's profile rule.
:::

::: terms
- **Tombstoning / Manhattan effect / drawbridging**: a chip component standing on one end after reflow.
- **Wetting force**: pull from molten solder's surface tension on a terminal.
- **Surface tension**: property of liquid solder that minimises its surface area; about 0.4–0.5 N/m for molten lead-free solder.
- **Liquidus**: temperature above which the solder alloy is fully liquid (about 217 °C for SAC305).
- **Thermal relief**: spoked connection between a pad and a plane that slows heat flow and balances heating.
- **Soak (preheat) zone**: a part of the reflow profile that equalises temperature before peak.
- **Stencil aperture reduction**: opening smaller than the pad to reduce paste volume.
- **Open (solder joint)**: missing connection, the electrical result of tombstoning.
:::

**An average answer** says "one side melts first and pulls the part up; make the pads the same".

::: mistakes
- **Blaming only the reflow profile.** The layout is often the main cause; profiles can only reduce the problem.
- **Thinking big components tombstone.** It's small, light chip parts (0603 and below, worst at 0201/01005).
- **Making pads huge "for strength".** Oversized pads put more paste and wetting force on the part.
- **Tying one pad to a plane with no thermal relief.** The plane heatsinks that pad so it melts late.
- **Confusing tombstoning with a skewed or "billboarded" part.** A tombstone stands upright on one end; billboarding means lying on its side.
- **"Fixing" it only by rework.** It will recur on the next panel unless the cause is fixed.
:::

## What tombstoning is

A chip resistor or capacitor has a terminal at each end that sits on a pad of solder paste. Reflow melts the paste, the solder wets terminals, and the part ends up flat with a fillet at each end. In a tombstone, only one end ends up soldered; the other end stands in the air.

## The physical picture: a timing race

Each pad is a little thermal system: the oven heats it through radiation and convection, the pad loses heat into attached copper. A pad connected to a big plane has a longer thermal time constant. Model each pad as first-order:

$$T(t) = T_\infty - (T_\infty - T_0)\,e^{-t/\tau}$$

The pad reaches liquidus $T_L$ at $t_L = \tau\ln\frac{T_\infty - T_0}{T_\infty - T_L}$. If the two pads have different $\tau$, they cross liquidus at different times. The window between them is when the unbalanced force acts.

## Force and torque in numbers

For an 0402 body about 1.0 × 0.5 × 0.5 mm with density about 5 g/cm³:

- mass ≈ 1.25 mg, weight ≈ 12 µN,
- wetting force ≈ surface tension × terminal width ≈ 0.5 N/m × 0.5 mm = 250 µN.

The wetting force is about 20 times the weight, so very little imbalance is needed. Scale the component by a factor $k$: weight scales as $k^3$ while wetting force scales as $k$, so the force-to-weight ratio goes as $1/k^2$. An 0201 (k ≈ 0.6) has a force-to-weight ratio about 2.8 times higher than 0402. That's why tombstoning got worse as parts shrank.

## Design rules that prevent it

1. **Symmetric connection.** If one pad goes to a plane, add a thermal relief (spokes) so heat flow resembles the other pad, or connect the other pad to equal copper. Avoid a wide trace on one pad and a narrow trace on the other.
2. **Equal, correct pad sizes.** Use IPC-7351 style land patterns. Oversized pads add paste and wetting force; undersized ones don't hold the part.
3. **No via-in-pad on one pad** (it wicks solder and acts as a heat sink). If vias are needed, place them away from the pad.
4. **Keep the part orientation consistent relative to oven flow**, so that both pads see similar heating, where the oven allows.
5. **Mind the neighbours.** A pad next to a large tall part, or a component shading airflow, can lag.

## Process fixes

- **Stencil:** equal apertures, 5–10% reduction or a home-plate shape; type 4 paste for 0201.
- **Placement:** pick-and-place accuracy, fiducials, appropriate force; avoid off-centre placement of more than about a quarter of the pad.
- **Profile:** ramp about 1–2 °C/s to the soak, soak long enough to equalise (about 60–120 s in the 150–180 °C range for many pastes, per paste datasheet), short time above liquidus.
- **Atmosphere:** nitrogen improves wetting and can speed it up, which changes how fast each end wets; evaluate when switching.
- **Materials:** fresh, solderable terminations; paste within shelf life.

## Diagnosing from the defect

Look at which end lifts across many boards. If it's always the end connected to the plane or the larger trace, the layout is the cause. If it's random, suspect paste volume or placement. If it varies across the panel, suspect oven temperature uniformity. Use AOI to collect defect locations and map them on the board.

> [!TIP]
> If asked how you'd cure a persistent tombstone problem quickly, say "check the thermal and paste symmetry first, then add or lengthen the soak and keep the ramp moderate". That order shows you start from root cause, not knob-twiddling.

## Worked example: how little imbalance does it take?

Use the 0402 numbers from above and a deliberately crude torque balance. The wetting force on the first-wetted terminal is about $F = 250\ \mu$N, acting at roughly the terminal height $h = 0.5$ mm above the board, so the tipping moment is $F h = 250\times10^{-6}\times0.5\times10^{-3} = 1.25\times10^{-7}$ N·m. The weight acts at the middle of the 1.0 mm body, so its restoring moment is $W L/2 = 12\times10^{-6}\times0.5\times10^{-3} = 6\times10^{-9}$ N·m. The ratio is about $0.048$. In words: gravity can resist only about 5% of the tipping moment from one fully wetted end. So the part stays down only if the far end is also wetted and supplies at least about 95% of the near end's moment; while the far end's paste is still solid it supplies essentially none, which is why the time gap matters. That is why tombstoning cares about timing and symmetry more than about the part's weight. A smaller imbalance in paste volume or wetted area still matters, because if end B has a 20% smaller wetted area it can never supply 95% of end A's moment even after it melts; the same torque balance then favors A. This is a toy model that ignores the fillet geometry, the changing angle as the part rotates and the solder's own lubrication effect, but it correctly says that imbalance of a few tens of percent in any of the contributing quantities is enough.

## How much does the soak actually help?

The cold-start estimate earlier (23 s between the two pads crossing liquidus) assumed both pads started at room temperature and the oven was effectively a step. After a proper soak both pads begin the final ramp near the same temperature. Using the same time constants (20 s and 32 s) and starting both pads at 180 °C with the oven air ramping linearly from 180 °C at $r$ °C/s, a numerical integration of the first-order model gives (computed):

| Air ramp from soak | Pad A reaches 217 °C | Pad B reaches 217 °C | Gap | Pad B shortfall when A melts |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 °C/s | 93.8 s | 104.8 s | 11.0 s | 5.2 °C |
| 1 °C/s | 55.8 s | 64.8 s | 9.0 s | 7.6 °C |
| 2 °C/s | 35.0 s | 41.8 s | 6.8 s | 9.5 °C |
| 3 °C/s | 27.2 s | 32.9 s | 5.7 s | 10.4 °C |
| 5 °C/s | 20.1 s | 24.5 s | 4.5 s | 11.2 °C |

The first lesson is the size of the effect: starting from the soak cuts the gap from 23 s to roughly 5 to 11 s. The soak, not the ramp rate, does the shrinking. The second lesson is that ramp rate is a mixed knob. Once the pads are on a steady linear ramp each one lags the air by $r\tau$, so they cross liquidus $\tau_B - \tau_A = 12$ s apart whatever the ramp rate, and the temperature gap between them is $r(\tau_B-\tau_A)$. A slower ramp therefore makes the temperature difference smaller (5 °C at 0.5 °C/s against 11 °C at 5 °C/s) but lets the time gap creep back toward the full 12 s; a faster ramp gives a shorter time gap only because the pads have not yet settled into their full lag. With a realistic cap on the air temperature (say 250 °C peak zone) the gap at 2–5 °C/s stays near 8–9 s, so in this model the ramp rate barely moves the time gap at all. Assemblers do recommend moderate ramps, and the physical reason is the smaller temperature difference across pads and across the board, which this model reproduces, but the main timing lever is equalising temperatures in the soak (and soaking closer to liquidus: from a 200 °C start at 2 °C/s the gap drops to about 4.8 s).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The effect of ramp rate on tombstoning comes from process experience and is not captured completely by a single-time-constant model. The time constants here are illustrative; real values depend on pad copper, part size and oven airflow. Treat the table as a picture of the trend, not a predictor.

## Where the explanation is incomplete

**Paste is not a liquid wedge from the start.** Before it melts, paste is a viscous mixture of powder and flux that can hold a part in place; flux also begins to flow and slump during the soak. A part sitting in a thick paste deposit may even float differently on the two ends depending on how deep the terminal is pressed into the paste, which is why placement force and print height matter.

**Rotation geometry changes the balance.** As the part tilts, the wetting force direction and the lever arm change, and the second end moves away from its paste. After a critical angle the process is irreversible; before it, a part can still settle back if the second end melts quickly. That makes the timing threshold sharper than a static torque balance implies.

**Pad shape is a design variable beyond symmetry.** Land patterns with a longer pad toe than heel favor self-alignment, whereas pads that extend far out under the part concentrate the pull close to the center of the body. The land-pattern standard gives a family of acceptable shapes, and the best choice for a given part size is a matter of experiment with the assembler.

## Bench verification

1. **Map the defects.** Use AOI output or a tally over a few hundred boards and plot which reference designators tombstone and which end lifts. A consistent end points to layout asymmetry. A random end points to paste or placement.
2. **Thermocouple both pads.** On a sacrificial board, solder fine-gauge thermocouples to the two pads of a problem part (or to equivalent pads on the same net structure) and log the profile. The gap between the two curves at the moment the first reaches 217 °C is the measured version of the table above.
3. **Run a controlled experiment.** Change one variable per lot: aperture reduction, ramp rate, or a thermal relief on the plane-side pad. Compare defect counts over enough boards to be meaningful, and keep the lot sizes equal.
4. **Look at the joint afterward.** Boards that did not tombstone can still hide a partial lift, so confirm both fillets reach the terminal and that the part sits flat with a microscope or X-ray cross-section of a sample.

::: applications
- **Dense consumer boards** with 0201/01005 passives where pad balance is part of the design review.
- **Decoupling capacitors** placed with one pad on a power plane and one on a ground plane: thermal reliefs and symmetrical connections are used to even out heating.
- **LED arrays** with small chip LEDs (0402/0603) that tombstone if pads are uneven.
- **Contract manufacturer DFM reports**, which flag pads connected to large copper as tombstone risks.
- **Process qualification** of new ovens or new paste, where tombstone rate on test boards is a key metric.
:::

::: assumptions
- Applies to **reflow soldering of two-terminal chip components** (resistors, capacitors, inductors, LEDs). BGAs and leaded parts have different failure modes (head-in-pillow, bridging).
- Lead-free (SAC305) liquidus about 217 °C is assumed; tin-lead melts near 183 °C and has different wetting speed.
- Numbers (surface tension, density, time constants) are illustrative estimates, not measurements.
- **Alternative views**: some sources emphasise placement offset and paste volume as dominant, others the thermal imbalance; both are real and often combine.
- Vapour-phase reflow heats more uniformly and generally reduces tombstoning compared with convection ovens, with caveats on process.
:::

::: followups
### Why are smaller parts more likely to tombstone?
Wetting force scales with the terminal width (length scale $k$) while weight scales with volume ($k^3$). So the force-to-weight ratio scales as $1/k^2$: an 0201 has a much larger ratio than an 0805. A smaller imbalance in melt timing lifts it.

### One end of a decoupling cap goes to a ground plane and the other to a thin trace. What would you do?
Add a thermal relief to the plane connection so heat flow into that pad is limited and comparable to the trace side. Keep the trace and the spokes of similar width, and if I must connect to a plane for current or inductance reasons, use a relief with enough spokes for current. Then check the first build for tombstones with AOI. This ties to [[q0637]] on thermal reliefs and reworkability.

### How does paste volume affect tombstoning?
More paste on one pad means a bigger molten volume and a stronger wetting force on that side, and more thermal mass to melt. Uneven prints (a smeared or offset stencil) therefore create imbalance. Reduce apertures a little, check print registration, and use SPI (solder paste inspection) to detect volume errors.

### What if the part is placed 0.1 mm off-centre?
A part off-centre has more of one terminal on paste, so that side wets sooner and with a bigger force, often causing tombstoning or skew. Using fiducials and a calibrated placer, plus the self-centring effect of balanced pads, keeps the offset within a quarter of the pad. For 0201s tolerances become very tight.

### Scenario: 3% of 0402 caps tombstone, all on the pad that connects to the ground pour. Your assembler suggests changing the oven profile. What do you recommend?
Change the layout first: thermal reliefs on that pad, and check the pad sizes. A profile change could help marginally (slower ramp, soak), and I'd let the assembler try it in parallel, but I wouldn't rely on it: the pattern points to a thermal imbalance built into the design. Quantify with the next lot's defect rate and keep the profile change if it helps without hurting other parts.
:::
