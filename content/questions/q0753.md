::: basics
### In plain words
An I2C bus needs two signal wires, SDA for data and SCL for the clock, plus a shared ground and pull-up resistors. Adding another chip costs no extra wires. It taps onto the same two lines, and the controller picks it by its address. So six sensors still cost two microcontroller pins. But it is not unlimited. Addresses must not collide, and the wires can carry only so much capacitance before the edges get too slow.

### Picture it
Think of a single bus route with many stops. Adding a stop needs no new road. Each stop has a number, and the driver calls the one they want. Two limits remain. There are only 112 usable numbers, and two stops with the same number cause confusion. And every stop adds load, which slows the rising edges.

### Words you need
- **SDA and SCL**: the shared data wire and clock wire. Each chip needs only these two bus pins.
- **Pull-up resistor**: one resistor per wire, shared by the whole bus, that lifts it to high ([[q0755]]).
- **Address**: the 7-bit number that picks one chip. There are 112 usable values.
- **Address collision**: two chips with the same address, so both answer at once.
- **Bus capacitance**: the load from all pins, connectors and traces, which slows rising edges ([[q0756]]).
- **Multiplexer**: a chip that connects one of several side-branches to the main bus ([[q0747]]).

### The simplest example
For 5 devices, an I2C controller needs 2 pins. An SPI controller with a select wire per device would need $3 + 5 = 8$. Now check the load. Say the pins and traces add up to 89 pF, and the pull-up is 2.2 kΩ. The rise time is about $0.85 \times 2200 \times 89\text{ pF} \approx 166$ ns, which is under the 300 ns limit for 400 kHz. Adding more devices pushes that number up. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the fixes.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know the **physical cost** of I2C and, more importantly, that "unlimited devices on two wires" is a half-truth. The interviewer wants to hear:

1. Two signal lines (SDA, SCL), plus a shared ground reference, plus pull-ups. Not "two pins on the bus", but two pins *per device*.
2. Devices are told apart by **address**, not by extra wires. That's the whole trick, and it's why adding a slave costs zero extra MCU pins.
3. The real limits: address collisions, and total **bus capacitance** (400 pF in the spec). Strong candidates name the fixes: address pins, an I2C mux or switch, a second bus, or a buffer.
:::

::: why
Pin count is one of the first things that decides a protocol on a real board. I2C is the default for low-speed sensors, EEPROMs, PMICs and RTCs precisely because six sensors still cost two MCU pins. The catch shows up in bring-up: you add one more sensor, two parts share an address, or the edges turn into sluggish ramps. Interviewers ask this to see if you understand why the "add more slaves" answer isn't just "add more slaves".
:::

::: concepts
- **Open-drain, shared bus**: every device connects to the same SDA and SCL lines, so adding a device adds no new MCU pins. See [[q0745]].
- **Addressing**: a 7-bit address picks the target; 128 values minus 16 reserved leaves 112 usable. See [[q0746]] and [[q0747]].
- **Pull-up resistors**: one per line for the whole bus, not one per device. See [[q0755]] and [[q0020]].
- **Bus capacitance**: every pin and trace adds to $C_b$, which slows the rising edge. See [[q0756]].
- **Common ground**: signals are voltages relative to ground, so every device must share it. See [[q0001]].
:::

::: answer
"I2C needs two signal wires, SDA for data and SCL for clock, and a common ground so everybody agrees what a zero is. Each device only needs those two pins. Then there's one pull-up resistor on each line, shared by the whole bus, because the drivers are open-drain.

If I have more slaves, I don't add wires. I just tap them onto the same SDA and SCL, and they're told apart by their 7-bit address, so the master sends the address first and only the matching slave answers. That's the big advantage over SPI, where every extra slave needs another chip-select.

The limits are practical. First, addresses: there are 112 usable, and two identical parts, say two of the same temperature sensor, will collide unless they have address-select pins. If they do collide I'd use an I2C multiplexer like a TCA9548A, which gives me eight separate downstream buses, or put them on a second I2C peripheral. Second, capacitance: every pin and every centimetre of trace adds to the bus capacitance, and the spec caps it at 400 pF. With too many devices the rising edges get too slow, so I'd lower the pull-up value, slow the clock down, or split the bus with a buffer or switch."
:::

::: technical
**Wire count.** Minimum: SDA, SCL and ground. VDD is usually shared as well, and each line needs a pull-up to the bus supply, but those are passive parts on the board, not extra MCU pins. A device has two bus pins; the MCU has two bus pins regardless of how many slaves exist.

**Addressing.** A 7-bit address gives 128 codes. The specification reserves 16 of them (0000 xxx and 1111 xxx, including general call, START byte, CBUS, Hs-mode master codes and the 10-bit prefix), leaving 112 for ordinary devices. A 10-bit mode exists but is rarely used. Many parts have a few strap pins (A0, A1, A2) that select 2 to 8 distinct addresses; some have fixed addresses.

**Capacitive limit.** Each line must satisfy $C_b \le 400$ pF for Standard and Fast mode. The 30%–70% rise time is $t_r = 0.8473\,R_p C_b$, with limits of 1000 ns (Standard), 300 ns (Fast) and 120 ns (Fast-mode Plus). So the real device limit is whichever hits first: addresses, 400 pF, or a rise time you can't meet with $R_p \ge R_{p,min}$.

**Fixes when the bus is full.**

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Two parts with the same address | Strap-pin address, or I2C mux/switch (e.g. TCA9548A-style, one channel per part), or second I2C controller |
| $C_b$ too big | Smaller $R_p$ (within sink-current limit), slower mode, shorter traces, bus buffer/repeater that splits the capacitance |
| Mixed voltages | Level-translating buffer, or a bidirectional FET shifter |
| Long cable | Differential I2C extender, or switch to a different bus (CAN, RS-485) |

> [!NOTE]
> Counting wires for I2C: **N devices cost still 2 MCU pins**. Counting for SPI: **N devices cost 3 + N pins** with individual chip-selects. See [[q0765]].
:::

::: short
"I2C takes two wires, SDA and SCL, plus a shared ground, and one pull-up on each line for the whole bus. More slaves go on the same two wires and are picked by their 7-bit address, so there are no extra pins. The limits are address collisions, which you fix with address straps or an I2C mux, and the 400 pF bus capacitance limit, which you fix with smaller pull-ups, a slower clock, or a buffer."
:::

::: long
"I2C is a two-wire bus: SDA is the bidirectional data line and SCL is the clock. Practically it's three connections, because every device has to share a ground, and then there's one pull-up resistor from each line up to the bus supply. The drivers are open-drain, so devices can only pull the lines low, and the resistors pull them high.

The part I like is what happens when you add slaves. You don't add wires. Each new device just taps onto the same SDA and SCL. The master starts a transaction by sending a 7-bit address, and only the device with that address acknowledges. So the MCU pin cost stays at two, whether I have one sensor or ten.

But it isn't unlimited. There are only about 112 usable addresses, and in practice the problem is two identical parts that both want the same one. Many chips give me a couple of address strap pins. If they don't, I'd put them behind an I2C multiplexer, which is a switch that connects one downstream branch at a time, or give one of them its own controller.

The other limit is bus capacitance. Every device pin, every connector and every centimetre of trace adds to the load, and the spec caps it at 400 pF for the standard and fast modes. The pull-up has to charge that capacitance, and the rise time goes as 0.85 times R times C, so the more I add, the slower the edges get. If I'm running out of budget I'd shrink the pull-up, as long as the devices can still sink the current, drop to a slower clock, shorten the traces, or split the bus with a buffer.

So: two wires, any number of slaves in principle, and addresses and capacitance as the real ceilings."
:::

::: terms
- **SDA / SCL**: serial data and serial clock. Both open-drain and bidirectional.
- **Open-drain**: a driver that can only pull a line low or let go. See [[q0761]].
- **Pull-up resistor**: one resistor per line, to $V_{DD}$, that defines the high level. See [[q0755]].
- **7-bit address**: the code that selects a device. 112 are usable.
- **Address strap pins**: pins (A0, A1, A2) tied to $V_{DD}$ or GND to choose among a few addresses.
- **I2C multiplexer / switch**: a device that connects the master to one of several downstream branches, so identical addresses don't collide.
- **Bus capacitance ($C_b$)**: total capacitance on a line: pins, traces, connectors. Spec max 400 pF.
- **Bus buffer / repeater**: splits the bus into segments so each sees less capacitance.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "two wires, SDA and SCL, and you can add many slaves with addresses".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Says "two wires **plus ground** and pull-ups" and notes the pull-ups are shared, one per line, not per device.
- Contrasts with SPI: pin cost is constant, not $3+N$.
- Gives the real ceilings with numbers: 112 usable addresses and $C_b \le 400$ pF.
- Has a concrete fix for the "two identical sensors" case (address strap, mux, or second bus) and for the capacitance case (smaller $R_p$, slower mode, buffer).
- Mentions that adding a device also changes the **effective pull-up** if it has its own on-board resistors, which many breakout boards do.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Only two wires, full stop."** You still need ground and pull-ups; leaving out the pull-ups means the bus never goes high.
- **"Unlimited slaves."** Limited by 112 addresses and 400 pF, whichever comes first.
- **Putting a pull-up on every device.** Pull-ups are in parallel, so five breakout boards with 10 kΩ each become 2 kΩ, and ten become 1 kΩ, which at 3.3 V is right at the ≈967 Ω minimum set by a 3 mA sink rating. See [[q0760]].
- **Forgetting identical-address parts.** The fix is a mux, a strap pin or a second bus, not "just add it".
- **Treating the 400 pF limit as a count of devices.** A short bus can hold many devices; a long cable with two devices can fail. Capacitance is the budget, not device count.
- **Assuming SPI-style chip-selects exist.** I2C has no chip-select; the address is in-band.
:::

The bus lines are shared copper; what makes this work is that **no device ever drives the line high**. Everyone either pulls low or releases, and the pull-up resistor supplies the high. That is why you can connect any number of devices without the contention problems you would get from several push-pull outputs fighting. See [[q0761]].

## Where the "unlimited" claim breaks: addresses

Seven bits give $2^7 = 128$ codes. The spec reserves 16 for special purposes, so

$$128 - 16 = 112 \text{ usable addresses.}$$

That sounds like a lot until you meet real parts. A typical sensor family offers 2 to 4 addresses through strap pins; a lot of parts have exactly one. Put two of those on one bus and both ACK the same address, and then both drive SDA during the read, which gives garbage.

Three standard escapes, in order of how often I'd reach for them:

1. **Pick a different part variant.** Many vendors sell the same chip with a different base address (e.g. an "A" and a "B" ordering code). Cheapest, but it needs a second SKU on the BOM.
2. **I2C multiplexer.** A switch such as the TCA9548A (8 channels) connects the upstream bus to one or more downstream channels, selected by writing a control register. Identical parts sit on different channels, so the collision disappears. Bonus: each channel has *its own* capacitance, so it also fixes problem 2.
3. **A second I2C controller** on the MCU. Most MCUs have two or three. Costs two more pins and no extra parts.

## Where it also breaks: capacitance

Each line is a capacitor to ground, with the pull-up as its charging resistor. A rise from 30% to 70% of $V_{DD}$ takes

$$t_r = R_p C_b \ln\frac{0.7}{0.3} \approx 0.8473\,R_p C_b$$

What goes into $C_b$? A device pin is typically a few pF (check the datasheet; 3 to 10 pF is a common range), a connector adds a few more, and a PCB trace adds roughly 1 pF per cm for a typical 50 Ω-ish microstrip (about 1.3 pF/cm from $C = t_d/Z_0$ with $t_d \approx 170$ ps/inch). So a budget is easy to build:

| Item | Capacitance |
|---|---|
| MCU pin + ESD | ~5 pF |
| 8 devices at ~8 pF | 64 pF |
| 15 cm total trace at ~1.3 pF/cm | ~20 pF |
| **Total** | **~89 pF** |

Take a 2.2 kΩ pull-up: $t_r = 0.8473 \times 2200 \times 89$ pF ≈ 166 ns. That is fine for Fast mode (limit 300 ns) but not for Fast-mode Plus (limit 120 ns). Now go to 16 devices (about 153 pF) and the same resistor gives about 285 ns, right at the Fast-mode edge.

Figure: Assumes 20 pF of fixed capacitance plus 10 pF per device (pin plus trace). Each added device slides you up the line until you hit the 300 ns Fast-mode limit at about 14 devices with this resistor. A smaller $R_p$ shifts the whole line down but costs sink current.

The resistor can't shrink forever. Its minimum value is set by the weakest device's sink capability: $R_{p,min} = (V_{DD} - V_{OL})/I_{OL}$. At 3.3 V with the standard 3 mA and 0.4 V, that is $(3.3-0.4)/3\text{ mA} \approx 967$ Ω. With 3.3 V and a 400 pF bus, the max resistor for a 300 ns edge is $300\text{ ns}/(0.8473 \times 400\text{ pF}) \approx 885$ Ω, which is **below** the minimum. In other words, a fully loaded Fast-mode bus at 3.3 V doesn't close with a plain resistor if you only count on the 3 mA rating. UM10204 also lists a 6 mA at 0.6 V rating for Fast-mode devices, which roughly halves $R_{p,min}$ and reopens the window if every part on the bus meets it. Otherwise this is when you reach for a buffer, a lower speed, or a current-source pull-up.

## Practical answer to "what if I have more slaves?"

Walk through it like a checklist:

1. **Do all addresses differ?** If not, strap pin, variant, mux, or second bus.
2. **What's the total $C_b$?** Add pins, connectors and trace. If under about 150 pF, 4.7 kΩ is usually fine at 100 kHz and 2.2 kΩ at 400 kHz.
3. **Can the weakest device sink the current?** Compute $R_{p,min}$ from the *smallest* $I_{OL}$ on the bus, and check the *effective* pull-up after paralleling breakout-board resistors.
4. **If it still fails**, split the bus (switch or buffer) or slow it down.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Don't answer "unlimited". Say "112 addresses, 400 pF, and I'd check both before adding a part". That sentence signals you've done it on a real board.
:::

::: applications
- **Sensor hub on a wearable**: IMU, barometer, temperature, and a PMIC all share one two-wire bus to a small MCU.
- **DIMM SPD EEPROMs**: memory modules use address-strap pins so several modules on one bus have different addresses.
- **I2C multiplexer for identical sensors**: eight same-address temperature sensors on an eight-channel switch (TCA9548A-style).
- **Camera modules and displays**: low-speed control of the sensor or panel over I2C, while pixel data uses a separate high-speed link.
- **Battery packs**: fuel gauge and charger ICs talk to the host over a small I2C bus.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Standard I2C with 7-bit addressing** and open-drain devices. 10-bit addressing changes the address count but not the wire count.
- **One master, normal-length PCB bus.** Multi-master adds arbitration but no wires; long cables change the capacitance story completely.
- **Capacitance numbers are typical.** Pin capacitance, trace capacitance and connector capacitance vary a lot by part and layout; use the datasheets and your own stackup.
- **"Pins" means MCU pins.** Counting board wires, you also have supply and ground (and in some systems an interrupt line per device, which is **extra** and common in practice).

If the interviewer says "ignore ground and power", the answer is two. If they say "I want 30 sensors", the answer becomes multiplexers or multiple buses, because the 400 pF budget will be the first thing to fail.
:::

::: followups
### What if two sensors have the same fixed address?
Put them behind an I2C multiplexer (each on its own channel) or on separate MCU I2C controllers. A third option is a part variant with a different address. Don't try to "just not talk to one": both will ACK, both will drive the data, and the read is corrupted.

### How many devices can I put on one I2C bus in practice?
The spec says 400 pF and 112 addresses, so a clean answer is "until one of those runs out". With about 10 pF per device (pin plus trace), you'd reach 400 pF at roughly 35 to 40 devices; but meeting a 300 ns edge at 3.3 V gets difficult well before that, so a dozen or so is a more comfortable number for Fast mode.

### Do I need a pull-up per device?
No. One pair per bus. Boards with multiple modules often end up with several pull-ups in parallel by accident, e.g. five modules with 10 kΩ gives 2 kΩ, ten gives 1 kΩ, and the effective $R_p$ can drop below $R_{p,min}$. Remove extras.

### How would you extend the bus if capacitance is the limit?
Use an I2C buffer or hub that isolates the capacitance into segments, or an I2C switch that connects only the active branch. For long distances, use an extender such as a differential I2C driver. As a last resort, drop the clock so the edges have time to settle.

### What if my I2C bus has 50 sensors and each is identical with a fixed address?
Eight-channel muxes: the TCA9548A has three strap pins, so eight of them on the upstream bus cover 64 channels, one sensor per channel. Or use a different bus altogether, like SPI with chip-selects. Mind the interrupt lines: 50 sensors may each need their own.
:::

::: interpretations
"How many pins/wires are needed?" may mean (a) the minimum signal conductors (two, plus ground), (b) pins on the MCU (two), or (c) pins on each slave (two plus power). I state all three. "What if I have more slaves?" is about scaling: addresses and capacitance. For wiring details see [[q0754]]; for the protocol side of "how many slaves" see [[q0747]].
:::

::: rubric
- Two signal wires (SDA, SCL), plus ground and pull-ups :: "Two wires" alone is incomplete; the pull-ups are part of the physical layer.
- Same two wires for any number of slaves; devices selected by address :: This is the point of the protocol and the contrast with SPI.
- 7-bit address, ~112 usable :: Shows you know the address space is finite.
- Address collision fix: strap pin, mux, second bus :: The most common practical failure.
- Capacitance limit (400 pF) and its effect on rise time :: The physical ceiling on how many devices fit.
- Remedies: smaller $R_p$, slower mode, buffer :: Shows you can act on the limit, not just quote it.
- Pull-ups are shared, not per-device :: A classic breakout-board mistake.
