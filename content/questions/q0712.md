::: basics
### In plain words
This question asks you to draw the voltage on a line, once with no reflections and once with reflections. With a matched line, you draw a clean step that arrives late by the travel time. With a mismatched line, you draw a step that overshoots, dips, and settles in stages. Pick one concrete example and use its numbers.

### Picture it
Picture dropping a ball on the floor. It bounces up and down, each bounce smaller than the last, and finally rests. A mismatched line does the same electrically: the voltage jumps past its final value, falls below it, and the swings shrink until it rests. A matched line is like setting the ball down gently.

### Words you need
- **Matched line**: a line ending in a resistance equal to $Z_0$, so nothing bounces back.
- **One-way delay ($t_d$)**: the time a signal takes to travel from one end to the other.
- **Launch voltage**: the size of the first step, set by the driver's resistance and $Z_0$ ([[q0710]]).
- **Overshoot and ringing**: going past the final value and then wiggling around it.
- **Final value**: where the voltage settles when all bounces have died out.

### The simplest example
Use a 3.3 V driver with 10 Ω output resistance, a 50 Ω line with $t_d$ = 1 ns, and a fast edge. The launch voltage is 3.3 × 50 / 60 = 2.75 V.

Matched case: the far end sees 2.75 V at 1 ns and stays flat. Draw a clean copy delayed by 1 ns.

Open-end case: the far end jumps to 5.5 V at 1 ns, drops to 1.83 V at 3 ns, climbs to 4.28 V at 5 ns, and keeps swinging, with smaller swings, toward 3.3 V.

That is the whole idea: draw one delayed step, or a staircase of shrinking swings around the final value. The rest of this page gives drawing rules and the fix with a series resistor.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can *draw* what you reasoned about in [[q0711]]: a clean, delayed edge when the line is matched, and the step-ringing, overshoot, staircase or rounded edge you get when it isn't. The interviewer wants you to pick a concrete setup (driver impedance, $Z_0$, load, delay), compute the first step, label the delay on the time axis, show both ends of the line, and say what each picture means (a false edge, a half-height shelf, a staircase). Drawing the *source end* as well as the load end is what separates strong answers.
:::

::: why
On a scope, reflections are not equations; they are shapes. Being able to look at a plateau, a shelf at half amplitude or an overshoot-and-dip and say "that's a mismatch of this size, this long away" is the practical form of transmission line theory. It's how you diagnose a board bring-up problem, and how you explain it to a teammate with a marker on a whiteboard. This question is the visual companion to [[q0711]] and the diagnostic companion to [[q0713]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Launch voltage** $V_1=V_sZ_0/(R_s+Z_0)$ and the arrival at the far end after $t_d$ ([[q0710]]).
- **Reflection coefficients** $\Gamma_L$ and $\Gamma_S$, and the rule that each round trip multiplies by $\Gamma_S\Gamma_L$ ([[q0711]]).
- **Plateaus of width $2t_d$**: waveforms on an ideal line change only at multiples of the one-way delay, so they look like staircases.
- **Rise time**: smooths the corners; the steps are drawn as ramps of width $t_r$ ([[q0709]]).
- **Thresholds**: whether a ring matters depends on where it crosses the receiver's switching level.
:::

::: answer
"I'd pick a concrete case so the pictures are honest: a 3.3 V driver with about 10 ohms output impedance, a 50 ohm trace with 1 ns of delay, and a fast edge. I'll draw two panels, with and without reflections, each showing the voltage at the source end and at the load end versus time.

Without reflections the load is 50 ohms. The source end steps up to 2.75 V, which is 3.3 times 50 over 60, and the load end sees exactly the same step one nanosecond later. Then both sit flat. A clean delayed copy.

With reflections, I leave the end open. The first step is still 2.75 V at the source. At one nanosecond it hits the open end, which reflects with plus one, so the load voltage jumps to 5.5 V. That wave goes back, hits the driver, which reflects with minus 0.67, so the load goes down to 1.8 V at three nanoseconds, then 4.3 V, then 2.6 V, ringing around 3.3 V with a four-nanosecond period. The source end shows a staircase: 2.75 V for two nanoseconds, then 3.67, 3.06 and so on.

The other shapes I'd mention: a series-terminated line shows a half-height shelf at the source for two delays and a clean edge at the load; a load below $Z_0$ gives a monotonic staircase; a short gives a pulse at the source; and a capacitive load gives a rounded edge that can still overshoot."
:::

::: technical
**Setup used in all sketches:** $V_s=3.3$ V (step), $R_s=10\ \Omega$ unless stated, $Z_0=50\ \Omega$, $t_d=1$ ns, $t_r=0.2$ ns. Launch $V_1=2.75$ V, $\Gamma_S=-0.667$.

| Case | $\Gamma_L$ | Load-end voltage after arrivals | Source-end voltage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matched ($R_L=50$) | 0 | 2.75 V flat after $t_d$ | 2.75 V flat |
| Open | +1 | 5.50, 1.83, 4.28, 2.65, 3.73, 3.01 … → 3.30 V | 2.75 (0 to 2 ns), 3.67, 3.06, 3.46 … |
| Open, strong ($R_s=5$) | +1 | 6.00, 1.09, 5.11, 1.82, 4.51 … → 3.30 V | 3.00, 3.55, 3.10 … |
| Series-terminated ($R_s=50$) | +1 | 3.30 V at $t_d$, clean | 1.65 V for $2t_d$, then 3.30 V |
| $R_L=25\ \Omega$ | −1/3 | 1.83, 2.24, 2.33 … → 2.36 V (staircase) | 2.75, 2.44, 2.38 … → 2.36 V |
| Short | −1 | 0 | 2.75, 1.83, 1.22, 0.81 … → 0 (decaying shelves) |

**Rules for drawing:**
1. Voltage on an ideal line changes only at multiples of $t_d$ at the load and at multiples of $2t_d$ at the source (after $t=0$).
2. At the load: $V_L=V_{prev}+(1+\Gamma_L)\cdot(\text{arriving wave})$. At the source: $V_S=V_{prev}+(1+\Gamma_S)\cdot(\text{arriving wave})$.
3. Each arriving wave is the previous one times $\Gamma_L$ then $\Gamma_S$.
4. Every step takes $t_r$ to rise; draw ramps, not vertical lines.
5. The final value is the DC divider $V_sR_L/(R_s+R_L)$.
6. The ring period for $\Gamma_S\Gamma_L<0$ is $4t_d$.
:::

"I'd start by fixing a concrete example so the drawing is honest. A 3.3 V driver with a 10 ohm output, a 50 ohm line, one nanosecond of delay and a 200 ps edge. I'd draw two panels, with voltage on the vertical axis and time on the horizontal axis, and show the source end and the load end in each.

First panel, no reflections: the load is 50 ohms. The source end steps to 3.3 times 50 over 60, 2.75 V, and the load end shows the same step exactly one nanosecond later. After that everything is flat at 2.75 V. That's the matched case: a clean, delayed copy.

Second panel, with reflections: I leave the end open. The first step is the same, 2.75 V at the source. So at the source the voltage steps up to 3.67 V, and the new wave of minus 1.83 V goes to the load, which then drops to 1.83 V at 3 ns. It rings with a four nanosecond period and decays by 0.67 per round trip towards 3.3 V. The source end is a staircase with steps every 2 ns.

Then I'd sketch a few variants. A series-terminated line, 50 ohms total at the driver: a half-height shelf of 1.65 V at the source for two nanoseconds, and a clean full-swing edge at the load. The thing I'd point out is that if the ring crosses the receiver threshold, say a strong 5 ohm driver dipping to 1.1 V, you get a false extra edge, which is the real reason we care."
:::

::: terms
- **Source end / load end (near end / far end)**: the two places on the line where you can probe.
- **Plateau**: a flat segment of the waveform between reflections, of duration $2t_d$ at the source and $2t_d$ at the load.
- **Staircase**: stepwise approach to the final value when $\Gamma_S\Gamma_L>0$.
- **Ringing**: oscillation around the final value when $\Gamma_S\Gamma_L<0$.
- **Shelf**: a half-height plateau at the source end of a series-terminated line, lasting $2t_d$.
- **Runt pulse / false edge**: an unintended partial or extra transition caused by a ring crossing the threshold.
- **Incident-wave switching**: the load switches on the first arriving wave, as with a matched or series-terminated line.
- **Reflected-wave switching**: the load only switches once the reflection returns; slow and ring-sensitive.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws a clean pulse and a ringing pulse and labels them "without" and "with" reflections.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Fixes the setup (driver $R_s$, $Z_0$, $t_d$, $t_r$) and *computes* the plateau values rather than sketching a vague ring.
- Draws **both ends** of the line and marks the delay $t_d$ and the $2t_d$ plateaus on the time axis.
- Shows at least the open, matched and series-terminated cases and explains the half-height shelf.
- Marks the receiver threshold and shows when a ring becomes a false edge.
- Reads the picture back: ring period tells the length ($4t_d$), the sign of the first reflection tells whether the load is high or low, the plateau at the source tells the line delay.
- Mentions the rise time effect: slower edges round the staircase and cut the peak.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing only the load end.** The source end is where TDR-type diagnosis happens, and the shelf of a series-terminated line is only visible there.
- **Drawing smooth sinusoidal ringing.** On an ideal lossless line the waveform is a staircase with flat plateaus; smoothing comes from finite rise time, loss and reactive loads.
- **Starting the load waveform at $t=0$.** The load sees nothing until $t_d$.
- **Wrong sign on a load below $Z_0$.** The reflection is negative, so the load voltage steps are *smaller* than twice the incident step ($1+\Gamma_L<1$).
- **Making the plateaus unequal.** At each end, steps occur every $2t_d$, with equal spacing.
- **Forgetting the final value.** It is the DC divider, not the source voltage, unless $R_L\gg R_s$.
:::

## The setup

All the sketches use the same example so you can compare them. A step source $V_s=3.3$ V with a 200 ps rise time and series resistance $R_s=10\ \Omega$ drives a $Z_0=50\ \Omega$ line with one-way delay $t_d=1$ ns (about 170 mm of FR-4 microstrip). The first wave is $V_1=2.75$ V and the source reflection coefficient is $\Gamma_S=(10-50)/(10+50)=-0.667$.

The waveforms below were computed with the exact lattice (superposition of delayed, ramped steps) and cross-checked with a time-domain simulation. Each ramp is 0.2 ns wide.

```
Figure: A stronger driver (5 Ω) has $\Gamma_S=-0.82$, so the reflected wave is much larger and dies out more slowly. The load jumps to 6 V (82% above the rail), then drops to only 1.09 V, below the 1.65 V switching threshold of a typical CMOS input, so the receiver sees a falling edge that wasn't sent: a false clock edge or a glitch on a data line. It then returns above the threshold at 5.2 ns. The same line with a gentler 10 Ω driver stays above 1.8 V in the first dip, which is why "it worked on the last board with the weaker driver" is such a common story.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 1.65 V threshold assumes a CMOS input that switches at about $V_{DD}/2$. Real thresholds are specified by datasheet limits (typical LVCMOS33 values are $V_{IL}=0.8$ V and $V_{IH}=2.0$ V, with hysteresis on some pins). A dip below $V_{IH}$ is an invalid high; a dip below the actual switching point is a false edge. A 5 Ω source is also an illustrative strong driver, not a datasheet value.

 "hlines":[{"at":3.3,"label":"3.3 V","color":"green"}]}
```
Figure: Making the total source resistance 50 Ω (for example a 10 Ω driver plus a 40 Ω resistor) launches half the swing, 1.65 V. The source end shows a *shelf* at 1.65 V for $2t_d$ (the wave goes out, reflects at the open end and returns), then steps to 3.3 V. The load end shows one clean 3.3 V edge, because the open end doubles the incident 1.65 V. The returning wave is fully absorbed at the source ($\Gamma_S=0$), so there are no further echoes. Anything connected *in the middle* of the line sees the shelf at half amplitude for a time, so series termination only suits receivers at the far end ([[q0716]]).

## How to draw it fast on a whiteboard

1. Draw the schematic: source and $R_s$, line (label $Z_0$ and $t_d$), load.
2. Compute $V_1=V_sZ_0/(R_s+Z_0)$, $\Gamma_S$ and $\Gamma_L$.
3. Axes: time with ticks every $t_d$; voltage with the final value marked.
4. Source end: level $V_1$ until $2t_d$; step by $V_1\Gamma_L(1+\Gamma_S)$; next step after another $2t_d$ by $V_1\Gamma_L^2\Gamma_S(1+\Gamma_S)\ldots$
5. Load end: nothing until $t_d$; step by $V_1(1+\Gamma_L)$; next step at $3t_d$ by $V_1\Gamma_L\Gamma_S(1+\Gamma_L)$ and so on.
6. Check by eye: both curves end at the DC divider value.

> [!TIP]
> Put thresholds on the load-end sketch. Interviewers will ask "does this fail?", and the answer is whether the ring crosses a threshold when it shouldn't.

## Eye diagram picture

With reflections, each bit's edge leaves echoes that land on later bits. On an eye diagram this shows as a narrower eye with more overshoot on top and more thickness on the crossing: the echo pattern depends on the data, so the traces spread (inter-symbol interference). A matched channel gives a wide, clean eye ([[q0703]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Bring-up debugging**: a scope at the source end of a suspect net immediately shows a shelf (series-terminated) or a step pattern (unterminated).
- **TDR**: the "pictures" are read as impedance versus distance: an upward step is a high-impedance discontinuity, a downward step is a low-impedance one.
- **Clock integrity checks**: overlay the receiver threshold on the load-end waveform to find double-clock risk.
- **Choosing driver strength** (drive-strength settings on FPGAs/MCUs): weaker drivers have higher $R_s$, smaller $|\Gamma_S|$ and less ringing.
- **Teaching and documentation**: whiteboard sketches of "good" and "bad" waveforms are the standard way to communicate an SI problem to a layout colleague.
- **Cable testing**: a pulse generator and scope on a coax reveal opens and shorts by the polarity of the returning echo.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal, lossless line.** Real lines add loss and dispersion, so plateaus slope slightly and corners round off with distance.
- **Linear resistive driver.** CMOS output impedance changes during the edge and differs between pull-up and pull-down. IBIS models describe it.
- **Step with a 0.2 ns ramp** and a quiescent line initially. A real data pattern superposes these responses.
- **Receiver input as an ideal open.** Real inputs have pin capacitance (about 2 to 10 pF) and clamp diodes that limit the peak to roughly $V_{DD}$ plus a diode drop and change the later part of the waveform.
- **No loss in the termination or crosstalk.** Both change the details but not the shapes.
- **The capacitive-load trace** is a numerical time-domain simulation with Euler integration; use it for shape, not for exact numbers.

If an interviewer asks for the *frequency-domain* version, say that $|S_{11}|$ or return loss replaces the picture.
:::

::: followups
### How can you tell from a scope picture whether the load end is open, short or high/low impedance?
Look at the sign of the first reflection at the source end (or the first step at the load end). Higher than $Z_0$: the voltage steps up (open gives the full +1 reflection). Lower than $Z_0$: it steps down (a short gives −1). A reflection of zero means matched. The time to the first echo is $2t_d$.

### The scope shows a half-height shelf at the driver lasting 2 ns, then a full-height step. What is it?
That's the signature of a source (series) terminated line with an open or high-impedance receiver at the end. The driver launches half the swing; the open end reflects and doubles it; when the reflection returns after $2t_d=2$ ns the source end rises to full amplitude. The shelf width gives the delay: $t_d=1$ ns, about 170 mm of FR-4 microstrip. If a second receiver is in the *middle* of the line, it will see the half-height shelf and may not switch.

### What changes if I slow the edge down?
The steps smear together and the peaks shrink. From the same lattice calculation: with $t_r=2$ ns ($t_d/t_r=0.5$) the first overshoot at the open end is still the full 5.5 V, but with $t_r=5$ ns ($t_d/t_r=0.2$) it falls to roughly 15% above the final value, about 3.8 V. The reflections still exist; they just return while the edge is still rising and blend into it ([[q0709]]).

### Scenario: the load-end waveform of a 100 MHz clock is clean on the bench but fails in the chassis. What would you check?
Compare the two setups: cable lengths or connectors in the chassis (extra delay and discontinuities), a different driver strength/voltage, or a shifted reference (return path through a chassis ground). I'd put the scope at the receiver pin with a short ground spring, and zoom on the edge for ring and threshold crossing. If the chassis has an extra cable stub or a connector, I'd look at it with a TDR.

### Harder: why does the capacitive-load waveform overshoot higher than the open-end case?
The capacitor first looks like a short ($\Gamma_L\to-1$, voltage near zero), so the reflected wave is inverted, goes back to the low-impedance driver, reflects with $\Gamma_S=-0.67$ and returns positive. By the time it arrives, the capacitor has charged and looks open ($\Gamma_L\to+1$), so the returning wave is doubled at the load, adding to the first. With a low $R_s$ and moderate $C_L$ the timing of those echoes lines up, giving about 6.2 V in this example. It depends strongly on $C_L$, $t_d$ and $R_s$, so simulate rather than guess.
:::
