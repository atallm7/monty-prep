::: testing
Whether you can explain the efficiency gap from **energy and charge conservation**, not just "bucks are switching so they're better". The interviewer wants:

1. The LDO as a variable resistor: all the current from the input passes through it, so loss is $(V_{in}-V_{out})\,I_{out}$ and efficiency is about $V_{out}/V_{in}$.
2. The buck as a lossless-in-principle energy transformer: switches that are fully on (low $V$) or fully off (no $I$), and an inductor and capacitor that store and return energy.
3. A worked comparison with numbers (12 V to 3.3 V at 2 A: 17 W of heat vs under 0.5 W).
4. Balance: the real losses of the buck, and the cases where the LDO wins (low dropout, light load, noise).
:::

::: why
This is the most basic reason switching regulators exist, and nearly every power-system decision starts here: thermal design, battery life, whether a heatsink is needed, and why a 12 V to 3.3 V rail on a board is never an LDO at amps. The follow-up "when can an LDO be more efficient?" ([[q0158]]) is why you need the mechanism, not just the headline. It also sets up the rest of this section: if the buck is nearly lossless in principle, the remaining questions ([[q0212]] to [[q0220]]) are about where the real losses are and how ripple trades against them.
:::

::: concepts
- **Power and efficiency**: $\eta=P_{out}/P_{in}$ ([[q0001]]).
- **LDO operation**: a pass transistor in series with the load, controlled by an error amplifier; input current equals output current plus quiescent current ([[q0157]]).
- **Switch losses**: an ideal switch dissipates zero: $V=0$ when on, $I=0$ when off.
- **Inductor energy storage**: $E=\tfrac12 LI^2$; energy moves from input to output without being burned ([[q0174]]).
- **Duty-cycle conversion**: $V_{out}=DV_{in}$, so $I_{in}=D\,I_{out}$ in the ideal buck.
:::

::: answer
"The difference is where the extra voltage goes. An LDO is basically a controlled resistor in series with the load. To drop 12 V to 3.3 V at 2 A, it has to drop the 8.7 V difference across the pass transistor while carrying the full 2 A, so it burns 8.7 V times 2 A, 17 W, to deliver 6.6 W. Efficiency is just $V_{out}/V_{in}$, 27%. The input current is also 2 A, because the same current flows through it.

A buck never lets the excess voltage sit across something carrying current. The switch is either fully on, so it has almost no voltage across it, or fully off, so it has no current. An ideal switch dissipates nothing. The inductor and capacitor store energy and give it back, so they don't dissipate either. What the buck does is chop the input into pulses and let the LC filter average them, so the output is $D\cdot V_{in}$.

Energy is conserved, so an ideal buck takes input current of only $D\cdot I_{out}$, here 0.55 A instead of 2 A. In a real buck you lose a few percent in switch resistance, switching transitions, inductor resistance and gate drive, so you get 90 to 95% instead of 27%, about 0.5 W of heat instead of 17 W.

The LDO still has a place: when $V_{in}$ is only slightly above $V_{out}$, at very light loads, or where you need low noise."
:::

::: technical
**LDO.** $I_{in}=I_{out}+I_q$. $P_{in}=V_{in}(I_{out}+I_q)$, $P_{out}=V_{out}I_{out}$.
$$\eta_{LDO}=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_q)}\approx\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}$$
$P_{diss}=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}+V_{in}I_q$. For 12 V to 3.3 V at 2 A: $\eta=27.5\%$, $P_{diss}=17.4$ W. With $\theta_{JA}=60$ °C/W the junction would rise by about 1000 °C, so a package-only LDO is physically impossible here.

**Buck (ideal).** $V_{out}=DV_{in}$ and, since $P_{in}=P_{out}$, $I_{in,avg}=D\,I_{out}=0.275\times2=0.55$ A. Power is converted by changing the voltage/current ratio, not by dissipating the difference.

**Buck (real).** $P_{loss}=P_{cond}+P_{sw}+P_{gate}+P_{DCR}+P_{core}+P_{Iq}+\dots$ ([[q0213]]). A typical 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, 500 kHz design has about 0.46 W of loss: $\eta\approx93.5\%$, $P_{in}=7.06$ W, $I_{in}=0.59$ A, loss $=0.46$ W (compare 17.4 W).

**Why an ideal switch is lossless.** $P=v\cdot i$ with either $v=0$ (on) or $i=0$ (off). Real switches dissipate $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ plus transition loss $\tfrac12VI(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}$, small compared with $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$.

**Crossovers.** The LDO is competitive when $V_{in}-V_{out}$ is small (efficiency $V_{out}/V_{in}\to$ 90%+), or when the load is so light that the buck's fixed losses (gate drive, quiescent current, switching) exceed the LDO's $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 93.5% buck figure is from a representative loss budget (see [[q0213]]). Real converters range from about 85 to 97% depending on $V_{in}$, load, frequency and component quality.
:::

::: short
"An LDO is a series resistor: all the output current passes through it and it burns $(V_{in}-V_{out})\times I_{out}$, so efficiency is about $V_{out}/V_{in}$, 27% for 12 V to 3.3 V. A buck uses switches that are either fully on or fully off, so they dissipate almost nothing, plus an inductor and capacitor that store and return energy instead of dissipating it. Power is conserved, so the input current drops to $D\cdot I_{out}$. Real bucks lose a few percent in resistance, switching and the inductor, so 90 to 95%."
:::

::: long
"Think about where the excess voltage goes. If I want 3.3 V from 12 V at 2 A, there is 8.7 V of difference that has to disappear somewhere.

In an LDO, it disappears across the pass transistor, which is acting like a variable resistor in series with the load. The full 2 A flows through it with 8.7 V across it, so it dissipates 17.4 W while delivering 6.6 W. Efficiency is $V_{out}/V_{in}$, about 27%, and the input current is the same 2 A as the output. In a real package that heat is impossible to manage without a big heatsink, which is why LDOs are used for small voltage drops.

A buck solves it by never putting voltage and current on the same device at the same time. The high-side switch is either fully on, so it has milliohms and a tiny voltage across it, or fully off, so it carries no current. Either way, $V\times I$ is almost zero. By switching at a duty cycle $D$ of 27.5%, it chops 12 V into pulses. The inductor and capacitor are the filter: the inductor stores energy while the switch is on and gives it to the load while the switch is off, and the capacitor smooths the voltage. Neither dissipates much, because ideal reactive elements don't.

So the energy balance is clean: power in equals power out. The input current is just $D$ times the output current, 0.55 A instead of 2 A.

In practice you have real losses: the on-resistance of the FETs and the inductor's DC resistance, the overlap during switching, the gate drive, core loss, and quiescent current. For a typical design they add up to something like 0.5 W, so about 93 to 94% efficient, instead of 27%.

The caveat is that the LDO wins when $V_{in}$ is just above $V_{out}$, in very light-load standby where the buck's fixed losses dominate, or when you need a quiet supply, so I'd often use a buck followed by an LDO for a noisy-sensitive rail."
:::

::: terms
- **Pass element**: the series transistor of an LDO.
- **Dropout voltage**: minimum $V_{in}-V_{out}$ for regulation.
- **Quiescent current ($I_q$)**: the regulator's own supply current.
- **Duty cycle ($D$)**: fraction of the period the high side conducts.
- **Linear vs switching regulator**: dissipating vs chopping and filtering.
- **Conduction loss / switching loss**: $I^2R$ and $V\,I$ overlap loss.
- **Energy conservation**: $P_{in}=P_{out}+P_{loss}$.
- **LC filter**: inductor plus capacitor that averages the switch-node pulses.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Bucks switch so they don't waste power; LDOs burn the difference as heat."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies both: 17.4 W vs about 0.46 W for 12 V to 3.3 V at 2 A.
- Explains *why* the switch is lossless ($V=0$ or $I=0$) and why the LC filter can average without dissipating.
- States the conservation consequence: $I_{in}=D\,I_{out}$ for the ideal buck versus $I_{in}=I_{out}$ for the LDO.
- Names the real buck losses by category and notes the typical 90 to 95%.
- Gives the crossover where an LDO wins (small headroom, light load, noise) and the usual buck-then-LDO architecture.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Bucks are more efficient because they're digital."** It is about switches being fully on or off, and reactive storage.
- **Quoting LDO efficiency as $V_{in}/V_{out}$.** It is $V_{out}/V_{in}$ (less than 1).
- **Saying the inductor dissipates the excess voltage.** An ideal inductor stores energy; real DCR loss is small.
- **Forgetting the buck is not 100% efficient.** Switching, conduction, gate-drive and core losses are real and dominate at light load.
- **Concluding bucks are always better.** At small headroom or microamp loads an LDO can win, and the LDO's output is much quieter.
:::

::: deep
## Follow the energy

Take $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I_{out}=2$ A. The load takes $P_{out}=6.6$ W.

**LDO.** The pass transistor sits between input and output carrying the *whole* load current. Everything not delivered becomes heat:

$$P_{in}=V_{in}I_{in}\approx12\times2=24\text{ W},\qquad P_{diss}=24-6.6=17.4\text{ W},\qquad\eta=27.5\%.$$

**Buck.** The switch chops. Energy is moved, not burned:

$$P_{in}=P_{out}+P_{loss}=6.6+0.46=7.06\text{ W},\qquad I_{in,avg}=\frac{7.06}{12}=0.59\text{ A}.$$

In the ideal limit $I_{in}=DI_{out}=0.55$ A. The input current is *lower* than the output current by the same ratio the voltage was reduced, just as in a transformer. That is the signature of a power converter instead of a dissipative regulator.

## Why a switch can be lossless

Instantaneous dissipation is $p(t)=v(t)\,i(t)$.

- **On**: $v\approx I\,R_{DS(on)}$ is tiny (20 mV for 2 A and 10 mΩ), so $p=I^2R_{DS(on)}=40$ mW.
- **Off**: $i\approx0$, so $p\approx0$.
- **Transition**: both $v$ and $i$ are nonzero for a few nanoseconds: $\tfrac12 V I\,(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}$, which is 60 mW for 12 V, 2 A, 10 ns, 500 kHz.

The LDO's pass device is *always* in the transition regime, with $v=V_{in}-V_{out}$ and $i=I_{out}$ simultaneously.

## Why the LC filter doesn't dissipate

An inductor obeys $v_L=L\,di/dt$. Over one cycle in steady state the average inductor voltage is zero, and the extra energy it absorbs while the switch is on (its current rises from the valley to the peak) is handed back to the load while the switch is off. The capacitor similarly stores and releases charge. Both are ideal energy buffers; their real dissipation is only the parasitic resistance ($I^2R$ in DCR and ESR).

## The real losses of the buck

For this 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, 500 kHz design ([[q0213]] has the itemization):

| Loss | Approx. | Share |
|---|---|---|
| FET conduction ($I^2R_{DS(on)}$) | 186 mW | 41% |
| Inductor (DCR and core) | 122 mW | 27% |
| Switching (overlap, $C_{oss}$, dead time, $Q_{rr}$) | 120 mW | 26% |
| Gate drive, capacitors, quiescent | 30 mW | 6% |
| **Total** | **about 460 mW** | **$\eta\approx93.5\%$** |

(Illustrative, from the model in [[q0213]].) Even if every one of these were three times worse, the buck would still waste about 1.4 W, one-twelfth of the LDO's loss.

## When the LDO wins

The efficiency of an LDO is $V_{out}/V_{in}$ (times the $I_q$ factor), so the gap narrows with headroom:

- **3.6 V in, 3.3 V out, 100 mA, $I_q=50$ µA**: LDO $\eta=91.6\%$.
- **Light load**: at 5 mA the buck's quiescent and switching losses may be several mW, comparable to the LDO's $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$.

## A practical consequence: thermal

The LDO's 17.4 W with a typical $\theta_{JA}$ of tens of °C/W would imply hundreds of degrees of rise, so a high-headroom LDO needs a heatsink, derating or a prior buck stage. The buck's 0.46 W with $\theta_{JA}=40$ to 60 °C/W gives 18 to 28 °C of rise (spread over several parts), easily handled on a board.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A one-line summary interviewers like: "The LDO burns the headroom; the buck moves it as energy through an inductor." Follow with the 12 V to 3.3 V numbers.

## The honest caveats

Bucks need an inductor (area, cost), produce ripple and EMI, need a compensated loop and have worse transient behavior than LDOs. An LDO has better noise, PSRR at high frequency and simplicity ([[q0157]]). Many designs combine both: a buck to an intermediate voltage with small headroom, then an LDO for the noise-sensitive load, gaining efficiency and cleanliness.

## Worked example: what it means for a battery, and for the heat budget

Efficiency percentages feel abstract until you turn them into hours and degrees. Use the same 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A rail, but feed it from a 3S Li-ion pack: 11.1 V nominal and 2.5 Ah, so 27.75 Wh of stored energy. Ignore the pack's voltage droop and cutoff, which matter in reality but do not change the comparison.

| Regulator | Input power | Run time | Heat |
|---|---|---|---|
| LDO ($\eta=3.3/11.1=29.7\%$) | $11.1\ \text{V}\times2\ \text{A}=22.2$ W | $27.75/22.2=1.25$ h | 15.6 W |
| Buck ($\eta=93.5\%$) | $6.6/0.935=7.06$ W | $27.75/7.06=3.93$ h | 0.46 W |

The buck runs the same load about 3.1 times longer on the same pack, and it does it without a heatsink. Note that the LDO input current is the full 2 A, but the buck draws only about 0.6 A from the pack, which means thinner wires and lower $I^2R$ in the cabling and connector too.

**Heat budget as a design check.** For the LDO, the largest dissipation a package can tolerate is $P_{max}=(T_{J,max}-T_A)/\theta_{JA}$. With an illustrative 40 °C/W, 125 °C junction limit and 50 °C ambient, that is $75/40=1.9$ W. At the 12 V rail's 8.7 V of headroom the LDO can then supply only $1.9/8.7=0.22$ A, or about a tenth of the 2 A load. This is the quickest way to rule an LDO in or out: compute $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$ and compare it with what the package can shed.

## Worked example: buck then LDO, and what it costs

The honest answer to "what about noise?" is often a cascade. Put a buck from 12 V down to 3.8 V at an assumed 93% efficiency (close to the 93.5% above), then an LDO from 3.8 V to 3.3 V:

$$P_{in}=\frac{3.8\times2}{0.93}=8.17\ \text{W},\qquad \eta_{total}=\frac{6.6}{8.17}=80.8\%,$$

with 1.0 W lost in the LDO ($0.5\ \text{V}\times2\ \text{A}$) and 0.57 W in the buck. That is 1.6 W total instead of 0.46 W for a direct buck, but the rail now has the LDO's quiet output and strong high-frequency rejection of the buck's switching ripple. If the load is a precision ADC or a PLL, those 1.1 W may be well spent. If it is a motor or a digital core, they are not. The 0.5 V headroom is chosen to be enough for the LDO's dropout at 2 A (check the datasheet) and to leave margin for the buck's own ripple.

## Limiting cases: when the ordering flips

The LDO efficiency, including its ground-pin current, is

$$\eta_{LDO}=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}\cdot\frac{I_{out}}{I_{out}+I_q}.$$

With $V_{in}=3.6$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I_{out}=100$ mA and $I_q=50\ \mu$A this is $0.917\times0.9995=91.6\%$. Few bucks beat that at 100 mA because their fixed switching and gate-drive losses are a larger fraction of a 330 mW load, so the LDO is a perfectly good choice. A rough crossover: if a buck manages 90% at your load, the LDO wins whenever $V_{in}<3.3/0.9=3.7$ V. Below that the LDO is not just simpler, it is more efficient.

At the other extreme, a 10 mA load on the 12 V rail makes the LDO draw about 120 mW to deliver 33 mW (27% efficient). A buck with a light-load mode might reach 85% there, drawing roughly 39 mW. The absolute saving is only 80 mW, but on a coin-cell or energy-harvesting design that difference sets battery life, and it is why LDO versus buck is decided by headroom and by quiescent current, not by output current alone.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 85% light-load efficiency of a buck and the 50 µA quiescent current of an LDO are typical orders of magnitude, not specific parts. Light-load efficiency in particular varies from under 60% to over 90% between regulators, and datasheets plot it explicitly.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Measure input power, not just output.** With a bench supply, read the supply's current display or a series DMM while the load is steady. For the LDO the input current should be almost equal to the output current. For the buck it should be near $D\,I_{out}\approx0.6$ A. If you see 2 A into a "buck", it is bypassing or you are measuring the wrong thing.
2. **Be careful with a DMM on a buck input.** The current is pulsed and the meter's burden voltage plus the wire inductance can disturb the converter. Put a large capacitor right at the converter so the meter sees a smooth average, or use the supply's own readout.
3. **Find the heat.** Run each regulator at full load for ten minutes with a thermal camera or thermocouple. The LDO's pass device (or the whole package) will show its heat problem within seconds, so stop early and be ready with a heatsink or a current limit.
4. **Plot efficiency against load.** At 10 mA, 100 mA and 1 A, with the same $V_{in}$ and $V_{out}$. This gives you the crossover for your specific parts.

::: applications
- **12 V to 3.3 V or 5 V point-of-load rails** on FPGA and embedded boards: buck converters at 90 to 95%.
- **Battery-powered devices (Li-ion 3.0 to 4.2 V to 1.8 V)**: buck converters to extend battery life where LDO efficiency would be 43 to 60%.
- **USB-C power supplies and chargers**: DC-DC stages in the 90 to 98% range.
- **Server and PC voltage regulators**: multiphase bucks delivering hundreds of amps at about 1 V.
- **Buck plus post-LDO** for ADC/PLL rails: efficiency from the buck, low noise from the LDO.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Fixed-frequency, continuous-conduction buck** with synchronous rectification at moderate load; light-load efficiency is lower and depends on the PFM scheme.
- **Standard LDO** with $I_{in}\approx I_{out}$; some "linear" regulators use charge pumps or switched pre-regulators.
- **Loss numbers** from a representative design, not from a particular part.
- **Same output current** and **steady state**; transient loss is not considered.
- If asked about **efficiency at very low $V_{in}-V_{out}$**, the LDO can meet or beat the buck; the answer changes to "depends on headroom and load".
:::

::: followups
### What is the maximum efficiency of an LDO with a 5 V input and a 3.3 V output?
$\eta\le V_{out}/V_{in}=3.3/5=66\%$, less any quiescent-current term. At 2 A that is 3.4 W dissipated. At $V_{in}=3.6$ V and $V_{out}=3.3$ V it is 91.7% before $I_q$.

### Where is the buck's loss, and what usually dominates?
Conduction in the FETs and inductor at heavy load; switching, gate drive and quiescent current at light load. For the example: about 70% of the loss is $I^2R$ in the FETs and inductor and about 26% is switching related ([[q0217]]).

### If the buck is so efficient, why not use it for everything?
The inductor adds cost and area, the ripple and EMI need filtering, the transient response is limited by the control loop, and the output is noisier than an LDO. For microamp loads or noise-sensitive rails with small headroom, an LDO is smaller, simpler and sometimes more efficient.

### What happens to buck efficiency as $V_{in}$ rises?
Switching loss ($\tfrac12VIt\,f$, $C_{oss}V^2f$, $Q_{rr}V_{in}f$) grows with $V_{in}$, and the ripple current increases, so efficiency drops by a percent or two between 12 V and 24 V for the same output. The LDO's efficiency collapses faster ($V_{out}/V_{in}$).

### A designer proposes an LDO from 12 V to 3.3 V at 500 mA. Reasonable?
Loss is $8.7\times0.5=4.35$ W: at $\theta_{JA}=40$ °C/W that is 174 °C of rise, not feasible without a heatsink. A 12 V to 5 V buck then LDO to 3.3 V, or a direct buck, is the right architecture.
:::
