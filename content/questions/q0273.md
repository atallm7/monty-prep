::: basics
### In plain words
You use a boost converter whenever your source voltage can be lower than the voltage your load needs. Common cases are one lithium cell making 5 V, two AA cells making 3.3 V, LED strings, and solar or car systems. Large boosts also shape the current drawn from the mains in power factor correction. The price is that the source must supply more current than the load takes.

### Picture it
Think of a seesaw. A boost trades current for voltage: if the output voltage goes up, the input current must go up too, so that power stays about the same. A short, strong push on one side and a long, light push on the other balance out. The seesaw misses the losses, since a real converter wastes a little power as heat.

### Words you need
- **Boost ratio**: the output voltage divided by the input voltage.
- **Input current**: the current drawn from the source, about $I_{out}/(1-D)$ ([[q0270]]).
- **Li-ion cell**: a battery cell that ranges from about 3.0 V (empty) to 4.2 V (full).
- **Power factor correction (PFC)**: a boost stage that makes mains input current follow the voltage waveform.
- **Minimum input voltage**: the worst case for a boost, so you design for it.

### The simplest example
A Li-ion cell at 3.0 V feeds a 5 V, 1 A load, which is 5 W. For the ideal case, $D=1-3.0/5=0.4$. If the converter is 90% efficient, the input power is $5/0.9\approx5.6$ W, so the cell supplies $5.6/3.0\approx1.85$ A. At a full 4.2 V cell the input current falls to about 1.3 A.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page goes through a table of applications and what drives each design, including PFC, solar, and automotive cases.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can connect a circuit topology to **the real constraint that forces it into a product**. "LED drivers and batteries" is the floor. The interviewer wants to hear *why* a boost fits (source below load voltage), what the design pressure is in each case (efficiency, quiescent current, input current, cost), and that you know when a boost is the wrong answer. It also tests whether you can recall real products and not just generic categories.
:::

::: why
In an internship you will not design a boost from a blank sheet. You will pick a boost IC for a subsystem, check that it survives the worst-case battery voltage, and explain why it is there. Knowing the common use cases tells you which numbers to ask about first: minimum input voltage, maximum load current, standby current, ripple. It also answers the follow-up that usually comes next, [[q0161]]: if step-down regulators dominate embedded systems, where do step-ups still win?
:::

::: concepts
- **Gain** $V_{out}/V_{in}=1/(1-D)$ means a boost fits when the source is *below* the required rail. See [[q0270]].
- **Input current** $I_{in}=I_{out}/(\eta(1-D))$: the source must tolerate it, especially at low $V_{in}$.
- **Battery discharge curve**: a cell's voltage falls as it empties, so the boost ratio and input current grow at the end of discharge. See [[q0297]].
- **Efficiency and quiescent current**: standby current matters for battery life. See [[q0162]] and [[q0165]].
- **Power factor correction**: shaping line current to follow line voltage. A boost is the usual front end.
- **Why step-down dominates**: most sources (adapters, batteries at full charge) are above the load rails. See [[q0161]].
:::

Solar systems use boost stages to lift a low panel voltage and track its maximum power point, and cars use them to keep rails alive when the battery sags during cranking. Then there are small high-voltage bias supplies: OLED displays, e-paper, flash units, sensor bias.

::: technical
A boost is chosen when $V_{in,min}<V_{out}$. Design drivers per application:

| Application | Typical $V_{in}$ | $V_{out}$ | Main design driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Li-ion to 5 V (power bank, speaker) | 3.0 to 4.2 V | 5 V | Efficiency at 1 to 3 A, thermal, input current at 3.0 V |
| 2xAA to 3.3 V MCU | 1.8 to 3.2 V | 3.3 V | Startup at low $V_{in}$, quiescent current, light-load efficiency |
| White-LED string | 2.7 to 4.2 V | 10 to 30 V | Constant-current regulation, high $D$ |
| PFC front end | 90 to 265 Vac (rectified) | about 400 V | Line-current shaping, EMI, efficiency at kW |
| Solar / MPPT | 5 to 40 V | 12 to 48 V | Tracking the maximum power point |
| Automotive cold-crank | 3 to 6 V (dip) | 5 V | Wide $V_{in}$, load dump protection |
| Display/OLED/e-paper bias | 3 to 4.2 V | 15 to 40 V (flash at about 300 V uses a flyback) | High ratio, small power |

**Ratios.** Single-cell to 5 V needs $D=1-V_{in}/5$: 0.40 at 3.0 V and 0.16 at 4.2 V (ideal). Input current at 5 V, 1 A, $\eta=0.9$: $5/(0.9V_{in})$, so 1.85 A at 3.0 V and 1.32 A at 4.2 V.

**PFC.** With the bus at 400 V and the rectified peak at $230\sqrt2=325$ V, ideal $D=1-325/400=0.19$ at the line peak, rising to 1 near the zero crossing, so the converter modulates $D$ across the half-cycle. A boost can only work if the bus is above the peak: 265 Vac peaks at 375 V, which is why 400 V is used.

**Limits.** Beyond about 5 to 10x ratio, parasitic losses make a plain boost impractical. Use a coupled-inductor boost, flyback, or a cascade. A boost cannot regulate when $V_{in}\ge V_{out}$ ([[q0270]]), so for a cell that straddles the output voltage use buck-boost.
:::

::: long
"A boost shows up wherever the source voltage is lower than what the load needs, which is a very common situation in anything battery powered.

The classic one is a single Li-ion cell, 3.0 to 4.2 V, making 5 V: power banks, Bluetooth speakers, handheld gadgets. At 1 A out and 90% efficiency, the cell has to supply about 1.85 A at 3.0 V, so the main design checks are efficiency, inductor saturation current and what the weak cell can supply at the end of discharge.

A second is low-voltage cells. Two AAs run between about 3.2 V fresh and 1.8 V depleted, feeding a 3.3 V microcontroller. Here startup at low input and tiny quiescent current matter more than peak efficiency.

Third are LED and display drivers. A string of six white LEDs is around 18 V, and a boost with current feedback regulates the LED current from one cell. Same idea for OLED and e-paper bias, flash and photo-multiplier supplies.

At the high-power end, boosts are the standard power factor correction stage. The rectified mains peaks at 325 V for 230 V rms, so a boost holds the bus at about 400 V and controls its input current to look like a sine wave in phase with the voltage. Solar charge controllers use boosts as well, because the converter can vary the panel's operating voltage while holding the output.

Lastly, automotive: during engine crank the battery can drop to 3 to 6 V, and a boost keeps 5 V and 3.3 V rails alive.

The constraint to name is input current: it rises as $1/(1-D)$, and it is worst at low $V_{in}$ and high load."
:::

::: terms
- **Step-up conversion**: raising a DC voltage with a switching converter.
- **Power factor correction (PFC)**: forcing input current to be sinusoidal and in phase with line voltage.
- **MPPT (maximum power point tracking)**: adjusting the converter's operating point to extract maximum power from a solar panel.
- **Cold crank**: the voltage dip on a car battery while the starter motor runs.
- **Constant-current LED driver**: a boost that regulates LED current (sensed with a resistor) instead of voltage.
- **Quiescent current ($I_Q$)**: current the converter draws with no load. Critical for battery life ([[q0165]]).
- **Pass-through / true shutdown**: whether the output is disconnected when the converter is off.
- **Boost ratio**: $V_{out}/V_{in}$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "LED drivers, batteries, solar".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Ties each application to the *constraint* that makes a boost the right tool.
- Gives numbers: Li-ion 3.0 to 4.2 V to 5 V, PFC bus about 400 V from a 325 V peak.
- Mentions the input-current penalty and worst-case at low battery.
- Distinguishes boost from buck-boost when $V_{in}$ straddles $V_{out}$ (a Li-ion cell making 3.3 V).
- Knows when *not* to use a boost: when the ratio is huge (use flyback or transformer), when the load must be isolated from the source, or when a load switch/true shutdown is needed.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Listing applications without the "why".** The interviewer will ask what makes a boost better than a charge pump or a series battery stack.
- **Ignoring the input side.** The battery current is higher than the load current. Many power-bank failures are weak or hot cells and undersized inductors at low state of charge, not the converter itself.
- **Forgetting the input can exceed the output.** A fresh 4.2 V cell feeding a 3.3 V rail through a plain boost gives about 3.8 V at the output (pass-through).
- **Claiming boosts are the best way to get a big step-up.** At ratios above roughly 5 to 10x, a flyback, coupled-inductor boost or a multi-stage approach is usually better.
- **Calling a PFC a "boost for efficiency".** The *purpose* is power factor and harmonic limits, with the boost being a convenient topology since the output is above the input peak.
- **No mention of load disconnect.** For battery products with long standby, you need to choose a part with true output disconnect or add a load switch.
:::

::: deep
## The unifying idea: the load rail is above the source

A boost is an answer to a mismatch problem: you have a source, you have a rail requirement, and the source is lower. Before reaching for a boost, the alternatives are worth knowing:

| Alternative | When it beats a boost |
|---|---|
| Stack more cells in series | If the battery pack has room. Series packs avoid the conversion loss entirely (and the high input current). |
| Charge pump ([[q0284]]) | Small power (tens of mA), tight space, no inductor, EMI sensitive. |
| Flyback / transformer | Large ratio or isolation required. |
| Buck-boost / SEPIC | $V_{in}$ crosses $V_{out}$ during operation. |
| Do nothing | If the load can run from the battery voltage directly. |

The boost wins in the middle: moderate ratio (1.2x to 5x), currents from tens of mA to many amps, and no isolation needed.

## Application 1: single-cell lithium to 5 V

The battery voltage during discharge is roughly 4.2 V full to 3.0 V empty ([[q0297]]). The converter has to work across that whole range, so the design is done at the *worst corner*, which is low $V_{in}$ and high load:

The inductor must not saturate at the worst-case peak, and the cell's internal resistance drops $V_{in}$ further under that current, so real designs look at the *loaded* battery voltage, not the open-circuit value.

Real power banks add a synchronous rectifier to remove the diode loss (about 0.4 W at 1 A, 8% of the 5 W output), light-load modes for standby, and input current limiting ([[q0272]]).

## Application 2: low-voltage cells to a microcontroller rail

Two alkaline AAs give about 3.2 V when fresh and fall to roughly 1.8 V near the end of useful life, with the voltage sagging further under load. A 3.3 V MCU needs a boost at the low end ($D=1-1.8/3.3=0.45$) and pass-through at the top (3.2 V is already close to 3.3 V). Here the two critical specs are **minimum start-up voltage** (some parts need 0.8 V or less, others 2.5 V) and **quiescent current** (a boost that idles at 20 µA uses $20\ \mu\text{A}\times8760\text{ h}=0.175$ Ah per year, about 7% of a 2.5 Ah pair, so what matters is how it sleeps), plus efficiency at light load because a sensor node sleeps nearly all the time. This is why low-power boost ICs use pulse-skipping or burst modes ([[q0230]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Minimum start-up voltages and quiescent currents vary widely between products (from well below 1 V start-up on specialised parts to 2.5 V or more on general ones). Always check the specific datasheet.

## Application 3: LED backlights and strings

A white LED has $V_F\approx3.0$ to 3.4 V. Six in series is about 18.6 V at 20 mA, which is only 0.37 W. From a 3.7 V cell the boost needs $D=1-3.7/19.1=0.81$ (with a small sense-resistor drop and about 0.5 V headroom for the current sink, assumed). The boost regulates the *current* (feedback from a sense resistor) rather than the voltage. The input current is $0.372/(3.7\times0.88)\approx0.114$ A: small, but at 81% duty the converter is near its limits, so efficiency is only 85 to 90% and a controller with a high maximum duty cycle is needed. Series strings are used because one current regulator controls all LEDs, so they match in brightness.

## Application 4: PFC from the mains

The rectified line voltage is $|v_{ac}|$, which is between 0 and its peak ($90\sqrt2=127$ V to $265\sqrt2=375$ V for universal input). A boost converter with output **above the peak** can draw current at any instant, since $V_{out}>v_{in}$ always. The controller makes the inductor (input) current follow a sinusoidal reference scaled to the rectified voltage, so the line sees a nearly resistive load. At 300 W, the rms line current is $300/(90\times0.95)=3.5$ A at 90 Vac (peak 4.96 A) and $300/(230\times0.95)=1.37$ A at 230 Vac. The usual bus voltage is about 380 to 400 V, comfortable above the 375 V peak. The reasons: regulatory harmonic limits, and better use of the mains outlet's current capacity.

## Application 5: solar and energy harvesting

A solar panel's output power peaks at one voltage that depends on light and temperature. A boost lets the controller choose the *input* voltage (by varying $D$) while the output holds the battery or bus. Maximum power point tracking perturbs $D$ and watches the power. Boost is natural when the panel voltage is below the battery bus (for example an 18 V panel to charge a 24 V battery bank: $D=1-18/24=0.25$).

## Application 6: very high ratio, low power

Camera flash capacitors (a few hundred volts), photomultiplier bias, e-paper and small OLED displays are all high-ratio, low-power loads. For 3.7 V to 300 V the ideal duty cycle is $0.988$: a plain boost cannot do that with any efficiency, so these use a **flyback with a transformer** (turns ratio supplies most of the gain). It is good to say this in an interview because it shows you know where the topology ends.

::: applications
- **Power-bank and USB-C charger ICs**: synchronous boost from the cell to a 5 V or higher bus.
- **Smart-watch and wearable sensors**: nano-power boost with load disconnect and burst-mode light-load operation.
- **Display backlights** in phones and e-readers: constant-current LED boost.
- **PFC pre-regulators** in computer, TV and LED-driver supplies (typically 75 W and above, depending on regulatory class).
- **Solar chargers and MPPT stages**: boost between panel and battery.
- **Automotive start-stop**: a boost holds the infotainment and cluster rails through crank dips.

::: assumptions
- Application figures are **typical** values to illustrate magnitudes. Real parts have wider operating ranges. Datasheet start-up voltages, quiescent currents and maximum duty cycles differ by vendor.
- "Boost" here means an **inductor-based non-isolated** step-up. Where isolation or very high ratio is needed, other topologies (flyback, forward, push-pull) take over.
- For battery chemistry numbers I assumed a **single Li-ion cell** (4.2 V full, about 3.0 V empty) and **alkaline** cells (about 1.5 V to 0.9 V per cell). Real values depend on load and temperature.
- A valid alternative answer is that many designs *avoid* boost converters altogether by using a higher-voltage battery stack and a buck, to save the input-current penalty. See [[q0161]].
:::

::: followups
### Why is a boost used for PFC rather than a buck?
A buck can only draw current when the input voltage is above the output voltage, so a buck from a rectified sine would have no input current near the zero crossings and distort the line current. A boost output is above the peak line voltage, so it can draw current at every instant and be forced to follow a sine. Also, the boost has the inductor on the input side, so the input current is naturally smooth and easy to control.

### How do you pick a boost IC for a two-AA application?
Start with what limits it: minimum start-up voltage (check it against the lowest battery voltage under load, near 1.8 V or less), quiescent current (microamps for a sleeping node), switch current limit against $I_{in}=I_{out}/(\eta(1-D))$ at 1.8 V, and whether the output disconnects in shutdown. Then check light-load efficiency: PFM or burst mode is needed for sensor nodes that sleep most of the time.

### A customer wants to power a 12 V, 2 A load from one Li-ion cell. Is a plain boost fine?
It's 24 W from 3.0 V: ideal $D=0.75$, input current about $24/(0.85\times3.0)=9.4$ A. That is brutal for a single cell and a standard boost IC. Ask whether the system can use 2 or 3 cells in series, which cuts the ratio and current. If it must be one cell, expect a multi-phase or high-current boost with a very good inductor and low $R_{DS(on)}$, and check that the cell can supply 9+ A at 3.0 V.

### Why not use a charge pump for a USB 5 V boost?
Charge pumps give fixed ratios (1x, 1.5x, 2x), lose efficiency when $V_{in}\cdot$ratio differs from the target, and have a high output resistance, so at 1 A the droop and loss are large. They are best at tens to a few hundred mA ([[q0287]]). An inductor-based boost gives a fully regulated output at higher current with better efficiency.

### Scenario: a battery-powered sensor lasts half as long as predicted. The boost is the only thing on the board with an obvious change. Where do you look?
Standby draw. Measure total current in sleep: the boost's quiescent current, the output-disconnect behavior (a pass-through boost keeps the load rail up and powers leakage paths), and whether the converter runs in PFM or forced PWM at light load. A device that burns 100 µA in sleep from the boost instead of 5 µA could cut battery life by up to 20x if the boost dominates the sleep budget. Also check whether the load needs the rail at all during sleep.
:::
