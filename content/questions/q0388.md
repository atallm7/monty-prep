::: testing
Whether you can turn a mechanical requirement into a motor choice with numbers. The interviewer expects you to ask what the load is (torque, inertia, speed, duty cycle), compute the peak and RMS torque, check against the torque-speed curve, choose a gear ratio and check inertia matching, then handle supply voltage, current, feedback resolution, environment and cost. Candidates who only say "look at the torque and speed" miss peak versus continuous torque, thermal limits and gear reflection.
:::

::: why
Under-specifying gives a motor that overheats or can't accelerate the load; over-specifying wastes cost, weight and power. In practice this exact calculation (a motion profile reduced to peak and RMS torque, then matched to a motor and gearbox) is what mechatronics and robotics engineers do daily, and a clean worked example in an interview signals that you can do it. It also shows the link to the supply, the driver and the control loop.
:::

::: concepts
- **Torque, speed, power**: $P = \tau\omega$; units and conversions (kg·cm, N·m, rpm, rad/s).
- **Continuous vs peak torque**: thermal vs magnetic/current limits. See [[q0386]].
- **Load inertia and acceleration**: $\tau = J\alpha$.
- **Gear ratio and reflected inertia**: $J_{refl} = J_L/N^2$.
- **Motion profile**: triangular or trapezoidal speed vs time.
- **Stall current and back-EMF**: sets driver and supply size. See [[q0336]] and [[q0341]].
- **Feedback resolution and loop bandwidth**: see [[q0389]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd start from the load and the motion, not from a catalogue. First, what does the mechanism need: how much torque to hold, how much to accelerate, what top speed, how often, and how accurate. From the motion profile I compute peak torque, which is load inertia times acceleration plus friction and gravity, and RMS torque over the cycle, which tells me the thermal duty.

Then I pick a gear ratio. The motor wants to run fast and light, the load wants slow and strong, and reflected inertia drops with the square of the ratio, so a gearbox helps the motor control the load. I check the motor's torque-speed curve covers every point of the profile, with the peak in the intermittent region and the RMS under the continuous rating, with margin of 1.5 to 2.

Then the electrical side: supply voltage must be high enough for the top speed plus back-EMF, the driver must deliver peak current, and the supply has to cover stall. The feedback needs enough resolution for the accuracy I want, and then the practical stuff: size, mounting, bearings and shaft loads, temperature and ingress, connector, and cost. For a hobby servo it's a simpler version: torque in kg·cm with margin, speed in seconds per 60 degrees, voltage and stall current."
:::

::: technical
**1. Motion profile.** For a move $\Delta\theta$ in time $t$ with a symmetric triangular velocity profile: $\alpha = 4\Delta\theta/t^2$ and $\omega_{pk} = \alpha t/2$. Example: $\Delta\theta = 90^\circ = \pi/2$ rad in $t = 0.3$ s gives $\alpha = 69.8$ rad/s² and $\omega_{pk} = 10.47$ rad/s = 100 rpm.

**2. Torque.** Peak accelerating torque $\tau_{pk} = J_L\alpha + \tau_{fric} + \tau_{grav}$. With $J_L = 0.02$ kg·m² and 0.1 N·m friction: $\tau_{acc} = 0.02\times69.8 + 0.1 = 1.50$ N·m; deceleration $-1.30$ N·m (friction helps).

**3. RMS torque** over a 1.0 s cycle (0.15 s accel, 0.15 s decel, 0.7 s dwell with no load torque):
$$\tau_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum \tau_i^2 t_i}{T}} = \sqrt{\frac{1.50^2(0.15)+1.30^2(0.15)}{1.0}} = 0.77\ \text{N·m}$$
Compare with continuous rated torque; peak with intermittent (peak) torque at the speed.

**4. Power.** $P_{pk} = \tau\omega = 1.50\times10.47 \approx 15.7$ W at the end of acceleration. Mechanical power sizes the electrical supply after dividing by motor and drive efficiency.

**5. Gearing.** With ratio $N$: motor sees torque $\tau/(\eta N)$, speed $N\omega$, and inertia $J_L/N^2$. Minimum motor torque for acceleration is achieved when reflected load inertia equals motor inertia: $N_{opt} = \sqrt{J_L/J_m}$. With $J_m = 10^{-5}$ kg·m² that is $N = 45$, though practical servos run with inertia ratio $J_L/N^2 : J_m$ somewhere up to a few to 10 depending on the application (vendor guidance varies). A 10:1 gearbox here makes the motor see 0.15 N·m at 1000 rpm and a load inertia ratio of 20:1.

**6. Electrical.** Required voltage $V \ge I R + K_e N \omega_{out}$; $I = \tau_{m}/K_t$. Choose supply with headroom (typically 20–30%), and driver peak current above $\tau_{pk,m}/K_t$. Stall current $V/R$ sizes the supply and protection.

**7. Feedback and accuracy.** Required position resolution and repeatability set the encoder: $\text{counts/rev} \ge 360^\circ/\text{resolution}$, divided by the gear ratio if the encoder is on the motor shaft.

**8. Hobby servo version.** Torque (kg·cm, at the working voltage) at least 2× static need; speed in s/60° at that voltage; stall current; weight; gear material (plastic, metal); bearings; range; analog or digital.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Inertia-ratio guidelines (for example 3:1 to 10:1 for precise servo motion) and safety margins are rules of thumb that vary by vendor and application. Check the drive and motor maker's sizing guidance.
:::

::: short
"I'd work from the load: compute the motion profile, then peak torque as inertia times acceleration plus friction and gravity, and RMS torque for thermal duty. Pick a gear ratio, remembering reflected inertia drops as N squared. Check the motor's torque-speed curve covers the peak and the RMS is below continuous with margin. Then supply voltage and current, feedback resolution, size and environment. For a hobby servo it's torque in kg·cm with 2x margin, speed in s per 60 degrees, and voltage."
:::

::: long
"I'll work from the application inward.

First the requirements: what moves, how far, how fast, how often, how accurately, and what is the load: mass, inertia, friction, gravity. Say a 90 degree move in 0.3 s with a load inertia of 0.02 kg·m². With a triangular velocity profile the acceleration is $4\Delta\theta/t^2$, about 70 rad/s², and the peak speed is about 100 rpm.

Second, torque. Peak torque is $J\alpha$ plus friction and gravity: 0.02 times 70 is 1.4, plus 0.1 friction, so 1.5 N·m for the acceleration. Then RMS torque over the whole cycle, including dwell, because heating follows RMS: here about 0.77 N·m for a one second cycle. The continuous rating must exceed that, and the peak rating must exceed 1.5 at that speed, with margin of at least 1.5 to 2.

Third, gearing. I choose a ratio so the motor runs near its sweet-spot speed, and so reflected inertia, load inertia over N squared, is not too many multiples of the rotor's own inertia, otherwise the loop is sluggish. A 10:1 gearbox would give motor-side torque of 0.15 N·m at 1000 rpm.

Fourth, the electrical side: the supply voltage has to cover the resistive drop plus back-EMF at top speed, the driver has to deliver peak current, and the supply must handle stall.

Fifth, feedback: encoder counts per revolution for the resolution I need, divided down through the gearbox.

Finally the boring but crucial things: size, shaft loads, temperature, ingress protection, cost, and what happens on failure. For a hobby servo it's the same logic in simpler units: torque in kg·cm with 2x margin, speed in seconds per 60 degrees, voltage and stall current."
:::

::: terms
- **Peak torque**: maximum short-term torque, limited by current and magnetics.
- **Continuous (rated) torque**: torque sustainable indefinitely without overheating.
- **RMS torque**: root-mean-square over the duty cycle; the thermal metric.
- **Reflected inertia**: load inertia as seen at the motor, $J_L/N^2$.
- **Inertia ratio**: reflected load inertia to rotor inertia.
- **Torque constant $K_t$**: N·m per amp.
- **Back-EMF constant $K_e$**: volts per rad/s.
- **Duty cycle**: fraction of the time the motor is actively loaded.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer produces numbers: the profile, peak torque, RMS torque, peak power and the gear ratio check. It distinguishes peak, continuous and stall torque, explains reflected inertia and the $N^2$ effect, checks the supply voltage against back-EMF at top speed and not just torque, mentions encoder resolution through the gearbox, and lists non-electrical requirements (shaft load, IP rating, temperature, brake). It also builds in margin explicitly and says what would change the choice.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Sizing on stall torque.** It is available only at zero speed and a short time; the working point is on the torque-speed curve.
- **Ignoring RMS torque.** A motor can meet the peak requirement and still cook on duty cycle.
- **Forgetting reflected inertia.** Gearbox ratio changes inertia by $N^2$, which strongly affects the response.
- **Neglecting back-EMF at top speed.** Supply voltage may be too low to reach the required speed under load.
- **Mixing units.** kg·cm, oz·in and N·m are easy to confuse; 1 kg·cm = 0.0981 N·m.
- **Leaving out gearbox efficiency and backlash.** Both affect torque and precision.
:::

::: deep
## Start with the profile

Every servo selection begins with a motion profile, because torque comes from acceleration, not speed. For a symmetric triangular move of $\Delta\theta$ in time $t$:

$$\alpha = \frac{4\,\Delta\theta}{t^2},\qquad \omega_{pk} = \frac{\alpha t}{2} = \frac{2\Delta\theta}{t}$$

For $\Delta\theta = \pi/2$ rad and $t = 0.3$ s, $\alpha = 69.8$ rad/s², $\omega_{pk} = 10.47$ rad/s (100 rpm).

## RMS and thermal

Motor temperature rise follows $I^2R$ with a thermal time constant of minutes. So the continuous rating is compared against RMS torque (which is proportional to RMS current):

$$\tau_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \tau^2\,dt}$$

For the example: $\sqrt{(1.50^2\times0.15 + 1.30^2\times0.15)/1.0} = 0.77$ N·m. If the cycle were repeated back to back with no dwell (cycle 0.3 s), $\tau_{rms}$ would be $\sqrt{(1.50^2+1.30^2)/2} = 1.40$ N·m, nearly double. Duty cycle is a design variable.

## Choosing the gear ratio

Everything on the load side reflects to the motor shaft through the gearbox: speed up by $N$, torque down by $N$ (and efficiency $\eta$), inertia down by $N^2$.

$$\tau_m = \frac{\tau_L}{\eta N}+ J_m\alpha_m,\qquad \alpha_m = N\alpha_L,\qquad J_{refl} = \frac{J_L}{N^2}$$

Total torque the motor must supply during acceleration is $(J_m + J_L/N^2)N\alpha_L$. Setting its derivative with respect to $N$ to zero gives the minimum at $J_m = J_L/N^2$, i.e. $N_{opt} = \sqrt{J_L/J_m}$. With $J_L = 0.02$ and $J_m = 10^{-5}$ kg·m² that's $N = 44.7$. In practice you also need to keep motor speed within its limit: $N\times100$ rpm = 4470 rpm, which is fine for many small servo motors, and you have to accept backlash and gearbox efficiency.

At $N=10$: motor torque about 0.15 N·m (plus its own rotor inertia, ignored here) at 1000 rpm; reflected inertia $0.02/100 = 2\times10^{-4}$ kg·m², an inertia ratio of 20:1 versus a rotor of $10^{-5}$ kg·m². Many drives work comfortably at 10:1 or lower for fast, precise motion, so you might choose a higher ratio or a motor with more inertia.

Voltage check at the motor: $V \ge I R + K_e \omega_m$. For $\tau_m = 0.15$ N·m, $K_t = K_e = 0.04$ (SI units): $I = 3.7$ A. At $\omega_m = 105$ rad/s (1000 rpm) back-EMF is 4.2 V, resistive drop 4.5 V, so a 24 V supply is ample; at 6000 rpm back-EMF alone would be 25 V and 24 V would be insufficient. So speed, not torque, can be what limits you.

## Electrical sizing

- **Supply voltage**: top-speed back-EMF plus IR plus 20–30% headroom.
- **Peak current**: $\tau_{pk,m}/K_t$; the drive's peak current rating must cover it.
- **Supply current**: average power over efficiency, plus inrush and regeneration handling. A servo decelerating a large inertia pumps energy back into the bus, which may need a brake resistor or capacitance.
- **Stall**: protect for $V/R$ if the load jams.

## Feedback

If the encoder is on the motor shaft, output resolution is counts per motor rev times $N$. A 1000-line encoder with 4× decoding is 4000 counts per rev, and through a 10:1 gearbox 40,000 counts per output rev (0.009°). Accuracy is then dominated by backlash and compliance, not resolution ([[q0389]]).

## Non-electrical specs

Shaft radial and axial load ratings, bearing life, backlash for the gearbox, mounting flange, brake need for vertical axes, ingress protection and operating temperature, noise, and connector. For hobby servos: gear material (plastic, composite, metal), bearings versus bushings, case size, rotation range, and digital versus analog.

## Hobby servo spec example

A joint needs 1.9 N·m static torque: 19.5 kg·cm. At 2× margin pick a servo with at least 39 kg·cm at the supply voltage you will actually use. Check speed: 0.12 s per 60° is 500°/s (83 rpm), enough for a 90° move in about 0.2 s with no load, but slower under load. Check supply: stall current may be 2–3 A for such a servo; the supply and wiring must handle it. Check duty cycle: hobby servos are not rated for continuous holding at stall; if it will hold the load for long periods, derate or choose a servo with a higher continuous rating or add a mechanical lock.
:::

::: applications
- **Robot joint selection**: motion profile to peak and RMS torque to gearbox and motor.
- **CNC axis**: ballscrew pitch converts rotary to linear; reflected inertia includes the table mass.
- **Camera gimbal**: low-inertia, low-torque motors with high bandwidth encoders.
- **Pick-and-place heads**: short, fast moves where acceleration torque dominates.
- **Hobby arms**: servo choice by torque in kg·cm, speed in s/60°, metal gears, stall current.
:::

::: assumptions
The example assumes a rotary axis, a symmetric triangular profile, constant inertia and friction, no gravity, and ideal gearing apart from stated efficiency. Real profiles are often trapezoidal or S-curve (lower peak jerk), which changes peak torque and RMS; a trapezoidal profile with the same move time has lower peak acceleration only if it has a cruise segment, but a higher peak speed. Linear axes (ballscrew, belt) use equivalent inertia $m(p/2\pi)^2$. Margins (1.5–2×) and inertia ratio limits are rules of thumb. Hobby servo ratings are stall values at a stated voltage; continuous capability is lower.
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between peak torque and continuous torque?
Continuous torque is the highest torque the motor can deliver indefinitely without exceeding its thermal limit, at a given ambient and mounting. Peak torque is a short-duration limit set by the drive current and magnetic saturation or demagnetisation, usable for acceleration. Sizing needs the profile's peak below the peak rating and RMS below continuous.

### Why does reflected inertia go with $N^2$?
Kinetic energy is preserved: $\tfrac12 J_L\omega_L^2 = \tfrac12 J_{refl}\omega_m^2$ with $\omega_m = N\omega_L$, so $J_{refl} = J_L/N^2$. The motor turns $N$ times faster for the same output speed, so the load looks much lighter, which also makes the load less able to upset the loop when it changes.

### The profile needs 100 rpm at the load but your motor's rated speed is 3000 rpm. What gear ratio?
Around 30:1 would put the load's top speed at the motor's rated speed, but you want margin, so 20–25:1 is typical. Then recheck motor torque: load torque 1.5 N·m over 25 with 90% efficiency is about 0.07 N·m, and check the inertia ratio $J_L/N^2$ against the rotor inertia.

### What if the load has a vertical axis?
Add the gravity torque $mgL$ continuously, including in dwell, so RMS torque rises sharply, and add a brake so the axis does not fall when power is lost. The motor may hold current all the time, so the continuous rating matters more.

### Harder: the chosen servo meets torque but overshoots and rings at the required speed. What do you look at?
Probably the inertia ratio is too high or there is compliance and backlash between motor and load: the load resonates on the gearbox and shaft stiffness, limiting the loop bandwidth. Options: higher gear ratio (reduces reflected inertia), a stiffer coupling or gearbox, lower backlash, a load-side encoder, or filters (notch) in the drive. Compare the resonance frequency $\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{k/J}$ with the desired loop bandwidth.
:::
