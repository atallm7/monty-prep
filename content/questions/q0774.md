::: basics
### In plain words
To wire two UART devices, you cross the data wires: one device's TX goes to the other's RX, and the other's TX goes back to the first's RX. You also join the grounds. Before powering up, you check that both devices use the same logic voltage. If they do not, you add a level shifter.

### Picture it
Picture two houses joined by water pipes. Each house has an outlet (TX) and an inlet (RX). You connect each outlet to the other house's inlet. Both houses must also measure pressure from the same drain level, which is ground. The pressure is the voltage. If a high-pressure house feeds a weak inlet, the inlet bursts, so you add a pressure reducer first.

### Words you need
- **Crossover**: connecting TX to RX and RX to TX. Pin names are from each device's own point of view.
- **Ground (GND)**: the shared 0 V reference. It must be joined between devices.
- **Logic level**: the voltage that means "1", such as 3.3 V or 5 V.
- **Level shifter**: a small circuit or chip that converts between two logic voltages.
- **USB-UART bridge**: an adapter that gives a PC a TX and RX pin at logic level.
- **RS-232**: an older standard that uses much larger voltages, both positive and negative. It needs a converter chip, such as a MAX3232, and can destroy a logic pin if wired directly.

### The simplest example
Wire a 3.3 V microcontroller's TX to a USB-UART bridge's RXD, the bridge's TXD to its RX, and ground to ground. Now suppose a 5 V sensor's TX must feed the 3.3 V microcontroller's RX. Put a 1 kΩ resistor in the wire, then a 2 kΩ resistor to ground. The midpoint sits at $5 \times 2/3 = 3.33$ V, which is safe. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers when a divider is not enough, wiring RS-232 and long cable runs, protecting the pins, and a bring-up checklist.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can **draw the connection and get the details right**: TX to RX crossover, shared ground, matching voltage levels, and the extra step when the voltages or standards differ (5 V to 3.3 V, RS-232, RS-485). It is the practical bring-up question: the interviewer wants to hear what you would physically do and what you would check before applying power.

Hidden sub-skills: pin-naming from each device's own point of view, level shifting (divider, MOSFET shifter, translator IC), protecting pins, and handling flow control and reset lines.
:::

::: why
The most common UART bug in the world is TX connected to TX. The second is a 5 V adapter wired to a 3.3 V MCU. Both are wiring errors that cost real time and sometimes real parts, and both are avoided by a ten-second mental checklist. An engineer who can state it unprompted comes across as someone who has brought up boards before. The wiring also depends on which standard sits underneath, so strong answers separate "chip to chip" from "board to PC".
:::

::: concepts
- **Crossover rule**: pin names are from each device's own viewpoint, so A's TX goes to B's RX and A's RX to B's TX. See [[q0773]].
- **Common ground**: must be tied between devices.
- **Logic-level compatibility**: $V_{OH}$ of the driver must exceed $V_{IH}$ of the receiver, and $V_{OL}$ must be below $V_{IL}$. See [[q0786]] for pull sizing and [[q0008]] for dividers.
- **Level translation**: resistor divider, single-FET shifter, or a dedicated translator IC. See [[q0785]] for open-drain/push-pull ideas used in shifters.
- **Line standards**: RS-232 (inverted, plus/minus 3 to 15 V), RS-485 (differential). Each needs a transceiver chip.
- **Flow-control and control lines**: RTS/CTS cross; DTR may be used for auto-reset on dev boards.
:::

::: answer
"The rule is: cross TX and RX, and tie the grounds. Each device names its pins from its own point of view, so TX on device A, the output, goes to RX on device B, the input, and B's TX goes back to A's RX. Then connect ground, since the logic levels need a common reference.

Before I power anything I check voltage levels. If both sides are 3.3 V logic, I just wire them. If one's 5 V and one's 3.3 V, I have to be careful. A 3.3 V TX into a 5 V RX is usually fine for TTL-compatible inputs, but a 5 V TX into a 3.3 V RX could overstress the pin, so I'd add a level shifter, or at minimum a resistor divider, say 1 k and 2 k, which gives 3.33 V from 5 V.

If I'm connecting to a PC, I'd use a USB-to-UART bridge at the right voltage. If the other end is a real RS-232 port, I'd put a transceiver like a MAX3232 in between, because RS-232 swings plus and minus several volts and would damage an MCU pin. And for long runs or noisy environments I'd use RS-485 transceivers.

Finally I'd add a small series resistor on lines that might get hot-plugged, and check the baud rate and framing match."
:::

::: technical
**Basic chip-to-chip (same logic voltage).**

$$\text{A.TX} \to \text{B.RX},\quad \text{B.TX} \to \text{A.RX},\quad \text{A.GND} \equiv \text{B.GND}$$

Optional: A.RTS to B.CTS and B.RTS to A.CTS.

**Level compatibility check.** For each direction require $V_{OH,\min}(\text{driver}) > V_{IH,\min}(\text{receiver})$ and $V_{OL,\max}(\text{driver}) < V_{IL,\max}(\text{receiver})$, and the driver's high must not exceed the receiver's absolute-maximum input voltage (often $V_{DD}+0.3$ V unless the pin is 5 V tolerant).

| Case | Fix |
|---|---|
| 3.3 V TX to 3.3 V RX | direct |
| 3.3 V TX to 5 V TTL RX ($V_{IH} \approx 2.0$ V) | direct usually works; verify $V_{IH}$ |
| 3.3 V TX to 5 V CMOS RX ($V_{IH} = 0.7V_{DD} = 3.5$ V) | level shifter needed |
| 5 V TX to 3.3 V RX (not 5 V tolerant) | level shifter or divider |
| 1.8 V to 3.3 V | translator IC |

**Divider for 5 V to 3.3 V.** With $R_1 = 1$ kΩ (series) and $R_2 = 2$ kΩ (to ground): $V_{out} = 5 \times \frac{2}{3} = 3.33$ V. The Thevenin resistance is $667\ \Omega$; with 15 pF of pin and trace capacitance the RC edge is $2.2 \times 667 \times 15\text{ pF} \approx 22$ ns, fine at 115200 baud (bit time 8.68 us) and even at a few Mbaud. This only works in the 5 V to 3.3 V direction on a line that is always driven.

**PC connection.** Use a USB-UART bridge (CP210x, CH340, FT232 families) set to the target voltage: TX/RX/GND only, with the target powered separately (do not connect the bridge's VCC to a self-powered board unless you intend to power it from there).

**RS-232.** A transceiver such as the MAX3232 generates plus/minus supplies from a single 3.3 V rail with charge pumps and level-shifts both directions; RS-232 is inverted (mark = negative voltage).

**RS-485.** A transceiver (e.g., 3.3 V devices in the MAX3485 or SN65HVD family) converts TX/RX to a differential pair, with the direction pin (DE/RE) driven by a GPIO for half duplex; add termination (about 120 Ω) at the two ends of the bus.
:::

::: long
"The basic rule is simple: transmit goes to receive. Pin names are from each device's own perspective, so my MCU's TX output goes to the module's RX input, and the module's TX goes back to my MCU's RX. Then I tie the grounds together. If I'm using hardware flow control, RTS goes to the other side's CTS and vice versa, so those cross too.

Then the part that actually bites people: voltage levels. If both ends are 3.3 V logic it's direct. If a 5 V device is talking to a 3.3 V MCU, I look at both directions separately. For the 3.3 V MCU's TX into a 5 V device's RX, a TTL-style input with a 2 V threshold is happy with 3.3 V, but a CMOS-style input wants about 3.5 V and might not see a clean high, so I'd check the datasheet. For the 5 V device's TX into the MCU's RX, I'd either use a 5 V tolerant pin, a divider such as 1 kΩ and 2 kΩ that turns 5 V into 3.33 V, or a proper translator chip. For bidirectional or open-drain lines, I'd use the single-FET level shifter.

For a PC, I'd use a USB-to-UART bridge, picking the one that runs at my logic voltage, and I'd connect only TX, RX and ground, not its power pin if my board has its own supply. For a classic RS-232 port, I'd use something like a MAX3232 to make the plus and minus levels, because wiring an MCU pin straight to RS-232 would damage it. And over long, noisy cables I'd use RS-485 transceivers and a terminated twisted pair.

Last, I'd verify the baud rate and 8N1 on both ends. If I see garbage, the usual suspects are swapped TX/RX, a wrong baud rate, or a missing ground."
:::

::: terms
- **Crossover (null-modem) connection**: TX to RX and RX to TX between two devices.
- **Level shifter / translator**: circuit that converts logic voltage between domains (for example 5 V to 3.3 V).
- **5 V tolerant pin**: an MCU input that can safely accept up to about 5 V even though its supply is 3.3 V.
- **$V_{IH}$ / $V_{IL}$**: minimum input voltage guaranteed to read high / maximum guaranteed to read low.
- **USB-UART bridge**: chip or cable that turns USB into TTL-level TX/RX.
- **RS-232 transceiver**: chip that converts logic levels to and from RS-232 swings, often with an on-chip charge pump.
- **DE/RE**: driver-enable and receiver-enable pins on an RS-485 transceiver.
- **Termination**: a resistor (about 120 Ω) at each end of an RS-485 line to absorb reflections.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "connect TX to RX and RX to TX".

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the crossover **and** the ground, and explains that names are from each device's viewpoint.
- Immediately raises **logic-level compatibility**, checks $V_{OH}/V_{IH}$ in each direction separately, and gives the divider numbers (1 k + 2 k gives 3.33 V).
- Distinguishes TTL UART, RS-232 and RS-485 and states what chip each needs (and that RS-232 is inverted and can kill an MCU pin).
- Mentions practical details: power the target separately from the USB-serial adapter, keep wires short at high baud, check framing settings, put a series resistor for hot-plug or probing.
- Knows the debugging checklist: scope TX, check polarity, swap pins.
:::

::: mistakes
- **TX to TX, RX to RX** (straight-through where a crossover is needed). Garbage or nothing.
- **Forgetting ground**, or relying on accidental return paths.
- **Driving a 3.3 V MCU RX pin from a 5 V TX** without a shifter or tolerant pin. This can overstress the input over time.
- **Connecting RS-232 levels directly to an MCU.** The voltage and inverted polarity will damage it.
- **Powering a target from the bridge's VCC while it has its own supply**, so two supplies fight.
- **Using a divider on a bidirectional line.** A divider is one-way; two-way lines need a FET shifter or translator.
- **Leaving the RX input floating** when the other device is off. The line wobbles and the receiver sees phantom bytes; use a pull-up on RX (typically 10 kΩ to 100 kΩ).
:::

Figure: The canonical connection. Both labels are written from each chip's own perspective, so the data lines cross: MCU TX lands on the bridge's receive pin (RXD). Some boards silkscreen the *other* device's name, so read the datasheet before you trust a pin label.

**Silkscreen hazard.** Some module vendors label pins from the connecting device's viewpoint ("connect to MCU TX"). If a module datasheet says its TX pin is an output, wire it to your RX. If the board says "TX" meaning "this goes to TX of the host", flip. When in doubt, probe it with the line idle: a live UART output sits firmly high and stays there if you load it with 10 kΩ to ground, while an input either floats or is held up only by a weak pull-up that the 10 kΩ drags down.

## Level compatibility: do the numbers

Take a 5 V sensor module that outputs 0 V / 5 V on its TX, and a 3.3 V MCU that is not 5 V tolerant. The absolute-maximum input is usually VDD + 0.3 V = 3.6 V, so 5 V overstresses the input protection diode, which clamps the pin and pushes current into the 3.3 V rail.

```circuit
 {"t":"gnd","at":[11,8]},
 {"t":"term","at":[13,3],"label":"3.3 V RX","side":"right"}
]}
```
Figure: A resistive divider for a one-way, always-driven line: $5\ \text{V}\times 2/(1+2) = 3.33$ V at the MCU's RX. Because R1 is in the wire path, it also limits the current into the protection diode if there is a transient. This does not work for lines the other device must also drive, or open-drain lines.

The divider's output resistance is $1\text{k}\parallel2\text{k} = 667\ \Omega$. With 15 pF at the pin, the edge time constant is 10 ns, so 10-90% is about 22 ns, far below the 8.68 us bit time at 115200. Even at 3 Mbaud (333 ns per bit) it is fine.

Going the other way, a 3.3 V TX into a 5 V device: a TTL-input device (about 2.0 V threshold) reads 3.3 V as high; a 5 V CMOS input with $V_{IH} = 0.7\times 5 = 3.5$ V does not. Check the receiver's datasheet. If it fails, use a translator IC or a bidirectional shifter.

## Bidirectional or open-drain: use a FET shifter

```sim

```
Figure: The classic single-FET shifter (BSS138-type) between a 3.3 V and a 5 V side with pull-ups on each. Toggle which side drives low and see both sides follow. This is the circuit that I2C level shifting uses, and it works for UART at modest speeds too. Note the limited speed: with the sim's 10 kΩ pull-ups and 50 pF the 10–90% rise is about 1.1 µs, fine against an 8.68 µs bit at 115200 but useless near 1 Mbaud.

For UART at hundreds of kbaud or more, a push-pull translator IC (direction-controlled or auto-sensing) is a better choice, since the FET shifter's rise time is limited by the pull-ups.

## To a PC: the USB-UART bridge

Bridges come as chips (CP210x, CH340, FT232 families), cables, or are integrated on dev boards. Practical points:

- **Pick the voltage**: 3.3 V or 5 V logic. Many have a jumper.
- **Don't back-power**: if your board has its own regulator, leave the bridge's VCC unconnected.
- **DTR/RTS for reset**: dev boards (Arduino-style, ESP32 boards) use DTR or RTS through a capacitor to reset the MCU into its bootloader; if you wire those up, you get automatic programming.
- **Baud limits**: cheap bridges may not reach several Mbaud; check the datasheet.

## To RS-232 and RS-485

RS-232 transceivers (the MAX232 family; the MAX3232 works at 3.3 V) take TX and RX at logic level and produce plus/minus swings, inverted. Connect MCU TX to the transceiver's driver input (T1IN), the line out (T1OUT) to the DB-9 pin 3 (TXD) when acting as a DTE, and R1IN from pin 2 (RXD) to MCU RX through R1OUT. For RS-485, the MCU TX goes into the driver's D pin, the receiver's R output goes to MCU RX, A/B go on a twisted pair, and a GPIO drives DE (and RE) for half duplex. Terminate both ends and bias the idle state to a known level so the receiver doesn't see noise as data.

## Protecting the pins and the signal

Connectors get plugged and unplugged, scope probes slip, and cables pick up static. A few cheap habits protect the UART pins. Put a 22 to 100 Ω series resistor at the MCU end of any line that leaves the board: it limits fault current into the pin's protection diodes and also damps ringing by slowing the edge slightly. Add a TVS or ESD diode array on exposed connectors. Give RX a pull-up (or pull-down, matching the idle level) so it never floats when the far device is unpowered; a floating RX with a noisy environment produces random characters. When the cable is long relative to the edge rate, keep the ground wire adjacent to the signal wires (twist them or use a flat ribbon with alternating ground) to keep the loop area, and therefore the pickup and radiation, small.

For layout on the PCB, keep UART traces short and away from switching nodes, avoid stubs, and on a board with an RS-485 transceiver place the termination and bias resistors close to the connector. None of this matters much at 9600 baud on a bench, but it separates a prototype that works from a product that works in a customer's plant.

## Bring-up checklist

1. Same ground. 2. TX to RX. 3. Same voltage domain (or translator). 4. Same baud, same frame (8N1). 5. Probe TX with a scope: you should see a clean 0 to 3.3 V pattern with a bit width of 8.68 us at 115200. 6. If nothing arrives, swap the two data wires; if garbage arrives, check the baud rate and clock source.
:::

::: applications
- **Dev board to PC**: USB-UART bridge on the board, TX/RX/GND only, with DTR/RTS optionally for auto-reset.
- **MCU to GPS or Bluetooth module**: TX/RX/GND plus module VCC; module logic level matched to the MCU.
- **5 V sensor to 3.3 V MCU**: resistor divider or translator on the sensor's TX only.
- **Legacy test equipment with RS-232**: MAX3232 between the MCU and a DB-9.
- **Factory floor Modbus over RS-485**: transceiver, terminated twisted pair.
:::

::: assumptions
- Standard asynchronous UART at TTL levels; both ends configured for the same framing.
- I take $V_{IH}$ ≈ 2.0 V for TTL-type and 0.7·VDD for CMOS-type inputs. Real thresholds are in the datasheet and vary.
- The divider answer applies to a one-way, always-driven signal. For open-drain, bidirectional or very high-speed links, use a proper shifter.
- Some MCUs have 5 V tolerant pins; if the datasheet says so (and in what conditions, since tolerance is sometimes lost when VDD is off) you may connect directly.
- Hot-plugging and ESD protection need extra parts (series resistor, TVS) not shown here.
:::

::: followups
### I connected everything correctly but I get garbage. What next?
Check the baud rate first. Then the framing (8N1 vs 8E1). Then the clock source on the MCU: an internal RC oscillator can be off by a percent or more, and with the wrong PLL setting the baud generator runs at the wrong rate. Put a scope on TX and measure a single bit: 8.68 us means 115200. If the pulse width is right and the other end still gets garbage, look at the voltage levels and the ground.

### Can I just connect a 5 V UART to a 3.3 V MCU through a series resistor?
Sometimes, if the pin is 5 V tolerant or the resistor limits the clamp diode current to a safe level, but that is risky and out of spec for many parts. A divider or translator is the proper fix. A 10 kΩ series resistor limiting clamp current to $(5-3.6)/10\text{k} = 0.14$ mA is sometimes used as a hack, but datasheets typically specify the max injected current, and you rely on the internal protection diode.

### Why do dev boards connect DTR to the reset pin?
DTR (or RTS) is toggled by the PC's serial software when a port opens. Through a capacitor it pulses the MCU's reset (and sometimes boot-select) pin so the bootloader starts, enabling one-click programming. It is why opening a serial monitor can reset the board.

### Two devices on one UART bus: can I just connect their TX pins together?
Not with push-pull outputs. Two drivers on one wire will fight when one drives high and the other low ([[q0788]]). Use RS-485 transceivers with tristate drivers, open-drain TX with a pull-up, or separate UARTs. If you must share, put a series resistor or a tri-state buffer, and have a protocol decide who talks.

### Harder: the link works at 9600 baud but fails at 921600 over 1 m of jumper wire. Why?
Edge rates, reflections and crosstalk. At 921600 the bit is 1.085 us, and a 1 m unterminated wire with a fast driver rings; with a ground wire far from the signal wire, the loop area also picks up noise. Shorten the cable, run ground close to the signal, add a series resistor near the driver (say 33 to 100 Ω), or drop the baud rate.
:::
