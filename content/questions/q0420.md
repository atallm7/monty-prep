::: basics
### In plain words
An H-bridge is two legs side by side, drawn like the letter H. Each leg has a high-side switch from the supply down to a middle node, and a low-side switch from that node to ground. The motor sits between the two middle nodes, as the crossbar of the H. A good drawing also shows the transistors (usually MOSFETs), their built-in diodes, and the gate drivers that switch them.

### Picture it
Picture a canal with four gates, two on each side, and a channel across the middle that holds a paddle wheel. Open the top-left and bottom-right gates, and water flows across the channel one way. Open the top-right and bottom-left gates, and it flows the other way, so the wheel reverses. Open both gates on the same side and the water rushes straight from supply to drain without touching the wheel.

### Words you need
- **Node**: a connection point in a circuit.
- **Leg (half-bridge)**: one high-side and one low-side switch in series, with the output at the midpoint. See [[q0421]].
- **Body diode**: the diode built into every MOSFET. It gives the motor's current a path when switches turn off.
- **Shoot-through**: both switches in one leg on at once, which shorts the supply.
- **Dead time**: a brief gap with both switches of a leg off, between transitions.

### The simplest example
Label the switches S1 (top left), S2 (top right), S3 (bottom left), and S4 (bottom right), with 12 V as the supply. S1 and S4 on puts the left node at 12 V and the right at 0 V, so the motor gets +12 V. S2 and S3 on reverses it to −12 V. Both low sides on, S3 and S4, shorts the motor to brake it. All off, it coasts. S1 and S3 together is the forbidden short. For real parts, add body diodes and gate drivers with dead time.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the MOSFET-level drawing, body diodes, losses, and integrated bridges.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can put a correct, *labelled* H-bridge on a whiteboard without hesitating, and whether the drawing shows that you understand it rather than just remember a shape. The interviewer is watching for:

1. Four switches in two legs, motor in the middle (the "H"), supply at the top and ground at the bottom.
2. Sensible device choices: which FET types go where, and **body diodes drawn in**, because they are the freewheel path for the motor current.
3. Each switch has its own gate signal, and you can name the four useful states (forward, reverse, brake, coast) and the one forbidden state (both FETs of the same leg on).
4. You call out the extras a real bridge needs: gate drivers, dead time, and supply decoupling.
:::

::: why
Every brushed motor, solenoid pair, voice coil and stepper winding that must be driven in **both directions** from a single supply uses an H-bridge. Three half-bridges give you a BLDC inverter, and a buck converter is a half-bridge plus a filter, so this one drawing is the root of a large part of power electronics. In an interview it is a quick filter: people who have only seen "four switches" often forget the body diodes and the gate-drive problem, and those two details are what turn a cartoon into a design.
:::

::: concepts
- **Switch-level model**: each leg is a high-side and a low-side switch that are never on together. See [[q0421]] for a single leg.
- **MOSFET symbols and body diode**: the intrinsic diode points from source to drain in an NFET and gives inductive current a path when the channel is off. See [[q0089]] and [[q0101]].
- **Motor as an inductive load**: current cannot change instantly, so it needs a freewheel path ([[q0144]], [[q0333]]).
- **Complementary drive**: NFET low side, PFET or bootstrapped NFET high side ([[q0107]], [[q0204]]).
- **Shoot-through**: both devices in one leg on = a short across the supply ([[q0428]]).
- **Bridge states**: forward, reverse, brake (both low sides or both high sides), coast (all off).
:::

::: answer
"Sure. I'll start with the idea and then put real transistors in it. An H-bridge is two legs side by side. Each leg is a high-side switch from the supply down to a middle node, and a low-side switch from that node to ground. The motor connects between the two middle nodes, which is the crossbar of the H.

If I close the top-left and bottom-right switches, the left node sits at the supply and the right node at ground, so current goes through the motor one way. Close the top-right and bottom-left and it goes the other way. That's forward and reverse.

Now with MOSFETs: low sides are NFETs with the source at ground. For the high side I'll draw PFETs with the source at the supply, because the gate drive is simplest, and I'll note that a bigger design uses NFETs there with a bootstrap or charge pump. I draw the body diode on every FET, because when the motor current has to keep flowing after I switch off, those diodes are what catch it. Then each gate gets its own control line, with a gate driver and dead time so the two FETs in one leg are never on together. I'd also drop a bulk capacitor across the supply."
:::

::: technical
**Topology.** Two half-bridges (legs A and B) share $V_M$ and ground. Leg A has $Q_1$ (high) and $Q_3$ (low) with midpoint $A$; leg B has $Q_2$ (high) and $Q_4$ (low) with midpoint $B$. Load between $A$ and $B$.

**Voltage across the load** (ideal switches): $V_{AB} = V_A - V_B$, with $V_A\in\{0, V_M\}$ and likewise $V_B$. That gives $+V_M$, $-V_M$ or $0$ (both nodes equal), which is why the bridge is bipolar from a single supply.

| State | $Q_1$ | $Q_2$ | $Q_3$ | $Q_4$ | $V_{AB}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Forward | on | off | off | on | $+V_M$ |
| Reverse | off | on | on | off | $-V_M$ |
| Brake (low-side) | off | off | on | on | 0, nodes shorted to ground |
| Brake (high-side) | on | on | off | off | 0, nodes shorted to $V_M$ |
| Coast | off | off | off | off | free (decays through body diodes) |
| **Illegal** | on | | on | | shoot-through, supply shorted |

**Device choice.** Low side: NFET, gate referenced to ground, driven directly from a 5 V or 12 V driver. High side: either PFET (gate pulled to $V_M$ to turn off, to $V_M - |V_{GS}|$ to turn on) or NFET (gate must reach $V_M + V_{GS(on)}$, so a bootstrap or charge pump is needed).

**Conduction loss.** Two FETs are in series with the motor at any time: $P = I^2(2R_{DS(on)})$. For 5 A and 20 mΩ per FET that is 1.0 W total.

**Flyback.** When a conducting pair turns off, the motor inductance forces the current to continue: in the forward-to-coast case the left node is pulled below ground and the right node above $V_M$ until the low-side body diode on A ($D_3$) and the high-side body diode on B ($D_2$) conduct and return the energy to the supply. Without them the node voltage would spike until something avalanches.

**Practical additions.** Gate drivers with dead time, a bulk capacitor plus a ceramic across $V_M$ at the bridge, current sense in the ground return, and a TVS or clamp if the supply cannot absorb regenerated energy.
:::

::: long
"I'll draw it as the letter H. The two vertical strokes are the two legs. Each leg is two switches in series between the supply at the top and ground at the bottom, and the node between them is where the motor attaches. The crossbar of the H is the motor.

With ideal switches that gives four states. Top-left and bottom-right on: left node at the supply, right node at ground, current flows left to right through the motor. Flip to top-right and bottom-left and the current reverses. If both bottom switches are on, both motor terminals are at ground, so the motor is shorted, which acts as a brake. If everything is off the motor coasts. The one state I never allow is both switches in the same leg on, because that's a straight short across the supply.

Now the real version. Each switch becomes a MOSFET. The low sides are NFETs with sources at ground. The high sides are PFETs, because to turn on a PFET with its source at the supply you just pull the gate a few volts below the supply. Bigger or higher-voltage designs use NFETs on the high side because they have lower resistance for the same die size, but then the gate has to go above the supply, which needs a bootstrap capacitor or a charge pump.

I draw the body diodes on every transistor. When the motor current is flowing and I switch things off, the inductance wants to keep that current going, and the body diodes give it a path back to the supply or ground. That's the flyback path.

Finally the practical parts: a gate driver on each FET with dead time, a bulk capacitor across the supply to absorb the current steps, and a sense resistor in the ground return for current limiting."
:::

::: terms
- **H-bridge**: four switches arranged as two legs with the load across the midpoints, giving either polarity from one supply.
- **Half-bridge / leg**: a high-side and low-side switch in series, with the output taken from the midpoint ([[q0421]]).
- **High-side / low-side**: the switch between the load and the supply / between the load and ground.
- **Body diode**: the intrinsic drain-source diode of a MOSFET; carries freewheeling current when the channel is off.
- **Shoot-through (cross-conduction)**: both switches of a leg on at once; a short across the supply.
- **Dead time**: a short interval in which both switches of a leg are off, inserted between transitions.
- **Brake**: both motor terminals tied to the same rail so the back-EMF drives current through the winding.
- **Coast (fast decay)**: all switches off; motor current decays through body diodes.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws four boxes labelled S1 to S4 with a motor in the middle.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws it with real transistor symbols, labelled by position (high-left, low-right) and by gate signal.
- **Includes the body diodes** and says what they do when current has to keep flowing.
- Gives the truth table including brake, coast and the **forbidden** state.
- Chooses device types with a reason: NFETs low, PFET or bootstrapped NFET high, and the trade-off between them.
- Mentions gate drivers and dead time, a bulk capacitor, and where the current-sense resistor goes, without being asked.
- Notices that the motor is an inductor: the bridge must tolerate the current continuing after a switch opens.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing the motor in the wrong place.** It goes between the two midpoints, not between the supply and one leg. Check that all four combinations make sense.
- **Leaving out the body diodes.** A bridge without a freewheel path destroys itself on the first switch-off. If you use switches with no intrinsic diode (BJTs, most discrete IGBTs), add external antiparallel diodes.
- **PFET/NFET pointing the wrong way.** The PFET source goes to the supply, the NFET source to ground; the arrow (body diode) points toward the supply on the high side.
- **Tying two gates of the same leg together** as in a CMOS inverter and calling it done. That works for logic but gives shoot-through when the edges overlap; power bridges use separate gate signals and dead time.
- **Forgetting the supply decoupling.** Current steps from zero to the motor current every edge, so the bulk and local ceramic capacitors are part of the circuit.
:::

::: deep
## The idea first: four switches and a crossbar

A DC motor reverses when the current through it reverses. With a single supply and a single switch, the current can only go one way. The trick is to give the motor **two terminals that can each be connected to either rail**. Then one terminal can be high while the other is low, or the other way around, and you have both polarities from a battery with only one voltage.

Figure: The H. S1/S3 form the left leg (midpoint A) and S2/S4 the right leg (midpoint B). The motor is the crossbar. Closing S1 and S4 puts A at V_M and B at ground; closing S2 and S3 does the opposite.

Each leg is a **half-bridge**: a high-side and a low-side switch in series with the output at the middle ([[q0421]]). An H-bridge is exactly two of them sharing a supply. The control rule that makes it safe is "never close both switches in one leg": that would connect $V_M$ to ground through nothing but the switch resistances.

Figure: PFETs on top (source at V_M), NFETs on the bottom (source at ground). The body diodes point from source to drain, so on the top FETs they point up toward V_M and on the bottom FETs up toward the motor node. Four independent gate signals G1 to G4.

How the polarity works out: a PFET turns on when its gate is below its source, so G1 or G2 pulled to 0 V turns it on (with the source at $V_M$, that is $V_{GS}=-V_M$), and held at $V_M$ turns it off. The NFET turns on with its gate above ground. So a "forward" command is $G_1=0, G_4=\text{high}$ with $G_2=V_M, G_3=0$.

> [!TIP]
> If a drawing asks you to "label every switch with its state", write the gate levels as well as on/off. For a PFET high side, *on* is a **low** gate. Mixing this up is the commonest whiteboard error.

## The four useful states

Figure: Click the switches to build forward, reverse, brake and coast. Try closing both switches of one leg and watch the shoot-through fault; then open everything after running forward and watch the current continue through the body diodes.

**Forward / reverse.** Two diagonal switches carry the motor current. The motor sees $+V_M$ or $-V_M$ minus the drops across two FETs.

**Brake.** Closing both low sides (or both high sides) ties the motor terminals together. The spinning rotor generates a back-EMF that drives current through the winding resistance and the on FETs, and the motor decelerates. [[q0424]] works through the numbers.

**Coast.** Everything off: the motor's inertia keeps it turning, and its winding current has to go somewhere. The body diodes route it through the supply (fast decay). If the bridge has just stopped driving forward, the current flows in $D_3$ (low side of A) and $D_2$ (high side of B).

**Illegal.** $Q_1$ with $Q_3$, or $Q_2$ with $Q_4$.

## What the body diodes are doing

The motor winding is inductive ($v=L\,di/dt$, [[q0039]]). If you turn off $Q_1$ and $Q_4$ while 5 A is flowing from $A$ to $B$, the current through the winding cannot change instantly. Node A gets pulled down below ground, node B pulled up above $V_M$, until the diodes conduct: $D_3$ (low side of A, from ground up to A) and $D_2$ (high side of B, from B up to $V_M$). The energy $\tfrac12 LI^2$ goes back into the supply or the bulk capacitor. This is the same flyback logic as a single-switch drive with a freewheel diode ([[q0144]]), just done with two diodes because the bridge has a diode on every device.

For a 12 V supply and 5 A, the clamp voltages are about $V_M+0.7$ V on the high node and $-0.7$ V on the low node, so every FET sees only about 13 V of $V_{DS}$ in the worst case. A bridge whose body diodes are slow (high $Q_{rr}$) will give a current spike when the opposite FET turns on; that is a design topic of its own ([[q0208]]).

## Real-world additions

| Piece | Why |
|---|---|
| Gate drivers | Logic-level GPIOs can't charge $C_{iss}$ fast enough, and the high side needs a floating or level-shifted drive ([[q0206]], [[q0427]]) |
| Dead time | Both FETs of a leg are off for tens to hundreds of ns around each transition ([[q0428]]) |
| Bulk plus ceramic capacitor | The bridge pulls current in steps; the cap supplies it and absorbs regenerated energy |
| Sense resistor (low-side, in the ground return) | Current limit and torque control |
| Gate resistors, gate pull-downs | Edge rate and defined state at reset ([[q0430]], [[q0431]]) |

An example of the loss budget: 5 A through two 20 mΩ FETs is $I^2\cdot 2R = 25\times0.04 = 1.0$ W, which is why higher-current bridges choose NFETs on both sides even though they need the bootstrap.

## Integrated bridges

You rarely build the bridge from discrete FETs for small motors. Motor-driver ICs integrate all four FETs, the gate drive, the charge pump, dead time and current limiting in one package, and you control them with two PWM/direction pins. The topology you drew is still what is inside. For high currents the driver stage is separate and you choose the FETs yourself.
:::

::: applications
- **Brushed DC motors** in robots, printers and power tools: direction plus speed through PWM duty.
- **Stepper motor windings**: one H-bridge per coil (bipolar stepper) with current chopping.
- **Voice-coil actuators and solenoid pairs**: bidirectional force in hard drives, camera autofocus.
- **Class-D audio amplifiers**: a BTL output stage is an H-bridge driving a speaker through a filter.
- **Piezo and ultrasonic transducers**: bipolar drive from a single rail.
- **Inverters and wireless-power transmitters**: full-bridge driving a transformer or resonant tank.
:::

::: assumptions
- Single supply with a ground, and a load that can accept both polarities.
- Ideal switches for the truth table. In real FETs, add the drops, the dead time and the body-diode conduction.
- High side drawn as PFETs for simplicity. For higher power or voltage use bootstrapped NFETs ([[q0426]]); the topology is unchanged.
- Motor modelled as a series $R$-$L$ plus back-EMF; the freewheel discussion holds for any inductive load.
- Logic-level control: separate gate signals per FET, with a driver IC to enforce dead time.

An alternate valid drawing is the **all-NFET** bridge with a bootstrap driver, or a bridge of IGBTs with antiparallel diodes for high voltage. If the interviewer allows, say you will draw the simple one first and note the variants.
:::

::: followups
### Why do you draw diodes across the transistors?
The motor is inductive, so current has to keep flowing after a switch opens. The body diode gives it a path to the supply (high side) or ground (low side) with a clamp of about one diode drop beyond the rail. In a MOSFET it is built in; in IGBTs or discrete BJTs you have to add it. Without it the switch node flies up until something avalanches.

### Which two switches do I close to run the motor in reverse?
The opposite diagonal: $Q_2$ and $Q_3$, so $B$ is at $V_M$ and $A$ at ground. Check by following the current: from $V_M$ through $Q_2$ to $B$, through the motor from $B$ to $A$, then through $Q_3$ to ground.

### Why not use four NFETs, and what changes?
Four NFETs give lower $R_{DS(on)}$ per area and cheaper parts ([[q0426]]), but the high-side gates have to be driven above the supply by a bootstrap capacitor or charge pump. Bootstrap alone cannot hold 100% duty because the capacitor droops, so motor-driver ICs add a charge pump.

### What does a logic-level GPIO need to drive this?
Nothing it can do directly for the high side or for power FETs: the gates have capacitance, the high side needs a different reference, and the shoot-through protection needs timing. Use a gate driver IC or an integrated bridge, with the GPIO providing PWM and direction. See [[q0429]].

### The motor stalls and the bridge dies. What do you check first?
Stall current is $V_M/R_a$; a 12 V motor with 1 Ω has 12 A at stall, which may be several times the FET and supply rating. Check the current limit, the FET $R_{DS(on)}$ heating $I^2R$ (12 A through two 20 mΩ FETs is 5.8 W), and the supply droop. Also check that the bulk capacitor and the flyback path can absorb regenerated energy if the load reverses.
:::
