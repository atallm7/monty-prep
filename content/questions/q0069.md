::: testing
A short question with a trap. The one-line answer is "holes in P-type, electrons in N-type", and the interviewer expects it instantly. What they are really checking is whether you can add the next layer without being asked:

1. The **minority** carriers and how many there are ($np=n_i^2$).
2. That "majority carrier" describes the *population*, not who carries the current in every situation (in a forward-biased junction the minority carriers carry the diffusion current across the junction).
3. That the dominant carrier is what makes the sign of the Hall voltage, and what decides whether a material conducts by electrons or holes in a device.
:::

::: why
Knowing which carrier dominates tells you the polarity of everything downstream: the direction of the field in a depletion region, which carriers cross a junction in forward bias, which one forms the MOSFET inversion layer, and why a BJT's base-region minority carriers set its gain. It's also the basis for debugging odd material measurements (a Hall-sign surprise, a resistivity that makes no sense), and it matters in sensor, solar and power-device work where majority versus minority carriers decide behaviour.
:::

::: concepts
- **Majority carrier**: the carrier type present in far larger numbers: electrons in N-type, holes in P-type. See [[q0062]].
- **Minority carrier**: the scarce type, with density $n_i^2$ divided by the majority density.
- **Mass-action law**: $np=n_i^2$ in thermal equilibrium (non-degenerate).
- **Dopant ionization**: majority density $\approx$ net doping once dopants are ionized. See [[q0065]].
- **Hall effect**: the sign of the transverse voltage in a current-carrying bar in a magnetic field tells which carrier dominates.
:::

::: answer
"In N-type material the majority carriers are electrons, and in P-type material they're holes. The doping sets it: donors like phosphorus add free electrons, acceptors like boron create holes, and the majority density is about equal to the dopant density.

The other carrier is still there, as the minority carrier, but far fewer, set by the mass-action law $np=n_i^2$. For silicon with $n_i\approx10^{10}$ per cm³ and a doping of $10^{16}$, the majority density is $10^{16}$ and the minority density is $10^{4}$. Twelve orders of magnitude apart.

Why does the minority carrier matter? In a diode, forward bias injects minority carriers across the junction, and their diffusion and recombination makes the current. In a BJT, the base current and gain depend on minority-carrier transport across the base. And thermally generated minority carriers are the source of reverse leakage current. So majority carriers carry current in the bulk of the material, but minority carriers do the interesting work at junctions."
:::

::: technical
**Equilibrium populations (non-degenerate).**

- N-type ($N_D\gg N_A$, $N_D\gg n_i$): majority $n_{n0}\approx N_D$, minority $p_{n0}=n_i^2/N_D$.
- P-type ($N_A\gg N_D$, $N_A\gg n_i$): majority $p_{p0}\approx N_A$, minority $n_{p0}=n_i^2/N_A$.

General exact result with net doping $N=N_D-N_A$:
$$n=\frac{N}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{N^2}{4}+n_i^2},\qquad p=\frac{n_i^2}{n}$$
When $|N|\gg n_i$ this reduces to the expressions above. When $|N|\lesssim n_i$ the material is nearly intrinsic and neither carrier clearly dominates.

**Numbers (Si, 300 K, $n_i=10^{10}$ cm⁻³).**

| Material | Majority | Minority |
|---|---|---|
| N-type, $N_D=10^{16}$ | $n=10^{16}$ | $p=10^{4}$ |
| P-type, $N_A=10^{16}$ | $p=10^{16}$ | $n=10^{4}$ |
| N-type, $N_D=10^{14}$ | $n=10^{14}$ | $p=10^{6}$ |
| P-type, $N_A=10^{18}$ | $p=10^{18}$ | $n=10^{2}$ |

**Fermi level.** $E_F$ sits above midgap in N-type, below in P-type: $|E_F-E_i|=kT\ln(N/n_i)$.

**Current components.** Total conductivity is $q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$. In N-type with $10^{16}$: the hole contribution is $p\mu_p/(n\mu_n)\approx\frac{10^{4}\times480}{10^{16}\times1250}\approx4\times10^{-13}$ of the electron contribution, utterly negligible in the bulk.

**Hall sign.** For current $I$ in a bar of thickness $t$ in field $B$: $V_H=\frac{IB}{qnt}$ for electrons with a polarity opposite to the hole case ($V_H=+\frac{IB}{qpt}$ with respect to the same orientation). The sign identifies the carrier.
:::

::: long
"For N-type silicon the majority carrier is the electron, for P-type it's the hole. The dopants set that: phosphorus in N-type gives about one free electron per atom, and boron in P-type gives about one hole per atom. So the majority density is about equal to the doping, something like $10^{15}$ to $10^{19}$ per cm³.

The minority carrier is never zero. It's set by the mass-action law, $np=n_i^2$, where $n_i$ is about $10^{10}$ per cm³ for silicon at room temperature. So in N-type with $10^{16}$ donors there are $10^{16}$ electrons and only $10^{4}$ holes per cm³, a factor of a trillion. If the doping goes up, the minority density goes down in inverse proportion.

You might ask, why care about something so small? A few reasons. In a PN junction at forward bias, minority carriers are injected across the junction, where they diffuse and recombine. That diffusion of minority carriers gives the exponential diode current. In a BJT, the base current and the current gain depend on minority carriers crossing the base. And minority carriers generated by heat in the depletion region are the reverse leakage current, which roughly doubles every ten degrees. So the majority carriers carry the bulk current, but the minority carriers decide how the junction behaves.

Also, how would you identify it in a measurement? A Hall measurement. Pass current through a bar in a magnetic field and the sign of the transverse voltage tells you whether the charge carriers are positive or negative. One caveat: if the material is nearly intrinsic, or at high temperature where $n_i$ exceeds the doping, there's no clear majority carrier."
:::

::: terms
- **Majority carrier**: the more abundant carrier (electrons in N-type, holes in P-type).
- **Minority carrier**: the less abundant carrier, with density $n_i^2$/majority density.
- **Thermal-equilibrium concentrations ($n_0$, $p_0$)**: carrier densities with no external excitation.
- **Mass-action law**: $n_0p_0=n_i^2$.
- **Injection**: raising a carrier density above equilibrium, e.g. by forward bias or light.
- **Low-level injection**: injected minority density much smaller than the majority density.
- **Recombination**: an electron and hole annihilating; the process that removes injected minority carriers.
- **Hall voltage**: transverse voltage in a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field; its sign identifies the carrier.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "holes for P-type, electrons for N-type."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the one-line answer immediately, then adds the minority carrier with its number from $np=n_i^2$.
- Explains that majority density is set by doping, and minority by thermal generation and doping.
- States why the minority carriers matter: diode injection, BJT gain, reverse leakage and photocurrent.
- Mentions that nearly intrinsic or very hot material has no clear majority carrier.
- Gives a way to measure the type (Hall sign, hot-probe, or a junction test).
- Distinguishes carrier *population* from *current*: majority carriers carry bulk current, minority carriers carry the diffusion current across a forward-biased junction.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"P-type has only holes and no electrons."** There are always electrons; they are the minority carrier, $n_i^2/p$.
- **Reversing the answer for holes and the sign.** Holes are positive carriers, but P-type is not "positively charged".
- **"The minority carriers are irrelevant because there are so few."** They control diode current, BJT gain, leakage and photodetector response.
- **"Majority carrier means the carrier that carries all the current."** True in bulk. At a forward-biased junction, minority-carrier diffusion dominates the current *across* the junction.
- **Applying $np=n_i^2$ under bias.** It holds only in equilibrium. Under forward bias $np>n_i^2$ at the junction edge; under reverse bias $np<n_i^2$.
- **Forgetting temperature.** At high temperature $n_i$ can exceed the doping and the "N-type" material becomes effectively intrinsic with $n\approx p$.
:::

::: deep
## The one-line answer and where it comes from

N-type: electrons are the majority carrier. P-type: holes are the majority carrier. Donors (Group V) release an electron for every atom, acceptors (Group III) create a hole for every atom. Both ionize easily (about 45 meV in silicon), so at room temperature
$$n\approx N_D\ \ (\text{N-type}),\qquad p\approx N_A\ \ (\text{P-type})$$
See [[q0062]] and [[q0065]] for how that comes about.

## Why the minority carrier is not zero

In thermal equilibrium electrons and holes are continually generated (a bond breaks and creates an electron-hole pair) and recombine at equal rates. Mass action follows from that balance:
$$np=n_i^2$$
Adding $N_D$ donors pushes $n$ up by orders of magnitude, so the recombination rate (proportional to $np$) would exceed the generation rate unless $p$ falls to compensate. The new equilibrium keeps $np=n_i^2$.

Worked example (silicon, $n_i=10^{10}$ cm⁻³):

- N-type $N_D=10^{16}$: $p=10^{20}/10^{16}=10^{4}$ cm⁻³.
- P-type $N_A=10^{16}$: $n=10^{4}$ cm⁻³.
- Heavily doped P+ $N_A=10^{19}$: $n=10^{1}$ cm⁻³. Ten electrons per cubic centimetre; a 1 µm cube ($10^{-12}$ cm³) holds on average $10^{-11}$ electrons, so at this level the minority density is a statistical average, not a literal count.

## Near the intrinsic limit

When doping is comparable to $n_i$, use the exact result. Example: $N_A=10^{10}$ in silicon at 300 K gives $p=\frac{N_A}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{N_A^2}{4}+n_i^2}=1.62\times10^{10}$ and $n=0.62\times10^{10}$. Neither dominates strongly. At 500 K ($n_i\approx1.2\times10^{14}$) even a $10^{14}$-doped sample is substantially intrinsic.

## What the carriers actually do in a bulk piece

Bulk conduction in doped material is dominated by majority carriers. Take the bar of N-type with $10^{16}$ donors: the electron contribution to conductivity is $q\mu_nn$, the hole contribution is $q\mu_pp$, and the ratio $\frac{p\mu_p}{n\mu_n}\approx4\times10^{-13}$. Ohm's law is entirely an electron story here.

## Where the minority carriers take over

At junctions the roles reverse. In a forward-biased PN junction, electrons from the N side are injected over the lowered barrier into the P side, where they are minority carriers. The current there is carried by *diffusion* of minority electrons, decaying as they recombine over a diffusion length $L_n=\sqrt{D_n\tau_n}$. The injected minority density at the edge of the depletion region is
$$n_p(0)=n_{p0}\,e^{V/V_T}$$
so the minority density goes from $10^{4}$ cm⁻³ at zero bias to a huge value at 0.6 V: $e^{0.6/0.02585}=1.2\times10^{10}$ times larger, i.e. about $1.2\times10^{14}$ cm⁻³. That is the origin of the diode's exponential $I$-$V$ ([[q0072]]). In reverse bias, minority carriers near the depletion edge are swept across, giving the small reverse saturation current.

In a BJT ([[q0085]]) the base is the thin region where injected minority carriers (electrons in an NPN) must diffuse to the collector before recombining; the fraction that recombines forms the base current.

## Identifying the carrier type

- **Hall effect.** A bar carrying current $I$ in a magnetic field $B$ develops a transverse voltage. For electrons $V_H=-\frac{IB}{qnt}$ and for holes $V_H=+\frac{IB}{qpt}$ (for the same geometry), so the sign shows the carrier type. Example: $n=10^{16}$ cm⁻³ $=10^{22}$ m⁻³, $I=1$ mA, $B=0.1$ T, $t=0.5$ mm: $|V_H|=\frac{10^{-3}\times0.1}{1.602\times10^{-19}\times10^{22}\times5\times10^{-4}}=125\ \mu$V.
- **Hot-probe (thermoelectric) test.** A heated probe makes majority carriers diffuse away from it; the polarity of the resulting voltage identifies the type.
- **Rectification test.** Contact a known-type sample and check which polarity conducts.

## Hardware relevance

- **Reverse leakage** of every diode and MOSFET drain-body junction is thermal generation of minority carriers; it roughly doubles per 10 °C.
- **Photodiodes and solar cells** create excess electron-hole pairs; the relative change in minority density is large, so the minority-carrier response gives the signal.
- **MOSFET inversion**: an NMOS has a P-type body, whose minority carriers (electrons) are drawn into the channel by the gate. The channel carriers are *minority* carriers of the body that have been made into the majority in the channel.
- **BJT design**: a heavily doped emitter and lightly doped base make emitter injection dominate, setting current gain.
:::

::: applications
- **Diode forward current**: minority-carrier injection and diffusion across the junction, the origin of the Shockley equation.
- **BJT amplifiers/switches**: current gain set by minority-carrier transport across the base.
- **Photodiodes, solar cells, CMOS image sensors**: light creates excess minority carriers collected by the junction.
- **Leakage and temperature drift**: thermally generated minority carriers in reverse-biased junctions limit low-current precision circuits (e.g., high-impedance op-amp inputs and sample-and-hold circuits).
- **MOSFET channels**: inversion layers use the body's minority carrier type, drawn in by the gate field.
- **Hall-effect sensors and wafer characterisation**: identify carrier type, density and mobility.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Thermal equilibrium and non-degenerate doping**, so $np=n_i^2$. Out of equilibrium (bias, light) the product changes and the quasi-Fermi-level picture is needed.
- **Silicon at 300 K** with $n_i=10^{10}$ cm⁻³ (sources quote $1.0$-$1.5\times10^{10}$). The minority densities scale with $n_i^2$, so a 1.5x change in $n_i$ changes them by 2.25x.
- **Full ionization and no compensation**. With both dopant types, majority density is the net doping.
- **Near-intrinsic or hot material**: use the exact quadratic solution and don't call either carrier "majority".
- **Valid alternative**: a short answer ("holes for P, electrons for N") is acceptable if the interviewer wants speed; expand only if asked.
:::

::: followups
### Compute the minority carrier density in P-type silicon with 5×10^16 cm⁻³ boron.
$n=n_i^2/N_A=(10^{10})^2/(5\times10^{16})=2\times10^{3}$ cm⁻³ (using $n_i=10^{10}$ cm⁻³). The hole density is about $5\times10^{16}$ cm⁻³, a ratio of $2.5\times10^{13}$. State your assumed $n_i$ because published values range from $1.0$ to $1.5\times10^{10}$ cm⁻³.

### Which carriers carry the current in a forward-biased PN diode?
Both. Far from the junction the current is majority-carrier drift. Near the junction, majority carriers cross the barrier and become minority carriers on the other side, where their diffusion current takes over, decaying as they recombine. The total current is continuous: electron current plus hole current is constant along the diode, but the split between them varies with position. The doping asymmetry determines which carrier dominates the injection, usually the one from the more heavily doped side.

### Why does reverse leakage double roughly every 10 °C?
Reverse current is dominated by minority-carrier generation (diffusion current $\propto n_i^2$, generation current $\propto n_i$). Since $n_i\propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/2kT}$ rises about 2.2x per 10 K near room temperature, generation current doubles roughly every 10 K, and the diffusion component rises faster, about 5x.

### What happens to the majority/minority distinction at very high temperature?
The intrinsic density grows exponentially and eventually exceeds the doping. For $10^{14}$ cm⁻³ doping in silicon this happens by roughly 450-500 K (about 200 °C). Beyond that, $n\approx p\approx n_i$ and the device loses its N/P character: junctions stop rectifying and transistors stop working. This is the fundamental high-temperature limit of silicon and the reason SiC and GaN are used for 200 °C+.

### How can you tell if a piece of silicon is N-type or P-type with just a voltmeter and a hot soldering iron?
Hot-probe test: touch a heated probe and a cold probe to the sample, and measure the voltage between them with a sensitive meter. Majority carriers diffuse away from the hot contact, so the hot probe is positive for N-type (electrons leave) and negative for P-type (holes leave). Polarity conventions depend on how the meter is connected, so calibrate with a known sample.

### Scenario: your N-type sample shows a positive Hall voltage. What might explain it?
Possibly it's actually P-type (mislabeled). But if it is a known N-type, the sign could be reversed if the sample is compensated and nearly intrinsic with holes of higher mobility, or if you are near a temperature where hole contribution dominates because the electron mobility has fallen. Also check your probe geometry and magnetic-field direction; wiring errors flip the sign. A temperature sweep of Hall sign and magnitude would separate these causes.
