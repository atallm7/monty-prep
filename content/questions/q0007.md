::: basics
### In plain words
Any wire, circuit-board trace (a flat copper track), or connector has a little resistance, and the power it wastes as heat is $I^2R$. The resistance is fixed, so the loss depends on the current, and it grows with the square of the current. For a given power delivered, a higher voltage needs less current, so the loss drops.

### Picture it
Imagine moving a fixed amount of cargo along a rough road. Bigger trucks mean fewer trips, so there is less wear on the road. Sending power at higher voltage is like using bigger trucks: the same power moves with less current. The analogy understates it, though. Wear grows with the number of trips, but electrical loss grows with the square of the current.

### Words you need
- **Conduction loss**: power wasted as heat because current flows through resistance.
- **$I^2R$ loss**: the power lost in a resistance $R$ carrying current $I$.
- **Power ($P = VI$)**: voltage times current. See [[q0006]].
- **Voltage drop**: the small voltage lost along the wire, equal to $IR$.
- **Switching loss**: energy lost each time a switch turns on or off, which depends on voltage too.

### The simplest example
Deliver 100 W through wires with 0.5 Ω total. At 10 V the current is 10 A, so the loss is $10^2 \times 0.5 = 50$ W. At 20 V the current is 5 A, so the loss is $5^2 \times 0.5 = 12.5$ W. Doubling the voltage cut the loss to one quarter. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows a fuller table, clears up why $V^2/R$ seems to say the opposite, and covers the cases where higher voltage does cost you.
:::

::: testing
This checks whether you understand $P = I^2R$ **in context**, not just as a formula:

1. The losses in wires, traces, connectors, windings and switches come from current flowing through their (small, unavoidable) resistance: $P_{loss} = I^2R$.
2. For a fixed delivered power $P = VI$, raising the voltage lowers the current, and the loss drops with the **square** of that: $P_{loss} = (P/V)^2R$.
3. You can resolve the classic confusion: "but $V^2/R$ says higher voltage means more loss!" (wrong $V$).
4. Bonus: you know where voltage *does* drive loss (switching, dielectric leakage, CMOS $CV^2f$), so "often" isn't "always".
:::

::: why
This principle shapes real systems at every scale: power grids transmit at hundreds of kV, data centres and cars are moving to 48 V buses, USB Power Delivery raises the voltage to 20 V or more to deliver 100 W through a thin cable, and multiphase buck converters split current across phases because conduction loss is quadratic. Interviewers (especially for power roles) use it to check that you can reason about efficiency and architecture, not just compute $P = VI$. It's also a common source of confusion, so a clean explanation stands out.
:::

::: concepts
- **Power delivered**: $P = VI$, so for a fixed power, $I = P/V$. See [[q0006]].
- **Conductor resistance**: every wire, trace, connector and switch has some $R$, set by $\rho L/A$. See [[q0015]].
- **Joule heating**: current through resistance dissipates $I^2R$, the energy lost to electron collisions. See [[q0002]].
- **Series path**: the load current is the same current that flows through the wiring (KCL), so wiring loss depends on the load's current. See [[q0004]].
- **Voltage across vs voltage of**: in $V^2/R$, $V$ is the voltage *across the resistance in question*.
- **Switching and dielectric losses**: the mechanisms where voltage matters directly. See [[q0110]].
:::

::: answer
"The loss in any conductor, like a wire, a PCB trace, a connector or a MOSFET's on-resistance, is $I^2R$. That resistance is fixed by the physical conductor, so the only lever you have is the current, and the loss goes with its square.

Now, for a given power I want to deliver, $P = VI$, so if I raise the voltage I need proportionally less current. Double the voltage, half the current, and the conduction loss drops to a quarter. That's why the grid transmits at hundreds of kilovolts and why USB-PD bumps up to 20 volts for 100 watts: 5 amps instead of 20.

The thing people trip on is $V^2/R$, which looks like higher voltage means more loss. But the $V$ in that formula is the voltage *across the wire*, the small drop $IR$, not the system voltage. Written correctly, it's the same $I^2R$.

I'd add that it's 'often', not 'always'. Switching losses in a converter scale with voltage times current times switching time, and insulation leakage and CMOS switching power scale with voltage too. So there's a trade-off: higher bus voltage cuts conduction loss but needs higher-rated parts and increases switching loss."
:::

::: technical
**Setup.** A source delivers power $P_L$ to a load at voltage $V$ through a path with total resistance $R_w$ (both conductors). By KCL the load current flows through the path:
$$I = \frac{P_L}{V},\qquad P_{loss} = I^2R_w = \frac{P_L^2R_w}{V^2}$$
At fixed $P_L$ and $R_w$, **loss ∝ 1/V²**. Fractional loss: $\frac{P_{loss}}{P_L} = \frac{P_LR_w}{V^2}$.

**Example** (100 W, $R_w$ = 0.5 Ω):

| Load voltage | Current | Wire loss | Source must supply | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 V | 8.33 A | 34.7 W | 16.2 V | 74% |
| 24 V | 4.17 A | 8.7 W | 26.1 V | 92% |
| 48 V | 2.08 A | 2.2 W | 49.0 V | 98% |
| 120 V | 0.83 A | 0.35 W | 120.4 V | 99.7% |

**Resolving $V^2/R$.** $P_{loss} = V_{drop}^2/R_w$, with $V_{drop} = IR_w$, which is identical to $I^2R_w$. The system voltage never appears in it.

**Where voltage drives loss:**
- **Hard-switching loss**: $P_{sw} \approx \tfrac12V_{in}I(t_r + t_f)f_{sw}$, plus $\tfrac12C_{oss}V^2f_{sw}$. Linear in $V$ for the overlap term.
- **CMOS dynamic power**: $\alpha CV^2f$.
- **Dielectric/leakage**: $V^2/R_{leak}$ and $\omega CV^2\tan\delta$.
- **Corona** on HV lines; **gate-drive** loss $Q_gV_{drv}f$.

**Device trade-off.** Higher-voltage MOSFETs have higher $R_{DS(on)}$ for a given die area (for silicon, specific on-resistance rises steeply, roughly as $V_{BR}^{2.5}$ in the ideal drift-region limit), so moving to a higher bus doesn't make conduction loss in the *switches* vanish.

**Multiphase/paralleling.** Splitting $I$ across $N$ equal paths of $R$ each: $N(I/N)^2R = I^2R/N$, so total conduction loss falls by $N$.
:::

::: short
"Losses in wires, traces and switches are $I^2R$, and the resistance is fixed by the conductor, so current is what you control. For a given power, $P = VI$, so raising voltage cuts current proportionally and cuts conduction loss with the square. That's why the grid uses high voltage and USB-PD goes to 20 volts. The $V^2/R$ form doesn't contradict it, because that $V$ is the drop across the wire. Switching and dielectric losses do scale with voltage, so it's a trade-off."
:::

::: long
"The key is to look at where the loss actually happens. Power is lost in the things *between* the source and the load: wires, PCB traces, connectors, fuses, the on-resistance of MOSFETs, inductor windings. Each of those is a resistance in series with the load, so the full load current flows through it and it dissipates $I^2R$.

That resistance is set by physics and cost: copper's resistivity, the wire gauge, the length. So for a given design the only thing I can change is the current. And because the loss goes as current squared, it's very sensitive to it.

Now combine that with $P = VI$. If I need to deliver 100 watts, I can do it at 12 volts and 8.3 amps, or 48 volts and 2.1 amps. Through half an ohm of cabling, the 12 volt version loses about 35 watts in the cable, while the 48 volt version loses about 2 watts. Four times the voltage, a sixteenth of the loss. That's the whole reason for high-voltage transmission lines, 48 volt server and automotive buses, and USB Power Delivery negotiating up to 20 volts.

A common objection is $P = V^2/R$: doesn't higher voltage mean more loss? The catch is which voltage. In $V^2/R$ for the wire, $V$ is the voltage drop across the wire, which is $I$ times $R_{wire}$. Substitute that in and you're back to $I^2R$. The system voltage doesn't appear.

I'd say 'often' is the right word, though. In a switching converter, the switching loss scales with input voltage times current times the transition time and frequency, and the energy in the switch node capacitance goes as $V^2$. CMOS switching power goes as $CV^2f$, and insulation leakage scales with voltage too. Higher voltages also need higher-rated MOSFETs, which have higher on-resistance. So in practice you pick the bus voltage that balances conduction loss against switching loss, safety and part cost, and then you attack the remaining current with fatter copper or by splitting it across phases."
:::

::: terms
- **Conduction loss**: $I^2R$ dissipated in any resistive element carrying current (wires, traces, $R_{DS(on)}$, DCR).
- **Switching loss**: energy lost each time a transistor transitions while it has both voltage across it and current through it.
- **IR drop**: the voltage lost across a conductor's resistance, $IR$; reduces the voltage available at the load.
- **Transmission/bus voltage**: the voltage at which power is distributed before conversion at the point of load.
- **Point-of-load (POL) converter**: a regulator placed next to the load to convert a higher bus voltage down locally.
- **Specific on-resistance**: $R_{DS(on)}\times$ area; rises with voltage rating, which is the device-level cost of high voltage.
- **Ampacity**: the current a conductor can carry within a temperature-rise limit.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Because $P = I^2R$."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Identifies *where* the loss is (series resistance of the delivery path) and why that resistance is fixed.
- Combines $P = VI$ and $I^2R$ to get $P_{loss} = P^2R/V^2$, and quantifies it ("4× voltage → 1/16 the loss").
- **Resolves the $V^2/R$ paradox** explicitly: the $V$ is the drop across the wire.
- Gives real examples: grid transmission, 48 V data-centre/automotive buses, USB-PD, PoE.
- Knows the counterweights: switching loss ∝ V, $CV^2f$, insulation/safety, higher $R_{DS(on)}$ at higher voltage rating, and splitting current across phases (multiphase) to reduce $I^2R$ as $1/N$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Higher voltage means more loss because $P = V^2/R$."** That $V$ must be the voltage across the lossy element. Across a wire, it's $IR_w$, which falls when you raise system voltage and lower current.
- **Thinking the load's resistance is the loss.** The load's power is the useful output. Loss is in the delivery path and the converters.
- **Forgetting the square.** Halving current cuts loss by 4×, not 2×. This is why paralleling conductors or phases is so effective.
- **Ignoring the voltage drop.** At 12 V and 8.3 A through 0.5 Ω, the load sees 4.2 V less than the source: a regulation problem as well as an efficiency one.
- **Claiming voltage never matters.** Switching losses, gate-drive losses, dielectric losses and safety/insulation all scale with voltage; the optimum is a trade-off.
- **Using average current for $I^2R$ with pulsating waveforms.** Conduction loss uses RMS current, which is larger than average for pulsed currents.
:::

The wiring resistance $R_w$ is fixed by copper, gauge and length. The *load* fixes the power $P_L$ it needs. The design freedom is the voltage $V$ at which you deliver it. Since $P_L = VI$:

$$I = \frac{P_L}{V}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad P_{loss} = I^2R_w = \frac{P_L^2\,R_w}{V^2}$$

So at fixed delivered power, **wiring loss falls as $1/V^2$**. The fractional loss is

$$\frac{P_{loss}}{P_L} = \frac{P_L R_w}{V^2}$$

which tells you directly how high the voltage must be for a given efficiency.

## Resolving the $V^2/R$ paradox

The objection always comes: "$P = V^2/R$, so a higher voltage should mean *more* loss." Let's be careful about *which* $V$.

For the wire, the power it dissipates is $V_{wire}^2/R_w$, where $V_{wire}$ is the voltage **across the wire**, from one end to the other. That drop is $V_{wire} = IR_w$, so

$$P_{loss} = \frac{(IR_w)^2}{R_w} = I^2R_w$$

The system voltage $V$ never appears. Raising $V$ at fixed power *reduces* $I$, which reduces the drop across the wire, which reduces the loss. The $V^2/R$ paradox comes from plugging the system voltage into a formula that wants the wire's own voltage drop.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Say it in one sentence: "$V^2/R$ and $I^2R$ are the same thing; the $V$ in $V^2/R$ is the drop across the lossy element, not the bus voltage." Interviewers often ask the follow-up specifically to see whether you can untangle this.

## Real systems built on this

**Power grid.** A power plant's output is stepped up to hundreds of kV for long-distance transmission, then stepped down in stages near consumers. Comparing 500 kV with 25 kV for the same power and line, the current is 20× lower and the $I^2R$ loss $20^2 = 400\times$ lower. AC won the "war of the currents" partly because transformers made this voltage change cheap.

**USB Power Delivery.** Original USB supplied 5 V. To deliver 100 W at 5 V would take 20 A, which would need impractically thick cables and connectors. USB-PD negotiates up to 20 V at 5 A (and the newer Extended Power Range goes higher still), so the same thin cable carries 4× less current for the same power. With, say, 0.1 Ω of cable and connector resistance, 5 A loses 2.5 W while 20 A would lose 40 W.

**48 V data-centre and automotive buses.** Server racks and mild-hybrid vehicles have moved from 12 V to 48 V distribution: 4× less current, 16× less distribution loss, thinner busbars and harnesses. A point-of-load converter near each processor then steps down to ≈1 V.

**Power over Ethernet** delivers tens of watts over thin twisted pairs at roughly 48–57 V for the same reason.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact limits evolve with standard revisions: USB-PD's Standard Power Range tops out at 20 V/5 A (100 W) and Extended Power Range at 48 V (240 W); PoE classes range from ~15 W to ~90 W at the source. Check the current revision of the USB-IF and IEEE 802.3 specifications for exact values.

## Inside a board: the same principle

The same reasoning applies at PCB scale:

- **Point-of-load conversion**: a 1 V, 50 A processor rail can't be distributed across a board from a distant regulator; even 1 mΩ of plane and via resistance drops 50 mV (5% of the rail) and burns 2.5 W. So the regulator sits right next to the processor, fed from a 12 V or 5 V intermediate bus at a few amps.
- **Multiphase bucks**: splitting $I$ across $N$ phases, each with its own inductor and switches of resistance $R$, gives total conduction loss $N\cdot(I/N)^2R = I^2R/N$. Four phases have a quarter of the conduction loss of one phase built from the same parts.
- **Paralleling MOSFETs**: two identical FETs in parallel halve conduction loss for the same total current.
- **Trace width**: doubling a trace's width halves $R$ and halves its loss at the same current, but it's the current that sets the requirement ([[q0015]]).

## When voltage does matter

The answer says "often", and a strong candidate explains why not "always". Several losses scale with voltage:

| Loss | Scales as | Where |
|---|---|---|
| Hard-switching overlap | $\tfrac12 VI(t_r + t_f)f_{sw}$ | MOSFETs in converters |
| Output-capacitance loss | $\tfrac12 C_{oss}V^2f_{sw}$ | each hard turn-on |
| CMOS dynamic power | $\alpha CV^2f$ | digital logic ([[q0110]]) |
| Dielectric loss | $\omega CV^2\tan\delta$ | capacitors, PCB at high frequency ([[q0058]]) |
| Leakage | $V^2/R_{leak}$ | insulation, reverse-biased junctions |
| Corona | rises steeply above a threshold field | HV transmission lines |

**Worked comparison.** A converter switching 5 A at 500 kHz with 20 ns total transition time: at 12 V the overlap loss is $\tfrac12(12)(5)(20\text{ ns})(500\text{ kHz}) = 0.3$ W; at 48 V it's 1.2 W. Raising the bus voltage moved loss from the cables into the switches.

**Device rating penalty.** A MOSFET rated for 100 V has substantially higher $R_{DS(on)}$ than a 30 V part of the same die size, because the drift region that holds off the voltage is thicker and more lightly doped. So a higher bus voltage partly gives back conduction-loss savings inside the converter itself.

**Safety.** Above about 60 V DC, safety standards treat circuits as hazardous, requiring more insulation and clearance. That's one reason 48 V (with headroom below 60 V) is a popular choice.

The system designer's job is to pick the bus voltage that minimises total loss (wires + conversion) within safety and cost constraints. Conduction loss pushes the voltage up; switching loss, device ratings and safety push it down.
:::

::: applications
- **High-voltage transmission**: grid transmission at hundreds of kV keeps $I^2R$ losses to a few percent over long distances.
- **USB Power Delivery**: 100 W at 20 V/5 A instead of 5 V/20 A, keeping cable and connector loss manageable.
- **48 V server and automotive power**: 16× lower distribution loss than 12 V for the same power, with POL converters stepping down near the loads.
- **Power over Ethernet**: tens of watts over thin twisted pairs at ~50 V.
- **Multiphase VRMs for CPUs/GPUs**: dividing 100+ A across many phases cuts $I^2R$ loss and spreads heat.
- **Battery packs**: higher-voltage EV packs (e.g. 400 V → 800 V architectures) reduce current, cable mass and charging-cable heating.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Fixed delivered power** and **fixed wiring resistance**: the comparison assumes the same load power and the same copper. If you're allowed fatter copper, you can also reduce loss that way.
- **Resistive path losses dominate**: true for distribution and conduction losses; in fast converters, switching losses can dominate instead.
- **DC or RMS quantities**: for AC or pulsating currents use RMS current; at high frequency, skin effect raises the effective $R_w$ ([[q0056]]).
- **Load voltage held at V**: the source must supply $V + IR_w$; if the source is fixed instead, the load voltage sags.

A valid alternative angle: the question can be read as "why are *conduction* losses current-driven", which is the core answer, or as "why do engineers raise voltage to save power", which is the system-level answer. Cover both.
:::

::: followups
### If you double the transmission voltage for the same power, what happens to the loss?
Current halves, so $I^2R$ falls to a quarter. In the example, going from 24 V to 48 V at 100 W through 0.5 Ω cuts loss from 8.7 W to 2.2 W.

### Then why not use 1000 V everywhere on a board?
Because the loads need low voltage (processors run at ≈1 V), so you'd need converters with high step-down ratios, which have worse efficiency and very short duty cycles. High-voltage MOSFETs have higher $R_{DS(on)}$ and switching losses grow with voltage. And above ~60 V DC you face safety rules, larger creepage and clearance distances, and more expensive parts. Typical architectures use an intermediate bus (12 V or 48 V) and point-of-load converters.

### How does a multiphase buck reduce conduction losses?
Each phase carries $I/N$. Conduction loss per phase is $(I/N)^2R$, and with $N$ phases the total is $I^2R/N$. Interleaving also cancels ripple current at the output capacitors, reducing their RMS current and loss. A 4-phase design carrying 40 A has a quarter of the conduction loss of a single 40 A phase built from the same parts.

### A 3.3 V, 3 A load is 30 cm from its regulator through a trace with 20 mΩ total round-trip resistance. What's the loss and drop?
Drop: $3\times0.02 = 60$ mV (1.8% of 3.3 V). Loss: $9\times0.02 = 0.18$ W. If the same 10 W were delivered at 12 V (0.83 A) and converted locally, the trace drop would be 17 mV and its loss 14 mW, about 13× lower.

### What if the load is a constant-resistance heater instead of a constant-power device? (harder probe)
Then raising the voltage *increases* the current ($I = V/R_L$) and the load power goes up as $V^2$, so the comparison changes: you're not delivering the same power any more. The "raise voltage to cut loss" argument assumes the load (or a converter at the load) draws a fixed power. For a fixed resistive load, the fraction lost in the wires is $R_w/(R_w + R_L)$, independent of voltage; to cut that fraction you'd rewind the heater for higher voltage (higher $R_L$), which is effectively the same choice.
:::

::: interpretations
"Why is power loss often due to current, not voltage?" assumes you're delivering a fixed amount of power through a resistive path, which is the standard context (transmission, power distribution, conduction loss). The word "often" invites you to mention the exceptions: switching losses, CMOS $CV^2f$, dielectric and leakage losses, all of which scale with voltage.
:::
