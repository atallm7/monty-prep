::: testing
Whether you know the body diode is **not a part someone added** but a consequence of how a MOSFET is built, and whether you can predict its consequences in a real circuit. The interviewer wants to hear:

1. The origin: the p-body to n-drain PN junction that exists whenever the body is tied to the source (NMOS: anode at source, cathode at drain).
2. Which way it conducts, which makes a MOSFET switch block voltage in only one direction when off.
3. The consequences: it conducts during dead time in half-bridges, has a poor forward drop (about 0.7-1 V) and **reverse-recovery charge** $Q_{rr}$, and sets limits for ideal-diode and back-to-back switch designs.
:::

::: why
Every synchronous buck, motor H-bridge and inverter has current flowing through a MOSFET body diode for part of every cycle, and it is a major source of loss, EMI and even failures (shoot-through from reverse recovery). Knowing where it comes from tells you why you can't remove it, how to bypass it (turn the channel on), and when to design around it (back-to-back FETs). It is also the key to why load switches and level shifters behave the way they do ([[q0104]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **PN junction**: forward biased above about 0.6-0.7 V in silicon; blocks in reverse. See [[q0072]].
- **MOSFET structure**: the p-type body (NMOS) touches the n-type drain, forming a PN junction. See [[q0096]].
- **Source-body tie**: the body is shorted to the source so the junction becomes a diode between source and drain. See [[q0091]].
- **Reverse recovery**: stored minority charge $Q_{rr}$ must be swept out when the diode turns off, causing a current spike.
- **Dead time**: the interval in a half-bridge when both switches are off; the inductor current flows through a body diode.
- **Synchronous rectification**: turning the FET channel on while the body diode would conduct so current flows through $R_{DS(on)}$ instead of a 0.7 V drop.
:::

::: answer
"The body diode is the PN junction between the MOSFET's body and its drain. In an N-channel device the body is p-type and the drain is n-type, so there's a diode with its anode at the body and its cathode at the drain. In a discrete power MOSFET the body is shorted to the source, so it looks like a diode from source to drain, anode at the source. For a PMOS it's flipped: anode at the drain, cathode at the source.

It comes from the construction, not from anything added. In silicon, any time you put n-type next to p-type you get a junction. The consequence is that an off MOSFET only blocks voltage in one direction. If the drain goes below the source on an NMOS, the diode conducts regardless of the gate.

In practice that matters in two ways. In a half-bridge, during dead time the inductor current freewheels through the body diode with a 0.7 to 1 V drop, wasting power, and when the opposite FET turns on the diode has to recover its stored charge, causing a current spike. So we turn the channel on during that interval, synchronous rectification, so current takes the low-resistance path. And if I need to block in both directions, like a load switch with a battery, I put two FETs back to back so one body diode always blocks."
:::

::: technical
**Origin.** In a vertical power NMOS, the p-body is diffused into an n⁻ drift epitaxial layer on an n⁺ substrate (drain). The source n⁺ is inside the p-body. The p-body to n⁻ drift junction is a PN junction; the body is shorted to the source by a metal contact, so the junction lies between source (anode) and drain (cathode).

**Polarity.**
- N-channel: anode = source, cathode = drain. Conducts when $V_{SD}>V_F$ (drain below source by about 0.7 V).
- P-channel: anode = drain, cathode = source. Conducts when $V_{DS}>V_F$.

**Parameters.**
- $V_{SD}$ (forward drop): about 0.7-1.2 V at rated current for silicon power MOSFETs. A SiC MOSFET's body diode drop is considerably higher, a few volts (check the datasheet).
- $Q_{rr}$ / $t_{rr}$: stored charge and recovery time; tens of nC for small devices, hundreds of nC and up for high-voltage super-junction devices. Super-junction parts can have especially poor recovery.
- Rated continuous diode current, $I_S$, and pulsed current.

**Loss example.** A synchronous buck with $I_L=10$ A, body diode drop 0.8 V, dead time 20 ns at each edge (40 ns per cycle), 500 kHz:

$$P_{dt}=I_LV_F\cdot t_{dead}\,f_{sw}=10(0.8)(40\text{ ns})(500\text{ kHz})=0.16\text{ W}$$

Add reverse recovery of the low-side body diode, roughly $Q_{rr}V_{in}f_{sw}$: with $Q_{rr}=50$ nC, $V_{in}=12$ V: $0.3$ W. Both are small fractions of 10 A output, but they grow with $f_{sw}$. If the body diode carried the whole freewheel current continuously it would dissipate $10\times0.8=8$ W versus $I^2R_{DS(on)}=100\times5\text{ m}\Omega=0.5$ W through the channel, which is why synchronous rectification turns the channel on.

**Parasitic BJT.** The n⁺ source / p-body / n⁻ drain also forms a parasitic NPN transistor. The body-source short keeps it off; fast dv/dt or avalanche can turn it on and damage the device, a reason for ruggedness specs (avalanche energy rating).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $V_F$, $Q_{rr}$ and $t_{rr}$ vary widely with voltage class, technology (planar, trench, super-junction, SiC, GaN) and with $di/dt$ and temperature. GaN HEMTs have no body diode in the PN sense but conduct in reverse with a higher drop by the channel itself. Use datasheet curves.
:::

::: short
"The body diode is the PN junction between the MOSFET's body and its drain, there because of how the transistor is built. With the body tied to the source, an NMOS has a diode with its anode at the source and cathode at the drain, so the FET can't block voltage in the reverse direction, and a PMOS is the opposite. It matters in half-bridges: during dead time inductor current flows through it at 0.7 to 1 V and it has reverse-recovery charge, so we turn the channel on for synchronous rectification, and use back-to-back FETs when we need two-way blocking."
:::

::: long
"A MOSFET is built from layers of doped silicon, and wherever p meets n there's a PN junction. In an NMOS the channel region is a p-type body sandwiched between an n⁺ source and an n-type drain. The body is shorted to the source to keep the parasitic NPN transistor off, which leaves the p-body to n-drain junction as a diode: anode on the source, cathode on the drain. In a PMOS everything flips, so the anode is on the drain.

The first consequence is directional blocking. When the transistor is off, it blocks positive drain-to-source voltage for an NMOS, but if the drain falls below the source by about 0.7 volts, the diode conducts, whatever the gate is doing. So a single MOSFET is not a bidirectional switch.

The second consequence is in switching converters. In a synchronous buck, the inductor current has to keep flowing during the dead time when both FETs are off, and it takes the low-side body diode, dropping about 0.7 to 1 volt. At 10 amps that's 8 or so watts if it stayed there, versus half a watt through the channel, so we turn the low-side channel on right after the dead time, which is synchronous rectification. We keep dead times short, and for very fast converters add a Schottky in parallel, or use a part with a soft, fast-recovering body diode.

The third is reverse recovery. When the high-side switch turns on, the diode has to clear its stored charge, which shows up as a spike of current through the high-side FET and a voltage ringing at the switch node. For back-to-back switches or battery load switches, I put the two FETs source to source so one body diode always blocks."
:::

::: terms
- **Body diode**: the PN junction between a MOSFET's body and drain, appearing from source to drain (NMOS) when the body is tied to the source.
- **Anode / cathode**: for NMOS the anode is the source, cathode the drain; reversed for PMOS.
- **$V_{SD}$ (diode forward voltage)**: drop across the body diode at a given current.
- **Reverse recovery ($Q_{rr}$, $t_{rr}$)**: charge and time needed to remove stored carriers when the diode is switched off.
- **Dead time**: the time both switches in a half-bridge are off.
- **Synchronous rectification**: turning on the MOSFET channel to carry current that would otherwise flow in the diode.
- **Parasitic BJT**: the NPN formed by source, body and drain; kept off by the source-body short.
- **Back-to-back FETs**: two MOSFETs in series with opposed body diodes to block in both directions.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "it's a diode from source to drain, it comes from the PN junction." An exceptional answer:

- Identifies which junction (p-body / n-drift) and why the **body-source short** makes it a two-terminal diode.
- Gets polarity right for both N and P devices and explains the **one-directional blocking** consequence.
- Quantifies the loss: forward drop $\times$ current $\times$ dead time, and $Q_{rr}$-related loss, and compares with channel conduction.
- Gives remedies: synchronous rectification, shorter dead time, parallel Schottky, soft-recovery devices, back-to-back FETs.
- Mentions the **parasitic BJT** and ruggedness, and notes that GaN and SiC behave differently.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying the diode is "added for protection".** It is intrinsic to the structure. Some devices rate it for avalanche or for continuous current, but the junction exists regardless.
- **Getting the polarity backwards.** NMOS: anode on the source. PMOS: anode on the drain. A frequent trap with high-side PMOS load switches.
- **Ignoring it in a reverse-voltage condition.** A PMOS load switch in "off" still conducts from drain to source through its body diode if the output is higher than the input.
- **Believing a MOSFET blocks in both directions.** Only in the direction opposite to the body diode.
- **Quoting 0.7 V without checking.** At high current the power MOSFET body diode drop is often closer to 1 V or more, and SiC's is much larger.
- **Ignoring $Q_{rr}$ in fast converters.** Recovery spikes cause ringing, EMI and extra loss, even though the forward drop looks small.
:::

::: deep
## Where the diode comes from

Look at the cross-section of a vertical power NMOS (a DMOS cell). From top to bottom: the gate sits over a p-type **body** region. Inside the body is the n⁺ **source**. Below the body is a lightly doped n⁻ **drift region** that supports the blocking voltage, and below that an n⁺ substrate that serves as the **drain** contact on the back of the die.

Because the body is shorted to the source metal, the junction sits between source and drain. When the gate is high, the channel forms along the surface of the p-body between n⁺ source and drift region. Not to scale.

The p-body must be in contact with something. If it floats, the n⁺/p/n⁻ stack is a bipolar transistor. Shorting the body to the source makes that BJT's base-emitter junction sit at zero volts, which keeps it off, and the cost is that the junction from the body to the drift region becomes a free-standing diode.

In an integrated CMOS process the same thing happens differently: each source/drain n⁺ region sits in a p-substrate (or well), forming diodes to the body node. In a typical IC the body is tied to ground (NMOS) or $V_{DD}$ (PMOS), so these diodes are reverse biased in normal operation and become the ESD diodes or leakage paths ([[q0112]]).

## What it does to the circuit

**Blocking is directional.** An off NMOS with drain at +10 V, source at 0 V: diode reverse biased, blocks. With drain at -10 V: diode conducts, regardless of the gate. So a single MOSFET is a *unidirectional* switch.

**Half-bridge dead time.** Consider a synchronous buck stage. When the high-side switch turns off, the inductor current must continue. The switch node swings below ground until the low-side body diode conducts at about $-V_F$ (the node falls until something can carry the current). If the controller then turns the low-side channel on, the current moves from the diode to the channel. After the dead time, the switch node is at $-I_LR_{DS(on)}$ instead of $-V_F$.

Once the low-side gate rises the channel takes over. The shorter the dead time, the less time spent in the lossy diode, but too short and both devices conduct (shoot-through, see [[q0109]]).

**Reverse recovery.** If the body diode was conducting, then when the high side turns on, the diode must be reverse-biased from $-0.8$ V to $+V_{in}$. Until its stored charge $Q_{rr}$ is removed it looks like a short, so a current spike $\approx I_{rr}$ flows from $V_{in}$ through the high-side FET and the diode, then the diode snaps off. The spike dissipates energy roughly $Q_{rr}V_{in}$ per cycle and excites ringing through the loop inductance.

## Worked numbers

Synchronous buck, 12 V in, 10 A load, 500 kHz, low-side FET with $R_{DS(on)}=5$ mΩ, $V_F=0.8$ V, $Q_{rr}=50$ nC, dead time 20 ns per edge:

| Loss mechanism | Formula | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Diode conduction during dead time | $I\,V_F\,t_{dead,tot}f$ | $10\times0.8\times40\text{ ns}\times500\text{ kHz}=0.16$ W |
| Reverse recovery | $Q_{rr}V_{in}f$ | $50\text{ nC}\times12\times500\text{ kHz}=0.30$ W |
| If diode carried the whole off-time (no sync) | $I\,V_F(1-D)$, $D=0.3$ | $10\times0.8\times0.7=5.6$ W |
| Channel conduction | $I^2R_{DS(on)}(1-D)$ | $100\times0.005\times0.7=0.35$ W |

The table shows why synchronous rectification is a big win: 5.6 W vs 0.35 W. It also shows the remaining body-diode losses, 0.46 W together, which scale linearly with frequency.

## Remedies

- **Sync rectification** with minimal dead time. Adaptive dead-time controllers sense the switch node.
- **Schottky in parallel** (some vendors integrate the Schottky on the same die as the MOSFET): the Schottky takes the dead-time current at 0.3-0.4 V with no stored charge. Layout inductance between the FET and Schottky matters; a discrete Schottky only helps if it is very close.
- **Soft-recovery or fast-recovery body diodes** (datasheets advertise these as fast-body-diode versions).
- **Different technology.** GaN HEMTs have no minority-carrier body diode (zero $Q_{rr}$) but conduct in reverse through the channel with a higher drop, so dead time is more critical.
- **Back-to-back FETs** (source-to-source or drain-to-drain) when bidirectional blocking is needed: battery disconnect switches, USB-C power paths, load switches that must prevent reverse current.

## The PMOS load switch trap

A PMOS high-side switch has its source on the input and its drain on the output, so its body diode has the anode on the output and the cathode on the input. If the input collapses (a USB cable is pulled out) while the output is held up by a capacitor or a second supply, the output back-feeds the input through that diode, even with the gate off. That is why power-OR-ing and ideal-diode circuits use back-to-back PMOS with the sources tied together.

## Real hardware

- A device's datasheet gives $V_{SD}$ vs $I_S$ curves and $Q_{rr}$, $t_{rr}$ at a specified $di/dt$ and temperature.
- Super-junction MOSFETs, used at 400-650 V, have a body diode with large $Q_{rr}$ and snappy recovery; they are often avoided in hard-switched half-bridges, which is why bridge-leg designs use dedicated fast-body-diode versions.
- Power modules and motor drives use an anti-parallel diode, either the body diode or a separate fast diode, for the inductive freewheel current ([[q0114]]).

> [!TIP]
> If you ever see a MOSFET "switching" at the wrong polarity and the circuit seems to conduct with the gate off, check the body diode first.
:::

::: applications
- **Synchronous buck/boost converters**: dead-time conduction through the low-side body diode and its reverse recovery.
- **H-bridge motor drivers**: freewheel path for motor current when the bridge is switched off (inductive kick) ([[q0092]]).
- **Reverse-polarity protection**: a PMOS in series with the supply; the body diode conducts initially and the channel takes over at low drop.
- **Battery disconnect / USB power-path switches**: back-to-back FETs to block current in both directions.
- **Single-NMOS level shifter**: the body diode charges the low side line on the first pull-up (see [[q0104]]).
- **Inductive load drivers (relays, solenoids)**: body diode as a flyback path in a low-side switch when the supply is clamped.
:::

::: assumptions
- Discussion is for **discrete enhancement MOSFETs with the body tied to the source** (power devices). In ICs the body is a common substrate or well, and the "diode" is the junction from each source/drain to it.
- $V_F$ and recovery numbers are **typical silicon** values; SiC and GaN differ (higher drop, minimal or no recovery respectively).
- The loss examples assume **continuous conduction** and rectangular dead-time windows; real converters have more nuanced timing.
- For **depletion or JFET** devices the intrinsic diode analysis differs.
- If the interviewer's context is a **half-bridge**, emphasize dead time and $Q_{rr}$. If it is a **load switch**, emphasize blocking direction and back-to-back FETs.
:::

::: followups
### Which way does the body diode point in an NMOS vs a PMOS?
NMOS: anode on the source, cathode on the drain (it conducts when the drain is below the source). PMOS: anode on the drain, cathode on the source. A way to remember it is the arrow on the symbol's body connection: the diode points the same way as the arrow, from the body (source) into the channel for NMOS.

### Why do we connect the body to the source in a discrete MOSFET?
To keep the parasitic NPN transistor (source, body, drain) off and to remove the body effect, which would raise $V_t$ as source rises. The cost is the intrinsic diode. A separate body terminal, as in IC processes, lets you reverse-bias the junction instead.

### How do you build a bidirectional switch from MOSFETs?
Two FETs in series with their sources tied together (or drains), so the body diodes point in opposite directions. Whichever polarity the voltage is, one diode is reverse-biased, and the two gates are driven together. Cost: twice the $R_{on}$.

### Estimate the energy lost per cycle to reverse recovery of the low-side diode with $Q_{rr}=50$ nC, $V_{in}=12$ V.
Roughly $Q_{rr}V_{in}=50\text{ nC}\times12\text{ V}=0.6$ µJ per cycle; at 500 kHz that is 0.3 W. Real $E_{rr}$ includes the diode's own and the stored energy in capacitances, so I'd read it from the datasheet or measure it.

### Harder: a Schottky diode in parallel with the FET doesn't seem to reduce dead-time loss. Why?
The Schottky only takes current if its path has lower impedance than the body diode path. Loop inductance between the Schottky and the switching node (long traces or leads, tens of nH) delays the commutation: during the fast $di/dt$ the inductance forces current through the body diode first. It works only if placed very close or integrated on the die. See also the effect on $Q_{rr}$ from the body diode part.

### Scenario: a PMOS load switch is "off" with its gate at the input voltage, but the output still sits near 4.3 V when the input rail is switched to 0 V. Why?
The output is held up by another source (a second supply or a charged capacitor with a light load). The PMOS body diode has its anode on the output and cathode on the input, so with the input at 0 V it conducts from the output back into the input rail and holds the output near $V_{in}+V_F$ or feeds the dead rail. The gate doesn't matter. The fix is a back-to-back PMOS pair (sources tied together) or a load-switch IC with reverse-current blocking.
:::

::: interpretations
"Where does it come from?" can be answered at two levels: the **device level** (the PN junction between body and drain exists because of the doping profile) and the **circuit level** (the body is tied to the source, making it a source-drain diode). A strong answer gives both. The question appears in the MOSFET section, but the buck-converter questions Q208-Q210 revisit the *consequences* of this diode in a power bridge.
