::: basics
### In plain words
Roll-off is how quickly a filter's output shrinks once you pass the corner frequency. A first-order low-pass rolls off at -20 dB per decade. In plain terms, every time you multiply the frequency by ten, the output voltage drops to one tenth.

### Picture it
Picture walking down a staircase where every step is the same height. Each step is a tenfold jump in frequency, and each step down is the same 20 dB drop. The staircase never gets steeper or shallower. That is why a log-frequency graph shows a straight line. The staircase only starts right at the corner, so close to the corner the real curve is gently rounded.

### Words you need
- **Roll-off**: how fast the output falls above the corner. Larger slope means a sharper filter.
- **Decade**: a factor of ten in frequency.
- **Octave**: a factor of two in frequency. For a first-order filter that is about -6 dB.
- **Decibel (dB)**: $20\log_{10}$ of a voltage ratio. -20 dB is one tenth.
- **Corner frequency**: the point where the output is down 3 dB. See [[q0129]].

### The simplest example
Take a low-pass with a 1 kHz corner and feed in a 1 V wave. At 10 kHz, the output is about 0.1 V (-20 dB). At 100 kHz it is about 0.01 V (-40 dB). Double the frequency instead, from 10 kHz to 20 kHz, and the output halves, from 0.1 V to about 0.05 V. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows why the math gives exactly $f_c/f$ far above the corner, and why more poles mean steeper slopes.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know the number **and** can derive it. The interviewer expects:

1. **−20 dB per decade** (equivalently about **−6 dB per octave**) for a first-order low-pass, above the corner.
2. A derivation: for $f\gg f_c$, $|H|\approx f_c/f$, so amplitude is inversely proportional to frequency.
3. How to use it: how much attenuation you get at a given frequency, and how many poles you need for a target.
4. The caveats: the slope is asymptotic (gentler near the corner), the slope is −20 dB/decade *per pole*, and real circuits flatten out at high frequency because of parasitics.
:::

::: why
The roll-off rate tells you how much a filter can reject. A first-order RC filter gives only 20 dB of attenuation one decade above its corner, which is often not enough for anti-aliasing, supply filtering or EMI. Knowing the slope lets you decide quickly how many poles are needed, where to put the corner, and whether a given filter will meet a spec before you simulate. It also links filters to op-amp gain-bandwidth behavior, where the same −20 dB/decade slope sets the closed-loop bandwidth (see [[q0126]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Decibels**: $|H|_{dB}=20\log_{10}|H|$; a factor of 10 in amplitude is 20 dB.
- **Decade and octave**: ×10 and ×2 in frequency.
- **First-order low-pass magnitude**: $|H| = 1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}$. See [[q0129]].
- **Asymptote**: for $f\gg f_c$, $|H|\to f_c/f$.
- **Poles and slope**: each real pole adds −20 dB/decade. See [[q0010]].
- **Integrator behavior**: a low-pass above its corner acts like an integrator (−90° phase, amplitude ∝ $1/f$).
:::

::: answer
"The roll-off of a first-order low-pass is minus 20 dB per decade, which is the same as about minus 6 dB per octave. So once you're above the corner frequency, every time you multiply the frequency by ten, the output amplitude drops by a factor of ten, and every time you double the frequency it halves.

Here's why. The transfer function magnitude is one over the square root of one plus $(f/f_c)^2$. When $f$ is much bigger than $f_c$ the one under the root is negligible, so the magnitude is just $f_c/f$. That's an inverse relationship: ten times the frequency, one tenth the amplitude, and twenty log of a tenth is minus 20 dB.

Physically, the capacitor is acting like a short, so the output is the current through the capacitor times the tiny impedance, and that impedance falls as one over frequency. It's behaving like an integrator.

The slope is only approached asymptotically. At the corner it's down 3 dB, and one decade above, it's minus 20.04 dB, so it's essentially exact there. And each additional pole adds another 20 dB per decade, so if I need 40 dB per decade I'd use a second-order filter."
:::

::: technical
**Slope derivation.** $|H(f)| = \left[1+(f/f_c)^2\right]^{-1/2}$. In dB: $20\log_{10}|H| = -10\log_{10}\left[1+(f/f_c)^2\right]$. For $f\gg f_c$:
$$|H|_{dB}\approx -20\log_{10}\frac{f}{f_c}$$
A decade ($f\to10f$) subtracts $20\log_{10}10 = 20$ dB: **−20 dB/decade**. An octave ($f\to 2f$) subtracts $20\log_{10}2 = 6.02$ dB: **−6.02 dB/octave**.

**Local slope.** The instantaneous slope in dB per decade is $-20\,\frac{(f/f_c)^2}{1+(f/f_c)^2}$. At $f=f_c$ it is −10 dB/dec; at $f = 3f_c$ it is −18 dB/dec; at $10 f_c$ it is −19.8 dB/dec.

**Attenuation table.**

| $f/f_c$ | $|H|$ | dB |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.707 | −3.01 |
| 2 | 0.447 | −6.99 |
| 10 | 0.0995 | −20.04 |
| 100 | 0.0100 | −40.00 |
| 1000 | 0.0010 | −60.00 |

**Higher order.** $n$ identical (buffered) poles give $-20n$ dB/decade, e.g. $-40$ dB/dec for second-order. Phase tends to $-90^\circ n$.

**Example.** A 1 kHz RC filter attenuates 1 MHz by 60 dB (a factor of 1000), ignoring parasitics.

**Real limits.** Parasitic capacitance across the resistor, capacitor ESL, and coupling between input and output set a floor on the stopband attenuation, so measured roll-off typically flattens or turns up at high frequency.
:::

::: long
"A first-order low-pass has a roll-off of minus 20 dB per decade, which is also minus 6 dB per octave, once you're past the corner frequency.

To see where that comes from, the magnitude is one over root of one plus $(f/f_c)^2$. Well above the corner, the one is negligible and the magnitude is just $f_c/f$. That says the output amplitude is inversely proportional to frequency. If I go up a decade, ten times the frequency, the amplitude drops to a tenth. In dB, that's twenty times log of a tenth, which is minus 20. If I double the frequency, the amplitude halves, and twenty log of a half is minus 6 dB.

Physically, the capacitor's impedance is $1/(2\pi f C)$, so it halves each time the frequency doubles. Once it's far smaller than the resistor, the circuit is a divider where the capacitor's share just scales with that impedance. It's behaving like an integrator, which is also why the phase heads toward minus 90 degrees.

Two practical points. First, the slope is only the asymptotic value. At the corner the actual response is down 3 dB, and the local slope is about minus 10 dB per decade. By ten times the corner you're at minus 20.04 dB, so the asymptote is very good from there on. Second, 20 dB per decade isn't much. A 1 kHz first-order filter has only 20 dB of attenuation at 10 kHz. If I need, say, 60 dB at 10 times the corner, I need three poles. I'd get them with cascaded buffered sections, or with a higher-order active filter such as Sallen-Key.

And in a real board, the measured roll-off flattens out at high frequency: capacitor ESL, stray capacitance across the resistor, and coupling around the filter limit the attenuation, so I'd keep the layout tight and put the filter close to what it protects."
:::

::: terms
- **Roll-off rate**: how fast the gain falls above the cutoff, in dB per decade or per octave.
- **Decade**: a tenfold change in frequency.
- **Octave**: a twofold change in frequency.
- **Asymptotic slope**: the slope far from the corner (−20 dB/dec for one pole).
- **Pole**: a transfer-function root, each real pole adds −20 dB/dec.
- **Attenuation**: reduction in amplitude, in dB: $-20\log_{10}|H|$.
- **Stopband**: the frequency range where the filter attenuates strongly.
- **Order**: the number of poles (for the filter types here).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "−20 dB/decade".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the number in **both units** (−20 dB/decade, −6 dB/octave) and explains each.
- **Derives** it from $|H|\approx f_c/f$ in one line, and links it to the capacitor impedance falling as $1/f$.
- Notes it's **asymptotic**: −3 dB at $f_c$, local slope of −10 dB/dec there, and −20.04 dB at $10f_c$.
- Uses it for a **calculation**: attenuation at a given frequency, or how many poles are needed for a spec.
- Mentions **practical limits**: the slope is per pole, cascaded unbuffered RC sections interact, and parasitics flatten the high-frequency response.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Quoting −20 dB/octave** or **−6 dB/decade**. The pairing is −20 dB/decade and −6 dB/octave.
- **Assuming the slope holds from the corner.** Near $f_c$, the actual curve is gentler, and −3 dB is the value at the corner, not −20.
- **Confusing amplitude with power.** −20 dB per decade is a factor of 10 in *voltage* and 100 in power.
- **Saying a first-order filter gives 40 dB/decade because there is both R and C.** The slope depends on the number of poles (energy-storing elements), not components.
- **Forgetting that each added pole adds −20 dB/dec only if buffered**; unbuffered cascades interact and shift the corner.
- **Expecting infinite attenuation.** A real filter has a finite stopband floor from parasitics and layout.
- **Counting poles from the number of capacitors** without checking whether they're independent (capacitors in parallel are one pole).
:::

::: deep
## Where −20 dB/decade comes from

Write the divider with the capacitor's impedance explicitly:
$$H = \frac{Z_C}{R+Z_C},\quad Z_C=\frac{1}{j2\pi fC}$$
For frequencies where $|Z_C|\ll R$:
$$|H|\approx\frac{|Z_C|}{R}=\frac{1}{2\pi fRC}=\frac{f_c}{f}.$$
So the transfer function is just the capacitor's impedance divided by $R$, and capacitor impedance is inversely proportional to frequency. Plot $f_c/f$ on log axes and you get a straight line of slope $-1$: one decade down in amplitude per decade up in frequency. In dB that's −20 per decade. The line crosses 0 dB at $f = f_c$, which is the origin of the "corner".

## Octaves and decades

A decade is a factor of 10 in frequency and an octave a factor of 2. Since $\log_{10}2=0.301$:
$$20\ \text{dB/decade}\times 0.301 = 6.02\ \text{dB/octave}.$$
So "−6 dB/octave" and "−20 dB/decade" are the same slope. That's the amplitude halving each time the frequency doubles.

## The slope near the corner is gentler

Differentiate the dB magnitude with respect to $\log_{10}f$. With $x = f/f_c$:
$$\frac{d|H|_{dB}}{d\log_{10}f} = -20\,\frac{x^2}{1+x^2}$$

| $f/f_c$ | local slope (dB/dec) |
|---|---|
| 0.1 | −0.2 |
| 0.5 | −4 |
| 1 | −10 |
| 3 | −18 |
| 10 | −19.8 |

So the "−20 dB/decade" number is a limit. If you read a Bode plot between 0.5 and 2 times $f_c$, you'll see a shallower slope. In practice, from about 3 times the corner you can treat it as −20.

## Using the slope: worked examples

**How much attenuation at 50 kHz from a 2 kHz first-order filter?** $50/2=25$ so the attenuation is $10\log_{10}(1+625)=27.97$ dB, close to the asymptote's $20\log_{10}25=27.96$ dB.

**How big should $f_c$ be to get 20 dB of attenuation at 60 Hz for a first-order low-pass?** $|H|=0.1$ at 60 Hz means $f/f_c=\sqrt{99}\approx 9.95$, so $f_c\approx 6$ Hz. But then the filter has a 6 Hz bandwidth, which is far too slow for many signals. That's the cost of a low slope.

**How many poles to reach −60 dB at $10f_c$?** Each buffered pole gives about −20 dB at $10f_c$, so three poles, giving −60 dB. But three poles also put the −3 dB point at $0.51f_c$ (for identical poles, $\sqrt{2^{1/3}-1}$ = 0.51) if the poles were all at the same $f_c$, so you'd retune.

**Cascading unbuffered RC sections.** Two identical RC sections give −40 dB/decade at high frequency, but because the second loads the first, the response is $1/(1+3sRC+(sRC)^2)$. The −3 dB point drops to $0.374/(2\pi RC)$.

## What the slope means in the time domain

A first-order low-pass above its corner is an integrator: $V_{out}\approx\frac{1}{RC}\int V_{in}\,dt$. Integrating a sine of frequency $f$ divides its amplitude by $2\pi f$, which is the $1/f$ behavior, and delays it by 90°, which is the phase. A square wave input gives a triangle output when the corner is far below the fundamental. This is the same math that makes an op-amp's open-loop gain fall at −20 dB/decade: a dominant pole gives integrator behavior above its corner (see [[q0126]]).

## Real hardware: where the slope stops

On a real board, measured attenuation doesn't continue down forever. Typical limits:
- **Capacitor ESL.** Above its self-resonant frequency the "capacitor" is an inductor, and the shunt impedance rises, which decreases attenuation.
- **Parasitic capacitance across the series resistor** (fractions of a pF): above $f=1/(2\pi R C_p)$ the signal bypasses the resistor. With 10 kΩ and 0.5 pF, that's about 32 MHz.
- **Layout coupling** from input to output traces or through shared ground impedance.
- **Op-amp bandwidth** in active filters: the op-amp's finite gain at high frequency lets signal feed through the feedback capacitor.

So a filter that should give −100 dB at 100 MHz may measure −50 dB. The remedy is a careful layout, smaller components, or a final high-frequency stage such as a ferrite bead or a small LC.

## Worked example: what the slope costs in a PWM-to-DC filter

Suppose a microcontroller makes a 3.3 V, 10 kHz PWM at 50% duty and you want the average (1.65 V) with no more than about 1 mV peak-to-peak ripple. How low must a first-order corner be, and what does a second pole buy?

**Time-domain view.** For an RC with $\tau\gg T$, the output is nearly constant and the ripple is a small triangle of peak-to-peak amplitude about $V\,D(1-D)\,T/\tau$. Setting this to 1 mV with $T=100$ µs gives

$$\tau=\frac{3.3\times0.25\times10^{-4}}{10^{-3}}=82.5\ \text{ms},\qquad f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi\tau}=1.93\ \text{Hz}$$

**Frequency-domain cross-check.** The fundamental of a 50% duty square wave has peak amplitude $\frac{2V}{\pi}\sin(\pi D)=2.10$ V. A first-order filter at 10 kHz passes the fraction $f_c/f=1.93/10{,}000$, leaving $0.405$ mV peak, or 0.81 mV peak-to-peak. That agrees with the 1 mV from the time-domain estimate (the fundamental alone slightly under-counts the ripple), which confirms that "ripple is set by the $f_c/f$ ratio" is the same statement as the −20 dB/decade slope.

**The cost.** A 1.93 Hz corner means the output needs $\tau\ln(1000)=0.57$ s to settle to 0.1% after a duty change. Now add a second buffered pole of the same frequency. Together the two poles contribute $1/(1+(f/f_c)^2)$ (each is $1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}$), so the same 0.405 mV needs $f_c\approx139$ Hz per pole, and the settling time to 0.1% for two identical poles is about $9.2\tau=10.6$ ms. The two-pole filter is roughly 54 times faster for the same ripple. This is the reason PWM DACs almost always use at least two stages, and the reason any "−20 dB/decade is not much" argument leads directly to a speed-versus-rejection trade-off.

## Why the asymptote is so good so quickly

The error between the true curve and the asymptote is $10\log_{10}(1+x^{-2})$ in dB for $x=f/f_c>1$. At $x=2$ it is 0.97 dB, at $x=3$ it is 0.46 dB, at $x=5$ it is 0.17 dB and at $x=10$ it is 0.04 dB. This is what makes straight-line Bode sketches useful: from about three times the corner on, the asymptote is within half a decibel. The phase takes longer to settle: at $10f_c$ it is still $-84.3^\circ$, not $-90^\circ$, because $\arctan(10)=84.3^\circ$.

## Limiting cases

- **$f\ll f_c$.** The slope is zero and the gain is 1. The filter is invisible.
- **$f=f_c$.** The magnitude is 0.707, the local slope is $-10$ dB/decade, and the phase is $-45^\circ$.
- **$f\to\infty$ (ideal parts).** The slope approaches $-20$ dB/decade forever; no ideal first-order filter has a stopband floor.
- **Real parts.** The floor is set by the parasitics discussed above and, in an active filter, by the op-amp's own falling gain, which turns the active low-pass into a roll-off that changes slope near the amplifier's GBW.

## Measuring the slope on the bench

Slope is a ratio of two measurements, so measure it between two points both well above the corner, for example at $10f_c$ and $100f_c$, and check that the readings differ by 20 dB. The practical difficulty is dynamic range: if the input is 1 V peak, the output at $100f_c$ is 10 mV and at $1000f_c$ it is 1 mV, which is near the resolution of an 8-bit scope at 1 mV/div. Use averaging (16 or 64 acquisitions), a narrow measurement bandwidth or a spectrum analyzer or lock-in for deeper points, and AC-couple the output channel. Keep the probe grounds short, because ground-loop pickup at the generator frequency shows up exactly as a floor on the measured stopband, and compare the generator's output with the scope's channel gain by moving the same probe across input and output so that probe tolerance cancels. If the measurement flattens at an amplitude independent of the input level, you are looking at the noise floor of the instrument or at coupling around the filter, not at the filter's real roll-off.

::: applications
- **Anti-aliasing filters**: choosing the order from the required attenuation at the Nyquist frequency.
- **Supply and sensor noise filtering**: an RC filter with a 1 kHz corner reduces 100 kHz noise by 40 dB.
- **PWM-to-DC filters**: ripple at the PWM frequency is reduced by $f_c/f_{PWM}$ (first order), so a 1 kHz-corner filter on a 100 kHz PWM gives about 1/100 ripple amplitude.
- **Op-amp gain-bandwidth**: the −20 dB/decade open-loop slope gives a constant GBW and a predictable closed-loop bandwidth.
- **Loop compensation**: a dominant pole with −20 dB/decade slope gives good phase margin.
- **EMI/RFI input filters**: first-order RC on sensor lines before an ADC.
:::

::: assumptions
I assumed a first-order linear passive filter, driven from a low-impedance source and unloaded, with ideal components. The −20 dB/decade value applies far above the corner; the slope is gentler within roughly a factor of 3 of $f_c$. For a **high-pass filter** the slope is +20 dB/decade below the corner. Filters with **zeros** (such as a lead network) have different slopes in different bands. For **order $n$** with all real poles the asymptotic slope is $-20n$ dB/decade, but resonant (complex-pole) filters of the same order have the same asymptotic slope with a peaking or flat region near the corner. Parasitics and non-ideal components set a floor on attenuation at high frequency.
:::

::: followups
### What's the attenuation at 5 times the corner frequency, exactly?
$|H| = 1/\sqrt{1+25}=0.196$, which is $-14.15$ dB. The asymptote says $-20\log_{10}5=-13.98$ dB, a difference of 0.17 dB, so the asymptote is quite accurate this far out.

### You need at least 40 dB attenuation at 10 kHz, and the passband edge is 1 kHz. Can a first-order filter do it?
No. A first-order filter with corner at 1 kHz gives only 20 dB at 10 kHz. Pushing the corner down to meet 40 dB at 10 kHz needs $f_c = 100$ Hz, which attenuates the 1 kHz signal by 20 dB. You need at least a second-order filter (−40 dB/decade): with a corner of ~1 kHz it gives about 40 dB at 10 kHz.

### Why is it called "−20 dB/decade" when the power falls by a factor of 100?
dB for voltage is $20\log_{10}$ of the amplitude ratio, and power is proportional to the amplitude squared, so 10× less amplitude is 100× less power, which is 20 dB in both conventions ($10\log_{10}100 = 20$). The "−20 dB" is the same number for power or voltage ratios as long as you use the matching formula.

### How would you measure the roll-off of a filter in the lab?
Sweep a sine through the filter with a function generator, and measure the amplitude at the input and output on an oscilloscope (or use a network analyzer / Bode-plot feature). Take readings at $f_c$, $10f_c$, $100f_c$ and compare to −3, −20, −40 dB. Make sure the generator output impedance and the scope probe loading are accounted for, and that the stopband amplitudes are above the scope's noise floor.

### Harder: your RC filter measures −20 dB at 10 kHz as designed but only −32 dB at 1 MHz, where theory says −60 dB. Why?
Parasitics bypass the filter at high frequency: stray capacitance across the resistor, capacitor ESL (above the self-resonant frequency the "capacitor" becomes an inductor), and capacitive or ground coupling between input and output wiring. At 1 MHz the ideal capacitor impedance is tiny, so even a few fF of coupling matters. Check with a smaller package, shorter leads, a ground plane, and measuring with a proper probe.
:::
