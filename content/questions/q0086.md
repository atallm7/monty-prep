::: basics
### In plain words
NPN and PNP are the two flavors of bipolar junction transistor (BJT), a part that uses a small current to control a larger one. They do the same job with every polarity flipped. An NPN switches on when its base is a little above its emitter, and a PNP switches on when its base is a little below its emitter.

### Picture it
Think of two doors that open in opposite directions. The NPN door opens when you push the base up above the emitter. The PNP door opens when you pull the base down below the emitter. Both let a big flow through once open. The analogy breaks down on speed: electrons move more easily than holes, so the NPN is usually the faster, higher-gain door.

### Words you need
- **Base, emitter, collector**: the three terminals. A small current at the base controls a larger current between collector and emitter. See [[q0085]].
- **Electrons and holes**: the two kinds of charge carrier. A hole is a missing electron that acts like a moving positive charge. Electrons do most of the carrying in an NPN, holes in a PNP.
- **Forward-biased junction**: a junction is the boundary between two differently treated layers of silicon. Forward-biased means pushed the way that lets current through, about 0.7 V for silicon.
- **Low-side / high-side switch**: a switch placed between the load and ground, or between the supply and the load.

### The simplest example
Put an NPN's emitter on ground. A base at 0 V keeps it off, and a base at about 0.7 V or more starts to turn it on. Now put a PNP's emitter on a 5 V supply. A base at 5 V keeps it off, and a base at about 4.3 V starts to turn it on. The NPN naturally sits on the ground side of a load, and the PNP on the supply side.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows the doping layers, the current directions, why NPNs are faster, and where each one fits in real circuits.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can compare the two polarities **structurally and in circuits**, not just recite "NPN is N-P-N". The interviewer is looking for:

1. Doping order, majority carriers in the emitter, and why the symbol arrow points opposite ways.
2. Polarity: which terminal is most positive/negative, direction of each current, sign of $V_{BE}$ vs $V_{EB}$.
3. Performance: electrons beat holes, so NPN is faster and has more gain for the same geometry.
4. Circuit placement: NPN as a low-side switch (emitter to ground), PNP as a high-side switch (emitter to supply), complementary push-pull pairs, and the level-shifting headache that PNPs bring.
:::

::: why
Real designs mix both polarities: a high-side switch from a microcontroller needs either a PNP or an NPN plus a level shifter; audio and servo output stages use a complementary pair; analogue ICs rely on NPN with only a few slow PNPs. Picking the wrong polarity, or forgetting that a PNP needs the base pulled *down* to turn on, is one of the commonest practical mistakes. This question also reinforces the mobility argument from [[q0070]] and parallels N-channel vs P-channel MOSFETs ([[q0097]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Structure**: NPN is N-P-N (electrons are the main carriers), PNP is P-N-P (holes). See [[q0081]].
- **Polarities**: NPN conducts when the base is about 0.7 V above the emitter; PNP when the base is about 0.7 V below. See [[q0080]].
- **Mobility**: electrons are about 2.8 times more mobile than holes in silicon, so NPNs are faster. See [[q0070]].
- **Current directions**: NPN currents into base and collector, out of emitter; PNP the reverse. See [[q0085]].
- **High-side vs low-side switching**: where the load sits relative to the transistor. See [[q0082]].
:::

::: answer
"They're mirror images. An NPN is an N-type emitter, a thin P-type base and an N-type collector, and its main carriers are electrons. A PNP swaps all the doping types, so the main carriers are holes.

In circuit terms everything flips polarity. An NPN turns on when the base is about 0.7 V above the emitter, current flows into the collector and base and out of the emitter, and the collector is the positive side, so it's the natural low-side switch with the emitter on ground. A PNP turns on when the base is about 0.7 V below the emitter, current flows into the emitter and out of the base and collector, and the emitter goes to the positive supply, so it's the natural high-side switch.

The performance differs too. Holes are about three times less mobile than electrons in silicon, so for the same geometry a PNP has a longer transit time, a lower transition frequency and typically lower gain, and integrated PNPs are particularly weak. That's why analogue ICs lean on NPN, and why 2N3904 and 2N3906, the complementary pair, are specified similarly but the PNP is a bit slower.

They're used together in complementary stages, for example a push-pull output where the NPN sources current and the PNP sinks it. The symbol arrow on the emitter points out for NPN and in for PNP."
:::

::: technical
**Structure.** NPN: N+ emitter, P base, N collector. PNP: P+ emitter, N base, P collector. Same design logic: heavily doped emitter, thin base, lightly doped collector.

**Bias (forward-active).**

| Quantity | NPN | PNP |
|---|---|---|
| Base-emitter | forward: $V_{BE}\approx+0.7$ V | forward: $V_{EB}\approx+0.7$ V ($V_{BE}\approx-0.7$ V) |
| Collector | $V_C>V_B>V_E$ | $V_E>V_B>V_C$ |
| $I_B$ | into base | out of base |
| $I_C$ | into collector | out of collector |
| $I_E$ | out of emitter | into emitter |
| Majority carriers in emitter | electrons | holes |
| Symbol arrow | out (not pointing in) | in |

**Performance.** Transit time $\tau_B=W_B^2/(2D)$ with $D_n=\mu_nV_T\approx35$ cm²/s versus $D_p\approx12.4$ cm²/s (lightly doped; base values are lower) so a PNP of the same geometry has about 2.8x longer base transit time. Typical discrete pairs: 2N3904 (NPN) and 2N3906 (PNP) have both $V_{CEO}=40$ V and $I_C(\max)=200$ mA, $h_{FE}$ about 100-300 at 10 mA, and $f_T$ (minimum) of about 300 MHz versus 250 MHz (check the datasheet).

**In ICs.** NPN is the standard high-performance device. PNPs are "lateral" (poor $\beta$ of 10-50, low $f_T$ of tens of MHz) or "substrate" (collector is the substrate, so it must be grounded). Complementary bipolar processes with good PNPs exist but cost more.

**Circuits.** NPN: low-side switch, common-emitter amplifier, emitter follower. PNP: high-side switch, current source feeding a load to ground, level shifting up. Complementary pair: class-AB output stage (NPN sources, PNP sinks); current mirrors use NPN mirrors for sinking and PNP mirrors for sourcing.
:::

::: long
"NPN and PNP are the same device with all polarities flipped. An NPN has an N-type emitter, a thin P-type base and an N-type collector, and the main carriers are electrons. A PNP has a P-type emitter, an N-type base and a P-type collector, so the main carriers are holes.

In circuits that flips everything. For the NPN, the base must be about 0.7 volts above the emitter to turn on. Current flows into the base and collector and out of the emitter, and the collector sits more positive than the emitter. So I'd use it as a low-side switch: the emitter goes to ground and the load is between the supply and the collector. For the PNP, the base must be about 0.7 volts *below* the emitter. Current flows into the emitter and out of the base and collector, and the emitter is the most positive terminal. So it's a natural high-side switch, with the emitter on the supply and the load between the collector and ground.

The practical catch with PNP high-side switching is the drive. The base has to be pulled down to within about 0.7 V of the supply rail to turn it on, and has to be brought back up to the rail to turn it off. If the supply is 12 V and my microcontroller output is 3.3 V, driving the base directly would leave the PNP on, with an enormous base current, so I need an NPN or MOSFET level shifter to pull the base down.

The other difference is speed and gain. Holes are about three times less mobile than electrons, so for the same geometry the PNP has a longer base transit time, a lower $f_T$, and often a lower gain. 2N3904 and 2N3906 are a complementary pair with similar ratings, but the PNP's minimum transition frequency is a bit lower. In integrated circuits the difference is bigger: lateral PNPs have gains of 10 to 50 and are quite slow, so designers use NPNs wherever they can.

They're used together in a push-pull output: the NPN sources current to the load on positive half-cycles and the PNP sinks it on negative half-cycles, as in an audio amplifier or a servo driver. They also form complementary current mirrors, one for sinking and one for sourcing."
:::

::: terms
- **NPN / PNP**: BJT polarity types, named by the doping order of emitter-base-collector.
- **Complementary pair**: an NPN and a PNP with matched ratings, such as 2N3904/2N3906.
- **High-side / low-side switch**: switching between the supply and the load vs between the load and ground.
- **Level shifter**: circuit translating a logic signal to a different voltage domain, e.g. an NPN pulling down a PNP base.
- **Push-pull stage**: NPN and PNP emitter followers sharing an output node to source and sink current.
- **Crossover distortion**: dead zone around 0 V in a class-B push-pull when neither transistor conducts ($|V_{in}|<0.7$ V).
- **Lateral PNP**: IC PNP built horizontally in an N-well; low $\beta$ and $f_T$.
- **Hole vs electron mobility**: about 480 vs 1350 cm²/V·s in lightly doped silicon.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"PNP has the opposite current direction so it works backwards."** It works the same way, with polarities mirrored; the mechanism and equations are identical, with $V_{EB}$ in place of $V_{BE}$.
- **Applying NPN voltage rules to a PNP.** For a PNP the emitter is the most positive terminal and $V_B=V_E-0.7$ V.
- **Driving a PNP high-side switch directly from a lower-voltage logic output.** Base stays below the emitter, so the transistor is on with unlimited base current; you need a level shifter and a pull-up between emitter and base.
- **Assuming a PNP is an exact swap-in for an NPN.** Gain, speed, saturation voltage and noise differ. Check datasheets.
- **Reading the arrow wrong.** NPN: arrow out. PNP: arrow in.
- **Forgetting the complementary pair's crossover distortion** when building a push-pull without bias (class B), and thermal runaway in class AB without emitter resistors or a bias network.
:::

## Physical mirror: carriers and mobility

An NPN injects electrons from the emitter into the base; a PNP injects holes. The structure is identical in logic: heavy emitter, thin base, light collector. The differences all come from the carrier:

- **Mobility:** $\mu_n\approx1350$, $\mu_p\approx480$ cm²/V·s (lightly doped; in a real base the numbers are lower).
- **Diffusivity:** $D=\mu V_T$: 35 vs 12.4 cm²/s.
- **Base transit time:** $\tau_B=W_B^2/(2D)$; for $W_B=0.5\ \mu$m with $D_n$ and $D_p$ from above, $\tau_{B,n}=36$ ps and $\tau_{B,p}=101$ ps (idealised, ratio 2.8).
- **Transition frequency:** $f_T\le1/(2\pi\tau_{total})$, so the PNP's is up to 2.8x lower.

In integrated processes, the vertical NPN is the best device. PNPs are either **lateral** (emitter and collector are side-by-side P regions in an N-well; the effective base width is set by lithography, typically microns, so $\beta$ is 10-50 and $f_T$ is tens of MHz) or **substrate** PNPs where the collector is the P substrate, which must be at the most negative potential. Complementary bipolar (CB) processes with good vertical PNPs exist but cost more.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Quoted $\beta$ and $f_T$ values for IC PNPs, and the 2N3904/2N3906 $f_T$ minimums (about 300 MHz and 250 MHz), come from typical datasheet and textbook values and vary by manufacturer and process. Check the specific part's datasheet.

Figure: When the logic input is high, Q1 pulls the PNP base down through $R_B$; the PNP turns on and connects +12 V to the load. When the input is low, Q1 is off and $R_{BE}$ pulls the base up to the emitter voltage so the PNP is firmly off. Without Q1, a 3.3 V output directly on the base would leave $V_{EB}=8.7$ V: the PNP would conduct heavily (and be destroyed) when the logic high should have turned it off.

**Design numbers** (load 100 mA, forced $\beta=10$): PNP base current 10 mA. $R_B=(12-0.7-0.2)/10\text{ mA}=1.11$ kΩ, so use 1.2 kΩ: $I_B\approx9.25$ mA. Drop across the PNP is about 0.2 V so the load sees about 11.8 V.

The same job can be done by an N-channel MOSFET with a charge pump or bootstrap, or by a P-channel MOSFET with a similar level shifter; the PNP/NPN pair is cheap and simple for small loads.

## Complementary pairs and mirrors

- **Push-pull output** (above): NPN sources, PNP sinks, giving bipolar swing and low output impedance.
- **Current mirrors:** an NPN mirror sinks current to ground; a PNP mirror sources current from the supply. Op-amps use both to convert differential signals to single-ended with good gain.
- **Differential pairs:** an NPN-input pair needs its input common-mode to stay a $V_{BE}$ plus tail-source headroom above the negative rail, so it works best toward the positive rail; a PNP-input pair lets the inputs go down to (or slightly below) the negative rail, hence single-supply op-amps whose input range includes ground.

## Equations are identical (with signs flipped)

NPN: $I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$, $I_E=(\beta+1)I_B$. PNP: $I_C=I_Se^{V_{EB}/V_T}$, with all currents reversed. $g_m=I_C/V_T$ is the same, which is why small-signal models are unchanged. Only the DC polarities and $I_S$ (lower for PNP, since $D_p$ is lower and $I_S\propto D$) change. Early voltages differ between parts.

## Worked example: how much slower is a lateral PNP?

The base-transit estimate $\tau_B=W_B^2/(2D)$ tells you why the integrated lateral PNP is so much slower than a vertical NPN. In the lateral device the base width is the distance between two P regions defined by lithography, say $W_B=3\ \mu$m. In the vertical NPN it is set by the depth of two implants, say $0.1\ \mu$m. Holes in an N-type base have $D_p=\mu_pV_T\approx480\times0.02585=12.4$ cm²/s. Then

$$\tau_{B,lateral}=\frac{(3\times10^{-4}\ \text{cm})^2}{2\times12.4}=3.6\ \text{ns},\qquad f\approx\frac{1}{2\pi\tau_B}\approx44\ \text{MHz}$$
$$\tau_{B,vertical\ npn}=\frac{(10^{-5}\ \text{cm})^2}{2\times35}=1.4\ \text{ps}\ \ (\text{base transit only})$$

The ratio is about 2,500, of which only the factor 2.8 comes from carrier mobility; the other factor of about 900 comes from the base width squared ($30^2$). That is the quantitative reason IC designers say "geometry beats physics": the lateral PNP's problem is mostly lithography, not hole mobility. It also explains the quoted $\beta$ of 10 to 50 for lateral PNPs: with a wide base, recombination ($W_B^2/2L_p^2$) is larger, and a significant fraction of the injected holes go down to the substrate rather than sideways to the collector.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Lateral base widths and the resulting $f_T$ vary with the process; 3 µm is an illustrative figure for an older bipolar process and modern complementary bipolar flows with vertical PNPs do far better. The transit-time bound ignores junction-capacitance charging, which is usually the bigger limit as seen in [[q0085]].

## Worked example: crossover distortion of the complementary follower

The push-pull stage at the top of this section has a dead zone: neither transistor conducts while $|v_{in}|<0.7$ V. Model the output as $v_{out}=v_{in}-0.7$ for $v_{in}>0.7$ V, $v_{in}+0.7$ for $v_{in}<-0.7$ V, and 0 in between, and drive it with a 1 kHz sine of peak amplitude $A$. Computing the Fourier components numerically (all odd harmonics, since the transfer curve is symmetric) gives:

| Peak input $A$ | Fundamental amplitude | Total harmonic distortion |
|---|---|---|
| 1 V | 0.19 V | 59 % |
| 2 V | 1.13 V | 23.6 % |
| 5 V | 4.11 V | 8.9 % |
| 10 V | 9.11 V | 4.4 % |

Two lessons. First, the distortion does not fall to zero as the signal grows: it falls roughly as $1/A$, since the dead zone is a fixed 1.4 V "bite" taken from every zero crossing. Second, it is catastrophic for small signals. A class B follower driven at 1 V peak is not a usable audio stage. Class AB bias fixes this by giving both transistors a small quiescent current so that each is already on when the other is turning off. A pair of series diodes or a $V_{BE}$ multiplier between the two bases supplies the roughly 1.3 V needed ($2V_{BE}$), and small emitter resistors (a fraction of an ohm to a few ohms) stabilize the quiescent current.

The sensitivity of that current is the design problem. Because $I_C\propto e^{V_{BE}/V_T}$, a 5 mV mismatch between the bias voltage and the pair's actual requirement changes $I_Q$ by $e^{5/25.85}=1.21$ (21 %), and 10 mV changes it by 47 %. A $V_{BE}$ multiplier mounted thermally on the output transistors tracks the roughly −2 mV/K temperature drift; without that tracking, the output stage heats, its $V_{BE}$ falls, the quiescent current rises and the pair can run away. At a quiescent current of 5 mA each device has $r_e=V_T/I_Q=5.2\ \Omega$, so near zero crossing both devices are on and the stage output resistance is about 2.6 Ω. Into an 8 Ω load the gain near zero is $8/(8+2.6)=0.76$, versus almost 1 when only one device carries the signal. That remaining gain step is the small residual crossover distortion class AB leaves, and is the reason negative feedback around the stage is used to reduce it.

## Using a PNP when the supply is single-ended

In a single-supply circuit, a PNP lets you switch the positive rail, which keeps the load's ground reference solid. Its drawbacks, beyond the lower speed, show up in practice:

- **Drive polarity.** The base must be pulled below the emitter, which in a 12 V system means sinking base current to ground through a resistor, not sourcing it from a 3.3 V logic pin. The level-shifting NPN in the circuit above, or an open-drain output, does this.
- **Turn-off.** A pull-up resistor ($R_{BE}$) must hold the base at the emitter potential, or the PNP may leak. If the logic pin can float during reset, a 10 kΩ pull-up resistor from base to emitter keeps the load off.
- **Saturation.** Low $V_{CE(sat)}$ requires a forced $\beta$ of 10 or lower, so the base drive current is typically 10 % of the load current; for loads of an ampere or more this is why designers use a P-channel MOSFET or a high-side driver IC.
- **Reverse polarity.** The collector of a substrate PNP is the substrate, so it can only connect to the most negative potential in the chip. Plan the topology for that restriction.

## How you would verify an NPN/PNP stage on the bench

- **Match the pair.** Measure $V_{BE}$ and $V_{EB}$ at the intended quiescent current (a few milliamps) for both transistors; sort devices that are within a few millivolts. Do this at the same temperature, since 2 mV/K moves the numbers more than the mismatch does.
- **Crossover and distortion.** Drive the push-pull stage with a 1 kHz sine at a small amplitude and look at the output on an oscilloscope: a flat spot at each zero crossing is the dead zone. With a spectrum analyzer or the scope's FFT, measure the odd harmonics and compare with the table: at 5 V peak you should see the third harmonic about 23 dB below the fundamental (0.29 V against 4.11 V in the ideal dead-zone model) before bias is added. Then adjust the bias and watch the flat spot disappear.
- **Quiescent current.** Put a 1 Ω resistor in series with each collector supply (or read the emitter resistor voltage drop), set the bias to the intended current, and watch it for five to ten minutes while the transistors warm up. A drifting current means the thermal tracking is poor.
- **High-side PNP switch.** Check the load voltage with the input low (it should be near zero, not a few volts because of leakage through the PNP) and with the input high (about 11.8 V in the example). Measure $V_{EC}$ at full load: it should be the 0.2 V saturation value.

::: applications
- **Low-side load switches with NPN** (2N3904, BC547) and **high-side with PNP** (2N3906, BC557) for LEDs, relays and small motors.
- **Class AB audio/servo output stages** with NPN/PNP complementary pairs (TIP31/TIP32, 2N3055/MJ2955).
- **Current mirrors and sources in op-amps and references**: NPN sink, PNP source.
- **Rail-to-rail and ground-sensing input stages**: PNP input pairs for inputs down to the negative rail in single-supply op-amps (e.g. LM358, LM324 family).
- **Level shifters for high-side drive**: NPN pulling down a PNP or P-channel MOSFET gate.
- **Constant-current sources for LED strings** using PNP referenced to the positive rail.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Silicon BJTs, forward-active operation**, with $V_{BE}$ or $V_{EB}\approx0.7$ V, $\beta\approx100$.
- **Typical datasheet values** (2N3904/2N3906) for illustration. Check the real part for gain, speed and saturation voltage.
- **Idealised mobility ratio** of about 2.8; actual base mobilities are lower and the NPN/PNP speed ratio in a given process can be 1.5-3x.
- **Alternative polarity choices**: modern designs often prefer an N-channel MOSFET with a driver instead of a PNP high-side switch, so "which to use" is context-dependent.
- **Complementary-pair performance** depends on matching; no two polarities are identical, so distortion and temperature tracking need design care.
:::

::: followups
### Which is faster, NPN or PNP, and why?
NPN, because electrons have higher mobility and diffusivity than holes (35 vs 12.4 cm²/s lightly doped), so the base transit time $W_B^2/(2D)$ is shorter by about 2.8x for the same base width. In practice, NPN $f_T$ is higher: for example 300 MHz (2N3904 min) versus 250 MHz (2N3906 min), and in ICs the difference is much larger because the PNPs are lateral devices with wide effective bases.

### How do you turn on a PNP in a high-side switch from a 3.3 V microcontroller on a 12 V rail?
You can't drive the base directly, because 3.3 V is far below the 11.3 V needed to turn it off (base within 0.7 V of the emitter) so it would be on all the time. Use an NPN (or N-MOSFET) as a level shifter: a high logic level turns the NPN on, which pulls the PNP base down through a resistor, and a pull-up resistor between PNP emitter and base turns it off when the NPN is off.

### Why would you ever choose a PNP over an NPN?
When the load must be referenced to ground and switched on the high side without a charge pump or bootstrap, or when you need a sourcing current mirror, a ground-sensing input stage or a complementary output. A PNP with the emitter on the rail turns on with a base pulled low and has no problem reaching the rail minus $V_{CE(sat)}$, while an NPN high-side switch can only pull the load up to about $V_B-0.7$ V.

### What is crossover distortion in a push-pull stage and how do you fix it?
When the input is within about $\pm0.7$ V of the output neither transistor conducts, so the output stalls and the waveform has a flat spot near zero crossings. Fix by biasing the bases about $2V_{BE}$ apart (two diodes or a $V_{BE}$ multiplier) so both conduct slightly at idle (class AB), usually with small emitter resistors and thermal coupling so idle current doesn't run away with temperature.

### Can you build a current mirror with PNP transistors and what does it do?
Yes: two matched PNPs with emitters to the positive rail, bases tied together, and one collector tied to its own base (diode-connected). It copies the reference current $I_{ref}$ into a load referenced to ground, with an error of $2/\beta$, so about 2% for $\beta=100$ (more for lateral PNPs with $\beta\approx20$, about 9%). NPN mirrors do the opposite, sinking current from a load.

### Scenario: you replaced an NPN with a PNP in your layout and everything is dead. What did you forget?
Polarities and supply connections. The emitter must now go to the positive rail (or the more positive node), the collector to the lower-voltage load, and the base must be pulled below the emitter. Pinout can differ (2N3906 vs 2N3904 are both EBC in TO-92, but check the datasheet), and drive logic needs inverting or level shifting. Also any bypass or protection devices (diodes, electrolytic polarity) that assumed the old orientation must be reversed.

The MOSFET analogue is N-channel vs P-channel ([[q0097]]), which has the same carrier-mobility story. The "difference" can be answered at the level of structure, polarity or performance, so I give all three and then add circuit placement because that is what interviewers usually want.
