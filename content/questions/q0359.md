::: testing
Whether you can list the real engineering concerns about brushes, group them, and connect each to its mechanism and consequence for a design: **life and wear**, **electrical** (voltage drop, loss, arcing/EMI, current limit), **mechanical** (friction, bounce and speed limit, noise, spring force) and **environmental/safety** (dust, humidity, altitude, ignition risk, temperature). A strong answer gives numbers for at least one concern and says what you would do: choose a grade, add suppression, derate speed or go brushless.
:::

::: why
Brush concerns are the reason you ask "do I really need brushed?" at the start of a design. They drive lifetime, maintenance, EMC compliance, efficiency at low voltage and where the product can be used (clean rooms, explosive atmospheres, low-pressure environments). Being able to rank these concerns for a given application is exactly the judgement an interviewer is looking for.
:::

::: concepts
- **Sliding contact and wear**: friction removes material; life is finite. See [[q0360]].
- **Contact voltage drop and loss** ($2V_bI$). See [[q0357]].
- **Arcing and EMI** from commutation. See [[q0356]] and [[q0355]].
- **Reactance voltage** $L\,di/dt$ of the commutated coil, which limits current and speed.
- **Brush material and spring force**: grade selection and contact pressure. See [[q0358]] and [[q0362]].
- **Environment**: humidity, altitude, contamination and temperature change brush behaviour.
:::

::: answer
"The main concerns fall into four groups.

First, wear and life. The brush rubs on the commutator, so it wears, and so does the commutator. Brush life is typically thousands of hours, and it depends strongly on current, speed, humidity and the quality of the contact. When the brush is worn down, the motor stops, and the dust it makes can also cause trouble.

Second, electrical issues. There's a contact voltage drop, roughly a volt per brush for carbon, which is a fixed loss and a big problem in low-voltage motors. There's arcing at commutation, because the coil being switched is inductive, which makes broadband noise that can upset electronics and radios, and it also erodes the contacts. And there's a limit on how much current and how fast: too much current density overheats the brush, and at high speed the brush can bounce and spark.

Third, mechanical: friction loss, audible noise or squeal, and the need for the right spring pressure. Too little pressure and it bounces and arcs, too much and it wears fast.

Fourth, environment and safety: carbon dust, humidity or very dry air or altitude changes the lubrication and film, and sparks can ignite flammable gases.

So I'd mitigate with the right grade, suppression capacitors, derating speed and current, or if the life and noise requirements are tough I'd go brushless."
:::

::: technical
**Concerns, mechanisms and consequences**

| Concern | Mechanism | Consequence | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wear / finite life** | Abrasion, arc erosion, chemical film wear ([[q0360]]) | Life of ~hundreds to thousands of hours depending on duty | Correct grade, spring force, clean commutator; replaceable brushes; or brushless |
| **Commutator wear** | Brush abrasion, arcing, mica high spots | Grooves, burnt bars, out-of-round | Run-in, undercut mica, resurface commutator |
| **Voltage drop / loss** | Film/contact drop ≈0.5–1.5 V per brush (grade dependent) | Loss $2V_bI$; big share at low voltage | Metal-graphite or precious metal for low voltage |
| **Arcing / sparking** | Reactance voltage $L_c\,\Delta i/t_c$, bounce ([[q0356]]) | EMI, ozone, erosion, ignition risk | Capacitors on terminals ([[q0355]]), correct brush position, grade, interpoles in large motors |
| **Current limit** | Current density $J$ (e.g. 8–12 A/cm² for carbon) | Overheating of brush and commutator | Larger/multiple brushes, metal-graphite |
| **Speed limit** | Brush bounce when $m e\omega^2$ approaches spring force; commutation time shrinks | Sparks, noise, wear at high speed | Stiffer springs, light brushes, round commutator |
| **Friction / noise** | Stick-slip, squeal | Loss, audible noise | Grade selection, film control, damping |
| **Dust and contamination** | Carbon dust generated by wear | Shorts across mica, tracking, contamination of optics/electronics | Enclosure, cleaning, brush-dust management |
| **Environment** | Dry air, vacuum, altitude, silicones, high temperature | Film breakdown, "dusting", rapid wear | Special grades, sealed or brushless motor |
| **Intermittent / dry-circuit contact** | Low current, film resistance, vibration | Noise in signals, startup failure at low voltage | Precious-metal brushes |

**Numbers.**
- Contact loss at 3 A with 2×0.5 V: 3 W (8% of a 36 W input).
- Reactance voltage example: coil inductance 20 µH, coil current 3 A reversing to −3 A, 12 segments on a 16 mm commutator. Commutation time $t_c \approx$ segment pitch / surface speed. At 6000 rpm: surface speed 5.0 m/s, pitch 4.2 mm, $t_c = 0.83$ ms, $e_r = L\cdot 2i_c/t_c = 20\,\mu\text{H}\times6/0.83\,\text{ms} = 0.14$ V. At 30,000 rpm: $t_c = 0.17$ ms, $e_r = 0.72$ V, comparable to the brush drop, so commutation worsens fast with speed.
- Bounce: a 2 g brush riding 20 µm of commutator runout with 0.5 N spring force; inertial force $m e\omega^2$ is 0.016 N at 6000 rpm, 0.39 N at 30,000 rpm and 1.6 N at 60,000 rpm (exceeds the spring force at about 34,000 rpm).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> These are illustrative numbers for a small motor. Real values for brush life, reactance-voltage limits and speed limits come from the brush manufacturer's data for the specific grade and the motor's design; they vary widely.
:::

They drop about a volt each, which matters at low voltage. Commutation of an inductive coil causes sparks and EMI. Too much current density overheats them, and at high speed they bounce. Humidity, altitude and contamination change their behaviour and sparks are an ignition risk. So pick the grade, suppress noise, derate, or go brushless."

::: long
"I'd group the concerns into life, electrical, mechanical and environmental.

Life first, because it's the headline. Brushes rub on the commutator, so both wear. Brush life is typically hundreds to thousands of hours depending on current, speed and environment, and the dust they produce can contaminate things or bridge insulation. Once the brush is worn down the motor stops, or arcs and wrecks the commutator, so brushes either need to be replaceable or the motor is a consumable.

Electrical: the contact drops roughly 0.5 to 1.5 volts per brush for carbon, a fixed loss of $2V_bI$, which is 8% of a 12 volt 3 amp motor and impossible in a 3 volt one. Commutation makes the coil's inductance reverse its current in a short time, a reactance voltage $L\,di/dt$. In my example, 20 microhenries, 3 amps and a commutation time under a millisecond gives a bit over 0.1 volt at 6000 rpm, but it grows in proportion to speed, so at 30,000 rpm it's comparable to the brush drop and arcing begins. That arcing is the EMI problem I fix with capacitors at the terminals. There's also a current-density limit, about 8 to 12 amps per square centimetre for carbon.

Mechanical: friction loss, squeal, and the spring force trade-off. With too little force the brush bounces at high speed, since the inertial force $m e \omega^2$ exceeds the spring, and arcs; too much force wears it quickly.

And environment: very dry air, altitude or vacuum break down the graphite lubrication film, silicone vapour poisons it, and sparks are an ignition risk in flammable atmospheres.

My response is to choose the right grade for the voltage and current, add suppression, derate the speed and current, or choose brushless if life, noise or hazards demand it."
:::

::: terms
- **Brush life**: time until brushes wear to the replacement limit.
- **Reactance voltage**: $L_c\,\Delta i/t_c$ from reversing current in the commutated coil.
- **Commutation time $t_c$**: the time a brush bridges adjacent segments.
- **Brush bounce**: brush momentarily losing contact because inertial force exceeds spring force.
- **Current density**: A/cm² of contact area.
- **Glazing**: a hard, shiny film on the commutator or brush face that raises noise and wear.
- **Dusting**: rapid brush wear in dry or low-pressure atmospheres.
- **Undercutting**: removing mica between segments so it doesn't stand proud.
- **Dry-circuit contact**: contact carrying very low voltage/current where films dominate.
- **Ozone**: oxidant formed by sparking that attacks rubber and plastics.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer lists "wear, noise, sparks". An exceptional answer:

- Groups concerns (life, electrical, mechanical, environmental) and gives the mechanism of each.
- Quantifies: loss $2V_bI$, reactance voltage with a worked number and its scaling with speed, bounce condition $m e\omega^2 > F_{spring}$, current density.
- Highlights that low-voltage and high-speed motors are most affected and that environment (dry air, altitude, silicones) matters.
- Links concerns to design decisions: grade, spring, suppression, derating, replaceable brush design, or brushless.
- Notes sparks as an ignition hazard, dust as contamination, and EMC compliance.
- Admits that numbers depend on grade and motor, and points to datasheets.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Calling wear the only concern.** Voltage drop, arcing/EMI, current/speed limits and environment can be more important than life for a given product.
- **Assuming a higher spring force is always better.** It reduces bounce but increases wear and friction.
- **Ignoring speed.** Reactance voltage and bounce both worsen with speed; a motor that is fine at 6000 rpm may spark badly at 30,000 rpm.
- **Ignoring dust.** Carbon dust can short between commutator segments and contaminate optics or bearings.
- **Treating sparks as harmless.** Small sparks are normal, but heavy sparking means poor commutation, a damaged commutator, high wear and EMI, and a hazard in flammable atmospheres.
- **Replacing brushes without checking the commutator.** Worn or burnt commutators will destroy new brushes quickly ([[q0361]]).
:::

::: deep
## Concern 1: wear and life

Brush and commutator wear is unavoidable in a sliding contact. Mechanisms ([[q0360]]): mechanical abrasion, electrical (arc) erosion, chemical/film wear and thermal effects. Typical life of small brushed motors ranges from hundreds of hours (cheap toy motors) to several thousand hours (good carbon-brush motors); industrial motors with large replaceable brushes can run for years between replacements. Brushed life estimates are strongly application dependent; consult the vendor's rated life at the intended current, speed and temperature.

An illustrative estimate: a brush has 5 mm of usable length and wears at 1 mm per 500 h: life 2500 h. At 5000 duty-hours a year, that's six months. If the product has to run 20,000 h (a pump or fan), a brushed motor needs service or a brushless design.

## Concern 2: electrical losses and voltage drop

The film-dominated contact drop, ~0.5–1.5 V per brush for carbon types, is roughly independent of current. Loss is $2V_bI$. This makes brushed motors disproportionately bad at low voltage: 1 V of drop in a 3 V motor is a third of the supply, but only 8% in a 12 V one. See [[q0358]] for grades that reduce it.

## Concern 3: commutation, arcing and noise

When the brush bridges adjacent segments, the shorted coil's current must reverse from $+i_c$ to $-i_c$ in time $t_c$. The coil's self-inductance $L_c$ resists that change, producing a reactance voltage
$$e_r = L_c\frac{2i_c}{t_c}.$$
If the current has not completed the reversal when the brush leaves the segment, the remainder jumps the gap as a spark ([[q0356]]). The commutation time $t_c$ is the time the brush takes to travel one segment pitch, so $t_c \propto 1/\text{speed}$ and $e_r \propto \text{speed}\times\text{current}$.

Example: $L_c = 20$ µH, $i_c = 3$ A, 12 segments, commutator radius 8 mm.

How machines fight it: resistive (carbon) brushes, brush shift off the geometric neutral, interpoles and compensating windings in large machines, low-inductance coils, and capacitors at the terminals for EMI ([[q0355]]).

## Concern 4: current density and heat

Each grade has a continuous current-density limit (order 8–12 A/cm² for carbon, up to about 20 or more for metal-graphite). Beyond it, contact heating ($V_bI$ at the interface plus friction) raises temperature, damages the film and accelerates wear. For a brush with 63 mm² contact carrying 5 A, $J = 7.9$ A/cm². Large currents use several brushes in parallel on the same track; equal current sharing requires matched grades and equal spring pressure.

Heat flows from the brush into the holder and the commutator; the commutator temperature (typically limited to ~100–150 °C by insulation and solder class) adds to the motor winding temperature.

## Concern 5: speed limit and brush bounce

The brush must follow the commutator's surface despite runout (out-of-roundness) $e$ and vibration. The inertial force required is $m e\omega^2$; if it exceeds the spring force $F_s$ the brush leaves the surface, the contact breaks, current is interrupted and arcs. The critical speed:
$$\omega_c = \sqrt{\frac{F_s}{m e}}.$$
For $m = 2$ g, $e = 20$ µm and $F_s = 0.5$ N: $\omega_c = \sqrt{0.5/(4\times10^{-8})} = 3536$ rad/s ≈ 33,800 rpm.

At 6000 rpm the requirement is only 0.016 N, so a small motor is nowhere near the limit; high-speed universal motors (20,000–30,000 rpm) are. Increasing the spring force raises the bounce limit but increases friction and wear ($P_f = \mu F v$), so the force has a design optimum ([[q0362]]).

## Concern 6: environment and safety

- **Humidity/altitude/dry air**: graphite needs vapour to lubricate; dusting occurs below a threshold humidity ([[q0358]]).
- **Contaminants**: silicone vapours, oils and halogens poison the film; sealed motors help.
- **Temperature**: both the brush grade and the commutator film have optimum ranges; very low temperature raises friction, very high temperature oxidises the brush.
- **Ignition hazard**: sparks ignite flammable vapours and dust. Motors for such areas are sealed/explosion-proof or brushless.
- **Dust**: black carbon dust can track across commutator segments and bearings; filters and enclosures help.
- **Ozone**: sparking produces ozone which degrades rubber and some plastics.

## What I would do in a design

1. Decide whether life, EMI and environment allow brushed at all; if not, go brushless ([[q0366]]).
2. Pick the grade for voltage, current density, speed and humidity; derate speed and current.
3. Add EMI suppression at the motor terminals ([[q0355]]).
4. Specify the spring force and brush geometry so the brush follows the commutator but doesn't over-wear ([[q0362]]).
5. Provide for brush inspection or replacement if the application justifies it ([[q0361]]).
6. Test over temperature, humidity and speed, and measure life under the real duty cycle.
:::

::: applications
- **Power tools and appliances**: universal motors at high speed push reactance voltage and bounce limits; brush wear is the usual life-limiting item.
- **Automotive actuators**: brush life and EMI (AM radio) are central design criteria.
- **Aerospace and high-altitude equipment**: dusting and special brush grades.
- **Medical and lab instruments**: dust and EMI push towards brushless or sealed motors.
- **Low-voltage battery devices (toys, pagers)**: voltage drop dominates, precious-metal brushes.
- **Hazardous locations**: brushless or explosion-proof motors because of spark ignition risk.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Small brushed PM motor** as the numerical example; large DC machines have different mitigations (interpoles, compensating windings) and different scales.
- **Simplified commutation model**: reactance voltage from a constant coil inductance and a linear current reversal; in reality the film resistance and the neutral-zone field shape the reversal.
- **Idealised bounce model**: rigid commutator runout and a single mass-spring brush; real limits are lower because of surface roughness, vibration and spring dynamics.
- **Typical values** (drop, density limit, life) vary by grade and manufacturer.
- Whether a given concern dominates depends on the application: for a low-duty toy, cost matters more than life; for a pump, life dominates.
:::

::: followups
### Which concern would drive you to a brushless motor first?
Usually life (a product that must run tens of thousands of hours), then EMI (sensitive radio/analog nearby), then efficiency at low voltage and operation in hazardous or unusual atmospheres. If the duty is intermittent and cost-sensitive, brushed is fine.

### Why does a brushed motor spark more at high speed?
The commutation time shrinks as the segments pass the brush faster, so the coil current must reverse in less time and the reactance voltage $L\,\Delta i/t_c$ grows in proportion to speed and current. Also the brush needs more force to stay on the commutator. At some speed the coil's current cannot finish reversing before the brush leaves, and sparks appear.

### How do you reduce brush sparking?
Use a resistive brush grade; set the brushes on the neutral axis (or slightly shifted); keep the commutator round and clean with mica properly undercut; reduce current/speed; add interpoles in large machines; and fit suppression capacitors at the terminals for the noise ([[q0355]]).

### Could you run a motor with worn brushes?
Briefly, but at the end of life contact is intermittent, so the current falls, arcing increases, the commutator burns, and the motor may stall or run erratically. Eventually the spring or the brush holder touches the commutator and destroys it. Replace at the wear limit ([[q0361]]).

### What if the motor must run at altitude or in a vacuum?
Graphite brushes will dust within minutes to hours. Use motors with special grades containing additives such as MoS₂ or with metal-graphite, or use brushless/sealed motors. Check the vendor for rated altitude.

### What inspection tells you the brush system is healthy?
A uniform, chocolate-brown commutator film; no burnt or grooved bars; brush faces evenly seated and shiny with no chipping; smooth, quiet running and low sparking; brush length above the minimum; springs with full tension.
:::

::: interpretations
"What are some concerns about brushes?" is open-ended: group them (life, electrical, mechanical, environmental) rather than listing at random. Different interviewers want different emphasis: a power electronics interviewer may want arcing, EMI and voltage drop; a reliability interviewer wants wear.
