::: testing
Whether you can turn a vague "design a circuit" prompt into requirements, an architecture and numbers. The interviewer is looking for: clarifying questions (rail voltage, thresholds, speed, tolerance, what the output drives), the **window comparator** idea (one divider tap per threshold compared with a reference), **hysteresis** so the output doesn't chatter, a **shunt plus amplifier plus comparator** chain for current, and a tolerance estimate that shows you know the trip points are not exact. Bonus: you mention integrated supervisors and eFuses and say when you would use them instead.
:::

::: why
Every protection scheme ([[q0454]]) starts with a detector: something must decide that "the rail is too high, too low, or the current is too big" before anything can react ([[q0456]]). Detectors are also a classic design problem because they combine dividers, references, comparators, hysteresis and current sensing in one small circuit. Getting the thresholds right, with real resistor tolerances, is where candidates show whether they can design or only recognise.
:::

::: concepts
- **Voltage divider**: scales the monitored rail to the reference level ([[q0008]]), and its Thevenin resistance matters for loading ([[q0224]]).
- **Comparator**: an open-loop op-amp that outputs a logic level depending on which input is higher ([[q0127]]).
- **Voltage reference**: a stable voltage (for example 1.24 V) that sets the threshold independent of the rail.
- **Hysteresis**: positive feedback that makes the rising and falling thresholds differ so noise does not cause chatter.
- **Shunt sensing and CSA**: a small series resistor converts current to voltage and a current-sense amplifier scales it ([[q0449]], [[q0439]]).
- **Window comparator**: two comparators defining a "good" range between a low and a high limit.
:::

::: answer
"I'd first pin down the requirements. Say a 12 V rail that should be 10.8 to 13.2 V in normal operation, I want the undervoltage to trip at about 10 V, overvoltage at about 14 V, and overcurrent at 5 A. I'd also ask how fast it must react and what the output drives.

For the voltage part, I'd use a window comparator. A resistor ladder from the rail to ground gives two taps. Each tap goes to a comparator whose other input is a 1.24 V reference. One comparator trips when its tap goes above the reference, which is overvoltage, and the other when its tap falls below it, which is undervoltage. The ladder ratios set the thresholds: $V_{trip} = V_{ref}\cdot R_{total}/R_{below}$. I'd use 1% resistors, add a little hysteresis, say a few percent, so noise doesn't chatter, and OR the outputs into one fault line.

For current, I'd put a milliohm shunt in the rail, amplify it with a current-sense amplifier, and compare to the same reference. With 5 mΩ and a gain of 50, 5 A gives 1.25 V, so the comparator trips at about 4.96 A. The shunt dissipates only about 0.12 W at the trip.

Finally, I'd check tolerances and add a small RC filter so short spikes don't trigger. If I just needed this on a board, I'd consider an integrated supervisor or eFuse, which does all of it in one part."
:::

::: technical
**Requirements (example).** Rail 12 V nominal. Undervoltage (UV) falling trip 10 V, overvoltage (OV) rising trip 14 V, overcurrent (OC) trip about 5 A. Reference $V_{ref}=1.24$ V.

**Window divider.** A three-resistor ladder $R_a$ (top), $R_b$, $R_c$ (bottom). The lower tap sees $V_{ref}$ at OV: $V_{OV} = V_{ref}\frac{R_a+R_b+R_c}{R_c}$. The upper tap sees $V_{ref}$ at UV: $V_{UV} = V_{ref}\frac{R_a+R_b+R_c}{R_b+R_c}$. With $R_{tot}=100$ kΩ: $R_c = 100\cdot 1.24/14 = 8.86$ kΩ, $R_b + R_c = 100\cdot1.24/10 = 12.4$ kΩ so $R_b = 3.54$ kΩ, and $R_a = 87.6$ kΩ. Nearest E96 values 86.6 k, 3.57 k, 8.87 k give trips of 13.85 V and 9.87 V without hysteresis. Ladder current is only $12/100\text{k} = 0.12$ mA.

**Hysteresis.** A feedback resistor $R_h$ (about 1 MΩ) from the OV comparator output to its non-inverting input moves the release point about 0.45 V below the trip point (13.96 V rising, 13.51 V falling).

**Current.** $V_{sense}=I R_s$, $R_s=5$ mΩ, so 5 A gives 25 mV, and a CSA with gain 50 gives 1.25 V. Compared with $V_{ref}=1.24$ V the trip is $1.24/(50\times0.005)=4.96$ A. Dissipation in the shunt at trip is $I^2R_s = 0.123$ W.

**Tolerance.** With 1% resistors and a 1% reference, worst-case OV trip falls between about 13.46 V and 14.24 V (±2.8%), UV between 9.60 V and 10.15 V.
:::

::: short
"I'd use a window comparator: a resistor ladder from the rail with two taps, each going to a comparator against a 1.24 V reference, one for overvoltage and one for undervoltage, with a little hysteresis so it doesn't chatter. The trip is the reference times the total divider over the lower part. For current I'd put a milliohm shunt in the rail, amplify it with a current-sense amp, and compare to the same reference. Then I'd OR the outputs into one fault line, check the tolerances, and add a small filter against glitches."
:::

::: long
"First I'd ask what we're protecting and what the numbers are, because the circuit follows from them. Let's say a 12 V rail, UV at 10 V, OV at 14 V and OC at 5 A, with an output that drives a shutdown pin.

For the voltage monitor, I'd build a window comparator. A three-resistor ladder from the rail to ground gives me two taps. The upper tap goes to the inverting input of the UV comparator, and the lower tap goes to the non-inverting input of the OV comparator. The other inputs go to a 1.24 V reference. The UV comparator trips when its tap falls below 1.24 V, so the threshold is $V_{ref}$ times the total resistance over the resistance from that tap to ground. The OV comparator is the same with the lower tap. For a 100 kΩ ladder that works out to about 87.6, 3.54 and 8.86 kΩ, and I'd use the nearest 1% values, which land the trips at about 9.9 V and 13.9 V.

Next, hysteresis. Without it, ripple or noise near the threshold makes the output chatter. A big resistor from the output back to the non-inverting input gives a few hundred millivolts of hysteresis.

For overcurrent, I'd put a 5 milliohm shunt in the rail. Current times 5 milliohms is 25 millivolts at 5 amps. A current-sense amplifier with a gain of 50 turns that into 1.25 volts, and a comparator against the same reference trips at about 4.96 amps. The shunt only dissipates about a tenth of a watt.

Then I'd combine the outputs with an OR into one fault signal. I'd check worst-case tolerance, about plus or minus 3% with 1% parts, and add an RC filter or blanking time so that inrush doesn't trip it.

And honestly, on a real board I might just use a supervisor IC or an eFuse that integrates all of this."
:::

::: terms
- **Window comparator**: two comparators that flag when a signal is outside a lower and upper limit.
- **Reference voltage ($V_{ref}$)**: a stable voltage that sets the comparator threshold.
- **Hysteresis**: the difference between the rising and falling trip points, created by positive feedback.
- **Shunt (sense) resistor**: a small series resistor that converts current to a measurable voltage.
- **CSA gain**: the multiplication applied to the small shunt voltage; see [[q0449]].
- **Kelvin sensing**: bringing the sense wires from the shunt's inner terminals so trace resistance is not measured.
- **Blanking / filter time**: a delay that ignores very short events, such as inrush.
- **Open-drain output**: a comparator output that only pulls low, allowing wired-OR with a pull-up.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws a comparator with a divider and a reference and says "it trips when voltage is too high".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts with **requirements and clarifying questions**: nominal rail, thresholds, speed, tolerance, output action, supply for the detector itself.
- Gives a **window comparator** with the trip formula, real E96 values and the **resulting trip points**.
- Adds **hysteresis** and explains the reason (noise on the rail near the threshold).
- Designs the **current path** with shunt value, power loss, gain and the resulting trip current.
- Estimates **tolerance** (resistors, reference, comparator offset) and states which trip is most critical.
- Mentions **filtering**, **Kelvin connections** and integrated alternatives (supervisors, eFuses).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Skipping hysteresis.** A noisy rail near the threshold makes the comparator chatter and the system toggles on and off.
- **Powering the detector from the rail it monitors without checking its minimum supply.** At the UV trip, the comparator must still work (use the reference rail or an always-on supply).
- **Divider resistances in the megaohm range with a leaky input.** Comparator bias current and PCB leakage then shift the thresholds. See [[q0224]].
- **Forgetting the shunt's voltage drop and power.** A 100 mΩ shunt at 5 A drops 0.5 V and dissipates 2.5 W.
- **Sensing across a shunt with long, shared traces.** The trace resistance and ground bounce corrupt the milliVolt signal. Use Kelvin connections.
- **Ignoring tolerance.** "Trip at 14.0 V" with 1% parts really means somewhere between 13.5 and 14.2 V.
:::

::: deep
## Step 0: clarify what the detector must do

An open prompt like "design a circuit to detect overcurrent and/or over/undervoltage" is a requirements question first. Useful questions:

- What is the rail (12 V, 5 V, battery 3 to 4.2 V) and its normal tolerance?
- Where should the trips be? Tight limits give false trips; loose limits fail to protect the weakest part.
- How fast must it respond? A short-circuit needs microseconds; a slow overload can take milliseconds.
- What does the output drive: a shutdown pin, an MCU interrupt, a latch, a gate driver?
- What supply does the detector itself run from? If it is the rail being monitored, it must work at the UV threshold.

For this page, take a 12 V rail that is nominally 10.8 to 13.2 V, with UV at 10 V, OV at 14 V and an OC trip near 5 A.

## The voltage window

A comparator compares two voltages. We want the comparison to say "the rail is above 14 V" or "below 10 V", so the rail is first scaled by a divider ([[q0008]]) to the level of a reference. With the ladder in the figure, $V_{tap2} = V_{in}\frac{R_c}{R_a+R_b+R_c}$ and $V_{tap1} = V_{in}\frac{R_b+R_c}{R_a+R_b+R_c}$.

- **OV** trips when $V_{tap2}$ rises above $V_{ref}$: $V_{OV} = V_{ref}\frac{R_{tot}}{R_c}$.
- **UV** trips when $V_{tap1}$ falls below $V_{ref}$: $V_{UV} = V_{ref}\frac{R_{tot}}{R_b+R_c}$.

Solve for resistors with $R_{tot} = 100$ kΩ and $V_{ref}=1.24$ V:

$$R_c = R_{tot}\frac{V_{ref}}{V_{OV}} = 8.857\ \text{k}\Omega,\quad R_b+R_c = R_{tot}\frac{V_{ref}}{V_{UV}} = 12.4\ \text{k}\Omega,\quad R_a = R_{tot}-12.4 = 87.6\ \text{k}\Omega$$

so $R_b = 3.543$ kΩ. Choosing the closest E96 values (86.6 k, 3.57 k, 8.87 k) shifts the trips slightly to 13.85 V and 9.87 V. Check this against the requirement; if 14.0 V is firm, pick values by search instead of by eye (see [[q0017]] for the E-series).

## Hysteresis

Real rails have ripple and noise. If the rail hovers at 13.95 V with 100 mV of noise, a plain comparator toggles repeatedly. Positive feedback fixes this. With $R_h$ from the output back to the non-inverting input, the node sees a different effective divider depending on the output state:

- Output low: $R_h$ goes to ground, in parallel with $R_c$, so the threshold goes slightly up.
- Output high (fault): $R_h$ goes to the output voltage (5 V), adding current into the node, so the rail must fall further to release the fault.

With $R_h = 1$ MΩ, the OV trip is 13.96 V rising and 13.51 V falling, giving 0.45 V (about 3%) of hysteresis. A larger $R_h$ gives less hysteresis. The same technique applies to the UV comparator, or you can choose a comparator with built-in hysteresis.

## Tolerance: the trip is a range, not a number

The OV trip is $V_{ref}R_{tot}/R_c$. Worst case with a 1% reference and 1% resistors (all in the worst direction) gives

$$V_{OV,max} = 1.24(1.01)\,\frac{1.01(86.6+3.57)+0.99(8.87)}{0.99(8.87)} \approx 14.24\ \text{V},\qquad V_{OV,min}\approx 13.46\ \text{V}$$

(a spread of about ±2.8%), and the UV trip lies between about 9.60 V and 10.15 V. Comparator input offset (typically a few mV at the 1.24 V node, so around 0.1 V at the 12 V rail) adds to this. If the real requirement is "never exceed 14.5 V", then 14.24 V worst-case still meets it; if the requirement was 14.0 V, you need tighter parts or a trim. The honest answer in an interview is the trip range, not the nominal.

## The current branch

Overcurrent detection reuses the pattern: turn the physical quantity into a voltage, then compare to a reference. A shunt $R_s$ in series with the rail makes $V_s = IR_s$. The shunt trades signal against loss: more resistance gives more signal and more heat. With a trip near 5 A:

- $R_s = 5$ mΩ gives 25 mV, loss $I^2R_s = 0.123$ W at 4.96 A.
- A CSA gain of 50 raises 25 mV to 1.25 V, which a comparator with $V_{ref} = 1.24$ V trips at $I = 1.24/(50\times0.005) = 4.96$ A.
- Amplifier offset matters: a 100 µV input offset is $100\ \mu V/5\ m\Omega = 20$ mA of current error, small here but critical at lower trips.

## Speed, filtering and what to do with the output

The detector response is limited by the CSA bandwidth and comparator propagation delay, from a few hundred nanoseconds for a fast CSA-plus-comparator chain to tens of microseconds for slow, filtered ones. A short-circuit protection needs the fast path (see the cycle-by-cycle limit in [[q0457]]). A slow overload can use a filtered path. A small RC (say 10 kΩ and 1 nF, $\tau = 10$ µs) ahead of the comparator ignores glitches and inrush but lengthens response, which is why protection is often split into a fast hard limit and a slower, filtered overload limit.

The FAULT line can drive a shutdown pin, a latch, a gate-driver disable, or an MCU interrupt. What to do is [[q0456]].

## When to use an integrated part

A supervisor IC contains the reference, comparators, hysteresis and delay with better accuracy (often ±1% over temperature) than discrete parts. An eFuse or hot-swap controller combines shunt amplifier, comparator and switch. Use a discrete design when the thresholds are unusual, when you need to learn or when cost is critical; use the integrated solution when accuracy, space and time-to-market matter.
:::

::: applications
- **Battery-powered products**: undervoltage lockout so a cell isn't over-discharged, plus overcurrent detection on the load switch ([[q0295]]).
- **12 V and 24 V industrial and automotive inputs**: OV/UV window monitors before downstream converters.
- **Power sequencing of FPGAs and SoCs**: power-good detectors check each rail's window ([[q0171]]).
- **Motor drivers**: shunt plus comparator for cycle-by-cycle current limit ([[q0425]]).
- **Server and telecom hot-swap cards**: sense resistor, amplifier and comparator inside a hot-swap controller.
- **Bench power supplies**: OVP, OCP trip circuits with front-panel adjustable thresholds.
:::

::: assumptions
I assumed a single DC rail around 12 V, a 1.24 V reference available from a stable source, and a comparator able to operate at the lowest rail voltage of interest. Thresholds and part values would change for a 3.3 V rail (a lower ladder ratio, with the ladder current maybe larger) or a battery rail (microamp-level dividers for low standby current). The shunt value assumes a 5 A trip; at 50 A a 1 mΩ shunt and a lower gain would be used and the shunt would need power rating and thermal design. A valid alternative: use the MCU's ADC and an on-chip comparator with firmware thresholds, slower but flexible; or use a dedicated supervisor or eFuse, which is usually the preferred production answer.
:::

::: followups
### How would you set the thresholds if the rail is 3.3 V and the supervisor must trip at 3.0 V?
With a 1.24 V reference, the divider ratio is $1.24/3.0 = 0.413$. For a 100 kΩ total, $R_{bottom} = 41.3$ kΩ and $R_{top} = 58.7$ kΩ (E96 neighbours 41.2 k and 59.0 k). Because the threshold is only 1.8 V above the reference, comparator offset and reference tolerance are a larger fraction of the margin, so check that the comparator works at 3.0 V supply. Many supervisor ICs avoid the problem by integrating all of this.

### How do you avoid false trips from motor inrush on the current detector?
Add a filter or blanking time: an RC after the CSA, or a digital delay on the fault of a few tens of microseconds to milliseconds, or a two-level scheme with a fast threshold at a high current (hard short) and a slower threshold at a lower current (overload). Also soft-start the load so inrush is smaller ([[q0343]]). Choose the delay shorter than the time the hardware can survive the fault.

### Why use Kelvin sensing on the shunt?
The load current flows through copper that has its own resistance, for example 1 mΩ of trace. If the sense wires tap the high-current path rather than the shunt's terminals, the measured voltage includes that trace drop, giving errors comparable to the shunt value (here 20%). Kelvin connections bring separate sense traces from the inner edge of the shunt pads.

### What if the rail drops to zero during a short and the detector loses power?
Then the detector must be designed to fail safe. Power it from a separate always-on supply or hold-up capacitor, use a latch with a defined power-up state, or choose a topology where loss of power disconnects the load (a normally-off switch). It is also why supervisors keep working down to about 1 V supply and drive their outputs to a defined state.

### Scenario: your OV trip is measured at 14.6 V instead of 14.0 V. What do you check?
First the arithmetic of the actual resistor values installed; then tolerance (a 2% reference and 1% resistors could explain about 4%); comparator input bias current through the divider (a 1 µA bias through a 9 kΩ source shifts only 9 mV, but through 1 MΩ it is 1 V); input offset; reference temperature drift; and the loading effect of $R_h$ or the ADC. Measure the tap voltage at the trip point and compare to $V_{ref}$ to see which part of the chain is off.
:::

::: interpretations
"And/or" in the question means the circuit may implement any combination: only voltage, only current, or all three. I treated it as all three, since the voltage window and the current trip share the same architecture (sense, scale, compare, combine). "Design a circuit" is open ended; I show the discrete version for teaching, and mention the integrated parts you would normally use.
