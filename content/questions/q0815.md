::: testing
Whether you can explain the physics of the hold phase: charge stored on a capacitor stays put if there is **nowhere for it to go**, so the whole job is to make every leakage path small. A strong answer names the paths (open-switch leakage, buffer bias current, capacitor dielectric leakage and absorption, PCB surface leakage), gives the droop equation $dV/dt=I/C$ with a worked number, explains why charge injection and feedthrough shift the held value, and lists the standard remedies. It's also a check that you can connect $Q=CV$ to a real circuit parameter such as "how long can I wait before converting".
:::

::: why
"The ADC reads a slightly different value if I wait longer" is a real bug, seen when someone increases the delay between sampling and conversion, runs the board hot, or uses a high-impedance multiplexer. The same physics explains why dynamic-memory cells need refreshing, why a SAR conversion has to finish within microseconds, and why precision hold circuits use guard rings and FET-input amplifiers. Understanding this question tells you how long a held value stays trustworthy, which sets the maximum conversion time and the minimum hold capacitor.
:::

::: concepts
- **Charge storage**: $Q=CV$; with no current path, $Q$ and hence $V$ stay constant. See [[q0022]].
- **Leakage current**: any unintended current out of the hold node. It discharges $C_H$ at $I/C$.
- **High input impedance buffer**: MOS or JFET input, picoamp-level bias.
- **Open-switch off-leakage**: reverse-biased junction and subthreshold current.
- **Parasitics**: dielectric absorption, surface leakage on the board ([[q0011]]).
- **Sample-and-hold basics** ([[q0814]]): track, hold, aperture.
:::

::: answer
"The value is held on a capacitor, and a capacitor keeps its voltage as long as no charge can leave it. When the sampling switch opens, the hold node is connected only to the capacitor plate, the open switch, and the input of a buffer amplifier. If I make all of those very high impedance, the charge stays.

The buffer is the key: it's a MOSFET or JFET input amplifier with an input current of picoamps, so it can read the voltage without drawing charge. The open switch is a MOSFET that's well off, so it leaks only a tiny reverse-junction current, typically picoamps to nanoamps.

What's left is the droop, $dV/dt = I_{leak}/C$. With 1 nA of leakage on a 10 picofarad capacitor, that's 100 V/s, or 0.1 mV per microsecond. A SAR conversion takes about a microsecond, so that's a tenth of a millivolt, well under one 12-bit LSB. But hold for 100 microseconds and you lose 10 millivolts.

So to keep it steady I use a bigger capacitor, a low-leakage switch, a low-bias buffer, short hold times, and on a board, guard rings and clean layout. And leakage gets worse with temperature, roughly doubling every 10 degrees, so the worst case is the hot corner."
:::

::: technical
**Hold-node equation.** With the switch open, KCL at the hold node:

$$C_H\frac{dv_H}{dt}=-\left(I_{sw,off}+I_{bias}+I_{diel}+I_{pcb}\right)\;\Rightarrow\;\frac{dv_H}{dt}=-\frac{I_{leak,total}}{C_H}$$

Over a hold time $t_h$ the error is $\Delta V=I_{leak}t_h/C_H$. For an $N$-bit converter you require $\Delta V<\tfrac12V_{LSB}$ (or a smaller fraction, since droop adds to other errors).

**Leakage sources.**
- *Open switch*: reverse-biased drain-body junction and subthreshold channel current. Strongly temperature dependent.
- *Buffer input bias current*: pA for CMOS/JFET inputs, tens to hundreds of nA for bipolar. Dominant in discrete designs.
- *Capacitor*: insulation resistance; for good dielectrics (C0G/NP0, film) the corresponding time constant is seconds to hours, but cheap high-K ceramics (X7R/Y5V) leak more and vary with voltage.
- *PCB surface leakage*: flux residue and moisture across a high-impedance node.

**Temperature.** Junction leakage roughly doubles every 8 to 10 °C, so the 25 °C datasheet value can be 30 to 60 times too optimistic at 85 °C.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The "doubles every 10 °C" rule is a common rule of thumb for silicon junction leakage. Exact rates depend on the device and the leakage mechanism (surface versus bulk).

**Non-droop errors in hold.**
- *Charge injection / pedestal*: $\Delta V=Q_{inj}/C_H$; fixed by design (dummy switch, differential, bottom-plate sampling) rather than waiting.
- *Dielectric absorption*: after a fast step, some charge diffuses back into the dielectric, so the held voltage creeps back toward its previous value by a fraction (hundredths of a percent for film; percent-level for some ceramics).
- *Feedthrough*: a fraction $C_{par}/(C_{par}+C_H)$ of the input swing couples through the open switch.

**Numerical droop budgets (12-bit, 3.3 V, half-LSB = 0.403 mV).**

| $C_H$ | $I_{leak}$ | Droop rate | Max hold time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 pF | 1 nA | 100 V/s | 4.0 µs |
| 10 pF | 100 pA | 10 V/s | 40 µs |
| 1 pF | 1 nA | 1000 V/s | 0.40 µs |
| 10 pF | 64 nA (hot) | 6400 V/s | 63 ns |
| 1 nF | 1 nA | 1 V/s | 400 µs |
:::

::: long
"The held voltage is just charge on a capacitor, $Q=CV$. If charge can't leave, the voltage can't change. So the whole question is: where could charge leak from the hold node, and how do I make each path tiny?

There are four paths. First, the open sampling switch. A MOSFET that's off still has reverse-biased junction leakage and a bit of subthreshold current, from picoamps up to nanoamps. Second, the buffer amplifier that reads the capacitor. I make that a MOSFET or JFET input so its input bias is picoamps; a bipolar input would pull hundreds of nanoamps and drain the cap quickly. Third, the capacitor itself, which has finite insulation resistance, and dielectric absorption, where the dielectric slowly gives back some stored charge. Fourth, on a board, surface leakage across flux and humidity.

All of that appears as one current out of the node, and the voltage droops at $I/C$. As an example, 1 nA on 10 pF is 100 volts per second, which sounds terrifying but it's 0.1 millivolt per microsecond. A SAR finishes in a microsecond or two, so droop is a fraction of an LSB in 12 bits. If instead I held for 100 microseconds I'd lose 10 millivolts, or about 12 LSBs.

Temperature matters, since junction leakage roughly doubles every 10 degrees, so at 85 °C it's tens of times worse.

To keep a sample steady, I'd choose a larger capacitor if the source and speed allow, a low-leakage switch like a T-switch or a guarded one, a FET-input buffer, a C0G or film capacitor in discrete designs, guard rings on the board, and above all keep the hold short: convert right away. Charge injection is a separate effect at the moment the switch opens, but it's a fixed step, not a drift."
:::

::: terms
- **Hold node**: the capacitor/buffer-input node isolated during hold.
- **Droop**: slow decay of the held voltage due to leakage.
- **Droop rate**: $dV/dt=I_{leak}/C_H$.
- **Off-leakage**: current through an open switch.
- **Input bias current**: DC current into an amplifier input.
- **Dielectric absorption**: a capacitor's slow recovery of voltage after being discharged or changed.
- **Guard ring**: a conductor held at the node's potential around a high-impedance trace to intercept leakage.
- **Hold time**: duration the sample must be valid (the conversion time).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a capacitor stores the voltage".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains **conservation of charge** and what must be true (no path) for the voltage to be held.
- Lists **all leakage paths** and which dominate (buffer bias in discrete, switch off-leakage in IC).
- Computes **droop** numerically and compares with half an LSB to set a maximum hold time.
- Mentions **temperature scaling** and the worst-case corner.
- Distinguishes **drift** (droop, absorption) from **step** errors (charge injection, feedthrough).
- Lists **design remedies**: bigger $C$, FET input, low-leakage or guarded switch, C0G/film, layout guard, shorter hold.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "the capacitor holds it forever".** It holds as long as leakage allows, microseconds for pF-scale IC capacitors and perhaps seconds for a film capacitor and electrometer amplifier.
- **Using an ordinary op-amp with nA-level bias current.** It drains a 10 pF capacitor at 100 V/s.
- **Ignoring temperature.** A design that passes at 25 °C can fail at 85 °C.
- **Blaming droop for a pedestal.** A fixed step at the moment of hold is charge injection, not leakage ([[q0814]]).
- **Making $C_H$ huge to fix droop** without checking the track-phase settling time ([[q0817]]).
- **Forgetting the board.** A 1 GΩ leakage path across contaminated FR-4 is 3 nA at 3 V, which is bigger than the IC's own leakage.
:::

::: deep
## Why a floating capacitor holds voltage

An ideal capacitor satisfies $i=C\,dv/dt$. If the current is zero, $dv/dt=0$. A hold node is a plate of a capacitor connected to three things: a switch that is open, the input of an amplifier, and stray insulation. Make all three high-impedance and the current is almost zero. That is the entire principle. The engineering is in "almost".

## The leakage budget

Suppose we need 12-bit accuracy on a 3.3 V range: $V_{LSB}=0.806$ mV, and we'll allow droop of at most $\tfrac14$ LSB (0.2 mV) to leave margin. With $C_H=10$ pF and a conversion that takes $t_h=1$ µs:

$$I_{max}=\frac{C_H\Delta V}{t_h}=\frac{10\text{ pF}\cdot0.2\text{ mV}}{1\text{ µs}}=2\text{ nA}$$

So a SAR with a microsecond conversion tolerates nanoamp-level leakage. Now ask the same for a 1 ms hold (a slow multiplexed system waiting for a delta-sigma): $I_{max}=2$ pA, which is below the typical leakage of an ordinary switch. That's why slow-hold systems use big film capacitors and electrometer-grade amplifiers, or avoid long holds altogether.

Add leakage through the capacitor's own dielectric and across the PCB surface, and the sum is the droop current. The remedies target each path in turn.

## Fighting each path

| Path | Typical size | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Open-switch junction leakage | pA to nA, doubles every ~10 °C | Series-shunt or T-switch; bootstrapped/guarded switch so the voltage across the open switch is near zero; larger $C_H$ |
| Buffer bias current | pA (CMOS/JFET), 10 to 1000 nA (bipolar) | MOSFET/JFET-input amplifier |
| Capacitor insulation / absorption | Negligible for C0G/film; worse for X7R/Y5V | C0G, polystyrene, PTFE or polypropylene |
| PCB surface leakage | pA to nA with contamination | Guard ring driven to $V_H$, clean flux, conformal coat, no vias in the node |
| Capacitor value too small | $dV/dt\propto1/C$ | Raise $C_H$, within tracking/settling limits |

The most elegant trick is **bootstrapping or guarding**: if the node on the other side of the open switch is driven (by the output buffer) to the same voltage as $C_H$, the voltage across the off-switch is zero, so its leakage is zero. A guard ring around the hold node on the PCB does the same trick for surface leakage.

## Not droop, but still moves the held value

Two other effects change the held voltage *without* continuing leakage:

1. **Pedestal.** When the switch opens, channel charge $Q_{ch}\approx WLC_{ox}(V_{GS}-V_t)$ splits between $C_H$ and the source. A 3 fC injection on 10 pF is 0.3 mV, a half-LSB-sized step. Cures: a small dummy switch driven with the opposite clock, a differential structure, or bottom-plate sampling where the sampling switch is the last to open and sees a fixed potential.
2. **Dielectric absorption.** Step the capacitor from 0 to 3 V, then quickly sample. Some charge sits in the dielectric and bleeds back afterwards, shifting the voltage by a fraction of the step. For a precision S/H that can be 0.01% on polystyrene and larger on ceramics.

## What this means for a microcontroller

You can't change the on-chip leakage, but you can control the *time* between sampling and the end of conversion by choosing the ADC clock, and you can control the input side. Many MCU datasheets specify a minimum ADC clock frequency (or a maximum conversion time), and hold-capacitor droop is one reason such a limit exists: below some clock rate, the held charge leaks away before the last bits are decided. Check the minimum $f_{ADC}$ in your part's datasheet.

At the board level, the high-impedance node *outside* the chip (a sensor line, a capacitor at the pin) has the same problem: a 100 nF capacitor at the ADC pin fed from a 10 MΩ source has a 1 s time constant but holds well, while on a contaminated board a 1 GΩ leak to ground in parallel with that 10 MΩ source forms a divider that pulls a 3.3 V node down by about 1%, roughly 33 mV (about 3 nA of leakage through 10 MΩ).

::: applications
- **SAR ADC front ends in MCUs**: conversions of 1 to 5 µs, so the hold only needs to last that long.
- **Multi-channel simultaneous sampling ADCs** (motor-phase current sensing): the held samples wait while the channels are converted one after another, so droop sets the order and timing.
- **Peak detectors and analog track-and-hold** for pulse measurements, using FET-input amplifiers and polystyrene/PTFE capacitors.
- **Switched-capacitor filters and DAC reference sample-holds** that store a value for a whole clock period.
- **DRAM-like storage on silicon**: the same physics, with refresh because leakage is unavoidable.
- **Precision DAC deglitchers** that hold the previous value while the new one settles.
:::

::: assumptions
- The dominant effect is **leakage-driven linear droop**. Real droop can curve if the leakage depends on voltage across the open switch.
- Leakage is **constant** during the hold. In practice it depends on temperature and the held voltage.
- The buffer is **ideal otherwise** (no offset, infinite bandwidth).
- We consider **short holds** (conversion time). For minute-long holds, the physics is the same but the engineering uses electrometer techniques and guarded fixtures.
- The "doubles every 10 °C" scaling is approximate and varies by leakage mechanism.
:::

::: followups
### If I double the hold capacitor, what happens to droop and to settling?
Droop rate halves ($I/C$). The track-phase time constant doubles ($RC$), so the minimum sampling time doubles and the kT/C noise drops by $\sqrt2$. This is the basic trade-off in [[q0817]].

### Why use a MOSFET or JFET-input buffer instead of a bipolar op-amp?
Bias current. A bipolar input draws tens to hundreds of nanoamps, which drains a 10 pF capacitor at 1000 to 10,000 V/s. A MOSFET gate draws femtoamps to picoamps, five orders of magnitude lower.

### How would you reduce the leakage of the open switch itself?
Make the voltage across it zero: use a series-shunt arrangement or a bootstrapped switch so that the open switch's other terminal is driven to the held voltage by the buffer. Also use a larger device in a low-leakage process option, and keep the node away from sources of heat.

### How long must the hold last in a SAR, and what does that imply?
Roughly $N$ ADC clock periods, so about 1 µs for 12 bits at 14 MHz. So droop, which is 0.1 mV at 1 nA and 10 pF, is about an eighth of an LSB. If you slow the ADC clock to 100 kHz the hold lasts 120 µs and droop is 12 mV, 15 LSB, which is why datasheets specify a minimum ADC clock.

### Harder: your reading drifts upward over 1 ms on a high-impedance sensor but is stable on a low-impedance one. Is that droop?
Probably not the S/H's droop, because the sensor doesn't connect during hold. More likely the sampling capacitor is *not fully charged* during track and a long source resistance slowly adds charge across successive conversions, or leakage in the external node is shifting the sensor voltage. Compare against a known low-impedance source to separate S/H droop from front-end effects.
:::
