::: testing
Whether you know the **physics behind each magnetic sensor** (Hall effect for DC and AC, Faraday induction for CT and Rogowski) and can turn that physics into pros and cons instead of reciting a table.

The question says "inductive/magnetic", so the interviewer expects you to separate the families: what works at DC and what doesn't, what is isolated, what saturates, what drifts. The strong signal is connecting a limitation back to its cause, for example "a CT can't do DC because DC flux doesn't induce voltage and just saturates the core".
:::

::: why
Magnetic sensing is what you reach for when a shunt is the wrong tool: mains-referenced products, motor drives on 400 V buses, EV chargers, anything with hundreds of amps, or any design that needs safety isolation. Knowing why it works and where it fails (offset, stray fields, saturation, bandwidth) lets you pick the right part and understand the datasheet numbers. The comparison against shunts is the heart of [[q0442]], and this page is the magnetic half of that discussion.
:::

::: concepts
- **Magnetic field of a conductor**: $B = \mu_0 I/(2\pi r)$ around a long straight wire. Sensors measure $B$ (or flux) instead of voltage drop. See [[q0439]].
- **Hall effect**: charge carriers moving in a magnetic field feel a sideways force; a transverse voltage $V_H = \frac{I_{bias}B}{n q t}$ appears across a thin plate.
- **Faraday's law**: $v = -N\,d\Phi/dt$, so a coil around a changing current produces a voltage. This is the principle of CTs and Rogowski coils.
- **Transformer action**: ampere-turns balance, $I_pN_p = I_sN_s$ when the core isn't saturated.
- **Magnetic saturation and hysteresis**: ferromagnetic cores flatten out at high flux and remember past flux, which limits accuracy.
- **Galvanic isolation**: no conductive connection between primary and sensing electronics. See [[q0447]] for why isolation sometimes forces the choice.
:::

::: answer
"Magnetic current sensing measures the field a current makes instead of the voltage it drops, so there's no resistor in the path. There are three main versions.

A Hall-effect sensor puts a thin semiconductor plate near the conductor. The field deflects carriers and produces a small voltage proportional to the field, which is proportional to the current. It works at DC and AC, and it's isolated.

A current transformer uses the conductor as a one-turn primary and a secondary of N turns, so the secondary current is $I_p/N$, and a burden resistor turns that into a voltage. It's rugged and nearly lossless, but AC only.

A Rogowski coil is an air-core coil that outputs $M\,dI/dt$, so you integrate it. It can't saturate, so it's great for huge fast currents, but also AC only.

The pros are isolation, almost no insertion loss, and high current range. The cons are cost, size, offset and gain drift, stray-field sensitivity, and limited accuracy at small currents. So I'd use them for isolation or high current, and use a shunt when I can."
:::

::: technical
**Hall effect.** For a plate of thickness $t$, carrier density $n$ and bias current $I_b$ in a field $B$ perpendicular to the plate:
$$V_H = \frac{I_b B}{n q t}$$
Silicon has a few hundred µV/mT at practical bias; integrated sensors add amplification, chopper stabilization for offset, and temperature compensation. A conductor with 10 A at 1 cm gives $B = 2\times 10^{-7}\times 10/0.01 = 0.2$ mT, which is why a core or an integrated U-shaped conductor close to the chip is used to concentrate the field.

**Open-loop vs closed-loop.** Open-loop: the Hall element sits in a gapped core and its output is amplified directly. Simple and cheap, but gain and offset depend on core, temperature, and Hall element. Closed-loop (compensated): a feedback winding drives the Hall output to zero by cancelling the flux; the compensating current is the output ($I_p N_p = I_s N_s$ again). Accuracy of a few tenths of a percent is typical, at higher cost and supply current.

**Current transformer.** $I_s = I_p N_p/N_s$, so with $N_s = 1000$ and $I_p = 10$ A, $I_s = 10$ mA, and a 100 Ω burden gives 1 V. The secondary must never be open-circuited while the primary is energized: the core flux has nowhere to go, the secondary voltage rises (volts per turn times turns), and it can be hazardous. DC in the primary biases the core and saturates it.

**Rogowski.** $v = M\,dI/dt$ with $M \approx \mu_0 n A$ (turns per length times cross-section area). For an illustrative $M = 0.2\ \mu\text{H}$ and a 100 A rms, 50 Hz current, peak $dI/dt = 2\pi\cdot 50\cdot 141 = 44$ kA/s gives about 8.9 mV peak. An integrator ($v_{out} = -\frac{1}{RC}\int v\,dt$) restores $I$.

**Error sources.** Offset (Hall), remanent flux (cores), temperature drift of sensitivity, stray fields from neighbouring conductors, core saturation at overcurrent, and limited bandwidth (open-loop Hall tens of kHz, closed-loop up to hundreds of kHz, CTs limited at low end by magnetizing inductance and at high end by leakage and self-resonance).
:::

::: short
"Magnetic sensing measures the field around the conductor instead of a voltage drop. Hall sensors detect the field directly and work at DC and AC. Current transformers and Rogowski coils use Faraday induction, so they only see AC. The big advantages are isolation, almost no insertion loss and high current range. The downsides are cost, size, offset and temperature drift, sensitivity to stray fields, and poor accuracy at small currents compared with a shunt."
:::

::: long
"Magnetic sensing means I measure the magnetic field around a conductor rather than the voltage across a resistor in it. That gives me isolation and almost no loss, which a shunt can't do.

There are three main flavors. The first is a Hall-effect sensor. A thin semiconductor plate carries a bias current, and a magnetic field perpendicular to it deflects the carriers, building a transverse voltage proportional to the field. Since the field is proportional to the current in the conductor, I get a voltage proportional to current, and it works at DC. Open-loop parts are cheap and simple. Closed-loop parts add a compensation coil that nulls the core flux, so accuracy is much better, but they cost more and consume more power.

The second is a current transformer. The conductor is the primary, the secondary has N turns, so secondary current is the primary divided by N, and a burden resistor converts that to voltage. It's tough and basically lossless, but it only works for AC, because DC doesn't induce voltage and just saturates the core. And I must never leave the secondary open while energized.

The third is a Rogowski coil, an air-core coil that outputs M times dI/dt. It can't saturate, so it handles huge, fast pulses, but I need an integrator and it's AC only.

Overall the pros are isolation, low loss, and large current range. The cons are cost and size, offset and gain drift with temperature, sensitivity to stray fields and neighbouring conductors, and poor accuracy at small currents, where a shunt wins. So my rule is shunt first, magnetic when I need isolation or hundreds of amps."
:::

::: terms
- **Hall effect**: transverse voltage in a current-carrying conductor placed in a perpendicular magnetic field.
- **Open-loop Hall sensor**: Hall element in a gapped core with its output amplified directly; simple, with drift.
- **Closed-loop (compensated) sensor**: feedback winding nulls the core flux; output is the compensation current.
- **Current transformer (CT)**: transformer with the measured conductor as primary; steps current down by the turns ratio.
- **Burden resistor**: the load resistor on a CT secondary that converts current to voltage.
- **Rogowski coil**: air-core, flexible coil whose output is proportional to $dI/dt$.
- **Magnetic saturation**: the core's permeability collapses at high flux, distorting or clipping the output.
- **Remanence**: flux left in a core after the field is removed; a source of offset in magnetic sensors.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "Hall effect and current transformers; they're isolated".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates DC-capable (Hall, fluxgate) from AC-only (CT, Rogowski) and explains *why* via Faraday's law.
- Gives the equations at the right level: $V_H \propto B$, $I_s = I_p/N$, $v = M\,dI/dt$.
- Quantifies something: for example, 10 A gives 0.2 mT at 1 cm, or a CT of 1000:1 with 100 Ω burden gives 100 mV per amp... hence 1 V at 10 A.
- Names the failure modes: stray fields, remanence, core saturation, open-circuited CT secondary, offset drift at small currents.
- Concludes with a use rule (shunt first, magnetic for isolation or very high current) rather than saying magnetic is "better".
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using a CT or Rogowski for DC.** They respond to $dI/dt$ or changing flux only, so DC reads zero (or saturates the core).
- **Saying magnetic sensors are "more accurate".** Closed-loop parts can be very good, but open-loop Hall sensors are typically percent-level, and all of them have offset that hurts at small currents.
- **Leaving a CT secondary open-circuit.** The secondary voltage can climb to dangerous levels; always keep the burden connected or shorted.
- **Forgetting stray fields.** A nearby busbar or magnet adds error. Shielding, differential Hall arrangements and layout matter.
- **Saying isolation is free.** Magnetic isolation doesn't remove noise; cables and the output stage can still couple it in, and the isolation voltage rating is specific to the package.
- **Ignoring the integrator on a Rogowski coil.** Without it, the output is a derivative, not the current, and integrator drift needs handling.
:::

Figure: Hall principle. A bias current flows through the plate; the field from the measured conductor pushes the carriers sideways, so a voltage appears across the plate, proportional to B and therefore to the measured current. Because the bias current is DC-biased, this works at DC, unlike induction.

**Faraday sensors** (CT, Rogowski): a coil linking the conductor sees flux $\Phi \propto I$ and develops $v = -N\,d\Phi/dt$. The signal exists only while the current *changes*, which is why DC is invisible.

Figure: The primary carries the load current (one turn through the core). The secondary, with a 1000:1 ratio, delivers 1/1000 of it into the burden resistor; at 10 A the secondary carries 10 mA and the burden shows 1 V. Never run it with the burden removed.

Design points:

- **Burden sets the voltage and the saturation limit.** The core can only support a flux level; secondary voltage $V_s = I_s R_b$ must stay below the saturation voltage, which is roughly $V_{sat} \approx 4.44 f N_s A_e B_{sat}$ for a sinusoid. A bigger burden gives more signal but saturates at lower primary current.
- **Frequency response.** The magnetizing inductance causes droop at low frequencies, and leakage inductance and winding capacitance limit the top end.
- **DC component.** A small DC offset in the primary will walk the core into saturation over time; measure rectified loads with care.

Figure: Op-amp integrator turning the coil's M·dI/dt into a voltage proportional to I. The 1/RC scaling sets sensitivity, and a real design adds a resistor in parallel with C (or a high-pass) so the DC offset doesn't ramp the output to the rail.

Rogowski coils have no iron, so no saturation and excellent linearity up to very large currents and fast edges, and the loose flexible coil can wrap around an existing conductor. They can't measure DC and they are susceptible to external fields and position of the conductor within the loop unless well made.

## Hall sensors: where they struggle

The fatal weakness is **offset relative to small signals**. An integrated sensor with a 66 mV/A sensitivity and a typical 10 to 20 mV of offset error corresponds to roughly 0.15 to 0.3 A of equivalent current error. A shunt amplifier with 100 µV offset on a 10 mΩ shunt has a 10 mA equivalent error, so it's more than 20x better at small currents.

> The sensitivity (66 mV/A) and offset (15 mV) are typical of low-cost integrated ±30 A Hall parts but vary by vendor, part and temperature. Precision closed-loop sensors do much better. Use your actual datasheet.

## Why isolation and loss are the selling points

A Hall sensor with an integrated primary conductor has some resistance (on the order of 1 mΩ for integrated parts, though check your device), so its loss at 30 A is on the order of 1 W; a CT's primary is just a wire through a hole, with essentially zero loss. In both cases the sensing electronics are separated from the high-voltage side by insulation, which is what safety standards demand when the measured conductor is on mains or a high-voltage bus.

::: applications
- **Motor drives and inverters**: phase-current sensing with closed-loop Hall or isolated shunt amplifiers, for field-oriented control on 400 V buses.
- **Energy meters**: current transformers or Rogowski coils on mains conductors.
- **EV charging stations**: Hall or CT sensing for current regulation, metering and ground-fault detection.
- **Battery packs**: Hall sensors on high-current busbars (hundreds of amps) where a shunt would dissipate too much.
- **Welding and induction heating**: Rogowski coils for large pulsed currents.
- **Integrated low-cost sensors**: parts like Allegro's ACS712 family (Hall, ±5 A to ±30 A) used in hobby and appliance designs.
:::

::: assumptions
- "Inductive/magnetic" is interpreted broadly: Hall-effect (field sensing), CT and Rogowski (induction), and compensated/fluxgate types. If the interviewer meant only transformer-type sensing, concentrate on CT and Rogowski.
- Illustrative numbers (Hall offset, Rogowski $M$, integrated-conductor resistance) are typical, not tied to any single part.
- Accuracy discussion assumes single-conductor sensing in a clean layout. Adjacent conductors, busbar geometry, and mounting tolerances all change accuracy in practice.
- Another valid reading: "inductive current sensing" as **inductor DCR sensing** in a converter ($V = I R_{DCR}$ through an RC network). That is a resistive method that happens to use an inductor, covered in [[q0439]] and [[q0225]].

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Bandwidth ranges listed for each sensor class vary widely with design and vendor.

::: followups
### Why can't a current transformer measure DC?
Its output depends on $d\Phi/dt$. A constant primary current gives constant flux and zero induced voltage, and the DC flux also pushes the core toward saturation, spoiling the AC response. For DC you need Hall, fluxgate or a shunt.

### What happens if you open-circuit the secondary of a CT?
The secondary current is forced to zero, so the primary's ampere-turns are no longer cancelled and drive the core hard into saturation. The flux changes extremely quickly through each cycle as it comes out of saturation, inducing a very large voltage across the open secondary winding, which can damage insulation and endanger people. Always keep a burden or shorting link on a live CT.

### How does a closed-loop Hall sensor improve on an open-loop one?
It uses the Hall element only as a null detector. A feedback amplifier drives a compensation winding until the net flux in the core is zero, so $I_pN_p = I_sN_s$. Gain then depends on the turns ratio, not on Hall sensitivity or core permeability, which drifts. The result is better linearity, lower temperature drift, and higher bandwidth, at the cost of supply power and price.

### Why does a Rogowski coil need an integrator, and what goes wrong with it?
Its output is $M\,dI/dt$, which is the derivative of the current. The integrator recovers $I$, but any DC offset in the input ramps the integrator output until it hits the rail, so practical circuits use a high-pass or leaky integrator, which also limits low-frequency accuracy.

### Scenario: your Hall current sensor reads 0.2 A when no current flows. Is it faulty, and how would you handle it?
Probably not faulty: a 0.2 A zero offset is plausible for a low-cost sensor (about 13 mV at 66 mV/A). Measure the zero-current output at start-up and subtract it in firmware, but remember the offset also drifts with temperature, so recalibrate periodically or pick a part with better offset specs. If you need accuracy at 100 mA, a shunt will be better.
:::

::: interpretations
"Inductive/magnetic current sensing" is a little ambiguous. The primary reading is **sensing the magnetic field produced by the current** (Hall, CT, Rogowski, fluxgate). A secondary reading is **inductor-based resistive sensing** (DCR sensing of a power inductor), which comes up in buck converters ([[q0225]], [[q0226]]). I'd answer the magnetic-field reading in full and mention DCR sensing in a sentence.
:::
