::: testing
Whether you understand **what physically changes** when the inductor current touches zero, and can quantify it. Interviewers listen for:

1. The definition by inductor current: CCM never reaches zero; DCM returns to zero and rests.
2. The boundary condition $I_{out}=\Delta I_L/2$ and what moves it ($L$, $f_{sw}$, $V_{in}$).
3. The consequences: in CCM the gain is just $D$; in DCM it depends on the load, so the duty cycle has to change with load. Also the effects on peak current, ripple, losses, ringing, and the small-signal model.
:::

::: why
Every buck passes through both regimes whenever the load varies by a decade or more, and the differences decide the compensation, the efficiency curve and the EMI. If a converter that was stable at full load oscillates at light load, or a rail overshoots at no load, the cause is almost always DCM behaviour that nobody accounted for. This also sets up the PFM questions that follow ([[q0231]], [[q0234]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Volt-second balance**: in steady state the average inductor voltage is zero over a period. See [[q0188]].
- **Inductor ripple current**: $\Delta I_L=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})D}{Lf_{sw}}$. See [[q0195]].
- **Synchronous vs diode rectification**: a diode blocks reverse current, so it enters DCM naturally; a synchronous FET needs ZCD. See [[q0203]].
- **Charge balance on $C_{out}$**: average capacitor current is zero, so the average inductor current equals the load current.
- **Parasitic resonance**: while the current is zero, $L$ rings with the switch-node capacitance.
- **Small-signal model**: CCM has an LC double pole; DCM has a single dominant pole.
:::

::: answer
"The difference is whether the inductor current ever hits zero. In continuous conduction, CCM, the current ramps up during the on-time and down during the off-time but never reaches zero, so there are two states per cycle. In discontinuous conduction, DCM, the current falls to zero before the cycle ends, and for the rest of the period both switches are effectively off and the inductor current sits at zero, so there are three states.

Which one you're in depends on load. The ripple current is fixed by $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$, $L$ and frequency, so as the load drops the average falls and eventually the valley touches zero. That boundary is at $I_{out}=\Delta I_L/2$. For my usual 12-to-3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz example that's about half an amp.

The big consequence is the gain. In CCM, $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$ regardless of load, so the loop has an easy job. In DCM the same duty cycle gives a higher output, and it rises as load falls, so the controller has to cut the duty cycle as the load drops. You also get higher peak current for the same average, ringing at the switch node during the idle time, and a different small-signal response, closer to a single pole. On the plus side, DCM turns on at zero current, so there's no reverse recovery loss, and at light load it's much more efficient if the low-side FET turns off at zero current, since it avoids circulating ripple current."
:::

::: technical
**States.** CCM: (1) $Q_1$ on, $i_L$ rises at $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$; (2) $Q_1$ off, freewheel path on, $i_L$ falls at $V_{out}/L$. DCM adds (3) both off, $i_L=0$, $v_L=0$, switch node floats at $V_{out}$ (with ringing).

**Boundary.** $I_{crit}=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{2Lf_{sw}}$, i.e. $K_{crit}=1-D$ with $K=\frac{2L}{RT}$. For 12 V to 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz: 0.51 A.

**DCM gain.** Volt-second balance gives $D_2=D\frac{V_{in}-V_{out}}{V_{out}}$ (the fraction of the period the inductor discharges). Average inductor current = $\frac12I_{pk}(D+D_2)=I_{out}$, with $I_{pk}=(V_{in}-V_{out})DT/L$. Solving:
$$M=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}=\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4K/D^2}},\qquad D=M\sqrt{\frac{K}{1-M}}$$
CCM: $M=D$.

**Example.** Hold 3.3 V at 0.2 A ($R=16.5$ Ω, $K=0.285$): $D=0.172$ (not 0.275), $I_{pk}=0.638$ A (3.2× the load), conduction 345 ns, discharge 909 ns, idle 746 ns.

**Other consequences.** Peak-to-average current is larger; RMS current is lower than forced CCM at the same load (0.29 A vs 0.36 A at 0.2 A); turn-on is at zero current (no diode reverse recovery); ringing at $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_{sw}})$ (about 3 MHz for 4.7 µH and 600 pF); small-signal: single pole at $\omega_p=\frac{2-M}{(1-M)RC}$ (about 1 kHz here) instead of the LC double pole.

**Spotting it on a scope.** In DCM the switch node shows a third level after the low side turns off (floating near $V_{out}$, ringing); in CCM it is a clean two-level square wave. The CCM compensation may not hold in DCM.
:::

::: short
"In CCM the inductor current never reaches zero; in DCM it falls to zero and rests for part of the cycle, so there are three switching states instead of two. The boundary is at $I_{out}=\Delta I_L/2$. In CCM the output is $D\,V_{in}$ regardless of load; in DCM the gain depends on the load, so the duty cycle must shrink as the load drops. DCM also has higher peak current, ringing at the idle interval, and a more first-order loop response, but better light-load efficiency."
:::

::: long
"Start with the inductor current. During the on-time it ramps up at $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$, during the off-time it ramps down at $V_{out}/L$. The ripple depends only on $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$, $L$ and the switching frequency, not on the load. The load sets the average, which is the midpoint of the triangle. If the load is high enough, the valley stays positive: that's CCM, with two states per cycle. As the load drops, the valley comes down, and at $I_{out}=\Delta I_L/2$ it just touches zero. For my usual example, 12 to 3.3 V with 4.7 µH at 500 kHz, that's about 0.5 A. Below that, the current wants to reverse, but a diode or a synchronous FET with zero-current detection blocks it, so the current sits at zero: DCM, with a third state where nothing conducts.

The headline consequence is the voltage gain. In CCM, volt-second balance on the inductor gives $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$ independent of load. In DCM, the inductor spends some of the period at zero, so the same duty cycle delivers more output voltage, and the gain rises as load falls, following $M=2/(1+\sqrt{1+4K/D^2})$ where $K=2L/(RT)$. To keep 3.3 V at 0.2 A in my example, the duty cycle drops from 0.275 to 0.172. At 10 mA it's down to 0.04, which is a 77 ns pulse, close to a controller's minimum on-time, which is why PFM takes over.

Other differences: peak current is about three times the average at that load, so the inductor and FETs see sharper pulses. The switch node rings while idle, at a few MHz, because the inductor resonates with the switch capacitance. The turn-on happens at zero current, so there's no diode reverse recovery. And the small-signal model changes: CCM has the LC double pole, DCM has one low-frequency pole around $(2-M)/((1-M)RC)$, which is about 1 kHz in this example, so a compensator tuned for CCM may be wrong at light load.

Efficiency-wise, DCM with diode emulation is better than forced CCM at light load because you're not circulating ripple current."
:::

::: terms
- **CCM / DCM**: continuous / discontinuous conduction mode, defined by whether $i_L$ reaches zero.
- **Critical (boundary) current**: the load where the valley just touches zero, $\Delta I_L/2$.
- **Conversion ratio $M$**: $V_{out}/V_{in}$.
- **Dimensionless load parameter $K$**: $2L/(RT)$; small $K$ means light load or small inductance.
- **$D_2$**: the fraction of the period during which the inductor discharges.
- **Idle interval**: the third state in DCM when $i_L=0$.
- **Zero-current detection (ZCD)**: a comparator that turns off the low-side FET when the current reaches zero.
- **Zero-current switching (ZCS) turn-on**: the high-side FET turns on with no inductor current, reducing turn-on loss.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "DCM is when the current goes to zero" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Counts the states (two vs three) and gives the boundary $I_{crit}=\Delta I_L/2$ with a number.
- Derives, or at least states, the **DCM gain** and explains the practical result: duty cycle must be reduced as the load falls to keep $V_{out}$ fixed.
- Points out the **unregulated** consequence: open-loop, the DCM output rises toward $V_{in}$ at no load.
- Lists the side effects: higher peak current, idle ringing, ZCS turn-on, single-pole small-signal model.
- Mentions that a diode (or synchronous FET with ZCD) is what makes DCM possible, and what happens without ZCD (forced CCM).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying $V_{out}=DV_{in}$ in DCM.** That is the CCM relation. In DCM the gain is above $D$ and rises as load falls.
- **Saying ripple current falls at light load in CCM.** It does not; only the average falls. In DCM the peak (and ripple) is set by on-time, so it falls as the duty cycle drops.
- **Thinking DCM is "bad".** It is a legitimate and often better operating regime at light load, and some designs (e.g. a small inductor for size) run DCM at all loads.
- **Forgetting that a synchronous converter in DCM needs zero-current detection.** Without it, the inductor current reverses and you get FCCM.
- **Confusing DCM with PFM.** DCM is about waveform shape; PFM is about pulse timing ([[q0228]]).
- **Using the CCM compensation at light load blindly.** The plant is different, so phase margin and bandwidth change.
:::

::: deep
## The two regimes side by side

Take 12 V to 3.3 V, $L=4.7$ µH, $f_{sw}=500$ kHz ($T=2$ µs). In steady state the inductor satisfies volt-second balance:
$$(V_{in}-V_{out})\,D\,T=V_{out}\,D_2\,T\quad\Rightarrow\quad D_2=D\,\frac{V_{in}-V_{out}}{V_{out}}$$
In CCM $D_2=1-D$, which gives $V_{out}=DV_{in}$. In DCM $D_2<1-D$, the remaining time $1-D-D_2$ is the idle interval.

The average inductor current is the area of the triangle divided by the period, and this must equal the load current:
$$I_{out}=\tfrac12 I_{pk}(D+D_2)f_{sw},\quad I_{pk}=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})DT}{L}$$

Substituting $D+D_2=DV_{in}/V_{out}$ and $I_{out}=V_{out}/R$ gives (with $M=V_{out}/V_{in}$ and $K=2L/(RT)$):
$$K M^2+D^2M-D^2=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad M=\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4K/D^2}}$$
This holds when $K<K_{crit}=1-D$; for larger $K$ (heavier load), $M=D$.

Left unregulated, at no load $M\to1$: the output floats up to $V_{in}$. This is why a DCM converter needs the controller to cut the duty cycle as the load falls.

## What the duty cycle has to do

To hold $V_{out}=3.3$ V ($M=0.275$), invert for $D=M\sqrt{K/(1-M)}$, with $K=2Lf_{sw}\,I_{out}/V_{out}$:

| Load | $K$ | Mode | $D$ needed | $t_{on}$ | $I_{pk}$ | Idle time |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 A | 2.85 | CCM | 0.275 | 550 ns | 2.51 A | 0 |
| 0.5 A | 0.712 | CCM edge | 0.272 | 545 ns | 1.01 A | 18 ns |
| 0.3 A | 0.427 | DCM | 0.211 | 422 ns | 0.78 A | 465 ns |
| 0.2 A | 0.285 | DCM | 0.172 | 345 ns | 0.64 A | 746 ns |
| 0.1 A | 0.142 | DCM | 0.122 | 244 ns | 0.45 A | 1114 ns |
| 10 mA | 0.0142 | DCM | 0.039 | 77 ns | 0.14 A | far longer |

(The 0.5 A row sits at the boundary of 0.509 A; the 2 A row's $I_{pk}$ is the CCM peak, load plus half the ripple.) The last row is the practical problem: 77 ns is near the minimum on-time of many controllers, so the controller cannot trim the duty cycle any further and has to skip pulses, which leads to PFM behaviour ([[q0231]]).

Below it the required duty falls roughly as $\sqrt{I_{out}}$, because the on-time needed to push the required charge goes with the square root of the load. The loop must do this automatically, which is why DCM looks like a higher loop gain at light load.

## Waveforms and losses

In DCM the current pulses are taller and narrower. At 0.2 A, the peak is 0.64 A, 3.2× the load. The RMS current is
$$I_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac{I_{pk}^2}{3}\cdot\frac{t_{on}+t_{off}}{T}}=0.29\text{ A}$$
while a forced-CCM converter at the same load has $\sqrt{0.2^2+1.018^2/12}=0.36$ A, so DCM has about 18% less RMS current, hence less conduction loss. More significant is that in DCM the ripple shrinks with load instead of staying at 1.02 A, and with diode emulation there is no circulating negative current. The costs that remain fixed per pulse are gate charge, $C_{oss}$ and the turn-off overlap.

- **Turn-on**: at zero current, there is no overlap loss and no body-diode reverse recovery.
- **Turn-off**: at $I_{pk}$, so turn-off overlap loss is a bit higher than in CCM at the same average current.
- **Peak current stress**: inductor saturation and FET current ratings must cover the DCM peak, not just the average.
- **Ringing**: after the inductor current hits zero, the switch node floats. The inductor and the total node capacitance (say 600 pF) resonate at $\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{4.7\text{ µH}\cdot600\text{ pF}}}=3.0$ MHz, a damped ring that radiates EMI. Sometimes the low-side FET is kept on or a snubber is added.

## Small-signal behaviour

In CCM, the duty-to-output transfer function has a double pole at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})=15.7$ kHz with a load-dependent $Q$ ([[q0227]]). In DCM, the inductor's current is no longer an independent state variable (it resets each cycle), so one pole disappears; the plant has a single pole near
$$f_p=\frac{2-M}{2\pi(1-M)RC}$$
which for $R=16.5$ Ω (0.2 A), $C=22$ µF, $M=0.275$ is about 1.0 kHz, and moves lower as the load lightens ($R$ rises). The DC gain also changes with load. A compensator designed for the CCM double pole may give too little phase at light load or, with current-mode control, the inner loop hides most of the difference.

## Choosing L with the boundary in mind

The boundary load $I_{crit}=V_{out}(1-D)/(2Lf_{sw})$ is a design handle. Suppose you want the converter to stay in CCM down to 10% of a 2 A full load, which is 0.2 A. Then $L\ge\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{2I_{crit}f_{sw}}=\frac{3.3\cdot0.725}{2\cdot0.2\cdot500\text{ k}}=11.96$ µH, so about 12 µH instead of the 4.7 µH that puts the boundary at 0.51 A. The price is a larger and costlier inductor with more DCR, and a slower response to load steps because the inductor current slews at $\Delta V/L$. Conversely, a designer who wants DCM at all but the highest loads picks a small inductor, accepts the higher peak and RMS currents, and enjoys faster transient response.

Neither choice is right in general: a notebook VRM is CCM-dominant, while a phone PMIC is happy to sit in DCM or PFM most of the time. The honest answer to "how would you pick $L$?" is: from the ripple target at full load, then check where the boundary lands and whether you can live with it.

A final practical note: at a given load, whether the converter is in CCM or DCM is decided by the inductor and frequency you chose, not by the converter. Changing $L$ or $f_{sw}$ in a design review changes which regime the idle and typical loads fall into, and with them the compensation and the shape of the efficiency curve.

::: applications
- **Phone/wearable bucks**: DCM with PFM at light load for battery life, CCM only when a processor wakes up.
- **Small asynchronous (diode) converters**: DCM at light load is automatic; designers account for the higher peak current in the inductor rating.
- **Low-power LED drivers and offline supplies**: the converter is *designed* to run in DCM (or boundary mode) so the diode reverse recovery disappears.
- **PFC front-ends**: critical (boundary) conduction mode is popular at medium power for the same reason.
- **Multiphase CPU VRMs**: phase shedding and diode emulation at light load to avoid circulating current.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Fixed-frequency PWM buck**, ideal components, constant $V_{in}$. Losses shift the numbers slightly; real DCM $D$ is somewhat higher than the ideal value.
- **Synchronous bridge with ideal zero-current detection** when I say "DCM". Without ZCD you are in forced CCM.
- **Example values** ($L=4.7$ µH, $C_{sw}=600$ pF, $C=22$ µF) are illustrative.
- **Small-signal discussion** is the voltage-mode duty-to-output plant. Peak current-mode control changes the picture (the inner loop dominates).
- Some authors call the boundary case "critical conduction mode" (CrCM) and treat it as its own control scheme with variable frequency.
:::

::: followups
### What is the output voltage if you leave the duty cycle at 0.275 and remove the load?
It rises toward $V_{in}$. With no load, $K\to0$ and $M=2/(1+\sqrt{1+4K/D^2})\to1$. In practice losses and the output capacitor's leakage limit it, but without regulation or a minimum load the output can overshoot dangerously. This is why feedback is essential in DCM and why some converters add a minimum-load bleeder.

### Why does a converter with a diode enter DCM automatically but a synchronous one does not?
A diode cannot conduct backwards, so the current stops when it reaches zero. A synchronous FET conducts both ways, so if it stays on the inductor current goes negative (forced CCM). To get DCM you need a zero-current detector that turns the low-side FET off, called diode emulation.

### How do you raise or lower the CCM/DCM boundary?
$I_{crit}=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{2Lf_{sw}}$. A larger $L$ or higher $f_{sw}$ lowers it (more CCM range). A smaller inductor raises it. At constant $L$ and $f$ the boundary is highest at high $V_{in}$ (small $D$). Designers choose $L$ so the boundary sits at the load where they want PFM to begin, typically 10 to 30% of full load.

### Why is the DCM peak current a concern for inductor selection?
For the same average current, DCM has taller pulses. At 0.2 A the peak is 3.2× the average, and the DCM peak at the CCM boundary (0.5 A load) is about 1.0 A while the same inductor in CCM at 2 A hits 2.5 A, so the full-load CCM peak dominates the rating. But in a small converter designed to be DCM at full load, the peak can be 2 to 3× the average and sets the saturation current.

### What if the compensation is tuned at full load and the converter oscillates at light load?
Probably the mode change moved the plant. In DCM the plant has one low-frequency pole and lower phase lag but its DC gain depends on load, so the loop gain may be higher than designed and the crossover lands in a region with extra phase lag from sampling or the output-capacitor ESR zero. Check the loop at the lightest load and in both modes, or use a peak current-mode controller where the plant is more uniform. See [[q0244]].
:::
