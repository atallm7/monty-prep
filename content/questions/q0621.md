::: basics
### In plain words
Test points work only if a probe can land on them reliably. So the layout has rules about pad size, spacing, side of the board, and distance from tall parts. The rules come from the size of the spring-loaded probes and the small errors in lining the board up. The factory's own guideline always overrides typical numbers.

### Picture it
Think of marked parking spaces. Each space must be big enough for the car, spaced so doors open, and kept clear of pillars. If the spaces are too small or too close, cars scrape and miss. A test pad is a parking space for a probe. The fixture plate is like a low ceiling: a tall part next to the pad is a pillar that blocks it. Putting all spaces on one level, the bottom, keeps the garage simple.

### Words you need
- **Pad**: a bare copper circle with the solder mask opened, about 1 mm across.
- **Pitch**: the center-to-center spacing between neighboring pads.
- **Keep-out**: space around a pad or tall part where nothing else may sit.
- **Tooling holes**: holes that align the board to the fixture.
- **Stub**: the short spur of copper joining a pad to its net.
- **Ground pad**: a pad on the ground net near signal pads, for a return connection. See [[q0618]].

### The simplest example
A typical rule set: pads about 1 mm wide, centers at least 1.27 mm apart, so the gap between neighboring pads is at least 0.27 mm. Keep about 1 mm away from small parts, more from tall ones, and about 3 mm from the board edge. Put every pad on the bottom and add two tooling holes at asymmetric spots. Connect each pad straight onto its net with no stub. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the reason for each number, how to treat fast nets, and how to count and document the points.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can turn "add test points" into **concrete layout rules** that a fixture, a flying-probe machine and a human with a scope can all use. Interviewers listen for: pad size and finish, pitch and spacing, keep-outs from components and edges, one-sided placement, mask openings, labelling, ground pads nearby, and signal-integrity care on fast nets. They want mechanisms (probe tip size, probe force, alignment tolerance), not a recited list.
:::

::: why
A test point that the probe can't land on is worse than none: it costs area and gives false failures. Fixture probes have finite tip diameter, alignment tolerance and spring force. A pad too small, too close to a tall part, covered with mask, or on the wrong side will give missed contacts or damaged boards. Getting layout rules right at design time avoids a fixture rework or a respin. See [[q0614]] for the factory economics.
:::

::: concepts
- **Fixture probes**: spring-loaded pins with a tip and a tolerance; their size sets pad and pitch. See [[q0612]].
- **Tooling holes and alignment**: the board registers on holes; error adds to pad requirements.
- **Solder mask opening**: pad must be bare.
- **Keep-out**: space around tall parts so the fixture plate can sit flat.
- **Ground return near the signal pad**: see [[q0618]].
- **Signal integrity**: stub length and capacitance. See [[q0623]].
- **DFM overlap**: see [[q0613]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd group the rules by what the probe needs.

Pad: a bare copper pad, mask opened, about 1 mm in diameter or larger, with a finish that survives probing, and not covered by silkscreen. Smaller works with finer probes but alignment gets tight.

Spacing: pads on a grid with at least about 1.27 mm centre-to-centre, and wider if the fixture vendor wants 2.54 mm for sturdier probes. Keep them away from tall components, typically a millimetre or more from the part body, and from the board edge, so the fixture plate and the probe sit flat.

Side: put them all on one side, usually the bottom, because a two-sided fixture is more expensive and slower. Include two tooling holes at asymmetric positions.

Connection: connect each pad directly to its net with a short trace, ideally on the net itself so there's no stub. On a fast net, make the pad the net's trace, in-line, or use a very short stub.

Ground: put ground pads near signal pads and spread several around the board.

And label them on the silkscreen, away from the pad, and make sure no vias or components sit under the probe area. Then I'd check against the factory's DFT guideline."
:::

::: technical
**Pad geometry and finish.**

- Diameter about 0.9–1.0 mm (35–40 mil) or larger is common for standard probes; smaller (down to about 0.6–0.75 mm) with finer probes and tighter alignment (fixture vendor dependent).
- Mask opening equal to or slightly larger than the copper so mask doesn't overlap the pad.
- Finish: flat and durable (ENIG is common); HASL can be uneven; OSP wears. Confirm with the factory.
- No silkscreen over the pad and no via-in-pad plugged with a dimple.

**Spacing.**

| Rule | Typical | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Pad-to-pad pitch | ≥ 1.27 mm (50 mil); 2.54 mm (100 mil) for robust probes | probe body diameter and plate density |
| Pad-to-component | ≥ about 1 mm from small parts; more from tall parts | probe/plate clearance; vacuum seal |
| Pad-to-board edge | ≥ about 3 mm (vendor dependent) | fixture clamps, edge rails |
| Tall components | keep-out proportional to height (fixture-vendor rule, e.g. 1 mm per few mm height) | the plate sits near the board |
| Tooling holes | 2+, asymmetric, 3.2 mm typical | registration |

**Sides.** One-sided access (bottom). Components on the probed side must clear the plate; ideally keep the bottom low-profile.

**Connectivity.**

- Use a **short trace or direct net connection** (net "T-in" at the pad) with the pad *on* the net where possible so there's no stub.
- Don't place a test pad on a tented/plugged via. If using a via, ensure it's exposed and unplugged.
- Don't place over planes splits; keep the return path continuous.
- On fast nets keep the stub short: for a 100 ps edge, anything beyond a few mm adds measurable reflection; capacitance of the pad adds more. See [[q0623]].

**Ground.** Several ground pads, one near each cluster of signal pads, connected to the plane with vias. See [[q0618]].

**Quantity and density.** Prefer fewer, well-chosen points: 1 probe per net (no duplicates for production test), spread them so no region of the fixture is overloaded, and keep the total within the fixture's pin count budget. Count affects fixture cost.

**Documentation.** Silkscreen the TP number near (not on) the pad; export a test-point list with coordinates and net names for the fixture.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> All numeric rules above are typical values; fixture vendors and factories publish their own, which override these. Probe tip choice (crown, spear, flat) also changes pad requirements.
:::

::: short
"Make each test point a bare, flat pad of about a millimetre or more, on one side of the board, on a grid with at least about 1.27 mm spacing, clear of tall parts and the edge, with tooling holes for alignment. Connect it in-line to the net with minimal stub, put ground pads nearby, label it on the silkscreen, and check against the factory's DFT rules. On fast nets, I keep the stub tiny or skip the pad."
:::

::: long
"I think of the guidelines as answering three questions: can the probe land on it, can the fixture sit down, and does the pad hurt the circuit?

For landing: the pad is bare copper, solder mask opened, about a millimetre or more across, with a flat durable finish, no silkscreen on it and no unplugged via under it. Larger is more forgiving of the fixture's alignment error.

For the fixture: I put all the test points on one side, usually the bottom, spaced at least about 1.27 millimetres between centres, or 2.54 if the vendor wants sturdy probes. I keep them about a millimetre or more away from components and further from tall ones, so the probe plate can sit flat and the vacuum seals, and away from the board edge. There are at least two tooling holes in asymmetric positions so the board registers the same way every time. And I spread points out so no one area has too many pins.

For the circuit: the pad should connect straight to the net with a short stub or be in-line with the trace, so there's minimal added capacitance and stub on fast nets. On power rails it doesn't matter, but on a fast edge a pad can reflect measurably, so for the fastest nets I use a tiny in-line pad or skip it. I add ground pads near the signal pads, connected with vias to the plane, so a scope's ground can be short.

Finally I label each point on the silkscreen next to the pad, not on it, export the coordinate list for the test engineers, and review against their guideline."
:::

::: terms
- **Test pad**: bare copper pad intended for probing.
- **Pitch**: centre-to-centre distance between pads.
- **Keep-out / keepout zone**: area where components or features are not allowed.
- **Tooling hole**: precise hole used to register the board in fixtures.
- **Mask opening**: region without solder mask exposing copper.
- **Probe tip styles**: crown, spear, flat, concave; different pad and contact needs.
- **Stub**: dead-end trace branch.
- **Vacuum fixture**: fixture that pulls the board down onto probes with vacuum.
- **Test point list (TP list)**: coordinates and nets for the fixture builder.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "make them big enough and spaced out".

**An exceptional answer**:

- Gives specific typical values (about 1 mm pads, ≥ 1.27 mm pitch, ~1 mm clearance) and explains the mechanism: tip size, alignment tolerance, plate and vacuum.
- Insists on one-sided, bare, flat, finished pads and no tented/plugged vias.
- Handles signal integrity: in-line pads and short stubs on fast nets, and ground pads near signal pads.
- Includes labelling, tooling holes and a TP list for the fixture builder.
- States that the factory's DFT rules override typical numbers and recommends consulting them early.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Pads covered by solder mask or silkscreen.** The probe contacts insulation.
- **Pads placed next to tall connectors or capacitors.** The fixture plate can't reach.
- **Test pads on both sides "just in case".** Doubles fixture cost.
- **Long stubs to the pad.** On fast nets they ring; even on slow ones they invite coupling.
- **Pitch tighter than the probe diameter allows.** Probes can't be placed; vendors will refuse or use expensive small probes.
- **Test points on a via filled with a dimple.** The surface isn't flat so the probe misses.
- **Not including a pad for ground, or only a single far ground pad.** See [[q0618]].
:::

## Think like the probe

A spring probe has a tip diameter, a barrel (receptacle) diameter much larger than the tip, a spring force, and a tolerance on where it lands. The fixture's drill precision plus board tooling tolerance plus board fab tolerance add up to an alignment budget.

## Pad size and alignment

The probe tip needs to land on copper after all tolerances. Suppose the tooling and fixture add up to ±0.15 mm of positional error. A pad of 1.0 mm diameter with a probe tip of 0.5 mm leaves a margin of $(1.0 - 0.5)/2 = 0.25$ mm > 0.15 mm: good. A 0.6 mm pad with the same tip leaves 0.05 mm < 0.15 mm: probe may miss or land on the mask edge. So size follows from tolerance:

$$d_{pad} \gtrsim d_{tip} + 2\,\varepsilon_{align}$$

With $d_{tip}=0.5$ mm and $\varepsilon=0.15$ mm: $d_{pad} \gtrsim 0.8$ mm; add margin and you get the "about 1 mm" rule. Check the vendor for real tolerances.

## Pitch and probe density

A standard probe receptacle is often 1.0–1.7 mm in diameter (for a 100 mil / 75 mil / 50 mil probe family), so the pitch cannot be smaller than the receptacle plus wall. That gives: 100 mil (2.54 mm) probes for the most robust and cheapest fixtures, 75 mil (1.9 mm) for medium density, and 50 mil (1.27 mm) for dense boards at higher cost. When asked, say "I'd start with the factory's preference; closer pitches cost more".

## Keep-outs

The probe plate sits a few millimetres from the board's surface when pressed. Tall components on the same side as probes either need holes in the plate (cost) or must be avoided near pads. Typical rule: the keep-out around a component is proportional to its height. The fixture vendor gives the exact rule. Also keep pads from the board edge, because the board is held by clamps or vacuum seals and edge rails.

## Signal integrity: the pad is part of the net

Make the pad **on the net** rather than at the end of a stub. A 1 mm round pad has a parallel-plate capacitance of 0.15–0.3 pF over a close plane. A 5 mm stub of 0.3 mm trace over a plane 0.2 mm away adds about 0.5 pF (≈ 0.1 pF/mm) and about 1.6 nH (≈ 0.3 nH/mm; the familiar 1 nH/mm rule is for wires far from their return, not traces over a plane). Once the stub's round-trip delay (≈ 6 ps/mm each way) approaches the edge time, it behaves as a resonant line rather than a lumped capacitor. For a 50 Ω line:

- 0.5 pF with 100 ps edge reflects about 12.5%
- 0.3 pF with 100 ps edge reflects about 7.5%
- 1 pF with 1 ns edge reflects about 2.5%

(using peak reflection = (τ/tr)(1−e^{−tr/τ}), τ = Z0C/2; see [[q0623]]).

Also consider **return path**: don't route a signal TP trace across a plane split; keep reference continuity.

## Grounds and returns

Spread ground pads across the board and put one near each cluster of signal test points. Connect them with vias to the plane. For fixtures the ground probes also carry return current for power-up tests, so give them solid copper and multiple vias. See [[q0618]].

## Vias, mask, finish and labels

- **Mask**: openings the pad size or slightly larger (e.g. +0.05 mm); no mask on the pad.
- **Via-in-pad**: avoid unless filled and capped flat; a dimple causes misses.
- **Finish**: ENIG or similar flat finish; HASL bumpy.
- **Silkscreen**: label beside the pad ("TP12 3V3"), never on it.
- **Data**: produce a TP list (net, X, Y, side) and fixture-ready data (ODB++/Gerber, drill) for the fixture builder.

## Review checklist

1. Every required net has a pad (see [[q0625]]).
2. All pads on one side.
3. Pad size, pitch and keep-outs meet the factory's DFT sheet.
4. Pads are bare and flat; no via dimples.
5. Fast nets have in-line or minimal-stub pads.
6. Ground pads near signal pads; wide spread.
7. Two tooling holes, non-symmetric.
8. Labels present; TP list exported.

::: applications
- **Contract-manufactured consumer boards** with a bottom-side pad field for a bed-of-nails fixture.
- **Server and storage boards**: pads on rails and resets, with high-speed lanes verified by built-in margining instead of pads.
- **Automotive modules**: test pads in a connector-adjacent area for end-of-line testers.
- **Power converters**: pads on input, output, switch node (carefully) and feedback with adjacent grounds.
- **Wearables**: tiny bottom-side pad arrays for pogo-pin fixtures.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes a bed-of-nails or flying probe contact method; for pure hand probing, rules are looser.
- Dimensions are typical; they depend on fixture vendor, probe size and board thickness.
- Fast-net reflection numbers assume a lumped capacitance on a 50 Ω line and ideal ramps.
- Alternate guidance exists for BGA-heavy boards: via-based pads under the BGA fanout or boundary scan instead of pads.
- Rules shift for flex and rigid-flex boards (stiffeners under probe areas).
:::

::: followups
### How large should a test pad be and why?
About 1 mm: large enough to absorb fixture alignment errors (probe tip plus misalignment must land on copper). Smaller pads work with finer probes and tighter tolerance at higher cost. Compute margin as (pad − tip)/2 minus alignment error.

### Why one side only?
A two-sided fixture needs a second probe plate, a more complex press and longer loading and costs more. Probing from one side keeps the fixture and handling simple. Put the pads and low-profile parts on that side.

### Can I use a via as a test pad?
Yes if it's exposed and unplugged, or filled and capped flat. Tented or dimpled vias cause missed contacts. State it in the fab notes.

### What spacing do you use between test pads?
At least about 1.27 mm for dense boards and 2.54 mm for standard probes, set by the factory. Closer pitch needs smaller probes that wear faster and cost more.

### Scenario: the fixture vendor says your test pads are too close to a tall electrolytic capacitor. What are your options?
Move the pads outside the keep-out (the vendor gives distance based on component height), or move the capacitor, or make the fixture plate with a cutout (extra cost), or move the test points for those nets to the other side of the component or to a connector pin. I'd choose by cost: moving pads in layout is cheapest.
:::
