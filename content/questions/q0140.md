::: testing
Whether you can look past the textbook "R, L or C" menu and describe a load by how it **behaves electrically**. The interviewer wants to hear that a SoC is not a resistor you can look up in a table. It is a time-varying, mostly capacitive CMOS load that acts like a **current sink** whose demand changes in nanoseconds. Hidden sub-skills: knowing where the power goes ($CV^2f$), knowing that the inductance in the path belongs to the *package and PCB*, not the die, and connecting all this to why the supply needs decoupling and a low impedance target.
:::

::: why
Every power-delivery decision for a processor starts with this model. If you treat the CPU as a resistor, you will under-design the decoupling and the regulator's transient response, and the chip will brown out or fail timing when the workload changes. If you treat it as a **dynamic current source with a few picofarads to nanofarads of switched capacitance**, you get the right questions: how fast can it step current, how much voltage droop can it take, and what supply impedance do I need? It also explains why the answer "capacitive" is half right and why interviewers like the follow-up "so what does the regulator see?"
:::

::: concepts
- **CMOS switching energy**: each logic transition charges or discharges a load capacitance, so dynamic power is $P = \alpha C_{sw} V^2 f$. See [[q0105]] and [[q0106]].
- **Capacitance**: charge storage, $i = C\,dv/dt$. The die has gate, wire and intrinsic decoupling capacitance. See [[q0022]].
- **Current source vs resistor**: a resistor draws current in proportion to the voltage across it; a CPU draws current set by the workload and clock, mostly independent of small changes in $V$.
- **Leakage**: transistors that are "off" still conduct. Leakage grows exponentially with temperature and with lower threshold voltage.
- **Parasitic inductance**: bond wires, bumps, package planes and PCB vias add series $L$ between the regulator and the die. See [[q0509]].
- **Impedance target**: $Z_{target} = \Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I_{step}$ sets how low the supply impedance must be. See [[q0142]].
:::

::: answer
"If I had to pick one word, it's capacitive, but I'd add that the better description is a time-varying current sink. The transistors are CMOS gates, so almost all of the dynamic power goes into charging and discharging capacitance every clock cycle. That's the $\alpha C V^2 f$ formula. So there's a lot of switched capacitance in there, plus a lot of on-die decoupling capacitance that helps it.

On average you can say it looks resistive. A core at 0.9 V pulling 54 A looks like about 17 milliohms at DC. But that number isn't a real resistor. If the workload goes idle, the current drops to leakage in microseconds, the voltage hasn't changed, so the 'resistance' just jumped. It acts like a current source that the software controls.

It's not really inductive. The inductance you see in the power path belongs to the package, the vias and the board, and it sits between the regulator and the chip. That inductance matters because when current steps by tens of amps in a few nanoseconds, $L\,di/dt$ gives you droop.

So the regulator and the decoupling network have to treat the SoC as a fast, large current step with a tight voltage tolerance, and design the supply impedance to match that."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** A digital SoC is a large array of CMOS gates. Averaged over many cycles, the supply current has three parts:

$$I_{DD}(t) \approx \underbrace{\alpha\, C_{sw}\, V_{DD}\, f}_{\text{dynamic}} + \underbrace{I_{sc}}_{\text{short-circuit}} + \underbrace{I_{leak}(V_{DD},T)}_{\text{leakage}}$$

- $\alpha$ is the activity factor, $C_{sw}$ the total switched capacitance, $f$ the clock frequency. Dynamic power is $P_{dyn} = \alpha C_{sw}V_{DD}^2 f$.
- Leakage grows roughly exponentially with temperature and with lower $V_{th}$. In advanced nodes it can be a large fraction of total power.

**Electrical behaviour.**
- *DC / average*: current is roughly proportional to $V_{DD}$ at fixed $f$ (because $P \propto V^2$), so the incremental resistance $dV/dI$ is positive but small, and the "effective resistance" $V/I$ is milliohms to ohms.
- *Fast transients*: current steps (clock gating, cache misses resolving, vector unit wake-up) can be tens of amps in tens of nanoseconds. The load is better modelled as an ideal current source $I(t)$ in parallel with the on-die capacitance.
- *Regulated-load view*: a CPU with DVFS is not a constant-power load. Only the *downstream converter* feeding a fixed-power load shows negative incremental resistance.

**Inductance and resistance in the path.** Package bumps, planes, BGA balls, vias and board planes add series $R$ and $L$ (tens of pH to nH). They are part of the **power delivery network**, not of the load. Worst-case droop is roughly $\Delta V \approx \Delta I\cdot Z_{PDN}$ at the frequency where the step has energy.

**Design target.** $Z_{target} = \dfrac{\Delta V_{allowed}}{\Delta I_{step}}$. For 0.9 V, 3% tolerance and a 30 A step, $Z_{target} = 27\text{ mV}/30\text{ A} = 0.9\text{ mΩ}$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 20 nF effective switched capacitance, 3 GHz clock and 0.9 V used in the examples are illustrative numbers, not any specific product. Real SoCs differ widely, and vendors publish transient and decoupling guidance for each part.
:::

::: short
"A SoC is best described as a capacitive, time-varying current sink. It's CMOS, so power goes into charging and discharging capacitance every cycle: $\alpha C V^2 f$. On average it looks resistive, like 17 milliohms for a 54 amp core, but that's just V over I, and it changes with the workload. It isn't really inductive. The inductance is in the package and board, and with fast current steps it causes droop, which is why the supply needs low impedance and lots of decoupling."
:::

::: long
"The short answer is capacitive, but I'd describe it as a dynamic current sink rather than picking one passive element.

It's built from CMOS gates. Every time a gate switches it charges or discharges a capacitance, so dynamic power is activity factor times switched capacitance times V squared times frequency. On top of that there's leakage, which depends on temperature and threshold voltage. So the current the chip pulls is set by what the software is doing and what the clock is, much more than by the voltage across it.

If I average over time I can force a resistive model on it. Take a core at 0.9 volts burning about 49 watts. That's roughly 54 amps, so about 17 milliohms. That's useful for a back-of-envelope IR drop calculation. But it's not a real resistor. When the workload stops, the current falls to leakage in microseconds without the voltage changing.

On a fast timescale the thing to model is a current source in parallel with the on-die capacitance, which helps absorb the first nanoseconds of a step. After that the current has to come from decoupling capacitors, then through the package and board inductance, and eventually from the regulator.

Is it inductive? Not the die. The inductance is in the package bumps, planes, vias and the board. That's what makes $L\,di/dt$ droop when the load steps 30 amps in 10 nanoseconds.

So the design takeaway is: define the allowed voltage ripple, divide by the current step, and get a target impedance. For 27 millivolts and 30 amps that's about a milliohm. Then you build a decoupling network, from bulk down to on-die capacitance, to keep the impedance under that line across frequency."
:::

::: terms
- **Switched capacitance ($C_{sw}$)**: effective capacitance charged and discharged per clock cycle, weighted by activity.
- **Dynamic power**: $\alpha C_{sw}V_{DD}^2 f$, the power spent charging and discharging gate and wire capacitance.
- **Leakage current**: current through transistors that are nominally off; grows with temperature.
- **Current sink / current-source load**: a load whose current is set by its own activity, not by Ohm's law across a fixed resistance.
- **di/dt**: rate of change of load current; with series inductance it produces $L\,di/dt$ droop.
- **PDN (power delivery network)**: the path from regulator through board, package and die that delivers current.
- **Target impedance**: $\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I_{step}$, the PDN impedance ceiling.
- **DVFS**: dynamic voltage and frequency scaling, which changes the load's operating point.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "capacitive" because of CMOS gate capacitance, and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Says **capacitive overall, but behaves like a time-varying current sink**, and explains why with $P = \alpha C V^2 f$.
- Gives a numeric feel: 0.9 V and 54 A is 17 mΩ average, and explains why that "resistance" is misleading.
- Separates the **load** (die) from the **path** (package/board $R$ and $L$) and says the inductance is not the load's.
- Mentions leakage (temperature-dependent) and DVFS as reasons current is not set by Ohm's law.
- Finishes with the consequence: a current step times PDN impedance gives droop, so you set $Z_{target}$ and build the decoupling hierarchy.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"It's a resistor."** At DC you can quote $V/I$, but the current is set by the workload. Correction: it is a current sink with a positive but small incremental resistance.
- **"It's inductive because it has big currents."** Inductance comes from the loop geometry of the package and board. The die's gates are capacitive. Correction: the series $L$ is in the *path* and causes droop.
- **Calling it a constant-power load.** That's the view from a converter that regulates its own output into a fixed power draw (with a negative incremental resistance). A CPU's power changes with $V^2 f$ and DVFS.
- **Ignoring leakage.** At hot corners leakage can be a large fraction of total power. Correction: model it as a temperature-dependent current in parallel with the dynamic current.
- **Forgetting that the load is not static.** The answer should always mention step changes and the time scale (nanoseconds to microseconds).
:::

::: deep
## What the die is made of

A modern SoC is billions of CMOS gates (see [[q0105]]). A CMOS inverter draws current from the supply only when its output switches (and when both transistors briefly conduct). The charge that moves per transition is $Q = C_L V_{DD}$. Multiply by the number of transitions per second and you get an average current:

$$I_{dyn} = \alpha\, C_{sw}\, V_{DD}\, f \quad\Rightarrow\quad P_{dyn} = V_{DD}I_{dyn} = \alpha C_{sw} V_{DD}^2 f$$

Here $C_{sw}$ is the total capacitance of everything that could switch and $\alpha$ is the fraction that actually does each cycle, so the product $\alpha C_{sw}$ is the **effective switched capacitance**. Using illustrative numbers, $\alpha C_{sw} = 20$ nF, $V_{DD} = 0.9$ V and $f = 3$ GHz:

$$P = 20\text{ nF}\times 0.81\text{ V}^2 \times 3\text{ GHz} = 48.6\text{ W},\quad I = 54\text{ A}.$$

At DC the ratio $V/I = 0.9/54 = 16.7$ mΩ. That is the "effective resistance" the regulator sees on average.

## Why the resistor model fails

An actual resistor of 16.7 mΩ would draw $I = V/R$. If the rail rises 10% from 0.9 to 0.99 V, the resistor's current rises 10%. For a CPU at fixed clock, current also rises roughly 10% (since $I\propto V f$), so on a slow timescale it does look like a resistor with a 17 mΩ slope. But on every faster timescale the picture breaks:

1. **Workload changes the current.** If the core goes idle (clock gated, or waiting on memory), current drops from 54 A to a few amps in nanoseconds. A resistor cannot change its value like that.
2. **Voltage changes the speed.** If $V_{DD}$ droops, gates slow down. The alpha-power law gives delay $\propto V_{DD}/(V_{DD}-V_{th})^{\alpha}$.
3. **Temperature changes leakage.** Leakage can double every 10-20 °C or so in some regimes, so the "resistance" changes with how hot the chip is.

So the right abstraction is: **a current source controlled by the workload, with a mild dependence on voltage and temperature**, in parallel with the die's own capacitance.

## The inductive part is in the path

Think about what happens at a 30 A step in 10 ns. The rate is $di/dt = 3\times10^9$ A/s. Through a loop inductance of 20 pH (a good, tight design) you get

$$\Delta V = L\frac{di}{dt} = 20\text{ pH}\times 3\times10^{9}\text{ A/s} = 60\text{ mV}.$$

With the rail at 0.9 V, that is over 6% droop, which is already too much for a typical 3-5% tolerance. That is why PDN design talks about picohenries. The die itself isn't inductive. The package, the BGA balls and the board are.

## Putting numbers on the impedance target

For a core rail with 3% tolerance at 0.9 V and a 30 A step:

$$Z_{target} = \frac{0.03\times 0.9\text{ V}}{30\text{ A}} = 0.9\text{ mΩ}.$$

The regulator can only provide low impedance below its loop bandwidth (hundreds of kHz). Above that, bulk capacitors help to a few MHz, ceramic capacitors to tens of MHz, and package and on-die capacitance above that. See [[q0142]] for why this requires many capacitors of different values.

## Real hardware notes

- **Clock gating and power gating** cause the biggest steps: a wake-up can add many amps in nanoseconds. Designers add ramp control so the step is spread over hundreds of nanoseconds.
- **Voltage regulators designed for CPUs** (multiphase bucks, see [[q0156]] for the family) use fast control loops, feed-forward and "adaptive voltage positioning" (a deliberate load line, droop $V = V_0 - R_{LL}I$) so that the full-load voltage sits lower and the transient overshoot is small.
- **IR drop** in the on-die grid, package and board is a DC effect: $I\times R$ along the path. At 54 A, a 0.3 mΩ path resistance already costs 16 mV.
- **Leakage** makes the idle load nonzero and temperature-dependent, so a "cold" bench test underestimates hot-silicon draw.

## Worked example: how much capacitance and how little inductance?

Take the 0.9 V core with a 30 A step and a 27 mV (3%) droop budget. If a source of capacitance $C$ has to supply the whole step by itself for a time $t$ before the next stage of the supply can respond, charge balance gives $C=I\,t/\Delta V$:

| Time the step must be held | Capacitance needed |
|---|---|
| 1 ns | 1.1 µF |
| 10 ns | 11 µF |
| 100 ns | 111 µF |
| 1 µs | 1.1 mF |

Each decade of time needs a decade more capacitance, which is exactly why a decoupling network has stages: on-die capacitance (hundreds of nF) and the package cover the first nanosecond, tens of microfarads of ceramics cover tens of nanoseconds, bulk capacitors cover the microsecond range, and the regulator takes over beyond its loop bandwidth. The 100 nF of on-die capacitance in the figure above, by itself, can hold a 30 A step for only $27\text{ mV}\times100\text{ nF}/30\text{ A}=90$ ps, so it is a first responder, not a solution.

Capacitance is necessary but not sufficient, because the current also has to *get there*. A ceramic capacitor mounted with a 500 pH loop inductance can change its current no faster than $\Delta V/L=27\text{ mV}/500\text{ pH}=5.4\times10^7$ A/s, so ramping 30 A would take 556 ns. Spread the same current over 20 such capacitors in parallel (the effective inductance falls to 25 pH) and the limit rises to $1.1\times10^9$ A/s, a 28 ns ramp. That is the reason PDNs use dozens of small capacitors close to the package, and why layout (loop area, via placement) matters as much as the capacitor values. Both requirements, enough charge and low enough inductance, must be met at every time scale, which is what a target-impedance plot summarizes.

## Limiting cases of the load model

- **Slow workload changes (milliseconds and longer).** The regulator's loop follows easily, and the load can be treated as a DC resistance for IR drop: at 54 A, each 0.1 mΩ of path resistance costs 5.4 mV.
- **Very fast steps (below a nanosecond).** Only on-die capacitance and the local grid can respond, and the die's own distributed resistance and inductance matter. Package-level models are no longer enough; the vendor's on-die model is needed.
- **Idle.** The load collapses to leakage current plus a small clock-tree component. A regulator designed for 50 A may need a light-load mode to avoid wasting power, and the decoupling network no longer sees a large step, only small ripple.
- **Hot silicon.** Leakage rises exponentially, so a part that passes on a cold bench can droop or throttle when warm, because both the DC current and the thermal feedback increase.

## Verifying the load model on the bench

The most direct measurement is the supply droop during a real or synthetic load step. Use a short coaxial or spring-ground connection at the rail test point nearest the package, set the scope to DC coupling with a vertical scale near 20 mV/div and an offset equal to the rail voltage, and trigger single-shot on the load event. Two traps: the scope's 20 MHz bandwidth-limit filter hides the nanosecond droop, so turn it off; and a long probe ground lead adds inductance that creates artificial ringing. To compare with the target impedance, inject a known current step with an electronic load or a software workload and compare the measured $\Delta V/\Delta I$ with $Z_{target}$. A vector network analyzer with a low-impedance two-port shunt-through method can measure the PDN impedance itself, which should stay under the target across the frequency band. If the measured droop exceeds the budget, the shape of the voltage waveform tells you which stage is short: a fast initial dip points at too little high-frequency capacitance or too much loop inductance, and a slower sag points at too little bulk capacitance or a slow regulator.

::: applications
- **Application processors in phones**: a PMIC supplies the core rail, and the DVFS governor changes both voltage and frequency; the PMIC must be designed for fast load steps.
- **Server CPUs and GPUs**: multiphase buck regulators with tens of phases, sub-milliohm target impedance, and load-line (droop) control.
- **FPGAs**: vendors publish decoupling recommendations per rail because switching activity depends on the design loaded.
- **Microcontrollers**: a Cortex-M core in sleep versus run mode swings the supply current by orders of magnitude, which is why the LDO or buck feeding it must be stable and efficient at both extremes.
- **Power-integrity sign-off**: engineers measure or simulate the PDN impedance against the target before releasing a board.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Digital CMOS-dominated SoC.** Chips with significant analog, RF or I/O content have additional loads (PLLs, PHYs, drivers) that need separate, cleaner rails.
- **Time scale matters.** At DC it behaves like a modest positive resistance; at nanoseconds like a current source plus capacitance; at microseconds the regulator loop is involved.
- **Linear small-signal view.** For large steps or large droops, the nonlinearity (delay vs $V_{DD}$, leakage vs $T$) matters.
- **Alternative valid answer.** Some interviewers say "mostly capacitive, with resistive characteristics at DC", or "a dynamic load / current sink". All are fine if you explain *why*. Calling it inductive is the only answer that is clearly wrong for the die itself.
- If the interviewer asks about the **converter feeding** the SoC, a regulated converter with constant-power output looks like a negative incremental resistance at its input, which is a stability issue for its own input filter. That is a different question from the CPU's own behaviour.
:::

::: followups
### Why isn't the SoC a constant-power load?
A constant-power load draws $I = P/V$, so current rises when voltage falls. A CPU at fixed clock does the opposite: $P\propto V^2$, so current falls slightly as $V$ falls. Constant-power behaviour belongs to a regulated converter downstream of a source, whose input draws more current as its input voltage drops. A processor with a closed-loop DVFS governor can be made to look different again, but it is not an inherently constant-power device.

### What happens on the rail when the CPU goes from idle to full load in 10 ns?
Current steps up by, say, 30 A. The on-die capacitance and nearest ceramics supply the first nanoseconds, so the rail dips first by $L\,di/dt$ (60 mV for 20 pH), then by the charge drained from bulk caps, before the regulator loop responds. The result is a droop (undershoot) and, when the load drops, overshoot. This is why the PDN has to be low impedance across a wide frequency band. See [[q0151]].

### Estimate the average resistance of a core running 1.0 V at 10 W.
$I = P/V = 10$ A, so $R_{eff} = V/I = 100$ mΩ. I'd stress that this is only an average, useful for IR-drop estimates.

### Why does the package matter if the load is capacitive?
Because the capacitance is on the die side of the package inductance. The first charge for a step must cross the package, so loop inductance is the thing that limits how fast you can feed the die from outside capacitors. That's why smaller, closer, lower-inductance capacitors and on-die decap matter.

### Scenario: your SoC randomly resets only when a particular app starts. How do you investigate?
I'd suspect a large current step causing droop below the brown-out or timing margin. I'd probe the rail at the package (or the closest test point) with a short ground spring and look at the minimum voltage during the event with the scope in single-shot mode, comparing with the spec. If it dips, I'd check the regulator's transient response, the decoupling population (missing or mis-valued capacitors), and whether the software can ramp the load gradually.
:::

The textbook answer is "predominantly capacitive (CMOS gates), with resistive behaviour on average". The engineering answer is "a dynamic, workload-controlled current sink". For duplicates of the decoupling theme, see [[q0142]] (power rail view) and [[q0506]] (PCB view).
