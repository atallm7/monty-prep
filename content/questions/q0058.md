::: basics
### In plain words
A dielectric is an insulator that sits between the plates of a capacitor, or inside a circuit-board laminate. An ideal one stores energy and gives it all back. A real one wastes a little as heat. That waste is dielectric loss. It comes from small leakage current, and mostly from charges inside the material that cannot keep up when the field flips back and forth.

### Picture it
Picture pushing a child on a swing through thick mud. A bit of the effort is stored in the swing's motion, but some is lost to friction in the mud every cycle. A dielectric is similar: the field twists molecules back and forth, and they rub against their neighbors, producing heat. Faster flipping means more twists per second. The analogy breaks down at very high frequency, where molecules cannot follow at all.

### Words you need
- **Dielectric**: an insulating material that stores energy in an electric field.
- **Polarization**: charges in the material shifting or turning in response to a field.
- **Leakage current**: a tiny current that flows through a nonperfect insulator.
- **Loss tangent ($\tan\delta$)**: the loss as a fraction of the stored energy. Smaller is better.
- **Parasitic**: an unwanted extra effect in a real part. See [[q0055]] for the materials side.

### The simplest example
Put 10 V at 1 kHz across a 1 µF capacitor. The reactive power is $V^2\omega C = 100 \times 6.28 \times 10^{-3} \approx 0.63$ VA. If the loss tangent is 0.01, the heat is 1% of that, about 6 mW. With a poorer dielectric at 0.1, it is about 63 mW. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the separate loss mechanisms, which are conduction, polarization lag and ferroelectric switching, and how to choose parts that stay cool.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know that an insulator in an **AC field is not lossless**, and can explain **where the energy goes** at the molecular level. The interviewer is listening for:

- The main mechanisms: **leakage conduction**, **polarization lag** (dipoles and ions can't follow the field instantly), interfacial polarization in mixed materials, **ferroelectric hysteresis** in high-κ ceramics, and **partial discharge** at high voltage.
- The engineering metric: **loss tangent** $\tan\delta$ (dissipation factor, Df), and how it turns into power $P = V_{rms}^2\,\omega C\tan\delta$ or ESR.
- Where it matters: capacitor heating (C0G vs X7R vs film), PCB laminate choice for high-speed links, microwave heating.
:::

::: why
Dielectric loss decides real design choices. A resonant converter or snubber capacitor can overheat if you pick a high-loss ceramic. A 10+ Gb/s SerDes channel on standard FR-4 can lose several dB to the laminate, which is why boards use low-Df materials like Rogers or Megtron. High-voltage insulation fails through dielectric heating and partial discharge. Even your microwave oven is a dielectric-loss machine. Being able to explain the mechanism and run the $\tan\delta$ numbers shows you understand capacitors and materials as more than ideal symbols.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitance and displacement current**: an ideal capacitor's current leads voltage by exactly 90°, so it stores and returns energy with zero average power. See [[q0022]].
- **Polarization**: an applied field shifts electron clouds, stretches ionic bonds and rotates polar molecules, storing energy; that's what raises εr above 1.
- **Complex permittivity** $\varepsilon = \varepsilon' - j\varepsilon''$: $\varepsilon'$ is storage, $\varepsilon''$ is loss.
- **Loss tangent / dissipation factor**: $\tan\delta = \varepsilon''/\varepsilon'$, the ratio of energy lost to energy stored (per radian).
- **Relaxation time** $\tau$: how fast a polarization mechanism can respond; loss peaks when the field period is comparable to $\tau$.
- **ESR**: lumping loss into a series resistor, $ESR = \tan\delta/(\omega C)$. See [[q0050]] for the inductor analogue.
- **Insulator band picture**: a dielectric has almost no free carriers, so its loss comes mostly from bound charges, not conduction. See [[q0055]].
:::

::: answer
"An ideal dielectric just stores energy: the field polarizes it, and when the field reverses you get that energy back. Real dielectrics turn some of it into heat, and there are a few ways that happens.

The first is plain conduction. No insulator is perfect, so a tiny leakage current flows and you get $I^2R$ loss. That dominates at DC and low frequency.

The bigger one at AC is polarization loss. When you apply a field, polar molecules rotate, ions shift and electron clouds distort. If the field alternates, those charges have to keep reorienting, and they can't follow instantly. They lag behind the field and lose energy to friction with neighbouring molecules, which shows up as heat. The loss peaks when the frequency is around one over the material's relaxation time. A microwave oven heats water exactly this way.

In high-κ ceramics like X7R there's also hysteresis from ferroelectric domains switching, and at high voltage you can get partial discharge in tiny voids.

We quantify all of it with the loss tangent, $\tan\delta$. Dissipated power is $V^2\omega C\tan\delta$, so it matters for capacitors with large AC voltage and for PCB laminates at gigahertz, which is why you'd pick C0G or film caps, and low-loss laminates for high-speed links."
:::

::: technical
**Complex permittivity.** For a sinusoidal field, write $\varepsilon_r = \varepsilon' - j\varepsilon''$. A capacitor $C = \varepsilon_r C_0$ then has admittance
$$Y = j\omega\varepsilon_r C_0 = \omega\varepsilon'' C_0 + j\omega\varepsilon' C_0$$
The real part is a conductance: power is dissipated. The current leads voltage by $90° - \delta$, with
$$\tan\delta = \frac{\varepsilon''}{\varepsilon'}\quad(\text{a.k.a. dissipation factor DF, or Df for laminates});\qquad Q = 1/\tan\delta$$

**Power.** For $V_{rms}$ at angular frequency $\omega$:
$$P = V_{rms}^2\,\omega C\tan\delta,\qquad \text{power density } p = \omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon'' E_{rms}^2$$
Equivalent circuit: $R_p = 1/(\omega C\tan\delta)$ in parallel, or $ESR = \tan\delta/(\omega C)$ in series (for small δ).

**Mechanisms.**
1. **Conduction (leakage)**: $\varepsilon''_{cond} = \sigma/(\omega\varepsilon_0)$, so its contribution to $\tan\delta$ falls as $1/f$. Dominant at DC/low frequency and at high temperature.
2. **Dipolar (orientational) relaxation**: permanent dipoles rotate against viscous drag. Debye model: $\varepsilon_r = \varepsilon_\infty + \frac{\varepsilon_s - \varepsilon_\infty}{1 + j\omega\tau}$, giving a loss peak $\varepsilon''_{max} = (\varepsilon_s - \varepsilon_\infty)/2$ at $\omega\tau = 1$. Water at room temperature relaxes near 20 GHz.
3. **Interfacial (Maxwell–Wagner) polarization**: charge piles up at boundaries in heterogeneous materials (glass/resin in FR-4, filled polymers); low-frequency loss.
4. **Ionic and electronic resonance**: lattice vibrations (infrared) and electron clouds (UV/optical); absorption at those frequencies, negligible at RF.
5. **Ferroelectric domain hysteresis**: in BaTiO₃-based Class II ceramics (X7R, X5R), domain walls move each cycle; tanδ rises with AC amplitude and varies with DC bias and temperature.
6. **Partial discharge / ionization**: voids in solid insulation break down each cycle at high voltage, producing loss and progressive damage.

**Typical tanδ** (order of magnitude; vendor-, frequency- and temperature-dependent): PTFE ~0.0002–0.001; polypropylene film ~0.0002–0.0005; C0G/NP0 ceramic ≤0.001; RO4350B 0.0037 @10 GHz; standard FR-4 ~0.015–0.025; X7R ≤0.025–0.05 (at 1 kHz); water ~0.1–0.15 at 2.45 GHz.

**Transmission lines.** Dielectric attenuation for a TEM line: $\alpha_d = \pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\tan\delta/c$ Np/m, i.e. **linear in f** (vs $\sqrt f$ for conductor loss).
:::

::: long
"An ideal capacitor's dielectric is lossless: the field polarizes the material, energy is stored, and when the field reverses you get all of it back. Current leads voltage by exactly 90 degrees, so average power is zero. Real dielectrics aren't like that, and there are several mechanisms.

First, conduction. No insulator has zero conductivity, so there's a small leakage current in phase with the voltage. It's mainly a DC and low-frequency effect, and it gets worse with temperature and contamination.

Second, and usually the most important at AC, is polarization loss. Polarization comes from charges moving: electron clouds shifting, ions displacing, and polar molecules rotating. Those motions take time and fight against their surroundings. If the field changes slowly, they keep up and there's little loss. If it changes very fast, they don't respond at all, also little loss. In between, when the period is comparable to the relaxation time, they lag the field and dissipate the most energy, like pushing a swing out of phase. Water's relaxation is around 20 GHz, so at 2.45 GHz it absorbs strongly, which is how a microwave oven works.

Third, in composite materials like FR-4, charge builds up at the glass-resin interfaces and adds loss at lower frequencies. Fourth, in ferroelectric ceramics like X7R, domains flip back and forth with a hysteresis loop. And at high voltage, voids in insulation can partially discharge every cycle, which also slowly destroys it.

All of this is summarized by the loss tangent, the ratio of the lossy to the stored part of the permittivity. Power lost is $V_{rms}^2\omega C\tan\delta$, or equivalently an ESR of $\tan\delta/\omega C$. As an example, a 100 nF cap with 50 V RMS at 100 kHz in a resonant tank dissipates about 0.16 W if it's C0G, but around 4 W if it's X7R. And on PCBs, dielectric loss grows linearly with frequency, so a 10-inch FR-4 trace can lose several dB at 5 GHz just to the laminate."
:::

::: terms
- **Loss tangent ($\tan\delta$) / dissipation factor (DF, Df)**: ratio of the lossy to the storage part of the permittivity; energy lost per cycle ÷ (2π × peak energy stored).
- **Complex permittivity ($\varepsilon' - j\varepsilon''$)**: storage ($\varepsilon'$) and loss ($\varepsilon''$) parts of the dielectric response.
- **Dielectric constant (Dk, $\varepsilon_r$)**: the relative permittivity, usually meaning $\varepsilon'$.
- **Relaxation time ($\tau$)**: characteristic response time of a polarization mechanism; loss peaks near $\omega\tau = 1$.
- **ESR**: equivalent series resistance; dielectric loss contributes $\tan\delta/(\omega C)$.
- **Quality factor (Q)**: $1/\tan\delta$ for a capacitor dielectric.
- **Partial discharge**: localized breakdown in voids or at edges without bridging the full insulation.
- **Class I vs Class II ceramic**: C0G/NP0 (paraelectric, stable, low loss) vs X7R/X5R (ferroelectric, high κ, lossy and bias-dependent).
:::

::: exceptional
**Average:** "Dielectric losses are when the insulator heats up. It's measured by loss tangent."

**Exceptional:**
- Starts from the **ideal case** (current leads by 90°, zero average power) and explains loss as a **phase shift δ** away from 90°.
- Names **distinct mechanisms** and their frequency signatures: conduction (∝1/f in tanδ), dipolar relaxation (peak at $\omega\tau = 1$), interfacial, ferroelectric hysteresis, partial discharge.
- Gives the **equations**: $\varepsilon' - j\varepsilon''$, $\tan\delta$, $P = V^2\omega C\tan\delta$, $ESR = \tan\delta/\omega C$.
- Runs a **worked number** (C0G vs X7R in a resonant tank, or FR-4 dB/inch at 5 GHz).
- Connects to **design decisions**: capacitor dielectric selection, laminate selection (Dk/Df), HV insulation design.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Dielectric loss is just leakage current."** Leakage is one mechanism, dominant at DC. At AC, polarization lag usually dominates and has nothing to do with DC insulation resistance.
- **Confusing ESR from the dielectric with ESR from electrodes/leads.** Metal resistance (and skin effect, [[q0056]]) also contributes ESR. Dielectric ESR falls as $1/f$ for constant tanδ; conductor ESR is flat or rises.
- **Treating tanδ as a constant.** It depends on frequency, temperature, AC amplitude and (for Class II ceramics) DC bias. Datasheet DF is usually specified at 1 kHz or 1 MHz.
- **Thinking low-κ means low-loss or vice versa.** They're separate properties. FR-4 and RO4350B have similar Dk (≈4.3 vs 3.5) but a 5× different Df.
- **Assuming electrolytic capacitor "tanδ" is dielectric loss.** For aluminium electrolytics it's dominated by electrolyte (conductive) resistance, so it's really an ESR spec, not oxide polarization loss.
- **Ignoring dielectric loss in PCB loss budgets** because "FR-4 is an insulator." At 10 GHz it can contribute about 1 dB per inch on standard FR-4.
:::

::: deep
## Start from the ideal capacitor

Apply $v(t) = V_p\sin\omega t$ to an ideal capacitor. The current $i = C\,dv/dt = \omega C V_p\cos\omega t$ leads the voltage by exactly 90°. Instantaneous power $p = vi \propto \sin\omega t\cos\omega t$ averages to zero: energy flows in during one quarter-cycle and back out in the next.

Inside the dielectric, the stored energy lives in **polarization**: the field stretches, shifts and rotates bound charges. Loss happens whenever that polarization *doesn't track the field perfectly*, or when some charge actually flows. Either way the current gets a component **in phase** with the voltage, and in-phase current times voltage is real power.

## Complex permittivity and the loss tangent

Write the relative permittivity as $\varepsilon_r = \varepsilon' - j\varepsilon''$. A parallel-plate capacitor $C = \varepsilon_r C_0$ (with $C_0$ the vacuum capacitance) has

$$I = j\omega\varepsilon_r C_0 V = \underbrace{\omega\varepsilon'' C_0 V}_{\text{in phase: loss}} + j\underbrace{\omega\varepsilon' C_0 V}_{\text{reactive}}$$

The ratio is the loss tangent:

$$\tan\delta = \frac{\varepsilon''}{\varepsilon'} = \frac{\text{energy lost per cycle}}{2\pi\times\text{peak energy stored}}$$

With $C = \varepsilon' C_0$, the average power is

$$\boxed{P = V_{rms}^2\,\omega C\tan\delta}$$

That can be drawn two equivalent ways:

## The mechanisms, from slowest to fastest

Each polarization mechanism has a characteristic response time. Below its frequency range it follows the field and contributes to $\varepsilon'$; above it, it can't respond at all and drops out; **near** it, it lags and dissipates.

| Mechanism | What moves | Where loss shows up | Typical example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conduction | free ions/electrons | DC to low f; tanδ ∝ 1/f | moisture, contamination, hot insulation |
| Interfacial (Maxwell–Wagner) | charge piling at material boundaries | mHz to kHz/MHz | FR-4 glass/resin, filled polymers |
| Ferroelectric domains | domain walls | at any f with large AC amplitude | X7R/X5R (BaTiO₃) MLCCs |
| Dipolar (orientational) | permanent molecular dipoles rotating | kHz to tens of GHz (material-dependent) | water (~20 GHz), polar polymers, epoxy |
| Ionic (atomic) | ions in the lattice vibrating | infrared (THz) | ceramics, glass |
| Electronic | electron clouds | UV/optical | all materials (gives refractive index) |

### Dipolar relaxation (the Debye model)

The classic mechanism. A polar molecule in a field feels a torque, but rotating it means shoving neighbours, which is like viscous friction. If the polarization relaxes toward equilibrium exponentially with time constant $\tau$, the response to a sinusoid is

$$\varepsilon_r(\omega) = \varepsilon_\infty + \frac{\varepsilon_s - \varepsilon_\infty}{1 + j\omega\tau}$$

$$\varepsilon' = \varepsilon_\infty + \frac{\Delta\varepsilon}{1 + (\omega\tau)^2},\qquad \varepsilon'' = \frac{\Delta\varepsilon\,\omega\tau}{1 + (\omega\tau)^2},\qquad \Delta\varepsilon = \varepsilon_s - \varepsilon_\infty$$

The loss peaks at $\omega\tau = 1$, with $\varepsilon''_{max} = \Delta\varepsilon/2$, and $\varepsilon'$ steps down from $\varepsilon_s$ to $\varepsilon_\infty$ across the same region.

### Conduction loss

Any conductivity σ adds an in-phase current density $\sigma E$, i.e. $\varepsilon''_{cond} = \sigma/(\omega\varepsilon_0)$. Its contribution to tanδ falls as $1/f$, so it dominates at DC and low frequency, and it grows quickly with temperature (more ions, more thermally excited carriers) and humidity. In high-voltage insulation this creates a positive-feedback risk: loss heats the insulation, heat raises σ and tanδ, which raises loss. That's **dielectric thermal runaway**.

### Interfacial (Maxwell–Wagner) polarization

In a composite like FR-4 (woven glass in epoxy), the two phases have different permittivities and conductivities. Charge accumulates at the boundaries and has to move through a resistive path to rearrange, which gives a slow, lossy polarization. This is one reason composite laminates have broad, smeared-out loss over frequency rather than a clean Debye peak.

### Ferroelectric hysteresis (Class II ceramics)

X7R and X5R MLCCs use barium-titanate formulations with spontaneous polarization in domains. A large AC field moves domain walls back and forth, tracing a P–E hysteresis loop; the loop area is energy lost per cycle. Result: tanδ of a few percent, rising with AC amplitude, plus the well-known capacitance drop with DC bias. Class I (C0G/NP0) dielectrics are paraelectric: no domains, tanδ around 0.1% or less, stable with voltage and temperature.

### Partial discharge

At high AC voltage, a gas-filled void inside solid insulation sees a higher field than the surrounding solid (lower εr) and can break down each half-cycle. Each tiny discharge dissipates energy and erodes the void wall; over time it tracks through and the insulation fails. That's why HV cables, transformers and isolation barriers are partial-discharge tested (and why digital isolator and optocoupler specs mention it).

## Worked example 1: capacitor heating in a resonant tank

A 100 nF capacitor in an LLC tank sees 50 V RMS at 100 kHz. The reactive current is $I = V\omega C = 50\times2\pi\times10^5\times10^{-7} = 3.14$ A RMS.

| Dielectric | tanδ (illustrative) | $P = V^2\omega C\tan\delta$ | Equivalent ESR |
|---|---|---|---|
| C0G/NP0 ceramic | 0.001 | 0.16 W | 16 mΩ |
| Polypropylene film | 0.0005 | 0.08 W | 8 mΩ |
| X7R ceramic | 0.025 | 3.9 W | 0.4 Ω |

Four watts in a small MLCC is a failure. That's why resonant tanks, snubbers and RF matching networks use C0G or polypropylene. X7R's tanδ under these conditions also depends on amplitude and bias, so the real number could be better or worse; the point is the 25× spread.

## Worked example 2: PCB dielectric loss

For a TEM transmission line, the dielectric attenuation is

$$\alpha_d = \frac{\pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\tan\delta}{c}\ \text{Np/m}\quad(\times 8.686\ \text{for dB/m})$$

At 5 GHz on standard FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r \approx 4.3$, $\tan\delta \approx 0.02$): $\alpha_d \approx 18.9$ dB/m ≈ **0.48 dB/inch**, so a 10-inch trace loses ~4.8 dB to the dielectric alone. On RO4350B ($\varepsilon_r = 3.48$, $\tan\delta = 0.0037$): ≈ **0.08 dB/inch**.

::: applications
- **Capacitor selection for resonant converters, snubbers and RF**: C0G/NP0 ceramics and polypropylene film for low tanδ; X7R/X5R only for decoupling and bulk where AC voltage is small.
- **High-speed PCB laminates**: low-Df materials (e.g. Rogers RO4000 series, Panasonic Megtron family, Isola low-loss resins) for 10–112 Gb/s SerDes, 5G radios and radar.
- **Microwave ovens and industrial RF/microwave heating**: deliberate dielectric loss heats water-containing materials, plastics welding, wood drying.
- **High-voltage insulation**: tanδ and partial-discharge testing of cables, transformers and bushings to detect moisture and ageing.
- **Audio and precision analog**: film or C0G capacitors in filters and sample-and-hold circuits; high-loss dielectrics also show *dielectric absorption* (soakage), a related relaxation effect.
- **Isolation devices**: digital isolators and optocouplers are qualified with partial-discharge tests to rate their working voltage.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear, small-signal behaviour**: $\varepsilon'$ and $\varepsilon''$ independent of amplitude. Fails for ferroelectric ceramics (amplitude- and bias-dependent) and at partial-discharge voltages.
- **Sinusoidal excitation**: for PWM or pulsed waveforms, compute loss harmonic by harmonic with each harmonic's tanδ, or use the vendor's loss tool.
- **Small δ**: the equivalence $ESR \approx \tan\delta/(\omega C)$ and $R_p = 1/(\omega C\tan\delta)$ assumes $\tan\delta \ll 1$.
- **Single Debye relaxation** is an idealization; most solids (epoxies, FR-4) show broad distributions of relaxation times, giving a nearly flat tanδ over decades (models like Djordjevic–Sarkar are used for PCBs).
- **Constant temperature**: tanδ and conductivity rise with temperature in many materials, which can cause thermal runaway in high-power or high-voltage parts.

If the interviewer means "losses in a capacitor" more broadly, also mention electrode/lead resistance, skin effect in plates, and ESL, which aren't dielectric losses but show up in the same ESR measurement.
:::

::: followups
### What's the difference between ESR and tanδ, and how do you convert?
tanδ is the material's loss ratio, dimensionless; ESR is the equivalent series resistance in ohms at a specific frequency. For small losses, $ESR = \tan\delta/(\omega C)$, or $\tan\delta = \omega C\cdot ESR$. Example: a 1 µF capacitor with tanδ = 0.01 at 1 kHz has $ESR = 0.01/(2\pi\times10^3\times10^{-6}) \approx 1.6$ Ω. Datasheet ESR also includes electrode and terminal resistance, which tanδ alone doesn't capture.

### Why do X7R capacitors have higher loss than C0G?
X7R uses a ferroelectric barium-titanate formulation with domains that switch under the field, giving hysteresis loss (and the high κ that makes them small). C0G is a paraelectric material with no domains, so it has low, stable loss (DF ≤ 0.1%) but much lower κ. That's the trade: X7R for capacitance density, C0G for low loss and stability.

### Why does dielectric loss grow linearly with frequency on a PCB while conductor loss grows as √f?
Per unit length, $\alpha_d = \pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\tan\delta/c$: the line stores energy in the field each cycle and loses a fixed fraction (tanδ) of it each radian, so loss per metre scales with f. Conductor loss scales with $R_{ac} \propto \sqrt f$ from skin effect. So at high frequencies dielectric loss eventually dominates, which is why laminate Df matters more as data rates rise.

### How does a microwave oven heat food, and why 2.45 GHz?
The field rotates water's polar molecules back and forth; at 2.45 GHz they lag the field (ε″ ≈ 9 with ε′ ≈ 77), so energy goes into the water as heat at $p = \omega\varepsilon_0\varepsilon'' E^2$. 2.45 GHz is an ISM band, not a water resonance. It's below water's ~20 GHz relaxation peak, which is a good thing: at the peak, absorption would be so strong the waves would only heat the surface.

### What if a high-voltage capacitor's tanδ doubles as it heats up?
That's a positive feedback loop: more loss, more heat, higher tanδ, more loss. If the thermal resistance to ambient can't remove heat faster than loss grows with temperature, the part runs away thermally and fails (often by dielectric breakdown). You'd derate the AC voltage, pick a dielectric with flat tanδ vs temperature (polypropylene, C0G), improve cooling, or monitor tanδ over life as an ageing indicator.
:::

::: interpretations
"Dielectric losses" might be asked in a capacitor context (why ESR, why capacitors heat), a PCB/signal-integrity context (Df, dB per inch), or a power/HV context (insulation heating, partial discharge). A good answer explains the physical mechanisms once and then shows how each context uses them. If the interviewer meant *all* capacitor losses, add conductor (electrode/lead) losses and leakage as separate items.
:::
