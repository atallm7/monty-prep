::: basics
### In plain words
A dielectric is an insulator that does not conduct, but its atoms can shift slightly when an electric field is applied. That small shift partly cancels the field from the charges on the plates. So for the same voltage, the plates can hold more charge, which means more capacitance. The number that says how much more is called the relative permittivity.

### Picture it
Picture rows of tiny compass needles between two plates. With no field, they point every which way. Apply a field and they all turn to line up. Their alignment creates a small field of their own that works against the main field. With part of the push canceled, you can pile more charge onto the plates before the voltage reaches the same value. The analogy simplifies the real physics, where the shift can also be in electron clouds and ions.

### Words you need
- **Insulator**: a material with almost no free charges, so it does not carry current.
- **Polarization**: the slight shifting or turning of charges inside an insulator in a field.
- **Relative permittivity ($\varepsilon_r$)**: the factor by which a dielectric multiplies capacitance compared with vacuum. Air is about 1.
- **Breakdown strength**: the field at which the insulator fails and conducts. It sets the voltage rating.
- **Capacitance**: charge held per volt. See [[q0022]].

### The simplest example
Take two 1 cm by 1 cm plates with a 0.1 mm air gap. The capacitance is $C = \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r A/d$, which works out to about 8.85 pF. Fill the gap with FR-4 circuit board material, where $\varepsilon_r$ is about 4.3, and the same plates give about 38 pF. Nothing else changed, only the material. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page explains the polarization types, the losses and the voltage limits, and why different ceramics behave so differently.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know what physically sits between a capacitor's plates and why it matters. The interviewer is checking:

1. The definition: an **electrical insulator that polarizes** in an electric field.
2. *Why* a dielectric increases capacitance (bound charges partially cancel the field, so more free charge fits per volt), quantified by relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r$.
3. That dielectrics have **limits and imperfections**: breakdown field, loss (dissipation factor), leakage, and dependence on temperature, voltage and frequency.
4. Whether you can link that to **part selection**: C0G vs X7R vs Y5V, film vs electrolytic, and PCB laminate choices like FR-4 vs low-loss materials.
:::

::: why
The dielectric is what makes one capacitor different from another. Two 10 µF parts in the same package can behave completely differently because one is X5R ceramic, one is tantalum and one is aluminium polymer: different DC-bias loss, temperature drift, ESR, voltage rating, and failure modes. The same physics shows up in PCB laminates (dielectric constant sets trace impedance and propagation speed; loss tangent sets high-speed signal loss) and in MOSFET gate oxides. Interviewers use this question to see whether you'll pick a capacitor by its *dielectric* rather than just by its capacitance value.
:::

::: concepts
- **Insulators vs conductors**: a dielectric has essentially no free charge carriers, so it blocks conduction current. See [[q0055]].
- **Capacitance**: $C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/d$; the dielectric enters through $\varepsilon_r$. See [[q0022]].
- **Polarization**: the field displaces bound charges (electron clouds, ions, molecular dipoles), creating tiny aligned dipoles.
- **Permittivity**: how much polarization a material gives per unit field; $\varepsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e$.
- **Dielectric strength**: the field at which the insulator breaks down and conducts, which sets voltage rating.
- **Dielectric loss**: polarization that lags the field dissipates energy, modelled as part of ESR ([[q0030]]).
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** A dielectric is an insulator (negligible free carriers, resistivity typically $>10^{10}$ Ω·m) in which an applied field $\mathbf E$ induces a polarization $\mathbf P$ (dipole moment per volume):
$$\mathbf D = \varepsilon_0\mathbf E + \mathbf P = \varepsilon_0(1+\chi_e)\mathbf E = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\mathbf E$$

**Effect on capacitance.** Bound surface charge $\sigma_b = P$ at the dielectric faces opposes the free charge on the plates. For a fixed voltage the plates hold $\varepsilon_r$ times more free charge: $C = \varepsilon_r C_{vacuum}$.

**Polarization mechanisms** (each "freezes out" above a characteristic frequency):
- Electronic (electron cloud shift): fastest, up to optical frequencies.
- Ionic/atomic (lattice ions displace): up to infrared.
- Orientational (permanent dipoles rotate): up to roughly kHz–GHz depending on the material; source of large $\varepsilon_r$ and much of the loss in polar materials.
- Interfacial (space charge at boundaries): slow; related to dielectric absorption.
- Ferroelectric domains (BaTiO₃): very high $\varepsilon_r$ but nonlinear with field, temperature and time (ageing).

**Key figures of merit.**
- **$\varepsilon_r$**: vacuum 1, PTFE ≈ 2.1, polypropylene ≈ 2.2, FR-4 ≈ 4–4.7, alumina/Al₂O₃ ≈ 9, Ta₂O₅ ≈ 25–27, Class I ceramics tens to ~100, Class II BaTiO₃ ceramics ≈ 1,000–10,000+.
- **Dielectric strength**: air ≈ 3 kV/mm at sea level; polymer films and oxides are 10–100× higher.
- **Dissipation factor** $\text{DF} = \tan\delta = \text{ESR}/|X_C|$: C0G ≲ 0.1%, X7R typically specified ≤ 2.5–5% at 1 kHz.
- **Insulation resistance / leakage**: finite resistivity creates a parallel leakage path.

**EIA class codes.** Class I "C0G/NP0": 0 ± 30 ppm/°C, no DC-bias effect. Class II "X7R": −55 to +125 °C, ±15%; "X5R": −55 to +85 °C, ±15%; "Y5V": −30 to +85 °C, +22/−82%.

**Field stress example.** 10 V across a 2 µm MLCC layer is 5 V/µm = 5 MV/m; 1 V across a 2 nm gate oxide is 500 MV/m, which is why thin oxides have tight voltage limits.
:::

::: long
"A dielectric is an electrical insulator that responds to an electric field by polarizing. It has essentially no free carriers, so no conduction current flows through it, but the charges that are bound to its atoms can move slightly. The electron cloud around each atom gets pulled off-centre, ions in a crystal lattice shift, and in polar materials whole molecules rotate to line up with the field. The result is billions of tiny aligned dipoles.

In a capacitor, those dipoles matter because their own field points opposite to the applied field. At the surfaces of the dielectric you effectively get a layer of bound charge that partially cancels the free charge on the plates. So to reach a given voltage, the plates have to hold more free charge, which is the definition of higher capacitance. We wrap that up in the relative permittivity: $C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/d$. Air is about 1, PCB FR-4 is around 4 to 4.7, tantalum pentoxide is about 25, and the barium titanate used in X7R ceramic capacitors is in the thousands.

But the dielectric also sets the limitations. First, breakdown: every insulator has a maximum field before it fails, which with the layer thickness sets the voltage rating. Second, loss: polarization doesn't follow the field perfectly, so energy gets dissipated as heat, which shows up as the dissipation factor and part of ESR. Third, stability: very high permittivity materials are usually ferroelectric, so their $\varepsilon_r$ depends on temperature, DC bias and even age.

That's the trade-off I think about when picking parts. C0G is a Class I ceramic with low permittivity, so you get small values, but it's flat over temperature, has no DC-bias effect and very low loss, so I'd use it for filters and timing. X5R and X7R pack in way more capacitance, which is great for decoupling, but a 10 µF part might give you only a few microfarads at its working voltage. And the same ideas apply to PCB laminates, where $\varepsilon_r$ sets trace impedance and loss tangent sets how much a high-speed signal attenuates."
:::

::: terms
- **Dielectric**: an insulator that can be polarized by an applied electric field.
- **Polarization ($\mathbf P$)**: induced dipole moment per unit volume.
- **Relative permittivity / dielectric constant ($\varepsilon_r$, $D_k$)**: factor by which a material increases capacitance relative to vacuum.
- **Dielectric strength**: the maximum field (V/m or V/µm) before breakdown.
- **Dissipation factor / loss tangent ($\tan\delta$, $D_f$)**: ratio of loss to stored energy per cycle; equals ESR/|X_C| for a capacitor.
- **Class I / Class II ceramic**: stable paraelectric (C0G) vs high-$\varepsilon_r$ ferroelectric (X5R, X7R, Y5V) dielectrics.
- **DC-bias effect**: the drop in a Class II ceramic's capacitance with applied DC voltage.
- **Dielectric absorption**: slow polarization that makes a discharged capacitor partially "recover" voltage.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"A dielectric is a conductor that stores charge."** It's an insulator. The *plates* hold the free charge; the dielectric holds bound, polarized charge.
- **Thinking higher $\varepsilon_r$ is always better.** High-$\varepsilon_r$ ferroelectrics trade stability, linearity and loss for density. Use C0G where accuracy matters.
- **Equating dielectric strength with voltage rating.** The rating includes thickness, safety margin, temperature and lifetime. Rated voltage is far below the breakdown field × thickness.
- **Assuming $\varepsilon_r$ is constant.** It varies with frequency (FR-4 drops from about 4.7 at 1 MHz toward ~4 at GHz), temperature and, for Class II ceramics, DC bias.
- **Mixing up X7R's letters.** X7R doesn't mean "7% tolerance". X = −55 °C, 7 = +125 °C, R = ±15% capacitance change over that range.
:::

::: deep
## What makes something a dielectric

All materials respond to an electric field. In a **conductor**, free electrons move until the field inside is zero: current flows. In a **dielectric**, there are essentially no free carriers. The electrons are tied to their atoms and the ions to their lattice sites, so the best the material can do is *stretch*: each atom or molecule becomes a small dipole aligned with the field. That response is **polarization**.

### Why polarization increases capacitance

Hold the voltage $V$ across the plates fixed. Then the field in the gap is fixed: $E = V/d$. In vacuum, the plate charge needed to produce that field is $\sigma_0 = \varepsilon_0 E$. With a dielectric, the bound surface charge $\sigma_b = P = \varepsilon_0\chi_e E$ partly cancels the plate charge's field, so the battery has to push *extra* free charge onto the plates to restore the same $E$:

$$\sigma_{free} = \varepsilon_0E + P = \varepsilon_0(1+\chi_e)E = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rE$$

More charge at the same voltage is more capacitance, by exactly the factor $\varepsilon_r$:

$$C = \frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A}{d}$$

The alternative view, with the *charge* held fixed (an isolated capacitor), is that inserting the dielectric reduces the field and therefore the voltage by $\varepsilon_r$. Same conclusion: $C = Q/V$ goes up.

## The polarization mechanisms, and why permittivity depends on frequency

Different charges move at different speeds, so different mechanisms contribute at different frequencies:

| Mechanism | What moves | Fades out around | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electronic | Electron cloud relative to nucleus | Ultraviolet/optical | Present in every material; sets refractive index |
| Ionic | Positive and negative ions in a lattice | Infrared | Important in ceramics and oxides |
| Orientational | Permanent molecular dipoles rotate | Roughly kHz to GHz | Big contributor in polar materials (water, some polymers); main source of loss peaks |
| Interfacial / space charge | Charges accumulating at grain boundaries | Below Hz to kHz | Causes dielectric absorption |
| Ferroelectric domains | Whole domains switch | Varies | Gives BaTiO₃ its huge $\varepsilon_r$, plus nonlinearity and ageing |

Every time a mechanism can't keep up, $\varepsilon_r$ drops and there's a loss peak where the polarization lags the field. That's why the dielectric constant of FR-4 is quoted *at a frequency*, and why designers of multi-GHz links specify low-loss laminates.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The frequency boundaries above are order-of-magnitude. The exact relaxation frequency of orientational polarization depends strongly on the material and temperature.

| Ta₂O₅ | ≈ 25–27 | Tantalum capacitor dielectric |
| Class I ceramic (C0G/NP0) | tens to ~100 | Precision MLCCs |
| Class II ceramic (BaTiO₃-based) | ≈ 1,000 to over 10,000 | X5R/X7R/Y5V MLCCs |

### Dielectric strength and voltage rating

Every dielectric fails above some field. Air at sea level breaks down around 3 kV/mm. Good polymer films and oxides withstand fields 10–100× higher, which is why you can make thin layers. The voltage rating is set by the layer thickness times a *derated* safe field, with margin for temperature, defects and lifetime. Two quick numbers:

- An MLCC layer 2 µm thick at 10 V sees **5 V/µm** (5 MV/m).
- A 2 nm MOSFET gate oxide at 1 V sees **500 MV/m**. Thin gate oxides are why logic-level FETs specify $V_{GS(max)}$ of ±8 to ±20 V and why ESD can punch through them.

Aluminium electrolytics use this directly: the oxide is grown electrochemically, with thickness roughly proportional to the forming voltage, so a higher-voltage electrolytic has a thicker oxide and lower capacitance per area.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Quoted dielectric strengths vary a lot with thickness, temperature, humidity, test method and defects. Use manufacturer ratings for design, not handbook breakdown fields.

### Loss: dissipation factor and loss tangent

Polarization that lags the field turns energy into heat. For a capacitor this is captured as the **dissipation factor**:

$$\text{DF} = \tan\delta = \frac{\text{ESR}}{|X_C|} = \omega C\cdot\text{ESR}$$

A 1 µF X7R with DF = 2.5% at 1 kHz has $|X_C| = 159$ Ω, so the dielectric-loss ESR at 1 kHz is about 4 Ω. That's a frequency-dependent ESR, much larger than the milliohm ESR you'd see near resonance, and it matters for precision filters but rarely for decoupling. C0G's DF of ≲0.1% is why it's preferred for filters and oscillators.

For PCBs the same idea appears as $D_f$: FR-4 is around 0.015–0.02, while low-loss RF laminates are in the 0.001–0.004 range.

A C0G part changes by at most about 0.3% across its whole range, while a Y5V part may lose over 80% of its capacitance. And this is temperature alone, before any DC-bias loss.

The three-character EIA code for Class II decodes as: first letter = low temperature (X = −55 °C, Y = −30 °C, Z = +10 °C), digit = high temperature (5 = +85 °C, 7 = +125 °C, 8 = +150 °C), last letter = allowed change (R = ±15%, V = +22/−82%, U = +22/−56%).

Class II ceramics are ferroelectric. That gives them their enormous $\varepsilon_r$, but also:

- **DC-bias loss**: the domains get "pinned" by a DC field, so incremental permittivity falls. Small-case parts near rated voltage can lose 50–80%.
- **Ageing**: permittivity decays logarithmically with time after the part was last heated above its Curie point (around 125 °C for BaTiO₃); reflow "resets" the clock.
- **Piezoelectricity**: they physically flex with voltage, which causes audible "singing" with kHz ripple and microphonic noise.

> [!TIP]
> Rule of thumb for part selection: C0G for anything where the value must be accurate (filters, oscillators, timing, sample-and-hold); X7R/X5R for decoupling and bulk where you just need "a lot" of capacitance; avoid Y5V/Z5U in new designs unless the wide variation is truly acceptable.

## Dielectric imperfections that show up in the capacitor model

The realistic capacitor model ([[q0030]]) traces several elements directly to the dielectric:

- **Leakage resistance** (parallel): finite resistivity of the dielectric. Electrolytics leak µA; film and C0G leak far less.
- **Dielectric loss** (part of ESR): $\tan\delta$ described above.
- **Dielectric absorption** (extra RC branches): slow polarization mechanisms. Short a capacitor, remove the short, and its voltage creeps back up a few percent or less depending on the material. Important in sample-and-hold and integrators; polypropylene and PTFE are best.
- **Breakdown** (failure): over-voltage punctures the dielectric, usually causing a short ([[q0036]]).

## Beyond capacitors

- **PCB laminates**: trace characteristic impedance scales roughly as $1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,eff}}$, and propagation speed is $c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,eff}}$, about 15 cm/ns in FR-4 stripline.
- **Gate oxides and high-k**: modern CMOS replaced SiO₂ ($\varepsilon_r$ = 3.9) with hafnium-based high-k dielectrics to get high gate capacitance with a physically thicker, less leaky layer.
- **Insulation in cables and isolation barriers**: here the goal is the opposite: high dielectric strength, low capacitance, low loss.
:::

::: applications
- **Choosing decoupling caps**: X7R/X5R 100 nF–10 µF MLCCs give high capacitance per area; a designer checks the DC-bias curve at the rail voltage.
- **Precision RC filters and oscillators**: C0G/NP0 capacitors for anti-alias filters, crystal load capacitors and timing because their capacitance barely moves with temperature or voltage.
- **Film capacitors** (polypropylene) in snubbers, audio crossovers and motor-run applications for low loss and self-healing.
- **Electrolytic and tantalum** bulk capacitors use extremely thin oxide dielectrics to reach tens to thousands of µF.
- **PCB stack-up design**: specifying FR-4 vs low-loss laminates ($D_k$, $D_f$) for multi-gigabit links.
- **MOSFET gate oxide**: the gate dielectric's thickness sets threshold voltage, gate capacitance and $V_{GS(max)}$.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear, isotropic dielectric**: $\mathbf P \propto \mathbf E$ in the same direction. Ferroelectric Class II ceramics are nonlinear, and some crystals are anisotropic.
- **Low frequency**: the $\varepsilon_r$ values quoted are near DC to MHz. At GHz, $\varepsilon_r$ and loss differ, so use values specified at the operating frequency.
- **Fields below breakdown** and at moderate temperature.
- The answer would focus differently in other contexts: in RF/PCB interviews, "dielectric" usually means the laminate ($D_k$, $D_f$); in device-physics interviews it often means gate oxide.
:::

::: followups
### What do C0G and X7R mean and when would you use each?
C0G (also called NP0) is a Class I ceramic: temperature coefficient 0 ± 30 ppm/°C, no DC-bias effect, very low loss, but low $\varepsilon_r$, so values top out around tens of nF in small packages. Use it for filters, oscillators, timing. X7R is Class II: −55 to +125 °C with ±15% change, high capacitance per volume, but it loses capacitance with DC bias and ages. Use it for decoupling and bulk where the exact value isn't critical.

### Why does a 10 µF X5R capacitor measure much less than 10 µF in circuit?
Because Class II dielectrics are ferroelectric: a DC field partially locks the domains, so the incremental permittivity drops. In a small package with thin layers, the field per layer at rated voltage is high and the loss can be 50% or more. Add tolerance (±10–20%), temperature and ageing, and the in-circuit value can be a fraction of nameplate. Pick a larger case or higher voltage rating, and check the vendor's DC-bias curve.

### What's the dissipation factor and how does it relate to ESR?
DF (or $\tan\delta$) is the ratio of energy lost to energy stored per cycle (divided by 2π), and for a series model it equals ESR/|X_C|, i.e. $\omega C\cdot\text{ESR}$. A 1 µF capacitor with DF = 2.5% at 1 kHz has ESR ≈ 0.025 × 159 Ω ≈ 4 Ω at that frequency. Low DF means low heating and sharp, accurate filters.

### What if you put the dielectric under too high a field?
It breaks down: electrons gain enough energy to ionise atoms, an avalanche forms, and a conductive channel punches through. In MLCCs and tantalums that's usually a permanent short. Some dielectrics, like metallised polypropylene film, can "self-heal": the arc vaporises the thin electrode around the fault, isolating it and losing a little capacitance. Air "heals" too, recovering once the arc is removed.

### Why does PCB dielectric constant matter for high-speed signals?
Signals propagate at $c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,eff}}$, so $\varepsilon_r$ sets delay and therefore length-matching. Trace impedance falls as $\varepsilon_r$ rises, so stack-up and trace width depend on it. And the loss tangent sets dielectric loss, which grows linearly with frequency and closes the eye on multi-gigabit links, which is why designers move to low-$D_f$ materials.
