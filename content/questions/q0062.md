::: testing
Whether you understand that "P" and "N" are about **which charge carrier is in the majority**, not about the material being electrically positive or negative. Both types are neutral overall. The interviewer is listening for:

1. Which dopant makes which type (donors give N, acceptors give P) and why, in terms of the valence-electron count versus silicon's four.
2. What a **hole** actually is, and that it is a useful bookkeeping idea for a missing electron in the valence band.
3. That the minority carrier is never zero, because of the mass-action law $np = n_i^2$.
4. The consequence: put the two together and you get a PN junction, the building block of every diode and transistor.
:::

::: why
Every semiconductor device is built out of regions of N-type and P-type material in some arrangement: a diode is one P region touching one N region, a BJT is N-P-N or P-N-P, and a MOSFET is N+ source and drain wells sitting in a P substrate (or the reverse). If you can't say what makes a region P or N, you can't explain why a junction conducts one way, why a MOSFET channel forms, or why a bipolar transistor's base must be thin. It also comes up as a quick check for confusion: a surprising number of candidates think N-type material is "negatively charged."
:::

::: concepts
- **Intrinsic silicon**: pure silicon has four valence electrons, forms a covalent crystal, and at room temperature has only about $10^{10}$ free carriers per cm³ out of $5\times10^{22}$ atoms. See [[q0055]].
- **Energy bands**: electrons sit in the valence band or, if excited across the bandgap, in the conduction band. See [[q0061]].
- **Donor (N-type) doping**: a Group V atom such as phosphorus adds a fifth electron that is barely bound and becomes a free electron. See [[q0065]], [[q0067]].
- **Acceptor (P-type) doping**: a Group III atom such as boron is one electron short, so it grabs an electron from the lattice and leaves a mobile hole behind.
- **Mass-action law**: $n\,p = n_i^2$ at equilibrium. Raise one carrier population and the other falls. See [[q0069]].
:::

::: answer
"Both are just silicon with a tiny amount of impurity added on purpose, and the difference is which carrier you have more of. For N-type I add something like phosphorus, which has five valence electrons. Four of them bond into the crystal and the fifth is left over and loosely held, so it becomes a free electron. Electrons are the majority carrier, which is why it's called N for negative carriers. For P-type I add boron, which has three valence electrons. It's one short of a full bond, so it picks up an electron from a neighbouring atom and leaves a hole. That hole acts like a mobile positive charge, so holes are the majority carrier.

Neither material is actually charged. The extra electron is balanced by the positive phosphorus ion it left behind, and the same for boron. And the minority carrier is never zero: in N-type with $10^{16}$ donors per cm³, electrons are about $10^{16}$, and holes are about $10^{4}$ from $np=n_i^2$. Put a P region next to an N region and you get a PN junction, which is where the diode and everything else comes from."
:::

::: technical
**Starting point.** Silicon is Group IV with four valence electrons, each forming a covalent bond with a neighbour. Intrinsic carrier density at 300 K is $n_i \approx 10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$ (commonly quoted between $1\times10^{10}$ and $1.5\times10^{10}$ depending on the source), against an atomic density of about $5\times10^{22}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$.

**N-type.** Substituting a donor (P, As, Sb) puts a fifth valence electron into the lattice. Its binding energy is only about 45 meV for phosphorus in silicon, smaller than $kT\approx 26$ meV times a few, so it is essentially fully ionized at room temperature. With donor density $N_D$:
$$n \approx N_D,\qquad p = \frac{n_i^2}{n}$$
Electrons are the **majority** carrier; holes are the **minority**.

**P-type.** An acceptor (B, Al, Ga, In) has three valence electrons and creates a vacancy in the covalent bonding. A nearby valence electron hops in, ionizing the acceptor (negative, fixed) and leaving a mobile hole. With acceptor density $N_A$:
$$p \approx N_A,\qquad n = \frac{n_i^2}{p}$$

**Charge neutrality.** Both materials are neutral: $p + N_D^+ = n + N_A^-$. Mobile charge is balanced by fixed ionized dopants.

**Fermi level.** In N-type, $E_F$ moves up toward the conduction band: $E_F - E_i = kT\ln(n/n_i)$. In P-type it moves down toward the valence band. For $N_D=10^{16}$ cm⁻³ that shift is about $0.0259\ln(10^{6})\approx 0.36$ eV.

**Numbers.** For $N_D=10^{16}$: $n=10^{16}$, $p=10^{20}/10^{16}=10^{4}$ cm⁻³, a $10^{12}$ ratio. The one dopant per $5\times10^{6}$ silicon atoms is only 0.2 ppm, yet it raises the carrier density by six orders of magnitude over intrinsic.
:::

::: short
"N-type and P-type are silicon doped on purpose so one carrier dominates. N-type uses a donor like phosphorus with five valence electrons, so the extra electron is free and electrons are the majority carrier. P-type uses an acceptor like boron with three valence electrons, which leaves a missing electron, a hole, that behaves like a mobile positive charge. Both are electrically neutral. The minority carrier is set by $np=n_i^2$. Joining a P and an N region makes a PN junction, the basis of diodes and transistors."
:::

::: long
"Let me start from pure silicon. Each atom has four valence electrons and shares them with four neighbours, so at room temperature there are very few free carriers, about $10^{10}$ per cm³. It's a poor conductor. Doping fixes that.

If I replace a few silicon atoms with phosphorus, which has five valence electrons, four of them make bonds and the fifth is left over. It's only bound by tens of meV, so room-temperature thermal energy frees it. That gives me a free electron without making any mobile positive charge, because the phosphorus ion stays fixed in the lattice. So I get an N-type semiconductor: electrons are the majority carrier, with $n\approx N_D$.

If I use boron instead, with three valence electrons, there's one bond missing. A neighbouring electron hops in to fill it, which makes the boron a fixed negative ion and leaves behind a hole. The hole moves around as electrons keep hopping into it, and it acts like a mobile positive carrier. That's P-type, with $p\approx N_A$.

Two things I always want to say. One, neither type is charged. The mobile carriers are balanced by the fixed ions. Two, there's always a minority carrier. The mass-action law says $np=n_i^2$, so with $10^{16}$ donors per cm³ you have $10^{16}$ electrons but only about $10^4$ holes. Those minority carriers matter a lot in leakage current and in bipolar transistors.

Then the payoff: put a P region and an N region together and you get a PN junction. Electrons diffuse one way, holes the other, they leave a depletion region and a built-in field, and that is the diode. A BJT is two junctions back to back, and a MOSFET is N+ or P+ regions in the opposite-type substrate. So all of the devices come out of these two doped materials."
:::

::: terms
- **Donor**: Group V dopant (P, As, Sb) that gives up an extra electron; becomes a fixed positive ion.
- **Acceptor**: Group III dopant (B, Ga, Al) that accepts an electron from the lattice, creating a hole; becomes a fixed negative ion.
- **Majority carrier**: the carrier type present in far greater numbers (electrons in N, holes in P).
- **Minority carrier**: the scarce carrier type, $n_i^2$ divided by the majority density.
- **Hole**: an absence of a valence electron that behaves like a positive charge carrier with its own effective mass and mobility.
- **Intrinsic carrier concentration ($n_i$)**: thermally generated electron-hole pair density in undoped material, about $10^{10}$ cm⁻³ for silicon at 300 K.
- **Mass-action law**: $np=n_i^2$ in equilibrium, regardless of doping.
- **Extrinsic semiconductor**: a semiconductor whose carrier population is dominated by dopants rather than thermal generation.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "N-type has extra electrons, P-type has extra holes" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names the dopant, its valence count and *why* that gives a free electron or a hole, rather than just stating the result.
- States clearly that both types are electrically neutral and that the fixed ion balances the mobile carrier.
- Explains the hole as the vacancy that moves when valence electrons hop into it, not a particle in a vacuum.
- Quantifies the minority carrier with $np=n_i^2$ and gives a real number such as $10^{16}$ versus $10^{4}$ cm⁻³.
- Mentions that mobilities differ (electrons are faster), connects to junctions, and points out the temperature caveat: at high temperature $n_i$ grows and the material goes intrinsic.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"N-type is negatively charged and P-type is positively charged."** Both are neutral. N and P name the sign of the majority *mobile carrier*.
- **"P-type has no electrons."** P-type still has electrons everywhere: they are the minority carrier, and the valence band is full of them. A hole is a vacancy among them.
- **"Holes are actual positive particles like protons moving through the crystal."** A hole is the collective motion of valence-band electrons hopping the other way. It is real in effect, but it is a quasiparticle with an effective mass.
- **Forgetting the minority carrier.** Saying "there are no holes in N-type" misses that $p=n_i^2/n$ is small but nonzero and drives reverse leakage and BJT operation.
- **Mixing up donor and acceptor.** Donors (Group V) make N-type. Acceptors (Group III) make P-type. A mnemonic: **P**hosphorus makes **N**; **B**oron makes **P**.
- **Saying doping changes the number of atoms in the crystal noticeably.** Typical doping is parts per million to parts per billion; the lattice is basically unchanged.
:::

::: deep
## Starting from the intrinsic crystal

In a perfect silicon crystal each atom shares its four valence electrons with four neighbours. At 0 K every electron is locked into a bond: the valence band is full, the conduction band is empty, and silicon is an insulator. At 300 K, thermal energy occasionally lifts an electron across the 1.12 eV bandgap, leaving a mobile electron in the conduction band and a hole in the valence band. These come in pairs, so $n=p=n_i$.

$$n_i \propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/2kT}$$

Roughly $10^{10}$ cm⁻³ at 300 K, against $5\times10^{22}$ atoms cm⁻³. Pure silicon has a resistivity of roughly $3\times10^{5}\ \Omega\cdot$cm, which is not useful for making circuits, and it is set by temperature rather than by anything you control.

## Doping: engineering the carrier population

Doping adds a controlled number of impurity atoms, typically $10^{14}$ to $10^{20}$ cm⁻³ (from lightly doped substrates to heavily doped "+" regions). Because the ionization energies of common dopants in silicon are only about 45 meV (P), 54 meV (As), and 45 meV (B), they are almost completely ionized at 300 K. That means $n\approx N_D$ in N-type and $p\approx N_A$ in P-type with no fiddling.

The doping must also pass the mass-action law. Thermal equilibrium requires
$$n\,p = n_i^2$$
which says that if you push electrons up by donors, holes fall by exactly the same factor. For $N_D=10^{16}$ cm⁻³ and $n_i=10^{10}$ cm⁻³:
$$p=\frac{10^{20}}{10^{16}}=10^{4}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$$

## What is a hole, physically?

When an electron in the valence band moves into a vacancy, the vacancy moves the opposite way. We track this vacancy rather than the $10^{22}$ electrons, and it behaves like a particle with charge $+q$, an effective mass of the order of 0.4-0.8 $m_0$ in silicon (the value depends on which averaging definition you use), and its own mobility. The hole is a convenient accounting device, but the current it describes is real. In a Hall measurement, P-type gives the opposite sign to N-type, which is how holes were established experimentally.

## Charge neutrality, not charge

A point that trips people up: doping adds *no net charge*. Every phosphorus atom adds one electron and one proton (in the nucleus), so the crystal stays neutral. After the extra electron is freed, there is a fixed $+q$ ion and a free electron, adding to zero. The same applies to boron.

$$p+N_D^+=n+N_A^-$$

This neutrality breaks down only near a junction, where mobile carriers diffuse away and uncompensated ions are exposed. That is the depletion region ([[q0063]]).

## Worked example: how different are the two types?

Compare silicon doped $10^{16}$ cm⁻³ with phosphorus (N-type) and with boron (P-type), at 300 K.

| Quantity | N-type ($N_D=10^{16}$) | P-type ($N_A=10^{16}$) |
|---|---|---|
| Majority carrier | electrons, $10^{16}$ cm⁻³ | holes, $10^{16}$ cm⁻³ |
| Minority carrier | holes, $10^{4}$ cm⁻³ | electrons, $10^{4}$ cm⁻³ |
| Majority mobility (approx.) | ~1250 cm²/V·s | ~410 cm²/V·s |
| Resistivity $1/(q\,n\,\mu)$ | ~0.50 Ω·cm | ~1.5 Ω·cm |
| Fermi level vs midgap | 0.36 eV above | 0.36 eV below |

At equal doping the N-type is roughly three times as conductive purely because electrons are faster than holes ([[q0070]], [[q0071]]). Mobility values drop at high doping, and sources differ a bit.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Mobility numbers depend on doping, temperature and source. The values here are representative of lightly doped silicon at 300 K ($\mu_n\approx1350$, $\mu_p\approx480$ cm²/V·s intrinsic limit, falling to roughly 1250 and 410 at $10^{16}$ cm⁻³). Use them for the ratio, not as design data.

## Where it matters in hardware

- **Diodes and junctions:** P touches N, giving a depletion region ([[q0063]]) and one-way conduction ([[q0072]]).
- **BJTs:** N-P-N or P-N-P with a thin, lightly doped base so minority carriers cross it before recombining.
- **MOSFETs:** an NMOS has N+ source/drain implants in a P-type body; the channel is an *inverted* N-type layer.
- **Resistors in ICs:** a "well resistor" is simply a doped region, with resistance set by doping and geometry.
:::

::: applications
- **Silicon diodes and rectifiers**: a P region and an N region form the junction used in every power supply.
- **NPN transistors like the 2N3904**: N emitter, P base, N collector; the doping levels (heavy emitter, light collector) set gain and breakdown.
- **CMOS logic**: NMOS in a P substrate and PMOS in an N-well, the complementary pair used in nearly every digital chip.
- **Solar cells**: a large-area PN junction where light-generated carriers are separated by the built-in field.
- **Hall-effect sensors**: the sign of the Hall voltage reveals whether the carriers are holes or electrons.
- **Thermistor-like silicon sensors** (e.g., silicon temperature sensors): lightly doped bulk silicon whose resistance changes with temperature through mobility and ionization.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Room temperature (300 K)** with complete ionization of dopants. At very low temperature dopants freeze out; at high temperature (above roughly 150 °C for lightly doped silicon) $n_i$ rises toward the doping level and the material stops behaving as strongly N or P.
- **Non-degenerate doping** (below roughly $10^{18}$ cm⁻³ in silicon). At heavy doping the Fermi level enters a band, the mass-action relation $np=n_i^2$ no longer holds exactly, and the material behaves nearly like a metal.
- **Silicon as the example.** The same picture holds for germanium and for compound semiconductors, but dopants differ (in GaAs, Si can be a donor or acceptor depending on which site it occupies; Zn and Te are other options).
- **Uniform doping** in the bulk. Real devices have doping profiles that vary with depth, which is what makes junction and transistor structures.
- **Valid alternative answer**: some interviewers want just "N-type = electrons majority, P-type = holes majority" with dopants. Give that first, then the physics.
:::

::: followups
### Is an N-type semiconductor negatively charged?
No. It is neutral. Each phosphorus atom contributes one electron *and* one extra proton in its nucleus. The mobile electron is balanced by the fixed $P^+$ ion left in the lattice. "N" refers to the polarity of the majority mobile carrier, not net charge.

### If I dope silicon with $10^{16}$ cm⁻³ phosphorus, what are $n$ and $p$ at 300 K?
$n\approx N_D=10^{16}$ cm⁻³ (donors fully ionized) and $p=n_i^2/n=(10^{10})^2/10^{16}=10^{4}$ cm⁻³. Electrons outnumber holes by $10^{12}$. The carrier density rose by six orders of magnitude over the intrinsic value, which is why a fraction of a part per million of dopant (here 0.2 ppm) changes resistivity so much.

### What happens to the minority carriers when you heat the sample?
$n_i$ rises roughly as $T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/2kT}$; for silicon from 300 K to 400 K it grows by a factor of about 350. Minority carriers go as $n_i^2$, so they grow far faster, about $10^5$ times here. This is why reverse leakage roughly doubles every ~10 °C and why circuits with high-impedance nodes drift when hot.

### Why do we say holes move if only electrons actually hop?
Because it's simpler to track one vacancy than the electrons around it. When a valence electron hops into a hole, the hole moves one lattice site the other way. Net charge motion is real, and holes have different mobility and effective mass from conduction electrons, so treating them as a separate carrier gives correct results for drift, diffusion, and Hall measurements.

### What happens if you dope the same region with both donors and acceptors?
They compensate. The net doping is $|N_D-N_A|$, and the type is set by whichever is larger. With $N_D=2\times10^{16}$ and $N_A=1\times10^{16}$ the material is N-type with $n\approx10^{16}$. Compensation also reduces mobility because the ions scatter carriers, so compensated material is more resistive than its net doping suggests. Process engineers use this when they implant a P-well into an N-type layer.

### You get a mystery silicon wafer. How would you find out if it's N or P type?
A hot-probe test: heat one probe and measure the sign of the thermoelectric voltage. Or a four-point probe for resistivity plus a Hall measurement, where the sign of the Hall voltage identifies the majority carrier. Or build a quick junction test: contact a known N-type reference and see if it rectifies.
:::
