::: testing
Whether you can walk through the signal chain of a servo from the command pulse to shaft motion and back to the sensor, without hand-waving. The interviewer is listening for: how the pulse width is decoded into a target, where the feedback comes from, how error becomes motor drive (direction and magnitude), why there is a gearbox, and what deadband and gain do. Candidates who separate the 50 Hz command PWM from the motor-drive PWM inside the servo clearly have hands-on experience.
:::

::: why
If you debug a twitching servo, a stalled robot joint or a brown-out when a servo moves, you need a mental model of what is happening inside the case. It also generalises: the same compare-amplify-drive structure appears in industrial drives, in voltage regulators (error amplifier driving a pass element) and in thermostats. Being able to narrate the loop step by step is what turns "I've used servos" into "I understand them".
:::

::: concepts
- **Servo definition**: motor plus sensor plus controller. See [[q0383]].
- **PWM as a command**: information encoded in pulse width, not average voltage. See [[q0387]].
- **Potentiometer as angle sensor**: a voltage divider whose ratio follows shaft angle. See [[q0008]].
- **H-bridge**: drives the motor in either direction. See [[q0411]].
- **Back-EMF and torque constant**: why current maps to torque and speed to voltage.
- **Proportional control**: drive proportional to error, with saturation.
:::

::: answer
"Inside a hobby servo you have a small DC motor, a gear train, a potentiometer attached to the output shaft and a control chip with an H-bridge. Every 20 ms I send it a pulse, and the width of that pulse, around 1 to 2 ms, is the target angle. The chip measures the pulse width and turns it into a target voltage or number.

At the same time it reads the potentiometer, which gives a voltage proportional to the actual shaft angle. The control chip subtracts the two to get an error. If the error is positive it turns the H-bridge on in one direction, if negative the other direction, and the drive is roughly proportional to the size of the error, so it moves fast when it's far and slows down as it arrives. When the error falls inside a small deadband, it stops driving.

The gearbox is there because small motors spin fast with little torque; the gears trade speed for torque, and they also put the potentiometer on the slow output side, which gives the angle sensor good resolution. If you push on the shaft, the pot reading changes, an error appears and the servo pushes back, which is why it holds position."
:::

::: technical
**1. Command decoding.** The control IC measures the high time $t_{pw}$ of each incoming pulse, repeated at roughly 50 Hz. Under the common convention $t_{pw}=1.0$–$2.0$ ms maps linearly onto the travel range; a mapping of 1.5 ms to center is typical. Servos do not average the pulse; they time it, so the information is the width, not the duty cycle. Many servos tolerate other repetition rates, but the width must be right. Analog servos typically convert width to a voltage with a one-shot timer and an RC ramp; digital servos time it with a microcontroller counter.

**2. Feedback.** The output shaft turns a potentiometer wired as a divider between supply and ground, so $V_{pot} = V_{ref}\,\theta/\theta_{max}$. An ADC (digital servos) or the analog comparator stage (analog servos) turns this into a comparable quantity.

**3. Error and drive.** Error $e = \theta_{target}-\theta_{meas}$. The controller sets motor drive $u = \text{sat}(K_p e)$ plus optionally a derivative term. The H-bridge implements it via internal PWM: duty proportional to $|e|$ up to a maximum, direction from the sign of $e$.

**4. Motor and gearbox.** Motor torque $\tau_m = K_t I$ with $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$ in steady state. A gear ratio $N$ gives output torque $\approx \eta N \tau_m$ and speed $\omega_m/N$, with efficiency $\eta$ typically well below 1 for small plastic or metal gears.

**5. Deadband.** If $|e| < e_{db}$ the drive is zero. This stops buzzing caused by sensor noise and backlash, at the cost of a resolution floor.

**6. Stall and protection.** If the load exceeds stall torque, error persists and the controller keeps full drive; current climbs to $V/R$. Some servos have no protection.

**Two PWM signals.** The 50 Hz pulse is a *command*. The motor drive PWM inside (a kilohertz range in many servos, vendor dependent) is *power*. Mixing them up is a common beginner error.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Internal PWM frequency, deadband and the precise pulse-to-angle mapping differ between servos and are often not published; digital servos typically use faster internal update rates and a smaller deadband than analog ones.
:::

::: short
"The servo measures the width of a 1 to 2 ms pulse that arrives every 20 ms and treats it as a target angle. A potentiometer on the output shaft reports the actual angle. The control chip subtracts them to get an error and drives an H-bridge so the motor turns toward the target, roughly proportional to the error, stopping inside a small deadband. A gearbox trades motor speed for torque. Push on the shaft and the error returns, so it pushes back."
:::

::: long
"I'll follow the signal. A command pulse arrives on the signal wire every 20 ms. The control chip times how long it's high, 1 to 2 ms typically, and that width becomes the target angle. The information is in the width, not in an average voltage.

On the output shaft there's a potentiometer, a voltage divider whose wiper moves with the shaft. The chip reads that as the actual angle. The difference between target and actual is the error.

Next, error becomes drive. The chip's H-bridge applies voltage to the small DC motor: sign of the error picks the direction, and the size sets how hard, normally through an internal PWM of its own. That's different from the 50 Hz command signal, which is information, not power. Far from the target the motor gets full drive and moves quickly. As the error shrinks the drive shrinks, and inside a small deadband the drive turns off completely so it doesn't jitter.

Between motor and output there's a gear train, usually a few hundred to one. The motor is a high-speed, low-torque machine; the gears turn that into the slow, strong motion you want, and they also mean the pot sees a large rotation for small motor motion, which gives good effective resolution.

Hold it against a push and you see the loop at work: the shaft moves, the pot reading changes, an error forms, and the motor pushes back. The limits are gain and damping: too much gain and the servo overshoots and hunts, too little and it's mushy, and if the load exceeds the stall torque it just sits at full current, getting hot."
:::

::: terms
- **Pulse width modulation (command)**: encoding the target in the width of a repeated pulse.
- **Frame period**: time between command pulses, about 20 ms at 50 Hz.
- **Potentiometer feedback**: resistive angle sensor on the output shaft.
- **Error amplifier**: the stage that forms target minus measured and scales it.
- **Deadband**: small error window inside which the servo does not drive the motor.
- **Gear ratio**: output torque multiplier and speed divider.
- **Saturation**: drive limited to its maximum for large error.
- **Backlash**: gear lash that creates a gap between motor motion and output motion.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer follows a single pulse all the way round the loop and quantifies it: for example "if 1000 to 2000 µs spans 180 degrees, 1 µs is 0.18 degrees, so a 5 µs deadband is about 1 degree". It distinguishes command PWM from drive PWM, mentions that the pot is on the output side so backlash is inside the loop, names saturation and the stall current, and notes that digital servos replace the analog error stage with a microcontroller and faster update rate. It also states the stability trade-off: gain versus damping versus backlash.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating the pulse as the motor voltage.** It is a command. The internal controller makes the motor drive.
- **Saying the servo averages the PWM.** It times the pulse width; duty cycle at 50 Hz is just width divided by 20 ms.
- **Forgetting the feedback is mechanical.** Without the pot on the output shaft there is no loop.
- **Ignoring backlash.** Because the sensor is after the gearbox, backlash sits inside the loop and can cause hunting.
- **Believing the motor holds position by power.** The servo holds by continually correcting small errors, so it draws current and may hum.
:::

::: deep
## The command: a pulse train

The command channel is a pulse repeated roughly every 20 ms, and the width carries the number.

```timing

```
Figure: Each character is 0.5 ms, so the frame shown is 20 ms. The three signals are three different commands: widths of 1.0, 1.5 and 2.0 ms. The servo ignores the frame period and just times how long the line stays high.

The mapping from width to angle is linear. If we assume 1000 µs maps to 0 degrees and 2000 µs to 180 degrees (an assumption; many servos span less), then

$$\theta_{target} = \frac{t_{pw} - 1000\,\mu s}{1000\,\mu s}\times 180^\circ,\qquad 1\,\mu s \leftrightarrow 0.18^\circ$$

so a 5 µs deadband would be 0.9 degrees.

## The feedback: a potentiometer

The pot is a three-terminal divider across the servo's supply. The wiper voltage is $V_{pot} = V_{cc}\,\theta/\theta_{pot}$. With a 10-bit conversion across 180 degrees, one LSB is $180/1024 = 0.176^\circ$, which is the same order as the command resolution, which is why designers rarely bother with more.

## Torque and current

For a brushed motor in steady state $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$. With $V = 6$ V, $R = 4\,\Omega$ and $K_e = 0.01$ V·s/rad, stall current is $6/4 = 1.5$ A and no-load speed is $6/0.01 = 600$ rad/s, about 5730 rpm. With a 1:300 gearbox at 70% efficiency and $K_t = 0.01$ N·m/A, stall output torque is $0.01\times1.5\times300\times0.7 = 3.15$ N·m, about 32 kg·cm. Those numbers show why small servos can lift real loads, and why a stalled one pulls an amp or more from the 5 V rail.

## The H-bridge and the second PWM

Inside the case the control IC drives the motor through an H-bridge ([[q0411]]). A common approach is to chop the bridge at a fixed frequency with duty proportional to $|e|$; the average voltage is then $D\,V_{cc}$ and the motor sees an inductive load that smooths the current. Digital servos run the loop faster (hundreds of hertz) and so can hold stiffer, but they also draw more current at standstill because they correct constantly.

## Why the pot is a limit

Potentiometers have finite resolution, nonlinearity of a few percent, wear and noise. That is why more demanding servos use hall-effect or magnetic angle sensors, and why industrial ones use optical or magnetic encoders. The control law may be the same; the sensor sets the achievable accuracy ([[q0389]]).

## Putting a number on the dynamics

Think of the servo as a second-order system. With a motor time constant much faster than the mechanical one, you can approximate it as an inertia with viscous damping from back-EMF: the back-EMF term $K_tK_e/R$ is actually free damping. That is why even $K_d = 0$ gives a stable response at low gain, and why increasing $K_p$ without adding damping makes the response ring.

::: applications
- **RC aircraft and cars**: each control surface or steering linkage has a servo reading the receiver's 50 Hz pulse train.
- **Hobby robot arms**: every joint is an RC servo or a serial-bus servo.
- **Camera pan-tilt heads**: potentiometer or encoder feedback holds the angle against wind and cable pull.
- **Industrial pick-and-place axes**: the same loop with an encoder and a faster current stage.
- **Automotive throttle bodies**: a DC motor, gear train and position sensor, closed in the ECU.
:::

::: assumptions
The description is for a standard analog or digital RC servo with potentiometer feedback and a 50 Hz command. Pulse range, angle range, deadband, supply voltage and internal PWM frequency are vendor dependent. Serial-bus smart servos replace the pulse with digital packets but have the same inner loop. Industrial servos use cascaded current, velocity and position loops with encoders and a separate drive. A servo with hall-effect or contactless sensor has no wearing pot but behaves the same way functionally. If the interviewer is thinking of a brushless industrial servo, add commutation: the drive uses the encoder angle to switch the phases.
:::

::: followups
### What does the 20 ms frame period do?
For most servos it is just a refresh interval: the servo needs a new pulse often enough to keep a valid command. Many tolerate faster or slower rates, though too slow and the servo may lose its hold or drift. The information is in the width, not the rate. Digital servos can accept higher rates and respond faster.

### Why is there a gearbox?
Small motors make torque proportional to current and are small, so they produce low torque at high speed. A gear ratio $N$ multiplies torque by about $\eta N$ and divides speed by $N$. It also reduces the inertia seen by the motor, by $N^2$, so the loop is easier to control.

### What happens if I send a pulse outside the usual 1 to 2 ms range?
Some servos clamp, some travel further (many go to 0.5 to 2.5 ms), and some hit the mechanical end stop and stall, drawing high current. Check the datasheet before sending out-of-range pulses.

### Why does a servo hum when holding a load?
Small corrections each cycle: the error alternates around the deadband boundary with noise, and the motor drives briefly in each direction. A higher-gain digital servo is more audible and draws more holding current.

### Harder: your servo twitches whenever a second motor on the same supply starts. What is happening?
The supply sags or rings on the second motor's start-up current, so the servo's pot reference (the pot is wired across the supply) moves and its internal reference changes, which looks like an angle change; the pulse train from the MCU may also glitch. Fix with separate regulation or a bulk capacitor at the servo, a common ground, and ensuring the pot and ADC share the same reference.
:::
