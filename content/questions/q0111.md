::: testing
Whether you reduce "slew rate" to its physics, $I=C\,dV/dt$, and then list levers in a structured way: **more drive current, less capacitance, smaller voltage excursion**, and the circuit tricks that deliver them (wider output devices, tapered buffer chains, higher supply, lower load). The interviewer also wants to hear the **cost of speed**: ground bounce, EMI, overshoot, shoot-through, power. A good answer ends with "and I'd only go as fast as the system needs".
:::

::: why
Edge rate decides whether a bus meets timing, whether a gate driver switches a power FET efficiently, and whether your board passes EMC. In CMOS digital design it's the buffer-sizing problem; in board design it's output-driver strength and series termination. Everything reduces to the same capacitor equation, so the answer transfers to amplifiers ([[q0117]]) and power switches.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor equation**: $I=C\,dV/dt$, so slew rate $=I/C$. See [[q0022]].
- **On-resistance and $RC$ rise time**: $t_r\approx2.2R_{on}C_L$ for a 10-90% edge.
- **Transistor drive**: $I_D\propto(W/L)(V_{GS}-V_t)^2$. See [[q0103]].
- **Fan-out and tapered buffers**: each stage drives the next with a gain of about 3-4.
- **Shoot-through and ground bounce**: the price of fast edges. See [[q0109]].
- **Slew in amplifiers**: $SR=I_{tail}/C_c$. See [[q0117]].
:::

::: answer
"Slew rate is just how fast the output voltage can change, and for a capacitive load that's current over capacitance: $dV/dt=I/C$. So to get a faster edge I either push more current into the load, reduce the capacitance, or reduce the voltage that has to move.

For more current, I make the output transistors wider, since drive current scales with W/L, which lowers the on-resistance and the $RC$ time constant. I can raise the supply voltage or gate drive so there's more overdrive, since the saturation current goes with overdrive squared. And I can drive those big output transistors with a tapered chain of inverters, each three or four times bigger than the last, because a tiny gate can't drive a huge one quickly.

For less capacitance, I shorten the trace, reduce fan-out, pick a smaller package or pin load, and use a smaller device if I can. For a smaller swing I use a lower-swing or differential standard.

Speed has a cost though: bigger edges draw peak current spikes, cause ground bounce and ringing, increase EMI and crosstalk, and wide devices increase power and shoot-through. So I'd pick the slowest edge that meets timing, often with a series resistor to tame it."
:::

::: technical
**Basic relations.**
$$\text{SR}=\frac{dV}{dt}\approx\frac{I_{drive}}{C_L},\qquad t_r(10\text{-}90\%)\approx2.2\,R_{on}C_L$$
with $R_{on}\approx\dfrac{1}{k_nV_{ov}}$ for the NMOS pull-down (or $k_p$ for the PMOS pull-up), $k=\mu C_{ox}W/L$.

**Levers**
1. **Width of the output stage**: $R_{on}\propto L/W$. Example: $C_L=50$ pF, 3.3 V swing; a 2 ns edge needs an average current $C\,\Delta V/t=50\text{ pF}\times3.3/2\text{ ns}=82.5$ mA and $R_{on}=t_r/(2.2C_L)=18$ Ω. A 50 Ω driver gives $t_r=2.2\cdot50\cdot50\text{ pF}=5.5$ ns.
2. **Supply / overdrive**: in saturation $I\propto(V_{DD}-V_t)^2$; raising $V_{DD}$ from 1.8 V to 3.3 V with $V_t=0.5$ V gives $(2.8/1.3)^2=4.6\times$ the current, so $dV/dt=I/C$ rises 4.6x, but the swing is also 1.83x larger, so the transition time only improves about $4.6/1.83\approx2.5\times$ while the energy per edge rises 3.4x.
3. **Tapered buffer chain.** To drive $C_L$ from a minimum-size input of capacitance $C_{in}$, total effort $F=C_L/C_{in}$. With $N$ stages, delay is minimized near $f=F^{1/N}\approx e$ (theory) and about 3-4 in practice with parasitics. For $C_{in}=5$ fF and $C_L=50$ pF: $F=10^4$, $N=\ln F/\ln4=6.6\approx7$ stages. Delay in units of inverter delay: direct drive $\sim F=10^4$; chain $\sim N\cdot f\approx27$ at $f=4$. Roughly 375x faster.
4. **Reduce $C_L$**: board trace, pin and package capacitance, input capacitance of loads, junction capacitance.
5. **Reduce the swing**: $t\propto\Delta V$ at fixed current. LVDS, SSTL and other reduced-swing standards.
6. **Shorter $L$** (process choice) and low-$V_t$ devices.
7. **Remove series resistance**: gate-drive path, output resistor, bond wires.

**Costs.** Peak edge current $\approx C\,dV/dt$ flows through supply inductance, giving $L\,di/dt$ noise (ground bounce). Faster edges increase high-frequency content: spectral corner about $1/(\pi t_r)$ (about 160 MHz for 2 ns). Ringing from $L$ and $C$ parasitics, crosstalk, EMI, and short-circuit current during gate transitions. See [[q0118]].

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The optimum fan-out per stage (about 3-4) depends on the process's ratio of parasitic to gate capacitance; some libraries use 4 as the standard (the FO4 delay metric). Treat it as a rule of thumb.
:::

::: short
"Slew rate is $I/C$, so to go faster I increase drive current, reduce capacitance, or reduce swing. In CMOS that means wider output transistors for lower on-resistance, more gate overdrive or a higher supply, and a tapered chain of inverters, each about 3 to 4 times larger than the last, to drive big output devices. I'd also cut the load capacitance by shortening traces and reducing fan-out. The trade-off is peak current, ground bounce, ringing and EMI, so I'd choose the slowest edge that meets timing."
:::

::: long
"Everything about slew rate comes from the capacitor equation: $I=C\,dV/dt$. So the slew rate of an output driving a capacitive load is the current the driver can supply divided by the load capacitance. To make it faster there are three directions: more current, less capacitance, less voltage to move.

More current first. The output transistor's current scales with W/L and with overdrive squared. So I make the output stage wider, which lowers its on-resistance and shortens the $RC$ time, with the rule that a 10-90% rise time is about 2.2 times $R_{on}C_L$. For a 50 pF load and a 2 ns edge I'd want around 18 ohms, whereas a 50 ohm driver gives 5.5 ns. I can also raise the supply or the gate drive for more overdrive.

But a big output transistor has a big gate, and the previous stage can't drive it quickly. So I use a tapered buffer chain, where each inverter is about 3 to 4 times bigger than the one before it. For something like a 10,000-to-1 capacitance ratio that's about 7 stages, and it's hundreds of times faster than driving directly from a minimum-size gate.

Less capacitance: shorter traces, fewer loads, smaller packages, lower pin capacitance. Less swing: reduced-swing signalling like LVDS moves fewer volts.

And the cost: faster edges mean larger peak currents, so ground bounce and supply droop, more EMI and crosstalk, ringing, and possibly shoot-through in the driver. Often I add a series resistor to slow things down on purpose. So I'd go as fast as the timing needs and no faster."
:::

::: terms
- **Slew rate (SR)**: maximum rate of change of output voltage, $dV/dt$.
- **Rise time ($t_r$)**: time for the output to go from 10% to 90% of the swing.
- **$R_{on}C$ time constant**: driver resistance times load capacitance; $t_r\approx2.2RC$.
- **Fan-out**: the ratio of load capacitance to input capacitance of a stage.
- **Tapered buffer (buffer chain)**: sequence of inverters increasing in size by a constant factor.
- **FO4 delay**: delay of an inverter driving four identical inverters; a process speed metric.
- **Ground bounce**: supply/ground voltage shifts caused by $L\,di/dt$ from switching currents.
- **Overdrive**: $V_{GS}-V_t$ of the driver.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer lists "bigger transistors, higher voltage." An exceptional answer:

- Starts from $I=C\,dV/dt$ and gives the three families of levers.
- **Quantifies**: current needed for a given edge, required $R_{on}$, the buffer-chain stage count with $F$ and $N$.
- Explains **why a tapered chain** is needed and what limits stage ratio.
- Names the costs (di/dt noise, EMI, ringing, shoot-through, power) and the countermeasures (series resistor, slew-rate-controlled drivers, spread of edges).
- Mentions that **slew can also be limited by the previous stage or the supply**, and relates to amplifier slew ($I_{tail}/C_c$).
- Frames the engineering judgement: the right edge rate is the slowest that meets timing.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Only widening the output transistor.** A huge device with a weak predriver is slow; the chain matters.
- **Believing faster is free.** Edge rate sets spectral content; 1 ns edges radiate far more than 10 ns edges for the same signal.
- **Ignoring the load.** Tracing the problem to the driver when the real culprit is 100 pF of cable and connector.
- **Mixing up rise time and slew rate.** Slew rate is $dV/dt$ (V/ns); rise time is the time to traverse 10-90% of the swing.
- **Raising supply voltage without checking oxide ratings** or noting it also raises power as $V^2$.
- **Using a pull-up resistor for speed.** A weak resistive pull-up gives $RC$ rise times; a push-pull stage or a stronger pull-up helps.
:::

::: deep
## Where the time goes

A CMOS output driving a capacitive load is an RC circuit. When the PMOS turns on for a rising edge, the load voltage follows $v(t)=V_{DD}(1-e^{-t/R_{on}C_L})$. Its 10-90% rise time is $R_{on}C_L\ln9=2.2R_{on}C_L$.

At the beginning the PMOS may be in saturation and acts as a current source, so the first part of the edge is *slew-limited* (constant current, linear ramp) and the end is *RC-limited* (exponential). Large devices with large overdrive spend less time in the slew phase.

## Lever 1: Drive strength

For the NMOS pull-down, $R_{on}\approx1/(k'(W/L)V_{ov})$ ([[q0103]]). Doubling $W$ halves $R_{on}$ and the rise time, but also doubles the drain junction capacitance (part of $C_L$, small) and, crucially, the gate capacitance the previous stage must drive.

## Lever 2: The tapered buffer chain

An inverter has gate input capacitance $C_g$ and output resistance $R_{out}\propto1/W\propto1/C_g$. Driving a load $C_L$ directly from a small gate gives a time constant $\propto C_L/C_g$, which is huge when the ratio is large. Use $N$ stages, each $f$ times bigger than the last:

$$f=\left(\frac{C_L}{C_{in}}\right)^{1/N},\qquad t_{total}\approx N\,(f+p)\,\tau_{inv}$$

where $p$ accounts for the self-loading parasitics. Ignoring $p$, $N f$ is minimized at $f=e$; with realistic parasitics ($p\approx1$) the optimum is closer to $f\approx3.6$, and choosing 4 is nearly flat.

Example: input capacitance 5 fF (a minimum inverter), load 50 pF: $F=10^4$, $N=\ln(10^4)/\ln4=6.6$, round to 7 stages (or 6 if you need the right polarity, with $f=4.6$). Each stage dissipates its own $CV^2f$, but the final stage dominates: the total is about $1/(f-1)$ more than the last stage alone (geometric series), about 33% extra.

## Lever 3: Overdrive and supply

$I_{sat}\propto(V_{DD}-V_t)^2$. Raising $V_{DD}$ from 1.8 V to 3.3 V with $V_t=0.5$ V raises current $4.6\times$, so $dV/dt=I/C$ rises $4.6\times$, but the swing is $1.83\times$ larger, so the time to complete the edge falls only about $2.5\times$, and power rises $1.83^2=3.4\times$. Voltage is a costly speed knob. Lowering $V_t$ is cheaper in power but raises leakage ([[q0110]]).

## Lever 4: Lower the load capacitance and swing

$C_L$ contributions: input capacitance of each receiver (3-10 pF per CMOS input pin), connector and cable ($\sim$100 pF/m for typical cable), trace (on the order of 1 pF per inch, depending on the stackup), and driver output capacitance. Reducing a 50 pF load to 20 pF gives a 2.5x speed-up at no cost. Reduced-swing and differential signalling (LVDS: about 350 mV swing) moves less voltage for a given current.

## The cost: what faster edges do

- **Ground bounce / supply droop.** A 3.3 V, 2 ns edge into 50 pF needs about 82 mA average (peak maybe 2x). Eight such outputs switching together draw about 0.66 A in 2 ns; through 2 nH of package inductance that is $L\,\Delta I/\Delta t\approx2\text{ nH}\times0.66/2\text{ ns}=0.66$ V of bounce. The ground-bounce simulator demonstrates this.
- **EMI.** The spectrum of a trapezoid rolls off beyond $1/(\pi t_r)$; halving $t_r$ pushes harmonics twice as high, where traces and cables radiate efficiently.
- **Reflections and ringing**: edges shorter than twice the line delay need termination ([[q0113]] discusses waveforms; transmission-line sections cover the rest).
- **Shoot-through / short-circuit current** in the driver's own stages ([[q0109]]) and extra power.

## Amplifier slew

For an op-amp the same equation appears as $SR=I_{tail}/C_c$: more tail current or a smaller compensation capacitor gives faster slew, at the cost of power and stability ([[q0117]]). A class-AB output stage supplies large load current, so output slew into $C_L$ is also $I_{out,max}/C_L$.

## Real hardware

- **Selectable drive strength / slew control** registers in MCU GPIO: choose the slowest that meets timing.
- **Series termination resistors** (22-33 Ω) at the driver reduce edge rate and ringing at the cost of delay.
- **Gate drivers** for power MOSFETs use the same ideas: strong output stage, tapered predrivers, with gate resistors chosen deliberately to slow edges.
- **Output buffers on ICs** are designed as tapered chains with a final stage of tens to thousands of micrometres of width.
:::

::: applications
- **GPIO and bus drivers**: selectable drive strength to meet timing while passing EMC tests.
- **Clock buffers and distribution**: tapered chains driving large clock-tree capacitance.
- **MOSFET gate drivers**: peak source/sink current sets the switching time ($t\approx Q_g/I$).
- **Memory and high-speed interfaces**: reduced-swing, terminated I/O for speed at low power.
- **Line drivers (RS-485, CAN)**: slew-rate-limited drivers to reduce EMI at long cable lengths.
- **Op-amp selection**: choosing slew rate against signal amplitude and frequency ([[q0117]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Capacitive load** (lumped); a transmission line sees $Z_0$ not $C$ until the round trip is complete.
- **Digital CMOS driver** with a long-channel $R_{on}$ model; velocity saturation limits benefits of ever wider devices and higher voltage.
- **First-order stage model** for buffer sizing (logical effort): real optimum depends on parasitics.
- If the question concerns an **op-amp output**, the relevant relation is $SR=I/C_c$ ([[q0117]]); this page treats the CMOS digital/driver reading, and mentions the amplifier reading briefly.
:::

::: followups
### If the edge is too slow, which would you change first: supply voltage or driver width?
Driver width (or a stronger drive setting). It costs only area and some power ($\propto W$), whereas raising $V_{DD}$ costs power as $V^2$, stresses the oxide and changes the logic levels of the whole system.

### Why is $2.2RC$ the 10-90% rise time?
$v=V(1-e^{-t/\tau})$. At 10%, $t_1=\tau\ln(1/0.9)=0.105\tau$; at 90%, $t_2=\tau\ln10=2.303\tau$. The difference is $2.197\tau\approx2.2\tau$.

### How many stages and what ratio to drive 100 pF from a 10 fF gate?
$F=10^4$ again, so about 7 stages at $f\approx3.7$, or 6 at 4.6. Using 4 per stage gives 6.6 stages. If the polarity must be preserved you need an even number: 6 or 8.

### How do you speed up a rising edge on an open-drain bus (I²C)?
The edge is $RC$ limited by the pull-up resistor, so lower $R_p$ (limited by the 3 mA sink spec), reduce bus capacitance, or use an active pull-up (current-source boosters). The falling edge is already fast because the open-drain FET is strong. See the I²C pull-up simulator.

### Harder: edges got faster after a die shrink and now the product fails EMC. What do you do?
Slow the output edges deliberately: choose lower drive strength, add series resistors (22-100 Ω) at the source, use spread-spectrum clocking where allowed, add ferrite beads or filters, and shorten return paths. Reducing $dV/dt$ and $di/dt$ lowers the harmonic content above $1/(\pi t_r)$.

### Scenario: your MCU's SPI clock to a flash looks fine at 4 MHz but corrupts at 40 MHz on a 15 cm cable. What would you try?
The trace and cable add capacitance and inductance, so the edge slows (RC) and rings. Check the edge on the scope with a good probe, set the GPIO to high drive strength, shorten the cable, add a series source resistor (about $Z_0-R_{drv}$), and keep a solid return path. If the edge is too slow, reduce $C$; if it rings, slow it down.
:::
