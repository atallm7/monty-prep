::: basics
### In plain words
An inductor is a coil that resists sudden changes in current. It does not cap how big the current gets, and it does not hold the current at zero for long. It only controls how fast the current can change. So a motor's starting surge is not a contradiction: the current does start at zero and rise smoothly, but it rises very fast, to a very large value.

### Picture it
Think of a heavy flywheel on a car engine. It cannot jump to full speed instantly, so it takes a moment to get going. But it does not decide the top speed. The engine does. The inductor is the flywheel: it sets how fast the current ramps. The winding resistance and the missing back-EMF set the final value. The analogy has one gap: the electrical ramp is far faster than anything mechanical.

### Words you need
- **Inductance ($L$)**: how strongly a coil resists changes in current, in henries.
- **Time constant ($L/R$)**: the time scale of the current's rise.
- **Back-EMF**: the voltage a spinning motor makes against the supply. See [[q0341]].
- **Inrush**: the large current right after turn-on. See [[q0343]].
- **Mechanical time constant**: how long the rotor takes to speed up.

### The simplest example
A 12 V motor has a 2 Ω winding and 1 mH of inductance. The final current at standstill is $V/R$ = 12 V / 2 Ω = **6 A**. The time constant is 1 mH / 2 Ω = 0.5 ms. So the current climbs to about 63% of 6 A in half a millisecond. The rotor needs milliseconds to tens of milliseconds to build back-EMF, so the current reaches nearly 6 A first.

That is the whole idea: the inductor sets the ramp rate, and the resistance sets the peak. The rest of this page shows the $v = L\,di/dt$ math and why the inductor only rounds the front edge of the inrush.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand what the inductor law actually says. "Inductors oppose changes in current" is a statement about the **slope** $di/dt$, not about the **size** of the current. The interviewer wants to hear you separate three things that students blur together: continuity (current cannot jump), rate ($di/dt = v_L/L$) and final value (set by resistance and back-EMF, not by $L$). They are also checking that you can put numbers on "fast": comparing the electrical time constant $L/R$ with the mechanical one, and noticing that a stalled rotor has no back-EMF to help.
:::

::: why
Every motor, solenoid and relay driver you design will see this. The inrush is what browns out a battery, trips a current limit, welds a relay contact or blows a gate-driver fuse. If you believe "inductors limit current" you will under-size the driver and the supply. Knowing that the inductor only buys you a few hundred microseconds (and then Ohm's law takes over) tells you where to put soft-start, current limiting and bulk capacitance. It also connects to inductive kick ([[q0340]]) because both come from the same equation, $v = L\,di/dt$.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor law**: $v_L = L\,\frac{di}{dt}$. Voltage across the coil sets the *slope* of the current. See [[q0041]].
- **Continuity**: current through an inductor cannot change instantaneously (that would need infinite voltage). See [[q0043]].
- **RL time constant**: $\tau_e = L/R$, the time for the current to get 63% of the way to $V/R$. See [[q0044]].
- **Back-EMF**: a spinning motor generates $E = K_e\omega$ opposing the supply, zero at standstill. See [[q0341]].
- **Stall / locked-rotor current**: $V/R$, the largest steady current the winding can draw from a given voltage. See [[q0342]].
- **Electrical vs mechanical time constants**: $\tau_e \ll \tau_m$ in most small motors, so the current peaks before the rotor has moved much.
:::

::: answer
"I'd say the question mixes up two things. An inductor doesn't limit how *big* the current gets, it limits how *fast* it can change. The law is $v = L\,di/dt$, so the current can't jump, it ramps at $V/L$. That's still a ramp, and for a motor winding $L$ is tiny. A small 12 V motor with 1 mH has an initial slope of 12 volts over a millihenry, which is 12 amps per millisecond.

The final value isn't set by the inductor at all. At standstill there's no back-EMF, so the only thing limiting current is the winding resistance, $V/R$. With 2 ohms that's 6 amps, versus a few hundred milliamps once it's spinning. The time to get there is $L/R$, here half a millisecond.

So it's not a contradiction. The current is continuous, it starts at zero, but on the scale of the electronics it rises extremely fast, to a value that's many times the running current. And the rotor is far slower than that: its mechanical time constant is milliseconds to tens of milliseconds, so the back-EMF that would rein the current in hasn't built up yet. That's the inrush. The inductor just rounds the very front edge of it."
:::

::: technical
**Governing equations** (brushed PM motor, linear model):
$$L\frac{di}{dt} = V - iR - K_e\omega,\qquad J\frac{d\omega}{dt} = K_t i - T_{load}$$

**At $t = 0^+$**: $i = 0$ (continuity), $\omega = 0$ (inertia), so $E = 0$ and $\frac{di}{dt}\Big|_{0^+} = \frac{V}{L}$.

**Locked rotor**: $\omega$ stays 0, so $i(t) = \frac{V}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_e}\right)$ with $\tau_e = L/R$. Final value $I_{stall} = V/R$.

**Free rotor**: eliminate $\omega$ (with $T_{load}=0$) to get a second-order system
$$s^2 + \frac{R}{L}s + \frac{K_eK_t}{LJ} = 0.$$
If the roots are real and well separated ($\tau_m \gg \tau_e$) the current rises with $\tau_e$ and then decays with $\tau_m$. The peak is somewhat below $V/R$ and occurs near a few $\tau_e$. Electrical and mechanical time constants are
$$\tau_e = \frac{L}{R},\qquad \tau_m \approx \frac{JR}{K_eK_t}.$$

**Example numbers** ($V=12$ V, $R=2\ \Omega$, $L=1$ mH, $K_e=K_t=0.02$, $J=10^{-6}$ kg·m²): $\tau_e = 0.5$ ms, $\tau_m = 5$ ms, initial slope 12 A/ms, stall current 6 A, free-running peak about 5.0 A at 1.3 ms, no-load running current 0.1 A (friction torque 2 mN·m). The inrush is therefore about 50 times the running current.

**Why "fast and large"**
- *Fast*: $L$ is small (µH to mH) and $R$ is small, so $L/R$ is sub-millisecond to a few milliseconds.
- *Large*: the limit is $V/R$, not anything involving $L$; $E=0$ at standstill.
- *Not instantaneous*: the first microseconds are limited by $L$ and by the source/wiring (battery internal resistance, cable inductance, bulk-capacitor ESR).

**Solenoids**: same $V/R$ final value, but $L$ rises as the plunger closes, so the current shows a characteristic dip during travel (motional back-EMF $i\,\frac{dL}{dx}v$) and then climbs to $V/R$. Time constants are tens of ms for large coils.
:::

::: long
"The inductor equation is $v = L\,di/dt$. That means the current through an inductor is continuous, it can't jump, but it says nothing about how big the current can get. The inductance only sets the *rate*: the current ramps at $V/L$. If the inductance is small, that ramp is steep.

Take a typical small brushed motor: 12 volts, a winding resistance of 2 ohms and an inductance of about a millihenry. At the instant I connect it the current is zero, so there's no drop across the resistor, and the full 12 volts is across the inductance. The slope is 12 volts over 1 millihenry, 12 amps per millisecond. Then the current approaches $V/R$, which is 6 amps, with a time constant $L/R$ of half a millisecond. So within a couple of milliseconds you've basically hit stall current.

Why doesn't something stop it? Because the thing that normally limits a running motor is back-EMF, $K_e\omega$, and at standstill that's zero. The rotor has inertia. Its mechanical time constant is $JR/(K_eK_t)$, which for this motor is around 5 milliseconds, ten times slower than the electrical one. So the current peaks, around 5 amps here, before the rotor has built much speed. Once it's spinning, back-EMF rises, the current falls to maybe 100 milliamps, and the motor settles.

The practical consequences: the supply sees a current step 50 times the running current. I'd size the driver and the source for the stall current, add bulk capacitance close to the driver, and use soft-start, PWM ramping or a current limit if the supply can't take it. Solenoids do the same thing, with a bigger time constant because their inductance is higher."
:::

::: terms
- **Inrush current**: the large transient current drawn when a load is first energised. For motors it is up to $V/R$ because back-EMF is zero. See [[q0343]].
- **Continuity condition**: inductor current cannot change instantaneously; capacitor voltage cannot either.
- **Electrical time constant $\tau_e$**: $L/R$, the RL charging time constant.
- **Mechanical time constant $\tau_m$**: roughly $JR/(K_eK_t)$, the time for the rotor speed to reach 63% of its final value at no load.
- **Back-EMF**: the voltage $K_e\omega$ the rotating armature generates, opposing the supply. See [[q0341]].
- **Stall (locked-rotor) current**: $V/R$, the current when the shaft is held still.
- **$di/dt$**: slope of current. It is limited to $V_L/L$ for an inductor.
- **Soft start**: ramping the voltage (usually PWM duty) so inrush is limited.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "the inductor opposes the change, but the resistance is low so the current still gets big". An exceptional answer:

- States clearly that $L$ limits **slope**, not **magnitude**, and that the final magnitude is $V/R$ with $E=0$.
- Gives the numbers: $\tau_e = L/R$ vs $\tau_m$, and why $\tau_e \ll \tau_m$ lets the inrush peak before back-EMF builds.
- Says the current is *continuous* at $t=0$, so there is no contradiction, and the real inrush starts at zero.
- Notes the source also limits things (battery resistance, bulk capacitor ESR), and offers mitigation: soft start, current limit, NTC, bulk C.
- Mentions solenoids as the variant with changing inductance and a current dip.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Inductors limit current."** They limit *rate of change*. At DC steady state an ideal inductor is a short and the resistance alone limits the current. See [[q0044]].
- **Calling the inrush "instantaneous."** It starts at zero and ramps over $\sim L/R$. On a fast scope you can see the ramp.
- **Forgetting back-EMF is zero at standstill**, so using the *running* current to size the driver.
- **Mixing up $\tau_e$ and $\tau_m$.** One is $L/R$ (electrical), the other is $JR/(K_eK_t)$ (mechanical). The inrush happens because the first is shorter than the second.
- **Assuming the supply can deliver $V/R$.** A real battery sags (internal resistance) and the supply may current-limit, lowering the peak and stretching the rise.
- **Thinking a bigger inductor fixes it.** A bigger $L$ only delays the rise; the final value is unchanged unless you add resistance, a current limit or reduce the voltage.
:::

::: deep
## The inductor law says slope, not size

Faraday's law gives the voltage across a coil as $v = L\,di/dt$. Rearranged: $di/dt = v/L$. Two consequences:

1. If the current were to jump by $\Delta i$ in zero time, $di/dt \to \infty$ and so would $v$. A real source can't supply that, so current is **continuous**.
2. For a *finite* voltage, the current changes at a finite rate. It says nothing about the value the current is heading towards.

The final value comes from the rest of the circuit. At DC steady state $di/dt = 0$ so $v_L = 0$: the coil is a plain resistor $R$ plus the back-EMF. That gives
$$I_{final} = \frac{V - E}{R}.$$
At standstill $E = 0$, so $I_{final} = V/R$. This is the **stall** (locked-rotor) current ([[q0342]]).

## RL step response: how fast is "fast"?

With the rotor locked (or a solenoid with its plunger fixed), $L\,di/dt + iR = V$ and
$$i(t) = \frac{V}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau_e}\right),\qquad \tau_e = \frac{L}{R}.$$

For our example motor ($V=12$ V, $R=2\ \Omega$, $L=1$ mH):

- Initial slope: $V/L = 12{,}000$ A/s = 12 A/ms.
- $\tau_e = 0.5$ ms. 90% of final value at $\tau_e\ln 10 = 1.15$ ms.
- Final: 6 A.

> "Fast" is relative. With a 100 mH industrial solenoid and 20 Ω, $\tau_e = 5$ ms, quite slow for electronics but still much faster than a human noticing. For a PCB-level motor, $\tau_e$ is hundreds of microseconds.

## Why a spinning motor is different: back-EMF and the mechanical clock

A real motor has two coupled first-order systems. The electrical part obeys $L\,di/dt = V - iR - K_e\omega$. The mechanical part obeys $J\,d\omega/dt = K_t i - T_{load}$. Back-EMF closes the loop: more speed, more opposing voltage, less current.

For $T_{load}=0$ the two combine into the second-order equation seen in the technical section. With separated time constants the current behaves like the difference of two exponentials:
$$i(t) = \frac{V}{L}\,\frac{e^{p_1 t} - e^{p_2 t}}{p_1 - p_2},\qquad p_{1,2} = \frac{-R/L \pm \sqrt{(R/L)^2 - 4K_eK_t/(LJ)}}{2}.$$
For our numbers, $p_1 = -225\ \text{s}^{-1}$ (time constant 4.4 ms, mechanical) and $p_2 = -1775\ \text{s}^{-1}$ (0.56 ms, electrical).

## The ratio that decides the shape: $\tau_m / \tau_e$

$$\frac{\tau_m}{\tau_e} = \frac{J R/(K_eK_t)}{L/R} = \frac{J R^2}{K_eK_t L}$$

Here it is 10. A ratio much greater than 1 means the electrical transient finishes long before the rotor moves, so the current reaches nearly $V/R$ (more precisely the peak is limited by how much speed has built during the rise). The roots are real only if $\tau_m/\tau_e > 4$ (that is the discriminant condition). Below 4 the poles are complex and the current and speed ring, which is unusual for small brushed motors but possible for large-$L$, low-inertia machines.

Large, heavy-rotor motors have long $\tau_m$ (hundreds of ms), so the inrush hits full stall value and stays a long time. That is why big motors use star-delta, soft starters or VFDs, and why a high-inertia fan on a small supply can brown it out.

## The source matters too

The equations assume an ideal voltage source. In real hardware the first microseconds are shaped by:

- **Source resistance and cable inductance.** A Li-ion pack with 100 mΩ of internal resistance supplying a 6 A step sags 0.6 V. A thin 30 cm lead adds a few hundred nH.
- **Bulk capacitor.** Close to the driver, a capacitor delivers the initial surge so the battery sees a slower ramp. The capacitor sees the full $di/dt$ and must have low ESR.
- **Driver current limit.** Many motor drivers current-limit (chopping at an I_LIM threshold), which truncates the spike. The motor then ramps with controlled current, giving torque limit during start.

## Solenoids: the same story with a twist

A solenoid coil has resistance $R$ and an inductance that depends on plunger position. With the plunger out, the air gap is large and $L$ is small, so the current rises quickly with small $\tau$. As the plunger moves in, the magnetic circuit closes, $L$ increases, and a **motional back-EMF** $e = i\,\frac{dL}{dx}\,v$ opposes the supply, causing the current to *dip* mid-stroke. When the plunger seats, $L$ is at maximum, and the current climbs again to the final value $V/R$. This dip is a handy signature: sensing the current waveform tells you the plunger has arrived (used in "stall detection" in some solenoid valves).

## What to do about it

1. **Spec the driver and supply for stall current**, not running current, or add a current limit and spec for the torque you want at start.
2. **Soft start**: ramp PWM duty from 0 to the target over tens of ms. At duty $D$, average voltage is $DV$ so stall current is $DV/R$.
3. **Bulk capacitance** next to the H-bridge: size it from $C \approx I\,\Delta t/\Delta V$. If the capacitor must cover a 5 A step for the 100 µs the cable and regulator need to catch up, with 0.3 V allowed droop, $C \approx 5\times10^{-4}/0.3 \approx 1.7$ mF is the worst case; in practice a few hundred µF plus a ceramic is common, and you check the droop on a scope.
4. **Series resistance or NTC** for AC machines and some DC supplies.
5. **Protect against the opposite end**: when the switch opens, the same inductance produces the kick ([[q0340]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Battery-powered robots and drones**: motor start inrush causes brownouts of the MCU rail; fix with bulk capacitors, soft-start PWM ramps and separate regulators.
- **Hobby servo stalls**: when a servo is blocked, its driver is effectively at stall current, and cheap supplies collapse.
- **Automotive starter motors**: cranking draws hundreds of amps from the battery, and the battery voltage sags visibly (the "dimming lights" effect).
- **Solenoid valve and relay drivers**: peak-and-hold drive reduces holding current once the plunger has closed ([[q0347]]).
- **Industrial fans and pumps**: soft starters and VFDs ramp voltage and frequency to avoid mechanical and electrical shocks.
- **Current-sense-based stall detection**: a window comparator on a shunt notes the inrush spike and the settled current to detect jams.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear motor model** with constant $R$, $L$, $K_e=K_t$ and viscous/constant friction. Real windings heat up (copper ~0.4%/°C), so $R$ rises and the current drops as the motor warms.
- **Ideal voltage source.** A real source with internal resistance reduces the peak; a current-limited source truncates it.
- **Fixed-shaft vs free-shaft** are the two bounding cases; real starts lie in between depending on load inertia and friction.
- **Brush contact drop** (roughly 1 V or so in total for carbon brushes, see [[q0357]]) slightly reduces the initial current; ignored here.
- **Saturation**: a motor with saturated iron has $L$ falling at high current, which makes the rise faster than the linear model predicts.
- Alternative answer: "because the inductor *resists* but doesn't *stop* the change, and its resistance is low" is acceptable if you add the $V/L$ and $V/R$ numbers.
:::

::: followups
### So does the inductor do anything useful at start-up?
Yes: it sets a finite initial slope, so there is no true instantaneous spike, and it filters PWM ripple during normal running. In this example it delays the 6 A stall level by about 1 ms. With a small $L$ the effect is minor; with a big $L$ (a large solenoid, a 100 mH coil) the delay can be tens of milliseconds and you can often keep the supply from seeing a hard edge.

### How big is the inrush compared to running current, and how would you measure it?
Ratio $= \frac{V/R}{(T_{load}/K_t)}$; for our motor at no load 6 A / 0.1 A = 60. Measure with a low-value shunt (10–50 mΩ) or a current probe, triggering the scope on the rising edge of the supply and using single-shot capture; watch for the current limit of the driver truncating it.

### How would you limit it without hurting performance?
Ramp the PWM duty, use a driver current limit set to the torque you actually need, and add bulk capacitors. A fixed series resistor wastes power continuously, so it is used only on small, low-cost designs. NTC thermistors (cold resistance high, hot low) are used on AC and larger DC supplies.

### Why does a stalled motor overheat?
Stall means $E=0$, so all electrical input goes to $I^2R$: for 12 V, 2 Ω, that's $6^2\times 2 = 72$ W in a winding rated for maybe a few watts continuous. Temperature rise follows $P R_{th}$; windings fail in seconds to minutes. Hence stall protection and current limits.

### What if the supply cannot deliver the full $V/R$?
Then the supply voltage sags (or it enters current limit), the current plateaus at $I_{lim}$ or follows the source's load line, the start-up torque is reduced, and the MCU supply might brown out if shared. The inrush still exists but it's shaped by the source impedance: $I \approx V_{oc}/(R + R_{source})$.

### A solenoid's current dips partway through the pull-in. Why?
As the plunger moves in, $L$ increases with position, so $d(Li)/dt = L\,di/dt + i\,\frac{dL}{dx}v$. The second term acts like a back-EMF and subtracts from the supply voltage, so $di/dt$ becomes negative for a moment. When the plunger seats, $v = 0$ and the current resumes its rise to $V/R$.
:::

The intended resolution is the one above: opposition to *change* is not opposition to *magnitude*. Adjacent questions cover related ground: [[q0343]] (what inrush is), [[q0344]] and [[q0336]] (when the draw is largest), and [[q0342]] (the 12 V / 1 Ω worked example). This page is specifically the "why doesn't the inductance stop it?" argument.
