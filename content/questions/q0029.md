::: testing
This question sits under *Capacitors* but asks about an **inductor**, so the interviewer is also checking whether you read carefully. They want:

1. The ideal limits for the inductor: **short circuit at DC** ($Z=\omega L\to0$) and **open circuit at infinite frequency** ($Z\to\infty$).
2. A correct sketch: a straight line of slope $+20$ dB/decade on log-log axes.
3. The reasoning in the time domain: $v=L\,di/dt$ ties the two limits to current continuity ([[q0043]]).
4. Real-world awareness: winding capacitance makes the real curve peak at the self-resonant frequency and then fall, so the real inductor looks like a *capacitor* at very high frequency.
5. Ideally, the capacitor counterpart (opposite limits), since this is the capacitor section.
:::

::: why
"What does this part do at the extremes?" is the quickest way to analyze a circuit: replace each inductor with a short and each capacitor with an open for DC, and the opposite for very fast edges. It sets initial and final conditions of every transient, tells you which filter you're building, and is the foundation for choosing chokes and decoupling capacitors that actually work at the frequencies where the noise is. The "real" curve is where interviews separate textbook answers from engineering answers.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor impedance** ([[q0040]]): $Z_L=j\omega L$; magnitude $\omega L$ grows linearly with frequency.
- **Capacitor impedance** ([[q0024]]): $Z_C=1/(j\omega C)$; magnitude falls as $1/\omega$.
- **Inductor differential equation** ([[q0041]]): $v=L\,di/dt$; a fast change in $i$ needs huge $v$.
- **DC steady state** ([[q0044]]): current constant, so $di/dt=0$ and $v=0$: a short.
- **Parasitics and self-resonance** ([[q0047]], [[q0031]]): winding capacitance and ESL limit the ideal behavior.
- **Log-log (Bode) magnitude plots**: a $+1$ slope on $|Z|$ is $+20$ dB/decade.
:::

::: answer
"For an ideal inductor, impedance is j omega L. At DC, omega is zero, so the impedance is zero: it's a short circuit, just a piece of wire, and a constant current produces no voltage. As the frequency goes up, the impedance rises linearly, and as the frequency goes to infinity it goes to infinity, so it's an open circuit. That matches the time-domain picture: v equals L di/dt, so to change the current quickly the inductor needs a huge voltage, which means it resists any very fast change, and at infinite frequency it behaves like an open.

On a log-log plot that's a straight line with a slope of plus 20 dB per decade, passing through 2 pi times L ohms at 1 Hz.

I'd add that this is the ideal behavior. A real inductor has winding capacitance in parallel, so the curve rises up to the self-resonant frequency, peaks, and then falls like a capacitor, so at very high frequency it's effectively a capacitor, not an open circuit. And since this is in the capacitor section, the capacitor is the mirror image: open circuit at DC and a short at infinite frequency, with impedance falling at minus 20 dB per decade, until ESL turns it back up into an inductor."
:::

::: technical
**Ideal inductor:** $Z_L(j\omega)=j\omega L=j2\pi fL$, $|Z_L|=2\pi fL$, phase $+90^\circ$.
- $f\to0$: $Z\to0$ (short). $f\to\infty$: $Z\to\infty$ (open).
- Slope on log-log axes: $+1$ ($+20$ dB/dec). Reference: $10\ \mu\text{H}$ gives 0.063 Ω at 1 kHz, 62.8 Ω at 1 MHz, 6.28 kΩ at 100 MHz.

**Ideal capacitor:** $Z_C=1/(j\omega C)$, $|Z_C|=1/(2\pi fC)$, phase $-90^\circ$. $f\to0$: open; $f\to\infty$: short; slope $-1$.

**Real inductor** (series $R_s$, shunt $C_w$): 
$$|Z|=\frac{\sqrt{R_s^2+\omega^2L^2}}{\sqrt{(1-\omega^2LC_w)^2+(\omega R_sC_w)^2}},\quad f_{SRF}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC_w}}$$
Below SRF it is inductive (+90° phase, increasing $|Z|$); at SRF $|Z|$ peaks (parallel resonance, limited by loss) and phase crosses 0°; above SRF it is capacitive, $|Z|\approx 1/(\omega C_w)$ and the phase is $-90^\circ$. At DC the impedance is the winding resistance $R_s$ (DCR), not zero.

**Real capacitor** (series $R_{esr}$, $L_{esl}$): $|Z|=\sqrt{R_{esr}^2+(\omega L_{esl}-1/\omega C)^2}$; minimum $=R_{esr}$ at $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{esl}C})$; above it, inductive.
:::

::: short
"An ideal inductor's impedance is j omega L, so at DC it's zero, a short circuit, and as frequency goes to infinity it goes to infinity, an open circuit. On a log-log plot that's a straight line rising at 20 dB per decade. Time-domain, v equals L di/dt: it resists fast current changes. A real inductor has winding capacitance, so it peaks at its self-resonant frequency and then acts like a capacitor. The capacitor is the mirror image: open at DC, short at high frequency."
:::

::: long
"For an ideal inductor, the impedance is j omega L, with omega equal to two pi f. At zero frequency, DC, that's zero. So a constant current through an inductor produces no voltage across it: it's a short circuit, just a wire. As frequency increases, the impedance magnitude grows in direct proportion, and as the frequency heads to infinity, so does the impedance, so it acts as an open circuit. The voltage leads the current by 90 degrees.

You can see the same thing from the time domain. v equals L di/dt. At DC, di/dt is zero, so v is zero. For a very fast signal, di/dt is huge, so even a small current change would require a big voltage; the inductor effectively refuses to let the current change quickly, which is an open circuit. That's the same as saying its current can't jump.

On a plot, I'd draw impedance magnitude versus frequency, both on log axes. It's a straight line sloping up at plus 20 dB per decade. For 10 microhenries, it hits about 63 ohms at 1 MHz.

But that's the ideal. A real inductor has a series resistance, DCR, so at DC it's not zero, it's that DCR. And it has winding capacitance across it. That means the curve flattens at DCR at the low end, rises, peaks at the self-resonant frequency, 22 MHz for 10 microhenries and 5 picofarads, and then falls at minus 20 dB per decade, because it's now a capacitor.

And if the question was meant for the capacitor, it's the mirror image: an open circuit at DC, impedance falling as one over omega C, a short at infinite frequency, until ESL makes it inductive again at its own self-resonance."
:::

::: terms
- **Impedance**: complex ratio $V/I$ for a sinusoid; $Z=R+jX$.
- **Reactance**: imaginary part, $X_L=\omega L$ and $X_C=-1/(\omega C)$ ([[q0042]], [[q0026]]).
- **Short circuit / open circuit**: $Z=0$ / $Z=\infty$ limits.
- **Magnitude Bode plot**: $|Z|$ vs frequency on log-log axes.
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: where parasitic capacitance (or ESL) cancels the intended reactance.
- **DCR**: DC winding resistance of an inductor.
- **ESR / ESL**: a capacitor's series resistance and inductance.
- **Decade**: a factor of ten in frequency.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "short at DC, open at high frequency".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives both limits and the formula, plus the +20 dB/decade straight-line plot with a numeric anchor (10 µH → 63 Ω at 1 MHz).
- Ties the limits to $v=L\,di/dt$ and current continuity.
- Points out that a real inductor has DCR (not zero at DC) and winding capacitance (not infinite at high frequency), and sketches the peak at SRF.
- Quotes an SRF with real numbers and says what the part looks like above it.
- Notices the section/wording mismatch and answers the capacitor version too.
- Mentions the practical consequence: an inductor used as an EMI choke stops working above its SRF.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying an inductor "blocks DC".** It's the opposite: it passes DC freely (short) and blocks fast changes. A capacitor blocks DC.
- **Drawing the real curve as a straight line forever.** Above SRF the impedance falls.
- **Forgetting DCR.** At DC the inductor's impedance is its winding resistance, which sets $I^2R$ loss and voltage drop.
- **Mixing up $+20$ and $-20$ dB/dec.** Inductor magnitude rises, capacitor magnitude falls.
- **Plotting on linear axes** where the SRF and the behavior across decades are invisible.
- **Mixing up the limits** with the *time-domain* statement: "open at t=0⁺" is for an inductor with zero initial current ([[q0044]]), which is consistent but is a different statement.
:::

::: deep
## The ideal curve and where the limits come from

Impedance of the ideal element is $Z_L=j\omega L$. Both limits are just limits of this expression, and the straight line on log-log axes is because $\log|Z|=\log(2\pi L)+\log f$.

Time-domain meaning: apply a DC step current. After the transient the current is constant, so $v=L\,di/dt=0$; the inductor is a wire. Apply a very fast sinusoid and the current needed to follow it would have a large $di/dt$ so the voltage required grows as $\omega L I$. If the source can only supply a finite voltage, the current amplitude is limited to $V/(\omega L)\to0$: the inductor is an open circuit.

## Phase tells the same story

Below SRF the inductor has $\approx+90^\circ$ phase (voltage leads current); at the SRF it is resistive (0°), above it capacitive ($-90^\circ$). The capacitor is $-90^\circ$ below its SRF, 0° at it, and $+90^\circ$ above.

## Seeing "short at DC, open at infinity" in a divider

The limits are easier to believe when they show up in a circuit. Put the inductor in series with a resistor and take the output across the inductor: a 100 Ω source resistance, $L = 10\ \mu$H, input 10 V amplitude. The voltage divider is

$$\frac{V_L}{V_{in}} = \frac{j\omega L}{R + j\omega L},\qquad \left|\frac{V_L}{V_{in}}\right| = \frac{\omega L}{\sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L^2}}$$

The corner is where $\omega L = R$, $f_c = R/(2\pi L) = 100/(2\pi\times10\ \mu\text{H}) = 1.59$ MHz. Evaluating the ratio:

| f | $\omega L$ | $V_L/V_{in}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 100 kHz | 6.3 Ω | 0.063 |
| 1 MHz | 62.8 Ω | 0.532 (5.3 V of 10 V) |
| 10 MHz | 628 Ω | 0.988 |
| 100 MHz | 6.28 kΩ | 0.9999 |

At low frequency almost no voltage appears across the inductor (short), and at high frequency almost all of it does (open: the inductor takes the entire input and the current is just $V/\omega L$). This is a first-order high-pass filter.

## Real inductor: where the peak really comes from

Take the ideal inductor with winding capacitance $C_w$ across it and a series loss $R_s$. Near resonance, the impedance of a high-Q parallel resonator is approximately a resistor,

$$Z_{peak}\approx Q\,\omega_0L = \frac{L}{R_sC_w},\qquad Q = \frac{\omega_0L}{R_s}$$

For $L = 10\ \mu$H, $C_w = 5$ pF: $\omega_0L = 1414\ \Omega$ at $f_0 = 22.5$ MHz. If the loss is a constant 1 Ω, that gives $Q = 1414$ and a peak of 2 MΩ, which the plot clips. No real inductor does that. At tens of MHz the effective series resistance (skin and proximity effect in the winding, core loss) is tens of ohms, and effective $Q$ values of tens are typical. With $Q = 50$, the peak is $50\times1414 \approx 71\ \text{k}\Omega$, still hundreds of times the DC resistance but not infinite.

Above the SRF the capacitor dominates, so $|Z|\approx 1/(\omega C_w)$: 318 Ω at 100 MHz and 64 Ω at 500 MHz for 5 pF. Notice how this value depends on $C_w$ and not at all on $L$. That is why "buying a bigger inductor" cannot improve the blocking above the SRF, and why high-frequency chokes use fewer turns (smaller $L$, much smaller $C_w$) or lossy ferrites.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The values of $C_w$, loss resistance and $Q$ vary widely with winding style, core material and vendor. The numbers above are for a plausible leaded or large chip inductor, not for a specific part.

## Worked example: the capacitor mirror, with numbers

A 100 nF ceramic capacitor with 1 nH of effective ESL (mounting inductance included) has an ideal impedance of $1/(2\pi\times10^8\times10^{-7}) = 0.016\ \Omega$ at 100 MHz. The ESL contributes $2\pi\times10^8\times10^{-9} = 0.63\ \Omega$, about 40 times larger, so at 100 MHz the "capacitor" is really a 1 nH inductor. Its SRF is $1/(2\pi\sqrt{10^{-9}\times10^{-7}}) = 15.9$ MHz, and everything above that is inductive.

The two parts are therefore the same kind of object with opposite emphasis. The inductor goes from short (DC) to open (high $f$) and ends up as a capacitor; the capacitor goes from open to short and ends up as an inductor. Both end up as the *other* element, which is why a good answer says that every real two-terminal part has an SRF above which its role reverses.

## What real hardware adds

- **DCR sets the DC value.** A 10 µH power inductor with 50 mΩ DCR carrying 3 A drops 0.15 V and dissipates $3^2\times0.05 = 0.45$ W, so "short at DC" is only a first approximation.
- **Saturation reduces $L$ at high current.** Beyond the saturation current the inductance falls sharply, so $\omega L$ drops and the part passes more ripple. The impedance curve you read on a datasheet is for small-signal conditions.
- **Ferrite beads are lossy on purpose.** Above their reactive range the impedance is mostly resistive, which absorbs noise energy instead of reflecting it. A bead is an inductor with a deliberately terrible Q.
- **Ringing.** An inductor excited by a step rings at its SRF, decaying with the loss, which is how a lot of EMI problems appear on a scope.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Impedance analyzer or VNA**: sweep $|Z|$ and phase; the SRF is where the phase crosses zero and $|Z|$ peaks. Method matters: a series-through S21 setup suits the large impedances near the SRF, a one-port reflection measurement suits the middle range, and a shunt-through setup suits very small impedances. Using the wrong one costs accuracy.
2. **Ring test**: hit the inductor with a fast edge through a small resistor and look at the ringing frequency. A 10 µH inductor with 5 pF of winding capacitance and a 10 pF probe rings at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{10\ \mu\text{H}\times 15\ \text{pF}}) = 13.0$ MHz. If you measure 12.9 MHz, you can back out $C_{total} = 15.2$ pF and subtract the probe to get $C_w \approx 5.2$ pF.
3. **Compare with ideal**: plot the measured $|Z|$ against $2\pi fL$ using the low-frequency value of $L$ from an LCR meter. The frequency where the two deviate by 10% is already a good limit for using the part as an inductor, usually a factor of three to five below the SRF.
4. **DC**: measure DCR with a four-wire meter and verify against the datasheet.

## Why DC and "infinity" are the two analysis shortcuts

Two quick rules that solve most circuit analysis questions:

| | DC steady state ($t\to\infty$) | Instantly after a change ($t=0^+$) |
|---|---|---|
| Capacitor | open (no current) | holds its voltage: short if uncharged |
| Inductor | short (no voltage) | holds its current: open if zero current |

These are the same facts as the frequency limits: high frequency behaves like the initial instant and DC like the final state ([[q0028]], [[q0044]]). For a series RL circuit driven by 10 V with $R=10\ \Omega$ and $L=1$ mH, the current starts at 0 A (inductor open) and ends at $10/10=1$ A (inductor short) with $\tau=L/R=100\ \mu$s.

## Design consequences

- **Power inductors** (buck converters) need low DCR and a high SRF, well above the switching frequency, so they act as inductors for harmonics too.
- **Chokes for EMI**: choose a part whose SRF is *above* the noise frequency; for 100 MHz noise, a 100 nH, 0.5 pF-class inductor or a ferrite bead is better than a 10 µH part with an SRF at 20 MHz.
- **Decoupling capacitors**: pick values/packages whose SRF is above the frequencies you need to cover ([[q0031]]).
- **Simulation**: use manufacturer SPICE/S-parameter models; an ideal inductor in SPICE is an open at "infinity" and will mislead you.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The height of the resonance peak depends on the effective loss at that frequency (ACR, core loss, dielectric loss), which is vendor- and material-dependent. Use measured or vendor data for the exact peak.
:::

::: applications
- **EMI chokes and ferrite beads** on cables and supply lines: high impedance at the noise frequency, low at DC.
- **Buck converter inductors**: pass DC load current with tiny drop (DCR) while smoothing switching ripple.
- **RF chokes** that feed DC bias into an amplifier while presenting high impedance to the RF signal.
- **Decoupling capacitors**: near-short to ground over their useful band, but only up to the SRF.
- **LC filters and matching networks**, with component choice limited by SRF.
- **Sizing initial conditions in transient analysis**: replace L by open, C by short at $t=0^+$, then solve.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal lumped element** with $L$ and $C$ constant; real inductors saturate and capacitors lose capacitance with bias.
- **Linear sinusoidal steady state**; impedance is defined for sinusoids.
- I answer the **inductor** question as worded, and give the capacitor version because the question sits in the capacitor section ([[q0045]] is the same question in the inductor section).
- Values for $R_s$, $C_w$, ESR and ESL are illustrative; real parts vary.
- At very high frequencies (hundreds of MHz–GHz) a lumped model breaks down; use S-parameters.

::: followups
### What is the impedance of a 1 µH inductor at 1 kHz, 1 MHz and 1 GHz?
Ideal: $2\pi fL=6.3\ \text{m}\Omega$, $6.3\ \Omega$ and $6.3$ kΩ. In reality the 1 GHz value is far lower, since the SRF of a 1 µH chip inductor is typically tens to a few hundred MHz, so at 1 GHz it's a small capacitor.

### Why is the inductor a short at DC but still limits the rate of rise of current?
At DC (steady state) $di/dt=0$, so there is no voltage. But a change from one current to another needs time: $di/dt=v/L$. In the transient, the voltage across it is what limits the current ramp, hence the $\tau=L/R$ behavior.

### How would you plot this quickly in an interview?
Draw log-log axes with frequency on x and $|Z|$ on y; mark one point (e.g. 62.8 Ω at 1 MHz for 10 µH) and draw a +1 slope line through it. Then sketch the peak at the SRF and a −1 slope beyond it, and optionally add the capacitor's −1 slope line crossing at the frequency where the magnitudes are equal.

### What happens if you put an ideal inductor and ideal capacitor in series (LC)?
Their reactances cancel at $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$: the net impedance goes to zero (series resonance). Parallel LC does the opposite: infinite impedance at $f_0$. The real parasitics of each part appear in the same way as the earlier model.

### What if you need an inductor to choke 500 MHz noise?
Pick a part whose SRF is above 500 MHz (a small-value, tiny-package RF inductor or a ferrite bead rated at 500 MHz), because a larger-value inductor would be capacitive and offer little blocking. Verify with the vendor impedance curve at 500 MHz, not the nominal inductance.
:::
