::: testing
Whether you know the **placement rule for each termination type and the reason**: series termination goes at the source, right at the driver pin; parallel (and Thevenin, AC, differential) termination goes at the sink, at the far end, past the last receiver; never in the middle. The reasoning is about where the reflection is absorbed and about **stub length**: any trace between the driver (or receiver) and its resistor is an unterminated stub, so it must be short compared with the edge.
:::

::: why
A perfectly valued terminator in the wrong place does nothing or makes things worse. Reviewers flag misplaced terminators immediately, and they are cheap to fix in layout but expensive after fabrication. It's also the quickest way to test whether a candidate really understands *how* termination works, rather than memorising "series at source, parallel at load".
:::

::: concepts
- **Series (source) termination**: resistor in series with the driver so $R_{out}+R_s=Z_0$; absorbs the reflection returning from the load. See [[q0716]].
- **Parallel (load) termination**: resistor $=Z_0$ at the far end absorbs the incident wave. See [[q0717]].
- **Stub length rule**: the connection between the net and the resistor must be electrically short ($\ll t_r$).
- **Topology**: point-to-point vs multi-drop vs fly-by determines "the end".
- **Source vs sink matching**: see [[q0717]].
- **Return path**: the termination's reference connection matters as much as its position. See [[q0530]].
:::

::: answer
"It depends on which kind of termination, but the rule is: series termination at the source, as close to the driver pin as possible, and parallel termination at the end of the line, at or just past the last receiver. Not in the middle.

Series termination works by making the driver's output impedance plus the resistor equal Z0. The driver launches half the voltage, the full voltage appears at the far end, and the reflection that returns is absorbed by the matched source. For that to work, the resistor has to sit right at the driver, because the bit of trace between the driver pin and the resistor is a stub that sees the unmatched driver, so I keep it to a few millimetres. Parallel termination absorbs the wave when it arrives, so it has to be at the far end, beyond the receiver, because anything beyond the terminator isn't terminated; and the connection from the line to the resistor needs to be short, so the receiver pin and resistor are effectively at the same point.

In the middle, a series resistor just splits the line into two mismatched pieces and a parallel resistor loads it. So the line passes through the receiver pad and ends at the resistor, with a short via to the plane or to the termination rail."
:::

::: technical
**Series (source) termination.** $R_s=Z_0-R_{out}$ placed at the driver pin. Launch amplitude $V_sZ_0/(R_{out}+R_s+Z_0)=V_s/2$; at an open/high-Z load the wave doubles to $V_s$ after $t_d$; the reflection returns to the matched source and is absorbed after $2t_d$. Placement constraint: the stub from driver pin to $R_s$ should have delay $\lesssim t_r/10$ (a heuristic; about 17 mm for 1 ns, 3.4 mm for 200 ps edges at 5.9 ps/mm).

**Parallel (end) termination.** $R_T=Z_0$ to ground or $V_{TT}$ (or Thevenin split, AC, differential) at the load. It absorbs the incident wave; all receivers must be *before* the terminator on the line (the line "ends" in the resistor). The stub between the line and terminator should be $\lesssim t_r/10$; the receiver pin should connect to the line, not through a long stub either.

**Topologies.**
- *Point-to-point*: series at source or parallel at load.
- *Daisy chain (multi-drop)*: parallel termination at the far end beyond the last receiver; no series termination (receivers along the line would see a half-amplitude step for $2t_d$).
- *Fly-by (DDR)*: each receiver is a very short stub; terminator (to $V_{TT}$) at the end.
- *Differential*: 100 Ω resistor across the pair at the receiver (often on-die).
- *Clock trees*: each branch point matched; series termination per branch near the buffer output.

**Why not in the middle.** A mid-line series $R$ creates two mismatched sections; a mid-line parallel $R$ is a shunt load that reflects ($\Gamma=-Z_0/(2R+Z_0)$ for $R$ in shunt) and leaves the true end unterminated.

**Verification.** Simulate with IBIS models or probe at the far end: monotonic edges, undershoot/overshoot within the receiver's limits.
:::

::: short
"Series termination goes at the source, right next to the driver pin, so that driver plus resistor equals Z0 and absorbs the reflection coming back. Parallel termination goes at the far end, at or just past the last receiver, so it absorbs the wave on arrival. In both cases the stub to the resistor must be short compared with the edge. Never in the middle: a mid-line series resistor splits the line into mismatched pieces, and a mid-line shunt resistor reflects and leaves the end unterminated."
:::

::: long
"The placement rule follows from how each termination works.

Series termination is a resistor in series with the driver, sized so that the driver's output impedance plus the resistor equals the line impedance. The driver launches a half-amplitude step into the line; at the far end, which is open or high-impedance, the wave doubles to full amplitude, and the reflection travels back to the source, where the matched source absorbs it. So it has to be at the source. And it has to be right at the driver pin, because the piece of trace between the pin and the resistor is a little stub on the unmatched side of the driver and anything on it sees full-amplitude and reflections. I keep that to a few millimetres, depending on the edge rate: roughly within a tenth of the rise time.

Parallel termination, a resistor equal to Z0 at the far end, absorbs the wave when it arrives, so it has to be at the end of the line, past the last receiver, so every receiver sees a clean, fully-terminated line. The route goes: driver, line, receiver pad, and then the resistor, with the resistor's connection to its rail or to ground as short as possible. If I put the resistor before the receiver, the receiver is on a stub off the line, and if I put it on a long stub the stub reflects.

In the middle, nothing good happens. A series resistor in the middle just makes two mismatched pieces of line, and a shunt resistor in the middle reflects part of the wave and leaves the true end unterminated.

For multi-drop buses with several receivers, I use a daisy chain with the terminator at the end, because series termination would give the receivers along the line a half-amplitude step for a round trip. For DDR fly-by it's the same idea, with a VTT terminator at the last device."
:::

::: terms
- **Series termination**: resistor in series at the source so $R_{out}+R_s=Z_0$.
- **Parallel (end) termination**: shunt resistor $\approx Z_0$ at the load end.
- **Thevenin / split termination**: resistor pair to $V_{CC}$ and ground whose parallel value is $Z_0$.
- **Stub**: unterminated section between the net and its termination or receiver.
- **Daisy chain**: bus where one line passes each receiver in turn.
- **Fly-by**: DDR topology with short stubs to each memory and a termination at the end.
- **On-die termination (ODT)**: termination built into the receiver or driver.
- **$V_{TT}$**: termination voltage rail (often $V_{DD}/2$).
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "series at the source, parallel at the load." An exceptional answer:
- Explains **why** each goes there (where the reflection is absorbed) and the **stub-length limit** with a number (≈ $t_r/10$).
- Describes **how topology changes the answer** (multi-drop, fly-by, differential, clock trees).
- Explains why **mid-line** placement is wrong, with $\Gamma$.
- Mentions **on-die termination** as the case where the terminator is already at the receiver silicon, and the return-reference/rail considerations.
- Says how to **verify** (simulation with IBIS, probe at the far end).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the series resistor "somewhere along the trace" or at the receiver.** It must be at the driver.
- **Terminating before the last receiver.** The receiver beyond the terminator sees an unterminated stub; the line should end in the resistor.
- **Long stubs to the resistor.** A 20 mm stub on a 200 ps edge is an inductor and a reflector.
- **Using series termination on a multi-drop bus.** Intermediate receivers see a half-height step for $2t_d$.
- **Placing parallel termination far from the receiver "for convenience".** Stub reflections and ringing return.
- **Ignoring the termination reference** (ground/VTT): a noisy rail injects noise.
:::

Figure: The resistor sits at the driver output. Together with $R_{out}$ it makes up $Z_0$. The short piece between the driver pin and the resistor is the only unterminated stub; keep it a few millimetres.

With $R_{out}=20\ \Omega$ and $Z_0=50\ \Omega$, $R_s=30\ \Omega$ (use 33 Ω). The launched step is $V_s\cdot50/(20+30+50)=V_s/2$. At the open end it doubles to $V_s$ at $t_d$; the reflection returns at $2t_d$ and finds a matched source ($\Gamma_s=0$), so there's no second bounce.

## The stub-length rule in numbers

The stub between the line and the terminator acts as a small series inductance; its delay $t_s$ must be well below $t_r$. Using $t_s\lesssim t_r/10$ and $t_{pd}=5.9$ ps/mm:

## Topologies

| Topology | Termination placement |
|---|---|
| Point-to-point, slow/short | none or series at source |
| Point-to-point, fast, clean edges needed at the load only | series at source |
| Point-to-point, edge needed along the line, or driver can't be matched | parallel at end |
| Daisy-chain multi-drop | parallel at the far end, after the last receiver |
| Fly-by (DDR) | $V_{TT}$ termination at the end; very short stubs to each device |
| Differential | 100 Ω across the pair at the receiver (ODT or resistor) |
| Clock tree | series at each buffer output; keep branches matched |

## Real hardware behaviour

- **ODT**: DDR and many SerDes terminate on-die, which is ideal because the stub is zero; the board then only needs the right trace impedance.
- **Driver impedance varies** with voltage, temperature and process (±20–30%), so $R_s$ is chosen from the IBIS output impedance at mid-swing; usually the nearest 33 Ω.
- **Probe at the receiver**, not at the driver; the waveform at the driver for a series-terminated line shows the half-amplitude step and the return reflection.
:::

::: applications
- **Clock outputs**: 22–33 Ω series resistor right at the clock buffer output.
- **DDR3/DDR4 address/command**: fly-by with $V_{TT}$ termination at the end.
- **Parallel LVDS or CML links**: 100 Ω termination at the receiver.
- **Backplane buses** with several receivers: end-of-line termination.
- **SPI/UART lines**: often a series resistor at the source for ringing and EMI.
:::

::: assumptions
- The "$t_r/10$" stub limit is a heuristic; stricter limits apply to multi-GHz links.
- Assumes a **linear driver** with known $R_{out}$ (real CMOS drivers vary with voltage and temperature).
- Receivers are assumed high-impedance and capacitive (a few pF) unless noted.
- For **slow edges** ($t_d<t_r/6$) termination placement is irrelevant, and for ODT receivers the terminator is on the chip.
- Some interfaces specify termination placement exactly (DDR layout guides); follow them.
:::

::: followups
### Why can't I use series termination for a multi-drop bus?
Series termination gives a half-amplitude step on the first pass; only the far end sees the full step after the reflection. Receivers along the line see the half-step for a round-trip time and may sit in the undefined region, giving false or delayed switching. Multi-drop buses need the full wave on first incidence: parallel termination at the end.

### What if the receiver is far from the end of the line?
Then I make the line end at the receiver (the receiver is the end), or route the line past the receiver with a very short tap, and put the terminator at the end of the line. A long unterminated tail after the receiver is a stub that reflects.

### How close is close enough for a series resistor on a 500 ps clock?
Stub delay below about $t_r/10=50$ ps, i.e. 8 mm of microstrip. In practice I put the 0402 resistor within 2–3 mm of the pin, which is easy.

### What if there's no room near the driver pin?
Move the resistor to the pin side and shift other parts. If still impossible, the termination isn't doing its job; use a lower drive-strength setting, slow the edges, or change the topology. A misplaced terminator is worse than none because it adds a stub.

### Where do I put the termination on a differential pair?
Across the pair at the receiver, with the stub from line to resistor as short as possible and symmetric. Many receivers have 100 Ω ODT; then I don't add one. For AC-coupled links the resistor sits after the capacitor, near the receiver.
:::

::: interpretations
"On the source, sink, or somewhere in between" — I read "sink" as the load/receiver end. The answer depends on termination type; the page answers for **series (source)**, **parallel (sink)**, and notes that "in between" is only correct for special cases such as **split/branch points** of a clock tree or **fly-by** with very short stubs. How to *route* the terminating parts is [[q0530]]; when they're needed is [[q0531]].
:::
