::: testing
Whether you see that a motor and a generator are **the same machine run in opposite energy directions**, not two different devices. The interviewer is probing for the link between torque and current ($T = K_t I$) and between speed and voltage ($E = K_e\omega$), and for the practical consequences: back-EMF inside every motor, regenerative braking, why a spinning motor can push current back into your supply, and why your H-bridge or battery needs a plan for that energy.
:::

::: why
Real hardware uses this constantly. Every drone, EV and robot arm both drives and brakes its motors, so the same winding moves energy in both directions. A wheel being back-driven downhill, a fan windmilling, or a motor being decelerated quickly will act as a generator and can pump the DC bus voltage above its limit and kill a driver or battery protection. Understanding the motor/generator duality also explains back-EMF, speed sensing with a tachometer-generator, and why shorting motor terminals brakes it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Lorentz force / motor action**: a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field feels force $F = BIL$. See [[q0334]].
- **Faraday induction / generator action**: a conductor moving through a field has EMF $e = BLv$. See [[q0341]].
- **Back-EMF**: the generator action that occurs inside a running motor, opposing the supply.
- **Motor model**: $V = IR + K_e\omega$ (plus $L\,di/dt$). Ohm's law and KVL: [[q0001]].
- **Four-quadrant operation**: motoring and generating in both rotation directions.
- **Power balance**: $VI = I^2R + T\omega$ in motor mode; sign flips in generator mode.
:::

::: answer
"Physically they're the same machine; the difference is which way the energy flows. In a motor you put in electrical power and get mechanical power out: the supply pushes current through the windings, the magnetic field makes a force, and the shaft turns. In a generator you spin the shaft and get electrical power out: the moving conductors in the field produce a voltage, and if you attach a load, current flows.

For a brushed DC motor you can see both effects at once. When it spins it generates a back-EMF $E = K_e\omega$ that opposes the supply, so the current is $(V-E)/R$. As long as the supply voltage is above $E$, current flows into the motor and it's a motor. If something else drives the shaft faster than $V/K_e$, $E$ exceeds the supply, the current reverses and the machine feeds energy back. That's regenerative braking.

So a motor is a generator running below the speed where its back-EMF equals the supply, and a generator is a motor being dragged above it. In practice they're optimised differently, for example generators for steady voltage or alternating output, but a permanent magnet DC motor really can be used as a small generator, which is how you can light an LED by spinning a toy motor."
:::

::: technical
**Same equations, different operating point.** For a PM brushed DC machine with terminal voltage $V$, resistance $R$, constants $K_t = K_e = K$ (SI):
$$V = IR + K\omega,\qquad T = K I$$
Electrical power into the terminals is $P_e = VI$. Mechanical power out is $P_m = T\omega = K I\omega$. Losses are $I^2R$ plus friction/iron loss. Then
$$VI = I^2R + K I\omega \;\Rightarrow\; P_e = P_{loss} + P_m.$$

- **Motor** ($V > K\omega$): $I>0$, $T>0$, $P_e > 0$, $P_m>0$.
- **Generator / regeneration** ($K\omega > V$): $I<0$, $T<0$ (opposes rotation, i.e. braking), $P_e<0$ (power flows out of the terminals), $P_m<0$ (shaft power in).
- **Dynamic braking** (terminals shorted or loaded by $R_b$): $V=0$ (or $-IR_b$), $I = -K\omega/(R+R_b)$, $T = -K^2\omega/(R+R_b)$. Braking torque is proportional to speed, and the kinetic energy turns into heat in $R$ and $R_b$.

**Example** ($R=2\ \Omega$, $K=0.02$, supply $V=12$ V): no-load speed is $\omega_0 = V/K = 600$ rad/s (5730 rpm). Driving the shaft externally at 700 rad/s: $E = 14$ V, $I = (12-14)/2 = -1$ A, so 12 W flows back into the supply, 14 W of shaft power is absorbed and 2 W is lost in $R$.

**Differences in practice.** Machines intended as generators (alternators) typically produce AC (rectified, or with a field winding for regulation); machines intended as motors are optimised for torque density, starting torque and commutation. A series-wound motor does not generate nicely without field flux reversal. A PM DC motor, a BLDC and a PMSM are all symmetric, which is why EVs use the same traction machine for driving and regeneration.
:::

::: short
"A motor and a generator are the same machine with the energy flowing in opposite directions. A motor converts electrical power to mechanical: current in the field makes torque. A generator converts mechanical to electrical: moving conductors in a field make a voltage. Inside every running motor there's also a back-EMF $K_e\omega$. If you drive the shaft faster than $V/K_e$, that back-EMF exceeds the supply, the current reverses and it regenerates. So the difference is just the sign of the power."
:::

::: long
"The short version is that they're the same device run in reverse, so the difference is which way energy flows.

In a motor I apply a voltage, current flows in the windings, and the Lorentz force $F = BIL$ makes torque. The shaft turns and I get mechanical power out. In a generator, I turn the shaft, the conductors move through the magnetic field and Faraday's law gives an EMF, $E = BLv$ or more usefully $E = K_e\omega$. If there's a load, that EMF drives current and I get electrical power out.

The nice thing about a brushed DC motor is you can see both at once. When it runs it's generating a back-EMF that opposes the supply. The current is $(V - K_e\omega)/R$. At standstill that's $V/R$, big, and as it speeds up the back-EMF grows and the current falls until the torque matches the load. At the no-load speed $V/K_e$ the current is basically zero.

If an external force pushes it past that speed, $K_e\omega$ exceeds $V$, the current reverses, torque becomes negative so it brakes the shaft, and power flows back into the supply. That's regenerative braking. With 12 volts, 2 ohms and $K_e = 0.02$, spinning it at 700 rad/s gives 14 volts of EMF, so one amp flows back and 12 watts return to the source.

The engineering consequence is that a drive has to handle that returned energy: a battery can absorb some of it, but a plain power supply can't, so the bus voltage rises and you need a brake resistor, a TVS or regen limits. And shorting the terminals is dynamic braking, with torque proportional to speed.

They aren't always optimised the same way, but with a permanent-magnet motor you can absolutely use it as a small generator."
:::

::: terms
- **Motor action**: electrical to mechanical conversion via the Lorentz force $F = BIL$.
- **Generator action**: mechanical to electrical conversion via Faraday induction $e = BLv$.
- **Back-EMF** ($K_e\omega$): voltage generated by a spinning motor; opposes the supply. See [[q0341]].
- **Torque constant $K_t$** (N·m/A) and **voltage constant $K_e$** (V·s/rad): numerically equal in SI units.
- **Regenerative braking**: returning energy to the source by running the machine as a generator.
- **Dynamic braking**: shorting or resistively loading the terminals so braking energy dissipates as heat.
- **Four-quadrant drive**: can motor and generate in both rotation directions.
- **Bus pumping**: the DC bus voltage rising because regenerated energy has nowhere to go.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "a motor turns electricity into motion, a generator turns motion into electricity". An exceptional answer:

- Says they are the same machine and shows it with $V = IR + K_e\omega$: sign of $I$ flips when $K_e\omega > V$.
- Gives the power balance $VI = I^2R + T\omega$ and what each sign means.
- Mentions practical use: regenerative braking, tach-generators, shorting the terminals to brake.
- Points out the hardware hazard: regenerated energy pumps the bus; you need absorbers (battery, brake resistor, clamp).
- Notes that optimisation differs (alternators with field control vs motors for torque), so "same machine" is exact in principle and approximate in practice.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating them as fundamentally different devices.** A PM DC motor works as a generator without modification. The roles are an operating point, not a device type.
- **Forgetting that a motor generates back-EMF while motoring.** That is generator action inside the motor, and it is why current falls as speed rises. See [[q0341]].
- **Thinking regeneration always charges the battery.** It only does if the source can absorb current. A bench supply or a full battery can't, and the bus voltage climbs.
- **Saying the generator voltage is independent of speed.** $E = K_e\omega$ is proportional to speed, so DC generators need regulation.
- **Ignoring losses.** Regeneration returns less than you put in; $I^2R$, friction, iron loss and driver loss all subtract.
- **Confusing "generator" with "alternator".** An alternator is a generator whose output is AC (often rectified); a DC generator uses a commutator.
:::

::: deep
## One machine, two directions of energy

Start with the two laws that make every electromagnetic machine work.

**Motor action** (Lorentz force). A conductor of length $L$ carrying current $I$ in flux density $B$ feels force $F = BIL$. For a rotor of radius $r$ with many conductors, the torque is the sum of these forces times the radius: $T = K_t I$.

**Generator action** (Faraday's law). A conductor moving at velocity $v$ through the same field has an EMF $e = BLv$. Summing over the rotor conductors: $E = K_e\omega$.

Because both come from the same $BL$ product, the constants are equal in SI units:
$$K_t\ [\text{N·m/A}] = K_e\ [\text{V·s/rad}].$$

One way to see it is energy conservation: the electrical power taken by the back-EMF, $EI = K_e\omega I$, must equal the mechanical power produced, $T\omega = K_t I\omega$. This is only possible if $K_e = K_t$. (The same argument is in [[q0353]].)

So a motor is *always* also a generator; the back-EMF is the generator voltage that appears whenever the rotor turns.

## Power flow, signs, and efficiency

| Quantity | Motoring ($K\omega<V$) | Generating ($K\omega>V$) |
|---|---|---|
| $I$ | $>0$ (into +) | $<0$ (out of +) |
| $T = KI$ | $>0$ (along rotation) | $<0$ (opposes rotation) |
| $P_e = VI$ | $>0$ (absorbed) | $<0$ (delivered) |
| $P_m = T\omega$ | $>0$ (delivered) | $<0$ (absorbed) |

The efficiency of motoring is $P_m/P_e = K\omega/V$ when only $I^2R$ loss is counted. The efficiency of generating is $P_e/P_m$ in magnitude $= V/(K\omega)$. At 700 rad/s: $12/14 = 86\%$.

## Dynamic braking and the "short the terminals" trick

Short the winding (or tie it to ground in a low-side-on H-bridge): $V = 0$, so
$$I = -\frac{K\omega}{R},\qquad T = -\frac{K^2\omega}{R}.$$
At 500 rad/s, $I = -5$ A, $T = -0.1$ N·m, and the winding dissipates $I^2R = 50$ W, taken from the rotor's kinetic energy. It is a viscous-like brake whose torque falls to zero as the motor stops, so it can't hold a load. The H-bridge brake mode (both low-side switches on) in the simulator does this:

## What happens to the bus

In regeneration, current flows *into* the supply side of the H-bridge. A battery can absorb a few amps within its charge-current rating; a diode-protected bench supply cannot, so the bus capacitor charges. The kinetic energy of the example rotor at 700 rad/s is $\tfrac12 J\omega^2 = 0.245$ J. If all of it went into a 1000 µF bus capacitor sitting at 12 V, then $\tfrac12 C(V_f^2 - V_i^2) = 0.245$ J gives $V_f = \sqrt{12^2 + 2\times0.245/10^{-3}} \approx 25$ V, twice the rail. A heavy load inertia (a robot wheel, an EV) is far worse. Fixes: a clamp (TVS/zener), a brake chopper (a MOSFET and resistor that turn on above a bus threshold), limiting deceleration, or a bidirectional source.

::: applications
- **Regenerative braking in EVs and e-bikes**: the traction machine acts as a generator on deceleration and charges the battery (within its charge-current limit).
- **Tachometer generators**: a small PM DC machine whose output voltage ($K_e\omega$) is used as a speed signal in older servo systems.
- **Hand-crank radios and flashlights**: a small PM DC motor used as a generator.
- **Wind turbines and hydro**: permanent-magnet synchronous generators with an inverter doing the regulation.
- **Drone and robot arms**: back-driven motors during hard decelerations send current into the battery; ESCs and drivers implement braking modes and bus clamps.
- **Dynamic braking resistors on industrial drives and cranes**: dissipate regenerated energy.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Permanent-magnet DC machine** with constant flux, so $K_t = K_e$ is constant. Wound-field machines let you vary flux and separate the roles more (series, shunt and compound).
- **Linear model** ignoring brush drop, saturation, armature reaction and temperature effects; resistance rises with temperature.
- **Ideal source that can sink current.** If it can't, regeneration is limited by the bus capacitance and clamps.
- **Steady state** so $L\,di/dt \approx 0$.
- Alternative answers: "A generator produces AC, a motor consumes DC" is imprecise. Generators can be DC (dynamos) and motors AC. Another valid view is design intent: generators are built for voltage regulation and cooling at constant speed; motors for torque and starting.
:::

::: followups
### Can any motor be used as a generator?
Mostly yes, but suitability varies. PM brushed DC, BLDC and PMSM machines work well because the field comes from magnets. Induction motors can generate if they are overspeeded and have excitation. Series-wound DC motors have no field without current, so they usually need modification. A stepper can also generate a voltage and is used as a crude generator or position sensor.

### What voltage does a motor produce if I spin it by hand at 1000 rpm?
$\omega = 1000\times 2\pi/60 = 104.7$ rad/s, so $E = K_e\omega = 0.02\times104.7 = 2.09$ V open circuit for our example motor ($K_e$ = 0.02 V·s/rad, equivalent to about 477 rpm per volt). With a load $R_L$, the terminal voltage is $E\,R_L/(R+R_L)$.

### What is regenerative braking, and what are its limits?
The drive makes the terminal voltage lower than the back-EMF so current flows back to the source and torque opposes motion. The limits are: the source must accept current; there must be enough speed ($K\omega>V$ or a boost stage that raises the effective voltage); the drive must handle the current; and at low speed the recoverable power is tiny, so friction brakes are still needed.

### Why does a short-circuited motor feel stiff when you turn it?
The shorted winding carries $I = K\omega/R$ and produces opposing torque $K^2\omega/R$. Turn faster and you feel more resistance. It is viscous damping with coefficient $K^2/R$ ($0.0002$ N·m·s/rad here).

### What happens to your DC bus when a motor is decelerated quickly with a diode-input supply?
The bus capacitor absorbs the energy because the diodes prevent current from returning. Voltage rises as $\Delta V$ from $\tfrac12C(V_f^2 - V_i^2) = E_{regen}$. If it exceeds the switch or capacitor ratings it will damage them. You need a brake chopper/resistor, a TVS clamp, a bus-voltage controller that limits deceleration, or a regenerative front end.

### If a motor is a generator, why don't they produce infinite power if I spin them fast?
Power available is limited by the input mechanical power and by losses; the output is $P = EI - I^2R$ with $I = E/(R+R_L)$ for a load, which peaks at $R_L = R$ (maximum power transfer), and the shaft torque rises with the electrical load. Spinning faster raises EMF, but the winding current limit and voltage ratings cap it.
:::
