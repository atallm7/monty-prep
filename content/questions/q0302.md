::: basics
### In plain words
A LiPo cell is a stack of thin layers, each with one job. Two of them are electrodes that store lithium, one is a thin film that keeps the electrodes apart, and a liquid-like gel lets lithium ions travel between them. Understanding what each layer is made of explains the voltage, the safety limits and why the cell swells.

### Picture it
Imagine a sandwich of two slices of bread with a thin paper napkin in between, all soaked in a bit of sauce and sealed in a plastic bag. The bread slices are the electrodes. The napkin stops the slices touching but lets sauce soak through. The sauce carries the lithium. The bag keeps air and moisture out. The sauce here is flammable, which is a big part of why batteries need care.

### Words you need
- **Cathode**: the positive electrode, a lithium metal oxide coated on aluminum foil.
- **Anode**: the negative electrode, graphite coated on copper foil.
- **Separator**: a very thin porous plastic film that blocks contact but passes ions ([[q0298]]).
- **Electrolyte**: lithium salt dissolved in organic solvent, held in a gel in a LiPo.
- **Current collector**: the metal foil that carries electrons out of each electrode.
- **Ion**: an atom carrying an electric charge. Here, a lithium atom that has lost an electron.

### The simplest example
Follow one discharge. Lithium ions leave the graphite, swim through the electrolyte and the separator, and settle into the cathode. The electrons they left behind cannot cross the separator, so they go around through your phone's circuit, and that flow is the current. The cell voltage is the cathode's level minus the anode's level. About 3.8 V minus about 0.1 V gives roughly 3.7 V, the usual nominal value.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page lists the materials layer by layer, shows the reactions, and explains why aluminum and copper go on opposite sides.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can open the cell in your head and name every layer and what it is made of *and why that material*: cathode (lithium metal oxide on aluminum foil), anode (graphite on copper foil), separator (porous polyolefin), electrolyte (lithium salt in organic carbonates), binders and conductive carbon, the laminate pouch, tabs, and the external protection circuit. Strong candidates explain the reasoning (why aluminum on the cathode and copper on the anode, why the separator melts as a safety shutdown, why the electrolyte is a flammable organic solvent and not water) and can state the electrode reactions.
:::

::: why
Materials explain behaviour: why the voltage is 3.7 V, why overcharge is dangerous, why cells swell, why there is a lower voltage limit (copper dissolves), why cold charging plates lithium, and why LiPo cells burn rather than just leak. For a hardware engineer, knowing the materials also means knowing what constrains the cell's temperature range, safety tests, recycling, shipping and supply chain (cobalt, lithium, graphite). Interviewers use it to check that you see the battery as a physical device and not a black box with two terminals.
:::

::: concepts
- **Cathode (positive electrode)**: lithium metal oxide (LCO, NMC, NCA...) coated on aluminum foil ([[q0300]]).
- **Anode (negative electrode)**: graphite coated on copper foil.
- **Separator**: thin porous polyolefin film that blocks electrons but passes ions.
- **Electrolyte**: lithium salt (LiPF₆) in organic carbonate solvents, gelled in the polymer for LiPo ([[q0298]]).
- **Binder and conductive additive**: PVDF or CMC/SBR binders; carbon black for electrical contact.
- **Pouch laminate and tabs**: aluminum-polymer foil package; Al and Ni tabs.
- **Protection circuit**: PCB with MOSFETs, protection IC, NTC, fuse ([[q0304]]).
:::

::: answer
"A LiPo cell is a sandwich. The positive electrode, the cathode, is a lithium metal oxide, like lithium cobalt oxide or nickel-manganese-cobalt oxide, mixed with a bit of carbon black and a binder and coated on aluminum foil. The negative electrode, the anode, is graphite coated on copper foil. Between them is a separator, a very thin porous polyethylene or polypropylene film that stops them touching but lets lithium ions through. The whole stack is soaked in electrolyte, which is a lithium salt, usually LiPF6, dissolved in organic carbonate solvents. In a LiPo it's held in a polymer gel. Then it's all sealed in a pouch, which is an aluminum foil laminated with plastic, with aluminum and nickel tabs coming out as the terminals.

Aluminum is used on the cathode side because it's stable at high potential, copper on the anode side because aluminum alloys with lithium at low potential. On discharge lithium ions leave the graphite, travel through the electrolyte and enter the cathode, while the electrons go around through the load. On top of the cell there's usually a small protection board with MOSFETs, a protection IC, and a thermistor. The electrolyte being flammable organic solvent is a big part of the safety story."
:::

**Layer by layer (typical, graphite/oxide cell).**

| Component | Typical material | Function | Typical thickness (indicative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positive current collector | aluminum foil | carries electrons; stable at high potential | 10-20 µm |
| Cathode coating | LiCoO₂ / NMC / NCA / LFP + carbon black + PVDF binder | hosts Li⁺ at about 3.7-3.9 V vs Li (average) | 50-100 µm per side |
| Separator | microporous PE, PP or PP/PE/PP, sometimes ceramic-coated | electronic insulation, ion pores, thermal shutdown | 10-25 µm |
| Electrolyte | LiPF₆ in EC/DMC/EMC (+ additives like VC, FEC); polymer gel in LiPo | ion conduction (about 10 mS/cm class) | fills pores |
| Anode coating | graphite + CMC/SBR (or PVDF) + carbon | hosts Li⁺ at about 0.1 V vs Li | 50-100 µm per side |
| Negative current collector | copper foil | carries electrons; stable at low potential | 6-12 µm |
| Package | Nylon / Al / polypropylene laminate | seals; barrier to moisture and air | about 100-150 µm |
| Tabs | Al (positive), Ni or Ni-plated Cu (negative) | external connection | 0.1-0.2 mm |

**Electrode reactions (graphite/LCO example), charge to the right.**

Cathode: $\mathrm{LiCoO_2} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Li_{1-x}CoO_2} + x\,\mathrm{Li^+} + x\,e^-$

Anode: $\mathrm{C_6} + x\,\mathrm{Li^+} + x\,e^- \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{Li_xC_6}$

Practical LCO cells use $x\approx0.5$ (to about 4.2 V), giving about 137 mAh/g from a theoretical 274 mAh/g for full delithiation; going further damages the structure. Graphite's theoretical capacity is 372 mAh/g (one Li per six carbons), computed from $F/(3.6\,M)$ with $M=72.07$ g/mol of C₆.

**Why Al and Cu.** Aluminum forms a passive oxide layer, stable up to about 4+ V vs Li, but alloys with lithium at low potential, so it can't be used on the graphite side. Copper is stable at the anode's low potential but dissolves at high potential, so it can't be used on the cathode side, and it also dissolves if the cell is over-discharged, which is the reason for the undervoltage limit ([[q0330]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Layer thicknesses, solvent blends, additives and separator coatings vary a lot between manufacturers and are often proprietary. The numbers are order-of-magnitude typicals.
:::

::: long
"Think of it as a sandwich, from outside in.

The outside is a pouch made of laminated foil: a layer of nylon, a layer of aluminum as the moisture and air barrier, and an inner layer of polypropylene that heat-seals. Two metal tabs come out through the seal, aluminum for the positive and nickel or nickel-plated copper for the negative.

Inside there's a stack of layers. The positive electrode is a lithium metal oxide, lithium cobalt oxide or NMC, mixed with carbon black for conductivity and a binder, PVDF, and coated on aluminum foil. The negative electrode is graphite with a binder, coated on copper foil. Aluminum is used on the positive because it's stable at high potential, but it would alloy with lithium at the anode's low potential. Copper is stable at low potential but would dissolve at the cathode's high potential.

Between them is the separator, a micron-scale porous film of polyethylene or polypropylene, about ten to twenty-five micrometres, which electrically isolates them. It's also a safety feature: polyethylene melts at about 130 degrees and closes its pores, shutting the cell down.

The electrolyte is a lithium salt, usually lithium hexafluorophosphate, in a mixture of organic carbonate solvents like ethylene carbonate and dimethyl carbonate, with small additives. In a LiPo it's held in a polymer gel. It's flammable and doesn't tolerate water, which is why the pouch has to seal well.

On discharge, lithium leaves the graphite as ions, moves through the electrolyte and enters the cathode, and the electrons go through the external circuit. And on the outside, there's a protection board with MOSFETs and a protection IC."
:::

::: terms
- **Cathode / positive electrode**: lithium metal oxide on Al foil (the electrode that gains Li⁺ on discharge).
- **Anode / negative electrode**: graphite on Cu foil.
- **Separator**: microporous polyolefin film.
- **Electrolyte**: LiPF₆ in organic carbonates (EC/DMC/EMC), gelled in LiPo.
- **Binder**: PVDF (cathode) or CMC/SBR (anode) that holds particles together.
- **Conductive additive**: carbon black that provides electron paths.
- **SEI**: solid electrolyte interphase formed on graphite from electrolyte decomposition ([[q0301]]).
- **Tab**: metal strip connecting the electrode foils to the outside.
- **Intercalation**: reversible insertion of Li⁺ into a layered host.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "lithium, cobalt, graphite, electrolyte, plastic".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names each layer *and its role*: foil collectors, active coatings with binder and carbon, separator, electrolyte, laminate, tabs.
- Explains the **Al-cathode / Cu-anode** choice from electrode potentials, and links Cu dissolution to the undervoltage limit.
- Gives the half-reactions and a number (graphite 372 mAh/g, LCO about 137 mAh/g practical).
- Knows the safety links: flammable organic electrolyte, LiPF₆ sensitivity to moisture, polyethylene shutdown separator, oxygen release from layered oxides.
- Distinguishes **active-material** energy density from **cell-level** (foils, separator, electrolyte and pouch halve it).
- Mentions the external protection board.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"LiPo contains lithium metal."** Ordinary LiPo/Li-ion cells have no metallic lithium (unless plating occurs); lithium is in ionic form inside the host materials.
- **"The polymer is the whole battery."** The polymer is the electrolyte matrix and the pouch layers; the energy is stored in the electrode materials.
- **"Graphite is the cathode."** Graphite is the *anode* in lithium-ion cells; the cathode is the metal oxide (naming flips on charge vs discharge, so use positive/negative to be safe).
- **"Electrolyte is a water solution."** Lithium cells use anhydrous organic solvents because water would be electrolysed at 4 V and reacts with LiPF₆.
- **Using aluminum on the anode (or copper on the cathode) in a sketch.** Each metal is chosen for its stable potential window.
- **Forgetting the SEI.** It forms from the electrolyte on the graphite and is both essential (passivation) and the main aging mechanism.
:::

::: deep
## The cell as a stack of coatings on foils

Most of the cell's volume and mass is not "active chemistry" but supporting structure. A pouch cell is a long sandwich of: Al foil with cathode coating on both sides, separator, Cu foil with anode coating on both sides, separator, and so on, either folded (Z-fold) or stacked as individual sheets, or wound and flattened. Each electrode layer is a *composite*: particles of active material, a little carbon black for electron conduction, and a polymer binder that holds it to the foil, all with pores filled with electrolyte so ions can reach every particle.

## Why each material

**Cathode: lithium metal oxide.** The oxide host has layers or channels that let Li⁺ in and out while the transition metal (Co, Ni, Mn, Fe) changes oxidation state to balance charge. The cathode potential vs lithium sets the cell voltage. Cobalt-rich oxides give high voltage and capacity but cost and thermal sensitivity; LFP is cheaper and safer but lower voltage ([[q0300]]).

**Anode: graphite.** Graphite stores lithium between its carbon sheets at about 0.1 V vs Li/Li⁺, near lithium metal's potential, which maximises cell voltage, and it expands only about 10% on lithiation. Capacity: one Li per six carbons gives 372 mAh/g.

**Foils.** Electrons need a metal highway to the tabs. Aluminum is light, cheap and self-passivating, so it survives the high potential, but it alloys with Li below about 0.3 V vs Li. Copper survives the low potential but dissolves above about 3.5 V vs Li, which is also what happens when a cell is over-discharged: the anode potential rises until copper dissolves and later deposits as dendrites, shorting the cell ([[q0295]]).

**Separator.** A 10-25 µm porous polyolefin film with about 40% porosity and pore size of about 0.1 µm. It must be an electronic insulator, wet well with electrolyte, resist puncture by particles and dendrites, and ideally shut down: polyethylene softens near 130 °C and its pores close, stopping ion flow. PP survives higher (about 165 °C), so tri-layer PP/PE/PP separators combine strength and shutdown. Ceramic (alumina) coatings add thermal stability.

**Electrolyte.** LiPF₆ (about 1 M) in a blend of cyclic carbonate (ethylene carbonate, which forms a good SEI on graphite) and linear carbonates (DMC, EMC, DEC, which lower viscosity), with additives like vinylene carbonate. The salt gives high ionic conductivity, but LiPF₆ reacts with moisture to form HF, which is why cells are made in dry rooms and why a breached pouch is hazardous. The solvents are flammable, and the electrolyte also oxidises at high voltage. The electrolyte is stable only from roughly 1 V to about 4.3 V vs Li; graphite works at about 0.1 V, below that window, and the SEI is the trick that lets it operate there without continuously reducing the electrolyte ([[q0301]]).

**Binder and conductive carbon.** PVDF (dissolved in NMP during coating) on the cathode, CMC with SBR (water-based) on the anode, typically 2-5% by mass, plus a few percent of carbon black. These are "inactive" but set mechanical integrity and rate performance.

**Package.** The pouch has to block moisture and oxygen for years, resist the electrolyte, and heat-seal. A typical laminate is nylon (outer abrasion layer), aluminum foil (barrier) and polypropylene (inner sealant), a total of about 100-150 µm.

## Active-material energy versus cell energy

Take 1 Ah of capacity. At about 140 mAh/g for the cathode you need 7.1 g of cathode active material. For the graphite at about 372 mAh/g, with a typical anode-to-cathode capacity ratio of about 1.1 (the anode is deliberately oversized so lithium doesn't plate), you need about $1000/372\times1.1=3.0$ g. Active material is therefore about 10.1 g per Ah, and at 3.7 V that is 3.7 Wh in 10.1 g, i.e. about 366 Wh/kg of *active material only*. Real cells deliver 150-270 Wh/kg because the foils, separator, electrolyte, binder, tabs and pouch typically account for roughly 40-50% of the mass. That is why thinner foils, thicker coatings and lighter packaging are each worth a few percent of energy density.

## Assembly and what could go wrong

Coating, drying and calendering the electrodes, slitting, stacking or winding with separator, welding tabs, inserting into the pouch, filling electrolyte, sealing, then formation cycling, which grows the initial SEI and is followed by ageing and testing. Defects that cause field failures include metal particles (short circuits), burrs on electrode edges, misalignment (anode must overhang the cathode to avoid plating), moisture contamination and poor seals.

## The part you add: the protection board

Outside the pouch, a small PCB with a protection IC, two back-to-back MOSFETs, a thermistor and sometimes a PTC and fuse protects the cell from overcharge, over-discharge and short circuit ([[q0304]]). It is not part of the electrochemical cell, but a LiPo pack bought for a product normally includes it.

::: applications
- **Battery manufacturing and quality**: coating thickness, electrode alignment and particle contamination controls determine yield and safety.
- **Cell selection**: LCO/NMC vs LFP cathode and thickness/porosity choices differentiate energy and power cells ([[q0292]]).
- **Safety design**: understanding separator shutdown, electrolyte flammability and oxygen release drives thermal-runaway testing.
- **Recycling**: cobalt, nickel, lithium, copper and aluminum recovery are the value drivers.
- **Failure analysis**: Cu dissolution after over-discharge, lithium plating after cold charging, and swelling from electrolyte decomposition all trace to the materials.
:::

::: assumptions
The answer describes a mainstream graphite/lithium-metal-oxide gel-polymer pouch cell. Silicon-graphite anodes, LFP or LTO electrodes, solid or ceramic electrolytes, and lithium-metal anodes are variants with different materials. Details such as the exact solvent blend, additives and coatings are manufacturer-specific and often proprietary.

Thicknesses, porosity and mass fractions are indicative; actual designs differ between energy cells and power cells.

::: followups
### Why is graphite used for the anode instead of lithium metal?
Lithium metal would give the highest capacity but it plates unevenly during charging, forming dendrites that can pierce the separator and short the cell. Graphite hosts lithium in its layers (intercalation), which is far safer and gives good cycle life, at a cost in energy density (372 mAh/g vs about 3860 mAh/g for lithium metal).

### What is the separator's job and how does it contribute to safety?
It keeps the electrodes electrically apart while letting ions pass through its pores. A polyethylene layer melts at about 130 °C and closes the pores (shutdown), stopping current if a cell overheats. Ceramic coatings keep the separator from shrinking at higher temperatures. If the separator fails (puncture, dendrite, particle), you get an internal short.

### Why does over-discharge damage the cell?
As the cell is pushed below about 2-2.5 V, the anode potential rises until the copper foil starts dissolving. The copper ions migrate and can deposit as metal on recharge, building shorts across the separator. A cell that was deeply over-discharged should be treated as damaged ([[q0330]]).

### Why do LiPo cells have a flammable electrolyte at all? Can't they use water?
Water decomposes at about 1.2 V (and well below 3.7 V in practice), and would react with lithium and LiPF₆. Organic carbonates are stable to about 4+ V and dissolve lithium salts, so they're used despite flammability. Solid and non-flammable electrolytes are active research areas.

### What if you replaced the aluminum cathode foil with copper?
It would corrode at the cathode potential (copper dissolves above about 3.5 V vs Li), releasing copper into the electrolyte and contaminating the cell. That is exactly why each foil is matched to its electrode's potential.
