::: basics
### In plain words
LiDAR accuracy is limited by several separate things: the timing electronics, how strong the return is, the shape of the beam, the weather, and the platform the sensor sits on. The first step in any accuracy question is to split error into two kinds. Random scatter is called precision. A steady offset is called bias, and it limits accuracy. Each kind needs a different fix.

### Picture it
Picture arrows shot at a target. Arrows that land tightly grouped but off to one side are precise but biased. You fix that by re-aiming, which is calibration. Arrows scattered evenly around the bullseye are unbiased but imprecise. Averaging many shots fixes that. A LiDAR has both kinds of error at once. The target picture misses angle errors across the beam.

### Words you need
- **Precision**: how tightly repeated measurements of the same thing cluster. This is random scatter.
- **Bias**: a steady offset from the true value. Averaging does not remove it.
- **Jitter**: random timing error. It is about the pulse's rise time divided by the signal-to-noise ratio. See [[q0865]].
- **Walk error**: a timing bias that depends on pulse strength, so strong and weak targets give different ranges.
- **Beam footprint**: the spot size on the target. A 3 mrad beam is about 30 cm wide at 100 m.

### The simplest example
A pulse edge takes 2 ns to rise, and the signal-to-noise ratio is 20. The timing jitter is 2 ns divided by 20, which is 100 ps, and each 100 ps is 1.5 cm of range. Averaging 16 pulses cuts this random part by 4, to about 0.4 cm, but a steady bias stays. Now an angular pointing error of 0.05 degrees. At 100 m it shifts the point sideways by 100 m times the tangent of 0.05 degrees, which is about 8.7 cm. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page works through each source and its fix.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can build an **error budget** for a time-of-flight sensor and separate the factors cleanly: the electronics and timing (jitter, walk, quantization, clock, temperature), the optics and geometry (beam divergence, mixed pixels, incidence angle, angular knowledge), the target (reflectivity, specular surfaces, retroreflectors), the environment (sun, fog, rain, other LiDARs), and the platform (motion, calibration, time sync). Strong answers distinguish **precision** (random scatter) from **accuracy** (systematic bias), quantify at least a few terms, and say how each is mitigated.
:::

::: why
A LiDAR spec sheet might claim "±2 cm", but in the field you will see 10 cm biases on dark objects, ghost points near glass and range drops in fog. A hardware engineer has to know which effect is behind which symptom, so that the fix is the right one: re-calibrate, change a filter, change the threshold circuit, or just accept a limit and fuse with another sensor. This question also exercises the general method of characterizing error that appears in [[q0871]] to [[q0873]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd group the factors into five buckets.

First, the sensor's own timing. Range error equals c over two times the timing error, so every 100 picoseconds is 1.5 centimeters. The main random error is jitter, roughly rise time divided by SNR; with a 2 nanosecond edge and an SNR of 20 that's 1.5 centimeters. Then there are systematic things: walk error, where a fixed threshold crosses a strong pulse earlier than a weak one and can cause 10 centimeters of bias, TDC quantization, clock error, and temperature drift of delays.

Second, the signal level, which depends on the target. Dark targets, steep incidence angles and long range cut the return and hurt SNR, and glossy surfaces or retroreflectors can give ghost points or saturate the receiver.

Third, the geometry: the beam isn't a line, it's a footprint, say 30 centimeters at 100 meters, so at edges you get mixed points, and on slanted surfaces a range bias. Angle errors from the encoder or mirror move points sideways: 0.05 degrees is 9 centimeters at 100 meters.

Fourth, the environment: sunlight adds noise, fog and rain attenuate and scatter, and other LiDARs cause interference.

Fifth, the platform: motion during the scan, and errors in extrinsic calibration or time sync.

For mitigation I'd calibrate offset, scale and walk over range and reflectivity, use waveform processing, narrow filters, multiple returns, and fuse with other sensors."

::: technical
**Random range error (precision).** $\sigma_d = c\,\sigma_t/2$ with $\sigma_t\approx t_r/\text{SNR}$. For $t_r$ = 2 ns: SNR 10 gives 3.0 cm, SNR 20 gives 1.5 cm, SNR 100 gives 0.3 cm. SNR falls with range as $1/R^2$ (power) and with reflectivity and $\cos\alpha$.

**Systematic range error (accuracy).**
- *Walk:* fixed-threshold crossing time depends on amplitude. For a 5 mV threshold on a 2 ns edge, a 14.6 mV pulse vs a 1.46 V pulse differ by 0.85 ns = 12.7 cm.
- *TDC quantization:* LSB of 100 ps is 1.5 cm, rms $=\text{LSB}/\sqrt{12}$ = 0.43 cm.
- *Clock error:* 20 ppm gives 2 mm at 100 m.
- *Thermal drift:* 5 ps/K of delay drift over 100 K is 7.5 cm unless compensated.
- *Fixed offsets:* cable and driver delays, calibrated out.

**Geometry.** Beam divergence 3 mrad gives a 30 cm footprint at 100 m. On a surface at incidence $\theta$ a first-return detector locks on the nearest part of the footprint, giving a bias up to $\tfrac12 w\tan\theta$ (the upper bound): 8.7 cm at 30°, 26 cm at 60°, 41 cm at 70°, 85 cm at 80° for $w$ = 30 cm. Mixed pixels at edges put points in empty space. Angular error converts to lateral error $R\tan\delta$: 0.05° at 100 m is 8.7 cm.

**Environment.** Two-way fog attenuation $A = 2\alpha R$ (dB): at 10 dB/km, 2 dB at 100 m; at 30 dB/km, 6 dB at 100 m (signal 25 percent). A sensor with 200 m range in clear air has an equivalent detection range of about 144 m at 10 dB/km and 100 m at 30 dB/km (same received-power criterion). Sunlight adds shot noise, lowering SNR.

**Budget example (100 m, 10 percent target).** Independent terms: jitter 2.0 cm, residual walk 1.0 cm, TDC 0.43 cm, clock 0.2 cm, temperature residual 1.0 cm: RSS $=\sqrt{4+1+0.19+0.04+1} = 2.5$ cm. Systematic terms (offset) add linearly if uncorrected.
:::

::: long
"I'd organize it as an error budget with five groups, separating random precision from systematic bias.

First, the timing chain. Range error is c over two times timing error, so 100 picoseconds is 1.5 centimeters. Random jitter is roughly the pulse rise time divided by SNR: a 2 nanosecond edge at SNR of 20 gives 100 picoseconds, 1.5 centimeters. Systematic errors include walk, where a fixed-threshold comparator trips earlier for strong pulses: about 13 centimeters between a very weak and a very strong return in a typical case. There's also TDC quantization, clock accuracy, and drift of delays with temperature: 5 picoseconds per kelvin across 100 kelvin is 7.5 centimeters. All of these can be calibrated against temperature and amplitude.

Second, the signal level. Return power scales with reflectivity times cosine of incidence over range squared. Dark targets, grazing angles and long range lower SNR and worsen jitter. At the other extreme retroreflectors saturate the receiver and cause blooming, and glossy surfaces and glass cause multipath ghosts or missing points.

Third, geometry. The beam has a footprint, 30 centimeters at 100 meters for a 3 milliradian beam, so at edges you get mixed points and on slanted surfaces the first return biases toward the near edge, as much as 40 centimeters at 70 degrees. Angular errors from encoders and mirrors become lateral errors: 0.05 degrees is 9 centimeters at 100 meters.

Fourth, the environment. Sunlight adds noise, fog attenuates the signal in both directions, 6 dB at 100 meters in heavy fog, and rain and dust create false returns. Other LiDARs can interfere.

Fifth, the platform: the sensor moves during the scan, and extrinsic calibration and time sync errors displace points.

If I had to improve accuracy I'd calibrate offset, scale and walk against range, reflectivity and temperature, digitize waveforms, use narrow optical filters and coincidence detection, use multi-echo logic, and fuse with other sensors."
:::

::: terms
- **Precision / repeatability**: random scatter of repeated measurements, a standard deviation.
- **Accuracy / bias**: systematic offset between the mean measurement and the true value.
- **Jitter**: random timing error, $\sigma_t\approx t_r/\text{SNR}$.
- **Walk error**: amplitude-dependent bias of a threshold-crossing timer.
- **Mixed pixel**: edge point from a beam straddling two surfaces.
- **Beam divergence / footprint**: spot size at the target, $R\times\text{divergence}$.
- **Multipath**: indirect path via a reflective surface producing ghost or biased points.
- **Blooming**: halo around a bright retroreflector from receiver saturation.
- **Two-way attenuation**: loss through the medium on both outward and return paths.
- **Extrinsic calibration**: the fixed transform between sensor and platform frames.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer lists "weather, reflectivity and distance." An exceptional answer:

- Builds a **budget**: names each term, puts a number on it, and shows how they combine (RSS for random, linear for bias).
- Separates **precision from accuracy** and ties each to a fix (averaging helps precision only; calibration fixes accuracy).
- Derives the main equations: jitter $=t_r/\text{SNR}$, footprint $=R\theta$, angle error $=R\tan\delta$, fog loss $=2\alpha R$.
- Names **walk error** and **incidence bias** as the non-obvious bias terms.
- Discusses **mitigations in hardware** (CFD, waveform digitization, filters, SPAD coincidence, temperature compensation) and **system** (fusion, multi-return logic, calibration campaigns).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Accuracy is the number in the datasheet."** A single number rarely states reflectivity, range, angle and temperature conditions. Ask: at what target, what range, and what confidence level?
- **Averaging to fix bias.** Averaging reduces random jitter by $\sqrt N$ but doesn't remove walk, offset or scale error.
- **Ignoring the footprint.** A narrow "ray" is a model; real beams are 10s of centimeters wide at 100 m, so edges and slanted surfaces matter.
- **Forgetting temperature.** Laser wavelength drifts about 0.3 nm/K for typical 905 nm edge emitters (vendor-dependent), which can walk out of a narrow filter, and electronic delays drift with temperature.
- **Treating fog as only reducing range.** It also creates false returns and range bias from backscatter; and for a sensor at 200 m in clear air, 30 dB/km fog leaves about 100 m effective range.
- **Blaming the sensor for platform errors.** Motion distortion, bad extrinsics or clock offsets show up as "inaccurate LiDAR" but live outside it.
:::

::: deep
## Define the problem: precision, accuracy, and what you're measuring

A single range measurement is $d_{meas} = d_{true} + b + n$, where $b$ is systematic bias (accuracy error) and $n$ random noise (precision). For a point cloud there are two more error directions: **lateral** (angle) and **temporal** (when the measurement was made). Total 3D position error combines range error along the beam and angular error across it. The first step in any accuracy question is to say what's measured against what: at which range, for which target (reflectivity, size, angle), under which environment, using which reference ([[q0871]]).

## 1. Timing electronics

**Jitter.** For a linear leading edge with rise time $t_r$ and noise $\sigma_v$, the crossing time error is $\sigma_t = \sigma_v/(dV/dt) = t_r/\text{SNR}$ ([[q0865]]). The range error is $\sigma_d = ct_r/(2\,\text{SNR})$:

Figure: Jitter falls as 1/SNR and rises with rise time. Getting 1 cm with a 2 ns edge needs SNR of 30; with a 5 ns edge, SNR of 75. SNR falls as $1/R^2$ (and with reflectivity), so precision degrades quickly toward the maximum range. Averaging 16 pulses improves it by 4x at the cost of point rate and motion blur.

**Walk.** Threshold timing depends on amplitude: crossing time $t_c = V_{th}t_r/(0.8A)$ for a linear edge. A range of amplitudes of 100:1 yields tens of centimeters of walk if uncorrected (12.7 cm in our example). Remedies: CFD, dual-threshold timing, amplitude (or pulse-width) based correction tables, waveform fitting.

**Quantization and clock.** A TDC LSB of 100 ps is 1.5 cm; rms quantization error is $\text{LSB}/\sqrt{12}$ = 0.43 cm (negligible if the noise exceeds the LSB, as dither smooths the quantization). A reference clock error of 20 ppm scales range by $(1+2\times10^{-5})$: 2 mm at 100 m, 4 mm at 200 m.

**Temperature.** Propagation delays in the laser driver, laser, comparator and TDC drift with temperature, maybe a few ps/K. Over 100 K (−40 to +60 °C) 5 ps/K is 500 ps = 7.5 cm. Fix: an internal reference channel (a known optical path measured each frame), calibration vs. temperature, or on-chip delay-locked loops.

## 2. Signal level and target properties

Peak return power: $P_r = P_t\rho\cos\alpha\,A_r\eta/(\pi R^2)$ ([[q0864]]). Every factor changes SNR:

- **Reflectivity.** A black tire at 4 percent returns 2.5x less than a 10 percent target and 22x less than a 90 percent white wall. SNR determines jitter and also whether there's any detection.
- **Incidence angle.** Lambertian return goes as $\cos\alpha$: 0.5 at 60°, 0.17 at 80°. At grazing angles on the road, far-range ground points drop out first.
- **Specular surfaces.** A mirror or glossy paint returns most light away from the receiver (dropout) unless pointing straight at it, when it saturates.
- **Retroreflectors** (signs, license plates, road studs) return 100 to 1000+ times more than diffuse white, saturating the TIA and ADC and causing blooming and walk errors; the sensor is blind for the saturation recovery time, hiding weaker nearby objects.
- **Transparent and translucent targets** (glass, rain, foliage) cause partial returns and multiple echoes. The reported range depends on whether you take first, strongest or last.

## 3. Geometry: footprint, edges, angle

**Footprint.** Divergence $\theta_d$ gives a spot of diameter $w = R\theta_d$: 30 cm at 100 m for 3 mrad, 5 cm for 0.5 mrad. Features smaller than the footprint are blurred in range.

**Mixed pixels.** At an object edge, the footprint straddles a near and a far surface; the two echoes may blend into one point at an intermediate range. These "flying points" appear as streaks along the beam between objects.

**Slanted surfaces.** A first-return detector keys on the earliest part of the returned pulse. If the footprint of width $w$ hits a surface tilted by $\theta$ from normal, the nearest part is $\frac{w}{2}\tan\theta$ closer than the center: 8.7, 26, 41 and 85 cm for 30°, 60°, 70° and 80° at $w$ = 30 cm (upper bound; centroid or peak detectors give less). It also broadens the pulse (range spread), which can be used to estimate surface slope.

**Angular knowledge.** Encoder resolution and eccentricity, mirror position error and polygon facet errors cause lateral displacement of $R\tan\delta$:

| Angular error | at 50 m | at 100 m | at 200 m |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01° | 0.9 cm | 1.7 cm | 3.5 cm |
| 0.05° | 4.4 cm | 8.7 cm | 17.5 cm |
| 0.1° | 8.7 cm | 17.5 cm | 34.9 cm |

At long range, angle error dominates over range error, so a "2 cm range accuracy" sensor can still have 17 cm lateral error at 200 m with 0.05° pointing error. Per-channel elevation and azimuth calibration (factory) and encoder correction tables reduce this.

## 4. Environment

**Sunlight.** Solar irradiance near 905 nm adds a background current and shot noise; the detector sees it all the time, not just at the pulse. With a 25 nm band-pass filter and a pixel field of view of 0.1°, the background at the detector is of the order of a nanowatt (an order-of-magnitude estimate that depends on sun angle, target reflectivity and filter), compared with tens of nanowatts for a 10 percent target at 100 m, so SNR falls. SPAD receivers see hundreds of Mcps of background and rely on time-correlated coincidence within the pulse window.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Solar background depends strongly on solar angle, atmosphere, target reflectance and the exact filter and detector geometry. The figure here is an order-of-magnitude illustration of the idea, not data for a specific sensor.

**Fog, rain, snow, dust.** Beer-Lambert attenuation: two-way transmission is $10^{-2\alpha R/10}$ for $\alpha$ in dB/km one-way.

With 10 dB/km fog the curve reaches that limit at about 144 m, and at 30 dB/km at about 100 m, so a "200 m" sensor behaves like a 100 m sensor in dense fog. Precision at the shorter range also degrades because SNR is lower. (The attenuation values are examples; real values depend on visibility and droplet size and are not tied to a standard here.)

Backscatter from droplets near the sensor creates clutter returns at close range, and the receiver may be partly blinded by the near-field scatter. Water droplets on the sensor window attenuate and scatter; dirt does as well, so window heating, wipers and self-test (monitoring the internal reflection level) are used.

**Interference.** Other LiDARs at similar wavelengths and timing can produce ghost points or noise. Mitigation: randomized pulse timing or coding, pulse-pair signatures, narrow time gating, plus post-filter on temporal consistency. FMCW designs are inherently more immune because they require phase coherence.

## 5. Platform: motion, calibration and time

- **Motion distortion:** 2 m at 20 m/s over 100 ms if not deskewed ([[q0868]]).
- **Extrinsic calibration:** a 0.1° yaw error between LiDAR and vehicle frame is 8.7 cm at 50 m.
- **Time synchronization:** a 10 ms offset at 20 m/s is 20 cm.
- **Vibration and mounting:** a rotating mirror or head bearing wobble adds angular error; a bent bracket changes extrinsics.

## An error budget

For a single point at 100 m on a 10 percent target at normal incidence, clear air, 25 °C:

| Term | Type | Value | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timing jitter | random | 2.0 cm | $t_r$ = 2 ns, SNR about 15 |
| Residual walk (after correction) | systematic, amplitude dependent | 1.0 cm | |
| TDC quantization | random | 0.43 cm | LSB 100 ps |
| Clock | systematic | 0.2 cm | 20 ppm |
| Temperature residual | systematic | 1.0 cm | after calibration |
| **RSS total (range)** | | **2.5 cm** | $\sqrt{4+1+0.19+0.04+1}$ |
| Angular (0.05°) | lateral | 8.7 cm | perpendicular to beam |

The RSS adds uncorrelated terms in quadrature; correlated or systematic terms should be added linearly in the worst case. The table shows that at this range the lateral term is 3.5x the range term, a good lesson in where to spend effort.

## 6. How to measure it: a sketch of the experiment

Put targets of several reflectivities (for example 5, 10, 50 and 90 percent calibrated panels) at known distances (verified with a total station or a laser distance meter whose uncertainty is at least 4x better), at several angles; collect thousands of frames per condition; compute the mean error (accuracy) and the standard deviation (precision) per condition; repeat over temperature and in controlled fog. The general experiment design is covered in [[q0871]] and the statistics in [[q0873]].

::: followups
### Which error dominates at long range: range or lateral?
Often lateral. Range error is about 1 to 5 cm and grows slowly with range (SNR falls), while angular error converts to $R\tan\delta$, growing linearly: at 200 m, 0.05° is 17.5 cm. Also, edges and footprint effects (60 cm at 200 m for 3 mrad) add lateral uncertainty. For localization or object boundaries lateral error often matters more.

### How would you calibrate out walk error?
Measure a target at a fixed distance while varying its reflectivity or the laser power (neutral density filters) across the dynamic range, record the apparent range versus amplitude (or pulse width), and fit a correction table or polynomial. In operation, apply it using the measured amplitude/pulse width for each return. Do it at multiple temperatures if walk drifts. With full-waveform digitization and a matched filter, walk is much smaller.

### Why can averaging not solve all LiDAR range errors?
Averaging $N$ pulses reduces independent random noise by $\sqrt N$, but errors that are the same every time (offset, scale, walk for a fixed target, incidence bias, angular error, extrinsic error) are not averaged out. It also costs point rate and can blur moving objects. So after averaging you hit a "systematic floor" that only calibration can lower.

### How do other LiDARs affect your sensor, and what can be done?
Another sensor's pulses at the same wavelength within your receive window cause false detections: random points or ghosts at consistent ranges if pulse timing is similar. Randomize pulse timing (jitter the PRF), use coded pulse trains with correlation at the receiver, narrow the acceptance window (time gating), use polarization or wavelength diversity, and filter points that are not consistent over frames.

### Scenario: your LiDAR reads 12 cm closer on a white wall than on a black wall at the same distance, same angle. Diagnose and fix.
That is amplitude-dependent bias: walk. The white wall returns much more power so the fixed threshold crosses earlier. Verify by measuring the same wall with attenuators in front of the receiver: apparent range should shift with amplitude. Fix: add constant-fraction timing, or calibrate range vs amplitude/pulse width and correct per return; check receiver saturation, which also stretches the pulse. See the walk analysis in [[q0865]].
:::
