::: basics
### In plain words
A half-bridge is two switches stacked between the supply and ground, with the output wire taken from the point between them. Close the top switch and the output connects to the supply. Close the bottom switch and it connects to ground. Flip between the two very fast and a motor sees an average voltage, so you can set its speed. An H-bridge is two half-bridges with the motor between them, which lets the current run either way.

### Picture it
Picture a tap that feeds a pipe from a high tank and a drain valve that dumps the pipe to the ground. Open the tap, then the drain, over and over, many times a second. The water in the pipe settles to an in-between level set by how long the tap stays open. The motor's coil plays the part of a heavy water wheel: it will not stop or start instantly, so it smooths the flicker. The analogy breaks down in one place. When the top switch opens, the coil's current must still go somewhere, so the bottom switch has to give it a path.

### Words you need
- **PWM (pulse-width modulation)**: switching on and off at a steady rate while changing how long each "on" lasts.
- **Duty cycle ($D$)**: the fraction of each cycle the top switch is on. $D=0.25$ means on 25% of the time.
- **Inductor (winding)**: a coil that resists sudden changes in current, so its current cannot jump. See [[q0144]].
- **Freewheel path**: the route the coil's current takes when the top switch opens, through the bottom switch or its body diode.
- **Body diode**: a diode built into every MOSFET that conducts if the current tries to flow backward.
- **Dead time**: a short gap with both switches off so they never conduct together. See [[q0428]].

### The simplest example
Use a 12 V supply and a motor on one half-bridge. Keep the top switch on for 25% of each cycle. The output is 12 V for a quarter of the time and 0 V for the rest, so the average is $0.25 \times 12 = 3$ V. The motor's current rises a little while the top is on and falls a little while the bottom path carries it, but it never stops. Raise the duty to 75% and the average becomes 9 V, so the motor runs faster. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page follows the current through every part of the cycle, then adds the second leg, the two PWM styles, dead time and what happens when the motor pushes energy back.
:::

::: why
The H-bridge is what actually sits between a microcontroller and nearly every small motor. If you only know its truth table you can't answer the real follow-ups: why the motor current looks the way it does, why efficiency depends on the freewheel path, why a bridge gets hot with no load, or why a motor that "coasts" pumps up the supply. This question is the link between the *drawing* questions ([[q0420]], [[q0421]]) and the *design* ones (braking, high-side drive, shoot-through).
:::

::: concepts
- **Switch node and complementary switching**: the output is pulled to $V_M$ or ground by one of two switches ([[q0421]]).
- **PWM average**: the load sees $D\,V_M$ if switching is fast compared with $L/R$ ([[q0145]]).
- **Inductor current continuity**: $v=L\,di/dt$, so the current cannot jump; it needs a path at every instant ([[q0043]], [[q0144]]).
- **Back-EMF**: a spinning motor is also a voltage source, $E=K_e\omega$ ([[q0333]], [[q0334]]).
- **Body diodes and synchronous rectification**: the freewheel path can be a diode or the opposite FET turned on.
- **Dead time**: both FETs off between transitions ([[q0428]]).
:::

::: answer
"Think of a half-bridge as a switch that connects the output either to the supply or to ground. If I flip it fast, with the top switch on for a fraction D of the time, the motor sees an average of D times the supply, so duty cycle sets speed. The motor winding is an inductor, so it smooths the pulses into a nearly steady current.

The important part is what happens when the top switch turns off. The inductor current can't stop, so the output node swings down until the bottom switch's body diode catches it, or until I turn the bottom FET on, and the current freewheels through the motor and that path. That's why a half-bridge always has two devices: one pushes current in, the other gives it somewhere to go.

An H-bridge is two half-bridges with the motor between them. If the left leg is high and the right leg is low, the current goes one way; swap them and it reverses. For speed control I can PWM one leg while holding the other low, which gives slow decay and low ripple, or PWM both legs in opposite phase so that 50% duty means zero average voltage, which gives bidirectional control from a single signal. Throughout, I add a bit of dead time on every leg so the top and bottom never conduct together."
:::

::: technical
**Half-bridge, inductive load, forward current.** Model the load as $R_a$, $L_a$ and back-EMF $E$. The output $V_{SW}$ is $V_M$ or 0.

- *On interval* ($Q_H$ on, $0<t<DT$): $L_a\,\dfrac{di}{dt}=V_M-iR_a-E$.
- *Off interval* ($Q_L$ on, or its diode conducting): $L_a\,\dfrac{di}{dt}=-iR_a-E-V_{drop}$, with $V_{drop}=0$ for the FET channel (ignoring $R_{DS(on)}$) and $\approx V_F$ for the diode.

Average voltage balance in steady state: $D V_M=\bar i R_a+E$. So speed, via $E=K_e\omega$, rises with $D$ and falls with load current.

**Ripple.** For $L/R\gg T$: $\Delta i\approx\dfrac{V_M D(1-D)T}{L_a}$, largest at $D=0.5$.

**H-bridge.** The load voltage is $v_{AB}=v_A-v_B$.

| Scheme | Switching | $\bar v_{AB}$ | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sign-magnitude (single-leg PWM) | Leg B held low, leg A PWM'd with duty $D$ | $D V_M$ | Load sees $V_M$ or 0; ripple at $f_{sw}$; freewheel through the low sides; direction chosen by which leg is PWM'd |
| Lock-anti-phase | Both legs complementary: $Q_1\&Q_4$ for $D$, $Q_2\&Q_3$ for $1-D$ | $(2D-1)V_M$ | Load sees $\pm V_M$; zero speed at $D=0.5$ with ripple current still flowing; ripple $2\times$ larger at the same duty (worse still at low output); more loss |

**Slow vs fast decay.** Slow decay: freewheel with both low sides on (winding shorted; decay set by $R$ only). Fast decay: all off, so the current is driven against $V_M$ through two diodes; decay rate $\approx (V_M+2V_F)/L$. Chopper current-control drives use both.

**Dead time and the diode.** Between transitions both FETs of a leg are off for $t_d$, so the diode conducts and dissipates $V_F\,i$; synchronous rectification (turning the low side on during the off interval) removes most of it.

**Regeneration.** If the motor is decelerating, $E>\bar v$ and the current reverses: the motor pushes charge into $V_M$. The supply must absorb it (a bulk capacitor, a battery) or the bus rises ([[q0424]]).
:::

::: short
"A half-bridge connects the output to the supply or to ground, and PWM duty sets the average output. The inductive motor current can't stop when the top switch opens, so it freewheels through the bottom FET or its body diode. An H-bridge is two half-bridges with the motor in between: one leg high and the other low gives one direction, the opposite gives reverse, and PWM on one leg, or on both in anti-phase, sets speed. Dead time keeps both FETs in a leg from conducting together."
:::

::: long
"Start with one leg. Two switches in series, supply on top, ground on the bottom, motor on the middle node. If the top is on, the node is at the supply and current flows into the motor. If I turn the top off, the motor's inductance wants to keep that current going, so the node gets pulled down until the bottom switch's body diode turns on. After a short dead time I turn the bottom FET on too, so the current freewheels through its low resistance instead of the diode drop.

Now if I toggle that at 20 kilohertz with a duty cycle D, the inductance averages the pulses, so the motor sees D times the supply, a nearly steady current, and a speed that tracks D. Back-EMF matters too: the motor current is set by the difference between the average drive voltage and the back-EMF, divided by the winding resistance. That's why at stall you get a big current and at speed you get a small one.

An H-bridge is two of those legs sharing the motor. For forward I hold the right leg low and PWM the left leg. For reverse I swap them. That's sign-magnitude: the motor sees zero or the supply voltage, so the ripple is small and the current freewheels through the low sides. The alternative is to PWM both legs in opposite phase. Then the motor sees plus or minus the supply, 50% duty means zero average voltage, and I get bidirectional control from a single duty-cycle number. It's easy to control and it has a nice linear response through zero, but the ripple is larger and the losses are higher.

Everything has to obey two rules. Never turn on both FETs in a leg, so I insert dead time. And always give the current somewhere to go when I switch off, which is the body diodes or the synchronous FET. One more thing: when the motor is decelerating it pushes current back into the supply, so the supply has to be able to handle it."
:::

::: terms
- **PWM duty ($D$)**: fraction of the period the high side is on.
- **Slow decay**: freewheel through the low-side FETs (or both high sides); current decays slowly through $R$.
- **Fast decay**: all switches off; current decays against the supply through the diodes.
- **Sign-magnitude drive**: one signal for direction, one for PWM magnitude.
- **Lock-anti-phase drive**: both legs PWM'd complementary; $\bar v=(2D-1)V_M$.
- **Back-EMF ($E=K_e\omega$)**: voltage generated by the rotating armature that opposes the drive.
- **Synchronous rectification**: turning the FET on during freewheeling to replace a diode drop with $I R_{DS(on)}$.
- **Regeneration**: energy returned to the supply when the motor decelerates.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying the motor sees the PWM waveform directly.** The winding inductance filters it; for $L/R\gg T$ the current is nearly constant and the average voltage is what matters.
- **Thinking the off interval has zero current.** The current continues through the freewheel path; if it didn't, the node would spike.
- **Assuming 50% duty gives zero voltage in sign-magnitude drive.** It gives $0.5V_M$; only lock-anti-phase has $\bar v=0$ at 50%.
- **Ignoring back-EMF.** Current is $(V-E)/R$, not $V/R$. Start-up and stall currents are far larger than the running current.
- **Neglecting regeneration.** A decelerating motor sends energy into the supply; a battery or large bulk cap may handle it, a bench supply or diode-fed rail may not.
:::

## Adding back-EMF

A spinning motor produces $E=K_e\omega$ against the drive. The steady-state average balance is
$$D V_M=\bar i R_a+K_e\omega,$$
so $\bar i=(D V_M-K_e\omega)/R_a$. At start-up ($\omega=0$) the current is $D V_M/R_a$, which for a 1 Ω, 12 V motor is 12 A at full duty. As the motor accelerates the back-EMF rises and the current falls to what the load torque needs ($T=K_t\,\bar i$). That's why **start-up current limiting** matters for the bridge, and why speed control by duty works: the speed settles where $K_e\omega=D V_M-\bar i R_a$ ([[q0345]]).

**Sign-magnitude.** For forward, keep $Q_4$ on and PWM $Q_1$ (with $Q_3$ complementary, or off and relying on the diode). The motor sees $V_M$ for $DT$ and zero for the rest, with ripple at $f_{sw}$. Reverse swaps the legs. Low ripple, low switching loss (only one leg switches), and the freewheel is slow decay through $Q_3$ and $Q_4$.

**Lock-anti-phase (bipolar).** Both legs switch together: $Q_1\&Q_4$ for $D$, $Q_2\&Q_3$ for $1-D$. The motor sees $+V_M$ for $DT$ and $-V_M$ for $(1-D)T$:
$$\bar v_{AB}=(2D-1)V_M.$$
The current ripple is $\Delta i_{pp}=2V_M D(1-D)T/L$, twice the sign-magnitude value at the same duty. At $D=0.5$ the average voltage is zero but the winding still carries a triangular ripple. Example: $V_M=12$ V, $T=50$ µs, $L=1$ mH: $\Delta i_{pp}=2\times12\times0.25\times50\,\mu s/1\,mH=0.3$ A, versus $0.15$ A for sign-magnitude at 50%. The benefit is a single smooth duty-to-voltage law through zero, which is why position servos often use it.

## Why slow decay is slower

In the off interval of sign-magnitude drive the freewheel voltage across the winding is about 0 (shorted through the low-side FETs): $di/dt=-iR/L$. For fast decay (all off) the winding sees $-(V_M+2V_F)$ and $di/dt\approx-(V_M+1.4)/L$. With $V_M=12$ V and $L=1$ mH that is 13.4 A/ms, versus 5 A/ms for slow decay at 5 A through 1 Ω. Fast decay tracks a falling current command (stepper chopping); slow decay keeps ripple down.

## Dead time, diode drops and synchronous rectification

When a leg changes state the gate signals leave a gap $t_d$ (50 to 200 ns). If the current leaves SW, then during the gap the low-side body diode conducts: loss $V_F\,i$ for $2t_d f_{sw}$ of each period. For 5 A, 100 ns, 20 kHz, 0.7 V that is 14 mW, negligible. But if the diode carried the whole off interval it would dissipate $V_F i=3.5$ W at 5 A, while turning the low FET on gives $i^2R_{DS(on)}=0.5$ W (20 mΩ). That is synchronous rectification. The cost is that the low FET must switch, and it must be off before the high side comes back ([[q0207]]).

## Regeneration and what the supply sees

When $D$ is lowered quickly the motor is still spinning at $E>D V_M$, so $\bar i<0$. The current flows from the motor back into the supply through the body diodes or synchronous FETs. If the source can't sink current (a diode-ORed supply, a bench supply, a regulator), the bus voltage rises. Example: a 3.6 J rotor stopping, with only a 470 µF capacitor to absorb it at 12 V: $\tfrac12 C(V^2-12^2)=3.6$ J gives $V=\sqrt{144+2\times3.6/470\,\mu F}=124$ V. Nothing survives that, so you need a battery or load that can absorb the energy, a TVS or brake chopper, a larger capacitor, or a gentler ramp ([[q0424]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Brushed DC motor drivers** (printers, robots, tools): sign-magnitude PWM with current limiting and brake.
- **Servo and positioning drives**: lock-anti-phase for smooth zero crossing.
- **Stepper chopper drives**: slow/fast decay switching to regulate winding current.
- **Class-D audio amplifiers**: the same bridge with a high-frequency PWM representing the audio waveform.
- **Solenoid and valve drivers**: peak-and-hold current using the half-bridge freewheel path.
- **Wireless-power and induction systems**: a full bridge exciting a resonant tank.
:::

::: assumptions
- Switching frequency high enough that $L/R\gg T$ (small ripple). At low $f_{sw}$ the motor current has large ripple and the average-voltage picture breaks down.
- Motor modelled as $R$-$L$ plus $E=K_e\omega$; saturation, brush drops and friction ignored.
- Ideal gate timing with dead time covering the delays.
- Continuous conduction (the current never reaches zero in the off interval). At light load with a diode freewheel the current can reach zero and the node floats at $E$ (discontinuous mode); with synchronous rectification the current goes negative instead.
- The supply can sink regenerated current unless stated.
:::

::: followups
### What does the motor current look like at 50% duty at stall?
The average is $D V_M/R_a$ (6 A for 12 V and 1 Ω) with a triangular ripple of $\Delta i\approx V_M D(1-D)T/L$; for 20 kHz and 1 mH that is 0.15 A pk-pk. At stall there is no back-EMF to oppose it, so it is far above the running current.

### Why does the bridge get hot with no load?
No load means no conduction loss, so the heat is switching: gate charge, $C_{oss}$, body-diode reverse recovery, and shoot-through if the dead time is too short. Lock-anti-phase also circulates $\pm V_M$ ripple current at zero speed.

### Which PWM scheme would you pick for a servo, and why?
Lock-anti-phase for positioning: one duty-cycle input, a linear response through zero speed and current always flowing so there's no dead zone. The cost is higher ripple and loss. For a fan or pump I'd choose sign-magnitude.

### What if the motor current reaches zero during the off interval?
That is discontinuous conduction. With a diode freewheel the motor terminal floats to the back-EMF $E$ once $i=0$ and the average output voltage is no longer $D V_M$ (it is higher, as in a DCM buck). With synchronous rectification the current can go negative, so the bridge behaves like CCM at the expense of circulating current.

### The bus voltage spikes when I command the motor to stop. Why?
Regeneration. The motor's kinetic energy $\tfrac12J\omega^2$ is pushed back through the bridge into the bus. If the bus can't absorb it, it rises by about $\sqrt{V^2+2E/C}-V$. Fixes: a larger bulk capacitor, a TVS or brake resistor chopper, a battery that can accept charge, or a slower deceleration ramp.
:::

::: interpretations
"Half/H-bridge" means both: the half-bridge as the building block and the H-bridge made from two. This page covers single-leg operation first because it explains everything the H-bridge adds. "How does it work" can be read as the switch-level truth table ([[q0420]]), the inductive/freewheel behaviour (this page) or the gate-drive details ([[q0426]], [[q0427]]); an interviewer may steer toward any of them.
