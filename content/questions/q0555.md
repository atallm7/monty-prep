::: testing
Whether you can say *which* mechanism punishes a long trace, because there are two different answers depending on which trace you mean:

1. **Power-side copper** between the resistor and where you measure: resistance (and its temperature drift) adds straight into the reading.
2. **The sense pair itself**: its resistance barely matters, but its *length and loop area* decide how much noise it picks up, and how much it lets the amplifier's bias current and filter interact.

A strong answer separates the two, quantifies the noise pickup, and doesn't just say "parasitics".
:::

::: why
In a current-sense circuit the signal is tiny (tens of millivolts) and the aggressors are large (a switch node with volts per nanosecond and an inductor with amps per nanosecond). Every extra millimetre of trace adds coupling capacitance, mutual inductance and loop area. Boards fail in the lab with "the current reading is noisy" or "the over-current trip fires randomly", and the root cause is nearly always geometry. Being able to explain it with a loop-area calculation is what makes the "keep it short" rule memorable instead of folklore.
:::

::: concepts
- **Faraday's law**: a changing magnetic flux through a loop induces $v = d\Phi/dt = A\,dB/dt$. A bigger loop picks up proportionally more. See [[q0565]].
- **Capacitive coupling**: $i = C_m\,dv/dt$, and $C_m$ grows with the length that runs alongside the aggressor.
- **Trace resistance** $R = \rho L/(wt)$, relevant for the *power* path in the measurement. See [[q0554]].
- **Common-mode vs differential noise**: only the part that differs between the two sense lines survives the amplifier. See [[q0449]].
- **Antenna length**: a longer conductor intercepts more of an ambient field.
- **Bias current and filter resistance**: the sense path sees $I_{bias}R_f$ as an offset. See [[q0452]].
:::

::: answer
"There are two reasons depending on which trace. For the power copper between the resistor and the measurement point, a long trace adds its own resistance to the measurement. A 1 mm wide, 1 ounce trace is about half a milliohm per millimetre, and a sense resistor might be 5 milliohms, so ten millimetres of trace is as big as the resistor, and it drifts with temperature because copper's coefficient is about 0.39 percent per degree.

For the sense pair itself, the problem is noise. The two traces carry a signal of tens of millivolts, so they're picking up whatever's nearby: capacitive coupling from the switch node and magnetic coupling from the power loop. Both grow with length. Faraday's law says the induced voltage is the loop area times dB/dt, and the loop area is the length times the spacing of the pair. So double the length and I double the area, and double the pickup. With a 0.2 millimetre gap, 50 millimetres of sense pair next to a 5 amp, 20 nanosecond edge can pick up tens of millivolts. That's the signal. Short traces make the loop small, keep the amplifier next to the resistor, and let the filter see the noise before it gets amplified."
:::

::: technical
**Case 1: power-side copper in the measurement.** Resistance $R = \rho L/(wt)$. For 1 oz copper at $\rho = 1.72\times10^{-8}$ Ω·m, a 1 mm wide trace has 0.49 mΩ/mm. In a non-Kelvin layout where the sense lines tap the power trace, this adds to $R_s$ as a gain error and drifts at about 3900 ppm/°C, versus tens of ppm/°C for the resistor alloy. Fix: Kelvin tap at the pad ([[q0554]], [[q0557]]) and a short, wide force path.

**Case 2: the sense pair.** Almost no current flows, so $R_{trace}$ is irrelevant: a 50 mm × 0.15 mm 1 oz trace is only about 164 mΩ, which is nothing next to a 10 Ω filter resistor. What counts is coupling.

- **Magnetic (loop area).** Induced voltage $v = A\,\dot B$ with $A = g\cdot L$ (gap × length). Near a conductor carrying $I$ at distance $r$, $B = \mu_0 I/(2\pi r)$. For 5 A at 10 mm, $B = 0.1$ mT; changing in 20 ns gives $\dot B \approx 5\times10^{3}$ T/s. With $g = 0.2$ mm and $L = 50$ mm, $v = 0.2\text{ mm}\cdot 50\text{ mm}\cdot 5\times10^3 = 50$ mV. That equals the full signal at 5 mΩ × 10 A.
- **Electric (capacitive).** $C_m$ scales with the parallel run length; the injected current is $C_m\,dv/dt$. Because it enters both lines, a *matched* pair converts most of it to common-mode, but the mismatch is proportional to length.
- **Pickup of ambient RF.** A longer pair is a bigger antenna, so it intercepts more of any external field ([[q0563]]).

**Amplifier-side effects.** A long unfiltered run lets noise arrive at the filter after it has already been rectified or amplified by the board's parasitics. Put the filter at the amplifier pins and keep the run before it short. Trace inductance (roughly 3 to 5 nH/cm for a narrow trace over a nearby plane, closer to 10 nH/cm with no nearby return) in series with a filter capacitor creates a resonance that the filter resistors have to damp.

**Rule of thumb.** Short, *and* tight (small gap), *and* matched, *and* over a plane. Length and gap multiply to give the loop area.
:::

::: short
"For the power copper between the resistor and the tap point, short matters because copper resistance adds to the measurement, about half a milliohm per millimetre at 1 mm wide, and it drifts with temperature. For the sense pair, resistance doesn't matter but noise does: pickup is loop area times dB/dt, and loop area is length times gap. A 50 mm pair with a 0.2 mm gap near a 5 amp, 20 ns edge can pick up tens of millivolts, which is the signal. Short, tight, matched pairs keep that small."
:::

::: long
"I'd split the question by which trace we're talking about. First, the power traces between the sense resistor and wherever the measurement is actually taken. Those carry the full load current, so they have a voltage drop, and if my sense lines are connected beyond them, that drop is part of my measurement. A 1 ounce, 1 millimetre wide trace is about half a milliohm per millimetre. A typical sense resistor is 5 or 10 milliohms. So 10 millimetres of copper is as big as the resistor, and copper has about 0.39 percent per degree temperature coefficient, so my reading drifts with temperature. The fix is to tap at the resistor pads, which is the Kelvin connection, and to keep the power path short and wide so it dissipates little.

Second, the sense lines themselves. They carry almost no current, so their resistance isn't the issue; fifty millimetres is only about 160 milliohms, and my filter resistors are 10 ohms. What matters is noise pickup. Faraday's law says the induced voltage is the area enclosed by the loop times the rate of change of field, and the loop area is basically length times the gap between the two traces. So twice as long is twice the area is twice the noise. Take a 0.2 millimetre gap, 50 millimetres long, near a hot loop carrying 5 amps with 20 nanosecond edges, and I get around 50 millivolts of induced voltage, which is as big as the signal. Capacitive coupling to the switch node also scales with length, and a longer pair is a bigger antenna for ambient RF.

So the rules are short, tightly spaced, matched in length, over a ground plane, away from the switch node, and the filter and amplifier go right at the end of the pair."
:::

::: terms
- **Loop area**: the area between the signal conductor and its return; magnetic pickup is proportional to it.
- **Faraday's law**: induced voltage equals the rate of change of magnetic flux through the loop.
- **Mutual capacitance / inductance**: the coupling between two circuits; both grow with the length they run together.
- **Differential-mode noise**: noise that differs between the two sense lines; the amplifier cannot reject it.
- **Common-mode noise**: noise that appears equally on both lines; a good amplifier rejects it.
- **Force path / sense path**: the high-current path versus the measurement path of a Kelvin connection.
- **CMRR**: common-mode rejection ratio of the amplifier.
- **Aggressor / victim**: the noisy circuit and the circuit that picks the noise up.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "short traces reduce noise and resistance".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Splits the question into the power-side copper (resistance and temperature drift) and the sense pair (coupling, with negligible resistance).
- Gives the loop-area reasoning (area = length × gap) and a quick number for the induced voltage.
- Says that a *tight* pair is just as important as a *short* one, because gap multiplies length.
- Notes that matching turns coupled noise into common-mode, and that mismatch scales with length.
- Mentions filter and amplifier placement and ties the answer back to Kelvin routing and to the switch node as the aggressor.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Short to reduce resistance" for the sense lines.** They carry almost no current, so their resistance is irrelevant. It is the coupling and loop area that matter.
- **Keeping the trace short but wide-gapped.** Length × gap is the loop area. A short pair with a big gap is not much better than a long one with a tiny gap.
- **Shortening only one line.** A mismatch in length or path converts common-mode noise to differential.
- **Ignoring the power path.** The wide copper to the resistor needs to be short too, because its resistance and inductance add to the measurement and its loop radiates.
- **Assuming the filter fixes it.** A filter removes noise above its corner but cannot remove noise that is in-band, and if the noise is coupled after the filter it does nothing.
:::

::: deep
## Two traces, two answers

"The traces" in a sense-resistor circuit are of two kinds, and short is good for both, for different reasons.

| Trace | Carries | Short matters because |
|---|---|---|
| Force path (wide copper to and from the resistor) | The full load current | Resistance, heating and loop inductance of the high-current loop |
| Sense pair (thin traces to the amplifier) | Microamps at most, a signal of tens of mV | Noise pickup, loop area and antenna length |

## Power-side copper

If the sense lines tap the force path away from the pads, the measurement includes the copper in between. Copper's resistivity is $1.72\times10^{-8}$ Ω·m, so

$$R = \rho\frac{L}{wt}.$$

For $t = 35$ µm and $w = 1$ mm, that's 0.49 mΩ/mm. Ten millimetres is 4.9 mΩ; against a 5 mΩ resistor, that is a 98 % error. Of that, the copper part has a temperature coefficient of 3900 ppm/°C (0.39 %/°C), versus 50 to 100 ppm/°C for typical alloy resistors. So the error is both large and a moving target. The layout solution is Kelvin routing; the second solution is a short, wide force path so that *whatever* ends up in the sense loop is small. See [[q0554]] and [[q0557]].

## The sense pair: it is a loop

Think of the two sense traces plus the amplifier input as a loop. Faraday's law says the loop's induced voltage is

$$v = \frac{d\Phi}{dt} = A\,\frac{dB}{dt},\qquad A = g\cdot L,$$

where $g$ is the gap between the two traces and $L$ the length. Here is a concrete estimate. A hot loop in a buck converter carries 5 A and switches in about 20 ns. At 10 mm from the nearest copper the field is $B = \mu_0 I/(2\pi r) = 2\times10^{-7}\cdot 5/0.01 = 0.1$ mT. Changing by that much in 20 ns, $\dot B = 5\times10^{3}$ T/s. So:

| Pair | Area | Induced voltage |
|---|---|---|
| 10 mm long, 0.2 mm gap | 2 mm² | 10 mV |
| 50 mm long, 0.2 mm gap | 10 mm² | 50 mV |
| 50 mm long, 1 mm gap | 50 mm² | 250 mV |

(These are illustrative estimates for one particular geometry: the real field depends on distance and orientation, and a plane underneath reduces it a lot. But the proportionality is the point.)

## Capacitive pickup and mismatch

A switch node swinging 12 V in 5 ns slews at 2.4 V/ns. Every 0.1 pF of mutual capacitance injects 0.24 mA into the victim. If it lands equally on both sense lines (matched pair, similar impedances) it is common-mode and mostly rejected. If it lands unequally, because one line is longer or runs closer to the aggressor, the difference is a differential error. The mismatch grows with length.

## The amplifier end

The amplifier input has a bias current, the filter has resistance, and the layout has a trace. The bias current times $R_f$ is an offset. A long, thin trace adds little to that, but long traces make the two lines differ in capacitance to ground and in coupling, which hurts CMRR. They also add inductance, which with the filter capacitor makes a resonance that needs damping (for a series RLC, $Q = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{L/C}$; with 50 nH and 100 nF, $\sqrt{L/C} = 0.71$ Ω, so 10 Ω filter resistors give $Q \approx 0.07$, heavily damped, while a 0.5 Ω resistor would give $Q \approx 1.4$ and visible ringing).

## What "short" means in practice

- Place the amplifier within a few millimetres of the resistor's sense pickoff.
- If the amplifier cannot be near, send the *amplified* signal over the distance, not the millivolt-level sense voltage ([[q0556]]).
- Keep the force path short, wide and symmetric, with a ground plane under it.
- Route the sense pair together, same layer, with no stubs, and keep it out of the hot loop's neighbourhood.
:::

::: applications
- **Buck converter current limit**: the controller's sense input sits within a few millimetres of the resistor; the pair is routed away from the switch node.
- **Battery gauge IC**: a short Kelvin pair to a gauge placed beside the shunt, even though the host MCU is across the board.
- **Motor drive shunts**: three shunts, each with its own amplifier close by, instead of long sense lines back to a central ADC.
- **Hot-swap controllers**: the sense pins sit right at the sense resistor, since a false trip from noise would shut the rail.
- **Bench power supplies / electronic loads**: precision shunts with four-terminal connections and short sense lines to the measurement chip.
:::

::: assumptions
- **The sense lines carry negligible current.** If the amplifier draws appreciable input current (some current-output types do), the trace and filter resistances matter more.
- **The noise sources are nearby switching nodes or loops.** In a quiet analog-only board, length matters less (but still sets antenna pickup).
- **The estimates use idealised single-loop fields.** A real board has a ground plane that cancels much of the magnetic pickup (the plane carries image currents); the proportionality to loop area is what holds.
- **Frequencies are well below the sense trace resonance.** A 50 mm trace is far shorter than $\lambda/10$ up to roughly 300 MHz in FR-4, so it behaves like a lumped loop.
- **"Short" assumes you can place the amplifier near.** If you cannot, the answer changes to conditioning at the source ([[q0556]]).
:::

::: followups
### Does the resistance of the sense traces matter?
Almost never. The amplifier input draws only the bias current (nanoamps to a few microamps for most parts), so a 50 mm × 0.15 mm 1 oz trace at about 164 mΩ drops essentially nothing. The exceptions are current-output sense amplifiers, where input current flows through the trace and external resistor, and the filter resistors, which are far larger than the trace anyway.

### How would you compute the pickup in a loop for a given aggressor?
Faraday: $v = A\,dB/dt$, with $A$ the loop area. For a nearby wire, $B = \mu_0 I/(2\pi r)$, and $dB/dt$ is that times the current's slew. Example: 5 A over 20 ns at 10 mm gives about 5×10³ T/s, so a 10 mm² loop sees 50 mV. Change the geometry (halve the gap or length) and it halves.

### The sense lines are short but the readings are still noisy. What next?
Check symmetry first: same layer, same length, same neighbours. Then check the reference: is there an unbroken plane under the pair, and does it have a via-fence or at least no cuts? Then filter: a differential RC with the amplifier's input resistors in mind. Finally relocate or shield: put ground between the pair and the aggressor, or move the aggressor.

### What if the sense resistor has to be 5 cm from the amplifier?
Then the pair should be as tight as possible, matched, over a continuous plane and flanked by ground vias, or the amplifier should be moved to the resistor and the output sent instead. See [[q0556]].

### Is shorter always better for the *force* path?
For resistance and loop inductance, yes. But the force path also has to dissipate heat: a very short trace into a tiny pad can create a hot spot. Use wide copper (or a pour) rather than a thin short neck.
:::
