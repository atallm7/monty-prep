::: testing
Whether you can design a first-order **RL filter** and, importantly, know its quirks compared with the RC version. The interviewer wants:

1. Both circuits: LPF is the inductor in series with the load; HPF is a series resistor with the inductor shunted to ground.
2. The corner $f_c=R/(2\pi L)$ and that $R$ here is the *total* resistance the inductor sees (source plus load for the LPF).
3. A design with real numbers and an honest comment on practicality: at audio frequencies RL needs huge inductors, so RL filters live at RF, in power circuits, or where DC current must pass.
4. Real-inductor effects: DCR sets a floor in the HPF's stopband and a DC loss in the LPF, and the SRF limits the high-frequency behaviour.
:::

::: why
Single-inductor filters are common in practice even when nobody calls them filters: a choke on a supply line, a ferrite bead on a cable, an RF choke in a bias line, a bias-tee. Designing one on the spot shows that you understand the duality with the RC circuits and the real constraints of inductors (size, DCR, saturation, SRF). It's also a good place to demonstrate you know why RC is the default at low frequency.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor impedance** ([[q0040]]): $j\omega L$, zero at DC, large at high frequency.
- **RL as a divider**: low-pass takes the output across the resistor; high-pass takes it across the inductor.
- **Series/shunt placement** ([[q0052]]): series L passes low frequencies; shunt L shorts them.
- **Time constant**: $\tau=L/R$.
- **Real inductor** ([[q0047]]): DCR, winding capacitance (SRF), saturation.
- **Standard values**: inductors come in E6/E12 sets with ±10–20 % tolerance.
:::

::: answer
"With one inductor I need a resistor too, since a filter is a divider. If the inductor is in series with the signal and the output is across the load resistor, that's a low-pass filter: at low frequency the inductor is a short and the signal passes, at high frequency its impedance is large and it blocks. If instead the signal goes through a series resistor and the inductor goes from the output to ground, the inductor shorts out low frequencies and passes high frequencies, so that's a high-pass.

For both, the corner is R over two pi L. To design a low-pass at 15.9 kilohertz into a 1 kilohm load, L equals R over two pi f, which is 10 millihenries. For a 50 ohm RF system with a 10 megahertz low-pass, L is about 800 nanohenries, so an 820 nanohenry part.

The caveat is practicality. At low frequencies you'd need enormous inductors: a 100 hertz corner with 1 kilohm needs 1.6 henries, which is why at audio and below we use RC filters instead. RL filters make sense at RF, or where the inductor has to carry DC current anyway, like a choke on a power line.

And a real inductor has winding resistance. In the high-pass, the DC resistance sets a floor: with 50 ohms of DCR against 1 kilohm, the stopband only reaches about minus 26 dB. In the low-pass, the DCR slightly drops the DC gain. And above its self-resonant frequency, the inductor becomes a capacitor, so the filter stops working."
:::

::: technical
**Low-pass (series L, source $R_s$, load $R_L$):**
$$H(s)=\frac{R_L}{R_s+R_L+sL},\quad f_c=\frac{R_s+R_L}{2\pi L},\quad H(0)=\frac{R_L}{R_s+R_L}.$$
For $R_s\to0$: $f_c=R_L/(2\pi L)$.

**High-pass (series $R$, shunt $L$ with DCR $r$):**
$$H(s)=\frac{sL+r}{sL+R+r},\quad f_c=\frac{R+r}{2\pi L},\quad |H|_{DC}=\frac{r}{R+r},\quad |H|_\infty=1.$$
For $r\to0$: $H=\dfrac{sL}{R+sL}$, $f_c=R/(2\pi L)$.

**Design:** $L=R/(2\pi f_c)$; round to a standard value and recompute. Check: $f\ll$ SRF; $I_{DC}<I_{sat}$; DCR effect.

**Examples**
- LPF 15.9 kHz into 1 kΩ: $L=10$ mH. Time constant $\tau=L/R=10$ µs.
- 50 Ω RF LPF at 10 MHz: $L=796$ nH → 820 nH gives 9.7 MHz.
- 50 Ω HPF at 100 MHz: $L=79.6$ nH → 82 nH gives 97 MHz.
- HPF 10 mH, R = 1 kΩ, DCR = 50 Ω: corner 16.7 kHz, stopband floor $=-26.4$ dB.
- 100 Hz corner into 1 kΩ: $L=1.6$ H (impractical).
:::

::: short
"Inductor in series with the load is a low-pass; resistor in series with the inductor to ground is a high-pass. The corner is R over two pi L. For a 15.9 kilohertz low-pass into 1 kilohm I need 10 millihenries. It's only practical at RF or on power lines, because at low frequency the inductors get huge. And real inductors have DCR, which limits the high-pass stopband, and an SRF above which they stop working."
:::

::: long
"A single inductor has no filtering action on its own; I need a resistor, which can be the load or the source, to form a divider. So there are two configurations.

Low-pass: the inductor is in series with the signal and the output is across the load resistor. At low frequency the inductor is nearly a short so the signal gets through; at high frequency its impedance is high, so the load sees less. The transfer function is R over R plus j omega L, with a corner at R over two pi L. The rolloff is 20 dB per decade, and the phase goes from 0 to minus 90 degrees.

High-pass: the signal goes through a series resistor and the output is across the inductor, which is connected to ground. At low frequency the inductor shorts the output to ground; at high frequency its impedance is high, so the signal passes. Same corner, R over two pi L.

Design: I pick the corner and the resistance level set by the system, then compute L. For a 10 megahertz low-pass in a 50 ohm system, L equals 50 over two pi times 10 megahertz, or 800 nanohenries; I'd take 820 nanohenries and get 9.7 megahertz. For a 1 kilohm load and a 16 kilohertz corner, I'd need 10 millihenries, whose time constant is 10 microseconds.

The practical considerations are what you'd expect for an inductor. First, size and cost: scaling goes the opposite way from RC, as lower frequency requires a bigger L. A 100 hertz corner into 1 kilohm needs 1.6 henries, so RC wins there. Second, DCR: in the high-pass the inductor isn't a perfect short at DC, so with 50 ohms DCR and a 1 kilohm resistor the stopband attenuation is limited to about 26 dB. Third, the self-resonant frequency: above it the winding capacitance dominates and the low-pass stops attenuating, and the high-pass response is distorted. And fourth, saturation if DC current flows. So RL filters show up as chokes, RF bias networks, and line filters, not as audio filters."
:::

::: terms
- **RL low-pass**: series inductor, output across the load resistor.
- **RL high-pass**: series resistor, output across a shunt inductor.
- **Corner frequency**: $R_{total}/(2\pi L)$.
- **Time constant $\tau=L/R$**: sets the step-response speed.
- **DCR**: inductor winding resistance, limits the high-pass stopband.
- **Stopband floor**: the minimum attenuation set by DCR, $r/(R+r)$.
- **SRF**: frequency above which the inductor behaves as a capacitor.
- **Inductance tolerance**: ±10–20 % typical, shifting the corner.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws the two circuits and quotes $R/(2\pi L)$.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws both with the output taken across the correct element, and says which filter is which.
- Includes the source resistance for the LPF and the DCR for the HPF in the corner and the stopband floor.
- Designs with real numbers and rounds to standard values, with the effect of tolerance on the corner.
- Explains why RL is rarely used at low frequency (huge $L$) and where it *is* used (RF, supply chokes, bias tees).
- Checks SRF, saturation current and DCR drop as design constraints.
- Notes the contrast with RC: the corner moves *up* when $R$ goes up, the opposite of RC.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Taking the output across the wrong element.** LPF: across the resistor; HPF: across the inductor.
- **Using the formula $1/(2\pi RC)$ by habit.** The RL corner is $R/(2\pi L)$.
- **Forgetting DCR.** A shunt inductor is not a perfect short at DC; the HPF stopband floor is $r/(R+r)$.
- **Ignoring the SRF.** A 10 mH inductor can have an SRF of a few hundred kHz; above it, the LPF stops attenuating.
- **Ignoring source resistance.** In the LPF the corner is set by $R_s+R_L$ and the DC gain is $R_L/(R_s+R_L)$.
- **Choosing RL where RC works.** At 1 kHz you'd need 160 mH for 1 kΩ, versus 160 nF in an RC.
- **Forgetting saturation** when the filter carries DC (a power-line choke).
:::

**Low-pass derivation.** Output across $R_L$: $V_{out}/V_{in}=R_L/(R_L+j\omega L)$ with $|H|=1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}$, $f_c=R_L/(2\pi L)$. As $f\to0$, $H\to1$ (inductor short); as $f\to\infty$, $H\to0$.

**High-pass derivation.** Output across $L$: $V_{out}/V_{in}=j\omega L/(R+j\omega L)$; $f\to0$: 0; $f\to\infty$: 1.

## Real inductor effects on the HPF

With winding resistance $r$ in series with the shunt inductor, the HPF becomes
$$H(s)=\frac{sL+r}{sL+R+r},$$
which flattens at $r/(R+r)$ at low frequencies and has corner $(R+r)/(2\pi L)$.

## Design walk-throughs

**1. 16 kHz low-pass into 1 kΩ (power or audio-rate line).** $L=R/(2\pi f_c)=10$ mH. Source resistance of 50 Ω shifts the corner to $(1050)/(2\pi\cdot0.01)=16.7$ kHz and the DC gain to $-0.42$ dB. A 10 mH, 1 kΩ-class load design is physically large: a ferrite or iron-core part rated for the signal.

**2. 50 Ω RF low-pass at 10 MHz.** $L=50/(2\pi\cdot10^7)=796$ nH → 820 nH standard (E12): $f_c=9.7$ MHz (−3 dB). Choose an RF inductor with SRF ≥ 5× the highest frequency of interest (≳ 100 MHz) and low Q-limiting loss. Remember only 20 dB/dec: at 100 MHz the attenuation is only about 20 dB.

**3. 50 Ω high-pass at 100 MHz (e.g. to reject FM-band noise below).** $L=50/(2\pi\cdot10^8)=79.6$ nH → 82 nH → 97 MHz. The inductor is a shunt, so its SRF must be well above 100 MHz and its DCR small relative to 50 Ω. 

**4. 100 Hz high-pass into 1 kΩ.** $L=1.6$ H: a physically heavy iron-core inductor with large DCR and saturation limits. An RC (1.6 µF) does the same job in a tiny package, which is why RL isn't used here.

> [!TIP]
> Rule of thumb: RC filters for low frequencies and high impedance; RL (or LC) filters for RF, low impedance and when DC current has to pass. The impedance level where the choice flips is around the characteristic impedance (tens of ohms) at MHz and above.

## Time-domain behaviour

LPF step response: $v_{out}=V\frac{R_L}{R_s+R_L}(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ with $\tau=L/(R_s+R_L)$. For 10 mH and 1 kΩ, $\tau=10\ \mu$s and the 10–90 % rise time is $2.2\tau=22\ \mu$s. HPF step: at $t=0^+$ the inductor current is zero (it acts as an open circuit), so the output jumps to $V$ and then decays as $v_{out}\approx V\,e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau=L/R$, toward the DCR floor $V\,r/(R+r)$. The inductor's current ramps at $v/L$, so a 5 V step across 10 mH gives 0.5 A/ms.

## Where the single-inductor filter shows up

- **Supply-line chokes:** inductor in series with the rail, load as R; the corner is $R_{load}/(2\pi L)$. A 10 µH choke feeding a 5 A (2.4 Ω) load has $f_c=38$ kHz, which is low for EMI purposes, so a capacitor is added to turn it into an LC filter ([[q0052]]).
- **Bias tees and RF chokes:** a shunt capacitor and series inductor on the DC path.
- **Ferrite beads:** series element with L and loss, acting as a frequency-dependent resistor at EMI frequencies.
- **Matching networks:** series and shunt inductors with capacitors set impedance transformation and filtering simultaneously.
:::

::: applications
- **Power-line chokes** for conducted EMI (series L, with capacitors).
- **RF bias feeds and bias-tees** (RF choke).
- **Ferrite beads** on cables and rails.
- **RF harmonic filtering** at 50 Ω with a series inductor in transmitter outputs.
- **Pulse shaping** in line drivers (L in series for edge-rate control).
- **Speaker crossovers** (series inductor to the woofer as a low-pass).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal first-order behavior** with resistive source/load; real inductors add DCR and SRF effects described above.
- **Linear small-signal**: no saturation (DC current below $I_{sat}$).
- **Frequencies well below the inductor's SRF**; above it the response changes character.
- **Standard values** E12 with ±10–20 % tolerance: the corner shifts correspondingly.
- If "single inductor" allowed a load capacitance or source capacitance, the behaviour would become second-order; the question implies first-order.
:::

::: followups
### Design a low-pass with a 100 kHz corner into 50 Ω.
$L=R/(2\pi f_c)=50/(2\pi\cdot10^5)=79.6$ µH → 82 µH gives 97 kHz. Check the SRF is much higher than 1 MHz, DCR ≪ 50 Ω (<1 Ω, so loss < 2 %), and current rating.

### Why are RC filters preferred for audio?
Audio corner frequencies are low and impedances moderate. For R = 10 kΩ and f_c = 1 kHz an RL filter needs 1.6 H, while an RC needs 16 nF. Large inductors are heavy, expensive, pick up magnetic interference and have large DCR and saturation limits.

### What is the stopband attenuation of an HPF with a 10 mH inductor, 1 kΩ resistor and 100 Ω of DCR at very low frequency?
It's the divider $r/(R+r)=100/1100=0.0909$, i.e. −20.8 dB. The DCR limits the attenuation, no matter how low the frequency.

### What if the 50 Ω RF low-pass seems to attenuate less than expected at 500 MHz?
The inductor has passed its SRF (e.g. 820 nH with 0.5 pF resonates at 250 MHz) and is capacitive, so it provides a bypass path around the inductance and the attenuation flattens or even falls. Use a smaller L (or two in series with a ground-referenced capacitor to make an LC filter) with SRF above the highest frequency of concern.

### Can one inductor and one capacitor make a better single-inductor filter?
Adding a capacitor makes it a second-order LC filter, with 40 dB/dec and a corner at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, as in [[q0052]]. It's no longer "a single inductor" but it's usually what you want when L is the main component: you can use a smaller L for the same attenuation.
:::
