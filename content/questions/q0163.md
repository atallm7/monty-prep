::: testing
Whether you can rearrange $\eta=P_{out}/P_{in}$ in your head for all three unknowns, keep the direction straight (input is always the bigger number), and carry the answer through to something an engineer cares about: loss in watts, input current, or the effect of cascading. A strong candidate also states units, handles percent versus fraction cleanly, and checks that the answer is physically possible ($\eta\le100\%$, $P_{in}\ge P_{out}$).
:::

::: why
This is the arithmetic behind every power budget: how much battery do I need, what current must my source supply, how much heat do I dissipate. The related question [[q0155]] gives voltages and currents instead of powers; together they are the "can you size a power supply" fundamentals. Interviewers use it to check calm accuracy and sanity checking under a little pressure.
:::

::: concepts
- **Efficiency**: $\eta=P_{out}/P_{in}$, a ratio less than or equal to 1 for a passive converter ([[q0162]]).
- **Power**: $P=VI$ (DC); for the input, $P_{in}=V_{in}I_{in}$.
- **Loss**: $P_{loss}=P_{in}-P_{out}$, which becomes heat ([[q0164]]).
- **Percent vs fraction**: 90% is 0.90 in the equations.
- **Cascaded stages**: efficiencies multiply.
- **Sanity checks**: $P_{in}>P_{out}$ always; $I_{in}$ for a buck is smaller than $I_{out}$, for a boost larger.
:::

::: answer
"There's one relation, efficiency equals output power over input power, and the three versions just rearrange it.

If I'm given input power and output power, efficiency is Pout over Pin. For example 10 watts in and 8.2 watts out is 82 percent, and the 1.8 watts difference is lost as heat.

If I'm given input power and efficiency, the output is Pin times eta. 12 watts in at 85 percent gives 10.2 watts out.

If I'm given output power and efficiency, the input is Pout divided by eta. So a 5 watt load at 90 percent needs 5.56 watts in. The mistake to avoid is multiplying instead of dividing there, because the answer must come out larger than the output.

Then I sanity-check: input is bigger than output, efficiency is between zero and one, and I convert to something useful like input current, so 5.56 watts from a 12 volt source is 0.46 amps. And if there are several stages, I multiply their efficiencies."
:::

::: technical
**Core relation.** $P_{out}=\eta P_{in}$, with $0<\eta\le1$ (use fractions in equations).

| Given | Find | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| $P_{in}$, $P_{out}$ | $\eta$ | $\eta=P_{out}/P_{in}$ |
| $P_{in}$, $\eta$ | $P_{out}$ | $P_{out}=\eta P_{in}$ |
| $P_{out}$, $\eta$ | $P_{in}$ | $P_{in}=P_{out}/\eta$ |

**Loss.** $P_{loss}=P_{in}-P_{out}=P_{in}(1-\eta)=P_{out}(1/\eta-1)$.

**Worked examples.**
1. $P_{in}=12$ W, $\eta=85\%$: $P_{out}=10.2$ W, $P_{loss}=1.8$ W.
2. $P_{out}=5$ W, $\eta=90\%$: $P_{in}=5.556$ W, $P_{loss}=0.556$ W; from a 12 V source $I_{in}=0.463$ A.
3. $P_{in}=10$ W, $P_{out}=8.2$ W: $\eta=82\%$, $P_{loss}=1.8$ W.

**Cascades.** $\eta_{total}=\prod\eta_k$. Example: a 1.8 V, 1 A load fed by an LDO from 3.3 V ($\eta=1.8/3.3=54.5\%$), which is fed by a 92% buck, which is fed by a 96% intermediate bus converter. Working backwards: $P_{LDO,in}=3.3$ W, $P_{buck,in}=3.3/0.92=3.587$ W, $P_{IBC,in}=3.587/0.96=3.736$ W, so $\eta_{total}=1.8/3.736=48.2\%$.

**Multiple outputs.** Add all output powers: $P_{out}=\sum V_kI_k$ then apply the relation.

**AC input.** Use *real* power $P_{in}=V_{rms}I_{rms}\,\mathrm{PF}$, not apparent power $VI$.
:::

::: short
"Efficiency is output power over input power. Given two, rearrange: eta is Pout over Pin; Pout is Pin times eta; Pin is Pout divided by eta. For example, 5 watts out at 90 percent needs 5.56 watts in, losing 0.56. Input must always be larger than output, and for cascaded stages you multiply the efficiencies."
:::

::: long
"Everything comes from one equation: efficiency equals output power over input power. I treat eta as a fraction between 0 and 1 so I don't mix it up with percent.

Case one: I know input and output power. Then eta is Pout over Pin. If a supply draws 10 watts and delivers 8.2, it's 82 percent efficient, and the 1.8 watts difference is the loss, which turns into heat.

Case two: I know input power and efficiency. The output is Pin times eta. A 12 watt input at 85 percent gives 10.2 watts at the output, with 1.8 watts lost.

Case three: I know output power and efficiency, which is the most common design case, because the load defines the output. The input is Pout divided by eta. A 5 watt load at 90 percent needs 5.56 watts from the source. The classic mistake is to multiply, and a quick check catches it, because the input can't be smaller than the output.

Once I have a power I usually turn it into current: 5.56 watts from 12 volts is 0.46 amps, and I'll use that to size the source, fuse and input capacitor. I also work out the loss, since that's the heat: 0.56 watts here.

For a chain, like a bus converter, then a buck, then an LDO, efficiencies multiply. With 96, 92 and 54.5 percent I get about 48 percent overall, which says the LDO dominates the loss and I should reconsider the last stage. If the input is AC, I use real power, not volts times amps, since power factor matters. And a final sanity check: eta can't exceed one."
:::

::: terms
- **Efficiency ($\eta$)**: ratio of output power to input power.
- **Input power**: $V_{in}I_{in}$ drawn from the source.
- **Output power**: sum of $V_kI_k$ delivered to loads.
- **Power loss**: input minus output, dissipated as heat.
- **Cascade**: stages in series, so $\eta$ multiplies.
- **Real power / power factor**: for AC, $P=V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\phi$ (or PF).
- **Load line / operating point**: the load current and voltage at which the efficiency is evaluated.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says $\eta=P_{out}/P_{in}$ and computes the requested value.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Writes all three rearrangements on the board and uses fractions for $\eta$.
- Sanity-checks the result ($P_{in}>P_{out}$, $\eta<1$) and flags the multiply-instead-of-divide mistake before it happens.
- Converts the answer to loss in watts and to input current, which is what the design needs.
- Brings up cascaded efficiency and multiple outputs without being asked.
- Notes that for AC sources the input must be real power, and that $\eta$ depends on operating point, so the number is only valid at the stated load.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Multiplying by $\eta$ to find the input.** Input is $P_{out}/\eta$, which is bigger. Multiplying makes the input smaller than the output, which is impossible.
- **Using 90 instead of 0.90.** Percent must be converted before computing.
- **Averaging efficiencies of cascaded stages.** They multiply: 92% and 90% give 82.8%, not 91%.
- **Using one stage's efficiency for a different load.** Efficiency changes with load, so use the value at the actual operating point.
- **Confusing loss as a percent of input vs output.** $P_{loss}/P_{in}=1-\eta$ but $P_{loss}/P_{out}=1/\eta-1$.
- **Using apparent power for an AC input.** Real power is lower by the power factor.
:::

::: deep
## The three rearrangements

Efficiency is defined as
$$\eta=\frac{P_{out}}{P_{in}}.$$
Treat it as a single linear relation $P_{out}=\eta P_{in}$. The three questions are the three ways to solve for one variable.

## Worked examples with full steps

**Example 1: given $P_{in}$ and $P_{out}$.** A USB adapter draws 10 W from the wall (real power) and delivers 5 V at 1.64 A. $P_{out}=5\times1.64=8.2$ W, so $\eta=8.2/10=0.82$ and $P_{loss}=1.8$ W. Is that plausible? Cheap linear adapters can be 50–70%, a modern switcher is 80–90%+; 82% is believable.

**Example 2: given $P_{in}$ and $\eta$.** A 12 W input stage at 85% delivers $0.85\times12=10.2$ W. Loss: $12-10.2=1.8$ W (note: the same 1.8 W as Example 1, by design, to show the loss can be the same while $\eta$ differs).

**Example 3: given $P_{out}$ and $\eta$ (the design case).** A sensor board needs 3 W and the converter is 85% efficient from a 3.7 V cell.
$$P_{in}=\frac{3}{0.85}=3.53\ \text{W},\quad I_{in}=\frac{P_{in}}{V_{in}}=\frac{3.53}{3.7}=0.954\ \text{A}.$$
The cell, wiring and fuse must handle about 1 A, not the 0.81 A you would estimate by dividing 3 W by 3.7 V directly. That 17% difference is the efficiency penalty.

**Example 4: multiple outputs.** A supply delivers 5 V at 2 A, 3.3 V at 1 A and 12 V at 0.5 A from a 24 V source drawing 0.9 A. $P_{out}=10+3.3+6=19.3$ W, $P_{in}=24\times0.9=21.6$ W, so $\eta=89.4\%$ and the loss is 2.3 W.

## Cascaded stages

Power flows from stage to stage; each stage's input is the previous stage's output (plus nothing else). So efficiencies multiply:
$$\eta_{total}=\eta_1\eta_2\cdots\eta_n.$$

```mermaid
flowchart LR
  S["Source"] -->|"P_in = 3.74 W"| B["IBC 96%"]
  B -->|"3.59 W"| K["Buck 92%"]
  K -->|"3.30 W"| L["LDO 54.5%"]
  L -->|"P_out = 1.80 W"| O["Load 1.8 V, 1 A"]
```
Figure: Working backwards from the load. The LDO throws away 1.5 W, 40% of the total input, while the buck and IBC together lose only about 0.4 W. Always look for the stage with the lowest $\eta$ first.

Total: $0.96\times0.92\times0.545=48.2\%$, so $P_{in}=1.8/0.482=3.74$ W. If you replaced the LDO with a 90% buck, the chain would be $0.96\times0.92\times0.90=79.5\%$ and $P_{in}=2.26$ W, saving 1.47 W.

## Units, signs and sanity checks

1. **Efficiency in the equation is a fraction.** Write 90% as 0.9.
2. **Direction.** $P_{in}\ge P_{out}$. If the answer violates this, you have the ratio upside down.
3. **Loss share.** $P_{loss}/P_{in}=1-\eta$; $P_{loss}/P_{out}=(1-\eta)/\eta$. At 80%, loss is 20% of the input but 25% of the output.
4. **Currents follow.** For a step-down converter, $I_{in}<I_{out}$ (roughly $I_{in}=I_{out}V_{out}/(\eta V_{in})$); for a step-up, $I_{in}>I_{out}$ ([[q0161]]).
5. **AC input.** $P_{in}=V_{rms}I_{rms}\,\mathrm{PF}$. A supply drawing 0.15 A at 230 V and PF = 0.6 consumes 20.7 W of real power, not 34.5 VA.
6. **Efficiency belongs to an operating point.** Quote it with the load and input voltage.

## Why an engineer calculates this

- **Battery sizing.** Required energy at the battery $=E_{load}/\eta$; add margin.
- **Source and fuse sizing.** $I_{in}=P_{out}/(\eta V_{in})$ at the *lowest* $V_{in}$, where current is highest. A 12 V (9–18 V) input at 3 A out at 3.3 V and 90%: at 9 V, $I_{in}=9.9/(0.9\times9)=1.22$ A.
- **Thermal design.** $P_{loss}$ drives temperature rise ([[q0164]]).
- **Architecture choice.** Comparing cascaded options by total $\eta$ ([[q0166]]).

> [!TIP]
> If you can't see which way to divide, plug in $\eta=50\%$: you obviously need *twice* the output power at the input, so it's $P_{out}/\eta$.

## Why efficiency is not a constant: a loss model

So far $\eta$ has been a number handed to you. In real hardware it is the output of a loss budget, and it changes with load. A useful first model has a fixed loss (gate drive, controller quiescent current, core loss) and a loss that grows as current squared (conduction in switch resistance and inductor DCR):

$$P_{loss}(I)=P_0+R_{eff}I^2,\qquad \eta(I)=\frac{V_{out}I}{V_{out}I+P_0+R_{eff}I^2}$$

Take a 3.3 V output with $P_0=50$ mW and $R_{eff}=40$ mΩ. Computing the model:

| $I_{out}$ | $P_{out}$ | $P_{loss}$ | $\eta$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 mA | 0.033 W | 0.050 W | 39.8% |
| 100 mA | 0.33 W | 0.050 W | 86.8% |
| 0.5 A | 1.65 W | 0.060 W | 96.5% |
| 1 A | 3.3 W | 0.090 W | 97.3% |
| 2 A | 6.6 W | 0.210 W | 96.9% |
| 5 A | 16.5 W | 1.050 W | 94.0% |

```plot

 "vars":{"v":3.3,"p0":0.05,"r":0.04},

 "vlines":[{"at":"sqrt(p0/r)","label":"1.12 A","color":"orange"}]}
```
Figure: At light load the fixed loss dominates and $\eta$ collapses; at heavy load the $I^2R$ term pulls it down again. The peak sits where the two loss terms are equal.

Setting $d\eta/dI=0$ gives $P_0=R_{eff}I^2$, so the peak is at $I^*=\sqrt{P_0/R_{eff}}=1.12$ A, where the two loss terms are equal (50 mW each) and $\eta_{max}=1/(1+2\sqrt{P_0R_{eff}}/V_{out})=97.4\%$. This is why a datasheet's headline "97% efficient" is meaningless without a load current: at 10 mA the same converter in this model is below 40%, and your battery sensor in sleep lives at exactly that corner. It also explains why the follow-up below about a 12-hour estimate turning into 10 hours so often traces back to quoting peak $\eta$ for a load that spends most of its time near the left edge of this curve.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The fixed-plus-$I^2R$ model ignores the switching loss that scales with $V_{in}$ and frequency, the diode or dead-time term, and pulse-skipping modes that change $P_0$ at light load. The constants above are illustrative, not from a datasheet. Real efficiency curves are best read from the manufacturer's graph at your input voltage and temperature.

## Limiting cases

- **$\eta\to1$**: loss goes to zero. Not achievable, but it tells you the sensitivity: at 99% the loss is 1% of the input, and halving it to 0.5% (a 0.5-point improvement) halves the heat. Near the top of the scale, loss is a better figure of merit than efficiency.
- **$P_{out}\to0$**: $\eta\to0$ for any nonzero fixed loss, because the denominator stays finite. A converter with a 50 mW idle loss powering a 1 mW load is 2% efficient.
- **$\eta<0.5$**: more power is lost than delivered. You see this with an LDO dropping 5 V to 1 V (20%) and it is a red flag to check whether a switcher is warranted.
- **Negative or >100% results**: the sign convention is wrong (a battery being charged supplies negative $P_{in}$), or a measurement is off.

## Measuring $\eta$ on the bench without fooling yourself

The input and output power are each measured with an instrument that has a tolerance, and the loss is the small difference between two big numbers. Suppose $P_{in}=10.00$ W and $P_{out}=9.50$ W, each measured to ±0.5%. Then $\eta=95.0\%$ with root-sum-square uncertainty $\pm0.71\%$ of the value ($\pm0.67$ points), which seems fine, but the loss is $0.50\pm0.07$ W, a ±14% uncertainty on the number you care about for thermal design. The closer $\eta$ is to 100%, the worse this gets, so for high-efficiency converters use a power analyzer, or calorimetry/thermal measurement on the loss directly.

Practical rules:

1. **Kelvin sense the voltage at the converter pins**, not at the supply or load terminals. A 0.1 Ω lead pair carrying 2 A drops 0.2 V, which is 6% of a 3.3 V output, and ruins both $V_{out}$ and the computed $P_{out}$.
2. **Measure input power at the converter's input capacitor**, since the source cable and any series sense resistor sit outside the converter's own loss.
3. **Do not use a long-aperture DMM on a pulsating input.** A buck draws pulsed input current; the DMM's current range with its burden voltage and averaging behaves differently from a bench supply's built-in readback. Cross-check the two.
4. **Let it thermally settle.** $R_{DS(on)}$ and DCR rise with temperature (copper about 0.39%/°C), so efficiency at power-on is not the same as after ten minutes.
5. **Record $V_{in}$, $I_{out}$ and temperature** with every efficiency number, as the earlier sections insist.

- **Sizing a wall adapter or battery** for a device whose load power and converter efficiency are known.
- **Fuse and connector rating** from worst-case input current at minimum input voltage.
- **Thermal budgeting** by computing loss for a block diagram of rails.
- **Comparing LDO vs buck chains** for a rail by multiplying stage efficiencies.
- **Energy audit of an IoT device** by adding up loads and converting to battery-side power.
- **Checking a datasheet claim** (e.g. "95% efficient at 1 A") against your measured $P_{in}$ and $P_{out}$.

::: assumptions
- DC conversion at a single operating point, with $\eta$ already specified for that load and input voltage.
- Only one source of input power and no energy storage changing during the interval (steady state); with energy storage, use energies over the interval instead of powers.
- Passive converters ($\eta\le1$). Anything above 100% indicates measurement error (or an extra energy source such as a light or heat input).
- For AC inputs, "input power" means real power; otherwise include power factor.
- If given $P_{in}$ in watt-hours (energy) the same equation holds for energies: $E_{out}=\eta E_{in}$.
:::

::: followups
### Given a 12 V input, 3.3 V at 2 A output, and 88% efficiency, what's the input current?
$P_{out}=3.3\times2=6.6$ W. $P_{in}=6.6/0.88=7.5$ W. $I_{in}=7.5/12=0.625$ A. Sanity check: for a buck, input current is much less than the 2 A output current, which matches.

### A supply is rated 90% efficient and dissipates 2 W at full load. What is the output power?
$P_{loss}=P_{in}(1-\eta)=2$ W, so $P_{in}=2/0.1=20$ W and $P_{out}=18$ W. Equivalently $P_{out}=P_{loss}\cdot\eta/(1-\eta)=2\times9=18$ W.

### Two cascaded converters are 95% and 90% efficient. What's the overall efficiency and what fraction of the input is lost?
Overall: $0.95\times0.90=0.855$, so 85.5% efficient and 14.5% of the input is lost. Not 92.5% (the average), and the lower-efficiency stage dominates the loss.

### What if the quoted efficiency is at a different load than yours?
Then I can't use it. Efficiency varies with load: a buck rated 94% at 1 A may be 60% at 10 mA. I'd read the efficiency-versus-load graph at my operating point, or estimate with a fixed-plus-proportional loss model, then do the same calculation with that $\eta$.

### Scenario: a battery-powered design gets 10 hours in the lab but the calculation said 12. Where could the 17% go?
Check the efficiency used: a datasheet peak (say 92%) versus the actual light-load value (say 80%), the quiescent current of regulators in sleep ([[q0165]]), battery derating at low voltage or temperature, and extra loads not in the budget. A 12-to-10 hour gap corresponds to $10/12=83\%$, just the sort of mismatch that comes from using peak instead of average efficiency.
:::
