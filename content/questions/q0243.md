::: testing
Whether you can explain **why** a multiphase buck responds faster and quantify it. The interviewer wants:

1. The inductor-slew argument: summed current slews at $N(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ because $L_{eq}=L/N$.
2. The control argument: the effective sampling/ripple frequency is $Nf_{sw}$, so the loop can be faster.
3. The result: less output capacitance for the same droop, or less droop for the same capacitance.
4. Limits: ESR/ESL, loop delay, phase overlap, shedding and the fact that falling (unload) transients are slower than rising ones.
:::

::: why
Processor loads can step by tens of amps in under a microsecond. The rail has to stay within a few percent, and the capacitors needed to hold it up by brute force are large and costly. Multiphase is the standard way to get the converter itself to respond faster. If you can explain the mechanism and estimate the droop you show real understanding of power delivery, and it connects to transient fundamentals in [[q0248]] and [[q0249]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor slew**: $di/dt=(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ while the high side is on, $-V_{out}/L$ while it is off ([[q0174]]).
- **Capacitor charge balance**: the output capacitor supplies the difference between load and inductor current: $\Delta V\approx Q/C$.
- **Effective inductance $L/N$**: all phases ramp together in a load step.
- **Loop bandwidth**: limited by the sampling effect to a fraction of the (effective) switching frequency ([[q0249]]).
- **Phase overlap**: allowing multiple phases to turn on at once during a transient.
- **ESR and ESL**: instantaneous $\Delta V=\Delta I\cdot ESR$ plus $L_{esl}\,di/dt$ ([[q0248]]).
:::

::: answer
"Multiphase improves transient response in two ways. The first is the inductor slew rate. When the load steps up, the output capacitor supplies the difference until the inductor current catches up, and the inductor current can only rise at V in minus V out over L. With N phases pushing at the same time, the total current rises N times faster, equivalent to a single inductor of L over N. So the capacitor has to cover a much shorter gap and the droop is smaller.

The second is control bandwidth. The loop crossover is limited to a fraction of the switching frequency because the converter samples the output once per cycle. With interleaving, the effective frequency is N times f sw, so the loop can have a higher crossover, and respond faster, without making each FET switch faster.

Those two combine. For a 12 V to 1 V rail with a 30 amp step, going from one phase to four cuts the ideal droop by about four times, so I can use around a quarter of the capacitance for the same limit.

The caveats: capacitor ESR and ESL add their own immediate step that no converter speed can fix; the unload step is slower than the load step because the down-slew is just V out over L, which is small at 1 volt; and when phases are shed at light load the response is slower unless the controller brings them back instantly."
:::

::: technical
**Slew.** Rising: $s_\uparrow=(V_{in}-V_{out})/L_{eq}$. Falling: $s_\downarrow=V_{out}/L_{eq}$, with $L_{eq}=L/N$. For 12 V to 1 V and $L=300$ nH:

| | $L_{eq}$ | up-slew | down-slew |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 phase | 300 nH | 36.7 A/µs | 3.3 A/µs |
| 4 phases | 75 nH | 146.7 A/µs | 13.3 A/µs |

**Ideal slew-limited droop** (load steps up by $\Delta I$ at $t=0$, controller instantly commands full duty on all phases): the capacitor supplies the missing charge $Q=\Delta I^2/(2s_\uparrow)$, so

$$\Delta V_{sag}=\frac{\Delta I^2}{2\,s_\uparrow\,C}=\frac{L_{eq}\Delta I^2}{2(V_{in}-V_{out})C}$$

For $\Delta I=30$ A and $C=1$ mF: one phase 12.3 mV, four phases 3.1 mV.

**Unload overshoot.** Energy stored in the inductors, $\tfrac12L_{eq}\Delta I^2$, must go into the capacitor: $\tfrac12C[(V_{out}+\Delta V)^2-V_{out}^2]=\tfrac12L_{eq}\Delta I^2$, so $\Delta V=\sqrt{V_{out}^2+L_{eq}\Delta I^2/C}-V_{out}$. One phase 127 mV, four phases 33 mV (for the same 30 A, 1 mF). Note this is much larger than the sag because the down-slew is only $V_{out}/L$.

**Loop-limited droop.** With a closed-loop crossover $f_c$ the small-signal droop is about $\Delta V\approx k\,\Delta I/(2\pi f_cC)$ with $k\approx0.46$ for a damping ratio of 0.7. A rule of thumb for $f_c$ is around $f_{eff}/10$, where $f_{eff}$ is the sampling frequency: 50 kHz for one phase at 500 kHz, up to 200 kHz for four interleaved phases at an effective 2 MHz. That gives 44 mV vs 11 mV for $C=1$ mF.

**Resistive/inductive steps.** $\Delta I\cdot ESR$ (30 A × 0.5 mΩ = 15 mV) appears instantly and $L_{esl}\,di/dt$ adds to it. These are independent of the converter.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The "$f_c\approx f_{eff}/10$" rule is a design rule of thumb. Real multiphase controllers can exceed it with per-phase compensation, and the achievable value depends on the modulator, delay and phase margin target.
:::

::: long
"Let me start with what happens in a load step. The load suddenly asks for more current, but the inductor current can't change instantly. It rises at V in minus V out over L. During that time the output capacitor supplies the difference, so the output voltage droops. The size of the droop is the charge deficit divided by the capacitance.

With N phases, all the inductors ramp at the same time, so the total current rises N times as fast. That's the same as having a single inductor of L over N. For 12 V in, 1 V out and 300 nH per phase, one phase slews at about 37 amps per microsecond and four phases at about 147. For a 30 amp step and a one-millifarad bank, the ideal droop goes from about 12 millivolts to about 3.

The second effect is control bandwidth. A switching converter can only update once per cycle, so the loop crossover is limited to a fraction of the switching frequency. Interleaved phases give an effective frequency of N times f sw, so the loop can be faster. A higher crossover means the converter reacts sooner and the droop is smaller. A typical small-signal estimate for droop is delta I over two pi f c C, so quadrupling the crossover cuts it by four.

Two things limit this. Capacitor ESR and ESL cause an instant step that no controller can fix, so I still need low-impedance capacitors close to the load. And the load release is slower than the load apply, because the down-slew is just V out over L, which is small at one volt. In this example the overshoot is about ten times the sag. Controllers help by turning all phases off at once.

Finally, shedding phases at light load raises the effective inductance, so controllers re-enable all phases the moment they see a step, which is called phase overlap or adaptive phase control."
:::

::: terms
- **Slew rate (di/dt)**: how fast inductor current can change, $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ or $V_{out}/L$.
- **Droop / undershoot**: the output voltage dip after a load step-up.
- **Overshoot**: the output voltage rise after a load release.
- **Phase overlap**: allowing several phases' high-side switches to be on at once in a transient.
- **Effective inductance**: $L/N$, seen by a step.
- **Crossover frequency ($f_c$)**: frequency where loop gain magnitude is 1 (0 dB).
- **Adaptive voltage positioning / load line**: deliberately lowering $V_{out}$ with load current to use the window better ([[q0184]]).
- **Target impedance**: $\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it responds faster because there are more phases".

**An exceptional answer**:

- Gives numbers: slew rates and droop for $N=1$ and $N=4$.
- Distinguishes load-apply (sag) and load-release (overshoot), noting down-slew is $V_{out}/L$ and so much slower.
- Brings up the loop bandwidth limit and the $N f_{sw}$ effective frequency.

- Mentions adaptive voltage positioning or load line as complementary.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying each phase's inductor responds faster.** It's the combination that slews N times faster, not each inductor.
- **Ignoring the loop.** Even an infinitely fast inductor cannot respond before the controller commands it.
- **Forgetting ESR/ESL.** Fast steps produce an instant $\Delta I\cdot ESR$ and $L_{esl}\,di/dt$.
- **Only considering load apply.** Overshoot on load release is typically bigger because of the slow down-slew.
- **Assuming the bandwidth can be anything.** It's limited by sampling, delay and stability margin.
- **Assuming shedding is free.** Fewer active phases means slower slew until phases are re-enabled.
:::

::: deep
## What happens in a step

Before the step the inductors carry the old load current. At $t=0$ the load asks for $\Delta I$ more. The output capacitor, which has no choice, supplies it: $C\,dv/dt=-(\Delta I-\Delta i_L(t))$. The controller sees the droop, commands maximum duty, and the inductor current ramps up at $s_\uparrow$ until it reaches the new load. The charge removed from the capacitor is the area between the load step and the inductor ramp: a triangle of area $\Delta I^2/(2s_\uparrow)$ in the ideal case.

$$\Delta V_{sag}=\frac{\Delta I^2}{2\,s_\uparrow C}$$

Halving $s_\uparrow^{-1}$ (doubling the slew) halves the droop. That's why $L_{eq}$ matters.

## Why $L/N$ and not smaller $L$?

You could get the same slew with one smaller inductor, but its ripple current would grow as $1/L$, and with it the AC loss, the peak current and the output ripple. A 75 nH single inductor at 500 kHz would have a 24.4 A p-p ripple ($V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf_{sw})$), compared with 6.1 A per phase and a summed 4.4 A in the four-phase design. Multiphase decouples the *small signal* inductance (small, from the sum) from the *ripple* inductance (large per phase, with cancellation).

## Unload: the harder edge

At release the inductor current is too high and the only way to lower it is to apply $-V_{out}$ across $L_{eq}$. With $V_{out}=1$ V this is slow: 13.3 A/µs for four phases and 3.3 A/µs for one. The excess energy $\tfrac12L_{eq}\Delta I^2$ goes into the capacitor, so

$$\Delta V_{over}=\sqrt{V_{out}^2+\frac{L_{eq}\Delta I^2}{C}}-V_{out}$$

which gives 33 mV for four phases and 127 mV for one phase (30 A, 1 mF). For a 1 V rail with a 5% window this is a serious problem for the single-phase design. Hence controllers turn all phases off (all low-side on) immediately on a release, and designers use adaptive voltage positioning ([[q0184]]) to bias the window.

## The controller side

- **Phase overlap**: normally one phase fires at a time, and the next slot is $T/N$ away. For a big step all high-sides may be commanded on together.
- **Phase add on demand**: re-enable shed phases within a cycle or two.
- **Sampling and delay**: the digital or analog modulator and sensing introduce delay, which reduces phase margin at high crossover.
- **Compensation**: with $L_{eq}=L/N$ the plant's LC corner moves up by $\sqrt N$ ([[q0246]], [[q0247]]), and the compensator is designed around that.

## What else sets the droop

Real bench droop is a sum: ESR step ($\Delta I\cdot ESR$), ESL step ($L_{esl}\,di/dt$), the capacitor-discharge term from the slow converter, and the loop's own delay. The multiphase converter attacks the third. For the first two you need low-ESR, low-ESL capacitors (ceramic MLCCs near the load, bulk polymer behind them) and good package/PCB design.

## Phase overlap is not optional

The $L/N$ slew argument assumes every phase's high-side switch is on at once. In normal interleaved operation that does not happen: with a duty cycle below $1/N$ each high-side turns on for less than $T/N$, so at most one phase is on at any instant. The summed slope at that instant is one phase rising and $N-1$ phases falling. For the four-phase 12 V to 1 V rail with 300 nH per phase:

$$\left.\frac{di_\Sigma}{dt}\right|_{\text{one on, three off}}=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})-(N-1)V_{out}}{L}=\frac{12-4}{300\text{ nH}}=26.7\ \text{A/µs}$$

That is actually *lower* than the 36.7 A/µs of a single phase, and 5.5 times lower than the 146.7 A/µs that "L/N" promises. The advantage only appears when all four high-sides are turned on together, which is what phase overlap does. A controller that keeps strict $T/N$ slots during a transient wastes the multiphase transient benefit; this is the single most common reason a bench droop is several times worse than the formula.

## Which limit binds: slew or loop?

There are two models of droop on this page, and they predict different things. The slew-limited formula assumes the controller reacts instantly. The loop-limited formula $k\Delta I/(2\pi f_cC)$ assumes a linear loop with finite crossover. For a 40 A step on 1 mF with four phases:

| Model | Droop |
|---|---|
| Ideal slew-limited | $75\text{ nH}\times40^2/(2\times11\times1\text{ mF})=5.5$ mV |
| Loop-limited, $f_c=50$ kHz | 58.6 mV |
| Loop-limited, $f_c=100$ kHz | 29.3 mV |
| Loop-limited, $f_c=200$ kHz | 14.6 mV |

The two expressions are equal when $f_c=k(V_{in}-V_{out})/(\pi L_{eq}\Delta I)=537$ kHz. Below that crossover the loop is the bottleneck, and the converter's ability to slew faster is not used; above it the inductors are the bottleneck. In reality the measured droop sits somewhere above both numbers, because large-signal controllers (for example those that command full duty immediately on a droop) behave more like the slew-limited model, while a plain linear loop behaves like the loop-limited one. So "four phases cut the droop by four" is accurate if both the slew and the crossover scale with $N$, which is why multiphase helps twice: it reduces $L_{eq}$ and it allows a higher crossover.

## A worked capacitor-bank sizing

Specify a 1 V rail with a 40 A step and a 30 mV total allowed deviation, four phases of 300 nH, a 200 kHz crossover, and a capacitor bank with 0.4 mΩ ESR and 20 pH effective ESL close to the load, with a load that slews 40 A in 200 ns (200 A/µs).

1. **ESR step:** $40\times0.4\text{ mΩ}=16$ mV, appearing immediately.
2. **ESL step:** $20\text{ pH}\times200\text{ A/µs}=4$ mV.
3. **Remaining for bulk capacitor discharge:** $30-16-4=10$ mV.
4. **Capacitance from the loop-limited model:** $C=k\Delta I/(2\pi f_c\,\Delta V)=0.46\times40/(2\pi\times200\text{ kHz}\times0.010)=1.46$ mF. With a 50 kHz single-phase-like crossover the same arithmetic gives 5.9 mF, about four times as much.
5. **Check the release:** with all phases turned off, $\Delta V_{over}=\sqrt{V_{out}^2+L_{eq}\Delta I^2/C}-V_{out}$ for 1.46 mF is $\sqrt{1+75\text{ nH}\times1600/1.46\text{ mF}}-1=40$ mV, which is over the 30 mV budget. Holding the overshoot to 30 mV needs $C=L_{eq}\Delta I^2/((V_{out}+0.03)^2-V_{out}^2)=1.97$ mF.

So in this example it is the *unload* edge, not the load apply, that sets the capacitor bank. (In practice an adaptive voltage positioning load line and special unload handling reduce this, and the sum of the ESR step and the capacitor droop does not occur at the same instant as the overshoot, so treat this as a conservative sizing.) Most of the 1.97 mF would be low-ESR ceramic or polymer capacitors, derated for DC bias; a rule of thumb is to check the effective capacitance at the actual rail voltage rather than the nominal value.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The ESR, ESL, crossover and step slew are illustrative, and the overshoot budget depends on the product's voltage specification and load-line. Use the processor or load vendor's transient specification for real designs.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Use a fast electronic load** with a programmable slew (at least as fast as the real load's, otherwise you understate the droop) and probe the output at the load pins with a short ground, not at the converter. A long probe ground loop adds an $L\,di/dt$ error that looks like extra droop.
2. **Look at the switch nodes during the step.** With phase overlap working, all four high-side pulses should merge into a wide pulse (or all four turn on together) in the first few hundred nanoseconds. If you see single pulses in their usual slots, the response is slot-limited.
3. **Measure the loop gain** with a network analyzer and an injection resistor to get $f_c$ and phase margin. If $f_c$ is 50 kHz rather than your 200 kHz design goal, that explains a droop four times too large, and it is a compensation problem rather than a power-stage problem.
4. **Capture both edges.** Trigger on the load step and on the release. If the release overshoot is larger than the droop (it usually is for low-voltage rails), check that the controller turns all high sides off promptly and that the bank is not sized only for the droop.
5. **Repeat at light load** with phases shed, to see how much the response slows before the controller re-enables phases.

::: applications
- **Processor core rails** with several-hundred A/µs load slew and 30 to 50 mV droop budgets.
- **GPU and AI accelerator rails**, where workload changes cause tens-of-amps steps.
- **FPGA core rails** that see large configuration or clock-enable steps.
- **Adaptive voltage positioning (load-line)** in VRMs, which uses the droop budget deliberately.
- **Bench characterization**: electronic load slew (for example 50 to 200 A/µs) is used to validate droop and recovery.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal slew-limited step** for the $\Delta V_{sag}$ and overshoot formulas: instant controller reaction, no ESR/ESL, full duty available.
- **Equal phases and phase overlap** so all phases ramp together.
- **Small-signal formula** $k\Delta I/(2\pi f_cC)$ for a well-damped second-order loop.
- **Fixed $C$** of 1 mF, ignoring ESR, ESL and capacitance derating of MLCCs under DC bias.
- The $f_c\approx f_{eff}/10$ rule is a guideline. High-end controllers exceed it.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the overshoot on load release larger than the droop on load apply?
The up-slew is $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ (11 V over $L$) while the down-slew is only $V_{out}/L$ (1 V over $L$), 11 times slower in the 12 V to 1 V example. The inductor holds excess current longer, delivering energy into the capacitor, so the overshoot is much bigger for the same step size.

### Does adding phases always reduce the droop proportionally?
Only in the slew-limited and loop-limited parts. Beyond some point ESR, ESL, loop delay and the minimum on-time dominate, so returns diminish. Past about six to eight phases the droop is mostly set by the capacitor network.

### How does phase shedding affect the transient?
With fewer phases the effective inductance is higher and the slew lower, so a step at light load droops more unless phases come back quickly. Good controllers sense the step through the output deviation or load-current signal and re-enable all phases within a cycle or two, trading some efficiency for response.

### What is adaptive voltage positioning?
Also called load line: the controller deliberately lowers $V_{out}$ as load current rises, by $R_{LL}I_{out}$. At a step up the rail starts from a lower voltage so the droop has less room to fall below the minimum, and at release the overshoot has more headroom. It cuts the capacitance needed, at the cost of a lower average voltage at heavy load. See [[q0184]].

### Scenario: your 4-phase rail droops 60 mV on a 40 A step but the calculation predicted 15 mV. What do you check?
First the capacitor network: ESR and ESL of the bank at the load (derated MLCC capacitance with bias, bulk capacitor ESR). A 40 A step with 1 mΩ total ESR alone is 40 mV. Then the measurement point and probe method (at the load vs at the VRM), the slew rate of the electronic load, the loop crossover and phase margin (Bode measurement), and whether phase overlap is enabled.
