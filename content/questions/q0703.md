::: basics
### In plain words
The two directions of an eye diagram show two different problems. Blur sideways is timing error, called jitter. Thickness up and down is voltage error, called noise. To put numbers on them, you count how many samples fall at each position. The shape of those counts also hints at the cause.

### Picture it
Imagine shooting arrows at a target. If the arrows land spread out left and right, your aim sideways is the problem. If they spread up and down, your aim in height is the problem. The eye is the same. Where the traces cross is the target for timing, so sideways scatter there is jitter. The flat parts at the 1 and 0 levels are the target for voltage, so up-and-down scatter there is noise.

### Words you need
- **Crossing point**: where the rising and falling edges cross, normally at mid-level.
- **Jitter**: the sideways scatter of the crossings ([[q0695]]).
- **Noise**: the vertical scatter of the 1 and 0 levels.
- **Histogram**: a bar chart counting how many samples landed at each position.
- **Random vs. deterministic**: random scatter is a smooth bell shape. Deterministic scatter has a cause that repeats, so it makes separate bands or lobes.

### The simplest example
Collect the time of every crossing and make a histogram. A smooth, single bell shape points to random jitter. Two or more separate bands point to a repeating cause such as smearing between bits or reflections.

Now convert noise into timing. Say an edge goes 400 mV in 60 ps, so its slope is about 6.7 mV/ps. Add 15 mV of random noise on that edge. The crossing time moves by 15 / 6.7, about 2.25 ps. Even a purely vertical noise source therefore adds sideways blur.

That is the whole idea: sideways scatter is jitter, vertical scatter is noise, and the shape of the histogram points to the cause. The rest of this page shows how to read the shapes, a table of signatures, and how to go from histograms to numbers.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can **read the two axes of the eye**: horizontal spread = timing jitter (the blur of the crossings, and the narrowing of eye width), vertical spread = amplitude noise (thickness of the rails, and the reduction of eye height). Good answers also show how to quantify them (histograms taken at the crossing for jitter and at the sampling point for noise), tell random from deterministic by their appearance, and mention the **coupling between the two**: noise on a sloped edge turns into jitter, $\sigma_t = \sigma_v/SR$.
:::

::: why
In a lab, you rarely get a spreadsheet of jitter; you get a picture of an eye. Being able to say "that horizontal fuzz is jitter, those thick rails are noise, those two distinct crossing bands are deterministic jitter from ISI" lets you diagnose in seconds and choose the right fix (clock quality or supply for jitter; crosstalk or receiver noise for amplitude; equalization for ISI). It also underpins how scopes compute jitter and BER numbers from eyes ([[q0699]], [[q0695]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Eye diagram**: all bits overlaid on one UI. See [[q0698]].
- **Jitter**: time deviation of edges; RJ (Gaussian) and DJ (bounded). See [[q0695]].
- **Noise and amplitude distortion**: random noise, crosstalk, ISI, reflections thicken the rails and lower the opening.
- **Histograms**: horizontal histogram at the crossing level (timing), vertical histogram at the sample point (level).
- **Slew-rate conversion**: amplitude noise on an edge moves the crossing by $\sigma_v/SR$. See [[q0689]].
- **Bathtub curve**: BER versus sampling position, derived from the timing histograms. See [[q0699]].
:::

::: answer
"On an eye diagram the two impairments show up in different directions. Timing jitter is the horizontal blur: the crossing points, where rising and falling edges intersect, spread out sideways instead of landing at one point, and that cuts the eye width. Amplitude noise is the vertical blur: the high and low levels aren't thin lines, they have thickness, and that cuts the eye height.

To quantify them you take histograms. A horizontal histogram of where the edges cross the mid-level gives the jitter distribution, and its standard deviation or peak-to-peak is the jitter. A vertical histogram at the centre of the eye gives the distribution of the 1 and 0 levels, whose width is the noise, and the separation of the two humps over their widths gives the Q factor.

You can tell the types apart by shape. Random jitter or noise gives smooth Gaussian tails that fade out. Deterministic effects have bounded, often distinct bands: ISI gives several discrete crossing positions or level bands, duty-cycle distortion shifts rising and falling crossings apart, and periodic jitter gives a two-lobed histogram.

They're also linked: amplitude noise on a sloped edge becomes timing jitter, so the crossing blur has a contribution from noise, divided by the slew rate."
:::

::: technical
**Horizontal axis: timing jitter.** At the crossing level (usually 50% of the amplitude) build a histogram of crossing times. For random jitter the histogram is Gaussian with rms $\sigma_{RJ}$; deterministic jitter widens or splits it (bounded). Eye width at BER $B$: $W_{eye} = UI - DJ - 2Q(B)\sigma_{RJ}$, from the dual-Dirac extrapolation of the histogram tails ([[q0695]]).

**Vertical axis: amplitude noise.** At the sampling instant (eye centre) build a histogram of voltage. The 1 and 0 levels each form a hump with $\mu_1,\mu_0,\sigma_1,\sigma_0$; eye height at $B$: $H_{eye} = (\mu_1-\mu_0) - Q(B)(\sigma_1+\sigma_0)$ and $Q = (\mu_1-\mu_0)/(\sigma_1+\sigma_0)$ ([[q0699]]).

**Signatures.**
| Feature in the eye | Cause |
|---|---|
| Crossings: smooth Gaussian blur | random jitter (thermal/shot noise in PLL, buffers) |
| Crossings: two or more distinct bands | data-dependent jitter (ISI), reflections |
| Rising and falling crossings offset from each other and from 50% | duty-cycle distortion / asymmetric rise/fall |
| Crossing histogram with two lobes | periodic (sinusoidal) jitter, e.g. supply ripple |
| Rails: thin and clean | little vertical noise |
| Rails: smooth thick band | random noise, thermal or crosstalk |
| Rails: several distinct levels | ISI (pattern-dependent levels) |

**Noise-to-jitter conversion.** For an edge with slope $SR$ at the crossing, vertical noise $\sigma_v$ gives timing error $\sigma_t = \sigma_v/SR$. With 400 mV swing in 60 ps (slope 6.7 mV/ps), 15 mV rms noise gives 2.25 ps rms. So the crossing histogram combines true timing jitter and noise-induced jitter; at the *centre* of the eye, noise appears only as vertical spread (the slope is zero there).

**Bathtub curve.** BER versus sampling position, computed from the jitter distributions at the left and right crossings; the width at the target BER is the eye width. For $DJ = 0.10$ UI and $\sigma_{RJ} = 0.01$ UI the eye width at $10^{-12}$ is about 0.76 UI.
:::

::: short
"Timing jitter is the horizontal spread of the eye: the crossing points blur sideways and the eye width shrinks. Amplitude noise is the vertical spread: the rails get thick and the eye height shrinks. You quantify them with histograms, a horizontal one at the crossing for jitter and a vertical one at the sampling point for noise. Gaussian fuzz is random, distinct bands are deterministic like ISI, and noise on a sloped edge also turns into jitter."
:::

::: long
"On an eye diagram, jitter and amplitude noise appear along the two different axes, and that's how I tell them apart.

Timing jitter is the horizontal one. In an ideal eye all the edges cross at exactly the same point. With jitter, the crossings spread out left and right, and the eye gets narrower: the eye width is the unit interval minus the total jitter. If I take a histogram of the crossing times at the mid-level, I get the jitter distribution.

Amplitude noise is the vertical one. In an ideal eye the high and low rails are thin lines. With noise, crosstalk or ISI, they thicken, and the eye height shrinks. If I take a vertical histogram at the middle of the eye, I see two humps, one for the ones and one for the zeros; the separation of the humps divided by the sum of their widths is the Q factor.

I can also tell random from deterministic by looks. Random jitter or noise gives smooth Gaussian edges that fade out, with tails that keep growing the longer I capture. Deterministic effects are bounded and structured. ISI gives several distinct crossing positions or rail levels depending on the previous bits. Duty-cycle distortion shows up as the rising and falling crossings sitting at different places. Periodic jitter, say from a switching regulator, gives a two-lobed crossing histogram.

One subtlety is the coupling between the two: amplitude noise on a sloped edge turns into timing error, noise divided by slew rate. So part of what looks like jitter at the crossing is really noise. At the eye centre, where the signal is flat, noise is purely vertical."
:::

::: terms
- **Crossing point**: where rising and falling edges intersect, normally at mid-level.
- **Eye width / eye height**: horizontal and vertical openings.
- **RJ / DJ**: random (Gaussian, unbounded) and deterministic (bounded) jitter.
- **DDJ (data-dependent jitter)**: pattern-dependent timing error, mainly from ISI.
- **DCD (duty-cycle distortion)**: rising and falling edges displaced from their ideal positions.
- **PJ (periodic jitter)**: jitter at a discrete frequency, e.g. from supply ripple or crosstalk.
- **Histogram (eye)**: distribution of crossing times or voltage levels used to extract jitter or noise.
- **AM-to-PM conversion**: amplitude noise becoming timing error on a sloped edge.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "jitter is horizontal blur and noise is vertical blur". An exceptional answer also says **where to measure** (histogram at the crossing for timing, at the sample point for level), **how to separate types** (smooth Gaussian tails versus bounded bands, DCD offsets, two-lobe periodic histograms), the **numbers** ($W = UI - DJ - 2Q\sigma_{RJ}$, $Q = \Delta\mu/(\sigma_1+\sigma_0)$), and the **AM-to-PM coupling** $\sigma_t = \sigma_v/SR$, including why the eye centre is a clean place to read vertical noise. It mentions the bathtub curve as the horizontal BER view, and that sample size limits how far into the tails you can see.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Reading all horizontal blur as jitter in the clock.** Some of it is noise converted by the edge slope, and some is ISI (deterministic); only part is true random jitter.
- **Measuring noise on the edge.** On the edge the vertical spread includes timing jitter times slope. Measure vertical noise at the flat part near the eye centre.
- **Using peak-to-peak for random distributions.** The peak-to-peak keeps growing with capture length; use rms and extrapolate to the BER.
- **Missing duty-cycle distortion.** If rising and falling crossings are displaced from each other, centering the sampling point on the average hides that the eye is narrower on one side.
- **Confusing thick rails from ISI with noise.** ISI gives discrete pattern-dependent levels that can be removed with equalization; random noise cannot.
- **Trusting a short capture.** A few thousand UIs show only the central part of the distributions; BER-level numbers need extrapolation.
:::

::: deep
## Two directions, two impairments

The eye has a time axis (one unit interval) and a voltage axis (the swing). Impairments that move the *when* of an edge show up on the time axis; impairments that move the *how much* of a level show up on the voltage axis.

## Reading the shape of a histogram

Take the left picture and look at the crossing histogram:

| Histogram shape | What it means |
|---|---|
| Single smooth bell, tails fade | random jitter (Gaussian) |
| Wider, flat-topped or with sharp edges | bounded deterministic jitter |
| Two or more separate peaks | data-dependent jitter (ISI) from particular bit patterns, or duty-cycle distortion if the peaks are the rising and falling edges |
| Two lobes at the edges, deep in the middle ("dual-peaked") | sinusoidal periodic jitter |
| Rising and falling histograms at different centres | DCD or threshold offset |

The same logic applies to the vertical axis. A smooth bell on each rail is random noise; several discrete rail levels mean ISI or reflections.

**Mixing them.** Real eyes combine them: a bounded deterministic part convolved with a Gaussian random part. That's the dual-Dirac model in [[q0695]]: two spikes convolved with a Gaussian, whose tails are used to extrapolate to the BER.

## Amplitude noise becomes timing jitter

Where the edge is steep, voltage noise moves the crossing time. At the crossing the slope is $SR = \Delta V/\Delta t$ and

$$\sigma_{t,from\,noise} = \frac{\sigma_v}{SR}$$

For 400 mV swing over a 60 ps edge: $SR = 6.7$ mV/ps. With $\sigma_v = 15$ mV: $\sigma_t = 2.25$ ps rms. If the clock has genuine random jitter of 3 ps rms, the crossing histogram has $\sqrt{3^2 + 2.25^2} = 3.75$ ps rms, so about a third of the jitter *variance* (2.25² out of 3.75²) comes from noise, not the clock, and the rms grows 25% above the clock's own 3 ps. The *centre* of the eye is flat (slope ≈ 0), so there the vertical histogram reads pure amplitude noise.

Practical implication: when a jitter number looks high, check whether noise or slew rate is responsible before blaming the clock: speeding the edge reduces the noise-induced part ([[q0689]]).

## Hunting the cause

| Symptom | Likely source | What to try |
|---|---|---|
| Wide Gaussian crossing, clean rails | random jitter from clock/PLL, or noisy supply | scope the reference clock, check PLL/regulator supply, compare to the clock's phase noise |
| Distinct crossing bands | ISI/reflections | equalization, fix stubs and impedance |
| Two-lobed crossing histogram at a regular spacing | periodic jitter | find the frequency (FFT of TIE); switching regulator or nearby clock |
| Thick rails, smooth | random noise or crosstalk | disable neighbouring aggressors one at a time |
| Rails with several levels | ISI | equalization, shorter channel |
| Rising vs falling crossings offset | DCD / threshold offset | check driver rise/fall symmetry, DC offset in the receiver or probes |

> [!INTERVIEW]
> When asked to point at an eye and say what's wrong, describe the direction of the blur, the histogram shape, and what you'd test next. Interviewers want the reasoning ("the crossings are bimodal, so it's deterministic, probably ISI, so I'd look at equalization") more than the label.
:::

::: applications
- **Scope jitter and noise analysis**: automatic RJ/DJ separation and eye measurements from histograms.
- **Transmitter characterisation**: comparing TX jitter and amplitude noise against a standard's limits before connecting to a channel.
- **Debugging supply coupling**: periodic jitter at the switching-regulator frequency visible as a two-lobed crossing histogram.
- **Crosstalk investigations**: toggling neighbours and watching rail thickness change.
- **Clock-source selection**: choosing oscillators or clock chips by their contribution to eye width.
- **Receiver margining**: relating eye width and height seen by the scope to the margins reported by the receiver.
:::

::: assumptions
- **NRZ eye with a defined clock reference**; different clock-recovery bandwidths change how much low-frequency jitter appears in the eye.
- **Gaussian random components** with independent jitter and noise; correlated sources need a joint treatment.
- **Dual-Dirac model** for deterministic jitter; $DJ_{\delta\delta}$ is a fit parameter and can differ from the measured peak-to-peak DJ.
- **Histogram level**: crossing histograms at 50% amplitude; some standards use other levels or measure at the BER contour.
- **Numbers are illustrative**; real equipment and standards define measurement filters and sample sizes.
:::

::: followups
### How do you separate random jitter from deterministic jitter from an eye?
Take the crossing-time histogram, fit the Gaussian tails (they are random) and extrapolate to the BER, and treat the offset of the two tails' centres (or the bounded part of the distribution) as deterministic. Scopes do this with spectral or tail-fit algorithms using a repeating pattern; with a known pattern you can also average out the random part and see the deterministic part directly.

### The crossings look fine but the eye height is small. What's going on?
The impairment is on the vertical axis: ISI levels, noise or reflections, not timing. Look at the vertical histogram at the eye centre: several discrete levels point to ISI, a smooth thick band to random noise or crosstalk. If it's ISI, equalize; if noise, find aggressors and supplies.

### Why measure vertical noise at the eye centre and not at the crossing?
At the crossing the edge is steep, so a voltage spread there is mostly timing jitter times slope. At the centre the signal is flat, so the vertical spread is amplitude noise only. Measuring at the crossing mixes the two.

### How would you tell periodic jitter from random jitter in a histogram?
Sinusoidal periodic jitter produces a bimodal ("bathtub-shaped") histogram with peaks at the extremes of the sinusoid, while random jitter gives a single bell. To confirm, compute the spectrum of the TIE: PJ shows spikes at a specific frequency (e.g. a 500 kHz switching regulator), random jitter gives a noise floor.

### If noise doubles on an eye with a 60 ps edge and 15 mV rms noise, how much extra jitter appears at the crossing?
Baseline noise-induced jitter is $15/6.67 = 2.25$ ps rms; doubled noise (30 mV) gives 4.5 ps, an increase of 2.25 ps if it is the only jitter source. If there's already 3 ps of clock jitter, the total goes from $\sqrt{3^2+2.25^2} = 3.75$ to $\sqrt{3^2+4.5^2} = 5.4$ ps.
:::
