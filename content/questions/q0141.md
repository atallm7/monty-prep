::: basics
### In plain words
Maximum power transfer says: a source delivers the most power to a load when the load's resistance equals the source's own internal resistance. It is for situations where the available power is small and you want to catch as much of it as possible, like a radio antenna. It is not what you want for a power supply.

### Picture it
Picture a battery with some resistance inside. If the load is a tiny resistor, a big current flows but almost no voltage appears across the load. If the load is a huge resistor, the voltage is high but almost no current flows. Power is voltage times current, so it is small at both ends and largest somewhere in the middle. That middle is where the load matches the source.

### Words you need
- **Source resistance** ($R_s$): the resistance inside the source.
- **Load resistance** ($R_L$): the resistance of the thing being powered.
- **Matched**: $R_L = R_s$.
- **Efficiency**: the fraction of total power that goes to the load. See [[q0006]].
- **Impedance**: the general word for how strongly a circuit opposes current, covering resistance and more.

### The simplest example
A 10 V source has $R_s = 10$ Ω. With $R_L = 10$ Ω the current is 0.5 A, so the load gets $0.5^2 \times 10 = 2.5$ W. That is the peak, $V_s^2/(4R_s)$. With $R_L = 1$ Ω the load gets only about 0.83 W. With $R_L = 100$ Ω it also gets about 0.83 W. At the match, the source's own resistor burns 2.5 W too, so efficiency is 50%. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the derivation, the complex-conjugate case for reactive sources, and why power supplies do the opposite.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can state the condition ($R_L = R_{source}$, or $Z_L = Z_s^*$ for AC), **derive it** rather than recall it, and, more importantly, know when it is the *wrong* goal. The interviewer is looking for three things: the calculus or algebra behind it, the **50% efficiency** consequence, and the judgment to say "power supplies do the opposite of this". Mentioning the complex-conjugate version for reactive sources, and RF matching as the place where it actually matters, shows you have seen it used.
:::

::: why
This is one of the most misapplied results in circuits. In RF, antennas and sensors, you really do want to extract the most power from a weak source, so matching matters. In power electronics you almost never want it, because matching means half of the power is burned inside the source. Knowing both sides keeps you from making a bad design choice, such as "matching" a battery to its load, and it ties directly to loading effects ([[q0008]]), efficiency ([[q0162]]) and to why a power rail must have a stiff, low-impedance source ([[q0148]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Thevenin equivalent**: any linear source network is an ideal $V_s$ in series with $R_s$. See [[q0008]].
- **Power in a resistor**: $P = I^2R = V^2/R$. See [[q0006]].
- **Voltage divider**: the load voltage is $V_s R_L/(R_s+R_L)$.
- **Maximization**: set $dP/dR_L = 0$ and check it is a maximum.
- **Efficiency**: $\eta = P_L/P_{source}$, which at matching is exactly 50% for a resistive source.
- **Complex conjugate matching**: for AC, reactances must cancel, so $Z_L = Z_s^*$.
:::

::: answer
"Maximum power gets delivered to the load when the load resistance equals the source resistance, $R_L = R_s$. If the source has a reactive part, the load impedance has to be the complex conjugate, $Z_L = Z_s^*$, so the reactances cancel.

To see why, model the source as a voltage $V_s$ with series $R_s$. The current is $V_s/(R_s+R_L)$ and the load power is $V_s^2 R_L/(R_s+R_L)^2$. If $R_L$ is tiny you get lots of current but almost no voltage across the load. If $R_L$ is huge you get voltage but no current. The product peaks in the middle, and if you take the derivative and set it to zero you get $R_L=R_s$. The maximum power is $V_s^2/(4R_s)$.

The catch is that at that point the source resistor dissipates just as much as the load, so efficiency is only 50%. That's fine for something like an antenna or a sensor where the available power is tiny and you want to capture as much as you can, and it's why RF is matched to 50 ohms. But for a power supply I want the opposite: a very low output resistance, so almost all of the power goes to the load and the voltage doesn't sag."
:::

::: technical
**Setup.** Source $V_s$ with series resistance $R_s$ drives a resistive load $R_L$.

$$I = \frac{V_s}{R_s+R_L},\qquad P_L = I^2R_L = \frac{V_s^2R_L}{(R_s+R_L)^2}$$

**Maximize.**
$$\frac{dP_L}{dR_L} = V_s^2\frac{(R_s+R_L)^2 - 2R_L(R_s+R_L)}{(R_s+R_L)^4} = V_s^2\frac{R_s - R_L}{(R_s+R_L)^3}$$
This is zero at $R_L=R_s$, positive below it and negative above it, so it is a maximum.

**Results at the optimum.**
$$P_{L,max} = \frac{V_s^2}{4R_s},\qquad V_L = \frac{V_s}{2},\qquad \eta = \frac{R_L}{R_s+R_L} = 50\%$$

**Sinusoidal sources.** With peak amplitude $V_p$, $P_{max} = V_p^2/(8R_s)$ (the average of $\sin^2$ gives a factor of 1/2). For a complex source impedance $Z_s = R_s + jX_s$ and load $Z_L = R_L + jX_L$, the average power is
$$P = \frac{1}{2}\frac{|V_p|^2 R_L}{(R_s+R_L)^2 + (X_s+X_L)^2}$$
which is maximized by $X_L = -X_s$ and $R_L=R_s$, i.e. $Z_L = Z_s^*$, giving $P_{max} = |V_p|^2/(8R_s)$.

**Mismatch.** The fraction of available power delivered is $1-|\Gamma|^2 = 4R_sR_L/(R_s+R_L)^2$. With $R_s=50$ Ω and $R_L=100$ Ω, $\Gamma = 1/3$ and the load receives 88.9% of the maximum available power.

**Limitations.** The theorem is about *power in the load for a fixed source*. It does not apply when you can choose the source resistance, when efficiency matters, when the source is nonlinear (a solar cell uses MPPT instead) or when the source can only deliver limited current. Also, it assumes the source resistance is fixed and independent of the load.
:::

::: short
"Maximum power goes to the load when the load resistance equals the source resistance, or for AC when $Z_L = Z_s^*$. The load power is $V_s^2R_L/(R_s+R_L)^2$, and setting the derivative to zero gives $R_L = R_s$, with $P_{max} = V_s^2/(4R_s)$. The catch is that efficiency is only 50% at that point, so it's used in RF and signal chains, not in power supplies, where you want low source impedance instead."
:::

::: long
"Take a source as a Thevenin equivalent: an ideal voltage $V_s$ in series with a resistance $R_s$, driving a load $R_L$. The load power is $V_s^2 R_L/(R_s+R_L)^2$.

Think about the two extremes. With $R_L$ at zero, the current is as high as it can be but the voltage across the load is zero, so no power. With $R_L$ very large, you have voltage but nearly zero current. The power has to peak somewhere in between. Taking the derivative gives a numerator proportional to $R_s - R_L$, so the maximum is at $R_L = R_s$. There the load gets half the source voltage and the power is $V_s^2/(4R_s)$. For example a 10 volt source with 50 ohm resistance gives 0.5 watts to a 50 ohm load. A 25 ohm or a 100 ohm load each get 0.444 watts, so the peak is fairly flat.

For AC, if the source impedance has a reactive part, you need the load reactance to cancel it, so the load is the complex conjugate of the source. That's conjugate matching.

The important caveat is efficiency. At the match, the source resistance dissipates the same power as the load, so efficiency is 50%. That's fine when the source is a small signal from an antenna or a sensor, and you just want the most signal. It's why RF systems are standardised around 50 ohms. But for a power supply you want the opposite. A battery with 0.1 ohm internal resistance could deliver about 34 watts into a matched load, but half of that is burned in the battery. So a regulator or battery is designed to have low output impedance so that the load receives nearly all the power and the voltage stays stiff. And for something like a solar panel, where the source characteristic is nonlinear, you use maximum power point tracking rather than a fixed match."
:::

::: terms
- **Maximum power transfer theorem**: a fixed linear source delivers maximum power to the load when $R_L=R_s$ (or $Z_L=Z_s^*$).
- **Thevenin equivalent**: ideal voltage source $V_{th}$ in series with $R_{th}$, representing any linear one-port.
- **Conjugate match**: $Z_L = Z_s^*$, which makes the total reactance zero.
- **Available power**: $V_s^2/(4R_s)$, the most a source can deliver to any load.
- **Efficiency ($\eta$)**: $P_L/P_{total}$, equal to $R_L/(R_s+R_L)$ for a resistive source.
- **Reflection coefficient ($\Gamma$)**: $(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$, which measures mismatch in transmission lines.
- **MPPT**: maximum power point tracking, used for nonlinear sources like solar panels.
- **Output impedance**: the source's $R_{th}$, which should be small for a power supply.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "load equals source resistance" and maybe "complex conjugate for AC."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives it in a few lines and gives the peak value $V_s^2/(4R_s)$.
- States the **50% efficiency** consequence unprompted and says when that is acceptable (RF, antennas, sensors) and when it is not (power supplies, batteries).
- Mentions the conjugate form and that reactances cancel.
- Notes the curve is flat near the peak (a factor of 2 mismatch only costs about 11%).
- Brings in nonlinear sources: for a solar panel, MPPT finds the operating point because the source is not a fixed Thevenin resistance.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking this maximizes efficiency.** It maximizes power only; efficiency at the match is 50%. For high efficiency you want $R_L\gg R_s$.
- **Applying it to power supplies.** A regulator is designed for $R_{out}\ll R_L$. A 12 V supply is not "matched" to its load.
- **Mixing up which resistance varies.** The theorem holds with $R_s$ fixed and $R_L$ variable. If you can vary $R_s$, the best choice is $R_s=0$ (for a voltage source).
- **Forgetting the AC condition.** For a reactive source, equal resistances are not enough; the reactances must cancel, $X_L=-X_s$.
- **Using $V_s^2/(8R_s)$ or $V_s^2/(4R_s)$ interchangeably.** The first is for a sine of peak amplitude $V_p$; the second is for DC (or rms).
:::

The current is $I=V_s/(R_s+R_L)$, so

$$P_L = \frac{V_s^2 R_L}{(R_s+R_L)^2}.$$

**Intuition before calculus.** Small $R_L$ means large current but a small voltage across the load: $P=I^2R_L\to 0$. Large $R_L$ means large voltage but small current: $P=V_L^2/R_L\to 0$. In between, there has to be a maximum.

**Algebra shortcut.** Write $P_L = V_s^2/(R_L + 2R_s + R_s^2/R_L)$. The denominator is minimized when $R_L + R_s^2/R_L$ is minimized, which by AM-GM happens when $R_L=R_s^2/R_L$, i.e. $R_L=R_s$. No derivative needed, which is a nice thing to do on a whiteboard.

## AC and the conjugate match

For sinusoidal steady state, replace resistances with impedances. The average power into the load is $P=\tfrac12\mathrm{Re}(V_LI^*)$. With $Z_s=R_s+jX_s$ and $Z_L=R_L+jX_L$:

$$P=\frac12\frac{|V_p|^2R_L}{(R_s+R_L)^2+(X_s+X_L)^2}$$

The reactive term in the denominator is a pure loss of power transfer, so the best case is $X_L=-X_s$ (an inductive source needs a capacitive load, and vice versa) and then $R_L=R_s$. The result is $Z_L=Z_s^*$ and $P_{max}=|V_p|^2/(8R_s)$.

## Where you really want it

- **RF front ends and antennas**: the available power is tiny, so you maximize what you capture. Matching networks (L-networks, transformers) transform the load impedance to the source impedance. On transmission lines, matching also removes reflections, which is a separate reason ([[q0148]] is about power rails; for lines see the reflection-focused questions in the PCB section).
- **Audio**: a speaker amplifier does **not** match. A typical amplifier output impedance is far below the speaker's 8 Ω. A voltage-source amplifier into 8 Ω with 0.1 Ω output gets 98.8% of the voltage across the speaker, so power delivery is efficient and the speaker is "damped".
- **Sensors and energy harvesters**: a small piezo or thermoelectric harvester only produces a few mW. Matching the load (often through a converter that presents an adjustable input resistance) captures the most energy.

## Where you must not use it: batteries and supplies

A 3.7 V Li-ion cell with 0.1 Ω internal resistance could deliver $3.7^2/(4\times0.1)=34.2$ W into a matched 0.1 Ω load, at 18.5 A and with half the power lost as heat inside the cell. The cell would overheat and the voltage at the load would be only 1.85 V. A real load might draw maybe 2 A, where the internal drop is just 0.2 V and efficiency is $>94\%$. Power supplies, batteries and wiring are all designed to keep $R_s\ll R_L$.

## Nonlinear sources

A solar panel's $I$-$V$ curve is not a straight line, so the Thevenin equivalent changes with the operating point. The maximum power point is where $d(VI)/dV=0$ on the actual curve. An MPPT converter hunts for this by adjusting the effective load resistance, as the fixed-resistor theorem cannot do it for you.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked "so would you match your power supply to the load?", the strong answer is: "No. Matching gives 50% efficiency and a load voltage that moves a lot with load. I want the source impedance as small as possible. Matching is for signals, not for power."

## Worked example: an L-network that makes the match

When the load does not equal the source, you do not change the source; you put a lossless network between them that *transforms* the load impedance into the source's. Suppose the source is the 10 V, 50 Ω generator from the table and the load is 12.5 Ω at 100 MHz. Directly connected, the load receives $V_s^2R_L/(R_s+R_L)^2=0.32$ W of the available 0.5 W, a mismatch efficiency of $4R_sR_L/(R_s+R_L)^2=64\%$ (a 1.94 dB loss).

An L-network fixes it with two reactive parts. The loaded quality factor is $Q=\sqrt{R_{high}/R_{low}-1}=\sqrt{50/12.5-1}=1.732$.

1. Put a series element next to the low-resistance load with reactance $X_s=Q\,R_{low}=21.65\ \Omega$. As an inductor at 100 MHz that is $L=21.65/(2\pi\cdot10^8)=34.5$ nH.
2. The series combination $12.5+j21.65\ \Omega$ is equivalent to a parallel pair: $R_p=R(1+Q^2)=12.5\times4=50\ \Omega$ and $X_p=X(1+1/Q^2)=28.87\ \Omega$ (inductive).
3. Cancel that parallel reactance with a shunt capacitor of reactance $-28.87\ \Omega$ across the high-resistance (source) side: $C=1/(2\pi\cdot10^8\cdot28.87)=55.1$ pF.

The source now sees exactly 50 Ω, resistive, so the conjugate-match condition is met and the load receives the full 0.5 W (an ideal lossless network passes everything). Real parts are not lossless. An inductor with a quality factor of 50 at this frequency has a series resistance of $21.65/50=0.43\ \Omega$ that sits in series with the 12.5 Ω load, so about $0.43/(12.5+0.43)=3.3\%$ of the power (0.15 dB) heats the inductor and the load receives roughly 0.48 W. Still much better than the 0.32 W without the network. Two further observations: the match is perfect only at the design frequency (at other frequencies the reactances no longer cancel the parallel part), and moving to a larger impedance ratio raises $Q$, which narrows the bandwidth and increases the inductor's loss.

## Limiting cases

- **$R_L\to0$ or $R_L\to\infty$.** The load power goes to zero from the delivered-power formula, while the efficiency goes to 0% or 100% respectively, which is why the maximum-power and maximum-efficiency goals diverge at the extremes.
- **Source with a current limit.** A bench supply in current-limit mode behaves as a current source, with a large effective source resistance; the "matching" load is then very different from the voltage-mode answer.
- **Time-varying or nonlinear source.** A solar cell, fuel cell or thermoelectric generator changes its effective $R_s$ with the operating point, so the optimum load is a moving target and is tracked, not set.
- **A mismatch of a factor of two** (say a 50 Ω source and a 100 Ω load) delivers 88.9% of the maximum, only a 0.5 dB loss, which is why loose RF matches are acceptable when the gain is plentiful.

## Measuring a source's impedance and the matching point

You can find a source's Thevenin resistance with two voltage measurements. Read the open-circuit voltage $V_{oc}$, then connect a known load $R_L$ and read $V_L$. The source resistance is $R_s=R_L\,(V_{oc}/V_L-1)$. For a Li-ion cell reading 3.70 V open and 3.63 V across 10 Ω, $R_s=10\times(3.70/3.63-1)=0.19\ \Omega$, plausible for a small cell (large cells are often tens of milliohms, and the figure varies with chemistry, age and temperature). For a low-power source that tolerates it (a signal generator, a small solar cell, a sensor output), an equivalent bench trick is the half-voltage method: adjust a decade box or potentiometer until the load voltage falls to exactly $V_{oc}/2$; the setting is then equal to $R_s$ and is also the maximum-power load. Never do this to a battery: loading a 0.19 Ω cell to half its voltage means about 10 A, close to a short circuit. Take the two-point measurement quickly with a high-impedance meter, because a battery's apparent resistance drifts with temperature, state of charge and measurement time, and because a long load connection adds its own resistance to the reading. For RF sources the equivalent tool is a network analyzer or return-loss bridge, which reads the reflection coefficient $|\Gamma|$ and therefore the mismatch directly.

:::

::: applications
- **50 Ω RF systems**: antennas, amplifiers, filters and test equipment are standardised on 50 Ω so that cables and instruments can be connected with minimal mismatch loss.
- **Impedance-matching networks**: LC or transformer networks on a power amplifier or LNA transform the antenna impedance to the device's optimum.
- **Energy harvesting**: piezoelectric and thermoelectric harvesters present a source resistance, and the harvesting IC adjusts its input impedance to extract the most power.
- **Solar MPPT chargers**: such as those in solar-powered devices, which track the maximum power point of a nonlinear source.
- **Audio line and microphone inputs**: instead use *bridging* (load impedance much larger than source impedance, often ~10 times) to transfer voltage, not power.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear source with fixed internal resistance** (Thevenin). Batteries, solar cells and supplies with current limiting are not exactly like this.
- **$R_s$ fixed, $R_L$ adjustable.** If you can pick $R_s$ instead, the best choice for a voltage source is zero.
- **Resistive loads at DC** or sinusoidal steady state for the AC version. For non-sinusoidal signals, matching at one frequency does not match all harmonics.
- **"Maximum power" is the goal.** If the goal is efficiency, voltage fidelity or low heat, a different condition ($R_L\gg R_s$) applies.
- **Alternative valid answer.** For a *transmission line* the equivalent condition is $Z_L=Z_0$ (the characteristic impedance) so there is no reflection. That is about signal integrity as much as power.
:::

::: followups
### What's the efficiency at maximum power transfer?
50% for a resistive source, because the same current passes through $R_s$ and $R_L=R_s$, so they dissipate equal power. At $R_L=9R_s$ the load gets 90% of the source power but only 0.36 of $P_{max}$ (since $4\cdot9/100=0.36$).

### A 12 V supply has 0.5 Ω output resistance. What load gets maximum power, and what is that power?
$R_L=0.5$ Ω, so $P_{max}=12^2/(4\times0.5)=72$ W. The load sees 6 V and the supply wastes another 72 W internally, so you'd need a 144 W-capable source. That's why nobody runs a supply this way.

### What if the source resistance can be changed too?
For a fixed $R_L$, power is maximized by making $R_s$ as small as possible, ideally zero (for a voltage source). Matching only applies when $R_s$ is fixed.

### Why do RF engineers match to 50 Ω if efficiency is only 50%?
Two reasons. First, for weak signals (antenna, receiver) you want the maximum signal power and the 50% loss is mostly irrelevant. Second, in a transmission line a matched load eliminates reflections, which matters for signal integrity, and with a matched source it also prevents re-reflection. Power amplifiers are typically matched to their *optimum load*, not conjugate-matched, because efficiency matters there.

### A solar panel gives 5 W at one load and 3 W at another. How do you find the maximum power point?
The panel is nonlinear, so its Thevenin resistance changes with the operating point. An MPPT algorithm perturbs the converter's input voltage, watches $P=VI$, and steps toward higher power ("perturb and observe"). The max point is where the load line is tangent to the constant-power hyperbola.

### Scenario: your sensor gives 100 mV through a 10 kΩ source impedance into an ADC. Should you match the ADC input to 10 kΩ?
Probably not. A matched 10 kΩ load would halve the signal to 50 mV, which hurts the signal-to-noise ratio. You want the opposite: a high-impedance input (a buffer or an ADC with MΩ input) so the voltage is preserved. Matching is for power transfer; this measurement is a voltage measurement.
:::

::: interpretations
"Condition for maximum power transfer" normally means the resistive case $R_L=R_s$ (and conjugate for AC). Some interviewers mean the transmission-line version ($Z_L=Z_0$), which is about avoiding reflections. State the circuits version first, then mention the other.
:::
