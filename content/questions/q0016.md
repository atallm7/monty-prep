::: testing
Whether you can draw the standard high-frequency resistor model **and justify each element physically**, not just recite "L and C". Interviewers listen for:

1. The correct topology: series $R$ and $L_s$, with $C_p$ in parallel across the whole thing.
2. The physical origin of each: leads and the current path for $L_s$, the terminations and body for $C_p$, and the trim spiral or wirewound coil for extra inductance.
3. The consequence: high-value resistors turn capacitive, low-value ones turn inductive, and there is a "best behaved" value near $\sqrt{L_s/C_p}$.
4. Awareness of non-element effects: thermal noise, TCR, voltage coefficient.
:::

::: why
Resistors are the most "boring" part, which is why their parasitics bite: nobody checks. A 100 kΩ feedback resistor with 0.2 pF across it sets a pole at 8 MHz; a 10 Ω gate resistor with a long axial lead can ring with the gate capacitance; a 50 Ω termination built from a leaded part misbehaves above a few hundred MHz; a mΩ-level shunt has an $L\,di/dt$ error. High-speed design, RF, probing and precision analog all depend on knowing when a resistor stops being one.
:::

::: concepts
- **Resistance** ([[q0014]]): the intended element, constant at low frequency.
- **Series inductance $L_s$**: any current path stores magnetic energy; leads, vias, and spiral trim cuts add to it.
- **Parallel (terminal-to-terminal) capacitance $C_p$**: the two terminations and the body form a small capacitor, with the film and substrate as dielectric.
- **Impedance of a parallel/series network**: $Z=(R+j\omega L_s)\,/\,(1-\omega^2L_sC_p+j\omega RC_p)$ ([[q0011]]).
- **Characteristic resistance $\sqrt{L_s/C_p}$**: the boundary between "mostly capacitive" and "mostly inductive".
- **Noise and drift**: Johnson noise $\sqrt{4kTR\,\Delta f}$ and TCR are real effects but not circuit elements ([[q0015]]).
:::

::: answer
"The standard model is the ideal resistance R in series with a small inductance Ls, and then a small capacitance Cp across the whole combination, terminal to terminal. 

The inductance comes from the current path. Current flowing through the leads and the resistive element creates a magnetic field, which is inductance. A wirewound resistor is literally a coil, so it's very inductive. A film resistor with a spiral trim has some inductance because the current goes around the spiral. A chip resistor is better, but there's still a few tenths of a nanohenry from the body and the pads.

The capacitance comes from the two end terminations facing each other, plus the body of the resistor acting as a dielectric, and a bit from the pads and the board. For a chip resistor that's about a tenth of a picofarad.

At low frequency, the capacitor is open and the inductor a short, so you just get R. As frequency rises, a high-value resistor shows its capacitance first, because the capacitor only needs to be lower impedance than R to bypass it. A 10 kilohm with 0.1 pF has a corner near 160 MHz. A low-value resistor, like 10 ohms, shows its inductance instead. The most well-behaved value is around the square root of L over C, which is about 80 ohms for a 0603."
:::

::: technical
**Model:** $R$ in series with $L_s$, then $C_p$ across the pair.

$$Z(j\omega)=\frac{R+j\omega L_s}{1-\omega^2L_sC_p+j\omega RC_p}$$

**Limits**
- Low $R$ ($R\ll\sqrt{L_s/C_p}$): inductive corner $f_L = R/(2\pi L_s)$; above it, $|Z|\approx\omega L_s$.
- High $R$ ($R\gg\sqrt{L_s/C_p}$): capacitive corner $f_C=1/(2\pi RC_p)$; above it, $|Z|\approx 1/(\omega C_p)$.
- Self-resonance (parallel) at $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_sC_p})$, where for high $R$ the impedance peaks until damped.
- Characteristic resistance $R_0=\sqrt{L_s/C_p}$: with $R=R_0$, the impedance is nearly flat to well beyond the corners.

**Typical values** (order of magnitude, vendor and package dependent): 0402/0603 chip: $L_s\approx0.3$–1 nH, $C_p\approx0.05$–0.2 pF; axial leaded 1/4 W: $L_s\approx$ 5–20 nH, $C_p\approx0.3$–1 pF; wirewound: hundreds of nH to µH unless non-inductively wound.

**Numbers** (0603: $L_s=0.7$ nH, $C_p=0.1$ pF): $R_0=84\ \Omega$; $f_C(10\ \text{k}\Omega)=159$ MHz; $f_C(1\ \text{k}\Omega)=1.6$ GHz; $f_L(10\ \Omega)=2.3$ GHz. At 1 GHz a nominal 10 kΩ looks like 1.6 kΩ.
:::

::: long
"If you ask me to draw it, I draw the ideal resistor R, a small inductor Ls in series with it, and then a small capacitor Cp across the whole series pair, terminal to terminal.

Where they come from: the inductance is the magnetic energy stored by the current path. Straight leads are already about a nanohenry per millimeter, a wirewound resistor is a coil, and film resistors that are laser-trimmed with a spiral have a bit of coil in them too. The capacitance is the parallel-plate capacitance between the two end caps, through the ceramic body, plus the pads on the board. For a chip resistor it's a tenth of a picofarad or so; for a leaded part, a few tenths.

What that does: at DC you only see R. As frequency goes up, the shunt capacitance matters when its reactance falls to R, so the corner is one over two pi R C. For 10 kilohms and 0.1 pF that's 160 megahertz, and at a gigahertz that resistor looks like 1.6 kilohms. For a small resistor, like 10 ohms, the capacitor is irrelevant, but the series inductor starts mattering when omega L equals R, which is a couple of gigahertz for 0.7 nanohenries. Between the two, around the square root of L over C, about 80 ohms, the part stays flat the longest, which is why termination resistors are in that range.

Beyond those two elements there are things I wouldn't draw as elements but mention: thermal noise, temperature coefficient, voltage coefficient, and self-heating. In a real design I'd use a 0402 or 0201 thin-film part for RF, keep the pads short, and check the vendor's S-parameters."
:::

::: terms
- **Series inductance $L_s$**: inductance of leads, body and trim pattern.
- **Shunt capacitance $C_p$**: terminal-to-terminal capacitance of the part.
- **Self-resonant frequency**: $1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_sC_p})$.
- **Characteristic resistance $R_0$**: $\sqrt{L_s/C_p}$, where the part is flattest.
- **Laser trim / spiral cut**: removes film to raise $R$ to target; lengthens the current path and adds inductance.
- **Wirewound resistor**: resistive wire wound on a former: low TCR, high inductance unless bifilar/Ayrton-Perry wound.
- **Thin/thick film**: resistive layer deposited on ceramic; thin-film is more precise and lower noise.
- **Johnson (thermal) noise**: $v_n=\sqrt{4kTR\Delta f}$, independent of construction.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws R, L and C and says "inductance from leads, capacitance between terminals".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gets the topology right (L in series with R, C across both) and writes $Z(j\omega)$.
- Explains why high-value resistors go capacitive while low-value ones go inductive and gives the crossover $\sqrt{L_s/C_p}$.
- Names real construction differences: wirewound coil, spiral-trim film, chip, MELF, and how each changes $L_s$ and $C_p$.
- Quotes numbers for a package and computes a corner frequency.
- Separates parasitic *elements* from *other* non-idealities (noise, TCR, VCR, self-heating, parasitics of the layout).
- States a practical consequence: probe tips, feedback networks, terminations, shunts.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting $C_p$ across only the resistor and putting $L_s$ in parallel.** Series inductance and shunt capacitance is the standard model; an inductor in parallel with the resistor would short out DC, which no real resistor does.
- **Saying the resistor "becomes a capacitor" at high frequency for all values.** Low values become inductive first.
- **Ignoring the layout.** A pad and via add 0.5–1 nH and a few tenths of a pF; for chip resistors, the board often dominates.
- **Using wirewound resistors for fast pulses or RF.** They are coils; use non-inductive winding or film/foil.
- **Assuming a gate resistor is harmless.** A leaded 10 Ω gate resistor adds nanohenries into the gate loop and changes ringing ([[q0030]] shows the capacitor analog).
:::

::: deep
## Building the model from physics

A resistor is a piece of resistive material with two metal terminations. Any current path has a magnetic flux per amp, which is its inductance, so $L_s$ is nonzero. Any two conductors at different potentials with a dielectric in between have capacitance; here those conductors are the two terminations (and the pads), so $C_p$ is nonzero. Loss in the dielectric is negligible, so we don't add more elements.

Construction determines the values:

| Type | Why $L_s$ | Why $C_p$ | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|

| Axial film, spiral-trimmed | leads (~10 nH total) + spiral | terminations and body | OK to tens of MHz |
| Chip (thick/thin film) | short flat current path | end caps over ceramic | good to GHz at 0402/0201 |
| MELF | cylinder, short | end caps | similar to chip; larger |
| Carbon composition | leads only | body | historically the best for RF |

## Impedance versus frequency

The complete impedance is the parallel combination of $(R+j\omega L_s)$ with $1/(j\omega C_p)$:

$$|Z|=\frac{\sqrt{R^2+\omega^2L_s^2}}{\sqrt{(1-\omega^2L_sC_p)^2+(\omega RC_p)^2}}$$

## What is *not* in the model

- **Thermal (Johnson) noise**: a 10 kΩ resistor at 300 K generates $\sqrt{4kTR}=12.9\ \text{nV}/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ ($\approx$ 12.9 µV over 1 MHz), regardless of construction. Excess (1/f) noise depends on construction; thin-film and wirewound are quiet, carbon composition noisier.
- **TCR, VCR and self-heating** ([[q0015]]): these change $R$ itself.
- **Layout parasitics**: pads, vias, a nearby ground plane (extra $C_p$), and loop area for $L_s$.
- **Distributed effects**: above a few GHz the part is a small transmission-line section; use S-parameters.

## Design consequences

1. **Feedback networks on fast op-amps:** high-value resistors plus input capacitance create a pole; add a small feedback capacitor (a few pF) to compensate and use lower-value resistors where noise and power allow.
2. **Terminations (50 Ω):** choose chip thin-film parts, keep the pads short, and avoid vias in series.
3. **Gate resistors and snubbers:** use low-inductance chip parts, not axial.
4. **Current sensing:** low-inductance (reverse-geometry or metal-strip) shunts; $L\,di/dt$ error is $v=L_s\,(di/dt)$, which for 1 nH and 1 A/ns is 1 V.
5. **Probing:** a scope probe's input is an RC (10 MΩ ∥ ~10 pF), a deliberate parasitic you must include.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $L_s$ and $C_p$ values depend heavily on vendor, film type and package; the figures here are plausible order-of-magnitude values for 0603 parts. Use vendor S-parameter or SPICE models for design work.
:::

::: applications
- **50 Ω terminations on high-speed lines** use thin-film chip resistors chosen for small $L_s$.
- **RF attenuator (pi/T pads)** built from 0402 or 0201 chip resistors, with layout parasitics included in the design.
- **Feedback resistors in transimpedance amplifiers** with a small compensating capacitor across them.
- **Series damping resistors (22–33 Ω)** on clock and bus lines.
- **Shunt resistors in switching supplies**, with low-inductance geometries.
- **Resistor ladders in high-speed DACs/ADCs**, where parasitic capacitance sets settling time.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Lumped model** valid when the part is much shorter than a wavelength; for 0603 up to a few GHz.
- **Linear and temperature-independent** values.
- Typical values for $L_s$ and $C_p$ are for **chip** parts; leaded and wirewound parts are much worse.
- The $C_p$-across-both topology is the usual one; some models instead put half the capacitance from each terminal to ground (a pi model) for distributed behaviour, and some include a small series resistance in the capacitor. The conclusions are the same.
- If an interviewer wants only a quick sketch, R, L in series with C in parallel across them is sufficient.
:::

::: followups
### Where does the inductance in a wirewound resistor come from and how can it be reduced?
It is a coil, so $L\approx\mu_0N^2A/\ell$ style inductance. Non-inductive winding (Ayrton–Perry: two windings in opposite directions, or bifilar) cancels the magnetic fields. It reduces $L_s$ by orders of magnitude but increases $C_p$ slightly.

### Why do high-value resistors get hit by parasitic capacitance first?
The shunt capacitor only has to beat $R$. At 10 kΩ, $X_C=R$ at $1/(2\pi RC_p)=159$ MHz for 0.1 pF. At 10 Ω, $X_C=R$ would be 159 GHz, far beyond other limits, but $\omega L_s$ reaches $R$ at 2.3 GHz.

### How would you check your model against a real part?
Measure |Z| and phase with a vector network analyzer or impedance analyzer on a fixture with known de-embedding, then fit $L_s$ and $C_p$ so the model matches the measured curve. Compare with the vendor's S-parameter file.

### What if you need a 50 Ω resistor that works to 10 GHz?
Use thin-film, 0201 or 0402 parts designed for RF, put them in a microstrip or CPW with a controlled pad geometry and reference ground, and simulate the footprint in an EM solver. At 10 GHz, even the pad geometry dominates and the lumped model fails.

### A gate-drive loop rings. Could the gate resistor be part of the cause?
Yes, if it's a leaded or wirewound part. The nanohenries add to the gate loop inductance, lowering the damping factor, since $\zeta=(R/2)\sqrt{C/L}$. Switch to a chip resistor and shorten the loop; see [[q0030]] for the capacitor view.
:::

::: interpretations
"Realistic circuit model" means the lumped high-frequency equivalent circuit. Some interviewers also want the **noise model** (resistor in series with a voltage noise source $\sqrt{4kTR}$ or in parallel with a current noise source $\sqrt{4kT/R}$). I give the inductance/capacitance model first and add noise as a bonus.
:::
