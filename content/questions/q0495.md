::: basics
### In plain words
Star grounding gives every circuit its own separate return path, and all those paths meet at one single point, like spokes meeting at a hub. It works because ground is not really zero volts: any copper has resistance and inductance. If a noisy circuit and a sensitive one share part of a return, the noisy one's current creates a voltage that shows up in the sensitive one's reference. In a star, they share only the hub.

### Picture it
Picture a bicycle wheel. Every spoke runs from the rim to the hub, and no two spokes share any length. Whatever happens on one spoke does not travel along another. Now picture instead a single chain of connected rods: stress at the far end passes through every rod on the way. A star is the wheel. A daisy chain is the chain of rods. The analogy breaks at high frequency, where long spokes turn into inductors and loops form.

### Words you need
- **Return current**: the current that flows back to the source through ground ([[q0004]]).
- **Common-impedance coupling**: voltage across a shared return segment that appears in other circuits' references ([[q0001]]).
- **Single-point ground**: all returns meet at exactly one location ([[q0494]]).
- **Ground loop**: a second path between two grounds that encloses area and picks up noise.
- **Kelvin connection**: a small star where the sense wires avoid the high-current path.

### The simplest example
Three loads return current to ground: a power stage at 1 A, logic at 20 mA and a sensor at 100 µA. Each ground segment is 20 mΩ. In a daisy chain with the sensor last, the sensor's ground shifts by about $(1.0201 + 0.0201 + 0.0001) \times 0.02 \approx 20.8$ mV. In a star, it shifts by only $100\ \mu\text{A} \times 20\ \text{m}\Omega = 2\ \mu$V. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers why it fails at high frequency, how to build a star on a real board, and how a second connection creates a ground loop.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can define star grounding precisely, explain **why** it works (it removes shared return impedance), and say where it breaks (high frequency, accidental loops). The hidden sub-skills:

1. Seeing ground return as current through an impedance, not an abstract 0 V.
2. Quantifying the benefit with an $I\cdot R$ example.
3. Knowing a star is a *topology* that can be built from traces or implemented by *placement* on a plane.
4. Knowing its limits: long legs are inductive, and any second connection creates a ground loop.
:::

::: why
Star grounding is the classic answer to "my sensitive sensor reads garbage when the motor or LED driver turns on". It is also how audio amplifiers, lab power supplies and precision instruments are wired. Even if your board is a plane, the *idea* of star grounding (bring returns together at one deliberate point, keep high-current returns away from quiet ones) shows up in power-entry layout, ADC placement and current-sense routing. Interviewers use it to see whether you reason about where current flows.
:::

::: concepts
- **Return current and common impedance**: voltage drop on a shared return segment appears in every circuit referenced to it. See [[q0001]], [[q0004]].
- **Single-point ground**: all returns meet at exactly one location. See [[q0494]].
- **Ground loop**: two paths between the same two grounds enclose area and pick up magnetic noise or carry circulating current.
- **Inductance of a trace**: roughly 1 nH/mm without a nearby plane, so long star legs become inductive at modest frequency ([[q0497]]).
- **Kelvin / sense connections**: a small-scale star, where a sensitive measurement is taken at the point of interest and not through shared current paths.
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** A star (parallel single-point) ground connects each subsystem's return to a common reference node with a dedicated conductor. No two subsystems share a return segment before the star point.

**Why it removes coupling.** For a daisy chain, the ground reference at the far circuit $N$ is

$$V_N = \sum_k R_k\,I_{\text{through }k}$$

where each segment carries the sum of all downstream return currents. In a star the reference of circuit $n$ relative to the hub is only $V_n = I_n\,(R_n + j\omega L_n)$, which is its own current through its own leg.

**Worked example.** Three loads return a total of 1.02 A: a power stage at 1 A, digital at 20 mA, an analog sensor at 100 µA. Each segment is 20 mΩ.

- Daisy chain with the analog circuit last: analog ground shifts by $(1.0201 + 0.0201 + 0.0001)\times 0.02 \approx 20.8$ mV relative to the star point. That is about 26 LSBs of a 12-bit, 3.3 V ADC (0.8 mV/LSB).
- Star: the analog ground shifts by $100\ \mu\text{A} \times 20\ \text{m}\Omega = 2\ \mu\text{V}$.

**Limits.**
- *High frequency.* Each leg has $L\approx 1$ nH/mm. A 100 mm leg is about 100 nH, so a 20 mA edge in 2 ns across it develops $L\,di/dt = 100\text{ nH}\times 20\text{ mA}/2\text{ ns} = 1$ V.
- *Ground loops.* If the analog subsystem also connects to chassis or another instrument's ground by a second path, the enclosed loop area picks up magnetic noise.
- *Return of high-frequency signals.* Signals referenced to a star leg that is not next to their signal path have a large loop.

**Implementation on a PCB.** (a) Separate traces to a hub at the bulk capacitor or the connector; (b) a plane, with the star realised by *placing* subsystems and connecting them at one location, e.g. an ADC package or the power entry; (c) the plane can be continuous and the "star" is where return currents are deliberately directed to.
:::

::: short
"Star grounding gives every circuit its own return path to one common point, so no two circuits share a return segment. That matters because ground has resistance and inductance, and a shared segment turns one circuit's current into noise on another's reference. It works best at low frequency, where legs are resistive, and for mixing power, digital and analog. The risks are long inductive legs at high frequency and accidental second connections that make ground loops."
:::

::: long
"A star ground is a topology where every subsystem has its own dedicated return conductor, and all of them meet at a single point, the hub. It's the opposite of daisy-chaining, where circuits share one ground wire.

The reason to do it is that ground isn't an ideal node. A trace of 20 milliohms carrying an amp drops 20 millivolts, and anything referenced downstream of that drop sees it. Say I have a power stage drawing an amp, some digital logic at 20 milliamps and a sensor at 100 microamps, and I daisy-chain them with the sensor last. The sensor's ground moves by about 20 millivolts relative to the supply return, which is roughly 25 LSBs of a 12-bit ADC. In a star, the sensor's reference only sees its own current across its own leg, so about 2 microvolts. That's the whole benefit: the circuits only interact at the hub.

Where it breaks: at high frequency the legs are inductive, around a nanohenry per millimetre, so a long leg isn't a low-impedance path anymore. A 100 millimetre leg is 100 nanohenries, and a fast edge across that gives you a volt. It also encourages big loops, and any extra connection between the subsystems, like a shield, a scope ground clip or a chassis screw, creates a ground loop.

So on a modern board with fast digital I use a continuous ground plane, and I do the star by placement. I put the power stage near the input connector, the quiet analog on the far side, and tie them together at one deliberate location, usually at the power entry or under the ADC. I keep high-current returns out of the sensitive area. For low-frequency, high-precision work, like load cells or audio, a real star wired with separate returns is still a good solution."
:::

::: terms
- **Star point (hub)**: the single node where all returns are joined.
- **Leg**: the dedicated return conductor from one subsystem to the hub.
- **Common-impedance coupling**: interference caused by sharing a conductor.
- **Ground loop**: a closed conductive loop formed by multiple ground connections, which picks up magnetic fields and carries circulating current.
- **Kelvin connection**: a separate sense connection made at the point of interest so the measurement does not include current-carrying drops.
- **Return current**: the current that flows back to the source after the load.
- **Virtual star**: a star created by placement and routing on a plane instead of by separate traces.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "everything connects to one point" and "it avoids ground loops". An exceptional one:

- Calls it a *parallel single-point* scheme and contrasts it with the series daisy chain.
- Shows the **numbers**: 20.8 mV vs 2 µV for the same legs, or the equivalent in ADC LSBs.
- States the frequency limit: legs are about 1 nH/mm, so a star is a low-frequency tool, and explains why a plane is used for fast logic.
- Warns about the **accidental second connection** (scope probe ground, shield, chassis) that spoils a star by creating a loop.
- Says where the hub goes (power entry, bulk capacitor, or under the ADC) and how it is realised on a PCB plane by placement.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"A star ground has no ground loops."** Only if there is truly a single connection. A shield or chassis tie between two subsystems makes a loop.
- **"Star means a star-shaped copper pour."** The topology is about *current paths*; a pour that lets currents mix is not a star.
- **Ignoring inductance.** Long legs work at DC and audio but are poor at tens of MHz; see [[q0494]].
- **Putting the hub at an arbitrary spot.** The best hub is where the supply returns, the bulk capacitor or the input connector, so that the return current is naturally defined.
- **Using a star for high-speed signal return.** Each fast signal needs its return directly beside it; routing it back to a distant hub creates a large loop.
:::

::: deep
## The problem star grounding solves

Every conductor has some resistance and inductance. If two circuits return their current through the same piece of conductor, the voltage across that conductor is the sum of the two circuits' contributions:

$$v_{\text{shared}}(t) = \big(i_A(t) + i_B(t)\big)\,R_g + L_g\,\frac{d(i_A + i_B)}{dt}$$

Circuit A (the sensitive one) uses the far end of that conductor as its reference, so circuit B's current shows up in A's measurement. That is **common-impedance coupling**, and it is something no amount of filtering on A's signal input can fix, because the error is *in the reference*.

The cure is to stop sharing. Give B a return that does not touch A's, so $v_A$ depends only on $i_A$. Taken to its natural end that is a star.

## Numbers: daisy chain vs star

Take the three loads from the figure: sensor 100 µA, logic 20 mA, motor 1 A, with every conductor segment at 20 mΩ.

| Scheme | Ground shift seen by the sensor | In LSBs of a 12-bit, 3.3 V ADC (0.8 mV) |
|---|---|---|
| Daisy chain, sensor last (shares three segments) | ≈ 20.8 mV | ≈ 26 LSB |
| Star | 100 µA × 20 mΩ = 2 µV | ≈ 0.0025 LSB |

The daisy-chain result is $(1.0201 + 0.0201 + 0.0001)\times 0.02 \approx 0.0208$ V. The star result is almost four orders of magnitude better because the sensor's reference no longer contains the motor's current.

For a precision 16-bit converter (LSB about 50 µV on 3.3 V), even 20 mV would be 400 LSBs. That is why precision analog gear is wired in a star.

## Why the star fails at high frequency

Each leg is a conductor with inductance of about 1 nH/mm if there is no nearby plane:

$$|Z_{\text{leg}}| = \sqrt{R^2 + (2\pi f L)^2}$$

For a 100 mm leg ($R\approx 0.2\ \Omega$, $L\approx 100$ nH): the corner $R/(2\pi L)$ is about 320 kHz. At 10 MHz the leg is $2\pi\times 10^7\times 10^{-7} \approx 6.3\ \Omega$. A logic signal's return that must travel to a hub like that creates a big loop: large magnetic coupling, ringing and radiated emission. So star grounding is a **low-frequency** tool. In a fast digital system the return current wants to flow under the signal trace, so the star must be replaced by a plane.

## Implementing a star on a real PCB

1. **Separate traces.** On a 2-layer board with a low-level analog front end, route the analog ground as its own trace back to the supply connector or bulk capacitor, the power ground as another, and join them only at that point.
2. **Plane with a "virtual" star.** On a 4-layer board use a continuous plane, but place the power stage next to the input connector, the digital in the middle, the analog far away, and give the analog's decoupling and reference grounds a local connection to the plane. Currents mostly stay in their neighbourhood because the plane's impedance is lowest there.
3. **Star at the ADC.** Many mixed-signal ICs have separate analog and digital ground pins. The datasheet usually says to tie them both to the same plane directly under the part: the chip itself is the star point.
4. **Current sense.** A shunt measured with a Kelvin connection is a micro-star: two sense traces leave the pads of the shunt and run together to the amplifier, so the current-carrying path is not part of the measurement.

## Ground loops: where stars go to die

A star implies exactly one path between any two subsystems. Once a second path exists, e.g. a shield on a cable, a screw to a metal chassis, or a scope ground clip, there's a loop. Two things go wrong:

- **Magnetic pickup.** A changing flux $\Phi$ through the loop induces $V = d\Phi/dt$ in series with the loop, which appears as an error between the two grounds.
- **Circulating current.** Any difference in the potential of the two ground points (for example mains-related) drives current round the loop through the "clean" conductor.

The fix is to break the second path (isolation, a ferrite or a capacitor at the shield end) or to put that path where it carries no sensitive current.

## Connecting back to planes

On a plane, "star" becomes a statement about **where current enters and leaves the plane**. If the high-current power connection enters at one end and the sensitive circuit is at the other, the DC return is a smooth distribution of current across the plane, and the voltage gradient across it is $I\times R_{sq}\times(\text{number of squares})$. For 1 A across a 100 mm long, 50 mm wide 1 oz region, that is 2 squares × 0.5 mΩ = 1 mV, small compared with the 20 mV of a thin trace, but not zero. Moving the high-current load next to the input connector removes it from the sensitive region. Ground-variation topics follow in [[q0497]]–[[q0499]].

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Sheet resistance and trace inductance figures here are typical for 1 oz copper and a trace with no nearby plane (about 1 nH/mm). Real values depend on stackup, trace width and plane spacing.
:::

::: applications
- **Audio power amplifiers**: speaker-return current goes straight to the supply capacitors' star, and the preamp ground connects at that same hub.
- **Lab bench supplies and precision instruments**: the analog ground, digital ground and chassis join at one deliberate point.
- **Load-cell and thermocouple front ends**: a reference ground wired as a star to avoid microvolt-level shifts.
- **Motor-driver boards**: the motor's power ground returns to the bulk capacitor, and the logic ground joins at one point near the shunt.
- **Kelvin sensing of shunts and battery cells**: a small star at the sense resistor ([[q0494]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- Low enough frequency that the leg impedance is dominated by resistance (below a few hundred kHz for 100 mm legs).
- Return currents are *known*; the sensitive circuit really is sensitive to reference shifts.
- One extra connection (shield, chassis, test equipment) can break the star; this answer assumes you control all grounds.
- On a solid ground plane, true star behaviour is approximated by placement, and a plane with a hub-style entry is often better than cut traces.
- Another valid answer is that star grounding is "single-point parallel grounding" as defined in EMC textbooks, and that it is often combined with multipoint (a hybrid) in real systems.
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between star grounding and a daisy chain?
In a daisy chain, circuits share segments of one ground wire, so every segment carries the sum of downstream currents and the farthest circuit sees the sum of all upstream drops. In a star each circuit has its own leg, so the shared impedance is only at the hub. Same current, much less cross-coupling.

### Where would you put the star point?
Where the supply returns: the input connector, the main bulk capacitor, or the regulator's ground pin. For mixed-signal ICs, the star can be under the ADC or codec. The point should be a place where the high-current return naturally terminates, so none of it flows through the quiet legs.

### Why isn't star grounding used for high-speed digital?
The legs are long and inductive: about 1 nH/mm, so a 100 mm leg is 100 nH, about 6 Ω at 10 MHz. Fast signals need their return directly under the trace to keep loop area small. A plane does this; a star doesn't.

### What if the star point is also a ground loop?
If a second path forms (a shield, USB ground, a scope probe), the loop picks up magnetic noise. I'd break the loop by isolating one connection, using an isolator or a ferrite or capacitor on the shield, or moving the second tie to the hub itself.

### Scenario: your thermocouple amplifier is noisy only when the heater relay switches. What do you check?
First I'd check whether the relay driver's return shares a trace with the amplifier ground. A relay coil of 100 mA across 50 mΩ gives 5 mV, which is huge against microvolt thermocouple signals. I'd move the relay driver's return to its own leg to the star point, add a flyback diode close to the coil, and verify with a scope and short ground spring across the amplifier's reference pins.
:::
