::: testing
Whether you can compare two regulators along real engineering axes and not just say "buck is efficient, LDO is quiet". The hidden sub-skills are: knowing *why* an LDO's efficiency is $V_{out}/V_{in}$ and a buck's is not; being able to quantify heat; knowing what a switcher costs you (ripple, EMI, inductor, layout, control-loop design); and choosing between them for a specific rail from the numbers, which is what real design reviews look like.
:::

::: why
This is the single most common regulator decision on a board. Pick a buck where an LDO would do and you spend board area, EMI-debug time and money for nothing; pick an LDO where a buck was needed and the part cooks or the battery dies. Interviewers use it to see if you reason from the physics (series pass dissipation versus inductor energy transfer) instead of memorized slogans. It also leads naturally to the two-stage buck-plus-LDO answer ([[q0169]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Series pass dissipation**: an LDO carries the full load current across $V_{in}-V_{out}$, so $P = (V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$. See [[q0277]].
- **Inductor energy transfer**: a buck chops $V_{in}$ and averages it with an LC filter, so ideally no power is dissipated in the conversion. See [[q0173]].
- **Efficiency**: $\eta = P_{out}/P_{in}$, and why it depends on different things for each regulator ([[q0162]]).
- **Ripple and noise**: switching produces ripple at $f_{sw}$ and harmonics; an LDO produces only broadband noise.
- **PSRR**: an LDO's ability to reject input ripple, falling with frequency.
- **Thermal limit**: $T_J = T_A + P\,\theta_{JA}$ caps how much an LDO can dissipate.
:::

::: answer
"The core difference is what happens to the extra voltage. An LDO is basically a variable resistor in series with the load, with feedback to keep the output fixed, so the difference between input and output times the load current is burned as heat. Efficiency is about Vout over Vin. A buck uses a switch and an inductor: it chops the input, the inductor and capacitor average it down to Vout, and the energy isn't dissipated, it's transferred. So efficiency is high, often around 90 percent, almost independent of the voltage drop.

The trade-offs follow from that. For 12 to 3.3 volts at an amp, an LDO throws away 8.7 watts, which is hopeless, while a buck loses a few tenths of a watt. But the LDO wins on simplicity and noise: a couple of capacitors, no switching ripple or EMI, very fast transient response, and tiny layout sensitivity. A buck needs an inductor, careful layout, has output ripple and switching noise, and it's more expensive and bigger.

So my rule: big drop or real current, buck. Small drop, low current, or a noise-sensitive load, LDO. And if I need both efficiency and cleanliness, buck down to just above the target and an LDO after it."
:::

::: technical
**LDO.** Input current is load current plus ground current: $I_{in}=I_{out}+I_q$.
$$P_{loss} = (V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}+V_{in}I_q,\qquad \eta = \frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_q)}\approx\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}.$$
Output is steady without any inductor; output noise is a few to a few tens of microvolts RMS for good parts; PSRR is typically 60–70 dB at low frequency, falling above the loop bandwidth. Dropout sets the minimum $V_{in}-V_{out}$.

**Buck.** Energy is moved by duty cycle: $V_{out}=DV_{in}$ in CCM (ideal). Losses are conduction ($I^2R_{DS(on)}$, inductor DCR), switching ($\tfrac12 V_{in}I\,(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}$ plus gate and $C_{oss}$ losses) and fixed (controller $I_q$). Efficiency is typically 85–95% at mid load and falls at very light load (fixed losses) unless the part enters a pulse-skipping or PFM mode. Output ripple appears at $f_{sw}$:
$$\Delta I_L = \frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})D}{L f_{sw}},\qquad \Delta V_{out}\approx \frac{\Delta I_L}{8 f_{sw}C_{out}}+\Delta I_L\,\mathrm{ESR}.$$

| | LDO | Buck |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | step down only | step down (boost/buck-boost are different topologies) |
| Efficiency | $\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$ | ~85–95%, mostly independent of $V_{in}-V_{out}$ |
| Output noise | very low, no switching ripple | ripple and spikes at $f_{sw}$ and harmonics |
| EMI | negligible | needs layout care, possibly shielding/filters |
| External parts | 1–2 capacitors | inductor, input/output caps, sometimes diode and FB divider, compensation |
| Transient | fast, simple loop | limited by loop bandwidth (typically 1/10 to 1/5 of $f_{sw}$ or lower) |
| Cost / area | small, cheap | larger, higher |
| Heat | high if drop or current is large | low |

Break-even rule of thumb: a buck pays off when the LDO's dissipation exceeds what is easy to remove, often above a few hundred mW to 1 W for small packages, or when battery life matters.
:::

::: short
"An LDO burns the extra voltage as heat in a series pass element, so its efficiency is about Vout over Vin, and it only steps down. A buck switches and uses an inductor to transfer energy, so it's around 90 percent efficient regardless of the voltage drop. The trade is that the LDO is tiny, quiet and fast, while the buck needs an inductor, has ripple and EMI, and needs careful layout. Big drop or high current, buck; small drop or noise-sensitive load, LDO."
:::

::: long
"Start with where the extra voltage goes. In an LDO, a pass transistor sits in series with the load and an error amp adjusts it so the output holds. Every amp of load current flows through that transistor with Vin minus Vout across it, so the dissipation is just that product. Efficiency is about Vout over Vin, so 5 volts to 3.3 volts is 66 percent at best, and 12 to 3.3 is only 27.5 percent. At one amp that's 8.7 watts of heat, which I can't dissipate in a small package.

A buck doesn't do that. A high-side switch connects the input to an inductor for a fraction D of each cycle, and a low-side switch or diode carries the inductor current for the rest. The inductor and output capacitor average that into Vout, which is D times Vin. Energy goes into the inductor's magnetic field and is delivered to the load, not dissipated, so efficiency is set by real losses: switch resistance, switching losses, inductor resistance and the controller's own current. That's why you see 90 percent whether the drop is 1 volt or 9.

What you pay for it: an inductor and more capacitors, so more area and cost; ripple at the switching frequency on the output; switching edges that radiate, so layout and EMI matter; and a control loop that limits transient response.

The LDO is the opposite: two caps, very low noise, fast, no EMI, but all the loss is heat. I decide by looking at the loss. At 12 to 3.3 volts at 1 amp, buck. At 3.6 to 3.3 volts, LDO is 92 percent efficient and quiet. For a clean analog rail from a high input, I'd buck to about 0.3 to 0.5 volts above the target and post-regulate with a low-noise LDO."
:::

::: terms
- **Dropout voltage**: minimum $V_{in}-V_{out}$ at which an LDO still regulates.
- **Quiescent current ($I_q$)**: supply current the regulator draws with no load. See [[q0165]].
- **Duty cycle ($D$)**: fraction of the period the high-side switch is on; $V_{out}\approx DV_{in}$ for a buck.
- **PSRR**: power supply rejection ratio, $20\log(\Delta V_{in}/\Delta V_{out})$ in dB.
- **CCM / DCM**: continuous / discontinuous conduction mode of the inductor current.
- **Switching loss**: energy lost each time the switch transitions, times $f_{sw}$.
- **$\theta_{JA}$**: junction-to-ambient thermal resistance in °C/W.
- **Synchronous rectification**: replacing the buck's diode with a low-side FET to cut conduction loss.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "buck is efficient but noisy, LDO is simple but inefficient."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the mechanism: an LDO dissipates $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$; a buck moves energy through an inductor.
- Backs it with numbers: 12 V to 3.3 V at 1 A gives 8.7 W in an LDO versus roughly 0.3 W in a buck.
- Lists *what the buck costs* (inductor, ripple, EMI, layout, loop compensation) and *what the LDO is great at* (noise, PSRR, speed, area).
- Mentions the crossover: small $V_{in}-V_{out}$ or tiny load currents favour the LDO ([[q0158]]).
- Offers the hybrid (buck then LDO) for clean rails ([[q0169]]) and checks the thermal limit with $\theta_{JA}$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Buck is always better because it's more efficient."** Efficiency is only one axis; on a 3.6 V to 3.3 V, 20 mA rail an LDO may be both more efficient and cleaner ([[q0158]]).
- **"LDO efficiency is Vout over Vin" with no qualification.** True to first order; the real number also includes $V_{in}I_q$, which dominates at light load.
- **Forgetting the thermal limit.** The question is rarely "is the efficiency good?"; it's "can the package survive?". Compute $P\theta_{JA}$.
- **Believing LDOs have no noise.** They have thermal and reference noise; they simply have no switching ripple.
- **Ignoring input-side effects.** A buck draws pulsed input current, requiring good input capacitors, while an LDO's input current equals its load current.
- **Quoting a single buck efficiency.** Efficiency varies with load, $V_{in}$ and $f_{sw}$, and drops at light load without a PFM mode.
:::

::: deep
## Why they behave so differently

Both regulators take $V_{in}$ and make a lower $V_{out}$. The difference is where the unused voltage goes.

**LDO: series resistor under feedback.** The pass element (a PMOS, NMOS or PNP) is controlled by an error amplifier that compares a divided-down $V_{out}$ to a reference. If the load draws more current, the output dips, the error amp opens the pass device more, and $V_{out}$ recovers. The input current *is* the load current (plus a small bias), so by conservation of charge

$$I_{in}\approx I_{out},\qquad P_{in}=V_{in}I_{out},\qquad P_{out}=V_{out}I_{out},\qquad \eta\approx \frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}.$$

Nothing in the circuit can change that: the extra $(V_{in}-V_{out})$ appears across the pass device with the full load current through it.

**Buck: inductor as an energy ferry.** With the high-side switch on, $V_{in}-V_{out}$ is across the inductor, so $i_L$ ramps up. With it off, the inductor current continues through the low-side device and ramps down at slope $-V_{out}/L$. The inductor has no resistance in the ideal model, so averaged over a cycle it dissipates nothing. The input current is pulsed and averages to $I_{in}\approx DI_{out}$, so $P_{in}\approx V_{in}DI_{out}=V_{out}I_{out}$.

## What the buck costs you

Take a typical design: 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, 500 kHz, $L=4.7\ \mu$H, so $D=0.275$.

$$\Delta I_L=\frac{(12-3.3)(0.275)}{4.7\,\mu\mathrm{H}\times 500\,\mathrm{kHz}}=1.02\ \mathrm{A}\ (\text{p-p}),$$

which is about 50% of 2 A; a larger $L$ or higher $f_{sw}$ would lower it. With $C_{out}=22\ \mu$F and ESR = 5 mΩ, output ripple is about $\Delta I_L/(8 f_{sw}C)=11.6$ mV from the capacitance plus $1.02\times0.005=5.1$ mV from ESR. That ripple is at 500 kHz with sharp spikes from the switch-node edges (nanosecond rise times), which couple through parasitic inductance and capacitance. A good LDO's output noise might be 10–50 µV RMS over 10 Hz–100 kHz, 2–3 orders of magnitude cleaner.

## What the LDO costs you (besides heat)

- **Dropout.** $V_{in}$ must exceed $V_{out}$ by $V_{DO}$ at the load current, which is a hard lower limit. See [[q0277]].
- **Quiescent/ground current.** $V_{in}I_q$ is paid even at no load. Tens of µA for modern parts, milliamps for older BJT-type regulators. See [[q0165]].
- **Thermal limit.** Maximum current is set by $T_{J(max)}=T_A+P\theta_{JA}$. For $T_J=125$ °C, $T_A=50$ °C, $\theta_{JA}=60$ °C/W, the allowed dissipation is 1.25 W. At 5 V to 3.3 V, that is $I_{max}=1.25/1.7=0.74$ A.
- **Stability.** Many LDOs need a minimum output capacitance with a certain ESR; others are "capacitor-agnostic".

## Putting it together

Choosing between the two is a calculation, not a preference:

1. Compute $P_{LDO}=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$. If that is more than the package can shed, or more than the battery can afford, it is a buck.
2. If the load is noise-sensitive, ask how much ripple it can take and what PSRR the LDO provides at $f_{sw}$. PSRR falls with frequency; a 70 dB LDO at 1 kHz may only offer 30–40 dB at 500 kHz, so a buck feeding an LDO often needs an extra filter. See [[q0168]].
3. If both matter, buck first to $V_{out}+V_{DO}+$ margin, then LDO ([[q0169]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Processor core from a 12 V bus:** synchronous buck, because the LDO would dissipate multiple watts.
- **3.3 V sensor rail from a 3.6 V Li-ion at low current:** LDO, since efficiency exceeds 90% and there is no switching noise.
- **PLL/VCO or RF transceiver supply:** low-noise LDO, often after a buck.
- **USB-powered 5 V to 3.3 V microcontroller board (about 100 mA):** an LDO is fine (about 0.17 W dissipated) and cheaper than a buck.
- **Battery-powered IoT node:** buck with PFM for the active state, LDO or load switch for always-on low-current rails.
- **ADC reference or precision analog front end:** LDO, because ripple would appear as measurement error.
:::

::: assumptions
- Typical numbers: buck efficiency of 90–93% at mid-load, LDO $I_q$ in the µA range; real values depend on the part and operating point.
- Ideal inductor and capacitor models in the ripple calculation, CCM operation, and ripple from capacitance and ESR added linearly (the phases are not identical, so this is a conservative estimate).
- "Buck" means a step-down switching regulator. If $V_{out}>V_{in}$ is needed, neither is the answer, and a boost or buck-boost is used ([[q0161]]).
- If the load current is extremely small (microamps), the comparison flips toward the LDO because a buck's fixed $I_q$ and switching overhead dominate unless it has a very low-power mode.
- If the interviewer cares about cost, an LDO wins at small power; if they care about thermal or battery, the buck wins above about 1 W of dissipation.
:::

::: followups
### Your LDO is dissipating 2 W in a package that can handle 1 W. What are your options?
Reduce the drop (lower $V_{in}$ with an upstream buck), reduce the current, add copper or a heatsink to lower $\theta_{JA}$, or replace the LDO with a buck. A common fix is a pre-regulator: buck to $V_{out}+0.5$ V, then keep the LDO for cleanliness; the LDO then dissipates only about $0.5I_{out}$.

### Why does a buck have a worse transient response than an LDO?
A buck's loop is limited by its switching frequency and the LC filter; crossover is typically at a tenth or so of $f_{sw}$, and the inductor current cannot change instantly (slew rate $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$). An LDO's pass device responds to the error amp directly with no inductor, so loop bandwidths of hundreds of kHz to MHz are common.

### Which has the better PSRR, and does it matter?
An LDO is designed to reject ripple at its input (60–70 dB at low frequency for good parts), but PSRR falls at high frequency. A buck has no PSRR concept; its output depends on its own control loop. It matters when a buck feeds an LDO: ripple at $f_{sw}$ needs to be attenuated enough by the LDO plus any filter. See [[q0168]].

### What if the load current is just 10 µA?
Then the converter's own quiescent current matters more than conversion losses. A buck with 20 µA $I_q$ would have a terrible efficiency; a nano-power LDO with 1 µA $I_q$ at 3.6 V to 3.3 V would be about 83%. That's why light-load rails on battery products often use an LDO or a buck with PFM and sub-5 µA $I_q$.

### Can an LDO ever be both more efficient and cleaner than a buck?
Yes, whenever $V_{in}-V_{out}$ is small and the load is light, for example 3.6 V to 3.3 V at 10 mA. The LDO's efficiency is $3.3/3.6\approx92\%$ minus quiescent, and the buck has switching losses and $I_q$ on top of the same $P_{out}$. See [[q0158]].
