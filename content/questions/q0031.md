::: basics
### In plain words
Every real capacitor has a little inductance in series with it, called the ESL. A capacitor's reactance falls as frequency rises, while an inductor's reactance rises. So at some frequency they cancel. That is the self-resonant frequency (SRF), $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{esl}C})$. Below it, the part acts as a capacitor. Above it, it acts as an inductor.

### Picture it
Picture a seesaw. One side is the capacitor's reactance, which is negative and shrinks in size as frequency rises. The other side is the inductor's reactance, which is positive and growing. At the SRF, the two sides balance exactly. Before that point, the capacitor side is heavier. After it, the inductor side wins. At the balance point, only a small resistance (the ESR) is left to oppose current.

### Words you need
- **ESL**: the small inductance of the capacitor's internal loop, leads and mounting.
- **ESR**: the small resistance in series with the capacitor.
- **Reactance**: the frequency-dependent opposition of a capacitor or inductor. See [[q0024]].
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: the frequency where capacitive and inductive reactances cancel.
- **Decade**: a tenfold range of frequency.

### The simplest example
A 100 nF ceramic with about 1 nH of total inductance has an SRF of $1/(2\pi\sqrt{10^{-9}\times10^{-7}})$, about 16 MHz. A 1 µF part with the same 1 nH has an SRF of about 5 MHz. The bigger capacitor resonates lower. That is why designers use several values in parallel. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the quality factor of the resonance, the slopes on either side, and how to pick parts from the vendor's impedance curve. See also [[q0030]].
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand self-resonance as a **simple LC resonance inside the part**, can compute it, and know what to do about it. The interviewer is looking for:

1. A definition: the frequency where the capacitive reactance equals the inductive reactance of the ESL, so net reactance is zero.
2. The formula $f_{SRF}=1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{esl}C})$ and a sensible number.
3. The behaviour on each side: capacitive below, resistive (only ESR) at SRF, inductive above.
4. The engineering consequences: a larger capacitor has a *lower* SRF; mounting inductance lowers it further; decoupling a rail needs several values and low-inductance layout.
:::

::: why
The SRF tells you the **useful bandwidth** of a capacitor. A capacitor used above its SRF is an inductor, so a "10 µF decoupling cap" at 50 MHz is not helping. This governs power-delivery design, EMI filtering, RF bypassing and matching network design, and it's also how you measure a part's ESL. A typical senior-level follow-up is "so why do we use several different capacitor values?", which comes straight from this.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitive reactance** ([[q0024]]): $X_C=-1/(\omega C)$, negative, falls with $\omega$.
- **Inductive reactance** ([[q0040]]): $X_L=+\omega L$, positive, rises with $\omega$.
- **Series LC resonance**: opposite-sign reactances cancel at $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$.
- **Capacitor model** ([[q0030]]): ideal $C$ in series with ESR and ESL.
- **Mounting inductance**: pad, via and trace loop inductance adds to ESL.
- **Impedance curve**: V-shaped on log-log axes; minimum at SRF equals ESR.
:::

::: answer
"Every real capacitor has a little series inductance, the ESL, from its internal current loop and its mounting. The capacitor's reactance is negative and falls with frequency, the inductor's is positive and rises, so at some frequency they cancel. That is the self-resonant frequency: f equals one over two pi times the square root of ESL times C.

Below that frequency, the part acts like a capacitor, and its impedance falls. At the SRF the reactances cancel and the impedance is only the ESR, so that's the minimum. Above it, the ESL dominates and the part looks like an inductor, with impedance rising at 20 dB per decade.

For numbers, a 100 nF ceramic in an 0402 with about 1 nH total inductance is around 16 MHz. A 1 µF with 1 nH is about 5 MHz, and a 10 nF with half a nanohenry is over 70 MHz. So a bigger capacitor has a lower SRF, which is why you use several values in parallel, each covering its own range, and why you keep the loop tiny, since mounting inductance adds right into the ESL.

Practically I pick the capacitor whose SRF sits near, or above, the frequency I need to bypass, and I check the vendor's impedance curve."
:::

::: technical
**Equivalent series model:** $Z=R_{esr}+j(\omega L_{esl}-1/\omega C)$.

**Condition:** $\omega_0L_{esl}=1/(\omega_0C)\Rightarrow$
$$f_{SRF}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{esl}C}}$$
At $f_{SRF}$: $|Z|=R_{esr}$ (minimum), phase $0^\circ$. Below: phase $\to-90^\circ$; above: $\to+90^\circ$.

**Slopes:** $-20$ dB/dec below, $+20$ dB/dec above. Away from resonance, $|Z|\approx1/\omega C$ or $\omega L_{esl}$.

**Quality factor of the series resonance:** $Q=\dfrac{1}{R_{esr}}\sqrt{\dfrac{L_{esl}}{C}}$; for 100 nF, 1 nH, 20 mΩ: $Q=\sqrt{10^{-9}/10^{-7}}/0.02=0.1/0.02=5$.

**Measuring ESL:** find the frequency of minimum $|Z|$, then $L_{esl}=1/((2\pi f_0)^2C)$; e.g. a 100 nF part with a minimum at 12 MHz has $L_{esl}=1.76$ nH.

**Table** (computed from the formula):

| C | $L_{esl}$ | SRF |
|---|---|---|
| 10 nF | 0.5 nH | 71 MHz |
| 100 nF | 0.5 nH | 22.5 MHz |
| 100 nF | 1 nH | 15.9 MHz |
| 100 nF | 2 nH | 11.3 MHz |
| 1 µF | 1 nH | 5.0 MHz |
| 10 µF | 2 nH | 1.1 MHz |
| 100 µF electrolytic | 10 nH | 160 kHz |
:::

::: long
"A real capacitor is basically a series RLC: the ideal capacitance, the ESR, and the ESL from the internal current loop and the mounting. The capacitor's reactance is negative and shrinks as frequency goes up; the inductor's is positive and grows. At one frequency they are equal and opposite, and the net reactance is zero. That's the self-resonant frequency, f equals one over two pi times the square root of L times C.

What does that look like? Plot impedance on log axes and you get a V or bathtub. Below the SRF it's falling with minus 20 dB per decade, like an ideal capacitor. At the SRF, the impedance is just the ESR, which is the lowest it gets. Above, it rises at plus 20 dB per decade as ESL dominates, so the capacitor is effectively an inductor.

Numbers: 100 nanofarads with one nanohenry is about 16 megahertz; a 10 nanofarad with half a nanohenry is over 70 megahertz; a 10 microfarad ceramic with 2 nanohenries is about 1.1 megahertz. Note the trend: a bigger C means a lower SRF, even at the same package.

The design consequence is that no single capacitor covers all frequencies. For rail decoupling, I put a bulk capacitor for low frequency, a few microfarads in the middle, and 100 nanofarads or 10 nanofarads close to each pin. I also minimise loop inductance, because 1 nH of via and trace adds to the ESL and drops the SRF. And I check the vendor's impedance plot, and remember that adding two capacitors with different SRFs can make an anti-resonant peak between them."
:::

::: terms
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: frequency where capacitive and parasitic inductive reactances cancel.
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance.
- **ESR**: equivalent series resistance; sets $|Z|$ at the SRF.
- **Series resonance**: LC in series gives minimum impedance at $\omega_0$.
- **Anti-resonance**: parallel resonance between two different capacitors, producing an impedance peak.
- **Mounting inductance**: inductance of the pads, traces and vias that connect the capacitor.
- **Q factor**: $\frac{1}{R}\sqrt{L/C}$ for the series resonance.
- **Bathtub curve**: shape of $|Z|$ vs. frequency for a capacitor.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the frequency above which a capacitor becomes inductive" and gives the formula.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives it from reactance cancellation and describes all three regions, including that $|Z|=$ ESR at the SRF.
- Calculates real examples and notes the trend (larger C ⇒ lower SRF; more ESL ⇒ lower SRF).
- Separates the part's own ESL from mounting inductance, and says the latter often dominates.
- Explains how to measure ESL from the minimum of the impedance curve.
- Uses it for design: multiple values, package choice, via placement, avoiding anti-resonance, and noticing that decoupling is effective around, not just below, the SRF.
- Mentions that the SRF isn't a cliff: the part is still decent for a decade or so around it, and what matters is $|Z|$ vs. target.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Believing the capacitor "stops working" at the SRF.** It has its *lowest* impedance there. It's above the SRF that increasing frequency makes the impedance rise.
- **Assuming a bigger capacitor is better at all frequencies.** Above its SRF a larger capacitor in the same package has the same $\omega L_{esl}$ impedance as a small one.
- **Using the datasheet ESL and ignoring layout.** 1–2 nH of via and trace often doubles the ESL, which lowers the SRF by a factor of √2 (about 30 %).
- **Mixing up series (capacitor) and parallel (inductor) resonance.** The capacitor's SRF is a *minimum* of impedance; the inductor's is a *maximum*.
- **Forgetting that DC bias lowers C**, which raises the SRF slightly but reduces low-frequency performance.
- **Quoting SRF without a context.** A "100 nF SRF = 20 MHz" is only true with ESL ≈ 0.6 nH.
:::

::: deep
## Derivation

The series impedance is
$$Z(j\omega)=R_{esr}+j\left(\omega L_{esl}-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right).$$
The net reactance is zero when $\omega_0^2=1/(L_{esl}C)$, so $f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{esl}C}}$. The magnitude at that point is $R_{esr}$.

Far from resonance the picture simplifies: below $f_0$, $|Z|\approx\frac{1}{\omega C}$; above, $|Z|\approx\omega L_{esl}$. The two straight lines (slopes $-1$ and $+1$ on log-log axes) cross at $f_0$ where both equal $\sqrt{L_{esl}/C}$ (the **characteristic impedance** of the LC, 100 mΩ for 1 nH/100 nF, 10 mΩ for 1 nH/10 µF), and the real curve rounds the corner down to $R_{esr}$.

Figure: SRF falls as $1/\sqrt{C}$ and as $1/\sqrt{L}$. Quadrupling either halves the SRF. This is why the same 100 nF part resonates at 22 MHz with 0.5 nH, 16 MHz with 1 nH, and 11 MHz with 2 nH.

## Mounting inductance often dominates

A bare 0402 MLCC might have 0.4–0.5 nH of internal ESL. Add the pads, a 0.2 mm via-in-pad to the plane (a few hundred pH), and a short trace, and the loop may be 1–2 nH. For the 100 nF part that's:

$$f_{SRF}(0.5\text{ nH})=22.5\text{ MHz}\ \to\ f_{SRF}(2\text{ nH})=11.3\text{ MHz}.$$

Layout tips that reduce it: vias directly at the pads (or in the pad), two vias per pad, thin dielectric between the power and ground planes, and short, wide connections to the pins you're decoupling. Reverse-geometry and multi-terminal capacitors reduce the ESL further ([[q0032]]).

## Using the SRF in design: a decoupling example

Suppose a processor needs low impedance up to 100 MHz. One 10 µF, 1.5 nH part is inductive above 1.3 MHz: at 100 MHz its $|Z|=\omega L=0.94\ \Omega$. A single 100 nF with 1 nH has $|Z|=0.63\ \Omega$ at 100 MHz; ten of them in parallel give 63 mΩ because the inductances parallel. This is why PDNs use many small capacitors: it's the *parallel inductance* that drops, not the individual capacitance.

## How wide is the "useful" band? Target impedance and the characteristic impedance

A decoupling capacitor is useful where $|Z|$ is below some target $Z_t$. Ignoring ESR, $|Z|\le Z_t$ requires $|\omega L - 1/\omega C|\le Z_t$, which gives a band from $f_{lo}\approx 1/(2\pi C Z_t)$ (where the capacitive leg crosses the target) up to $f_{hi}\approx Z_t/(2\pi L)$ (where the inductive leg crosses it). The band exists only if $f_{lo}<f_{hi}$, which simplifies to $Z_t > Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C}$. In words: **a capacitor can only reach a target impedance if its characteristic impedance is below that target.**

Take $Z_t = 50\ \text{m}\Omega$ and the 100 nF, 1 nH, 20 mΩ part of the previous pages. Its $Z_0 = 100\ \text{m}\Omega$ is above the target, so the formulas give no band, and a direct computation shows the part only dips below 50 mΩ over a narrow window from 12.7 to 20.0 MHz around its 15.9 MHz notch. Add parallel copies. $N$ identical parts have $L/N$, $C\cdot N$ and $R_{esr}/N$, so $Z_0$ falls by $N$ and the band widens:

| N identical 100 nF parts | $Z_0$ | Band with $\lvert Z\rvert\le 50$ mΩ (computed) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 mΩ | 12.7 to 20.0 MHz |
| 2 | 50 mΩ | 9.9 to 25.5 MHz |
| 4 | 25 mΩ | 6.6 to 38.3 MHz |
| 10 | 10 mΩ | 3.1 to 82.6 MHz |

This is the quantitative reason for "use many small capacitors": the band widens at both ends, because the inductance goes down by $N$ and the capacitance goes up by $N$. It also shows the limit of the approach. Adding more identical parts moves the upper edge only as fast as $N$, while mounting inductance shared by the group (a common via, a long trace to the plane) does not divide.

## The anti-resonance peak, with numbers

Paralleling two different values creates a peak between their SRFs, because one part is inductive there and the other is capacitive. For a 1 µF part (1.5 nH, 10 mΩ) in parallel with the 100 nF part (1 nH, 20 mΩ), the SRFs are 4.1 MHz and 15.9 MHz. The two reactances cancel where $Z_1+Z_2=0$:

$$f_{ar}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(L_1+L_2)\,\dfrac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2}}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{2.5\ \text{nH}\times 90.9\ \text{nF}}}=10.6\ \text{MHz}$$

Evaluating the network with the ESRs included gives a peak of about 0.25 Ω near 10.5 MHz, three times the roughly 85 mΩ each part presents alone at that frequency. So the pair is worse than either capacitor at that one frequency, even though both are fine at their own notches. The ESR is the only damping in the circuit, which is why very low-ESR ceramics can make these peaks sharper, and why PDN designers sometimes add a part with a deliberately higher ESR, or a bulk polymer capacitor, to fill the gap.

## Extracting ESL from a measurement

Two routes give the same number. At the minimum, $L_{esl}=1/((2\pi f_0)^2C)$, which is exact for the series model but needs $C$ to be known at the test bias. Above the notch, the part is inductive, so a single point works too: if $|Z|=1.3\ \Omega$ at 200 MHz, then $L_{esl}\approx |Z|/\omega=1.3/(2\pi\times2\times10^8)=1.03$ nH. Agreement between the two is a quick check that the lumped model is valid; if they disagree, you are looking at fixture inductance, a second resonance, or a bias-dependent capacitance.

On a VNA with the capacitor shunted from the signal line to ground between two 50 Ω ports, the impedance follows from $S_{21}$:

$$Z = \frac{25\,S_{21}}{1-S_{21}}\ \Omega$$

At the SRF the part presents its ESR. For 20 mΩ, $S_{21}\approx 0.04/(0.04+50)=8\times10^{-4}$, or about −62 dB. That is a deep notch near the noise floor of many instruments, so you need a calibrated setup, low IF bandwidth and short, well-grounded fixture connections. The measured depth is limited by the fixture's own inductance and contact resistance long before it reaches the true ESR. Plan on ESR values from this method being upper bounds.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Large multilayer ceramics can show additional resonances above the main SRF from the way current distributes along the electrodes. How strong they are depends on the construction, so use the vendor's measured impedance curves or S-parameter models rather than assuming a single clean notch.

## What the SRF is not

- It's not the frequency where the part "fails". Regions within a decade of the SRF are often the best-performing part of the part's curve (lowest $|Z|$).
- It's not the same as the SRF of an **inductor**, which is a *parallel* resonance with a *maximum* impedance ([[q0045]], [[q0047]]).
- It doesn't directly consider the nonlinearity (DC bias) or temperature variation of $C$.

## Practical measurement

Use a vector network analyzer with a shunt-through fixture (S21 notch) or an impedance analyzer. A shunt-through S21 shows a notch at SRF; $L_{esl}$ follows from $f_0$ and $C$. De-embed the fixture, since a 5 mm trace to the part can add several nH and spoil the result. Manufacturers also publish simulated impedance curves, typically measured with a specific mounting model.

::: applications
- **Power-delivery decoupling**: choosing 10 nF/100 nF/1 µF/10 µF to cover the bands up to a few hundred MHz.
- **RF bypass and DC blocks**: choose a value whose SRF is at or above the carrier frequency (e.g. 10 pF–100 pF at 1–2 GHz).
- **EMI filter design**: feedthrough or 3-terminal capacitors for SRF in the hundreds of MHz.
- **Measuring parasitics**: finding ESL from the impedance minimum.
- **Switching converters**: ceramic output capacitors with SRF above the switching ringing frequency.
- **Probing/measurement**: scope probe ground-lead resonance is the same LC mechanism ([[q0016]] for a related example).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Series RLC model** with constant $C$, ESR and ESL; real ESR varies with frequency, and $C$ varies with bias/temperature.
- ESL values are typical for chip ceramics (0.4–1 nH) plus mounting; leaded and electrolytic parts are several times larger.
- Single resonance; real parts show secondary resonances (electrode-length modes) at high frequency, usually above a few hundred MHz.
- In an interview, quoting a rounded number (100 nF 0402 ≈ 15–25 MHz) is better than a false-precision figure.
- Equivalent: the SRF can be stated in terms of the characteristic impedance $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$.
:::

::: followups
### What is the SRF of a 1 µF capacitor with 1.5 nH?
$f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{1.5\times10^{-9}\cdot10^{-6}})=4.1$ MHz. Above that, its impedance is $2\pi f\cdot1.5\text{ nH}$: 0.94 Ω at 100 MHz. For noise there you'd want a 100 pF–10 nF part.

### Why does a 10 µF capacitor have a lower SRF than a 100 nF capacitor in the same package?
$f\propto1/\sqrt{LC}$. With the same ESL, 100× more C gives a 10× lower SRF. The 10 µF part also tends to be a larger case with more internal inductance, which lowers it further.

### Why can parallel capacitors of different values make a peak?
Below its SRF the large capacitor is capacitive and above it inductive; the small one is still capacitive at that frequency. The inductance of the large part resonates with the capacitance of the small one, creating a parallel resonance with high impedance, limited by the ESR. Adding ESR (damping) or more intermediate values flattens it ([[q0032]]).

### What if the measured minimum is at 12 MHz for a 100 nF capacitor?
$L_{esl}=1/((2\pi\cdot12\text{ MHz})^2\cdot100\text{ nF})=1.76$ nH. That's more than an 0402's internal ESL, so most of it is mounting and fixture inductance, so check the layout or de-embed.

### Does the SRF change with DC bias?
Yes, indirectly: DC bias lowers $C$ for class-II ceramics, so the SRF rises (by $\sqrt{C_0/C}$, a factor 1.4 for a 50 % loss), but the low-frequency impedance rises too. The ESL doesn't change, so the high-frequency floor stays the same.
:::
