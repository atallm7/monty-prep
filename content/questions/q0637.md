::: basics
### In plain words
A pad that is joined solidly to a big copper plane is hard to desolder, because the plane carries the heat away as fast as your tool delivers it. A thermal relief joins the pad to the plane through a few thin spokes, so less heat drains away and the solder melts more easily. Thin spokes carry less current, so power pads often stay solid.

### Picture it
Imagine filling a bucket with a big hole in the side up to a marked line. Water level stands for temperature, and the marked line is the solder's melting point. A big hole (a plane) means you must pour very fast. A thermal relief shrinks the hole. Preheating the board is like filling the bucket partway before you start.

### Words you need
- **Copper plane**: a large sheet of copper in the board, usually for power or ground.
- **Thermal relief**: a pad joined to a plane by thin spokes. See [[q0538]].
- **Melting point of solder**: the temperature where it turns fully liquid, about 217 °C for common lead-free solder.
- **Thermal resistance**: how many degrees hotter the joint stays than the board for each watt of heat you push in, in K/W (a kelvin step equals a degree Celsius).
- **Preheat**: warming the whole board first, so the tool has less temperature to add.

### The simplest example
Say a solid pad has a thermal resistance of about 41.4 K/W to the rest of the board. From 25 °C you need 192 degrees more to reach 217 °C. That takes 192 ÷ 41.4 ≈ 4.6 W. Add a relief and the resistance rises to about 58 K/W, so you need only about 3.3 W. Preheat to 120 °C and the step is only 97 degrees, so the solid pad needs about 2.3 W. It is a rough model: trust the trends, not the exact watts.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows how the model works, how extra layers make it worse, and bench fixes for solid pads.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand thermal reliefs as a trade-off between **rework and assembly ease** and **electrical and thermal performance**, in the specific context of reworking a part. The interviewer wants the direction of the effect (reliefs make rework easier, solid connections make it harder), the physics (copper planes are heat sinks that pull heat away from the joint you are trying to melt), what to do when you can't have a relief (preheat, bigger tool, more flux, leaded solder trick), and ideally a number showing how much heat it takes. Seeing that the question's wording says power or ground parts "often" have reliefs is a hint that you should discuss where reliefs are *not* appropriate.
:::

::: why
Anyone who has tried to desolder a ground pin on a multilayer board knows the experience: the iron tip is at 350 °C, the solder will not melt, and you are about to cook the pad. Understanding why lets you design around it (reliefs on signal and ground pins) and handle it at the bench (preheat, high heat capacity tools). It also explains why power and thermal pads are the hardest joints to rework, and why a layout decision about copper connection is also a manufacturing and service decision. It is the follow-up to [[q0538]] and a common DFM interview question.
:::

::: concepts
- **Thermal relief**: pad connected to a plane through narrow spokes with a gap, to limit heat flow. See [[q0538]].
- **Heat sinking by planes**: copper's thermal conductivity (about 385 W/m·K) makes planes efficient heat spreaders.
- **Thermal resistance**: $R_\theta = \Delta T / P$; the power needed to hold a joint at temperature is $\Delta T / R_\theta$.
- **Liquidus**: SAC305 about 217 °C; eutectic SnPb 183 °C. See [[q0632]].
- **Preheat**: raising the board's baseline temperature, reducing $\Delta T$ the local tool must supply.
- **Current capacity of spokes**: narrow spokes limit current, so power pads are a different case. See [[q0544]].
- **Tombstoning link**: unequal heat sinking also causes tombstoning ([[q0615]]).
:::

::: answer
"Thermal reliefs mostly *help* reworkability. A pad that's solidly connected to a plane sits on a big heat sink: when I put a hot iron or a hot-air nozzle on it, the plane conducts the heat away as fast as I add it, so the joint struggles to reach solder liquidus, about 217 degrees for lead-free. I end up overheating the area, lifting the pad, or giving up. A thermal relief connects the pad through a few narrow spokes, so the pad behaves much more like an isolated pad and heats up quickly.

The catch is that power and ground components often *need* the low-resistance, low thermal resistance connection, so reliefs on those pads cost current capacity and heat spreading, and designers sometimes turn them off, for example on a power MOSFET's source or an exposed thermal pad. Those joints are then the hardest to rework. At the bench I'd compensate: preheat the board from underneath so the temperature step is smaller, use hot air or a big iron tip with lots of thermal mass, add fresh flux and sometimes a little leaded solder to lower the melting point, and take my time. So reliefs are good for rework, solid connections are good for the circuit, and the layout has to pick per pad."
:::

::: technical
**Physical picture.** The heat needed to hold a joint at $T_{joint}$ equals the heat leaving it through all thermal paths. With the joint at temperature rise $\Delta T = T_{joint} - T_{board}$ above the surrounding board and an equivalent thermal resistance $R_\theta$ from pad to board,

$$P_{required} = \frac{\Delta T}{R_\theta}.$$

A larger or better-connected plane means a lower $R_\theta$ and a higher required power. If the tool cannot deliver $P_{required}$ into the joint (tip-to-joint contact and airflow limit it), the joint won't melt.

**Simple model.** Treat a plane layer of thickness $t$ as a thin sheet spreading heat radially from a pad of radius $r_1$ out to an effective radius $r_2$:

$$R_{spread} = \frac{\ln(r_2/r_1)}{2\pi k t N},$$

with $k \approx 385$ W/m·K (copper), $N$ the number of connected layers (thickness multiplied by copper weight). With $r_1 = 0.75$ mm, $r_2 = 25$ mm, $t = 35$ µm (1 oz), one layer gives $R = \ln(33.3)/(2\pi\cdot385\cdot35\times10^{-6}) = 41.4$ K/W.

A relief with four spokes (0.25 mm wide, 0.3 mm long) on 1 oz copper has per-spoke resistance $L/(kwt) = 89$ K/W, so four in parallel give 22.3 K/W, in series with spreading from the outer radius (about 1.25 mm): total about 57.6 K/W for one layer. A temperature step of 192 K (25 °C to 217 °C) needs about 4.6 W with a solid connection and about 3.3 W with a relief, about 28% less. Four 1 oz layers need 18.5 W solid or 13.3 W relieved; four 2 oz layers need 37 W solid or 27 W relieved. Preheating the board to 120 °C cuts the step to 97 K, about half in every case.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> This is a steady-state, axisymmetric order-of-magnitude model. It ignores convection, the heat capacity of the part and solder, the plane's finite extent, via barrels spreading heat between layers, and tip contact resistance. Treat it as a way to see which factors matter and by roughly how much, not as a prediction of what a particular board will do.

**Current limit of spokes.** Four 0.25 mm × 35 µm spokes (cross-section about 13.6 mil² each) carry roughly 1.2 A each at a 20 °C rise using the IPC-2221 external-layer fit $I = 0.048\,\Delta T^{0.44}A^{0.725}$ (A in mil²), so about 4.7 A total. Most planes are inner layers, where the IPC-2221 internal fit ($k = 0.024$) halves that to about 0.6 A per spoke, about 2.4 A total. That is why high-current pads often get solid copper or wider spokes.
:::

::: long
"Thermal reliefs mostly help rework. The reason is heat sinking. A pad that's tied directly to a plane is attached to a big copper heat spreader. When I try to melt its solder with an iron or a hot-air nozzle, the plane pulls the heat away, so the joint may never reach the liquidus temperature, around 217 degrees for lead-free. I end up cranking the tool, burning flux, and lifting the pad.

A relief connects the pad through a few narrow spokes. They act as a thermal resistance in series with the plane. In a simple model with 1 ounce copper, four 0.25 millimetre spokes add about 22 kelvin per watt, and the plane's own spreading resistance is about 41 for one layer, so the relief reduces the power needed to melt the joint by roughly a quarter to a third. The bigger levers are the number of plane layers and the copper weight: four 2 ounce layers need around 37 watts with solid copper, while a single 1 ounce layer needs under 5. Preheating the board to 120 degrees halves it again, because the step to 217 shrinks from 192 degrees to 97.

The trade-off is electrical: those spokes add resistance and limit current, roughly half an amp to an amp each depending on the layer, so power and ground pads of power ICs, MOSFETs and exposed thermal pads often use solid connections. They're the hardest to rework. At the bench I'd preheat from the bottom, use hot air or a big tip with lots of thermal mass, add flux, and sometimes a bit of leaded solder to lower the melting point locally. In layout I'd use reliefs on ordinary signal and ground pins and solid copper only where the circuit needs it."
:::

::: terms
- **Thermal relief**: spoked connection from pad to plane that restricts heat flow while keeping electrical connection.
- **Spoke**: one of the narrow copper bridges in a relief, typically 0.2 to 0.3 mm wide.
- **Thermal resistance ($R_\theta$)**: temperature rise per watt of heat flow, in K/W.
- **Spreading resistance**: resistance to radial heat flow in a plane from a small source.
- **Liquidus**: temperature above which the solder is fully liquid.
- **Preheat**: bringing the board to an elevated temperature before local heating.
- **Exposed thermal pad**: large pad under a package (QFN, power IC) that is soldered to copper for heat removal.
- **Heat capacity (thermal mass)**: energy to raise a body's temperature by 1 K; larger tips store more heat.
- **IPC-2221**: generic PCB design standard, source of the conductor current vs temperature rise charts.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "reliefs make rework easier because they stop heat escaping".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames it with **$P_{required} = \Delta T / R_\theta$** and shows that the plane's thermal resistance is the bottleneck.
- Gives **numbers** (about 41 K/W for one 1 oz layer, about 5 K/W for four 2 oz layers, spokes about 22 K/W) and concludes which levers matter most: layers, copper weight, preheat.
- States the **electrical cost** and the **IPC-2221 current limit** for spokes, and which pads therefore get solid connections.
- Gives **bench remedies** for solid pads (preheat, hot air or big tip, flux, leaded solder trick).
- Flags that the model is **approximate**.
- Links to **tombstoning** and DFM, where unbalanced heat sinking causes defects.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Believing reliefs remove the problem.** They reduce the heat sinking by perhaps a third in a simple model, but multilayer boards and heavy copper still need preheat.
- **Cranking the iron temperature.** More temperature doesn't solve a heat-flow-rate problem; it damages flux, pad and laminate. Increase heat capacity and preheat instead.
- **Putting reliefs on high-current or thermal pads.** That adds resistance and creates hot spots; use solid copper and plan rework accordingly.
- **Forgetting the barrel.** A through-hole pin contacts planes on every layer through its barrel, so a multilayer board multiplies the heat sinking.
- **Forgetting that "ground" pads on a plane are not special electrically.** A relief on a ground pin has a small effect on a signal, but not on a power-delivery pin.
- **Blaming only the tool.** Often the layout (solid pad on 2 oz inner planes) made rework nearly impossible.
:::

::: deep
## Why the plane is the problem

Copper's thermal conductivity, $k \approx 385$ W/m·K, is about 1000 times FR-4's through-plane value (roughly 0.3 W/m·K), so heat applied to a pad escapes mostly into the copper. A 35 µm thick layer is thin, but a plane is wide, and the heat spreads radially. Each additional plane layer in a through-hole connection is another parallel path, and the plated barrel shorts them all thermally to the pin.

Think of it like an electrical circuit: temperature is voltage, heat flow is current, thermal resistance is resistance. The tool is a current source limited by the contact (tip) or airflow; the plane is a resistor to ambient. If the resistor is small, you need a lot of current for the same voltage.

## Numbers for a single layer

Using $k = 385$ W/m·K, $t = 35$ µm per oz, pad radius $r_1 = 0.75$ mm, effective outer radius $r_2 = 25$ mm:

- Spreading resistance, solid: $R = \dfrac{\ln(25/0.75)}{2\pi\cdot385\cdot35\times10^{-6}} = \dfrac{3.51}{0.0847} = 41.4$ K/W.
- Relief with four spokes of $w = 0.25$ mm, $L = 0.3$ mm: each spoke $R = \dfrac{L}{kwt} = \dfrac{0.3\times10^{-3}}{385\cdot0.25\times10^{-3}\cdot35\times10^{-6}} = 89$ K/W; four in parallel: 22.3 K/W.
- Spreading from the relief ring ($r = 1.25$ mm): $\ln(20)/0.0847 = 35.4$ K/W. Total: 57.6 K/W.

Power to hold a joint 192 K above the board (25 °C → 217 °C): $P = 192/41.4 = 4.6$ W (solid) versus $192/57.6 = 3.3$ W (relief).

## Layers and copper weight

A through-hole barrel connects every plane layer, and the spokes are repeated on each layer, so both the spreading and the spoke resistance divide by $N$ (layers × oz). Required power is then $P = \Delta T\cdot 2\pi k t N / \ln(r_2/r_1)$ for solid, which scales linearly with $N$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Station wattage ratings (for example 60 to 80 W) are not the same as heat delivered into a joint; tip geometry, contact area, solder bridge and airflow limit the rate, and the figures depend on equipment. The model is for relative comparisons.

For lead-free vs SnPb: reaching 183 °C instead of 217 °C needs a step of 158 K instead of 192 K, about 18% less power. That's the logic behind the bench trick of adding a little leaded solder to lower the melting point locally, though mixed alloys have their own reliability caveats.

## What the transient adds

The steady-state figure understates the benefit of a relief at the start of heating. In the first second or two, heat flows into the pad and the copper right around it. A relief isolates the pad's heat capacity from the plane's, so the pad temperature rises faster at first. The effect helps reflow soldering and tombstoning too ([[q0615]]) because it shortens the lag between a plane-connected pad and an isolated one.

## Spokes limit current

Four 0.25 mm spokes on 1 oz copper each have cross-section $A = 0.25\text{ mm}\times 35\,\mu\text{m}$, about 13.6 mil². With IPC-2221's fit for external conductors, $I = 0.048\,\Delta T^{0.44}A^{0.725}$ gives roughly 0.88 A at 10 °C rise, 1.19 A at 20 °C and 1.42 A at 30 °C per spoke, so about 4.7 A total at 20 °C. If the plane is an inner layer, which is the usual case, the internal-conductor constant 0.024 halves these to about 0.6 A per spoke (2.4 A total at 20 °C). A power MOSFET source or an inductor pad that carries 10 A would overheat the spokes and add resistance and a hot spot, so it should be a solid connection or have wide spokes. [[q0544]] discusses current limits of traces and planes.

> [!NOTE]
> IPC-2221 curves are conservative fits for long traces in still air. A short spoke between a pad and a plane is cooled by both ends, so the real limit can be higher, but don't count on it for a power pad.

## What to do when the pad is solid

1. **Preheat from below** (bottom heater or hot plate) to roughly 100 to 150 °C, which halves the needed step.
2. **Use hot air or IR over the whole footprint** for multi-pin parts instead of fighting one joint at a time.
3. **Use a large chisel tip with solder bridging** the tip and joint for through-hole pins, or a desoldering station with a heated nozzle that sucks while it heats.
4. **Add fresh flux** and, if allowed, a small amount of leaded solder to lower the melting point.
5. **Cut the part off** for through-hole parts (clip the body and remove each lead separately) to avoid heating all pins at once.
6. **Don't lift the part until all joints are liquid**, or you'll tear the pad.

## What to do in layout

Use reliefs on ordinary signal and ground pins of connectors and through-hole parts. Use solid or wide-spoke connections for power pads, and add plain vias nearby for current. For exposed thermal pads, give the assembler a stitching and paste plan, and note in the notes that rework needs a preheater. [[q0636]] lists these along with other reworkability measures, and [[q0538]] explains the relief geometry.

- **Power inductors and MOSFETs in power stages**: solid pads and preheated rework.

- **Mixed-signal boards** with solid ground planes: reliefs on single-pin ground connections, solid for return paths where needed.

- **High-copper boards (2 oz and up)**: motor drives and power supplies where rework needs planning.

::: assumptions
The page uses a simplified model (circular plane, steady heat flow, outer radius 25 mm, no convection) to compare options. The 25 mm radius stands in for how far heat spreads in the few seconds of rework; a larger effective radius lowers the thermal resistance logarithmically, so the numbers change modestly but the trends don't. In practice, via arrays, internal planes, neighbouring parts and board temperature all matter. ::: followups
### Why not just use thermal reliefs on every pad?
Because they add resistance and limit current. Four 0.25 mm spokes on 1 oz copper carry about 4.7 A at a 20 °C rise on an outer layer by an IPC-2221 style estimate, and roughly half that on an inner plane, and add electrical and thermal resistance in the path. For power MOSFETs, inductors, and exposed thermal pads, that costs efficiency and creates hot spots. For those I use solid copper or wide spokes and handle rework with preheat. Reliefs are for ordinary signal, ground and shield pins where current is small.

### How much does a relief actually help rework?
In a simple steady-state model with 1 oz copper, four 0.25 mm spokes cut the required heating power by about 28% (4.6 W to 3.3 W for one layer). In practice the transient benefit, where the relief keeps the pad's heat from draining into the plane in the first seconds, is larger. But the number of plane layers and copper weight dominate: four 2 oz layers need about eight times the power of one 1 oz layer even with reliefs, so preheat is what really saves you.

### Why does preheat help so much?
Because the heat needed is proportional to the temperature step $\Delta T$ from the board to liquidus. Raising the whole board to 120 °C reduces the step from 192 K to 97 K, about half. That nearly halves the required power for the same thermal resistance, and it also lowers thermal gradients that crack parts. It's why bottom heaters are standard for BGA and heavy-board rework.

### How would you replace a through-hole connector with its shield soldered to inner planes and no reliefs?
Preheat the board, use a desoldering station with a heated nozzle or a hot-air nozzle on the pins, add flux, and add a little leaded solder to lower the melting point. If it's still stubborn I'd cut the connector body off and remove the leads individually. I'd clean the barrels with a vacuum desoldering tool and inspect the barrels for damage before fitting the new part, and record that the design should have reliefs on the shield pins.

### What if the exposed pad of a QFN is tied to ground with 12 vias?
It's the hardest case. The pad needs a solid connection for thermal and electrical reasons and the vias also pull heat into the inner planes. Rework needs bottom preheat, hot air over the whole package, a controlled profile, and after replacement X-ray to check voiding. I'd also avoid placing the vias directly under the pad unless filled and capped, because solder can wick into them, and I'd confirm any via-in-pad guidance with the assembler.

### Scenario: a production board's ground pin on a through-hole connector consistently has cold joints on the wave solder line. Reliefs are disabled in the layout. What do you change?
Cold joints at that pin mean it's not reaching solder temperature during the wave: the planes sink the heat. I'd enable thermal reliefs on that pin (four spokes of about 0.25 to 0.3 mm), check that the current is small, and ask the assembler about pre-heating or a different wave profile or selective solder. I'd verify with X-ray or cross-sections of hole fill after the change, because IPC-A-610 requires a minimum percentage of hole fill and the cold pin would fail it.
:::
