::: basics
### In plain words
A buck converter turns a higher steady (DC) voltage into a lower one by switching it on and off very fast. Each switch pulls a sharp pulse of current from a small input capacitor next to the chip. The wiring to that capacitor acts like a tiny inductor, and fast current changes make voltage across it. So you put the capacitor right against the chip's input and ground pins.

### Picture it
Think of a water pipe with a valve at the end. Slam the valve shut and the moving water can't stop at once, so you get a pressure spike. The longer the pipe, the bigger the bang. The pulsed current is the water, and long wiring is the long pipe. A small tank right beside the valve absorbs the surge. The picture covers only the spike; real circuits also ring.

### Words you need
- **Buck converter**: a circuit with two switches and a coil that steps a DC voltage down. See [[q0545]].
- **Input capacitor**: a small part that stores a little charge and supplies the current pulses.
- **Inductance**: how strongly a wire or loop resists changes in current. Longer loops have more. 1 nH is a billionth of a henry.
- **Hot loop**: the path the pulses take: capacitor, upper switch, lower switch, back. See [[q0546]].
- **VIN and PGND pins**: the chip's input-voltage pin and its power-ground pin.

### The simplest example
The voltage across an inductance is $V = L \times$ (how fast the current changes). Say the current jumps 5 A in 3 ns. A tight layout has about 3 nH, so the spike is 3 nH × 5 A / 3 ns = **5 V**. With the capacitor far away the loop is 10 nH, and the spike is about **17 V**, a lot on a 12 V input.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page gives the exact placement rules, why the smallest capacitor goes closest, and how voltage reduces a ceramic capacitor's real value.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know the input capacitor is the single most layout-critical part of a buck: it closes the hot loop with the two switches, so its connection to the VIN and PGND pins is a loop-inductance problem. The interviewer wants specifics: closest to the pins, smallest value closest, short wide connections on the same layer, ground return via right at the capacitor, no vias between the capacitor and the pins, power flowing through the capacitor pads, and a bulk capacitor behind it. Mentioning DC-bias derating and the numbers behind the inductance is the extra mile.
:::

::: why
Most EMI and ringing problems on first-spin buck boards come from an input capacitor placed a centimetre away or tied through a via. Fixing it costs a board spin. The rule is simple and the physics are small-numbers physics (nanohenries), which is exactly why people get it wrong: a few millimetres looks like nothing on the screen but is several volts at the switch node.
:::

::: concepts
- **Hot loop**: $C_{in}$ → high-side FET → low-side FET → $C_{in}$ carries pulsed current. See [[q0546]].
- **Loop inductance**: set by loop area, $\approx \mu_0 h\,l/w$ over a plane; $V = L\,di/dt$.
- **ESL and SRF of ceramic capacitors**: a small package has lower ESL and higher self-resonant frequency. See [[q0509]].
- **DC-bias derating** of ceramic capacitors.
- **Input ripple current**: $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$.
- **Input filtering and damping** of the source.
:::

::: answer
"The input capacitor is part of the hot loop with the high-side and low-side switches, so I route it first and treat its connection as an inductance problem. It goes right against the VIN and power-ground pins of the IC, on the same layer, with short, wide copper straight from the capacitor pads to the pins, and no vias between the capacitor and the pins. If the capacitor's ground side goes to the plane, the via goes right at the capacitor's ground pad so the return current drops straight into the plane under the loop.

I use a small ceramic, something like 0.1 to 1 µF in 0402 or 0603, closest to the pins, because it has the lowest ESL and highest self-resonant frequency, and then a larger ceramic, say 10 µF, next to it, and a bulk capacitor further away to handle the lower frequencies and damp the source. If the IC has several VIN/PGND pin pairs, I place a capacitor at each. And I like the input power to flow *through* the capacitor pads before it reaches the IC, so the capacitor isn't on a stub. I check the effective capacitance at the DC bias, because a 10 µF 25 V ceramic can lose half at 12 V.

The reason is that a few nanohenries in this loop gives volts of overshoot and ringing: a 5 A pulse in 3 ns across 3 nH is 5 V."
:::

::: technical
**Why it is critical.** The loop $C_{in}\to Q_H\to Q_L\to C_{in}$ carries current pulses of amplitude $\approx I_{out}$ with $t_r \approx$ 1 to 5 ns. $V_{overshoot}\approx L_{loop}\,di/dt$. For $I = 5$ A, $t_r = 3$ ns: 1 nH → 1.7 V; 3 nH → 5 V; 10 nH → 17 V. Ring frequency $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{loop}C_{oss}})$ (168 MHz at 3 nH, 300 pF).

**Placement rules.**
1. Smallest, lowest-ESL ceramic (0.1 to 1 µF, 0402/0603) *at* the VIN and PGND pins, within 1 to 2 mm.
2. Short, wide copper on the same layer from capacitor pads to pins; no vias on the path from capacitor to pins; if the pins are far apart, rotate/choose the package so that both capacitor pads are adjacent to the pins.
3. If the IC's ground is via-connected to the plane, put the ground via(s) right at the capacitor pad and the IC's ground pin; the plane directly below (dielectric $h \le 0.2$ mm) gives the return a low-inductance path ($\approx\mu_0 h\,l/w$).
4. Next capacitor (e.g. 4.7 to 22 µF ceramic) beside the first; bulk electrolytic or polymer farther away with some ESR to damp the source.
5. Multiple VIN pin pairs: a capacitor per pair, symmetric.
6. Feed the input through the capacitor pads.

**Sizing.** Input ceramic ripple: $\Delta V_{in}\approx I_{out}D(1-D)/(C_{in}f_{sw})$: for 5 A, $D = 0.275$, $C = 10$ µF, 500 kHz: 0.2 V. RMS current: $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)} = 2.2$ A. Check ceramic capacitance at DC bias (can fall 50% or more for X5R at high bias in small packages) and temperature, and its ripple-current rating.

**Layout of the source side.** A long input cable has inductance (about 1 µH/m); with $C_{in}$ it forms an LC with a negative-resistance interaction against the converter (buck input impedance is negative incremental). Add damping (electrolytic or an RC damper) in the bulk stage.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The loop-inductance targets (a few nH) are typical of good layouts; the achievable value depends on package, stack-up and pin arrangement. Capacitor derating varies strongly by part, voltage rating and case size; use the manufacturer's DC-bias curves.
:::

::: long
"The input capacitor is part of the hot loop: the input cap, the high-side switch, the low-side switch, back to the cap. That loop carries pulses of the load current with nanosecond edges, so I route it first and treat it as an inductance problem. Three nanohenries with 5 amps in 3 nanoseconds is 5 volts of overshoot, and the loop is also the biggest radiator on the board.

So the capacitor goes right against the VIN and power-ground pins, on the same layer, with short, wide copper from its pads to the pins. No vias between the capacitor and the pins, because each via adds a nanohenry. If the ground goes to a plane, I put the via at the capacitor's ground pad, and a solid plane right below, one thin dielectric away, so the return current flows under the loop and the loop area is tiny.

I use a small ceramic first, 0.1 to 1 microfarad in 0402 or 0603. A small package has lower ESL and a higher resonant frequency, so it handles the fast edge. Next to it goes a bigger ceramic, maybe 10 microfarads, for the ripple current and the switching-frequency content, and a bulk capacitor farther back that also damps the source. If the IC has two VIN and ground pin pairs, I place a capacitor at each pair. And I like the power to enter through the capacitor pads rather than T-ing off.

Last, I check the real capacitance at the bias voltage: a 10 microfarad, 25 volt ceramic can lose half its value at 12 volts, and ripple current rating matters, since RMS is about 2.2 amps here."
:::

::: terms
- **Hot loop**: $C_{in}$ plus the high-side and low-side switches.
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance of a capacitor, including its mounting.
- **SRF**: self-resonant frequency, $1/(2\pi\sqrt{ESL\cdot C})$.
- **DC-bias derating**: loss of ceramic capacitance under applied voltage.
- **Flow-through (Kelvin) routing**: supplying the IC through the capacitor pads.
- **Bulk capacitor**: large electrolytic/polymer for low-frequency charge and damping.
- **Input ripple current**: the RMS current in $C_{in}$, $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$.
- **Via-in-pad**: a via placed inside a pad; reduces inductance but needs fill/cap for assembly.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "put it as close as possible to the VIN and ground pins."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the loop and quantifies the overshoot (3 nH, 5 A, 3 ns = 5 V).
- Specifies same layer, no vias on the capacitor-to-pin path, ground via at the capacitor, plane beneath.
- Uses a multi-capacitor hierarchy (small closest) and gives the reason (ESL, SRF).
- Mentions flow-through routing and capacitors per pin pair.
- Checks DC-bias derating, ripple current rating and source damping.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the bulk electrolytic closest to the pins.** Its ESL is tens of nH; it cannot supply the edge. The small ceramic goes first.
- **Connecting through a via to a plane on the way to the pins.** The vias add inductance in the hot loop.
- **Placing the capacitor on the other side of the board "to save space".** The vias make it a long loop.
- **Rounding up on "close" to a centimetre.** Millimetres matter; see [[q0549]].
- **Ignoring DC-bias derating.** The effective capacitance may be half, and the ripple voltage double.
- **Skipping damping on a long input cable.** A low-ESR ceramic plus cable inductance can ring or interact with the converter's negative input resistance.
:::

::: deep
## What the capacitor actually does

When the high-side FET turns on, the inductor current $I_L$ (5 A here) must flow from the input. In the instant of switching the source cannot supply it, because the cable has inductance and the source cannot respond in nanoseconds. $C_{in}$ supplies the current locally. When $Q_H$ turns off, the current diverts to $Q_L$ and the capacitor recharges from the source more slowly. The result is a pulse train in $C_{in}$ with RMS value $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$: 2.2 A for the 12 V → 3.3 V, 5 A example. The current that flows in the edge, between $C_{in}$ and the two FETs, is the loop that matters.

## Loop inductance numbers

To a first approximation, loop inductance scales with loop area. For a loop over a close plane, the partial inductance per unit length of the signal conductor is $\mu_0 h/w$; so a 5 mm run, 1 mm wide, at $h = 0.1$ mm adds about $0.126\times5 = 0.63$ nH. Without a close plane (or with a via), the figure is closer to 5 nH for 5 mm. A ceramic 0402 has an ESL of about 0.4 to 0.7 nH, a 0805 closer to 0.8 to 1.2 nH, and an electrolytic tens of nH.

The effect on the switch node:

| $L_{loop}$ | $L\,di/dt$ at 5 A / 3 ns | Ring frequency (300 pF) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 nH | 1.7 V | 291 MHz |
| 3 nH | 5 V | 168 MHz |
| 10 nH | 17 V | 92 MHz |

(Peak overshoot is usually below $L\,di/dt$ because of damping and finite edge shape, but the trend is real: lower $L$ means lower overshoot and higher ring frequency, which is easier to filter.) See [[q0549]] for the far-away case.

## Capacitor selection and hierarchy

**High-frequency capacitor (closest).** 0.1 to 1 µF, 0402 or 0603, X7R; ESL under 1 nH gives an SRF above 30 MHz for 1 µF and above 50 MHz for 0.1 µF, and a low impedance at the ring frequency. This is the part you want within 1 to 2 mm.

**Ripple capacitor.** The ripple voltage on a ceramic input capacitor is approximately $\Delta V \approx I_{out}D(1-D)/(C\,f_{sw})$: $5\times0.199/(10\ \mu\text{F}\times500\ \text{kHz}) = 0.2$ V for 10 µF. Check the capacitance at bias: a 25 V X5R 10 µF 1206 may deliver 5 to 7 µF at 12 V. Paralleling several smaller ceramics is better for ESL and ripple current.

**Bulk capacitor.** A polymer or electrolytic, 22 to 100 µF, at the input connector side, with some ESR. Its job is the low-frequency charge and damping of the cable-inductance resonance. Without ESR the cable's $L$ and the ceramics' $C$ form a high-Q resonator, and the converter's negative input resistance ($-V_{in}^2/P_{in}$) can destabilise it. (For 12 V and 18 W input the magnitude is 8 Ω; the damping resistance should be of the order of $\sqrt{L/C}$.)

## Routing details

1. **Same layer, flow-through.** Bring the input power to the capacitor pad first, then from the pad to the VIN pin. The capacitor is then *in* the path rather than a stub, so the high-frequency current takes the capacitor path.
2. **Pad-to-pin copper.** Short and as wide as the pad; avoid thin necks.
3. **Ground.** Short, wide copper to the PGND pin. If the PGND pin connects to a plane through vias at the IC's exposed pad, add a via at the capacitor ground pad as well, so the return current flows in the plane under the loop. Place the via adjacent to the pad, not at the end of a stub. Via-in-pad (filled and capped) is better if the process allows.
4. **Multiple pairs.** If there are two VIN/PGND pin pairs on opposite sides, one capacitor per pair.
5. **Do not share.** Do not use the same ground vias for the hot loop and for noisy ground returns of other circuits.
6. **Check the plane.** Layer 2 must be solid under the loop and the whole IC; a slot there nullifies the benefit.
7. **Probe point.** Leave room for a probe at the capacitor to measure the ripple.

## Validate

Look at the SW node with a short ground spring: overshoot and ringing should be well within the FET's rating. Look at $C_{in}$'s ripple voltage at the capacitor pads, and at conducted and radiated emissions. If the ring is too large, first shrink the loop; next add a second, smaller ceramic in parallel; finally slow the edge.

::: applications
- **Integrated buck/boost regulators** with VIN and PGND on adjacent pins (QFN) where the capacitor sits within a millimetre.
- **Controller plus external FET designs**, where the input capacitor connects to the drain of $Q_H$ and the source of $Q_L$ in a tight triangle.
- **Multiphase converters**, where each phase has its own local input capacitor.
- **USB-C power input stages** at 5 to 20 V with a small ceramic at the regulator and a bulk capacitor near the connector.
- **Battery chargers and motor drivers**, using the same hot-loop principle for the bridge's bulk capacitors.
:::

::: assumptions
The answer assumes a synchronous buck in a QFN/DFN-style package with VIN and PGND pins near each other, on a four-layer board with a ground plane just below the top layer. If the pins are far apart in the package, the datasheet layout (often showing the capacitor wrapping around or placed on the opposite side) overrides the generic rule, and some packages include integrated input capacitors. On a two-layer board the return path is a bottom pour with vias, and the loop is larger. The inductance numbers are order-of-magnitude. For very high currents, several capacitors and a bus-bar-like geometry are used. For boost converters, the same logic applies to the output capacitor (the hot loop is output capacitor, switch and diode).
:::

::: followups
### Why the smallest capacitor closest, not the largest?
Because the closest capacitor must respond to the fastest edge, and a small package capacitor has the lowest ESL and highest self-resonant frequency. A bigger capacitor has more ESL (and more mounting loop), so it is better placed farther away where it handles lower-frequency ripple. The hierarchy follows frequency: small and close for ns edges, larger for the switching-frequency ripple, bulk for low frequency.

### What do you do if the VIN and PGND pins are on opposite sides of the package?
I check the datasheet: some parts split the pins deliberately and show capacitors on both sides, or a capacitor under the part on the bottom layer. If not, I place one capacitor at each pin with the ground via and plane connecting the two grounds, or use a bottom-layer capacitor directly under the IC with vias in the pads. The goal remains a minimal loop area.

### How does a via in the input capacitor path hurt?
Each via adds roughly 0.5 to 1.3 nH depending on length and diameter, and a via sends current to another layer, enlarging the loop area unless the plane is directly adjacent. At 5 A in 3 ns, 1 nH extra gives 1.7 V more overshoot. If vias are unavoidable, use several in parallel and put the plane on layer 2.

### Why does a buck's input look like a negative resistance?
At constant output power, a higher input voltage means lower input current: $I_{in}=P/(\eta V_{in})$, so $dI/dV = -P/V^2$, a negative incremental resistance of magnitude $V^2/P$ (8 Ω for 12 V and 18 W). Together with the source's inductance and the input capacitor's low ESR this can oscillate; adding damping (an electrolytic with ESR or an RC damper) fixes it.

### How would you verify the input capacitor layout on the bench?
Measure SW with a short ground spring and check overshoot and ringing against the FET's rating, measure ripple at the capacitor, do a near-field scan for the hot loop, and compare against a version with the capacitor moved. A good layout shows a clean edge with a ring that dies in a few cycles.
:::
