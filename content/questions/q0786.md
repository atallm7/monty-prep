::: testing
Two things: whether you know **when** a pull is *necessary* (floating inputs, open-drain outputs, buttons, enables, gate drives, boot straps) and whether you can **size** one as a two-sided trade-off with numbers: small enough for speed, noise immunity and leakage; large enough for static current, driver sink capability and power. This is the embedded view of [[q0020]], so tie your answer to GPIO pins.

Hidden sub-skills: logic thresholds, leakage error as $I\cdot R$, RC rise time, $V_{OL}/I_{OL}$ floor, and recognising internal pulls as weak.
:::

::: why
A missing pull-up is the cause of a good fraction of "works on the bench, flaky in the field" bugs, and an oversized or undersized one causes the rest: an I2C bus too slow to work at 400 kHz, a button that drains a coin cell, an input that reads wrong when the PCB is humid. Interviewers want to hear a method, not "10k, always": list the constraints, compute both bounds, choose a standard value in between, and verify with the datasheet and a scope.
:::

::: concepts
- **Floating input**: a CMOS input with nothing driving it picks up noise, can read randomly, and can burn crowbar current. See [[q0783]].
- **Pull-up / pull-down**: resistor to $V_{DD}$ or ground giving an undriven node a default level. See [[q0020]].
- **Open-drain needs a pull-up**: the only source of the high level. See [[q0785]].
- **Leakage error**: input leakage $I_{leak}$ across $R$ gives a voltage error $I_{leak}R$.
- **RC rise time**: $t_r\approx2.2RC$ (10-90%) or $0.8473RC$ (30-70%).
- **Sink limit**: when the line is pulled to the opposite level, the driver must absorb $V/R$ within $I_{OL}$. See [[q0008]] for the divider view.
:::

::: answer
"A pull-up or pull-down is necessary whenever a node could otherwise be undriven. Floating inputs: a button that is open some of the time, an input from a chip that might be tri-stated, an unused pin. Open-drain outputs: the pull-up is what makes the line go high at all, so I2C, shared interrupts and resets. And things that must have a safe default while the MCU boots: a MOSFET gate needs a pull-down so the load stays off, an enable or chip-select needs a pull-up so the device stays idle, boot-strap pins need a defined level at reset.

Sizing is a trade-off between two limits. Too big a resistor and it's weak: leakage current times R shifts the level, the RC with the pin and trace capacitance slows the edge, and it's easy for noise to overpower. Too small and it wastes current every time the line is pulled the other way, 3.3 volts over 1 kΩ is 3.3 mA, and a driver may not be able to sink that while staying below its low-level voltage.

So I compute both bounds. For a button, 10 kΩ: 0.33 mA only while pressed, a clean edge, and leakage effects negligible. For I2C at 400 kHz with 100 pF, the maximum is about 3.5 kΩ for rise time, and the minimum is about 1 kΩ from a 3 mA sink at 0.4 V, so 2.2 to 3.3 kΩ. For a MOSFET gate pull-down, 100 kΩ is typical, with the gate capacitance setting turn-off time."
:::

::: technical
**When a pull is needed.**
1. Inputs that can be undriven: switches, jumpers, connectors, tri-state buses, unused pins.
2. Open-drain/open-collector outputs ([[q0785]]).
3. Safe-default control lines: MOSFET gates (pull-down), active-low enables and chip selects (pull-up), reset (pull-up), boot straps.
4. Establishing idle state of a bus: UART RX idle high, RS-485 bias.

**Sizing constraints.**

| Constraint | Gives | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| Leakage / level error | upper bound | $I_{leak}R<\text{margin}$; e.g. margin $=V_{IL,\max}-V_{pull-down\ level}$ |
| Rise time | upper bound | $R<\dfrac{t_r}{0.8473\,C}$ (30-70%) or $\dfrac{t_r}{2.2C}$ (10-90%) |
| Noise immunity | upper bound | lower $R$ = stiffer node |
| Static current | lower bound | $I=V/R$ whenever the node is driven opposite |
| Driver sink/source limit | lower bound | $R>\dfrac{V-V_{OL}}{I_{OL}}$ |
| Power budget | lower bound | $V^2/R\cdot\text{duty}$ |

**Worked numbers (3.3 V).**
- Button, 10 kΩ: 0.33 mA while pressed. With a 1% press duty, average 3.3 uA. 100 kΩ: 33 uA when pressed.
- Leakage: 1 uA across 100 kΩ gives 0.1 V; across 1 MΩ gives 1 V, which is too much against $V_{IL}\approx0.3V_{DD}=0.99$ V. Contamination can raise leakage to 10 uA or more.
- I2C (100 pF): $R_{max}=1000\text{ ns}/(0.8473\times100\text{ pF})=11.8$ kΩ at 100 kHz; $300\text{ ns}\to3.5$ kΩ at 400 kHz. $R_{min}=(3.3-0.4)/3\text{ mA}=967$ Ω.
- MOSFET gate pull-down: 100 kΩ with $C_{gs}=1$ nF gives $\tau=100\ \mu s$ turn-off, 10 kΩ gives 10 us. Choose by how quickly the load must turn off versus pin loading.
- Internal pulls: typically 20 to 50 kΩ (vendor-specific), with large spread: fine for buttons, too weak for fast buses.
- Fighting an internal pull: a 40 kΩ internal pull-up against a 10 kΩ external pull-down gives $3.3\times10/50=0.66$ V, which reads as a low.
:::

::: long
"They're necessary wherever a node could end up undriven, because a high-impedance CMOS input with nothing attached floats, picks up noise, and can read anything. The typical cases are mechanical switches and jumpers, inputs from chips that tri-state, unused pins, and open-drain outputs like I2C, shared interrupts and resets, where the pull-up is the only thing that ever makes the line high. Another family is safe defaults while the MCU is booting and its pins are high-Z: a pull-down on a MOSFET gate so the motor stays off, a pull-up on an active-low chip select or enable, and boot-strap pins at a defined level.

Sizing is two-sided. The upper bound comes from weakness. Input leakage times R is an error voltage, so a megohm and a microamp is a full volt. The resistor and the node capacitance make an RC, and the edge takes about 2.2 RC, which for 100 kΩ and 20 pF is 4.4 microseconds. And a weak node picks up noise easily. The lower bound comes from cost and capability. Whenever the node is pulled the opposite way, V over R flows: 3.3 mA for 1 kΩ, and the driver has to sink that and still stay below its low-level voltage. On a battery, a button with a 10 k pull-up pressed one percent of the time costs about 3 microamps average, which is fine, but a permanently low line costs the full 0.33 mA.

Putting that together: 10 kΩ is a sensible default for buttons, resets and enables. For I2C I'd compute from rise time and sink current, for 400 kHz and 100 pF that's between about 1 kΩ and 3.5 kΩ, so 2.2 kΩ or 3.3 kΩ. For a gate pull-down 100 kΩ works because the pin only needs to override it, and the gate capacitance gives a turn-off time constant of 100 microseconds with 1 nF, which I'd check against the application.

Internal pulls are convenient, 20 to 50 kΩ with a wide spread, so I use them for buttons but not for fast buses."
:::

::: terms
- **Pull-up / pull-down**: resistor to $V_{DD}$ / ground setting the default level.
- **Floating (high-Z) node**: node with no defined voltage.
- **Weak vs strong pull**: high vs low resistance; a stronger pull gives a stiffer node, more current.
- **Leakage current ($I_{leak}$)**: small current into or out of an input pin (nA to uA).
- **$V_{IL}$, $V_{IH}$**: thresholds for logic 0 and 1.
- **$I_{OL}$, $V_{OL}$**: sink current spec and the output-low voltage it guarantees.
- **RC rise time**: edge time set by pull-up and node capacitance.
- **Boot strap**: pin whose level at reset selects an option.
- **Bias (idle) resistor**: pull defining the idle state of a bus (UART RX, RS-485).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use a pull-up on buttons, 10 kΩ".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Lists the classes of need (undriven inputs, open-drain outputs, safe boot-time defaults) with an example of each.
- States the two-sided sizing: upper bound from leakage, RC and noise, lower bound from static current and $I_{OL}$.
- Computes at least one bound with numbers (I2C 3.5 kΩ max at 400 kHz and 100 pF; 0.97 kΩ min; 10 kΩ button costing 0.33 mA when pressed).
- Knows internal pulls are weak and variable, and when to use an external one.
- Mentions verifying on a scope and with worst-case leakage, and choosing a standard E-series value.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Always 10k."** Fine for buttons, wrong for I2C at speed, or for a 1 nF gate that must turn off in microseconds.
- **Omitting the pull-down on a MOSFET gate.** At reset the pin is high-Z and the gate floats, so the load may turn on.
- **Using the internal pull for a fast bus.** 40 kΩ with 100 pF is a 4 us time constant, so the 30-70% rise alone takes 3.4 us.
- **Ignoring leakage and contamination.** Megohm-scale pulls on a humid or flux-contaminated board produce wrong levels.
- **Forgetting that a pull-up burns power when the line is low** on a battery product.
- **Using both a pull-up and pull-down on the same node** without computing the divider they form.
- **Pull-up to the wrong rail** (e.g. 5 V on a 3.3 V pin), pushing current through the clamp.
:::

::: deep
## Why a floating input is a problem

A CMOS input is a pair of MOSFET gates with picofarads of capacitance and nanoamps of leakage. If nothing drives it, its voltage is set by whatever charge or coupling reaches it: 50/60 Hz mains from nearby wiring, edges from adjacent traces, your hand. In the middle of the supply range both input transistors partly conduct, so the buffer draws extra supply current, and its output can chatter and trigger spurious interrupts. A pull resistor gives the node a single stable state toward which any stray charge decays: with $R=10$ kΩ and $C=20$ pF the node returns to its default in about $4.6\tau=0.9$ us.

## The sizing triangle: speed, current, level

Think of the resistor as sitting between three demands.

**Speed.** The edge toward the pulled level is an RC charge. For the 10-90% time $t_r=2.2RC$:

Figure: Rise time is proportional to R. For a 100 pF I2C-like bus, staying under 1 us means R below about 4.5 kΩ (10-90%). For a slow button node at 20 pF, even 1 MΩ gives 44 us, irrelevant for human timescales but too slow for a UART RX or an SPI line.

**Current.** When the node is pulled the opposite way (switch closed, driver low), the pull-up passes $V/R$:

| $R$ | Current at 3.3 V | Power |
|---|---|---|
| 1 kΩ | 3.3 mA | 10.9 mW |
| 4.7 kΩ | 0.70 mA | 2.3 mW |
| 10 kΩ | 0.33 mA | 1.09 mW |
| 100 kΩ | 33 uA | 0.11 mW |
| 1 MΩ | 3.3 uA | 11 uW |

The driver (or switch) must sink that current at a low enough voltage: $R>(V-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$. A button does not care. A GPIO configured as open-drain with $I_{OL}=3$ mA needs at least 967 Ω.

**Level.** The pull-up and any load form a divider ([[q0008]]). Input leakage $I_{leak}$ drops $I_{leak}R$ across the resistor. With $V_{IH}\approx0.7V_{DD}=2.31$ V, a pull-up must keep the node above that. With 10 uA leakage (dirty board) a 100 kΩ pull-up loses 1 V and leaves 2.3 V: right on the threshold. This is why high-value pulls on dirty boards cause "works in the lab, fails in the field".

## Four worked cases

**1. Push-button into an MCU pin.** Internal pull-up (about 40 kΩ) is fine; mechanical switch bounces for a millisecond or so and the edge speed is irrelevant. With an external 10 kΩ: 0.33 mA pressed. If the product is a battery-powered remote and the button is held rarely, average current is negligible; but if the contact can stay closed (a door switch) use 100 kΩ to 1 MΩ and check leakage, or switch the pull on only during sampling.

**2. I2C bus at 400 kHz, 3.3 V, $C_b=100$ pF.** Rise-time spec (30-70%) 300 ns: $R_{max}=300/(0.8473\times0.1)=3.54$ kΩ. Sink: $R_{min}=967\ \Omega$. Choose 2.2 kΩ (standard value). Current when low: 1.5 mA. The simulation below checks the pull-up.

**4. Open-drain interrupt shared by 4 sensors.** Each sensor's leakage when released is, say, 1 uA max: four of them are 4 uA. With a 10 kΩ pull-up the drop is 40 mV: no problem. Rising edge for 50 pF: $2.2\times10\text{k}\times50\text{ pF}=1.1$ us, fine for an event line. Low-state current 0.33 mA only while some sensor asserts.

## Internal vs external pulls

Internal pulls save a part and a pad but are weak and imprecise: typically 20 to 100 kΩ, wide spread, and sometimes with a quirk (some parts disable them in deep sleep). Use internal pulls for buttons and idle states, external ones for buses, anything that must be defined before firmware runs (internal pulls are off at reset on many parts, see [[q0787]]), and anything where the value matters.

## Verification

Measure the node with a scope at the pin, not the source, during a rising edge, and with the worst-case load connected (all devices on the bus, probe capacitance subtracted). Check the datasheet's worst-case $I_{leak}$ at temperature, and the resistor tolerance (1% or 5%). For production, simulate or calculate at the corners: $R_{max}$ with +5% tolerance for rise time, $R_{min}$ with -5% for sink current.

::: assumptions
- 3.3 V CMOS logic, $V_{IL}\approx0.3V_{DD}$, $V_{IH}\approx0.7V_{DD}$; capacitances of 20 to 100 pF; leakage of 1 uA typical and 10 uA worst-case on a contaminated board (illustrative).
- I2C numbers use the 30-70% rise time convention: 1000 ns standard mode, 300 ns fast mode, $0.8473RC$. Check the specification (NXP UM10204) for exact limits.
- Internal pull values (20 to 100 kΩ) vary widely by vendor and part.
- The answer changes for very low power (you may gate the pull) and for very fast lines (use active termination or push-pull instead of a pull-up).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Internal pull-up/pull-down resistances and their state at reset and in sleep modes are vendor-specific; do not assume 40 kΩ or that the pull is on at power-up. Check the reference manual and the datasheet's electrical table.
:::

::: followups
### Why not just use the smallest pull-up that works?
Because it burns $V/R$ whenever the line is pulled the other way and may exceed the driver's sink limit, and it costs battery life. 1 kΩ at 3.3 V is 3.3 mA continuous for a line that is low most of the time, and it needs a driver that can pull down within $V_{OL}$. Choose the largest value that meets rise time and level.

### What if I use an internal pull-up on I2C?
Usually too weak: 40 kΩ with 100 pF gives $0.8473\times40\text{k}\times100\text{ pF}=3.4$ us, much slower than the 1 us standard-mode limit, so it only works at very low speeds with very short buses. Use external resistors.

### Where does a pull-down go on a MOSFET gate and why?
From gate to source (ground). It keeps the gate at 0 V when the driver is high-Z (reset, bootloader, firmware crash), so the FET stays off. Typical 10 to 100 kΩ. Too small loads the driver; too large lengthens turn-off.

### Both a pull-up and pull-down on a line: when does that make sense?
For defining the idle state on a bus that may be undriven (RS-485 fail-safe biasing) or setting a mid-level threshold. They form a divider: $V=V_{DD}R_{pd}/(R_{pu}+R_{pd})$. Otherwise it wastes current.

### Harder: your pull-up is correct on the bench, but a customer's board reads a stuck low. Why?
Possibly leakage from contamination or humidity across a high-value resistor, a failed or shorted neighbour, a sensor's open-drain output stuck low, or the pull-up connected to a rail that is off during sleep. Measure the node voltage and current, and compare with the datasheet's worst-case leakage; lower the pull-up value or add conformal coating.
:::
