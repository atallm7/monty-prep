::: testing
Whether you can sketch the diode's current–voltage characteristic **from memory, with the right shape and the right numbers on it**, and explain each part. The interviewer looks for:

- correct **axes and polarity** ($V_D$ = anode minus cathode on x, $I_D$ anode→cathode on y),
- an **exponential** forward branch with a knee around 0.6–0.7 V for silicon (not a straight line, not a step),
- a **tiny, nearly flat** reverse branch (leakage),
- a sharp **breakdown** at $-V_{BR}$,
- awareness that the axes are not to scale: forward mA, reverse nA, breakdown tens of volts.

Strong candidates also draw the simplified models (ideal, constant-drop, piecewise-linear) and say when each is used.
:::

::: why
The I–V curve is how you reason about every diode circuit. Load-line analysis, picking a Zener, sizing an LED resistor, knowing why a diode string is a poor reference, and understanding $V_{BE}$ in a BJT all start from this curve. In interviews it's a quick check of whether you think of components as *curves* rather than labels. Later questions in this section ([[q0075]] operating regions, [[q0076]] which region am I in?) and the BJT and MOSFET I–V questions ([[q0084]], [[q0095]]) build directly on being comfortable with it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Shockley equation**: $I_D = I_S(e^{V_D/nV_T}-1)$ gives the forward and reverse-leakage branches. See [[q0072]].
- **Thermal voltage**: $V_T = kT/q \approx 25.9$ mV at 300 K, which sets the ~60 mV/decade slope.
- **Breakdown**: at $V_D = -V_{BR}$, avalanche or Zener tunnelling makes reverse current rise sharply.
- **Operating regions**: forward bias, reverse bias, breakdown. See [[q0075]].
- **Small-signal (dynamic) resistance**: $r_d = nV_T/I_D$, the local slope of the curve at an operating point.
- **Load line**: the straight line from the external circuit whose intersection with the curve is the operating point. See [[q0076]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd put diode voltage, anode minus cathode, on the x-axis and diode current on the y-axis.

In the first quadrant, forward bias, the curve is basically flat near zero and then bends up exponentially. For silicon the knee is around 0.6 to 0.7 volts, and above that the current shoots up almost vertically. I'd mention it's exponential: roughly ten times more current for every 60 millivolts.

To the left of the origin, reverse bias, the current is a tiny negative value, basically flat. That's the leakage, nanoamps for a small silicon diode, so on any normal scale it looks like it's sitting on the axis.

Then at some large negative voltage, the breakdown voltage, the curve drops almost vertically down. For a regular rectifier that's maybe 100 to 1000 volts and you never want to go there; for a Zener it's designed to be a specific voltage like 5.1 volts.

I'd also point out the axes aren't to scale: forward current is milliamps, reverse leakage is nanoamps, and breakdown is tens of volts away. And for hand analysis I'd usually replace the curve with a model: an ideal switch, or a switch plus a 0.7 volt source, or that plus a small series resistance."
:::

::: technical
**Axes and sign convention.** x: $V_D = V_{anode} - V_{cathode}$. y: $I_D$, positive when flowing anode → cathode inside the diode.

**Forward branch** ($V_D > 0$): $I_D = I_S(e^{V_D/nV_T}-1) \approx I_S e^{V_D/nV_T}$ for $V_D \gg V_T$. With $I_S = 10^{-14}$ A, $n = 1$: 52 nA at 0.4 V, 2.5 µA at 0.5 V, 1 mA at 0.655 V, 10 mA at 0.714 V. At high currents, series resistance $R_S$ bends the curve to a straight line of slope $1/R_S$.

**Slope.** $\frac{dV_D}{d\log_{10}I_D} = nV_T\ln 10 \approx 59.5n$ mV/decade. Local resistance $r_d = nV_T/I_D$: 25.9 Ω at 1 mA, 2.6 Ω at 10 mA.

**Reverse branch** ($-V_{BR} < V_D < 0$): for $V_D \ll -V_T$ (a few × 26 mV), $I_D \to -I_S$ plus generation current. Real small-signal silicon diodes: pA to tens of nA at 25 °C, rising with $V_R$ and roughly doubling per ~10 °C.

**Breakdown** ($V_D \le -V_{BR}$): current rises steeply. Zener diodes specify $V_Z$ at a test current $I_{ZT}$ and a dynamic impedance $Z_{ZT}$ (a few ohms to tens of ohms). Rectifiers specify a minimum $V_{RRM}$ instead.

**Temperature.** At constant current the forward branch shifts left by ≈2 mV/°C. Reverse leakage increases exponentially. Avalanche breakdown voltage increases with temperature; Zener (tunnelling) breakdown decreases.

**Hand-analysis models** (piecewise-linear approximations of the same curve):

| Model | Forward | Reverse | Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ideal | short for $I>0$ | open | large voltages, logic of the circuit |
| Constant drop | $V_D = V_{D0}$ (0.7 V) | open | most DC hand calculations |
| PWL | $V_D = V_{D0} + I_Dr_D$ | open | when currents vary widely |
| Small-signal | $r_d = nV_T/I_{DQ}$ | — | AC analysis about a bias point |

Different devices shift the knee: Schottky ~0.2–0.45 V, germanium ~0.2–0.3 V, red LED ~1.8–2.2 V, blue/white LED ~2.8–3.3 V. See [[q0078]].
:::

:::

::: long
"Let me set up the axes first, because the sign convention matters. On the x-axis I put the diode voltage, anode minus cathode. On the y-axis, the diode current, positive when it flows from anode to cathode.

Starting at the origin and going right, that's forward bias. The current is tiny at first, microamps at half a volt, and then it bends upward exponentially. For silicon the knee is around 0.6 to 0.7 volts, and past that the curve is almost vertical. The equation behind it is the Shockley equation, $I = I_S(e^{V/nV_T} - 1)$. A useful way to quote it is that the current goes up ten times for every 60 millivolts. At really high current the curve straightens out, because the diode's series resistance starts to dominate.

Going left from the origin is reverse bias. The current settles almost immediately at a small negative value, the saturation or leakage current. That's picoamps to nanoamps for a small silicon diode at room temperature, so on the same scale as the forward branch it's indistinguishable from zero.

Further left, at the breakdown voltage, the curve turns sharply downward and the current increases a lot with very little extra voltage. For a rectifier like a 1N4007 that's at least 1000 volts; for a Zener diode it's deliberately set to something like 3.3 or 5.1 volts, and that's where the Zener operates.

I'd mention two more things. First, I'd label that the axes aren't to scale, since forward current is in milliamps and reverse leakage in nanoamps. Second, temperature: heating the diode shifts the forward curve left by about two millivolts per degree, and the leakage gets much bigger.

For hand analysis, I'd replace the curve with a model: an ideal diode that's a short forward and open reverse, a constant 0.7 volt drop, or 0.7 volts plus a small series resistance. Which one I'd use depends on how accurate I need to be."
:::

::: terms
- **I–V characteristic**: the plot of a device's current versus the voltage across it.
- **Knee / cut-in / turn-on voltage**: the informal voltage where forward current becomes significant, ~0.6–0.7 V for silicon at mA levels. Not a sharp threshold.
- **Saturation current $I_S$**: the asymptotic reverse current and the scale factor of the forward exponential.
- **Breakdown voltage $V_{BR}$ (or $V_Z$)**: the reverse voltage where current rises sharply.
- **Dynamic resistance $r_d$**: slope $dV/dI = nV_T/I_D$ at an operating point.
- **Series resistance $R_S$**: bulk and contact resistance that straightens the curve at high current.
- **Piecewise-linear (PWL) model**: approximating the curve with straight-line segments for hand analysis.
- **Ideality factor $n$**: stretches the exponential; 1 for ideal diffusion current, up to ~2 with recombination.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws a hockey stick: flat, then vertical at 0.7 V, and maybe forgets the reverse side.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Labels axes with polarity ($V_D$ anode-to-cathode, $I_D$ anode→cathode) and marks the knee at ~0.6–0.7 V.
- Draws all three regions: exponential forward, flat leakage, sharp breakdown at $-V_{BR}$, and gives the scale of each (mA, nA, tens/hundreds of V).
- Says "exponential, ~60 mV per decade", and ideally sketches the forward branch on a **log current axis**, where it becomes a straight line.
- Shows how temperature moves the curve (−2 mV/°C forward, leakage up).
- Draws the simplified models and says which one they'd use for which problem.
- Notes that Schottky, LED and Zener curves differ in knee voltage and breakdown voltage.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing the forward branch as a straight line from the origin.** That's a resistor. The diode curve is exponential with a pronounced knee.
- **Drawing a perfect step at 0.7 V.** That's the constant-drop *model*, not the real curve. Say so if you draw it.
- **Putting breakdown in the first quadrant** or drawing it as current going up. Breakdown is in the third quadrant: negative voltage, negative (reverse) current.
- **Drawing reverse leakage as a noticeable slope.** On a scale where forward current is visible, leakage is invisible. If you want to show it, use a separate scale and label it.
- **Forgetting the sign convention.** Without labelling which way is anode to cathode, "positive" and "negative" voltage are meaningless.
- **Claiming the reverse current is exactly $-I_S$.** In real silicon diodes generation current in the depletion region usually dominates and leakage increases somewhat with reverse voltage.
:::

## The forward branch to scale

The Shockley equation, $I_D = I_S(e^{V_D/nV_T}-1)$, generates the forward branch. With $I_S = 10^{-14}$ A and $n = 1$ the numbers are:

| $V_D$ | $I_D$ |
|---|---|
| 0.40 V | 52 nA |
| 0.50 V | 2.5 µA |
| 0.655 V | 1 mA |
| 0.714 V | 10 mA |

On a linear axis scaled to tens of mA, everything below ~0.55 V looks like zero. That's the origin of the "0.7 V turn-on" idea. Change the y-axis scale to µA and the knee moves left: the knee is an artefact of the scale you plot on, not a physical threshold.

## The forward branch on a log axis

Plot $\log I_D$ against $V_D$ and the exponential becomes a straight line with slope $1/(nV_T\ln 10)$, i.e. one decade per 59.5 mV for $n = 1$. Real diodes deviate at both ends: at low current, recombination in the depletion region gives $n \to 2$ (a shallower slope); at high current, series resistance flattens the line.

## The reverse branch and breakdown

For $V_D$ more negative than a few $V_T$, $e^{V_D/V_T}\to 0$ and $I_D \to -I_S$. In a real silicon diode the measured leakage is larger than $I_S$ because of thermal generation inside the depletion region, which grows with the depletion width, and so with reverse voltage.

At breakdown the current rises steeply. Here's a full curve for a 5.1 V Zener on a linear scale, where the breakdown branch is modelled as a dynamic resistance of 10 Ω beyond $-V_Z$:

```plot
 "xspans":[{"from":-7,"to":-5.1,"label":"breakdown","color":"red"},{"from":-5.1,"to":0,"label":"reverse (leakage ≈ 0)","color":"blue"},{"from":0,"to":1.2,"label":"forward","color":"green"}]}
```
Figure: A Zener is just a diode with a low, well-controlled breakdown voltage. Forward it behaves like any silicon diode (~0.7 V). Between 0 and −5.1 V it blocks. Beyond −5.1 V the current rises steeply with a slope set by the dynamic resistance r_z (10 Ω here), which is why a Zener holds its voltage roughly constant. Breakdown is drawn as a straight line for simplicity.

> [!NOTE]
> The breakdown knee is softer for low-voltage Zeners (tunnelling) and sharper for higher-voltage avalanche diodes. Low-voltage Zeners (≈3 V) have a noticeably rounded knee and leak more before breakdown, which is why datasheets specify $V_Z$ at a test current.

## Simplified models: replacing the curve with lines

For hand analysis you rarely carry the exponential. Each model is a piecewise-linear approximation of the curve:

```plot

```
Figure: The ideal model ignores the drop entirely (fine when the other voltages are ≫ 0.7 V). The constant-drop model is good to ±0.1 V across several decades of current. The piecewise-linear model adds a slope (series resistance) and tracks the real curve better at higher currents.

**Worked example.** 5 V through 1 kΩ into a silicon diode ($I_S = 10^{-14}$ A):

- Ideal model: $I = 5/1000 = 5.0$ mA.
- Constant-drop model: $I = (5 - 0.7)/1000 = 4.30$ mA.
- Exact (iterating the Shockley equation): $I = 4.31$ mA, $V_D = 0.692$ V.

The constant-drop model is within 0.2% here, which is why it's the default. The ideal model is 16% off because 0.7 V isn't negligible compared with 5 V.

## Small-signal view: the slope

The slope of the curve at an operating point is the diode's **dynamic conductance**: $g_d = dI_D/dV_D = I_D/(nV_T)$, so $r_d = nV_T/I_D$. At 1 mA, $r_d \approx 26$ Ω; at 10 mA, $\approx 2.6$ Ω. That's why a forward-biased diode looks like a small resistor to AC signals, and it's the same idea as $r_e = V_T/I_E$ in a BJT ([[q0085]]).

::: applications
- **LED current-limiting resistor**: read $V_F$ at your target current off the LED's I–V curve (e.g. 2.0 V at 10 mA) and size $R = (V_S - V_F)/I$.
- **Zener shunt regulator**: operate on the steep breakdown branch so that changes in current barely change voltage, $\Delta V \approx \Delta I \cdot r_z$.
- **Rectifier heat calculation**: read $V_F$ at the actual current (1N4007 is ~1 V at 1 A, not 0.7 V) to estimate $P = V_FI$.
- **Temperature sensing**: on-die sensors bias a junction at two currents and use the difference $\Delta V = nV_T\ln(I_2/I_1)$, which is proportional to absolute temperature.
- **Log amplifiers**: the exponential I–V of a diode or BJT in an op-amp feedback path gives an output proportional to $\ln(V_{in})$.
:::

::: assumptions
- Silicon PN diode at room temperature (300 K) unless stated. Numbers use illustrative $I_S = 10^{-14}$ A, $n = 1$; real diodes have $I_S$ from ~1 fA (small signal) to µA (large rectifiers, Schottkys) and $n$ from 1 to 2.
- DC (static) characteristic. Under fast switching, capacitance and reverse recovery make the dynamic behaviour depart from this curve.
- The breakdown branch is drawn for a Zener-like diode. Rectifiers break down at much higher voltage, and operating there is usually destructive.
- If the interviewer wants an "ideal diode" curve, the right drawing is the L-shape: vertical line along the positive I axis, horizontal line along the negative V axis. Say that's the ideal model, then offer the real one.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the curve look like it has a threshold at 0.7 V if it's really exponential?
Because we usually plot current on a linear scale in milliamps. The current at 0.5 V (µA) is a thousand times smaller than at 0.7 V (mA), so it's invisible. Rescale the y-axis to µA and the "knee" appears around 0.5 V instead. On a log axis there's no knee at all, just a straight line at ~60 mV/decade.

### Draw the I–V curve of an ideal diode.
An L-shape: the vertical positive current axis (any positive current at $V_D = 0$, i.e. a short) and the negative voltage axis (zero current for any $V_D < 0$, i.e. an open). It has no forward drop, no leakage and no breakdown. It's useful for understanding circuit topology (rectifiers, clamps) when voltages are much larger than 0.7 V. See [[q0079]] for building something close to it.

### How does temperature change the curve?
At constant current the forward branch moves left by about 2 mV/°C. At 125 °C a silicon diode that read 0.65 V at 1 mA at 25 °C reads about 0.45 V. Reverse leakage rises exponentially, roughly doubling every ~10 °C. Avalanche breakdown voltage rises with temperature; Zener-tunnelling breakdown (below ~5 V) falls; around 5–6 V the two effects roughly cancel.

### What is the dynamic resistance at 2 mA and why does it matter?
$r_d = nV_T/I_D = 25.85\text{ mV}/2\text{ mA} \approx 13$ Ω (for $n = 1$). It's the slope of the curve at that point, i.e. how much the diode voltage changes for a small change in current. It sets the small-signal behaviour: a diode biased at 2 mA attenuates or couples AC signals as if it were a 13 Ω resistor. It's also why a diode is a poor voltage reference compared with a Zener at its operating point plus active regulation.

### What does the I–V curve of a forward-biased diode look like at very high current, say 10 A in a small diode?
The exponential gives way to a straight line set by the series resistance $R_S$: $V_D \approx V_{junction} + I R_S$. For a 1 A rectifier, $V_F$ at its rated 1 A is ~1 V rather than 0.75 V; pushing 10 A would add several more volts and the self-heating (which itself shifts the curve) quickly destroys it. Power diodes are characterised with a $V_F$ vs $I_F$ curve at multiple temperatures in the datasheet.
:::

::: rubric
- Labels axes with polarity: $V_D$ (anode − cathode) and $I_D$ (anode → cathode) :: Without a sign convention, forward vs reverse is ambiguous.

- Shows breakdown at $-V_{BR}$ in the third quadrant :: Many candidates forget it or draw it in the wrong quadrant.
