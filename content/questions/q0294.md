::: testing
Whether you can measure two simple-sounding quantities *correctly* in both situations: with bench instruments (DMM, scope, source-measure unit) and inside a product (ADC, sense resistor, fuel-gauge IC). The interviewer is listening for: voltage is measured **in parallel**, current **in series**; a shunt turns current into a small voltage; burden voltage and instrument loading change what you measure; offset errors dominate small shunt voltages; and how to bring a 4.2 V cell into a 3.3 V ADC. Mentioning Kelvin sensing, the difference between open-circuit and loaded voltage, and integration for coulomb counting separates real experience from textbook recall.
:::

::: why
Every battery-powered product measures its battery: for fuel gauging, low-battery warnings, protection and charge control. Every battery test measures it too. A wrong measurement gives wrong state of charge, false protection trips or missed faults. The classic traps (a DMM current range that browns out the device, a 10 mΩ shunt with a 100 µV amplifier offset, a battery divider that drains the cell in storage) are things interviewers ask because they only happen to people who have built it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Voltmeter in parallel, ammeter in series**: a voltmeter should have very high impedance; an ammeter should have very low. See [[q0001]].
- **Voltage divider**: scales a battery voltage into an ADC's range ([[q0008]]).
- **Shunt (sense) resistor**: $V_{sh}=I R_{sh}$ converts current into a small voltage; a differential amplifier scales it.
- **Burden voltage**: the voltage an ammeter or shunt drops in the current path.
- **Kelvin (4-wire) sensing**: separate force and sense paths so lead resistance does not corrupt the reading.
- **Coulomb counting**: integrating measured current over time to track charge ([[q0308]]).
- **ADC resolution and reference**: LSB size sets measurement granularity ([[q0293]] for how that feeds a sizing check).
:::

::: answer
"For voltage I measure in parallel across the terminals. On the bench that's a DMM, which has about ten megohms input impedance so it barely loads the cell. In a product I use an ADC, but the battery's 4.2 V is above a 3.3 V ADC range, so I put a resistor divider in front, something like two equal resistors, with a small capacitor on the ADC pin. I'd make the divider high enough to avoid draining the battery, but low enough that the ADC can sample it, and I might gate it with a switch so it doesn't leak when the device is off.

For current, it has to go in series. On the bench I can break the circuit and put a DMM in current mode, but the meter has a burden voltage, so it can drop the supply to the device. In a product I put a small shunt resistor in the current path, say ten milliohms, measure the voltage across it with a differential amplifier or a current-sense IC, and convert to amps using Ohm's law. Two amps through ten milliohms is twenty millivolts, so offset and noise matter, and I'd sense with a Kelvin connection. For fuel gauging I integrate that current over time. And I always remember that voltage under load is lower than the open-circuit voltage, so I'd read it in the right context."
:::

::: technical
**Voltage.**

- Bench: DMM across the terminals (input impedance typically 10 MΩ, so it draws about 0.4 µA from a 4.2 V cell; through 100 mΩ of internal resistance that is a 40 nV error, negligible). Use a scope for transients and ripple with a short ground spring.
- On-board: divider $V_{adc}=V_{bat}\dfrac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}$. For $R_1=R_2=100$ kΩ the divider ratio is 0.5, so 4.2 V maps to 2.1 V; quiescent drain $4.2/200\text{k}=21$ µA. With a 12-bit, 3.3 V reference ADC, 1 LSB = 0.806 mV at the pin = 1.61 mV at the battery. Source impedance $R_1\parallel R_2=50$ kΩ is high for many SAR ADCs; add a capacitor (about 100 nF) at the pin or a buffer. Account for resistor tolerance (1% resistors give about 1% worst-case ratio error; more if unmatched) and ADC reference error.
- Fuel-gauge and charger ICs measure cell voltage directly with a built-in ADC (often 1 mV resolution class), no divider required.

**Current.**

- Series ammeter: DMM mA/A range. Its burden voltage can be hundreds of mV at full scale (instrument-dependent; check the manual). Never connect a DMM in current mode directly across a battery: that is a short circuit through the shunt and fuse.
- Shunt: $I=V_{sh}/R_{sh}$. Choose $R_{sh}$ so that $V_{sh}$ at full-scale current is large enough to beat offset, but small enough to waste little power ($P=I^2R_{sh}$) and drop little burden voltage.
- Sense amplifier: op-amp or current-sense amplifier with gain $G$; $V_{adc}=G\,I\,R_{sh}$. For $R_{sh}=10$ mΩ, $G=50$, $I=2$ A: $V_{adc}=1.0$ V (about 1.6 mA per ADC LSB for a 12-bit, 3.3 V converter).
- Amplifier offset $V_{os}$ produces a current error $\Delta I=V_{os}/R_{sh}$: 100 µV offset on 10 mΩ is a 10 mA error, which matters for sleep-current measurement.
- High-side vs low-side: low-side shunts are easy but lift the load ground; high-side shunts keep ground but need a common-mode-tolerant sense amp.
- Hall-effect and other isolated sensors are used above tens of amps or where isolation is needed.
:::

::: short
"Voltage goes in parallel, current in series. On the bench: a DMM across the terminals for voltage, and for current either break the circuit with the DMM in current mode, minding its burden voltage, or use a shunt and measure the voltage across it. In a product: a resistor divider into the ADC for voltage, since 4.2 V exceeds a 3.3 V ADC, and a milliohm shunt with a differential amplifier for current. Use Kelvin sensing, watch amplifier offset since 100 µV on 10 mΩ is 10 mA, and integrate current for coulomb counting."
:::

::: long
"Let me do bench first, then in-product.

On the bench, voltage is easy: the DMM goes in parallel across the cell terminals. It has around 10 megohms input impedance so it doesn't load the battery. I'd measure at the terminals, not at the end of a long wire, because under load wire resistance subtracts from what I read. For current the meter goes in series, so I break the circuit and insert it in the mA or A range. The catch is the burden voltage: the meter is effectively a small resistor with a fuse, and it can drop enough voltage that the device under test misbehaves. For better accuracy and dynamic range I'd use a shunt resistor and measure the voltage across it with a scope or a source-measure unit.

Inside a product, voltage goes through a resistor divider into the ADC because the battery runs up to 4.2 V and the ADC is 3.3 V. Say two 100 kilohm resistors halve it, and a 100 nanofarad capacitor at the pin supplies the sample-and-hold charge. That drains about 21 microamps, so for a product that sits on a shelf I'd put a switch in the divider or use a higher-value divider with a buffer.

For current, I'd put a small sense resistor in series with the battery, maybe 10 milliohms. Two amps gives 20 millivolts, which is small, so I'd amplify it with a current-sense amplifier. That's where the details live: amplifier offset, because 100 microvolts across 10 milliohms looks like a 10 milliamp error; common-mode range if it's on the high side; and routing the sense traces as a Kelvin connection straight to the resistor pads. A fuel-gauge IC does all that and integrates current to count coulombs."
:::

::: terms
- **Shunt resistor**: a low-value precision resistor placed in series to convert current to voltage.
- **Burden voltage**: voltage dropped by the measuring instrument or shunt in the current path.
- **Kelvin sensing**: separate current-carrying and voltage-sensing connections to a resistor so lead resistance is excluded.
- **Common-mode voltage**: the average voltage of the sense inputs relative to ground (large for high-side sensing).
- **Input offset voltage ($V_{os}$)**: amplifier error that appears as a fixed current error across a shunt.
- **LSB**: least significant bit; ADC step size $V_{ref}/2^N$.
- **Coulomb counting**: integrating current over time to track charge.
- **Open-circuit vs loaded voltage**: terminal voltage without and with current; differ by $IR_{int}$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "voltmeter in parallel, ammeter in series".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives both a bench method and an in-product method, with a worked example for each (ADC divider numbers; 10 mΩ shunt with gain).
- Names burden voltage and instrument loading, and warns never to put a DMM in current mode across a battery.
- Points out that **offset / resolution sets the minimum measurable current**: 100 µV on 10 mΩ = 10 mA.
- Mentions Kelvin routing and the high-side vs low-side shunt trade-off.
- Says what you measure *for*: loaded voltage for protection, rested voltage for SoC, integrated current for coulomb counting, temperature alongside.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting an ammeter in parallel.** A DMM in current mode has almost zero resistance, so across a battery it is a short circuit that can blow the fuse or hurt the cell. Always in series.
- **Ignoring burden voltage.** A 200 mV drop in the meter can reset a device running from 3.3 V. Use a shunt and a scope or SMU instead.
- **Reading battery voltage through the wrong path.** Measuring after a protection MOSFET, connector or long cable includes their drops. Measure at the cell terminals (or Kelvin-sense there).
- **A divider that never turns off.** Two 100 kΩ resistors drain 21 µA forever, about 184 mAh in a year (21 µA × 8760 h). Gate it, or use a high-value divider and buffer.
- **Forgetting ADC source impedance.** A 50 kΩ divider into a SAR ADC needs a capacitor or buffer, otherwise readings are wrong and depend on sample rate.
- **Using a shunt with no thought for offset or temperature.** Small offset errors and shunt tempco become large percentage errors at low currents.
:::

::: deep
## Voltage: what you are really measuring

A cell is approximately $V_{oc}$ in series with $R_{int}$. A voltmeter reads $V_{term}=V_{oc}-I R_{int}$. At rest with no current that is the open-circuit voltage, which relates to state of charge ([[q0308]]). Under load it is lower, which is what the protection circuit and your load see. So the question "what is the battery voltage?" always needs "at what current?".

A DMM has about 10 MΩ input resistance. For a cell whose source resistance is 100 mΩ, the loading error is about $0.1/10^7 = 10^{-8}$, utterly negligible. For high-impedance sources (e.g. a divider node of 1 MΩ) the same meter creates a 9% error, so the loading logic of [[q0008]] applies.

Figure: A battery-voltage divider into an ADC. The 0.5 ratio brings 4.2 V to 2.1 V. The capacitor supplies the ADC's sampling charge so the 50 kΩ source impedance does not distort the reading. The divider draws 4.2 V / 200 kΩ = 21 µA continuously.

**ADC arithmetic.** With a 3.3 V, 12-bit ADC the LSB at the pin is $3.3/4096=0.806$ mV; through the 0.5 divider it is 1.61 mV at the battery. That is plenty for a protection threshold but marginal for a 5%-per-10-mV fuel gauge on a flat LFP curve ([[q0300]]). The accuracy is set by the reference, divider tolerance and gain/offset, not only the LSB: 0.1% resistors and a good reference are what you pay for to get ±5 mV.

## Current: from amps to a voltage you can measure

A shunt turns current into voltage, $V_{sh}=IR_{sh}$. Picking $R_{sh}$ is a three-way trade-off:

| Larger $R_{sh}$ | Smaller $R_{sh}$ |
|---|---|
| bigger signal vs offset and noise | smaller burden voltage |
| more power loss $I^2R$ | less heating |
| more resolution at low current | harder to read small currents |

For $R_{sh}=10$ mΩ and $I=2$ A: $V_{sh}=20$ mV and dissipation $I^2R=40$ mW, with a 20 mV burden. At 10 A the numbers are 100 mV, 1 W, so the resistor needs a suitable power rating (and low tempco).

Figure: Low-side current measurement. The load current flows through $R_{sh}$ to ground, producing $V_{sh} = I R_{sh}$ above ground. A current-sense amplifier (here gain 50) reads the voltage across the shunt differentially and scales it to the ADC range: 2 A gives 20 mV × 50 = 1.0 V. In layout, route both sense inputs from the shunt's own pads (Kelvin) rather than from bulk ground copper, so ground-plane drops are not read as current.

> [!WARNING]
> A low-side shunt lifts the load's ground by $IR_{sh}$ (20 mV here) and breaks the common ground between battery and load. That's fine for many designs, but it can upset communication with other grounded equipment. A high-side shunt keeps ground solid but needs a sense amp rated for the battery's common-mode voltage.

## Offset: why small currents are hard

The amplifier offset $V_{os}$ is indistinguishable from signal, so it makes a current error $\Delta I=V_{os}/R_{sh}$.

Figure: Error current equals offset divided by shunt resistance. A 100 µV offset on a 10 mΩ shunt is a 10 mA error; zero-drift amplifiers with a few µV of offset cut this to about 0.5 mA. To measure µA sleep currents you need either a large shunt or a range-switching setup.

This is also why coulomb counters drift: a constant 1 mA offset error accumulates 24 mAh per day, which is 1.2% of a 2 Ah cell every day ($0.001\times24/2$). See [[q0308]] and the simulator below.

## Bench techniques

- **Ammeter in series**: only for modest currents. Check the burden voltage in the manual; many handheld DMMs drop a few hundred millivolts on the mA range and have a fused path (the fuse resistance adds). Start on the highest range.
- **Scope plus shunt or current probe**: for fast pulses (a radio burst lasting 5 ms) a DMM average is meaningless. A 1 Ω shunt in the supply path and a scope trace shows the whole waveform; account for the voltage drop on the supply (use a low value).
- **Source-measure unit / battery emulator**: acts as the supply and the meter, with selectable ranges and 4-wire sensing.
- **Kelvin voltage measurement** at the cell terminals when the cell is under load.

## Practical checks

Measure voltage at rest and under load and compute $R_{int}=\Delta V/\Delta I$ ([[q0291]]); measure temperature alongside (an NTC divider is another voltage measurement); and check protection thresholds ([[q0295]]) by measuring the cell voltage when the protection triggers.
:::

::: applications
- **Fuel-gauge ICs**: measure cell voltage and the voltage across a milliohm sense resistor, integrate current for coulomb counting.
- **Smartphone battery monitoring**: ADC channel for cell voltage plus charger IC current/voltage telemetry.
- **Bench power profiling**: source-measure unit or shunt plus scope to capture a wireless sensor's sleep and burst currents.
- **Battery-powered IoT devices**: switched divider so the battery voltage monitor does not drain the cell in storage.
- **E-bike/robot BMS**: high-side shunt or Hall sensor for pack current, per-cell voltage ICs for each series cell.
:::

::: assumptions
The answer assumes a single Li-ion-type cell (0-4.2 V) and moderate currents (milliamps to several amps). For packs, per-cell voltage measurement needs dedicated multi-cell monitors with level-shifting; for high currents (>50 A) Hall or fluxgate sensors replace shunts. For primary cells the same principles apply but the voltage range and divider values change.

Instrument figures such as DMM input impedance (about 10 MΩ) and burden voltage vary between meters; check the specific model's manual. The offset and ADC numbers are examples; real amplifier offset also drifts with temperature.
:::

::: followups
### Why not just put a DMM in current mode across the battery to measure its current?
Because in current mode the DMM is almost a short circuit (a fuse and a small shunt). Across a 4.2 V cell with 50 mΩ internal resistance that could pull tens of amps, blowing the meter's fuse or heating the cell. A current measurement must be in series with the load so the load limits the current.

### How would you measure a 50 µA sleep current and a 200 mA active burst with the same setup?
A single shunt cannot do both without compromise: 50 µA through 10 mΩ is 0.5 µV, below any offset. Use a switched-range approach (a large shunt for the sleep range and a small one for the burst), a source-measure unit with auto-ranging, or a dedicated power profiler. A DMM average also hides bursts.

### How would you reduce the divider drain to nearly zero?
Use a high-value divider (e.g. 2 MΩ + 2 MΩ is 1 µA) with a buffer op-amp or a high-impedance ADC input, or put a MOSFET in series with the bottom resistor and enable it only when sampling. Check the leakage and the capacitor charging time constant $\tau = R_{th}C$.

### The shunt reading says 0.8 A but a bench DMM in series says 0.9 A. What do you check?
First the DMM's burden voltage: if it drops 200 mV on a 3.3 V device, the supply droops and the current changes. Then the amplifier offset and gain error, the shunt tolerance and temperature coefficient, the Kelvin connection and any parallel current path bypassing the shunt. Finally the sampling: an average of a pulsed load depends on the meter's integration time.

### How does current-measurement error turn into SoC error?
Coulomb counting integrates it. A constant offset of 5 mA on a 2 Ah cell accumulates $5\text{ mA}\times24\text{ h}=120$ mAh per day, 6% of capacity daily. That's why gauges recalibrate against open-circuit voltage at rest ([[q0308]]).
:::

::: interpretations
"Battery voltage/current" can mean the open-circuit voltage and charge/discharge current of a cell, or the pack's terminal values in a BMS. I treat the cell and pack the same way at the measurement level; the pack case adds level shifting for series cells ([[q0304]]). If the interviewer means *measuring while charging*, add that you measure at the cell through a Kelvin connection because charger current makes drops in cables.
:::
