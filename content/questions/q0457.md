::: basics
### In plain words
A power supply must stay alive when the things it feeds go wrong, and it can also fail itself. Outside faults include shorts, overloads and input spikes. Inside faults include a broken feedback resistor or a shorted switch. A buck converter limits current on every switching cycle, but a hard short can still let the inductor current creep upward, so controllers add a valley limit, hiccup mode and thermal shutdown. For overvoltage you also need a clamp or crowbar that does not depend on the regulator.

### Picture it
Imagine climbing a staircase where each step up is two stairs but each step back down is only one and a half. Every cycle you gain half a stair, so you keep going up even though each step is small. That is the inductor current in a hard short: it rises a little more than it falls each cycle. The fix is to make you wait at the bottom until you are back down, which is the valley limit. The analogy leaves out saturation: if the inductor saturates, the stairs suddenly get much steeper.

### Words you need
- **Peak current limit**: turning the switch off each cycle when the inductor current hits a ceiling.
- **Valley current limit**: skipping the next cycle until the current has fallen below a threshold.
- **Hiccup mode**: repeated short restarts with long pauses during a persistent fault. See [[q0456]].
- **OVP**: shutting off switching when the output rises above a set level.
- **Crowbar**: a device that shorts the rail on overvoltage to force a fuse to open.

### The simplest example
Short the output of a buck fed from 12 V with a 4.7 µH inductor. During a minimum on-time of 150 ns, the current rises by $12 \times 150\text{ ns} / 4.7\ \mu\text{H} = 0.383$ A. During the long off-time, only a 0.7 V diode drop pulls it down, which removes about $0.7 \times 1.85\ \mu\text{s} / 4.7\ \mu\text{H} = 0.276$ A. The net gain is 0.107 A per cycle, so it reaches 5 A in about 47 cycles. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers valley limit and foldback fixes, overvoltage from an open feedback resistor or shorted FET, an independent clamp, and input protection.
:::

The interviewer wants specific mechanisms, not generic "add a fuse": cycle-by-cycle current limit, why a short can make a buck's inductor current *ratchet up* even with a current limit, hiccup, OVP on the feedback, a crowbar or clamp that does not depend on the regulator, and thermal shutdown. The strongest answers explain what physically happens in a buck during a short.
:::

::: why
A power supply is the part of the system that must stay alive when everything downstream is misbehaving, and the part whose failure destroys everything downstream. Inductors saturate and current runs away, a feedback resistor goes open and the output rises to the input voltage, a hot-plugged cable rings to twice the input. These are the failures that give supplies a reputation for burning boards, so interviewers ask how you would stop them. It is also the bridge between the generic protection overview ([[q0454]]) and real converter design ([[q0265]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor current and saturation**: $v = L\,di/dt$, and once the core saturates, $L$ collapses and current rises very quickly ([[q0046]]).
- **Cycle-by-cycle current limit**: terminate each switching cycle when the switch current reaches a peak limit.
- **Valley / low-side current limit**: check the current at the end of the off-time and delay the next cycle until it falls.
- **Hiccup and thermal shutdown**: restart policies and over-temperature protection ([[q0456]]).
- **Feedback failure**: an open or shorted divider makes the controller push the output toward either rail ([[q0223]], [[q0224]]).
- **Clamp, crowbar and fuse**: protection independent of the regulator ([[q0144]]).
:::

::: answer
"I'd think about it in layers: what the converter can do for itself, and what has to be independent of it.

For overcurrent, the standard is a cycle-by-cycle peak current limit, where the controller turns off the high-side switch each cycle when the inductor current hits the threshold. That stops the inductor from saturating on an overload. During a hard short there's a subtlety: the output is at zero, so the inductor only has a diode drop to ramp down during the off-time, and even a minimum on-time can add more current than the off-time removes. So you also want a valley current limit, which skips pulses until the current has come down, or frequency foldback. Then hiccup mode, so a persistent short only sees a few percent duty, and thermal shutdown as a backstop.

For overvoltage, the controller's OVP comparator on the feedback pin turns off the high-side switch if the output rises above something like 10% over nominal. But if the fault is the regulator itself, like a shorted switch or an open feedback resistor, it can't protect the load, so I'd add an independent clamp or a crowbar with a fuse.

On the input I'd add a fuse or eFuse, reverse polarity protection, a TVS, and undervoltage lockout, plus soft start to limit inrush."
:::

::: technical
**Overcurrent in a buck.** Inductor ramps: on-time $\Delta I_{on} = (V_{in}-V_{out})\,t_{on}/L$, off-time $\Delta I_{off} = (V_{out}+V_f)\,t_{off}/L$. In a short, $V_{out}\approx0$, so the on-slope is $V_{in}/L$ and the off-slope is only $V_f/L$ (about 0.7 V). Example: $V_{in}=12$ V, $L=4.7$ µH, $f_{sw}=500$ kHz, minimum on-time 150 ns. Rise per cycle: $12\times150\text{ ns}/4.7\ \mu\text{H}=0.383$ A. Fall per cycle: $0.7\times1.85\ \mu\text{s}/4.7\ \mu\text{H}=0.276$ A. Net +0.107 A each cycle, reaching 5 A after about 47 cycles (94 µs) even though the converter is "limited". The fixes: valley limit, frequency foldback (stretch the period), or a longer effective off-time.

**Overvoltage.** If the top feedback resistor opens, $V_{FB}\to0$, the controller sees a low output and drives maximum duty, pushing $V_{out}\to V_{in}$ in a buck (and far higher in a boost). If the high-side FET shorts, $V_{in}$ appears at the output directly. An OVP comparator at roughly 105 to 115% of $V_{ref}$ (vendor-dependent) can turn off the high side, but cannot help with a shorted FET. Independent protection: TVS or Zener clamp sized for the fault current, or an SCR crowbar plus fuse.

**Linear supplies.** Current limit plus thermal shutdown; dissipation in a short is $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{lim}$ ([[q0456]]).
:::

::: long
"I'd split it into what the converter does for itself and what must be independent of it.

Overcurrent first. In a buck the key mechanism is the inductor. A cycle-by-cycle peak current limit turns the high-side switch off each cycle when the sensed current reaches a threshold, so an overload can't push the inductor into saturation, where the inductance collapses and current shoots up. But a hard short has a trap. With the output at zero volts, the inductor ramps up at Vin over L during the on-time, but ramps down at only a diode drop over L during the off-time. For 12 V, 4.7 microhenries and a 150 nanosecond minimum on-time, it goes up about 0.38 amps per cycle and down about 0.28, so it ratchets upward even with the peak limit. The fix is a valley current limit, which won't start a new cycle until the current has come down, or frequency foldback. On top of that, hiccup mode so a persistent short only sees a couple of percent duty, and thermal shutdown.

Overvoltage. Most controllers watch the feedback pin and turn off the high side if the output is above, say, 110% of nominal. But the dangerous failures are the ones the controller can't see: a shorted high-side FET puts Vin on the output, and an open top feedback resistor makes the controller think the output is low and drive full duty. So I'd add something independent downstream, a TVS or Zener clamp, or an SCR crowbar that shorts the rail and blows a fuse, or an OVP load switch.

At the input: a fuse or eFuse, reverse-polarity protection, a TVS for spikes, undervoltage lockout, and soft start to limit inrush into the output capacitors."
:::

::: terms
- **Peak current limit (cycle-by-cycle)**: turn the switch off each cycle when current reaches a ceiling.
- **Valley current limit**: delay the next cycle until the inductor current has fallen below a threshold.
- **Hiccup mode**: repeated short restarts with long pauses during a persistent fault.
- **Minimum on-time**: the shortest pulse a controller can generate; it sets how low a duty cycle it can reach.
- **OVP (feedback-based)**: shutting down switching when the sensed output exceeds a threshold.
- **Crowbar**: an SCR that shorts the rail on overvoltage to force a fuse or current limit to act.
- **Soft start**: ramping the output (or current limit) up gradually after start-up.
- **Thermal shutdown**: disabling the output when die temperature exceeds a limit, typically with hysteresis.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists a fuse, a Zener and "current limit".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the **buck-specific mechanism** of a short: slope $V_{in}/L$ up and $V_f/L$ down, and how the inductor current ratchets even with a peak limit.
- Names **valley limit / frequency foldback** and **hiccup** as the remedies and quantifies the current per cycle.
- Identifies **internal causes of overvoltage** (open top feedback resistor, shorted pass or high-side FET, load dump) and notes they defeat the regulator's own OVP.
- Uses an **independent layer** (clamp, crowbar plus fuse, OVP switch) and states the sizing rule (fault energy, I²t).
- Covers the **input side** (reverse polarity, TVS, UVLO, inrush) and thermal shutdown.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Believing a peak current limit makes a buck short-proof.** With minimum on-time and a near-zero off-slope, current can still ratchet up.
- **Relying on the regulator's own OVP for a regulator failure.** If the FET is shorted, the control loop no longer controls anything.
- **A Zener or TVS with no fuse.** A clamp that is sized for brief spikes burns out under sustained overvoltage and then conducts a short or opens.
- **Setting the OVP threshold inside the normal tolerance band.** Load transient overshoot ([[q0151]]) would cause nuisance trips.
- **Ignoring saturation current.** The peak limit must be below the inductor's saturation current, including tolerance and temperature ([[q0259]]).
- **Forgetting reverse polarity and hot-plug transients on the input.** Many dead supplies were killed by the connector, not the load.
:::

::: deep
## What a buck does during an overload

Take the standard buck from [[q0173]]. During the on-time the inductor sees $V_{in}-V_{out}$ and its current ramps up; during the off-time it sees $-(V_{out}+V_f)$ and ramps down. In steady state the up and down changes are equal, which sets the duty cycle. Under an overload the output voltage sags, which changes both slopes. A **cycle-by-cycle peak current limit** adds a comparator on the switch current: when it reaches the threshold $I_{pk}$, the high-side switch turns off for the rest of the cycle. This limits the peak but not the average in all cases, and it has a trap in a hard short.

### The short-circuit ratchet

With $V_{out}\approx0$ the equations become

$$\Delta I_{on} = \frac{V_{in}\,t_{on}}{L},\qquad \Delta I_{off} = \frac{V_f\,t_{off}}{L}$$

The on-slope is large, but the off-slope is tiny because only a diode (or synchronous FET) drop opposes the current. The controller wants to reduce the duty cycle to nearly zero, but it cannot go below its **minimum on-time** $t_{on,min}$ (typically tens to a few hundred nanoseconds, because of leading-edge blanking and gate-drive delays). With $V_{in}=12$ V, $L=4.7$ µH, $f_{sw}=500$ kHz and $t_{on,min}=150$ ns:

- up per cycle: $12\times150\text{ ns}/4.7\ \mu\text{H}=0.383$ A
- down per cycle: $0.7\times(2\ \mu\text{s}-150\text{ ns})/4.7\ \mu\text{H}=0.276$ A
- net: $+0.107$ A per cycle, so 5 A is reached after about 47 cycles (94 µs).

Figure: Each cycle the current steps up by 0.383 A in 150 ns and decays only 0.276 A over the long off-time, so the current staircases upward even though the controller has already reduced the on-time to its minimum. The peak limit cannot stop it: the on-time is already at its minimum (and inside the blanking time), so the current keeps climbing until resistive drops balance the slopes or the inductor saturates.

Fixes used by real controllers:

- **Valley current limit**: after the off-time, measure the current in the low-side switch; if it is above the valley threshold, skip the next on-pulse. This directly limits the average.
- **Frequency foldback**: when $V_{out}$ is low (detected on feedback), reduce $f_{sw}$ so the off-time grows and the inductor current has time to fall.
- **Hiccup**: if the current-limit condition persists for a number of cycles (or $V_{out}$ is below the power-good threshold after soft-start), turn off for many milliseconds and retry.

### Why saturation makes it worse

A ferrite inductor loses inductance abruptly above its saturation current ([[q0046]]). Then $di/dt = V_{in}/L_{sat}$ is much larger than expected and the peak limit's comparator delay allows a big overshoot. Choose the current limit with margin below $I_{sat}$ over temperature, and note that the converter's current limit tolerance (often ±10 to 20%) is part of the budget.

## Protecting the output voltage

### Inside the loop: feedback OVP

Most converters monitor $V_{FB}$ with a comparator at 105 to 115% of $V_{ref}$ (vendor-specific, check the datasheet). If tripped, the high-side switch is turned off; a synchronous design may also turn on the low-side switch until the current reverses to pull the output down. This protects against load release overshoot, a runaway control loop or a noisy reference.

### Failures the loop cannot see

The control loop assumes that its own sensors and switches work. Three common ways it does not:

1. **Open top feedback resistor.** $V_{FB} = V_{out}R_{bot}/(R_{top}+R_{bot})$. If $R_{top}$ opens, $V_{FB}=0$; the controller believes the output is far too low and drives maximum duty, so $V_{out}\to V_{in}$ (in a buck) or until something breaks (in a boost). The feedback OVP does not help because it also reads FB (some devices have a separate OVP pin or detect FB near zero as a fault).
2. **Shorted high-side or pass FET.** The input appears at the output through a low resistance.
3. **Wrong input voltage** (a 24 V adapter into a 12 V design) exceeding switch ratings.

For these, protection must be independent of the regulator.

> OVP thresholds (105 to 115% of the reference), minimum on-time (tens to hundreds of ns) and current-limit tolerance (±10 to 20%) vary by controller. The numbers above are typical for teaching, not for any specific device; always use the datasheet.

### The crowbar

A **crowbar** uses an SCR across the rail that is triggered when a Zener-based detector sees overvoltage. The SCR latches on and stays on until the current falls below its holding level, which means the supply is forced to current-limit or the fuse opens. It is the strongest overvoltage protection because the load sees only the SCR's on-state voltage, but it takes the rail down. It is suited to expensive loads (processors, FPGAs) where overvoltage destroys the load at a higher cost than the supply outage.

## Input-side protection

- **Fuse or eFuse** for gross overcurrent ([[q0459]]).
- **Reverse polarity**: a series diode, or better a P-FET or ideal-diode controller with a lower voltage drop.
- **TVS** across the input for hot-plug spikes (an undamped cable plugged into a ceramic input capacitor can ring to nearly twice $V_{in}$) and ESD.
- **UVLO** with hysteresis so the converter does not start in a way that collapses a weak source ([[q0222]]).
- **Soft start** so charging the output capacitance (inrush, see [[q0343]]) doesn't trip the current limit: $I_{inrush}=C\,dV/dt$, so a 1 ms ramp into 100 µF at 5 V is 0.5 A.

## Layering summary

| Layer | Protects against | Independent of regulator? |
|---|---|---|
| Fuse / eFuse | Gross overcurrent, shorts | Yes |
| TVS / input clamp | Spikes, hot-plug | Yes |
| Peak + valley current limit | Overload, short inside converter | No |
| Hiccup, thermal shutdown | Sustained fault, overheating | No |
| Feedback OVP | Overshoot, loop faults | No |
| Clamp / crowbar / OVP switch | Regulator failure | Yes |
:::

::: applications
- **Point-of-load buck converters** with cycle-by-cycle limit, hiccup and OVP in the controller IC ([[q0263]]).
- **USB power switches** and eFuses that limit and report faults for each port ([[q0459]]).
- **Server power supplies** with output crowbar or OVP latches to protect CPUs and memory.
- **Automotive 12 V and 24 V regulators** with load-dump TVS, reverse battery protection and thermal shutdown.
- **Battery-powered products** that combine a fuse or PTC, a protection IC and an LDO with current limit.
- **Bench power supplies**: adjustable OCP and OVP trip circuits plus constant-current mode.
:::

::: assumptions
I assumed a typical DC-DC supply (buck as the running example) with a controller IC; discrete designs implement the same ideas with external comparators. Boost and flyback converters behave differently under short: a boost has a direct DC path from input to output through the inductor and diode, so a short on the output cannot be limited by the switch at all and needs a series disconnect switch (true-disconnect boost) or an input fuse. Isolated supplies add primary-side current limit and secondary OVP with an optocoupler. LDOs rely on current limit and thermal shutdown, with $P=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{lim}$ of heat. Different numeric assumptions (minimum on-time, inductor value, switching frequency) change how quickly the ratchet occurs, but not its existence.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a boost converter need a different short-circuit strategy from a buck?
A boost has a DC path from the input through the inductor and diode to the output. If the output is shorted, the switch cannot control the current: the input source drives current through the inductor and diode into the short regardless of switching. You need a fuse or current-limited source at the input, or a **true-shutdown** boost that has a series switch (or back-to-back FETs) disconnecting the output. Also, the diode has to survive the full short current.

### What happens if the inductor saturates during a current-limit event?
Inductance collapses, so $di/dt=V/L$ jumps. The current comparator and gate drive have a propagation delay (tens to hundreds of ns), and during that delay the current overshoots well above the setpoint, possibly beyond the switch's rating. Choose an inductor with $I_{sat}$ comfortably above the current limit over temperature and tolerance, or a soft-saturating powder core ([[q0259]]).

### How would you protect a 3.3 V microcontroller rail against a failure of its LDO that passes 12 V through?
Place a clamp or crowbar on the 3.3 V rail that triggers around 4 to 5 V, with a fuse or the upstream current limit to remove the fault current; or use an OVP load switch in series that disconnects above about 3.6 V. A Zener or TVS alone is not enough: the failed LDO can supply amps, so the clamp must either be sized for that fault current until the fuse opens or be paired with a crowbar and fuse. Many MCU rails use a supervisor plus a series switch for this reason.

### Why is hiccup mode better than continuous current limiting for a persistent short?
In continuous limit the supply delivers $I_{lim}$ into the short indefinitely, so the dissipation (for a linear stage $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{lim}$, for a switcher the conduction loss in switch and inductor) persists. Hiccup duty of 1 to 3% reduces average dissipation by 30 to 100 times, and the supply recovers automatically after the fault clears.

### Scenario: your buck converter is destroyed whenever the output is shorted at power-up. What do you check?
Check the minimum on-time versus the input voltage (the ratchet), whether the current limit is above $I_{sat}$, whether there is a valley limit or frequency foldback, how fast the current-sense blanking is, layout inductance on the switch node, and whether the input source is stiff enough to collapse. Scope the inductor current during the short; a staircase upward with $\Delta I_{up} > \Delta I_{down}$ confirms the ratchet. Consider a larger inductor, a lower switching frequency, or a controller with valley limit.
:::
