::: basics
### In plain words
A process corner is an extreme case of manufacturing variation. Chips contain two kinds of transistor, called NMOS and PMOS. Each can come out slow, typical or fast. A corner pairs one speed for each kind. The first letter is NMOS and the second is PMOS, so TT, SS, FF, FS and SF. Designers verify a chip at all of them.

### Picture it
Picture a seesaw with two children, one pushing down and one pushing up. NMOS pulls a signal down, and PMOS pulls it up. The balance point is where the signal flips from 0 to 1. If both children are strong, or both weak, the seesaw stays balanced, but the push is faster or slower. That is FF and SS. If one is strong and the other weak, the balance point tilts. That is FS and SF.

### Words you need
- **NMOS / PMOS**: the two types of transistor in a CMOS chip. NMOS pulls a signal low, and PMOS pulls it high.
- **Threshold voltage**: the voltage a transistor needs before it turns on. Lower means faster.
- **Corner**: one extreme set of device models, standing for the edge of the manufacturing spread.
- **Setup check**: confirms that data arrives before the next clock tick.
- **Hold check**: confirms that data does not change too soon after a clock tick.

### The simplest example
Setup fails when signals are too slow, so it is checked at SS, with a low supply voltage. Hold fails when signals are too fast, so it is checked at FF. Leakage is also worst at FF, because fast transistors have a low threshold. For the seesaw, take a simple inverter model on a 1.0 V supply. At TT the switching point is 0.500 V. With fast NMOS and slow PMOS (FS), it moves to about 0.475 V. With the reverse (SF), it moves to about 0.525 V. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers voltage and temperature combinations and what corners miss.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can define process corners precisely and use them to reason about verification, not just recite "SS, TT, FF". The interviewer is checking:

1. The definition: a corner is an extreme combination of **NMOS and PMOS speed** (fast, typical, slow) that bounds global manufacturing variation, typically at about ±3 sigma.
2. The naming: first letter is NMOS, second is PMOS; TT, FF, SS, FS, SF.
3. What physical parameters move: $V_t$, channel length, oxide thickness, mobility (and separate RC corners for interconnect).
4. Which corner exercises which check: SS for setup, FF for hold and leakage, FS/SF for skew-sensitive circuits (SRAM, ratioed logic, clocks, analog).
5. What corners do *not* cover: local mismatch (Monte Carlo/statistical), and that corners are combined with voltage and temperature.
:::

::: why
Corners are the language foundries and design teams use to promise that a chip will work: "timing met at SS, 0.81 V, 125 °C". Without them you could not sign off a tape-out of billions of transistors. They also explain why a design that simulates perfectly at typical can fail in production, and why leakage and speed move together. Questions like this check that you understand the bridge between device physics and verification flow, and it sits right after PVT ([[q0949]]) and before validation temperatures ([[q0951]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **PVT**: the three variation sources; corners are the *process* axis of it. See [[q0949]].
- **NMOS/PMOS independence**: the two device types are manufactured with different steps, so their speeds vary semi-independently.
- **Threshold voltage and drive current**: fast means lower $V_t$ and shorter $L$, so more current. See [[q0947]].
- **Setup vs hold**: max-delay vs min-delay timing checks that need opposite corners.
- **Global vs local variation**: corners capture die-wide shifts; mismatch is local.
- **RC corners**: separate extremes for interconnect resistance and capacitance.
:::

::: answer
"Process corners are a way of bounding manufacturing variation. In a real fab, transistors from different wafers and lots come out faster or slower than typical: threshold voltage, gate length, oxide thickness and so on drift. The foundry characterizes this and provides model files for extreme-but-realistic cases, usually around plus or minus three sigma of the global distribution.

They're named by NMOS first and PMOS second, each one slow, typical, or fast. So TT is typical-typical, SS is slow NMOS and slow PMOS, FF is fast-fast. Then there are the skewed corners: FS is fast NMOS with slow PMOS, and SF is the reverse. NMOS and PMOS are built with different process steps, so they can drift in opposite directions.

We use them because different checks have different worst cases. Setup timing, the max-delay check, fails on slow silicon, so we use SS at low voltage and appropriate temperature. Hold timing fails when things are too fast, so FF at high voltage. Leakage power is worst in FF at high temperature, because fast devices have low threshold and leak. The skewed corners matter for circuits where the NMOS to PMOS balance is important: SRAM read and write margins, ratioed or pass-gate logic, the switching threshold of inverters, clock duty cycle, and analog biasing.

Each corner is combined with voltage and temperature, like SS at 0.81 volts and 125 C. And corners only cover global variation, so I'd add Monte Carlo or on-chip variation derates for local mismatch."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** A process corner is a set of SPICE model parameters representing an extreme of the *global* (die-to-die, wafer-to-wafer, lot-to-lot) variation distribution of the fabrication process. Foundries obtain them from process control monitor data and sometimes from deliberately skewed lots, and typically place the corner at about $\pm3\sigma$ for the key parameters (some foundries/flows use different sigma, such as 2σ for yield-driven or 4σ for high-reliability; check the PDK).

**Naming.** Two letters: NMOS speed then PMOS speed, each F (fast), T (typical), S (slow). Five digital corners: TT, FF, SS, FS, SF. Often called the *skewed* corners for FS and SF. Plus RC extremes for the back end: $C_{worst}$ and $C_{best}$ (max/min capacitance) and $RC_{worst}$ and $RC_{best}$.

**What changes in a "fast" device.** Lower $V_t$, shorter effective length $L$, thinner oxide, higher mobility, giving a higher drive current $I_{on}$ and, at the same time, much higher subthreshold leakage $I_{off}\propto e^{-V_t/(nkT/q)}$. "Slow" is the opposite. So speed and leakage move together along the corner axis.

**Corner usage.**
| Corner | NMOS | PMOS | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| SS | slow | slow | setup (max-delay) timing, minimum-speed, worst-case path delay |
| TT | typical | typical | nominal simulation, power estimates (with caveats) |
| FF | fast | fast | hold (min-delay), worst-case leakage and dynamic power and peak current |
| FS | fast | slow | skew effects: inverter threshold shifts low, SRAM read stability, rising edges slow |
| SF | slow | fast | skew effects: threshold shifts high, SRAM write ability, falling edges slow |

**Inverter switching threshold example.** For a simple square-law inverter, $V_M=\dfrac{V_{DD}-|V_{tp}|+r\,V_{tn}}{1+r}$ with $r=\sqrt{k_p/k_n}$. TT ($V_t=0.35$ V, $k_n=k_p$): $V_M=0.500$ V at $V_{DD}=1.0$ V. A hypothetical FS ($V_{tn}=0.31$, $|V_{tp}|=0.39$, $k_n=1.2$, $k_p=0.8$): 0.475 V. SF: 0.525 V. So the skew moves the switching point by about ±25 mV, which erodes noise margin and skews rise/fall times, distorting clock duty cycle.

**Combination with V and T.** A sign-off corner is a triple, e.g. `ss_0p81v_125c`, `ff_0p99v_m40c`. Different checks use different triples, and temperature inversion at advanced nodes means setup might need the cold slow corner too ([[q0949]]).

**Limits.** Corners cover global variation only. Within-die random mismatch (Pelgrom) is handled by OCV/AOCV/POCV derates in timing, and Monte Carlo or statistical simulation for SRAM, analog and matching-critical circuits.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The exact sigma level, parameters skewed, and number of corners depend on the foundry and PDK. The tables here describe common practice, not any specific process.
:::

Fast means lower threshold and more current, but more leakage. ::: long
"Process corners are how we capture manufacturing variation in simulation. A fab doesn't make identical transistors. Threshold voltage, gate length, oxide thickness and doping differ between lots, wafers and across a die. The foundry measures that spread and provides model files for the extremes, usually around three sigma, so that we can verify the design works for any die that passes through the process.

They're named by the speed of NMOS then PMOS. Typical-typical is nominal. Slow-slow, SS, means both device types are slow: higher threshold, less current. Fast-fast, FF, is the opposite. Then you have the skewed corners, FS and SF, where NMOS and PMOS move in opposite directions. That's physically possible because they're built with different implants and steps.

Each one matters for a specific check. Setup timing is a max-delay check, so the slow corner SS, usually with low voltage, is the worst. Hold timing is a min-delay check, so FF with high voltage is the worst. Fast corners also have the most leakage and highest peak current, so the leakage and power sign-off use FF at high temperature. The skewed corners test balance. If NMOS is strong and PMOS weak, an inverter's switching threshold moves down and rising edges are slow. That affects clock duty cycle, SRAM write and read margins, level-sensitive and ratioed logic, and analog biasing.

In practice each corner is combined with a supply voltage and a temperature, like SS at 0.81 volts and 125 degrees, and there are separate RC corners for interconnect. A modern chip is signed off at dozens of these. And remember that corners capture global variation only; local random mismatch needs on-chip variation margins or Monte Carlo simulation."
:::

::: terms
- **Process corner**: an extreme set of device models bounding global process variation.
- **TT / FF / SS**: typical, fast-fast and slow-slow (NMOS then PMOS).
- **FS / SF**: skewed corners with opposite NMOS and PMOS speeds.
- **Global variation**: shifts common to all devices on a die (and between dies).
- **Local variation / mismatch**: random differences between neighboring devices.
- **RC corner**: extremes of interconnect resistance and capacitance (e.g. $C_{worst}$).
- **PDK**: process design kit with models and design rules from the foundry.
- **Monte Carlo**: statistical simulation sampling random variation.
- **Sigma ($\sigma$)**: standard deviation of a parameter's distribution; corners often sit at ±3σ.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists TT, FF, SS, FS, SF with "fast and slow" and says they're for worst-case simulation.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains that the letters are **NMOS then PMOS** and that NMOS and PMOS vary semi-independently, which is *why* skewed corners exist.
- Says what physically changes (**$V_t$, $L$, $t_{ox}$, mobility**) and ties **speed and leakage together** along the axis.
- Maps **checks to corners** (SS setup; FF hold, leakage, peak current; FS/SF for SRAM, clock duty, ratioed logic, analog bias) and notes that PVT is combined.
- Mentions **RC corners** and the **limits**: global only; local mismatch needs Monte Carlo/OCV/POCV.
- Gives a quantitative illustration such as the inverter switching threshold shifting by tens of mV, or the ±3σ convention with a caveat that it's foundry specific.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"FF is the best case."** It's best for setup timing and worst for hold, leakage and peak current. No corner is "best" for everything.
- **"Corners are a model of temperature or voltage."** They model *process* only; temperature and voltage are separate axes combined with them.
- **Reading the letters backwards.** The first letter is NMOS and the second PMOS (FS means fast NMOS, slow PMOS).
- **Believing corners include random mismatch.** They bound global shifts; within-die mismatch needs OCV, Monte Carlo or statistical analysis.
- **Thinking corners are actual chips.** They are model abstractions. Real dies lie inside the corner box and the corners are deliberately slightly pessimistic.
- **Ignoring interconnect.** Wire RC varies too, and can make a "typical" FEOL corner slow when the back-end corner is $RC_{worst}$.
:::

::: deep
## Where variation comes from

Chip fabrication is a long sequence of steps (oxidation, implantation, lithography, etch, deposition, CMP, anneal). Each has a tolerance. The key physical outcomes for a transistor:

- **Effective channel length $L_{eff}$**: shorter means more drive current but higher leakage and DIBL.
- **Gate oxide thickness $t_{ox}$**: thinner raises the gate capacitance and current.
- **Doping and work function**: set $V_t$.
- **Mobility and strain**: set the drive current per unit width.

NMOS and PMOS devices are formed with separate implants and (in modern nodes) different stress engineering steps, so their parameters are not perfectly correlated. A plot of NMOS speed against PMOS speed for many dies gives an elongated cloud; corners are chosen to enclose most of that cloud.

## What each corner stresses

**SS: slow-slow.** Highest $V_t$ and longest $L$: smallest drive current, longest delays. Used for setup (max-delay) analysis, usually with low supply voltage and the slow temperature (hot for older/high-voltage designs, possibly cold at low voltage with inversion). Also relevant for minimum-frequency guarantees and some analog bandwidth checks.

**FF: fast-fast.** Lowest $V_t$, shortest $L$: highest speed, highest peak and leakage current. Used for hold (min-delay) analysis, maximum power and IR drop, maximum short-circuit current, and electromigration current checks. With high voltage and high temperature it is the leakage-power worst case.

**TT.** Reference point for functional simulation and typical power; not enough for sign-off.

**FS and SF.** Here the pull-up and pull-down strengths are mismatched, which affects anything that depends on the *ratio* of NMOS to PMOS strength:
- **Inverter switching threshold** $V_M$ shifts (see the next section).
- **Rise/fall asymmetry** distorts clock duty cycle and pulse widths; clock-tree and pulse-based circuits are checked here.
- **SRAM**: write ability is usually worst when the access NMOS is weak and the PMOS pull-up strong (SF); read stability is typically stressed with strong access NMOS and weak PMOS (FS). The exact worst case depends on cell design.
- **Ratioed logic, pass-gate logic, level shifters and sense amplifiers** whose correct operation depends on one device overpowering another.
- **Analog**: bias points, offset and current mirror ratios move with NMOS/PMOS skew.

## Worked example: inverter threshold

Square-law CMOS inverter, $V_{DD}=1.0$ V. The switching threshold satisfies $k_n(V_M-V_{tn})^2=k_p(V_{DD}-V_M-|V_{tp}|)^2$, giving $V_M=\dfrac{V_{DD}-|V_{tp}|+rV_{tn}}{1+r}$, $r=\sqrt{k_p/k_n}$.

| Corner | $V_{tn}$ | $|V_{tp}|$ | $k_n$ | $k_p$ | $V_M$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TT | 0.35 | 0.35 | 1.0 | 1.0 | 0.500 V |
| FS | 0.31 | 0.39 | 1.2 | 0.8 | 0.475 V |
| SF | 0.39 | 0.31 | 0.8 | 1.2 | 0.525 V |
| FF | 0.31 | 0.31 | 1.2 | 1.2 | 0.500 V |
| SS | 0.39 | 0.39 | 0.8 | 0.8 | 0.500 V |

FF and SS (with matched devices) leave $V_M$ unchanged but change speed; FS and SF shift it by 25 mV, i.e. 5% of the supply. A signal with tight noise margins, or a pulse that must keep 50% duty cycle through a clock tree of many inverters, accumulates the effect: after many stages rise and fall delays differ and duty cycle drifts. This is why clock networks are checked at the skew corners.

## Corners are triples, plus RC

A sign-off corner combines process with V and T and with an interconnect corner:

| Name | Process | V | T | RC | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `ss_0p81v_125c_rcworst` | SS | 0.81 V | 125 °C | RCworst | setup, hot |
| `ss_0p81v_m40c_cworst` | SS | 0.81 V | -40 °C | Cworst | setup, cold (inversion) |
| `ff_0p99v_m40c_rcbest` | FF | 0.99 V | -40 °C | RCbest | hold |
| `ff_0p99v_125c` | FF | 0.99 V | 125 °C | typical | leakage power, IR drop |

Names are illustrative; real flows follow the PDK's conventions. A modern SoC may be signed off at dozens of such combinations across several operating modes (multi-corner multi-mode, MCMM).

## What corners are not

- **Not temperature or voltage.** Those are separate sign-off axes.
- **Not mismatch.** Two adjacent identical transistors differ randomly (Pelgrom: $\sigma_{\Delta V_t}=A_{V_t}/\sqrt{WL}$). Corners shift all devices together, so they say nothing about that. Timing handles it with on-chip-variation derates; SRAM and analog use Monte Carlo.
- **Not real chips.** Corners are model abstractions at the edge of the distribution; real shipped parts lie inside.
- **Not equally probable.** FF and SS are rare (at about ±3σ). Most chips are near TT, which is why binning can sell the faster dies for more.

## From corners to product decisions

Design meets timing at SS so that the slowest acceptable die still runs at the rated frequency. Production test measures actual speed and leakage, so a die near FF may be sold as a high-frequency SKU but must be tested against the leakage and power limit; a die near SS may be sold at lower frequency or higher voltage. This is why a corner-based design flow and **binning** go hand in hand ([[q0949]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Static timing sign-off**: setup at slow corners, hold at fast corners, in multi-corner multi-mode runs.
- **Power sign-off**: leakage and peak current at FF/high temperature; IR drop and electromigration at FF.
- **SRAM and register-file design**: read/write/hold margin simulations at skewed corners plus Monte Carlo.
- **Clock-tree design**: duty cycle and skew checks at skewed corners.
- **Analog and mixed-signal**: bias, offset, bandwidth and PLL lock range verification across corners.
- **Foundry/PDK qualification**: process control monitors and split lots establish where corners lie.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Digital CMOS corners** (five-corner model) as provided in typical PDKs. Some foundries also provide extra corners (e.g. low-leakage, high-$V_t$ flavors, or statistical/“sigma” corners).
- **±3σ** is common but not universal; the exact definition is in the PDK documentation.
- **Square-law inverter model** for the threshold example is a simplification; real FinFET and short-channel devices need SPICE, and the shift will differ.
- **Interconnect corners** are decoupled from front-end corners in many flows, so combinations multiply.
- If the interviewer is asking in a **board-level context**, "corners" may mean component tolerance corners (resistor/regulator tolerance) rather than semiconductor process; the logic (worst-case combinations) is the same.
:::

::: followups
### What does FS mean, and which circuits care?
Fast NMOS, slow PMOS. Pull-down is stronger than pull-up, so inverter thresholds move down, rising transitions are slow and falling ones fast. Circuits that depend on the N/P balance care: clock duty cycle, SRAM read margin, ratioed logic, sense amplifiers, level shifters and analog bias circuits.

### Which corner would you use for setup, which for hold, and why?
Setup is violated when data arrives too late, so use the slowest condition: SS with low voltage (and the slow temperature). Hold is violated when data changes too soon after the clock edge, so use the fastest: FF with high voltage. Each is checked at its own extreme, so you can't pick one corner for both.

### Why not just design for the worst-case corner everywhere and be done?
You'd over-design: designing for SS speed everywhere with FF power limits would waste area and voltage. Also the worst corners for different metrics are different, so no single corner is worst for all. And binning and adaptive voltage scaling let typical dies run efficiently while only the slow ones pay the cost.

### How are corners different from Monte Carlo simulation?
Corners are a small number of fixed, deterministic parameter sets bounding global variation; they are quick and cover the extremes. Monte Carlo draws many random samples of both global and local parameters to estimate a distribution and yield, which is needed for mismatch-sensitive circuits like SRAM and analog, where the failure probability matters at 5 to 6 sigma. It's slower so it's used selectively.

### Scenario: your chip passes timing at all corners in sign-off but 3% of dies fail at speed in production. What do you suspect?
Likely something not covered by global corners: local mismatch or on-chip-variation effects on a critical path, supply droop worse than modeled, a pessimistic-but-wrong corner assumption (for example temperature inversion not checked), clock skew or duty-cycle issues at skewed corners, or a systematic process shift outside the model. Characterize failing dies for speed/leakage vs passing ones, and check which paths fail and at what voltage and temperature.
:::

::: interpretations
"Process corners" in this list means the semiconductor corners (TT, SS, FF, FS, SF), in the context of PVT. In board-level or analog contexts people sometimes use "corners" for worst-case component tolerance combinations; the logic is analogous but the content differs. The question asks both *what are they* and *what do they refer to*, so define them (named combinations of NMOS/PMOS speed) and say what they refer to physically ($V_t$, $L$, $t_{ox}$, mobility, extremes of global variation).
:::
