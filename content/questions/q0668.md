::: testing
Whether you can go beyond "twice the frequency." The textbook answer is Nyquist: $f_s>2f$. The interviewer wants you to recognise that the right answer depends on **what you want to measure**: detect the frequency, see the waveform shape, or get the peak amplitude accurately; and to give practical numbers (2.5× with a proper anti-alias filter, 5–10× on a scope, 20+ samples per cycle for 1% peak error without interpolation). Hidden sub-skills: aliasing, reconstruction with interpolation, and the difference between waveform display and RMS or FFT measurement.
:::

::: why
Choosing a sample rate is a daily decision: setting an MCU ADC's timer for a sensor, picking a scope timebase, or specifying an ADC for a signal chain. Too low and you alias; too high and you waste power, memory and bandwidth. Understanding why "just above 2×" is the *theoretical* minimum and not a practical design point is the difference between a textbook answer and an engineer's answer ([[q0669]], [[q0670]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Sampling theorem**: a signal containing no frequency at or above $f_{max}$ is completely determined by samples taken at $f_s>2f_{max}$. See [[q0669]].
- **Aliasing**: if $f>f_s/2$, the sampled data looks like a lower frequency. See [[q0670]].
- **Samples per cycle**: $N=f_s/f$, the practical measure of waveform fidelity.
- **Reconstruction (sinc interpolation)**: recovering the continuous waveform from samples, including peaks between samples.
- **Anti-alias filter**: an analog low-pass before the sampler; it needs a transition band, which pushes $f_s$ above $2f_{max}$. See [[q0665]].
- **Coherent sampling**: sampling an integer number of cycles so FFT and RMS results are exact.
:::

::: answer
"The textbook answer is the Nyquist criterion: sample at more than twice the sine wave's frequency. If I have a 1 kilohertz sine, I need more than 2 kilosamples per second to avoid aliasing and to capture its frequency uniquely.

But 'just over twice' is a theoretical minimum, and what you need depends on what you want to measure. At exactly twice, you could land on the zero crossings and see nothing. If I just want the frequency from an FFT of a long record, a bit over 2× is fine, though in practice with a real anti-alias filter I'd go 2.5 times or more. If I want to see the waveform on a scope, I'd use 5 to 10 times the frequency, and the scope interpolates between points. If I want the peak amplitude from raw samples without interpolation, the worst-case error is one minus cosine of pi over N, where N is samples per cycle, so 10 samples per cycle gives up to about 5 percent error, and I'd need roughly 23 samples per cycle to get under 1 percent.

For RMS or amplitude using a whole number of cycles, you can get exact results with just a few samples per cycle. So my answer is: more than 2× in theory, around 2.5× in practice with a filter, 5 to 10× for viewing, and more for accurate raw-peak measurement, and always an anti-alias filter in front."
:::

::: technical
**Theory.** For a sinusoid at $f$, the sampling theorem requires $f_s>2f$ (strictly greater). At $f_s=2f$ the samples are $A\sin\varphi$ and $-A\sin\varphi$ alternately, so the apparent amplitude is $A|\sin\varphi|$, which can be zero for $\varphi=0$.

**Aliasing.** If $f>f_s/2$ the samples are identical to those of a sine at $f_a=|f-kf_s|$ for the integer $k$ that brings it into $[0,f_s/2]$ ([[q0670]]).

**Peak amplitude from raw samples.** With $N=f_s/f$ samples per cycle, the nearest sample to a peak is at most half a sample interval away in phase, $\pi/N$ rad, so the worst-case peak reading is $A\cos(\pi/N)$ and the error is

$$\varepsilon_{peak}=1-\cos(\pi/N).$$

N = 5: 19.1%; N = 10: 4.9%; N = 20: 1.2%; for ≤1% you need $N\ge\pi/\arccos(0.99)=22.2$, so 23 samples per cycle.

**With interpolation.** Sinc (ideal) or sin(x)/x interpolation recovers the continuous waveform from $f_s>2f$ if the signal is band-limited. Oscilloscopes use sin(x)/x interpolation; vendors commonly recommend sample rate of roughly 4–5× the scope bandwidth for accurate edge and amplitude reconstruction.

**RMS and fits.** For $N\ge3$ uniformly spaced samples over an integer number of cycles, $\sum\sin^2(\varphi+2\pi k/N)=N/2$ exactly, so the RMS of a pure sine is exact for any phase. A three-parameter sine fit (amplitude, phase, offset at known frequency) needs just three well-spread samples in the noise-free case, and more for noise averaging; four-parameter fits also estimate frequency.

**Practical choice.** $f_s\approx2.5$–$4\times f_{max}$ for ADC systems with an analog anti-alias filter; $f_{max}$ means the highest frequency of significance (including harmonics if you care about distortion).

**FFT.** Record length $T_{rec}=N/f_s$ sets resolution $\Delta f=1/T_{rec}$; use coherent sampling or a window to avoid leakage ([[q0666]]).
:::

::: long
"The standard answer is the Nyquist criterion: you must sample at more than twice the highest frequency in the signal. For a sine wave at 1 kilohertz, that means more than 2 kilosamples per second. If you sample slower than that, the sine aliases: it's indistinguishable from a lower-frequency sine. For example sampling a 900 hertz sine at 1 kilosample per second looks exactly like a 100 hertz sine.

But that's the theoretical bound, and the practical answer depends on what I'm trying to measure. At exactly two times, the result depends on phase: you could land on the zero crossings and read zero. So it has to be strictly more than two.

If I only need the frequency, say from an FFT over a long record, something like 2.5 times is enough, because a real anti-alias filter can't cut off instantly and needs a transition band between the signal and the Nyquist frequency.

If I need to see the waveform on a scope, I'd go to 5 to 10 samples per cycle, because the scope uses sin x over x interpolation to draw the points in between, and at that rate the reconstruction looks like a sine.

And if I need the peak amplitude from the raw samples, with no interpolation, the nearest sample could be half a sample interval away from the true peak, so the worst-case error is one minus cosine of pi over N, where N is samples per cycle. N equal to 10 gives about 5 percent, N equal to 20 gives about 1.2 percent, and you need about 23 samples per cycle for under 1 percent.

Finally, if I only need RMS or amplitude over a whole number of cycles, a few samples per cycle can be enough, as long as the sampling is uniform and I average a full period.

So: more than 2× in theory, 2.5× with a filter in practice, 5 to 10× for display, and 20 plus for raw-peak accuracy, always with an anti-alias filter ahead of the ADC."
:::

::: terms
- **Nyquist criterion**: sample rate must exceed twice the highest signal frequency.
- **Nyquist frequency**: half the sample rate, $f_s/2$, the highest frequency that can be represented.
- **Aliasing**: frequencies above $f_s/2$ folding into lower frequencies.
- **Samples per cycle ($N$)**: $f_s/f$.
- **Interpolation (sin(x)/x)**: reconstructing the waveform between samples.
- **Anti-alias filter**: low-pass filter before the sampler to remove content above $f_s/2$.
- **Coherent sampling**: an integer number of signal cycles in the record.
- **Oversampling**: sampling much faster than $2f_{max}$.
- **Peak error**: difference between true peak and largest sample.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "twice the frequency." An exceptional answer:
- Says strictly greater than $2f$ and shows what happens at exactly $2f$ (phase-dependent amplitude, possibly zero).
- Distinguishes goals: frequency identification, waveform display, raw peak accuracy, RMS/amplitude over whole cycles.
- Gives the $1-\cos(\pi/N)$ peak error formula with numbers (5: 19%, 10: 4.9%, 20: 1.2%).
- Mentions anti-alias filter transition bands and the practical 2.5–4× range, and scope interpolation.
- Notes that "a sine" in the real world has harmonics and noise which set the bandwidth you must cover.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using exactly $2f$.** The theorem requires strictly greater; at $2f$ the amplitude depends on phase.
- **Forgetting the anti-alias filter.** Without it, out-of-band noise and harmonics alias into your band.
- **Equating "enough to detect" with "enough to measure."** Detecting frequency is easy; measuring peak amplitude accurately needs more samples or interpolation.
- **Ignoring harmonics.** A distorted "sine" has harmonics, and the sample rate must cover them if they matter.
- **Believing more is always better.** Excessive sampling wastes power and memory, and may add noise bandwidth; match $f_s$ to need.
:::

::: deep
## What the theorem promises

If a signal $x(t)$ has no content at or above $f_{max}$, then $f_s>2f_{max}$ lets you reconstruct it exactly:

$$x(t)=\sum_{n}x[n]\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{t-nT_s}{T_s}\right),\qquad T_s=\frac{1}{f_s}.$$

The key words are "no content at or above". A pure sine at $f$ has content exactly at $f$; the condition $f_s>2f$ is strict.

### Why exactly 2× fails

At $f_s=2f$ the samples are taken every half cycle. If the sine is $A\sin(2\pi ft+\varphi)$, the samples are $A\sin\varphi$, then $A\sin(\varphi+\pi)=-A\sin\varphi$, and so on. Their magnitude is $A|\sin\varphi|$.

## What rate is enough depends on the goal

| Goal | Typical sample rate | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Detect/measure frequency (long record, FFT) | 2.5–4× $f$ | Anti-alias filter needs a transition band |
| View waveform on a scope (with sin(x)/x interpolation) | 5–10× $f$ | Interpolation fills in the shape |
| Raw peak amplitude, no interpolation, ≤1% error | ≥23 samples per cycle | Nearest sample may miss the peak |
| RMS/amplitude over whole cycles | ≥3 samples per cycle (coherent) | Averaging over uniformly spaced phases is exact |
| Distortion measurement (harmonics up to $kf$) | >$2kf$ | Must cover the harmonics you care about |

### Peak error from raw samples

Suppose samples are $N=f_s/f$ per cycle, equally spaced in phase by $2\pi/N$. The worst case is a peak falling exactly midway between two samples, i.e. $\pi/N$ from the nearest, so the largest sample is $A\cos(\pi/N)$:

$$\varepsilon=1-\cos\frac{\pi}{N}\approx\frac{\pi^2}{2N^2}\quad\text{for large }N.$$

A scope's interpolation and its "peak detect" mode avoid this for display, but raw-sample code (like a microcontroller reporting the max ADC value) does not.

**Example.** To read the peak of a 1 MHz sine within 1% from raw ADC codes, you need $f_s\approx23\ \text{MS/s}$. Contrast with the Nyquist minimum of 2 MS/s. For a 50 Hz mains waveform, 20 samples per cycle (1 kS/s) gives ≤1.2% peak error, with RMS computed over a full cycle exact.

### RMS is more forgiving

For a pure sine of amplitude $A$ sampled uniformly over an integer number of cycles with $N\ge3$ samples per cycle,

$$\frac1N\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}A^2\sin^2\!\left(\varphi+\frac{2\pi k}{N}\right)=\frac{A^2}{2}$$

for any phase $\varphi$. So $V_{rms}=A/\sqrt2$ exactly, even with only 3 samples per cycle. This is why energy meters can use modest sample rates, provided the record covers whole cycles and the signal has no harmonics above $f_s/2$ (otherwise they alias and corrupt the result).

## Practical constraint: the anti-alias filter

The theorem assumes *no* content above $f_s/2$. Real signals have noise and harmonics, so you must filter before sampling ([[q0670]]). The filter can't drop from passband to stopband instantly. Suppose the signal is at 1 kHz and you need 60 dB of attenuation at the alias frequency $f_s-f$. For $f_s=2.5$ kHz, the alias of 1 kHz is 1.5 kHz, a transition from 1 to 1.5 kHz: a very steep filter (a high-order design). For $f_s=10$ kHz, the transition is 1 kHz to 9 kHz, which a simple 2-pole or 3-pole filter handles. Hence **oversampling** (e.g., 10×) lets you use a simple analog filter and then do the sharp filtering in digital, and also lowers quantization noise density.

## Putting numbers on a decision

*Task*: measure a 20 kHz sine from a sensor with a microcontroller ADC and want amplitude within 1% from raw max readings. $f_s\ge23\times20\ \text{kHz}=460$ kS/s. If the MCU ADC does only 200 kS/s, choose a different approach: compute amplitude from an RMS over whole cycles, fit a sine to the samples, or undersample deliberately with a synchronised clock (equivalent-time sampling) if the signal is repetitive.

## Scopes and vendors

Oscilloscope vendors typically recommend a sample rate of at least 4–5× the bandwidth for accurate waveform capture with interpolation (some also state 2.5× as the minimum with a Gaussian response). The reason is that a scope's front-end is not brick-wall: it passes some content above the rated bandwidth, which must still be below $f_s/2$ to avoid aliasing.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The "2.5×, 4×, 5×" multipliers differ between vendors and depend on the scope's frequency response shape (Gaussian vs flat). Treat them as rules of thumb and check the instrument's documentation.
:::

::: applications
- **MCU ADC sensing**: choose timer-triggered sampling at 5–10× the signal bandwidth, plus a simple RC anti-alias filter.
- **Oscilloscope setup**: ensure the sample rate is several times the signal bandwidth, and use sin(x)/x interpolation on.
- **Audio**: 44.1/48 kHz for 20 kHz of content, with oversampling converters and digital filtering.
- **Power metering**: sampling 50/60 Hz mains at 4–32 kS/s to capture harmonics and compute RMS.
- **Vibration monitoring**: sample rate set by the highest bearing/gear frequency of interest, plus an anti-alias filter.
:::

::: assumptions
- The signal is a single stationary sine and band-limited. Real-world "sines" have harmonics, noise and distortion; the sample rate must satisfy the highest significant component.
- Peak-error formula assumes uniform sampling and a worst-case phase; the *expected* error is smaller.
- RMS exactness assumes an integer number of cycles and no components above $f_s/2$.
- The 2.5–4× recommendations assume a practical anti-alias filter with a modest transition band.
- If the question is about bandpass signals (a narrow band around a high carrier), the answer changes: bandpass sampling needs only $2B$ with careful choice of $f_s$ ([[q0669]]).
:::

::: followups
### What happens if you sample a 1 kHz sine at 1.5 kS/s?
That's below Nyquist (2 kS/s). The sine aliases to $|1000-1500|=500$ Hz, so the data looks like a 500 Hz sine, and nothing in the samples tells you otherwise. The only fix is a higher sample rate or an analog anti-alias filter before sampling that removes the 1 kHz content if it wasn't intended.

### How many samples per cycle do you need to see a decent sine on a scope?
Around 5 to 10 with sin(x)/x interpolation, since the interpolation reconstructs the in-between shape. Without interpolation (dots only), 10–20 gives an acceptable picture, and for amplitude accuracy better than 1% from raw peaks you need 23.

### Why does a scope with 100 MHz bandwidth typically sample at 1 GS/s or more?
So the sample rate is 10× the bandwidth: the scope's response isn't a brick wall, it has some content above 100 MHz (and the signal edges contain harmonics), and 1 GS/s provides enough samples per cycle to reconstruct the waveform and avoid aliasing, leaving an interpolation filter to do the rest.

### Can you measure a signal whose frequency is higher than the sample rate?
Yes, in special cases. Bandpass (undersampling) works when the signal occupies a narrow band $B$ around a high frequency and you choose $f_s>2B$ so the band aliases into the baseband without overlapping itself. Equivalent-time sampling on scopes reconstructs repetitive signals by sampling at different phases over many periods. In both cases you need to know the signal is band-limited or repetitive.

### Scenario: your MCU reports a 5 V-peak, 60 Hz signal as varying between about 4.65 and 5.0 V peak on successive reads at 500 S/s. What do you suspect?
500 S/s is 8.3 samples per cycle, and 60 Hz does not divide evenly into 500, so the sample phases relative to the peaks drift; the raw peak reading moves between about $\cos(\pi/8.3)=0.93$ and 1.0, i.e. 4.65–5 V, which is exactly the spread you see. (A spread much larger than this would point to noise or a reference problem instead.) To fix it: sample faster (≥1.4 kS/s for 1% worst-case), compute peak from a fit or RMS over whole cycles, or synchronise the sampling to the signal.
:::

::: interpretations
"Measure a sine wave" is open-ended, so a good answer first asks *what* is to be measured (frequency, waveform shape, amplitude or RMS) and what accuracy is needed. The Nyquist criterion is the starting point ([[q0669]]), while the practical choices (anti-alias filter, interpolation, samples per cycle) are what turn it into a design decision.
:::
