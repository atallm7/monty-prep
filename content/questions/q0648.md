::: basics
### In plain words
In a PoP stack, the memory sits right on top of the processor, which is the hottest chip. Heat that would have escaped up through the top of the package now has to pass through the memory first. More heat goes down into the board instead, the processor runs hotter, and the memory gets warmed from below. Memory works worse when it is hot.

### Picture it
Imagine a crowded room with two exit doors, one up and one down. People are the heat. If you narrow the upper door, the crowd backs up, and the room fills more than before. More people also use the lower door. Adding the memory package narrows the upper door.

### Words you need
- **Thermal resistance**: how many degrees of temperature rise each watt of heat flow causes, in K/W. A lower number is better.
- **Junction temperature**: the temperature of the silicon chip itself.
- **DRAM refresh**: memory cells leak their stored charge, so they must be rewritten regularly. Hotter cells leak faster, so refreshing costs more power and speed.
- **Throttling**: the chip slowing itself down to cut heat.
- **PoP**: memory stacked on the processor package. See [[q0647]].

### The simplest example
These are round numbers chosen to show the method. A processor's heat has two exits. Down through the board costs 32 K/W. Up through the top costs 20 K/W with no memory. Because the heat shares two routes, together they act like about 12.3 K/W. At 3 W, the chip runs about 3 × 12.3 = 37 degrees above ambient.

Add a memory package that costs 15 K/W on the upper route. The top route becomes 35 K/W, and the two routes together act like about 16.7 K/W. At 3 W the chip now runs about 50 degrees above ambient, 13 degrees hotter.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the memory's own temperature limit, and ways to cool the stack.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can reason about heat flow in a stack as a thermal circuit rather than just saying "the memory traps heat". The interviewer wants: where the heat is generated (SoC die), the paths it can take (up through the memory package, down through the balls and board, sideways through the package), what the memory does to those paths (adds thermal resistance on top, absorbs heat from below), what that means for the DRAM (it is heated by the SoC and has temperature-dependent refresh and limits), and what you can do about it (board copper, thermal interface and spreader on top, throttling, alternatives). A candidate who builds the thermal-resistance network and puts numbers on it stands out.
:::

::: why
In a phone-class device the processor's power budget is set largely by thermal limits, and stacked memory is part of that limit. A hot DRAM needs more frequent refresh (more power, less bandwidth), may throw errors, and degrades reliability. If you design with PoP parts you need to know why the vendor gives thermal limits and throttling thresholds for the stack, how board copper and a heat spreader change them, and why PoP is not used for high-power chips. It's also an application of basic thermal circuit analysis that interviewers like: junction-to-ambient as series and parallel resistances.
:::

::: concepts
- **Thermal resistance and thermal circuit**: $R_\theta = \Delta T/P$; series and parallel combine like resistors. See [[q0540]].
- **Junction temperature**: $T_J = T_A + P R_{JA}$ for a single dominant path.
- **Heat paths in a package**: to the board (through balls, planes) and to the top (case, lid, heat sink).
- **Heat spreading**: lateral conduction in substrates, board copper and metal sheets.
- **DRAM temperature behaviour**: refresh rate increases at high temperature; limits on operating temperature. See [[q0647]].
- **Thermal throttling**: reducing power to keep temperature within limits. See [[q0945]], [[q0949]].
- **Heat sinks and thermal interface materials**: reduce top-side resistance. See [[q0541]].
:::

::: answer
"A PoP stack changes the thermal picture in two ways. The memory sits right on top of the SoC, which is the hottest part. Normally an SoC can push a good chunk of its heat up through the top of its package into a heat spreader or the case; with PoP, that path goes through the memory package first, which is a stack of mould compound, substrate and DRAM silicon, so the top path has much higher thermal resistance. Heat therefore goes mostly down through the solder balls into the board's copper, and some goes up through the memory.

The result is that the SoC runs hotter for the same power, and the DRAM is heated from underneath by the SoC. DRAM is temperature-sensitive: above about 85 degrees C its cells leak faster, so the controller has to refresh more often, which costs power and bandwidth, and past its maximum rating it can't be used reliably.

I think of it as a thermal circuit. If the SoC has 32 K/W down through the board to ambient and 20 K/W up with no memory, those are in parallel, so about 12.3 K/W. Add the memory's 15 K/W in series with the top path and the up path becomes 35 K/W, so the total becomes about 16.7 K/W, a 36% increase. At 3 W that's about 50 K rise instead of 37. To manage it you improve the board path with copper and vias, put a thermal pad or graphite sheet over the memory to the case, and use the SoC's own thermal throttling. Past a few watts of sustained power, PoP stops being practical, which is why it's a mobile technology and not used on high-power chips."
:::

::: technical
**Thermal network.** Let the SoC dissipate $P$ at its junction node $T_J$. Two parallel paths to ambient:

- **Down path**: junction → die attach/substrate → balls → PCB (copper planes) → ambient: $R_{down}$ (illustrative 32 K/W, comprising 12 K/W to the board and 20 K/W board to ambient).
- **Up path**: junction → package top → (memory package) → surface → ambient: $R_{up}$. Without PoP: 5 + 15 = 20 K/W (junction-to-case plus case-to-ambient). With PoP: the memory package adds 15 K/W in series: 5 + 15 + 15 = 35 K/W.

Combined: $R_{JA} = R_{down}\parallel R_{up}$:

$$R_{JA,\text{no PoP}} = 32\parallel20 = 12.3\ \text{K/W},\qquad R_{JA,\text{PoP}} = 32\parallel35 = 16.7\ \text{K/W}.$$

For $P = 3$ W: $\Delta T_J = 36.9$ K versus 50.1 K, so $T_J = 61.9$ °C versus 75.1 °C at 25 °C ambient. With PoP, the heat splits as $\Delta T/R_{up} = 1.43$ W up through the memory and 1.57 W down; without PoP it is 1.85 W up and 1.15 W down. The memory therefore takes about 1.4 W of heat through it.

**DRAM temperature.** The memory package is in the up path, so its temperature lies between $T_J$ and ambient. Taking the memory's resistance as 15 K/W and the top-surface-to-ambient resistance as 15 K/W, the heat flow $Q_{up}=1.43$ W makes the bottom of the memory $25 + 1.43\times30 = 68$ °C and its top $25 + 1.43\times15 = 46.5$ °C. The DRAM dies sit near the bottom and add their own dissipation (a few tenths of a watt; for example 0.4 W through 22.5 K/W raises the dies about 9 K more). So the DRAM sees roughly 65 to 75 °C when the SoC is at 3 W and ambient is 25 °C. At 40 °C ambient add 15 K.

**Power limit.** With the DRAM bottom temperature approximately $25 + 14.3\,P$ °C (slope $= R_{JA}\times30/35 = 14.3$ K/W): it reaches 85 °C at $P = 4.2$ W, while the SoC junction reaches 105 °C at $P = 4.8$ W (PoP) or 6.5 W (no PoP). So in this model the DRAM limit bites before the SoC's, which shows why PoP is thermally limited by the memory.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> All resistances are illustrative round numbers chosen to show the method; real values come from the vendor's thermal model or measurements and differ by package, board and airflow. DRAM temperature limits and refresh behaviour depend on the part and speed grade: for LPDDR4-class parts, normal operation is specified up to about 85 °C with refresh rate requirements that increase at higher temperature and an extended range to about 105 °C, but you must check the specific datasheet and JEDEC standard.
:::

::: long
"PoP puts the DRAM directly over the processor, which is the hottest die, and that changes how heat leaves the SoC. Without a stacked memory, the processor can push heat both down through its balls into the board and up through the top of the package into a case or heat spreader. With PoP, the top path has to go through the memory package: mould, substrate, DRAM silicon. That adds thermal resistance in series on the top path, so heat prefers to go down into the board.

I can model it as a thermal circuit. Say the down path is 32 kelvin per watt, junction through the board to ambient, and without the memory the up path is 20. In parallel those give about 12.3. Add 15 for the memory package in the up path and it becomes 35, so the parallel combination is 16.7. At 3 watts, the junction temperature rise goes from 37 to 50 kelvin, so the processor runs 13 degrees hotter for the same power. Because the up path has 35 K/W, only about 1.4 watts go through the memory, and the DRAM bottom sits near 68 degrees at 25 degrees ambient.

That matters because DRAM is temperature sensitive: above about 85 degrees C the cells leak faster, so the controller must refresh more often, which uses power and steals bandwidth, and further up you hit the part's maximum. In this model the DRAM reaches 85 degrees at about 4.2 watts of SoC power, before the SoC reaches 105 at 4.8 watts. So the memory is the limiting factor, and that's why PoP is used for mobile-class processors and not for high-power ones.

Mitigation: improve the board path with more copper and thermal vias, add a thermal interface and a graphite sheet or metal spreader over the memory to the case, lower the SoC's power with DVFS and throttling, and place the stack away from other heat sources. If that's not enough, go to a different architecture: memory beside the SoC or on a shared substrate rather than stacked on top."
:::

::: terms
- **Thermal resistance ($R_\theta$)**: temperature rise per watt, K/W.
- **$R_{JA}$ / $R_{JC}$ / $R_{JB}$**: junction-to-ambient / case / board thermal resistances.
- **Thermal circuit**: analogy where temperature is voltage, heat flow is current and $R_\theta$ is resistance.
- **Heat spreader**: high-conductivity layer (copper, graphite) that spreads heat laterally.
- **TIM (thermal interface material)**: pad, grease or gel filling gaps between surfaces to reduce contact resistance.
- **DVFS**: dynamic voltage and frequency scaling, a way to cut power when hot.
- **Refresh**: periodic rewriting of DRAM cells; needed more often at higher temperature.
- **Thermal throttling**: automatic reduction of performance to keep temperature within limits.
- **Thermal via**: plated hole conducting heat from a pad to inner or back copper layers.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the memory on top traps heat so the SoC gets hotter".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Builds the **thermal resistance network** with up and down paths and **computes** the change (12.3 → 16.7 K/W, 37 → 50 K at 3 W).
- Notes the **memory is also heated**, estimates its temperature, and links to **refresh and limits**.
- Identifies **which limit binds first** (DRAM at about 4.2 W vs SoC at 4.8 W in the example).
- Lists **mitigations** by path: board copper and vias, top spreader/TIM, throttling, placement, alternative packaging.
- Mentions the **transient** aspect (thermal capacitance, bursts) and that datasheet $R_{JA}$ is board dependent.
- Flags all numbers as **illustrative** and cites vendor models.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"PoP always overheats."** It raises temperature, but within the power range of mobile SoCs the design works with proper thermal management.
- **Ignoring the DRAM.** The memory's own limits (and refresh penalty) are often the binding constraint.
- **Using datasheet $R_{JA}$ as is.** It's measured on a standard board; your board and enclosure change it.
- **Adding a heat sink over the memory and assuming the SoC is fixed.** It helps the up path only; the board path and package internals still dominate.
- **Treating heat as only going up.** In a PoP stack much of it goes down into the board.
- **Forgetting transient behaviour.** Short bursts are absorbed by thermal mass; sustained loads are what hit the steady-state limit.
:::

## What the memory is exposed to

Memory junction temperature is not the SoC's: it's lower, between $T_J$ and ambient, set by position in the up path. With the memory bottom reached after $R_{mem}+R_{ca} = 30$ K/W of resistance from ambient: $T_{mem,bottom} = T_A + Q_{up}\times30$. At $P = 3$ W: 68 °C; the top surface (after only $R_{ca} = 15$ K/W) is at 46.5 °C. Add the memory's own heating (for example 0.4 W through about 22 K/W of path to ambient adds about 9 K).

> [!NOTE]
> The values 85 °C and 105 °C are used as typical references: DRAM normal-temperature operation is often specified up to about 85 °C case and an extended range above that with higher refresh, and mobile SoCs have junction limits around 100 to 125 °C, but each part's datasheet decides.

## Why DRAM cares about temperature

DRAM stores charge on a capacitor; leakage through the access transistor and junction rises exponentially with temperature (retention time falls roughly by half for each 10 to 15 K rise in many measurements, though it varies). Standards respond by requiring a higher refresh rate above a threshold temperature (commonly twice the refresh rate above about 85 °C). More refresh means more power, since refresh energy scales with rate, and less availability, because the banks are busy refreshing. At the temperature limit, correct operation isn't guaranteed. Memory controllers read the device's temperature sensor (or an on-package sensor) and adapt the refresh rate; operating systems throttle the SoC when the stack gets too hot.

## Mitigation by path

**Board path (down).** More copper area and planes under and around the stack, thermal vias in the board near the ground and power balls, thicker copper (2 oz), a thermally conductive path to the chassis, and avoiding other heat sources nearby. This improves $R_{down}$, which carries about half of the heat.

**Top path (up).** A thermal interface pad or graphite sheet on the memory top connecting to a metal frame, shield or the device back cover; a vapour chamber in high-end phones. This reduces $R_{ca}$. For example, cutting $R_{ca}$ from 15 to 8 K/W lowers the up path from 35 to 28 K/W and $R_{JA}$ from 16.7 to 14.9 K/W, a 10% improvement; the SoC at 3 W runs about 5.3 K cooler.

**Package path.** Use thinner memory packages and mould compounds with better conductivity, copper-pillar or heat-spreading features, or an exposed-die option. Some stacks put a heat spreader in the mould between the packages.

**System level.** Throttle the SoC (DVFS) when the DRAM sensor or SoC sensor reports high temperature, change workload scheduling to spread heat over time, and use the thermal mass of the device: short bursts of high power are absorbed by the heat capacity of the stack. If the thermal time constant is $\tau = R_{JA}C_{th}$ with $C_{th} \approx 0.5$ J/K for the package, $\tau \approx 8$ s, so a sub-second burst doesn't reach steady state.

## When PoP isn't thermally viable

Above about 5 to 10 W of sustained SoC power with a small enclosure, or in a hot ambient environment, the stack can't keep the DRAM under its limit without large copper and active cooling. Then designers move the memory beside the SoC (on the PCB, or on the same substrate in a memory-on-package arrangement), or use a different architecture (HBM next to the processor on an interposer with its own cooling path). [[q0647]] compares alternatives, and [[q0540]] gives board-level thermal measures.

## Measuring it

Use on-die and DRAM temperature sensors read through the OS, a thermal camera on a bare board (without the case), thermocouples on the top of the memory package, and compare against the thermal model. Calibrate your own $R_{JA}$: apply a known power and measure $\Delta T$. Be wary that cameras see the top surface, which is 20 K cooler than the SoC junction in the example (46.5 versus 75.1 °C), so the camera under-reads. Use the package characterisation parameter $\psi_{JT}$ from the SoC vendor to relate top-surface temperature to junction temperature.

::: applications
- **Phone and tablet thermal design**: sustained performance limits are often set by the SoC-DRAM stack.
- **Gaming phones and handheld consoles**: vapour chambers and graphite sheets over the stack.
- **Compact single-board computers**: heat spreaders or thermal pads on the memory top surface.
- **Automotive infotainment**: higher ambient temperatures push DRAM to extended temperature grades and tougher cooling.
- **Thermal throttling firmware**: DRAM temperature polling and refresh-rate control.
- **Alternative packages**: memory-on-package and chiplet designs justified partly by thermal behaviour.
:::

::: assumptions
All resistances, ambient temperature, power values and the 15 K/W memory-package figure are illustrative, chosen so the numbers are easy to follow; the method (parallel thermal paths with the memory in the series up-path) is the point. Real stacks have lateral heat spreading, multiple interface layers, and heat generated in the memory; a full analysis uses a vendor compact thermal model or finite-element simulation. The 85 °C and 105 °C thresholds are typical for LPDDR-class memory and mobile SoCs but vary. Steady-state analysis ignores thermal capacitance; real devices benefit from bursty usage. Heat sinks or vapour chambers can change the top-path resistance by a large factor; the answer would then be different. A valid alternative statement is that the memory also acts as a thermal spreader for the SoC's top surface, but with the plastic and DRAM stack this is usually a smaller effect than the added resistance.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the SoC get hotter with PoP?
The memory package is in series with the top heat path, so it adds thermal resistance there. The heat that would have gone up now goes mostly down into the board, whose path is longer and more resistive. In the example, the top path rises from 20 to 35 K/W, and the overall junction-to-ambient resistance goes from 12.3 to 16.7 K/W, so at 3 W the junction rises 50 K instead of 37 K.

### How does temperature affect DRAM?
Higher temperature increases cell leakage, shortening retention time, so the controller must refresh more often above a threshold (commonly around 85 °C). More refresh uses power and reduces available bandwidth, and above the maximum rating correct operation isn't guaranteed. Controllers read the memory's temperature sensor and adjust refresh and may throttle the SoC.

### What can a designer do to improve PoP thermals?
Improve the board path with more copper, planes and thermal vias; add a thermal pad or graphite sheet from the memory's top to the case or a shield; cut SoC power with DVFS and throttling; keep the stack away from other heat sources; and choose thinner or higher-conductivity packages. If sustained power is too high, change the architecture to put the memory beside the SoC.

### Why not put a heat sink directly on the SoC in a PoP?
Because the memory package sits where the heat sink would go. You can attach a heat spreader over the memory, but the heat still has to go through the memory package, or you need a package variant that exposes the SoC die in a cavity (rare). That's why the design usually relies on the board and the top-surface path through the memory.

### How would you check the thermal model against hardware?
Run a known SoC power load (a stress test), log the SoC and DRAM sensors, and measure the top surface with a thermocouple or camera. Compute $\Delta T/P$ for the junction and compare with the model's 16.7 K/W; adjust the resistances. Use $\psi_{JT}$ from the vendor to convert the top-surface reading to junction temperature, because the camera sees only the case.

### Scenario: your product is stable at 2.5 W but at 4 W the DRAM begins to throw errors after a minute. What do you investigate?
First check the DRAM temperature sensor against its limit and refresh settings; at 4 W my model says the DRAM bottom is about 82 °C at 25 °C ambient and over 85 °C at warmer ambient, where refresh doubles and timing margins shrink. Then look for hot spots from neighbouring parts, enclosure airflow, and the contact quality of any thermal pad. Fixes: better top-side conduction with a pad and spreader, more board copper, lowering the sustained power with throttling, and checking memory timing margins at temperature. If the problem is the specification, use a memory part with an extended temperature range.
:::
