::: basics
### In plain words
An LDO needs very few outside parts. The minimum is an input capacitor, an output capacitor, and, for an adjustable part, two resistors that set the voltage. The output capacitor is not optional, because it is part of the feedback loop. A wrong value or type can make the output oscillate.

### Picture it
Think of each capacitor as a small water tank placed right next to the tap. The input tank gives the regulator a steady supply when it suddenly needs more. The output tank steadies the output and also helps the control loop keep from overshooting. A tank too small, or too far from the tap, will not do its job. The analogy does not show that a ceramic tank shrinks when the voltage is applied.

### Words you need
- **Input capacitor**: a capacitor near the IN pin, typically about 1 µF, that supplies fast current changes.
- **Output capacitor**: a capacitor near the OUT pin that the datasheet requires for stability.
- **ESR**: a capacitor's small internal series resistance, which some older LDOs need within a range.
- **Effective capacitance**: what the capacitor really provides at its operating voltage.
- **Feedback divider**: two resistors that set the output on an adjustable part ([[q0278]]).

### The simplest example
Say you want 3.3 V from an adjustable LDO with a 0.8 V reference. Use $R_2=10$ kΩ and $R_1\approx31.6$ kΩ, which gives $0.8\times(1+3.16)\approx3.33$ V. The divider draws about 80 µA. Add a 1 µF input capacitor and a datasheet-approved output capacitor, both close to the pins, and you are done.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the optional parts, such as enable, power-good, and noise-reduction capacitors, and how to place and size everything.
:::

::: testing
Whether you have **actually used an LDO**, or at least read the typical-application page of a datasheet. The expected list is: **input capacitor, output capacitor** (with the right value and ESR for stability), and for adjustable parts a **feedback divider**. Strong candidates add the optional pins (enable, power-good with pull-up, noise-reduction or soft-start capacitor, feed-forward capacitor), reverse-current protection, and thermal considerations (copper pour). They also know that the *output capacitor is part of the control loop* and that ceramic capacitors lose capacitance under DC bias.
:::

::: why
Most LDO problems on first boards are not the LDO, they are the parts around it: an output capacitor that is too small or the wrong type (output oscillation), an input capacitor placed far from the pin (ringing, noise), a feedback divider with leaky or noisy values, a missing pull-up on the power-good pin, or a part that overheats on a minimal pad. Being able to list and justify the external parts shows you can take an IC from a datasheet to a working schematic, which is a daily task for hardware interns. It also leads straight into capacitor selection ([[q0030]], [[q0032]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Input capacitor**: lowers the source impedance seen by the regulator, supplies fast current edges, and stabilizes the loop when the source is far away. See [[q0032]].
- **Output capacitor**: part of the feedback loop (output pole and ESR zero) and the energy reservoir for load steps. See [[q0278]].
- **Feedback divider**: sets $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$ for adjustable parts. See [[q0008]].
- **Realistic capacitor model**: ESR, ESL, DC-bias derating, temperature. See [[q0030]].
- **Enable and power-good pins**: control and status. PG is usually open-drain and needs a pull-up.
- **Thermal pad / copper**: dissipation $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$ must leave the package. See [[q0282]].
:::

::: answer
"At minimum, an input capacitor and an output capacitor, and if the part is adjustable, a feedback divider.

The input capacitor goes close to the IN pin. It gives the regulator a low-impedance source for fast current changes and keeps the loop stable if the supply is a few centimeters away. A 1 µF ceramic is a typical datasheet value.

The output capacitor goes close to OUT, and it's not optional: it's inside the control loop. The datasheet gives a minimum capacitance and sometimes an ESR range, and if you pick something too small or the wrong type the output can oscillate. Ceramics lose capacitance with DC bias, so I check the effective value at the output voltage, not the nominal.

For an adjustable LDO, I add two resistors from the output to the feedback pin and ground, $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$, with values chosen so the divider current isn't a big part of the quiescent budget. Then the optional things: an enable pin tied to $V_{in}$ or a GPIO, a pull-up on the power-good output if I use it, a small bypass or noise-reduction capacitor if I need low noise or a slow soft-start, a feed-forward capacitor across $R_1$ for better transient response, and if the output could be driven above the input, reverse-current protection. And enough copper under the part to get rid of the heat."
:::

::: technical
**Required**
- $C_{in}$: close to IN/GND, typically 1 µF or more (ceramic X5R/X7R), rated at least $1.25\times V_{in,max}$ with derating. Reduces input impedance; avoids oscillation with long leads or a high-impedance source.
- $C_{out}$: close to OUT/GND, value and ESR within the datasheet's stable range. Modern low-$I_Q$ LDOs are often stable with 1 to 10 µF ceramics. Older PNP/NPN types may *require* ESR in a window (e.g. 0.1 to 5 Ω).

**Adjustable parts**
- $R_1$ (OUT to FB), $R_2$ (FB to GND): $V_{out}=V_{ref}\left(1+\dfrac{R_1}{R_2}\right)$. Divider current $V_{out}/(R_1+R_2)$ should be much larger than the FB pin's bias/leakage current and small compared with the load or standby budget. Example: $V_{ref}=0.8$ V, 3.3 V out, $R_2=10$ kΩ, $R_1=31.6$ kΩ gives 3.328 V and 79 µA of divider current.
- Optional feed-forward capacitor $C_{ff}$ across $R_1$: adds a zero for phase lead and faster transient, with a value from the datasheet.

**Optional / feature pins**
- EN: tie to $V_{in}$ (always on) via a resistor if $V_{in}$ can exceed the EN pin's rating, or drive from logic. Do not leave floating.
- PG (power-good): open-drain, needs a pull-up resistor to a logic rail.
- NR/BYP/SS: capacitor to ground that filters the reference noise and sets soft-start rate. Larger value gives lower noise and slower startup.
- Reverse-current protection: a series Schottky or an anti-series FET if $V_{out}$ can exceed $V_{in}$ (battery backup, input shorted).

**Board**
- Thermal: copper pour and vias under the exposed pad. $T_J=T_A+\theta_{JA}P_D$.
- Layout: short, wide traces $C_{in}\to$ IN/GND, $C_{out}\to$ OUT/GND, Kelvin sense of FB to the load if needed.
:::

::: long
"For an LDO IC, the parts around it come in three groups: required, adjustable-only, and optional.

Required are the two capacitors. The input capacitor sits right at the IN pin, typically 1 µF or more of ceramic. It provides the fast current the regulator draws when the load steps, and it keeps the regulator stable if the upstream supply is far away. The output capacitor sits right at OUT, and it's part of the feedback loop. The datasheet specifies a minimum capacitance and, for some parts, an ESR range. If I use too little, or a capacitor that's lost most of its value under DC bias, the output can ring or oscillate. So I check effective capacitance at the output voltage and temperature.

If it's the adjustable version, two resistors from the output to the FB pin and ground set the voltage with $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$. For a 0.8 V reference and 3.3 V out I'd use something like 31.6 kΩ and 10 kΩ, which draws about 80 µA. In a standby-sensitive design I'd scale those up to hundreds of kΩ, watching leakage and noise.

Optional parts depend on the features. EN gets tied to $V_{in}$ through a resistor, or to a GPIO. Power-good is open drain, so it needs a pull-up. A noise-reduction or soft-start capacitor on a bypass pin lowers output noise and slows startup. A feed-forward capacitor across the top feedback resistor can improve transient response. If the output can be held above the input, say by a battery backup, I'd add a Schottky or use a part with reverse-current blocking. Finally, a good copper pour under the thermal pad, because the part dissipates $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$."
:::

::: terms
- **Input capacitor ($C_{in}$)**: decouples the source, placed at IN.
- **Output capacitor ($C_{out}$)**: loop element and charge reservoir, placed at OUT.
- **ESR window**: range of ESR for which an older LDO is stable.
- **DC-bias derating**: capacitance loss of ceramics under applied voltage.
- **Feedback divider**: $R_1/R_2$ network for adjustable parts.
- **Power-good (PG)**: open-drain status output.
- **Noise-reduction / bypass (NR/BYP) pin**: filters the reference and often sets soft-start.
- **Feed-forward capacitor ($C_{ff}$)**: across $R_1$, adds a zero and improves transient response.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "input and output capacitors".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains *why* each capacitor is there, and that the output cap is inside the loop with a stability range.

- Gives the adjustable divider equation with a number, and the divider-current trade-off.

:::

::: mistakes
- **Skipping the output capacitor** or using a value below the minimum. The loop can oscillate.
- **Using a capacitor whose effective value collapses**: a 1 µF 0402 X5R at 3.3 V may retain far less than 1 µF.
- **Putting the input capacitor far from the pin**, so the lead inductance defeats its purpose.
- **Leaving EN floating**, so the part may turn on or off randomly.
- **No pull-up on an open-drain PG**, so it never goes high.
- **Using high-value divider resistors near leakage-sensitive FB pins**, so board leakage or bias current shifts $V_{out}$.
- **No thermal plan.** The part is fine at 100 mA and in thermal shutdown at 800 mA.
:::

## Input capacitor

The LDO draws whatever current the load demands, with fast edges, and the loop is only fast up to its bandwidth. The input capacitor supplies the difference. It also lowers the source impedance seen by the regulator. A long lead to a supply adds series inductance, say 20 nH per 2 cm, and with a ceramic input capacitor that forms a resonant tank that, if the regulator's input impedance looks like a negative resistance (some designs), can ring. The rule: place $C_{in}$ within a couple of millimetres of IN and GND. Value: datasheets typically specify 1 µF minimum; if the upstream source is far or a switching regulator, add more.

For **hot-plug** from a supply, an undamped ceramic with lead inductance can overshoot the plug-in voltage by up to about 2x, so a TVS and/or some series resistance or a bulk electrolytic is used when the input rating is tight.

## Output capacitor: part of the loop

As in [[q0278]], the output pole $1/(2\pi R_{out}C_{out})$ and the capacitor's ESR zero shape the loop's phase margin. The datasheet will state one of:

- **Ceramic-stable** (low ESR OK): minimum capacitance, say 1 µF, and a maximum.
- **ESR-window** (older bipolar-pass LDOs): ESR must be within, for example, 0.1 to 5 Ω, so a ceramic alone can oscillate, and a small series resistor or tantalum/polymer capacitor is needed.
- **Capacitor-free**: tiny output capacitance allowed but load transient suffers.

Beyond stability, $C_{out}$ sets the load-step droop. A first estimate: $\Delta V\approx\Delta I/(2\pi f_cC_{out})$ where $f_c$ is the loop bandwidth. For a 250 mA step with $f_c=200$ kHz and 2.2 µF: $0.25/(2\pi\times2\times10^5\times2.2\times10^{-6})=90$ mV.

**DC-bias derating**: take a 10 µF, 6.3 V, 0603 X5R. At 3.3 V bias it might retain about 50% (5 µF effective), and with a temperature and tolerance allowance maybe 40%. If the stable range minimum is 4.7 µF, this part fails.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The fraction of capacitance retained at bias depends strongly on case size, voltage rating, dielectric and vendor. I use 50% as an illustration. Always read the capacitor maker's DC-bias curve for the exact part, and compare against the LDO datasheet's minimum *effective* capacitance.

## The feedback divider

For adjustable parts: $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$ ([[q0008]]). Design choices:

- **Absolute value.** Larger resistors save divider current (3.3 V across 41.6 kΩ is 79 µA, which may exceed the LDO's own $I_Q$ of 20 µA!). But large resistors raise thermal noise, make the FB node sensitive to leakage across a dirty board and to the FB pin bias current, and slow the response with the pin capacitance. A common compromise is a divider current from a few µA to 100 µA, with the FB bias current at least 100x lower.
- **Example, low standby.** $V_{ref}=0.8$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $R_2=100$ kΩ: $R_1=100\text{ k}\times(3.3/0.8-1)=312.5$ kΩ, giving 8 µA.
- **Tolerance.** 1% resistors give about 1.5% worst-case shift of $V_{out}$ at 3.3 V from 0.8 V; add the reference tolerance.
- **Feed-forward capacitor.** A small capacitor $C_{ff}$ in parallel with $R_1$ adds a zero at $1/(2\pi R_1C_{ff})$, improving phase margin and transient response, but the value is part-specific (follow the datasheet).

## Feature pins

- **EN**: logic-level enable. If it is tied to $V_{in}$ and $V_{in}$ can exceed the pin's absolute maximum, use a resistor or a partition. A floating EN is undefined unless the datasheet says there's an internal pull-down.
- **PG**: open-drain output that goes low when $V_{out}$ is out of regulation. Needs a pull-up resistor (10 to 100 kΩ) to a logic rail. Used for power sequencing and reset ([[q0171]]).
- **NR/BYP/SS**: a capacitor (for example 10 nF to 1 µF) filters the bandgap reference noise and sets a soft-start time constant. Bigger means lower noise and slower start, and the startup time must be fast enough for sequencing.
- **Current limit / foldback and thermal shutdown** are internal. No external parts, but they affect behavior under overload.

## Protection and reverse current

A PMOS pass device has a body diode from drain (OUT) to source (IN). If $V_{out}>V_{in}$, for example a backup battery on the output while the main supply is off, the body diode conducts backward into the input rail. Parts with reverse-current protection use a back-to-back switch. If yours doesn't, add a series Schottky (with its dropout penalty) or a blocking FET.

For inputs from outside the board add a TVS and fuse as appropriate ([[q0265]]).

## Thermal and layout

- Exposed pad soldered to a copper area with thermal vias. $T_J=T_A+\theta_{JA}P_D$ with $P_D=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}+V_{in}I_Q$ ([[q0282]]).
- $C_{in}$ and $C_{out}$ placed at the pins with short, wide ground returns.
- FB trace short, away from SW nodes and inductors, with the divider close to the IC.
- Remote loads: add local decoupling at the load, and if needed, a Kelvin sense of the output for the FB divider.

- **Microcontroller board**: fixed 3.3 V LDO with $C_{in}$, $C_{out}$ only.
- **ADC reference supply**: adjustable low-noise LDO with NR capacitor and RC post-filter.
- **Battery device**: ultra-low-$I_Q$ LDO with high-value divider and an EN driven by the system.

- **RF module**: LDO with PG, NR and extra decoupling at the load.

::: assumptions
- Values are **typical datasheet-style** (1 µF input, 1 to 10 µF output). Each IC specifies its own minimum capacitance and ESR range, and the datasheet overrides anything here.
- I assumed a **PMOS-pass, ceramic-stable** LDO. Older bipolar-pass parts (ESR window) and capacitor-free parts have different rules.
- A **fixed-output** LDO needs no divider. A **tracking** or **programmable** LDO may use a reference input instead.
- EN, PG and NR/SS pin names and presence **vary** by vendor.
- Reverse-current behavior is part-dependent: some LDOs include reverse blocking.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the output capacitor not optional?
It is inside the feedback loop: it sets the output pole and with its ESR a zero, so it determines phase margin. It also supplies current in the first microseconds of a load step. Without it, or with too small a value, the loop can oscillate, and the datasheet's stability guarantee no longer holds.

### What is the effective capacitance of a 4.7 µF X5R ceramic at 3.3 V bias?
It depends on case size and voltage rating. A 4.7 µF in a larger case rated 10 V might keep 60 to 70%, a small 6.3 V case might keep 30 to 40%. The design check is: effective capacitance at $V_{out}$, at the maximum temperature, and with tolerance, still above the datasheet's minimum. Read the vendor's DC-bias curve.

### How would you choose the divider resistors for a battery-powered LDO?
Pick $R_2$ so the divider current is a small fraction of the standby budget but still much larger than the FB bias/leakage: for a 1 µA total quiescent target, $R_1+R_2$ maybe 3.3 MΩ at 3.3 V, but at that level leakage and noise matter, so I'd use a fixed-output LDO or a part with an internal divider instead. If adjustable, choose $R_2$ between 100 kΩ and 1 MΩ, use a $C_{ff}$ as recommended, keep the FB trace short and guarded.

### What happens if EN is left floating?
The pin may sit at an indeterminate level, so the LDO can start up randomly, chatter, or draw extra current. Some parts have an internal weak pull-down, which keeps them off. Tie it to $V_{in}$ through a resistor, or drive it with a defined logic level.

### Scenario: your LDO output rings at about 100 kHz when you swap the output capacitor for a smaller package. Why?
The smaller-package ceramic has less effective capacitance (more DC-bias loss) and maybe lower ESR. The output pole moves up in frequency while the ESR zero moves up too, so the phase margin falls. Check against the datasheet's stable range, add capacitance in parallel, or add a small series resistor (say 0.1 to 0.5 Ω) if the part requires ESR, and verify with a load-step test.
:::
