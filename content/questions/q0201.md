::: testing
Whether you can name the standard buck power stage (a half bridge of two NFETs with synchronous rectification) **and justify each choice with numbers**, not just recite it. The interviewer is listening for:

1. You know "power bridge" here means the switching leg that ties the switch node to $V_{in}$ or to ground, and that a buck needs only one leg (a half bridge), not an H-bridge.
2. You can say why the low side is a FET instead of a diode (conduction loss), and why both FETs are N-channel (silicon physics: electrons beat holes).
3. You know what that choice costs: a bootstrap or charge-pump driver, dead time, and the shoot-through risk. Strong candidates mention the cost before being asked.
:::

::: why
Every modern point-of-load converter, from the 3 A buck on a microcontroller board to the multiphase stage feeding a CPU, uses some version of this bridge. Questions 201 to 210 are all follow-ups on this one design decision: sync vs diode, NFET vs PFET, how to drive the high side, how to avoid cross-conduction. If you can explain why the bridge looks the way it does, the rest of the Power Bridge section becomes consequences rather than facts to memorize. It is also the part of the buck that dominates efficiency, so it is where the layout, thermal and component-selection conversations all start.
:::

::: concepts
- **Half bridge**: two switches in series from $V_{in}$ to ground; the midpoint is the switch node (SW). A buck uses one leg, a motor H-bridge uses two. See [[q0175]].
- **Freewheel path**: the inductor current must keep flowing when the high-side switch opens, so something must conduct from ground to SW ([[q0197]]).
- **Synchronous rectification**: replacing that diode with a FET driven complementary to the high side ([[q0203]]).
- **MOSFET $R_{DS(on)}$ and silicon area**: for a given die area, an NFET has roughly 2 to 3 times lower on-resistance than a PFET because electron mobility is higher than hole mobility.
- **Bootstrap gate drive**: an NFET on the high side needs a gate voltage above $V_{in}$ ([[q0206]]).
- **Dead time**: the deliberate gap where both FETs are off so they never short $V_{in}$ to ground ([[q0207]]).
:::

::: answer
"The usual choice is a half bridge with two N-channel MOSFETs and synchronous rectification. The high-side NFET connects the switch node to $V_{in}$, and the low-side NFET connects it to ground. They're driven complementary, with a little dead time between them.

There are two reasons. First, the low side is a FET instead of a diode because a diode drops a fixed 0.3 to 0.7 V whenever the inductor current flows through it, while a FET drops $I \cdot R_{DS(on)}$, which can be tens of millivolts. On a 12 V to 3.3 V converter at 2 A, a Schottky wastes about 0.65 W out of 6.6 W, a 10 milliohm FET about 30 mW. And the lower the output voltage, the longer the low side conducts, so the win gets bigger.

Second, both are NFETs because for the same die area an NFET has about two to three times lower on-resistance than a PFET, since electrons are more mobile than holes. Same resistance means a smaller, cheaper device with less gate charge and capacitance.

The price is that the high-side NFET needs a gate voltage above the input, so you need a bootstrap capacitor or charge pump, and you need dead-time control so the two FETs never conduct together."
:::

::: technical
**The topology.** The power bridge is the leg that alternately connects the switch node to $V_{in}$ (high-side FET $Q_1$ on, $Q_2$ off, duration $DT$) and to ground ($Q_1$ off, $Q_2$ on, duration $(1-D)T$). The inductor and output capacitor average SW to $V_{out} = D\,V_{in}$.

**Why a FET in place of the diode.** The diode carries $I_L$ for a fraction $(1-D)$ of the period, so $P_D \approx (1-D)\,I_{out}V_f$. The low-side FET dissipates $P_{Q2} \approx (1-D)\,I_{L,rms}^2 R_{DS(on)}$. Equating them, the FET only loses when $I \cdot R_{DS(on)} > V_f$, which for a 10 mΩ FET and a 0.45 V Schottky is 45 A.

Worked example ($12\to 3.3$ V, 2 A, $D=0.275$): Schottky $0.725\times2\times0.45 = 0.65$ W, efficiency from this term alone $91\%$. 10 mΩ FET: $0.725\times4.09\times0.01 = 0.030$ W, efficiency $99.6\%$ from this term. At $V_{out}=1$ V the diode alone caps efficiency near $71\%$.

**Why NFET on both sides.** In the triode region $R_{DS(on)} \propto \dfrac{L}{\mu\,C_{ox}\,W\,(V_{GS}-V_{th})}$. Electron mobility in silicon is roughly 2 to 3 times hole mobility, so a PFET needs 2 to 3 times the width $W$ (die area) for the same $R_{DS(on)}$. Area drives gate charge $Q_g$, output capacitance $C_{oss}$ and cost, all of which show up as switching loss and price.

**What it costs.**
- High-side gate drive above $V_{in}$ (bootstrap or charge pump).
- Dead time, and body-diode conduction during it.
- Cross-conduction (shoot-through) risk, including dv/dt-induced turn-on.
- Possible reverse inductor current at light load unless diode emulation is used.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The "2 to 3 times" NFET/PFET area ratio depends on process and voltage class. Treat it as a typical silicon range, not a constant.
:::

::: short

The low-side FET replaces the diode because $I\cdot R_{DS(on)}$ is far below a diode's fixed 0.3 to 0.7 V drop, which matters most at low output voltage and high current. NFETs on both sides because electrons are more mobile than holes, so an NFET has about two to three times lower on-resistance per area. The cost is a bootstrap driver and dead-time control."
:::

::: long
"The standard buck power stage is a half bridge made of two N-channel MOSFETs, run with synchronous rectification. The high-side NFET connects the switch node to the input, the low-side NFET connects it to ground, and the controller drives them complementary with a short dead time so they never overlap.

I'll explain it as three decisions. First, why a half bridge: a buck only needs to connect one node, the switch node, alternately to $V_{in}$ and ground. The inductor and capacitor do the averaging. An H-bridge is for motors where you need to reverse polarity.

Second, why a FET on the low side instead of a diode. When the high side opens, the inductor current needs a path. A diode gives you a fixed forward drop, 0.3 to 0.7 V. A FET gives you $I$ times $R_{DS(on)}$. At 12 V in, 3.3 V out and 2 A, a Schottky burns about 0.65 W of a 6.6 W output. A 10 milliohm FET burns about 30 milliwatts. And the effect grows as $V_{out}$ drops, because the low side conducts for $1-D$ of the cycle.

Third, why both NFETs. Electrons are more mobile than holes, so for the same die area an NFET has roughly two to three times lower on-resistance. That gives a smaller die with less gate charge and capacitance. A PFET high side is simpler to drive, but you pay in area.

Then the costs. The high-side NFET needs its gate pulled above $V_{in}$, so you use a bootstrap capacitor or a charge pump. You need dead time so there's no shoot-through, and during that dead time the body diode conducts, which costs a bit of efficiency and reverse-recovery charge. For very low-cost or high-$V_{out}$ converters, a diode or a PFET can still be the right answer."
:::

::: terms
- **Power bridge / half bridge**: two series switches from $V_{in}$ to ground whose midpoint is the switch node.
- **Switch node (SW)**: the midpoint node, pulsing between about $V_{in}$ and about 0 V.
- **Synchronous rectification**: using a FET (low side) in place of the freewheel diode.
- **Asynchronous buck**: a buck with a diode as the low-side element.
- **$R_{DS(on)}$**: drain-source on-resistance; sets conduction loss $I^2R$.
- **Bootstrap**: a capacitor charged while SW is low that floats the high-side driver above SW.
- **Dead time**: the gap with both FETs off, to avoid shoot-through.
- **Body diode**: the intrinsic drain-source diode of a MOSFET, which carries current during dead time.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "two MOSFETs, half bridge, synchronous" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates the three decisions (half bridge, FET vs diode, NFET vs PFET) and justifies each with a number.
- Quantifies the diode penalty at the given operating point (for example 0.65 W at 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A) and notes it grows as $V_{out}$ falls.
- Gives the physical reason for NFETs (mobility, area, $Q_g$), not just "NFETs are better".
- Names the costs unprompted: bootstrap drive, dead time, body-diode loss, light-load reverse current.
- Says when the answer changes: PFET high side for 100% duty and simple drive at low voltage, diode for low cost or high $V_{out}$, GaN for high frequency.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "H-bridge".** A buck uses a half bridge. An H-bridge is two half bridges and is used to reverse a load ([[q0175]]).
- **"The low side is a FET because FETs switch faster than diodes."** The reason is conduction loss, $I\cdot R_{DS(on)}$ vs a fixed $V_f$. Speed is secondary.
- **Claiming PFETs are "worse" without a reason.** The reason is hole mobility: more area per milliohm. PFETs are still valid when drive simplicity matters.
- **Forgetting the high-side drive problem.** An NFET source sits at SW, so it needs $V_{GS}$ above $V_{in}$. "Just drive it with 5 V" does not work.
- **Assuming sync is always better.** At light load a sync FET can allow reverse inductor current, and the added gate-drive loss can lower light-load efficiency unless diode emulation or PFM is used.
:::

::: deep
## What "power bridge" means here

A buck converter needs one thing from its switching network: connect the inductor's input end to $V_{in}$ for a time $DT$, and to ground for the remaining $(1-D)T$. That is a single leg of two series switches, a **half bridge**. An H-bridge is two such legs, used when the load needs both polarities ([[q0175]]).

There are four sensible ways to build the two switches:

| Bridge | High side | Low side | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asynchronous NFET | NFET (bootstrapped) | Schottky diode | Cheap or high-$V_{out}$ designs, old controllers |
| Asynchronous PFET | PFET | Schottky diode | Very simple low-power parts |
| Complementary | PFET | NFET | Low-voltage, up to 100% duty, simple drive |
| **Synchronous NFET** | NFET (bootstrapped) | NFET | The default for most modern buck ICs and controllers |

## Decision 1: FET instead of diode

When $Q_1$ turns off, $L$ keeps forcing $I_L$ in the same direction ([[q0185]]). SW is pulled below ground until something conducts. A diode conducts at a drop of $V_f$; a FET channel conducts at $I\cdot R_{DS(on)}$. The low side carries current for $(1-D)$ of every cycle, so:

$$P_{\text{diode}} = (1-D)\,I_{out}V_f,\qquad P_{\text{FET}} = (1-D)\,I_{L,rms}^2\,R_{DS(on)}$$

It helps to turn both into an "equivalent voltage drop" in series with the load: the diode is a constant $V_f$, the FET is $I\,R_{DS(on)}$. For 2 A and 10 mΩ that is 20 mV, versus 450 mV.

> [!NOTE]
> This is why sync rectification became standard as core voltages dropped from 5 V toward 1 V. A 0.45 V diode drop is nothing at 24 V out, and a disaster at 1 V out.

## Decision 2: both NFETs

In the triode region the channel resistance is

$$R_{DS(on)} \approx \frac{L}{\mu\,C_{ox}\,W\,(V_{GS}-V_{th})}$$

For identical geometry, $R_{DS(on)} \propto 1/\mu$. Electron mobility in silicon is higher than hole mobility by roughly a factor of 2 to 3 (process-dependent). To match an NFET, a PFET must be 2 to 3 times wider. Wider means more gate charge $Q_g$ (more drive loss and slower edges), more $C_{oss}$ (more switching loss), and more silicon cost.

Power MOSFETs are usually compared by figure of merit $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_g$ (conduction vs switching trade) and $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{oss}$. NFETs win both. That is why even the high side, which is harder to drive, is usually an NFET (see [[q0204]] for the full comparison).

## Decision 3: what the NFET high side costs

The high-side NFET's source is SW, which swings from about 0 V to $V_{in}$. To hold $V_{GS} \approx 5$ V when SW is at 12 V the gate must reach about 17 V. The standard fix is a **bootstrap**: a diode and capacitor that charge to about 5 V while SW is low, then ride SW upward ([[q0206]]). Extras that come with the bridge:

1. **Dead time** so $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ are never on together ([[q0207]]).
2. **Body-diode conduction** during dead time and its reverse recovery ([[q0208]], [[q0209]], [[q0210]]).
3. **Light-load behavior**: a fully synchronous bridge lets $I_L$ go negative, pulling energy out of $C_{out}$. Many ICs use diode emulation (turn $Q_2$ off when $I_L$ reaches zero) or PFM.

## When a different bridge is right

- **Diode (async)**: high $V_{out}$ relative to the diode drop, tiny cost budget, or very low current where gate-drive loss would dominate.
- **PFET high side**: input of a few volts or less where no bootstrap is wanted, or where 100% duty (low dropout) matters, because a PFET can hold on indefinitely.
- **GaN/SiC**: high-frequency or high-voltage stages; GaN has no body diode and very low $Q_g$ but needs careful dead-time control.

::: applications
- **Integrated synchronous buck regulators** (the common 3 A to 6 A, 4.5 V to 17 V class): a monolithic NFET/NFET half bridge with an integrated bootstrap diode or switch, usually with a small external bootstrap capacitor on a BOOT pin.
- **CPU/GPU core supplies**: multiphase controllers with external smart-power-stage or DrMOS devices holding the NFET pair plus driver in one package.
- **Battery-powered devices at 5 V in or less**: some parts use a PFET high side to allow 100% duty as the battery discharges.
- **USB-C and 12 V board rails**: 12 V to 3.3 V or 1.8 V buck stages in routers and FPGA boards, all sync NFET.
- **Wide-input (24 V to 60 V) and automotive pre-regulators**: synchronous parts dominate, but asynchronous versions with an external Schottky are still sold where cost and simplicity win and the output voltage is high.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM, fixed frequency, good gate drive.** At light load in DCM, the conduction-loss argument weakens; gate-drive loss, $Q_{oss}$ and quiescent current dominate.
- **Silicon MOSFETs.** With GaN the "body diode" is a channel conduction with a 2 to 3 V reverse drop, and PFETs are not available, so the bridge is GaN/GaN.
- **Low-to-mid voltage.** At several hundred volts the diode (SiC Schottky) can again be preferable because of FET body-diode recovery.
- **"Often implemented" means by volume.** Valid alternative answers exist for specific products: PFET/NFET complementary for low-voltage simplicity, and a diode for cheap high-$V_{out}$ converters. A good answer names the default and its alternatives.
:::

::: followups
### Why not a PFET on the high side, since it's easier to drive?
It is easier: pulling the gate down to $V_{in}-V_{GS}$ turns it on, and to $V_{in}$ turns it off, with no bootstrap. And it supports 100% duty. The cost is die area: for equal $R_{DS(on)}$ a PFET needs about 2 to 3 times the area of an NFET, so more $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$, more switching loss at high frequency. It is a good fit at low $V_{in}$ and modest frequency; see [[q0204]].

### Why is the half bridge enough? When would you need an H-bridge?
The buck only needs SW to be at $V_{in}$ or ground, with the LC filter producing a positive average. An H-bridge adds a second leg so the load or inductor can see both polarities, as in motor drives or a 4-switch buck-boost.

### At what current does a sync FET stop beating a Schottky?
When $I\cdot R_{DS(on)} = V_f$. For 10 mΩ and 0.45 V that is 45 A, far beyond typical designs. Real parts also have switching and gate-drive loss, so at very light load the diode (or diode emulation) can still win overall.

### What happens at light load with a fully synchronous bridge?
The ripple current is independent of load, so once $I_{out} < \Delta I_L/2$ the inductor current goes negative during the off phase. Energy flows from $C_{out}$ back through the inductor, wasting power as extra circulating current. Controllers use diode emulation to turn off $Q_2$ at zero crossing, or switch to PFM.

### The same bridge, but the converter gets noisy. What could be going on?
Check the SW edges. Fast turn-on of $Q_1$ into the body diode of $Q_2$ gives a reverse-recovery spike and ringing with loop inductance, and the dv/dt can couple into $Q_2$'s gate through $C_{gd}$ ([[q0207]]). A boot resistor, gate resistor or snubber trades some efficiency for lower EMI.
:::

::: interpretations
"Power bridge" in this list means the switching half bridge in the buck. The section note says many questions assume synchronous rectification and some compare it with a diode. If the interviewer means H-bridge (motor driver), the answer changes to "full bridge of four switches", but in a buck context the half bridge is almost certainly what is being asked.
:::
