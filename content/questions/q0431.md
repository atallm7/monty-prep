::: testing
Whether you can see the gate loop as an **RLC circuit** and use second-order system language. The question has three parts, and strong candidates answer each separately:

1. *Why a series resistor*: limit GPIO peak current, control edge rate (dv/dt, EMI, Miller), and damp ringing.
2. *What is parasitic oscillation, and where does it come from*: the loop inductance of bond wire, lead and trace, the FET's $C_{iss}$ and $C_{gd}$, the driver output, and, in linear operation or with paralleled devices, feedback that makes the FET an unintended oscillator.
3. *What the resistor does*: raises damping so the series RLC is not underdamped, $R\ge2\sqrt{L/C}$.
:::

::: why
Gate ringing and oscillation are some of the nastiest "it works on the bench for a while" failures: the gate overshoots past its $\pm20$ V rating, the FET turns on and off randomly at 50 MHz, EMI fails, and parts die. A 10 to 100 Ω resistor that costs nothing prevents most of it. Interviewers use this to test whether you can connect an unglamorous part to the second-order response and to real parasitics ([[q0011]], [[q0047]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Series RLC step response**: damping ratio $\zeta=\dfrac R2\sqrt{\dfrac CL}$; underdamped for $\zeta<1$ ([[q0010]], [[q0012]]).
- **Gate capacitance ($C_{iss}$, $Q_g$)**: the load the driver sees ([[q0102]]).
- **Loop inductance**: bond wire, package lead, PCB trace, plus the return path ([[q0011]]).
- **Miller capacitance**: feeds drain dv/dt back to the gate ([[q0207]]).
- **Driver impedance**: GPIO or gate-driver output resistance adds to the series $R$.
- **dv/dt and EMI**: slower edges mean less high-frequency content ([[q0145]]).
:::

::: answer
"The gate of a MOSFET looks like a capacitor, a nanofarad or so, and the wire to it, the bond wires, the package lead and the trace, has inductance, maybe 10 to 30 nanohenries. A driver with a stiff, fast edge feeding an inductor into a capacitor is a series LC circuit. If there's no resistance in it, it rings, and the ringing frequency is one over two pi root LC, around 35 megahertz for 20 nanohenries and a nanofarad. The gate voltage overshoots well above the drive voltage, maybe 70% for a lightly damped loop, so a 3.3 volt drive could see over 5 volts, and with a 12 volt driver you can approach the gate rating. That ringing can also turn the FET on and off repeatedly, which is parasitic oscillation.

Parasitic oscillation more broadly is when the FET, with its feedback through the gate-drain capacitance and the source inductance, forms an unintended oscillator at tens or hundreds of megahertz. It's especially common when the FET is in its linear region, or when several FETs are paralleled with a shared gate wire.

The series resistor attacks all of that. It adds damping: with enough resistance, $R \ge 2\sqrt{L/C}$, about 9 ohms here, the response is critically damped with no overshoot. It limits the peak current out of the GPIO, since a microcontroller pin can only give 10 or 20 milliamps. And it slows the edge, which reduces dv/dt, EMI and Miller coupling. The price is slower switching: more resistance means longer transitions and more switching loss, so I'd pick the smallest value that gives a clean gate waveform and current within the pin limit."
:::

::: technical
**Gate loop model.** Driver (step $V_D$, output resistance $R_{drv}$), series resistor $R_g$, loop inductance $L_g$ (package + trace + return), and gate capacitance $C_{iss}$ (treated as constant in the threshold region):
$$\frac{V_{gate}(s)}{V_D(s)}=\frac{1}{s^2L_gC+sR_tC+1},\qquad R_t=R_{drv}+R_g.$$
Natural frequency $f_0=\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_gC}}$; characteristic impedance $Z_0=\sqrt{L_g/C}$; damping ratio $\zeta=\dfrac{R_t}{2Z_0}$.

**Example.** $L_g=20$ nH, $C_{iss}=1$ nF: $f_0=35.6$ MHz, $Z_0=4.47$ Ω, critical damping at $R_t=8.9$ Ω.

| $R_t$ (Ω) | $\zeta$ | Overshoot | Peak for a 3.3 V step |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.11 | 70% | 5.6 V |
| 5 | 0.56 | 12% | 3.7 V |
| 10 | 1.12 | none | 3.3 V |
| 22 | 2.46 | none | 3.3 V |

Overshoot for $\zeta<1$: $\exp\!\left(-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\right)$.

**Where $L_g$ comes from.** A package bond wire contributes about 1 to 5 nH, the PCB trace roughly 5 to 10 nH per cm if the return path is far, and the shared source/Kelvin path adds more. *Approximate values; they vary with layout.*

**Peak GPIO current.** $I_{pk}=V_{OH}/(R_{out}+R_g)$: with $R_{out}=25$ Ω, $R_g=220$ Ω, $I_{pk}=13.5$ mA; with $R_g=0$ it is 132 mA for a driver that can only source 20 mA, so the pin droops and the "step" isn't a step.

**Switching time.** In the Miller plateau, $t_{sw}\approx Q_{gd}\,(R_g+R_{drv})/(V_{drive}-V_{plateau})$. For $Q_{gd}=1.5$ nC, $V_{drive}=3.3$ V, $V_{plat}=2$ V: $R_t=25$ Ω gives 29 ns; 125 Ω gives 144 ns; 245 Ω gives 283 ns. Switching loss grows with this time: more resistance, slower edge, more heat.

**Parasitic oscillation (the FET as an oscillator).** With the source inductance $L_s$ and $C_{gd}$ forming a feedback path, a high-$g_m$ FET can satisfy the oscillation condition at tens to hundreds of MHz, especially in the saturation/linear region (slow switching, analog use, current limiting) or when FETs are paralleled with a common gate wire. Cures: individual gate resistors (and sometimes a ferrite bead) at each gate, short loops, and a snubber or a drain-gate capacitor in analog use. *Oscillation conditions are device- and layout-specific.*
:::

::: short
"The gate is a capacitor, and the wire and package feeding it add nanohenries of inductance, so a fast driver edge makes a series LC that rings at tens of megahertz and overshoots the gate; with enough gain and feedback through the Miller capacitance it can even oscillate. A series gate resistor damps the loop, requiring $R\ge2\sqrt{L/C}$, caps the GPIO's peak current, and slows the edge to cut dv/dt and EMI, at the cost of some switching loss."
:::

::: long
"I'll take the three parts of the question in order. Why the resistor? A MOSFET gate looks like a capacitor of about a nanofarad, and the path to it has inductance from the bond wire, the package lead and the PCB trace, maybe 10 to 30 nanohenries. So a driver with a fast edge is driving a series LC. Without any resistance, that's an underdamped second-order circuit: it rings at one over two pi root LC, about 35 megahertz for 20 nanohenries and 1 nanofarad, and the overshoot can be 70 percent. On a 3.3 volt drive that peaks around 5.6 volts. On a 12 volt driver it can exceed the gate rating.

Second, what's parasitic oscillation and where does it come from? It's an unintended sustained oscillation, usually tens to hundreds of megahertz, which comes from the parasitics: the gate loop inductance with the gate capacitance, and feedback from the drain to the gate through the Miller capacitance, with the source inductance in the return. A power FET has a lot of transconductance, so it can provide enough gain to sustain it, especially in the linear region or with slow edges, and when paralleling FETs on a shared gate trace, where the FETs oscillate against each other through the shared inductance. The effects are gate overshoot, random turn-on and turn-off, extra heating, and EMI at that frequency.

Third, what does the resistor do? It adds the damping the loop lacks. The loop is critically damped at R equals two root L over C, which is about 9 ohms for these values, and anything above that gives no overshoot. In practice the driver's own output resistance contributes, but a fast gate driver might be only an ohm or two so the external resistor does the job. It limits the peak current from a GPIO that can only give 10 to 20 milliamps. And it slows the edge, reducing dv/dt, EMI and Miller coupling. The cost is slower switching and more switching loss, so I pick the smallest value that gives a clean gate waveform, check it on a scope, and use separate resistors for each paralleled FET."
:::

::: terms
- **Parasitic oscillation**: unintended high-frequency oscillation caused by parasitic L and C with gain and feedback.
- **Damping ratio ($\zeta$)**: $R/(2Z_0)$ for a series RLC; $\zeta<1$ means ringing.
- **Critical damping**: $R=2\sqrt{L/C}$, the fastest response without overshoot.
- **Gate loop inductance ($L_g$)**: inductance of the gate and return path (bond wire, lead, trace).
- **Miller capacitance ($C_{gd}$)**: gate-drain capacitance that couples drain dv/dt to the gate.
- **Source inductance ($L_s$)**: common-source inductance that adds negative feedback ($L_s\,di/dt$) and can resonate.
- **Ferrite bead**: lossy inductor at gate that attenuates oscillation frequencies.
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: $\sqrt{L/C}$, the scale for the damping resistance.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it limits current and reduces ringing".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Models the gate loop as a series RLC and states the damping criterion $R\ge2\sqrt{L/C}$ with a worked number.
- Separates **ringing** (a transient with an underdamped loop) from **sustained oscillation** (feedback and gain, e.g. FET in linear region, paralleled devices).
- Gives the resistor's other jobs: GPIO current limit, dv/dt/EMI control, Miller immunity.
- Names the trade-off: switching time and loss rise with $R_g$; picks the smallest value that damps.
- Mentions practical cures: Kelvin source, small loop, individual gate resistors for paralleled FETs, ferrite bead, scope with short ground.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating the gate as a pure capacitor.** The inductance is what makes it ring.
- **Believing ringing is only a cosmetic problem.** Overshoot can exceed the gate rating and cause false switching.
- **Using the same resistor value for every design.** The right value depends on $L$, $C$, the driver and the speed you need.
- **Putting one resistor at the shared node of paralleled FETs.** Each FET needs its own gate resistor so the devices can't oscillate against each other.
- **Choosing a huge resistor "to be safe".** It slows the transition, extends the linear-region time and raises switching loss and dead-time needs.
:::

## Why a series resistor: four jobs

1. **Damping.** $R_g+R_{drv}\ge2\sqrt{L_g/C_{iss}}$.
2. **Pin-current limit.** An MCU GPIO can source maybe 10 to 20 mA. A direct connection has a peak of $3.3/25=132$ mA (a typical output resistance of 25 Ω), so the pin voltage sags and the drive is distorted, and the stress on the pin stack isn't good for reliability. $R_g=220$ Ω reduces it to 13.5 mA ([[q0429]]).
3. **Edge-rate control.** Slower $dv/dt$ on the drain means less radiated and conducted EMI, less Miller injection into the other FET in a bridge ([[q0428]]), and a gentler $di/dt$ for inductive loads.
4. **Isolation of the driver from the gate transient.** The FET's Miller feedback pushes current back into the gate; the resistor limits that current into the driver pin.

## Where parasitic oscillation comes from

**Ringing** is a *transient* response of an underdamped loop. **Parasitic oscillation** is *sustained*: the circuit has enough gain and feedback to keep going at a frequency set by parasitics.

A FET has high $g_m$ and a drain-gate capacitance $C_{gd}$. Add inductance in the gate and source leads and the structure can form a Colpitts or Hartley-like oscillator at a frequency where the net feedback satisfies the Barkhausen condition. Typical conditions where it appears:

- **FET in the linear/saturation region for a long time**: slow turn-on, current limiting, linear regulators with pass FETs, hot-swap controllers. Gain is available during the transition, and a slow gate lets any resonance grow.
- **Paralleled FETs with a shared gate trace**: the FETs and the shared gate and source inductances form coupled resonators; the FETs oscillate against each other at 50 to 200 MHz. Fix: one gate resistor per FET, close to its gate, and symmetric layout.
- **Long gate wires or a remote driver**: more inductance, lower $f_0$, lower damping resistance needed.
- **High-$g_m$, low-$C_{iss}$ devices** (GaN, small SiC): even smaller inductance can create a resonance; they need smaller loops and often negative turn-off bias.

Symptoms: gate waveform with persistent high-frequency ripple, drain waveform fuzz on the "flat" part, a FET that runs hot at low duty with no obvious reason, noise at one frequency in a spectrum analyser or EMI scan.

## Fixing it, in order of effort

1. **Add or increase $R_g$**; confirm with a scope on the gate (measure at the gate and source pins using a short ground spring, not a long ground lead, which adds its own loop and ringing).
2. **Reduce loop inductance**: shorten and widen the gate trace, keep the return path directly under it, place the driver next to the FET, use the Kelvin source pin if present.
3. **Individual gate resistors** for paralleled FETs, plus a ferrite bead if needed.
4. **Add a small gate-source capacitor or an RC snubber** (a few hundred pF in series with a resistor) to lower the resonance's Q; this slows the edge too.
5. **Use a separate turn-on/turn-off resistor with a diode**: slow on to limit dv/dt, fast off to hold the FET off ([[q0428]]).
6. **Negative turn-off bias** for fast devices.

## A worked check for a GPIO-driven FET

GPIO 3.3 V, $R_{out}\approx25$ Ω, $C_{iss}=400$ pF, a 15 cm wire to the FET (about 150 nH at 10 nH per cm, a loose estimate), $Z_0=\sqrt{150\text{ nH}/400\text{ pF}}=19$ Ω, so critical damping needs $R_t\ge38$ Ω: the GPIO's own 25 Ω is not enough, so $R_g\ge15$ Ω; choose 100 to 220 Ω for current limit and margin. Resonance $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{150\text{ nH}\times400\text{ pF}})=20.5$ MHz. That is why a long wire from a GPIO to a FET without a resistor often rings visibly, and why the resistor is cheap insurance.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Loop inductance per length depends strongly on the return path geometry; 10 nH/cm is a rough figure for a wire far from its return, and a trace over a solid ground plane is far lower (a few nH per cm). Measure or simulate for a real layout.
:::

::: applications
- **GPIO-to-MOSFET connections** of all kinds: 47 to 330 Ω is typical.
- **Gate-driver outputs in half-bridges** with separate on/off resistors and a diode.
- **Paralleled power MOSFETs and IGBTs**: individual gate resistors for each device.
- **Linear regulators and hot-swap circuits** using pass FETs: gate resistor and sometimes a ferrite bead to prevent oscillation.
- **GaN and SiC gate drive**: tight loops, low-value resistors, negative turn-off bias.
- **Motor drives and inverters**: $R_g$ tuned to trade dv/dt (EMI, bearing currents) against switching loss.
:::

::: assumptions
- The gate is modelled as a constant $C_{iss}$; in reality it changes during the transition (the Miller effect); this only changes the numbers, not the conclusion.
- Loop inductance values are estimates; use measurement or a field solver.
- The driver is a voltage source with a series resistance; real GPIOs are current-limited and slew-limited.
- Oscillation conditions are device- and layout-specific; the presence of gain and feedback is the issue, not any single number.
- For a very small FET driven slowly with short traces ($L$ of a few nH, $C$ of 100 pF) the loop may already be well damped by the driver's output resistance.

If the interviewer pushes on "oscillation", mention that a FET in linear mode can oscillate at higher frequencies than the gate-loop resonance, and that the cure combines damping, shortened loops and individual resistors.
:::

::: followups
### How would you choose the resistor value in practice?
Calculate $R_{crit}=2\sqrt{L/C}$ for an estimated $L$ and the datasheet $C_{iss}$, add margin, check the GPIO or driver peak current, and then measure the gate waveform with a short-ground probe. Increase until the overshoot is below a few percent, and check the switching time and loss against the budget.

### Why not use a very large resistor, like 10 kΩ?
It would make the gate time constant $10\text{ k}\times1\text{ nF}=10$ µs. The FET would linger in the linear region, dissipate switching loss, and need a long dead time in a bridge. It also makes the gate easier to disturb by dv/dt coupling since the holding impedance is high.

### What if the FET is paralleled with another?
Give each FET its own gate resistor right at its gate, and keep the gate and source loops symmetric. A shared resistor lets the two gates, the shared inductance and $C_{iss}$ form a coupled resonator that can oscillate at the frequency where the FETs swap current.

### How does the resistor interact with the pulldown?
The pulldown ($R_{pd}$) and the series resistor form a divider: $V_{gate}=V_{OH}R_{pd}/(R_{pd}+R_g+R_{out})$. With $R_{pd}=100$ kΩ and $R_g=220$ Ω the loss is 0.2%. They don't interfere, but keep $R_{pd}\gg R_g$ ([[q0430]]).

### A scope shows the gate waveform is clean, but the drain shows 100 MHz ringing. What is happening?
That is the drain-loop resonance of the switch-node inductance and $C_{oss}$, not the gate loop. Slowing the gate edge (higher $R_g$) reduces its excitation, and an RC snubber across the FET or a lower-inductance power loop damps it directly ([[q0213]]).
:::

::: interpretations
The question has three parts: **why** the series resistor, **what** parasitic oscillation is in this context, and **what the resistor does** to it. "In the context" means the GPIO-to-MOSFET gate connection of [[q0429]]. Some interviewers use "parasitic oscillation" to mean the transient ringing; others mean the sustained oscillation of a FET with feedback. This page covers both and keeps them distinct. The resistor's role in limiting pin current is also part of the answer, since a GPIO is not a gate driver.
:::
