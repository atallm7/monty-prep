::: basics
### In plain words
In a solid, electrons can only have certain energies, and those allowed energies form wide groups called bands. Current flows when electrons have empty energy levels nearby to move into. A band that is completely full cannot carry current. A semiconductor has a full lower band, an empty upper band, and a gap between them.

### Picture it
Picture a parking garage with two floors and a ramp gap between. The lower floor is completely full of cars, so none of them can move. The upper floor is empty, so any car that gets up there can drive freely. When a car does leave the lower floor, it leaves an empty space, and the cars next to it can shuffle into it. That moving empty space is a hole. The analogy is rough, since electrons are not really cars on floors.

### Words you need
- **Valence band**: the lower band, holding the electrons that form the bonds between atoms.
- **Conduction band**: the upper band, where electrons can move freely. See [[q0055]].
- **Band gap**: the range of energies between the two bands where no electron is allowed to sit.
- **Hole**: an empty spot in the valence band that moves around and acts like a positive charge.
- **Carrier**: anything that moves and carries current, an electron or a hole.

### The simplest example
Take pure silicon at absolute zero. The valence band is full and the conduction band is empty, so no current flows. At room temperature, heat kicks a tiny number of electrons across the 1.12 eV gap (eV, the electron-volt, is a tiny unit of energy). That is about $10^{10}$ electrons in each cubic centimeter, which holds $5\times10^{22}$ atoms. Each leaves a hole behind, so both electrons and holes carry current. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers how electrons and holes move, and why metals conduct so well when their top band is only partly full.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand **band theory** well enough to say *where* conduction happens and *why*:

- Electrons that carry current move in the **conduction band**. In a semiconductor, the **valence band** also contributes, through **holes**.
- Why a **full band can't conduct** and an **empty band has nothing to conduct with**, so the gap between them matters.
- Where bands come from: atomic orbitals overlapping and splitting when atoms form a crystal (Pauli exclusion).
- Metals vs semiconductors: in a metal the conducting band is partially filled; in a semiconductor carriers have to be created by heat, light or doping.

The odd wording ("atom bands") is part of the test: can you translate a fuzzy question into the right physics?
:::

::: why
Band language is how every device datasheet, process document and textbook talks about semiconductors: band gap, conduction band edge, donor levels, Fermi level, band bending in junctions and MOS capacitors. If you can't place electrons and holes in the right bands, PN junctions ([[q0063]]), doping ([[q0065]]) and MOSFET threshold voltage won't make sense. It's also a quick filter: candidates who say "electrons orbit in shells and jump between atoms" reveal that they're thinking in chemistry pictures rather than solid-state ones.
:::

::: concepts
- **Atomic energy levels**: an isolated atom's electrons occupy discrete levels (1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p…); the outermost are the valence electrons.
- **Pauli exclusion principle**: no two electrons can share the same quantum state, so when $N$ atoms combine, each level splits into $N$ closely spaced levels: a band.
- **Valence band**: the band formed from the bonding (valence) orbitals; full at 0 K in a semiconductor.
- **Conduction band**: the next band up (from antibonding orbitals); empty at 0 K in a semiconductor; electrons here move freely. See [[q0055]].
- **Band gap**: the energy range with no allowed states between them (1.12 eV for Si).
- **Holes**: a missing electron in the valence band behaves as a mobile positive carrier. See [[q0069]].
- **Fermi–Dirac statistics**: the probability a state at energy $E$ is occupied; sets how many electrons reach the conduction band.
:::

::: answer
"I'd answer in terms of energy bands. In a solid, the atoms' outer energy levels merge into bands: the valence band, which holds the bonding electrons, and above it the conduction band. In a semiconductor they're separated by the band gap.

The electrons that carry current move in the conduction band. Those are electrons that got enough energy, from heat, light, or from a donor atom, to jump the gap. Up there, there are lots of empty states around them, so they can pick up energy from an electric field and move through the crystal.

The valence band matters too. When an electron leaves it, it leaves behind an empty state, a hole. Neighbouring valence electrons can hop into that hole, so the hole effectively moves, and it acts like a positive charge carrier. So in a semiconductor, current is carried by electrons in the conduction band and holes in the valence band.

The key idea is that a completely full band can't carry current, because there's no empty state to move into, and an empty band has no electrons to move. Metals conduct so well because their top band is only partly full. Core electrons in the inner shells never move at all; they stay tied to their atoms."
:::

::: technical
**Band formation.** In an isolated Si atom the outer configuration is 3s²3p². In a crystal, the 3s and 3p orbitals of neighbouring atoms overlap and hybridize (sp³). By Pauli exclusion, $N$ atoms produce $N$-fold split levels, which (with $N \sim 5\times10^{22}$ cm⁻³) form quasi-continuous **bands**. In Si they separate into a lower **bonding band (valence band)** holding 4 electrons/atom and an upper **antibonding band (conduction band)**, with a gap $E_g = 1.12$ eV at 300 K. Inner shells (1s, 2s, 2p) barely overlap and stay as narrow, fully occupied core levels; those electrons never conduct.

**Which band conducts.** Current needs a band with *both* electrons *and* empty states:
- A **full band** carries zero net current: for every electron with crystal momentum $\hbar\mathbf k$ there's one with $-\hbar\mathbf k$.
- An **empty band** has no carriers.
- **Conduction band** with a few electrons near its bottom edge $E_c$: these behave like free particles with effective mass $m_n^*$ and carry current.
- **Valence band** with a few empty states near its top $E_v$: the collective motion of all the remaining electrons is equivalent to the motion of a few **holes** with charge $+q$ and effective mass $m_p^*$.

**Occupancy.** Fermi–Dirac: $f(E) = 1/(1 + e^{(E-E_F)/kT})$. In intrinsic Si, $E_F$ is near midgap, so at $E_c$ (0.56 eV above), $f \approx e^{-0.56/0.0259} \approx 4\times10^{-10}$. Times the effective density of states (~$10^{19}$ cm⁻³) gives $n_i \approx 10^{10}$ cm⁻³ electrons in the CB and the same number of holes in the VB.

**Metals.** The top band is partly filled (Cu: half-filled 4s band) or bands overlap (Mg, Zn), so $E_F$ lies inside a band and ~$10^{22}$–$10^{23}$ cm⁻³ electrons participate. In metals the "valence" and "conduction" band are effectively the same band.

**Doping** adds states inside the gap: donor levels ~0.045 eV below $E_c$ (P in Si) that release electrons into the CB; acceptor levels ~0.045 eV above $E_v$ (B in Si) that capture valence electrons and create holes.
:::

::: short
"In a solid, atomic levels merge into bands. Current-carrying electrons move in the conduction band, the band above the band gap, where there are plenty of empty states to move into. In a semiconductor the valence band below the gap also carries current, via holes: missing electrons that act as positive carriers. A completely full band can't conduct and an empty one has no carriers, so you need electrons in the conduction band or holes in the valence band. In metals the top band is only partly full, so it conducts directly."
:::

::: long
"I'd interpret 'atom bands' as energy bands, because that's how electrons in a solid are described.

In a single atom, electrons sit in discrete energy levels. When you bring billions of atoms together into a crystal, the outer orbitals of neighbouring atoms overlap. The Pauli exclusion principle says no two electrons can have the same state, so each level splits into a huge number of very closely spaced levels. That's a band. In silicon, the four outer electrons per atom form covalent bonds, and those bonding states make up the valence band. The next set of states up, the antibonding ones, form the conduction band. Between them is a 1.12 eV gap with no allowed states. The inner-shell electrons stay as narrow core levels tied to their atoms and never conduct.

Now, which band do electrons move in? To carry current, an electron has to be able to accept a tiny bit of energy from the field and change its state, so it needs empty states right next to it. In a completely full band there are none. In fact a full band carries exactly zero net current. So at absolute zero, pure silicon has a full valence band and an empty conduction band, and it's an insulator.

At room temperature, a few electrons get thermally kicked across the gap, about $10^{10}$ per cm³ in silicon. Those electrons are in a nearly empty conduction band, so they move freely: that's electron conduction. They leave behind empty states in the valence band. Another valence electron can move into that empty state, and so on, so effectively the vacancy moves the opposite way, behaving like a positive charge. That's a hole.

So the answer is: electrons conduct in the conduction band, holes conduct in the valence band. Doping just adds states inside the gap that either donate electrons to the conduction band or accept them from the valence band. And in a metal, the top band is only partly filled, so its electrons conduct without needing to cross any gap."
:::

::: terms
- **Energy band**: a quasi-continuous range of allowed electron energies formed from overlapping atomic orbitals in a crystal.
- **Valence band**: the highest band that is full at 0 K in a semiconductor; holds the bonding electrons.
- **Conduction band**: the lowest band that is empty at 0 K in a semiconductor; electrons here are mobile.
- **Band edges ($E_c$, $E_v$)**: the bottom of the conduction band and the top of the valence band.
- **Band gap ($E_g$)**: $E_c - E_v$, the forbidden energy range.
- **Hole**: the absence of an electron in the valence band, acting as a $+q$ mobile carrier.
- **Fermi level ($E_F$)**: the energy with 50% occupation probability; its position relative to the band edges sets carrier densities.
- **Core levels**: deep, fully occupied, localized states from inner shells; they don't participate in conduction.
:::

::: exceptional
**Average:** "Electrons move in the conduction band. The valence band is where electrons are bound."

**Exceptional:**
- Explains **where bands come from** (overlapping orbitals plus Pauli exclusion splitting levels).
- Explains **why** conduction needs a partly filled band: a full band carries zero net current; an empty band has no carriers.
- Names **both** carriers: electrons in the conduction band and holes in the valence band, and explains a hole physically.
- Gives numbers: Si gap 1.12 eV, $kT = 26$ meV, $n_i \approx 10^{10}$ cm⁻³, donor/acceptor levels ~45 meV from the band edges.
- Contrasts **metals** (partly filled band; "valence" and "conduction" are the same band) and notes **core electrons never conduct**.
- Addresses the **wording**: "atom bands" interpreted as energy bands, with a note on the chemistry "valence shell" picture.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Electrons in the valence band can't move."** They can, but in a full band their motions cancel. Once a few states are empty, the valence band conducts via holes.
- **"Holes are positrons" or "holes are positive ions."** A hole is a missing valence electron, a collective effect, not a particle you could remove from the crystal. Donor and acceptor *ions* are fixed and don't conduct.
- **"Bands are the orbits/shells around each atom."** Bands belong to the whole crystal; electrons in them are delocalized over many atoms.
- **"The band gap is a physical space between atoms."** It's a range of *energies*, not a region of space.
- **Saying electrons move "between the bands" to conduct.** Conduction happens *within* a band; crossing the gap is generation (creating carriers), not conduction.
- **Forgetting that metals don't have a conduction band "gap" to cross.** Their conduction happens in a partly filled band.
:::

::: deep
## From atomic levels to bands

Start with one silicon atom: 14 electrons in 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p². The first ten are tightly bound core electrons; the four in the n = 3 shell are the **valence electrons** that form bonds.

Now bring two atoms together. Their outer orbitals overlap, and quantum mechanics forces each shared level to split into two (one bonding, one antibonding), because the Pauli exclusion principle forbids two electrons from occupying identical states. With $N$ atoms, each level splits into $N$ levels. In a crystal $N \sim 10^{22}$ per cm³, so the levels are so close they form a continuous **band**.

The deep core levels (1s, 2s, 2p) barely overlap with neighbours, stay narrow, are completely full and stay localized: those electrons **never** take part in conduction.

## Why a full band can't conduct

Inside a band, each state has a crystal momentum $\hbar k$. In a full band, every state with $+k$ has a partner with $-k$, so the total momentum (and current) is zero. Apply a field: every electron would like to shift its $k$ slightly, but every neighbouring state is already occupied, and Pauli exclusion forbids doubling up. The band as a whole can't respond. Net current: zero.

An empty band has no electrons to respond. So conduction needs a band that's **partly** occupied. In a semiconductor that happens two ways:

1. **A few electrons near the bottom of the conduction band.** Lots of empty states around them, so they accelerate in the field like free particles, with an effective mass $m_n^*$ that encodes the crystal's influence ($\approx0.26m_0$ for conductivity in Si).
2. **A few empty states near the top of the valence band.** Now the valence band isn't full, so it can carry current. It's mathematically equivalent (and much easier) to track the few empty states as **holes**: particles of charge $+q$ moving in the direction of the field.

## How many electrons make it to the conduction band?

The probability a state at energy $E$ is occupied is the Fermi–Dirac function:

$$f(E) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{(E - E_F)/kT}}$$

In intrinsic silicon, $E_F$ sits near the middle of the gap. The conduction band edge is 0.56 eV above it, which is about 22 $kT$ at room temperature.

Multiplying by the effective density of states ($N_c \approx 3\times10^{19}$ cm⁻³ for Si) gives $n \approx 10^{10}$ cm⁻³: a tiny population compared to $5\times10^{22}$ atoms/cm³, but it's those few electrons (and the equal number of holes) that conduct.

## How doping changes the picture

Doping doesn't change *which* bands carry current. It changes *how many* carriers are in them:

- A **donor** (P, As in Si) introduces a state about 45 meV below $E_c$. That's only ~2 $kT$, so the extra electron is almost always released into the **conduction band** ([[q0065]]). n-type: lots of conduction-band electrons.
- An **acceptor** (B in Si) introduces an empty state about 45 meV above $E_v$. A valence electron easily hops into it, leaving a hole in the **valence band**. p-type: lots of valence-band holes ([[q0062]], [[q0069]]).

## Metals, for contrast

In copper, each atom contributes one 4s electron to a band that can hold two per atom, so it's **half full**. The Fermi level sits in the middle of a band and there are empty states immediately above occupied ones, so ~$8.5\times10^{22}$ electrons/cm³ can respond to a field. There's no meaningful distinction between "valence" and "conduction" band: it's the same partly filled band. That's why metals conduct at any temperature, even near 0 K.

## Wording note: shells vs bands

In chemistry, electrons are described by **shells** (n = 1, 2, 3…) and the outermost is the **valence shell**. That's still useful: the valence-shell electrons are the ones that form the valence band. But "electrons move in the valence shell" is the wrong picture for conduction in a solid: the conducting states are delocalized bands belonging to the whole crystal, not orbits around individual atoms.

::: applications
- **Device physics language**: band diagrams of PN junctions, MOS capacitors and heterojunctions are drawn with $E_c$, $E_v$ and $E_F$; reading them is a core skill ([[q0063]]).
- **Optoelectronics**: LEDs emit when conduction-band electrons recombine with valence-band holes, emitting photons near $E_g$ (GaN-based blue LEDs at ~450 nm); photodiodes do the reverse ([[q0057]]).
- **Doping and transistors**: MOSFET channels are inversion layers where the bands bend enough to put the conduction band (NMOS) or valence band (PMOS) near the Fermi level.
- **Thermistors and leakage**: thermally generated electron-hole pairs across the gap cause reverse leakage in diodes and junction leakage in ICs, roughly doubling every ~10 °C in silicon.
- **Wide-bandgap power devices**: SiC and GaN's larger gaps keep thermally generated carriers negligible at high temperature and support higher breakdown fields.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Crystalline semiconductor** (Si) at room temperature, near equilibrium. In amorphous materials, band tails and localized states blur the edges and conduction can be by hopping.
- **Interpretation**: "atom bands" taken to mean electronic **energy bands** in a solid. If the interviewer meant atomic shells/orbitals, the answer is the outermost (valence) shell electrons; the band picture is what those become in a crystal.
- **Holes as carriers** is a model: physically, it's still electrons moving in the valence band, but the hole description is exact for the current and much simpler.
- **Non-degenerate doping.** At very high doping (above ~$10^{19}$ cm⁻³ in Si), donor levels merge into the conduction band and the material behaves more like a metal.
:::

::: followups
### What's a hole, physically?
An empty state near the top of the valence band, left when an electron is excited to the conduction band or captured by an acceptor. When the field pushes a neighbouring valence electron into that vacancy, the vacancy moves the opposite way. The net effect of all the valence electrons' motion equals one positive charge moving along the field, with its own effective mass and mobility. It's a convenient, exact description of a nearly full band.

### Why don't core electrons (like silicon's 1s, 2s, 2p) conduct?
Their orbitals are small and tightly bound, so they barely overlap with neighbouring atoms. They form extremely narrow, completely full bands lying many eV below the valence band. A full band carries no net current, and there's no realistic way to empty those states at normal energies.

### At 0 K, is pure silicon a conductor or an insulator?
An insulator. The valence band is completely full and the conduction band completely empty, so neither can carry current. It only conducts at room temperature because thermal energy excites about $10^{10}$ electrons/cm³ across the 1.12 eV gap (and leaves the same number of holes).

### Where are the donor levels for phosphorus in silicon, and why are nearly all donors ionized at room temperature?
About 45 meV below the conduction-band edge. Room-temperature $kT$ is 26 meV and there are vastly more conduction-band states than donor states, so the donors almost all give up their electron (the probability of staying neutral is small). Below roughly 100 K they start to recapture electrons: that's freeze-out.

### What if the band gap were zero?
You'd have a semimetal or metal-like material, like graphite or graphene (zero gap at specific points). There'd always be carriers available, so you couldn't switch it off with a gate, which is exactly why graphene transistors struggle with on/off ratio, despite their high mobility.
:::

::: interpretations
"Which atom bands do electrons move in?" is oddly worded. The most sensible reading is **"In which energy bands do the current-carrying electrons move?"**, with the expected answer: the conduction band (electrons) and, in semiconductors, the valence band (via holes). It could also be a garbled way of asking about the **valence shell** (outermost electrons) from a chemistry viewpoint. The strongest answer clarifies: "Bands belong to the crystal rather than individual atoms, so I'll answer in terms of energy bands," and briefly links shells to bands.
:::
