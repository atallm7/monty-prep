::: testing
Whether you know why a machine that places parts with tens of microns of accuracy needs help from the board itself. The interviewer wants: fiducials are copper alignment marks that a camera locates; they let the pick-and-place and the paste printer correct for the board's actual position, rotation and scale; there are global (panel/board) and local (per-component) kinds; and they have design rules (bare copper, a clear mask opening, no nearby clutter, placed asymmetrically). Bonus: they also help AOI and screen-printing alignment.
:::

::: why
A placement machine knows where its head is, not where your board is. Boards sit in a conveyor with mechanical slop, panels are not perfectly square, and fabrication adds a few tenths of a millimetre of registration and scale error. On a 0.5 mm pitch QFP the pad is about 0.25 mm wide, so a 0.15 mm error is a bridge or an open. Fiducials are cheap (a dot of copper) and rescue the yield, which is why a missing or badly designed fiducial is one of the most common DFM comments you will get from a contract manufacturer ([[q0613]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Optical alignment**: a camera finds a high-contrast mark and the machine computes the transform between CAD coordinates and the real board position.
- **Global fiducials**: marks on the panel or board corners that fix X, Y offset, rotation and scale for the entire board.
- **Local fiducials**: marks next to one fine-pitch part (QFP, BGA, 0201) that correct residual error for that component.
- **Contrast**: bare copper (or plated finish) against a clear, matte solder-mask opening, so the camera sees a clean circle.
- **Panel features**: rails, tooling holes and mousebites share the panel with fiducials. See [[q0594]], [[q0595]].
- **Pick-and-place**: the machine that uses them. See [[q0600]].
:::

::: answer
"Fiducials are small alignment marks on the board, usually a round dot of bare copper about a millimetre across with a clear area around it, that a machine's camera can find. The pick-and-place machine and the solder paste printer use them to figure out exactly where the board really is: its X and Y offset, how much it's rotated, and even whether it's stretched or shrunk slightly. Without them the machine would trust that the board sits perfectly in the conveyor, and it doesn't.

There are two types. Global fiducials sit on the panel rails or board corners, ideally three of them in an asymmetric pattern, so the machine can correct for position, rotation and scale. Local fiducials sit next to a fine-pitch part like a QFN or a BGA to fine-tune the placement of that one component, since at fine pitch even small local errors from the fab matter.

For design, the mark should be bare copper, around 1 mm diameter, with a solder-mask opening about two to three times that size and no silkscreen or traces inside it, so the camera sees clean contrast. I'd also place them away from the board edge and make the three global ones asymmetric so a board can't be loaded rotated 180 degrees."
:::

::: technical
**Function.** A vision system measures the pixel centroid of each fiducial, converts to machine coordinates using calibration, and compares to the CAD positions. Two fiducials define a translation and a rotation. A third (non-collinear) one also resolves scale and shear (non-orthogonality). The placement program applies the transform to every component coordinate.

**Why rotation matters.** A rotation error of 0.1° moves a point 100 mm from the pivot by $100\tan 0.1^\circ \approx 0.17$ mm: already too large for 0.5 mm pitch parts. A scale error of 0.05% on a 250 mm panel is 0.125 mm. Fiducial correction removes these.

**Geometry (typical, vendor dependent).**
- Shape: solid circle is most common (a square or cross also works; the key is a symmetric, sharp-edged shape).
- Diameter: about 1.0 mm (commonly quoted range 0.5-3 mm, as long as all fiducials on a board are the same size).
- Solder-mask opening: about 2-3x the diameter, giving a clear ring of bare laminate around the copper. For a 1 mm dot in a 3 mm opening the ring is 1 mm wide.
- Keep-out: no copper, silkscreen, or via inside the clear ring other than the dot itself.
- Placement: global marks in at least two (preferably three) corners of the panel/board, at least a few mm from the edge; local marks diagonally on either side of a fine-pitch part.
- Surface: flat, same finish as pads; shiny HASL can reflect and confuse cameras, so a flat ENIG/OSP finish or a matte mask behind it helps.

**Other users.** Solder paste printers align the stencil to the board using fiducials, AOI ([[q0608]]) and flying probe testers use them to register to the board, and screen printing and laser marking use them too.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact diameter, clear zone and edge distance are machine- and vendor-specific (Common ranges quoted in assembly-house guides: 1 mm dot, 2-3 mm opening, 3-5 mm from edge). Confirm with your assembler's DFM guideline.
:::

::: long
"A fiducial is a reference mark for machines. It's usually a round dot of exposed copper about a millimetre in diameter, sitting in a larger opening in the solder mask so the camera sees a bright dot on a dark background.

The reason we need them is that boards are never perfectly where the machine thinks they are. The conveyor clamps it with some slop, the panel is a bit rotated, and the fab's etching and lamination leave small scale and registration errors. A pick-and-place head is accurate to tens of microns, but that only helps if it knows where the board is. So before placing, the machine takes a picture of two or three global fiducials, computes the offset, rotation and scale of the board, and shifts every placement coordinate accordingly. With two fiducials you get offset and rotation; a third gives you scale and skew.

On top of that, fine-pitch parts like a 0.4 mm pitch QFN or a BGA get local fiducials, one or two close to the component, because the board might be locally distorted, and the best position reference is the nearest one.

Design-wise: the dot is bare copper, the mask opening is a few times larger, nothing else is inside that opening, no silkscreen, and they're placed away from the edge. The global ones go in an asymmetric pattern, say three corners, so a rotated board is detected. Paste printers, AOI machines and flying probe testers also use them, so a good set of fiducials helps every step, and I'd confirm the size and clearance with the assembly house."
:::

::: terms
- **Fiducial**: a machine-readable reference mark on a PCB.
- **Global fiducial**: mark used to align the panel or whole board.
- **Local fiducial**: mark used to align one component or small region.
- **Registration**: how accurately layers and features line up with their intended position.
- **Scale / shrink**: uniform stretching or shrinking of the board relative to the artwork.
- **Skew / shear**: non-orthogonality of the X and Y axes.
- **Clear zone / mask opening**: solder-mask-free area around a fiducial for contrast.
- **Vision system**: camera and software that find the fiducial centroid.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "they're marks for alignment."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains *why*: placement-machine accuracy is useless without knowing the board's true pose, and fabrication introduces offset, rotation and scale errors.
- Distinguishes global from local fiducials and says how many, where, and why asymmetric.
- Gives design rules: bare copper dot of about 1 mm, 2-3x mask opening, no silkscreen or copper in the clear zone, surface finish and contrast.
- Quantifies something: 0.1° rotation is 0.17 mm at 100 mm, which is larger than a fine-pitch pad.
- Notes other users (paste printer, AOI, flying probe) and that exact numbers come from the assembler's guideline.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Covering fiducials with solder mask.** The camera then sees low-contrast green-on-green and may reject the board.
- **Putting silkscreen or a trace through the clear zone.** The vision algorithm sees a distorted shape.
- **Using different sizes for fiducials on the same board.** The machine's template expects consistency.
- **Placing all fiducials symmetrically.** A board loaded rotated 180° looks identical and gets assembled backwards.
- **Skipping local fiducials on fine-pitch parts** (0.5 mm pitch and finer, BGAs), then wondering why that one part has bridging.
- **Using shiny HASL on the fiducial** without checking that the camera copes; bumpy solder reflects light and the centroid shifts.
- **Forgetting the second side.** Double-sided boards need fiducials on both sides.
:::

::: deep
## What problem do they solve?

A placement machine has an XY gantry with an encoder resolution of microns, and a placement repeatability of about 25-50 µm for good machines (vendor dependent). Those numbers describe *where the head goes*, not where the pads are. The pad positions depend on:

- how the board sits in the conveyor clamp (offset, rotation),
- fabrication error in etched artwork versus the CAD (registration, scale),
- thermal distortion in earlier processes (a board that has been through one reflow shrinks slightly and may warp).

With a 0.5 mm pitch QFP the pads are about 0.25 mm wide with 0.25 mm gaps. Budget for placement error is about half of that gap before you risk touching the neighbour, and after paste and reflow self-alignment is helpful only for small errors. So you need the total error well below ±0.1 mm.

## The correction math

The machine knows each fiducial's nominal CAD position $\mathbf{p}_i$ and measures the real position $\mathbf{q}_i$. It fits an affine transform $\mathbf{q} = A\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{t}$ with a 2x2 matrix $A$ (rotation, scale, shear) and a translation $\mathbf{t}$.

- 1 fiducial: only $\mathbf{t}$ (offset).
- 2 fiducials: offset, rotation and uniform scale.
- 3 fiducials: full affine, including non-uniform scale and shear.

Numbers: a rotation of 0.1° gives $100\ \text{mm}\times\tan(0.1^\circ) = 0.175$ mm at 100 mm from the pivot. A uniform scale error of 0.05% over 250 mm is 0.125 mm. Both would wreck fine-pitch placement without correction.

A subtle point: fiducial *spacing* matters. If two fiducials are 200 mm apart and the camera has a 0.05 mm measurement error on one of them, the angle error is $\arctan(0.05/200)=0.014^\circ$, which at 100 mm out becomes just 0.025 mm. Widely spaced fiducials make the transform estimate less sensitive to measurement noise: put them as far apart as possible.

## Design rules (typical)

| Parameter | Typical value |
|---|---|
| Shape | Solid circle, 1 mm (0.5-3 mm accepted) |
| Mask opening | 2-3x copper diameter, so about 2-3 mm |
| Keep-out | No copper, silkscreen or vias within the opening |
| Edge distance | At least 3-5 mm from edge, inside any rail |
| Global count | 3, asymmetric (2 minimum), on each side that has parts |
| Local count | 1-2 per fine-pitch (<= 0.5 mm) part, diagonal |
| Flatness | Same finish as pads; avoid shiny uneven HASL |

If the copper dot is on a ground pour, give it a clear annulus in the pour so the camera sees dot-on-laminate and not copper-on-copper.

## Why asymmetry helps

Two fiducials on opposite corners of a rectangle look identical after the board is rotated 180 degrees, so the machine would happily place 400 parts on a backwards board. Making the layout asymmetric (offsetting one fiducial by 5-10 mm, or using three corners) means a wrongly loaded board shows no fiducial where the program expects one, and the machine rejects it before placing anything.

## Panel vs board fiducials

When boards are panelized ([[q0594]]), the rails carry the global panel fiducials, and each board in the panel may carry its own set, because individual boards sit on slightly different positions inside the panel (routing and V-score accuracy). The machine uses "panel" fiducials for the panel transform and, if needed, "board" fiducials for individual boards, which is how good arrays survive small within-panel errors.

## Use by other machines

- **Solder paste printer**: aligns the stencil to the board's fiducials before printing, so the paste lands on pads.
- **AOI** ([[q0608]]): finds fiducials to register its image grid.
- **Flying probe / ICT** ([[q0611]], [[q0609]]): optical alignment of probe coordinates to the board.
- **Laser marking, dispensing, selective soldering**: same idea.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A great one-liner: "Fiducials let the machine measure the board's pose instead of assuming it. Two points give position and rotation, three add scale, and local ones fix the last tenth of a millimetre on fine-pitch parts." Then mention the contrast rule: bare metal, clear opening, nothing else inside.
:::

::: applications
- **Any SMT assembly**: global fiducials on panel rails; the paste printer and placer use them every run.
- **0.4 mm pitch QFN or BGA boards**: local fiducials beside the part.
- **Double-sided assembly**: fiducials on both sides, since each side is a separate run.
- **Flying-probe testing** ([[q0611]]): fiducials register probe coordinates.
- **Rework stations**: BGA rework systems use board fiducials to align the vision system with the pads.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes **vision-based** placement. Older or low-cost machines using mechanical edge locating rely on tooling holes instead.
- Dimensions are typical; machines accept a range and require consistent size per board.
- Assumes an affine model of distortion. Warped boards have non-affine distortion that fiducials cannot fully correct; that needs board support or warp limits.
- A single hand-placed prototype board with no machine needs no fiducials, but they cost nothing to include.
:::

::: followups
### How many fiducials do you need and where?
Three global fiducials per side, in three corners with an asymmetric offset, so the machine solves offset, rotation, scale and rejects a rotated board. Two is the minimum for position and rotation. Add local fiducials (one or two) at fine-pitch parts. Put them as far apart as the board allows to reduce sensitivity to measurement noise.

### Why bare copper rather than painted or silkscreened marks?
Copper is etched with the same artwork as the pads, so its position is tied to the pads' position within a few microns. Silkscreen is registered separately and is much looser, so a legend mark would not represent the pad locations.

### Why do you need a mask opening around the fiducial?
Contrast. The camera segments the bright copper disc from the dark surrounding. If solder mask covers the copper the contrast drops, and green mask over copper gives a poor edge. A clear ring of bare laminate also stops mask misregistration from eating into the dot.

### A board keeps getting rejected with "fiducial not found" on the placer. What do you check?
Fiducial covered by mask or silkscreen, different diameter than the program expects, oxidised or contaminated finish, a ground pour touching the dot (no clear ring), a board warp that defocuses the camera, or the wrong position in the program. Look at the camera image of a failed board.

### You're short on space. Can you use a component pad as a fiducial?
Sometimes the machine can use a feature like a unique pad or via as a "natural fiducial", but it's less reliable than a designed dot. A designed local fiducial costs around 2-3 mm of space and is worth it for fine-pitch parts.
:::

::: interpretations
"What are fiducials?" in a PCB context means optical alignment marks. A different meaning exists in medical imaging and surveying (reference markers), but the same idea. In DFM discussions, "fiducial" sometimes also means the *tooling hole*, but those are mechanical pins, not optical marks. See [[q0594]] for both on a panel.
:::

::: references
- IPC-7351 (land pattern standard) and IPC-7525 / IPC-7527 stencil and solder-paste references: standards by name; the fiducial recommendations commonly cited in assembly-house guides derive from them. Check the current revisions.
- Contract manufacturer DFM guides: the actual dimension requirements for your line.
- Pick-and-place vendors' manuals (any large vendor): fiducial recognition and board transform description.
:::
