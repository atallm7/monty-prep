::: testing
Whether you can compare two devices by **physics first, conventions second**, and then say which wins under which constraints. The interviewer is looking for:

1. The silicon reason NFETs are better per unit area (mobility), and what that means for $R_{DS(on)}$, $Q_g$, $C_{oss}$ and cost.
2. The drive-circuit consequences: PFET gate is referenced to $V_{in}$ (easy), NFET gate must exceed $V_{in}$ (bootstrap or charge pump).
3. The system consequence: PFETs allow 100% duty; bootstrapped NFETs have a maximum duty set by the refresh time.
4. A balanced conclusion with a decision rule, not "NFET is better".
:::

::: why
Choosing the high-side device is one of the first decisions in a buck design and it drives the gate-drive circuit, the dropout behavior, the efficiency at frequency, and the cost. In practice you will see both: monolithic buck ICs for 2.5 to 5.5 V input often use a PFET on the high side so they can go to 100% duty, while 12 V and higher parts almost always use a bootstrapped NFET. Being able to say *why* shows you understand the device and the circuit, which interviewers read as real design judgment. See [[q0201]] for the bridge overview and [[q0206]] for how to build the NFET drive.
:::

::: concepts
- **Carrier mobility**: electrons in silicon move faster than holes, so for equal geometry $R_{DS(on)}$ of an NFET is lower ([[q0201]]).
- **$V_{GS}$ reference**: the gate drive that matters is $V_{GS}$, the gate relative to the source. The source of a high-side NFET is SW; the source of a high-side PFET is $V_{in}$.
- **Bootstrap / charge pump**: how an NFET gets $V_{GS}$ above $V_{in}$ ([[q0206]]).
- **Duty-cycle limits**: PFETs can stay on indefinitely; bootstrapped NFETs need periodic refresh.
- **$R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_g$ and $R_{DS(on)}\cdot C_{oss}$**: figures of merit for conduction vs switching trade-offs.
:::

::: answer
"The short version is that an NFET is the better transistor and a PFET is the easier one to drive.

For the same die area, an NFET has roughly two to three times lower on-resistance, because electrons are more mobile than holes. So for a given $R_{DS(on)}$ the NFET is smaller, which means less gate charge and less output capacitance, so lower switching loss and lower cost. That's why high-current or high-frequency converters use NFETs.

The catch is the high side. An NFET's source is the switch node, which swings up to $V_{in}$, so to turn it on you need the gate about 5 V above $V_{in}$. That takes a bootstrap capacitor and diode or a charge pump, and it limits the maximum duty cycle, because the bootstrap cap needs the low side to turn on periodically to recharge.

A PFET's source sits at $V_{in}$. You turn it on by pulling the gate down toward ground and off by pulling it up to $V_{in}$, so no bootstrap. It can also stay on forever, so 100% duty and low dropout. The cost is area, gate charge and capacitance, so more loss at high frequency. I'd use a PFET for low input voltages and simplicity, and an NFET when efficiency or current matters."
:::

::: technical
**Device physics.** $R_{DS(on)} \approx \dfrac{L}{\mu C_{ox} W\,(V_{GS}-V_{th})}$ for a given channel length. With $\mu_n/\mu_p \approx 2$ to $3$ in silicon, a PFET needs 2 to 3 times the width. $Q_g$, $C_{iss}$, $C_{oss}$ scale with area, so at equal $R_{DS(on)}$ the PFET has roughly 2 to 3 times the capacitance and charge.

**Gate drive.**
- **PFET high side**: $V_{GS} = V_G - V_{in}$. On: $V_G \approx V_{in} - V_{drv}$ (for example 0 V when $V_{in}$ equals the allowed $|V_{GS}|$). Off: $V_G = V_{in}$. Requires a level shifter from ground-referenced logic and, if $V_{in}$ exceeds the rated $|V_{GS}|$ (typically 20 V), a clamp.
- **NFET high side**: $V_{GS} = V_G - V_{SW}$. On: $V_G \approx V_{in} + V_{drv}$. Needs bootstrap (floating supply, [[q0206]]).

**Duty cycle.** Bootstrap refresh needs a minimum off-time $t_{off,min}$ (typically 100 to 200 ns, often set by the controller): $D_{max} = 1 - t_{off,min}f_{sw}$. For 150 ns: 92.5% at 500 kHz, 85% at 1 MHz, 70% at 2 MHz. Some ICs add a small charge pump to hold the high side on for 100% duty. A PFET reaches 100% with $V_{GS}$ held.

**Loss comparison (illustrative).** Two 20 mΩ devices: NFET $Q_g=10$ nC at 5 V drive, PFET $Q_g=25$ nC at 10 V drive, 500 kHz: gate loss 25 mW (NFET) vs 125 mW (PFET). PFET $C_{oss}$ is about 2.5 times larger as well.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Mobility ratio and resulting area penalty vary with process; 2 to 3 times is typical for silicon but not universal. Figures for gate charge are illustrative and device-specific.
:::

::: short
"An NFET is the better switch and a PFET is the easier one to drive. NFETs have two to three times lower $R_{DS(on)}$ per area because electrons are more mobile than holes, so less gate charge and capacitance and lower cost, but a high-side NFET needs its gate above $V_{in}$, so a bootstrap or charge pump, and it can't reach 100% duty without refresh. A high-side PFET just pulls its gate down relative to $V_{in}$ and supports 100% duty, at the cost of larger die, more switching loss and slower edges. PFET for low voltage and simplicity, NFET for efficiency and current."
:::

::: long
"I'd frame it as: the NFET is the better transistor, the PFET is the easier drive.

Start with silicon. Electron mobility is higher than hole mobility by about two to three times, so for the same geometry an NFET has about a third to half the on-resistance. Equivalently, for a target $R_{DS(on)}$ the NFET die is two to three times smaller. A smaller die has proportionally less gate charge and output capacitance, so lower switching and gate-drive loss, and it costs less. That's why most power stages above a few amps, or above a megahertz or so, use NFETs.

The problem is the high side. The NFET's source is the switch node, which sits at $V_{in}$ when it's on. To keep $V_{GS}$ at 5 V you need the gate at $V_{in}$ plus 5 V. So you add a bootstrap diode and capacitor, charged while the low side is on, or a charge pump. That has side effects: a bootstrap needs the switch node to go low periodically to recharge, so the maximum duty is below 100%, say 90 to 95% at hundreds of kilohertz.

The PFET's source is $V_{in}$, so you just pull its gate down toward ground to turn it on and up to $V_{in}$ to turn it off. No bootstrap, and it can stay on indefinitely, which gives 100% duty and a clean low-dropout mode. The costs: bigger die for the same $R_{DS(on)}$, more $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$, slower edges, and you need a level shifter, plus a clamp if $V_{in}$ is above the gate rating.

So: low $V_{in}$, low frequency, wide duty range, simple, PFET. Efficiency, high current, high frequency, NFET."
:::

::: terms
- **$V_{GS}$**: gate-to-source voltage; what actually turns a MOSFET on.
- **Carrier mobility ($\mu$)**: how fast carriers move per unit field; $\mu_n > \mu_p$ in silicon.
- **Bootstrap**: a diode-capacitor circuit that creates a floating supply riding on SW.
- **Charge pump (gate drive)**: a switched-capacitor circuit that makes a rail above $V_{in}$.
- **100% duty / dropout mode**: the high side held on continuously, $V_{out} \approx V_{in} - I\,R_{DS(on)}$.
- **$Q_g$, $C_{oss}$**: gate charge and output capacitance; scale with die area.
- **Level shifter**: converts a ground-referenced logic signal to a gate drive referenced to $V_{in}$ or SW.
- **Figure of merit (FOM)**: $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_g$ for switching devices.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "NFET is more efficient but needs bootstrap; PFET is simpler."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the physical reason (mobility ratio, area at equal $R_{DS(on)}$, $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$ scaling).
- Explains the gate reference in one line: NFET $V_{GS}$ is relative to SW, PFET relative to $V_{in}$.
- Quantifies the duty limit: $D_{max} = 1 - t_{off,min}f_{sw}$ with an example.
- Notes the PFET's own drive issues: level shifter, $|V_{GS}|$ limit and clamp, slower edges.
- States a decision rule with voltage, current, frequency and duty ranges, and mentions that the "100% duty" capability can be recovered for NFET designs with a charge pump or an always-on boost.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"PFETs are always worse."** They win on drive simplicity and 100% duty at low $V_{in}$.
- **"A PFET high side needs bootstrap too."** It does not; the source is at $V_{in}$ and the gate just goes below it.
- **Mixing up gate swing and $V_{GS}$.** A PFET's gate swings between $V_{in}-V_{drv}$ and $V_{in}$, not between 0 V and $V_{in}$ in general.
- **Ignoring $|V_{GS}|$ max.** Pulling a PFET gate to ground with $V_{in} = 24$ V gives $V_{GS} = -24$ V, beyond the typical $\pm20$ V rating; add a clamp or divider.
- **Claiming a bootstrapped NFET can hold 100% duty.** The capacitor leaks and droops; the controller must force a refresh or use a charge pump.
:::

## Why the NFET wins the transistor contest

In the triode region:

$$R_{DS(on)} \approx \frac{L}{\mu\,C_{ox}\,W\,(V_{GS}-V_{th})}$$

At equal $L$, $V_{GS}-V_{th}$ and oxide, $R_{DS(on)} \propto 1/(\mu W)$. Silicon's $\mu_n$ is roughly 2 to 3 times $\mu_p$ at the doping levels typical of these devices. So equal $R_{DS(on)}$ needs $W_P \approx (2\text{ to }3)\,W_N$.

The knock-on effects scale with area:

| Quantity at equal $R_{DS(on)}$ | NFET | PFET |
|---|---|---|
| Die area | 1× | 2 to 3× |
| $Q_g$, $C_{iss}$ | 1× | 2 to 3× |
| $C_{oss}$, $Q_{oss}$ | 1× | 2 to 3× |
| Cost | lower | higher |
| Typical $V_{DS}$ ratings available | wide range | narrower |

Switching loss from $C_{oss}$ is $\tfrac12 C_{oss}V_{in}^2 f_{sw}$ (roughly), and gate drive is $Q_g V_{drv} f_{sw}$. At 500 kHz with 20 mΩ devices: NFET $Q_g=10$ nC at 5 V is 25 mW; PFET $Q_g=25$ nC at 10 V is 125 mW. At 5 A that is still only a few percent, but at 2 MHz it is five times worse.

## The duty-cycle limit of a bootstrapped NFET

A bootstrap capacitor $C_B$ is charged through a diode while SW is low ([[q0206]]). In the on-state it supplies the gate charge and the controller's high-side quiescent current, drooping slightly. To recharge it the converter needs SW to go low for at least $t_{off,min}$ each cycle (or every few cycles), so

$$D_{max} = 1 - t_{off,min}\,f_{sw}$$

## Other differences you can mention

- **PFET body diode orientation**: for a high-side PFET the body diode runs from drain (SW) to source ($V_{in}$), clamping SW overshoot to $V_{in}+0.7$ V. For an NFET high side, the body diode is from SW (source) to $V_{in}$ (drain), the same function.
- **Drive power**: the PFET's gate swing is a full $V_{GS}$ referenced to $V_{in}$; the NFET's bootstrap swing is a smaller 5 V.
- **Speed**: the PFET's larger capacitance and the level shifter's slow pull-up through $R_{pu}$ make turn-off slower, adding switching loss and limiting $f_{sw}$.
- **Level-shifter static current**: while $Q_{LS}$ is on, $R_{pu}$ draws $V_{in}/(R_{pu}+R_g)$ continuously (small but non-zero).
- **Voltage range**: 12 V and up use NFETs almost exclusively; low-$V_{in}$ ICs (Li-ion 2.5 to 5.5 V) often use a PFET to reach 100% duty.

## A decision rule

| Constraint | Prefer |
|---|---|
| $V_{in} \le$ about 6 V and 100% duty/low dropout needed | PFET (or NFET with charge pump) |
| High current (above about 5 A) or $f_{sw}$ above 1 MHz | NFET |
| Very low cost, slow, simple controller | PFET |
| $V_{in}$ above about 20 V | NFET (PFET gate needs clamps) |
| Bidirectional or synchronous multiphase | NFET |

## Worked example: one Li-ion cell to 3.3 V at 1 A

The rule of thumb says "PFET for low $V_{in}$ and 100% duty". Put numbers on it. A single Li-ion cell runs from about 4.2 V full to about 3.0 V empty, and the rail must stay at 3.3 V at 1 A, so the converter has to keep regulating as the cell sags toward 3.4 V. Assume (for the exercise) a PFET with 60 mΩ at $|V_{GS}|=4.5$ V that rises to about 80 mΩ at $|V_{GS}|=3.4$ V, because a low gate overdrive hurts $R_{DS(on)}$. Add 30 mΩ of inductor winding resistance.

**PFET at 100% duty.** The drop is $1\ \text{A}\times(0.08+0.03)\ \Omega=0.11$ V, so the output holds up until $V_{in}=3.3+0.11=3.41$ V. Below that it simply follows the cell at 0.11 V less, which is the dropout behavior of a low-dropout converter.

**Bootstrapped NFET at 1 MHz.** Suppose the driver needs $t_{off,min}=150$ ns to refresh the boot capacitor. Then $D_{max}=1-150\ \text{ns}\times1\ \text{MHz}=0.85$, and the lowest input that still reaches 3.3 V (allowing the same 0.11 V of drop) is $V_{in,min}\approx(3.3+0.11)/0.85=4.0$ V. A cell is below 4.0 V for most of its discharge curve, so this converter would fall out of regulation almost immediately. The fixes are a lower frequency (at 500 kHz, $D_{max}=0.925$ gives 3.69 V), a charge pump that holds the gate up at 100% duty, or the PFET.

The check on the opposite side. At 12 V in and 3.3 V out, $D=0.275$ and none of this matters. The NFET is perfectly fine and the PFET's extra area is just waste. Which switch is better depends on how close the converter runs to the duty-cycle limit, not on a universal ranking.

## What "slow level shifter" really costs

The PFET drive circuit in the figure above turns the FET off by pulling the gate up through $R_{pu}$ to $V_{in}$. That is a plain RC charge of the gate capacitance. Take $C_{iss}=2$ nF as an illustrative PFET input capacitance:

| $R_{pu}$ | $\tau=R_{pu}C_{iss}$ | Turn-off (about $2.2\tau$) | Static current while $Q_{LS}$ is on, at 12 V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 Ω | 0.2 µs | 0.44 µs | 120 mA (1.4 W) |
| 470 Ω | 0.94 µs | 2.1 µs | 26 mA (0.31 W) |
| 1 kΩ | 2 µs | 4.4 µs | 12 mA (0.14 W) |

A 500 kHz converter has a 2 µs period, so only the first row is even close to usable, and it wastes more than a watt. The 1 kΩ row would hold the PFET half-on for longer than the entire cycle. That is why real PFET stages use a push-pull gate driver, which actively drives the gate both ways, and not a resistor pull-up. The simple circuit is fine for a few tens of kHz. This is also a good illustration that the "PFET is easier to drive" claim is true for DC and slow switching but not at megahertz.

## Boot capacitor droop, in numbers

The same style of check for the bootstrapped NFET. With $Q_g=6$ nC, an assumed 100 µA of high-side driver quiescent current, $t_{on}=0.55$ µs ($D=0.275$ at 500 kHz) and $C_B=100$ nF:

$$\Delta V_{C_B}=\frac{Q_g+I_q\,t_{on}}{C_B}=\frac{6\ \text{nC}+100\ \mu\text{A}\times0.55\ \mu\text{s}}{100\ \text{nF}}=0.06\ \text{V}$$

That is negligible against a 5 V drive. At 100% duty, with nothing to recharge it, the cap loses 1 V in $C_B\Delta V/I_q=100\ \text{nF}\times1\ \text{V}/100\ \mu\text{A}=1$ ms, after which $R_{DS(on)}$ climbs and the FET heats. This is the reason datasheets quote a maximum on-time or force a refresh pulse.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Quiescent current of the high-side driver, and the minimum off-time a particular controller needs, vary widely between parts. The 100 µA and 150 ns figures are assumptions for the exercise.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Duty ceiling.** Slowly lower $V_{in}$ with the load fixed and watch the PWM on SW. The duty cycle rises to a limit and then the output drops. The measured $D_{max}$ should match $1-t_{off,min}f_{sw}$ for the controller you have. For the PFET version the duty should go to 100% and the output should track $V_{in}$ minus the $IR$ drop.
2. **Gate edges.** Probe the PFET gate against $V_{in}$ with a differential probe or a scope with floating mode, and measure the turn-off time. Compare with $2.2R_{pu}C_{iss}$. If it is longer than the dead time you are budgeting, you will see shoot-through current in the supply.
3. **Boot voltage.** Probe $V_{BST}-V_{SW}$ with a differential probe at the highest duty you expect. It should stay within the driver's undervoltage threshold. Dropping toward it is a warning.
4. **Efficiency.** Compare $V_{in}I_{in}$ against $V_{out}I_{out}$ at 1 A with the PFET and NFET versions at the same frequency. The difference at low frequency is small, and the gap opens as you push $f_{sw}$ up.
:::

::: applications
- **Single-cell Li-ion buck regulators (2.5 to 5.5 V in)**: PFET high side with an NFET low side so the converter can pass through at 100% duty as the battery discharges.
- **12 V to point-of-load bucks in FPGAs and routers**: bootstrapped NFET/NFET, high efficiency at hundreds of kilohertz to megahertz.
- **High-current VRMs**: NFET only, with multiphase and DrMOS packages.
- **Automotive buck pre-regulators (up to 40 V or more)**: NFET with bootstrap and a charge pump for near-100% duty during cold crank.
- **Load switches / reverse-polarity protection**: a high-side PFET is a simple switch because the gate is driven relative to the input.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Silicon MOSFETs** with typical mobility ratios. GaN devices are N-type (no p-channel GaN power FET), so the choice does not arise; SiC similarly.
- **Fixed switching frequency and constant on-resistance** for the comparison; temperature rises $R_{DS(on)}$ by 50 to 100% over the range, in both types.
- **Gate-charge figures** and $t_{off,min}$ values are representative.
- **Monolithic ICs** blur the line: the controller may use an internal charge pump to give an NFET high side effectively 100% duty. The PFET advantage is then smaller.
- At **very low voltages** (below about 3 V) bootstrap voltage may be too low for logic-level gates, which further favors a PFET or a charge pump.
:::

::: followups
### Why can't a bootstrapped NFET stay on at 100% duty?
The bootstrap capacitor supplies the gate and driver quiescent current while the FET is on, and its only recharge path is through the diode when SW is low. With SW held at $V_{in}$, the cap droops: $\Delta t \approx C_B\Delta V/I_{q,boot}$. For 100 nF, 0.5 V allowed droop and 100 µA the on-time is about 500 µs. Controllers force a short off-pulse before that, or add a charge pump.

### How do you drive a PFET gate from a 3.3 V MCU when $V_{in}$ is 12 V?
Use a small NFET (or NPN) level shifter: its gate takes the 3.3 V logic, its drain pulls the PFET gate down through a resistor, and a pull-up resistor to $V_{in}$ turns the PFET off when the NFET is off. The resistor values trade speed against shoot-through current. If $V_{in}$ exceeds the PFET's $V_{GS}$ rating, add a Zener or resistor divider so the gate pulls to $V_{in}-10$ V instead of ground.

### What fails if you pull a PFET gate to ground with a 24 V input?
$V_{GS} = -24$ V exceeds the common $\pm 20$ V maximum and can rupture the gate oxide. You clamp with a Zener from source to gate (about 10 to 12 V) in series with a resistor or use a divider.

### Which would you pick for a 5 V to 1.2 V, 3 A converter at 2 MHz?
NFET on both sides. The duty is 0.24 so 100% duty is irrelevant; at 2 MHz the PFET's larger $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$ cost real efficiency; and 5 V is enough to bootstrap logic-level NFETs if the driver is integrated. A PFET would only be tempting if a very simple controller or a wide input range down to dropout were required.

### What if the input falls below the output target (battery nearly dead)?
A PFET converter goes to 100% duty and just passes $V_{in} - I R_{DS(on)}$ to the output (dropout). A bootstrapped NFET converter hits its $D_{max}$ and the output falls out of regulation sooner, unless a charge pump holds the gate. This is why battery-powered buck ICs sell "100% duty cycle" as a feature.
:::

::: interpretations
"High-side FET" is the transistor between $V_{in}$ and the switch node. The question compares N-channel and P-channel versions for that role. A candidate may also be asked about the low side; the low side is nearly always an NFET because its source is at ground and it is trivially driven. This page is about the high-side trade-off, which differs from Q201 (which bridge) and Q206 (how to drive the NFET).
:::
