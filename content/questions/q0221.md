::: testing
Whether you can treat switching frequency as a **design knob with a cost on both sides** rather than a number you copy from a datasheet. The interviewer wants to hear:

1. What gets better as $f_{sw}$ goes up: ripple falls, and the inductor and capacitors can shrink.
2. What gets worse: every per-cycle loss (gate charge, $C_{oss}$, switch overlap, dead-time diode conduction) grows linearly with $f_{sw}$, EMI harmonics move, and the minimum on-time starts to cap how high you can go.
3. That you think in terms of the whole system: bandwidth, noise-sensitive frequency bands, light-load behaviour, and thermals, not just "faster is smaller".
:::

::: why
Frequency is the first decision in almost every buck design, and it quietly drives the bill of materials, the efficiency, the thermal budget, and the EMI test result. A phone PMIC at 2-4 MHz and an industrial 48 V converter at 100-400 kHz are not different because of taste; they sit at different points on the same trade-off. This question is also the doorway to related ones: [[q0216]] (efficiency and ripple), [[q0220]] (why not 1 THz or 1 Hz), and [[q0212]] (sizing L and C). If you can derive both sides of the trade with numbers, you sound like someone who has actually picked a frequency.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor ripple current**: $\Delta I_L = \frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})D}{L f_{sw}}$, inversely proportional to $f_{sw}$. See [[q0195]].
- **Output capacitor ripple**: $\Delta V_C \approx \frac{\Delta I_L}{8 C f_{sw}}$ plus an ESR term. See [[q0196]].
- **Per-cycle switching losses**: gate-charge, $C_{oss}$ and overlap energy are paid once per cycle, so loss $= E_{cycle}\cdot f_{sw}$. See [[q0217]].
- **Minimum on-time**: the controller cannot make a pulse shorter than $t_{on,min}$, which limits $f_{sw}$ for large step-down ratios.
- **Control bandwidth**: the loop crossover is conventionally kept to a fraction (often 1/10 to 1/5) of $f_{sw}$, so a higher $f_{sw}$ permits a faster loop.
- **Spectral content / EMI**: the switch-node waveform has harmonics at multiples of $f_{sw}$. See [[q0144]].
:::

::: answer
"Switching frequency is a trade-off knob. Going up in frequency makes the ripple smaller, because each cycle the inductor only has a short time to ramp, so the current ripple scales as one over $f_{sw}$. That means I can use a smaller inductor and smaller capacitors for the same ripple, and the control loop can be faster because I can push the crossover higher.

The price is efficiency and heat. Every cycle I pay a fixed bit of energy: charging and discharging the gate, charging the switch-node capacitance, the overlap of voltage and current during the transitions, and the body-diode conduction in dead time. Multiply by frequency and those losses scale linearly. Core loss and EMI also get worse to deal with, and there's a hard ceiling from the minimum on-time: if I'm going from 12 V to 1.2 V at 2 MHz I need a 50 ns pulse, which a lot of controllers can't make.

So the design is about finding the sweet spot. For a 12-to-3.3 V, 2 A converter I'd probably choose something like 500 kHz to 1 MHz. Lower if I care about peak efficiency, higher if board area or transient response dominates."
:::

::: technical
**Ripple falls as $1/f_{sw}$.** For a buck in CCM, $\Delta I_L = \frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{L f_{sw}}$ and the capacitor-driven output ripple is $\Delta V_{out}\approx \frac{\Delta I_L}{8 C_{out} f_{sw}} + \Delta I_L\cdot ESR$. For fixed $L$, the first term goes as $1/f_{sw}^2$. Equivalently, to hold ripple constant, $L$ and $C$ scale roughly as $1/f_{sw}$.

**Losses rise roughly linearly with $f_{sw}$.**
- Gate drive: $P_G = Q_G V_{drv} f_{sw}$.
- Output capacitance: $P_{Coss} \approx \tfrac12 C_{oss} V_{in}^2 f_{sw}$.
- V-I overlap: $P_{ov}\approx \tfrac12 V_{in} I (t_r+t_f) f_{sw}$.
- Dead time: $P_{dt} \approx V_F I\,(t_{d1}+t_{d2})\,f_{sw}$.
- Core (AC) loss: grows with $f_{sw}$ but falls with the smaller flux swing, so the net is material-dependent.

Conduction loss is nearly independent of $f_{sw}$, except that a very low frequency means a big ripple and extra RMS current.

**Upper bound: minimum on-time.** $t_{on}=D/f_{sw}=V_{out}/(V_{in}f_{sw})$ must be at least $t_{on,min}$, so $f_{sw}\le \frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}\,t_{on,min}}$. With $t_{on,min}=80$ ns, 12 V to 1.2 V caps at 1.25 MHz.

**Other effects.** Loop crossover $f_c \lesssim f_{sw}/10$ to $f_{sw}/5$. Harmonics land in different regulatory or sensitive bands (AM radio, IF stages). Light-load efficiency drops because the fixed per-cycle losses do not scale with load.
:::

::: short
"Higher switching frequency means less ripple and smaller inductors and capacitors, and it lets the loop be faster. The cost is that every per-cycle loss, like gate charge, switch-node capacitance and transition overlap, scales linearly with frequency, so efficiency and heat get worse. It also pushes against the minimum on-time limit for big step-downs and moves your EMI harmonics around. So you pick a frequency that balances size, efficiency and noise for the application."
:::

::: long
"I think of it as a trade-off between size and performance on one side and loss on the other.

On the good side, ripple current in the inductor is $\Delta I = V_{out}(1-D)/(L f_{sw})$, so doubling the frequency halves the ripple for the same inductor, or lets me halve the inductor for the same ripple. The output voltage ripple from the capacitor goes as $\Delta I / (8 C f_{sw})$, so it improves even faster, as one over frequency squared. Smaller passives also mean a faster transient response, because I can run the control loop crossover at maybe a tenth of the switching frequency.

On the bad side, almost every loss term is energy per cycle. Gate charge times drive voltage, half $C_{oss}V^2$, the voltage-current overlap during the edges, and the dead-time diode conduction all multiply by $f_{sw}$. For a 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A example I estimate that going from 500 kHz to 2 MHz moves the switching loss from about 0.1 W to about 0.4 W, which costs several points of efficiency.

There are also hard limits. The minimum on-time of the controller sets a ceiling: 12 V to 1.2 V is a 10 percent duty cycle, so at 2 MHz the on-time is 50 ns, which is too short for many parts. And EMI changes, since the harmonics shift and I may need to avoid certain bands.

So I'd pick the frequency from the constraints. Battery-powered and efficiency-critical: lower. Space-constrained or needing a fast response: higher."
:::

::: terms
- **Switching frequency ($f_{sw}$)**: how many times per second the high-side switch turns on. Period $T=1/f_{sw}$.
- **Ripple current ($\Delta I_L$)**: peak-to-peak inductor current variation, set by $V$, $D$, $L$ and $f_{sw}$.
- **Switching loss**: energy lost during transitions and in charging parasitic capacitances, proportional to $f_{sw}$.
- **Minimum on-time ($t_{on,min}$)**: the shortest pulse the controller and gate driver can deliver reliably.
- **Dead time**: the interval where both switches are off to prevent shoot-through, during which the body diode conducts.
- **Crossover frequency ($f_c$)**: where loop gain crosses 0 dB. It sets the transient bandwidth.
- **Harmonics**: multiples of $f_{sw}$ present in the switch-node waveform. They set the conducted and radiated EMI spectrum.
- **Core loss**: hysteresis and eddy-current loss in the inductor core, which rises with frequency and flux swing.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "higher frequency gives smaller components but lower efficiency" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the scaling laws: ripple current $\propto 1/f$, capacitor ripple $\propto 1/f^2$ at fixed $L$, per-cycle losses $\propto f$.
- Separates which losses depend on $f_{sw}$ (gate, $C_{oss}$, overlap, dead time) from the ones that do not (conduction).
- Brings up the **minimum on-time** limit and computes it for a specific step-down ratio.
- Mentions loop bandwidth ($f_c\sim f_{sw}/10$) and EMI band avoidance.
- Names an application-driven choice, such as a high frequency for a small phone rail and a low frequency for a high-input-voltage converter where overlap loss is large.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Higher frequency is always better because the parts get smaller."** Smaller parts cost efficiency, thermal margin and EMI. Past a point the inductor DCR/AC loss and the switching loss dominate.
- **Saying losses scale with $f_{sw}^2$.** Per-cycle switching energy times frequency gives a *linear* dependence. The *capacitor ripple* is the one that goes as $1/f^2$.
- **Forgetting that conduction loss does not fall with frequency.** Raising $f_{sw}$ to cut ripple does nothing for $I^2R$ loss.
- **Ignoring minimum on-time.** A high step-down ratio at high frequency leads to pulse skipping and jitter, and the ripple gets worse rather than better.
- **Treating inductance and frequency independently.** At fixed ripple, $L f_{sw}$ is the product that matters. Doubling both gives 4× less ripple.
:::

::: deep
## What scales with frequency

Almost everything a buck does is either "once per cycle" or "continuous". Things that happen once per cycle scale with $f_{sw}$; things that are continuous do not.

| Quantity | Scales as | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Inductor ripple current | $1/f_{sw}$ | Fixed volt-seconds per cycle shrink with the period |
| Output cap ripple (cap term) | $1/f_{sw}^2$ | Ripple current falls *and* the charge integrates over a shorter time |
| Required $L$ or $C$ for fixed ripple | $1/f_{sw}$ | Same equations solved the other way |
| Gate-drive, $C_{oss}$, overlap, dead-time loss | $f_{sw}$ | Energy per event times events per second |
| Conduction loss | Roughly constant | $I_{rms}^2R$, with a small ripple correction |
| Inductor AC core loss | Material dependent | More cycles per second, but a smaller flux swing |
| Loop crossover ceiling | $f_{sw}$ | Sampling/modulator limit, typically $f_c\lesssim f_{sw}/10$ |
| Minimum duty ($D_{min}$) | $t_{on,min}f_{sw}$ | The pulse cannot shrink below $t_{on,min}$ |

## Side 1: ripple and size

Take $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, so $D=0.275$, and $L=4.7$ µH. The inductor current ripple is
$$\Delta I_L=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})D}{L f_{sw}}$$

Now the other direction: what inductor do I need for a 30% ripple target at 2 A (0.6 A peak to peak)?
$$L=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})D}{\Delta I\, f_{sw}}$$

| $f_{sw}$ | $L$ for 0.6 A ripple | Cap for 10 mV cap-term ripple, with that ripple current |
|---|---|---|
| 200 kHz | 19.9 µH | 0.6 A/(8·10 mV·200 kHz) = 37.5 µF |
| 500 kHz | 8.0 µH | 15 µF |
| 1 MHz | 4.0 µH | 7.5 µF |
| 2 MHz | 2.0 µH | 3.75 µF |

(Each row: $C=\Delta I/(8 f \Delta V)$ with $\Delta I=0.6$ A.) A 2 µH inductor at 2 MHz is a fraction of the volume of a 20 µH one at 200 kHz. This is why phone PMICs sit at 2 to 4 MHz.

Capacitor ripple has a second part, $\Delta I\cdot ESR$. That term only falls as $1/f$ (through $\Delta I$), so at high frequency ESR often dominates, which is why ceramics matter.

## Side 2: losses

Take a representative device set (my assumption, not a datasheet): $Q_G=12$ nC at $V_{drv}=5$ V total over both FETs, $C_{oss}=300$ pF at the switch node, $t_r+t_f=10$ ns, dead time 10 ns on each edge with $V_F=0.7$ V, and $I=2$ A at $V_{in}=12$ V.

- Overlap: $\tfrac12\cdot12\cdot2\cdot10\text{ ns}\cdot f = 120$ nJ per cycle
- Gate: $12\text{ nC}\cdot5\text{ V}=60$ nJ per cycle
- $C_{oss}$: $\tfrac12\cdot300\text{ pF}\cdot144 = 21.6$ nJ per cycle
- Dead time: $0.7\cdot2\cdot20\text{ ns}=28$ nJ per cycle

Total ≈ 230 nJ per cycle, so 0.115 W at 500 kHz and 0.46 W at 2 MHz. Conduction loss is about 0.08 W (with $R_{HS}=15$ mΩ, $R_{LS}=8$ mΩ, DCR = 10 mΩ), nearly flat.

> The device parameters above are illustrative. Real FETs, drivers, and inductors vary a lot, and real converters of this size typically measure a few percent lower than this model. Use the scaling, not the absolute values.

## The ceiling: minimum on-time

The duty cycle at a step-down ratio $V_{out}/V_{in}$ forces an on-time $t_{on}=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in} f_{sw}}$.

- 12 V to 3.3 V at 500 kHz: 550 ns. Easy.
- 12 V to 1.2 V at 2 MHz: 50 ns. Many parts specify $t_{on,min}$ somewhere around 50 to 150 ns (check the datasheet), so this cannot be done reliably. The controller starts **skipping or jittering pulses**, and ripple gets worse.
- 48 V to 3.3 V at 500 kHz: 137 ns. Marginal.

The max frequency is $f_{max}=V_{out}/(V_{in}t_{on,min})$. For $t_{on,min}=80$ ns: 12 V to 1.2 V gives 1.25 MHz, and 24 V to 1.2 V gives 625 kHz. This is why high-input-voltage, low-output-voltage converters run at lower frequencies, or use two stages.

## Dynamics, noise and EMI

A faster switching frequency allows a higher loop crossover. A 100 kHz crossover needs $f_{sw}\gtrsim500$ kHz to 1 MHz. In the time domain, the load-step undershoot scales as roughly $\Delta I_{step}/(2\pi f_c C_{out})$, so doubling $f_c$ at the same capacitance halves it, or you can halve the capacitance.

For EMI, a higher $f_{sw}$ shifts harmonics up. This can help (less low-frequency noise for audio circuits), or hurt (the harmonics now land in FM or cellular bands, and the edge rate matters more than the fundamental). A common practice is to choose the frequency so that the fundamental and low harmonics miss the AM band (about 530 to 1700 kHz) or a sensitive IF, which is one reason some converters sit at 400 kHz or 2.2 MHz.

## Light load

At light load the efficiency is dominated by fixed per-cycle losses, which scale with $f_{sw}$ regardless of load. A high-frequency converter in forced-PWM mode wastes proportionally more at 10 mA. This is why light-load modes (PFM, [[q0231]]) exist.

::: applications
- **Phone/SoC PMICs (2 to 4 MHz)**: tiny 0.47 to 1 µH inductors and 10 µF ceramics. Efficiency is traded for area and fast response, and light-load PFM recovers efficiency.
- **Automotive 12 V to 5 V/3.3 V rails (often 2.1 MHz)**: chosen above the AM band to reduce radio interference, accepting the switching loss. (A common practice; specific frequency plans vary by OEM.)
- **48 V telecom/industrial buck stages (100 to 400 kHz)**: large $V_{in}$ makes overlap loss big, and the low duty cycle at high input needs a long enough on-time.
- **Point-of-load converters for FPGAs/CPUs (500 kHz to 1 MHz, multiphase)**: balance efficiency at tens of amps against transient response. See [[q0236]].
- **Battery-powered IoT (low hundreds of kHz plus PFM)**: efficiency at light load dominates the battery life.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM operation** and an ideal, fixed inductor value. In DCM, ripple and conversion ratio depend differently on frequency (see [[q0229]]).
- **Fixed-frequency PWM.** Constant on-time and hysteretic controllers change their frequency with $V_{in}$ and load, so "switching frequency" is itself a variable.
- **Component values held constant** when I say "ripple scales as $1/f$". If you also shrink $L$ to keep ripple fixed, DCR and saturation current change, which feeds back into efficiency.
- **Loss numbers are illustrative.** Real $Q_G$, $C_{oss}$, edge times and core loss vary widely. The *scaling* is robust, the *magnitudes* are not.

A valid alternative framing: someone may say "frequency has an optimum" and show the efficiency curve peaking at low-to-mid frequencies. That is consistent with the above once inductor DCR and AC core loss are included.
:::

::: followups
### If I double the switching frequency, what happens to the output ripple?
With $L$ and $C$ unchanged, the inductor ripple current halves, and the capacitor-driven part of the output ripple, $\Delta I/(8 C f)$, drops by 4×. The ESR part only halves. So ripple falls by 2× to 4× depending on whether ESR or capacitance dominates. In ceramic-output designs it is often close to 4× at moderate frequency.

### Why do modern PMICs run at 2 to 4 MHz if efficiency drops?
Because size, cost and transient response matter more than a few percent of efficiency on a low-power rail, and modern low-$Q_G$ FETs have made the penalty small. Tiny inductors (0.47 µH) and few MLCCs fit in a phone. Light-load modes (PFM) win back the efficiency at low load, where phones spend most of their time.

### Your converter's ripple gets worse when you increase the frequency from 1 MHz to 2 MHz at 12 V to 1.2 V. Why?
Probably the minimum on-time. At 2 MHz the on-time should be 50 ns; if $t_{on,min}$ is about 80 ns the controller skips or alternates pulse widths, creating sub-harmonic ripple at a lower frequency. The fix is to lower $f_{sw}$ (the ceiling is 1.25 MHz here), use a lower input voltage, or pick a part with a smaller $t_{on,min}$.

### How would you choose the switching frequency for a new 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A design?
Start from constraints: efficiency target, board area, EMI sensitivity, and transient spec. I'd pick a trial value in the 400 kHz to 1 MHz range, size $L$ for a 30% ripple, check the $t_{on,min}$ margin, compute loss at that frequency, and check the thermal rise. If the efficiency is short, I'd lower $f_{sw}$ and use a larger inductor; if the area is short, raise it and accept the loss.

### What if the inductor saturates at high frequency but not low?
Saturation depends on peak current, not frequency, so it is actually the opposite: low frequency gives larger ripple and a higher peak $I_{pk}=I_{out}+\Delta I/2$, which saturates first. High frequency can heat the core through AC loss, which lowers $I_{sat}$ in some materials. See [[q0259]].
:::
