::: basics
### In plain words
For a path that carries real current, wide copper is better. The resistance and heating fall as one over the width. But width is also area, and more area means more capacitance to nearby copper and a better antenna for noise. So for a fast-switching node, a high-impedance feedback line, a controlled-impedance trace or a small pad, a narrower trace is the better choice. Pick the width for the job the net does.

### Picture it
Think of a road. A highway carries heavy traffic with no jams, so wide is right for a power path. But a big empty parking lot next to a noisy intersection catches every sound and passes it on. A switching node is like that lot. A thin footpath is plenty for a feedback signal, and it picks up less noise.

### Words you need
- **Current density**: current per unit of cross-section, $I/(wt)$. Heat grows with its square.
- **Displacement current**: $i = C\,dv/dt$, the current a changing voltage pushes through a capacitance ([[q0550]]).
- **Switching node**: the point in a converter that swings between the supply and ground in nanoseconds.
- **Feedback line**: a high-impedance, low-current sense trace ([[q0552]]).
- **Tombstoning**: a small part standing on one end after reflow because one pad melted first ([[q0538]]).

### The simplest example
Take 50 mm of 1 oz copper at 5 A. At 1 mm wide it is 24 mΩ, so a 120 mV drop. At 4 mm wide it is 6 mΩ, so 30 mV. Wide wins for current. Now look at a switching node. A 10 mm² patch over a plane 0.2 mm below has about 1.9 pF. At 12 V in 5 ns, $i = C\,dv/dt \approx 4.6$ mA. A 100 mm² patch has ten times the capacitance and ten times the current, about 46 mA. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds IPC widths, assembly limits, and a table of widths by net type.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can resist the lazy "thick is always better" answer. For high-current paths thick wins for resistance, heating and drop, and you should say that first. But the question is phrased as a trap: the interviewer wants to hear where skinny is *better*: sense and feedback lines, switching nodes (small area), controlled-impedance signals, thermal necks and spokes, and small-pad connections that would otherwise tombstone. The hidden skill is choosing width to fit the job rather than as a default.
:::

::: why
Width is the first knob you turn in layout and it trades current capacity against capacitance, antenna area, routing density and assembly behaviour. Get it wrong and a power path overheats, a switching node radiates, or an 0402 capacitor stands on end during reflow. The judgement that "it depends on what the net does" is exactly what a layout lead looks for in an intern.
:::

::: concepts
- **Current capacity and IR drop** scale with width. See [[q0534]].
- **Parasitic capacitance** scales with copper area facing other conductors: $C \approx \varepsilon A/h$.
- **Antenna and coupling area**: a wide node on a fast edge injects more displacement current into everything around it.
- **Impedance**: for transmission lines, width sets $Z_0$, not current. See [[q0486]].
- **Thermal conduction through narrow necks**: a narrow link limits heat flow and so affects soldering. See [[q0538]].
- **Fabrication limits**: minimum width and spacing set what "skinny" can be, and wide copper meets thermal and balance rules.
:::

::: answer
"For anything carrying real current, thicker is preferred. Resistance goes as one over the width, so doubling the width halves the drop and the heating, and there's more cross-section to carry current without the copper getting hot. One ounce copper at 1 mm and 50 mm long is 24 milliohms; at 4 mm it's 6.

But I wouldn't say thick is always better. If a net carries almost no current, like a feedback or sense line, a skinny trace is better because it has less area to pick up noise or to couple capacitance, and it's easier to route in a tight space. The switch node of a buck carries the full inductor current, so it needs enough width for the current, but I keep it as small an area as I can, since it's a high dV/dt node and a big copper patch there just radiates and couples. Controlled-impedance lines are set by the width the stack-up needs. And sometimes I deliberately neck down to a small pad: a thin stub into a small capacitor pad stops the pour from heat-sinking one end and tombstoning the part in reflow. So the rule is: wide for current, narrow for signals and for nodes where area is a liability, and in between where noise and current compete."
:::

::: technical
**Current paths.** $R = R_\square L/w$, so for fixed length the drop and heating fall as $1/w$. Current density $J = I/(wt)$ falls the same way. For 1 oz copper, 5 A: 0.5 mm → 286 A/mm², 4 mm → 36 A/mm². IPC-2221 for a 20 °C rise gives about 1.8 mm for 5 A on an outer layer. Inductance falls only logarithmically with width, so widening buys little for $L$.

**Capacitive coupling and radiated emission.** For a conductor of area $A$ over a plane at height $h$: $C \approx \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/h$ (plus fringe). With $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$ and $h = 0.2$ mm, 10 mm² is 1.9 pF and 100 mm² is 19 pF. A switch node swinging 12 V in 5 ns ($2.4\times10^9$ V/s) injects $i = C\,dv/dt$: 4.6 mA vs 46 mA. That is common-mode current flowing through the plane and out of the cables, which is what an EMC lab measures. Narrow the area to what current needs.

**High-impedance nodes.** A node with source resistance $R$ and stray capacitance sees noise current $i = C_c\,dv/dt$ turned into $iR$. Keep these lines thin and short, far from aggressors; see [[q0552]].

**Controlled impedance.** Characteristic impedance depends on width, height and $\varepsilon_r$; a 50 Ω microstrip on a 0.2 mm FR-4 dielectric is roughly 0.35 mm wide, not "as wide as possible".

**Assembly.** A wide copper connection to a small pad drains heat during reflow. If one end of a two-terminal part is on a big pour and the other on a thin trace, the thin end melts first and surface tension can lift the other end (tombstoning). Use a short, narrow neck or a thermal relief ([[q0538]]).

**Fabrication.** Minimum width/space depends on copper weight (heavier copper needs wider minimums), and very wide copper needs copper balancing.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The microstrip width for 50 Ω quoted above is approximate; use a field solver or your fabricator's stack-up for actual values (the microstrip-calc simulation in this app gives an estimate).
:::

::: short
"For current, thicker is better: resistance and heating fall as one over the width. But not everywhere. Sense and feedback lines want to be skinny to cut coupling and antenna area, the switching node should be only as big as its current needs because it's a high dV/dt node, controlled-impedance lines are sized for impedance, and a thin neck into a small pad prevents tombstoning. So wide for current, narrow where area is a liability."
:::

::: long
"For anything carrying current, I prefer thick, because resistance goes as length over width. Take one ounce copper, 50 mm long: at 1 mm it's 24 milliohms, at 4 mm it's 6. That's a quarter of the drop and a quarter of the heat, and the current density falls from about 140 amps per square millimetre at 5 amps to 36. So for supplies, grounds, motor phases and battery paths I go wide, or use a pour.

But I wouldn't say thick is always better, because width is also area, and area costs you capacitance and antenna. Take the buck converter's switch node. It carries the inductor current, so it needs real width, but it also swings from the input voltage to ground in a few nanoseconds. A 100 square millimetre patch over a plane is about 19 picofarads, and 12 V in 5 ns through that is roughly 46 milliamps of common-mode current. A 10 square millimetre patch is ten times less. So I make the switch node as short and just as wide as the current requires, and no more.

Feedback and sense lines are the opposite: almost no current, high impedance, so thin and short and kept away from aggressors. Controlled-impedance traces are whatever width the stack-up needs. And at assembly, a wide copper connection into a small pad acts like a heat sink; if one end of a capacitor is on a pour and the other end isn't, it can tombstone. A short, narrow neck or a thermal relief fixes that.

So the answer: wide for current, narrow for signals and for nodes where area is a liability, and check which one dominates."
:::

::: terms
- **Current density ($J$)**: current per unit cross-section, $I/(wt)$; heating density scales with $J^2$.
- **Displacement current**: $i = C\,dv/dt$, the current driven through a capacitance by a changing voltage.
- **Common-mode current**: current that flows the same direction on the signal and the return, through parasitic capacitance, and is the main radiated-emission source.
- **Tombstoning (Manhattan effect)**: a small SMD part standing on one end after reflow because one pad wet before the other.
- **Neck**: a short, narrow section between a pad and a large copper area.
- **Controlled impedance**: a trace width chosen to give a specified $Z_0$.
- **Copper balancing**: keeping similar copper area across a layer and across layers to avoid warping.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "thick for power, thin for signals."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives resistance and current-density numbers for the power case, and the IPC width.
- Explains *why* skinny helps for the switching node and sense lines with $C\,dv/dt$ numbers.
- Distinguishes the nodes where current dominates (SW) from where area dominates (FB), and the compromise.
- Brings up tombstoning and thermal necks, which show assembly awareness.
- Notes impedance control, fabrication minimums and copper balance.
- Observes that inductance only improves logarithmically with width, so "widen it" is a weaker fix than "shorten it" or "add a plane".
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Thicker is always better."** Wide copper on a switching node increases displacement current and radiation. See [[q0550]].
- **Making a feedback trace fat for "robustness".** More area means more capacitive pickup. See [[q0552]].
- **Hooking a wide pour directly to a tiny SMD pad.** It heat-sinks one end and can cause tombstoning or a cold joint. Use a neck or relief.
- **Assuming width fixes inductance.** For an isolated strip the log dependence means 4x width yields only a modest drop; shorten the path or add a return plane. (Once a plane sits close underneath, loop inductance goes roughly as $\mu_0 h\ell/w$, so width then helps nearly in proportion.)
- **Necking a power trace to reach a pad without widening again.** The neck becomes the hot spot; keep the full width as close to the pad as possible.
- **Ignoring fabrication limits.** A 0.1 mm line at 2 oz copper may not be manufacturable.
:::

::: deep
## Why thick wins for current

For fixed length $l$, the resistance is $R = \rho l/(wt)$. Doubling $w$ halves $R$; the heating $I^2R$ halves; and since the area to shed heat doubles, the temperature rise falls by more than half. The picture is clear in numbers (1 oz, 50 mm, 5 A):

| Width | R | Drop | Dissipation | Current density |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 mm | 96 mΩ | 480 mV | 2.4 W | 571 A/mm² |
| 0.5 mm | 48 mΩ | 240 mV | 1.2 W | 286 A/mm² |
| 1 mm | 24 mΩ | 120 mV | 0.6 W | 143 A/mm² |
| 2 mm | 12 mΩ | 60 mV | 0.3 W | 71 A/mm² |
| 4 mm | 6 mΩ | 30 mV | 0.15 W | 36 A/mm² |
| 8 mm | 3 mΩ | 15 mV | 0.075 W | 18 A/mm² |

## Why skinny wins for area-sensitive nodes

A conductor of area $A$ with a dielectric thickness $h$ to the plane beneath has capacitance

$$C \approx \frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A}{h}$$

Any voltage change on that copper forces a displacement current $i = C\,dv/dt$ through the capacitance into the plane (a return path for the noise) and into neighbouring conductors. With $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$ and $h = 0.2$ mm:

- 10 mm² of switch-node copper: 1.9 pF
- 100 mm² of switch-node copper: 19 pF

At 12 V in 5 ns ($2.4\times10^9$ V/s) these inject 4.6 mA and 46 mA respectively. Because the plane current flows back through the supply and ground wiring, it becomes common-mode emissions and conducted noise at the harmonics of the edge. The same area acts as a radiating patch and a coupling plate to nearby high-impedance nodes. So for a switching node, the design goal is: minimum copper that carries the current, on one layer, no unneeded vias, no pour. See [[q0550]], [[q0547]].

For a high-impedance sense or feedback line the argument is reversed in magnitude but similar in kind: current is negligible, so there is no benefit to width, and a skinny line has less area facing aggressors. A feedback divider of 7.6 kΩ with a 0.1 pF coupling to the switch node and 5 pF at the pin gets about $12\times0.1/5.1 = 0.24$ V spike per edge. Halving the coupled area halves that. See [[q0552]].

## Where width is set by something else

**Controlled impedance.** A microstrip's $Z_0$ depends on $w/h$ and $\varepsilon_r$. On a 0.2 mm FR-4 dielectric, a 50 Ω line is a bit over 0.3 mm wide. Making it fatter lowers $Z_0$ and causes reflections.

**Fabrication.** Fabs have minimum width and spacing by copper weight; a 2 oz layer typically needs wider minimums than 0.5 oz because etching undercuts more.

**Fuses and shunts.** A deliberately narrow section opens at a known current or provides a known resistance.

## Assembly: tombstoning and necks

During reflow, solder at each end of a small chip part melts at slightly different times. If one pad is attached to a big pour that wicks heat away, the pad on the other side, tied to a thin trace, melts first; surface tension at that end can lift the part. Standard fixes:

1. Route a short, narrow trace (say 0.2 to 0.3 mm) from the pad before the pour, so each pad has similar thermal mass. 
2. Use a thermal relief on pours that touch small pads ([[q0538]]).
3. Keep pad geometry symmetric.

For *power* pads, the neck must still carry the current. A neck of 0.25 mm × 1 mm is 4 squares or about 2 mΩ and a poor choice for 10 A. This is the place for an engineering compromise: use several parallel spokes, or accept a direct connection and rely on a good reflow profile.

## Thermal consequences

A narrow link between a hot area and a cool one is a thermal resistor. A long skinny spoke can protect a sensor from a hot regulator; conversely, if you want to spread heat, make the copper wide and short ([[q0540]]). The same geometric parameters ($L/w$) control heat flow and electrical flow, and copper's thermal conductivity (≈385 W/m·K) is about a hundred times FR-4's, so board copper dominates heat flow.

## A decision table

| Net type | Width choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Supply, ground, motor, battery | Wide, or a pour | Minimise $IR$ and heating |
| Switch node | As wide as current needs, no more | Limit dV/dt coupling area |
| Feedback, sense, high-Z | Thin, short | Reduce pickup and coupling |
| Controlled impedance | Per stack-up calculation | Reflections |
| Pad entries (small parts) | Short neck | Avoid tombstoning |
| Fuse / shunt | Narrow by design | Deliberate resistance |

::: applications
- **Gate-drive and logic signals** routed at the fab's comfortable minimum, because current is negligible.
- **Buck converter** layouts with a compact, moderately wide switch-node trace and a wide input and ground.
- **Battery-pack traces** that are wide pours, since losses of milliohms cost watts at tens of amps.
- **Feedback dividers** routed as thin, short traces away from the inductor and switch node.
- **Small decoupling capacitors** with a short narrow neck from a pour to avoid tombstoning.
- **Fusible links** and **PCB heaters** where a thin trace is the whole point.
:::

::: assumptions
The numbers assume 1 oz copper on FR-4, outer layer, still air, DC current for the resistance and temperature statements, and a uniform plane at 0.2 mm beneath for capacitance. For high frequency, skin effect and proximity effect make the effective width of a trace smaller, and for very fast edges even a "narrow" node can couple strongly; for those, shielding and layer choice matter as much as width. Tombstoning also depends on paste, profile and pad design: neck rules are a heuristic, and many modern processes are tolerant. Alternative answer: some engineers say "thick unless there's a specific reason", which is equivalent to the answer above with the exceptions stated up front.
:::

::: followups
### Why is a wide switch-node pour a bad idea even though it lowers resistance?
Because the switch node's resistance is not its problem: the inductor current is modest and the node is short. Its problem is dV/dt. A wide pour increases capacitance to the plane and to neighbouring nets, which drives common-mode current and radiation, and it couples noise into the feedback and analog lines. I would keep it short, with width set by the IPC calculation and a small margin.

### How wide should I make the feedback trace?
At the fab's comfortable minimum, around 0.15 to 0.25 mm, since it carries microamps. The point is not current but to keep the exposed area small and the trace short. Width only matters if it is so thin it creates a manufacturing risk, so I would not go below the fab's recommended minimum.

### What is tombstoning and how does trace width cause it?
During reflow the two pads of a small part must melt at about the same time. If one pad connects to a large pour, copper conducts heat away and delays melting, while the other pad melts and its surface tension pulls the part upright. Using thin, equal necks, or reliefs on both pads, evens the thermal mass.

### A trace must carry 3 A but the connector pad is only 0.5 mm wide. What do you do?
Widen the trace up to the pad and widen the pad by using several pads or a pour, so the narrow section is as short as possible. For 3 A a 0.5 mm × 1 mm neck is 2 squares or about 1 mΩ and carries about 3 A at a high temperature, so it needs a thermal check. In the worst case use two parallel connector pins or a larger connector.

### Is a thicker copper weight an alternative to a wider trace?
Yes. Going from 1 oz to 2 oz halves resistance without more width. It costs more, limits minimum width and spacing, and makes fine-pitch routing harder, but it helps where space is tight. A mixed approach is common: 1 oz inner layers for signals, 2 oz outer layers for power.
:::

::: interpretations
"Skinny shapes/traces" and "thick ones" most likely refer to width, not to copper thickness. If the interviewer means copper thickness, the answer is similar in spirit: heavier copper for current, but finer pitch needs lighter copper. Siblings: length ([[q0536]]), planes ([[q0535]]), thermal reliefs ([[q0538]]).
:::
