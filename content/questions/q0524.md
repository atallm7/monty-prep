::: basics
### In plain words
A trace's characteristic impedance depends on its shape and the materials around it. Anything that adds capacitance to the plane lowers the impedance. A wider trace, a thinner dielectric, or a higher dielectric constant all do that. The opposite changes raise it. In layout you mostly change trace width, and the board stackup sets the dielectric thickness.

### Picture it
Picture a trampoline. A large, close ground surface makes it easy for the signal to "grab" the plane, which is more capacitance and lower impedance. Move the surface farther away and the grip weakens, so impedance goes up. A wider trace is like a bigger paddle facing the plane. A thicker dielectric is like moving the plane away.

### Words you need
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: $\sqrt{L'/C'}$, set by geometry ([[q0522]]).
- **Trace width ($w$)**: the copper width. It is your main layout control.
- **Dielectric height ($h$)**: the gap between trace and plane. It has the strongest effect.
- **Relative permittivity ($\varepsilon_r$)**: how much the insulator boosts capacitance. About 4.3 for FR-4.
- **Coplanar ground**: copper on the same layer next to the trace. It also adds capacitance.

### The simplest example
Since $Z_0 \propto 1/\sqrt{C'}$, raising $C'$ by 21 percent with $L'$ unchanged gives $50 / \sqrt{1.21} = 50/1.1 = 45.5\ \Omega$. In a real stackup, 0.36 mm width over 0.2 mm FR-4 gives about 50 Ω. Making the trace 10 percent wider drops it by about 2.6 Ω. Making the dielectric 10 percent thicker raises it by about 2.9 Ω. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page gives the sensitivity of each knob, extra levers like solder mask and pair spacing, and how the fab verifies the result.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can name every knob that moves $Z_0$, give its **direction and rough sensitivity**, and say which knobs you actually have in layout (width, spacing, layer/stackup) versus at the fab (dielectric thickness, material, copper weight). The core reasoning is $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$: anything that adds capacitance per length (wider trace, thinner dielectric, higher $\varepsilon_r$, nearby coplanar copper) lowers $Z_0$; anything that adds loop inductance raises it.
:::

::: why
Hitting 50/90/100 Ω is a daily layout task, and fixing an impedance problem (a pad that dips, a neck-down that peaks, a fab error, [[q0528]]) means knowing which lever to pull. Interviewers also use it to see if you know that width is the layout lever but dielectric height is the strongest and most variable one.
:::

::: concepts
- **$Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$**: capacitance up means impedance down. See [[q0522]].
- **Width $w$**: wider trace gives more $C'$ and slightly less $L'$, so lower $Z_0$.
- **Dielectric height $h$**: thicker dielectric reduces $C'$ and raises loop $L'$, so higher $Z_0$.
- **Permittivity $\varepsilon_r$**: higher $\varepsilon_r$ gives more $C'$, lower $Z_0$ (and slower signals).
- **Copper thickness $t$**: thicker copper acts like a wider trace; small effect.
- **Coupling**: nearby traces or coplanar ground lower $Z_0$ (single-ended) and set $Z_{diff}$. See [[q0519]].
:::

::: answer
"Impedance is square root of L over C per unit length, so I change geometry to change that ratio. The layout knob is trace width: wider means more capacitance to the plane, so lower impedance; narrower raises it. The biggest physical knob is the dielectric height between trace and plane: thicker dielectric means less capacitance and a higher impedance, thinner means lower. Dielectric constant works the other way: higher epsilon means more capacitance and lower impedance, which is why RF boards use lower-epsilon laminates and end up with wider traces for the same 50 ohms.

Smaller effects: thicker copper behaves a bit like a wider trace, so impedance drops slightly, and solder mask over microstrip lowers it by a couple of ohms. Putting coplanar ground pour beside the trace lowers impedance, which is how coplanar waveguide is tuned by the gap. For a differential pair, spacing matters too: closer coupling lowers the differential impedance.

For numbers: on 0.2 mm FR-4 with 1 oz copper, a 0.36 mm trace is 50 ohms. Ten percent wider drops it about 2.6 ohms; ten percent more dielectric height raises it by about 2.9 ohms. In practice I choose the stackup with the fab, then set the width with their field solver."
:::

::: technical
For microstrip, a quick first-order estimate is $Z_0\approx\dfrac{87}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r+1.41}}\ln\dfrac{5.98\,h}{0.8w+t}$ (IPC-2141, valid roughly $0.1<w/h<2$). It is good for direction and rough size, but typically a few percent off: for the geometry below it gives 47.7 Ω. Calculators and field solvers use more accurate closed forms such as Hammerstad–Jensen (with its copper-thickness correction), which is accurate to about 1% for an ideal uniform dielectric, and that is the model behind every number in the table below and on this page.

**Sensitivities** (Hammerstad–Jensen; FR-4 $\varepsilon_r=4.3$, $h=0.2$ mm, $t=35$ µm, $w=0.36$ mm, $Z_0=49.9\ \Omega$; the IPC-2141 formula gives 47.7 Ω here and somewhat larger slopes, compared in the deep-dive):
| Change | New $Z_0$ | Δ |
|---|---|---|
| width +10% | 47.3 Ω | −2.6 Ω (−5%) |
| dielectric height +10% | 52.8 Ω | +2.9 Ω (+6%) |
| $\varepsilon_r$ +10% | 47.9 Ω | −2.0 Ω (−4%) |
| copper 35→18 µm | 50.9 Ω | +1.0 Ω |
| copper 35→70 µm | 48.4 Ω | −1.5 Ω |

So $h$ and $w$ dominate; copper weight and $\varepsilon_r$ variation are secondary.

**Other levers.** Solder mask over microstrip lowers $Z_0$ by roughly 1–3 Ω (it raises $\varepsilon_{eff}$). Coplanar pour near the trace lowers $Z_0$ (CPW). Stripline between planes has lower $Z_0$ than microstrip of the same $w$, $h$, since the field sees dielectric on both sides. Differential: $Z_{diff}\approx2Z_0(1-0.48e^{-0.96s/h})$; reducing $s$ lowers $Z_{diff}$. Plane cut-outs under pads raise local $Z_0$ by increasing effective $h$. Local width changes (necks, tapers) change local $Z_0$ ([[q0516]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Solder-mask and copper-roughness effects depend on the mask thickness and process; treat the ±1–3 Ω figure as an estimate and let the fab's stackup tool decide.
:::

::: long
"Characteristic impedance is the square root of inductance per length over capacitance per length. A microstrip is basically a trace over a plane, so capacitance comes from the trace area facing the plane and inductance from the loop. So the knobs are the ones that change those.

Trace width is the one I control in layout. A wider trace has more area over the plane, more capacitance, a little less inductance, so lower impedance. The dielectric height between the trace and its plane is the strongest knob: a thicker dielectric means less capacitance and more loop inductance, so higher impedance. That's why the fab's thickness tolerance matters so much. Dielectric constant: higher epsilon, more capacitance, lower impedance, which also slows the signal. Copper thickness has a smaller effect: thicker copper acts like a slightly wider trace.

Then there are structural changes. Stripline versus microstrip: a buried trace has dielectric on both sides, so for the same width and height it's lower impedance. Solder mask over microstrip lowers it a couple of ohms. Coplanar ground pour next to the trace lowers it and is how you tune coplanar waveguide. For a differential pair, the spacing matters: moving the traces closer lowers the differential impedance, so a tighter pair needs narrower traces for the same 100 ohms.

With numbers: on 0.2 millimetre FR-4 with one-ounce copper, 0.36 millimetres is 50 ohms. Ten percent wider drops it about 2.6 ohms, ten percent thicker dielectric adds about 2.9. In practice I give the target to the fab, use their stackup and field solver, and request a test coupon to verify."
:::

::: terms
- **Trace width ($w$)**: copper width; primary layout control.
- **Dielectric height ($h$)**: spacing between trace and reference plane.
- **Relative permittivity ($\varepsilon_r$)**: dielectric constant, about 4.3 for FR-4.
- **Copper thickness ($t$)**: 17, 35, 70 µm (½, 1, 2 oz).
- **Coplanar gap**: spacing between trace and adjacent same-layer ground.
- **Edge spacing ($s$)**: gap between pair traces.
- **Anti-pad / plane cut-out**: removal of plane copper under a pad to raise local $Z_0$.
- **Impedance coupon**: test structure on the panel for TDR verification.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "change the width." An exceptional answer:
- Starts from $\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and predicts the direction of every knob from capacitance.
- Gives **sensitivities** with numbers and says which knobs are layout versus fab controlled.
- Mentions **solder mask, coplanar copper, stripline vs microstrip, plane cut-outs and spacing** for pairs.
- Says the **tolerance** (±10%) and verification (coupon/TDR) and how to ask the fab.
- Notes the trade-offs: wide traces for low $Z_0$ use area; narrow traces for high $Z_0$ add loss.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking only width matters.** Dielectric height varies most between stackups and often dominates.
- **Getting the direction of $\varepsilon_r$ backwards.** Higher $\varepsilon_r$ means lower $Z_0$.
- **Changing the width but not the reference.** If the reference layer is different (thicker prepreg), width alone won't hit target.
- **Ignoring solder mask and roughness** when comparing with measurement.
- **Using the single-ended width for a differential pair** without accounting for coupling.
- **Assuming $Z_0$ depends on length or on DC resistance.**
:::

::: deep
## Why each knob works

Treat the microstrip as a parallel-plate capacitor with fringing: $C'\approx\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_{eff}\,w_{eff}/h$. Loop inductance per length falls with wider conductor and smaller $h$. With $v=1/\sqrt{L'C'}$ nearly set by the dielectric,
$$Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}=\frac{1}{vC'}\ \Rightarrow\ Z_0\propto\frac{h}{w_{eff}\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}}\ \text{(roughly)}$$
That single proportionality predicts all the arrows: $Z_0$ rises with $h$, falls with $w$ and $\varepsilon$. It's only a wide-trace approximation; the real dependence is logarithmic for narrow traces.

## Lever by lever

1. **Width.** Free in layout, but constrained by fab minimums and by space. Local changes (necks, tapers) create reflections ([[q0516]]).
2. **Dielectric height.** Chosen by the stackup (core/prepreg thickness). A 4-layer board with 0.2 mm prepreg to the plane gives 0.36 mm 50 Ω traces; a 2-layer board with 1.6 mm core needs about 3 mm traces. This is the reason 2-layer boards are bad for impedance control.
3. **Material.** Lower $\varepsilon_r$ (e.g. 3.0) needs a wider trace (0.47 mm at h = 0.2 mm), and gives faster, lower-loss lines.
4. **Copper weight.** Thick copper lowers $Z_0$ slightly but the etch undercut makes narrow traces harder.
5. **Coplanar and solder mask.** Adding ground pour at a gap $g$ lowers $Z_0$ and gives tighter fields; mask lowers $Z_0$ by a few ohms.
6. **Reference plane position.** Moving the signal to a layer next to a different plane (or removing plane copper below a pad) changes $h$ locally.
7. **Pair spacing.** For $h=0.2$ mm, $w=0.242$ mm: $s=0.1$ mm gives 86 Ω, 0.2 mm gives 100 Ω, 0.4 mm gives 114 Ω.

## Worked example

Target 50 Ω on a 6-layer board where the fab offers prepreg of 0.1 mm or 0.2 mm to the reference. With h = 0.1 mm: $w=0.17$ mm (tight, higher loss, narrower than many fabs like for volume); with h = 0.2 mm: $w=0.36$ mm. Choose 0.2 mm if routing space allows. Tolerance check: ±10% on $h$ gives about ±3 Ω, ±10% on $w$ about ±2.6 Ω; combined root-sum-square about ±4 Ω (8%), consistent with a ±10% specification.

## Which formula produced the numbers above

Be clear about the model behind a number. The sensitivity table above (and the 0.36 mm width for 50 Ω) come from the Hammerstad–Jensen model that most calculators use, which gives $Z_0 = 49.9\ \Omega$ for $w = 0.36$ mm, $h = 0.2$ mm, $t = 35\ \mu$m, $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$ (and $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3.13$). The simpler IPC-2141 expression quoted in the technical section gives 47.7 Ω for the same geometry, about 5% low, so it would put the 50 Ω width at 0.335 mm instead of 0.36 mm, about 0.025 mm too narrow. The two models also disagree a little on slopes, which is what matters for tolerance:

| Change | Hammerstad–Jensen | IPC-2141 formula |
|---|---|---|
| $h$ +10% | +5.7% | +7.3% |
| $w$ +10% | −5.3% | −6.5% |
| $\varepsilon_r$ +10% | −4.1% | −3.6% |

Both agree on the ranking (height and width dominate, permittivity is third), so the qualitative lever list is safe, but for a number you send to the fab, use a calculator or the fab's field solver with the real stack-up. A good habit is to run the same geometry through two tools and treat any gap larger than a few percent as a reason to ask the fab.

## Why the simple "parallel plate" picture only works for very wide traces

The proportionality $Z_0\propto h/(w\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}})$ comes from treating the trace and plane as a parallel-plate capacitor with $Z_0 = (377\ \Omega)\,h/(w\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}})$. Check it against the full model for $h=0.2$ mm and $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$ (the estimate uses each width's own $\varepsilon_{eff}$):

| $w/h$ | Full model | Parallel-plate estimate |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 87 Ω | 453 Ω |
| 1 | 67 Ω | 220 Ω |
| 2 | 47 Ω | 106 Ω |
| 4 | 30 Ω | 51 Ω |
| 8 | 17.7 Ω | 24.6 Ω |
| 16 | 9.8 Ω | 12.0 Ω |

The estimate converges only for $w/h \gg 10$, because fringing fields at the trace edges add capacitance that the simple formula ignores, and they matter more the narrower the trace is relative to $h$. The 50 Ω traces you actually build have $w/h\approx1.5$ to $2$, firmly in the fringing-dominated region, which is why the real dependence is logarithmic (as in the IPC formula) and not simply $1/w$. Use the proportionality only for the signs of the arrows, not for numbers.

## Worked example: the fab reports different dielectric thickness

Your calculator says 0.36 mm for 50 Ω on $h=0.2$ mm prepreg. The fab's as-built report comes back with the prepreg pressed to 0.18 mm (resin has flowed into dense copper). If you leave the drawn width at 0.36 mm the impedance drops to roughly 47 Ω. Resolving for 50 Ω at each height with the Hammerstad–Jensen model gives:

| As-built $h$ | Width for 50 Ω |
|---|---|
| 0.18 mm | 0.32 mm |
| 0.20 mm | 0.36 mm |
| 0.22 mm | 0.40 mm |

The same effect arises from $\varepsilon_r$: at 4.0 the 50 Ω width is 0.38 mm and at 4.6 it is 0.34 mm. A ±10% variation in $h$ shifts the width needed by about ±0.04 mm, which is the reason fabs ask for a target impedance and adjust the width themselves, and why a controlled-impedance order comes with a tolerance (often ±10%, sometimes tighter at higher cost).

## Local impedance changes: necks and pads

A discontinuity is only a problem if it is long compared with the signal's rise time. The trace delay here is about $\sqrt{3.13}/c = 5.9$ ps/mm. Consider a 0.2 mm neck (the trace narrows to squeeze between two pads): the model gives 67 Ω, so the reflection coefficient at the step is $\Gamma = (67-50)/(67+50) = +0.145$ (a 14.5% positive reflection). If the neck is 2 mm long, its round-trip delay is 24 ps, which is a small fraction of a 200 ps edge, so the neck acts like a small series inductance with a modest bump, not a full reflection. If it is 20 mm long, it is a proper 67 Ω line segment and reflects almost the whole step. The rule of thumb that a discontinuity shorter than about a sixth of the rise time (in distance) can be ignored is the logic behind keeping necks short. See [[q0516]].

A connector or test-point pad is the opposite: a 1.0 mm wide pad over the same $h=0.2$ mm computes to only about 26 Ω (excess capacitance), a negative dip. Cutting the reference plane away under the pad pushes the return to the next plane. If we assume that plane is 0.735 mm away, the same model gives about 60 Ω: now it's inductive and over-corrected. The fix is to size the cutout so the pad lands near 50 Ω, which is what a field solver or a TDR measurement on a prototype tells you. Each of these is a "lever" from the list above applied locally.

## Bench: confirming it with a TDR

A time-domain reflectometer launches a fast step into the line and shows the reflected voltage as an impedance profile, $Z(t)=50\,(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)$ for a 50 Ω system. Practical notes:

- Measure a **coupon** or a test trace of the same width and layer on the same panel. The fab's coupon is the contractual measurement and is made for exactly this.
- The instrument's rise time sets the spatial resolution. With a 35 ps edge the edge occupies about $35\text{ ps}/5.9\text{ ps/mm} \approx 6$ mm of line, and because the reflection travels there and back, features closer together than about half that (3 mm) blur together, so a short neck will be smeared; use the fastest edge available for fine features.
- Read the impedance in the middle of the line, not at the launch where the probe or connector discontinuity sits, and report the average over a stretch.
- Expect a few ohms of spread between boards, and ask whether the difference tracks the as-built thickness, because that is the usual culprit.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The cutout example assumes a uniform microstrip model with an assumed 0.735 mm to the next plane. Real pad impedance depends on pad shape, the cutout outline, vias and nearby ground, so treat these numbers as qualitative and verify by simulation or TDR.

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Etch factor**: traces are trapezoidal, the top narrower than the bottom; the fab compensates the drawn width.
- **Resin flow**: prepreg thickness after lamination is less than nominal where copper is dense; the fab supplies "as-built" heights.
- **Test**: TDR on coupons at the panel edge; ±10% is the usual acceptance.
:::

::: applications
- **Controlled-impedance stackups** for DDR, PCIe, USB: choose $h$ then solve for $w$.
- **RF boards**: low-$\varepsilon_r$ laminate and grounded coplanar waveguide with via fences.
- **BGA breakout**: neck-downs with plane cut-outs to keep local impedance.
- **Pad and connector footprints**: anti-pad cut-outs raise impedance to cancel pad capacitance.
- **Impedance tuning of a rework**: adding copper tape or a reference-plane patch to modify a mis-built line ([[q0528]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- Numbers use a Hammerstad–Jensen model for FR-4 microstrip; field solvers differ by about 1–3 Ω, and the fab's measured stackup is the authority.
- Sensitivity percentages are local (small changes) and vary with $w/h$.
- The "capacitance up, impedance down" rule assumes $L'$ changes less than $C'$, which is true for ordinary geometries.
- Active/dynamic changes of impedance (e.g. varactor-tuned lines) aren't considered.
:::

::: followups
### Which is the more effective way to lower the impedance by 10 Ω: widen the trace or thin the dielectric?
Both work. From 50 Ω, widening 0.36→0.60 mm gives about 37 Ω, a 13 Ω drop but a big routing cost. Halving $h$ (0.2→0.1 mm) at the same width gives about 32 Ω... but needs a stackup change. Width is the layout fix; $h$ is a fab/stackup choice.

### How do you raise the impedance of a short section, for example under a big pad?
Cut away the reference plane under the pad (an anti-pad) so the effective height is the distance to the next plane. This lowers $C'$ and raises $Z_0$, cancelling the pad's capacitance.

### Why do RF boards use low-$\varepsilon_r$ laminate?
For the same impedance the line is wider, which has lower conductor loss and is less sensitive to etch tolerance; low loss tangent also helps. Delay per length also shrinks.

### What if the measured impedance is 10% off the target?
Check the as-built stackup first (dielectric height and $\varepsilon_r$ explain most error), then width after etch. Ask the fab to adjust the width for the next lot, or change the design width using their measured $h$.

### How does a nearby trace change a single-ended impedance?
Coupling adds capacitance, so $Z_0$ drops a little, only a few percent when spacing is ≥ 3 W, more when close. That is the same coupling that sets differential impedance.
:::
