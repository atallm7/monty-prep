::: testing
Whether you can go from $v = L\,di/dt$ to the triangle waveform and, crucially, *state the assumption* that makes it linear: the voltage across the inductor is (nearly) constant during each state. Strong candidates also say what controls the slope (volts and inductance, not the load) and when the ramp stops being linear (saturation, large resistive drops, DCM edge cases).
:::

::: why
The linear ramp is the foundation for the ripple-current formula, inductor sizing, peak-current limits, DCM boundary, and the current-mode control that senses the ramp. If you understand why it is linear you also understand why it *stops* being linear when an inductor saturates, which is a classic way for a buck to blow up its FET. Interviewers use this to test whether you can reason from a differential equation instead of drawing a memorized triangle.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor equation** $v_L = L\,di_L/dt$: the voltage sets the *rate of change* of current. See [[q0041]].
- **Constant voltage ⇒ constant slope**: integrate a constant to get a line.
- **Continuity**: $i_L$ cannot jump, so the ramps join end to end into a triangle. See [[q0043]].
- **Switch node as a two-level source**: it applies either $V_{in}$ or $\approx0$.
- **Small-ripple approximation**: $V_{out}$ barely changes in a cycle. See [[q0194]].
- **RL step response**: the exact solution is an exponential; with $L/R\gg T$ it is a line. See [[q0044]].
:::

::: answer
"It comes straight from the inductor equation: $v = L\,di/dt$. The voltage across the inductor decides how fast the current changes. In the buck, during the on state the inductor sees the input minus the output, so about 8.7 V in my example, and that's essentially constant because the output is held steady by the capacitor and the input is a stiff supply. A constant voltage means a constant $di/dt$, so the current is a straight line with slope $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$. During the off state the inductor sees minus $V_{out}$, again constant, so the current ramps down in a straight line with slope $-V_{out}/L$.

What determines it is just the volts across the inductor and the inductance. The load current doesn't change the slope; it only sets where the triangle sits, because the average of the triangle is the load current. With 4.7 µH that's about 1.85 A per microsecond up and 0.7 down.

It's an approximation: it relies on the ripple being small, so the output voltage isn't moving. If the inductor saturates, $L$ collapses and the slope gets steeper, and if resistive drops are large the ramp is slightly curved because it's really an RL exponential."
:::

::: technical
**Governing equation.** $v_L(t)=L\,\frac{di_L}{dt}$.

**On state** ($0<t<DT$): $v_L = V_{in}-I_L R_{on} - v_{out}\approx V_{in}-V_{out}$. Integrating,
$$i_L(t) = i_L(0) + \frac{V_{in}-V_{out}}{L}\,t.$$

**Off state** ($DT<t<T$): $v_L = -V_{out}-V_f$ (or $-I_L R_{ds}$) $\approx -V_{out}$, so
$$i_L(t) = i_L(DT) - \frac{V_{out}}{L}(t-DT).$$

**Periodicity** gives $\Delta I_L = \frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})DT}{L} = \frac{V_{out}(1-D)T}{L}$, which for the example is 1.02 A with slopes $+1.85$ and $-0.70$ A/µs.

**Exact form.** The honest equation is $L\,di/dt = V_{applied} - v_C - i R_{total}$ with $R_{total}$ = DCR plus switch resistance. This is an RL charging curve with time constant $L/R_{total}$. For $L=4.7$ µH and $R_{total}=30$ mΩ, $\tau=157$ µs $\gg T = 2$ µs, so over a cycle the exponential is straight to within $T/(2\tau)\approx0.6\%$. Likewise $v_C$ varies by about 12 mV against 8.7 V, 0.14%.

**When it fails.**
- Core saturation: $L$ drops, slope rises (sim below).
- Very low $L/R$ (large DCR or heavily loaded switches).
- DCM: after $i_L=0$ it stays flat for a while (diode blocks).
- Very large output ripple or when $V_{out}$ is sagging.

**Load independence.** The slopes do not contain $I_{out}$. Changing the load shifts the triangle vertically (and the DCM/CCM boundary at $I_{out}=\Delta I/2$).
:::

::: short
"It follows from $v = L\,di/dt$. In the on state the inductor sees roughly $V_{in}-V_{out}$, constant, so the current rises linearly with slope $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$. In the off state it sees $-V_{out}$, constant, so it falls linearly at $V_{out}/L$. The slope depends only on those voltages and $L$, not on the load; the load just sets the average of the triangle. It's only approximately linear, assuming small ripple and negligible resistive drops, and it breaks if the inductor saturates."
:::

::: long
"The inductor's defining equation is $v = L\,di/dt$. It says the voltage across it sets how fast the current changes. So if I know the voltage as a function of time I can integrate to get the current.

In the buck the switch node jumps between the input voltage and roughly zero. The output is held almost constant by the capacitor, 3.3 V in my example, so the voltage across the inductor is just a two-level square wave: 12 minus 3.3, that's 8.7 V, during the on time and minus 3.3 V during the off time. A constant voltage integrates to a straight line. So during the on time the current climbs at 8.7 over 4.7 µH, 1.85 A per microsecond, and during the off time it falls at 3.3 over 4.7 µH, 0.7 A per microsecond. The two ramps join because the inductor current can't jump.

What determines the slope? Only the voltage across the inductor and its inductance. Not the load: a heavier load shifts the whole triangle up but doesn't tilt it. If I want less ripple, I increase $L$ or the frequency so that the current has less time to climb.

I should say it's an idealization. The true waveform is a slightly curved RL exponential, and the output has a few mV of ripple, but with a time constant of hundreds of microseconds against a 2 µs period you can't tell. The case where the linear picture really breaks is saturation: when the core saturates the inductance drops, and the slope $V/L$ steepens dramatically, which is how a buck destroys its high-side FET."
:::

::: terms
- **$di/dt$ slope**: rate of current change, $v_L/L$, in A/µs.
- **Volt-second**: voltage × time across the inductor; sets the current change, $\Delta I = (V\cdot t)/L$.
- **Ripple current $\Delta I_L$**: the peak-to-peak value of the triangle.
- **DCR**: DC resistance of the inductor winding; makes the ramp slightly curved.
- **Saturation current $I_{sat}$**: current at which inductance falls (typically by 20 to 30%) due to core saturation.
- **Time constant $L/R$**: sets how well the ramp is approximated by a line.
- **Small-ripple approximation**: treating $V_{out}$ as constant over a cycle.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Because the voltage across the inductor is constant, so $di/dt$ is constant."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Writes $L\,di/dt = v_L$ and gives both slopes with numbers.
- Explains *why* $v_L$ is constant: the output is held by the capacitor and the input by the source or its capacitor.
- States that the slope is independent of load, and that load sets the DC offset.
- Quantifies the approximation (exponential with large $\tau$, ripple of the output tiny).
- Brings up saturation as the failure case and why a peak current limit matters.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The ramp is linear because the inductor charges at a constant current."** It is the *voltage* that is constant; the current is what ramps.
- **"Heavier load makes the current ramp faster."** The load only shifts the average; the slopes depend on $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$ and $L$.
- **Using one slope for both states.** The on and off slopes differ (here +1.85 and −0.70 A/µs) because the voltages differ.
- **Claiming it is an exponential.** It is only if you include resistance and a *large* ripple; the time constant is orders of magnitude longer than the cycle.
- **Assuming it stays linear above $I_{sat}$.** Past saturation, $L$ falls and the slope gets steeper. Choose $I_{sat}$ above the peak current plus margin.
:::

::: deep
## From the inductor equation to a line

The voltage across an ideal inductor sets the *derivative* of its current:

$$v_L = L\frac{di_L}{dt}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad i_L(t) = i_L(t_0)+\frac1L\int_{t_0}^{t} v_L\,d\tau.$$

If $v_L$ is a constant $V$ over a time interval, the integral is $V\,(t-t_0)$ and the current is a straight line with slope $V/L$. That is all the "linear ramp" is. Everything depends on the question: *is $v_L$ really constant in each state?*

Why is it constant?
- The switch node is $V_{in}$ or $\approx 0$ depending on the FET, a hard, stiff square wave.
- The output node is $V_{out}\pm$ a few mV (output capacitor).
- So $v_L=V_{SW}-V_{out}$ changes only at the switching edges.

$$\left.\frac{di_L}{dt}\right|_{on}=\frac{V_{in}-V_{out}}{L},\qquad \left.\frac{di_L}{dt}\right|_{off}=-\frac{V_{out}}{L}$$

So four things matter: $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$, $L$, and, via resistive drops, the DCR and switch resistances. Not $I_{out}$. The next figure keeps everything the same and varies only the load:

## How linear is it, really?

Include the resistances in the on state: $L\,di/dt = V_{in}-v_C-i R$, where $R=R_{DCR}+R_{ds(on)}$ (about 30 mΩ). That is a first-order RL charge toward $(V_{in}-v_C)/R$ with $\tau=L/R$:

$$\tau=\frac{4.7\ \mu H}{0.03\ \Omega}=157\ \mu s\gg T=2\ \mu s.$$

Over a cycle the exponential $1-e^{-t/\tau}$ is straight to within a fraction of a percent ($t_{on}/(2\tau)\approx0.2\%$). The capacitor ripple of 12 mV on 8.7 V adds about 0.14% curvature. So "linear" is a very good approximation for a well-designed buck.

> [!TIP]
> Say "it's the beginning of an RL exponential with a time constant of hundreds of microseconds, so over one 2 µs cycle it's a straight line." That one sentence shows you understand why a linear model is acceptable.

## When it is not linear: saturation

Magnetic inductance is $L = N^2/\mathcal{R}$. In a saturated core the permeability collapses and $L$ falls. The slope $V/L$ then gets larger and the current runs away within a cycle.

## DCM: the ramp is still linear, but incomplete

If the load is light, the falling ramp reaches zero before the cycle ends. The current is flat at zero (the diode blocks) and the ramps are *still linear*, just truncated. The slopes are unchanged. See [[q0185]] for the switch-node ringing that follows.

::: applications
- **Ripple-current sizing**: $\Delta I_L = V_{out}(1-D)/(L f)$ and the 20 to 40% of $I_{out}$ design rule come directly from this ramp.
- **Peak current-mode control**: the controller compares the ramping inductor current (sensed through a resistor or the FET) with a threshold; it relies on predictable slopes.
- **Current limits and short-circuit protection**: knowing $di/dt = V/L$ tells you how fast current rises during a fault before the comparator reacts.
- **Inductor selection**: $I_{sat}$ must be above $I_{out}+\Delta I/2$ at the highest temperature.
- **Simulation sanity checks** and compensation design (slope compensation for current-mode converters above 50% duty cycle).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Constant $V_{in}$ and $V_{out}$** during a cycle (small ripple).
- **Linear inductor** (below saturation, constant $L$ over current and temperature).
- **Negligible resistive drops**, or $L/R\gg T$.
- **CCM or DCM**: slopes are the same, only the shape of the ends changes.
- **Frequency**: at high frequency, core and winding losses and parasitic capacitance make the real current non-ideal, with spikes at edges.
- **Alternative answer**: some interviewers want the analogy "a constant voltage across an inductor is like a constant force on a mass: velocity (current) increases linearly".
:::

::: followups
### What would the current look like if the voltage across the inductor were sinusoidal?
Then $i_L = \frac{1}{L}\int V\sin\omega t\,dt$, a cosine of amplitude $V/(\omega L)$, 90° behind the voltage. The buck's triangular current is the same integral applied to a rectangular wave.

### The inductor current ramps up faster than it ramps down in my example. Why?
Because the on-state voltage ($8.7$ V) is larger than the off-state voltage ($3.3$ V). The areas are equal ($8.7\times0.55\ \mu s = 3.3\times1.45\ \mu s$) so the up and down changes match. At $D=0.5$ the voltages would be equal in magnitude only if $V_{out}=V_{in}/2$.

### What happens to the slope if the inductor saturates?
$L$ falls (a drop to 50% or less is common past the knee), so $di/dt=V/L$ rises. The current runs away, and the peak current is no longer limited by the design equation. Controllers use cycle-by-cycle current limit to turn off the high-side FET quickly, but if the ramp is steep enough the limit comparator delay (tens of ns) lets the current overshoot: with 8.7 V and an effective 1 µH, 50 ns of delay gives an extra 0.4 A.

### How do you pick $L$ so the current ramp stays within a spec?
Pick the ripple ratio $r=\Delta I/I_{out}$ (typically 0.2 to 0.4), then $L = \frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{r I_{out} f}$ at the highest $V_{in}$. For $r=0.3$: $L = 3.3\times0.725/(0.3\times2\times5\times10^5)= 8\ \mu H$. Check $I_{sat} > I_{out}(1+r/2)$ with margin.

### If the slope is $V/L$, why not make $L$ enormous?
Larger $L$ means a larger, higher-DCR inductor with lower saturation current for the same volume, and a slower response: the inductor can only change its current at $V/L$, so a load step takes longer to be supplied, and the capacitor must cover it.
:::

::: interpretations
"Linear ramp" means constant slope, which is what the idealized buck gives. The real waveform is a slightly curved exponential (and a different shape in DCM edges and saturation). The question "what determines this behavior" is answered by the voltage across the inductor and its inductance; the companion question [[q0195]] asks the same from the perspective of what the slope depends on.
:::
