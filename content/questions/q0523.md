::: basics
### In plain words
Characteristic impedance is the fixed ratio of voltage to current for a wave traveling down a line. A handful of standard values are used everywhere: 50 Ω for most RF and board traces, 75 Ω for video, and 90, 100 or 120 Ω for differential pairs depending on the interface. The standard for each interface sets the number. You pick the board stackup and trace width to hit it.

### Picture it
Think of garden hoses and nozzles. If the hose and the nozzle are the same size, the water flows through without splashing back. If they differ, some water bounces back. Impedance values work the same way: everything along the signal path (driver, trace, cable, receiver) should use the same standard, so nothing reflects.

### Words you need
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: $\sqrt{L'/C'}$, set by the line's geometry and materials, not its length ([[q0710]]).
- **Differential impedance ($Z_{diff}$)**: the impedance between the two traces of a pair, about twice the odd-mode impedance ([[q0518]]).
- **Reflection coefficient ($\Gamma$)**: the fraction of a wave that bounces back at a mismatch, $(Z_2 - Z_1)/(Z_2 + Z_1)$ ([[q0714]]).
- **Tolerance**: the allowed error, usually about 10 percent on a board.
- **Controlled impedance**: a board ordered with a target impedance that the fab checks on a test coupon.

### The simplest example
A 60 Ω trace connects to a 50 Ω system. The reflection coefficient is $(60 - 50)/(60 + 50) = 0.09$. So about 9 percent of the wave bounces back. For a 10 percent tolerance, the worst case is about 5 percent. On a short trace with slow edges, you will not notice it. On a long, fast channel, small reflections add up. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds where each standard value comes from, why 50 Ω became the default, and how a coupled pair differs from two 50 Ω lines.
:::

::: why
Impedance is the first number on a fab note and the first question in a high-speed design review. If you quote "50 Ω everywhere" you'll mis-design USB or CAN; if you only recite a list you won't know how to defend a deviation. The values also tell you what the driver, termination and cable must be, tying back to matching ([[q0714]]) and termination ([[q0532]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Characteristic impedance $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$**: set by geometry and dielectric, not length. See [[q0710]], [[q0522]].
- **Single-ended vs differential**: $Z_{diff}=2Z_{odd}$; common-mode impedance $Z_{cm}=Z_{even}/2$. See [[q0518]].
- **Impedance matching**: source, line and load impedances equal means no reflection. See [[q0714]].
- **Standards define the target**: each interface specifies its impedance and tolerance (typically ±10–15%).
- **Why 50 Ω**: a historical compromise in coax between minimum loss (≈77 Ω) and maximum power handling (≈30 Ω).
- **Impedance and trace geometry**: lower $Z_0$ means wider traces over the same dielectric. See [[q0524]].
:::

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> PCIe impedance guidance has varied (100 Ω in the CEM/connector context, 85 Ω in some Gen3+ channel guidelines), and DDR single-ended impedance targets differ by generation and vendor. Always take the number from the interface specification or the SoC/memory layout guide.

**Why 50 Ω in coax.** For an air-filled coax with fixed outer radius, conductor loss is minimised at $D/d=3.59$ ($Z_0=60\ln(D/d)=77\ \Omega$), and maximum power handling (before breakdown) occurs at $D/d=1.65$ ($Z_0=30\ \Omega$). 50 Ω gives about 10% more loss than the minimum and about 86% of the maximum power: a good compromise.

**On a PCB.** With a 0.2 mm FR-4 dielectric ($\varepsilon_r=4.3$, 1 oz Cu): 50 Ω needs $w\approx0.36$ mm; 75 Ω needs $w\approx0.15$ mm (hard to fabricate and lossy); 28 Ω needs $w\approx0.88$ mm. 50 Ω balances width, loss and driver strength. For a 100 Ω pair with $s=0.2$ mm: $w\approx0.24$ mm; 90 Ω: $w\approx0.30$ mm.

::: terms
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: voltage-to-current ratio of a single travelling wave on the line.
- **Single-ended impedance**: $Z_0$ of one trace relative to the plane.
- **Differential impedance ($Z_{diff}$)**: impedance between the two lines of a pair, $2Z_{odd}$.
- **Odd / even mode**: modes in which the lines are driven oppositely / identically.
- **Common-mode impedance**: $Z_{even}/2$, the impedance of both lines driven together.
- **Controlled impedance**: a board specification with tolerance (±10% typical) verified by coupons.
- **Matched termination**: resistor equal to $Z_0$ that absorbs the wave.
- **Free-space impedance ($\eta_0$)**: $\approx377\ \Omega$.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "50 Ω and 100 Ω." An exceptional answer:

- Explains the **50 Ω compromise** (77 Ω minimum loss, 30 Ω maximum power) with the numbers.
- Distinguishes **single-ended and differential** and shows that coupled traces are not simply $2\times50$ Ω.
- Gives **width examples** for a stackup and notes the ±10% tolerance, coupons and field-solver use.

:::

::: mistakes
- **"All traces should be 50 Ω."** Slow traces needn't be controlled, and differential standards specify 90/100/120 Ω.
- **Treating 100 Ω diff as two 50 Ω singles.** Coupling lowers $Z_{odd}$, so the trace impedances differ. See [[q0519]].
- **Quoting 50 Ω as a physical constant.** It's a convention; some systems use 75 Ω or 28 Ω.
- **Forgetting tolerance.** ±10% on $Z_0$ is typical (about 5% reflection at worst).
- **Using the wrong value for USB** (90 Ω, not 100 Ω).
- **Confusing characteristic impedance with the resistance of the trace.** A 50 Ω line has milliohm DC resistance.
:::

::: deep
## Where the standard numbers come from

**50 Ω: a coax compromise.** For an air coax with inner diameter $d$ and outer diameter $D$,
$$Z_0=\frac{\eta_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac{D}{d}\approx60\ln\frac{D}{d}\ \Omega$$
Conductor loss at fixed $D$ scales as $\alpha\propto\frac{1}{D}\cdot\frac{1+D/d}{\ln(D/d)}$, which has a minimum at $D/d=3.59$, i.e. $Z_0=77\ \Omega$. The maximum voltage before breakdown is $V\propto d\ln(D/d)$ and the power $P\propto V^2/Z_0$, which peaks at $D/d=\sqrt e=1.65$, i.e. $Z_0=30\ \Omega$. The geometric mean-ish compromise, 50 Ω, costs ~10% more loss than the optimum and keeps 86% of the maximum power.

At 50 Ω loss is about 1.10× the minimum and power is about 0.86× the maximum. Cable and instrument makers settled on it, and the PCB world inherited it.

**75 Ω.** Video and cable TV use 75 Ω because they care about loss (lowest-loss coax is 77 Ω) rather than power; a bonus is that a 75 Ω dipole's impedance is near 73 Ω.

**Differential values.** Twisted-pair cables have a characteristic impedance of about 100 Ω (Ethernet, 100 Ω Category cable) or 120 Ω (CAN/RS-485 industrial twisted pair). PCB designs for these links mimic the cable so that the whole channel is matched. USB chose 90 Ω; the PCB pair is built to 90 Ω ±15%.

**DDR.** Memory buses care about drive strength and signal swing in a multi-drop/fly-by topology; the SoC/DRAM vendor defines single-ended trace impedance (often in the 40–60 Ω range) and the termination (on-die termination, ODT) values.

## Differential vs single-ended numbers

For a coupled pair, the differential impedance is $Z_{diff}=2Z_{odd}$, and $Z_{odd}<Z_0$ because coupling adds capacitance between the lines. For an edge-coupled pair with $h=0.2$ mm and $s=0.2$ mm:

| Target $Z_{diff}$ | Width $w$ | Single-ended $Z_0$ of each trace (alone) |
|---|---|---|
| 100 Ω | 0.24 mm | ≈61 Ω |
| 90 Ω | 0.30 mm | ≈55 Ω |
| 85 Ω | 0.33 mm | ≈52 Ω |

So a 100 Ω pair is built from traces that would be about 61 Ω alone, narrower than the 50 Ω single-ended trace on the same stackup (0.36 mm). These widths use the Hammerstad–Jensen microstrip model (the one in the calculator above) with the IPC-2141A coupling factor $1-0.48e^{-0.96s/h}$. The simpler IPC-2141 formula $Z_0\approx\frac{87}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r+1.41}}\ln\frac{5.98h}{0.8w+t}$ gives traces about 5 % narrower on this stackup (0.335 mm for 50 Ω, as in [[q0476]]; 0.285 mm for the 90 Ω pair, as in [[q0491]]). The difference is the single-ended model, not the physics, and it is smaller than fab etch tolerance.

## Why impedance values matter for the rest of the system

- **Driver output and series termination**: a driver of $R_{out}=20\ \Omega$ + 30 Ω series resistor matches 50 Ω. See [[q0532]].
- **Terminations**: 50 Ω to VTT, 100 Ω differential, 120 Ω CAN.
- **Return loss**: with $Z_{load}$ off by 10%, $\Gamma\approx0.05$ ($-26$ dB); with a 75 Ω trace into a 50 Ω system, $\Gamma=0.2$.
- **Mixed systems** use matching pads or transformers (75↔50 Ω minimum-loss pad about 5.7 dB).

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Fab tolerance:** ±10% is standard, ±5% available; width, dielectric thickness and $\varepsilon_r$ all vary.
- **Frequency dependence:** the number is defined at high frequency (hundreds of MHz–GHz); at low frequency a real line looks different ([[q0525]]).
- **Impedance of connectors, cables and packages** also matter; a link is only as matched as its worst section.

## Worked example: what an impedance error costs

The tolerances in the table (±10 %, ±15 %) make more sense once you convert them into reflections and loss. The reflection coefficient at a step from $Z_1$ to $Z_2$ is $\Gamma=(Z_2-Z_1)/(Z_2+Z_1)$, the return loss is $-20\log_{10}|\Gamma|$, and the power lost to the mismatch is $-10\log_{10}(1-\Gamma^2)$:

| Mismatch | $\Gamma$ | Return loss | Mismatch loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| 55 Ω trace into a 50 Ω system (+10 %) | 0.048 | 26.4 dB | 0.010 dB |
| 60 Ω into 50 Ω | 0.091 | 20.8 dB | 0.036 dB |
| 75 Ω into 50 Ω | 0.200 | 14.0 dB | 0.177 dB |
| 100 Ω pair into a 90 Ω USB receiver | 0.053 | 25.6 dB | 0.012 dB |
| 85 Ω pair into a 100 Ω receiver | −0.081 | 21.8 dB | 0.029 dB |

Two things stand out. The power lost to a single ±10 % mismatch is a hundredth of a decibel: the amplitude is barely affected. What matters is the reflected wave, about 5 % of the signal, which travels back to the driver and then forward again, arriving as a delayed echo (inter-symbol interference). Mismatches at both ends form a resonator whose echoes add. In the worst case, two ±10 % errors at opposite ends of a trace with delay $T$ produce an echo of $\Gamma_1\Gamma_2\approx0.0023$ after $2T$, tiny, but for a ±15 % USB tolerance and its $\Gamma_{max}\approx0.07$ the primary reflection alone uses a visible slice of the eye. That is why interface specs set tolerance together with a channel-level budget, and why they are tighter at higher rates.

## Worked example: the minimum-loss pad between 75 Ω and 50 Ω

The table above mentions a 5.7 dB pad for connecting 75 Ω and 50 Ω equipment. The numbers follow from matching both ports with one series and one shunt resistor. For source $Z_1=75$ Ω and load $Z_2=50$ Ω (with $Z_1>Z_2$), the series arm on the high-impedance side and the shunt arm on the low side are

$$R_s=\sqrt{Z_1(Z_1-Z_2)}=\sqrt{75\times25}=43.3\ \Omega,\qquad R_p=Z_2\sqrt{\frac{Z_1}{Z_1-Z_2}}=50\sqrt3=86.6\ \Omega$$

Looking from the 75 Ω side the network is $R_s+R_p\parallel Z_2=43.3+(86.6\times50)/(136.6)=43.3+31.7=75\ \Omega$, and from the 50 Ω side $R_p\parallel(R_s+Z_1)=86.6\parallel118.3=50\ \Omega$. Both ports are matched, and the insertion loss of a minimum-loss pad is $20\log_{10}\left(\sqrt{Z_1/Z_2}+\sqrt{Z_1/Z_2-1}\right)=20\log_{10}(1.225+0.707)=5.7$ dB. Nothing is free: the price of a perfect match between unequal impedances is 5.7 dB of loss, whereas simply connecting the two with a 0.18 dB mismatch loss and a 14 dB return-loss echo is sometimes the better engineering trade for non-critical signals. On a PCB the analogous decision is whether to taper between two impedances or accept a step.

## Worked example: why coupling turns 50 Ω into 100 Ω differential

Two isolated 50 Ω lines carrying opposite signals form a 100 Ω differential path, since each wave sees its own 50 Ω and the differential voltage across two lines is twice the single-ended voltage at the current of one: $Z_{diff}=2Z_0=100\ \Omega$. Real pairs are coupled. In the odd (differential) mode the neighboring line pulls each line's field toward it, adding capacitance between the lines and lowering $Z_{odd}$ below $Z_0$. A common approximation is $Z_{diff}\approx2Z_0\left(1-0.48\,e^{-0.96\,s/h}\right)$. For an alone-impedance of $Z_0=50$ Ω at $s=h=0.2$ mm the pair gives $2(50)(1-0.48\times0.383)=81.6$ Ω, so a pair built from 50 Ω traces at that spacing is an 82 Ω differential line, not a 100 Ω one. To reach 100 Ω at the same spacing the traces must be narrower (about 61 Ω alone, as in the table). If instead you space the traces far apart, $s\gg h$, the coupling vanishes and $Z_{diff}\to2Z_0$: a loosely coupled pair of 50 Ω traces is a 100 Ω pair, at the price of more routing area and more susceptibility to common-mode noise. That is the trade between tightly and loosely coupled pairs.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The coupled-pair formula is a closed-form approximation; the width values in the table above and the 82 Ω estimate differ by a few percent between formulas and field solvers. Use the fab's field solver with your stackup, and treat these as a first pass.

## Limiting cases and when the standard values don't apply

- **Very short links.** On a board where every trace is shorter than $t_r/6$ in delay terms, none of the standard impedances matter for signal integrity; the choice is made on routing convenience.
- **Power transmission versus signal.** The 30 Ω power-handling optimum matters for RF power cables, not for PCB signals, which carry milliwatts; the 77 Ω loss optimum matters for long cables and not for a few inches of trace. For PCB, the standard values are driven by drivers and specifications.
- **Driver strength.** A CMOS driver with about 25 Ω output impedance and a 3.3 V swing into a 50 Ω line launches roughly $3.3\times50/(50+25)=2.2$ V into a 50 Ω line; adding a 25 Ω series resistor makes the source 50 Ω, so the launch is half the supply (1.65 V) and doubles to the full swing at an open receiver. A 75 Ω line would reduce the driver current needed but needs 0.15 mm traces that most fabs hold less tightly.
- **Mixed-impedance channels.** Connectors and cables often differ from the board (a 100 Ω board pair into a 90 Ω cable); the right target is whichever the channel model assumed, not a uniform number.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **Impedance includes the solder mask and the finish.** Calculators with mask thickness and dielectric constant parameters give results a few ohms different from the bare-trace formula.
- **Via transitions and pads dominate.** A 50 Ω trace with a via adds a discontinuity several times larger than a 5 % impedance error along the trace; time spent on the via and pad transitions is usually better than tuning the width from 50 to 49 Ω.
- **Reference stack.** Differential impedance depends on the distance to the plane as well as on the spacing; a pair routed on two different layers with different reference distances has different impedance on each layer, so the same width does not give the same impedance on every layer.
- **Test coupons.** Fabs build impedance coupons with a nominal geometry on the panel edge; ask for the measured value and the coupon geometry (single-ended or differential, layer) to check that it matches your design intent.

## Bench verification

A TDR with an SMA launch and a fast step gives the impedance profile of the trace: with a 50 Ω system the flat region should read within the tolerance, the connector and the via appear as small dips and bumps, and a step in the trace's impedance appears as a plateau at a different level. Convert the reading to impedance with $Z=Z_0(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)$ for a reflection $\rho$: a flat reading of 0.05 above the baseline corresponds to $50\times1.05/0.95=55.3$ Ω. Measure a differential pair with a two-channel TDR or a differential probe and compare it with the target (90, 100 Ω). Record the dielectric thickness from the fab's cross-section report so that discrepancies can be traced to the stackup.

::: assumptions
- Values are **typical conventions**; each standard specifies its own target and tolerance, and the numbers given for PCIe and DDR vary by generation and guide.
- Trace widths assume a 0.2 mm FR-4 dielectric and 1 oz copper; other stackups give different widths.
- The 50 Ω argument is for **air-filled coax**; for dielectric-filled coax the optimum values shift slightly, but the conclusion is the same.
- At very low frequencies or very short lines the characteristic impedance is less meaningful than lumped behaviour.

::: followups
### Why isn't 75 Ω used for PCB digital traces?
A 75 Ω trace is narrow (0.15 mm on 0.2 mm FR-4), so conductor loss and etch tolerance are worse, and it is harder for a CMOS driver (typical 20–50 Ω) to drive. The lower loss that motivated 75 Ω in coax is a minor gain on a short board trace.

### Why are differential pairs 100 Ω rather than 50?
Because each line sees the other: the pair's differential impedance is the sum of two odd-mode impedances. Two 50 Ω lines with weak coupling would give 100 Ω differential, which fits twisted-pair cables (100 Ω) and gives a convenient swing/current combination (e.g. LVDS 3.5 mA × 100 Ω = 350 mV).

### What happens if I use a 60 Ω trace in a 50 Ω system?
$\Gamma=(60-50)/(60+50)=0.09$. A 9% reflection, about −21 dB return loss. On a short trace with a slow edge it's invisible; on a long multi-GHz channel it adds up with other discontinuities, so spec tolerances (±10%) keep it in budget.

### Why do USB and PCIe use different impedances?
Each standard picked its own cable/connector and channel model: USB's 90 Ω reflects its cable design, PCIe's 100 Ω (and in some guides 85 Ω) reflects connector and channel optimisation. Both are acceptable if the receiver and transmitter are designed for them; the board must follow the specification.

### What if the datasheet doesn't give an impedance?
Check the app note/reference design; if none, use the system convention: 50 Ω single-ended for general digital, 100 Ω differential for LVDS-type links. Verify with a simulation, and with a series resistor in the driver to absorb a mismatch.
:::

::: interpretations
"Common characteristic impedance values" is answered for both **single-ended** and **differential** lines, and for coax as well as PCB traces. The standard-specific numbers (USB, PCIe, DDR) are quoted from common practice and flagged where sources differ; the board's actual target is always set by the relevant specification.
:::
