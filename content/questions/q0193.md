::: testing
Whether you notice the *asymmetry* of a buck: the output current is smooth but the input current is a pulse train. The input capacitor exists because the source (and the wiring from it) cannot supply fast current pulses. A strong answer gives the physical reason (source impedance and loop inductance), the numbers (pulse height, rms current, ripple), what happens without it (input droop, ringing and overshoot, EMI, FET and controller damage), and practical placement and selection rules (ceramic at the pins, rms rating, DC-bias derating).
:::

::: why
The input capacitor is the most commonly under-designed part of a buck. Missing or poorly placed input capacitance is a leading cause of ringing on the switch node, FET breakdown, failing EMC tests, regulators that oscillate or reset the system when they start, and capacitors that overheat. Interviewers ask it because the answer reveals whether you understand current loops, di/dt, and parasitic inductance, not just the ideal-circuit story.
:::

::: concepts
- **Pulsed input current**: the high-side FET only conducts for $D$ of the cycle, so the input current is a pulse train. See [[q0185]].
- **Source impedance and wiring inductance**: real sources and cables have inductance that resists fast current changes. See [[q0011]].
- **Capacitor as a local charge reservoir**: supplies the pulses and is recharged between them. See [[q0032]].
- **$v=L\,di/dt$**: a fast current step through an inductance makes a large voltage. See [[q0041]].
- **RMS ripple current**: the capacitor must tolerate $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$.
- **Hot loop**: the loop of $C_{in}$, high-side FET and low-side device carries the fast current edges.
:::

::: answer
"In a buck the input current isn't smooth. The high-side FET is only on for a fraction of the cycle, so the input current is a train of pulses, up to the inductor's peak current, say 1.5 to 2.5 A in my 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A example, with an average of only 0.55 A. The supply and the cable feeding the converter have inductance and resistance, so they can't deliver those pulses with fast edges. The input capacitor sits right at the FET and acts as a local reservoir: it supplies the pulses during the on time and gets recharged by the source at a nearly steady 0.55 A during the off time.

If you remove it, the pulses would have to come through the cable inductance. A 1 µH cable and a current step of 1.5 A in 10 ns wants 150 V, so the voltage at the FET collapses at turn-on, and at turn-off the cable current has to stop abruptly, so it rings or spikes with $V = I\sqrt{L/C}$, easily over the FET rating. You'd also have massive conducted EMI and a supply that droops or oscillates, and the controller could hit under-voltage lockout.

So the cap needs a low ESR and ESL, to be placed right at the high-side FET drain and low-side source, rated for the rms ripple current of about 0.9 A here, and sized so the input ripple is something like 50 to 100 millivolts."
:::

::: technical
**Input current.** The high-side FET carries $i_L$ during $DT$: pulses from 1.49 A to 2.51 A, average $D I_{out}=0.55$ A, rms $\sqrt{D(I_{out}^2+\Delta I^2/12)}=1.06$ A.

**Capacitor current.** $i_{Cin}=i_{HS}-I_{in,avg}$: rms
$$I_{Cin,rms}\approx I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}=0.893\ \text{A}\quad(\text{max } I_{out}/2\text{ at }D=0.5).$$

**Voltage ripple** (ideal capacitor): the capacitor supplies $(1-D)I_{out}$ during $DT$ and is recharged at $DI_{out}$ for $(1-D)T$:
$$\Delta V_{in}=\frac{I_{out}D(1-D)}{f\,C_{in}}\quad(+\ I_{pk}\,ESR\text{ term}),$$
$=80$ mV for 10 µF at 500 kHz, plus about 12.5 mV of ESR step ($2.51\ \text{A}\times5$ mΩ).

**Sizing for a ripple target**: $C_{in}\ge\frac{I_{out}D(1-D)}{f\,\Delta V}$, e.g. for 100 mV, 7.98 µF *effective*. Ceramics lose capacitance with DC bias, so you may need two to four times the nominal value.

**Current sharing at $f_{sw}$.** $|Z_{C}|=\frac{1}{2\pi f C}=32$ mΩ for 10 µF at 500 kHz, versus a 1 µH cable $2\pi fL=3.1\ \Omega$. The capacitor takes about 99% of the AC current.

**Without $C_{in}$ (illustrative).** Cable 1 µH, FET edge 10 ns:
- Turn-on: needs $L\,di/dt=1\ \mu H\times1.49\ A/10\ ns=149$ V; the node collapses toward 0 V until the cable current builds ($12\ \text{V}$ across 1 µH gives only 12 A/µs, so 208 ns to reach 2.5 A).
- Turn-off: $\tfrac12LI^2=3.1\ \mu J$ in the cable dumped into the node capacitance (say 200 pF): $V\approx I\sqrt{L/C}=2.5\sqrt{1\ \mu H/200\ pF}\approx177$ V ideal peak, so in practice the FET avalanches or breaks down (the numbers are illustrative; the actual clamp depends on the devices).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The cable inductance (about 1 µH per metre of wire pair is a rough rule of thumb) and the node capacitance (200 pF) are illustrative; real values vary with geometry and FET. The point is the order of magnitude, not the exact spike.
:::

::: short
"The buck draws pulsed current from the input, only while the high-side FET is on, with a small average and tall pulses. A real source and its wiring have inductance and can't supply those fast pulses, so the input capacitor sits at the FET as a local reservoir, supplying the pulses and getting recharged smoothly. Without it the input would droop and ring, the switch node could overshoot beyond the FET rating, EMI would blow up, and the supply or controller could trip. It needs low ESR, a rating for about 0.9 A rms here, and tight placement."
:::

::: long
"The key observation is that a buck's output current is smooth, thanks to the inductor, but its input current is not. The high-side FET is in series with the input and only conducts during the on time, so the input sees pulses at the inductor current, about 1.5 to 2.5 amps for 550 nanoseconds each cycle in my example, while the average is just 0.55 amps.

A real supply can't deliver that. There's a cable or PCB trace with inductance and resistance, and the supply itself has a limited bandwidth. So there has to be a local energy store right at the FET: the input capacitor. It provides the pulse during the on time, then the source recharges it during the off time with a nearly steady current. The ripple current in that capacitor is the pulse minus the average, rms about 0.9 amps here, which is large relative to the load current and is why the part must have a ripple-current rating and low ESR.

What if you remove it? The pulse has to flow through the cable. With a microhenry of cable inductance and a 1.5 amp step in 10 nanoseconds, you would need 150 volts to supply it, so the voltage at the FET collapses instead at turn-on. At turn-off the cable current, which is also still flowing, has to stop, and energy of half L I squared dumps into the little node capacitance. That voltage could be in the hundreds of volts in theory, which breaks the FET in practice. You also get huge conducted EMI on the supply line, the input rail drooping and oscillating, and possibly the controller's UVLO tripping.

So I'd choose the capacitor for ripple voltage and rms current, remember the DC-bias derating on ceramics, and place it right at the FET with the shortest possible hot loop."
:::

::: terms
- **Pulsed input current**: current drawn only when the high-side FET is on.
- **Hot loop**: the loop through $C_{in}$, the high-side FET and the low-side FET/diode carrying fast current edges.
- **Ripple current rating**: maximum rms AC current the capacitor can carry without overheating.
- **ESR / ESL**: series resistance and inductance; determine the voltage step and heating.
- **DC-bias derating**: loss of ceramic capacitance with applied voltage.
- **Bulk capacitor**: a larger capacitor (electrolytic/polymer) upstream, for low-frequency energy.
- **Conducted EMI**: noise on the input lines caused by the ripple current.
- **UVLO**: under-voltage lockout, shuts the controller down if $V_{in}$ droops.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "It smooths the input voltage and supplies current to the converter."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains *why* it is necessary: the input current is pulsed, and the source and wiring have inductance.
- Gives the numbers: pulse amplitude, average $D I_{out}$, rms $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$, ripple $I_{out}D(1-D)/(fC)$.
- Describes failure modes without it (droop, spikes above $V_{ds}$ rating, EMI, UVLO), with a $L\,di/dt$ or $\sqrt{L/C}$ estimate.
- Notes ceramic derating, ESR, ripple-current rating and the tight hot-loop placement.
- Mentions the bulk-plus-ceramic hierarchy and input filters for EMI.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"It is there to filter the input supply noise."** It primarily supplies the converter's own pulsed current; filtering is a side effect.
- **Sizing it only by capacitance.** The rms current (0.9 A here, up to $I_{out}/2$ at $D=0.5$) and ESR heating matter; check the part's ripple rating.
- **Using nominal ceramic capacitance.** At 12 V a 10 µF X5R in 0603/0805 can fall below 5 µF effective.
- **Placing it far from the FET.** The loop inductance of the trace then does what the cable did; keep the hot loop to a few nH.
- **Believing one big electrolytic is enough.** Its ESR/ESL leave the fast edge uncovered; you need ceramics at the FET as well.
- **Thinking it is optional in a "low-current" design.** Even at 100 mA, edges of a few ns through a few nH of wiring make noticeable spikes.
:::

## The input current is a pulse train

In [[q0185]] the high-side FET carries the inductor current for $DT=550$ ns and nothing for the remaining 1.45 µs. That makes the input current a pulse train.

The capacitor is therefore a charge reservoir. Per cycle it supplies a charge $Q=(1-D)I_{out}\,DT$ and gets it back during the off time:

$$\Delta V_{in}=\frac{Q}{C_{in}}=\frac{I_{out}D(1-D)}{f\,C_{in}}.$$

## Who supplies the pulses? A current-divider argument

At the switching frequency the capacitor and the source+cable are two parallel paths for the AC current:

| Path | Impedance at 500 kHz |
|---|---|
| 10 µF ceramic ($1/(2\pi fC)$) | 32 mΩ |
| 1 µH cable ($2\pi fL$) | 3.14 Ω |

The capacitor carries about $3.14/(3.14+0.03)\approx99\%$ of the AC component. The cable carries essentially only the DC average, 0.55 A. That is how a capacitor "decouples" the source: it provides a much lower impedance than the source at the frequencies of interest.

Numbers for the example, taking 1 µH of cable and a 10 ns edge (illustrative):

- **Turn-on.** The step is the valley current, 1.49 A in 10 ns. Sourcing that through the cable would need $L\,di/dt=149$ V. The available voltage is only 12 V, which gives 12 A/µs, so the cable current takes about $L I/V=208$ ns to rise to 2.5 A. In that time the current the FET needs comes from the stray capacitances, so the local $V_{in}$ node collapses toward ground.
- **Turn-off.** The cable is carrying up to 2.5 A. Interrupting it dumps $\tfrac12LI^2=3.1\ \mu J$ into the node capacitance (say 200 pF), a peak of $I\sqrt{L/C}=177$ V in the ideal case. A 30 V FET will avalanche long before that.

> [!WARNING]
> This is the failure mechanism behind "my converter runs fine on the bench with a short lead and dies with a long cable", and also behind hot-plug spikes in the other direction: a battery or bench supply connected through a long cable to a regulator with a ceramic input cap can ring up to twice the input voltage. Add bulk (electrolytic) damping or a TVS where necessary.

## Choosing and placing it

1. **Ripple voltage**: $C_{eff}\ge I_{out}D(1-D)/(f\,\Delta V)$. For 100 mV: 8 µF effective. At 12 V bias a 10 µF 0805 X5R may only deliver 4 to 6 µF (check the DC-bias curve), so two 10 µF or a 22 µF part would be used.
2. **RMS current**: at worst case $D=0.5$, $I_{out}/2=1$ A. A pair of ceramics handles several amps rms easily; electrolytics may not. Heating: $I^2R=0.89^2\times0.005=4$ mW.
3. **Voltage rating**: above $V_{in,max}$ plus ringing margin, typically 1.5 to 2 times for ceramics because of the bias derating.
4. **Placement**: the smallest loop is $C_{in}\to Q_1\to Q_2/D\to C_{in}$. Keep it within a few mm. The loop inductance of a few nH carries di/dt of about 1 A/ns, so $L\,di/dt=5\ nH\times1\ A/ns=5$ V of ringing if it is larger than it should be.
5. **Hierarchy**: ceramic at the FET (fast edge), bulk electrolytic or polymer upstream (low frequency, damping of the cable/LC resonance).

## EMI view

The pulse train has a rich harmonic spectrum. Without a capacitor the supply wires carry that current and radiate and conduct it. With the capacitor the AC current stays in a small loop. For EMC you add a differential LC input filter after the capacitor; the same capacitor is the filter's first element. See [[q0143]] and [[q0142]] for related decoupling concepts.
:::

::: applications
- **Any buck regulator layout**: the input capacitor is the first component placed, right at $V_{in}$ and ground pins.
- **Automotive 12 V converters** with long harnesses: bulk capacitor plus ceramics to survive load-dump and cranking.
- **Battery-powered products**: the battery's internal resistance and wiring act as source impedance; $C_{in}$ prevents the pulses from shortening battery life and causing brown-outs.
- **EMC compliance**: conducted-emission filters rely on a well-placed input capacitor.
- **Point-of-load converters on server boards** with tens of amps of pulsed input current and distributed input capacitance.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM, constant load current**; in DCM the pulses have a different shape and rms.
- **Ideal capacitor** for the ripple calculation; ESR and ESL add steps and spikes.
- **Cable/source model**: a simple inductance; real supplies have bandwidth limits and sometimes their own capacitance, which changes the spike numbers.
- **The no-capacitor numbers are illustrative**; real FET breakdown and node capacitances vary.
- **If a large upstream bulk capacitor sits within a few cm** with low inductance, the local ceramic can be smaller; the principle (a low-impedance source at $f_{sw}$) is unchanged.
:::

::: followups
### What is the ripple current rating you need for the input capacitor?
About $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$: 0.89 A rms for the example, with a worst case of $I_{out}/2=1$ A at $D=0.5$. Add margin for temperature and the ripple ratio of the inductor current. Ceramics mostly have no practical limit at this level; for electrolytics check the datasheet.

### Does the input capacitor reduce the input current?
It does not change the average, 0.55 A, but it changes what the *source* sees: nearly DC instead of 1.5 to 2.5 A pulses. The converter-side pulse current is unchanged and flows locally in the capacitor loop.

### Why is placement as important as value?
Because the pulse edges are fast. The loop formed by $C_{in}$, $Q_1$ and $Q_2/D_1$ carries current that changes by amps in nanoseconds. Even 5 nH of loop inductance gives $5\ \text{nH}\times1.5\ \text{A}/5\ \text{ns}=1.5$ V and rings with the FET capacitance, causing overshoot, EMI and extra loss. A 1 µF capacitor 3 cm away can be worse than a 100 nF at the pin.

### What if the input capacitor is a high-ESR electrolytic only?
The ESR drops $I\times ESR$ at each edge: 2.5 A × 0.3 Ω = 0.75 V steps in the rail, and the ESL leaves the edge uncovered, so ringing and EMI appear. Add ceramic capacitors in parallel at the pins; the electrolytic then provides bulk energy and damping.

### Harder: the input capacitor and a long cable form a resonant circuit. What goes wrong at hot-plug?
A 10 µF ceramic with 1 µH cable has $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})=50$ kHz and a characteristic impedance $\sqrt{L/C}=0.32\ \Omega$ with very low damping. Plugging into a 12 V supply can ring up to nearly 24 V (twice), threatening components rated at 16 or 20 V. Cure it with bulk electrolytic capacitance (ESR provides damping, roughly matching $\sqrt{L/C}$), a series resistor/ferrite damping network, or a TVS diode.
:::
