::: testing
Whether you can *derive* and draw the brushed-DC motor characteristic curves from $V = IR + K_e\omega$ and $T = K_tI$ rather than recalling a picture, and then read engineering consequences from them: stall torque and stall current, no-load speed, the point of maximum power vs maximum efficiency, why you cannot run at stall, and how supply voltage shifts the curves. The follow-up "repeat for current and voltage" tests whether you understand that current tracks torque, back-EMF tracks speed, and the resistive drop $IR$ is what is left.
:::

::: why
Every motor selection, driver sizing and control design starts from these lines. They tell you what current to expect at a given load, what voltage you need for a target speed, when the motor runs hot, and where its efficiency is best. Interviewers use this question as a compact test of electromechanical intuition: if you can draw four straight lines and explain them, you understand motors.
:::

::: concepts
- **Motor electrical equation**: $V = IR + K_e\omega$ (steady state). See [[q0341]] for back-EMF.
- **Torque constant**: $T = K_tI$, with $K_t = K_e$ in SI units. See [[q0353]].
- **Stall condition**: $\omega = 0$, back-EMF zero, current $V/R$. See [[q0342]].
- **No-load condition**: only friction torque to overcome; current is the small $I_0$.
- **Mechanical power**: $P_{out} = T\omega$ (and $P_{in} = VI$); efficiency $\eta = P_{out}/P_{in}$.
- **Linear systems**: all of these curves are straight lines or simple parabolas, so the "drawing" is easy once the endpoints are known.
:::

::: answer
"I'd start from two equations: $V = IR + K_e\omega$ and $T = K_tI$. From the first, the current is $(V - K_e\omega)/R$ and from the second the torque is proportional to current. Substituting gives torque falling linearly with speed: $T = \frac{K_t}{R}(V - K_e\omega)$.

So the torque-versus-RPM curve is a straight line. On the torque axis it starts at stall torque, $K_tV/R$, with the shaft held still: that's the maximum torque, with the maximum current $V/R$. It falls to zero torque at the no-load speed, about $V/K_e$ — a bit under, because of friction. The slope is the speed regulation, $R/(K_eK_t)$: a small $R$ makes a stiff motor whose speed barely drops with load.

Current versus torque is also a straight line through the origin, $I = T/K_t$, plus a small offset for friction. Current versus speed falls linearly from stall current to almost zero at no-load speed. Back-EMF versus speed is a line from zero up to nearly $V$, and versus torque it falls from nearly $V$ to zero. The $IR$ drop is whatever is left.

The implications: peak mechanical power is at half the stall torque and half the no-load speed, but peak efficiency is much closer to no-load speed, at low current. So you size a motor to run at maybe 10 to 30% of stall torque, and never sit at stall, because there it's all heat. Changing the supply voltage slides the line parallel to itself, which is how PWM sets speed."
:::

::: technical
**Model** (steady state, PM brushed motor, friction torque $T_f$ constant):
$$V = IR + K_e\omega,\quad T_{shaft} = K_tI - T_f,\quad K_t = K_e = K.$$

**Torque-speed**: $T = \frac{K}{R}(V - K\omega) - T_f$. Stall torque $\approx KV/R$, no-load speed $\omega_0 \approx (V - I_0R)/K$ where $I_0 = T_f/K$.

**Current**: $I = \frac{T + T_f}{K}$ (so linear in torque) and $I = \frac{V - K\omega}{R}$ (linear in speed).

**Voltage split**: $V = E + IR$ with $E = K\omega$. Back-EMF rises linearly with speed, falls linearly with torque: $E = V - R(T+T_f)/K$.

**Power**: $P_{in} = VI$, $P_{out} = T\omega$, $P_{cu} = I^2R$. Ignoring friction, $P_{out} = \frac{K\omega(V - K\omega)}{R}$, a parabola with maximum $P_{max} = \frac{V^2}{4R}$ at $\omega = \omega_0/2$, $T = T_{stall}/2$ — where efficiency is only 50% (matched-load argument).

**Efficiency**: $\eta = \frac{T\omega}{VI}$. Ignoring friction $\eta = K\omega/V = E/V$. With friction: $\eta_{max} \approx \left(1 - \sqrt{I_0/I_{stall}}\right)^2$ at $I = \sqrt{I_0I_{stall}}$.

**Worked example** ($V = 12$ V, $R = 2\ \Omega$, $K = 0.02$ N·m/A, $T_f = 2$ mN·m):

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Stall current | 6 A |
| Stall shaft torque | 118 mN·m |
| No-load current $I_0$ | 0.10 A |
| No-load speed | 590 rad/s = 5634 rpm |
| Max power | 17.4 W at 3.05 A, 59 mN·m, 2817 rpm ($\eta$ = 48%) |
| Max efficiency | 75.8% at 0.775 A, 13.5 mN·m, 4990 rpm (7.05 W out) |
| Stall heating | $6^2\times2 = 72$ W |

**Effect of voltage**: the torque-speed line shifts parallel with $V$ (slope unchanged): stall torque $\propto V$ and no-load speed $\propto V$. At $V/2$: half stall torque, half speed, but the same slope.
:::

::: short
"With $V = IR + K_e\omega$ and $T = K_tI$, torque falls linearly with speed from stall torque $K_tV/R$ at zero rpm to zero at the no-load speed $V/K_e$. Current is proportional to torque, so it's highest at stall and lowest at no-load. Back-EMF rises with speed, so the remaining voltage across the winding, $IR$, shrinks. Peak power is at half stall torque and half no-load speed, but efficiency peaks at low torque near no-load speed, so you design to run in that region and never sit at stall."
:::

::: long
"I'll derive it from two relations: the electrical side, $V = IR + K_e\omega$, and the torque relation, $T = K_tI$.

Torque versus RPM: solve the first for current, $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$, multiply by $K_t$ and you get $T = \frac{K_t}{R}(V - K_e\omega)$. That's a straight line with a negative slope. The intercept on the torque axis is stall torque, $K_tV/R$: the shaft is stopped, there's no back-EMF, current is $V/R$. The intercept on the speed axis is the no-load speed, about $V/K_e$, where the back-EMF almost equals the supply and only a tiny current is left to overcome friction. For a 12 volt, 2 ohm motor with $K = 0.02$, that's 6 amps and roughly 0.12 newton-metres at stall, and about 5600 rpm no-load.

Now current. Against torque it's a line through the origin, $I = T/K_t$, offset slightly by friction: this is why current is the torque indicator. Against speed it falls linearly from 6 amps at stall to about 0.1 amp at no-load.

Voltage: I'd split the supply into the back-EMF, $K_e\omega$, and the resistive drop, $IR$. Back-EMF is a line rising with speed from zero to nearly $V$; against torque it falls. The $IR$ drop does the opposite, so the two always add up to the supply.

What do the curves tell you? Mechanical power is torque times speed, a parabola, with its peak, $V^2/4R$, at half stall torque and half no-load speed. But at that point efficiency is only about 50%, since half the input power goes into $I^2R$. Peak efficiency is up near no-load speed, here about 76% at 0.8 amps. So you choose the motor so the continuous operating point sits at maybe 10 to 30% of stall torque. At stall, all 72 watts is heat in the winding. And raising the voltage slides the curve outward, parallel, which is how PWM controls speed."
:::

::: terms
- **Stall torque**: torque at zero speed, $K_tV/R$; accompanied by stall current $V/R$.
- **No-load speed**: speed with zero external load torque, near $V/K_e$.
- **Speed regulation / slope**: $\Delta\omega/\Delta T = R/(K_eK_t)$; lower is stiffer.
- **Back-EMF constant $K_e$** and **torque constant $K_t$**: equal in SI units. See [[q0353]].
- **No-load current $I_0$**: current needed to overcome friction and windage at no-load speed.
- **Mechanical output power**: $T\omega$.
- **Copper loss**: $I^2R$ heating in the winding.
- **Operating point**: intersection of the motor line with the load line (the load's torque-speed demand).
- **Continuous (rated) torque**: the highest torque the motor can deliver without overheating.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer draws a downward-sloping torque-speed line and says "high torque at low speed". An exceptional answer:

- Derives the line from $V = IR + K_e\omega$ and $T = K_tI$, and labels the intercepts with equations and numbers.
- Draws current as the *same* line (scaled by $K_t$) and says "current is the torque meter".
- Splits the supply into back-EMF and $IR$ and shows they trade off along the curve.
- Identifies max power at half stall/half no-load, then points out max efficiency is somewhere else and why ($I^2R$).
- Mentions the effect of voltage (parallel shift), temperature (R rises, so slope rises and stall torque falls), and friction offset.
- Draws the conclusion for design: keep the operating point in the efficient, continuous-rated region; avoid stall.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting max power at stall or at no-load.** Output power is zero at both ends; the maximum is in the middle.
- **Saying max efficiency is at max power.** Efficiency peaks much nearer no-load speed ([[q0344]], [[q0337]]).
- **Drawing the torque-speed line as curved** for a PM brushed motor. In the linear model it is straight; curvature appears only with saturation, armature reaction, brush drop and heating.
- **Forgetting that current tracks torque, not speed.** At constant speed, doubling the load torque doubles the current.
- **Confusing the supply voltage with the winding voltage.** The winding sees $V - E = IR$ in steady state; at no-load that is only $I_0R$.
- **Ignoring temperature.** Copper resistance rises ~0.4%/°C and magnets weaken, so a hot motor has lower stall torque and a steeper slope than the datasheet curve.
:::

::: deep
## Deriving the lines

Steady state means $di/dt = 0$ and $d\omega/dt = 0$. With the winding inductance out of the way, the electrical equation is an ordinary loop law:
$$V = IR + E,\qquad E = K_e\omega.$$
The mechanical equation says the shaft torque produced balances the load torque and friction:
$$K_tI = T_{load} + T_f.$$
Eliminate $I$ to get the speed as a function of load:
$$\omega = \frac{V}{K_e} - \frac{R}{K_eK_t}\left(T_{load} + T_f\right).$$
That is an equation of a straight line: intercept $V/K_e$ on the speed axis, slope $-R/(K_eK_t)$. (Rearranged, it is the torque-speed line used above.) We'll use our example, $V = 12$ V, $R = 2\ \Omega$, $K = 0.02$, $T_f = 2$ mN·m.

**Implication:** speed *drops* as load increases, by $\Delta\omega = R\Delta T/(K_eK_t)$. For our motor, adding 10 mN·m of load costs $2\times0.01/0.0004 = 50$ rad/s = 477 rpm, about 8%. Open-loop speed is therefore load dependent; a controller closes the loop around it.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked "what is the best operating point?" say: maximum efficiency at about $\sqrt{I_0/I_{stall}}$ of stall current, maximum power at half of stall, and the continuous rating limit (thermal) sets what you can actually hold.

## Why max power is half stall: the matched-load argument

Treat the winding resistance as the source resistance and the back-EMF $E$ as the voltage across the "load". The electrical power converted to mechanical form is $P = EI = E(V-E)/R$, which is maximised at $E = V/2$, the maximum-power-transfer condition. That is half the no-load speed, with half the stall current. So $P_{max} = V^2/4R = 18$ W in the ideal case, and the efficiency there is $E/V = 50\%$. Power and efficiency pull in opposite directions.

## Effect of supply voltage and temperature

- **Voltage.** Replace $V$ with $DV$ (PWM duty $D$): the line moves left/down parallel to itself. Stall torque and no-load speed scale with $D$; the slope stays. So a given load torque needs the same *current* at any speed, and speed changes by $\Delta V/K_e$.
- **Temperature.** Copper resistance rises 0.39%/°C: at +80 °C $R$ is 31% higher, so stall torque drops 24% and the line gets steeper. Neodymium magnets lose roughly 0.1%/°C of flux, lowering $K$ slightly, and the speed at a given voltage goes up a little.
- **Brush drop.** A fixed ~1 V loss ($V_b$) subtracts from $V$, shifting the line by $V_b$; at low voltages (PWM at small duty) the motor does not start until $V > V_b + I_0R$ (a "dead zone").
- **Inductance** only matters for transients and PWM ripple ([[q0345]]).

## What the curves mean for design

1. **Match with the load line.** Plot the load's required torque vs speed on the same axes; the operating point is the intersection. A fan load ($T\propto\omega^2$) crosses at a speed well below no-load; friction load ($T$ = const) gives a steady speed.
2. **Stay in the continuous region.** Datasheets quote a continuous torque and the corresponding current; the intermittent range extends to stall but is thermally limited.
3. **Choose a gear ratio** so the load operates near the max-efficiency region of the motor ([[q0354]]).
4. **Sizing the driver** for stall current, since a load jam puts you at the left edge ([[q0342]]).
5. **Speed control** with voltage works well when load varies little; with variable load, close the speed loop or use a current limit for torque control.
:::

::: applications
- **Choosing a gearmotor for a robot wheel**: overlay wheel load torque vs speed on the motor's line to find the operating point and verify it is below the continuous rating.
- **Current-limited torque control**: setting a driver's current limit to cap torque (e.g. for a gripper) uses $T = K_tI$ directly.
- **Stall detection**: monitoring current to see it near $V/R$ to detect jams in valves, windows and gates.
- **Motor characterisation in the lab**: measure current and speed at several loads to extract $K$, $R$ and $I_0$ from the lines.
- **PWM speed control in fans and pumps**: sets duty to scale the line; speed regulation is load-dependent unless feedback is added.
- **Battery runtime estimates**: use $I = (T + T_f)/K_t$ and $P_{in} = VI$ for the load profile.
:::

::: assumptions
- **PM brushed DC motor in steady state** with constant $R$, $K_t = K_e$ and constant friction torque; no saturation or armature reaction.
- **Ideal voltage source** (no sag) and negligible brush voltage drop. Real brushes add a fixed drop of the order of 1 V (see [[q0357]]).
- **No thermal effects**: curves are quoted at a reference temperature, usually 25 °C.
- **Linear and ideal PWM**: the average voltage is used and inductance smooths the current. At low duty or high ripple the average current differs from the average-voltage prediction.
- **Torque definition**: "shaft torque" excludes friction torque (the motor's internal losses); air-gap torque is $K_tI$.
- The same curves apply to BLDC motors in trapezoidal drive, and to a linear motor with $F$ and $v$.
:::

::: followups
### What changes if I halve the supply voltage?
The torque-speed line shifts parallel: stall torque and no-load speed halve, the slope (speed regulation) is unchanged. At the same load torque the current is the same ($I = (T+T_f)/K$) but speed is lower by $\Delta V/K_e = 6/0.02 = 300$ rad/s (2865 rpm). So halving V from 12 to 6 V takes the no-load speed from 5634 to about 2770 rpm: $(6 - 0.2)/0.02 = 290$ rad/s.

### Where is the best operating point and why isn't it at max power?
Max power (here 17.4 W) is at ~50% efficiency: half the electrical power becomes copper loss. Max efficiency (76%) is at 0.775 A, only 7 W out. For continuous duty you want to be near the efficiency peak and under the thermal limit; max power is available only briefly or when you need to minimise motor size, not energy.

### How do you extract $K$ and $R$ experimentally?
Hold the shaft (briefly) with a small voltage to measure $R = V/I$ (watch heating), or measure at two or more speeds: $K = (V - IR)/\omega$ using a tachometer or encoder. Open-circuit back-EMF while spinning by hand or with another motor gives $K_e$ directly: 2.09 V at 1000 rpm would imply $K = 0.02$ V·s/rad.

### What happens to the curves as the motor heats up?
Winding resistance rises, so slope increases and stall torque falls (R from 2 to 2.6 Ω at +80 °C reduces stall torque by 23%). Magnet strength typically falls with temperature (ferrite and neodymium differ), which lowers $K$ and raises no-load speed slightly. Net effect: the motor gets "softer" and less torquey when hot.

### The load is a fan with $T = 5\times10^{-8}\omega^2$ N·m. At 12 V what is the operating speed?
Set the motor line equal to the load: $0.01(12 - 0.02\omega) - 0.002 = 5\times10^{-8}\omega^2$, i.e. $5\times10^{-8}\omega^2 + 2\times10^{-4}\omega - 0.118 = 0$. The positive root is $\omega \approx 522$ rad/s (about 4985 rpm). Check: load torque $= 5\times10^{-8}\times522^2 = 13.6$ mN·m, current $= (0.0136 + 0.002)/0.02 = 0.78$ A, back-EMF $= 10.4$ V, $IR = 1.6$ V, which sums to 12 V. That operating point happens to sit right near the efficiency peak.

### Why does the motor draw high current but produce no power at stall?
Mechanical power is $T\omega$ and $\omega = 0$. Electrically $P = VI = I^2R$ is all heat (72 W here). Stall is therefore only tolerable for very short times unless the winding is rated for it.
:::

::: interpretations
The wording "Draw a torque vs RPM curve. What are its implications? Repeat for current and voltage vs torque and RPM" asks for four more relationships: current vs torque, current vs RPM, voltage vs torque, voltage vs RPM. "Voltage" is ambiguous. The supply is constant, so the *interesting* voltage is the **back-EMF** (rising with RPM, falling with torque), with the $IR$ drop as its complement; some interviewers also mean "the voltage you must apply to get a given speed", which for a fixed load is $V = IR + K_e\omega$ (rising with both). Both are covered. Related: [[q0353]] (which electrical parameter maps to torque and speed) and [[q0354]] (how to spec a motor).
