::: testing
Whether you understand that the MOSFET gate is a **capacitor with an extremely thin insulator**, so a modest voltage means a huge electric field, and no current path to bleed off charge. A complete answer includes:

1. The oxide breakdown mechanism: dielectric strength is finite (order 10 MV/cm), oxide is nanometers to tens of nanometers thick, so the safe $V_{GS}$ is only some volts. Breakdown is permanent.
2. Why it happens in practice: ESD (tiny capacitance + zero DC path), gate-loop ringing, Miller coupling from fast drain dv/dt, and floating gates picking up charge.
3. The standard protections: series gate resistor, Zener/TVS clamp, pull-down resistor, ESD diodes on chip inputs, handling procedures.
:::

::: why
Gate-oxide failure is one of the most common ways to kill a power stage and one of the most common ESD failures on a bench. It is also why an unconnected MOSFET input floats to a random voltage, why you never leave a gate pin open, and why every MOSFET driver layout is fussed over. An engineer who can calculate the oxide field and the ringing overshoot will make the right call on gate resistors and clamps.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor and field**: $E=V/t_{ox}$. A thinner dielectric gives a higher field for the same voltage. See [[q0022]].
- **Dielectric breakdown**: when $E$ exceeds the oxide's strength, a conductive path forms and the damage is permanent. See [[q0058]].
- **Gate capacitance**: $C_{ox}WL$ is tiny (femtofarads in logic, nanofarads in power FETs).
- **$Q=CV$ for ESD**: a small charge on a small capacitance produces a huge voltage.
- **Gate loop inductance and ringing**: an RLC circuit made of the driver, gate inductance and $C_{iss}$. See [[q0096]].
- **Miller capacitance ($C_{gd}$)**: couples drain dv/dt back onto the gate.
:::

::: answer
"The gate of a MOSFET is basically a capacitor: a metal or polysilicon plate, a very thin layer of silicon dioxide, and the silicon underneath. That oxide is only a few nanometers thick in a logic transistor, and maybe tens of nanometers to around a hundred in a power MOSFET. Silicon dioxide can only withstand an electric field of roughly 10 megavolts per centimeter, and the field is just the voltage over the thickness, so a few volts across a few nanometers is already close to the limit. If you exceed it, the oxide breaks down and the gate shorts to the channel, permanently. That's why the abs-max $V_{GS}$ on a power FET is often only about ±20 V, and on a logic-level one ±8 to ±12 V.

It's also unforgiving because the gate has no DC path and tiny capacitance. A little static charge, say a nanocoulomb from a fingertip, on a few-picofarad gate gives hundreds of volts. And in a circuit, the gate drive loop has inductance, so it rings and overshoots the drive voltage, and a fast drain edge couples a spike back through the Miller capacitance.

So I'd protect the gate with a series resistor to damp ringing, a Zener or TVS across gate and source to clamp, a pull-down resistor so it never floats, and ESD diodes at chip inputs."
:::

::: technical
**The gate stack.** Gate (poly/metal) | $\text{SiO}_2$ of thickness $t_{ox}$ | silicon channel. Capacitance per area $C_{ox}=\varepsilon_{ox}/t_{ox}$ with $\varepsilon_{ox}=3.9\varepsilon_0$.

**Field and breakdown.** $E_{ox}=V_{GS}/t_{ox}$. Intrinsic breakdown of thermally grown oxide is of order 10 MV/cm (instant breakdown), but reliability (time-dependent dielectric breakdown, TDDB) requires working fields well below that, typically a fraction of it, over the product lifetime.
- $t_{ox}=5$ nm: 5 V gives 10 MV/cm. A 1.8-3.3 V-class process therefore specifies about 2-3.6 V max.
- $t_{ox}=100$ nm: 20 V gives 2 MV/cm, which is well below the intrinsic limit; the datasheet ±20 V rating includes margin for lifetime, process variation and field enhancement at corners.

**Why charge kills.** $V=Q/C$. A charge of 1 nC on a 500 pF power MOSFET gate: $V=2$ V (harmless). The same charge on a 5 pF small-signal gate: $V=200$ V. On a 34.5 fF logic gate (10 µm × 1 µm at 3.45 fF/µm²), 10 pC gives about 290 V. Small gates are far more fragile than big ones. A human-body-model event (100 pF through 1.5 kΩ at 2 kV) delivers $Q=200$ nC and a peak current of 1.33 A.

**Circuit mechanisms in power stages.**
1. *Ringing.* Gate loop inductance $L_g$ and $C_{iss}$ form a series RLC with the driver. With $L_g=10$ nH, $C_{iss}=1$ nF: $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})=50$ MHz, damping $\zeta=\tfrac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$. At $R_g=1$ Ω, $\zeta=0.16$ and overshoot is $e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}=60\%$: a 12 V drive peaks at about 19 V. At $R_g=3$ Ω, 18% (14 V). Critical damping is $R=2\sqrt{L/C}=6.3$ Ω.
2. *Miller coupling.* A fast drain edge injects $C_{gd}\,dv/dt$ into the gate. Through $R_g$: $\Delta V_{gs}\approx R_gC_{gd}\,dv/dt=5\ \Omega\times50\ \text{pF}\times5\ \text{V/ns}=1.25$ V. It can turn the device on (shoot-through) or push $V_{GS}$ negative past the limit.
3. *Floating gate.* Leakage and coupled charge push an undriven gate anywhere.
4. *Inductive/avalanche spikes* on the source or drain that capacitively lift the gate.

**Protection.** Series $R_g$ (about 2-10 Ω, tuned for $\zeta\approx1$); Zener or TVS from gate to source (e.g. 15-18 V for a ±20 V part, placed close); pull-down (10-100 kΩ) to hold off during power-up; ESD diodes at IC inputs ([[q0112]]); good gate loop layout; ESD-safe handling.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Oxide thickness and the intrinsic breakdown field depend on process, geometry and oxide quality; the 10 MV/cm number is a typical intrinsic value, not a design limit. Always use the datasheet absolute-maximum $V_{GS}$ and de-rate it.
:::

::: long
"A MOSFET gate is a capacitor: conductor, a thin layer of silicon dioxide, and the channel. The insulator is the whole point, because it lets the gate control the channel with no DC current. But it's very thin, a few nanometers in a modern logic transistor and tens of nanometers up to about a hundred in a power MOSFET. The electric field is just voltage divided by thickness, and oxide can only stand roughly 10 megavolts per centimeter before it breaks down. Five volts across 5 nanometers is already at that number. Breakdown makes a permanent conductive path, so the gate shorts to the channel and the transistor is dead. That's why datasheet gate-source ratings are small: around ±20 V for typical power parts and ±8 to ±12 V for logic-level ones.

The second reason is that the gate is easy to overshoot. It has almost no capacitance compared with what you can inject, so $V=Q/C$ does the damage. A nanocoulomb of static onto a 5 pF gate is 200 V, and a human-body ESD event can carry hundreds of nanocoulombs. And it has no resistive path to drain that charge away.

In a real power stage there are three more culprits. The gate drive loop has inductance and the gate capacitance is large, so it rings; with 10 nH and 1 nF and only 1 ohm of gate resistance I get about 60% overshoot, so a 12 V drive peaks near 19 V. Fast drain edges push current into the gate through the Miller capacitance. And a floating gate drifts to wherever stray charge puts it.

My toolkit is: a series gate resistor sized for critical damping, a Zener or TVS from gate to source close to the pins, a pull-down resistor, and on ICs the input ESD diodes and a series resistor. And on the bench, ESD-safe handling."
:::

::: terms
- **Gate oxide**: the thin $\text{SiO}_2$ (or high-k) insulator between the gate and channel.
- **Dielectric strength / breakdown field**: the maximum electric field an insulator withstands, about 10 MV/cm for thermal oxide (intrinsic).
- **$V_{GS(max)}$**: datasheet absolute maximum gate-source voltage.
- **TDDB**: time-dependent dielectric breakdown, long-term wear-out of oxide at fields below the instant-breakdown limit.
- **ESD (electrostatic discharge)**: a short, high-voltage event from static charge.
- **Miller capacitance ($C_{gd}$)**: gate-drain capacitance that couples drain dv/dt into the gate.
- **Gate resistor ($R_g$)**: series resistance that sets switching speed and damps gate ringing.
- **Clamp (Zener/TVS)**: a device that limits $V_{GS}$ by conducting at a set voltage.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "the oxide is thin and breaks down with too much voltage." An exceptional answer:

- Connects breakdown to the **field** $E=V/t_{ox}$ with a number (about 10 MV/cm) and shows why 5 V is dangerous at 5 nm.
- Explains the **ESD amplification** $V=Q/C$ with the example that small gates are more fragile than large ones.
- Names the **in-circuit mechanisms**: ringing (with a quick $\zeta$ and overshoot estimate), Miller coupling, floating gate.
- Gives layered protection: $R_g$, clamp, pull-down, on-chip diodes, layout (tight gate loop), handling.
- Notes that damage can be latent (TDDB) so a surviving part may fail later.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The gate draws no current so it can't be hurt by voltage."** The lack of DC current is the problem: there is no path to bleed excess charge, and overvoltage breaks the insulator instead.
- **Treating $V_{GS(max)}$ as a design target.** Design to a margin, include ringing overshoot, and use a clamp if the supply or driver can exceed it.
- **Ignoring negative $V_{GS}$.** The limit is usually symmetric (±20 V). Turn-off with a negative gate drive is fine to the limit, but inductive undershoot isn't.
- **Leaving a gate floating** or relying on an MCU pin's high-impedance state at reset. Add a pull-down.
- **Using a Zener rated at the abs max.** A 20 V Zener clamps *above* where it starts conducting; choose a clamp voltage below the rating including tolerance and clamping rise.
- **Assuming only static discharge matters.** Ringing and Miller coupling overstress the oxide in normal operation.
:::

::: deep
## The gate is a very good capacitor with a very thin insulator

Treat the gate as a parallel-plate capacitor with area $A=WL$ and gap $t_{ox}$:

$$C_g=\frac{\varepsilon_{ox}WL}{t_{ox}},\qquad E_{ox}=\frac{V_{GS}}{t_{ox}}$$

This is the same capacitor from [[q0022]], with one difference: in a MOSFET the insulator must be as thin as possible. A thin oxide gives a large $C_{ox}$, so a small $V_{GS}$ induces a large channel charge $Q=C_{ox}(V_{GS}-V_t)$. High transconductance and low switching voltage need thin oxide. Thin oxide is also exactly what makes it vulnerable.

| Device class | $t_{ox}$ (typical order) | Typical rated $V_{GS}$ | Field at the rated voltage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Advanced logic (1.0 V core) | about 1-2 nm | about 1.0-1.2 V | several MV/cm |
| 3.3 V I/O device | about 5-7 nm | 3.3-3.6 V | 5-7 MV/cm |
| Logic-level power MOSFET | tens of nm | ±8 to ±12 V | a few MV/cm |
| Standard power MOSFET | about 50-100 nm | ±20 V | 2-4 MV/cm |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Oxide thicknesses for commercial power MOSFETs are not usually published and vary by vendor and generation; the figures above are order-of-magnitude estimates to show the pattern, not data. Advanced logic nodes also use high-k dielectric stacks, so "oxide thickness" is an equivalent thickness.

The "rated" field is far below the instantaneous breakdown field (10 MV/cm) because of **TDDB**: even at a lower field, defects accumulate and the oxide eventually shorts. Raising the field shortens the life roughly exponentially. So the rated limit buys a lifetime (ten years or more of operation), and a one-time spike above it can cause latent damage that appears later.

## Static charge: $V=Q/C$

An insulator with no DC path holds charge indefinitely. The gate has a tiny capacitance, so the same charge produces a very different voltage on different devices:

| Charge | Gate capacitance | Resulting voltage |
|---|---|---|
| 1 nC | 500 pF (power FET $C_{iss}$) | 2 V |
| 1 nC | 5 pF (small signal FET) | 200 V |
| 10 pC | 34.5 fF (logic gate, 10 µm × 1 µm) | about 290 V |
| 200 nC (HBM, 2 kV, 100 pF) | 500 pF | about 400 V *(if all of it reached the gate)* |

Large power MOSFETs tolerate more ESD simply because they're big, though the real defence is protection. Tiny RF and logic devices are fragile, which is why CMOS ICs have protection circuits at every pin ([[q0112]]) and why you handle bare parts in ESD-safe packaging.

## In-circuit overstress: the gate loop rings

The driver, gate trace, bond wire and package inductance $L_g$, internal gate resistance, and $C_{iss}$ form a series RLC:

$$\zeta=\frac{R_g}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C_{iss}}{L_g}},\qquad \text{overshoot}=e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\ (\zeta<1)$$

**Miller coupling.** When the drain swings by $dv/dt$, current $C_{gd}\,dv/dt$ flows into the gate network. With a weak pull-down the gate rises: $\Delta V_{gs}\approx R_{pd}C_{gd}\,dv/dt$. At 5 Ω, 50 pF and 5 V/ns this is 1.25 V (benign), but with a 1 kΩ pull-down it would be 250 V in this simple formula, which obviously means the gate would turn on and clamp, causing shoot-through ([[q0109]]). Strong turn-off drive, or a negative turn-off bias, solves it.

## Real hardware

- **Common-source inductance.** Source-lead inductance $L_s$ shared by the power and gate loops lifts the source during a fast di/dt, making the effective $V_{GS}$ seen by the die smaller during turn-on and larger (more negative) during turn-off. Kelvin source pins on power packages separate the two loops.
- **Zener behaviour.** A Zener's clamp voltage rises with current and it has its own capacitance; a 15 V Zener may hold $V_{GS}$ at 17-18 V during a large spike. A TVS is faster and rated for pulses.
- **ESD protection on IC pins.** A series resistor plus diode clamps to the rails ([[q0112]]).
- **Latent damage.** A part that survived a spike can have a partly damaged oxide with elevated gate leakage ($I_{GSS}$ at the µA level is a red flag).
:::

::: applications
- **Gate drivers for power MOSFETs**: series $R_g$ and gate clamp to survive ringing.
- **MOSFET input stages / MCU pins**: on-chip ESD structures and series resistors keep transient energy off the gate oxide ([[q0112]]).
- **Handling and assembly**: ESD wrist straps, conductive foam, and shorting bars on bare discrete MOSFETs.
- **Gate-drive layout**: minimizing gate-loop inductance, Kelvin source connections.
- **Negative turn-off bias in half bridges** to prevent Miller turn-on, with a clamp to protect against over-negative excursions.
- **Hot-swap and load-switch circuits** with a gate-source Zener to protect the pass FET when the gate-drive supply is higher than the FET's rating.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Thermal SiO₂ with the intrinsic breakdown field of order 10 MV/cm** as the textbook number; high-k and nitrided oxides, thin gate stacks and process defects change both breakdown and reliability.
- **Abs-max ratings are datasheet limits**; the exact $t_{ox}$ and design margin are vendor-specific and unpublished.
- **Series RLC model** of the gate loop is a first approximation; real ringing involves distributed inductance and the Miller effect.
- For **GaN HEMTs** the gate rating is much tighter (about 6 V max in many parts, with 5 V drive), so the same concerns are even more acute; for **IGBTs** the gate is also an insulated oxide.
- If asked about **JFETs/BJTs**, the answer differs: a forward-biased junction conducts rather than breaking down.
:::

::: followups
### What is the oxide field in a MOSFET with $t_{ox}=5$ nm and $V_{GS}=3.3$ V? Is that safe?
$E=3.3/(5\times10^{-7}\text{ cm})=6.6$ MV/cm. It's below the 10 MV/cm intrinsic limit but high enough that the process designers allow only a limited margin: this is a 3.3 V I/O device whose rating is typically about 3.6 V. A 5 V spike would hit about 10 MV/cm and break it down.

### Why do power MOSFETs with similar ratings differ so much in ESD robustness?
The gate capacitance differs. A 5 nC gate with 1 nF of $C_{iss}$ turns 1 nC of static into only about 1 V, whereas a small-signal part with 5 pF turns it into 200 V. Bigger dies survive better, so small-signal MOSFETs and RF parts often ship with built-in gate protection diodes.

### How do you choose a gate resistor to avoid overshoot?
Compute the critical value $R_{crit}=2\sqrt{L_g/C_{iss}}$ (6.3 Ω for 10 nH and 1 nF) and choose near or above it, then check switching loss with that gate resistance; some designers use a faster turn-on and slower turn-off with a diode in parallel. Then verify on the bench with a differential or well-grounded probe, because probe inductance adds ringing of its own.

### Why can a floating gate be destructive and not merely "indeterminate"?
With no pull-down, leakage and coupling charge the gate to any voltage. It may pass through the threshold and turn the FET partly on (linear mode, heating) or drift beyond the oxide limit. Always provide a defined state with a pull-down or pull-up.

### Harder: how does a clamp Zener that's too slow or too distant fail to protect?
The lead and trace inductance between the Zener and the gate/source pins adds voltage during a fast edge ($L\,di/dt$), and the Zener takes time to turn on and has a series resistance, so the oxide sees a higher voltage than the clamp's rating. Put it at the pins, use a TVS if the events are fast, and check the dynamic clamp voltage on the datasheet.

### Scenario: a half-bridge works, but one low-side FET in each unit fails short gate-source after a few hours at high dv/dt. What do you suspect?
Miller-induced spikes and gate ringing. The high-side turn-on makes a large dv/dt on the low-side drain, which injects current through $C_{gd}$ into the gate. If the gate pull-down is weak or the loop inductance is large, the gate can overshoot above the rating or momentarily turn on (shoot-through). Scope the low-side $V_{GS}$ at the pins during the high-side turn-on; add a stronger pull-down, a clamp, or a negative turn-off drive.
:::
