::: basics
### In plain words
A filter lets some frequencies through and blocks others. A low-pass filter (LPF) passes slow signals and blocks fast ones. A high-pass filter (HPF) does the opposite. A band-pass filter (BPF) passes only a middle range. A resistor and capacitor can build the simplest ones, because a capacitor's behavior changes with frequency.

### Picture it
Think of a car's suspension driving down a road. It smooths out quick, small bumps but follows the slow rise and fall of the hills. That is a low-pass filter. A high-pass filter would be the reverse: it would ignore the hills and react only to the quick bumps. Both are really just a voltage divider ([[q0008]]) whose bottom or top part changes with frequency.

### Words you need
- **Frequency**: how many cycles per second a signal repeats, in hertz (Hz).
- **Corner (cutoff) frequency**: the point where the output has fallen to 0.707 of the input. For an RC filter, $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$.
- **Capacitor**: a part that stores charge. It blocks steady, one-direction (DC) current but passes fast changes. See [[q0022]] and [[q0024]].
- **dB (decibel)**: a way to say how much a signal shrinks. −3 dB is about 0.707 of the input, and −20 dB is one tenth.
- **Roll-off**: how steeply the output falls past the corner, here 20 dB (ten times smaller) per tenfold rise in frequency.

### The simplest example
Build a low-pass with a 1 kΩ resistor in series and a 159 nF capacitor to ground. Then $f_c = 1/(2\pi \times 1000 \times 159\times10^{-9})$, about 1 kHz. At 100 Hz almost everything passes. At 1 kHz the output is 0.707 of the input. At 10 kHz it is only about 10%. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page derives the gain and phase, swaps R and C for the high-pass, and combines stages for a band-pass.
:::

::: testing
The interviewer wants to see you go from a schematic to a frequency response:

1. Treat the filter as an **impedance divider** and write $H(j\omega) = V_{out}/V_{in}$.
2. Find the **corner frequency** ($f_c = 1/2\pi RC$ for first order) and know what happens there: −3 dB, 0.707 amplitude, ±45° phase.
3. Sketch the **Bode plot**: flat passband, −20 dB/decade per pole, and the phase transition.
4. Reason by **limiting cases** (cap open at DC, short at high frequency) so you can identify LPF vs HPF by inspection.
5. Build a **BPF** from an HPF and an LPF (or an RLC), and know about centre frequency, bandwidth, $Q$ and loading between stages.
:::

::: why
Filters are in nearly every board: RC anti-aliasing in front of ADCs, debounce filters on buttons, AC-coupling (high-pass) capacitors on audio and high-speed links, PWM-to-analog conversion, EMI filters on power inputs, LC output filters on buck converters, bandpass filters in radios and sensor front ends. In debugging you'll constantly ask "what does this RC do to my signal?" Interviewers use filter analysis to test whether you can combine impedance, dividers and logarithmic thinking, which also underpins op-amp and control-loop analysis.
:::

::: concepts
- **Impedance of C and L**: $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)$ falls with frequency; $Z_L = j\omega L$ rises. See [[q0024]], [[q0040]].
- **Voltage divider with impedances**: $H = Z_2/(Z_1+Z_2)$. See [[q0008]].
- **Transfer function, poles and zeros**: $H(s)$ with $s = j\omega$; each real pole adds −20 dB/dec and −90° eventually. See [[q0129]].
- **Decibels**: $20\log_{10}|H|$; −3 dB ≈ 0.707 amplitude ≈ half power; −20 dB = 0.1×.
- **Corner (cutoff) frequency and bandwidth**: where the response is 3 dB below the passband. See [[q0132]], [[q0133]].
- **Loading**: a following stage's input impedance changes the filter, just like loading a divider.
:::

::: answer
"I treat every passive filter as a voltage divider made of impedances, $H = Z_2/(Z_1 + Z_2)$, and then sanity-check with the limiting cases: a capacitor is open at DC and a short at high frequency, an inductor the opposite.

For an RC low-pass, with R in series and C to ground, I get $H = 1/(1 + j\omega RC)$. At DC the cap is open so the gain is 1; at high frequency the cap shorts the output. The corner frequency is $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$, where the output is down 3 dB, 0.707 of the input, with −45° of phase. Above that it rolls off at −20 dB per decade. So 1 kΩ and 159 nF gives about 1 kHz, and at 10 kHz the signal is down to about 10%.

Swapping R and C gives a high-pass: $H = j\omega RC/(1 + j\omega RC)$, same corner, blocks DC, passes high frequencies with phase going from +90° to 0.

For a bandpass, I cascade a high-pass and a low-pass, with the corners setting the band edges, ideally with the second stage's impedance much higher or a buffer in between so they don't load each other. Or I use a series RLC with the output across R, which peaks at $1/\sqrt{LC}$ with bandwidth set by $Q$."
:::

::: technical
**Method.** (1) Replace elements by impedances. (2) $H(j\omega)$ from the divider rule. (3) Put it in standard form; read off poles/zeros. (4) Check $\omega\to0$ and $\omega\to\infty$. (5) Asymptotic Bode: each pole −20 dB/dec and −90°, each zero +20 dB/dec and +90°.

**RC low-pass** (R series, C shunt):
$$H(j\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega RC},\quad |H| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_c)^2}},\quad \angle H = -\arctan\frac{f}{f_c},\quad f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$$
At $f_c$: $|H| = 1/\sqrt2$ (−3.01 dB), −45°. Far above: $|H| \approx f_c/f$ (−20 dB/dec). Time domain: $\tau = RC$, 10–90% rise time $t_r = 2.2\tau \approx 0.35/f_c$.

**RC high-pass** (C series, R shunt):
$$H(j\omega) = \frac{j\omega RC}{1 + j\omega RC},\quad |H| = \frac{f/f_c}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_c)^2}},\quad \angle H = 90^\circ - \arctan\frac{f}{f_c}$$

**RL versions:** RL low-pass (L series, R shunt) and RL high-pass (R series, L shunt) with $f_c = R/(2\pi L)$.

**Second-order (series RLC):** output across C → LPF $\frac{1}{s^2LC + sRC + 1}$ (−40 dB/dec); across L → HPF $\frac{s^2LC}{s^2LC + sRC + 1}$; across R → BPF $\frac{sRC}{s^2LC + sRC + 1}$, centre $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$, bandwidth $\Delta f = \frac{R}{2\pi L}$, $Q = f_0/\Delta f = \frac1R\sqrt{L/C}$. Across L and C together → band-stop (notch).

**BPF by cascade:** HPF ($f_L$) then LPF ($f_H$), $f_L \ll f_H$: passband $f_L$–$f_H$, centre $\sqrt{f_Lf_H}$, $Q \approx f_0/(f_H - f_L) < 0.5$ for well-separated real poles. Loading: LPF input impedance must be ≫ HPF output impedance ($R_2 \gg R_1$), else buffer.

**Example values.** $R$ = 1 kΩ, $C$ = 159 nF → $f_c$ = 1.00 kHz. At 100 Hz: 0.995 (−0.04 dB, −5.7°). At 10 kHz: 0.0996 (−20.0 dB, −84.3°). At 100 kHz: 0.0100 (−40.0 dB).
:::

::: long
"The way I analyse any passive filter is as an impedance divider. Replace the capacitor with $1/j\omega C$ and the inductor with $j\omega L$, write $V_{out}/V_{in} = Z_2/(Z_1 + Z_2)$, and then check the limits: at DC a capacitor is open and an inductor is a short; at very high frequency it's the reverse. That alone tells you whether it's low-pass or high-pass before any algebra.

Take the RC low-pass: resistor in series, capacitor to ground. $H = 1/(1 + j\omega RC)$. At low frequency that's 1, at high frequency it falls off as $1/\omega$, which is −20 dB per decade. The corner is where $\omega RC = 1$, so $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$. There the magnitude is $1/\sqrt2$, which is −3 dB or half power, and the phase is −45°. With 1 kΩ and 159 nF, the corner is 1 kHz; at 10 kHz the output is about 10% of the input and the phase is about −84°.

The high-pass just swaps the parts: capacitor in series, resistor to ground. $H = j\omega RC/(1 + j\omega RC)$. It blocks DC, has the same corner frequency, rises at +20 dB per decade below it, and the phase goes from +90° down to 0. That's an AC-coupling capacitor.

For a bandpass, there are two common ways. One is a high-pass followed by a low-pass: the high-pass corner sets the lower edge, the low-pass corner sets the upper edge, and the centre is the geometric mean. The catch is loading: the second stage's impedance needs to be much bigger than the first's, say 10×, or I put a buffer in between. The other is a series RLC with the output across R: it peaks at $1/2\pi\sqrt{LC}$, and the bandwidth is $R/2\pi L$, so Q sets how narrow it is.

Second-order versions, like an LC low-pass, roll off at −40 dB per decade but can peak near resonance if lightly damped, which matters for things like a buck converter's output filter."
:::

::: terms
- **Transfer function $H(j\omega)$**: complex ratio $V_{out}/V_{in}$ versus frequency; magnitude and phase.
- **Cutoff / corner frequency ($f_c$)**: where $|H|$ is 3 dB below the passband; $1/2\pi RC$ for first-order RC.
- **Passband / stopband**: frequencies passed with little attenuation / strongly attenuated.
- **Roll-off**: slope of the magnitude response beyond the corner; −20 dB/decade (−6 dB/octave) per pole.
- **Pole / zero**: roots of the denominator / numerator of $H(s)$; set breakpoints in the Bode plot.
- **Bode plot**: magnitude in dB and phase in degrees versus log frequency.
- **Centre frequency, bandwidth, Q**: for a bandpass, $f_0$, $\Delta f = f_H - f_L$, and $Q = f_0/\Delta f$.
- **Loading**: when the next stage's input impedance alters the filter's response.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** gives $f_c = 1/2\pi RC$ and says "low-pass passes low frequencies".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives $H(j\omega)$ from the **impedance divider** and identifies LPF/HPF by **limiting cases** before calculating.
- States what happens at $f_c$ exactly (0.707, −3 dB, half power, ±45°) and the asymptotic slopes in both dB/decade and dB/octave.
- Sketches **both magnitude and phase**.
- Explains **two ways to build a BPF** and addresses **loading** quantitatively ($R_2 \ge 10R_1$ or a buffer).
- Connects to the **time domain** ($\tau = RC$, $t_r \approx 0.35/f_c$) and real uses (anti-aliasing, AC coupling, PWM DAC).
- Mentions second-order behaviour (−40 dB/dec, possible peaking) and when you'd need it.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using $f_c = 1/RC$**: that's $\omega_c$ in rad/s. In hertz it's $1/(2\pi RC)$, a factor of 6.28 difference.
- **Thinking the signal is "cut off" sharply at $f_c$.** A first-order filter only reaches −3 dB there; at 2× $f_c$ it's still at 0.45 (−7 dB). Steep cutoffs need higher order.
- **Mixing up which element goes where.** Use the limits: output across a shunt capacitor → low-pass; output across a shunt resistor after a series capacitor → high-pass.
- **Cascading passive stages and multiplying their individual $H$'s.** That ignores loading. Only valid with a buffer or a large impedance ratio between stages.
- **Forgetting the source and load impedances.** A 50 Ω source in series with the filter's R, or a 10 kΩ ADC input loading the output, shifts $f_c$ and the passband gain.
- **Saying −3 dB means "half the voltage".** It's half the *power*; the voltage is 0.707×. Half the voltage is −6 dB.
:::

## The one idea: filters are frequency-dependent dividers

From [[q0008]], a divider gives $V_{out} = V_{in}\frac{Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}$. With resistors the ratio is constant. Replace one resistor with a capacitor ($Z_C = 1/j\omega C$, large at low frequency, small at high) and the ratio depends on frequency. That's a filter.

## Deriving the RC low-pass

$$H(j\omega) = \frac{Z_C}{R + Z_C} = \frac{1/(j\omega C)}{R + 1/(j\omega C)} = \frac{1}{1 + j\omega RC}$$

Define $\omega_c = 1/RC$, i.e. $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$. Then

$$|H| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_c)^2}},\qquad \angle H = -\arctan(f/f_c)$$

Three regions:
- $f \ll f_c$: $|H| \approx 1$ (0 dB), phase ≈ 0.
- $f = f_c$: $|H| = 1/\sqrt2 = 0.707$ (−3.01 dB), phase −45°. The real and imaginary parts of the denominator are equal.
- $f \gg f_c$: $|H| \approx f_c/f$, so every 10× in frequency gives 10× less output: **−20 dB/decade** (−6 dB/octave). Phase → −90°.

**Worked numbers** ($R$ = 1 kΩ, $C$ = 159 nF, $f_c$ = 1.00 kHz):

| $f$ | $\lvert H\rvert$ | dB | phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 Hz | 0.995 | −0.04 | −5.7° |
| 1 kHz | 0.707 | −3.0 | −45° |
| 10 kHz | 0.0996 | −20.0 | −84.3° |
| 100 kHz | 0.0100 | −40.0 | −89.4° |

The **high-pass** is the complement: $H_{HP} = \frac{R}{R + Z_C} = \frac{j\omega RC}{1 + j\omega RC}$. Note $H_{LP} + H_{HP} = 1$: the two outputs always add back to the input.

## The time-domain view

The same RC has time constant $\tau = RC$ = 159 µs. A step into the LPF gives an exponential rise, $1 - e^{-t/\tau}$; the 10–90% rise time is $2.2\tau \approx 0.35/f_c$ = 350 µs. A step into the HPF gives a jump followed by exponential decay back to zero (it "differentiates" edges). This is the bridge to [[q0035]] (building filters from one capacitor) and why a slow RC on a digital line rounds its edges.

> [!TIP]
> Rule of thumb: $f_c \cdot t_r \approx 0.35$ for any first-order system. A 1 kHz LPF can't pass edges faster than ~350 µs; a scope with 100 MHz bandwidth shows rise times no faster than ~3.5 ns.

## Band-pass, option 1: HPF then LPF

Put a high-pass with corner $f_L$ in front of a low-pass with corner $f_H > f_L$. Low frequencies are blocked by the HPF, high frequencies by the LPF, and the band in between gets through.

Figure: R₂ is 10× R₁, so the LPF barely loads the HPF. If R₂ were comparable to R₁, the stages would interact and the corners would shift; then you'd insert a buffer op-amp.

With an ideal buffer between them, $H = H_{HP}\cdot H_{LP}$. The centre is the geometric mean $f_0 = \sqrt{f_Lf_H} = \sqrt{100\times10^4} = 1$ kHz, the bandwidth ≈ 9.9 kHz, and $Q = f_0/\Delta f \approx 0.1$: a wide band. Without the buffer, KCL at both nodes gives

$$H(s) = \frac{sR_1C_1}{s^2R_1R_2C_1C_2 + s\left(R_1C_1 + R_2C_2 + R_1C_2\right) + 1}$$

The extra $R_1C_2$ term is the loading. With $R_2 = 10R_1$ it's small: the mid-band gain at 1 kHz is −0.095 dB versus −0.086 dB buffered.

## Band-pass, option 2: series RLC

Series R, L, C with the output across R: $H = \frac{sRC}{s^2LC + sRC + 1}$. It peaks at $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ with unity gain, and the −3 dB bandwidth is $\Delta f = R/(2\pi L)$, so $Q = \frac1R\sqrt{L/C}$. Unlike the RC cascade, an RLC can be *narrow* ($Q \gg 1$), because the complex poles can sit close together. That's why tuned circuits in radios use L and C. The detailed analysis and a Bode plot for different $Q$ are in [[q0009]].

| Bandpass type | Q range | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| RC HPF + LPF | < 0.5 | no inductors, cheap | wide only, loading |
| Series/parallel RLC | any, high possible | narrow, selective | inductors: size, parasitics |
| Active (op-amp) | any | gain, no inductors | needs supply, GBW limits |

## Second-order filters and why you'd want them

A first-order filter only gives −20 dB/decade. To attenuate a 100 kHz switching ripple by 60 dB while passing 1 kHz, a first-order filter would need its corner at 100 Hz, which would also attenuate the signal you want. A second-order LC low-pass rolls off at −40 dB/decade, so a corner at 10 kHz gives about 40 dB at 100 kHz. Higher-order active filters (Butterworth, Chebyshev, Bessel) trade flatness, steepness and phase linearity; they're beyond "basic" but worth naming.

The catch with second-order LC filters: with light damping they **peak** near resonance (gain above 1). A buck converter's output LC filter is exactly this, and its peaking and phase drop are why the control loop needs compensation ([[q0246]]).

## Practical design notes

- **Account for source and load.** Source resistance adds to $R$ in an RC LPF; load resistance appears in parallel with the shunt element. A 10 kΩ ADC input across a 10 kΩ/10 nF LPF halves the DC gain and doubles $f_c$.
- **Pick impedance levels deliberately.** Too low wastes drive current; too high makes the filter sensitive to loading, leakage and noise.
- **Capacitor type matters.** Class 2 ceramics (X7R, X5R) lose capacitance with DC bias, shifting $f_c$; use C0G/NP0 for precise filters.
:::

::: applications
- **ADC anti-aliasing**: an RC low-pass (e.g. 10 kΩ, 10 nF → 1.59 kHz) before a slow ADC; the capacitor also supplies the sample-and-hold's charge.
- **AC coupling**: a series capacitor into a bias resistor is an HPF that removes DC from audio or blocks DC between differently-biased high-speed transceivers.
- **PWM to analog (PWM DAC)**: a low-pass with $f_c$ far below the PWM frequency extracts the average voltage.
- **Button debouncing**: an RC low-pass smooths contact bounce before a Schmitt-trigger input.
- **EMI and power filtering**: LC or ferrite-bead + capacitor low-passes on supply inputs; the buck converter's output LC.
- **Sensor and radio front ends**: bandpass filters select a frequency band (e.g. an ultrasonic receiver near 40 kHz, or an RF channel).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal source** (zero output impedance) and **unloaded output** (infinite load impedance). Otherwise include them in the divider.
- **Ideal components**: real capacitors have ESR/ESL and voltage coefficients, inductors have DCR and self-resonance ([[q0011]]); at high frequency a real RC low-pass stops attenuating because the cap's ESL takes over.
- **Linear, sinusoidal steady state** for $H(j\omega)$. Transients use the same poles via the Laplace transform.
- **"Basic"** means first-order RC/RL and simple second-order RLC; active filters (Sallen-Key, multiple feedback) follow the same principles with op-amps.
:::

::: followups
### What's the attenuation of a first-order RC LPF one octave above its corner?
At $f = 2f_c$: $|H| = 1/\sqrt{1+4} = 0.447$, which is −7.0 dB. Asymptotically it approaches −6 dB per octave, but near the corner the true curve is below the asymptotes. At $10f_c$ it's −20.0 dB.

### How do you make a steeper filter?
Increase the order: cascade stages (with buffering) or use LC/active topologies. Each pole adds −20 dB/decade. A Butterworth design places poles for a maximally flat passband; Chebyshev trades passband ripple for a steeper transition; Bessel trades steepness for nearly constant group delay (good pulse shape). Cascading identical buffered RC stages doesn't give a sharp knee: two stages are −6 dB at the original $f_c$, not −3 dB.

### Your RC LPF (10 kΩ, 10 nF) drives an ADC with a 10 kΩ input resistance. What happens?
The load is in parallel with C. By Thevenin, the source sees $V_{th} = V_{in}/2$ and $R_{th} = 10\text{k}\parallel10\text{k} = 5$ kΩ, so the DC gain drops to 0.5 and $f_c$ rises from 1.59 kHz to $1/(2\pi\cdot5\text{k}\cdot10\text{n}) = 3.18$ kHz. Fix it with a buffer or a much higher ADC input impedance.

### What's the phase shift of an RC high-pass at its corner, and does the output lead or lag?
+45°: the output leads the input. Below the corner the HPF behaves like a differentiator (≈+90°), above it the phase approaches 0.

### What if you need a narrow bandpass (Q = 20) at 1 kHz without inductors? (harder probe)
Passive RC cascades can't exceed $Q = 0.5$ because their poles are real. You'd use an active bandpass (e.g. multiple-feedback or state-variable topology) where feedback creates complex poles, or a digital filter after the ADC. A Q of 20 at 1 kHz means a 50 Hz bandwidth; component tolerances and the op-amp's gain-bandwidth must be checked, since high-Q active filters are sensitive to both. An LC version would need large inductors at 1 kHz (e.g. 100 mH with 253 nF), which is why inductorless active filters dominate at audio frequencies.

"Basic LPF/BPF/HPF filter analysis" covers three filter types; I treat first-order RC LPF/HPF in depth, then two BPF constructions (RC cascade and series RLC). The source has a dedicated Filters section ([[q0129]]–[[q0133]]) that drills into Bode plots, circuits, roll-off and the 3 dB point; this page is the overview that ties them together. RLC resonance itself is covered in [[q0009]].
:::

::: references
- Alexander & Sadiku, *Fundamentals of Electric Circuits*: frequency response chapter (transfer functions, Bode plots, passive filters, series/parallel resonance).
- Horowitz & Hill, *The Art of Electronics*, 3rd ed.: Chapter 1 (RC filters, frequency analysis) and Chapter 6 (active filters).
- Sedra & Smith, *Microelectronic Circuits*: chapter on filters and tuned amplifiers.
- Texas Instruments, *Active Filter Design Techniques* (chapter 16 of Op Amps for Everyone, SLOD006): filter types, orders and topologies.
:::

::: rubric
- Analyses the filter as an impedance divider and writes $H(j\omega)$ :: The universal method for any passive filter.
- Uses limiting cases (C open at DC, short at high f) to identify LPF vs HPF :: Fast sanity check interviewers love to see.
- States $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$ and the behaviour there (−3 dB, 0.707, ±45°) :: The most-asked specific facts.
- Gives the roll-off (−20 dB/decade per pole) and sketches magnitude and phase :: Shows Bode-plot fluency.
- Builds a BPF (HPF+LPF cascade or RLC) and addresses loading/buffering :: Demonstrates practical design awareness.
- Connects to applications or the time domain (τ, rise time) :: Ties analysis to real hardware use.
:::
