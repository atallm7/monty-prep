::: basics
### In plain words
A schematic symbol is a quick drawing that stands for a real part. The resistor has two common styles. In US-style drawings it is a zigzag line, and in IEC (international, common in Europe) drawings it is a plain rectangle. They mean exactly the same thing. A resistor has no polarity, so it works the same either way around.

### Picture it
Think of road signs, which look a little different from country to country but carry the same message. The zigzag and the rectangle are two dialects for one idea. Small changes to the picture, like an arrow added through or into the body, tell you the resistor is a special type. The analogy is loose, since the symbol also carries labels like R1 and 10 k that make it unambiguous.

### Words you need
- **Schematic symbol**: the standard drawing used in place of a real part.
- **Reference designator**: the label like R1 or R2 that names one specific part.
- **Polarity**: having a required direction, like a battery. Resistors have none.
- **Potentiometer**: a variable resistor with three terminals, including a movable wiper.
- **Thermistor**: a resistor whose value changes with temperature.

### The simplest example
Draw a short zigzag with a wire at each end. Write R1 next to it and 10 k beneath it. That is a complete resistor on a schematic. If you flip it around, nothing changes, because it has no polarity. Now add an arrow pointing at the middle of the zigzag. It becomes a potentiometer with three terminals. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page lists the other variants, such as the thermistor, and the labeling habits that keep a schematic clear.
:::

::: testing
This is a warm-up, and interviewers use it to see two things: (1) whether you can draw cleanly and label a part completely (reference designator, value, terminals), and (2) whether you know the standard variants. Strong candidates draw the zigzag, say "or a rectangle in IEC/European drawings", and then extend it unprompted to the symbols for a potentiometer, a variable resistor and a thermistor. Hidden skill: schematic reading, because most real schematics are a mix of conventions.
:::

::: why
You read symbols constantly: schematics from vendors, reference designs, colleagues, and datasheets use different conventions, and you have to recognize all of them quickly. A good drawing also communicates information. A resistor symbol with "R12 10k 1% 0402" tells a technician what to place, and a wrong symbol (for example a variable resistor where a fixed one is needed) can cause a bad board. Drawing precisely is a small signal of general engineering habits.
:::

::: concepts
- **Resistance as a two-terminal element** ([[q0014]]): no polarity, so the symbol is symmetric and either end can connect to either node.
- **Reference designator and value**: "R" plus a number (R1, R2, ...) identifies the part; the value (10 kΩ) and often tolerance/power are written beside it.
- **Two standard symbol families**: ANSI/IEEE zigzag and IEC rectangle.
- **Variable resistors**: a rheostat (two terminals, variable) and a potentiometer (three terminals, a divider; see [[q0008]]).
- **Sensing resistors**: thermistors, LDRs, varistors use the box or zigzag plus a modifier showing what they respond to.
:::

::: answer
"The resistor in the US-style schematic is a zigzag line, a few peaks, drawn between two leads. In IEC or European drawings it's a plain rectangle with a lead at each end. Both mean the same thing, and neither has polarity, so you can flip it either way.

I'd label it with a reference designator like R1 and its value, say 10 k, and for a real design I'd add tolerance and power rating, something like 1%, 1/10 watt, in the BOM or on the schematic.

The variations are: a potentiometer is the same resistor symbol with an arrow, the wiper, pointing at the middle of the body, giving three terminals. A rheostat or trimmer uses a resistor with a diagonal arrow through it. A thermistor is a resistor symbol with a diagonal line or a small 't' degree mark through it and sometimes a '-t' or '+t' for NTC or PTC. A varistor, a light-dependent resistor and a fuse each have their own small modifiers. If I'm drawing for a teammate I'll also draw it connected, with both ends on wires and a dot at junctions, so there's no ambiguity about what's attached."
:::

::: technical
| Symbol | Standard | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Zigzag (3–4 peaks) | ANSI/IEEE (US, Japan, most EDA tools) | Two terminals; no polarity |
| Rectangle | IEC 60617 (Europe), also common internationally | Same meaning |
| Rectangle/zigzag with arrow through body | Variable resistor / rheostat | 2 terminals, adjustable |
| Resistor with a third arrow terminal at the middle | Potentiometer | 3 terminals: ends A, B and wiper W |
| Resistor with a line and "t°" (and ±) | Thermistor (NTC or PTC) | Resistance varies with temperature |
| Resistor with two arrows pointing in | LDR/photoresistor | Resistance falls with light |
| Resistor with a "U" and a line | Varistor (VDR) | Nonlinear, voltage-dependent |

Common reference designator conventions (formalised in standards such as ASME Y14.44 / IEEE 200 and IEC 81346, though company practice varies): R for resistors, RN or RP for networks, VR or RV for variable resistors, TH or RT for thermistors, F for fuses. Net values in schematic: "10k" or "10 kΩ" with 'k' written in place of the decimal point as 4k7 sometimes used to avoid losing a decimal point on a print.

Behaviorally: the symbol represents $V = IR$ with $R$ constant (fixed) or controlled by a second quantity. Direction of current through it is arbitrary; the voltage sign convention is set by your annotation (passive sign convention: current enters the + terminal).
:::

::: short
"A resistor is a zigzag line in US-style schematics, or a plain rectangle in IEC drawings. It's a two-terminal part with no polarity. I label it with a reference designator like R1 and a value like 10 k. A potentiometer adds an arrow to the middle for the wiper, giving three terminals. A thermistor adds a diagonal line and a little t. I'd also mark junction dots so the connections are clear."
:::

::: long
"In a US-style or most CAD library schematic, a resistor is a zigzag: a lead going in, three to four peaks, and a lead going out. In IEC style it's a rectangle with leads on each side. Both mean the same thing: a two-terminal element obeying V equals I times R. It has no polarity, so there's no plus or minus and I can wire it either way around.

When I draw it I give it a reference designator, so R1, R2 and so on, plus the value like 4.7k, and if it matters, tolerance and power rating. A common shorthand is writing 4k7 instead of 4.7k so a decimal point can't get lost on a printout.

For variants: a potentiometer is a resistor with a third terminal. The arrow touches the middle of the body, and that's the wiper. If I only use the wiper and one end, it's a variable resistor, which is sometimes drawn by sticking a diagonal arrow through the resistor. A thermistor is a resistor with a slanted line through it and a little 't' with a degree sign, often with a minus for NTC, meaning resistance falls as it gets hotter. A photoresistor has two arrows pointing in at the resistor body. A varistor has a 'U' or a line with the voltage label.

I'd also be careful about how it connects: junction dots where wires truly join and no dot where they just cross, and ground symbols where they matter. That way the drawing carries the same information as a netlist."
:::

::: terms
- **Resistor**: a two-terminal element with $V=IR$ ([[q0014]]).
- **Reference designator**: the unique name of a part on a schematic and PCB (R1, R2, ...).
- **ANSI/IEEE symbol**: the zigzag style.
- **IEC symbol**: the rectangular style.
- **Potentiometer ("pot")**: a three-terminal variable resistor used as an adjustable divider.
- **Rheostat**: a two-terminal variable resistor used to adjust current.
- **Thermistor (NTC/PTC)**: a resistor whose value changes strongly with temperature (negative or positive coefficient).
- **Wiper**: the movable contact of a pot.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws a zigzag and says "that's a resistor".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws the zigzag, mentions the IEC rectangle, and says they are equivalent.
- Notes the part has no polarity and shows the reference designator, value, tolerance and power as a complete annotation.
- Volunteers the pot, rheostat, thermistor and LDR variants and how they differ electrically (3 terminals vs 2).
- Draws connections with junction dots and a ground symbol, as a real schematic would.
- Knows common shorthand (4k7, 0R for a zero-ohm jumper) and the reference-designator letter conventions.
- If asked, connects the symbol to a real part: "an 0402 thick film 1 % resistor".
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing an arrow or "+/−" marks on a fixed resistor.** It has no polarity. Marking polarity suggests a capacitor or diode.
- **Confusing the pot and the rheostat.** A pot has three terminals and works as a divider; a rheostat is a variable series resistance with two. Using a pot as a rheostat (wiper tied to one end) is fine; the reverse is not.
- **Omitting value or designator.** A symbol without them is not a design.
- **Wiring errors from drawing**: crossing wires with no dot (not connected) vs. dot (connected). Draw T-junctions with a dot, avoid four-way junctions without dots.
- **Forgetting the zero-ohm resistor.** It's a real part (a jumper) that appears as a resistor with "0" or "0R" and is not a wire in a BOM.
:::

::: deep
## What the symbol represents

A resistor symbol is a statement: "between these two terminals, voltage and current are proportional, $V = IR$, with power $P = I^2R$ dissipated as heat." Because that's true for either direction of current, the symbol is symmetric. All of the information is in two things: which two nodes it connects and what its value is.

> [!TIP]
> A pot has three terminals: the two ends of the resistive track and the wiper. If only two are used it acts as a rheostat; tie the wiper to one end so a broken wiper contact does not leave the circuit open.

## Part specs belong near the symbol

A real resistor isn't just R. On a schematic or in the BOM you also record:

- **Value** (and the E-series it comes from, see [[q0017]]).
- **Tolerance** (1 %, 5 %, 0.1 %).
- **Power rating** ($P = V^2/R$ must be below the rating with derating; see [[q0006]]).
- **Package** (0402, 0603, ...; see [[q0018]]).
- **Temperature coefficient** if it is a precision part.
- **Voltage rating**, important for high-value resistors in divider strings.

Reference designator conventions: R for resistors, RN for networks, RV for variable, TH or RT for thermistors. A zero-ohm link is "R" with value 0, so it appears in the BOM and the pick-and-place file, unlike a wire.

## Why the symbol is symmetric: where $V = IR$ comes from

It helps to know what the zigzag is hiding. In a conductor, charge carriers accelerate in the field, scatter off lattice vibrations and impurities, and settle at an average drift velocity proportional to the field. That gives a current density $J = \sigma E$, and integrating over a bar of length $L$ and cross-section $A$ gives

$$R = \rho\,\frac{L}{A}$$

Nothing in that picture cares which way the field points, which is why the symbol has no polarity. A chip resistor is a thin film, so engineers use the **sheet resistance** $R_s$ in ohms per square: $R = R_s \cdot (L/W)$. A film of $R_s = 1\ \text{k}\Omega/\square$ patterned as a strip ten squares long is $10\ \text{k}\Omega$. Real parts are laser-trimmed by cutting a notch into the film until the measured value lands inside tolerance, which is also why a trimmed part has a bit of extra inductance and noise compared with an untrimmed one.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Typical sheet resistances and film materials (ruthenium-oxide thick film, nichrome or tantalum-nitride thin film) vary by manufacturer and product line. Treat any specific "ohms per square" figure as an example, not a datasheet value.

## Worked example: a potentiometer is a divider, and loading bends it

The pot symbol is just the divider of [[q0008]] with the junction made movable. Let the wiper sit at fractional position $x$ (0 at the ground end, 1 at the top) on a track of total resistance $R_{pot}$ driving a load $R_L$. The lower part of the track is $xR_{pot}$ in parallel with $R_L$, the upper part is $(1-x)R_{pot}$, so

$$V_{out} = V_{in}\,\frac{xR_{pot}\parallel R_L}{(1-x)R_{pot} + xR_{pot}\parallel R_L}$$

Take a 10 kΩ pot at mid-travel ($x = 0.5$) feeding a 10 kΩ load:

1. Lower leg: $5\ \text{k}\Omega \parallel 10\ \text{k}\Omega = 3.333\ \text{k}\Omega$.
2. Upper leg: $5\ \text{k}\Omega$.
3. $V_{out}/V_{in} = 3.333/(5 + 3.333) = 0.400$.

So the knob that "says" halfway delivers 40%. With a 100 kΩ load it is 0.488, and with 1 MΩ it is 0.499. The worst-case deviation from a straight line is about 12.2% of full scale when $R_L = R_{pot}$ and about 1.4% when $R_L = 10R_{pot}$. That is the same "load at least ten times the source resistance" rule, now applied to a moving source.

Figure: The unloaded pot is a straight line. A load equal to the track resistance sags the curve to 0.40 at mid-travel; a load ten times larger keeps it within about 1.4% of the line.

Two practical consequences follow. First, audio "log" or "audio taper" pots deliberately use a nonlinear track so that angle maps roughly to perceived loudness; the symbol is the same, so the part number carries the taper. Second, the wiper has a contact resistance that can be tens to hundreds of ohms and varies as it moves, which is harmless into a high-impedance input and a real problem if the wiper carries current. That is exactly the "tie the wiper to one end" advice from the tip above.

## Worked example: reading a thermistor symbol

The thermistor symbol says "this resistance is controlled by temperature", but not how. For an NTC the common model is the beta equation, with $T$ in kelvin:

$$R(T) = R_0\,\exp\!\left[B\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_0}\right)\right]$$

For a 10 kΩ NTC at 25 °C with $B = 3950\ \text{K}$ (a typical catalog value, not universal):

| T (°C) | R (Ω) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 33,620 |
| 25 | 10,000 |
| 50 | 3,588 |
| 85 | 1,087 |

Placed as the bottom leg of a divider with a 10 kΩ resistor from 3.3 V, the output is 2.54 V at 0 °C, 1.65 V at 25 °C, 0.87 V at 50 °C and 0.32 V at 85 °C. Differentiating at 25 °C gives $dR/dT = -BR_0/T_0^2 \approx -444\ \Omega/\text{K}$, about 4.4% per kelvin: far more sensitive than a platinum RTD, but strongly nonlinear. The self-heating check is quick: at 25 °C the thermistor sees 1.65 V across 10 kΩ, which is 0.27 mW. With a dissipation constant of a few mW per kelvin (vendor-dependent) that is a small fraction of a degree of error, but it grows if you pick a 1 kΩ top resistor.

## A real resistor behind the symbol

The symbol assumes an ideal $R$. On the board the part also has:

- **Tolerance and temperature coefficient.** A 1% part with a 100 ppm/°C coefficient moving from 25 °C to 85 °C changes by $10\,\text{k}\Omega \times 100\times10^{-6} \times 60 = 60\ \Omega$, or 0.6%, on top of its initial tolerance.
- **Power and voltage limits.** A 10 kΩ resistor across 12 V dissipates 14.4 mW, comfortable for a 1/16 W 0402. The same package across 12 V as 470 Ω would burn 306 mW and cook. The package also has a maximum working voltage, so $\sqrt{PR} = 25$ V for 10 kΩ at 1/16 W is not the only limit; the datasheet's rated voltage can be lower.
- **Parasitics.** Series inductance (a fraction of a nanohenry for a chip, several nanohenries for a leaded part) and pad capacitance of a fraction of a picofarad matter once the signal is in the tens of MHz or the resistor is large ([[q0016]]).
- **Markings.** Many chip resistors carry a three-digit code (103 is 10 kΩ), a four-digit code, or an EIA-96 code for 1% parts (two digits for the value, a letter for the multiplier, so 01C is 10.0 kΩ). Very small sizes such as 0402 are usually unmarked, so you identify them from the reel label or by measuring.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Identify the part** from the marking or BOM and read it with a DMM in ohms mode. A DMM sources a small test current, so measure out of circuit or at least with the board unpowered, because parallel paths through ICs and other resistors pull the reading low.
2. **Check that the schematic symbol matches the part.** A pot should read the full track value between its end pins and a smoothly varying value between wiper and either end. A "resistor" that reads 0.05 Ω is a 0 Ω link, not a mistake.
3. **For thermistors**, measure at room temperature and compare with the curve, then warm it gently in your fingers and confirm the resistance falls for an NTC.
4. **Under power**, probe the divider midpoint with a scope or high-impedance DMM and compare with the calculated ratio; a reading that is low points to a load you forgot to draw.

## Reading a schematic

When you read someone else's schematic, translate each symbol into the equation it implies. A zigzag between a 3.3 V net and a signal net with the note "SDA" is a pull-up resistor ([[q0020]]); a zigzag in series with an LED is a current limiter; a zigzag with a diagonal arrow in a sensing circuit is a variable element. Knowing the symbol is only the start; the circuit tells you the role.

- **Schematics for any board**: every PCB design package (KiCad, Altium, OrCAD) offers both symbol styles for resistors.
- **Datasheet application circuits**: LED drive, pull-ups, feedback dividers all use the resistor symbol with designators.
- **Teaching and whiteboard interviews**: quick sketches of dividers, filters and bias networks all start here.
- **Pots on audio and test gear**: volume knobs, bias trim pots on calibration circuits.
- **Thermistor temperature sensing and inrush limiting** in power supplies, drawn with the thermistor symbol.

::: assumptions
- I assume the **ANSI/IEEE zigzag** is the default (as used by most US interviewers and EDA tools), with the IEC rectangle as the alternative.
- Variant symbols differ in minor details between standards and CAD libraries; use the one the team uses.
- A real resistor also has parasitic inductance and capacitance ([[q0016]]), which the simple symbol does not show. You draw them only when they matter for the analysis.
- If asked to draw a "resistor" on a whiteboard, you can include the leads and a label; no need for exact peak counts.

::: followups
### Draw a potentiometer used as a variable voltage divider. Which terminal is the output?
Draw the resistor body with its two ends tied to the supply and ground, and the wiper (arrow) as the output. The output voltage is $V_{in}\cdot x$, where $x$ is the wiper's fractional position along the track from the ground end. Add a series resistor at each end if you want to limit the range.

### What is the difference between a pot and a rheostat?
A pot uses all three terminals as a divider; a rheostat uses two (one end and the wiper) as an adjustable resistance to control current. Electrically, a pot wired with the wiper tied to one end is a rheostat.

### How do you draw an NTC thermistor and what does "NTC" mean?
A resistor symbol with a slanted line through it and "t°" (often with a minus sign) next to it. NTC means negative temperature coefficient: resistance falls as temperature rises. PTC is the opposite. NTCs are used for temperature sensing, PTCs as resettable fuses.

### Why would a schematic include a 0 Ω resistor?
It is a jumper that can be populated or omitted (to select a configuration), a way to cross a trace on a single-layer board, or a place to attach a probe or break a ground connection for testing. It carries about the resistance of a short trace plus tolerance, usually tens of mΩ at most, with a current rating on the datasheet.

### What if the schematic shows a rectangle with a diagonal line and no label?
The diagonal line means it is variable (or trim). Without a value I'd ask for the BOM or the design intent, since the symbol alone doesn't say whether it's a pot, a thermistor or something else. That's why part labels matter.
:::
