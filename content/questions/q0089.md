::: basics
### In plain words
A MOSFET is a transistor controlled by a voltage on its gate. Engineers draw it with a standard symbol, and the symbol itself tells you which type it is. The N-channel (NMOS) and P-channel (PMOS) symbols are almost identical, and the one clue that separates them is the direction of a small arrow.

### Picture it
Picture the symbol as a tiny diagram of the device. A plate on the left is the gate, held apart from the channel by a gap, which stands for the insulating oxide. The channel is a broken vertical line on the right with drain at the top and source at the bottom. The arrow points from the P side to the N side (P and N are the two kinds of specially treated silicon). It points in for NMOS and out for PMOS.

### Words you need
- **Gate (G)**: the control terminal, insulated from the rest of the device.
- **Drain (D) and source (S)**: the two ends of the channel that current flows between.
- **Body (B)**: the piece of silicon the channel forms in. The arrow sits on this line. See [[q0090]].
- **Enhancement mode**: no channel until you apply gate voltage. A broken channel line shows this.
- **Body diode**: a built-in one-way valve for current (a diode) from source to drain, often drawn on power MOSFETs.

### The simplest example
To draw an NMOS in words: a gate line goes to a vertical plate, a small gap follows, then a broken vertical channel line. The drain leaves the top, the source leaves the bottom, and the body arrow points into the channel. For a PMOS, point the arrow out, and usually flip it so the source is at the top, toward the supply.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows the actual drawings, the arrow rule, and the body diode details.
:::

::: testing
This is a warm-up, but it's a fast filter. The interviewer wants to see:

1. You can draw **both** symbols from memory with the arrow pointing the right way (into the channel for NFET, out for PFET) and the drain and source on the correct sides.
2. You put the transistors in their **natural orientation**: NFET source at the bottom toward ground, PFET source at the top toward the supply. That tells them you've actually wired these up.
3. You know the symbol *encodes physics*: the arrow is the body-to-channel PN junction, the broken line means enhancement mode, and the gate plate not touching the channel means an insulated gate.
4. Bonus: you know there are several conventions (3-terminal discrete with body diode, 4-terminal IC, simplified digital with a bubble) and can read all of them.
:::

::: why
You'll read MOSFET symbols on every schematic you ever touch: load switches, buck converters, reverse-polarity protection, level shifters, CMOS logic. Misreading the type or orientation is a classic way to design a switch that never turns off because its body diode conducts, or a "high-side NFET" that can't turn on because nobody provided a gate voltage above the rail. In an interview, drawing the symbol cleanly and correctly signals hands-on experience, and it sets up the next ten questions in this section (terminals, body tie, N vs P, enhancement vs depletion, body diode).
:::

::: concepts
- **PN junction direction**: the arrow on a semiconductor symbol always points from P to N, like a diode's anode to cathode. See [[q0062]] and [[q0072]].
- **MOSFET terminals**: gate, drain, source and body (bulk). Discrete parts tie body to source internally. See [[q0090]] and [[q0091]].
- **N-channel vs P-channel**: the channel carriers are electrons (NFET) or holes (PFET), which flips every voltage polarity. See [[q0097]].
- **Enhancement vs depletion mode**: whether a channel exists at $V_{GS}=0$. The symbol shows it with a broken or solid channel line. See [[q0098]].
- **Intrinsic body diode**: the body-drain junction appears as a diode from source to drain in an NFET. See [[q0101]].
- **Comparison with the BJT symbol**: same "arrow = PN junction" logic. See [[q0080]].
:::

::: answer
"Sure. For an N-channel enhancement MOSFET I draw the gate as a line coming in from the left to a vertical plate, then a small gap, then a broken vertical line for the channel. The gap is the gate oxide, so the gate doesn't touch the channel. The drain comes off the top of the channel, the source off the bottom, and there's a body connection in the middle with an arrow pointing *into* the channel. On a discrete part that body line is tied to the source.

The P-channel is the same drawing with the arrow pointing *out*, and I usually flip it so the source is on top, because in a real circuit the PFET source goes to the supply and the NFET source goes to ground.

The way I remember it: the arrow is the PN junction between body and channel and it points from P to N. NMOS has a P body and N channel, so it points in. PMOS has an N body and P channel, so it points out. The broken line means enhancement mode, so there's no channel until you apply gate voltage. On power MOSFET datasheets you'll also see the body diode drawn from source to drain, which is a real part of the device."
:::

::: technical
**Enhancement NMOS (discrete, 3-pin).** Gate lead → gate plate (separated from the channel by a gap = $\text{SiO}_2$ insulator) → broken channel line with three segments. Drain on top, source on bottom. The middle segment connects to the body, drawn with an arrow **pointing toward the channel**, and the body line is wired to the source. A body diode is often drawn with anode at source, cathode at drain.

**Enhancement PMOS.** Identical geometry, arrow **pointing away** from the channel. Conventionally drawn with source on top (to $V_{DD}$) and drain on the bottom. Body diode: anode at drain, cathode at source.

**Arrow rule.** The arrow marks the body–channel PN junction and points P → N:
- NMOS: P-type body, N-type inversion channel → arrow points in.
- PMOS: N-type body (N-well), P-type channel → arrow points out.

**Channel line.**
- Broken (three dashes) = enhancement mode, normally off, channel must be induced ($V_t > 0$ for NMOS).
- Solid = depletion mode, normally on ($V_t < 0$ for NMOS).

**Variants you must be able to read.**
- **4-terminal** (IC design): body brought out as a separate pin B, needed whenever $V_{SB}\neq 0$ (body effect, see [[q0091]]).
- **Simplified digital**: plain gate plate and a single channel line; PMOS marked by a **bubble on the gate** (active-low: on when the gate is low). Sometimes an arrow on the source shows conventional current direction (out of the NMOS source, into the PMOS source), like a BJT emitter.
- **Power MOSFET datasheet**: 3-terminal enhancement symbol plus the body diode, sometimes a gate-source Zener for parts with integrated ESD protection.

**Natural orientation.** NMOS: $V_{GS} > V_t$ turns it on, so source at the lowest potential (ground). PMOS: $V_{SG} > |V_t|$ turns it on, so source at the highest potential (supply). That's why CMOS gates are drawn PMOS-on-top, NMOS-on-bottom ([[q0105]]).
:::

::: short
"An NFET symbol is a gate plate separated from a broken channel line, drain on top, source on the bottom, and an arrow on the body pointing into the channel. The PFET is the same with the arrow pointing out, and I flip it so the source is on top at the supply. The arrow is the body-channel PN junction pointing from P to N, the broken line means enhancement-mode, normally off, and the gap shows the gate is insulated. Discrete parts also show the body diode from source to drain."
:::

::: long
"Let me draw both and explain what each piece means, because the symbol is basically a cartoon of the physics.

Starting with the N-channel enhancement MOSFET. The gate comes in from the left and ends at a vertical plate. Next to it, with a small gap, I draw the channel as three short segments. The gap is the gate oxide, so it's telling you the gate is insulated and draws essentially no DC current. The three segments, the broken line, mean it's enhancement mode: there's no channel until I put a positive gate-to-source voltage above threshold. The drain comes off the top segment, the source off the bottom one, and the middle segment connects to the body with an arrow pointing into the channel. On a discrete part, that body line is tied to the source internally.

For the P-channel, everything is the same except the arrow points out. The rule I use is that the arrow is the PN junction between the body and the channel, and it points from P to N like a diode. NMOS has a P-type body and an N-type channel, so the arrow points in. PMOS has an N-type body and a P-type channel, so it points out.

Orientation matters too. I draw the NFET with its source at the bottom going to ground, and the PFET upside down with its source at the top going to the supply. That's how they're used: an NFET turns on when the gate is above the source, so the source wants to be at the lowest voltage, and a PFET turns on when the gate is below the source.

Two more versions worth knowing. Power MOSFET datasheets draw the body diode from source to drain, because it really conducts if you reverse-bias the device. And in digital schematics people often use a simplified symbol where the PMOS just has a bubble on the gate, meaning it turns on when the gate is low. If I'm doing IC design I'd use the four-terminal version with the body as its own pin."
:::

::: terms
- **Gate (G)**: the control terminal, capacitively coupled to the channel through a thin oxide.
- **Drain (D) / Source (S)**: the channel's two ends. Carriers flow *from* the source *to* the drain.
- **Body / bulk / substrate (B)**: the semiconductor the channel forms in. The fourth terminal.
- **Enhancement mode**: no channel at $V_{GS}=0$. Drawn with a broken channel line.
- **Depletion mode**: channel exists at $V_{GS}=0$. Drawn with a solid line.
- **Body diode (intrinsic diode)**: the body-to-drain PN junction, which appears source→drain in an NFET when the body is tied to the source.
- **Bubble**: inversion marker on a PMOS gate in digital schematics: on when the gate is low.
- **High-side / low-side**: switch between the supply and the load vs between the load and ground.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws something MOSFET-shaped and maybe gets the arrow right.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws both symbols cleanly with labelled G, D, S, gets the arrows right, and **explains the arrow as the P→N body-channel junction** instead of a memorized rule.
- Draws the PFET source-on-top and says why (it turns on when the gate goes below the source, so the source sits at the supply).
- Explains the broken line (enhancement, normally off) and the gate gap (insulated gate, near-zero DC gate current).
- Mentions the **body diode** on discrete symbols and its consequence: a single MOSFET only blocks current in one direction.
- Recognises the other conventions: 4-terminal for ICs, bubble-on-gate for digital, depletion-mode solid line.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Arrow backwards.** NMOS arrow points *in*, PMOS *out*. Fix: "arrow points P to N". NMOS = P body → N channel.
- **Drawing a PFET with the source at the bottom near ground.** It's not electrically "wrong" as a symbol, but it suggests you'd wire it as a low-side switch, which needs a negative gate drive. Draw it source-up.
- **Confusing the arrow with current direction.** In the standard symbol it marks the junction, not the drain current. (Only the simplified "arrow on source" style shows current direction, and it's the same direction as the junction arrow would suggest for each type.)
- **Swapping drain and source on a discrete part.** On an IC MOSFET they're symmetric, but on a discrete power MOSFET the body is tied to the source, so swapping them puts the body diode the wrong way and the switch conducts when you think it's off.
- **Drawing the gate touching the channel.** The gap is the whole point: the gate is an insulated capacitor plate, not a junction like a BJT base or a JFET gate.
- **Mixing up depletion and enhancement lines.** Solid = normally on.
:::

::: deep
## The symbol is a cartoon of the cross-section

A MOSFET is a sandwich: a metal (or polysilicon) **gate**, a thin **oxide** insulator, and a **body** of doped silicon. Two heavily doped regions of the *opposite* type, the **source** and **drain**, sit on either side of the gate. Apply enough gate voltage and a thin inversion layer, the **channel**, connects them. Every line in the symbol maps onto that picture:

| Symbol element | Physical meaning |
|---|---|
| Gate lead and vertical plate | The gate electrode |
| Gap between plate and channel | The gate oxide (insulator), so DC gate current ≈ 0 |
| Channel line, broken into 3 segments | Source end, channel region, drain end. The breaks say "no channel until you induce one" (enhancement) |
| Arrow on the middle segment | Body-to-channel PN junction, pointing P → N |
| Body line tied to source | The discrete device's internal source-body short |

In both, the body is tied to the source, which creates the drawn body diode. For the NFET it points source→drain, for the PFET drain→source. Either way it's reverse-biased in normal operation.

## Why the arrow points the way it does

Every arrow in semiconductor symbols follows one convention: it marks a PN junction and **points from P to N**, the direction a diode conducts.

- **NMOS**: built in a P-type body. The channel is an *inversion layer* of electrons, so it behaves as N-type. The junction is P (body) → N (channel), so the arrow points **into** the channel.
- **PMOS**: built in an N-type body (an N-well in CMOS). The channel is an inversion layer of holes, P-type. The junction is P (channel) → N (body), so the arrow points **out**.

This is the same rule that makes the NPN emitter arrow point out (P base → N emitter) and the diode triangle point from anode to cathode ([[q0080]]). A mnemonic if you want one: "**P**MOS: **P**ointing out", or "NMOS: arrow *iN*".

> [!TIP]
> When you see an unfamiliar symbol in a schematic, find the arrow first. Arrow in on a MOSFET = N-channel. Then find which leg the body line ties to: that's the source. Now you know the polarity of everything.

## Four-terminal symbol: when the body isn't the source

In an integrated circuit, all the NMOS transistors usually share one P-substrate tied to ground, and the PMOS transistors sit in N-wells tied to $V_{DD}$. So an NMOS stacked above another one has its source *above* ground while its body is *at* ground. To show that, IC designers bring the body out as a fourth pin.

Because an IC MOSFET is **physically symmetric**, its source and drain are only distinguished by voltage: for NMOS the source is whichever end is at the lower potential. A discrete power MOSFET is **not** symmetric: it's a vertical structure with the body shorted to the source, so the pins are fixed by construction.

Here the body arrows are still drawn, so the bubble is redundant; in purely digital schematics the arrows are usually dropped and the bubble alone marks the PMOS. It reads as "active low": the PMOS conducts when IN is low, pulling OUT up to VDD. Notice the orientation: PMOS source at VDD, NMOS source at ground.

Things you'll see in practice:
- **Bubble on the PMOS gate**, no arrows at all. The bubble *is* the type indicator.
- **Arrow on the source lead** instead of the body: points out of the NMOS source and into the PMOS source, showing the conventional current direction when the device is on. Common in older textbooks and in some EDA libraries.
- **Solid channel line with no breaks** used loosely for any MOSFET, even enhancement-mode parts. Read the part number, not just the line.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Symbol conventions are not perfectly standardized across EDA libraries, textbooks and vendors (IEEE 315 / IEC 60617 style vs. "textbook" vs. vendor styles). Some libraries draw enhancement parts with a solid line, or omit the body diode. When in doubt, the datasheet's own symbol and pinout are authoritative.

## Depletion-mode symbols

If the channel line is **solid**, the device is depletion-mode: a channel exists at $V_{GS} = 0$ and you need to drive the gate *negative* (for an N-channel) to turn it off. These are rare in modern boards but show up in high-voltage start-up circuits and constant-current sources ([[q0098]]).

## Drawing checklist for the whiteboard

1. Gate plate, then a visible gap, then the channel.
2. Broken channel for a normal (enhancement) part.
3. Arrow: in for N, out for P. Say "P to N" out loud as you draw it.
4. Label G, D, S. For PFET put S on top.
5. On a discrete/power part, add the body diode: NFET anode at S, PFET anode at D.
6. If the interviewer is IC-focused, offer the 4-terminal version.
:::

::: applications
- **Power MOSFET datasheets** (e.g. a logic-level NFET like the 2N7002 or a power part in a D2PAK) show the 3-terminal symbol with the body diode. Reading it tells you which way the device blocks.
- **CMOS logic schematics** use PMOS-on-top / NMOS-on-bottom with gate bubbles, as in the inverter above and in every standard cell ([[q0105]]).
- **Load switches and reverse-polarity protection** in the power path use PFETs drawn source-to-supply. The body-diode direction on the symbol is the whole design ([[q0101]]).
- **Analog IC schematics** (current mirrors, differential pairs) use 4-terminal symbols because the body connection changes threshold voltage ([[q0091]]).
- **Bidirectional I²C level shifters** (BSS138-style) depend on the symbol's body diode to explain how the high side gets pulled low ([[q0104]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- I've assumed **enhancement-mode** devices, which are what "NFET/PFET" means in almost every board-level and digital context. Depletion-mode parts use a solid channel line.
- I've used the common **North American textbook convention** (Sedra & Smith, Razavi style). European and some EDA libraries differ in details such as where the arrow sits or whether there's an envelope circle around discrete parts.
- For discrete parts I've assumed **body tied to source**. That's true for essentially all 3-pin power MOSFETs. Some small-signal and analog-switch parts bring the body out as a separate pin, in which case use the 4-terminal symbol.
- A valid alternative answer is to draw the simplified digital symbols (bubble = PMOS). Make sure you can explain why the bubble means "on when low."
:::

::: followups
### Which way does the body diode point in an NFET and why?
Anode at the source, cathode at the drain. The body is P-type and tied to the source, the drain is N+, so the body-drain PN junction runs P (source side) → N (drain side). In normal operation the drain is above the source, so it's reverse-biased. If the drain goes below the source, it conducts at about 0.7 V. See [[q0101]].

### Why is the PFET usually drawn with the source on top?
Because the source is the reference for $V_{GS}$, and a PFET turns on when $V_{GS} < -|V_t|$, i.e. when the gate is pulled *below* the source. To control it with signals between ground and the supply, the source must sit at the supply. So the PFET goes between the supply and the load (high side), source up. The NFET mirrors that: source at ground, low side.

### How do you tell drain from source on an IC MOSFET?
Physically you can't: the device is symmetric. Electrically, for an NMOS the source is whichever terminal is at the lower potential (carriers, electrons, come *from* the source). For a PMOS it's the higher one. In a pass-transistor or transmission gate, the "source" swaps sides depending on which way the signal is moving.

### What does a gate-source Zener on a datasheet symbol mean?
Some MOSFETs include back-to-back Zeners between gate and source for ESD protection. They clamp $V_{GS}$ to protect the thin gate oxide, at the cost of some gate leakage (µA rather than nA) and a lower absolute max $V_{GS}$ clamp. See [[q0102]].

### What if the interviewer draws an NMOS with the arrow on the source pointing out? Is that wrong?
No, that's the simplified style where the arrow sits on the source and shows conventional current flowing out of the source when the device conducts, like an NPN emitter. It still identifies an N-channel device. Same for PMOS with the arrow into the source. Explain both conventions and you look like someone who has read lots of schematics.
:::

::: interpretations
"Draw a PFET/NFET symbol" means draw both, P-channel and N-channel MOSFETs. Unless told otherwise, draw **enhancement-mode** devices, since those are by far the most common. Draw the discrete 3-terminal version with the body diode for a board-level role, or the 4-terminal version (or both) for an IC-design role. "FET" technically also includes JFETs, whose symbol has an arrow on the gate itself, but in this section (MOSFETs) the question clearly means MOSFETs.
:::
