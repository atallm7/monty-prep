::: basics
### In plain words
Materials differ in how easily electric current flows through them. A conductor, such as copper, lets current flow very easily. An insulator, such as glass, almost never does. A semiconductor, such as silicon, sits in between, and you can control how well it conducts. That control is what makes transistors and diodes possible.

### Picture it
Picture a parking lot with a fence. Electrons are cars. In a conductor, there is no fence, and the cars roll freely. In an insulator, the fence is very tall, and no car gets over. In a semiconductor, the fence is low enough that a few cars hop over when they get a boost from heat or light. The analogy breaks down in one way: electrons really sit in energy levels, not in a lot.

### Words you need
- **Free electron**: an electron that can move through the material and carry current.
- **Valence band**: the energy levels of electrons stuck in bonds between atoms.
- **Conduction band**: the energy levels where electrons move freely. See [[q0061]].
- **Band gap**: the energy needed to jump from the valence band to the conduction band.
- **Doping**: adding a tiny amount of impurity to change how well a semiconductor conducts.

### The simplest example
Compare band gaps. Copper has no gap, so electrons move freely. Silicon has a gap of about 1.1 eV, so at room temperature only a tiny fraction of electrons cross it. Silicon dioxide, a glass, has a gap of about 9 eV, so essentially none cross. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers how doping changes silicon's conductivity by about six orders of magnitude, and the quick test that metals get more resistive when heated while semiconductors get less.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can explain the three classes with **one physical model** (energy bands and the band gap) instead of a list of examples. The interviewer wants to hear:

1. The classification is about **how many charge carriers are free to move**, not about some vague "willingness to conduct".
2. **Band picture**: metals have a partially filled band (or overlapping bands), semiconductors have a small gap (≈0.5–3.5 eV), insulators a large gap (≳4–5 eV and often 9 eV).
3. The tell-tale experimental signature: metals get **more** resistive when hot, semiconductors get **less** resistive.
4. Why semiconductors are useful: their conductivity can be set over many orders of magnitude by **doping**, fields and light. That bridges into the whole device-physics section.
:::

::: why
Every component you'll ever use is built from these three classes: copper traces and bond wires (conductors), FR-4, oxides and capacitor dielectrics (insulators), and silicon dies (semiconductors). Knowing *why* they differ explains real behaviour: why copper trace resistance rises about 0.39%/°C, why diode leakage doubles roughly every 10 °C, why a gate oxide can hold off volts across nanometres, and why silicon's conductivity can be engineered. It's also the first rung of the device-physics ladder: band gap → doping ([[q0065]]) → PN junction ([[q0063]]) → diodes and transistors.
:::

::: concepts
- **Resistivity and conductivity** ($\rho = 1/\sigma$, Ω·m or Ω·cm): a material property, independent of shape. Resistance is $R = \rho L/A$. See [[q0001]].
- **Conductivity from carriers**: $\sigma = q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$, so conductivity is (number of mobile carriers) × (how easily they move). See [[q0068]].
- **Energy bands**: in a solid, atomic energy levels broaden into bands; the top filled one is the valence band, the next one up is the conduction band. See [[q0061]].
- **Band gap** $E_g$: the forbidden energy range between them. Its size versus thermal energy $kT \approx 0.0259$ eV at room temperature decides how many electrons get promoted.
- **Holes**: an empty state in the valence band behaves like a positive mobile carrier. See [[q0069]].
- **Doping**: adding parts-per-million impurities to change carrier density by orders of magnitude. See [[q0065]].
:::

::: answer
"The difference comes down to how many electrons are free to move, and band theory explains it.

In a solid, the atoms' energy levels spread into bands. The valence band is the electrons tied up in bonds, and the conduction band is where electrons can move freely through the crystal. Between them there can be a band gap with no allowed states.

In a **conductor** like copper, there's no gap to cross. The highest band is only partly full, so there are around $10^{23}$ free electrons per cm³ and it conducts really well. In an **insulator** like glass or SiO₂, the gap is huge, around 9 eV for oxide, so at room temperature basically no electrons get across and it barely conducts. A **semiconductor** like silicon sits in between with a gap of about 1.1 eV. A tiny fraction of electrons get thermally kicked across, so pure silicon conducts a little.

The useful part is that semiconductor conductivity is controllable. Doping can change it by about six orders of magnitude, and so can light or an electric field, which is how diodes and transistors work. One quick way to tell them apart: heat a metal and its resistance goes up, heat a semiconductor and its resistance drops."
:::

::: technical
**Classification by carrier density.** Conductivity $\sigma = q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$. Mobilities don't differ by more than a factor of ~100 between materials, but carrier densities differ by more than 30 orders of magnitude, so **carrier density is what really separates the classes**.

| Class | Band structure | Free carriers (300 K) | Resistivity (Ω·cm) | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conductor | Partially filled or overlapping bands, no gap | ~$10^{22}$–$10^{23}$ cm⁻³ (Cu: $8.5\times10^{22}$) | ~$10^{-6}$ (Cu $1.7\times10^{-6}$) | Cu, Al, Ag, Au |
| Semiconductor | Gap ≈ 0.5–3.5 eV | intrinsic Si $n_i \approx 10^{10}$; doped $10^{14}$–$10^{20}$ | ~$10^{-3}$ to $10^{5}$ (depends on doping) | Si 1.12 eV, Ge 0.66 eV, GaAs 1.42 eV |
| Insulator | Gap ≳ 4–5 eV | effectively zero | ~$10^{10}$–$10^{18}$ | SiO₂ (~9 eV), glass, PTFE, FR-4 |

**Why the gap matters exponentially.** Intrinsic carrier density is
$$n_i = \sqrt{N_cN_v}\;e^{-E_g/2kT}$$
With $kT = 0.0259$ eV, the factor $e^{-E_g/2kT}$ is about $4\times10^{-10}$ for Si (1.12 eV), but about $10^{-76}$ for SiO₂ (9 eV). A semiconductor is "an insulator with a gap small enough that heat, light and impurities can supply carriers."

**Temperature coefficient (the experimental fingerprint).**
- Metals: $n$ is fixed; more lattice vibration (phonon scattering) lowers $\mu$. $\rho(T) \approx \rho_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]$ with $\alpha_{Cu} \approx 0.0039$/K. **Positive** TC.
- Intrinsic semiconductors: $n_i$ rises exponentially (roughly ×2 per 8–10 K for Si near room temperature), swamping the mobility drop. **Negative** TC (NTC thermistors exploit this).
- Doped semiconductors in the normal "extrinsic" range: carrier density is pinned at the doping level, so resistance rises mildly with T (mobility-dominated) until the material goes intrinsic at high temperature.

**Insulators aren't perfect.** Real ones leak via impurities, surface contamination and moisture, conduct via tunnelling when nanometres thin, and break down when the field exceeds the dielectric strength (~3 MV/m for air at sea level, ~10 MV/cm for good thermal SiO₂).

The boundaries are conventions. Wide-bandgap SiC (3.26 eV) and GaN (3.4 eV) are called semiconductors because they can be doped, even though undoped they behave as insulators.
:::

::: long
"I'd explain all three with one model: energy bands.

When you put atoms together in a crystal, their discrete energy levels broaden into bands. The valence band holds the electrons involved in bonding. The conduction band above it holds electrons that are free to move through the crystal. Between them there may be a band gap, a range of energies where no electron states exist. And for current to flow, you need electrons sitting in a band that has empty states right next to them, so they can pick up a little energy from the field and move.

In a conductor like copper, the top band is only half full, or bands overlap, so there's no gap at all. Every atom contributes roughly one free electron, about $8.5\times10^{22}$ per cm³, and resistivity is around $1.7\ \mu\Omega\cdot$cm.

In an insulator, the valence band is completely full and the next band is a big jump away, around 9 eV for silicon dioxide. Thermal energy at room temperature is only about 26 meV, so the chance of an electron jumping is something like $10^{-76}$. No carriers, no current, until the field gets so high that it breaks down.

A semiconductor has the same structure but a small gap, 1.12 eV for silicon. That's still big compared to $kT$, so pure silicon only has about $10^{10}$ carriers per cm³ and is a pretty poor conductor. What makes it special is control. Add one phosphorus atom per five million silicon atoms and the conductivity goes up by nearly a million times. Shine light, apply a gate voltage, or form a junction, and you can switch it on and off. That's the whole basis of diodes, transistors and solar cells.

The way you'd tell them apart in the lab is temperature. Heat copper and its resistance rises about 0.4% per degree, because the electrons scatter more. Heat intrinsic silicon and its resistance falls fast, because more electrons get across the gap."
:::

::: terms
- **Valence band**: the highest band that is (nearly) full at 0 K; its electrons are tied up in covalent bonds.
- **Conduction band**: the next band up; electrons here are free to move through the crystal.
- **Band gap ($E_g$)**: the energy range with no allowed electron states between valence and conduction bands.
- **Fermi level**: the energy at which a state has a 50% chance of being occupied; it sits inside a band for metals and inside the gap for semiconductors and insulators.
- **Intrinsic carrier concentration ($n_i$)**: the electron (= hole) density in pure semiconductor from thermal generation alone, ≈ $10^{10}$ cm⁻³ in Si at 300 K.
- **Resistivity ($\rho$)**: a material's intrinsic opposition to current, in Ω·m or Ω·cm.
- **Temperature coefficient of resistance**: positive for metals, negative for intrinsic semiconductors.
- **Dielectric strength**: the field at which an insulator breaks down and conducts.
:::

::: exceptional
**Average:** "Conductors let electricity through, insulators don't, semiconductors are in between. Copper, rubber, silicon."

**Exceptional:**
- Frames it as **carrier density** and backs it with $\sigma = q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$.
- Uses the **band-gap picture** with real numbers: Si 1.12 eV, SiO₂ ~9 eV, $kT$ = 26 meV, and explains the exponential $e^{-E_g/2kT}$ dependence.
- Gives the **temperature-coefficient signature** (metals PTC, semiconductors NTC) and why: mobility vs carrier count.
- Says what makes semiconductors *useful*: conductivity is **tunable** by doping, light and fields, which leads straight to junctions and transistors.
- Notes the boundary is fuzzy (SiC/GaN, semi-insulating GaAs) and that real insulators break down and leak.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Semiconductors are materials that conduct half as well."** Their defining feature is controllability, not a middle value. Intrinsic silicon is actually a fairly poor conductor (~$3\times10^{5}$ Ω·cm); doped silicon can reach $10^{-3}$ Ω·cm.
- **"Insulators have no electrons."** They have plenty. They're all locked in a full band with no nearby empty states, so they can't respond to a field.
- **Saying metals conduct because electrons are "loose" in the valence band.** Better: their top band is partially filled, so there are empty states immediately above the occupied ones.
- **Assuming all semiconductors have negative TC.** A doped silicon resistor in the extrinsic range has a *positive* TC because its carrier count is fixed by doping and mobility falls with temperature.
- **Treating the categories as absolute.** Diamond (5.5 eV) is an insulator that can be doped; GaN is a wide-gap semiconductor; graphene is a semimetal with zero gap.
:::

::: deep
## Start from what current needs

Current density in any material is charge × density × velocity: $J = q n v_d$. With drift velocity $v_d = \mu E$, that becomes Ohm's law in its microscopic form:

$$J = \sigma E,\qquad \sigma = q\,(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$$

So a material conducts well if it has **many mobile carriers** ($n$, $p$) and they **move easily** ($\mu$). Mobility varies maybe a factor of 100 across common materials. Carrier density varies by a factor of $10^{30}$ or more. That's the whole classification.

The real question is therefore: *why do some solids have free carriers and others don't?* Band theory answers it.

## Bands and the gap

An isolated atom has sharp energy levels. Bring $N$ atoms together into a crystal and their outer orbitals overlap; quantum mechanics forbids identical states, so each level splits into $N$ closely spaced levels: a **band** ([[q0061]] goes through this in detail). Two bands matter:

- The **valence band**, formed from the bonding orbitals.
- The **conduction band**, formed from the next-higher (antibonding) orbitals.

To carry current, an electron must gain a tiny bit of energy and momentum from the applied field. That's only possible if there are **empty states just above it**. A completely full band carries no net current (every electron moving one way is cancelled by one moving the other way), and a completely empty band has nobody to carry anything.

### Conductor

In copper, each atom contributes one 4s electron to a band that can hold two per atom, so the band is half full. The Fermi level sits inside the band and there's a sea of about $8.5\times10^{22}$ free electrons per cm³. Resistivity is $1.68\times10^{-8}$ Ω·m ($1.68\ \mu\Omega\cdot$cm). Some metals (e.g. Mg, a divalent metal) would have a full band in isolation but their bands overlap, which gives the same effect.

### Insulator

In SiO₂ the valence band is full and the conduction band is about 9 eV above. The thermal energy scale is $kT = 0.0259$ eV at 300 K. The fraction of electrons promoted goes as $e^{-E_g/2kT}$:

$$e^{-9/(2\times0.0259)} \approx 10^{-76}$$

That's zero for any practical purpose. Measured resistivities of good insulators ($10^{14}$–$10^{18}$ Ω·cm) come from impurities, ions and surface contamination, not from band-gap excitation.

### Semiconductor

Same structure, smaller gap. For silicon, $E_g = 1.12$ eV, so $e^{-E_g/2kT} \approx 4\times10^{-10}$. Multiplied by the effective density of states (~$10^{19}$ cm⁻³) that gives $n_i \approx 10^{10}$ cm⁻³. Each promoted electron leaves behind a **hole** in the valence band, which acts as a positive carrier ([[q0069]]). With $\mu_n \approx 1400$ and $\mu_p \approx 450$ cm²/V·s:

$$\rho_i = \frac{1}{q\,n_i(\mu_n + \mu_p)} = \frac{1}{1.6\times10^{-19}\times10^{10}\times1850} \approx 3.4\times10^{5}\ \Omega\cdot\text{cm}$$

So pure silicon is about **$10^{11}$ times more resistive than copper**. It's not a good conductor. What makes it valuable is that this number can be changed.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Quoted values of $n_i$ for Si at 300 K range from about $9.7\times10^{9}$ (modern measurements) to the older textbook $1.5\times10^{10}$ cm⁻³, so intrinsic resistivity is quoted anywhere from about $2.3\times10^5$ to $3.5\times10^5$ Ω·cm. The order of magnitude is what matters in an interview.

## Why semiconductors matter: controllability

Add phosphorus at $10^{16}$ cm⁻³ (one atom per five million silicon atoms; Si has $5\times10^{22}$ atoms/cm³). Each phosphorus donates one electron ([[q0065]]), so $n \approx 10^{16}$ and

$$\rho \approx \frac{1}{1.6\times10^{-19}\times10^{16}\times1200} \approx 0.5\ \Omega\cdot\text{cm}$$

That's nearly a **million times** lower than intrinsic silicon, set by a recipe you control. Doping one region with donors and the neighbouring region with acceptors gives a PN junction ([[q0063]]), and a gate field can create or destroy a conducting channel (MOSFET). Light with photon energy above $E_g$ creates electron-hole pairs (photodiodes, solar cells, [[q0057]]). None of this works in a metal (carrier density is too high to modulate) or an insulator (carriers too hard to create).

This simple model ignores the mobility drop and the small shrinkage of $E_g$ with temperature, but the shape is right.

The physics behind each curve:

- **Metals**: carrier density is fixed. Hotter lattice → more phonon scattering → lower mobility → higher $\rho$. $\alpha_{Cu} \approx 0.39\%$/K, which is why a copper trace's resistance (and a current-sense trace's reading) drifts with temperature.
- **Intrinsic semiconductors**: $n_i \propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/2kT}$ rises about 8% per kelvin for Si near room temperature, swamping the mobility loss. NTC thermistors (usually doped metal oxides) use exactly this behaviour.
- **Doped semiconductors**: in the useful range the carrier count is pinned by the dopants, so they actually show a mild *positive* TC until temperature gets high enough that $n_i$ approaches the doping level. For silicon doped at $10^{15}$ that's around 550–600 K, which is part of why silicon electronics stop working much above ~150–200 °C junction temperature and why wide-gap SiC and GaN are attractive for hot environments.

## Real insulators: leakage and breakdown

Insulators in hardware are never perfect, and knowing how they fail is a good follow-up answer:

- **Leakage**: surface contamination, flux residue and moisture conduct far better than the bulk. That's why high-impedance nodes (>10 MΩ) need clean boards and guard rings.
- **Tunnelling**: once an oxide is a few nanometres thick, electrons quantum-tunnel straight through. Gate leakage in thin-oxide CMOS led to high-κ dielectrics.
- **Breakdown**: above the dielectric strength, a few free carriers accelerate enough to knock out more (avalanche) and the insulator conducts. Air breaks down near 3 MV/m (3 kV/mm) at sea level in a uniform field; thermal SiO₂ holds roughly 10 MV/cm. Lightning ([[q0060]]) is air breakdown at enormous scale.
- **Dielectric loss**: an insulator in an AC field still dissipates energy through polarization lag ([[q0058]]).

## Edge cases worth knowing

- **Semimetals** (graphite, bismuth) have a tiny band overlap: low carrier density for a metal, but no gap.
- **Wide-bandgap semiconductors** (SiC 3.26 eV, GaN 3.4 eV, diamond 5.5 eV) are insulators when pure but can be doped, so we call them semiconductors.
- **Semi-insulating GaAs** is deliberately compensated to ~$10^{7}$–$10^{9}$ Ω·cm for RF substrates.
- **Ionic conductors** (electrolytes) conduct by moving ions, not electrons. They follow different rules.
:::

::: applications
- **Copper and aluminium conductors**: PCB traces, bus bars, cables. Copper's +0.39%/°C TC matters for current sensing with copper traces and for motor-winding temperature estimation.
- **Insulators as dielectrics**: FR-4 PCB substrate, SiO₂ and high-κ gate dielectrics in MOSFETs, polypropylene film and ceramic in capacitors, PTFE in RF cables.
- **Doped silicon devices**: diodes, BJTs, MOSFETs and integrated resistors (polysilicon and diffused resistors) all depend on controlling silicon's conductivity.
- **NTC thermistors**: semiconducting ceramics whose resistance drops sharply with temperature, used for inrush limiting and temperature sensing.
- **Wide-gap power devices**: SiC MOSFETs and GaN HEMTs in EV inverters and fast chargers exploit the larger gap for high breakdown field and high-temperature operation.
- **Photodetectors and solar cells**: only possible because a semiconductor's gap matches photon energies (Si absorbs below about 1.1 µm).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Room temperature (≈300 K).** At cryogenic temperatures semiconductors "freeze out" and become insulating; at very high temperatures wide-gap insulators can start to conduct.
- **Pure, crystalline materials** for the band picture. Amorphous materials (glass, a-Si) have band tails and hopping conduction, but the gap idea still holds.
- **Electronic conduction.** Ionic conduction (electrolytes, some glasses when hot) follows different rules.
- **The gap thresholds are conventions**, not physics constants. Some texts call anything above ~3 eV an insulator; others call SiC and GaN semiconductors because they can be doped. Both are acceptable if you explain the criterion.
- **Low field.** At high enough field any insulator breaks down, and in thin films tunnelling dominates.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a metal's resistance increase with temperature but a semiconductor's decreases?
In a metal the number of free electrons is fixed (~one per atom), so the only thing temperature changes is scattering: more lattice vibration means lower mobility, so resistance rises roughly linearly (~0.39%/K for copper). In an intrinsic semiconductor, temperature also creates new carriers across the gap, $n_i \propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/2kT}$, which grows about 8% per kelvin for Si. That exponential increase dominates the mobility loss, so resistance falls.

### Is pure silicon a good conductor?
No. Intrinsic silicon has about $10^{10}$ carriers per cm³ versus copper's $8.5\times10^{22}$, giving roughly $3\times10^5$ Ω·cm compared to $1.7\times10^{-6}$ Ω·cm. That's about eleven orders of magnitude worse. Silicon is useful because doping can lower its resistivity to the 0.001–10 Ω·cm range on demand and because junctions and gate fields can switch it.

### What is the band gap of silicon and why does it matter for electronics?
About 1.12 eV at 300 K. It sets $n_i$ (and so leakage currents, which roughly double every ~10 °C), the maximum practical junction temperature (~150–175 °C for most parts), the longest wavelength silicon can detect (1240/1.12 ≈ 1.1 µm), and indirectly the ~0.6–0.7 V forward drop of a silicon diode.

### Why are SiC and GaN called semiconductors if their gaps are above 3 eV?
Because they can be doped n-type and p-type (at least n-type easily) to controlled conductivity, which is the practical definition. The wide gap is a feature: critical breakdown field scales strongly with gap (roughly 10× higher than Si for SiC), so you can block the same voltage with a much thinner, more heavily doped drift layer and lower on-resistance, and they keep working at higher temperatures.

### What if you keep increasing the voltage across an insulator?
Eventually you reach the dielectric strength. The few free carriers present get accelerated enough between collisions to ionize atoms, creating more carriers (avalanche), and the material breaks down, often permanently for solids (a carbonized track or punched-through oxide). Air at sea level breaks down near 3 kV/mm in a uniform field, lower around sharp points. This is why PCBs have creepage/clearance rules and why MOSFET gates have a max $V_{GS}$ of typically ±20 V.
:::
