::: basics
### In plain words
The DC gain of an op-amp is how much it amplifies very slow signals. Open loop, it is huge: from about 100,000 to a million. But in a real circuit, what matters is the closed-loop gain, which feedback sets with resistors. The op-amp's own gain just has to be big enough to make the error small.

### Picture it
Imagine a very strong spring scale that you use with a small correction dial. The scale's raw strength is huge and sloppy. But because you keep nudging the dial until the reading matches, the final answer depends on the dial, not the scale. The bigger the raw strength, the smaller the leftover error.

### Words you need
- **Open-loop DC gain ($A_0$)**: the gain with no feedback at zero frequency. See [[q0121]].
- **Closed-loop gain**: the gain set by the feedback network.
- **Loop gain**: the open-loop gain divided by the closed-loop gain. It sets the error.
- **Dominant pole**: the low frequency where the gain starts to fall. See [[q0116]].
- **dB**: 20 times the log of the gain ratio. 100,000 is 100 dB.

### The simplest example
Take $A_0=100{,}000$ and a closed-loop gain of 10. The loop gain is $100{,}000/10=10{,}000$, so the gain error is about 1/10,000, or 0.01%. For a closed-loop gain of 1000 the loop gain is only 100, so the error is about 1%. Also, a 1 MHz GBW with this $A_0$ means the gain starts falling at $1\text{ MHz}/100{,}000=10$ Hz.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows how this error arises, and why the gain only holds at low frequency.
:::

::: testing
Whether you separate three things that beginners blur together:

1. The **open-loop DC gain** $A_0$ (also $A_{VOL}$ or large-signal voltage gain), a huge but loosely specified number, typically $10^5$ to $10^6$, i.e. 100 to 120 dB for general-purpose parts.
2. The **closed-loop DC gain** you actually design, set by resistors and approximately $1/\beta$.
3. How **gain falls with frequency** (the dominant pole at $f_p = GBW/A_0$) so that "DC gain" is only the low-frequency plateau.

They also want to hear the dB-to-ratio conversion and the point that the *exact* $A_0$ barely matters if loop gain is high.
:::

::: why
Every error budget in a precision analog design includes finite loop gain. If you amplify a thermocouple signal by 1000 with an op-amp whose $A_0$ is "only" $10^5$, the closed-loop gain is off by about 1%. Knowing $A_0$ tells you how large a closed-loop gain you can build before accuracy suffers, and it tells you at what frequency the loop gain drops so that feedback stops helping. It also explains why DC-accurate integrators and high-gain DC amplifiers are limited by the op-amp itself, and why zero-drift parts, which have extra-high $A_0$, exist.
:::

::: concepts
- **Open-loop gain $A_0$**: gain at DC with no feedback; $V_{out}/V_d$ at low frequency. See [[q0121]].
- **Decibels**: $A_{dB} = 20\log_{10}A$; 100 dB is $10^5$.
- **Dominant pole**: the low-frequency pole that makes gain fall at 20 dB/decade. See [[q0116]].
- **Loop gain $A\beta$**: sets closed-loop accuracy; error $\approx 1/(1+A\beta)$. See [[q0124]].
- **Closed-loop gain**: $A/(1+A\beta)\to 1/\beta$. See [[q0125]].
- **Bode plot**: gain magnitude vs. log frequency. See [[q0010]].
:::

::: answer
"Open loop, the DC gain of a typical op-amp is huge: somewhere between a hundred thousand and a million, so 100 to 120 dB. It's not a tightly controlled number. It changes with temperature, with the load, and part to part, and datasheets usually give a minimum and a typical.

But in a circuit, what matters is the closed-loop DC gain, which feedback sets. In a non-inverting amplifier it's about one plus $R_f$ over $R_g$, and the op-amp's own gain only shows up as a small error: roughly one over the loop gain. With $A_0 = 10^5$ and a gain of 10 the error is about 0.01%. For a gain of 1000 it's about 1%.

The 'DC' part is important, because that gain only holds at low frequency. The op-amp has a dominant pole, often just a few hertz to a few hundred hertz, after which gain falls at 20 dB per decade until it hits unity at the gain-bandwidth product. So a part with a 1 MHz GBW and $10^5$ DC gain has its pole at 10 hertz. If the interviewer means the ideal op-amp, the DC gain is infinite."
:::

::: technical
**Open-loop DC gain.** $A_0 = \left.\frac{V_{out}}{V_+ - V_-}\right|_{f\to 0}$. Typical orders of magnitude:
- general-purpose bipolar/CMOS: $10^5$ to $10^6$ (100 to 120 dB);
- precision and zero-drift amplifiers: higher, often 130 dB or more (vendor-dependent);
- very fast or very low-power parts: lower, tens of dB up to ~100 dB.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $A_0$ varies by device family, load resistance, output level and temperature, and datasheets often quote only a minimum and a typical value. Use the datasheet "large-signal voltage gain" ($A_{VOL}$) with its stated load.

**Frequency dependence.** A single dominant pole: $A(f) = \frac{A_0}{1+jf/f_p}$, with $f_p = GBW/A_0$. For $A_0 = 10^5$ and GBW = 1 MHz, $f_p = 10$ Hz. Above $f_p$, $|A|\approx GBW/f$.

**Closed-loop DC gain with finite $A_0$.**
$$A_{CL} = \frac{A_0}{1+A_0\beta} = \frac{1}{\beta}\cdot\frac{1}{1+\frac{1}{A_0\beta}}\approx\frac{1}{\beta}\left(1-\frac{1}{A_0\beta}\right)$$
Relative gain error $\approx 1/(A_0\beta) = G/A_0$, where $G = 1/\beta$ is the nominal gain. Example, $A_0 = 10^5$: $G = 10\to 0.01\%$, $G = 100\to 0.1\%$, $G = 1000\to 1\%$.

**Non-ideal consequences.** The gain is also *nonlinear*: $A_{VOL}$ falls near the output rails and varies with load, which makes a small distortion term, reduced by loop gain. Input offset voltage, not gain error, usually dominates DC accuracy in high-gain circuits.
:::

::: short
"Open loop, an op-amp's DC gain is very large, typically $10^5$ to $10^6$, or 100 to 120 dB, though it's loosely specified. In a circuit what matters is the closed-loop gain, set by the feedback resistors: $1/\beta$, such as $1 + R_f/R_g$. The finite open-loop gain only adds a small error of about $G/A_0$, so a gain of 10 on a $10^5$ part is off by 0.01%. And that high gain only holds at low frequency: a dominant pole rolls it off at 20 dB per decade down to unity at the GBW."
:::

::: long
"I'd split the answer in two, open-loop and closed-loop, because 'DC gain of an op-amp' can mean either.

Open loop: the DC gain is the gain at very low frequency, with no feedback. For a typical general-purpose op-amp it's on the order of a hundred thousand to a million, 100 to 120 dB, and for precision or zero-drift parts it can be higher. The datasheet calls it large-signal voltage gain, or $A_{VOL}$, and it's specified at a particular load. It isn't a tight number: it drifts with temperature, with output level and from unit to unit, which is exactly why we never use an op-amp open loop as an amplifier.

Closed loop: with negative feedback, the DC gain is $A_0/(1 + A_0\beta)$, which is about $1/\beta$ when the loop gain is large. So for a non-inverting stage it's $1 + R_f/R_g$. The finite $A_0$ shows up as a relative error of roughly the closed-loop gain over $A_0$. With $A_0 = 10^5$, a gain of 10 gives 0.01% error, a gain of 100 gives 0.1%, a gain of 1000 gives 1%. So if I need a gain of 1000 and 0.1% accuracy, I either need a higher-$A_0$ part or I split the gain into two stages.

And it's called 'DC' gain for a reason. The op-amp has a dominant pole, so the open-loop gain starts falling at something like 10 Hz for a 1 MHz GBW part with $10^5$ DC gain, then falls 20 dB per decade until it reaches unity at the GBW. At frequencies above the pole, the loop gain is lower, so the closed-loop accuracy gets worse, which is why precision at 100 kHz is harder than precision at DC. In the ideal op-amp model, the DC gain is simply infinite."
:::

::: terms
- **Open-loop DC gain ($A_0$, $A_{VOL}$)**: low-frequency gain with no feedback.
- **dB**: $20\log_{10}$ of a voltage ratio; 60 dB = 1000, 100 dB = $10^5$, 120 dB = $10^6$.
- **Closed-loop gain**: gain with feedback, about $1/\beta$.
- **Loop gain ($A\beta$)**: product of op-amp gain and feedback factor.
- **Dominant pole ($f_p$)**: the lowest-frequency pole, where gain falls to $0.707A_0$.
- **Gain-bandwidth product (GBW)**: $A_0 f_p$; the unity-gain frequency of a single-pole amp.
- **Gain error**: deviation of the closed-loop gain from $1/\beta$, about $1/(A\beta)$.
- **Zero-drift (chopper) amplifier**: a topology with very high DC gain and tiny offset.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "very large, like 100 dB" or "infinite".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives a **typical range with units** (100 to 120 dB, $10^5$ to $10^6$) and says it is loosely specified and load/temperature dependent.
- **Distinguishes open-loop from closed-loop DC gain** and says which one the circuit really depends on.
- Computes the **error** from finite gain ($G/A_0$) with a number, and uses it to decide when two stages are needed.
- Mentions the **dominant pole** and that $f_p = GBW/A_0$, so "DC gain" is only valid below it.
- Notes that in practice **offset and bias current** dominate DC accuracy before finite gain does for most low-gain circuits.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying the gain is infinite for a real part.** For the *ideal* model yes; a real op-amp has a finite, loosely specified $A_0$.
- **Quoting a single number as if it were exact.** The gain varies by 2× or more across units and conditions, and the datasheet gives min/typ.
- **Confusing open-loop and closed-loop gain.** The closed-loop gain is the one you set with resistors.
- **Assuming the open-loop gain is flat over frequency.** It starts to fall at a few Hz to a few hundred Hz.
- **Mixing dB and V/V.** 100 dB is $10^5$, not $100\times$ anything. Remember 20 dB per factor of 10.
- **Ignoring the load.** $A_{VOL}$ is specified for a given load (such as 2 kΩ or 10 kΩ). With a heavy load the gain drops.
:::

::: deep
## What sets the number

In a two-stage op-amp, DC gain is the product of the stage gains: $A_0 = (g_{m1}R_{1})(g_{m2}R_{2})$, where $R_1$ and $R_2$ are the output resistances at the first and second stage nodes. Each stage gives gain of order tens to a few hundred, limited by transistor output resistance (Early voltage or channel-length modulation). Two stages of a few hundred each give ~$10^5$, and cascodes or a third stage push it higher. That is why $A_0$ is large but uncontrolled: it depends on output resistances (Early voltage, channel-length modulation) that vary with process and temperature and load.

More stages (three-stage amplifiers) or cascoding give more gain, at the cost of compensation complexity. Zero-drift/chopper-stabilized parts use auto-zeroing, which effectively gives the main amplifier a huge DC gain on top of the nulled offset.

The takeaway: the number that determines accuracy at your signal frequency is $|A(f)| = GBW/f$ (for $f\gg f_p$), not $A_0$. At 1 kHz, a 1 MHz GBW amplifier has an open-loop gain of only 1000 (60 dB), regardless of whether $A_0$ is 100 dB or 120 dB.

## Closed-loop accuracy from $A_0$

For a non-inverting stage with nominal gain $G = 1/\beta$:

| Nominal gain $G$ | Loop gain $A_0\beta$ ($A_0 = 10^5$) | Actual gain | Error |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100,000 | 1.0000 | 0.0010% |
| 10 | 10,000 | 9.9990 | 0.010% |
| 100 | 1,000 | 99.900 | 0.10% |
| 1,000 | 100 | 990.10 | 0.99% |
| 10,000 | 10 | 9,090.9 | 9.1% |

This is the practical lesson. Beyond a closed-loop gain of about $A_0/100$, error rises quickly, and by $G = A_0/10$ you're at ~10%. In high-gain applications (a thermocouple, a strain-gauge bridge, a current-sense amplifier) you either use two stages of lower gain, or a part with higher $A_0$.

At frequency, replace $A_0$ with $A(f)\approx GBW/(jf)$. For $G = 10$, GBW = 1 MHz and $f = 10$ kHz, the loop-gain magnitude is only 10. Because that loop gain is at −90° rather than real, the magnitude error is smaller than the DC formula suggests ($|A_{CL}| = G\,|T|/|1+T| = 10\times10/\sqrt{101}\approx 9.95$, about 0.5% low), but there is already about 6° of phase shift, and the error grows quickly as $f$ approaches the 100 kHz closed-loop bandwidth.

## Why $A_0$ is not tightly specified

The datasheet's $A_{VOL}$ is a minimum, and the typical value is often 10 to 20 dB higher. Because it is a ratio of huge to tiny quantities, it is hard to test and trim and no one pays for precision there. Feedback means you don't need to. A design is correct if it works with the *minimum* spec $A_0$.

## A special case: integrators

An ideal integrator (no resistor across the capacitor) has infinite DC gain, but an op-amp can only supply $A_0$. In practice a real integrator will ramp to a rail from the op-amp's input offset: a 1 mV offset with $R = 10$ kΩ and $C = 1$ µF gives a slope of 0.1 V/s. That is why practical integrators add a feedback resistor in parallel with $C$, giving a finite DC gain $-R_f/R$ and a corner at $1/(2\pi R_f C)$.

## Worked example: estimating $A_0$ from device physics

It is worth being able to derive why $10^5$ to $10^6$ is the natural ballpark, not just quote it. Take a bipolar differential pair with a current-mirror active load. The small-signal gain of that stage is $g_m$ times the parallel output resistances of the NPN and the PNP mirror transistor. With collector current $I_C$ per side, $g_m=I_C/V_T$ and each device has $r_o=V_A/I_C$, where $V_A$ is the Early voltage. Then

$$A_{stage}=g_m\,(r_{o,n}\parallel r_{o,p})=\frac{I_C}{V_T}\cdot\frac{V_A}{2I_C}=\frac{V_A}{2V_T}$$

The bias current cancels, which is why this gain is so reproducible in form even though it is not in value. With $V_T=25.85$ mV (room temperature) and an assumed $V_A=50$ V, one stage gives $50/(2\times0.02585)\approx967$. A second stage built the same way, if it does not load the first (the second stage input must be a follower or a MOS gate, otherwise $r_\pi$ cuts the first stage's gain), gives another 967, and the total is $967^2\approx9.4\times10^5$, or 119 dB. With $V_A=100$ V it would be $3.7\times10^6$ (131 dB). So the whole range from $10^5$ to a few million comes from two stages, each limited by the transistor's intrinsic gain $V_A/V_T$.

Temperature enters directly through $V_T=kT/q$. Warming from 25 °C to 85 °C raises $V_T$ by 20%, and the gain of each stage falls as $1/T$. Over that range the factor per stage is $298.15/358.15=0.83$, so the two-stage product changes by $0.83^2=0.69$, or about $-3.2$ dB, before counting any change in $V_A$ and load.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Real op-amps add gain boosting, cascodes, buffers and loading from the output stage, and $V_A$ for a given process varies with the device geometry and with bias. Treat the numbers above as an estimate of the ballpark, not of any specific part.

## Limiting cases of the closed-loop error

- **$G\ll A_0$.** The error is $G/A_0$ and is negligible compared with resistor tolerance. For $G=10$ and $A_0=10^5$ the error is 0.01% against 1% resistors.
- **$G\approx A_0$.** The loop gain approaches 1; the closed-loop DC gain is only half the nominal value ($A_0/2$ when $G=A_0$), and feedback no longer defines the behavior.
- **$G=1$ (follower).** Error $=1/(1+A_0)$, about 10 ppm. The follower is the most accurate configuration, and an LDO's DC regulation error comes from its loop gain in exactly the same way.
- **Load dependence.** $A_{VOL}$ is specified into a stated load; a heavier load lowers $r_o$ at the output node and therefore $A_0$. The change shows up as a gain error that depends on output current, a signature of finite $A_0$ rather than of resistor error.

## Measuring $A_0$ on the bench

You can extract $A_0$ from a closed-loop measurement because the error is directly related to it. Build a non-inverting stage with a nominal gain of 1000 (for instance 100 Ω and 99.9 kΩ), apply a small DC input such as 1 mV, and measure the output with a 5.5 digit meter. Do the measurement at two input levels (for example 1 mV and 2 mV) and compute the slope $\Delta V_{out}/\Delta V_{in}$, which cancels the offset voltage. If the slope is 990.1 and $\beta=1/1000$, then $A_0=A_{CL}/(1-A_{CL}\beta)=990.1/(1-0.9901)\approx1.0\times10^5$.

The practical catch is that the resistor ratio must be known much better than the effect you are measuring. Even 0.1% resistors leave roughly 0.1-0.2% uncertainty in $\beta$, which turns the 1% finite-gain effect into a 10-20% uncertainty in $A_0$, so use 0.01% resistors or measure the divider ratio first. Also keep the output well inside the rails, because $A_{VOL}$ drops near the swing limits, and let the part warm up, since self-heating shifts the offset during the measurement.

:::

::: applications
- **Precision DC amplifiers** (thermocouples, load cells, shunt monitors): the loop gain at DC determines gain error. Zero-drift parts help.
- **Voltage references and LDO error amplifiers**: DC gain of the error amplifier sets line and load regulation. A regulator with 80 dB loop gain regulates to 0.01%.
- **Active filters and integrators**: low-frequency behavior (and the integrator's DC saturation) depends on $A_0$.
- **Sample-and-hold, ADC drivers**: settling to 16-bit accuracy needs loop gain of at least $2^{16}\approx 65{,}000$ (96 dB) at the signal frequency.
- **Comparators**: the same gain at DC sets the minimum input overdrive for a given output swing, see [[q0127]].
:::

::: assumptions
I treated a single-pole voltage-feedback op-amp driving a nominal load. Multi-stage and multi-pole amplifiers (for example three-stage parts or those with extra poles) have a more complex roll-off, though the DC plateau idea is the same. For **current-feedback** amplifiers, "DC gain" in the voltage sense is defined differently: the open-loop quantity is a transimpedance. In an **ideal op-amp** problem, the DC gain is infinite and there is no frequency dependence. If asked about the *closed-loop* DC gain of a specific circuit, it is $1+R_f/R_g$ or $-R_f/R_{in}$ with a small correction. For capacitors in the circuit (integrators, high-pass networks), DC means "capacitors open".

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> I did not give a number for the highest $A_0$ available. Zero-drift parts quote DC gains above 130 dB, but exact figures differ by product and test condition; check the datasheet.
:::

::: followups
### What's the DC gain in dB if $A_0 = 2\times10^5$?
$20\log_{10}(2\times10^5) = 20\times5.301 = 106$ dB. Each doubling adds 6 dB: $10^5$ is 100 dB, so $2\times10^5$ is 106 dB.

### If an op-amp has $A_0 = 100$ dB and GBW = 2 MHz, where is the dominant pole?
$A_0 = 10^5$, so $f_p = GBW/A_0 = 2\times10^6/10^5 = 20$ Hz. Above 20 Hz the gain falls at 20 dB per decade.

### How much gain error do I get at DC for a non-inverting gain of 200 with $A_0 = 10^5$?
Loop gain is $10^5/200 = 500$. The closed-loop gain is $200/(1 + 200/10^5) = 199.6$, an error of about 0.2%. If I need 0.05%, I'd need $A_0\ge 4\times10^5$ or two stages of gain ~14 each.

### Why does the datasheet give minimum and typical open-loop gain, not a precise value?
Because it depends on transistor output resistances, process, temperature, and load, and no one needs it to be accurate: feedback makes the circuit insensitive to it. Designs should be checked against the minimum.

### Harder: your gain-of-10 amplifier has an excellent DC gain error but is about 14% low at 60 kHz. Why?
Loop gain falls with frequency: $|A(f)|\approx GBW/f$. For GBW = 1 MHz at 60 kHz, $|A|\approx 16.7$, so with a gain of 10 the loop gain $T$ is only about 1.7 (at −90°). Then $|A_{CL}| = 10\times1.67/\sqrt{1+1.67^2}\approx 8.6$, about 14% low, with roughly 31° of phase shift: the signal is close to the 100 kHz closed-loop bandwidth. DC gain was never the limit; the *loop gain at the signal frequency* is. The fix is a higher-GBW op-amp or lower closed-loop gain per stage.
