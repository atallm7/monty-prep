::: testing
Whether you understand a plane as a *conductor with width*, not a magic net, and can say why that matters for resistance, inductance and heat. The interviewer is checking the definition (a plane is a whole layer of copper; a shape or pour is an area of copper on a layer with other routing), the two or three physical reasons planes beat traces (low resistance, low inductance, spreading heat), and whether you also know the catches: plane splits, slots, islands, and where a shape is the wrong tool (a switching node, a sense line).
:::

::: why
Almost every real board uses planes: ground, a few power rails, sometimes both on dedicated layers. Getting comfortable with *why* tells you where to put the cuts, how to connect a pour, and why a board with a broken ground plane misbehaves in EMC testing. It is also the quickest way to make a high-current path or a power-delivery network (PDN) meet its drop and impedance targets without fighting the router. Interviewers ask it to see whether you think of layout as physics (squares, loop area, return paths) rather than rules you memorised.
:::

::: concepts
- **Sheet resistance**: a plane is a very wide resistor; resistance is set by squares, not by length alone. See [[q0534]].
- **Return current and loop area**: high-frequency current returns directly beneath its signal or supply, and the closer the plane the smaller the loop. See [[q0486]].
- **Parallel-plate capacitance**: two adjacent plane layers form a distributed capacitor that helps decoupling at very high frequency.
- **Inductance per square**: a plane pair of separation $h$ has sheet inductance $\mu_0 h$, which is tiny for thin dielectrics.
- **Thermal spreading**: copper conducts heat well, and a plane spreads it in two dimensions.
- **Stack-up**: which layers are planes and why. See [[q0480]].
:::

::: answer
"A power plane is a whole layer of the PCB that's solid copper tied to one net, like ground or 3.3 V. A shape, or a pour or polygon, is the same thing but only over part of a layer, where it shares space with routing. Either way, you're making the supply a big sheet of copper instead of a skinny wire.

They're preferred because current in a sheet spreads out in two dimensions. A trace is a resistor with a length-to-width ratio, so a 1 mm trace over 25 mm is 25 squares, about 12 milliohms for one ounce copper. A plane spreading out from a via is more like a fraction of a milliohm because the current fans out and the resistance only grows with the log of the distance. That means less voltage drop and less heating.

It's also much lower inductance. The plane sits right above its return plane, so the loop area is tiny, and the two planes also form a small distributed capacitor that helps decoupling at high frequency. And you can reach it from anywhere with a via, which makes routing simpler, plus it spreads heat.

The things I watch for are splits and slots in the plane that force return current around, isolated islands, and not using a big shape on a node that switches or is sensitive, because a big copper area there is an antenna or a noise coupler."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.** A *plane* is a dedicated layer, mostly or fully continuous copper, assigned to one net (for example the L2 ground plane and L3 3V3 plane). A *shape* (also called pour, polygon, zone) is a copper region on a routing layer assigned to a net; it flows around other nets and can be solid or hatched.

**Resistance.** For a square sheet, $R_\square = \rho/t$ = 0.48 mΩ for 1 oz copper. A trace of $n$ squares has $nR_\square$. Current injected through a via of radius $r_1$ and collected at radius $r_2$ in an infinite sheet sees

$$R = \frac{R_\square}{2\pi}\ln\frac{r_2}{r_1}$$

For $r_1 = 0.5$ mm and $r_2 = 25$ mm: $0.48/(2\pi)\cdot\ln 50 = 0.30$ mΩ. The same 25 mm in a 1 mm trace is 12 mΩ, forty times more.

**Inductance and capacitance.** Two planes separated by $h$ have a sheet inductance $\mu_0 h$: 0.13 nH per square at $h = 0.1$ mm, versus 1.3 nH per square at $h = 1$ mm. The plane-pair capacitance is $C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/h$; a 100 mm × 100 mm pair separated by 0.1 mm of FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r\approx4.3$) is about 3.8 nF.

**Other benefits.** Heat spreading, lower impedance for the return of every signal above it, shielding between layers, and uniform copper balance for fabrication.

**Costs and cautions.** Slots and splits that cross return paths, isolated islands that radiate, plane resonances in large boards, thermal relief on through-hole connections, and clearance cutouts around every via which can themselves perforate a plane into a slotted "Swiss cheese" if vias are packed too tightly.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Plane-pair capacitance depends on laminate and prepreg thickness and the laminate $\varepsilon_r$ varies by vendor and frequency (roughly 4.0 to 4.7 for FR-4). Use your fabricator's stack-up values.
:::

"A power plane is a whole layer of the board that's solid copper tied to one net, like ground or a supply rail. A shape, also called a pour or a polygon, is the same idea on a layer that also carries routing, so it fills the free area instead of the whole layer.

The main reason to use them is resistance. A trace is a resistor whose value is its length divided by its width in squares. One ounce copper is about half a milliohm per square, so a 1 mm trace that's 25 mm long is 25 squares, about 12 milliohms. If I instead inject the current through a via into a big sheet, the current fans out, and the resistance only goes up with the natural log of the distance. From a half-millimetre via to 25 mm away that's about 0.3 milliohm, around forty times lower. That means less drop, less heat and more current for a given board.

The second reason is inductance. A plane sits one thin dielectric away from its return plane, so loop area is tiny, and the sheet inductance is just mu-zero times the separation, about 0.13 nanohenry per square for a tenth of a millimetre. Two adjacent planes also form a capacitor, a few nanofarads on a 100 by 100 millimetre board, which helps at high frequency. A plane is also a great heat spreader and you can reach it anywhere with a via, which keeps routing simple.

The things I'd watch are slots and splits that force return current to detour, islands that float, and using a big pour on the wrong node. A switching node or a sensitive feedback line should not be a big copper area."
:::

::: terms
- **Plane**: a layer of copper (mostly solid) dedicated to a net, such as GND or 3V3.
- **Shape / pour / polygon / zone**: a copper area on a layer assigned to a net; fills the space around other routing.
- **Sheet resistance ($R_\square$)**: resistance of a square of the conductor; $\rho/t$.
- **Spreading resistance**: resistance from a point contact into a sheet, growing as $\ln(r_2/r_1)$.
- **Split plane**: a plane divided into separate regions, usually for different supplies or grounds.
- **Island**: a copper region not connected to any net or with only a single thin connection; can act as an antenna.
- **Clearance / anti-pad**: the copper-free ring around a via that is not connected to a plane.
- **Copper balance**: roughly equal copper area on each layer to keep the board flat through fabrication.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a plane is a big sheet of copper with lower resistance than a trace."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Distinguishes a plane (layer) from a shape (pour) and says which they would use.
- Gives numbers: 0.48 mΩ/sq, 25 squares vs a spreading resistance of a fraction of a milliohm.
- Explains the *log* spreading behaviour, which is why bigger is better but with diminishing returns.
- Mentions inductance ($\mu_0 h$) and plane-pair capacitance, not just DC resistance.
- Names the failure modes: splits, slots, islands, via-perforated planes.
- Says where a shape is the wrong answer (switch node, sense lines), showing judgement.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking a plane is "just a big trace".** The current spreads in two dimensions, so the maths (and the behaviour) is different; resistance is logarithmic in distance, not linear.
- **Only mentioning DC resistance.** The inductance and capacitance benefits are as important for fast loads.
- **Cutting a slot under a signal or supply.** The return current must detour around the slot, enlarging the loop and radiating. See [[q0497]].
- **Leaving floating copper islands.** They couple capacitively and can radiate; tie them to a net or remove them.
- **Making the switch node a big pour.** Large area on a high dV/dt node couples noise. Use the smallest area that carries the current. See [[q0550]].
- **Packing too many vias in a row.** The anti-pads can chop the plane into a slot-like perforation that raises resistance and inductance.
:::

::: deep
## What a plane is, physically

Imagine laying a 1 oz copper layer across the whole board. The copper is 35 µm thick and a few hundred mm on a side, so its aspect ratio is extreme. Electrically that is a **sheet conductor**: it has resistance per square ($R_\square$), and any two points on it are connected by an enormous number of parallel paths.

The practical result is that current does not take one path; it takes all of them, in proportion to their conductance. Where current enters and leaves at small points (vias or pads) it crowds near those points, and the resistance of the plane is dominated by the region close to the contacts.

## The logarithmic resistance

Treat the sheet as infinite and inject current at a small circular contact of radius $r_1$, collecting it at radius $r_2$. By symmetry the current spreads radially. The current density at radius $r$ is $J = I/(2\pi r t)$, the field is $E = \rho J$ and integrating gives

$$R = \frac{\rho}{2\pi t}\ln\frac{r_2}{r_1} = \frac{R_\square}{2\pi}\ln\frac{r_2}{r_1}$$

So each doubling of distance adds only $R_\square\ln2/(2\pi) = 53$ µΩ for 1 oz copper. A trace instead adds 0.48 mΩ for every extra millimetre of its 1 mm width. That is why a plane scales so well.

## Inductance and capacitance

A fast load needs the PDN to have low impedance up to hundreds of MHz. A plane pair behaves like a parallel-plate transmission structure whose inductance per square is

$$L_\square = \mu_0 h$$

with $h$ the dielectric thickness. At $h = 0.1$ mm that is 0.126 nH/sq; at 1.0 mm it is 1.26 nH/sq. A trace of the same length might add 1 nH per mm. The planes also give you capacitance $C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/h$: about 3.8 nF for a 100 mm × 100 mm pair at 0.1 mm, or 1.9 nF at 0.2 mm. That is small compared with the discrete decoupling capacitors, but it is nearly inductance-free, so it helps above about 100 MHz where discrete capacitors are limited by their mounting inductance ([[q0509]]).

The practical lesson for stack-ups: put the power plane next to its ground plane with a thin dielectric, as in [[q0480]]. That gives you the low inductance and the capacitance, and it makes a near-ideal return for signals above or below.

## Heat

Copper conducts heat at about 385 W/(m·K), a hundred times better than FR-4's 0.3 W/(m·K) through-thickness. A plane spreads the heat from a hot component laterally, lowering its junction temperature. That is why power parts have exposed pads soldered to large pours. See [[q0540]].

## Shapes (pours) on routing layers

On a routing layer you usually cannot afford a full plane, so you pour copper into whatever space remains, or draw a polygon for a specific supply (a "power shape" feeding a regulator output or a motor driver). Practical rules:

- Connect pads directly or with generous thermal relief ([[q0538]]).
- Keep shape width at necks as wide as the current requires; a pour that necks to 0.5 mm between two parts is a trace.
- Stitch the pour to the same-net layers with via arrays ([[q0539]]).
- Remove dead copper islands and thin slivers, which the fab can etch away in unpredictable ways.
- Do not pour under or around a switching node or a sensitive analog input beyond what is needed.

## Where planes go wrong

**Splits and slots.** At high frequency return current flows directly under the signal. If a plane is split, the return must go around, enlarging the loop and radiating. Leave the plane intact under every fast signal and supply ([[q0497]]). If you must cut, bridge it with a capacitor or route the signal elsewhere.

**Via perforation.** Every via passing through the plane has a clearance ring (anti-pad). A tight row of vias can merge those rings into a slot. Stagger via rows or use smaller anti-pads where the fab allows.

**Plane resonance.** A large plane pair is a cavity that resonates at $f \approx c/(2L\sqrt{\varepsilon_r})$: for a 100 mm board length that is about 0.7 GHz. Decoupling capacitors and stitching vias damp this ([[q0539]]).

**Wrong place for a big shape.** A switching node has a very high dV/dt; a large copper area there couples to everything nearby and radiates. Use only the area the current needs ([[q0550]]).

::: applications
- **Four-layer MCU boards**: L1 signals, L2 solid ground, L3 power shapes, L4 signals. The ground plane gives every signal a return and carries all the return current.
- **Point-of-load converters**: the output shape from the inductor to the load, often 10 to 20 mm wide and on multiple layers stitched with vias.
- **Motor controllers and inverters**: heavy-copper shapes for the DC bus, phase outputs and the sense return.
- **RF and mixed-signal boards**: unbroken ground plane under the RF section; power shapes on layers that do not cross it.
- **Power modules with exposed pads**: soldered to a large pour on both sides so the pour spreads and removes heat.
:::

::: assumptions
The log formula assumes an infinite, uniform sheet with point-like contacts; real boards have edges, cutouts, via clearances and finite contact size, so use it as an estimate and check with a field solver for critical rails (many layout tools include a DC IR-drop or PDN analysis). It also assumes DC or low frequency. At high frequency the current moves to the surface nearest the signal (skin and proximity effects), so the effective sheet resistance rises and the picture of current "fanning out" is replaced by current concentrated beneath the signal path. An alternative valid answer is "shapes rather than planes", when layer count or cost prevents a dedicated plane. Also note that very high-current paths sometimes use thicker copper or a bus bar instead of a plane.
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between a plane and a pour?
A plane is a dedicated layer of copper for one net, usually most of that layer. A pour (or shape, polygon, zone) is copper filling an area of a routing layer, sharing the layer with traces. A plane's job is global (return path, supply distribution); a pour's job is local (a high-current shape, a heat spreading area, or grounding space between traces). Both are the same conductor physically.

### Why does resistance only grow with the log of distance in a plane?
Current spreads over an ever-larger circumference as it moves away from the contact, so the cross-section area that carries it grows with the radius. The resistance of each radial shell is $\rho\,dr/(2\pi r t)$ and integrating gives $\ln(r_2/r_1)$. Most of the resistance is in the shells near the contact, which is why via placement and contact size matter more than overall plane size.

### Is a hatched pour as good as a solid one?
No. Hatching removes copper, so resistance and inductance rise, and the shielding gets worse. Hatched pours were used to avoid copper warping on old processes. Modern fabs handle solid pours fine; I'd use solid, and keep copper balance by adding planes on the opposite layer.

### What happens to a high-speed signal if it crosses a gap in the plane beneath it?
Its return current cannot flow directly under it, so it detours around the gap, creating a larger loop with higher inductance. That radiates, couples crosstalk to other nets, and changes the characteristic impedance. The fix is to keep the plane continuous beneath it, or to move it, or to add a stitching capacitor across the gap so the return can hop across.

### How would you decide between a 2-layer board with pours and a 4-layer board with planes?
I'd compare switching speed and current. Two layers with pours work for slow, low-noise designs, but a 4-layer board with solid ground and power planes is almost always cheaper than debugging EMI and drop problems. For any fast edge (tens of MHz) or any high-current rail with tight regulation, I'd want the planes.
:::
