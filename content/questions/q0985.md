::: testing
Whether you can **turn an unknown component into a measurable quantity** with the right instrument or a simple circuit, and whether you know what each method hides. R, C and L are measured differently: R with a current and a voltage, C and L through their frequency-dependent impedance or a time constant or a resonance. The interviewer is looking for: DMM for resistance with 4-wire when small; LCR meter and its settings (frequency, level, series vs parallel model, D/Q); simple bench methods (RC time constant, resonance with a known capacitor, a divider with a known resistor); the VNA or impedance analyzer for high frequency; and the real-world traps (in-circuit measurement, lead resistance, bias dependence, parasitics).
:::

::: why
Components are not ideal: a "100 nF" capacitor has ESR and ESL, an inductor has DCR and saturates, a resistor has parasitic inductance, and all of them change with frequency, DC bias, temperature and age. Being able to measure them properly lets you verify incoming parts, characterize a design, find which part drifted, and check simulation models. It is also a classic debug skill: "the filter is off; what is the actual capacitor value at the frequency it operates?" The conceptual background is in the impedance questions ([[q0024]], [[q0040]], [[q0031]]) and in the instrument pages ([[q0984]] for the VNA, [[q0986]] for DMMs).
:::

::: concepts
- **Impedance**: $Z=R+jX$ with $X_C=-1/(\omega C)$ and $X_L=\omega L$. See [[q0024]], [[q0040]].
- **Series vs parallel equivalent models**: the same impedance can be a series $R_s$–$C_s$ or parallel $R_p$–$C_p$; the choice affects the number you read.
- **Dissipation factor $D$ and quality factor $Q$**: $D=\tan\delta=1/Q$, a measure of loss.
- **Time constant and resonance**: $\tau=RC$, $\tau=L/R$, $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ allow measurement with just a scope and a generator.
- **Four-wire (Kelvin) sensing**: removes lead resistance for low-resistance measurements.
- **Parasitics and self-resonance**: above the self-resonant frequency a capacitor behaves as an inductor. See [[q0031]].
:::

::: answer
"It depends on the component, but the idea is the same: apply a known stimulus, measure the response, and compute.

Resistance is easiest: a DMM forces a small current and measures the voltage. For small resistances I use four-wire mode so lead resistance doesn't add, and out of circuit so other paths don't interfere.

For capacitance and inductance, the best bench tool is an LCR meter. It applies an AC test signal at a chosen frequency and level, measures the complex impedance, and reports the value plus loss, like dissipation factor or ESR. I pick the frequency near how the part will be used, because the value changes with frequency and bias, and I choose the series or parallel model appropriate to the impedance. Many DMMs also have a capacitance range, which is fine for rough values.

Without an LCR meter I can use simple circuits. For a capacitor: put it in an RC with a known resistor, drive it with a square wave, and measure the time constant on a scope, so C equals tau over R. For an inductor: resonate it with a known capacitor and measure the frequency, then L is one over the quantity two pi f squared times C. Or build a voltage divider with a known resistor and use the amplitude and phase.

For the general impedance versus frequency, up to high frequencies, I'd use an impedance analyzer or a VNA, which gives the self-resonance, ESR and the full picture."
:::

::: technical
**Resistance.** DMM 2-wire: $R=V/I_{test}$, including lead and contact resistance (typically 0.1 to 0.5 Ω total). Four-wire: separate force and sense leads, so the measurement ignores lead resistance. Out of circuit, power off, and no parallel paths.

**Capacitance and inductance with an LCR meter.** The meter applies a sine of known frequency $f$ and level, measures $V$ and $I$ (auto-balancing bridge), computes $Z=R+jX$, then reports $C_s$ or $C_p$ (or $L_s$/$L_p$) with $D$, $Q$ or ESR. Relations: $C=\frac{-1}{\omega X}$ for a series model, $L=\frac{X}{\omega}$, $D=R_s/|X|$ (series), and $ESR=\frac{D}{\omega C}$. Example: a 10 µF capacitor with $D=0.02$ at 1 kHz has $ESR=0.02/(2\pi\cdot1000\cdot10^{-5})=0.32\ \Omega$.

**Time-constant method (capacitor).** Drive $R$–$C$ with a square wave much slower than $\tau$; read the 63.2 % time, or the 10–90 % time $t_r=2.2\,RC$. With $R=10$ kΩ and $\tau=1.0$ ms, $C=\tau/R=100$ nF. Include the scope probe's ~10 pF and the source resistance.

**Resonance method (inductor).** Put $L$ in series or parallel with a known $C$ (say 100 nF) and find the peak or null frequency: $L=\frac{1}{(2\pi f_0)^2C}$. For $f_0=15.9$ kHz, $L\approx1.00$ mH.

**Divider method (any impedance).** Series known $R_{ref}$ with the unknown, drive with a sine, read both amplitudes and the phase on a scope. For a 100 nF capacitor at 1 kHz with $R_{ref}=1$ kΩ, $|Z|=1592\ \Omega$, $V_C/V_{in}=0.847$ at $-32.1^\circ$.

**High frequency.** Impedance analyzer or VNA (one-port $S_{11}$ or shunt-through $S_{21}$), with calibration and fixture compensation; capacitors show an ESR minimum and self-resonance, inductors a parallel self-resonance. See [[q0984]].
:::

Without one, a capacitor in an RC with a known R gives C from the time constant on a scope, and an inductor with a known C gives L from the resonant frequency. ::: long
"The general idea is to apply a known stimulus, measure the response and compute.

Resistance first. A DMM forces a small current and measures the voltage. For anything small, below about an ohm, I'd use four-wire mode so the lead and contact resistance doesn't add; a 0.2 ohm lead resistance on a 1 ohm resistor is a 20 percent error. I'd also measure out of circuit, because other parts in parallel will change the reading.

For capacitance and inductance the right tool is an LCR meter. It applies an AC test signal at a chosen frequency and level, measures the complex impedance, and gives me the value plus the dissipation factor or ESR. I choose a test frequency near where the part will be used, because the value changes with frequency, and also with DC bias for ceramics and inductors. I'd pick the series model for low-impedance parts and the parallel model for high-impedance ones.

If I don't have an LCR meter I can do it with a scope and a function generator. For a capacitor, build an RC with a known resistor, drive it with a slow square wave, and measure the time constant: with 10 kilohms and a 1 millisecond time constant, C is 100 nanofarads. For an inductor, resonate it with a known capacitor and measure the peak frequency; L is one over two pi f squared times C. For any impedance, a voltage divider with a known resistor, measuring amplitude and phase, works too.

And when I care about the whole picture across frequency, self-resonance, ESR, the point where a capacitor turns inductive, I'd use an impedance analyzer or a VNA."
:::

::: terms
- **Four-wire (Kelvin) measurement**: separate current-force and voltage-sense leads to eliminate lead resistance.
- **LCR meter**: instrument that measures R, L, C and impedance by applying an AC signal.
- **Auto-balancing bridge**: LCR technique that nulls the current through the unknown to measure $Z$ accurately.
- **Dissipation factor ($D$, tan δ)**: ratio of the resistive to reactive part; $D=1/Q$.
- **ESR / ESL**: equivalent series resistance and inductance of a capacitor.
- **Series vs parallel model**: two equivalent ways to represent a lossy reactive part.
- **DC bias dependence**: the change of capacitance (MLCC) or inductance (saturation) with DC voltage or current.
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: frequency where a part's parasitic reactance cancels its intended reactance.
- **Test level / frequency**: the AC amplitude and frequency applied by the instrument.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use a multimeter for resistance and an LCR meter for the rest".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains each measurement as **stimulus + response**: $V/I$, time constant, resonance, divider.
- Names the **traps**: lead resistance, in-circuit parallel paths, test frequency and level, DC bias, series vs parallel model, fixture parasitics.
- Gives **numbers**: ESR from $D$, $C=\tau/R$, $L$ from $f_0$ and a known $C$, error from 0.2 Ω leads on 1 Ω.
- Knows when to switch tools: **DMM** (DC resistance), **LCR** (kHz to MHz), **impedance analyzer/VNA** (MHz to GHz and SRF).
- Mentions that **datasheet values are at a specific condition** (e.g., 1 kHz, 1 V RMS, 0 V bias) and real use differs.
- Ties back to the **application**: measure at the operating frequency and bias, not just the nominal.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Measuring resistance in-circuit with power on.** Parallel paths give a lower reading and power can damage the meter. Power off, and lift one end if needed.
- **Two-wire measurement of milliohm resistors.** Lead and contact resistance dominate. Use four-wire.
- **Touching the part with your fingers** during a high-resistance measurement. Skin resistance (kΩ–MΩ) is in parallel.
- **Ignoring test frequency and level.** An MLCC at 1 kHz and 1 V may read very differently from its value at 100 kHz and 5 V DC bias.
- **Choosing the wrong model.** A small capacitor at high impedance should use the parallel model; a large electrolytic at low impedance, the series model.
- **Forgetting probe and fixture capacitance** in the time-constant method: a 10 pF probe on a 100 pF capacitor is a 10 % error.
- **Assuming the nominal is the actual.** Tolerances of 5 to 20 % and big DC-bias or temperature derating are normal for ceramics.
:::

::: deep
## One principle: apply a stimulus, measure a response

All component measurements reduce to the same recipe: apply a known stimulus (DC current, AC voltage, step, sweep), measure the response (voltage, current, time, frequency), and use the component's defining equation to solve. The defining equations:

$$ V=IR,\qquad I=C\frac{dV}{dt},\qquad V=L\frac{dI}{dt}, \qquad Z_C=\frac{1}{j\omega C},\quad Z_L=j\omega L. $$

The methods below differ in how directly they apply them.

## Resistance

**DC method (DMM).** The meter forces a known small current $I_{test}$ (for example 1 mA on a kΩ range), measures $V$, and displays $R=V/I$. Pitfalls:

- **Lead and contact resistance**: with two wires the meter measures $R_x+R_{leads}$; typical 0.1 to 0.5 Ω. A 0.2 Ω error on a 1 Ω resistor is 20 %.
- **Four-wire (Kelvin)**: a second pair of leads senses the voltage directly at the resistor, so the voltage drop in the current-carrying leads is excluded. Use it below about 10 Ω (and always for milliohms).
- **Test current heating** and voltage limits: a low-power ohms range for sensitive or non-linear parts.
- **Thermal EMF** at junctions gives µV offsets that matter for mΩ measurements; use offset compensation (reversing the current).

**In-circuit.** Other components in parallel reduce the apparent resistance, and semiconductor junctions may conduct at the test voltage. Measure out of circuit, or lift one end, or measure the in-circuit value only as a sanity check.

**High resistance** (MΩ–GΩ): leakage over board surfaces and cables, and your fingers, matter. Use guarding, a clean fixture and a higher-voltage test (an insulation-resistance meter or electrometer).

## Capacitance and inductance with an LCR meter

An LCR meter applies a sine at frequency $f$ with a stated level, uses an auto-balancing bridge to measure the complex voltage and current, computes $Z=R+jX$, then converts into an equivalent model. Key settings:

| Setting | What it changes | Typical choices |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency | Value of $C$, $L$, loss | 100 Hz, 1 kHz, 10 kHz, 100 kHz (to MHz) |
| Test level | Non-linear parts and signal-to-noise | 0.1 to 1 V RMS; 20 mV for sensitive parts |
| DC bias | MLCC and inductor values | 0 V up to rated, or DC current for inductors |
| Model | Series ($C_s$-$R_s$) or parallel ($C_p$-$R_p$) | Auto, or choose by impedance |
| Speed/averaging | Noise vs. time | Medium or slow |

**Series vs parallel model.** A lossy capacitor can be written as $C_s$ in series with $R_s$ (ESR) or $C_p$ in parallel with $R_p$. They're related by $C_p=C_s/(1+D^2)$ and $D=\omega R_sC_s=1/(\omega R_pC_p)$. For small $D$ they agree; for lossy parts they differ. Use the series model for low impedance (large C, small L) because the loss is dominated by series resistance; use the parallel model for high impedance (small C, large L) where leakage and core loss appear in parallel.

**What it reports.** $D$ (tan δ), $Q$, ESR and $|Z|$, phase. ESR from $D$: a 10 µF capacitor with $D=0.02$ at 1 kHz has $ESR=D/(\omega C)=0.02/(2\pi\cdot10^3\cdot10^{-5})=0.318\ \Omega$.

**Bias and level effects.** Class II ceramics (X5R/X7R) lose capacitance with DC bias, often a large fraction at rated voltage for small packages (values vary strongly by part; check the manufacturer's DC-bias curve). Inductors lose inductance as the core saturates ([[q0051]] discusses AC resistance effects). An LCR meter with a DC bias option or a bias tee reproduces real operating conditions.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The size of the capacitance loss under DC bias depends on dielectric class, package size and voltage rating. It can range from a few percent to well over 50 %. Use the specific manufacturer's curve rather than a rule of thumb.

### Inductor by the L/R time constant or resonance

*Time constant:* a step into $L$ in series with a known $R$ gives $\tau=L/R_{total}$ ($R_{total}$ includes the inductor's DCR and the source). With $R_{total}=10\ \Omega$ and $\tau=100\ \mu s$, $L=1$ mH.

*Resonance:* connect $L$ with a known $C$ and sweep a sine generator, looking for the peak (series) or the null (parallel) at

$$ f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}\ \Rightarrow\ L=\frac{1}{(2\pi f_0)^2C}. $$

With $C=100$ nF and $f_0=15.9$ kHz, $L=1.00$ mH. The same circuit gives $Q$ from the −3 dB width ($Q=f_0/\Delta f$), which is also a way to estimate the inductor's resistance: for a series resonance, $R=\omega_0L/Q$. Use a stable, known capacitor (C0G/NP0 or film) because an MLCC's value shifts with voltage and temperature.

## Impedance at a given frequency: the divider method

For an unknown $Z_x$ in series with a known $R_{ref}$, driven by a sine $V_{in}$:

$$ \frac{V_x}{V_{in}}=\frac{Z_x}{R_{ref}+Z_x} \;\Rightarrow\; Z_x=R_{ref}\frac{V_x/V_{in}}{1-V_x/V_{in}} $$

with $V_x/V_{in}$ taken as a complex ratio (amplitude and phase on a two-channel scope). For a 100 nF capacitor at 1 kHz with $R_{ref}=1$ kΩ: $|Z|=1/(2\pi\cdot1000\cdot10^{-7})=1592\ \Omega$, giving $|V_x/V_{in}|=1592/\sqrt{1000^2+1592^2}=0.847$ with a phase of $-32.1^\circ$. Measuring amplitude and phase and solving gives both $C$ and the loss. Choose $R_{ref}$ near $|Z_x|$ for best sensitivity.

## High frequency: impedance analyzer and VNA

Above a few MHz, LCR meters' accuracy and test-fixture residuals degrade. Use:

- **Impedance analyzer** (RF I–V or auto-balancing up to ~100 MHz): wide impedance range with fixture compensation (open/short/load).
- **VNA one-port $S_{11}$**: $Z=Z_0\frac{1+\Gamma}{1-\Gamma}$; best near $Z_0$ (tens to hundreds of ohms). For very low impedance (PDN, decoupling capacitors at mΩ), a **shunt-through $S_{21}$** measurement with two ports is more accurate. See [[q0984]].

These show the real behavior of passives: capacitor ESR minimum and self-resonant frequency, inductor SRF and Q peak, resistor parasitic inductance. A 100 nF ceramic's SRF is often in the 10s of MHz; above it the part looks inductive and no longer decouples ([[q0031]]).

## Choosing the method

| Quantity | Frequency | Best tool | Alternative |
|---|---|---|---|
| R above 10 Ω | DC | DMM 2-wire | LCR at 1 kHz |
| R below 10 Ω | DC | DMM or milliohm meter 4-wire | Shunt + current source + voltmeter |
| C (pF–µF) | 1 kHz to 1 MHz | LCR meter | DMM range; RC time constant |
| L (µH–mH) | 10 kHz to 1 MHz | LCR meter | Resonance method |
| ESR | 100 kHz | LCR (ESR mode) | VNA at SRF |
| $Z$ vs frequency, SRF | 10 kHz to GHz | Impedance analyzer / VNA | Divider + scope + generator |
| Component under bias | any | LCR with DC bias | Bias tee + VNA |

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If you're asked to measure an unknown capacitor with only a scope and a function generator, the RC time constant on a square wave is the answer. State the resistor you'd use, the number you'd read (63.2 % or 10–90 %), and the correction for the probe. It shows you can measure with minimal tools.
:::

::: applications
- **Incoming inspection**: LCR meter checks of capacitor and inductor values and ESR against tolerance.
- **Decoupling design**: VNA or impedance analyzer shunt-through measurements of capacitor and PDN impedance to find the SRF.
- **Sensor interface**: measuring thermistor, strain-gauge or capacitive-sensor values (small changes in R or C).
- **Failure analysis**: finding a drifted capacitor in a filter by measuring $C$ and ESR.
- **Inductor/transformer characterization**: inductance vs DC bias current to find saturation.
- **Cable and PCB characterization**: capacitance per length and inductance per length from open/short measurements.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes **linear, passive, two-terminal** components. Non-linear parts (varactors, saturating inductors, MLCC under bias) need bias- and level-aware measurement.
- The time-constant and resonance methods assume **ideal parts at the frequency of interest**; real parasitics (ESL, DCR, probe capacitance) bias the result.
- LCR meter accuracy figures vary by model and frequency; read the accuracy chart for the impedance and frequency you use.
- At **very high or very low impedance** (mΩ or GΩ) specialized methods (4-terminal-pair, shunt-through, electrometer) are needed.
- **"Impedance" at DC** is just resistance; the term is meaningful at a stated frequency.
- Series/parallel models are **equivalent circuits**, not physical truths; either can describe the same measurement.
:::

::: followups
### Why does my 100 nF ceramic read 60 nF on an LCR meter with 5 V bias?
Class II dielectrics (X5R, X7R) lose effective permittivity as DC field increases, so capacitance drops with DC bias, sometimes by half or more at rated voltage, especially in small packages. Re-measure at 0 V bias to confirm the nominal, then use the manufacturer's DC-bias curve for the operating voltage. Also check the test level and frequency.

### How do you measure a 5 mΩ resistance?
Four-wire measurement with a stable test current (for example 1 A or 100 mA, giving 5 mV or 0.5 mV), a precise voltmeter on the sense leads placed right at the resistor's terminals, and current reversal or offset compensation to cancel thermal EMFs. A milliohm meter or SMU does this automatically. A 2-wire DMM would read mostly lead resistance.

### How would you tell a capacitor's ESR from its capacitance on a bench?
An LCR meter at a frequency where ESR is meaningful (100 kHz for electrolytics), reading ESR or $D$ and computing $ESR=D/(\omega C)$. Without it: apply a square-wave current step and look at the instantaneous voltage step ($\Delta V=I\cdot ESR$) followed by the slower $I\,t/C$ ramp.

### How do you find an inductor's saturation current?
Measure inductance versus DC bias current with an LCR meter plus a bias source, or pulse a current through it and look at the current waveform: when the core saturates, $di/dt$ suddenly increases and the current ramp bends upward. Define the saturation current at a stated inductance drop (for example 20 or 30 %), as manufacturers do.

### Scenario: your low-pass filter's cutoff is off by 30 %. How do you find the culprit?
Measure each part in the circuit's operating condition: the resistors with a DMM (out of circuit), the capacitors on an LCR meter at the filter's frequency and at the DC bias present, and look at the whole filter response with a sweep. A ceramic with heavy DC-bias derating, or a pull from the wrong dielectric class, is a common cause.
:::

::: interpretations
"Resistance/capacitance/inductance/impedance" are four related quantities: resistance at DC, capacitance and inductance as the reactive parts of impedance, and impedance as the frequency-dependent combination. A good answer gives one method for each and then the general rule: *apply a stimulus and measure the response at a frequency like the operating one*. It may also mean "in circuit" (a component on a board); then the answer includes the parallel-path caveats and perhaps firmware-based measurement (such as charge timing with an MCU).
:::
