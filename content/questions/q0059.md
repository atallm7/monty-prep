::: testing
This is deliberately open-ended. The interviewer is checking whether you can build a **clear, layered explanation** from first principles without hand-waving or falling into the classic misconceptions. Hidden sub-skills:

- Defining **charge, voltage, current, resistance and power** precisely and showing how they connect ($V = IR$, $P = VI$).
- Knowing what actually moves: electrons in metals drift **very slowly** (fractions of a mm/s), but the **field and energy** propagate at a large fraction of the speed of light.
- Explaining that a source doesn't supply electrons, it supplies **energy** (a potential difference) that pushes charges already in the wire around a closed loop.
- Communication: can you pitch it at the right level and then go deeper when asked?
:::

::: why
Every circuit you'll debug depends on these ideas being correct in your head. "Where does the current go?", "why does the ground bounce?", "why does the signal arrive in nanoseconds when electrons crawl?", and "why must there be a return path?" all come back to fields, charge conservation and energy. Interviewers also use this question to gauge communication: hardware engineers constantly explain things to firmware, mechanical and product people. A crisp, accurate explanation with one good analogy and its limits is a strong signal.
:::

::: concepts
- **Electric charge**: the property of electrons (−) and protons (+) that makes them feel electric forces; measured in coulombs (one electron is $1.602\times10^{-19}$ C).
- **Electric field and voltage**: a field pushes charges; voltage is the energy per unit charge between two points (1 V = 1 J/C). See [[q0002]].
- **Current**: the rate of charge flow, $I = dQ/dt$ (1 A = 1 C/s ≈ $6.24\times10^{18}$ electrons/s).
- **Resistance and Ohm's law**: collisions limit how fast charges drift, so current is proportional to voltage, $V = IR$. See [[q0001]].
- **Conservation laws**: charge conservation gives KCL; energy conservation gives KVL. See [[q0004]].
- **Power**: the rate energy is delivered, $P = VI$. See [[q0006]].
- **Conductors**: metals have a sea of free electrons available to move. See [[q0055]].
:::

::: answer
"At the most basic level, electricity is the movement of electric charge, and the energy that charge carries.

A source like a battery uses chemistry to create a voltage, which is just a difference in potential energy per unit charge between its terminals. When you connect a closed loop of wire and a load, that voltage sets up an electric field along the conductor. Metals already have tons of free electrons, so the field pushes them along. That flow of charge is current.

The electrons don't move freely though. They keep bumping into the vibrating atoms of the metal, which is resistance, so the current ends up proportional to the voltage: Ohm's law, $V = IR$. Wherever there's resistance, the energy turns into heat or light or motion, and the power is $V$ times $I$.

A couple of things people find surprising. The battery doesn't supply electrons. They were already in the wire; it supplies energy to push them around the loop, which is why you need a complete circuit. And the electrons themselves move really slowly, something like a tenth of a millimetre per second in a typical wire. But the light turns on instantly because the field, and the energy, travel close to the speed of light."
:::

::: technical
**Charge and field.** Electrons ($-q$, $q = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ C) and protons ($+q$) exert forces through the electric field: $\mathbf F = Q\mathbf E$. Voltage between two points is the work per unit charge to move a charge between them: $V_{AB} = -\int_B^A \mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l$, in J/C.

**Sources.** A battery's chemical reactions separate charge until the internal field balances the chemical driving force; the result is an EMF (≈1.5 V for alkaline, ≈3.7 V nominal for Li-ion). A generator does the same using magnetic induction ($\mathcal E = -d\Phi/dt$).

**Current and drift.** In a metal, free electrons move randomly at the Fermi velocity (~$1.6\times10^6$ m/s in copper) with zero net motion. A field adds a tiny net drift: $v_d = \mu E$. Current is
$$I = nqv_dA$$
For copper ($n = 8.5\times10^{28}$ m⁻³), 1 A in 1 mm² gives $v_d \approx 7.4\times10^{-5}$ m/s (0.07 mm/s).

**Ohm's law (microscopic).** Between collisions (mean free time $\tau \approx 2.5\times10^{-14}$ s in copper at room temperature), electrons accelerate in the field, then scatter. This gives $\mathbf J = \sigma\mathbf E$ with $\sigma = nq^2\tau/m$, which integrates to $V = IR$, $R = \rho L/A$.

**Energy and power.** Each coulomb crossing a voltage $V$ gains or loses $V$ joules, so $P = VI$; in a resistor $P = I^2R = V^2/R$.

**Propagation speed.** The field (signal) travels as an electromagnetic wave guided by the conductors, at $c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,eff}}$: ~$3\times10^8$ m/s in air, ~$2\times10^8$ m/s in coax, ~175 ps/inch in FR-4 stripline. Energy actually flows through the fields around the wires (the Poynting vector $\mathbf S = \mathbf E\times\mathbf H$), and the wires guide it.

**Conservation.** Charge can't pile up in a steady state, so currents into a node sum to zero (KCL). The field is conservative at DC, so voltages around a loop sum to zero (KVL). **AC** simply reverses the field periodically; electrons jiggle in place and energy is still delivered.
:::

::: short
"Electricity is charge moving and the energy it carries. A source like a battery creates a voltage, which is energy per unit charge. Connect a closed circuit and that voltage sets up a field in the wire, which pushes the free electrons that are already there, and that's current. Collisions with the metal's atoms are resistance, so $V = IR$, and energy gets delivered at $P = VI$. The electrons drift really slowly, fractions of a millimetre per second, but the field and energy move near the speed of light."
:::

::: long
"I'd build it up in layers.

First, charge. Electrons are negative, protons are positive, and charges exert forces on each other through the electric field. In a metal like copper, each atom gives up roughly one electron to a shared sea of free electrons, so the wire is full of mobile charge but it's neutral overall.

Second, voltage. A source like a battery uses a chemical reaction to push charge from one terminal to the other, creating a potential difference. Voltage is just energy per unit charge: a 9 V battery gives each coulomb 9 joules.

Third, current. When you connect a complete loop, the source sets up an electric field along the wire, and that field pushes on all those free electrons at once. They start drifting toward the positive terminal, and that net flow of charge is current. One amp is about six billion billion electrons per second past a point. Conventional current is defined in the opposite direction, from plus to minus, for historical reasons.

Fourth, resistance. The electrons constantly collide with the vibrating lattice, so they don't accelerate forever, they reach an average drift speed proportional to the field. That's where Ohm's law comes from, and it's also why resistors get hot: those collisions turn electrical energy into heat. Power delivered is $V$ times $I$.

Then there are two things I think are worth knowing because they're surprising. One, the electrons are really slow. In a 1 mm² copper wire carrying an amp, the drift speed is under a tenth of a millimetre per second. A light still turns on instantly because the field propagates along the wire at close to the speed of light, so all the electrons start moving almost together. Two, the energy isn't carried inside the electrons like water in a bucket. It flows through the electric and magnetic fields around the wires, and the wires guide it, which is why things like return paths and loop area matter so much in real boards."
:::

::: terms
- **Charge (Q)**: the property that makes particles feel electric force; measured in coulombs.
- **Voltage (potential difference)**: energy per unit charge between two points; 1 V = 1 J/C.
- **EMF**: the energy per unit charge a source provides by non-electrostatic means (chemistry, induction).
- **Current (I)**: rate of charge flow, 1 A = 1 C/s. Conventional current flows from + to −, opposite to electron flow.
- **Drift velocity**: the small average velocity of carriers along the field, superimposed on fast random motion.
- **Resistance**: opposition to current from carrier collisions, $R = \rho L/A$.
- **Power**: rate of energy transfer, $P = VI$.
- **Closed circuit / return path**: a complete loop is needed for steady current, because charge is conserved and can't pile up.
:::

::: exceptional
**Average:** "Electrons flow from the battery through the wire to the light bulb. Voltage is like pressure and current is like flow."

**Exceptional:**
- Builds the explanation in layers: **charge → field/voltage → current → resistance → power**, each defined precisely.
- States that the source supplies **energy, not electrons**, and why a **closed loop** is required (charge conservation).
- Quantifies the **drift velocity** (≈0.07 mm/s for 1 A in 1 mm²) vs the **signal speed** (near c), and explains the difference (the field propagates; electrons are already everywhere in the wire).
- Explains where **Ohm's law** comes from (collisions limit drift) and where the **energy** goes (heat in collisions; energy flows in the fields around the wires).
- Uses an analogy *and says where it breaks* (water pressure/flow is good for V and I, bad for propagation speed and energy flow).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Electrons travel from the battery to the bulb at nearly the speed of light."** Electrons drift at fractions of a mm/s. It's the field (the signal) that propagates fast.
- **"The battery stores electrons" or "the battery runs out of electrons."** A battery stores chemical energy; the number of electrons in the circuit doesn't change. It "runs out" when the reactants are used up.
- **"Current gets used up in the load."** Current is the same going in and coming out (KCL). Energy is used, not current.
- **Confusing conventional current with electron flow.** Conventional current is defined from + to −; electrons move the other way. Both give the same answers if used consistently.
- **"Voltage flows through a circuit."** Voltage is a difference *between* two points; current flows *through* elements.
- **Ignoring the return path.** Current always returns to its source; on PCBs, where it returns (under the trace, around a split plane) determines EMI and noise.
:::

::: deep
## Layer 1: charge and fields

Everything starts with charge. Electrons carry $-1.602\times10^{-19}$ C, protons $+1.602\times10^{-19}$ C, and like charges repel. Rather than thinking of charges reaching out to each other, physics describes it with an **electric field** $\mathbf E$: a charge $Q$ in a field feels force $\mathbf F = Q\mathbf E$.

The **voltage** between two points is the work done per unit charge moving between them. If moving 1 C from A to B releases 9 J, then $V_{AB} = 9$ V. That's all a voltage is: an energy-per-charge difference. Voltage is always *between* two points, which is why every measurement needs a reference.

## Layer 2: where the mobile charge comes from

Copper atoms each release about one outer electron into a shared "sea" of free electrons ([[q0055]] explains why using band theory): about $8.5\times10^{28}$ per m³. The wire is still neutral, because the positive ions stay put. Those free electrons zip around randomly at about $1.6\times10^{6}$ m/s, but in random directions, so with no field there's no net current.

## Layer 3: a source creates a field, the field pushes charge

A battery is a chemical charge pump. Reactions at the electrodes move charge internally until the resulting voltage balances the chemical driving force. Connect a wire loop and the battery's terminals set up a field throughout the conductor (surface charges on the wire arrange themselves to guide the field along it, even around bends). Every free electron in the wire feels that field and picks up a small **net drift** opposite to $\mathbf E$.

**Why you need a closed loop.** Charge is conserved. If current flowed out of the battery into a dead-end wire, charge would pile up at the end and its field would oppose the source and stop the flow within nanoseconds. Only a complete loop lets current flow steadily. That's KCL ([[q0004]]).

## Layer 4: collisions give resistance (and Ohm's law)

If nothing got in the way, the field would accelerate electrons forever. Instead they scatter off lattice vibrations (phonons) and impurities roughly every $\tau \approx 2.5\times10^{-14}$ s in copper. Between collisions each electron gains velocity $qE\tau/m$ along the field, then gets randomized. The average is the **drift velocity**:

$$v_d = \frac{q\tau}{m}E = \mu E$$

Current density is charge density times drift velocity:

$$J = nqv_d = \frac{nq^2\tau}{m}E = \sigma E$$

That's Ohm's law in its microscopic form. Integrate over a wire of length $L$ and area $A$ and you get $V = IR$ with $R = \rho L/A$ ([[q0001]]). Each collision dumps the energy the electron gained into the lattice as heat, which is why resistors get warm: $P = I^2R$.

## The surprising numbers: slow electrons, fast signals

**How fast do the electrons move?** For 1 A in a 1 mm² copper wire:

$$v_d = \frac{I}{nqA} = \frac{1}{8.5\times10^{28}\times1.6\times10^{-19}\times10^{-6}} \approx 7.4\times10^{-5}\ \text{m/s}$$

That's **0.07 mm/s**. An electron would take nearly four hours to travel one metre. In an AC circuit at 60 Hz, electrons just jiggle back and forth by well under a micrometre.

**So why does the lamp turn on instantly?** The tube is already full. When you close the switch, the *field* propagates down the wire as an electromagnetic wave, at close to the speed of light in air (on PCB traces in FR-4, about half that, ~175 ps per inch). Every electron along the wire starts drifting almost simultaneously. A good analogy: a garden hose that's already full of water. Open the tap and water comes out of the far end immediately, even though no single water molecule travelled the length of the hose.

## Layer 5: where the energy actually goes

It's tempting to picture electrons carrying energy from the battery to the lamp like delivery trucks. They don't: the same electrons circulate, and they barely move. The energy flows through the **electromagnetic field** in the space around the conductors. The Poynting vector $\mathbf S = \mathbf E\times\mathbf H$ points from the battery, through the space between the wires, into the resistor, where it's absorbed and turned into heat. The wires act as guides.

That's not just a physics curiosity. It's exactly why:
- **Loop area** matters for EMI (the field region between outgoing and return currents radiates and picks up noise).
- High-speed signals must have a **continuous return path** in the plane under the trace: the field lives between trace and plane.
- Transmission lines have a characteristic impedance set by **geometry and dielectric**, not by the copper's resistance.

## AC versus DC

With **DC**, the field always points the same way and electrons drift steadily. With **AC** (mains at 50/60 Hz), the source reverses the field periodically, so electrons oscillate around fixed positions. Energy still flows from source to load every half-cycle, because power $p = vi$ is positive whenever $v$ and $i$ have the same sign. AC is used for power distribution mainly because transformers can step it up for low-loss transmission ($P_{loss} = I^2R$, [[q0007]]) and back down for use.
:::

::: applications
- **Power distribution**: understanding $P = VI$ and $I^2R$ loss explains high-voltage transmission lines and why USB-PD raises voltage (up to 48 V in EPR) instead of current for high-power charging.
- **PCB return paths**: knowing current flows in loops and energy lives in the fields guides ground-plane design and EMI control.
- **Trace and wire sizing**: current density and resistive heating set trace widths (IPC-2221/IPC-2152) and wire gauges.
- **Signal timing**: propagation delay (~150–175 ps/inch on FR-4) matters for clock skew and DDR length matching.
- **Batteries**: a battery stores chemical energy, delivers it as EMF, and its internal resistance limits current, which is why phones sag in voltage under load.
- **Safety**: current through the body, not voltage alone, causes harm; voltage just determines how much current a given body resistance allows.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Metallic conduction by electrons.** In electrolytes, plasmas and semiconductors, ions or holes also carry current, but the same field-pushes-charge picture applies.
- **Steady state (DC) or slowly varying (low-frequency AC).** At high frequency, wires behave as transmission lines, skin effect ([[q0056]]) moves current to the surface, and lumped KVL/KCL need care.
- **Classical (Drude) picture.** It gives the right form of Ohm's law but the wrong temperature dependence details; quantum mechanics (only electrons near the Fermi energy participate) fixes those. For an interview, the classical picture is the right level unless asked.
- **Ohmic materials.** Diodes, transistors, lamps (whose filament resistance rises steeply with temperature) and gas discharges don't follow a fixed $R$.

If the interviewer means "how does electricity get to my house", pivot to generation (turbines and induction), step-up transformers, high-voltage transmission, step-down distribution and the 120/240 V service.
:::

::: followups
### If electrons move so slowly, why does a light turn on instantly?
Because the wire is already full of free electrons and the field propagates at nearly the speed of light. Closing the switch launches an electromagnetic wave along the wires; within nanoseconds every electron in the loop starts drifting. Like a full hose: water comes out the far end immediately even though each molecule moves slowly.

### What's the drift velocity of electrons in a 2.5 mm² copper wire carrying 16 A?
$v_d = I/(nqA) = 16/(8.5\times10^{28}\times1.6\times10^{-19}\times2.5\times10^{-6}) \approx 4.7\times10^{-4}$ m/s, about 0.47 mm/s. Still slower than a snail.

### Does a battery run out of electrons?
No. The number of electrons in the circuit is unchanged; they go around the loop. A battery stores chemical energy. When the reactants are consumed, the EMF drops and internal resistance rises, so it can't push current any more. Its capacity is rated in amp-hours (charge moved) or watt-hours (energy).

### Why does a circuit need to be a closed loop?
Charge is conserved. In an open circuit, a brief current flows until charge builds up at the open end and its field cancels the source's push, then current stops (that's a tiny capacitor charging). A steady current needs a path back to the source so charge can circulate without accumulating.

### What if you connect a wire directly across a battery?
The only resistance is the wire's and the battery's internal resistance, so current is huge: a fresh AA (internal resistance roughly 0.1–0.3 Ω) can push several amps. Nearly all the power goes into the battery's internal resistance and the wire as heat. Lithium cells can deliver tens or hundreds of amps, which can melt wires, vent the cell or start a fire. That's why packs have fuses and protection ICs.
:::

::: interpretations
"How does electricity work?" is intentionally vague. Good strategy: give a **30-second layered answer** (charge, voltage as energy per charge, current, resistance, power), then offer to go deeper in whichever direction they want: the microscopic picture (drift, collisions), the energy picture (fields, Poynting), circuits (KCL/KVL), or the power grid. Asking "Do you want the physics level or the circuits level?" is a perfectly good move.
:::
