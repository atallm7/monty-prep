::: basics
### In plain words
Ripple is the small wobble left on a converter's output voltage. We reduce it because the load and the converter itself both suffer from it. The load sees a supply that is not quite steady, which can cause errors. The converter sees extra peak current, heat and noise.

### Picture it
Picture reading a book on a train. If the ride is smooth, you read normally. If the ride shakes a little, you can still read, but a small shake is enough to ruin fine handwriting. Sensitive circuits are like that fine handwriting, while a rough digital circuit is like reading a big headline. The picture breaks down in that ripple is not random: it repeats at the switching frequency, so it shows up as a steady unwanted tone rather than random shaking.

### Words you need
- **Ripple**: the small repeating variation on the output voltage.
- **Peak-to-peak**: the full height of the wobble, from its lowest to its highest point.
- **Tolerance**: how far a supply may stray from its target value, such as ±3%.
- **Ripple current**: the up-and-down swing of the inductor current ([[q0194]]).
- **EMI**: unwanted electrical noise that a circuit radiates or conducts to others.

### The simplest example
A processor core that runs at 1.0 V allows ±3%, which is ±30 mV. The converter has 20 mV peak-to-peak ripple, which is ±10 mV around the average. That ripple alone uses one third of the allowed error, before any DC error or load transient is counted. A sensitive analog circuit might care even more. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows how ripple turns into errors in sensitive circuits, such as ADCs (which measure voltages) and PLLs (which make clock signals), what it costs the converter, and what you can do about it ([[q0215]]).
:::

::: testing
Whether you can connect a number on a scope (millivolts of ripple) to **what it does to the system**. The interviewer wants:

1. Load-side effects: ripple adds to the supply tolerance budget, and couples into sensitive analog, RF and timing circuits through finite PSRR.
2. Converter-side effects: ripple current means higher peak current, more core and capacitor stress, and more conducted EMI.
3. A quantitative example (a PLL spur or an ADC), not just "noise is bad".
4. The counterweight: reducing ripple costs size, efficiency or money, so you reduce it only as far as needed ([[q0219]]).
:::

::: why
Ripple is the most visible symptom of a switching supply and the usual reason a switcher "can't be used" for a sensitive rail. Knowing exactly how it hurts lets you set a sensible ripple spec instead of a blanket "as low as possible", which is what separates a cost-effective design from an over-filtered one. It also connects the circuit-level ripple equations ([[q0212]]) to system-level consequences like spurs, ADC noise and EMI compliance.
:::

::: concepts
- **Supply tolerance budget**: DC accuracy + ripple + transient must fit within the load's allowed window.
- **PSRR**: a load's rejection of supply variation, falling with frequency.
- **Supply pushing (VCO)**: oscillator frequency shifts with supply, so ripple becomes FM spurs.
- **Ripple current heating**: $I_{rms}^2\cdot ESR$ in capacitors, core loss in inductors ([[q0213]]).
- **Conducted EMI**: ripple current on input and output lines at $f_{sw}$ and harmonics.
- **Ripple levers**: $L$, $C$, ESR, $f$, interleaving, filtering ([[q0215]]).
:::

::: answer
"Ripple matters on both sides of the converter.

On the load side, ripple eats into the supply tolerance. If a 1.0 V core allows plus or minus 3%, that's 30 mV each way, and 17 mV peak-to-peak of ripple uses 8.5 mV of that, over a quarter, before DC error and transients. Worse, sensitive circuits convert ripple into errors: an ADC's reference or supply ripple appears as noise or spurs, and a VCO or PLL has supply pushing. Say 5 MHz per volt of pushing: 10 mV of ripple at 500 kHz offset gives a modulation index of 0.1 and a spur about 26 dB below the carrier before any filtering, which is awful for RF. Even with 40 dB of rejection it's minus 66 dBc, still marginal.

On the converter side, ripple current means higher peak current, so a bigger saturation margin and current-limit headroom, more core loss in the inductor, ripple heating in the capacitors, and more conducted EMI at the switching frequency and harmonics.

So I reduce ripple to the level the load needs: more inductance, capacitance, frequency, interleaving or a post filter. But each costs size, efficiency or money, so a digital core with a wide tolerance may not need much."
:::

::: technical
**Tolerance budget.** For $V_{nom}\pm\varepsilon$: $\varepsilon V_{nom}\ge\varepsilon_{DC}+\tfrac12\Delta V_{pp}+\Delta V_{transient}$. Example 1.0 V, $\pm3\%$: 30 mV; ripple 16.7 mV pp consumes $8.35$ mV, 28% of the window.

**Supply pushing / FM spur.** A VCO with pushing $K_p$ (Hz/V) and supply ripple amplitude $V_r$ at offset $f_m$: peak deviation $\Delta f=K_pV_r$, index $\beta=\Delta f/f_m$, spur $\approx20\log(\beta/2)$ dBc for $\beta\ll1$. $K_p=5$ MHz/V, $V_r=10$ mV (peak), $f_m=500$ kHz: $\Delta f=50$ kHz, $\beta=0.1$, $-26$ dBc. With 40 dB of supply rejection: $-66$ dBc. (Real $K_p$ ranges from about 0.1 to tens of MHz/V; PLL loop filtering also suppresses offsets inside the loop bandwidth.)

**ADC.** A 12-bit ADC at 3.3 V full scale has 0.81 mV LSB. Supply ripple $V_r$ appears at the output reduced by PSRR: 17 mV with 60 dB PSRR is 0.017 mV, 2% of an LSB. At 40 dB it is 0.17 mV, 21% of an LSB, and at 20 dB it exceeds an LSB.

**Converter stress.** $I_{pk}=I_{out}+\Delta I_L/2$: 2.51 A vs 2 A load. Capacitor RMS ripple current $\Delta I/\sqrt{12}=0.29$ A, loss $0.43$ mW in 5 mΩ. Core loss rises with flux swing ($\propto\Delta I^\beta$). Larger ripple lowers the usable load before the inductor saturates or the current limit trips.

**EMI.** Ripple current at $f_{sw}$ appears on the input and output; the triangular and pulsed waveforms have harmonics. Lower ripple means smaller filters to meet limits.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Pushing coefficients, PSRR values and tolerance windows are load-specific; the examples are illustrative.
:::

::: long
"Ripple hurts in two places.

First, at the load. Every supply has a tolerance window; a 1 V core at plus or minus 3% allows 30 millivolts each way. A 17 millivolt peak-to-peak ripple uses 8.5 millivolts of that, more than a quarter, before DC accuracy and load transients. If the ripple makes the supply dip below the minimum, a digital core can fail timing or reset.

Then there's coupling into analog and RF. Circuits reject supply variation by their PSRR, and PSRR falls with frequency, so at 500 kilohertz it's often only 20 to 40 dB. An ADC with a 0.8 millivolt LSB and 20 dB of rejection sees 17 millivolts of ripple as nearly 2 LSB of error. A VCO with 5 megahertz per volt of pushing and 10 millivolts of ripple gets a 50 kilohertz deviation at a 500 kilohertz rate, a modulation index of 0.1, about 26 dB below the carrier, which would violate most RF specs. Even with 40 dB of rejection it's minus 66 dBc.

At the converter, ripple current is the other face of ripple voltage. Peak current is the load plus half the ripple, so you need more saturation and current-limit margin, the inductor sees more core loss, the capacitors heat from ripple current, and conducted EMI at the switching frequency and its harmonics rises, which means bigger filters.

So I reduce ripple until the load is comfortable and the EMI passes, by raising $L$, $C$ or frequency, interleaving or adding a post filter, and not beyond, since each step costs size, efficiency or money."
:::

::: terms
- **Peak-to-peak ripple**: max minus min of the AC component of the output.
- **Supply tolerance window**: the allowed range of supply voltage for a load.
- **PSRR**: ratio of supply variation to the resulting output variation (dB).
- **Supply pushing**: oscillator frequency shift per volt of supply.
- **Spur (dBc)**: spurious tone level relative to the carrier.
- **LSB**: least significant bit; $V_{FS}/2^N$.
- **Conducted EMI**: noise currents on power leads.
- **Ripple current rating**: capacitor or inductor RMS current limit.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Ripple is noise that can disturb sensitive circuits and cause EMI."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames ripple in the supply tolerance budget with numbers.
- Gives a quantitative coupling example (PLL spur with pushing, ADC LSBs with PSRR) and notes PSRR degrades with frequency.
- Covers converter-side costs: peak current and saturation margin, core loss, capacitor heating, EMI filter size.
- Distinguishes ripple from spikes and notes both matter differently.
- Closes with the trade: specify ripple from the load's need ([[q0219]]), not minimum possible.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Ripple is only a noise problem."** It also consumes tolerance budget and stresses the converter's own parts.
- **Assuming PSRR is the DC spec.** PSRR at 500 kHz is far lower than at DC or 1 kHz.
- **Spec by peak-to-peak without bandwidth.** Ripple measured with a 20 MHz limit vs full bandwidth differs, spikes included.
- **Over-filtering everything.** A digital rail with wide margin doesn't need sub-millivolt ripple.
- **Forgetting ripple current.** Lowering voltage ripple with more $C$ does nothing for inductor peak current; only $L$ and $f$ do.
:::

::: deep
## Ripple on the load side

### Tolerance budget

A load specifies a supply window. The worst-case supply at the load is the sum of every deviation:

$$V_{min}=V_{nom}-\varepsilon_{DC}-\tfrac12\Delta V_{ripple}-\Delta V_{transient}-\Delta V_{IR}.$$

For a 1.0 V core with $\pm3\%$: 30 mV. If the regulator's DC accuracy is 1% (10 mV) and the load step droop 10 mV, ripple may use only about 10 mV of window, i.e. 20 mV peak-to-peak. A converter with 17 mV pp consumes 8.35 mV: tolerable, but not with 40 mV pp.

### Digital loads

CMOS timing depends on supply: delay changes by roughly 1 to 2% per percent of supply near nominal. Ripple causes cycle-to-cycle delay variation (supply-induced jitter), significant for high-speed SerDes and PLL clocks. Brown-out and reset thresholds can trip on a ripple dip. See [[q0219]] for why tolerant digital rails don't need much.

### ADC and analog front ends

The load converts supply variation into output error through PSRR$(f)$:

$$\Delta V_{err}=\Delta V_{ripple}\cdot 10^{-PSRR/20}.$$

| Ripple | PSRR at 500 kHz | Error | In LSBs (12 bit, 3.3 V) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 17 mV | 60 dB | 17 µV | 0.02 |
| 17 mV | 40 dB | 0.17 mV | 0.21 |
| 17 mV | 20 dB | 1.7 mV | 2.1 |

Amplifiers, references and LDOs all have PSRR rolling off with frequency, which is why the ripple frequency matters as much as the amplitude. Aliasing: if the ADC samples at a rate near a multiple of $f_{sw}$, the ripple folds into band as a low-frequency error.

### RF and clocks

A VCO responds to supply with pushing $K_p$ Hz/V. Ripple $V_r$ at $f_m$ is frequency modulation: $\beta=K_pV_r/f_m$, spur level $20\log(\beta/2)$.

## Ripple on the converter side

- **Peak current**: $I_{pk}=I_{out}+\Delta I_L/2$. Lower ripple means a lower peak for the same load: 2.51 A vs 2.0 A. Saturation current and current limit must exceed it, so large ripple needs a larger inductor ([[q0212]]).
- **RMS current and conduction loss**: $I_{rms}^2=I_{out}^2+\Delta I^2/12$: 4.086 vs 4.0 A²: only 2% even at this 51% ripple ratio ($r^2/12$), and 8% at a 100% ripple ratio.
- **Core loss**: grows with flux swing as $\Delta B^\beta$ ($\beta\approx2$ to 2.8): halving ripple current cuts core loss to about a fifth ([[q0213]]).
- **Capacitor heating**: $\Delta I^2/12\cdot ESR$: here 0.43 mW (tiny), but for electrolytics with 100 mΩ ESR it is 8.7 mW, a real thermal limit on life.
- **Conducted EMI**: ripple current at $f_{sw}$ and harmonics flows on input and output leads; reducing it reduces the filter needed to pass limits.
- **DCM boundary**: larger ripple moves the CCM/DCM boundary to higher load ($I_{out}=\Delta I/2$), changing behavior at light load.

## Why not reduce it to zero?

Every lever costs ([[q0215]]): bigger $L$ (size, DCR, slower transient), more $C$ (area, cost), higher $f$ (switching loss, [[q0216]]), interleaving (more parts), filters (DC drop, loop interaction). A rail that feeds a digital core with 3% tolerance and a sleeping RF section does not justify an LC and LDO chain.

## How to set a ripple spec

1. List loads on the rail and find the most sensitive (ADC reference? PLL? digital core?).
2. Take the allowed supply window and subtract DC accuracy, transient droop and IR drop; the remainder is the ripple budget.
3. For analog and RF, compute the coupling (PSRR at $f_{sw}$, pushing) and set the ripple so the effect is below the noise floor, or isolate the load with a filter or LDO.
4. Choose the cheapest set of levers that meets the spec ([[q0215]]).

## Worked example: how much ripple can a 16-bit ADC tolerate?

Turn the abstract "ripple must be small enough" into a number. A 16-bit ADC with a 3.3 V full scale has $\text{LSB}=3.3/65{,}536=50.4\ \mu$V. If you want supply-induced error below half an LSB, that is 25 µV at the output. The ripple allowed at the supply is that error divided by the supply rejection at the ripple frequency:

| PSRR at 500 kHz | Allowed supply ripple (pk-pk) | Buck ripple (17 mV) is too big by |
|---|---|---|
| 60 dB | 25 mV | already fine |
| 40 dB | 2.5 mV | 17 mV / 2.5 mV = 6.8x, or 16.6 dB |
| 20 dB | 0.25 mV | 68x, or 36.6 dB |

PSRR figures in this table are illustrative. Real PSRR falls with frequency, so a part quoted at 80 dB at 1 kHz may be only 30 to 40 dB at 500 kHz. Read the PSRR-versus-frequency plot at the actual ripple frequency in the datasheet.

If the analog supply has 40 dB of rejection, the converter needs another 16.6 dB, so you add an LC filter after the buck or an LDO with good high-frequency PSRR. Take a 1 µH inductor and 10 µF capacitor. The corner is $f_c=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})=50.3$ kHz, and at 500 kHz the ideal attenuation is $1/((f/f_c)^2-1)=1/98.8$, which is $-39.9$ dB. The 17 mV ripple becomes 0.17 mV, which leaves about 23 dB of margin beyond the 16.6 dB needed.

**Damping matters.** The same filter has a characteristic impedance $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}=0.32\ \Omega$. If the series resistance (inductor DCR plus capacitor ESR) is only 25 mΩ, then $Q=Z_0/R=12.6$, and the filter peaks by about 22 dB at 50 kHz. A load step or a loop disturbance near that frequency will ring, and the filter can interact with the buck's control loop. Fix it with a damping network, which is a resistor in series with an extra bulk capacitor in parallel with the main one, or with a ferrite bead and capacitor whose loss is built in. The bead's DC resistance also costs voltage: 20 mΩ at 300 mA is 6 mV.

## Where the high-frequency spikes come from

The ripple on a scope is not the clean triangle in the textbook. Edges at SW swing 12 V in a few nanoseconds, and every stray capacitance across the inductor couples that edge to the output. Take an illustrative 5 pF of inductor winding capacitance and a 12 V swing in 3 ns, so $dv/dt=4$ V/ns. The injected current is $C\,dv/dt=5\ \text{pF}\times4\ \text{V/ns}=20$ mA. It flows into the output capacitor, whose impedance at 100 MHz is dominated by its inductance: with 0.5 nH of ESL, $|Z|=2\pi\times100\ \text{MHz}\times0.5\ \text{nH}=0.31\ \Omega$. The spike is therefore about $20\ \text{mA}\times0.31\ \Omega\approx6$ mV, which sits on top of the triangular ripple. It does not move with the output capacitance, and a bigger capacitor does nothing for it. What helps is an LC or bead filter, reducing edge rate with a gate resistor, shielding the inductor, or a smaller loop area. The ESL contribution from the inductor current slope itself is much smaller: $0.5\ \text{nH}\times(8.7\ \text{V}/4.7\ \mu\text{H})=0.9$ mV.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Winding capacitance, ESL and edge rate depend on the specific inductor, capacitor, layout and gate drive. The 5 pF, 0.5 nH and 3 ns values are assumed for the exercise. The method (current injected by $C\,dv/dt$ times the impedance at the ringing frequency) is general.

## Verifying ripple on the bench

1. **Probe with a tip and barrel.** Remove the probe's ground clip and spring. Touch the tip to the output capacitor pad and the barrel to its ground pad. A 15 cm ground lead picks up the switch-node edges and shows tens of millivolts that are not on the rail.
2. **Choose the bandwidth deliberately.** At 20 MHz bandwidth you see the triangle plus the first spike harmonics, which is what most loads respond to. At full bandwidth you also see 100 MHz ring that may or may not matter to your load. Record both settings.
3. **Use AC coupling carefully.** Offset the DC level instead if you want to see the ripple relative to the tolerance window. A 1 V rail with 17 mV ripple needs a 10 mV/div scale or better.
4. **Measure at the load.** Ripple at the converter's capacitor and ripple at the sensitive IC differ by the PCB's impedance and the local bypassing. Probe at the IC's pins.
5. **Correlate with the symptom.** If the ADC noise changes when you shift $f_{sw}$ by 10%, the buck is the source. If you can see the ripple frequency as a spur in an FFT of the ADC data, you have found the coupling path.
:::

::: applications
- **ADC and sensor supplies**: low-ripple rails or post-LDO to protect LSB-level accuracy.
- **PLL/VCO and RF transceivers**: strict spur limits on supplies.
- **Audio**: switching ripple and its harmonics kept out of the audible band and the amplifier supply.
- **High-speed digital (SerDes, DDR)**: supply-induced jitter and timing margin.
- **Battery chargers and instruments**: accurate measurements require low ripple at the sensing point.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear small-signal coupling** through PSRR and pushing; large ripple or slew-limited behavior may not follow.
- **Ripple frequency** equals $f_{sw}$ (single phase); interleaving moves it to $Nf$ where PSRR may differ.
- **Spur formula** assumes $\beta\ll1$ and ignores PLL loop suppression.
- **Typical values** (PSRR 20 to 60 dB, $K_p$ 5 MHz/V) vary widely; use datasheet data.
- **Tolerance budgets** depend on the load datasheet; some loads specify ripple separately.
:::

::: followups
### How does ripple become a spur in a PLL?
The VCO's frequency depends slightly on its supply (pushing). Ripple at 500 kHz modulates the VCO frequency at that rate, producing sidebands at plus and minus 500 kHz from the carrier at $\approx20\log(K_pV_r/2f_m)$ dBc. The PLL loop suppresses offsets inside its bandwidth, so spurs at offsets above the loop bandwidth (like 500 kHz) are the problem, which is why VCO supplies are usually LDO-regulated.

### Why does lower ripple reduce inductor core loss?
Core loss depends on the flux swing, proportional to the ripple current $\Delta I_L$. With a Steinmetz-type exponent $\beta\approx2.5$, halving $\Delta I_L$ cuts core loss by $2^{2.5}\approx5.7$. Also reduces AC winding loss. That's why larger inductors, despite higher DCR, can win efficiency at high frequency.

### The ADC is noisy only when the buck is running. What would you try?
Check whether the noise is at $f_{sw}$ or its alias: change $f_{sw}$ or the sample rate. Measure ADC supply and reference at the pin with a good probe. Add or improve the filter or LDO before the analog supply, improve the reference decoupling, and check ground return paths. If the noise follows the switching edges rather than the fundamental, suspect spikes and layout.

### If the load tolerates 100 mV of ripple, how would you design differently?
Use a smaller inductor and capacitor (30 to 60% ripple ratio), perhaps a lower-cost capacitor, even PFM at light load, and no post filter. You save area and cost, and possibly gain transient response with smaller $L$. Check peak current vs saturation and the EMI.

### Does reducing ripple always improve efficiency?
Not necessarily. A larger $L$ lowers RMS current and core loss but raises DCR; higher $f$ lowers ripple but increases switching loss. The net depends on the part ([[q0216]]).
