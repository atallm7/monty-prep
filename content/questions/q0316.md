::: testing
Whether you can sketch **both curves from memory and explain every feature physically**. Discharge: an immediate $IR$ step, a quick fall off the top, a long sloping plateau around 3.6 to 3.8 V, then a knee and a cliff toward the cutoff. Charge: constant current with rising voltage until 4.2 V, then constant voltage with an exponentially decaying current. You should also say how rate and temperature move the curves, and why a flat plateau makes voltage a poor fuel gauge.
:::

::: why
These two curves are the daily vocabulary of battery design: they decide regulator input ranges, brownout thresholds, charge time, fuel-gauge strategy and thermal load. Sketching them with correct numbers is a fast way for an interviewer to see whether you have actually watched a cell on a bench.
:::

::: concepts
- **OCV vs SoC**: the rested voltage curve is the backbone of both plots ([[q0305]]).
- **Terminal voltage**: $V_t = V_{OCV} \mp IR \mp V_{RC}$, minus for discharge, plus for charge ([[q0303]]).
- **C-rate**: scales the current and therefore the displacement of the curve ([[q0315]]).
- **CC-CV charging**: the standard Li-ion algorithm ([[q0321]]).
- **Cutoff and termination**: the stopping criteria (voltage on discharge, current on charge).
- **Temperature**: resistance rises in the cold and shifts both curves ([[q0306]]).
:::

::: answer
"For discharge, the voltage-versus-capacity curve starts at 4.2 volts for a full cell. As soon as a load is applied there's a step down by $I$ times the internal resistance, and then a quick fall of a couple hundred millivolts as the electrodes polarize. After that there's a long, gently sloping plateau, from about 3.9 down to about 3.6 volts, which holds most of the capacity, and then a knee near 3.4 and a steep cliff towards the 3.0 volt cutoff. A higher current slides the whole curve down by $IR$ and ends it earlier; the cold does the same more severely.

For charge, it's constant current, constant voltage. I apply a fixed current, 1C for example, and the voltage climbs from the rested value, with an $IR$ jump at the start, to 4.2 volts. In my model of a 2 amp-hour cell at 1C that takes about 51 minutes and gets the cell to roughly 85 percent. Then the charger holds 4.2 volts, and the current decays exponentially as the cell's open-circuit voltage approaches 4.2. When the current falls to about C over 20 or C over 10, it terminates. That tail adds about 26 minutes in the model for the last 15 percent.

The shape matters: the flat middle of the discharge curve makes voltage a poor state-of-charge indicator, and the CV tail is why the last 20 percent charges slowly."
:::

::: technical
**Discharge (CC).** $V_t(t) = V_{OCV}(SoC) - I R_0(SoC,T) - V_1(t)$, $\dot V_1 = I/C_1 - V_1/(R_1C_1)$, $SoC = 1 - \frac{1}{Q}\int I\,dt$. Regions (graphite/NMC, 25 °C):
1. Instant step $IR_0$ (0.1 V at 2 A and 50 mΩ).
2. Polarization drop over tens of seconds ($V_1$ builds up with $\tau \approx 40$ s in the model).
3. Plateau from about 90 % to 15 % SoC, voltage falling from 3.9 to 3.6 V at 1C.
4. Knee near 10 to 15 % SoC and the cliff below 5 %, where both OCV and resistance degrade.

Model values for a 2 Ah, 50 mΩ cell, cutoff 3.0 V: 0.2C delivers 1.989 Ah (7.46 Wh); 1C 1.951 Ah (7.08 Wh); 2C 1.887 Ah (6.58 Wh).

**Charge (CC-CV).** Phase 1 (CC): $I = I_{cc}$, $V_t = V_{OCV}(SoC) + I(R_0 + R_1) $ rising to 4.2 V. Phase 2 (CV): $V_t = 4.2$ V, so $I = (4.2 - V_{OCV}(SoC))/(R_0 + R_1)$, which decays because $V_{OCV}$ rises toward 4.2 V. Terminate when $I < I_{term}$ (C/20 to C/10 typical).

**Model numbers (2 Ah, 50 mΩ, from empty):** 1C: CC lasts 50.7 min and reaches 84.5 % SoC; total 76.9 min to C/20 termination; 0.5C: CC 111.7 min to 93 %, total 130.6 min; 2C: CC 19.5 min to 65 %, total 53.9 min. Higher CC currents save less time than expected because the CV tail grows.

**Temperature.** Cold raises $R$, so the discharge curve sags more and ends earlier; on charge $V_t$ reaches 4.2 V sooner, switching to CV at a lower SoC ([[q0306]]).
:::

::: long
"Let me take discharge first. Plot terminal voltage against capacity delivered. A fully charged cell rests at 4.2 volts. The instant I apply a load there's a step of $I$ times $R_0$: with 2 amps and 50 milliohms that's 0.1 volt. Then over a minute or so it drops a bit more as the electrodes polarize. After that I'm on the plateau: from roughly 3.9 volts down to 3.6 over most of the capacity, which is why 3.7 is the nominal figure. Near 10 to 15 percent remaining, there's a knee, and below that the voltage plunges to the 3.0 volt cutoff within a few percent of capacity. With a higher C-rate the whole curve is displaced down by $IR$ and ends a bit earlier, so in my model I get 1.99 amp-hours at 0.2C, 1.95 at 1C and 1.89 at 2C. In the cold, resistance is two to six times higher, and the curve sags a lot more.

Charge is the mirror image, with two phases. In constant current the charger forces, say, 1C, and the terminal voltage climbs from about 3.5 to 4.2 volts, including the $IR$ rise. At 1C that takes around 50 minutes and brings the cell to about 85 percent. Then it switches to constant voltage: the charger holds 4.2 volts, and the current falls roughly exponentially as the open-circuit voltage approaches 4.2, because current equals the voltage difference over the resistance. It stops when the current drops to C over 20 or so, about 26 more minutes for the last 15 percent.

Two takeaways: the plateau is so flat that voltage alone is a poor fuel gauge, and the CV tail is why going from 1C to 2C doesn't halve the total charge time."
:::

::: terms
- **Plateau**: the flat middle part of the discharge curve, about 3.6 to 3.8 V.
- **Knee**: the region where the discharge voltage starts to fall steeply, around 10 to 15 % SoC.
- **Cutoff voltage**: end-of-discharge voltage (about 3.0 V).
- **CC phase**: constant-current charge until the voltage limit.
- **CV phase**: constant-voltage charge with decaying current.
- **Termination current**: current threshold ending the CV phase (C/20 to C/10).
- **Polarization**: slow voltage change from diffusion and charge-transfer limits.
- **Hysteresis**: small difference between charge and discharge OCV paths.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** describes "voltage slowly drops then falls off a cliff; charge is CC then CV".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains each feature physically ($IR$ step, polarization with a time constant, flat plateau from graphite staging and cathode slope, cliff from electrode depletion).
- Quantifies the dependence on **rate and temperature** with numbers from a model or bench.
- Derives the CV current: $I = (V_{set} - V_{OCV})/R$, so it decays as OCV rises.
- Gives **time budgets** (CC vs CV time) and explains why higher C-rate yields diminishing returns.
- Draws the consequence for design: regulators must tolerate 3.0 to 4.2 V, gauges can't rely on the plateau voltage.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing the discharge curve as a straight line from 4.2 to 3.0 V.** It has a steep start, a flat middle and a steep end.
- **Forgetting the $IR$ step on both curves.** The step is instantaneous; the polarization part builds over tens of seconds.
- **Saying CV holds the current constant.** In CV the voltage is fixed and the current decays.
- **Terminating at 4.2 V.** Reaching 4.2 V only ends CC; the cell is about 85 % full there at 1C and needs the CV phase.
- **Plotting against time with no stated rate.** The discharge curve vs Ah depends on C-rate and temperature; label them.
- **Thinking the plateau is the nominal voltage's cause for "3.7".** It is an average of the curve, not a regulated value.
:::

Figure: At $t=0$ each curve steps down by $I R_0$ (0.02, 0.1 and 0.2 V for 0.2C, 1C and 2C; the first plotted point after zero also includes some polarization). Notice the plateau slope, the knee near 1.7 Ah, and how the higher rate shifts the curve down and ends it sooner.

Features, in order:
1. **Instant step** $I R_0$: ohmic resistance of electrolyte, foils and contacts. At 2 A and 50 mΩ, 0.1 V.
2. **Polarization**: the RC branch ($R_1 \approx 0.6 R_0$, $\tau \approx 40$ s in the model) adds a further $I R_1(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, about 0.06 V at steady state for 2 A.
3. **Plateau**: OCV falls slowly because graphite staging gives flat potentials and the cathode curve is shallow; this is the region with most of the energy.
4. **Knee and cliff**: OCV falls steeply below 10 % SoC and $R$ rises (the model uses a factor $1 + 0.6e^{-SoC/0.1}$), so the loaded voltage drops fast.
5. **Cutoff** at about 3.0 V: stop with a margin above the over-discharge region, where there is almost no energy left anyway.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The exact positions of knee and plateau vary by cell design and chemistry. The values above come from a representative equivalent-circuit model, not from measurement of a particular cell.

In CV the voltage is pinned and the current decays smoothly, reaching C/20 (0.1 A) at about 77 min. The simulator charge starts from an empty cell with no precharge stage for simplicity.

**CV-phase equation.** At $V_t = V_{set}$, $I(t) = \dfrac{V_{set} - V_{OCV}(SoC(t))}{R_0 + R_1(\cdot)}$, and $dSoC/dt = I/Q$. With $\dot{SoC} = I/Q$ and $dV_{OCV}/dSoC \approx 1.2$ V per unit SoC near full, $\dot I = -\frac{1}{R}\frac{dV_{OCV}}{dSoC}\frac{I}{Q}$, so $I$ decays exponentially with time constant $\tau_{CV} \approx R\,Q/(dV_{OCV}/dSoC)$. For this model cell $R \approx R_0 + R_1 = 0.08$ Ω, giving $0.08 \times 7200/1.2 = 480$ s (8 min), close to the roughly 9 min decay in the plot; a 0.1 Ω cell would give 600 s ([[q0321]]).

## Effect of rate and temperature

| Condition | Discharge effect | Charge effect |
|---|---|---|
| Higher C-rate | More $IR$ sag, earlier cutoff, less Ah and Wh | CC ends at lower SoC, longer CV tail |
| Cold (0 °C) | $R$ ×2.5: lower curve, 1.63 Ah at 1C | Hits 4.2 V sooner; charging forbidden below 0 °C |
| Aged cell | Higher $R$, lower capacity | Same: earlier CV, less capacity |
| Hot | Lower $R$, curve higher | Faster, but ages cell |

## Design consequences

- Regulators or loads fed from the cell must handle about 3.0 to 4.2 V, plus the sag at peak current.
- A voltage-only gauge sees a nearly flat 3.6 to 3.8 V over half of the capacity ([[q0308]]).
- Charge time is not inversely proportional to C-rate because of the CV tail; budgets should use the full profile.
- Surface temperature rises with $I^2R$ during both curves; the CV tail is gentle, the CC phase at high C is not.
:::

::: applications
- **Fuel-gauge tables**: the OCV curve used to initialize and correct SoC.
- **Regulator selection**: a buck-boost for 3.3 V rails covers the 3.0 to 4.2 V range ([[q0323]]).
- **Charger IC verification**: checking CC/CV transitions with a source meter or oscilloscope.
- **Battery characterization**: capture of rate-dependent discharge curves for product runtime models ([[q0311]]).
- **Low-battery warning thresholds**: set from the knee location at the product's typical current.
:::

::: assumptions
- Curves are from a **simplified equivalent-circuit model** (2 Ah, 50 mΩ, graphite/NMC OCV table, Arrhenius resistance) and are illustrative, not data for a particular cell.
- The charge starts from an **empty cell without a precharge phase**; real chargers precharge below about 3.0 V ([[q0320]]).
- Termination at **C/20** is assumed; many products use C/10, which shortens the tail.
- Hysteresis and relaxation after current stops are not drawn.
- Other chemistries differ: LiFePO$_4$ has a much flatter plateau at 3.2 to 3.3 V, with a sharper CV transition.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the voltage jump immediately when current is applied or removed?
The ohmic resistance ($R_0$: electrolyte, current collectors, contacts) responds instantly, so $\Delta V = I R_0$ appears at once. The slower part (polarization) is from diffusion and charge-transfer processes with time constants of tens of seconds, which is why the voltage keeps relaxing for minutes after the current stops.

### Why does CV current decay exponentially rather than linearly?
In CV, current is $(V_{set} - V_{OCV})/R$. As charge accumulates, $V_{OCV}$ rises toward $V_{set}$, which reduces the driving difference, and the rate of change of $V_{OCV}$ is proportional to the current. A decaying quantity whose decay rate is proportional to itself is exponential, with $\tau \approx RQ/(dV_{OCV}/dSoC)$, about 10 min for 100 mΩ and 2 Ah.

### How would the charge curve look in the cold?
$R$ is higher, so $V_t = V_{OCV} + I R$ hits 4.2 V at a lower SoC; the CV phase begins earlier and the cell is charged mostly under CV, which takes longer. More importantly, the anode potential is more negative in the cold and lithium plating becomes a risk, so charge current is reduced or charging is blocked below about 0 °C ([[q0306]]).

### If you double the charge current from 1C to 2C, does charge time halve?
No. In the model, 1C takes 77 min total, 2C takes 54 min, because CC only reaches 65 % SoC at 2C (vs 85 %) and the CV tail is longer. The gain is about 30 %, and the cell gets hotter ($I^2R$ is 4× larger) and experiences more plating risk.

### What would a LiFePO4 curve look like compared with this?
A much flatter plateau near 3.2 to 3.3 V over most of the capacity, with a steeper rise near 3.4 to 3.6 V at the top and a sharper cliff at the bottom. It is more tolerant of high-rate use, but SoC from voltage is nearly impossible in the middle.
:::
