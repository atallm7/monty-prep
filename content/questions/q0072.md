::: basics
### In plain words
A diode is a part that lets current flow easily in one direction and blocks it in the other. Inside, it is a piece of silicon that is P-type on one side and N-type on the other, joined together. The join forms a barrier. Pushing the right way lowers the barrier, and current flows. Pushing the wrong way raises it, and almost nothing flows.

### Picture it
Picture a turnstile at a subway gate, or a check valve in a water pipe. Push one way and it swings open, and people or water pass through. Push the other way and it locks shut. A diode does that for current. The analogy breaks down in one place: a real diode needs a small push, about 0.6 to 0.7 V in silicon, before it opens fully, like a stiff valve spring.

### Words you need
- **Anode and cathode**: the P side and the N side. Forward current flows anode to cathode.
- **Forward bias**: the anode is more positive than the cathode, which lowers the barrier.
- **Reverse bias**: the cathode is more positive, which raises the barrier.
- **Depletion region**: the carrier-free layer at the junction. See [[q0063]].
- **Forward voltage**: the drop across a conducting diode, about 0.7 V for silicon.

### The simplest example
Connect a 5 V source, a 1 kΩ resistor and a silicon diode in series, with the diode pointing the right way. The diode drops about 0.7 V, so the resistor gets 4.3 V and the current is 4.3 mA. Flip the diode around, and the current falls to a tiny leakage, nanoamps. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows how the barrier works inside, why the forward current rises about ten times for each 60 mV, and what breakdown is.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can explain the diode as a **physical device**, not just as "a one-way valve for current". The interviewer wants to hear the chain of cause and effect:

1. Joining P-type and N-type silicon makes carriers diffuse across, leaving a **depletion region** of fixed ions and a **built-in potential barrier**.
2. **Forward bias** lowers that barrier, so diffusion current grows *exponentially* with voltage. **Reverse bias** raises it, leaving only a tiny leakage current.
3. You can connect that physics to the **Shockley equation** and to the practical numbers (≈0.6–0.7 V for silicon, nA–µA of leakage, breakdown).

The hidden sub-skill is moving between levels: physics, equation, and how it behaves on a bench.
:::

::: why
Diodes are the simplest semiconductor device and the foundation for everything after them: a BJT is two PN junctions back to back, a MOSFET has body diodes and junction-isolated wells, LEDs and solar cells are diodes, and every ESD clamp on every IC pin is a diode. If you understand why the current is exponential in voltage, you already understand $V_{BE}$ in a BJT, the temperature coefficient of a bandgap reference and why a reverse-biased junction leaks more when hot. Interviewers use this as the opening question of the semiconductor section precisely because so much else builds on it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Doping and majority carriers**: N-type silicon has extra free electrons (donors such as phosphorus), P-type has extra holes (acceptors such as boron). See [[q0062]], [[q0065]], [[q0069]].
- **Diffusion**: carriers move from high concentration to low concentration, like ink spreading in water, with no electric field needed.
- **Drift**: carriers are pushed by an electric field; electrons against the field, holes along it. See [[q0068]].
- **Depletion region**: the zone near the junction stripped of mobile carriers, leaving fixed ionized dopant atoms that create an electric field. See [[q0063]].
- **Built-in potential**: the voltage across the depletion region at equilibrium, about 0.7–0.9 V in typical silicon junctions; it exactly balances diffusion against drift.
- **Boltzmann statistics**: the number of carriers with enough energy to climb a barrier of height $qV$ scales as $e^{-qV/kT}$. This is where the exponential comes from.
:::

::: answer
"A diode is a PN junction: a piece of silicon doped P-type on one side and N-type on the other. When you first form the junction, electrons from the N side diffuse into the P side and holes diffuse the other way, and they recombine. That leaves a thin region near the junction with no free carriers, just fixed ions: negative on the P side, positive on the N side. Those ions set up an electric field and a built-in potential barrier, and at equilibrium the field's drift exactly cancels the diffusion.

If I forward bias it, positive on the P side, I'm lowering that barrier. The number of carriers energetic enough to get over it goes up exponentially, so the current goes up by about ten times for every 60 millivolts. That's why a silicon diode looks like it 'turns on' at roughly 0.6 to 0.7 volts.

If I reverse bias it, I make the barrier taller and the depletion region wider. Now majority carriers can't cross, and the only current is a tiny leakage from minority carriers, nanoamps for a small-signal diode. Push the reverse voltage far enough and the field gets strong enough to cause avalanche or Zener breakdown.

So the one-line summary is: it conducts easily one way and blocks the other, and the forward current follows the Shockley equation, $I = I_S(e^{V/nV_T} - 1)$."
:::

::: technical
**Structure.** A PN junction: acceptor-doped (P, concentration $N_A$) region against donor-doped (N, $N_D$) region. The P terminal is the **anode**, the N terminal the **cathode**. Conventional current flows anode → cathode when forward biased.

**Equilibrium.** Majority carriers diffuse across the metallurgical junction and recombine, uncovering ionized dopants: $N_A^-$ on the P side, $N_D^+$ on the N side. The resulting field opposes further diffusion. Zero net current means drift balances diffusion, giving the built-in potential

$$V_{bi} = V_T \ln\!\left(\frac{N_A N_D}{n_i^2}\right),\qquad V_T = \frac{kT}{q} \approx 25.9\text{ mV at }300\text{ K}$$

For $N_A = 10^{17}$, $N_D = 10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$, $n_i = 10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$: $V_{bi} \approx 0.77$ V. You cannot measure $V_{bi}$ with a voltmeter; contact potentials at the metal terminals cancel it.

**Forward bias** ($V_D > 0$) reduces the barrier to $V_{bi} - V_D$. Minority-carrier concentrations at the depletion edges rise by $e^{V_D/V_T}$ (the "law of the junction"). Those injected carriers diffuse away and recombine, giving

$$I_D = I_S\left(e^{V_D/(nV_T)} - 1\right)$$

with $n \approx 1$–2 (ideality factor) and $I_S$ from about $10^{-15}$ A to $10^{-8}$ A depending on area, doping and material. With $I_S = 10^{-14}$ A and $n = 1$: 1 mA at 0.655 V, 10 mA at 0.714 V. Slope: 59.5 mV per decade at 300 K. At high current the series (bulk) resistance $R_S$ adds $I_D R_S$.

**Reverse bias** widens the depletion region ($W \propto \sqrt{V_{bi} + V_R}$) and current saturates at $-I_S$ plus generation current in the depletion region. Practical leakage: pA–nA for small-signal silicon at 25 °C, roughly doubling every ~10 °C.

**Breakdown** at $V_{BR}$: avalanche multiplication (higher $V_{BR}$) or band-to-band tunnelling, called Zener (below roughly 5 V, heavily doped junctions).

**Capacitance.** Reverse-biased: depletion capacitance $C_j = C_{j0}/(1+V_R/V_{bi})^m$. Forward-biased: diffusion capacitance $\propto I_D \tau_T$ from stored minority charge, which causes reverse-recovery time $t_{rr}$.
:::

Reverse bias raises the barrier and only a tiny leakage current flows, until breakdown. That's the Shockley equation: $I = I_S(e^{V/nV_T}-1)$."

::: long
"I'd start with the structure. A diode is a single piece of silicon that's P-type on one side, the anode, and N-type on the other, the cathode. The P side is full of holes and the N side is full of free electrons.

At the moment the junction forms, those carriers diffuse: electrons wander into the P side, holes into the N side, and they recombine. What they leave behind near the junction are ionized dopant atoms that can't move: negative acceptor ions on the P side, positive donor ions on the N side. That's the depletion region. Those fixed charges create an electric field pointing from N to P, and that field pushes carriers back. Equilibrium is reached when the drift from the field exactly cancels the diffusion. The voltage across that region is the built-in potential, around 0.7 to 0.9 volts for normal silicon doping.

Now apply forward bias, positive on the anode. That subtracts from the barrier. Carriers have a Boltzmann distribution of energies, so the fraction that can climb the barrier goes up exponentially as the barrier drops. That gives the Shockley equation, $I = I_S(e^{V/nV_T}-1)$, where $V_T$ is about 26 millivolts at room temperature. In practice the current increases ten times for every 60 millivolts or so, which is why a silicon diode looks like a switch that closes at about 0.6 to 0.7 volts, even though there's no real threshold.

Under reverse bias, the barrier gets taller and the depletion region gets wider. Majority carriers can't get across, so the only current is from minority carriers and thermal generation, which is tiny, like nanoamps. If you keep increasing the reverse voltage, the field eventually gets strong enough for avalanche multiplication or tunnelling, which is breakdown. Zener diodes are designed to operate there.

Two real-world details I'd add: the forward voltage drops about 2 millivolts per degree as temperature rises, and the junction has capacitance and stored charge, so a diode can't switch off instantly. That's reverse recovery, and it matters in switching power supplies."
:::

::: terms
- **Anode / cathode**: the P-side and N-side terminals. Conventional current flows anode → cathode in forward bias; the cathode is marked with a band on the package.
- **Depletion region (space-charge region)**: the carrier-free zone at the junction containing fixed ionized dopants.
- **Built-in potential $V_{bi}$**: the equilibrium barrier, $V_T\ln(N_AN_D/n_i^2)$, about 0.7–0.9 V in silicon.
- **Forward bias / reverse bias**: anode more positive than cathode / cathode more positive than anode.
- **Saturation current $I_S$**: the scale current in the Shockley equation; also roughly the ideal reverse leakage.
- **Thermal voltage $V_T$**: $kT/q$, 25.9 mV at 300 K.
- **Ideality factor $n$**: 1 for pure diffusion current, closer to 2 when recombination in the depletion region dominates.
- **Breakdown voltage $V_{BR}$**: reverse voltage where avalanche or tunnelling makes current rise sharply.
- **Reverse recovery $t_{rr}$**: time to remove stored minority charge when switching from forward to reverse.
:::

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains *why* there's a barrier: diffusion, recombination, uncovered fixed ions, field, equilibrium between drift and diffusion.

- Points out that 0.7 V is not a hard threshold but a consequence of the exponential and the current level: "10× current per ~60 mV".
- Gives reverse-bias numbers (nA leakage, doubling every ~10 °C) and names both breakdown mechanisms.
- Mentions one real consequence: −2 mV/°C forward-voltage tempco, junction capacitance, or reverse recovery.
- Uses the correct anode/cathode language and draws the symbol with the bar on the cathode.

::: mistakes
- **"Electrons flow from anode to cathode."** Conventional current flows anode → cathode in forward bias; electrons go the other way. Say "conventional current" explicitly.
- **"The diode has a 0.7 V threshold and zero current below it."** The I–V curve is exponential with no sharp threshold. At 0.5 V a silicon diode still conducts microamps; 0.7 V is just where typical mA currents occur.
- **"The depletion region is an insulator made of nothing."** It contains fixed ionized dopant atoms. Their charge is what creates the field and the barrier.
- **"Forward bias removes the depletion region."** It narrows it and lowers the barrier; it doesn't vanish. You can't forward bias past $V_{bi}$ in practice because series resistance takes up the extra voltage.
- **"You can measure the built-in voltage with a DMM."** At equilibrium the contact potentials at the metal–semiconductor contacts cancel it, so the terminal voltage is zero.
- **"Reverse current is zero."** It's small but real, strongly temperature dependent, and matters in high-impedance or low-power circuits.
:::

::: deep
## From two slabs of silicon to a barrier

Start with N-type silicon: donors like phosphorus each contribute a free electron, so electrons are the majority carrier. P-type silicon uses acceptors like boron, each of which grabs an electron from the lattice and leaves a mobile **hole**. Each side on its own is electrically neutral: every free carrier is balanced by its parent ion.

Now put them together (in reality one crystal is doped differently on each side). There are about $10^{16}$ free electrons per cm³ on the N side and only about $10^4$ on the P side. That concentration difference drives **diffusion**: electrons spread into the P side, holes into the N side, and when they meet they recombine.

Every electron that leaves the N side leaves behind a positive donor ion that cannot move. Every hole that leaves the P side leaves a negative acceptor ion. So a thin layer near the junction becomes:

- **depleted** of mobile carriers, and
- **charged**: negative on the P side, positive on the N side.

Figure: At equilibrium the junction is surrounded by a carrier-free depletion region holding fixed ions: negative acceptors on the P side, positive donors on the N side. Their field points from N to P and pushes holes back to P and electrons back to N, opposing diffusion.

Those fixed charges set up an electric field pointing from the N side to the P side. The field pushes holes back toward P and electrons back toward N: that's **drift**, in the opposite direction to diffusion. Diffusion keeps going until the field is strong enough that drift cancels it exactly. At that point there's zero net current and a fixed voltage across the depletion region, the **built-in potential**:

$$V_{bi} = V_T\ln\!\left(\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}\right)$$

With $N_A = 10^{17}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$, $N_D = 10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$ and $n_i \approx 10^{10}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$ for silicon at 300 K:

$$V_{bi} = 0.02585 \times \ln(10^{13}) = 0.02585 \times 29.9 \approx 0.77\text{ V}$$

> [!NOTE]
> The built-in potential is real but you can't measure it at the terminals. When you connect metal leads, contact potentials appear at the two metal–silicon interfaces that exactly cancel $V_{bi}$ around the loop. If they didn't, a diode on a shelf would be a free battery.

## Forward bias: lowering the barrier

Apply a voltage $V_D$ with the anode positive. That voltage appears almost entirely across the depletion region (the neutral regions are good conductors), and it **subtracts** from the barrier: the barrier height becomes $V_{bi} - V_D$.

Why does this produce an *exponential* current? Carriers in the N region have a spread of thermal energies described by Boltzmann statistics. The fraction with enough energy to climb a barrier of height $q(V_{bi} - V_D)$ is proportional to $e^{-q(V_{bi}-V_D)/kT}$. Every time you lower the barrier by $V_T\ln 10 \approx 59.5$ mV, ten times more carriers can get over. This is the **law of the junction**: the minority-carrier concentration at the edge of the depletion region rises as $e^{V_D/V_T}$.

Those injected minority carriers (electrons in the P side, holes in the N side) diffuse away from the junction and recombine with majority carriers within a diffusion length. The current needed to keep supplying them is the diode current. Putting it together gives the **Shockley diode equation**:

$$I_D = I_S\left(e^{V_D/(nV_T)} - 1\right)$$

The $-1$ term is the reverse drift component that was there at equilibrium. At $V_D = 0$ the two cancel and $I_D = 0$, as they must.

### Worked example

Take a small silicon diode with $I_S = 10^{-14}$ A, $n = 1$, at 300 K ($V_T = 25.85$ mV). Solve for voltage: $V_D = nV_T\ln(I_D/I_S + 1)$.

| Current | Forward voltage |
|---|---|
| 1 µA | 0.476 V |
| 100 µA | 0.595 V |
| 1 mA | 0.655 V |
| 10 mA | 0.714 V |
| 100 mA | 0.774 V (plus $I R_S$ in reality) |

Six decades of current only move the voltage by about 0.36 V. That's why we can approximate a conducting silicon diode as "0.6–0.7 V" for almost any reasonable current, and why it *looks* like a switch with a threshold.

## Reverse bias: raising the barrier

Flip the polarity. Now the applied voltage **adds** to the barrier, $V_{bi} + V_R$, and the depletion region widens:

$$W = \sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_s (V_{bi}+V_R)}{q}\left(\frac{1}{N_A}+\frac{1}{N_D}\right)}$$

Majority carriers are blocked completely. The only carriers that cross are minority carriers that wander to the edge of the depletion region (electrons thermally generated in the P side, holes in the N side), plus electron-hole pairs generated thermally *inside* the depletion region. The field sweeps these across, so the current is set by how fast they're generated, not by the voltage. It saturates at about $-I_S$ (plus the generation term), which for a small-signal silicon diode is pA to nA at room temperature.

Two practical facts follow:

- **Leakage is very temperature sensitive.** It's proportional to $n_i^2$ (diffusion part) or $n_i$ (generation part), both of which climb steeply with temperature. A common rule of thumb is that leakage roughly doubles every ~10 °C.
- **Width depends on voltage, so capacitance does too.** The depletion region is a parallel-plate capacitor whose gap widens with $V_R$. That's how a varactor (tuning diode) works.

## Breakdown

The field in the depletion region grows with reverse voltage. Eventually one of two things happens:

- **Avalanche breakdown**: carriers gain enough energy between collisions to knock new electron-hole pairs loose (impact ionisation). Those new carriers do the same, and the current multiplies. Dominant for breakdown voltages above roughly 6 V.
- **Zener (tunnelling) breakdown**: in very heavily doped junctions the depletion region is so thin that electrons tunnel directly from the valence band on the P side into the conduction band on the N side. Dominant below roughly 5 V.

Breakdown isn't destructive by itself. A Zener diode lives there. It becomes destructive when the power $V_{BR}\cdot I$ overheats the junction. See [[q0077]] for the diode types built on this.

## What happens in real hardware

- **Series resistance.** At high current the bulk silicon and contact resistance $R_S$ (tens of mΩ for a power diode, ohms for a small-signal one) add $I_DR_S$, so the curve straightens out instead of staying exponential.
- **Temperature.** At fixed current, $V_F$ falls about 2 mV/°C. $I_S$ rises much faster with temperature than $e^{V/V_T}$ falls. This is why a diode or $V_{BE}$ junction is used as a temperature sensor inside ICs, and why paralleled diodes can thermally run away.
- **Stored charge.** In forward conduction, injected minority carriers are stored in the neutral regions. To turn the diode off, that charge must be removed, so for a short time the diode conducts in reverse: **reverse recovery**. A 1N4148 recovers in a few ns; a 1N4007 rectifier takes microseconds; Schottky diodes have essentially no minority-carrier storage. This matters a lot in switch-mode power supplies ([[q0197]]).
- **Schottky diodes** are a different structure: a metal-semiconductor junction instead of PN. The barrier is lower, so $V_F$ is about 0.2–0.45 V, and conduction is by majority carriers only. See [[q0078]].

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A clean structure for the answer is: **structure → equilibrium (depletion, barrier) → forward bias (barrier lowered, exponential) → reverse bias (barrier raised, leakage) → breakdown → one real-world consequence.** It takes about a minute and touches everything the interviewer is listening for.
:::

::: applications
- **Rectification**: four 1N4007-class diodes in a bridge turn the AC from a mains transformer into pulsating DC ([[q0160]]).
- **Reverse-polarity protection**: a series Schottky diode on a battery input blocks current if the battery is inserted backwards, at the cost of ~0.3–0.5 V drop.
- **Flyback / freewheeling**: a diode across a relay coil gives the inductor current a path when the transistor turns off ([[q0144]]).
- **ESD protection**: every CMOS input pin has diodes to VDD and GND that clamp transients; TVS diodes on connectors do the same at board level.
- **Temperature sensing**: the −2 mV/°C forward-voltage tempco of a junction is how on-die temperature sensors work.
- **Light**: LEDs emit photons when carriers recombine across a direct bandgap; photodiodes run the process backwards.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal-diode (Shockley) model**: assumes low-level injection, no recombination in the depletion region ($n = 1$), and negligible series resistance. Real diodes show $n$ closer to 2 at low currents, and $R_S$ dominates at high currents.
- **Room temperature** (300 K, $V_T \approx 26$ mV). Forward voltage and especially leakage depend strongly on temperature.
- **Silicon PN junction.** Germanium, Schottky (metal–semiconductor), and wide-bandgap diodes (SiC, GaN, LEDs) work on the same principle but have very different forward voltages and leakage.
- **DC / quasi-static behaviour.** At high frequency or fast switching, junction capacitance and stored charge matter.

An alternative valid way to explain it is with **energy-band diagrams**: forward bias reduces the step in the conduction band between N and P; reverse bias increases it. If the interviewer has a physics background, offering that view is a plus.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the current exponential in voltage and not linear like a resistor?
Because current is set by how many carriers can get over the barrier, and carrier energies follow a Boltzmann distribution. The fraction above an energy barrier of height $qV_B$ is $\propto e^{-qV_B/kT}$. Forward bias lowers $V_B$ linearly, so current rises exponentially: about 10× per 59.5 mV at 300 K. A resistor has no barrier; its current is limited by scattering, which gives a linear $I = V/R$.

### What happens to the forward voltage when the diode heats up?
At constant current it drops by about 2 mV/°C for silicon. Going from 25 °C to 125 °C, a diode that read 0.65 V at 1 mA reads roughly 0.45 V. The cause is $I_S$, which grows very steeply with temperature (∝ $n_i^2$), outweighing the increase in $V_T$. This is used for temperature sensing and is the root of thermal runaway when diodes or BJTs are paralleled without ballast resistors.

### What sets the reverse leakage current, and when does it matter?
Thermal generation of minority carriers near and inside the depletion region. It's $I_S$ plus a generation current proportional to depletion volume, so it grows with junction area, reverse voltage (slightly) and especially temperature. It matters for battery-powered circuits (µA of Schottky leakage can drain a coin cell), sample-and-hold or peak detectors where leakage discharges a capacitor, and high-impedance sensor inputs. Schottky diodes leak far more than PN diodes, often tens to hundreds of µA at high temperature.

### What happens if you forward bias a diode with no resistor in series?
Because $I$ is exponential in $V$, a small voltage source above ~0.8 V would push enormous current, limited only by series resistance and the source's own resistance. The diode overheats and fails (usually shorts, then may fuse open). Always set diode current with a resistor or a current source; that's why LEDs need a series resistor ([[q0806]]).

### If forward bias lowers the barrier by V_D, why can't you apply more than V_bi and remove the barrier entirely?
Long before $V_D$ approaches $V_{bi}$, the current is so large that the voltage drops across the neutral regions and contacts ($I R_S$) take up any extra applied voltage, and high-level injection changes the physics. In practice terminal voltages above ~1 V on a small silicon diode just mean more $IR_S$ drop and more heat; the junction voltage itself stays below $V_{bi}$.

### How is a Schottky diode different physically?
It's a metal–semiconductor junction, not a PN junction. The barrier height is set by the metal's work function relative to the semiconductor, and it's lower than a PN barrier, so $V_F$ is about 0.2–0.45 V. Current is carried by majority carriers (thermionic emission), so there's no stored minority charge and essentially no reverse recovery. The price is higher leakage and usually lower reverse-voltage ratings. See [[q0077]] and [[q0078]].
