::: basics
### In plain words
Can you put a via in a surface-mount solder pad? Yes, but you have to handle it. An open via pulls molten solder down the barrel during soldering. That starves the joint and can leave bubbles. It is fine if the via is filled and capped, or is a tiny laser-drilled microvia. It is also normal on a chip's thermal pad.

### Picture it
Imagine gluing two boards together when one has a hole drilled in the middle of the glued area. The glue runs through the hole, and the joint is left dry. Melted solder acts the same way: it flows into narrow channels. Filling and capping the hole fixes it, and so does moving the via beside the pad.

### Words you need
- **Via-in-pad**: a via placed directly inside a solder pad.
- **Dogbone**: a short trace that connects a pad to a via set beside it.
- **Thermal pad**: the exposed metal pad under a chip, used to carry heat into the board.
- **Fill and cap**: fill the barrel, sand it flat, and plate copper over it. See [[q0578]].

### The simplest example
An open 0.3 mm via through a 1.6 mm board holds about 0.079 mm³, and one 0.3 mm solder ball holds about 0.014 mm³. So one open via can drain about five balls' worth of solder. On a chip's thermal pad, vias are still wanted to carry heat. Nine hollow 0.3 mm vias together pass heat at about 21 °C per watt. For a 2 W chip that is about 43 °C of temperature rise across them. Sixteen vias give about 24 °C. To keep solder from draining, use small vias, close them from the back, and print the solder paste in small patches.

That is the whole idea: allowed, but only with a plan for the solder. The rest of this page covers wicking, thermal pads and small parts.
:::

::: testing
Whether you answer "yes, but" instead of a flat yes or no. The interviewer wants the condition and the reason:

1. Physically you can; the CAD tool allows it. The issue is **solder wicking** into an open barrel.
2. The standard fixes: **fill and cap** (via-in-pad plated over), a **dogbone** off the pad, **tenting/plugging** on the far side, or **small microvias**.
3. The cases where it is normal: exposed thermal pads (QFN, power packages), fine-pitch BGAs, tiny passives in dense designs.
4. The costs and the risks (tombstoning on small passives, void content, fab cost).
:::

::: why
Dense boards often leave no room beside a pad for a via, and thermal and decoupling performance both improve when the via is right at the pad. But a careless via-in-pad is one of the classic assembly defects: starved joints, voids and opens that only X-ray reveals. Knowing the rule and its exceptions is routine for any engineer who has had a board assembled.
:::

::: concepts
- **Solder wicking**: capillary action pulls molten solder into an open barrel. See [[q0578]].
- **Filled and capped via**: the barrel is filled, planarised and plated over so the pad is flat.
- **Dogbone / fanout**: a short trace from the pad to an adjacent via.
- **Thermal pad vias**: arrays under exposed pads that carry heat to inner/bottom copper.
- **Tombstoning**: a small chip component stands on one end because the two pads pull unequally during reflow.
- **Via closure types**: tent, plug, fill, cap. See [[q0579]].
:::

::: answer
"Yes, you can, but you have to handle it. If the via is open, then during reflow the molten solder runs down the barrel by capillary action. The joint is left starved, there are voids, and sometimes solder shows up on the other side of the board. So the answer is yes if the via is filled and capped, so the pad is flat again, or if the vias are small enough and the process is designed for it, like laser microvias.

The place it's completely normal is the exposed thermal pad of a QFN or a power package: you want an array of vias there to carry heat into a ground plane. Even then, I'd use small vias, tent or plug them from the back so solder doesn't escape, and use a segmented paste pattern so the part doesn't float on a pool of solder.

For fine-pitch BGAs where there's no room for a dogbone, I'd use filled and capped via-in-pad. For small passives like 0402s I'd be careful, because a via in only one pad pulls solder away asymmetrically and can tombstone the part.

If there's room, the cheaper answer is to move the via off the pad with a short trace and cover it with mask."
:::

::: technical
**Rule.** An open via in a solder pad wicks solder. Volume check: a 0.3 mm drill (0.25 mm finished) through 1.6 mm holds about 0.079 mm³, versus about 0.014 mm³ for a 0.3 mm ball, so one via can drain around five balls' worth.

**Acceptable ways.**
1. **Filled and capped** (IPC-4761 type VII): flat, solderable pad. Standard for BGA via-in-pad ("VIPPO").
2. **Microvia in pad**: laser vias (~0.1 mm) hold about 0.0008 mm³, copper-filled in HDI.
3. **Thermal pad vias**: small drills (0.3 mm or less), tented or plugged from the back (non-component) side, with a windowed stencil on the pad.
4. **Dogbone** off-pad via with mask: no process add-on if space allows.

**Thermal pad example.** Nine hollow 0.3 mm vias are about 21 K/W in parallel (vias alone); sixteen are about 12 K/W; copper-filled ones about three times lower.

**Small passives.** An open via in one pad of a 0402 steals solder from that side and can tombstone it. Avoid, or use fill and cap and keep both pads symmetric.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Maximum via drill for thermal pads, paste-reduction percentage (often quoted around 50-80 % coverage) and void acceptance limits come from the component datasheet, the assembly house and the IPC class. Follow the package's land-pattern guidance.
:::

::: short
"Yes, but not open. An open via in a pad wicks solder away and gives starved joints and voids. So I either fill and cap it, use laser microvias, or move it off the pad with a short dogbone and tent it. The normal exception is the thermal pad of a QFN or power part, where I use an array of small vias, tented on the back, with a windowed paste pattern."
:::

::: long
"You physically can, and in dense designs you often have to, but an open via in a solder pad is a classic assembly defect. During reflow the solder melts and capillary action pulls it down the barrel. A 0.3 millimetre via through a 1.6 millimetre board holds about 0.08 cubic millimetres, which is around five 0.3 millimetre solder balls. So the joint is starved, voided, or the solder comes out the back.

There are four ways I handle it. One, filled and capped via-in-pad: the fab fills the barrel and plates copper over it, so the pad is flat. That's what I use under fine-pitch BGAs. Two, laser microvias, which are so small they hold almost no solder. Three, move the via off the pad with a short dogbone trace and cover it with mask, the cheapest option when there's room. Four, for the exposed thermal pad of a QFN or power package, an array of small vias is intended; I'd tent or plug them from the back and use a segmented paste pattern so the package doesn't float.

Small passives are the special case: a via in only one pad of an 0402 drains one side and can tombstone it.

The tradeoff is fab cost and process risk against board area, so I only use via-in-pad where I have to."
:::

::: terms
- **Via-in-pad (VIPPO)**: via placed in a pad, normally filled and plated over.
- **Dogbone**: pad-to-via connecting trace.
- **Thermal pad / exposed pad**: bottom metal of a package that conducts heat.
- **Paste windowing**: splitting stencil openings on a large pad into smaller apertures.
- **Tombstoning**: a chip part lifting on one end during reflow.
- **Voiding**: gas pockets in a solder joint, seen on X-ray.
- **Tent / plug**: closure methods for vias. See [[q0579]].
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Yes but it wicks solder so fill it."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies the wicked volume against a ball.
- Lists the four remedies with when each fits, and names type VII.
- Treats thermal pads as the normal case, with via size, back-side tenting and paste windowing.
- Raises tombstoning on small passives.
- Defaults to the cheaper dogbone when there is room.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Never put a via in a pad."** Thermal pads and BGAs routinely need it.
- **Plugged instead of filled-and-capped.** A plug isn't flat. See [[q0579]].
- **Full-coverage paste on a thermal pad with open vias.** Solder floods, package floats and voids.
- **Via in one pad of a small passive.** Causes asymmetry and tombstoning.
- **Not saying it on the fab drawing.** Specify filled and capped explicitly.
:::

## Why it's a problem

Molten solder wets copper and is pulled into narrow channels. A plated barrel is exactly that. Consequences: starved joints, voids, solder on the far side (which can short or interfere with fixtures), and flux trapping.

## Thermal pad design

For a 2 W package through nine 0.3 mm hollow vias (about 21 K/W), the vias alone give about 43 K of temperature drop; sixteen vias give about 24 K; copper-filled nine give about 13 K. Real heat also spreads laterally. So via count and fill matter, but the planes carry most of the heat to ambient.

## The physics of wicking, with numbers

Solder in the molten state wets clean copper and has a surface tension of very roughly 0.4–0.5 N/m (it varies with alloy, flux and temperature). A plated barrel with a finished radius $r$ is a capillary tube, and the pressure driving solder into it is

$$\Delta P=\frac{2\gamma\cos\theta}{r}$$

For $\gamma\approx0.5$ N/m, a contact angle near zero (good wetting) and $r=0.125$ mm (a 0.25 mm finished hole):

$$\Delta P\approx\frac{2(0.5)}{0.125\times10^{-3}}=8\ \text{kPa}$$

Gravity is no help: the hydrostatic head of a 1.6 mm column of solder ($\rho\approx7400$ kg/m³) is $\rho g h\approx116$ Pa, about 70 times smaller. Capillary action wins by a wide margin, and a smaller hole pulls harder, because $\Delta P\propto1/r$. That is why a laser microvia (smaller, and much shorter) can hold little solder while a mechanically drilled via of the same drill size will drain.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Surface tension and contact angle depend on alloy, flux activity and temperature, and the finished-hole radius depends on plating thickness. The 8 kPa figure shows the order of magnitude, not a process limit.

## A solder budget for one pad

Here is why "just a little wicking" isn't harmless. Take a 0402 land of 0.5 mm × 0.5 mm printed through a 0.1 mm stencil. Paste is about half metal by volume, so the pad is printed with roughly

$$0.5\times0.5\times0.1\times0.5\approx0.0125\ \text{mm}^3$$

of solder alloy. An open 0.25 mm hole holds up to $\frac{\pi}{4}(0.25)^2(1.6)=0.0785$ mm³, over six times the entire pad's solder. Even if the solder only runs 0.3 mm down the barrel before it freezes, that is $\frac{\pi}{4}(0.25)^2(0.3)=0.0147$ mm³, more than the pad had to begin with. So it doesn't take a clean "drain": any wicking at all is a large fraction of the joint volume. The BGA numbers are no better (a 0.3 mm ball is 0.014 mm³).

A joint with a fraction of its solder lost is thinner (lower standoff), has a smaller fillet, and is more vulnerable to thermal-cycling fatigue, even when it looks acceptable electrically on the day it is built.

## Via-in-pad is sometimes about electrical performance

The reason an engineer takes on the cost of fill and cap for a decoupling capacitor is inductance. The capacitor's series resonance is $f_{SRF}=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, where $L$ includes the capacitor ESL plus the mounting loop (pad, dogbone, via, and the plane gap). For a 100 nF capacitor:

| Total series $L$ | $f_{SRF}$ | $\lvert Z\rvert$ at 100 MHz (inductive part) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.3 nH | 29 MHz | 0.19 Ω |
| 0.7 nH | 19 MHz | 0.44 Ω |
| 1.5 nH | 13 MHz | 0.94 Ω |
| 3 nH | 9.2 MHz | 1.9 Ω |

## The paste window arithmetic

On an exposed pad, the target is a paste coverage that gives a controlled solder volume without flooding. With a 3.2 mm square pad (10.24 mm²) and four 1.2 mm square windows, the coverage is

$$\frac{4(1.2)^2}{(3.2)^2}=\frac{5.76}{10.24}=56\%$$

which is inside the commonly quoted range of roughly half to three quarters (the actual number comes from the package land-pattern guidance). The gaps between windows serve three jobs: they let flux gas escape (lower voiding), they keep paste away from the via holes, and they leave channels so the part sits down evenly as the paste collapses.

## Closing the via from the back is not the same as filling it

IPC-4761 distinguishes tenting (type I/II), plugging (type III/IV), filling (type V/VI), and filling-and-capping (type VII). A tented via is covered by a mask film that may crack or leave a void; a plugged via is blocked with ink or resin, but the top surface is not flat or solderable. For a pad that must be soldered over the via, only type VII gives a flat plated surface. Putting "tent both sides" on the drawing is not enough; the fab note must say "filled and capped (IPC-4761 type VII) for these pads" and name the pads or the drill size.

## What to look for on the bench

1. **Before assembly**: ask the fab for a micro-section of a via-in-pad on the panel coupon, and check that the cap is flat and the fill has no voids that would gas out during reflow.
2. **After assembly**: X-ray the BGA or QFN to see voids under the thermal pad, and compare with the package vendor's acceptance limit. A void clustered above a via is a sign that the closure failed.
3. **Process change**: if a lot shows tombstoning or voids, the cause is usually a change in via closure, paste volume or profile, so compare lot to lot before blaming the design.
4. **Electrical confirmation**: a thermal pad that is voided shows up as a higher junction temperature at the same dissipation, and a measurable rise in $\theta_{JA}$ if the part has an on-die sensor.

## Fine-pitch BGA

At 0.5 mm pitch, pad 0.3 mm, there's no room for a dogbone. Filled and capped via-in-pad puts the via straight under the ball. At 0.8 mm pitch you may have room for a dogbone with tented via, which is cheaper.

## Small passives

0402 pads are about 0.5 mm wide. A via in one pad of a decoupling cap shortens the loop (good for inductance, see [[q0575]]) but the unbalanced solder volume can tombstone it unless the via is filled and capped. Many designers use via-in-pad only on decoupling pads where inductance matters, with fill.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Say: "Open vias in pads are a no. Filled and capped vias in pads are a tool. I use them when density or inductance demands it and I tell the fab explicitly."

## Verification

X-ray after assembly to check voids; cross-section of first articles; follow the package's void limits.

- **QFN/DFN exposed pads**: thermal via arrays.
- **BGAs at 0.5 mm pitch and below**: filled via-in-pad.
- **Decoupling caps next to power pins**: via-in-pad to cut loop inductance.
- **Power MOSFET and regulator pads**: heat-spreading vias.
- **HDI phones**: copper-filled microvias in pads.

- Thermal figures count the vias only; spreading is ignored.
- Via-in-pad filled/capped capability varies by fab.
- Wave-soldered through-hole pads are a different case.

::: followups
### Why tent the thermal vias from the back?
So solder can't flow out the far side and so the package isn't drained. Back-side tent or plug keeps solder in the pad region.

### Why window the paste on a thermal pad?
A single large paste area floods, the package floats and tilts, and gas can't escape. Windows give controlled volume and escape channels for flux gas, lowering voids.

### Filled-and-capped via-in-pad costs more. When is a dogbone better?
Whenever there's room: pitch of 0.8 mm or more on BGAs and normal passives. It uses standard processes.

### How do you check for via-in-pad problems after assembly?
X-ray for voids and opens, and cross-section a sample. Compare void percentage with the datasheet or IPC limit.

### Scenario: 0402 decoupling caps tombstone on one build lot only. What changed?
Likely paste or via-fill: open or poorly capped vias in one pad. Inspect fill quality, check stencil aperture and reflow profile, and move to filled/capped or off-pad vias.
:::
