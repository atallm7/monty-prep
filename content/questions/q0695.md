::: testing
Whether you can define jitter precisely (deviation of edge timing from ideal), distinguish the **types** (period, cycle-to-cycle, TIE; random versus deterministic) and the **sources**, and say what it breaks: setup/hold budget, ADC SNR, serial-link BER. A good answer gives one or two quantitative links: $\sigma_t = \sigma_v/SR$ for noise-to-jitter conversion, $TJ = DJ + 2Q\sigma_{RJ}$ for total jitter at a BER, and $SNR = -20\log_{10}(2\pi f_{in}\sigma_t)$ for ADC sampling. Bonus for saying how it's measured (TIE histogram, bathtub, phase noise) and how it shows up in an eye ([[q0703]]).
:::

::: why
Every timing-critical interface ends up as a statement about jitter: a clock that wobbles reduces setup/hold margin, a sampling clock that wobbles limits an ADC's dynamic range, and a data stream with jitter closes its eye and raises BER. At multi-gigabit rates the jitter budget *is* the design. Interviewers ask this because it connects noise, PLLs, power integrity and SI in one concept, and it separates people who have only seen clocks as ideal squares from people who have measured them.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ideal vs real edge times**: an ideal clock has edges at $nT$; a real one has $nT + \Delta t_n$. See [[q0687]].
- **Noise-to-jitter conversion**: amplitude noise at the threshold shifts the crossing by $\sigma_v/SR$. See [[q0689]].
- **Setup/hold budget**: jitter subtracts directly. See [[q0693]].
- **Random versus deterministic**: unbounded Gaussian (RJ) versus bounded (DJ) components.
- **Eye diagram**: jitter is the horizontal spread of crossings. See [[q0698]] and [[q0703]].
- **PLL / clock source**: phase noise integrates to jitter.
:::

::: answer
"Jitter is the deviation of a signal's edges from their ideal positions in time. If you have a 100 MHz clock, the edges should land every 10 nanoseconds exactly; in reality each one is a little early or late, by picoseconds.

There are a few ways to quote it. Period jitter is the variation in the length of one period; cycle-to-cycle jitter is how much consecutive periods differ; and TIE, time interval error, is the error of each edge against an ideal reference clock. It's also split into random jitter, which is Gaussian from thermal and shot noise and has unbounded tails, and deterministic jitter, which is bounded and comes from things like inter-symbol interference, duty-cycle distortion, crosstalk and supply noise at specific frequencies.

It comes from noise on the signal turning into timing error at the threshold, supply and ground noise modulating the delay of buffers and PLLs, crosstalk and reflections, and the source oscillator's phase noise.

It matters because it eats into the timing budget, limits the SNR of ADC sampling, and closes the eye on a serial link so BER goes up. I'd measure it as a TIE histogram on a scope, and for links, as total jitter at a specified BER."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.** Let $t_n$ be the actual time of the $n$th edge and $nT_0$ the ideal.
- **TIE (phase jitter)**: $x_n = t_n - nT_0$ (against an ideal or recovered reference).
- **Period jitter**: $J_{per,n} = (t_{n+1}-t_n) - T_0$.
- **Cycle-to-cycle jitter**: $J_{cc,n} = (t_{n+1}-t_n) - (t_n - t_{n-1})$. For uncorrelated white period jitter $\sigma_{cc} = \sqrt2\,\sigma_{per}$.
- **Accumulated (long-term) jitter** grows with the number of cycles for an open-loop oscillator, which is why a PLL with a reference is used.

**Components.** Total jitter $TJ = RJ + DJ$:
- *RJ*: Gaussian, unbounded; quoted as rms $\sigma_{RJ}$. Peak-to-peak depends on the number of observations.
- *DJ*: bounded; includes data-dependent jitter (ISI), duty-cycle distortion, periodic jitter (e.g. from a switching regulator), and bounded uncorrelated jitter (e.g. crosstalk).

**Total jitter at a BER (dual-Dirac model):**

$$TJ(BER) = DJ_{\delta\delta} + 2\,Q(BER)\,\sigma_{RJ}$$

with $Q = 7.03$ for BER $10^{-12}$ (so $2Q \approx 14.07$). For $DJ = 0.10$ UI and $\sigma_{RJ} = 0.01$ UI: $TJ = 0.10 + 0.1407 = 0.241$ UI. At 10 Gb/s ($UI = 100$ ps) that's 24.1 ps.

**Noise to jitter:** $\sigma_t = \sigma_v/SR$. With 15 mV rms noise on a 3.3 V, 1 ns (10–90%) edge, $\sigma_t = 5.7$ ps.

**Phase noise to jitter:** $\sigma_t = \dfrac{1}{2\pi f_0}\sqrt{2\int \mathcal{L}(f)\,df}$. If integrated single-sideband phase noise over the band of interest is $-60$ dBc, a 100 MHz clock has $\sigma_t = \sqrt{2\cdot10^{-6}}/(2\pi\cdot10^8) = 2.25$ ps.

**Impact on ADC SNR:** the jitter-limited SNR for a full-scale sine at $f_{in}$ is $SNR = -20\log_{10}(2\pi f_{in}\sigma_t)$. With $f_{in} = 100$ MHz and $\sigma_t = 1$ ps: 64.0 dB, about 10.3 effective bits.
:::

::: long
"Jitter is how much a signal's edges deviate from where they should be in time. If I have a 100 megahertz clock, in an ideal world the edges land at exact multiples of 10 nanoseconds. In reality each edge is a few picoseconds early or late.

There are several ways of quantifying it. Period jitter is the variation of each period from the average. Cycle-to-cycle jitter is the difference between adjacent periods, and for uncorrelated jitter that's about 1.4 times the period jitter. TIE, time interval error, is each edge's offset from an ideal reference clock, and it's what you histogram on a scope or integrate from phase noise.

We split it into random and deterministic. Random jitter is Gaussian and unbounded, from thermal and shot noise in the oscillator and buffers; you quote it as an rms. Deterministic jitter is bounded and has an identifiable cause: inter-symbol interference from the channel, duty-cycle distortion, periodic jitter from a switching regulator, crosstalk. For a serial link you combine them as total jitter at a BER: deterministic plus about 14 sigma of random for $10^{-12}$.

The common sources are noise on the signal being converted to timing error, which is noise divided by slew rate, supply noise modulating buffer delay, reflections and crosstalk, and the oscillator's own phase noise.

The consequences: it subtracts from the setup and hold budget, it limits an ADC's SNR, since 1 picosecond on a 100 megahertz input caps you around 64 dB, and it closes the eye, raising BER. Fixes are clean supplies and decoupling, faster clean edges, better references, and equalization to reduce ISI."
:::

::: terms
- **Jitter**: deviation of edge timing from the ideal position.
- **TIE (time interval error)**: each edge's time offset versus an ideal reference.
- **Period jitter / cycle-to-cycle jitter**: variation in a period / difference between successive periods.
- **RJ / DJ / TJ**: random (Gaussian), deterministic (bounded) and total jitter.
- **UI (unit interval)**: one bit time; jitter on serial links is quoted in UI or ps.
- **Phase noise**: frequency-domain view of jitter, in dBc/Hz versus offset.
- **Bathtub curve**: BER versus sampling position across the UI.
- **Wander**: very low-frequency jitter (conventionally below about 10 Hz).
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer defines jitter as "timing variation" and lists a few causes. An exceptional answer separates **period, cycle-to-cycle and TIE**, **RJ versus DJ** (and why RJ's peak-to-peak depends on sample count), links jitter to **noise/slew-rate** with a calculation, and writes **TJ at BER** with the $Q$ factor and a number. It ties jitter to its consequences with equations: **ADC SNR** $-20\log_{10}(2\pi f\sigma_t)$ and eye width. It also knows measurement pitfalls (scope noise floor and trigger jitter, reference clock choice, integration band for phase noise) and the fix list (supply integrity, slew rate, equalization, low-jitter PLL).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Quoting jitter without saying which kind or bandwidth.** "5 ps jitter" could be rms or peak-to-peak, period or TIE, over any observation window. State all.
- **Treating peak-to-peak RJ as a fixed number.** Gaussian tails grow with sample count; use rms plus a BER-based $Q$ multiplier.
- **Confusing jitter with skew.** Skew is a static offset between two signals; jitter is a random or time-varying deviation of one.
- **Ignoring the measurement floor.** A scope's own jitter and the trigger path can dominate for ps-level clocks; use a phase-noise analyzer or a better reference.
- **Believing a faster clock always means more jitter.** The absolute jitter of a good PLL may be the same; what matters is jitter as a fraction of the UI or period.
- **Forgetting supply noise.** Power-supply-induced jitter (PSIJ) is a leading source in real boards; see [[q0704]] and [[q0705]].
:::

::: deep
## Edges that arrive when they feel like it

Imagine a clock with period $T_0 = 10$ ns. If each edge instead lands at $t_n = nT_0 + x_n$, then $x_n$ is the **TIE** sequence. Its histogram is the jitter distribution. Take the difference between successive edges to get the **period** sequence and its variation; difference those again to get **cycle-to-cycle** jitter.

For jitter that's uncorrelated from edge to edge (white phase noise), the period jitter is $\sigma_{per} = \sqrt2\,\sigma_{TIE}$ and the cycle-to-cycle jitter is $\sigma_{cc} = \sqrt2\,\sigma_{per}$. For correlated jitter (low-frequency wander from a supply ripple) TIE can be large while period jitter is tiny. That's why you must say which one you're quoting.

## Where jitter comes from

1. **Amplitude noise becomes timing noise** at the receiver threshold: $\sigma_t = \sigma_v/SR$. A slow edge converts the same noise into more jitter ([[q0689]]).
2. **Supply and ground noise modulates delay.** A CMOS buffer's delay changes by roughly a few ps per mV of supply wobble in some processes (device-dependent). A 10 mV, 500 kHz buck ripple on a clock buffer's supply appears as periodic jitter at 500 kHz. This is *power-supply-induced jitter*.
3. **Oscillator phase noise.** Thermal and flicker noise in a VCO or crystal oscillator; the PLL shapes it (VCO noise high-passed, reference noise low-passed).
4. **Inter-symbol interference.** Data-dependent edge shifts because the channel hasn't settled from the previous bits: deterministic jitter.
5. **Crosstalk and reflections.** An aggressor edge landing near the victim's crossing moves it; a reflection shifts the crossing time ([[q0694]]).
6. **Duty-cycle distortion.** Asymmetric rise/fall thresholds or delays make alternate edges early/late.

## Random and deterministic: a model you can calculate with

Most links use the **dual-Dirac model**: the jitter PDF is two Dirac impulses separated by $DJ_{\delta\delta}$, each convolved with a Gaussian of standard deviation $\sigma_{RJ}$. Then the probability of a crossing outside $\pm x$ gives the tail, and the width at probability $BER$ is

$$TJ(BER) = DJ_{\delta\delta} + 2Q(BER)\,\sigma_{RJ}$$

where $Q(10^{-12}) = 7.03$ is the Gaussian tail multiplier (the number of sigmas to the tail probability $10^{-12}$).

Random jitter's peak-to-peak keeps growing with observation time: a scope measuring 1000 edges sees about $\pm3.3\sigma$; at $10^{12}$ edges $\pm7\sigma$. This is why rms plus the $Q$ factor is the right way to specify it.

## What jitter costs you

**Setup/hold budget** ([[q0693]]): jitter on the capture clock and on the data subtracts from slack, typically peak jitter at the BER or cycle count of interest.

**ADC sampling.** A sine at $f_{in}$ has maximum slope $2\pi f_{in}A$; a sample-time error $\sigma_t$ becomes a voltage error $2\pi f_{in}A\sigma_t$, so

$$SNR_{jitter} = -20\log_{10}(2\pi f_{in}\sigma_t)$$

A 1 ps rms clock limits a 100 MHz input to 64 dB, roughly 10.3 effective bits, so sampling a 100 MHz IF with a 12-bit ADC needs a clock below about 0.3 ps rms. Jitter limits high-frequency sampling regardless of how good the ADC's resolution is.

**Serial links:** jitter narrows the eye horizontally ([[q0703]]) and moves the BER. Equalization removes ISI-type DJ; a low-jitter reference removes RJ.

## Measuring jitter

- **Oscilloscope TIE or period histogram** with a reference or recovered clock: easiest for tens-of-ps to ps. The scope's own noise floor (a few hundred fs to ps depending on model) sets a lower bound.
- **Phase-noise analyzer / signal-source analyzer**: for clocks and oscillators, down to fs; integrate $\mathcal{L}(f)$ over the band that matters (e.g. 12 kHz–20 MHz for some SONET-style definitions).
- **BERT with bathtub scan**: for links; sweeps the sampling point and plots BER versus position to extract TJ at a BER directly.
- **Real-time scope with jitter decomposition software**: separates RJ, DJ, PJ, DDJ; results depend on the algorithm and pattern length.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Jitter numbers depend on the measurement bandwidth, the reference-clock model and the algorithm used to separate RJ and DJ. Different instruments and standards define the integration band differently (for example 12 kHz to 20 MHz or 1.875 MHz to 20 MHz in some telecom conventions). Always quote the method with the number.
:::

::: applications
- **SerDes links (PCIe, USB, Ethernet, SATA)**: jitter budgets are split between transmitter, channel and receiver in the standards, with TJ at a stated BER.
- **ADC/DAC sampling clocks**: low-jitter oscillators and clock-cleaning PLLs protect SNR at high input frequencies.
- **PLL and clock-generator design**: loop bandwidth, VCO and reference quality are chosen to minimise integrated jitter.
- **DDR memory**: clock and strobe jitter directly eat the read and write timing windows.
- **Digital audio (I²S, S/PDIF)**: clock jitter produces sidebands in the audio DAC output.
- **LiDAR and time-of-flight systems**: timing jitter converts directly to range noise ($1$ ps $\approx 0.15$ mm of one-way range).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Jitter is stationary and mostly Gaussian** for the RJ calculations; non-stationary effects (a burst of supply noise) need different treatment.
- **Dual-Dirac DJ is an extrapolated model**: $DJ_{\delta\delta}$ is a fit parameter, not necessarily equal to the measured peak-to-peak DJ.
- **White jitter** for the $\sqrt2$ relationship between TIE, period and cycle-to-cycle jitter; correlated jitter (e.g. spread-spectrum clocking) breaks it.
- **ADC jitter formula** assumes a full-scale sine, ideal ADC otherwise, and Gaussian aperture jitter.
- Numbers (ps, UI, dBc) are illustrative; use your standard's definitions for compliance.
:::

::: followups
### What's the difference between period jitter and TIE and when do they differ a lot?
TIE is each edge's offset against an ideal clock; period jitter is the change in the length of a period. They are related by differencing. For white (uncorrelated) jitter the period jitter is about $\sqrt2$ times the TIE rms. For low-frequency jitter such as wander from a 1 kHz supply ripple, TIE can be large (many ps) while period jitter is tiny because adjacent edges move together.

### How does supply noise become jitter?
Gate delay depends on the supply voltage: higher supply means faster edges. A noisy supply modulates every buffer's delay, so the timing of edges moves with the noise. A clock buffer with sensitivity of about 0.5 ps/mV and 10 mV of supply ripple has about 5 ps of periodic jitter at the ripple frequency. The cure is a cleaner supply for clock buffers (LDO, ferrite plus decoupling), and a PLL with good supply rejection.

### What jitter would a 14-bit ADC sampling a 50 MHz input need so that jitter isn't the limit?
A 14-bit ADC has ideal SNR of $6.02\cdot 14 + 1.76 = 86$ dB. Solve $-20\log_{10}(2\pi\cdot 50\text{ MHz}\cdot\sigma_t) = 86$: $2\pi f\sigma_t = 10^{-86/20} = 5.0\times10^{-5}$, so $\sigma_t = 5.0\times10^{-5}/(3.14\times10^8) = 0.16$ ps rms. That's an exceptionally clean clock, which is why high-speed ADC boards use dedicated low-jitter oscillators.

### My scope shows 15 ps rms jitter on a clock I expect to be 2 ps. What do you check?
Instrument and setup first: scope noise floor and bandwidth, probe and trigger source, and whether I'm triggering on the signal itself (which hides jitter) versus a separate clean reference. Then the signal: noise on the edge divided by slew rate, supply ripple at the clock buffer, crosstalk from a nearby aggressor, and a slow edge (check rise time). I'd also compare with a phase-noise measurement.

### Is random jitter really unbounded? What does that mean for a spec?
Yes, a Gaussian has infinite tails, so peak-to-peak depends on how long you look. A spec needs a BER target: the allowed jitter at that probability. For $10^{-12}$ you use $\pm7.03\sigma$, i.e. $14.07\sigma_{RJ}$ total width; for $10^{-15}$ about $\pm7.94\sigma$.
:::

::: interpretations
"Jitter" in the SI section means **timing jitter on digital signals and clocks**. It also appears in ADC sampling (aperture jitter), PLLs (phase noise), and networking (packet delay variation). The core definition, timing deviation from ideal, applies across them; I've included the ADC and serial-link consequences, the two most asked-about ones.
