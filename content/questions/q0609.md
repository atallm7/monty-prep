::: testing
Whether you understand ICT as a *structural* test that measures the parts on the board in place, not a test of whether the product works. The interviewer wants the mechanism (a fixture with spring probes contacts test points; a tester measures each component with the rest of the circuit "guarded" out), the defect classes it catches (shorts, opens, wrong or missing parts, wrong values, reversed polarity), what it misses (function, timing, firmware, hidden marginal joints), the dependence on test access, and the economics (fixture cost, speed, volume).
:::

::: why
ICT is the workhorse of high-volume board test: it takes a few seconds and tells the technician exactly which component is bad. But it only works if you gave it access: a test pad on each net. Interns who design boards get asked to add test points ([[q0619]]) and to say "this net needs a probe pad", so knowing why ICT needs them, and why it also needs guarding and spacing, makes your layouts easier to test ([[q0614]], [[q0616]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **In-circuit measurement**: measuring a component while it is soldered into the board, surrounded by other parts.
- **Guarding**: forcing nodes to the same potential so that parallel paths carry no measurement current.
- **Bed-of-nails fixture**: array of spring probes ([[q0612]]).
- **Opens/shorts test**: measuring resistance between nets.
- **Analog component tests**: R, C, L, diodes, transistors, regulators.
- **Digital/boundary tests**: powered tests on ICs, vectorless coupling tests, boundary scan.
- **Test access**: a probeable pad on each net ([[q0621]]).
:::

::: answer
"In-circuit test is an electrical test of a populated board that checks whether each component is present, correct and properly soldered, by contacting the board with a fixture of spring-loaded probes, the bed of nails, on test points. A tester then measures each part in place.

The clever bit is measuring one component while it's surrounded by others. If I want to measure a 10 kΩ resistor that has another path in parallel, a naive measurement reads low. ICT uses guarding: it drives and senses at the resistor's nodes and grounds a guard node on the other path so no measurement current flows through it. That way you get the real value.

What it catches: shorts and opens on the nets, missing or wrong parts, wrong values outside tolerance, reversed diodes and capacitors, and bad solder joints that show up as opens. It can also do powered tests on some ICs, and with boundary scan it can test interconnect for pins it can't touch. What it doesn't tell you is whether the board works as a product, since it won't catch firmware, timing or system-level problems.

It's very fast, a few seconds per board, and it pinpoints faults to a component or net, so it's great at high volume. The costs are the fixture, thousands of dollars, and the need for test pads on every net, which affects layout."
:::

::: technical
**How a measurement works.** The fixture presses probes onto test pads. A switching matrix connects the measurement instrument to chosen nodes. For a two-terminal part between nodes A and B the tester forces a voltage (DC for resistors, small AC for capacitors and inductors) at A and measures current at B, which sits at a virtual ground at the input of a current-sensing op-amp. Any path from A to B through other parts would add current; guard probes ground intermediate nodes so those paths either carry no current (both ends at 0 V) or divert it to the guard.

**Worked guarding example.** Measure $R_1 = 10\ \text{k}\Omega$ between A and B, with a parallel path $R_2 = 4.7\ \text{k}\Omega$ then $R_3 = 10\ \text{k}\Omega$ through a middle node G. Unguarded, the tester sees $R_1\parallel(R_2+R_3)=10\parallel14.7=5.95\ \text{k}\Omega$ (computed), a 40% error that would fail a good part. With G grounded as a guard, $R_3$ sees 0 V at both ends (B is the virtual ground) so carries no current, and the current through $R_2$ goes to the guard; the sense current is exactly $V/R_1$ and the reading is 10 kΩ.

**Defects found.** Shorts (bridges, debris), opens (lifted leads, cold joints on probed nets), missing parts, wrong values, wrong orientation (diodes, electrolytics via polarity-sensitive tests), wrong part types (e.g. a different transistor beta/diode drop), and, with powered tests, some IC faults (bad pin, wrong device via leakage/protection-diode signature). **Vectorless** capacitive-coupling tests detect open pins on ICs (a sensor plate above the IC picks up a signal driven into the pin), while boundary scan covers JTAG-capable ICs.

**What it misses.** Function, timing, firmware, intermittent marginal joints, parts hidden or with no access, and values hidden by low-impedance parallel paths that guarding cannot isolate (for example, a 100 nF capacitor across a low-ohm path). Also small capacitors below roughly picofarad levels are hard to measure in the presence of stray capacitance.

**Access requirements (typical).** Test pads about 0.9 mm (35 mil) diameter or larger, probe pitch of 2.54 mm (100 mil) is standard, 1.27 mm (50 mil) and 1.0 mm possible with smaller probes, and one-sided access is cheapest. Keep-outs around pads for fixture pins.

**Economics.** Fixture cost depends on net count (hundreds to a few thousand probes), often thousands of USD, test time of seconds. See the break-even arithmetic in [[q0605]]. Probes exert force on the order of 100-200 g each (vendor dependent); 1,500 probes at 100 g is 150 kgf (computed), so the fixture uses vacuum or pneumatic actuation.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Pad sizes, pitches, probe forces and fixture costs are typical ranges from fixture-vendor guidelines and vary by vendor and probe family. Use your fixture vendor's DFT guide for real values.
:::

::: long
"In-circuit test, ICT, is a structural test: it asks whether the board was built correctly, not whether the product works. A fixture with hundreds or thousands of spring-loaded probes, the bed of nails, presses onto test pads on the nets. A tester then measures each component in place.

The difficulty is that components are connected to a network of other components, so measuring one resistor in the circuit would normally include everything in parallel with it. ICT solves that with guarding. Say I measure a 10 kΩ resistor with another path of 4.7 kΩ plus 10 kΩ in parallel. A plain measurement reads about 6 kΩ and the good part fails. With a guard probe holding the middle node at ground, the extra path doesn't contribute to the sensed current, so I read the real 10 kΩ.

So ICT can check shorts and opens between nets, that each resistor, capacitor, inductor and diode is present and in tolerance, polarity, and some IC behaviour. With powered tests it can check certain digital pins, and a vectorless capacitive sensor test or boundary scan covers open pins on ICs you can't probe.

The strengths are speed, a few seconds, and diagnosis: the report says exactly which component or net failed. The weaknesses are that it doesn't test function, timing or firmware, needs test access to nets, which takes board space, and the fixture is expensive and must be redone when the layout changes. So it fits high volume, stable designs, and I'd pair it with AOI before and functional test after."
:::

::: terms
- **ICT**: in-circuit test, structural test of a populated board.
- **Guard / guarding**: grounding or driving nodes to remove unwanted parallel paths from a measurement.
- **Virtual ground**: op-amp input held at 0 V by feedback.
- **Bed-of-nails**: fixture of spring probes ([[q0612]]).
- **Test pad / test point**: probeable copper feature ([[q0619]]).
- **Vectorless test**: capacitive-coupling test to detect open IC pins without a pattern.
- **Backdrive**: forcing digital pin states from the tester to exercise logic.
- **Opens / shorts test**: continuity and isolation between nets.
- **Fixture**: custom mechanical interface between board and tester.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying ICT checks that the board works.** It checks construction, not function.
- **Forgetting guarding.** Without it, in-circuit measurements are wrong.
- **Assuming all nets can be probed.** Test access has to be designed in.
- **Putting test points on both sides.** Two-sided fixtures cost much more.
- **Believing every defect shows up as a failing measurement.** Marginal joints may measure fine and fail later.
- **Using ICT on low volume with unstable designs.** Fixture cost and rework dominate; flying probe is better ([[q0611]]).
:::

## Component-level tests

| Component | Method |
|---|---|
| Resistor | DC force/sense, guarded |
| Capacitor | AC (e.g. 1 kHz to 100 kHz) current at a known small amplitude; $C = I/(2\pi f V)$ |
| Inductor | AC, measure impedance and phase |
| Diode | Forward drop at a set current; reversed part reads a different drop or leakage |
| Transistor | Junction tests, gain, in-circuit with guarding |
| Regulator, opamp | Powered functional tests of the part |
| Digital IC | Backdrive/vector tests, protection-diode (leakage) signature for open pins |
| Jumper/fuse | Resistance close to 0 / continuity |

Capacitor example: with 1 V rms at 10 kHz across 100 nF, the current is $2\pi\cdot10^4\cdot10^{-7}\cdot1=6.3$ mA (computed), easy to measure. A 10 pF capacitor gives only 0.63 µA, comparable to the effect of stray fixture capacitance, which is why small capacitors are hard to verify.

## Opens and shorts

Before measuring parts, ICT checks isolation between nets (shorts) by measuring resistance between each net and its neighbours, and checks continuity of each net across its probe points. Thresholds for shorts are typically a few ohms to tens of ohms depending on what's legitimately low (planes, shunts). Open pins on ICs are covered by the vectorless method or boundary scan, since a joint could be open while the net still connects elsewhere.

## Access and layout rules (typical)

- Test pad diameter about 0.9 mm or larger; keep pad centres at least about 1.27 mm apart for fine probes (2.54 mm for standard), plus clearance from component bodies and board edges (vendor specific).
- Put all probed pads on one side (usually bottom) so a single-sided fixture works.
- At least one test point per net, including power and ground (several for power and ground to reduce contact resistance and allow guard).
- Don't probe on component leads or vias in pads without a plan; probe pads should be flat and not covered with mask or tall silk.
- Keep tall parts away from test points on the fixture side.
- Tooling holes and fiducials for alignment ([[q0596]]).

## Fixture mechanics

All the probes contact simultaneously. With 1,500 probes at 100 g each, total force is 150 kgf, which would bow the board unless it is supported, so fixtures use vacuum to pull the board down onto the probes, plus support pins and clamps. Probe lifetime depends on type and cleanliness. Fixtures are custom, built from the CAD data, so a layout change usually means rework or a new fixture, a reason to freeze the design before building one.

## Coverage view

ICT covers what the PCOLA/SOQ-style fault spectrum calls presence, correctness (value), orientation (for polarised parts), shorts, opens on accessible nets, and some liveness. It doesn't cover alignment or solder quality *per se*, function or timing. Compare AOI ([[q0608]]) for visible issues, flying probe ([[q0611]]) as the fixtureless relative, and FCT ([[q0610]]) for behaviour.

## Worked example: how good does the guard have to be?

The guarded circuit above is exact only if node G sits exactly at the same potential as the sense node B. A real guard is an op-amp or a driver with finite output impedance and offset, so suppose G sits 5 mV above ground while the test source is 1 V. The tester should read $I = 1\text{ V}/10\text{ k}\Omega = 100\ \mu$A through $R_1$. The leftover path from G through $R_3$ into B carries $5\text{ mV}/10\text{ k}\Omega = 0.5\ \mu$A, so the reading is $100.5\ \mu$A, a 0.5% error. The same 5 mV error is a much bigger fraction of a low-level measurement: if you drive only 100 mV to avoid forward-biasing junctions elsewhere, the error becomes 5%. That is the trade-off behind the choice of test voltage. Too high and you turn on diodes and protection structures in parallel paths. Too low and the guard's offset and the sense amplifier's noise become a large fraction of the signal.

Contact resistance is the second error. A spring probe contacts a pad with something like tens to a few hundred milliohms (vendor and cleanliness dependent), which is nothing against 10 kΩ and a lot against a 10 Ω shunt. If the probe resistance is 0.5 Ω on each side, a two-wire measurement of a 10 Ω part reads $10 + 1 = 11\ \Omega$, a 10% error. The standard answer is a four-wire (Kelvin) measurement with separate force and sense probes on each end, which costs twice the probes on those nets and is the reason low-ohm shunts often get two test points each.

## Capacitors and the stray-capacitance problem, with numbers

The technical section noted that tiny capacitors are hard to measure. Here is why and what actually helps. With 1 V rms at 10 kHz, a 10 pF capacitor passes $2\pi \cdot 10^4 \cdot 10^{-11} \cdot 1 = 0.63\ \mu$A. Fixture wiring and the board have capacitance to ground at every node. But note which strays matter. A stray from A to ground is driven by the source, and a stray from B to ground sits at virtual ground, so neither carries sense current. Only a stray directly between A and B adds to the reading. If that is 0.3 pF, it adds $0.3/10 = 3\%$, which is what a capacitor tolerance band can absorb. Guarding converts a hopeless measurement into a marginal one. Raising the frequency raises the current in proportion, which is why capacitor tests use the highest frequency that keeps parasitic inductance and the sense amplifier's bandwidth out of the picture.

For a large electrolytic the problem is reversed: the series ESR is what you can resolve, and a polarity or wrong-value error shows up in the phase and magnitude of the current. A 100 nF part at 10 kHz has a reactance of $1/(2\pi \cdot 10^4 \cdot 10^{-7}) = 159\ \Omega$, so a parallel low-ohm path (a 10 Ω shunt) completely hides it even with guarding if the shunt connects A to B directly. That is the "what it misses" case from the technical section in numbers, and it is where the design should add a test point or an isolation jumper.

## Test time and when ICT pays off

Test time is the number of measurement steps times the settling time of each. At 800 steps with 5 ms each, the electrical test takes about 4 s plus the time to load and clamp the board, which together might be 10 to 15 s (all assumed). A flying-probe tester doing the same job might take 90 s because it has to move probes between every pair of nets. Suppose machine time costs USD 60 an hour (assumed). The saving per board is $86\text{ s} \times 60/3600 = $ USD 1.43. If the fixture costs USD 8,000, break-even is $8000/1.43 \approx 5{,}600$ boards. Below that volume flying probe wins, and above it the fixture wins, ignoring that a fixture is also tied to one revision of the layout. See [[q0605]] for the fuller comparison, and note that all three inputs are assumptions, so the shape of the argument matters more than the number.

## Where the model breaks down

**Powered ICs fight the measurement.** Measuring a resistor next to a powered IC means the IC's pins can drive current into the node. ICT tests unpowered, but even then the protection diodes in an IC conduct if any node goes more than about 0.6 V from a rail, so test voltages are kept under that.

**Capacitors in parallel with everything.** Large decoupling capacitors on a rail make the DC settling time long, so resistance tests of nodes tied to the rail wait for the capacitors to charge. The tester's settling delay can dominate test time on boards with many bulk capacitors.

**Fixture wear and contamination.** Probe tips accumulate flux residue, raising contact resistance over thousands of cycles. A test that passes on a clean fixture can false-fail later. Fixtures are cleaned on a schedule, probes are replaced by count.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Probe contact resistance, settling times, per-step times and machine hourly costs are shop and vendor dependent. The numbers above are assumptions chosen to show how the calculation works.

## Verifying the tester itself

1. **Golden board.** Keep a known-good board and run it daily. A change in its readings points at the fixture or the instrument, not the product.
2. **Gauge repeatability.** Measure the same board repeatedly, then remove and reinsert it, and look at the spread of each measured value against its test limit. A limit set inside the measurement spread produces false failures.
3. **Seeded faults.** Build a few boards with known faults (a lifted lead, a wrong value, a reversed polarized part, a solder bridge) and confirm the tester catches each. Fault coverage that has not been shown on seeded boards is a claim, not a measurement.
4. **Contact check.** Measure a pad pair with a known short or the board's own ground net across probes. A high reading flags a worn probe before it fails real boards.

- **Power supplies**: ICT for passives and semiconductors before powered FCT.

- **Programming station integration**: flash programming through the ICT fixture to save a step.
- **Process feedback**: component-level failures point at feeders, reels or stencil issues.

::: followups
### Why does ICT need guarding?
Components are connected to other parts in parallel. A direct measurement includes those paths and would misread the part. Guard probes ground or drive intermediate nodes so those paths carry no measurement current, isolating the component under test.

### Can ICT test an IC?
Partly. It can check protection-diode signatures on pins (detecting opens and shorts), power the board and backdrive some digital logic, run vectorless capacitive coupling tests, or use boundary scan. It won't verify a complex IC's function; that is for functional test.

### What are the cost drivers of an ICT fixture?
Number of probes (nets), board size, single vs double-sided access, probe type (fine pitch is dearer), and fixture complexity (vacuum, support pins). Redesigns invalidate it, so a stable layout is needed.

### You can't add test points on a dense board. What do you do?
Use boundary scan for JTAG-capable parts, flying probe at accessible pads, X-ray for hidden joints, vectorless tests where possible, and rely on functional test self-checks for the rest. Also negotiate test points on critical nets (power, resets, buses).

### ICT passes but the board fails function. Why?
ICT checks construction, not behaviour. Faults can be firmware, timing, analog accuracy, intermittent joints, or a part that measures within ICT limits but misbehaves under real load or frequency. Functional test covers those ([[q0610]]).
:::
