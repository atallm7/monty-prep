::: testing
Whether you understand the scope as a **voltage-versus-time instrument** and know what it is good and bad at, not just "the thing with the screen." The interviewer wants to hear that you can say what the axes are, what the key specs are (bandwidth, sample rate, memory depth, vertical resolution, channels), and when you would pick it over a multimeter. They also want to see if you know the scope measures *the voltage at the probe tip relative to the scope's ground*, which is where a lot of beginner mistakes come from.

Hidden sub-skills: time-domain thinking, knowing that a measurement has its own bandwidth and loading, and having actually used one.
:::

::: why
Almost every hardware problem eventually becomes "what is that node actually doing over time?" A multimeter gives you one averaged number. A scope shows you the ringing on a switch node, the droop on a supply rail when the processor wakes up, the glitch on an enable line, whether an I²C edge is too slow. It is the first instrument you reach for in bring-up and debug. Interviewers ask this to find out whether you can use it as a thinking tool, and whether you know its limits, because a scope that is too slow or a probe that loads the circuit will lie to you convincingly.
:::

::: concepts
- **Voltage vs. time display**: the horizontal axis is time (s/div), the vertical axis is voltage (V/div). A DMM reports a number; a scope reports a *shape*.
- **Periodic and transient signals**: amplitude, period, frequency, duty cycle, rise time, overshoot, noise. See [[q0022]] for why capacitors make rise times finite.
- **Bandwidth and rise time**: a scope is a low-pass system, $t_r \approx 0.35/BW$. See [[q0983]].
- **Sampling**: a digital scope turns the analog input into numbers with an ADC, so sample rate, memory depth and resolution matter. See [[q0991]].
- **Triggering**: the mechanism that makes a repetitive or one-off event appear steady on screen. See [[q0992]].
- **Probing and loading**: the probe is part of the circuit while you measure. See [[q0997]] and [[q0999]].
:::

::: answer
"An oscilloscope is an instrument that plots voltage against time. The horizontal axis is time, the vertical axis is volts, and it redraws that picture continuously so I can actually watch a signal evolve instead of reading one number off a multimeter.

What makes it useful is that it shows shape. I can see a clean 3.3 V logic edge versus one that rings and overshoots, a power rail that has 50 mV of ripple on it, a glitch that lasts 20 nanoseconds, or two signals and the delay between them. With a multimeter all of those just look like 'about 3.3 V.'

The specs I care about are bandwidth, which says how fast a signal the front end can pass without rounding it off; sample rate and memory depth, which say how much detail and how long a window I can capture; vertical resolution, usually 8 bits, which sets how small a change I can see; and the number of channels. Most modern scopes are digital storage scopes: they sample the input with an ADC, store it in memory, and draw it.

The thing I always keep in mind is that the probe is part of the measurement. It adds capacitance and a ground path, so I pick the probe and the bandwidth to suit the signal."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** An oscilloscope measures the instantaneous voltage between its probe tip and its ground reference, as a function of time, and displays $v(t)$. A modern digital storage oscilloscope (DSO) does this with an input attenuator/amplifier, a high-speed ADC, acquisition memory, a trigger system, and a display/processing engine ([[q0991]] covers the pipeline).

**Key specifications**
- **Analog bandwidth** (−3 dB point of the front end). Rise time and bandwidth are linked by $t_r \approx 0.35/BW$ for a Gaussian-like response, and closer to $0.4$–$0.45/BW$ for the sharper roll-off typical of faster scopes. Measured rise time combines with the signal's: $t_{meas}=\sqrt{t_{sig}^2+t_{scope}^2}$.
- **Sample rate** (Sa/s): needs to be well above Nyquist; for good edge reconstruction people aim for 4–5× the bandwidth.
- **Memory depth**: $T_{window}=N_{pts}/f_s$. 10 Mpts at 1 GSa/s covers 10 ms.
- **Vertical resolution**: an 8-bit ADC across 8 divisions gives 256 levels, so at 1 V/div one LSB is $8/256$ V $=31.25$ mV. Zoom in (smaller V/div) to use the whole range, or use hi-res/averaging modes.
- **Input impedance**: usually 1 MΩ ∥ ~15 pF, or a selectable 50 Ω path for high-speed work. See [[q0999]].
- **Channels**: 2 or 4 analog (plus optional digital channels in a mixed-signal scope). See [[q0994]].
- **Update rate / dead time**: how often it re-arms. A fast waveform-capture rate is what lets you catch rare glitches.

**What it cannot do.** It reads voltage, not current directly (you use a shunt or a current probe, [[q0993]], [[q0998]]), and it measures relative to its own ground, which is usually tied to earth. Probing a floating node with a grounded probe can short something.
:::

::: short
"An oscilloscope plots voltage versus time, so instead of one number like a multimeter I see the actual waveform: edges, ripple, ringing, glitches, timing between signals. The specs that matter are bandwidth, sample rate, memory depth, vertical resolution and channel count. Modern ones sample the input with an ADC and store it. I always remember that the probe loads the circuit and the scope's bandwidth limits what I can see, so I choose both to fit the signal."
:::

::: long
"An oscilloscope is a voltage-versus-time instrument. Time runs left to right, volts run up and down, and it keeps redrawing so I can see how a signal behaves rather than just its average level.

That is the big difference from a multimeter. A DMM would tell me a rail is at 3.3 volts. The scope tells me whether that rail has 40 millivolts of ripple at the switching frequency, or dips 200 millivolts for a microsecond when a core wakes up. It lets me look at logic edges and see overshoot, slow rise times, or glitches, and with two channels I can measure delay between a clock and data.

Internally, a digital scope has an attenuator and amplifier to scale the input, an ADC that samples it, memory that stores the samples, and a trigger circuit that decides where on the signal to start the picture. The trigger is what makes a repeating waveform sit still.

When I choose or set up a scope, I think about four numbers. Bandwidth: for a signal with a 2 nanosecond edge I need roughly 0.35 over 2 nanoseconds, about 175 megahertz, just to see the edge without slowing it, so I'd want more like 350 or 500. Sample rate and memory depth: enough samples across each edge and a long enough window for the event I want. Vertical resolution: typically 8 bits, so I scale the volts per division to fill the screen. And channels, depending on how many signals I need to compare.

Finally, probing. The probe adds capacitance and the ground lead adds inductance, so a bad probing setup can make a clean signal look like it is ringing. I use a 10x probe, a short ground spring, and match the bandwidth to the signal."
:::

::: terms
- **Bandwidth**: the frequency at which the scope's response is down 3 dB (about 70.7% amplitude). Sets the fastest edge it can show faithfully.
- **Sample rate**: how many ADC samples per second, in Sa/s.
- **Memory depth (record length)**: how many samples can be stored per capture. Longer memory means longer windows at high sample rate.
- **Volts/div and time/div**: the vertical and horizontal scales of each grid square.
- **Trigger**: the event (edge, level, pulse width, pattern) that anchors the display. See [[q0992]].
- **Rise time**: the 10–90% transition time of an edge, $t_r\approx 0.35/BW$ for a scope.
- **Input coupling**: DC (everything), AC (blocks the DC component with a series capacitor), GND (reference).
- **DSO**: digital storage oscilloscope, the standard modern type.
- **Probe**: the accessory that connects the circuit to the scope input; it has its own impedance and bandwidth.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it shows waveforms," maybe names voltage and time.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames it as a time-domain voltage instrument with a specific reference (its ground), and says what it reveals that a DMM cannot (shape, timing, transients).
- Names the five specs and gives one quantitative rule, like $t_r\approx0.35/BW$, applied to a real signal.
- Mentions that 8-bit resolution means you should scale the vertical axis to use the screen.
- Brings up probe loading and ground-lead inductance unprompted.
- Gives an example from real debug: "I caught a reset glitch on the enable line that the multimeter averaged away."
:::

::: mistakes
- **"It measures AC signals."** It measures DC just as well (DC coupling). AC coupling is a choice, not a limitation.
- **Ignoring bandwidth.** A 100 MHz scope will happily display a 1 ns edge, but slowed to about 3.5 ns. The picture looks plausible and is wrong.
- **Treating the probe as invisible.** A 1× probe has ~100 pF of capacitance; on a high-impedance node it can change the circuit's behavior.
- **Clipping the ground lead to a non-ground node** on a mains-referenced scope. The scope ground is earth-referenced; you create a short circuit. See [[q0993]].
- **Using too large a V/div.** With 8 bits, a 20 mV ripple measured at 5 V/div is under one LSB. Zoom in.
- **Confusing sample rate with bandwidth.** They are related but different; see [[q0983]].
:::

::: deep
## What a scope fundamentally does

A scope answers one question: *what is the voltage at this node, relative to my ground reference, at each instant?* It plots that as $v(t)$. Everything else (triggering, math, FFT, protocol decoding) is built on that picture.

The reason this is so valuable is that circuits are dynamic. Capacitors charge ($\tau=RC$), inductors fight changes in current ($v=L\,di/dt$), logic lines switch in nanoseconds, and power rails respond to load steps. A DMM averages over tens of milliseconds and gives you only the mean (or RMS in AC mode). Two very different signals can have the same DMM reading:

## Reading the display

The screen is a grid, typically 10 horizontal and 8 vertical divisions. You set **time/div** and **volts/div** and read:

- **Amplitude**: divisions × V/div. Peak-to-peak of a sine, or high/low of a logic level.
- **Period and frequency**: divisions between like points × time/div; $f=1/T$.
- **Rise/fall time**: the 10–90% time on an edge.
- **Offset and coupling**: the vertical position knob shifts the trace; AC coupling puts a series high-pass filter (corner often a few Hz) in front of the amplifier.

Worked example: a trace shows 3.2 divisions peak to peak at 500 mV/div, and one cycle spans 4.0 divisions at 50 µs/div. Amplitude $=3.2\times0.5=1.6\,V_{pp}$; period $=4\times50\,\mu s=200\,\mu s$, so $f=5$ kHz.

## The five numbers that matter

**1. Bandwidth.** The input path is effectively a low-pass filter. For a single-pole-like response, a 10–90% rise time is $0.35/BW$ (this is the time a first-order low-pass takes to go from 10% to 90%: $2.2\,\tau$ with $\tau=1/(2\pi BW)$, which gives $2.2/(2\pi)=0.35$). When a fast edge goes through, the displayed rise time is
$$t_{meas}=\sqrt{t_{sig}^2+t_{scope}^2}$$
For a 2 ns edge: a 100 MHz scope has $t_{scope}=3.5$ ns, so you read $\sqrt{4+12.25}\approx4.0$ ns (twice the truth). A 350 MHz scope has 1 ns, so you read 2.24 ns (12% error). A common rule is to pick a scope with at least 3–5× the bandwidth of the fastest signal content, with the probe's bandwidth included as another term in the root-sum-square.

**2. Sample rate.** Samples are discrete; to draw a convincing edge you want several samples across it. A 2.24 ns edge sampled at 1 GSa/s has only 2 samples on it. At 5 GSa/s it has 11. The Nyquist limit (half the sample rate) is the theoretical bound, but for time-domain shape most scopes spec 4–5 samples per rise time as the working minimum.

**3. Memory depth.** Capture window $=N/f_s$. If you want to see a 20 ms power-up event at 1 GSa/s you need 20 Mpts; if the memory is only 1 Mpts, the scope reduces the sample rate to 50 MSa/s to cover the window, and now you can't see a 5 ns glitch. This trade-off between window length and detail is among the most commonly overlooked.

**4. Vertical resolution.** 8-bit scopes have 256 levels. With 8 divisions, each LSB is 1/32 of a division. Measuring 20 mV of ripple on a 3.3 V rail means setting the scale to 10–20 mV/div with offset, not 1 V/div. High-resolution modes average neighboring samples to gain effective bits at the cost of bandwidth, and 12-bit scopes exist.

**5. Input impedance and coupling.** The standard input is 1 MΩ ∥ 10–20 pF. A 50 Ω input is used for matched high-speed signals (and is easy to overload: it is typically limited to about 5 V RMS).

## Analog vs. digital scopes

Old analog scopes drew the waveform live: the vertical signal deflected an electron beam in a CRT while a sweep ramp moved it horizontally. They have no sampling and show true real-time brightness. Digital storage scopes replaced them because they can capture one-off events, store and zoom, measure automatically, and trigger on complex conditions. Some modes, like equivalent-time sampling for repetitive signals, let a DSO exceed its real-time sample rate, but only for repeating signals.

## Real-hardware behavior

- **Probe and ground lead set the true bandwidth.** The scope's spec is for the instrument alone. A probe with a long ground clip can ring at 100 MHz even on a 500 MHz scope; see [[q0997]].
- **Scope noise floor.** At the most sensitive ranges, a scope's own noise is a fraction of a millivolt to a few mV RMS. If you are looking at 1 mV of switching noise you need a low-noise setup.
- **Earth ground.** The scope chassis connects to mains earth through the power cord, so the probe ground clips to earth. Clipping it onto a "hot" node (or a half-bridge switch node) causes a short through the ground path. Use a differential probe or isolated scope ([[q0995]]).
- **Aliasing.** If you set a slow time base the scope may undersample a fast signal and show a false slow waveform. Always zoom out in time to confirm the true frequency.

> [!TIP]
> When anything on the screen looks "too nice" or "too bad," change one thing: V/div, time/div, probe, or bandwidth limit, and see if the displayed behavior changes. Real signal features survive those changes; artifacts do not.
:::

::: applications
- **Power supply debug**: measure ripple and switching-node ringing on a buck converter, catch load-transient droop on a rail.
- **Digital bring-up**: confirm clock amplitude and edges, check setup/hold relationships between two signals, verify power sequencing.
- **Serial protocols**: trigger and decode I²C, SPI or UART on an MSO and see the waveform that caused a NACK.
- **Motor drives and inverters**: look at PWM gate signals, dead time, phase currents (with a current probe).
- **Sensor and analog work**: check an op-amp's response to a step, look at ADC input settling.
- **Production and RMA**: compare a failed unit's waveform with a good unit's.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes a **digital storage oscilloscope**, the type you will meet in nearly every lab. An analog scope works differently but displays the same quantity.
- Assumes a **standard high-impedance (1 MΩ) input** with a passive probe. A 50 Ω-input scope changes the loading story.
- The $0.35/BW$ rule assumes a roughly single-pole/Gaussian response. Fast scopes with sharper "brick-wall" roll-off follow nearer 0.4–0.45/BW.
- "8-bit" is typical but not universal; many newer scopes offer 10–12 bits. The principle of scaling the display to use the full range is the same.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact input capacitance (e.g. 13 pF vs 16 pF), maximum 50 Ω input voltage, and whether 4–5× bandwidth or 5× is "the rule" differ by vendor and application note. Treat the numbers here as typical and check your scope's datasheet.
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between a scope and a multimeter?
A multimeter gives one slow, averaged (or RMS) number with high accuracy: good for DC levels, resistance, continuity. A scope gives the full time waveform at much lower accuracy (a few percent, limited by 8 bits) but with bandwidth in the hundreds of MHz. I use the DMM when I need the precise value of a DC level and the scope when I need to know what the signal is doing. See [[q0989]].

### What bandwidth do I need to look at a 100 MHz clock?
A square wave has harmonics at odd multiples, so for good shape I want at least the 5th harmonic, 500 MHz, and ideally the edge: if the edge is 1 ns, $0.35/1\text{ ns}=350$ MHz minimum, and I'd want more margin. A 100 MHz scope would show a rounded sine-like wave with the wrong amplitude. I'd use 500 MHz–1 GHz and an appropriate active probe.

### Why would my scope show a different peak-to-peak than a hand calculation?
Common causes: probe attenuation setting wrong (a 10× probe with the scope set to 1×), bandwidth limiting, AC coupling dropping the DC component, loading from the probe, or an 8-bit scale set too coarse. Check the probe multiplier first.

### How do you view a signal that happens only once?
Use single trigger mode with the trigger level and pre-trigger position set so the event lands inside the window, with enough sample rate and memory depth. Then the scope stores one record and stops. See [[q0992]].

### What if the signal you want to measure is 600 V AC on a floating node?
Don't use a grounded passive probe directly. Use a high-voltage differential probe rated for the voltage and category, or an isolated-channel scope. The ground lead of a standard probe is earth-referenced, and the probe's own voltage rating is typically a few hundred volts at most.
:::
