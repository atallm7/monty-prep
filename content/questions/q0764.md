::: testing
Whether you can explain SPI beyond the acronym. The interviewer wants:

1. **Serial Peripheral Interface**: synchronous, full-duplex, master/slave (controller/peripheral), four wires.
2. The signals: SCLK, MOSI, MISO, and CS/SS, and what each does.
3. The mechanism that makes it so simple and fast: two shift registers connected in a ring, exchanging bits on every clock.
4. What SPI *doesn't* have: no addressing, no ACK, no flow control, no formal standard, and what the four modes are.
:::

::: why
SPI is the workhorse for anything that needs speed over a few wires: flash, ADCs/DACs, displays, SD cards, IMUs, radios. You will meet it in nearly every embedded design. Explaining it clearly, especially the shift-register picture, is a quick way to show you understand synchronous serial communication and can compare it with I2C and UART ([[q0738]], [[q0741]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Synchronous serial**: a shared clock tells the receiver when to sample each bit. See [[q0741]].
- **Shift register**: bits move one position per clock; two registers in a ring swap contents. See [[q0886]] and [[q0882]].
- **Master/slave (controller/peripheral)**: the master generates the clock and chooses the slave.
- **Push-pull drivers**: fast, symmetric edges without pull-ups. See [[q0761]].
- **Chip select**: a per-slave line that frames a transaction and enables the slave.
- **Clock polarity and phase (CPOL/CPHA)**: four modes defining the idle level and sample edge.
:::

::: answer
"SPI stands for Serial Peripheral Interface. It's a synchronous, full-duplex serial bus where one master talks to one or more slaves, originally from Motorola. It uses four signals: SCLK, the clock from the master; MOSI, master out slave in; MISO, master in slave out; and CS, chip select, which the master pulls low to pick a slave and frame the transfer.

The neat way to understand it is as two shift registers connected in a ring. The master has one, the slave has one. On every clock edge, the master shifts one bit out on MOSI while the slave shifts one bit out on MISO, and each side shifts the other's bit in. After eight clocks the two bytes have been swapped. That's why it's full-duplex: sending and receiving happen at the same time, and a read is just 'send a dummy byte and look at what came back'.

What it doesn't have is anything fancy: no addresses, because the chip select does that; no acknowledge, so the master doesn't know if anyone is listening; and no formal standard, just conventions like the four clock modes. In exchange, since the drivers are push-pull and there are no pull-ups, it runs at tens of megahertz, which is why flash, displays, ADCs and SD cards use it."
:::

::: technical
**Name and origin.** Serial Peripheral Interface. A de facto standard introduced by Motorola (it appears in the 68HC11-era microcontrollers); there is no governing standards document, so details (bit order, word length, modes) are set by each device's datasheet. Modern naming (controller/peripheral, COPI/CIPO or SDO/SDI) replaces master/slave, MOSI/MISO.

**Signals.**
- **SCLK**: clock, driven by the master.
- **MOSI** (master out, slave in; also SDO on master, SDI on slave): data from master.
- **MISO** (master in, slave out): data from the selected slave.
- **CS / SS** (active low): selects the slave and frames the transfer.

**Operation.** Master and slave each contain a shift register. On each clock cycle one bit is shifted out of each register and into the other, so $n$ clocks exchange $n$ bits in both directions. The master chooses the number of clocks; the slave has no way to say "stop" except by device-specific protocol.

**Modes.** CPOL sets the idle level of SCLK; CPHA sets whether data is sampled on the first or the second edge. Four combinations (modes 0–3). Mode 0 (CPOL=0, CPHA=0): idle low, sample on the rising edge, shift (change) on the falling edge. Master and slave must agree.

**Electrical.** Push-pull outputs, so no pull-ups are needed, and edges are fast. Logic levels follow each device's supply ([[q0769]]). Clocks range from kHz to hundreds of MHz ([[q0770]]).

**Features it doesn't have.** No addressing, no ACK/NACK, no error detection, no flow control, no defined maximum length of transfer. Higher-level protocols (a command byte, a register address, a CRC) are device-specific.

**Costs.** One CS pin per slave (or decoding/daisy-chain), 4+ wires, and no hot-plug-style arbitration.
:::

::: long
"SPI stands for Serial Peripheral Interface. It was introduced by Motorola and has become the standard way to hook fast peripherals to a microcontroller: flash memory, ADCs and DACs, displays, SD cards, IMUs and radios. It's synchronous, so there's a dedicated clock, and it's full-duplex, so data flows both directions at once.

There are four signals. The master drives SCLK, the clock. It sends data out on MOSI, master out slave in, and receives on MISO, master in slave out. Then there's chip select, usually called CS or SS and active low, which the master pulls low to select a particular slave and to mark the start and end of a transfer.

The best mental model is two shift registers connected in a ring: the master's output goes to the slave's input, and the slave's output goes back to the master's input. Each clock edge moves every bit one place, so one bit leaves each register and enters the other. After eight clocks the two bytes have swapped. That is why SPI is symmetric: to read from a slave you send a dummy byte and capture what comes back, and in many chips the first byte you send is a command and the data that follows is the reply.

The clock has four modes, set by two bits: CPOL, the idle level of the clock, and CPHA, which edge samples data. Master and slave have to match, and the datasheet says which mode a part uses; mode 0 is the most common.

What SPI doesn't give you is overhead protection: no addresses, since chip select does the job; no acknowledge, so the master can't tell if the slave heard it; and no formal standard, so word size and bit order are per-device. But because the drivers are push-pull with no pull-ups, it runs at tens of megahertz or more, which is why it's the default for anything fast."
:::

::: terms
- **SCLK / SCK**: serial clock from the master.
- **MOSI / SDO (master) / COPI**: master out, slave in.
- **MISO / SDI (master) / CIPO**: master in, slave out.
- **CS / SS / CSn**: chip select, active low; selects the slave and frames the transfer.
- **CPOL**: clock polarity, the idle level of SCLK.
- **CPHA**: clock phase, which edge samples data.
- **Full-duplex**: both directions at the same time.
- **Shift register**: register whose bits move one place per clock.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "Serial Peripheral Interface, four wires, master and slave".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the shift-register ring and why that implies full-duplex and "read = write dummy".
- Names signals and the modern names (COPI/CIPO) and describes what CS does (selects *and* frames).
- Gives the four modes with mode 0's timing, and notes mismatched mode is the classic bug.
- Lists what is *absent* (addressing, ACK, standard, flow control) and the consequences.
- Mentions speed range and push-pull as the reason, plus variants (dual/quad SPI).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"SPI is a standard like I2C".** It's a de facto convention; read the datasheet for mode, bit order and word length.
- **Saying MOSI connects to the slave's MISO.** MOSI connects to the slave's *input* (SDI/MOSI). Label by function, not by the pin name that looks similar.
- **Thinking SPI is half-duplex.** Every clock moves a bit each way, even if one direction is just dummy data.
- **Forgetting CS.** Without CS the slave won't know where a transfer starts or ends.
- **Assuming an ACK exists.** If the slave is unpowered, the MISO just floats or reads all 1s or 0s.
- **Mismatched mode (CPOL/CPHA).** Garbled data that "looks shifted by a bit" is usually this.
:::

Say the master holds 0xA5 (1010 0101) and the slave holds 0x3C (0011 1100). Each clock, one bit leaves each register (MSB first) and the other's bit enters at the LSB. After eight clocks, the master holds 0x3C and the slave holds 0xA5. They have swapped bytes. In many devices the *first* byte from the master is a command, and the slave's reply appears in the *following* bytes while the master clocks out dummy data.

## Why it's fast and simple

- **Push-pull drivers**: each line has exactly one driver at a time, so there's no pull-up resistor and the rising edge is as fast as the falling edge ([[q0761]]).
- **Dedicated clock**: the receiver samples on a clock edge, so no oversampling or baud-rate matching ([[q0741]]).
- **No addressing overhead**: bits on the wire are all payload (plus whatever command bytes the device defines).
- **Trivial hardware**: a shift register and a bit counter.

A rough comparison:

| Feature | SPI | I2C | UART |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wires | 4 (+1 per extra slave) | 2 | 2 (TX, RX) |
| Clock | shared (synchronous) | shared (synchronous) | none (asynchronous) |
| Duplex | full | half | full |
| Addressing | chip select | 7/10-bit address | none (point-to-point) |
| ACK | no | yes | no |
| Typical speed | 1 to 100+ MHz | 0.1 to 1 MHz | 9600 to ~1 Mbaud |

## The four modes

CPOL (clock polarity) sets the idle level of SCLK. CPHA (clock phase) sets whether data is sampled on the leading edge (first edge after idle) or the trailing edge.

| Mode | CPOL | CPHA | Idle SCLK | Sample on | Change on |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | low | rising | falling |
| 1 | 0 | 1 | low | falling | rising |
| 2 | 1 | 0 | high | falling | rising |
| 3 | 1 | 1 | high | rising | falling |

If a master and slave disagree on mode, data looks like a bit-shifted version of itself or comes back all ones or zeros. Always check the slave's datasheet for the mode and the maximum clock.

## How a transaction usually looks

Most SPI peripherals define a mini-protocol on top:

1. Master pulls CS low.
2. Master sends a command/register byte, which often includes a read/write bit.
3. Master clocks in or out the data bytes.
4. Master releases CS high, which often latches or ends the operation.

Example: an SPI NOR flash read is the command 0x03, a 24-bit address, then as many data bytes as you clock. See [[q0766]] for the interfacing recipe.

## What's missing, and why it matters

- **No addressing:** the master selects the slave with a physical CS line ([[q0765]]).
- **No acknowledge:** if the slave isn't there, MISO is high or low or floating, and the master has no direct way to know. Production code often reads an ID register as a presence check.
- **No flow control:** a slow slave can't pause the clock (some devices add a BUSY/READY pin).
- **No standard:** bit order (MSB first usually), word length (8 bits usually, sometimes 16 or 24) and mode vary by device.
- **No error detection:** add CRC at the protocol level if it matters (SD cards, some sensors do).

## Variants you'll hear about

- **3-wire SPI**: MOSI and MISO share one bidirectional data line.
- **Dual, Quad, Octal SPI**: use 2, 4 or 8 data lines to multiply throughput (flash memory).
- **QSPI/OSPI** peripherals on MCUs often mean quad/octal SPI for memory-mapped flash.
- **Microwire**: National Semiconductor's similar interface.
- **SSI / SSP**: names used by some vendors for related synchronous serial ports.

::: applications
- **SPI NOR/NAND flash** and **SD cards** (SPI mode) for storage and boot code.
- **ADCs and DACs** from tens of kHz to MHz sample rates, with precision timing.
- **TFT/OLED displays** where pixel data streams at tens of MHz.
- **IMUs and pressure sensors** (many support both I2C and SPI).
- **Radios and Ethernet controllers** (for example SX127x-class LoRa transceivers and W5500-class Ethernet chips).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Four-wire, single master**, push-pull, MSB-first, 8-bit words. Variants change word size and bit order.
- **No formal standard**; the device datasheet is the specification.
- **Logic levels follow the supply of each device.**
- **Naming**: the question uses "slave"; modern documents use "peripheral" or "target". I use both.

If the interviewer asks "is SPI a standard?" the honest answer is "no, it's a convention; the datasheets are the spec".
:::

::: followups
### Why is SPI full-duplex?
Because of the shift-register ring: every clock edge shifts a bit out on MOSI and simultaneously shifts a bit in on MISO. Even if only one direction carries useful data, the other still moves. To read a register, the master sends a command and then dummy bytes while the slave's reply streams in on MISO.

### What does CS actually do?
It selects which slave is listening and frames the transaction. When CS is high, the slave ignores SCLK and MOSI and tri-states MISO (a requirement when sharing MISO). When it falls, the slave resets its bit counter and shift register. In many devices the rising edge of CS latches data (a DAC output updating) or ends a command.

### What happens if master and slave use different modes?
They sample on different edges, so each bit is read half a clock off, giving data that looks shifted by one bit or inverted at the edges. Typically you see all-ones, all-zeros or a pattern shifted by one bit. Check CPOL/CPHA against the datasheet.

### Is there a speed limit, and where does it come from?
The device's max SCLK from its datasheet, set by its internal timing and its output delay on MISO, and the board's propagation and level shifters. See [[q0770]] for the budget calculation.

### How do you read a register from an SPI sensor?
CS low, send the register address with the read bit set (often bit 7), then clock out one dummy byte; the slave's register value arrives on MISO during that dummy byte (or the byte after, depending on the part); CS high. Always check whether the reply is in the same byte as the command (for some ADCs) or the following one.
:::
