::: testing
Whether you can organise a *group* of capacitors into a coherent layout rather than treating each in isolation. The interviewer wants:

1. A hierarchy by value and distance: smallest at the pin, mid-value nearby, bulk at the source.
2. A way to decide how far each tier may sit (an inductance budget by frequency).
3. Routing discipline: each capacitor with its own vias to the planes, no daisy-chains, no shared bottlenecks.
4. Awareness of the planes' role (power/ground pair as a distributed capacitor), mutual inductance tricks, and verification.
:::

::: why
A high-pin-count part like an FPGA or application processor can need dozens of capacitors in several values. Placed carelessly, the pile does little; laid out with a plan, it holds the rail within spec up to the frequency where the package takes over. Interviewers use this question to check you can scale the single-capacitor rules ([[q0503]]) up to a real board.
:::

::: concepts
- **Loop inductance per capacitor** and the inductance budget ([[q0504]]).
- **Parallel banks**: $N$ capacitors divide inductance if each has its own connection ([[q0506]]).
- **Frequency tiers**: different values cover different bands, and tier placement follows from the band ([[q0502]]).
- **Plane pair**: the power/ground plane pair provides distributed capacitance and a low-inductance route between capacitors and pins.
- **Mutual inductance**: adjacent opposite-direction currents partially cancel.
- **Anti-resonance**: peaks between tiers, needing damping.
:::

::: answer
"I organise them as tiers by value and distance, and I route each capacitor to the planes on its own.

The smallest values, 100 nF or less, go right against the supply pins: on the same side for a QFP or QFN, or on the back directly under the balls for a BGA. The mid values, a microfarad to ten, go close but less critical, within about a centimetre. The bulk, tens to hundreds of microfarads, goes near the regulator or the connector where the power enters.

For routing, every capacitor gets its own pair of vias, power and ground side by side, straight into the power and ground planes, which are on adjacent layers close to the surface. I don't connect capacitors in a chain with traces, and I don't share one via between several. Power and ground vias are interleaved so currents are in opposite directions next to each other and the magnetic fields partly cancel. The planes themselves carry the current between tiers, so I keep them solid in that area.

Then I check the combined impedance against a target, in simulation or on the bench, because mixing values creates anti-resonance peaks and I may need more mid-value capacitors or some ESR to flatten them."
:::

::: technical
**Tiering by inductance budget.** Let $Z_{target}=\Delta V/\Delta I$. A capacitor group connected to the chip through inductance $L_c$ keeps $|Z|<Z_{target}$ up to about

$$f_{max}\approx\frac{Z_{target}}{2\pi L_c}\quad\Rightarrow\quad L_c\le\frac{Z_{target}}{2\pi f}$$

For $Z_{target}=10$ mΩ:

| Frequency to cover | Allowed $L_c$ | Tier | Typical distance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 MHz | 16 pH | on-package / backside array with many parts | under the BGA |
| 10 MHz | 0.16 nH | 100 nF array (10s of parts) | under/beside the part |
| 1 MHz | 1.6 nH | 1–10 µF | within ~10 mm |
| 100 kHz | 16 nH | 22–100 µF bulk | several cm; regulator vicinity |

(Allowed distance also depends on stackup; with planes 0.1–0.2 mm away, each mm of connection is ≈0.1–0.3 nH.)

**Routing rules.**
1. Own vias: each capacitor has a dedicated power via and ground via at its pads, side by side. Parallel vias (2 per pad) for the largest or most critical.
2. Plane-based distribution: connect capacitors through planes, not traces. Keep the power/ground pair thin (≤ 0.1–0.2 mm) and close to the capacitor layer so $L_{via}$ is small.
3. Alternate polarity: place neighbouring capacitors with power/ground pad order alternated so adjacent vias carry opposing currents (mutual inductance reduces $L_{eq}$).
4. No daisy chains: don't route capacitor A's connection through B, and don't feed a small capacitor *through* a large one's via.
5. Symmetry: spread equal values evenly around the part so currents share.
6. Keep other routing out of the capacitor zone, especially under a BGA where vias already compete for space.

**Number of parts.** $N\approx L_{one}/L_{eq,needed}$, where $L_{one}$ is a single capacitor's total loop inductance. For $L_{one}=0.8$ nH and needing 20 pH: $N=40$ parts.
:::

::: short

:::

::: long
"I plan it as a hierarchy, and then route it so each part keeps its own low-inductance connection.

The hierarchy is by value and distance. The smallest capacitors, 100 nanofarads and below, carry the highest-frequency current, so they must have the lowest connection inductance: on the same side right against the supply pins for a QFP or QFN, or on the back directly under the ball field for a BGA. The mid values, a microfarad up to ten, go within a centimetre or so. The bulk goes near the regulator or the connector. A useful way to decide distances is an inductance budget: the target impedance divided by two pi f gives the maximum connection inductance for a tier. For a ten milliohm target, a tier covering 10 megahertz can have only 0.16 nanohenries, which means it has to be right at the part, while a 100 kilohertz bulk tier can have 16 nanohenries, which is several centimetres.

For routing, every capacitor gets its own pair of vias, power and ground side by side, at its pads, dropping to adjacent power and ground planes near the surface. I don't chain capacitors with traces, and I avoid one via serving several capacitors, since that via becomes the bottleneck. I alternate the polarity of neighbouring capacitors so the opposing currents cancel some field. And I let the planes do the distribution between tiers, so I keep them solid and the spacing between power and ground thin.

Finally, I verify. Mixed values produce anti-resonance peaks, where one capacitor is inductive and its neighbour capacitive. I'd run a PDN simulation or measure impedance, and if there's a peak above target I add more mid-value parts, add ESR, or use a polymer capacitor for damping."
:::

::: terms
- **Tier**: a group of capacitors of similar value serving a frequency band.
- **Inductance budget**: the maximum connection inductance for a tier, $Z_{target}/(2\pi f)$.
- **Backside array**: capacitors mounted on the opposite side of the board under a BGA.
- **Via pair**: power via and ground via at one capacitor, placed side by side.
- **Interleaved polarity**: alternating power/ground pad order so adjacent currents oppose.
- **Plane pair**: adjacent power and ground planes that act as a distributed capacitor and low-inductance connection.
- **Anti-resonance**: impedance peak between two tiers.
- **Target impedance**: $\Delta V/\Delta I$.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "small caps near the chip, big caps far away". An exceptional one:

- Derives tier distances from an **inductance budget** $L\le Z_{target}/(2\pi f)$ and gives numbers (16 pH at 100 MHz, 1.6 nH at 1 MHz for 10 mΩ).
- Specifies **own vias**, side by side, adjacent planes, alternated polarity, and no daisy-chains or shared via bottlenecks.
- Mentions the BGA backside array and via-in-pad with fill.
- Treats the planes as part of the network (spreading inductance, plane capacitance).
- Verifies with a PDN simulation or impedance measurement and manages anti-resonance.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Daisy-chaining**: connecting capacitor to capacitor with a trace, so the far ones see the sum of inductances.
- **One via pair for a whole row of capacitors.** The vias dominate and parallel benefit disappears.
- **Placing the big capacitor next to the pin and the small ones farther away.** The hierarchy is reversed.
- **Routing power to capacitors with thin traces** rather than planes, so their effective connection is high-inductance.
- **Forgetting the ground vias**: only the power pad gets attention, but the ground path is half the loop.
:::

::: deep
## A worked example: FPGA core rail

Suppose an FPGA core rail is 1.0 V ±3 % (30 mV) and the worst-case current step is 3 A. Then

$$Z_{target}=\frac{0.03}{3}=10\ \text{m}\Omega$$

from DC up to the frequency at which package and die capacitance take over (assume 100 MHz).

**Step 1: allowed inductance per frequency.** The connection inductance that keeps $|Z|\le 10$ mΩ is $L\le Z_{target}/(2\pi f)$:

Figure: The faster the current you must supply, the smaller the inductance you can afford between the capacitors and the chip. Bulk capacitors at 100 kHz can sit at 16 nH (centimetres away); capacitors serving 100 MHz must have 16 pH, which means a large parallel array of parts with their own vias under the BGA.

**Step 2: tiers.**

| Tier | Parts | Total $L_{one}$ per part | Number needed | Placement |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High (up to ~100 MHz) | 100 nF 0402 | ≈0.8 nH (ESL + mount) | $0.8\text{ nH}/16\text{ pH}=50$ | backside under BGA, own vias |
| Mid (~1–10 MHz) | 4.7 µF 0603 | ≈1.5 nH | ≈10 with L_eq ≈ 0.15 nH | within ~10 mm |
| Low (100 kHz–1 MHz) | 47–100 µF | ≈2.5 nH | 1–2 | near regulator |
| Regulator | output caps and loop | | | at regulator |

(These counts are illustrative. In practice vendors provide a recommended network, with counts per rail, and you verify against it.)

The dashed lines on the right are the plane paths linking the tiers to the VRM: they are low impedance only if the planes are solid and close together.

**Via and pad detail.** For each 0402 capacitor, put the power via and ground via immediately next to its two pads. With via-in-pad (filled and capped) you save another 0.5 mm of fan-out. Two vias per pad halve the via inductance for the highest-frequency tier.

**Alternating polarity.** If two capacitors sit side by side, flip the second one's orientation: power-ground, ground-power. Adjacent via currents then flow in opposite directions, and mutual coupling reduces the pair's effective inductance (by 10–30 % in typical geometries; geometry dependent).

**Under a BGA.** The ball field has power and ground balls interleaved in a checkerboard-like pattern; vias drop through the board in the escape pattern. Place capacitors in the gaps on the back, with their vias adjacent to or shared with the ball vias only where the capacitor's own via pair isn't available. The best design lets each capacitor connect directly to a power/ground via pair already in the pattern, so the loop is short and nothing is shared.

## The plane pair is part of the network

Between capacitors and between the capacitors and the chip, current travels in the power/ground plane pair. Spreading inductance of a plane with spacing $h$ is about $\mu_0 h$ per square (0.25 nH at 0.2 mm), so keep $h$ small: 0.1 mm or less in power-hungry designs, and keep planes unbroken. A thin dielectric also gives plane capacitance of $\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/h$: about 38 pF per square cm at 0.1 mm for FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r\approx4.3$), so a 10 cm × 10 cm plane pair holds roughly 4 nF. That is small in value but has almost no inductance, so it matters above ~100 MHz.

## Verification

- Compute or simulate $|Z|$ across 10 kHz–200 MHz and compare to $Z_{target}$.
- Measure with a VNA two-port shunt-through method or a scope with a short-lead probe while the device runs a worst-case workload.
- Look for peaks: add mid-value capacitors near the peak, add some ESR, or change values to move the peak.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Mention "inductance budget" and "own vias, no daisy-chain". Those two phrases signal that you know how to design the network, not just place parts.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The benefit of alternating polarity (10–30 %) and the capacitor counts in the example are order-of-magnitude figures that depend on geometry and vendor models. Use the device vendor's recommended decoupling network and a PDN tool where possible.
:::

::: applications
- **FPGA and SoC core rails**: arrays of 100 nF on the back under the BGA, a mid-value ring, and bulk at the VRM.
- **DDR4/DDR5 memory power**: per-pin or per-group decoupling around the device with its own vias.
- **Processor VRM output**: multiple ceramics plus polymer capacitors close to the socket.
- **RF/PA supplies**: a bank of capacitors at the amplifier with a bulk capacitor nearby for burst current.
- **Multi-IC boards**: each IC gets its own local tier-1 and shares tier-2/3 on the rail.
:::

::: assumptions
- High-current, fast-edge devices needing a network; for small MCUs a capacitor per pin and one bulk per rail is enough.
- Four-layer or higher stackup with a power/ground pair close to the component side.
- MLCC values, ESL and mounting inductances are typical; real network design uses vendor guidance and measurement.
- Capacitor counts here follow from the simple inductance budget; mutual inductance and plane effects shift real numbers.
- Another valid approach is to start with the vendor's recommended capacitor list and placement drawing and verify it.
:::

::: followups
### Which capacitors go closest to the IC?
The smallest values: they carry the highest-frequency current, so they need the lowest connection inductance. Bulk capacitors handle slow load steps where several nH of connection are acceptable.

### Why not connect two capacitors with a trace and use one via pair for both?
Then the via pair carries the combined current and its inductance is shared by both, and the trace between them adds more. The second capacitor's parallel benefit is lost. Each capacitor gets its own vias.

### How do you handle the anti-resonance peak between tiers?
Add more capacitors in the gap value, add ESR (polymer bulk or a small series resistor on a tier), shrink the plane spacing for distributed capacitance, or shift values to move the peak where it matters less. Then re-simulate.

### How do you know how many capacitors you need?
$Z_{target}=\Delta V/\Delta I$, then the inductance budget $L_{eq}\le Z_{target}/(2\pi f_{max})$, then $N=L_{one}/L_{eq}$ for the high-frequency tier. Cross-check with the vendor's recommended network.

### Scenario: there's no room on the back of the BGA. What do you do?
Move to smaller packages (0201), use via-in-pad, lower the number of vias by using larger-value capacitors for the next tier, add embedded or on-package capacitance via vendor options, use thinner dielectric between power and ground planes to increase plane capacitance, and accept the risk that the highest tier is limited by what the package provides. Then verify the rail on the bench under worst-case load.
