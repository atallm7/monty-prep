::: basics
### In plain words
First ask what you need: speed, density and standby power. For fast memory inside a processor chip, the answer is the SRAM cell. A loop of two inverters holds the bit, and two access transistors connect it to the bit lines for reading and writing. For dense main memory, the answer is a DRAM cell, one transistor and one capacitor.

### Picture it
A rocker switch stays where you leave it, as long as it has power, which is SRAM. A bucket with a tiny leak holds water for a while, but you must top it up regularly, which is DRAM. The bucket is smaller, so you fit billions. The bucket picture misses that reading a DRAM cell drains it, so the value must be written back.

### Words you need
- **SRAM**: static RAM. Each bit sits in a loop of two inverters (gates whose output is the opposite of their input), each feeding the other. It needs no refresh. See [[q0882]].
- **DRAM**: dynamic RAM. Each bit is charge on a tiny capacitor, which leaks.
- **Word line and bit line**: the word line selects a row of cells. Bit lines carry the data to and from them.
- **Access transistor**: a switch that connects a storage node to a bit line when the word line is on.
- **Refresh**: periodically reading and rewriting DRAM cells before their charge leaks away.

### The simplest example
An SRAM cell has 4 transistors in the inverter loop plus 2 access transistors, 6 in all. To write, drive the bit lines and turn on the word line; they overpower the loop. Now a DRAM read. Say the supply is 1.2 V, the cell capacitor is 25 fF (femtofarads, a tiny capacitance), and the bit line is 100 fF, precharged to 0.6 V. A stored 1 shares charge and moves the bit line by 0.12 V, or 120 mV, which a sensitive amplifier (the sense amplifier) detects. The rest of this page covers read and write sizing, noise margin and refresh.
:::

::: testing
Whether you start from requirements (volatile or not, speed, density, power) and then build a one-bit store from the primitive you already know: feedback for SRAM, a capacitor for DRAM. The interviewer wants to see the 6T SRAM cell drawn correctly, an explanation of read and write operation, and the sizing constraints (cell ratio for read stability, pull-up ratio for writability). Mentioning the 1T1C DRAM cell and its charge-sharing read shows breadth.
:::

::: why
Memory dominates the area and power of most chips: caches, register files, FIFOs and buffers are mostly SRAM cells, and main memory is DRAM. The same questions appear in hardware interviews as "design a latch" or "how does an SRAM work". This question is also a test of design process: asking what kind of memory, then picking a topology and reasoning about its failure modes (read disturb, write failure, leakage).
:::

::: answer
"First I'd ask what the memory needs to be: volatile or non-volatile, how fast, how dense, how much standby power. For on-chip fast memory like a cache, I'd design an SRAM cell. The core is a pair of cross-coupled CMOS inverters, which gives two stable states, so it stores one bit as long as power is on. That's four transistors. Then I add two NMOS access transistors that connect the two storage nodes to a pair of bit lines, BL and BLB, with their gates on a word line. Six transistors in total.

To write, I drive BL and BLB to the new value and its complement, raise the word line, and the bit lines overpower the cell and flip it. To read, I precharge both bit lines high, raise the word line, and the side storing a zero pulls its bit line down slightly. A sense amplifier detects the small difference.

The design constraints are about ratios. For read stability, the pull-down NMOS must be stronger than the access transistor, so the node holding 0 doesn't rise high enough to flip the cell. For writability, the access transistors must beat the PMOS pull-up. If the question is about density instead, I'd use a DRAM cell: one transistor and one capacitor, which is smaller but needs refresh, because the charge leaks, and reading is destructive."
:::

::: technical
**Requirements first:** volatility, access time, density (area/bit), standby power, endurance, interface (single-port, dual-port).

**6T SRAM cell.**
- Storage: inverter 1 (MP1, MN1) and inverter 2 (MP2, MN2) cross-coupled; nodes Q and $\overline Q$.
- Access: MA1 (BL to Q), MA2 (BLB to $\overline Q$), gates on word line WL.
- **Hold**: WL=0, loop holds. No static current beyond leakage.
- **Read**: precharge BL, BLB to $V_{DD}$; WL=1. The side with Q=0 sinks current through MA1 and MN1, so BL falls, BLB stays high. A sense amplifier resolves a differential of about 50 to 100 mV.
- **Write**: drive BL/BLB to data and complement, WL=1. The side being written to 0 is pulled down through the access NMOS against the PMOS pull-up; once Q falls below the other inverter's trip point, the loop flips.

**Sizing ratios.** *Cell ratio* $\beta=\frac{(W/L)_{pull-down}}{(W/L)_{access}}$ typically 1.5 to 2.5 for read stability. *Pull-up ratio* $\gamma=\frac{(W/L)_{pull-up}}{(W/L)_{access}}$ is typically below 1 (weak PMOS) for writability. Read stability and writability pull the sizing in opposite directions: that is the central trade-off of SRAM design.

**Worked read-disturb check** ($V_{DD}=1.2$ V, $V_t=0.4$ V, access NMOS in saturation, pull-down in triode): the voltage on the Q=0 node rises to $V_q\approx0.23$ V for $\beta=1$, 0.15 V for $\beta=2$, 0.11 V for $\beta=3$. This must stay well below the other inverter's trip point (about 0.6 V).

**1T1C DRAM cell.** One NMOS and a storage capacitor $C_s\approx$ tens of fF. Read: bit line precharged to $V_{DD}/2$, WL on, charge sharing gives $\Delta V=\frac{V_{DD}}{2}\frac{C_s}{C_s+C_{BL}}$; the sense amp amplifies it and restores the cell (destructive read). Refresh needed because of leakage.

**Other options:** flip-flop/latch (fast, larger, used in register files); flash/EEPROM floating-gate cell for non-volatile storage.
:::

::: long
"I'd start with requirements, because 'memory cell' covers a lot. Does it need to survive power loss? How fast? How dense? How much standby power? For fast, on-chip, volatile storage, the answer is SRAM.

The core is a bistable loop: two CMOS inverters, cross-coupled so each one's output feeds the other's input. That has two stable states, Q high and Q-bar low, or the reverse, so it holds one bit as long as power is on, with no refresh, and nearly zero static power apart from leakage. That's four transistors. To get data in and out I add two NMOS access transistors, one from each storage node to a bit line, BL and BL-bar, with both gates tied to a word line. That's the six-transistor cell.

Writing: put the data on BL and its complement on BL-bar, raise the word line. The bit line that is low pulls its storage node down through the access transistor, against the PMOS holding it high. If the access transistor is stronger than the PMOS, the node falls past the trip point of the other inverter and the cell flips.

Reading: precharge both bit lines high, raise the word line. The node storing zero pulls its bit line down a little, tens of millivolts, and a sense amplifier amplifies that differential. The danger is read disturb: the access transistor and the pull-down form a divider that lifts the zero node. So the pull-down has to be stronger than the access transistor, a cell ratio of about 1.5 to 2.

So sizing is a tug of war: strong pull-down for reads, weak pull-up for writes.

If the goal is density, I'd switch to a DRAM cell: one transistor and one capacitor. It's much smaller, but the charge leaks, so it needs refresh, and the read is destructive, so the sense amplifier has to write the value back."
:::

::: terms
- **SRAM**: static RAM, a cross-coupled inverter latch per bit; no refresh.
- **DRAM**: dynamic RAM, one transistor and a capacitor per bit; needs refresh.
- **Word line (WL)**: row select line that turns on the access transistors.
- **Bit line (BL, BLB)**: column lines that carry data to and from the cell.
- **Access transistor**: pass NMOS connecting a storage node to a bit line.
- **Cell ratio ($\beta$)**: pull-down strength over access strength; sets read stability.
- **Pull-up ratio**: pull-up strength over access strength; sets writability.
- **Sense amplifier**: circuit that amplifies a small bit-line differential into a logic level.
- **Read disturb**: unintended flip or upset caused by the read operation itself.
- **Static noise margin (SNM)**: largest noise voltage the cell tolerates before flipping.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use a flip-flop" or just draws two cross-coupled inverters.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Begins with requirements and names SRAM, DRAM and non-volatile options.
- Draws the 6T cell correctly with the word line on the access gates and two complementary bit lines.
- Walks hold, read and write and says what happens electrically in each.
- Explains the sizing conflict (read stability versus write ability) with ratio values and a worked divider estimate.
- Mentions the DRAM alternative with charge sharing numbers, refresh and destructive read, and notes variation and low-voltage trends.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Forgetting the access transistors.** Two inverters store the bit, but without access devices there is no way to read or write it.
- **Using a single bit line.** The classic cell uses both BL and BLB: it makes writing strong and reading differential and noise-tolerant.
- **Making the access transistors the strongest.** That makes the cell easy to write but unstable on reads (read disturb).
- **Calling SRAM "static" because it needs no clock.** It means no refresh is needed; it still needs power.
- **Believing DRAM reads leave the data intact.** Charge sharing destroys it, so the sense amplifier restores it.
- **Ignoring leakage and variation** at advanced nodes, where margins shrink and assist circuits are used.
:::

## Step 0: choose the kind of memory

| | Latch/flip-flop | 6T SRAM | 1T1C DRAM | Flash (floating gate) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Storage | feedback loop | feedback loop | charge on capacitor | charge on floating gate |
| Transistors per bit | 8 to 24 | 6 | 1 plus a capacitor | 1 |
| Volatile | yes | yes | yes, needs refresh | no |
| Speed | fastest | fast (sub-ns to ns) | slower (tens of ns) | read fast, write slow |
| Typical use | registers, pipelines | caches, register files | main memory | storage |

For an interview "design a memory cell", the expected answer is almost always the 6T SRAM cell, with the DRAM cell as the alternative.

## From a latch to the SRAM cell

The bistable loop from [[q0882]] is two cross-coupled inverters, four transistors. In a flip-flop you control it with logic gates in front; in a memory array, you want the *smallest* possible gate to control it, so two NMOS pass transistors do it.

Node Q (between MP1 and MN1) is wired straight to the gates of MP2/MN2, and node Q̄ (between MP2 and MN2) returns to the gates of MP1/MN1 through the matching Q̄ labels: that is the bistable loop. MA1 and MA2 connect Q and Q̄ to BL and BLB, and share the word line WL.

Check the loop: the left inverter's gates are on net Q̄, and its output node (between MP1 and MN1) is Q. The right inverter's gates are on net Q, and its output is Q̄. So each inverter's output drives the other's input: the bistable loop.

## Operation, with equations

**Hold (WL=0).** The access devices are off. Say Q=1, Q̄=0: MP1 and MN2 are on, MN1 and MP2 are off. Static current is only leakage, which is why SRAM standby power is low and why a cache can sit idle for free.

**Read (WL=1, BL and BLB precharged to $V_{DD}$).** Assume Q=0. MA1 connects BL (at $V_{DD}$) to Q (at 0 V), and MN1 (on) pulls Q down. BL discharges through MA1 and MN1 in series. The risk: node Q rises above 0 because MA1 and MN1 form a divider. If Q rises past the trip point of the other inverter ($\approx V_{DD}/2$) the cell flips by accident, destroying the data.

First-order estimate with MA1 in saturation and MN1 in triode: with $\beta=k_{MN1}/k_{MA1}$,
$$\tfrac12(V_{DD}-V_q-V_t)^2=\beta\left[(V_{DD}-V_t)V_q-\tfrac12V_q^2\right].$$
Solving numerically for $V_{DD}=1.2$ V and $V_t=0.4$ V:

| $\beta$ | Read-node rise $V_q$ |
|---|---|
| 1 | 0.234 V |
| 1.5 | 0.180 V |
| 2 | 0.147 V |
| 3 | 0.107 V |

All sit comfortably below the 0.6 V trip point, but real margins are much smaller because of threshold variation, supply droop, temperature and the fact that the other inverter's trip point shifts. Foundry cells typically use $\beta$ of about 1.5 to 2 as a compromise with area.

**Write (WL=1, BL=0, BLB=$V_{DD}$ to write a 0 into Q=1).** MA1 now fights MP1: MA1 pulls Q down from the BL side; MP1 holds Q up. Q must fall below the right inverter's trip point so that Q̄ rises and MP1 turns off. So MA1 must be *stronger* than MP1. This sets the pull-up ratio: make the PMOS small (hole mobility is lower anyway) and the access NMOS larger. The two constraints:

- Read stability: pull-down (MN) $\gg$ access (MA).
- Write ability: access (MA) $\gg$ pull-up (MP).

So the sizing order is MN > MA > MP, roughly in a ratio such as 2 : 1.2 : 1. Raising $V_{DD}$ helps writing (the access device gets more drive) and also changes the read margin; this is why "assist" circuits (negative bit line, word-line boost, write assist) appear at low $V_{DD}$.

## The DRAM alternative

A DRAM cell is a single access NMOS and a storage capacitor $C_s$ holding charge for "1" and none for "0". The bit line has a capacitance $C_{BL}$ many times larger than $C_s$.

**Read by charge sharing.** The bit line is precharged to $V_{DD}/2$. When the word line turns on, charge redistributes: $V_{BL}=\frac{C_sV_{cell}+C_{BL}V_{DD}/2}{C_s+C_{BL}}$. With $V_{DD}=1.2$ V, $C_s=25$ fF, $C_{BL}=100$ fF and a stored "1" ($V_{cell}=1.2$ V): $V_{BL}=(30+60)/125=0.72$ V, so $\Delta V=+120$ mV; a stored "0" gives $-120$ mV. A sense amplifier amplifies this to full rail and writes the value back, since the read destroyed the cell's charge.

**Refresh.** Leakage drains $C_s$. If the cell may lose 0.3 V before the sense margin is gone, leakage of 1 fA gives $25\text{ fF}\cdot0.3\text{ V}/1\text{ fA}=7.5$ s, while 100 fA (hot, worst-case cell) gives 75 ms. DRAM standards refresh every row within a set window, on the order of tens of milliseconds, to cover the leakiest cells.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Cell capacitance, leakage and refresh windows vary by technology, temperature and generation. The numbers above are illustrative, not from a specific device.

## Verification you would mention

1. **Static noise margin** for hold, read and write modes across PVT corners and with threshold-voltage variation (Monte Carlo).
2. **Write margin**: can the bit lines flip the cell at the weakest corner?
3. **Read current**: enough to discharge the bit line by the sense threshold in the available time.
4. **Leakage** per cell times millions of cells.
5. **Soft errors**: radiation can flip a stored bit; ECC protects large arrays ([[q0887]] is related only through bistability).

> [!TIP]
> If the interviewer wants "a memory cell" in the digital-design sense (not transistor-level), the D latch or D flip-flop from [[q0882]] is a valid answer: one bit of state. Say so, then offer the denser SRAM cell for arrays.

::: applications
- **CPU caches and register files**: 6T (or 8T) SRAM for L1/L2/L3 and the register file.
- **FPGA configuration and block RAM**: SRAM-based cells.
- **Main memory** (DDR DRAM): billions of 1T1C cells.
- **Embedded MCU RAM**: SRAM macros that can retain data at low standby power.
- **Non-volatile storage** (flash, EEPROM, FRAM): floating-gate or ferroelectric cells for code and data.
:::

::: assumptions
- Standard CMOS six-transistor cell with single-port access; dual-port and 8T cells separate read and write ports to relax the ratio constraints.
- First-order MOSFET equations (square law) and a symmetric cell; real cells have threshold variation and short-channel effects.
- The DRAM example uses assumed capacitances and leakage, not data for a specific part.
- "Memory cell" is treated as one bit of storage. If the question means a register or memory word, extend with multiple bits sharing a word line ([[q0886]]).
:::

::: followups
### Why does the 6T cell use both BL and BLB?
Differential access doubles the signal swing and cancels common-mode noise (supply noise, coupling) in the sense amplifier. For writing, one side pulls down hard through its access device while the other side is driven high, which gives a strong, reliable flip. A single-ended cell (like the 5T) can be written only with extra assist circuitry.

### How do you size the transistors?
Make the pull-down NMOS strongest (read stability, cell ratio about 1.5 to 2.5), the access NMOS in the middle, and the pull-up PMOS weakest (writability). Then validate static noise margins and write margin at slow and fast corners with variation. Area pressure pushes toward minimum sizes, so the tuning is a compromise.

### What happens if the word line is on while both bit lines are held at 0 V?
Both access transistors pull their storage nodes down. Both Q and Q̄ are dragged low, the loop loses its complementary states, and when WL turns off the cell recovers to a random state: the data is destroyed. This is the SRAM version of the SR latch's forbidden input.

### Why does DRAM need refresh but SRAM doesn't?
SRAM actively restores its state through the inverters connected to the rails, while DRAM stores charge on a capacitor with no restoring path. Leakage through the access transistor and junctions drains it, so each row is read and rewritten periodically.

### The array runs fine at 1.2 V but corrupts data at 0.7 V. What do you suspect and how do you fix it?
Lower $V_{DD}$ shrinks the SNM and write margin because thresholds do not scale: the cell gets harder to write and easier to disturb. Fixes include separate (higher) array supply, word-line boost or negative bit line write assist, 8T cells with separate read port, and larger transistors in the critical devices.
:::

::: interpretations
"Design a memory cell" could mean (a) a one-bit storage element built from logic gates (latch/flip-flop), (b) the transistor-level SRAM cell, or (c) a DRAM cell. I answer with SRAM as the main design, show the connection to the latch ([[q0882]]), and cover DRAM as the density-optimized alternative. If the interviewer is a digital-logic person, a D latch from gates is acceptable; if a circuit person, they want the 6T cell and sizing.
:::
