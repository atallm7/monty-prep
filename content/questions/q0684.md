::: testing
Whether you can define the differential voltage precisely ($V_d=V_+-V_-$), keep sign and amplitude conventions straight (peak vs peak-to-peak, per-leg vs differential), connect it to the receiver's decision (threshold, hysteresis, sensitivity), and measure it correctly. The classic traps are the factor of two between per-leg swing and differential swing and the ambiguity of "amplitude" in datasheets. Strong answers also contrast it with common-mode voltage ([[q0683]]) and mention that polarity (which wire is P and which is N) sets the logic value.
:::

::: why
The differential voltage is the signal: every spec that matters in a differential link, such as driver output swing ($V_{OD}$), receiver sensitivity ($V_{ID}$ threshold), eye height, noise margin and the amplitude you check on a scope, is defined on it. Misreading "350 mV" as per-leg instead of differential, or peak instead of peak-to-peak, gives a factor of two to four error in margin and termination calculations. Understanding it is also the prerequisite for eye diagrams and signal-integrity analysis of pairs ([[q0697]], [[q0698]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Definition**: $V_d=V_+-V_-$, so $V_\pm=V_{cm}\pm V_d/2$ ([[q0679]]).
- **Sign/polarity**: $V_d>0$ conventionally means logic 1; swapping P and N inverts the data.
- **Swing conventions**: peak ($V_{d,pk}$), peak-to-peak ($2V_{d,pk}$), and per-leg swing.
- **Receiver threshold ($V_{ID}$) and hysteresis**: minimum differential input for a valid decision.
- **Driver output ($V_{OD}$)**: differential output amplitude into a specified load.
- **Noise margin**: $|V_d|$ minus the receiver threshold ([[q0682]]).
:::

::: answer
"The differential voltage is the voltage between the two wires of the pair, V-plus minus V-minus. It's the signal. If each wire is the common-mode level plus or minus half of it, then the differential voltage is how far apart the wires are.

Its sign carries the data: if V-plus is above V-minus the receiver reads a one, if it's below it reads a zero, and the zero crossing is the decision threshold. So the decision doesn't depend on any fixed reference voltage.

The numbers need care. For LVDS the driver output is about 350 millivolts differential. That's the voltage across the 100 ohm termination, from 3.5 milliamps. Since it swings both ways, the differential waveform goes between plus 350 and minus 350, which is 700 millivolts peak to peak, while each individual wire only moves 350 millivolts peak to peak around its common-mode level. People often mix those up and are off by a factor of two.

The receiver needs a minimum differential input to decide, around 100 millivolts for LVDS, which gives about 250 millivolts of margin. I'd measure it on a scope as channel one minus channel two with matched probes, or with a differential probe."
:::

::: technical
**Definition and relations**
$$V_d=V_+-V_-,\qquad V_+=V_{cm}+\frac{V_d}{2},\qquad V_-=V_{cm}-\frac{V_d}{2}.$$
For a balanced swing the **per-leg peak-to-peak** equals the **differential peak** amplitude: legs each swing $V_{leg,pp}$, the differential swing is $\pm V_{leg,pp}$, i.e. $2V_{leg,pp}$ peak-to-peak.

**LVDS example**: $I_{drive}=3.5$ mA into $R_T=100\ \Omega$: $V_{OD}=3.5\text{ mA}\times100\ \Omega=350$ mV. Legs: 1.375 V and 1.025 V in one state (swapped in the other), $V_{cm}=1.2$ V. $V_d=\pm350$ mV (700 mV$_{pp}$), each leg 350 mV$_{pp}$.

**Receiver decision.** Output high if $V_d>V_{IT+}$, low if $V_d<V_{IT-}$; LVDS thresholds are typically $\pm100$ mV, so noise margin is about 250 mV; RS-485 thresholds $\pm200$ mV with $V_{OD}\ge1.5$ V into 54 Ω (margin about 1.3 V). Hysteresis (often tens of mV) prevents chatter.

**Differential current**: $I_d=(I_+-I_-)/2$ flows around the loop through the termination; the common-mode current $I_{cm}=I_++I_-$ is separate ([[q0683]]).

**Power and impedance.** For a differential impedance $Z_d$: $P=V_{d,rms}^2/Z_d$. Odd-mode voltage per leg $V_{odd}=V_d/2$, and $Z_d=2Z_{odd}$.

**Polarity.** Swapping P and N negates $V_d$ and inverts the data. Many high-speed receivers support lane polarity inversion to ease routing.

**Measurement.** $V_d=CH_1-CH_2$ with two matched single-ended probes (limited by probe skew and CMRR at high frequency), or a differential probe (check bandwidth, common-mode range, CMRR). Report the convention (peak, peak-to-peak, or per-leg).

> [!NOTE]
> Datasheets use "differential output voltage" for the magnitude across the specified load, and "differential input voltage" ($V_{ID}$) for the voltage applied between the inputs. Check whether numbers are peak, peak-to-peak or RMS.
:::

::: long
"The differential voltage is the voltage between the two lines of the pair, V-plus minus V-minus. It's the only thing the receiver cares about. Remember I can write each line as the common-mode voltage plus or minus half the differential voltage, so the differential voltage tells me how far apart the lines are, and its sign says which is higher.

The decision is simple: if it's positive, the receiver outputs a one; if it's negative, a zero; and the crossing at zero volts is the threshold. That's why the link doesn't depend on a fixed logic threshold. Swap the two wires and you invert the data, which is why some serial links let you flip the polarity of a lane in firmware to make routing easier.

The part that catches people is amplitude conventions. Take LVDS. The driver pushes about 3.5 milliamps through the 100 ohm termination, so the differential voltage is 350 millivolts. It swings both ways, so on a scope the differential waveform is plus and minus 350, 700 millivolts peak to peak. Each single wire moves from about 1.025 to 1.375 volts, so 350 millivolts peak to peak, around a common-mode of 1.2 volts. So 'the signal is 350 millivolts' is true, but is it per-leg peak-to-peak, differential peak, or differential peak-to-peak? Here the first two are the same number and the third is twice. Datasheet reading discipline matters.

For margin, the receiver needs a minimum differential input, about 100 millivolts for LVDS, so there's roughly 250 millivolts of margin. RS-485 has a much larger driver swing, 1.5 volts minimum into the 54 ohm load, against a 200 millivolt threshold, because it has to survive long cables.

To measure it I'd use two matched probes with scope math, channel one minus channel two, or a differential probe with enough bandwidth and common-mode range."
:::

::: terms
- **Differential voltage ($V_d$)**: $V_+-V_-$.
- **$V_{OD}$**: differential output voltage of a driver into a specified load.
- **$V_{ID}$**: differential input voltage applied across the receiver inputs.
- **Threshold ($V_{IT}$)**: differential input at which the receiver output changes state.
- **Hysteresis**: difference between rising and falling thresholds.
- **Peak-to-peak (pp)**: full excursion; twice the peak for a symmetric swing.
- **Odd-mode voltage**: voltage on each leg relative to $V_{cm}$ in differential drive: $\pm V_d/2$.
- **Polarity inversion**: swapping P/N, which negates $V_d$.
- **Noise margin**: $|V_d|$ minus threshold.
:::

::: exceptional

:::

::: mistakes
- **Quoting per-leg swing as the differential swing** (or the reverse), a factor of 2 error in margins.
- **Mixing peak, peak-to-peak and RMS.** Check the datasheet's definition.
- **Ignoring the load in $V_{OD}$.** $V_{OD}=I\times R_{load}$; a missing termination doubles it, a lower load reduces it.
- **Measuring $V_d$ with a single probe on one leg** and assuming the other mirrors it; skew, offset and asymmetry get missed.
- **Forgetting that polarity is part of the signal.** A swapped pair inverts data (and may violate protocol such as USB idle/J/K states).
- **Using single-ended thresholds** for a differential receiver; the threshold is on $V_d$ (often $\pm$ tens to hundreds of mV).
:::

::: deep
## Anatomy of the signal

Write the pair as the sum of a common part and a differential part:
$$\begin{pmatrix}V_+\\V_-\end{pmatrix}=V_{cm}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}+\frac{V_d}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.$$
The two vectors are orthogonal: "same on both" and "opposite on both". Any wire-voltage pattern is a unique mix of the two. $V_d$ is the amount of the second pattern, times two.

## Where 350 mV comes from

An LVDS driver is a current source steered between the two legs. With $I=3.5$ mA through a 100 Ω termination resistor (the load across the pair), Ohm's law gives $V_d=I R_T=0.35$ V. The common-mode level is the driver's output offset (about 1.2 V). Steering the current the other way flips the polarity:
$$V_d=\pm I R_T.$$
So the amplitude is a *current times a load* quantity, which is why the termination matters electrically and not only for reflections: a missing resistor makes $V_d$ much larger (or ill-defined), and a 50 Ω load halves it.

## Decision and noise margin

The receiver is, in effect, a comparator on $V_d$ with threshold at zero (plus a small offset and hysteresis). Specified minimum input $|V_{ID}|$ (for LVDS about 100 mV) and guaranteed maximum threshold determine the margin:
$$M=|V_d|_{min}-V_{IT}.$$
For LVDS driver $V_{OD,min}\approx250$ to 350 mV (the standard allows a range around 350 mV) and $V_{IT}=100$ mV the margin is 150 to 250 mV. For RS-485, driver minimum 1.5 V against receiver $\pm200$ mV gives $\ge1.3$ V, much more because of cable loss, noise and ground differences.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> LVDS and RS-485 numbers are typical values from the commonly cited standards. Min/max limits vary with standard revision and temperature; take them from the datasheet.

## Amplitude conventions

Take a sine-like differential signal with amplitude $A$ (peak). Then:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Differential peak | $A$ |
| Differential peak-to-peak | $2A$ |
| Per-leg peak (around $V_{cm}$) | $A/2$ |
| Per-leg peak-to-peak | $A$ |
| Differential RMS (sine) | $A/\sqrt2$ |

Transmitter specs for PCIe, SATA and similar links are usually quoted as differential peak-to-peak (for example, a minimum of about 800 mV differential peak-to-peak for early PCIe generations; check the revision), and 800 mV differential peak-to-peak means each leg swings only 400 mV peak-to-peak. If you are asked to size a receiver or an ESD diode, convert to the same basis first.

## Measurement

- **Two matched probes** into two scope channels, math $CH_1-CH_2$. Check that the probes and cables are matched in delay (skew creates false common-mode and distorts $V_d$ edges), and that the scope's bandwidth and vertical range cover the common-mode plus swing.
- **Differential probe**: its input common-mode range (typically several volts) and CMRR at the signal frequency matter. A probe with 30 dB CMRR at 1 GHz leaves 3% of a 1 V common-mode swing as error.
- **Termination and probing point**: measure at the receiver end, with the termination in place; probing adds capacitance that can shift the result at high speed.
- **Eye diagrams** are drawn on $V_d$; eye height, eye width and jitter are all differential measurements ([[q0698]]).

## From $V_d$ to a logic level: hysteresis and a worked margin budget

Inside the receiver, a comparator with a small hysteresis band turns $V_d$ into a logic output. Suppose thresholds of $\pm25$ mV hysteresis around an input offset of up to $\pm20$ mV (typical figures for a generic receiver; read your datasheet). The driver provides at least 250 mV differential (worst case) after cable and connector loss of, say, 30% (a loss factor of 0.7): $250\times0.7=175$ mV at the receiver. Subtracting the 100 mV threshold leaves 75 mV of margin for noise on $V_d$, mode conversion and receiver offset. Putting numbers on it this way shows why differential swing is a budget: every dB of channel loss and every mV of mode-converted noise reduces it directly.

Mode-converted noise is what is left of common-mode noise after imbalance. With 1 V of common-mode noise and 1% conversion, 10 mV appears in $V_d$; with 5% (a bad connector) it is 50 mV, two thirds of the 75 mV margin. This is the quantitative reason that connector and layout symmetry matter as much as the nominal swing.

## Polarity and logic mapping

Convention: $V_d>0$ is logic high. Swapped pairs give inverted data. Where polarity matters (USB, where D+ and D- states define J and K), swapping is a real bug. For SerDes links, a receiver can invert polarity in firmware; a pair routed with intentional P/N swap to avoid vias would be fixed in this way. In scope captures, always say which leg is P.

## Related quantity: differential current and impedance

The differential current flows out on one leg and back on the other: $I_d=V_d/Z_d$ for an infinite line. For a 100 Ω pair with $V_d=350$ mV, $I_d=3.5$ mA: the same current the LVDS driver supplies. Power into the termination is $V_d^2/R=1.2$ mW at that level.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> When asked for an amplitude, always state the basis: "350 mV differential, which is 700 mV peak-to-peak differential, 350 mV peak-to-peak per leg." It shows you have been bitten by the ambiguity before.

::: applications
- **LVDS, SATA, PCIe, HDMI/DisplayPort transmitters**: driver specs defined on the differential swing.
- **RS-485/CAN transceivers**: driver $V_{OD}$ and receiver threshold define link margin.
- **Scope measurements**: differential eye, amplitude and rise time.
- **Differential ADC inputs**: full-scale range specified as differential peak-to-peak.
- **Instrumentation amplifiers**: the input is a millivolt-level $V_d$ on a large $V_{cm}$.
- **Signal-integrity simulation**: channel S-parameters in differential mode (Sdd21) describe $V_d$ transfer.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Balanced swing**: legs equal and opposite. If one leg moves more than the other, $V_d$ is still $V_+-V_-$ but $V_{cm}$ moves too.
- **Datasheet numbers** are typical or specified into a stated load; amplitude depends on termination.
- **Zero-crossing threshold** with small offset; real receivers have offset and hysteresis.
- **Linear measurement** with adequate bandwidth and common-mode range.
- **Peak vs pp vs RMS** are as stated; always check the convention in the spec.

Alternative convention: some texts define the differential mode voltage as $(V_+-V_-)/2$ (the odd-mode amplitude) so that $V_\pm=V_{cm}\pm V_{dm}$. It changes scale factors, not the physics.
:::

::: followups
### A driver sends 4 mA through a 100 Ω termination. What are $V_d$ and the pp swing?
$V_d=4\text{ mA}\times100\ \Omega=0.4$ V; the differential waveform swings $\pm0.4$ V, 0.8 V peak-to-peak; each leg moves 0.4 V peak-to-peak.

### The scope shows each leg of a pair swinging 0.8 V peak-to-peak. What is the differential peak-to-peak swing?
1.6 V peak-to-peak if the legs are perfectly complementary (the difference of two opposite 0.8 V swings). If only one leg moves (pseudo-differential), it would be 0.8 V.

### If the termination resistor is open on an LVDS link, what happens to $V_d$?
The driver's 3.5 mA current has nowhere to go but the receiver's high input impedance, so the driver output voltage rises until the output stage saturates, giving a large $V_d$ (of order a volt or more) with severe reflections. With a typical 100 Ω termination missing, the link may still decode but signal integrity is poor. It's a classic bring-up bug, easy to check with a DMM (about 100 Ω between P and N when unpowered).

### How would you check the differential eye height of a pair at 5 Gb/s?
Use a real-time scope with bandwidth of about 3 to 5 times the fundamental (or an equivalent-time scope) with a differential probe or two matched probes, trigger on a clean clock or recovered clock, and accumulate $V_d$ over many UIs. Eye height is measured at the centre of the unit interval from the lower inner to upper inner level. Include the effect of the probe on loading and use the scope's de-embedding if available.

### What if the pair's common-mode voltage is large (e.g. 40 V) but the differential signal is only 1 mV?
Now it is a CMRR problem. For an error under 1% of the signal (10 µV) you need $40\text{ V}/10\ \mu\text{V}=4\times10^6$, about 132 dB of common-mode rejection. A 100 dB part would leave 0.4 mV, a 40% error. In practice you use an isolation amplifier or a high-voltage-input instrumentation amplifier with a precision input divider or attenuator that rejects the common-mode before gain, and you keep the source impedances balanced.
