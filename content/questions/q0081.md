::: testing
Whether you can name the three terminals **and say what each one does and why it's built the way it is**. The interviewer wants:

1. Base, collector, emitter and the role of each: the emitter *emits* carriers, the base *controls* the flow, the collector *collects* them.
2. Why the structure is asymmetric: heavily doped emitter, thin lightly doped base, larger moderately/lightly doped collector, so the terminals are **not interchangeable**.
3. Typical voltages and current directions for an NPN (and the flip for a PNP).
4. A practical angle: pinouts vary by package, so you read the datasheet.
:::

::: why
Every BJT circuit starts with identifying which pin is which and what voltage it should sit at. Mixing up collector and emitter on a breadboard gives a transistor that "works" with a fraction of the expected gain, which is a classic debugging trap. Knowing why the three regions differ also explains key datasheet limits: why $V_{CEO}$ can be tens of volts but $V_{EBO}$ is only about 6 V, why the base is thin, and why the base is the terminal that draws the control current.
:::

::: concepts
- **Three-layer structure**: N-P-N (or P-N-P) with two junctions. See [[q0080]].
- **Doping profile**: emitter heavily doped, base thin and moderate, collector lightly doped. See [[q0065]].
- **Forward-biased base-emitter, reverse-biased base-collector** is the normal amplifying condition. See [[q0087]].
- **Current gain**: a small base current controls a larger collector current. See [[q0088]].
- **PN junction behaviour**: forward conduction, reverse blocking, breakdown. See [[q0072]].
:::

::: answer
"A BJT has three terminals: the base, the collector and the emitter. The emitter is the terminal that emits charge carriers into the base. The base is the thin middle layer, and it's the control terminal: a small current into the base controls a much larger current from collector to emitter. The collector collects the carriers that make it across the base.

They aren't interchangeable because the three regions are built differently. The emitter is very heavily doped so it can inject lots of carriers, the base is very thin and lightly doped relative to the emitter so most injected carriers cross it instead of recombining, and the collector is lightly doped and physically larger so it can support a high voltage and dissipate heat.

For an NPN in normal operation the collector is the highest voltage, the base sits about 0.7 V above the emitter, and the emitter is the lowest. Base and collector current go in, and the emitter current, which is their sum, comes out. For a PNP it's all reversed. And in practice the pin order depends on the package, so I'd always check the datasheet."
:::

::: technical
**Regions and roles (NPN).**
- **Emitter (N+)**: doping about $10^{19}$-$10^{20}$ cm⁻³. Its job is to inject electrons into the base with high efficiency, so electron injection dominates over hole back-injection.
- **Base (P)**: doping about $10^{17}$-$10^{18}$ cm⁻³ and a thickness of order 0.05-1 µm, thinner than the electron diffusion length (tens of µm), so most injected electrons diffuse across without recombining.
- **Collector (N-)**: doping about $10^{15}$-$10^{16}$ cm⁻³ and the largest physical region. Lighter doping supports the reverse bias (a wide depletion region) and gives a higher breakdown voltage; the large area dissipates power.

**Normal-operation voltages (NPN, forward-active):** $V_{BE}\approx0.65$-$0.7$ V (about 0.6-0.8 V depending on current), $V_{CE}>V_{CE(sat)}\approx0.1$-$0.3$ V, so $V_C>V_B>V_E$. For a PNP: $V_E>V_B>V_C$ with $V_{EB}\approx0.7$ V.

**Currents:** $I_C=\beta I_B$, $I_E=(\beta+1)I_B$, $I_E=I_C+I_B$.

**Datasheet limits:** $V_{CBO}$ (collector-base, emitter open), $V_{CEO}$ (collector-emitter, base open, lower than $V_{CBO}$), $V_{EBO}$ (emitter-base, typically only about 5-7 V for small-signal parts, due to the heavily doped emitter), $I_C(\max)$, $P_D$.

**Interchangeability.** Swapping collector and emitter gives the **reverse-active** mode: the roles of the junctions invert, but because the emitter is heavily doped and the collector lightly doped the injection efficiency is poor and the reverse gain is very low (typically around 1-10, device-dependent), and $V_{EBO}$ (about 6 V) limits the reverse voltage.
:::

::: short
"A BJT has a base, a collector and an emitter. The emitter injects carriers, the thin base controls the flow, and the collector collects the carriers. A small base current controls a large collector current. They aren't symmetric: the emitter is heavily doped, the base is thin and lighter, and the collector is lightly doped and large to handle voltage and heat. In an NPN, base and collector current go in and emitter current comes out, with the collector most positive and the base about 0.7 V above the emitter."
:::

::: long
"A BJT has three terminals: base, collector and emitter. The names tell you the job. The emitter emits charge carriers, the collector collects them, and the base is the control terminal between them.

Physically an NPN is a sandwich: an N-type emitter, a thin P-type base, and an N-type collector. The emitter-base junction is forward biased, so electrons flow from the emitter into the base. Because the base is very thin, only about a tenth to a micron, most of those electrons diffuse across it and get swept into the collector by the field in the reverse-biased collector-base junction. A small fraction recombine in the base or are lost to hole injection into the emitter, and that's what the base current replaces. So a small base current controls a large collector current, with a ratio $\beta$ of about 50 to 300.

The three regions are deliberately different. The emitter is heavily doped, around $10^{19}$ to $10^{20}$ per cm³, so almost all of the current across the emitter-base junction is carriers injected into the base rather than the other way round. The base is thin and moderately doped. And the collector is lightly doped and physically larger so it can hold the reverse voltage and spread the heat. This asymmetry is why you can't swap collector and emitter. If you do, you get a very low gain, maybe 1 to 10, and the emitter-base junction breaks down at only about 6 V.

For an NPN in normal use, the collector is the most positive terminal, the base is about 0.7 volts above the emitter, and current goes into the base and collector and out of the emitter, with $I_E=I_C+I_B$. A PNP is the mirror: emitter most positive, current out of base and collector. And pinouts depend on the package: a TO-92 2N3904 is E-B-C looking at the flat side, but others are different, so I always check the datasheet."
:::

::: terms
- **Emitter**: the heavily doped region that injects majority carriers into the base; marked with the arrow on the symbol.
- **Base**: the thin, controlling region; a small current at this terminal controls the main current.
- **Collector**: the region that collects carriers that cross the base; lightly doped and large.
- **Forward-active region**: base-emitter forward biased, base-collector reverse biased; amplification happens here.
- **$V_{CEO}$ / $V_{CBO}$ / $V_{EBO}$**: breakdown voltage ratings (collector-emitter with base open, collector-base with emitter open, emitter-base with collector open).
- **Injection efficiency**: fraction of emitter-junction current carried by carriers injected into the base (helped by heavy emitter doping).
- **Base transport factor**: fraction of injected carriers that reach the collector (helped by a thin base).
- **Reverse-active mode**: emitter and collector roles swapped; works, but with much lower gain.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "base, collector, emitter" and "the base controls the current."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names the roles by the physics (emit, collect, control) and ties them to the N-P-N structure.
- Explains why the regions are doped and sized differently (injection efficiency, thin base, high-voltage collector).
- Quantifies: emitter $10^{19}$-$10^{20}$, base $10^{17}$-$10^{18}$, collector $10^{15}$-$10^{16}$ cm⁻³, base width about 0.1 µm, $\beta\sim100$.
- States the normal bias voltages and current directions for NPN and PNP.
- Mentions practical points: $V_{EBO}\approx6$ V limit, reverse-active gain being poor, pinout variation by package.
- Notes that the base is the only terminal you "drive", and that in a current-controlled sense $I_B$ is the input.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Collector and emitter are interchangeable."** The device is deliberately asymmetric. Swapped, the gain collapses and $V_{EBO}$ is only a few volts.
- **"The base is thick so it controls the collector by field effect."** The base is a very thin region through which carriers diffuse; the BJT is current-controlled (a $V_{BE}$ exponential), unlike the field-controlled MOSFET.
- **"The collector emits electrons."** It's the other way round for names: the emitter emits.
- **Assuming the pinout is universal.** TO-92 transistors come as EBC, CBE and others. Check the datasheet, since a wrong pin gives a "mysterious" low-gain circuit.
- **Applying more than a few volts reverse across base-emitter.** $V_{EBO}$ for small-signal BJTs is about 5-7 V; exceeding it degrades the device (hFE drift).
- **Forgetting the sign convention for PNP.** In a PNP the emitter is the highest-voltage terminal and the currents flow out of base and collector.
:::

Figure: A simplified 1D picture of the NPN. Electrons injected by the heavily doped emitter (left) cross the very thin base and are swept into the lightly doped collector by the reverse-biased junction. The dimensions are not to scale (the base is drawn wider than reality, about 0.1-1 µm versus tens of µm for the collector). Real devices are laid out vertically with the emitter on top in a planar process.

**Emitter.** To get gain, nearly all of the forward current in the base-emitter junction must be electrons going into the base (for an NPN) rather than holes going back into the emitter. The ratio of these is set by the doping ratio, so the emitter is doped much more heavily than the base. The injection efficiency is
$$\gamma\approx\frac{1}{1+\frac{N_{AB}\,W_B\,D_E}{N_{DE}\,W_E\,D_B}}$$
(in a simple form). With $N_{DE}/N_{AB}=100$ and similar widths and diffusivities, $\gamma\approx0.99$.

**Base.** The base must be thinner than the diffusion length of the injected carriers so they cross before recombining. The base transport factor is approximately $\alpha_T\approx1-\frac{W_B^2}{2L_n^2}$. For $W_B=0.5\ \mu$m and $L_n=50\ \mu$m: $\alpha_T=1-\frac{0.25}{2\times2500}=0.99995$, essentially all electrons reach the collector. The base also needs enough doping to keep its resistance and punch-through voltage acceptable.

**Collector.** The base-collector junction is reverse biased, so most of the voltage is dropped across its depletion region. Light doping makes this region wide and gives a high breakdown voltage; high-voltage power BJTs have thick, very lightly doped collectors. The price is a high series resistance, which shows up as the saturation voltage $V_{CE(sat)}$.

## Why you can't swap the collector and emitter

Take a typical transistor and swap C and E. The base-collector junction (lightly doped collector) becomes the injecting junction, and its injection efficiency is poor because the collector is lightly doped relative to the base, so a large fraction of the current is hole injection from base into collector. The result is a reverse $\beta_R$ typically a few (sometimes under 1, sometimes up to about 10 depending on the process). Also $V_{EBO}$ is low (about 5-7 V for small-signal devices) because the heavy doping of the emitter makes its junction break down at a low reverse voltage, so the reversed device is limited in voltage.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Doping levels, base widths, reverse gains and $V_{EBO}$ vary widely between processes and part types. The numbers quoted are typical orders of magnitude for small-signal planar silicon BJTs, not specifications for any one device. Pinouts also differ between manufacturers and packages, so always check the datasheet for the specific part.

## The same terminals for power BJTs and ICs

- **Power BJTs** have thick, lightly doped collectors for voltage and a large emitter area for current; often the collector is the metal package tab.
- **In ICs** the NPN's collector is an N-well or epitaxial layer with a buried N+ layer to lower collector resistance, and the substrate acts as a parasitic collector of a PNP.
- **Multi-emitter transistors** appear in TTL input stages.
- **HBTs** (heterojunction bipolar transistors) use a wider-gap emitter (or SiGe base) to improve injection efficiency without needing extreme emitter doping.
:::

::: applications
- **2N3904 / 2N3906** small-signal NPN/PNP in TO-92: base-controlled switches and amplifiers.
- **Power BJTs like TIP120/TIP31 or 2N3055**: collector often tied to the metal case; used in linear regulators and audio output stages.
- **IC differential pairs and current mirrors**: matched emitters set by area ratios.
- **Bandgap references**: $V_{BE}$ of transistors with different emitter areas.
- **Optocoupler outputs**: phototransistor with the base driven by light.
- **RF SiGe HBTs**: thin, heavily doped SiGe base for GHz speeds.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Discrete silicon NPN**, forward-active unless stated; the PNP is the polarity mirror.
- **Typical doping and geometry values**; they vary by process, voltage class and frequency class. High-frequency devices have thinner bases; high-voltage devices thicker collectors.
- **Pinouts** depend on package and part number; I quote the 2N3904 TO-92 as an example only.
- **$V_{EBO}$ and reverse gain** are typical orders of magnitude; consult the datasheet.
- **Alternative answer**: a short version listing terminals and roles with the arrow convention is acceptable in a screening interview; the doping rationale is the differentiator in a technical round.
:::

::: followups
### Which terminal is the input and which is the output in a common-emitter stage?
The input is the base (the signal drives $V_{BE}$ or $I_B$), the output is the collector, and the emitter is the common terminal (to ground, often through a degeneration resistor). In a common-collector (emitter follower) the output is the emitter; in a common-base stage the input is the emitter and the output the collector. The name of the configuration is the terminal common to input and output.

### Why is the base so thin?
So carriers injected from the emitter cross it before recombining. The fraction that reaches the collector (base transport factor) is about $1-W_B^2/(2L^2)$, where $L$ is the diffusion length. With $W_B=0.5\ \mu$m and $L=50\ \mu$m it is about 0.99995. A thick base would make nearly all carriers recombine and the base current would be as large as the collector current, i.e. no gain. The base current is the recombination that does occur plus hole back-injection.

### What happens if I connect the transistor backwards (collector and emitter swapped)?
It still conducts, in the reverse-active mode, but with a gain of only a few. The collector is lightly doped, so the injection efficiency is poor. The transistor's emitter-base breakdown voltage is also only about 6 V, so reversed it may break down when you put full supply across it. Such circuits often "work" at tiny currents and mislead the designer, so check pinouts.

### Why is $V_{EBO}$ much lower than $V_{CEO}$?
The emitter is heavily doped on both sides of its junction with the base, which gives a very thin depletion region with a high field, so Zener-type breakdown occurs at about 5-8 V. The collector junction involves a lightly doped N- region, so the depletion region is wide and the breakdown voltage is tens of volts or more. Hence $V_{EBO}$ ≈ 6 V versus $V_{CEO}$ ≈ 40 V for a 2N3904.

### How does the PNP's terminal behaviour differ?
Polarities flip: the emitter is the most positive terminal, current flows into the emitter and out of the base and collector, and $V_{EB}\approx0.7$ V. Holes are injected from the emitter into the N base and collected by the P collector. All the same structural logic applies: heavy emitter, thin base, lightly doped collector. See [[q0086]].

### Scenario: you measure $V_{BE}=0.65$ V and $V_{CE}=0.05$ V on a transistor that is supposed to be amplifying. What's wrong?
$V_{CE}=0.05$ V is below the saturation voltage (typically 0.1-0.3 V), so the transistor is saturated or the measurement indicates the collector is pulled down too much. The base is over-driven for the load, or the load current demanded is too high, or the collector load resistor is too large. Reduce base drive, check $I_C(\text{demand})=(V_{CC}-V_{CE})/R_C$ against $\beta I_B$, and confirm the bias network. In saturation the base-collector junction is forward biased, so the device no longer amplifies.
:::

::: interpretations
"What are the terminals?" sits in the BJT block, so the expected answer is **base, collector, emitter**. The same wording is used for MOSFETs in [[q0090]], where the answer is gate, drain, source (and body). Don't confuse the two: the BJT's base is the *control* terminal and takes a current, while the MOSFET's gate takes essentially no DC current.
:::
