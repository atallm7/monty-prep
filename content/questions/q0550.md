::: testing
Whether you can resolve the switch node's built-in conflict: it carries the full inductor current, so it needs real copper width, and it swings the full input voltage in nanoseconds, so it must have minimal area. The interviewer wants the rule ("short, just wide enough, one layer, no vias, plane underneath, keep sensitive nets away"), the numbers behind it (IPC width for the current, displacement current for the area), and awareness of the neighbours: bootstrap capacitor, snubber, test point, inductor pad, feedback.
:::

::: why
The switch node is the loudest point in a buck: dV/dt of billions of volts per second. How you draw a few square millimetres of copper determines your radiated emissions, how much noise your feedback picks up, and how cleanly the converter regulates. It also determines whether you can probe it without ruining the measurement. People who treat it like any power trace (a wide pour, a few vias) get the worst of both worlds, and it is an easy mistake to avoid once you know the logic.
:::

::: concepts
- **dV/dt and displacement current**: $i = C\,dv/dt$, with $C \propto$ area. See [[q0537]].
- **Current capacity**: the SW trace carries the inductor current. See [[q0534]].
- **Return plane**: a solid plane beneath terminates the field locally. See [[q0535]].
- **Nodes and mechanisms in a buck**: [[q0546]].
- **Noise coupling to feedback**: [[q0552]].
- **Bootstrap and snubber placement**.
:::

::: answer
"The switch node swings from ground to the input voltage in a few nanoseconds, so any copper on it is a noise source through capacitance: current equals C dV by dt. But it also carries the inductor current, so it can't be a hair. My rule is: short and just wide enough. I route it directly from the IC's switch pins to the inductor pad, on one layer, with no vias, as wide as the current requires from the IPC chart and no wider, say 1.5 to 2 mm for 5 A, and I don't pour copper on it.

Underneath it I keep a solid ground plane on the next layer so the field lines end there instead of in the neighbours, and I keep other traces, especially feedback and compensation, well away on both sides and on the layer beneath. If there are several switch pins I join them with a small patch, not a big one. The bootstrap capacitor connects tight between the BOOT pin and the switch node, and if I have a snubber it goes right on the node. For probing I leave a very small test pad, or a spot for a ground spring, rather than a big test point.

The numbers: a 2 by 8 mm trace is about 3 pF over a plane and injects about 12 mA at 4 volts per nanosecond, while a 10 by 10 pour is 19 pF and about 76 mA. Resistance of the short trace is about 2 milliohms, so shrinking it costs nothing."
:::

::: technical
**Conflict and compromise.** Current: $I_{L,rms} \approx \sqrt{I_{out}^2 + \Delta I^2/12} \approx 5.0$ A; IPC-2221 outer-layer 1 oz, 20 °C rise: about 1.8 mm for 5 A (1.5 mm gives 4.4 A, 2 mm gives 5.4 A). Noise: $C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rA/h$, $i = C\,dv/dt$ with $dv/dt = 12\ \text{V}/3\ \text{ns}=4\times10^9$ V/s and $h = 0.2$ mm: 2 × 8 mm → 3.0 pF → 12 mA; 2 × 20 mm → 7.6 pF → 30 mA; 10 × 10 mm → 19 pF → 76 mA.

**Resistance is a non-issue for a short run.** 8 mm × 2 mm = 4 squares = 1.9 mΩ; $P = 25\times1.9$ mΩ = 48 mW in the copper. Long and thin is bad for another reason (inductance, antenna length); short and moderately wide is the optimum.

**Rules.**
1. Shortest path from SW pins to the inductor pad; place the inductor adjacent.
2. Width = IPC for the DC current (plus margin), not more; no pour; join multiple SW pins with a minimal patch.
3. One layer, no vias; if the inductor is on the other side, use several vias but keep the area minimal (not recommended).
4. Solid GND plane directly below (L2), no cut-outs; no other nets on the adjacent layers underneath the SW copper.
5. Keep FB, COMP, sense, enable, and other high-impedance nets at least a few mm away, on the far side of the IC, and cross only at right angles on another layer.
6. BOOT capacitor tight between BOOT and SW; snubber (if used) between SW and PGND at the low-side FET.
7. Small test pad or footprint for a ground spring.
8. Pour copper near SW only if it's tied to the quiet side, and never as an unconnected island.

**Damping.** If SW rings, use a BOOT resistor (a few ohms) or a snubber, but fix the hot loop first ([[q0548]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Capacitance numbers use a parallel-plate estimate with $h=0.2$ mm and $\varepsilon_r=4.3$; fringing and the actual stack-up change them by tens of percent. Always check the specific datasheet's layout guidance.
:::

Compare noise: 2 by 8 millimetres over a plane 0.2 millimetre away is about 3 picofarads and injects about 12 milliamps at 4 volts per nanosecond. A 10 by 10 millimetre pour is 19 picofarads and 76 milliamps.

Underneath, I keep a solid ground plane so the field ends there, and I keep other nets off the adjacent layers beneath the node. Feedback, compensation and other high-impedance traces stay on the opposite side of the IC, a few millimetres away, crossing at right angles if they must cross. The bootstrap capacitor goes tight between BOOT and switch node, and a snubber, if I need one, sits right on the node. For probing I leave a small pad for a ground spring.

If it still rings, I fix the hot loop first, then add a boot resistor or snubber."
:::

::: terms
- **Switch node (SW)**: node between the switches and inductor.
- **dV/dt**: voltage slew rate; $4\times10^9$ V/s for 12 V in 3 ns.
- **Displacement current**: $i = C\,dv/dt$ through parasitic capacitance.
- **IPC-2221 width**: the trace width for a given current and temperature rise.
- **BOOT resistor**: a small resistor in series with the bootstrap capacitor to slow the high-side turn-on.
- **Snubber**: RC across the low-side FET to damp ringing.
- **Return plane**: the ground plane under a trace.
- **Anti-pad / cutout**: a clearance in a plane; must not sit under SW.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "keep it short and small."

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the conflict (current width vs dV/dt area) and resolves it with numbers (IPC width, $C\,dv/dt$ for two sizes).
- Notes resistance is negligible for a short run, so "wide for resistance" isn't a reason to enlarge it.
- Gives the full rule list: one layer, no vias, plane below and keep other nets off the adjacent layers, keep FB away, bootstrap and snubber location, test pad.
- Mentions what to do if it still rings, in order.
- Notes that area, not just length, is what matters.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Pouring SW "for current capacity".** The extra area adds capacitance and noise with no real benefit.
- **Running SW through vias to the bottom layer** to reach the inductor. Adds inductance, area and a noise path.
- **Placing FB or compensation traces beside or under SW.** Pickup. See [[q0552]].
- **Cutting the plane under SW** for isolation or a creepage reason without a plan. It breaks the return path.
- **Big test points and long probe stubs on SW.** They add area and corrupt measurements.
- **Making SW as thin as a signal trace.** Then the resistance/inductance and heating rise; size by IPC.
:::

::: deep
## The conflict in numbers

Two facts about SW pull in opposite directions:

1. **Current**: the node carries the inductor current, DC 5 A plus a ripple of about 1 A p-p. $I_{rms}=\sqrt{25+1.02^2/12}=5.0$ A. IPC-2221 for an outer 1 oz trace at a 20 °C rise gives 1.8 mm; 1.5 mm gives 4.4 A, 2 mm gives 5.4 A.
2. **Voltage slew**: $12\ \text{V}/3\ \text{ns} = 4\times10^9$ V/s. Capacitance to the plane below (spacing $h = 0.2$ mm, $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$) is $C = 8.854\times10^{-12}\cdot4.3\,A/h = 0.19\ \text{pF/mm}^2$:

| SW copper | Area | $C$ | $i = C\,dv/dt$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 mm × 8 mm | 16 mm² | 3.0 pF | 12 mA |
| 2 mm × 20 mm | 40 mm² | 7.6 pF | 30 mA |
| 10 mm × 10 mm pour | 100 mm² | 19 pF | 76 mA |

The injection scales with area, so the answer is: size the width for current, keep the length minimum. The resistive argument for more copper is weak: 8 mm × 2 mm is 4 squares or 1.9 mΩ, losing 48 mW at 5 A, which barely matters in a 16 W converter (0.3%).

## Path of the injected current

The displacement current from SW flows into the plane below and neighbouring conductors, then returns to the converter's ground and input. If the return path is short and local (a solid plane directly below, ground vias nearby), the loop is small and the energy stays there. If the plane is cut or the return goes via a cable, the loop is large and radiates. So the plane is part of the solution: it terminates the field close to the source.

## Specific rules in detail

**Shortest path.** The inductor goes next to the SW pins; the SW trace is typically 3 to 10 mm. Choose an inductor footprint whose SW terminal faces the IC.

**Single layer, no vias.** Each via adds about 1 nH and area on other layers; if the inductor must be on the opposite side, use the minimal number of vias that carry the current (about 5 to 6 for 5 A at 1 A/via), not a pour.

**Several SW pins.** Join them with a small, tight patch; copper over a multi-pin IC's SW pads is already substantial.

**Plane beneath.** Solid ground on L2 with no cutouts, antipads or slots directly below; no other nets in the SW region on the adjacent layer. Dielectric from L1 to L2 is thin on power boards, which also reduces hot-loop inductance.

**Neighbours.** FB, COMP, enable and sense traces stay on the opposite side of the IC, and if they must cross SW they do so at 90° on another layer with the plane between. Keep at least a few mm of clearance from sensitive analog.

**Bootstrap.** BOOT capacitor from BOOT to SW placed immediately beside the pins; its other leg is wired directly to the BOOT pin. Optional series resistor (2 to 10 Ω) slows $Q_H$ turn-on and damps the ring.

**Snubber.** If needed: R and C in series from SW to PGND at the low-side FET, with the capacitor next to the SW pad, the loop through the plane short.

**Probing.** A tiny pad (or exposed via not connected to other layers) to attach a ground spring. A long probe ground lead rings and misleads.

**Thermal.** The IC dissipates heat through its exposed pad, not the SW trace; don't enlarge SW for cooling. The inductor DCR heat goes through its pads; give them copper but keep it off the SW net where possible.

## When SW has to be longer

If mechanical constraints force the inductor farther, widen modestly for the inductance and resistance, keep the plane directly below, and shield with a ground pour on the layer above or beside (tied to the plane with vias) rather than leaving neighbours exposed. Accept a bit more EMI and check with a pre-compliance scan; better, move the inductor.

::: applications
- **Integrated buck regulators (QFN)** with the inductor placed immediately beside the SW pins, as in most datasheet layout figures.
- **Boost and buck-boost converters**, where the same logic applies to their switch node.
- **Motor-driver half-bridges**, where the phase node is the equivalent of SW, with larger currents and slower, controlled edges.
- **Isolated supplies**, where the primary switch node and the transformer winding play the same role.
- **Low-noise supplies for sensors/RF**, where SW area is minimised and shielded.
:::

::: assumptions
The numbers assume a 12 V input, 3 ns edges, 1 oz outer copper, a plane 0.2 mm below on L2 and a 5 A converter at hundreds of kHz; values scale with input voltage, edge rate and stack-up. For very high currents (tens of amps) a short, wider SW (or multiple parallel stubs) is needed, and the area increases; the compromise shifts but the principle stands. For integrated modules (with the inductor inside) the SW node is hidden, and the layout concerns move to VIN, GND and the output. A thick dielectric between L1 and L2 weakens the plane's termination and enlarges the hot loop. If the datasheet shows a different recommended shape, follow it.
:::

::: followups
### Why not use a wide pour on SW for lower resistance and better heat?
The DC resistance of a short SW run is only a couple of milliohms, so there is little to gain, while the area adds capacitance in proportion, which means more noise current into planes and neighbours and more radiation. The IC and inductor cool through their pads and planes. A pour on SW trades a negligible benefit for a real EMI cost.

### What if the inductor must be on the other side of the board?
Use the minimum number of vias that carry the current (about 5 or 6 for 5 A) arranged close together, keep the SW copper on each layer tiny, and make sure the planes between layers are solid except for the via antipads. It is a compromise: more inductance and area, so I'd verify with a scope and scan, and avoid it if I can.

### How does the plane below SW help?
It terminates the electric field close to the source, so the displacement current flows into the plane directly beneath and returns locally through the ground vias, instead of radiating or coupling into neighbours. It also shortens the hot loop. A slot or antipad under SW breaks this.

### Where do you place a snubber for the switch node?
Between SW and PGND right at the low-side FET's drain/source, with the capacitor beside the SW pad and the ground connection straight to PGND, so the snubber loop is tiny. If it's far, its own inductance prevents it from damping the high-frequency ring.

### How do you probe the switch node correctly?
With a high-bandwidth scope, a probe with a spring ground tip or a coax tip, touching SW and the nearest PGND point, so the loop is a few mm. A long ground lead adds inductance and rings with the probe's capacitance, showing false overshoot. I leave a small pad on the board for it.
:::

::: interpretations
"Route the switch node of a buck" is about the SW copper between the IC's SW pins and the inductor. If the question is for a controller with external FETs, the "switch node" is the junction of the FET source/drain pads plus the inductor pad, and the same rules apply with a compact triangle for the FETs and inductor. Siblings: noisy nodes ([[q0547]]), inductor routing ([[q0551]]) and feedback routing ([[q0552]]).
