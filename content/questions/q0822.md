::: testing
Whether you can compute a DAC's **resolution** (LSB, full scale, steps) and then go beyond it to **accuracy**: offset, gain error, reference error, DNL and INL, monotonicity, settling and glitch. As with the ADC version ([[q0819]]), the interviewer wants you to say what information you need ("given all the required info"), do clean arithmetic with units, and show that you know a 12-bit DAC is not a 12-bit-accurate DAC. A DAC-specific twist worth mentioning: unlike an ADC, a DAC has no quantization *noise* in the measurement sense, because the output levels are exact numbers of LSBs from the ideal only up to the errors listed here.
:::

::: why
Every DAC datasheet leads with its bit count, but what your system sees at the output is limited by the reference, the linearity and the output stage. A set-point that must be within 1 mV, a bias that must be monotonic so a control loop doesn't hunt, a waveform whose distortion must stay under a given level, all depend on these specs. Doing the error budget before choosing a part is how you avoid buying a 16-bit DAC with a 0.5% reference, or using an 8-bit DAC where 12 bits are needed.
:::

::: concepts
- **Resolution**: $V_{LSB}=V_{ref}/2^N$; full scale $V_{ref}(1-2^{-N})$ ([[q0820]]).
- **Offset and gain error**: shift and slope of the whole transfer line.
- **DNL / INL**: step-size error and cumulative deviation from the line; monotonic if DNL > −1 LSB.
- **Reference accuracy and drift**: scale the whole output.
- **Settling time and glitch energy**: dynamic errors after a code change.
- **Error combination**: worst-case sum versus root-sum-square ([[q0819]]).
:::

::: answer
"Precision of a DAC has two parts. The first is resolution: the smallest step it can make, one LSB, which is the reference over $2^N$. A 12-bit DAC with a 3.3 V reference has 0.806 mV steps, and the maximum output is 3.3 V times 4095 over 4096, which is 3.299 V, one LSB short of the reference.

The second part is accuracy, how close the output is to what the code says. For that I need the datasheet numbers. Say offset error 3 mV, gain error 0.2% of full scale, a 0.1% reference, and INL of plus or minus 2 LSB. In LSB those are 3.7, 8.2, 4.1 and 2. Added up in the worst case that's 18 LSB, about 14.5 mV; if they're independent I'd root-sum-square them and get about 10 LSB or 8 mV. So that '12-bit' DAC is accurate to about 8 to 9 bits in absolute terms.

If I calibrate offset and gain, including the reference error, I'm left with INL, about 2 LSB, which is close to 11 bits. DNL tells me about monotonicity: if it's above minus one LSB, every step goes the right way, which matters for control loops. There are also dynamic specs: settling time, roughly nine time constants for 12 bits, and glitch energy at the major carry. So I'd report the resolution as 0.8 mV and the accuracy as a few millivolts, and say which parts of the error I can calibrate out."
:::

::: technical
**Resolution.**

$$V_{LSB}=\frac{V_{ref}}{2^N},\quad V_{out}(D)=V_{LSB}\,D,\quad V_{FS}=V_{ref}\left(1-2^{-N}\right)\ (\text{some parts: }V_{ref}\cdot D/(2^N-1))$$

| Example | $V_{LSB}$ | Max output |
|---|---|---|
| 8-bit, 5 V | 19.53 mV | 4.980 V |
| 10-bit, 2.5 V | 2.441 mV | 2.4976 V |
| 12-bit, 3.3 V | 0.806 mV | 3.2992 V |
| 16-bit, 2.5 V | 38.15 µV | 2.49996 V |

**Static errors.**
- *Offset error*: output at code 0 (or the intercept of the transfer line).
- *Gain error*: deviation of the slope from ideal, often quoted in % of FS or LSB.
- *DNL* (step $k$): $\text{DNL}_k=(V_{k}-V_{k-1})/V_{LSB}-1$. DNL > −1 LSB for all $k$ guarantees monotonicity.
- *INL* (code $k$): deviation from the straight line (end-point or best-fit), in LSB; accumulates DNL.
- *Reference error and drift*: relative error $\varepsilon_{ref}$ scales every output; a tempco of $\alpha$ ppm/°C over $\Delta T$ gives $\alpha\Delta T$.

**Dynamic errors.** *Settling time* to $\tfrac12$ LSB: $t_s=\tau\ln2^{N+1}=9.01\tau$ at 12 bits ($\tau$ = output RC or amplifier time constant). *Glitch energy* at major carries, in nV·s. *Output noise* in µV rms or nV/√Hz.

**Worked budget (12-bit, 3.3 V).** Offset ±3 mV (3.7 LSB), gain ±0.2% FS (8.2 LSB), reference ±0.1% (4.1 LSB), INL ±2 LSB:

| Combination | Result | LSB | Effective bits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Worst-case sum | 14.5 mV | 18.0 | $12-\log_2 18=7.8$ |
| RSS | 8.1 mV | 10.1 | $12-\log_210.1=8.7$ |
| After offset and gain calibration (and reference trimmed) | about ±1.6 mV | about ±2 (INL) | 11.0 |

Temperature: a 10 ppm/°C reference over a 50 °C swing drifts 500 ppm = 2.05 LSB at 12 bits, which survives calibration at a single temperature.

**Interpretation.** "Effective bits" here is $N-\log_2(E_{LSB})$; a convention, not a standard definition.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Conventions for "full scale" ($2^N$ versus $2^N-1$), INL reference line (end-point versus best fit), and whether gain error includes the reference differ between vendors. The numbers above are a typical-style example, not a particular part.
:::

::: short
"A DAC's resolution is one LSB, the reference over $2^N$: 0.806 mV for 12 bits at 3.3 V, with a maximum output one LSB below the reference. Accuracy is different: add offset, gain error, reference error and INL. With 3 mV offset, 0.2% gain, 0.1% reference and 2 LSB INL, that's about 18 LSB worst case, 10 LSB RSS, so about 8 to 9 real bits. Calibrating offset and gain leaves INL, about 11 bits. DNL above −1 LSB guarantees monotonicity. Settling takes about nine time constants at 12 bits."
:::

::: long
"I treat DAC precision as resolution plus accuracy, and I'd say that out loud because they're different.

Resolution first. One LSB is the reference divided by two to the N. For a 12-bit DAC on 3.3 V, that's 0.806 millivolts, which is about 0.024% of the range. And the top code gives 4095 over 4096 of the reference, 3.299 volts, so the DAC never quite reaches the reference.

Accuracy needs the datasheet numbers. Let me use offset of 3 millivolts, gain error of 0.2% of full scale, a reference with 0.1% tolerance, and INL of plus or minus 2 LSB. In LSB units that's 3.7, 8.2, 4.1 and 2. If I add them all, the worst case is 18 LSB, 14.5 millivolts. If the errors are independent I'd use root-sum-square, which gives about 10 LSB, 8 millivolts. So while it's a 12-bit part, its absolute accuracy is 8 to 9 bits.

The good news is that most of that is systematic. Offset and gain error, including the reference, can be calibrated with two measurements, and then what's left is mainly INL, about 2 LSB, so around 11 bits. What I can't calibrate away so easily is drift: a 10 ppm per degree reference over 50 degrees is 2 LSB.

Next, DNL. It tells me about step sizes; if it's above minus one LSB, the DAC is monotonic, which matters if the DAC is inside a control loop. And the dynamic specs: settling time to half an LSB is about nine time constants at 12 bits, and glitch energy at major code transitions.

So the summary I'd give is: 0.8 millivolt steps, a few millivolts of accuracy before calibration, about 11 bits after, assuming the reference is stable."
:::

::: terms
- **Resolution**: bit count $N$, or the LSB size.
- **Accuracy**: closeness of the output to the ideal for a given code.
- **Offset error**: output error at zero code or intercept error.
- **Gain (full-scale) error**: slope error of the transfer line.
- **DNL**: per-step error, in LSB.
- **INL**: cumulative deviation from the ideal line, in LSB.
- **Monotonic**: output never decreases with increasing code.
- **Settling time**: time to reach the final value within a specified band.
- **Glitch energy**: area of the transient at a code change (nV·s).
- **Temperature coefficient (ppm/°C)**: fractional drift per degree.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "LSB equals Vref over 2 to the N".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Computes **LSB, full scale (one LSB below $V_{ref}$)** and states the units and percentages.
- Separates **resolution from accuracy** and lists **offset, gain/reference, INL, DNL** with numbers.
- Builds an **error budget** (worst-case and RSS) and converts it to **effective bits**.
- Explains **what calibration can remove** (offset, gain, reference scaling) and what it can't (INL, noise, drift).
- Knows **DNL > −1 LSB implies monotonic**, and why that matters in control loops.
- Mentions **dynamic specs** (settling, glitch) and **reference drift**.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Equating bits with accuracy.** A 16-bit DAC with a 0.1% reference is accurate to about 10 bits.
- **Saying full scale equals $V_{ref}$.** It's one LSB below, unless the part defines codes differently.
- **Mixing DNL and INL.** DNL is the step error, INL the cumulative bow; small DNL can still give large INL.
- **RSS-ing systematic errors.** Offset and gain are repeatable; sum them in worst case or calibrate them.
- **Ignoring the output buffer.** Its offset and gain error add to the DAC's, and its load regulation matters.
- **Forgetting settling.** A DAC updated faster than its settling time has errors that depend on the previous code.
:::

::: deep
## Resolution: the number you can always compute

An $N$-bit DAC has $2^N$ output levels spaced $V_{LSB}=V_{ref}/2^N$ apart. From a 12-bit, 3.3 V example:

- $V_{LSB}=3.3/4096=0.8057$ mV (0.0244% of $V_{ref}$)
- code 2048: 1.65 V
- code 4095: 3.2992 V
- the output can be set to any of 4096 values; you cannot request 1.2345 V exactly, only the nearest multiple of 0.8057 mV (code 1532, 1.2343 V).

A resolution requirement is the inverse: if the output must be settable to 1 mV on a 3.3 V range you need $2^N\ge3300$, $N\ge11.7$, so 12 bits. If it must be settable to 100 µV on 10 V, you need $2^N\ge10^5$, 17 bits.

## Accuracy: what the output really is

An ideal transfer is a straight line through the origin of slope $V_{LSB}$ per code. Real DACs deviate in four ways.

**INL** is the deviation of the real code-to-voltage curve from the straight line. A typical shape is a bow (positive in the middle, zero at the ends once offset and gain are removed), as in the plot below, or a sawtooth from segment mismatch.

INL like this is a property of the resistor network or reference loading; it can't be removed by two-point calibration, but a lookup table would remove the repeatable part.

**DNL** concerns each step: ideal 1 LSB, real $1+\text{DNL}$. With DNL = −1 the step is zero (a missing level); below −1 the output goes backwards (non-monotonic). A control loop that increments the DAC to increase a quantity can then oscillate or run away at that code.

**Reference.** The output is proportional to $V_{ref}$, so a reference with ±0.1% tolerance contributes 0.1% of full scale: $3.3$ mV at 3.3 V, 4.1 LSB at 12 bits. Its temperature coefficient (for example 10 ppm/°C) adds 2 LSB over 50 °C. Many DACs use the supply as the reference, so supply noise couples directly.

## The error budget, step by step

| Source | Value | LSB (0.806 mV) |
|---|---|---|
| Offset | ±3 mV | 3.72 |
| Gain error (excl. reference) | ±0.2% FS = ±6.6 mV | 8.19 |
| Reference tolerance | ±0.1% FS = ±3.3 mV | 4.10 |
| INL | ±2 LSB | 2.00 |
| **Worst-case sum** | **±14.5 mV** | **18.0** |
| **RSS** | **±8.1 mV** | **10.1** |

Effective accuracy bits: $N-\log_2(E_{LSB})$ gives 7.8 (worst case) to 8.7 (RSS). After a calibration that removes offset, gain and reference scale, the remaining error is INL, ±2 LSB, 11 bits. (INL, DNL and noise remain; if the calibration is done at one temperature, add the drift terms back.)

## Dynamic precision

**Settling.** After a code change the output approaches its new value with a time constant $\tau$ (ladder resistance times load capacitance, or the buffer's closed-loop bandwidth). To be within ½ LSB at 12 bits: $t_s=\tau\ln2^{13}=9.01\tau$. With a 10 kΩ ladder and 10 pF load, $\tau=100$ ns and $t_s=0.9$ µs. If you update faster than this, each output error depends on the previous code: this is code-dependent distortion.

**Glitch.** At the major carry (0x7FF to 0x800) mismatch in switch timing creates a spike. Datasheets quote glitch energy (for example a few nV·s), which, divided by the settling time, gives an average error. Deglitching or segmentation reduces it ([[q0820]]).

**Noise.** Output noise (reference, resistor thermal noise $\sqrt{4kTR}$ for 10 kΩ about 12.9 nV/√Hz, buffer) sets the smallest repeatable change. With 20 kHz bandwidth that's about 1.8 µV rms from the ladder alone, well under an LSB.

## Where the numbers come from for an R-2R

The R-2R ladder's linearity is set by resistor matching. From the Monte Carlo in [[q0821]] (uniform tolerance, 8-bit), 0.5% resistors gave a worst INL of 0.54 LSB and a worst DNL of 0.98 LSB, 1% resistors gave worst DNL 1.97 LSB and occasional non-monotonic units. For a 12-bit R-2R at 0.1% the same method gave about 3.3 LSB worst DNL, so non-monotonic parts occur (about 29% of trials in that simulation), which is why real 12-bit DACs use laser-trimmed or segmented structures.

## How to answer "given all the required info"

1. $N$, $V_{ref}$: gives $V_{LSB}$, full scale and relative resolution.
2. Offset, gain error, reference tolerance and drift: gives systematic error.
3. DNL, INL: linearity and monotonicity.
4. Settling time, glitch energy, noise: dynamic limits.
5. Temperature range: scale the drift.
If the datasheet lists total unadjusted error (TUE), use it as the accuracy directly.
:::

::: applications
- **Programmable bias and set-point sources**, where absolute accuracy sets product performance.
- **Audio DACs**: resolution is high (16 to 24 bits) but what matters is SNR, THD and glitch, not DC accuracy.
- **Instrumentation and calibrators**: precision DACs with trimmed references, 16 to 20 bits, with calibration constants stored in memory.
- **Industrial 4–20 mA outputs**: 12 to 16 bits with accuracy of 0.05% to 0.1% of span.
- **Closed-loop control**: DNL and monotonicity of the DAC in the loop affect limit cycles.
- **Programmable power supplies and laser drivers**: reference and gain error set output accuracy.
:::

::: assumptions
- A **unipolar, voltage-output** DAC with a fixed reference. For current-output or bipolar DACs, scale and range definitions change, but the error types are the same.
- The example error values (3 mV offset, 0.2% gain, 0.1% reference, ±2 LSB INL) are illustrative, typical of mid-range parts.
- **Independent random errors** are assumed for RSS; offset, gain and reference are systematic, so worst case or calibration is more honest for those.
- The question says "calculate it given all the required info": I take that to mean the datasheet specs; without them only resolution can be computed.
- The "effective bits" measure is a convention, defined as $N-\log_2(E_{LSB})$.
:::

::: followups
### What is the maximum output of an 8-bit DAC with a 5 V reference?
$5\cdot255/256=4.980$ V. One LSB is 19.53 mV, and the range between codes is 19.53 mV.

### If DNL is −0.6 LSB at the major carry, is the DAC monotonic?
Yes. The step there is $1-0.6=0.4$ LSB, still positive, so the output keeps rising. A DNL of −1.2 LSB would mean the step is $-0.2$ LSB: output falls, which is non-monotonic.

### How much does a reference tempco of 25 ppm/°C matter for a 12-bit DAC over 40 °C?
$25\times10^{-6}\cdot40=1000$ ppm $=0.1\%$ of full scale, $4.1$ LSB at 12 bits. That is bigger than the INL, so for precise outputs a reference below 5 ppm/°C is used, or the temperature is compensated.

### How do offset and gain calibration work for a DAC?
Command two codes (say 5% and 95% of full scale), measure the outputs with a meter, and solve $V=a\cdot D+b$. Then pre-correct each code: $D'=(D_{wanted}-b/V_{LSB})/a$. The procedure removes offset, gain and reference scale errors at that temperature; INL stays.

### Harder: your DAC drives a heater controller and the temperature hunts around a set-point. Could the DAC be the cause?
Yes: if there's a non-monotonic step (DNL below −1 LSB) near the set-point code, the loop's integrator can be trapped on both sides of a "backwards" step and oscillate. Check by sweeping the codes and measuring each step. Other causes: DAC glitch at a major carry, reference noise or insufficient resolution, so that one LSB corresponds to a larger temperature step than the sensor can resolve.
:::

::: interpretations
"Precision" is again used loosely. I give (a) resolution (always computable: LSB, full scale), (b) accuracy via a budget, and (c) dynamic accuracy (settling, glitch). The DAC mirrors the ADC question [[q0819]], with the difference that a DAC's levels are *generated* rather than *measured*, so quantization appears as limited output step size rather than as noise on a measurement.
:::
