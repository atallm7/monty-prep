::: testing
Whether you understand the *engineering reasons* CMOS won, not just that "it's what chips use". The interviewer is looking for:

1. **Near-zero static power** and the reason for it (one network is always off, so no DC path between the rails).
2. **Full rail-to-rail swing** and the large noise margins and robustness that come with it.
3. **High input impedance**, which gives huge fan-out and easy interfacing.
4. **Scalability and density**: MOSFETs shrink well, CMOS needs no resistors, and the process integrates logic, memory and analog on one die.
5. A strong answer also names the costs: dynamic power, leakage at advanced nodes, ESD sensitivity, latch-up.
:::

::: why
This question checks whether you know why the entire digital world, from a USD 0.30 microcontroller to a GPU with tens of billions of transistors, is built the same way. That reasoning drives everyday decisions: why an idle MCU draws microamps, why a floating CMOS input wastes power, why lowering the core voltage saves so much energy, why you use a 74HC part rather than a 74LS part in a new design. It also sets up [[q0108]] (TTL vs CMOS) and [[q0110]] (CMOS power analysis), which push on the same trade-offs in more depth.
:::

::: concepts
- **Complementary pull-up/pull-down**: in steady state exactly one network conducts. See [[q0105]].
- **Static vs dynamic power**: static power flows all the time; dynamic power $\alpha C V_{DD}^2 f$ flows only when nodes switch. See [[q0110]].
- **MOSFET gate as a capacitor**: the insulated gate draws no DC current, so one output can drive many inputs. See [[q0093]].
- **Noise margin**: the gap between what a driver guarantees ($V_{OH}$, $V_{OL}$) and what a receiver requires ($V_{IH}$, $V_{IL}$).
- **Scaling**: shrinking transistor dimensions lowers capacitance and voltage per gate and raises density.
- **Ratioed vs ratioless logic**: in CMOS the output levels don't depend on transistor size ratios, unlike resistor-load NMOS logic.
:::

::: answer
"CMOS is used so much mainly because of power. In a CMOS gate the PMOS pull-up and NMOS pull-down are complementary, so whatever the input is, one of them is off and there's no DC path from $V_{DD}$ to ground. Static power is basically just leakage. You only pay energy when a node actually switches, about $CV^2$ per charge-discharge cycle. Older NMOS logic with a resistor or depletion load burned current every time an output was low, which is a non-starter when you have a billion gates.

There are a few other big wins. The outputs swing rail to rail, so you get large noise margins and the logic levels don't depend on transistor sizing. The inputs are MOSFET gates, so they draw essentially no DC current, which means one output can drive lots of inputs. And MOSFETs scale beautifully: shrink them and you get lower capacitance, lower voltage and more transistors per mm², and you can put logic, SRAM and analog on the same die.

The trade-offs are that dynamic power grows with frequency and $V^2$, leakage becomes significant at small nodes, and CMOS inputs are sensitive to ESD and need care with floating pins and latch-up. But the static-power advantage is the reason it took over."
:::

::: technical
**1. Static power ≈ leakage only.** For any steady input, either the PUN or the PDN is off, so $I_{DD,static} = I_{leak}$ (subthreshold + gate + junction leakage). Compare resistor-load NMOS: when the output is low, $P = V_{DD}^2/R_L$ flows continuously. At $V_{DD} = 3.3$ V and $R_L = 10$ kΩ that's 1.09 mW per gate, about 545 W for a million gates that are low half the time.

**2. Power proportional to activity.** $P = \alpha C_L V_{DD}^2 f + I_{sc}V_{DD} + I_{leak}V_{DD}$. Idle logic (clock-gated) costs almost nothing, which enables µA sleep currents and the whole battery-powered device market.

**3. Full-swing, ratioless outputs.** $V_{OH} = V_{DD}$, $V_{OL} = 0$ with capacitive loads, independent of $W/L$. Noise margins are a large fraction of $V_{DD}$ (e.g. 74HC at 4.5 V: $V_{IH} = 3.15$ V, $V_{IL} = 1.35$ V, outputs within 0.1 V of the rails at light load).

**4. High input impedance.** DC gate current is pA–nA, so static fan-out is effectively unlimited. In practice fan-out is limited by capacitance (speed), not DC current.

**5. Wide supply range and robustness.** Classic 4000-series CMOS runs from about 3 V to 18 V; 74HC from 2 V to 6 V. Static CMOS keeps working as $V_{DD}$ drops toward the thresholds, which allows voltage scaling (DVFS).

**6. Scaling and integration.** Gate delay ∝ $CV/I$ falls with feature size. CMOS needs no resistors and only two device types, so it's dense and cheap per transistor. SRAM, flash (with extra steps), ADCs, PLLs and I/O all share the process, which is what makes an SoC possible.

**Costs.** Dynamic power ∝ $f V^2$; leakage grows exponentially as $V_t$ falls; thin gate oxides need ESD protection; parasitic PNPN structures can latch up; MOSFETs have lower $g_m/I$ than BJTs for analog ([[q0120]]).
:::

::: short
"Mainly power. In CMOS the PMOS pull-up and NMOS pull-down are complementary, so one is always off and there's no static current, only leakage. You pay energy only when a node switches, about $CV^2$ per cycle. On top of that, outputs swing rail to rail for big noise margins, inputs are gates that draw no DC current so fan-out is huge, and MOSFETs scale and integrate densely. The costs are dynamic power at high frequency, leakage at small nodes and ESD sensitivity."
:::

::: long
"The headline reason is static power. In a CMOS gate the pull-up network is PMOS and the pull-down is NMOS, driven by the same inputs. For any steady input, one of the two networks is off, so there's no DC path from the supply to ground. The only static current is leakage, which in a mature process is nanoamps or less per gate.

To see why that matters, compare it with the NMOS logic that came before, where the pull-up was a resistor or a depletion transistor. Every time an output was low, current flowed from $V_{DD}$ through the load to ground. With a 10 k load at 3.3 V that's about a milliwatt per gate. Multiply by a million gates and you're at hundreds of watts doing nothing. CMOS only burns energy when a node switches, about $CV^2$ for each charge and discharge, so power scales with activity. That's why an MCU can sleep at microamps and a phone can idle for days.

Second, the outputs go all the way to the rails. That gives big noise margins and means the logic levels don't depend on sizing the transistors in some ratio, which makes design robust and simple.

Third, the inputs are MOSFET gates, basically capacitors, so they draw no DC current. One output can drive many inputs; the limit is how much capacitance you can charge quickly, not DC loading.

Fourth, it scales. Smaller transistors mean less capacitance, lower voltage and more gates per square millimetre, and the same process gives you logic, SRAM and analog blocks on one die, which is what makes an SoC possible.

It's not free. Dynamic power grows with frequency and with $V^2$, leakage becomes a real problem in advanced nodes, the thin gate oxide needs ESD protection, and you have to watch out for floating inputs and latch-up. But no other technology matches the energy per operation, which is why everything from a 74HC gate to a GPU is CMOS."
:::

::: terms
- **Static power**: power drawn with no switching activity; leakage only in CMOS.
- **Dynamic (switching) power**: $\alpha C V_{DD}^2 f$, the energy spent charging and discharging node capacitances.
- **Leakage current**: subthreshold, gate-tunnelling and junction currents through "off" devices.
- **Rail-to-rail swing**: outputs reach $V_{DD}$ and ground.
- **Noise margin**: $NM_H = V_{OH} - V_{IH}$, $NM_L = V_{IL} - V_{OL}$.
- **Fan-out**: how many inputs one output can drive while meeting its specs.
- **Ratioless logic**: output levels independent of transistor size ratios.
- **Latch-up**: a parasitic PNPN thyristor in the CMOS substrate triggering into a low-impedance supply short.
- **DVFS**: dynamic voltage and frequency scaling, exploiting $P \propto V^2 f$.
:::

::: exceptional
**Average answer:** "CMOS uses less power and is cheap."

**Exceptional answer:**
- Explains *why* static power is near zero (one network always off) and contrasts it with resistor-load NMOS or TTL, with a number showing how bad continuous current would be at scale.
- Distinguishes static from dynamic power and writes $\alpha C V^2 f$, noting power scales with activity.
- Lists the secondary advantages with mechanisms: full swing → noise margin, gate input → fan-out, scaling → density and speed, one process → SoC integration.
- Names the costs honestly: dynamic power at high frequency, leakage at small nodes, ESD/latch-up, and that BJTs still win some analog jobs ([[q0120]]).
- Makes it practical: µA sleep currents, floating inputs wasting power, picking 74HC over 74LS.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"CMOS uses zero power."** Static power is near zero, but a busy chip burns watts in dynamic power. Always split static and dynamic.
- **"CMOS is fast."** Early CMOS (4000 series) was *slower* than TTL. CMOS won on power and density, and became fast through scaling.
- **Ignoring leakage.** At advanced nodes, leakage can be a large fraction of total power, which is why power gating and high-$V_t$ cells exist.
- **Saying the inputs draw no current at all.** They draw no *DC* current, but every edge charges the input capacitance, which is real current at high frequency.
- **Forgetting the floating-input problem.** A CMOS input left floating can sit mid-rail and draw shoot-through current, the opposite of low power. See [[q0109]].
:::

::: deep
## The problem CMOS solved

Any logic gate needs a way to pull its output up and a way to pull it down. The question is what you use for the "other" side when the switch is on.

In this inverter, when $A = 1$ the NMOS is on and current $\approx V_{DD}/R_L$ flows continuously. With 3.3 V and 10 kΩ:
$$P_{static} = \frac{V_{DD}^2}{R_L} = \frac{3.3^2}{10\,\text{k}\Omega} = 1.09\ \text{mW per gate (while low)}$$

A million such gates, half of them low at any moment, would burn about **545 W** doing nothing. You also face a design tension: a small $R_L$ gives a fast rising edge but more static power and a worse $V_{OL}$ (the output low is the divider $V_{DD}\frac{R_{on}}{R_{on}+R_L}$), while a big $R_L$ saves power but makes the rising edge an $R_L C$ crawl. This is **ratioed** logic: the logic levels depend on the ratio of the pull-down strength to the load.

CMOS replaces $R_L$ with a PMOS that's driven by the same input. Now the pull-up is *on* exactly when the pull-down is *off*:

- No static current in either state (only leakage).
- $V_{OL} = 0$ and $V_{OH} = V_{DD}$ regardless of sizing: **ratioless**.
- The rising edge is actively driven by the PMOS rather than relying on a resistor.

## Power that scales with activity

Each time a node with capacitance $C$ is charged from 0 to $V_{DD}$, the supply delivers charge $Q = CV_{DD}$ at voltage $V_{DD}$, so energy $CV_{DD}^2$ leaves the supply. Half of it is dissipated in the PMOS during charging, the other half is stored on $C$ and later dissipated in the NMOS. With activity factor $\alpha$ (fraction of cycles in which the node makes a 0→1 transition):
$$P_{dyn} = \alpha\,C\,V_{DD}^2\,f$$

The consequence is huge: a gate that doesn't switch costs only leakage. Clock-gate a block and its power nearly vanishes. That's why a microcontroller can drop to single-digit µA in sleep with RAM retained.

## Full swing and noise margins

Because the on transistor carries no DC current with a purely capacitive load, the CMOS output sits exactly on a rail. And because the transfer curve is steep in the middle, the input thresholds can be far from the rails. For 74HC at $V_{CC} = 4.5$ V, the datasheet guarantees $V_{IH} \ge 3.15$ V, $V_{IL} \le 1.35$ V, and $V_{OH} \ge 4.4$ V / $V_{OL} \le 0.1$ V at 20 µA load. That gives about **1.25 V** of noise margin each side, versus **0.4 V** for standard TTL. See [[q0108]].

## Inputs that draw no DC current

A MOSFET gate is insulated by oxide, so the DC input current is leakage (74HC datasheets specify ±1 µA max, real parts are typically far lower). The consequences:

- **Fan-out** is limited by capacitance and speed, not by DC loading. TTL, by contrast, specifies fan-out as a count (10 standard loads) because each input sinks real current.
- **Easy interfacing**: a high-value pull-up or a sensor's weak output can drive a CMOS input.
- **The flip side**: an unconnected CMOS input is a tiny floating capacitor that picks up any charge nearby. It can float to mid-rail and draw shoot-through current or oscillate. Always tie unused inputs.

## Scaling and integration

Gate delay is roughly $t_d \propto \frac{C V_{DD}}{I_{on}}$. Shrinking feature size reduces $C$, and for decades (the "Dennard scaling" era) voltage scaled down too, so each generation gave faster gates with lower energy per operation at roughly constant power density. Even after voltage scaling slowed in the mid-2000s, density kept rising. A static CMOS process needs only NMOS and PMOS devices and no resistors in the logic, so it's dense and regular. The same process supports SRAM, PLLs, ADCs and I/O, which made the system-on-chip possible.

| Property | Resistor-load NMOS | Bipolar TTL (74LS) | Static CMOS (74HC / on-chip) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Static current | Large when output low | mA per package | Leakage only |
| Output swing | Ratioed $V_{OL}$ | $V_{OH}$ ≈ 3.4 V typ. at 5 V | Rail to rail |
| Input current | ~0 | Up to −0.4 mA low (LS) | ~pA–nA |
| Density | Medium | Low | Highest |
| Power scaling | Poor | Poor | ∝ activity |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The TTL numbers are typical datasheet values for 74LS parts; exact limits differ between manufacturers and subfamilies.

## The costs you should mention

- **Dynamic power** grows with $f$ and $V^2$: this is the term that limits modern processor clocks.
- **Leakage** grows exponentially as thresholds are lowered for speed. Advanced designs add power gating and multiple $V_t$ cell libraries.
- **ESD and overvoltage**: a gate oxide a few nm thick breaks down at a few volts, so every pin needs protection ([[q0112]], [[q0102]]).
- **Latch-up**: the n-well/p-substrate structure forms a parasitic PNPN thyristor. Overvoltage or current injection on a pin can trigger it into a supply short.
- **Analog performance**: for some amplifiers, BJTs still give more $g_m$ per amp and lower $1/f$ noise ([[q0120]]).

## Why buffers specifically are everywhere

If "these" means CMOS *buffers* (following [[q0105]]), the reason is the same physics. Any internal gate is small and has limited current. A buffer isolates it, restores clean rail-to-rail levels, and adds the drive needed for a long trace, a bus or a clock tree, for almost no static power.
:::

::: applications
- **Microcontrollers**: µA-level sleep current with RAM retention is only possible because idle CMOS logic draws only leakage.
- **Processors and GPUs**: DVFS lowers $V$ and $f$ under light load, exploiting $P \propto V^2 f$.
- **74HC/74LVC glue logic** replaced 74LS TTL in new designs for lower power and better noise margin.
- **Image sensors**: CMOS image sensors integrate pixels, ADCs and logic on one die, displacing CCDs in most cameras.
- **Battery-powered IoT**: coin-cell devices running for years depend on near-zero static current.
- **Mixed-signal SoCs**: radio, ADC, PMU and CPU on one CMOS die.
:::

::: assumptions
- I'm assuming **static CMOS logic** in a reasonably modern process. Dynamic CMOS logic, current-mode logic (CML) and ECL deliberately draw static current for speed, so the "no static power" argument doesn't apply to them.
- The power comparison assumes outputs drive **capacitive loads**. A CMOS output driving a resistive load (an LED, a pull-down) draws static current like any other driver.
- Leakage numbers depend strongly on process and temperature. At advanced nodes, leakage can be a substantial fraction of total power, so "static power ≈ 0" is a mature-process or low-leakage-library statement.
- If the interviewer means "why are CMOS buffers used so often", the answer shifts to isolation, level restoration and drive strength, covered at the end of the deep dive.
:::

::: followups
### If CMOS has no static power, why does my MCU still draw current in sleep?
Leakage through billions of off transistors, retention of SRAM, the always-on domain (RTC oscillator, brown-out detector, wake-up logic), any analog bias currents (regulators, references) and I/O: a pin driving an LED or fighting a pull-up draws real DC current. Typical deep-sleep currents of a few µA are mostly these, not switching.

### How does lowering $V_{DD}$ from 3.3 V to 1.8 V affect dynamic power?
$P \propto V^2$, so $(1.8/3.3)^2 = 0.30$: about 70% less dynamic power at the same frequency and capacitance. The catch is speed: drive current falls as $V_{DD}$ approaches $V_t$, so the maximum frequency drops. That trade-off is the basis of DVFS.

### Why did CMOS replace TTL even though early CMOS was slower?
Power and density. As integration grew, the static current of bipolar and NMOS logic made large chips impossible to cool. CMOS power scaled with activity, and scaling made CMOS fast enough. By the 1980s high-speed CMOS (74HC) matched 74LS speed at a fraction of the power. See [[q0108]].

### What happens if you leave a CMOS input floating?
It can drift to mid-supply where both FETs are partly on, causing crowbar current, extra heat and possibly oscillation or random logic state. Tie unused inputs to a rail (directly or through a resistor) or enable an internal pull-up/pull-down.

### What if you clock a CMOS chip so fast that dynamic power exceeds the thermal budget?
Then the chip overheats or throttles. You either lower $V$ and $f$, reduce switched capacitance (clock gating, smaller datapaths), or improve cooling. This "power wall" is why single-core clock speeds plateaued in the mid-2000s and designs moved to parallelism.
:::
