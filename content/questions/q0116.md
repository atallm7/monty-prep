::: testing
Whether you know that an op-amp's open-loop gain **rolls off like a single-pole low-pass filter**, and that feedback trades gain for bandwidth at a fixed product. The interviewer wants:

1. The definition: GBW is the frequency at which open-loop gain falls to 1 (unity-gain frequency $f_T$), and for a single-pole amplifier, gain $\times$ bandwidth is constant.
2. The use: closed-loop bandwidth $=\text{GBW}/(\text{noise gain})$, so choose an op-amp with enough GBW for your gain and frequency.
3. The caveats: it applies to **voltage-feedback** op-amps, noise gain is not always the signal gain, and slew rate (a large-signal limit) is separate.
:::

::: why
Every op-amp circuit has a bandwidth limit that comes straight from this one number. If you amplify a 100 kHz signal by 100 with a 1 MHz GBW op-amp, you get a gain of 10 at best, with phase shift. Picking an amplifier for an ADC driver, a transimpedance amplifier for a photodiode or a sensor front-end starts with the GBW budget. It's also the entry to stability, compensation and loop-gain concepts used in regulators and control loops.
:::

::: concepts
- **Open-loop gain $A(s)$**: a large DC gain $A_0$ with a dominant pole at $f_p$: $A(s)=\dfrac{A_0}{1+s/\omega_p}$. See [[q0126]].
- **Bode plot / -20 dB/decade roll-off**: above $f_p$ the gain falls 20 dB per decade. See [[q0129]].
- **Unity-gain frequency ($f_T$ / GBW)**: where $|A|=1$; equals $A_0f_p$.
- **Feedback factor $\beta$ and noise gain**: closed-loop gain $A_{cl}=\dfrac{A}{1+A\beta}$; noise gain $=1/\beta$.
- **Closed-loop bandwidth**: $f_{-3dB}\approx\beta\,\text{GBW}$.
- **Phase margin**: how close the loop is to oscillation near the crossover.
:::

::: answer
"The gain-bandwidth product is the product of an amplifier's gain and the bandwidth over which that gain is available. For a typical voltage-feedback op-amp, the open-loop gain starts huge, around $10^5$ at DC, but there's a dominant pole at a very low frequency, maybe 10 Hz, and above that the gain rolls off at 20 dB per decade. Because it falls at exactly that rate, gain times frequency stays constant along the roll-off, and that constant is the GBW. It's also the frequency where the open-loop gain hits 1, the unity-gain frequency. So a 1 MHz GBW op-amp has a gain of 100,000 at 10 Hz, 100 at 10 kHz and 1 at 1 MHz.

When you close the loop, you trade gain for bandwidth. The closed-loop bandwidth is roughly GBW divided by the closed-loop (noise) gain. A gain-of-10 amplifier built with a 1 MHz op-amp has about 100 kHz bandwidth, and a gain-of-100 one has about 10 kHz.

So when I choose an op-amp I take the highest frequency I need, multiply by the gain, and then add margin, say 5 to 10 times, for gain accuracy and phase. For example, gain of 50 at 200 kHz with 5% accuracy needs about 30 MHz. Two caveats: it only works like this for voltage-feedback amps, and slew rate is a separate large-signal limit."
:::

::: technical
**Single-pole model.**
$$A(s)=\frac{A_0}{1+s/\omega_p},\qquad |A(f)|\approx\frac{A_0f_p}{f}=\frac{\text{GBW}}{f}\ \ (f\gg f_p),\qquad \text{GBW}=A_0f_p=f_T$$
Example: $A_0=10^5$, GBW $=1$ MHz $\Rightarrow f_p=\text{GBW}/A_0=10$ Hz.

**Closed loop.** With feedback factor $\beta$ (the fraction of the output fed back), $A_{cl}=\dfrac{A}{1+A\beta}$. For $A_0\beta\gg1$:
$$A_{cl}(s)\approx\frac{1/\beta}{1+s/(\beta\omega_T)},\qquad f_{-3dB}=\beta\,\text{GBW}=\frac{\text{GBW}}{\text{NG}}$$
where the **noise gain** NG $=1/\beta$.
- Non-inverting: NG $=1+R_f/R_g$ = signal gain. Gain 10, GBW 1 MHz: BW $=100$ kHz.
- Inverting: signal gain $=-R_f/R_{in}$ but NG $=1+R_f/R_{in}=|A_v|+1$, so a gain of $-10$ has NG 11 and BW $=1\text{ MHz}/11=91$ kHz.
- Buffer: NG $=1$, BW $=$ GBW.

**Accuracy and rise time.** Magnitude error at frequency $f$: $|A_{cl}(f)|/A_{cl}(0)=1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}$ with $f_c=\text{GBW}/\text{NG}$. At $f=f_c/10$: 0.995 (-0.5%, -0.04 dB). A 20 kHz signal on a gain-10, 1 MHz GBW stage ($f_c=100$ kHz): 0.9806 (1.9% low). Step response rise time (10-90%): $t_r\approx0.35/f_c=3.5$ µs for $f_c=100$ kHz.

**Design example.** Gain 50 at 200 kHz with at most 5% gain error: $1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}\ge0.95\Rightarrow f/f_c\le0.329\Rightarrow f_c\ge608$ kHz, so GBW $\ge50\times608\text{ kHz}=30.4$ MHz. In practice choose 50 MHz or more.

**Limits.** (i) Slew rate limits large signals separately ([[q0117]]). (ii) Current-feedback amplifiers do not follow a constant GBW; their bandwidth depends mainly on $R_f$. (iii) GBW varies with temperature, supply and load; datasheets give a typical value. (iv) Real op-amps have additional poles beyond $f_T$, limiting phase margin.
:::

::: short
"The gain-bandwidth product is the constant you get by multiplying gain by frequency along the amplifier's roll-off. A voltage-feedback op-amp has a huge DC gain with a dominant pole at a low frequency, then falls 20 dB per decade, so gain times frequency is constant and equals the unity-gain frequency. With feedback, closed-loop bandwidth is the GBW divided by the closed-loop noise gain, so a 1 MHz op-amp running at a gain of 10 gives about 100 kHz. I use it to pick an op-amp: the gain I need times the highest signal frequency, with margin."
:::

::: long
"Every voltage-feedback op-amp has a dominant internal pole, usually from the compensation capacitor, at a very low frequency, around 10 Hz for a general-purpose part. Below that pole the open-loop gain is flat at maybe 100 dB, above it the gain falls at 20 dB per decade, a factor of 10 for every decade of frequency. Because gain drops exactly in proportion to frequency, their product is constant: that's the gain-bandwidth product. It's also where the open-loop gain reaches 1, the unity-gain frequency. A 1 MHz part has gain of 100 at 10 kHz, 10 at 100 kHz, and 1 at 1 MHz.

Feedback lets you trade gain for bandwidth along that line. The closed-loop bandwidth is the GBW divided by the noise gain, which is $1+R_f/R_g$. So with a 1 MHz op-amp, a gain of 10 gives 100 kHz of bandwidth, and a gain of 100 gives 10 kHz. For an inverting amp, remember the noise gain is one more than the magnitude of the signal gain, so a gain of minus 10 has bandwidth of about 91 kHz, not 100.

What does 'bandwidth' mean for accuracy? At the -3 dB frequency the gain is 30% down; if I need 5% accuracy I have to stay under about a third of that frequency. So for gain 50 at 200 kHz, I need $f_c$ about 600 kHz and a GBW of about 30 MHz, and I'd pick a 50 MHz part for margin. The rise time of a step is about 0.35 over the bandwidth.

Finally, GBW is a small-signal number. Big signals are limited by the slew rate, and current-feedback amplifiers don't have a constant GBW. And a close relative: if the loop crosses unity gain with poor phase margin you get peaking or oscillation, which is why the second pole location matters."
:::

::: terms
- **Gain-bandwidth product (GBW)**: the constant gain $\times$ frequency of a single-pole amplifier; equals the unity-gain frequency.
- **Unity-gain frequency ($f_T$)**: frequency at which open-loop gain magnitude is 1 (0 dB).
- **Dominant pole**: the lowest-frequency pole, which sets the roll-off.
- **Open-loop gain ($A_0$)**: DC gain without feedback, about $10^5$ for general-purpose op-amps.
- **Noise gain**: $1/\beta$, the gain seen by the amplifier's own error voltage; sets closed-loop bandwidth.
- **Closed-loop bandwidth**: $-3$ dB frequency of the feedback circuit, GBW/NG.
- **Roll-off**: -20 dB/decade for a single pole.
- **Phase margin**: $180^\circ$ plus loop phase at the unity-loop-gain frequency; indicates stability.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "gain times bandwidth is constant." An exceptional answer:

- Derives it from the **single-pole** model and shows $\text{GBW}=A_0f_p=f_T$ with numbers.
- Applies it with the **noise gain**, including the inverting configuration's $1+|A_v|$.
- Converts bandwidth into **accuracy and rise time**: gain error $1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}$ and $t_r=0.35/f_c$.
- Does a **selection calculation**: gain 50 at 200 kHz with 5% error needs 30 MHz GBW.
- States the **limits**: slew rate, current-feedback amplifiers, extra poles and phase margin, variation with temperature and load.
- Mentions that GBW isn't the usable bandwidth for large signals (full-power bandwidth).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"GBW is the bandwidth."** GBW is the product; the closed-loop bandwidth is GBW divided by noise gain.
- **Forgetting the inverting configuration's noise gain** ($1+R_f/R_{in}$).
- **Applying a constant GBW to current-feedback amplifiers**, whose bandwidth stays nearly constant as gain changes (set by $R_f$).
- **Using full GBW as usable bandwidth for 1% accuracy.** For 1% gain error, signals must be below roughly $f_c/7$.
- **Ignoring slew rate** for large signals; a 10 V, 100 kHz sine needs 6.3 V/µs regardless of GBW. See [[q0117]].
- **Assuming GBW is guaranteed.** It's a typical value and varies with temperature, supply and load; datasheets list min/typ.
:::

::: deep
## Where the constant comes from

An op-amp's internal gain stages have a huge DC gain. Compensation adds a dominant pole at low frequency, by putting capacitance on a high-impedance node (Miller compensation, [[q0117]]), so that the gain falls at 20 dB/decade all the way to the frequency where it equals 1. Writing $A(s)=A_0/(1+s/\omega_p)$, at $\omega\gg\omega_p$:

$$|A(j\omega)|\approx\frac{A_0\omega_p}{\omega}\ \Rightarrow\ |A|\cdot f=A_0f_p=\text{GBW}$$

## Feedback trades gain for bandwidth

Closing the loop with feedback factor $\beta$:

$$A_{cl}=\frac{A}{1+A\beta}=\frac{A_0}{1+A_0\beta+s/\omega_p}\approx\frac{1/\beta}{1+s/(\omega_p A_0\beta)}$$

The pole moves from $\omega_p$ to $\omega_p(1+A_0\beta)\approx\beta\omega_T$. So the closed-loop gain is $1/\beta$ and bandwidth is $\beta\,\text{GBW}$:

| Closed-loop gain (non-inverting) | Bandwidth with GBW = 1 MHz | Rise time ($0.35/f_c$) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (buffer) | 1 MHz | 0.35 µs |
| 10 | 100 kHz | 3.5 µs |
| 100 | 10 kHz | 35 µs |
| 1000 | 1 kHz | 350 µs |

## Noise gain vs signal gain

The relevant gain for bandwidth is the gain from the amplifier's *input error* to its output, $1/\beta$. For an inverting amplifier with $R_f=100$ kΩ and $R_{in}=10$ kΩ:

- Signal gain $=-10$.
- $\beta=R_{in}/(R_{in}+R_f)=1/11$, noise gain 11.
- Bandwidth $=1\text{ MHz}/11=91$ kHz.

For a summing amplifier with $n$ inputs the noise gain grows with $n$. For a transimpedance amplifier the noise gain is frequency-dependent ($1+Z_f/Z_s$), which is the stability trap that requires a feedback capacitor.

## Gain accuracy vs frequency

Closed-loop gain magnitude relative to its DC value, $f_c=\text{GBW}/\text{NG}$:

$$\frac{|A_{cl}(f)|}{|A_{cl}(0)|}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}}$$

| $f/f_c$ | Magnitude | Error | Phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.995 | -0.5% | -5.7° |
| 0.2 | 0.981 | -1.9% | -11.3° |
| 0.33 | 0.950 | -5% | -18° |
| 1 | 0.707 | -29% (-3 dB) | -45° |

The phase shift matters, too, for feedback systems and for measurement of timing. So "bandwidth" is a conservative statement of where gain is still accurate: choose GBW at least 10 to 20 times (gain $\times$ signal frequency) for 1% accuracy.

## Worked design: pick an op-amp

Requirement: gain of 50 (non-inverting), sinusoid up to 200 kHz, gain error at most 5%.

1. NG $=50$.
2. $1/\sqrt{1+(200\text{k}/f_c)^2}\ge0.95\Rightarrow200\text{k}/f_c\le0.329\Rightarrow f_c\ge608$ kHz.
3. GBW $=\text{NG}\times f_c\ge50\times608\text{k}=30.4$ MHz.
4. Add margin for temperature and unit-to-unit spread (GBW is typically specified with ±30% or more) and pick GBW $\ge50$ MHz.

Alternative: two cascaded stages of gain $\sqrt{50}=7.07$ each. The two stages' magnitude errors multiply, so for 5% total each stage needs $\dfrac{1}{1+(f/f_c)^2}\ge0.95$, i.e. $f/f_c\le0.229$ and $f_c\ge872$ kHz. That is GBW $\ge7.07\times872\text{ kHz}\approx6.2$ MHz per stage, far less than the 30 MHz a single stage needs. That is the classic reason to split a high gain across stages, at the cost of more parts and noise.

## Where it gets more complicated

- **Second pole and phase margin.** Real amplifiers have non-dominant poles near or beyond $f_T$. If the closed-loop gain curve crosses the open-loop curve where the phase has fallen to -135° or worse, you get peaking or ringing. A unity-gain buffer is the most demanding configuration.
- **Capacitive loads** add a pole with the output resistance and reduce phase margin.
- **Current-feedback amplifiers (CFA).** Their bandwidth depends primarily on the feedback resistor, not the gain, so GBW is not constant; some have bandwidths of hundreds of MHz at moderate gains.
- **Large signals.** GBW says nothing about how fast the output can slew; see [[q0117]] and [[q0118]].
- **Temperature and process**: $f_T$ depends on transconductance and compensation capacitor, typically dropping at hot temperature.

## Real hardware

- A general-purpose op-amp (about 1 MHz GBW) is fine for audio-rate and sensor signals at gains of 10 to 100. For a gain of 100 on a 100 kHz signal you want more than 50 MHz. ADC drivers, TIAs and video amps are chosen on GBW first.
- A photodiode transimpedance amplifier with a large input capacitance and a megaohm feedback resistor needs a small feedback capacitor for stability, and a GBW well above its signal bandwidth.
- Comparing datasheet figures: a "unity-gain stable" part guarantees stability at gain 1; "decompensated" parts have much higher GBW but are stable only at gains above, say, 5 or 10.
:::

::: applications
- **Choosing op-amps** for sensor, audio and ADC-driver stages by required gain and bandwidth.
- **Transimpedance amplifiers** (photodiodes, LiDAR): bandwidth, noise gain and compensation.
- **Active filters**: op-amp GBW should be well above (filter $f_0\times Q\times$ gain).
- **Current-sense amplifiers and instrumentation amps**: gain-bandwidth trade-offs at high gain.
- **Control loops and regulators**: error-amplifier GBW sets the loop crossover achievable.
- **Multi-stage amplifier design**: splitting gain across stages to preserve bandwidth.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Voltage-feedback op-amp with a single dominant pole** and ample phase margin; second poles and capacitive loading reduce the usable bandwidth.
- **Small-signal, linear** operation; large signals are slew-limited ([[q0117]]).
- **Resistive feedback network**; reactive feedback (integrators, TIAs) has frequency-dependent noise gain.
- **Typical datasheet GBW** at room temperature; production spread of ±30% or more is common.
- **Current-feedback amplifiers** are the exception: bandwidth set by $R_f$ and nearly independent of gain.
- If the question means the **gain-bandwidth of a single transistor stage or filter**, the idea (gain trades against bandwidth) is the same, but the constants differ.
:::

::: followups
### An op-amp has GBW = 10 MHz. What's the bandwidth of a non-inverting amplifier with gain 25?
$f_c=10\text{ MHz}/25=400$ kHz. For 1% gain accuracy keep the signal below about $f_c/7\approx57$ kHz.

### Does GBW apply to an inverting amplifier with gain -10?
Use the noise gain: $1+10=11$, so the bandwidth is GBW/11. For a 1 MHz part, 91 kHz, not 100 kHz. The difference is small at high gain but significant at gain -1 (noise gain 2, so half the GBW).

### Why can't I just use a 100 MHz op-amp for everything?
Higher-GBW parts usually cost more, draw more supply current, have more noise bandwidth (more integrated noise), can be less stable with capacitive loads and may be harder to lay out (parasitic oscillation). Choose what the application needs with margin.

### What happens if you operate a decompensated op-amp at unity gain?
Decompensated parts have a smaller compensation capacitor, so GBW is higher but the second pole is closer, giving poor phase margin at low closed-loop gain. At unity gain the amplifier peaks badly or oscillates. The datasheet specifies a minimum stable gain.

### Harder: why does GBW not determine full-power bandwidth?
Large signals are limited by how fast the internal compensation capacitor can be charged: slew rate $SR=I_{tail}/C_c$. The full-power bandwidth is $\text{SR}/(2\pi V_p)$. For $SR=0.5$ V/µs and a 10 V peak, that's only 8 kHz, even if GBW is 1 MHz and the small-signal gain is flat to 100 kHz.

### Scenario: your gain-of-100 stage, using a 1 MHz op-amp, has a flat response to 10 kHz but you see 30% less gain at 10 kHz on the scope. What happened?
At gain 100 the closed-loop bandwidth is $1\text{ MHz}/100=10$ kHz, so at 10 kHz you are at the -3 dB point: 29% down, with 45° phase shift. The op-amp is behaving correctly; use a higher-GBW part, or split into two stages of gain 10 (each with 100 kHz bandwidth).
:::
