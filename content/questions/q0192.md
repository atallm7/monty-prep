::: testing
Whether you can talk about "sinking" and "supplying" precisely. In a buck the inductor current always flows the same way, so the words have to mean something else: energy and power. The inductor **sinks (absorbs) power** during the on state, when it is charging from the input, and **supplies (releases) power** during the off state, when it is feeding the output. A strong answer says this, backs it with $p=v\,i$, shows the energy balance, and also points out the exceptions: the inductor supplies the output current continuously (from the load's viewpoint) and, in forced-CCM synchronous operation, can sink current from the output at light load.
:::

::: why
The sink/supply picture is the energy-flow model of the converter: the input is only connected for part of the cycle, the load needs power all the time, and the inductor is the buffer in between. This is why the buck is efficient, why the input current is pulsed, why a bigger inductor reduces ripple, and why the inductor can be thought of as a current source. It is also how to reason about regenerative or reverse-current behaviour of synchronous converters.
:::

::: concepts
- **Instantaneous power** $p=v\,i$: positive means absorbing, negative means delivering (passive sign convention). See [[q0006]].
- **Inductor energy** $E=\tfrac12Li^2$. See [[q0041]].
- **Continuity of current**: the inductor can keep supplying after the input disconnects. See [[q0043]].
- **Current direction** is constant in CCM. See [[q0187]].
- **Volt-second balance**: net energy per cycle is zero in steady state.
:::

::: answer
"I'd phrase it in terms of energy, because the current in the inductor always flows the same direction. In the on state, the input is connected through the high-side FET, the inductor sees about 8.7 V while carrying about 2 A, so it's absorbing power, roughly 17 W at that instant, and its stored energy and current are rising. Part of the input power goes into the inductor and part goes straight to the load. In that state the inductor is a sink for the input.

In the off state the input is disconnected, but the load still wants 2 A. The inductor has current and energy stored, and its voltage reverses, so it now delivers power: about 3.3 V times 2 A, 6.6 W, which goes to the load and capacitor. The inductor is now the supply and its current and energy fall.

From the load's perspective, the inductor supplies current the whole time, with the capacitor smoothing the difference. The energy the inductor absorbs in the on state equals what it gives back in the off state, about 9.6 microjoules per cycle in my example.

The exception is a synchronous converter in forced-CCM at light load, where the inductor current goes negative for part of the cycle and the inductor then sinks current from the output."
:::

::: technical
**Power into the inductor** (passive convention, SW to output current $i_L>0$): $p_L=v_L i_L$.

On: $v_L=+8.7$ V, $i_L$ from 1.49 A to 2.51 A, $p_L=+13$ W to $+21.8$ W (absorbing). Energy absorbed over $DT=550$ ns: $\Delta E=\tfrac12L(I_{pk}^2-I_{v}^2)=9.57\ \mu J$.

Off: $v_L=-3.3$ V, $p_L=-8.3$ W to $-4.9$ W (delivering). Energy released over $(1-D)T=1.45$ µs: $9.57\ \mu J$. Net over the cycle: zero.

**Closed-form check.** $\Delta E=L\,I_{out}\Delta I_L=4.7\ \mu H\times2\ A\times1.018\ A=9.57\ \mu J$, and the output takes $V_{out}I_{out}(1-D)T=3.3\times2\times1.45\ \mu s=9.57\ \mu J$ during the off state.

**Where the energy goes:**
- On: input delivers $V_{in}i_L$; the inductor absorbs $(V_{in}-V_{out})i_L$ and the rest, $V_{out}i_L$, goes straight to the output.
- Off: the inductor delivers $V_{out}i_L$ to the output (with $V_f$ loss in the diode).
- Output power is always $V_{out}\,i_L$ into the output node (load plus capacitor), so the output receives power continuously. The inductor *does not* sink or supply from the output's viewpoint; it always injects current into it.

**Total stored energy** swings between $\tfrac12L I_v^2=5.2\ \mu J$ and $\tfrac12 LI_{pk}^2=14.8\ \mu J$; the average $\tfrac12 LI_{out}^2$ is 9.4 µJ.

**Exceptions.** DCM: the inductor energy goes to zero every cycle. Forced-CCM with negative valley: for the part of the cycle with $i_L<0$ the inductor sinks current *from* the output, pumping energy back toward the input through $Q_1$'s body diode or channel (boost action).
:::

::: short
"The inductor current always flows toward the output, so I think in power terms. During the on state the input is connected, the inductor has 8.7 V across it and rising current, so it absorbs energy, it's sinking power from the input. During the off state the input is disconnected and the inductor's voltage reverses, so it delivers its stored energy to the output, supplying the load. In steady state those energies match, about 9.6 µJ per cycle in my example."
:::

::: long
"Sink and supply are about energy, because the inductor current never reverses in normal operation. I use power equals voltage times current across the inductor with the current entering the plus end.

During the on state the high-side FET connects the switch node to 12 V. The inductor has plus 8.7 V across it and between 1.5 and 2.5 amps flowing in, so it's absorbing between about 13 and 22 watts. That energy is going into the magnetic field: the current ramps up and the stored energy increases from about 5 to 15 microjoules. So in the on state the inductor is the sink, taking power from the input. At the same time the output node still gets current and the load is fed directly from the input via the inductor.

During the off state the input is disconnected, the inductor's voltage flips to minus 3.3 volts, and the same current now flows with the polarity opposite, so the power is negative: the inductor is supplying 5 to 8 watts and its stored energy drops back down. That energy goes to the load and the output capacitor. The output sees continuous power because the inductor current is always flowing into the output node, only its source of energy changes between the input and the stored field.

The numbers balance: absorbed during the on time is about 9.6 microjoules, and 3.3 volts times 2 amps times 1.45 microseconds is also 9.6 microjoules delivered in the off time.

The one case where the inductor sinks *from the output* is a synchronous converter that is allowed to conduct in reverse, for example forced-CCM at light load: the inductor current dips negative, which means it's pulling current from the output capacitor and pushing it back toward the input."
:::

::: terms
- **Sink (absorb)**: take power ($p=vi>0$) and store it as magnetic energy.
- **Source (supply)**: deliver stored energy ($p<0$).
- **Passive sign convention**: current enters the + terminal; positive $p$ = absorbed.
- **Stored energy** $\tfrac12Li^2$: in the magnetic field.
- **Energy transfer per cycle**: $L\,I_{out}\Delta I_L$.
- **Forced CCM**: synchronous mode allowing negative inductor current.
- **Reverse current**: current flowing from the output back to the switch node.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "It charges (sinks current) when the FET is on and discharges (supplies current) when it's off."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Clarifies that direction never changes, so sink/supply means energy absorbed/delivered, via $p=vi$.
- Quantifies with numbers ($+8.7$ V × current, 9.6 µJ per cycle) and shows the balance.
- Observes that from the output's perspective the inductor supplies current *all* the time.
- Notes that the stored energy is $\tfrac12Li^2$ and swings by $L\,I_{out}\Delta I$.
- Mentions the reverse-current case and DCM.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The inductor sources current only in the off state."** It delivers current into the output in both states; what changes is whether the input or the stored energy pays for it.
- **"The inductor is a sink because it consumes power."** In steady state it consumes none; it borrows in the on state and repays in the off state.
- **Assuming the energy absorbed equals $V_{in} I\,t$.** Only $(V_{in}-V_{out})I\,t$ goes into the inductor; the rest passes to the output.
- **Thinking the current reverses to "discharge".** The voltage reverses, not the current.
- **Forgetting reverse current in synchronous converters** at light load and the implications for the input rail.
:::

::: deep
## What "sink" and "supply" mean for an inductor

For a two-terminal element with current entering the $+$ terminal, $p=vi$ is the power it **absorbs**. If $p<0$ it is **delivering** power to the rest of the circuit. For the buck inductor (current SW → output, $v_L=V_{SW}-V_{out}$):

| State | $v_L$ | $i_L$ | $p_L=v_Li_L$ | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| On | +8.7 V | 1.49 to 2.51 A | +13 to +22 W | absorbs (sink) |
| Off | −3.3 V | 2.51 to 1.49 A | −8.3 to −4.9 W | delivers (supply) |

## Energy per cycle

Stored energy: $E_L=\tfrac12Li_L^2$.

- At the valley (1.49 A): $E=\tfrac12\times4.7\ \mu H\times1.49^2=5.2\ \mu J$.
- At the peak (2.51 A): $E=\tfrac12\times4.7\ \mu H\times2.51^2=14.8\ \mu J$.
- Swing: 9.6 µJ.

The output takes $V_{out}I_{out}(1-D)T=9.57\ \mu J$ during the off state, exactly the swing. That identity holds in general:
$$\Delta E=\tfrac12L(I_{pk}^2-I_v^2)=L\,I_{out}\Delta I_L=L\,I_{out}\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{Lf}=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}(1-D)}{f}.$$
So the inductor's energy swing is the off-time output energy. As power, $\Delta E\times f=4.8$ W of the 6.6 W passes through the inductor's storage, and the remaining 1.8 W flows from the input straight to the output during the on time ($V_{out}I_{out}D=1.8$ W).

Check: $6.6\ W=4.785\ W+1.815\ W$. ✓.

> [!TIP]
> Interview line: "About 27% of the output power goes straight from the input to the load during the on state; the other 73% is stored in the inductor and delivered later." $D=0.275$ is both a duty cycle and the direct-transfer fraction.

## The input and output views

| Perspective | What it sees |
|---|---|
| Input | A pulsed load: draws $i_L$ during the on state, nothing else. $C_{in}$ smooths it. |
| Output | A continuous current source $i_L$ with ripple. The capacitor takes the ripple. |
| Inductor | Borrows energy from the input in the on state, repays the load in the off state. |

So from the output's point of view the inductor supplies current continuously; from the energy balance's point of view it sinks in the on state and supplies in the off state. Both are true; saying which one you mean is the point of the question.

## Exceptions

**DCM.** In DCM the inductor current reaches zero before the cycle ends, so the stored energy returns to zero every cycle: all of $\tfrac12LI_{pk}^2$ is absorbed and then fully released, and the inductor holds no energy between cycles. The sink and supply roles still alternate, with an extra zero-power interval at the end.

**Forced CCM, light load.** Consider average $I_{out}=0.2$ A with the same 1.02 A ripple. The valley is $0.2-0.51=-0.31$ A: for a while the inductor current is negative, flowing from the output through $L$ to the switch node and out through $Q_2$ to ground (or back to the input through $Q_1$ on the next on time). For that part of the cycle the inductor is **sinking** current from the output and returning energy toward the input. At 0.2 A the net flow is still forward (the average current is positive), so this is a small circulating energy that costs efficiency rather than a rail-pumping problem; the pumping case is covered in the follow-ups.

## Reading it off a scope

Clip a current probe on the inductor leg and a differential probe across it. The product, if you multiply the traces, has the sign pattern above: positive during the SW-high interval, negative during the SW-low interval. The average of the product over a cycle is the inductor's net power, which is ~0 minus the DCR loss.

## Real hardware

- **DCR loss**: $i_{rms}^2R_{DCR}$ is always dissipated, so the net power is slightly positive (absorbed).
- **Core loss**: from the ripple flux swing; also positive.
- **Saturation**: stored energy capacity $\tfrac12LI_{sat}^2$ sets the maximum you can store per cycle. For a power-hungry converter you pick the inductor so that $I_{pk}<I_{sat}$.
:::

::: applications
- **Explaining why the buck is efficient**: the inductor buffers energy without dissipating it.
- **Input-current analysis**: the input only supplies power during the on state, so $C_{in}$ is needed.
- **Reverse-current protection**: a synchronous buck with a negative-current limit prevents pumping the input rail up.
- **Bidirectional converters** (battery chargers, USB-PD sink/source): the same inductor sinks or supplies energy in either direction depending on the control.
- **Energy-based sizing**: $\tfrac12LI_{pk}^2$ vs the core's energy-storage capability.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM, steady state**, small DCR/switch losses.
- **Passive sign convention** with $i_L$ from SW to output; reversing the convention flips the sign of $p_L$ but not the physical role.
- **"Sink/supply" interpreted as absorbing/delivering power**, not as current direction.
- **Ideal switches.** Real dead time and diode drops shift small amounts of energy to losses.
- **Alternative reading:** some interviewers intend "when does the inductor draw current from the input vs from ground", which is [[q0200]].
:::

::: followups
### In which state does the inductor "supply" current to the load?
Both, in the sense that its current always flows into the output node. In terms of power: it absorbs in the on state and delivers in the off state. The load always gets power; in the on state the input pays, in the off state the inductor's stored energy pays.

### How much energy does the inductor exchange each cycle?
$\Delta E=L\,I_{out}\Delta I_L=9.57\ \mu J$ in the example, equal to $V_{out}I_{out}(1-D)T$. Higher ripple means a bigger exchange per cycle.

### What if the inductor were twice as large?
The ripple halves, but the energy exchanged per cycle does not change: $\Delta E=L\,I_{out}\Delta I_L=V_{out}I_{out}(1-D)/f$ has no $L$ in it, because $L\Delta I_L$ is fixed by the volt-seconds. What changes is the total stored energy $\tfrac12LI^2$, which rises with $L$, so there is a bigger reservoir that varies by a smaller fraction.

### When does an inductor in a buck sink current from the *output*?
When the inductor current is negative: in forced-CCM synchronous mode at light load (valley below zero), or during a fast load release/downward output step where the controller keeps $Q_2$ on to pull $V_{out}$ down. Energy then flows from the output capacitor back to the input.

### Harder: when can a synchronous buck pump its own input rail up?
When the *average* inductor current goes negative, for example when the output is commanded down (dynamic voltage scaling) and the controller keeps $Q_2$ on to discharge the output capacitor. The inductor sinks from the output and pushes energy into $C_{in}$ through $Q_1$. Taking 22 µF going from 3.3 V to 1.8 V releases $\tfrac12C(3.3^2-1.8^2)=84\ \mu J$. Dumped into a 10 µF input capacitor at 12 V that raises it by about $E/(C V_{in})=0.7$ V. If the source cannot sink current (a series diode, or a charger that blocks reverse flow), nothing removes it, so designs include a reverse-current limit, a forced-DCM mode, or enough input capacitance.
:::

::: interpretations
"Sink/supply current" is read as absorb/deliver energy since the current direction itself never changes in CCM. If the interviewer instead means "from where does the current come," see [[q0200]]. If the interviewer means current direction, see [[q0187]].
