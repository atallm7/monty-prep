::: testing
Whether you can predict, from a layout fact (the capacitor is 1 to 2 cm from the pins), the electrical consequences: extra loop inductance, bigger overshoot and ringing on the switch node, stress on the FET's voltage rating, more EMI, a noisier input rail, possible false triggers in the controller, and some loss. The interviewer wants numbers ($L\,di/dt$), the ring-frequency picture, and the fix hierarchy: move the capacitor, add a small ceramic at the pins, then damp.
:::

::: why
This is the failure that sends teams into a second board spin: the converter works at light load but rings badly at full load, FETs fail intermittently, or EMC fails at 100 to 200 MHz. If you can reason from "the cap is far" to the symptoms and the cure, you can debug a real board quickly and you can justify the layout rule to a sceptical colleague.
:::

::: concepts
- **Hot loop and its inductance**: [[q0548]], [[q0546]].
- **$L\,di/dt$ overshoot** and LC ringing with the FET's $C_{oss}$.
- **Capacitor ESL and mounting inductance**: a far capacitor is a capacitor with a long lead. See [[q0509]].
- **Voltage rating of the FET**: overshoot must fit under $V_{DS(max)}$ with margin.
- **EMI**: ring frequency and loop area set the radiated emissions. See [[q0547]].
- **Input source impedance and damping**.
:::

::: answer
"If the input capacitor is too far from the VIN and ground pins, the wiring to it is extra inductance in the hot loop. The loop carries pulses of the load current with nanosecond edges, so that inductance makes V equals L di by dt. For example, 5 A in 3 ns across 3 nH is about 5 V, and a poorly placed capacitor can easily add 10 nH or more, which is 17 V of potential overshoot on a 12 V rail. It also rings with the FET's output capacitance, say 300 pF, at roughly 100 to 170 MHz.

So the effects are: the switch node overshoots and rings, which can exceed the FET's voltage rating and cause avalanche or damage over time; it radiates a lot more, so you fail EMI around the ring frequency; it wastes a bit of energy in the ring; the noise couples into the controller, causing jitter or false current-limit triggers; and the pulsed current has to come through the long path from the capacitor, so the local input rail at the IC droops and spikes.

The fix is to put a small ceramic right at the pins, which carries the fast edge, and leave the far capacitor as the bulk. Beyond that I'd shrink the loop, slow the edges with a gate resistor, and add an RC snubber if I still need damping."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism.** Loop $C_{in}\to Q_H\to Q_L\to C_{in}$ with inductance $L_{loop}=L_{good}+L_{par}$. When the current $I\approx I_{out}$ commutates in $t_r$, $v_L = L_{loop}\,di/dt$.

| $L_{loop}$ | $L\,di/dt$ (5 A, 3 ns) | Ring frequency (with $C_{oss}$ = 300 pF) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 nH | 1.7 V | 291 MHz |
| 3 nH | 5 V | 168 MHz |
| 10 nH | 17 V | 92 MHz |
| 20 nH | 33 V | 65 MHz |

On a 12 V input a 30 V FET has about 18 V headroom; 10 nH or more can exceed it in the worst case (damping and edge shape usually make real overshoot smaller, but the trend is clear).

**Typical numbers for "far".** A 10 mm trace pair plus a via on each leg easily adds 10 to 20 nH without a close plane (about 1 nH/mm per conductor); a nearby plane reduces it several-fold but not to the 1 to 3 nH of a proper layout.

**Consequences.**
1. Overshoot and ringing at SW; $V_{DS}$ stress (avalanche energy, long-term reliability).
2. EMI: more radiation at the ring frequency and harmonics; conducted noise on VIN.
3. Ringing energy $\tfrac12 L I^2 f_{sw}$: 10 nH, 5 A, 500 kHz ≈ 62 mW of the loop energy cycled each period (a fraction is lost), small versus the output but not nothing.
4. Controller disturbance: noise on VIN, GND and the current-sense or leading-edge blanking, giving jitter, false OCP or subharmonic-like behaviour.
5. Gate-drive disturbances: the ring couples to the gate through $C_{gd}$ and ground bounce, risking shoot-through or extra loss.
6. Local input droop: the IC sees a pulsed current from a distant source, so its own VIN pin dips and rings.

**Fix hierarchy.** (1) Move a small ceramic (0.1 to 1 µF, 0402/0603) to the pins within 1 to 2 mm; (2) shorten and widen the path; plane beneath; (3) edge control (gate/boot resistor); (4) RC snubber across $Q_L$; (5) bulk capacitor with ESR to damp the source side.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The overshoot figure $L\,di/dt$ is an upper-bound style estimate; real peaks depend on edge shape, $C_{oss}$, damping and the diode/body-diode recovery. Use a scope with a short ground spring to measure.
:::

::: long
"A far input capacitor means the wiring to it is extra inductance in the hot loop, and that loop carries pulses of the full load current with nanosecond edges. So V equals L di by dt. Take 5 amps in 3 nanoseconds. A good layout has maybe 3 nanohenries, giving about 5 volts of potential overshoot. If the capacitor is a centimetre away on narrow traces and a via, you can easily add 10 nanohenries or more, so 17 volts or more of potential overshoot on a 12 volt input. The loop also rings with the FET's output capacitance, about 300 picofarads, at 90 to 170 megahertz.

What you see: the switch node overshoots and rings, and that can exceed the FET's voltage rating, so you get stress, avalanche and eventually failures. You radiate a lot more near the ring frequency, so you fail EMC. The ring couples into the controller and gate drive, so you get jitter or false current-limit trips. The ringing wastes a bit of energy. And the input pin at the IC sees spiky voltage because the current has to come from far away.

To fix it, first move a small ceramic, 0.1 to 1 microfarad in 0402 or 0603, right to the VIN and ground pins, because it has the lowest ESL and handles the edge. The far capacitor can stay as bulk. Then shrink the loop and put a solid plane under it. If it still rings, slow the edge with a gate or bootstrap resistor and add an RC snubber across the low-side switch. And I'd verify with a scope using a short ground spring."
:::

::: terms
- **Loop inductance**: total inductance of the hot loop, including wiring to the capacitor.
- **Overshoot**: the peak of the switch-node voltage above the input rail.
- **Ringing**: damped oscillation of the loop $L$ with $C_{oss}$.
- **$C_{oss}$**: FET output capacitance.
- **Avalanche**: breakdown of a FET when $V_{DS}$ exceeds its rating.
- **Snubber**: RC network that damps ringing.
- **Leading-edge blanking**: a short blanking window to ignore switching spikes in current-mode control.
- **Gate resistor**: slows the edge at the cost of switching loss.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it increases inductance and causes ringing."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies with $L\,di/dt$ for the good and bad case and the ring frequency from $L$ and $C_{oss}$.
- Lists all consequences: overshoot against $V_{DS}$ rating, EMI, loss, controller disturbance, gate coupling, VIN pin droop.
- Notes that estimates are bounds and that damping reduces real overshoot.
- Gives a prioritised fix list and a measurement method.
- Mentions interaction with the source impedance and damping of the bulk stage.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The far capacitor still holds the voltage up."** At DC and low frequency yes; at nanosecond edges its inductance isolates it.
- **Relying on the bulk capacitor alone.** Its ESL is tens of nH; it cannot supply the edge.
- **Only adding more capacitance.** Capacitance doesn't fix an inductance problem; a smaller, closer capacitor does.
- **Measuring SW with a long ground lead.** The probe loop adds its own ringing and misleads; use a spring tip.
- **Assuming the overshoot is harmless because the average is fine.** The peak stresses the FET and sets EMI.
- **Adding a big snubber instead of fixing the layout.** It costs efficiency and the underlying loop remains.
:::

::: deep
## From "far" to inductance

The extra distance has two electrical parts: more wire (inductance) and more vias (inductance and resistance). A rough inventory, for a 0603 ceramic placed 10 mm from the pins, routed with 0.5 mm traces:

- Trace pair: about 10 mm × 1 nH/mm × 2 conductors, less coupling credit if they run close together over a plane: 5 to 20 nH.
- Two vias to a plane: 1 to 2.6 nH.
- Capacitor body ESL: 0.5 to 1 nH.

Total: 10 to 20 nH is a plausible range. A tight layout: 1 to 3 nH.

## Why the capacitor "stops working" at the edge

An ideal capacitor holds the voltage; a real capacitor with series inductance $L_{par}$ has impedance $|Z| = \omega L_{par}$ above its self-resonant frequency $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{par}C})$. For 10 µF with 10 nH, $f_0 = 504$ kHz; above that the capacitor is an inductor. At the ring frequency of ~100 MHz, $|Z| = 2\pi\cdot10^8\cdot10^{-8} = 6.3$ Ω, which means the switching current sees 6 Ω of impedance and develops volts across it: 5 A × 6.3 Ω = 31 V. For 1 nH: 0.63 Ω, so 3 V. The capacitor that matters at the edge is the one with the smallest total inductance.

## The ring and EMI

The loop inductance and $C_{oss}$ ring at $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$: 168 MHz at 3 nH, 92 MHz at 10 nH, 65 MHz at 20 nH with 300 pF. Emissions in the 30 to 300 MHz band, where limits such as CISPR 32 apply, sit right there. A larger loop is both a lower-frequency, higher-amplitude ring and a larger antenna ($\propto$ loop area), so the radiated field rises faster than the inductance does. See [[q0547]].

## What the controller sees

The ring appears on VIN and ground at the IC. Current-mode controllers sense the high-side or low-side current and use leading-edge blanking to ignore the turn-on spike; a larger and longer ring can outlast the blanking and trip the current limit or add jitter to the PWM comparator. Noise on ground can shift the reference seen by the feedback; drive strength and shoot-through margin can also be affected by gate-ground bounce. Symptoms look like "random" instability: jitter on the SW edge, subharmonic-looking behaviour at certain loads, or hiccup operation.

## Source-side effects

If the nearest low-impedance source is a far bulk capacitor, the IC's VIN pin is fed through that same long path, so it sees a pulsed droop and spike of $L\,di/dt$ every cycle. Also note the buck's negative input resistance: $-V_{in}^2/P_{in}$ (8 Ω for 12 V, 18 W), which interacts with source inductance (a 0.5 m cable has about 0.5 µH) and the input capacitor: with 10 µF the resonance is near 71 kHz and the characteristic impedance is $\sqrt{L/C}=0.22$ Ω. The filter's characteristic impedance (0.22 Ω here) is well below 8 Ω, which is good, but the resonance is still high-Q unless damped, so the input stage needs ESR near $\sqrt{L/C}$ (an electrolytic or RC damper); otherwise it can ring or oscillate. A far ceramic has little ESR, making this worse.

## Fixing it

1. **Small ceramic at the pins.** 0.1 to 1 µF in 0402/0603 within 1 to 2 mm. This alone usually removes most of the overshoot, because the fast edge is supplied locally and the far capacitor only provides the lower-frequency content.
2. **Reduce the loop.** Wide, short copper, same layer, plane directly below, ground via at the capacitor ([[q0548]]).
3. **Edge control.** A gate or bootstrap resistor (a few ohms) slows $di/dt$: overshoot falls in proportion but switching loss rises.
4. **Snubber.** RC across $Q_L$ with R ≈ $\sqrt{L/C_{oss}}$ (3 Ω for 3 nH/300 pF) and C a few times $C_{oss}$; its loss is about $C V^2 f$.
5. **Damping the source.** An electrolytic or polymer with ESR near $\sqrt{L/C}$ at the input.
6. **Verify.** Scope the SW node with a spring ground and compare overshoot with the rating; near-field scan before and after.

::: applications
- **Bring-up debugging**: a new buck rings or runs hot because the input ceramic went to the other side of the board.
- **EMC fixes**: moving or adding a small ceramic at the pins is the first-line fix before ferrites or shields.
- **Layout reviews**: checking capacitor distance in millimetres against the datasheet recommendation.
- **High-current POL regulators**, where a few nH is a visible fraction of the loop.
- **Battery- and USB-powered products**, where long cables make source-side damping important.
:::

::: assumptions
The analysis assumes a synchronous buck with a hot loop dominated by wiring inductance and FET output capacitance, edges of a few ns and currents of a few amps. Peak overshoot is bounded by $L\,di/dt$ for an abrupt commutation but in practice is reduced by finite edge time and damping, and in some parts by the body-diode recovery adding to it. The numbers (nH per mm) are order-of-magnitude and depend on plane distance. Controllers differ in how sensitive they are to ringing. For converters with very slow edges (large gate resistors) a farther capacitor is more tolerable, at the cost of efficiency. If the datasheet explicitly allows a larger distance, follow it.
:::

::: followups
### How far is "too far"?
There's no universal distance, but within 1 to 2 mm of the pins is the target and beyond about 5 mm the loop inductance grows to where overshoot and EMI become visible on a scope. The number to think about is nanohenries, not millimetres: each mm of conductor over a plane adds a fraction of a nH, and vias add about 1 nH each, so vias in the path are worse than distance.

### Will a bigger capacitor fix a far-placement problem?
No. A bigger capacitor in the same place has the same wiring inductance, and larger packages have more ESL, so the edge still sees the same impedance. A smaller, closer capacitor fixes it. Adding capacitance helps the ripple voltage, not the nanosecond edge.

### Why is the overshoot worse at higher load current?
Because $di/dt$ scales with the commutated current: 5 A over 3 ns versus 1 A over 3 ns is five times the $L\,di/dt$. The ring is therefore more noticeable at full load, and a layout that looks clean at light load can fail at full load. Always test at maximum current.

### What does a snubber cost and when do you use it?
It dissipates about $C V^2 f_{sw}$ (with C a few times $C_{oss}$, say 1 nF at 12 V and 500 kHz: 72 mW), so a small efficiency loss. I use it after the layout is as good as it can be, when the remaining ring is too high for EMI or the FET's margin. It is the last resort, not the first.

### How would you tell from a scope trace that the input capacitor is too far?
The SW rising and falling edges show a large overshoot and a ring of 50 to 200 MHz that lasts several cycles; the VIN pin of the IC shows spikes at the same frequency; moving a small ceramic to the pins (or even tack-soldering one across them) reduces both immediately. That quick test is a classic bench diagnosis.
:::
