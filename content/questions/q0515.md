::: testing
Whether you understand that a via is a **discontinuity with its own parasitic inductance, capacitance and a stub**, not a free wire, and whether you can say *when* it is acceptable and *how* to make it benign. The honest answer is "avoid if you reasonably can, minimise and tune them if you can't", and the interviewer wants numbers (roughly 1 nH and 0.3–0.5 pF; stub resonance; reflection size versus edge rate) plus the mitigation toolbox: ground return vias, back-drilling, anti-pad sizing, symmetric via pairs, shorter stubs.
:::

::: why
Real boards must change layers: fan-out from a BGA, getting to a connector, crossing other nets. At 100 Mb/s a via is invisible; at 10 Gb/s a poorly designed via can eat most of the channel's loss and return-loss budget. Knowing which regime you are in, and how to improve a via cheaply, is a standard SI/layout interview probe. It also links to the via-type questions ([[q0576]]) and the "when not to use vias" questions ([[q0581]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Via anatomy**: barrel (plated hole), pads on each layer, anti-pads (clearance holes in planes), and the stub (unused barrel length). See [[q0574]].
- **Parasitic L and C**: the barrel is a small series inductance; the pads and plane clearances add shunt capacitance. See [[q0510]].
- **Impedance discontinuity and reflection**: a lumped L or C in a line reflects an edge in proportion to $L/t_r$ or $C/t_r$. See [[q0711]].
- **Return path continuity**: a signal that moves between reference planes drags its return current with it. See [[q0486]].
- **Via stub resonance**: a stub is a quarter-wave notch at $f = c/(4\ell\sqrt{\varepsilon_r})$.
- **Via types**: through, blind, buried, microvia; each leaves a different stub. See [[q0576]].
:::

::: answer
"Short answer: avoid it when I can, but I don't treat it as forbidden. A via is a discontinuity: the barrel adds series inductance, roughly a nanohenry for a 1.6 mm board, the pads and plane cutouts add a few tenths of a picofarad, and whatever barrel is left below the exit layer is a stub that resonates.

Whether that matters depends on the edge rate. A 1 nH via in a 50 ohm line reflects about 1% of a 1 ns edge, but around 10% of a 100 ps edge. So on a 20 MHz SPI line I don't care; on a 10 Gb/s pair I care a lot.

When I do have to via, I do four things. One, minimise the count: no layer hopping for convenience. Two, put a ground via right beside the signal via, so the return current has a short path to the next plane. Three, keep the stub short, by routing to the layer nearest the exit side, or back-drilling, or using blind or micro vias. Four, for differential pairs, make both vias identical and symmetric. And I size the anti-pads so the via impedance is close to the trace, then check the result in a 3D or via-model simulation if the speed justifies it."
:::

::: technical
**Parasitics (first order).** For a through via of barrel length $h$ and drill diameter $d$, a classic closed-form estimate (Johnson & Graham) gives

$$L_{via}\approx 5.08\,h\left[\ln\!\frac{4h}{d}+1\right]\ \text{nH}\quad (h,d\text{ in inches}),\qquad C_{via}\approx\frac{1.41\,\varepsilon_r\,T\,D_1}{D_2-D_1}\ \text{pF}$$

where $T$ is the board thickness, $D_1$ the pad diameter and $D_2$ the anti-pad diameter (inches). For a 62 mil board, 10 mil drill, 20 mil pad and 40 mil anti-pad with $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$: $L\approx1.3$ nH, $C\approx0.38$ pF, so $\sqrt{L/C}\approx 59\ \Omega$. Use these as order-of-magnitude values; real via models come from 3D field solvers.

**Reflection size.** For a small series $L$ in a $Z_0$ line, the peak reflected fraction for a ramp edge is approximately $\rho\approx \frac{L}{2Z_0 t_r}$ (valid when $L/2Z_0\ll t_r$); for a small shunt $C$, $\rho\approx\frac{Z_0C}{2t_r}$. With $L=1$ nH, $Z_0=50$ Ω: $L/2Z_0 = 10$ ps, so $\rho\approx 1\%$ at $t_r=1$ ns and $10\%$ at $t_r=100$ ps. Reflections add up: several vias in a channel can resonate.

**Stub resonance.** An open stub of length $\ell$ in a dielectric with $\varepsilon_r$ is a quarter-wave notch at $f_0=\dfrac{c}{4\ell\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}$: 36 GHz for 1 mm, 18 GHz for 2 mm, 12 GHz for 3 mm (with $\varepsilon_r=4.3$). The notch's skirts begin well below $f_0$, so keep $f_0$ at several times the Nyquist frequency (≥ about 2–3×).

**Return path.** If the signal changes from a layer referenced to ground plane A to a layer referenced to ground plane B, the return current must transfer between planes: provide ground vias within a few mm. If the references are a ground and a power plane, add a nearby decoupling capacitor.

**Mitigation:** fewer vias, shorter stubs (back-drill or route to the bottom), blind/buried/micro vias, anti-pad tuning, via-in-pad for BGAs, ground via fences, matched vias on both pair members, and layer changes only between layers with the same reference.
:::

::: short
"I avoid vias on fast signals where I can, because each one adds around 1 nH of series inductance, a few tenths of a picofarad of shunt capacitance and a stub that resonates. A 1 nH via reflects about 1% of a 1 ns edge but about 10% of a 100 ps edge, so it depends on edge rate. When I must use one I keep the count low, put a ground via next to it for the return current, shorten or back-drill the stub, and make the two vias in a differential pair identical."
:::

::: long
"I treat a via as a small circuit, not a wire. The barrel adds series inductance, about a nanohenry for a standard 1.6 millimetre board. The pads and the clearance holes in the planes add shunt capacitance, a few tenths of a picofarad. And the part of the barrel below the layer where the signal exits is an open stub that acts as a quarter-wave resonator.

Whether that matters depends on the edge rate. The reflection from a small series inductance is roughly L over two Z0 times the rise time. For a nanohenry in a 50 ohm line that's 10 picoseconds over the rise time: about one percent for a one nanosecond edge, about ten percent for a hundred picosecond edge. The stub resonance for a 2 millimetre stub is around 18 gigahertz, which is fine at 5 gigabit but starts to bite at 25.

So the rule is: for slow buses I use vias freely; for fast signals I avoid unnecessary layer changes. When I need one, first I keep the return path alive. If the signal moves to a layer with a different reference plane, I put a ground via within a couple of millimetres so the return current can hop planes. Second, I control the stub: route to the layer closest to the exit side, use blind or microvias in HDI designs, or ask the fab to back-drill. Third, I tune the geometry: smaller pads and the right anti-pad size so the via looks close to 50 or 100 ohms, and I check it with a field solver or the fab's via model. Fourth, in a differential pair I use matching vias, symmetric ground vias, and compensate length.

And if a high-speed connection can be done on one layer, I keep it on one layer."
:::

::: terms
- **Via barrel**: the plated hole that connects layers; the main source of series inductance.
- **Pad / anti-pad**: the copper ring on a layer / the clearance hole in a plane around the barrel; set the via's capacitance.
- **Via stub**: unused barrel length beyond the layer where the signal exits; a quarter-wave resonator.
- **Back-drilling**: re-drilling from the back with a larger bit to remove the stub.
- **Blind / buried / microvia**: vias that do not run through the whole board, leaving little or no stub. See [[q0576]].
- **Return (stitching) via**: ground via placed next to a signal via to carry the return current to the next plane.
- **Via-in-pad**: via placed in a component pad, shortening the connection (needs filled and capped vias).
- **Return loss / insertion loss**: S-parameter measures of reflection and attenuation; vias degrade both.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "vias add inductance so avoid them." An exceptional answer:
- Gives **magnitudes**: ~1 nH, 0.3–0.5 pF, stub resonance ~12–36 GHz for 3–1 mm, and the $L/(2Z_0t_r)$ reflection estimate.
- Says it **depends on edge rate** and sorts nets into "don't care" and "must engineer".
- Brings up the **return path** and the ground via next to a signal via, which is the single most common omission.
- Knows the **stub** issue and the options (back-drill, blind/micro vias, route to the outer layer).
- Mentions **differential-pair via symmetry** and that real designs use a simulation or the fab's via model, not just rules of thumb.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Vias are just short wires."** At a 100 ps edge a 1 nH via reflects about 10%. See the estimate above.
- **Forgetting the return via.** If the signal changes reference planes with no nearby ground via, the return current takes a long detour (loop inductance, EMI, crosstalk).
- **Ignoring the stub.** A 1.6 mm through via leaving the signal at L2 has a stub of up to ~1.3 mm; at 10+ GHz that is a notch in the channel.
- **Using different via counts or positions on the two halves of a pair.** Creates skew and mode conversion.
- **Banning vias universally.** Slow nets and low-speed buses are fine; a rule that blocks routing creates worse problems.
- **Putting a via in the middle of a pad without capping or filling it** (solder wicks into the hole). That's a manufacturing issue, not signal integrity; see [[q0582]].
:::

Treat the via as a short piece of non-uniform transmission line: the barrel (a thin cylinder inside a bigger cylindrical clearance) has its own $Z_{via}=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ that is generally a bit different from the trace. The "classic" formulas give a quick lumped version:

$$L_{via}\approx 5.08\,h\left[\ln\frac{4h}{d}+1\right]\ \text{nH},\qquad C_{via}\approx \frac{1.41\,\varepsilon_r\,T\,D_1}{D_2-D_1}\ \text{pF}$$

For the standard 62 mil (1.57 mm) board, 10 mil drill, 20 mil pad, 40 mil anti-pad: $L\approx 1.33$ nH and $C\approx 0.38$ pF. A via with $C$ dominant looks capacitive (a dip in $Z$), and an inductive via looks like a peak; a real via often does both in sequence (a dip then a peak on a TDR trace).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> These closed-form formulas come from *High-Speed Digital Design* and are known to be rough (they ignore stubs, neighbouring vias and plane thickness). Treat the values as order-of-magnitude. Fab-specific 3D field-solver models give the real numbers.

## Reflection amplitude depends on the edge

For a small series inductance in a line with impedance $Z_0$, the load seen by the edge is $Z_0 + sL$ in series with the far line. The characteristic time constant is $\tau_L = L/(2Z_0)$. When $\tau_L\ll t_r$, the reflection is the derivative of the edge scaled by $\tau_L$, so its peak is $\tau_L/t_r$. A shunt capacitance has $\tau_C = Z_0C/2$ and the same behaviour.

## The stub: a free notch filter you did not want

A through via that connects L1 to L3 on a 6-layer board leaves a barrel from L3 to L6 hanging off the signal path. Its open end reflects the wave back with the same polarity, and when the round-trip delay is half a period it cancels the forward wave: a quarter-wave notch at

$$f_0=\frac{c}{4\,\ell_{stub}\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}$$

| Stub length | $f_0$ ($\varepsilon_r = 4.3$) |
|---|---|
| 1 mm | 36 GHz |
| 2 mm | 18 GHz |
| 3 mm | 12 GHz |

The impact starts well below $f_0$ (roughly the attenuation is noticeable above $f_0/3$). For a 10 Gb/s NRZ link (Nyquist 5 GHz) a 3 mm stub is marginal, while a 25 Gb/s link (Nyquist 12.5 GHz) can't tolerate it. Fixes: back-drill to leave less than ~0.2–0.3 mm, use blind/buried vias, or route the signal to the far-side layer to keep the stub short (e.g. route to L6 from L1: the stub ends at the exit layer).

## The return path moves with the signal

The current in the signal via must have a return current. Between two reference planes of the *same* net (ground–ground) a close ground via lets it hop. Between *different* nets (ground–power), the return must cross through plane capacitance or a decoupling capacitor, and the planes can ring. This is why guidance is "reference the same plane on both layers if you can", and "stitch with ground vias within ~1–2 mm" if you can't.

## Differential pairs

Use two identical vias with a symmetrical ground-via arrangement and identical anti-pads. If one trace is longer on the outer layer, add the compensation locally ([[q0521]]). A via pair that is close together couples (good for common-mode rejection), but anti-pads that merge into a "slot" in the plane degrade the return path; check the stackup rules.

## Worked example

A 5 Gb/s SerDes ($t_r\approx 60$ ps in the package, 100 ps on the board, Nyquist 2.5 GHz) crosses two vias with $L = 1$ nH, $C = 0.4$ pF each and a 2 mm stub (notch 18 GHz). Each via: $\tau_L=10$ ps, $\tau_C=10$ ps, so reflections up to ~10% each on a 100 ps edge, with opposite sign so they partly cancel in a good design. The 18 GHz stub notch is 7× the Nyquist: negligible. The design passes if the via anti-pad is sized to hold the via near 50 Ω. At 25 Gb/s on the same stackup (Nyquist 12.5 GHz, edges ~20 ps) the same stub notch is only 1.4× Nyquist, and the design needs back-drilling or blind vias.

## Practical checklist

1. Same layer if you can, same reference plane if not.
2. A ground via at each signal-via transition.
3. Short stub: back-drill, blind via, or route to the last layer.
4. Optimise pad and anti-pad (smaller pad, bigger anti-pad lowers $C$).
5. Match both vias of a pair; keep spacing symmetric.
6. Validate with the fab's via model in a channel simulation for ≥ 5 Gb/s.
:::

::: applications
- **BGA fan-out for DDR and SerDes**: escape routing needs vias; designers keep stubs short by using layer sets adjacent to the exit side and use microvias on HDI.
- **Backplane connectors (e.g. 10–25 Gb/s)**: press-fit via barrels are back-drilled to remove stubs.
- **RF boards**: via fences (stitched ground vias) shield microstrip and coplanar lines.
- **USB/HDMI/PCIe lane switching**: AC-coupling capacitor pads and the via pair beside them are the first things optimised for return loss.
- **Digital IO and I²C/SPI**: vias are not a concern; use them freely for routing.
:::

::: assumptions
- The estimates assume a **plated through-hole** in FR-4 with typical 1.6 mm thickness. Thin boards (0.8 mm) have proportionally smaller $L$ and stubs.
- The reflection formulas assume $\tau\ll t_r$, **small discontinuities**, and a single via. Multiple vias or nearby via pairs can resonate; use a simulator.
- The advice "avoid vias" applies to **fast edges**. For slow edges (≥ 5 ns) vias are irrelevant to SI.
- Some interviewers want **power vias** or **thermal vias** (a different topic: current capacity and heat). The question here is about signal traces; see [[q0584]] for power planes.
:::

::: followups
### What actually causes a via's impedance discontinuity?
Two things: the barrel is a thin conductor with partial inductance (about 1 nH), and the pads and anti-pads form a capacitor with the planes it passes through (a few tenths of pF). The far end of the barrel past the exit layer is an open stub. Together they cause a dip (capacitive), a peak (inductive) and a resonance (stub) in the line's impedance profile.

### How do you handle a layer change between two different ground planes?
Place at least one, preferably two, ground vias within about 1–2 mm of the signal via to carry the return current between planes. If the planes are at different potentials, put a decoupling capacitor next to the via pair. Without this, the return current detours and the loop radiates.

### Is back-drilling always worth it?
No. It adds cost and requires the fab's tolerance; the stub shortened to about 0.2–0.3 mm moves the notch above ~100 GHz. It's worth it for ≥ 10 Gb/s on thick boards or backplanes. For lower speeds or thin boards, routing to the right layer or using blind vias is cheaper.

### What happens if the two traces of a differential pair have different via counts?
Skew and mode conversion. Each via adds about 10–15 ps of delay for a 1.6 mm barrel; unequal via counts add skew that length-matching on the board must compensate. Differential-to-common conversion also grows. Always use symmetric vias.

### What if the board is 3.2 mm thick and your signal exits at L2?
The stub is about 3 mm long, resonating around 12 GHz and acting as a notch that starts to hurt above ~4 GHz. For a 10 Gb/s link that is close to the edge, so I'd back-drill or move to a thinner stackup or use blind vias. I'd run a channel simulation with the via model before committing.
:::

::: interpretations
"Vias through high-speed traces" is interpreted as **signal vias placed in the path of a high-speed trace (layer changes)**, not vias that pass *through* the trace's copper, which would be a manufacturing error. Another reading is "should there be vias *near* the trace", in which case ground stitching vias are encouraged, covered by [[q0514]] and [[q0487]].
:::
