::: testing
Whether you can give a **physical reason**, not a slogan, for the speed gap, and whether you separate three different things people blur together: the *clock rate* a bus allows, the *protocol overhead* it adds, and the *effective throughput* you actually get. The best answers say: "SPI is faster mainly because its push-pull drivers actively drive both edges, while I2C's rising edge is an RC charge through a pull-up, and on top of that I2C spends 9 clocks per byte plus an address." Then they back it with one number. The interviewer may also be probing whether you know that I2C can be sped up (smaller pull-ups, Fast-mode Plus) but only within hard limits.
:::

::: why
Bus speed decides whether a sensor sample rate fits, how long a firmware update takes over flash, how many frames a display can refresh, and whether an ADC can stream continuously. Engineers also debug speed problems on the bench: an I2C bus with slow, rounded rising edges, or an SPI link that works at 8 MHz but corrupts data at 25 MHz because of setup-time violations. Understanding the underlying limits (RC rise time for I2C, timing budget for SPI) tells you what to change: pull-up value, trace length, mode or the clock.
:::

::: concepts
- **Push-pull drive**: the output actively sources and sinks current, so both edges are fast ([[q0761]]).
- **Open-drain + pull-up**: only the falling edge is driven; the rising edge is an RC charge ([[q0755]]).
- **Rise time**: $t_r = 0.8473\,R_pC_b$ (30% to 70%) for an RC-limited edge ([[q0759]], [[q0756]]).
- **Bus capacitance $C_b$**: total pin, trace and connector capacitance (max 400 pF in Standard/Fast modes) ([[q0756]]).
- **Protocol overhead**: bits on the wire that are not payload (address, ACK, start/stop).
- **Timing budget**: clock-to-output delay, propagation delay and setup time must fit in the clock period ([[q0770]]).
:::

::: answer
"SPI is faster, and there are two reasons, one electrical and one protocol.

Electrically, SPI drivers are push-pull: the output transistors actively pull the line up and down, so edges take a few nanoseconds and the clock can run at 10 to 50 MHz or more. I2C is open-drain. A device can only pull the line low; to go high, a pull-up resistor has to charge the bus capacitance. That's an RC curve, with a rise time of about $0.85\,R_pC_b$. With 4.7 kΩ and 100 pF that's about 400 nanoseconds, which is already too slow for 400 kHz mode. You can shrink the resistor, but then you burn current every time the line is low, and the spec caps how much current devices must sink, so I2C tops out around 1 MHz in practice.

The second reason is overhead. SPI sends 8 clocks per byte. I2C sends 9, because of the ACK bit, plus an address byte and often a register-pointer byte and a repeated start. So reading 14 bytes is about 380 µs on 400 kHz I2C versus 12 µs on a 10 MHz SPI link, roughly 30 times faster.

The one caveat is that UART isn't clocked, so its speed is just baud rate, usually 115200 up to a few Mbaud."
:::

::: technical
**Electrical limit (I2C).** The line rises as $V(t) = V_{DD}\left(1 - e^{-t/R_pC_b}\right)$. The spec measures rise time between 30% and 70% of $V_{DD}$:
$$t_r = R_pC_b\ln\frac{0.7}{0.3} = 0.8473\,R_pC_b$$
Spec limits on $t_r$: 1000 ns (Standard, 100 kHz), 300 ns (Fast, 400 kHz), 120 ns (Fast-mode Plus, 1 MHz). Bus capacitance limit: 400 pF (Standard/Fast), 550 pF (Fm+). For 300 ns and $C_b = 100$ pF, $R_p \le 3.54$ kΩ. The lower bound on $R_p$ comes from the sink current spec: $R_{p,min} = (V_{DD} - V_{OL,max})/I_{OL}$; with $V_{DD}=3.3$ V, $V_{OL}=0.4$ V and $I_{OL}=3$ mA that is 967 Ω. The usable window between those two bounds narrows as $C_b$ grows, and at 400 pF in Fast mode the maximum $R_p$ is 885 Ω, below the minimum for many devices, so you must reduce $C_b$ rather than just the resistor.

**Electrical limit (SPI).** Push-pull edges are ns-scale, so the limit is a timing budget. In mode 0 the target changes MISO on the falling edge and the controller samples it on the next rising edge, half a period later:
$$\frac{T}{2} \ge t_{co} + 2t_{pd} + t_{su}$$
With $t_{co}=15$ ns, round-trip propagation $2t_{pd}=2$ ns and $t_{su}=5$ ns, $T \ge 44$ ns, so $f_{max} \approx 22.7$ MHz. Many controllers sample later to relax this.

**Overhead.** I2C: 9 clocks per byte (8 + ACK) plus address overhead; max payload rate on a long write is $\frac{8}{9}f_{SCL}$: 11.1 kB/s at 100 kHz, 44.4 kB/s at 400 kHz, 111 kB/s at 1 MHz. SPI: 8 clocks per byte, so 1.25 MB/s at 10 MHz, and the data goes both ways simultaneously. UART 8N1: 10 bit times per byte, so 11.5 kB/s at 115200 baud and 300 kB/s at 3 Mbaud.

| Bus | Typical max clock | Payload rate (one direction) |
|---|---|---|
| I2C Standard | 100 kHz | 11.1 kB/s |
| I2C Fast | 400 kHz | 44.4 kB/s |
| I2C Fast-mode Plus | 1 MHz | 111 kB/s |
| UART 115200 8N1 | n/a | 11.5 kB/s |
| UART 3 Mbaud | n/a | 300 kB/s |
| SPI 10 MHz | 10 MHz | 1.25 MB/s |
:::

::: short
"SPI is faster for two reasons. First, its push-pull outputs drive both edges hard, so you can clock at tens of MHz. I2C's rising edge is an RC charge through a pull-up, about $0.85\,R_pC_b$, which caps it around 100 kHz to 1 MHz. Second, I2C has more overhead: 9 clocks per byte plus an address and often a register byte. So a 14-byte read is roughly 380 µs on 400 kHz I2C versus 12 µs on 10 MHz SPI."
:::

::: long
"SPI is faster, and the reason is both physics and protocol.

Start with the physics. SPI outputs are push-pull: a PMOS pulls the line up and an NMOS pulls it down, so both edges are driven by a low-impedance transistor and rise times are a few nanoseconds. So the limit is the timing budget: the target's clock-to-output delay, the propagation delay on the trace, and the controller's setup time have to fit in half a clock. That works out to tens of MHz for typical parts.

I2C is open-drain. The device can only pull the line low. When it lets go, the pull-up resistor charges the bus capacitance, and the 30 to 70 percent rise time is $0.8473\,R_pC_b$. With 4.7 kΩ and 100 pF that's about 400 ns. Fast mode needs under 300 ns, so I'd reduce the pull-up to around 3 kΩ. But smaller resistors mean more current when the line is low, and devices are only required to sink about 3 mA, so there's a floor on the resistor too. That squeeze is why I2C stops around 1 MHz.

Then the protocol. SPI has 8 clocks per byte and no addressing. I2C has 9 clocks per byte because of the ACK, plus an address byte, usually a register pointer, and a repeated START to switch to read. For 14 bytes that's 153 clocks, about 380 µs at 400 kHz, versus 120 clocks, 12 µs at 10 MHz on SPI.

So in the end: roughly 30 times for the same payload. UART's the odd one out because it's not clocked, so it's just baud rate, 11.5 kB/s at 115200 and up to a few hundred kB/s at 3 Mbaud, but only between two devices."
:::

::: terms
- **Rise time ($t_r$)**: time for a signal to go from 30% to 70% of $V_{DD}$ in the I2C spec (10% to 90% in many others; check the definition).
- **Bus capacitance ($C_b$)**: total capacitance seen by the line: pins, traces, connectors, probes.
- **Pull-up resistor ($R_p$)**: resistor from the line to $V_{DD}$ that provides the high level.
- **Fast-mode Plus (Fm+)**: 1 MHz I2C using stronger drivers (20 mA sink).
- **Clock-to-output delay ($t_{co}$)**: time from the clock edge to valid data at the output.
- **Setup time ($t_{su}$)**: time data must be stable before the sampling edge.
- **Payload throughput**: useful data bytes per second after protocol overhead.
- **Overhead**: non-payload bits: address, ACK, start/stop, command bytes, framing.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "SPI is faster because it has more wires and no addressing."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names the **RC rise time** limit with the formula $t_r = 0.8473\,R_pC_b$ and the spec limits (300 ns at Fast mode, 120 ns at Fm+).
- Shows the **squeeze**: pull-up too large fails rise time, too small exceeds the sink current ($R_{p,min} = (V_{DD} - V_{OL})/I_{OL}$).
- Distinguishes **clock rate from effective throughput**, by counting 9 clocks per byte and the address/register overhead.
- States that **SPI is limited by a timing budget** ($t_{co}$, propagation, $t_{su}$) not by RC.
- Notes that **I2C can be sped up** (Fm+, lower $C_b$, active pull-ups, or moving to SPI/I3C) and **UART** is baud-limited.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"I2C is slow because it's serial."** SPI is serial too. The difference is the open-drain RC rise and the overhead.
- **"Just use a smaller pull-up to go faster."** There's a floor set by the sink current (3 mA typical), and smaller values also raise power and $V_{OL}$.
- **Comparing headline clocks and ignoring overhead.** Even at equal clocks, a one-register write is 27 clocks on I2C and 16 on SPI, so I2C is about 1.7 times slower before electrical limits are considered.
- **Quoting I2C rise time as 10%-to-90%.** The I2C spec uses 30% to 70%. The 10%-90% value is about $2.2\,RC$, 2.6 times larger.
- **Assuming SPI is unlimited.** Setup/hold, trace delay and target $t_{co}$ cap it. A mode-0 link with 22 ns total delay tops out near 22 MHz.
- **Forgetting the target's own limit.** A flash may do 100 MHz, a sensor 1 MHz; the bus runs at the slowest device on it for I2C, per-target for SPI.
:::

::: deep
## Where the speed limit comes from

Think of each bus as a transmission scheme with two bottlenecks: how fast can the *wire* change state, and how many *wire-cycles* does one useful byte cost?

### Bottleneck 1: edge speed

#### I2C: the pull-up has to charge the wire

When no device pulls SDA or SCL low, the pull-up resistor $R_p$ charges the total bus capacitance $C_b$ toward $V_{DD}$. The circuit is just an RC network.

Figure: When the device's switch is closed it pulls the line low quickly (a transistor, ohms). When it opens, only $R_p$ is left to charge $C_b$, so the rise is an exponential with time constant $R_pC_b$. The fall is fast, the rise is slow, which is why I2C waveforms look asymmetric.

The voltage after releasing the line is $V(t) = V_{DD}(1-e^{-t/R_pC_b})$. The spec's thresholds are $V_{IL}=0.3V_{DD}$ and $V_{IH}=0.7V_{DD}$, so the transition time between them is

$$t_r = R_pC_b\ln\frac{0.7}{0.3} = 0.8473\,R_pC_b$$

Plugging common values into the $t_r$ limits tells you the maximum pull-up:

#### The lower bound on $R_p$

A smaller $R_p$ makes the rise faster but increases the current when a device pulls the line low. Devices only guarantee they can sink $I_{OL} = 3$ mA (Standard and Fast) while holding $V_{OL}\le 0.4$ V:

$$R_{p,min} = \frac{V_{DD} - V_{OL}}{I_{OL}} = \frac{3.3 - 0.4}{3\text{ mA}} = 967\ \Omega$$

Fast-mode Plus requires 20 mA sink capability, so $R_{p,min}$ drops to about 145 Ω at 3.3 V, enabling $t_r \le 120$ ns at larger capacitance. That is how 1 MHz is achievable: much stronger drivers, not a different protocol.

#### SPI: no resistor in the way

SPI outputs are CMOS push-pull. The on-resistance of the driver is on the order of 10 to 50 Ω, so even with 20 pF the RC is under a nanosecond. In practice rise time is set by the driver's slew-rate control and trace parasitics, and the limit becomes a **timing budget** rather than an RC:

$$\frac{T}{2} \ge t_{co} + 2t_{pd} + t_{su}$$

for mode 0 with the controller sampling on the first rising edge after the target's falling-edge output. With $t_{co} = 15$ ns (target), $2t_{pd}=2$ ns (1 ns each way, about 15 cm of trace), and $t_{su}=5$ ns (controller):

$$f_{max} = \frac{1}{2(15+2+5)\text{ ns}} = 22.7\text{ MHz}$$

Run faster and the controller samples before MISO is valid. Fixes: later-sample option (many MCUs have a "delay MISO sampling" setting), a lower clock, mode choices that give a full period instead of a half, or shorter traces.

### Bottleneck 2: overhead per byte

Even with the same clock, I2C would be slower because every byte costs 9 clocks and every transaction costs an address byte (9 clocks) plus often a register byte (9) and a repeated-START address-with-read byte (9):

| Task | I2C clocks | SPI clocks |
|---|---|---|
| Write 1 register (addr, reg, data) | 27 | 16 |
| Read 1 byte from a register | 36 | 16 |
| Read 6 bytes | 81 | 56 |
| Read 14 bytes | 153 | 120 |

Dividing out the clock rates gives times. At 400 kHz I2C vs 10 MHz SPI, the 14-byte read is 382.5 µs vs 12 µs, a ratio of about 32 (clock ratio 25 times overhead ratio 1.28).

Note the effect: even at the *same* clock, SPI would be 28% faster for large reads and up to 1.7 times faster for small ones, because I2C carries so much more overhead. The big gap comes from the clock.

### Throughput comparison with UART

UART needs no clock wire, so its speed is whatever the baud rate generator and the line allow. At 8N1 only 80% of bits are payload (10 bit times per byte). A 115200 UART gives 11.5 kB/s per direction; a 3 Mbaud UART gives 300 kB/s per direction, which beats Fast-mode I2C's 44 kB/s, though it only connects two devices.

## Making I2C faster

- **Reduce $C_b$**: shorten traces, fewer devices, remove connectors, split the bus with a buffer (for example an I2C bus buffer or mux).
- **Lower $R_p$** within the sink-current limit; check each device's $I_{OL}$.
- **Use Fast-mode Plus (1 MHz)** if all devices support it.
- **Active pull-up / rise-time accelerators**: ICs that add current during the rising edge.
- **High-speed mode (3.4 MHz)** exists but requires special controller circuitry and a master code; support is rare.
- **I3C** is a newer MIPI standard that uses push-pull drivers for data phases and is backward compatible with I2C targets in a limited way; check the specification for the exact rates.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact speed numbers for High-speed mode, Ultra Fast-mode (reported as a 5 MHz unidirectional push-pull mode) and I3C vary by revision and mode. Confirm against the current NXP UM10204 and MIPI documents if asked for precise values.

## Making SPI faster (and where it fails)

- **Raise the clock** until setup timing fails; check the target datasheet's maximum.
- **Shorten and match** the clock/MISO traces; put a series resistor (~22 to 33 Ω) near the driver to damp ringing.
- **Use dual or quad SPI** for more data lines on the same clock (common with serial flash).
- **Insert delay/late-sample** on MISO in the controller if the round-trip delay is the limit.

Interview-level summary: I2C's limits are in the **analog** domain (RC, drive current), SPI's are in the **digital timing** domain (setup, delay).
:::

::: applications
- **Firmware update and boot from serial flash**: SPI/QSPI at tens of MHz so code loads in milliseconds.
- **Display refresh**: SPI at 20 to 40+ MHz to push pixel data at 30 to 60 fps; I2C displays are limited to small panels.
- **Battery/PMIC telemetry**: I2C at 100 or 400 kHz is plenty for a few bytes per second.
- **High-rate IMU streaming**: SPI at 1 to 10 MHz with FIFO bursts for kHz sample rates.
- **Long chains of GPIO expanders or EEPROMs**: I2C, because bandwidth needs are tiny and pins matter.
- **Console/modem links**: UART at 115200 to a few Mbaud depending on the module.
:::

::: assumptions
- "Faster" means **achievable throughput on a typical board** with standard parts. Corner cases (quad SPI, I3C, Hs-mode) change the picture.
- I2C limits assume a **7-bit address** with a register pointer and a repeated START for reads, which is the common sensor pattern.
- SPI timing assumes **mode 0**, a push-pull target, and a single controller sample point; real parts have more subtle constraints.
- $t_r$ is given in the I2C 30%-70% definition ($0.8473\,RC$). Other definitions differ (10%-90% is $2.2\,RC$).
- Pull-up numbers are for **3.3 V** and **3 mA** sink; 5 V and 1.8 V systems change $R_{p,min}$.
- The speed of an actual link is limited by the **slowest** device on a shared I2C bus and by the **target's** maximum on each SPI link.
:::

::: followups
### Can you make I2C as fast as SPI by lowering the pull-up?
No. Going from 4.7 kΩ to about 1 kΩ buys a factor of 4 in rise time, but you hit the sink-current floor ($R_{p,min} = 967$ Ω at 3.3 V and 3 mA), power goes up, and the protocol's 9-clocks-per-byte plus address overhead remains. Fm+ at 1 MHz is the practical ceiling for standard parts.

### An I2C bus has 300 pF of capacitance. What is the maximum pull-up for Fast mode, and what does that imply?
$R_p \le 300\text{ ns}/(0.8473\times300\text{ pF}) = 1.18$ kΩ. With $V_{DD} = 3.3$ V that sinks 2.5 mA when low (about 0.4 V drop budget), close to the 3 mA limit. So this bus is marginal at 400 kHz; reduce $C_b$ or fall back to 100 kHz where the limit is 1000 ns and $R_p$ up to 3.9 kΩ works.

### Your SPI link works at 8 MHz but fails at 20 MHz with random bit errors. What do you check?
Timing budget first: target $t_{co}$ plus round-trip delay plus controller setup against a 25 ns half-period. Then signal integrity: ringing or overshoot on SCLK at the target (series damping), crosstalk, ground return. Try late-sampling on MISO if the controller supports it. Check the target's datasheet max SCLK (it might be less than 20 MHz).

### Which is faster for a single 1-byte write: I2C or SPI at the same 1 MHz clock?
SPI: 16 clocks (command + data) versus 27 clocks (address, register, data, each with an ACK bit) on I2C. So 16 µs vs 27 µs, about 1.7 times faster even with equal clocks.

### Why do I2C waveforms look asymmetric on a scope?
The falling edge is driven by an NMOS pulling the line to ground in ns. The rising edge is the pull-up charging $C_b$ with time constant $R_pC_b$, hundreds of ns. So you get a sharp fall and a slow, curved rise.
