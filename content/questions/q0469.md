::: testing
Whether you can choose a dielectric by the properties that actually matter: **dielectric constant $\varepsilon_r$** (impedance, delay), **loss tangent $\tan\delta$** (high-frequency attenuation), **glass transition $T_g$ and decomposition temperature** (assembly and reliability), **thermal conductivity**, **moisture absorption**, **mechanical stability**, and **cost**. The interviewer wants the main materials (FR-4 and its variants, polyimide, PTFE/RF laminates, ceramics, metal-core dielectrics) with a typical number or two and the application each suits. A strong answer connects the property to a consequence, for example loss at 10 GHz.
:::

::: why
The dielectric sets the electrical behaviour of every trace (impedance and delay through $\varepsilon_r$, loss through $\tan\delta$), the thermal path (heat leaves through the dielectric), the survival of the board through lead-free reflow and thermal cycling, and a large fraction of the board cost. Interviewers like this question because a candidate who says only "FR-4" has not designed beyond a few hundred MHz. It also follows naturally from [[q0466]] (layers) and leads to [[q0470]] (what FR-4 means).
:::

::: concepts
- **Dielectric constant (relative permittivity)**: sets capacitance per length, impedance and propagation speed ([[q0023]]).
- **Loss tangent**: fraction of energy dissipated per cycle; dielectric attenuation $\propto f\tan\delta\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$ ([[q0058]]).
- **Glass transition temperature ($T_g$)**: above it the resin softens and expands rapidly.
- **Thermal conductivity and thermal expansion**: heat spreading and stress in vias and solder joints.
- **Moisture absorption**: water raises $\varepsilon_r$ and loss and can cause delamination in reflow.
- **Reinforcement**: woven glass gives stiffness but makes properties non-uniform at the fibre scale.
:::

::: answer
"The standard is FR-4, woven glass in epoxy: cheap, stiff, easy to process, with a dielectric constant around 4.2 to 4.7 and a loss tangent around 0.02. It's the right choice up to a gigahertz or so and for most products. Its trade-offs are loss at high frequency, a dielectric constant that varies with frequency and with the glass weave, a glass transition around 130 to 180 degrees Celsius, and poor thermal conductivity, about 0.3 W/mK.

For more demanding cases you move to other materials. High-Tg FR-4 survives lead-free assembly better. Polyimide, with a Tg above 250 degrees, is used for flex and high-temperature boards, at higher cost and with more moisture uptake. For RF and microwave you use PTFE or ceramic-filled hydrocarbon laminates with a loss tangent of 0.001 to 0.004, stable permittivity, and much lower attenuation, but they cost several times more and are harder to process. Ceramics like alumina and aluminium nitride handle high temperature and conduct heat much better, but are brittle and expensive. And metal-core boards use a thin filled-epoxy dielectric on an aluminium base to get heat out of LEDs and power parts.

So the choice trades electrical performance, temperature, thermal conduction and cost, and I'd use FR-4 unless a requirement forces something else."
:::

::: long
"The standard insulation is FR-4, which is woven fibreglass impregnated with epoxy. It's cheap, stiff, easy to drill, laminate and plate, and it's good enough for most boards. Its dielectric constant is about 4.2 to 4.7, which sets impedance and delay, and its loss tangent is around 0.02, so signal attenuation grows with frequency: roughly 0.4 dB per inch at 5 GHz. Its glass transition is about 130 to 140 degrees for standard grades and 170 to 180 for high-Tg, which matters for lead-free reflow and thermal cycling. And its thermal conductivity is poor, about 0.3 W/mK, so heat has to be moved by copper and vias. It also absorbs a little moisture and has a weave structure that can skew differential pairs at high speed.

The alternatives each fix one weakness. High-Tg and mid-loss epoxy-glass improve temperature capability and loss at modest cost. Polyimide has a Tg above 250 degrees and is the base for flex circuits, but is pricier and absorbs more moisture. For RF and microwave we use ceramic-filled hydrocarbon or PTFE laminates, with loss tangents of 0.001 to 0.004 and a stable dielectric constant, so loss is five to fifty times lower, but they're several times the cost, and PTFE is soft and harder to process. Ceramics like alumina and aluminium nitride tolerate very high temperatures and conduct heat well, 20 to 170 W/mK, but are brittle and expensive. Metal-core boards use a thin filled-epoxy dielectric on aluminium for LED and power heat sinking.

The trade-offs run between electrical performance, thermal performance, reliability and cost. I'd stay with FR-4 unless frequency, temperature or heat flux forces a change, and mixed stack-ups let you put the expensive laminate only on the layers that need it."
:::

::: terms
- **$\varepsilon_r$ (dielectric constant)**: ratio of the material's permittivity to that of vacuum.
- **$\tan\delta$ (loss tangent)**: ratio of dissipated to stored energy per cycle in the dielectric.
- **$T_g$**: glass transition temperature, where the resin changes from rigid to rubbery.
- **$T_d$**: decomposition temperature, where the resin starts to lose mass.
- **Prepreg**: partially cured glass-epoxy used to bond layers ([[q0466]]).
- **CAF**: conductive anodic filament, a failure where copper migrates along glass fibres under bias and humidity.
- **Glass-weave effect**: variation in local $\varepsilon_r$ between glass bundles and resin that skews high-speed signals.
- **Halogen-free laminate**: a flame-retardant system without bromine or chlorine ([[q0470]]).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "FR-4, and Rogers for high frequency".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names materials and gives **$\varepsilon_r$, $\tan\delta$, $T_g$ and thermal conductivity** for at least FR-4 and one alternative.
- Translates a property into a **consequence** (dB per inch, impedance shift, delamination at reflow).
- Mentions **glass-weave skew, moisture and CAF** for FR-4, and **mixed stack-ups** as a cost lever.
- Recognises **polyimide for flex**, **ceramics and metal-core for heat**, **PTFE for microwave**.
- States a **selection rule**: use FR-4 unless a measurable requirement demands otherwise.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Rogers is always better."** It costs more, can be harder to process, and is unnecessary below a few GHz.
- **Treating $\varepsilon_r$ as one number.** It varies with frequency, resin content, glass style and moisture.
- **Ignoring $T_g$ with lead-free assembly.** Standard FR-4 near its $T_g$ during reflow can warp or have via-barrel cracking in cycles.
- **Confusing loss tangent with conductivity.** Dielectric loss is displacement-current heating in the insulator, separate from conductor resistance.
- **Using FR-4 for heat removal.** Its 0.3 W/mK is 1,000 times worse than copper, so heat paths need copper or a metal-core board ([[q0464]]).
- **Forgetting the weave.** Differential pairs routed along one glass bundle and one resin gap see different delays.
:::

::: deep
## What the dielectric does

Every trace is a transmission line over a reference plane, so the dielectric determines three things:

1. **Impedance and speed**: $Z_0$ falls and delay rises with $\varepsilon_r$.
2. **Loss**: energy is dissipated in the dielectric in proportion to $\tan\delta$ and frequency.
3. **Thermal and mechanical behaviour**: stiffness, expansion, temperature capability.

### Dielectric constant

Capacitance per unit length of a trace increases with $\varepsilon_r$, so $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ falls and the propagation delay $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/c$ rises. For FR-4 microstrip ($\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3.2$) the delay is about 6 ps/mm (150 ps/inch); for a PTFE board ($\varepsilon_{eff}\approx1.9$) about 4.6 ps/mm. With a given geometry, $Z_0$ moves: a trace that is 50 Ω on $\varepsilon_r=4.3$ would be about 54 Ω at 3.5 and 63 Ω at 2.2. So when you change laminate you re-compute trace widths.

### Loss tangent

In an AC field the dielectric's polarisation lags and dissipates energy. The attenuation from dielectric loss is

$$\alpha_d \approx \frac{\pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}\tan\delta}{c}\ \text{(Np/m)} \ \Rightarrow\ 8.686\,\alpha_d\ \text{dB/m}$$

It grows linearly with frequency (conductor loss grows as $\sqrt f$ plus roughness effects), so dielectric loss dominates above a few GHz in FR-4.

### Glass weave and moisture

Woven glass has $\varepsilon_r\approx6$ and the epoxy about 3.2, so a trace over a glass bundle sees a higher local $\varepsilon_r$ than one over a resin gap. For fast differential pairs this causes **skew** (one leg slower) and impedance variation. Mitigations: spread-glass (flatter weave) styles, routing the pair at a slight angle to the weave, or lower-resin-content laminates. Moisture raises both $\varepsilon_r$ and $\tan\delta$ (water has $\varepsilon_r\approx80$) and can cause blistering in reflow, so boards are baked before assembly.

## Thermal properties

- **$T_g$**: standard FR-4 is about 130 to 140 °C and high-Tg grades 170 to 180 °C. Above $T_g$ the z-axis expansion jumps (from roughly 50 to 70 ppm/°C below to several hundred ppm/°C above), stressing via barrels and solder joints. Lead-free reflow peaks at about 245 to 260 °C, above $T_g$, so the time above $T_g$ and the decomposition temperature $T_d$ (typically about 300 °C or more for good grades) matter.
- **Thermal conductivity**: FR-4 is about 0.3 W/mK, so heat must flow laterally through copper and vertically through thermal vias. For a 1 cm² area, a 1.6 mm FR-4 slab is 53 K/W, whereas a 0.63 mm alumina substrate ($k\approx24$ W/mK) is 0.26 K/W and AlN ($k\approx170$) is 0.04 K/W.
- **CTE**: ceramics match silicon (4 to 7 ppm/°C) better than FR-4 (14 to 17 ppm/°C in-plane), important for large ceramic-package or bare-die attach.

## Materials in context

- **FR-4 family** (including high-Tg, low-halogen, mid-loss grades): most boards; the grades differ in $T_g$, loss and flammability ([[q0470]]).
- **CEM-1 / CEM-3**: composite epoxy materials with paper or non-woven glass cores; cheaper, lower performance, used in low-cost consumer boards (single-sided CEM-1 is common in appliances).
- **Polyimide**: flex and high-temperature boards ([[q0465]]).
- **PTFE and hydrocarbon-ceramic laminates**: RF, microwave, radar, with controlled $\varepsilon_r$.
- **Ceramics (alumina, AlN, LTCC)**: high-temperature, hermetic, RF modules and power substrates.
- **Metal-core dielectrics**: epoxy filled with ceramic particles (1 to 3 W/mK and up) bonded to aluminium or copper ([[q0464]]).
- **Build-up films** (ABF, ajinomoto-type) for IC substrates: thin low-loss films for packages.

## Hybrid stack-ups

RF laminate is expensive. A common cost-saving design uses an RF material only for the layers carrying the RF signal (with a ground plane under them) and standard FR-4 for the remaining layers, bonded in one stack. The trade-off is process complexity and warp from the CTE mismatch, so the fabricator must agree the stack-up.

## Choosing

| Requirement | Material |
|---|---|
| General electronics, below about 1 GHz | Standard FR-4 |
| Lead-free, many thermal cycles, thick boards | High-Tg FR-4 |
| Multi-gigabit digital, long traces | Mid- or low-loss epoxy-glass |
| Radar, microwave, mmWave | PTFE or ceramic-filled hydrocarbon |
| Bending / hinges / high temperature | Polyimide |
| Heat spreading for power or LEDs | Metal-core, ceramic |
| Very low cost, low complexity | CEM-1 or FR-2-class materials (single-sided) |
:::

::: applications
- **Consumer and industrial electronics**: standard and high-Tg FR-4.
- **Servers and switches (10 to 112 Gb/s links)**: low-loss epoxy-glass with spread-glass reinforcement.
- **Automotive radar (77 GHz) and satellite links**: PTFE or ceramic-filled RF laminates in hybrid stack-ups.
- **Flexible interconnects**: polyimide ([[q0465]]).
- **LED lighting and power modules**: metal-core or ceramic substrates ([[q0464]]).
- **Aerospace and downhole electronics**: polyimide and ceramic for temperature extremes.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a higher dielectric constant slow signals down?
Propagation velocity is $c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$: the electric field stores energy in the dielectric and the polarisation of the material slows the wave. With $\varepsilon_{eff}=3.2$ the velocity is 0.56 c (about 168 mm/ns), so 100 mm of trace takes about 0.6 ns. A lower-$\varepsilon_r$ laminate makes signals faster, reduces capacitance per length and widens traces for a given impedance.

### How does loss tangent differ from conductor loss, and which dominates?
Conductor loss comes from skin-effect resistance and grows with $\sqrt f$ and foil roughness; dielectric loss grows linearly with $f$. On FR-4 conductor loss typically dominates below about 1 to 2 GHz and dielectric loss above, so for 5 GHz and beyond the laminate choice is critical, while below 1 GHz FR-4 is fine.

### What is the glass-weave effect and how do you mitigate it?
The glass bundles have higher $\varepsilon_r$ than the resin between them, so each leg of a differential pair sees a different average permittivity depending on where it sits relative to the weave, causing skew and mode conversion at multi-gigabit rates. Mitigations: spread (flat) glass styles such as 1067 or 1078, routing at 10 degrees to the weave, or zig-zag routing, or low-skew laminate.

### What if the board must survive 260 °C lead-free reflow ten times?
Use a laminate with a high decomposition temperature $T_d$ (above about 340 °C for robust grades) and high $T_g$ (170 °C or more), a low z-axis CTE, and confirm with multiple-reflow tests (IPC test methods). Standard FR-4 with $T_g$ 135 °C can delaminate or crack via barrels over repeated cycles.

### Scenario: your 25 Gb/s link fails the loss budget on FR-4 by 3 dB. Options?
Shorten the trace (dielectric loss alone is about 1 dB/inch at the 12.5 GHz Nyquist frequency for FR-4, and conductor loss adds to it, so cutting about 3 inches saves roughly 3 dB), switch to a mid-loss or low-loss laminate (3 to 4 times lower), use smoother copper foil to reduce roughness loss, add equalisation or a retimer, or use a hybrid stack-up with the premium material only on the signal layers.
:::
