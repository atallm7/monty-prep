::: testing
Whether you can produce a **correct, labeled schematic from memory** and talk through it as you draw. Three things are being checked at once:

1. **Topology**: the inductor is in *series with the input*, the switch goes from the inductor's far end to ground, and the diode goes from that node to the output. Swap the order and you have drawn a buck or a short circuit.
2. **Orientation**: diode anode at the switch node, cathode at the output, capacitor polarity, and a ground reference.
3. **Awareness of what the drawing implies**: where the switch node is, where current flows in each phase, and what real converters add (gate drive, feedback divider, input capacitor).
:::

::: why
Interviewers hand you a marker because a drawing exposes misunderstanding faster than words. Nearly everyone remembers "inductor, diode, capacitor, switch" and then places them wrongly under pressure. A clean boost sketch is also the base for the next questions: waveforms, component roles ([[q0275]]), flyback ([[q0276]]) and debugging. If you can place the parts and explain *why* each is where it is, the follow-ups become easy.
:::

::: concepts
- **Series inductor on the input**: the inductor sits between $V_{in}$ and the switch node. See [[q0270]] for why.
- **Low-side switch to ground**: the switch shorts the inductor's far end to ground to charge it. Typically an N-channel MOSFET ([[q0101]] covers the body diode).
- **Diode orientation**: anode toward the switch, cathode toward the output, so it conducts only when SW rises above $V_{out}$.
- **Output capacitor**: from the output node to ground, polarity marked for electrolytic or polymer parts. See [[q0022]].
- **Schematic symbols**: inductor [[q0038]], and the buck drawing [[q0175]] for comparison.
- **Feedback divider**: two resistors from $V_{out}$ to the IC's FB pin. See [[q0008]].
:::

::: answer
"I'll start with the input source on the left and draw a line out to the right. In series with that line I put the inductor. At the right end of the inductor is the switch node. From there I draw the switch, a MOSFET, going down to ground, with its gate driven by a PWM signal. Then, also from the switch node, I put a diode going to the right with the anode at the switch node and the cathode toward the output. After the diode is the output node, with a capacitor to ground and then the load.

So the path is $V_{in}$, inductor, switch node, diode, $V_{out}$, and the switch hangs off the switch node to ground. I'd label the switch node, mark the inductor current direction, and add a ground symbol.

If I have space I'd add the real-world bits: an input capacitor, a feedback divider from the output back to the controller, and I'd mention that in a synchronous version the diode gets replaced by a second MOSFET. The easy check: the inductor goes on the input side, which is different from a buck where it's on the output side."
:::

::: technical
**Netlist (non-synchronous boost):**

| From | To | Part |
|---|---|---|
| $V_{in}$ (+) | SW | Inductor $L$ |
| SW | GND | N-MOSFET $Q$ (drain at SW, source at GND, gate from PWM driver) |
| SW | $V_{out}$ | Schottky diode $D$ (anode SW, cathode $V_{out}$) |
| $V_{out}$ | GND | Output capacitor $C_{out}$ (+ at $V_{out}$) |
| $V_{out}$ | GND | Load |
| $V_{in}$ | GND | Input capacitor $C_{in}$ (recommended) |

**Feedback network:** $V_{out}\to R_1\to$ FB pin $\to R_2\to$ GND, giving $V_{out}=V_{FB}\left(1+\dfrac{R_1}{R_2}\right)$.

**Typical sizing for 3.7 V to 5 V at 1 A, 500 kHz** (see [[q0270]]): $D\approx0.26$, $L\approx4.7\ \mu$H ($\Delta I_L\approx0.41$ A, peak 1.56 A so $I_{sat}>2$ A), $C_{out}\approx22\ \mu$F (ripple about 24 mV, plus ESR), $C_{in}\approx10\ \mu$F, diode and MOSFET rated for at least 2 A.

**Voltage ratings:** the switch node reaches $V_{out}+V_F$, so the MOSFET and diode need $V_{DS},V_R>V_{out}$ with margin (commonly 20 to 50% above $V_{out}$ plus ringing).

**Synchronous version:** replace $D$ with a second MOSFET $Q_2$ (drain at $V_{out}$, source at SW) driven by the complement of $Q$'s gate with dead time, which removes the diode forward drop loss.
:::

::: short
"Input on the left, inductor in series, then a switch node. From the switch node a MOSFET goes to ground, and a diode goes to the output with its anode on the switch node. The output has a capacitor to ground and the load. So it's source, inductor, diode, output, with the switch pulling the middle node to ground. I'd mark the switch node, the inductor current, the diode direction and ground, and mention the feedback divider and the sync-FET option."
:::

::: long
"I'll draw it left to right and narrate. On the left is the input source, $V_{in}$, with the positive side going out along the top rail and the negative side to ground along the bottom.

The first thing on the top rail is the inductor, in series with the input. Past the inductor is the switch node. From here two things branch: straight down, a MOSFET to ground, which is the switch, with its gate fed by the PWM signal. And to the right, a diode with its anode on the switch node and its cathode on the output side.

At the output node I put the output capacitor to ground, marked with polarity if it's an electrolytic, and then the load, a resistor or whatever is being powered.

The way I check I've drawn it correctly is by walking both phases. With the switch closed, current goes from $V_{in}$ through the inductor and the MOSFET to ground, so the inductor charges, and the diode is reverse-biased. With the switch open, the inductor current goes through the diode into the capacitor and load. If the diode were backwards or the inductor were on the wrong side, one of those paths wouldn't exist.

If there's room I'd add three things. An input capacitor, because the input current is the inductor current and is reasonably smooth, but you still need local decoupling. A feedback divider from the output to the controller's FB pin, $V_{out}=V_{FB}(1+R_1/R_2)$. And I'd say that in a synchronous boost the diode is replaced by a second MOSFET to save the diode drop, at the cost of dead-time control."
:::

::: terms
- **Switch node (SW)**: the node joining inductor, switch and diode. It is the noisiest node in the layout.
- **Low-side switch**: the MOSFET referenced to ground, driven directly by a gate driver.
- **Rectifier diode / catch diode**: usually a Schottky for low $V_F$ and fast recovery.
- **Freewheel path**: the route inductor current takes when the switch opens (here, the diode to the output).
- **Feedback divider ($R_1/R_2$)**: scales $V_{out}$ to the controller's reference.
- **Input capacitor**: local decoupling for the source, and a low-impedance return for the switching current.
- **Synchronous rectification**: replacing the diode with a MOSFET driven in complement.
- **Body diode**: the parasitic diode inside the MOSFET, which can conduct during dead time.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws four parts in roughly the right order and gets the diode direction right.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws the inductor on the *input* side and says that is the defining difference from a buck.
- Labels the switch node, the input-to-output path (the "always on" diode path), and the current directions.
- Adds an input capacitor, a feedback divider and the gate drive without being asked.
- Verifies the drawing by tracing current in both phases.
- Mentions the synchronous variant and why you'd use it.
- Notes the practical layout point: the loop of switch, diode and output capacitor must be tight and the SW node small, because it is the high $dV/dt$ node.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the inductor on the output side** (that is a buck, [[q0175]]).
- **Drawing the diode backwards**, or putting the diode in series with the switch to ground.
- **Putting the switch in series with the inductor.** The switch is a *shunt* from SW to ground, not in the input line.
- **Forgetting ground symbols**, so it isn't clear what the switch is connected to.
- **No input capacitor and no feedback path.** Fine for a first sketch, but a real circuit has both and a good answer mentions them.
- **Missing the polarity on an electrolytic output capacitor**, or using an electrolytic symbol for an MLCC.
- **Not realizing the boost has a DC path from input to output** through the inductor and diode. It matters for short-circuit protection and for turning the output off.
:::

::: deep
## Draw it in four moves

Practise this order until it is automatic.

1. **Source and rails.** A source $V_{in}$ on the left. Top rail is the input, bottom rail is ground.
2. **Inductor in series on the top rail.** The end of the inductor farthest from the source is the *switch node*. Mark it.
3. **Switch to ground from the switch node.** An NMOS: drain to SW, source to ground, gate from the PWM. Draw the body diode if you want to show you know it exists ([[q0101]]).
4. **Diode to the output, then capacitor and load.** Anode on SW, cathode on the output node. Capacitor from the output node to ground. Load across it.

## Sanity checks: trace the current

Run both phases on your drawing.

- **Switch on:** current goes $V_{in}\to L\to$ SW $\to Q\to$ ground $\to V_{in}$. The diode's cathode (at $V_{out}$) is higher than its anode (at SW, near 0 V), so it is off. $C_{out}$ supplies the load.
- **Switch off:** the current in $L$ continues to SW, finds the switch open, SW rises until $D$ turns on, and the current goes $L\to D\to$ (output node) $\to$ capacitor/load $\to$ ground $\to V_{in}$.

If you cannot trace both loops, the drawing is wrong. This check also catches the two classic errors: with the inductor on the output side you would have drawn a buck, and with the diode reversed the energy has no path out.

Another useful check: **what does the drawing say with the switch permanently open?** Current can go $V_{in}\to L\to D\to$ output with no switching at all. So the output sits at about $V_{in}-V_F$ and there is no way to turn it off or protect it against an output short with the converter alone. Many boost ICs add a load switch for that reason.

Figure: Most boost ICs integrate the MOSFET (and sometimes the diode), so the schematic shows an IC with SW, VIN, GND, EN and FB pins. $R_1/R_2$ sets the output voltage and $C_{in}$ is placed right at the VIN pin.

The feedback divider sets $V_{out}=V_{FB}(1+R_1/R_2)$. For $V_{out}=5$ V with a 0.6 V feedback reference (value is part-dependent, check the datasheet) and $R_2=10$ kΩ: $R_1=R_2(V_{out}/V_{FB}-1)=10\text{ k}\times(5/0.6-1)=73.3$ kΩ. The nearest E96 value is 73.2 kΩ, giving $0.6\times(1+7.32)=4.992$ V, about 0.2% low before resistor and reference tolerance. The feedback node is a high-impedance, noise-sensitive trace and must be routed away from SW.

> [!NOTE]
> The input capacitor in a boost is less critical than in a buck, because the input current is the inductor current and is nearly DC with a triangular ripple of $\Delta I_L$. The *output* capacitor is the one with pulsed current. In a buck it is reversed: the input current is pulsed. This is a good detail to volunteer.

## Synchronous version

```circuit
{"caption":"Synchronous boost: the diode becomes a second switch","elements":[{"t":"V","a":[0,6],"b":[0,12],"label":"V_{in}"},{"t":"wire","pts":[[0,6],[0,4],[4,4]]},{"t":"L","a":[4,4],"b":[10,4],"label":"L"},{"t":"wire","pts":[[10,4],[10,7]]},{"t":"dot","at":[10,4]},{"t":"nmos","at":[8,9],"label":"Q_1 (low side)","bodyDiode":true},{"t":"wire","pts":[[8,9],[5,9]]},{"t":"term","at":[5,9],"label":"G1","side":"left"},{"t":"wire","pts":[[10,11],[10,12]]},{"t":"dot","at":[10,12]},{"t":"SW","a":[10,4],"b":[16,4],"label":"Q_2 (G2 = not G1)"},{"t":"dot","at":[16,4]},{"t":"wire","pts":[[16,4],[16,5]]},{"t":"Cpol","a":[16,5],"b":[16,8],"label":"C_{out}"},{"t":"wire","pts":[[16,8],[16,12]]},{"t":"dot","at":[16,12]},{"t":"wire","pts":[[16,4],[22,4],[22,5]]},{"t":"R","a":[22,5],"b":[22,9],"label":"R_{load}"},{"t":"wire","pts":[[22,9],[22,12]]},{"t":"wire","pts":[[0,12],[22,12]]},{"t":"gnd","at":[10,12]}]}
```
Figure: The diode is replaced by a second MOSFET $Q_2$ whose gate is driven with the complement of $Q_1$'s gate plus a short dead time. The switch conducts with $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ instead of $V_F\cdot I$, which at about 1.16 A RMS and $R_{DS(on)}=50$ mΩ is roughly 67 mW against 400 mW for a 0.4 V Schottky.

For the same current: Schottky loss $=V_F I_{out}=0.4\times1=0.4$ W (8% of 5 W), versus a synchronous FET with $R_{DS(on)}=50$ mΩ: $I_{D,rms}^2R\approx(1.35\sqrt{0.74})^2\times0.05\approx0.07$ W. That is roughly the efficiency gain a sync boost buys at 1 A. The cost is gate-drive complexity, dead-time control (shoot-through risk, [[q0201]] discusses the buck analog), and the need to prevent reverse current at light load.

## Component values to put next to the drawing

For the 3.7 V to 5 V, 1 A, 500 kHz example:

| Part | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| $L$ | 4.7 µH, $I_{sat}\ge2.5$ A | peak 1.56 A plus margin ([[q0259]]) |
| $Q$ | 20 V NMOS, $R_{DS(on)}\le50$ mΩ, logic-level gate | $V_{DS}$ sees $V_{out}$ plus ringing |
| $D$ | 20 V Schottky, 2 A | $V_F\approx0.4$ V at 1 A |
| $C_{out}$ | 22 µF ceramic (derated for DC bias) | ripple about 24 mV plus ESR step |
| $C_{in}$ | 10 µF ceramic close to the IC | source decoupling |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Component values here are illustrative design-starting points, not recommendations for a specific part. Ceramic capacitors lose a large fraction of their nominal capacitance at DC bias (X5R/X7R, small packages can lose over half at rated voltage). Check the manufacturer's DC-bias curves.

::: applications
- **USB power banks**: a synchronous boost takes the Li-ion cell up to 5 V (and higher with a buck-boost for fast-charge rails).
- **Handheld flashlights and LED drivers**: a boost IC with a current-sense feedback instead of a voltage divider.
- **Coin-cell sensor nodes**: nano-power boost ICs with a start-up circuit that can start from under 1 V.
- **Automotive cold-crank support**: boost stage keeps a 5 V or 3.3 V rail alive when the battery drops during engine start.
- **LCD, OLED and e-paper bias supplies**: boost from 3.3 V or a battery to 15 V to 30 V.
:::

::: assumptions
- A **non-synchronous, voltage-fed** boost with a **low-side N-channel MOSFET** is the standard textbook drawing and the one I assumed.
- The load is a resistor, but in a product it is the downstream circuit, which may need a **load switch** because the boost cannot cut off its own output.
- IC symbols vary: some parts integrate the diode (synchronous), some have a ground-referenced current-sense resistor, some use a current-sense feedback instead of a voltage divider (LED drivers).
- An interviewer might also accept a drawing with a generic switch symbol instead of a MOSFET. State the transistor type if you draw one.
- If asked for a **buck-boost or SEPIC**, the parts are the same but arranged differently. Do not blend them.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the inductor on the input side?
Because the topology relies on the inductor being in *series* with the source. Its current must be continuous ([[q0043]]), and when the switch opens that current is forced into the output at a voltage above $V_{in}$. An inductor on the output side gives you a buck, where the inductor smooths the output current and the switch chops the input.

### Could the low-side switch be a P-channel MOSFET?
Not sensibly. The switch is referenced to ground, so an N-channel device can be driven directly by a ground-referenced gate driver and has about a third of the $R_{DS(on)}$ per area. A P-channel is used elsewhere in a boost: as an output **load-disconnect** switch (to cut the DC path from input to output), or as the synchronous rectifier, where its gate must be driven relative to $V_{out}$, which needs a level shift or a charge-pump supply.

### Where would you put the current-sense resistor?
For peak current mode control, a small series resistor (tens of mΩ) in the source of $Q$ senses switch current during the on-time, which is also the inductor current. Many ICs sense $R_{DS(on)}$ of the internal MOSFET instead. For input current limiting a sense resistor sits in series with $V_{in}$. See [[q0225]] for current-sensing options.

### What does the boost do when the output is shorted to ground?
The diode and inductor form a DC path from $V_{in}$ to the short, with nothing limiting the current except the inductor DCR, diode and source resistance. A non-synchronous boost with no load switch cannot stop it, so designs add a series load switch or use parts with true-shutdown (output disconnect) capability.

### Scenario: you draw a boost and the interviewer says, "Now give me one that works from 3 V to 4.2 V in and 3.3 V out." What do you say?
That is both above and below the output, so a plain boost won't do: when $V_{in}=4.2$ V a boost output would sit at about 3.8 V (the pass-through path), above the 3.3 V target. I'd propose a buck-boost: either a four-switch buck-boost, or a SEPIC/Zeta, or cascading a buck followed by a boost if efficiency allows. If the 3.3 V load tolerates 3.3 V to 3.8 V, an LDO after a boost is also possible, with a cost in efficiency.
:::

::: interpretations
"Draw a boost converter circuit" most plausibly means the **power-stage schematic** (inductor, switch, diode, capacitor). Some interviewers expect the **control** side too: a feedback divider, error amplifier, PWM comparator and gate driver. The deep section shows the power stage first and then the IC-level version with the divider. The buck counterpart is [[q0175]]; the charge-pump drawing is [[q0286]].
:::

::: rubric
- Inductor in series with the input :: This is the defining topological feature and the most common error.
- Switch from the switch node to ground :: It shows you understand how the inductor is charged.
- Diode with anode at SW and cathode at the output :: Wrong orientation means no energy path.
- Output capacitor to ground and load across it :: Completes the circuit and identifies the output.
- Labels: $V_{in}$, SW, $V_{out}$, ground, PWM gate :: Shows you are thinking about what each node does.
- Traces current in both phases :: Verifies the drawing and demonstrates understanding of operation.
- Mentions feedback divider, input capacitor, or synchronous variant :: Signals real-design awareness.
:::
