::: testing
Whether you understand sampling as **measuring a continuous signal at discrete, evenly spaced instants**, and can say what is preserved and what is lost. A strong answer states the sampling theorem ($f_s>2f_{max}$), explains aliasing as the price of breaking it, and knows the practical consequences: an anti-alias filter before the ADC, sample-clock jitter, and the difference between sampling (discrete in time) and quantization (discrete in amplitude). In the ADC section of an embedded interview, the interviewer wants the ADC view of sampling rather than a pure DSP lecture.
:::

::: why
Every digital system that touches the physical world samples. Choosing the sample rate wrongly produces garbage that looks like real data: a 900 Hz vibration that appears as 100 Hz, a PWM ripple that shows up as a slow drift in your ADC reading. Understanding sampling lets you pick $f_s$, size the anti-alias filter, schedule ADC triggers inside a PWM period and judge when a jittery clock is limiting your measurement. It underlies [[q0668]], [[q0669]] and [[q0670]] in the signals section, and the sample-and-hold ([[q0814]]) is how hardware implements it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Continuous versus discrete time**: an analog signal $x(t)$ has a value at every instant; samples $x[n]=x(nT_s)$ exist only at multiples of $T_s=1/f_s$. See [[q0665]].
- **Sampling theorem (Nyquist–Shannon)**: a signal band-limited to $f_{max}$ is fully recoverable if $f_s>2f_{max}$. See [[q0669]].
- **Aliasing**: content above $f_s/2$ folds back to $|f-kf_s|$. See [[q0670]].
- **Spectrum replication**: sampling makes copies of the spectrum centred on multiples of $f_s$. See [[q0666]].
- **Anti-alias filter**: low-pass before the sampler.
- **Quantization**: separate step that discretizes amplitude ([[q0808]]).
:::

::: answer
"Sampling is taking a continuous signal and recording its value only at regular instants, say every $T_s$ seconds, so I end up with a list of numbers instead of a curve. In an ADC, that's the sample-and-hold grabbing the voltage on a capacitor at each clock edge.

What matters is what that costs you. If the signal is band-limited and I sample at more than twice its highest frequency, no information is lost: in principle I can reconstruct the original exactly. That's the Nyquist criterion. If I sample too slowly, frequencies above half the sample rate fold back down and look like lower frequencies. A 900 Hz tone sampled at 1 kHz looks exactly like a 100 Hz tone. Once it's sampled, you can't tell them apart.

So in practice I put a low-pass anti-alias filter in front of the ADC and sample comfortably faster than twice my bandwidth, usually five to ten times, so the filter can be simple. I also keep in mind that sampling is separate from quantization. Sampling discretizes time; quantization discretizes amplitude. And the clock needs to be clean, because timing jitter converts directly into amplitude noise on fast signals."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** Ideal sampling of $x(t)$ at period $T_s=1/f_s$: $x[n]=x(nT_s)$. Mathematically it is multiplication by an impulse train, $x_s(t)=x(t)\sum_n\delta(t-nT_s)$.

**Spectrum.** Multiplying by an impulse train in time convolves with an impulse train in frequency, so

$$X_s(f)=f_s\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}X(f-kf_s)$$

The spectrum of $x$ is repeated at every multiple of $f_s$. If $X(f)=0$ for $|f|>f_{max}$ and $f_s>2f_{max}$, the copies don't overlap and an ideal low-pass filter recovers $x(t)$. If they overlap, the overlap is **aliasing** and is irreversible.

**Alias frequency.** A tone at $f_{in}$ appears at $f_{alias}=|f_{in}-\text{round}(f_{in}/f_s)\,f_s|$, always within $[0,f_s/2]$. Example: $f_{in}=900$ Hz, $f_s=1000$ Hz gives 100 Hz.

**Anti-alias filter requirement.** To keep aliased noise below one LSB for an $N$-bit converter, the filter must attenuate content at $f_s-f_{max}$ by about $6.02N$ dB. A first-order RC filter attenuates by only 20 dB/decade, so oversampling is usually combined with a filter of order $\ge2$.

**Sampling-clock jitter.** For a sine of frequency $f$ and rms timing error $\sigma_t$, the jitter-limited SNR is

$$\text{SNR}_{jitter}=-20\log_{10}(2\pi f\sigma_t)$$

| $f$ | $\sigma_t$ | SNR limit |
|---|---|---|
| 1 kHz | 1 ns | 104 dB |
| 100 kHz | 1 ns | 64 dB (about 10.3 bits) |
| 10 MHz | 1 ps | 84 dB |
| 100 MHz | 1 ps | 64 dB |

**Aperture.** A real sampler averages over a small aperture time and has finite bandwidth, which is why the S/H ([[q0814]]) matters.

**Uniform versus non-uniform.** The theorem as stated is for uniform sampling; compressed sensing and non-uniform schemes can beat it for sparse signals, but that's beyond this question.
:::

::: short
"Sampling is measuring a continuous signal at regular time intervals, giving a sequence of numbers. If the signal's highest frequency is below half the sample rate, which is Nyquist, the samples contain all the information. If not, higher frequencies fold back as aliases: a 900 Hz tone sampled at 1 kHz looks like 100 Hz. So you put an anti-alias low-pass filter ahead of the ADC and sample comfortably faster than twice the bandwidth. Sampling discretizes time; quantization discretizes amplitude."
:::

::: long
"Sampling means looking at a continuous signal only at regular instants, every $T_s$ seconds, and keeping just those values. In an ADC, a sample-and-hold switch closes briefly, a capacitor takes on the input voltage, the switch opens, and that held value is what gets converted.

The natural question is what you lose by throwing away everything between the samples. The surprising answer is nothing, provided the signal is band-limited and you sample faster than twice its highest frequency. That's the Nyquist–Shannon theorem. In the frequency domain, sampling makes copies of the signal's spectrum centred on every multiple of the sample rate. If the signal stays below $f_s/2$, the copies don't touch and a low-pass filter recovers the original.

If the signal has content above $f_s/2$, the copies overlap and that content folds down. That's aliasing. A 900 Hz tone sampled at 1000 samples a second produces exactly the same numbers as a 100 Hz tone, and no amount of processing afterwards can tell which it was. This is why every ADC input needs an anti-alias filter, an analog low-pass before the sampler.

In practice I'd choose the sample rate at five to ten times the bandwidth I care about so the filter can be a simple RC, and I'd think about aliases from things I don't care about: PWM switching noise, mains pickup, a motor driver. Also, sampling is not quantization. Sampling is discrete in time; quantization is discrete in amplitude, and each adds its own error.

The last thing is timing. Sampling at the wrong instant by $\Delta t$ gives an amplitude error equal to the signal slope times $\Delta t$. At 100 kHz with 1 ns of clock jitter the SNR is limited to about 64 dB, roughly 10 bits, even if the ADC has 16."
:::

::: terms
- **Sample rate ($f_s$)**: samples per second; $T_s=1/f_s$.
- **Nyquist frequency**: $f_s/2$, the highest frequency representable without aliasing.
- **Nyquist rate**: $2f_{max}$, the minimum sample rate for a signal with highest frequency $f_{max}$.
- **Aliasing**: a frequency above $f_s/2$ appearing as a lower frequency $|f-kf_s|$.
- **Anti-alias filter**: analog low-pass filter ahead of the sampler.
- **Oversampling**: sampling much faster than $2f_{max}$, which relaxes the filter and enables averaging.
- **Jitter**: random variation in the sampling instant.
- **Aperture time**: window over which the sampler actually captures the input.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "taking measurements at intervals" and "you need twice the frequency".

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the theorem **with its condition**: *band-limited* signal and $f_s>2f_{max}$, strictly greater.
- Explains **why** through spectrum replication, not just the rule.
- Gives a **numerical alias example** (900 Hz at 1 kHz gives 100 Hz) and knows aliasing is irreversible.
- Separates **sampling from quantization**.
- Mentions the **anti-alias filter** and that real filters need $f_s$ well above $2f_{max}$ (guard band).
- Brings up **jitter** with the formula, or ADC trigger timing relative to PWM in embedded systems.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Sample at exactly twice the frequency."** The theorem requires strictly more than twice, and at exactly $2f$ a sine sampled at its zero crossings gives all zeros. Real systems need 5 to 10×.
- **Thinking you can filter aliasing out after sampling.** The alias is indistinguishable from real signal; the filter has to be analog and before the sampler.
- **Only considering the signal you want.** Noise and interference above $f_s/2$ alias too (switching ripple, mains harmonics).
- **Confusing sampling with quantization.** One is time, the other amplitude.
- **Believing a higher resolution ADC fixes aliasing.** Bits don't help; the sample rate and filter do.
- **Ignoring sampling-clock quality.** Jitter and phase noise limit SNR at high input frequencies regardless of bit count.
:::

::: deep
## What sampling is physically

An ADC does not watch a signal continuously. At each sample instant a switch connects the input to a small capacitor, the capacitor charges to $V_{in}$, and the switch opens ([[q0814]]). That held voltage is converted at leisure. The sequence of held values is the sampled signal $x[n]$.

The remarkable claim is that, under the right conditions, those isolated values contain everything about the signal. Here is why that is plausible: a band-limited signal can't wiggle arbitrarily fast between samples, because "no content above $f_{max}$" means it's smooth on the scale of $1/(2f_{max})$.

## What it looks like in the frequency domain

Sampling replicates the spectrum around $0,\pm f_s,\pm2f_s,\dots$. For a signal occupying 0 to $B$:
- If $f_s>2B$, the gap between $B$ and $f_s-B$ is empty. A low-pass in that gap recovers the signal.
- If $f_s<2B$, the replica of the upper edge folds into the baseband.

That gap is also where the **anti-alias filter** transition band must fit. A filter that goes from passband at $B$ to full attenuation at $f_s-B$ gets easier as $f_s$ rises. With $B=10$ kHz and $f_s=100$ kHz, the transition band is 10 kHz to 90 kHz, a factor of 9, so a 2nd to 4th order filter will do. With $f_s=25$ kHz the transition is from 10 kHz to 15 kHz, so you'd need a brick-wall filter of high order.

## A number: how much does a first-order RC buy you?

An RC corner at 1.59 kHz ($R=1$ kΩ, $C=100$ nF) attenuates by $-10\log_{10}(1+(f/f_c)^2)$ dB. At 9.9 kHz that is $-16$ dB. If $f_s=10$ kS/s, an interferer at 9.9 kHz aliases to 100 Hz with only 16 dB of attenuation, so a 1 V interferer becomes 160 mV at 100 Hz. For a 12-bit ADC you'd want attenuation near 74 dB. Hence the usual advice: oversample (move $f_s$ far above the interferers) and use a higher-order filter.

## Sampling in embedded firmware

- **ADC trigger timing**: sample a motor current at the centre of the PWM on-time so you avoid switching spikes. Sampling is a deliberately timed event, not just "periodic".
- **Aliasing of PWM ripple**: a 20 kHz ripple sampled at 19.9 kS/s appears as a 100 Hz wobble in the reading.
- **Multiplexing**: scanning $M$ channels on one ADC gives each channel $f_s/M$.
- **Timer-triggered conversion**: use a hardware timer to trigger the ADC so the period is exact. A software loop with `delay()` has jitter of microseconds.

## Jitter: the clock's contribution

A sine of amplitude $A$ and frequency $f$ has a maximum slope $2\pi fA$, so a timing error $\Delta t$ produces a voltage error up to $2\pi fA\Delta t$. With random jitter of rms $\sigma_t$, relative to the signal's rms,

$$\text{SNR}_{jitter}=-20\log_{10}(2\pi f\sigma_t)$$

At 100 kHz with 1 ns of jitter that is 64 dB, i.e. about $(64-1.76)/6.02=10.3$ effective bits. For a 10 MHz signal to keep 84 dB (13.7 bits), jitter must be 1 ps. This is why high-speed ADCs need low phase-noise clocks and why a sloppy software timer limits slow ADCs only at higher input frequencies.

## Aside: sampling versus averaging

An ADC conversion with a short aperture captures an instant; a delta-sigma or integrating ADC averages over a window, which is a built-in low-pass filter (a sinc). The result is still discrete in time, but with much less aliasing, which is one reason integrating converters are favoured for noisy DC measurements.

::: applications
- **ADC inputs on every microcontroller**: choosing sample rate and an RC anti-alias filter on a sensor input.
- **Audio**: 44.1 or 48 kS/s for 20 kHz audio, with the anti-alias filter in the codec.
- **Motor control**: timing current samples to PWM so ripple doesn't alias into the control loop.
- **Oscilloscopes**: sampling rate versus bandwidth, and why a scope shows false low-frequency signals when under-sampled ([[q0980]]).
- **Vibration and IMU data**: sampling at 1 kHz or more with a filter ahead of an accelerometer to avoid aliased resonances.
- **Software-defined radio**: deliberate undersampling (sub-sampling) of a band-pass signal to downconvert it.
:::

::: assumptions
- The signal is **band-limited** and the samples are **uniform in time**. Real signals are only approximately band-limited, and noise has no limit, hence the filter.
- **Ideal sampling** (instant, no aperture or jitter). Real sampling adds aperture droop, finite bandwidth and jitter.
- Baseband sampling: signal between 0 and $f_s/2$. For band-pass signals you can sample below $2f_{max}$ provided the bandwidth $B<f_s/2$ and the band lies in a single Nyquist zone, which is deliberate aliasing.
- The statement "no information lost" excludes quantization, which is a separate loss.
- This page answers "what is sampling" in the ADC context. [[q0668]] asks the sample rate for a sine; [[q0669]] asks what Nyquist is; [[q0670]] asks aliasing. Together they fill out the signals section.
:::

::: followups
### What sample rate do I need to measure a 20 kHz signal?
Strictly more than 40 kS/s in theory. In practice 100 kS/s or more with an anti-alias filter whose cutoff is near 20 kHz and whose stopband starts below $f_s-20$ kHz = 80 kHz. That is a factor of four between passband and stopband edge, which a 3rd-order filter can handle.

### What does a 6 kHz tone look like when sampled at 8 kS/s?
Nyquist is 4 kHz, so 6 kHz aliases to $|6-8|=2$ kHz. The processor sees a 2 kHz tone with the same amplitude, and there is no way to detect it afterwards.

### How does sampling differ from quantization?
Sampling picks discrete times, $x(nT_s)$, and is lossless if Nyquist is met. Quantization rounds each sample's amplitude to one of $2^N$ levels and is always lossy (error ±½ LSB). An ADC does both.

### Why do ADCs have a sample-and-hold?
Conversion takes time, and the input would move during it, giving an inconsistent result. The S/H freezes the value at the sampling instant ([[q0814]]). It also defines the sampling instant precisely.

### Harder: a 16-bit ADC at 100 kHz input gives only about 10 effective bits. What do you check?
First jitter: $\text{SNR}=-20\log_{10}(2\pi\cdot10^5\sigma_t)$; for 10 bits (62 dB) you'd need $\sigma_t\approx1.3$ ns, so a 2 ns jittery clock would explain it. Then check source-impedance settling, reference noise, and aliasing of out-of-band noise, which adds noise even with perfect timing.
