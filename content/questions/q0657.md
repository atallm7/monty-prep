::: basics
### In plain words
Ground is supposed to be a perfect 0 V reference. But ground on a chip is real metal, with some resistance and some inductance. Whenever current flows through it, and especially when the current changes fast, a voltage appears. So the chip's internal ground can sit above or below the board's ground. The biggest cause in digital chips is many outputs switching at once, which is called ground bounce.

### Picture it
Think of the floor of a building as your zero height. If a large crowd stomps at the same moment, the floor flexes, and everything resting on it seems to jump relative to the world outside. Ground bounce is the same. The more people stomp, and the faster they do it, the bigger the bounce. When the floor moves, every reading taken from it is off.

### Words you need
- **Ground**: the reference point that everything else is measured against, called 0 V.
- **Inductance**: how strongly a connection resists fast changes in current. See [[q0039]].
- **Ground bounce**: the die's ground voltage jumping because of current changes through the package. See [[q0688]].
- **Simultaneous switching**: many outputs changing at the same moment, so their currents add.
- **Decoupling capacitor**: a small capacitor next to the chip that supplies quick bursts of current locally.

### The simplest example
Say four outputs each charge a small load (10 pF, a tiny capacitance) to 3.3 V, and each switch takes 2 ns. Together they change the ground current by about 0.13 A every nanosecond. The package ground path has 1 nH of inductance. The voltage across an inductor is its inductance times how fast the current changes: 1 nH × 0.13 A/ns ≈ 0.13 V, or 130 mV. That can make a quiet pin glitch. Double the outputs and the bounce doubles. Halve the switching time and it roughly quadruples.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the other causes, such as voltage lost in the chip's wiring and noisy circuits sharing a return path, plus the fixes.
:::

::: why
Ground bounce and supply droop are among the commonest causes of "mystery" failures: outputs glitch when many pins switch, an ADC reads noisy codes when the digital side is busy, an MCU resets when a motor starts. Package and board designers allocate pins, planes and capacitors specifically to keep the chip's local ground close to the board ground. Understanding why ground moves tells you where to look with a scope and what to change. It links directly to bond wires ([[q0654]]), flip chip ([[q0656]]) and ground bounce itself ([[q0688]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Ground is a conductor, not an ideal node**: any real return path has resistance and inductance, so currents through it create voltage differences. See [[q0039]].
- **$V = L\,di/dt$**: fast current steps through package or PCB inductance generate voltage.
- **IR drop**: $V = IR$ along the on-die ground grid, package and planes.
- **Return current and shared impedance**: two circuits sharing a ground path couple into each other.
- **Simultaneous switching output (SSO) noise**: many outputs switching together add their current into the same ground pins.
- **Decoupling capacitors**: local charge reservoirs that supply the fast current so it does not have to come through the inductive path.
:::

::: answer
"The ground on a chip varies because ground isn't a perfect zero-ohm, zero-inductance node. It's metal, so it has resistance and inductance, and whenever current flows through it, you get a voltage drop. There are a few main mechanisms.

The biggest one for digital chips is inductance. When several outputs switch at once, they dump charge into the die's ground, and that current changes quickly. It flows out through the bond wires or bumps, pins and vias to the board, which have maybe a few nanohenries in total, so $V = L\,di/dt$ lifts the on-die ground above board ground. That's ground bounce, and it's worse with more outputs switching, faster edges, and fewer ground pins.

Second is plain IR drop. The on-die power grid, package traces and board planes have resistance, so a chip that draws amps sees its local ground and supply sag relative to the regulator, especially far from the pins.

Third, shared return paths and coupling: noisy circuits and sensitive ones sharing the same return path, substrate noise on a mixed-signal die, ground loops between boards, and ESD or other transients that inject current into ground.

The practical fixes are more ground pins and low-inductance packages like flip chip, decoupling capacitors right at the die, slower edges, and solid ground planes with good return paths. And the effect matters because everything is measured relative to ground, so if the chip's ground moves, thresholds, ADC references and clocks all see errors."
:::

::: technical
**1. Inductive noise (ground bounce).** For $N$ outputs each sinking current step $\Delta i$ in rise time $t_r$, the ground voltage is $V_{gb} \approx N L \,\frac{\Delta i}{t_r}$ (for the portion of the edge where current ramps up). For a CMOS output charging $C_L$ to $V_{DD}$ in $t_r$, the average current is $C_LV_{DD}/t_r$ and the peak about twice that for a triangular pulse, so $\frac{di}{dt}\approx \frac{4C_LV_{DD}}{t_r^2}$ per output. Example: $N=4$, $C_L=10$ pF, $V_{DD}=3.3$ V, $t_r=2$ ns, $L=1$ nH gives $di/dt = 0.033$ A/ns per output and $V_{gb}=4\times1\text{ nH}\times0.033\text{ A/ns}=0.13$ V.

**2. Resistive drop.** $\Delta V = I R$: e.g. 1 A through 20 mΩ of package and board plane is 20 mV. On a 0.9 V core with ±5% budget (45 mV), that is a big fraction. On-die grids add more.

**3. PDN resonance.** Package inductance with on-die capacitance form an LC with $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$; with $L = 1$ nH and $C = 10$ nF, $f_0 \approx 50$ MHz. Load steps near that frequency ring the supply and ground.

**4. Shared impedance and return path.** Return current follows the least-impedance path, which at high frequency is directly under the signal. Plane splits, missing stitching vias or shared return pins make unrelated circuits modulate each other's ground.

**5. Substrate coupling.** Switching digital blocks inject current into the shared silicon substrate, which moves local ground for analog blocks.

**6. External effects.** Ground loops between boards or via cable shields, mains-frequency currents, and ESD/EFT events produce ground potential differences of volts.

**Effects.** Reduced noise margin, false triggers on quiet inputs, ADC reference error, jitter, and logic upsets. Mitigate with more V<sub>SS</sub> pins distributed among I/O, decoupling, edge-rate control, differential signalling, flip-chip packages, and good PCB return paths.
:::

::: long
"The short answer is that ground isn't ideal. People draw it as a zero-volt node, but physically it's copper or aluminium, with resistance and inductance, so any current through it creates a voltage and the chip's ground can sit above or below the board's ground.

The most important mechanism for digital ICs is inductive. Suppose eight outputs switch together, each charging a 15 picofarad load through a 1 nanosecond edge. They draw tens of milliamps each with a fast ramp, and all that current returns through the die's ground, through bond wires or bumps, the package pins and the vias to the board. A bond wire alone is about a nanohenry per millimetre, so with two or three nanohenries of ground path and an amp per nanosecond of di/dt, you get $L\,di/dt$ of a few volts in the worst case, or hundreds of millivolts typically. That is ground bounce, and it's the same thing on the supply side as supply droop.

The second mechanism is just resistance. The on-die grid, the package and the board planes each have milliohms, and amps of current gives tens of millivolts of IR drop, which matters on a sub-1 volt core.

Third are coupling effects: sharing a return path between noisy and sensitive circuits, switching noise injected through the substrate on mixed-signal chips, ground loops between boards, and external events like ESD.

The consequences are lost noise margin, a quiet input falsely switching, ADC readings off, and jitter. The remedies: spread many ground pins through the I/O, use low-inductance packages such as flip chip, put decoupling capacitors as close to the die as possible so the fast current comes from them, slow down edges or limit simultaneous switching, and give return currents a continuous plane."
:::

::: terms
- **Ground bounce**: transient shift of a chip's local ground relative to the board ground, caused mainly by $L\,di/dt$.
- **SSO (simultaneous switching output) noise**: ground and supply noise from many outputs switching together.
- **IR drop**: static or slow voltage drop $I\cdot R$ along supply and ground conductors.
- **Return path**: the path current takes back to its source; at high frequency it hugs the signal path.
- **Common-impedance coupling**: interference from two circuits sharing part of a conductor.
- **PDN (power delivery network)**: the whole path from regulator to transistors, including planes, caps, package and die grid.
- **Substrate noise**: noise injected through the silicon substrate from switching digital circuits.
- **Ground loop**: a loop formed by multiple ground connections that picks up or carries unwanted current.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "because of noise and parasitic inductance." An exceptional answer:
- Says ground is a conductor and explains the three physical mechanisms: $L\,di/dt$, $IR$, and shared/return-path coupling.
- Computes a number, for example 4 outputs, 10 pF, 3.3 V, 2 ns edges, 1 nH gives about 130 mV.
- Explains what scales it: number of outputs, edge rate ($1/t_r^2$ for $di/dt$), package inductance, and number of ground pins.
- Names consequences (false triggering, reduced noise margin, ADC errors) and fixes (pin allocation, decoupling, flip chip, edge control).
- Mentions on-die versus board ground, and substrate coupling in mixed-signal chips.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating ground as 0 V everywhere.** It is a reference that moves relative to other references.
- **Blaming only resistance.** At switching speeds, inductance dominates; IR is the static part.
- **"Add one big capacitor."** A capacitor with long leads or far from the die is itself inductive; placement matters ([[q0506]]).
- **Believing parallel ground pins give $1/n$ inductance.** Mutual coupling reduces the benefit; also on-die grid resistance limits it.
- **Ignoring the return path.** Splitting planes or slotting under a fast signal forces the return current around a longer, more inductive loop.
:::

::: deep
## A ground node is a conductor

Draw any chip with a ground symbol and you imply infinite conductivity. In reality, the die's on-chip ground sits at the end of a chain: on-die grid, bumps or bond wires, package traces, balls or leads, board vias, board plane, then (finally) the regulator or battery return. Every link has $R$ and $L$.

The voltage between die ground and board ground is

$$v_{gb}(t) = R_g\, i(t) + L_g \frac{di}{dt}$$

so there is a resistive part proportional to current and an inductive part proportional to its slope.

## Where the current comes from

When a CMOS output switches, it charges or discharges its load capacitance $C_L$ (pad, trace, receiver). For $V_{DD}=3.3$ V, $C_L=10$ pF, edge time $t_r=2$ ns the charge moved is $Q=C_LV_{DD}=33$ pC; the average current over the edge is $Q/t_r=16.5$ mA. The current waveform rises then falls, so a triangular approximation with peak at mid-edge is $I_{pk}\approx 2Q/t_r = 33$ mA, reached in $t_r/2=1$ ns, giving $di/dt = 33\ \text{mA}/1\ \text{ns}=0.033\ \text{A/ns}$.

For $N$ outputs switching simultaneously the currents add:

$$V_{bounce} \approx N\,L_g\,\frac{4 C_L V_{DD}}{t_r^2}$$

Notice the scaling: linear in $N$ and $L_g$, and inverse square in rise time. Halving $t_r$ quadruples the bounce. With $N=4$, $L_g=1$ nH: $4\times1\text{ nH}\times0.033\text{ A/ns}=0.132$ V.

## Other mechanisms in detail

**IR drop in the grid.** A core drawing 10 A through an effective 5 mΩ of package and on-die grid loses 50 mV at DC. That is 5.5% of a 0.9 V rail, which already exceeds a typical ±5% tolerance. Designers model this as "IR drop analysis" and add bumps, thicker top metals and decoupling to reduce it.

**PDN resonance.** Package inductance in series with on-die capacitance forms a resonant tank: $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$. With $L=1$ nH and $C=10$ nF: $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{10^{-9}\cdot10^{-8}}) = 50$ MHz. Load current activity near this frequency causes large supply and ground oscillations, which is why decoupling is staged from board bulk caps to on-die caps.

**Shared return and plane discontinuities.** At high frequency return current flows directly under the signal trace. If the reference plane is split or the signal changes layers without a nearby ground via, the return takes a long path, adding inductance in the return, and a ground potential difference forms between the two sides of the discontinuity.

**Substrate coupling.** On mixed-signal chips, digital switching injects currents into the substrate, which couples to analog circuits by resistive paths. Guard rings and separate supplies and pads reduce this.

**Ground loops and external events.** Two boards connected by cables and also by chassis have a loop that picks up magnetic fields and carries mains-related current, giving millivolts to volts between "grounds". ESD and fast transient events inject amps for nanoseconds, with $L\,di/dt$ of kilovolts per ampere-nanosecond across microhenry loops.

## What it does to the chip

- **Noise margin** shrinks: a logic-low output now sits above 0 V relative to the board.
- **Quiet pins glitch**: an output held low is tied to the die ground, so at the pin it glitches up by the bounce, and a receiver referenced to board ground can see a false edge. Inputs are measured against the raised die ground, so their thresholds effectively shift.
- **Analog/ADC errors**: the reference or input ground moves relative to the signal.
- **Timing and jitter**: supply and ground noise modulates gate delays.

## How designers reduce it

1. **More ground and supply pins, distributed among I/Os** so currents divide and loop areas shrink (power/ground pins near the signal pins they serve).
2. **Low-inductance packages**: flip-chip BGA instead of wire-bonded leaded parts ([[q0656]]).
3. **Decoupling capacitors close to the die**, including on-package caps, to supply fast transients locally ([[q0509]]).
4. **Edge-rate control**, drive-strength selection, and staggering output transitions.
5. **Differential and low-swing I/O**, which have nearly constant supply current.
6. **Solid reference planes** and stitching vias, so return currents have short, low-inductance paths.
7. **Separate analog and digital grounds only where it helps**, with a single connection point and attention to the return paths.
:::

::: applications
- **FPGA and MCU board design**: pin planning rules limit simultaneous switching outputs per bank.
- **High-speed memory interfaces (DDR)**: package and board decoupling designed around SSO noise and PDN impedance targets.
- **ADCs and mixed-signal SoCs**: analog ground pins, guard rings and layout to avoid digital ground noise.
- **Motor drivers and power ICs**: high-current switching with careful Kelvin ground sensing and separate power and signal grounds.
- **Automotive and industrial systems**: ground offsets between modules are expected, so isolated or differential interfaces (CAN, RS-485) are used.
:::

::: assumptions
- The calculation assumes a triangular current pulse and a linear model with constant inductance. Real drivers self-limit, and package inductance includes mutual coupling.
- "Chip ground" here means the on-die ground relative to board ground. A similar analysis applies to supply droop.
- Numbers are illustrative for a 3.3 V CMOS part; modern low-voltage parts have smaller absolute bounce but much smaller noise margins.
- Different answers are valid depending on context: a digital designer emphasises SSO and L·di/dt, an analog designer emphasises substrate noise and grounding scheme, and a system designer emphasises ground loops.
- If the question means "why does the chip's ground differ across the die," emphasise on-die IR drop and the power grid.
:::

::: followups
### What is ground bounce and how would you measure it?
It is the transient shift of the die's ground relative to the board ground. To measure, probe the board ground and a quiet input held low or high with a short ground spring on a fast scope while other outputs toggle; a held-low output pin will show the bounce. You cannot see the die ground directly, so you infer it from a quiet pin.

### How does the number of ground pins affect ground bounce?
More pins in parallel reduce effective inductance and resistance, though mutual coupling means it falls less than $1/n$. Spreading them near the switching outputs matters too, because the current's loop area shrinks.

### Why does faster edge rate make ground bounce worse?
$di/dt$ scales as $1/t_r^2$ for a fixed charge $C V$. Halving the rise time quadruples the bounce. That is why slew-rate control, and slow-edge drive modes, are used on non-critical pins.

### Why can bounce make a quiet pin switch?
A quiet output held low is tied to the die ground, so when the die ground bounces up by 0.8 V that pin glitches up by 0.8 V relative to the board. A receiver referenced to board ground sees a false pulse that can cross its threshold. Similarly, a quiet input that is held high by an external driver is now measured against a raised die ground, so its apparent level changes and it can drop below $V_{IH}$ or glitch.

### Scenario: an MCU resets only when a relay driver on the same board switches. What do you check?
Look at supply and ground at the MCU pins with a scope for dips or spikes, compare to the brown-out threshold, and check the relay coil's return path: does its current share a ground trace with the MCU? Add a flyback diode, separate the high-current return from the sensitive return, add local decoupling at the MCU, and verify the plane continuity. The goal is to keep the relay's $L\,di/dt$ transient out of the MCU's ground reference.
:::
