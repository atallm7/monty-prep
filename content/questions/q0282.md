::: testing
Whether you follow energy all the way to its destination. "It turns into heat in the pass transistor" is the first half. The interviewer wants the second half: **how much** ($(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}+V_{in}I_Q$), **where it flows** (junction, case, board copper, air), **what limits it** (junction temperature, thermal shutdown), and **what you do about it** (reduce headroom, pre-regulate, spread the heat, derate). This is energy conservation applied to a real component, and the same reasoning applies to any converter's losses ([[q0164]]).
:::

::: why
In LDO designs the *thermal* limit, not the electrical rating, usually sets the maximum current. A part that works at 100 mA on the bench goes into thermal shutdown at 500 mA in an enclosure at 60 °C. Heat also shortens component life and drifts precision parts nearby, and wastes battery energy. Hardware interns are routinely asked "will this LDO survive?", and the answer is a three-line calculation that you should be able to do on a whiteboard.
:::

::: concepts
- **Energy conservation**: all input power is either delivered to the load or dissipated. See [[q0154]].
- **Pass-device dissipation**: $P_D=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}+V_{in}I_Q$. See [[q0279]].
- **Thermal resistance ($\theta$)**: temperature rise per watt. $\Delta T=P\theta$. See [[q0164]].
- **Junction temperature**: $T_J=T_A+\theta_{JA}P_D$, limited by the maximum rating (often 125 or 150 °C).
- **Thermal shutdown**: an on-chip sensor turns the regulator off when $T_J$ exceeds a threshold.
- **Quiescent current**: current the regulator itself uses, which also becomes heat. See [[q0165]].
:::

::: answer
"The lost power turns into heat in the pass transistor. The LDO's input current is basically the output current, so the power wasted is $(V_{in}-V_{out})\times I_{out}$, plus a small $V_{in}\times I_Q$ for the regulator's own quiescent current. For 5 V to 3.3 V at 500 mA that's 0.85 W, about half of the 1.65 W the load receives.

That heat has to get out of the chip. It flows from the junction to the package, to the PCB copper and out to the air. The temperature rise is power times thermal resistance: with 60 °C/W the junction sits 51 °C above ambient. So at 70 °C ambient it's about 121 °C, close to a typical 125 °C rating. If it gets past the limit, the part's thermal shutdown turns it off, then it cools and tries again, so you get an output that pulses or drops out.

To deal with it I'd reduce the headroom, because the power is proportional to $V_{in}-V_{out}$: put a buck in front, tap a lower rail, or even drop some voltage in a series resistor off-chip. Or improve the thermal path with copper area, thermal vias and a package with an exposed pad. Or derate the current. And at some point the answer is that this should be a buck converter."
:::

::: technical
**Power balance.** Input current $I_{in}=I_{out}+I_Q$:
$$P_{in}=V_{in}(I_{out}+I_Q),\quad P_{out}=V_{out}I_{out},\quad P_D=P_{in}-P_{out}=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}+V_{in}I_Q$$
The loss is entirely heat in the pass device and bias circuitry. Nothing is recovered, and there is no inductor or capacitor to store it.

**Fraction lost.** $P_D/P_{out}\approx(V_{in}-V_{out})/V_{out}$. For 5 V to 3.3 V that is 52%.

**Thermal model** (steady state):
$$T_J=T_A+\theta_{JA}P_D,\qquad \theta_{JA}=\theta_{JC}+\theta_{CA}$$
Example: $V_{in}=5$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I_{out}=0.5$ A, $I_Q=50\ \mu$A, $\theta_{JA}=60$ °C/W: $P_D=0.85+0.00025=0.850$ W, $\Delta T=51$ °C, $T_J=121$ °C at $T_A=70$ °C.

**Maximum current from the thermal limit:** $I_{max}=\dfrac{(T_{J,max}-T_A)/\theta_{JA}}{V_{in}-V_{out}}$: 0.54 A in this example.

**Protection.** Thermal shutdown (typically at around 150 to 170 °C junction, with some hysteresis, vendor-specific) disables the pass device. Current limit or foldback limits $I_{out}$ in a short, which reduces $P_D$ if $V_{out}$ collapses (but a *foldback* limit raises the voltage across the device, so the thermal effect depends on implementation).

**Mitigation:** lower $V_{in}-V_{out}$ (pre-regulator or series dropper off-chip), lower $\theta_{JA}$ (copper, vias, exposed pad, airflow), reduce $I_{out}$, or use a switching regulator.
:::

::: short
"It becomes heat in the pass transistor: $(V_{in}-V_{out})\times I_{out}$ plus $V_{in}I_Q$. 5 V to 3.3 V at 500 mA is 0.85 W. That heat flows through the package and PCB, raising the junction by $P\times\theta_{JA}$, 51 °C at 60 °C/W. If it passes the limit, thermal shutdown kicks in. I reduce it by lowering headroom (pre-regulate with a buck, or drop some voltage in a series resistor), improving copper and airflow, or switching to a buck."
:::

::: long
"All the power the LDO doesn't deliver becomes heat, and the cleanest way to see it is to treat the pass transistor as a series resistor: the input current is the output current plus a small quiescent current, so the loss is $(V_{in}-V_{out})\times I_{out}$ plus $V_{in}\times I_Q$.

Take 5 V to 3.3 V at 500 mA. The load gets 1.65 W and the regulator dissipates 1.7 V times 0.5 A, which is 0.85 W, so about 34% of the input power is wasted. Quiescent current of 50 µA adds only 0.25 mW.

Where does the heat go? Out of the junction, through the package, into the PCB copper and the air. The temperature rise is power times thermal resistance. With a θJA of 60 °C/W on a decent board, the junction is 51 °C above ambient: at 70 °C that's 121 °C, right at a typical 125 °C rating. With a small SOT-23 package at 200 °C/W it would be way over. If the die gets too hot, the thermal shutdown trips and the output turns off. It cools, turns on again, and you see the output cycling.

So the design response is in order of preference. Reduce headroom, since the loss scales with it: a buck up front to a few hundred mV above the LDO's needs, or a lower upstream rail. Improve the thermal path: exposed pad, copper pour, vias, airflow. Move some heat off the chip with a series resistor in front, which dissipates part of the same total. Reduce the current. And if none of that works, the answer is to use a switching regulator."
:::

::: terms
- **Dissipated power ($P_D$)**: heat generated in the regulator, $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}+V_{in}I_Q$.
- **Thermal resistance ($\theta_{JA}$)**: junction-to-ambient temperature rise per watt.
- **$\theta_{JC}$**: junction-to-case thermal resistance, used with a heatsink.
- **Junction temperature ($T_J$)**: die temperature, limited by the maximum rating.
- **Thermal shutdown**: protection that disables the pass device above a threshold temperature.
- **Hysteresis**: the temperature drop required before restart.
- **Derating**: reducing allowed power or current as ambient temperature rises.
- **Exposed pad / thermal pad**: package feature that connects the die to PCB copper.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it turns into heat" and "use a heatsink".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Writes $P_D=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}+V_{in}I_Q$ and evaluates it for a real case.
- Converts power to temperature with $\theta_{JA}$ and compares with the rating, including ambient temperature.
- Explains thermal shutdown behavior (cycling) and its consequences for the system.
- Offers a **hierarchy of fixes**: reduce headroom, improve thermal path, share the dissipation, derate, or change topology.
- Notes the heat is also a battery energy cost and a reliability/accuracy issue (nearby precision parts drift).
- Uses the thermal-electrical analogy (power = current, $\theta$ = resistance, $T_A$ = voltage).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using the output power in the formula.** Dissipation depends on the *difference* $V_{in}-V_{out}$, not on $V_{out}$.
- **Forgetting ambient temperature.** $T_J=T_A+\theta P$. A part safe at 25 °C may not be safe at 70 °C.
- **Trusting a datasheet $\theta_{JA}$ blindly.** It is measured on a standard JEDEC board. Your board with less copper is worse, and one with large planes and vias can be better.
- **Believing thermal shutdown is "protection, so I'm fine".** It is a fault behavior. Hitting it in normal operation means the rail drops out and cycles.
- **Ignoring $I_Q$ at high $V_{in}$.** At 24 V and 100 µA, the quiescent term is 2.4 mW: negligible at high load, important at standby.
- **Assuming a bigger heatsink is always the answer.** It helps, but a buck stage is often cheaper than the thermal solution at several watts.
:::

::: deep
## Energy conservation, then thermal path

Input power splits between the load and the heat:
$$V_{in}I_{in}=V_{out}I_{out}+P_D.$$
There is no energy storage in the LDO, so in steady state $P_D$ is exactly what's left. A switching regulator moves most of the voltage difference through an inductor and recovers it; an LDO has no such path. The loss is the *integral* of $\Delta V\cdot I$ over time, and for a load that varies, the *average* current matters for energy, but the *peak* sets the temperature of a small package with short thermal time constant.

Example sweeps, all at $V_{in}=5$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I_Q=50\ \mu$A:

| $I_{out}$ | $P_D$ | $T_J$ rise at 60 °C/W | $T_J$ at $T_A=70$ °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 A | 0.170 W | 10 °C | 80 °C |
| 0.3 A | 0.510 W | 31 °C | 101 °C |
| 0.5 A | 0.850 W | 51 °C | 121 °C |
| 0.7 A | 1.19 W | 71 °C | 141 °C |
| 1.0 A | 1.70 W | 102 °C | 172 °C |

## The thermal circuit

Heat flow obeys the same math as Ohm's law, which is a very useful analogy: power is current, thermal resistance is resistance, temperature difference is voltage.

## Thermal shutdown behavior

If $T_J$ rises above the shutdown threshold (typically on the order of 150 to 170 °C, with 10 to 20 °C hysteresis; vendor-specific), the pass device turns off. The output drops, so the power drops, the die cools with a time constant of milliseconds to seconds, and then the part restarts. Under a constant overload, the result is a periodic output with a duty cycle determined by thermal time constants, which resets the load repeatedly. In a battery system this can show up as brown-out resets with no clear electrical cause.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Thermal shutdown threshold, hysteresis and thermal time constants vary between devices. The 150 to 170 °C and 10 to 20 °C hysteresis are typical ranges, not a rule. Read the electrical characteristics table of the specific part.

## What to do with the heat

**1. Lower the headroom.** The loss is proportional to $V_{in}-V_{out}$. Options:
- Use a lower upstream rail. A 3.6 V LDO input instead of 5 V reduces 0.85 W to 0.15 W at 0.5 A (efficiency from 66% to 92%).
- Insert a buck in front, then a small LDO for cleanup ([[q0280]]).
- Drop part of the voltage in a **series resistor** off-chip. A 1 Ω resistor at 0.5 A drops 0.5 V and dissipates $I^2R=0.25$ W *in the resistor*, leaving the LDO 4.5 V of input and $1.2\times0.5=0.6$ W. The total is still 0.85 W (energy conservation), but 0.25 W is now in a resistor, off the die. It works best for a *constant* load: for a varying load the resistor drop changes with current, and at high current the LDO may drop out.

**2. Improve $\theta_{JA}$.** Use an exposed-pad package, soldered to a copper pour (a few cm² or more) with thermal vias to inner planes, airflow, and keep other hot components away. Going from 100 to 40 °C/W cuts the rise by 60%.

**3. Reduce the load.** Duty-cycle the load, or split it across two LDOs (each gets half of the dissipation, with a common upstream rail).

**4. Change the topology.** If the loss is more than a watt or two, a buck is almost always better: at 12 V to 3.3 V and 1 A the LDO burns 8.7 W, the buck about 0.4 W.

## What about the energy itself?

Heat is not just a temperature problem: it's wasted energy. In a battery-powered product, 0.85 W from a 5 V rail at 66% efficiency shortens runtime by a third relative to a perfect converter. For a battery that runs from 4.2 V to 3.5 V into a 3.3 V LDO at a fixed current, the LDO wastes $(V_{bat}-3.3)/V_{bat}$ of the power: from 21% at 4.2 V to 6% at 3.5 V ([[q0283]]).

::: applications
- **Thermal budgeting** for a board: sum $P_D$ for every LDO, and check each junction temperature at worst-case ambient.
- **LDO selection**: choose a package with the right $\theta_{JA}$ for the dissipation and an exposed pad if above a few hundred mW.
- **Pre-regulation**: buck + LDO designs choose the intermediate voltage to minimise LDO dissipation while keeping PSRR.
- **Dropper resistors** in always-on small loads like LEDs or reference bias rails.
- **Enclosure design**: ventilation and board placement driven by the sum of linear-regulator losses.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Steady state.** Short load pulses with a duration much shorter than the thermal time constant see a lower temperature rise than $P\theta_{JA}$.
- **Constant thermal resistance** and a uniform ambient temperature. In practice $\theta_{JA}$ depends on airflow, board layout and neighbours.
- **Linear model.** Real $\theta_{JA}$ varies somewhat with temperature, and $I_Q$ can rise with temperature or load.
- **$\theta_{JC}$/$\theta_{CA}$ split** of 10/50 °C/W is an illustration, not a datasheet value.
- A **foldback** current limit changes $P_D$ in a short circuit. Behavior depends on the part.
:::

::: followups
### What happens to the heat if the load current is only 1% of the maximum?
The pass-device term $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$ falls to 1% of its peak, so the quiescent term $V_{in}I_Q$ may dominate. For 5 V in and 50 µA, $P_Q=0.25$ mW, versus 1.7 V × 5 mA = 8.5 mW at 5 mA load. Thermally irrelevant, but efficiency at light load is dominated by $I_Q$ ([[q0283]]).

### Is the heat dissipated equally for a 1 A load for 1 ms every second vs a constant 1 mA?
No. Average power is the same only if average current is the same: $1\text{ A}\times1\text{ ms}$ per second averages 1 mA. But the pulse's instantaneous power ($1.7$ W) heats the die for its duration, and the temperature rise depends on the thermal time constant. For a pulse much shorter than the die's time constant (a fraction of a millisecond for the die, longer for the package), the rise is lower than steady state would predict, so check the part's transient thermal impedance curve.

### How would you measure the actual junction temperature?
Not directly: measure the **case/top-of-package** temperature with a thermocouple or IR camera and add $\theta_{JC}P_D$ (or $\Psi_{JT}P_D$, the junction-to-top characterisation parameter), or measure the part's thermal shutdown threshold. Another approach is to read a built-in temperature sensor if the device has one. IR cameras need correct emissivity (matte coating).

### Two LDOs share a 5 V rail, each making 3.3 V at 250 mA. How does that compare to one LDO at 500 mA?
Each dissipates $1.7\times0.25=0.425$ W, versus 0.85 W for one. With separate packages and copper, each junction rises 25.5 °C (at 60 °C/W) instead of 51 °C. The total heat is the same, but it is spread, so the peak temperature falls. It costs one more part and board space, but it can avoid a thermal redesign.

### Scenario: an LDO board works on the bench but resets in a closed plastic enclosure at 55 °C inside. How do you find out whether it is thermal?
Log the output and PG signal over time and see whether resets occur after minutes of heat-up, not immediately. Check the temperature of the LDO package with an IR camera or thermocouple in the enclosure. Calculate $T_J=T_A+\theta_{JA}P_D$ with $T_A$ = measured enclosure temperature, not room temperature. Then confirm by cooling the part with a fan (resets stop) or by lowering $V_{in}$ temporarily (if the reset disappears, thermal shutdown was the cause).
:::
