::: testing
Whether you understand that a SoM is a **board-level product that contains a SoC**, not an alternative kind of chip, and whether you can weigh module versus chip-down design on facts. The interviewer wants:

1. A correct definition: a small PCB with SoC, DRAM, flash, power management and a board-to-board connector, sold as a unit.
2. The benefits: faster time to market, less high-speed layout risk, pre-validated hardware and software, easier certification, longer-lifecycle support.
3. The costs: higher unit price, fixed form factor and pinout, less optimisation, dependency on one vendor.
4. A volume-based decision with a break-even calculation, and the SoM vs SiP vs COM distinction.
:::

::: why
A large share of embedded products ship on modules, because getting DDR routing, power sequencing and a Linux board-support package right is expensive and risky. A hardware engineer must know when a module is the right call and how to design the carrier board around one. The same make-versus-buy logic as [[q0929]] applies, one level up from the silicon.
:::

::: concepts
- **SoC**: the chip itself. See [[q0926]].
- **SoM / COM**: a module containing the SoC and essential support (DRAM, flash, PMIC) on its own PCB. See [[q0930]].
- **Carrier board**: the product-specific board that hosts the module and provides connectors and application circuits.
- **Chip-down design**: placing the SoC and its memory directly on the product PCB.
- **NRE vs unit cost, break-even volume**: the decision tool. See [[q0929]].
- **Standard form factors**: SMARC, Qseven, COM Express and similar define module size and pinout.
- **Certification and software support**: pre-certified radios, board-support packages.
:::

::: answer
"A system on module is a small board that packages a SoC together with the parts that are hardest to design around it: the DRAM, boot flash, the power-management chip, and often wireless. It plugs into your own carrier board through a connector or castellated edges. So the SoC is the silicon, and the SoM is a ready-made mini computer built around that silicon.

The advantages are speed and risk. The module vendor has already done the high-speed DDR layout, the power sequencing, the signal-integrity validation and the Linux or Android board-support package, so you design just the carrier, which is often a simple four-layer board with your connectors and sensors. It can reduce time to market by months, and some modules are pre-certified for wireless regulations.

The cost is that you pay for that integration on every unit, and you're locked into the module's form factor, pinout and vendor. At high volume, the per-unit premium adds up and a chip-down design with the same SoC will be cheaper, smaller, and optimised for your product.

So I'd use a SoM for low to medium volume, tight schedules or when the team lacks high-speed layout experience, and do chip-down once volume justifies the extra NRE. A break-even calculation tells you where that is."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** A SoM (system on module) or computer-on-module (COM) is a small PCB integrating a SoC plus its minimum support: DRAM, non-volatile boot storage, power management and often Ethernet PHY or wireless. It exposes the SoC's interfaces (USB, PCIe, MIPI, GPIO, and so on) on a board-to-board connector or castellated pads. A **carrier board** supplies connectors, power input, and application circuits.

**SoC versus SoM.**
| Aspect | SoC (chip-down) | SoM |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Silicon die/package | A board containing a SoC and support parts |
| Unit cost | Lowest at volume | Higher (module vendor margin, extra board) |
| NRE | High (DDR layout, bring-up, BSP) | Low (carrier only) |
| PCB complexity | High-density, controlled impedance, 8+ layers common for DDR | Carrier often simple (4 layers) |
| Time to market | Longer | Shorter, months saved |
| Size | Smallest | Larger due to connector and two boards |
| Flexibility | Full | Limited to module's pinout and options |
| Software | You port the BSP | Vendor BSP and support |
| Lifecycle | Chip vendor's longevity | Module vendor's longevity plus chip's |

**Break-even (illustrative).** SoM: NRE 60 kUSD, unit cost 38 USD (30 module + 8 carrier). Chip-down: NRE 250 kUSD, unit cost 24 USD. $V^*=(250{,}000-60{,}000)/(38-24)\approx13{,}600$ units. Below that the SoM is cheaper overall; above it, chip-down wins.

**Related packaging terms.** A SiP integrates several dies in one package; a SoM integrates several packages on a small board.
:::

::: short
"A SoM is a small board containing a SoC plus DRAM, flash and power management, plugged into your carrier board. The SoC is the silicon; the SoM is a ready-made mini-system around it. A SoM cuts time to market and layout risk, since DDR routing, sequencing and the software support are done, but costs more per unit and locks you into its form factor. Chip-down with the bare SoC wins at high volume."
:::

::: long
"They sit at different levels: a SoC is a chip, a system on module is a small board built around a SoC.

A SoM carries the SoC, the DRAM, boot flash or eMMC, a PMIC and often a radio or Ethernet PHY, and exposes the interfaces on a connector. You then design a carrier board that gives it power, connectors for USB, Ethernet or display, and your application circuits.

The benefits all come from not designing the hard part. DDR routing needs controlled impedance, length matching and a good stackup, usually six to ten layers. Power sequencing and decoupling for the SoC are tricky. And then there's bring-up of the bootloader and Linux board-support package. A module vendor has done and validated all of that, so you design a simple carrier, often four layers, and your schedule shrinks by months. Wireless modules may even be pre-certified, which saves EMC and regulatory cost. You also get a supported long-term product.

The trade-offs: unit cost is higher, because you pay for the vendor's margin and an extra PCB and connector. The product is bigger and thicker. You're stuck with the module's pinout and options, so if you need a different memory size or a missing interface you can't change it. And you depend on one vendor for supply and software.

The decision comes down to volume. With, say, 60 thousand NRE and 38 dollars per unit for the SoM versus 250 thousand and 24 dollars for chip-down, the crossover is around 13 thousand units. Below that use the SoM, above it go chip-down, and many products start on a module to prove the market and move to chip-down once volumes grow."
:::

::: terms
- **SoM / COM**: system-on-module / computer-on-module; board with SoC plus support hardware.
- **Carrier board**: application-specific board hosting the module.
- **Chip-down**: SoC and parts placed directly on the product PCB.
- **BSP**: board-support package; bootloader, kernel and drivers for the hardware.
- **SMARC, Qseven, COM Express**: standardised module form factors and pinouts.
- **Castellated edge**: half-plated holes letting a module be soldered onto a carrier.
- **SiP**: system in package; several dies in one package.
- **Pre-certified module**: a module with regulatory approval (for example for a radio) that reduces product testing.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a SoM is a SoC on a small board, easier but more expensive".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Places SoC, SiP, SoM and carrier on a **hierarchy** (die, package, module, product).
- Lists what the module hides (DDR layout, power, BSP, certification) and why those are the risks.
- Does a **break-even** calculation including NRE and schedule value.
- Names the downsides: size, connector reliability, vendor lock-in, thermal path through the module, EOL risk.
- Mentions the migration path: prototype on a module, move to chip-down using the vendor's reference design.
- Notes standards (SMARC, COM Express) that allow module swapping.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating a SoM as a type of chip.** It is a PCB assembly containing a SoC.
- **Assuming a SoM is always cheaper.** Only at low volume or when schedule value is high.
- **Ignoring the connector.** Board-to-board connectors affect height, vibration reliability and signal integrity for fast links.
- **Forgetting thermal design.** The SoC's heat must pass through the module to a heat sink or the carrier.
- **Ignoring vendor lifecycle.** The module vendor's support and end-of-life policy are a dependency.
- **Assuming the carrier is trivial.** It still needs correct power input, high-speed pair routing (USB, Ethernet, PCIe) and ESD protection.
:::

::: deep
## The levels of integration

Moving up from silicon, each level packages the previous one:

1. **Die**: the silicon, a SoC ([[q0926]]).
2. **Package**: the die in a BGA (possibly with stacked DRAM in a package-on-package or several dies in a SiP).
3. **Module (SoM/COM)**: several packages (SoC, DRAM, flash, PMIC) on a small PCB with a connector.
4. **Board/product**: the carrier board with connectors and application circuits plus the enclosure.

## What the module saves you

**DDR layout.** A 32-bit LPDDR interface requires length-matched byte lanes, controlled impedance (commonly 40 to 50 Ω single-ended), tight routing rules, and usually a six- to ten-layer HDI stackup with the DRAM placed within a centimetre or two of the SoC. A mistake means failed training or intermittent crashes found only on bring-up. The module vendor has validated this across voltage, temperature and part lots.

**Power and bring-up.** Sequencing, decoupling and PMIC programming are validated, and the vendor ships a bootloader and Linux (or other OS) support that matches the exact DRAM and flash on the module. Porting a BSP to a custom board can take months.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Schedule savings of "months" and layer counts of 6 to 10 for DDR boards are typical rather than universal; they depend on DRAM type, data rate, SoC family and the team's experience.

**Certification.** Modules with integrated radios are often pre-certified for regional regulations (for example FCC/CE modular approvals), which can reduce product-level radio testing; you must still test the finished product for unintentional emissions and follow the module's integration rules. Check the vendor's documentation for scope and conditions.

## What the module costs you

- **Unit cost**: the module price includes the vendor's design, test, logistics, support and margin plus the extra PCB and connector.
- **Size and height**: two boards and a connector stack. Typical connector stack heights are several millimetres, a problem for thin products.
- **Signal integrity**: high-speed signals cross a connector, which adds discontinuity; PCIe or USB 3 may be limited by the connector rating.
- **Fixed pin map**: peripherals the SoC supports might not be on the connector. You can't swap DRAM size, add a second I2C or change boot storage.
- **Thermal**: the heat has to leave through a module whose design you don't control; a heat spreader is usually specified.
- **Vendor dependence**: supply, lead times, lifecycle (the module may go end-of-life while the SoC is still sold).

## Break-even analysis

Same structure as [[q0929]]: total cost $=NRE+V\cdot c_u$.

| Option | NRE | Unit cost |
|---|---|---|
| SoM + custom carrier | 60,000 USD (carrier design, bring-up) | 38 USD (module 30 + carrier 8) |
| Chip-down on custom board | 250,000 USD (high-speed layout, bring-up, BSP port, extra spins) | 24 USD (SoC, DRAM, flash, PMIC 17 + larger HDI board and assembly 7) |

$$V^*=\frac{250{,}000-60{,}000}{38-24}=13{,}571\text{ units}$$

Two extra items shift the answer. First, **schedule value**: if the SoM gets you to market 6 months earlier, the revenue of those months should be credited to it. Second, **risk**: chip-down NRE has uncertainty (an extra board spin could add 50 to 100 kUSD), so the expected NRE is higher than the nominal estimate.

## Migration path

A common strategy: prototype and launch version 1 on a SoM, validate the market and software, then do a chip-down version 2 when volumes grow, reusing the vendor's reference schematic for the SoC section. Choose a SoM whose SoC has a published reference design and an available chip supply so that migration is possible. Standard form factors (SMARC, Qseven, COM Express) also allow you to change module vendor without redesigning the carrier, at the price of constraining the pinout to the standard.

## SoM versus SiP versus module-like packages

- **SiP** (system in package): several dies in one package, soldered like a chip. It is smaller, but the package itself is a custom item; the integration is at silicon-packaging level ([[q0932]]).
- **SoM**: several packaged chips on a small PCB with a connector or castellations. Larger, but built with standard components and processes.
- **Package-on-package (PoP)**: DRAM package stacked on the SoC package, common in phones; here the DRAM-to-SoC connection never touches the PCB.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A strong closing line: "I'd start on a module to get the product and software running, and decide on chip-down once I can show the volume and the NRE justify it."
:::

::: applications
- **Single-board computers and compute modules**: Raspberry Pi Compute Module-style parts that plug into a customer carrier.
- **Edge AI products**: NVIDIA Jetson modules are sold as modules mounted on carriers.
- **Industrial HMI and gateways**: SoMs with i.MX, Sitara or similar SoCs on custom carriers.
- **Medical and instrumentation devices**: low volume, high certification burden; modules reduce schedule and risk.
- **Radio modules**: castellated Wi-Fi/BLE modules with integrated SoC and antenna.
- **Prototyping before ASIC or chip-down**: validate the software and market on a SoM.
:::

::: assumptions
- Costs are illustrative. Real quotes, volumes, support costs and team rates change the break-even by large factors.
- The module is assumed to contain the same SoC you would use chip-down; some modules offer variants with different memory sizes.
- The comparison assumes the SoM meets size and thermal limits; if it does not, chip-down is forced regardless of cost.
- Certification benefits apply to pre-certified radio modules; not every SoM is pre-certified.
- "SoM" and "COM" are used loosely; some vendors use "SoM" for smaller castellated modules and "COM" for standardised connectors.
:::

::: followups
### Is a SoM the same as a SiP?
No. A SiP puts multiple dies into a single package that is soldered like a chip. A SoM is a small PCB with several packaged devices plus a connector. A SiP is smaller and made in a packaging process; a SoM is larger and uses standard board assembly. Some modules even contain a SiP.

### When would you choose chip-down instead of a SoM?
When volume is high enough that the per-unit saving repays the extra NRE, when size, height or thermal needs rule out a module, when you need a different memory size or pinout, or when you need control of long-term supply and cost. Having a team with DDR and BSP experience also tips the balance.

### What are the main design risks of the carrier board?
Power input and sequencing with respect to the module's requirements, ESD and protection on external ports, high-speed pair routing (USB, Ethernet, PCIe) through the connector, mechanical fit and connector stress, and thermal design. The module datasheet and carrier design guide define pinout notes, voltage levels and layout constraints.

### How do you reduce vendor lock-in with a SoM?
Pick a module in a standard form factor such as SMARC, or one from a vendor with a long product-lifecycle commitment and multiple variants. Use a mainstream SoC with upstream Linux support so the software is portable, and keep the carrier design modular so a different module can be dropped in. Plan for a last-time-buy or chip-down migration as the fallback.

### Your SoM-based product will ship 500,000 units over its life. What do you do?
Do a break-even analysis with real quotes. With a 14 USD per-unit premium, 500,000 units is 7 MUSD of extra cost against perhaps 190 kUSD of extra NRE for chip-down, so chip-down is strongly favoured even after allowing for risk. I'd prototype and launch on the module if time matters, and plan a chip-down revision.
:::
