::: testing
Whether you can explain **shoot-through (cross-conduction)** in a PFET/NFET leg, estimate its severity and name prevention at more than one layer. The interviewer wants more than "add dead time":

1. The consequence: a near-short from the supply to ground through two on-resistances, limited by the FETs' saturation current and the loop, with big current spikes, heating, rail droop and EMI.
2. Why it happens even when the controller is "correct": the gates turn off more slowly than they turn on, thresholds and delays vary, and fast switch-node edges can turn a FET on through the Miller capacitance.
3. A layered prevention list: dead time, interlocks or adaptive drivers, asymmetric gate drive, stiff pull-downs, current limit.
:::

::: why
Shoot-through is the most common way a hand-built half-bridge dies. It also shows up as an efficiency problem that is not obvious: a bridge that gets warm with no load, a supply that sags at every switching edge, an EMI failure at harmonics of the switching frequency. The same effect exists in every CMOS inverter ([[q0109]]), so interviewers like this question because it connects power electronics with digital CMOS. The buck-converter version is [[q0207]]; this page keeps the PFET-high, NFET-low leg of a motor bridge ([[q0420]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Complementary leg**: a PFET high side and an NFET low side with a shared output node ([[q0107]]).
- **CMOS inverter overlap**: with tied gates both devices conduct while $V_{th,n}<V_G<V_{DD}-|V_{th,p}|$ ([[q0109]]).
- **Saturation current limits the short**: with large $V_{DS}$, current is set by $g_m$ and $V_{GS}$, not by $R_{DS(on)}$.
- **Gate charge and turn-off delay**: gates take tens of ns to charge or discharge ([[q0431]]).
- **Miller turn-on**: switch-node dv/dt couples through $C_{gd}$ into the off FET's gate ([[q0207]]).
- **Dead time**: both gates off for a deliberate interval ([[q0421]]).
:::

::: answer
"If both devices in the same leg are on, you've connected the supply directly to ground through the PFET and NFET. The only things limiting the current are their resistance and the inductance of the loop, so you get a big current spike. Ideally it'd be the supply over two on-resistances, which for a 24 volt bridge and 20 milliohm FETs is hundreds of amps, though in practice the FETs go into saturation and it's lower, but still tens of amps. The result is a lot of wasted power, a sagging supply, heating, noise, and often a dead FET.

It happens in a few ways. If the gates are tied together, like a CMOS inverter, there's a range of gate voltage where both are on. If they're driven separately, one FET turns off slower than the other turns on, because of gate charge and delays. And there's the nasty one: when one FET turns on, the switch node jumps, and that dv/dt couples through the Miller capacitance into the other FET's gate and can turn it on briefly.

To prevent it: separate gate drives, dead time so both are off for tens to hundreds of nanoseconds between transitions, ideally from a driver with built-in shoot-through protection, plus faster turn-off than turn-on with a diode across the gate resistor, a stiff pull-down on each gate, and a current limit or fuse as a backstop."
:::

::: technical
**Magnitude.** With both on and $V_{DS}\approx V_M/2$ each, the current is set by saturation: $I\approx\tfrac12k(V_{GS}-V_{th})^2$ for each device, limited to whichever is smaller, further limited by loop inductance and the supply impedance. The ideal resistive limit is $V_M/(R_P+R_N)$, for $24$ V and $2\times20$ mΩ that is $600$ A (not reached in practice).

**Loss per event.** $E\approx k\,V_M\,I_{pk}\,t_{ov}$ with $k\approx0.5$ for a triangular pulse. Illustrative: 24 V, 100 A peak, 50 ns: $60\ \mu$J; at 20 kHz $\to1.2$ W of extra heating, in a bridge that otherwise dissipates a few watts ([[q0207]]).

**Tied-gate PFET/NFET (CMOS-style).** Both conduct for $V_{th,n}<V_G<V_M-|V_{th,p}|$. For $V_M=12$ V and 2.5 V thresholds: window of 7 V. A gate edge slewing at 1 V/µs spends 7 µs in the window, a very long overlap; each edge is a short-circuit event. Tying gates together is acceptable in a logic inverter (small currents) but not in a power stage.

**Separately driven legs.** Required non-overlap: $t_{dead}>(t_{d(off),1}+t_{f,1})-t_{d(on),2}+t_{skew}$ over the PVT range. Typical: 50 to 200 ns for small bridges, fixed or programmable.

**Miller-induced turn-on.** When the NFET turns on, $dV_{SW}/dt$ injects $C_{gd}dv/dt$ into the PFET's gate. For $C_{gd}=100$ pF and 2 V/ns: 0.2 A. Through a pull-up impedance $R$ the gate moves by $\Delta V\approx R\,C_{gd}\,dv/dt$, bounded by the capacitive divider $V_M C_{gd}/(C_{gd}+C_{gs})$. If that exceeds the threshold, the FET turns on briefly.

**Prevention layers.**
1. Controller: complementary PWM with a dead-time generator (timer peripherals have a dead-time register).
2. Driver IC: shoot-through protection (interlock, adaptive dead time), or an input logic that forbids both on.
3. Discrete: asymmetric gate network (slow on, fast off), gate-source pull resistors, break-before-make logic.
4. Miller: stiff pull-down, negative turn-off voltage, separate on/off resistors, controlled dv/dt.
5. Backstop: current-limit or desaturation protection, a fuse or an electronic fuse on the bridge supply.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The peak shoot-through current depends strongly on the FETs, gate drive and loop impedance. The 100 A and 50 ns figures are illustrative orders of magnitude, not datasheet values.
:::

::: short
"Turning on the PFET and NFET in the same leg shorts the supply to ground through two on-resistances, so you get big current spikes, heat, rail droop and often a dead FET. It happens with overlapping gate edges, tied gates, mismatched turn-off and turn-on delays, or Miller-coupled dv/dt. I prevent it with separate gate drives and dead time, a driver with built-in shoot-through protection, faster turn-off than turn-on, stiff gate pull-downs, and current limiting as a backstop."
:::

::: long
"Both devices on means a direct path from the supply to ground through the PFET and the NFET. In the ideal resistive picture the current would be the supply voltage over the two on-resistances, 600 amps for a 24 volt bridge with 20 milliohm parts. In reality the FETs are in saturation with a large drain-source voltage, so the current is limited to what the gate drive and transconductance allow, maybe tens or a hundred amps, and by the loop inductance, but it's still a huge spike. Even a few nanoseconds of it, maybe 100 amps for 50 nanoseconds at 24 volts, is about 60 microjoules, and at 20 kilohertz that's over a watt of heat, plus a drooping supply, ringing and EMI. A longer overlap just kills the FETs.

Why does it happen? First, if the gates are tied together as in a CMOS inverter, there's a window of gate voltage where both are on. With 12 volts and 2.5 volt thresholds that window is 7 volts wide, so a slow edge spends microseconds there. Second, if I drive the gates separately but the controller switches them at the same moment, the turn-off of one FET takes longer than the turn-on of the other because of gate charge and propagation delays. Third, and subtler, when the NFET turns on the switch node jumps up fast, and that dv/dt couples through the PFET's gate-drain capacitance and can turn it on briefly even though its driver says off.

Prevention is layered. The controller gives complementary signals with dead time, so both gates are off for 50 to 200 nanoseconds between transitions. A gate-driver IC adds its own interlock or adaptive dead time. I make turn-off faster than turn-on with a diode across the gate resistor, hold each gate firmly with a low-impedance pull-down, and keep the loop inductance low. Finally, as a backstop, I'd have current limiting or a fuse. The cost of dead time is the body diode conducting for a moment, which is a few milliwatts."
:::

::: terms
- **Shoot-through (cross-conduction)**: both devices in a leg conduct, shorting the supply.
- **Dead time ($t_d$)**: a deliberate interval with both devices off.
- **Interlock**: logic that prevents one gate from being turned on until the other is confirmed off.
- **Adaptive dead time**: dead time set by sensing gate or switch-node voltage rather than a fixed delay.
- **Miller (dv/dt) turn-on**: false turn-on by charge injected through $C_{gd}$.
- **Break-before-make**: opening one switch before closing the other.
- **Propagation-delay skew**: difference in delay between the two gate paths.
- **Desaturation protection**: detecting an over-current $V_{DS}$ and shutting the FET off.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it shorts the supply; use dead time".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Estimates the current honestly (saturation-limited, not $V/2R$) and the energy per event and the average loss.
- Names several causes: tied gates, unequal turn-on/turn-off delays, PVT variation, and Miller dv/dt turn-on.
- Gives a dead-time budget formula and mentions its cost (body-diode loss).
- Lists prevention at four layers: controller, driver, gate network, protection.
- Notes the CMOS inverter analogy and why a power stage can't tolerate what a logic gate does.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Quoting $V/(2R_{DS(on)})$ as the real current.** FETs saturate; the current is lower, but still large, and limited by the loop.
- **Believing "complementary PWM" is safe.** Complementary signals with no dead time overlap during every transition.
- **Setting dead time from the typical datasheet delay.** Use worst-case delays over temperature and process.
- **Ignoring Miller turn-on** because the gate signal is "clean". The false turn-on comes from the switch node, not the driver.
- **Too much dead time.** It increases diode conduction loss, distortion in the output voltage and, for motor control, current distortion.
:::

How big? Suppose each FET has $R_{DS(on)}=20$ mΩ at full gate drive, $V_M=24$ V. In the *ideal resistive* limit $I=24/0.04=600$ A. The FETs can't actually do that: each device is in saturation with $V_{DS}\approx12$ V, so the current is set by the saturation (pulsed) rating, which for a power FET driven at $V_{GS}\approx10$ V might be a few tens to a few hundred amps. Either way, the supply droops and the FETs heat. If the overlap lasts $t_{ov}$ with current rising roughly linearly, $E\approx\tfrac12V_MI_{pk}t_{ov}$. For $I_{pk}=100$ A and $t_{ov}=50$ ns: $60$ µJ and at 20 kHz $1.2$ W; for $t_{ov}=1$ µs it is 1.2 mJ per event, 24 W, and probably a failed FET.

## Three ways both devices end up on

### 1. Tied gates (the CMOS inverter way)

If the gates share one drive, the leg is a CMOS inverter. As $V_G$ rises from 0 to $V_M$:

- PFET on while $V_G<V_M-|V_{th,p}|$.
- NFET on while $V_G>V_{th,n}$.
- **Both** on for $V_{th,n}<V_G<V_M-|V_{th,p}|$.

Figure: Between 2.5 V and 9.5 V of gate voltage both FETs conduct. A gate driven at 1 V/µs spends 7 µs in that window. This is fine in a logic gate that can only pass milliamps, and a disaster in a power stage ([[q0109]]).

Power legs therefore always get **separate gate drives** with enforced non-overlap.

Figure: Dead time: both devices are off for a slot at each transition. During each gap the load current flows through one body diode, chosen by the direction of the load current (for current flowing out of the node into the load, the NFET's diode in both gaps). There is no overlap and no shoot-through current.

**Dead-time budget.** The gap must cover the slowest turn-off and the fastest turn-on:
$$t_{dead}\ge(t_{d(off),A}+t_{f,A})-t_{d(on),B}+t_{skew,\,PVT}.$$
With $t_{d(off)}=80$ ns and $t_f=40$ ns on one device, $t_{d(on)}=20$ ns on the other and a 30 ns margin: $t_{dead}\ge150$ ns. The cost is $P_{dt}=V_F I\,(2t_{dead})f_{sw}=0.7\times5\times300\text{ ns}\times20\text{ kHz}=21$ mW.

### 3. Miller (dv/dt) turn-on

When $Q_N$ turns on, $V_{SW}$ falls with a slew $dv/dt$ and injects $C_{gd}\,dv/dt$ into $Q_P$'s gate network. With $C_{gd}=100$ pF and 2 V/ns, 0.2 A flows; into a gate pull-up resistor $R=5$ Ω that is 1 V, and with $C_{gs}$ the divider limit is $V_M\,C_{gd}/(C_{gd}+C_{gs})=24\times0.09=2.2$ V. If this exceeds the threshold, which also drops at high temperature, the "off" FET conducts briefly.

Fixes: stiffer pull-down (lower $R_{off}$), negative turn-off bias, slower dv/dt (larger turn-on $R_g$ on the switching device), and a FET with a low $C_{gd}/C_{gs}$ ratio.

## Gate-driver protection and the other layers

- **Controller peripherals** (advanced timers) generate complementary PWM with a dead-time register ([[q0145]]).
- **Driver ICs** often enforce non-overlap in hardware: an interlock (both inputs high is rejected) and fixed or adaptive dead time.
- **Adaptive dead time** senses the gate or switch-node voltage and releases the second device only when the first is off, so the dead time shortens when the FETs are fast and lengthens when they are slow.
- **Current protection**: a sense resistor in the supply return, desaturation detection, or a hot-swap/e-fuse on the bridge supply. These do not prevent shoot-through but stop it from becoming a failure.

## Diagnosing it on a real board

A bridge that gets hot with no load, a supply current that is higher than expected, or a drain-source waveform with a spike in the middle of each transition suggests shoot-through. Measure the supply current with a current probe at the bridge (not at the supply, where capacitors hide the pulse), probe both gate-source voltages at the same time against the switch node, and look for overlap of the on-regions. Increase the dead time until the extra supply current disappears, then verify across temperature and with the bridge at both current polarities (the switch node dv/dt, and so Miller turn-on, changes with load current direction).

::: applications
- **Half-bridge and H-bridge gate drivers** with built-in dead time and shoot-through protection.
- **Synchronous buck converters** (the same issue; see [[q0207]]).
- **Class-D audio output stages**: dead time causes crossover distortion, a known design trade-off.
- **Three-phase inverters**: dead-time compensation in the motor controller.
- **CMOS I/O drivers**: designed so the overlap time and crowbar current are small ([[q0109]]).
- **Power-supply input stages and totem-pole PFC**: fast devices and Miller immunity are critical.
:::

::: followups
### How does dead time affect a PWM motor drive?
It adds a gap during which the current flows through the body diode, so the output voltage deviates from the command in proportion to $t_{dead}f_{sw}$ and the sign of the current. It distorts low-speed control and audio. Drives compensate in firmware or minimize dead time with fast, well-matched drivers and adaptive timing.

### What's the Miller effect problem and how would you check it?
The off FET's gate is pulled up by $C_{gd}\,dv/dt$ from the switch node. Probe its gate-source voltage right at the pins while the other FET turns on: any spike approaching the threshold means risk. Fix with a stiffer pull-down, negative bias or slower edges.

### What if the dead time is long enough that it no longer protects at high temperature?
Delays and thresholds vary with temperature; a margin set at 25 °C may be too small at 125 °C. Size the dead time for the worst case or use adaptive dead time, then confirm with a supply-current measurement across temperature.

### Why are two PFETs or two NFETs in a leg safer or riskier than a PFET/NFET pair?
An NFET/NFET leg needs a floating gate drive for the high side but lets the drive be matched and fast, so dead time can be shorter. The PFET/NFET leg is simpler to drive but has mismatched delays and larger Miller capacitance on the PFET. The risk of shoot-through is similar; what changes is how well you can control the timing.

### The bridge fails only when the motor is under heavy load. What could be happening?
Large load current changes the commutation: with a large current the switch-node dv/dt is faster (the body diode takes over sooner) and the Miller coupling is stronger, or the ground bounce from $L\,di/dt$ shifts the gate threshold. Check the gate waveforms under load, increase dead time, slow the dv/dt, or add negative turn-off bias.
:::
