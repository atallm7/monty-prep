::: basics
### In plain words
The silkscreen, also called the legend, is the layer of ink printed on top of a board, usually white. It carries the human-readable labels: part names like R1 and U3, outlines, polarity marks, pin-1 dots, test point names and the board revision. It exists so that the people who build, probe and repair the board can find and orient every part.

### Picture it
Think of the street names and landmarks on a map. The roads exist without them, but nobody could find their way. The silkscreen labels a crowded board the same way. A map is useless if the labels sit under a building or cover the road. In the same way, silkscreen text hidden under a part, or ink spilled on a solder pad, causes mistakes and soldering problems.

### Words you need
- **Reference designator**: the label tying a part to the schematic, such as R1 or C5.
- **Polarity mark**: a symbol showing which way a part must face, like a diode bar or a "+".
- **Pin 1**: the dot or mark showing where an IC's first pin is.
- **Legend / nomenclature**: other names for the silkscreen layer ([[q0466]]).
- **Soldermask**: the coating the ink is printed on, never on pads ([[q0473]]).

### The simplest example
Picture a board with one LED and a resistor named R1. The silkscreen draws the resistor's outline, prints "R1" beside it (not under it), and puts a mark next to the LED's cathode side. An assembler can now place and orient both parts without the schematic. A common rule of thumb is text at least 1 mm tall with strokes at least 0.15 mm wide, which is 15% of the height, and ink kept about 0.15 to 0.2 mm away from pads. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers how the legend is printed, what belongs on it, and the design rules.
:::

::: testing
A quick vocabulary check with a practical follow-up. The answer is **silkscreen**, also called **legend** or **nomenclature** (and "overlay" in some CAD tools), but the interviewer is really checking whether you know what goes on it (reference designators, polarity and pin-1 marks, outlines, test points, logos, revision), how it is made and why the **design rules** matter: keep ink off pads, keep text legible, and match the legend colour to the mask. A candidate who has actually assembled or debugged boards will mention polarity and pin-1 markings.
:::

::: why
The silkscreen is the interface between the designer and everyone who touches the board: the assembler who must orient a diode, the technician who wants to probe test point TP3, the rework engineer who needs to find U7 under a heat gun. A good legend saves hours; a bad one (text under components, ink on pads, no polarity marks) causes mistakes and, occasionally, soldering defects. It is also the cheapest layer to get right and the one most often forgotten until the first prototypes arrive.
:::

::: concepts
- **Silkscreen / legend / nomenclature**: the printed ink layer on the outside of the board, above the soldermask ([[q0466]]).
- **Reference designators**: R1, C5, U3 that tie each part to the schematic and BOM.
- **Polarity and orientation marks**: pin-1 dots, diode bars, "+" signs.
- **Soldermask relationship**: ink is printed on the mask, never on pads ([[q0473]]).
- **Contrast**: legend colour is chosen against the mask colour ([[q0471]]).
- **Fabrication vs assembly drawings**: information that does not fit on the board goes into documents, not onto the silkscreen.
:::

::: technical
**Names.** Silkscreen, legend, nomenclature, overlay (CAD "top overlay"/"top silk").

**Material and process.** Epoxy-based (thermally or UV-cured) ink, usually white, 15 to 25 µm thick, applied after soldermask and surface finish. Methods: screen printing through a mesh (older, coarser), and direct legend printing (DLP, inkjet) with higher resolution, a common option now. The legend sits on top of the mask; it must not cover exposed copper pads.

**Typical design limits (fab-specific).** Minimum line width about 0.15 mm (6 mil); minimum text height about 0.8 to 1.0 mm for screen printing, smaller for inkjet; clearance from pads about 0.15 to 0.2 mm; ink is clipped from pads and holes by the fab's CAM. Aspect: line width/height of at least about 15% for legibility.

**Content checklist.** Refdes of every part; pin-1 mark for ICs and connectors; polarity for diodes, electrolytic capacitors, LEDs; board name, revision, date; connector pin labels; test points; mounting-hole numbers; logos; warnings and ratings (high voltage).

**Colour pairing.** White legend on green, red, blue and black masks; black legend on white or yellow masks.
:::

::: long
"The layer with text is called the silkscreen, or legend, or nomenclature. It's an ink layer, usually white epoxy ink, printed on top of the soldermask. The name comes from the old process of pushing ink through a mesh screen; today a lot of fabs use direct inkjet printing, which gives finer detail.

What goes on it is everything a human needs: reference designators like R1 or U3 that match the schematic and BOM, outlines showing where each part sits, polarity marks for diodes, electrolytic capacitors and LEDs, pin-1 dots for ICs and connectors, test point labels, connector pin names, the board name, revision and date, logos, and any warnings, like high voltage.

There are design rules. The ink must stay off the pads and the holes: ink on a pad stops solder wetting, and the fab's CAM software clips the silk from exposed copper anyway, so text that overlaps a pad gets cut and becomes unreadable. Text needs a minimum size, typically a line width of about 0.15 millimetres and a height of about a millimetre for screen printing, but check the fab's capability. I'd keep the text in one or two orientations, put it where it won't be covered by a component, and avoid putting it over vias. Colour has to contrast with the mask: white on green, red, blue or black, and black on white or yellow.

And I'd put the silkscreen to use: a good legend with polarity marks and test points makes assembly, test and rework much faster and safer. Information that doesn't fit goes into the assembly drawing, not onto the board."
:::

::: terms
- **Silkscreen**: the printed ink legend layer on the board's outer surfaces.
- **Legend / nomenclature**: other names for the same layer.
- **Reference designator (refdes)**: the unique label of a part (R1, C2, U3).
- **Pin-1 marker**: a dot, notch or triangle indicating pin 1 of a package.
- **Direct legend printing (DLP)**: inkjet printing of the legend directly onto the board.
- **CAM**: the fabricator's computer-aided manufacturing step that checks and modifies the data (for example clipping silk off pads).
- **Assembly drawing**: a document showing component positions and notes for assembly.
- **Fabrication drawing**: a document giving board outline, stack-up, drill chart and notes for the fab.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "silkscreen" and "it has the labels".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the **names** (silkscreen, legend, nomenclature) and **what it's made of and how** (epoxy ink on top of mask, screen versus inkjet).
- Lists **content**, especially **polarity and pin-1 marks** and **test points**.
- States **design rules**: off pads and vias, minimum line width and text height, clearance, orientation, contrast with mask colour.
- Explains **consequences**: ink on pads blocks soldering and gets clipped, hidden text slows rework.
- Mentions **assembly and fabrication drawings** for what does not fit.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting text on pads or vias.** Ink contaminates pads or fills via holes; the fab removes it, cutting your text.
- **Text too small.** Below the fab's minimum, characters fill in or blur.
- **No polarity or pin-1 marks.** The assembler guesses, and parts go on backwards.
- **Colour clash.** White legend on a white or yellow mask is unreadable.
- **Burying the legend under components.** Refdes hidden by the part cannot be read after assembly, so place text outside the body or provide an assembly drawing.
- **Treating silk as precise.** Registration of silkscreen is looser than copper (a few tenths of a millimetre), so do not rely on it for alignment.
:::

The silkscreen is applied after the soldermask and the surface finish (see the build sequence in [[q0466]]). It is a decorative-looking but functional layer in the same family as the mask: a cured polymer, 15 to 25 µm thick, printed over the mask only. On a two-sided board there is a top and a bottom legend.

## How it is made

- **Screen printing**: epoxy ink pushed through a fine polyester or steel mesh with a patterned stencil, then thermally cured. Typical minimum line width 0.15 mm or a bit more, with registration tolerances of a few tenths of a millimetre.
- **Direct legend printing (inkjet)**: the board is printed directly from the data with UV-curable ink. No screens, finer detail (line widths down to about 0.1 mm in some processes), and tighter registration. It has become common for fine-pitch boards and short runs.
- **Laser marking**: used for serial numbers and 2D codes, and on some special boards.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Minimum line width, minimum text height, pad clearance and registration depend on the fabricator and process (screen versus inkjet). The numbers here are typical; check the fab's design rules for your project.

## What belongs on it

1. **Reference designators** for every part, matching the schematic and BOM, so a technician can find R47 or U12.
2. **Outlines** of component bodies, in a layer that does not cover pads.
3. **Polarity and orientation**: diode cathode bar, LED anode or cathode, electrolytic "+" mark, connector pin 1, IC pin-1 dot.
4. **Test points and connectors**: TP names, connector pin names, jumper positions, switch positions.
5. **Board identification**: name, revision, date code, fab and assembly part number (often a space for a label).
6. **Warnings and ratings**: "HIGH VOLTAGE", maximum input, ESD-sensitive symbol.
7. **Logos and certification marks** where permitted (although many certification marks go on a label for traceability).

Information that does not fit, or that changes often, belongs in the assembly drawing and the BOM, not on the board.

## Design rules and their reasons

| Rule | Reason |
|---|---|
| Keep ink off exposed copper (pads, test points, fingers) | Ink blocks solder wetting and contact; the fab removes it |
| Keep ink off via holes unless tented | Ink can fill holes or cause contamination |
| Use at least the fab's minimum line width and text height | Smaller features fill in or blur |
| Keep a clearance of 0.15 to 0.2 mm from pads | Registration tolerance of the legend |
| Orient text in one or two directions (left-to-right, bottom-to-top) | Faster reading during assembly and debug |
| Mark pin 1 and polarity on the outside of the footprint | Visible after the part is placed |
| Mirror text on the bottom layer in CAD (the tool usually does it) | So the printed text reads correctly from below |
| Choose legend colour for contrast with mask | Legibility ([[q0471]]) |

A typical rule of thumb for text: character height at least 1 mm and stroke width at least 0.15 mm gives a stroke-to-height ratio of 15%, which stays legible after the ink spreads slightly.

## Footprint outlines: two different layers

CAD libraries keep several outline layers. The **silkscreen outline** is what is printed. The **courtyard** (the keep-out boundary around the part, used for placement spacing) and the **assembly layer** (the actual body outline and refdes, used for drawings) are not printed. Do not confuse them: placing text in the assembly layer does not put it on the board.

## Mistakes seen on first prototypes

- Refdes hidden under parts, so a board with 0402 parts cannot be read after assembly. Solution: refdes beside the part and an assembly drawing.
- Diodes or LEDs with no polarity mark, assembled backwards.
- A pin-1 dot placed under the IC body.
- Silk outlines overlapping pads of neighbouring parts, producing clipping artefacts.
- Text across vias, which gets cut or contaminates.

The cost to fix any of these is just a legend revision, but it happens only after the first boards arrive. A review pass on the printed legend in the CAD tool's 3D view or a PDF of the silk layer catches most.

## Other "text" in the stack

Some labelling is not silkscreen: a copper text (etched into the top copper layer) is permanent and can serve for logos or revisions, immune to ink wear but visible through mask in a different shade. Laser-etched or printed labels are applied after assembly for serial numbers and compliance information. A fab sometimes adds a manufacturer's marking (date code, UL logo) in a place you reserve, usually in a copper-free area.
:::

::: applications
- **Assembly**: pick-and-place and hand assembly use refdes and polarity marks to avoid orientation errors.
- **Test and debugging**: labelled test points and connector pins speed up probing and bring-up.
- **Rework and repair**: technicians locate parts by refdes.
- **Product identification and traceability**: board name, revision, date codes.
- **Safety and compliance**: high-voltage warnings, ESD symbols.
- **Branding**: logos and artwork on consumer products and development boards.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the fab remove silkscreen ink from pads?
Ink on copper pads would act as an insulating film and stop solder from wetting, producing open or weak joints. The fab's CAM software clips the legend away from exposed copper and holes by a clearance, so any text that overlaps pads is cut. That is why designers keep legend inside the keep-out area outside pads.

### What is the minimum text size you would use?
Check the fab's capability sheet. As a typical starting point for a general-purpose process, text height of 1 mm and stroke width of 0.15 mm are safe; inkjet processes handle somewhat smaller. Use larger text for critical marks (pin 1, polarity) and for items that must be readable in the field.

### Where do you put refdes on a dense board where there is no room?
Prioritise: polarity and pin-1 marks first (they must be readable after assembly), then test points and connectors, then refdes for parts a technician needs. For the rest, rely on the assembly drawing and an interactive BOM tool. Don't squeeze unreadable text onto the board; text that is too small is worse than none.

### What if a board must be completely free of silkscreen (for example a high-frequency or sensor board)?
Specify no legend in the fabrication notes. Ink is a lossy, uncontrolled dielectric, and over a sensor or an RF line it can affect the fields slightly, and it may outgas or contaminate optical surfaces. Use copper text in a non-critical area, or laser marking after assembly, and rely on the assembly drawing.

### Scenario: boards arrive with the silkscreen text cut off by solder pads on the bottom layer. What happened?
Either the bottom text was not mirrored (some tools require it) and ended up misplaced over pads, or the text was placed within the clearance of pads and clipped by the fab's CAM. Check the legend layers in the Gerber viewer: confirm the bottom layer reads correctly when viewed from the bottom, increase clearance from pads, and re-submit.
:::
