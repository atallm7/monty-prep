::: testing
Whether you understand flying probe as the *fixtureless* cousin of ICT, and can reason about when it wins and loses. The interviewer wants the mechanism (a handful of motor-driven probes touching pads, vias and component leads in sequence), what it measures (opens, shorts, component values, diode orientation, some capacitance and inductance, bare-board netlist), what it cannot do (at-speed, powered function, high-volume throughput) and the economics versus bed-of-nails: no fixture cost, fast to program, but minutes per board.
:::

::: why
Prototypes, low-volume products and boards still in flux (frequent ECOs) cannot justify a fixture costing thousands of dollars. Flying probe gives structural test coverage with just the CAD data. It also appears in bare-board fabrication, where fabs use it to netlist-test small runs. As a designer, knowing that it can probe component pads directly, and that it needs reachable features and fiducials, helps you lay out a board that is testable even without dedicated test points ([[q0619]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Moving probes**: 4 to 8 or more spring or needle probes on XY(Z) heads, on top and bottom, driven by linear motors.
- **No fixture**: programmed from CAD (netlist, centroid) and fiducials.
- **Measurements**: same family as ICT ([[q0609]]): resistance, capacitance, inductance, diodes, opens and shorts.
- **Probe-to-pad contact**: touching pads, vias, leads or test points; leaves small marks.
- **Test time vs steps**: time scales with the number of probe moves.
- **Volume trade-off**: low setup cost, high per-board time ([[q0605]]).
:::

::: answer
"Flying probe testing is an electrical test of a board without a custom fixture. Instead of hundreds of probes pressed on all at once, the machine has a small number of probes, typically four to eight or more, on motorised arms that fly over the board, touch down on pads, vias or component leads, take a measurement, lift, and move to the next spot. The program comes straight from the CAD data, so there's no tooling to build.

What it can capture is basically the structural set: shorts and opens between nets, missing or wrong components, resistor, capacitor and inductor values, diode orientation, and some limited powered checks. You can also use it on bare boards to verify continuity against the netlist.

The trade-off is speed. Each measurement requires moving probes, so a board might take several minutes, where a bed-of-nails fixture takes seconds. But there's no fixture cost, so for prototypes, low or medium volume, or products that change often, it wins. It also reaches places a fixed fixture may not, because it can touch pads on both sides and tiny features. Downsides: it can leave marks on pads, it can't test at speed, and it won't tell you whether the product functions, so I'd pair it with functional test."
:::

::: technical
**Machine.** A flatbed that holds the board; two or more heads above (and often below) the board, each carrying one or two probes, moving on linear-motor gantries with positioning accuracy of a few tens of micrometres or better, using fiducials to register the board. Test steps are generated from the netlist and component data. Probes are spring-loaded or needle-type; probing force and tip shapes vary (crown, spear) to break through flux or oxide without damaging pads.

**Measurements.** Two-wire and guarded measurements (the machine uses additional probes as guards, as in ICT): resistance, capacitance, inductance, diode drop, transistor junctions; opens/shorts using resistance or capacitance signature; and some powered tests (power the board, check a rail with a probe). Some systems also add boundary scan, LED/opto tests, and vision (camera-assisted pad finding or an AOI-like check of polarity marks).

**Test time.** Each *step* is a probe move plus a measurement. With a step time of $t_s$ and $N$ steps and $p$ probes working in parallel, time ≈ $N t_s/p_{eff}$. With 2,000 steps at 0.1 s each (single-sequence), that's 200 s = 3.3 min (computed). Vendor figures for probe speed vary; faster machines reach step times of a few tens of ms in good conditions.

**Economics.** Illustrative: flying probe 800 USD programming, 8 min per board at 40 USD/h, versus a 9,000 USD fixture with 30 s at 60 USD/h (see [[q0605]]): the crossover is about 1,700 boards. Below that, flying probe is cheaper; above, a fixture is cheaper. ECOs hurt fixtures far more.

**Advantages.** No fixture; programs in hours from CAD; reaches fine-pitch features; adapts to design changes; both sides tested in one pass; ideal for prototypes and NPI.

**Limitations.** Slow; probe marks on soft finishes or pads can be an issue if a pad needs to stay pristine (e.g. for later wire bond or cosmetic reasons); no at-speed tests; limited power/load capability; can't contact features hidden under packages (BGA pads, QFN ground pads unless vias), so coverage for hidden pins uses vias, boundary scan or other methods.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Probe counts, positioning accuracy, step times and minimum pad sizes differ by machine generation and vendor; the figures here are illustrative orders of magnitude.
:::

::: short
"Flying probe test checks a board without a custom fixture: a few motorised probes touch down on pads, vias and leads one after another and measure opens, shorts and component values, programmed straight from CAD. It's flexible, so great for prototypes, low volume and changing designs, and can reach fine features on both sides. But it takes minutes per board and can't test at speed or check real function, so above some volume, around 1,700 boards in a typical cost example, a bed-of-nails fixture is cheaper."
:::

::: long
"Flying probe is structural electrical test with no fixture. A bed-of-nails fixture has a probe for every net all pressed on at once. A flying probe machine has only a handful of probes, say four to eight, mounted on motorised heads above and below the board. The program built from the CAD data tells the heads where to go: probe lands on a pad or component lead, takes a measurement, lifts, moves to the next point.

Because the measurements are the same family as ICT, it catches the same defects: shorts, opens, missing or wrong components, resistor, capacitor and inductor values, diode polarity, and with a bit of extra capability some powered checks and boundary scan. Fabs also use it to netlist-test bare boards in small runs.

The big advantage is no fixture: there's no tooling cost or lead time, programming is mostly automatic from the design files, and when the design changes you just regenerate the program. It can reach features a fixture can't, like very fine-pitch pads, and it tests both sides in one pass. The disadvantage is time. If there are 2,000 probe steps at a tenth of a second each, that's over three minutes per board. The probes can also leave marks on pads, can't stimulate at speed, and can't access hidden pins under BGAs except through vias.

So the rule is volume. For prototypes, NPI and low-volume products, flying probe. When you build thousands, the fixture pays for itself, in my example at around 1,700 boards, and you move to ICT. Either way, I'd follow with a functional test, since neither tells me the product works."
:::

::: terms
- **Flying probe**: fixtureless test with moving probes.
- **Test step**: one probe movement and measurement.
- **Guard probe**: additional probe grounding a node to isolate a measurement ([[q0609]]).
- **Fiducial registration**: optical alignment of the board in the machine ([[q0596]]).
- **NPI**: new product introduction.
- **Probe mark**: small dent/scratch left by probe tip.
- **Netlist test**: continuity/isolation against the design netlist.
- **Fixtureless test**: any test needing no custom tooling.
- **Boundary scan**: JTAG-based interconnect test.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "probes that move around and test the board."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Describes the machine (probe count, XY heads, both sides, fiducial registration) and the measurement types.
- Lists what it captures and its blind spots, including hidden pins and no at-speed or functional tests.
- Gives test-time arithmetic and a break-even versus bed-of-nails with assumptions stated.
- Mentions probe marks and finish compatibility, and ECO flexibility.
- Gives the decision rule: prototypes/low volume/changing designs vs high-volume stable.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying flying probe tests function.** It's structural, like ICT.
- **Ignoring test time.** Minutes per board makes it a bottleneck at volume.
- **Believing it can reach everything.** Hidden pads (BGA, QFN) need vias, boundary scan or X-ray.
- **Forgetting probe marks.** On soft or cosmetic finishes (gold) this may matter; some products restrict probing areas.
- **Using it as the only test.** Functional behaviour still has to be checked.
- **Omitting fiducials.** The machine needs them to register the board accurately.
:::

## Why test time grows

Time per step includes: the move (distance / speed with acceleration), the Z touch-down, settle time, the measurement (tens of ms for analog settling, longer for capacitors), and the lift. If the average step takes 0.1 s with sequential steps, then time per board is $0.1\,N$ seconds. With multiple heads moving at once, effective time is shorter, but measurements that need two probes consume two heads.

## Reducing the step count

Test programs aim for coverage per step. Smart choices:
- Test each net at one end plus a shorts check by measuring net-to-neighbour in groups (capacitive or resistance), not all pairs.
- Skip components with low coverage value, or test only critical ones.
- Use boundary scan for IC-to-IC interconnect instead of probing every pin.
- Probe vias and large pads rather than tiny leads when possible (more robust contact).

## Contact quality

Probe tips and pad finishes matter. OSP and ENIG are fine; heavy flux residue on no-clean boards can insulate pads, so probes need a wiping motion or sharp tips. Soft solder on HASL pads can deform. Probe force is kept low (tens of grams), but repeated touches leave marks, so some designs keep dedicated test pads.

## Design rules (typical)

| Item | Guideline |
|---|---|
| Access | Pads/vias reachable on at least one side; leave probe approach space (probes touch at an angle) |
| Pad size | Larger pads give margin; machines can probe small features but accuracy and reliability drop |
| Fiducials | At least two or three global fiducials ([[q0596]]) |
| Keep-outs | Tall parts near test pads can block probe heads |
| Test points | Add on critical nets, power and ground ([[q0619]]) |
| Hidden pins | Provide vias or boundary scan |

## Choosing between flying probe and ICT

Use the cost model from [[q0605]]: fixed cost $F$, per-board time cost. Flying probe: $F\approx800$ USD, per-board about 5.33 USD; ICT: $F\approx9000$ USD, per-board about 0.50 USD; breakeven near 1,700 boards (computed). Additional factors: design stability (ECOs favour flying probe), board size and density, access, how many variants, and how long tests may take without bottlenecking the line.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A strong sentence: "Flying probe trades throughput for flexibility: no fixture, seconds of programming per ECO, but minutes per board." Then do the break-even, naming it as an example with assumptions.

::: applications
- **Prototype and NPI builds** where designs change weekly.
- **Low-volume, high-mix manufacturing** (industrial, medical, aerospace) with many board variants.
- **Bare-board netlist test at fabs** for small lots.
- **Failure analysis and rework verification**, probing specific nets.
- **Boards with extremely fine-pitch or dense layouts** that cannot accept fixture pins.

::: followups
### When would you choose flying probe over a bed-of-nails fixture?
For prototypes, low volume, or products with frequent changes, where fixture cost and lead time are not justified; also for very dense boards or when fixture access is impossible. Above a few thousand boards of a stable design, a fixture wins on time cost.

### What can't flying probe test?
Function, firmware, timing and at-speed behaviour, anything needing a real operating load, and features without probe access, such as pins under BGAs. It also generally can't detect marginal joints that look electrically fine.

### How does it measure a component in-circuit?
Like ICT: it contacts both pads, uses guard probes to ground intermediate nodes, applies a voltage or AC signal and measures current, then compares the computed value to limits. See [[q0609]].

### Test time is too long for the line. How do you reduce it?
Reduce steps (smarter net coverage, skip low-value steps, boundary scan for interconnect), add a second machine, use more probe heads, run the machine in parallel with other processes, or move to a fixture if volume justifies.

### Can flying probe damage the board?
Light probe force rarely does, but repeated touches can mark soft finishes and sensitive pads, and probes can contact the wrong spot if fiducial registration is poor. Specify test pad locations and avoid probing pads that must stay pristine.
:::

::: interpretations
"What can it capture?" means detected defect classes: the same structural ones as ICT (shorts, opens, wrong/missing/reversed components, wrong values), not function. Note flying probe is also used on *bare boards* at the fab, for netlist continuity, a slightly different use ([[q0593]]).
:::
