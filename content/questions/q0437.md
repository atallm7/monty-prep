::: testing
Whether you can reason about a PFET high-side switch when the motor supply is **much higher than the logic voltage**, say 48 V against a 3.3 V MCU. The PFET looks easy ("pull the gate low to turn it on") until you notice what the gate has to do relative to its own source. The interviewer is listening for:

1. The gate must be driven **relative to the supply**, so logic levels don't work; you need a level shifter that can stand the full supply.
2. The gate-source limit ($\pm20$ V typical) means you can't pull the gate to ground when the supply is 48 V; you need a clamp or a divider.
3. Turn-on and turn-off are set by resistors and the level shifter, making them slow and lossy.
4. The part itself: high-voltage PFETs are larger, more resistive and less available than NFETs.
5. System effects: supply transients, Miller coupling, shoot-through with asymmetric delays, quiescent power in the level-shift path.
:::

::: why
A PFET high-side switch is the "no bootstrap" option: it gives 100% duty cycle, works down to DC, and needs no charge pump. At 12 V it is easy. At 48 V, 60 V or in an automotive 12 V system with load dump, the same circuit has real problems: gate overvoltage, level-shifter ratings, slow switching and dissipation. This question tests whether you understand why the simple picture breaks, and therefore when to choose a PFET, an NFET with bootstrap ([[q0426]]), or a driver IC.
:::

::: concepts
- **PFET turn-on condition**: $V_{GS}=V_G-V_S\le-|V_{th}|$, with the source at the supply ([[q0097]]).
- **Gate-source rating**: typically $\pm20$ V; exceeding it destroys the oxide ([[q0102]]).
- **Level shifting**: translating a ground-referenced logic signal to a gate drive referenced to the supply.
- **RC gate drive**: pull-up/pull-down resistors with $C_{iss}$ set the edge times ([[q0431]]).
- **Miller effect**: switch-node dv/dt couples into the gate through $C_{gd}$ ([[q0428]]).
- **PFET vs NFET silicon**: lower hole mobility, larger die for the same $R_{DS(on)}$ ([[q0204]]).
:::

::: answer
"The PFET's appeal is that it needs no bootstrap: the source is at the supply, and to turn it on you pull the gate a few volts below the supply. At 12 volts you can almost do that from logic. At something like 48 volts with a 3.3 volt microcontroller it's a different story.

The first problem is that the gate drive is referenced to the supply, not to ground. To turn the PFET off the gate has to be at the supply, 48 volts. To turn it on it has to be about 10 volts below that, 38 volts. My GPIO swings between zero and 3.3 volts, so if I connect it directly the gate is always way below the source: V_GS is minus 45 volts, the FET is always on, and the oxide, rated for plus or minus 20 volts, is destroyed. So I need a level shifter, typically a small NFET that pulls the gate down through a resistor, and that NFET has to withstand the full supply voltage across it.

Second, I can't pull the gate all the way to ground, because that would put minus 48 volts across the gate oxide. So I need a clamp, a Zener of about 12 volts from gate to source, or a divider. That adds a standing current and dissipation in the resistor and Zener whenever the FET is on.

Third, the edges are slow and lossy. Turn-on is through the pull-down resistor and turn-off through the pull-up, each fighting the gate capacitance, so you get microsecond transitions and switching loss that limits PWM to maybe a kilohertz unless I add a push-pull stage. Add that high-voltage PFETs have two to three times the on-resistance of equivalent NFETs, that supply transients and Miller coupling can disturb the gate, and that the asymmetric delays make shoot-through worse in a bridge. So I'd use it for simple load switches and low-frequency control, and an NFET with a bootstrap or charge-pump driver for fast, efficient PWM."
:::

::: technical
**Gate drive requirement.** PFET with source at $V_{in}$: off for $V_G=V_{in}$ ($V_{GS}=0$), on for $V_G\approx V_{in}-V_{GS,on}$ (for $V_{in}=48$ V and 10 to 12 V drive: 36 to 38 V). The drive swing (10 to 12 V) rides on the supply, so the signal must be level-shifted by $V_{in}-V_{GS,on}$.

**Direct GPIO connection.** $V_{GS}=3.3-48=-44.7$ V: always on, and 25 V beyond a $\pm20$ V gate rating. At power-up of a 12 V system it is "on" at 3.3 V with only $V_{GS}=-8.7$ V.

**Level shifter.** An NFET (or NPN) pulls the gate node down through a resistor $R_c$. It must be rated for $V_{DS}\ge V_{in,max}$ plus margin (a 60 V NFET isn't enough for a 48 V bus with transients; use $\ge100$ V).

**Gate clamp.** A Zener $V_Z$ (10 to 15 V) from gate to source limits $|V_{GS}|$. With $R_c=10$ kΩ, $V_{in}=48$ V, $V_Z=12$ V: on-state current $(48-12)/10\text{ k}=3.6$ mA, dissipating 130 mW in $R_c$ (230 mW at 60 V) and 29 mW in the Zener (at $R_{gs}=10$ kΩ shunt). Without a clamp, $V_{GS}=-V_{in}R_{gs}/(R_{gs}+R_c)$ scales with the supply (−24 V at 48 V with equal resistors: over the rating).

**Speed.** Turn-on through $R_c$ and turn-off through $R_{gs}$: $\tau=RC_{iss}=10\text{ k}\times2\text{ nF}=20$ µs (2.2τ=44 µs). The Miller plateau is the expensive part: gate current at the plateau $(V_{in}-5)/R_c=4.3$ mA, so $t_{pl}=Q_{gd}/I=20\text{ nC}/4.3\text{ mA}=4.7$ µs. Switching loss per edge $\approx\tfrac12V_{in}I_Lt_{pl}$: for 5 A that is 0.56 mJ, so 1.1 W at 1 kHz and 22 W at 20 kHz: unusable for fast PWM. A push-pull stage with 0.3 A gives $t_{pl}=67$ ns and 0.32 W at 20 kHz.

**Device.** For equal $R_{DS(on)}$, PFETs need 2 to 3 times the area; at 100 V and above the choice of PFETs is narrow and $R_{DS(on)}$ is high. 5 A through 60 mΩ is 1.5 W; through a 30 mΩ NFET 0.75 W.

**Transients and Miller.** The supply can ring or spike; the gate follows the source only through $R_{gs}$ and the Zener, so a fast supply step can briefly turn the FET on or off or stress the gate. Miller injection $C_{gd}\,dv/dt$: 100 pF and 5 V/ns is 0.5 A into the gate network.

**Bridge shoot-through.** The PFET's turn-off (through the pull-up) is slow, while the low-side NFET turns on quickly: the dead time must cover tens of µs for the resistor-pull design.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Part-specific numbers ($Q_{gd}$, $C_{iss}$, $V_{GS(th)}$, availability of high-voltage PFETs and their $R_{DS(on)}$) vary widely. The values here are illustrative examples for the arithmetic.
:::

You need a level shifter, a small NFET rated for the full supply, a Zener clamp so the gate-source never exceeds about 12 V, and resistors that make turn-on and turn-off slow and lossy. Add the PFET's higher on-resistance, supply transients, Miller coupling and shoot-through, and it's good for low-frequency switching, not fast PWM."
:::

::: long
"A PFET is attractive on the high side because there's no bootstrap: its source is at the supply, and you turn it on by pulling the gate below the supply by 10 volts or so. And it can hold 100 percent duty indefinitely. The challenges show up when the supply is much higher than the logic, say 48 volts and a 3.3 volt MCU.

First, the gate is referenced to the supply. Off means gate at 48. On means gate at about 38. My GPIO goes between zero and 3.3. If I connect it directly, V_GS is minus 45 volts all the time, the FET is permanently on and the oxide is way past its plus or minus 20 volt rating. So I need a level shifter: typically a small N-channel FET that pulls the gate down through a resistor. That FET sees the full 48 volts across it when off, plus transients, so it needs a 100 volt rating.

Second, the clamp. When the level shifter pulls the gate down, I can't let it go to ground, which would be minus 48 volts of V_GS. A Zener of about 12 volts from gate to source limits it, and a resistor limits the current, so I burn a few milliamps at the full supply, around 130 milliwatts for 10 kilohms.

Third, speed. The gate is charged and discharged through those resistors. With 10 kilohms and a 2 nanofarad gate that's a 20 microsecond time constant, and the time in the Miller plateau is a few microseconds with 5 amps flowing at 48 volts. That's about half a millijoule per edge, 1 watt at 1 kilohertz and over 20 watts at 20 kilohertz. So PWM is limited to about a kilohertz, unless I add a push-pull stage, which cuts it by a factor of 70.

Fourth, the part. High-voltage PFETs have two to three times the on-resistance for the same cost, and there are far fewer to choose from. And finally system effects: supply spikes can disturb the gate, Miller coupling from dv/dt, and in a bridge, the slow turn-off through a resistor means long dead time against a fast NFET. So it works well for load switches and slow control, but I'd go to an NFET with a bootstrap or a charge-pump driver for efficient fast PWM."
:::

::: terms
- **High-side PFET**: P-channel FET with source at the supply and drain at the load.
- **Level shifter**: circuit that translates a ground-referenced logic level to a gate drive referenced to a higher rail.
- **Gate clamp (Zener)**: limits $|V_{GS}|$ to protect the oxide.
- **$V_{GS}$ rating**: absolute maximum gate-source voltage (typically $\pm20$ V).
- **Miller plateau**: interval during switching where $V_{GS}$ is flat while $V_{DS}$ changes.
- **Gate pull-up ($R_{gs}$)**: resistor from gate to source that holds a PFET off.
- **Load dump / supply transient**: large, brief supply voltage excursions (automotive and industrial).
- **Push-pull driver**: complementary transistors giving the gate a strong source and sink.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **Wiring the GPIO straight to the PFET gate** in a high-voltage system. $V_{GS}$ is wrong and over-rated.
- **A 60 V level-shifter NFET on a 48 V bus** with no transient margin.
- **No gate clamp.** Pulling the gate to ground at 48 V violates the $\pm20$ V rating.
- **Believing PFET turn-off is as fast as the NFET's.** It's through a pull-up resistor and $C_{iss}$.
- **Ignoring standing dissipation** in the level-shift resistor and Zener at the maximum supply (60 V case 230 mW).
:::

::: deep
## The core problem: the gate lives on the supply

For a PFET with source at $V_{in}$, the device is *off* at $V_G=V_{in}$ and *on* when $V_G\le V_{in}-|V_{GS,on}|$. The whole gate signal rides on the supply rail. Think of the gate drive as a 10 to 12 V swing sitting between $V_{in}-12$ and $V_{in}$. At $V_{in}=12$ V this is roughly 0 to 12 V, close to logic. At 48 V it is 36 to 48 V.

Figure: The GPIO can only pull the gate between 0 and 3.3 V, but the source is at 48 V. In both logic states $V_{GS}$ is hugely negative: the PFET is permanently on and the gate oxide is overstressed. A gate that never rises near 48 V can never turn the device off.

The two requirements are in conflict with what a GPIO offers:

1. **Turn-off needs $V_G\approx V_{in}$**, i.e. 48 V: a ground-referenced pin can't produce it. A pull-up resistor to $V_{in}$ does this when nothing pulls the gate down.
2. **Turn-on needs $V_G\approx V_{in}-10$**, i.e. 38 V: the gate must be pulled *down by a limited amount*, not to ground.

That is a **level shifter with a clamp** ([[q0438]] designs it).

## Challenge 1: the level shifter must survive the bus

A small NFET (or NPN) with its source at ground pulls the gate node down through a resistor. When it is off, the gate node sits at $V_{in}$, so the NFET sees $V_{DS}\approx V_{in}$. For a 48 V bus with transients (supply ringing, hot-plug, an inductive load kicking the bus) a 60 V part is marginal and a 100 V part appropriate. The same applies to any resistor, Zener and diode in the path: ratings are set by the bus *maximum*, not nominal.

## Challenge 2: the gate-source limit

Power MOSFET gate oxide is typically rated $\pm20$ V (some logic-level parts $\pm8$ to $\pm12$ V). If the level shifter pulled the gate fully to ground, $V_{GS}=-V_{in}$: −48 V. Options:

- **Resistive divider** ($R_{gs}$ to source, $R_c$ to the level shifter): $V_{GS}=-V_{in}R_{gs}/(R_{gs}+R_c)$. It scales with the supply: fine for a fixed 12 V bus, unsafe if $V_{in}$ varies: at 48 V and equal resistors it is −24 V, over the rating, and it falls to −6 V at 12 V (not fully on).
- **Zener clamp** from gate to source ($V_Z$ 10 to 15 V): $|V_{GS}|$ never exceeds $V_Z$ and is almost independent of supply. This is the standard answer. Cost: standing current.

| $V_{in}$ | Divider only ($R_{gs}=R_c=10$ kΩ) | With 12 V Zener |
|---|---|---|
| 12 V | −6 V | −6 V |
| 24 V | −12 V | −12 V |
| 48 V | −24 V (over rating) | −12 V |
| 60 V | −30 V (over rating) | −12 V |

## Challenge 3: standing dissipation

With the FET on, current flows from $V_{in}$ through the Zener (and $R_{gs}$) and $R_c$ to ground through the level shifter: $I=(V_{in}-V_Z)/R_c$. With $R_c=10$ kΩ: at 48 V, 3.6 mA, 130 mW in $R_c$; at 60 V, 230 mW. Raising $R_c$ saves power but slows the turn-on (more on this below). That is a low power but a real heat source in a small package.

## Challenge 4: slow, lossy switching

The gate sees the $R_c$ and $R_{gs}$ resistors. Gate time constant $\tau=RC_{iss}=10\text{ k}\times2\text{ nF}=20$ µs. More important is the Miller plateau, during which $V_{DS}$ swings through 48 V with the load current flowing:

- Gate current at the plateau: $(V_{in}-5)/R_c=4.3$ mA.
- Plateau time: $t_{pl}=Q_{gd}/I_g=20\text{ nC}/4.3\text{ mA}=4.7$ µs.
- Energy per edge $\approx\tfrac12V_{in}I_Lt_{pl}=0.5\times48\times5\times4.7\ \mu s=0.56$ mJ.

## Challenge 5: the PFET itself

$R_{DS(on)}\propto L/(\mu_p\,C_{ox}W\,(V_{GS}-V_{th}))$, with $\mu_p\approx\mu_n/2$ to $/3$ in silicon, so a PFET has 2 to 3 times the area, charge and capacitance of an NFET at the same $R_{DS(on)}$ and voltage rating. At 100 V and above that penalty is worse in practice: fewer PFETs are made, and the ones that are have higher $R_{DS(on)}$. For 5 A: 60 mΩ gives 1.5 W; a 30 mΩ NFET gives 0.75 W ([[q0426]]).

## Challenge 6: supply transients and Miller coupling

- **Supply spikes**: the PFET's gate is tied to the supply through $R_{gs}$, and the Zener and $C_{iss}$ create a path. A fast positive spike on $V_{in}$ is passed to the gate through $R_{gs}$ with a lag, so $V_{GS}$ is momentarily negative (turning on) or positive (a positive $V_{GS}$ beyond the rating can damage), so a TVS on the bus and a gate TVS or Zener are common.
- **Miller coupling**: with the load node moving at 5 V/ns and $C_{gd}=100$ pF, 0.5 A flows into the gate network. Through a 10 kΩ pull-up the induced voltage is bounded by the capacitive divider ($48\times100\text{ pF}/(100\text{ pF}+2\text{ nF})=2.3$ V), possibly enough to switch a threshold-sensitive FET briefly.

## Challenge 7: a bridge leg with a PFET

In a leg with a PFET high side and an NFET low side, the NFET is driven directly from a fast low-voltage driver while the PFET turns off through a pull-up resistor in tens of µs. The dead time must cover the slow PFET turn-off ([[q0428]]):

$$t_{dead}\ge t_{off,PFET}-t_{on,NFET}+\text{margin}\approx2.2R_{gs}C_{iss}=44\ \mu s.$$

That is large compared with a 50 µs PWM period at 20 kHz, another reason to restrict resistor-driven PFET legs to low-frequency drives.

## What the PFET still buys you

- **No bootstrap, no charge pump**; works at **100% duty** and at DC.
- Simple load-switch behaviour: off when unpowered (with $R_{gs}$), on when the shifter pulls down.
- **Reverse-battery** or ideal-diode arrangements with a back-to-back PFET.
- At low supply voltage (12 V) it is the simplest choice.

## Summary of the challenges

| Challenge | Why it appears at high $V_{in}$ | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Level shifting | Gate swing is referenced to $V_{in}$ | NFET/NPN level shifter rated $>V_{in}$ |
| Gate-source overvoltage | Pulling to ground gives $V_{GS}=-V_{in}$ | Zener clamp (10 to 15 V) |
| Standing dissipation | Current $(V_{in}-V_Z)/R_c$ whenever on | Larger $R_c$, or an active pull-down |
| Slow, lossy edges | $RC_{iss}$ and Miller plateau | Push-pull buffer; limit PWM frequency |
| PFET silicon | 2 to 3 times area, high-voltage choice limited | Use an NFET with bootstrap/charge pump |
| Transients and Miller | Gate tied to a moving rail | Bus TVS, gate clamp, stiff drive |
| Bridge shoot-through | Slow PFET turn-off | Long or adaptive dead time |
:::

::: applications
- **Load switches and power-path control** on 12 to 48 V rails where PWM isn't needed.
- **Reverse-polarity protection and ideal-diode circuits** using a PFET (source toward the load).
- **Battery-pack disconnect switches** (low-frequency or static).
- **Slow-speed motor and fan control** at tens of volts.
- **Automotive 12 V high-side switches** with load-dump protection (with clamp and TVS).
- **Hot-swap and enable circuits** where a PFET is held off by a pull-up and turned on by a level shifter.
:::

::: assumptions
- Supply of 48 V nominal (up to 60 V with transients) and a 3.3 V ground-referenced MCU.
- Gate-source rating $\pm20$ V; $V_{th}$ about −2 to −4 V; 10 to 12 V gate drive for full enhancement.
- $C_{iss}\approx2$ nF, $Q_{gd}=20$ nC (illustrative power PFET).
- Resistor-based level shifting with $R_c=R_{gs}=10$ kΩ unless stated.
- Load current of 5 A for the switching-loss arithmetic.

At 12 V and low current the same circuit is simple and cheap; at 100 V or above with fast PWM, an NFET and a driver IC ([[q0427]]) is typical. If the interviewer asks about a PFET in a CMOS-style bridge, the dead-time points apply directly.
:::

::: followups
### Why not use a resistor divider from the supply instead of a Zener?
A divider gives $V_{GS}$ proportional to $V_{in}$: −12 V at 24 V, but −24 V at 48 V, over the rating, and only −6 V at 12 V, not fully on. A Zener holds $|V_{GS}|$ near 12 V over a wide supply range, at the price of a small standing current.

### How could you speed up the PFET's switching?
Replace the resistive pull with a push-pull NPN/PNP stage referenced between $V_{in}$ and $V_{in}-12$ V (the Zener provides the lower rail), driven from the level shifter. That gives a few hundred mA, turning an edge that lasted microseconds into tens of ns, and cuts switching loss by roughly 70 times in the example.

### What if the supply spikes upward by 20 V for a few ms (automotive)?
The gate follows through $R_{gs}$ (RC-delayed), so $V_{GS}$ goes briefly positive or negative; the Zener clamps negative $V_{GS}$ at $V_Z$ and its forward diode drop on positive excursions. A bus TVS and a level shifter and resistors rated for the clamped voltage keep everything inside limits.

### Which is better at 48 V and 20 kHz, a PFET or an NFET high side?
An NFET with a bootstrap or charge-pump driver: lower $R_{DS(on)}$ and gate charge, faster edges, many more devices available. The PFET's loss with resistor drive is far too high at 20 kHz; it needs a buffer, at which point the simplicity advantage disappears.

### Why does the PFET help with 100% duty?
Because its gate-drive supply is the main supply itself: pulling the gate to $V_{in}-12$ V holds it on forever with no refresh, whereas a bootstrap cap droops. That is the PFET's main advantage and why it's popular in static load switches.
:::
