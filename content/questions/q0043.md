::: basics
### In plain words
The continuity condition says the current through an inductor cannot change instantly. The current just after a switching event equals the current just before it. The voltage across an inductor can jump, but the current cannot. This is how an inductor resists changes in current.

### Picture it
Picture a heavy merry-go-round. Its rotation speed is like the inductor current. You cannot stop it in zero time, however hard you try, since that would take an infinite force. Its speed carries over from just before to just after. The force you apply, like the voltage, can change suddenly and be as large as needed. The analogy fits well, but a merry-go-round has friction and an ideal inductor does not.

### Words you need
- **Continuity condition**: the inductor current is the same just before and just after a switch changes.
- **State variable**: the stored quantity that cannot jump, current for an inductor.
- **Inductive kick**: the large voltage that appears when you try to interrupt the current suddenly.
- **Flyback diode**: a diode across a coil that gives the current a safe path.
- **Inductor equation**: $v = L\,di/dt$. See [[q0041]].

### The simplest example
An inductor carries 2 A. A switch then moves so that the only path for the current is through a 5 Ω resistor. The inductor current cannot jump, so 2 A flows through the resistor. The resistor voltage is $2 \times 5 = 10$ V, and it appears instantly. The current stayed the same, but the voltage jumped. If there were no path at all, the voltage would rise until something conducts. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page gives the proof, the energy argument, and the flyback diode in detail. See also [[q0039]].
:::

::: testing
Whether you know **which quantity is continuous** in an inductor and can use it:

1. Statement: inductor **current** cannot change instantaneously, $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$. The voltage can jump.
2. Reason: $v = L\,di/dt$, so a current step needs infinite voltage; equivalently, the stored energy $\tfrac12 Li^2$ can't change in zero time.
3. Answer to the second part: inductors resist changes in **current** (capacitors resist changes in voltage).
4. Consequence: interrupting inductor current causes a large voltage spike (inductive kick), which you must give a path to. Strong candidates solve a quick switching transient with it.
:::

::: why
This is the single rule you use to start every RL transient problem, and it's behind one of the most common ways hardware dies: a MOSFET or relay contact switching an inductive load without a flyback path, killed by the voltage spike. It also explains why buck converters need a freewheeling diode or low-side FET, why relay coils have diodes across them, and why motor drivers need dead-time handling. Interviewers ask it because it separates candidates who can *predict* what a circuit does at a switching edge from those who only know steady-state formulas. Compare the capacitor version in [[q0037]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor law**: $v_L = L\,di_L/dt$ ([[q0041]]); finite voltage means finite di/dt.
- **Energy storage**: $E = \tfrac12 Li^2$ ([[q0039]]); a current jump would require infinite power.
- **Flux-linkage conservation**: $\lambda = Li$ can only change through $\int v\,dt$, and that integral over zero time is zero.
- **Initial and final conditions**: at $t = 0^+$ an inductor acts as a current source equal to $i_L(0^-)$; at DC steady state it's a short ([[q0044]]).
- **Inductive kick / flyback**: forcing current to stop quickly produces $L\,di/dt$ spikes that must be clamped ([[q0144]]).
- **Duality**: a capacitor's voltage is continuous ([[q0037]]).
:::

::: answer
"The continuity condition for an inductor is that the current through it can't change instantaneously. The current just after a switching event equals the current just before: $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$. So inductors resist changes in current.

The reason is the inductor equation, $v = L\,di/dt$. If the current jumped, di/dt would be infinite, which needs infinite voltage. You can also see it from energy: the inductor stores $\tfrac12 LI^2$, and changing that in zero time would need infinite power. Notice the voltage across an inductor *can* jump; it's only the current that's continuous. Capacitors are the dual: their voltage is continuous.

That's how I start any switching transient. At the instant after the switch moves, I replace the inductor with a current source equal to whatever it was carrying, and solve the circuit.

The practical consequence is inductive kick. If an inductor is carrying current and you open a switch, the inductor will raise its voltage as high as it needs to keep that current flowing, even if it means arcing across the switch or avalanching a MOSFET. That's why relay coils get a flyback diode across them: it gives the current somewhere to go so it can decay gracefully."
:::

::: technical
**Statement.** For an inductor with bounded voltage,
$$i_L(t_0^+) = i_L(t_0^-)$$

**Proof from the integral form.**
$$i_L(t_0^+) - i_L(t_0^-) = \frac{1}{L}\int_{t_0^-}^{t_0^+} v_L\,dt = 0 \quad\text{if } v_L \text{ is finite}$$
Only an impulse of voltage (infinite amplitude, zero width, finite volt-seconds) can make the current jump.

**Energy argument.** $E = \tfrac12 Li^2$. A jump $\Delta E$ in zero time requires $p = dE/dt \to \infty$.

**Flux argument.** $\lambda = N\Phi = Li$ is conserved across an instant; in coupled windings it's the total flux that's continuous, which is why a flyback transformer's current transfers from primary to secondary when the switch opens.

**What can jump:** $v_L$. At a step, $v_L(0^+)$ is whatever the rest of the circuit forces, given $i_L(0^+)$.

**Solving a transient.**
1. Find $i_L(0^-)$ from the circuit before switching (DC steady state: inductor = short).
2. At $0^+$, replace the inductor with a current source $i_L(0^-)$; solve for all voltages, including $v_L(0^+)$.
3. Find $i_L(\infty)$ (inductor = short again) and $\tau = L/R_{th}$ seen by the inductor.
4. $i_L(t) = i_L(\infty) + [i_L(0^+) - i_L(\infty)]e^{-t/\tau}$.

**Inductive kick magnitude.** If 1 A in 10 mH is suddenly given only a 100 Ω path, $v = -1\text{ A}\times100\ \Omega = -100$ V at $0^+$, even in a 12 V circuit, decaying with $\tau = 100\ \mu$s. With no path except ~100 pF of parasitic capacitance, the peak is $V \approx I\sqrt{L/C} = 10$ kV (ringing at 159 kHz) unless something breaks down first. The energy, 5 mJ, is the same in every case; the path decides the voltage and the time.

**Resists:** inductors resist changes in **current** (and in flux linkage).
:::

::: short
"The continuity condition says inductor current can't change instantaneously: $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$. That comes from $v = L\,di/dt$: a jump in current would need infinite voltage, and it would change the stored energy $\tfrac12 LI^2$ in zero time. So inductors resist changes in current, while their voltage can jump. Practically, if you interrupt an inductor's current, its voltage spikes as high as needed to keep current flowing, which is why relay coils need flyback diodes."
:::

::: long
"The continuity condition for an inductor is that its current is continuous in time. Whatever current it carries just before a switch changes state, it carries just after: $i_L$ at zero-plus equals $i_L$ at zero-minus. So the short answer to the second part is: inductors resist changes in current.

There are two ways to see why. From the equation, $v = L\,di/dt$: if the current stepped, di/dt would be infinite and you'd need infinite voltage, which no real circuit can provide. From energy: the inductor stores $\tfrac12 LI^2$ in its magnetic field. A jump in current means a jump in energy, which means infinite power. Either way, the current has to move smoothly.

What's allowed to jump is the voltage. That's the key difference from a capacitor, where it's the voltage that's continuous and the current that can jump.

I use this as step one of every transient problem. First, find the inductor current before the switch event, usually from DC steady state where the inductor is a short. Then at the instant after, I replace the inductor with a current source of that value and solve the circuit for the voltages. Then I find the final current and the time constant, L over the Thevenin resistance, and write the exponential.

The most important practical consequence is the inductive kick. Say a relay coil of 10 millihenries carries 1 amp from a 12 volt supply, and I turn off the transistor. The coil insists on keeping that amp flowing. If the only path is a 100 ohm resistor, the voltage jumps to 100 volts in the opposite direction, even though the supply is only 12. If there's no path at all, the voltage rises until something breaks down: the transistor avalanches or a contact arcs. So we give the current a path, usually a flyback diode, which clamps the voltage near one diode drop and lets the current decay."
:::

::: terms
- **Continuity condition**: $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$; inductor current is continuous for finite voltage.
- **$0^-$ / $0^+$**: the instants just before and just after a switching event.
- **Inductive kick**: the large voltage an inductor produces when its current is forced to change quickly.
- **Flyback (freewheeling) diode**: a diode that gives inductor current a path when the drive switch opens, clamping the kick.
- **Initial condition**: the stored current (energy) the inductor carries into a transient.
- **Time constant (τ = L/R)**: how quickly the current settles once it has a resistive path.
- **Avalanche**: breakdown of a MOSFET or diode junction when the inductive kick exceeds its rating; it dissipates the energy, sometimes destructively.
- **Volt-second (impulse)**: the only thing that can step inductor current is a voltage impulse with finite area.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "current can't change instantly".

**An exceptional answer**:
- States it precisely ($i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$) and says explicitly that **voltage can jump**.
- Justifies it two ways: $v = L\,di/dt$ and energy $\tfrac12 Li^2$.
- Shows how to use it: replace the inductor with a current source at $0^+$, solve, then $\tau = L/R_{th}$.
- Quantifies an inductive kick with numbers (1 A into 100 Ω gives 100 V from a 12 V circuit) and explains why the energy is fixed but the path sets the voltage.
- Names the fix (flyback diode, TVS, snubber) and the trade-off: a plain diode clamps hard but makes the current decay slowly, which matters for relays and solenoids ([[q0144]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying the inductor's voltage is continuous.** It's the current. The voltage can and does jump, often enormously.
- **Swapping with the capacitor.** Capacitor: voltage continuous, resists voltage change. Inductor: current continuous, resists current change. Q27 in the source list puts the inductor question under capacitors; don't let that confuse you ([[q0027]]).
- **Assuming the current drops to zero when you open a switch.** It can't, instantly. It finds a path: through the parasitic capacitance, an arc, an avalanching transistor, or your flyback diode.
- **Thinking a bigger resistor in the discharge path is "safer".** It makes the current decay faster but raises the peak voltage, $V = I\cdot R$.
- **Forgetting that the inductor's $0^+$ current direction is unchanged.** The inductor keeps pushing current the same way, which is why the voltage across it reverses polarity when it starts supplying energy.
:::

::: deep
## Why current must be continuous

Start from the integral form of the inductor law ([[q0041]]):

$$i_L(t) = i_L(t_0) + \frac{1}{L}\int_{t_0}^{t} v_L(\tau)\,d\tau$$

Across an instant, from $t_0^-$ to $t_0^+$, the interval has zero width. As long as $v_L$ is finite, the integral is zero, so

$$\boxed{i_L(t_0^+) = i_L(t_0^-)}$$

Three equivalent ways to say why:

- **Circuit law:** a step in $i$ means $di/dt = \infty$, which needs $v = \infty$.
- **Energy:** $E = \tfrac12 Li^2$. Changing $E$ by a finite amount in zero time needs infinite power.
- **Flux:** the flux linkage $\lambda = Li$ can only change by $\int v\,dt$. In a transformer it's the total flux in the core that's continuous, which is why current can "jump" from the primary to the secondary of a flyback transformer while the core flux stays smooth.

The voltage, however, is not constrained. At a switching instant it takes whatever value the rest of the circuit forces on it, given that $i_L$ is pinned.

> [!NOTE]
> The capacitor is the dual: $v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)$, because $i = C\,dv/dt$ and $E = \tfrac12 CV^2$. Inductors resist changes in current; capacitors resist changes in voltage ([[q0037]]).

## The recipe for switching transients

1. **Before the switch ($t = 0^-$):** assume DC steady state, so the inductor is a short ([[q0044]]). Find $i_L(0^-)$.
2. **Just after ($t = 0^+$):** replace the inductor with a **current source** of value $i_L(0^-)$, same direction. Solve the resistive circuit for every voltage, including $v_L(0^+)$.
3. **Long after ($t \to \infty$):** inductor is a short again; find $i_L(\infty)$.
4. **Time constant:** $\tau = L/R_{th}$, where $R_{th}$ is the resistance seen by the inductor in the new circuit.
5. **Write it:** $i_L(t) = i_L(\infty) + [i_L(0^+) - i_L(\infty)]\,e^{-t/\tau}$.

### Trade-off: the discharge path sets voltage and time

The energy to get rid of is fixed at $\tfrac12 LI^2 = 5$ mJ. What you choose is how:

| Discharge path | Peak voltage | Decay |
|---|---|---|
| 10 Ω resistor | −10 V | $\tau = 1$ ms (slow) |
| 100 Ω resistor | −100 V | $\tau = 100\ \mu$s |
| 1 kΩ resistor | −1000 V | $\tau = 10\ \mu$s (fast) |
| Diode (≈0.7 V) | ≈ −0.7 V | Slow: roughly linear ramp-down, $\Delta t \approx LI/V$ plus DCR decay |
| Nothing (only ~100 pF stray C) | up to $I\sqrt{L/C} \approx 10$ kV | Rings at ~159 kHz until something breaks down |

Higher clamp voltage gives a faster current decay, because $di/dt = v/L$. That's the core design trade-off in protecting relay and solenoid drivers: a plain diode is gentle on the switch but makes the relay release slowly; a Zener or TVS clamp at a higher voltage releases fast but needs a switch rated above the clamp voltage. See [[q0144]] and [[q0340]].

## Continuity in power converters

Continuity is why switching converters need a second switch or a diode. In a buck converter, when the high-side MOSFET turns off, the inductor current can't stop, so it immediately pulls the switch node negative until the low-side FET's body diode (or the catch diode) conducts. During dead time you see the switch node sit at about −0.7 V: that's the inductor enforcing continuity. In a boost converter, opening the low-side switch makes the inductor's voltage rise until the output diode conducts, and that's how the output gets above the input.

## When "instantaneous" isn't quite instantaneous

In the real world nothing switches in zero time and every node has capacitance, so the current is continuous anyway. What continuity really predicts is: **the faster you try to change inductor current, the larger the voltage**. A MOSFET turning off a 2 A load in 20 ns through 50 nH of stray loop inductance sees $L\,di/dt = 50\text{ nH}\times 2\text{ A}/20\text{ ns} = 5$ V of overshoot. Same law, parasitic scale. That's why buck converter hot loops are kept tiny.

> When an interviewer draws a switch and an inductor, say the rule and then immediately "so at $t = 0^+$ I replace the inductor with a current source of $i_L(0^-)$." It shows you know how to use it, not just recite it.
:::

::: applications
- **Relay and solenoid drivers**: a flyback diode (e.g. a 1N4148 or 1N4007 across a relay coil) gives the coil current a path when the driving transistor turns off ([[q0144]]).
- **Buck and boost converters**: continuity forces the switch node to swing until a diode or synchronous FET conducts, which is the mechanism behind power conversion ([[q0187]], [[q0200]]).
- **Ignition coils**: interrupting primary current deliberately creates a large $L\,di/dt$, stepped up by the transformer to kilovolts for the spark.
- **Motor drivers (H-bridges)**: during dead time, winding current continues through the MOSFET body diodes.
- **Unclamped inductive switching (UIS) rating**: MOSFET datasheets specify single-pulse avalanche energy because some designs rely on the FET to absorb the inductor's energy.
- **Parasitic overshoot**: drain-voltage overshoot at MOSFET turn-off is continuity acting on stray loop inductance.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Finite voltage.** Continuity holds whenever the voltage across the inductor is bounded. Ideal-circuit textbook problems with an ideal switch and no other path would predict infinite voltage; that means the model is missing something (stray C, arcing, breakdown), not that current jumps.
- **Fixed inductance.** If the geometry changes (moving plunger) or the inductor is coupled to another winding, it's flux linkage that's continuous, not necessarily each winding's current.
- **Lumped circuit.** The inductor is small relative to the wavelength; current is the same at both ends.
- The transient recipe assumes the circuit was in **DC steady state** before switching. If not, use the actual $i_L(0^-)$.
:::

::: followups
### How does the continuity condition for an inductor differ from a capacitor's?
Inductor: current continuous, voltage may jump; at $0^+$ it behaves as a current source. Capacitor: voltage continuous, current may jump; at $0^+$ it behaves as a voltage source. They come from $v = L\,di/dt$ and $i = C\,dv/dt$ respectively. See [[q0037]].

### If 2 A flows in a 5 mH inductor and the switch opens with a 47 Ω resistor as the only path, what is the initial voltage and the time constant?
$v(0^+) = 2 \times 47 = 94$ V (reverse polarity) and $\tau = L/R = 5\text{ mH}/47\ \Omega \approx 106\ \mu$s. The stored energy, $\tfrac12\cdot5\text{ mH}\cdot4 = 10$ mJ, ends up in the resistor.

### Why does a relay release more slowly with a plain flyback diode, and how do you speed it up?
The diode clamps the coil to about −0.7 V, so $di/dt = -0.7/L$ is small and the current decays mostly through the coil's own resistance. The magnetic force holds the armature until the current falls enough. Adding a Zener in series with the diode raises the clamp voltage (e.g. 24 V), so $di/dt$ is much larger and the current collapses faster. The cost is that the switch must withstand supply + clamp voltage.

### What actually happens if you open an inductive circuit with no flyback path?
The inductor voltage rises until something conducts: parasitic capacitance charges up (ringing at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ with peak around $I\sqrt{L/C}$), a MOSFET avalanches, or mechanical contacts arc. For 1 A in 10 mH with 100 pF stray capacitance, the theoretical peak is ~10 kV. In practice a transistor would avalanche long before that and may fail if the energy (5 mJ) exceeds its avalanche rating.

### What if an interviewer says: "Current can't change instantly in an inductor, so why do motors draw huge inrush current immediately?"
Continuity only says the current can't *step*; it still rises at $di/dt = V/L$. Motor winding inductance is small (e.g. 1 mH), so 12 V gives 12 A/ms: the current reaches near its stall value $V/R$ within a few $L/R$ time constants, which is milliseconds. The large inrush is because at startup there's no back-EMF, so the current is limited only by winding resistance. See [[q0345]].
