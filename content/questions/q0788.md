::: basics
### In plain words
Bus contention means two or more outputs are driving the same wire at the same time, to different levels. One is pushing the wire up to the supply while another pulls it down to ground. That makes a direct path from supply to ground through both outputs. The result is a wire stuck at a mid-level that reads as garbage, a large wasted current, and hot or damaged output circuits.

### Picture it
Imagine a tug of war over a marker on a rope. One team pulls the marker toward the high end. The other team pulls it toward the low end. If the teams are equally strong, the marker stays in the middle and neither team wins. But both teams strain hard the whole time, which is wasted effort that tires them out. Here the strain is current, and the tiring is heat.

### Words you need
- **Contention**: two outputs driving one wire to opposite levels at once.
- **On-resistance**: the small resistance, tens of ohms, that an output's switch has when it is on. It is all that limits the current in a fight.
- **Tri-state (high impedance)**: an output that can switch off completely, so another device may drive the wire. See [[q0783]].
- **Open-drain**: an output that can only pull low, so it cannot fight. See [[q0785]].

### The simplest example
Two pins on a 3.3 V board each have about 25 Ω of on-resistance. One drives high and one drives low. The current is $3.3/(25+25) = 66$ mA, well above a typical pin's rating. The wire sits at the midpoint, 1.65 V. A receiver treats below 30% of the supply as low and above 70% as high, so 1.65 V is neither. A 100 Ω resistor in series with each pin cuts the current to about 13 mA. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the causes, ways to prevent it, and debug steps.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can define **bus contention** precisely (two or more drivers actively driving one net to different levels at the same time), quantify the consequence with a quick calculation, and list how real designs avoid it. The interviewer is checking that you connect the concept to the transistor-level picture, and that you know both the *static* cases (two masters, a stuck driver) and the *dynamic* ones (turnaround overlap on a bidirectional bus).

Hidden sub-skills: voltage-divider reasoning on the output impedances, power and heating estimates, tri-state enable timing, and recognising contention from symptoms.
:::

::: why
Contention looks harmless in software ("two pins wired together") and is destructive in hardware. It causes hot chips, mid-level voltages that read as garbage, intermittent bus errors, and damaged output stages, often only on some boards or at some temperatures. It is the reason for open-drain buses, tri-state discipline, chip selects, dead time in H-bridges and direction-control lines on RS-485. Knowing how to reason about it is a core debugging skill for embedded and digital hardware.
:::

::: concepts
- **Driver output impedance**: a push-pull driver is a low resistance to a rail, tens of ohms. See [[q0783]].
- **Tri-state**: a driver that can be disabled (high-Z) so another may drive the line.
- **Resistive divider**: two fighting drivers form a divider between $V_{DD}$ and ground. See [[q0008]].
- **Open-drain / wired-AND**: the contention-safe alternative. See [[q0785]].
- **Enable (OE) timing**: the disable time of one driver must be shorter than the enable time of the next.
- **Arbitration / protocol**: rules that decide who may drive when. See [[q0776]].
:::

::: answer
"Bus contention is when two or more drivers try to drive the same wire at the same time with different values: one is pulling it high and another is pulling it low. With push-pull outputs that's a direct path from supply to ground through the two output stages, limited only by their on-resistances.

Quick numbers: two pins each with about 25 ohms of on-resistance fighting on a 3.3 volt line. The current is 3.3 over 50, about 66 milliamps, and the wire sits around half the supply, 1.65 volts, which is neither a valid high nor a valid low. That's bad on three counts: the receivers read garbage, the pins are probably beyond their current rating, and the heat, roughly 100 milliwatts in each output transistor, can damage or at least age the parts.

It happens when two masters drive a shared line, when software sets two pins as outputs at once, when a device fails to release the bus, and dynamically on a bidirectional bus when one driver turns on before the other has fully turned off.

To prevent it: only one driver at a time, enforced by design. Use tri-state drivers with enable control and a dead time between turning one off and the next on; use open-drain outputs with a pull-up for shared lines, like I2C; use chip selects so only one slave drives MISO; add series resistors to limit the fault current; and in firmware make sure pins come up as inputs at reset."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** Contention = two or more enabled drivers on a net outputting conflicting levels.

**Static case (push-pull vs push-pull).** Driver A high ($R_{on,P}$ to $V_{DD}$), driver B low ($R_{on,N}$ to ground). Current and bus voltage:

$$I=\frac{V_{DD}}{R_{on,P}+R_{on,N}+R_{ext}},\qquad V_{bus}=V_{DD}\frac{R_{on,N}+R_{ext,B}}{R_{on,P}+R_{on,N}+R_{ext}}$$

For 25 Ω each at 3.3 V: $I=66$ mA, $V_{bus}=1.65$ V, power $3.3\times66=218$ mW total (109 mW in each on-resistance). With 20 Ω (A) and 30 Ω (B): $V_{bus}=3.3\times30/50=1.98$ V, which is between $V_{IL}=0.99$ V and $V_{IH}=2.31$ V (undefined).

**Dynamic case.** On a bidirectional bus, if driver B enables $t_{en}$ before driver A has disabled ($t_{dis}$), there is an overlap of $t_{en}-t_{dis}$ with both on. A 10 ns overlap at 66 mA dissipates $3.3\times0.066\times10\text{ ns}=2.2$ nJ, which at 1 MHz is 2.2 mW average, but it also makes supply and ground current spikes.

**Mitigations.**
- Design rule: one driver per net at any time, or open-drain/wired-AND with pull-up.
- Tri-state enables with dead time: ensure $t_{dis,max}<t_{en,min}$ across process, voltage and temperature, plus margin.
- Series resistors $R_s$: limit current to $V_{DD}/(R_{on,P}+R_{on,N}+2R_s)$: 13 mA with 100 Ω each.
- Reset states: pins default to inputs; external enables with pull-ups/downs so devices are safe at power-up.
- Protocol: master/slave, chip select, arbitration.
- Pin protections: current limiting and thermal shutdown in some drivers (RS-485 transceivers, many bus buffers).

**Symptoms.** Mid-rail line voltage, warm devices, higher supply current, garbled data, intermittent behaviour at temperature (on-resistance rises with heat, changing the divider).
:::

::: long
"Bus contention means two or more drivers are enabled on the same net and trying to force different levels at once. With push-pull outputs, that's one PMOS pulling up and another driver's NMOS pulling down, which is a short from supply to ground through the two on-resistances.

The numbers are quick to do. Say each on-resistance is 25 ohms and the supply is 3.3 volts. The current is 3.3 over 50, 66 milliamps. The wire sits at the divider midpoint, 1.65 volts, in the forbidden zone between valid low and valid high, so every receiver reads garbage or oscillates. And each output stage dissipates roughly 110 milliwatts, concentrated in a tiny transistor, and typical pin ratings are 20 or 25 milliamps. If it persists, you get hot parts, electromigration, latch-up, or a dead pin. If it's momentary, you see supply glitches and bit errors.

How does it occur? One: a shared line with two masters or two outputs, for example two boards plugged together, each driving a signal line. Two: software configuring both pins of a shared line as outputs, or a boot state where a pin comes up driving. Three: a slave that doesn't release MISO when its chip select is off. And four, the subtle one, dynamic contention on a bidirectional bus, where the next driver enables a few nanoseconds before the previous one has fully disabled.

To avoid it: use a protocol, with only one driver allowed at any time, enforced by chip selects or direction pins; use tri-state drivers with dead time greater than the worst-case disable time; use open-drain outputs with a pull-up for any shared line like I2C, where nobody ever drives high, so nothing can fight; add a small series resistor, say 100 ohms, to limit the damage to about 13 milliamps; and make sure the reset state of every pin is an input.

When debugging, I look for a mid-level voltage on the line and extra supply current or a warm chip."
:::

::: terms
- **Bus contention**: two or more drivers simultaneously driving one net to conflicting levels.
- **Tri-state (high-Z)**: driver state in which the output is disabled.
- **Output enable (OE)**: control that activates a tri-state driver.
- **Dead time / break-before-make**: gap guaranteed between disabling one driver and enabling another.
- **$t_{en}$, $t_{dis}$**: output enable and disable times of a driver.
- **Shoot-through**: supply-to-ground current when both transistors of one stage conduct (on-chip equivalent).
- **Wired-AND**: contention-safe sharing of open-drain outputs.
- **Arbitration**: protocol mechanism that decides who may drive the bus.
- **Fault current limit**: series resistance or driver feature that caps contention current.
:::

- Lists multiple preventions (tri-state with dead time, open-drain, chip selects, protocols, series resistors, safe reset states) and where each is used (RS-485 DE, SPI MISO, I2C).

- Mentions that many transceivers have built-in short-circuit and thermal protection but that is a safety net, not a design.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"It just causes a wrong logic value."** It also causes large current and heating, and can destroy a pin.
- **Assuming the stronger driver "wins" cleanly.** With similar strengths the line sits at an invalid mid level.
- **Thinking contention only happens with two masters.** A slave that does not tri-state MISO, a reset state, or an enable overlap suffice.
- **Ignoring dynamic contention** because the static design is fine: nanosecond overlaps still cause current spikes, EMI and sometimes latch-up.
- **Relying on firmware alone** to avoid it, with no hardware safeguard for crash or reset states.
- **Using a pull-up/pull-down to "solve" contention.** A resistor cannot overpower a driver and doesn't stop the fight.
- **Confusing contention with a wired-AND**, which is intentional and safe with open-drain outputs.
:::

Figure: Equivalent circuit of two push-pull drivers in contention. A's on PMOS is $R_{on,P}$ to the supply, B's on NMOS is $R_{on,N}$ to ground. The shared wire is the divider tap. Nothing in the circuit limits the current except those two small resistors.

$$I=\frac{V_{DD}}{R_{on,P}+R_{on,N}}=\frac{3.3}{50}=66\ \text{mA},\qquad V_{bus}=V_{DD}\frac{R_{on,N}}{R_{on,P}+R_{on,N}}=1.65\ \text{V}$$

Power: $P_{total}=3.3\times0.066=218$ mW, split in proportion to resistance: $I^2R=0.066^2\times25=109$ mW in each. For a transistor with a few thousand square microns of channel area, that is a lot. Absolute-maximum ratings per pin are commonly in the 20 to 25 mA range, so this is about three times beyond the limit; the failure mode may be a gradual one (electromigration in the metal, oxide stress, degraded on-resistance) rather than an instant burnout.

## How contention happens

**1. Two masters, one wire.** Two boards or two modules each push-pull driving the same signal, e.g. a debug probe and an MCU both driving a reset or boot pin, or two MCU TX pins connected on a shared UART line ([[q0776]]).

**2. A slave that doesn't release.** On SPI, MISO is shared by all slaves. Each slave must tri-state MISO when its chip select is inactive. A device that doesn't (some cheap sensors, or a slave still booting) fights the active slave. Fix: a MISO tri-state buffer or a series resistor, and check the datasheet for "MISO high-Z when CS high".

**3. Software and reset states.** A pin set as output while another device drives the net; firmware that enables outputs before determining direction; a pin that comes out of reset driving (rare but real for some boot pins). Defence: pins default to input; external pulls define the idle state.

**4. Bidirectional bus turnaround (dynamic).** A parallel data bus or a half-duplex line changes direction. If driver B enables $t_{en}$ after driver A disables $t_{dis}$ there is a gap (both off, bus floating: harmless); if $t_{en}<t_{dis}$ there is an overlap.

## Preventing and containing it

| Technique | How it helps |
|---|---|
| Single driver by protocol | chip select, master/slave, direction pin |
| Open-drain with pull-up | nobody drives high, so no fight ([[q0785]]) |
| Tri-state with dead time | $t_{dis,max}+\text{margin}<t_{en,min}$ |
| Series resistors | cap the fault current: $3.3/(50+200)=13$ mA with 100 Ω per driver |
| Bus buffers/transceivers | isolate segments, add short-circuit protection |
| Input default at reset | pins float, so nothing drives before firmware does |
| Current-limited drivers | RS-485/CAN transceivers survive contention |

## Finding it on a real board

Look for (1) a mid-rail voltage on a digital net (on a scope it may look like a flat line at 1.5 to 2 V or a heavily distorted square wave), (2) more supply current than expected, (3) a chip that gets warm, (4) behaviour that depends on temperature or on which board is connected. Isolate by removing one driver (lifting a resistor, powering one board off) and see if the net returns to a clean 0 or 3.3 V. Measuring the net with the receiver disconnected helps, because the receivers' loading is negligible anyway; the fight is between the drivers.

## On-chip cousin: shoot-through

Inside a single push-pull output, if the PMOS and NMOS are both on during the switching transient, there is a brief supply-to-ground current ("crowbar"). Output drivers use staggered gating to avoid it, and H-bridges insert dead time between turning one leg off and the other on, for the same reason as the bus turnaround dead time above.
:::

::: applications
- **SPI buses with several slaves**: MISO tri-state requirement and chip selects.
- **I2C and 1-Wire**: open-drain by design, so contention cannot occur.
- **Parallel memory and data buses**: OE/WE timing and dead cycles in the bus controller.
- **RS-485 networks**: DE control and current-limited transceivers.
- **H-bridges and half-bridges**: dead time to avoid shoot-through.
- **Board bring-up**: two debug probes or a programmer fighting the MCU's reset or boot pins.
:::

::: assumptions
- Push-pull 3.3 V CMOS with on-resistances of 20 to 30 Ω; real pins range from about 10 to over 100 Ω depending on drive setting and temperature.
- Thresholds $V_{IL}=0.3V_{DD}$, $V_{IH}=0.7V_{DD}$ for the "undefined zone" discussion.
- Static contention figures ignore wiring resistance and pad current limiting; real parts may limit the current internally, or fail earlier than the calculation suggests.
- The 10 ns overlap and 1 MHz rate are illustrative.
- If one driver is open-drain, there is no contention in the static sense: pulling low against a pull-up is normal operation.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> How much sustained contention a particular pin tolerates is device-specific and rarely guaranteed beyond the absolute-maximum current rating. Treat any contention as a defect, and consult the datasheet's short-circuit or "output short-circuit current" specification where it exists (for some bus buffers it is rated for one second).
:::

::: followups
### Two push-pull pins on one line, both outputting the same value. Is that contention?
Not electrically: no current flows if the levels agree. But it is one skew, glitch or software bug away from contention, and during transitions the two edges are never exactly aligned. Treat it as a design error and use a gate, open-drain or a single driver.

### Why is open-drain contention-safe?
Because only the NMOS exists. If one device pulls low and the other releases, the line is low and no current flows from supply to ground except through the pull-up. If both pull low, they share the same low level. Nobody ever drives high, so nobody can fight.

### How do you calculate the dead time on a tri-state bus?
Take the worst-case driver disable time $t_{dis,max}$ and the best-case enable time $t_{en,min}$ over temperature and supply, add clock skew and a margin: dead time $\ge t_{dis,max}-t_{en,min}+t_{skew}+t_{margin}$. For example, 8 ns disable, 3 ns enable, 1 ns skew and 2 ns margin gives 8-3+1+2=8 ns, so insert at least one clock of 10 ns.

### What does contention look like on a scope?
A level stuck in the middle (1 to 2 V on a 3.3 V net) or a square wave with flattened tops and bottoms; edges that look slow because the wire is being pulled both ways; supply droop at the same time. Disconnecting one driver restores the clean signal.

### Harder: a slave's MISO line is shared with two others; after adding a third slave the system becomes unreliable at high temperature. Hypothesis?
The new slave probably doesn't tri-state MISO when CS is high (or has a long disable time), so it fights the selected slave. At high temperature on-resistances rise and the divider shifts, making the bus voltage cross a threshold. Check: probe MISO with all but one slave disconnected, measure supply current of idle slaves, and check the datasheet. Fixes: a tri-state buffer on that slave's MISO, a series resistor, or a different part.
:::
