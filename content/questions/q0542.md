::: testing
Whether you see power-supply placement as a trade between competing forces rather than one rule. The headline answer is "close to the load", but the interviewer wants the full set: distance to the load (drop, inductance, transient), distance to the input connector (the input loop and filtering), heat (airflow, neighbours), noise (keep switchers away from analog, RF, crystals and sensors), mechanical constraints (tall inductors, board edge, enclosure), grounding, and testability. Strong candidates rank them and say which wins when they conflict.
:::

::: why
Placement is done in the first hour of layout and cannot be fixed later by clever routing. A regulator put at the far side of the board forces long, resistive, inductive supply runs; one put next to the ADC injects noise into every measurement. Experienced layout engineers decide power placement before anything else, and the question is a quick test of whether you do too. It is also the entry point to the more detailed component-level layout questions ([[q0545]] onward).
:::

::: concepts
- **IR drop and loop inductance** grow with distance from supply to load. See [[q0534]], [[q0536]].
- **Switching noise sources**: the switch node (high dV/dt) and the hot loop (high di/dt). See [[q0547]].
- **Thermal management**: regulators dissipate $P_{out}(1/\eta - 1)$, and that heat must go somewhere. See [[q0540]].
- **Power flow**: connector, protection, filter, regulator, decoupling, load; a natural left-to-right order.
- **Magnetic coupling**: the field of a small loop falls roughly as $1/r^3$ near the source.
- **Grounding**: the supply's return and the sensitive reference must meet in a controlled way. See [[q0495]].
:::

::: answer
"I place the power supply by ranking a few forces. The biggest one is distance to the load: the closer the regulator and its output capacitors are to what they feed, the less IR drop, less inductance in the supply path, better transient response, and less trace to pick up or radiate noise. So the regulator goes next to its load, which is why point-of-load regulators exist.

Second is the input side: the regulator should also be reasonably close to where power enters, because the input capacitor and the regulator form a high-current loop that needs to be tiny, and I don't want to drag a pulsating input current across the board.

Then heat and noise. A regulator dissipates real power, for example about 1.1 W for a 10 W output at 90% efficiency, so I want it on a copper area with airflow and away from temperature-sensitive parts. A switcher is also a noise source, so I keep it and its inductor away from sensitive analog, ADCs, RF, crystals and sensors. Magnetic coupling drops quickly with distance, roughly as one over distance cubed.

Finally the practical bits: tall inductors and capacitors need clearance, I want test points, and the return path needs a solid plane. When forces conflict I give noise isolation and the hot loop priority, then shorten the output path as much as I can."
:::

::: technical
**Forces and how to quantify them.**
1. **Distance to load.** DC drop $\Delta V = I\,R_\square (l/w)\times 2$ (supply and return); transient $\Delta V = L\,di/dt$. For a 1 V rail at 10 A, 20 mm of 1 mm trace each way drops 0.19 V. See [[q0543]].
2. **Distance to the input.** The input capacitor and high-side/low-side switches form the hot loop; keep that loop to a few mm. Keep the lengthy path on the *input* side, where the current is steady, rather than between the regulator and its input capacitor.
3. **Thermal.** $P_{diss} = P_{out}(1/\eta - 1)$: 10 W out at 90% is 1.1 W; at 80% 2.5 W. Need copper area, vias, airflow, and distance from heat-sensitive parts ([[q0540]]).
4. **Noise.** Place the switch node, inductor and hot loop away from ADCs, references, RF, crystals, microphones and sensor front ends. Near-field magnetic coupling from a small loop falls as $1/r^3$, so doubling the distance cuts it by about 18 dB; electric (capacitive) coupling falls more slowly.
5. **Mechanical.** Inductor height, large electrolytic capacitors, board edge for heat sinks, connector clearance.
6. **Ground.** Keep a continuous plane under the power stage and its return paths; avoid forcing noisy current across sensitive ground regions ([[q0495]]).
7. **Test and rework.** Test points on the output, a place to probe the switch node properly, access to the feedback divider.

**Centralised vs point-of-load.** A single regulator near the connector with long distribution is simpler but suffers drop and noise on all rails; point-of-load (POL) regulators near each load need an intermediate bus, but give better regulation, local control, and shorter paths.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The $1/r^3$ falloff is the near-field magnetic dipole approximation; real coupling depends on loop orientation, shielding, and nearby planes. Use it to rank placements, not to predict dB.
:::

::: short
"Place the supply close to its load, because distance costs IR drop, loop inductance, slower transient response and noise pickup. Keep the input loop tiny and near where power enters. Account for heat, since a regulator dissipates real watts, and keep switchers away from sensitive analog, RF and crystals, because magnetic coupling falls fast with distance. Allow room for tall parts, give it a solid ground plane and test points, and when forces conflict I protect the hot loop and noise isolation first."
:::

::: long
"Placement is a ranking of competing forces, so I go through them in order.

The first is distance to the load. A supply that sits next to its load has less IR drop, less inductance in the path, a faster transient response and less trace to radiate or pick up noise. For a 1 volt rail at 10 amps, 20 millimetres of 1 millimetre trace each way already costs about 190 millivolts, nearly a fifth of the rail. That's why I like point-of-load regulators.

Second is the input side. The input capacitor and the power switches form the hot loop, and that loop must be tiny. So the input capacitor sits right at the regulator, and I'd rather have the long run be the steady input current than the pulsed switching current. 

Third is thermal. A regulator dissipates output power times one over efficiency minus one, so 10 watts out at 90% efficiency is about 1.1 watts. I want it on copper with vias, in the airflow, spaced from other hot parts and from temperature-sensitive things like references and crystals.

Fourth is noise. The switch node and the hot loop are the noisiest spots on the board, so I keep the converter and its inductor away from ADCs, RF, sensors and clocks. Magnetic coupling falls roughly as one over distance cubed, so doubling the distance helps a lot.

Last are the practical constraints: the height of the inductor, the board edge, test points and a solid ground plane.

If these conflict, I protect the hot loop and the isolation from sensitive circuits first, then I get the regulator as close to the load as those allow, and I use a POL regulator if a central one can't reach."
:::

::: terms
- **Point-of-load (POL) regulator**: a regulator placed next to the circuit it powers.
- **Intermediate bus**: a higher-voltage rail distributed to POL regulators.
- **Hot loop**: the high-di/dt loop of input capacitor and switches in a switching regulator. See [[q0548]].
- **Switch node**: the node between the high-side and low-side switches and the inductor; high dV/dt.
- **IR drop**: $I\cdot R$ lost in the wiring between supply and load.
- **Keep-out / partition**: a board region reserved to separate noisy and sensitive circuits.
- **Remote sense**: sensing the output voltage at the load to compensate for wiring drop.
- **Thermal derating**: current reduction at high temperature.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "close to the load" and maybe "away from noise."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Lists several forces and ranks them, and says what wins on conflict.
- Quantifies drop (e.g. 190 mV at 1 V/10 A over 20 mm) and heat (1.1 W at 90% for 10 W).
- Treats the input loop and the output path separately and says where the long run belongs.
- Distinguishes capacitive and magnetic noise coupling and uses distance, orientation and ground planes.
- Knows POL vs centralised architectures and where each fits.
- Mentions mechanical, testing and rework needs.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Optimising for "close to the load" and ignoring noise.** A regulator next to the ADC's reference can ruin the measurement. Check both.
- **Placing it close to the load but far from the input capacitor.** The hot loop then grows, and EMI and ringing follow.
- **Forgetting heat.** A 2 W regulator in the corner of a tight enclosure with no copper will derate or shut down.
- **Letting power-stage current cross sensitive grounds.** A noisy return passing under an ADC injects error. See [[q0495]].
- **Placing tall inductors where they block airflow or exceed enclosure height.**
- **No test access.** A regulator you can't probe is hard to debug; add test points and a ground spring location near the output.
- **Assuming one central regulator suits every rail.** Low-voltage, high-current rails often need POL.
:::

::: deep
## A ranking of forces

Imagine the board as a map and the regulator as a piece you have to put down. Six forces push on it:

| Force | Pulls the regulator towards | Cost of ignoring it |
|---|---|---|
| IR drop and inductance | the load | Brown-outs, droop, poor regulation |
| Input hot loop | the input capacitor (always adjacent) | EMI, ringing, FET stress |
| Input current path | the connector | Pulsed current across the board |
| Heat | airflow, copper, edges | Derating, shutdown, drift of neighbours |
| Noise | away from sensitive circuits | ADC error, RF desense, jitter |
| Mechanical/test | clearance, access | Rework, no probing |

## Distance to the load: quantify it

Resistive drop (supply plus return) over a path of $n$ squares each way is $\Delta V = 2 n R_\square I$. For 1 oz copper, a 1 mm trace 20 mm long is 20 squares = 9.6 mΩ each way, so 19.2 mΩ at 10 A is 0.19 V, 19% of a 1 V rail. Planes cut this by an order of magnitude ([[q0535]]), but inductance remains: 10 nH with a 5 A step in 100 ns is 0.5 V. So distance affects both DC regulation and transient droop. See [[q0543]] and [[q0544]].

The regulator's feedback typically senses at its own output capacitor, so it cannot correct for drop *between* the capacitor and the load, only before it. Closer placement shrinks the uncorrected portion. When distance is unavoidable, use remote sense, a wider pour, or POL regulation.

## Keep the input hot loop adjacent

In a buck, the input capacitor, high-side FET and low-side FET form a loop that carries trapezoidal current pulses at the switching frequency. Its inductance drives overshoot at the switch node ($V = L\,di/dt$). Therefore the input capacitor goes right at the VIN and PGND pins ([[q0548]]), regardless of where the connector is. This sets a hard constraint on placement: the regulator, input capacitor and switches are one tiny module that you then place as a unit.

The long run between the connector and the module then carries mostly DC with only a small ripple (the input capacitor filters the pulses), which is a much better signal to run for tens of millimetres than a pulsed switch current.

## Heat

A regulator's loss is $P_{diss} = P_{out}(1/\eta - 1)$. 10 W at 90% is 1.1 W; at 80% it's 2.5 W. A buck with a 3 mm exposed pad and a good pour handles 1 W or two on a few-cm board, but only if the copper isn't shared with a sensitive part. Put it where it has copper, vias to inner layers, airflow and the board edge ([[q0540]]), and away from references, crystals, batteries and electrolytic capacitors.

## Noise

The switch node swings by $V_{in}$ in a few nanoseconds, an electric-field source; the hot loop carries tens of amps per microsecond, a magnetic-field source. Both couple to nearby circuits, and both fall off with distance: near-field magnetic coupling from a small loop goes roughly as $1/r^3$, so 2x distance cuts it about 18 dB; capacitive coupling falls with the area facing the victim and with a ground plane between.

Practical consequences:
- Keep the inductor, switch node and hot loop away from ADC inputs, references, RF front ends, crystals, microphones and sensor front ends. The inductor's stray field is a classic culprit; shielded inductors help.
- Do not route sensitive traces under or near the inductor or switch node.
- Orient two inductors on a board at 90° or keep them apart to reduce mutual coupling.
- Synchronise multiple converters or space their frequencies so beat notes stay out of band.
- Keep return currents of the power stage on the power ground and join it to the quiet ground at one point ([[q0495]]).

## Central vs point-of-load architectures

| | Central regulator | POL regulators |
|---|---|---|
| Layout | One block, easy to shield and cool | Several blocks near loads |
| Drop to load | Long runs, high | Short runs, low |
| Noise | One source, but long distribution | Several sources, each local |
| Regulation | Worse at the load | Better at the load |
| Cost and area | Lower | Higher, plus an intermediate bus |
| Best for | Low-current rails, simple boards | High-current/low-voltage cores, FPGAs, sensitive analog |

A common pattern: a 12 V or 5 V intermediate rail comes in from the connector, and a small buck or LDO sits next to each load. LDOs placed after a switcher at the analog block provide a quiet rail ([[q0158]]).

## Mechanical and test

- Inductors and electrolytic capacitors are the tall parts; check enclosure height and airflow.
- Place the regulator so the heat sink or enclosure contact area is accessible if one is needed ([[q0541]]).
- Keep test points for input, output, switch node (with a short ground return for probing) and the feedback node; leave room for a scope probe ground spring.
- Think about rework: a regulator with a bottom pad is harder to replace; keep neighbours at least a few mm clear for hot-air tools.

## A short procedure

1. List rails: voltage, current, noise sensitivity and loads.
2. Decide architecture (central, POL, hybrid).
3. Place connector and input protection, then regulators near their loads, in the power-flow order.
4. Draw the hot loop and the keep-out; move sensitive blocks out of it.
5. Check copper area, vias and airflow for each regulator.
6. Route returns as planes; confirm no sensitive return crosses a power return.
7. Review drop and transient for each rail before routing.
:::

::: applications
- **FPGA and processor boards** with several POL buck converters, each placed beside the pins of the rail it feeds.
- **Sensor and data-acquisition boards** where switchers sit near the connector and a quiet LDO sits next to the ADC.
- **USB-powered devices**, with the input protection and a buck near the connector, and LDOs near the RF section.
- **Motor-control boards**, where the gate driver supply and logic supply sit away from the power stage but near their loads.
- **Battery-powered IoT designs**, with an efficient buck-boost next to the battery connector and the radio on the far side with its own filtered rail.
:::

::: assumptions
The answer assumes a switching regulator on a multilayer PCB feeding digital or mixed-signal loads from a single input connector. For a linear regulator, placement is driven more by heat than by switching noise (it has none), but dissipation is higher so thermal comes first. For very small, low-current boards (a few mA) the drop and noise arguments weaken and convenience can win. For high-voltage or isolated supplies, creepage and the isolation barrier add constraints. An alternative valid answer lists "close to the load" as the single most important consideration, then discusses the rest as secondary; both are fine so long as the trade-offs are explained.
:::

::: followups
### Why not place the regulator right at the connector and run power across the board?
Because the long run sits between the regulator and the load, where the current is highest and the transients are fastest. You'd have drop and inductance on the regulated side, and the regulator can't compensate for them. The long run should be the input side, which carries steady current, or use POL regulators at the loads.

### How would you place an LDO that powers an ADC reference?
Close to the ADC, with its input and output capacitors at its pins, and its input fed from the switcher through a filter or a ferrite bead. Heat is small for a few mA, but I'd still keep it away from the switcher's inductor and the hot loop and put it on the quiet side of the board.

### What if the enclosure forces the regulator far from the load?
I would widen the supply and return into pours or planes, add output capacitance at the load, use remote sense to the regulator's feedback so it compensates for drop, and check transient droop with the added inductance. If the distance still costs too much, I'd move to a higher intermediate voltage and a POL stage at the load.

### How does placement change for a multiphase regulator?
Each phase has its own inductor and switches and the phases must be near the load and near each other for current sharing and matched loop impedances. The cluster is bigger so heat spreads, but the layout must keep phase paths symmetric and short. See [[q0236]].

### Could a regulator be too close to the load?
Yes, if the proximity exposes the load to switch-node or inductor noise (for example an RF front end or a precision ADC), or if the heat degrades it. Then I would add distance or shielding and use a quiet LDO or filter for the sensitive load. The best placement is as close as the noise and heat budget allow.
:::
