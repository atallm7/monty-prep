::: basics
### In plain words
A buck converter wastes a little power as heat in almost every part. Each part also shapes the ripple on the output. Going part by part shows where the heat comes from and which part controls which ripple. This is how you find what to fix.

### Picture it
Think of a water delivery system with a pump, valves, long pipes and a storage tank. Every valve and pipe leaks or resists a little, so some energy is lost at each stage. The tank decides how steady the final flow is. You can ask of each piece two questions: how much does it waste, and how does it affect the smoothness? The analogy is loose on one point: in a buck, a part wastes power in two different ways (while on, and while changing state).

### Words you need
- **Conduction loss**: power lost as heat because current passes through a resistance, $I^2R$.
- **Switching loss**: energy lost each time a switch turns on or off, so it grows with frequency.
- **DCR**: the DC resistance of the inductor winding.
- **ESR**: the small resistance inside a capacitor.
- **Efficiency**: output power divided by input power ([[q0211]]).

### The simplest example
A 12 V to 3.3 V buck delivers 2 A, so the output power is $3.3 \times 2 = 6.6$ W. Suppose the parts add up to 0.46 W of loss: the switches (FETs) and the inductor take most of it. The input power is 6.6 + 0.46 = 7.06 W, so efficiency is $6.6/7.06 \approx 93.5\%$. The output ripple, meanwhile, comes from the inductor and output capacitor, not from the FETs. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page assigns a loss number and a ripple role to each component and shows the trade-offs between them.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can hold the **whole buck in your head at once** and say, for every part on the schematic, what it costs in efficiency, what it does to ripple, and what you give up by "improving" it. The interviewer is looking for:

1. A systematic walk through input capacitor, high-side FET, low-side FET (or diode), driver/controller, inductor and output capacitor, with the loss mechanism and the ripple mechanism of each.
2. Numbers for a concrete design so the ranking is believable (which parts dominate the loss, which parts set the ripple, and which do both).
3. Awareness of the coupled trade-offs (low $R_{DS(on)}$ vs high $Q_g$; big $L$ vs high DCR and slow transient; ESR vs capacitance).
:::

::: why
In a design review nobody asks "what is the efficiency of a buck": they ask "why is this one 91% and what would you change?" You answer by attributing the loss to components, and the ripple problem to the one or two parts that set it. This question also leads straight to Q214 (improve efficiency), Q215 (reduce ripple) and Q217 (what loses the most), so a good answer here is the map for all three.
:::

::: concepts
- **Conduction loss**: $I_{rms}^2R$ in FETs, inductor DCR and capacitor ESR ([[q0211]]).
- **Switching loss**: overlap $\tfrac12V I(t_r+t_f)f$, $C_{oss}V^2f$, $Q_{rr}Vf$ ([[q0203]]).
- **Gate-drive loss**: $Q_gV_{drv}f$ per FET.
- **Core loss and DCR** of the inductor ([[q0212]]).
- **Ripple mechanism**: $\Delta I_L=V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf)$ and $\Delta V=\Delta I/(8fC)+\Delta I\cdot ESR$.
- **Parasitics**: ESL, loop inductance and capacitance, which create high-frequency spikes.
:::

::: answer
"I go around the schematic and ask each part two questions: what does it dissipate, and what does it do to ripple.

The input capacitor carries the pulsed input current: RMS about $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$, so its ESR loss is small but its ESR and ESL set the input ripple and the spike on the switch-loop. The high-side FET has conduction loss $D\,I^2R_{DS(on)}$ and the biggest switching loss, because it turns on and off against the full voltage. The low-side FET conducts for $(1-D)$ so it has the largest conduction loss, plus dead-time and reverse-recovery losses. The driver and controller add gate-drive loss, $Q_gVf$, and quiescent current, which matter at light load.

The inductor loses power in its winding resistance, $I^2\cdot DCR$, and in its core, and its value sets the ripple current. The output capacitor barely affects efficiency, because the ripple current through its ESR is small, but it sets the output ripple through $C$ and ESR.

In a 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, 500 kHz example that works out to about 460 milliwatts of loss: the low-side FET about 180, the high-side about 140, the inductor about 120, and everything else small. So efficiency is about 93.5%, and the output ripple, about 17 mV, comes from the inductor and output capacitor, not from the FETs. The design tension is that making the FETs lower-resistance makes them bigger with more gate charge, making $L$ bigger lowers ripple but raises DCR and slows the transient."
:::

::: long
"I'll walk the schematic and for each part say what it costs and what it does to ripple, using a 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, 500 kHz example.

The input capacitor sees the pulsed input current. Its RMS ripple current is about $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$, here 0.9 A, so with a few milliohms of ESR the heat is a couple of milliwatts: negligible for efficiency, but its capacitance and ESR set the input voltage ripple, and its placement sets the switch-loop inductance and the spike on the node.

The high-side FET conducts for $D$, so conduction loss is $D\,I_{rms}^2R_{DS(on)}$, about 70 milliwatts for 60 milliohms. It also does the hard switching, overlap loss $\tfrac12V_{in}I\,t\,f$, about 60 milliwatts, so it's the part where you want low gate charge more than the lowest resistance.

The low-side FET conducts $(1-D)$, so it has the largest conduction loss, about 120 milliwatts, and it adds dead-time diode loss and reverse recovery, together about 50 milliwatts.

The inductor loses $I^2\cdot DCR$, about 80 milliwatts, plus some core loss, and its value sets the ripple current $V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf)$. The output capacitor has almost no efficiency effect, about half a milliwatt in ESR, but sets the voltage ripple: $\Delta I/(8fC)$ plus $\Delta I\cdot ESR$, here about 17 millivolts. The driver adds gate-drive loss and about 12 milliwatts of quiescent current.

Total is about 460 milliwatts for a 93.5% converter. And the couplings: a lower-resistance FET is a bigger die with more $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$; a bigger $L$ lowers ripple but raises DCR and slows the transient; higher frequency shrinks $L$ and $C$ but raises switching loss. So I'd choose by the load: heavy load, optimize conduction; light load, switching and quiescent."
:::

::: terms
- **Conduction loss**: $I_{rms}^2R$ in on-state resistance.
- **Switching (overlap) loss**: $\tfrac12 V I(t_r+t_f)f$ during transitions.
- **$C_{oss}$ loss**: energy $\tfrac12C_{oss}V^2$ dissipated each cycle.
- **Gate-drive loss**: $Q_gV_{drv}f$.
- **DCR**: inductor winding DC resistance.
- **Core loss**: hysteresis and eddy losses in the magnetic core, depends on flux swing and frequency.
- **ESR / ESL**: capacitor equivalent series resistance and inductance.
- **Loop inductance**: parasitic inductance of the switching loop, creates ringing.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: lists components and says "FETs have conduction and switching loss, the inductor has DCR, caps have ESR".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies a reference design (about 457 mW, 93.5%) and ranks parts, noting that the low-side FET and inductor dominate at heavy load.
- Separates ripple (set by $L$, $C$, ESR, $f$) from spikes (set by edges, ESL, loop inductance) and from efficiency.
- Names the coupled trade-offs for each part (FET area vs $Q_g$; $L$ vs DCR vs saturation vs transient; ESR vs $C$ vs derating).
- Notes the input capacitor's RMS current and its role in ringing, and that output-cap ESR loss is small yet ESR sets ripple.
- Says how the ranking shifts with load, $V_{in}$ and $f_{sw}$ ([[q0217]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating the output capacitor as a loss component.** Its ESR loss is below 1 mW here; it matters for ripple and transients.
- **Attributing all switching loss to both FETs equally.** The high side hard-switches; the low side switches near zero voltage but has dead-time and recovery effects.
- **Ignoring the inductor.** DCR plus core loss is typically one of the top two loss sources.
- **Optimizing $R_{DS(on)}$ alone.** Lower $R_{DS(on)}$ raises $Q_g$, $C_{oss}$ and cost; the best choice depends on duty and frequency ([[q0205]]).
- **Mixing up ripple and noise.** The triangular ripple (inductor and cap) is low-frequency; spikes come from ESL and loop inductance.
:::

## Component by component

### Input capacitor $C_{in}$

- **Current**: pulsed; RMS $=I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}=0.89$ A.
- **Efficiency**: $I_{rms}^2\,ESR=0.8\times3\text{ mΩ}=2.4$ mW. Negligible directly, but a poor input capacitor (high ESL or far from the FETs) increases the switch-loop inductance, causing ringing, overshoot, extra $C_{oss}$/$Q_{rr}$ effects and EMI.
- **Ripple**: input voltage ripple $\approx I_{out}D(1-D)/(f\,C_{in})$ plus $I\cdot ESR$ and a spike $ESL\cdot di/dt$. It is the input-side noise source.

### High-side FET $Q_1$

- **Conduction**: $D\,I_{rms}^2R_H=0.275\times4.086\times0.06=67$ mW.
- **Switching**: hard turn-on and turn-off at $V_{in}$: $\tfrac12V_{in}f\,(I_{valley}t_r+I_{pk}t_f)=60$ mW, dominated by the transition time (set by $Q_{gd}$ and gate current).
- **Other**: $\tfrac12C_{oss}V^2f$ and half of the gate-drive loss.
- **Ripple**: none at the low frequency; its edge rate sets the spike amplitude and EMI.
- **Trade-off**: bigger die lowers $R_{DS(on)}$ but raises $Q_{gd}$, $C_{oss}$: the high side is usually optimized for $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{gd}$ ([[q0205]]).

### Low-side FET $Q_2$

- **Conduction**: $(1-D)\,I_{rms}^2R_L=0.725\times4.086\times0.04=119$ mW: the biggest single term, because it conducts 72.5% of the time.
- **Dead time and recovery**: $V_{SD}It_df=22$ mW and $Q_{rr}V_{in}f=30$ mW ([[q0209]]).
- **Ripple**: none directly; recovery spike and ringing affect high-frequency noise.
- **Trade-off**: optimize for low $R_{DS(on)}$ and low $Q_{rr}$; with a diode (async), loss is $(1-D)I_{out}V_f=0.65$ W instead of about 0.17 W ([[q0201]]).

### Gate driver and controller

- **Gate drive**: $2Q_gV_{drv}f=15$ mW; scales with $f$ and gate voltage.
- **Quiescent current**: $I_qV_{in}=12$ mW: independent of load, so it dominates at light load.
- **Dead time and edge speed**: set the dead-time loss and switching loss ([[q0207]]).
- **Ripple**: modulator type (PFM vs PWM), jitter and load-regulation accuracy all affect low-frequency ripple and noise; a controller in pulse-skipping mode gives bursts with larger ripple.

### Inductor $L$

- **Conduction**: $I_{rms}^2\,DCR=4.086\times0.02=82$ mW.
- **Core loss**: depends on flux swing $\propto\Delta I_L$ and frequency; assumed 40 mW. Powder-iron cores have higher core loss than ferrite but soft saturation.
- **Ripple**: sets $\Delta I_L=V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf)$: the base of every ripple number.
- **Trade-off**: higher $L$ lowers ripple and core loss but typically raises DCR (more turns or a larger part) and slows the transient. Saturation current matters ([[q0212]]).

### Output capacitor $C_{out}$

- **Efficiency**: ripple current in the ESR: $\Delta I^2/12\cdot ESR=0.43$ mW. Effectively zero.
- **Ripple**: $\Delta V=\dfrac{\Delta I}{8fC}+\Delta I\cdot ESR=11.6+5.1\approx16.7$ mV. Also sets transient droop/overshoot.
- **Trade-off**: more capacitors cost area and cost; ceramics lose capacitance under DC bias; electrolytics have large ESR; ESL matters for fast loads.

### Everything else

- **PCB copper**: 10 mΩ of trace/via resistance in the power path costs $I_{rms}^2\times0.01=41$ mW; layout adds loss and noise.
- **Feedback divider**: $V_{out}/(R_1+R_2)$ of a few µA to hundreds of µA; sets output accuracy and noise pickup.
- **Current sense**: shunt loss $I^2R_{sense}$ if used.

## The couplings (what you give up)

| Change | Efficiency | Ripple / other |
|---|---|---|
| Lower $R_{DS(on)}$ (bigger FET) | conduction down; $Q_g$, $C_{oss}$ up | no direct ripple change; slower edges for same drive |
| Bigger $L$ | core loss down, DCR up, flat net | ripple down, transient slower |
| Smaller $L$, same $f$ | DCR down but ripple current and RMS up | ripple up, more peak current |
| Higher $f$ | switching loss up | $L$ and $C$ can shrink; ripple down for same parts |
| More $C_{out}$ | none | ripple down, bigger |
| Lower ESR | negligible loss change | ripple down, more cost |
| Slower edges | switching loss up | EMI and ringing down |

Numbers from the model: dropping the low-side resistance from 40 to 20 mΩ while raising its gate charge and $C_{oss}$ by 50% (and $Q_{rr}$ to 8 nC) still gains 0.4 points; making $L$ 10 µH with 30 mΩ of DCR changes efficiency by only $-0.06$ points but halves the ripple current; slowing the edges to 10 ns costs 0.8 points.

## What dominates depends on the operating point

At 2 A the big three are low-side conduction, high-side loss and the inductor. At 0.2 A the same design drops to about 84% because the fixed losses (switching, gate, dead time, $Q_{rr}$, $I_q$ about 130 mW) divide into a tiny output power. At 24 V in, $V_{in}$-proportional terms (switching, $Q_{rr}$, $C_{oss}$) grow. [[q0217]] ranks the sources across operating conditions.

::: applications
- **Selecting a buck IC or controller plus external FETs**: using the loss budget to decide which parts to upgrade.
- **Thermal design**: attributing watts to parts to size copper and airflow.
- **Design reviews and efficiency debugging**: comparing measured loss to the budget to find the part that is out of line.
- **Cost optimization**: choosing which parts can be cheaper without a large penalty (output capacitor ESR, for example).
- **Battery-powered products**: knowing that quiescent current and gate-drive loss dominate at standby.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM, fixed 500 kHz, synchronous** with silicon FETs; the table shows one operating point and one set of assumed parts.
- **Linearized loss models**; real $R_{DS(on)}$ varies with temperature (about +50 to 100% at 125 °C), $Q_{rr}$ and edges vary with $di/dt$.
- **Core loss** is assumed (40 mW) and flagged; it can easily be half or double depending on the inductor.
- **Capacitor ESR** is treated as frequency independent.
- **Ripple numbers** assume ideal triangular currents and nominal (not derated) capacitance.
- At **different operating points** the ranking changes; the table is a snapshot, not a rule.
:::

::: followups
### If you could upgrade only one component, which would it be here and why?
The low-side FET: it has the largest single loss (about 180 mW including dead time and recovery). Halving its resistance, even with higher $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$, gains about 0.4 points (0.8 points if gate charge doesn't rise). The next candidate is the inductor's DCR (halving it gains about 0.5 points but usually means a larger part).

### Does a bigger output capacitor improve efficiency?
Essentially no: the ripple-current ESR loss is below 1 mW. It lowers output ripple and improves the load transient. The only efficiency effect is indirect: with a bigger $C$ you can accept a bigger $\Delta I_L$ (smaller $L$ with lower DCR), or lower frequency.

### Which components affect ripple but not efficiency?
The output capacitor's capacitance and ESR (ripple voltage), and the output filter. The input capacitor affects input ripple and EMI. The inductor affects both, since its value sets the ripple current and its DCR and core set the loss.

### The SW node and output show 80 mV spikes at the switching edges but the 500 kHz ripple is fine. Which components would you suspect?
The high-frequency spikes come from edge rates and parasitics, not from $L$ and $C$ values: loop inductance in the input-capacitor/FET loop, the ESL of the output capacitor, the inductor's winding capacitance, and the recovery of the body diode. Fix with a better layout, a local small ceramic, slower edges or a snubber ([[q0209]]).

### How does the budget change at 0.2 A?
Conduction terms scale with $I^2$ and shrink to a few milliwatts; fixed terms (switching, gate drive, dead time, recovery, $I_q$) stay near 130 mW. Efficiency falls to about 84%. A PFM mode or diode emulation reduces those fixed terms by lowering the effective frequency ([[q0203]]).
:::
