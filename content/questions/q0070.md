::: basics
### In plain words
Mobility tells you how quickly a carrier moves when pushed by an electric field. In silicon, electrons are more mobile than holes: about 1350 versus 480 in the same units, a ratio of roughly 2.8. That one fact has big practical effects. N-type devices are faster and conduct better than P-type devices of the same size.

### Picture it
Picture two runners crossing a field. The electron runs a smooth, simple track. The hole has to move by a more crowded route, where neighbors keep passing the gap along, and it ends up slower. Same push, different path, so a different speed. The analogy is only a picture: a hole is really an empty spot, and its "heaviness" comes from the way the silicon's energy bands are shaped.

### Words you need
- **Mobility ($\mu$)**: how fast a carrier drifts per unit of field. See [[q0068]].
- **Effective mass**: how heavy a carrier acts when pushed. Heavier means slower.
- **Majority carrier**: the plentiful carrier type. See [[q0069]].
- **NMOS and PMOS**: transistors whose current is carried by electrons or by holes.
- **Drive current**: how much current a transistor can supply when on.

### The simplest example
Say an NMOS transistor whose conducting channel is drawn 1 µm wide gives some amount of current. A PMOS of the same width carries holes, so it gives only about 1/2.8 as much. To match the NMOS, you make the PMOS about 2.8 times wider, so roughly 2.8 µm. In practice designers use about two to three times. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page explains why holes are heavier, and shows the ratios in other materials, such as about 20 in GaAs and about 2 in germanium.
:::

::: testing
The one-word answer is "electrons". The interview value is in the *why* and the *so what*:

1. Electrons are more mobile in silicon (about 1350 vs 480 cm²/V·s), roughly 2.8x.
2. The reason is band structure: effective mass and the way the valence band mixes heavy and light holes, which gives more scattering paths.
3. The consequence in circuits: PMOS is made wider than NMOS, NPN is faster than PNP, N-type resistors beat P-type.
4. Caveats: the ratio depends on the material (about 20x in GaAs, only 2x in Ge) and shrinks in short channels because of velocity saturation and strain engineering.
:::

::: why
This single ratio shapes digital and analogue design. It's why CMOS inverters use PMOS devices two to three times wider than NMOS, why integrated PNP transistors are slower with lower gain than NPN, why N-type wafers and N-channel power MOSFETs are the default, and why III-V materials with huge electron mobility dominate RF. It also drives process technology: strained silicon and SiGe channels exist largely to close the hole-versus-electron gap.
:::

::: concepts
- **Mobility**: drift velocity per unit field, $\mu=q\tau/m^*$. See [[q0068]].
- **Effective mass ($m^*$)**: how the band curvature makes a carrier respond to a force as if it had a different mass.
- **Band structure**: conduction-band minima for electrons; valence-band maxima (heavy hole, light hole, split-off) for holes.
- **Majority carriers in N and P material**: see [[q0069]].
- **CMOS sizing**: drive current is proportional to $\mu\,C_{ox}W/L$, so lower mobility needs more width.
:::

::: answer
"Electrons are more mobile than holes. In silicon, about 1350 cm²/V·s for electrons versus about 480 for holes at room temperature in lightly doped material, a ratio of roughly 2.8.

The reason is that mobility is $q\tau/m^*$, so it depends on the scattering time and the effective mass. Electrons in the conduction band have a lighter conductivity effective mass, about 0.26 times the free electron mass, and a single simple band to move in. Holes live in the valence band, which has a heavy-hole band and a light-hole band that are degenerate at the top, plus a split-off band nearby. The heavy-hole band has a larger effective mass, and holes can scatter between these bands, so the average is both heavier and scatters more.

The practical result is that in CMOS the PMOS is made roughly two to three times wider than the NMOS to match the drive current. NPN transistors are faster and have more gain than PNP of the same process, and N-type resistors have lower resistance than P-type at the same doping. In other materials the ratio changes: it's about 20 in GaAs, and only about 2 in germanium."
:::

::: technical
**Values (300 K, lightly doped):** Si $\mu_n\approx1350$, $\mu_p\approx480$ cm²/V·s (ratio 2.8); Ge 3900 and 1900 (ratio 2.1); GaAs ~8500 and ~400 (ratio ~20); values vary with source.

**Why electrons are faster.**
$$\mu=\frac{q\tau}{m^*}$$
- Silicon conduction band: six equivalent ellipsoidal valleys with longitudinal mass 0.92 $m_0$ and transverse mass 0.19 $m_0$; the *conductivity* effective mass averages to $\approx0.26\,m_0$.
- Silicon valence band: heavy-hole ($m^*\approx0.5\,m_0$) and light-hole ($\approx0.16\,m_0$) bands degenerate at $k=0$, plus a split-off band about 44 meV lower. The two bands mix, so the effective conductivity mass is about 0.37 $m_0$ in common estimates, and the many available final states increase interband scattering (shorter $\tau$).

Mass alone gives $0.37/0.26=1.4\times$. The rest of the factor of 2.8 comes from the shorter scattering time for holes.

**Einstein relation.** $D=\mu kT/q$, so electron diffusivity is also higher: 35 versus 12.4 cm²/s.

**High field.** Velocity saturates near $10^7$ cm/s for electrons and perhaps $0.8\times10^7$ cm/s for holes (model-dependent), so in short-channel devices the electron advantage shrinks from about 2.8x toward 1.2-2x.

**Circuit consequences.** Saturated MOSFET current is $I_D=\tfrac12\mu C_{ox}\frac WL(V_{GS}-V_T)^2$ (long channel). For equal current and length, $W_p/W_n\approx\mu_n/\mu_p$ (about 2-3 in older nodes; closer to 1-2 in modern strained processes).
:::

::: long
"Electrons are more mobile than holes in silicon. At room temperature the numbers are about 1350 cm²/V·s for electrons and 480 for holes in lightly doped silicon, so electrons are around three times faster.

To explain why, I go back to mobility equals $q\tau/m^*$. Two things matter: the effective mass and the scattering time. Electrons move in the conduction band, where the effective conductivity mass is around 0.26 times the free electron mass. Holes are in the valence band, which is more complicated: at the top there are two degenerate bands, a heavy-hole band and a light-hole band, plus a split-off band a few tens of meV below. Heavy holes have an effective mass of about half the free electron mass, and holes can scatter between these bands, which reduces their scattering time. So holes are heavier on average and get scattered more.

The consequences show up in circuits. In a CMOS inverter, a PMOS of the same W/L as the NMOS only delivers about a third of the current, so the pull-up is slower than the pull-down and the switching threshold moves down. To balance, the PMOS is made two to three times wider in older processes. With strained silicon and in short channels where velocity saturates, the ratio is smaller, roughly one to two. Similarly, an NPN has faster transit time and higher gain than a PNP in the same process because the minority carriers crossing the base are electrons in an NPN. And N-type resistors and N-channel power MOSFETs have lower resistance than the P-type equivalents at the same size and doping.

It's not universal. In germanium the ratio is about two, and in GaAs it's about twenty because the hole mobility is poor. This is why GaAs and other III-V devices are built using electron transport (HEMTs, HBTs with electron-based gain), and why a good p-channel device in those materials is hard. Engineers close the gap in silicon with strain: compressive strain in the PMOS channel raises hole mobility by tens of percent."
:::

::: terms
- **Mobility ($\mu$)**: drift velocity per unit electric field.
- **Effective mass ($m^*$)**: the apparent mass of a carrier derived from band curvature, $1/m^*=\frac{1}{\hbar^2}\frac{d^2E}{dk^2}$.
- **Heavy hole / light hole**: two valence-band branches degenerate at the band top, with different curvature.
- **Split-off band**: a third valence band lowered by spin-orbit coupling (about 44 meV in Si).
- **Conductivity effective mass**: the averaged mass that gives the correct mobility when the band is anisotropic or multi-valley.
- **Strain engineering**: applying mechanical stress to the channel to modify band structure and raise mobility.
- **$\beta_n/\beta_p$ ratio**: ratio of NMOS to PMOS transconductance parameters, set by mobility and $W/L$.
- **Velocity saturation**: limit on drift velocity at high field, which reduces the mobility advantage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "electrons are more mobile because they're lighter" and that PMOS is bigger.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quotes numbers (1350 vs 480, ratio 2.8) and the formula $\mu=q\tau/m^*$.
- Explains the band-structure reason (heavy/light hole bands, split-off band, interband scattering), not just "holes are heavier."
- Gives circuit consequences with numbers: PMOS sizing, switching-threshold shift, NPN vs PNP speed.
- Notes the ratio isn't universal: ~2 in Ge, ~20 in GaAs, and shrinks in short channels (velocity saturation) and with strain.
- Mentions the inversion-layer mobilities differ from bulk, so layout ratios use measured process values.
- Mentions that holes aren't always slower in every sense: in strained Ge channels hole mobility can exceed that of electrons in silicon.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Holes are slower because they are positive."** Charge sign is irrelevant to mobility. It is the band curvature and scattering.
- **"A hole is a heavy atom moving."** A hole is a collective vacancy motion of valence electrons; its heaviness is an effective mass from the band structure.
- **"The ratio is always 2.8."** It is for bulk silicon at low field. It is different in other materials, in MOS inversion layers, at high field and with strain.
- **Using 2.8 to size PMOS in a modern process.** Real PDKs give the ratio; in nanometre nodes it is often near 1.2-2.
- **Thinking mobility alone sets switching speed.** In short channels it is saturation velocity and capacitance that set speed.
- **Swapping the answer in PNP/NPN discussions.** In an NPN the minority carriers in the base are electrons (fast). That's why NPNs are generally the faster, higher-gain device.
:::

## Where the mobility comes from: band curvature

In a crystal, a carrier's energy depends on its crystal momentum $k$. Near a band extremum the curve is approximately a parabola, and the curvature defines an effective mass:
$$E(k)=E_0\pm\frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m^*}$$
A sharply curved parabola means a light carrier that accelerates easily; a flat one means a heavy carrier.

The flat heavy-hole curve corresponds to a large effective mass; holes populate it heavily because it has a larger density of states, and they scatter readily between branches and into the nearby split-off band. The parabolic picture is only a rough sketch; real silicon has six anisotropic conduction valleys and warped valence bands.

Silicon specifics:
- **Conduction band:** six equivalent valleys along the $\langle100\rangle$ directions, each ellipsoidal with longitudinal mass $m_l=0.92\,m_0$ and transverse mass $m_t=0.19\,m_0$. The conductivity mass is $3/(1/m_l+2/m_t)=0.26\,m_0$.
- **Valence band:** heavy- and light-hole bands degenerate at $k=0$ and warped, plus a split-off band. The effective conductivity mass is about 0.37 $m_0$, from the weighted combination of heavy and light holes.

Mass ratio alone gives $0.37/0.26\approx1.4$. Hole scattering is roughly 2x stronger: more final states to scatter into, and interband scattering between the heavy and light bands. Together: $1.4\times2\approx2.8$ (a rough decomposition to show where the factor comes from, not a precise calculation).

## Scale of the effect: CMOS inverter

A CMOS inverter wants equal pull-up and pull-down strength. With equal $W/L$ the NMOS-to-PMOS drive ratio equals $\mu_n/\mu_p\approx2.8$ in bulk (inversion-layer ratios are often 2-3 in older nodes), so the PMOS is typically widened to $W_p\approx2$-$3\,W_n$.

The switching threshold (where $V_{in}=V_{out}$) is
$$V_M=\frac{V_{Tn}+\sqrt{k_p/k_n}\,(V_{DD}-|V_{Tp}|)}{1+\sqrt{k_p/k_n}}$$
With $V_{DD}=1.8$ V, $V_{Tn}=|V_{Tp}|=0.5$ V: for $k_p/k_n=1$, $V_M=0.9$ V; for $k_p/k_n=1/2.8=0.36$ (equal widths, bulk mobilities) $V_M=0.80$ V. The inverter threshold moves 100 mV low, and the rising output edge is about 2.8x slower than the falling edge.

## Bipolar transistors: NPN vs PNP

In an NPN the base minority carriers are electrons; in a PNP they are holes. The base transit time $\tau_B\approx W_B^2/(2D)$ (uniform base), so a 3x higher $D$ gives 3x shorter transit time. For a base width of 0.1 µm: $\tau_{B,n}=\frac{(10^{-5})^2}{2\times35}=1.4$ ps while $\tau_{B,p}=\frac{(10^{-5})^2}{2\times12.4}=4.0$ ps (idealised numbers; real devices are dominated by other delays). Discrete NPN/PNP pairs like the 2N3904 and 2N3906 show the same trend, with typical transition frequencies a bit higher for the NPN (check the datasheets for exact figures). See [[q0086]].

## Do the advantages change in short channels?

Yes. In modern MOSFETs two effects narrow the gap:

1. **Velocity saturation.** Current is proportional to $W C_{ox}v_{sat}(V_{GS}-V_T)$ instead of $\mu$; $v_{sat}$ differs by only about 20-30% between holes and electrons, so the NMOS advantage shrinks to about 1.2-2x.
2. **Strain engineering.** SiGe source/drain regions compress the PMOS channel, raising hole mobility by tens of percent, while tensile nitride liners improve NMOS. Process engineers have used this to bring the optimum $W_p/W_n$ ratio closer to 1-1.5 in advanced nodes.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked "why is PMOS bigger than NMOS?" give the mobility ratio, then immediately add the caveat that modern processes use strain and velocity saturation, so the real ratio comes from the PDK and simulation. It shows you know the rule of thumb and where it stops.

## Worked example: the scattering time hiding inside the mobility ratio

The decomposition "mass gives 1.4x, scattering gives 2x" above can be checked directly by inverting $\mu=q\tau/m^*$ for the scattering time $\tau$, using the conductivity masses quoted earlier ($0.26\,m_0$ and $0.37\,m_0$):

$$\tau_n=\frac{\mu_nm^*_n}{q}=\frac{(0.135\ \text{m}^2/(\text{V}\cdot\text{s}))(0.26)(9.109\times10^{-31})}{1.602\times10^{-19}}=2.0\times10^{-13}\ \text{s}$$
$$\tau_p=\frac{(0.048)(0.37)(9.109\times10^{-31})}{1.602\times10^{-19}}=1.0\times10^{-13}\ \text{s}$$

So an electron travels about 0.2 ps between scattering events and a hole about 0.1 ps. The ratio $\tau_p/\tau_n=0.51$ confirms that holes scatter roughly twice as often, and the remaining factor $0.37/0.26=1.4$ is the mass. Multiplying them gives $1.4\times(1/0.51)\approx1.4\times1.98=2.8$, which matches the measured mobility ratio, so the decomposition is self-consistent. These are order-of-magnitude pictures because the conductivity masses come from averaging over anisotropic bands.

The Einstein relation gives the diffusion constants quickly: at 300 K, $kT/q=25.85$ mV, so $D_n=1350\times0.02585=34.9$ cm²/s and $D_p=480\times0.02585=12.4$ cm²/s, the values used in the base-transit-time estimate above.

## Worked example: how velocity saturation shrinks the ratio

The bulk ratio of 2.8 is a low-field number. To see how short channels erode it, use a simple, widely used empirical model for the saturation current,
$$I_{D,sat}=\frac{\tfrac12\mu_{eff}C_{ox}\frac{W}{L}V_{ov}^2}{1+V_{ov}/(E_cL)},\qquad E_c=\frac{2v_{sat}}{\mu_{eff}}$$
where $E_c$ is the critical field at which the carrier velocity has fallen to half its low-field extrapolation. The denominator is 1 when the channel is long (the usual square law) and approaches $V_{ov}/(E_cL)$ in short channels, where the current becomes linear in overdrive and independent of $\mu$.

Choose illustrative numbers: oxide 4 nm thick ($C_{ox}=3.9\varepsilon_0/t_{ox}=8.6$ fF/µm²), overdrive $V_{ov}=0.7$ V, inversion-layer mobilities $\mu_n=300$ and $\mu_p=100$ cm²/V·s (ratio 3, lower than bulk because surface scattering hits both carriers), and saturation velocities $10^7$ cm/s for electrons and $0.8\times10^7$ cm/s for holes. Then $E_{c,n}=6.7\times10^4$ V/cm and $E_{c,p}=1.6\times10^5$ V/cm. Current per micrometer of width:

| Gate length | $I_n$ (µA/µm) | $I_p$ (µA/µm) | $I_n/I_p$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 µm | 57 | 20 | 2.8 |
| 0.18 µm | 223 | 95 | 2.4 |
| 0.05 µm | 409 | 226 | 1.8 |

Two things are visible. First, the ratio falls steadily from near the mobility ratio to well under 2 because both current expressions drift toward $C_{ox}v_{sat}V_{ov}$. Second, the holes saturate later: their critical field is more than twice the electrons', so velocity saturation sets in at a shorter length for holes (below roughly 44 nm in this model) than for electrons (below about 105 nm). Low mobility actually postpones the penalty, which is part of why the PMOS catches up as devices shrink.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Every input here (effective mobilities, $v_{sat}$, oxide thickness, the empirical form of the saturation-current model) is illustrative. Real nanometer devices have series resistance, drain-induced barrier lowering and partly ballistic transport, so the numbers show the trend only. Use the foundry model for design.

## Temperature, layout orientation and other real-hardware knobs

- **Temperature.** Lattice (phonon) scattering makes mobility fall as temperature rises, roughly $\mu\propto T^{-1.5}$ to $T^{-2.4}$ depending on carrier, doping and field. In a CMOS gate that means both rising and falling edges slow down when hot, and at low temperature they speed up. Since the two exponents differ, the optimum $W_p/W_n$ drifts slightly with temperature, but not enough to redesign for; libraries are characterized across corners instead.
- **Crystal orientation.** Hole mobility in an inversion layer is higher on a (110) wafer surface than on the usual (100), while electron mobility is lower there. Hybrid-orientation processes exploit this; the ordinary rule is that the standard (100) wafer favors electrons.
- **Strain.** Compressive uniaxial stress along the channel, introduced with embedded SiGe source/drain, lifts the degeneracy of the valence bands and reduces hole effective mass, which is where the tens-of-percent PMOS gain comes from. Tensile stress helps NMOS by repopulating the lower-mass valleys.
- **Doping.** Mobility falls as doping rises for both carriers (see [[q0068]]), so the absolute numbers in this section apply to lightly doped material and the ratio is usually quoted for that case.

## How you would verify the mobility ratio on the bench

- **Hall measurement** on a van der Pauw sample gives mobility directly as $\mu_H=|R_H|/\rho$ (up to a Hall scattering factor close to 1), so measuring an N-type and a P-type sample with similar doping reproduces the ratio.
- **Transistor extraction.** On an NMOS/PMOS pair with identical $W/L$, measure the saturation current at the same overdrive. In the long-channel regime $I_n/I_p$ equals the effective mobility ratio; repeat on a short-channel pair and watch it shrink, as in the table.
- **Ring oscillator or inverter test.** On a discrete CD4007-style array or a test chip, build an inverter with equal widths and view the output on an oscilloscope. The rising edge should be visibly slower than the falling edge, and the switching threshold on the transfer curve should sit below $V_{DD}/2$. Rebuild with a wider PMOS and see the edges and the threshold re-center. Compare the measured edge times against the $\mu_n/\mu_p$ estimate to calibrate the effective ratio for that device.

::: applications
- **CMOS standard cells**: PMOS widths set 2-3x NMOS (older nodes) to balance rise and fall delays.
- **Analog IC design**: NPN-based bipolar processes with PNP as a slower "lateral" device used sparingly.
- **Power MOSFETs**: N-channel devices give lower $R_{DS(on)}$ per area than P-channel, which is why high-side switches often use an NMOS with a bootstrap/charge pump rather than a PMOS.
- **RF GaAs/InP/GaN**: transistors designed around electron transport (HEMT, HBT) because hole mobility is poor.
- **Strained silicon / SiGe PMOS**: engineered to raise hole mobility in advanced logic.
- **Sensors and Hall elements**: N-type material generally gives higher Hall mobility and sensitivity.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Bulk, low-field, lightly doped silicon at 300 K.** Inversion layers, high fields, heavy doping and temperature change the ratio.
- **Parabolic-band, simple effective masses** for the explanation. Real band structure is anisotropic, non-parabolic and warped.
- **Mobility values** differ by source and measurement (Hall vs drift). I quote typical textbook values.
- **Material dependence.** The answer "electrons" holds for nearly every common semiconductor but the ratio varies from about 2 to more than 20. In strained germanium or some strained Si channels, hole mobility can be unusually high.
- **Valid alternative**: a short version ("electrons, because of lower effective mass and less scattering; hence PMOS is wider") is perfectly fine for a quick screen.

::: followups
### Why is the PMOS usually drawn wider than the NMOS in a CMOS inverter?
Because it delivers less current per unit width: with $\mu_p\approx\mu_n/2.8$ the PMOS pull-up is weaker. To equalise rise and fall delays we widen the PMOS by about the mobility ratio, 2-3x in older processes. Wider PMOS also adds gate and drain capacitance, so some designers pick smaller ratios (1.5-2x) to trade a little symmetry for lower power and area. Modern nodes with strain have ratios closer to 1-1.5x.

### Why does the hole have a larger effective mass if it's just a missing electron?
The effective mass describes how the carrier responds to a force given the band curvature, and the valence band is flatter than the conduction band with two overlapping branches. A "missing electron" at the top of the valence band responds as if it had the mass associated with that band's curvature. The heavy-hole branch has a larger mass than the conduction band electron. The vacancy picture is just bookkeeping; the physics is in the band structure.

### Calculate the ratio of drive currents for equal-size NMOS and PMOS in silicon.
With the same $W/L$, $C_{ox}$ and overdrive, $I_p/I_n=\mu_p/\mu_n=480/1350=0.356$ in the bulk-mobility approximation. Inversion-layer mobilities are lower than bulk (typically a few hundred cm²/V·s), but their ratio is similar, about 2-3x depending on process. Hence the PMOS carries about a third of the current.

### How does the electron/hole mobility ratio differ in GaAs, and what does that imply for circuits?
It's about 20 (8500 vs ~400 cm²/V·s). The very weak holes make P-channel GaAs devices poor, so GaAs logic and RF circuits use only N-type devices (MESFET/HEMT/HBT) in direct-coupled or source-coupled logic rather than complementary logic. It also makes GaAs great for microwave amplifiers where only electron transport is needed.

### In a short-channel device does the electron mobility advantage still hold?
Partly. Both carrier types approach saturation velocity near $10^7$ cm/s, and at the field in a deep-submicron channel the drive current becomes roughly proportional to $v_{sat}$ rather than $\mu$. Since $v_{sat}$ is only about 20-30% higher for electrons, the NMOS to PMOS advantage falls from about 2.8x towards 1.2-2x. Also, in sub-100 nm channels transport is partly ballistic and "mobility" is a poor descriptor.

### Scenario: your CMOS logic has equal NMOS/PMOS widths and the rising edges are slow with duty cycle skew on a clock buffer. What would you do?
The pull-up is weaker by about the hole/electron mobility ratio. Fix by widening the PMOS (typical $W_p/W_n$ of 1.5-3 depending on process) or by using the library's balanced cells. Check with simulation across corners since mobility changes with temperature and process, and review whether the load or edge rate is dominated by the PMOS. Adding PMOS width also raises input capacitance, so the preceding stage must drive it.
