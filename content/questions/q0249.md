::: testing
Whether you know **what to measure and how to say it** in a way another engineer can reproduce. A strong answer covers:

1. **Time-domain metrics** from a load step: peak deviation (undershoot/overshoot), recovery (settling) time to a stated band, ring frequency and damping, and the test conditions (step size, slew rate, repetition, operating point).
2. **Frequency-domain metrics**: loop gain crossover $f_c$, phase margin, gain margin, and closed-loop output impedance $Z_{out}(f)$ against a target impedance.
3. How the two relate and how to measure each (electronic load, probing technique, injection and analyzer).
4. Corners: line, load, temperature and capacitor tolerance.
:::

::: why
"Good transient response" means nothing until it is a number with conditions. Datasheets and processor power-delivery specs state droop in millivolts for a stated step and slew rate, and the validation team signs off on measured loop margins. Interviewers ask this to see if you can specify and verify, not only design.
:::

::: concepts
- **Load-step test**: a current step of stated amplitude $\Delta I$ and slew rate, from a stated base load ([[q0248]]).
- **Peak deviation and recovery time**: the maximum excursion and the time to return to within a band (for example ±1%).
- **Damping**: ring frequency and the ratio of successive peaks ([[q0245]]).
- **Loop gain measurement**: $T(f)$ with crossover, phase margin and gain margin ([[q0244]]).
- **Output impedance**: $Z_{out}=\Delta V/\Delta I$ versus frequency; closed-loop $Z_{out}=Z_{ol}/(1+T)$.
- **Target impedance**: $Z_t=\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I_{step}$ ([[q0184]] for load-line).
:::

::: answer
"I characterize it in both the time and frequency domains. In the time domain I apply a load step of a defined size and slew rate, from a defined starting load, and measure the peak deviation, undershoot on the step up and overshoot on the release, the recovery time to within a band like plus or minus one percent, and the ringing. The ringing frequency and how fast the peaks decay tell me the damping. These all depend on the test conditions, so a spec reads something like 'less than 50 millivolts of droop for a 2 amp step at 1 amp per microsecond, recovering within 20 microseconds'.

In the frequency domain I measure the loop gain by injecting a small signal in the feedback path and looking at the crossover frequency, the phase margin, and the gain margin. Higher crossover means faster response, and the phase margin tells me how much it rings. Another frequency-domain view is the output impedance versus frequency. If I know the largest step and the allowed voltage deviation, the target impedance is delta V over delta I, and the closed-loop output impedance should stay under that across the frequencies where the load has content.

I'd run all of this at the corners: minimum and maximum input voltage, light and full load, hot and cold, and with the minimum capacitance, because ceramic capacitors derate."
:::

::: technical
**Time-domain metrics** (for a step of $\Delta I$ at slew rate $s$ from base load $I_0$):

| Metric | Definition | Example (12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A step, simulated) |
|---|---|---|
| Peak undershoot | $V_{nom}-V_{min}$ | 71 mV (includes 20 mV ESR step) |
| Peak overshoot (release) | $V_{max}-V_{nom}$ | 76 mV |
| Recovery time | time to stay in ±1% band | 8 µs (±1% = 33 mV); 22 µs for ±0.5%; 32 µs for ±5 mV |
| Ring-back | opposite-polarity swing after the main peak | +22 mV about 14 µs after the minimum |
| Damping | ratio of successive peaks, $e^{-2\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$ | estimate $\zeta$ and PM |

**Rules of thumb.** Droop $\approx k\,\Delta I/(2\pi f_cC)$ with $k\approx0.46$ for $\zeta\approx0.7$; ring frequency $\approx f_c$; recovery $\approx$ a few cycles of $1/f_c$ for good damping.

**Frequency-domain metrics.** Loop gain $T(f)$: $f_c$, PM (target 45° to 60°), GM (6 to 10 dB or more). Closed-loop output impedance $Z_{out,cl}(f)=Z_{ol}(f)/(1+T(f))$, where $Z_{ol}=(sL+R_L)\parallel(r+1/sC)\parallel R$. At low frequency the loop gain reduces it ($Z\approx Z_{ol}/|T|$); near $f_0$ the open-loop peak is large (1.66 Ω at 10.7 kHz for the example) and is cut by $1+T$; above crossover it follows the capacitor impedance.

**Example.** Closed-loop $|Z_{out}|$ of the 40 kHz, 53° design peaks at 118 mΩ near 28 kHz. For $\Delta V=100$ mV and $\Delta I=2$ A, $Z_t=50$ mΩ, so a *sustained* 2 A load swinging near 28 kHz would breach the budget badly (about 236 mV). A single 2 A step through this same design gives about 135 mV in the embedded averaged simulation, less than the sinusoidal worst case but still over budget. (The 71 mV in the table comes from the differently tuned loop simulated in [[q0248]], which has a lower impedance peak.) The target-impedance method is conservative because it assumes worst-case excitation at the peak frequency.

**Measurement.** Load step: electronic load or switched resistor with controlled slew rate, scope at the output capacitor or the load with a short ground spring (not the long clip). Loop: injection resistor (10 to 50 Ω) in series with the feedback path and a network/frequency-response analyzer. $Z_{out}$: inject a small AC current and measure the AC voltage.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Recovery band and test slew rate are application-specific. Processor and ASIC rails publish their own load-line and transient specs; the values here are examples.
:::

::: short
"I characterize it in the time domain with a load step of defined size and slew rate: peak undershoot and overshoot, recovery time to a stated band like plus or minus one percent, and ringing, which gives the damping. In the frequency domain I measure the loop gain by injection to get crossover and phase and gain margin, and I look at closed-loop output impedance against the target impedance, delta V allowed over delta I. I do it at line, load, temperature and capacitance corners."
:::

::: long
"There are two views and I use both.

In the time domain, the standard test is a load step: an electronic load going from one level to another at a specified slew rate, for example 1 to 3 amps at 1 amp per microsecond. On the output I look at four things: the peak undershoot on the step up, the peak overshoot on the release, the recovery time, defined as the time to get back and stay within a band like plus or minus 1 percent, and the ringing after the first peak. The ring frequency is roughly the loop crossover frequency and the ratio of successive peaks gives me the damping ratio, and from that the phase margin. For my example 12 V to 3.3 V converter, a 2 amp step gives about 71 millivolts of droop, 76 of overshoot, and recovery within 1 percent in about 8 microseconds.

In the frequency domain I measure loop gain with a small injection resistor in the feedback path and a frequency-response analyzer. That gives the crossover frequency, which sets how fast the converter responds, and the phase and gain margins, which set the damping and the robustness. The other frequency-domain tool is the closed-loop output impedance. If the load can change by delta I and the rail may move delta V, the target impedance is delta V over delta I. The output impedance has to stay below that over the frequencies where the load has current content. My example peaks at 118 milliohms near 28 kHz against a 50 milliohm target, so a repetitive 2 amp load right at that frequency would break the budget even though a single step passes. That's the reason to check both.

And I repeat it at the corners: minimum and maximum input voltage, light and full load, temperature, and with capacitor tolerance and DC-bias derating, since a design that's fine at nominal can lose 20 or 30 degrees of margin at a corner."
:::

::: terms
- **Undershoot / droop**: peak negative deviation after a load increase.
- **Overshoot**: peak positive deviation after a load decrease.
- **Recovery (settling) time**: time to enter and stay within a specified band.
- **Load slew rate**: di/dt of the load step, a key test condition.
- **Loop gain $T(f)$**: measured by injection; gives $f_c$, PM, GM.
- **Output impedance $Z_{out}(f)$**: voltage deviation per unit load current at a frequency.
- **Target impedance**: $\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I_{step}$.
- **Frequency-response analyzer (FRA)**: instrument that injects a swept sine and measures gain and phase.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "undershoot, overshoot and settling time".

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the test conditions (step size, slew rate, base load, input voltage, temperature) and why they matter.
- Defines the settling band and the metrics, and extracts damping from successive peaks.
- Relates time-domain results to loop crossover and phase margin (droop $\propto1/f_c$).
- Introduces closed-loop output impedance and target impedance and shows the example numbers.
- Explains measurement technique (short ground spring, injection resistor, FRA).
- Mentions corners and capacitor derating.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Quoting droop without the step slew rate.** A 2 A step at 1 A/µs and at 100 A/µs give different results.
- **Measuring with a long ground lead.** The ground loop picks up switching noise and exaggerates the transient.
- **Measuring at the converter instead of at the load.** PCB resistance and inductance between them matter.
- **Judging margin only from one step.** Check the loop gain, and check at the corners.
- **Treating target impedance as exact.** It is a conservative worst-case bound, so exceeding it at one frequency does not mean a single step will fail.
- **Mixing up small-signal and large-signal.** Small-signal loop gain doesn't capture slew limits or duty saturation in big steps.
:::

**Peak deviation** depends on three things in sequence ([[q0248]]): ESR/ESL (instant), the capacitor's $\Delta I/C$ until the inductor catches up, and the loop's response time. **Recovery time** is dominated by the loop's crossover and phase margin: for a damped second-order response the time to enter a ±1% band is a few times $1/(\zeta\omega_n)$. **Ring-back** is the signature of margin: the ratio of the first opposite-polarity peak to the main deviation corresponds to a particular $\zeta$.

**Estimating damping.** Successive same-polarity peaks one cycle apart have ratio $e^{-2\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$. Opposite-polarity peaks are half a cycle apart, so their ratio is $e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$. Here the 71 mV droop and the +22 mV ring-back are opposite polarity, so $22/71=0.31$ gives $\zeta\approx0.35$, which suggests a phase margin in the 35° to 50° region. The real loop is higher order and the 20 mV ESR step is part of the first peak, so this is only a rough estimate.

## Loop gain measurement

1. Insert a small resistor (10 to 50 Ω) between the output and the top of the feedback divider (or at another point where impedance looking in one direction is much higher than the other).
2. Inject a swept sine across it using the analyzer's source.
3. Measure the AC at both sides. The ratio, after the sign convention, is $T(f)$.
4. Read $f_c$, PM, and GM.
5. Repeat at corners.

## The physics floor: how small can the droop possibly be?

Before judging a measured transient, compute the best any controller could do. When the load steps by $\Delta I$ the inductor current cannot change instantly. It slews at $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ going up and $V_{out}/L$ coming down, and the output capacitor supplies (or absorbs) the difference until the inductor catches up. For the example ($V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $L=4.7$ µH, $C=47$ µF, ESR 10 mΩ):

$$\text{slew}_{up}=\frac{8.7}{4.7\ \mu H}=1.85\ \text{A/µs},\qquad \text{slew}_{down}=\frac{3.3}{4.7\ \mu H}=0.70\ \text{A/µs}.$$

The inductor needs $\Delta I/\text{slew}$ to catch up: $T=1.08$ µs going up, 2.85 µs coming down. During that time the capacitor supplies (or absorbs) a triangle of charge, $Q=\tfrac12\Delta I\,T$, so the capacitive part of the deviation is

$$\Delta V_{C,up}=\frac{\Delta I^2L}{2C(V_{in}-V_{out})},\qquad \Delta V_{C,down}=\frac{\Delta I^2L}{2CV_{out}}.$$

For a 2 A step that is 23 mV going up and 60.6 mV on release. The ESR does **not** simply add on top. The ESR drop $\Delta I\cdot ESR=20$ mV is largest at $t=0$, when the capacitor current is the full $\Delta I$. The capacitive term peaks at $t=T$, when the inductor has caught up and the capacitor current is zero. Maximizing $v(t)=ESR\,i_C(t)+v_C(t)$ along the ramp puts the peak at $t^*=T-ESR\cdot C$ (0.47 µs early here), and

$$\Delta V_{floor}=\frac{\Delta I^2L}{2CV_L}+\frac{ESR^2\,C\,V_L}{2L}\quad(\text{for }T>ESR\cdot C;\ \text{otherwise just }\Delta I\cdot ESR),$$

where $V_L$ is the voltage across the inductor ($V_{in}-V_{out}$ up, $V_{out}$ down). The second term is small: 4.4 mV up and 1.7 mV down. So the floors are about **27 mV** of undershoot and **62 mV** of overshoot. A numerical integration of the ideal controller, including the 20 mΩ DCR and the rising output voltage, gives the same 27 mV and 61 mV. The quick estimate "capacitive term + $\Delta I\cdot ESR$" (43 mV and 81 mV) is a conservative upper estimate for design budgets, not a floor. These numbers assume an instantaneous step and an idealized "perfect" controller: duty jumps to 100% (or 0%) at the step and lets go exactly when the inductor current matches the load. With the 90% duty limit used in the embedded simulation, the step-up floor rises to about 30 mV. A slower load edge lowers both floors.

1. **Release is worse than step-up** whenever $V_{out}<V_{in}-V_{out}$, i.e. for any buck with $D<0.5$: the inductor has only $V_{out}$ across it to unload its current. The simulated 76 mV release overshoot on this page sits 14 mV above the 62 mV floor. The loop is already close to the physical limit on release, and the inductor's slow unload dominates.
2. **The simulated 71 mV undershoot is far above the 27 mV floor.** The gap, about 44 mV, is the price of a 40 kHz-bandwidth loop that needs several microseconds to react. A faster loop (or a controller with a large-signal boost such as an adaptive on-time or constant-on-time architecture) could close it, but only down to the floor. To go below the floor you must add capacitance or reduce $L$. That is a design decision independent of the compensator.

## Worked example: converting a spec into a capacitor

Suppose the budget is 100 mV for a 3 A release on the same rail ($V_{out}=3.3$ V, $L=4.7$ µH). Allow 30 mV for ESR/ESL (a 10 mΩ bank gives $3\times0.01=30$ mV). Adding it in full is conservative, because the ESR peak and the capacitive peak do not coincide, but the margin is welcome for ESL and a non-ideal loop. That leaves 70 mV for the energy term: $C\ge\Delta I^2L/(2V_{out}\,\Delta V)=9\times4.7\ \mu/(2\times3.3\times0.07)=91.6$ µF of effective capacitance at the operating bias. With a ceramic that loses half its capacitance at 3.3 V (vendor-dependent: see the DC-bias curve), the nominal rating must be about 180 µF, a very different parts list from "47 µF looks fine". This is the quickest way to tell during design review whether a transient spec is plausible with the chosen inductor.

## Measurement pitfalls that fake or hide the answer

- **Cable inductance.** A loose wire pair from the electronic load typically runs somewhere between 0.5 µH and 1 µH per meter (it varies with spacing). At 1 A/µs, 1 µH produces $L\,di/dt=1$ V. If you probe at the converter and the load sits at the end of a long cable, the load's local capacitance, not your converter, decides the waveform. Probe at the load pins or use a short fixture with a dedicated test capacitor and a probe tip-and-barrel connection.
- **Load slew.** Many bench loads cannot slew faster than a few A/µs and some are limited by their own cabling. The converter sees a gentler step than the real processor, which makes the droop look better than it will be in the product.
- **Bandwidth limits.** A 20 MHz scope bandwidth limit smooths the ESR/ESL spike, so the first 50 ns of droop can disappear. State the probe and bandwidth setting with the result.
- **Triggering and averaging.** Use single-shot or a high-bandwidth, non-averaged display for the peak. Averaging across 64 triggers can hide a few-hundred-millivolt rogue spike.
- **Output impedance measurement.** Because $|Z_{out}|$ is milliohms, the shunt-through method with an FRA (the output is placed across the source and receiver as a two-port shunt, rather than in series with a 50 Ω input) gives far better resolution than a direct measurement. Keep the injected current small enough to stay linear (tens of mA to a few hundred mA is typical) and make sure the converter is regulating, not clipping.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The cable inductance per meter and the usable signal level for output-impedance injection depend on the physical setup and the instrument. Treat the figures above as typical orders of magnitude, not specifications.

## How the numbers tie together

Three numbers on one page of a validation report let you cross-check each other. The floor above says what the power stage alone permits (27 mV and 62 mV here). The closed-loop impedance peak (118 mΩ at about 28 kHz) says how much a sustained 2 A load at that frequency would add (about 236 mV for a sinusoid of 2 A amplitude, which exceeds the roughly 135 mV that a single 2 A step produces in the same design, because a step spreads its energy). The ring frequency and peak ratio say whether the damping is consistent with the margin measured on the analyzer. If the three disagree, one of them was measured wrong. The most common culprit is a probing or load-cable artifact, followed by capacitor derating, then a model that omitted ESL.

::: followups
### What's the difference between peak deviation and recovery time?
Peak deviation is how far the output moves, set by capacitance, ESR/ESL and how quickly the loop and inductor respond. Recovery time is how long it takes to return to within a tolerance band, set by crossover frequency and damping. A converter can have a small peak and a long, lightly damped recovery, or a large peak with quick, well-damped recovery.

### How do you get phase margin from a step response if you don't have an analyzer?
Estimate the damping ratio from the overshoot or successive peak ratio ($\zeta$) and use $\text{PM}\approx100\zeta$ (for roughly 20° to 60°). For example, 20% overshoot gives $\zeta\approx0.46$, PM about 47°. It is only approximate for higher-order loops, so use it as a sanity check, not for sign-off.

### Why might the loop gain look fine but the load step still fail the spec?
Large-signal effects: duty-cycle saturation, current limit, or inductor slew limit. Or ESR/ESL and layout inductance that create the instant step. Or the test slew rate is faster than the loop can follow. Small-signal margins say nothing about those.

### Define target impedance and tell me where it breaks down.
$Z_t=\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I_{max}$. It assumes the load's current content can sit at any frequency with full amplitude, so it is conservative for real loads with known spectra. It also ignores the nonlinearity of the VRM and treats the PDN as linear. For loads with periodic activity at a specific frequency, it can be optimistic because resonance with the PDN can cause worse noise than a single step.

### Scenario: measured droop is twice the simulation. Where do you look?
Capacitor derating and ESR (measure the effective capacitance at bias), probing point and technique, the load's actual slew rate, the loop (margin and crossover differ from the model: measure with an analyzer) and sensing delays. Compare the first 100 ns (ESR/ESL step) separately from the slower part to find which stage is off.
