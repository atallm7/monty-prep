::: basics
### In plain words
"Parasitic" means a small unwanted effect that comes along with every real wire. Every trace has resistance, inductance and capacitance spread along its length. The inductance and capacitance per meter decide its characteristic impedance, the resistance it looks like to a fast signal. The resistance only wastes a little energy. Vias and pads add small extra bits of inductance or capacitance at single spots, and those spots can bounce part of a fast edge back.

### Picture it
Picture a signal as a wave moving down a canal. A side pool beside the canal soaks up some water as the wave passes, so the wave briefly weakens: that is extra capacitance. A narrow stretch resists the flow and pushes back: that is extra inductance. A slow, gentle swell barely notices either. A sharp, fast wave feels both.

### Words you need
- **Parasitic**: a small unwanted resistance, inductance or capacitance that comes with a real part or wire.
- **Per-meter inductance and capacitance ($L'$, $C'$)**: how much of each one meter of line has ([[q0718]]).
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: the resistance the line looks like to a traveling signal, $\sqrt{L'/C'}$ ([[q0710]]).
- **Via**: a plated hole joining two board layers. It adds a little inductance.
- **Pad**: the copper patch a part is soldered to. It adds a little capacitance.
- **Rise time**: how long an edge takes to go from low to high, often in picoseconds (ps, trillionths of a second).

### The simplest example
A coax cable (a center wire inside a round shield) has 250 nH of inductance and 100 pF of capacitance per meter. Then $Z_0=\sqrt{250\text{ nH}/100\text{ pF}}=50$ Ω. Now add a 0.5 pF pad on a 50 Ω line. It dips the local impedance and reflects about 12% of a 100 ps edge. A slow 500 ps edge reflects only about 2.5%. So the same pad is harmless at low speed and a problem at high speed. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page derives each parasitic and covers fixes like cutting away the plane under a pad.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can explain the **physical origin** of each distributed parameter and how it moves $Z_0$, and whether you recognize that "parasitic" also means **local lumped R, L, C** from vias, pads and connectors that cause impedance discontinuities. The interviewer is checking that you:

1. Say $Z_0\approx\sqrt{L'/C'}$, with $R'$ and $G'$ only perturbing it.
2. Know what sets $L'$ and $C'$ in microstrip and coax and can give the coax formulas.
3. Understand that a small shunt $C$ lowers local impedance and a small series $L$ raises it, and that the damage depends on the edge rate.
:::

::: why
Real traces aren't uniform: every via, pad, connector pin and package bump adds a little local R, L or C, and the line itself has distributed R, L and C. Knowing which parasitic makes the impedance dip or spike lets you read a TDR trace, predict what a footprint will do to a fast edge, and decide where to spend layout effort (anti-pads, back-drilling, pad cutouts). It's the bridge from the clean $\sqrt{L/C}$ theory to a real board.
:::

::: concepts
- **Distributed $L'$ and $C'$** set $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and delay $\sqrt{L'C'}$. See [[q0718]].
- **Series resistance $R'$** (DC, skin effect, roughness) is a loss term and only modifies $Z_0$ at low frequency. See [[q0725]].
- **Shunt conductance $G'$** (dielectric loss) is similarly small near $Z_0$ at high frequency.
- **Lumped parasitics**: via inductance and capacitance, pad capacitance, bond wires, connector pins.
- **Discontinuity**: a local change in $Z_0$ gives a reflection $\Gamma=(Z_2-Z_1)/(Z_2+Z_1)$. See [[q0713]].
- **Edge rate vs lumped size**: a parasitic matters when its time constant is comparable to the rise time.
:::

::: answer
"The line has three kinds of parasitics per unit length, and then the board adds lumped ones on top.

$L'$ is the loop inductance of the trace and its return path: the magnetic field energy stored around the current. $C'$ is the capacitance between trace and plane, the electric field energy. Together they set the impedance, $\sqrt{L'/C'}$, and the delay, $\sqrt{L'C'}$. For a coax I can write them directly: $L'=\frac{\mu}{2\pi}\ln\frac Dd$ and $C'=\frac{2\pi\varepsilon}{\ln(D/d)}$, so $Z_0=\frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\frac Dd$.

$R'$ is the conductor resistance, which rises with $\sqrt f$ from the skin effect, plus a dielectric conductance $G'$. They mostly cause loss, not a change in $Z_0$, except at low frequency where $R'$ comparable to $\omega L'$ makes $Z_0$ complex and larger.

On top of that, local parasitics: a via adds series inductance of around a nanohenry, a pad or connector adds shunt capacitance of a few tenths of a pF. A shunt C makes a local impedance dip, a series L makes a bump, and they reflect part of the edge. Whether it matters depends on the rise time: 0.5 pF on a 50 Ω line gives about a 12% reflection with a 100 ps edge but only about 2.5% with a 500 ps edge."
:::

::: technical
**Distributed parameters.**
- $L'=L'_{ext}+L'_{int}$: external inductance from the field between conductors, plus a small internal part from field inside the conductor that becomes $R'/\omega$ at high frequency (skin effect).
- $C'$: the sum of the parallel-plate part $\varepsilon w/h$ and fringing.
- $R'$: $\rho/(wt)$ at DC, then $\approx\rho/(w\delta)$ above the skin corner, with roughness adding to it.
- $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$.

**Coax:**
$$L'=\frac{\mu}{2\pi}\ln\frac Dd,\quad C'=\frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}{\ln(D/d)},\quad Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}=\frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\frac Dd$$
For polyethylene and $D/d=3.49$: $L'=250$ nH/m, $C'=100$ pF/m, $Z_0=50$ Ω.

**Microstrip 50 Ω on FR-4** (0.39 mm over 0.2 mm): $L'\approx301$ nH/m, $C'\approx120$ pF/m. The naive parallel-plate estimate $\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r w/h=74$ pF/m misses about 40% of the capacitance because the trace is only 2× wider than the dielectric is thick, and fringing is large. Likewise $\mu_0h/w=644$ nH/m badly overestimates inductance. Don't use wide-plate formulas when $w/h\lesssim5$.

**Effect of loss on $Z_0$.** With $Z_0=\sqrt{(R'+j\omega L')/(G'+j\omega C')}$ and $R'\ll\omega L'$, $G'\ll\omega C'$:
$$Z_0\approx\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}\left[1-\frac j2\left(\frac{R'}{\omega L'}-\frac{G'}{\omega C'}\right)\right]$$
so the real part is unchanged to first order. Low frequency ($R'\gg\omega L'$) gives $|Z_0|\approx\sqrt{R'/\omega C'}$.

**Lumped discontinuities on a $Z_0$ line.**
- Shunt $C$ sees $Z_0/2$ (the line both ways), $\tau=CZ_0/2$. For rise time $t_r$ (linear ramp): peak $\Gamma\approx-\frac{\tau}{t_r}(1-e^{-t_r/\tau})$. For 0.5 pF, 50 Ω: $\tau=12.5$ ps; $t_r=100$ ps gives $\Gamma=-0.125$ and a local dip to about 39 Ω.
- Series $L$ sees $2Z_0$, $\tau=L/2Z_0$. 1 nH, $t_r=100$ ps: $\Gamma\approx+0.10$, a bump to about 61 Ω.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Typical via and pad parasitics (about 0.5–1 nH, 0.2–1 pF) depend strongly on board thickness, via size, anti-pad size and pad geometry. Use a 3D field solver or vendor-provided models for real numbers.
:::

::: short
"Per unit length, $L'$ is the magnetic energy of the trace-plus-return loop, $C'$ is the electric energy between trace and plane, and together they set $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$. $R'$ and $G'$ add loss and only matter for $Z_0$ at low frequency. On a real board the bigger problem is lumped parasitics from vias, pads and connectors: a shunt capacitance dips the impedance, a series inductance bumps it, and the size of the reflection depends on how that parasitic compares with the edge rate."
:::

::: long
"Start with the three distributed elements. $L'$ is the loop inductance of the signal and its return, the magnetic field around the current. It drops if the return plane is closer. $C'$ is the capacitance between the trace and the plane, which goes up with a wider trace or higher $\varepsilon_r$. Those two set the impedance, $\sqrt{L'/C'}$, and the delay, $\sqrt{L'C'}$. For a coax you can write them exactly: $L'=\frac{\mu}{2\pi}\ln(D/d)$, $C'=2\pi\varepsilon/\ln(D/d)$. For 50 Ω polyethylene coax that's 250 nH/m and 100 pF/m.

$R'$ is the resistance of the copper. It's tiny at DC, about an ohm per meter on a typical trace, but it grows as the square root of frequency because of the skin effect. $G'$ is dielectric loss, proportional to frequency. Both are loss mechanisms. They don't change $Z_0$ much at high frequency. At low frequency, where $R'$ is comparable to $\omega L'$, the impedance becomes complex and larger than $\sqrt{L'/C'}$.

Then there are the lumped parasitics added by the board. A via is series inductance of about a nanohenry plus a few tenths of a pF of capacitance. A pad, a connector pin or a package adds shunt capacitance. A shunt capacitor on a 50 Ω line sees 25 Ω in parallel, so its time constant is $C\times25$ Ω, and it pulls the local impedance down. A series inductor sees 100 Ω and pushes it up. Whether it hurts depends on the edge. A 0.5 pF pad with a 100 ps edge reflects about 12% and dips the impedance to about 39 Ω; with a 500 ps edge it reflects 2.5% and is almost invisible. So I design around the fastest edge in the system: anti-pads, pad cutouts, short stubs."
:::

::: terms
- **Per-unit-length parameters** $R', L', G', C'$: distributed line parameters.
- **Internal inductance**: inductance from magnetic field inside the conductor; becomes the surface-impedance term at high frequency.
- **Fringing capacitance**: capacitance from field lines at the trace edges, not directly between the plates.
- **Via stub**: the unused portion of a through-hole via that acts like an open-circuited stub.
- **Anti-pad**: the clearance hole in a plane around a via, which trades capacitance for inductance.
- **Lumped parasitic**: a size-small $R$, $L$ or $C$ associated with a feature rather than a length of line.
- **TDR (time-domain reflectometry)**: measuring reflected step to display impedance vs. position.
- **Discontinuity**: any abrupt change in $Z_0$ along the path.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "R is resistance, L is inductance, C is capacitance, they set impedance".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and says what physically sets each (field energy in the loop vs between conductors).
- Writes the coax formulas and the microstrip numbers (301 nH/m and 120 pF/m for 50 Ω FR-4).
- States that $R'$ and $G'$ are small perturbations at high frequency but complex $Z_0$ at low frequency.
- Distinguishes distributed parasitics from **lumped** ones (via, pad, connector).
- Quantifies a lumped C or L discontinuity vs rise time: 0.5 pF, 100 ps edge, about −12.5% reflection.
- Mentions mitigation: pad cutouts, anti-pad tuning, shorter stubs, back-drill.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying $R$ sets $Z_0$.** For a lossy line at high frequency $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and $R'$ only adds loss. The 50 Ω isn't "a 50 Ω resistance" along the trace.
- **Using parallel-plate formulas for narrow traces.** With $w/h\approx2$, fringing dominates: simple $\varepsilon w/h$ misses 40% of $C'$.
- **Confusing total and per-unit-length values.** $Z_0$ is independent of length; total $L$ and $C$ grow with length.
- **Treating a small via as "just a wire".** A 1 nH via is 6 Ω of reactance at 1 GHz and matters at multi-gigabit speeds.
- **Ignoring edge rate.** The same 0.5 pF pad is invisible at 500 ps and visible at 50 ps. It's the ratio that matters.
- **Forgetting the return path.** $L'$ includes the return loop. A plane gap under the trace increases local $L'$ and $Z_0$.
:::

::: deep
## The distributed parasitics, one by one

A transmission line is a trace plus its return, and its behavior is the combination of four per-unit-length quantities. Here is what each means physically, and what it does to $Z_0$.

**Inductance $L'$.** Current in the trace creates magnetic field that loops around it and through the return plane. The energy stored per meter is $\frac12L'I^2$. Bringing the plane closer squeezes the loop, so $L'$ falls. A split in the plane forces the return current to detour, which enlarges the loop and raises local $L'$, so $Z_0$ rises there.

**Capacitance $C'$.** The trace and plane are the plates of a capacitor with the substrate between them. Energy per meter is $\frac12C'V^2$. A wider trace or higher $\varepsilon_r$ increases it. Because microstrip's field partly runs through air, it sees $\varepsilon_{eff}$, between 1 and $\varepsilon_r$.

**Resistance $R'$.** Copper conductivity gives $R'_{dc}=\rho/(wt)$. For the 0.39 mm × 35 µm trace this is about 1.2 Ω/m, only 0.03 Ω in 25 mm. But at 1 GHz the skin depth in copper is 2.1 µm and the current is crammed into the surface, so $R'$ is closer to 20 Ω/m (rough estimate, geometry dependent). Over 25 mm that's 0.5 Ω, a small series resistance, small compared with 50 Ω, which is why it's a *loss* mechanism and not an *impedance* one.

**Conductance $G'$.** $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$. At 1 GHz with $\tan\delta=0.02$: $G'=2\pi\times10^9\times120\times10^{-12}\times0.02=0.015$ S/m, and $1/G'=66$ Ω·m, effectively infinite across a meter-scale line. Again loss, not impedance.

**Putting it together:** $Z_0\approx\sqrt{L'/C'}$ with relative corrections of order $R'/\omega L'$ and $G'/\omega C'$. For the FR-4 trace above: $\omega L'=2\pi\times10^9\times301\times10^{-9}=1890$ Ω/m against $R'\approx20$ Ω/m, a 1% effect that cancels in the real part of $Z_0$ to first order.

## The coax case is exact

For a coaxial cable, field solutions give closed forms:
$$L'=\frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac Dd,\qquad C'=\frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}{\ln(D/d)}$$
Check with polyethylene $\varepsilon_r=2.25$, $D/d=3.49$: $L'=2\times10^{-7}\times1.25=250$ nH/m, $C'=2\pi\times8.854\times10^{-12}\times2.25/1.25=100$ pF/m, $Z_0=\sqrt{250\text{ nH}/100\text{ pF}}=50$ Ω. This matches common 50 Ω coax data (about 100 pF/m, 250 nH/m is a well-known pair), a good memory anchor. The same formulas show how a microstrip differs: the geometry isn't a closed shape, so no exact closed form exists and field solvers or approximate equations are used ([[q0715]], [[q0719]]).

A shunt capacitor $C$ on a line of impedance $Z_0$ sees the line in both directions, $Z_0/2$. Its time constant is $\tau=CZ_0/2$. A step arriving at the C finds it initially a short (reflection $-1$) and then charges, so the reflected step is $-e^{-t/\tau}$. For a finite ramp of duration $t_r$, the reflection is smoothed to
$$v_r(t)=-\frac{\tau}{t_r}\Big[(1-e^{-t/\tau})-u(t-t_r)\,(1-e^{-(t-t_r)/\tau})\Big]$$

That is why the same layout is fine at 100 MHz and unacceptable at 10 Gb/s: it's the ratio of $\tau$ to $t_r$ that matters. Drag C up to 2 pF: the 100 ps edge now reflects about −43%, and even the 500 ps edge reflects about −10%. Shrinking the pad to 0.1 pF makes the 30 ps edge reflect only about −8%.

Numbers for the peak $\Gamma=-\frac{\tau}{t_r}(1-e^{-t_r/\tau})$ with $\tau=12.5$ ps: 30 ps → −0.379 (local $Z$ down to 22.5 Ω); 100 ps → −0.125 (38.9 Ω); 500 ps → −0.025 (47.6 Ω).

A series inductor $L$ sees $2Z_0$ ($\tau=L/2Z_0$) and reflects a positive pulse. 1 nH on 50 Ω with a 100 ps edge: $\tau=10$ ps, peak $\Gamma\approx+0.10$, local bump to about 61 Ω. A real via is both: they separate in time on a TDR trace into a bump then a dip (or the reverse), and an experienced engineer can read which parasitic dominates from the sign of the first excursion.

> [!TIP]
> Quick rule of thumb: a discontinuity is "electrically small" if its delay $\sqrt{LC}$ is much shorter than the rise time (below $t_r/6$ or so). Then you can model it as a lumped $L$ or $C$. Longer than that, treat it as a short piece of transmission line with its own $Z_0$.

## Fixing them

- **Pads and connectors (too much C):** cut a void in the reference plane under the pad, so the local $C$ falls and impedance recovers. Typical for SMA edge launches and for AC-coupling capacitor pads on high-speed links.
- **Vias (L and C):** use ground vias near signal vias to give return current a short path, tune the anti-pad diameter (larger anti-pad means less C but more L), and back-drill the unused stub.
- **Bond wires / package leads:** modeled with a few nH of inductance and compensated in the IC design.
- **Wider line section** next to a series-L feature (a so-called "tapered launch"), lowering local $Z_0$ to cancel the L bump.

::: applications
- **TDR on a PCB**: reading dips (capacitive) and bumps (inductive) to locate the pad, via or connector that's mis-tuned.
- **High-speed connector footprints**: SMA, USB-C and BGA breakouts all use pad cutouts and anti-pad tuning to hold 50/90/100 Ω through the transition.
- **IC package models**: bond-wire L and pad C appear in SPICE/IBIS models for DDR and SerDes pins.
- **Coax cable specs**: datasheets list capacitance per length (about 100 pF/m for 50 Ω PE-dielectric coax, about 67 pF/m for 75 Ω), a direct readout of $C'$.
- **RF layouts**: stub length, pad size and via ground fence on microwave PCBs.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Quasi-TEM propagation** so $L'$ and $C'$ are well defined.
- **Frequency regime**: $\omega L'\gg R'$ and $\omega C'\gg G'$, i.e. above roughly a MHz for a PCB trace.
- **Local parasitic values** like "1 nH via, 0.5 pF pad" are order-of-magnitude, and the answer changes by factors of 2 with stackup and geometry.
- **Lumped treatment** is valid only for features much shorter than the rise-time length.
- **Linear, passive** parasitics. Diode or ESD structures add voltage-dependent capacitance, which isn't covered here.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a shunt capacitor see $Z_0/2$ and not $Z_0$?
At the capacitor, the incident wave sees the line continuing on the far side (impedance $Z_0$) in parallel with the capacitor. For the capacitor itself, looking out, the Thevenin resistance is the two line halves in parallel, $Z_0/2=25$ Ω for 50 Ω. So $\tau=CZ_0/2$.

### What is the rule of thumb for how long a via can be before it matters?
It depends on the edge rate. Treat it as lumped if its one-way delay is under about $t_r/6$. A 1.6 mm via in FR-4 has a delay of about 10 ps, so it's roughly lumped for edges slower than 60 ps. For 10 Gb/s edges (about 30–40 ps) it already matters, and a stub of a few mm resonates in the GHz range.

### How would you reduce the capacitance of a connector pad on a 50 Ω trace?
Cut a void (anti-pad) in the reference plane directly under the pad, so the pad has less capacitance to the plane. If you remove too much, the pad's inductance dominates and impedance overshoots. Tune with a field solver and verify with TDR.

### Does a series resistance in the trace change $Z_0$?
Not meaningfully in the high-frequency regime: $Z_0=\sqrt{(R'+j\omega L')/(j\omega C')}$ and with $R'\ll\omega L'$ the change is second order. The effect is attenuation. Only at low frequency does $R'$ push $|Z_0|$ above $\sqrt{L'/C'}$.

### What if the trace crosses a gap in the reference plane?
The return current has to detour, and the local loop inductance rises. $C'$ also falls because the nearest conductor is farther away. $Z_0$ jumps up locally, you get a reflection and a lot of EMI. The fix is to stitch the planes with a capacitor or reroute the trace so it stays above solid copper. See [[q0463]] for the general parasitics picture on a PCB.

### Scenario: a TDR with a 100 ps edge shows a local dip from 50 Ω to 40 Ω in the middle of your trace. Estimate the capacitance.
A dip to 40 Ω means $\Gamma=(40-50)/(40+50)=-0.111$. For a shunt C with $\tau\ll t_r$ the peak is about $\tau/t_r$, so $\tau\approx0.111\times100\text{ ps}=11$ ps. Then $C=\tau/(Z_0/2)=11\text{ ps}/25\ \Omega\approx0.45$ pF. A pad or via of that size is typical, so I'd check the footprint under the dip. I'd also state the assumption that the dip is a small lumped C: if the dip were wide, it would be a stretch of lower-impedance line instead.
