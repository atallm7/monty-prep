::: basics
### In plain words
To run a DC motor both ways, you must reverse the current through it. The standard circuit is an H-bridge: four switches arranged around the motor like the letter H. Closing one diagonal pair sends current through the motor in one direction, and closing the other diagonal pair sends it the other way. A PWM signal on one switch sets the speed. The one forbidden state is turning on both switches on the same side.

### Picture it
Imagine a toy car with a battery you can flip by hand. Put the battery in one way and the car goes forward. Flip it and it reverses. An H-bridge flips the battery electronically, using four switches, two at a time. The danger is the same as touching both battery ends together with a wire: if the top and bottom switch on one side close at once, they short the supply.

### Words you need
- **H-bridge**: four switches in an H shape, with the motor as the crossbar.
- **Shoot-through**: both switches on one side (one leg) on at once, shorting the supply. See [[q0428]].
- **PWM**: switching fully on and off very fast. The fraction of time on (the duty) sets the average voltage.
- **Dead time**: a short gap between one switch turning off and its partner turning on.

### The simplest example
Call the switches Q1 and Q2 on the left (top, bottom) and Q3 and Q4 on the right. With 12 V, Q1 and Q4 on gives forward at +12 V. Q3 and Q2 on gives reverse at −12 V. Both low sides on, Q2 and Q4, shorts the motor and brakes it. All off is coast. Q1 and Q2 together is the forbidden state: with two 10 mΩ switches, the short is $12 / 0.02 = 600$ A on paper. For speed, chop one switch at 50% duty and the motor sees an average of 6 V.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers where the current flows when switches turn off, dead time, ways to apply PWM, driving the top switches, microcontroller connections, and simpler options like a relay.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can reverse a DC motor's current safely. The expected answer is the H-bridge: four switches, two conducting diagonally, and the truth table for forward, reverse, brake and coast. The hidden skills: avoiding shoot-through (dead time), freewheel paths through body diodes, how PWM is applied (sign-magnitude versus lock-antiphase), how a microcontroller talks to it (PWM + DIR, or IN1/IN2), and the existence of simpler alternatives (a DPDT relay, split supplies).
:::

::: why
Any actuator that must go forwards and backwards (a window lift, a robot wheel, a printer carriage, a valve) needs reversible drive. A mistake in the bridge, such as turning on both switches of one leg, is a dead short across the supply and usually destroys the part in microseconds, so interviewers like this as a test of both circuit knowledge and fault thinking.
:::

::: concepts
- **Current reversal**: motor torque follows current direction, so reversing the voltage across the motor reverses torque. See [[q0341]].
- **H-bridge**: four switches arranged so the motor can be connected either way round. See [[q0420]] and [[q0422]].
- **Shoot-through**: both switches of one leg on at once, shorting the supply. See [[q0428]].
- **Dead time**: a deliberate gap between turning one switch off and the other on.
- **Freewheel path**: body or external diodes giving the inductive current a route after switching.
- **Braking and coast states**: see [[q0410]].

For the PWM I'd typically chop one switch while keeping the diagonal partner on, which gives lower ripple and good control.

If I only need occasional reversal at low speed, a DPDT relay does it, and if I have a split supply, a half-bridge with the motor to the middle point works too."

::: technical
**Topology.** Legs A (Q1 high, Q2 low) and B (Q3 high, Q4 low); motor between the two midpoints.

| Q1 | Q2 | Q3 | Q4 | Motor |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | forward ($+V$ across) |
| 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 | reverse ($-V$) |
| 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | brake (low sides) |
| 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | brake (high sides) |
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | coast (current freewheels through body diodes) |
| 1 | 1 | x | x | **forbidden**: shoot-through |

**Shoot-through.** When one leg has both switches on, only the FET resistances limit current: $12\text{ V}/(2\times10\text{ mΩ}) = 600$ A at the switches (in practice limited by the supply, wiring and the gate-drive transient). The cure: **dead time** $t_{dead}$ between one FET's turn-off command and the other's turn-on. Sum the turn-off delay and fall time ($t_{d(off)} + t_f \approx 40 + 20$ ns), driver propagation mismatch (about 20 ns) and a margin (typically doubling): about 150 to 250 ns. Dead time costs duty cycle, $2t_{dead}f$ per period (0.8% at 20 kHz with 200 ns, 8% at 200 kHz) and produces a small voltage error $\approx V\cdot 2 t_{dead}f$, which can be significant at low duty. Many drivers have fixed or programmable dead time and cross-conduction lockout.

**PWM schemes.**
- **Sign-magnitude (drive/brake or drive/coast)**: direction fixed by which diagonal; PWM only one switch (e.g. Q4 on, Q1 PWM). In the off-time either Q2 turns on so the motor is shorted through the low sides ("slow decay"), or all switches turn off and the current returns to the supply through the body diodes ("fast decay"). The motor sees 0 or $V$. Ripple $\Delta I = V D(1-D)/(Lf)$.
- **Lock-antiphase (complementary, bipolar)**: both legs PWM'd in antiphase; the motor sees $+V$ or $-V$; average $V(2D-1)$. Duty 50% is zero speed and the current still ripples at full amplitude. Simple (one PWM and no direction pin) and good for servo loops (linear, no deadband, current always flows both ways), but more losses and ripple.
- **Unipolar (3-level)**: legs PWM'd 180° apart; the motor sees $+V$, 0 or $-V$ and the ripple frequency is $2f$.

Numbers: 12 V, $L = 1$ mH, 20 kHz, average motor voltage 6 V: sign-magnitude $\Delta I = 12\times0.25/(10^{-3}\times20{,}000) = 0.15$ A; bipolar ($D = 0.75$) $(12-6)\times0.75\times50\ \mu\text{s}/1\text{ mH} = 0.225$ A; unipolar 3-level $(12-6)\times0.25\times50\ \mu\text{s}/1\text{ mH} = 0.075$ A.

**High-side drive.** Q1 and Q3 need gate voltage above the supply (N-channel) via bootstrap/charge pump ([[q0426]], [[q0427]]), or use P-channel high sides (simpler drive but higher $R_{DS(on)}$ per area, [[q0438]]).

**Interfaces.** Common: PWM + DIR (+ BRAKE); IN1/IN2 with truth table; PH/EN. The driver blocks illegal combinations.
:::

::: long
"To reverse a DC motor I need to reverse the current through it, and the standard circuit for that is an H-bridge. Picture four switches, two from the supply to each side of the motor and two from each side to ground. If I close the top-left and bottom-right, current flows through the motor left to right; top-right and bottom-left, right to left. Speed comes from PWM on one of the conducting switches.

The danger is the two switches in the same leg: if both are on, that's a direct short across the supply, with only milliohms of FET resistance to limit it. So I add dead time, a gap of a couple hundred nanoseconds between turning one off and the other on, and a driver IC normally builds in shoot-through protection so that the controller can't command the illegal state.

The FET body diodes matter too: when I turn everything off, the motor's inductive current freewheels through them, which is why I don't need external flyback diodes in a bridge. If I turn on both low sides instead, I get braking. The full set of states is forward, reverse, brake and coast.

For PWM I have choices. In sign-magnitude, I chop one switch, which is efficient and lower-ripple. In lock-antiphase, both legs switch in opposite phases, so 50% duty is zero speed. That's a bit lossier but gives a nice linear control law for servo loops.

On the high side I need gate drive above the supply, so I'd use a bootstrap driver or P-channel FETs. The microcontroller side is typically PWM plus a direction pin on a driver IC.

For simple needs, a DPDT relay or two SPDT relays also reverse polarity, with no speed control and slow switching."
:::

::: terms
- **H-bridge**: four switches in an H shape with the motor as the crossbar.
- **Leg / half-bridge**: one high-side and one low-side switch sharing a midpoint.
- **Shoot-through (cross-conduction)**: both switches of a leg on, shorting the supply. See [[q0428]].
- **Dead time**: delay between one switch's turn-off and its complement's turn-on.
- **Sign-magnitude PWM**: direction from the active diagonal, magnitude from duty.
- **Lock-antiphase PWM**: both legs complementary; average voltage $V(2D-1)$.
- **Bootstrap**: capacitor-based supply for high-side gate drive ([[q0427]]).
- **Body diode**: the intrinsic MOSFET diode providing freewheel conduction.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Turning on both switches of one leg.** A dead short. Use dead time and interlocks.
- **Adding flyback diodes thinking the bridge needs them.** The FET body diodes do the job (add Schottkys only if the body diodes are too slow or lossy).
- **Forgetting the gate drive for the high side.** An N-FET high side needs a gate above the supply.
- **Changing direction at full speed with no current limit.** Reversing applies $(V + E)/R$: up to twice the stall current from no-load speed (about 1.5x at half speed). Ramp or limit.
- **Assuming lock-antiphase at 50% duty means "no current".** The motor sees $\pm V$ switching; ripple current is at its largest.
- **No dead-time compensation at low duty.** Dead-time error is a larger fraction of a small voltage.
:::

## The freewheel path

When a conducting diagonal turns off, the motor current $I_0$ continues. Suppose Q1 and Q4 were on, and both turn off. The motor current must keep flowing left to right, so it leaves the right terminal into the supply through Q3's body diode and is fed at the left terminal from ground through Q2's body diode: the current is *pushed back into the supply* while it decays, with the voltage across the motor $-V_M - 2V_f$. This is "fast decay": quick, but it pumps the bus (see [[q0412]]). If instead only Q1 turns off while Q4 stays on, the current recirculates through Q2's body diode (or Q2 turned on for synchronous rectification) and Q4, with only about $-(V_f + IR_{DS(on)})$ across the motor (or $-2IR_{DS(on)}$ if Q2 is turned on): "slow decay" with lower ripple.

## Shoot-through and dead time

Let Q1 and Q2 be the left leg. During the switching transition both gates are commanded at nearly the same time, but a FET's turn-off is not instantaneous (it needs the gate to discharge below threshold, plateau, and so on). If Q2 turns on before Q1 is fully off, a current spike flows:

- Limited only by the two FET resistances plus supply and wiring: $12\text{ V}/20\text{ mΩ} = 600$ A on paper.
- In practice it is a very short spike (tens of ns) that heats the FETs, makes noise on the rail and can cause false trips or destroy a gate driver.

**Dead time** solves it. A driver that gets a single PWM input generates the complementary gate signals with a delay between them: $t_{dead} \ge t_{d(off),max} + t_{f,max} - t_{d(on),min} + \text{margin}$. Typical figures are 50 to 500 ns depending on FET size. During the dead time, motor current flows through a body diode, which has a higher voltage drop than the FET channel; so don't use more dead time than needed (efficiency and distortion).

At 20 kHz, 200 ns dead time per edge: 0.4% of the period per edge, 0.8% for the pair. At 200 kHz it would be 8%, the reason high-frequency bridges need very small dead times (GaN, fast drivers).

Sign-magnitude uses D from 0 to 1 for each direction and a separate direction signal; the motor sees 0 V at zero duty, so there is no circulating ripple.

**Ripple at 20 kHz, 1 mH, 12 V, average 6 V:** lock-antiphase 0.225 A peak to peak; sign-magnitude 0.15 A; unipolar 3-level 0.075 A (and 40 kHz ripple). Ripple means extra $I^2R$ heating (but small) and torque ripple, and the antiphase scheme also keeps the current flowing in the motor at zero average, which gives a small "dither" that can overcome static friction.

## Interface from a microcontroller

Typical driver IC inputs:
- **PWM + DIR**: one pin for speed, one for direction; the IC makes the switch states.
- **IN1 / IN2**: (1,0) forward, (0,1) reverse, (1,1) brake, (0,0) coast; PWM applied to one input.
- **EN / PH (phase)**: similar to PWM + DIR.

The MCU never directly drives all four gates when using a bridge IC, so shoot-through is prevented in hardware. If you build the bridge from discrete FETs, use a half-bridge gate driver per leg with built-in dead time and shoot-through lockout ([[q0416]], [[q0417]]).

## Alternatives for reversing

| Method | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| H-bridge | speed control, fast, brake, small | four switches, high-side drive |
| DPDT relay | trivial, isolation, high current | no PWM, slow, contacts arc, wear ([[q0414]]) |
| Split supply, half-bridge | two switches only | needs $\pm V$ rails |
| Two half-bridges + motor to mid-rail (push-pull) | 2 switches, uses a mid-point | half the voltage across the motor |

> [!TIP]
> If asked "what is the minimum number of switches to reverse a motor?" the answer is two with a split (bipolar) supply, or four in the single-supply H-bridge. Say why: the motor needs current in both directions through its two terminals.

::: followups
### Why don't you need external flyback diodes on an H-bridge?
Each MOSFET has a body diode that points from source to drain, i.e. across the switch with its cathode toward the higher potential. For a bridge, those diodes connect each motor terminal to both rails, so when the bridge turns off the motor current finds a path to either rail. External Schottkys across each FET are sometimes added because body diodes are slow (reverse recovery) and have a higher drop.

### How would you design the dead time?
Take the datasheet turn-off delay and fall time (worst case), subtract the turn-on delay of the complementary FET (best case), add driver mismatch and a margin. For a FET with $t_{d(off)} = 40$ ns and $t_f = 20$ ns, a driver mismatch of 20 ns and 100% margin, about 160 to 200 ns. Verify on a scope with the gate and switch-node waveforms at hot and cold, and look for a current spike from the supply.

### What's the downside of lock-antiphase PWM?
The motor sees the full $\pm V$ at all times, so ripple current and iron/copper losses are higher at low speed, and the bridge switches both legs every cycle, doubling switching losses. The upside is linearity through zero and the same control law for both directions.

### My H-bridge gets hot at stall even though the PWM duty is low. Why?
At stall there is no back-EMF, so the motor current settles at $V D/R$: on 12 V with a 1 Ω motor even 25% duty means a steady 3 A, far more than the same duty would draw while the motor spins. That current flows continuously through two conducting switches (one of them the recirculation path during the off-time), so conduction loss $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ plus dead-time diode loss heats the bridge even though the duty is low. Add a current limit and stall detection ([[q0402]]).

### Can you use a relay to reverse the motor, and what are the issues?
Yes. A DPDT relay swaps polarity with no semiconductor losses and provides isolation. But it switches slowly (ms), can't do PWM, and the contacts arc when breaking an inductive DC load, so contact life is reduced. You must also never reverse while the motor is running at speed without a delay, or you get $(V + E)/R$ plugging current through the contacts ([[q0414]]).
:::
