::: basics
### In plain words
MOSFETs are everywhere because they are easy to drive, waste little power, and switch fast. The gate is insulated, so once it is charged it needs almost no current to stay on. When fully on, the transistor acts like a small resistor. And in digital chips, paired N-channel and P-channel devices draw almost no power when idle.

### Picture it
Picture a door held open by a latch instead of by your hand. You give it one push, and then it stays open with no effort. A BJT is more like holding the door open the whole time. The latch comparison is not perfect, since the MOSFET gate does need a burst of charge each time it switches.

### Words you need
- **Gate capacitance**: the gate acts like a small capacitor. It takes current only while charging or discharging.
- **$R_{DS(on)}$**: the on-resistance. Power lost is $I^2R$.
- **Majority carrier**: the main charge carrier. MOSFETs use only one type, so no stored charge slows them down.
- **CMOS**: paired N- and P-channel MOSFETs where one is always off. See [[q0092]].
- **Switching loss**: energy wasted each time a switch changes state.

### The simplest example
Carry 10 A through a MOSFET with 5 mΩ of on-resistance. The power lost is $10^2 \times 0.005 = 0.5$ W. A saturated BJT dropping, say, 0.3 V at the same current would waste $10 \times 0.3 = 3$ W. The MOSFET also needs no steady gate current, while a power BJT needs a steady base current.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page explains the four reasons in detail, and the cases where IGBTs, SiC or BJTs still win.
:::

::: testing
The interviewer wants **reasons with mechanisms**, not "they're better." Hidden checks:

1. You know the core advantage is the **insulated gate**: voltage control, zero static drive current, easy to drive from logic.
2. You can explain the power advantages: **resistive on-state** ($I^2R_{DS(on)}$, no fixed 0.2–0.7 V drop), **majority-carrier** switching with no stored charge, and **positive temperature coefficient** of $R_{DS(on)}$ for paralleling.
3. You know the **digital** reason: CMOS pairs give near-zero static power, and MOSFETs scale down beautifully, which is why billions fit on a chip.
4. You can say where MOSFETs *aren't* the best choice (high-voltage high-current, precision analog), which proves the reasons are understood, not memorized.
:::

::: why
Device choice is one of the first real decisions you'll make as a hardware engineer: MOSFET vs BJT vs IGBT vs relay for a driver ([[q0414]]), silicon vs GaN for a converter, logic-level vs standard gate. Knowing *why* MOSFETs won lets you predict when they won't: a 1200 V motor drive, a low-noise preamp, or a cost-sensitive 10 mA indicator where a cheap BJT works fine. It's also the root of the "BJT vs MOSFET" ([[q0094]]) and "why CMOS" ([[q0106]]) questions.
:::

::: concepts
- **Insulated gate**: the gate is a capacitor, so it needs charge to switch but no current to stay on. See [[q0090]].
- **Triode-region on-resistance**: a fully-on MOSFET looks like a small resistor $R_{DS(on)}$. See [[q0099]].
- **Majority-carrier conduction**: no minority-carrier storage, so no BJT-style storage time or diode-like recovery in the channel. See [[q0094]].
- **CMOS**: complementary pairs mean one device is always off in steady state. See [[q0105]].
- **Scaling**: shrinking $L$ and oxide thickness makes devices faster, smaller and lower-power per operation. See [[q0103]].
- **Temperature coefficient**: $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature, which naturally balances current between paralleled devices. See [[q0094]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd give four reasons.

First, the gate is insulated, so it's voltage-controlled. Once the gate capacitance is charged, it takes basically zero current to hold the MOSFET on. A microcontroller pin can drive one directly, whereas a power BJT carrying 10 A might need half an amp of base current the whole time.

Second, when it's fully on it behaves like a resistor, not a fixed voltage drop. Modern parts have milliohms of $R_{DS(on)}$, so 10 A through 5 mΩ is 0.5 W, versus several watts for a saturated BJT. And because $R_{DS(on)}$ goes up with temperature, you can parallel MOSFETs and they share current.

Third, they switch fast. Conduction is by majority carriers, so there's no stored minority charge to pull out like in a saturated BJT. That's what makes hundreds-of-kHz to MHz converters practical.

Fourth, and the biggest by volume, is digital. NMOS and PMOS pair up into CMOS, where one transistor is always off, so a gate draws almost no static current. And MOSFETs scale: shrink them and they get smaller, faster and cheaper, which is why a chip can have billions.

They're not perfect: at very high voltage IGBTs or SiC win on conduction loss, and BJTs still have an edge in some low-noise analog work. But for most switching and all digital, MOSFETs are the default."
:::

::: technical
**1. Voltage drive, near-zero static gate current.** $I_G \approx 0$ at DC (pA–nA). Switching needs gate charge $Q_g$: average driver current $Q_g f_{sw}$, driver power $Q_g V_{drive} f_{sw}$. Example: $Q_g = 20$ nC, 10 V, 100 kHz → 2 mA average, 20 mW. A power BJT at $I_C = 10$ A with forced $\beta \approx 20$ needs $I_B = 0.5$ A continuously (≈ 0.45 W at $V_{BE} \approx 0.9$ V).

**2. Resistive on-state.** $V_{DS} = I_D R_{DS(on)}$, no junction offset. Low-voltage silicon MOSFETs reach ~1 mΩ. 10 A × 5 mΩ = 50 mV, 0.5 W. A BJT has $V_{CE(sat)}$ of roughly 0.2–1 V at high current: 0.5 V × 10 A = 5 W.

**3. Majority-carrier switching.** No minority-carrier storage in the channel, so turn-off has no storage time. Switching speed is set by charging $C_{GS}$ and $C_{GD}$ through the driver: tens of ns for power parts, ps for logic. (The body diode is a PN junction and *does* have reverse recovery.)

**4. Positive tempco of $R_{DS(on)}$** (roughly 1.5–2× from 25 °C to 150 °C for silicon), from falling mobility. Paralleled devices self-balance in full enhancement. Caveat: at low $V_{GS}$ (linear-mode operation, below the zero-temperature-coefficient point), $I_D$ rises with temperature and hot-spotting can occur, so check SOA.

**5. CMOS and scaling.** Static power ≈ leakage only. Dynamic power $\alpha C V^2 f$. Dennard-style scaling (until ~mid-2000s) shrank $L$, $t_{ox}$ and $V_{DD}$ together, raising density and speed at constant power density. The planar self-aligned gate process is simple and dense.

**6. Ruggedness and convenience.** No secondary breakdown of the BJT kind, avalanche-rated parts, built-in body diode useful for freewheeling, huge catalogue of logic-level parts.

**Limits.** Silicon unipolar limit: $R_{on,sp} \propto V_{BR}^{\approx 2.5}$, so high-voltage MOSFETs have high $R_{DS(on)}$ and IGBTs (conductivity modulation) or SiC take over above roughly 600–1200 V at high current. Oxide is fragile to overvoltage and ESD ([[q0102]]). For low-noise, high-$g_m$-per-current analog, BJTs can win ([[q0120]]).
:::

::: short
"Four reasons. The gate is insulated, so it's voltage-driven and draws no current to stay on, which makes it trivial to drive from logic. When it's on it's just a small resistor, milliohms in modern parts, so conduction loss is low and paralleling works because $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature. It's a majority-carrier device, so it switches fast without storage time. And for digital, CMOS pairs draw almost no static power and MOSFETs scale down to billions per chip."
:::

::: long
"There are a few distinct reasons, and they apply differently to power and digital.

Start with the gate. It's insulated by an oxide, so the MOSFET is controlled by voltage and the gate is basically a capacitor. You need charge to switch it, but once it's on, it takes no current to stay on. Compare a power BJT carrying 10 amps: with a forced beta of around 20, that's half an amp of base current the whole time. A MOSFET with 20 nanocoulombs of gate charge switching at 100 kHz needs only about 2 milliamps on average. That's why you can drive a MOSFET straight from a microcontroller pin.

Second, conduction. A fully-on MOSFET is a resistor, not a fixed drop. With a 5 milliohm part at 10 amps, that's 50 millivolts and half a watt. A saturated BJT might drop half a volt, which is 5 watts. And since $R_{DS(on)}$ increases with temperature, a hotter device takes less current, so paralleled MOSFETs share current naturally, unlike BJTs, which tend toward thermal runaway.

Third, speed. Current flows by majority carriers in the channel, so there's no stored minority charge to remove. A saturated BJT has a storage time, but a MOSFET's switching speed just depends on how fast you charge its capacitances. That's what lets buck converters run at hundreds of kilohertz to megahertz, with smaller inductors and capacitors.

Fourth, digital. Pair an NMOS with a PMOS and you get CMOS, where in either logic state one device is off, so the gate only draws leakage. MOSFETs also scale: shrink the channel and the oxide, and they get smaller, faster and use less energy per switch. That's the engine behind Moore's law.

Where they lose: at high voltage, silicon MOSFET on-resistance rises steeply, so IGBTs or silicon carbide win in big motor drives. The thin gate oxide is easy to destroy with overvoltage or ESD. And for some low-noise analog work, a BJT gives more transconductance per milliamp. But for most switching and essentially all logic, MOSFETs are the default."
:::

::: terms
- **Insulated gate**: the gate is separated by oxide, giving voltage control and near-zero DC gate current.
- **Gate charge $Q_g$**: total charge needed to switch the gate between off and fully on. It sets drive current and losses.
- **$R_{DS(on)}$**: on-state drain-source resistance at a specified $V_{GS}$ and temperature.
- **Majority-carrier device**: conducts with one carrier type (electrons in NMOS), so there's no minority-carrier storage delay.
- **Positive temperature coefficient**: resistance rises with temperature, which helps current sharing.
- **CMOS**: complementary NMOS/PMOS logic with near-zero static current.
- **Scaling (Dennard scaling)**: shrinking dimensions and voltages together for higher density and speed.
- **Unipolar limit**: the trade-off where silicon MOSFET specific on-resistance rises steeply with breakdown voltage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "they're efficient and easy to drive."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates **power** reasons (voltage drive, resistive on-state, fast switching, paralleling) from **digital** reasons (CMOS static power, scaling).
- Quantifies one: "10 A needs 0.5 A of base drive for a BJT, but about 2 mA average gate current for a MOSFET at 100 kHz," or "5 mΩ at 10 A is 0.5 W vs 5 W."
- Explains *why* switching is fast (majority carriers, no storage time) and *why* paralleling works (positive tempco), with the linear-mode caveat.
- Names the **limits**: high-voltage conduction loss (IGBT/SiC territory), gate oxide fragility, BJT advantage for some analog.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"MOSFETs need no gate current."** Only at DC. Switching a power MOSFET fast needs amps of peak gate current. The win is that *holding* it on is free.
- **"MOSFETs are always more efficient than BJTs."** At high voltage and high current, IGBTs often have lower conduction loss. At tiny currents, the difference is irrelevant.
- **"Positive tempco means MOSFETs can always be paralleled safely."** True when fully enhanced. In linear mode at low $V_{GS}$, the tempco of $I_D$ flips and one device can hog current.
- **Ignoring the digital reason.** By transistor count, logic and memory dwarf everything else. CMOS static power and scaling are *the* reason MOSFETs dominate.
- **Treating "fast switching" as free.** Fast edges cause EMI and ringing, and switching losses still scale with frequency.
:::

::: deep
## Reason 1: the gate is a capacitor, not a junction

A BJT is current-controlled: to keep $I_C$ flowing you must keep injecting base current, roughly $I_C/\beta$. A MOSFET's gate sits on an insulator, so the only current is what charges the gate capacitance.

Worked comparison for switching a 10 A load at 100 kHz:

| | Power BJT | Power MOSFET |
|---|---|---|
| Control requirement | $I_B \approx I_C/\beta_{forced}$ = 10 A / 20 = **0.5 A continuous** | $Q_g$ = 20 nC per transition |
| Average drive current | 0.5 A (while on) | $Q_g f$ = 20 nC × 100 kHz = **2 mA** |
| Drive power | $I_B V_{BE}$ ≈ 0.5 A × 0.9 V ≈ 0.45 W | $Q_g V_{drive} f$ = 20 nC × 10 V × 100 kHz = **20 mW** |
| Can a GPIO drive it directly? | No (needs a driver or Darlington) | Often yes, with a logic-level part at low frequency |

That's a 20-fold drop in drive power and 250-fold drop in average control current. For digital logic, where a transistor holds its state almost all the time, it means static power is just leakage.

## Reason 2: on means "small resistor," not "fixed drop"

A saturated BJT always has a collector-emitter voltage of a few hundred millivolts, and it grows with current. A MOSFET in triode is a resistor, so at low current its drop is tiny, and you can make $R_{DS(on)}$ as small as you like by paralleling more cells (power MOSFETs are thousands to millions of cells in parallel).

At 10 A: a 5 mΩ MOSFET drops 50 mV and dissipates 0.5 W. A power BJT with $V_{CE(sat)} \approx 0.5$ V dissipates 5 W, plus the base-drive loss above. That difference is the difference between no heatsink and a real heatsink.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $V_{CE(sat)}$ at 10 A depends heavily on the part and the forced beta. 0.5 V is a representative value for a power BJT driven hard; small-signal BJTs at low current can be under 0.1 V. The comparison's point (fixed-ish drop vs resistive) holds regardless.

But the resistive on-state has a dark side at high voltage. For a silicon MOSFET, the drift region that blocks voltage must be thicker and more lightly doped as $V_{BR}$ rises, giving roughly $R_{on,sp} \propto V_{BR}^{2.5}$. A BJT-like device (the IGBT) floods its drift region with minority carriers when on (conductivity modulation), giving a near-constant drop instead.

## Reason 3: majority carriers switch fast

In a saturated BJT, the base region is flooded with excess minority carriers. To turn it off, those have to be swept out or recombine: the **storage time**, often hundreds of ns to µs for power BJTs. In a MOSFET, current is carried by majority carriers in a surface channel. Remove the gate charge and the channel vanishes. The switching speed is just the time to charge and discharge $C_{GS}$ and $C_{GD}$ through the driver resistance.

Why that matters: converter passives shrink with frequency. A buck's inductor ripple is $\Delta I = \frac{(V_{in} - V_{out})D}{L f_{sw}}$, so going from 50 kHz to 500 kHz lets you use a 10× smaller inductor for the same ripple. MOSFETs (and now GaN) made that possible.

## Reason 4: positive temperature coefficient (mostly)

Electron mobility in silicon falls as temperature rises (more lattice scattering), so a fully-on MOSFET's $R_{DS(on)}$ typically rises by around 1.5–2× from 25 °C to 150 °C. If two paralleled MOSFETs share a current and one gets hotter, its resistance rises and it takes *less* current: negative feedback. A BJT is the opposite: $V_{BE}$ falls about 2 mV/°C, so a hotter BJT takes more current, gets hotter still, and paralleled BJTs need emitter ballast resistors to avoid thermal runaway.

> [!WARNING]
> In linear-mode operation (hot-swap, electronic loads, linear regulators), $V_{GS}$ sits near threshold. There, the falling threshold with temperature dominates the falling mobility, so $I_D$ *increases* with temperature below the "zero temperature coefficient" point on the transfer curve. Hot spots can form inside one die. That's why you check the SOA curve, not just $R_{DS(on)}$, for linear-mode use.

## Reason 5: CMOS and scaling (the reason by volume)

The overwhelming majority of MOSFETs ever made sit in digital chips. Two properties make them unbeatable there:

1. **Complementary operation.** An NMOS pull-down and PMOS pull-up are never both on in steady state, so static current is only leakage. A BJT logic family (TTL, ECL) draws bias current all the time.
2. **Scaling.** Shrinking the channel length, oxide thickness and supply voltage together (Dennard scaling) gave smaller, faster, lower-energy transistors each generation. Dynamic energy per switching event is $\sim C V_{DD}^2$, so lower $C$ and $V_{DD}$ cut it quadratically. A BJT's vertical structure and its fixed $V_{BE} \approx 0.7$ V don't scale nearly as well.

## Where MOSFETs *don't* win

| Situation | Better choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hundreds of volts, high current (traction inverters, industrial drives) | IGBT, SiC MOSFET | Silicon MOSFET $R_{DS(on)}$ rises roughly as $V_{BR}^{2.5}$ |
| Very high frequency, high voltage (compact chargers) | GaN HEMT | Lower charge for a given $R_{DS(on)}$ |
| Low-noise, high-precision analog | BJT | Higher $g_m$ per unit current, lower 1/f noise, better $V_{BE}$ matching ([[q0120]]) |
| Cheap indicator LED at 10 mA from a 5 V MCU | Either; a BJT is fine | Drive current is trivial, cost dominates |
| Galvanic isolation, AC mains switching | Relay, SSR, triac | Isolation and AC blocking ([[q0414]]) |

> [!INTERVIEW]
> End with a limit. "They're the default for switching and logic, but above about 600 V and tens of amps I'd look at IGBTs or SiC, and for a low-noise front end I might still use a BJT." It shows you understand the trade-offs, not just the hype.
:::

::: applications
- **Every microcontroller-driven load switch**: logic-level NFETs driven straight from GPIO with no extra driver transistor.
- **Point-of-load buck converters** switching at 500 kHz–2 MHz on laptop and server boards, enabled by fast MOSFET switching.
- **Paralleled MOSFETs** in high-current DC applications like e-bike controllers and battery disconnects, relying on positive tempco for sharing (with gate resistors per device).
- **CMOS processors, FPGAs and memory**: billions of MOSFETs per chip, practical only because of near-zero static power and scaling.
- **Counterexample**: EV traction inverters historically used IGBT modules and now increasingly SiC MOSFETs, showing where silicon MOSFETs run out.
:::

::: assumptions
- Comparisons assume **silicon** devices at **low to moderate voltage** (under ~200 V), where MOSFETs are strongest. At 600 V+ the conduction-loss argument weakens.
- BJT numbers assume **hard saturation** with forced beta around 10–20, typical for power switching. Small-signal BJTs at low current behave differently.
- "Zero gate current" is a **DC** statement. Gate charge still costs driver power at high frequency.
- The CMOS argument assumes **bulk or FinFET CMOS**. Leakage at advanced nodes is not negligible, but it's still far below bipolar logic's bias currents.
- A valid alternative emphasis for an analog interviewer: "MOSFETs are common in analog *ICs* because they're what CMOS processes provide cheaply, even where a BJT would be technically better."
:::

::: followups
### How much gate drive power does a MOSFET need at high frequency?
$P = Q_g V_{drive} f_{sw}$, dissipated mostly in the driver and gate resistance, not the MOSFET. With $Q_g = 40$ nC, 12 V drive and 500 kHz: $40\text{n} \times 12 \times 500\text{k} = 0.24$ W per FET. That's why high-frequency designs pick low-$Q_g$ parts, and why GaN's much smaller gate charge matters.

### Why do MOSFETs share current when paralleled but BJTs don't?
MOSFET $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature (falling mobility), so a hotter device takes less current: negative thermal feedback. A BJT's $V_{BE}$ drops ~2 mV/°C, so at fixed $V_{BE}$ a hotter BJT takes more current: positive feedback, leading to current hogging and thermal runaway unless you add emitter ballast resistors. Even with MOSFETs, use separate gate resistors to avoid oscillation between parallel gates.

### When would you choose an IGBT over a MOSFET?
High voltage (roughly 600 V and above) at high current and moderate switching frequency (up to a few tens of kHz), such as motor drives and welders. The IGBT's conductivity modulation gives a near-fixed ~1.5–2 V drop instead of a large $R_{DS(on)}$. The cost is a turn-off tail current that limits frequency. SiC MOSFETs are now taking over much of this space.

### Do MOSFETs really have no storage time? What about the body diode?
The channel has none: it's majority-carrier conduction. But the body diode is a PN junction, so when it conducts (dead time in a half-bridge) it stores minority charge and has reverse recovery ($Q_{rr}$). In hard-switched bridges that recovery can be a major loss and EMI source, which is why some designs use Schottky-integrated or fast-recovery-body-diode MOSFETs, or GaN, which has no body diode.

### Harder: why did CMOS replace NMOS-only logic, if both use MOSFETs?
NMOS-only logic uses a pull-up load (a resistor or depletion-mode transistor) that conducts whenever the output is low, so a gate burns static power half the time. With millions of gates, that was untenable. CMOS replaces the load with a PMOS that's off whenever the NMOS network is on, so static current drops to leakage, at the cost of roughly twice the transistors.
:::

::: interpretations
"Why are MOSFETs used so often?" can be read as either "why MOSFETs instead of BJTs for discrete switching" or "why MOSFETs dominate electronics overall." Answer both: the power-switching reasons (voltage drive, resistive on-state, fast switching, paralleling) and the digital reasons (CMOS static power and scaling). The detailed head-to-head comparison is its own question ([[q0094]], with the BJT-section duplicate [[q0083]]).
