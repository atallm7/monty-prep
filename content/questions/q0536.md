::: testing
Whether you can justify "short is better" with two separate physical effects (resistance and inductance) and a third (antenna / coupling), and whether you know the few legitimate reasons to make a trace long (length matching, a deliberate fuse or heater, thermal isolation, a sense element). The interviewer wants numbers or at least scaling laws: $R \propto L$, $L \propto L\ln L$, and what each costs the circuit in volts and watts.
:::

::: why
Every unnecessary millimetre of a power or return path costs you voltage drop, heat and loop inductance, and it gives noise a bigger antenna to couple through. Most "mysterious" power problems on a first board turn out to be a regulator, a decoupling capacitor, or a sense resistor placed a few centimetres from where it should have been. The question is simple, but a strong answer connects it to placement decisions ([[q0543]], [[q0549]]) and shows you think in terms of what each millimetre buys.
:::

::: concepts
- **Resistance scales with length**: $R = R_\square L/w$, so doubling the length doubles the drop and the $I^2R$ heating. See [[q0534]].
- **Inductance scales (a bit worse than) linearly with length**: a straight strip is roughly 1 nH per mm for a thin trace with no nearby plane, and $V = L\,di/dt$.
- **Loop area**: what matters is the loop of supply plus return, not the trace alone. See [[q0486]].
- **Antenna length**: structures much shorter than a wavelength radiate poorly; long ones radiate and receive efficiently.
- **Propagation delay and resonance**: a long trace with a capacitor is an LC resonator.
- **Intentional long conductors**: fuses, heaters, meander-line length matching.
:::

::: answer
"For power and ground, shorter is almost always better. A trace is a resistor and an inductor, and both scale with length. For resistance it's straight proportional: a 1 mm wide trace at one ounce is about half a milliohm per millimetre, so 50 mm is 24 milliohms, and at 5 A that's 120 mV and 0.6 W for no benefit. The inductance goes up even a bit faster than linearly, around 1 nH per millimetre if there's no plane close by, so fast load steps develop volts across it: 50 nH with 5 A in 100 ns is 2.5 V. A long trace is also a better antenna, it picks up and radiates more noise, and it makes a resonant circuit with whatever capacitance is on it.

So I place the parts to make the high-current and fast-switching connections as short as I can, and give them a close return.

There are exceptions where long is deliberate: matching lengths on a bus with a meander, a sense or fuse element where you actually want a known resistance, or a long thin spoke to thermally isolate something. Those are specific choices, not a default."
:::

::: technical
**Resistance and heating.** $R = R_\square\,L/w$; with 1 oz copper $R_\square = 0.48$ mΩ. Power $P = I^2R$ and drop $\Delta V = IR$ both grow linearly with $L$. A 1 mm wide trace carrying 5 A: 10 mm is 24 mV / 0.12 W, 50 mm is 120 mV / 0.6 W, 100 mm is 240 mV / 1.2 W.

**Inductance.** A straight rectangular strip of length $l$, width $w$, thickness $t$ (all in mm) has partial self-inductance approximately

$$L \approx 0.2\,l\left[\ln\frac{2l}{w+t} + 0.5 + 0.2235\frac{w+t}{l}\right]\ \text{nH}$$

giving 7 nH at 10 mm, 22 nH at 25 mm, 51 nH at 50 mm and 115 nH at 100 mm for $w = 1$ mm. Loop inductance over a nearby plane is much lower: roughly $\mu_0 h\,l/w$, so 6.3 nH for 50 mm × 1 mm at $h = 0.1$ mm. Either way it grows at least linearly. Induced voltage is $V = L\,di/dt$.

**Radiation and pickup.** A conductor shorter than about $\lambda/20$ is an inefficient antenna; in free space $\lambda/20$ is 150 mm at 100 MHz and 15 mm at 1 GHz. A long trace carrying the harmonics of a switching edge (tens to hundreds of MHz) radiates and receives.

**Resonance.** A long supply trace $L_{tr}$ with a capacitor $C$ resonates at $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$; this can produce peaking in the PDN impedance ([[q0553]]).

**Legitimate long conductors.** Length-matching meanders, a fusible trace, a PCB heater or shunt, and thermal spokes.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The "about 1 nH per mm" figure is an isolated-strip rule of thumb; real loop inductance depends on the return path and plane spacing and can be several times lower or higher. Use it to rank layouts, not to predict exact ringing.
:::

::: short
"Shorter is better for power and ground. Resistance scales with length, so the drop and the heating do too: 50 mm of 1 mm trace at 5 A is 120 mV and 0.6 W. Inductance grows with length as well, about a nanohenry per millimetre, so a fast load step makes volts across it. Long traces are also better antennas and resonators. The exceptions are deliberate ones like length matching, fuses and sense elements."
:::

::: long
"For power and ground connections, shorter is better, and the reasons are resistance, inductance and noise.

Resistance first. A trace is a resistor whose value goes with length over width. One ounce copper is about half a milliohm per square, so a 1 mm trace that's 50 mm long is 50 squares, 24 milliohms. At 5 amps that's 120 millivolts and 0.6 watts, all wasted and all in a tiny area, so it also heats. Make it 10 mm and every number drops by five.

Inductance is the one people forget. A trace is roughly a nanohenry per millimetre if there's no plane close, so 50 millimetres is on the order of 50 nanohenries. When the load changes by 5 amps in 100 nanoseconds, that's V equals L di by dt, about 2.5 volts. The supply droops or rings at the load even though the DC drop looked fine. A close return plane reduces it, but it still scales with length.

Third, a long trace is a better antenna and a better receiver. When it carries the harmonics of a switching edge, it radiates, and it also picks up noise, and it forms an LC resonance with any capacitor on it.

So in placement I put the regulator, the decoupling and the sense parts so the critical paths are short and the returns are close. The exceptions are when length is the point: a meander to match delays in a bus, a fusible trace, a PCB heater, a thermally isolating spoke, or a shunt where you want known resistance. Those are intentional and I'd note them on the schematic."
:::

::: terms
- **Partial inductance**: the inductance attributable to a segment of a current loop; grows with length.
- **Loop inductance**: total inductance of the supply-plus-return loop; set by loop area.
- **$L\,di/dt$ drop**: the voltage across inductance when current changes quickly.
- **IR drop**: the DC drop $I\cdot R$.
- **Electrically short**: a structure much shorter than the wavelength of the signals on it (below about $\lambda/20$).
- **Meander / serpentine**: a deliberately lengthened trace for delay matching.
- **Fusible trace**: a narrow conductor designed to open at a known current.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "short traces have less resistance and less inductance."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies both: 0.48 mΩ/sq, tens of mV at amps, about 1 nH/mm, volts at A/100 ns.
- Notes that the *loop* matters, and a nearby return plane reduces inductance but not to zero.
- Mentions antenna efficiency and resonance, not just DC.
- Gives legitimate reasons for long conductors (length matching, fuses, heaters, thermal isolation) to show nuance.
- Connects to practical placement: input caps, decoupling, regulators, sense resistors ([[q0549]], [[q0555]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Counting only the resistance.** At fast edges the inductance dominates; a trace that is 5 mV of IR drop can produce volts of $L\,di/dt$.
- **Treating the supply trace alone as the problem.** The return is half the loop and counts equally.
- **Assuming a plane removes the length penalty.** A plane lowers inductance, but a long route still has more inductance and more resistance than a short one.
- **Rejecting all long conductors.** Length-matched buses and fusible traces are deliberate; say so.
- **Zig-zagging to "tidy" a power route.** Extra bends add length and loop area.
- **Using length as a heat-spreading argument.** A long trace concentrates heat along one narrow line; spreading is done by width, not length. See [[q0537]].
:::

::: deep
## Two penalties that both scale with length

Take a trace of length $l$ and width $w$ carrying current $i(t)$. The voltage across it is

$$v(t) = R\,i(t) + L\,\frac{di}{dt}, \qquad R = R_\square\frac{l}{w}$$

and $L$ increases at least linearly with $l$. Both terms are added to the load's supply, so any length you can remove comes straight off the drop.

### Resistive penalty

For 1 oz copper at 5 A, 1 mm wide:

| Length | Squares | R | Drop at 5 A | Heat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 mm | 10 | 4.8 mΩ | 24 mV | 0.12 W |
| 25 mm | 25 | 12 mΩ | 60 mV | 0.30 W |
| 50 mm | 50 | 24 mΩ | 120 mV | 0.60 W |
| 100 mm | 100 | 48 mΩ | 240 mV | 1.20 W |

At 3 mm width every number divides by three. On a 1 V rail that 120 mV is 12%, which is already outside a ±5% regulation spec before the regulator's own error. That is why [[q0543]] insists on placing a regulator close to its load.

### Inductive penalty

The inductance of a thin strip grows slightly faster than length because of the logarithm. For 1 mm wide, 35 µm thick copper:

Figure: Without a nearby return, a 1 mm trace behaves like about 1 nH per millimetre and slightly worse for long runs. Placing a plane 0.1 mm below cuts it by roughly eight. Even then it keeps growing linearly with length, so the shortest route still wins.

A load step of 5 A in 100 ns across 51 nH produces $51\times10^{-9}\cdot5/10^{-7}\approx 2.5$ V, and across 7 nH (10 mm) just 0.35 V. In practice the load's local capacitor supplies the transient and the trace sees a smoothed current, which is the whole point of decoupling ([[q0504]]), but the further the capacitor is, the more of that inductance sits between it and the chip.

### Radiation, pickup and resonance

A strip shorter than $\lambda/20$ is a poor antenna. At 1 GHz that is 15 mm in free space; at 100 MHz it is 150 mm. A switching converter's edges contain energy well into the hundreds of MHz, so a 50 mm switching-node trace is inside the efficient range. For the same reason a long, high-impedance feedback trace is a good receiving antenna ([[q0552]]).

A long supply trace plus a local capacitor is a resonator at $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$. 50 nH with 10 µF resonates near 225 kHz, right in the range of many regulator control-loop crossovers, which can cause oscillation or peaking. That is why input filters have damping and why remote capacitors need care ([[q0549]]).

## A worked placement example

A 12 V → 3.3 V, 5 A buck converter feeds an FPGA through 60 mm of 2 mm wide trace on a 1 oz outer layer, with the return through the plane.

- Resistance: 30 squares × 0.48 mΩ = 14.4 mΩ, so 72 mV and 0.36 W.
- Plane return: assume 0.5 mΩ, negligible.
- Result: 72 mV is 2.2% of 3.3 V, probably acceptable alone, but the FPGA's tolerance might be ±3% *including* the regulator's ±1% and the ripple.

Move the regulator to 15 mm from the FPGA and the same trace gives 18 mV. The 54 mV you recover is worth more than any component choice on that rail. This is exactly why remote sensing and the point-of-load architecture exist: they remove the length from the equation or compensate for it.

## When long is right

- **Length matching.** A DDR byte lane or a differential pair is lengthened with meanders to equalize delays.
- **Fusible links.** A deliberate narrow trace that opens at a known current. See [[q0544]].
- **Thermal isolation.** Thin long spokes between a hot area and a sensitive one reduce heat flow; see [[q0538]].
- **PCB heaters and shunts.** A long serpentine of known resistance can be a heater, or a low-cost sense element (using the copper's temperature coefficient of about 0.39%/°C).
- **Delay or filtering.** Trace inductance used as part of an LC filter, rare and controlled.

In all of these the length is a specified part of the design. For ordinary power and ground, it is a cost.

## Rules that follow from this

1. Place the regulator, its input capacitor and output capacitor as a group, as close to the load as thermal and EMI constraints allow ([[q0542]]).
2. Place decoupling at the pins it serves, and connect it with short, wide, close-return paths ([[q0553]]).
3. Place the sense resistor next to the load or the source and use Kelvin routing ([[q0555]], [[q0557]]).
4. Route the return directly under the supply on the adjacent layer so the loop area scales with length × tiny spacing ([[q0486]]).
5. After routing, measure the length of every high-current net and challenge any that is twice the straight-line distance.

::: applications
- **Buck converter input capacitor** placed against the VIN and PGND pins: the loop is a few millimetres, a few nH.
- **Point-of-load regulators next to FPGA cores** so that sub-1 V rails survive the drop budget.
- **Motor-driver boards** where the supply and return runs are kept short and paired to limit inductive spikes when phases switch.
- **Current-sense shunts** placed close to the part they protect so the sense lines are short ([[q0555]]).
- **Length-matched DDR memory buses**, where meanders are intentionally added.
- **Fuse-link traces** on low-cost consumer boards where a thin copper segment opens as a last-resort protection.
:::

::: assumptions
The scaling statements assume a uniform trace and DC to hundreds of MHz. The inductance numbers are for partial self-inductance of an isolated strip; the quantity that really governs ringing is the *loop* inductance, and with a close plane it is several times lower. The antenna statements assume the trace carries significant energy at wavelengths where it is longer than about $\lambda/20$, which depends on edge speed. For a very slow, low-current rail, length barely matters, and routing convenience can reasonably win. An alternative answer is "short for the supply and return, but sometimes long in return for isolation", which is covered above.
:::

::: followups
### Why is a long thin trace worse than a long wide one for inductance?
Inductance depends on the geometry: a narrower strip has a larger partial inductance since the magnetic flux is concentrated in a smaller conductor cross-section. A 100 mm strip at 1 mm is about 115 nH, at 3 mm about 94 nH, a modest change, so width helps resistance much more than inductance. To drop inductance by large factors you need a closer return, not just a wider conductor.

### How much does a long supply trace matter to a fast MCU?
Quite a lot. If the MCU's core draws 0.5 A in 10 ns steps, $di/dt$ is 50 MA/s, and 20 nH of trace gives 1 V. Decoupling next to the pins supplies the charge locally so the trace carries only a smoothed current, which is why placement of the capacitor is more important than the length of the trace to the regulator.

### Would you ever make a high-current trace longer on purpose?
Only for a specific reason: a fusible link, matching delay, or building a shunt or heater. In a power path I would not, since it adds drop and heat. If I needed a known resistance for current sensing I'd use a proper sense resistor, not a copper trace, because copper's resistance varies about 0.39% per °C.

### What happens at 1 GHz to a 20 mm power trace?
It is longer than $\lambda/20 = 15$ mm in free space, so it becomes an effective antenna and also a real transmission line, with impedance and reflections. The inductance of 20 mm is roughly 17 nH, which at 1 GHz is about 100 Ω of reactance, so the trace isolates the load from the supply entirely at that frequency. Only the local decoupling supplies current at GHz frequencies.

### A layout reviewer says your regulator can be 80 mm from the load because the IR drop is only 20 mV. Do you agree?
Not automatically. IR drop is only one metric. At 80 mm the inductance could be 80 to 100 nH, which gives large transient droop and a resonance with the output capacitor, and the long trace is a larger antenna. I would ask what the load step and edge rates are, and check the transient response and EMI, not just the DC drop.
:::

::: interpretations
"Shapes/traces" means any conductor on the PCB, whether a narrow trace or a copper pour. The question could be read as "long vs short for *signals*" too; the same logic applies (delay, reflections, crosstalk) but here the answer is framed around high-current and power routing. Siblings: width ([[q0537]]), planes ([[q0535]]), and the sense-resistor versions of the question ([[q0555]]).
:::
