::: testing
Whether you know the **ladder of current-limiting methods** and when each is right: a series resistor, a fuse, a resettable PTC, a current-limited supply or linear limiter, an eFuse or load switch, and the inherently limited driver (LED constant-current, motor current chopping). The interviewer wants trade-offs, not a list: voltage drop and power loss, response time, whether it resets, and the heat generated in the limiter during a fault. Being able to draw and size a simple active limiter (sense resistor, transistor, pass FET) is the strongest answer.
:::

::: why
Loads misbehave: a motor stalls, a cable shorts, a capacitor charges, a bulb filament is cold, a connector gets a paper clip across it. If nothing limits the current, the supply, wiring and load decide how bad it gets. Choosing the right limiter is a daily design task, and the wrong one either nuisance-trips (a fuse sized for the running current that blows on start-up) or fails to protect (a fuse that outlasts the trace). It follows [[q0454]] (why protect) and complements [[q0457]], which protects the supply itself.
:::

::: concepts
- **Series resistance limits current**: $I_{max}=V/R$, at the cost of voltage drop and power $I^2R$ in normal operation ([[q0001]], [[q0006]]).
- **Fuse $I^2t$ and melting time**: a fuse opens when the integral of $i^2$ over time reaches its melting value.
- **PTC (polyfuse)**: a polymer device whose resistance rises sharply when it heats from overcurrent, then resets when cooled.
- **Linear current limiter**: a transistor in series that is throttled by a sense-resistor feedback loop.
- **eFuse / load switch**: an integrated FET with current sensing, limit and fault reporting.
- **Inrush current**: $I=C\,dV/dt$ into a discharged capacitor, which can look like a short ([[q0343]]).
:::

::: answer
"I'd match the method to the fault and the cost of a false trip.

The simplest is a fuse. It opens when the current times time passes its rating, and it's cheap and reliable, but one-shot and slow for small overloads. A resettable PTC polyfuse does the same job and recovers when it cools, though it's slow and its resistance adds a bit of drop. A plain series resistor limits the fault current to V over R, which is fine for an LED, but for a power rail the drop and the I-squared-R loss kill it.

For real power rails I'd use active limiting. A linear current limiter is a pass MOSFET with a sense resistor and a transistor that throttles the gate when the drop across the sense resistor reaches about 0.6 V, so the limit is roughly 0.6 V over R. In a short the pass device dissipates the whole supply voltage times the limit, so it needs thermal protection. Modern designs use an eFuse or load switch that integrates the limit, a soft-start, thermal shutdown and a fault flag, and can latch or retry.

Then there are loads limited by design: constant-current LED drivers, motor drivers that chop the current, and soft-start or NTC limiters for inrush."
:::

::: technical
**Fuse.** Opens when $\int i^2dt$ reaches the melting $I^2t$. At large currents $t\approx I^2t/I^2$; for a 2 A fast fuse with $I^2t=0.5$ A²s (illustrative), a 10 A fault opens in 5 ms and 50 A in 0.2 ms. Continuous rating is typically derated (commonly to about 75% of the rated current at 25 °C; check the datasheet).

**PTC.** Hold current $I_h$ (no trip) and trip current $I_t$ (commonly about twice $I_h$; vendor-dependent). Trip time is seconds at modest overload and milliseconds at hard shorts. After tripping it conducts a small leakage current until reset.

**Series resistor.** $I_{fault,max}=V/R$. 5 V through 5 Ω limits a short to 1 A but at a 0.5 A load drops 2.5 V and wastes 1.25 W.

**Linear limiter.** $I_{lim}\approx V_{BE}/R_s\approx0.6\ \text{V}/R_s$. For 1 A: $R_s=0.6$ Ω (0.6 W at the limit). $V_{BE}$ varies from about 0.55 to 0.7 V with temperature and part, so the limit can be 0.92 to 1.17 A. In a short from 12 V the pass FET dissipates $12\ \text{V}\times1\ \text{A}=12$ W.

**Inrush.** Charging 1000 µF to 12 V with source resistance 0.1 Ω would demand up to 120 A; a 5 Ω NTC reduces it to about 2.4 A. A soft-start ramp bounds it by $I=C\,dV/dt$.
:::

::: long
"I'd start by asking what the fault looks like and what a false trip costs, then pick from a ladder of methods.

At the passive end, a fuse is the cheapest and most reliable. It opens when current squared times time reaches its rating, so it's fast for a hard short and slow for a small overload, and it's one-shot. A resettable PTC polyfuse does the same job and recovers once the fault is gone, but it's slow, it adds a bit of resistance, and its trip current depends on temperature. A plain series resistor limits a short to V over R, which is perfect for an LED, but on a power rail the voltage drop and I-squared-R loss make it impractical. For example, 5 ohms on a 5 volt rail limits a short to 1 amp, but at half an amp it drops 2.5 volts.

For real rails I'd use an active limiter. A discrete version uses a P-channel MOSFET in series, a sense resistor, and a PNP transistor whose base-emitter junction watches the sense resistor. When the drop reaches about 0.6 volts, the PNP turns on and pulls the gate up, throttling the FET. So the current limit is about 0.6 over R, with 0.6 ohms giving 1 amp. The cost is that in a short the FET dissipates the whole supply voltage times that current, 12 watts at 12 volts, so it needs thermal shutdown or a timer. Integrated eFuses and load switches do the same with accurate limits, soft-start, thermal protection and a fault flag, and can latch off or retry.

Finally, some loads are limited by design: constant-current LED drivers, motor drivers with current chopping, and soft-start or NTC limiters for capacitor inrush."
:::

::: terms
- **Fuse $I^2t$**: the energy-like integral $\int i^2dt$ that melts the fuse element.
- **Hold / trip current (PTC)**: the maximum current that does not trip, and the current at which it will trip.
- **Current limit**: the maximum current the limiter allows; above it, the output voltage falls.
- **Foldback**: reducing the limit as the output voltage falls ([[q0456]]).
- **Sense resistor**: a small series resistor whose voltage drop measures the current ([[q0450]]).
- **eFuse**: an integrated current-limited switch with fault flag, OVP and thermal shutdown.
- **Inrush current**: the transient current to charge input capacitance when power is applied.
- **SOA (safe operating area)**: the voltage-current-time region where a FET can run without damage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use a fuse" or "use a resistor".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Offers a **ladder of options** and the trade-offs of each (drop, loss, speed, reset, cost).
- Sizes a **discrete linear limiter** and states the limit equation $\approx V_{BE}/R_s$, its **tolerance** and the **fault dissipation** $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{lim}$.
- Knows fuse behaviour in terms of **$I^2t$** and **derating**, and the difference between **hold and trip** current for a PTC.
- Separates **inrush** from a real fault and handles it with soft-start or an NTC.
- Notes that some loads are **limited by design** (CC LED, motor chopping).
- Mentions **thermal shutdown** and the **retry policy** ([[q0456]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Sizing a fuse at exactly the running current.** It will nuisance-blow from tolerance, temperature derating and inrush; size to about 1.5 to 2 times typical current while keeping it below the weakest conductor's rating.
- **Using a fuse to protect against a small overload only.** A fuse may take minutes or never to open at 1.2 to 1.5 times rating; it is for gross faults.
- **Using a series resistor on a power rail.** The drop varies with load current and wastes power; the output is no longer a stiff voltage ([[q0148]]).
- **Forgetting the limiter's own heat.** A linear limiter in a short dissipates $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{lim}$, which can destroy the pass FET unless there is thermal protection or a timer.
- **Treating inrush as a fault, or ignoring it.** Charging a large capacitor looks like a short for a few milliseconds. See [[q0343]].
- **Relying on a PTC for fast protection.** Polyfuses are slow thermal devices and cannot protect semiconductors from microsecond faults.
:::

::: deep
## Two questions hiding in one

"Protect a load from pulling too much current" can mean two things. Either the load is a legitimate consumer that might fail or be overdriven (an LED, a motor), or the load is a fault that the supply must be protected from. In both cases the fix is to put something between the supply and the load that bounds the current. The rest of this page works through the options from simplest to most sophisticated.

## Passive limiters

### Series resistor

The simplest: $I_{fault,max} = V/R_{series}$. Perfect for an LED: with a 5 V supply, 2 V forward drop and 20 mA target, $R = (5-2)/0.02 = 150\ \Omega$. For a power rail it is a poor choice: a 5 V rail with a 5 Ω resistor limits a short to 1 A but a 0.5 A load sees only 2.5 V and the resistor burns $0.5^2\times5=1.25$ W. The output voltage depends on load ([[q0148]]).

### Fuse

A fuse is a calibrated thin conductor. It heats as $I^2R$ and melts when the accumulated $\int i^2dt$ reaches the melting $I^2t$ value. At large overcurrents the heat has no time to leave and $t\approx I^2t/I^2$:

Figure: On log-log axes the melting time falls with the square of the current: ten times the current opens the fuse a hundred times faster. This is why a fuse is excellent against hard shorts and poor against small overloads (near the rated current heat escapes as fast as it is generated, the $I^2t$ approximation breaks down, and opening times become very long or infinite).

Practical rules: choose the fuse **rated current** with derating (continuous load no more than about 75% of rating is a common guideline; check the datasheet), choose the **voltage and interrupting rating** above the circuit's, and choose **fast or slow-blow** according to inrush: a slow-blow (time-delay) fuse tolerates a motor start-up or capacitor charge, a fast fuse protects semiconductors. The fuse must protect the weakest element: a trace or wire must survive until the fuse opens (compare the conductor's heating curve with the fuse curve).

### PTC polyfuse

A polymer with conductive particles, which expands when heated by overcurrent, breaks the conductive paths and its resistance rises by orders of magnitude. It resets as it cools. Specs: hold current $I_h$ and trip current $I_t$ (typically around twice $I_h$; vendor-dependent), maximum voltage and maximum fault current. Drawbacks: slow (seconds near trip current), resistance (tens to hundreds of mΩ), trip current falls at high ambient temperature, and it never fully resets to its original resistance. Good for USB ports and battery-powered gadgets where the cost of replacement matters.

### NTC thermistor for inrush

An NTC has high resistance when cold, which limits inrush when a capacitor is first charged, then heats and drops resistance. With 1000 µF at 12 V and a 0.1 Ω source the unlimited peak could approach $12/0.1=120$ A; with a 5 Ω NTC it is about $12/5.1=2.4$ A. Drawback: after a brief power cut the NTC is still hot and low-resistance, so a quick re-power sees full inrush.

## Active limiters

### A discrete linear current limit

The classic circuit uses a series P-FET as the pass element, a sense resistor $R_s$ ahead of it and a PNP transistor whose base-emitter junction is wired across $R_s$:

Figure: $R_g$ normally pulls the P-FET gate low so it is fully on. When the drop across $R_s$ reaches about 0.6 V, Q2 turns on and feeds current into the gate node, raising the gate and reducing the FET's $V_{GS}$. The loop holds the current at roughly $0.6\ \text{V}/R_s$ (1 A here).

**Operation.** Below the limit Q2 is off, the gate is pulled low by $R_g$ and the FET is on, with a small drop of $IR_s$ ($0.5\text{ A}\times0.6\ \Omega=0.3$ V). As load current approaches $0.6\ \text{V}/R_s$, Q2 begins to conduct and lifts the gate, so the FET enters its linear region and the output voltage drops to keep the current at the limit. The limit is $I_{lim}\approx V_{BE}/R_s$.

**Design numbers.** For 1 A, $R_s=0.6$ Ω and its dissipation at the limit is $I^2R_s=0.6$ W (size it for at least 1 W). $V_{BE}$ falls about 2 mV/°C and varies between parts, so the limit is somewhere between 0.92 A and 1.17 A for $V_{BE}$ from 0.55 to 0.7 V, and it reduces as the PNP warms (an inherent thermal-protection-like effect, but not something to rely on). The pass FET in a hard short from 12 V dissipates $12\times1=12$ W: far above what a small package handles for long. Real designs add a thermal shutdown or a timer that latches the FET off after a fault window, which is what integrated parts do.

> [!NOTE]
> This circuit also needs the gate voltage kept inside the FET's $V_{GS}$ limits: for $V_{in}$ above about 15 to 20 V a Zener or divider is added between source and gate. Use it for low-voltage rails, or use an integrated eFuse.

### eFuse and load switch

An eFuse combines the sense element, a precisely controlled pass FET (adjustable current limit, typically ±5 to 10%), thermal shutdown, soft-start (controlled $dV/dt$), OVP/UVLO, reverse-current blocking and a fault output ([[q0455]], [[q0456]]). The restart behaviour is selectable: latch-off or auto-retry. An eFuse is the modern answer for protecting an individual rail or connector.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Typical numbers for limit accuracy, trip time and retry behaviour differ widely by vendor and part; treat the figures here as typical ranges and use the datasheet for the part you select.

### Inrush control by soft-start

$I_{inrush}=C\,dV/dt$. Ramping 100 µF to 5 V in 1 ms draws 0.5 A; in 100 µs, 5 A. A load switch with a slew-rate pin (or a capacitor on the gate of a P-FET switch) turns an abrupt step into a controlled ramp, so the limit can be set close to the real operating current without nuisance trips.

## Loads that are limited by design

- **LEDs**: a constant-current driver or a series resistor fixes the current regardless of small supply changes.
- **Motors**: a driver with current-mode PWM chopping caps the winding current at a programmed limit, which also limits torque and stall heating (see [[q0343]]).
- **Batteries**: charger ICs have constant-current phases ([[q0321]]) and packs have protection ICs.
- **Linear regulators and bench supplies**: built-in current limit, sometimes with foldback.

## Choosing: a quick decision

| Situation | Reasonable choice |
|---|---|
| LED or signal path | Series resistor or constant-current driver |
| Gross short, low cost, one-shot acceptable | Fuse |
| Consumer port that must self-reset | PTC or eFuse |
| Rail with capacitive load and hot-plug | eFuse or hot-swap controller with soft-start |
| Motor | Driver current limit plus a fuse upstream |
| Sensitive load needing precise limit | Active current limit with sense resistor and comparator |

The limiter always trades **accuracy, speed, loss and reset behaviour**; the best answer states which one matters for the case at hand.
:::

::: applications
- **USB ports and chargers**: eFuse or load switch per port, with accurate current limit and fault flag.
- **Battery-powered devices**: PTC or fuse plus the battery protection IC's overcurrent trip ([[q0295]]).
- **Automotive modules**: fused branches and smart high-side switches with limit and diagnostics.
- **Hot-swap server cards**: hot-swap controllers with sense resistors, soft-start and retry timers.
- **LED lighting**: constant-current drivers, so brightness and heating do not depend on supply variation.
- **Motor drives**: peak current limiting at the H-bridge level ([[q0425]]) plus an upstream fuse.
:::

::: assumptions
I assumed a DC low-voltage system (a few to tens of volts, up to a few amps) so that integrated eFuses and discrete linear limiters are applicable. At mains voltage the choice of fuse and protective device is governed by safety standards (certified fuses, breakers, creepage and clearance) and cannot be improvised. The linear limiter's dissipation limits its use to modest powers; at high power a switch-mode current limit (a buck in current mode, or a hysteretic switched limiter) or a simple fuse plus a robust supply is preferable. If the load is an LED or motor, the right answer shifts to constant-current drive. The numbers (fuse $I^2t$, PTC trip ratio, $V_{BE}$) are typical teaching values: check datasheets.
:::

::: followups
### How would you size a fuse for a 12 V rail that normally draws 1 A with a 3 A start-up surge lasting 20 ms?
Choose a slow-blow (time-delay) fuse. A rating of about 2 A (continuous load 50% of rating, comfortably under the common 75% derating guideline) with a melting $I^2t$ larger than the surge's $3^2\times0.02=0.18$ A²s, with margin for repeated cycling (fuses fatigue with repeated surges, so use roughly 2 to 3 times that). It must still open for the fault level before the wiring is damaged and its voltage rating must exceed 12 V.

### Why is a PTC a poor choice to protect a MOSFET from a short?
A PTC is a slow thermal device: at a hard short it can take milliseconds to trip, while a MOSFET may fail in microseconds from excess current or SOA violation. It's suited for protecting wiring and connectors from sustained overload, not for protecting semiconductors. Use a fast electronic limit or a fast fuse upstream for that.

### What happens to the linear limiter's FET during a long short, and how do you protect it?
It dissipates $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{lim}$ (12 W for 12 V and 1 A) and heats quickly, because small FETs have junction-to-ambient thermal resistances of tens of °C/W (a 12 W fault could heat a SOT-23 FET by hundreds of degrees). Add thermal shutdown, a fault timer that latches the switch off after a few milliseconds, or foldback, or use a part whose SOA covers the fault time.

### What if the load legitimately draws much more current for a short time (motor start)?
Use a limit with a time delay, or a two-level scheme: a high instantaneous limit for hard shorts and a lower limit that trips only after a timer for overloads; or soft-start the motor so start-up current is lower. The fuse or limiter must be above the legitimate peak and still below the damage level; if they overlap, you need to reduce the peak, not widen the limit.

### Scenario: your USB-powered gadget resets whenever you plug it in. What do you check?
Plug-in charges input capacitance, so inrush ($C\,dV/dt$) may exceed the host's current limit (500 mA for USB 2.0 in the standard, usually much less before negotiation: check the spec) and the host's eFuse trips or the 5 V sags. Check the input capacitance (a 100 µF cap with a fast edge is amps), add a load switch with controlled slew, or a series inrush limiter. Scope the VBUS during plug-in to confirm the dip.
:::

::: interpretations
The wording is ambiguous: it can mean limiting how much current the load draws from the supply (protecting supply and wiring from a faulty or stalled load) or protecting the load from receiving too much current (an LED or a sensor). The methods overlap, so the page covers both, with the circuit-level answer being a current limiter between supply and load. Related: [[q0457]] looks from the supply's side and [[q0456]] covers what happens after the limit is reached.
:::
