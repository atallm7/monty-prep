::: basics
### In plain words
Boosts often lose more power than bucks, for two reasons. The inductor and switch carry more current than the load does, since the input current is $I_{out}/(1-D)$. And in a simple boost, the diode carries all of the output current all of the time, wasting power in its forward drop. The gap is not fundamental: a synchronous boost with similar parts gets close to a buck.

### Picture it
Picture a toll booth. In a buck, the cars pay the toll only part of the time. In a simple boost, every car that reaches the output has to pay the toll, which is the diode's forward voltage. So the toll collected over a day is larger. If you replace the toll booth with an open lane (a synchronous switch), the extra cost almost disappears.

### Words you need
- **Conduction loss**: power lost as heat as current passes through a resistance, $I^2R$.
- **Forward voltage ($V_F$)**: the voltage a diode drops while conducting, so the loss is $V_F\times I$.
- **Schottky diode**: a diode with a low forward drop, about 0.4 V in the low-voltage case.
- **Synchronous rectification**: replacing the diode with a transistor that has a much lower drop ([[q0272]]).
- **Efficiency**: output power divided by input power.

### The simplest example
Take a boost with a 5 V, 1 A output, so 5 W out. A Schottky diode with $V_F=0.4$ V wastes $0.4\times1=0.4$ W, about 8% of the output power. Replace it with a synchronous MOSFET of 50 mΩ. Its current comes in pulses bigger than 1 A, so it loses a bit more than $1^2\times0.05=0.05$ W: about 0.067 W in the page's 3.7 V to 5 V example. That one change saves about a third of a watt, most of the gap.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page gives the full loss budget for a boost and a buck at the same power, and the smaller secondary effects.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can explain a **real, quantitative reason** instead of repeating a rule of thumb. Boosts *tend* to be less efficient than bucks at the same power, but the reason is not a magic property of the topology. The interviewer is listening for:

1. The key mechanism: inductor (and switch) current in a boost is $I_{out}/(1-D)$, larger than the output current, while in a buck the inductor current equals $I_{out}$.
2. The diode: in a non-synchronous boost the diode carries the whole load current at $V_F$ for the entire off-time.
3. The honesty to qualify the claim: with synchronous rectification, equal parts and equal power the two are close.
4. Secondary effects: pulsed output current, high duty cycle limits, the right-half-plane zero, and higher switch-node voltage.
:::

::: why
In a real project you will choose between a boost and a buck-based architecture (stack more cells and buck, or boost from fewer cells) and you need to quantify the cost. Understanding where boost losses come from also tells you how to improve them: go synchronous, pick lower DCR inductors, cap the boost ratio, and keep $D$ away from 90%. The same loss-budget method is used throughout power design ([[q0162]], [[q0164]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Conduction loss** $P=I_{rms}^2R$: grows with the *square* of the current, so a higher inductor current costs disproportionately. See [[q0001]].
- **Energy conservation**: $I_{in}=I_{out}V_{out}/(\eta V_{in})$. Step-up means higher current on the source side. See [[q0154]].
- **Diode loss** $V_F\cdot\bar I_D$. See [[q0202]] for the buck freewheel diode.
- **Switching loss**: overlap loss, $C_{oss}$ loss $\tfrac12C_{oss}V^2f$, gate charge. See [[q0221]].
- **Duty cycle limits**: near $D=1$ the off-time is too short for anything to be delivered.
- **Efficiency definition**: $\eta=P_{out}/(P_{out}+P_{loss})$. See [[q0162]].
:::

::: answer
"The first reason is current per amp of load. In a buck the inductor carries the output current. In a boost, energy conservation says the inductor carries the input current, $I_{out}/(1-D)$, so for the same load current the inductor and switch carry more, and at a high boost ratio a lot more. Conduction loss goes with current squared, so the DCR and $R_{DS(on)}$ losses get bigger. To be precise, the inductor always sits on the low-voltage side, so at the same power and the same two voltages a buck and a boost have the same inductor current; the penalty shows up when you need the same load current, or a big ratio.

The second reason, and usually the biggest, is the diode. In a non-synchronous boost the diode carries the entire load current, so you lose $V_F\times I_{out}$ continuously. A Schottky at 0.4 V and 1 A is 0.4 W on a 5 W output, 8% gone. In a buck the diode only conducts for $(1-D)$ of the time, so the loss is smaller at high duty cycle.

Then there are secondary things: the output current is pulsed, so the capacitor has higher ripple current and loss, the switch node is at the *output* voltage, which is higher, and at high duty cycle the off-time gets short and losses climb fast.

To be fair, it isn't fundamental. With a synchronous boost and similar parts, the loss budget is close to a synchronous buck at the same power. The penalty shows up mostly with non-synchronous parts and high ratios."
:::

::: technical
**Currents (CCM, ideal).**
- Buck: $\bar i_L=I_{out}$, $\bar i_{in}=DI_{out}$.
- Boost: $\bar i_L=I_{in}=\dfrac{I_{out}}{1-D}$, switch RMS $\approx I_{in}\sqrt D$, diode average $I_{out}$.

**Boost loss budget:**
$$P_{loss}\approx\underbrace{I_{in,rms}^2R_{DCR}}_{\text{inductor}}+\underbrace{I_{in,rms}^2D\,R_{DS(on)}}_{\text{switch}}+\underbrace{V_FI_{out}}_{\text{diode}}+P_{sw}+P_{gate}+P_Q$$

with $I_{in,rms}^2=I_{in}^2+\Delta I_L^2/12$. For 3.7 V to 5 V at 1 A, $L$ DCR 30 mΩ, $R_{DS(on)}$ 50 mΩ, $V_F$ 0.4 V, $f=500$ kHz:

| Loss | Boost (non-sync) | Buck (5 V to 3.7 V, same 5 W) |
|---|---|---|
| Inductor DCR | 55 mW | 55 mW |
| Main switch | 24 mW | 68 mW |
| Diode | 400 mW | 141 mW |
| Total (conduction) | 479 mW | 264 mW |
| $\eta$ | **91.3%** | **95.0%** |

Same hardware, same 5 W: the boost loses about twice as much. Note that the inductor current is the same 1.35 A in both (the inductor sits on the 3.7 V side in each), so the gap here is almost entirely the diode, which conducts for 74% of the period in the boost but only 26% in the buck.

**With synchronous rectification** both topologies have identical conduction loss in this comparison (147 mW, $\eta=97.1\%$), because they are duals: at equal power the boost switch carries $I_{in}$ for $D$ and the other FET for $(1-D)$, just as the buck does.

**Where the boost really loses:** at equal *output current* (not power), boost inductor current is $I_{out}/(1-D)$ and loss grows as $1/(1-D)^2$. At $D=0.9$ the inductor current is 10x the load current. Combined with duty-cycle limits and the RHP zero, high-ratio boosts are pushed to the edge.
:::

"Two things: current and the diode. In a boost the inductor and switch carry $I_{out}/(1-D)$, more than the load current, and conduction loss goes with current squared, so DCR and $R_{DS(on)}$ losses are bigger. In a non-synchronous boost the diode also carries the full load current at $V_F$, which is about 8% of a 5 V, 1 A output. Add pulsed output current, higher switch-node voltage and duty-cycle limits. With synchronous rectification and a modest ratio the gap mostly closes."
:::

::: long
"The efficiency gap is real but it's mostly about currents and the diode, not about boosts being fundamentally worse.

Start with current. In a buck the inductor is in series with the output, so it carries $I_{out}$. In a boost the inductor is in series with the *input*, and by energy conservation the input current is $I_{out}/(1-D)$. At 3.7 V to 5 V and 1 A that's 1.35 A, and at a 2x ratio it's 2 A. Conduction loss goes with current squared, so inductor DCR and switch $R_{DS(on)}$ cost more. At $D=0.9$ the inductor carries ten times the load current.

Second, the diode. In a non-synchronous boost the diode carries the whole load current, average $I_{out}$, so loss is $V_F\times I_{out}$ regardless of duty cycle. Take a 0.4 V Schottky at 1 A: 0.4 W on 5 W, which is 8%. In a buck the diode conducts only for $(1-D)$ of the cycle, so with $D=0.74$ it loses about a third of that.

I ran numbers for the same inductor and FET: the boost came out at about 91% and the buck at about 95% for 5 W. Make both synchronous and both land around 97%, because they're duals.

Third, secondary issues. The boost output current is pulsed so the output capacitor sees larger RMS ripple. The switch node swings to $V_{out}$, so Coss loss uses the higher voltage. At high $D$ the short off-time limits delivered energy and the right-half-plane zero forces a slower loop.

So in an interview I'd say: usually yes, a few percent, because of current scaling and the diode, but you can close most of the gap with a synchronous rectifier and by limiting the boost ratio."
:::

::: terms
- **Conduction loss**: $I_{rms}^2R$ in the switch, inductor DCR and (if used) synchronous FET.
- **Diode forward loss**: $V_F\cdot\bar I_D$, constant for given $I_{out}$ in a boost.
- **Switching loss**: overlap, output-capacitance, reverse-recovery and gate-drive losses that scale with $f_{sw}$.
- **DCR**: DC resistance of the inductor winding.
- **Synchronous rectification**: replacing the diode with a FET to cut forward-drop loss.
- **Duality**: the boost's switch positions mirror the buck's. Their loss budgets mirror each other for the same power and ratio.
- **Right-half-plane zero**: limits loop speed and indirectly efficiency of the compensation choices.
- **Quiescent loss**: controller supply current, dominant at light load.
:::

**An average answer** says "boosts have higher current so they lose more".

**An exceptional answer**:

- Shows that synchronous buck and boost have equal budgets at equal power, which proves it is parts and conditions, not topology.
- Adds the output-current-pulse and RHPZ points and ties efficiency to the duty-cycle limit.
- Gives a quantitative example (91% vs 95%) and recommends how to close the gap.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Boosts are inherently less efficient."** Not fundamentally. Compare like with like (sync vs sync, equal power, equal ratio).
- **Blaming the inductor energy storage.** Storing and releasing energy in an ideal inductor is lossless. The losses are all in resistances, diode drop and switching.
- **Forgetting the diode loss is constant** with $D$ at fixed $I_{out}$: $V_FI_{out}$. That is why a boost with a Schottky is bad at *high current, low voltage*.
- **Comparing at equal output current instead of equal power** without saying so. A 3.7 V to 5 V boost at 1 A and a 5 V to 3.7 V buck at 1 A deliver different powers.
- **Ignoring light load.** At light load, fixed losses (gate drive, controller current) dominate in *both* topologies, and the efficiency gap is about quiescent current and mode (PFM) rather than conduction loss.
- **Using the ideal duty cycle in the loss estimate** at high ratios. With resistive drops the real $D$ is larger and losses rise faster than the ideal formula says.
:::

::: deep
## Start with the current

Take a boost with $V_{in}=3.7$ V, $V_{out}=5$ V, $I_{out}=1$ A. Power conservation gives $I_{in}\ge1.35$ A. Compare with a buck delivering 5 W: 5 V in, 3.7 V out, $I_{out}=1.35$ A and $I_{in}=1.0$ A. Both converters process 5 W, both have a 1.35 A inductor, and the *dual* relationship means they are electrically symmetric. The difference appears when you ask for the **same output current**:

| Quantity | Buck (5 V to 3.7 V) at 1 A out | Boost (3.7 V to 5 V) at 1 A out |
|---|---|---|
| Output power | 3.7 W | 5 W |
| Inductor current (avg) | 1.0 A | 1.35 A |
| Duty cycle | 0.74 | 0.26 |
| Conduction loss (same parts, non-sync) | 172 mW | 479 mW |
| Efficiency | 95.6% | 91.3% |

At equal output *current* the boost inductor works 35% harder, which is 82% more $I^2R$. The ratio gets steeper with boost ratio: $I_L/I_{out}=1/(1-D)$, so 1.35x at $D=0.26$, 2x at $D=0.5$, 5x at $D=0.8$, 10x at $D=0.9$.

## The diode is the big-ticket item

A rectifier diode that carries the load for $(1-D)T$ at peak current $I_{pk}$ has *average* current $I_{out}$ in a boost. Its loss is $V_FI_{out}$, **independent of duty cycle**. For a 0.4 V Schottky:

$$P_D=0.4\text{ V}\times1\text{ A}=0.4\text{ W}\;\Rightarrow\;8\%\text{ of 5 W}$$

A buck's freewheel diode conducts for $(1-D)$ of the cycle only: $P_D=V_FI_{out}(1-D)=0.4\times1.35\times0.26=0.14$ W. That is why nearly every modern boost IC above a watt or so moves to a synchronous FET. At 50 mΩ and 1.16 A RMS in the off-time the FET loses only $1.16^2\times0.05=67$ mW, a saving of about 330 mW, which brings the boost to 97.1%, identical to the sync buck.

## What the model leaves out

- **Switching loss.** The switch node swings to $V_{out}+V_F$. Overlap loss is about $\tfrac12V_{out}I_{pk}(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}$. With 5 V, 1.56 A, 10 ns total transition, 500 kHz: $0.5\times5\times1.556\times10^{-8}\times5\times10^5=19$ mW. At 24 V or 48 V outputs, Coss and overlap losses grow with $V_{out}^2$ and $V_{out}$.
- **Diode reverse recovery** (silicon PN diodes in high-voltage boosts like PFC). A silicon-carbide Schottky removes it, which is why PFC boosts use SiC diodes.
- **Gate drive loss** $Q_gV_{drv}f$, the same in both topologies.
- **Core and AC winding losses** in the inductor: ripple current is the same $\Delta I_L$ in both, but boost DC bias is higher, which reduces inductance near saturation ([[q0046]]).
- **Output-capacitor RMS ripple.** $I_{C,rms}\approx I_{out}\sqrt{D/(1-D)}$ for a boost (0.59 A at $D=0.26$, 1.73 A at $D=0.75$) vs a buck whose output capacitor sees only inductor ripple (about $\Delta I_L/\sqrt{12}\approx0.12$ A).

## Higher ratio, steeper penalty

The ideal expression $V_{out}=V_{in}/(1-D)$ fails at high $D$. With a series resistance $R$ in the inductor/switch path, the conversion ratio peaks and then collapses as $D$ rises, because the resistive drop is multiplied by $1/(1-D)^2$ relative to the load resistance. Practically, the usable range is roughly up to $D\approx0.8$ to 0.9 depending on the controller's minimum off-time, which is one reason datasheets list a "maximum duty cycle" (often in the range of 85% to 95%, vendor-dependent).

## Light-load efficiency

At light load all converters lose efficiency to fixed overheads (gate drive, controller $I_Q$, core loss). For a boost, one extra point: a non-synchronous boost can never turn off its output (the input-output diode path), and a *synchronous* boost at very light load is prone to reverse inductor current, so many parts run in forced-diode (pseudo-non-synchronous) or burst mode. See [[q0230]].

## Worked example: a 3.3 V to 12 V boost at 0.5 A

The 3.7 V to 5 V example sits at a gentle ratio. Push it harder with 3.3 V in, 12 V out, 0.5 A (6 W), 500 kHz and 4.7 µH. Assumed parts: inductor DCR 40 mΩ, switch 60 mΩ, a 0.4 V Schottky (or a 40 mΩ synchronous FET), and 10 ns total switching transitions. With the diode drop, the duty cycle is $D=(V_{out}+V_f-V_{in})/(V_{out}+V_f)=9.1/12.4=0.734$.

**Currents.** The input current is $I_{in}=I_{out}/(1-D)=0.5/0.266=1.88$ A, 3.8 times the load current. The ripple is $\Delta I=V_{in}D/(Lf)=3.3\times0.734/(4.7\ \mu\text{H}\times500\ \text{kHz})=1.03$ A, so the peak is $1.88+0.52=2.39$ A, which sets the inductor saturation rating. The RMS current squared is $1.88^2+1.03^2/12=3.62\ \text{A}^2$. For comparison, a buck delivering 0.5 A would carry only 0.5 A in its inductor. The $I^2R$ penalty at equal output current is $1/(1-D)^2=14$ times.

| Loss | Calculation | Diode boost | Synchronous boost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inductor DCR | $3.62\times0.040$ | 145 mW | 145 mW |
| Switch conduction | $D\times3.62\times0.060$ | 159 mW | 159 mW |
| Rectifier | diode: $0.4\times0.5$; sync: $(1-D)\times3.62\times0.040$ | 200 mW | 39 mW |
| Overlap | $\tfrac12\times12.4\times(1.36+2.39)\times5\ \text{ns}\times500\ \text{kHz}$ (turn-on at the valley, turn-off at the peak) | 58 mW | 58 mW |
| $C_{oss}$ (150 pF assumed) | $\tfrac12C V^2f$ | 6 mW | 6 mW |
| Gate drive | one or two FETs | 10 mW | 20 mW |
| Dead time (sync only) | $0.7\times1.88\times2\times20\ \text{ns}\times f$ | | 26 mW |
| **Total** | | **578 mW** | **453 mW** |
| **Efficiency** ($6\ \text{W}$ out) | | **91.2%** | **93.0%** |

Three things show up. First, the diode is not the dominant term here: at a 12 V output and 0.5 A, its 200 mW is smaller than the two series resistances together (304 mW). The sync advantage is 1.8 points, not the 6 points seen at a 5 V output, because the diode's 0.4 V is only 3% of 12 V. Second, the $I^2R$ terms are 14 times what a buck would see at the same output current and resistances, which is where the penalty hides at this ratio. Third, the inductor DCR is the largest single term. Copper's resistance rises 0.39% per °C, so at a 100 °C winding temperature it is about $1+0.0039\times75=1.29$ times its 25 °C value (52 mΩ), adding about 42 mW and costing 0.6 points. The same effect on the switch is typically larger, because silicon $R_{DS(on)}$ usually rises faster with temperature than copper.

The lesson for an interview is that the answer to "why is a boost less efficient" depends on the ratio and the output voltage: the diode dominates at low voltage out, the series resistances dominate at high ratio, and a synchronous rectifier fixes the first but not the second.

## Output capacitor stress

A buck has a smooth output current (the inductor feeds the capacitor continuously) while a boost delivers current to the output only during the off time. The capacitor RMS current is $I_{C,rms}\approx I_{out}\sqrt{D/(1-D)}=0.5\times\sqrt{0.734/0.266}=0.83$ A, against a buck's inductor-ripple-only current of about $\Delta I/\sqrt{12}$. At 0.83 A in a capacitor with 10 mΩ of effective ESR that is $0.83^2\times0.01=7$ mW. That is small here, but with an electrolytic at 150 mΩ it is 100 mW, more than the overlap, $C_{oss}$ and gate-drive terms combined, and it is also a lifetime limit. The same current flows in the pulsed output loop, so the output capacitor and the rectifier must be placed tightly. Check the ripple-current rating of the capacitor, not just its capacitance.

## Limiting cases

As $D\to0$ (a boost with $V_{out}$ barely above $V_{in}$), the boost behaves like a nearly pass-through element and its efficiency tends toward the diode or synchronous-FET drop over $V_{out}$. As $D$ increases the $1/(1-D)^2$ penalty takes over: at $D=0.9$ the inductor carries ten times the output current and the series resistance loss is 100 times the equal-current buck's. At that point, either choose a different topology (a flyback or a transformer-coupled stage), cascade two boosts, or accept a loss budget of 15% or more.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The DCR, $R_{DS(on)}$, $C_{oss}$ and transition-time values in this section are illustrative. Datasheet efficiency curves are for a particular inductor and conditions, and published numbers often exclude the inductor's AC and core loss.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Measure the inductor current.** A current probe on the inductor should show a triangle riding on 1.88 A with a 1.03 A ripple. A flattened top near 2.4 A means the inductor is saturating, which will raise losses sharply.
2. **Measure input and output power with the same instruments.** Efficiency is a ratio, so any calibration error appears twice. Use the same meter to read both sides if you can, at steady state.
3. **Find the rectifier loss.** On a diode design, measure the voltage across the diode at its on-time current with a scope and a short probe. You will see 0.3 to 0.5 V depending on current and temperature. Multiply by $I_{out}$.
4. **Thermal check at the corners.** Run at minimum $V_{in}$ and maximum load, since $1/(1-D)^2$ is worst there, and note which part is hottest: inductor, switch or diode. That is the part to upgrade first.

::: applications
- **Choosing between boost and a higher-voltage cell stack + buck**: a 2S Li-ion pack feeding a buck to 5 V often beats a 1S cell with a boost, because the boost current is 1.5 to 2x the load current.
- **Synchronous boost ICs in power banks**: chosen to remove the diode loss at 2 to 3 A.
- **PFC design**: SiC or ultrafast diodes in a boost to minimize reverse-recovery loss at 400 V.
- **Low-power sensor nodes**: nano-power boost where quiescent current dominates and the diode loss is irrelevant at microamp loads.
- **Thermal budgeting**: a boost IC at its current limit at low $V_{in}$ is typically the hot spot in a portable device.
:::

::: assumptions
- Efficiency comparisons depend on **component choice, load, ratio and switching frequency**. I compared equal parts ($R_{DS(on)}$ 50 mΩ, DCR 30 mΩ) at equal power for fairness. Real parts differ.
- **Non-synchronous with Schottky diode** unless noted. Synchronous improves boost more than buck at the same ratio because the diode loss $V_FI_{out}$ is larger.
- **CCM** at moderate load. In DCM and at light load the dominant losses are fixed (gate, $I_Q$, core).
- The statement "boosts are less efficient than bucks" is a **tendency** valid for typical ratios and parts. A boost at 1.2x ratio can be more efficient than a buck at 10x step-down.
- Reported efficiencies in datasheets are at specific $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$, load and temperature. Compare like conditions.
:::

::: followups
### Does a synchronous boost fix the efficiency gap?
Mostly. It replaces $V_FI_{out}$ (0.4 W at 1 A) by $I_{rms}^2R_{DS(on)}$ (0.07 W for 50 mΩ), moving 3.7 V to 5 V at 1 A from about 91% to 97%. It adds dead-time loss (body diode conduction) and gate-drive loss and complicates light-load operation, but above a watt or so it is the standard choice.

### At what boost ratio do you start to worry?
Roughly beyond 4 to 5x ($D>0.75$ to 0.8). Inductor current is 4 to 5 times the load current, so conduction loss is 16 to 25 times larger relative to the output current, and the controller's maximum duty cycle becomes the limit. Above about 5 to 10x, use a coupled-inductor boost, a flyback or two stages.

### Would you choose a boost or a buck from a 2S pack for a 5 V rail?
From 2S Li-ion (6 to 8.4 V) a buck gives a clean step-down with inductor current equal to the load current and good efficiency, while a 1S cell needs a boost with 1.3 to 1.85x the input current. Choosing 2S usually wins on efficiency. The trade-off is pack cost, balancing electronics and the need to protect two cells ([[q0295]]).

### How does temperature affect the comparison?
$R_{DS(on)}$ and copper DCR rise with temperature (copper about 0.39% per °C), so conduction loss grows as the part heats up. A boost running hot at low $V_{in}$ and high current is more at risk of thermal runaway-like feedback than a buck, because its losses scale as the square of the larger current.

### Scenario: your boost's efficiency is 8% lower than the datasheet says. Where do you look?
First check *conditions*: datasheets quote at specific $V_{in}$, load, inductor and temperature. Then measure: input and output power with the same calibrated meter at steady state. Inductor: is DCR or $I_{sat}$ worse than the datasheet curve? Switch: is $V_{GS}$ at the intended value? Diode: Schottky $V_F$ is much higher at high current and temperature. Layout: long SW loops raise ringing and overlap loss. Also check that you are not in a low-efficiency mode (forced PWM at light load).
:::
