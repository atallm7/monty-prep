::: basics
### In plain words
A power plane is a whole layer of copper that carries a supply voltage, such as 3.3 V, across the board. It goes on an inner layer, right next to a ground plane, with the thinnest insulation the stackup allows. Packed that close, the two planes act like a large, low-inductance capacitor that helps feed fast current demands from the chips.

### Picture it
Think of a town's water supply. Pumping water from a distant reservoir takes time, and the pipes resist sudden demand. A water tower right in town answers instantly when everyone turns on a tap. The plane pair is the tower, sitting right beside the chips. The decoupling capacitors are buckets you carry in, and they do most of the work at lower frequencies. The analogy breaks in one place: the plane is a capacitor storing charge, and what makes it fast is its tiny inductance.

### Words you need
- **Plane capacitance**: $C=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/h$. The thinner the insulation $h$, the more capacitance ([[q0022]]).
- **Spreading inductance**: the inductance a via sees when feeding a plane pair. It grows with $h$ ([[q0509]]).
- **Power distribution network (PDN)**: the planes, vias and decoupling capacitors that deliver current ([[q0506]]).
- **Target impedance**: the PDN impedance you must stay under so the rail does not droop too much.
- **Plane split**: dividing a power layer into islands for different rails ([[q0493]]).

### The simplest example
A 3.3 V rail may droop 5%, which is 0.165 V, with a 1 A fast step. So the target is 0.165 V / 1 A = 0.165 Ω. A 100 mm by 100 mm plane pair with 0.1 mm of insulation has about 3.8 nF. At 500 MHz its impedance is about 0.084 Ω, under the target. With 1 mm of insulation it is 0.38 nF and 0.84 Ω, five times over. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers spreading inductance, plane resonance, split planes, high-current rails and the capacitor and plane interplay.
:::

::: why
Power integrity problems (rail droop, ground bounce, supply noise coupling into ADCs or clocks) are as common as signal integrity problems, and plane placement is one of the few fixes that costs no components. A good answer shows you understand that the plane's *neighbour* matters more than its absolute layer number, and that the power plane is a return path for signals referenced to it. See [[q0501]] and [[q0506]] for the decoupling-capacitor side of the same story.
:::

- **Spreading inductance**: the inductance a via sees feeding a plane pair, proportional to $h$. See [[q0509]].
- **Power distribution network (PDN)**: planes, vias and decoupling capacitors together deliver current across frequency. See [[q0506]].
- **Return path**: a signal over a power plane returns through it, then through decoupling capacitors. See [[q0478]].

::: answer
"I put power planes on inner layers, directly next to a ground plane, with as thin a dielectric between them as the stackup allows.

On a four-layer board that's layer 3, right under the ground on layer 2. On a six-layer, I'd put power on layer 4, next to the ground on layer 5, so the power and ground planes are neighbours. The reason is that a power plane and ground plane close together act like a big parallel-plate capacitor, with very little inductance, and that helps decoupling at high frequency, where discrete capacitors stop helping because of their own inductance. It also keeps the loop for the current feeding the chips small.

I avoid putting full power planes on the outer layers because that's where the components and the routing are, and I'd rather use pours there for high-current rails. If there are several rails, I split the power layer into islands, put the biggest or the one with the fastest load closest to the chip it feeds, and keep fast signals from crossing the gaps, since a signal referenced to a split plane loses its return path. And I still use decoupling capacitors, because at 50 or 100 MHz and below they're doing most of the work."
:::

::: technical
**Where:** inner layers, adjacent to ground, smallest practical spacing $h$.

**Plane capacitance** (100 mm × 100 mm, $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$):

| $h$ | $C=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rA/h$ | Spreading $L$ (via $a=0.15$ mm, edge $b=50$ mm) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 mm | 3.8 nF | 0.12 nH |
| 0.2 mm | 1.9 nF | 0.23 nH |
| 1.0 mm | 0.38 nF | 1.2 nH |

with $L \approx \frac{\mu_0 h}{2\pi}\ln\frac{b}{a}$. At 100 MHz, 0.12 nH is 0.07 Ω and 1.2 nH is 0.73 Ω.

**Why this matters:** discrete capacitors have their own mounting inductance (0.5–1.5 nH typical for a 0402 with vias). Above about 100–200 MHz the planes' capacitance and low spreading inductance take over. A tight P–G pair therefore extends the PDN's low impedance to higher frequency.

**Plane resonance.** A rectangular plane pair resonates at $f=\frac{c}{2\ell\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}$ for length $\ell$: 723 MHz for 100 mm. Decoupling caps and lossy designs damp it.

**Constraints:**
- Power planes (and splits) should not be adjacent to the thick core only: spacing matters.
- High-current rails: use copper weight and width for the current; 1 mm of 1 oz outer trace carries roughly 2–2.4 A for a 10 °C rise (IPC-2221 outer-layer curve; inner layers carry less).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The spreading inductance formula assumes a circular via feeding a large plane pair and neglects fringing; the plane resonance estimate assumes an unloaded rectangular cavity. Both are first-order. Use PDN tools for sign-off. Current ratings from IPC-2221 are conservative curves and depend on airflow and copper thickness.
:::

::: short
"Power planes go on inner layers, right next to a ground plane with a thin dielectric between them. That pair forms a low-inductance capacitor that helps decoupling at high frequency and keeps current loops small. In four layers that's layer 3 under the layer 2 ground; in six layers, layer 4 next to the layer 5 ground. I keep full planes off the outer layers, use pours there for high-current rails, split inner planes for multiple rails and avoid routing fast signals across the splits."
:::

::: long
"For power planes I care about two things: where they sit in the stack, and what they sit next to.

Placement-wise, they go on inner layers. The outer layers are where the parts are and where most signals are routed, so a solid power plane would be cut to pieces. Inside, a power plane is protected, and it can serve as a reference plane for signals on adjacent layers if it has to.

The more important thing is the neighbour. I want the power plane directly next to a ground plane, separated by a thin dielectric. Together they form a parallel-plate capacitor: for a hundred millimetre square board with a tenth of a millimetre of FR-4, that's almost four nanofarads. More importantly, the loop inductance of the pair is tiny, so the spreading inductance a via sees is around a tenth of a nanohenry. Discrete capacitors have a nanohenry or so of mounting inductance, so above a couple of hundred megahertz the planes do the work. In a four-layer board, that means power on layer 3 under ground on layer 2, though the core between them is thick, so I'd lean on discrete capacitors. In a six-layer board, I'd put power on layer 4 beside ground on layer 5.

The practical stuff: with several rails I split the power layer into islands and keep fast signals off the gaps so they don't lose their return. For heavy current I use wide pours or heavier copper rather than relying on the plane's resistance. And I never drop the discrete decoupling capacitors, because planes only help at high frequency."
:::

::: terms
- **Power plane**: a solid copper layer carrying a supply rail.
- **Plane pair**: adjacent power and ground planes forming a capacitor.
- **Spreading inductance**: inductance of current fanning out through a plane pair from a via.
- **PDN**: power distribution network; its impedance should stay below a target across frequency.
- **Target impedance**: $Z_{target}=\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I_{transient}$.
- **Split plane**: one layer divided into several rail regions.
- **Anti-pad**: clearance in a plane around a via that isn't connected to it.
- **Plane resonance**: standing-wave resonance of the cavity between two planes.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer talks about *adjacency and spacing*, quantifies plane capacitance and spreading inductance, and puts planes in context: planes extend the PDN above the range where discrete capacitors are effective, but don't replace them. It addresses splits, return paths and resonance, and it is honest about the 4-layer compromise (thick core between P and G, so little plane capacitance). It mentions that heavy-current rails get copper pours, not planes, and that high-speed signals should not be referenced across splits.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Power plane gives me lots of capacitance"** on a 4-layer board with a 1 mm core between the planes. It gives 0.38 nF for 100 × 100 mm, little compared with a pair of 100 nF caps.
- **Putting the power plane on the outer layer** and then routing signals around it.
- **Skipping decoupling caps because there's a plane.** Planes only help above ~100 MHz; the caps are needed below that.
- **Splitting the power plane and routing fast signals over the gap.** The return current has to jump the gap.
- **Giving a high-current rail a thin inner plane and no pours.** Inner copper is thinner and can't dissipate heat as well; check the current rating.
- **Ignoring plane resonances.** A big unbroken plane pair is a cavity; at its resonant frequency it can radiate or amplify noise.
:::

## A power plane is a transmission structure

At DC, a power plane is a resistive sheet. At 1 MHz it's a resistor and inductor. At hundreds of MHz it's a two-dimensional transmission line (a parallel-plate waveguide) with wave propagation and resonances. All three regimes matter in a PDN.

## Why a thin dielectric between power and ground helps twice

**Capacitance** scales as $1/h$:

$$C=\frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A}{h}$$

**Inductance** scales as $h$. For a pair of planes, loop inductance per square is $\mu_0 h$, and the spreading inductance from a via of radius $a$ out to distance $b$ is

$$L\approx \frac{\mu_0 h}{2\pi}\ln\frac{b}{a}$$

Together, the characteristic impedance of the plane pair $\sqrt{L/C}$ falls as $h$ falls (since $L\propto h$ and $C\propto 1/h$ gives $Z\propto h$). So halving $h$ halves the impedance that the planes present. In a plot:

## The numbers in context

Take a 3.3 V rail with a 5% allowed ripple and a 1 A fast transient: $Z_{target}=0.165$ V/1 A = 0.165 Ω. A 100 × 100 mm plane pair with $h=0.1$ mm has $C=3.8$ nF, so at 500 MHz its capacitive impedance is $1/(2\pi\cdot5\times10^8\cdot3.8\times10^{-9})=0.084$ Ω. This is below the target. With $h=1$ mm, $C=0.38$ nF and the impedance at 500 MHz is 0.84 Ω, five times over. The planes alone can't meet the target, so you rely on capacitors for most of the band, and the on-die and package capacitance beyond.

## Plane resonance

A power–ground plane pair is a rectangular cavity, resonant when the side is a multiple of half a wavelength in the dielectric:

$$f_{mn}=\frac{c}{2\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\sqrt{\left(\frac{m}{a}\right)^2+\left(\frac{n}{b}\right)^2}$$

For a 100 mm side (first mode): 723 MHz; for 60 mm: 1.2 GHz. If a noisy source excites such a frequency, the cavity rings, producing high impedance peaks and radiation from the edges. Remedies: decoupling capacitors spread across the board, a tight P–G spacing (lower Q and impedance), lossy materials (some designs use embedded-capacitance laminates), and not placing fast sources near high-field spots.

## Split planes and returns

With several rails, a power layer may contain islands, say 3.3 V, 1.8 V and 1.2 V. Rules:
- Keep each island under or beside the IC it feeds.
- Don't route fast signals across gaps between islands. If a signal on the adjacent layer references that plane, its return current has to jump the gap, via the nearest decoupling capacitor or around the split. Reference such signals to the ground plane on its other side instead.
- Leave enough width in each island: a narrow neck between two regions is a resistor and an inductor.
- Alternatively dedicate one inner layer to power with a pour and keep one rail per layer.

## High-current rails

For a rail carrying several amps, use outer-layer pours in 1–2 oz copper, with plenty of vias to inner layers. As a rough guide, an external 1 mm × 1 oz trace carries about 2.4 A for a 10 °C rise by IPC-2221; inner layers carry about half as much. The plane layer itself is a convenient low-resistance second path ($0.48$ mΩ/□ for 1 oz), but it doesn't carry far without droop.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Give two numbers if you can: "a 100 mm square pair at 0.1 mm gives ~4 nF and ~0.1 nH of spreading inductance; at 1 mm both are 10× worse." That one sentence shows you know why a six-layer board with a tight P–G core is different from a four-layer one.

## Worked example: the anti-resonance between a capacitor and the plane

Adding a decoupling capacitor next to a power plane pair creates a parallel resonance, and that peak, not the nominal capacitance, is what you must design around. At frequencies above the capacitor's own self-resonance it looks like its mounting inductance $L$ (ESL plus via loop), and that inductance sits in parallel with the plane pair's capacitance $C_{pl}$. Take a 0402 ceramic with 1 nH of total mounting inductance on the 100 mm by 100 mm, $h=0.1$ mm pair ($C_{pl}=3.8$ nF):

$$f_{ar}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L\,C_{pl}}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(10^{-9})(3.8\times10^{-9})}}\approx 82\ \text{MHz}$$

Two other numbers frame this. The capacitor's own series resonance with a 100 nF value is $1/(2\pi\sqrt{10^{-9}\cdot10^{-7}})=15.9$ MHz, so above 16 MHz it is inductive and the plane capacitor must take over. And the characteristic impedance at the crossover, $\sqrt{L/C_{pl}}=0.51\ \Omega$, sets how high the peak can climb. For a series loss of $R=20$ mΩ in the inductive branch, an undamped parallel resonance peaks near $(L/C_{pl})/R=0.26/0.02\approx13\ \Omega$. That is a worst-case upper bound: real plane dielectric loss, many capacitors in parallel and the other branches damp it by a large factor. But it shows why a target impedance of 0.165 Ω can be violated at one frequency by a design that looks fine at DC and at both ends of the band.

With a thick 1 mm core the plane capacitance is 0.38 nF and the same capacitor resonates with it at $f_{ar}=258$ MHz, with $\sqrt{L/C}=1.6\ \Omega$. A tighter pair pulls the anti-resonance down in frequency but with a lower characteristic impedance; a looser pair pushes it up with a higher one. Neither is automatically better: the cure is to lower $L$ (several capacitors in parallel, short wide vias, vias beside or in the pad) and to add loss, not to chase one value.

## Worked example: IR drop in a power plane

A plane's DC resistance matters on the heavy rails. For 35 µm copper, $R_\square=\rho/t=1.72\times10^{-8}/35\times10^{-6}=0.49$ mΩ per square. A 1.2 V core rail that allows 5 % total DC drop (60 mV) and draws 10 A can afford at most $R=60\ \text{mV}/10\ \text{A}=6$ mΩ, which is $6/0.49\approx12$ squares of 1 oz plane between regulator and load. A plane 50 mm wide and 600 mm long would be 12 squares, but real boards are far shorter; the practical hazard is the neck. A rail island that narrows to 5 mm wide for 40 mm is 8 squares: 3.9 mΩ and 39 mV at 10 A before counting via resistance. Vias add up too: a 0.3 mm drill plated through a 1.6 mm board is about 1 mΩ, so a 10 A rail wants a dozen or more vias in parallel at the regulator and again at the load.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **Plane capacitance is a function of overlap, not board area.** Only the region where the power island overlaps ground counts, and splits and anti-pads remove it. The 3.8 nF figure is the upper bound for a solid, full overlap.
- **Dielectric properties move.** FR-4's $\varepsilon_r$ and loss tangent change with frequency and temperature, and plane capacitance can shift 10-20 % across vendors (flag as vendor-dependent). Embedded-capacitance laminates with 8 to 25 µm dielectrics exist and are specified by their vendors; check the datasheet for the capacitance density rather than assuming.
- **Via quality.** A capacitor fed through a long, thin via from an inner plane is dominated by that via. Place the plane layers close to the capacitor's side of the board when you have a choice: the layer closest to the parts gives the shortest via.

## Bench verification

The standard PDN measurement is a two-port shunt-through measurement with a vector network analyzer. Connect one port to the rail at the IC's location and the other port to the same rail with the part unpowered, and read $S_{21}$. For a small impedance relative to 50 Ω, $Z\approx 25\,S_{21}/(1-S_{21})$ ohms, which is roughly $25\,S_{21}$ when $|S_{21}|\ll1$; at $S_{21}=-40$ dB (0.01) the impedance is about 0.25 Ω. Plot it against your target. Check the anti-resonance peaks: a peak that exceeds target is fixed by adding loss (a series resistor in the capacitor's mounting, or a capacitor with a higher ESR such as a tantalum or polymer part) or by changing the capacitor values so the peaks fall between harmonics of the noise source. Also measure on a powered board with a scope on the rail, bandwidth limit off, using a spring-tip probe, to see the real droop during a load step.

::: applications
- **Six-layer processor boards**: power on L4 over ground on L5, with thin core; island-split by rail.
- **FPGA boards**: multiple split rails (core, I/O, auxiliary) on one or two inner power layers, with via-in-pad decoupling.
- **Four-layer MCU boards**: power plane on L3, with discrete decoupling carrying most of the load.
- **High-current motor controllers**: power distributed on 2 oz outer-layer pours; inner plane as a secondary path.
- **Embedded-capacitance stackups**: very thin (e.g., 12–50 µm) P–G dielectric for GHz-range decoupling in dense designs.
:::

::: assumptions
- FR-4 rigid multilayer, $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$, plane pair spacing quoted from the stackup.
- First-order formulas for capacitance, spreading inductance and cavity resonance; real PDN design uses tools and measurements.
- Power–ground adjacency is assumed possible; in a 4-layer board with a thick core the benefit is small and you rely on discrete capacitors.
- Power distribution by pours rather than planes is common for single-rail or high-current boards, and for 2-layer boards there are no planes at all.
- Some designs use ground–power–ground triplets or place power next to a *signal* layer on purpose; this answer is the general rule.
:::

::: followups
### Why not put the power plane on the top layer?
Because that's where parts and signals are. The plane would be broken into slivers by pads and traces and wouldn't give uniform spreading or capacitance, and it would be exposed to probes and ESD. If the rail is heavy current, a pour on top is fine, with the power plane inside as well.

### Is a power plane better than a wide trace for distribution?
For low resistance and inductance, yes, particularly for many loads across a board. A wide trace or pour is fine for a few amps to one load. A plane wins when many devices share the rail and it also gives the signals a return path and some capacitance.

### How does a power plane relate to decoupling capacitors?
They cover different frequency bands: bulk and ceramic capacitors dominate from kHz to ~100 MHz; the plane pair's capacitance and low inductance extend that to GHz. The plane doesn't replace capacitors, since 4 nF at 10 MHz is 4 Ω, much higher than a 100 nF cap (0.16 Ω), but the capacitors' inductance limits them above ~100 MHz.

### What if your 4-layer board has a 1 mm core between power and ground?
Then plane capacitance is only about 0.38 nF for 100 × 100 mm and the spreading inductance is above 1 nH. I'd plan for discrete capacitors at each IC pair with short vias, consider a thinner core if the fab can build it, or choose a 6-layer stackup with a tight P–G pair if the design needs high-frequency decoupling.

### Scenario: noise at 700 MHz appears on a quiet rail in a 100 mm board even though you have lots of decoupling caps. What might be going on?
A plane cavity resonance: the first mode is about 720 MHz for a 100 mm side. Capacitors have little effect that high because of their ESL, so the cavity rings when excited by a nearby fast edge. I'd locate the source, add capacitors near it and across the board to break up the mode, tighten the P–G spacing if possible, and check the signals referenced to that plane for return-path problems.
:::
