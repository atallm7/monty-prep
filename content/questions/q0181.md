::: testing
Whether you can reason about a buck **at the edges of its operating range**, not just the happy middle. The interviewer is probing:

1. 100% duty: the high-side is permanently on, $V_{out}\approx V_{in}$ minus drops, there's no switching and no regulation. This is dropout.
2. 0% duty: the high-side is permanently off, the inductor discharges into the load, and $V_{out}$ falls (or current reverses, in forced-PWM synchronous parts).
3. The *practical* catch: real controllers can't sit at either extreme because of bootstrap refresh, minimum on-time and minimum off-time.
4. What each extreme means for the loop, efficiency and the input source.
:::

::: why
Edge cases are where real designs fail. A battery sags until the converter hits 100% duty and the "regulated" rail follows it down. A fault or overvoltage drives duty to 0% and a synchronous converter starts pumping energy backward into the input. A high-side NFET driver that depends on a bootstrap capacitor can't stay on forever. Questions like this separate candidates who have only seen the ideal equation from those who have thought about what the circuit physically does when the loop saturates. It also feeds directly into dropout specs, minimum on-time limits and PFM operation ([[q0222]], [[q0229]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Duty cycle limits**: $D\in[0,1]$, but real controllers have $D_{min}=t_{on,min}f_{sw}$ and $D_{max}=1-t_{off,min}f_{sw}$ ([[q0179]]).
- **Dropout**: at 100% duty $V_{out}=V_{in}-I(R_{hs}+R_{DCR})$ and no regulation is possible.
- **Inductor behaviour**: $v_L=L\,di/dt$. At 100% the inductor sees constant DC; at 0% it sees $-V_{out}$ and its current ramps down.
- **Bootstrap supply**: a high-side NFET's gate drive is stored on a capacitor refreshed only when SW goes low ([[q0206]]).
- **Diode vs synchronous rectification**: a diode blocks reverse inductor current; a forced-PWM synchronous FET does not ([[q0203]]).
- **Control saturation**: the error amplifier output is clamped at the modulator limits when the loop can't reach the setpoint.
:::

::: answer
"At 100 percent duty the high-side switch never turns off. The switch node sits at the input voltage minus a small drop, the inductor has a constant DC voltage across it, so there's no ripple, and the output is just the input minus the drops across the switch and inductor resistance. Nothing is regulating anymore; the converter is acting as a low-resistance pass element. That's dropout. For 2 A through 110 mΩ, the output is about 0.22 V below the input.

Practically, a high-side NFET driven with a bootstrap capacitor can't stay on forever, because the bootstrap cap is only recharged when the switch node goes low, and it slowly drains. So controllers have a minimum off-time or a periodic refresh pulse, which limits the maximum duty to maybe 95 to 99 percent, unless there's a charge pump or a PMOS high-side.

At 0 percent the high-side is off all the time. The inductor sees minus $V_{out}$ so its current ramps down to zero at $V_{out}/L$. In a diode or diode-emulating converter it stops at zero and the output decays through the load. In a forced-PWM synchronous converter the current continues negative, so the output capacitor pushes energy back into the input, which can raise the input voltage if nothing sinks it.

In both cases the loop is saturated, so regulation is lost."
:::

::: technical
**100% duty ($D=1$).** $Q_1$ continuously on, $Q_2$/diode off.
- SW $=V_{in}-I R_{hs}$ (DC). Inductor: $v_L=V_{in}-IR_{hs}-IR_{DCR}-V_{out}=0$ in steady state, so no ripple.
$$V_{out}=V_{in}-I\,(R_{hs}+R_{DCR})$$
- Efficiency $\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$, like an LDO with very small dropout. Example: $R_{hs}=80$ mΩ, $R_{DCR}=30$ mΩ, 2 A: dropout $=0.22$ V.
- If $V_{in}$ is above the target but the loop is faulty (open feedback), $V_{out}\to V_{in}$ and the inductor current runs to the current limit.

**0% duty ($D=0$).** $Q_1$ continuously off.
- Inductor: $v_L=-V_{out}$, so $di/dt=-V_{out}/L$. From 2 A with $V_{out}=3.3$ V and $L=4.7$ µH it reaches zero in $LI/V_{out}=2.85$ µs.
- Diode/diode-emulation: current stops at zero (DCM), SW floats, $V_{out}$ decays at $\approx I_{load}/C$ ($0.09$ V/µs for 2 A and 22 µF).
- Forced-PWM synchronous: current goes negative at slope $-V_{out}/L$ and flows from $C_{out}$ back to $C_{in}$.

**Practical limits.**
- $D_{min}=t_{on,min}f_{sw}$: 100 ns at 500 kHz gives 5%. Below that, the controller skips pulses.
- $D_{max}=1-t_{off,min}f_{sw}$: 100 ns at 500 kHz gives 95%. This allows the bootstrap capacitor to recharge each cycle.
- True 100% operation needs a PMOS high-side, a charge-pump gate supply, or a controller with a bootstrap refresh.
:::

::: short
"At 100 percent duty the high-side is always on, there's no switching or ripple, and $V_{out}$ is the input minus the drops across the switch and inductor, so the converter is in dropout and not regulating. At 0 percent the high-side is always off, the inductor current ramps down at $V_{out}/L$, and the output decays; in a forced-PWM synchronous buck the current even reverses and feeds energy back to the input. In real parts, bootstrap refresh and minimum on and off times keep the duty cycle away from both extremes."
:::

::: long
"I'll take the two extremes separately.

At 100 percent duty, the high-side switch is on all the time. The switch node is at the input minus a small drop, and the inductor sees a constant DC voltage, so there's no ripple and the current just sets itself to the load. In steady state the inductor voltage is zero, which gives $V_{out} = V_{in} - I(R_{hs}+R_{DCR})$. So the converter behaves like a very low-resistance pass element: for 2 amps through about 110 milliohms, the output is 0.22 volts below the input. This is dropout, and it's usually how a battery-powered buck behaves as the battery runs down: the regulator saturates at maximum duty and the output follows the input.

The practical complication is the high-side gate drive. An NFET high-side needs a gate voltage above the input, supplied by a bootstrap capacitor that's recharged each time the switch node goes low. If the switch never goes low, the capacitor slowly discharges through the driver, so many controllers force a short off pulse periodically, or limit maximum duty to something like 95 to 99 percent. Parts that advertise true 100 percent duty use a charge pump or a PMOS.

At 0 percent duty, the high-side never turns on. The inductor now has minus $V_{out}$ across it, so its current ramps down at $V_{out}/L$, to zero in a few microseconds. If the low side is a diode, or a synchronous FET with diode emulation, it stops there, and the output simply discharges through the load. If it's forced PWM, the current keeps going negative and the output capacitor pushes energy back into the input. If the input can't absorb that, the input rail rises.

Both extremes are saturation of the control loop. And in practice the minimum on-time keeps the duty from getting really small. A controller asked for a lower duty cycle will skip pulses instead."
:::

::: terms
- **Dropout**: the minimum $V_{in}-V_{out}$ at which a converter can still regulate; for a buck at 100% duty it's $I(R_{hs}+R_{DCR})$.
- **Maximum duty ($D_{max}$)**: the highest duty cycle the controller allows, typically 90% to 100%.
- **Minimum on-time ($t_{on,min}$)**: the shortest pulse the controller can generate.
- **Minimum off-time ($t_{off,min}$)**: the shortest off interval, needed for current sensing and bootstrap refresh.
- **Bootstrap capacitor**: a flying capacitor that supplies the high-side gate driver, recharged when SW is low.
- **Forced PWM (FPWM)**: synchronous operation where the low-side stays on even if the current reverses.
- **Diode emulation**: turning off the low-side FET when current reaches zero, preventing reverse current.
- **Pulse skipping**: omitting switching cycles when the required duty falls below the minimum.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "100% means output equals input; 0% means output is zero."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the dropout equation $V_{out}=V_{in}-I(R_{hs}+R_{DCR})$ with a number and says regulation is lost.
- Explains *why* real controllers limit maximum duty: bootstrap refresh of the high-side NFET drive, plus minimum off-time for current sensing.
- Describes the inductor's behaviour at 0%: $-V_{out}/L$ ramp, then DCM (diode) or reverse current (forced PWM) and says the reverse current can push the input voltage up.
- Mentions minimum on-time, $D_{min}=t_{on,min}f_{sw}$, and pulse skipping as the realistic 0% case.
- Knows how to fix each: lower frequency, a PMOS or charge-pump high-side, a part with a lower minimum on-time, or reverse-current protection.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"At 100% duty the output equals the input exactly."** There are always drops: $I\,R_{DS(on)}$ and the inductor DCR.
- **"At 0% duty the output is zero instantly."** The output capacitor holds charge; it decays at about $I_{load}/C$ unless the converter actively sinks current.
- **Assuming a buck with a bootstrapped NFET can hold the high-side on indefinitely.** The bootstrap capacitor discharges; real parts limit duty or refresh it.
- **Thinking a synchronous buck is harmless at 0% duty.** In forced-PWM mode it can pump energy from the output back into the input, raising $V_{in}$ and potentially stressing the input capacitor or source.
- **Ignoring minimum on-time.** At high $V_{in}$ and high frequency the required duty may be below $D_{min}$, so the output ripple jumps due to pulse skipping.
- **Treating dropout as "efficient so who cares."** Efficiency is high, but the output now follows the input, with all its noise and sag.
:::

::: deep
## The ideal picture

If $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$, then $D=1$ gives $V_{out}=V_{in}$ and $D=0$ gives $V_{out}=0$. Those are the endpoints of the straight-line transfer function in [[q0179]]. What does the hardware actually do at each end?

## 100% duty: dropout

With $Q_1$ permanently on, the switch node is a DC voltage:

$$V_{SW}=V_{in}-I\,R_{hs}$$

and the inductor sees $V_{SW}-V_{out}$ across its inductance plus winding resistance. There is no switching, so no ripple. In steady state the inductor behaves as a resistor $R_{DCR}$ carrying a DC current, and

$$\boxed{V_{out}=V_{in}-I\,(R_{hs}+R_{DCR})}$$

Figure: Above $V_{in}=3.52$ V the loop holds 3.3 V by choosing $D<1$. Below that, $D$ is stuck at its maximum and $V_{out}$ follows $V_{in}$ minus 0.22 V. Regulation is lost, so any ripple or sag on $V_{in}$ goes straight to the load.

The numbers: $3.3+2\times0.11=3.52$ V is the minimum input for regulation. A lithium cell approaching 3.5 V feeding a 3.3 V rail is a typical case where this matters.

**What the loop does.** The feedback voltage sits below the reference, so the error amplifier output rails at its maximum. Controllers with an integrator can wind up, so when $V_{in}$ recovers there may be an overshoot unless anti-windup is built in.

**Efficiency.** In dropout, loss is $I^2(R_{hs}+R_{DCR})$ and efficiency is $V_{out}/V_{in}$, e.g. $3.28/3.50=93.7\%$ at the edge. Switching loss disappears. In that sense dropout mode behaves like an LDO with a tiny dropout, with $P_{loss}=0.44$ W at 2 A.

**Fault version.** If the loop is broken so that the controller always commands maximum duty (open top feedback resistor, floating FB pin), the output rises to $V_{in}$ regardless of the target. The load sees $V_{in}$ and may be destroyed unless there's overvoltage protection ([[q0178]]).

## Why true 100% is hard with an NFET

Figure: When $Q_2$ pulls SW to ground, $C_{boot}$ charges to about $V_{CC}$ through $D_b$. When $Q_1$ turns on, SW rises to $V_{in}$ and the BST pin floats up to $V_{in}+V_{CC}$, powering the gate driver. If SW stays high, nothing recharges $C_{boot}$.

How fast does it droop? Each high-side turn-on moves the gate charge $Q_g$: with $Q_g=10$ nC and $C_{boot}=100$ nF, that's 0.1 V per cycle (recharged next cycle). But if the driver holds the gate on, its own quiescent draw (say $100$ µA, an illustrative value) removes $I\,t/C=1$ V per millisecond, so after about 0.5 ms the gate drive has fallen by 0.5 V and $R_{DS(on)}$ starts to rise. Controllers therefore either:

- limit $D_{max}$ (typically 90 to 99%) by enforcing a minimum off-time so SW goes low every cycle,
- fire a periodic refresh pulse (briefly turn on the low-side) when BST has drooped, or
- use a charge pump or a PMOS high-side, which allow true 100% duty at the cost of extra circuitry or higher $R_{DS(on)}$ ([[q0204]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Maximum duty cycle, refresh behaviour and "100% mode" support are controller-specific. Some datasheets quote 100% explicitly; many quote 90% to 98%. Check the part's electrical characteristics table.

## 0% duty

With $Q_1$ off, $Q_2$ (or the diode) provides the only path. The inductor sees $-V_{out}$:

$$\frac{di_L}{dt}=-\frac{V_{out}}{L}=-\frac{3.3}{4.7\text{ µH}}=-0.70\text{ A/µs}$$

Starting at 2 A, $i_L$ reaches zero after $\dfrac{L\,I}{V_{out}}=\dfrac{4.7\text{ µ}\times2}{3.3}=2.85$ µs.

Figure: Both versions ramp down at the same rate until zero. A diode (or an emulated diode) then blocks reverse current. A forced-PWM synchronous stage keeps going, pulling current *out of the output* and sending it toward the input. The plot holds $V_{out}$ constant, which is only valid for a few microseconds.

**With a diode / diode emulation.** At zero current SW floats; the inductor and SW-node capacitance ring briefly, and the output simply discharges through the load. At 2 A and 22 µF, $dV/dt=-I/C=-0.09$ V/µs, so the output would fall from 3.3 V to zero in about 36 µs (if nothing else supplies it).

**With forced PWM.** The low-side stays on, so the current keeps ramping negative: it flows from $C_{out}$ through $L$ into SW and out through $Q_2$, discharging the output (actively pulling it down). The energy goes into the inductor. When the controller next turns $Q_2$ off, the inductor forces SW upward until $Q_1$'s body diode (or channel) conducts, dumping that energy into $C_{in}$. As an upper bound, if $C_{out}$ gave up all of its energy ($\tfrac12 C_{out}V_{out}^2=120$ µJ) into a 10 µF input capacitor with no source able to sink current, the input rail would rise from 12 V to about 12.96 V. This is "boosting the input", a risk in forced-PWM converters when the output is overdriven or the load is removed with a stiff output capacitance. Controllers add a reverse (negative) current limit for this reason ([[q0229]]).

**When does the loop command 0%?** At startup before the ramp begins, after a load release when $V_{out}$ overshoots above the reference, during an overvoltage event, or in light-load pulse skipping. Using forced PWM to deliberately sink current is a feature when you want a fast recovery from overshoot, for instance in processor rails with dynamic voltage scaling.

## The real limits: minimum on-time and off-time

The ideal extremes are never reached. Practical controllers limit:

$$D_{min}=t_{on,min}\,f_{sw},\qquad D_{max}=1-t_{off,min}\,f_{sw}$$

Figure: The window between the two lines is the duty range the converter can actually produce. It closes as the switching frequency rises. At 12 V in and 500 kHz, a 5% minimum duty means the lowest regulated output without pulse skipping is 0.6 V; at 2 MHz, $D_{min}=20\%$ and the lowest output is 2.4 V.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 100 ns values in the plot are illustrative only. Actual $t_{on,min}$ and $t_{off,min}$ are controller- and layout-dependent and vary with temperature and $V_{in}$; check the datasheet.

When a requested duty falls below $D_{min}$ the controller skips pulses: it alternates short bursts of minimum-width pulses with idle gaps. The average still tracks the demand, but the output ripple and the switching spectrum become irregular (sub-harmonic content), which can matter for noise-sensitive loads ([[q0231]]). When the required duty rises above $D_{max}$ the converter drops out earlier than the 100% equation predicts: the dropout becomes $V_{in}\approx V_{out}/D_{max}$ plus drops.

## Side-by-side summary

| | 100% duty | 0% duty |
|---|---|---|
| High-side switch | Always on | Always off |
| SW node | $V_{in}-IR_{hs}$ (DC) | about 0 or $-V_f$ (diode); floating after current hits zero |
| Inductor voltage | about 0 (DC) | $-V_{out}$ |
| Inductor current | constant, no ripple | ramps down at $V_{out}/L$; stops (diode) or reverses (FPWM) |
| $V_{out}$ | $V_{in}-I(R_{hs}+R_{DCR})$, follows $V_{in}$ | decays toward 0 via the load; or is actively pulled down |
| Regulation | Lost (dropout) | Lost, until duty returns |
| Main practical issue | Bootstrap refresh, max duty, $V_{in}$ noise passes through | Reverse current, input pump-up, minimum on-time and pulse skipping |

::: applications
- **Battery-powered products** whose Li-ion cell sags toward the regulated rail: designers pick a buck with a "100% duty" (low-dropout) mode or switch to a buck-boost when the cell goes below $V_{out}+$drops ([[q0161]]).
- **Datasheet reading**: maximum duty cycle, minimum on-time and minimum input voltage are limits set by exactly these edge behaviours.
- **High-ratio step-downs** (24 V to 1 V at 1 MHz) that run into the minimum on-time and need a lower frequency or a different architecture.
- **Overvoltage and fault handling**: forced-PWM parts sink current at 0% to pull down an overshooting rail, while diode-emulation parts avoid back-feeding the input.
- **Debugging**: seeing SW stuck high means the loop is saturated at maximum duty (input too low, an overload, or broken feedback).
- **Pulse-skipping and PFM modes** at light load, where requested duty falls below what the hardware can produce ([[q0231]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Synchronous or asynchronous**: both reach 100% the same way; they differ at 0% (diode blocks reverse current, forced PWM does not).
- **Bootstrapped NFET high-side** is assumed for the duty-limit discussion; a PMOS high-side or charge-pump parts can reach true 100%.
- **Ideal output capacitor holding $V_{out}$** during the inductor-ramp calculation; over tens of µs the output moves.
- **Resistive drops constant.** Real $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature, and DCR with current and temperature.
- **Illustrative numbers**: 100 ns minimum times, 100 µA driver current and 10 nC gate charge are examples, not specific part values.
:::

::: followups
### Why can't a buck with a bootstrapped NFET run at 100% duty forever?
The bootstrap capacitor supplies the gate drive and is only recharged when SW goes low. With SW held high, the driver's quiescent draw and gate leakage slowly discharge it, so the gate voltage sags and $R_{DS(on)}$ rises. Controllers enforce a minimum off-time or fire a periodic refresh pulse, limiting maximum duty to maybe 90 to 99%. A PMOS high-side or a charge pump can hold the gate without a refresh.

### What happens to the input voltage if a forced-PWM synchronous buck is commanded to 0% with a big output capacitor?
The inductor current goes negative and moves charge from $C_{out}$ back to $C_{in}$. With 22 µF at 3.3 V (about 120 µJ) dumped into a 10 µF input capacitor with no source that can sink it, $V_{in}$ rises from 12 V to about 13 V. With a smaller input capacitor or a stiffer output, it could rise further. This is why FPWM controllers have a reverse-current limit, and why input sources like battery chargers may need a way to absorb energy.

### How do you extend a buck's usable input range at the low end?
Use a part with 100% duty (dropout) operation so the output follows the input smoothly, minimise $R_{hs}$ and DCR (they set the dropout), or use a buck-boost if you must hold the output when $V_{in}$ falls below it. For a 3.3 V rail from a Li-ion cell, a plain buck stops regulating around 3.5 V ([[q0161]]).

### At 24 V in and 1 V out, 1 MHz, the output ripple gets jittery. Why?
$D=1/24=4.2\%$, so the on-time is 42 ns, probably below the controller's minimum on-time. The controller then skips pulses: it emits minimum-width pulses (which deliver more than the required energy) and waits. Fixes: lower $f_{sw}$ (e.g. 300 kHz gives 139 ns), use a part with a shorter minimum on-time, or do the step-down in two stages ([[q0166]]).

### Scenario: a 5 V buck from a 5.3 V input reads 4.88 V at 3 A. Is it broken?
Probably not: it's in dropout. With $R_{hs}+R_{DCR}\approx0.14$ Ω, $3\times0.14=0.42$ V is lost, so $V_{out}\approx5.3-0.42=4.88$ V. Confirm that SW is stuck high (or has only brief refresh pulses) and that $V_{out}$ tracks $V_{in}$ when you vary it. The fix is a higher input voltage, a lower-resistance FET and inductor, or a lower load current.
:::

::: interpretations
"What happens at 100% and 0% duty cycle?" can be read as the ideal-equation answer ($V_{out}=V_{in}$ and $V_{out}=0$) or as the practical behaviour. I've given both: the ideal result first, then what the circuit really does and what controllers do to avoid these extremes. For the buck, $D$ refers to the high-side switch; for the 0% case, I treated both diode and synchronous freewheel elements because their behaviour differs.
