::: basics
### In plain words
Phone buzzers come in two kinds. An ERM is a tiny motor with a lopsided weight, so it shakes as it spins. An LRA is a coil that pushes a small weight back and forth on a spring, like a tiny speaker. LRAs give sharper, more controllable buzzes and are common in modern phones.

### Picture it
An ERM is like a washing machine with all the laundry bunched on one side: the unbalanced spin shakes everything. An LRA is like pushing a child on a swing at just the right rhythm: small pushes at the right pace make a big swing. The swing example also shows the catch. Push at the wrong rhythm and little happens, so the LRA must be driven at its resonant frequency.

### Words you need
- **ERM**: eccentric rotating mass, a small motor with an off-center weight.
- **LRA**: linear resonant actuator, a coil driving a mass on a spring.
- **Resonance**: the natural rhythm at which a mass on a spring moves most easily.
- **Frequency (Hz)**: how many shakes happen per second. 100 Hz means 100 per second.
- **Haptics**: using vibration to give touch feedback.

### The simplest example
An ERM spinning at 6,000 rpm (revolutions per minute) turns 6,000 / 60 = 100 times a second, so it shakes at 100 Hz. Spin it faster and it buzzes harder and higher, since strength and frequency are tied together. An LRA driven at its resonance of, say, 200 Hz moves back and forth every 1/200 = 5 ms. It starts and stops in a few milliseconds, so you get a crisp tap.

That is the whole idea: ERM is simple and a bit mushy, LRA is sharp and programmable. The rest of this page covers the driver requirements, spin-up time, and why you cannot just apply a steady DC voltage to an LRA.
:::

::: why
Haptic feedback is a product feature that depends on a very specific piece of electromechanical design: a small actuator, a driver IC and firmware that turns events into waveforms. It touches battery current budgets, EMI from brushes, acoustic noise, mechanical mounting and user perception. It is also a neat example of choosing an actuator from requirements (size, latency, power, cost).

::: concepts
- **Brushed DC motor**: voltage sets speed, current sets torque. See [[q0348]].
- **Unbalanced mass**: a rotating off-centre mass produces a rotating force $F = m\,e\,\omega^2$.
- **Voice coil / linear actuator**: a coil in a magnetic field produces force $F = BlI$. See [[q0350]].
- **Second-order resonance**: a mass-spring system has $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{k/m}$ and a quality factor $Q$.
- **H-bridge and PWM drive**: how a haptic driver delivers voltage and reverse braking. See [[q0349]].
- **Inrush and back-EMF** limit the response time of a small motor ([[q0345]]).

"Phones use two kinds. Older and cheaper phones use an ERM, an eccentric rotating mass motor, which is a tiny brushed DC motor, either a coin or a cylinder, with an off-centre weight on the shaft. When it spins the unbalanced mass shakes the whole phone. You control it with a DC voltage or PWM, and the stronger vibration comes with higher speed because the force is $m e \omega^2$, so intensity and frequency are tied together. It's cheap, but it takes tens of milliseconds to spin up and wind down, so the buzz is a bit mushy.

Modern phones mostly use LRAs, linear resonant actuators. That's a voice coil pushing a small mass on a spring along one axis. You drive it with an AC signal at its resonant frequency, usually around 150 to 250 hertz, so a small drive makes a large motion. It starts and stops in a few milliseconds, so you get crisp taps and can program waveforms, and amplitude is independent of frequency. You also can't just apply DC to it. It needs a driver IC that generates the waveform, often with closed loop resonance tracking and active braking.

Piezo actuators are a third option on some devices with very fast response. In short: ERM is simple, LRA is the tight, programmable one."
:::

::: technical
**ERM (eccentric rotating mass).** A brushed PM DC motor, coin (pancake) or cylindrical, with an off-axis mass $m$ at eccentricity $e$. Centripetal force on the mass, transferred to the housing, is
$$F = m\,e\,\omega^2,\qquad f = \omega/2\pi.$$
Example: $m = 0.5$ g, $e = 1.5$ mm. At 6000 rpm (100 Hz) $F = 0.30$ N; at 12,000 rpm (200 Hz) $F = 1.18$ N. Since speed is roughly proportional to voltage, the force goes about as $V^2$ and frequency and amplitude are coupled. Typical drive: 2–3.6 V, tens to ~100 mA (varies by part; see the vendor datasheet). Startup takes tens of ms because of $\tau_m$; braking is done by short or reverse drive.

**LRA (linear resonant actuator).** A voice coil (force $F = BlI$) moves a magnet/mass $m$ against a spring $k$ with damping. Transfer function from force to displacement is second order:
$$f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},\qquad |H| \propto \frac{1}{\sqrt{(1 - r^2)^2 + (r/Q)^2}},\ r = f/f_0.$$
Example: $m = 0.5$ g, $f_0 = 170$ Hz needs $k = (2\pi f_0)^2m = 570$ N/m. With $Q = 10$, the half-power bandwidth is $f_0/Q = 17$ Hz, so the drive must track $f_0$ to within a few Hz; $f_0$ shifts with temperature, mounting and mass. Drive is an AC waveform (sine or square) at $f_0$; amplitude, not frequency, sets intensity.

**Driver ICs** (e.g. the TI DRV2605 family exists for both ERM and LRA) provide H-bridge outputs, waveform libraries, auto-calibration of resonance, overdrive and active braking.

**Which one?** ERM: lowest cost, simple DC drive, but slow, coupled amplitude/frequency, brush wear and EMI. LRA: faster, crisp, programmable, longer life (no brushes), but narrow-band and needs a dedicated AC drive. Larger phones use LRAs of a few hundred mm³.
:::

::: long
"There are two main kinds in phones.

The older, simpler one is the ERM, the eccentric rotating mass motor. It's literally a tiny brushed DC motor, either a flat coin shape or a small cylinder, with an off-centre weight on the shaft. When it spins the unbalanced mass produces a rotating force $F = m e \omega^2$, which shakes the phone. You drive it with DC or PWM, and because speed is roughly proportional to voltage, force goes up with the square of speed. With a half-gram mass at 1.5 millimetres offset, 12,000 rpm is about 1.2 newtons. The drawbacks are that amplitude and frequency are locked together, it takes tens of milliseconds to spin up and coast down, and it has brushes so there's wear and electrical noise.

Modern phones mostly use LRAs, linear resonant actuators. It's a voice coil: a coil in a magnetic field pushing a small magnet mass that's hung on a spring, moving along one axis. You drive it with an AC waveform at the resonant frequency, $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{k/m}$, typically 150 to 250 hertz. Operating at resonance means a small drive current gives a large displacement. The Q is high, so you need to track $f_0$ accurately, which varies with temperature and how it's mounted. LRAs are fast, a few milliseconds to start and brake, they allow crisp taps and programmable waveforms, amplitude is independent of frequency, and no brushes. The catch is you can't drive them with DC, so you need a haptic driver IC that makes the waveform, tracks resonance and applies active braking.

So: ERM for cheap and simple, LRA for the quality of feel that phones now want. Piezo actuators are a third option for very fast response."
:::

::: terms
- **ERM**: eccentric rotating mass motor, a brushed DC motor with an off-centre weight.
- **LRA**: linear resonant actuator, a spring-mass voice-coil actuator driven at resonance.
- **Resonant frequency $f_0$**: $\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{k/m}$ where the actuator responds most strongly.
- **Quality factor $Q$**: $f_0$ divided by the half-power bandwidth; high $Q$ means narrow band and slow ring-down.
- **Overdrive / active braking**: briefly driving harder to start faster, and driving in reverse to stop faster.
- **Haptic driver IC**: chip with an H-bridge and waveform engine for ERM/LRA.
- **Eccentricity $e$**: offset of the mass centre from the rotation axis.
- **Haptic waveform / effect library**: pre-defined drive patterns (click, buzz, ramp).
- **Coin (pancake) motor**: flat ERM with no shaft protruding.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "a stepper" or "BLDC" for the vibration motor.** The common ones are brushed ERM and the LRA, a linear voice-coil actuator.
- **Driving an LRA with DC.** It will just move to one end and sit there (and burn current); it needs AC at resonance.
- **Driving an LRA far from resonance.** With $Q = 10$ a 10% frequency error cuts the amplitude drastically.
- **Assuming amplitude and frequency are independent on an ERM.** The unbalanced force goes as $\omega^2$, so changing the intensity changes the frequency.
- **Ignoring the supply impact.** An ERM start draws inrush well above its running current ([[q0345]]) and its brushes make noise ([[q0355]]), which can reset a weak rail.
- **Forgetting mounting.** The phone's mass and the mounting adhesive change the effective resonance and the feel.
:::

::: deep
## Why vibration needs an unbalanced force

To make a phone buzz you need a time-varying force on its body. A rotating mass spinning about an axis that does *not* pass through its centre produces a force that rotates with it. The housing sees a sinusoidal force at the rotation frequency. An LRA does it differently: it oscillates a mass back and forth in a line, so the reaction force on the housing is directly sinusoidal. Both exploit conservation of momentum: moving a small mass fast moves a heavy phone a little.

## ERM: a motor with a deliberate imbalance

A coin ERM contains a flat brushed motor with a few commutator pads and an eccentric weight (a half-moon of tungsten alloy). Electrically it is the usual model $V = IR + K_e\omega + L\,di/dt$ with a few ohms of resistance, small $K_e$ and small inertia.

Centripetal force on the eccentric mass $m$ at radius $e$ from the axis: $F = me\omega^2$. For $m = 0.5$ g and $e = 1.5$ mm:

Figure: Force grows with the square of speed, so doubling voltage (and so speed) gives about four times the force *and* doubles the vibration frequency. You cannot have "strong but low-frequency" buzz from an ERM.

Because torque for the motor is $K_tI$, the start-up involves a current inrush set by $V/R$ and a mechanical time constant of a few ms to tens of ms ([[q0345]]); coasting down takes just as long unless you brake by shorting or reversing the drive ([[q0346]]). So an ERM "tick" is a smeared burst of vibration, not a crisp pulse. Drivers use **overdrive** (a short burst at higher than rated voltage to spin up faster) and **active braking** (a reverse-polarity pulse) to sharpen it.

## LRA: a resonant voice coil

An LRA contains a magnet/mass suspended by springs inside a housing with a coil fixed to the housing (or the reverse). Current through the coil produces a force $F = BlI$ on the mass, moving it back and forth along one axis. Dynamics are those of a driven damped mass-spring:
$$m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = BlI,\qquad f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}},\qquad Q = \frac{\sqrt{km}}{c}.$$

Figure: At resonance the amplitude is Q times the static displacement. Drive 10% off resonance (187 Hz) and the Q = 10 actuator falls from 10 to about 4. A lower Q is more forgiving but needs more current for the same motion and has less ring. Drag Q down to 4 and the solid curve lands on the dashed one; drag f₀ down to 150 Hz and the fixed 187 Hz drive (orange line) sits 25% above resonance, where even Q = 10 gives less than twice the static displacement.

For $m = 0.5$ g and $f_0 = 170$ Hz, $k = 570$ N/m. Half-power bandwidth is $f_0/Q = 17$ Hz at $Q = 10$, so the drive must track $f_0$ within several Hz. Temperature, aging and how the phone mounts the actuator move $f_0$, so haptic drivers include **auto-resonance tracking**: they measure the back-EMF (zero-crossings of coil voltage when the drive is briefly paused) to find $f_0$ and update the drive frequency.

**Why drive an LRA at resonance?** A drive of fixed current produces *Q times* more displacement at $f_0$ than far from it, so the coil current (and battery drain) for a given feel is a fraction. It also means that to change the feel you change the drive amplitude and envelope (a 30 ms burst with a ramp, a click, a double click), not the frequency.

**Response time.** The ring-up time constant of a resonator is $\tau = \frac{2Q}{\omega_0} = \frac{Q}{\pi f_0}$. For $Q = 10$, $f_0 = 170$ Hz: $\tau = 18.7$ ms. So the envelope builds in tens of ms with a plain sine. Haptic drivers speed this up with overdrive and stop the vibration with a phase-inverted braking burst, giving effective ~5–10 ms clicks. (An ERM still needs tens of ms or more.)

## Driving them

| | ERM | LRA |
|---|---|---|
| Drive signal | DC or PWM, one polarity (H-bridge to reverse for braking) | AC at $f_0$ (sine or square), H-bridge |
| Intensity control | Voltage / PWM duty (also changes frequency) | Drive amplitude (frequency fixed at $f_0$) |
| Typical response | Tens of ms up, coasts down | Few ms with overdrive and braking |
| Wear | Brushes | None in the drive; mechanical fatigue of the spring |
| EMI | Brush arcing noise ([[q0355]]) | Clean, but driven by switching edges |
| Haptic quality | Buzzy, one effect | Crisp clicks, rich effect library |

A typical haptic driver IC contains an H-bridge, a waveform memory (the TI DRV2605 family offers a built-in library of effects) and an I²C interface; some parts also integrate a boost converter to overdrive above the battery voltage. The MCU sends a command like "play effect 1" and the driver does the rest.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact currents, voltages and frequencies of phone actuators vary by vendor and model; typical coin ERMs are around 3 V and tens to ~100 mA, LRAs around 1–2 V RMS and 150–250 Hz. Always take values from the datasheet of the actual part.

## Other options

- **Piezo haptic actuators** bend a ceramic strip with a high-voltage drive (tens of volts, boosted from the battery); extremely fast and thin, but need a high-voltage driver.
- **Solenoid-style linear actuators**: larger, used in game controllers and gaming touchpads.
- **Bigger ERMs** exist in controllers (rumble motors with two different masses for "large" and "small" effects).

## Design considerations

1. **Current budget**: peak start current (inrush) of an ERM is $V/R$; with 3 V and 25 Ω, 120 mA, easy for a phone but enough to dip a weak rail, so decouple near the driver.
2. **Mounting**: a rigid, well-bonded actuator transfers force; a compliant mount shifts the resonance and damps the feel. The phone's mass sets how big a displacement you feel: acceleration $a \approx F/M$, e.g. 1 N on a 200 g phone is 5 m/s².
3. **Acoustic noise**: any vibration at 150–250 Hz is audible. Use burst envelopes, not continuous buzz, and tune against the phone chassis' resonances.
4. **Safety and EMC**: ERM brushes spark; a small capacitor across the motor and a TVS/diode on the drive help ([[q0355]]).
5. **Test**: measure acceleration with a small accelerometer on a fixture, sweep drive frequency to see $f_0$ and $Q$, and calibrate per unit.

::: applications
- **Smartphones**: haptic taps, keyboard feedback, notifications, ring buzz (LRA in most current phones; ERM in older and low-cost models).
- **Game controllers**: rumble from twin ERMs of different sizes, and richer effects from LRAs or voice-coil actuators in newer controllers.
- **Smartwatches and fitness bands**: tiny LRAs for silent alerts.
- **Pagers and hearing devices**: ERM alerts.
- **Touchscreens in cars and appliances**: click feel on a touch panel using piezo or LRA actuators.
- **Wearables for accessibility**: vibration cues for navigation and alerts.

- **Rigid, massive phone** compared with the actuator: transferred force equals actuator force; a flexible mount changes the transfer.

::: followups
### Why do phones use LRAs instead of ERMs now?
Faster start/stop for crisp taps and programmable patterns, independent amplitude and frequency, no brushes (longer life, less EMI), and a flatter form factor. The cost is a dedicated AC driver and a narrower tuning band.

### How do you drive an LRA from a microcontroller?
Not directly from a GPIO: use a haptic driver IC (H-bridge with waveform generation and resonance tracking) or build an H-bridge with a PWM-generated sine/square at $f_0$. Include a boost or overdrive voltage if you want fast starts, an I²C interface for effects, and measure or auto-detect $f_0$.

### Your LRA feels weak on one unit but fine on another. Why?
Resonance shift from part tolerance, mounting differences (glue, case, battery position) or temperature. A fixed-frequency drive that matches $f_0$ on one unit is off-resonance on the other. With $Q = 10$ a 10% error cuts the amplitude to roughly 40% ($1/\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2 + (r/Q)^2}$ at $r = 1.1$ gives about 4.2 versus 10 at resonance). Fix: auto-resonance tracking or per-unit calibration.

### How would you get a sharper "click" from an ERM?
Overdrive: a brief higher-voltage pulse (above rated, short enough to avoid overheating) to spin up quickly, followed by a reverse-polarity brake pulse to stop it, which needs a full H-bridge rather than a single low-side switch ([[q0346]]). It still won't match an LRA's crispness, because of the rotor inertia and the commutator.

### What if the battery is at 3.0 V and the driver needs to overdrive?
A boost converter (or charge pump) in the driver raises the drive voltage for the LRA/ERM at low battery. Otherwise overdrive is limited and the effects feel weaker as the battery drains. This is why some haptic ICs integrate a boost (others, such as the DRV2605, run from the battery and rely on overdrive within that limit).

### Can you drive an ERM with PWM, and is there a downside?
Yes: PWM duty sets average voltage. Choose a PWM frequency above the mechanical bandwidth and above audio (> 20 kHz) so it isn't heard. The motor inductance smooths current. The downside is switching noise coupled to the rest of the system; keep traces short and add decoupling.
:::
