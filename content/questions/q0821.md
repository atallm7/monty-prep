::: basics
### In plain words
Designing a DAC starts with the requirements: how many bits, what output range, how fast it must update, and how accurate it must be. Then you choose a way to build it that fits. The common cheap choices are a resistor ladder driven from microcontroller pins, a PWM signal with a filter, or a ready-made DAC chip.

### Picture it
Think of a set of measuring cups for building a recipe. The biggest cup has to be accurate to within the smallest cup's size, or the recipe goes wrong. In a DAC, the biggest bit has the same problem: its resistors must be matched very closely, or one step comes out too large or too small. More bits means a harder matching job.

### Words you need
- **LSB** ([[q0820]]): the smallest output step, the range divided by $2^N$.
- **R-2R ladder** ([[q0820]]): a resistor network that turns a binary code into a voltage.
- **Tolerance**: how far a resistor's real value can be from its label, such as 1% or 0.1%.
- **Monotonic**: the output never goes down when the code goes up.
- **PWM** ([[q0790]]): switching on and off quickly. A filter made of a resistor and capacitor averages it into a steady voltage.
- **Buffer**: an amplifier that lets the output drive a load without its voltage sagging.

### The simplest example
You need an 8-bit output from 0 to 3.3 V. That is 256 steps, so each step is 3.3 / 256, about 12.9 mV. You build an R-2R ladder from eight pins with a buffer. With 1% resistors, the ladder can be non-monotonic and be off by up to about 2 steps. With 0.1% resistors the error drops to about 0.2 of a step, so you choose those.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows how matching limits the bits, the buffer design, the PWM alternative, and how to verify by sweeping every code.
:::

::: testing
Whether you run a **design process**: clarify requirements (resolution, range, update rate, accuracy, load, supply, cost), select an architecture with reasons, size the key parts with numbers (resistor tolerance for the linearity you need, settling time, buffer specs, filter corner), and plan verification. The interviewer is also checking that you can build something real from an embedded toolbox: an R-2R network on GPIOs with a buffer, a PWM output with a reconstruction filter, or an external DAC chip, and that you know each option's limits (matching, GPIO output resistance, ripple versus settling).
:::

::: why
Embedded engineers add DACs constantly, for bias voltages, set-points, programmable current limits, waveform generation or audio, and the choice between "use the MCU's DAC", "PWM and a filter" and "add a DAC chip" is a real design decision. The same reasoning appears in its mirror question, [[q0811]] (design an ADC), and the accuracy numbers lead to [[q0822]]. A good answer shows judgement: you do not need 12 bits for an LED brightness, and you cannot get 12 bits from 1% resistors.
:::

::: answer
"I'd start with the requirements: how many bits, what output range, how fast it updates, how accurate it has to be, what it drives, and what supply I have. Say I need an 8-bit, 0 to 3.3 V output updating at 100 kS/s for a bias or control signal.

At 8 bits and 12.9 mV per step, the options are: an R-2R ladder from eight GPIO pins with an op-amp buffer, a PWM output with a filter, or a DAC chip. For this, I'd use the R-2R. Resistor tolerance sets the linearity: from a quick Monte Carlo, 8 bits with 1% resistors can be non-monotonic and have up to 2 LSB of DNL, while 0.1% resistors keep DNL under about 0.2 LSB, so I'd use a 0.1% resistor network. R is 10 kΩ, so the ladder settles in well under a microsecond. I'd add a rail-to-rail op-amp as a unity-gain buffer with offset under half an LSB, 6 mV, because the ladder has 10 kΩ output resistance. The GPIO high level is the reference, so I'd either accept ratiometric accuracy or drive the ladder from buffered logic on a clean reference.

If I had a spare timer and no pins, I'd go PWM: 8 bits at 16 MHz gives 62.5 kHz, and a two-stage RC at about 47 µs per stage keeps ripple to about half an LSB and settles in under a millisecond. For 12 bits I'd buy a DAC chip.

Then I'd verify by sweeping all codes, checking monotonicity and step size with a good meter, and measuring settling and glitch on a scope."
:::

::: technical
**Step 1: requirements (example).** 8-bit, 0 to 3.3 V, 100 kS/s update, load 10 kΩ or more, ±1 LSB accuracy after calibration, single 3.3 V supply, cost-sensitive.
- $V_{LSB}=3.3/256=12.9$ mV; full scale $=3.3\cdot255/256=3.287$ V.

**Step 2: architecture choice.**

| Option | Resolution reachable | Update rate | Parts | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MCU DAC peripheral | 8 to 12 bits | MS/s | none | Check output buffer drive, noise, accuracy |
| R-2R on GPIO + op-amp | 6 to 10 bits | 100s of kS/s | 16 resistors + op-amp | Matching limits bits; GPIO levels are the reference |
| PWM + RC filter | 8 to 12 bits | ~10 Hz to kHz | 2 R + 2 C (+ buffer) | Slow; resolution traded for speed |
| External DAC IC (SPI/I²C) | 10 to 16 bits | up to MS/s | 1 chip + bypass | Best accuracy, has reference and buffer, costs money and pins |

**Step 3: R-2R linearity versus resistor tolerance.** A Monte Carlo of the voltage-mode ladder (uniform tolerance, 200 trials per point, endpoint-fit) gave the worst DNL seen:

| Tolerance | 8-bit worst DNL (LSB) | 10-bit worst DNL (LSB) |
|---|---|---|
| 0.05% | 0.10 | 0.43 |
| 0.1% | 0.20 | 0.87 |
| 0.2% | 0.39 | 1.74 (non-monotonic in 9% of trials) |
| 0.5% | 0.98 | 4.34 |
| 1% | 1.97 (non-monotonic in 13% of trials) | 8.68 |

An empirical rule from this: worst-case DNL $\approx0.8\cdot\text{tol}\cdot2^N$ LSB. To keep DNL below 0.5 LSB (safely monotonic) you need tolerance $\lesssim0.6/2^N$: about 0.25% at 8 bits, 0.06% at 10 bits. This is a simulation of random errors, not a bound; the tightest errors appear at the major carry where the MSB leg switches.

**Step 4: switch and driver effects.** GPIO output resistance $R_{out}$ (typically tens of ohms; vendor- and drive-setting dependent) adds to the 2R legs. With $2R=20$ kΩ, a 30 Ω output resistance is a 0.15% error in each leg relative to the $2R$ terminator, and a 5 Ω difference between high-side and low-side drive adds a code-dependent 0.025%. That is comparable to the resistor budget, so use $R=10$ kΩ or more, or buffered switches (a logic buffer per bit from a clean reference).

**Step 5: settling.** Ladder output resistance $R=10$ kΩ with $C_{load}\approx10$ pF (op-amp input plus wiring): $\tau=100$ ns, and half-LSB settling is $\tau\ln2^{9}=0.62$ µs, so a 10 µs update period (100 kS/s) is plenty.

**Step 6: output buffer.** Unity-gain rail-to-rail op-amp. Specs: input offset $<\tfrac12$ LSB (6 mV) or trim it, output swing to within a few mV of both rails (otherwise codes near 0 and 255 clip), input bias current $\times$ 10 kΩ $\ll$ LSB, bandwidth much above the update rate. Alternatively gain it up to 5 V or 10 V.

**Step 7: PWM alternative (8-bit).** Timer at 16 MHz with 256 counts gives $f_{PWM}=62.5$ kHz. PWM fundamental amplitude at 50% duty is $(2/\pi)V_{DD}=2.1$ V. A passive two-stage RC with equal $R$ and $C$ attenuates the ripple by $1/|(1-x^2)+3jx|$ with $x=2\pi f\tau$. For ripple amplitude $\le\tfrac12$ LSB (6.4 mV): $x\approx18$, $\tau=46$ µs; take $R=4.7$ kΩ, $C=10$ nF ($\tau=47$ µs). Settling to half an LSB takes about $16.8\tau=0.79$ ms, so the output updates at roughly 1 kS/s. A single RC for the same ripple needs $RC\approx0.85$ ms and settles in about 5.3 ms. For 12 bits at 3.9 kHz PWM the same method gives $\tau\approx2.9$ ms and settling of tens of ms.

**Step 8: verification.** Sweep all codes with a 6.5-digit meter and compute DNL and INL; check monotonicity; step response and glitch at 0x7F to 0x80 on a scope; noise with the code fixed; temperature and supply sensitivity.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> GPIO output resistance, rail-to-rail swing and the Monte Carlo numbers are device- and run-dependent. The Monte Carlo is a simulation with uniformly distributed independent tolerances; real resistors in one array are correlated and usually match better than their absolute tolerance.
:::

::: long
"First I'd pin down the requirements, because they decide the whole design: how many bits, what output range, how fast it updates, what accuracy, what it drives, and what supply I have. Say I want 8 bits, 0 to 3.3 V, updating at 100 kilosamples per second, driving something a few kilohms or more.

At 8 bits the step is 12.9 millivolts. I have three realistic options: an R-2R ladder on GPIO pins, a PWM output with a filter, or an external DAC chip. If the MCU already has a DAC peripheral I'd check its resolution and drive first.

For the R-2R, the key design number is resistor tolerance. I ran a quick Monte Carlo on the ladder: with 1% resistors, 8-bit DNL gets to about 2 LSB and some units are non-monotonic; with 0.1% it's about 0.2 LSB. A rule of thumb is worst DNL is about 0.8 times the tolerance times two to the N. So I'd use a 0.1% resistor array, with R at 10 kΩ. That makes the output impedance 10 kΩ, so I'd add a rail-to-rail op-amp buffer with offset under about 6 millivolts, half an LSB. Settling is R times the load capacitance, 100 nanoseconds, so half-LSB settling is under a microsecond, which easily meets 100 kS/s. One caveat is that the GPIO pins have their own output resistance and their high level is the reference, so I'd consider a logic buffer on a clean reference rail.

If I'm out of pins, PWM plus a filter works. At 16 MHz and 8 bits the PWM runs at 62 kHz, and a two-stage RC with 47 µs per stage gives about half an LSB of ripple and settles in under a millisecond. For 12 bits that gets very slow, so I'd use a DAC chip instead.

Verification: sweep all the codes, measure each step with a meter, check monotonicity, then look at settling and glitch on a scope at the major carry."
:::

::: terms
- **Architecture selection**: choosing among R-2R, PWM, string, current-steering or a chip based on requirements.
- **Matching**: relative accuracy of resistors in a network, which sets DNL.
- **Monotonic**: output never falls when the code increases.
- **Worst-case DNL**: largest step error, at the MSB transition for R-2R.
- **Reconstruction filter**: low-pass that removes PWM or staircase images.
- **Ripple**: residual switching component on a filtered PWM output.
- **Rail-to-rail output**: op-amp output that swings near both supplies.
- **Ratiometric output**: output scales with the supply or reference, so absolute accuracy depends on it.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws an R-2R ladder.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts with **requirements and a spec table**, then chooses architecture **by elimination**.
- Quantifies **linearity versus tolerance** (for example 0.1% resistors for 8 bits, rule of thumb DNL $\approx0.8\,\text{tol}\,2^N$) rather than saying "use precision resistors".
- Includes **output buffering with real op-amp requirements** (offset, rail-to-rail, bias).
- Notes the **GPIO as a switch problem** (output resistance, level is the reference).
- Offers **PWM with numbers** (frequency, filter order, ripple, settling) and states when it fails (12 bits).
- Plans **verification** (code sweep, monotonicity, settling, glitch) and calibration.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using 1% resistors for an 8-bit ladder.** The MSB error alone can exceed an LSB, causing non-monotonic steps.
- **Forgetting the output buffer.** A 10 kΩ ladder loaded by 10 kΩ halves the output.
- **Trusting the GPIO level as an accurate reference.** It is the supply minus a drop that varies with load and temperature.
- **Using a single RC for a PWM DAC at high resolution.** Ripple below 1 LSB then needs RC of milliseconds and settling of tens of ms.
- **Using a non-rail-to-rail op-amp on a 3.3 V supply.** It clips codes near 0 and full scale.
- **Skipping verification.** Without sweeping codes you won't see a missing or non-monotonic step.
:::

::: deep
## A requirements-first design

Design always starts with a table, because every number below follows from it.

| Requirement | Example value | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Resolution | 8 bits | $V_{LSB}=12.9$ mV |
| Output range | 0 to 3.3 V | single supply; buffer must swing to the rails |
| Update rate | 100 kS/s | settling under ~1 µs |
| Load | 10 kΩ or higher | buffer needed; ladder can't drive it |
| Accuracy | ±1 LSB DNL, ±2 LSB INL | resistor matching, offset trim |
| Supply | 3.3 V ± 5% | ratiometric unless a reference is added |
| Cost/pins | 8 GPIOs available | R-2R on GPIO is feasible |

Different requirements, different choice. If it were a 12-bit audio-rate output, you would use a DAC chip. If it were a 0 to 3.3 V motor speed set-point updated at 10 Hz, PWM plus a filter would do.

## Matching sets the bits you can get from an R-2R

The ladder's MSB leg and the rest of the network must be in exact ratio. A relative error $\varepsilon$ in the MSB leg shifts its half-scale contribution by about $\varepsilon/2$ of full scale, which in LSB is $\varepsilon\cdot2^{N-1}$, so a 0.1% error at 8 bits is $0.001\cdot128=0.13$ LSB at the major carry; several resistors contribute and the worst-case sum is about twice that. A Monte Carlo of the voltage-mode ladder with uniform random resistor errors confirms the scaling:

Figure: DNL grows in proportion to tolerance and doubles with every extra bit (each point is the worst of 200 random builds). To stay under 0.5 LSB, 8 bits tolerates about 0.25% resistors, while 10 bits needs about 0.06%. Past 1 LSB the DAC can step backward, which is dangerous in a control loop.

So: **8-bit**, buy 0.1% resistors (or a matched network). **10-bit**, 0.05% matched arrays or trimming. **12-bit**: use a DAC chip, or calibrate.

**Sizing the buffer.** Offset: the op-amp's input offset adds directly to the output, so keep $V_{os}<\tfrac12$ LSB $=6.4$ mV, easily met by most CMOS rail-to-rail op-amps; for 12 bits the limit becomes 0.4 mV. Output swing: a part that swings within 10 mV of the rails will clip codes 0 and 255 slightly; accept it or run the buffer from a higher supply. Settling: output resistance $R=10$ kΩ into the op-amp input capacitance (about 5 pF plus 5 pF of wiring) gives $\tau=100$ ns and half-LSB settling in $0.62$ µs.

Figure: A two-stage passive RC (47 µs per stage) followed by a buffer. The PWM at 62.5 kHz is attenuated to about half an LSB amplitude while the output still settles within a millisecond. The buffer isolates the filter from the load, which would otherwise change the response.

**Why two stages?** For ripple below $\tfrac12$ LSB the fundamental (2.1 V at 50% duty) needs attenuation of $6.45\text{ mV}/2.1\text{ V}=3\times10^{-3}$. A single pole at 62.5 kHz attenuates by $1/(2\pi f\,RC)$, so $RC=1/(2\pi\cdot62.5\text{ kHz}\cdot3\times10^{-3})=0.85$ ms. But settling to half an LSB takes $\ln(512)=6.2$ time constants, 5.3 ms. Two stages attenuate as $1/x^2$, so $x=18$, $\tau=46$ µs each, with settling about $16.8\tau=0.79$ ms, 7 times faster than one pole.

## Using the MCU's own DAC or an external chip

Check the MCU DAC's datasheet for resolution, output impedance (often high unless the buffer is enabled), rail-to-rail behaviour, update rate, and its INL/DNL. For higher accuracy an external DAC (SPI or I²C, 10 to 16 bits) includes a reference option, a rail-to-rail buffer, power-on reset to a defined code and sometimes a settable gain. That is the right answer above about 10 bits. Add a 100 nF bypass at its supply and reference.

## Verification and calibration

1. **Code sweep**: step through all 256 codes and record $V_{out}$ with a 6.5-digit DMM. Compute step sizes (DNL) and deviation from the best-fit line (INL). Check monotonic.
2. **Major-carry check**: examine 0x7F to 0x80 on an oscilloscope for glitch height and width.
3. **Settling**: step 0x00 to 0xFF, measure time to half an LSB.
4. **Noise**: fix a mid-scale code, record rms noise with a scope and 20 MHz bandwidth limit.
5. **Drift**: repeat across temperature and supply.
6. **Calibration**: store a correction table (or just offset and gain) if the application needs better than the raw ladder.
:::

::: applications
- **Bias and set-point voltages** for amplifiers, laser or LED drivers, and programmable current limits.
- **Programmable power supplies** where a DAC sets the reference or feedback node of a regulator.
- **Waveform and tone generation** on a microcontroller, with a DAC fed by a timer-triggered DMA table.
- **Audio output** from a small MCU using PWM and a filter, or an I²S DAC chip for quality.
- **Motor speed commands** for analog motor drivers (0 to 10 V or 0 to 5 V inputs).
- **Test equipment**: arbitrary stimulus using a fast DAC feeding a reconstruction filter.
:::

::: assumptions
- The example spec (8-bit, 0 to 3.3 V, 100 kS/s) is illustrative; with other specs, redo the same steps.
- **Independent, uniformly distributed resistor tolerances** in the Monte Carlo; the empirical rule $\text{DNL}\approx0.8\,\text{tol}\,2^N$ is a fit to those results, not a theorem.
- **Ideal switches** in the ladder; GPIO output resistance and level variation are handled qualitatively.
- A **voltage-mode** ladder with a buffered output; a current-mode R-2R with a transimpedance amplifier avoids the switch-resistance problem and is common in IC DACs.
- PWM analysis uses the fundamental ripple only; the exact ripple depends on the duty cycle and higher harmonics, which are smaller.
- The question might mean a *chip-level* DAC design (current steering, segmented). The embedded context stated by the author makes the board-level answer the main one.
:::

::: followups
### Why does an R-2R DAC's worst error show up at mid-scale?
At 0x7F to 0x80 the MSB switches on while all other bits switch off, so the whole error of the MSB leg relative to the sum of the others appears in a single step. Any mismatch between the MSB's weight and the sum of the remaining weights plus one LSB shows up as DNL there. That's also where glitches are worst.

### How do you pick the PWM filter for a 10-bit PWM DAC at 16 MHz?
$f_{PWM}=16\text{ MHz}/1024=15.6$ kHz; ripple target $\tfrac12$ LSB $=1.6$ mV on 3.3 V. With two equal passive RC stages, $x=\sqrt{2.1/0.0016}\approx36$, $\tau=x/(2\pi f)=0.37$ ms, and half-LSB settling takes about $20\tau=7$ ms. So you update at under 150 Hz. If you need faster, use a higher PWM clock (fewer bits), more filter stages, or a DAC chip.

### What happens at the output if the op-amp buffer isn't rail-to-rail?
Near 0 V and 3.3 V the op-amp can't reach the rails (for example, 0.1 V from each), so the lowest and highest codes clip: several codes at the bottom all give the same output and the step at the top flattens, creating a dead-zone. Use a rail-to-rail output part, or run the amplifier from a higher supply and scale.

### How would you make the DAC's accuracy independent of the MCU supply?
Don't use GPIO levels as the reference. Drive the ladder through analog switches or CMOS buffers powered by a precision reference, or use an external DAC with its own reference. Then the output is $V_{REF}\cdot D/2^N$, accurate to the reference's tolerance and drift.

### Harder: your 10-bit R-2R DAC is monotonic on the bench at 25 °C but shows a negative step at 85 °C. Why?
Resistor temperature coefficients and the GPIO's output resistance (which rises with temperature) change the MSB leg ratio. If the MSB step's DNL was −0.8 LSB at 25 °C (close to the limit), a small drift can push it below −1 LSB, and the step goes negative. Fix by using resistors with matched TCs (same-package array), lowering GPIO resistance effects with buffers, or reducing the bit count.
:::
