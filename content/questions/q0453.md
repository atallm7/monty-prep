::: basics
### In plain words
Choosing the gain means making the biggest current you expect land just below the top of the amplifier's usable output range. You compute the shunt voltage at full current, divide the usable output limit by it, and round down to a gain the part actually offers. Then you check what that gives you in resolution at the low end. If the current can reverse, you measure the room from the reference voltage to the nearer limit instead.

### Picture it
Think of zooming a camera so the subject fills the frame. If you zoom too little, the subject is a small speck and the picture wastes detail. If you zoom too far, the edges of the subject are cut off. The right zoom lets the biggest subject just fit. The gain is the zoom, and clipping at the top of the output is the cut-off edge. The analogy leaves out one wrinkle: the zoom also magnifies the camera's own dust, which is the amplifier's offset and noise.

### Words you need
- **Gain ($G$)**: how many times the amplifier multiplies the shunt voltage. See [[q0449]].
- **Usable output range**: the smaller of the ADC range and the amplifier's output swing, minus headroom.
- **Standard gain steps**: gains a part family offers, such as 20, 50, 100 and 200.
- **LSB**: one ADC step, giving $\Delta I = V_{LSB} / (G R_s)$ per count. See [[q0819]].
- **Clipping**: the output sticking at its limit when the signal is too large.

### The simplest example
Take a 10 mΩ shunt and a 5 A maximum, so the shunt voltage is $5 \times 0.01 = 50$ mV. The amplifier can swing to 3.2 V, so $G_{max} = 3.2 / 0.05 = 64$. Round down to a standard gain of 50. Then 5 A gives $50 \times 0.05 = 2.5$ V, and one 12-bit ADC step is $0.806\text{ mV} / (50 \times 0.01) = 1.6$ mA. A gain of 100 would give 5 V and clip near 3.2 A. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the bidirectional case with a reference voltage, what to do when no standard gain fits, and the low-end and bandwidth checks.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can run the **gain calculation** cleanly: $G_{max}=\dfrac{V_{out,max}}{I_{max}R_s}$, using the *usable* output range (not the nominal ADC range), then pick a standard gain at or below it, verify the result, and handle the bidirectional case and the low-current end.

The checks the interviewer wants to see: headroom, output-swing limits near the rails, standard gain steps, the effect on resolution (mA per LSB) and on the low end (offset, floor), and the bandwidth trade-off with higher gain.
:::

::: why
This is the last step in sizing a shunt-CSA-ADC chain: with the shunt fixed by loss and drop ([[q0445]]), the gain is the knob that maps the current range onto the ADC. Too little gain wastes ADC bits; too much clips at the top. It is a five-minute calculation on every current-monitor design and a favourite whiteboard question because it exercises units, margin and judgment together. It follows [[q0446]] (where the shunt came from the ADC range directly) and is the counterpart of [[q0452]] (overall spec).
:::

::: concepts
- **CSA transfer function**: $V_{out}=G\,I\,R_s+V_{REF}$. See [[q0449]].
- **Usable output range**: the ADC's range intersected with the CSA's output swing, minus headroom.
- **Standard gains**: fixed steps (commonly 20, 50, 100, 200 V/V in popular families), so the computed value is rounded down.
- **ADC LSB and resolution**: $\Delta I=V_{LSB}/(G R_s)$. See [[q0819]].
- **Bidirectional range**: the REF voltage splits the output range into positive and negative halves.
- **Gain vs offset/bandwidth**: higher gain improves resolution but amplifies offset and cuts bandwidth. See [[q0452]].
:::

::: answer
"I take the shunt voltage at maximum current, $I_{max}R_s$, and divide the largest output voltage I can use by it. That's the maximum gain: $G_{max}=V_{out,max}/(I_{max}R_s)$. 'Usable' means the smaller of the ADC full-scale and the CSA's output swing limit, with 10 percent or so of headroom for overload and tolerance.

Then I round down to a gain the part actually offers, like 20, 50, 100 or 200, and verify: the output at full current should sit just below the top of the range, and I compute amps per LSB to be sure the resolution at the low end is acceptable.

Example: 10 milliohms, 5 amps, 3.3 volt ADC. The shunt gives 50 millivolts. The CSA can swing to about 3.2 volts, so the maximum gain is 64, and the standard value below is 50, which gives 2.5 volts at full scale.

If the current is bidirectional, the output is centered on the reference, so only the distance from the reference to the nearest limit counts. For plus or minus 10 amps on 5 milliohms with a 1.65 volt reference, the maximum gain is about 32, so I'd choose 20."
:::

::: technical
**Unidirectional.**
$$G_{max}=\frac{V_{out,max}-V_{out,min}}{k\,I_{max}\,R_s}$$
with $V_{out,max}$ the lower of the ADC full-scale and the CSA's maximum swing, $V_{out,min}\approx0$ (or the swing floor), and $k$ a headroom factor of 1.0 to 1.2. Select $G\le G_{max}$ from the available steps.

**Bidirectional with reference** $V_{REF}$ (typically mid-scale):
$$G_{max}=\min\left(\frac{V_{out,max}-V_{REF}}{I_{max,+}R_s},\ \frac{V_{REF}-V_{out,min}}{I_{max,-}R_s}\right)$$

**Checks.**
- Output at full-scale: $V_{out}=G I_{max}R_s\ (+V_{REF})$, below the swing limit.
- Resolution: $\Delta I_{LSB}=\dfrac{V_{FS}}{2^N\,G\,R_s}$.
- Offset floor: output offset $=G V_{os}$; current floor $V_{os}/R_s$ (gain-independent).
- Bandwidth at the chosen gain vs the requirement.

**Example 1 (unidirectional).** $R_s=10$ mΩ, $I_{max}=5$ A, ADC 0 to 3.3 V, swing limit 3.2 V.
$V_s=50$ mV, $G_{max}=3.2/0.05=64\Rightarrow G=50$: $V_{out}(5\text{ A})=2.5$ V; 1.6 mA/LSB at 12 bits; 6 counts at 10 mA. Gain 100 would give 5 V and clip at 3.2 A.

**Example 2 (bidirectional).** $R_s=5$ mΩ, ±10 A, $V_{REF}=1.65$ V, swing 0.05 to 3.25 V: $G_{max}=1.6/(10\times0.005)=32\Rightarrow G=20$; $V_{out}$ spans 0.65 to 2.65 V.
:::

::: short
"Maximum gain is the largest usable output voltage divided by the shunt voltage at full current: $G_{max}=V_{out,max}/(I_{max}R_s)$. Usable means within both the ADC range and the CSA's output swing, with some headroom. I round down to a standard gain such as 20, 50, 100 or 200, and check amps per LSB. ::: long
"The calculation is short, but I try to do it in a fixed order so I don't miss the checks.

Step one: shunt voltage at full current. Say the shunt is 10 milliohms and the maximum current is 5 amps. That's 50 millivolts.

Step two: usable output range. The ADC runs 0 to 3.3 volts, but the CSA's output can't quite reach the rails, so say it swings to 3.2 volts, and I want a little headroom for overload and tolerance. So the target for full-scale output is about 3 volts.

Step three: divide. Maximum gain is the output limit over the shunt voltage, so 3.2 over 0.05 is 64, or 60 with 10 percent headroom. CSAs come in discrete steps, say 20, 50, 100 and 200, so I round down to 50.

Step four: verify. At 5 amps the output is 2.5 volts, which is about 76 percent of the ADC range and leaves room for overload up to 6.4 amps before clipping. The resolution is 0.8 millivolts per count divided by 50 times 10 milliohms, about 1.6 milliamps per LSB. And at the low end, 10 milliamps gives 5 millivolts of output, about six counts, so I check whether that's enough, and compare the amplifier's offset against it.

If the current is bidirectional, the output rests at the reference, usually mid-scale, so I measure from the reference to the nearest limit. For plus or minus 10 amps on 5 milliohms, a 1.65 volt reference and a 3.25 volt top give a maximum gain of about 32, so I'd choose 20.

If a standard gain doesn't fit well, I can change the shunt a little rather than the gain, or use a part with an adjustable gain. And I always check bandwidth, because the higher gain options are slower."
:::

::: terms
- **Gain ($G$)**: ratio of output voltage to input differential voltage, in V/V.
- **Output swing**: the range the output can reach, limited near the rails.
- **Headroom**: margin between the maximum expected output and the limit.
- **Reference (REF) voltage**: output level at zero current; centers bidirectional output.
- **LSB**: ADC step, $V_{FS}/2^N$.
- **Full-scale current**: the current that gives the maximum allowed output.
- **Standard gain steps**: discrete gains offered by a part family, for example 20, 50, 100, 200 V/V.
- **Clipping/saturation**: output stuck at the swing limit when the signal is too large.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** divides the ADC range by the shunt voltage and quotes a gain.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Uses the usable output range (ADC and CSA swing together), with headroom, not the nominal 3.3 V.
- Rounds down to a standard gain and re-verifies the full-scale output and overload margin.
- Handles bidirectional current by splitting the range around REF.
- Computes the resulting resolution (mA/LSB) and the count at the lowest current, and compares offset ($V_{os}/R_s$).
- Notes the bandwidth cost of high gain and offers the alternative of adjusting the shunt when the standard steps don't fit.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using 3.3 V as the output limit.** CSAs don't swing fully to the rails, and the ADC reference tolerance eats margin.
- **Rounding up.** Picking a gain above the computed maximum guarantees clipping at the top.
- **Ignoring the reference in bidirectional sensing.** Positive and negative ranges are each about half the span.
- **Forgetting overload headroom.** A current spike above the rated maximum clips and may hide the fault magnitude.
- **Treating higher gain as strictly better.** It amplifies offset and noise equally and reduces bandwidth.
- **Mixing mΩ and Ω.** Write units through each step: $5\ \text{A}\times0.010\ \Omega=0.050$ V.
:::

::: deep
## The formula, derived

The chain is $V_{ADC}=G\,I\,R_s\ (+V_{REF})$. We want the largest current $I_{max}$ (times a headroom factor) to land at or just below the highest usable output, $V_{out,max}$. So

$$G\,k\,I_{max}R_s\le V_{out,max}\ \Rightarrow\ G_{max}=\frac{V_{out,max}}{k\,I_{max}R_s}$$

where $V_{out,max}$ is the **lower** of the ADC's full-scale and the CSA's maximum output swing. If the CSA is powered from 3.3 V and swings within 50 mV of the rail, the practical limit is 3.25 V; with a 3.0 V ADC reference, it is 3.0 V.

## Example 1: unidirectional

$R_s=10$ mΩ, $I_{max}=5$ A, $I_{min}=10$ mA, ADC 12 bits, 0 to 3.3 V, swing 3.2 V.

$V_s(I_{max})=0.05$ V. $G_{max}=3.2/0.05=64$ ($k=1$) or 58 ($k=1.1$).

| Gain | $V_{out}$ at 5 A | Headroom to 3.2 V | Max current before clipping | mA/LSB | Counts at 10 mA |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 1.0 V | large | 16 A | 4.03 | 2.5 |
| **50** | **2.5 V** | **28%** | **6.4 A** | **1.61** | **6.2** |
| 100 | 5 V (clips) | none | 3.2 A | 0.81 | 12.4 |
| 200 | 10 V (clips) | none | 1.6 A | 0.40 | 24.8 |

Gain 50 is the right pick: the largest standard step that doesn't clip. Gain 100 and 200 look better on resolution but fail the range check.

## Example 2: bidirectional

$R_s=5$ mΩ, ±10 A (charge and discharge), 3.3 V ADC, $V_{REF}=1.65$ V, swing 0.05 to 3.25 V.

$V_s(10\text{ A})=50$ mV. Positive room: $3.25-1.65=1.6$ V. Negative room: $1.65-0.05=1.6$ V.

$G_{max}=1.6/0.05=32\Rightarrow$ choose **20** (50 would give $1.65\pm2.5$ V and clip).

At $G=20$: $V_{out}$ spans $0.65$ to $2.65$ V; resolution $=0.806\text{ mV}/(20\times0.005)=8.06$ mA/LSB. If that's too coarse, use a 14 to 16-bit ADC, or increase the shunt (at the cost of loss), not more gain.

## Example 3: no standard gain fits

$R_s=25$ mΩ, ±3 A, ADC reference 2.5 V, $V_{REF}=1.25$ V, swing 0.05 to 2.45 V. Room each way: 1.2 V. $G_{max}=1.2/(3\times0.025)=16$. Available gains are 20, 50, 100: even 20 clips (at 20 the full-scale would be $1.25\pm1.5$ V).

Options: (a) reduce the shunt so $G=20$ fits: $R_s\le1.2/(3\times20)=20$ mΩ; (b) use a part with a 10 V/V option or adjustable gain; (c) add a resistor divider on the output (costs resolution and adds error); (d) accept clipping above ±2.4 A. Option (a) is usually simplest: one resistor value changes.

## Checking the low end

At the bottom of the range, three effects matter:

- **Resolution**: counts at $I_{min}$ are $I_{min}GR_s/V_{LSB}$ (Example 1: 6 counts at 10 mA).
- **Offset floor**: $I_{os}=V_{os}/R_s$. At 100 µV, 10 mΩ gives 10 mA, the same as $I_{min}$, so the reading at $I_{min}$ is 100 percent uncertain. The output offset is $G V_{os}=5$ mV, about six counts.
- **Output swing floor**: if the CSA can't output below 30 mV, then currents with $G I R_s<30$ mV (here $I<60$ mA) read as the floor value. A bidirectional configuration or a CSA with true zero output avoids that.

Gain doesn't change the offset floor in current terms; it only changes how many ADC counts the offset occupies. To improve the low end: a bigger shunt (more loss), a zero-drift CSA, a higher-resolution ADC, or firmware calibration of zero.

## Bandwidth check

Closed-loop bandwidth falls with gain. If the family offers 350 kHz at G = 50 and 100 kHz at G = 200 (illustrative), a design with a 20 kHz PWM and a protection response of 5 µs needs about $0.35/5\ \mu\text{s}=70$ kHz at least, so both are fine for response, but anything sampled at the PWM edge needs settling to well within the window. Always read the datasheet's bandwidth at the selected gain.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The gain steps (20/50/100/200), swing limits and bandwidth figures above are typical of popular CSA families but not universal; check the datasheet of the part you pick.

## A reusable procedure

1. $V_s=I_{max}R_s$.
2. $V_{out,max}$ = min(ADC full-scale, CSA swing limit), minus headroom; for bidirectional use the room from $V_{REF}$ to the nearer limit.
3. $G_{max}=V_{out,max}/V_s$; pick the next standard step down.
4. Verify full-scale output, overload headroom, mA/LSB, counts at $I_{min}$.
5. Check offset floor, swing floor, and bandwidth at that gain.
6. If it doesn't fit: change $R_s$ (preferred), the reference/ADC, or the part.
:::

::: applications
- **BLDC motor drives**: choose gain so stall current uses about 80 percent of the ADC range.
- **Battery packs**: bidirectional CSAs with REF at mid-scale for charge and discharge.
- **Power-rail monitors**: gain chosen so the rated rail current maps to the ADC range.
- **USB-C power monitoring**: 5 A max, 5 to 20 mΩ shunts, gains of 20 to 50.
- **Solenoid/LED drivers**: modest gains (20 to 50) with 100 to 500 mA full-scale on larger shunts.
- **Test equipment**: multiple shunt and gain ranges to cover decades of current.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes a single-supply CSA driving a ground-referenced ADC with an output swing close to the supply and a REF pin for bidirectional use.
- Assumes the ADC reference equals the supply (or lower); if the ADC reference is below the CSA's swing limit, use the ADC's full-scale as the limit.
- Assumes the available gains are 20/50/100/200 V/V. If the part has other steps (10, 25, 500, 1000) or adjustable gain, use the same procedure with those values.
- Headroom factor of 1.0 to 1.2 is a typical choice; tighter if a hardware current limit guards the load, looser if overloads are common.
- The shunt value is assumed fixed by loss and drop ([[q0445]]); if it isn't, iterate $R_s$ and $G$ together.
:::

::: followups
### What if the ideal gain is 64 but your part only offers 50 and 100?
Use 50 and accept the lower resolution (about 22 percent less range used), or adjust the shunt: with $G=100$ you'd need $R_s\le3.2/(100\times5)=6.4$ mΩ. Reducing the shunt to 6.2 mΩ (a standard value) with gain 100 uses nearly the same output range, with less power, but a larger offset-equivalent current ($V_{os}/R_s$ increases by 1.6x).

### How does the gain change if the ADC reference is 2.5 V instead of 3.3 V?
$V_{out,max}$ falls to about 2.4 V (reference less headroom), so for the same shunt and current $G_{max}$ drops by roughly 25 percent. In Example 1: $2.4/0.05=48$, so gain 50 would now slightly clip at 5 A and you would choose 20 or reduce $R_s$ slightly.

### How do you handle a very wide current range, like 10 mA to 10 A, with one CSA?
A single gain gives 1000:1 range, so a 12-bit ADC leaves only about 4 counts at the bottom. Use two parallel signal paths (low-gain and high-gain) read by different ADC channels, a switched shunt, a 16-bit or better ADC, or an autoranging scheme. Zero-drift CSAs help the low end.

### Scenario: with gain 50 your reading at 5 A is correct but at 6 A it stops increasing. What's happening?
The output is clipping. At 50 V/V × 10 mΩ × 6 A the ideal output is 3.0 V; with a 3.2 V swing limit the clip should only start at 6.4 A, so if it flattens at 6 A the real limit is lower than assumed: an ADC reference of 3.0 V, a CSA supply that has sagged, or an output swing that is worse under load than the datasheet's light-load figure. Reduce gain (to 20), reduce the shunt, or raise the ADC reference. Also check whether the clipped region matters for protection, since a clipped output hides the overload magnitude.

### What if the CSA's bandwidth at gain 200 is too low for fast PWM sampling?
Use a lower gain and make up the resolution with a bigger shunt or a higher-resolution ADC; or use a family with higher bandwidth at high gain; or filter and sample at a slower rate. Bandwidth falls with gain (about GBW/G), so the trade is between resolution and speed.
:::

::: interpretations
"Given a range of input currents, a sense resistor value, and ADC input voltage range, find an appropriate CSA gain." The "range of input currents" (minimum and maximum) is used in two ways: the **maximum** sets the gain via the output limit, and the **minimum** is used to check that resolution and offset are acceptable at the bottom. If the current is bidirectional, the range is signed and the REF voltage splits the output range. The version of the question that asks for the shunt itself is [[q0446]].
:::
