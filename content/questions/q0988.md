::: basics
### In plain words
A multimeter does not watch the signal continuously the way a scope does. It averages the input over a short window, then shows one number. "Sample rate" really means readings per second. The averaging window is what makes a multimeter accurate and quiet on a steady signal, and what makes it blind to anything that changes quickly.

### Picture it
Think of a long-exposure photograph. A still building comes out sharp. Cars driving past blur into nothing, or vanish. A multimeter's reading is like that long exposure. A steady battery voltage is the building, and a brief glitch is a car that drives through during the exposure. Longer exposures give cleaner pictures of still things but erase more motion.

### Words you need
- **Reading rate**: how many readings per second the meter shows.
- **Integration time**: how long the meter averages the input for each reading.
- **Mains hum**: pickup at the power-line frequency, 50 or 60 Hz.
- **NPLC**: number of power-line cycles in the averaging window. 1 NPLC at 60 Hz is $1/60\ \text{s} = 16.7$ ms.
- **Aliasing**: a fast variation looking like a slow one because readings come too rarely. See [[q0983]].

### The simplest example
Set a meter to 1 NPLC on 60 Hz mains. Each reading averages for 16.7 ms, so the top speed is about 60 readings a second. Averaging over a whole number of mains cycles also cancels 60 Hz hum, because a sine wave averages to zero over a full cycle. Averaging longer cuts random noise too.

For a steady 3.3 V rail, slow is better. But say the rail dips by 1 V for 1 ms and resets a processor. Averaged over 100 ms, that dip moves the reading by only about 10 mV. The meter shows a calm 3.30 V while the processor resets.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the speed, noise and hum trade-off in numbers, the cases where reading rate matters, and when a scope is the right tool.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand that a DMM's "sample rate" is really a **trade-off between speed, noise and mains rejection**, and that it matters only for some measurements. The interviewer wants: what the number means (readings per second, set by integration time in power-line cycles, NPLC), why slower gives lower noise and better 50/60 Hz rejection, when it matters (anything that changes within a reading: ripple, transients, pulsed loads, aliasing of slow variation), and when it doesn't (a steady rail, a battery voltage). A good answer ties it back to [[q0983]]: the integration time acts as the anti-alias filter.
:::

::: why
People assume a multimeter "samples" continuously and shows the truth. In fact it takes one averaged number every few tens to hundreds of milliseconds. That is exactly right for a stable DC rail and exactly wrong for a power-up glitch, a load transient or a 5 Hz oscillation. The same design choice that makes DMMs accurate (long integration) makes them blind to anything fast. Knowing how to trade reading speed for noise, and recognizing when the DMM can't answer the question, is a core measurement skill and the heart of the DMM-versus-scope decision ([[q0989]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Reading rate vs ADC sample rate**: the displayed or output readings per second versus the converter's internal sampling.
- **Integration time and NPLC**: the window over which the input is averaged; $T=N/f_{line}$.
- **Normal-mode rejection**: the ability of integration to suppress line-frequency interference.
- **Noise vs bandwidth**: averaging for time $T$ gives an effective bandwidth of about $1/(2T)$ and noise falling as $1/\sqrt{T}$. See [[q0980]].
- **Aliasing**: slow variations faster than half the reading rate appear as slower false variations. See [[q0669]], [[q0670]].
- **Auto-zero and settling**: overhead that reduces the maximum reading rate.
:::

::: answer
"A DMM's sample rate is basically how many readings per second it produces, and it's a trade-off, not a fixed spec.

Most DMMs use an integrating ADC. They average the input over a chosen time, usually a whole number of power-line cycles, called NPLC. At 1 NPLC on 60 Hz mains that's about 16.7 milliseconds per measurement, so a maximum of about 60 readings a second, less if auto-zero is on. A handheld typically gives 2 to 5 readings a second. Bench meters let you pick: fast with fewer digits and more noise, or slow, like 10 or 100 NPLC, for the best resolution and noise.

Longer integration does two things: it averages noise down, roughly as one over the square root of the time, and it rejects 50 or 60 hertz interference, because integrating over whole line cycles averages a sine at the line frequency to zero.

Does it matter? For a steady DC rail, a battery voltage, a resistance, no: slow is better. It matters when the signal changes within a reading. A DMM will average a glitch away, miss a power-up transient, and a signal that varies near the reading rate can even alias, so you see a slow wobble that isn't really there. For anything fast, I'd use a scope or a faster digitizing mode. So I choose the reading rate based on the signal: slow for accuracy on stable signals, fast, or a scope, for dynamic ones."
:::

::: technical
**What "sample rate" means for a DMM.** Two different numbers:

- **Reading rate**: readings per second delivered to the display or bus. Typical: 2–5 rdg/s on handhelds; on bench meters from about 0.5 rdg/s (high-resolution, slow) to 1000s of rdg/s (reduced digits, fast mode).
- **Internal ADC sample rate**: how often the converter's raw samples are taken (for sigma-delta or multi-slope types the modulator samples at kHz–MHz, but the output reading is a filtered average).

**Integration time.** For line frequency $f_{line}$, $T_{int}=N_{PLC}/f_{line}$. At 60 Hz: 1 NPLC = 16.67 ms, 10 NPLC = 167 ms, 100 NPLC = 1.67 s; at 50 Hz, 1 NPLC = 20 ms. Maximum reading rate $\approx 1/T_{int}$ (halve it if auto-zero measures the offset between readings).

**Noise.** For white noise, averaging over $T$ reduces RMS noise as $1/\sqrt{T}$: going from 1 to 100 NPLC lowers noise by 10×, until the $1/f$ and thermal-EMF floor is reached. Equivalent noise bandwidth for a boxcar average is $\approx 1/(2T)$.

**Normal-mode rejection (NMR).** An integrator of length $T$ has magnitude response $\left|\frac{\sin(\pi fT)}{\pi fT}\right|$, with nulls at $f=k/T$. With $T$ a whole number of line cycles, line frequency and its harmonics are nulled. Because real mains drifts by about ±1 %, practical rejection is around 40 dB for 1 NPLC (for 1 % deviation, $|\text{sinc}|\approx0.01$). Using the wrong line-frequency setting (50 Hz meter on 60 Hz mains, or vice versa) leaves only about 14 dB of rejection at 1 NPLC.

**Dynamic signals.** A signal changing faster than the integration window is averaged: a 100 Hz sine measured on a 100 ms window reads its DC average (zero), and a 1 ms glitch contributes only a fraction of its height. Also, a periodic input at frequency $f$ read at rate $f_r$ (with a short window) aliases to $|f-kf_r|$: a 5.1 Hz variation read at 5 rdg/s appears as a 0.1 Hz drift.
:::

"A DMM doesn't sample continuously the way a scope does. Its 'sample rate' is the number of readings per second, and that's set by how long its ADC integrates each measurement.

Most DMMs use an integrating converter, and the integration time is a number of power-line cycles, NPLC. At one cycle on 60 hertz mains, that's about 16.7 milliseconds, so the top speed is about 60 readings a second; with auto-zero on it's roughly half. A handheld gives a few readings a second. A bench meter lets me choose: a fast mode with fewer digits and more noise, or 10 or 100 NPLC for the best resolution.

Why integrate over line cycles? Two reasons. Averaging over a longer time reduces random noise, roughly as one over the square root of the time. And a sine at the line frequency integrates to zero over a whole number of cycles, so the meter rejects 50 or 60 hertz pickup. In practice, mains isn't perfectly stable so the rejection is around 40 dB, and if the meter is set for the wrong line frequency it drops to roughly 14 dB.

Does it matter? For a steady DC rail, a battery, a resistance, no, and slower is better. It matters whenever the signal changes inside a reading. A power-up glitch gets averaged out, ripple on a rail just reads as its average, and a slow oscillation near the reading rate can alias: a 5.1 hertz variation read five times a second looks like a 0.1 hertz drift. For those cases I'd use a scope, or a bench DMM's digitizing mode if I need accurate numbers on a fast event."
:::

::: terms
- **Reading rate**: readings per second produced by the meter.
- **NPLC (number of power-line cycles)**: integration time in units of the mains period.
- **Integrating ADC**: converter that averages the input over a time window (dual-slope, multi-slope, sigma-delta).
- **Normal-mode rejection (NMR)**: attenuation of interference superimposed on the signal, such as line pickup.
- **Auto-zero**: periodic measurement of the meter's own offset, which costs time.
- **Settling time**: delay needed after a range or input change for the reading to stabilize.
- **Digitizing mode**: a fast sampling mode on some bench meters for capturing waveforms with limited resolution.
- **Aliasing**: false low frequency produced when sampling a faster variation. See [[q0983]].
- **Noise bandwidth**: effective bandwidth of the measurement, about $1/(2T)$ for averaging time $T$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a few samples per second, and no, it doesn't matter for DC."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates **reading rate** from **ADC sample rate** and explains **integration/NPLC**.
- Quantifies: **16.7 ms at 1 NPLC on 60 Hz**, up to ~60 rdg/s, **noise ∝ 1/√T**, about **40 dB** line rejection (and **14 dB** if the line-frequency setting is wrong).
- Connects it to **anti-aliasing and bandwidth**: the integration is the filter ([[q0983]]).
- Gives the **trade-off** and says when to choose each setting.
- Says clearly **when it matters** (transients, ripple, pulsed loads, aliasing of slow variation) and what to use instead (scope, digitizing mode, data logger).
- Mentions **auto-zero and settling** overhead, and the fact that a **slow reading is not a data capture**.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Calling a DMM "slow = bad".** Slow integration is exactly why it is accurate and quiet; the trade-off is speed.
- **Expecting a DMM to catch a glitch or a power-up transient.** It averages over tens of ms at best; use a scope with a single-shot trigger.
- **Setting the wrong line frequency** (50 vs 60 Hz) and then wondering about noisy readings: rejection falls to about 14 dB at 1 NPLC.
- **Treating the reading rate as the bandwidth of the instrument.** The AC and DC bandwidths are separate; the reading rate also depends on settings like auto-zero, resolution and range.
- **Logging a fast signal at the DMM's default rate** and interpreting the aliased trace as real.
- **Using the fastest mode for accuracy.** Fast modes cut digits and raise noise; the quoted accuracy is for the slow, high-resolution setting.
:::

::: deep
## What the DMM is actually doing between readings

A typical integrating DMM cycle has several phases: an optional **auto-zero** phase (measure its own offset with the input shorted), the **integration** of the input over $T_{int}$, then conversion and processing. The reading rate is $1/(T_{int}+T_{overhead})$ at best. For a 60 Hz meter at 1 NPLC without auto-zero, $T_{int}=16.67$ ms and the maximum is about 60 readings per second. With auto-zero enabled the offset measurement takes another integration, halving it to about 30. At 10 NPLC the numbers are 167 ms and about 6 (or 3) readings per second.

| NPLC | $T_{int}$ at 60 Hz | Max readings/s (no auto-zero) | Noise relative to 1 NPLC |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 1.67 ms | ≈ 600 | ≈ 3.2× |
| 1 | 16.7 ms | ≈ 60 | 1× |
| 10 | 167 ms | ≈ 6 | ≈ 0.32× |
| 100 | 1.67 s | ≈ 0.6 | ≈ 0.1× |

The noise column follows $1/\sqrt{N_{PLC}}$, valid until other noise sources (1/f noise, thermal EMFs, reference noise) dominate. Meters often also drop displayed digits at fast settings because the noise makes the last digits meaningless.

## Why integrate over line cycles: the filter view

Averaging a signal over a window $T$ is a low-pass filter with a sinc-shaped frequency response:

$$ |H(f)|=\left|\frac{\sin(\pi fT)}{\pi fT}\right| $$

It has nulls at $f=k/T$ for integers $k$. When $T=N/f_{line}$, a null sits exactly at the line frequency and its harmonics, which is why the meter ignores mains hum.

```plot
 "vlines":[{"at":60,"label":"60 Hz"},{"at":50,"label":"50 Hz"}]}
```
Figure: the 1 NPLC response has a deep null at 60 Hz and 120 Hz; the 10 NPLC response has nulls every 6 Hz (including 60 and 120) and much lower side lobes, so even 50 Hz, which is not on a null, is attenuated by about 30 dB. If the mains drifts 1 % off its nominal value the null isn't exact, which is why the real rejection is about 40 dB rather than infinite. At 50 Hz hum on a 1 NPLC (60 Hz) setting the gain is only about −14 dB.

Check of the numbers: at 60.6 Hz (1 % high) with $T=1/60$ s, $\pi fT=\pi\times1.01$ and $|H|=\sin(0.01\pi)/(1.01\pi)=0.0099$, which is −40 dB. At 50 Hz with the same $T$, $\pi fT=\pi\times0.8333$ gives $|H|=0.19$, which is −14.4 dB.

## When the sample rate matters

### Cases where it doesn't

- A **DC supply rail** or battery voltage: slow, accurate readings are what you want.
- **Resistance** of a component or a sensor at rest.
- **Long-term drift or logging** every few seconds or minutes (battery discharge, temperature).

### Cases where it does

1. **Transient events.** A 1 ms droop on a rail that resets a processor changes the 100 ms average by 1 % of the droop's depth. The DMM shows 3.30 V while the processor has been reset. A scope with single-shot trigger is the tool ([[q0989]]).
2. **Ripple and noise.** A 100 kHz ripple on DC averages out; the DMM reads the mean. In AC mode with a limited bandwidth you may see only part of it.
3. **Pulsed or switching loads.** A device that draws 200 mA for 1 ms every 100 ms averages to 2 mA over 100 ms. The DMM correctly reports the average, which is fine for battery life, but says nothing about peak current. Burden voltage on the current range may also make the circuit misbehave during pulses.
4. **Slow oscillations near the reading rate.** A signal at 5.1 Hz measured at 5 readings/s aliases to 0.1 Hz. Run the simulation:

A DMM's displayed trace has this property whenever the input changes at a rate comparable to the reading rate (the integration window gives a partial anti-alias filter but not a steep one).

5. **Logging at the wrong rate.** Data-logging a signal with frequency content above half the logging rate yields misleading trends. Set the integration time (which acts as the anti-alias filter) to match the logging interval.

## Faster modes and other options

Some bench DMMs have **digitizing** or **fast-sampling** modes that sample DC volts (or current) at rates from tens to hundreds of kS/s with reduced resolution (for example 16 to 18 bits), sometimes with a trigger and memory. They bridge toward a scope, offering higher accuracy but much lower bandwidth and no probing ecosystem. Data acquisition (DAQ) systems and source-measure units (SMUs) offer similar fast sampling with precision.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Maximum digitizing sample rates, resolution at those rates and available trigger features differ greatly between DMM models and are not universal; check the datasheet for the model you will use. The same applies to the quoted reading rates and the amount of rejection at a given NPLC.

## Settling, auto-zero and range changes

The reading rate you can *use* is lower than the maximum:

- **Auto-zero** doubles the cycle time but removes offset drift. Turn it off for speed only if you accept offset drift.
- **Auto-range** changes the range during measurement: each change takes time and settling. Fixed range is faster and more predictable for logging.
- **Settling**: cable capacitance, source resistance and dielectric absorption create RC settling that may need more than one reading. Measuring a high-impedance node with a 10 MΩ input and 100 pF of cable has $\tau=1$ ms, and reaching 1 ppm of the final value needs about $14\tau$.
- **Filters**: many meters offer a digital averaging filter (a moving or repeating average) that further reduces noise at the cost of response time.

## A rule for choosing

| Goal | Setting |
|---|---|
| Best DC accuracy and low noise on a steady signal | 10–100 NPLC, auto-zero on, fixed range |
| Quick check of a rail | 1 NPLC or handheld default |
| Logging slow drift | Integration time ≈ logging interval |
| Catching transients, ripple, glitches | Not a DMM job: scope, or digitizing mode with trigger |
| Production test speed | 0.1–1 NPLC with averaging, fixed range, minimal digits |

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Say the sentence "the integration time is the DMM's anti-alias filter." It ties the DMM into the sampling theory the interviewer cares about, and it explains why both noise and line-frequency rejection improve at the same time as speed worsens.
:::

::: applications
- **Precision DC measurement** of references and calibrators at 10 to 100 NPLC with auto-zero on.
- **Battery discharge logging**: one reading every few seconds, integrated over about 1 to 10 NPLC.
- **Production test**: fast NPLC settings with fixed ranges to meet cycle time.
- **Temperature logging** from thermocouples or RTDs with a scanner at a modest rate.
- **Detecting slow oscillation** in a control loop by logging a DC signal (with care about aliasing).
- **Checking supply consumption** of pulsed devices: the average over a window equals charge per window.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes an **integrating ADC** meter. Some modern DMMs and DAQ instruments use sigma-delta or SAR converters with digital filters; the same trade-offs (bandwidth, noise, rejection) apply but the formulas differ.
- Numbers for NPLC use **60 Hz mains** (16.67 ms); for **50 Hz** use 20 ms per NPLC.
- The $1/\sqrt{T}$ noise scaling assumes **white noise**; 1/f and thermal drift set a floor.
- The sinc response is for an ideal **boxcar integrator**; real meters have additional filtering and auto-zero timing.
- Line-rejection figures of 40 dB assume about **±1 % mains frequency deviation**.
- Handheld reading rates of 2–5 rdg/s are **typical**; bar-graph update rates are much faster than the digit display.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a DMM show a stable 3.30 V while my microcontroller resets?
The DMM averages over its integration window (tens to hundreds of ms), so a brief droop of a few ms and a few hundred mV barely changes the average. The reset is caused by the instantaneous voltage dipping below the brown-out threshold. A scope in single-shot mode, triggered below 3.0 V, will show it.

### What does 100 NPLC buy you?
Roughly 10× lower random noise than 1 NPLC (until a floor), and higher resolution, at the cost of about 1.7 s per reading at 60 Hz. It also narrows the equivalent bandwidth to about 0.3 Hz. It's for precision DC work, like measuring a voltage reference or a µV-level signal, not for monitoring changing signals.

### How does a DMM reject 60 Hz noise but a scope doesn't?
A DMM integrates over exactly one or more line periods, so a 60 Hz sinusoid averages to zero. A scope samples the instantaneous voltage and shows everything within its bandwidth including the hum; you can average over many acquisitions or use a bandwidth limit, but there's no inherent mains rejection.

### Will turning on a DMM's data logger at 1 reading/s catch a 0.9 Hz oscillation?
It will record something, but the logged waveform shows a false slow beat, since 0.9 Hz is close to the sampling rate (it appears as a 0.1 Hz variation). The integration window gives only a partial anti-alias filter. Log faster (at least 2.5× the highest frequency of interest) or use a scope.

### Scenario: you need to measure the average current of a pulsed IoT device (1 ms 100 mA pulse every second). What setup?
Average current is $100\ \text{mA}\times1\ \text{ms}/1\ \text{s}=0.1$ mA plus sleep current. A DMM integrating over several seconds, or 100 NPLC, gives the average well, but the burden voltage in the pulse (shunt of a few ohms on the mA range) can brown out the device. Better: a low-burden shunt and scope, or a power profiler or SMU in a fast sampling mode that reports both average and peak.
:::
