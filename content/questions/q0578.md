::: basics
### In plain words
A filled via has its hole filled with epoxy (a hard resin) or copper and then covered with a flat copper cap. The surface is smooth, like an ordinary pad. You use one when a via has to sit inside a solder pad, because an open hole steals solder. You also use them to stack microvias and move heat. They cost more, so fill only where needed.

### Picture it
Picture a puddle of water sitting next to a drain. The water runs down the hole and leaves the puddle empty. Melted solder behaves the same way, because the via's copper-lined hole pulls it in like a straw. A filled via is a stopper pushed into the drain, sanded flat, and capped, so the puddle stays where it was meant to be.

### Words you need
- **Via-in-pad**: a via placed directly in a component's solder pad, used when there is no room beside the pad.
- **Solder wicking**: liquid solder being pulled down into an open via.
- **Fill and cap**: filling the barrel, sanding it flat, and plating copper over the top.
- **Microvia**: a tiny laser-drilled via spanning one layer. See [[q0576]].
- **Void**: an empty bubble in a solder joint that weakens it.

### The simplest example
Take a 0.3 mm via with a 0.25 mm finished hole in a 1.6 mm board. Its inner volume is about 0.079 mm³. A 0.3 mm solder ball, the tiny bump under some chips, holds only about 0.014 mm³. So an open via under a ball can swallow the solder of about 5 or 6 balls in the worst case. In practice the barrel does not fill completely, but even part of that loss starves the joint and leaves voids. A filled and capped via keeps the solder on the pad.

That is the whole idea: filling fixes a specific problem, a hole that steals solder or breaks a stack. The rest of this page covers fill types, thermal results, and how to specify them.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know **why** a via would be filled, not just that fills exist. The interviewer is checking that you connect the fill to a concrete problem:

1. **Via-in-pad** under BGA, QFN or fine-pitch passives: an open barrel steals solder.
2. **Stacked microvias** in HDI, which need a flat, copper-filled via to build the next layer on.
3. **Thermal and current** paths where the fill adds copper.
4. You can tell **filled** from **plugged** and **tented**, and know the trade-offs (cost, conductive vs non-conductive fill, capped vs uncapped).
:::

::: why
A via under a solder pad is a hole in the middle of a soldering surface. In reflow, molten solder is pulled down into the barrel by capillary action, leaving a starved joint on the component side and voids inside it. Filled vias are the standard answer when the layout leaves you no room to move the via off the pad. They are also the building block of HDI. The choice between non-conductive, conductive and copper fill, and whether the via is capped, comes up in real fab notes and in interviews that probe manufacturing awareness.
:::

::: concepts
- **Via anatomy**: a plated barrel with a hollow centre. See [[q0574]].
- **Via-in-pad**: a via placed within a component's solder pad. See [[q0582]].
- **Solder wicking**: molten solder drawn into any narrow open channel by capillary action.
- **Filled vs plugged vs tented**: three different ways of closing a via. See [[q0579]].
- **Capping (planarisation)**: plating copper over the fill so the pad is flat and solderable.
- **Thermal conduction**: heat flow $Q=\Delta T/R_{th}$ with $R_{th}=L/(kA)$.
:::

::: answer
"I want filled vias whenever a via has to sit in a solder pad or when the stack needs a flat surface to build on. The classic case is a BGA or a fine-pitch QFN where there's no space for a dogbone, so the via goes directly under the ball. If that via is open, solder runs down the barrel during reflow, so you get a starved joint on top and voids, and the part can open or fail later. If the fab fills the via with epoxy and then plates copper over it, the pad is flat again, and the barrel can't steal solder.

The second case is HDI. Stacked microvias have to be filled, usually with plated copper, so that the next microvia can land directly on top without a dimple.

The third is thermal or current. A copper-filled via has more cross-section than a hollow one, so it conducts heat and current better. A non-conductive epoxy fill doesn't help with that, though; it's there for flatness.

The trade-off is cost. Filling and planarising are extra process steps, so I only call for it where it solves a problem, and I specify the type on the fab notes, for example filled and capped, IPC-4761 type VII."
:::

::: technical
**What a filled via is.** A via barrel filled with a material, then (normally) planarised flat and plated over with copper ("capped") so that the surface can be used as a normal pad. Fill materials:
- **Non-conductive epoxy** (the most common for via-in-pad): fills the barrel, cured, ground flat, capped with copper.
- **Conductive paste** (copper- or silver-filled epoxy): similar process; adds some thermal/electrical conduction, but far less than copper.
- **Electroplated copper fill**: standard for laser microvias, and used for stacking.

**When to specify filled.**
1. **Via-in-pad** on fine-pitch BGAs (about 0.65 mm pitch and below, depending on rules), QFNs and small passives: prevents solder loss and voids.
2. **Stacked microvias** (HDI): a copper-filled via supports the next level.
3. **Thermal pads**: copper-filled vias have lower thermal resistance, and filling stops solder draining through the pad.
4. **Preventing flux and fluid trapping**, and keeping paste from escaping during stencil printing.
5. **Vacuum fixture testing**: a filled/plugged via seals the board; see [[q0579]].

**Numbers.** A 0.3 mm via (0.25 mm finished hole, 1.6 mm board) has an internal volume of about 0.079 mm³. A 0.3 mm solder ball holds about 0.014 mm³, so an open via can swallow the solder of roughly **5–6 balls**. For a 0.5 mm ball (0.065 mm³) it's still 1.2 balls' worth.

Thermally, the plated barrel alone is about 192 K/W per via. Epoxy fill with $k\approx1$ W/m·K adds almost nothing (the fill column alone is about 33,000 K/W), and conductive paste at about 4 W/m·K adds only around 2 % to the conductance. A solid copper-filled via of the same 0.3 mm diameter is about 59 K/W, roughly 3.3 times better.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Thermal conductivity of conductive pastes varies by product and is often much lower than bulk metal. Check the fab's datasheet. Cap thickness, dimple limits and fill-void acceptance depend on the fab and the IPC class.

**Naming.** IPC-4761 describes via protection types: I tenting, II tenting and covering, III plugging, IV plugging and covering, V filling, VI filling and covering, VII filling and capping. Via-in-pad typically needs type VII (fill and cap), and the fab note should say it explicitly.
:::

::: short
"I use filled vias when a via has to sit in a solder pad, like under a BGA or QFN, because an open barrel wicks solder away and starves the joint. The fab fills the barrel with epoxy and plates copper over it so the pad is flat. I also use copper-filled vias for stacked microvias in HDI and for better thermal conduction. The cost is extra process steps, so I specify it only where it solves a problem."
:::

::: long
"The main reason to fill a via is that I need to put it in a solder pad. Take a fine-pitch BGA, say 0.5 millimetre pitch. There's no room for a dogbone, a short trace to a via beside the pad, so the via goes right in the pad. If it's left open, then in reflow the molten solder wicks down the barrel by capillary action. A 0.3 millimetre via through a 1.6 millimetre board holds about 0.08 cubic millimetres, which is several solder balls' worth. The joint ends up starved or voided, and you get opens or weak joints.

The fix is a filled via: the fab fills the barrel, usually with non-conductive epoxy, grinds it flat, and plates copper over the top, a cap. That gives a flat, solderable pad again. On the drawing I'd call it out as filled and capped, IPC-4761 type VII.

The second reason is HDI. Stacked microvias are copper-filled so that the next layer's microvia can land right on top of the one below, giving the densest routing.

The third reason is heat and current. Copper-filled vias have a solid cross-section, so a 0.3 millimetre one is about 3 times better thermally than a hollow one. A non-conductive epoxy fill doesn't help there, since it conducts about 400 times worse than copper.

What it costs me is money and lead time, because fill, planarising and capping are extra steps. And it's a reliability consideration too, since voids in the fill can trap gas. So I use filled vias where there's a real reason, I tell the fab exactly which type I want, and otherwise I'd move the via off the pad."
:::

::: terms
- **Filled via**: a via whose barrel is filled with epoxy, conductive paste or copper.
- **Capped (planarised) via**: a filled via ground flat and plated over so the surface is a normal pad.
- **Via-in-pad (VIPPO)**: a via placed directly inside a component pad, usually filled and plated over (via-in-pad plated over).
- **Dogbone**: a short trace connecting a pad to an adjacent via so the via is not in the pad.
- **Solder wicking**: capillary action drawing molten solder into the via barrel.
- **Non-conductive fill**: epoxy fill used mainly for flatness.
- **Copper-filled via**: via completely plated solid; standard for microvias and thermal.
- **IPC-4761**: the standard describing via protection types (tenting, plugging, filling, capping).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "When you want a flat surface, like a via in a BGA pad."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the failure mechanism: capillary wicking, starved joints and voids, with a volume comparison of via to solder ball.
- Lists the three motivations (via-in-pad, stacked microvias, thermal/current) and ties each to a design condition.
- Distinguishes **filled** from **plugged** and **tented**, and non-conductive from conductive and copper fill, with the real thermal difference (epoxy barely helps).
- Mentions capping and naming the type on the fab note (IPC-4761 type VII).
- Notes the cost and that moving the via off the pad (dogbone) is cheaper when space allows.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Conductive fill makes a great thermal via."** Conductive paste has a conductivity far below copper, so the improvement is modest. Copper plating or copper fill is what carries heat.
- **Confusing filled with plugged or tented.** Plugged vias are only closed, not planarised and capped, so they are not flat enough for via-in-pad. See [[q0579]].
- **Leaving the cap off.** An uncapped epoxy-filled via has a non-solderable dimple in the pad.
- **Filling every via.** It's expensive. Fill only vias that need it (in pads, stacked microvias, key thermal vias).
- **Ignoring the fab's rules.** Maximum drill and aspect ratio for fill (often around 0.15–0.5 mm) and dimple limits differ by fab.
- **Assuming a filled via is always more reliable.** Voids and poor adhesion in the fill can cause cracking or outgassing in reflow.
:::

::: deep
## The problem filled vias solve

Put a normal via in the pad of a small component. During reflow the solder paste melts and wets the copper pad. Wherever there is an opening, the liquid is pulled into it by capillary action, and a plated barrel is a perfect capillary tube. Three bad things follow:

1. **Starved joint.** The solder that left the pad is not available to make the joint, so the ball or lead sits on too little solder.
2. **Voids.** Gas (flux vapour) that can't escape bubbles up in the joint, creating voids that hurt strength and thermal conduction.
3. **Solder on the other side.** Solder can run through the barrel and bulge on the back of the board, interfering with assembly, or short to neighbouring pads.

## Quantifying the loss

An open 0.3 mm via with a 0.25 mm finished hole in a 1.6 mm board has an internal volume of

$$V=\frac{\pi}{4}(0.25)^2(1.6)\approx0.079\ \text{mm}^3.$$

A 0.3 mm solder ball (a typical size for 0.5 mm-pitch BGAs) is $\frac{4}{3}\pi(0.15)^3\approx0.014\ \text{mm}^3$. The ratio is about 5.6 balls. Even a 0.5 mm ball (0.065 mm³) is smaller than one open via. In practice the barrel does not fill completely, because flux and trapped air resist, but the wicked volume is large enough to starve a joint.

For a microvia the numbers change: a 0.1 mm × 0.1 mm microvia holds only 0.0008 mm³, so it can be put in a pad even unfilled in some processes, although filling is still common for flatness.

## Types of fill and when each is used

| Fill | Main purpose | Thermal/electrical help | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Non-conductive epoxy, capped | flat pad, stop wicking | almost none | via-in-pad under BGA/QFN |
| Conductive paste, capped | flat pad, some conduction | small (a few percent) | where a spec asks for conductive fill |
| Plated copper fill | flat pad, solid copper | large (roughly 3x per via for 0.3 mm) | microvias, stacked vias, power/thermal |
| None, with mask (tented/plugged) | cheap closure | none | vias off pads. See [[q0579]]. |

Capping matters: after fill is cured, the fab planes the surface and plates copper over it so the pad surface is a continuous copper layer ready for surface finish (ENIG, HASL and so on). Without a cap, the pad has a depression over the fill that can trap flux and isn't reliably solderable.

## Thermal and electrical value

For a QFN exposed pad dissipating heat through a via array, the vias' thermal resistance matters. With $R_{th}=L/(kA)$:

| Via (0.3 mm, 1.6 mm board) | $R_{th}$ per via | 9 vias | 16 vias |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hollow plated barrel (25 µm) | 192 K/W | 21 K/W | 12 K/W |
| Copper-filled | 59 K/W | 6.5 K/W | 3.7 K/W |

For 2 W through nine vias, the hollow barrel gives a temperature difference of about 43 K across the board thickness (vias alone), the filled version about 13 K. Real boards also have lateral spreading and the plane resistance, so these are upper bounds. The point: **copper** fill matters; epoxy does not.

## Stacked microvias and HDI

In HDI, a microvia on layer 2 connects down to layer 3 and the next microvia on top of it connects layer 1 to 2. To stack them, the lower one must be filled so that the upper laser-drilled via lands on a flat surface. Electroplated copper fill is the usual method. This is why stacked microvias are sensitive to plating quality: voids or dimples in the fill become initiation sites for cracks in thermal cycling.

## Real-hardware gotchas

- **Fill voids**: air can be trapped in the epoxy; outgassing during reflow can blow a cap.
- **Dimples**: cap thickness and planarity limits (often a few tens of micrometres) are in the fab spec.
- **Maximum drill size**: fill processes work for limited hole sizes, usually a small drill (about 0.15–0.5 mm); large holes are not fillable.
- **CTE mismatch**: epoxy fill expands differently from copper, adding stress to the barrel over many thermal cycles.
- **Cost**: filling plus planarising can be one of the larger add-ons on a board price, so only apply it to specified vias.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked "when wouldn't you fill?", say: "Whenever I can put the via off the pad. A dogbone or a via beside the pad, tented or plugged, is cheaper and has fewer process risks."

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Limits for via drill, fill thickness, dimple depth and acceptance of voids are fab- and class-dependent. IPC-4761 names the via protection types, but how a fab maps its processes onto those types varies; confirm on the fab quote.
:::

::: applications
- **BGAs at 0.5 mm pitch and below**: filled and capped via-in-pad under the ball.
- **QFN/LGA exposed pads**: copper-filled thermal vias in the centre pad where draining solder would cause voids.
- **0201/0402 passives in dense layouts**: via-in-pad with filled and capped vias to save space.
- **HDI phone and wearable boards**: copper-filled stacked microvias.
- **Power modules**: filled vias for current and thermal paths between top-side parts and inner copper.
- **Boards needing vacuum fixture testing**: vias filled/plugged so the vacuum seals. See [[q0579]].
:::

::: assumptions
- Standard FR-4, 1.6 mm board, 0.3 mm drill with about 25 µm plating, 0.25 mm finished hole.
- Solder joints formed by reflow with a standard paste; wave soldering behaves differently, since the barrel exposure is on the other side.
- Thermal values are vias alone; real heat paths include spreading in copper planes and interface resistance.
- Epoxy conductivity taken as about 1 W/m·K and conductive paste about 4 W/m·K as illustrative values.
- "Filled" here means a fab-processed fill with capping. Some people use "filled" loosely for any closed via (plugged or tented), which is covered in [[q0579]].
:::

::: followups
### Why not just make the via smaller so it can't wick?
Smaller helps, and a 0.1 mm laser microvia holds so little volume that unfilled microvia-in-pad is tolerated in some processes. But small holes in a thick board have an aspect-ratio problem. A 0.15 mm drill in 1.6 mm is 10.7:1. So you would move to a thinner stack, microvias, or fill the larger via.

### What's the difference between a filled via and a plugged via?
Filled means the barrel is filled and then planarised and (usually) capped with copper, so the pad is flat. Plugged means the hole is blocked with mask or epoxy, but the surface is not flat or plated over; it's good enough to keep paste or fluid out but not for a via-in-pad. See [[q0579]].

### Would you use conductive-fill vias for a thermal pad?
Only if the spec asks for it. Conductive paste adds only a few percent of the conductance compared with the plated barrel, so the benefit is small. For serious thermal performance I'd use copper-filled vias, more vias, or a thicker plane. I'd flag this uncertainty and ask the fab for the paste's conductivity.

### What problems can a filled via cause?
Voids or poor adhesion in the fill can trap gas that bursts in reflow ("popcorning"), cap dimples can trap flux, and the fill and copper expand differently under thermal cycling, which adds stress to the barrel. Fab control and testing coupons are the answer, which is why you only fill the vias that need it.

### Scenario: after reflow, a BGA on your board shows intermittent opens on balls that sit over vias. What do you suspect?
Solder wicking into open vias-in-pad. I'd check the fab notes: were those vias filled and capped? I'd do an X-ray to look for voids, cross-section a failed joint, and compare with an assembly where the via is in a dogbone. The corrective actions are to specify type VII fill and cap, or move vias off the pads. Enlarging the stencil apertures on those pads to add paste is at best a stopgap.
:::
