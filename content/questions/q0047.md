::: basics
### In plain words
A real inductor is not just an ideal coil. The wire has resistance. The turns sit close together and act like a small capacitor. The core wastes some energy as heat. A realistic model adds those extras to the ideal inductor, so you can predict where the part stops acting like an inductor.

### Picture it
Think of a garden hose that is supposed to be a perfect pipe. In real life the hose is slightly narrow, so it has some friction. It also bulges a little, so it stores some water. A real inductor is the same: the ideal coil is the pipe, and the extras are the friction and the bulge. The model is a simplification, since the extras are spread through the part, not sitting in separate lumps.

### Words you need
- **DCR**: the DC resistance of the wire, in series with the ideal inductor. See [[q0011]].
- **Winding capacitance ($C_w$)**: tiny capacitance between the turns, drawn in parallel with the coil.
- **Core loss**: energy wasted in the magnetic core as heat, drawn as a resistor.
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: where $L$ and $C_w$ resonate, $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_w})$.
- **Quality factor ($Q$)**: how good the inductor is, $\omega L / R$. High $Q$ means low loss.

### The simplest example
Take a 10 µH inductor with 5 pF of winding capacitance. Below about 22 MHz it acts like an inductor. At about 22 MHz the inductance and capacitance resonate, and the impedance peaks. Above that, the part acts like a capacitor. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows how to draw the model, explains where each element comes from, and covers effects the lumped model does not show, such as saturation.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can draw the real-inductor equivalent circuit and **explain where each element comes from**. The interviewer is checking that you know:

1. The standard model: ideal $L$ with series winding resistance (DCR, more precisely ACR at frequency), a **parallel winding capacitance** across the whole coil, and a loss resistance that represents core loss.
2. Origins: wire resistance $\rho\ell/A$; turn-to-turn and layer-to-layer capacitance; hysteresis and eddy-current loss in the core.
3. Consequences: a self-resonant frequency $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_w})$, above which the part is capacitive; $Q=\omega L/R$; apparent inductance that rises near the SRF.
4. Non-element effects: saturation (inductance falls with current), temperature, and mutual coupling to neighboring parts.
:::

::: why
Inductors are the least ideal passive: every converter inductor dissipates real power in DCR and the core, an EMI choke stops working above its SRF, an RF matching inductor shifts the circuit if its SRF is too close, and saturation can destroy a switching converter. The model is the starting point for choosing a part (DCR vs. $I_{sat}$ vs. SRF), estimating efficiency, and understanding why the datasheet says "inductance at 100 kHz, 0.1 V".
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductance and flux** ([[q0039]]): $L=N^2/\mathcal R$; flux linkage $\lambda=Li$.
- **Impedance** ([[q0040]]): $Z=j\omega L$ for the ideal part; the model adds elements in series/parallel.
- **Resistance from wire**: $R=\rho\ell/A$, plus skin/proximity effects at frequency ([[q0051]]).
- **Capacitance between turns**: any two conductors at different potentials have capacitance ([[q0011]]).
- **Core losses** ([[q0050]]): hysteresis and eddy-current loss depend on $f$ and flux swing.
- **Saturation** ([[q0046]]): above $B_{sat}$ the core permeability collapses and $L$ falls.
:::

::: answer
"The realistic model is an ideal inductor L in series with a resistor, which is the winding resistance. Then there's a capacitor in parallel with the whole thing, which is the winding capacitance, and if I'm being thorough, a resistor in parallel with L representing the core loss.

The series resistance is just the copper: the wire has resistance rho times length over area, and at higher frequencies it increases because of skin and proximity effects, so we talk about DC resistance and AC resistance. The parallel capacitance comes from the turns: adjacent turns are conductors at slightly different voltages with insulation between them, so you have a capacitor between every pair, and in total they act like one capacitor across the winding, a few picofarads for a small chip inductor. The core-loss term comes from hysteresis and eddy currents in the magnetic material, which dissipate power that depends on frequency and flux swing.

What that gives you is a self-resonant frequency, one over two pi root L times Cw. Up to there it behaves like an inductor, the apparent inductance rising a bit near resonance, at the SRF its impedance peaks, and above it it's a capacitor. For example, 10 microhenries with 5 picofarads resonates near 22 megahertz. And besides these, a real inductor saturates, so the inductance drops at high current, which isn't one of the lumped elements."
:::

::: technical
**Model:** $(R_s + sL)\,\|\,R_p$ (core loss) in parallel with $C_w$:
$$Y(s)=sC_w+\frac{1}{R_s+\dfrac{sLR_p}{R_p+sL}},\qquad Z=\frac{1}{Y}.$$
Neglecting $R_p$:
$$Z\approx\frac{R_s+j\omega L}{1-\omega^2LC_w+j\omega R_sC_w},\qquad f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC_w}}\ (R_s\text{ small}).$$

**Quality factor** (below SRF): $Q=\omega L/R_s$ (series), reduced by the core loss and by the parallel capacitance: $Q_{eff}\approx Q\,(1-(f/f_0)^2)$.

**Effective inductance:** $L_{eff}(f)=\dfrac{L}{1-(f/f_0)^2}$ for $f<f_0$; so a part measured at 100 kHz may be quoted as 10 µH but looks like 13 µH at $0.5f_0$ and infinite at $f_0$.

**Components:**
- $R_s$: DCR $=\rho\,\ell_{wire}/A_{wire}$; example 30 turns of 0.5 mm wire at 20 mm per turn: $1.68\times10^{-8}\cdot0.6/(1.96\times10^{-7})=51$ mΩ. ACR = DCR·$F_R(f)$ ([[q0051]]).
- $C_w$: 1–10 pF (chip), 5–100 pF (power inductors), larger for multilayer/many-turn windings.
- $R_p$ or series $R_{core}$: from core loss density $P_v$ (Steinmetz) at the operating flux swing ([[q0050]]).

**Non-ideal:** $L(I)$ falls above $I_{sat}$; $L(T)$ drifts by a few %; DCR rises 0.39 %/K with copper temperature.
:::

A 10 microhenry coil with 5 picofarads resonates near 22 MHz. It also saturates, so L drops at high current."

::: long
"If I had to draw a realistic inductor, I'd start with the ideal inductance L. In series with it goes a resistance, which is the winding: that's the copper's DC resistance, rho times length over area, and at higher frequencies the effective resistance goes up from skin and proximity effects. Across the whole thing goes a capacitor, the winding capacitance, which is the sum of capacitances between turns and layers, with insulation as the dielectric. And in parallel with L, or sometimes folded into the series resistor, goes a loss term for the core: hysteresis and eddy currents turn some of the magnetic energy into heat.

What does that do? The inductor and its winding capacitance form a parallel resonance at one over two pi root L Cw. Below that frequency the part acts like an inductor, though its apparent inductance increases as you approach resonance; at the self-resonant frequency, the impedance peaks, limited by the losses; and above it, the part is a capacitor. 10 microhenries and 5 picofarads gives about 22 megahertz. So if I want to block 100 megahertz noise with a 10 microhenry choke, I'd actually get a few hundred ohms at best, not several kilohms.

The resistance matters for efficiency and heating: a buck inductor with 20 milliohms carrying 3 amps burns 0.18 watts. The quality factor, omega L over R, tells how good the inductor is, which matters for filters and RF.

And outside of the lumped elements, saturation: the core can only hold so much flux, so above a certain current, called Isat, the inductance falls, sometimes sharply. I'd say that's the most important non-ideality for power inductors, and it's why the datasheet gives both Isat and Irms."
:::

::: terms
- **DCR / ACR**: DC and AC series resistance of the winding.
- **Winding capacitance $C_w$**: parallel capacitance from turn-to-turn and layer-to-layer coupling.
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_w})$; the parallel resonance.
- **Quality factor $Q$**: $\omega L/R$, ratio of stored to lost energy per cycle (×2π).
- **Core loss**: hysteresis plus eddy-current loss in the magnetic material.
- **$I_{sat}$**: current at which inductance drops by a stated amount (e.g. 20–30 %).
- **$I_{rms}$ (heating current)**: current that causes a specified temperature rise.
- **Apparent (effective) inductance**: $L_{eff}$ measured near the SRF.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "L with a series resistance and a parallel capacitor".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws the correct topology and names the optional core-loss resistor, with the physical origin of each element.
- Computes DCR from wire geometry and an SRF with numbers.
- Describes the impedance curve (rising, peak at SRF, capacitive after) and the effective-inductance increase near SRF.
- Distinguishes DCR from ACR and mentions skin/proximity and core loss.
- Mentions saturation and temperature as non-lumped effects and ties to datasheet specs ($I_{sat}$ vs $I_{rms}$).
- Gives the design implication: choose the part by DCR/Q for efficiency, SRF above the highest frequency of interest, $I_{sat}$ above the peak current.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the capacitance in series.** Winding capacitance is in parallel with the winding; the series elements are $R_s$ and (for a capacitor) ESL.
- **Believing the inductance is constant to the SRF.** The apparent inductance increases as $1/(1-(f/f_0)^2)$ near resonance.
- **Using DCR for AC loss.** At switching frequencies the AC resistance and core loss can be much larger than the DC value.
- **Ignoring saturation** because it isn't in the lumped model: the part can lose half its inductance at the peak current.
- **Specifying only inductance and DCR** when the SRF or $I_{sat}$ is the failing limit.
- **Assuming a high-Q inductor is always better.** For damping an EMI filter or a switching node you may want loss.
:::

::: deep
## Where each element comes from

An inductor is a conductor wound to concentrate magnetic flux, usually around a magnetic core to raise the permeability and the inductance per turn ([[q0048]]).

1. **Series resistance $R_s$.** The wire has $R=\rho\ell/A$, set by its length and cross-section. For a 30-turn winding with 0.5 mm wire at 20 mm of wire per turn, $R_{dc}=51$ mΩ. Large power inductors use thick wire or flat foil to push DCR down to milliohms. At high frequency, skin and proximity effects crowd the current ([[q0051]]), so $R_{ac}>R_{dc}$.
2. **Winding capacitance $C_w$.** Neighboring turns sit at different potentials separated by wire insulation (a few µm of enamel), forming small capacitors; across the whole winding they appear as one capacitance of a few picofarads (single-layer solenoid) to tens of pF (multilayer). Bobbin geometry, number of layers and core proximity matter. Winding the layers so adjacent turns have a small voltage difference (progressive winding, "bank winding", sectioned bobbins) reduces $C_w$.
3. **Core loss.** Magnetic material dissipates energy as the flux alternates: hysteresis (area of the B–H loop per cycle) and eddy currents (induced circulating currents in a conductive core). Modeled as a parallel resistor $R_p$ (or series $R_{core}$) that depends on frequency and flux amplitude.

## What the model predicts

For $L=10\ \mu$H, $C_w=5$ pF: $f_0=22.5$ MHz. Below $f_0$, the effective inductance is
$$L_{eff}=\frac{L}{1-(f/f_0)^2},$$
which is only +10 % at $0.3f_0$ but +33 % at $0.5f_0$ and +178 % at $0.8f_0$.

**Loss and Q.** DC loss is $I_{dc}^2R_{dc}$: for 3 A and 20 mΩ, 0.18 W. In a converter the ripple current adds AC loss $I_{ac,rms}^2R_{ac}$ and the core loss $P_v\cdot V_{core}$. $Q=\omega L/R$: for the same part at 1 MHz, $Q=2\pi\cdot10^6\cdot10^{-5}/0.02=3100$ if the resistance stayed at DCR, but real parts have an AC resistance of several times DCR plus core loss, so the measured $Q$ at 1 MHz is typically tens to a few hundred for power inductors and higher for air-core RF coils.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Typical Q values and capacitance values depend strongly on construction (shielded vs unshielded, ferrite vs powdered iron, multilayer vs wirewound). The numbers here are order-of-magnitude; check vendor impedance and Q-vs-frequency curves.

## Beyond the lumped model

- **Saturation.** The model is for small signals in the linear region. At large DC bias the core's permeability falls, so $L=L_0\cdot f(I)$; see [[q0046]] and the simulation below.
- **Temperature.** DCR rises 0.39 %/K; ferrite permeability changes with temperature and the Curie point.
- **Coupling.** An unshielded inductor couples magnetic field to neighbors and the board; shielded types contain the field. Mutual inductance between inductors near each other changes behavior.
- **Gap and fringing.** Air gaps reduce permeability and lower inductance but improve saturation; fringing flux causes eddy loss in nearby copper.

## Worked example: extracting the model from an impedance measurement

A practical skill is turning a measured curve into the three or four numbers of the model. Suppose an impedance analyzer gives these readings for an unmarked 10 µH-class power inductor (illustrative data, built to be self-consistent):

- $|Z|=6.28\ \Omega$ at 100 kHz with phase near +90°.
- A sharp impedance peak at $f_0=18$ MHz where the phase crosses zero, with $|Z_{pk}|=3.2$ kΩ.
- A four-wire DC resistance of 45 mΩ.

**Step 1: inductance from the low-frequency slope.** Well below SRF, $L\approx|Z|/(2\pi f)=6.28/(2\pi\times10^5)=10.0\ \mu$H. Use a frequency where the phase is close to 90°, so DCR does not contaminate the reading (at 100 kHz, $X_L$ is 140 times the DCR).

**Step 2: winding capacitance from the SRF.** Rearrange $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_w})$:

$$C_w=\frac{1}{(2\pi f_0)^2L}=\frac{1}{(2\pi\times18\times10^6)^2\times10^{-5}}=7.8\ \text{pF}.$$

**Step 3: what limits the peak.** For a parallel resonance with series loss $R$, the peak impedance is $L/(C_wR)$. Solving for $R$ gives an *effective* series resistance of 400 Ω at 18 MHz, which cannot be copper loss: the 45 mΩ DCR (even with skin effect multiplying it by ten) would give a peak around a thousand times higher. The peak height, 3.2 kΩ, is set by loss that grows steeply with frequency: core loss, dielectric loss in the winding and the proximity effect. The quality factor at the peak is $|Z_{pk}|/(\omega L)=3200/1131=2.8$, whereas DCR alone would predict $\omega L/R_{dc}=1131/0.045\approx25{,}000$. The takeaway is that the copper resistance in the model describes the part only at low frequency, and a single fixed $R_s$ is a poor model around SRF. A more faithful model adds the parallel core-loss element $R_p$ from the circuit above, or frequency-dependent $R(f)$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The numbers above are an illustrative, self-consistent data set, not a measurement of a real part. The qualitative result (peak impedance of a few kΩ to tens of kΩ and a Q of a few at SRF for a power inductor) is typical, but the exact values depend on core material, winding style and shielding.

## Worked example: a bench method for $C_w$ that needs no analyzer

Without an impedance analyzer you can still find $C_w$ from the SRF twice. Measure $f_0$ with a gentle probe coupling (a sweeping generator and a scope looking for the amplitude peak, or a grid-dip approach), then add a known capacitor $C_x$ across the winding and measure the new resonance $f_1$. Since $f\propto1/\sqrt{C_w+C_x}$,

$$\left(\frac{f_0}{f_1}\right)^2=\frac{C_w+C_x}{C_w}\ \Rightarrow\ C_w=\frac{C_x}{(f_0/f_1)^2-1}.$$

For the part above with $C_x=10$ pF, $f_1=18\sqrt{7.8/17.8}=11.9$ MHz. Plugging back, $C_w=10/((18/11.9)^2-1)=7.8$ pF, consistent. The method does not require knowing $L$, which is useful for components where you only trust the measurement near SRF. Use a small, low-inductance capacitor and the shortest possible leads, because the extra lead length adds inductance that affects the second reading, and remember that the probe's own capacitance (often about 10 pF for a passive scope probe) acts like an extra $C_x$ unless you measure it out with loose coupling.

## Worked example: where the losses actually go in a converter

Return to the loss paragraph. A 10 µH, 20 mΩ inductor carries 3 A DC in a buck converter. The DC loss is $3^2\times0.02=0.18$ W. Now add the ripple. For a triangular ripple of peak-to-peak $\Delta I$, the AC RMS current is $\Delta I/(2\sqrt3)$. Assume (as an illustration) that the AC resistance at the switching frequency is three times DCR, 60 mΩ:

| Ripple $\Delta I$ | $I_{ac,rms}$ | AC copper loss | DC copper loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 A p-p | 0.289 A | 5 mW | 180 mW |
| 3 A p-p | 0.866 A | 45 mW | 180 mW |

With 1 A of ripple the AC copper loss is nearly negligible and the DC term dominates. Tripling the ripple multiplies the AC loss by nine, to 45 mW, but the core loss (which depends on the flux swing, and so on the ripple, with a power-law exponent) typically grows faster. The design takeaway is a trade-off: a smaller inductance gives smaller, cheaper parts, more ripple, and more AC and core loss; a larger inductance lowers the ripple but raises DCR and size. Temperature also feeds back: copper resistance rises 0.39 % per kelvin, so a part that heats from 25 to 100 °C goes from 20 mΩ to $20\times(1+0.0039\times75)=25.9$ mΩ and its DC loss from 180 mW to 233 mW. A thermal resistance of 40 K/W (illustrative) turns the original 0.18 W into a 7 K rise, so the iteration converges quickly for this part, but for a small inductor near its thermal limit it could run away.

## Limiting cases

- **$C_w\to0$.** The SRF goes to infinity and the model collapses to $R_s+j\omega L$. An air-core coil with a single layer is the nearest real example.
- **$R_s\to0$.** The Q goes to infinity below SRF and the peak at SRF goes to infinity as well: an ideal LC tank.
- **Very high L.** More turns means more $C_w$ and more DCR, so both SRF and Q-limited bandwidth fall. This is why a high-value inductor "stops being an inductor" at a much lower frequency than a small one.

## Verifying on the bench

1. **DCR.** Measure with a four-wire ohmmeter, and measure the temperature of the part since it shifts at 0.39 %/K.
2. **Inductance versus frequency.** Sweep from low frequency up on an LCR meter or analyzer, with the signal level specified in the datasheet. The curve should be flat at low frequency and rise toward the SRF, following $L/(1-(f/f_0)^2)$.
3. **Inductance versus DC bias.** Use a bias fixture that blocks the DC current from the instrument. The inductance should fall around the part's saturation rating ([[q0046]]).
4. **Compare with the model.** Overlay the measured $|Z|$ on the lumped model. If the shape matches up to about $0.5f_0$ but misses the peak, you have found the limit of the simple model; add $R_p$ or use the vendor's S-parameter model.

## Using the model in design

1. **EMI chokes:** choose SRF well above the noise frequency. If not, parallel a smaller one, or use a ferrite bead, designed to be lossy over a broad band.
2. **Converter inductors:** select by $L$, $I_{sat}$ (> peak current with margin), $I_{rms}$ (heating), DCR (efficiency), SRF (≫ switching frequency harmonics).
3. **RF inductors:** high-Q air-core or ceramic-core parts; use the vendor's Q and SRF curves and S-parameters.
4. **Simulation:** include $R_s$, $C_w$ and (for accuracy) $R_p$; use vendor SPICE models, which include saturation and frequency effects.
:::

::: applications
- **Buck/boost converter inductors**: DCR and saturation current set efficiency and robustness.
- **EMI common-mode and differential chokes**: SRF and impedance at the noise frequency determine attenuation.
- **RF chokes and matching inductors**: Q and SRF determine insertion loss and tuning.
- **LC filters on audio and power lines**: inductor loss and SRF limit the stopband.
- **Wireless-power and NFC coils**: Q is crucial.
- **Ferrite beads**: deliberately lossy inductors modelled as L, R and C.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Small-signal, linear model** below saturation, with constant element values.
- The topology is the common one (series $R_s$, parallel $C_w$, parallel core loss); other models exist (series core-loss resistor with frequency-dependent elements, multi-pole ladder models) that fit broadband data better.
- Parameter values are typical and depend on core material, winding style, shielding and temperature.
- At very high frequencies (hundreds of MHz+), multiple resonances appear and a single $C_w$ is inadequate.
- If asked to draw "a realistic model" quickly, $R_s$, $L$ and $C_w$ is sufficient; mention core loss and saturation verbally.
:::

::: followups
### What is the SRF of a 4.7 µH inductor with 3 pF winding capacitance?
$f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{4.7\times10^{-6}\cdot3\times10^{-12}})=42.4$ MHz. Use it comfortably below about a third of that (14 MHz) to keep $L_{eff}$ within ~12 % of nominal.

### How does winding capacitance depend on construction?
More layers and tighter coupling between turns raise $C_w$; a single-layer solenoid has the lowest; bank or pie-section winding reduces it by limiting the voltage between adjacent turns; ferrite cores with high permittivity can add capacitance. A larger-value inductor needs more turns, so $C_w$ grows and SRF falls faster than $1/\sqrt{L}$ alone suggests.

### What does Q tell you and how is it limited?
$Q=\omega L/R_{total}$. At low frequency it's limited by the winding DCR; at higher frequency by ACR (skin/proximity) and core loss; near the SRF it collapses because the parallel capacitance diverts current. A Q-vs-frequency curve has a hump, peaking somewhere below the SRF.

### What if an inductor is specified as "10 µH at 100 kHz, 0.1 V"?
The datasheet measures with a small AC signal and no DC bias. In circuit, DC bias can reduce L (saturation), the frequency may differ (L varies with $f$), and the temperature may be high. Use the vendor's L vs. DC-current and L vs. frequency graphs to find the actual value.

### How would you measure the DCR, inductance and SRF of an unknown inductor?
DCR with a four-wire (Kelvin) resistance measurement, since it can be milliohms. Inductance with an LCR meter at a specified frequency and drive level (and, for power parts, with DC bias applied through a bias-tee fixture). The SRF and Q with an impedance analyzer or a VNA, looking for the |Z| peak where the phase crosses zero. Keep fixture leads short, or de-embed them, because stray capacitance in the fixture shifts the SRF.
