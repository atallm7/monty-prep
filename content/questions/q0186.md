::: basics
### In plain words
The switch node is a square wave, but the load wants a steady voltage and current. The inductor and capacitor turn one into the other. The inductor keeps the current smooth by storing and returning energy, and the capacitor keeps the voltage smooth by soaking up what is left of the current's wobble.

### Picture it
Imagine pumping water in jerky strokes. A heavy flywheel on the pump keeps the flow going between strokes, and that is the inductor. A water tower at the end evens out the pressure that the houses see, and that is the capacitor. Together they turn jerky pumping into a steady supply, with almost no energy wasted.

### Words you need
- **Inductor**: a coil that stores energy in a magnetic field and resists changes in current ([[q0041]]).
- **Capacitor**: a part that stores charge and resists changes in voltage ([[q0025]]).
- **Ripple**: the small leftover wobble after filtering.
- **Low-pass filter**: a circuit that passes slow changes (the average) and blocks fast ones.
- **Switch node**: the pulsing point that feeds the filter.

### The simplest example
Take a 4.7 µH inductor and a 22 µF capacitor. Together they form a filter that starts to cut signals above about 16 kHz. The switching runs at 500 kHz, about 32 times higher. A two-part filter like this cuts a signal that far above its corner by roughly $32^2 \approx 1000$ times. So only the average of the pulses gets through. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page explains each part's job in more detail and why a plain resistor would not work.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand *why* a buck needs both parts and what job each does, rather than "they filter the output". The hidden sub-skills are:

1. The inductor is an **energy store and current source**: it turns a chopped voltage into a smooth current without dissipating power.
2. The capacitor is a **voltage stabiliser**: it absorbs the current ripple and supplies load transients.
3. Together they form an LC low-pass whose corner is far below the switching frequency.
4. You can say why a resistor in their place would not work (loss).
:::

::: why
Every sizing, ripple, efficiency and stability question about a buck comes back to these two components. The inductor sets ripple current, transient slew rate and the CCM/DCM boundary. The capacitor sets output ripple and load-step droop, and together they set the double pole that the control loop must cope with. Interviewers use this question to see whether you can explain a power stage in terms of energy flow rather than only memorized formulas.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor law** $v = L\,di/dt$: an inductor resists changes in current, so a pulsed voltage produces a slowly varying current. See [[q0041]] and [[q0043]].
- **Capacitor law** $i = C\,dv/dt$: a capacitor resists changes in voltage; it soaks up current ripple. See [[q0025]].
- **Energy storage**: $E_L = \tfrac12 L I^2$ and $E_C=\tfrac12 CV^2$ are stored and returned, not burned.
- **LC low-pass filter**: corner $f_c = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ with $-40$ dB/decade rolloff. See [[q0010]].
- **Averaging**: the filter passes the DC average of the switch node, $D V_{in}$.
:::

::: answer
"The switch node is a 12 V square wave, but the load wants a clean 3.3 V and a steady current. The inductor and capacitor are what turn one into the other.

The inductor does the energy work. Its current can't jump, so during the on time it absorbs energy from the input and ramps its current up, and during the off time it releases that energy to the output. Because it's storing and returning energy instead of dropping it across a resistor, the conversion is nearly lossless. Effectively it acts like a current source feeding the output, with a bit of triangular ripple. A resistor in series would do the averaging too, but it would burn the whole voltage difference as heat, which is the LDO problem.

The capacitor does the voltage work. The inductor current has ripple, and the load might need a perfectly steady current, so the capacitor absorbs the difference, charging when the inductor current is above the load and discharging when it's below. That leaves millivolts of ripple. It also acts as a local reservoir for load steps while the control loop catches up.

Together they form a second-order low-pass. With 4.7 µH and 22 µF the corner is about 16 kHz, so a 500 kHz switching signal is attenuated by about 60 dB and only the DC average, $D V_{in}$, gets through."
:::

::: technical
**Inductor.** Steady state requires zero average inductor voltage (volt-second balance), giving $V_{out}=D V_{in}$. The inductor delivers current $I_L$ to the output node with ripple $\Delta I_L = \frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{L f_{sw}}$. It stores $\tfrac12 L I^2$; per cycle the energy exchanged is $L\,I_{out}\,\Delta I_L$, equal to $V_{out} I_{out}(1-D)T$ delivered in the off time.

**Capacitor.** Charge balance: the average capacitor current is zero, so the average inductor current equals $I_{out}$. The ripple current $i_C = i_L - I_{out}$ gives
$$\Delta V_{C} = \frac{\Delta I_L}{8 f_{sw} C},\qquad \Delta V_{ESR} = \Delta I_L\,R_{ESR}.$$

**LC filter.** From the switch node to $V_{out}$ the transfer function is $\frac{1}{1+s L/R + s^2LC}$ (with load $R$), a double pole at $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$. For $L = 4.7$ µH and $C=22$ µF: $f_0 = 15.7$ kHz. At $f_{sw}=500$ kHz the attenuation is $(f_0/f_{sw})^2 \approx 9.8\times10^{-4}$ (−60 dB). The fundamental of the 0.275-duty switch-node pulse train has amplitude $\frac{2 V_{in}\sin(\pi D)}{\pi} = 5.8$ V, which becomes 5.7 mV, i.e. about 11.4 mV p-p. That matches the 11.6 mV from the triangle-integral formula.

**Why not R or a bare capacitor?** A series $R$ drops $V_{in}-V_{out}$ at $I_{out}$, so efficiency equals $V_{out}/V_{in}$. A capacitor alone directly connected to the switch would draw a huge spike every edge (limited only by parasitics). The inductor is what limits the current slew to $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$.

**Transient role.** For a load step $\Delta I$, the inductor slews at $\le V/L$ (about 1.85 A/µs rising or 0.70 A/µs falling here), so the capacitor supplies or absorbs the difference during the response time.
:::

::: short
"The inductor stores energy and releases it, so the chopped 12 V pulse becomes a smooth current without burning power; it's the lossless alternative to a series resistor. The capacitor absorbs the inductor's current ripple and holds the output voltage steady, and it supplies load steps until the inductor catches up. Together they're a second-order low-pass with a corner well below the switching frequency, here 16 kHz against 500 kHz, so only the DC average, $D V_{in}$, gets through."
:::

::: long
"The input to the filter is the switch node, a 12 V rectangular wave. The load wants a flat 3.3 V. The question is how to average a pulse train without wasting energy.

The inductor is the first half. Its voltage is $L\,di/dt$, so the current can only ramp. While the high-side FET is on, the inductor sees 8.7 V and its current ramps up, storing energy. When the FET turns off, the inductor sees minus 3.3 V and the current ramps down while it feeds the output. It never burns energy as heat in the ideal case; it just moves energy from the input to the output in packets. Because of that, the average of the switch node, 3.3 V, appears at the output but the input current is only a fraction of the output current, and efficiency can be above 90 percent.

The inductor alone leaves a triangular current, about 1 A peak to peak in my example. That's where the capacitor comes in. At the output node the inductor current splits into the load and the capacitor. The load takes the 2 A average and the capacitor takes the ripple: it charges while the inductor current is above 2 A and discharges when it's below. The result is a few to a dozen millivolts of ripple, set by the ripple current, the switching frequency and the capacitance, plus the ESR.

You can also see it as a double-pole LC filter. With 4.7 µH and 22 µF, the corner is about 16 kHz; at 500 kHz that's around minus 60 dB, so the switching fundamental drops from nearly 6 V to a few millivolts.

Finally, the capacitor is the buffer for load transients. The inductor can only change its current at a limited rate, so for a sudden load step the capacitor covers the difference until the loop responds."
:::

::: terms
- **Energy storage**: the inductor stores $\tfrac12LI^2$ in its magnetic field and returns it later.
- **Current ripple $\Delta I_L$**: the peak-to-peak triangular variation of the inductor current.
- **Output ripple $\Delta V_{out}$**: the small AC voltage on the output, from capacitor charging and ESR.
- **LC corner frequency**: $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, the double pole of the filter.
- **ESR**: equivalent series resistance of the capacitor; adds a ripple term $\Delta I\cdot ESR$.
- **Volt-second balance**: steady-state condition that sets $V_{out}=DV_{in}$.
- **Charge balance**: the average capacitor current is zero in steady state.
- **Load transient**: sudden change in load current that the capacitor must cover.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "They filter the output ripple."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates the two jobs: the inductor transfers energy and sets the *current* ripple; the capacitor sets the *voltage* ripple and handles transients.
- Explains *why* a resistor would not work (loss) and why the capacitor alone would not work (no current limit).
- Quotes the LC corner with real values and the resulting attenuation at $f_{sw}$.
- Mentions that bigger $L$ lowers ripple current but slows transient response and costs size/DCR, and that bigger $C$ lowers voltage ripple and droop but raises cost and affects loop dynamics.
- Notes that ESR often dominates ripple in non-ceramic capacitors.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The inductor filters voltage."** It smooths *current*; the capacitor smooths voltage. In the LC filter they work together, but their roles differ.
- **Treating the inductor as a resistor.** Its average voltage is zero; it dissipates (ideally) nothing, which is why the buck is efficient.
- **Saying the capacitor "stores the output voltage" and supplies the load all the time.** In steady state the capacitor supplies only the ripple component; the inductor supplies the load's average.
- **Treating the LC corner as the whole story.** The $f_0$ estimate works here because the ripple is dominated by the switching fundamental, but with ESR the capacitor stops attenuating at high frequency and the ripple has an ESR-set floor.
- **Ignoring ESR.** With electrolytics or polymer capacitors the ESR term can exceed the capacitive term.
- **Ignoring the transient role.** An inductor limits slew rate; a larger inductor makes the converter slower to respond.
:::

::: deep
## The problem: average a pulse train without losing energy

The switch node alternates between $V_{in}$ and about 0 V with duty cycle $D$. Its DC average is $D V_{in}$, which is what we want. But the instantaneous voltage is a 12 V pulse, and the load needs a steady 3.3 V. The standard ways to average something are:

1. **A resistor** (and a capacitor): works, but the series resistor drops $(V_{in}-V_{out})$ at the full load current, so efficiency $=V_{out}/V_{in}=27.5\%$. That is just an LDO.
2. **A capacitor only**: the capacitor would charge to $V_{in}$ through a very low resistance every cycle, drawing a current spike limited only by FET resistance. With no inductor to hold a voltage difference, the capacitor simply charges to about $V_{in}$ on every on-pulse and the output is not $D V_{in}$ at all: there is no conversion, only an unregulated rail plus huge current spikes.
3. **An inductor then a capacitor**: the inductor forces the current to ramp rather than spike, and has no resistance to dissipate heat. This is the buck's LC filter.

> [!TIP]
> The key sentence: "the inductor lets the *current* be smooth while the *voltage* across it jumps; the resistor would let the voltage be smooth but burn power."

When the high-side FET is on, the inductor sees $V_{in}-V_{out}$ and the current ramps up. When it is off, the voltage across it reverses and the current ramps down. Voltage across the inductor is $L\,di/dt$, so with a larger $L$ the slope shrinks and the output current is nearly constant; this is the sense in which the inductor acts as a current source.

Energy view. In the on time the inductor absorbs energy, and in the off time it hands it to the load:
$$E_{cycle}= L\,I_{out}\,\Delta I_L = V_{out} I_{out}(1-D)T.$$
For the example: $4.7\ \mu H\times 2\ A\times 1.018\ A = 9.57\ \mu J$, and $3.3\times2\times1.45\ \mu s = 9.57\ \mu J$. Same number because the inductor is just a buffer between input and output.

## The capacitor as the voltage stabiliser

The inductor current has a triangular ripple, but the load does not want ripple (a processor core wants steady current and steady voltage). Charge balance forces the capacitor current to average zero, so:

- When $i_L > I_{out}$ the capacitor takes the extra current and its voltage rises.
- When $i_L < I_{out}$ the load takes current from the capacitor and the voltage falls.

The ripple voltage is the integral of the triangular current divided by $C$, giving $\Delta V_C = \frac{\Delta I_L}{8 f C}$ = 11.6 mV, plus $\Delta I_L R_{ESR}$ = 5.1 mV. The combined p-p is about 12.3 mV, since the two terms are 90° out of phase. See [[q0189]] and [[q0194]].

## The same thing as a low-pass filter

Seen from the switch node, $L$ and $C$ form a second-order low-pass, with the load providing damping:

$$\frac{V_{out}}{V_{SW}}(s) = \frac{1}{1 + s\,L/R + s^2 LC}.$$

The peak near $f_0$ is limited by the load resistance here; it is the resonance the control loop has to manage ([[q0185]] shows what the filter output looks like in the time domain).

Check the numbers by treating the switch node as a pulse train. The fundamental has amplitude $2V_{in}\sin(\pi D)/\pi=5.8$ V. After attenuation by $(f_0/f_{sw})^2 = 9.8\times10^{-4}$ it is 5.7 mV, 11.4 mV p-p. The time-domain triangle calculation gave 11.6 mV. Two different methods agree.

## What each component buys you in design

| If you increase... | Ripple current | Output ripple | Transient | Cost/size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $L$ | down ($\propto 1/L$) | down (indirectly) | slower slew ($V/L$) | bigger, higher DCR |
| $C$ | no change | down ($\propto 1/C$) | less droop | bigger/costlier |
| $f_{sw}$ | down | down | faster response | more switching loss |

## Real hardware

- **Inductor**: DCR sets conduction loss; saturation current must exceed the peak $I_{out}+\Delta I/2$ with margin (2.51 A here); core loss rises with ripple.
- **Capacitor**: ceramic X5R/X7R lose capacitance with DC bias (often 30 to 60% at rated voltage in small packages), so 22 µF nominal may be 12 µF effective. The ESR and ESL set high-frequency ripple spikes at the edges.
- **Transient**: for a load step $\Delta I$ before the loop reacts, $\Delta V\approx \Delta I\cdot ESR + \frac{\Delta I\,t}{C}$.
:::

::: applications
- **Processor core supplies**: low $V_{out}$, large capacitor banks, small inductors for fast transients.
- **Battery-powered devices** where lossless conversion is needed.
- **Point-of-load regulators** (12 V to 1.8 V, 3.3 V) on motherboards and FPGA boards.
- **LED drivers**: the inductor makes the buck a current source.
- **Notebook and USB-PD charging stages**.
- **Class-D amplifier output filters** use the same LC averaging principle on a PWM signal.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM, steady state**, small ripple relative to the DC output.
- **Constant load current** and ideal switch node; real nodes ring.
- **Ideal $L$ and $C$**; in practice DCR, core loss, ESR, ESL and bias-dependent capacitance matter.
- **Voltage-mode** or **current-mode** control only change how $D$ is found, not the roles of $L$ and $C$.
- **Other topologies** (boost, flyback) also use an inductor as an energy store, but the output capacitor then supplies the load while the inductor charges, so the output current is pulsed, unlike in the buck.
:::

::: followups
### Why not use a resistor and capacitor to filter the switch node?
You would drop $(V_{in}-V_{out})$ across the resistor at the full load current. At 2 A that is 17.4 W lost for 6.6 W delivered. The inductor stores energy instead of dissipating it, so the filter is ideally lossless.

### What happens to ripple if you double $L$? If you double $C$?
Doubling $L$ halves $\Delta I_L$ and (for the capacitive part) halves the output ripple too, since $\Delta V_C\propto\Delta I_L/C$. Doubling $C$ leaves $\Delta I_L$ unchanged and halves the capacitive output ripple, but not the ESR term, which is $\Delta I\cdot ESR$.

### What limits how big you can make the inductor?
Size, DCR (copper loss $I^2 R_{DCR}$), saturation current rating (bigger $L$ for a given size means lower $I_{sat}$) and transient response: the slew rate $V/L$ falls, so a load step takes longer to be supported by the inductor.

### Does the capacitor ever supply the full load?
During a load transient, yes, for a short while, until the inductor current catches up. In steady state, only the ripple. A 1 A load step with a 22 µF capacitor droops at 45 mV/µs while the inductor catches up at 1.85 A/µs (rising), so the inductor needs about 0.5 µs to catch up and the droop is on the order of 10 to 25 mV if the loop reacts quickly.

### What if the output capacitor were removed?
The ripple current would flow into the load, and the output voltage would be $i_L\cdot R_{load}$: a ripple of $\Delta I_L R=1.02\times1.65 = 1.7$ V p-p in the example, with possible ringing from the inductor and the load's own capacitance. For a constant-current load it would be worse, and the loop would likely be unstable.
:::

::: rubric
- Inductor: stores/releases energy, current cannot jump, outputs smooth current :: This is the core job and the reason the buck can be efficient.
- Capacitor: absorbs ripple current, holds voltage, supplies load transients :: The capacitor's role is distinct from the inductor's.
- LC low-pass with $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ far below $f_{sw}$ :: Shows you can quantify the filtering.
- Why not a resistor: dissipates $(V_{in}-V_{out})I$, efficiency $=V_{out}/V_{in}$ :: Contrasts buck and LDO behaviour.
- Ripple dependence ($\Delta I\propto 1/L$, $\Delta V\propto 1/C$ plus ESR) :: Connects the roles to design.
- Mentions transient slew limit $V/L$ and capacitor reserve :: Shows system-level thinking.
