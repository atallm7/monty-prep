::: basics
### In plain words
Test points come in a few common styles, and the right one depends on who will measure and how fast the signal is. The main styles are a flat surface pad, a through-hole loop or post, an exposed via, a header or connector, and a coaxial connector for radio signals. Each trades space, ease of use and effect on the signal.

### Picture it
Think of the ways to grab something: a flat tab you tap with a tool, a hook you can clip onto, a hole you slip a probe into, or a proper plug. A factory machine wants a flat tab it can land on from below. A bench engineer wants a hook for the scope clip. A radio signal wants a plug that does not disturb it. The analogy has one limit: here every choice also loads the signal a little.

### Words you need
- **SMD pad**: a flat copper pad on the surface, about 1 mm across, with the solder mask opened.
- **Through-hole loop or post**: a wire loop or pin soldered into a hole, easy to clip onto.
- **Via test point**: an exposed via used as a pad, saving space.
- **Header**: a row of pins for programming or debug.
- **Bed-of-nails fixture**: factory spring pins that land on pads. See [[q0612]].

### The simplest example
One board has four needs. A factory fixture needs flat pads about 1 mm wide, spaced at least 1.27 mm apart. The power rail gets a through-hole loop, so an engineer can clip a scope on it. A crowded corner uses an exposed via, which must not be covered or filled. A fast radio signal gets a tiny pad or a coaxial connector. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page compares the styles in detail and shows how to pick by who is measuring and what the net carries.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know the **menu of physical test-point styles** and, more importantly, can match each to a user (hand probe, scope clip, fixture pin, RF cable) and a cost. Interviewers listen for categories such as SMD pads, through-hole loops/posts, vias, connectors/headers, RF connectors and component pins, and for the trade-offs: area, hand-probing ease, fixture compatibility, signal integrity and cost.
:::

::: why
Choosing the wrong style wastes area or leaves you unable to measure. A bare SMD pad is perfect for a factory fixture but awkward for a hand-held clip; a through-hole loop is easy to clip a scope onto but can't be probed by a fixture on the opposite side and adds inductance; an RF connector is great for GHz signals and expensive. Knowing the options lets you pick per net and per audience. See [[q0619]] for what test points are for and [[q0621]] for layout rules.
:::

::: answer
"There are about six styles I'd think about.

First, a plain SMD pad, a round copper pad of about a millimetre with the solder mask opened. It's cheap, tiny and ideal for factory fixtures and spring probes, and you can hand-probe it with a pointed tip.

Second, a through-hole loop or post, the little wire loop or pin soldered into the board. You can clip a scope hook or a mini grabber onto it, so it's the friendliest for bench work, but it takes space on both sides and can't be placed for a fixture under the board.

Third, a via as a test point, an exposed via barrel or pad. It saves space and is handy for fixtures, but it needs to be untented and unplugged, and it's easy to damage, and filled-over vias can't be probed reliably.

Fourth, headers or connectors, like a debug or programming header, pin headers for rails or buses, and castellated or edge pads.

Fifth, RF test connectors like SMA or U.FL, for signals where a pad would ruin the response.

And sixth, probing component leads or pads directly, which isn't really a test point but is the fallback.

I choose by who's measuring: fixture, bench engineer, or field technician, and by how fast the net is."
:::

::: technical
**Common types.**

| Type | Typical form | Strengths | Weaknesses | Typical users |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| SMD pad | round or square copper, about 1 mm, bare finish | smallest; cheap; fixture and flying-probe friendly; low stub | hard to hook a clip onto; easy to lift if abused | factory fixtures, spring probes, hand probes |
| Through-hole loop / post | wire loop or square post in a plated hole, soldered | easy to clip scope hooks, mini grabbers, alligator clips; robust | takes area on both sides; adds lead inductance; component height; extra assembly step | bench debugging, power rails |
| Via test point | exposed via barrel/annular pad (untented, unplugged) | no extra area when vias exist; fixture-reachable | easy to damage; solder or contaminant can fill it; must not be filled/plugged/tented over; probe tip can slip | dense boards, fixtures |
| Pin header / debug connector | 1×N header or 2×N header, or pogo pads | grouped signals; programmers plug in | cost and height; area | programming, debug, rails |
| Edge/castellated pads | half-holes on board edge | module-to-module; probe from edge | needs process support | modules |
| RF test connector | SMA, MMCX, U.FL or switch-coax | controlled impedance, minimal disturbance | cost, area, fragile (U.FL rated for limited mating cycles) | RF and high-speed |
| Component pin / pad | existing resistor or capacitor lead | zero cost | hard to reach; shorting risk | last resort |
| Test clip or hook-on | clips onto an IC leads (SOIC/TSSOP) | no board change | fragile, shorting risk | rework/debug |

**Specialised test-point components.** Keystone-style loops and posts, and various SMD "test point" components exist. These are the SMD or PTH hardware; check availability and footprints in your library.

**Sizing for fixtures.** Typical test pad about 0.9–1.0 mm or larger; pitch ≥ 1.27 mm. See [[q0621]].

**Choosing by net type.**

- **Power rails/ground**: through-hole loop or large pad; several grounds.
- **Digital GPIO and slow buses**: SMD pad or header.
- **Clocks and high-speed**: tiny in-line pad, coax, or no pad ([[q0623]]).
- **Programming/debug**: standard header or pogo pads.
- **RF**: coax connector or footprint for a connector you may depopulate.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Probe tolerances, pad sizes and pitches depend on the fixture vendor and factory. Specific product families (Keystone, etc.) are examples of what exists, not recommendations.

::: long
"I group test points by the contact style, and then pick by who'll use them and how sensitive the net is.

The workhorse is the SMD pad: a round copper pad about a millimetre across, solder mask opened. It's tiny, cheap, adds almost no stub, and a factory spring probe or flying probe can land on it. The downside is it's hard to hook a clip on, so for bench work it's less friendly.

Then through-hole loops and posts: a little wire loop or square pin soldered into a plated hole. These are the best for the bench, since you can hook a scope clip on them and walk away, and they're robust. But they cost area on both sides, add lead inductance and need an assembly step.

Exposed vias can serve as test points in dense boards: no extra area, and fixtures can contact them. But they have to be left untented and unplugged, and a filled or covered via can't be probed.

Headers and connectors cover programming and debug: SWD, JTAG, UART, or rails grouped on a pin header. Pogo-pad patterns do the same without a connector.

For RF and multi-gigabit signals I'd use a coax connector like SMA, MMCX or U.FL, or a footprint for one that I can depopulate in production, because a plain pad would disturb the signal.

And as a last resort, probing a component pin, which costs nothing but is fragile.

So for rails I use loops, for slow signals pads, for programming a header, and for the fastest nets coax or built-in self-test."
:::

::: terms
- **SMD test pad**: surface-mount copper pad with exposed finish used as a probe target.
- **Test loop / post**: through-hole wire loop or pin for clip-on probing.
- **Via test point**: via used as the probe target; must be exposed.
- **Tenting**: covering a via with solder mask; prevents probing.
- **Plugged/filled via**: via barrel filled with epoxy or resin, possibly capped.
- **Pogo pad pattern**: a set of pads for spring-pin programming adapters.
- **Castellation**: half-plated holes on a board edge.
- **SMA/MMCX/U.FL**: RF connectors.
- **Pitch**: center-to-center spacing of probe points.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "pads, loops, vias, headers".

**An exceptional answer**:

:::

::: mistakes
- **Using tented or plugged vias as test points.** They can't be contacted.
- **SMD pads where the bench engineer needs a clip.** Add a loop for power rails.
- **Through-hole loops under a fixture area.** Tall loops block fixture plates and need a clearance zone.
- **Hanging a long stub to a header on a fast net.** The header adds far more than a pad. See [[q0623]].
- **Using OSP finish pads and expecting repeated probing.** Finish and wear matter; ask the factory.
- **Forgetting pin 1 or net label on headers.** Misconnected programmers can damage the board.
:::

## Walking through each style

### SMD test pad

A round pad, about 1 mm, with solder mask opened (mask-defined or non-mask-defined doesn't matter much for probing but follow your fixture vendor's advice), on the side the fixture contacts. It's the default choice for production because it is small and has almost no parasitic stub when attached directly to a trace. Probe tip needs to land on it; hand probing with a pointed tip works well, clips don't.

### Through-hole loop or post

Used for rails and ground because clip-ons are quick and robust. Inductance of a 5 mm lead is roughly 5 nH (1 nH/mm rule), so for DC rails it's irrelevant, but for fast edges it shapes the loop. Components of this type are tall: leave a keep-out for fixtures. They need an assembly step (hand solder or selective/wave) unless reflowed as SMD-compatible loops.

### Via as test point

A via has a pad on top and bottom. If both are exposed (no mask, no plug), it can serve as a test point. Benefits: no new copper, can appear under a part. Risks: tenting or plug/fill options in the fab notes cover it; probe tips can slip down the hole; and contamination. Choose fixtures with crown-tip probes if they'll contact vias (check with the factory).

### Headers and pogo patterns

A 2×5 1.27 mm header is a standard SWD/JTAG connector. Where space is limited, a pad pattern for a spring-pin cable (Tag-Connect style) saves the header; there are also edge fingers and castellated pads.

### RF test connectors

A 50 Ω coax connector gives a controlled-impedance interface. In production, a switch-type connector (the mating plug connects the signal; otherwise it passes through) is a common choice for RF test and keeps the signal path intact when unplugged. A pad here would add reflections.

## Finish and wear

Fixture pins hit the pad hundreds or thousands of times. Common surface finishes: HASL, ENIG (gold over nickel), immersion tin or silver, OSP. ENIG pads are durable and flat; OSP wears and oxidises, and fixture probes can scrape through it, but contact resistance may vary. HASL is uneven in thickness. Ask your assembler about their preferred finish for fixture contacts.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Finish preferences for probing differ by factory and probe tip type. Don't state a rule as universal.

## Mechanical considerations

- **Height**: loops and posts are tall (several mm); plan keep-outs for fixtures and enclosures.
- **Robustness**: pads lift if you solder wires to them repeatedly; through-hole posts survive.
- **Size**: SMD pad about 1 mm; through-hole loop needs a hole of about 1 mm and pad of about 1.8 mm (check parts).
- **Polarity/labelling**: mark pin 1 on headers; number or name each TP.

::: applications
- **Dev boards**: loops on rails, pin headers on GPIO, SMA footprints on RF/ADC inputs.
- **Production boards**: bottom-side SMD pad fields for fixtures and pogo patterns for programming.
- **Power supplies**: loops on VIN/VOUT, Kelvin pads on shunts.
- **Modules** (Wi-Fi, LTE): castellated edge pads that double as test and soldering interfaces.
- **RF transceivers**: switch-type coax connectors for conducted production tests.
:::

::: followups
### Which style would you use for a 3.3 V rail on a prototype?
A through-hole loop or post, with a ground loop or pad next to it, because I'll clip a scope or DMM onto it many times and the lead inductance doesn't matter for a DC rail. On the production version, I'd keep a bottom-side SMD pad for the fixture.

### Can a via be a test point? What do you need to specify?
Yes: it must be untented (mask opened on the pad side that will be probed) and unplugged/unfilled, or filled and capped flat if the fab supports it. Mention it in fab notes. Also check the probe tip type so it doesn't slip into the hole. See also [[q0582]] for vias on pads.

### Why not put a header pin on a 1 GHz clock?
A pin header adds a few pF and nH. At a 300 ps edge a 3 pF load reflects about 25% (using the estimate above) and the stub resonates. Use an in-line pad of tiny size, a coax footprint, or measure with an active probe at a nearby component pad.

### How do test points on the same net differ for fixtures and for hand probing?
Fixtures need a flat pad of about 1 mm, bare, on the fixture side, at fixed pitch from other pads. Hand probing wants something you can hold or clip, like a loop, and spacing so you don't slip to a neighbour. I often provide both on important rails.

### Scenario: you can't afford a through-hole loop on a tiny wearable. How do you provide bench access?
Use bare SMD pads with ground pads next to them, designed for a pogo-pin adapter or a fine-tipped probe, and put a row of them on the back. Add a Tag-Connect-style pattern for programming. Use a micro-grabber on a nearby capacitor for temporary access if needed.
