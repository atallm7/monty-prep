::: testing
Whether you can list the **properties that define a sensor's quality** and group them sensibly instead of rattling off terms: static accuracy terms (offset, gain, linearity, hysteresis, resolution), noise and repeatability, dynamic terms (bandwidth, response time, latency), environmental terms (temperature, supply, humidity, vibration), stability over time, and system-level behavior (fault detection, startup, interfaces). The hidden skill is prioritizing: what matters for *this* application, and how you would measure each item.
:::

::: why
A sensor datasheet is a set of claims made under ideal conditions. Validation is how you find out which claims survive on your board, in your product, over your temperature range. Missing one property, for example response time or supply sensitivity, is how a design that works on the bench fails in the field. Interviewers also use this question to see whether you think in terms of a **test plan**, since that is what a hardware or systems engineer actually writes before building a product around a sensor.
:::

::: concepts
- **Static performance**: offset, gain error, nonlinearity, hysteresis, resolution and accuracy at steady state.
- **Precision and noise**: repeatability and noise density; what averaging can and cannot fix. See [[q0873]].
- **Dynamic performance**: bandwidth, rise time, group delay and sample rate.
- **Environmental sensitivity**: temperature coefficient, supply rejection, humidity, shock, EMI.
- **Long-term stability**: drift, aging and warm-up behavior.
- **Fault and edge behavior**: saturation, overrange recovery, stuck-at and open-circuit detection.
- **Calibration context**: which of these terms can be trimmed. See [[q0871]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd validate against the spec in groups. First static accuracy: offset, gain, nonlinearity and hysteresis across the full range, compared to a reference. Second, noise and repeatability: sit at a fixed input and look at the standard deviation, the noise density, and the Allan deviation if drift matters. Third, dynamics: step response, bandwidth, latency, and whether the output rate and the filter actually give what the datasheet says. Fourth, environment: temperature coefficient, supply voltage and ripple sensitivity, humidity, vibration, and EMI from whatever's nearby on the board. Fifth, stability: warm-up and long-term drift. And last, the weird cases: saturation and recovery, power-up behavior, and what the sensor says when it's broken or disconnected.

Which ones matter most depends on the product. For a gyro in a drone, bias stability and latency matter more than absolute accuracy. For a battery current sensor, offset and gain over temperature matter most. I'd write the plan so that each property has a method, a reference, a number and a pass limit."
:::

::: technical
**Static.** *Offset* (output at zero input), *gain/sensitivity error*, *nonlinearity* (maximum deviation from the best-fit or end-point line, as % full scale), *hysteresis* (maximum difference between up and down sweeps), *resolution* (smallest detectable change, limited by noise and ADC LSB), *accuracy* (total error including all these).

**Noise and precision.** Repeatability, RMS noise, noise density in units/√Hz, peak-to-peak noise (usually about 6 to 6.6 times RMS for Gaussian noise at typical record lengths), and **Allan deviation** to separate white noise, 1/f noise and drift. Resolution in practice is about the RMS noise, not the LSB: a 16-bit ADC with 8 LSB of noise delivers about 13 effective bits.

**Dynamic.** Bandwidth ($f_{-3dB}$), settling time, rise time $t_r \approx 0.35/f_{-3dB}$ for a first-order system, group delay/latency, output data rate, and aliasing if the output is decimated.

**Environmental.** Temperature coefficient (ppm/°C or %FS/°C), sensitivity to supply voltage (PSRR) and ripple, mechanical stress and vibration (especially for MEMS and strain-based sensors), humidity and contamination, EMI/ESD immunity, self-heating.

**Stability.** Warm-up time, short-term drift, long-term drift/aging (%/year), and shift after thermal cycling or mechanical shock.

**Robustness.** Overrange and recovery time, saturation behavior, power-up and brownout, fault flags, stuck outputs, and communication errors (CRC, timeouts).

**Unit-to-unit.** All of the above as distributions across units and lots, not single numbers.
:::

::: short
"I group it: static accuracy (offset, gain, nonlinearity, hysteresis), noise and repeatability, dynamics (bandwidth, response time, latency), environment (temperature, supply, vibration, EMI), long-term drift, and fault behavior like saturation, startup and open-circuit. For each one I'd define a method, a reference and a pass limit. Which ones get priority depends on the application, a gyro cares about bias stability and latency, a current sensor cares about offset and gain over temperature."
:::

::: long
"I'd think of it as a test plan with five buckets, and I'd decide the priorities from the application.

Bucket one is static accuracy. Against a good reference I sweep the range up and down and extract offset, gain error, nonlinearity and hysteresis. Bucket two is noise and repeatability. I hold the input fixed and record a long time. The standard deviation gives me RMS noise, the spectrum gives noise density, and an Allan deviation plot shows where averaging stops helping and drift takes over. That also sets the real resolution, which is about the noise, not the ADC LSB.

Bucket three is dynamics. I apply a step or a sine sweep and measure the bandwidth, the rise time and the latency from the physical event to the digital sample. Datasheet bandwidth and the bandwidth you actually get through the on-chip filter and your own firmware can be different.

Bucket four is environment. Temperature is the big one: I measure offset and gain over the full range and get a temperature coefficient. Then supply voltage and ripple, because a noisy rail can show up directly in the output. Then vibration, EMI and humidity if the product sees them.

Bucket five is stability and robustness: warm-up, long-term drift, overrange and recovery, power-up, and what happens when the sensor or cable fails. A sensor that reports a plausible-looking wrong number when it's disconnected is dangerous.

Finally I do all of it on several units so I know the spread. And I'd say which items I'd drop if the schedule was tight, probably humidity and vibration for a benign indoor product."
:::

::: terms
- **Accuracy**: closeness of the reading to the true value, including all systematic and random errors.
- **Precision (repeatability)**: closeness of repeated readings to each other.
- **Resolution**: the smallest change the system can reliably detect, limited by noise and quantization.
- **Linearity error**: maximum deviation from a straight line, usually as % of full scale.
- **Hysteresis**: dependence of the output on the direction of approach.
- **Noise density**: RMS noise per square-root hertz, so noise scales with the square root of bandwidth.
- **Allan deviation**: a statistic showing noise vs averaging time and where drift begins.
- **Temperature coefficient (tempco)**: change of offset or gain per degree.
- **Response time / bandwidth**: how quickly the output follows a changing input.
- **PSRR**: how much supply variation couples to the output.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "accuracy, precision, range, resolution, sensitivity" from a textbook.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Groups the list into static, noise, dynamic, environmental, stability and fault behavior.
- Ties priorities to the application and says why.
- Gives a method for each item ("step response for latency", "Allan deviation for drift") and a quantity with units.
- Distinguishes ADC LSB from true resolution.
- Points out that the datasheet bandwidth and your system's bandwidth differ.
- Includes failure behavior and unit-to-unit spread, the things that cause field returns.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Equating resolution with the ADC bit count.** A 16-bit ADC with noisy input has far fewer effective bits.
- **Mixing up accuracy and precision.** A tight cluster of wrong readings is precise but not accurate; calibration can fix that, and averaging cannot.
- **Validating at room temperature only.** Tempco is often the dominant error term in the real product.
- **Forgetting latency.** In a control loop, delay costs phase margin even if the amplitude response is perfect.
- **Testing one unit.** Production variation is part of the specification.
- **Ignoring fault behavior.** Open, shorted or saturated inputs must produce a detectable state.
- **Averaging to fix everything.** Averaging reduces white noise but not drift, bias or nonlinearity.
:::

::: deep
## Why a list is not enough

"What do you validate" invites a long list, but a long list tells the interviewer nothing about judgment. The structure that works is to ask, for each property, three questions: *what failure does this catch*, *how do I measure it*, and *what number passes*. Then rank by what the product cares about.

## Static accuracy: the sweep

Sweep the input over the full range, up and then down, at steady state. From the data you extract:

- **Offset**: output at zero input.
- **Gain error**: slope compared with the ideal.
- **Nonlinearity**: maximum distance from the fitted line, as % of full scale (state whether the line is end-point or best-fit, because they give different numbers).
- **Hysteresis**: maximum gap between the up and down curves at the same input.

Report the total error as well, since the individual terms often partly cancel or add, and the customer sees only the total.

## Noise, resolution and averaging

Hold the input constant and record. The RMS noise sets resolution. A useful rule: a 16-bit ADC with 3.3 V full scale has an LSB of $3.3/65536 = 50.4$ µV. If the sensor chain's RMS noise is 400 µV, that is about 8 LSB rms, and the effective resolution is about $\log_2(65536/8) = 13$ bits. The last three bits are noise.

For white noise the density $e_n$ in µV/√Hz scales the RMS noise as $e_n\sqrt{\text{BW}}$. 20 mV rms over 100 Hz is $20\,\text{mV}/\sqrt{100} = 2$ mV/√Hz (2000 µV/√Hz). Cutting bandwidth by 4 halves the noise, but only if the signal itself doesn't need the bandwidth.

Averaging $N$ samples reduces white noise by $\sqrt N$, but real sensors have 1/f noise and drift, so there is a point where averaging stops helping. An **Allan deviation** plot shows it: for white noise it falls as $1/\sqrt\tau$, then flattens at the bias-instability floor, then rises with drift.

## Dynamics: does it keep up?

Apply a step or a swept sine and measure the response. Three numbers matter: **bandwidth**, **rise time**, and **latency**. For a first-order response, $t_r = 0.35/f_{-3dB}$; a 10 Hz sensor takes about 35 ms to rise. Latency (delay from the physical event to the sample appearing at your MCU) is separate from bandwidth. In a control loop, a delay $T_d$ costs phase $360\,f\,T_d$ degrees at frequency $f$: 5 ms of latency at a 20 Hz crossover is 36° of phase margin.

Also check that the output data rate, the internal filter and your sampling are consistent. If the sensor decimates without a good anti-alias filter, high-frequency noise folds into the band.

## Environment and stability

| Test | What it catches | Typical method |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature sweep | Offset and gain tempco | Chamber, soak at each step, compare to reference |
| Supply sweep and ripple | PSRR, reset thresholds | Vary Vdd across spec, inject ripple |
| Warm-up | Self-heating, thermal settling | Log from power-on |
| Long-term drift | Aging, creep | Soak or burn-in, periodic checks |
| Vibration/shock | Mechanical resonances, stress shifts | Shaker, drop test (MEMS especially) |
| EMI/ESD | Coupling, latch-up, resets | Pre-compliance tests |
| Humidity | Leakage, optical fogging | Humidity chamber |

Worked example for tempco. A current sensor's offset moves from 0.0 mA at 25 °C to 3.0 mA at 85 °C. That is 3 mA / 60 °C = 0.05 mA/°C. On a 1 A full-scale sensor it is 0.3% over the range, so a room-temperature-only calibration would miss almost all of the error budget.

## Robustness: what the sensor does when things go wrong

Check saturation and recovery (a gyro that takes 200 ms to recover after a shock is useless during that time), power-up and brownout behavior, communication faults, and open or short on the sensing element. A good sensor flags these; a bad one returns the last value or midscale.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Peak-to-peak noise is often quoted as about 6.6 times RMS for Gaussian noise, but this assumes a specific confidence level (about 99.9%) and record length. Other conventions use 6 times RMS. Check which convention a datasheet uses.
:::

::: applications
- **Drone and robot IMUs**: bias stability, noise density, latency and temperature sensitivity matter more than absolute accuracy.
- **Battery current sensors**: offset, gain and tempco decide state-of-charge accuracy.
- **Medical thermometers and pulse oximeters**: accuracy over a narrow range, validated across skin tones and conditions.
- **Automotive sensors**: temperature range, EMI immunity, fault detection and long-term drift under qualification regimes.
- **Camera and LiDAR modules**: range accuracy, precision, resolution, field of view and ambient-light sensitivity (compare [[q0861]] and [[q0870]]).
- **Industrial pressure and load cells**: hysteresis, creep, overrange recovery and calibration traceability.
:::

::: assumptions
I treated "a sensor" as a generic analog or digital single-channel sensor and listed the properties that apply broadly. The priorities change by sensor type: a multi-axis IMU adds cross-axis sensitivity, scale-factor and axis misalignment; a camera adds spatial properties such as MTF, dynamic range and fixed-pattern noise; a LiDAR adds range walk, divergence and reflectivity dependence. The priority also changes with the application: safety-critical systems need fault detection and diagnostics validated first. Another valid structure is by lifecycle (incoming inspection, qualification, production test, field monitoring). The five-bucket structure here is one way to organize the same content.
:::

::: followups
### Which single property would you validate first and why?
It depends on the application, but in general I'd start with the one that can silently ruin everything: offset and gain over temperature for a measuring sensor. Noise is easy to see; a temperature-driven bias looks fine on the bench.

### How do you measure the true resolution of a sensor?
Hold the input fixed and record a long sequence. RMS noise (in output units) is the resolution to first order; many define it as 6.6 times RMS as the noise-free range. Compare with the ADC LSB: if noise is larger than one LSB, extra ADC bits do not help.

### The datasheet says 1 kHz bandwidth, but your control loop sees 10 ms latency. Is that a contradiction?
No. Bandwidth describes amplitude roll-off; latency is the delay through filters, sample-and-hold, interface transfer and firmware buffering. A 1 kHz sensor with a digital filter and SPI poll can easily add several milliseconds. Measure end to end with a physical stimulus and a timestamped pin toggle.

### How would you detect that a sensor has failed?
Combine range checks (reading outside physical limits), rate-of-change checks, cross-checks against a second sensor or a model, built-in self-test where available, and checks on the interface (CRC, heartbeat). Open and short conditions should land outside the valid range so they are distinguishable.

### How do you know your validation has enough samples?
Pick the metric whose confidence you care about (for instance worst-case error) and estimate how the number changes with more units. If a 5-unit study gives a spread of 0.3 °C and a 20-unit study gives 0.5 °C, the early number was optimistic. For safety-critical uses, use a statistical tolerance interval rather than a minimum-sample habit.
:::

::: rubric
- Groups properties into static, noise, dynamic, environmental, stability and fault behavior :: Shows structure, not a memorized list.
- Gives a measurement method for each property :: Validation is about how to measure, not just what.
- Distinguishes accuracy, precision and resolution :: These are the most commonly confused terms.
- Includes temperature and supply sensitivity :: They dominate real-world error.
- Includes dynamic behavior (bandwidth, latency) :: Controls and fusion depend on timing as much as amplitude.
- Covers failure and edge cases :: Safety and reliability depend on them.
- Prioritizes by application :: Shows engineering judgment.
- Mentions unit-to-unit spread :: Production quality depends on distributions.
:::
