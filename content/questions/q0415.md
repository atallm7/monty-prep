::: basics
### In plain words
A solenoid's cycle time is pull-in time plus release time plus return time. To speed up pull-in, you over-excite it: apply a much higher voltage for the first few milliseconds, then drop to a low hold current. To speed up release, you let the coil's current drain faster by using a Zener or TVS clamp in place of a plain diode. You also tame bounce with a soft landing. The limits are coil heating and the transistor's voltage rating.

### Picture it
Picture filling a bucket and emptying it. A higher-pressure hose fills the bucket faster, which is over-excitation. A bigger drain empties it faster. The plain diode is a thin drain: the coil's stored energy trickles away slowly. A Zener or TVS is a wide drain. Once the bucket is full, a thin trickle just keeps it topped up, which is the hold current.

### Words you need
- **Over-excitation (peak-and-hold)**: a high pull-in voltage or current first, then a lower, regulated hold current.
- **Flyback clamp**: the part that sets the coil's reverse voltage at turn-off. A diode gives about 0.7 V, and a Zener or TVS gives much more.
- **Hold current**: the lower current that keeps a closed plunger shut. The small air gap needs far less.
- **Soft landing**: slowing the plunger just before the end stop, to cut bounce, noise, and wear.

### The simplest example
Take a 24 V solenoid with a 12 Ω coil, so 2 A at rated voltage. Driven at 24 V steady, it closes in about 19.5 ms. With 72 V applied until it closes, it takes about 7.5 ms. Then hold at 0.5 A, which dissipates $0.5^2 \times 12 = 3$ W instead of 48 W. On release, a plain diode lets the plunger start leaving about 28 ms after turn-off. A 60 V clamp cuts that to about 4 ms. The cycle shrinks from about 65 ms to about 22.5 ms, roughly 15 Hz up to 44 Hz.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the peak-and-hold circuit, the clamp trade-off, and the thermal budget.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can reason about a solenoid as an electro-mechanical system with an electrical time constant, a mechanical time constant and a heat limit, and then improve each. The key ideas are over-excitation (peak-and-hold: a high voltage to pull in fast, then a low hold current), a faster current decay at turn-off (a Zener/TVS clamp instead of a plain diode), soft landing to cut bounce, and mechanical choices (mass, spring). A strong answer quantifies the gain, states the limits (coil heating, FET voltage rating, impact wear) and knows that "faster" has trade-offs.
:::

::: why
Valves, injectors, printheads, sorters, locks and pinball flippers all live or die by solenoid response time. A drive that simply switches the rated voltage on and off with a diode leaves a lot of speed on the table: the pull-in is slow because the current builds with $L/R$, and the release is slow because the diode lets the current decay with $L/R$ too. Better drive electronics can often triple the cycle rate without touching the mechanics, which is why this question appears in design interviews.
:::

::: concepts
- **Force from current**: $F = \tfrac12 i^2\,\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x$, so the plunger moves only once the current is high enough. See [[q0334]] and [[q0406]].
- **RL current rise**: $i(t) = (V/R)(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ with $\tau = L/R$.
- **Over-excitation / peak-and-hold**: temporarily applying more than the continuous rating.
- **Clamped flyback**: a Zener/TVS in the flyback path speeds the decay. See [[q0413]].
- **Hold current**: far below pull-in current once the air gap closes. See [[q0409]].
- **Bounce and impact**: end-of-stroke velocity limits noise, wear and the time to settle.
:::

"Cycle time is pull-in time plus release time plus return stroke, so I'd attack each.

Pull-in is limited by how fast current builds against the coil's inductance. The force is proportional to current squared, so the plunger doesn't move until the current reaches a threshold. If I drive at the rated voltage the current rises as $L/R$, slow. The fix is over-excitation: apply a much higher voltage, say 3x, for the first few milliseconds, then switch to a lower holding voltage or PWM current. In an example 24 volt solenoid, 72 volts pulls it in in about 7.5 ms instead of 19.5 ms. Once the plunger is closed the gap is small and a quarter of the current holds it, so I chop to a hold current and cut the heating by 90%, which is also what makes the overdrive thermally okay.

Release is limited by how fast the current decays. A plain flyback diode holds the coil near zero volts so the current decays slowly. If I put a Zener or TVS in series with the diode, say 60 volts, the coil sees minus 60 volts and the current collapses in a couple of milliseconds instead of tens. The price is that the FET must withstand supply plus clamp.

Beyond the electronics: lighter plunger, stiffer return spring for faster release, shorter stroke, and a soft landing so the plunger doesn't bounce, because the bounce limits how soon you can fire again. And watch the thermal budget, because overdriving heats the coil."
:::

::: technical
**Pull-in time.** The plunger starts moving when $F_{mag} \ge F_{spring,0}$: $\tfrac12 i_{pull}^2\,\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x \ge F_0$. With $L$ roughly constant at the open gap, $i(t) = (V/R)(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, so the time to reach $i_{pull}$:
$$t_{pull} = -\tau\ln\!\left(1 - \frac{i_{pull}R}{V}\right)$$
Raising $V$ makes the argument smaller and the time shorter, approaching $t \approx i_{pull}L/V$ (a linear ramp) for $V \gg i_{pull}R$. The total transit also depends on mass, spring and the force law; a time-stepping model gives the full stroke time.

**Illustrative model** (24 V, 12 Ω coil, 2 A final current, 10 mm stroke, 20 g plunger, 1.2 N preload, 100 N/m spring; $L$ rising from 48 mH open to 170 mH closed). Pull-in time with the peak voltage $V_p$ applied until closure (then 6 V average hold):

| Peak voltage | Closure time | Peak current |
|---|---|---|
| 24 V (no overdrive) | 19.5 ms | 1.84 A |
| 36 V | 12.8 ms | 2.5 A |
| 48 V | 10.1 ms | 3.1 A |
| 72 V | 7.5 ms | 4.1 A |
| 96 V | 6.2 ms | 4.9 A |

Diminishing returns: tripling the voltage cuts the time by about 2.6x, not 3x or more, because the plunger's mass and the spring also limit it.

**Hold.** Once closed, the air gap is small, $\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x$ is large and a fraction of the pull-in current holds the plunger. Hold at 0.5 A (6 V average from 24 V at 25% PWM): coil dissipation $I^2R = 3$ W, versus 48 W at the full 2 A. A PWM current regulator with a sense resistor (peak-and-hold chopper) does this.

**Release.** With a clamp of voltage $V_c$ across the coil during decay, $L\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t = -(V_c + iR)$ and the time to reach the release current is $t = \tau\ln\frac{V_c + I_0R}{V_c + I_rR}$. The plunger leaves once the current falls below about 0.22 A (where $\tfrac12 i^2\,\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x$ at the closed gap equals the 2.2 N compressed-spring force). In the model above, from a settled 2 A at 24 V: plain diode (0.7 V), the plunger starts to leave 28 ms after turn-off; 60 V clamp: 4.1 ms. From a settled 0.5 A hold current with the 60 V clamp: about 1 ms. The return stroke then takes about another 14 ms (spring limited), giving a total open time of 15 ms against 45 ms for the baseline.

**Cycle estimate** (close plus fully open, ignoring any required dwell). Baseline (24 V steady, diode): 19.5 ms + 45 ms: about 65 ms, 15 Hz maximum. Optimised (72 V peak, hold, 60 V clamp): 7.5 + 15 = 22.5 ms, 44 Hz: about 2.9x.

**Thermal budget.** One pull-in at 72 V dissipates about 0.88 J in the coil (model). At 20 Hz that is 17.6 W plus hold, so check the coil's continuous rating or limit the repetition rate.

**FET voltage.** With a drain-to-ground TVS of $V_{BR}$, the coil sees $-(V_{BR} - V_{sup})$. Rate the FET above the clamp and the overdrive supply.
:::

Force goes as current squared times dL/dx, so the plunger doesn't move until the current gets high enough, and with the rated voltage that happens on an L over R time scale. Hold at about a quarter of the pull-in current cuts coil power from 48 watts to 3.

Release is the other half. A plain flyback diode lets the current decay with the coil's L over R, which for a closed solenoid is tens of milliseconds. If I add a Zener or TVS in series, the coil sees maybe minus 60 volts and the current falls in milliseconds. The cost is that the FET sees the supply plus the clamp, so I need a higher-voltage FET.

Then mechanics: a lighter plunger, a stronger return spring for faster release, but that slows pull-in a bit, and a shorter stroke. And I'd control the end velocity, a soft landing, because bounce limits how soon the next cycle can start and it makes noise and wear.

:::

::: terms
- **Over-excitation**: applying more than rated voltage for a short time to accelerate pull-in.
- **Peak-and-hold**: a drive profile with a high pull-in current followed by a lower regulated hold current.
- **Flyback clamp**: the device that sets the coil's reverse voltage at turn-off (diode, diode + Zener, TVS).
- **Pull-in current**: the current at which magnetic force exceeds spring preload.
- **Hold current**: the lower current that keeps the plunger closed.
- **Soft landing**: controlling impact velocity at the end of stroke.
- **Eddy-current delay**: flux lag from induced currents in solid iron.
- **Cycle rate**: maximum repetition frequency: $1/(t_{close} + t_{open})$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Overdriving without a duty limit.** 72 V across a 24 V coil heats it quickly. Time-limit the boost and regulate the hold.
- **Releasing with a plain diode and expecting fast release.** The diode keeps the coil near 0 V and slows the decay.
- **Choosing the clamp above the FET rating.** The FET sees $V_{sup} + V_c$ (plus ringing).
- **Increasing the return spring without checking pull-in.** A stiffer spring speeds release but needs more force (more current or time) to close.
- **Ignoring bounce.** A faster plunger hits harder; bounce delays the settled state and shortens life.
- **Assuming inductance is constant.** It rises as the gap closes, so the real current waveform dips during the stroke ([[q0406]]).
- **Making the hold current too low.** Vibration or a supply dip drops the plunger out; leave margin.
:::

::: deep
## Where the time goes

A solenoid cycle has four phases: build-up (current rises until force exceeds the spring), travel (plunger accelerates and arrives), release (current falls until the spring wins) and return (plunger travels back). Each has a different limiter:

| Phase | Limited by | Lever |
|---|---|---|
| Build-up | $L/R$ and applied voltage | higher voltage, lower $L$ |
| Travel | force, mass, spring, speed voltage | lighter plunger, force profile, overdrive |
| Release | current decay speed | clamp voltage |
| Return | spring force, mass, damping | stiffer spring, lighter plunger |

## Build-up: why overdrive works

With the plunger still, $i(t) = (V/R)(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, $\tau = L/R$. For the model solenoid at the open gap $L = 48$ mH, $R = 12\ \Omega$, so $\tau = 4$ ms. At rated 24 V the final current is 2 A and 1.2 N of preload needs $i_{pull} = \sqrt{2F_0/(\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x)} = \sqrt{2.4/1.59} = 1.23$ A: $t = -4\ln(1 - 1.23/2) = 3.8$ ms. With 72 V the final current would be 6 A and $t = -4\ln(1 - 1.23/6) = 0.9$ ms. So the build-up gets 4x faster. The travel phase is also faster because higher current gives more force throughout the stroke.

## The mechanical side

- **Moving mass**: a lighter plunger accelerates faster in both directions ($a = F/m$). Often changing the material or hollowing the plunger is cheaper than electronics.
- **Spring**: a stiffer return spring speeds release and return but needs more pull-in force. Use a spring just strong enough to release reliably at the minimum friction and orientation.
- **Stroke**: shorter stroke gives faster travel and higher force at the start, but may not meet the application.
- **Magnetic circuit**: laminated or slitted plungers and housings reduce eddy currents, which delay flux build-up and release; ferrite or powder parts for fast solenoids. Eddy-current effects are why measured response is slower than the simple RL model, and why vendors quote response times at specific voltages.
- **Latching/dual-coil designs**: a permanent magnet holds the plunger with zero power; a short pulse switches states, leaving no hold heating.

## Soft landing and bounce

Impact velocity in the model is about 2.7 m/s at 24 V and about 6.7 m/s with the 72 V overdrive (an overestimate, because the model has no magnetic saturation, so its force near closure is unrealistically high, but the trend is real). At the end stop the plunger rebounds; each bounce delays "settled closed" by milliseconds. Remedies:

1. **Drop the voltage before arrival**: detect the dip ([[q0406]]) or time it, then switch to hold or even briefly reverse the current to brake the plunger.
2. **Mechanical damping**: rubber or polymer end stop; air or fluid damping; a slight taper in the pole piece.
3. **Controlled current profile** with a current loop.

Soft landing costs a little closure time but gains quieter operation, longer life and a faster *settled* time.

## Putting numbers on the benefit

| Configuration | Close | Release start | Fully open | Cycle |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 24 V steady, plain diode | 19.5 ms | 28 ms | 45 ms | 65 ms (15 Hz) |
| 24 V steady, 60 V clamp | 19.5 ms | 4.1 ms | 18 ms | 38 ms (27 Hz) |
| 72 V peak, 0.5 A hold, plain diode | 7.5 ms | 10.7 ms | 28 ms | 35 ms (28 Hz) |
| 72 V peak, 0.5 A hold, 60 V clamp | 7.5 ms | 1.0 ms | 15 ms | 22.5 ms (44 Hz) |

Both electronics changes help, and they combine. Release times assume the dwell is long enough for the current to settle (2 A or 0.5 A). The remaining 15 ms is mostly the spring-return stroke, set by the mechanics.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> These timings come from an illustrative lumped model that ignores eddy currents, saturation, friction and air damping. The *ratios* and the qualitative ordering are typical; absolute times for a real solenoid must be measured. Vendors usually quote operate and release times only at specific voltages and conditions.

::: applications
- **Fuel injectors**: peak-and-hold drivers (high-impedance injectors use saturated drive, low-impedance injectors use peak-and-hold).
- **Industrial and pneumatic valves**: over-excite for fast response, then hold with PWM to reduce heat.
- **Inkjet and dispensing valves**: fast, repeatable cycling with controlled landing.
- **Latches and locks**: pulse-and-hold, and latching types with no hold current.
- **Pinball flippers and kickers**: high-voltage pull-in followed by a lower-voltage hold winding.
- **Sorters and coin mechanisms**: high-rate solenoids with clamped release.
:::

::: assumptions
- The numbers come from a **lumped model** (illustrative solenoid), with $L(x)$ rising as the gap closes, no eddy currents, no saturation and ideal switches.
- **Overdrive is time-limited** by detection or timing; if the plunger jams, the high voltage must be removed by a timeout or the coil overheats.
- The clamp assumes a **TVS or Zener of appropriate rating** and a FET rated above supply plus clamp.
- Mechanical parameters (mass, spring, friction) are held fixed except where noted.
- Real solenoids vary: AC solenoids (shaded-pole), proportional solenoids and voice coils have different dynamics.
:::

::: followups
### How do you decide the peak voltage and duration?
Choose the peak voltage from the speed target and the limits: FET and driver ratings, coil insulation, thermal budget and impact velocity. Duration: until arrival (detected by sensor, current dip or timer set from the characterised closing time plus margin). Add a maximum time so a jam can't overheat the coil. Here 72 V for at most about 10 ms.

### Why does the plunger bounce, and how does it limit the cycle rate?
The plunger hits the end stop elastically and rebounds. While it bounces the magnetic force pulls it back, but it takes several ms to settle; valve flow or contact reliability is poor during that time. Higher impact velocity makes bounce worse. Fix with dampers, lower final velocity (drop the voltage at arrival) or a controlled brake pulse.

### Why would a faster release need a higher-voltage FET?
The FET (or clamp) sees $V_{sup} + V_c$. A faster decay needs a larger reverse coil voltage. For the 60 V clamp on a 24 V supply the drain reaches 84 V (if the TVS is drain-to-ground with $V_{BR}$ = 84 V, the coil sees $-60$ V), so you need a 100 V FET, not a 40 V one.

### The solenoid still releases slowly even with a TVS. What else could be wrong?
Eddy currents in a solid plunger or housing (flux decays slowly), residual magnetism and sticking (add a non-magnetic shim or a residual air gap), too weak a return spring, friction or fluid damping, or the current is actually flowing through another path (for example, the body diode of an H-bridge or a second clamp path that sets a lower voltage than you think).

### How would you detect that the plunger has arrived to switch from peak to hold?
Use the current dip: the speed voltage makes the current fall during the stroke, and the minimum marks arrival. Or use a Hall or optical switch at the end of stroke, or a fixed timer based on characterisation. The current method needs no sensor but needs calibration across voltage and temperature ([[q0406]]).
:::
