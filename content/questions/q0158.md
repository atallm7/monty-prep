::: testing
Whether you can turn the slogan "buck beats LDO on efficiency" into a precise condition. A strong candidate says *when the voltage drop is small* and *when the load is very light so overhead dominates*, writes the break-even as $V_{out}/V_{in} > \eta_{buck}$, and mentions quiescent current on both sides. It also checks that you do not confuse efficiency with other reasons to pick an LDO (noise, size, cost).
:::

::: why
In real products the surprise is the reverse of what students expect: a 3.6 V battery feeding a 3.3 V sensor rail via a fancy buck can *lose* to a one-capacitor LDO, and an always-on rail at 5 µA load is dominated by regulator quiescent current, not conversion. Knowing the crossover saves board area, cost and battery life, and shows you evaluate regulators from numbers. It is the natural follow-up to [[q0157]] and the reverse of "why are buck converters more efficient than LDOs?" ([[q0211]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **LDO efficiency**: $\eta_{LDO}=\dfrac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_q)}$. See [[q0283]] and [[q0277]].
- **Buck efficiency**: set by conduction, switching and fixed (controller) losses, roughly 85–95% at mid load but falling at light load. See [[q0162]].
- **Break-even headroom**: the LDO is better when $V_{out}/V_{in}>\eta_{buck}$.
- **Quiescent current**: what the regulator draws with no load; dominates at µA loads ([[q0165]]).
- **Light-load modes**: PFM or burst mode in bucks reduces overhead but not to zero.
- **Dropout operation**: a buck at 100% duty is basically a switch plus inductor and can be very efficient too.
:::

::: answer
"An LDO's efficiency is about Vout over Vin, so it can only compete when the drop is small. The break-even is simple: the LDO is more efficient if Vout over Vin is greater than the buck's efficiency. If a buck is 90 percent, that's anything above about 3.67 volts in for 3.3 out, so 3.6 volts to 3.3 volts gives the LDO 92 percent.

The other case is very light load. A buck has fixed overhead: the controller's quiescent current, gate drive, a bit of switching loss every cycle. If the load is, say, 10 microamps, that overhead can be bigger than the load itself, and a nano-power LDO with one microamp of Iq can beat it. Of course an LDO also has quiescent current, so it depends on the numbers.

But a big drop is a big drop. At 12 to 3.3 volts at any serious current the LDO is hopeless. And the LDO has other reasons to win that aren't efficiency: no inductor, no switching noise, less area."
:::

::: technical
**LDO:** $P_{loss}=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}+V_{in}I_{q}$, so
$$\eta_{LDO}=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_{q,LDO})}.$$

**Buck (simple model):** $P_{in}=V_{in}I_{q,B}+P_{out}/\eta_c$, where $\eta_c$ lumps conduction and switching efficiency (typically 0.9–0.95):
$$\eta_{buck}=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}I_{q,B}+V_{out}I_{out}/\eta_c}.$$

**Condition 1: heavy enough load that $I_q$ is negligible.** The LDO wins if
$$\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}>\eta_c \iff V_{in}<\frac{V_{out}}{\eta_c}.$$
For $V_{out}=3.3$ V and $\eta_c=0.92$ the break-even is $V_{in}=3.59$ V. A 3.6 V to 3.3 V LDO is 91.7%, a tie with a good buck; for $\eta_c<0.917$ the LDO wins.

**Condition 2: light load.** Divide by $I_{out}$ and the overhead terms matter: the LDO wins when $V_{in}I_{q,LDO}$ is much less than the buck's $V_{in}I_{q,B}$ plus its switching overhead. For 3.6 V to 3.3 V with an LDO $I_q=1$ µA and a buck $I_q=25$ µA:

| $I_{out}$ | LDO ($I_q$ = 1 µA) | Buck ($I_q$ = 25 µA, $\eta_c$ = 92%) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 µA | 83% | 26% |
| 100 µA | 91% | 74% |
| 1 mA | 92% | 90% |
| 10 mA | 92% | 92% |

**Why not always?** At large drop the LDO is capped by $V_{out}/V_{in}$, whatever its $I_q$; at higher load currents the buck's overhead is amortised. The crossover therefore depends on three numbers: $V_{out}/V_{in}$, $\eta_c$, and the two $I_q$ values.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The quiescent currents and buck efficiencies above are representative round numbers chosen to illustrate the trend. Real parts range from sub-µA to hundreds of µA of $I_q$, and a buck's light-load efficiency depends strongly on its PFM mode, so use the datasheet efficiency-versus-load curve at your actual $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$ and temperature.
:::

::: short
"An LDO is more efficient when the voltage drop is small or the load is tiny. Its efficiency is about Vout over Vin, so it beats a buck whenever that ratio is higher than the buck's efficiency, like 3.6 volts down to 3.3 at 92 percent. At very light loads, a buck's own quiescent current and switching overhead can exceed the load, so a nano-amp-class LDO can win. At a big drop and real current, the buck always wins."
:::

::: long
"The way I think about it is by writing both efficiencies down. The LDO is Vout times Iout over Vin times Iout plus its quiescent current. To first order that's Vout over Vin. A buck is Vout times Iout over the sum of the load power divided by its conversion efficiency plus its own overhead.

So there are two regimes where the LDO can come out ahead.

First, small headroom. If the buck is around 90 to 92 percent at that load, the LDO wins whenever Vout over Vin is above that. For a 3.3 volt rail that means an input below roughly 3.6 to 3.7 volts. A single Li-ion cell feeding a 3.3 volt rail through an LDO is 86 to 92 percent for most of its discharge and about 87 percent averaged if the cell sits near 3.8 volts, while using one capacitor and no inductor. The same logic applies to something like 1.8 volts to 1.5 volts inside a board.

Second, very light load. A buck has fixed costs: the controller's quiescent current, gate-drive charge, and the loss of waking up for each burst. At 10 microamps of load, a 25 microamp buck is 26 percent efficient, while a 1 microamp LDO at 3.6 to 3.3 volts is 83 percent. The same reasoning is why many always-on, microamp rails use a nano-power LDO or a load switch instead of a buck.

But I'd also say what doesn't flip: with a 12 volt input or a 2 volt drop at significant current, the LDO is capped at Vout over Vin, and wins nothing. And at light load with a big drop, a buck with a nanoamp-class quiescent current might still beat the LDO. So I'd compute both numbers for the real operating point instead of guessing."
:::

::: terms
- **Headroom**: $V_{in}-V_{out}$, the voltage an LDO has to burn.
- **Break-even**: the point at which two regulator choices dissipate the same power.
- **Quiescent current ($I_q$)**: the regulator's own supply current at no load ([[q0165]]).
- **PFM / burst mode**: light-load control mode where a buck skips cycles to cut switching loss.
- **100% duty / dropout mode**: a buck with the high-side switch always on when $V_{in}\approx V_{out}$.
- **Conversion efficiency ($\eta_c$)**: efficiency ignoring fixed controller overhead.
- **Nano-power LDO**: an LDO whose $I_q$ is in the nA to low-µA range.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "when Vin and Vout are close" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the break-even as an inequality, $V_{out}/V_{in}>\eta_{buck}$, and evaluates it (3.3 V rail: about 3.6–3.7 V).
- Separates the *heavy-load* regime (conversion loss) from the *light-load* regime (quiescent current and fixed overhead).
- Shows a small table or two numbers from each regime.
- Mentions that a buck at 100% duty is also efficient, and that nA-$I_q$ bucks exist, so "light load" is not an automatic LDO win.
- Adds non-efficiency reasons (noise, area, cost) while keeping them separate from the efficiency argument.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"LDO efficiency is Vout/Vin" with no mention of $I_q$.** At 10 µA load, a 50 µA-$I_q$ LDO is under 20% efficient at any headroom.
- **Assuming bucks are 90% at every load.** Efficiency falls steeply below roughly 1% of full load unless the part has an excellent light-load mode.
- **Comparing at different operating points.** Compare both at the same $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$ and $I_{out}$, using the datasheet curves.
- **Judging an LDO by peak efficiency only.** A 3.6 V cell goes to 4.2 V when full; efficiency at 4.2 V is 78.6%, not 92%.
- **Ignoring system effects.** A buck's pulsed input current stresses the source and its EMI may force filters, which cost area and add loss.
- **Calling an LDO "more efficient" because it has no switching loss.** It still burns the full headroom; there is only a regime in which the headroom is small.
:::

::: deep
## Start from the two loss models

An LDO has one dominant loss: current times headroom. The load current passes through the pass element; there is also a small ground current $I_q$ that flows from $V_{in}$ to ground. So

$$P_{in}=V_{in}(I_{out}+I_q),\qquad P_{out}=V_{out}I_{out},\qquad \eta=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_q)}.$$

At high enough load, $I_q\ll I_{out}$, so $\eta\to V_{out}/V_{in}$: a straight line in $V_{in}$, independent of load. At low load, $\eta$ falls as $I_{out}/(I_{out}+I_q)$.

A buck has many loss terms; the useful lumped model is "fixed plus proportional":

$$P_{loss,buck}\approx \underbrace{V_{in}I_{q,B}}_{\text{controller}}+\underbrace{k_{sw}f_{sw}}_{\text{gate drive and switching}}+\underbrace{I_{out}^2R}_{\text{conduction}}+\ldots$$

The efficiency curve rises with load as fixed losses are amortised, flattens at 90–95%, then declines slightly as $I^2R$ grows. That shape (low at the left, flat then slightly drooping on the right) is why the comparison depends on load.

## Regime 1: headroom is small

When both devices are in their flat region, the condition for the LDO to win is $V_{out}/V_{in}>\eta_c$. Let me tabulate for a 3.3 V rail with a 92% buck.

| $V_{in}$ | LDO $\eta$ (no $I_q$) | Buck | Winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.5 V | 94.3% | 92% | LDO |
| 3.6 V | 91.7% | 92% | tie |
| 4.2 V (full Li-ion) | 78.6% | 92% | Buck |
| 5 V | 66% | 92% | Buck |
| 12 V | 27.5% | 92% | Buck |

The Li-ion case is instructive. A cell goes from 4.2 V (full) through about 3.7 V (nominal) to 3.0–3.3 V (cutoff). The LDO's *instantaneous* efficiency is 79%, 89% and 100% (until dropout), but what matters for runtime is energy: because the LDO draws the same charge as the load, the energy the load receives is $V_{out}\,Q$ rather than the cell's full $\int V\,dQ$. For a 3.3 V rail, the fraction wasted is the area between the discharge curve and the 3.3 V line, a roughly 10–15% loss across the useful range. A good buck recovers that but loses its own 5–10%; the net gain can be small, which is why many wearables and sensors ship with LDOs from a single cell. The cost is that the LDO stops regulating when the cell drops below $V_{out}+V_{DO}$, while a buck-boost could continue.

## Regime 2: the load is tiny

At very low load $I_q$ and fixed overhead dominate. Take $V_{in}=3.6$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V:

- Nano-power LDO, $I_q=1\ \mu$A: $\eta(10\ \mu\text{A})=\dfrac{3.3\cdot 10}{3.6\cdot 11}=83\%$.
- Buck, $I_q=25\ \mu$A, $\eta_c=0.92$: $\eta(10\ \mu\text{A})=\dfrac{33}{3.6\cdot25+33/0.92}=26\%$.

This is why the answer to "when can an LDO be more efficient" always includes "and choose a low-$I_q$ one". A standard LDO at 50 µA $I_q$ and a 10 µA load is 15% efficient: worse than almost any buck.

Now the converse check: if the drop is big, a *low-$I_q$ buck* can beat an LDO even at light load. Consider a CR2032-class coin cell at 3.0 V feeding a 1.8 V, 10 µA rail.

- LDO ($I_q$ = 0.5 µA): $\eta=\dfrac{1.8\times10}{3.0\times10.5}=57\%$.
- Nano-power buck ($I_q$ = 0.4 µA, $\eta_c$ = 85% at this load): $P_{in}=3.0(0.4\ \mu\text{A})+18\ \mu\text{W}/0.85=22.4\ \mu$W, so $\eta=80\%$.

## When a buck runs out of room: 100% duty

A buck cannot regulate when $V_{in}\approx V_{out}$: the duty cycle would need to be 100% (the high-side FET always on), limited by minimum off-time and bootstrap requirements. Many parts have a "100% duty" mode in which the output follows the input minus $I(R_{DS(on)}+R_{DCR})$. That is *efficient* (for 1 A with 100 mΩ total, about 97% at 3.3 V) but unregulated; in contrast an LDO in dropout also tracks the input minus $I\,R_{DS(on),pass}$, also at similar efficiency. This is why the headroom comparison is only meaningful when you also say "and the output stays regulated".

## Non-efficiency reasons the LDO still wins

Even when the numbers tie, an LDO often wins because it has no inductor, no switching ripple, fast transient response and lower cost. For a noise-sensitive load, a clean rail is worth the loss, and a cleaner approach for high-drop rails is a buck followed by an LDO with small headroom ([[q0169]]). In that arrangement, the LDO's dissipation is $(V_{headroom})I_{out}$, which is the same few-percent penalty you accept from the "small headroom" regime above.

## How to answer a design question quickly

1. Compute $\eta_{LDO}=V_{out}/V_{in}$ at the highest expected $V_{in}$ and the lowest $V_{in}$ the battery spends time at.
2. Compare it with the datasheet buck curve at your load. If the load is below 1% of the buck's rated current, apply the buck's light-load efficiency, not the headline 90%.
3. Add the $I_q$ of each device at the always-on load.
4. If the numbers are within a couple of percent, choose on noise, area and cost.
:::

::: applications
- **Single Li-ion cell to a 3.0–3.3 V logic rail** at tens of mA: an LDO is often chosen for simplicity and low noise, accepting about 10% loss.
- **Always-on real-time clock or wake-up logic** at a few µA: nano-power LDO or a load switch rather than a general-purpose buck.
- **Post-regulator after a buck** (e.g., 3.8 V to 3.3 V): the LDO dissipates only $0.5I_{out}$ and cleans the rail ([[q0169]]).
- **Local 1.8 V to 1.2 V sub-rail** inside a board that already has 1.8 V: a small LDO costs less than another buck and loses only about 33% of a small load.
- **Coin-cell sensor node** at 1.8 V: a nano-power buck or an LDO depending on the exact $V_{in}$, load profile and sleep current.
- **Battery-powered always-on supervisor:** the regulator's $I_q$ sets the standby life ([[q0165]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- Efficiency comparisons use representative numbers: a mid-load buck at 92%, LDO and buck $I_q$ as stated in the tables. Real values vary by part, temperature and operating point.
- Both regulators operate in regulation (the LDO above dropout, the buck below 100% duty).
- Efficiency is the electrical $P_{out}/P_{in}$ at the regulator; system-level effects (extra input filtering for a buck, battery chemistry) are discussed in prose but not in the numbers.
- If the interviewer defines efficiency over a whole battery discharge, integrate energy over the discharge curve rather than quoting an instantaneous value.
- A different valid answer emphasises *any* reason an LDO might win (noise, cost, size). Give that as a second sentence, not as the efficiency answer.
:::

::: followups
### What's the crossover input voltage for a 3.3 V rail if the buck is 90% efficient?
$V_{in}=V_{out}/\eta_c=3.3/0.9=3.67$ V. Below that, an ideal LDO (negligible $I_q$) is more efficient. Above it, the buck wins. With a real LDO $I_q$ the crossover shifts slightly toward the buck at light load.

### Your battery rail is 3.0 V to 4.2 V and you need 1.8 V at 5 µA average. LDO or buck?
Compute both at the actual voltages. At 3.0 V in, an LDO with 0.5 µA $I_q$ gives $1.8\cdot5/(3.0\cdot5.5)=55\%$; at 4.2 V it is 39%. A buck with sub-µA $I_q$ and, say, 80% conversion efficiency would give about 70%. The buck wins on efficiency, but only if it genuinely has nA-class $I_q$ and a low-power mode, so I'd look at its datasheet curve at 5 µA.

### Does lower quiescent current always mean a better LDO?
No. Very low $I_q$ LDOs often have slower transient response and lower PSRR, because the error amp and pass-device bias is tiny. If the load wakes up in a burst, the output sags. Some parts adaptively raise the bias with load to get both.

### A buck is stuck at 100% duty at 3.6 V in, 3.3 V out. Is that "efficient"?
It can be: with a 100 mΩ total path resistance at 1 A, the loss is 0.1 W against 3.3 W, so about 97%. The catch is that the output is no longer regulated, and it tracks $V_{in}$ minus the drop. An LDO in dropout behaves similarly. The regulated comparison above only applies while both are in regulation.

### Why do designers sometimes choose an LDO even when it's 5% less efficient than a buck?
Because efficiency isn't the only cost. The LDO has no inductor, no switching noise and fewer parts, which reduces board area, EMI risk and design time. For a 100 mA, 3.6 V to 3.3 V rail, a 5% loss is about 17 mW, which usually doesn't justify an extra inductor and layout risk.
:::

::: interpretations
"More efficient" can be read at a single operating point (what the datasheet curve says at 3.6 V in, 50 mA) or over a whole usage profile (battery runtime). The answer is built so that you can state the instantaneous break-even first and then mention the energy-over-discharge view if the interviewer asks about battery life. Compare with [[q0211]], which asks the opposite question (why bucks are more efficient than LDOs), and [[q0157]], the general comparison.

::: rubric
- States $\eta_{LDO}\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$ and compares it to the buck's efficiency :: This is the core quantitative condition and is the cleanest way to answer.
- Identifies small headroom as the first case, with a number (e.g. 3.6 V to 3.3 V gives about 92%) :: A number shows the idea is real, not abstract.
- Identifies very light load, where quiescent current and fixed buck overhead dominate, as the second case :: This is the less obvious regime that distinguishes a good answer.
- Includes the LDO's own $I_q$ in the efficiency expression :: Without it the light-load argument is wrong.
- Notes that a large drop at real current is a clear buck win :: Prevents over-generalising the LDO result.
- Mentions non-efficiency reasons (noise, size, cost) separately :: Shows judgment without muddling the efficiency argument.
- Mentions a nano-$I_q$ buck or datasheet curves as the way to settle it :: Shows you would verify rather than guess.
