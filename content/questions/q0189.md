::: testing
Whether you can reason from $i = C\,dv/dt$ and the *shape of the current* instead of repeating the premise. The question says "linear ramp", and the correct answer is a polite correction: a constant capacitor current gives a linear ramp, but in a buck the capacitor current is itself a triangle, so the voltage is made of **parabolic** segments. It looks linear when ESR dominates (then it is a triangle copying the current), or over a short window, or in a load-step response. Pointing out the nuance and then giving the derivation is the strong answer.
:::

::: why
Output ripple, ESR budgeting, transient droop and the "which term dominates" decision for a capacitor choice (ceramic vs polymer vs electrolytic) all rest on the true shape of $v_C$. Using the wrong model (charging with the *peak* current for half a period) overestimates the ripple by 2x, because the correct peak-to-peak value is $\Delta I/(8fC)$. Interviewers like it because the premise is slightly off and good candidates notice.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor equation** $i_C = C\,dv_C/dt$: slope of voltage is proportional to current. See [[q0025]].
- **Integral of a triangle is a parabola**: current ramp ⇒ quadratic voltage.
- **Capacitor current in a buck** $i_C = i_L - I_{out}$, a zero-mean triangle. See [[q0187]].
- **ESR**: voltage across the series resistance is $i_C R_{ESR}$ and is in phase with the current. See [[q0030]].
- **Continuity**: $v_C$ cannot jump, so it is smooth with no corners (unlike $i_C$). See [[q0037]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd start from the capacitor equation, $i = C\,dv/dt$. The slope of the capacitor voltage is the capacitor current divided by $C$. If the current were constant, the voltage would be a straight ramp. That is what happens in a load step or when charging with a constant current source.

In a buck in steady state, though, the capacitor current isn't constant. It's the inductor current minus the load current, which is a triangle centred on zero: it ramps from minus half the ripple to plus half the ripple and back. The voltage is the integral of a triangle, and the integral of a ramp is a parabola. So the output voltage is made of smooth parabolic arcs, with its slope changing linearly. It's at its minimum when the capacitor current crosses zero going up and at its maximum when it crosses zero going down.

It looks like a linear ramp in two situations: when ESR dominates, because the ESR drop is proportional to the current so you literally see a triangle; and if you zoom in on a short segment. The peak-to-peak is $\Delta I/(8fC)$, which for my example is about 11.6 mV plus 5 mV of ESR contribution, about 12 mV combined."
:::

::: technical
Charge balance: $\langle i_C\rangle=0$. Let $i_C(t) = i_L(t)-I_{out}$, a triangle with slopes $s_{on}=(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ and $s_{off}=-V_{out}/L$ and peak $\pm\Delta I/2$.

**Capacitive part.** Integrate from the zero crossing at $t_1=DT/2$ (where $v_C$ is minimum):
$$v_C(t)-v_{min} = \frac{s_{on}}{2C}(t-t_1)^2 \quad (t_1<t<DT)$$
and for the falling ramp $v_C(t)=v_{max}-\frac{|s_{off}|}{2C}(t_2-t)^2$ up to $t_2=DT+(1-D)T/2$. Peak-to-peak is the area of the positive half of the current triangle divided by $C$:
$$\Delta V_C=\frac{1}{C}\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac T2\cdot\frac{\Delta I}{2}=\frac{\Delta I\,T}{8C}=\frac{\Delta I}{8 f C}.$$

**ESR part.** $v_{ESR}=i_C R_{ESR}$, a triangle of peak-to-peak $\Delta I\,R_{ESR}$.

**Numbers** (example: $\Delta I=1.018$ A, $C=22$ µF, ESR 5 mΩ, $f=500$ kHz): $\Delta V_C=11.6$ mV, $\Delta V_{ESR}=5.1$ mV. They are in quadrature (the parabolic part lags the current by 90°), so the true peak-to-peak is 12.3 mV, not 16.7 mV. When $R_{ESR}\gg 1/(8fC)=11.4$ mΩ the shape becomes a triangle (a linear ramp, up and down), which is where the premise holds.

**Linear ramp cases.** A constant current gives $\Delta v = It/C$: a 1 A load step into 22 µF droops at 45 mV/µs until the inductor catches up.
:::

::: short
"A constant capacitor current gives a linear ramp, but in a buck the capacitor current is a triangle, the inductor current minus the load. The integral of a triangle is a parabola, so the capacitor voltage is made of parabolic arcs, with its extremes where the capacitor current crosses zero. It only looks like a linear ramp when ESR dominates, because the ESR drop follows the current. Ripple is about $\Delta I/(8fC)$, here around 12 mV."
:::

::: long
"The statement 'the capacitor voltage is a linear ramp' is only true if the capacitor current is constant, because $i = C\,dv/dt$. So I'd check what the capacitor current actually is in the buck.

At the output node, the inductor current splits into the load and the capacitor. The load takes the average, so the capacitor takes the difference: a triangle with zero mean, about plus and minus 0.5 A in my example. That's not constant. Over each half of the period the current ramps linearly, from zero to a peak and back to zero, so the voltage is the integral of that: the slope of the voltage ramps linearly, which means the voltage is a parabola. In the on state and the off state the parabolas have different curvature, because the current slopes differ, and they join smoothly because the voltage and its slope are continuous.

The minimum of the voltage is where the capacitor current crosses zero heading up, and the maximum where it crosses zero heading down, one half period apart. The peak-to-peak is the area under the half-triangle of current divided by C, which gives $\Delta I/(8fC)$: with 1 A ripple, 500 kHz and 22 µF, 11.6 mV.

Then ESR adds a voltage proportional to the current, which is a triangle. With a ceramic capacitor the parabolic part dominates; with an electrolytic the ESR dominates and the output ripple looks like a triangle, which is what people call the linear ramp. Since the two are 90 degrees apart, they combine in quadrature rather than adding directly."
:::

::: terms
- **Parabolic arc**: shape of the capacitive part of ripple, $\propto (t-t_0)^2$.
- **ESR**: series resistance of the capacitor; its drop mirrors the current.
- **ESL**: series inductance; adds spikes at the switching edges.
- **Charge balance**: average capacitor current is zero; the area of positive and negative current are equal.
- **Peak-to-peak ripple**: $\Delta V_{pp}$; capacitive part $\Delta I/(8fC)$.
- **Quadrature**: the capacitive and ESR parts are 90° apart so they do not add arithmetically.
- **Load-step droop**: the constant-current (linear) ramp when the load changes.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Because the capacitor charges with the inductor current, so the voltage ramps."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Notes politely that the voltage is *not* linear in steady state; it is piecewise parabolic.
- Derives it from $i_C=i_L-I_{out}$ and shows the peak-to-peak formula $\Delta I/(8fC)$.
- Says where the extremes are (zero crossings of $i_C$, mid-ramp, not at the edge).
- Distinguishes the ESR-dominated (triangular) regime from the capacitance-dominated (parabolic) regime, with the crossover $R_{ESR}\approx 1/(8fC)$.
- Mentions the load-step droop as the case where a true linear ramp appears.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The capacitor charges at constant current during the on state."** The current is $i_L - I_{out}$, which varies from $-0.51$ A to $+0.51$ A across the on state.
- **Using $\Delta V = I t/C$ with the peak current for half a period.** That gives $\Delta I\,T/(4C)$, twice the correct $\Delta I\,T/(8C)$. The average of the triangle half is half the peak.
- **Adding capacitive and ESR ripple arithmetically.** They are in quadrature; the sum overestimates (16.7 vs 12.3 mV here).
- **Placing the voltage extremes at the switching edges.** They are at the zero crossings of $i_C$.
- **Ignoring ESL.** With ceramics and fast edges the high-frequency spikes on top of the ripple are not captured by this model.
:::

::: deep
## Start from the equation

$$i_C = C\frac{dv_C}{dt}\;\Longrightarrow\;v_C(t)=v_C(t_0)+\frac1C\int_{t_0}^{t} i_C\,d\tau.$$

If $i_C$ is a constant $I$, then $v_C$ is a straight ramp with slope $I/C$. For a buck, the question is: what is $i_C$?

KCL at the output node: $i_L=i_C+i_{load}$. The load current is essentially constant (the capacitor is large, the ripple small), so $i_C = i_L - I_{out}$. That is exactly the inductor triangle with its average removed.

## Integrate a triangle and get a parabola

Take the interval where $i_C$ rises linearly through zero: $i_C = s\,(t-t_1)$. Then

$$v_C(t)=v_{min}+\frac{s}{2C}(t-t_1)^2.$$

The slope of $v_C$ is $i_C/C$, which itself grows linearly. That is the definition of a parabola.

It bottoms out 275 ns after the high-side FET turns on, when $i_C$ crosses zero going positive, and peaks 1.275 µs after the start of the cycle, when $i_C$ crosses zero going negative. The arcs are slightly different in curvature because the on-slope ($+1.85$ A/µs) differs from the off-slope ($-0.70$ A/µs).

The peak-to-peak value is the area of the positive half of the current waveform divided by $C$:

$$\Delta V_C = \frac1C\cdot\underbrace{\frac12\cdot\frac{T}{2}\cdot\frac{\Delta I}{2}}_{\text{triangle area}} = \frac{\Delta I\,T}{8C}.$$

Numerically: $\frac{1.018\times 2\times10^{-6}}{8\times22\times10^{-6}}=11.6$ mV. Equivalent: charge $Q=\Delta I\,T/8 = 0.254\ \mu C$ moves in and out of the capacitor each cycle.

> [!TIP]
> If you remember only one ripple formula, make it $\Delta V = \Delta I_L/(8 f_{sw} C)$. The 8 comes from the area of the positive half-triangle of $i_C$: $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac T2\cdot\tfrac{\Delta I}{2}=\Delta I\,T/8$.

## Where the "linear ramp" picture comes from

**ESR dominated.** The ESR contributes $v_{ESR}=i_C R_{ESR}$. That is a scaled copy of the current: a triangle with straight edges. When the ESR term exceeds the capacitive term, i.e. $R_{ESR} > \frac{1}{8fC}$ (11.4 mΩ for 22 µF at 500 kHz), the output ripple is essentially a triangle with linear ramps, and the "linear ramp" statement is valid.

**Short windows.** Any smooth curve looks like a straight line over a small enough interval; this is why a zoomed-in scope shot of a ceramic-capacitor ripple can look like a ramp.

## Quadrature addition

The parabolic term is the integral of $i_C$ and so lags it by 90° (for a sine wave of that frequency); the ESR term is in phase. Peak values add only if the two peak at the same time, which they do not. The numbers for the example:

| Term | p-p |
|---|---|
| Capacitive $\Delta I/(8fC)$ | 11.6 mV |
| ESR $\Delta I\cdot R_{ESR}$ | 5.1 mV |
| Arithmetic sum (upper bound) | 16.7 mV |
| Actual combined (numerical integration) | 12.3 mV |

> [!NOTE]
> Datasheet-style estimates often just add them: $\Delta V\approx\Delta I\,(R_{ESR}+1/(8fC))$. That is a conservative bound and fine for design margin, but if an interviewer asks for the *true* waveform, say they are 90° apart.

## What it means for capacitor choice

| Type | ESR | Dominant term |
|---|---|---|
| Ceramic X7R | 1 to 5 mΩ | Capacitive (parabolic), plus ESL spikes |
| Polymer / POSCAP | 5 to 20 mΩ | Mixed |
| Aluminium electrolytic | 50 to 500 mΩ | ESR (triangle / "linear") |

These ESR ranges are typical and vary with part, temperature and frequency.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Typical ESR values by capacitor family differ widely by manufacturer, voltage rating and temperature. Treat them as order-of-magnitude, and use the datasheet impedance-versus-frequency curve for a real design.

::: applications
- **Choosing an output capacitor for ripple**: ceramics for the capacitive term, with ESR small enough; electrolytics or polymers if the ESR dominates.
- **Predicting the scope picture**: a triangular ripple means ESR dominates; rounded arcs mean capacitance dominates.
- **Load-step droop estimates**: $\Delta V\approx \Delta I\cdot ESR + \Delta I\,t_{resp}/C$.
- **Processor core rails**: very low target impedance (often around a milliohm or below) met with banks of parallel ceramics and polymers, so both the ripple and the load-step droop stay within a few percent.
- **Damping and loop design**: the ESR zero $1/(2\pi R_{ESR}C)$ sits in the loop gain, and the same ESR shapes the ripple.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Steady-state CCM** with a constant load current.
- **Ideal capacitor plus ESR** and linear capacitance. Ceramic capacitance falls with DC bias, so the effective $C$ may be half the nominal value.
- **Negligible ESL.** With fast edges, ESL adds $L_{ESL}\,di_C/dt$ steps: with 1 nH and a 1.85 A/µs slope the step is $L\,di/dt=1.85$ mV, a visible fraction of the 12 mV ripple, and real edge currents and loop inductances can be larger.
- **Resistive load** gives a slightly different shape (load current ripples with $V_{out}$), a second-order effect here.
- The "linear ramp" reading is valid as an approximation in the ESR-dominated regime or for step loads.
:::

::: followups
### So is the capacitor voltage ever really a linear ramp?
Yes, in the ESR-dominated case (the ESR drop is proportional to $i_C$, which is piecewise linear), and in a load transient where a nearly constant current is drawn from the capacitor, e.g. 1 A from 22 µF gives 45 mV/µs. In steady state with a ceramic capacitor, it's parabolic.

### How would you estimate the ripple with a 100 µF electrolytic with 100 mΩ ESR at the same operating point?
Capacitive: $1.018/(8\times5\times10^5\times100\times10^{-6})=2.5$ mV. ESR: $1.018\times0.1=102$ mV. ESR dominates by 40x, so the ripple is about 100 mV, shaped like a triangle. To cut it you need lower ESR (polymer or ceramic in parallel), not more capacitance.

### Why is the peak-to-peak ripple $\Delta I/(8fC)$ and not $\Delta I/(4fC)$?
Because the capacitor is charged by the area of only *half* of the zero-mean triangle: the positive lobe has height $\Delta I/2$ and width $T/2$, area $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac T2\cdot\tfrac{\Delta I}{2}=\Delta I T/8$. Using the full peak current for half a period would double-count.

### What if the inductor current ripple doubles?
The capacitor current peak doubles and the capacitive ripple doubles (it scales with $\Delta I$). Halving $L$ does that. Ripple $\propto 1/(L C f^2)$ in the capacitive regime, so doubling frequency cuts it by 4.

### Harder: the ripple you measure is 40 mV but the formula says 12 mV. What could cause it?
Capacitance lower than nominal (DC-bias derating, e.g. 22 µF at 3.3 V might be 12 µF), higher ESR (poor part or temperature), ESL or loop-inductance spikes at the edges, a bad probe ground lead picking up switch-node noise, or an inductor that is smaller than assumed (e.g. saturating, giving more ripple current). Re-measure with a spring ground tip across the capacitor terminals.
:::

::: interpretations
"Linear ramp" is taken in the sense of "constant slope". In steady state that is not accurate for the capacitive part, so this page explains why it is parabolic and when it looks like a ramp. The related questions [[q0190]] (when it charges/discharges) and [[q0196]] (what determines its slope) refer to the same $i_C/C$ relation.
