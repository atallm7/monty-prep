::: basics
### In plain words
When you join P-type and N-type silicon, a thin layer near the join empties of free carriers. That layer is the depletion region. It forms because electrons and holes spread toward each other, and then are held back by the fixed charge they leave behind. It is the heart of the diode, since it is the barrier that current must cross.

### Picture it
Picture two crowded rooms with a door in between. When the door opens, people spill into the other room, drawn to where it is less crowded. But the people who left are missing from their original spots, and that leaves a lopsided imbalance near the door that pushes the crowd back. The flow stops when the push-back equals the spreading. The analogy breaks down since the push-back here is electric, from fixed charges.

### Words you need
- **Diffusion**: carriers spreading from where there are many to where there are few. See [[q0062]].
- **Donor and acceptor ions**: the fixed charged atoms left behind when carriers leave. See [[q0065]].
- **Depletion region**: the layer with almost no free carriers, only fixed charge.
- **Built-in potential**: the voltage across the region once balance is reached, about 0.7 V in silicon.
- **Drift**: carriers moving because of an electric field.

### The simplest example
Join P and N silicon. Electrons cross from N to P, and holes cross from P to N, and they cancel when they meet. The N side is left with fixed positive ions and the P side with fixed negative ones. The field from them pushes carriers back. When drift equals diffusion, the region stops growing. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows the numbers for the built-in potential, and how forward and reverse bias shrink or widen the region.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can tell the **sequence of cause and effect** at a PN junction and not just define a term. The interviewer wants to hear:

1. Carriers diffuse because of a concentration difference, not because of any field at first.
2. They leave behind **fixed ionized dopants**, which are uncompensated charge and create an electric field.
3. That field drives drift current opposing diffusion, and the system settles where the two cancel: the **built-in potential**.
4. The region is "depleted" because the field sweeps out any mobile carrier that wanders in.
5. Bonus: bias changes its width, which is the reason diodes behave as they do and why a reverse-biased junction acts as a voltage-dependent capacitor.
:::

::: why
The depletion region is the physical reason a diode rectifies, why a junction has capacitance, why reverse leakage and breakdown exist, and why a MOSFET's drain-to-body junction sets a parasitic capacitance. Varactor diodes, photodiodes, solar cells and the isolation between transistors on a chip all depend on it. In an interview it's usually the follow-up to "how does a diode work?" ([[q0072]]), and a clean explanation of it separates people who have read the diode equation from those who understand where it comes from.
:::

::: concepts
- **Diffusion**: carriers move from high concentration to low concentration, driven by random thermal motion. No field needed.
- **Drift**: carriers move in response to an electric field. Opposes diffusion at a junction. See [[q0068]].
- **Fixed ionized dopants**: after a donor loses its electron it is a fixed $+q$ ion; after an acceptor takes one it is a fixed $-q$ ion. See [[q0062]], [[q0065]].
- **Space charge and Poisson's equation**: charge density produces a field, $dE/dx=\rho/\varepsilon$. A field integrates to a potential.
- **Built-in potential**: $V_{bi}=\frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}$, the potential step across the junction at zero bias.
:::

::: answer
"When you put P-type and N-type silicon together, there's a big imbalance. The N side is full of free electrons and the P side is full of holes. So electrons diffuse from N to P and holes diffuse from P to N, just like a drop of ink spreading, and when they cross they recombine with the other carrier.

What matters is what they leave behind. When an electron leaves the N side, it exposes a fixed positive donor ion. When a hole leaves the P side, it exposes a fixed negative acceptor ion. So near the junction there's a layer with no mobile carriers, only fixed charge: positive on the N side, negative on the P side. That's the depletion region.

That fixed charge creates an electric field pointing from N to P, which pushes electrons back toward N and holes back toward P. That drift opposes the diffusion. It keeps growing until drift exactly balances diffusion, and then there's no net current. The potential across it is the built-in potential, around 0.7 to 0.8 volts in a typical silicon junction. Forward bias shrinks the region, reverse bias widens it, and that width is also why a reverse-biased junction behaves like a capacitor."
:::

::: technical
**Formation.** At the metallurgical junction, $n$ drops from $N_D$ on the N side to $\approx n_i^2/N_A$ on the P side, and $p$ the opposite. The concentration gradient drives diffusion current: electrons N→P, holes P→N. Each carrier that crosses recombines, leaving ionized dopants: $+qN_D$ on the N side and $-qN_A$ on the P side.

**Depletion approximation.** Treat the region as fully depleted of mobile carriers over a width $W=x_n+x_p$, with abrupt edges. Charge neutrality requires equal charge on both sides:
$$N_A\,x_p = N_D\,x_n$$
so the depletion region extends further into the **lighter-doped** side.

**Equilibrium.** The field builds until the drift current cancels diffusion for each carrier type, leaving the built-in potential
$$V_{bi}=\frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}$$

**Width.** Integrating Poisson's equation twice gives, for applied voltage $V$ (positive = forward):
$$W=\sqrt{\frac{2\varepsilon_{Si}\,(V_{bi}-V)}{q}\left(\frac1{N_A}+\frac1{N_D}\right)}$$
$$E_{max}=\frac{qN_Dx_n}{\varepsilon_{Si}}=\frac{2(V_{bi}-V)}{W}$$

**Numbers.** For $N_A=10^{17}$, $N_D=10^{16}$ cm⁻³ (silicon, 300 K, $n_i=10^{10}$ cm⁻³, $\varepsilon_{Si}=11.7\varepsilon_0$): $V_{bi}=0.774$ V, $W=0.33\ \mu$m (0.30 µm of it in the lightly doped N side), $E_{max}\approx4.7\times10^{4}$ V/cm. At 5 V reverse, $W=0.91\ \mu$m and $E_{max}\approx1.3\times10^{5}$ V/cm.

**Capacitance.** The region acts as a parallel-plate capacitor with plate spacing $W$: $C_j=\varepsilon A/W$, so $C_j\propto(V_{bi}-V)^{-1/2}$ for an abrupt junction. At zero bias that is about 31 nF/cm² for the example above.
:::

::: short
"The depletion region forms because electrons diffuse from the N side to the P side and holes go the other way. Each carrier that leaves exposes a fixed ionized dopant, positive donors on the N side and negative acceptors on the P side. That fixed space charge makes an electric field that pushes carriers back, and the field grows until drift balances diffusion. What's left is a carrier-free layer with a built-in potential of about 0.7 volts for silicon. Reverse bias widens it, forward bias narrows it."
:::

::: long
"Think about the moment you join the two materials. The N side has lots of free electrons and almost no holes, and the P side is the opposite. That concentration difference drives diffusion: electrons spread into the P side and holes spread into the N side. When they cross, they find the opposite carrier in the majority and recombine.

The key detail is what they leave behind. The dopant atoms don't move, because they're locked in the lattice. When an electron leaves the N side, the phosphorus donor it came from is now a fixed positive ion. When a hole leaves the P side, the boron acceptor is now a fixed negative ion. So you get a thin layer on each side of the junction with no mobile carriers but with fixed charge, positive on the N side and negative on the P side. That layer is the depletion region.

That charge produces an electric field pointing from N to P. The field pushes electrons back to the N side and holes back to the P side. That's drift current, and it opposes diffusion. As more carriers diffuse, the field gets stronger, until drift and diffusion cancel and the net current is zero. The voltage across the layer at that point is the built-in potential, $V_{bi}=\frac{kT}{q}\ln(N_AN_D/n_i^2)$, which is around 0.7 to 0.8 V for silicon. You can't measure it with a voltmeter because the metal contacts cancel it, but it sets the barrier that forward bias must reduce.

The depletion layer reaches further into the lighter-doped side because the charge on both sides has to match. It's typically a fraction of a micron wide. Under reverse bias the barrier grows and the layer widens, so only tiny leakage flows and the junction behaves like a voltage-dependent capacitor. Under forward bias the barrier shrinks, the layer narrows, and diffusion current rises exponentially. That's the diode."
:::

::: terms
- **Depletion region (space-charge region)**: the layer around a junction that has been swept free of mobile carriers, leaving only fixed ionized dopants.
- **Built-in potential ($V_{bi}$)**: the equilibrium potential step across the junction, $\frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}$.
- **Diffusion current**: current due to a carrier concentration gradient.
- **Drift current**: current due to carriers moving in an electric field; opposes diffusion at equilibrium.
- **Space charge**: net charge density from uncompensated ionized dopants.
- **Depletion approximation**: simplification assuming abrupt edges with zero mobile carriers inside and perfect neutrality outside.
- **Junction capacitance ($C_j$)**: capacitance of the depletion layer, $\varepsilon A/W$, falling with reverse bias.
- **Asymmetric (one-sided) junction**: $N_A\gg N_D$ or the reverse, where the depletion layer lies almost entirely in the lighter side.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it's a region near the junction with no free carriers" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Tells the story in order: concentration gradient, diffusion, exposed ions, field, drift, balance.
- Explains *why* it is called depleted: the field sweeps any carrier out, and the charge there is fixed ions only.
- Notes that it extends further into the lighter-doped side, and says why (charge balance $N_Ax_p=N_Dx_n$).
- Gives numbers: $V_{bi}\approx0.7$-$0.8$ V and a width of the order of 0.1-1 µm.
- Links bias to width and from there to rectification, junction capacitance (varactors) and breakdown.
- Mentions that you can't measure $V_{bi}$ with a voltmeter, because contact potentials at the metal ohmic contacts cancel it.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The depletion region has no charge."** It has no *mobile* charge. It contains fixed ionized dopants, which is where the field comes from.
- **"Electrons cross because of the electric field."** Initial crossing is diffusion. The field appears as a result and opposes it.
- **"Equilibrium means nothing is moving."** Diffusion and drift are both large and cancel exactly. There is no *net* current.
- **"You can measure the built-in potential across the diode with a meter."** You can't. At the metal contacts, equal and opposite contact potentials appear, so the sum around the loop is zero.
- **Assuming the region is symmetric.** It extends mostly into the lightly doped side. In a P+N junction the depletion region is nearly all in the N region.
- **Saying forward bias "removes" the depletion region.** It narrows and lowers the barrier, but at normal forward voltage (0.6-0.7 V) a depletion layer still exists since the applied voltage is below $V_{bi}$.
:::

::: deep
## Step 1: a concentration cliff

At the instant two blocks are joined (a thought experiment, since real junctions are formed by doping a single crystal), the electron density jumps from about $10^{16}$ cm⁻³ in the N region to about $10^{4}$ cm⁻³ in the P region, a factor of $10^{12}$. Random thermal motion makes electrons wander across that boundary even though nothing is pushing them. The same is true for holes in the other direction. The net diffusion current density for electrons is
$$J_n^{diff}=qD_n\frac{dn}{dx}$$
with the diffusion constant $D_n=\mu_n kT/q$ (the Einstein relation; about 35 cm²/s for electrons in silicon at 300 K, from $\mu_n\approx1350$ cm²/V·s and $kT/q=25.85$ mV).

## Step 2: leaving the ions behind

Electrons that cross into the P side find holes in abundance and recombine within a short distance. The same is true for holes entering the N side. What they leave behind is the key.

- On the N side, near the junction: electrons gone, donors $N_D^+$ remain. Charge density $+qN_D$.
- On the P side, near the junction: holes gone, acceptors $N_A^-$ remain. Charge density $-qN_A$.

## Step 3: the field and drift balance

Poisson's equation links charge to field:
$$\frac{dE}{dx}=\frac{\rho(x)}{\varepsilon}$$
Integrating the box-shaped charge gives a triangular field with its peak at the junction. Integrating the field gives the potential step, and the total step is the built-in potential:
$$V_{bi}=\frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}$$
At equilibrium, for each carrier type, drift balances diffusion exactly ($J_n^{drift}+J_n^{diff}=0$), and the Fermi level is flat across the junction. Setting this up is how you derive $V_{bi}$: the bands bend by $qV_{bi}$ to keep the Fermi level constant.

> [!TIP]
> A neat consistency check for any depletion calculation: $V_{bi}-V=\tfrac12E_{max}W$, the area of the triangular field. For the numbers below, $0.5\times4.66\times10^4\ \text{V/cm}\times0.332\times10^{-4}\ \text{cm}=0.774$ V, equal to $V_{bi}$.

## Step 4: sizes and numbers

Silicon, $N_A=10^{17}$, $N_D=10^{16}$ cm⁻³, 300 K:

| Quantity | Zero bias | +0.5 V forward | -5 V reverse |
|---|---|---|---|
| $V_{bi}-V$ | 0.774 V | 0.274 V | 5.774 V |
| Width $W$ | 0.33 µm | 0.20 µm | 0.91 µm |
| $x_n$ (N side) | 0.30 µm | 0.18 µm | 0.82 µm |
| $x_p$ (P side) | 0.030 µm | 0.018 µm | 0.082 µm |
| $E_{max}$ | $4.7\times10^4$ V/cm | $2.8\times10^4$ V/cm | $1.3\times10^5$ V/cm |

The depletion layer lives mainly in the lighter-doped N side ($x_n/x_p=N_A/N_D=10$). Breakdown happens when $E_{max}$ reaches roughly $3\times10^5$ V/cm in silicon (impact ionization; the exact number depends on doping), so lighter doping gives higher breakdown voltage because the same voltage is spread across a wider region.

## Step 5: what changes with bias

- **Forward bias** ($V>0$): the external voltage opposes the built-in field, reducing the barrier to $V_{bi}-V$. The depletion region narrows and diffusion beats drift, so net current flows, growing as $e^{V/V_T}$. See [[q0072]].
- **Reverse bias** ($V<0$): the barrier is $V_{bi}+|V|$, the depletion layer widens and the field rises. Diffusion is choked off, and only the small generation current of thermally generated pairs inside the layer flows (nA or less for small diodes). Depletion width also gives the junction capacitance $C_j=\varepsilon A/W$, which is what a varactor exploits.
- **Breakdown**: at high enough field, carriers gain enough energy to ionize atoms (avalanche) or tunnel across a narrow, heavily doped junction (Zener). See [[q0075]].

## Real hardware consequences

- **Isolation:** on an IC, a reverse-biased PN junction isolates one transistor's well from the substrate, using the depletion region as a non-conducting barrier.
- **Capacitance:** the drain-body and source-body junction capacitances of a MOSFET load the switching node and shrink at higher reverse bias; this is why $C_{oss}$ in power-MOSFET datasheets is quoted versus $V_{DS}$ and drops dramatically at high voltage.
- **Breakdown voltage:** a lighter-doped, wider drift region gives higher breakdown voltage but higher on-resistance, the trade-off at the heart of power-device design.
- **Photodiodes and solar cells:** light creates pairs in or near the depletion region and the built-in field separates them, producing photocurrent.
:::

::: applications
- **Rectifier and signal diodes** (e.g. the 1N4148, 1N4007): one-way conduction from the junction barrier.
- **Varactor diodes** used in VCOs and tuners: reverse bias adjusts depletion width, so capacitance becomes voltage-controlled.
- **Photodiodes and solar cells**: light-generated pairs inside the depletion region are swept out by the built-in field.
- **IC junction isolation and wells**: reverse-biased junctions separate transistors sharing a substrate.
- **MOSFET parasitics**: the drain-body junction gives $C_{oss}$ and the body diode.
- **Zener and avalanche TVS devices**: reverse-biased junctions designed to break down at a defined field.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Abrupt junction and depletion approximation**: mobile carriers are exactly zero inside $W$ and exactly neutral outside. Real edges are blurred over a few Debye lengths, which matters for very lightly doped or very narrow regions.
- **Silicon at 300 K** with $n_i=10^{10}$ cm⁻³ and $\varepsilon_r=11.7$. Other materials change $V_{bi}$ (it scales with bandgap) and widths.
- **One-dimensional, uniform doping**. Real junctions are graded (diffused or implanted), which changes the $C$-$V$ exponent from $-1/2$ toward $-1/3$.
- **Low-level injection and no breakdown** for the bias dependence of $W$ shown here.
- **Alternative answer**: some interviewers want just a qualitative answer ("a region depleted of carriers with a built-in field that stops further diffusion"). Give that first, then add the numbers if asked.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $n_i$ for silicon at 300 K is quoted from about $1.0\times10^{10}$ to $1.5\times10^{10}$ cm⁻³ in different texts, which changes $V_{bi}$ by a few tens of millivolts. Treat the exact $V_{bi}$ as approximate.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the depletion region extend further into the lightly doped side?
Charge on both sides must be equal: $N_Ax_p=N_Dx_n$. A lightly doped side has fewer ions per unit length, so it needs more length to supply the same total charge. For $N_A=10^{17}$ and $N_D=10^{16}$, $x_n$ is ten times $x_p$ (0.30 µm vs 0.03 µm). In a P+N diode the region is almost entirely in the N side.

### What is the built-in potential for a silicon junction doped 10^17 and 10^16, and why can't you measure it with a voltmeter?
$V_{bi}=0.02585\times\ln(10^{17}\times10^{16}/10^{20})=0.774$ V. A voltmeter reading would add contact potentials at the metal-semiconductor contacts around the loop, and in equilibrium the total around any closed loop is zero, so you read nothing. It's a real internal potential step but you can't tap it.

### How does the depletion capacitance change with reverse bias, and what is it used for?
$C_j=\varepsilon A/W$ with $W\propto\sqrt{V_{bi}+V_R}$, so $C_j$ falls as reverse voltage rises. For the example above the width goes from 0.33 µm to 0.91 µm at 5 V, so the capacitance drops by a factor of 2.7. Varactor diodes exploit this to tune VCOs and radio front-ends. Hold the varactor in reverse bias, or the forward conduction swamps the capacitance.

### What happens to the depletion region as you forward bias a diode to 0.7 V?
The barrier $V_{bi}-V$ shrinks, so the region narrows (0.33 µm to about 0.2 µm at 0.5 V in our example). But forward current rises exponentially and the series resistance and high-level injection take over before the applied voltage reaches $V_{bi}$. Real silicon diodes conduct heavily at 0.6-0.7 V, below $V_{bi}$ of 0.7-0.9 V, so there's still a (thin) depletion region.

### Scenario: you need a diode with a higher reverse breakdown voltage. What do you change in the doping and why?
Lower the doping on the lighter side. A lower $N$ means a wider depletion region for the same voltage, so the peak field $E_{max}=2(V_{bi}+V_R)/W$ is smaller, and breakdown (about $3\times10^5$ V/cm in silicon) happens at a higher voltage. The cost is higher series resistance in the wide lightly doped region, which is the reason high-voltage diodes and MOSFETs conduct less well.

### If the junction is at equilibrium and nothing is flowing, why isn't the depletion region collapsing?
It's a dynamic equilibrium. Diffusion still pushes carriers across and drift still pulls them back, and both are large (the current density of each is much greater than the net zero). If the region shrank, diffusion would win, expose more ions and widen it again. If it grew, drift would win and shrink it.
:::

::: interpretations
"Depletion region" refers to the **PN junction depletion layer**. In MOSFET questions ([[q0096]]) the term also appears for the depleted layer under the gate before inversion; the physics is the same (fixed ionized dopants, no mobile carriers), but the field comes from the gate rather than from a junction. This page answers for the PN junction, which is the context of this section, and the formation story differs slightly in a MOS capacitor.
:::
