::: testing
Whether you know the brush material families (carbon/graphite grades, metal-graphite, precious-metal alloys for small motors) and, more importantly, **why** each is chosen: the trade-offs among electrical conductivity, contact voltage drop, current-carrying capacity, friction, hardness and wear, commutation behaviour and cost. The expected insight is that brushes are a compromise: graphite lubricates and tolerates arcing, metal improves conductivity, and the choice depends on current density, voltage and speed.
:::

::: why
Selecting or substituting a brush grade changes motor efficiency, life, noise and commutator wear. A wrong grade can burn a commutator within hours or leave a small, low-voltage motor starved of voltage. When you spec a motor for a product, the brush material is often the main determinant of life and cost ([[q0354]]), and when you diagnose a failing motor ([[q0360]], [[q0361]]), knowing the material tells you what wear and film to expect.
:::

::: concepts
- **Conductor/insulator/semiconductor** and conductivity: copper conducts best; graphite is a conductor with lower conductivity. See [[q0055]].
- **Contact voltage drop** at a sliding interface ([[q0357]]), set by the film and contact area, not bulk resistance.
- **Current density**: A per unit contact area; each grade has a limit.
- **Friction and wear**: graphite is a lamellar solid lubricant.
- **Commutation**: resistive brushes help limit the short-circuit current in the commutated coil.
- **Commutator film**: patina of copper oxide and graphite that makes sliding stable.
:::

::: answer
"Most motor brushes are made of carbon, in practice a carbon-graphite composite. Carbon is chosen because graphite is conductive and also a natural solid lubricant: its layered crystal structure slides easily, so it has low friction and leaves a thin lubricating film on the copper commutator. It's also soft enough not to chew up the commutator, tolerant of a bit of arcing, and slightly resistive, which actually helps commutation by limiting the circulating current in the coil that's being switched.

There are several families. Carbon-graphite and electrographite, which is graphite baked at very high temperature, are the workhorses for general use, with a contact drop of about a volt per brush. Metal-graphite brushes mix copper or silver powder into graphite; they have lower resistance and voltage drop and carry more current, so they're used in low-voltage, high-current motors like car starters. Then in tiny motors, like those in toys, cameras and pagers, you often see precious-metal brushes: thin leaf-spring fingers of silver, gold or palladium alloys, which have very low contact resistance and noise at low current.

The grade is a compromise between conductivity, drop, hardness, friction and wear, and it depends on the current density and speed."
:::

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Resistivities, voltage drops and allowable current densities vary widely between manufacturers and grades. The figures above are typical order-of-magnitude ranges from the general literature; always use the brush maker's grade data for design.

**Why graphite?** Graphite's lattice has weakly bonded layers (basal planes) that slide easily, giving a friction coefficient around 0.1–0.3 in the right environment. This lubrication relies on adsorbed water vapour or other vapour; in very dry, low-pressure or high-altitude conditions ordinary carbon brushes can wear catastrophically ("dusting"), and special grades with additives are used.

**Bulk resistance vs contact drop.** A carbon brush block ($\rho \approx 40\ \mu\Omega\cdot$m, 8 mm long, 63 mm² area) has a bulk resistance of only about 5 mΩ, i.e. 25 mV at 5 A. The ~1 V drop is the **contact interface** (film and tiny contact spots), not the block. Metal in the mix lowers the contact drop and lets the film form differently.

**Sizing by current density.** $A_{brush} = I/J$. For 5 A and $J = 8$ A/cm²: 0.625 cm² = 62.5 mm² (e.g. 7 × 9 mm). With $J = 20$ A/cm² (metal-graphite) it is 25 mm². Large machines use several brushes in parallel.

**Loss comparison** at 5 A: carbon at 1.0 V per brush loses $2\times1.0\times5 = 10$ W; metal-graphite at 0.3 V loses 3 W. At low motor voltage (3 V and 2 A) 0.6 V total from metal-graphite is 20% of the supply; carbon's 2 V would be 67%, i.e. unusable, hence precious metal in small low-voltage motors.

**Commutator side.** The mating surface is copper (sometimes silver-plated in miniature motors) with mica or plastic insulation between segments. The brush and commutator are a tribological pair: the brush grade is tuned to form a stable film on copper.
:::

::: long
"Typically brushes are made from carbon, in practice carbon-graphite composites, and the reason is that graphite is both conductive and a solid lubricant. Its crystal structure is layered, the layers slide over each other easily, so it gives low friction and forms a thin lubricating film on the commutator. It's soft, so it doesn't chew up the copper, and it tolerates the small arcs at commutation. It's also moderately resistive, which actually helps, because it limits the current circulating in the coil that is being switched.

There are a few families. Hard carbon and carbon-graphite are the general-purpose ones for DC and universal motors. Electrographite is carbon heat-treated at very high temperature into graphite; it's softer, smoother and better at commutation, for bigger machines. Then metal-graphite mixes copper or silver powder with graphite: lower resistance, much lower contact drop, say 0.3 volts instead of a volt, and more current per square centimetre, so it's used in low-voltage, high-current things like starter motors. And tiny motors in toys, cameras or pagers use precious-metal brushes, thin fingers of silver, gold or palladium alloys, because at a few volts you can't afford a volt of brush drop.

For sizing, I'd use the current density limit: 5 amps at about 8 amps per square centimetre needs around 60 square millimetres of contact. And I'd note that the voltage drop isn't the block's resistance, which is only milliohms, it's the film and contact interface.

So the grade is a compromise among conductivity and drop, hardness and wear, friction, commutation behaviour and cost, and you'd take it from the manufacturer's grade table for the current density, speed and environment."
:::

::: terms
- **Carbon-graphite**: composite of amorphous carbon and graphite; general-purpose brush material.
- **Electrographite**: carbon heat-treated to a graphite structure; softer, better commutation.
- **Metal-graphite**: brush with copper or silver powder in graphite for low resistance and high current.
- **Precious-metal brush**: thin finger of silver, gold, palladium alloys used in miniature motors.
- **Resin-bonded**: graphite bound with resin; high resistivity.
- **Contact drop**: voltage across the brush-commutator interface.
- **Current density $J$**: A/cm² of contact area.
- **Patina (film)**: stable surface layer on the commutator.
- **Dusting**: rapid brush wear in dry or low-pressure environments.
- **Brush grade**: manufacturer's designation of composition and properties.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying brushes are copper.** Copper brushes exist (in some low-voltage machines) but they are not the norm and cause heavy commutator wear and poor commutation; carbon/graphite dominates.
- **Thinking a more conductive brush is always better.** Low resistance means high circulating current in the commutated coil, sparking and commutator wear; some resistance helps.
- **Treating the voltage drop as the brush's ohmic resistance.** It is mostly a contact phenomenon.
- **Ignoring environment.** Graphite lubrication needs water vapour; in dry or vacuum conditions use special grades.
- **Mixing grades on one machine.** Brushes on the same commutator should be the same grade, otherwise current sharing and film are unbalanced.
- **Quoting material properties as exact.** They vary by manufacturer; use the grade datasheet.
:::

::: deep
## What a brush must do, and why one material can't do it all

A brush runs against a copper commutator and must simultaneously:

1. **Carry current** with low loss.
2. **Slide** with low friction and low wear for thousands of hours.
3. **Commutate**: permit the current in the shorted coil to reverse smoothly, without damaging sparks.
4. **Not destroy the commutator** (so it can't be much harder than copper).
5. **Operate in the real environment**: temperature, humidity, vibration, altitude, contamination.

These conflict. High conductivity (metal) conflicts with good commutation (some resistance needed) and with low wear (metals weld to copper). Graphite gives lubrication and arc tolerance but has poor conductivity. So brush materials are composites, with the mix tuned to the application.

## Carbon and graphite

**Carbon (amorphous) vs graphite (crystalline).** Amorphous carbon is hard and abrasive; it cleans and polishes the commutator and has high resistivity. Graphite is crystalline carbon with layered structure: strong covalent bonds within planes, weak van der Waals bonds between them, so planes shear easily, giving a low friction coefficient. The bulk is a mix: carbon black, coke, and graphite powder bound with pitch or resin and baked ("carbon-graphite"), or the carbon is further heated to ~2500–3000 °C so that it graphitises ("electrographite"). Higher graphite fraction gives lower friction, lower hardness, lower resistivity and better commutation in many cases; higher amorphous carbon gives higher resistance and more cleaning action.

**Lubrication depends on environment.** Graphite lubricates by adsorbed vapours (notably water). In very dry air (humidity below a few g/m³), at high altitude and in vacuum, the friction rises and wear accelerates. Aerospace brushes use grades impregnated with additives (e.g. certain salts or oxides) or use metal-graphite. Contaminants such as silicone vapours and oils can poison the film and cause rapid wear.

## Metal-graphite

Add copper or silver powder, pressed and sintered: copper content from about 10% to 95%. As metal fraction goes up, resistivity and contact drop go down and current density limit goes up, while friction and commutator wear increase and commutation gets harder. These grades suit low-voltage, high-current machines: automotive starters and low-voltage DC motors, where a volt of drop would waste much of the supply. High-metal brushes also work as slip-ring brushes where there is no commutation.

**Example.** A 12 V starter draws hundreds of amps; at carbon's 1 V per brush the loss is huge and the voltage left for the motor is reduced noticeably. Copper-rich brush grades (and large contact area) keep the drop to a few tenths of a volt.

## Precious-metal brushes

Miniature motors (6–7 mm to 16 mm can motors, with low voltage like 1.5–6 V, and currents of tens of mA to a few A) use leaf-spring fingers or multi-finger "brushes" made of silver, gold, palladium or platinum alloys, on commutators of gold-plated or silver-copper segments. Advantages: very low contact resistance and noise at low current and low voltage; spring-like fingers need no separate spring; compact. Drawbacks: higher cost, limited current capacity, more commutator wear in some combinations, and limited life at high speeds. They are common in small coreless motors in cameras, medical devices and precision instruments.

## Choosing a grade: a worked sizing example

A 24 V motor draws 5 A at full load. Choose a carbon-graphite grade with $J_{max} = 8$ A/cm². Required area: $5/8 = 0.625$ cm² = 62.5 mm². A 7 × 9 mm brush (63 mm², 7.9 A/cm²) only just meets the limit with no margin, so choose a slightly larger one (e.g. 8 × 10 mm, 6.25 A/cm²) to allow for start-up peaks and uneven contact. The brush losses: $2\times1.0\times5 = 10$ W out of 120 W input = 8.3%. If we substituted a metal-graphite grade (0.3 V drop): loss 3 W (2.5%), at the price of higher friction and a different film. At 3 V, 2 A (6 W) in a small motor: carbon's 2 V loss isn't possible; metal-graphite's 0.6 V is 20% of the supply, and precious-metal fingers at ~0.1–0.2 V total are the only workable option.

| Case | Grade | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 24 V, 5 A DC tool | Carbon-graphite or electrographite | cheap, good commutation, V drop small relative to 24 V |
| 12 V automotive starter | Copper-rich metal-graphite | very high current, low voltage |
| 3 V, 2 A micro motor | Precious-metal fingers | low voltage, low-noise, miniature |
| 230 V AC universal motor | Carbon/electrographite | high speed, high voltage, arc tolerance |
| Slip rings (no commutation) | Metal-graphite, silver-graphite | low drop, high current, continuous ring |

## How the film and the grade interact

The commutator is copper; after run-in, a thin film of copper oxide and graphite builds up and gives a stable low-friction surface and a repeatable contact drop. The film depends on the brush grade, humidity, current and temperature. If you change grade, expect a run-in period and sometimes a different commutator colour (chocolate-brown is a healthy film; bright copper, black burnt marks or streaks indicate problems; see [[q0359]], [[q0360]]).

## Practical selection notes

1. Take current density and the commutator peripheral speed from the application, then pick a grade family from the manufacturer's chart.
2. Check the voltage drop is acceptable for the motor voltage (low-voltage motors need low-drop grades).
3. Confirm environment: temperature, humidity, altitude/vacuum, contamination (silicones are bad for most carbon grades).
4. Use the same grade on all brushes of the commutator.
5. Brush hardness vs commutator: soft enough to protect it, hard enough to resist wear.
6. For tiny motors rely on the motor manufacturer's design; changing brushes isn't an option ([[q0361]]).

::: applications
- **Power tools and appliances (universal motors)**: carbon or electrographite brushes in plastic holders, sometimes with auto-stop (wear-limit) features.
- **Automotive starters and alternator slip rings**: metal-graphite or copper-graphite brushes with high current capacity.
- **Industrial DC motors and generators**: electrographite or resin-bonded grades, selected to commutator speed and load.
- **Miniature motors in cameras, toys and medical devices**: precious-metal leaf-spring brushes.
- **Wind-turbine and radar slip rings**: silver-graphite or metal-graphite for continuous low-noise contact.
- **Spacecraft mechanisms**: specially formulated brushes for vacuum operation.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Typical brush families and properties** as in the literature; exact data depends on manufacturer and grade.
- **Copper commutator** with carbon-graphite family brushes as the reference; silver or gold commutators pair with precious-metal brushes.
- **Atmospheric operation** (some humidity) for graphite lubrication. Dry, vacuum or contaminated environments change the answer.
- **Current density** and drop figures are typical at nominal speed and temperature; they change with load, speed and temperature.
- **Steady current**; high peak currents (stall or starting) can exceed the density limit briefly.
- Alternative framings: in small motors the "brush" may be a metal leaf spring (copper-beryllium or phosphor bronze with a precious-metal contact), so "what are brushes made of" depends on the motor class.
:::

::: followups
### Why is graphite used rather than a simple metal?
Metals weld and gall against copper and give high friction and commutator wear, and they conduct too well for commutation (large short-circuit current in the commutated coil). Graphite has low friction thanks to its layered structure, forms a lubricating film, tolerates arcing, and its higher resistance helps commutation. Metals are added only where current and drop requirements demand it.

### What does "voltage drop per brush" mean for a small low-voltage motor?
It's a fixed loss in volts, so it's a bigger fraction at low supply voltage. A 3 V motor with a carbon pair dropping 2 V would have just 1 V for the motor. That's why such motors use precious-metal or metal-graphite contacts with total drop under about 0.5 V.

### How would you select a brush for a 5 A, 24 V motor?
Start from the manufacturer's grade chart for the commutator speed and application; pick a carbon-graphite/electrographite grade with allowable $J\approx8$ A/cm² and size the contact at $I/J = 0.625$ cm². Check the voltage drop (≈1 V per brush, 2 V of 24 V is acceptable), the spring pressure and the film behaviour. Then test: look at the commutator colour and brush wear after a run-in.

### What is the problem with using a brush of a much harder material?
It will scour the commutator and cause grooves and rapid wear; a brush should be softer than copper and wear preferentially, because brushes are cheap and replaceable while the commutator is expensive. Hard grades are used in some cases to polish the commutator, but with caution.

### Why might brushes wear out in seconds in a vacuum or at altitude?
Graphite lubrication needs adsorbed water vapour. Without it, friction rises, the film breaks down, and the brush wears extremely fast ("dusting"). Vacuum-rated brushes use molybdenum disulfide or other additives, or the motor is sealed or brushless.

### Can I mix an old brush of one grade with a new one of another?
No. Different grades have different resistivity and drop, so they share current unevenly and form different films, which causes hot-spots, sparking and uneven commutator wear. Replace all brushes on a commutator with the same grade as a set ([[q0361]]).
:::
