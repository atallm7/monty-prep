::: testing
Whether you decouple the capacitor's behaviour from the switch states. The "obvious" answer (charges when the FET is on, discharges when it is off) is wrong. A strong candidate applies KCL at the output node and says: the capacitor charges whenever the inductor current is above the load current and discharges whenever it is below, with timing set by the inductor current's crossing of its average. They then extend the answer to transients and to DCM.
:::

::: why
Knowing *when* the capacitor charges tells you where the output voltage minimum and maximum occur, why ripple is $\Delta I/(8fC)$, how long the capacitor must support the load in a transient, and what happens when the inductor runs empty (DCM). It also connects to debugging: if you probe $V_{out}$ and the ripple peaks line up with the switching edge, you are probably looking at ESL or ESR spikes, not the capacitive ripple.
:::

::: concepts
- **KCL at the output node**: $i_L=i_C+i_{load}$. See [[q0004]].
- **Capacitor equation**: $i_C=C\,dv/dt$; $i_C>0$ means $v_C$ rising. See [[q0025]].
- **Charge balance**: the capacitor charges and discharges equal charge per cycle in steady state.
- **Inductor current crossing**: the capacitor current changes sign when $i_L=I_{out}$. See [[q0187]].
- **Capacitor continuity**: the voltage cannot jump. See [[q0037]].
:::

::: answer
"The capacitor charges whenever the inductor is delivering more current than the load wants, and discharges whenever it's delivering less. That's just KCL at the output node: inductor current equals capacitor current plus load current. So it doesn't line up with the switch being on or off. It lines up with where the inductor current is relative to the load current.

In steady state, the inductor current is a triangle centred on the load current. The capacitor starts charging when the rising inductor current crosses the load current, which is halfway up the on-time ramp, continues charging through the rest of the on time and into the first part of the off time, and then the falling inductor current crosses the load current again, halfway down the off ramp. After that the capacitor discharges until the next crossing. In my example with 12 V to 3.3 V, 500 kHz, 2 A, the capacitor charges for exactly half the period, 1 µs, from 275 ns to 1.275 µs.

During transients it changes: if the load steps up, the inductor current is below the new load for a while, so the capacitor discharges until the inductor catches up. In DCM, the capacitor alone feeds the load whenever the inductor current is zero."
:::

::: technical
**Rule.** $i_C = i_L - i_{load}$. With $i_{load}\approx I_{out}$ (constant): charging iff $i_L>I_{out}$.

**CCM timeline (example).** $D=0.275$, $T=2\ \mu s$, $i_L$ valley 1.49 A at $t=0$, peak 2.51 A at $t=DT=550$ ns.
- $0<t<275$ ns: $i_L<2$ A, discharging, $v_C$ falling to its minimum at $t=275$ ns.
- $275<t<1275$ ns: $i_L>2$ A, charging (275 ns on the on-ramp, then through the first 725 ns of the off-ramp), $v_C$ rising to its maximum at $t=1.275\ \mu s$.
- $1275<t<2000$ ns: discharging.
General: charging from $t_1=DT/2$ to $t_2=DT+(1-D)T/2$, duration $T/2$ for *any* $D$ in CCM.

**Why $T/2$.** $i_L$ is a triangle with a mean of $I_{out}$. The time above its mean equals the time below, since the mean of $i_C$ is zero and the waveform is piecewise linear with these two slopes: the area above equals the area below, and each lobe is a triangle of height $\Delta I/2$ and width $T/2$ (the widths are $DT/2+(1-D)T/2$ on the positive side).

**Charge moved.** $Q=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac T2\cdot\tfrac{\Delta I}2=\tfrac{\Delta I T}{8}=0.254\ \mu C$ each cycle, giving $\Delta V_C=Q/C=11.6$ mV.

**Transients.** Load step up by $\Delta I$ at $t_0$: $i_C = i_L - (I_{out}+\Delta I)<0$, the capacitor discharges at $\approx\Delta I/C$ until $i_L$ reaches the new load. Load step down: the capacitor charges.

**DCM.** While $i_L=0$, $i_C=-i_{load}$: the capacitor discharges at $I_{out}/C$ alone.
:::

::: short
"The capacitor charges when the inductor current is above the load current and discharges when it's below, because the capacitor current is just inductor current minus load current. In steady state that's the top half and bottom half of the inductor triangle, not the on and off states: the capacitor starts charging halfway up the on ramp and stops halfway down the off ramp, and charging lasts exactly half a period. In a load step it discharges until the inductor current catches up."
:::

::: long
"The way to answer this is KCL at the output node. The inductor current comes into the node and goes out as load current and capacitor current. So the capacitor current is the inductor current minus the load current. If that's positive the capacitor is charging, and if it's negative it's discharging. The switch state doesn't enter into the sign directly.

In steady state the inductor current is a triangle whose average is the load current. So the capacitor charges during the part of the triangle above the average and discharges during the part below. In my 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A example the inductor current ramps up from 1.49 A to 2.51 A during the 550 ns on time. It crosses 2 A at 275 ns, so the capacitor is still discharging during the first half of the on state, then starts charging. The charging continues past the switching edge, because the inductor current is still above 2 A, until it falls through 2 A, 725 ns into the off state. Then it discharges for the rest of the period. The two intervals are each exactly half the period, 1 µs.

That also tells you where the voltage extremes are: the minimum at the first crossing, the maximum at the second.

It changes in transients. If the load suddenly asks for more current, the inductor can't follow immediately, so the capacitor supplies the shortfall and discharges until the inductor current catches up. If the load drops, the inductor current exceeds the load for a while and the capacitor charges, giving an overshoot. And in discontinuous mode the capacitor alone feeds the load while the inductor current is zero."
:::

::: terms
- **Charging**: net current into the capacitor, voltage rising ($i_C>0$).
- **Discharging**: net current out of the capacitor, voltage falling ($i_C<0$).
- **Zero crossing of $i_C$**: instant where $i_L=I_{out}$; the capacitor voltage is at a peak or valley.
- **Charge balance**: net charge over a cycle is zero in steady state.
- **Load transient**: sudden change in $I_{out}$; the capacitor covers the difference.
- **DCM**: discontinuous mode; $i_L$ sits at zero for part of the cycle.
- **Overshoot / undershoot**: output deviation after a load release/step.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "It charges when the FET is on and discharges when it's off."

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the rule ($i_L > I_{out}$ ⇒ charging) from KCL and explains it.
- Gives the timeline with numbers and notes that the intervals are $T/2$ each and independent of duty cycle.
- Points out that the voltage extremes occur at the crossings, not the switching edges.
- Adds the transient behaviour (step up ⇒ discharge, step down ⇒ charge) and the DCM case.
- Notes that in practice ESR/ESL add edge-aligned steps to the measured waveform even though the capacitive voltage does not change at the edges.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Tying charge/discharge to the on/off states.** The sign flips mid-ramp.
- **Assuming the capacitor never discharges in steady state.** It discharges for half of every cycle; only the *net* is zero.
- **Thinking the capacitor supplies the load during the off state.** The inductor does; the capacitor only supplies the shortfall when $i_L<I_{out}$.
- **Believing the capacitor charge intervals depend on duty cycle.** The charging duration is $T/2$ for any $D$ (the *position* moves, the duration does not).
- **Forgetting the sign flips in a transient.** After a load step the capacitor can discharge for many cycles.
:::

## The rule: KCL at the output node

At the output node three currents meet: inductor current in, capacitor current into the capacitor, and load current out.

$$i_C=i_L-i_{load}.$$

## Check with a different duty cycle

At $V_{in}=24$ V (D = 0.1375): crossing times become $DT/2=0.1375$ µs and $DT+(1-D)T/2 = 0.275+0.8625 = 1.1375$ µs, still 1 µs apart. The capacitor starts charging at $0.1375\ \mu s$ and stops at $1.1375\ \mu s$. At $D=0.5$ they are at 0.5 µs and 1.5 µs, symmetric around the switching edge.

## Transients: the capacitor covers the gap

A buck cannot change its inductor current faster than $V/L$: 1.85 A/µs rising, 0.70 A/µs falling. If the load jumps from 2 A to 3 A, the inductor is still near 2 A, so the capacitor supplies about 1 A, discharging at $1\ A/22\ \mu F=45$ mV/µs until the inductor has caught up.

## DCM

With a light load, the inductor current hits zero and stays there until the next cycle. During that interval the diode (or a properly-timed low-side FET) is off and the capacitor alone supplies the load: it discharges at $I_{out}/C$.

## Worked transient: the same capacitor, charged and discharged by the load

In steady state the capacitor charges and discharges by equal amounts. The interesting question is what happens when the load breaks that balance. Use the same 12 V to 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 22 µF example and suppose the controller reacts instantly by pinning the duty cycle at its limit (an upper bound on how fast any real loop can be).

**Load step up, 2 A to 3 A.** The inductor can rise at most at $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L=1.85$ A/µs, so the capacitor must supply the shortfall $i_C=i_L-3$, starting from $-1$ A at the step. It reaches zero when the inductor current has risen by 1 A: $t_1=1/1.85=0.54$ µs. The charge removed from the capacitor is the triangle area $\tfrac12\times1\ \text{A}\times0.54\ \mu\text{s}=0.27\ \mu\text{C}$, so the droop is $0.27/22=12.3$ mV, plus $1\ \text{A}\times5\ \text{mΩ}=5$ mV of ESR step at the instant of the step.

**Load step down, 3 A to 2 A.** Now the inductor current must fall by 1 A, but with the switch off the only voltage across the inductor is $-V_{out}$, so the slope is only $-0.70$ A/µs. The capacitor absorbs the excess for $t_2=1/0.70=1.42$ µs. The area is $\tfrac12\times1\times1.42=0.71\ \mu\text{C}$, so the overshoot is $32.4$ mV. The ratio of overshoot to droop is $(V_{in}-V_{out})/V_{out}=2.64$, exactly the ratio of the two slopes.

This asymmetry is a general buck property. The higher the input voltage relative to the output, the worse the load-release overshoot is compared with the load-apply droop, because the inductor can be energized quickly but can only be de-energized through the low output voltage. The same reasoning is why low-voltage, high-current rails (1 V from 12 V) are often limited by overshoot on release, and the inductor value is chosen with this in mind.

> [!NOTE]
> These are ideal lower bounds with an infinitely fast loop and no delay. A real loop with a crossover of a few tens of kilohertz adds several times these values, as in the transient simulation above.

## Asymmetry inside the steady-state cycle

In the steady-state ripple the positive and negative lobes of $i_C$ have equal area, but not equal height or width on each side of the switching edge. The up-slope is steep and short (550 ns) and the down-slope is shallow and long (1.45 µs), and the zero crossings sit at the middle of each. The capacitor therefore charges for exactly 1 µs and discharges for 1 µs (from mid-on-ramp to mid-off-ramp and back), so the rise and fall times of the voltage are equal. What differs is the shape: each arc is built from half of the steep on-ramp and half of the shallow off-ramp, so the arcs are lopsided rather than symmetric parabolas, with the sharper curvature during the on-state.

## Light load: the idle interval

Take the load down to 0.3 A, well below half the 1.02 A ripple. In DCM, the high-side on-time at fixed frequency settles at $D=\sqrt{2LI f V_{out}/(V_{in}(V_{in}-V_{out}))}=0.211$ (422 ns), and the inductor current peaks at $(12-3.3)\times0.422\ \mu\text{s}/4.7\ \mu\text{H}=0.78$ A. The current decays to zero after a further 1.11 µs, which leaves an idle gap of $2-0.42-1.11=0.46$ µs. During that gap the capacitor supplies the full 0.3 A on its own and falls by $0.3\times0.46/22=6.3$ mV, at 13.6 mV/µs. In DCM the capacitor therefore has three phases per cycle: it charges, it discharges while the inductor falls below the load, and it discharges at a constant rate during the idle gap, which is exactly the "linear ramp" that appears only in that segment.

## Charge bookkeeping you can do on a napkin

Per cycle, the charge into the capacitor equals the charge out. In CCM that charge is $Q=\Delta I\,T/8=0.254\ \mu\text{C}$, and the voltage change is $Q/C$. In a step-load event the charge is the area between the inductor current and the new load current. The recipe in both cases is to draw $i_L$, draw the load, and shade the area between them: shaded above the load is charge into the capacitor, shaded below is charge out. The voltage change is the shaded area divided by $C$.

## Verifying on the bench

- Capture $V_{out}$ AC-coupled together with a current probe on the inductor. Place cursors where $i_L$ crosses the load current. The $V_{out}$ extremes should line up with those crossings. This is the cleanest demonstration of the charge/discharge rule.
- Differentiate the $V_{out}$ waveform if the scope has a math channel: $C\,dV/dt$ gives a direct trace of $i_C$ that crosses zero at those same points. Heavy noise makes this hard at small ripple, so use averaging.
- Use a programmable load for the 1 A step. Compare the measured droop and overshoot with 12 mV and 32 mV as a floor. If you measure much more, the loop is the limit, not the physics.

::: applications
- **Locating ripple extremes** when probing and debugging output ripple.
- **Output capacitor sizing** for ripple and for load-step droop.
- **Hold-up and transient design** in processor core rails where the capacitor bank supplies the first microseconds of a step.
- **DCM light-load design** (PFM, burst mode) where the capacitor must carry the load between pulses.
- **Control-loop analysis**: the capacitor charge/discharge dynamics set the output pole.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM, steady state, constant load current** for the timeline.
- **Ideal capacitor** (ESR and ESL change the *measured* waveform but not the charging intervals of the ideal capacitance).
- **Small ripple** so the inductor slopes are constant.
- **Resistive load** adds a small ripple to the load current, shifting the crossings very slightly.
- **Other modes** (DCM, forced-CCM with negative current, PFM) change when the capacitor is the sole source.
:::

::: followups
### Does the capacitor ever charge during the off state?
Yes: from the beginning of the off state until $i_L$ falls to $I_{out}$ (725 ns in the example). It then discharges for the remaining 725 ns of the off state, and for the first 275 ns of the next on state.

### How much charge does it move each cycle?
$Q=\Delta I\,T/8=1.018\times2\ \mu s/8=0.254\ \mu C$, in and out, giving 11.6 mV across 22 µF.

### What happens to the capacitor when the load is suddenly removed?
The inductor current is still 2 A, so all 2 A goes into the capacitor, which initially charges at $2\ \text{A}/22\ \mu\text{F}=91$ mV/µs. Even if the controller holds the high-side FET off immediately, the inductor current ramps down at only 0.70 A/µs, taking about $2/0.70=2.9\ \mu s$ to reach zero, so the capacitor gains $\tfrac12\times2\ A\times2.9\ \mu s=2.9\ \mu C$, about $2.9/22=130$ mV of overshoot (less in practice, since $V_{out}$ rising steepens the down-slope). That is the energy-stored-in-inductor overshoot.

### Is the output capacitor ever the main supply of the load for a full cycle?
Only in light-load modes: in DCM the capacitor alone supplies the load during the zero-current interval, and in burst/PFM mode it supplies the load for many cycles between bursts, discharging slowly from the upper threshold down to the lower.

### Harder: two buck converters, identical except one has twice the inductance. How do the charge/discharge intervals compare?
The same, $T/2$ each, but the current lobes are half as tall, so the charge moved per cycle is half and the ripple halves. The crossing instants are at the same times because the zero crossings depend only on the duty cycle.
:::

::: interpretations
"Charge/discharge" is read as "net current into/out of the capacitor" (voltage rising/falling), not the continuous high-frequency current through its ESR. The question is closely related to [[q0187]] (current directions) and [[q0189]] (voltage shape).
:::
