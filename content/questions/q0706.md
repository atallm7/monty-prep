::: basics
### In plain words
Power sequencing means controlling the order and timing in which a board's supply voltages (called rails) turn on, and turn off. Many chips need several rails, such as one for the core and one for the pins. If the rails come up in the wrong order, the chip can misbehave or be damaged. Sequencing is the plan that prevents that.

### Picture it
Think of getting dressed. Socks go on before shoes. Do it the other way around and the job is awkward, even if both items end up on your feet. Chips are the same: order matters. Some chips do not care; others list the required order in their datasheet.

### Words you need
- **Rail**: one supply voltage line on a board, such as 1.0 V or 3.3 V.
- **Back-powering**: current flowing into an unpowered chip through a driven pin and the chip's protection diodes, which partly powers it in an unplanned state.
- **Power-good**: a signal from a regulator that says its output is ready and within range.
- **Soft-start**: a controlled slow rise of a rail, to limit the surge of current that charges the capacitors.
- **Reset**: holding a chip inactive until its rails and clock are stable.

### The simplest example
Chip A runs from a 3.3 V rail that is already up, and it drives a signal line high. Chip B shares that line, but its own 3.3 V rail is still off. The line pushes current backward through a protection diode (a one-way valve for current) built into B's pin, into B's off rail, partly powering B in a state nobody designed.

The fix is sequencing. Bring B's rail up first, or hold A in reset until B's power-good signal says B is ready.

Ramp speed matters too: charging a 1 mF capacitor to 1 V in 1 ms takes 1 A of current.

That is the whole idea: order and ramp both matter. The rest of this page covers how chips get hurt, the sequencing styles, and power-down.

::: why
Any board with more than one rail (every SoC, FPGA, DDR and mixed-signal design) has a sequencing problem, and ignoring it produces the nastiest kind of failures: boards that work "most of the time", lock up on a slow ramp, draw several amps at start-up, or are damaged after hundreds of power cycles. In an interview it shows whether you read datasheet power-up requirements and understand that rails couple through the chip: ESD structures, substrate and shared references. It also connects to power integrity: inrush current, soft-start and supply droop at turn-on.
:::

::: concepts

- **ESD/protection diodes and parasitics** that conduct when a pin exceeds its rail ([[q0112]]).
- **Inrush current** $I=C\,dV/dt$ into the output and decoupling capacitance ([[q0343]]).
- **Power-good, enable, soft-start and UVLO** control signals of regulators.
- **Supply overshoot and undershoot** at start-up ([[q0151]]).
- **Reset and clocking**: a processor must not run before its rails and clock are stable.

::: answer
"Power sequencing is controlling the order and timing in which a board's supply rails come up, and go down. Many chips need their rails in a defined relationship: core before I/O, say, or analog before digital, with I/O never more than a few hundred millivolts above the core, and a memory termination rail only after its supply. So instead of letting all the regulators start whenever they like, you chain them: the power-good of one enables the next, or a sequencer or PMIC does it, and you hold reset until everything is stable.

It matters for several reasons. Every I/O pin has ESD diodes to its supply, so if a pin is driven while that chip's rail is off, current flows backwards into the rail, partly powering the chip in an undefined state and sometimes triggering latch-up. With the core up and I/O off, or the reverse, outputs may be in undefined states and fight each other on shared buses. Analog references need to be settled before converters run. And from a power-integrity point of view, starting everything at once charges all the output and decoupling capacitors simultaneously: $I=C\,dV/dt$ on a milli-farad of capacitance is an amp, which can trip current limits or collapse the input rail. Staggering the rails and controlling the ramps keeps the inrush manageable. The rules come from each chip's datasheet, and I'd verify the ramps on a scope across temperature."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** Power sequencing = controlling when each rail turns on and off, how fast it ramps and in what relation to other rails. Dimensions: *order* (which first), *timing* (delay or threshold-based), *ramp rate/monotonicity* ($dV/dt$ between datasheet limits), and *relationship during ramp* (maximum difference between rails).

**Styles.**
- *Sequential*: rail B starts after rail A's power-good (PG), plus optional delay.
- *Simultaneous (coincident)*: rails track so $V_B\approx V_A$ until $V_B$ reaches nominal.
- *Ratiometric*: all rails ramp as the same fraction of nominal, reaching it together.
- *Power-down*: normally the reverse order, with controlled discharge.

**Why (physical mechanisms).**
1. *Back-powering through ESD diodes*: a driven pin on an unpowered chip forward-biases the clamp to the (floating) rail, partially powering the chip through its internal load.
2. *Latch-up*: an input above its rail by more than a diode drop can trigger the parasitic thyristor.
3. *Undefined state/contention*: outputs powered before the core is initialised.
4. *Reset and clock*: logic released from reset before rails and oscillator are stable.
5. *Datasheet limits on rail-to-rail differences* (e.g. I/O not above core by more than a stated amount) and on ramp time.
6. *Analog/mixed-signal ordering* (references, PLL, then digital) and memory ordering (VDDQ with VDD, VTT after).
7. *Power integrity*: inrush $I=C\,dV/dt$, source collapse, soft-start, pre-biased outputs and non-monotonic ramps.

**Inrush example.** Core 1.0 V with 1 mF, aux 1.8 V with 100 µF, I/O 3.3 V with 200 µF, all ramped in 1 ms: output currents 1 A, 0.18 A and 0.66 A; charging power at the end of the ramp 1 + 0.32 + 2.18 = 3.5 W; from 12 V at 90% efficiency the input current peaks at 0.32 A *just for charging*. Staggering the three rails limits the peak to 0.20 A.
:::

::: short
"Power sequencing is controlling the order, timing and ramp of the rails on a multi-rail board, up and down. It matters because chips have rules between their rails: ESD diodes back-power an unpowered rail, I/O above core can cause latch-up or contention, resets must release only when everything is stable, and analog and memory rails have ordering. It also matters for power integrity: starting every regulator at once charges all the capacitance together, an inrush of $C\,dV/dt$ that can trip limits. You use power-good chaining, sequencers or a PMIC, and verify on a scope."
:::

::: long
"Power sequencing is controlling the order, timing and ramp shape of a board's supply rails as they come up and go down. A modern chip, an SoC or FPGA, might have a core rail around a volt, an auxiliary rail, several I/O rails and a memory rail, and the datasheet says things like 'core before I/O', 'I/O no more than 0.3 V above core', 'ramp each rail between 0.2 and 50 ms', and 'monotonic'.

The reasons come down to what the silicon does if you ignore it. Every pin has ESD diodes to its supply. If an upstream chip drives a pin while this chip's rail is still off, the diode conducts into that rail and partly powers the chip through its internal circuitry: you get a partial, undefined state, possibly a floating rail that disturbs the regulator's start-up, and in the worst case latch-up. With core and I/O up in the wrong order, logic can be in an undefined state, and outputs can fight each other on a shared bus. A processor released from reset before its clock and rails are stable will boot wrongly. Analog parts need their references settled before conversions start, and DDR needs its supply and termination rails in a given relationship.

There's also a power-integrity side. Each rail has output and decoupling capacitance, and charging it takes $C\,dV/dt$. With a millifarad on the core rail, ramping in a millisecond draws an amp; all the rails together can draw several amps from an input that may not be able to deliver it, so the input dips, the regulators hit UVLO or current limit and the system hiccups. Soft-start and staggered rails prevent that. Non-monotonic ramps from a hiccupping regulator can even re-trigger the chip's power-on reset.

In practice: chain power-good to enable, add an RC delay or use a sequencer or PMIC, hold reset until all rails have been good for a few milliseconds, do the reverse on shutdown and make sure rails discharge, and then check the waveforms across temperature and load."
:::

::: terms
- **Power sequencing**: controlling order, timing and ramp of supply rails.
- **Power-good (PG / PGOOD)**: a regulator output flag indicating the output is within regulation (often open-drain).
- **Enable (EN)**: regulator input that starts or stops it, with a defined threshold.
- **Soft-start**: controlled output ramp that limits inrush current.
- **Ratiometric / tracking**: rails ramp together as a fixed fraction of their final value.
- **Monotonic ramp**: a rise with no dips; non-monotonic ramps can re-trigger power-on reset.
- **Back-powering**: current from a driven pin through ESD clamps into an unpowered rail.
- **UVLO**: under-voltage lockout; a regulator or chip stays off below a threshold.
- **Power-on reset (POR)**: circuit that holds a chip in reset until its rails are valid.
- **Pre-biased output**: a regulator starting into a rail that already holds voltage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "turn rails on in the right order so the chip isn't damaged".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Distinguishes **order**, **timing**, **ramp rate/monotonicity** and the **rail-to-rail voltage difference** during the ramp.
- Explains the mechanisms: ESD diode back-powering, latch-up, undefined states, contention, reset and clock, analog and memory ordering.
- Adds the **power-integrity** view with numbers: inrush $C\,dV/dt$ and the input-source loading ([[q0343]]).
- Names the three styles (sequential, simultaneous, ratiometric) and the power-down order including discharge.
- Gives the practical tools: PG-to-EN chain, supervisors, sequencers/PMICs, soft-start and tracking pins, load switches ([[q0172]]).
- Emphasises *reading the datasheet's power-up requirements* and verifying on a scope across corners.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Assuming rails can come up in any order if they all get there eventually.** Intermediate states matter: it is the transient relationship that can latch up or damage a part.
- **Sequencing only the up-ramp.** On shutdown rails decay at different speeds; I/O can be driven by a part that is still up while another is nearly off.
- **Using a fixed RC delay and calling it robust.** Delay depends on temperature, tolerance and load; threshold-based power-good is robust.
- **Ignoring inrush.** Large decoupling capacitance on a fast soft-start can pull multi-amp spikes from the input and trip protection.
- **Overlooking pre-bias.** A regulator starting into a rail that is already charged may discharge it or ramp non-monotonically ([[q0151]]).
- **Forgetting that external devices drive the chip's pins.** A sequenced board can still be back-powered by a sensor, a cable or another board that is already on.
- **Sequencing by "it works on my bench".** Slow ramps, cold temperatures, battery sag and hot-plug all change the relationships.
:::

## A concrete system

Take a small SoC board with these rails (typical, not a specific part):

| Rail | Voltage | Purpose | Decoupling/bulk (total) |
|---|---|---|---|
| VDD_CORE | 1.0 V | CPU core, large current | 1 mF |
| VDD_AUX | 1.8 V | PLLs, auxiliary, I/O bank | 100 µF |
| VDD_IO | 3.3 V | peripheral I/O | 200 µF |
| VIN | 12 V | source | 100 µF bulk |

Typical datasheet requirements: core and aux valid before I/O; I/O not more than a few hundred mV above core during ramp; each rail monotonic with ramp time between a minimum (to limit inrush) and a maximum (so the chip's POR sees a defined edge); reset held low until all rails have been valid for some milliseconds; power-down in reverse order.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> These requirements are device-specific. The example values (core before I/O, ramp windows and the allowed rail difference) illustrate the kind of rules found in SoC and FPGA datasheets; always take the actual numbers and order from the device's power-sequencing section, which may allow any order or enforce a strict one.

## The mechanisms

**Back-powering and latch-up.** Each I/O pin has a clamp to its supply rail. If a driven signal arrives at the pin while that chip's rail is off, the clamp becomes forward biased and conducts into the rail. With the clamp conducting, the rail floats up to a diode drop below the signal minus the drop across the driver's output resistance, and the chip's internal load consumes the current. A quick estimate with a 3.3 V driver of 50 Ω output resistance and a 330 Ω equivalent load on the unpowered chip gives the rail at about 2.3 V and 6.8 mA flowing ([[q0171]] works this out in detail). At 2.3 V the chip is partially alive, possibly out of reset in an undefined state; the regulator's start-up sees a pre-biased output. If an input pin rises more than a diode drop above its rail while the rails are in transition, a parasitic PNPN structure can latch, shorting the supply to ground until power is removed.

**Undefined state and contention.** With I/O powered and core off, pin buffers may be enabled by default or floating; two such parts on one bus drive against each other. With the core on and I/O off, the chip tries to drive unpowered buffers. Holding the chip in reset (output buffers tristated by the reset state) until all rails are valid avoids both.

**Memory and analog.** DDR needs its main and I/O supply together, then the termination rail and a defined reset hold; analog front ends need their reference stable before the ADC samples; PLLs need clean supplies before the clock is used.

## The power-integrity side: ramps and inrush

Every rail has capacitance $C$ (decoupling plus bulk plus the load's own), and a ramp of $\Delta V$ in time $t_{ss}$ requires an average charging current

$$I_{inrush}=C\frac{\Delta V}{t_{ss}}$$

Using the table: core 1 mF to 1.0 V in 1 ms: 1.0 A; aux 100 µF to 1.8 V: 0.18 A; I/O 200 µF to 3.3 V: 0.66 A. They're drawn from the output of each regulator (which must have a current limit above this plus the load) and, converted with efficiency $\eta$, from the input. The *power* drawn from the 12 V input is $P=V\cdot I$ for each rail, rising as the rail rises: at the end of the ramp the three rails together draw $1.0+0.32+2.18=3.5$ W, or $3.5/(0.9\cdot12)=0.32$ A of input current *only for charging*.

Figure: The area under each curve is the charge needed; staggering doesn't change that, only the peak: here it falls from 0.32 A to 0.20 A. The peak of each ramp occurs at the *end* because $P=VI$ rises with the rail voltage. In a real board the load current adds to this, and an input source with a high impedance (a long cable, a battery near empty, a USB port with a 0.5 A limit) can collapse under the peak and re-trigger UVLO, producing the classic "hiccup" at start-up.

**Choosing the soft-start time.** Given the available current $I_{avail}$ (regulator limit minus the load's start-up draw): $t_{ss}\ge C\Delta V/I_{avail}$. For the core rail with 1 mF, 1 V and a 0.5 A budget: $t_{ss}\ge2$ ms. Many regulators set it with a capacitor on the SS pin or a fixed internal ramp; too slow is also a problem if the chip's datasheet limits the maximum ramp time.

**Pre-bias and monotonicity.** If an output already holds some charge (back-powered through an I/O) a regulator that starts by forcing its output to zero would discharge it and then re-ramp, producing a non-monotonic rail. Look for "pre-bias start-up" or "monotonic start-up" in the regulator's features; a non-monotonic ramp can re-trigger the chip's POR or confuse its on-chip sequencing.

## Power-down

Rails fall at different rates because each has different capacitance and load. A 1 mF rail with a 1 kΩ residual load has $\tau=1$ s, so it stays alive long after a 33 µF rail has collapsed. In that window the surviving rail drives (or is driven by) circuits whose supplies are gone. Remedies: reverse-order enable with PG monitoring, active discharge (a few hundred ohms across the rail at shutdown; with 100 Ω across 1 mF, $\tau=100$ ms), and load switches that disconnect peripherals. Brown-out is the same problem at an unplanned time: when the input sags slowly, UVLO and reset thresholds ensure the chip stops in a defined state.

## How you implement it (brief)

The simplest is PG chaining as in the diagram, adding an RC for extra delay. A voltage supervisor or reset IC holds RESET# for a fixed time after the last PG. A sequencer IC or PMIC handles complex orders with programmable delays; an MCU or CPLD can do arbitrary conditions at the cost of its own boot dependency. Tracking pins implement ratiometric or simultaneous ramps. Details and trade-offs are in [[q0172]].

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Start from the datasheet: "First I'd read the device's power-up requirements: order, ramp limits, allowed rail difference. Then I'd choose the simplest scheme that guarantees them with margin, usually PG chaining plus a reset supervisor, and verify with a scope across temperature and with the slowest and fastest input ramps."
:::

::: applications
- **FPGAs and SoCs** with core, auxiliary, I/O and transceiver rails, each with order and ramp requirements.
- **DDR memory subsystems**: VDD/VDDQ together, then VTT, with reset and clock stable before activity.
- **Mixed-signal boards**: reference and analog supply first, then digital, so converters never see a half-powered state.
- **Motor drivers and power stages**: gate-driver supply and logic up before the power stage is enabled.
- **Hot-plug and battery products**: load switches and soft-start limit inrush when a module is inserted or the battery is connected.
- **Multi-board systems**: backplane or cable connections where one board is powered before the other.
- **Automotive and industrial**: cold-crank and brown-out behaviour; defined shutdown on supply loss.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Datasheet-defined rules.** Some devices tolerate any order; others forbid it. The examples use a typical SoC/FPGA pattern, not a specific part.
- **Capacitance values** are illustrative; real boards vary widely, and the effective value drops with DC bias.
- **Linear ramps** for the inrush estimate: real soft-start ramps are close to linear, but current limits and foldback make them nonlinear.
- **Constant efficiency (90%)** and no load current during the ramp.
- **Single input source** with enough current; real sources have limits, and cable and source impedance lower the input rail further.
- **Resistive discharge** approximations for power-down; real loads are nonlinear (a CPU stops drawing current below its threshold voltage).

Another valid answer, for a battery-powered MCU system with a single rail plus a few load switches, is that sequencing reduces to enabling the peripherals after the MCU is up.
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between sequential, simultaneous and ratiometric start-up?
Sequential: rail B starts only after rail A is valid (PG), so they ramp one after another. Simultaneous (coincident): rail B follows rail A's voltage until it reaches its nominal and then stays there, so the difference never exceeds a set amount. Ratiometric: both ramp so they are always the same fraction of their nominal, finishing together; for $V_A=3.3$ V and $V_B=1.8$ V, $V_B=0.545V_A$ throughout. Choose by what the chip wants: strict order, bounded difference, or tracking.

### How would you limit the inrush current of a rail with 470 µF that must reach 1.2 V?
Use soft-start so the charging current is bounded: $I=C\Delta V/t_{ss}$. With $t_{ss}=2$ ms: $470\text{ µF}\cdot1.2\text{ V}/2\text{ ms}=0.28$ A. If the available current is smaller, lengthen the ramp (a bigger SS capacitor), subject to the device's maximum ramp time. A load switch with slew-rate control does the same for a rail that is switched rather than regulated.

### Why does a board sometimes only fail at power-up with a slow supply (such as a bench supply with a gentle ramp)?
Power-on reset circuits and sequencing assume rails reach thresholds within a window. A slow input ramp can leave one regulator in UVLO hysteresis, start rails at inconsistent times, or let a pre-biased output keep a chip half-awake. Testing needs fast and slow ramps, cold and hot, with and without the input source's current limit.

### Scenario: an FPGA board draws 4 A for a few ms at every power-on and occasionally doesn't boot. What do you check?
Look at the input rail on a scope during power-up: if it dips below the converters' UVLO threshold, they restart (hiccup). Compare inrush with the source limit: the sum of $C\,dV/dt$ for every rail plus the FPGA's configuration current. Fixes: longer or staggered soft-start, enable delays, a bigger input capacitor or lower-impedance source, and a supervisor that holds reset until the input is stable.

### Harder: could a perfectly sequenced board still be back-powered?
Yes. If a connected device (a sensor, a cable to another board, a USB host) drives pins while your board's rails are still off or already off, the protection diodes conduct regardless of your internal sequence. You protect against it with series resistors or isolators, level shifters/switches that are powered-off high-impedance (partial-power-down or "Ioff" parts), and by sequencing the interface's enable with your rails.
:::
