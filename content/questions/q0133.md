::: testing
Three connected questions, answered with numbers:

1. **What is the 3 dB point?** The frequency where the gain has fallen to $1/\sqrt2\approx0.707$ of the passband: half the power. For RC: $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$, where $R=|X_C|$ and the phase is −45°.
2. **What happens after it?** The response keeps falling, asymptotically −20 dB/decade per pole. It's a gradual roll-off, not a brick wall.
3. **How much gets through?** At $f_c$: 70.7% of the voltage, 50% of the power; at $2f_c$: 44.7%; at $10f_c$: about 10%. A great answer also notes how little of the passband is actually "flat".
:::

::: why
Every filter, amplifier and probe is specified by its −3 dB frequency. You'll use it to size anti-aliasing filters, compare op-amps, choose scope probes and decide whether a signal survives a channel. The pitfalls are practical: assuming the signal "stops" at $f_c$ (it doesn't), forgetting that 3 dB is a power statement (a 30% voltage loss at the corner), or ignoring that accuracy-sensitive systems need to stay far below the corner. Being able to quote the percent, dB and phase at a few multiples of $f_c$ shows real fluency.
:::

::: concepts
- **Decibel (voltage vs. power)**: $20\log_{10}$ for voltage ratios, $10\log_{10}$ for power ratios. −3 dB ≈ ×0.707 in voltage, ×0.5 in power. See [[q0129]].
- **Corner frequency**: $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$, where $|X_C|=R$. See [[q0130]].
- **First-order magnitude**: $|H|=1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}$.
- **Phase at the corner**: −45° (low-pass).
- **Roll-off**: −20 dB/decade above the corner. See [[q0131]].
- **Bandwidth**: the span out to the −3 dB point. See [[q0132]].
:::

::: answer
"The 3 dB point is the frequency where the output has fallen to 70.7 percent of its passband value, which is minus 3 dB. At that frequency the output power is half the input power, which is why it's sometimes called the half-power point. For an RC low-pass it's $1/(2\pi RC)$, where the capacitor's reactance equals the resistance, and the phase shift is exactly 45 degrees.

After that point the response keeps falling, but gradually. The asymptotic slope is minus 20 dB per decade for a first-order filter, so at twice the corner frequency you're at about minus 7 dB, 45 percent of the voltage. At ten times you're at minus 20 dB, a tenth of the voltage. It's not a brick wall: a signal just above the corner still passes with most of its amplitude.

So 'how much gets through' depends on where you are: exactly at the corner, 70.7 percent of the voltage and 50 percent of the power. At half the corner it's about 89 percent, and at ten times it's about 10 percent, at a hundred times 1 percent.

One practical point: if I need accuracy, say within 1 percent amplitude, I have to stay below about 0.14 times the corner frequency, so the useful flat region is much smaller than the quoted bandwidth."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** $f_{-3dB}$: $|H(f)|=|H_0|/\sqrt2$, i.e. $20\log_{10}(1/\sqrt2) = -3.01$ dB in voltage (or $10\log_{10}(1/2)=-3.01$ dB in power).

**First-order low-pass.** $|H| = [1+(f/f_c)^2]^{-1/2}$, $\angle H = -\arctan(f/f_c)$, $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$.

| $f/f_c$ | $|H|$ (voltage) | dB | Power fraction | Phase |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.995 | −0.04 | 99.0% | −5.7° |
| 0.5 | 0.894 | −0.97 | 80.0% | −26.6° |
| 1 | 0.707 | −3.01 | 50.0% | −45° |
| 2 | 0.447 | −6.99 | 20.0% | −63.4° |
| 3 | 0.316 | −10.0 | 10.0% | −71.6° |
| 10 | 0.0995 | −20.04 | 0.99% | −84.3° |
| 100 | 0.0100 | −40.00 | 0.01% | −89.4° |

**Beyond the corner.** Local slope $-20x^2/(1+x^2)$ dB/dec ($x = f/f_c$), approaching −20 dB/dec.

**Why 3 dB?** For a first-order system the −3 dB point is where the real and imaginary parts of the denominator are equal ($R=|X_C|$), making $|H|=1/\sqrt2$. Conventionally "half power", which is the generally accepted definition for bandwidth, even though other thresholds are used for particular specs.

**Accuracy limits.** For $|H|\ge 0.99$ (1% amplitude error): $f\le0.1425f_c$. For 0.1%: $f\le 0.0447f_c$. For phase error below 1°: $f\le 0.0175f_c$.

**Multiple stages.** $n$ identical buffered poles: at $f_c$ each, the total is $-3n$ dB (−6 dB for two); the overall −3 dB point moves to $f_c\sqrt{2^{1/n}-1}$ (0.644$f_c$ for $n=2$).
:::

::: long
"The 3 dB point, or cutoff frequency, is where the gain has dropped by 3 dB from its passband value, which is a factor of 0.707 in voltage. Since power goes as voltage squared, that's half the power, which is why it's also called the half-power point. For an RC low-pass, it occurs at $1/(2\pi RC)$, which is where the capacitor's reactance equals the resistance. At that frequency the two voltage drops are equal in magnitude and 90 degrees apart, so the output is 0.707 of the input, lagging by 45 degrees.

What happens after it? The response keeps falling, smoothly. For a first-order filter it approaches minus 20 dB per decade. So it's not that the signal 'stops': at twice the corner the output is down about 7 dB, so 45 percent of the voltage, 20 percent of the power. At three times, about 32 percent of the voltage, minus 10 dB. At ten times, about 10 percent, minus 20 dB, and at a hundred times, 1 percent, minus 40 dB.

So, how much gets through? Right at the corner, 70.7 percent of the voltage and 50 percent of the power. Below the corner nearly all of it: at half the corner 89 percent, at a tenth of the corner 99.5 percent. Above, it's a gradual falloff.

Two practical points. First, the passband isn't really flat out to the corner. If I need less than one percent amplitude error I need to stay below about 14 percent of the corner, and for less than one degree of phase error below about 2 percent. Second, if I cascade filters, the losses add in dB: two identical buffered stages are 6 dB down at the original corner, so the overall 3 dB point moves down to about 0.64 times that frequency."
:::

::: terms
- **−3 dB point (cutoff, corner)**: where gain is $1/\sqrt2$ of passband, i.e. −3.01 dB.
- **Half-power point**: the same frequency, seen as 50% of the power.
- **dB (voltage)**: $20\log_{10}(V_{out}/V_{in})$.
- **dB (power)**: $10\log_{10}(P_{out}/P_{in})$.
- **Passband / stopband**: the region that is passed / attenuated.
- **Roll-off**: how fast gain falls beyond the corner (−20 dB/decade per pole).
- **Phase shift at cutoff**: −45° for a first-order low-pass.
- **Insertion loss**: attenuation in the passband caused by the filter itself.
:::

- Connects to the circuit: **$R=|X_C|$, $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$, −45°**.
- Says clearly that it is a **gradual roll-off, not a cliff**, with a table of percentages at $0.5$, 1, 2, 10 × $f_c$.
- Notes the **passband isn't flat up to $f_c$**: 1% accuracy needs $f<0.14f_c$.

:::

::: mistakes
- **"Half the signal gets through at the cutoff."** Half the *power*; the voltage amplitude is 70.7%.
- **"Nothing gets through above the cutoff."** The signal is attenuated gradually; at $2f_c$ about 45% of the voltage remains.
- **Using 3 dB and 3 as interchangeable in amplitude**: −3 dB is a factor 0.707, not 0.5 (that would be −6 dB) and not 0.3.
- **Saying the phase at the corner is −90° or 0°.** It's −45°.
- **Assuming "flat up to $f_c$".** The response droops gradually: −0.04 dB at 0.1$f_c$, −0.97 dB at 0.5$f_c$.
- **Expecting −20 dB/decade right at $f_c$.** The local slope there is only −10 dB/decade.
- **Cascading two identical sections and still quoting the original $f_c$** as the overall −3 dB frequency.
- **Mixing up 20log and 10log** when converting power vs. voltage dB.
:::

::: deep
## What the −3 dB point means physically

Take the RC low-pass. The same current flows through $R$ and $C$. The resistor's voltage is in phase with the current. The capacitor's voltage lags by 90°, so the two voltages form a right-angled triangle with the input as the hypotenuse:
$$|V_{in}|^2 = |V_R|^2+|V_C|^2.$$
At the corner $|X_C| = R$, so $|V_R| = |V_C|$, and each equals $|V_{in}|/\sqrt2$: the output across $C$ is 0.707 of the input and the current has a phase of 45° relative to $V_{in}$. Because $|V_C|^2$ is half of $|V_{in}|^2$, any resistive load across the output would receive half the power it gets in the passband: that is where "half-power" comes from. (Inside the filter, the resistor's real power equals the capacitor's reactive power at this frequency, since $|V_R| = |V_C|$.)

## Why use −3 dB and not another level?

It's a convention with a good reason. For a first-order system the −3 dB point coincides with $\omega RC=1$, the natural scale of the circuit, and it equals the half-power point, which is physically meaningful (energy-based). Other thresholds appear in some specifications (for example −1 dB, −6 dB, or a 0.1 dB flatness limit), and a higher-order maximally-flat (Butterworth) filter is *defined* to have −3 dB at its design cutoff regardless of order. Always check which convention a datasheet uses.

## The passband is not flat up to the cutoff

If you need accuracy, the useful band is much lower than $f_c$:

| Allowed amplitude error | Max $f/f_c$ |
|---|---|
| 10% (−0.9 dB) | 0.48 |
| 3% | 0.25 |
| 1% | 0.14 |
| 0.1% | 0.045 |

| Allowed phase error | Max $f/f_c$ |
|---|---|
| 5° | 0.087 |
| 1° | 0.017 |

This is why a precision ADC driver or measurement chain is specified with a −3 dB bandwidth many times the signal bandwidth, and why a filter's phase response often matters more than its magnitude for timing-critical signals.

## Multiple stages

Each buffered identical pole contributes −3 dB at $f_c$, so two stages are at −6 dB there and the overall −3 dB frequency moves down to $f_c\sqrt{2^{1/2}-1}=0.644f_c$. With three it's 0.510 $f_c$. If you need a particular overall corner, you have to scale the stage corners up accordingly. Higher-order filter families (Butterworth, Bessel, Chebyshev) are designed so the overall response, not each stage, meets a defined point (−3 dB for Butterworth, or the ripple edge for Chebyshev).

## A power example

A 1 V rms sine through an RC low-pass at its corner, into a 50 Ω measurement: input power $1^2/50=20$ mW. Output voltage 0.707 V rms gives $0.5/50 = 10$ mW. So half of the power is lost, which is $10\log_{10}(0.5)=-3.01$ dB. In a design with a 1 mW signal and a noise floor 40 dB below it, a filter that is exactly 3 dB down at the carrier leaves 0.5 mW of signal, and the signal-to-noise ratio may fall by 3 dB if the noise isn't attenuated equally.

## High-pass and band-pass

For a high-pass the −3 dB point is the lower edge of the passband, and below it the response falls at +20 dB/decade. For a band-pass there are two −3 dB points. The same percentages apply by symmetry: at half the HPF corner, the output is 44.7% of the input, and at twice the corner 89.4%.

## Worked example: finding $f_c$ from measurements that are not at $f_c$

You rarely get to sit exactly on the corner, and the −3 dB point is often awkward to measure directly because the passband level it is referenced to is itself uncertain. If you trust the first-order model, any one point on the curve gives you $f_c$:

$$|H|_{dB}=-10\log_{10}\!\Big[1+\Big(\tfrac{f}{f_c}\Big)^2\Big]\ \Rightarrow\ f_c=\frac{f}{\sqrt{10^{A/10}-1}}$$

where $A$ is the measured attenuation in dB (positive). Suppose a filter measures 1.2 dB down at 2 kHz and 7.9 dB down at 8 kHz.

- From the first point: $10^{0.12}-1=0.318$, so $f/f_c=0.564$ and $f_c=2/0.564=3.55$ kHz.
- From the second: $10^{0.79}-1=5.17$, so $f/f_c=2.27$ and $f_c=8/2.27=3.52$ kHz.

The two estimates agree within 1%, which both gives the answer (about 3.5 kHz) and confirms the response is first order. If they disagreed by 20% you would conclude that the filter has a second pole, a load, or a gain error. The sensitivity is worth noting: a 0.1 dB error in the 1.2 dB reading moves the estimate by about 5% (3.39 kHz for 1.3 dB, 3.73 kHz for 1.1 dB), so points far below the corner give poor accuracy. The best place to measure for a precise $f_c$ is within a factor of two of the corner, where the attenuation is large enough to be measured to a few percent and the model is least sensitive. The phase gives a third check: at 2 kHz the predicted lag is $\arctan(2/3.5)=29.7^\circ$, and at 8 kHz it is $66.4^\circ$.

## Limiting cases and referencing the passband

The "−3 dB" is relative to the *passband* gain, not to unity or to the input. An amplifier with a passband gain of 20 dB (×10) has its −3 dB point where the gain has fallen to 17 dB (×7.07); a filter with 1 dB of insertion loss in the passband has it at −4 dB absolute. Always identify the reference level first, and measure it at a frequency low enough that it is truly in the passband (a decade below the corner for the first-order case, where the droop is only 0.04 dB).

Systems that start above DC need two reference points. A band-pass whose peak is at −0.8 dB (like the overlapping 1 kHz and 10 kHz sections earlier in this section) has its −3 dB edges at −3.8 dB absolute, not at −3 dB. Systems with resonant peaking (low-damping second-order stages) complicate things further, because the −3 dB edge may be defined relative to the peak or to the DC level, so state which.

## Using phase to find the corner on the bench

Because the phase at the corner is exactly −45° for a single pole, you can find $f_c$ without a precise amplitude reference. Put the input on the scope's X channel and the output on Y in XY mode. The trace is an ellipse. The ratio of its y-axis intercept to its maximum y extent equals $\sin\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the phase lag. Tune the generator until that ratio is $0.707$ (45°); you are then at the corner, and the amplitude ratio should read 0.707 as a cross-check. The method is insensitive to the absolute calibration of both channels, but it relies on the two channels having the same delay, so use identical probes and cables. A source impedance (the generator's 50 Ω) adds to $R$ and a scope's input capacitance adds to $C$, so check both against the size of the filter's own components; for a 10 kΩ and 16 nF filter they are negligible, but for a 50 kΩ and 100 pF filter, a 13 pF probe is a 13% change in $C$.

::: applications
- **Anti-aliasing filters**: pick the corner so the signal band is passed within the allowed droop and the Nyquist frequency is attenuated enough.
- **Oscilloscope probes and scopes**: "100 MHz" means −3 dB at 100 MHz, where a sine's amplitude reads 30% low.
- **Op-amp closed-loop bandwidth**: the −3 dB point sets where the gain stage is no longer accurate.
- **Audio crossovers and tone controls**: the corner (−3 dB) frequencies, with −6 dB at the crossover point for two combined stages.
- **RF filters and matching networks**: −3 dB bandwidth quoted for channel selection.
- **Sensor and signal-chain design**: choosing the corner above the signal band with an acceptable amplitude error.
:::

::: assumptions
I used a first-order low-pass with a unity passband gain, driven from a low impedance and unloaded. The percentages in the table are specific to a **first-order** response: higher-order filters have steeper roll-off after the corner, and the response at $f_c$ may differ (a Chebyshev filter's "cutoff" is at the ripple limit, not at −3 dB; a Bessel filter is −3 dB at a frequency that depends on how it is normalized). I assumed the −3 dB convention; some data sheets use −1 dB or −6 dB. The value $1/\sqrt2$ is $-3.0103$ dB, and "3 dB" is a rounded figure; the power ratio at −3 dB exactly is 0.501. For a **high-pass** or **band-pass** the same numbers apply at the relevant edges. Phase numbers are for minimum-phase single-pole filters.
:::

::: followups
### At what frequency is a 1 kHz first-order low-pass down by 1 dB, and by 6 dB?
$|H|=10^{-1/20}=0.891\Rightarrow f/f_c=\sqrt{1/0.891^2-1}=0.508$, so 508 Hz. For −6 dB, $|H|=0.501\Rightarrow f/f_c=\sqrt{1/0.501^2-1}=1.73$, so 1.73 kHz. Notice the −6 dB point is not at $2f_c$ (the asymptote says 2 kHz, but the actual curve is already at −6.99 dB there).

### If a signal contains power at many frequencies, how much total power survives a filter whose −3 dB point is at the signal's highest frequency?
It depends on the spectrum. A component at $f_c$ keeps 50% of its power; components well below keep nearly 100%; components above lose more. For a flat (white) spectrum up to $f_c$, the passed power is the integral of $1/(1+x^2)$ from 0 to 1, which is $\arctan(1)=\pi/4$, so about 78.5% of the power in the band. This is why the bandwidth is not the whole story for signal fidelity.

### Two identical buffered RC low-pass stages each have $f_c=1$ kHz. Where's the overall −3 dB point and what's the attenuation at 1 kHz?
At 1 kHz each stage is −3.01 dB, so −6.02 dB total. The overall −3 dB point is $f_c\sqrt{\sqrt2-1}=0.644$ kHz. If you want a 1 kHz overall corner you'd set each stage to 1/0.644 = 1.55 kHz.

### Why does the phase at the corner equal exactly −45° for an RC filter?
At $f_c$, $\omega RC=1$, so $H=1/(1+j)$, which has magnitude $1/\sqrt2$ and angle $-\arctan(1)=-45^\circ$. Equivalently the reactance equals the resistance, so the current leads $V_{in}$ by 45°, and $V_C$ lags the current by 90°.

### Harder: a 100 MHz scope probes a 100 MHz sine. How accurate is the amplitude, and what would you do about it?
At the −3 dB point the display reads 70.7% of the true amplitude, a 29% error (for a first-order response; real scopes are roughly Gaussian, with a similar −3 dB at their rating). To measure amplitude to within about 3% you need a scope with roughly 4 times the sine's frequency in bandwidth ($f/f_c\le 0.25$). For edge measurements also consider the scope's rise time. Alternatively, de-embed the response or use a spectrum analyzer or RF power meter.
:::

I answered for that case and noted the differences for higher-order, high-pass and band-pass filters. For a related discussion of what bandwidth characterizes, see [[q0132]].
