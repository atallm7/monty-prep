::: testing
Whether you understand that a boost converter does not "make" energy, it **moves** it, and that the trick is an inductor that refuses to let its current change instantly. The interviewer is listening for:

1. A correct one-line definition: a switching DC-DC converter whose output is higher than its input, $V_{out} = V_{in}/(1-D)$.
2. A physical reason the voltage goes *up*, not a memorised formula: store energy in the inductor, then dump it onto the output on top of $V_{in}$.
3. Energy conservation: if voltage goes up, input current must be higher than output current ($I_{in} \approx I_{out}/(1-D)$).
4. The inductor current continuity condition ([[q0043]]) used as the engine of the whole circuit.
:::

::: why
Plenty of products run from a battery that sags below what the electronics need: one Li-ion cell (3.0 to 4.2 V) feeding a 5 V USB or LED rail, two AA cells (about 2 to 3 V) feeding a 3.3 V microcontroller, a solar panel feeding a 12 V bus. A boost converter is the standard answer, and it is usually the first step-up topology a hardware interviewer asks about after the buck ([[q0173]]). It also teaches the idea behind half of power electronics: an inductor plus a switch can make voltages that the source never had. The boost is the best "show me you understand inductor continuity" question there is.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor current continuity**: current through an inductor cannot jump, so if you interrupt it the inductor voltage flips sign and does whatever it takes to keep the current flowing. See [[q0043]].
- **Inductor energy storage**: $E = \tfrac12 L I^2$. Energy goes into the magnetic field while the inductor has voltage across it in the direction of current. See [[q0039]].
- **Duty cycle $D$**: fraction of each switching period the low-side switch is on. See [[q0179]].
- **Volt-second balance**: in steady state the average voltage across an inductor is zero. This gives the gain equation.
- **Energy conservation**: $P_{in} \approx P_{out}$, so a higher output voltage means lower output current than input current. See [[q0154]].
- **Step-up vs step-down**: a passive network (divider, resistor) cannot raise voltage. A switching converter can. Compare [[q0161]].
:::

::: answer
"A boost converter is a switching DC-DC converter that takes a lower input voltage and produces a higher output voltage. It's built from an inductor, a switch, a diode and an output capacitor.

The way it gets the voltage up is that the inductor does the work. While the switch is on, the inductor is connected across the input to ground, so its current ramps up and it stores energy in its magnetic field. Then the switch turns off. The inductor current can't just stop, so the inductor flips its voltage polarity and keeps pushing current, now through the diode into the output. Because the inductor is in series with the input at that point, its voltage adds on top of $V_{in}$, and the output sits above the input.

Averaged over a cycle, the inductor can't gain or lose volt-seconds, which gives $V_{out} = V_{in}/(1-D)$. At 50% duty you get double the input, at 75% you get four times, in the ideal case.

It isn't free energy though. Power in is about equal to power out, so if the output voltage is higher, the input current has to be higher too, by a factor of $1/(1-D)$. That's why boosts get stressed at low $V_{in}$ and high load."
:::

::: technical
**Topology.** Input source in series with an inductor $L$. The far end of $L$ is the switch node (SW). A low-side switch $Q$ connects SW to ground. A diode $D$ (anode at SW) feeds the output capacitor $C_{out}$ and load.

**Switch on ($DT$):** SW is pulled to ground, so $v_L = V_{in}$ and
$$\frac{di_L}{dt} = \frac{V_{in}}{L}$$
$D$ is reverse biased by $V_{out}$. The load is supplied by $C_{out}$ alone.

**Switch off ($(1-D)T$):** $i_L$ must continue, so SW rises until $D$ conducts: $V_{SW} = V_{out} + V_F$, and
$$v_L = V_{in} - V_{out} - V_F < 0,\quad \frac{di_L}{dt} = \frac{V_{in}-V_{out}-V_F}{L}$$
The inductor current falls while it feeds $C_{out}$ and the load.

**Volt-second balance** (steady state, CCM): $V_{in}DT = (V_{out}+V_F-V_{in})(1-D)T$, so
$$V_{out} = \frac{V_{in}}{1-D} - V_F \;\approx\; \frac{V_{in}}{1-D}$$

**Currents.** Average inductor current equals the input current. With $\eta \approx 1$: $V_{in}I_{in} = V_{out}I_{out}$, so $I_L = I_{in} = \dfrac{I_{out}}{1-D}$. Peak-to-peak ripple is $\Delta I_L = \dfrac{V_{in}D}{Lf_{sw}}$.

**Limits.** An ideal boost reaches infinite gain as $D\to1$, but real converters stop around $D\approx 0.8$ to $0.9$ because the off-time becomes too short, losses in $R_{DS(on)}$ and DCR dominate, and the right-half-plane zero ([[q0274]]) hurts the loop. Also note: the diode path means $V_{out} \ge V_{in} - V_F$ even with the switch off, so a boost cannot regulate *below* its input and cannot turn its output off without a load switch.
:::

::: short
"A boost converter is a switching regulator whose output is higher than its input, built from an inductor, a switch, a diode and a capacitor. With the switch on, the inductor charges from the input. When it opens, the inductor current has to keep flowing, so the inductor voltage flips and adds on top of $V_{in}$, pushing current through the diode into the output. Volt-second balance gives $V_{out} = V_{in}/(1-D)$. Energy is conserved, so the input current is higher than the output current by the same factor."
:::

::: long
"A boost converter takes a lower DC voltage and gives you a higher one efficiently. The parts are an inductor in series with the input, a switch from the inductor's far end to ground, a diode to the output, and an output capacitor.

Think of two phases. In phase one the switch is closed. The full input voltage sits across the inductor, so current ramps up at $V_{in}/L$ and energy builds up in the magnetic field, $\tfrac12 L I^2$. The diode is blocked, and the output capacitor alone supplies the load.

In phase two the switch opens. The inductor current can't change instantly, so the inductor voltage flips polarity, the switch node flies up until the diode turns on, and the inductor now delivers its current into the output. Since the inductor is in series with the input, its voltage adds to $V_{in}$, so the output is higher than the input. The current ramps down because the inductor is pushing against a voltage larger than $V_{in}$.

In steady state the average inductor voltage has to be zero, which gives $V_{in}D = (V_{out}-V_{in})(1-D)$ and so $V_{out} = V_{in}/(1-D)$. A controller adjusts $D$ to hold the output at its target.

Two things I always mention. First, conservation of energy: output voltage up means output current down, so input current is $I_{out}/(1-D)$. With a 3.7 V cell making 5 V at 1 A, the battery supplies about 1.35 A plus losses. Second, a boost can only step up. The diode keeps a DC path from input to output, so you can't go below $V_{in}$ and can't disconnect the load without an extra switch."
:::

::: terms
- **Boost (step-up) converter**: a switching DC-DC topology with $V_{out} > V_{in}$.
- **Duty cycle ($D$)**: on-time fraction of the low-side switch. Ideal gain is $1/(1-D)$.
- **Switch node (SW)**: the junction of inductor, switch and diode. It swings between about 0 V and $V_{out}+V_F$.
- **Volt-second balance**: average inductor voltage is zero in steady state. It is how you derive every converter's gain.
- **Flyback (inductive) voltage**: the polarity reversal that happens when inductor current is interrupted. See [[q0276]].
- **CCM / DCM**: continuous or discontinuous conduction mode, depending on whether $i_L$ stays above zero all cycle.
- **Inductor ripple current ($\Delta I_L$)**: peak-to-peak variation of $i_L$, $V_{in}D/(Lf_{sw})$.
- **Non-synchronous / synchronous**: diode versus a second switch for the output rectifier.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it steps up the voltage using an inductor and a switch" and writes $V_{out}=V_{in}/(1-D)$.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains *why* the voltage goes up from inductor continuity and the series connection, not from the formula.
- Derives the gain from volt-second balance in two lines.
- Volunteers energy conservation: $I_{in}=I_{out}/(1-D)$, so input current and inductor stress scale with the boost ratio.
- Notes the practical limit on $D$ and that a boost has no inherent short-circuit protection (the diode path from $V_{in}$ to $V_{out}$ is always there).
- Has a concrete example with numbers (3.7 V to 5 V at 1 A: $D\approx 0.26$, $I_{in}\approx 1.35$ A).
- Compares to a buck in one sentence ([[q0173]]): buck chops the input, boost stores then adds.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The inductor amplifies voltage."** It does not create energy. It temporarily stores it and releases it at a higher voltage and lower current. $P_{in}\approx P_{out}$.
- **"The capacitor is what boosts the voltage."** The capacitor only smooths and holds $V_{out}$. Without the inductor flyback there is nothing to push the output above $V_{in}$. (A charge pump *does* use capacitors to step up. See [[q0284]].)
- **Forgetting the input current.** Input current is *larger* than output current. At $D=0.75$, 1 A out needs 4 A in.
- **Writing $V_{out}=V_{in}\cdot D$** (that's a buck) or $V_{in}/D$. For a boost, $1/(1-D)$.
- **Claiming it works for any $D$ up to 100%.** At $D\to1$ the off-time vanishes and nothing is delivered to the output.
- **Thinking the diode is optional or can face either way.** Anode at SW, cathode at $V_{out}$. Flip it and there is no path for the stored energy.
:::

::: deep
## The core idea: an inductor is a current-continuity machine

A resistor sets current from voltage. An inductor does the reverse: **voltage across it sets how fast its current changes**, $v_L = L\,di/dt$. The corollary is that its current cannot change instantly ([[q0043]]). If you try to interrupt the current, $di/dt$ would be enormous, so the inductor generates whatever voltage it needs, of whatever polarity, to keep the current flowing. This is the inductive kick, and a boost converter is that effect deliberately controlled.

Figure: The inductor sits in *series* with the input and the diode sits in series with the output. The switch node (SW) is where the inductor's far end meets the switch and diode. Notice there is always a DC path from $V_{in}$ through $L$ and $D$ to the output.

**Phase 1, switch on ($0<t<DT$).** SW is shorted to ground, so the inductor has $V_{in}$ across it. The current ramps up linearly with slope $V_{in}/L$. The diode sees $V_{out}$ across it in reverse and blocks. The output capacitor supplies the load. The inductor is *absorbing* energy from the source.

**Phase 2, switch off ($DT<t<T$).** The inductor current is, say, 1.5 A and has nowhere to go through the switch. SW swings up. As soon as $V_{SW}$ exceeds $V_{out}+V_F$ the diode turns on and the inductor current flows into the output node. The inductor voltage is now $V_{in}-V_{SW}$, which is negative: the current *falls*. The inductor is *delivering* energy to the output.

Why is the output higher than the input? Because during phase 2 the inductor is delivering current to a node that is *above* $V_{in}$. The only way a current can flow "uphill" into a higher voltage is if the inductor's own voltage is lifting it, so $v_L = V_{in}-V_{out}<0$ with the current still positive, and that means power is flowing *out of* the inductor into the output. The source in series is helping: the inductor and $V_{in}$ together form the "voltage source" pushing current into $C_{out}$.

## The gain equation

In steady state the current at the start of each period equals the current at the end, so the net change in $i_L$ over a cycle is zero:
$$\frac{V_{in}}{L}DT - \frac{V_{out}+V_F-V_{in}}{L}(1-D)T = 0$$
$$\Rightarrow \; V_{out} = \frac{V_{in}}{1-D} - V_F$$
Equivalently the average inductor voltage is zero. For the ideal case $V_F=0$: $D = 1 - V_{in}/V_{out}$.

## Energy conservation and input current

If the converter has efficiency $\eta$, $V_{in}I_{in}\eta = V_{out}I_{out}$, so
$$I_{in} = \frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{\eta V_{in}} = \frac{I_{out}}{\eta(1-D)}$$
**Worked example:** a single Li-ion cell (take $V_{in}=3.7$ V) powering a 5 V, 1 A load (5 W). Ideal $D = 1-3.7/5 = 0.26$. Input current at 100% efficiency is $5/3.7 = 1.35$ A. At 90% efficiency it is $1.50$ A. The battery sees a 50% bigger current than the load does, and at 3.0 V (almost empty) it is $5/(3.0\times0.9) = 1.85$ A, which stresses a weak cell through its internal resistance. This is why boost converters are sized for the *lowest* input voltage.

## Where the energy goes in one cycle

With $L = 4.7\ \mu$H, $f_{sw}=500$ kHz and the numbers above, the ripple is $\Delta I_L = V_{in}D/(Lf_{sw}) = 3.7\times0.26/(4.7\,\mu\times500\,k) = 0.41$ A, so $i_L$ swings between about 1.15 A and 1.56 A. The load takes $5\text{ W}\times2\ \mu\text{s} = 10\ \mu$J per cycle. Where does it come from?

- During the on-time the source puts $V_{in}I_{in}DT = 2.6\ \mu$J into the inductor. That is the energy the inductor stores, $\tfrac12L(I_{pk}^2-I_{min}^2)=2.6\ \mu$J.
- During the off-time the inductor hands back that 2.6 $\mu$J, **and** the source keeps pushing current through the inductor into the output: $V_{in}I_{in}(1-D)T = 7.4\ \mu$J.

So only about a quarter of the energy is *stored* in the inductor each cycle. The rest flows straight from the source, through the inductor, to the output, which is why a boost behaves like "the source in series with a controlled extra voltage source". This is also why the inductor's DC current (1.35 A) matters far more than its ripple when you check saturation ([[q0046]]). A real design picks $L$ so ripple is roughly 20 to 40% of the average current.

## What a real boost adds

A real device has a switch $R_{DS(on)}$, inductor DCR, diode forward drop ($V_F\approx0.3$ to 0.5 V for a Schottky), and dead-time or overlap effects. The corrected gain with series resistances is lower than $1/(1-D)$, and the shortfall grows rapidly as $D\to1$. That's why controllers cap the maximum duty cycle (commonly in the 85% to 95% range depending on the part) and why the real-world boost ratio is usually limited to around 4 to 5x unless you use a coupled-inductor or multi-stage design.
:::

::: applications
- **Li-ion to 5 V**: one-cell phones, power banks and Bluetooth speakers boost 3.0 to 4.2 V to the USB 5 V rail.
- **LED backlights and flashlight drivers**: boost from a single cell to drive a series string of white LEDs that needs more than the battery voltage.
- **Power-factor correction (PFC)**: the front end of many mains power supplies is a boost running from the rectified line to a roughly 400 V bus.
- **Energy harvesting / solar MPPT**: boost stages lift a low panel voltage and let the controller sweep the operating point for maximum power.
- **Automotive**: start-stop systems use boost-type stages to keep infotainment rails alive when cranking pulls the battery low.
- **Camera flash and e-ink / OLED supplies**: boost to tens of volts from a small battery.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal components** ($V_F=0$, zero $R_{DS(on)}$ and DCR) for the headline $V_{out}=V_{in}/(1-D)$. Real converters fall below it, especially near high $D$.
- **CCM** (inductor current never reaches zero). In DCM the gain depends on load as well as duty cycle and the answer changes ([[q0229]]).
- **Steady state**: output capacitor large enough that $V_{out}$ ripple is small.
- **Non-synchronous** drawn here (diode). A synchronous boost replaces $D$ with a second MOSFET to cut the diode loss, at the cost of needing dead-time control and a way to avoid reverse current in light-load mode.
- An interviewer asking "what is a boost converter?" may also accept the answer "a charge pump" if the context is switched-capacitor circuits, but with inductor and diode specified the answer above is what is wanted. Charge-pump coverage: [[q0284]].
:::

::: followups
### What duty cycle do you need to boost 3.3 V to 12 V?
$D = 1 - V_{in}/V_{out} = 1-3.3/12 = 0.725$ in the ideal case. Real losses mean a bit more, perhaps 0.75 to 0.8. Also check the controller's maximum duty cycle: some parts top out near 90%, which leaves reasonable margin here but not for 3.3 V to 36 V ($D\approx0.91$).

### Why does the input current go up when the output voltage goes up?
Because power is conserved. $P_{out}=V_{out}I_{out}$ is delivered from $P_{in}=V_{in}I_{in}$. If $V_{out}=5$ V and $V_{in}=3.7$ V at 1 A out, then $I_{in}\ge1.35$ A. The inductor is not a transformer, but averaged over a cycle it moves charge at a lower voltage and delivers it at a higher one, so the current has to be higher on the low-voltage side.

### What happens if the switch stays on forever (D = 100%)?
The inductor current ramps until something limits it: the source resistance, $R_{DS(on)}$ and DCR, or the inductor saturates. Nothing is delivered to the output, so $V_{out}$ decays as the load drains $C_{out}$. In a real controller this is the reason for a maximum duty-cycle clamp and a cycle-by-cycle current limit.

### Can a boost converter step down?
No. Even with the switch off, the inductor and diode give a DC path from $V_{in}$ to $V_{out}$, so $V_{out}\approx V_{in}-V_F$ at best (this is "pass-through" or "wire" mode). A buck-boost, SEPIC, or 4-switch buck-boost is needed when the input can be above or below the output, such as a Li-ion cell feeding 3.3 V.

### Scenario: your boost converter output is stuck at about $V_{in}-0.4$ V and never rises. Where would you look?
That is the signature of the converter not switching: the output is just the input passing through the inductor and diode. Check enable, UVLO, the soft-start/fault state, and whether the PWM or the gate drive is reaching $Q$. If the switch node is flat at $V_{in}$ the switch is never turning on. If it switches but the output doesn't rise, suspect a shorted or backwards diode, a saturated inductor, or the switch being too small for the current limit.
:::
