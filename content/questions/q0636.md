::: testing
Whether you can turn "make rework easy" into concrete layout and schematic decisions, and give a reason for each. The interviewer wants more than "leave some space": spacing and keepouts around large parts, thermal reliefs where heat sinking would stop a joint melting, no hidden parts under BGAs, optional footprints and 0 Ω links, test points, clear silkscreen, accessible pads, and awareness of what heavy bottom-side parts do during rework. Bonus for quantifying a trade-off, such as the solder surface-tension holding force on heavy parts.
:::

::: why
Every board you release will be touched by someone with a hot-air nozzle: you in bring-up, a technician on the line, a field-repair shop. A layout that is rework-friendly saves hours per board and protects pads; one that isn't turns a USD 0.10 resistor swap into a scrapped USD 300 board. It also overlaps with DFM and DFT ([[q0613]], [[q0614]], [[q0616]]), so these decisions pay off in yield as well as repair.
:::

::: concepts
- **Hot-air nozzle clearance**: the nozzle needs an air gap around the part so it doesn't heat neighbours or blow them away.
- **Thermal relief on plane-connected pads**: limits heat sinking so the joint can melt. See [[q0538]], [[q0637]].
- **Solder surface tension and part weight**: why heavy bottom-side parts can fall off during reflow.
- **Options and DNP footprints**: a fix becomes a part swap, not a wire. See [[q0634]].
- **Test points and probing access**: help diagnose which part to rework. See [[q0619]].
- **Silkscreen and pin-1 markings**: show polarity and refdes outside the part body.
- **Via-in-pad and fine pitch**: affect how clean pads can be after rework.
:::

::: answer
"I'd treat rework as a user of the board. First, **space**: leave a keepout around large or hidden-joint parts like BGAs and QFNs so a nozzle can sit over them without heating or blowing away neighbours, and keep tall parts like electrolytic caps or connectors away. Second, **heat**: use thermal reliefs on pads tied to big planes so the joint can reach solder temperature, but not on high-current or exposed thermal pads, where I'd rely on preheat. Third, **put the likely changes into the design**: a footprint for an alternate pull-up, zero-ohm links to isolate a rail, and test points near the nets I'd probe. Fourth, **don't hide anything**: no parts or test points under a BGA on the other side where I'd need to reach them, and keep heavy parts off the bottom below a rework area, because they can fall off when the board is reheated. Fifth, **clear markings**: silkscreen pin 1 and polarity outside the part body and reference designators that aren't covered. And for the big parts, I'd avoid via-in-pad or use filled and capped vias so solder doesn't wick away, and I'd keep the footprint to IPC land-pattern recommendations so the rework joint is standard."
:::

::: technical
**Layout rules and the reason behind each.**

| Design choice | Why it helps rework | Typical value (check with your assembler) |
|---|---|---|
| Keepout around BGA/QFN/large parts | Nozzle sits over the part without melting or blowing neighbours | A few millimetres is common; many BGA rework guides suggest several mm, vendor dependent |
| Keep tall parts away from rework areas | Nozzle and shield need clearance | Follow the rework equipment's nozzle size |
| Thermal reliefs on plane-connected signal and ground pins | Joint reaches liquidus without draining heat ([[q0538]]) | Four spokes of about 0.25 to 0.3 mm |
| Solid connection with preheat for power pads | Current capacity and thermal performance win over rework ease | Use a preheater and larger tip |
| Optional footprints and 0 Ω links | Fix = population change | Choose 0402/0603 options |
| Test points near critical nets ([[q0619]]) | Diagnose before rework | Per fixture or probe |
| Pin-1 and polarity marks outside the body | Right orientation after replacement | Silkscreen, avoid pads |
| No parts directly under a BGA on the opposite side | Heat and X-ray access; heavy parts may fall | Avoid or glue |
| Fiducials near fine-pitch parts | Alignment of replacement | 1 mm fiducial per local group |

**Heavy bottom-side parts.** When a top-side BGA is reworked, board heat can remelt bottom-side joints. Surface tension holds the part up; a rough estimate of the holding force is $F \approx \gamma L$ where $\gamma \approx 0.5$ N/m is the surface tension of molten lead-free solder and $L$ is the wetted contact perimeter. Compare with weight $mg$:

| Part | Weight | Holding force | Ratio F/W |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0603 resistor (2 mg, 1.6 mm total) | 0.02 mN | 0.8 mN | about 41 |
| SOIC-8 (75 mg, 3.2 mm total) | 0.74 mN | 1.6 mN | about 2.2 |
| 1 g power inductor (12 mm total) | 9.8 mN | 6.0 mN | about 0.6 |
| 5 g connector (24 mm total) | 49 mN | 12 mN | about 0.24 |

Small passives are safe; heavy parts are not, so keep them off the underside of rework regions or secure them with adhesive.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The holding-force estimate is an order-of-magnitude model: the wetted perimeter, solder volume, joint geometry and board orientation change the real figure. Surface tension of SAC alloys is roughly 0.4 to 0.55 N/m depending on temperature and atmosphere. Assembly houses have their own rules for the maximum weight per pad area; ask yours rather than relying on this estimate.
:::

::: short
"I'd leave keepouts around BGAs and big parts so a nozzle can sit there, keep tall parts away, add thermal reliefs on plane-connected signal pins, and put alternate footprints and zero-ohm links where I expect changes. I'd keep heavy parts off the bottom under rework areas because they can fall during reheat, avoid hiding parts under BGAs, add test points to diagnose faults, and mark pin 1 and polarity clearly."
:::

::: long
"I think of the board as something that will be reworked at least once. So first, I'd leave space: a keepout around big or hidden-joint parts like BGAs and QFNs so a nozzle can sit over them without melting neighbours, and I'd keep tall parts like electrolytic capacitors and connectors a good distance away, because they block the nozzle and shield airflow. How much depends on the assembler's rework equipment, so I'd ask.

Second is heat. A plane next to a pad sinks heat, so I'd use thermal reliefs on ordinary signal and ground pins tied to planes. That limits the heat sinking so the joint reaches solder temperature. For high-current pads, I'd keep a solid connection and plan on preheating the board.

Third, I'd design in options. If a pull-up value or a strap might change, I'd put in a footprint for both and populate one. A zero-ohm link lets me isolate a rail to measure current. Then a fix is a part swap instead of a bodge wire.

Fourth, I'd think about what gets reheated. When you rework a top-side BGA, the bottom side warms too, and a heavy part there can fall off, because surface tension holds only so much. A 1 gram inductor with 12 millimetres of wetted perimeter is held by roughly 6 millinewtons but weighs about 10, so it's marginal. I'd keep heavy parts away from the underside of rework areas or glue them.

Finally, silkscreen: pin 1 and polarity marks outside the part body, and reference designators that don't sit under the part. And test points so I can diagnose the problem before I pick up the hot-air gun."
:::

::: terms
- **Keepout**: area where no other component is placed so tools can reach a part.
- **Thermal relief**: spoked connection from pad to plane that limits heat flow. See [[q0538]].
- **DNP (do not populate)**: footprint present but unpopulated on a given build.
- **Zero-ohm link**: a 0 Ω resistor used as a removable jumper.
- **Hot-air nozzle**: tool tip that directs heated air at a part's footprint.
- **Surface tension (γ)**: force per unit length that holds molten solder and hence the part in place, about 0.5 N/m.
- **Fiducial**: small copper mark used by pick-and-place machines and rework tools to align.
- **Courtyard**: the keepout outline around a component footprint in the ECAD library.
- **Via-in-pad**: via placed in a component pad; solder can wick into it unless it's filled and capped.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "leave space and make it accessible".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Lists **specific** measures and a reason for each: keepouts, thermal reliefs, options and 0 Ω, test points, silkscreen.
- Separates **ordinary pads** (use reliefs) from **power and thermal pads** (solid, plan preheat), noting the trade-off.
- Brings up the **heavy-part-falls-off** issue with a surface-tension estimate.
- Mentions **DFM overlap**: footprints to IPC land patterns, symmetric pads, fiducials.
- Says to **ask the assembler** about their nozzle sizes and keepouts, rather than inventing numbers.
- Treats **options on the board** as the best form of reworkability.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting a big electrolytic next to a BGA.** The nozzle and shield have no room; the cap may also be damaged.
- **Using thermal reliefs on a power MOSFET source pad.** You lose current capacity ([[q0538]]); instead plan preheat.
- **No test points on key rails.** Debugging without access leads to more rework.
- **Hiding parts under BGAs on the underside.** They block X-ray access and may fall off.
- **Silkscreen under parts.** After the part is placed, refdes and pin 1 are hidden.
- **Via-in-pad without fill and cap.** Solder wicks away and joints fail; rework makes it worse.
- **Assuming one rule fits all.** Keepouts and weights depend on your assembler and tools.
:::

::: deep
## Think like the person with the nozzle

Rework is a physical operation done by a person or machine with a tool that has a size, an airflow pattern, and a heat output. So reworkability is mostly geometry and thermal design.

## Thermal design: reliefs where it helps, solid where it must

The heat needed to melt a joint on a plane pad is dominated by the plane. A relief limits that flow ([[q0538]]), at the cost of electrical and thermal resistance. A practical policy:

- **Signal and ground pins on through-hole parts and connectors**: reliefs, so hand rework is possible.
- **Small passives to planes**: reliefs help with tombstoning ([[q0615]]) and rework.
- **Power pads that carry amps, and exposed thermal pads**: solid or well-stitched connection, and rely on bottom preheat and hot air during rework. [[q0637]] works through the numbers.

## Options on the board

The cheapest rework is the one that is only a population change:
- **Footprint pairs** for pull-up/pull-down and strap options, with only one populated.
- **0 Ω links** in series with optional blocks and rails, so you can isolate, measure, or disconnect.
- **Provision for a filter or damping component** on a noisy line (resistor series footprint, capacitor to ground), populated with 0 Ω or DNP.
- **Cut pads**: a thin trace between two pads that is designed to be cut.

Use options on slow nets; on high-speed lines, a footprint stub or 0 Ω link adds capacitance and a discontinuity, so place them carefully or use a controlled solution ([[q0634]]).

## What the board looks like to a heat source

During reflow-based rework of a top-side BGA the board is heated from below to maybe 100 to 150 °C and from above to peak. Parts on the bottom side near that region may see temperatures approaching their joints' melting points. Heavy parts must be held by surface tension, so:

$$F_{hold} \approx \gamma L_{wet}, \qquad F_{hold} > mg \text{ for retention.}$$

With $\gamma \approx 0.5$ N/m: a 0603 resistor (2 mg, about 1.6 mm of wetted contact) has $F = 0.8$ mN against a weight of 0.02 mN, a safety factor of about 41. A 1 g inductor with 12 mm of wetted contact has $F = 6$ mN but weighs $0.001\times9.81 = 9.8$ mN, so the factor is 0.6: it falls. Fixes: keep heavy parts out of the underside of rework regions, use adhesive, or place the part on the same side.

## Pad and via design

- **Footprints to IPC land-pattern guidance** (IPC-7351) so the joint is standard and rework tools have proven settings.
- **Symmetric pads and balanced thermal connections** to reduce tombstoning on replacement.
- **Via-in-pad**: if you must, use filled and capped (planarised) vias; open via-in-pad wicks solder and gives voids or opens, and rework makes it worse.
- **Pad size and trace entry**: a thin trace leaving a pad is the weak point if the pad lifts; widen near the pad where space permits (teardrops).
- **Avoid vias and traces that sit within the nozzle zone without a mask** if solder could flow onto them.

## Marking and documentation

Silkscreen pin 1 and polarity outside the footprint (visible after placement), keep reference designators legible and unobscured, label test points, and provide an assembly drawing with component orientation. Rework personnel work from the drawing and the board; unclear polarity leads to a wrong-way replacement.

## Test and diagnostic access

You can't rework what you can't find. Put test points on rails, resets, clocks (properly, see [[q0623]]), and debug interfaces; provide boundary-scan chain access (JTAG) to find opens under BGAs; and consider ground probe points ([[q0618]]). Diagnose first, then swap the part you proved bad rather than the nearest suspect.

## A checklist you can reuse

1. Nozzle keepout around BGAs, QFNs, big connectors.
2. Tall parts away from fine-pitch rework areas.
3. Reliefs on signal/ground plane pads; solid on power pads with preheat plan.
4. Options (DNP, 0 Ω, alternate footprints) for uncertain circuits.
5. Heavy parts on the top side, not under rework regions.
6. Silkscreen pin 1, polarity, refdes outside the body.
7. Test points and JTAG for diagnosis.
8. Filled/capped via-in-pad only when necessary.
9. Fiducials near fine-pitch parts.
10. Ask the assembler to review.

## Worked example: reviewing a board for rework

Take a 15 mm BGA, a USB connector, and a few 0402 passives on a 1.6 mm, four-layer board. A reviewer walks through four checks.

**1. Nozzle zone.** Suppose the assembler's rework nozzle needs 3 mm of free space around the body. Then the clear zone is $15 + 2\times3 = 21$ mm square, an area of 441 mm². Any part with a body inside that square, tall or short, will see hot air. The reviewer overlays that square on the layout and moves a 0603 decoupling capacitor and a tall tantalum capacitor that are inside it. The passives under the BGA on the back side are allowed, but only if they are light, which is the next check.

**2. Weight on the underside.** The reviewer lists back-side parts below the BGA: two 0402 capacitors (about 0.5 mg each) and one 0.5 g shielded inductor with two pads each 2.5 mm wide, so a wetted perimeter of about $2\times2\times2.5 = 10$ mm. Holding force is $0.5\text{ N/m}\times0.010\text{ m} = 5$ mN against a weight of $0.0005\times9.81 = 4.9$ mN: a safety factor of about 1.0, so the inductor is marginal and would be glued or moved to the top side. The 0402s have a factor above 100 and are fine.

**3. Plane connections.** The USB connector shell has four through-hole tabs tied to ground on four layers. With no relief the tabs are very hard to desolder ([[q0637]]); the reviewer adds four-spoke reliefs to the shell tabs and keeps the signal-pin ground reliefs too. The 5 V input pad of the power switch stays solid for current, with a note that rework needs bottom preheat.

**4. Diagnosis and options.** The reviewer checks that the main rails, the reset and the debug header have test points, that the I²C pull-ups have DNP alternates, and that the pin-1 mark on the BGA is visible outside its body outline.

The output is a short list of changes: two parts moved, one part glued, four reliefs added, five test points. Each is a few minutes of layout work, and each removes a failure mode that otherwise costs hours at the bench or a scrapped board. That asymmetry is the argument for doing a reworkability review before release rather than after the first board comes back damaged.

::: applications
- **Prototype boards** with options for pull-ups, straps, and rail isolation to speed bring-up.
- **Production boards** with BGAs: a defined keepout and rework-friendly neighbours.
- **Power boards**: solid connections on current-carrying pads with plans for preheating during service.
- **Connector-heavy boards**: reliefs on through-hole shells and pins so a connector can be replaced.
- **Field-serviceable products**: modular parts and accessible, labeled test points.
- **Medical/industrial boards**: documented rework limits and clear assembly drawings.
:::

::: assumptions
The numbers (keepout of a few mm, relief spoke widths, weight rules) are typical values and depend on your assembler's rework equipment, board thickness and component mix, so confirm them with the assembly house. The surface-tension holding model is a rough physical estimate. In high-density products (phones, wearables), keepouts are traded away for size; those boards accept lower reworkability and rely on higher first-pass yield and scrap-based repair. In high-reliability products, rework may be restricted or prohibited, and the answer shifts to "design so it doesn't need rework". On boards with only through-hole parts or hand assembly, thermal reliefs and pad size dominate the discussion.
:::

::: followups
### Why is a thermal relief good for rework but sometimes bad for the design?
A relief throttles heat flow from the pad into the plane, so the joint reaches solder temperature and rework is easier. But the spokes add electrical and thermal resistance; four 0.25 mm spokes on 1 oz copper carry roughly 1 A each at about a 20 °C rise by IPC-2221 style estimates, so they limit a power pad. For those I'd use a solid connection and preheat the board during rework. [[q0637]] has the numbers.

### How close can other components be to a BGA?
It depends on the rework equipment and the BGA size. A common practice is to leave a keepout of several millimetres for the nozzle and shield, wider for tall parts. I'd ask the assembler or rework-station vendor for their requirement rather than quote a single number. In dense designs you accept less keepout, but you then protect neighbours with shields and Kapton and accept more risk.

### Why avoid heavy parts on the bottom side?
When a top-side part is reworked, bottom-side joints near it can remelt. Molten solder holds a part by surface tension: roughly $\gamma L$, with $\gamma \approx 0.5$ N/m. A 1 g inductor with 12 mm of wetted perimeter gets about 6 mN of holding force against 9.8 mN of weight, so it can drop off. Small parts are fine, heavy ones are not, so keep them on the top or glue them.

### What options would you put on a new design?
Footprints for both pull-up and pull-down on strap pins, 0 Ω links in series with power rails to subsystems for current measurement, a series resistor and shunt capacitor footprint on noisy or sensitive nets, and a spare footprint for a critical filter. I'd also add a test point on each major rail, reset and clock, and pads for a debug header.

### What if the layout team insists on tight spacing for size?
I'd quantify the trade-off. Tight spacing saves board area, but each rework risks damaging neighbours or scrapping the board. If the product is cost-sensitive and high-volume, I might accept scrap-based repair and rely on good DFM and test to keep yield high. If the board is expensive or low-volume, I'd keep the keepouts. I'd also compromise: keep the keepout on the sides most likely to need tool access and protect the rest with shields and tape.

### Scenario: a board has a QFN power IC with an exposed pad tied solidly to a ground plane. It fails in testing and must be reworked. How do you design for that?
Keep the thermal pad solid because it needs the thermal and electrical path, but design access: a nozzle keepout, no heavy parts on the other side, and stitched vias with a solder-mask plan so paste doesn't wick away. In rework you'd use a bottom preheater to raise the board temperature, hot air at a controlled profile, flux, and then X-ray to verify the exposed pad joint and voiding. I'd also add a thermal and current test to confirm performance after rework.
:::

::: interpretations
"Improve reworkability" can mean making rework physically easier (access, heat), lower risk (less damage), or less needed (options, test). The answer covers all three, with the strongest items being options and test access. Some people would also include design for repair of the finished product, such as modularity and connectors; that is mentioned in applications but beyond board-level layout.
:::
