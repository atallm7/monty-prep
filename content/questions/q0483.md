::: basics
### In plain words
Floorplanning means deciding where each functional block goes on the board before drawing any traces. You group circuits by job, separate the noisy ones from the sensitive ones, put each power supply near the part it feeds, and keep connectors where the enclosure needs them. Most layout trouble is decided at this stage, and clever routing cannot fix a bad floorplan.

### Picture it
Think of zoning a city. You put the noisy factory away from the hospital and the school, you put the power station near the factories it serves, and you let roads follow the flow of goods. Board blocks work the same way. A switching power supply is the factory, a precision measurement circuit is the hospital, and the traces are the roads. The analogy breaks in one place: on a board every "road" also needs a clean return path beside it.

### Words you need
- **Functional partitioning**: grouping circuits by role, such as power, digital, analog and RF ([[q0493]]).
- **Noise source and victim**: a block that gives off interference, and one that suffers from it.
- **Linear regulator**: a simple supply that lowers a voltage by burning the difference as heat.
- **Switching regulator (buck)**: a supply that lowers a voltage by switching rapidly. It wastes little power but is electrically noisy.
- **Thermal resistance**: how many degrees a part heats up per watt it burns, in °C/W ([[q0540]]).

### The simplest example
A linear regulator drops 5 V to 3.3 V at 0.5 A. It burns (5 - 3.3) × 0.5 = 0.85 W. With 60 °C/W of thermal resistance it heats up about 51 °C. A buck converter that is 90% efficient wastes only about 0.18 W, so in the same package it heats up about 11 °C, but it makes electrical noise instead. Either way the choice shapes the floorplan: keep the hot linear part away from a precision voltage reference that drifts with temperature, and keep the noisy buck away from sensitive inputs. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the placement order, supply-to-load distance, return currents and how coupling falls with distance.
:::

::: testing
Whether you do **floorplanning** before routing: you partition the board by function, identify which blocks are noisy, sensitive, hot, fast or connector-bound, and place them to minimise interference and routing length. The interviewer wants a process (block diagram, constraints, floorplan, iterate) with concrete criteria: signal flow, noise sources vs. victims, thermal, power entry and supply-to-load distance, mechanical constraints, assembly and test access. A good answer also mentions return currents and ground partitioning rather than splitting planes.
:::

::: why
Most layout trouble is decided in the first hour. A switching supply next to an ADC input, a crystal far from its MCU, or a connector at the wrong edge can't be fixed by clever routing. Doing the floorplan on a napkin before opening the CAD tool is the single most valuable layout habit, and it's what separates "I routed a board" from "I designed a board". It also underlies power-supply placement ([[q0542]], [[q0543]]) and EMI control ([[q0558]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Functional partitioning**: group circuits by function and noise class. See [[q0493]].
- **Signal flow**: arrange blocks along the path signals take, so nets are short and don't cross.
- **Noise sources vs. victims**: switching nodes, clocks, motor drivers vs. ADCs, references, small-signal sensors. See [[q0558]].
- **Return currents**: each region's return current stays in its own area of the ground plane. See [[q0486]], [[q0496]].
- **Thermal**: spread heat sources; consider airflow, copper and the enclosure. See [[q0540]].
- **Mechanical and manufacturing constraints**: connectors, mounting holes, antenna keep-outs, both-side assembly, test access.
:::

::: answer
"I do it as a floorplan before I route anything, starting from the block diagram.

First I list the hard constraints. Connectors and mounting holes are usually fixed by the enclosure. Antennas want to be at an edge with a keep-out. Hot parts and heat sinks have to be near airflow. Then I group the circuits by role: power entry and conversion, digital core with its memory and clocks, analog front end, RF, motor or high-current stages, and I/O.

The main rule is to separate the noisy from the sensitive and let the signal flow run through the board in a line. So power input and big switching stuff go near the power connector, digital sits in the middle, and the analog front end sits in a quiet corner next to its sensor or connector, away from the switching regulator and the clocks. I put each supply close to the load it feeds, because long power runs mean drop and inductance, and I put decoupling capacitors and crystals right next to the IC they serve.

I keep the ground plane solid and decide where each section's return current flows, rather than cutting it into pieces. And I check practical things: heat, test points, and whether it can be assembled. Then I place, look at the ratsnest for tangles, and move blocks until the nets are short and don't cross."
:::

::: technical
**Process**
1. Block diagram with interfaces and power tree.
2. Tag every block: noise source (switching, clocks, drivers), sensitive (ADC, reference, sensor front end, RF receive), hot, fast/high-speed, connector-bound.
3. Hard constraints from mechanics (enclosure, connectors, mounting holes, height), antenna keep-out, and regulatory (creepage/clearance for isolation).
4. Draw a floorplan: signal flow left to right (or input → process → output), noisy blocks separated from sensitive ones by distance and by plane/return-path partitioning.
5. Place key parts: connectors, main IC, power stages, then decoupling and crystals adjacent to pins.
6. Inspect the ratsnest, iterate, then route per [[q0482]].

**Criteria**

| Criterion | Rule | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Noise vs. sensitive | As far apart as practical; different regions of the plane; no sensitive routing near switch nodes | Coupling falls with distance and with a shared return path |
| Supply to load | Short, wide path; regulator near the load; input cap at the converter | Drop $I R$ and $L\,di/dt$ |
| Power entry | Protection and bulk capacitance at the connector | Surges and ESD are stopped before reaching the board |
| Clocks and crystals | Adjacent to the consumer, away from the board edge and connectors | Short loops, less radiation |
| Heat | Spread dissipation, keep heat-sensitive parts away from heat sources | Temperature rise and reference drift |
| Connectors | Group by function; keep noisy and quiet I/O on separate edges | Cables act as antennas |
| Assembly/test | Parts on as few sides as possible; test points accessible | Cost and bring-up |

**Thermal example.** A linear regulator from 5 V to 3.3 V at 0.5 A dissipates $(5-3.3)\times0.5 = 0.85$ W. With a package-plus-copper thermal resistance of about 60 °C/W the part rises about 51 °C. A buck at 90% efficiency loses $3.3\times0.5\times(1/0.9-1)=0.18$ W in total, about 11 °C at the same $\theta$. So heat from a linear regulator affects where it can sit.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $\theta_{JA}$ values depend heavily on package, copper area and airflow. Use the datasheet's value for a layout like yours.
:::

::: short

Then I let the signal flow run in a line through the board, keep noisy and sensitive blocks apart, put each supply close to its load, decoupling and crystals at their ICs, and keep the ground plane solid. I check the ratsnest and thermals, then route."
:::

::: long
"I treat placement as a floorplanning problem and I do it from the block diagram, before touching routing.

The first step is constraints. Connectors, mounting holes and the board outline usually come from the enclosure, so they're fixed. Antennas need a keep-out at an edge. High-power parts want to be near airflow or a heat sink. Then I classify the circuits: which are noisy, like switching regulators, clock sources and motor drivers; which are sensitive, like ADC inputs, voltage references and sensor front ends; which are hot; and which are high speed.

The layout principle is signal flow. I arrange the blocks so signals travel through the board in a line from input to output, not crossing each other. Power comes in at one connector, goes through protection and the converters, and feeds the digital core. The analog front end goes in a quiet area close to its input connector or sensor, as far as possible from the switching regulator and the clocks.

Two details matter a lot. Each supply goes close to the load it feeds; a long path adds resistance and inductance, and it makes the converter's loop worse. And decoupling capacitors, crystals and their load caps go right next to the pins they serve.

For grounds, I don't cut the plane. I keep it solid and choose where each block sits so its return currents stay in its own area; digital returns shouldn't flow under the analog section.

Finally I check thermal, assembly and test: spread the hot parts, keep sensitive references away from them, keep the parts on as few sides as possible, and make sure there are test points. Then I look at the ratsnest and move blocks until the nets are short and uncrossed."
:::

::: terms
- **Floorplan**: the arrangement of functional blocks on the board before detailed placement.
- **Signal flow**: the path signals take through the system, from input to output.
- **Partitioning**: grouping circuits by function and noise class and assigning areas.
- **Ratsnest**: lines in CAD showing unrouted connections between pads.
- **Keep-out**: area where copper, parts or planes are excluded, e.g. under an antenna.
- **Aggressor / victim**: noise source and the circuit that suffers from it.
- **Power tree**: the hierarchy of regulators and rails.
- **Thermal relief / spreading**: layout techniques that move heat away from hot parts.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer shows a *process* and a set of *reasons*: noise classes, signal flow, supply-to-load distance, return currents, thermal and mechanical constraints. It quantifies one or two things, such as the 51 °C vs 11 °C thermal difference or voltage drop, says "I partition by placement, not by cutting the ground plane," mentions that iteration is normal, and can name what they'd do differently for RF (antenna keep-out) or high voltage (creepage). It also notes that a module or a different part choice can solve a placement problem.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Placing parts where they fit** rather than by function, then routing around the mess.
- **Splitting the ground plane by function** and creating return-path problems. Partition by placement and let the plane stay whole. See [[q0493]].
- **Putting the regulator far from the load** because it's convenient, causing voltage drop and slow transient response.
- **Putting the crystal far from the MCU or near the board edge** where it picks up noise.
- **Placing sensitive analog near the switching regulator** because "it's close to the power input".
- **Ignoring heat:** putting a precision reference next to a hot LDO or MOSFET.
:::

## From block diagram to floorplan

Start with the block diagram of the system and annotate each block with:
- noise it emits (switching frequency, edge rate, peak current),
- noise it tolerates (resolution, bandwidth, gain),
- power dissipation,
- connector or mechanical needs.

## Why separation by distance helps

Capacitive and inductive coupling both fall with distance. For two traces over a plane at height $h$ and spacing $s$, a rough proxy is $k\approx1/(1+(s/h)^2)$, so doubling $s$ from 4$h$ to 8$h$ cuts $k$ from 0.059 to 0.015, about 4×. For loops (switching currents) the magnetic coupling to a nearby loop falls as roughly $1/d^3$ for small loops at distances large compared with their size; this is a standard near-field result, but real boards deviate. The practical point: a few centimetres of separation plus a continuous plane between blocks is far more effective than clever routing next to the aggressor.

## The supply-to-load distance

The converter should sit near the load for three reasons:

1. **Resistive drop:** 100 mm of 0.5 mm × 1 oz trace carrying 1 A drops $1.68\times10^{-8}\times0.1/(0.5\times10^{-3}\times35\times10^{-6})\times1 = 96$ mV, a lot for a 1.0 V core rail.
2. **Inductive drop:** a 50 mm trace with a poorly defined return has on the order of 50 nH (over a solid plane it is nearer 15 nH). A 0.5 A load step in 10 ns gives $L\,\Delta I/\Delta t = 50\text{ nH}\times0.5\text{ A}/10\text{ ns} = 2.5$ V of transient. Local decoupling takes the step, but the longer the feed, the more capacitance you need and the more noise couples.
3. **Control loop:** the regulator senses its output at the feedback point; a long path between that point and the load shifts the regulated voltage by the drop. See [[q0543]] and [[q0544]].

## Thermal placement

A linear regulator dropping 5 V to 3.3 V at 0.5 A dissipates 0.85 W; with $\theta_{JA}=60$ °C/W that's a 51 °C rise. A buck at 90% efficiency loses 0.18 W, about 11 °C. So the LDO needs copper area, airflow or a different location, and anything with temperature-dependent behaviour (voltage references, crystals, thermistor reference networks, ADCs) should not sit next to it. Spread the heat sources; the board's copper spreads heat laterally, so one hot spot has more effect on its neighbours than the same power spread over four parts. See [[q0540]].

## Return currents and ground

Don't cut the ground plane to separate analog from digital. Instead, put the digital circuits in one region and analog in another; the return current of each flows under its own traces in the plane (high-frequency return follows the trace). A solid plane under the boundary between regions is continuous; signals crossing from one region to another have a clean return. Only where a genuine reason exists (isolation, a very noisy motor ground, low-level measurement) do you separate. See [[q0493]] for when and how.

## Practical placement order

1. Connectors and mounting holes (fixed).
2. Main IC(s) near the middle, oriented so most connections face their destinations.
3. Power stages and their input capacitors, compact loops.
4. Decoupling capacitors adjacent to every supply pin.
5. Crystals, oscillators and load capacitors adjacent to the IC.
6. Analog front end near its input connector.
7. Passives, pull-ups, protection and test points.

## Check before routing

- Does any sensitive net have to cross a noisy block?
- Are all high-speed nets short and over solid ground?
- Can the heat get out?
- Can it be assembled, probed and reworked?
- Does each connector have its ESD or filter parts at the connector?

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Mention that you'd draw the floorplan on paper first and review it with whoever owns the enclosure and the firmware. Placement problems are cheapest to fix before any trace exists.

## Worked example: how far is far enough from the buck?

The rule "keep sensitive circuits far from switchers" is more convincing with numbers. Model the buck's hot loop (input capacitor, high-side switch, low-side switch) as a small current loop of area $A_1 = 50\ \text{mm}^2$ (say 10 mm by 5 mm), and the ADC's input loop as a victim of area $A_2 = 20\ \text{mm}^2$, both lying flat on the board. The hot loop's current steps from 0 to 2 A in about 5 ns, so $dI/dt = 4\times10^{8}$ A/s. In the equatorial plane of a small loop the magnetic dipole field is $B \approx \mu_0 m/(4\pi d^3)$ with moment $m = I A_1$, so the open-circuit voltage induced in the victim loop is

$$v = A_2\frac{dB}{dt} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{A_1A_2}{d^3}\,\frac{dI}{dt} = 10^{-7}\,\frac{(50\times10^{-6})(20\times10^{-6})}{d^3}\,(4\times10^{8})$$

| Distance $d$ | Induced voltage |
|---|---|
| 20 mm | 5.0 mV |
| 40 mm | 0.62 mV |
| 80 mm | 78 µV |

Each doubling of distance cuts the pickup by 8 (18 dB), which is the $1/d^3$ near-field law. Put these against a 16-bit, 3.3 V converter whose LSB is $3.3/65536 = 50\ \mu$V: at 20 mm the spike is 100 LSB, at 80 mm about 1.5 LSB. That is a believable reason to put the ADC corner on the opposite side of the board. Caveats matter, though. The dipole formula assumes the distance is large compared with the loop size, so the 20 mm line is only approximate for a 10 mm loop. And a continuous ground plane under both loops reduces the real coupling substantially because the plane carries image currents; the number above is an upper bound for loops with no plane. Also, this is magnetic coupling only; shared impedance in the ground return and capacitive coupling from the switch node add on top. The honest conclusion is the ranking (distance and plane both help), not the millivolts.

## Worked example: what the LDO's heat does to a reference

The page's thermal example gives a linear regulator a 51 °C rise at 0.85 W and 60 °C/W. Heat spreads through the copper, so a neighboring part sees a fraction of it. Suppose a precision 2.5 V reference with a temperature coefficient of 5 ppm/°C (typical for good bandgap references; the datasheet value is what counts) sits where the board warms by 10 °C because of the regulator:

$$\Delta V = 2.5\ \text{V}\times 5\times10^{-6}/{}^\circ\text{C}\times10\ {}^\circ\text{C} = 125\ \mu\text{V}$$

Against the 50 µV LSB of a 16-bit converter with a 3.3 V full scale that is 2.5 LSB of gain drift, enough to ruin a high-resolution measurement. Moving the reference 30 mm away or, better, replacing the LDO with a buck and putting the heat elsewhere, solves it. The corollary is that a 0.85 W linear regulator dumping into a small 4-layer board deserves its own thermal sketch at the floorplan stage, not after the first thermal image.

## Limiting cases: when the floorplan rules bend

- **Small boards.** On a 30 mm by 40 mm board there is no room to put 80 mm between anything. The tools become shielding by plane, a compact hot loop (smaller $A_1$ cuts coupling linearly), a filtered analog supply, and a deliberate layer of ground between the buck's inductor and the sensor. Shrinking the loop from 50 mm² to 10 mm² gives the same pickup reduction as moving the victim from 20 mm to 34 mm.
- **Everything is noisy.** On a motor-controller board the "quiet" region may need to sit on the far side of an isolation barrier. The floorplan then follows creepage and clearance distances before it follows noise.
- **Antenna and RF.** The keep-out beneath and around an antenna is mechanical and non-negotiable; place the RF block first, and fit everything else around it.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **Component height and assembly.** Tall parts (electrolytics, inductors, connectors) near the board edge can collide with the enclosure, and tall parts beside small ones shadow them in reflow. Check the height keep-out map in CAD against the mechanical model before routing.
- **Reflow thermal balance.** A large ground-connected pad next to a small passive can cause tombstoning; the floorplan is the right stage to avoid big thermal asymmetries.
- **Probe and test access.** Reserve places for scope probes and ground springs near each rail and for a programming connector. Bring-up goes much faster when the first thing you do isn't solder wires to vias.
- **Revision margin.** Leave a few millimeters of free area near the sensitive parts for a filter, shield can pads or a zero-ohm option. Shield-can footprints that cost nothing on the first spin are very useful after a failed scan.

## Bench verification

Compare the floorplan to reality with three measurements. First, probe the analog supply and reference with the scope in AC-coupled, 20 mV/div, using a spring-tip ground, and see if the buck's switching frequency shows up as a spike train; then move the probe away from the converter and see the amplitude fall. Second, run the ADC with its input shorted and take an FFT of a few thousand samples: a line at the converter's switching frequency or its harmonics points at the layout, not the firmware. Third, a thermal camera or a thermocouple on the reference and the LDO confirms the temperature gradient you assumed. Fix the biggest coupling first and repeat; the measured improvement tells you which assumption was wrong.

::: applications
- **Battery-powered IoT board**: buck converter beside the battery connector, RF module with antenna at the board edge, sensor front end on the far side from the buck.
- **Motor controller**: gate drivers and MOSFETs near the motor connector with tight loops, current-sense amp near the shunt, MCU on the quiet side.
- **Data acquisition board**: ADC and reference placed next to the analog input connector, digital isolators and MCU on the other side.
- **Single-board computer**: SoC in the centre, DDR adjacent on the same side, power converters near the input and distributed around the SoC for its rails.
- **Audio board**: preamplifier near the input jack; class-D output stage near the speaker terminals; switching supply at the far end.
:::

::: assumptions
- A mixed-signal board with a few power rails, standard multilayer FR-4 with a continuous ground plane.
- Layout conventions apply to boards where the main interferers are switching converters, clocks and drivers. For RF and high-voltage boards, additional rules (antenna keep-outs, creepage) dominate.
- Mechanical constraints (enclosure, connectors) can override ideal placement; then partition using plane stitching and shielding.
- In very dense designs, you can't place by ideal separation; you rely on shielding cans, filtering and careful return-path control.
- Every number here is a first-order estimate.

::: followups
### Why shouldn't I split the ground plane between analog and digital?
Because signals that cross the split lose their return path directly beneath them; the return current detours around the split, creating a large loop that radiates and couples noise. Better: place analog and digital in separate regions over one continuous plane so each block's return current stays under its own traces. Split only for genuine reasons such as isolation. See [[q0493]] and [[q0496]].

### Where do you put the crystal relative to the MCU?
Directly next to the oscillator pins, with short traces and its load capacitors adjacent, over unbroken ground, away from the board edge and noisy traces. A guard ring of ground around it is optional. The loop is small, so it neither radiates nor picks up noise, and the stray capacitance is predictable (it affects load capacitance).

### How would you place a switching regulator relative to an ADC?
As far apart as the board allows, with the regulator near its load and the ADC near its input connector. Ground plane continuous between them; the regulator's input loop (cap, switch, ground) kept compact; no ADC traces running under or next to the switch node or inductor. If space is limited I'd add a filter on the ADC supply and place the ADC reference and its capacitors tight to the part.

### What if the enclosure forces the connector next to your sensitive input?
Then the connector region becomes part of the analog design: put the noisy part of the connector (power, digital I/O) at the other end, add filtering and ESD parts directly at the connector, and route the analog from the connector straight to the front end over a solid plane. If the cable itself is noisy, a shield connection or common-mode choke at the entrance may be needed.

### Scenario: after fabrication, the ADC readings show a 100 kHz tone that matches the buck's switching frequency. What in the floorplan could cause it?
Likely coupling via the shared ground return or by proximity: the buck's switching current loop returns near the ADC, or the ADC reference/analog supply shares a path with the buck's output. I'd check where the buck's input capacitor returns, whether any ADC trace runs near the inductor or switch node, and whether the analog supply is filtered. Fixes: move or rotate parts, tighten the buck loop, add a ferrite and capacitor on the analog supply, or relocate the ADC supply tap.
:::
