::: testing
Whether you can **design**, not just name, a bootstrap gate drive and list what can go wrong. The interviewer is watching how you work:

1. You start from the requirement ($V_{GS}$ must be above threshold *relative to the source*, and the source is the switch node) before drawing a circuit.
2. You produce a working circuit (driver, bootstrap diode and capacitor, supply) and size $C_{boot}$ with a real calculation.
3. You volunteer the considerations: refresh and maximum duty, start-up with a pre-biased output, negative SW swing, dv/dt immunity, dead time, gate resistor trade-offs and layout.
4. You know alternatives (charge pump, isolated supply, PFET) and when each wins.
:::

::: why
Almost every buck, half-bridge motor driver and inverter has this problem. A high-side NFET looks easy until the first prototype: it works at light load, then dies at start-up into a pre-biased output, resets at high duty because the bootstrap cap droops, or fails because the SW pin undershoots below the driver's rating. The interviewer is looking for the checklist of failure modes more than the schematic. It is also the standard "design X" structure ([[q0150]], [[q0263]]): requirements, architecture, calculation, trade-offs, verification.
:::

::: concepts
- **$V_{GS}$ is relative to the source**: for a high-side NFET the source is SW, which swings to $V_{in}$ ([[q0204]]).
- **Bootstrap**: a diode and capacitor form a floating supply that rides on SW.
- **Gate charge**: $Q_g$ sets the capacitor size and the drive energy per cycle ([[q0203]]).
- **Dead time and shoot-through**: a high-side driver must never overlap with the low side ([[q0207]]).
- **dv/dt and negative SW swing**: SW slews at several V/ns and undershoots during dead time ([[q0208]]).
- **Miller plateau and gate resistor**: control switching speed and ringing.
:::

::: answer
"The problem is that an NFET needs its gate about 5 V above its source, and its source is the switch node, which swings up to $V_{in}$. So I need a gate-drive supply that floats with the switch node. The standard answer is a bootstrap: a gate driver whose supply pin is a capacitor $C_{boot}$ connected from the driver's BST pin to the switch node, charged through a Schottky diode from a 5 V or 12 V rail. When the low side is on, SW is near ground and the cap charges to about the rail minus a diode drop. When the high side turns on, SW rises, the cap's top plate rides up with it, and the driver holds the gate at $V_{SW}$ plus about 4.6 V.

To size the cap, I take the gate charge, say 12 nC, divide by the droop I can tolerate, about 0.3 V, which gives about 40 nF, then use 100 nF or more to cover derating and driver quiescent current.

Considerations: the cap only recharges when the low side or its body diode pulls SW low, so there's a minimum off-time and a maximum duty cycle. At start-up into a pre-biased output the cap might never charge. SW undershoots below ground in dead time, so the driver pins need the right rating. dv/dt can false-trigger the driver, so I want good common-mode immunity. A gate or boot resistor controls edge speed. And layout: cap right at the pins. If I needed 100% duty I'd add a charge pump or use a PFET."
:::

::: technical
**Requirement.** $V_{GS,on} \ge$ about 4.5 V for a logic-level NFET (or 10 V for a standard one) while $V_S = V_{SW}\in[0, V_{in}]$. So $V_G$ must reach $V_{in}+V_{GS,on}$ (about 16.5 V for 12 V in).

**Architecture.** Half-bridge driver IC (floating high-side channel) or discrete level shifter, powered from $V_{DRV}$ (5 V or 12 V) through $D_{boot}$ to the BST pin, with $C_{boot}$ from BST to SW.

**Charging.** When SW $\approx0$ V (low side on): $V_{boot}\approx V_{DRV}-V_{f,D}-V_{SW,low}$. For 5 V, 0.4 V Schottky, 30 mV low-side drop (SW sits slightly below ground): about $4.6$ V.

**Sizing $C_{boot}$.** Charge drawn per cycle: $Q_{tot}=Q_g + I_{Q,BST}\,t_{on}+Q_{LS}$ ($Q_{LS}$ is the level shifter charge, $I_{Q,BST}$ the high-side driver leakage). Allowable droop $\Delta V$:
$$C_{boot} \ge \frac{Q_{tot}}{\Delta V}.$$
Worked: $Q_g=12$ nC, $\Delta V=0.3$ V: $C_{boot}\ge40$ nF. Allowing for DC-bias derating of a ceramic (a 100 nF 6.3 V X7R at about 4.6 V may give about 60 nF), use 100 nF: droop $\approx12/60=0.2$ V. Do not oversize: inrush through $D_{boot}$ and charge time grow.

**Refresh / max duty.** Droop with load: $t_{max}\approx C\Delta V/I_{Q,BST}$; for 100 nF, 0.5 V, 60 µA about 0.8 ms of continuous on-time. Normal buck operation refreshes every cycle (low-side on-time $(1-D)T$), but $D_{max}=1-t_{off,min}f_{sw}$ ([[q0204]]).

**Switching edge.** $I_g\approx(V_{DRV}-V_{plateau})/R_{g,tot}$, $t_{sw}\approx Q_{gd}/I_g$. With 5 V drive, 3 V plateau, 4 Ω total and $Q_{gd}=3$ nC: $I_g=0.5$ A, $t\approx6$ ns, dv/dt about $2$ V/ns for 12 V.

> [!WARNING]
> Gate voltage rating check: $V_{boot}$ can exceed $V_{DRV}-V_f$ when SW undershoots (by $|V_{SW,min}|$). With 12 V drive and 3 V undershoot: about 14.6 V, still under a typical 20 V $V_{GS}$ limit, but verify with a scope.
:::

::: long
"Let me start from the requirement. An NFET needs $V_{GS}$ of maybe 4.5 V to be fully on. On the high side its source is the switch node, which sits at $V_{in}$ when it's on. So the gate has to be about $V_{in}$ plus 5 V, 17 V for a 12 V rail. You can't get that from a ground-referenced 5 V supply, so I need a supply that floats with the switch node.

The standard solution is a bootstrap. I'd use a half-bridge gate-driver IC. Its high-side channel runs from a BST pin to the SW pin. I put a capacitor, $C_{boot}$, between BST and SW, and a Schottky diode from the 5 V supply to BST. When the low side is on, SW is at ground and the diode charges the cap to about 4.6 V. When the high side turns on, SW rises toward $V_{in}$, the diode reverse biases, and the capacitor floats up with the node, so the driver can hold the gate 4.6 V above the source.

Sizing: the cap supplies the gate charge each cycle, so $C\ge Q_g/\Delta V$. With 12 nC and 0.3 V droop that's 40 nF, and I'd use 100 nF or more to cover ceramic derating and the driver's own quiescent current.

Now the considerations. One: it only recharges when SW goes low, so there's a minimum off-time and a maximum duty, 90 to 95% at hundreds of kilohertz. For 100% duty I need a charge pump or a PFET. Two: start-up into a pre-biased output, because if the low side can't turn on the cap isn't charged, so the controller does a precharge pulse. Three: SW goes below ground during dead time, so I need the driver's SW pin rated for that, and I may add a resistor in series with the diode. Four: dv/dt on SW can false-trigger the level shifter, so I'd pick a driver with good common-mode transient immunity. Five: a gate resistor, or a boot resistor to slow the turn-on only, trades EMI for switching loss. Six: dead time against the low side. And layout: cap right at the pins."
:::

::: terms
- **Bootstrap circuit**: diode plus capacitor creating a floating supply referenced to SW.
- **BST / VB pin**: the driver's high-side supply pin, at $V_{SW}+V_{boot}$.
- **Level shifter**: moves the ground-referenced PWM signal to the floating high-side domain.
- **$Q_g$**: total gate charge to reach the on-state $V_{GS}$.
- **Miller plateau**: the flat part of $V_{GS}$ during the $V_{DS}$ transition, set by $Q_{gd}$.
- **UVLO (undervoltage lockout)**: the high-side driver stays off if $V_{boot}$ is too low.
- **CMTI**: common-mode transient immunity, the dv/dt a driver tolerates without false switching.
- **Charge pump**: switched-capacitor circuit that creates a supply above $V_{in}$ even at 100% duty.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Use a bootstrap capacitor and diode and a gate driver."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts with the requirement ($V_{GS}$ is referenced to SW; gate must reach $V_{in}+V_{GS,on}$) and states the options (bootstrap, charge pump, isolated supply, PFET).
- Sizes $C_{boot}$ with a formula and numbers, including derating and droop, and says what goes wrong if it is too small or too large.
- Calls out refresh, maximum duty and start-up with a pre-biased output.
- Covers the SW undershoot / pin rating, dv/dt and CMTI, and $V_{GS}$ overshoot from boot-cap overcharge.
- Mentions gate resistor/boot resistor trade-offs, diode choice (reverse voltage $\ge V_{in}+V_{DRV}$, low $Q_{rr}$), layout, and verification (scope $V_{GS}$ and SW directly).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Drive the gate with 5 V from the MCU."** With the source at SW $\approx V_{in}$, $V_{GS}$ would be $5-V_{in}$, which is negative: the FET never turns on, or turns off as SW rises.
- **Sizing $C_{boot}$ as 1 nF "because gate caps are nF".** The cap must be about 10 times $C_{iss}$ (gate charge over allowed droop), usually 100 nF or more.
- **Forgetting the boot-cap recharge.** Without a low-side on-time (or body-diode conduction), the cap never charges.
- **Wrong diode.** It sees reverse voltage $V_{in}+V_{DRV}$, so rate it at least that, with fast recovery.
- **Ignoring SW undershoot.** The SW pin can go $-1$ V or more in dead time, plus ringing; check the driver's absolute-maximum rating.
:::

::: deep
## Define the problem precisely

Take a 12 V to 3.3 V synchronous buck at 500 kHz. High-side FET $Q_1$: logic-level NFET, $Q_g=12$ nC at 5 V, $Q_{gd}=3$ nC, $V_{th}\approx2.5$ V (plateau about 3 V), $R_{DS(on)}=20$ mΩ. The requirement: $V_{GS}\ge4.5$ V while the source swings between about $-0.7$ V and 12 V.

A ground-referenced supply is useless: at $V_{SW}=12$ V a 5 V gate gives $V_{GS}=-7$ V. We need a supply whose negative terminal moves with SW.

**Charging the cap.** With SW low, $C_{boot}$ charges through $D_{boot}$ to

$$V_{boot} = V_{DRV} - V_{f} - V_{SW,low}.$$

For 5 V, $V_f=0.4$ V and $V_{SW,low}=-I R_{DS(on),2}\approx-0.02$ V (the low side carries current from ground into SW, so SW sits slightly below ground): $V_{boot}\approx4.62$ V, call it 4.6 V.

**Operation.** When $Q_1$ turns on, SW rises to about $V_{in}$. The cap's negative plate follows; the BST pin goes to $V_{in}+V_{boot}\approx16.6$ V. $D_{boot}$ is now reverse biased: its anode sits at $V_{DRV}$ and its cathode at $V_{in}+V_{boot}$, so it blocks about $V_{in}+V_{boot}-V_{DRV}\approx12$ V (plus ringing, and up to $V_{in}+V_{DRV}$ in the worst case with a 0 V driver rail), so a 30 V Schottky is fine. The driver now holds $V_G=V_{SW}+V_{boot}$, so $V_{GS}\approx V_{boot}$ in steady state.

## Sizing $C_{boot}$ and the diode

Charge removed per cycle from the cap:

$$Q_{tot} = Q_g + I_{Q,BST}\,t_{on} + Q_{LS}$$

For $Q_g=12$ nC, $I_{Q,BST}=60$ µA, $t_{on}=0.55$ µs: the quiescent term is only 0.03 nC. So $C\ge12\text{ nC}/0.3\text{ V}=40$ nF. Real ceramic caps lose capacitance with DC bias: a 100 nF X7R in a small package at 4.6 V may give 60 nF, so droop is $12/60=0.2$ V, acceptable. 

Why not 10 µF? Start-up charge time and inrush: the cap charges from the low-side on-time each cycle at the diode and resistor limited current $(V_{DRV}-V_f)/(R_{boot}+R_{diode})$. A huge cap takes many cycles to charge, delaying the first high-side pulse.

Diode: reverse rating $\ge V_{in,max}+V_{DRV}$ (plus ringing), low forward drop, low capacitance and fast recovery (Schottky or fast-recovery). A series resistor of 1 to 5 Ω in the diode path limits the recharge current; a resistor between the BST pin and $C_{boot}$ instead slows high-side turn-on (the gate charge is drawn through it).

## What happens at long on-times: the refresh limit

Droop during continuous on-time: $\Delta V = I_{Q,BST}\,t/C$. For 100 nF, 60 µA, 0.5 V droop allowed: $t=0.83$ ms. A buck refreshes every cycle because the low side conducts $(1-D)T$, but:

- $D_{max}=1-t_{off,min}f_{sw}$ ([[q0204]]).
- At low dropout (input barely above output) the controller must periodically force an off-pulse.
- At 100% duty requirements add a charge pump (keeps BST up) or use a PFET.

## The checklist of real-world considerations

| Consideration | What goes wrong | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Max duty / refresh | Cap droops at long on-time | Minimum off-time, charge pump, or PFET |
| Pre-biased start-up | Low side never on, cap never charged | Precharge pulse, or body-diode path, or driver with internal precharge |
| SW undershoot below 0 V | Violates driver pin rating, over-charges cap, injects substrate current | Pin rated for negative SW, series $R_{boot}$, low-inductance layout, snubber |
| dv/dt on SW (5 to 20 V/ns) | Level shifter false triggers, noise on logic | High-CMTI driver, slow edges with $R_g$ |
| Dead time | Shoot-through ([[q0207]]) | Programmable or adaptive dead time |
| $V_{GS}$ overshoot | Cap charged above rating | Zener or lower $V_{DRV}$ |
| Gate loop inductance | Gate ringing, spurious turn-on | Short, wide gate loop, local ceramic, $R_g$ |
| Gate resistor | Edge speed vs EMI vs loss | Typically 1 to 10 Ω; separate turn-on/off with a diode |
| Layout | Cap far from pins adds inductance and noise | $C_{boot}$ within millimeters of BST and SW pins |

## Edge speed

The turn-on time of $Q_1$ is set mostly by the Miller charge: $t_{sw}\approx Q_{gd}/I_g$ with $I_g=(V_{drv}-V_{plateau})/R_{g,tot}$. With 5 V drive, 3 V plateau and 4 Ω total (driver plus gate plus internal): $I_g=0.5$ A, $t_{sw}=6$ ns, giving dv/dt $\approx12\text{ V}/6\text{ ns}=2$ V/ns. Slower edges lower EMI and ringing and reduce $Q_{rr}$ spikes at the cost of overlap loss $\tfrac12 V_{in} I\,t\,f$. A resistor in series with only the turn-on path (or a boot resistor between the BST pin and $C_{boot}$) gives slow turn-on and fast turn-off, which also helps avoid dv/dt-induced turn-on of the low side ([[q0207]]).

## Alternatives

- **Isolated gate driver with isolated supply**: needed when SW swings hundreds of volts or the high side must stay on indefinitely.
- **Charge pump**: a small switched-capacitor stage keeps BST above $V_{in}$ even at 100% duty; common in automotive bucks.
- **PFET high side**: no bootstrap, 100% duty, at the cost of area ([[q0204]]).
- **Integrated bootstrap**: many monolithic converters integrate the diode and capacitor.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> For a "design" question, say the structure out loud: "Requirement, options, chosen architecture, key calculation, failure modes, verification." Interviewers score the process, not just the circuit.

## Verification

Probe $V_{GS}$ of $Q_1$ (differential or with a floating-probe technique) and SW with a short ground spring. Check: $V_{GS}$ reaches $\ge4.5$ V, droop at max on-time, SW undershoot, and no gate dip when $Q_2$ turns on.
:::

::: applications
- **Monolithic synchronous bucks with internal BST capacitor or a pin for an external 100 nF** (for example a typical 3 A converter).
- **Half-bridge gate-driver ICs for motor drives and inverters**: bootstrap channel plus a separate low-side driver.
- **Multiphase CPU/GPU regulators** with driver or DrMOS chips containing the bootstrap switch.
- **Class-D amplifiers** and half-bridge inverters.
- **Automotive buck converters** that add a charge pump to maintain the high-side gate during cold-crank (very low dropout) conditions.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Logic-level NFET, 5 V drive, 12 V input, 500 kHz**; for a standard-level FET use 10 to 12 V drive and the same method with different numbers.
- **$Q_g$ is the datasheet value** at the actual $V_{GS}$; $C_{iss}$-based estimates are less accurate because of the Miller effect.
- **Ceramic derating** is typical; check the actual part's DC-bias curve.
- **Refresh** assumes the low side (or body diode) conducts each cycle. In PFM or skip modes the low side may not turn on for a long time; the controller then forces a refresh.
- If the input exceeds about 100 V or a long on-time is required, an isolated driver supply is the better answer.
:::

::: followups
### Why not just use a transformer or an optocoupler gate drive?
Both work and give real isolation, but they need a supply for the high side too (a transformer can provide both signal and power for the on-time, an optocoupler cannot). A transformer-coupled drive has limited duty range and needs reset; an optocoupler is slow and has limited dv/dt immunity. For a 12 V buck, bootstrap is simpler, smaller and cheaper.

### What happens if $C_{boot}$ is too small? Too large?
Too small: $V_{GS}$ droops each cycle, so $R_{DS(on)}$ rises, conduction loss increases, and eventually UVLO trips. Too large: slower initial charging, higher charge current peaks through $D_{boot}$ (EMI), and at start-up the high side cannot turn on until it charges. 100 nF to 1 µF is typical for 10 to 50 nC gates.

### How do you start up a bootstrap converter into a pre-biased output?
If $V_{out}>0$ and the low side stays off (to avoid discharging the output), the cap never charges. Controllers do a short low-side precharge pulse, or charge the cap through the body diode when SW is pulled down, or use a separate precharge path. Otherwise the high side never turns on and the output stays stuck.

### What if SW undershoots to $-3$ V in dead time?
The BST cap is charged to $V_{DRV}-V_f+3$ V (about 7.6 V on a 5 V rail), which may exceed the gate rating on a 12 V drive design (check: 12 V drive gives about 14.6 V, under the 20 V limit). The bigger risk is the driver pin: many have an abs-max of $-0.3$ to $-1$ V DC, with a short transient rating down to $-5$ V or more. Add a series resistor in the SW path, reduce loop inductance, or use a driver rated for negative SW.

### The converter works at 50% load but the high-side FET gets hot at full load and high duty. Why?
Likely $V_{GS}$ droop: at high duty the off-time is short, the cap does not fully recharge, and $V_{GS}$ sags, raising $R_{DS(on)}$ and possibly pushing the FET toward linear operation. Measure $V_{BST}-V_{SW}$ at the worst duty and load; increase $C_{boot}$, reduce $f_{sw}$ or check the maximum duty setting.
:::
