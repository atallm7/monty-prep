::: basics
### In plain words
An RLC circuit combines a resistor (R), an inductor (L) and a capacitor (C). The capacitor and the inductor both store energy, and their opposition to current changes with frequency in opposite ways. In a series RLC circuit, at one special frequency, the resonant frequency, their effects cancel and only the resistor limits the current. The resistor also decides whether the circuit rings after a sudden change or settles smoothly.

### Picture it
Picture a child on a swing. The swing wants to go back and forth at its own natural rate. That is resonance. Friction is the resistor: with a lot of friction the swing creeps to rest, and with very little it keeps swinging for many cycles. Pushing at the natural rate builds a big swing. The capacitor is like the swing at the top of its arc, holding energy as height. The inductor is like the swing at the bottom, holding energy as speed. Energy trades between them every cycle.

### Words you need
- **Capacitor / inductor**: a capacitor stores charge; an inductor is a coil of wire that resists changes in current. See [[q0022]] and [[q0039]].
- **Frequency**: how many times per second a back-and-forth (AC) signal repeats, in hertz (Hz).
- **Reactance / impedance**: a capacitor's or inductor's opposition to AC, in ohms; impedance is the total, including resistance. See [[q0024]] and [[q0040]].
- **Damping**: how quickly the ringing dies out.
- **Q (quality factor)**: how sharp the resonance is. High Q means little damping.

### The simplest example
Take a series circuit with 20 Ω, 1 mH and 1 µF. It resonates at $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, about 5 kHz. There both reactances are about 31.6 Ω, so they cancel and the impedance is just 20 Ω. A 1 V source then drives 50 mA. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds how the circuit rings over time (overshoot and ringing) and the damping ratio that predicts it.
:::

::: testing
"Basic RLC circuit analysis" is open-ended, so the interviewer is checking whether you have a structured toolkit:

1. **Frequency domain**: impedances $R$, $j\omega L$, $1/(j\omega C)$, series/parallel combination, and **resonance** at $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ where the reactances cancel.
2. **Time domain**: the second-order differential equation, the **damping ratio** $\zeta$, and the three responses (overdamped, critically damped, underdamped/ringing).
3. **Quality factor** $Q$ and what it means physically (sharpness of resonance, voltage magnification, ringing cycles).
4. Limiting-case intuition: capacitor open and inductor short at DC; capacitor short and inductor open at high frequency.
:::

::: why
RLC behaviour is everywhere in hardware, often by accident: the LC output filter of a buck converter, ringing on a switch node from trace inductance and MOSFET capacitance, a scope probe's ground lead resonating with its tip capacitance, decoupling capacitors with ESL that resonate, gate-drive ringing, LC filters for EMI, crystal oscillators. Being able to estimate $f_0$, $Q$ and whether something will ring lets you predict and fix overshoot, EMI and instability. Interviewers ask it to see if you can move between time and frequency domains, which is the core skill for filters, power supplies and signal integrity.
:::

::: concepts
- **Impedance**: $Z_R = R$, $Z_L = j\omega L$, $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)$; they combine like resistors. See [[q0024]], [[q0040]].
- **Element laws**: $v_L = L\,di/dt$ and $i_C = C\,dv/dt$, giving a second-order ODE when both are present.
- **Continuity**: capacitor voltage and inductor current can't jump. See [[q0027]], [[q0037]].
- **DC and high-frequency limits**: C open / L short at DC, the opposite at high frequency. See [[q0028]], [[q0044]].
- **Resonance**: the frequency where inductive and capacitive reactance cancel and energy sloshes between L and C.
- **Damping**: resistance dissipates energy each cycle; $\zeta$ and $Q$ quantify how quickly oscillation dies.
:::

::: answer
"I'd analyse an RLC circuit two ways. In the frequency domain, I treat each element as an impedance: $R$, $j\omega L$ and $1/j\omega C$. For a series RLC that gives $Z = R + j(\omega L - 1/\omega C)$. At low frequency the capacitor dominates and blocks current, at high frequency the inductor does, and at the resonant frequency, $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, the two reactances cancel, so the impedance is just $R$ and the current peaks.

In the time domain, KVL gives a second-order differential equation. The behaviour is set by the damping ratio, which for series RLC is $\zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$. If $\zeta > 1$ it's overdamped and creeps up with no overshoot; $\zeta = 1$ is critically damped, the fastest with no overshoot; and $\zeta < 1$ it rings at roughly $\omega_0$ with an exponentially decaying envelope.

Quality factor is the other way to say it: $Q = 1/(2\zeta)$. For example, 20 Ω, 1 mH and 1 µF resonates at about 5 kHz with $\zeta \approx 0.32$, so a step response overshoots by about 35%. That's the same physics behind switch-node ringing or a buck converter's output filter peaking."
:::

::: technical
**Series RLC** (source $V_s$, current $i$ common to all):
$$Z(j\omega) = R + j\left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right),\qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad Q_s = \frac{\omega_0L}{R} = \frac{1}{R}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}$$
At $\omega_0$: $Z = R$, current is maximum ($V_s/R$), and $|V_L| = |V_C| = Q\,V_s$. The current's −3 dB bandwidth is $\Delta\omega = R/L = \omega_0/Q$.

**Parallel RLC** (common voltage): $Y = \frac1R + j\left(\omega C - \frac{1}{\omega L}\right)$, same $\omega_0$, $Q_p = R\sqrt{C/L}$. At resonance impedance is *maximum* (= $R$). Note $R$ plays opposite roles: large $R$ means high $Q$ in parallel, low $Q$ in series.

**Time domain (series, step $V_s$ at $t = 0$, zero initial conditions).** KVL: $L\frac{di}{dt} + Ri + v_C = V_s$ with $i = C\frac{dv_C}{dt}$:
$$\frac{d^2v_C}{dt^2} + \frac{R}{L}\frac{dv_C}{dt} + \frac{1}{LC}v_C = \frac{V_s}{LC}$$
Standard form $s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_0 s + \omega_0^2 = 0$: $\alpha = \frac{R}{2L}$, $\zeta = \frac{\alpha}{\omega_0} = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}}$, roots $s = -\alpha \pm \sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2}$.
- $\zeta > 1$ (overdamped): two real roots, sum of two decaying exponentials.
- $\zeta = 1$ (critical, $R = 2\sqrt{L/C}$): $v_C = V_s[1 - (1 + \omega_0t)e^{-\omega_0t}]$.
- $\zeta < 1$ (underdamped): $v_C = V_s\left[1 - e^{-\alpha t}\left(\cos\omega_dt + \frac{\alpha}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\right)\right]$, $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$.
- Overshoot $= e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$; envelope time constant $1/\alpha = 2L/R$; settling (2%) ≈ $4/\alpha$.

**Example.** $R$ = 20 Ω, $L$ = 1 mH, $C$ = 1 µF: $\omega_0$ = 31.6 krad/s ($f_0$ = 5.03 kHz), $\alpha = 10^4$ s⁻¹, $\zeta$ = 0.316, $Q$ = 1.58, $\omega_d$ = 30 krad/s, overshoot 35.1% (5 V step peaks at 6.75 V at $t = \pi/\omega_d$ = 105 µs). Critical damping needs $R$ = 63.2 Ω.
:::

::: short
"Treat R, L and C as impedances: $R$, $j\omega L$ and $1/j\omega C$. In series, the reactances cancel at $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, so impedance drops to $R$ and current peaks. In the time domain you get a second-order equation, and the damping ratio $\zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$ decides the response: overdamped above 1, critically damped at 1, ringing below 1. $Q = 1/(2\zeta)$ tells you how sharp the resonance is and how much it rings."
:::

::: long
"I'd split RLC analysis into the frequency-domain view and the time-domain view, because they're two sides of the same thing.

In the frequency domain, I replace each element with its impedance: $R$ for the resistor, $j\omega L$ for the inductor, and $1/j\omega C$ for the capacitor. In a series RLC they just add: $Z = R + j(\omega L - 1/\omega C)$. At low frequency the capacitor's reactance is huge, so no current flows; at high frequency the inductor's reactance is huge. In between there's a frequency where $\omega L = 1/\omega C$, which is resonance, $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$. There, the reactances cancel, the circuit looks purely resistive, and the current peaks at $V/R$. A neat consequence is that the voltage across the inductor and capacitor individually can be $Q$ times the source voltage, because they're large and opposite.

In the time domain, KVL gives a second-order differential equation, and its characteristic equation is $s^2 + (R/L)s + 1/LC = 0$. I normalise it to $s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_0 s + \omega_0^2$. For series RLC the damping ratio is $\zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$. If $\zeta$ is greater than one, the response is overdamped: slow, no overshoot. At exactly one it's critically damped, the fastest response without overshoot. Below one, it's underdamped: the circuit rings at about $\omega_0$ with a decaying envelope, because energy keeps sloshing between the inductor's magnetic field and the capacitor's electric field while the resistor bleeds a bit off each cycle.

To make it concrete: 20 ohms, 1 millihenry and 1 microfarad resonates at about 5 kilohertz. The damping ratio is about 0.32, so a 5 volt step overshoots to about 6.75 volts, roughly 35%. To make it critically damped I'd need about 63 ohms.

The quality factor, $Q = 1/(2\zeta)$, ties both views together: high Q means a sharp, narrow resonance peak and a lot of ringing cycles. That's exactly what I'd look for when a switch node rings or an LC filter peaks."
:::

::: terms
- **Impedance ($Z$)**: complex ratio $V/I$ for sinusoids; $R$, $j\omega L$, $1/j\omega C$.
- **Reactance ($X$)**: imaginary part of impedance; $X_L = \omega L$ (positive), $X_C = -1/\omega C$ (negative).
- **Resonant (natural) frequency ($\omega_0$)**: $1/\sqrt{LC}$, where $X_L + X_C = 0$.
- **Neper frequency / decay rate ($\alpha$)**: $R/2L$ for series RLC; the envelope decays as $e^{-\alpha t}$.
- **Damping ratio ($\zeta$)**: $\alpha/\omega_0$; classifies the response as over-, critically or under-damped.
- **Quality factor ($Q$)**: $\omega_0/\Delta\omega$ = $1/(2\zeta)$; also $2\pi\times$(energy stored)/(energy lost per cycle).
- **Damped natural frequency ($\omega_d$)**: $\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$, the actual ringing frequency.
- **Characteristic equation**: the polynomial in $s$ whose roots (poles) determine the natural response.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** writes $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ and maybe "it resonates".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Shows **both** domains and how they connect (poles of the transfer function = roots of the ODE's characteristic equation).
- Writes $\zeta$ and $Q$ correctly for series *and* knows that parallel inverts the role of $R$.
- Gives the three damping cases with a physical picture (energy sloshing between L and C, resistor bleeding it off).
- Mentions **voltage magnification** at resonance ($Q\times V_s$ across L and C), a real-world hazard.
- Works a number: $f_0$, $\zeta$, overshoot, and the $R$ needed for critical damping.
- Connects to real hardware: switch-node ringing, LC output filters, snubbers, probe ground-lead resonance.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using the series $Q$ formula for a parallel circuit.** Series: $Q = \frac1R\sqrt{L/C}$ (small $R$ → high $Q$). Parallel: $Q = R\sqrt{C/L}$ (large $R$ → high $Q$).
- **Mixing $\omega$ and $f$.** $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ is in rad/s; $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ in Hz. A 2π error is the most common numeric mistake.
- **Thinking critically damped means "slowest" or "has a little overshoot".** It's the fastest response with *zero* overshoot.
- **Saying the ringing frequency is exactly $\omega_0$.** It's $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$; close to $\omega_0$ only for light damping.
- **Forgetting initial conditions.** Capacitor voltage and inductor current are continuous; the step response depends on their values at $t = 0^-$.
- **Assuming component voltages can't exceed the source.** At resonance, $|V_L| = |V_C| = Q\cdot V_s$, which can destroy parts rated only for the source voltage.
:::

The physical story is energy exchange. Current builds up in the inductor, charges the capacitor, the capacitor pushes current back, and so on. Without $R$ this would oscillate forever at $\omega_0$. With $R$, every cycle loses some energy as heat, so the oscillation decays. How *fast* it decays relative to how fast it oscillates is the damping ratio.

## Frequency domain: impedance and resonance

With sinusoidal drive, replace elements by impedances and add (series):

$$Z = R + j\omega L + \frac{1}{j\omega C} = R + j\left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right)$$

- **Low frequency**: $1/\omega C$ dominates → large capacitive reactance → little current (the cap is "open").
- **High frequency**: $\omega L$ dominates → large inductive reactance → little current (the inductor is "open").
- **Resonance**: $\omega L = 1/\omega C$ → $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$. The reactances cancel and $Z = R$.

For the example values: $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{10^{-3}\cdot10^{-6}} = 31.6$ krad/s, $f_0 = 5.03$ kHz. At $f_0$, both reactances equal $\sqrt{L/C} = 31.6$ Ω, the **characteristic impedance**.

**Voltage magnification.** At resonance, $I = V_s/R$ and $|V_L| = I\omega_0L = V_s\cdot\frac{\omega_0L}{R} = Q\,V_s$. With $Q = 1.58$ a 1 V source puts 1.58 V across *both* L and C (in antiphase, so they cancel in KVL). With a 1 Ω resistor instead, $Q = 31.6$ and the capacitor sees 31.6 V from a 1 V source.

> [!WARNING]
> Voltage magnification is a real failure mode: a high-Q LC filter driven near resonance can over-voltage its capacitor, and an input filter's LC resonance can peak and interact with a switching regulator's negative input impedance. Damping resistors exist for this reason.

**Bandpass behaviour.** Take the output across $R$: $H(s) = \frac{sRC}{s^2LC + sRC + 1}$. It's a bandpass centred on $f_0$ with −3 dB bandwidth $\Delta f = R/(2\pi L)$ and peak gain 1.

**Parallel RLC** is the dual. Admittances add: $Y = \frac1R + j\left(\omega C - \frac{1}{\omega L}\right)$. Same $\omega_0$, but at resonance the *impedance is maximum* (a "tank"), and $Q_p = R\sqrt{C/L}$: a large parallel $R$ means little loss and a high $Q$. This is the structure of an LC oscillator tank and of the resonance between a decoupling capacitor and plane inductance.

## Time domain: the step response

KVL around the loop with $i = C\,dv_C/dt$:

$$LC\frac{d^2v_C}{dt^2} + RC\frac{dv_C}{dt} + v_C = V_s$$

Divide by $LC$ and compare with the standard second-order form:

$$\frac{d^2v_C}{dt^2} + 2\zeta\omega_0\frac{dv_C}{dt} + \omega_0^2v_C = \omega_0^2V_s,\qquad \omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad \zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}}$$

The characteristic roots are $s_{1,2} = -\alpha \pm\sqrt{\alpha^2 - \omega_0^2}$ with $\alpha = R/2L = \zeta\omega_0$. These are exactly the **poles** of the transfer function $1/(s^2LC + sRC + 1)$; time and frequency domain are the same math.

| Case | Condition | Roots | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overdamped | $\zeta > 1$, $R > 2\sqrt{L/C}$ | two real, negative | slow, no overshoot |
| Critically damped | $\zeta = 1$, $R = 2\sqrt{L/C}$ | repeated real | fastest without overshoot |
| Underdamped | $\zeta < 1$ | complex pair | decaying oscillation at $\omega_d$ |

**Underdamped formulas for the example** ($R = 20$ Ω):
- $\alpha = R/2L = 10^4$ s⁻¹, so the envelope time constant is 100 µs and the response settles (2%) in about $4/\alpha = 400$ µs.
- $\omega_d = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2} = \sqrt{10^9 - 10^8} = 30$ krad/s.
- Peak at $t_p = \pi/\omega_d = 105$ µs, overshoot $= e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}} = e^{-1.047} = 35.1\%$, so 6.75 V peak.
- $Q = 1/(2\zeta) = 1.58$. A useful rule of thumb: the number of visible ringing cycles is roughly $Q$.

## Two energy views of Q

$Q$ has a physical definition that works for any resonator:

$$Q = 2\pi\cdot\frac{\text{energy stored}}{\text{energy lost per cycle}}$$

For series RLC at resonance, stored energy is $\tfrac12LI_p^2$ and loss per cycle is $\tfrac12I_p^2R\cdot\frac{2\pi}{\omega_0}$, giving $Q = \omega_0L/R$. ✓. A high-Q resonator (a quartz crystal reaches tens of thousands or more) rings for a very long time; a well-damped power filter has $Q$ near 1 or below.

## RLC in real hardware

| Where | L | C | R | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buck output filter | power inductor | output caps | load, ESR, DCR | LC double pole at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ sets the control-loop design ([[q0246]]) |
| Switch-node ringing | loop/package inductance (nH) | MOSFET $C_{oss}$ (pF–nF) | small | ringing at 100s of MHz; fixed with RC snubbers, tighter layout |
| Scope probe ground lead | ~15 cm lead ≈ 100+ nH | probe tip + input capacitance | small | false ringing on fast edges; use a ground spring |
| Decoupling capacitor | ESL (≈0.5–1 nH) | C | ESR | self-resonance; above it the cap behaves inductively ([[q0031]]) |
| Gate drive | gate loop inductance | $C_{iss}$ | $R_g$ | gate ringing; series gate resistor provides damping |

**Snubber example.** A switch node ringing at 100 MHz with $C_{oss}$ = 500 pF implies loop $L = 1/(\omega^2C) \approx 5$ nH, and $\sqrt{L/C} \approx 3.2$ Ω. Adding an RC snubber with $R$ on the order of that characteristic impedance (and $C$ several times $C_{oss}$) damps the resonance toward $\zeta \approx 1$. That's RLC analysis directly turned into a design rule.

> [!TIP]
> To find hidden L or C on a board: measure the ringing frequency, add a known capacitor $C_{add}$ across the node, and measure the new frequency. Since $f \propto 1/\sqrt{C}$, the original capacitance is $C_{add}/((f_1/f_2)^2 - 1)$, and then $L = 1/((2\pi f_1)^2C)$.
:::

::: applications
- **Buck converter output filter**: the LC double pole at $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ (e.g. 4.7 µH and 22 µF → ≈15.7 kHz) shapes the compensation design.
- **RC snubbers on switch nodes**: damp parasitic LC ringing from loop inductance and MOSFET output capacitance to reduce EMI and voltage overshoot.
- **LC EMI filters**: input filters on DC-DC converters, with damping to avoid resonant peaking.
- **Oscillators and tuned circuits**: LC tanks in RF oscillators and radio front ends; quartz crystals as very-high-Q equivalent RLC circuits.
- **Decoupling networks**: each capacitor's ESL and ESR form a series RLC with a self-resonant frequency.
- **Signal integrity**: package and trace inductance with pin capacitance cause overshoot on fast digital edges, fixed with series termination resistors.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal, linear components**: real inductors have DCR and saturation, real capacitors have ESR and ESL, and those parasitic resistances often set the actual damping.
- **Lumped circuit**: valid when the physical size is much smaller than the wavelength at $f_0$.
- **Zero initial conditions** for the step-response formulas. With initial capacitor voltage or inductor current, the constants change but $\zeta$, $\omega_0$ and $\omega_d$ don't.
- **Series topology** for the formulas shown; parallel RLC uses $\alpha = 1/(2RC)$, $\zeta = \frac{1}{2R}\sqrt{L/C}$, $Q = R\sqrt{C/L}$.

Interviewers may also accept Laplace-domain analysis (write $H(s)$, find poles) as the "basic analysis"; it's the same thing as solving the characteristic equation.
:::

::: followups
### What R gives critical damping for L = 10 µH and C = 100 µF in series?
$R = 2\sqrt{L/C} = 2\sqrt{10^{-5}/10^{-4}} = 2\sqrt{0.1} = 0.632$ Ω. Below that it rings. For context, $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{10^{-9}}) \approx 5.03$ kHz, and the capacitor's ESR plus the inductor's DCR may already provide much of that damping.

### What's the difference between ω₀ and ω_d?
$\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ is the undamped natural frequency, where reactances cancel. $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$ is the frequency you actually see ringing in the time domain. For $\zeta = 0.316$, $\omega_d$ is 95% of $\omega_0$; for light damping they're nearly equal, and for $\zeta \ge 1$ there's no oscillation at all.

### At resonance in a series RLC, the capacitor voltage is larger than the source voltage. How is that possible, and doesn't it violate KVL?
The inductor and capacitor voltages are equal in magnitude ($Q\cdot V_s$) but 180° out of phase, so they cancel in the KVL sum, leaving the source voltage across R. Energy-wise, the circuit accumulates stored energy over many cycles until losses per cycle equal energy input per cycle; the stored energy is $Q/2\pi$ times the loss per cycle, which is why the individual voltages can be large.

### How would you tell from a scope capture whether a ringing waveform is lightly or heavily damped?
Count the cycles and measure successive peaks. The logarithmic decrement $\delta = \ln(x_n/x_{n+1})$ gives $\zeta \approx \delta/2\pi$ for light damping, and $Q \approx \pi/\delta$. If the ring decays to ~4% of its first peak after about $Q$ cycles, that's consistent ($e^{-\pi} \approx 4.3\%$). A heavily damped node shows one overshoot and settles.

### What if you replaced R with a diode in the series circuit? (harder probe)
The circuit becomes nonlinear. The LC still rings when the diode conducts, but the diode stops conduction when current tries to reverse, so the capacitor is left holding its peak voltage (up to nearly $2V_s$ minus the diode drop for a lightly damped circuit). That's the principle behind resonant charging circuits and why you see a "half-cycle" of ringing in diode-clamped flyback circuits. You can't use $\zeta$ or $Q$ for the whole cycle; analyse each conduction interval separately.
