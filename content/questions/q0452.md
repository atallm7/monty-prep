::: basics
### In plain words
Speccing a current sense amplifier means turning your system needs into a short list of numbers and checking each against the datasheet. You start with where the shunt sits and how high the bus voltage can spike. Then you pick a gain that fits the ADC without clipping, check that the amplifier's offset and common-mode leakage are small compared with your smallest current, and check that it is fast enough. Finally you add up all the errors to see that the total meets your accuracy target.

### Picture it
Imagine buying a ruler for a job. It must be long enough to reach the object (the common-mode range), have the right scale so the object fills the page (the gain), and have fine enough marks that a tiny object is not a blur (the offset). It must also be quick to read if the object is moving (the bandwidth). One ruler can fail on any of these even if the others are perfect. The analogy leaves out that the ruler's own markings can be slightly off, which is the gain error.

### Words you need
- **Common-mode range**: the input voltage range where the amplifier works and meets its specs. See [[q0447]].
- **Gain and gain error**: the multiplier and how far the real value strays from it. See [[q0453]].
- **Input offset ($V_{os}$)**: a zero error that sets the minimum current, $V_{os} / R_s$. See [[q0445]].
- **CMRR**: how well the amplifier ignores the bus voltage. See [[q0451]].
- **Output swing**: how close the output can get to ground and to the supply.

### The simplest example
Take a 24 V motor, 20 A maximum, and a 2 mΩ shunt, which gives 40 mV. With a 3.2 V usable output, the largest gain is $3.2 / 0.04 = 80$, so choose the standard 50, giving 2.0 V. A 100 µV offset means $100\ \mu\text{V} / 0.002 = 50$ mA of error, which is 5% at 1 A. A CMRR of 100 dB on 24 V adds $24 / 10^5 = 240$ µV, or 120 mA, so you would want about 110 dB or better. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page works through each parameter in order, adds the dynamics and an error budget, and gives a checklist.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can turn system requirements into a **parameter list and numbers** for choosing a current sense amplifier: common-mode range, gain, offset, gain error, CMRR, bandwidth, output swing, supply, unidirectional vs bidirectional, plus extras like input protection and a comparator.

The strong answer isn't a list from memory; it is organized as "requirement → parameter → how I compute the limit", ending with an error budget. The interviewer also listens for the interactions: offset sets the lowest current, gain is limited by output swing, bandwidth drops with gain, and common-mode range must cover transients.
:::

::: why
The same shunt can give a precise gauge or a useless reading depending on the amplifier chosen. Datasheets list dozens of parameters; knowing which five or six drive the decision (and how to check them against the system) saves redesigns. It is the integration point for the whole section: loss and drop ([[q0445]]), ADC range ([[q0446]]), side of the bus ([[q0447]]), and gain ([[q0453]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Common-mode voltage range and transients**: must cover bus voltage plus spikes. See [[q0447]].
- **Gain and gain error**: maps full-scale current to the ADC range; error is a direct scale error. See [[q0453]].
- **Offset voltage and drift**: sets the minimum current, $V_{os}/R_s$. See [[q0445]].
- **CMRR and PSRR**: how much bus voltage and supply ripple leak into the output. See [[q0451]].
- **Bandwidth, slew and settling**: must meet the control or protection response time.
- **Output swing and REF**: determines usable range, negative currents and the near-zero floor.
:::

::: answer
"I'd start from the system and work down to parameters.

First, topology: is the shunt high-side or low-side, and is current unidirectional or bidirectional? That fixes the common-mode range I need, which has to cover the bus voltage plus any spikes or load-dump, and whether I need a REF pin.

Second, signal chain: given the shunt and the ADC range, I pick a gain so full-scale current lands just under the output's maximum swing, and I check the output can't clip. Gain error comes straight into my accuracy.

Third, accuracy at the low end: the input offset divided by the shunt is my minimum current, so I compare that to the smallest current I care about. That tells me whether a standard part is OK or I need a zero-drift one. I also check offset drift and CMRR, because on a 24 volt bus 100 dB of CMRR still leaks 240 microvolts.

Fourth, dynamics: bandwidth and settling at that gain, versus the PWM frequency or the protection response time. Bandwidth falls with gain.

Then the supporting things: supply voltage and quiescent current, input protection, package, and extras like a built-in comparator or a digital output. Finally I run an error budget with the shunt tolerance and tempco to confirm it meets the accuracy spec."
:::

::: technical
**Spec flow.**

1. **Topology and common-mode range.** $V_{cm,max}\ge V_{bus,max} + \text{transients}$ (hot-plug, load dump, inductive spikes). High-side: rating well above bus. Low-side: input range must include slightly below ground for tiny negative signals.
2. **Gain.** $G\le\dfrac{V_{out,max}}{I_{max}R_s}$ where $V_{out,max}$ is the output swing limit (supply minus swing-to-rail), then round down to an available gain. Use headroom (10 to 25 percent).
3. **Offset and low-end accuracy.** $I_{min,eq}=V_{os}/R_s$ (plus drift over temperature). Require $I_{min,eq}\ll I_{min}\times\text{target error}$.
4. **Gain error.** Typical 0.05 to 1 percent plus drift (ppm/°C); combines with shunt tolerance and tempco.
5. **CMRR/PSRR.** Error from common-mode: $V_{cm}/10^{CMRR/20}$; equivalent current $=$ this over $R_s$. Constant bus: a calibratable offset; PWM or ripple on the bus: uncorrectable noise.
6. **Bandwidth and settling.** BW$(G)\approx GBW/G$ (check the datasheet curve); settling after a PWM edge or common-mode step must finish before sampling. Protection: response time (µs).
7. **Output.** Swing near ground and near the rail; REF range and accuracy; drive capability into the ADC's sample capacitor, with an RC filter if needed.
8. **Supply and power.** $V_S$ range, $I_Q$, startup, relation to the ADC reference (ratiometric).
9. **Robustness and extras.** Input differential overload, ESD, fault tolerance, comparator/alert output, temperature range, package.

**Worked example.** 24 V motor, $I_{max}=20$ A, $R_s=2$ mΩ (40 mV, 0.8 W), unidirectional, 3.3 V ADC.
- Gain: $G\le3.2/(20\times0.002)=80$; standard gain **50** gives 2.0 V (61 percent of 3.3 V).
- Offset 100 µV: equivalent 50 mA (0.25 percent of full-scale, but 5 percent at 1 A).
- CMRR 100 dB at 24 V: 240 µV (120 mA equivalent). Larger than the offset, so choose ≥ 110 dB or calibrate it.
- Error at full scale (RSS): shunt 1 percent, tempco 0.3 percent, CSA gain 0.5 percent: 1.16 percent; with offset 0.25 percent: 1.18 percent.
- Bandwidth: a 20 kHz PWM and 10 µs response needs a few hundred kHz of bandwidth at $G=50$.
- Common-mode: 24 V bus plus spikes: pick a part rated for at least 40 V (or higher for automotive).
:::

::: long
"I like to spec it as a chain from requirements to numbers rather than reading the datasheet cold.

First, the topology. High-side or low-side, unidirectional or bidirectional? That sets the common-mode range. For a 24 volt bus I'd want a part rated comfortably above that, say 40 volts or more, to survive spikes. Bidirectional means I need a reference pin so negative current shows up as a voltage below mid-scale.

Second, gain. With the shunt chosen from power and drop, say 2 milliohms at 20 amps for 40 millivolts, and a 3.3 volt ADC, the maximum gain is the output swing limit divided by the full-scale sense voltage, here about 80. CSAs come in steps like 20, 50, 100, 200, so I'd choose 50, which puts full-scale at 2 volts and leaves headroom.

Third, low-current accuracy. Offset divided by shunt is my floor: 100 microvolts on 2 milliohms is 50 milliamps. If I need accuracy at 1 amp, that's 5 percent, so I'd go to a zero-drift part with a few microvolts. I also check CMRR: on a 24 volt bus, 100 dB is still 240 microvolts, which is 120 milliamps equivalent, even bigger than the offset, so I want 110 dB or better, or I calibrate it out if the bus is steady.

Fourth, speed. Bandwidth falls with gain, so I check it at gain 50 against my PWM and protection response, and the settling after common-mode steps.

Fifth, the output: swing near ground and the rail, and whether it can drive the ADC input. Plus supply, quiescent current, input protection and extras like a comparator.

Last, an error budget: combine shunt tolerance and tempco, CSA gain error, offset and CMRR terms, and ADC error to check I meet the spec at both ends of the current range."
:::

::: terms
- **Common-mode voltage range (CMVR)**: range of input voltage for which the amplifier operates and meets its specs.
- **Gain error**: percent deviation from nominal gain, often with drift in ppm/°C.
- **Input offset voltage ($V_{os}$)**: input-referred zero error; drives minimum current.
- **CMRR / PSRR**: rejection of common-mode and supply variations.
- **Output swing**: how close the output gets to the rails (and to 0 V).
- **Settling time**: time for the output to reach final value within a tolerance after a step.
- **Overload / differential input limit**: maximum differential voltage the inputs tolerate.
- **Error budget**: combination of all error sources (usually RSS) at a given current.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists datasheet parameters: gain, bandwidth, offset, common-mode range.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Organizes by requirement → parameter → calculation, in a sensible order (topology, gain, low-end accuracy, dynamics, output, extras).
- Gives formulas: $G\le V_{out,max}/(I_{max}R_s)$, $I_{min,eq}=V_{os}/R_s$, $V_{cm}/10^{CMRR/20}$.
- Notes the interactions: bandwidth falls with gain, gain amplifies offset, output can't reach ground.
- Runs an error budget at both ends of the range (RSS of gain terms plus offset-dominated low end).
- Spots the CMRR term that often exceeds offset on a high bus and distinguishes steady (calibratable) from PWM-induced (not).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Choosing by gain and bandwidth only.** Offset, CMRR and common-mode range often decide whether it works.
- **Ignoring transients on the bus.** A 24 V system may see 40 V or more with inductive kicks or load-dump; the part's absolute maximum must cover it.
- **Using the datasheet's typical offset for worst-case design.** Use the max over temperature, including drift.
- **Forgetting output swing near ground.** A unidirectional CSA that can't go below 20 to 50 mV hides small currents.
- **Taking bandwidth from the wrong gain.** Bandwidth is specified per gain option; the 200 V/V version is the slowest.
- **Ignoring the ADC interface.** A CSA output driving an ADC's sample capacitor needs an RC filter or sufficient drive.
:::

::: deep
## Start with the topology

Two decisions fix most of the rest.

**High-side or low-side** ([[q0447]]) sets the required common-mode range. Low-side: inputs near 0 V (with small negative excursions for a bidirectional part referenced to ground), any CSA with ground-compatible inputs works. High-side: $V_{cm}$ = bus voltage, which may be well above the CSA's own supply. The required rating is

$$V_{cm,rating}\ge V_{bus,max}+V_{transients}$$

**Unidirectional or bidirectional** sets whether a REF pin is needed. Bidirectional parts output $V_{REF}+GV_s$. The available swing is about half per direction.

## Gain and output swing

Output is $V_{out}=G\,I\,R_s\ (+V_{REF})$. The output has a high and low swing limit (for example the output swings to within ~50 mV of the supply and ~10 mV of ground; check the datasheet's conditions). Require

$$G\,I_{max}R_s\le V_{out,max}-V_{REF}$$

and round down to a standard gain (often 20, 50, 100, 200 V/V for common families, but not universal). Unidirectional example: $R_s=2$ mΩ, $I_{max}=20$ A, 3.3 V rail, swing limit 3.2 V: $G\le80$, choose 50 (2.0 V).

## Low-current accuracy: offset and CMRR

The minimum current equivalent is the input-referred error divided by the shunt:

$$I_{err}=\frac{V_{os}+V_{cm}/10^{CMRR/20}+\dots}{R_s}$$

For $R_s=2$ mΩ:

| Term | Value | Equivalent current |
|---|---|---|
| Offset 100 µV (typical CSA) | 100 µV | 50 mA |
| Offset 5 µV (zero-drift) | 5 µV | 2.5 mA |
| CMRR 100 dB on a 24 V bus | 240 µV | 120 mA |
| CMRR 120 dB on a 24 V bus | 24 µV | 12 mA |

Note that CMRR can be a bigger error than offset. A steady bus makes it a constant offset, which firmware calibration at zero current removes. A rippling or PWM bus makes it signal-dependent noise that cannot be calibrated away. Also check offset drift (µV/°C) over the operating range, since a zero calibration only holds at the temperature it was done.

## Gain error and the whole-chain budget

Gain terms combine with the shunt. Using RSS (independent sources) at full-scale:

| Source | Value |
|---|---|
| Shunt tolerance | 1.0% |
| Shunt tempco × ΔT (50 ppm × 60 °C) | 0.3% |
| CSA gain error (max) | 0.5% |
| RSS gain terms | 1.16% |
| Offset at 20 A (50 mA / 20 A) | 0.25% |
| Total at full scale (RSS) | ≈1.18% |
| Offset at 1 A (50 mA / 1 A) | 5% |

At full scale it's gain-limited (shunt tolerance dominates), at the bottom it's offset-limited (5 percent at 1 A). The remedy differs: a 0.1 percent shunt for the top, a zero-drift CSA for the bottom. Calibration of gain at end-of-line removes most of the tolerance part.

## Dynamics: bandwidth, settling, response time

Closed-loop bandwidth falls with gain (roughly $\text{GBW}/G$): a part with 350 kHz at $G=50$ may be 1 MHz at $G=20$ and 100 kHz at $G=200$. Check:

- **Control loops**: bandwidth at least several times the loop crossover, and total delay compatible with the phase margin.
- **PWM**: the output must settle after switching edges within the ADC's sampling window. Use the datasheet's settling or "slew and recovery" and place the sample accordingly.
- **Protection**: a 350 kHz small-signal bandwidth gives a time constant $\approx 0.45\ \mu$s; with delay and comparator, expect about 1 to 3 µs to trip. If the requirement is under 1 µs, use a faster dedicated comparator part or a CSA built for it.
- **Overload recovery** after a large overshoot, important in short-circuit tests.

## Other parameters

| Parameter | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Supply voltage, $I_Q$ | Fits the 3.3 V or 5 V rail; battery life |
| Input bias current | Matters with filter resistors in series at the inputs |
| Differential input overload | Surges across the shunt during faults |
| Input filter compatibility | A series R at the inputs adds gain error from bias current; match both sides |
| Ratiometric operation | Output scales with supply, helpful when ADC reference = supply |
| Comparator / alert | Saves a part for protection |
| Digital version | Integrated ADC, I²C, power and energy registers |
| Temperature grade, package | Automotive, small SC70 or larger for thermal reasons |

## A short checklist

1. Topology and $V_{cm,max}$ with transients.
2. $G\le V_{out,max}/(I_{max}R_s)$, standard value, bidirectional REF if needed.
3. $V_{os}$ (max, over temperature) and CMRR vs $I_{min}$.
4. Gain error and drift into the budget.
5. Bandwidth/settling at that gain vs PWM and protection.
6. Output swing and ADC drive.
7. Supply, $I_Q$, protection, extras, package, cost.
:::

::: applications
- **Motor drives**: CSAs with PWM rejection and fast settling on low-leg or inline shunts.
- **Battery packs**: low-offset CSAs or gauge ICs for accurate coulomb counting.
- **Server power**: high-side CSAs or digital monitors with 12 V to 48 V common-mode.
- **Automotive**: CSAs with transient tolerance (load-dump) and wide temperature grades.
- **Industrial 24 V I/O**: CSAs with 40 V to 80 V common-mode range and input protection.
- **Hot-swap/eFuse**: CSAs with built-in comparators for sub-µs overcurrent response.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes an analog-output CSA feeding an MCU ADC. A digital power monitor shifts some parameters (conversion time, resolution) but follows the same logic.
- Typical numbers (offset, gain error, bandwidth, swing) are generic; real parts range widely, so always use the datasheet's worst-case over temperature.
- RSS combination assumes independent errors; for a conservative design, add worst-case terms linearly.
- Gain list 20/50/100/200 V/V is a common set, not universal; some parts have 500 or 1000 V/V or adjustable gain.
- Bandwidth-versus-gain behaviour is representative (about GBW/G) but depends on architecture.
:::

::: followups
### Which parameter would you check first on a 48 V high-side design?
Common-mode voltage rating (including transients), because it is pass/fail: if the absolute maximum is below bus plus spikes, the part dies, whatever its other specs. After that, CMRR at the relevant frequency and the supply that the CSA will run from.

### How do you decide between a standard and a zero-drift CSA?
Compute $V_{os}/R_s$ at the lowest current of interest and its drift over temperature. If the equivalent error is a large fraction of $I_{min}$ (say >20 percent of the accuracy budget), either increase the shunt, calibrate the zero, or use a zero-drift part (a few µV). Zero-drift parts may have more output noise and lower bandwidth, so check those too.

### What does CMRR vs frequency matter for?
On a PWM bus (motor phase or a switching rail), the common-mode swings by the bus voltage at the PWM edges. CMRR is highest at DC and falls with frequency, so the edge leaks through as a spike on the output. Check the datasheet's CMRR at your PWM frequency, the dV/dt rating, and settle before sampling.

### Scenario: your CSA works on the bench at 12 V but fails at 48 V in the system. What do you check?
Absolute maximum and common-mode range (48 V plus ringing), CMRR errors scaled by four, thermal rise in the shunt, and spikes at hot-plug that exceed the rating (add TVS or input filtering). Also whether the part's supply is ratiometric to something that sags.

### What if you need both a precise reading and sub-microsecond overcurrent protection?
Use two paths: a standard precision CSA for the ADC and a fast comparator (or a CSA with an integrated fast comparator) for protection. A single high-gain precision CSA is too slow or too noisy to do both well.

::: rubric
- Starts from topology: high/low side, uni/bi-directional, common-mode range with transients :: These are pass/fail constraints.
- Chooses gain from $I_{max}$, $R_s$ and output swing, rounded to available gains :: Directly tied to the system numbers.
- Computes offset/shunt as the minimum current and checks against $I_{min}$ :: The main accuracy driver at low current.
- Considers CMRR in terms of bus voltage and the frequency of the common-mode :: Often the largest hidden error.
- Checks bandwidth and settling at the chosen gain vs PWM/protection :: Dynamics are often forgotten.
- Mentions output swing, REF, ADC drive, supply and protection :: The practical details.
- Builds an error budget (RSS) including shunt tolerance and tempco :: Shows the whole chain, not just the amplifier.
:::
