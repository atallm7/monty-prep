::: testing
Whether you understand that **the same circuit gets designed differently depending on what failure costs and who pays**. The interviewer is testing breadth and judgment, not memorized standards: can you contrast cost, size, temperature, lifetime, reliability, safety, EMC and regulation across four industries, explain *why* each differs, and say what you would do differently on a board for each? Strong candidates link each constraint to a root cause (a human life, an unreachable spacecraft, a production run of ten million units) and name a few real standards at the right level without bluffing details.
:::

::: why
Hardware engineers rarely get to pick the industry's rules. A buck converter for a phone, a car, a satellite and an infusion pump may share a topology but not component grade, derating, validation, layout, documentation or test. Understanding the constraint stack lets you reason about unfamiliar products and choose parts sensibly, for example why a "good enough" commercial capacitor is wrong in a car, or why a space design may use a 20-year-old part. It also shows maturity: junior engineers say "automotive is harsher"; senior engineers say why, by how much, and what it costs.
:::

::: concepts
- **Environmental stress**: temperature range, humidity, vibration, radiation. Reliability follows temperature (Arrhenius) and mechanical stress. See [[q0976]] for reliability maths.
- **Reliability vs. cost**: the more consequence a failure has, the more you pay in parts, test and documentation to cut it.
- **Functional safety**: systematic processes and diagnostics that keep a failure from causing harm (ISO 26262, IEC 61508, IEC 60601).
- **EMC and ESD**: emissions and immunity requirements differ by industry. See [[q0974]] for the system view.
- **Component grades and derating**: commercial, industrial, automotive (AEC-Q), mil/space screening; derating is running parts well below their ratings.
- **Lifecycle**: how long you must build, support and keep the design stable.
:::

::: answer
"The big difference is the cost of failure, and that drives everything else.

Consumer electronics are driven by cost, size, battery life and time to market. You use commercial-grade parts, a temperature range around 0 to 70 degrees or sometimes a bit wider, a product life of a few years, and the regulatory work is mainly EMC and safety marks like FCC and CE. You accept some failure rate because returns are cheaper than gold-plating every part.

Automotive adds harsh conditions and a long life: minus 40 to 125 degrees or more under the hood, a 10 to 15 year life, vibration, a dirty 12 volt supply with load dump and cold crank, and AEC-Q qualified parts. Safety standards like ISO 26262 grade functions by ASIL, and defect rates are measured in parts per million or better.

Aerospace, space and defense add radiation, extreme temperature swings, vacuum, shock, no repair, and heavy documentation and screening. Parts are often derated and radiation-tolerant, and weight and power are expensive.

Medical devices are driven by patient safety and regulation: IEC 60601, risk management, strict isolation and leakage current limits, traceability, and design controls. Failure can hurt someone, so the process and paperwork matter as much as the circuit.

So the same buck converter goes from 'cheap and efficient' to 'robust, qualified and documented' depending on where it ships."
:::

**Temperature and life: Arrhenius.** Thermally activated failure mechanisms accelerate roughly as $AF=\exp\!\left[\frac{E_a}{k}\left(\frac{1}{T_{use}}-\frac{1}{T_{stress}}\right)\right]$. With $E_a=0.7$ eV, 1000 h at 125 °C is equivalent to about 77,700 h at 55 °C (about 8.9 years). Automotive parts see long hot hours, so the same part that lasts years in a phone may not last in an engine bay.

**Automotive power.** The 12 V system must tolerate reverse battery, jump-start (24 V), cold crank (the battery sags to a few volts), and load dump (a large positive transient when the battery disconnects while the alternator is charging; the unsuppressed pulse is tens of volts above nominal). Designs use TVS clamps, wide-input regulators and input-protection stages.

**Medical.** Applied-part leakage current limits are very low (order of 10 µA in normal condition for the most protective class), and isolation is specified in "means of patient protection" with creepage, clearance and test voltages in the kV range.

**Space.** Total ionizing dose, single-event upsets, latch-up, and thermal vacuum cycling dominate. Commercial parts are sometimes used with screening and shielding (a "new space" approach), but flight-critical designs favour qualified parts and redundancy ([[q0976]]).
:::

::: short
"The same circuit is designed differently because failure costs differ. Consumer is driven by cost, size and battery life with commercial parts and EMC marks. Automotive adds minus 40 to 125 degrees, 10 to 15 year life, a rough 12 volt supply with load dump, AEC-Q parts and ISO 26262. Aerospace, space and defense add radiation, vacuum, shock, no repair and heavy screening and documentation. Medical is dominated by patient safety: IEC 60601 leakage and isolation limits, risk management and traceability."
:::

::: long
"I think about it as one question: what does a failure cost, and who's paying? That sets the constraints.

Consumer electronics are cost, size, battery life and time to market. You use commercial-grade parts, a temperature range around zero to 70 degrees, a life of a few years, and you meet FCC and CE EMC and safety. A small percentage of returns is acceptable, because it's cheaper than overdesigning ten million units.

Automotive keeps the cost pressure but adds severity. Minus 40 to 125 degrees or more near the engine, a 10 to 15 year life, vibration, and a nasty 12 volt supply with reverse battery, cold crank and load dump. Parts are AEC-Q qualified, functional safety follows ISO 26262 with ASIL levels, and you track defects in parts per million. So you add input protection, derate, and keep suppliers stable for years.

Aerospace, space and defense have extreme environments and no repair. Think wide temperature cycling, vacuum, shock, vibration and radiation, which means total dose, single event upsets and latch-up. Parts are screened, rad-tolerant or rad-hard, derated heavily, and weight and power are expensive. The documentation and review load is huge, DO-254 and DO-178C for avionics for example.

Medical devices are driven by patient safety and regulation. IEC 60601 sets very low leakage current limits and isolation requirements, ISO 14971 means formal risk analysis, and you keep a design history file so every change is traceable.

So for a buck converter the topology is the same, but I'd pick, derate, protect, test and document it very differently in each."
:::

::: terms
- **AEC-Q100/101/200**: automotive stress-qualification standards for integrated circuits, discrete semiconductors and passives.
- **ASIL (Automotive Safety Integrity Level)**: ISO 26262 risk class from A (lowest) to D (highest), with QM meaning no safety requirement.
- **Load dump**: voltage surge on a vehicle supply when the battery is disconnected while the alternator is charging.
- **Cold crank**: severe sag of battery voltage during engine start in cold weather.
- **TID / SEU / latch-up**: total ionizing dose; single-event upset (a bit flip); and a parasitic-thyristor short triggered by radiation.
- **Derating**: using a part well below its rated voltage, current, power or temperature to improve life.
- **Means of patient protection (MOPP)**: IEC 60601-1 measure of insulation or protective impedance between mains and a patient.
- **Leakage current**: small current that can flow through a patient or user, tightly limited in medical devices.
- **DFM / DFR**: design for manufacturing / design for reliability.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "automotive is harsher, medical has regulation, aerospace is expensive".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts with **what failure costs** and shows how that explains the rest.
- Gives **numbers** (temperature ranges, life years, Arrhenius factor) instead of adjectives.
- Names **standards correctly** at the right level: AEC-Q100, ISO 26262, DO-254/DO-178C, IEC 60601-1, FCC/CE, without pretending to quote clauses.
- Uses a concrete circuit example: one buck converter, four ways: capacitor choice, derating, input protection, test.
- Notes that boundaries blur: consumer devices in cars, "new space" using commercial parts with screening, and medical wearables that are consumer-like.
- Flags the cost: each tier adds BOM cost, development time and paperwork, and the right answer is **fit to purpose, not maximum protection**.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating "reliable" as one scale.** Mean time to failure, defect rate in ppm, mission success probability and regulatory compliance are different things.
- **Saying automotive is just a wider temperature range.** It is also supply transients, life, vibration, EMC emission limits, functional safety and long-term supply.
- **Assuming space means "rad-hard parts only".** Many modern missions use screened commercial parts, shielding and redundancy depending on orbit, life and cost.
- **Believing medical safety is only about isolation.** It is also risk management, software lifecycle, usability, traceability and manufacturing quality.
- **Overdesigning consumer products.** Adding gold-plated protection that no one pays for is also an engineering failure.
- **Quoting exact standard numbers or clauses from memory.** Naming the standard and what it covers is enough, and safer.
:::

::: deep
## One lens: consequence, volume, repairability

Every constraint falls out of three questions:

1. **Consequence of failure**: inconvenience, recall, injury, loss of a mission, death.
2. **Volume and cost sensitivity**: ten million phones versus five satellites.
3. **Repairability and exposure**: a phone sits in a pocket; a satellite is unreachable; a car lives outside for 15 years; an implant can't be opened.

High consequence and no repair push you toward screening, derating, redundancy and documentation. High volume with small margins pushes you toward cost-optimisation and test automation. Most industries combine these differently, which is why this is the right frame for the answer. The next sections turn it into concrete engineering consequences.

## Temperature, life and Arrhenius

Many failure mechanisms in electronics (electromigration, dielectric breakdown, corrosion, solder fatigue contributors) accelerate with temperature. The Arrhenius model gives an acceleration factor relative to use conditions:

$$ AF = \exp\left[\frac{E_a}{k_B}\left(\frac{1}{T_{use}} - \frac{1}{T_{stress}}\right)\right] $$

with temperatures in kelvin, $k_B = 8.617\times10^{-5}$ eV/K. With $E_a = 0.7$ eV (a commonly used illustrative value; real mechanisms vary) and $T_{use}=55$ °C:

Figure: failure acceleration climbs steeply with temperature. A device run at 125 °C ages about 78 times faster than at 55 °C, which is why automotive and aerospace derate junction temperature and why high-temperature operating life (HTOL) tests are used to qualify parts.

Worked example: an HTOL test of 1000 h at 125 °C represents $1000\times 77.7 \approx 77{,}700$ h, about 8.9 years, at 55 °C for this mechanism. A 15-year automotive life with hot under-hood conditions therefore needs parts qualified more stringently than a phone chip, plus thermal design that keeps junctions cooler.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The activation energy depends on the failure mechanism (typical quoted values span roughly 0.3 to 1.0 eV or more). 0.7 eV is used here as an illustrative round number, not a universal constant. Real qualification uses mechanism-specific models and manufacturer data.

## Consumer electronics

- **Cost**: the BOM is often counted in cents; one extra connector or a larger capacitor is a business decision. Designers use the cheapest part that meets spec, and tolerate parts spread by design margin.
- **Size and thickness**: densely packed boards, HDI, package-on-package, tiny passives. Thermal limits are set by the skin temperature the user feels, not only by the silicon.
- **Battery life and charge time**: efficiency, sleep currents, power gating, fast-charging safety ([[q0295]]).
- **Time to market**: product cycles of 6 to 18 months, reuse of reference designs, frequent firmware updates.
- **Regulatory**: FCC Part 15 and CE for emissions and immunity, UL/IEC product safety, battery safety (UN 38.3 for transport of lithium cells is a known requirement), RoHS and similar material rules. ESD immunity commonly follows IEC 61000-4-2 levels (for example ±8 kV contact, ±15 kV air as a common target).
- **Acceptable failure**: warranty returns of a percent or so are budgeted. Field returns are tolerated in exchange for cost.

## Automotive

- **Environment**: AEC-Q100 grade 1 covers –40 to +125 °C ambient (grade 0 up to 150 °C). Under-hood and powertrain electronics see more; cabin electronics less.
- **Supply**: the 12 V system is not 12 V. It swings from a few volts at cold crank to well over 14 V while running, reverse-battery faults, jump starts, and load dump transients in the tens of volts. ISO 16750-2 defines electrical test pulses for vehicle supply (and the older ISO 7637 pulses are also widely referenced). Designs add TVS diodes, series reverse-protection, wide-input or pre-regulators, and thermal derating.
- **Life**: 10–15 years and often 100,000+ miles. Components must be qualified, grade-appropriate and available for years. Passive parts such as ceramic capacitors need cracks-resistance (flex-term, soft-termination, AEC-Q200).
- **Functional safety**: ISO 26262 assigns ASIL A to D based on severity, exposure and controllability. Higher ASIL needs diagnostic coverage, redundancy or monitoring ([[q0976]]), and process rigor: requirements traceability, safety analysis (FMEA/FTA) and independent assessment.
- **EMC**: automotive emissions limits (CISPR 25) protect on-board radios; immunity tests protect against transients and RF.
- **Cost**: still volume-driven. Automotive pays more per part than consumer, but not like space, so the discipline is **robustness at low cost**.

## Aerospace, space and defense

- **Environment**: wide temperature cycling (mil range –55 to 125 °C is typical for qualification), vibration and shock at launch or in a missile, vacuum (no convective cooling, outgassing concerns), and radiation in space. Aircraft add altitude, lightning and high EMI environments.
- **Radiation**: total ionizing dose (cumulative, shifts thresholds and increases leakage), single-event effects (SEU bit flips, SEL latch-up, SEB burnout in power MOSFETs) and displacement damage. Mitigations: radiation-hardened parts, shielding, ECC and scrubbing, watchdogs, latch-up current limiters, redundancy and voting.
- **No repair**: reliability must be demonstrated rather than hoped for. Derating guidelines (for example stress ratios on voltage and power) are applied systematically.
- **Weight, volume, power**: each gram and watt are expensive, which pushes toward efficient power conversion and careful thermal design.
- **Certification**: DO-254 (airborne electronic hardware), DO-178C (software), DO-160 (environmental test), MIL-STD-810 (environmental) and MIL-STD-461 (EMI) are widely used. Space missions follow agency standards and project-specific requirements.
- **Obsolescence**: designs last decades; parts need long-term availability, last-time buys or qualified alternatives.

## Medical devices

- **Patient safety first**: regulation (FDA in the US, MDR in Europe) classifies devices by risk. ISO 14971 defines the risk-management process; ISO 13485 the quality system; IEC 62304 the software lifecycle.
- **Electrical safety**: IEC 60601-1 limits leakage currents and requires isolation between mains and patient-contacting parts. For the most protective class of applied part (type CF, for direct cardiac contact), normal-condition patient leakage is limited to about 10 µA, with higher limits for less protective classes, and applied-part isolation is tested at kV levels.
- **Wearables and implants**: biocompatibility, battery safety, hermeticity, and very low power. Implants emphasise long battery life, minimal heat, and failure modes that are safe.
- **EMC**: medical EMC (IEC 60601-1-2) addresses immunity so that life-supporting function isn't disturbed, and emissions so it doesn't disturb others.
- **Traceability and change control**: every part, process and design change is recorded in a design history file; changes may need re-validation or regulatory notification. This slows iteration but is essential.
- **Usability and alarms**: human factors and alarm management are engineering requirements.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Leakage-current limits, isolation voltages and MOPP definitions depend on the edition of IEC 60601-1, the applied-part type and the use case. The figures above are the commonly quoted orders of magnitude, so check the current standard before using them in a design.

## Same circuit, four answers

Take a 12 V-to-3.3 V buck converter feeding an MCU:

| Choice | Consumer | Automotive | Space | Medical |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Part grade | Commercial | AEC-Q100 qualified controller, AEC-Q200 caps | Screened or rad-hard controller | Medical-supplier approved part list |
| Input protection | Small TVS or none | TVS for load dump, reverse-battery FET, wide-input range (up to ~40 V rated parts) | Current limiting, latch-up protection, input filter | Isolation barrier, fusing |
| Capacitors | X5R/X7R ceramic, minimum count | Soft-termination, flex-crack aware, derated to 50% voltage | Derated heavily, screened, tantalum restrictions | Controlled sources, traceability |
| Layout | Minimum area, cost-optimised | Creepage, EMC filtering, thermal design for 125 °C | Thermal and outgassing, conformal coating, redundancy | Creepage/clearance, leakage control |
| Validation | Bench plus EMC lab | Environmental, EMC, ISO 26262 safety analysis, PPAP | Thermal vacuum, vibration, radiation, extensive review | Design verification, risk file, EMC 60601-1-2 |

This kind of table is also a great way to *answer* the question aloud: choose one circuit and walk the columns.

## Where the boundaries blur

- **Consumer in the car**: infotainment is consumer-like but must survive automotive supply and temperature.
- **New space**: commercial parts with screening, shielding and redundancy to cut cost for LEO constellations, with shorter mission life.
- **Consumer medical**: wellness wearables sit in a softer regulatory tier than diagnostic devices.
- **Industrial** (not asked) sits between consumer and automotive: –40 to 85 °C, 10+ year life, long supply.

Saying this shows you understand constraints as a continuum rather than four boxes.
:::

::: applications
- **Smartphone**: cost-optimised SoC, aggressive power management, skin-temperature limits, FCC/CE compliance ([[q0974]]).
- **Engine control unit**: AEC-Q-qualified parts, load-dump protection, ISO 26262 safety mechanisms, 15-year supply.
- **Satellite power system**: radiation-tolerant regulators, derated components, redundancy, thermal vacuum testing.
- **Avionics flight computer**: DO-254/DO-178C process, dissimilar redundancy, lightning and EMI protection.
- **Infusion pump or patient monitor**: IEC 60601 isolation, alarm safety, design history file.
- **Implantable device**: ultra-low power, hermetic packaging, conservative component choices.
:::

::: assumptions
- The industries are described by **typical** practice. Individual programmes (a rugged consumer device, a low-cost cubesat, an in-vitro diagnostic) shift the answer.
- Temperature and life numbers are **typical** and for illustration. Specific grades, missions and regulatory classes differ.
- Standards are named at the **topic level**. Edition numbers, clause details and regional variants (US vs EU) change.
- "Consumer" assumes mass-market battery or USB-powered electronics, not appliances or industrial equipment.
- The Arrhenius calculation assumes a **single dominant mechanism** with $E_a=0.7$ eV. In practice, several mechanisms operate and thermal cycling (not steady temperature) drives solder-joint fatigue.
- Valid alternative framing: group by **risk to humans** (medical, automotive, aviation) versus **risk to mission/cost** (space, defense, consumer).
:::

::: followups
### Why can't I just use consumer parts in a car?
Because the temperature range, life and supply environment are beyond what they are qualified for, and because the supplier may change the part without notice. Automotive-qualified parts pass stress tests (AEC-Q) over –40 to 125 °C, have documented process control and long-term supply. Some consumer parts do work but you'd have to qualify them yourself, and the process documentation is still needed for functional safety.

### What is load dump and how do you protect against it?
When a charged battery is disconnected while the alternator is still delivering current, the alternator output surges before regulation catches up, producing a positive transient that can reach tens of volts above nominal for tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Protect with a TVS clamp, input regulators rated for 40 V or more, series resistance or a protection FET that disconnects above a threshold. The clamp must absorb the pulse energy, which is a power and thermal calculation.

### Why does medical equipment care about microamps of leakage?
A patient connected to equipment can have a low-impedance path to their body (for example electrodes or a catheter near the heart). Currents as small as tens of microamps can be hazardous for direct cardiac contact. So standards limit patient leakage in normal and single-fault conditions and require robust isolation.

### Radiation single-event upset: how do you design around it?
Use ECC on memory with scrubbing, triple modular redundancy for critical logic, watchdogs and safe-state logic, radiation-tolerant parts for critical functions, and latch-up current limiters on supplies. The mix depends on orbit, mission length and cost.

### Scenario: you're asked to put a consumer-grade charger design into a car. What do you change?
Re-select the parts to AEC-Q grades, extend the input range and protection to cover load dump and reverse battery, verify thermal performance at 85 to 105 °C ambient, add EMC filtering for CISPR 25, check capacitors for board-flex cracking, and perform the stress and EMC test plan the OEM requires. If it connects to a safety function, add ISO 26262 analysis. The topology may stay the same but nearly everything around it changes.
:::

::: interpretations
"Design constraints" is broad. I interpret it as **what limits your design choices**: cost, size, power, temperature, reliability, safety, regulation and lifecycle. The list "consumer electronics vs automotive vs aerospace/space/defense vs medical devices" invites a compare-and-contrast. Aerospace, space and defense are grouped in the question but differ: commercial aviation is certification-driven, space is no-repair and radiation-driven, and defense often adds ruggedness and security. Say so, then pick the most telling contrasts.
:::
