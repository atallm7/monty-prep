::: basics
### In plain words
It sounds as if a coil of many thin turns must carry a big current, but it is the reverse. More turns of thinner wire means more resistance, so at a given voltage the current is smaller. A coil's force depends on ampere-turns, which is the number of turns times the current. Designers choose the number of turns to fit the supply voltage. Big currents appear only at start-up, stall, or overload. Coils burn out only when heat outruns cooling.

### Picture it
Think of pushing water through a garden hose. A longer, thinner hose lets less water through at the same pressure. Winding more turns of thinner wire is like swapping in a longer, narrower hose: the flow (current) drops sharply. To get the same push from the coil, you pair that long thin hose with a higher-pressure supply.

### Words you need
- **Ampere-turns ($NI$)**: turns times current. It sets how strongly a coil magnetizes.
- **Current density ($J$)**: current per square millimeter of wire. It sets the heating.
- **Back-EMF**: the voltage a spinning motor generates against the supply. It cuts the running current.
- **Duty cycle**: the fraction of time a solenoid may stay energized.

### The simplest example
Take a coil with room for about 2 cm³ of copper and a limit of 4 A/mm². That gives 200 ampere-turns. On 5 V you wind about 1860 turns, which draws about 108 mA. On 12 V you wind about 4460 turns of thinner wire, which draws about 45 mA. Both make 200 ampere-turns. At 4 A/mm², copper heats at only about 0.08 K per second. At 20 A/mm² it heats at about 2 K per second, so a stall can cook the coil in under a minute.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the $N^2$ resistance rule, running versus start current, solenoid duty cycles, and why coils survive in normal use.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can resolve an apparent paradox with physics rather than a slogan: coils have many turns of thin wire, yet motors and solenoids are said to draw large currents, so why don't they all burn out? The interviewer is looking for four ideas: (1) more turns means higher resistance and *lower* current for a given voltage, since what matters for force is ampere-turns; (2) the "large current" cases are start-up, stall and overload, not normal running, where back-EMF or duty cycle limits current; (3) the real design limit is current density and heat, set by $J^2\rho$ per volume and by cooling; (4) coils do burn out, but when abused, and good design (duty cycle, insulation class, protection) keeps them alive.
:::

::: why
The question exposes whether you understand how an electromagnetic actuator is sized. Many people assume force needs "lots of current"; the engineering reality is that force needs ampere-turns, and turns and current can be traded against each other through the supply voltage. Knowing this lets you choose coil windings for a given supply, understand why a solenoid has a duty-cycle rating, and explain why a 12 V coil connected to 24 V burns out even though the wire "can carry current".
:::

- **Current density $J$**: A/mm² in the conductor; sets heating and allowable current.
- **Heating per volume**: $P/\text{vol} = J^2\rho$.
- **Back-EMF and duty cycle**: why running currents are lower than $V/R$. See [[q0336]], [[q0343]].
- **Insulation class and cooling**: thermal limits. See [[q0397]].

::: answer
"I'd say the premise is slightly off in two ways. First, many turns of thin wire doesn't mean lots of current; it means the opposite. Force depends on ampere-turns, so if I use ten times as many turns I need only a tenth of the current. More turns of thinner wire has higher resistance, and at a given supply voltage it draws less current. A designer chooses the number of turns so that the current at the rated voltage suits the wire: for instance 4500 turns of about 0.12 mm wire draws 45 milliamps at 12 V and makes the same ampere-turns as 1900 turns drawing 107 milliamps on 5 V.

Second, the large currents only happen in specific situations: the instant of start when back-EMF is zero, a stall, or overload. A running motor draws much less than $V/R$ because the back-EMF opposes the supply. A solenoid is rated for a duty cycle because it draws $V/R$ continuously and heats.

So do coils always burn out? No. They fail when heat gets ahead of cooling: stall, too much voltage, too much duty, poor ventilation, high ambient. The wire survives because the design keeps current density to a few amps per square millimetre in continuous duty and the coil has the thermal mass and cooling to carry the heat away. Beyond that, a coil is a thermal clock: at 20 A/mm² it heats at about 2 K per second, so it can tolerate a short pulse but not a stall."

::: technical
**Force depends on $NI$.** The magnetomotive force $\mathcal F = NI$ drives flux through the magnetic circuit; for a given geometry, force or torque depends on $NI$, not on $N$ or $I$ separately.

**Trading turns for current.** For a coil occupying a window of copper area $kA_w$ (fill factor $k$) and mean turn length $\ell_t$, with wire area $a = kA_w/N$:
$$R = \frac{\rho N\ell_t}{a} = \frac{\rho N^2\ell_t}{kA_w},\qquad I = \frac{V}{R},\qquad NI = \frac{V\,kA_w}{\rho N\ell_t}$$
So at fixed supply voltage $V$, **more turns means thinner wire, much higher resistance ($\propto N^2$), lower current ($\propto 1/N^2$) and fewer ampere-turns ($\propto1/N$)**. Choosing $N$ fixes the operating point; the designer sets $N$ so that dissipation equals the allowed power.

**Dissipation sets ampere-turns.** $P = V^2/R = I^2R$, hence
$$NI = \sqrt{\frac{P\,kA_w}{\rho\,\ell_t}}$$
independent of $N$ and $V$: for a given coil geometry and allowed heating, the achievable ampere-turns are fixed, and turns are chosen to match the supply.

**Worked example.** Copper window $kA_w = 50\ \text{mm}^2$, mean turn 40 mm: copper volume 2 cm³ (18 g). Allowed current density $J = 4$ A/mm² gives $P = J^2\rho V_{cu} = 0.54$ W and $NI = 200$ A-turns:

| Supply | Resistance | Turns | Current | Wire diameter | $NI$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 V | 47 Ω | 1860 | 108 mA | 0.185 mm | 200 |
| 12 V | 268 Ω | 4460 | 45 mA | 0.119 mm | 200 |
| 24 V | 1070 Ω | 8930 | 22 mA | 0.084 mm | 200 |
| 48 V | 4290 Ω | 17,860 | 11 mA | 0.060 mm | 200 |

The same force-producing ampere-turns, at very different currents. (Copper at 20 °C; hot copper has about 39% higher resistance after a 100 K rise, giving slightly less current and power.)

**Heating rate depends only on current density.** For adiabatic heating of copper: $dT/dt = J^2\rho/(\rho_m c)$, with $\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}$ Ω·m, density 8960 kg/m³, $c = 385$ J/kg·K. At $J = 4$ A/mm²: 0.078 K/s; 10 A/mm²: 0.49 K/s; 20 A/mm²: 1.95 K/s. Time for +100 K (no cooling): 1280 s, 205 s and 51 s respectively. That is why short pulses at high current density are fine, and why a stall at the same density is not.

**Running versus start current.** For a motor, $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$: only at start and stall is it $V/R$. Armature windings also use parallel paths, so each conductor carries a fraction of the terminal current. For a DC solenoid, current $V/R$ is steady, so the coil is rated for a duty cycle ($ED\%$), where lower-duty coils use thinner or fewer-turn windings with more force per watt of average power.

**Typical design values.** Continuous duty with natural cooling: a few A/mm² (roughly 2–6, depending on cooling and insulation class); intermittent duty or forced cooling: 10–20 A/mm² or more for short times. Wire is also protected by enamel/varnish insulation rated to the thermal class.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Allowable current density varies with cooling, thermal class, enclosure and duty cycle. The 2–6 A/mm² figure is a typical range for small naturally cooled windings, not a standard.
:::

::: long
"Let me address the premise first. It sounds like more turns means more current, but it's the other way round. The force from a coil depends on ampere-turns, the product of turns and current. If I wind ten times as many turns with thinner wire, the resistance goes up about a hundred times for the same coil volume, so for the same supply voltage the current drops by a hundred and the ampere-turns drop by ten. The designer picks the number of turns to fit the supply: for a coil with about 2 cm³ of copper and a 4 amp per square millimetre limit, I get 200 ampere-turns whether I wind 1900 turns for 5 V, at about 110 milliamps, or 4500 turns for 12 V at 45 milliamps. The wire is thin because the current is small.

So when are the currents large? At the instant of start, when the motor has no back-EMF, during a stall, or in an overload. Once a motor is spinning, the back-EMF subtracts from the supply and the current falls to what the load needs. A solenoid is different: DC current is just V over R regardless of the plunger, so it heats whenever it's on, which is why solenoids come with a duty cycle rating.

Do coils always burn out? No. They burn out when heat generation exceeds cooling. The heating rate in the copper is current density squared times resistivity, about 0.08 K per second at 4 A per mm² and 2 K per second at 20 A per mm². So 20 A/mm² for a few seconds is fine; held for a minute it cooks the insulation. In continuous duty designers keep to a few amps per square millimetre, use varnish impregnation and thermal paths to the frame, and the drive adds current limits, thermal protection and, for solenoids, peak-and-hold. So the coil survives the normal case and fails in stall, overvoltage, excessive duty or bad cooling."
:::

::: terms
- **Ampere-turns ($NI$)**: coil current times number of turns, which sets the magnetic field drive.
- **Current density ($J$)**: current per unit conductor cross-section, A/mm².
- **Fill factor ($k$)**: fraction of the winding window occupied by copper.
- **Duty cycle / ED%**: fraction of time a coil may be energised.
- **Adiabatic heating**: temperature rise with no heat loss, an upper bound for short events.
- **Back-EMF**: voltage generated by the rotating armature opposing the supply.
- **Magnet wire**: enamelled copper wire used in coils.
- **Insulation class**: standard maximum winding temperature rating.

::: mistakes
- **"Thin wire has to carry big current."** In a well-designed coil the current is matched to the wire; more turns means less current.
- **Equating resistance with a small current limit.** A coil's current is set by the supply voltage and resistance.
- **Believing motors always draw $V/R$.** Only at start and stall. Running current is $(V - K_e\omega)/R$.
- **Putting a 12 V coil on 24 V.** Power quadruples ($V^2/R$), and the coil overheats in seconds to minutes.
- **Assuming "rated current" is a wire limit.** It is a thermal limit that depends on cooling and duty cycle.
- **Saying coils never burn out.** They do, when abused or poorly cooled.
:::

::: deep
## The paradox, resolved by geometry

Take a fixed coil: a bobbin with a window of area $A_w$, filled with copper at fill factor $k$ and mean turn length $\ell_t$. You can wind $N$ turns of wire with area $a = kA_w/N$. Increasing $N$ makes the wire thinner (each turn is smaller) and the *total length* longer ($N\ell_t$), so the resistance grows as $N^2$:

$$R = \rho\frac{N\ell_t}{a} = \frac{\rho\,\ell_t}{kA_w}\,N^2$$

On a fixed supply $V$, the current is $I = V/R \propto 1/N^2$. The force-producing ampere-turns are $NI \propto 1/N$. So adding turns *reduces* force on a fixed voltage. The reason coils have many turns is not to make more force at a given voltage; it is to make a coil that works on the available voltage without burning out. At higher supply voltages you wind more turns of thinner wire to get the same ampere-turns at the same dissipation:

$$NI = \sqrt{\frac{P\,kA_w}{\rho\,\ell_t}}$$

This depends only on allowed power $P$ and the coil geometry. It is a neat design rule: the magnetic drive you can get from a coil is set by how much heat it can shed, and turns are a free parameter to match the supply.

## Where the "lots of current" comes from

A motor's current is limited by back-EMF when running:

$$I = \frac{V - K_e\omega}{R}$$

At start ($\omega = 0$) and stall the current is $V/R$ (10 A for a 12 V, 1.2 Ω motor), several times the running value ([[q0345]], [[q0343]]). The winding is *designed* so that normal running current gives acceptable current density. For lap-wound armatures there are multiple parallel paths, so each conductor carries only a fraction of the terminal current.

Figure: The current spikes toward V/R at start and then falls as speed rises and back-EMF builds. The area under the spike is the thermal stress of each start; raising the load torque keeps the running current higher. Imagine the same coil with the rotor locked: the current stays at the spike value.

A **DC solenoid** has no back-EMF in steady state (the plunger stops): current is $V/R$ whenever energised, so power $V^2/R$ is continuous while on. That is why solenoid datasheets give duty cycle (ED%): a 25% ED coil can be overdriven because the off-time lets it cool, while a 100% ED coil is designed for continuous operation and delivers less force for the same size. Peak-and-hold drivers reduce hold power drastically ([[q0392]]).

## Heating: what actually decides survival

The power per unit copper volume is $J^2\rho$. For a short pulse with no cooling, the temperature rises at

$$\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{J^2\rho}{\rho_m c}$$

(using copper's density $\rho_m = 8960$ kg/m³ and heat capacity $c = 385$ J/kg·K). This depends only on the current density, not on turns or wire size.

Figure: Time to heat by 100 K falls as $1/J^2$. A coil carrying 4 A/mm² takes over 20 minutes to rise 100 K even with no cooling, and real heat flow adds a steady-state limit. At 20 A/mm² you have under a minute. This is why a stalled motor or a solenoid left on at overvoltage can destroy itself within seconds to minutes while the same wire is happy in normal duty.

Cooling extends all of this: the coil's heat flows through insulation and varnish to the iron and frame. Impregnation with varnish fills air gaps and greatly improves conduction. Steady-state temperature rise is $\Delta T = P\,R_{th}$, and what you may call "rated current" is the current at which $\Delta T$ equals the allowed rise for the insulation class ([[q0397]]). Typical continuous current densities for small naturally cooled windings are a few A/mm², but forced-air or liquid cooling allows far more, and short-duty designs go higher.

## So do coils always burn out?

No. Most motors and solenoids last years because:

1. **Current is matched to the wire** by choosing $N$ for the supply voltage (so continuous $J$ is a few A/mm²).
2. **Running current is below $V/R$** for motors, thanks to back-EMF; start and stall currents are brief or prevented.
3. **Duty cycle ratings** keep solenoid average power within the thermal limit.
4. **Insulation and impregnation** are rated for the expected temperature; wire enamel is typically 155–220 °C class.
5. **Protection** (fuses, current limiting, thermal cutouts, peak-and-hold drivers) catches abuse ([[q0390]], [[q0394]]).

They burn out when one of those fails: stalled motors, wrong (higher) supply voltage, stuck plunger in an AC solenoid, continuous operation of an intermittent-duty coil, blocked ventilation, or high ambient temperature. A diagnostic fingerprint of an overheated coil is a drop in resistance (shorted turns) or open circuit, a burnt smell, and discoloured varnish.

## Thin wire at high current: the exceptions

Some designs *do* push high current in thin conductors: voice-coil actuators, high-force-density pulsed solenoids, and fuse-like elements. These rely on short duty cycle, high thermal mass relative to pulse energy, or active cooling. Design checks for them use the $J^2\rho t$ energy (adiabatic) calculation above.

## A quick design recipe

1. Choose allowable power $P$ (from cooling and duty) → ampere-turns needed for the force.
2. Choose supply voltage → $R = V^2/P$ → turns $N = \sqrt{R\,kA_w/(\rho\,\ell_t)}$ → wire area $a = kA_w/N$.
3. Check $J = I/a$ against the thermal class and cooling, and check the stall / worst-case duty.
4. Add protection for the abnormal cases.
:::

::: applications
- **Relay and valve coils**: the same 100 mW to a few W of coil power wound for 5, 12 or 24 V by changing turns and wire gauge.
- **Brushed and brushless motor windings**: wire gauge and turns chosen to match the supply voltage and speed constant; high-voltage versions use more turns of thinner wire.
- **Pulsed solenoids (fuel injectors, printers)**: high current density for milliseconds, cooled between pulses.
- **Voice-coil actuators in disk drives and speakers**: thin wire, short strokes, careful thermal design.
- **Peak-and-hold solenoid drivers**: pull in at high current, hold at a fraction to cut heating.
:::

::: assumptions
The ampere-turn scaling assumes the same coil volume, fill factor and mean turn length, a linear magnetic circuit (no saturation), and constant resistivity (it rises about 0.39 %/K, so hot coils draw less current and dissipate somewhat less power at fixed voltage). The adiabatic heating figures ignore cooling and the latent thermal mass of the former and iron, so are an upper bound on heating rate for short events. Continuous current-density guidance is approximate. For motors, armature winding paths, commutation and slot geometry change the details; the principles are the same. Solenoid force also depends on air gap and magnetic circuit, so ampere-turns alone does not determine force, but it sets the magnetic drive.
:::

::: followups
### If I double the number of turns, what happens to current and force at the same voltage?
Resistance quadruples if the wire area halves, so current drops to a quarter, and ampere-turns $= 2N\times I/4$ is half. Force (roughly proportional to $(NI)^2$ in a solenoid at fixed air gap, below saturation) falls to a quarter. More turns on a fixed supply therefore weakens the coil; you need the higher voltage to match.

### Why are 24 V solenoid coils made with thinner wire than 12 V coils of the same size?
For equal power, a 24 V coil needs four times the resistance ($R = V^2/P$), so twice the turns with half the wire area, and half the current. Ampere-turns and force are the same at the same dissipation, but the wire is thinner and the current lower.

### What happens if you put a 12 V coil on 24 V?
Current doubles and power rises by four times ($V^2/R$), so the heating rate per volume is four times higher. A coil designed for continuous duty at 4 A/mm² would be running at 8 A/mm², which is about 0.31 K/s adiabatic, so it passes its insulation class limit within minutes. The iron may also saturate, so force does not double. It might survive a short pulse but not continuous duty.

### Why does a motor not burn out when running at its rated speed with a heavy load?
At its rated operating point the current is the rated current, chosen so that heating is within the thermal class at the specified ambient and cooling; the winding was designed for exactly that. If the load rises beyond rated torque, current rises proportionally (about $\tau/K_t$) and heating as $I^2$, so it will heat beyond its rating unless protected.

### Harder: how would you design a solenoid coil that must pull strongly for 50 ms once a second, from 12 V, without overheating?
Duty cycle is 5%, so average power is 5% of peak, and thermal mass averages the pulses. Allow a pulse current density of say 15–20 A/mm² (heating about 1–2 K/s, only 0.1 K per pulse at 50 ms, and cooling between pulses), so choose a thinner wire and higher resistance to get the required ampere-turns from a 12 V pulse, then check the average power and steady-state temperature rise against the insulation class. Add a peak-and-hold or current-limit if the on time could extend, and a thermal cutoff.
:::
