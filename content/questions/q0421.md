::: basics
### In plain words
A half-bridge is two switches stacked in series between the supply and ground, with the output taken from the node between them. When the top switch is on, the output is at the supply. When the bottom one is on, it is at ground. Flip between them quickly, and the average output is the fraction of time the top is on, times the supply.

### Picture it
Picture mixing water by flicking a mixer handle between a hot tap and a cold tap, very fast. If hot is on a quarter of the time, the water comes out a quarter of the way from cold to hot. The duty cycle sets the average. The one thing you must never do is open both taps into the same pipe at the same instant, because that connects hot supply straight to cold drain. In a half-bridge, that is shoot-through.

### Words you need
- **Switch node**: the junction between the two switches. It is the output.
- **Duty cycle ($D$)**: the fraction of each period the high side is on. Average output is $D V_M$.
- **Dead time**: a short gap with both switches off between transitions, so they never overlap.
- **Body diode**: the diode inside each MOSFET. It carries the load current during dead time.
- **Inductive load**: a load with a coil, like a motor, whose current cannot change instantly.

### The simplest example
With a 12 V supply and a 25% duty cycle, the output averages $0.25 \times 12 = 3$ V. Put a motor with 1 mH of inductance on it, switching at 20 kHz. Its coil smooths the current, which wiggles by only about 0.11 A each cycle. With 100 ns of dead time on each edge, 5 A flowing through a 0.7 V body diode wastes only $0.7 \times 5 \times 200\ ns \times 20\ kHz = 14$ mW.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the three output states, how the top switch gets its gate supply, load connections, and how the same circuit becomes a step-down (buck) converter or an H-bridge.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can draw the smallest useful power stage and say exactly what each part is for. The half-bridge is the building block under buck converters, H-bridges and inverters, so interviewers use it to see if you know it cold. They are checking:

1. Two switches in series from the supply to ground, with the **output at the midpoint** (the switch node).
2. Both switches **never on together**, and a dead time to guarantee it.
3. Where the load goes (ground-referenced, supply-referenced or mid-rail) and what that does to the achievable voltage.
4. Body diodes on both FETs for an inductive load, and a gate driver for the high side.
5. That you can name the three output states: high, low and high-impedance.
:::

::: why
Almost every switching power stage is a half-bridge in disguise. A synchronous buck is a half-bridge plus an LC filter ([[q0201]]), three of them make a BLDC or servo inverter, and two of them make an H-bridge ([[q0420]]). If you can draw it and explain the switch node, the interview questions on dead time, body diodes, bootstrap drivers and braking all follow from the same drawing. Interviewers also like that it takes thirty seconds to draw but has many layers of follow-ups.
:::

::: concepts
- **Series switch pair**: one switch to the supply, one to ground; the middle is the switch node ($V_{SW}$).
- **PWM average**: with fast switching the load sees $\bar V = D\,V_M$ ([[q0145]]).
- **Inductive load / freewheel**: current continues through the body diode of the off switch ([[q0144]], [[q0101]]).
- **High-side drive**: an NFET source follows the switch node, so the gate must go above the supply ([[q0426]]).
- **Shoot-through and dead time**: avoiding both switches on together ([[q0428]], [[q0207]]).
- **Complementary drive**: when one switch is on the other is off, so the output is always actively driven (CMOS inverter analogy, [[q0107]]).
:::

::: answer
"A half-bridge is just two switches stacked in series between the supply and ground, and the output comes from the node between them. The top one is the high-side switch, the bottom one is the low-side switch. If the high side is on, the output is at the supply; if the low side is on, it's at ground; if both are off, the output floats and the load current finds its own way through a body diode.

For a real one I'd use two NFETs. Low side has its source at ground, easy to drive. High side has its drain at the supply and its source on the output, so its gate has to go a few volts above the supply, which means a gate driver with a bootstrap capacitor. I draw the body diode on both FETs because the load is inductive. I show the load from the output node to ground, or to the supply, or to a mid-point made of two capacitors if I need both polarities.

The controller turns on one FET, turns it off, waits a bit of dead time, then turns on the other. The dead time is there so the two can never be on at once, which would short the supply. The average output is duty cycle times supply, and if I put an inductor and capacitor after it I've just drawn a buck converter."
:::

::: technical
**Topology.** High-side switch $Q_H$ from $V_M$ to the switch node SW; low-side switch $Q_L$ from SW to ground. Load connected from SW to a reference: ground, $V_M$, or $V_M/2$ (two series capacitors across the supply).

**Output states.**
- $Q_H$ on, $Q_L$ off: $V_{SW}\approx V_M - I R_{DS(on)}$.
- $Q_H$ off, $Q_L$ on: $V_{SW}\approx -I R_{DS(on)}$, with $I$ the current flowing out of SW into the load (slightly below ground while sourcing the load, slightly above when sinking).
- Both off: SW is high-impedance; an inductive load pushes $V_{SW}$ to $-V_{F}$ (current leaving SW, low-side body diode on) or $V_M+V_F$ (current entering SW, high-side body diode on).

**Average voltage.** Over a period $T$ with high-side duty $D$ (ignoring dead time and drops):
$$\bar V_{SW} = D\,V_M$$
With the load to ground that is also the load's average voltage; with the load to $V_M/2$ it is $(D-\tfrac12)V_M$, giving $\pm V_M/2$.

**Current ripple** for an R-L load at switching period $T$ (small ripple): $\Delta I \approx \dfrac{(V_M - \bar V)\,D\,T}{L} = \dfrac{V_M D(1-D)\,T}{L}$. Example: $V_M=12$ V, $D=0.25$, $T=50$ µs ($f_{sw}=20$ kHz), $L=1$ mH: $\Delta I=0.1125$ A.

**Dead time.** The gate timing is $\text{HIN}$, then delay $t_d$, then $\text{LIN}$, so there is a window of $t_d$ with both off. During it the load current flows in a body diode; loss $\approx V_F\,I\,2t_d f_{sw}$. For 0.7 V, 5 A, 100 ns, 20 kHz: $14$ mW.

**Gate drive.** $Q_L$ is driven from a ground-referenced supply; $Q_H$ (NFET) needs $V_G\approx V_{SW}+V_{GS(on)}$, which is typically generated with a bootstrap capacitor ([[q0206]]) or charge pump. A PFET high side avoids this at the cost of area and speed ([[q0204]]).
:::

I'd use two NFETs with body diodes, a bootstrapped gate driver for the high side, and dead time so the two never overlap. ::: long
"I'd draw the supply rail at the top, then two transistors stacked vertically, then ground at the bottom. The middle node between them is the switch node, and that's the output.

The logic is simple. If the top switch is on and the bottom is off, the output is at the supply. If it's the other way around, the output is at ground. By switching between them fast with a duty cycle D, the average voltage at the output is D times the supply. That's PWM.

For the transistors I'd use NFETs because they have the lowest on-resistance for their size. I draw the body diode on each one, because the load is usually inductive. When both switches are off, the load current has to go somewhere, and it goes through whichever body diode points the right way. That clamps the switch node to about 0.7 V above the supply or below ground, which is what keeps the transistors safe.

The low-side gate is easy: it's referenced to ground, so a driver pulls it up to 5 or 12 V. The high-side gate is harder. When the top FET is on, its source is at the supply voltage, so the gate has to be above the supply. A bootstrap capacitor, charged from a small supply while the low side is on, floats the gate driver up with the switch node. That's what a half-bridge driver IC does inside.

The most important control rule is dead time. When I switch from one FET to the other, I turn the first off, wait something like 50 to 200 nanoseconds, then turn the second on. Without that, both would conduct for a moment and short the supply.

Where does the load go? Between the switch node and ground is the simplest, between the switch node and the supply works too, and with two capacitors I can make a mid-rail so the load sees plus or minus half the supply. Add an LC filter and this is a buck. Put two of these side by side with a motor between them and it's an H-bridge."
:::

::: terms
- **Half-bridge / phase leg**: high-side and low-side switch in series with the output at the midpoint.
- **Switch node (SW, phase node)**: the junction of the two switches; the output.
- **Duty cycle ($D$)**: fraction of the period the high side is on.
- **Complementary switching**: the low side is on when the high side is off (with dead time in between).
- **Dead time**: a window where both gates are off to prevent shoot-through.
- **Bootstrap**: a capacitor and diode that create a gate supply riding on the switch node ([[q0206]]).
- **Body diode**: intrinsic MOSFET diode that carries current during dead time.
- **Split-rail / mid-point reference**: two capacitors that create $V_M/2$ so the load sees both polarities.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws two transistors and a node and says "PWM".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws the switch node and labels the **three** output states, including high-impedance.
- Includes body diodes and **says which one conducts for which current direction**.
- Names the high-side gate-drive problem (gate above the rail) and the standard fixes.
- States the dead-time rule and what it costs (diode conduction loss, distortion).
- Shows where the load connects and what that does: ground-referenced gives $0$ to $V_M$; mid-rail gives $\pm V_M/2$.
- Connects it to other topologies: buck, H-bridge, inverter, CMOS inverter.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Wiring the load across the supply.** The load goes between SW and a reference, not between $V_M$ and ground.
- **Omitting dead time** or tying the two gates together without any delay. That is shoot-through.
- **Driving the high-side NFET like the low-side one** from a ground-referenced 5 V supply. When SW is at $V_M$ the gate has $V_{GS} = 5 - V_M$, which is negative.
- **Saying the output is "off" when both switches are off.** The node is not 0 V; it is high-impedance and follows the load current through a diode.
- **Forgetting the output voltage is limited to 0 to $V_M$** with a ground-referenced load. You cannot reverse the motor without a second leg or a split supply.
:::

::: deep
## The smallest switching power stage

Take a supply $V_M$, put two switches in series and look at the node between them. The two switches are complementary: when one conducts the other doesn't. That's a **single-pole double-throw switch** from SW to either $V_M$ or ground, and if you can toggle fast and know the duty cycle, you have an adjustable *average* voltage with almost no loss in the switches themselves.

## Three output states

| $Q_H$ | $Q_L$ | $V_{SW}$ | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| on | off | $\approx V_M$ | The load sees the supply. |
| off | on | $\approx 0$ | The low side holds the node at ground. |
| off | off | floating, clamped by body diodes | Dead time. Current direction decides which diode conducts. |
| on | on | short | **Never.** |

| Return | Load voltage | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Ground | $0$ to $V_M$ | Unidirectional: fans, heaters, solenoids, LEDs, buck converters |
| $V_M$ | $-V_M$ to $0$ | Same, load on the high side (current into the load from the switch) |
| $V_M/2$ (split caps) | $\pm V_M/2$ | Bidirectional with half the voltage; class-D audio, simple ±drive |

Using the split-cap return halves the usable voltage and the caps carry the load's AC current. An H-bridge ([[q0420]]) gets the full $\pm V_M$ by using a second leg instead of the capacitors.

## Using it: duty cycle, ripple and energy flow

Figure: Toggle the two switches. Try the dead-time view and look at the body-diode conduction when both are off, then close both at once to see the shoot-through fault.

Take $V_M=12$ V, $D=0.25$, $f_{sw}=20$ kHz, motor $L=1$ mH. The inductor sees $+9$ V for $12.5$ µs and $-3$ V for $37.5$ µs, so $\Delta I=9\times12.5\,\mu s/1\,\text{mH}=0.1125$ A, and the average voltage is $3$ V. Larger $L$ or higher $f_{sw}$ means less ripple, which means less heating and torque ripple ([[q0145]]).

Energy can flow both ways. When the load current flows *out* of SW the high side sources and the low side's body diode (or the low FET, if turned on) freewheels. When the current is negative (the motor is regenerating or the inductor is discharging) the roles swap. Leaving the low side on while freewheeling (**synchronous rectification**) cuts the loss from $V_F I$ to $I^2R_{DS(on)}$: for 5 A, from 3.5 W to 0.5 W with $R_{DS(on)}=20$ mΩ.

## Why the gate drive needs a floating supply

When $Q_H$ is on, its source sits at $V_M$. A gate at 5 V would give $V_{GS}=5-12=-7$ V and turn it off. For a 5 V gate drive, the gate must be at about 17 V. A bootstrap capacitor ($C_{boot}$) charges from a 5 V supply through a diode when the low side is on, then "lifts" with SW: gate = SW + 5 V ([[q0206]], [[q0427]]). With a PFET the gate only has to go from $V_M$ down by a few volts, so it needs a level shifter instead.

## From half-bridge to everything else

- **Buck converter**: half-bridge + L + C; $V_{out}=D V_{in}$ ([[q0173]], [[q0201]]).
- **H-bridge**: two half-bridges sharing a load ([[q0420]]).
- **3-phase inverter**: three half-bridges, one per motor phase ([[q0363]]).
- **CMOS inverter / output buffer**: the same pair on a chip, at logic scale ([[q0107]]).
- **Class-D amplifier**: a half-bridge with an audio-modulated duty cycle.
:::

::: applications
- **Synchronous buck converters**: the switch pair plus an inductor and capacitor.
- **Single-direction motor and fan drives**: PWM speed control with the motor between SW and ground.
- **Half-bridge gate-driver ICs**: bootstrap driver plus level shifter plus dead time in one package.
- **Class-D audio output stages**: half-bridge from a split rail, speaker through an LC filter.
- **Resonant half-bridge converters (for example LLC)**: a half-bridge driving a resonant tank and transformer.
- **BLDC and servo drives**: each motor phase is one half-bridge.
:::

::: assumptions
- Ideal switches for the three-state analysis; real FETs add $R_{DS(on)}$ drops, finite edges and body-diode recovery.
- Load is inductive (motor, solenoid, or a buck inductor) so that the diode paths matter. For a purely resistive load the diodes never conduct.
- Single positive supply; no dead-time distortion.
- NFETs on both sides. PFET high side is a legitimate alternative with simpler drive but poorer $R_{DS(on)}$ per area.
- Small-ripple approximation for the ripple formula, valid when $L/R \gg T$.

If asked to draw it with a specific load (say a solenoid), keep the topology but add the clamp explicitly; if asked for a bidirectional motor, say a half-bridge is not enough on its own and draw the H-bridge ([[q0423]]).
:::

::: followups
### What is the voltage at the switch node when both FETs are off?
It is not defined by the switches. The inductive load current forces it: if the current was leaving SW, the low-side body diode conducts and SW sits near $-0.7$ V; if the current was entering SW, the high-side diode conducts and SW sits near $V_M+0.7$ V. If the load current is zero, SW floats to whatever the load or the parasitic capacitances set (it can ring).

### Why do you need dead time if the controller makes the gates complementary?
Turn-off is slower than turn-on in real FETs, and drivers and gate resistors add delay mismatches. If the low side starts turning on before the high side has finished turning off, you get a shoot-through pulse of tens to hundreds of amps for a few nanoseconds. Dead time costs a little diode-conduction loss (14 mW in the example above) but protects the FETs. See [[q0428]].

### How do you get a negative voltage out of a half-bridge?
Not with a ground-referenced load. Return the load to $V_M/2$ (two capacitors) and you get $\pm V_M/2$, or add a second half-bridge and make an H-bridge, which gives $\pm V_M$ ([[q0423]]).

### What if the high-side NFET sees a 12 V supply but its gate driver only has 5 V?
Use a bootstrap: charge $C_{boot}$ to about 5 V from the low-side-on interval; when SW rises to 12 V the gate sits at 17 V, so $V_{GS}\approx5$ V. The limits are the maximum duty cycle (the cap needs refreshing, [[q0206]]) and the dead-time and layout details.

### The half-bridge runs hot with no load. What do you suspect?
Shoot-through (check the dead time, gate resistor and driver), or $C_{oss}$ and gate-drive losses at high $f_{sw}$. With no load there is no conduction loss, so the heat is switching, gate-charge, or cross-conduction. Measure the supply current with the load disconnected.
:::
