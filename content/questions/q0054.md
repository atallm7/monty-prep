::: testing
Whether you can connect the physics of inductors to realistic failure mechanisms and to **datasheet ratings**. The interviewer wants:

1. **Saturation** from overcurrent (and why it's destructive in a converter).
2. **Thermal overstress**: copper and core losses, insulation breakdown, derating, and copper's positive temperature coefficient.
3. **Electrical and mechanical damage**: open or shorted windings, cracked cores, solder-joint or termination failure, vibration and thermal-cycling fatigue.
4. **Aging and environment**: iron-powder thermal aging, moisture, Curie-temperature excursions.
5. Prevention: choosing $I_{sat}$ and $I_{rms}$ with margin, derating, protection, layout and handling.
:::

::: why
In switching converters the inductor is a stressed, heat-generating part whose failure often kills the power transistor and the load with it. Many "mystery" converter failures are saturation under a transient (start-up, load step, short circuit) rather than a steady-state rating violation. Knowing the failure modes and the difference between saturation current and heating current prevents the classic mistake of choosing an inductor by inductance and DCR alone.
:::

::: concepts
- **Saturation** ([[q0046]]): above $B_{sat}$ the inductance collapses and current ramps rapidly ($di/dt=V/L$).
- **Copper and core loss** ([[q0050]]): heating sets the $I_{rms}$ rating.
- **Winding resistance and temperature**: DCR rises 0.39 %/K, so loss rises as the part heats.
- **Insulation system**: enamel/varnish rated for a maximum temperature class.
- **Mechanical properties**: ferrite is brittle; windings and terminations flex with thermal cycling and vibration.
- **Inductor specs**: $I_{sat}$ (L drop of 20–30 %), $I_{rms}$ (temperature rise, e.g. 40 K), SRF, DCR.
:::

::: answer
"The most important failure mode for an inductor in a power circuit is saturation. If the current gets high enough, the core can't carry any more flux, the inductance collapses, and the current ramps almost without limit, only limited by the resistance. In a buck converter that means the switch sees huge current and can fail. It usually happens during start-up, a load step, or a short, not at normal load.

The second is thermal: copper loss and core loss heat the part. The copper resistance rises with temperature, so losses go up as it heats. If the temperature exceeds the insulation rating, the enamel breaks down, turns short together, which reduces inductance and raises loss, and eventually it can burn open. Datasheets give a heating current, I rms, for a given temperature rise, in addition to the saturation current.

Then there are mechanical and open or short failures: ferrite is brittle and can crack from board flex or thermal shock, changing the gap and the inductance; the fine wire can break at the termination from vibration or thermal cycling and open; solder joints can fatigue. And aging: iron powder cores can thermally age at high temperature, with permeability dropping and loss rising.

To avoid these I pick the saturation current above the peak current including worst-case transients, derate for temperature, check I rms and the thermal rise, and consider current limiting in the controller."
:::

::: technical
| Failure mode | Cause | Effect | Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Saturation** | Peak current > $I_{sat}$ (overload, start-up, short, high temp lowers $B_{sat}$) | $L$ collapses, $di/dt=V/L$ jumps, switch overcurrent, overheating | $I_{sat}$ ≥ peak + margin at max temperature; cycle-by-cycle current limit; soft-saturating core |
| **Thermal overstress** | $I_{rms}^2R_{ac}$ + core loss; high ambient; poor thermal path | Insulation degrades, DCR rises, part discolors, solder reflows | Derate; check ΔT ≤ rating; copper pours; airflow |
| **Turn-to-turn short** | Insulation breakdown (heat, voltage spike, abrasion) | Lower L, high local loss, runaway | Insulation class, derating, controlled voltage spikes |
| **Open winding** | Wire break at termination, fusing, fatigue | Open circuit; converter loses current path (voltage spike if switch opens) | Robust terminations, strain relief, vibration testing |
| **Cracked/chipped core** | Board flex, thermal shock, mechanical shock | Gap change → L shift, audible noise, loss increase | Layout away from board edges/holes; handling; shielded constructions |
| **Core aging (iron powder)** | Prolonged temperature above spec | Permeability drops, loss rises, then runaway | Use sendust/MPP/high-flux or limit temperature |
| **Curie-temperature excursion** | Overheating a ferrite | Permeability collapses (reversible if cooled) | Keep below $T_C$ with margin |
| **Solder/termination fatigue** | Thermal cycling, vibration, CTE mismatch | Intermittent or open connection | Adequate pad design, underfill/staking for heavy parts |
| **Moisture/contamination** | Humidity, residues | Leakage, corrosion of winding | Conformal coating, sealed/molded parts |

**Rating definitions (typical):** $I_{sat}$: current where $L$ drops by 20–30 % (vendor-specific); $I_{rms}$ (heating current): DC current causing 40 K rise (vendor-specific); use the *lower* of the two plus margin.

**Heating with temperature-dependent DCR:** $\Delta T=\dfrac{I^2R_0\theta}{1-\alpha I^2R_0\theta}$, $\alpha=0.0039\ \text{K}^{-1}$: the loss rises as the part heats, and runaway occurs at $I=1/\sqrt{\alpha R_0\theta}$ (17.9 A for $R_0=20$ mΩ, $\theta=40$ K/W).
:::

::: long
"The failure mode I worry about most is saturation. An inductor stores energy in its core, and the flux density is limited, so above the saturation current the inductance collapses, in some cores abruptly. With constant voltage across it, di/dt equals V over L, so as L collapses the current slope jumps, say from 1.9 amps per microsecond to five or ten times that. In a buck converter that means the peak current goes through the switch and the inductor without limit, and the FET can overheat or fail. It's usually caused not by the steady-state load but by transients: start-up into a charged or short-circuited output, load steps, or an overcurrent fault, and saturation current is lower at high temperature.

Second, thermal. Copper loss and core loss heat the inductor, copper's resistance rises about 0.4 percent per kelvin, so there's positive feedback; for a 20 milliohm, 40 kelvin-per-watt part the self-heating gets out of hand at around 18 amps. If the winding exceeds its insulation class, the enamel breaks down, turns short, inductance and Q fall, local heating rises, and the winding can burn open. That's why datasheets give a heating current, I rms, at a given temperature rise, as well as I sat.

Third, mechanical. Ferrite is brittle: board flex or thermal shock can crack it, which changes the gap and the inductance, makes noise, or breaks the winding at the termination. Vibration and thermal cycling can fatigue the fine wire where it connects to the pad, or the solder joint, giving an intermittent or open circuit, and an open inductor in a converter can create a large voltage spike.

And aging: iron powder cores slowly age at high temperature, with permeability falling and loss rising, which accelerates heating. Moisture can corrode windings if the part isn't sealed.

To prevent all this I choose I sat above the worst-case peak current at the highest temperature, I rms with margin on the temperature rise, a controller with cycle-by-cycle current limit, a robust, shielded or molded construction, and good layout practice."
:::

::: terms
- **Saturation current $I_{sat}$**: current at which inductance falls by a stated percentage.
- **Heating (rms) current $I_{rms}$**: current giving a specified temperature rise.
- **Soft vs hard saturation**: gradual vs abrupt inductance loss.
- **Interturn short**: insulation failure between adjacent turns.
- **Insulation class**: maximum continuous winding temperature rating.
- **Curie temperature**: temperature above which a ferromagnet loses its magnetization.
- **Thermal aging**: permanent change of core properties after prolonged heat.
- **Cycle-by-cycle current limit**: converter protection that terminates the switch on-time at a peak current.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "inductors can overheat or saturate".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Puts saturation first and explains the runaway ($di/dt=V/L$ jumping when $L$ collapses) with numbers.
- Explains when it happens in practice (start-up, load step, short, hot, and lower $B_{sat}$ at temperature).
- Distinguishes $I_{sat}$ from $I_{rms}$ and quotes the vendor's definitions, using the lower of the two.
- Describes thermal feedback from copper's positive TCR and core loss, and insulation/turn-short failure.
- Covers mechanical (cracked core, termination fatigue), aging (iron powder), and environment (moisture, Curie).
- Gives protection strategies: peak current limit, soft saturating core, derating, shielded/molded construction, layout.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Choosing an inductor by L and DCR only.** Without $I_{sat}$ and $I_{rms}$ you don't know if it survives.
- **Using the room-temperature $I_{sat}$.** $B_{sat}$ falls with temperature (ferrite 0.5 → 0.4 T at 100 °C), so $I_{sat}$ at 100–125 °C is lower.
- **Sizing for the average or rms current only.** Saturation depends on the *peak* current, including ripple and transients.
- **Assuming a vendor's "$I_{sat}$" is a hard limit.** It's the point of, say, 30 % inductance drop; the part continues to degrade above it, and the definition differs by vendor.
- **Ignoring start-up and fault currents.** Soft-start and current limit are part of protecting the inductor.
- **Believing a hot-looking inductor is healthy because it still measures the right L.** A partial turn short or aging may not show on a quick LCR check.
- **Not considering mechanical stress.** Ferrite near board edges or screw holes can crack.
:::

::: deep
## 1. Saturation: the dominant power-circuit failure

Recap ([[q0046]]): the flux density is $B=Li/(NA_e)$; when it reaches $B_{sat}$, the permeability falls and $L$ collapses. With a fixed applied voltage, $di/dt=v/L$.

Example: a buck inductor $L=4.7\ \mu$H with $V_{in}-V_o=8.7$ V has $di/dt=1.85$ A/µs normally. If it saturates and $L$ falls to 20 % (0.94 µH), the slope becomes 9.3 A/µs: five times faster. In a short delay (a 200 ns current-sense blanking time plus comparator delay) the current can overshoot by an extra ~1.9 A beyond the limit; the saturated inductor can no longer limit the rate, so the controller's current limit must act very quickly.

When it happens:
- **Start-up / output short:** the output is at 0 V so the inductor sees the full input voltage with a long on-time; the current ramps and saturates before the limit reacts.
- **Load step or fault** raising the average current.
- **High temperature** lowering $B_{sat}$ and so $I_{sat}$.
- **Component tolerance:** inductance tolerance (±20 %) and $I_{sat}$ tolerance; and peak = average + half the ripple.
- **Hard-saturating cores** (ferrite) are more dangerous than soft-saturating powder cores for the same rating.

Consequences: switch and diode overcurrent, overheating, EMI spikes, and in the worst case a destroyed switch and a rail dumped into the load. Rating practice: $I_{sat}\ge1.2$–1.3× the maximum peak current including ripple and current-limit tolerance.

## 2. Thermal overstress and winding insulation

Heating: $P=I_{dc}^2R_{dc}+I_{ac}^2R_{ac}+P_{core}$, $\Delta T=P\theta$ ([[q0050]]). Copper resistance rises with temperature, so the true heating is higher:

$$\Delta T=\frac{I^2R_0\theta}{1-\alpha I^2R_0\theta}.$$

Real parts rarely get there because other things fail first (insulation, solder, core), but it shows why derating matters at high ambient temperature.

At the insulation limit (enamel class, e.g. 155 °C or 180 °C depending on type), the varnish degrades, causing **turn-to-turn shorts**: a shorted turn is a lossy shorted secondary of a transformer, drawing a large circulating current for the same flux, producing local heating that destroys neighbouring turns. Symptoms: inductance drops, Q collapses, the part runs hot at low load, discoloration/odor. A voltage spike (inductive kick, ringing) can also puncture insulation between layers.

## 3. Mechanical failure modes

- **Cracked core.** Ferrite and powder cores are brittle. Board flex, press-fit connectors nearby, drops, and thermal shock cause cracks, which act like additional gaps (L falls, fringing increases, audible noise and loss).
- **Winding/termination breaks.** Fine wires are welded or soldered to terminals; thermal cycling and vibration fatigue the connection. An open inductor in a buck converter can cause the switch node to ring or generate a large voltage spike when the current path vanishes ([[q0144]]).
- **Solder joints.** Heavy inductors have large thermal mass and weight; use pad design, adhesive/staking and avoid mixing with small parts that reflow differently.
- **Acoustic noise** (magnetostriction, loose windings) is not a failure but is a symptom of mechanical looseness, particularly at light-load modes.

## 4. Aging and environment

- **Iron-powder aging:** the organic binder degrades at sustained high temperature; permeability declines and loss rises, accelerating heating. Sendust/MPP/high-flux don't have that issue ([[q0049]]).
- **Ferrite:** above its Curie temperature the permeability collapses (reversible after cooling) and $B_{sat}$ is lower when hot.
- **Moisture and contamination:** corrosion of windings and terminals, leakage across the bobbin.
- **Radiation, shock and vibration** requirements in automotive/aerospace use call for molded or potted constructions.

## 5. Specs to read

| Spec | Meaning | Common pitfall |
|---|---|---|
| $I_{sat}$ | $L$ drops 20 % or 30 % (vendor-specific) at a given temperature | Using the 25 °C value; ignoring tolerance |
| $I_{rms}$ (heating) | DC current causing a 40 K rise (or 20 K) | Ignoring ambient and enclosure heating; using DC rating for high ripple |
| DCR (and ACR) | Winding resistance at 20–25 °C | Not applying the temperature coefficient |
| SRF | Parasitic resonance | Operating near or above it |
| Shielding | Magnetic containment | Neighbouring components affected |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $I_{sat}$ and $I_{rms}$ definitions (percent inductance drop, allowed temperature rise, test conditions) differ between manufacturers, and some quote both at 25 °C. Always read the footnotes of the specific datasheet.

## 6. Protecting the inductor

1. **Cycle-by-cycle current limit** in the controller set below $I_{sat}$ with propagation delay accounted for.
2. **Soft-start** and proper pre-bias handling to avoid start-up saturation.
3. **Margin and derating:** $I_{sat}$ ≥ 1.2–1.3× worst-case peak at hot temperature; $I_{rms}$ ≥ ~1.2× the rms current; temperature rise within limits including the neighbors.
4. **Soft-saturating cores** for harsh load profiles.
5. **Layout:** keep brittle parts away from board edges; give heat a path (copper pours, vias); allow airflow.
6. **Validation:** measure the inductor current waveform at start-up, load step and short-circuit with a proper current probe, and check the temperature rise at the worst case. Check for a visible flattening/knee in the current waveform, a sign of saturation.
:::

::: applications
- **Switching converter inductors** (buck, boost, flyback): saturation and thermal margins determine robustness.
- **Automotive and industrial converters**: high ambient temperature and vibration require molded, AEC-Q200 parts and derating.
- **EMI chokes**: saturation of common-mode chokes by imbalance current, and bead saturation with DC bias.
- **LED drivers and motor drivers**: start-up transients and fault currents.
- **RF coils**: small wire bonds/terminations failing from thermal cycling.
- **Failure analysis**: diagnosing "why did the FET blow", checking for inductor saturation.
:::

::: assumptions
- Ratings follow the typical **vendor convention** ($I_{sat}$ at 20–30 % L drop, $I_{rms}$ at ~40 K rise), which varies.
- The thermal model is a single lumped $\theta$; real parts have gradients and the board/ambient matter.
- Saturation analysis assumes a constant applied voltage and ignores winding resistance during the fast ramp.
- Failure rates are not quoted; reliability depends on the part's qualification (AEC-Q200, etc.).
- Different from capacitors ([[q0036]]) and resistors ([[q0019]]): inductors rarely "wear out"; they mostly fail from overstress or mechanical damage.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the converter's current limit sometimes fail to protect against inductor saturation?
The controller has a delay (blanking and comparator/driver propagation) of 50–200 ns or more. If the inductor has saturated, $di/dt$ is several times higher, so the current overshoots the threshold by $\Delta I=(V/L_{sat})\,t_{delay}$; for 0.94 µH, 8.7 V and 200 ns that's about 1.9 A extra. Choose $I_{sat}$ above the limit plus this overshoot, or use a faster limit.

### Your inductor is rated 8 A $I_{sat}$ and 6 A $I_{rms}$ but the converter runs 5 A DC with 3 A p-p ripple. Is it fine?
Peak current is 5 + 1.5 = 6.5 A < 8 A (with margin ≈ 1.23×, acceptable but low if the current limit is at 8 A or the part is hot). RMS is $\sqrt{25+3^2/12}=5.1$ A, below 6 A. Check $I_{sat}$ at the hot temperature and the AC loss at high ripple.

### How would you tell a saturated inductor from a short in a converter by looking at the waveforms?
Saturation shows the inductor current ramp *bending upward* (increasing slope) near the peak of each cycle, at higher current; the knee is visible with a current probe. A turn-to-turn short typically shows low inductance throughout (a large ripple at all currents, with higher than expected ripple and heating), so the slope is steeper everywhere and the LCR measurement is off.

### What if an inductor measures correct inductance on an LCR meter but the circuit still fails?
The LCR meter tests at small signal and no DC bias. The part could saturate at the operating current, have a bad connection that only opens under thermal stress or vibration, or have a high AC/core loss at the operating frequency. Measure under bias (DC-bias test fixture) and at the real waveform.

### Which is safer in a fault: a ferrite or a powder-core inductor of the same rated current?
Powder cores saturate gradually, so the current rises more slowly after the knee, giving the controller time to respond; ferrite saturates abruptly, so a fault can cause a sudden current spike. That's one reason powder or composite cores are favored in harsh-load converters, at the cost of higher core loss and size.
:::
