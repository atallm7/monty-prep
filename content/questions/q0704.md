::: testing
Whether you can answer "how good is this supply?" with a **structured, load-driven list** rather than "low ripple". The interviewer wants DC accuracy and regulation, ripple and noise, spikes, transient response, output impedance/PSRR, start-up behaviour, efficiency and protection, each with how you would measure it and what the load actually needs. The strongest answers say that quality is relative to the load's tolerance budget (a 1 V core at ±3% has about 30 mV for *everything*), and that bandwidth, probing and load conditions change the number you read.
:::

::: why
Power rails fail silently: a marginal supply gives random resets, ADC noise, jitter and "works on my bench" bugs. Specifying and verifying supply quality is a standard part of hardware bring-up and design review, and it connects power electronics ([[q0152]], [[q0218]]) with signal and power integrity. Being able to translate an IC's supply requirement into a measurable list (accuracy, ripple, transient, impedance) is the practical skill behind "make sure the rail is clean".
:::

::: concepts
- **Accuracy and regulation**: initial tolerance, temperature drift, line regulation and load regulation ([[q0152]]).
- **Ripple and noise**: periodic and random AC content on the DC level ([[q0705]], [[q0194]]).
- **Transient response**: droop, overshoot and settling after a load step ([[q0248]], [[q0249]]).
- **Spikes**: fast narrowband excursions at switching edges ([[q0707]]).
- **Output impedance / target impedance**: $Z_{target}=\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I_{step}$, the PDN view ([[q0142]], [[q0143]]).
- **Start-up, sequencing and protection** ([[q0171]], [[q0343]]).
- **Efficiency and thermal behaviour** ([[q0162]], [[q0164]]).
:::

::: answer
"I'd characterise it against what the load needs, in about five groups.

First, DC accuracy: the output voltage at room temperature, then how it moves with input voltage, which is line regulation, with load current, which is load regulation, and with temperature. Second, AC on top of the DC: ripple, which is the periodic part at the switching frequency, plus broadband noise and fast spikes. Third, dynamic behaviour: how far the rail droops or overshoots when the load steps and how long it takes to settle, which is really the output impedance of the supply across frequency, and for a linear regulator its PSRR. Fourth, start-up and shutdown: monotonic ramp, no overshoot, inrush, sequencing with other rails. And fifth, the practical side: efficiency, dropout, quiescent current, thermal rise, and protection such as current limit and under-voltage lockout.

How good does it need to be? That comes from the load. If a 1 V core allows 3 percent total, that's 30 mV for DC error, ripple and transients combined, so I'd budget something like 10 for accuracy, 5 for ripple and 15 for the load step, and the 15 mV over, say, a 5 A step gives me a target output impedance of 3 milliohms.

To measure, I'd use a DMM for DC, a scope with the bandwidth limit, a short ground spring and a proper load for ripple and spikes, an electronic load for steps, and a network analyser for impedance or loop response."
:::

::: technical
**DC.**
- *Set-point accuracy*: initial tolerance (reference ±0.5 to 1%, feedback resistors ±0.1 to 1%) plus drift. For a divider with $V_{FB}$ and resistor tolerances: $\Delta V/V\approx\Delta V_{FB}/V_{FB}+(1-V_{FB}/V_{out})(\varepsilon_{R1}-\varepsilon_{R2})$ worst case $\approx$ sum of magnitudes.
- *Line regulation*: $\Delta V_{out}/\Delta V_{in}$ (mV/V or %/V). *Load regulation*: $\Delta V_{out}/\Delta I_{out}$, equivalent to a DC output resistance $R_{out}$ (mΩ).
- *Efficiency* $\eta=P_{out}/P_{in}$; *dropout* for LDOs.

**AC (steady state).**
- *Ripple* at $f_{sw}$ (or $2f_{line}$): for a buck $\Delta V\approx\Delta I_L/(8f_{sw}C)+\Delta I_L\,ESR$. Measured in mVpp, bandwidth-limited (commonly 20 MHz) so the number must state how it was measured.
- *Noise*: broadband, quoted in µVrms over a band or nV/√Hz; *spikes*: tens of ns, set by loop inductance ($V=L\,dI/dt$).
- *PSRR* of a linear regulator: $20\log_{10}(\Delta V_{in}/\Delta V_{out})$ at a frequency; 60 dB at 1 kHz may fall to 30 dB at 1 MHz.

**Dynamic.**
- *Load-step response*: droop $\Delta V_{droop}\approx\Delta I\cdot Z_{out}$ (for a loop of bandwidth $f_c$, $Z_{out}\approx1/(2\pi f_cC)$); overshoot on release; settling time.
- *Target impedance* $Z_{target}=\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I_{step}$, flat across frequency up to the load's highest transient content ([[q0142]]).
- *Stability margins*: phase margin (45 to 60° typical), gain margin ($>$ 6 to 10 dB).

**Start-up and protection.** Soft-start time, monotonic rise, no overshoot, inrush $I=C\,dV/dt$, power-good thresholds, UVLO, current limit, thermal shutdown, short-circuit behaviour.

**Measurement.** DMM (DC, 4-wire for tiny drops); scope with BW-limit, short ground spring, 1× or 10× probe, AC coupling and offset ([[q0997]]); electronic load with defined slew rate (for example 1 A/µs); injection transformer plus network analyser for loop gain; VNA shunt-through for $Z_{out}$ below 1 Ω; thermal camera and power analyser for efficiency.
:::

::: short
"I'd characterise a supply against the load's tolerance budget. DC: set-point accuracy, line and load regulation, temperature drift. AC: ripple at the switching frequency, broadband noise and spikes, quoted with the measurement bandwidth. Dynamic: droop and overshoot on a load step, output impedance and PSRR. Start-up: monotonic, controlled inrush, sequencing. Plus efficiency, thermal and protection. I'd measure with a DMM, a scope with short ground and bandwidth limit, an electronic load and a network analyser."
:::

::: long
"I'd group it into five things, all judged against what the load can tolerate.

DC accuracy: the output voltage at nominal conditions, then line regulation, how it moves when the input changes, load regulation, how it moves with load current, which is effectively a DC output resistance, and temperature drift. The set-point error comes from the reference and the feedback resistors, so I'd add them up worst-case.

AC on top of the DC: ripple, the periodic component at the switching frequency; noise, the broadband part, quoted in microvolts RMS over a band; and spikes, fast narrow excursions at the switching edges. These depend on how you measure them. A 20 MHz bandwidth limit shows the ripple and hides the spikes, and a long ground lead adds fake spikes, so I always state the setup.

Dynamic behaviour: what happens when the load steps. I look at droop, overshoot, settling time, and the underlying quantity, the output impedance across frequency. For an LDO, PSRR matters: how much input ripple makes it through at the ripple frequency. For the load I'd define a target impedance, allowed voltage change divided by the current step.

Start-up and shutdown: a monotonic ramp, no overshoot, controlled inrush, and the right order relative to other rails.

And the practical items: efficiency, dropout, quiescent current, thermal rise and protection like current limit and under-voltage lockout.

For a number I'd budget from the load: a 1 V core at plus or minus 3 percent has 30 mV total, so maybe 10 for DC accuracy, 5 for ripple and 15 for transients, and with a 5 A step that's a 3 milliohm target impedance. Then I'd verify with a scope, an electronic load and an impedance or loop measurement."
:::

::: terms
- **Line regulation**: change in output voltage per change in input voltage (mV/V or %/V).
- **Load regulation**: change in output voltage per change in load current (mV/A, or as $R_{out}$).
- **Ripple**: periodic AC component on the DC output, at the switching or rectified-line frequency.
- **Noise**: random broadband voltage fluctuation, quoted in µVrms or nV/√Hz.
- **Spike (glitch)**: a brief, high-frequency excursion, typically at switching edges.
- **PSRR**: power supply rejection ratio, input-ripple attenuation of a regulator at a given frequency.
- **Output impedance ($Z_{out}$)**: the supply's impedance as seen by the load across frequency.
- **Target impedance**: $\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I_{step}$, the PDN design goal.
- **Transient response**: voltage deviation and recovery time after a load step.
- **Soft-start**: controlled output ramp to limit inrush.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "voltage, current, ripple, efficiency".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames quality *against the load's requirement* and builds a **tolerance budget** (accuracy + ripple + transient) with numbers.
- Separates **DC, steady-state AC, dynamic, start-up and protection**, and gives the metric and unit for each (mV/V, mΩ, mVpp, µVrms, dB, µs).
- Ties droop to **output impedance** and target impedance, not just "transient response".
- Says how each is **measured** and the traps: scope bandwidth limit, probe ground lead, load conditions, AC coupling.
- Mentions PSRR for linear regulators and the ripple/spike distinction ([[q0705]], [[q0707]]).
- Notes that "quality" also includes behaviour in abnormal conditions: start-up, brownout, short circuit, hot plug.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Quoting ripple without bandwidth or probing details.** "20 mV ripple" on a 500 MHz scope with a 15 cm ground lead isn't comparable with a datasheet's 20 MHz-limited, tip-and-barrel number.
- **Equating "low ripple" with a good supply.** An accurate, quiet rail can still collapse on a load step because its output impedance is too high.
- **Measuring at the supply instead of at the load.** The voltage at the IC pins includes board drops and inductance ([[q0142]]).
- **Using only a static load.** Dynamic and pulsed loads (processors, RF bursts, motors) expose problems that a resistor never shows.
- **Ignoring temperature and tolerance stack-up.** Resistors, references, capacitors (DC bias derating) all drift.
- **Confusing line regulation with PSRR.** Line regulation is DC; PSRR is AC and frequency dependent.
- **Forgetting start-up and shutdown behaviour.** Overshoot at power-up or a non-monotonic ramp can damage or lock up the load.
:::

::: deep
## Start from the load

A supply is "good" if the load works correctly with it across all conditions. So the first step is the load's requirements (datasheet "recommended operating conditions"): the allowed voltage window, the current range and slew rate, noise sensitivity (ADC, PLL, RF), and sequencing needs. Everything else is derived. For example, a processor core at 1.0 V with a $\pm3\%$ window:

| Budget item | Allocation | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| DC accuracy (reference, resistors, load/line regulation, temperature) | 10 mV | reference ±0.5% and 0.1% resistors, 2 mV load regulation |
| Ripple and noise (peak, at the load pin) | 5 mV | switching ripple after filtering, plus random noise |
| Transient (droop or overshoot) | 15 mV | load step response |
| **Total** | **30 mV** | ±3% of 1.0 V |

With a 5 A load step the allowed 15 mV gives $Z_{target}=15\text{ mV}/5\text{ A}=3\ \text{m}\Omega$. That is the number the supply plus decoupling must meet from DC up to the frequency where the load's current demand ends ([[q0142]], [[q0143]]). If the supply loop bandwidth is 100 kHz, bulk capacitors must take over between there and the point where the on-die capacitance takes over.

## DC: accuracy and regulation

- **Set-point accuracy.** For a feedback divider ([[q0008]]) with $V_{out}=V_{FB}(1+R_1/R_2)$, the worst-case error combines the reference tolerance and both resistors: with $V_{FB}$ ±1% and 0.1% resistors at $V_{out}=3.3$ V and $V_{FB}=0.8$ V the divider adds about $(1-0.8/3.3)\cdot0.2\%=0.15\%$, so the total is about 1.15%.
- **Line regulation**, for example 0.02%/V: for a 12 V ±1 V input on a 3.3 V rail the change is $0.0002\cdot3.3\cdot2=1.3$ mV.
- **Load regulation**: 5 mV for a 1 A change on a 3.3 V rail is 0.15% and an equivalent $R_{out}=5\ \text{m}\Omega$.
- **Temperature drift**: reference tempco (tens of ppm/°C) and resistor tempcos; over 60 °C a 50 ppm/°C reference moves 0.3%.

## AC: ripple, noise, spikes

Three different things, often confused:

| | Ripple | Noise | Spikes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | periodic at $f_{sw}$ | random, broadband | brief, at edges |
| Cause | inductor current ripple through $C$ and ESR | reference, regulator, thermal, shot | $L\,dI/dt$, reverse recovery, ringing |
| Typical metric | mVpp | µVrms, nV/√Hz | peak mV, width ns |
| Fix | larger $L$, $C$, filter | quieter reference, LDO post-regulation | smaller loops, snubbers ([[q0707]]) |

Ripple details are on [[q0705]]; spike capture on [[q0707]]. For noise-sensitive loads, compare the rail's noise density with the load's sensitivity: for example a PLL or ADC reference may need under 10 µVrms in 10 Hz to 100 kHz, which is below what an unfiltered switcher offers, so an LDO with good PSRR follows it ([[q0168]]).

**PSRR in practice.** An LDO with 40 dB PSRR at 500 kHz turns 100 mVpp of switcher ripple into 1 mVpp. At 1 MHz with PSRR of 30 dB the same ripple gives 3.2 mV. PSRR falls with frequency as the pass-device loop gain falls, so a ferrite bead or LC filter ahead of the LDO helps at high frequencies.

## Dynamic: transient response and output impedance

When the load current steps by $\Delta I$ the supply's loop cannot respond instantly, so the output capacitors supply the difference. The voltage change is $\Delta V\approx\Delta I\cdot Z_{out}$ where $Z_{out}$ is evaluated at the frequencies in the step's spectrum. A crude estimate: for a converter with crossover frequency $f_c$ and output capacitance $C$, $Z_{out}\approx1/(2\pi f_cC)$ near $f_c$; for $f_c=100$ kHz and $C=200$ µF that is 8 mΩ, so a 5 A step gives 40 mV, which exceeds the 15 mV budget. Raising the crossover frequency, adding capacitance, or placing capacitors at the load all lower the impedance ([[q0249]]).

Loop stability is checked with a Bode injection measurement: phase margin 45 to 60° keeps the step response well damped ([[q0244]]).

## Start-up and shutdown

Record the rail during power-up with the scope triggered on the enable or input. Look for: monotonic rise, a ramp time consistent with the soft-start capacitor, no overshoot above the load's absolute maximum, an inrush current within the source's limit ($I=C_{out}\,dV/dt$, for example 200 µF ramped in 2 ms to 3.3 V draws 0.33 A) and correct order with other rails ([[q0171]], [[q0706]]). Also check shutdown: how fast the rail decays, and whether it back-feeds something.

## Efficiency, thermal and protection

Efficiency $\eta=P_{out}/P_{in}$ across the load range (light load behaviour matters for battery life, [[q0165]]), temperature rise of inductor and FETs, current-limit behaviour (foldback, hiccup), UVLO and brownout response, and what happens on short circuit, reverse polarity or hot-plug.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Offer the five-bucket structure and then ask: "What's the load? That sets the budget." Interviewers love hearing you derive the target impedance from the load's tolerance and current step.

## Measurement traps

- **Scope bandwidth limit (20 MHz)** is the convention for datasheet ripple numbers; spikes need full bandwidth but also a proper probe technique. Spikes seen with a 10 cm ground lead may be partly probe artifacts ([[q0997]]).
- **Probe 1× vs 10×**: 1× has higher sensitivity and lower noise floor at low amplitude, but a lower bandwidth and heavier loading.
- **AC coupling** removes the DC so you can zoom on mV ripple; use 50 Ω coax with a DC block for the cleanest view of fast noise.
- **Measure at the load pins** (or the nearest decoupling capacitor), not at the regulator output.
- **Use a realistic load**: pulsed or dynamic, at the temperature and input voltage extremes.
:::

::: applications
- **FPGA/SoC core rails**: ±3% budgets, target impedance in the single-digit mΩ, with large load steps.
- **ADC/DAC and sensor supplies**: low noise, ripple rejected through LDO and filter; ratiometric references.
- **PLL/clock supplies**: noise on the supply becomes jitter (a few mV of ripple can cause picoseconds of jitter).
- **RF power amplifiers**: pulsed loads (such as TDMA bursts) demand fast transient response and low droop.
- **Motor drives and relays**: spikes and sag at load changes, plus brownout resets.
- **Battery products**: efficiency at light load, UVLO and soft-start inrush.
- **Lab and production test**: pass/fail limits on voltage, ripple, current limit and power-good timing.
:::

::: assumptions
- **The load defines "quality".** The same supply may be fine for LEDs and unacceptable for an ADC reference.
- **Numbers are examples**: 1 V ±3%, 5 A steps, 500 kHz ripple; real parts have their own datasheet limits.
- **Measurements are bandwidth-dependent.** A 20 MHz limit (common convention) hides high-frequency spikes; full bandwidth shows probe artifacts as well.
- **Small-signal assumptions** for $Z_{out}$ and PSRR; large steps are nonlinear (current limiting, slew-rate-limited inductor current).
- **Temperature and tolerance** are assumed to be accounted for by worst-case or statistical stack-up.

Some references define "power quality" for AC mains (harmonics, sags, THD, flicker). That is a different usage; here the question is about a DC supply rail on a board.
:::

::: followups
### What's the difference between ripple and noise?
Ripple is deterministic and periodic at the switching (or twice the line) frequency and its harmonics. It comes from the inductor current charging and discharging the output capacitor. Noise is random broadband (reference, regulator internal circuitry, resistors). On a scope they are separated in the frequency domain: ripple is a line at $f_{sw}$, noise is the floor between the lines. Spikes are a third category: fast edges caused by switching transitions.

### How would you measure ripple correctly on a 3.3 V rail?
Scope on the output at the load, probe tip-and-barrel or a coax with a 50 Ω input and DC block (no long ground clip), 20 MHz bandwidth limit if comparing with datasheet numbers, AC coupling or an offset to zoom, a load drawing representative current, a timebase covering several switching cycles, and then a second full-bandwidth capture to look for spikes. I'd note the setup alongside the number.

### A rail meets the ripple spec but the processor still resets on a load step. Why?
Ripple is steady-state; the reset is a transient problem. The output impedance (loop bandwidth, bulk capacitance, decoupling) isn't low enough: $\Delta V=\Delta I\cdot Z_{out}$ exceeds the brown-out threshold. I'd measure the droop at the processor pins with a step of the actual load, and compare it with the target impedance. Fixes: more capacitance close to the load, a faster loop, a lower-ESR bulk capacitor, or a smaller inductor.

### Scenario: your 1.0 V core rail passes at room temperature but fails at hot. What do you suspect?
Several things drift with temperature: reference and resistor drift (DC offset), higher on-resistance (more droop in the FETs and PCB copper: resistance rises about 0.4%/°C), capacitor derating (MLCC DC bias and temperature class), reduced loop phase margin, and the load's own current increasing (leakage). I'd compare DC, ripple, and transient at both temperatures and look for margin that's lost in one place.

### Harder: how do you separate "supply is noisy" from "my probe is noisy"?
Short the probe tip to its ground at the measurement point (the "probe loop test"): whatever remains is the setup noise floor. Then compare 1× and 10×, tip-and-barrel versus clip lead, and a coax connection. If the "noise" disappears with a better probe technique, or doesn't change when the supply is turned off or replaced by a battery, it was the setup. A spectrum analyser or low-noise preamp then gives the actual noise density.
:::
