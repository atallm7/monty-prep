::: basics
### In plain words
A multilayer circuit board is built up from alternating layers of copper and insulation, stacked in a fixed order and pressed into one solid piece. The copper layers carry signals, power and ground. The insulating layers keep the copper apart and hold the board flat. How thick the insulation is between two copper layers decides important things later, such as how wide a trace must be.

### Picture it
Think of plywood. Thin planks are glued together with glue that is runny when hot and hard when cured. In a board, a core is like a finished plank that is already hard, and prepreg is the half-cured glue layer that melts, flows into every gap and then sets. Press the stack under heat and you get one solid sheet. Unlike plywood, the board has copper that is etched into patterns before it is pressed in.

### Words you need
- **Core**: a fully cured glass-epoxy sheet with copper already bonded to both faces.
- **Prepreg**: glass cloth with partly cured epoxy. It melts and glues cores and foils together.
- **Stackup**: the ordered list of every layer and its thickness ([[q0466]]).
- **Reference plane**: a solid copper layer, usually ground, that a fast trace runs next to ([[q0022]]).
- **Impedance**: how a trace resists a fast signal. It depends on trace width and dielectric thickness.

### The simplest example
A common 4-layer board is, top to bottom: top copper, thin prepreg, inner copper, thick core, inner copper, thin prepreg, bottom copper. For a 50 Ω trace on the top layer with a 0.2 mm prepreg to the plane below, the page finds a width of about 0.335 mm. Put the same trace 1.6 mm above its plane (a 2-layer board) and you need about 3 mm of width for 50 Ω. Thinner dielectric means a narrower trace for the same impedance. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers each layer's material, the electrical effects of layer thickness, and why stackups are kept symmetric.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can describe a board as a **built-up sandwich** rather than "a green board with traces". The interviewer wants the full vertical order: solder mask, copper, prepreg, core, and how each layer is made. They are also checking that you know *why* the layers alternate the way they do. Copper carries signals and current, glass-epoxy insulates and holds everything flat, mask protects, silkscreen labels. Strong candidates also separate **core** (cured, copper-clad) from **prepreg** (uncured glue layer) and mention that the thickness of those dielectrics is what sets trace impedance later.
:::

::: why
Every layout decision after this (impedance control, return paths, crosstalk, decoupling effectiveness, cost) is made on top of the stackup. If you can't name what is between two copper layers and roughly how thick it is, you can't reason about a 50 Ω trace or the capacitance of a power plane. Interviewers also use this as a warm-up: it is a fast way to tell who has actually ordered a board from a fab and read the fabrication notes. This page is the *stackup* framing of the question. Its sibling [[q0466]] sits under "Layers and Materials", and the pages on [[q0475]] and [[q0476]] build on it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Copper layers**: etched foil, usually 0.5, 1 or 2 oz/ft² (about 17.5, 35 or 70 µm). Outer layers carry parts and signals; inner layers are signals or planes. See [[q0467]].
- **Core**: a rigid, fully cured glass-epoxy laminate that arrives from the laminate maker already bonded to copper foil on both sides.
- **Prepreg**: "pre-impregnated" glass cloth with partly cured (B-stage) epoxy. It melts, flows and cures during lamination, gluing cores and foils together. See [[q0469]] and [[q0470]].
- **Solder mask and silkscreen**: the outer polymer coatings that keep solder where you want it and print the legend. See [[q0472]] and [[q0473]].
- **Surface finish and plated holes**: HASL, ENIG or OSP on exposed copper, plus copper plating inside drilled holes that joins layers.
- **Dielectric thickness and $\varepsilon_r$**: they set capacitance between layers and trace impedance. See [[q0022]].
:::

::: answer
"I think of a PCB as a layered sandwich. Starting at the top there's a thin silkscreen ink layer for the labels, then solder mask, which is a green epoxy polymer that covers the copper and has openings only at the pads. Under that is the top copper, which is etched foil, usually one ounce, so about 35 microns thick.

Then comes the dielectric. That's FR-4, which is woven fiberglass cloth soaked in epoxy. There are two forms of it: **core**, which is a fully cured sheet with copper already bonded on both sides, and **prepreg**, which is the same glass and resin but only partly cured, so it works as the glue. In a four-layer board you get top copper, a thin prepreg, an inner copper layer, a thick core, another inner copper layer, a thin prepreg and bottom copper, then mask and silkscreen on the bottom as well.

Drilled holes get plated with copper to connect layers, and exposed pads get a surface finish like ENIG or HASL so they stay solderable. The dielectric thickness matters because it decides trace impedance and how tightly the planes couple. That's also why the usual four-layer stackup is signal, ground, power, signal: each outer signal layer sits one thin prepreg away from a solid plane, and the stack is symmetric so it doesn't warp."
:::

::: technical
**Standard 4-layer, 1.6 mm board (typical, fab-dependent).**

| Layer | Material | Typical thickness |
|---|---|---|
| Silkscreen | epoxy ink | ~15–25 µm, not electrically relevant |
| Solder mask | LPI epoxy | ~20–30 µm over copper |
| L1 top copper | foil + plating | ~35 µm (1 oz), a bit thicker after plating |
| Prepreg | glass cloth + epoxy | ~0.1–0.2 mm |
| L2 inner copper | foil | 17.5–35 µm |
| Core | cured FR-4 | ~0.8–1.2 mm |
| L3 inner copper | foil | 17.5–35 µm |
| Prepreg | glass + epoxy | ~0.1–0.2 mm |
| L4 bottom copper | foil + plating | ~35 µm |

**Materials.** Copper is electrodeposited or rolled foil. FR-4 is E-glass cloth with brominated epoxy resin (the "FR" is flame retardant). Relative permittivity is roughly 4.2–4.6 at low frequency and varies with resin content, glass style and frequency, with loss tangent around 0.015–0.025. Standard Tg is about 130–140 °C; high-Tg grades go to ~170 °C. Solder mask is a liquid photoimageable (LPI) epoxy. Silkscreen is epoxy ink (or inkjet legend).

**Electrical consequences.** Two planes separated by dielectric height $h$ form a capacitor $C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/h$. One square inch with $h = 0.2$ mm and $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$ is about 0.12 nF. A microstrip on a 0.2 mm prepreg over a ground plane needs roughly 0.33 mm of width for 50 Ω, but on the full 1.6 mm substrate of a 2-layer board it would need about 3 mm.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Thicknesses, $\varepsilon_r$ and finished copper weights vary by fab, laminate vendor and glass style. Always take the numbers from your fabricator's stackup sheet before doing impedance calculations.
:::

::: short
"A PCB is a stack of copper layers separated by FR-4 glass-epoxy. From the top: silkscreen, solder mask, top copper, a prepreg glue layer, inner copper, a thick cured core, more inner copper, prepreg, bottom copper, mask and silkscreen. Copper carries signals and current, FR-4 insulates and gives stiffness, mask protects against solder bridges, silkscreen is labeling. Plated holes link the layers. The dielectric thicknesses matter most electrically, since they set impedance and plane coupling."
:::

::: long
"If I draw a four-layer board in cross section, I start at the top with silkscreen, which is just ink for reference designators. Under that is solder mask, an epoxy coating that covers copper everywhere except the pads, so solder doesn't bridge and the copper is protected from oxidation.

Then top copper, usually one ounce foil, about 35 microns, etched into traces and pads. Below it is a thin prepreg layer. Prepreg is fiberglass cloth that's been soaked in epoxy but only partly cured, so in the lamination press it flows, fills gaps and hardens, gluing the stack together. Next is the first inner copper layer, and under that a thick core, which is fully cured FR-4 that comes from the laminate supplier already clad in copper on both sides. Then the second inner layer, another prepreg, the bottom copper, and mask and silkscreen again.

Holes are drilled and plated with copper, which makes vias and through-hole pads. The pads left uncovered by mask get a surface finish, like HASL, ENIG or OSP, so they remain solderable.

The reason this matters electrically is that the dielectric thickness and permittivity set the characteristic impedance of traces and the capacitance between planes. A thin prepreg between the top layer and a ground plane gives you narrow 50 ohm traces and tight coupling, which is exactly what you want for high speed designs. And for any real design I'd ask the fab for their stackup table, because thicknesses and dielectric constants vary by vendor."
:::

::: terms
- **Core**: cured laminate (glass-epoxy) with copper bonded to both faces; the rigid building block of a multilayer.
- **Prepreg (PP)**: uncured/B-stage glass-epoxy sheet that bonds cores and foils during lamination.
- **Copper weight (oz)**: foil thickness expressed as mass per ft²; 1 oz ≈ 35 µm.
- **FR-4**: flame-retardant woven-glass epoxy laminate, the default PCB dielectric.
- **Solder mask (LPI)**: polymer coating over copper with openings at pads.
- **Silkscreen / legend**: printed text and outlines for assembly and debug.
- **Surface finish**: HASL, ENIG, OSP, immersion silver or tin protecting exposed copper.
- **PTH (plated through-hole)**: drilled hole plated with copper, connecting layers.
- **Stackup**: the ordered list of copper and dielectric layers with thickness and material.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer lists "copper, FR-4, solder mask, silkscreen" and stops. An exceptional answer:

- Distinguishes **core from prepreg** and says why both exist (rigid cured building block vs. adhesive that flows during lamination).
- Gives real numbers: 1 oz ≈ 35 µm, 1.6 mm total thickness, prepreg 0.1–0.2 mm.
- Notes the **symmetry** requirement. An asymmetric stack warps because the copper and resin contract differently on each side.
- Connects the physical layers to electrical outcomes: the thickness between a signal layer and its reference plane sets $Z_0$, and planes close together make interplane capacitance.
- Mentions that numbers come from the fab's stackup sheet and that $\varepsilon_r$ is only approximately 4.2–4.6.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Calling every insulating layer "the substrate"** and not separating core from prepreg. They behave differently in manufacturing, and the stackup sheet specifies both.
- **Saying solder mask is an insulator between copper layers.** It only covers the outside. The inner insulation is FR-4.
- **Thinking silkscreen has electrical function.** It doesn't, but silkscreen ink over pads can ruin solderability, so keep it off them.
- **Assuming all boards are 1.6 mm with 1 oz copper.** Those are just common defaults; 0.8 mm, 1.0 mm, 2.4 mm boards and 2 oz copper are routine.
- **Forgetting symmetry.** An unbalanced stackup can bow or twist during reflow.
- **Quoting FR-4 $\varepsilon_r$ as exactly 4.7 or 4.0.** It depends on resin content, glass style and frequency. Quote a range.
:::

::: deep
## Why a board is a sandwich

A single copper-clad sheet gives you a 1- or 2-layer board. Once routing gets dense, you can't fit all the connections, and the return current paths get long. The fix is to stack more copper layers. But you can't just glue etched sheets together with arbitrary glue: you need an insulator that is stable through soldering at over 240 °C, flame retardant, flat, and has a known thickness and permittivity. That material is glass-reinforced epoxy.

## The layers one by one

**Copper.** Foil is specified by weight: 1 oz of copper spread over 1 ft² is about 35 µm thick. Outer layers start as ½ oz foil and are plated up, so the finished outer thickness is more than the foil number suggests. Inner layers stay at their foil thickness, usually ½ or 1 oz. The resistance of a square of 1 oz copper is

$$R_\square = \frac{\rho}{t} = \frac{1.68\times10^{-8}}{35\times10^{-6}} = 0.48\ \text{m}\Omega/\square$$

so a 1 mm wide, 100 mm long trace (100 squares) is 48 mΩ. That is why power routes and planes go on heavy copper or wide pours.

**Core.** The laminate maker presses glass cloth and resin between copper foils, cures it completely, and sells it in standard thicknesses. A core has rigid, predictable thickness, so the thick middle of a multilayer board is nearly always a core.

**Prepreg.** Glass cloth is dipped in resin and partially cured until it is dry to the touch but still meltable. During lamination (heat, vacuum, pressure) the resin flows into the etched copper gaps, cross-links, and bonds the stack. Because it flows, a prepreg's *finished* thickness after pressing is a bit less than its nominal thickness, more so over areas with little copper. Fabs publish a recommended build for each requested thickness. The cloth style (for example 1080 or 7628) sets how thick and how uniform the layer is.

**Solder mask.** A photoimageable epoxy that is coated, exposed through your mask layer and developed so that only pad openings remain. It prevents solder bridges, protects copper, and offers some insulation. It is not controlled-impedance material, but it slightly lowers the impedance of outer layer traces because it adds a thin high-$\varepsilon_r$ layer on top of the trace.

**Silkscreen.** Ink (or inkjet legend) for designators, polarity marks and logos. Keep it off pads and vias that need solder.

## From layers to numbers: two electrical consequences

**1. Impedance of an outer trace.** For a microstrip of width $w$, height $h$ above its plane, copper thickness $t$ and permittivity $\varepsilon_r$, the IPC-2141 approximation is

$$Z_0 \approx \frac{87}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r+1.41}}\ln\frac{5.98h}{0.8w+t}$$

(valid for roughly $0.1 < w/h < 2$). With $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$, $t=0.035$ mm and $h = 0.2$ mm, a width of 0.335 mm gives 50 Ω. If the same trace sat over a plane 1.6 mm away (a 2-layer board with ground on the bottom), you'd need about 3 mm of width. That's the whole reason high-speed boards use *thin* prepreg to the nearest plane.

**2. Plane capacitance.** Adjacent planes act as a parallel plate capacitor. For 1 in² (645 mm²):

| Dielectric thickness $h$ | $C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/h$ |
|---|---|
| 1.0 mm | 0.025 nF |
| 0.2 mm | 0.12 nF |
| 0.1 mm | 0.25 nF |

A power-ground plane pair 0.1 mm apart gives 0.25 nF/in², effective as very high-frequency decoupling and with extremely low inductance. See [[q0480]].

## Why symmetry and balance matter

Copper expands at about 17 ppm/°C, FR-4 in-plane at roughly 14–17 ppm/°C but with much larger out-of-plane expansion. If the top has dense copper and the bottom is mostly bare, the resin shrinks differently and the board bows. Fabs therefore ask that dielectric thicknesses be symmetric about the centre and that copper distribution be roughly balanced. This is also a good reason to fill empty areas with hatched or solid ground pours.

## Other constructions you may hear about

- **Rigid-flex and flex** use polyimide instead of FR-4. See [[q0465]].
- **High-frequency laminates** (e.g., PTFE-based, or low-loss glass-epoxy variants) reduce dielectric loss above a few GHz. See [[q0469]].
- **HDI** boards add microvias and sequential lamination, so the stackup describes build-up layers (for example 1+N+1).
- **Metal-core (aluminum) boards** put a thermally conductive dielectric over a metal base for LEDs and power.

> [!TIP]
> If asked to "draw the layers", draw the cross-section with real order and symmetry, label each layer's function, and say out loud which two dielectric thicknesses you would ask the fab about: the one between the top layer and its plane (impedance) and the one between the power and ground planes (decoupling).
:::

::: applications
- **Hobby/Arduino-style 2-layer boards**: two copper layers on a single 1.6 mm core with mask and silkscreen on both sides; cheap, fine for slow logic.
- **Microcontroller boards with USB and a 3.3 V rail**: 4-layer (signal / ground / power / signal) so USB differential pairs can be impedance controlled over a nearby ground plane.
- **DDR memory and application-processor boards**: 6–10 layers with several ground planes sandwiching stripline signal layers.
- **High-power LED or motor boards**: 2 oz copper outer layers, or an aluminum metal-core board for heat spreading.
- **RF front ends**: a hybrid stack with a thin low-loss laminate on top and standard FR-4 beneath for mechanical layers.
:::

::: assumptions
- The numbers are for a **rigid, glass-epoxy, 4-layer, 1.6 mm** board built by a typical commercial fab. Other constructions (HDI, flex, metal-core) are differently stacked.
- "Layers of a PCB" can mean either the **manufacturing materials** (mask, copper, core, prepreg, silkscreen) or the **CAD layers** (top copper, bottom paste, drill drawing, board outline). I answer the former, and mention the CAD layers in the interpretations section.
- Copper weight is always quoted as the starting foil; finished thickness includes plating.
- $\varepsilon_r$ of 4.3 is a round figure. Real laminates have 4.0–4.8 depending on resin content, glass weave and frequency, so real impedance calculators need the fab's data.
- If the interviewer says "inside the chip", they mean IC metal layers instead. See [[q0462]].
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between core and prepreg, and why do you need both?
Core is fully cured laminate with copper on both sides: rigid, flat, predictable thickness. Prepreg is the same glass-epoxy but partly cured, with no copper, so it melts and flows during lamination and acts as glue between cores or between a core and outer foil. You need cores for stiffness and well-defined thickness, and prepreg to bond them while filling the etched gaps between traces.

### Why is a four-layer board almost always symmetric about the middle?
Different materials expand and shrink by different amounts when the board is laminated and soldered. If the dielectric and copper content differ top to bottom, the board bows or twists (warp), which hurts BGA soldering. A symmetric stack (for example 0.2 / 1.0 / 0.2 mm dielectrics) with balanced copper keeps stresses equal on both halves.

### How thick is 1 oz copper and what is its resistance per square?
About 35 µm (1.37 mil). With $\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}$ Ω·m the sheet resistance is $\rho/t = 0.48$ mΩ/□, so a 1 mm wide, 50 mm long trace (50 squares) is 24 mΩ. Heavier copper (2 oz) halves that.

### What if the fab says the prepreg you specified isn't available?
They will propose an equivalent build (different glass style or two thinner plies) that hits the same finished thickness. I'd re-run the impedance calculation with their numbers, check the resulting trace width, and confirm in the fab notes that the controlled-impedance traces are listed with the tolerance (often ±10%) and which layers they sit on. A thicker prepreg to the reference plane needs wider traces, so I would update the design rather than assume it still works.

### Scenario: your 50 Ω USB traces measure 62 Ω on the first board. What went wrong in the stackup?
Likely the dielectric between the signal layer and its reference plane is thicker than assumed, or the effective $\varepsilon_r$ is lower, or the etch left the trace narrower than drawn. From the IPC formula, going from $h = 0.2$ mm to 0.25 mm at 0.335 mm width raises $Z_0$ to about 58 Ω, so ±25% thickness variance matters. I'd compare the fab's final stackup report with my calculation, check the measured trace width, and request a coupon TDR measurement if controlled impedance was specified.
:::

"Layers of a PCB" appears twice in the question list ([[q0466]] and this one). I treat this version as the **stackup** view: the ordered vertical build and what each layer does electrically. If the interviewer is a layout person, mention both: manufacturing layers are the physical stack, CAD layers are the data that describe them.
