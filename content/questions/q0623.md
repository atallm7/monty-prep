::: basics
### In plain words
On the fastest and most sensitive signals, a test point is not free. The pad adds a little capacitance (stored charge that blunts sharp changes) and a stub, and these disturb the signal you want to measure. So for these signals you use a special kind of point, such as an in-line pad or a coaxial connector, or you use a built-in self-test instead of a pad.

### Picture it
Picture a fast channel of water with a small side basin cut into its bank. A smooth, slow flow barely notices the basin. A sharp, rushing wave slaps into it, and part of the wave bounces back up the channel. A stub on a fast net is that basin. The faster the edge, the more the pad matters. One caveat: a long stub also rings like a struck bell at certain frequencies (it resonates), which adds extra harm.

### Words you need
- **Edge**: the fast rise or fall of a signal; its rise time is how quickly it changes.
- **Stub**: a short dead-end spur of copper joining the pad to the signal.
- **Reflection**: part of the edge bouncing back from a bump in the line.
- **In-line pad**: a pad that the trace itself runs through, so there is no stub.
- **Built-in self-test (BIST)**: the chip checking its own link, so no probe is needed.

### The simplest example
A pad with its stub adds about 0.5 pF to a typical 50 Ω signal line. With a 100 ps edge, about 12.5 % of the step reflects. At 1 pF it is about 24.5 %. A 10 mm open stub also resonates near 3.75 GHz, and a 100 ps edge carries frequencies up to about 3.5 GHz. So the pad would visibly distort the signal. The fix is an in-line pad, a tiny stub, or measuring at the receiver. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the reflection table, an isolation resistor at the tap, and sensitive analog nodes.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can reason about a test point as a **circuit element**: shunt capacitance plus a stub on a transmission line (or a leakage/loading path on a sensitive node), and then offer concrete mitigations. The interviewer wants a nuanced answer, not "never" or "always":

1. Why a pad hurts: capacitance, stub reflections, resonance, coupling, loading, leakage.
2. How much it hurts: rise time versus stub length and capacitance, with numbers.
3. What to do instead: in-line pads, short stubs, isolation resistors, coax, buffered points, built-in self-test, measurement at receivers.
4. Which sensitive signals matter: clocks, crystals, RF, reference voltages and high-impedance analog nodes.
:::

::: why
High-speed links and precision analog circuits are where a test point can *cause* the failure you wanted to debug. A pad that is invisible on a 3.3 V rail can close a 10 Gb/s eye or shift a reference by millivolts. Yet these are the signals engineers most want to observe. The answer determines whether a design passes its first bring-up. Interviewers use it to see if you understand transmission-line effects and probing physics beyond "keep traces short".
:::

::: concepts
- **Transmission line and reflections**: a discontinuity reflects part of an edge. See the reflection numbers below and the `tline-lab` sim in [[q0620]].
- **Rise time versus length**: a stub matters when its delay is a significant fraction of the edge. Knee frequency $f \approx 0.35/t_r$.
- **Shunt capacitance**: pad plus stub capacitance on a line, $\tau = Z_0C/2$.
- **Stub resonance**: quarter-wave resonance of an open stub: $f = v/(4\ell)$.
- **Probe loading**: the measurement itself adds capacitance. See [[q0627]].
- **Leakage and guarding** on high-impedance nodes.
- **Alternatives**: coax, BIST, buffered outputs. See [[q0622]].
:::

::: answer
"Generally I avoid them on the fastest and most sensitive nets, or I design special ones, because a test point isn't free electrically.

On a fast net, the pad is a bit of capacitance, around half a picofarad with its stub, hanging off a 50 ohm line. With a 100 picosecond edge that reflects around 12 percent of the step, and the stub can resonate, which closes the eye on a multi-gigabit link. On a sensitive analog or reference net, the pad and the probe load it, add leakage and pick up noise, so the thing you measure isn't what the circuit does.

So for high-speed nets I'd use an in-line pad where the trace itself passes over the pad so there's no stub, or a very short stub with an isolation resistor right at the tap, or an RF connector or footprint I can populate for validation, and rely on built-in eye scan or margining and measure at the receiver or connector. For sensitive analog nets I'd add a buffered test output, a guarded point, or skip it and read the value through the ADC or a status register.

If I must have a pad in bring-up, I'd place it, measure, and remove it in production if it affects margin. And I'd put it at the point that gives the most information, usually the receiver end."
:::

::: technical
**Electrical model of a test point.** A pad of capacitance $C_p$ on a stub of length $\ell$ attached to a line of impedance $Z_0$.

1. **Capacitive reflection** (short stub): shunt $C$ across the line sees $Z_0/2$, so $\tau = Z_0C/2$. For a ramp of rise time $t_r$: $\rho_{pk} = \frac{\tau}{t_r}(1 - e^{-t_r/\tau})$.

| C (pF) | $t_r$ = 1 ns | 300 ps | 100 ps | 50 ps |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.3 | 0.8% | 2.5% | 7.5% | 15% |
| 0.5 | 1.3% | 4.2% | 12.5% | 24.5% |
| 1.0 | 2.5% | 8.3% | 24.5% | 43% |
| 2.0 | 5.0% | 16.6% | 43% | 63% |

2. **Stub resonance.** An open stub of length $\ell$ at propagation velocity $v \approx 1.5\times10^8$ m/s (outer-layer FR-4, order of magnitude) has quarter-wave resonance $f_{qw} = v/4\ell$: 5 mm gives 7.5 GHz, 10 mm gives 3.75 GHz, 15 mm gives 2.5 GHz. A 100 ps edge has significant content up to $0.35/t_r = 3.5$ GHz, so a 10 mm stub is already a problem. Rule of thumb: keep stubs well under a tenth of the edge's spatial length (about $t_r\,v/10$: 1.5 mm for 100 ps).

3. **Isolation resistor at the tap.** Put a series resistor $R_{iso}$ (a few hundred ohms) *right at the junction*, with the pad at the far end. The line now sees a shunt resistor initially: $\rho \approx -\frac{Z_0}{2R_{iso}+Z_0}$. With 500 Ω: $-50/1050 = -4.8\%$; with 1 kΩ: 2.4%; with 250 Ω: 9.1%. The pad capacitance is hidden behind $R_{iso}$. Use for medium-speed nets where a probe (10 MΩ || ~10 pF) is applied to the pad: the resistor and probe C form a low-pass (e.g. 500 Ω × 10 pF = 5 ns) that limits the observed bandwidth, so this is a DC/slow-signal tool only.

4. **In-line (feed-through) pad.** The trace itself widens to a pad and continues: no stub. The pad adds capacitance at the same point (impedance dip) but no stub, and you can fine-tune with a plane cutout beneath to restore impedance.

5. **Probing the receiver.** Measure at the receiver end where the signal is terminated; the added loading of a probe is small relative to the termination; or use a differential active probe at a designed probe via.

6. **Sensitive analog nodes.** Concerns: input bias/leakage (nA to pA across megaohm nodes), pickup, contamination, and probe loading. Use guard rings, buffered monitors and sampling through the system itself.

**Built-in alternatives.** SerDes eye scan and BER counters, DDR training margin reports, PLL lock indicators, ADC readback of reference voltages.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Propagation velocity depends on stack-up (microstrip versus stripline); 1.5–1.8e8 m/s is a typical range. The quoted pad capacitances are estimates. For critical links use a field solver or the vendor's probing guidelines.
:::

::: short
"I avoid or special-case them on fast and sensitive nets. A pad plus stub adds roughly half a picofarad that reflects about 12% on a 50 ohm line at 100 ps edges, and the stub can resonate and close an eye. On sensitive analog nodes, pads and probes add loading, leakage and pickup. So I use in-line pads, tiny stubs with an isolation resistor, coax footprints, buffered monitors or built-in eye scan and measure at the receiver."
:::

::: long
"I treat a test point on a fast or sensitive net as a circuit element I have to budget for. Short answer: usually don't put a plain pad there; use something designed.

On a fast net, the pad and its stub act like shunt capacitance on a transmission line. A half-picofarad pad on a 50 ohm line looks like a 12 picosecond time constant, and with a 100 picosecond edge it reflects roughly 12 percent of the step. Make the stub long and it resonates at a quarter wavelength, like 7.5 gigahertz for a 5 millimetre stub, which is right inside a multi-gigabit signal's spectrum. That closes the eye. And then your measurement isn't of the real circuit.

So I'd do one of a few things. Make the pad in-line, where the trace itself runs over the pad so there's no stub. Or put a few-hundred-ohm isolation resistor right at the tap, which cuts the reflection to under 5 percent but limits bandwidth, so it's for slow views. Or add a footprint for a coax connector I populate only for validation. Or, best, use the chip's own eye-scan and margining and measure at the receiver instead of probing.

On sensitive analog nodes, like references, crystals or high-impedance inputs, the pad and the probe load the node, add leakage and pick up noise. I'd use a guarded or buffered test point, or read the value through the ADC and not probe it at all.

And if I add a pad for bring-up, I'd plan to remove it in production, after confirming margins."
:::

::: terms
- **Stub**: short branch from a line to a pad or component; can reflect and resonate.
- **Quarter-wave resonance**: an open stub resonates when its length equals a quarter wavelength.
- **In-line (feed-through) pad**: pad that the trace passes over, with no stub.
- **Isolation resistor**: series resistor at the tap that hides stub capacitance from the line.
- **Eye scan**: SerDes feature that measures the received eye internally.
- **Knee frequency**: $f \approx 0.35/t_r$, the bandwidth needed to pass an edge.
- **Guard ring**: conductor at the same potential around a sensitive node to intercept leakage.
- **Active probe**: low-capacitance (sub-pF to ~1 pF) high-bandwidth probe.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "no, because it adds capacitance".

**An exceptional answer**:

- Quantifies: τ = Z0C/2, peak reflection versus rise time (about 12.5% for 0.5 pF at 100 ps), and stub resonance (about 7.5 GHz for 5 mm).
- Presents a toolbox: in-line pad, isolation resistor with its bandwidth trade-off, coax footprint, receiver-end measurement, built-in eye scan, buffered monitors, depopulate in production.
- Distinguishes fast digital nets from sensitive analog nets (loading, leakage, pickup) and oscillators.
- Notes the measurement side: the probe adds capacitance too; use active probes.
- Says "it depends": use the pad if its effect is within the budget.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Never put test points on high-speed signals."** Sometimes you must; the answer is *how*.
- **"A pad is just copper so it's invisible."** At 100 ps it's a 12% reflection.
- **Long stubs "just for convenience."** A 10 mm stub resonates near 3.75 GHz.
- **Isolation resistor placed at the pad, not at the tap.** Then the stub is still hanging on the net.
- **Probing the crystal.** Probe capacitance detunes it. See [[q0622]].
- **Ignoring the probe's own load.** Even an in-line pad plus a 10 pF passive probe adds more than the pad.
- **Assuming a good DC reading means a good signal.** DC tells you nothing about edges.
:::

::: deep
## Why speed changes the answer

On a slow net, a trace is just a wire: current charges the node and every piece of the net is at the same voltage. A pad adds a bit of capacitance that the driver charges, which is negligible next to the receiver's pin capacitance. On a fast net, the trace is a transmission line: edges travel as waves, and any change in the line's impedance reflects part of the wave. A test pad is a change in impedance.

A useful criterion: a net behaves as a transmission line when its one-way delay exceeds about a sixth of the rise time, i.e. when its length exceeds roughly $t_r v/6$. For 100 ps edges and $v=1.5\times10^8$ m/s that's 2.5 mm, so in practice every fast net is one.

## A budget for a pad

1. Pick a reflection budget, say ≤ 5% for the pad's discontinuity on a link whose total allowed reflection is bigger but shared with vias, connectors and packages.
2. From $\rho_{pk} \approx \frac{\tau}{t_r}$ for $t_r \gg \tau$: $C \lesssim \frac{2\rho t_r}{Z_0}$. For $t_r = 100$ ps: $C \lesssim 2(0.05)(100\text{ ps})/50 = 0.2$ pF. For a 300 ps edge: 0.6 pF. That's why a pad may be fine on a 300 ps DDR3-class signal and not on a 30 ps SerDes edge (budget about 0.06 pF, i.e. effectively "no pad").

(Approximation: $\rho_{pk} \approx \tau/t_r$ for $t_r \gg \tau$.)

## Techniques in order of preference

1. **Don't probe: use the chip's built-ins.** Eye scan, margining, BER counters, PRBS built-in tests. Zero hardware effect.
2. **Measure at the receiver.** The signal at the receiver is what matters; a probe via near the receiver's decoupling capacitor can be less disruptive.
3. **In-line pad.** Trace widens to a pad and continues: no stub. Remove plane copper under the pad to restore impedance (a plane cutout reduces C).
4. **Short stub (< 1.5 mm) with isolation resistor.** Resistor at the junction, pad beyond. Use for debug where bandwidth demands are modest.
5. **Footprint for a coax connector or micro-probe.** Populate only for validation; with the connector absent, the stub is tiny. A zero-ohm series-position option (a "chain") lets you break the net and insert a cable.
6. **Active probes at a designed pad.** Probes with 0.1–1 pF input capacitance lessen the loading but still need ground connection control. See [[q0618]].

## Sensitive analog nodes

- **Leakage**: a contaminated pad with 10 GΩ leakage to a rail adds 0.3 nA from 3 V; on a 10 MΩ node that's 3 mV error. Use guard rings and clean assemblies.
- **Probe loading**: a DMM's 10 MΩ halves the voltage of a 10 MΩ source; the loaded node's reading is wrong. See the sim.

- **Pickup**: high-impedance nodes couple noise via the pad's area and the probe lead.
- **Crystals and oscillators**: load capacitance sets frequency; a probe's capacitance shifts or stops it.
- **References**: probing a bandgap or ADC reference node loads it and injects noise; add a buffered monitor or low-pass-filtered point, or measure the converter's code.

## Stack-up and layout touches

- Place the pad on the layer with the shortest stub to the trace; keep the plane beneath continuous, or use a small cutout to offset capacitance.
- Keep ground pads close (a few mm); long probe grounds corrupt fast measurements anyway. See [[q0629]].
- For differential pairs, add pads symmetrically to both legs; an unbalanced pad converts differential to common mode.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Show you can do the quick estimate out loud: "half a picofarad on a 50 ohm line is a 12 ps time constant; with a 100 ps edge that's about 12% reflection." Numbers make the answer credible.

## Worked example: a probe point on a 10 Gb/s differential pair

Pick a concrete case and carry it through. A 10 Gb/s lane has a unit interval of 100 ps, an edge of roughly 30 to 40 ps, and a fundamental (Nyquist) frequency of 5 GHz. You want a probe point on one leg of the pair. Use 0.5 pF for the pad, 50 Ω per leg (a simplification; the pair is differentially about 100 Ω).

1. **Reflection.** $\tau = Z_0C/2 = 50\times0.5\text{ pF}/2 = 12.5$ ps. With a 35 ps edge, $\rho_{pk} = (12.5/35)(1-e^{-35/12.5}) = 0.357\times0.939 = 33.5\%$. That is far past any sensible budget, and even the 5% budget formula from above gives $C \lesssim 2(0.05)(35\text{ ps})/50 = 0.07$ pF. A normal pad is excluded.
2. **Skew from loading one leg only.** A shunt capacitance on a line adds delay of about $Z_0C/2 = 12.5$ ps (to first order, and valid for small $C$). On one leg of a pair that is a 12.5 ps skew, 12.5% of the unit interval. At 5 GHz a 12.5 ps skew is a phase error of $360 \times 5\text{ GHz}\times12.5\text{ ps} = 22.5°$, and the differential-to-common-mode conversion it creates (mixed-mode $|S_{cd21}| = \sin(\phi/2)$ for a pure skew $\phi$) is $\sin(11.25°) = 0.195$, which is $-14$ dB. A link that needs mode conversion well below $-20$ dB is hurt. The consequence for the layout is the symmetry rule in the stack-up section: if you must have a pad on one leg, put an identical (dummy) pad on the other leg at the same distance, which keeps the pair symmetric at the price of reflections on both legs.
3. **Do the isolated version.** With $R_{iso} = 500\ \Omega$ at the tap and a probe attached (10 MΩ ‖ about 10 pF, plus 1 pF of pad), the probe sees $R_{iso} + Z_0/2 = 525\ \Omega$ and about 11 pF, so the pole is $1/(2\pi \cdot 525\ \Omega \cdot 11\text{ pF}) = 27.6$ MHz and the 10–90% rise time you can observe is about $2.2RC = 12.7$ ns. The measurement is useless for a 100 ps edge. It shows the DC level and slow behavior (a link-up pull-up, a bias) but not the signal.

So for this lane the answer is the first item in the preference list: use the receiver's eye scan or an in-line footprint for a coax connector that is not populated in production. This is also the honest answer to give in an interview, because you can justify it with three numbers (33.5% reflection, 12.5 ps skew, a 27 MHz observation bandwidth) in about 20 seconds.

## What the lumped model misses

**The pad is not just a capacitor.** A real test pad has its own inductance to the via, a plane-cutout, and a solder-mask opening; for a footprint that will carry a coax connector or probe tip the transition is several millimeters of geometry that a field solver treats as a small 3D structure. The lumped $C$ is accurate when the pad is far smaller than the edge's spatial length. At 35 ps with $v = 1.5\times10^8$ m/s the spatial length of an edge is about 5 mm, so a 1 mm pad is within a factor of five of "not lumped".

**Reflections combine.** A pad 10 mm from a via and 5 mm from a connector gives round-trip times of 130 ps and 67 ps (at $v = 1.5\times10^8$ m/s), both comparable to a 100 ps edge, so the reflections can add or cancel depending on the spacing. This is why published budgets give a total for the whole link and why a single pad that looks fine in isolation can push a link over the edge.

**Loss and equalization hide the problem.** On a link with a receiver equalizer, a small reflection may be absorbed without any visible eye change on the bench, while it uses up margin that appears later at temperature or with a worse cable. A pad's cost is margin, not an immediate failure.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The delay added by a shunt capacitor ($Z_0C/2$) is a first-order approximation for small $C$ relative to the edge, and the mode-conversion figure assumes a purely skew-driven conversion. For a real link, use a 3D field solver or the transceiver vendor's probing guidance.

## Verifying on the bench

1. **TDR before and after.** Use a TDR, or a VNA with time-domain transform, on a test coupon or on the first board. Measure the line with the pad populated and with the pad removed or cut. The impedance dip or the reflection amplitude is the pad's cost, directly comparable with the $\tau/t_r$ number computed above.
2. **Eye or BER margin.** Run the link with the pad in the circuit and compare its eye opening or bit-error counters, over temperature if you can, against the same board with the pad cut. If there is no measurable change at the worst corner, the pad is within budget; if there is, it is not, however small the calculated reflection.
3. **Check symmetry.** For a pair, measure each leg's TDR separately. Unequal delays or impedance dips on the two legs mean the probe point has made the pair asymmetric.
4. **Prove the probe path.** If the plan is a removable coax footprint, verify that the footprint with the connector absent returns the same TDR as the plain trace. A stub left behind can ruin an otherwise good link.

::: applications
- **DDR memory boards**: no pads on data lines; margin and read-leveling reports from the controller, and special probing interposers in validation.
- **PCIe and Ethernet**: eye scan and loopback tests; measurements at the connector with test fixtures defined by the standard.
- **Clock distribution**: in-line or coax footprints for validation, depopulated later.
- **Precision ADC front ends**: buffered monitor outputs for reference and input nodes.
- **RF front-ends**: switched coax connectors for conducted tests, with the connector as part of the matching network.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes a roughly 50 Ω controlled-impedance net and a lumped pad; for high-density packages the via, pad and plane cut-out geometry all matter.
- Velocity 1.5×10⁸ m/s and pad capacitance 0.3–1 pF are estimates for FR-4.
- Reflection estimates use a linear ramp and a single discontinuity; real nets have several discontinuities interacting.
- Differential signalling and AC-coupled links change the picture: AC coupling caps and connectors are natural measurement points, and the receiver's equalization makes the raw eye less informative.
- Valid alternative: for a few hundred megabit nets, plain pads are fine.
:::

::: followups
### How much capacitance can a test pad add to a 50 Ω line with a 200 ps edge before it exceeds 5% reflection?
Using ρ ≈ τ/tr = Z0 C/(2 tr): C = 2 ρ tr / Z0 = 2 × 0.05 × 200 ps / 50 = 0.4 pF. A 1 mm pad over a thin dielectric (0.15–0.3 pF) plus a short stub fits; a header pin (several pF) does not.

### What does an isolation resistor buy you, and what does it cost?
It hides the stub capacitance from the line: with 500 Ω the initial reflection is about −4.8% instead of −24.5% (1 pF, 100 ps). The cost is bandwidth for the measurement: 500 Ω with 10 pF of probe is a 5 ns time constant (about 32 MHz), so the pad shows only slow content: fine for DC levels and slow edges, not for eye diagrams.

### How would you measure a 5 Gb/s signal on a board without adding a pad?
Use the receiver's eye-scan or margin feature, loop-back tests, or measure at a connector with a fixture designed for the standard. If I need a scope, I'd plan a footprint for a micro-coax or an in-line probe via near the receiver, populated only for validation, with the plane cutout designed in.

### Why can an unbalanced test pad on one leg of a differential pair hurt?
It changes the impedance and delay of one leg: the pair becomes asymmetric, converting some differential signal to common mode (mode conversion), which raises EMI and reduces margin. Use symmetric pads on both legs, or none.

### Scenario: your reference voltage drifts when you probe it with a scope but is stable in the system. What happened?
The probe loaded the node or injected noise: a 10 MΩ scope input with 10 pF on a high-impedance reference changes DC by Rsource/(Rsource+10 MΩ) and adds high-frequency pickup. I'd measure it with a high-impedance buffer or read it back with the on-board ADC, or add a buffered monitor pad; also check for leakage on the pad.
:::
