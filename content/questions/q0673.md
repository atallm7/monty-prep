::: testing
Whether you can **sketch and explain** a line spectrum from first principles: spectral lines at integer multiples of $f_0 = 1/T$, a DC line equal to the average, a $\sin(x)/x$ envelope set by the pulse width, nulls where the pulse width fits an integer number of times in a harmonic's period, and the extra roll-off that a finite rise time adds. It is a drawing question, so labelled axes matter. The interviewer also checks that you can connect duty cycle to *which* harmonics exist (odd only at 50%) and that you know a train of pulses gives discrete lines while a single pulse gives a continuous spectrum.
:::

::: why
Spectra are how EMI problems, filter requirements and bandwidth needs get estimated in practice. If you can sketch the spectrum of a clock or PWM waveform, you can predict where a radiated-emissions peak will land, which harmonic a ferrite or filter must attack, and how much scope or channel bandwidth the signal needs. It also explains design tricks such as picking a duty cycle that nulls an offending harmonic and why a faster edge costs high-frequency energy ([[q0677]], [[q0678]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Fourier series**: a periodic signal has lines at $nf_0$; line heights are the Fourier coefficients ([[q0666]], [[q0667]]).
- **Duty cycle and pulse width $\tau$**: $D = \tau/T$. It sets the envelope shape and null positions ([[q0672]]).
- **Sinc envelope**: $|\sin(\pi f\tau)/(\pi f\tau)|$, from the Fourier transform of one rectangular pulse.
- **Odd harmonics only at 50%**: [[q0671]].
- **Rise time**: a second sinc factor $\sin(\pi f t_r)/(\pi f t_r)$ multiplies the envelope ([[q0676]]).
- **Log (dB) scale**: lets you see $-20$ dB/decade and $-40$ dB/decade slopes as straight lines.
:::

::: answer
"I'd draw frequency on the horizontal axis and amplitude on the vertical. For a periodic signal the spectrum is discrete lines, spaced at the repetition frequency f0, which is one over the period. There's a line at DC equal to the average, then lines at f0, 2f0, 3f0 and so on.

The line heights follow a sinc envelope that depends on the pulse width tau. The nth harmonic has amplitude 2V times the absolute value of sine of n pi D, over n pi, where D is the duty cycle. The envelope first goes to zero at 1 over tau, so the nulls fall at multiples of f0 over D.

For a 50% square wave that means the first null is at the second harmonic, so only odd lines survive, and they go as 1 over n: 1, one third, one fifth. For a 25% pulse train every fourth harmonic is missing. A narrower pulse spreads the energy out: the envelope is wider, so more harmonics have similar height.

If I'm being careful, I'd add that a real pulse has a finite rise time, which multiplies the envelope by a second sinc. Above a knee near 1 over pi times the rise time, the lines fall at 40 dB per decade instead of 20. A single non-repeating pulse has the same envelope, but as a continuous curve, not lines."
:::

::: technical
**Periodic pulse train**, amplitude $V$ (0 to $V$), pulse width $\tau$, period $T$, $D = \tau/T$, $f_0 = 1/T$. Complex Fourier coefficients: $c_n = DV\,\text{sinc}(nD)$ where $\text{sinc}(x) = \sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)$. Single-sided peak amplitudes:
$$A_0 = DV,\qquad A_n = 2DV\left|\frac{\sin(n\pi D)}{n\pi D}\right| = \frac{2V}{n\pi}\left|\sin(n\pi D)\right|$$

**Features of the line spectrum**
- Lines at $nf_0$, spacing $1/T$.
- Envelope $2DV\,|\text{sinc}(f\tau)|$, with nulls at $f = k/\tau$ ($k=1,2,\dots$). In harmonic numbers: nulls at $n = k/D$.
- Number of harmonics in the main lobe (before the first null): $\approx 1/D - 1$. For $D = 25\%$ lines 1, 2, 3 are in the lobe and the 4th is the null.
- **Square wave ($D=0.5$)**: nulls at $n = 2, 4, 6\dots$ so only odd harmonics; $A_n = 2V/(n\pi)$.
- **Asymptote**: for $f \gg 1/\tau$ the lobe peaks follow $2V/(n\pi)$, i.e. $-20$ dB/decade, independent of $D$. The flat region below $f = 1/(\pi\tau)$ has height $2DV$.

**Finite rise time** $t_r$ (0 to 100% linear ramp, equal rise and fall): multiply by a second sinc, $|\sin(\pi f t_r)/(\pi f t_r)|$. The envelope is then flat to $1/(\pi\tau)$, falls $-20$ dB/decade to $1/(\pi t_r)$, and falls $-40$ dB/decade after that.

**Single pulse**: the Fourier transform is continuous with the same sinc shape; scaling by $1/T$ gives the line heights of the periodic version.

**Ideal impulse train** (the limit $\tau \to 0$ at constant area): equal-height lines at every harmonic.

> [!NOTE]
> Peak amplitudes above are single-sided (cosine amplitude). RMS of a line is $A_n/\sqrt2$. Spectrum analyzers show RMS-referenced dBm or dB$\mu$V, so subtract 3 dB from peak-based dB figures.
:::

::: long
"For the drawing, I'd label the horizontal axis frequency and the vertical axis amplitude, ideally in dB so the slopes are visible.

A periodic signal gives a line spectrum. The spacing between lines is the repetition frequency f0. There's a DC line equal to the average, which is duty times amplitude. Then the lines at f0, 2f0, 3f0 follow an envelope.

The envelope is a sinc. It comes from the Fourier transform of one pulse of width tau. The nth harmonic amplitude is 2V sin of n pi D over n pi, where D is the duty cycle. The envelope is zero at 1 over tau, 2 over tau, and so on. In harmonic number, the nulls are at 1 over D, 2 over D and so on.

So for a 50% square wave, the nulls land on the even harmonics, which is why I only see odd lines falling as 1 over n: 1, one third, one fifth, so minus 9.5 dB at the third and minus 14 dB at the fifth. For 25% duty, every fourth harmonic is a null, and the fundamental is 3 dB below the 50% case, with a flatter spread of lines. For a very narrow pulse, the envelope is wide and flat, with many lines of nearly equal height.

If I'm being thorough I'd mention rise time. A real edge multiplies the envelope by a second sinc with its first null at 1 over the rise time. The result is that the envelope is flat up to 1 over pi tau, falls at 20 dB per decade up to about 1 over pi times rise time, and 40 dB per decade after that. That's why a slow edge is the cheapest EMI fix.

And for a non-repeating pulse, it's the same envelope but continuous, not lines."
:::

::: terms
- **Line (discrete) spectrum**: the spectrum of a periodic signal, nonzero only at $nf_0$.
- **Harmonic**: integer multiple $nf_0$ of the fundamental.
- **Sinc function**: $\text{sinc}(x) = \sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)$, the Fourier transform of a rectangular pulse.
- **Envelope**: the smooth curve through the tops of the lines, here $2DV|\text{sinc}(f\tau)|$.
- **Null (zero)**: frequency where the envelope crosses zero; harmonics landing there vanish.
- **Main lobe**: the region between DC and the first null at $1/\tau$.
- **Knee frequency**: where the slope steepens, about $1/(\pi\tau)$ for the pulse width and $1/(\pi t_r)$ for the edge.
- **dBV / dB$\mu$V**: logarithmic amplitude units; 20 dB per factor of 10 in voltage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws a few vertical lines at odd harmonics with decreasing height and says "it is a sinc".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws labelled axes, a DC line, and lines at $nf_0$, then the dashed sinc envelope through them.
- Writes the amplitude formula $\frac{2V}{n\pi}|\sin(n\pi D)|$ and uses it to place nulls at $n = k/D$.
- Shows two cases side by side (50%: odd only; 25% or 10%: every 4th or 10th missing, more lines in the main lobe).
- Explains the slopes: flat to $1/(\pi\tau)$, $-20$ dB/dec, then $-40$ dB/dec past $1/(\pi t_r)$, and links the knee to EMI and bandwidth.
- Distinguishes a periodic train (lines) from a single pulse (continuous) and an ideal impulse train (all lines equal).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing a continuous curve for a periodic signal.** A repeating wave has lines. A continuous spectrum belongs to a single non-repeating pulse.
- **Putting even harmonics on a 50% square wave.** The sinc has nulls exactly there.
- **Forgetting the DC line.** Its height is $DV$ and it can dominate a unipolar wave.
- **Wrong null spacing.** Nulls are at multiples of $1/\tau$ (pulse width), not $1/T$. Lines are spaced by $1/T$.
- **Mixing the lobe envelope with the line heights.** Lines sit *on* the envelope only at integer harmonics; the envelope itself continues between them.
- **Ignoring rise time.** The textbook sinc alone predicts $-20$ dB/decade forever; real edges fall at $-40$ dB/decade above the knee.
- **Mixing peak and RMS dB.** Instruments report RMS; there is a 3 dB offset.
:::

::: deep
## Where the lines come from

Take one period of the pulse train, centered on $t=0$: amplitude $V$ for $|t|<\tau/2$, zero elsewhere in $|t|<T/2$. The Fourier-series coefficient is
$$c_n = \frac{1}{T}\int_{-\tau/2}^{\tau/2} V e^{-j2\pi n f_0 t}\,dt = \frac{V}{T}\cdot\frac{\sin(\pi n f_0\tau)}{\pi n f_0} = DV\,\frac{\sin(n\pi D)}{n\pi D}$$
using $f_0\tau = D$. The one-sided amplitude is $2|c_n|$. Setting $n=0$ gives $c_0 = DV$, the DC line. Where the sine in the numerator is zero, $nD = k$, the harmonic is missing.

Two statements fall out of this formula:
1. **Line spacing is $1/T$.** Repeating the pulse more often spreads the lines apart; repeating less often packs them closer, in the limit becoming a continuum (the single pulse).
2. **Envelope width is $1/\tau$.** A shorter pulse widens the main lobe and flattens the spectrum. A pulse that is $10\times$ narrower has a main lobe $10\times$ wider.

You can read off general rules from these two figures:
- At $D = 1/k$ the $k$th harmonic is missing; $D = 1/3$ kills the 3rd, 6th, 9th; $D = 1/10$ kills every 10th.
- $D$ and $1-D$ give the same line amplitudes ($|\sin(n\pi D)| = |\sin(n\pi(1-D))|$); only the DC term and phases change.
- All spectra share the same $-20$ dB/dec upper envelope $2V/(n\pi)$.

## Adding finite rise time

Convolving the rectangular pulse with a rectangular smoothing window of width $t_r$ (a linear ramp edge on both sides) multiplies the spectrum by $\text{sinc}(f t_r)$. The envelope becomes
$$A(f) = 2DV\,\left|\text{sinc}(f\tau)\right|\,\left|\text{sinc}(f t_r)\right|$$
(here $\tau$ is the width measured at 50% for equal rise and fall). Asymptotically:

| Region | Frequency | Slope |
|---|---|---|
| Flat | $f < 1/(\pi\tau)$ | 0 dB/dec |
| First roll-off | $1/(\pi\tau) < f < 1/(\pi t_r)$ | $-20$ dB/dec |
| Second roll-off | $f > 1/(\pi t_r)$ | $-40$ dB/dec |

## What the line heights mean in practice

At the output of a spectrum analyzer, each line shows a narrowband peak. If the analyzer resolution bandwidth is smaller than $f_0$ you see the discrete lines; if larger, adjacent lines blend and look like a continuum. For a 100 MHz clock the lines are 100 MHz apart, so any practical RBW resolves them. For a low-frequency PWM at 1 kHz, with RBW 100 kHz you would see only the envelope. Emissions measurements (quasi-peak or peak detector, with the standard's RBW) therefore treat narrowband clock harmonics differently from broadband burst noise.

For a switching regulator with a 500 kHz fundamental and $D = 0.2$ on the switch node, the first null falls at the 5th harmonic (2.5 MHz) and harmonic 10 as well; the fundamental amplitude is $2V_{in}\sin(0.2\pi)/\pi = 0.374V_{in}$, which at a 12 V input is 4.5 V peak. You will see these lines in the conducted-emissions scan, with ringing of the switch node putting extra energy at tens to hundreds of MHz.
:::

::: applications
- **EMC pre-compliance**: predict which clock or PWM harmonic will exceed a limit and where on the frequency axis.
- **Choosing a PWM duty or switching frequency** so that a nasty harmonic lands in a null or outside a sensitive band (e.g. keep an AM-band or RF receiver frequency away from a converter harmonic).
- **Sizing an anti-alias or EMI filter**: knowing the first strong harmonics tells you the corner and order.
- **Scope bandwidth selection**: a signal's spectrum shows how far up the harmonics matter ([[q0676]]).
- **Spread-spectrum clocking**: modulating $f_0$ smears each line into a band, lowering the peak at each harmonic ([[q0674]]).
- **Class D amplifier and motor-drive design**: sets how much carrier and sideband energy the output filter must remove.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal repetition**: perfectly periodic, so the spectrum is exact lines. Jitter or modulation smears them.
- **Rectangular pulse with $\tau$ measured at 50%**; the trapezoid formula assumes equal rise and fall times and linear edges.
- **Single-sided peak amplitudes**, referenced to the voltage across a stated impedance; instruments show RMS in dBm or dB$\mu$V.
- **Unipolar (0 to $V$) wave**: a symmetric $\pm A$ wave removes the DC line and has the same harmonic amplitudes with $V = 2A$.
- **Linear, memoryless channel**: any filtering (cables, PCB loss) multiplies the envelope further.

If asked for "the spectrum of a pulse train" in a different context (radar, sampling theory), the ideal impulse train case is the relevant limit: equal-height lines at all harmonics.
:::

::: followups
### What are the first five harmonic amplitudes of a 50% duty, 0 to 3.3 V square wave?
$A_n = 2V/(n\pi)$ for odd $n$: $n=1$: 2.10 V, $n=3$: 0.70 V, $n=5$: 0.42 V, $n=7$: 0.30 V, $n=9$: 0.23 V. DC is 1.65 V. Even harmonics are zero.

### Where are the nulls for a 10% duty pulse train at 10 MHz?
$n = k/D = 10, 20, \dots$, i.e. at 100 MHz, 200 MHz, and so on (that is $k/\tau$ with $\tau = 10$ ns). Harmonics 1 to 9 are in the main lobe with amplitudes $2V|\sin(0.1n\pi)|/(n\pi)$: for $V = 1$ they are 0.197, 0.187, 0.172, 0.151, 0.127 (n=5)... so the lines are almost equal in height at low $n$, the opposite of the 50% case.

### How does the spectrum change if the pulse train has 5 ns rise/fall edges at 10 MHz, 50% duty?
The period is 100 ns, so a 5 ns edge is 5% of it. The edge sinc has its first null at $1/t_r = 200$ MHz (harmonic 20) and the knee at $1/(\pi t_r) = 64$ MHz (about harmonic 6). Harmonics 1, 3 and 5 are trimmed by less than 1 dB; above roughly the 7th the lines fall at 40 dB per decade instead of 20.

### Why do the spectra of single and repeated pulses look similar?
The repeated train is the single-pulse spectrum multiplied by a comb of impulses spaced $1/T$ (sampling in frequency). The envelope is the single-pulse transform; the lines sample it. Increase $T$ and the lines get denser; as $T\to\infty$ you recover the continuous single-pulse spectrum.

### A 50% clock shows a strong 2nd harmonic on the analyzer. What does it tell you?
The 2nd harmonic is a null at exactly 50% duty. A strong one means duty-cycle distortion. Fraction-of-period error $\delta$ gives a 2nd harmonic of $2V|\sin(2\pi(0.5+\delta))|/(2\pi) = V|\sin(2\pi\delta)|/\pi$; for $\delta = 0.02$ (a 48/52 duty) that is $0.039V$, about 24 dB below the 0.637 V fundamental, and it grows linearly with $\delta$.
:::

::: interpretations
"Square wave/pulse train" is written as a pair, so the intended answer covers both the 50% duty square wave (odd harmonics) and a general duty-cycle pulse train (sinc envelope with nulls). "Draw" means a labelled sketch; the figures above are what a good whiteboard answer looks like. If "pulse train" means an ideal impulse train, the spectrum is also an impulse train with spacing $1/T$ and constant height.
