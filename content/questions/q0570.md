::: testing
Whether you can explain shielding with physics, not slogans:

1. **Reflection** from the impedance mismatch between the wave and the metal.
2. **Absorption** through eddy-current loss over a few skin depths.
3. **The E-field vs H-field distinction**: electric fields are easy to shield (a grounded conductor), low-frequency magnetic fields are hard (need high-permeability material or distance/loop control).
4. **Leakage** through apertures, which usually dominates the real shielding effectiveness.
:::

::: why
"Put a metal can over it" works so often that people stop asking why, and then misapply it: a thin copper can does little against a 100 kHz magnetic field from an inductor, and an ungrounded can can make things worse. Understanding the mechanism tells you which material, what thickness, what grounding and where leaks occur.
:::

::: concepts
- **Skin depth** $\delta = 1/\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$: how deep fields penetrate a conductor. See [[q0056]].
- **Wave impedance and reflection**: mismatch between $377$ Ω (far field) and the metal's surface impedance.
- **Eddy currents**: induced currents in the metal that oppose the incident field.
- **Electrostatic shielding**: a grounded conductor terminating E-field lines. See [[q0573]].
- **Near field vs far field**: high-impedance (E-dominant) vs low-impedance (H-dominant) sources.
- **Apertures**: leakage through seams and holes. See [[q0569]].
:::

::: answer
"A shield works by two mechanisms: reflection and absorption. When a wave hits a metal surface, the wave impedance, 377 ohms in the far field, is far higher than the metal's surface impedance, milliohms, so almost all of it reflects. The small fraction that enters is absorbed: the field induces eddy currents in the metal that oppose it, so it decays exponentially with a skin depth. For copper at 100 MHz the skin depth is 6.6 microns, so a 35 micron foil is more than five skin depths and gives about 46 dB of absorption on its own.

In the near field the picture changes. An electric field source is high impedance, so reflection is very high, and you can shield it with any grounded conductor: it's just electrostatic shielding, the field lines terminate on the shield. A magnetic field source like an inductor is low impedance, so reflection is poor, and at low frequencies a thin non-magnetic shield absorbs little; for 100 kHz, copper foil is hardly better than nothing, so you either use thick or high-permeability material like steel or mu-metal, or reduce the loop and increase distance.

And whatever the metal does in theory, the real shielding is set by leakage: seams, vents and unfiltered cables. A 10 mm slot only gives about 16 dB at 2.4 GHz."
:::

::: technical
**Total shielding effectiveness** $SE = R + A + B$ (dB): reflection loss $R$, absorption loss $A$, and a multiple-reflection correction $B$ (negative, only significant when absorption is below about 9 dB).

**Absorption.** $A = 8.686\,t/\delta$ dB, with skin depth $\delta = 1/\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$. For copper ($\sigma$ = 5.8×10⁷ S/m, $\mu_r$ = 1): $\delta\approx 66\,\mu\text{m}/\sqrt{f_{MHz}}$: 66 µm at 1 MHz, 6.6 µm at 100 MHz, 1.35 µm at 2.4 GHz. For $t$ = 35 µm: $A$ = 4.6 dB at 1 MHz, 46 dB at 100 MHz, 225 dB at 2.4 GHz. At 1 kHz, 0.2 mm of copper has only 0.8 dB.

**Reflection (plane wave).** $R = 20\log_{10}\dfrac{\eta_0}{4|Z_s|}$, with $|Z_s|=\sqrt2/(\sigma\delta)$. For copper this is $R\approx168-10\log_{10}f$ dB: 138 dB at 1 kHz, 88 dB at 100 MHz.

**Near field.** For a magnetic source at distance $r$ (m), Ott's approximation: $R_H\approx14.6+10\log_{10}(f\,r^2\,\sigma_r/\mu_r)$ dB (with $\sigma_r$ relative to copper): for copper, $r$ = 5 cm: 18.6 dB at 1 kHz, 38.6 dB at 100 kHz, 58.6 dB at 10 MHz. So a 35 µm copper shield gives only about 19 dB at 1 kHz and 40 dB at 100 kHz against a close magnetic source. Electric fields are reflected far more strongly.

**Magnetic materials.** High-$\mu_r$ materials (steel, mu-metal) increase absorption ($\delta\propto1/\sqrt\mu$) and *shunt* low-frequency flux; they saturate at high field and lose permeability at high frequency.

**Apertures.** For a slot of length $l$: $SE_{ap}\approx20\log_{10}(\lambda/2l)$ dB (valid for $l<\lambda/2$); arrays of holes and thick walls behave as waveguides below cutoff.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The near-field reflection formula and the slot formula are engineering approximations (Ott and others); measured shielding effectiveness depends on geometry, seams and the source's field type.
:::

::: long
"There are two main mechanisms, reflection and absorption. For reflection, think impedance mismatch. A far-field wave has a wave impedance of 377 ohms. A copper surface has a surface impedance of milliohms, about 3.7 milliohms at 100 MHz. That mismatch reflects almost everything, and the reflection loss is 88 dB at 100 MHz in the far field, and 138 dB at 1 kHz.

For absorption, the field that does enter the metal induces eddy currents that oppose it, so it decays exponentially with a skin depth delta equal to one over root pi f mu sigma. In copper that's 66 microns at 1 MHz and 6.6 microns at 100 MHz. The loss is 8.7 dB per skin depth, so 35 micron copper gives 46 dB at 100 MHz, and over 200 dB at 2.4 GHz.

But the near field is different. A source with high voltage and low current, like a trace or a switch node, is an electric field source with high wave impedance, so reflection is huge, and any grounded conductor shields it, that's electrostatic shielding, the field lines terminate on the shield. A source with high current, like an inductor or a power loop, is a magnetic source with low wave impedance, so reflection is weak, and at low frequencies, say 1 kHz to 100 kHz, a thin copper shield gives almost nothing: about 19 dB at 1 kHz for a source 5 cm away. For that you use steel or mu-metal, which shunts the flux, or you reduce the loop area and move away.

And above all, the practical limit is leakage. A 10 millimetre slot is only 16 dB at 2.4 GHz, so seams, vents and any wire crossing the wall define the real shielding."
:::

::: terms
- **Shielding effectiveness (SE)**: ratio of field without and with the shield, in dB.
- **Reflection loss ($R$)**: attenuation from the impedance mismatch at the surface.
- **Absorption loss ($A$)**: attenuation from eddy-current dissipation inside the metal.
- **Skin depth ($\delta$)**: depth at which field amplitude falls to $1/e$ in a conductor.
- **Wave impedance**: ratio $E/H$ of the field; 377 Ω in the far field, higher for E-field sources, lower for H-field sources near field.
- **Eddy current**: circulating current induced in a conductor by a changing magnetic field.
- **Mu-metal**: high-permeability nickel-iron alloy for low-frequency magnetic shielding.
- **Aperture leakage**: field passing through gaps and holes in the shield.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the metal reflects or absorbs the field".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates reflection and absorption, with formulas and numbers (skin depth for copper, 8.7 dB per $\delta$).
- Distinguishes electric from magnetic near-field shielding and explains why thin copper fails for low-frequency magnetic fields.
- Mentions magnetic materials (steel, mu-metal) and their limits (saturation, frequency).
- Insists that apertures and seams set real performance and gives the slot formula.
- Ties grounding into electric shielding (shield must be grounded; [[q0571]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Thicker is always better."** At RF a few skin depths are enough; beyond that, thickness doesn't matter, leakage does.
- **Expecting copper foil to stop 50/60 Hz or kHz magnetic fields.** Absorption is negligible; use magnetic material or distance.
- **Thinking the shield works only by absorbing.** At RF, reflection dominates.
- **Leaving an electric shield ungrounded.** It then floats and re-radiates or couples capacitively.
- **Ignoring apertures** (vents, seams, cable entries).
- **Confusing steel's advantage.** Steel helps low-frequency magnetic shielding but it is worse at reflection (lower conductivity) and saturates.
:::

## Shielding as a loss budget

A wave hitting a shield of thickness $t$ passes three stages: reflection at the front surface, attenuation inside the metal, and partial reflection at the back, with re-reflections inside. In dB:

$$SE = R + A + B.$$

## Absorption and skin depth

Inside a good conductor a field decays as $e^{-x/\delta}$ with

$$\delta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}}.$$

Over a thickness $t$ the amplitude falls by $e^{-t/\delta}$, i.e. $A = 8.686\,t/\delta$ dB.

| Frequency | $\delta$ (Cu) | $A$ for 35 µm | $A$ for 0.2 mm |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 kHz | 2.09 mm | 0.15 dB | 0.8 dB |
| 100 kHz | 209 µm | 1.5 dB | 8.3 dB |
| 1 MHz | 66 µm | 4.6 dB | 26 dB |
| 100 MHz | 6.6 µm | 46 dB | 263 dB |
| 2.4 GHz | 1.35 µm | 225 dB | 1288 dB |

Absorption is negligible at low frequency and enormous at RF. For frequencies above a few MHz, even a copper flash a few skin depths thick works.

## Reflection and the impedance picture

A plane wave in air has $\eta_0$ = 377 Ω. The metal's surface impedance has magnitude $|Z_s|=\sqrt2/(\sigma\delta)$, 3.7 mΩ for copper at 100 MHz. The mismatch gives

$$R=20\log_{10}\frac{\eta_0}{4|Z_s|}=88\ \text{dB at 100 MHz}.$$

Reflection loss falls with frequency (138 dB at 1 kHz down to 88 dB at 100 MHz for copper plane waves), but absorption rises, so the sum has a minimum in the low MHz range.

## Near-field: electric and magnetic sources differ

Close to a source (distance below $\lambda/2\pi$) the wave impedance isn't 377 Ω:

- **High-voltage, low-current sources** (switch nodes, high-impedance traces): *E-field dominant*, wave impedance higher than 377 Ω, reflection even stronger. A grounded conductor of any thickness blocks them: this is *electrostatic shielding*; the field lines end on the shield and the current returns to ground. It requires a good ground connection ([[q0571]]).
- **Low-voltage, high-current sources** (inductors, power loops, transformers): *H-field dominant*, wave impedance below 377 Ω, so reflection loss is much lower, especially at low frequency. Only absorption or flux shunting helps.

## What to do about low-frequency magnetic fields

1. **Use magnetic material.** Steel or mu-metal ($\mu_r$ from hundreds to tens of thousands) increases absorption, since $\delta\propto1/\sqrt{\mu}$, and provides a low-reluctance path that diverts flux around the protected region. Limits: saturates at high flux density and permeability falls with frequency.
2. **Reduce the source loop** and increase distance: field falls with distance from a small loop as $1/r^3$.
3. **Use a closed conductive loop or "shorted turn"** to cancel low-frequency fields by induced current (only effective when the loop impedance is mostly resistive at the frequency; thick copper helps).
4. **Twist and balance** conductors that carry current to cancel the field at source.

## Leakage dominates in practice

Metal is cheap; closing the gaps is not. A slot of length $l$ in a wall radiates through as

$$SE_{ap}\approx20\log_{10}\frac{\lambda}{2l}.$$

At 1 GHz (λ = 300 mm), $l$ = 10 mm gives 23.5 dB; at 2.4 GHz, 15.9 dB. Thick walls with small holes act as waveguides below cutoff and give extra attenuation of about $27.3\,t/w$ dB for a rectangular opening of width $w$, and about $32\,t/d$ dB for a circular hole of diameter $d$ (use vent patterns with long thin holes). Conductors passing through the wall must be filtered or they behave as antennas that carry fields through.

Design rules:
- Seam contact spacing at most $\lambda/20$ at the highest frequency of concern.
- Gaskets for enclosure seams; conductive finish on mating surfaces.
- Keep holes small; use honeycomb vents.
- Filter every wire crossing the wall.

## Worked example

A 3.3 V switch node at 1.2 MHz, 5 cm from a sensitive coil. A 0.2 mm aluminium can ($\sigma$ = 3.5×10⁷ S/m, so $\delta$ = 85 µm at 1 MHz) gives $A = 8.686\cdot200/85 = 20$ dB. Ott's near-field term with $\sigma_r\approx0.6$ adds about 46 dB, for roughly 66 dB against the switching loop's magnetic field at 1.2 MHz, which is adequate. The same can against a 1.5 kHz PWM-related field has $\delta$ = 2.2 mm, so $A$ = 0.8 dB and $R_H\approx18$ dB: about 19 dB in total. For that you reach for steel or mu-metal, or shrink the loop.
:::

::: applications
- **Shield cans over RF and PA sections**: reflection and absorption are enormous at GHz; leakage rules.
- **Power inductors with shielded cores**: ferrite or steel cap confines the magnetic field.
- **Mu-metal cans around sensitive magnetic sensors and CRTs/photomultipliers**: low-frequency magnetic shielding.
- **Shielded cables**: braid and foil, effective against electric fields and high-frequency magnetic fields.
- **Faraday enclosures and screen rooms**: closed conductive boxes with gasketed doors ([[q0573]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Good, continuous conductor** with thickness well above a skin depth for RF cases.
- **Plane-wave (far-field) reflection** unless stated; near-field sources need $R_E$ or $R_H$.
- **Copper properties**: $\sigma$ = 5.8×10⁷ S/m, $\mu_r$ = 1. Other metals scale with $\sqrt{\mu_r\sigma}$ in absorption.
- **Formulas for $R_H$ and slot SE** are engineering approximations; numeric results are indicative.
- **Multiple-reflection correction $B$** is neglected (significant only for thin shields at low frequency).
- **Alternative view**: shielding by "return-current cancellation" (a conductor near a signal path carrying equal and opposite induced current) is what ground planes do at low frequency.
:::

::: followups
### Why is a thin copper foil a good shield at 1 GHz but poor at 100 kHz for a magnetic source?
At 1 GHz the skin depth is 2 µm, so even 35 µm is many skin depths and absorption is huge. At 100 kHz the skin depth is 209 µm, so 35 µm absorbs only about 1.5 dB, and near a magnetic source the reflection is weak too (about 39 dB at 5 cm), giving a total of about 40 dB at best. For tens of kHz and below you need magnetic material or distance.

### Why does an electric field get blocked by any grounded metal, but not an ungrounded one?
A conductor in an electric field redistributes its charges so the interior field is zero (electrostatic shielding), but the charge on its surface must be able to flow. If the shield is grounded, those charges drain to ground and the field outside is terminated on it; if it floats, its potential follows the field and it capacitively couples a share of the field through to the other side. So grounding matters ([[q0571]]).

### What is the shielding effectiveness of an aperture?
For a slot of length $l$ in a thin wall, about $20\log_{10}(\lambda/2l)$ dB, valid for $l<\lambda/2$. At 2.4 GHz a 10 mm slot gives 15.9 dB. So seam contact spacing and hole sizes must be small compared with $\lambda$. Many small holes beat one large hole.

### Does steel shield better than copper?
Not at RF: steel has lower conductivity so reflection is poorer, but it has high permeability, so absorption at low frequency is stronger (skin depth is smaller) and it shunts magnetic flux. For kHz-range magnetic fields steel or mu-metal wins; for GHz, plated steel works because plating plus high absorption is sufficient. Steel saturates at high field.

### Scenario: a 100 kHz power inductor is coupling into a nearby sense coil even with a copper foil between them. What do you do?
Copper foil gives only about 40 dB at best at 100 kHz for a source 5 cm away and much less if the foil is small or has gaps, and eddy currents in the foil also add loss to the inductor. I'd move the sense coil or rotate it to be orthogonal to the field, shrink the inductor loop, use a shielded or toroidal inductor, add a steel or ferrite shield layer, and check with a pickup coil to confirm.
