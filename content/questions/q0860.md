::: basics
### In plain words
The light in a scene changes a camera's picture in three ways: how much light there is, what color it is, and how steady it is. Too little light gives grain and blur. Tinted light makes colors look wrong. Flickering light makes bands in the image.

### Picture it
Your own eyes do the same. In a dim room you struggle to see fine detail. Under a yellow bulb a white sheet looks yellow until your brain adjusts. Under a flickering tube light you see a faint strobing. Your brain fixes much of this on its own, but a camera must calculate each fix. The eye picture hides one thing: a camera also has a limit on how much contrast it can capture in one shot.

### Words you need
- **Lux**: a brightness unit based on what the human eye sees. See [[q0851]].
- **Color temperature**: how warm or cool a light looks. A candle-like bulb is warm, near 2700 K. Daylight is cooler, near 6500 K.
- **White balance**: adjusting red, green and blue so that white objects look white under that light.
- **Flicker**: brightness that varies rapidly, such as 100 times a second under 50 Hz mains power.
- **Dynamic range**: the span from darkest to brightest detail a camera can capture in one shot.

### The simplest example
An office is about 500 lux. A night street is about 5 lux, so 100 times darker. A small phone pixel might collect about 510 electrons in 1/30 s at the office, and only about 5 on the street. The grain is about the square root of the count. That gives a signal-to-noise ratio of about 23 at the office and about 2 on the street. Now flicker: under 50 Hz mains, the lamp flickers at 100 Hz, so each flicker lasts 10 ms. An exposure of 1/100 s covers exactly one flicker, and the bands vanish. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows how each effect is handled in the camera.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can connect **light level, light spectrum and light time-structure** to concrete camera behavior. The interviewer is listening for three separate effects: brightness (SNR, exposure time, noise), spectrum (white balance, color accuracy, infrared), and temporal flicker (banding, rolling shutter). Strong candidates also bring up dynamic range (backlit scenes), glare and flare, and autofocus failure in low light, and they back it up with orders of magnitude rather than vague statements.
:::

::: why
In product work, "the camera looks bad in this room" is one of the most common bug reports, and the cause is almost always the light. Knowing the three levers (how much, what color, how steady) lets you decide whether it's a sensor, ISP tuning, lens or lighting problem. It also matters for vision-based systems such as robots, cars and AR headsets, where the lighting is not under your control and the algorithms (and the safety case) must work from direct sun to a dark tunnel.
:::

::: concepts
- **Illuminance (lux)**: light power per unit area weighted for the eye; scenes span from about $10^{-1}$ lux (moonlight) to $10^5$ lux (full sun) ([[q0851]], [[q0852]]).
- **Photon budget and SNR**: signal electrons scale with illuminance, exposure time and pixel area, and shot noise sets SNR ([[q0856]], [[q0859]]).
- **Color temperature and spectrum**: the illuminant's spectrum changes raw RGB ratios, handled by white balance ([[q0862]]).
- **Flicker**: AC-powered lights and PWM-dimmed LEDs vary in brightness at 100 or 120 Hz and above, which interacts with exposure time and rolling shutter.
- **Dynamic range**: ratio of brightest to darkest usable signal in one scene or one capture ([[q0861]]).
- **Autofocus and metering dependence**: contrast and phase detection both need signal.
:::

::: answer
"Ambient light affects a camera in three ways: how much, what color, and how steady.

How much is the big one. A scene in full sun is around 100,000 lux, an office is about 500, a dim living room is 50 and a street at night is a few lux. The signal the sensor collects scales with that, and photon shot noise means SNR goes as the square root of the signal. So in the dark, the image gets noisy, the camera has to use long exposures that blur, and it cranks gain. Autofocus also gets slower or fails because contrast and phase signals drop.

What color matters because different lights have different spectra: daylight is around 6500 K, incandescent is 2700 K, and some LEDs have spiky spectra. The sensor just sees raw red, green and blue, so the ISP has to estimate the illuminant and apply white balance, and under bad spectra the colors come out wrong or the blue channel is noisy.

How steady matters because mains lights flicker at 100 or 120 Hz, and PWM-dimmed LEDs even faster. If the exposure isn't a multiple of the flicker period, or with a rolling shutter, you get brightness banding across the frame.

On top of that, scenes with strong contrast, like a backlit person, exceed the sensor's dynamic range, so you need HDR, and bright sources cause glare and lens flare."
:::

::: technical
**Brightness.** Electrons collected scale as $S \propto E\,T_{exp}\,A_{pix}/N^2$ (illuminance, exposure time, pixel area, f-number). For a 1.0 µm pixel behind an f/1.8 lens at 1/30 s, a rough estimate is about 1 electron per lux (order of magnitude). That gives about 510 e⁻ at 500 lux (SNR 27 dB with 3 e⁻ read noise), 51 e⁻ at 50 lux (16 dB), and 5 e⁻ at 5 lux (2.7 dB). Each factor of 10 in light is 10 dB of SNR in the shot-noise-limited regime ($\sqrt{10}$ in amplitude), and a bit more than that in the read-noise-limited regime.

**Exposure and blur.** Dropping from 500 lux to 5 lux is a factor of 100, or 6.6 stops. The exposure time (blur risk), gain (noise), or aperture (fixed on phones) must absorb this. Hence blur at low light ([[q0857]]) and multi-frame night capture ([[q0858]]).

**Spectrum.** Each pixel's response is $\int E(\lambda)\,\rho(\lambda)\,QE_c(\lambda)\,d\lambda$ for channel $c$. Different illuminants $E(\lambda)$ give different (R,G,B) for the same surface. White balance multiplies channels by gains; if blue carries one quarter of the signal of green, its SNR is lower by $\sqrt{4} = 2$, or 6 dB, even after balancing.

**Flicker.** For light $I(t) = I_0[1 + m\cos(2\pi f t)]$ and exposure $T$, the recorded modulation depth is $m\,|\text{sinc}(fT)|$ where $\text{sinc}(x) = \sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)$. It is zero when $fT$ is an integer: exposure times that are multiples of 10 ms (100 Hz flicker, 50 Hz mains) or 8.33 ms (120 Hz, 60 Hz mains) cancel it.

**Dynamic range.** A sunlit/shaded scene at 100,000 vs 1,000 lux is 6.6 stops of illumination; surface reflectance from 3 to 90 percent is another 4.9 stops, so 11.5 stops in total, comparable to a single exposure's 11 to 12 stops, so highlights or shadows will clip or be noisy.
:::

::: short

Spectrum: different lights shift raw color, so white balance has to estimate the illuminant, and LED spectra can cause color errors. And time structure: mains and PWM lights flicker, which causes banding unless exposure is matched. High contrast scenes also exceed dynamic range, so you need HDR."
:::

::: long
"I think of ambient light as three separate things: amount, color and steadiness.

Amount first. Scenes go from about a tenth of a lux under moonlight, through 50 for a living room and 500 for an office, to 100,000 in sun. The pixel collects electrons in proportion to that. A 1 micron phone pixel at f/1.8 and 1/30 second gets a few hundred electrons in an office but only about five at 5 lux. Photon shot noise is the square root of the signal, so the SNR drops from about 27 dB to under 3 dB. The camera has to choose between a longer exposure, which blurs, or more gain, which just amplifies noise. That's why phones add multi-frame night modes. Autofocus suffers too, because contrast and phase signals are weaker.

Color second. The sensor's raw RGB depends on the light's spectrum. Daylight, tungsten and LED lights give very different raw ratios, so white balance estimates the illuminant and applies gains. Under low color temperature light, the blue channel receives far less signal, so after gain it is noisier. Narrow-band or spiky LEDs can cause color errors that a simple matrix cannot fix.

Steadiness third. Lights on 50 or 60 Hz mains flicker at 100 or 120 Hz, and PWM dimmed LEDs flicker at kilohertz. If the exposure isn't an integer number of flicker periods, brightness varies between frames, and with a rolling shutter it varies across rows, giving dark bands. The fix is exposure times that are multiples of the flicker period, or flicker detection in the ISP.

Finally, contrast: a backlit subject or a window can exceed 12 stops, beyond a single exposure, so we use HDR bracketing and tone mapping, and bright sources cause lens flare."
:::

::: terms
- **Illuminance (lux)**: luminous flux per unit area, weighted by the eye's sensitivity.
- **Correlated color temperature (CCT)**: temperature of a blackbody with similar color; 2700 K is warm incandescent, 6500 K is daylight.
- **White balance (WB)**: per-channel gains that make neutral surfaces look neutral under the scene's illuminant.
- **Flicker / banding**: periodic brightness variation of the light causing bands in rolling-shutter images.
- **PWM dimming**: LEDs dimmed by switching on and off at a duty cycle, producing flicker at the PWM frequency.
- **Dynamic range**: ratio of largest to smallest signal that can be captured, in stops or dB.
- **Lens flare / veiling glare**: stray light reflections inside the lens that reduce contrast near bright sources.
- **IR cut filter**: filter that blocks near-infrared that silicon can detect but the eye cannot see.
- **3A**: auto exposure, auto focus, auto white balance.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "low light is noisy and some lights look yellow." An exceptional answer:

- Separates **amount, spectrum and temporal structure** and ties each to a hardware or algorithm response.
- Gives the lux scale and a worked electron count, showing how SNR falls with illuminance.
- Explains why exposure is constrained (blur) and what the camera trades: exposure, gain, aperture, binning, multi-frame.
- Explains flicker quantitatively with $\text{sinc}(fT)$ and the 10 ms or 8.33 ms rule, and mentions rolling shutter.
- Mentions IR (cut filter, night-vision IR illuminators), glare, HDR and autofocus assist (laser, ToF, PDAF).
- Notes that ambient light also affects LiDAR and other active sensors ([[q0870]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Just increase the gain in low light."** Gain doesn't add photons; SNR is set by collected light. It only changes where noise is relative to the ADC.
- **"Color shift is the lens or sensor fault."** It's usually the illuminant. White balance is estimated, and can be wrong in mixed lighting or with narrow-spectrum LEDs.
- **"Banding is a sensor defect."** It is a beat between light flicker and exposure/readout timing, fixable by exposure times or flicker detection.
- **Ignoring infrared.** Without an IR cut filter, sunlight and incandescent light add near-IR that turns foliage and black fabric strange colors.
- **Confusing lux with watts.** Lux is photometric (weighted by the eye); sensors have their own spectral response, so equal lux of different sources doesn't mean equal electrons.
- **Forgetting that autofocus also depends on light.** Many "bad low-light photos" are just out of focus.
:::

::: deep
## 1. How much light: from lux to electrons

A useful chain is scene illuminance $E_s$ (lux) to scene luminance $L = E_s\rho/\pi$ (cd/m², for a diffuse surface with reflectance $\rho$) to image-plane illuminance $E_i \approx \pi L T/(4N^2)$ to photons per pixel to electrons.

Figure: The 1/30 s curve is about 1.02 electrons per lux (order of magnitude, spectrum-dependent). At the typical office 500 lux the pixel is comfortably in shot-noise territory (27 dB); at 5 lux it's barely above 0 dB. Shortening the exposure to 1/250 s to freeze motion costs 8.3x less light: about 9 dB of SNR where shot noise dominates, and up to 18 dB at the read-noise-limited dark end; that's the trade-off between blur and noise.

Rule of thumb: every factor of 10 in light is +10 dB SNR in the shot-noise-limited regime ($\sqrt{10}$ in amplitude), and every factor of 2 is +3 dB.

**What the camera does about it.** Auto exposure picks $(T, G)$ from a program line. It first lengthens exposure up to a limit set by motion (gyro) and frame rate (30 fps caps video at 1/30 s), then raises analog gain, then digital gain, then, if enabled, switches to multi-frame night mode ([[q0858]]). In video you cannot stack as freely, so dark video looks noisier than dark photos.

## 2. Color: the light's spectrum changes the raw image

A sensor channel's output is an integral over wavelength of illuminant × surface reflectance × channel sensitivity. A white wall under 2700 K tungsten reflects light rich in red and poor in blue, so raw RGB is far from equal. The ISP applies per-channel gains so that gray appears gray.

- Typical correlated color temperatures: candle about 1900 K, incandescent about 2700 K, warm-white LED 2700 to 3000 K, daylight about 5500 to 6500 K, overcast sky above 6500 K.
- If the blue channel starts with a quarter of the green signal, then SNR in blue is $\sqrt{1/4} = 0.5$ of green's (6 dB lower) after balancing: tungsten-lit photos have blue channel noise, a classic sign of "colored noise" in the shadows.
- **Mixed lighting** (window daylight plus tungsten) makes a single global gain wrong; phones apply spatially varying WB or learned color constancy.
- **Spiky spectra** (some fluorescent and cheap LED lamps): the 3x3 color correction matrix is calibrated for broad, smooth illuminants. A light with narrow spikes can make colors that look similar to the eye differ in raw RGB (metamerism), so colors come out shifted. The color rendering index (CRI) of the light predicts this.
- **Infrared**: silicon responds to about 1000 to 1100 nm, so IR cut filters are in the stack. Tungsten and sunlight have a lot of near-IR. Security cameras remove the IR cut filter at night and use 850 or 940 nm illuminators, trading color for sensitivity.

## 3. Time structure: flicker

Mains-powered lights flicker at twice the mains frequency (100 Hz in 50 Hz countries, 120 Hz in 60 Hz countries). LED lights with simple drivers flicker too, and PWM-dimmed LEDs (including displays and car lights) flicker at hundreds to tens of thousands of Hz.

If the light is $I(t) = I_0[1 + m\cos(2\pi f t)]$, the exposure integral over time $T$ gives a recorded modulation of $m\,|\text{sinc}(fT)|$.

Figure: Nulls occur when the exposure is an integer number of flicker periods. At 1/250 s (4 ms) the recorded modulation is still 76 percent (100 Hz) or 66 percent (120 Hz): strong flicker. At 1/100 s under 50 Hz mains it's exactly zero. That is why the camera's auto-exposure uses a "50 Hz / 60 Hz anti-banding" setting and why short exposures in bright indoor scenes show bands.

With a **rolling shutter**, each row's exposure window starts at a different time, so the phase of the flicker differs between rows. If readout takes 30 ms and the flicker period is 10 ms, there are 3 dark bands across the frame; at 120 Hz (8.33 ms) there are 3.6. Whether the bands stand still or roll depends on the frame rate: at 30 fps a frame lasts exactly 4 periods of 120 Hz flicker, so the bands are stationary, but 3.33 periods of 100 Hz flicker, so under 50 Hz mains they roll from frame to frame, which looks like a moving stripe in video. Solutions: lock exposure to the flicker period (needs knowing the mains frequency, which can be detected from the frame statistics or an ambient flicker sensor), use a short global-shutter exposure, or use flicker-correction ISP gains per row. For very fast PWM (kHz), exposure of at least one PWM period averages it out, but at very short exposure in bright light (to avoid saturation) that isn't possible, which is a known problem with LED traffic signs and car lights in automotive cameras. Sensors with LED-flicker mitigation use multiple capture, split pixels, or charge-overflow capacitors to extend exposure without saturating.

## 4. Dynamic range and glare

An outdoor scene with sun and shade: illuminance ratio 100:1 (6.6 stops) multiplied by surface reflectance range of about 30:1 (4.9 stops) gives about 11.5 stops. A single 6000 e⁻ / 2.5 e⁻ sensor has 11.2 stops ([[q0856]]), so it is on the edge: the shadows are noisy or the highlights are clipped. Windows in an indoor scene routinely exceed 16 stops. HDR methods: bracketed exposures merged in raw, dual-conversion-gain pixels, or split pixels with different sensitivities. [[q0861]] uses these numbers when it defines dynamic range as a camera-quality metric.

**Glare and flare.** Light from a bright source scatters off lens surfaces and the sensor cover glass, adding a veil of stray light that fills the shadows and lowers contrast; reflections between surfaces form ghost images. Anti-reflective coatings and a lens hood reduce them. It's an optical problem that image processing cannot fully fix, and the stray-light floor limits the usable dynamic range regardless of the sensor.

## 5. Autofocus and metering also depend on the light

- **Contrast AF** looks for maximum local contrast; with few photons the contrast signal drowns in noise. It becomes slow and hunts.
- **Phase-detect AF (PDAF)** compares left and right sub-images from masked or dual-photodiode pixels; it is faster but still needs SNR and degrades in the dark.
- **Active assist**: a laser or time-of-flight ranger (a near-IR time-of-flight measurement as in [[q0865]]) gives distance in the dark; some cameras use a short-burst of IR or visible AF-assist illumination.
- **Metering** can be fooled by a bright window or a spotlighted stage; it needs scene-adaptive weighting and face-priority.

::: applications
- **Phone camera tuning**: AE/AWB/flicker tuning across lighting conditions (tungsten, LED, fluorescent, daylight), validated in a lightbox with calibrated sources.
- **Automotive cameras**: tunnel exits and LED headlights need HDR sensors and LED-flicker mitigation.
- **Security cameras**: day/night mode with a switchable IR cut filter and IR illuminators.
- **Machine vision**: controlled lighting (strobes, shrouds) to remove ambient light variation, often with narrow-band filters.
- **Robotics and drones**: visual odometry that must survive sun glare and shadows; some use event cameras or IR illumination.
- **Photography / video studios**: lighting choice (high-CRI, flicker-free LED) to avoid banding in high-speed or slow-motion capture.
:::

::: assumptions
- Illuminance values are typical orders of magnitude (moonlight about 0.1 to 1 lux, office about 300 to 500, overcast day about 1,000 to 10,000, full sun about 100,000) and vary a lot with conditions.
- The electrons-per-lux figure for the 1.0 µm pixel is a rough order-of-magnitude estimate that assumes broadband white light and typical optics; it changes with the spectrum, lens transmission, microlens efficiency and QE.
- Flicker analysis assumes sinusoidal modulation at a single frequency. Real LED drivers produce non-sinusoidal waveforms with harmonics, so the nulls are only approximate.
- The dynamic range example uses reflectance 3 to 90 percent and 100:1 sun/shade; actual scenes vary.
- If the question means *active* sensors (LiDAR, ToF, structured light), ambient light is a noise source (sunlight background) rather than the signal; see [[q0870]].
:::

::: followups
### Why does a phone video look noisier than a photo in the same dim room?
A photo can use multi-frame night capture and exposures up to a second or more. Video is limited to the frame time (1/30 s at 30 fps, and typically shorter because of blur and rolling shutter), and temporal denoising has less freedom because motion causes ghosts. So video runs at lower signal and higher gain than a photo of the same scene.

### What exposure time would you pick to avoid flicker banding in a European office, and why?
Multiples of 10 ms: 1/100 s, 1/50 s, 1/33 s, and so on, because European mains is 50 Hz and lights flicker at 100 Hz. At $T = 10$ ms the flicker integrates to zero. In the US, multiples of 8.33 ms (1/120 s, 1/60 s). In a bright room where 1/100 s saturates, you'd close the aperture or use an ND filter if possible, since you can't use shorter exposures without banding.

### Your product shows green or magenta tint under certain LED lights but looks right under daylight. How would you debug it?
Measure the LED's spectrum with a spectrometer (and check CRI), capture raw frames of a color chart under it, and compare the raw RGB ratios and the estimated WB gains to the ideal. Likely the AWB algorithm has no calibration point near that spectrum, or the 3x3 CCM is only calibrated for broadband illuminants. Fix by adding an illuminant calibration point or a learned AWB, and if the sensor's color filters are broad enough, a spectrally tuned CCM.

### How does ambient light affect an active sensor like LiDAR or a ToF camera, and how does it differ from a normal camera?
For an active sensor, sunlight at the detector's wavelength is a background that adds shot noise and can saturate the detector, while the signal is the reflected laser pulse. Mitigations are narrow optical band-pass filters, short pulses with high peak power, time gating and coincidence detection. See [[q0870]].

### Scenario: customers report a faint moving dark band in video recorded under their LED ceiling lights. Rolling shutter or the lights?
Both: the cause is the beat between the light's flicker frequency and the sensor's readout. Check the band motion: if it drifts at a frequency equal to the difference between the light flicker and a multiple of the frame rate, it's flicker. Test by recording at exposure times of 1/100 and 1/120 s and see if one removes it, or record with a lower-flicker lamp. If the bands persist at the matched exposure, the light has PWM at a higher frequency or more than one flicker component; then look at the exposure time vs PWM period, or use the sensor's flicker-mitigation mode.
:::
