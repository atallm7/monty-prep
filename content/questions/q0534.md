::: testing
Whether you treat copper as a real resistor with a temperature limit instead of an ideal wire. The interviewer wants to hear that you can size a conductor from current, allowed temperature rise and allowed voltage drop, and that you know the tricks that follow from that: short and wide, planes instead of traces, vias in parallel, no necks, careful pad connections, and a clean return path. The hidden sub-skills are quick mental numbers (about 0.5 mΩ per square for 1 oz copper), knowing the IPC-2221 trace-current rule exists, and spotting the weak links, which are usually vias, pads and connectors rather than the trace itself.
:::

::: why
High-current boards fail in boring ways: a trace that glows, a connector that browns, a regulator that browns out because 200 mV disappeared in the wiring, a via that fuses. Most of these are layout mistakes that cost nothing to avoid at design time and a lot to fix after fab. In an interview this question is also a quick way to see whether you have laid out a real power board, because the rules are practical rather than theoretical. The same rules carry over to motor drivers, battery packs, and every switching regulator, which is why this section leads into the power-supply layout questions that follow ([[q0542]] onward).
:::

::: concepts
- **Copper resistance**: $R = \rho L/(wt)$, and for 1 oz copper (35 µm) that is about 0.48 mΩ per square at 20 °C. See [[q0001]].
- **$I^2R$ heating**: dissipated power sets the temperature rise, and copper resistance rises about 0.4% per °C, so hot traces get worse. See [[q0019]].
- **IPC-2221 / IPC-2152 current capacity**: standard charts and formulas relating width, copper weight and temperature rise.
- **Voltage drop budget**: often the real limit is the IR drop, not the temperature. See [[q0543]].
- **Vias and pads as bottlenecks**: a via barrel is a tiny copper tube; a pad neck or thermal relief spoke is a narrow constriction. See [[q0538]], [[q0539]].
- **Return path**: every amp out must come back, and the return needs the same care as the supply.
:::

::: answer
"My rules of thumb start from the fact that copper is just a resistor. One ounce copper is about half a milliohm per square, so I think in squares: a trace that is 50 squares long is 25 mΩ, and at 10 A that is a quarter of a volt and 2.5 W. That's already a lot, so rule one is short and wide, and wherever I can I use a pour or a plane instead of a trace.

Rule two is size it properly. I pick a temperature rise, say 10 to 20 degrees, and use the IPC-2221 or IPC-2152 charts for the width, then I separately check the voltage drop against what the load can tolerate, because that often needs more copper than the heating limit does. Outer layers carry more current per width than inner layers since they cool better.

Rule three is watch the weak links: use several vias in parallel when current changes layers, not one; don't neck the trace down at pads; use direct connections or wide spokes on power pads instead of tiny thermal reliefs; and make sure the connector has enough pins.

Last, route the return with the same care, keep the loop small, and keep noisy high-current paths away from sensitive analog. If it's really big I'll go to 2 oz copper, add solder or a bus bar, and check it with a thermal camera on the first board."
:::

::: technical
**Resistance.** $R = \rho L/(wt)$ with $\rho_{Cu} = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot$m at 20 °C. Sheet resistance is $\rho/t$: 0.48 mΩ/sq for 1 oz (35 µm), 0.24 mΩ/sq for 2 oz. Resistance rises roughly 0.39% per °C, so at 100 °C copper is about 31% more resistive.

**Current capacity (IPC-2221).** $I = k\,\Delta T^{0.44}A^{0.725}$ with $A$ the cross-section in mil², $k = 0.048$ for external layers and $0.024$ for internal layers, $\Delta T$ the temperature rise in °C. For 1 oz copper and $\Delta T = 20$ °C this gives about 0.9 mm for 3 A, 1.8 mm for 5 A and 4.7 mm for 10 A on an outer layer, and roughly 2.6x those widths on an inner layer.

**Voltage drop.** Budget it separately. 10 A through 0.024 Ω (1 mm wide, 50 mm long, 1 oz) drops 0.24 V and dissipates 2.4 W. The same run as a 6 mm trace drops 40 mV and dissipates 0.4 W.

**Rules that follow.**
1. Short, direct, wide; use pours or planes for the supply and for the return.
2. Prefer outer layers, or parallel the same net on several layers with many vias.
3. Vias: roughly 1 A per 0.3 mm via is a conservative planning number; use at least twice the count the current needs and spread them so current does not crowd through one.
4. No necks: keep the full width up to the pad; round or chamfer corners.
5. Solid connection on power pads; thermal relief spokes only where soldering needs it, and widened.
6. Return path with equal care; keep high-current return away from the analog reference, joined at one point.
7. Derate, check creepage clearance, and verify with a thermal camera.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> IPC-2221 charts are conservative for outer layers and generous for inner ones. IPC-2152 (which replaced the older data) shows the result depends on plane copper nearby, board thickness and airflow, so treat any width as a starting point and verify on hardware.
:::

::: long
"I start by remembering that a trace is just a resistor, and for one ounce copper that's about half a milliohm per square. That gives me two budgets to check: heating and voltage drop. For heating I use the IPC-2221 or IPC-2152 charts. Say I accept a 20 degree rise on an outer layer: that's about 1.8 mm for 5 A and 4.7 mm for 10 A, and an inner layer needs roughly two and a half times as much. For voltage drop I just count squares. A 50 mm long, 1 mm wide trace is 50 squares, so 24 milliohms, which at 10 A is a quarter of a volt and two and a half watts, so that fails even though a thin trace might survive the heat.

From there the rules follow. Make it short and wide, and prefer a pour or a plane, because it spreads the current over a huge area. Use outer layers because they shed heat better, or parallel the net on several layers.

Then I look for weak links, because the failure is rarely the middle of the trace. Vias: when current changes layer I use an array, and I plan about an amp per small via with margin. Pads: I don't neck the trace down, and on power pads I use a direct connection or wide thermal-relief spokes. Connectors: I count pins against their rating.

Finally the return path. The current has to get back, so the return needs the same width, and I keep that loop small, both for inductance and so the high-current return doesn't pollute the analog reference. If it's still tight, I go to two ounce copper, add solder or a bus bar, and then verify on the first build with a thermal camera."
:::

::: terms
- **Copper weight (oz)**: copper thickness; 1 oz is about 35 µm (1.4 mil), 2 oz about 70 µm.
- **Sheet resistance (Ω/sq)**: resistance of a square of the conductor of any size; $\rho/t$.
- **Temperature rise ($\Delta T$)**: how far the trace gets above ambient for a given current.
- **IPC-2221 / IPC-2152**: the PCB design standard with the classic width-current charts, and the later standard with measured data.
- **Current crowding**: current bunching into a corner, via or neck so local density and heating are much higher than the average.
- **Via array**: several vias in parallel for the same net.
- **Thermal relief**: spoked connection of a pad to a plane that eases soldering but adds resistance. See [[q0538]].
- **IR drop**: the voltage $I\cdot R$ lost in the wiring.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "make the traces wide" and maybe mentions the IPC chart.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Uses squares and gives a number (0.5 mΩ/sq for 1 oz) so the width choice is quantified.
- Separates the **temperature** limit from the **voltage-drop** limit and notes that drop often decides the width.
- Names the weak links: vias, pad necks, thermal reliefs, connector pins.
- Knows outer layers carry more than inner and that copper resistance rises with temperature.
- Mentions the return path, loop inductance, and keeping the high-current return away from analog reference.
- Closes the loop: thermal-camera check, derating, and what happens at a fault (the trace as an unintended fuse, [[q0544]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Sizing only for temperature rise.** A trace that stays cool can still drop 300 mV and starve the load. Check $I\cdot R$ against the load tolerance too.
- **Using one via for the whole current.** A 0.3 mm via is a copper tube with a resistance near 1 mΩ and a limited current rating. Use an array.
- **Using the external-layer chart for an inner layer.** Inner layers cool worse, so the same current needs a much wider trace.
- **Necking the trace to fit a pad or a footprint.** The narrow bit runs hot and is where it fails. Widen up to the pad or use a pour.
- **Forgetting the return.** A beautifully wide supply trace with a 0.5 mm return squeezed through a gap is still a bottleneck, and the loop is large.
- **Believing "wider is always better".** For switching nodes and sense lines you also care about capacitive coupling and antenna area. See [[q0537]].
:::

::: deep
## Think in squares

The most useful habit is to stop thinking about a trace as a line and start thinking about it as a number of squares of copper in series. A square of copper has the same resistance whether it is 1 mm on a side or 100 mm, because the length-to-width ratio is all that counts:

$$R = \rho\,\frac{L}{wt} = R_\square\cdot\frac{L}{w}, \qquad R_\square = \frac{\rho}{t}$$

For 1 oz copper $R_\square = 1.68\times10^{-8}/35\times10^{-6} = 0.48$ mΩ. For 2 oz it halves to 0.24 mΩ. So a 1 mm wide, 50 mm long trace is 50 squares, 24 mΩ. At 10 A that is 0.24 V and 2.4 W concentrated in a tiny area, which is why it would cook. Widen it to 6 mm and it is about 8.3 squares, 4 mΩ, 40 mV and 0.4 W.

This also explains why a pour is so effective. A pour that is 20 mm wide and 30 mm long is only 1.5 squares, 0.72 mΩ, and the same current is spread over 20 mm of copper instead of 1 mm. See [[q0535]].

## Two budgets: heat and drop

There are two independent limits.

**Temperature.** Heat generated is $I^2R$ and is removed by conduction into the board and by convection and radiation from the surface. IPC-2221 condenses data into

$$I = k\,\Delta T^{0.44}A^{0.725}$$

with $A$ in mil² and $k = 0.048$ (outer) or $0.024$ (inner). The exponents less than 1 tell you something physical: doubling the cross-section less than doubles the current allowed, because a wider trace has a bigger area to shed heat but the current per unit width rises.

**Voltage drop.** The load sees $V_{supply} - I(R_{out}+R_{ret})$. For a 1 V core rail at 10 A, a 20 mm run of 1 mm trace each way costs $2\times 9.6$ mΩ$\times 10$ A = 0.19 V, 19% of the rail. The trace would survive the heat but the processor would not run. Drop usually wins for low-voltage rails, and heat wins for high-voltage rails where a 200 mV drop is irrelevant.

## The weak links

**Vias.** A plated through-hole via is a copper tube. For a 0.3 mm drill, 25 µm plating and a 1.6 mm board its resistance is

$$R_{via} = \rho\frac{h}{\pi d\,t_p} = \frac{1.68\times10^{-8}\cdot1.6\times10^{-3}}{\pi\cdot0.3\times10^{-3}\cdot25\times10^{-6}} \approx 1.1\ \text{m}\Omega$$

That is small in resistance but it has a modest current rating (planning figures of 1 A per via are common, with more for larger drills), and it adds about 1.3 nH of inductance (Johnson's approximation). So a 10 A path that hops layers wants at least 10 to 20 vias, spread out so each carries its share. One via in the middle of a pour forces all current to crowd in from the edges.

**Pads and necks.** If a 5 mm wide pour meets a 0.5 mm wide pad entry, the narrow bit is a 10x constriction. Current crowds, local density can be hundreds of A/mm², and that spot is where it fails. Keep the width right up to the pad, or fan out into the pour.

**Thermal reliefs.** A four-spoke relief with 0.25 mm × 0.25 mm spokes has about 0.12 mΩ and 18 K/W thermal resistance in parallel, which is fine for a signal pin and bad for the source of a power FET. See [[q0538]].

**Connectors.** A 2.54 mm header pin might be rated 1 to 3 A; a 30 A feed on a 4-pin connector is a design error no matter how good the copper is.

## Current density at a glance

10 A in a 1 mm, 1 oz trace is 286 A/mm². That is not a typo; it is why that trace is a fuse. The same current in a 10 mm pour is under 30 A/mm². Heat per unit volume scales with $J^2$, so doubling the width cuts the heating density by four.

## Return paths and loops

Current is a loop. The return conductor needs the same width and the same via count. Beyond resistance, the loop area sets inductance and radiated emission, so for anything that switches the return should sit directly underneath the supply on the adjacent layer ([[q0486]]). Do not join a noisy high-current return into an analog ground in the middle of a sensitive circuit; bring them together at a single point ([[q0495]]).

## Practical workflow

1. Write the current budget per net, including peaks and fault current.
2. Pick copper weight and layer count, preferring pours on outer layers.
3. Compute width from IPC and drop from squares; take the larger.
4. Draw polygons, not routes, where possible; add via arrays; widen necks.
5. Run a DRC-style check of current paths in the CAD tool if it has one (many have a "power net" or current density viewer; a field solver is better for planes).
6. First article: apply rated current, thermal camera, measure drop with a 4-wire (Kelvin) measurement across the path.

::: applications
- **Motor driver boards**: a 20 A H-bridge uses 2 oz copper on both outer layers, via arrays at every layer change and a dedicated power return.
- **Battery packs and BMS boards**: the main pack path is a stitched polygon with heavy copper and often a bus bar on top of solder, because a few milliohms means watts at pack current.
- **Point-of-load regulators**: output polygons feeding FPGA or processor cores, where the voltage-drop budget (tens of mV) sets the width more than temperature does.
- **USB-C power input at 3 to 5 A**: input traces and connector pads sized so the plug's contact resistance, not the board, is the weak point.
- **LED lighting and automotive boards**: heavy copper and many vias to the back side for current and for heat spreading.
:::

::: assumptions
The width numbers here assume 1 oz copper on FR-4 in still air at room temperature, a straight uniform trace, DC or low-frequency current (at tens of MHz the skin effect starts pushing current to the edges, [[q0056]]), and steady state rather than short pulses. The answer changes when: the trace carries a short pulse, so thermal mass matters and a thin trace survives far more than its continuous rating; the board has forced air or a heat spreader; there are large copper planes on adjacent layers (IPC-2152 shows these help a lot); or the board runs in a hot enclosure, in which case ambient adds to your $\Delta T$ and you need to size against the absolute copper limit (often around 105 to 130 °C for FR-4, depending on the laminate Tg). Alternatives to just widening copper include bus bars, heavier copper, parallel layers, and jumper links.
:::

::: followups
### What width would you use for 10 A on the outer layer, and how would you check it?
For 1 oz copper and a 20 °C rise, IPC-2221 gives about 4.7 mm, and I would take 6 mm or a pour for margin. At 6 mm, 50 mm long, the resistance is about 4 mΩ, so 40 mV and 0.4 W, which I'd confirm against the voltage-drop budget. Then I measure it with a 4-wire probe across the finished path and look at it with a thermal camera at rated current.

### Why is an inner layer worse than an outer layer for current?
An inner trace is surrounded by dielectric, which conducts heat poorly, and it cannot radiate or convect into air. Outer traces lose heat from their exposed face. IPC-2221 halves the $k$ factor for inner layers, so you need about 2.6x the width for the same rise. Planes on adjacent layers improve inner-layer performance, which is why IPC-2152 is less pessimistic.

### How many vias do I need to carry 15 A between layers?
Using a conservative 1 A per 0.3 mm via, 15 vias would be the floor; I would use 25 to 30 and cluster them across the whole width of the pour so each takes a share. Also remember all those vias add inductance in parallel, which is a bonus: 30 vias at 1.3 nH each is about 43 pH.

### What if the board is only briefly pulsed at high current?
Then the trace does not reach steady state. The heat capacity of the copper limits the rise, so a short pulse can be far above the DC rating. The Onderdonk equation gives the adiabatic fusing current; 1 mm × 35 µm copper fuses at about 10 A in 1 s and about 32 A in 0.1 s. I would still design for the worst-case continuous fault, since a stuck-on load is not short.

### A coworker routes the high-current return as a thin trace and says the plane will take care of it. Is that fine?
Only if the plane really is the return and is continuous under the supply. A thin trace in the return leg forms a bottleneck with high resistance and inductance and drops voltage at the load's ground, which is the reference for everything else. I would make the return a pour or plane and bring sensitive grounds to it at one point.
:::

"High-current" is not defined in the question. ::: rubric
- Treats copper as a resistor and quantifies it (about 0.5 mΩ/sq for 1 oz, or sizes by squares) :: A number makes the answer checkable rather than a slogan.
- Sizes for both temperature rise (IPC chart) and voltage drop :: Drop often needs more copper than heating does, and missing it causes brownouts.
- Prefers short, wide paths and pours/planes over traces :: Lowers resistance, heating and inductance at once.
- Notes outer vs inner layer capacity and copper weight :: Shows you know the chart is not one number.
- Uses via arrays and avoids necks and thermal-relief bottlenecks on power pads :: The weak links, not the straight trace, are where boards actually fail.
- Treats the return path as carefully as the supply :: Current is a loop, and the return sets drop, inductance and noise.
- Mentions verification (thermal camera, 4-wire drop measurement) and derating :: Closes the loop from calculation to hardware.
:::
