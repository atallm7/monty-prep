::: testing
Whether you can explain the **mechanism**, not just "it steps": how a stepping *sequence* of coil currents moves a rotor's equilibrium, why it works open loop, and why the torque falls with speed. The interviewer wants to hear:

1. The attraction picture: energise a coil, the rotor aligns; energise the next, it moves to the next.
2. The drive sequences: wave drive, full-step (two phases on), half-step, microstepping.
3. The role of the driver's current control (chopping) and why supply voltage must be much higher than $IR$.
4. The speed limits: inductance, back-EMF, resonance, pull-in/pull-out.
:::

::: why
If you understand how the field walks the rotor from tooth to tooth, you can answer almost every stepper follow-up: why microstepping gives smoother motion but not proportional accuracy, why a 24 V supply drives a 3 V motor, why 3D-printer motors lose steps when you accelerate too hard, why the motor gets hot while standing still. It is also a good test of whether you see a stepper as a high-pole-count synchronous motor driven open loop.
:::

::: concepts
- **Magnetic alignment**: a rotor with a magnet or teeth seeks the minimum-energy position relative to the stator field. See [[q0376]].
- **Two-phase driving**: two independent windings, currents $I_A, I_B$ set the field direction. 
- **Electrical vs mechanical angle**: one electrical cycle moves one tooth pitch (7.2° for 50 teeth). See [[q0367]].
- **Inductive current rise**: $\tau = L/R$ limits how fast current can build. See [[q0334]].
- **Current chopping**: the driver PWMs to regulate winding current. See [[q0380]].
- **Back-EMF**: a moving rotor induces a voltage that opposes the drive at speed. See [[q0341]].
:::

::: answer
"A stepper motor works by moving a magnetic field in small jumps and letting the rotor chase it. The stator has coils arranged in phases; the rotor is a permanent magnet or a toothed iron piece. If I energise one coil, it creates a magnetic pole and the rotor rotates until its nearest tooth or opposite pole lines up with it, and it stays there. If I then switch off that coil and energise the next one, the equilibrium position moves by one step, and the rotor turns to it. Repeating the sequence A, B, A-bar, B-bar rotates the shaft one step at a time; reversing the sequence reverses direction.

In a typical hybrid motor with two phases and 50 rotor teeth, one full cycle of the sequence moves the rotor one tooth pitch, 7.2 degrees, in four 1.8 degree steps. Energising both phases at once, full-step, gives the most torque. Alternating one then two phases gives half steps, and driving the two phase currents as a sine and a cosine, microstepping, lets the rotor settle between teeth for smoother motion.

Position control is open loop: I count the pulses. That works as long as the motor produces enough torque to follow. The torque falls with speed because the coil inductance slows the current rise, and back-EMF grows with speed, which is why drivers use a high supply voltage with current chopping, so the current can reach its rated value quickly. If the load or acceleration exceeds the available torque, the rotor falls out of step and loses position, with no warning."
:::

::: technical
**Torque production.** With coil A energised at its rated current, the rotor's torque versus displacement $\Delta\theta$ from the equilibrium is approximately sinusoidal in the electrical angle:

$$T \approx -T_h\sin\!\big(N_r\,\Delta\theta\big)$$

where $T_h$ is the holding torque and $N_r=50$ teeth (so the electrical angle is $50\,\Delta\theta_{mech}$). One full step is a $90^\circ$ electrical shift of the target. With the two phases set to $I_A=I\cos\varphi,\ I_B=I\sin\varphi$, the equilibrium sits at electrical angle $\varphi$, so microstepping can place it anywhere.

**Sequences (two-phase).**

| Mode | Phase states per electrical cycle | Step | Torque |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wave drive (one phase on) | 4: A, B, A', B' | 1.8° | lower: one coil used |
| Full-step (two phases on) | 4: AB, A'B, A'B', AB' | 1.8° | highest, ×$\sqrt2$ vs wave |
| Half-step | 8: alternating one/two phases | 0.9° | torque alternates (about 30% dip on one-phase steps unless current is boosted) |
| Microstep ($n$ per full step) | sine-cosine currents | $1.8^\circ/n$ | smooth; incremental torque per microstep falls |

**Torque vs microstep.** With both phases at rated current (classic full-step) the torque is $\sqrt2$ times the single-phase value; microstepping uses $I_A^2+I_B^2=I^2$ so torque is constant in magnitude, and the incremental torque to move one microstep is $T_h\sin(90^\circ/n)$: about 9.8% of $T_h$ for $n=16$ (see [[q0382]]).

**Electrical dynamics.** Each winding: $V = IR + L\,di/dt + e$, with $e = K_e\omega$ the back-EMF. Time to rise to $I$ from a supply $V_s$: $t = -\tau\ln(1 - IR/V_s)$ with $\tau=L/R$. For a motor with $R=1.5\ \Omega$, $L=2.8$ mH, $I=1.7$ A: at 24 V it takes 210 µs, at 12 V 446 µs, and at just the rated 2.55 V it never gets there (it asymptotes). At 1000 rpm (3333 full steps/s) the step period is 300 µs, so the current needs to rise in a fraction of that, hence high supply voltage and chopping.

**Speed limits.** Torque falls with step rate because (i) the current cannot reach its full value in a short step period and (ii) back-EMF subtracts from the available voltage. The torque-speed curve has a *pull-in* (start/stop without ramp) region and a larger *pull-out* region (reachable only by accelerating gradually). Resonance appears near the rotor's natural frequency, typically tens to a couple of hundred hertz of step rate for NEMA 17 motors.
:::

::: long
"A stepper works by moving a magnetic field in discrete jumps and letting the rotor follow. The stator has two phases of coils; the rotor is either a permanent magnet, toothed iron, or both in a hybrid. When I energise one coil, it creates a pole that attracts the nearest rotor tooth or magnet pole, and the rotor settles into a stable position. That's an equilibrium: if I push it away, the magnetic torque pulls it back. Then I turn that coil off and energise the next one, which is offset by a quarter of a tooth pitch, so the equilibrium moves one step and the rotor turns to it.

In a typical hybrid motor with 50 teeth and two phases, the current pattern repeats every four steps, and one pattern repeat moves the rotor one tooth pitch, which is 7.2 degrees, so each step is 1.8. There are a few ways to drive it: wave drive, one coil at a time; full step, two coils at a time, which gives about 40% more torque; half step, alternating one and two coils for 0.9 degrees; and microstepping, where I drive the two coil currents as a sine and cosine so the equilibrium sits between the full-step positions. That gives smoother motion and finer resolution.

The control is open loop. I count steps and set speed with the pulse rate, and it works as long as the motor has enough torque to keep up with the rotating field. The limit is electrical: each coil is an inductor, so the current rise time is L over R, and back-EMF grows with speed. To get full current within a short step period, drivers use a supply much higher than the rated voltage, say 24 V for a 3 V motor, and chop the current with PWM to hold it at the setpoint. Even so, torque falls with speed, and if the load or acceleration is too high, the rotor loses sync and skips steps. There's also a resonance at low speed that you handle with microstepping or damping."
:::

::: terms
- **Equilibrium (detent) position**: rotor angle where torque is zero and restoring.
- **Full-step / half-step / microstep**: drive modes giving 1.8°, 0.9°, and finer increments.
- **Wave drive**: energising one phase at a time.
- **Holding torque**: peak restoring torque with rated current.
- **Pull-in / pull-out torque**: max torque at which the motor can start or stay in sync at a given speed.
- **Chopper drive**: PWM current regulation from a high bus.
- **Resonance**: oscillation at the rotor's natural frequency, causing lost steps.
- **Back-EMF**: speed-proportional voltage induced in the windings.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "coils are energised in sequence and the rotor follows". An exceptional answer:

- Explains the equilibrium: each state has a stable position with a restoring torque $T\approx -T_h\sin(N_r\Delta\theta)$.
- Computes the geometry: 50 teeth, 7.2° per electrical cycle, 1.8° per full step.
- Distinguishes wave, full, half and microstep drive and what each does to torque.
- Gives the electrical reason for torque falling with speed, with a rise-time number ($L/R$, supply voltage).
- Mentions resonance and open-loop failure (missed steps) and how drivers mitigate them.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The rotor spins because the field rotates smoothly."** At low step rates the rotor jumps and rings; it is only smooth at speed or with microstepping.
- **"Microstepping gives proportional accuracy."** Resolution rises, but absolute accuracy stays about ±5% of a full step because of tooth and magnetic imperfections, and incremental torque per microstep gets small.
- **"Just apply the rated voltage."** Rated voltage is $IR$; at that voltage current rises too slowly at speed. Use a chopper driver at a much higher supply.
- **"Half the steps, half the torque."** Torque with two phases on is $\sqrt2$ greater than one phase; half-stepping alternates between those, so torque varies.
- **"Steppers can't run fast."** They can, with proper drive and acceleration ramps, but torque falls steeply.
- **Starting at high speed directly.** Beyond the pull-in rate the rotor can't lock on; ramp the step rate.
:::

## Microstepping: currents as sine and cosine

Figure: With full-step drive the two currents are square waves. Here they are drawn at 0.707 of the peak, as a microstepping driver sets them at its full-step positions, so the vector magnitude is 1 like the microstep case; a classic full-step driver puts rated current in both phases, giving a $\sqrt2$ larger vector and torque. With microstepping the currents follow cos and sin, and the field vector rotates smoothly; the equilibrium sits at electrical angle $\varphi=\arctan(I_B/I_A)$. 16 microsteps per full step move the rotor $1.8^\circ/16 = 0.1125^\circ$ per microstep.

Microstep current ratios (8 microsteps per full step) are $\sin(k\pi/16)$ and $\cos(k\pi/16)$ for $k=0\ldots8$: 0, 0.195, 0.383, 0.556, 0.707, 0.831, 0.924, 0.981, 1.0. The torque magnitude stays constant because $\sin^2+\cos^2=1$; the restoring torque for a one-microstep displacement is $T_h\sin(90^\circ/n)$, so $n=16$ gives about 9.8% of $T_h$, and $n=32$ about 4.9%. This is why very fine microsteps do not give proportionally more accuracy: friction and detent torque can exceed that small restoring torque.

## Why torque falls with speed

Each winding is an inductor in series with a resistor. Applying voltage $V_s$ makes the current rise as $i(t)=\frac{V_s}{R}(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ with $\tau=L/R$.

Worked numbers for a NEMA 17-class motor: $R=1.5\ \Omega$, $L=2.8$ mH, rated $I=1.7$ A (rated voltage $IR=2.55$ V), so $\tau=1.87$ ms.

| Supply | Time to reach 1.7 A |
|---|---|
| 3.05 V (just above rated) | 3.4 ms |
| 12 V | 0.45 ms |
| 24 V | 0.21 ms |

At 1000 rpm and 200 steps/rev the step rate is 3333 steps/s (300 µs per step), so with a 12 V supply the current barely reaches its target before the next step; with 24 V it does in 210 µs and chopping holds it. In addition, the winding's reactance at the electrical frequency $f_e = f_{step}/4=833$ Hz is $X_L = 2\pi f_e L = 14.7\ \Omega$, ten times $R$, so the current achievable from a 24 V supply is limited to about $V/\sqrt{R^2+X_L^2} \approx 1.63$ A even before back-EMF. Back-EMF (proportional to speed) takes further voltage, so available torque falls with speed.

## Open-loop behaviour: pull-in, pull-out, resonance

- **Pull-in:** the highest rate at which the motor can start and stop without acceleration ramps, with a given load. Low (a few hundred to a thousand steps/s).
- **Pull-out:** the highest rate the motor can sustain once running, which is larger; it needs an acceleration ramp to reach. Below the pull-out curve the rotor stays synchronised.
- **Resonance:** the rotor, after each step, rings like a damped torsional spring with natural frequency $f_n=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{k/J}$; when step rate approaches $f_n$ (or sub-harmonics) the oscillation grows and steps are lost. Remedies: microstepping, avoiding that speed range, mechanical damping, or driver anti-resonance algorithms.

## Putting it together

1. Pulses (STEP) and direction (DIR) tell the driver which way and how far.
2. The driver's sequencer (translator) steps through the current table.
3. For each winding, the chopper regulates current to the table value, using the high supply voltage.
4. The rotor follows the equilibrium; the shaft turns $1.8^\circ/n$ per microstep, as long as torque suffices.

::: followups
### Why is a 24 V supply used for a motor rated at 2.55 V?
The rated voltage is just $IR$ in steady state. To get the current to rise quickly in a short step period, the driver uses a much higher supply voltage and chops to regulate current; at 24 V the 1.7 A is reached in 210 µs versus about 3.4 ms at 3 V. The chopper keeps the average voltage across the motor at the right level.

### What is the difference between full-step and half-step torque?
Full-step energises both phases, so torque vector magnitude is $\sqrt2$ times that of one phase. Half-step alternates one-phase and two-phase states, so torque varies by about 30% unless the driver raises the current in the one-phase states. Microstepping keeps the magnitude constant.

### Why does a stepper get hot when stationary?
The driver holds full rated current in the windings to keep the holding torque, so it dissipates $I^2R$ in both phases: $2\cdot1.7^2\cdot1.5=8.7$ W for the example motor, with no mechanical output. Drivers offer current reduction at standstill to cut this.

### What is resonance and how do you fight it?
After a step the rotor oscillates at its natural frequency, and if the step rate matches it the amplitude grows and torque drops, possibly stalling. Use microstepping, accelerate through the resonant range, add a damper or inertia, or use a driver with anti-resonance or mid-band damping.

### Scenario: your stepper stalls at 600 rpm but runs fine at 300. What do you check?
Torque available at 600 rpm (step rate 2000/s for 200 steps/rev): check the pull-out curve for your motor and supply voltage, whether current reaches the setpoint (supply voltage too low, chopping decay mode), acceleration too aggressive, resonance near that speed, and mechanical friction. Raising supply voltage, using a different current-decay mode or lowering acceleration usually helps.
:::
