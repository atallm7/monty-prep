::: testing
Whether you can **define monotonic precisely** (an edge that passes through the receiver's threshold window once and never reverses), recognise how it fails on a scope (ringback, ledges/stair-steps, notches), and tie it to causes (impedance mismatch, stubs, crosstalk, ground bounce). A good candidate says it is judged **against the logic thresholds** at the receiver, not against the final level, and that it is a property of the transition, not of the settled levels. Distinguishing "ringing that stays above $V_{IH}$" (acceptable) from "ringback into the threshold window" (a violation) is the key insight.
:::

::: why
Receivers and clock inputs assume that when a signal crosses the threshold it stays crossed. A non-monotonic edge breaks that assumption and shows up as double clocking, extra counter pulses, or an erratic state machine that only fails on certain boards. Many logic and memory specs include ringback or "monotonic through the threshold region" requirements, and SI simulation reports list "non-monotonic edge" as a violation. [[q0691]] asks why it matters; this page focuses on **what it is**, how it appears and how to check it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Logic thresholds**: $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ define a window where the receiver's output is not guaranteed. See [[q0687]] and [[q0692]].
- **Reflections**: reflection coefficient $\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$ at source and load. See [[q0713]].
- **Lattice (bounce) diagram**: the way multiple reflections sum into the receiver waveform. See [[q0711]].
- **Underdamped response**: lumped $L$ and $C$ ring; overshoot depends on damping ratio ([[q0696]]).
- **Termination**: matching source or load removes the reflections. See [[q0714]].
:::

::: answer
"A signal edge is monotonic if it moves in one direction only while it passes through the receiver's threshold region. A rising edge that goes from low to high without dipping back down on the way is monotonic. A non-monotonic edge rises, then falls back, then rises again; for example ringing or a reflection that pulls the voltage back into the region between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$.

What matters is where that reversal happens. Overshoot above the final value is fine as long as it stays well above $V_{IH}$. But if the ringback dips back into or below the threshold window, the receiver can see multiple edges. It's usually caused by an impedance mismatch, like a low-impedance driver into an open line, or by stubs and branches on the net, or by noise like crosstalk or ground bounce hitting at the wrong moment.

I'd check it by looking at the edge at the receiver on a scope, zoomed into the threshold window, and I'd fix it with proper series or parallel termination, shorter stubs and a cleaner return path."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** For a rising transition, let $v(t)$ be the voltage at the receiver pin. The edge is *monotonic through the threshold window* if $dv/dt \ge 0$ for all $t$ such that $V_{IL} \le v(t) \le V_{IH}$ (and symmetric for falling). Equivalently: $v$ passes $V_{IL}$ once and $V_{IH}$ once and never returns below $V_{IH}$ after first reaching it (the ringback must stay above $V_{IH}$, or for falling edges below $V_{IL}$).

**Cause: reflections from a mismatched source.** An open-ended line driven by a source $R_s$ gives $v_{inc} = V\,Z_0/(R_s+Z_0)$, doubled at the open load, and then reflected at the source with $\Gamma_s = (R_s - Z_0)/(R_s+Z_0)$. For $Z_0 = 50\ \Omega$, $V = 3.3$ V (idealized, no clamps):

| $R_s$ | $\Gamma_s$ | Receiver voltage after each round trip |
|---|---|---|
| 50 Ω | 0 | 3.30 V, clean |
| 100 Ω | +0.33 | 2.20, 2.93, 3.18, 3.26, ... staircase, always rising (monotonic, but with a plateau) |
| 10 Ω | −0.67 | 5.50, **1.83**, 4.28, 2.65, ... reverses at every step |
| 5 Ω | −0.82 | 6.00, **1.09**, 5.11, 1.82, ... deep ringback |

When $R_s < Z_0$, $\Gamma_s$ is negative and the returning wave subtracts, so the voltage swings above and below the final value. When $R_s > Z_0$ the staircase never reverses but stalls on plateaus that may sit between thresholds.

**Other causes.** Stubs and T-branches (a reflection from a stub arrives at a delay set by stub length); a lumped $L$-$C$ ring ([[q0696]]); crosstalk or ground bounce injecting a notch in the middle of an edge ([[q0694]], [[q0688]]); via stubs and connector discontinuities; and probing artifacts.

**Fixes.** Series termination ($R_{drv}+R_{series} \approx Z_0$) removes the source reflection; parallel/Thevenin termination removes the load reflection; shorten stubs; lower return-path inductance; slow the edge only as a last resort ([[q0689]]).
:::

::: short
"Monotonic means the edge goes one way only through the receiver's threshold region, with no reversal. A rising edge shouldn't dip back toward $V_{IL}$ after it passes $V_{IH}$. Overshoot is fine if it stays high, but ringback into the threshold window means the receiver can see multiple edges. It comes from impedance mismatches, stubs, or noise like crosstalk, and I check it at the receiver pin and fix it with termination and layout."
:::

::: long
"Signal monotonicity is about the shape of the transition at the receiver. An edge is monotonic if, while the voltage crosses the receiver's uncertain region between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$, it only moves in one direction. For a rising edge, once it has crossed up through that window it never comes back down into it.

A concrete way to see it: a 3.3 volt driver with 10 ohms of output impedance driving an open 50 ohm line. The first incident wave is 2.75 volts, the open end doubles that to 5.5 at the receiver, but then the reflection comes back to the source, which has a negative reflection coefficient of about minus 0.67, so a negative wave goes back out and brings the receiver to 1.83 volts. That is below $V_{IH}$ of 2.0, so it's a ringback into the threshold window. Then it swings up again to 4.3. That's non-monotonic.

If the driver is instead 100 ohms, the reflection coefficient is positive, so the receiver goes 2.2, 2.9, 3.2 and so on, a staircase. It's technically monotonic but can have a plateau right where a threshold is, which delays or destabilises the decision.

The causes are impedance mismatch, stubs, lumped LC ringing, or noise events like crosstalk and ground bounce. I'd check by probing at the receiver with a short ground and zooming in around the threshold window, and I'd fix it with series termination near the driver, shorter stubs and a clean return path."
:::

::: terms
- **Monotonic edge**: a transition whose voltage moves in one direction through the receiver's threshold window.
- **Ringback**: the dip back toward the starting level after overshoot.
- **Threshold window**: the range between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ where the receiver's output isn't guaranteed.
- **Ledge / plateau**: a stalled part of an edge, often from a partial reflection, typically between thresholds.
- **Reflection coefficient ($\Gamma$)**: $(Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)$; sign and size set the swing of reflections.
- **Lattice diagram**: bookkeeping of waves bouncing between source and load.
- **Series termination**: a resistor near the driver so that $R_{drv} + R_s = Z_0$.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "the signal only goes up or down without wiggles". An exceptional answer **ties monotonicity to the receiver's threshold window** (ringing that stays above $V_{IH}$ is acceptable), gives the reflection-coefficient reason (negative $\Gamma_s$ for $R_s < Z_0$ leads to overshoot and ringback, positive $\Gamma_s$ gives a staircase) with a number, mentions stubs and noise as other causes, and describes **how to verify** (probe at the receiver, zoom on the threshold window, check ringback margin against $V_{IH}$/$V_{IL}$). It also notes that monotonic isn't the same as "clean": a long plateau near threshold can be monotonic and still a timing problem.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Monotonic means no ringing at all."** Overshoot and ringing that stay beyond the threshold window are fine; what matters is not re-entering the window.
- **Judging monotonicity at the driver.** The receiver sees the sum of the incident and reflected waves; the driver-end waveform can look fine.
- **Confusing "monotonic edge" with "monotonic DAC".** In a DAC/ADC context monotonic means the output never decreases when the code increases. Different concept ([[q0691]] touches on the difference).
- **Ignoring plateaus.** A stair-step edge that never reverses but stalls near $V_{IL}$ or $V_{IH}$ is "monotonic" but can still cause late or erratic sampling.
- **Fixing with slow edges only.** Slowing the edge hides ringing but costs timing margin and may only move it; fix the impedance mismatch.
- **Using a long ground lead.** The probe can *create* a ring that looks like non-monotonic behaviour.
:::

::: deep
## A simple definition you can check on a scope

Look at one rising edge arriving at a receiver. The receiver has two thresholds, $V_{IL}$ (below which it guarantees a low) and $V_{IH}$ (above which it guarantees a high). Between them, nothing is guaranteed: different units, temperatures and noise can read 0 or 1. An edge is **monotonic through the threshold window** if it enters that band once, leaves it once, and never re-enters. In practice you place the cursors at $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ on the scope and look for any ringback that touches them.

## Where non-monotonic edges come from

The lattice diagram gives the whole story for a lossless line. With source resistance $R_s$, line $Z_0$ and an open load ($\Gamma_L = +1$), the receiver voltage after $k$ round trips is a sum of steps, each equal to twice the previous reflected wave:

$$v_{inc} = V_s\,\frac{Z_0}{R_s+Z_0},\quad v_{rx}(k) = 2v_{inc}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\Gamma_s^{\,j}$$

For $R_s = 5\ \Omega$ (a strong driver with no series resistor), $\Gamma_s = -0.82$ and $v_{inc} = 3.0$ V:

- first arrival: $2\cdot 3.0 = 6.0$ V,
- after one round trip: $6.0 - 2\cdot 3.0\cdot 0.818 = 1.09$ V,
- next: 5.11 V, then 1.82 V, then 4.51 V,

and it converges to 3.3 V. The reversals are what make it non-monotonic.

## The staircase case: monotonic but still bad

If the receiver sits *mid-line* rather than at the end, a high-impedance driver ($R_s = 100\ \Omega$) shows a different artifact. The incident wave is only $3.3\cdot 50/150 = 1.1$ V. The receiver sees that 1.1 V until the load's reflection comes back, then jumps to 2.2 V:

Ledges like this on clock lines are often counted as signal-integrity violations as well.

## Other causes and a quick triage

| Symptom on the scope | Probable cause | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Ringing at 1/(4 t_d), amplitude set by $\Gamma_s$ | low $R_s$ into open/high-Z load | series termination |
| Ledge at ~half swing for $2t_d$ | $R_s > Z_0$ with receiver away from the end | lower the driver impedance or terminate at the load |
| Notch that moves with other signals | crosstalk or ground bounce ([[q0694]], [[q0688]]) | spacing, return path, slew control |
| Extra step after a fixed delay | stub or branch reflection | shorten the stub, daisy-chain properly |
| Ring at probe tip frequency, goes away with a different probe | measurement artifact | short ground spring |

## Checking monotonicity in simulation and on the bench

**In simulation.** Pre-layout tools drive the net with IBIS or SPICE driver models and report, per receiver, the minimum voltage after the first rising crossing of $V_{IH}$ (for rising edges) and the maximum voltage after the first falling crossing of $V_{IL}$. Their difference from the threshold is the *ringback margin*. Run it at fast and slow driver corners, because a fast corner has a lower output impedance and a more negative $\Gamma_s$. For the $R_s = 10\ \Omega$ driver above, the 1.83 V dip is the ringback; making the source resistance 20 Ω ($\Gamma_s = -0.43$) gives the lattice sequence 4.71, 2.69, 3.56, 3.19, 3.35 V, so the dip of 2.69 V stays above $V_{IH} = 2.0$ V and the edge is monotonic through the window.

**On the bench.** Probe the receiver pin (not the driver) with a short ground spring and trigger on the edge. Zoom the vertical scale so the 0.8–2.0 V window fills the screen and count crossings. A single-shot capture is not enough: repeat at low and high temperature and with neighbouring signals active, because ground bounce and crosstalk add notches in the middle of the edge only for certain data patterns.

**Real drivers and clamps.** The lattice numbers assume a linear driver and no clamp diodes. A real CMOS input clamps near $V_{DD} + 0.7$ V, so the first overshoot is truncated and the energy goes into the supply; the ringback dip is therefore usually *smaller* than the ideal lattice predicts, but the clamp current can stress the receiver and disturb the supply. Driver output impedance also changes during the edge (the transistor moves from saturation into the linear region), so the effective $R_s$ is not one number.

## Rules of thumb for checking

1. Draw the thresholds on the scope ($V_{IL}$, $V_{IH}$) and examine each edge at the receiver.
2. Require **ringback margin**: after the first overshoot, the voltage must not re-enter the window. For example, with $V_{IH} = 2.0$ V and a minimum post-overshoot value of 1.83 V, margin is $-0.17$ V (fail).
3. Check at worst-case process, voltage and temperature, since driver impedance shifts: a "50 Ω" driver can be 35 Ω to 65 Ω.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Standards such as DDR memory specs and logic-family datasheets define ringback and monotonicity requirements differently (some give a ringback limit relative to a reference voltage, others only a pulse-width or slew rule). Check the specific standard for your interface.
:::

::: applications
- **Clock nets**: SI reports flag non-monotonic clock edges because extra edges cause double-clocking.
- **DDR/LPDDR memory**: address/command and strobe signals have ringback and slew requirements around the reference threshold.
- **Reset and enable lines**: a glitchy reset can bounce a processor in and out of reset.
- **Long cables between boards (SPI, UART, parallel)**: series resistors at the driver restore monotonic edges.
- **Daisy-chained multi-drop buses**: stub length determines whether the ledge falls in the window.
- **Pre-layout simulation (IBIS)**: engineers check monotonicity and ringback at each receiver before releasing a board.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Lossless line and ideal step source** for the lattice numbers; loss, driver nonlinearity and clamp diodes will round and truncate the peaks. The *qualitative* result (negative $\Gamma_s$ gives reversals) holds.
- **Open-ended load** (CMOS input has very high impedance, a few pF); a capacitive load changes the details, producing a lumped LC ring.
- **Thresholds are static $V_{IL}$/$V_{IH}$**. For differential or reference-based receivers (DDR with $V_{REF}$), the equivalent test is relative to the reference and AC/DC thresholds.
- **"Monotonic" in the DAC/ADC sense** (output never decreases for increasing input code) is a separate definition; see interpretations.
:::

::: followups
### How does a Schmitt-trigger input change the monotonicity requirement?
Hysteresis widens the *allowed* ringback: once the input has crossed the upper threshold, it takes a fall below the lower threshold to flip back. If the ringback is smaller than the hysteresis window, the output sees one clean edge. It doesn't fix the underlying reflection but it relaxes the requirement ([[q0692]]).

### What is the ringback timing for a driver mismatch, and how would you estimate the ring frequency?
Round trip time is $2t_d$; with an open end and a low source, the receiver waveform changes polarity every $2t_d$, so the ring period is $4t_d$. A 1 ns line gives 4 ns, or 250 MHz. A 5-inch FR4 trace (about 850 ps) gives about 294 MHz.

### The edge is monotonic at the receiver but my clock still double-triggers. What else could be wrong?
Look at a noisy supply or ground bounce at the capturing chip, a threshold shifted by supply droop, a slow edge with noise riding on it ([[q0689]]), or crosstalk landing during the edge. Also check that the scope probe point matches the actual receiver pin (and for BGAs, that you're not probing at a via stub).

### How would you fix a non-monotonic edge on a 4-inch trace driven by a 10 Ω output?
Add series termination so $R_{drv} + R_s \approx Z_0$: with a 50 Ω line, about 40 Ω in series close to the driver (22–33 Ω if the driver is stronger than 10 Ω nominal and you want margin for process spread). Then the launched wave is $V/2$, the open load doubles it, and the source reflection is absorbed, giving a single clean step.

### When is a non-monotonic edge acceptable?
When the ringback doesn't reach the threshold window at the receiver in worst-case corners, or when the signal is sampled only after it has settled (slow data, enable signals) so the ringing is gone by the sampling instant. It's never acceptable on clock, reset, or edge-triggered inputs.
:::

::: interpretations
"Signal monotonicity" in the SI context means the edge shape, as above. In **converter** contexts, a DAC is *monotonic* if its output never decreases as the input code increases (guaranteed if DNL > −1 LSB), and an ADC is monotonic if the output code never decreases as the input rises. Those are different properties, so confirm the context if the interviewer's section isn't signal integrity.
:::
