::: testing
Whether you can combine two simple models: the **CMOS buffer as an ideal switched voltage source** (with a small on-resistance) and the **RC step response**. The interviewer expects you to:

1. Draw the right waveforms: the buffer output as a clean square wave, the capacitor voltage as exponential charge/discharge, the current as a decaying exponential that changes sign.
2. Say which transistor conducts when: **PMOS on during the positive step** (sourcing current from $V_{DD}$ to charge the capacitor), **NMOS on during the negative step** (sinking current as the capacitor discharges), and neither carries steady-state current.
3. Quantify with $\tau=RC$, the initial current $V_{DD}/R$ and the energy $\tfrac12CV^2$ per edge.
:::

::: why
Every digital output driving a pin, trace or input capacitance through some resistance (a series termination, a filter, an LED driver with a cap) behaves this way. It is the standard first model for edge rates, power ([[q0110]]) and signal integrity. Understanding which FET carries which current direction also explains current-return paths, decoupling needs, and why a buffer can both source and sink.
:::

::: concepts
- **CMOS buffer / inverter pair**: PMOS pulls up, NMOS pulls down, only one conducts in steady state. See [[q0105]].
- **RC step response**: $v_C(t)=V(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ charging, $Ve^{-t/\tau}$ discharging. See [[q0028]].
- **Current in a capacitor**: $i=C\,dv/dt$, so the current is largest at the edge and decays to zero.
- **Transistor on-resistance**: $R_{on}$ adds to the series resistance: $\tau=(R+R_{on})C$. See [[q0099]].
- **Source vs sink**: positive current out of the buffer is sourced by the PMOS; negative current is sunk by the NMOS.
- **Energy per edge**: $\tfrac12CV^2$ is dissipated in the resistances each edge.
:::

::: answer
"Model the buffer as a switch that connects its output to $V_{DD}$ or to ground, with a small on-resistance. The input to the RC is therefore an ideal square wave between 0 and $V_{DD}$. On a positive step the output goes to $V_{DD}$: the PMOS is on and the NMOS is off. The capacitor is initially at zero, so the full $V_{DD}$ appears across $R$ and the current jumps to $V_{DD}/R$ flowing from the supply through the PMOS and the resistor into the capacitor. Then it decays exponentially with time constant $RC$ while the capacitor voltage rises as $V_{DD}(1-e^{-t/RC})$ towards $V_{DD}$. After about five time constants the current is essentially zero.

On the negative step, the output switches to ground: the NMOS turns on and the PMOS turns off. The capacitor is still at $V_{DD}$, so the current flips sign, $-V_{DD}/R$, now flowing out of the capacitor through the resistor and into the NMOS to ground. The capacitor voltage decays as $V_{DD}e^{-t/RC}$ and the current decays to zero.

So the output waveform is a square wave, the capacitor voltage is a rounded square wave, and the current is a train of alternating-sign spikes that decay exponentially. In steady state with the output held, neither transistor carries current. That's why CMOS only draws power at the edges."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** Buffer output $V_o(t)\in\{0,V_{DD}\}$ with on-resistance $R_p$ (PMOS) or $R_n$ (NMOS) in series with the external $R$; load $C$ to ground.

**Positive step ($V_o:0\to V_{DD}$, PMOS on):**
$$v_C(t)=V_{DD}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau_p}\right),\quad i(t)=\frac{V_{DD}}{R+R_p}e^{-t/\tau_p},\quad \tau_p=(R+R_p)C$$
**Negative step ($V_o:V_{DD}\to0$, NMOS on):**
$$v_C(t)=V_{DD}\,e^{-t/\tau_n},\quad i(t)=-\frac{V_{DD}}{R+R_n}e^{-t/\tau_n},\quad \tau_n=(R+R_n)C$$
(taking positive current as flowing from the buffer into the capacitor, and assuming the capacitor has fully settled before each edge).

**Numbers.** $V_{DD}=3.3$ V, $R=1$ kΩ, $C=1$ nF, $R_p\approx R_n\approx50$ Ω (ignored in the plot): $\tau\approx1$ µs (1.05 µs with the on-resistance), $I_{pk}=3.3$ mA (3.14 mA with $R_p$), 10-90% rise time $2.2\tau=2.2$ µs, 99% settle at $4.6\tau$.

**Which FET conducts.**

| Interval | Buffer output | PMOS | NMOS | Current in R | Cap voltage |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Positive step, $0<t<5\tau$ | $V_{DD}$ | on (sourcing) | off | $+$, decaying | rising to $V_{DD}$ |
| Held high, $t>5\tau$ | $V_{DD}$ | on (no current) | off | 0 | $V_{DD}$ |
| Negative step | 0 | off | on (sinking) | $-$, decaying | falling to 0 |
| Held low | 0 | off | on (no current) | 0 | 0 |

**Energy.** On the rising edge the supply gives $CV_{DD}^2=10.9$ nJ ($C=1$ nF, 3.3 V); half, 5.4 nJ, is stored, half dissipated in $R+R_p$. On the falling edge the stored 5.4 nJ is dissipated in $R+R_n$. Average power with $f=100$ kHz and $\tau\ll T/2$: $CV_{DD}^2f=1.09$ mW. See [[q0110]]. The split between $R$ and the transistor is in proportion to resistance, so a 1 kΩ external with 50 Ω transistors puts about 95% of the heat in the resistor.

**If $R$ is very small** (say 5 Ω), the initial current $V_{DD}/(R+R_p)$ would exceed what the PMOS can supply in saturation; the PMOS then acts as a current source for the first part of the edge (linear ramp), then goes into triode (exponential). The capacitor voltage ramp is slew-limited ([[q0111]]).
:::

::: short
"Treat the buffer as a switch to $V_{DD}$ or ground. On the positive step the PMOS is on and the NMOS is off: current $V_{DD}/R$ flows from the supply into the capacitor and decays with $\tau=RC$ while the capacitor voltage rises exponentially. On the negative step the NMOS is on and the PMOS is off: the capacitor discharges through the resistor into the NMOS, so the current reverses and again decays. The output is a square wave, the capacitor voltage is a rounded square wave, and the current is alternating decaying spikes at each edge, zero in between."
:::

::: long
"I'd start by drawing the buffer's output stage, a PMOS from $V_{DD}$ to the output and an NMOS from the output to ground, driving a series resistor and then a capacitor to ground. For analysis I treat each transistor as a small on-resistance, so the buffer output is an ideal 0-to-$V_{DD}$ square wave behind that resistance.

Positive step: the input to the buffer goes high, and the output goes high. The PMOS is on, the NMOS off. At that instant the capacitor is uncharged, so the whole $V_{DD}$ sits across the resistor and the current spikes to $V_{DD}/R$, flowing from the supply through the PMOS and the resistor into the capacitor. As the capacitor charges, the voltage across the resistor falls and the current decays exponentially with the time constant $RC$. The capacitor voltage is $V_{DD}(1-e^{-t/RC})$. After about five time constants the current is gone, the capacitor is at $V_{DD}$, and the PMOS is still on but carrying nothing.

Negative step: the output goes low, the NMOS turns on, the PMOS turns off. The capacitor is at $V_{DD}$, so current flows out of the capacitor, back through the resistor and into the NMOS to ground. It's the same exponential with the opposite sign. So on the waveform plot: the buffer output is a square wave, the capacitor voltage is a rounded square wave, and the current is a series of decaying spikes, positive on rising edges and negative on falling edges, with zero current in between.

With numbers: 3.3 V, 1 kΩ and 1 nF gives a 1 µs time constant and 3.3 mA peak. Each edge dissipates half of $CV^2$, 5.4 nJ, in the resistances, and at 100 kHz that's about 1 mW. The key point: current only flows during edges, and the PMOS sources while the NMOS sinks."
:::

::: terms
- **Push-pull output stage**: PMOS to $V_{DD}$ and NMOS to ground, driven together, one on at a time.
- **Time constant ($\tau$)**: $RC$; the time for the capacitor to cover 63% of the change.
- **Source / sink current**: current flowing out of / into the buffer output.
- **Rise time (10-90%)**: $2.2\tau$ for a single RC.
- **Settling time**: time to get within a tolerance of the final value; $\approx4.6\tau$ to 1%.
- **On-resistance ($R_{on}$)**: the transistor's channel resistance when conducting in triode.
- **Square-wave drive**: periodic 0/$V_{DD}$ input with a duty cycle.
- **Charge per edge**: $Q=CV_{DD}$ moved in each transition.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer sketches rounded edges and says "PMOS pulls up, NMOS pulls down". An exceptional answer:

- Draws **three aligned waveforms**: buffer output, capacitor voltage, and current (with sign), and marks $\tau$, $I_{pk}=V_{DD}/R$ and the 63% point.
- States **which FET conducts and in which direction**, including that the on-state FET carries zero current once settled.
- Includes the transistor $R_{on}$ in $\tau$ and notes the saturation (slew-limited) case if $R$ is small.
- Computes energy per edge ($\tfrac12CV^2$) and relates it to power $CV^2f$.
- Notes what happens if the period is shorter than $\sim5\tau$ (the capacitor never reaches the rail: triangle-like swing with reduced amplitude and a settled DC offset).
- Mentions supply current spikes and decoupling.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing the output as an RC-rounded waveform.** The buffer output is the square wave; the rounding is the *capacitor* voltage.
- **Saying the NMOS conducts during the positive step.** The PMOS sources, the NMOS is off. On the negative step it is reversed.
- **Showing the current as a square wave.** A capacitor's current decays to zero after each edge; only a resistive load gives a steady current.
- **Forgetting the current sign change.** The current reverses on the falling edge (the capacitor sources the current into the NMOS).
- **Using $\tau=R C$ with the external $R$ only** when $R_{on}$ is comparable; add it.
- **Assuming full swing at high frequency.** If the half period is not much longer than $\tau$, the capacitor voltage never reaches the rails.
:::

## What determines the shape

- **Peak current $V_{DD}/(R+R_{on})$.** With $R=1$ kΩ and 50 Ω transistors, 3.14 mA. With $R=0$ the peak is limited by the transistor's saturation current.
- **Slew vs RC regime.** If $V_{DD}/R$ exceeds the PMOS's saturation current $I_{sat}$, the PMOS limits the current to $I_{sat}$ and the capacitor ramps linearly at $I_{sat}/C$ until the transistor exits saturation, then follows the RC exponential ([[q0111]]).
- **Frequency.** If the half-period is shorter than about $3\tau$, the capacitor can't reach the rails: the swing shrinks to $\Delta V=V_{DD}\dfrac{1-e^{-T/2\tau}}{1+e^{-T/2\tau}}$ around the mean $V_{DD}/2$. At $T/2=\tau$, that is $V_{DD}\times0.462$, about 1.5 V peak-to-peak.

## Energy and the supply current

On each rising edge the supply gives $CV_{DD}^2$: 10.9 nJ here, of which 5.4 nJ is stored. The power is the same $CV_{DD}^2f$ discussed in [[q0110]] even though most of it heats $R$ rather than the transistor, which matters when sizing the resistor's power rating: at 100 kHz the path dissipates 1.09 mW in total, about 95% of it (roughly 1 mW) in the 1 kΩ resistor. The supply current is a train of positive spikes, so $V_{DD}$ needs local decoupling: a 100 nF capacitor on $V_{DD}$ supplies the spike with only $\Delta V=Q/C_{dec}=3.3\text{ nC}/100\text{ nF}=33$ mV droop.

## Real hardware

- **Series termination resistor + trace capacitance**: the same picture; $R\approx$ 22-50 Ω and C of tens of pF gives sub-ns time constants.
- **RC low-pass filter fed by a GPIO** (a poor man's DAC with PWM): the capacitor voltage is the average of the PWM square wave, ripple depends on $\tau$ vs the PWM period.
- **LED with a capacitor**, relay snubber, or an RC debounce: same equations with different component names.
- **Gate drive**: a driver output through $R_g$ into the gate capacitance of a power MOSFET is exactly this circuit, with the gate charge as $C$; in that case $\tau=R_gC_{iss}$ sets the switching speed (see [[q0102]]).

## Worked example: edge timing including the transistor's own resistance

The waveforms above used $\tau=RC=1\ \mu$s. A real buffer adds its on-resistance in series with $R$. With 50 Ω for the conducting FET the total is $R_{tot}=1050\ \Omega$ and $\tau=1.05\ \mu$s. The standard timing numbers for a single-pole step response are then:

| Quantity | Formula | Value |
|---|---|---|
| 50 % delay | $0.693\tau$ | 0.73 µs |
| 10 %–90 % rise or fall time | $2.2\tau$ | 2.31 µs |
| to within 1 % of the rail | $4.6\tau$ | 4.8 µs |
| corner frequency | $1/(2\pi\tau)$ | 152 kHz |

The 100 kHz square wave has a half period of 5 µs $=4.76\tau$, so it nearly settles: the capacitor swings between 0.028 V and 3.27 V, a peak-to-peak value of $3.3\times\frac{1-e^{-4.76}}{1+e^{-4.76}}=3.24$ V. The fundamental of the square wave (peak amplitude $\tfrac2\pi\times3.3=2.10$ V) is attenuated by $1/\sqrt{1+(100/152)^2}=0.83$ to 1.75 V, and the higher harmonics are cut more, which is why the capacitor waveform is a rounded triangle of exponentials rather than a square wave. If you push the frequency to 500 kHz (half period $0.95\tau$) the swing falls to $3.3\times\frac{1-e^{-0.95}}{1+e^{-0.95}}=1.45$ V peak-to-peak, centered on 1.65 V.

## Worked example: when the transistor current-limits the start of the edge

The exponential response assumes the transistor is a resistor. At the start of the rising edge the full $V_{DD}$ is across the PMOS and $R$, and the largest current the PMOS can deliver is its saturation current $I_{sat}$. Suppose a smaller resistor $R=100\ \Omega$ is used, so that the ideal initial current $V_{DD}/R=33$ mA exceeds a PMOS $I_{sat}=20$ mA (an illustrative value). Then:

1. For as long as $(V_{DD}-V_C)/R>I_{sat}$, the capacitor is charged at a constant 20 mA. The slew rate is $I_{sat}/C=20\text{ mA}/1\text{ nF}=20\text{ V/}\mu\text{s}$. The current stops being limited when $V_C=V_{DD}-I_{sat}R=3.3-2=1.3$ V, which takes $1.3/(20\text{ V/}\mu\text{s})=65$ ns.
2. After that the PMOS is in triode, the circuit is a normal RC, and $V_C=3.3-2e^{-t'/100\text{ ns}}$ rises toward 3.3 V.

The output crosses 50 % (1.65 V) at $65+19=84$ ns, against $0.693\times100=69$ ns for the ideal RC; it reaches 90 % at 245 ns, against 230 ns. The current-limited start delays the edge by about 15 ns here, and what the load sees is a ramp followed by an exponential, which is how real buffers behave when $R$ is small. The current waveform in this regime is a flat-topped pulse at 20 mA rather than a spike at 33 mA, which is also kinder to the supply. See [[q0111]] for the edge-rate trade-offs.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 20 mA saturation current and 50 Ω on-resistance are illustrative; actual drivers vary with process, device size, supply and temperature, and datasheets specify output drive as source/sink current at a given output voltage rather than as a simple resistor.

## Worked example: the same circuit as a PWM filter

Replace the 100 kHz square wave with a 50 % duty-cycle PWM output and the load with $R=1$ kΩ, $C=1\ \mu$F so $\tau=1$ ms. The capacitor now sees a nearly constant average of $0.5\times3.3=1.65$ V. The ripple on the capacitor is a small triangle: the peak-to-peak value is approximately
$$\Delta V_{pp}\approx\frac{D(1-D)V_{DD}}{f\,RC}=\frac{0.25\times3.3}{10^5\times10^{-3}}=8.25\ \text{mV}$$
Because the average is set by duty cycle, this is a one-pole "DAC", and the ripple falls inversely with $f\tau$. The compromise is settling time: a step in duty cycle takes about $4.6\tau=4.6$ ms to settle to 1 %, so a quieter output is a slower one. A second RC stage steepens the roll-off. Precision of the output also depends on the buffer's high and low levels. If the PMOS and NMOS on-resistances are equal, they simply add to $R$ and the average stays at $D\,V_{DD}$; but a mismatch between them (a 50 Ω versus 70 Ω pair is 2 % of a 1 kΩ resistor) or any DC load on the capacitor shifts the average. That is why $R$ is made large compared with $R_{on}$ and the capacitor is buffered when accuracy matters.

## Real-hardware details that change the picture

- **Supply and ground bounce.** The current spikes flow through the package and board inductance. For the 3.14 mA peak with a 2 ns edge, $di/dt=1.6\times10^{6}$ A/s, and 5 nH of lead inductance gives a transient of only 8 mV: negligible here. But a bus of 32 such outputs switching together multiplies the current, and the same arithmetic for tens of milliamps per pin and sub-nanosecond edges gives hundreds of millivolts of bounce, which is why drivers have slew-rate control and chips have many power and ground pins.
- **Probing.** A 10x oscilloscope probe adds about 10 pF at the capacitor node. With $C=1$ nF that is negligible, but with $C=20$ pF in a real termination it is not: measure the buffer output directly and use the probe's short ground spring to avoid ringing on the edges.
- **Output resistance changes with level.** The transistor's $R_{on}$ in triode depends on its remaining $V_{GS}$ overdrive, and as the output approaches the rail the PMOS's overdrive stays at $V_{DD}-|V_t|$ while its current falls, so the drive resistance is not constant during an edge: at the start the device may be saturated (current-limited, as in the example above), and only near the end of the edge, in deep triode, does it settle to $R_{on}\approx1/[\beta(V_{DD}-|V_t|)]$. A single $R_{on}$ is therefore an approximation that is best for the tail of the edge.
- **Duty-cycle and level distortion.** If the NMOS and PMOS $R_{on}$ differ (a PMOS of the same size is weaker, [[q0070]]), the rising and falling time constants differ and the capacitor waveform is not symmetric. In the filter example this shows up as a slightly different effective output level for 50 % duty cycle.

## How you would verify the waveforms on the bench

- **Build it.** Use a logic-level buffer or inverter (74HC- or 74AHC-family) with a 1 kΩ resistor and a 1 nF capacitor to ground, a 100 kHz square wave at the input, and a scope on both the buffer output and the capacitor.
- **Check the numbers.** Measure the 10 %–90 % rise time of the capacitor voltage (expect about 2.3 µs), the 50 % delay (about 0.73 µs) and the peak-to-peak swing (about 3.24 V). If the rise time is lower, look for a smaller $R$ or $C$ than marked; if the waveform has a visible kink at the start, the driver is current-limiting.
- **Identify the conducting FET.** Measure the current with a small sense resistor in the supply or the ground leg: the supply current has spikes only on rising output edges, the ground current only on falling output edges. This answers the interview question about which transistor is conducting without opening the chip.
- **Sweep frequency.** Raise the input frequency from 10 kHz to 1 MHz and plot the peak-to-peak capacitor voltage against frequency. The knee should land at about 152 kHz, and above it the swing should fall like $1/f$.

::: applications
- **GPIO driving a trace or input with series termination**: edge rate and overshoot control.
- **PWM into an RC low-pass filter** to produce an analog voltage; capacitor voltage ripple.
- **Gate drivers** charging MOSFET gate capacitance through a gate resistor.
- **I/O power estimation**: edge energy $\tfrac12CV^2$ in the load resistance.
- **Debounce and delay circuits**: an RC after a logic output with a Schmitt input.
- **Output stage characterization on a scope**: reading $\tau$ from the capacitor waveform and inferring $R_{on}$.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear $R_{on}$** for the transistors (deep triode) and a constant $C$; real MOSFETs are in saturation during the first part of large current edges.
- **Ideal square-wave input** to the output stage (infinitely fast input edge); a real stage's input has a finite rise time ([[q0109]]).
- **Settled** conditions: the half period is at least about $5\tau$. Otherwise the waveform reaches a periodic steady state with a reduced swing.
- **No inductance** in the supply or load path; real traces ring ([[q0111]]).
- If the buffer is a **non-inverting buffer** (two inverters), the output stage described is the second inverter; for a single **inverter** the polarity of the input is reversed but the output-stage behaviour is identical.
:::

::: followups
### What if the half period is only $\tau$?
The capacitor can't reach the rails. In steady state it swings between $V_L$ and $V_H$ with $V_H=V_{DD}\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-T/2\tau}}$, e.g. $T/2=\tau$ gives $V_H=0.731V_{DD}$ and $V_L=0.269V_{DD}$ (2.4 V and 0.89 V for 3.3 V), a swing of $0.462V_{DD}=1.5$ V around $V_{DD}/2$.

### What current does the supply see, and how would you decouple it?
Spikes of up to $V_{DD}/R$ at each rising edge only. The charge per edge is $CV_{DD}=3.3$ nC, so a 100 nF local capacitor sags 33 mV. Place it close to the buffer's supply pin so the loop inductance is small.

### How would the waveforms change if the load were a resistor instead of an RC?
The current would be a steady $V_{DD}/R$ during the high phase (PMOS conducting continuously) and zero when low: a square wave in phase with the output. That's DC power, the static dissipation that CMOS gates avoid.

### What if $R=0$?
The only resistances are the transistors. The PMOS saturates and supplies a constant current $I_{sat}$, so $V_C$ ramps linearly at $I_{sat}/C$, then rounds off in the triode region. The current spike is limited by the transistor, so the peak is large and short, with ground bounce and EMI as the cost.

### Harder: which device dissipates more energy, the PMOS or the NMOS?
Each edge dissipates $\tfrac12CV^2$ in the *total* series resistance; the share in the transistor is $R_{on}/(R+R_{on})$. With similar on-resistances the PMOS and NMOS dissipate equal energies per cycle; with the external resistor dominant, most is in the resistor, not the FETs.

### Scenario: your PWM-driven RC filter output has too much ripple. How do you fix it?
The ripple is $\Delta V\approx\dfrac{V_{DD}\,D(1-D)}{RCf}$ for $RC\gg T$. Increase $RC$ (but that slows the response) or $f$, or use a second-order filter. For example, at 100 kHz, 3.3 V, $D=0.5$, $RC=100$ µs: $3.3\times0.25/(100\ \mu\text{s}\times100\text{ kHz})=82$ mV; a 1 ms time constant gives about 8 mV.
:::

::: interpretations
"Voltage/current output waveforms" could mean (i) the output node of the buffer and the current into the load, or (ii) the voltage across the capacitor. This page shows all three: buffer output, capacitor voltage and current. "Positive/negative step" is taken as a rising and falling edge at the buffer output, and "series RC load" as a resistor in series with a capacitor to ground (not a parallel RC).
:::
