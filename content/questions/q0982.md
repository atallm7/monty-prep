::: testing
Whether you know that **you never measure current directly; you measure a voltage or a magnetic field that current produces**, and whether you can pick between the methods on a stated trade-off: insertion loss (burden), accuracy, bandwidth, DC capability, isolation, power and cost. A thorough answer lists the families (series shunt, DMM ammeter, clamp/current probe, Hall sensor, Rogowski coil, inductor DCR, indirect methods) and works one number (shunt voltage and power, amplifier offset error) to show the real constraints.
:::

::: why
Current is the quantity you most often need and least often can see: efficiency, battery life, fault detection, motor control, charging and safety all rely on it. The method sets what you can learn: a DMM ammeter tells you average current but breaks the circuit and adds burden; a shunt with a scope shows the waveform; a current probe doesn't touch the circuit but needs care at DC and low levels; a Hall sensor isolates. Interviewers use this question to see if you understand loading, resolution and the shunt-resistor trade-off, which also leads directly into system design ([[q0447]], [[q0449]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Ohm's law as a sensor**: $I=V/R$ across a known resistance; see [[q0001]].
- **Burden voltage**: the voltage a series ammeter drops in the circuit, which changes the circuit being measured.
- **Shunt trade-off**: bigger $R$ gives bigger signal and more power loss and voltage drop. See [[q0446]].
- **High-side vs low-side sensing**: where the sense element sits relative to ground. See [[q0447]].
- **Magnetic sensing**: Hall effect and induced voltage (Rogowski, transformers). See [[q0441]].
- **Common-mode and offset**: amplifier input offset sets the smallest measurable current. See [[q0449]].
:::

::: answer
"The main ways are: put a meter in series, put a small sense resistor in series and measure the voltage across it, or sense the magnetic field around the wire without breaking the circuit.

The simplest is a multimeter in current mode, which is accurate at DC but you have to break the circuit, it adds burden voltage, and it only gives an average. A shunt resistor is the workhorse: I measure the voltage across it with a DMM, a scope or a current-sense amplifier into an ADC. With a 10 milliohm shunt, one amp gives 10 millivolts and 10 milliwatts of loss. The trade-off is signal versus loss: a bigger resistor gives more signal but drops more voltage and burns more power, and a tiny one gets buried in amplifier offset.

When I can't break the circuit or need isolation, I'd use a clamp-on or a current probe. Hall-effect probes work at DC and AC, Rogowski coils work for AC and large currents, and current transformers for AC only. On boards you can use Hall-effect sensor ICs. Another trick is to use the DC resistance of the inductor, if you filter it properly.

So I choose based on the current range, whether it is DC, how fast it changes, how much voltage I can afford to lose, whether I need isolation, and the accuracy needed."
:::

::: technical
**Shunt (resistive) sensing.** $V_{sense}=I\,R_{sh}$, power $P=I^2R_{sh}$. Example: $R_{sh}=10$ mΩ: at 1 A, 10 mV and 10 mW; at 10 A, 100 mV and 1 W. A current-sense amplifier with gain $G$ gives $V_{out}=G\,I\,R_{sh}$; with $G=50$, 1 A gives 0.5 V. With a 12-bit, 3.3 V ADC: full scale $=3.3/(50\cdot0.01)=6.6$ A and one LSB $=0.805\ \text{mV}/(50\cdot0.01)=1.6$ mA.

**Offset sets the floor.** An amplifier input offset $V_{os}$ produces a current error $V_{os}/R_{sh}$: 100 µV offset with 10 mΩ gives 10 mA, so measuring 1 mA is hopeless. Use a larger shunt, a zero-drift amplifier (offsets of a few µV), or a switched range.

**Series DMM ammeter.** The meter inserts a shunt (a few Ω on mA ranges, mΩ on amp ranges) and measures its voltage. Burden voltage is quoted in the datasheet and can be several hundred mV at full scale on mA ranges. Good for accurate DC averages; bad for low-voltage circuits and fast waveforms.

**Current probe/clamp.** Hall-effect (DC to some MHz, up to tens of MHz for scope probes) or a transformer/Rogowski type (AC only, high bandwidth, very large currents). Non-invasive, adds small insertion impedance. DC probes need degauss and zero-adjust. Sensitive to the conductor position, external fields and saturation.

**Hall-effect IC (on board).** Isolated, handles high current; typical sensitivities are tens to hundreds of mV/A, with an offset at zero current and a few-percent accuracy over temperature, bandwidth in the tens to hundreds of kHz range.

**Inductor DCR sensing.** An RC across the inductor with $RC=L/R_{DCR}$ reproduces $I_L R_{DCR}$. Free (no extra loss) but the copper's temperature coefficient (about 0.39 %/°C) must be compensated.

**Indirect.** Voltage across a known resistive load, FET $R_{DS(on)}$ sensing (±tens of %), battery fuel gauges that integrate shunt current, or a power supply's current readback.
:::

::: long
"Nothing reads current directly. You turn it into a voltage or a magnetic field and measure that.

First option, a multimeter in series. It's accurate at DC and simple, but you have to break the circuit, it adds a burden voltage because it has a shunt inside, and it only gives you the average.

Second, and most common, a sense resistor. I put a small resistor in series and measure the voltage across it with a meter, a scope or a current-sense amplifier. The trade-off is signal versus loss. With 10 milliohms, 1 amp gives 10 millivolts and burns 10 milliwatts. Bigger is easier to measure but wastes voltage and power; smaller is lossless but gets buried in the amplifier's offset. A 100 microvolt offset on 10 milliohms is a 10 milliamp error, so small currents need a bigger shunt or a zero-drift amplifier. I'd also think about high-side versus low-side: low-side is easy but lifts the load's ground; high-side needs an amplifier with a good common-mode range.

Third, if I can't break the circuit or I want isolation, I use a magnetic method. A Hall-effect clamp or current probe works at DC and AC; a Rogowski coil or a current transformer is AC only but handles huge currents and high frequency. On a board, a Hall-effect sensor IC gives isolation at the cost of offset and a few percent accuracy.

There's also inductor DCR sensing in power supplies, which costs nothing in loss but needs temperature compensation. So: pick by range, DC versus AC, bandwidth, allowed voltage drop, isolation and accuracy."
:::

::: terms
- **Shunt resistor**: a low-value, precision resistor placed in series to convert current to a small voltage.
- **Burden voltage**: voltage dropped by the ammeter or sense element in the circuit.
- **Four-terminal (Kelvin) shunt**: a shunt with separate sense terminals so contact resistance doesn't matter.
- **Current-sense amplifier (CSA)**: an amplifier designed for high common-mode input and low offset to read a shunt voltage.
- **Hall effect**: voltage across a conductor carrying current in a magnetic field, used to sense field proportional to current.
- **Rogowski coil**: an air-core coil whose induced voltage is proportional to $dI/dt$; integrated to give current.
- **Current transformer (CT)**: AC-only transformer whose secondary current is proportional to primary current.
- **DCR sensing**: using an inductor's series winding resistance as the shunt.
- **Common-mode voltage**: voltage common to both amplifier inputs, here the rail the shunt sits on.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use an ammeter or a clamp meter".

**An exceptional answer**:
- States that current is measured **indirectly** and groups methods: series resistance (DMM, shunt), magnetic (Hall, Rogowski, CT), and inferred (DCR, FET $R_{DS(on)}$).
- Works a **number**: shunt voltage, power loss, ADC LSB in mA, and the offset-limited minimum current.
- Names the **trade-offs** each time: burden, accuracy, bandwidth, DC capability, isolation, cost.
- Knows **DC vs AC** limits: Rogowski and CTs can't do DC; Hall probes can but drift and need zeroing.
- Mentions **high-side vs low-side**, Kelvin connections and shunt self-heating and tempco.
- Adds **verification**: calibrate against a known current, check with a second method.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting a DMM current range across a voltage source.** It's a near-short through the shunt and blows the fuse (or the supply).
- **Ignoring burden voltage.** On a 3.3 V rail a 0.3 V burden changes the circuit's behaviour and the reading.
- **Choosing the shunt only by signal level.** Too big wastes power and drops the rail; too small is lost in offset, and at high current it self-heats and changes value.
- **Using an AC-only probe or Rogowski coil for DC.** They don't respond at DC.
- **Forgetting probe zeroing/degauss** on a DC Hall probe: it shows offset or residual magnetisation.
- **Measuring across the shunt's solder joints** instead of Kelvin pads, which adds contact and trace resistance into a milliohm element.
:::

::: deep
## All current measurement is indirect

Current is charge flow. You can't count electrons; you use one of two physical effects:

1. **Voltage across resistance** (Ohm's law): $V=IR$. Cheap, accurate, but in series with the circuit.
2. **Magnetic field around the conductor** (Ampère's law): $\oint B\cdot dl=\mu_0I$. Non-invasive and isolated, but more error sources.

Everything else (DCR sensing, FET sensing, fuel gauges, power-supply readback) is a variation on these.

Design equations:

$$ V_{sh}=IR_{sh},\qquad P_{sh}=I^2R_{sh},\qquad V_{out}=G\,I\,R_{sh} $$

Worked example with a 10 mΩ shunt, $G=50$, 12-bit ADC at 3.3 V:

- At 1 A: $V_{sh}=10$ mV, $P=10$ mW, $V_{out}=0.5$ V.
- Full scale: $I_{max}=3.3/(50\times0.01)=6.6$ A (use 5 A max for headroom).
- One LSB: $3.3/4096=0.805$ mV at the ADC, so $0.805\ \text{mV}/(50\times0.01\ \Omega)=1.6$ mA of current resolution.
- At 5 A: $P=I^2R=0.25$ W. The shunt's rated power should be at least 2× this and the resistor sized in a package that can dissipate it.

**Offset limit.** An amplifier with $V_{os}=100$ µV produces an error of $V_{os}/R_{sh}=10$ mA in the current. To measure 1 mA with 1 % accuracy (10 µA) you need $R_{sh}\ge V_{os}/10\ \mu A$: with a zero-drift amp at 5 µV, $R_{sh}\ge0.5\ \Omega$; with 100 µV it would need 10 Ω.

**Shunt choice.** Use low-TCR alloys (manganin, Evanohm type) for precision, four-terminal (Kelvin) layout, and keep self-heating low; at the rated power a metal-foil or alloy shunt's resistance shifts, so derate. The sense traces should connect at the resistor's inner pads, not through the high-current path.

Figure: vary the sense resistor and gain. Watch the trade-offs together: the resistor's power dissipation, the headroom to full-scale at maximum current, and the mA-per-LSB resolution at the smallest current. This is a three-way constraint, not an independent choice.

**High-side vs low-side.** A low-side shunt (between load and ground) is easy: the amplifier input is near ground and you can use a simple op-amp. The cost is the ground shift and that you can't see a short from load to ground. A high-side shunt (between supply and load) keeps the load grounded and detects shorts but needs an amplifier with common-mode range to the supply (or beyond). Dedicated CSAs handle common-mode far above their own supply. See [[q0447]] and [[q0449]].

## Method 2: the DMM ammeter

The DMM's current mode places a shunt in series, measures its voltage, and displays current. The burden voltage is spec'd per range (for example a few hundred mV at full scale on the mA ranges, much less on the amp range; check the datasheet). Procedure: turn off power, break the circuit, move the lead to the current jack, connect the meter in series, power on. Never connect in parallel with a source. The next simulation shows the burden.

Figure: in current mode the meter's internal shunt sits in series with the circuit. Lower the circuit resistance or pick a more sensitive range (bigger shunt) and watch the burden voltage grow and the reading fall below the true current. The rule: the shunt must be much smaller than the circuit resistance; on a low-voltage rail that burden changes the very circuit you are measuring, so use a higher range or a shunt and a voltmeter.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Burden voltage values depend on meter model and range. Typical handheld meters drop a few tenths of a volt on mA ranges at full scale. Always read the specification for the exact meter and range.

## Method 3: current probes and clamps

A clamp meter or scope current probe encircles one conductor. No breaking, no insertion loss beyond a few tens of nH and mΩ.

| Type | Principle | DC? | Bandwidth | Range |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Current transformer / AC clamp | Transformer | No | Hz to MHz | A to kA |
| Rogowski coil | Air-core coil, output ∝ $dI/dt$ (integrated) | No | up to tens of MHz | A to kA, flexible |
| Hall-effect (DC/AC) clamp | Hall sensor in a gapped core | Yes | DC to kHz–tens of MHz | mA to hundreds of A |

Practicalities: **degauss and zero** a DC probe before use (residual core magnetisation shows up as offset); clamp **one conductor only** (a pair with equal and opposite current reads zero); keep the conductor centred; note the minimum measurable current (typically tens of mA on sensitive probes); and for small currents, loop the wire several times through the clamp to multiply the signal (reading ÷ turns). See [[q0998]] for current probes in detail.

## Method 4: Hall-effect sensor ICs

A Hall IC puts the conductor through a loop on the package; the field is sensed and converted to a proportional voltage. Benefits: galvanic isolation, low insertion resistance (often around 1 mΩ for the primary path), AC and DC. Costs: a few-percent total accuracy, zero-current offset and its drift, limited bandwidth and noise floor (tens of mA RMS for common 5–30 A parts), and sensitivity to external fields. See [[q0441]] and [[q0442]].

## Method 5: inferred or "free" current sensing

- **Inductor DCR sensing**: a series RC across the inductor reproduces the DCR drop if $R C=L/R_{DCR}$. No added loss, but copper's resistance rises about 0.39 %/°C, so the reading drifts; compensate with an NTC or calibrate.
- **MOSFET $R_{DS(on)}$ sensing**: $I=V_{DS}/R_{DS(on)}$. Loose accuracy (process and temperature vary tens of %), but fine for overcurrent protection.
- **Fuel gauges** integrate shunt current (coulomb counting) for battery state of charge ([[q0294]]).
- **Power-supply readback**: a bench supply or SMU displays the current it delivers; convenient for total input current.

## Choosing: a decision table

| Need | Good choice |
|---|---|
| Accurate DC current in a lab, circuit can be broken, loose burden | DMM ammeter or SMU |
| Waveform of switching current | Shunt + scope, or current probe |
| Can't break the circuit | Clamp/current probe |
| High current, AC | Rogowski or CT |
| Isolation needed | Hall IC, isolated CSA |
| Very small currents (nA–pA) | Picoammeter/SMU, feedback ammeter (no burden) |
| Embedded monitoring, wide range | Shunt + CSA + ADC (autoranging if needed) |

Very small currents deserve a note: a **feedback ammeter** (transimpedance amplifier, see [[q0854]]) holds its input near zero volts so it has almost no burden, reaching pA. For tiny currents, leakage across the PCB and cable noise dominate.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Close the loop with a number: "With a 10 milliohm shunt, 1 amp gives 10 millivolts and 10 milliwatts. A 100 microvolt amplifier offset makes that a 10 milliamp error, so for small currents I'd choose a bigger shunt or a zero-drift amp." That line shows you understand the trade-off, not just the list.

::: applications
- **Battery packs and chargers**: shunt + CSA/fuel gauge for coulomb counting and charge control ([[q0294]]).
- **Motor drives**: low-side shunts in the inverter legs for phase currents; Hall sensors for isolation ([[q0393]]).
- **Switching regulators**: inductor DCR sensing for current-mode control and over-current limiting.
- **Power electronics debug**: scope with DC-capable current probe to see inductor and switch currents.
- **Efficiency testing**: precision shunts or power analyzers on input and output.
- **Low-power IoT**: nA–µA sleep currents with SMU or dedicated power profilers.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes **DC or low-frequency** current for the shunt discussion. At high $di/dt$ a shunt's parasitic inductance adds an $L\,di/dt$ error, so use coaxial or low-inductance shunts.
- Resistances and offsets are **typical**; use the actual shunt and amplifier datasheets.
- Hall sensor and probe numbers are **indicative** of common parts; specifications vary widely.
- The answer changes with the application: a lab technician cares about burden and probes, a battery designer about microamp sleep currents and calibration, a motor-drive engineer about common-mode voltage and bandwidth.
- Voltage-across-load or other inference methods assume the load is **known and linear**, which often it is not.
:::

::: followups
### How would you size the sense resistor for a 5 A max load on a 3.3 V ADC?
Choose the amplifier gain and resistor so full scale maps to the ADC range, while keeping the voltage drop and power acceptable. For example, 10 mΩ gives 50 mV at 5 A and 50 mW; with a gain of 50 that is 2.5 V full scale (headroom under 3.3 V). Check that the smallest current of interest is above the offset error and above one ADC LSB.

### What's the difference between high-side and low-side current sensing?
Low-side puts the shunt between load and ground: simple amplifier, but ground shifts and shorts to ground aren't seen. High-side puts it in the supply path: load stays grounded and shorts are detected, but the amplifier must tolerate common-mode voltage near the supply. See [[q0447]].

### Why can't a Rogowski coil measure DC?
Its output is proportional to $dI/dt$ (induced by changing flux). With constant current, $dI/dt=0$ so the output is zero. An integrator recovers AC current but drifts at DC.

### How do you measure a 1 mA signal with a 100 µV offset amplifier?
Use a larger shunt, since the offset error is $V_{os}/R_{sh}$. A 10 Ω shunt gives 10 mV at 1 mA and a 10 µA error (1 %), but it drops 10 mV and burns 10 µW. Alternatively use a zero-drift or chopper amplifier with a few µV offset, or a feedback (transimpedance) ammeter.

### Scenario: your efficiency measurement shows 102 %. What might be wrong?
Measurement error. Check burden voltage in the input DMM (it affects the apparent input), lead resistance in the voltage measurement (use Kelvin sense at the converter), mismatched averaging or bandwidth between the two measurements, shunt thermal drift, and ripple causing average-responding meters to misread. Re-measure with the same instrument on input and output and with voltage sensed at the converter terminals.
:::

::: interpretations
"How can I measure current?" is general, so the answer should be a **survey with trade-offs** and one worked example. Some interviewers mean "with a scope" (shunt or current probe), some "on a PCB" (shunt + CSA or Hall IC), some "at the bench" (DMM). Cover all three quickly and ask which setting they care about. For the narrower "voltage across a sense resistor on a scope, how do I get current" see [[q0993]].
