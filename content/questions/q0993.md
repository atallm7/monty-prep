::: testing
Whether you can turn a voltage on a screen into an amps number **and** know when that number is trustworthy. The calculation is one line, $I = V/R$. The hidden sub-skills are: knowing the resistor's *real* value and tolerance, choosing high-side versus low-side probing, spotting that the voltage across a milliohm resistor is tiny (so scope resolution and noise matter), and knowing that the resistor's inductance can fake a current waveform at fast edges. Interviewers also like to hear the cleaner alternatives (math channel, current probe) and when you would switch.
:::

::: why
Almost every power, motor and battery bring-up ends with "what current is it actually drawing, and what does it do during the transient?" A DMM only shows an average. A sense resistor plus a scope shows the inrush spike, the ripple current, the stall current of a motor, or the sleep-to-wake step of an IoT board. It is also the cheapest current measurement you can build into a board, so you will do it constantly. Getting the units wrong by 10x, or trusting a spike that is really inductance, is how people ship the wrong fuse or the wrong inductor.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ohm's law** ($I = V/R$): the sense voltage is directly proportional to current. See [[q0001]].
- **Sense resistor**: a small, precise, low-inductance resistor placed in series with the load so its drop is proportional to current. See [[q0443]] and [[q0445]].
- **Power in the shunt** ($P = I^2R$): the resistor must survive the current and must not drop too much voltage (burden).
- **Scope vertical resolution**: an 8-bit scope has only 256 levels across the screen, so millivolt-scale signals can look coarse. See [[q0990]].
- **Common-mode / grounding**: a ground-referenced probe can only measure a resistor with one end at ground; otherwise you need a differential measurement ([[q0995]]).
- **Kelvin connection**: probe the resistor's sense pads, not the high-current trace, to avoid measuring copper resistance. See [[q0557]].
:::

::: answer
"Short version: divide by the resistor. If I measure 20 mV across a 10 milliohm sense resistor, the current is 20 mV divided by 10 mΩ, which is 2 A. On the scope I would either do that division in my head, set the channel's units or scale so the screen reads amps directly, or use a math channel that divides channel 1 by 0.01.

But there are a few things I check so I trust it. First, where is the resistor? If it is on the low side, one end is ground, so a normal probe works. If it is on the high side, both ends are floating above ground, so I need a differential probe, or two probes subtracted, which is much less accurate. Second, the voltage is small, so I put the probe on the resistor's own pads, turn on bandwidth limit to cut noise, and keep the vertical scale tight enough to use the ADC range. Third, the resistor has tolerance and some inductance, so for fast edges the waveform can show spikes that are really L di/dt, not current. If that matters, I cross-check with a current probe or a DMM in series."
:::

::: technical
**Core relation.** For a resistor $R_s$ in series with the load, $I(t) = \dfrac{v_{sense}(t)}{R_s}$. The scope shows $v_{sense}(t)$, so the current waveform is the same shape scaled by $1/R_s$. Example: $R_s = 10\ \text{m}\Omega$ and $v_{sense}=20$ mV gives $I = 2$ A. A reading of 5 mV/div then equals 0.5 A/div.

**How to display amps.**
1. Mental scaling or a sticker of "A per div".
2. Probe attenuation or unit setting: many scopes let you set a custom scale, e.g. 100 A/V for a 10 mΩ shunt (1/0.01).
3. Math channel: $\text{CH1}/0.01$. If you also have the rail voltage on CH2, $\text{CH1/R}\times\text{CH2}$ gives instantaneous power.

**Probing arrangement.** Low-side sensing: the resistor is between load and ground, a normal single-ended probe works, but the load's ground is lifted by $IR_s$. High-side sensing: both ends float at the rail voltage, so use a differential probe or an isolated channel. Subtracting two single-ended channels works only for slowly varying, large signals because the common-mode rail is much bigger than the sense voltage and the probes' gains do not match exactly.

**Limits.**
- *Resolution.* An 8-bit scope at 5 mV/div has 40 mV across 8 divisions, so one code is 40 mV/256 ≈ 0.16 mV, which is 16 mA per LSB on a 10 mΩ shunt.
- *Power and burden.* $P = I^2R_s$ and the voltage lost is $IR_s$.
- *Inductance.* A real 0805 or 2512 shunt has a few nH. $v_L = L\,di/dt$: 5 nH with a 1 A/10 ns edge gives 0.5 V, larger than the whole 10 mV signal from a 1 A DC current.
- *Tolerance and tempco.* A 1% resistor gives a 1% gain error, and self-heating shifts it further.
:::

::: long
"The relation is just Ohm's law. The scope measures the voltage across the sense resistor, and the current is that voltage divided by the resistor value. Say I have a 10 milliohm shunt and the scope shows 20 millivolts. That is 2 amps. On a scope I would not do that in my head for a whole waveform: I would either set the probe scale so the vertical axis reads in amps, or use a math channel that divides by 0.01. If I also probe the supply voltage on another channel I can multiply them for instantaneous power.

Then I think about whether the measurement is valid. Where is the resistor? On the low side, one end is ground, so a normal probe is fine, but that adds a small offset to the load's ground. On the high side both ends float at the rail, and I need a differential probe because subtracting two normal probes has terrible common-mode rejection when the signal is only millivolts and the rail is volts.

Second, resolution. An eight-bit scope with a tight vertical scale might have a step of about 0.16 millivolt, which on a 10 milliohm shunt is 16 milliamps per code, so tiny currents look noisy or stepped. I would use averaging or high-resolution mode for slow signals.

Third, the shunt is not a perfect resistor. It has tolerance, tempco and a few nanohenries of inductance, so on fast edges the waveform includes L di/dt. If I see a big spike at a switching edge I would check whether it is real current or inductive. Finally I might use a current probe for fast currents because it adds almost no series drop, or a DMM for a precise DC average."
:::

::: terms
- **Shunt / sense resistor**: low-value precision resistor in series with the load; its voltage drop is proportional to current.
- **Burden voltage**: the voltage the measurement steals from the load, $IR_s$.
- **High-side sensing**: shunt between the supply and the load, so both ends sit at a high common-mode voltage.
- **Low-side sensing**: shunt between load and ground, easy to probe but lifts the load's ground.
- **Common-mode voltage**: the average voltage of the two measured nodes relative to scope ground.
- **Kelvin (4-wire) connection**: sense taps taken on the resistor's pads so trace resistance does not count.
- **Math channel**: scope function computing, for example, $\text{CH1}/R$ or $\text{CH1}\times\text{CH2}$ on the fly.
- **Vertical resolution (ENOB)**: the smallest voltage step the scope can distinguish; it sets the smallest current step you can see.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "divide by R" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the number with units, then shows how to get the scope to display amps (scale or math channel).
- Immediately asks **high-side or low-side**, and links that to probe type and common-mode rejection.
- Quantifies the weak point: tiny voltage means resolution and noise. Gives mA per LSB.
- Mentions shunt inductance and compares $L\,di/dt$ to $IR$ for the real edge speed.
- Names alternatives (current probe, DMM, current-sense amp) and picks one for the given situation.
- Notes the burden: the sense resistor changes the circuit it measures, so the value must be a compromise.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using the wrong value.** A "0.01" resistor is 10 mΩ, not 10 Ω. Check the marking (R010 = 0.010 Ω) and the tolerance, and do the division on a numbers sanity check (20 mV / 10 mΩ = 2 A, not 2 mA).
- **Probing the wrong place.** Touching the thick high-current trace instead of the resistor's own pads includes copper resistance in the reading. Use the sense pads ([[q0557]]).
- **Measuring a floating resistor with a grounded probe.** If the resistor is not at ground, clipping the probe ground to one end can short the circuit or inject large ground currents. Use a differential or isolated probe.
- **Ignoring bandwidth and inductance.** A 100 MHz scope across a nH-range shunt will show L di/dt ringing that is not current.
- **Reading the offset as current.** Probe or scope offset of a few mV looks like hundreds of mA on a 10 mΩ shunt. Zero the channel or ground the probe tip first.
- **Forgetting the burden.** A 0.5 Ω shunt in a 5 V, 1 A rail lets the load see 4.5 V, changing the very thing you measure.
:::

::: deep
## The relation and what it assumes

A sense resistor works because a current $I$ through resistance $R_s$ creates a voltage $v = IR_s$ ([[q0443]]). The scope then measures the voltage as a function of time. Assuming the resistor is ideal, the current waveform is $i(t)=v(t)/R_s$: the same shape, rescaled. That already tells you the scaling is purely a vertical-axis relabel.

## Worked example: a motor start

Suppose a small motor draws 0.4 A running and 6 A at start, and you place a 25 mΩ resistor in its supply return. Running: $v = 0.4\times0.025=10$ mV. Start: $v=6\times0.025=150$ mV. Power in the shunt at stall is $I^2R=36\times0.025=0.9$ W, so you need a 2 W (or at least 1 W with margin) part, not a 1/8 W resistor. A scope at 20 mV/div shows the start spike at 7.5 divisions, a good use of the screen, but the 10 mV running current is half a division. You would need a compromise: use two captures, or rely on a current probe or a DMM for the steady value. This is the main practical lesson: **a shunt has limited dynamic range on a scope** because both the biggest and smallest currents must fit within the 8 divisions of a roughly 8-bit display.

## Resolution and noise

An 8-bit ADC has 256 codes. If you set the scope to 5 mV/div (8 divisions = 40 mV), one code is $40\text{ mV}/256 \approx 0.16$ mV. On a 10 mΩ shunt that is $0.16\text{ mV}/10\text{ m}\Omega=16$ mA per code. So seeing a 5 mA sleep current is hopeless in that setup, but seeing a 2 A load step is easy. For small currents:

- Enable **averaging** (for repeating signals) or **high-resolution/ERES mode** (trades bandwidth for effective bits).
- Use a bigger shunt (the burden costs you), or an amplifier such as a current-sense amp ([[q0449]]) ahead of the scope.
- Turn on the **bandwidth limit** (20 MHz) to cut broadband noise when you do not need the fast edges.

## High-side versus low-side: why probe choice matters

With the shunt in the low side, the bottom of it is ground, so the probe ground clip and tip go on its two ends and everything is single-ended. The cost: the load's ground rises by $IR_s$ (e.g. 0.15 V in the motor start), and the sense element breaks ground continuity between circuits.

On the high side, both ends sit near the rail, maybe 12 V or 48 V, with only a 10 mV difference between them. If you clip a probe ground lead to one end you create a short from the rail to scope ground through its earth. A **differential probe** ([[q0995]]) measures just the difference. Using two single-ended probes with a CH1−CH2 math channel is a poor man's approach: the common-mode signal (12 V) is 1000x the signal, so a 1% gain mismatch between channels produces $12\text{ V}\times1\%=120$ mV of error, far bigger than the 10 mV you wanted. Differential probes are specified by CMRR for exactly this reason.

## Inductance of the shunt

Every real shunt has a series inductance $L_s$. The voltage is

$$v(t)=R_s\,i(t)+L_s\frac{di}{dt}$$

The two terms are equal at the corner frequency $f=R_s/(2\pi L_s)$. With 10 mΩ and 5 nH, $f \approx 318$ kHz, so for a switching converter with ripple at 500 kHz or edges in the ns range the inductive term dominates. An edge of 1 A in 10 ns gives $5\text{ nH}\times 10^{8}\text{ A/s}=0.5$ V, vs a resistive drop of only 10 mV for 1 A. Low-inductance (metal-strip, reverse-geometry) shunts push the corner up to MHz and beyond, and a current probe ([[q0998]]) avoids the problem by not being in the current path at all.

## Use the scope to compute power

Put the rail voltage on CH2 and the shunt voltage on CH1. A math channel of $\text{CH2}\times\text{CH1}/R_s$ gives instantaneous power in watts. Averaged over a cycle that is real power consumption. This is how you see that a converter draws bursts, or how much energy a sensor wake-up costs.

## Choosing between methods

| Method | Good for | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Scope across shunt | Time-domain current, same board, mA to tens of A | Resolution at low I, inductance at fast edges, high-side needs diff probe |
| Current probe ([[q0998]]) | Fast transients, no series drop | Cost, noise floor, loop adds inductance |
| DMM in series ([[q0982]]) | Accurate DC average | Burden, no waveform, must break the circuit |
| Current-sense amp + ADC | Permanent monitoring | Bandwidth, design effort |
:::

::: applications
- **Power-profile of an IoT device**: a 1 Ω shunt in the battery line with a scope shows sleep-to-radio bursts of tens of mA over milliseconds.
- **Motor stall and start current**: a 10-50 mΩ shunt with a scope reveals the inrush that sizes the fuse and driver.
- **Buck converter inductor current**: a small series shunt in the inductor leg (low-inductance type) shows ripple current and when it saturates ([[q0992]] can trigger on the saturation spike).
- **Hot-swap and inrush testing**: a high-side shunt with a differential probe shows the charge current into bulk capacitors.
- **Battery charge/discharge logging**: shunt plus DAQ for coulomb-counting reference.
:::

::: assumptions
- The resistor is a **pure resistance** at the frequencies of interest. Above $R_s/(2\pi L_s)$ it is not.
- The resistor value and tolerance are known, and it does not self-heat much. A 100 ppm/°C part at a 50 °C rise drifts 0.5%.
- The probe adds little loading and its ground does not create a second current path. For high-side sensing, a differential probe is assumed.
- The scope offset is zeroed, and the vertical scale is tight enough to use many ADC codes.

If the question is about a **permanent** circuit measuring current (feeding an ADC), the answer shifts to a current-sense amplifier ([[q0449]]) and the sense-resistor design questions in [[q0445]]; this question is specifically about reading it on the scope.
:::

::: followups
### What value of sense resistor would you pick?
It is a trade between signal and burden. Pick the largest value whose drop and power are acceptable at the maximum current: for example, to keep burden under 100 mV at 5 A, $R_s \le 20$ mΩ, and the power $25\times0.02=0.5$ W. Then check the smallest current you must resolve: at 10 mA you get 0.2 mV, which an 8-bit scope will not see well. If both ends of the range matter, use two ranges or a current-sense amp.

### The high-side rail is 48 V and the shunt drop is 50 mV. How do you measure it?
A differential probe, or better an isolated one if the scope ground matters. The common-mode is 48 V against a 50 mV signal, ratio about 1000:1. A probe with 80 dB CMRR (10,000:1) at the relevant frequency leaves a 4.8 mV error, about 10%, so I would check the CMRR at that frequency. Alternatively use a current-sense amp that rejects common mode, then probe its output, or a current probe.

### Why do I see a big spike on my sense voltage at switching edges that doesn't show on a current probe?
Probably $L\,di/dt$ in the shunt and its loop, and possibly capacitive coupling of the switch-node dv/dt into the sense probe's ground lead. Short ground leads, a low-inductance shunt, and comparison with a current probe tell which. The current probe will show the true shape.

### How do you measure a few microamps with a scope?
Not with a plain shunt. 1 µA through 1 kΩ is only 1 mV, so use a larger resistor, or an amplifier with a known gain in front of the scope, or a picoammeter/SMU. Also consider the DUT's behavior with a large burden: a 100 kΩ shunt at 10 µA drops 1 V.

### Your shunt reads 5% lower than the DMM in series. Where could the error come from?
Resistor tolerance and temperature, the sense probe point including some copper trace, scope vertical gain error (typically a few percent for an 8-bit scope), probe attenuation not set correctly (a 10x probe with the scope set to 1x is a factor 10, not 5%), or waveform that is not DC so the DMM is averaging differently.
:::
