::: testing
Whether you understand AOI as a camera-based *appearance* check: how it works (lighting, imaging, comparison with a reference or rules), what it can reliably find (presence, position, polarity marks, bridges, tombstones, lifted leads, some solder-fillet anomalies), what it cannot (hidden joints, electrical values, function) and the practical issues (programming effort, false calls, shadowing, resolution). A strong answer also places it in the line (pre- and post-reflow) and gives a number on resolution or false-call economics.
:::

::: why
AOI is the cheapest, fastest way to catch most assembly defects, and it needs no test points and no fixture. It also gives rapid feedback to the placer and printer. As a designer, you influence AOI success with clear polarity marks, adequate spacing so cameras see joints, fiducials, and not hiding fillets under large parts. Knowing its limits is equally important because it is blind to values and hidden joints, and that is why ICT, X-ray and functional test exist ([[q0604]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Optical imaging**: cameras with controlled multi-angle lighting capture images of each component and joint.
- **Reference comparison / rules**: the system compares images against golden boards or CAD-driven rule sets.
- **2D vs 3D AOI**: 3D adds height measurement (laser, structured light or multiple-angle).
- **False calls vs escapes**: good boards flagged vs bad boards passed.
- **Pre-reflow vs post-reflow AOI**: before reflow checks placement; after reflow checks soldering.
- **Fiducials and registration**: needed for positioning ([[q0596]]).
:::

::: answer
"AOI is a machine with a camera, or several cameras, and carefully controlled lighting, that scans an assembled board and checks it for visible defects. It's usually placed after the reflow oven, and sometimes also before it to catch placement errors while they're easy to fix.

It works by capturing images of every component and solder joint under lighting from different angles, and comparing them to a reference. The reference is either a golden board, or rules derived from the CAD data like where each part should be, its size, rotation, polarity marking. The software flags differences and a human verifies the flagged ones at a review station.

What it catches: missing components, wrong or shifted parts, rotated or reversed polarised parts if the marking is visible, tombstoned parts, solder bridges, insufficient or excess solder, lifted leads, and foreign material. What it can't do is see under BGAs and QFNs, check component values or electrical behaviour, so it's paired with X-ray, in-circuit test and functional test.

The main practical issue is tuning: if the thresholds are too tight you get false calls that slow the line down, and too loose you let defects through."
:::

::: technical
**Hardware.** Cameras (typically 5-25 MP area-scan, or line-scan), telecentric or standard lenses, resolution commonly 10-25 µm/pixel for general boards and finer for 01005 parts, multi-angle LED lighting (top ring, side, coloured light at different elevations to highlight fillet slopes), conveyor with board clamp. Field of view is stitched to cover the board. Example: a 4,000-pixel-wide sensor at 15 µm/pixel covers $15\ \mu\text{m}\times4000=60$ mm; a 0201 part (0.6 mm long) is 40 pixels long (computed).

**Algorithms.** Template matching and rule checks: presence (is there a blob of the expected colour/height), position and rotation relative to CAD, polarity (marking pattern), text OCR for part markings, solder joint classification from colour/intensity patterns under angled lighting, bridging detection between pads. 3D AOI measures actual height (coplanarity, lifted leads, tall parts, paste/solder volume).

**What it detects well vs poorly.**

| Detects well | Detects poorly or not at all |
|---|---|
| Missing/wrong/shifted/rotated parts | Component value (identical-looking resistors) |
| Tombstones, billboarding | Hidden joints under BGA, QFN, shields |
| Solder bridges on visible leads | Internal voids, cracks |
| Lifted leads (3D) | Electrical function, firmware |
| Polarity marks (if visible) | Parts with no or poor markings |
| Insufficient/excess solder (visible fillets) | Parts shadowed by tall neighbours |

**Quality metrics.** *False call rate* (good features flagged) and *escape rate* (bad features missed). With, say, 1,000 inspected features per board and a false-call rate of 0.1% per feature, expect $1000\times0.001=1$ false call per board (computed), which would overwhelm the review operator, so real systems target far lower rates (a fraction of a percent of boards flagged for false reasons). Escapes are controlled with test coverage.

**Placement in the line.** Pre-reflow AOI (after placement) lets you correct before the solder freezes; post-reflow AOI verifies the final joint. Many lines only do post-reflow.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Resolution, false-call targets and inspection speed vary a lot by machine and board. Claimed defect-detection percentages in vendor material depend on the defect mix and programming; treat any single coverage number with caution.
:::

::: short
"AOI is camera-based inspection of an assembled board, usually after reflow. Multi-angle lighting and cameras image each component and joint, and software compares them to a golden board or CAD rules to flag missing, shifted, rotated or tombstoned parts, bridges and bad fillets. It's fast and needs no fixture, but it only sees the surface: no hidden BGA joints, no component values, no function, so it's combined with X-ray, ICT and functional test."
:::

::: long
"Automated optical inspection is machine vision for circuit boards. After the reflow oven, and sometimes also after placement, the board goes into a machine with one or more high-resolution cameras and programmable lighting from several angles. The machine takes images of every part and joint and compares them with a reference: either a golden board it learned from, or rules built from the CAD data, like where each part should be and how it's oriented.

Different lighting makes different features pop. A low-angle ring light shows the slope of a solder fillet, a top light shows markings, and some systems use coloured lights. Newer 3D systems measure height too, which helps with lifted leads and solder volume.

It's very good at gross assembly defects: missing parts, wrong parts, shifted or rotated parts, reversed polarised parts if the marking is visible, tombstones, solder bridges and obvious insufficient or excess solder. It's fast, no fixture, no test points needed, and it gives quick feedback to the placement machine or printer.

But it's blind in several ways. It can't see joints hidden under BGAs or QFN packages, can't tell a 10 kΩ resistor from a 1 kΩ one if they look the same, and can't tell whether the board works. So I'd always pair it with X-ray for hidden joints and with ICT or flying probe plus functional test.

The practical challenge is tuning. Thresholds too tight give false calls where an operator keeps reviewing good boards; too loose gives escapes. Good fiducials, clear polarity marks and sensible spacing in the design help a lot."
:::

::: terms
- **AOI**: automated optical inspection.
- **False call**: good feature flagged as defective.
- **Escape**: defective feature that passes inspection.
- **Golden board**: known-good reference for teaching.
- **2D / 3D AOI**: image-only vs height-measuring inspection.
- **Telecentric lens**: lens with constant magnification across depth, reduces perspective error.
- **Shadowing**: tall part hides a neighbouring joint from the camera.
- **Review station**: where an operator confirms flagged defects.
- **Billboarding**: chip part resting on its side.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a camera checks the board for defects."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains how it works (multi-angle lighting, reference comparison, CAD rules, 3D height) and why lighting matters.
- Lists what it catches and, equally, what it can't, and names the complementary tests.
- Quantifies something: pixel resolution versus a 0201 part, or false-call arithmetic.
- Places it in the line (pre/post reflow) and mentions feedback to upstream processes.
- Mentions design aids: fiducials, polarity marks, spacing to avoid shadowing, and clear silkscreen.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Believing AOI tests electrical correctness.** It doesn't; the part could look perfect and be wrong or dead.
- **Expecting it to see under BGAs.** Hidden joints need X-ray.
- **Ignoring false calls.** They consume operator time and erode trust until people ignore real alarms.
- **No polarity mark on the board.** AOI can't verify polarity without a visible marking.
- **Programming AOI only once.** Library tweaks are needed when parts, vendors or paste change.
- **Assuming 100% defect coverage.** Coverage depends on defect type and how well the program is tuned.
:::

## Resolution and speed

Pixel size (µm/pixel) sets what's resolvable. A rule of thumb is that you need several pixels across the smallest feature of interest; a 0201 (0.6 x 0.3 mm) at 15 µm/pixel is 40 x 20 pixels, plenty. For 01005 (0.4 x 0.2 mm) you'd want about 5-10 µm/pixel, which needs a smaller field of view and more images, hence slower inspection. Inspection time scales roughly with board area divided by field of view area, plus stage motion; a board of 200 x 150 mm with a 60 x 45 mm field of view needs about $\lceil200/60\rceil\times\lceil150/45\rceil=4\times4=16$ images (computed).

## The false-call economics

Let $N_f$ be the number of inspected features per board, $p_{fc}$ the per-feature false-call probability. Expected false calls per board is $N_f\,p_{fc}$. At 1,000 features and $p_{fc}=0.1\%$ that is 1 per board, so every good board needs a review. If you tighten the system to $p_{fc} = 0.005\%$ you get 0.05 per board, i.e. 1 board in 20 sent for review. But a tighter-to-looser threshold trade also shifts the escape rate, so each setting is validated with known-defect boards (seeded defects) to measure both. This is why AOI programming is as much statistics as imaging.

## What the designer controls

| Design choice | AOI effect |
|---|---|
| Polarity marking visible after assembly | Allows polarity verification |
| Silkscreen not over pads | Prevents false calls |
| Spacing between tall and small parts | Avoids shadowing of fillets |
| Fiducials | Registration ([[q0596]]) |
| Exposed solder fillets (not hidden under bodies) | Allows joint inspection |
| Consistent markings on components | OCR and reference checks |
| Part colour contrast with board | Reliable segmentation (dark parts on dark boards are hard) |

## AOI before and after reflow

Pre-reflow AOI sees the part on paste: it checks presence, position and rotation, and the part can still be fixed by hand or re-placed with no rework. Post-reflow AOI sees the final joint: bridges, opens, tombstones, shifted parts that were pulled by surface tension, and wrong fillets. Some of the defects first show up after reflow, like tombstoning ([[q0615]]) and solder balls, so post-reflow AOI is the near-universal minimum.

## Where AOI sits relative to other tests

AOI is *surface appearance only*. ICT and flying probe ([[q0609]], [[q0611]]) measure electrical values and connectivity, X-ray sees hidden joints, and functional test ([[q0610]]) checks operation. Together they cover different columns of the defect table in [[q0604]].

> [!INTERVIEW]
> The strong closing line: "AOI is great at what looks wrong and blind to what is wrong inside or electrically, so it's one gate among several." Then mention false calls and escapes as the two numbers you'd track.
:::

::: applications
- **Post-reflow inspection on every SMT line** for missing parts, shifts, tombstones and bridges.
- **Pre-reflow placement check** on expensive boards where rework is costly.
- **Through-hole inspection after wave soldering** (solder fill, bridges), with different lighting.
- **Conformal-coating inspection** under UV light for coverage.
- **Feedback loops** to placers and printers: AOI offset statistics drive nozzle or stencil maintenance.
:::

::: assumptions
- Describes optical (visible light) inspection; X-ray is covered in [[q0607]] and [[q0604]].
- Resolution, speed and false-call numbers are illustrative.
- Assumes components have visible markings and joints; shielded, underfilled or potted assemblies can't be inspected optically.
- 3D AOI differs from 2D in the defects it can reliably identify (height-related).
:::

::: followups
### What can't AOI catch, and what covers the gap?
Hidden joints (X-ray), wrong values and electrical faults (ICT or flying probe), and functionality (functional test). Also marginal joints that look fine but are weak: those need cross-sections, reliability tests, or X-ray.

### Why do AOI systems use multi-angle and coloured lighting?
Different features reflect differently depending on angle and wavelength. Low-angle light highlights fillet slope and edges, top light shows markings and presence, coloured light separates solder from pad from board. Combining them makes defects distinguishable.

### How do you reduce false calls?
Improve lighting and library models, tune thresholds with real good and bad samples, stabilise the process (consistent paste, part markings), and design with AOI in mind (clear marks, spacing). Track false-call rate per feature and fix the worst offenders.

### What's the advantage of 3D AOI?
Height information: lifted leads, coplanarity, tall or missing parts, solder volume. 2D can mistake colour variations for defects and miss subtle height problems. 3D is slower and costlier.

### A board has dark parts on a dark solder mask. Problem?
Low contrast hurts segmentation and presence checks, giving false calls. Options: use coloured light that separates them, use 3D height, choose a lighter solder mask or silkscreen outlines, or tune per-part lighting.
:::

::: interpretations
"What can it capture?" means "what defects can it detect". Answer with both what it finds and its blind spots. Do not confuse AOI (visible light, surface) with automated X-ray inspection (AXI), which is a different station.
:::
