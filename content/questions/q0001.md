::: basics
### In plain words
Ohm's law connects three things you can measure on a resistor: the voltage across it, the current through it, and its resistance. It says voltage equals current times resistance, $V = IR$. Know any two and you can find the third. Engineers use it to predict how much current a given voltage will push through a part.

### Picture it
Think of water flowing through a pipe. Pressure is the voltage, the flow rate is the current, and a narrow section of pipe is the resistance. More pressure gives more flow. A narrower pipe gives less flow for the same pressure. The picture breaks down for parts like diodes, where doubling the pressure does not simply double the flow.

### Words you need
- **Voltage (V)**: the electrical "push" between two points, measured in volts.
- **Current (I)**: how much charge flows past a point each second, measured in amps (A).
- **Resistance (R)**: how strongly a part resists current, measured in ohms (Ω).
- **Resistor**: a part made to have a known, steady resistance.
- **kΩ and mA**: kilo-ohm is 1,000 Ω, and milliamp is one thousandth of an amp.

### The simplest example
Put 5 V across a 1 kΩ resistor. Current is voltage divided by resistance: $I = 5 / 1000 = 0.005$ A, which is **5 mA**. Double the voltage to 10 V and the current doubles to 10 mA. Keep 10 V but double the resistance to 2 kΩ and the current falls back to 5 mA. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows why the straight-line relationship only holds for some parts, and where the law shows up in real design work.
:::

::: testing
On the surface it's a freebie, which is exactly why it's asked. The interviewer is checking:

1. That you state it **precisely**: $V = IR$, with the right quantities and units (volts, amps, ohms), and that you know the three rearrangements without fumbling.
2. That you know its **scope**: it describes *ohmic* (linear) elements at a fixed temperature. Diodes, LEDs, lamps and transistors don't obey a single constant $R$.
3. That you can **use it instantly** in a real situation: an LED resistor, a sense-resistor voltage, a wire drop.
4. Composure. A one-line answer is fine; a confident one-line answer plus one sentence of nuance is what gets remembered.
:::

::: why
Every other circuit calculation leans on Ohm's law: dividers, current limiting, sense resistors, IR drop on power rails, pull-up sizing, trace and cable losses. In the lab, it's the tool you use dozens of times a day ("the rail sagged 150 mV and we're pulling 3 A, so there's about 50 mΩ in that path"). Interviewers open with it to calibrate you. If the basic answer is shaky, they'll keep the rest of the interview basic too. If it's crisp and you add the "only for linear elements" caveat, you've signalled you think about *when* equations apply, not just what they are.
:::

::: concepts
- **Voltage** ($V$, volts): the energy per unit charge between two points, i.e. the electrical "push". See [[q0059]].
- **Current** ($I$, amps): the rate of charge flow, $I = dQ/dt$, 1 A = 1 C/s.
- **Resistance** ($R$, ohms): how strongly an element opposes current; $1\ \Omega = 1$ V/A. See [[q0014]].
- **Linearity**: an ohmic element has a straight-line I–V curve through the origin; its slope is $1/R$.
- **Power**: once you know $V$ and $I$, power is $P = VI = I^2R = V^2/R$. See [[q0006]].
- **Physical origin** of the proportionality (drift of electrons that keep colliding with the lattice) is the conceptual follow-up in [[q0002]].
:::

::: answer
"Ohm's law says the voltage across a resistor equals the current through it times its resistance: $V = IR$. So if I know any two, I get the third: $I = V/R$ or $R = V/I$. For example, 5 volts across a 1 kΩ resistor gives 5 milliamps.

The way I think about it is that for a resistor, current is proportional to voltage. Double the voltage and you double the current, and the I–V curve is a straight line through the origin with slope $1/R$.

The one nuance I'd add is that it's really a property of *ohmic* materials, not a universal law like KCL or KVL. It holds for resistors and metal conductors at a fixed temperature. A diode or LED doesn't follow it, since its current is exponential in voltage, and a light bulb filament's resistance climbs a lot as it heats up. I use it constantly, though: sizing an LED resistor, working out the drop across a sense resistor, or figuring out how much a power rail sags through a trace."
:::

::: technical
**Statement.** For an ohmic two-terminal element,
$$V = IR,\qquad I = \frac{V}{R},\qquad R = \frac{V}{I}$$
with $V$ in volts, $I$ in amperes and $R$ in ohms ($1\ \Omega = 1\ \text{V/A}$). Conductance $G = 1/R$ (siemens) gives the equivalent $I = GV$.

**Sign convention (passive).** If current enters the terminal marked $+$, then $V = +IR$ and the element absorbs power $P = VI = I^2R \ge 0$. Flipping one reference direction gives $V = -IR$. A resistor never delivers net power.

**Linearity.** An ohmic element has a straight-line I–V characteristic through the origin, independent of the polarity and magnitude of $V$. That linearity is what enables superposition and Thevenin/Norton analysis ([[q0003]]).

**Microscopic (point) form.** $\vec J = \sigma \vec E$, where $J$ is current density (A/m²) and $\sigma = 1/\rho$ is conductivity. Integrating over a uniform bar of length $L$ and area $A$ gives $V = I\,\rho L/A$, so $R = \rho L/A$ ([[q0015]]).

**Limits of validity.**
- **Temperature:** $R(T) \approx R_0[1 + \alpha(T - T_0)]$. Copper has $\alpha \approx 0.0039$/°C, so its resistance rises about 24% from 25 °C to 85 °C. The law is still "true" at each instant but $R$ is no longer constant.
- **Non-ohmic devices:** diodes ($I = I_S(e^{V/nV_T}-1)$), transistors, gas discharges, varistors. Here you can define a *static* $V/I$ at one point or a *dynamic* (small-signal) $r = dV/dI$, but there's no single $R$.
- **AC:** generalises to $\mathbf V = \mathbf I\,Z$ with complex impedance $Z = R + jX$ for capacitors and inductors.

**Typical numbers.** 3.3 V across a 10 kΩ pull-up: 330 µA. 5 A through a 10 mΩ shunt: 50 mV. 1 m of 24 AWG copper (≈84 mΩ/m) at 2 A: ≈0.17 V drop.
:::

::: short
"Ohm's law is $V = IR$: the voltage across a resistor is the current through it times its resistance, so $I = V/R$ and $R = V/I$ as well. 5 volts across 1 kΩ is 5 milliamps. It means current is proportional to voltage, a straight-line I–V curve. The caveat is that it only applies to ohmic, linear elements at a steady temperature. Diodes and LEDs aren't ohmic, and a bulb's resistance changes as it heats."
:::

::: long
"Ohm's law relates the three most basic quantities in a circuit: $V = IR$. Voltage in volts equals current in amps times resistance in ohms. Rearranged, $I = V/R$ tells me how much current flows for a given voltage, and $R = V/I$ lets me back out a resistance from a measurement.

The intuition is proportionality. For a resistor, if I double the voltage, the current doubles. If I plot current against voltage, I get a straight line through the origin with slope $1/R$. A bigger resistor means a shallower line: less current for the same push.

I use it all the time in practice. If I want 10 milliamps through a red LED from 5 volts and the LED drops about 2 volts, the resistor sees 3 volts, so $R = 3/0.01 = 300\ \Omega$; I'd pick 330 and get about 9 milliamps. If a board's 5 volt rail sags 150 millivolts while pulling 3 amps, there's about 50 milliohms in the path. And a 10 milliohm current-sense resistor at 5 amps gives me 50 millivolts to measure.

The nuance I'd add is that Ohm's law isn't a fundamental law like KCL and KVL. It's a property of certain materials. It holds for metals and resistors at a fixed temperature. It doesn't hold for diodes, where current grows exponentially with voltage, or for incandescent bulbs, whose resistance climbs as the filament heats up. For those you can still compute $V/I$ at one operating point, but it's not a constant.

Finally, it pairs with power: once I have $V$ and $I$, $P = VI$, which is also $I^2R$ or $V^2/R$. So the next check after any Ohm's law calculation is whether the resistor can handle the heat."
:::

::: terms
- **Ohmic element**: one whose current is proportional to voltage (constant $R$) over its operating range.
- **Resistance ($R$)**: ratio $V/I$ for an ohmic element, in ohms (Ω = V/A).
- **Conductance ($G$)**: $1/R$, in siemens (S). Handy for parallel branches, where conductances add.
- **I–V characteristic**: the plot of current vs voltage for a device; a straight line through the origin means ohmic.
- **Passive sign convention**: current enters the $+$ terminal, so absorbed power $VI$ is positive.
- **Static vs dynamic resistance**: $V/I$ at a point vs the slope $dV/dI$; equal only for ohmic elements.
- **Temperature coefficient (TCR, α)**: fractional change in $R$ per °C, e.g. ≈0.39%/°C for copper.
- **IR drop**: the voltage lost across the resistance of a wire, trace or connector carrying current.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "V equals I times R" and stops, or recites it with the wrong units.

**An exceptional answer** does the following in about 30 seconds:
- States $V = IR$ *and* the meaning: current is proportional to voltage for a resistor, slope of the I–V line is $1/R$.
- Throws in one real number to show fluency ("5 V across 1 kΩ is 5 mA").
- Knows the **scope**: it's a material property (ohmic conductors at fixed temperature), not a law of nature like KCL/KVL. Names a non-ohmic example (diode, LED, bulb filament).
- Connects it to something useful: LED resistor sizing, sense resistors, IR drop on a power rail.
- Mentions power ($P = I^2R$) as the natural next check.

The interviewer is hoping you don't treat it as beneath you. Delivering the obvious answer cleanly and adding one insight is the move.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating it as universal.** Applying $R = V/I$ to a diode at one point and then reusing that "resistance" at another voltage gives nonsense. Ohm's law holds for ohmic elements; diodes need their exponential I–V curve or a small-signal model.
- **Unit slips** with prefixes: 5 V / 1 kΩ is 5 **mA**, not 5 A. Volts divided by kilohms gives milliamps directly, a useful mental shortcut.
- **Thinking voltage "flows".** Current flows *through* an element; voltage exists *across* it (between two nodes). Saying "the voltage through the resistor" is a red flag in an interview.
- **Forgetting temperature.** A copper trace or motor winding warms up and its resistance rises ≈0.4%/°C. "Ohm's law failed" usually means $R$ changed.
- **Forgetting the sign convention** when writing node equations, which flips terms in KCL/KVL problems. Pick reference directions first, then write $V = IR$ consistently.
- **Using it on an ideal source.** An ideal voltage source has no "resistance" defined by $V/I$; its current is set by whatever it's connected to.
:::

::: deep
## What the law actually says

Put a voltage $V$ across a resistor and measure the current $I$. Repeat at different voltages. For a resistor (or a metal wire at constant temperature) the ratio $V/I$ comes out the same every time. That constant is the resistance:

$$R = \frac{V}{I}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad V = IR \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad I = \frac{V}{R}$$

The law has three faces, and you should be fluent in all of them:

| Known | Want | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| $V$, $R$ | current | $I = V/R$ | 3.3 V across a 10 kΩ pull-up → 330 µA |
| $I$, $R$ | voltage | $V = IR$ | 5 A through a 10 mΩ shunt → 50 mV |
| $V$, $I$ | resistance | $R = V/I$ | rail sags 150 mV at 3 A → 50 mΩ in the path |

> [!TIP]
> Prefix shortcut: V / kΩ = mA, and mA × kΩ = V. So 12 V across 4.7 kΩ is 12/4.7 ≈ 2.55 mA with no exponents to track.

## The I–V picture

The best way to "see" Ohm's law is the I–V curve. An ohmic element is a straight line through the origin, and the slope is the **conductance** $G = 1/R$. Small resistance means a steep line (lots of current for a little voltage).

The diode curve is the key contrast. You can still compute $V/I$ at one operating point (at 0.7 V and about 5.8 mA it's ≈120 Ω), but move to 0.75 V and the current is about 40 mA, a "resistance" of ≈19 Ω. A quantity that changes by 6× for a 50 mV change isn't a resistance in Ohm's sense. That's why diode circuits use the exponential model, a constant-drop model, or a small-signal resistance $r_d = dV/dI$ around a bias point.

## Why it's "a law" but not like KCL/KVL

KCL is conservation of charge and KVL is conservation of energy (for lumped circuits). Those hold for *every* circuit element. Ohm's law is different: it's an **empirical constitutive relation** that describes how a particular class of materials behaves. Metals obey it extremely well over huge ranges of current because their conduction is dominated by electrons drifting at a velocity proportional to the field (the conceptual picture is in [[q0002]]). Plenty of useful things don't:

- **Diodes and LEDs**: exponential I–V curve ([[q0074]]).
- **Incandescent lamps**: tungsten's resistance rises steeply with temperature, so a cold filament draws an inrush current many times its running current. The I–V curve bends over as V increases.
- **Thermistors**: NTC parts are deliberately non-constant with temperature.
- **MOSFETs**: only in the deep triode region does the channel look like a resistor ($R_{DS(on)}$), and even that rises with temperature.
- **Varistors/TVS diodes**: almost no current until a threshold, then a near-vertical rise. That's their whole job.

In the AC world Ohm's law generalises to $\mathbf V = \mathbf I Z$, where impedance $Z$ is complex. A capacitor has $Z = 1/(j\omega C)$, an inductor $Z = j\omega L$ ([[q0024]], [[q0040]]). The *form* survives, which is what makes phasor analysis of filters and RLC circuits so convenient ([[q0009]], [[q0010]]).

## Temperature: the most common "violation"

For a resistor or conductor, $R$ drifts with temperature:

$$R(T) \approx R_0\left[1 + \alpha\,(T - T_0)\right]$$

Copper has $\alpha \approx 0.00393\ /^\circ\text{C}$. A copper trace that measures 20 mΩ at 25 °C reads about $20 \times 1.236 = 24.7$ mΩ at 85 °C. Precision resistors are specified with a TCR in ppm/°C (e.g. ±25 ppm/°C for decent thin film, ±100–200 ppm/°C for generic thick film), which is the same idea scaled down.

> [!NOTE]
> Strictly, Ohm's law still holds at each instant even as a resistor heats up; the *value* of $R$ changes. What breaks is the assumption that $R$ is a fixed constant you can use across operating conditions.

## Worked examples you should be able to do in your head

**1. LED current limiting.** 5 V supply, red LED with $V_F \approx 2$ V, target 10 mA. The resistor sees $5 - 2 = 3$ V, so $R = 3\text{ V}/10\text{ mA} = 300\ \Omega$. The nearest common E12 value is 330 Ω, giving $I = 3/330 \approx 9.1$ mA. Resistor power: $3 \times 0.0091 \approx 27$ mW, fine for any SMD size.

**2. Current-sense resistor.** A 10 mΩ shunt carrying 5 A develops $V = 0.01 \times 5 = 50$ mV. Power: $I^2R = 25 \times 0.01 = 0.25$ W, so you'd want at least a 0.5 W part for margin. This is exactly how current-sense amplifiers work ([[q0443]]).

**3. Wire drop.** 24 AWG copper is about 84 mΩ/m. Two metres of round trip (power plus return) at 2 A drops $0.168 \times 2 = 0.34$ V. Feed a 3.3 V load that way and it sees under 3.0 V. That's the classic "the board works on the bench, not on the long cable" bug.

**4. Backing out a resistance.** A 12 V supply drives a motor that draws 3 A while stalled. The winding resistance is about $12/3 = 4\ \Omega$. (While running, back-EMF reduces the current, so $V/I$ at speed is *not* the winding resistance, see [[q0342]].)

## Measuring it in the lab

An ohmmeter applies a small known current, measures the voltage and reports $V/I$. For low resistances (milliohms), the test-lead resistance is comparable to what you're measuring, so you use a **4-wire (Kelvin)** measurement: one pair of leads forces current, a separate pair senses voltage right at the part, carrying almost no current and so dropping almost nothing. That's Ohm's law applied carefully: you only want the $IR$ of the device, not the leads.

::: applications
- **LED current limiting**: $R = (V_{supply} - V_F)/I_{LED}$, e.g. (5 − 2) V / 10 mA = 300 Ω, use 330 Ω.
- **Current sensing**: shunt resistors (1–100 mΩ) convert current to a small voltage for a current-sense amplifier or ADC; 5 A × 10 mΩ = 50 mV.
- **IR-drop budgeting**: checking that a regulator's output still meets the load's tolerance after the drop through traces, connectors and cables.
- **Pull-up/pull-down resistors**: $I = V/R$ sets the current wasted when a line is pulled low (3.3 V / 10 kΩ = 330 µA). See [[q0020]].
- **Fault finding**: measuring a rail's sag under a known load to estimate path resistance, or a stalled motor's current to estimate winding resistance.
- **Heater and fuse design**: resistive heaters (PCB heaters, nichrome wire) are sized with $P = V^2/R$, straight from Ohm's law.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear, ohmic element**: true for resistors, metal wires and traces over normal ranges. Not true for diodes, LEDs, transistors, lamps, varistors.
- **Constant temperature**: $R$ changes with temperature (≈0.39%/°C for copper). At high power, self-heating makes the effective I–V curve non-linear.
- **DC or resistive AC**: with capacitors/inductors present, use impedance $Z$ and phasors; for very high frequencies, skin effect raises a conductor's AC resistance ([[q0056]]).
- **Lumped element**: the element is small compared with the signal wavelength, so "the current through it" is one well-defined value.

A valid alternative framing: some textbooks define Ohm's law only as the microscopic relation $J = \sigma E$, and call $V = IR$ its consequence for a uniform conductor. Both are fine; the interviewer wants $V = IR$.
:::

::: followups
### Is Ohm's law always true?
No. It's an empirical property of ohmic materials, not a conservation law. It holds very well for metals and resistors at a fixed temperature. It fails for semiconductors like diodes (exponential I–V), gas discharges, and anything whose resistance depends strongly on temperature or voltage. KCL and KVL, by contrast, hold for all lumped circuits.

### A 12 V supply is connected to a 1 kΩ resistor. What current flows, and how much power does the resistor dissipate?
$I = 12/1000 = 12$ mA. $P = VI = 12 \times 0.012 = 0.144$ W, also $V^2/R = 144/1000$. That's 144 mW, which is over the typical 0.1 W rating of an 0603 resistor ([[q0018]]), so I'd use an 0805 (0.125 W) only with caution, or more comfortably an 1206 (0.25 W).

### Does a diode obey Ohm's law?
No. Its current is approximately $I_S(e^{V/nV_T} - 1)$, so the ratio $V/I$ changes enormously with operating point. You can linearise it around a bias point with a small-signal resistance $r_d \approx nV_T/I_D$ (≈2.6 Ω at 10 mA, for $n=1$), but that's a local slope, not an Ohm's-law constant.

### How would you measure a 5 mΩ resistor accurately?
Use a 4-wire (Kelvin) measurement: force a known current, say 1 A, through one pair of leads and measure the voltage with a separate pair touching right at the resistor's pads. The sense leads carry almost no current, so their resistance doesn't add error. Expect 5 mV across the part, so you need a meter with good millivolt resolution, and watch thermal EMFs at that level.

### What if the resistor heats up during the measurement? (harder probe)
Its resistance changes according to its TCR. A 100 ppm/°C thick-film part warming by 50 °C shifts by 0.5%. For copper (≈3900 ppm/°C) the same rise is about 20%. So you either measure with a short pulse or low current to avoid self-heating, or you measure at the actual operating temperature if that's what you care about. Sense resistors for precision current measurement are chosen for low TCR (e.g. ≤50 ppm/°C metal-element shunts) for this reason.
:::
