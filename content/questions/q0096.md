::: testing
"How does it work?" is an invitation to show **physical intuition**, not to recite equations. The interviewer is looking for:

1. The **structure**: gate, thin oxide, doped body, source and drain of the opposite type, so the default state is two back-to-back PN junctions that block current.
2. The **field-effect mechanism**: a gate voltage above threshold repels majority carriers, then attracts minority carriers into an **inversion layer** (the channel) that bridges source and drain.
3. How **$V_{DS}$** then drives current through that channel, and why the current **saturates** (pinch-off).
4. Ideally, a link from physics to the equation: channel charge ≈ $C_{ox}(V_{GS}-V_t)$ per area, so current scales with $W/L$, mobility and overdrive.
:::

::: why
Every MOSFET behaviour you'll rely on in practice follows from this picture: why the gate draws no DC current, why there's a threshold, why on-resistance drops with more gate drive, why PMOS is weaker than NMOS, why a power MOSFET has a body diode, why the gate oxide is fragile, why leakage rises in modern chips. If you can explain the mechanism, you can reason through unfamiliar questions on the spot instead of hoping you memorized the right fact. It's also the foundation for the I-V curves ([[q0095]]) and regions ([[q0099]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **N-type and P-type silicon**: majority and minority carriers. See [[q0062]] and [[q0069]].
- **PN junctions and depletion regions**: why source/drain and body block current by default. See [[q0063]].
- **Capacitor physics**: $Q = CV$ across the gate oxide; field through a dielectric. See [[q0022]].
- **Carrier mobility**: how fast carriers drift per unit field; electrons are faster than holes. See [[q0068]] and [[q0070]].
- **Drift current**: current = charge density × velocity, with velocity = mobility × field.
- **Terminals and body tie**: see [[q0090]] and [[q0091]].
:::

::: answer
"I'll describe an N-channel enhancement MOSFET. You start with a P-type silicon body and two heavily doped N-type regions, the source and drain. Between them, on top of the silicon, there's a thin insulating oxide with a conductive gate on top. With the gate at zero, going from source to drain you hit N, then P, then N, so it's two back-to-back diodes and no current flows.

Now put a positive voltage on the gate. The gate and the silicon form a capacitor. First, the positive gate pushes the holes in the P-type body away from the surface, leaving a depletion region. Push harder, past the threshold voltage, and the field pulls enough electrons to the surface that it effectively flips to N-type right under the gate. That thin inversion layer is the channel, and now there's a continuous N-type path from source to drain.

Then $V_{DS}$ pushes electrons from source to drain through that channel. The more gate voltage above threshold, the more channel charge, so the lower the resistance. That's the triode region. If you keep increasing $V_{DS}$, the voltage between gate and channel at the drain end drops until the channel pinches off there. After that, extra drain voltage doesn't add current, so it saturates at about $\frac{1}{2}k_n(V_{GS}-V_t)^2$.

The key thing is that the gate never conducts: it controls the channel purely through the electric field, which is where the name 'field-effect transistor' comes from."
:::

::: technical
**Structure (NMOS).** P-type body ($N_A$), N+ source and drain, gate (n+ poly or metal) over oxide of thickness $t_{ox}$. $C_{ox} = \varepsilon_{ox}/t_{ox}$ (3.45 fF/µm² at 10 nm SiO₂).

**MOS capacitor regimes** (vs $V_{GB}$):
- **Accumulation** ($V_{GB} < V_{FB}$): holes pile up at the surface.
- **Depletion** ($V_{FB} < V_{GB} < V_t$): holes pushed away, ionized acceptors exposed, depletion width $W_d = \sqrt{2\varepsilon_{Si}\psi_s/(qN_A)}$.
- **Strong inversion** ($V_{GB} > V_t$): surface potential reaches $2\phi_F$, electron density at the surface exceeds the bulk hole density. Further gate charge goes into the inversion layer, not the depletion region.

**Threshold voltage.**
$$V_t = V_{FB} + 2\phi_F + \frac{\sqrt{2q\varepsilon_{Si}N_A(2\phi_F)}}{C_{ox}},\qquad \phi_F = \frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_A}{n_i}$$
For $N_A = 10^{17}$ cm⁻³, $t_{ox} = 10$ nm, n+ poly gate: $2\phi_F \approx 0.83$ V, depletion term ≈ 0.48 V, $V_{FB} \approx -0.98$ V, so $V_t \approx 0.34$ V (before threshold-adjust implants and oxide charge).

**Channel charge.** $Q_n(y) = -C_{ox}[V_{GS} - V_t - V(y)]$ where $V(y)$ is the channel potential.

**Gradual-channel current.** $I_D = W\mu_nC_{ox}[V_{GS}-V_t-V(y)]\frac{dV}{dy}$. Integrating from 0 to $L$:
$$I_D = \mu_nC_{ox}\frac{W}{L}\left[(V_{GS}-V_t)V_{DS} - \tfrac12V_{DS}^2\right]$$
**Pinch-off** at $V_{DS} = V_{GS}-V_t$ (where $Q_n(L) = 0$) gives saturation $I_D = \tfrac12\mu_nC_{ox}\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS}-V_t)^2(1+\lambda V_{DS})$.

**Numbers.** $\mu_n \approx 400$ cm²/V·s (inversion layer), $C_{ox} = 3.45$ fF/µm² → $k_n' \approx 138$ µA/V². At $W/L = 10$ and $V_{ov} = 1$ V: $I_D \approx 0.69$ mA.

**PMOS**: N body, P+ source/drain, negative $V_{GS}$ inverts to a hole channel; $\mu_p \approx \mu_n/2$–$/3$.
:::

::: short
"Take an NMOS: a P-type body with N-type source and drain, and a gate on a thin oxide in between. With no gate voltage, it's two back-to-back diodes, so no current. A positive gate voltage first pushes holes away, then, past threshold, pulls enough electrons to the surface to form an N-type channel linking source and drain. $V_{DS}$ drives current through it, and more overdrive means more channel charge and lower resistance. At high $V_{DS}$ the channel pinches off at the drain and the current saturates. The gate controls everything through the field, with no DC current."
:::

::: long
"I'll walk through an N-channel enhancement MOSFET, starting from the structure.

You have a P-type silicon body. Into it are diffused two heavily doped N-type regions: the source and the drain. Between them, on the surface, sits a very thin layer of silicon dioxide, and on top of that a conductive gate. So with no gate voltage, the path from source to drain is N, P, N: two diodes back-to-back. Whichever polarity you put across it, one junction is reverse-biased, so essentially no current.

Now the gate. The gate, the oxide and the body form a capacitor. If I put a small positive voltage on the gate, the field repels the holes, the majority carriers in the P-type body, away from the surface. That leaves a depletion region of fixed negative acceptor ions. Increase the gate voltage further and the field starts attracting electrons, the minority carriers, to the surface. At the threshold voltage, the electron concentration at the surface is as high as the hole concentration was in the bulk: the surface has been inverted to N-type. That inversion layer is the channel, and now there's a continuous N-type path from source to drain.

Beyond threshold, the channel charge per unit area is roughly the oxide capacitance times the overdrive, $C_{ox}(V_{GS} - V_t)$. So more gate voltage, more electrons, more conductance.

Then I apply $V_{DS}$. Electrons drift from source to drain. At small $V_{DS}$ the channel is uniform and the FET is a resistor controlled by the gate: triode. As $V_{DS}$ rises, the channel potential near the drain rises too, so the gate-to-channel voltage there falls and the channel thins. When $V_{DS}$ reaches $V_{GS} - V_t$, the channel pinches off at the drain. Electrons still get across, swept through the short high-field pinch-off region, but the current stops growing: saturation, at $\frac{1}{2}\mu_n C_{ox}\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS} - V_t)^2$.

So the whole thing is a voltage-controlled conductor: the gate field creates and modulates a channel, and the insulating oxide means the gate draws no DC current. A PMOS is the same with everything flipped, using holes, which are slower, so it's weaker for the same size."
:::

::: terms
- **Field effect**: controlling conductivity with an electric field rather than injected current.
- **Gate oxide / $C_{ox}$**: the insulator under the gate and its capacitance per unit area, $\varepsilon_{ox}/t_{ox}$.
- **Accumulation, depletion, inversion**: the three states of the surface under increasing gate voltage.
- **Threshold voltage $V_t$**: the gate voltage at which strong inversion begins (surface potential = $2\phi_F$).
- **Inversion layer / channel**: the thin sheet of minority carriers that connects source and drain.
- **Overdrive $V_{ov}$**: $V_{GS} - V_t$, which sets channel charge.
- **Pinch-off**: channel charge reaching zero at the drain end when $V_{GD} = V_t$.
- **Gradual-channel approximation**: assumes the vertical field (from the gate) dominates the lateral field, so the channel can be treated slice by slice.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "voltage on the gate creates a channel and current flows."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts from the **back-to-back diode** picture to explain why it's off by default.
- Walks through **accumulation → depletion → inversion**, explaining that the gate first fights the depletion charge (which is why there's a threshold, and why body doping sets $V_t$).
- States **channel charge ≈ $C_{ox}(V_{GS}-V_t)$** and derives (or sketches) the triode equation from charge × velocity.
- Explains **pinch-off** physically: gate-to-channel voltage at the drain falls to $V_t$, and why current saturates rather than stopping.
- Connects to practice: insulated gate (no DC current, ESD-fragile), $W/L$ sets current, PMOS is weaker because holes are slower, power MOSFETs are thousands of these cells in parallel with a vertical drain.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Electrons flow through the gate."** Nothing crosses the oxide (except tiny tunnelling leakage). The gate acts purely through the field.
- **"The channel is made of the dopant."** The channel is an *induced* inversion layer of minority carriers. Below threshold it doesn't exist (for enhancement devices).
- **"At pinch-off the current stops."** It saturates. Electrons are injected from the end of the channel into the high-field depletion region near the drain and swept across, like carriers crossing a reverse-biased collector junction.
- **Ignoring the depletion charge.** The gate voltage must first support the depletion region's charge; that's a big part of $V_t$ and the root of the body effect ([[q0091]]).
- **Confusing depletion *region* with depletion *mode*.** Every MOSFET has a depletion region under the gate; "depletion-mode" means a channel exists at zero gate bias ([[q0098]]).
- **Assuming the channel is uniform at all $V_{DS}$.** It's thickest at the source and thinnest at the drain once $V_{DS}$ is significant.
:::

The positive gate has pushed holes away (orange depletion region of ionized acceptors) and pulled a thin sheet of electrons to the surface (the channel). Without that sheet, source-body-drain is N-P-N: two back-to-back diodes, so nothing flows.

Without gate bias, try to push current from drain to source: the drain-body junction is reverse-biased. Try the other way: the source-body junction is. Either way, the device blocks. That's the "off" state, and it's why an enhancement MOSFET is **normally off**.

## Step 1: the gate as a capacitor

The gate, oxide and body form a parallel-plate capacitor with

$$C_{ox} = \frac{\varepsilon_{ox}}{t_{ox}} = \frac{3.9 \times 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}}{10\ \text{nm}} \approx 3.45\ \text{mF/m}^2 = 3.45\ \text{fF/µm}^2$$

Whatever charge you put on the gate, an equal and opposite charge must appear in the silicon just below the oxide. *What kind* of charge appears is the whole story:

| Gate voltage (NMOS) | Charge that appears in the silicon | Surface state |
|---|---|---|
| Negative | Extra holes pulled to the surface | **Accumulation** |
| Small positive | Holes pushed away, exposing fixed negative acceptor ions | **Depletion** |
| Above $V_t$ | Depletion stops growing; mobile electrons gather at the surface | **Strong inversion**: the channel |

Only the third kind of charge, mobile electrons, can carry current between source and drain. The fixed ions in the depletion region can't move.

## Step 2: why there's a threshold

Before the gate can attract a useful number of electrons, it has to (a) overcome the work-function difference between gate and body (the flat-band voltage $V_{FB}$), (b) bend the bands until the surface is as N-type as the bulk is P-type (a surface potential of $2\phi_F$), and (c) support the depletion charge that accompanies that band bending. Adding those:

$$V_t = V_{FB} + 2\phi_F + \frac{\sqrt{2q\varepsilon_{Si}N_A\cdot 2\phi_F}}{C_{ox}}$$

Worked numbers for $N_A = 10^{17}$ cm⁻³, $t_{ox} = 10$ nm, n+ polysilicon gate, $n_i = 10^{10}$ cm⁻³, 300 K:

- $\phi_F = V_T\ln(N_A/n_i) = 0.0259 \times \ln(10^7) \approx 0.417$ V, so $2\phi_F \approx 0.83$ V.
- Depletion term: $\sqrt{2 \cdot 1.6\times10^{-19} \cdot 1.04\times10^{-10} \cdot 10^{23} \cdot 0.83}/3.45\times10^{-3} \approx 1.66\times10^{-3}/3.45\times10^{-3} \approx 0.48$ V.
- $V_{FB} \approx -(E_g/2q + \phi_F) \approx -0.98$ V for an n+ poly gate on P-type silicon (ignoring oxide charge).
- $V_t \approx -0.98 + 0.83 + 0.48 \approx 0.34$ V.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> This is a simplified textbook calculation. Real processes adjust $V_t$ with channel implants, have fixed oxide charge, use metal gates with engineered work functions, and show short-channel effects that lower $V_t$. Power MOSFETs are deliberately designed for $V_{GS(th)}$ of roughly 1–4 V so they stay off with noise on the gate.

The physical takeaways are what matter in an interview: **more body doping or thicker oxide → higher $V_t$**, and anything that widens the depletion region (like raising the source above the body) raises $V_t$ further: the body effect ([[q0091]]).

## Step 3: the channel carries current

Above threshold, extra gate charge goes into the inversion layer. With a channel potential $V(y)$ along the channel (0 at the source, $V_{DS}$ at the drain), the mobile charge per unit area at position $y$ is

$$Q_n(y) = -C_{ox}\left[V_{GS} - V_t - V(y)\right]$$

The current is that charge × width × drift velocity, with velocity $= \mu_n E = \mu_n\, dV/dy$:

$$I_D = W\mu_nC_{ox}\left[V_{GS}-V_t-V(y)\right]\frac{dV}{dy}$$

$I_D$ is the same at every slice (KCL), so separate variables and integrate from source ($y=0$, $V=0$) to drain ($y = L$, $V = V_{DS}$):

$$I_D L = W\mu_nC_{ox}\left[(V_{GS}-V_t)V_{DS} - \tfrac12V_{DS}^2\right] \;\Rightarrow\; I_D = \mu_nC_{ox}\frac{W}{L}\left[(V_{GS}-V_t)V_{DS} - \tfrac12V_{DS}^2\right]$$

That's the triode equation. Every factor has a physical meaning: $\mu_n$ (how fast electrons move), $C_{ox}(V_{GS}-V_t)$ (how many there are), $W$ (how wide the road is), $1/L$ (how long it is).

With $\mu_n \approx 400$ cm²/V·s for electrons in an inversion layer (lower than in bulk silicon because of surface scattering), $k_n' = \mu_nC_{ox} \approx 0.04 \times 3.45\times10^{-3} \approx 138$ µA/V². At $V_{ov} = 1$ V, the channel holds about $C_{ox}V_{ov}/q \approx 2\times10^4$ electrons per µm².

## Step 4: pinch-off and saturation

As $V_{DS}$ rises, the channel potential near the drain rises, so the gate-to-channel voltage there, $V_{GS} - V(y)$, falls. The channel gets thinner toward the drain:

Figure: From the triode solution, Q_n(y) ∝ sqrt(V_ov² − (2V_ov·V_DS − V_DS²)·y/L). At V_DS = 0 the channel is uniform. As V_DS grows, it thins at the drain end, and at V_DS = V_ov the charge reaches zero right at the drain: pinch-off.

When $V_{DS} = V_{GS} - V_t$, i.e. $V_{GD} = V_t$, the channel charge at the drain reaches zero. Beyond that:

- The pinch-off point moves slightly toward the source, and the extra $V_{DS} - V_{ov}$ drops across a short, high-field depleted region next to the drain.
- Electrons arriving at the end of the channel are swept across that region by the strong field, much like carriers crossing a reverse-biased collector junction in a BJT. Current doesn't stop.
- The channel itself still sees roughly $V_{ov}$ across it, so the current stays at the triode value evaluated at $V_{DS} = V_{ov}$:

$$I_{D,sat} = \tfrac12\mu_nC_{ox}\frac{W}{L}(V_{GS}-V_t)^2$$

Because the pinch-off point creeps toward the source as $V_{DS}$ grows, the effective channel length shrinks a little and current rises slightly: channel-length modulation, $I_D \propto (1 + \lambda V_{DS})$.

## PMOS: same physics, flipped

A PMOS is built in an N-type body (an N-well in CMOS) with P+ source and drain. A gate voltage *below* the source by more than $|V_t|$ inverts the surface to a **hole** channel. Holes have lower mobility than electrons (roughly 2–3× lower in silicon inversion layers), so a PMOS conducts less than an NMOS of the same size. That's why CMOS PMOS devices are drawn wider ([[q0097]], [[q0103]]).

## Power MOSFETs: the same idea, built vertically

Discrete power MOSFETs use the same field-effect channel, but arranged vertically: the source and P-body are on top, the gate forms a channel along the surface (planar) or along the walls of a trench, electrons then flow down through a lightly doped N− drift region to the N+ substrate, which is the drain on the back of the die. Thousands to millions of these cells sit in parallel, giving an enormous effective $W$ and milliohm $R_{DS(on)}$. The drift region sets the voltage rating, and the source metal shorting P-body to source creates the body diode ([[q0101]]).

## Why the gate draws no current, and why that's both good and bad

The oxide is an excellent insulator, so at DC the gate is a pure capacitor: pA–nA leakage. Good: zero static drive power, enormous input impedance. Bad: the oxide is only nanometres (logic) to tens of nanometres (power) thick, so a few tens of volts can puncture it, and there's no conduction path to bleed off static charge. That's why MOSFETs are ESD- and gate-overvoltage-sensitive ([[q0102]]).

::: applications
- **Choosing gate drive**: knowing that channel charge ∝ $V_{GS} - V_t$ explains why a power MOSFET's $R_{DS(on)}$ is specified at 4.5 V or 10 V, not at $V_{GS(th)}$.
- **CMOS sizing**: hole mobility being lower explains PMOS-to-NMOS width ratios of about 2–3 in logic gates.
- **Threshold engineering**: low-$V_t$ cells for speed and high-$V_t$ cells for low leakage in modern chips come straight from the $V_t$ equation (doping, work function).
- **Flash memory**: stored charge on a floating gate shifts $V_t$; reading the cell is checking whether the channel forms at a given gate voltage.
- **MOS capacitors**: the same structure biased in accumulation or inversion is used as on-chip capacitors and varactors.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Long-channel, square-law** model with constant mobility, the gradual-channel approximation and no body effect (body tied to source).
- **N-channel enhancement** device; PMOS and depletion-mode follow the same physics with polarity or implant changes.
- Short-channel devices (sub-100 nm) add velocity saturation, drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL) and quantum effects. The qualitative picture holds, the square law doesn't.
- The threshold calculation ignores oxide charge and threshold-adjust implants, so treat 0.34 V as illustrative.
- A valid shorter answer at internship level is the qualitative story (fields, inversion, channel, pinch-off) without the derivation. Offer the math if they want it.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the channel N-type in an NMOS if the body is P-type?
Because it's an inversion layer: the gate field pulls minority electrons to the surface until their concentration exceeds the bulk hole concentration. The surface is electrically N-type even though it's doped P-type. That's why NMOS is built on a P-type body.

### What determines the threshold voltage?
The gate/body work-function difference, the band bending needed to invert ($2\phi_F$), and the depletion charge the gate must support, divided by $C_{ox}$. Higher body doping or thicker oxide raises $V_t$; raising the source above the body raises it further (body effect). Fabs tune it with implants and gate-metal work function.

### Why does the current saturate instead of dropping to zero at pinch-off?
Pinch-off means the *inversion* charge vanishes at the drain end, not that the path is broken. Electrons reaching the end of the channel enter a high-field depletion region and are swept to the drain. The channel still sees about $V_{ov}$, so the current stays at the value it had at the onset of pinch-off.

### Why is a PMOS slower or weaker than an NMOS of the same size?
Current is proportional to mobility, and hole mobility in silicon inversion layers is roughly a half to a third of electron mobility. So a PMOS needs about 2–3× the width to match an NMOS's current and on-resistance, which costs area and capacitance.

### Harder: what happens if you make the oxide thinner?
$C_{ox}$ rises, so more channel charge per volt of overdrive: higher current, higher $g_m$, better gate control of the channel (reducing short-channel effects), and lower $V_t$ for the same doping. The costs: lower gate voltage rating (field = $V/t_{ox}$), more gate capacitance per area, and below roughly 2 nm direct tunnelling leakage through SiO₂ becomes severe, which is why modern processes moved to high-k dielectrics that give high capacitance with a physically thicker layer.
:::
