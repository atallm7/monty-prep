::: testing
Whether you can **classify a load by how it behaves electrically**. The expected answer is "inductive loads", but the interviewer wants more:

1. You know what inductive means: current lags voltage in AC, cannot change instantly in DC, and energy is stored in a magnetic field.
2. You can say what that implies for a designer: current ramps, spikes at turn-off (flyback), the need for a freewheel path, ripple in PWM, power factor in AC.
3. You can add the nuance: a motor is *not purely* inductive: it is $R + L$ plus a back-EMF source, and it is a non-linear, dynamic load whose current depends on speed.
:::

::: why
How you drive and protect a load depends entirely on its type. A resistive load needs a switch; an inductive load needs a switch plus a freewheeling path and a rating for $L\,di/dt$; a capacitive load needs inrush control; a constant-power load can destabilize a supply. Relay and switch datasheets list different contact ratings for resistive and inductive loads. In an interview, naming the load type is the first step toward the drive circuit and protection a hardware engineer is expected to design.
:::

::: concepts
- **Resistive vs reactive loads**: a resistor's current follows voltage instantly; an inductor's current lags. See [[q0022]].
- **Inductor behaviour**: $v = L\,di/dt$, energy $\tfrac12LI^2$, current continuity.
- **RL time constant**: $\tau = L/R$. See [[q0010]].
- **Back-EMF source**: a motor includes a speed-dependent voltage. See [[q0341]].
- **Flyback**: stored energy must go somewhere at turn-off. See [[q0340]].
:::

::: answer
"Motors and solenoids are inductive loads. Both are coils of wire, so electrically each is a resistance, the copper, in series with an inductance, the magnetic field of the coil. What makes an inductive load different from a resistor is that its current can't change instantly: the voltage is L di/dt, so the current ramps up with time constant L over R, and when you turn it off it tries to keep the current flowing, which creates a big voltage spike across the switch, the flyback or inductive kick. So you need a freewheeling diode or snubber. In AC the current lags the voltage, so there's reactive power and a power factor below one.

A motor adds one more thing: back-EMF. A spinning motor generates a voltage proportional to speed, so it behaves like R plus L plus a source that depends on speed. That makes it a dynamic load: the current depends on the mechanical load and speed, high at stall and low at speed, and it can even regenerate. A solenoid's inductance also changes as the plunger moves.

So the one-line answer is 'inductive, with a back-EMF for motors', and the design consequences are inrush at startup, flyback protection, and PWM current ripple set by the inductance."
:::

::: technical
**Load taxonomy.**

| Load type | $v$–$i$ relation | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Resistive | $v = Ri$ | Current follows voltage instantly |
| Inductive | $v = L\,di/dt$ | Current lags; stores $\tfrac12LI^2$; spikes at turn-off |
| Capacitive | $i = C\,dv/dt$ | Voltage lags; large inrush into discharged capacitance |
| Constant power | $i = P/v$ | Negative incremental resistance (switching converters) |
| Motor | $v = Ri + L\,di/dt + K_e\omega$ | Inductive plus speed-dependent source, can regenerate |

**Motor/solenoid model.** $R$ in series with $L$ (and $E = K_e\omega$ for the motor). Time constant $\tau = L/R$ (about 1 ms for the example motor, $\tau = 0.15\ \text{H}/48\ \Omega = 3$ ms for the solenoid).

**DC consequences.**

- Turn-on: $i(t) = \frac{V - E}{R}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$.
- Turn-off: the switch sees $v_{sw} = V + L\,|di/dt|$. Without a clamp it rises until something breaks down ([[q0340]]).
- PWM: ripple $\Delta I = \dfrac{V D(1-D)}{Lf}$; for $V = 12$ V, $D = 0.5$, $L = 1$ mH, $f = 20$ kHz: $\Delta I = 0.15$ A. The inductance acts as an averaging filter.

**AC consequences.** $Z = R + j\omega L$; current lags voltage by $\phi = \arctan(\omega L/R)$; power factor $\cos\phi < 1$; reactive power $Q = I^2X_L$ is exchanged with the source. Small induction motors typically have lagging power factors around 0.7 to 0.9 at rated load.

**Other attributes.** Non-linear: saturation reduces $L$ at high current; $L$ varies with plunger position; $R$ rises with temperature (+0.39%/K). Dynamic: speed and load change the current. Regenerative: can return power.
:::

::: long
"Both are inductive loads. That follows from what they are, coils of wire. Electrically each one looks like the coil's resistance in series with its inductance, and a motor also has a back-EMF source that's proportional to its speed.

The key property of an inductive load is that its current can't jump. The voltage across it is L times di/dt, so when you apply a voltage the current ramps up exponentially with a time constant L over R, about a millisecond for a small motor. And the same law bites when you turn it off: the current wants to keep flowing, so if you open a switch the inductance forces a voltage as high as it needs to be, L times di over dt, across the switch. That's the flyback or inductive kick, and it's why every driver needs a freewheeling diode, a snubber, or an active clamp.

In AC the same property shows up as current lagging voltage, so there's reactive power and a power factor below one. With PWM drive the inductance averages the pulses, and the ripple is V D times one minus D over L f.

Then a motor has extra personality. The back-EMF makes it a dynamic, non-linear load: current is high at stall and low at speed, it depends on mechanical load, and above its no-load speed it generates, so current reverses. A solenoid's inductance also changes with plunger position.

So for design I'd say: it's an inductive load, so plan for startup current, flyback protection, current ripple and, for a motor, regeneration."
:::

::: terms
- **Inductive load**: load whose behaviour is dominated by inductance: current lags voltage and cannot change instantly.
- **Reactive power**: power exchanged back and forth with the source by an inductor or capacitor.
- **Power factor**: $\cos\phi$ between voltage and current in AC.
- **Flyback (inductive kick)**: voltage spike when current through an inductor is interrupted.
- **Freewheel (flyback) diode**: diode giving the inductor current a path at turn-off.
- **Time constant $\tau = L/R$**: rise/decay time of RL current.
- **Back-EMF**: speed-proportional voltage generated by a spinning motor.
- **Constant-power load**: load drawing fixed power regardless of voltage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "they are inductive loads" and mentions the flyback diode.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains **what inductive means** physically ($v = L\,di/dt$, current continuity, stored energy).
- States the **model** ($R + L$, plus back-EMF) and says why a motor is **more than just inductive** (dynamic, non-linear, can regenerate).
- Lists the **design consequences**: ramp-up, flyback protection, PWM ripple, AC power factor, contact ratings for inductive loads.
- Compares against **other load types** (resistive, capacitive, constant-power) to show classification skill.
- Mentions solenoid specifics: inductance varies with plunger position; latching and holding behaviour.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Motors are purely inductive."** The coil is, but the back-EMF makes the load dynamic and the resistance dominates at DC steady state.
- **Using the resistive rating of a relay contact for a motor.** Inductive DC loads arc more and have lower ratings.
- **Thinking inductance limits the stall current.** It delays the rise but at steady state only $R$ limits it.
- **Omitting the flyback path when using a low-side MOSFET** and then blaming a "mysteriously dying" MOSFET.
- **Assuming AC and DC behaviour are the same.** An AC solenoid's impedance (and its inrush) differs from the DC case.
- **Calling a coil capacitive or resistive "at high frequency" without checking.** Winding capacitance appears only above the self-resonant frequency (typically MHz).
:::

## Turn-off: where inductive loads bite

At turn-on the inductor limits $di/dt$, which helps. At turn-off it hurts: when the switch opens the inductor current $I$ cannot stop. With no other path, the inductor drives whatever capacitance is present until the voltage reaches a breakdown value; the peak is roughly $V_{peak} \approx I\sqrt{L/C_{par}}$ plus the supply. For $I = 12$ A, $L = 1$ mH and a parasitic $C = 100$ pF, that is $12\times\sqrt{10^{-3}/10^{-10}} = 12\times3162 \approx 38$ kV in the ideal calculation, which is why switches avalanche or arc long before that. The cure is to give the current a place to go.

## PWM and averaging

With a switch chopping the supply, the inductance integrates the pulses: the load sees the average voltage $DV$ and a triangular ripple $\Delta I = V D(1-D)/(Lf)$. For the example 1 mH, 12 V, 20 kHz at $D = 0.5$, $\Delta I = 0.15$ A peak-to-peak, small relative to 5 A average. The inductance is what lets a motor driver be a switch: without $L$, the current would chase every edge.

## AC view

In AC the load is $Z = R + j\omega L$. For a 50 Ω, 0.15 H solenoid coil on 60 Hz: $X_L = 2\pi\cdot60\cdot0.15 = 56.5\ \Omega$, $|Z| = \sqrt{50^2 + 56.5^2} = 75.4\ \Omega$ and the phase lag is $\arctan(56.5/50) = 48.5°$, a power factor of 0.66. That is why AC solenoid coils have an impedance that depends strongly on the plunger position (inductance changes), explaining their large inrush with the plunger out ([[q0336]]).

## Motors: inductive plus a source

The motor's equivalent circuit has a series source $E = K_e\omega$. That has three consequences:

1. **Current depends on speed:** $I = (V - E)/R$ (DC steady state).
2. **It is non-linear and time-varying:** $E$ follows the mechanics, so the electrical and mechanical equations couple.
3. **It can source power:** when $E > V$ the motor feeds the supply (regeneration), so a bridge driver or bus must handle reverse current ([[q0337]]).

## Compare with other loads

| Load | At turn-on | At turn-off | Protection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resistor | none | none | none |
| Inductor/motor/solenoid | slow ramp, then (motor) high stall current | flyback spike | diode, TVS, snubber, active clamp |
| Capacitor/bulk | large inrush | none | soft start, NTC, current limit |
| Incandescent lamp | cold inrush about 10x | none | soft start |
| Switching converter input | negative resistance | none | input filter damping |

## Why this classification matters for hardware

- **Switch rating:** relay contacts rated for a resistive load may fail on an inductive load of the same current because of arcing at break.
- **Driver choice:** MOSFET or H-bridge must tolerate the flyback energy and the stall current.
- **EMI:** switching an inductive current produces fast edges and ringing.
- **Current sensing:** inductive current is smooth; sampling is easier in the middle of the PWM period.

## Worked example: a 12 V solenoid valve and its release

Take a small solenoid valve with a 24 Ω coil and 40 mH of inductance with the plunger seated. These numbers are illustrative, but the arithmetic is the general pattern. Steady current is $I = 12/24 = 0.5$ A, coil power $6$ W and stored energy $\tfrac12LI^2 = \tfrac12\times0.04\times0.25 = 5$ mJ. The electrical time constant is $L/R = 40/24 = 1.67$ ms, so turn-on takes roughly $5\tau \approx 8$ ms to reach full current, plus the mechanical pull-in delay on top of that.

At turn-off the question is how quickly the valve lets go. With a bare diode across the coil (0.7 V drop), the current decays as $i(t) = (I + V_d/R)\,e^{-t/\tau} - V_d/R$. It falls to 50 mA (a typical drop-out level, vendor-dependent) after about 3.2 ms. Gentle on the switch, which only sees the 12 V supply plus 0.7 V, but the valve may stay open for several milliseconds longer than you want. Now add a 33 V Zener in series with the diode, so the coil is clamped at about 33.7 V while it discharges. The current falls almost linearly at $V_c/L = 33.7/0.04 = 842$ A/s, and reaches 50 mA in about 0.45 ms, seven times faster. The price is a switch-node peak of $12 + 33.7 = 45.7$ V, so the low-side MOSFET needs a margin above that: a 60 V part is the usual minimum, not 30 V. Without any clamp, the energy has to go somewhere in the switch itself. Five millijoules per cycle is far above what a small MOSFET's avalanche rating tolerates repeatedly, which is the usual story of a driver dying after thousands of cycles.

## Limiting cases and where "inductive load" stops being the whole story

- **Very low frequency.** When the drive period is much longer than $L/R$, the coil is effectively a resistor and the inductance is invisible except for the edges. That is why the same motor looks resistive on a bench supply and inductive at PWM rates.
- **Very high frequency.** Above the winding's self-resonant frequency the coil's turn-to-turn capacitance dominates and the winding stops being an inductor and starts looking capacitive. That is typically in the hundreds of kHz to MHz for a motor, depending on winding construction (vendor-dependent), and it is where common-mode currents through the motor frame appear in EMI tests.
- **Saturation.** Real iron-cored coils lose inductance at high current, so the current ramp steepens late in the rise. A relay coil at its rated current is usually below saturation, but a motor at several times rated current at stall may not be.
- **Position dependence.** For a solenoid, inductance rises as the plunger closes (the air gap shrinks). So the current has a characteristic dip during pull-in: motion generates a back-EMF-like term $i\,dL/dt$, visible on a scope as a notch in the current waveform. That notch is a free indicator that the valve actually moved.

## What you would see on the bench

1. Measure the coil with an LCR meter at a stated test frequency, and note both $L$ and series resistance. Inductance is a function of frequency and plunger position, so measure with the plunger seated and with it removed to see the range.
2. Probe the switch node with a scope (a good ground spring, not a long ground lead) with and without the flyback diode. Without it you will see a spike or an avalanche plateau at the transistor's breakdown. With it you should see a clean step to supply plus a diode drop.
3. Put a current probe or a small shunt in the coil path and capture the turn-on ramp. Fit the 63% point to extract $L/R$ and compare with the LCR meter. A disagreement means the coil has saturated or the supply sagged.
4. Run a PWM drive at two frequencies and verify the ripple scales as $1/f$.

::: applications
- **Relay and contactor coil drivers**: flyback diode or TVS across the coil.
- **H-bridge motor drivers**: body diodes and decay modes (fast vs slow) manage the inductive current.
- **Solenoid valve drivers**: peak-and-hold PWM with active clamps for fast release.
- **Industrial AC motors**: power-factor correction capacitors compensate lagging reactive current.
- **Automotive**: load-dump and inductive-load switching tests in supply protection design.
:::

::: followups
### Is a motor a resistive, inductive or capacitive load?
Inductive, because of the winding inductance, with a significant resistive component and a speed-dependent back-EMF. At DC steady state the resistance and back-EMF dominate; at switching and AC time scales the inductance does.

### Why can the same relay switch 10 A resistive but only a few amps of a DC motor?
Breaking an inductive current makes an arc at the contacts (the inductor forces current to continue), which erodes the contacts. DC arcs also have no zero crossing to self-extinguish, so inductive DC ratings are much lower. The usual remedy is a flyback diode or snubber across the load or the contact.

### What is the difference between a freewheel diode and a snubber?
A freewheel diode gives the current a low-voltage path so it decays slowly with $\tau = L/(R + R_d)$; it clamps the voltage to about one diode drop. A snubber (RC or RCD) absorbs the energy or limits $dv/dt$, trading a larger voltage excursion for a faster decay or lower stress.

### How does inductance affect PWM choice?
Higher inductance means lower ripple, so you can use a lower switching frequency. With low-inductance motors (coreless, small) the ripple is large at a few kHz, so drivers use 20 kHz or higher, which also moves the noise out of audio but increases switching losses.

### Scenario: your MOSFET driving a small 12 V solenoid fails after a few thousand cycles. What's your first suspicion?
Unclamped inductive turn-off: avalanche energy per cycle. $\tfrac12 LI^2$, say 5 mJ, dissipated in the MOSFET's avalanche each time exceeds its single-pulse rating or heats cumulatively. Add a flyback diode, and if fast release matters, a Zener or TVS in series to raise the clamp voltage ([[q0340]]).

### What happens to the power factor of an AC solenoid when the plunger seats?
Inductance rises when the gap closes, so $X_L$ rises, $|Z|$ rises and the current drops (the inrush falls to the holding current). The phase angle grows toward 90° so power factor falls.
:::
