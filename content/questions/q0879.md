::: basics
### In plain words
This shifter translates an open-drain line, such as I²C, between two supply voltages using one small N-channel transistor and two pull-up resistors. It works in both directions with no direction pin. Whichever side pulls the line low, the transistor passes that low to the other side. When both sides let go, each side rises to its own supply.

### Picture it
Picture two balloons, each held up by its own air pump, at different pressures. A pinch valve between them opens whenever either balloon is squeezed flat. Squeeze one and the other deflates too. Let go and each re-inflates to its own pump's pressure. The pumps are the pull-up resistors. The picture only holds for open-drain devices: a chip that actively drives its line high would fight the other side.

### Words you need
- **Rail**: a supply line, such as the 3.3 V or 5 V rail.
- **NMOS transistor**: a voltage-controlled switch. It turns on when its gate pin is more than about 1 V above its source pin.
- **Body diode**: a one-way path built into the transistor, running from its source to its drain.
- **Open-drain**: a device that can only pull a line low, never drive it high.
- **Pull-up resistor**: the resistor from a line to its own rail that makes the high level. See [[q0877]] for the voltage-compatibility checks.

### The simplest example
Tie the transistor's gate to the 3.3 V rail, its source to the 3.3 V line and its drain to the 5 V line, with a 4.7 kΩ pull-up on each side. When idle, the gate-to-source voltage is 0, so the transistor is off. If the 3.3 V side pulls low, the source drops, the gate-to-source voltage becomes 3.3 V, and it turns on, pulling the 5 V side low. If the 5 V side pulls low first, the body diode drags the source to about 0.7 V, so the gate-to-source voltage is 2.6 V and the transistor turns on. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers pull-up sizing and failure modes.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can draw the one-transistor bidirectional shifter from memory *and explain why it works in each of its three states*. The key insight is that the FET's gate is tied to the low rail, so it turns itself on when either side pulls low, using the source-to-drain body diode to start the process from the high side. Interviewers also check that you know where it fails: push-pull drivers, very low rails, and speed.
:::

::: why
This circuit is the standard answer for translating I²C, 1-Wire and other open-drain lines between 1.8 V, 3.3 V and 5 V. It costs one small transistor and two resistors per line, and it is bidirectional with no direction pin. It is also a good "can you reason about transistor operation" test, because the source and drain swap roles depending on which side drives.
:::

::: concepts
- **N-channel MOSFET switching**: on when $V_{GS}>V_{th}$; in a discrete FET the source is the pin bonded to the body (and the body diode's anode), so it must face the low-voltage side ([[q0092]]).
- **Body diode**: intrinsic diode from source to drain (anode at source) in an NMOS.
- **Open-drain bus and pull-ups**: devices only pull low; pull-ups create the high level ([[q0755]], [[q0785]]).
- **Level-shifting thresholds** ([[q0877]]) and general shifter design ([[q0878]]).
- **RC rise time**: $t_r=0.8473\,RC$ for I²C's 30 to 70% measurement.
:::

::: answer
"I'd use one N-channel FET, like a BSS138. The gate goes to the low-voltage rail, say 3.3 V. The source connects to the 3.3 V side signal, the drain to the 5 V side signal, and each side gets its own pull-up resistor to its own rail, around 4.7 kΩ to 10 kΩ.

It works in three cases. When nobody is pulling low, both sides are high, the source is at 3.3 V, the gate is at 3.3 V, so Vgs is zero and the FET is off. The 3.3 V side sits at 3.3 V and the 5 V side sits at 5 V.

If the low-voltage device pulls its line low, the source drops to about zero, Vgs becomes 3.3 V, the FET turns on, and it pulls the 5 V side low too.

If the 5 V device pulls low, the drain falls, but the FET is off at first. The body diode conducts from source to drain, pulling the source down to about 0.6 V. Now Vgs is about 2.7 V, so the FET turns on and pulls the source the rest of the way to the low level.

So either side can pull the line low and the other follows, while each high stays at its own rail. It only works for open-drain signals, not for strongly driven push-pull ones."
:::

::: technical
**Circuit.** NMOS (for example BSS138): gate to $V_L$; source to the low-voltage signal node (with pull-up $R_L$ to $V_L$); drain to the high-voltage node (with pull-up $R_H$ to $V_H$). $V_H>V_L$. A pull-up of 4.7 to 10 kΩ per side is typical for I²C; the I²C spec bounds it ([[q0755]]).

**Three states** (take $V_L=3.3$ V, $V_H=5$ V, $V_{th}\approx1.3$ V typical):
1. **Both idle**: $V_S=3.3$ V, $V_G=3.3$ V, $V_{GS}=0\Rightarrow$ off. LV node $=3.3$ V, HV node $=5$ V. The drain sits at 5 V, which is fine up to the FET's $V_{DS}$ rating, 50 V on a BSS138-class part.
2. **LV side low**: source pulled to about 0 V, $V_{GS}=3.3$ V, FET on, $R_{DS(on)}$ in the ohms range, so HV node follows to near 0 V.
3. **HV side low**: drain falls; body diode (anode source, cathode drain) conducts and pulls the source to $\approx V_F\approx0.6$ to 0.7 V. $V_{GS}=3.3-0.7=2.6$ V $>V_{th}$, so the FET turns on and the source continues to the true low level.

**Releasing.** When the pulling device lets go, both nodes rise. The LV node rises through $R_L$ (and through the FET while it conducts). The FET turns off once $V_S>V_L-V_{th}\approx2.0$ V, after which the HV node continues alone toward 5 V through $R_H$. This produces a two-slope rising edge on the HV side.

**Pull-up sizing, I²C fast mode** ($t_r\le300$ ns, $C=50$ pF per side): $R\le300\text{ ns}/(0.8473\cdot50\text{ pF})=7.1$ kΩ; with 4.7 kΩ, $t_r=199$ ns. Low-level current when the LV device pulls low: $3.3/4.7\text{ k}+5/4.7\text{ k}=0.70+1.06=1.77$ mA, within the 3 mA I²C sink spec. With 1 kΩ pull-ups it would be 8.3 mA, which is too much.

**Limits.** Open-drain or tri-state signals only. $V_L-V_{th}$ must leave real overdrive: a 1.2 V low rail with a 1.3 V threshold fails. Speed is limited by $R\cdot C$ rise (typically up to about 1 to 2 MHz with small pull-ups and low capacitance).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The BSS138's $V_{GS(th)}$ range and $R_{DS(on)}$ vary by manufacturer. Check the specific datasheet for the maximum threshold at your minimum $V_L$.
:::

::: long
"The circuit is one N-channel MOSFET, something like a BSS138, plus two pull-up resistors. The gate is tied to the lower rail, say 3.3 V. The source goes to the 3.3 V signal line and the drain to the 5 V signal line, and each line has its own pull-up, around 4.7 to 10 kΩ, to its own supply.

There are three cases. In idle, neither side pulls low, so the source is at 3.3 V, same as the gate. Vgs is zero and the FET is off, so the 3.3 V line sits at 3.3 and the 5 V line sits at 5.

Second case, the 3.3 V device pulls its line low. The source goes to about zero, the gate is at 3.3 V, so Vgs is 3.3 V. The FET turns on and conducts, which pulls the 5 V line low too.

Third case, the 5 V device pulls low. The drain goes low. At first Vgs is still zero, but the FET's body diode goes from source to drain, so it conducts and pulls the source down to about 0.6 V. Now Vgs is about 2.7 V, the FET turns on, and the source goes the rest of the way to a real low. So either side can drive the line low and the other follows.

For I²C fast mode with about 50 pF per side, 4.7 kΩ pull-ups give about a 200 ns rise, and the low-side device sinks about 1.8 mA, under the 3 mA spec.

The limits are that it only works for open-drain or tri-state signals, because a push-pull output fights the pull-up and the FET. It also needs the low rail to be comfortably above the FET's threshold, and speed is limited by the pull-up RC, so for fast SPI I'd use a translator IC."
:::

::: terms
- **Body diode**: parasitic diode between source and drain of the FET; in an NMOS the anode is the source.
- **Threshold voltage ($V_{th}$)**: gate-source voltage at which the channel starts to conduct.
- **Pull-up resistor**: connects an open-drain line to its supply and defines the high level.
- **Open-drain**: output that can sink current but not source it.
- **$R_{DS(on)}$**: channel resistance when fully on; adds to the low-level voltage.
- **Overdrive ($V_{GS}-V_{th}$)**: how hard the FET is turned on.
- **Rise time**: time for the line to go from low to high, set by $R_{pull}C_{bus}$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws the FET and says "it level shifts".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Ties the gate to the *lower* rail and explains the three states, including the body diode starting conduction when the high side pulls low.
- Notes that the channel conducts in either direction once on, and that the FET turns itself off when both sides are released.
- Quantifies pull-ups (rise time and low-level sink current) with real I²C numbers.
- States the limitations: open-drain only, threshold versus low rail, speed, and no help with push-pull.
- Mentions what to check: $V_{GS(th)}$ max versus $V_L$, $V_{DS}$ rating, leakage, and when to use a translator IC.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the gate on the high rail.** Then $V_{GS}=V_H-V_S$ is large even when idle and the two sides clamp together. The gate must go to the *lower* rail.
- **Forgetting one of the pull-ups.** Each side needs a pull-up to its own rail or it never goes high at its own level.
- **Using it with push-pull outputs.** A push-pull high on one side fights the other side's low driver through the FET.
- **Using a FET whose threshold is near the low rail.** With $V_L=1.2$ V and $V_{th}$ up to 1.5 V it never turns on; choose a low-threshold FET or a translator.
- **Over-sized or under-sized pull-ups.** Too small exceeds the 3 mA I²C sink limit; too large gives slow edges (10 kΩ into 100 pF is 847 ns, beyond fast mode).
- **Claiming it needs the body diode to work in both directions.** The diode only matters for starting conduction when the *high* side pulls low.
:::

Check the orientation: in a discrete FET the source is the pin bonded to the body, so it is fixed by the part, not by the circuit. It must face the LV node, because that node never rises above $V_L$ while the drain can sit at $V_H$. Flip the FET and the body diode would conduct from the 5 V side into the 3.3 V side whenever the bus is idle.

## Why the gate sits at the low rail

With the gate at $V_L$ and the source at the LV node, $V_{GS}=V_L-V_{LV}$. That quantity is:

- 0 V when the LV node is high (at $V_L$): the FET is off.
- $V_L$ when the LV node is pulled to ground: the FET is fully on.

This is the entire trick: the LV-side signal *is* the gate-source voltage, so the FET automatically conducts when the LV node is low and blocks when it is high, while the drain can sit at a different, higher voltage. The only complication is when the HV side pulls low first, because at that moment $V_{GS}$ is still zero.

## The three states, with numbers

Take $V_L=3.3$ V, $V_H=5$ V, $R_L=R_H=4.7$ kΩ, $V_{th}=1.3$ V.

| State | LV node | HV node | $V_{GS}$ | FET |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Idle | 3.3 V | 5 V | 0 V | off |
| LV pulls low | about 0.1 V | about 0.1 V | 3.2 V | on |
| HV pulls low (start) | 3.3 V falling | 0 V | 0 V | off, but body diode conducts |
| HV pulls low (settled) | about 0.1 V | about 0.1 V | 3.2 V | on |

**HV pulls low in detail.** The HV driver pulls the drain to about 0 V. The source is still at 3.3 V, held up by $R_L$. The body diode (source to drain) is forward biased by $3.3$ V, so it conducts and pulls the LV node down. The diode drop is about 0.6 to 0.7 V, so the LV node falls to about 0.7 V while current through $R_L$ is $(3.3-0.7)/4.7\text{ k}=0.55$ mA. At that moment $V_{GS}=3.3-0.7=2.6$ V $>V_{th}$, the channel turns on, and conduction shifts from the diode to the channel. The LV node continues falling to the HV driver's $V_{OL}$ plus a few mV of $I\cdot R_{DS(on)}$.

**Release.** When the driver lets go, the HV node rises through $R_H$ and the LV node through $R_L$. Both are tied together by the FET while it conducts. The FET turns off when $V_S>V_L-V_{th}=2.0$ V. After that, the LV node finishes rising to 3.3 V through $R_L$, and the HV node finishes rising to 5 V through $R_H$, independently. The HV edge therefore has a slower first segment while it shares charge with the LV capacitance, then a second, steeper segment.

## Sizing the pull-ups

Two bounds, both from the I²C spec's electrical rules ([[q0755]]):

- **Maximum** (rise time): $R_{max}=\dfrac{t_{r,max}}{0.8473\,C_b}$. Fast mode: 300 ns and, for example, 50 pF gives 7.1 kΩ; at 100 pF, 3.5 kΩ. Standard mode (1 µs) at 100 pF gives 11.8 kΩ.
- **Minimum** (sink current): the driver must hold the low level while sinking 3 mA, so $R_{min}=(V_{DD}-V_{OL(max)})/3\text{ mA}$, which is 0.97 kΩ at 3.3 V and 1.53 kΩ at 5 V.

When the LV device pulls low, *both* pull-ups conduct into it: $I=V_L/R_L+V_H/R_H$ (through the FET). With 4.7 kΩ and 4.7 kΩ that is $0.70+1.06=1.77$ mA, within the 3 mA sink rating. With 1 kΩ pull-ups it would be 8.3 mA, which would violate the spec, so you cannot just use "strong" pull-ups to speed up the edges.

Resulting low level on the HV side: $V_{OL,HV}\approx V_{OL,LV\,driver}+I\cdot R_{DS(on)}$. With 0.1 V from the driver and about 1.06 mA through a few ohms of $R_{DS(on)}$, that is about 0.11 V, well below any $V_{IL}$.

## Failure modes

- **Gate threshold vs low rail.** Overdrive $V_L-V_{th}$ sets $R_{DS(on)}$. At $V_L=1.8$ V and $V_{th}=1.3$ V the overdrive is only 0.5 V: $R_{DS(on)}$ rises sharply, the low level on the HV side grows, and the diode starting-voltage path (HV pulls low) leaves less margin. Use a low-threshold FET for 1.8 V and below, and always check *maximum* $V_{GS(th)}$.
- **Push-pull drivers.** If the LV MCU pin is push-pull and drives high while the HV device pulls low, the FET connects them: the push-pull high sources current into the low. It "works" only if the contention current is tolerable, which it usually is not. Use it for open-drain or for push-pull lines that are strictly one-direction-at-a-time with the pin released (tri-stated).
- **Speed.** The edge is $R\cdot C$, so I²C at 400 kHz works, 1 MHz is marginal, and SPI at 10 MHz or more does not. The FET's capacitances ($C_{iss}$, $C_{oss}$, tens of pF in small FETs) add to the bus capacitance.
- **Leakage.** At high temperature, FET off-state leakage and the pull-up/gate network can shift the idle levels slightly. Not usually significant.
- **Rail sequencing.** If $V_L$ is off while $V_H$ is on, the gate is at 0 V, the source floats low and the FET stays off, so the HV side idles high unaffected. If $V_H$ is off while $V_L$ is on, $R_H$ ties the HV node to the dead (0 V) rail, the body diode conducts from the LV node, and the FET then turns on. Now $R_L$ and $R_H$ form a divider from $V_L$ to ground, so with equal pull-ups the live LV bus idles at about $V_L/2=1.65$ V, right in the forbidden zone (lower still if unpowered chips on the HV side clamp it through their ESD diodes). A powered I²C bus can be killed by an unpowered peripheral on the other side of the shifter, so check this case when rails are switched.

::: applications
- **I²C bridging**: a 1.8 V sensor or PMIC on a bus with 3.3 V or 5 V devices.
- **1-Wire and other single-wire open-drain buses**: shifting DS18B20 lines.
- **Boot/strap and interrupt lines**: open-drain INT outputs crossing power domains.
- **Dev boards and shields**: the common four-channel "logic level converter" modules use one BSS138 plus two pull-ups per channel.
- **Debug connectors**: shifting slow reset or enable lines between target and programmer.
:::

::: assumptions
- Open-drain (or tri-stated) signals only, each side with a pull-up to its own rail. Push-pull signals violate this.
- $V_H\ge V_L$, and $V_L$ comfortably above the FET's maximum threshold.
- Numbers use a BSS138-style FET: threshold about 0.8 to 1.5 V, drain-source rating tens of volts, $R_{DS(on)}$ in the ohms range. Verify with the actual datasheet.
- Speed limit estimates are for ~50 to 100 pF bus capacitance and 4.7 kΩ pull-ups.
- If both rails can be equal or reversed (the "high" side is not always higher), the circuit still works symmetrically as long as the gate stays on the *lower* of the two rails.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the gate tied to the lower rail instead of the higher rail?
Because the LV signal is the source, and the FET must be off when it is high. With the gate at $V_L$, $V_{GS}=0$ when the source is at $V_L$, so the FET is off in idle; pulling the source low makes $V_{GS}=V_L$ and turns it on. A gate at $V_H$ would leave the FET on in idle ($V_{GS}=V_H-V_L>V_{th}$), shorting the two nodes together at $V_L$ level.

### What does the body diode do and what happens if it is missing (or the FET is a type without one)?
It starts conduction when the HV side pulls low first: it drags the source to about 0.6 V so $V_{GS}$ exceeds threshold. Without it, the LV node would stay at $V_L$ ($V_{GS}=0$) and the HV low would not propagate. MOSFETs always have an intrinsic diode; in practice avoid discrete parts that are made with it deliberately blocked.

### How does this change if the low rail is 1.8 V?
Overdrive shrinks: $V_{GS}=1.8$ V with a 1.3 V threshold is only 0.5 V, so $R_{DS(on)}$ is far higher, maybe hundreds of ohms, and the low level on the HV side degrades. Pick a low-$V_{th}$ FET (for example an NMOS rated at $V_{GS}=1.5$ V or below), or a dedicated I²C translator.

### Can I use it for SPI?
Not reliably. SPI lines are push-pull and fast (MHz). MISO/MOSI are driven high by one device, which contends with a low-driving device through the FET, and the pull-up RC limits edges. Use dual-supply buffers or a translator IC with a direction pin.

### What if I put extra pull-ups to "speed up" an I²C bus through the shifter?
Each pull-up adds sink current in the low state: $I=V_L/R_L+V_H/R_H$ flows into the pulling device. Going below about 1.5 kΩ per side (at 5 V) exceeds the 3 mA I²C limit. Use a rise-time accelerator or reduce the bus capacitance instead.
:::
