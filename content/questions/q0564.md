::: basics
### In plain words
To protect a circuit from outside interference, you build layers. At the connector, you add a surge-clamping diode and a filter so noise is stopped where it enters. At each sensitive input, you add a small filter and keep wires short. In the layout, you keep loops small and bond connector shells to the enclosure. Shielding comes last.

### Picture it
Think of a castle. The moat and gatehouse are the connector protection, and they stop most trouble at the border. An inner wall is the small filter at each sensitive input. The treasure sits in the middle, away from the gate. A tall outer wall, the shield, is costly, so it comes last.

### Words you need
- **TVS diode**: a protective diode that clamps a surge or static zap to a safe voltage.
- **Low-pass filter**: a circuit, such as a resistor and capacitor, that passes slow signals and blocks fast noise.
- **Corner frequency**: the frequency where a filter starts to cut. For an RC filter it is $1/(2\pi RC)$.
- **Ferrite bead**: a small part that acts like a resistor for fast noise, absorbing it.
- **Chassis bond**: connecting a connector's metal shell to the enclosure with a short, wide path.

### The simplest example
Put a 1 kΩ resistor and a 1 nF capacitor in front of a sensor amplifier. The corner is about 159 kHz, so a slow sensor signal passes. Noise at 100 MHz is about 630 times above the corner, and an ideal filter cuts it by about that factor (56 dB). Real capacitors lose effect at high frequency, so use short leads. At the connector, the TVS needs a short, wide return: a loop with 10 nH of inductance (how strongly wiring fights fast current changes) and a static edge rising 10 A per nanosecond adds 100 V.

That is the whole idea: stop noise at the entry, then harden the victim. The rest of this page covers beads, chokes, power-input filters and testing.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can design the *immunity* side of EMI: protecting a victim circuit. This is different from Q562 (reducing what your board emits), though many techniques overlap. The interviewer wants a layered approach:

1. **At the boundary**: connector filtering and transient protection.
2. **In the geometry**: small loops, short high-impedance nodes, differential signalling.
3. **At the victim**: input filters, low impedance, balanced sources.
4. **In the structure**: solid planes, partitioning, chassis bonding, shielding last.
:::

::: why
Products get tested against ESD, radiated and conducted RF and fast transients, and they live in noisy environments. Protection design is mostly cheap parts (a resistor, a capacitor, a bead, a TVS) in the right place with the right return path. Putting them in the wrong place, or with a long ground trace, makes them useless, which is a classic hardware-review catch.
:::

::: concepts
- **Source–path–victim**: here you harden the victim and interrupt the path. See [[q0565]].
- **RC and LC filtering**: attenuation above a corner frequency. See [[q0010]].
- **Ferrite beads and common-mode chokes**: lossy impedance at RF; CM chokes pass differential, block common mode.
- **TVS/ESD diodes**: clamp transients; effective only with a low-inductance return.
- **Differential signalling**: common-mode noise cancels at the receiver. See [[q0566]].
- **Chassis ground bonding**: giving transients a path that avoids the logic. See [[q0571]].
:::

::: answer
"I think in layers, starting where the interference enters. At the connector, every line that leaves the board gets protection and filtering right at the connector: a TVS or ESD diode with a short, wide return to chassis or ground for transients, a ferrite bead or common-mode choke for RF, and a small capacitor. Putting it there matters, because anything between the connector and the filter gets the full noise.

Second, I make the victim hard to disturb. Sensitive analog inputs get an RC low-pass right at the amplifier pins, with low and balanced source impedance, because RF will rectify in input junctions otherwise. I keep high-impedance nodes short and put guard or ground around them, use differential signalling for anything long, and use a solid ground plane so the loops are small and returns are controlled.

Third, partition the board so sensitive circuitry is away from the connectors and noisy sections, and bond connector shells to chassis with low inductance so ESD current goes around the logic, not through it.

And in firmware and reset circuits, add robustness: filtered resets, watchdog, brown-out, retry and checksums. Shield cans and enclosure shielding come last."
:::

::: technical
**Layered defence.**

1. **Boundary protection** at each connector: 
   - ESD/transient: TVS or ESD diode, clamp voltage below the protected pin's absolute maximum, capacitance small enough for the signal speed, with a *short, wide* path to the reference: a few nH of via/trace inductance adds $L\,di/dt$ (10 nH at 10 A/ns is 100 V) on top of the clamp voltage.
   - RF/conducted noise: ferrite bead (specified by impedance at 100 MHz, for example hundreds of Ω; at RF it's resistive/lossy), common-mode choke for differential pairs and power, pi filters (C–L–C) on supplies.
   - Chassis bond: connector shell to chassis ground with low-inductance contacts; Y-capacitor from signal ground to chassis for RF.
2. **Input filtering at the victim:** RC low-pass with corner $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$. With 1 kΩ and 1 nF, $f_c$ = 159 kHz and attenuation at 100 MHz is 56 dB in the ideal case; real capacitors lose effectiveness above their self-resonance, so use a small-value, small-package capacitor and short leads.
3. **Geometry**: minimise loop area (planes), keep high-impedance and low-level traces short, differential routing for long runs, guard traces or ground around sensitive nodes.
4. **Impedance**: lower the impedance of vulnerable nodes where possible: induced voltage is the same but the *current* injected, and the rectification sensitivity, fall with lower impedance. Balance the source impedances of differential inputs.
5. **Structure**: solid planes, partitioning, shield cans or enclosure last ([[q0569]]).
6. **Firmware/system**: watchdog, brown-out detection, input debounce, error-checking.

**Verification:** ESD gun, injection probes, handheld transmitter, near-field scans; compare before/after with a repeatable setup.
:::

Every wire that leaves the board is an antenna, so at each connector I put protection and filtering right there, before the noise can spread: a TVS or ESD diode for transients, with a short wide return path to chassis or ground, because the inductance of that return adds directly to the clamp voltage; a ferrite bead or a common-mode choke for RF; and a small capacitor. Second, protect the victim. For sensitive analog inputs I put an RC low-pass right at the amplifier pins. With 1 kilohm and 1 nanofarad the corner is 159 kHz, which is about 56 dB down at 100 MHz. I keep source impedances low and matched on both inputs so that anything rectified appears as common mode, and I choose amplifiers with good EMI rejection ratio. High-impedance nodes are kept short, with ground around them.

Third, the geometry. A solid ground plane keeps loops small, which reduces pickup, since pickup is proportional to loop area. Differential signalling for anything long or exposed, because common-mode noise cancels at the receiver. And partitioning: sensitive circuitry away from connectors, switching supplies and radios.

Fourth, the system. Reset lines get a series resistor and capacitor and the processor a watchdog and brown-out reset, with retries and checksums in the protocol. If the board is still susceptible, shield cans or a shielded enclosure, but I design assuming they're a last resort."
:::

::: terms
- **TVS diode**: transient voltage suppressor that clamps surges and ESD.
- **Ferrite bead**: component whose impedance rises with frequency, mostly resistive/lossy at RF.
- **Common-mode choke**: coupled windings that present high impedance to common-mode current and little to differential signals.
- **Pi filter**: capacitor–inductor–capacitor low-pass.
- **Chassis ground**: the enclosure's metal reference, to which shells and shields are bonded.
- **Y-capacitor**: capacitor from a signal/ground node to chassis to shunt common-mode RF.
- **Guard ring/trace**: grounded conductor around a sensitive node.
- **EMIRR**: amplifier's EMI rejection ratio.
:::

::: exceptional

**An exceptional answer**:

- Gives a quantified filter example (1 kΩ, 1 nF is 159 kHz, 56 dB down at 100 MHz) and notes capacitor self-resonance limits.

- Mentions firmware/system robustness and treats shielding as last resort.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the TVS far from the connector** or returning it through a thin trace; the inductance wipes out the clamp.
- **Using a ferrite bead as a cure-all.** At the wrong impedance or with a DC bias that saturates it, it does little.
- **Unbalanced input filters** (different R or C on the two inputs of a differential amplifier), which turns common-mode noise into differential error.
- **Large filter capacitors with long leads.** Above the self-resonant frequency they look inductive and stop filtering.
- **Grounding the shield at a pigtail** instead of a 360° low-inductance bond.
- **Relying on firmware retries alone** when the analog front end is rectifying RF into a persistent offset.
:::

## Layer 1: the boundary

Interference enters on wires. The best place to stop it is at the *connector*, because once it's on the board it spreads by capacitive and inductive coupling and shared grounds.

Figure: The TVS sits at the connector with the shortest possible return; the bead blocks RF from entering the board; the RC at the receiving end finishes the job before the amplifier. All the shunt parts return to the same low-impedance reference.

**Why the TVS return matters.** A clamp's datasheet voltage assumes the part is directly at the point of protection. A 10 nH loop in its return with a 10 A/ns ESD edge adds $L\,di/dt$ = 100 V above the clamp voltage, which can exceed the protected pin's rating. A short, wide return (or via directly to the plane under the pad) drops this to a few volts.

**Beads and chokes.** A ferrite bead is specified by its impedance at 100 MHz (for example 120 Ω to several hundred Ω for small parts). At low frequency it is almost a short; at RF it is resistive and absorbs energy rather than reflecting it. A common-mode choke has two windings on one core: differential current produces opposing flux and sees little impedance, while common-mode current adds flux and sees a high impedance. Use CM chokes on USB, Ethernet, CAN and power inputs. Check the bead's DC current rating: the impedance falls near saturation.

**Pi filters on power inputs.** An L–C section gives 40 dB/decade above its corner; a C–L–C pi is third order, up to 60 dB/decade with resistive source and load impedances (less in practice, since a stiff source makes the first capacitor nearly useless and parasitics flatten the slope). Either keeps conducted noise out of (and off of) the supply.

## Layer 2: the victim

### RC filter at the input

Figure: An ideal single-pole filter gives 20 dB per decade: 56 dB down at 100 MHz. Real capacitors have a self-resonant frequency (typically 100 MHz to a few hundred MHz for small 0402 or 0603 parts of 1 nF), above which they act as inductors and the attenuation stops improving, so keep the leads short and consider a second stage or a bead.

The corner must be above the wanted signal's bandwidth (a 159 kHz corner is fine for a slowly varying sensor), and low enough to remove RF. The series resistor also limits the current into the amplifier's ESD diodes during a transient.

### Impedance and balance

Rectified RF error depends on the RF voltage at the junction. Filtering reduces it; so does a low source impedance. And for a differential amplifier, matching the source impedances on both inputs makes the rectified error common-mode, which the amplifier rejects. A filter with 1 kΩ on one side and 1.1 kΩ on the other converts 10 % of the common-mode RF to differential.

### Amplifier choice

Parts with a specified EMIRR and with integrated RF filters on the inputs are much better in noisy environments. Compare the datasheet figures at the frequency of concern (for example 900 MHz to 2.4 GHz for cellular and Wi-Fi).

## Layer 3: geometry and signalling

- **Small loops**: induced voltage $v = 2\pi fEA/c$ is proportional to area ([[q0563]]). Planes under traces, return via next to signal via.
- **Differential signalling** for long runs: noise couples equally into both wires, and the receiver rejects the common part. Try it below with the simulation.

Figure: At the default 0.5 V of common-mode noise the single-ended receiver already misreads over a third of the bits while the differential receiver decodes every one; lower the noise below the 0.2 V single-ended swing to see where single-ended stops failing. Now add skew between the two lines to see mode conversion: a small timing or imbalance difference turns common-mode noise into a differential error, which is why pair matching matters.

- **Short high-impedance traces**, with ground (or a guard trace tied to the node's potential for leakage-sensitive circuits) around them.
- **Twisted pair** for off-board wiring of sensors: cancels magnetic pickup by alternating loop orientation.

## Layer 4: structure and chassis

- **Solid planes** control return paths and reduce loop areas.
- **Partition**: connectors and noisy areas on one side, sensitive analog away from both.
- **Chassis bonding**: connector shells and shield terminations bond to the enclosure through short, wide connections (spring fingers, screws, multiple vias), so an ESD discharge current flows in the chassis, not through the signal ground ([[q0571]]).
- **Y-capacitors**, small (for example 1–10 nF) from signal ground to chassis, give RF a path out of the cable.
- **Shielding** of a sensitive block ([[q0569]]) or of the whole product.

## Layer 5: firmware and system

Make the system tolerate residual upsets: watchdog timer, brown-out reset, filtered reset line (series resistor and capacitor), debounce on inputs, input validation, checksums and retries on communication, periodic re-initialisation of peripherals. These do not fix a front end that rectifies RF, but they stop a glitch from becoming a crash.

## Worked example

An ECG front end sees 1 mV signals; a phone at 900 MHz induces 20 mV in a 10 cm² loop. Shrinking the loop to 1 cm² gives 2 mV. A 1 kΩ/1 nF RC at the amplifier pins is ideally 75 dB down at 900 MHz ($20\log(900\text{ MHz}/159\text{ kHz})$), though capacitor self-resonance means the real figure is far smaller, and a matched source impedance makes the remaining rectified error common-mode. Together with an amplifier with high EMIRR, the buzz is below the signal.
:::

::: applications
- **USB, Ethernet and HDMI ports**: ESD arrays, common-mode chokes and shell bonding.
- **Sensor front ends (ECG, thermocouple, strain gauge)**: RC input filters, matched impedances, guarded high-impedance nodes.
- **Automotive and industrial I/O**: TVS and RC filters on each input, reverse-polarity and surge protection, chassis bonding.
- **Power inputs**: pi filters, common-mode chokes and TVS to pass conducted immunity tests.
- **Reset and interrupt lines in microcontroller boards**: series resistor, capacitor and Schmitt input.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Component values are examples**; real design uses the signal bandwidth, transient levels and part ratings.
- **Capacitor self-resonance** limits real RC filters at RF; the ideal 56 dB figure is a ceiling.
- **ESD current slope** (10 A/ns) is illustrative for a human-body-model-style event; the standard test generator defines the real waveform.
- **A chassis exists.** In ungrounded plastic products the transient path is different (the "ground" is the board itself and the discharge couples capacitively), requiring more attention to layout and TVS placement.
- **Alternatives**: opto-isolation or isolated interfaces break the ground path entirely for the toughest environments.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the TVS need a short return path?
A transient has very fast edges (about 1 ns). The loop inductance of the TVS and its return adds $L\,di/dt$ to the clamp voltage: 10 nH and 10 A/ns gives 100 V. With a short, wide return that falls to a few volts. Place the TVS right at the connector pin and via straight to the ground plane or chassis.

### Ferrite bead or common-mode choke: when each?
A bead is a series impedance, in a single line, good for filtering RF on power rails and single-ended signals. A common-mode choke has two windings and rejects CM current while passing differential current, so it suits pairs (USB, Ethernet, CAN) and two-wire power inputs. The bead also impedes the signal; the choke leaves the differential signal alone.

### How do you protect a high-impedance sensor input against RF?
RC low-pass at the amplifier pins (corner above the signal bandwidth), short leads, matched source impedances to the two inputs so the rectified error is common-mode, low-impedance reference nodes, guard or ground around the traces, and an amplifier with specified EMIRR. And a shield or at least a ground pour over the front end if necessary.

### Why twisted pair and differential signalling?
Twisting makes adjacent loops pick up opposite-sign induced voltages that cancel, and the differential receiver subtracts noise that appears equally on both wires. The result is rejection of both magnetic and common-mode pickup. It depends on balance: skew or unequal impedance converts common mode to differential.

### Scenario: your device resets when a nearby motor starts, but only with the long sensor cable attached. What do you do?
The cable is bringing in common-mode or conducted noise (or the motor's current spike raises the ground). I'd put a CM choke or ferrite and capacitor at the cable connector, bond the cable shield to chassis at the connector with a 360° contact, and check the reset line (series R, capacitor, Schmitt input) and the supply (pi filter, brown-out threshold). Then verify with a scope on the reset and supply while starting the motor.
:::
