::: testing
Whether you can explain the **terms** and not just the trade-offs: what is "open" in open-drain, what is "pushed" and "pulled" in push-pull, what the three output states are, and what happens on the wire in each case. This page is the definitions-and-mechanism view; the head-to-head comparison is [[q0784]].

Hidden sub-skills: reading a transistor-level output stage, truth tables including high-Z, the idea of a wired-AND, and knowing the pull-up is a separate component.
:::

::: why
Datasheets say "open-drain output" or "push-pull output" in the first line of a pin description, and a wrong guess means a missing pull-up resistor or a dead bus. Reset pins, interrupt outputs, comparators, I2C pins and level shifters all use these words. Being able to say exactly what they mean in terms of transistors is a cheap way to show you can read a schematic and a datasheet properly.
:::

::: concepts
- **Drain / collector**: the output terminal of the pull-down transistor. In open-drain it connects to nothing inside the chip. See [[q0089]].
- **Push-pull**: one transistor pushes the pin up to $V_{DD}$, the other pulls it down to ground. See [[q0783]].
- **Open-drain**: only the pull-down exists. "Open" means the drain is left open for the outside world to connect.
- **Pull-up resistor**: the external part that supplies the high level. See [[q0786]].
- **High-impedance (released) state**: the open-drain output with its transistor off.
- **Wired-AND / wired-OR**: sharing a line among open-drain outputs. See [[q0788]] for why push-pull cannot do this.
:::

::: answer
"Both describe how the output stage of a pin is built.

Push-pull means two transistors: a PMOS connecting the pin to VDD and an NMOS connecting it to ground. To output a 1, the PMOS turns on, which pushes the pin up; to output a 0, the NMOS turns on, which pulls it down. One of them is always on, so the pin is always actively driven and has a low impedance in both states.

Open-drain means there's only the NMOS, and its drain goes to the pin with nothing else attached inside the chip, that's the 'open' part. To output a 0, the NMOS turns on and pulls the pin to ground. To output a 1, the NMOS turns off, and the pin is just released, floating. It only goes high if something outside, a pull-up resistor to some voltage, pulls it up.

The practical consequences: with open-drain I need that external resistor, the high level is set by whatever voltage the resistor goes to, and several open-drain outputs can share one line because none of them ever fights. That's the wired-AND used on I2C, interrupts and resets. Push-pull needs no resistor and is faster, but two of them can't safely share a line."
:::

::: technical
**Push-pull output stage.** PMOS from $V_{DD}$ to pad, NMOS from pad to ground. Output levels: high $=V_{DD}-I\,R_{on,p}$, low $=I\,R_{on,n}$. Output impedance low in both states (tens of ohms). Optional third state: both off (high-Z), as in a tri-state pad ([[q0783]]).

**Open-drain output stage.** NMOS only. States: *low* (NMOS on, pad at $I\,R_{on,n}$, low impedance) and *released* (NMOS off, pad high-impedance, voltage set externally). With a pull-up $R_{pu}$ to $V_{pull}$ and load current $I_L$: $V_{high}=V_{pull}-I_LR_{pu}$.

**Truth tables.**

| Input (gate drive) | Push-pull pad | Open-drain pad (with pull-up) |
|---|---|---|
| NMOS on / logic 0 | low, driven | low, driven |
| NMOS off / logic 1 | high, driven by PMOS | high, pulled by $R_{pu}$ (weak) |
| output disabled | high-Z (tri-state variant) | n/a, same as logic 1 (released) |

**Wired-AND.** For open-drain outputs $A$ and $B$ sharing a line with one pull-up: line $=A\cdot B$ in positive logic (low if either pulls low). In active-low interpretation this is a wired-OR of the requests, as with a shared interrupt.

**Open-collector** is the same idea with an NPN: collector open, saturation voltage $V_{CE(sat)}\approx0.1$ to 0.3 V as the low level.

**Rise time asymmetry.** Push-pull: both edges driven, $\tau\approx R_{on}C$. Open-drain: rising edge $\tau=R_{pu}C$, much slower. See [[q0784]] for numbers.

**Pad voltage limits.** An open-drain pad pulled up to a higher voltage than the chip's supply is safe only if it is rated for it, since the pad is a standard NMOS plus ESD structure (some have no diode to $V_{DD}$ for this reason).
:::

::: short
"Push-pull means the output has two transistors: a PMOS that pushes the pin up to VDD and an NMOS that pulls it to ground, so it actively drives both high and low. Open-drain means there's only the NMOS, and its drain is left open: it can pull the pin low, or release it. To get a high you need an external pull-up resistor. So push-pull is fast and needs nothing else, while open-drain needs a resistor but lets several outputs share a line, with the line low if any one pulls low."
:::

::: long
"Both terms describe the output stage of a pin.

Push-pull has two transistors in a totem pole: a PMOS from VDD to the pin and an NMOS from the pin to ground. For a 1, the PMOS conducts and pushes the pin up to VDD with the NMOS off. For a 0, the NMOS conducts and pulls the pin down with the PMOS off. Exactly one is on, so the pin is always actively driven through a low impedance, a few tens of ohms, both high and low. No external parts needed, and both edges are fast.

Open-drain has only the NMOS. 'Drain' because it's a MOSFET drain that goes to the pin, and 'open' because nothing inside the chip connects that drain to anything else: it's left open for the outside world to complete. The NMOS can pull the pin to ground for a 0. For a 1, the NMOS just turns off, and the pin is released, in effect high impedance. It only reaches a high level if something external, a pull-up resistor to some voltage, pulls it there. The bipolar version is open-collector.

What follows from that? One: I always need the pull-up resistor, usually a few kilohms, and its value sets the rise time. Two: the high level is whatever the pull-up goes to, which can differ from the chip's own supply, so it can translate levels. Three: several open-drain outputs can connect to one wire safely, since none of them ever drives high, and the line is low if any one of them is low, a wired-AND. That's why I2C, shared interrupt lines and reset lines use it. A push-pull pin can't do this, because two of them in disagreement would short VDD to ground through the transistors."
:::

::: terms
- **Totem pole**: the stacked PMOS-over-NMOS arrangement of a push-pull output.
- **Open-drain**: output with a MOSFET drain brought to the pin and no internal pull-up.
- **Open-collector**: the bipolar equivalent with an NPN collector at the pin.
- **Pull-up resistor**: external resistor from the line to the logic-high voltage.
- **Released / high-Z**: transistor off, pin neither sourcing nor sinking.
- **Wired-AND**: connection of open-drain outputs that makes a line low if any output is low.
- **$V_{OL}$**: low-level output voltage: $I\,R_{on}$ or $V_{CE(sat)}$.
- **Active-low signal**: a signal asserted by pulling it to 0, typical for open-drain lines.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "push-pull drives high and low; open-drain needs a pull-up".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the words: what is pushed and pulled, and that "open" means the drain is open to the outside.
- Describes the three states in transistor terms (driven high, driven low, released).
- Gives the wired-AND consequence and the equation for the line level with a load: $V_{pull}-I_LR_{pu}$.
- Connects to concrete parts: I2C, shared IRQ, reset, comparators with open-drain outputs, level shifting.

:::

::: mistakes
- **"Open-drain" means the pin is not connected.** It means the drain terminal is brought out without an internal pull-up. The pin still pulls low strongly.
- **Thinking open-drain can output a high on its own.** It cannot; a high comes only from the external resistor.
- **Using the MCU's weak internal pull-up for a fast bus.** The 20 to 50 kΩ internal resistor makes edges far too slow for I2C at 400 kHz.
- **Confusing push-pull with tri-state.** Push-pull describes the drive structure; tri-state is a third (both off) state.
- **Pulling an open-drain line above the pad's rating.** The pull-up voltage must stay within the pin's maximum.
- **Writing 1 to mean "high" in open-drain mode and expecting 3.3 V without a pull-up.**
:::

::: deep
## Where the words come from

Early logic families used bipolar transistors, so the output stage had a transistor whose *collector* went to the pin: "open-collector". The totem pole in TTL had two transistors stacked, one pulling up and one pulling down; people called the structure "totem-pole" or "push-pull". In CMOS the terminal is the *drain*, hence open-drain. In every case the idea is the same: the pull-down transistor is the essential part, the pull-up transistor is optional.

## Three states, two meanings

An output stage can be in one of:

1. **Driven high**: the upper transistor conducts and the pin sits at $V_{DD}-I\,R_{on,p}$.
2. **Driven low**: the lower transistor conducts and the pin sits at $I\,R_{on,n}$.
3. **Released (high-Z)**: neither conducts and the pin has no driver.

A push-pull pin uses states 1 and 2, and (if tri-state capable) 3. An open-drain pin uses 2 and 3: "logic 1" in an open-drain pin *is* the released state. This is why software that writes 1 to an open-drain pin without a pull-up sees nothing happen.

## The three-line truth table for sharing

| A (NMOS) | B (NMOS) | Line |
|---|---|---|
| released | released | high (via pull-up) |
| pulling low | released | low |
| released | pulling low | low |
| pulling low | pulling low | low (current shared) |

Compare with two push-pull outputs, one high and one low: the line is neither level and the current through the two on-resistances is $V_{DD}/(R_p+R_n)$, about 66 mA with 25 Ω each ([[q0788]]).

## Calculating the high level

With the line released, the high level is the divider of the pull-up against the load the line sees. If the receiver input draws a leakage current $I_L$: $V_{high}=V_{pull}-I_LR_{pu}$. With $R_{pu}=4.7$ kΩ and 1 uA of leakage the loss is 4.7 mV, irrelevant; with a 1 MΩ pull-up and 1 uA it is 1 V, a large error. This is the dual of the loading argument for voltage dividers ([[q0008]]): the pull-up is the top resistor, the load is the bottom one.

When the NMOS is on, the low level is $V_{OL}=V_{pull}\cdot R_{on}/(R_{pu}+R_{on})$. For 25 Ω and 4.7 kΩ, that is $3.3\times25/4725=17$ mV, comfortably below any threshold. The pull-up current is the bus's static cost: 0.7 mA.

## Configuring a pin

On most MCUs, one bit chooses the type (for example an "output type" register selecting push-pull or open-drain, plus separate pull-up enables). Typical open-drain use: set output type to open-drain, enable the internal pull-up only if the line is slow and short, otherwise add an external resistor. Writing 0 pulls the line low; writing 1 releases it. To *read* the line the input buffer stays connected, so a master can see whether another device is also holding the line low, which is how I2C arbitration and clock stretching work.

## Why not always push-pull?

Because the wire might be shared, or the other chip might run at a different voltage, or the line might need to be held low by whichever of several devices has something to say. Push-pull optimises one driver and one receiver; open-drain optimises for many. And why not always open-drain? Speed and static current. That comparison is the subject of [[q0784]].

## Where you see the terms

Datasheets: "RESET: open-drain, active-low, requires external pull-up"; "INT: open-drain"; "SDA/SCL: open-drain"; "MOSI: push-pull". Voltage supervisors often offer both versions of a part, one open-drain (so several supervisors can be OR-ed on a reset line) and one push-pull. Comparators like the LM393 have open-collector outputs: two comparators' outputs can be tied together as a wired-AND window detector with one pull-up.
:::

::: applications
- **I2C SDA/SCL lines**: open-drain with 2.2 to 10 kΩ pull-ups ([[q0753]]).
- **Open-drain reset and supervisor outputs**: several sources can hold the MCU in reset.
- **Shared interrupt lines**: wired-OR of active-low requests.
- **LM393-style comparators**: open-collector outputs with a pull-up to any rail, giving level translation.
- **SPI MOSI/SCK, UART TX**: push-pull for speed.
- **1-Wire sensors**: a single open-drain data line.
:::

::: assumptions
- CMOS output stages with NMOS/PMOS; the bipolar version is mentioned for completeness.
- Typical on-resistance 25 Ω and pull-ups of a few kΩ; real values vary by part.
- Positive logic for the wired-AND statements; for active-low signals the same circuit is a wired-OR of requests.
- "Open-drain" configuration of a GPIO pad assumes the pad is rated for the pull-up voltage.
:::

::: followups
### What does "open" mean in open-drain?
That the drain of the output transistor is not connected to anything inside the chip: no internal pull-up. The circuit is left "open" and completed externally by a resistor to a supply.

### Can an open-drain pin source current?
No. It can only sink current through its NMOS. Any current into a load at the high level comes from the pull-up resistor (or whatever the pull-up connects to).

### Can I use open-drain to drive an LED?
Yes: connect the LED and resistor from the supply to the pin, and the NMOS pulls the cathode low to turn it on. This is a classic use of open-drain or open-collector outputs: the supply can be a different voltage from the chip, e.g. an LED on 5 V driven from a 3.3 V part, within the pad's voltage rating.

### What is the difference between open-drain and tri-state?
Tri-state is a push-pull stage with an extra "both off" state. Open-drain never has the pull-up device; its released state is also high-Z, but the line's high level then comes from an external resistor, and several outputs can safely drive "low" together.

### Harder: an I2C line never goes high even though firmware writes 1. What do you check?
Whether the pull-up exists and goes to the right rail (measure with the MCU disconnected); whether the pin is actually configured open-drain with the internal pull enabled, or push-pull accidentally; and whether another device is holding the line low (check with a scope). With a 4.7 kΩ pull-up the line should rise in about 400 ns; no rise at all means no pull-up or a stuck-low device.
:::
