::: testing
Whether you know that an eye mask is a **pass/fail template**: keep-out regions in the centre of the eye (and amplitude limits above and below) defined by a standard in normalised time and voltage coordinates, and that the test is "no (or very few) signal samples inside the mask". The interviewer wants to hear what a violation means (not enough margin for a compliant receiver, so the link or device fails the spec), the difference between a mask test and a BER test, and what you would do next (find whether time, amplitude or both are closing the eye, then fix the cause).
:::

::: why
Interoperability depends on masks. A transmitter manufacturer and a receiver manufacturer can only build parts that work together if there is an agreed, measurable definition of "good enough". Eye masks give that definition for USB, PCIe, Ethernet, HDMI, SONET and many other interfaces. In the lab, "we hit the mask" is a concrete failure with a location you can read off the plot; "we have 25% mask margin" is a number a design review can accept.
:::

::: concepts
- **Eye diagram**: all bits overlaid. See [[q0698]].
- **Normalised coordinates**: masks are specified in UI (x) and as fractions of eye amplitude (y), so they scale with data rate and swing.
- **BER and Q**: a mask pass is a proxy for a BER target. See [[q0699]].
- **Jitter and noise**: the horizontal and vertical ways a trace reaches the mask. See [[q0695]].
- **Compliance versus margin**: passing is binary; margin says how far you are from failing.
:::

::: answer
"An eye mask is a template drawn on the eye diagram, usually a hexagon or rectangle in the centre plus limits above and below, defined by the standard in normalised time and voltage. A compliant signal's eye has to stay out of it: no signal samples are allowed inside the mask, or only a very small, specified fraction.

The central part guarantees the minimum opening a receiver needs, a minimum eye height and width. The top and bottom limits constrain overshoot and amplitude. The mask is chosen so that a transmitter or channel that clears it will give a compliant receiver an acceptable bit error rate.

If part of the signal enters the mask, that's a mask violation or 'hit': the signal fails the compliance test, because it doesn't provide enough margin in time or voltage. Where the hits are tells you why. Hits at the left and right sides mean too much jitter or too slow an edge, hits at the top and bottom mean ISI or noise closing the eye vertically or too much overshoot. To fix it, you improve the channel or equalization until it passes, and ideally measure mask margin, the amount you can scale the mask before it gets hit, so you know how comfortably you pass."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** An eye mask is a set of polygons in the $(t/UI, v/A)$ plane. A typical NRZ mask comprises:
1. a central **keep-out polygon** (hexagon, diamond or rectangle) that defines the minimum eye opening;
2. **upper and lower regions** bounding the maximum and minimum amplitude (overshoot/undershoot limits).

Coordinates are normalised: x as fractions of the UI, y as fractions of the eye amplitude (or in absolute mV for electrical interfaces). The specific numbers (often named $X_1$, $X_2$, $Y_1$, $Y_2$, etc.) come from the interface standard and are not universal.

**Test.** Acquire $N$ unit intervals with the specified pattern, clock recovery and measurement filter; count samples (or waveform points) falling inside the polygons: *mask hits*. Pass criteria are standard-specific: zero hits over a specified number of UIs, or a hit ratio below a stated limit.

**What passing means statistically.** If $k = 0$ hits are seen in $N$ UI, then at 95% confidence the true hit probability is below about $3/N$ (rule of three). With $N = 10^6$ that is $3\times10^{-6}$; the mask test therefore does *not* directly prove a BER of $10^{-12}$. The mask is designed with margin so that a signal clearing it should produce an acceptable BER on a compliant receiver, and for BER-level confidence standards also specify jitter and noise tests or BERT checks.

**Mask margin.** Scale the mask outward by a factor until the hit ratio reaches the allowed limit; the margin is the percentage of scaling achieved. A signal that tolerates a mask 25% larger has 25% margin.

**Location of hits.**
- Hits at the horizontal extremes of the keep-out: excessive jitter or slow edges (eye too narrow).
- Hits at the top/bottom of the keep-out: ISI, noise, reflections (eye too short).
- Hits in the amplitude-limit regions: overshoot, amplitude too high, ringing.
:::

::: short
"An eye mask is a pass/fail template overlaid on the eye: a hexagon or rectangle in the middle plus limits above and below, defined by the standard in normalised time and voltage. A compliant signal keeps out of it. If some of the signal enters the mask, that's a violation, a hit: the signal doesn't give enough margin in voltage or timing, so it fails compliance. Where the hits are tells you whether it's jitter, noise or ISI, and you fix that and check the mask margin."
:::

::: long
"An eye mask is a template that a standard puts on top of the eye diagram. It's normally a hexagon or rectangle in the centre of the eye, sometimes with extra bars above and below. It's defined in normalised units, so the horizontal axis is a fraction of the unit interval and the vertical axis a fraction of the eye amplitude, which means it scales with data rate and swing.

What it means: the central keep-out area represents the minimum opening that a compliant receiver needs to decode the data with an acceptable bit error rate. The top and bottom parts limit overshoot and amplitude. A compliant signal means the eye stays out of the mask, with none or only a tiny, specified number of samples inside it.

If part of the signal enters the mask, that's a mask violation, a hit. It means the signal fails compliance: it doesn't provide enough margin in time or voltage. And the location is diagnostic. Hits at the left and right sides of the keep-out are timing problems, too much jitter or slow edges. Hits at the top and bottom are amplitude problems, ISI from loss, reflections or noise. Hits in the outer bars are overshoot.

A couple of caveats. A pass over a million bits only proves the hit probability is below a few parts per million with some confidence, so it's not a direct proof of a 10 to the minus 12 BER; the mask is built so that clearing it gives a reasonable BER margin. And rather than just pass or fail, we measure mask margin, how much we can scale the mask before it's hit, so a design review can see that we pass with, say, 25 percent margin. If we fail, I'd identify whether it's jitter, loss or reflections, and fix the cause with equalization, better routing or a cleaner clock."
:::

::: terms
- **Eye mask**: standard-defined polygons on the eye diagram that a compliant signal must not enter.
- **Keep-out region**: the central polygon defining the minimum acceptable opening.
- **Mask hit / violation**: a sample falling inside the mask.
- **Hit ratio**: number of hits divided by number of samples or UIs.
- **Mask margin**: percentage by which the mask can be scaled before failure.
- **Normalised coordinates**: time in UI, amplitude as a fraction of the eye swing.
- **Compliance test**: measurement per the standard with specified fixtures, pattern and filters.
- **Reference equalizer / reference clock recovery**: standard-specified receiver models used in the measurement.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "a template the eye must not touch". An exceptional answer gives the **structure** (central keep-out plus amplitude limits, in normalised UI/amplitude units), the **meaning** (minimum receiver opening, overshoot limits), and the **interpretation of hits by location** (left/right jitter, top/bottom ISI/noise, outer bars overshoot). It also states the **statistical caveat** (zero hits in $N$ UI only bounds probability to about $3/N$, so the mask proxies for BER and is paired with margin tests and BER/jitter tests) and distinguishes **pass/fail from mask margin**, and notes that the measurement conditions (pattern, bandwidth, reference clock recovery, fixtures) are part of the definition.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking mask pass equals BER pass.** The mask is a proxy; it doesn't directly prove $10^{-12}$ BER.
- **Using the wrong measurement setup.** The standard specifies pattern, scope bandwidth/filter, fixture and clock recovery; a different setup gives a different eye and a meaningless result.
- **Comparing masks across data rates in absolute units.** Masks are normalised; a 100 ps wide mask at 10 Gb/s is not the same as at 5 Gb/s.
- **Treating one hit as an outlier to ignore.** Some standards allow a small hit ratio, others zero; a single hit can be real. Check the allowed count and look at the waveform responsible.
- **Fixing only the eye on the scope.** Mask tests at the transmitter output differ from a receiver's equalised eye; fix the right reference plane.
- **Ignoring top/bottom hits.** Overshoot and ringing outside the amplitude limits can fail the test even with a wide-open centre.
:::

## What a mask looks like

A typical electrical NRZ mask consists of a **central polygon** — a hexagon or diamond whose width and height are fractions of the UI and of the eye amplitude — and sometimes **outer limits**. In the left panel the eye clears the hexagon. In the right panel, loss and jitter bring traces into it. The numbers here are purely illustrative; real masks come from the standard for the interface.

## Why a polygon in the middle?

The centre of the eye is where the receiver samples. A receiver needs a minimum voltage swing at the sampling point and a minimum timing window around it. The hexagon's height is the minimum swing needed, and its width is the minimum timing window, with sloped sides because a bit of horizontal slack is traded for vertical margin: the receiver can tolerate sampling a little off-centre only if the signal is correspondingly bigger there. The top and bottom limits keep the transmitter from exceeding amplitude (damaging receivers or causing crosstalk and EMI).

Standards typically state the mask by a few normalised numbers and the conditions: for example a time coordinate as a fraction of UI and an amplitude coordinate as a fraction of the eye swing, along with the test pattern and the measuring filter.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The specific mask coordinates, allowed hit ratios and measurement filters differ by standard and sometimes by speed grade. The ones here are generic and unlabeled on purpose; always use the interface specification for numbers.

## What counts as a hit, and how many bits you need

The scope takes $N$ unit intervals, places each sample point in the $(t, v)$ plane, and counts how many fall inside the mask polygon. With zero hits in $N$ UI:

$$P_{hit} \lesssim \frac{3}{N}\quad(95\%\text{ confidence})$$

| UIs captured $N$ | Bound on hit probability |
|---|---|
| $10^{4}$ | $3\times10^{-4}$ |
| $10^{6}$ | $3\times10^{-6}$ |
| $10^{9}$ | $3\times10^{-9}$ |
| $3\times10^{12}$ | $10^{-12}$ |

A standard that requires zero hits in $10^6$ UI therefore tells you only that the hit probability is below a few parts per million. The mask dimensions are chosen conservatively (smaller than what the receiver strictly needs) so that clearing them implies a good BER with margin. For stricter confidence, compliance suites add separate jitter tests and BER tests.

## Mask margin

Pass/fail is binary; mask margin is more informative. The scope scales the mask outward (e.g. 5%, 10%, ... 40%) and finds the largest scale at which the hit count is still within the limit. A signal with a 30% margin has room for 30% more jitter-and-noise degradation before failing. Because production variation, temperature and aging all eat margin, designs typically want a healthy margin and not just a pass.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked what margin is "enough", say that it depends on the interface and company policy, give the logic (production spread, temperature, aging, fixture repeatability) and that you'd see how the margin moves over temperature and voltage corners. Avoid quoting a made-up universal number.

## Reading a violation

1. **Where are the hits?** Left/right of the hexagon: timing (jitter, slow edges, DCD). Top/bottom of the hexagon: amplitude (ISI, noise, reflections, low swing). In the outer bars: overshoot or ringing.
2. **When are they happening?** Certain bit patterns (long runs followed by a transition) imply ISI; hits spread randomly imply noise or random jitter; periodic ones suggest supply or crosstalk coupling.
3. **Where is the reference plane?** A transmitter mask at the connector tests the transmitter plus fixture; a receiver mask tests the channel; make sure the measurement uses the right fixture and de-embedding.
4. **Fix and re-test.** Equalization (TX de-emphasis, RX CTLE/DFE), better channel, cleaner clock, power-supply integrity; re-measure and look at margin ([[q0701]], [[q0702]]).

## A mask is not the receiver

The mask is a *specification device*; real receivers equalize, so their effective eye can be larger than the scope eye. Standards therefore often measure the eye after a **reference equalizer** or at specific test points, so measurements are repeatable across labs. For a product you also care about BER in the real system, which depends on the actual receiver, its sensitivity and the real channel.
:::

::: applications
- **Transmitter compliance testing** for USB, PCIe, SATA, HDMI/DisplayPort and Ethernet with standard fixtures and patterns.
- **Optical transceiver testing**: electrical and optical eye masks (the latter given in normalised coordinates in standards).
- **Production screening**: a fixed mask-hit threshold used as a go/no-go test on the line.
- **Silicon bring-up**: sweeping TX equalization settings to find those with the best mask margin.
- **Channel qualification**: cables or backplanes characterised by mask margin at the receiver end.
- **Regression testing**: mask margin tracked across firmware, temperature and voltage to catch drift.
:::

- **NRZ mask** in the style described; PAM4 uses multiple smaller masks for the three eyes and different statistics.

::: followups
### Why is the mask a hexagon rather than a rectangle?
A hexagon (or diamond) mimics the receiver's trade-off: it can tolerate sampling off-centre only if the signal there is larger, so the allowed region shrinks toward the sides. A rectangle would demand full height across the whole width, which is more conservative than necessary. The sloped corners also match the shape real eyes have at the crossings.

### How do you tell if hits are from jitter or from ISI?
Look at where they lie and what pattern causes them. Hits at the sides (crossings drifting into the keep-out) are timing: jitter or slow edges. Hits at the top/bottom of the keep-out are amplitude. Triggering on specific bit patterns (e.g. an isolated 1 after a long run of 0s) shows if it's ISI, since it produces hits deterministically for that pattern. Random, unpatterned hits suggest noise or random jitter.

### I pass the mask test with zero hits but the link has errors. What's going on?
A mask test over $10^6$ UI can't see rare events; BER $10^{-9}$ would produce thousands of errors per second at 10 Gb/s and still be invisible in a short capture. Also the receiver's own noise, CDR jitter tolerance, and coding or protocol issues aren't in a transmitter mask test. I'd run a BERT or check error counters and measure margin with the real receiver.

### What does mask margin of 0% mean and what's a reasonable target?
0% means the signal is at the edge of passing: any degradation fails. A reasonable target depends on the standard and the product, but you want enough to absorb temperature, voltage, process and fixture variation; many teams define internal minimums (such as 20–30% or more). I'd treat any number as organisational policy, not a universal rule.

### How would you set up a mask test properly?
Use the specified fixture and cables with de-embedding, the standard test pattern (e.g. a PRBS or specific compliance pattern), the prescribed scope bandwidth/filter and clock recovery, enough UIs to meet the stated confidence, and the standard's mask file loaded in the scope. Verify the fixture and calibration, and repeat to check repeatability.
:::

:::
