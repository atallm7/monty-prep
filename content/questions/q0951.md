::: basics
### In plain words
Chips are sold in temperature grades that match where they will be used. Typical ranges are 0 to 70 °C for commercial parts, -40 to 85 °C for industrial, and up to 125 or 150 °C for automotive. Engineers test chips at the cold end and the hot end, because different problems show up at each. The real limit is the temperature of the silicon itself, which is higher than the air around it, since the chip heats itself.

### Picture it
Think of a hiker and the weather. A thin jacket suits mild days, a winter coat suits snow, and an arctic parka suits extreme cold. Those are the grades. But a hiker generates heat, so the body is warmer than the air. The chip's silicon works the same way: its own temperature is the air temperature plus the heat it makes.

### Words you need
- **Ambient temperature**: the temperature of the air around the chip.
- **Junction temperature**: the temperature of the silicon die. The physics responds to this one.
- **Thermal resistance ($\theta_{JA}$)**: how many degrees the junction warms per watt, in K/W (kelvin per watt; 1 K is a 1 °C step).
- **Temperature grade**: a standard range, such as commercial or industrial, that a part is guaranteed to work in.
- **AEC-Q100**: the automotive standard that defines the automotive grades.
- **HTOL**: a long test that runs parts hot to age them quickly.

### The simplest example
A chip dissipates 2 W, and its package (the case around the die) has $\theta_{JA}=30$ K/W. In a sealed box at 85 °C, the junction reaches $85 + 2\times30 = 145$ °C. That is above a 125 °C limit, even though the box is within the 85 °C rating. The most this package can dissipate there is $(125-85)/30\approx1.33$ W. With a heat sink giving 15 K/W, the junction is only 115 °C. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers what fails at cold and at hot, and how reliability tests age parts.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know the **standard temperature grades** and, more importantly, *why* those ranges exist and what is validated at each end. The interviewer is checking:

1. Standard ranges: commercial (0 to 70 °C), industrial (-40 to 85 °C), automotive grades (AEC-Q100 up to 125 °C or 150 °C), military/space (-55 to 125 °C).
2. The distinction between ambient, case and **junction** temperature, and the equation $T_j = T_a + P\theta_{JA}$.
3. What fails at cold (timing at low-voltage inversion, cold boot, analog drift, brittle behavior) versus hot (leakage, reliability, throttling, setup timing in classic nodes).
4. How validation is structured: min, nominal and max temperature with voltage and process corners, plus temperature cycling and burn-in for reliability.
5. Honest hedging: ranges vary by company and product.
:::

::: why
The temperature range decides which markets a part can address (a phone chip, a car chip and a satellite chip have different specs), how much it costs to qualify, and how aggressive the cooling must be. A validation engineer will be asked to run a part at the hot and cold corners, so knowing the usual numbers and *why* we test them separates someone who has memorized "corners" from someone who understands them. This question closes the PVT triple ([[q0949]], [[q0950]]) with the "T" axis in practical terms, and links to thermal management ([[q0953]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Ambient, case and junction temperature**: $T_j = T_a + P\,\theta_{JA}$; specs refer to different points. See [[q0945]].
- **Temperature grades**: commercial, industrial, automotive (AEC-Q100 Grades 0 to 3), military.
- **Cold-temperature effects**: mobility rises, $V_t$ rises, temperature inversion at low $V$. See [[q0949]].
- **Hot-temperature effects**: leakage grows exponentially, reliability mechanisms accelerate.
- **Qualification tests**: temperature cycling, high-temperature operating life (HTOL), burn-in.
- **Validation matrix**: process corners x voltage x temperature. See [[q0950]].
:::

::: answer
"There are a few standard grades. Commercial parts are usually specified from 0 to 70 degrees C. Industrial is minus 40 to 85. Automotive follows AEC-Q100 grades: Grade 3 is minus 40 to 85, Grade 2 is minus 40 to 105, Grade 1 is minus 40 to 125, and Grade 0 goes up to 150 degrees ambient. Military and space parts typically use minus 55 to 125. Those are the ambient or case ranges a part is guaranteed to work in.

The thing to keep straight is that validation cares about junction temperature, which is higher than ambient: $T_j = T_a + P\theta_{JA}$. A chip dissipating 2 W with 30 K per watt thermal resistance at 85 C ambient is at 145 C junction, past a 125 C limit, so the thermal solution matters as much as the silicon.

In validation I'd test at the cold end, a room temperature point, and the hot end, across supply voltage extremes and process corners. Cold finds timing and startup problems, like slow-at-cold behavior from temperature inversion, analog drift, and cold boot. Hot finds leakage-driven power and thermal limits, setup failures in older nodes, and reliability. On top of that, qualification runs reliability tests like temperature cycling and high-temperature operating life at 125 C or more. And these numbers aren't universal; the actual range depends on the product's market and the company's spec."
:::

**Junction vs ambient.** $T_j = T_a + P\theta_{JA}$. Validation limits are often written in junction terms (e.g. $T_{j,max}=125$ °C, $T_{j,min}=-40$ °C for industrial, with a commercial CPU often 0 to 100 °C or so; vendor specific). The same ambient range gives different junction ranges depending on power and cooling.

**What is tested at each end.**
- *Cold (-40 or -55 °C)*: cold boot and reset, oscillator start-up, flash/EEPROM read/write margins, analog offset/reference drift, I/O timing, setup/hold at the cold corner (inversion at low V), condensation and mechanical issues (solder joints, connectors).
- *Room (about 25 °C)*: reference and nominal characterization.
- *Hot (85/105/125/150 °C junction)*: maximum leakage and power, thermal throttling behavior, setup timing (classic nodes), data retention, reliability margins.
- *Corners*: each of these combined with supply min/max and process corners (skewed lots) in characterization.

**Reliability tests.** Temperature cycling (e.g. repeated swings between -55 and 125 °C in a JEDEC-style test) stresses solder joints and die attach through coefficient-of-thermal-expansion mismatch; high-temperature operating life (HTOL, commonly at 125 °C or higher for about 1000 hours) accelerates wear-out following an Arrhenius law $AF=\exp\!\big[\tfrac{E_a}{k}\big(\tfrac{1}{T_{use}}-\tfrac{1}{T_{stress}}\big)\big]$. With $E_a=0.7$ eV, 55 °C use and 125 °C stress, $AF\approx78$; 1000 h of HTOL then represents about 9 years at 55 °C.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Temperature specs and test conditions vary by company, product and market. AEC-Q100 grades are standardized (ambient operating ranges, Grades 0 to 3), but commercial CPU/SoC junction-temperature limits, qualification durations and activation energies are product specific. Treat the ranges above as typical, and quote the datasheet in real work.
:::

"There are some standard grades. Commercial is typically 0 to 70 degrees C. Industrial is minus 40 to 85, and many microcontrollers offer 105 or 125 variants. Automotive uses the AEC-Q100 grades: Grade 3 is minus 40 to 85, Grade 2 to 105, Grade 1 to 125, and Grade 0 to 150 degrees C ambient. Military and space use roughly minus 55 to 125 or wider.

A part dissipating 2 W with a 30 K/W package at 85 C ambient ends up at 145 C, which is outside a 125 C junction limit. So validation covers both the environment range and the self-heating.

For validation I test cold, room and hot, each combined with minimum and maximum voltage and with the process corner lots. Cold exposes failure modes like cold boot, oscillator start-up, flash timing, analog drift, and, at advanced nodes with low voltage, slow logic from temperature inversion. Hot exposes leakage and thermal limits, setup-time failures, throttling behavior and data-retention and reliability issues.

Then there's reliability qualification on top: temperature cycling, which stresses solder joints and die attach through thermal expansion mismatch, and high-temperature operating life, where parts run at 125 C or above for around a thousand hours to accelerate wear-out. With Arrhenius acceleration and an activation energy of 0.7 eV, a thousand hours at 125 C is roughly nine years of life at 55 C. All of these ranges are typical rather than universal: each company and product defines its own spec."
:::

::: terms
- **Ambient temperature ($T_a$)**: the temperature of the air around the device.
- **Case temperature ($T_c$)**: package surface temperature.
- **Junction temperature ($T_j$)**: temperature of the silicon die; what the physics responds to.
- **Thermal resistance ($\theta_{JA}$, $\theta_{JC}$)**: temperature rise per watt (K/W), junction to ambient or to case.
- **AEC-Q100**: automotive IC stress-qualification standard defining Grades 0 to 3 by ambient range.
- **HTOL**: high-temperature operating life test, accelerating wear-out.
- **Temperature cycling**: repeated hot-cold swings to stress mechanical interfaces.
- **Burn-in**: stress screening at elevated temperature/voltage to weed out early failures.
- **Temperature inversion**: at low voltage, cold circuits can be slower than hot ones.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** gives "0 to 70, -40 to 85, -55 to 125" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the grades *and* distinguishes **ambient, case and junction** with $T_j = T_a + P\theta_{JA}$ and a worked example showing a junction limit violation.
- Explains **what fails at each end**: cold (timing via inversion, cold boot, oscillators, flash, analog drift) vs hot (leakage, setup, reliability, throttling).
- Describes validation as a **matrix** of temperature x voltage x process corner, not temperature alone.
- Mentions **reliability tests** (temperature cycling, HTOL with Arrhenius acceleration) and distinguishes them from functional validation.
- Notes the numbers are **market- and company-specific** and flags when unsure, rather than overclaiming.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Quoting ambient limits as junction limits.** A "125 °C" automotive part is 125 °C *ambient*; the junction can be far hotter.
- **Testing only hot.** Cold failures (cold boot, oscillator start-up, inversion-related timing, flash write) are common.
- **Assuming hot is always the slowest.** At low voltage in advanced nodes cold can be the slow corner ([[q0949]]).
- **Conflating validation with qualification.** Validation checks function and margins across conditions; qualification stress tests (cycling, HTOL, burn-in) check lifetime and reliability.
- **Forgetting self-heating.** At the max ambient the part's own power can push $T_j$ past its limit.
- **Treating the ranges as universal.** Consumer SoCs, servers and automotive chips have different junction limits and qualification flows.
:::

::: deep
## Why there are standard ranges

Chips go into environments with very different temperatures:

- A lab or office: roughly 15 to 30 °C, the origin of the commercial 0 to 70 °C convention (which allows for warm enclosures and self-heating).
- Industrial plants, outdoor cabinets, telecom cabinets: -40 °C winter nights up to 85 °C in sealed enclosures.
- Automotive: -40 °C cold start to 125 °C or more in under-hood or sunlit dashboards; engine-mounted electronics can reach 150 °C.
- Military, aerospace and space: wide swings, often -55 to 125 °C, with thermal cycling at orbital eclipse transitions.

Rather than designing a new part for each, the industry bins products into grades with a known range and qualification flow. Standardization also lets customers compare parts.

## Ambient vs junction: the part that really matters

Silicon responds to **junction temperature**. Using $T_j = T_a + P\,\theta_{JA}$:

$$T_j = 85 + 2\times30 = 145\ ^\circ\text{C}$$

for a 2 W part in a package with $\theta_{JA}=30$ K/W at 85 °C ambient, exceeding a 125 °C $T_j$ limit. Options: reduce power, improve $\theta_{JA}$ (heat sink, copper pour, airflow) or relax the ambient. For $\theta_{JA}=15$ K/W: $T_j = 115$ °C, which passes.

## What is validated at each end

| Condition | What typically fails or is checked |
|---|---|
| **Cold** (-40 / -55 °C) | cold boot and power-on reset; crystal/oscillator and PLL lock; flash and EEPROM read/write margin; analog reference drift and offset; I/O and memory timing; setup at low-V inversion; LCD/battery behavior; mechanical contraction (solder cracks, connectors) |
| **Room** (25 °C) | reference characterization, datasheet typicals, calibration |
| **Hot** (85 to 150 °C junction) | leakage and total power; thermal throttling and thermal-sensor accuracy; setup timing (classic corner); retention of DRAM/SRAM/flash; analog bandwidth, gain and bias drift; reliability margin; peak current |

Hot also changes DRAM: refresh must be more frequent (commonly doubled above about 85 °C in DDR devices), and the mode-register temperature settings matter; check the DRAM spec.

## Validation matrix, not a line

A characterization plan sweeps a matrix: *temperature* (cold, room, hot, maybe intermediate points) x *supply voltage* (min, nominal, max, and low-power states) x *process* (typical lots plus corner/skew lots) x *frequency* (shmoo). Results are summarized in a shmoo plot of pass/fail vs voltage and frequency at each temperature, which gives the operating window and the margin.

## Reliability qualification (separate from functional validation)

- **Temperature cycling**: repeated swings (for example between -55 and +125 °C for hundreds to thousands of cycles in typical JEDEC-style conditions) to expose solder-joint, wire-bond and die-attach fatigue caused by CTE mismatch.
- **HTOL (high-temperature operating life)**: parts operated at elevated voltage and temperature (commonly 125 °C or higher) for about 1000 hours to accelerate wear-out failures.
- **Burn-in**: a similar elevated-stress screening on production parts to remove infant-mortality failures.
- **Acceleration**: Arrhenius, $AF=\exp[\tfrac{E_a}{k}(\tfrac{1}{T_{use}}-\tfrac{1}{T_{stress}})]$, $k=8.617\times10^{-5}$ eV/K. With $E_a=0.7$ eV: 55 °C to 125 °C gives $AF\approx78$, to 150 °C gives $AF\approx259$. So 1000 h at 125 °C equals 77,700 h, about 8.9 years, at 55 °C.

> [!NOTE]
> The activation energy depends on the failure mechanism (0.7 eV is a common default, but electromigration, TDDB and NBTI each have their own value). Qualification plans use mechanism-specific numbers.

## Consumer and server specifics

Consumer devices (phones, laptops) have ambient ranges of roughly 0 to 35 or 40 °C in typical use but their SoCs run at junction temperatures near their limits (often around 100 to 105 °C junction maximum before throttling; vendor specific), and the skin-touch limit rather than the silicon limit often dictates throttling ([[q0953]]). Data-center parts are validated for sustained high junction temperatures, tens of watts to hundreds of watts of self-heating, and ambient inlet temperatures in a controlled range. Automotive adds long lifetimes (15 years, thousands of hours of operation) and wide ranges, hence the strict AEC-Q100 flow.

## Thermal sensors and what "validated" means in practice

Modern chips carry on-die temperature sensors (typically a diode-connected transistor whose voltage drops by about 2 mV per kelvin, read by a small ADC) at several hot spots. Validation engineers correlate that reading with an external reference (a thermal head holding the case at a set point, or a chamber) because the set point of a chamber is the *ambient*, not the die. Sensor accuracy (often a few °C) becomes part of the margin: if the sensor can be off by ±3 °C, the throttle threshold sits that much below the true limit. Hot spots matter as well, since a core can be 10 °C or more hotter than the average die temperature, so the worst-case local temperature, not the mean, is what must stay inside the validated range.

## Interview-ready summary

If asked for "typical temperature ranges", give the grades, then add: "but I'd also ask about junction vs ambient, since junction is what the silicon sees". That one sentence shows you understand what validation really targets.

::: applications
- **Consumer electronics**: commercial or extended-commercial parts, with thermal throttling to stay below skin-temperature and junction limits.
- **Automotive**: AEC-Q100 Grade 1 or 2 microcontrollers and sensors for under-hood and cabin electronics; Grade 0 near engines.
- **Industrial control and outdoor equipment**: -40 to 85 or 105 °C parts in sealed enclosures with limited cooling.
- **Aerospace and space**: -55 to 125 °C (or wider) parts with thermal cycling through orbit eclipses.
- **Data-center CPUs and GPUs**: validated to high sustained junction temperatures with power capping and fan control.
- **Medical implants and wearables**: narrow body-temperature ambient, but cold storage and sterilization temperatures in qualification.

::: assumptions
- **Ranges are typical**; AEC-Q100 grades are standardized, but commercial/industrial boundaries and CPU junction limits are vendor and product specific.
- **Ambient vs junction** distinctions matter: the figure shows ambient grades, whereas the junction range is wider at the hot end by $P\theta$.
- **Single thermal resistance** model; real systems have multiple paths (junction to case, board, ambient) and transient behavior.
- **Arrhenius acceleration** with a single activation energy is a simplification; real qualification uses mechanism-specific models and standards.
- If the interviewer is asking about a **specific product class** (phones, cars, space), adjust: the same logic of "market defines range, junction is what matters" holds.

::: followups
### Why test at cold, not just hot?
Cold exposes different failure mechanisms: cold boot and oscillator start-up, flash and EEPROM write margins, analog reference drift, and timing at the cold corner (with temperature inversion, low-voltage logic can be slowest when cold). Mechanical contraction can also crack solder joints. Many field returns in cold climates trace back to cold-start behavior never exercised in a hot-only test.

### How do you get from ambient to junction temperature?
$T_j = T_a + P\,\theta_{JA}$, or through the chain $T_j = T_a + P(\theta_{JC}+\theta_{CS}+\theta_{SA})$ when a heat sink is used. E.g. 85 °C ambient, 2 W, 30 K/W gives 145 °C. In practice you measure with the on-die thermal diode or sensor, or with a thermocouple on the case plus $\theta_{JC}$.

### What does HTOL test, and how do you relate it to real life?
High-temperature operating life runs parts at elevated temperature (often 125 °C) and voltage for about 1000 hours to accelerate wear-out mechanisms. An Arrhenius factor converts the stress time into equivalent use time; with $E_a=0.7$ eV, 1000 h at 125 °C is roughly 9 years at 55 °C. The activation energy is mechanism specific, so it is an estimate.

### What's different about validating memory (DRAM, flash) across temperature?
DRAM retention gets worse as temperature rises, so refresh rate must increase at high temperature (commonly doubled above about 85 °C; check the device spec). Flash program/erase and retention vary with temperature: cold may need different write margins, hot shortens data retention. Memory timing and voltage margins are therefore characterized at both extremes.

### Scenario: a device passes everything at 25 °C and 85 °C but occasionally fails to boot at -20 °C. What do you check?
Look at the cold corner: oscillator or PLL start-up margin, regulator start-up or power-good sequencing with colder (higher-ESR) capacitors and battery internal resistance, flash read timing, reset thresholds, analog references, and logic setup at the cold corner with low supply (inversion). Reproduce in a thermal chamber and vary supply to find the shmoo edge, and check the cold-soak duration since the problem may depend on how cold the whole board is.
:::

::: interpretations
"Typical temperature ranges for validation" could mean (a) the **market grade ranges** (commercial, industrial, automotive, military), or (b) the **junction temperature points** an engineer actually sets in a chamber or on a thermal head during validation (often -40, 25, 85/105/125 °C). The page covers both, plus the distinction between ambient and junction. Specific numbers differ by company, so it's good practice to say "typically" and offer to look at the datasheet. Related: temperature control loops ([[q0954]]) and throttling ([[q0953]]).
