::: testing
Whether you can define common-mode voltage exactly ($V_{cm}=(V_++V_-)/2$), explain what it represents (the part of the pair that is the same on both wires: DC offset plus noise plus ground shift), say why it matters (receiver input range, CMRR, headroom, EMI from common-mode current) and give typical values. A strong candidate also distinguishes common-mode *voltage* from common-mode *current*, and knows how to measure it. The interviewer is also listening for the partner idea: the signal is the differential voltage ([[q0684]]), everything else is "common".
:::

::: why
Most real differential-link failures are common-mode failures: a ground offset that pushes the pair outside the receiver's input range, a driver whose common-mode is wrong for the receiver (AC-coupled links need the right bias), a skewed or imbalanced pair that converts common-mode noise into signal, or common-mode current on a cable that fails EMC. Being fluent with $V_{cm}$ lets you read datasheets (input common-mode range, output offset voltage), choose biasing and coupling, and debug with a scope.
:::

::: concepts
- **Definition**: $V_{cm}=(V_++V_-)/2$, equivalently $V_\pm=V_{cm}\pm V_d/2$ ([[q0679]]).
- **Differential voltage** $V_d=V_+-V_-$: the signal ([[q0684]]).
- **Common-mode range (CMR)**: window of $V_{cm}$ where the receiver works.
- **CMRR**: how strongly the receiver suppresses $V_{cm}$ ([[q0682]]).
- **Ground offset and noise** are common-mode disturbances.
- **Common-mode current** flows in the same direction on both wires and returns via ground or the environment; it radiates ([[q0559]]).
:::

::: answer
"The common-mode voltage is the average of the two wires of a differential pair, V-plus plus V-minus over two. It's what's left when you remove the signal. If I write each wire as the common-mode level plus or minus half the differential voltage, then the signal is the part that's opposite on the two wires and the common-mode is the part that's the same.

In a clean link it's a fixed DC offset set by the driver, for example 1.2 volts for LVDS, around 2.5 volts for CAN, and whatever the bias network sets for an AC-coupled link. In a real system it also carries noise and ground offset, which is what the receiver is meant to reject.

It matters in three ways. First, the receiver has a common-mode input range. If a ground offset or a bad bias pushes the average outside it, the input stage saturates and the link fails even though the differential signal is fine. Second, how much it leaks into the output is set by the CMRR. Third, a common-mode current on a cable is a very efficient antenna, so a noisy or unbalanced common-mode shows up as an EMI problem.

To measure it, I'd probe both wires single-ended against ground and use the scope math function, channel one plus channel two divided by two."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** $V_{cm}=\dfrac{V_++V_-}{2}$ and $V_d=V_+-V_-$. Inverse: $V_+=V_{cm}+V_d/2$, $V_-=V_{cm}-V_d/2$. Orthogonal decomposition: the two-wire space splits into a differential mode and a common mode.

**Typical values** (check specs):
| Interface | Nominal $V_{cm}$ | Receiver common-mode range |
|---|---|---|
| LVDS | about 1.2 V | about 0 to 2.4 V (with margin for $V_d$) |
| CAN | about 2.5 V (recessive; dominant mean about 2.5 V) | about $-2$ to $+7$ V (transceiver dependent) |
| RS-485 | set by bias; mid-rail | $-7$ to $+12$ V |
| PCIe, SATA, HDMI | AC-coupled / receiver-biased | receiver sets it |

**Effect on the receiver.** $V_{out}=A_dV_d+A_{cm}V_{cm}$; input-referred error $V_{cm}/CMRR$. Outside the common-mode range the input stage saturates: required headroom, e.g. a differential pair needs $V_{cm}\pm|V_d|/2$ to remain within range at each leg.

**Sources of common-mode voltage**: driver offset ($V_{OS}$), ground potential difference, supply and ground noise, coupled interference, skew between legs (common-mode glitches), and bias networks in AC-coupled links.

**Skew-generated common-mode**: $V_{cm,pk}\approx\Delta V_{leg}t_s/(2t_r)$ (35 mV for 0.35 V legs, 20 ps skew, 100 ps edges).

**Common-mode current**: $I_{cm}=I_++I_-$ (both in the same direction) returning through ground/shield. Even microamps of $I_{cm}$ can radiate on a cable; the differential current $I_d=(I_+-I_-)/2$ does not.

**Measurement**: two single-ended probes with matched cables, math $(CH_1+CH_2)/2$ for $V_{cm}$ and $CH_1-CH_2$ for $V_d$; or a differential probe whose own common-mode range and CMRR are adequate (common-mode range can be a few volts).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact input common-mode ranges (CAN, LVDS) vary by transceiver and revision. Use the datasheet of the part you are designing with.
:::

::: long
"Common-mode voltage is the average of the two wires: V-plus plus V-minus, divided by two. I like to think of it as splitting each wire into two parts. Each wire is the common-mode level, plus or minus half of the differential voltage. The part with opposite signs is the signal. The part that's the same on both is the common-mode.

In a well-behaved link it's mostly a constant DC level set by the driver. LVDS is around 1.2 volts, CAN sits near 2.5 volts, RS-485 is around mid-rail depending on bias, and in AC-coupled links like PCIe it's set separately at each end by the transmitter and receiver. On top of that, in a real system the common-mode carries everything that couples equally onto both wires: ground offset, supply noise, interference.

Why it matters. First, the receiver has a common-mode input range. The pair has to stay within it, including the half of the differential swing on each side. A ground offset or a mismatched bias can push the pair out of range, and then the receiver clips and the link fails even though the difference is perfect. Second, the receiver's CMRR determines how much of the common-mode ends up in the output; 60 dB means a volt becomes a millivolt. Third, common-mode current is the dangerous kind for EMI. Equal-direction currents on both wires create a net current on the cable that acts like a monopole antenna, so imbalance, skew and shield problems often show up as common-mode radiation and fail emissions tests.

To measure it, I'd use two single-ended probes and a scope math channel of channel one plus channel two, divided by two. And a differential probe measures the difference, not the common-mode."
:::

::: terms
- **Common-mode voltage ($V_{cm}$)**: $(V_++V_-)/2$, the same-on-both-wires component.
- **Differential voltage ($V_d$)**: $V_+-V_-$, the signal ([[q0684]]).
- **Output offset voltage ($V_{OS}$)**: the driver's nominal common-mode level.
- **Common-mode range**: allowed $V_{cm}$ window at the receiver input.
- **CMRR**: ratio of differential to common-mode gain.
- **Common-mode current**: current flowing in the same direction on both conductors.
- **Common-mode choke**: component that blocks common-mode current while passing differential.
- **AC coupling**: series capacitors that remove the DC common-mode and let each end set its own.
- **Mode conversion**: swapping between common and differential modes due to asymmetry.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it's the average of the two signals" and perhaps "it's the DC offset".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives $V_{cm}=(V_++V_-)/2$ and the decomposition $V_\pm=V_{cm}\pm V_d/2$, and says the mode split is orthogonal.
- Names typical values (LVDS 1.2 V, CAN 2.5 V, RS-485 range) and where they come from (driver offset, bias, AC coupling).
- Explains three consequences: input range/headroom, CMRR leakage, EMI from common-mode current; and the difference between common-mode voltage and current.
- Mentions skew and imbalance as sources and mode conversion as the failure.
- Describes how to measure it correctly (two probes + math, probe CM range).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating $V_{cm}$ as always zero.** It is the DC level of the pair relative to ground; many interfaces operate at 1.2 V or 2.5 V.
- **Confusing it with the average current** or with a signal. It is a voltage, the sum divided by two.
- **Forgetting headroom.** Both legs must remain in the allowed range: $V_{cm}\pm|V_d|/2$ within the input window.
- **Equating common-mode voltage with ground voltage.** A ground offset *produces* common-mode, but the common-mode level can differ from ground.
- **Measuring with a differential probe and expecting to see $V_{cm}$.** It shows $V_d$.
- **Ignoring common-mode current.** Even with a quiet $V_{cm}$ you can have radiating $I_{cm}$ from imbalance.
:::

Figure: The common-mode source lifts both wires; the two half-signals push them in opposite directions around that level. The receiver sees $V_d=V_+-V_-$ (the two half-signals add) and its common-mode input is $V_{cm}$.

Adding the two wires, $V_++V_-=2V_{cm}$ (the half-signals cancel). Subtracting, $V_+-V_-=V_d$ (the common level cancels). Those are the two measurements a scope or an amplifier makes.

Common-mode sources, in rough order of frequency of causing trouble:

1. **Ground potential difference** between driver and receiver (cables, building wiring).
2. **Driver offset**: output common-mode not matching what the receiver expects (e.g. a PECL driver feeding an LVDS receiver without level shifting).
3. **Interference** coupled equally onto both wires.
4. **Supply noise** modulating a driver whose offset follows its supply.
5. **Skew and edge asymmetry**: rising and falling edges that differ create common-mode glitches at each transition.

## Headroom: the real constraint

The receiver cares about each leg's absolute voltage, not just the difference. With $V_\pm=V_{cm}\pm V_d/2$, the top leg reaches $V_{cm}+|V_d|/2$ and the bottom leg $V_{cm}-|V_d|/2$. Both must be inside the allowed input window. Example: an LVDS receiver specified for inputs from 0 to 2.4 V. With a 350 mV differential swing each leg goes $\pm175$ mV from $V_{cm}$, so the allowable $V_{cm}$ window is about 0.175 V to 2.225 V. With 1.2 V nominal, the margin is about $\pm1$ V of ground offset. RS-485 $-7$ to $+12$ V gives about $\pm9.5$ V around mid, hence its use between buildings.

If the driver common-mode and the receiver window are mismatched (LVPECL at about $V_{CC}-1.3$ V into an LVDS input), you must AC-couple and re-bias, or use a level translator.

## Measuring $V_{cm}$ properly

- Two single-ended probes on P and N, ground to the same reference, matched lengths; scope math: $V_{cm}=(CH_1+CH_2)/2$, $V_d=CH_1-CH_2$.
- A differential probe reports $V_d$; check its stated *common-mode range* (±several V) and *CMRR* (falls with frequency, 40 to 60 dB at 100 MHz typical for a good one; check yours); a mis-specified probe may clip when $V_{cm}$ is large.
- Ground-lead loops and probe skew create their own errors: a 50 ps skew between probes on a 100 ps edge shows up as spurious $V_{cm}$ glitches.
- For EMC, measure common-mode **current** on the cable with a current probe around both conductors together. A few µA at hundreds of MHz can exceed limits.

## Common-mode current and EMI

$V_{cm}$ at the connector drives a current into the cable's capacitance to its environment (the cable is a monopole against ground). Equal-direction current $I_{cm}$ on a 1 m cable radiates efficiently because the loop it forms with the environment is large, even if the differential currents (equal and opposite) cancel. This is why:

- **Skew and imbalance** matter for emissions: they convert differential signal energy into $V_{cm}$ at the connector.
- **Common-mode chokes** are used on USB, Ethernet, HDMI: they present high impedance to $I_{cm}$ and ~zero to $I_d$.
- **Shield termination** matters: a shield connected only at one end or via a pigtail can carry $I_{cm}$ in the wrong place.

## How little common-mode current is too much

A short-cable monopole estimate (the standard Ott/Paul style approximation for a cable shorter than a wavelength) gives the far-field from a common-mode current $I_{cm}$ on a cable of length $L$, at distance $r$ in free space:
$$E\approx1.26\times10^{-6}\,\frac{f\,L\,I_{cm}}{r}\ \text{V/m}$$
with $f$ in Hz, $L$ and $r$ in m, $I_{cm}$ in A. For $f=100$ MHz, $L=1$ m, $r=3$ m and just $I_{cm}=5\ \mu$A: $E=2.1\times10^{-4}$ V/m, about 46 dB$\mu$V/m, which is above a typical 3 m Class B radiated limit of roughly 43 dB$\mu$V/m in that frequency range. A few microamps of common-mode current can therefore fail a product whose differential signals are perfect. Since $V_{cm}$ across a cable common-mode impedance of a few hundred ohms produces that current, 5 µA corresponds to only about 1 to 2 mV of common-mode voltage at the connector.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> This is a simplified free-space estimate (real results depend on the ground plane, cable routing and test setup, and the limit line depends on the standard, class and frequency band). Use it for orders of magnitude, not compliance.

## Common-mode in other contexts

- **Op-amps and difference amplifiers** have common-mode input range (headroom to the rails) and CMRR; a fully differential amplifier has a separate common-mode feedback loop that sets $V_{cm,out}$ (often the ADC's required mid-level).
- **ADCs** with differential inputs specify an input common-mode voltage (e.g. $V_{REF}/2$); driving them from a single-ended source requires a level-shifting driver.
- **Transformers and baluns** block DC and pass differential, defining their own common-mode relationship.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> One-liner: "Differential voltage is the signal, common-mode is the average, and every bad thing in a differential link is either common-mode that gets out of range, or common-mode that converts into differential." Then give an example value (1.2 V for LVDS).
:::

::: applications
- **LVDS receivers and drivers**: 1.2 V nominal offset, input range roughly 0 to 2.4 V.
- **RS-485 and CAN in industrial/automotive installations** with large ground differences.
- **Differential ADC drivers**: setting the input common-mode to the ADC's required level (commonly mid-supply).
- **AC-coupled serial links (PCIe, HDMI, SATA)**: independent common-mode at transmitter and receiver.
- **Instrumentation amplifiers**: bridge sensors with half-excitation common-mode.
- **EMC fixes**: common-mode chokes and ferrites on USB, Ethernet and display cables.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Voltages referenced to a common ground** for the definition $V_{cm}=(V_++V_-)/2$; with isolated systems, reference to the receiver's local ground.
- **Linear, symmetric** receiver and pair; otherwise some $V_{cm}$ appears as $V_d$.
- Typical values quoted are nominal; actual ranges depend on part and supply voltage.
- "Common-mode" here is voltage; common-mode *current* is mentioned for EMI but depends on cable and system geometry.
- Single-ended probing assumes probes with equal delay and sufficient bandwidth.

Alternative definition: some texts use the sum $V_++V_-$ (without the factor of 1/2) as common-mode, and the half difference $(V_+-V_-)/2$ as the differential mode; this changes only scale factors.
:::

::: followups
### $V_+=2.6$ V and $V_-=2.4$ V. What are $V_{cm}$ and $V_d$?
$V_{cm}=(2.6+2.4)/2=2.5$ V and $V_d=2.6-2.4=0.2$ V. This is roughly CAN in a state where the two lines are 0.2 V apart around the 2.5 V recessive level.

### Why do AC-coupled links not care about the driver's common-mode?
Series capacitors block DC, so the receiver sets its own common-mode with a bias network, independent of the transmitter. The cost is that the signal needs DC balance (e.g. 8b/10b or scrambling) so the average stays near zero and the capacitors do not charge up.

### A scope shows 1.7 V and 1.0 V on the two wires of an LVDS pair. Is the link healthy?
$V_{cm}=1.35$ V and $V_d=0.7$ V; both legs are in range. But 0.7 V differential is double the nominal 350 mV, so perhaps the scope is showing peak-to-peak of the combined swing, or the driver's current is double (check the termination: a missing 100 Ω load or 200 Ω would double the swing since $V=I\times R$). It points to an open or high-value termination, a good thing to verify.

### How can common-mode voltage cause EMI even if the differential signal is clean?
A common-mode voltage swing at the cable connector drives a net current along the cable (return through ground or the environment), forming a large effective antenna. Even a few mV of common-mode at hundreds of MHz can exceed radiated emission limits. Fix: improve pair balance and skew, add a common-mode choke, and terminate shields correctly.

### What if the receiver's common-mode range is 0 to 2.4 V and the driver offset is 1.2 V, but two nodes have 1.5 V of ground difference?
$V_{cm}$ at the receiver could be 2.7 V (or $-0.3$ V depending on polarity), outside the 0 to 2.4 V range. Options: use a transceiver with a wider range (RS-485 style), isolate the link (digital isolator, transformer), AC-couple and re-bias, or fix the ground difference (single-point grounding).
:::
