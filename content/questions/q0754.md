::: basics
### In plain words
To draw an I2C bus, put the controller on one side with two pins, SCL and SDA. Run two straight lines from those pins to every other chip, connecting SCL to SCL and SDA to SDA, with no crossing as for UART. At one point, add one resistor from each line up to the supply, called a pull-up. Tie all the grounds together. Give every chip a small capacitor at its supply pin. Strap each chip's address pins so no two chips share an address.

### Picture it
Imagine two shared water pipes, each fed from a supply tank through a thin tap. Any device can open a valve to a drain and pull its pipe empty. A pipe is full only when every valve is closed, and the thin tap refills it. That is a pull-up resistor and open-drain outputs. Since nobody pushes water in, no two devices can fight. In the drawing, the taps appear once for the whole bus, not at each device.

### Words you need
- **SCL and SDA**: the shared clock line and data line. Each connects to the same-named pin on every chip.
- **Pull-up resistor**: one resistor per line, to the supply, that makes the line high when nobody pulls it low ([[q0755]]).
- **Open-drain**: an output that can only pull the line low ([[q0761]]).
- **Common ground**: all chips share one ground. Without it, voltages on the lines mean nothing.
- **Decoupling capacitor**: a small capacitor, typically 100 nF, at each chip's supply pin to steady it.
- **Address strap**: tying an address pin to supply or ground to choose the chip's address.

### The simplest example
Draw a controller, two sensors, and two lines. Sensor A has its address pin tied to ground (0x48), and sensor B has it tied to supply (0x49), so the addresses differ. Add one 2.2 kΩ resistor from each line to 3.3 V. With about 100 pF of bus capacitance, the rise time is $0.85 \times 2200 \times 100\text{ pF} \approx 186$ ns, under the 300 ns limit for 400 kHz. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows the full drawing, a checklist, and the common drawing mistakes.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can produce a **correct, complete schematic** under pressure, not just say "SDA to SDA, SCL to SCL". A good drawing shows:

1. SDA and SCL are shared **straight through** (not crossed like UART TX/RX).
2. One pull-up resistor per line, tied to the bus supply, placed once for the whole bus.
3. A common ground, and the devices powered from a voltage compatible with the pull-up rail.
4. You understand *why* each piece is there (open-drain, wired-AND), and you label the address pins and any extras (interrupt, reset, decoupling).

It's also a quiet test of whether you can draw clean and talk while drawing.
:::

::: why
Half of all I2C bugs on a new board are wiring bugs: a missing pull-up, a pull-up tied to a rail that isn't powered, SDA and SCL swapped, two breakout boards with their own pull-ups stacked in parallel, or a ground that wasn't shared. If you can draw the standard topology and annotate it, you can also debug it. It's a classic whiteboard question because it takes 90 seconds and exposes everything.
:::

::: concepts
- **Bus topology**: all devices connect in parallel to the same SDA and SCL nets. See [[q0753]].
- **Open-drain with pull-ups**: each line has one resistor to $V_{DD}$; devices only pull low. See [[q0755]] and [[q0761]].
- **Common ground**: signals are referenced to ground; no shared ground, no communication. See [[q0001]].
- **Address selection**: strap pins A0 to A2 set the low bits of the address. See [[q0746]].
- **Decoupling**: a 100 nF capacitor at every device's supply pin. See [[q0032]].
:::

::: answer
"Sure. I'll draw the master on the left, say a microcontroller, and two horizontal lines coming out of it: SCL on top and SDA below. Every slave hangs off those two lines in parallel: SCL goes to SCL, SDA goes to SDA, no crossing like you'd do for UART. Then at the far end of the bus I put one resistor from each line up to the supply, typically around 2.2 to 4.7 kΩ at 3.3 V, so that's my pull-ups, one per line for the whole bus, not one per device.

Then I add the stuff people forget. All the grounds tie together. Each chip has its own VDD pin with a 100 nF decoupling capacitor, and the pull-ups go to the same rail the I2C pins are powered from. Slaves with address pins get A0, A1 and A2 strapped high or low so no two share an address. If a slave has an interrupt or alert pin, that's a separate wire back to an MCU GPIO, not part of the bus.

I'd also say what's happening electrically: every driver is an open-drain transistor that can only pull the line to ground, and the resistors pull it high when nobody's talking, which is why any number of devices can share the wire without fighting."
:::

::: technical
**Topology.** Two shared nets, SDA and SCL, plus ground. Every device's SDA pin goes to the SDA net, and every SCL pin goes to the SCL net. There is no crossover (the I2C names are the *same function*, not a direction). All devices must be at logic levels compatible with the bus; if the supplies differ, add a level translator that is rated for open-drain bidirectional lines. See [[q0877]].

**Pull-ups.** One $R_p$ per line to the bus supply $V_{DD}$, placed anywhere on the net (commonly at the master, or the far end for long runs). Value is bounded by

$$R_{p,min} = \frac{V_{DD}-V_{OL}}{I_{OL}},\qquad R_{p,max} = \frac{t_r}{0.8473\,C_b}$$

For 3.3 V, 3 mA, 0.4 V: $R_{p,min} \approx 967$ Ω; for $C_b = 100$ pF and Fast mode ($t_r = 300$ ns): $R_{p,max} \approx 3.5$ kΩ. A 2.2 kΩ resistor fits in the window. Details in [[q0755]].

**Per-device extras.**
- VDD pin: 100 nF ceramic as close as possible; bulk elsewhere.
- Address straps: A0..A2 to VDD or GND (never leave floating).
- Optional interrupt/alert/data-ready: open-drain output to a GPIO with its own pull-up.
- Optional reset or enable.
- Optional series resistors (22 to 100 Ω) on SDA and SCL at connector boundaries for ESD and ringing, but check that they don't push $V_{OL}$ above $V_{IL}$. See [[q0760]].

**What the drawing must make obvious:** which rail powers the pull-ups, that there is exactly one pull-up pair, and that ground is common.
:::

::: long
"I'd start with the master on the left, with two pins, SCL and SDA. From those I run two horizontal lines across the page, and every slave connects to them in parallel: SCL to SCL, SDA to SDA. That's straight through, unlike UART where you cross TX and RX.

On the right, I draw one resistor from each line up to the supply rail. That's the pull-up, around 2.2 kΩ to 4.7 kΩ for a 3.3 V bus. The important point is there's one per line, shared by the whole bus. I'd mention why they're there: every device's output is an open-drain transistor, so the only thing that can pull a line high is the resistor.

Then I clean it up so it's a real schematic and not just a cartoon. I add a ground that's common to every device, because the signals are relative to ground. Each slave has a VDD pin with a 100 nF decoupling cap, and I make sure the pull-ups go to the same rail the I2C pins are powered from, or at least a rail that's up whenever the bus is used. For slaves with address pins, I tie A0, A1, A2 high or low and write the resulting address next to each one so there's no collision. If a slave has an interrupt or alert output, that's its own wire to a GPIO, not part of the bus.

If I wanted to be thorough I'd add a small series resistor near any connector for ESD and edge control, and if the slaves run at a different voltage from the master, I'd put a level translator in the middle. And I'd label the pull-up values with the reasoning: low enough for the rise time with the bus capacitance, high enough that the weakest device can still pull the line below its low threshold."
:::

::: terms
- **SDA / SCL**: serial data and serial clock, the two bus lines.
- **Pull-up resistor ($R_p$)**: resistor from each line to the bus supply that provides the logic high.
- **Open-drain**: the driver is a MOSFET to ground with no pull-up transistor. See [[q0761]].
- **Wired-AND**: any device pulling low makes the whole line low.
- **Address strap**: tying A0..A2 to VDD or GND to set a device's address.
- **Decoupling capacitor**: local 100 nF supply bypass. See [[q0032]].
- **Level translator**: bidirectional buffer or FET shifter between voltage domains. See [[q0877]].
- **Controller / target**: current names for master and slave in the NXP spec.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws two wires from the MCU to two slaves and writes "pull-ups" somewhere.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws both pull-ups to the *same* supply as the bus domain, once, and shows their approximate value with the reason for it.
- Shows the shared ground and decoupling without being asked.
- Annotates addresses (e.g. 0x48, 0x49) next to the straps so a reviewer can verify no collision.
- Points out **what it isn't**: no crossover, no chip-select, interrupt lines go separately.
- Offers one-line failure modes: a missing pull-up gives a bus that never goes high, so the master sees no ACK; pull-ups to an unpowered rail pull the bus through an ESD diode; breakout-board pull-ups stack up.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Crossing SDA and SCL like UART TX/RX.** The names are functions, not directions; connect like-to-like.
- **No pull-ups, or "internal pull-ups will do".** MCU weak pull-ups are typically tens of kΩ, which gives a rise time many times too slow at anything above a few kHz. Fine on a bench with two inches of wire at 10 kHz, but not at 100 or 400 kHz.
- **One pull-up on each device.** Gives parallel resistors that may fall below $R_{p,min}$.
- **Pull-ups to a different rail than the device supply.** If the rail powering the pull-up is up but a device is off, current flows through the device's ESD clamp; if the pull-up goes to 5 V into a 3.3 V part you may exceed its absolute maximum.
- **Forgetting the common ground** between boards connected only by two signal wires.
- **Leaving address pins floating.** Floating means an undefined address and intermittent behaviour.
:::

## Draw it in this order (whiteboard routine)

1. **Master box, two bus pins.** Label them SCL and SDA.
2. **Two horizontal lines.** Run them the whole width.
3. **Slave boxes below,** each with SCL and SDA pins; tap with dots.
4. **Pull-ups at one end.** Resistor to the rail, one per line, labelled with a value.
5. **Ground and power.** Common ground, VDD rail, 100 nF decoupling.
6. **Annotations.** Addresses, maximum speed, supply voltage of the bus.

Saying each step out loud is half the answer.

## Why the circuit looks like this: one line, up close

Zoom in on a single wire. Every device connected to it has an N-channel MOSFET from the line to ground. Nothing connects the line to $V_{DD}$ except the resistor.

Figure: The line is high only if **both** transistors are off (resistor wins) and low if **either** is on. That is a wired-AND, and it's why the lines never short out when two devices disagree: a device that wants "1" simply doesn't drive.

When all transistors are off, the line charges through $R_p$ with time constant $R_pC_b$. When one turns on, it discharges through its on-resistance, which is much smaller than $R_p$, so falls are fast and rises are slow. That asymmetry is why I2C waveforms look like shark fins. See [[q0756]].

## Choosing the numbers you write on the drawing

Pick a bus speed and supply, and compute the pull-up window. For a 3.3 V, 400 kHz bus with about 100 pF:

- $R_{p,min} = (3.3-0.4)/3\text{ mA} = 967$ Ω
- $R_{p,max} = 300\text{ ns}/(0.8473\times100\text{ pF}) = 3.54$ kΩ

Pick something in the window, closer to the low side for speed margin: 2.2 kΩ. Check: $t_r = 0.8473\times2200\times100\text{ pF} = 186$ ns, under 300 ns. Low-state current is at most $2.9/2200 = 1.3$ mA (a little more if the driver pulls below 0.4 V), comfortably under 3 mA. Static power while low is at most $V_{DD}^2/R_p = 3.3^2/2200 \approx 5$ mW per line.

## Real-board details that don't fit in the cartoon

- **Where the pull-ups go**: anywhere on the net electrically; in practice near the master for short buses, or split between ends on a long one so reflections and capacitance are balanced. Don't put one at *each* slave.
- **Breakout boards**: cheap sensor modules often include 4.7 kΩ or 10 kΩ pull-ups to their own 3.3 V. Four of those in parallel gives about 1.2 kΩ to 2.5 kΩ.
- **Mixed voltages**: if the master is 3.3 V and a slave is 1.8 V, either run the pull-ups to the lower rail (if everyone tolerates it) or use a level translator. See [[q0877]].
- **Cable or inter-board**: add series resistors and ESD protection at the connector; keep SDA and SCL close together and away from noisy lines. A ground between them (or run both on a twisted pair with ground) reduces crosstalk.
- **Hot-plug**: the open-drain outputs and the high-impedance state when unpowered make I2C naturally hot-plug friendly, as long as the device input has no clamp diode to VDD that would pull the bus down. Many parts spec this explicitly.

## A pre-flight checklist you can run on the drawing

Before you call a bus drawing finished, walk it like a reviewer:

1. **Count the nets.** SDA, SCL, GND and the supply. If the drawing has a fifth shared signal, something is wrong (an interrupt line is separate, per device).
2. **Count the resistors.** Exactly two pull-ups on the bus (or one pair per physically separate segment behind a translator or mux). If every module drawn also has its own pull-ups, redo the parallel combination: $R_{eq}=1/\sum(1/R_i)$.
3. **Check the rail.** Does the pull-up rail exist whenever any bus device is powered? A pull-up to a rail that is off while the MCU is on holds the bus low through ESD diodes.
4. **Check each device's absolute maximum on SDA/SCL.** A 3.3 V part with pins rated to $V_{DD}+0.3$ V will not enjoy a 5 V pull-up.
5. **Write addresses next to every device.** Convert the strap pins to hex and make sure the list has no duplicates.
6. **Check speed against capacitance.** Estimate $C_b$, compute $t_r$, compare with 1000/300/120 ns.

Doing this on a whiteboard in 30 seconds is impressive; doing it on a real schematic review catches most first-spin I2C failures.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Draw first, talk while drawing, then offer one value-with-reasoning ("2.2 kΩ because it keeps a 100 pF bus under 300 ns, with room"). That one sentence proves you understand why the resistors exist.

::: applications
- **Sensor board on a microcontroller**: a 3.3 V MCU with a temperature sensor at 0x48 and a pressure sensor at 0x76, one pull-up pair on the board.
- **Camera module control (SCCB/I2C)**: the register interface of a CMOS image sensor is I2C-like while pixel data uses a separate interface.
- **Power management ICs**: a host configures regulators and chargers over I2C with an interrupt line for fault reporting.
- **Server DIMM SPD**: serial presence detect EEPROMs on memory modules share an I2C bus with strapped addresses.
- **Prototyping boards**: Qwiic / STEMMA-style daisy-chain connectors carry SDA, SCL, 3.3 V and GND on one cable, with the pull-ups on the host.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Single supply, single master, all devices at the same logic voltage.** Different voltages need translators; multiple masters add arbitration but not wires.
- **Short on-board bus.** Long cables need lower $R_p$, ESD parts, and maybe differential extenders.
- **3.3 V, 100 to 400 kHz.** The example values change with supply and speed; the topology does not.
- **Slaves with fixed behaviour on address pins.** Some parts latch addresses at power-on, and the strap must be stable then.

If asked "what about 5 V?", the minimum pull-up goes up ($4.6/3\text{ mA} \approx 1.5$ kΩ) and the maximum stays set by rise time; check all devices for 5 V tolerance.
:::

::: followups
### Where do you put the pull-up resistors?
One pair for the bus, usually near the master. It's electrically the same anywhere on the bus for short runs. On a long bus I might split into two parallel resistors at both ends, so the equivalent value is the same but the edges are cleaner at both ends. What I avoid is a pull-up at every device.

### Do you have to cross SDA and SCL?
No. They're shared lines, not directional TX and RX. SDA to SDA, SCL to SCL, for every device. Crossing them is one of the first things I'd check on a dead bus.

### My bus works with one sensor but fails when I add a second breakout board. Why?
Probably the extra module's own pull-ups: two 4.7 kΩ in parallel is 2.35 kΩ, three is 1.57 kΩ, and the effective $R_p$ may now be below $R_{p,min}$ so $V_{OL}$ rises above $V_{IL}$. Or an address collision. Or too much capacitance for the pull-up. Measure with a scope: the low level and the rise time tell you which.

### How would the drawing change for 1.8 V peripherals and a 3.3 V MCU?
Add a bidirectional open-drain level shifter (an auto-direction I2C translator or the classic single N-FET shifter) between the domains, with a pull-up on each side to its own rail. Don't pull the shared bus to 3.3 V if the 1.8 V part isn't rated for it.

### How would you draw this for a multi-master bus?
Same two wires; a second master just connects to the same SDA and SCL. Each master monitors SDA while it drives and backs off if it sees a 0 when it sent a 1 (arbitration). No extra wires, and it only works because the lines are wired-AND.
:::

::: rubric
- SCL and SDA shared by every device, no crossover :: Core of the topology; crossing is a red flag.
- One pull-up per line to the bus supply, with a value :: Shows you know why the bus works and that pull-ups aren't optional.
- Common ground drawn :: Signals are relative to ground; omitting it is a common sloppy mistake.
- Supply and decoupling for each device :: Distinguishes a schematic from a cartoon.
- Address straps annotated, no two slaves collide :: Practical check that the bus will actually work.
- Mentions open-drain / wired-AND as the reason :: Connects the picture to the physics.
- Notes level translation or extra pull-ups when voltages or modules differ :: Shows awareness of real-board hazards.
