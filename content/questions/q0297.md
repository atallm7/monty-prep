::: testing
Whether you can name the chemistry (lithium-ion, delivered as lithium polymer pouch cells in phones and thin devices and as cylindrical 18650/21700 cells in laptops and tools) and, more importantly, justify it with engineering reasons rather than "it's the best". The reasons are quantitative: energy per kilogram and per litre, a high cell voltage, low self-discharge, no memory effect, high power capability, and good cycle life. Strong candidates compare with the alternatives (NiMH, NiCd, lead-acid, alkaline) using numbers, and also mention the price paid: a protection circuit and care around safety.
:::

::: why
This is the "why did the industry converge here" question. If you can explain it with numbers, you can reason about the next-generation question: why not switch to something else? It also frames everything about batteries in a modern product: the 3.0-4.2 V supply window your regulators must handle, the charger, the protection IC, the thermal limits, and the safety regulations. In an interview it shows whether you understand the underlying trade-offs or just repeat "lithium is better".
:::

::: concepts
- **Energy density**: Wh/kg and Wh/L; lithium-ion is roughly 2-3 times NiMH and 5-8 times lead-acid at cell level ([[q0292]]).
- **Cell voltage**: about 3.6-3.7 V nominal vs 1.2 V for nickel cells, so one cell often runs a whole device.
- **Self-discharge**: a few percent per month vs tens of percent for NiMH.
- **Memory effect**: significant in NiCd, negligible in lithium-ion.
- **Form factor**: lithium polymer pouch cells can be made thin and in custom shapes ([[q0298]]).
- **Cost of the advantage**: needs charging/protection electronics and has safety constraints ([[q0295]]).
:::

::: answer
"Lithium-ion. In phones, wearables and tablets it's usually a lithium polymer pouch cell, and in laptops and power tools it's cylindrical lithium-ion like the 18650. Same chemistry family, different packaging.

Why? The big one is energy density. A lithium-ion cell stores something like 150 to 270 watt-hours per kilogram, which is two to three times NiMH and five to ten times lead-acid. For a phone that means a day of runtime in a few millimetres of thickness. Second, voltage: a cell is about 3.7 volts, so one cell can run the electronics through a regulator, where nickel cells at 1.2 volts need three in series to get the same voltage. Third, low self-discharge, a few percent a month, so it's still charged after sitting in a drawer. Fourth, there's no memory effect, so you can top up whenever. And it handles high current well and gets hundreds to a thousand-plus cycles.

The downsides are cost, the need for a charger and protection circuit, because overcharging or overheating is dangerous, and aging with temperature and time. But for portable electronics the energy density and compact size outweigh that, which is why it took over from NiCd and NiMH."
:::

::: technical
**The chemistry.** A lithium-ion cell has a graphite anode (lithium intercalated between carbon layers), a lithium metal-oxide cathode (LiCoO₂, NMC or NCA are the usual consumer ones), a separator and a lithium-salt organic electrolyte. On discharge Li⁺ moves from anode to cathode through the electrolyte while electrons flow through the external circuit ([[q0302]]).

**Numbers (typical, cell level).**

| Chemistry | Nominal V | Energy (Wh/kg) | Self-discharge | Cycle life (to 80%) | Memory effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Li-ion (LCO/NMC) | 3.6-3.7 | 150-270 | about 1-3 %/month | 500-1500+ | none |
| NiMH | 1.2 | 60-120 | about 15-30 %/month (low-self-discharge types much lower) | 300-1000 | minor |
| NiCd | 1.2 | 40-60 | about 10-20 %/month | 500-1500 | yes |
| Lead-acid | 2.0/cell | 30-50 | about 3-5 %/month | 200-500 (deep) | none |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> All table values are typical order-of-magnitude ranges; they depend on cell design, rate, temperature and test method. Use datasheets for design numbers.

**Why voltage helps.** Energy is $E=Q\times V_{avg}$. At 3.7 V a 1 Ah cell holds 3.7 Wh; at 1.2 V, 1.2 Wh. The high voltage per cell means fewer cells in series for a given voltage, fewer interconnects, and less mass in cases and tabs per Wh.

**Example.** A phone battery of 4000 mAh at 3.7 V holds 14.8 Wh. Lead-acid at 35 Wh/kg would weigh about 420 g, NiMH at 90 Wh/kg about 165 g, and Li-ion at 250 Wh/kg about 59 g.

**Power.** Li-ion has low internal resistance (tens of mΩ per cell), so it can deliver several C continuously; the efficiency (energy out/in) is typically above 90-95% at moderate rates, higher than NiMH.
:::

::: long
"The common chemistry is lithium-ion, in phones and wearables as a lithium polymer pouch cell, and in laptops and tools as cylindrical lithium-ion cells such as the 18650. It's the same electrochemistry, a graphite anode and a lithium metal-oxide cathode, just packaged differently.

The reasons are quantitative. First, energy density. A lithium-ion cell stores around 150 to 270 watt-hours per kilogram. NiMH is 60 to 120, lead-acid 30 to 50. So for a 15 watt-hour phone battery, lithium is about 60 grams, NiMH would be about 165, and lead-acid over 400. In a device you hold or wear, that's decisive. Volumetric density matters too, and pouch cells use space very efficiently.

Second, voltage. A lithium-ion cell is about 3.7 volts versus 1.2 for nickel cells, so one cell can supply the system through a regulator. Fewer cells in series means less connection and packaging overhead.

Third, self-discharge is low, a few percent a month, so the device is still charged after sitting. NiMH can lose a fifth of its charge in a month. Fourth, there's no meaningful memory effect, so partial charging is fine. And fifth, low internal resistance gives good power capability, with hundreds to over a thousand cycles.

The price we pay: it needs a proper charger and a protection circuit because overcharge, over-discharge, over-current and overheating are dangerous, it ages with temperature and high state of charge, and it's more expensive than lead-acid. But the energy density wins for portable electronics, which is why lithium replaced NiCd and NiMH in nearly everything."
:::

::: terms
- **Lithium-ion (Li-ion)**: rechargeable cell where lithium ions shuttle between a graphite anode and a metal-oxide cathode.
- **LiPo / lithium-ion polymer**: Li-ion cell with a gel/polymer electrolyte, usually in a flexible pouch ([[q0298]]).
- **18650**: cylindrical cell, 18 mm diameter, 65 mm long.
- **Energy density**: stored energy per unit mass or volume.
- **Memory effect**: capacity loss caused by repeated partial discharge (NiCd).
- **Self-discharge**: loss of charge during storage.
- **Intercalation**: reversible insertion of ions into a layered host (graphite, metal oxide).
- **Cycle life**: cycles to a stated capacity threshold.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "lithium-ion because it has high energy density and is rechargeable".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names the chemistry *and* the packaging (LiPo pouch vs 18650/21700) and clarifies they are the same electrochemistry.
- Backs each reason with a number: Wh/kg, 3.7 V vs 1.2 V, %/month self-discharge, cycles.
- Does a mass comparison for a real battery (15 Wh phone battery: 60 g vs 165 g vs 420 g).
- Explains why voltage matters (fewer series cells, better $E=QV$) and why no memory effect matters in daily use.
- States the trade-offs honestly: protection electronics, aging, safety, cost.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Calling LiPo a different chemistry from Li-ion.** The electrochemistry is mostly the same; "polymer" refers to the electrolyte/packaging ([[q0298]]).
- **Comparing mAh across chemistries.** 1000 mAh at 3.7 V is 3.7 Wh; at 1.2 V it is 1.2 Wh.
- **Saying lithium has "no downsides".** It has aging, safety risk, cold-charging limits and requires protection ([[q0295]]).
- **Claiming it has no self-discharge.** It is low, not zero, and a cell with a protection circuit also draws a small quiescent current.
- **Forgetting that "consumer electronics" includes several lithium variants.** LCO in phones, NMC in laptops/tools, LiFePO4 in some power banks and lights ([[q0300]]).
- **Ignoring that the voltage window is wide.** 4.2 V to 3.0 V is neither 3.3 V nor 5 V, so a regulator is almost always needed.
:::

::: deep
## What a Li-ion cell is

Think of two layered host materials separated by a porous insulating membrane soaked in electrolyte. Lithium ions can slot in and out of both hosts. When charged, the lithium sits in the graphite (the anode, at a low electrochemical potential); when discharged, it sits in the metal-oxide (the cathode, at a high potential). The cell voltage is the potential difference between the two, about 3.6-3.7 V on average for graphite versus an NMC/LCO cathode. Charging forces the ions back to the graphite by pushing current backwards; discharging lets them flow spontaneously and drives electrons through your load.

## The reasons, one at a time

**1. Energy density: lithium is light and electropositive.** Lithium is the lightest metal and has the most negative standard electrode potential, so a lithium-based cell gets a high voltage from very little mass. Energy per kg is $E/m = Q V_{avg}/m$; both the high voltage (3.7 V) and the high specific capacity of the electrodes (graphite about 372 mAh/g theoretical for the anode material alone; cathodes about 140-220 mAh/g in practice) contribute.

**2. Voltage per cell.** $E=QV$ per cell means 3.7 V cells hold 3× the energy of 1.2 V cells at the same capacity. It also means a 3.3 V logic supply can be made from a single cell using a buck-boost or LDO, and a 5 V system with a single boost converter. A NiMH design needs three cells just to reach about 3.6 V, with three times the cases, tabs and connections.

**3. Self-discharge.** Li-ion cells lose about 1-3% per month (temperature-dependent); a 1.5%/month cell loses about 17% per year (computed: $1-0.985^{12}$). NiMH loses about 15-30% in the first month for standard types. For a product used intermittently (a phone, a camera) that is a major usability gain.

**4. No memory effect and flexible charging.** NiCd shows an apparent capacity loss after repeated partial discharges. Li-ion doesn't: you can top up at any state of charge, and partial cycles are actually *gentler* than full ones ([[q0301]]).

**5. Power and efficiency.** Li-ion cells have internal resistance of tens of milliohms, so they supply multi-C pulses with a modest voltage sag and round-trip energy efficiency of 90-95% at moderate rates.

**6. Form factor.** Lithium-polymer pouch cells have no rigid can, so they are made thin and in custom shapes ([[q0298]]); cylinders are cheap, mass-produced and mechanically robust.

## Why it is not perfect

| Cost of the benefit | What it means for the designer |
|---|---|
| Narrow safe voltage window (about 2.5/3.0-4.2 V) | Need a charger with 1% accuracy and a protection IC |
| Flammable electrolyte, energetic cathode | Safety standards (IEC 62133, UN 38.3), thermal design, abuse testing |
| Aging with temperature and high SoC | Avoid heat, consider charge limits (80%) for longevity |
| Cannot be charged cold | Temperature sensing and charge inhibit |
| More expensive than lead-acid per kWh at small scale | Cost-sensitive products still use alkaline or lead-acid |

## Why other options still exist

Lead-acid remains cost-effective for starter batteries and backup power because it tolerates abuse and delivers huge surge current. NiMH is a drop-in for AA/AAA format and still used in some toys, remotes and flashlights, plus hybrid cars historically. LiFePO4 is chosen when cycle life and safety dominate ([[q0300]]). Primary lithium and alkaline cells suit devices that are rarely used.

::: applications
- **Smartphones and tablets**: single-cell or two-cell LiPo/Li-ion pouch cells (about 10-20 Wh), with a fuel gauge and charger IC.
- **Laptops**: 2-4S packs of cylindrical or prismatic cells with a BMS.
- **Wireless earbuds and smartwatches**: tiny LiPo or coin-form Li-ion cells (tens to hundreds of mAh).
- **Power tools and e-bikes**: 18650/21700 cells in series/parallel strings.
- **Power banks, cameras, drones**: Li-ion/LiPo for compactness and current.
:::

::: assumptions
"Consumer electronics" is taken to mean phones, laptops, wearables, cameras, tools and similar. The answer assumes the standard graphite-anode Li-ion family (LCO/NMC/NCA cathodes); some products use LiFePO4 or other variants. Numbers are cell-level typical values and ignore pack mass overhead (casing, protection, connectors), which reduces pack-level density by typically 20-40%.

A fair counter-argument: for very cost-sensitive or high-abuse applications (starter batteries, toys, backup power) the "best" chemistry is not Li-ion. And future technologies (solid state, silicon anodes, sodium-ion) may change the answer.
:::

::: followups
### Is a LiPo battery different from a Li-ion battery?
Mostly packaging and electrolyte form. Both use lithium-ion intercalation chemistry. A "LiPo" typically uses a polymer/gel electrolyte in a soft aluminum-laminate pouch, which allows thin custom shapes, while "Li-ion" often means a rigid metal can (cylindrical or prismatic) with liquid electrolyte. See [[q0298]] for the meaning of the name.

### Why does a single Li-ion cell need a regulator for a 3.3 V device?
Its voltage swings from about 4.2 V full to about 3.0 V empty. A 3.3 V rail must be higher than 3.0 V at the low end, so a buck-boost is needed to hold it, or the device must run directly from the cell's range. A plain LDO works only while the cell stays above 3.3 V plus dropout, wasting capacity below that.

### Why not use NiMH, since it's cheaper and safer?
NiMH has roughly a third to half of Li-ion's energy density, needs three cells in series for about 3.6 V, and self-discharges faster, so a phone would be thick and heavy. It is a fine choice where AA format, robustness and low cost matter more than size.

### What is the downside of the high energy density?
The stored energy can be released violently if the cell is abused: overcharge, short circuit, puncture or heat can lead to thermal runaway ([[q0327]]). So Li-ion needs a protection circuit, careful charger design, and certification.

### What if you needed a battery that survives 5000 cycles in stationary storage?
I'd pick LiFePO4 or LTO instead of the high-energy NMC/LCO used in phones. They give up energy density but reach thousands of cycles and run safer. In stationary storage weight does not matter much, so the trade is sensible.
:::
