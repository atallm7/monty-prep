::: basics
### In plain words
A sample-and-hold circuit grabs the input voltage at one instant and holds it steady. An ADC needs time to do its measurement. If the voltage moves during that time, the answer is garbage. Holding a frozen copy fixes that.

### Picture it
It is like taking a photo of a runner. The runner keeps moving, but the photo does not. You can take your time measuring the runner's position in the photo. The circuit does this with a switch and a capacitor: while the switch is closed, the capacitor follows the input. When the switch opens, it keeps the last voltage, like the photo.

### Words you need
- **Capacitor** ([[q0022]]): a part that stores electric charge, and with it a voltage.
- **Track mode**: the switch is closed and the capacitor follows the input.
- **Hold mode**: the switch is open and the capacitor keeps its last value.
- **Acquisition time**: how long the switch must stay closed for the capacitor to catch up with the input.
- **Buffer**: an amplifier that copies a voltage without drawing current from it.

### The simplest example
The input is 1.2 V and the switch is closed, so the capacitor sits at 1.2 V. Then the switch opens. The input moves on to 2 V, but the capacitor still holds 1.2 V. The ADC takes its time converting 1.2 V and the answer is clean. Without the hold, a 1 kHz sine that swings across the whole 3.3 V range can move about 0.8 mV, one 12-bit step, in just 78 ns, far shorter than a conversion.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the circuit, how fast an input can change before it matters, settling in track mode, and what goes wrong during the hold.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can say **why** an ADC needs a sample-and-hold and show that you understand its two phases: track (the switch is closed and the capacitor follows the input) and hold (the switch opens and the capacitor keeps the value). The interviewer wants a drawn circuit (switch, capacitor, buffer), a clear reason (the input must not move while the converter works), and at least one number, such as how fast a signal can change before it causes a one-LSB error during the conversion. This question is the first of a set ([[q0815]], [[q0816]], [[q0817]]), so the answer should set up the vocabulary for those.
:::

::: why
The sample-and-hold (S/H) is the front door of nearly every ADC. Its quality sets the ADC's real-world performance at high input frequencies: aperture error, droop, charge injection and kT/C noise all start here. For an embedded engineer it explains why a microcontroller ADC has a "sampling time" setting, why you need a capacitor on a high-impedance input, and why a signal that moves quickly gives a different result depending on when you sample it. It is the hardware that implements sampling ([[q0813]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor as memory**: $Q=CV$; an isolated capacitor keeps its voltage. See [[q0022]].
- **Switch**: a MOSFET (or transmission gate) with on-resistance $R_{on}$ and off-leakage.
- **Track and hold phases**: tracking is an RC settling; holding is an open circuit.
- **Buffer**: a high-impedance follower that isolates the capacitor from the load.
- **Sampling theory**: the S/H is the physical sampling operation. See [[q0813]].
- **Conversion time**: the ADC needs the input frozen for $N$ cycles in a SAR ([[q0810]]).
:::

::: answer
"A sample and hold is a circuit that grabs the input voltage at a specific instant and holds it steady while the ADC converts it. The simplest version is a switch and a capacitor. When the switch is closed, which we call the track or sample phase, the capacitor charges up and follows the input. When the switch opens, the capacitor is isolated and keeps its voltage. That's the hold phase. Usually there's a buffer amplifier after the capacitor so whatever is reading it doesn't discharge it.

Why do we need it? An ADC takes time to convert, a SAR takes N clock cycles. If the input moves during that time, the early bits get decided at one voltage and the late bits at another, and the result is garbage. The hold freezes the value.

A rough number: a 1 kHz, full-scale 3.3 V sine moves at up to about 10 volts per millisecond, so in 78 nanoseconds it moves by one LSB of a 12-bit converter. So the sampling instant has to be defined much more precisely than that, and the hold removes the problem for the rest of the conversion. In a microcontroller the sampling capacitor is inside the ADC, which is why you set a sampling time and why a high-impedance source needs a longer one."
:::

::: technical
**Structure.** Input buffer (optional) → switch $S$ ($R_{on}$) → hold capacitor $C_H$ to ground → output buffer. Clock $\phi$ closes $S$ in track mode and opens it in hold mode.

**Track phase.** The capacitor charges through $R_{on}$ (plus any source resistance $R_s$):

$$v_C(t)=V_{in}-(V_{in}-v_0)e^{-t/\tau},\qquad\tau=(R_s+R_{on})C_H$$

To settle to within $\tfrac12$ LSB of an $N$-bit converter you need $t_{acq}\ge\tau\ln2^{N+1}$: $9.0\tau$ for 12 bits, $11.8\tau$ for 16 bits.

**Hold phase.** With the switch open the capacitor voltage changes only by non-idealities:
- *Droop*: $dV/dt=I_{leak}/C_H$. A 1 nA leakage on 10 pF is 100 V/s, 0.1 mV per microsecond.
- *Charge injection / clock feedthrough*: opening the switch dumps channel charge $Q_{inj}$ onto $C_H$, a pedestal $\Delta V=Q_{inj}/C_H$.
- *Dielectric absorption*: a capacitor "remembers" a previous voltage and creeps back.
- *Feedthrough*: input signal coupling across the open switch's capacitance.

**Aperture error.** The sampling instant is when the switch opens, but it has uncertainty (aperture jitter $t_{aj}$) and delay. A sine of amplitude $A$ and frequency $f$ changes at up to $2\pi fA$, so the maximum error is $2\pi fA\,\Delta t$. Requiring error below 1 LSB of an $N$-bit converter gives

$$\Delta t<\frac{V_{FS}/2^N}{2\pi f A}$$

For a 12-bit, 3.3 V converter ($V_{LSB}=0.806$ mV) and $A=1.65$ V at 1 kHz: $\Delta t<77.7$ ns. Without a hold the whole conversion window would need to be that short.

**Noise.** Sampled thermal noise on $C_H$ is $\sqrt{kT/C_H}$: 64 µV at 1 pF, 20 µV at 10 pF at 300 K.

> [!NOTE]
> Many SAR ADCs do not have a separate buffered S/H. The capacitor DAC itself is the hold capacitor, and the input switch closing is the sampling event ([[q0810]]).
:::

::: short
"A sample and hold captures the input voltage at one instant and holds it constant while the ADC converts. It's a switch plus a capacitor, usually followed by a buffer. Close the switch and the capacitor tracks the input. Open it and the capacitor holds the value. It's needed because a conversion takes time, and if the input moves during it, different bits would be decided at different voltages. Real ones have droop, charge injection and kT/C noise, and the track phase must be long enough to settle."
:::

::: long
"A sample-and-hold is the circuit that turns a moving analog voltage into a still one that an ADC can measure. The core of it is a switch and a capacitor, with a buffer amplifier on the output.

There are two phases. In track, or sample, mode the switch is closed, so the capacitor is connected to the input through the switch's on-resistance. It charges exponentially toward the input with a time constant of the total resistance times the capacitance. In hold mode the switch opens. The capacitor is now isolated, and since a capacitor stores charge, it keeps its voltage. The buffer, which has extremely high input impedance, lets the following circuit read it without draining it.

The reason we need it is that conversion isn't instant. A SAR takes N clock cycles, and the answer is only meaningful if the input is the same during all of them. If you're measuring a 1 kHz, 3.3 V sine and it moves at up to about 10 V per millisecond, then in just 78 nanoseconds the input changes by one LSB at 12 bits. Without a hold, the converter would need to be that fast. With a hold, the sampling instant defines the measurement, and the conversion happens in peace.

It's not perfect though. During hold the capacitor slowly droops from leakage current. When the switch opens, a little channel charge gets injected, which creates a small step called the pedestal. The capacitor adds kT/C noise, so you'd like it bigger, but a bigger capacitor takes longer to charge, so there's a trade-off, which is the subject of the next questions. And in track mode you need enough time, about 9 time constants for 12 bits, for the capacitor to reach the input.

In a microcontroller, the capacitor is inside the ADC and the sampling time register controls how long the switch stays closed."
:::

::: terms
- **Track (sample) mode**: switch closed; output follows the input.
- **Hold mode**: switch open; output frozen at the last value.
- **Acquisition time**: time in track mode to settle within the required accuracy.
- **Aperture time / delay / jitter**: the time the switch takes to open, its fixed delay, and the random variation of that instant.
- **Droop rate**: change of the held voltage per unit time, $I_{leak}/C_H$.
- **Pedestal / charge injection**: step in the held voltage when the switch turns off.
- **Feedthrough**: input signal leaking through the open switch.
- **Hold capacitor ($C_H$)**: the capacitor that stores the sampled voltage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it samples the voltage and holds it for the ADC".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws **switch, $C_H$ and buffer** and labels track and hold.
- Gives **why** with a number: the aperture calculation (about 78 ns for a 1 kHz full-scale sine at 12 bits).
- Gives the **settling requirement** $t\ge\tau\ln2^{N+1}$.
- Lists the **non-idealities** (droop, charge injection, feedthrough, kT/C) and what each depends on.
- Notes that in a **SAR the capacitor array is the hold capacitor**, so the S/H is built in.
- Distinguishes **sampling** (time) from the S/H hardware and connects to the MCU's sampling-time setting.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying the S/H "is the ADC".** It is a front-end block. The quantizer is separate.
- **Forgetting the buffer.** Without one, the load discharges the capacitor or the source impedance disturbs it.
- **Treating hold as perfect.** Leakage and charge injection are real, and temperature doubles leakage roughly every 10 °C.
- **Ignoring the track phase.** If it's too short the capacitor hasn't reached the input and the sample is wrong, which looks like a gain error that depends on source impedance.
- **"Sample and hold is needed because the ADC is slow."** It's needed because the input *moves*. A very slow input (a thermistor) doesn't need one in practice.
- **Confusing track-and-hold with sample-and-hold.** The difference is mostly terminology: a T/H tracks continuously and holds on command; an S/H is idealised as an instant sample. Many data sheets use them interchangeably.
:::

::: deep
## The problem it solves

Imagine a SAR ADC measuring a ramp. Bit 11 is decided at time $t_1$ when the input is 1.00 V, bit 10 at $t_2$ when it is 1.05 V, and so on. The final code is the result of comparisons against 12 different input values and corresponds to none of them. The comparator's "is the input above the trial voltage?" question is only meaningful if the input is the same on every trial. The S/H guarantees that.

For slow inputs the problem vanishes, because the signal doesn't move in 1 µs. For fast ones it dominates.

## How fast can the input move? The aperture calculation

If the input changes by more than one LSB while it's being sampled, the sample is ambiguous. For a sine $A\sin(2\pi ft)$ the maximum slope is $2\pi fA$. For the 12-bit, 3.3 V case ($V_{LSB}=0.806$ mV, $A=1.65$ V):

| $f$ | max slope | time to move 1 LSB |
|---|---|---|
| 100 Hz | 1.04 V/ms | 777 ns |
| 1 kHz | 10.4 V/ms | 77.7 ns |
| 10 kHz | 104 V/ms | 7.8 ns |
| 100 kHz | 1.04 V/µs | 0.78 ns |

Without a hold, a SAR taking 1 µs per conversion would see the input move by 13 LSB at 1 kHz. With the hold, the effective window is the *aperture uncertainty*, which for a good switch is tens of picoseconds.

## Track phase: settling

During track, the capacitor charges through $R_s+R_{on}$:

$$\varepsilon(t)=e^{-t/\tau}$$

Required settling to half an LSB in 12 bits: $e^{-t/\tau}<1/8192\Rightarrow t>9.01\tau$. With $R_{on}=1$ kΩ, $R_s=10$ kΩ and $C_H=8$ pF: $\tau=88$ ns and $t_{acq}=0.79$ µs. This is the origin of every MCU's "minimum sampling time" table, covered in [[q0817]].

## What goes wrong in hold

- **Droop**: $\Delta V=I_{leak}t/C_H$. With 1 nA and 10 pF over a 1 µs conversion, 0.1 mV, just 0.12 LSB. Over a 100 µs hold it would be 10 mV, 12 LSB. Leakage roughly doubles every 10 °C, so at 85 °C it could be 60 times larger than at 25 °C.
- **Pedestal**: if opening the switch injects 3 fC, a 10 pF hold capacitor shifts by 0.3 mV. It is a constant offset if the injection is signal-independent, and a gain/non-linearity error if it depends on the input level (it does, since channel charge depends on $V_{GS}-V_t$).
- **Feedthrough**: a 0.1 pF switch capacitance against a 10 pF hold cap lets $0.1/10.1=1\%$ of the input swing through. Designs minimise this with series-shunt switches.
- **Noise**: $\sqrt{kT/C_H}$ is 20 µV at 10 pF.

## The S/H is often the capacitor DAC

In a SAR ([[q0810]]) the capacitor array is the hold element. The input switch closing is "track", opening it is "hold", and there is no separate buffer. This is why the datasheet gives an *input sampling capacitance* of a few pF and a *maximum source resistance*: they are the S/H parameters.

## Worked example: sizing the hold capacitor

The earlier sections quote $C_H=8$ to 10 pF without saying why. The capacitor is a three-way compromise, and computing the three limits shows where it must sit.

**Lower limit from thermal noise.** Closing the switch leaves a random $kT/C$ voltage on the capacitor, with rms value $\sqrt{kT/C_H}$. At 300 K and 10 pF that is 20 µV, as stated above. For 12 bits and a 3.3 V range ($V_{LSB}=0.806$ mV), keep the sampling noise below a quarter of an LSB, 0.2 mV, which gives

$$C_H\ge\frac{kT}{(0.2\ \text{mV})^2}=\frac{4.14\times10^{-21}}{4\times10^{-8}}=0.10\ \text{pF}.$$

This is a tiny capacitor, so a 12-bit converter is not noise-limited by $kT/C$. Repeat it for 16 bits ($V_{LSB}=50.4\ \mu$V, quarter-LSB 12.6 µV) and the requirement jumps to $kT/(12.6\ \mu\text{V})^2=26$ pF. At 1 pF the rms noise would be 64 µV, more than one 16-bit LSB. High-resolution converters therefore carry tens of picofarads of sampling capacitance, and that determines how hard they are to drive.

**Upper limit from acquisition time.** The track time constant is $\tau=(R_s+R_{on})C_H$. With 11 kΩ total series resistance and 8 pF, $\tau=88$ ns. Settling to half an LSB needs $\ln(2^{N+1})$ time constants: 9.0 for 12 bits (0.79 µs) and 11.8 for 16 bits. For the 16-bit case with the same resistance and a 26 pF capacitor, $\tau=286$ ns and the acquisition time is $11.8\times286=3.4\ \mu$s, so acquisition alone limits the rate to under about 0.3 MS/s before conversion time is even added. Bigger $C_H$ means slower tracking, or a lower source resistance with a stronger input buffer.

**A second lower limit from droop and charge injection.** Leakage droop is $I_{leak}t_{hold}/C_H$, and falls as $C_H$ grows. For 1 nA at 25 °C and 10 pF the droop is 0.124 LSB per microsecond, so the half-LSB limit is reached after $0.403\ \text{mV}\times10\ \text{pF}/1\ \text{nA}=4.0\ \mu$s. A SAR whose conversion takes 1 µs is safe; a slow converter or a shared S/H that must hold for 5 µs is not. At 85 °C, with the leakage assumed 64 times larger, the same capacitor droops $64\ \text{nA}\times1\ \mu\text{s}/10\ \text{pF}=6.4$ mV in one microsecond, which is 8 LSB, a clear failure. Charge injection also favors a larger capacitor: the pedestal $\Delta V=Q_{inj}/C_H$ halves when $C_H$ doubles.

So the design window for $C_H$ is bounded below by noise and droop and above by settling speed. The values in real parts, in the single-digit to tens of picofarads, sit where those three limits cross for the intended resolution and sample rate.

## Worked example: aperture jitter limits the fastest input

Section "How fast can the input move" said the aperture uncertainty is tens of picoseconds. Turn that into a requirement. For a full-scale sine of frequency $f$ and rms timing jitter $\sigma_t$ on the sampling instant, the best possible SNR from jitter alone is $-20\log_{10}(2\pi f\sigma_t)$. For this to stay at the 12-bit ideal of 74 dB, the jitter allowed is

$$\sigma_t\le\frac{10^{-74/20}}{2\pi f}.$$

At $f=100$ kHz this is 318 ps. A hardware timer trigger clocked from a crystal-derived system clock usually meets that, but a software-started conversion (nanoseconds of interrupt-latency jitter) does not. At 1 MHz it is 32 ps, and a microcontroller's timer-triggered conversion starts to be the limit, because the clock tree and trigger path rarely hold below tens of picoseconds. At 10 MHz the allowance is 3.2 ps, which is the domain of dedicated converters with low-jitter clock inputs and careful clock distribution. This is a different limit from settling: you can settle perfectly and still lose bits because the sampling instant is uncertain.

The switch's own aperture uncertainty adds in quadrature with the clock jitter: $\sigma_{total}=\sqrt{\sigma_{clk}^2+\sigma_{ap}^2}$. A system with 20 ps of clock jitter and 10 ps of switch uncertainty has 22 ps total, so improving the switch gets you little unless the clock improves too.

## Limiting cases and when the simple model fails

- **Switch resistance depends on signal.** A single MOS switch has an on-resistance that rises sharply when the input approaches either rail, so the time constant and the distortion depend on the input level. Real switches use complementary (CMOS transmission-gate) pairs, bootstrapped gates to hold $V_{GS}$ constant, or both. A bootstrapped switch keeps $R_{on}$ nearly independent of the input, which flattens the distortion.
- **Slewing in the input buffer.** The open-loop model assumes A1 can charge $C_H$ instantly. If the buffer's slew rate is insufficient for a large step, the track time is set by slewing, not by $\tau$.
- **Hold-mode feedthrough at high frequency.** With the switch open, the capacitance across it (0.1 pF against 10 pF) leaks 1% of the input, about 33 mV on a 3.3 V swing, into the held voltage. To first order this capacitive-divider ratio is flat with frequency, but it matters most at high input frequencies because the input moves a lot during the hold time. Series-shunt switch arrangements put a grounded switch to shunt the leak while holding.
- **Dielectric absorption** in external hold capacitors (as in older discrete designs) makes the capacitor "remember" its previous voltage; for a discrete S/H use a polystyrene, PTFE or NP0 capacitor rather than an X7R part.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Leakage current, its temperature doubling rate, switch charge injection and clock jitter figures used here are illustrative. Real values depend on process, switch geometry and package; consult the specific ADC datasheet.

## How to verify a sample-and-hold on the bench

1. **Droop test.** Drive a DC level, trigger a conversion, and insert a longer delay between sample and conversion if the part allows it (some converters have a settable hold delay). Plot the code against delay; the slope is droop in LSB per microsecond, and it should rise with temperature.
2. **Acquisition test.** Hold the source at 0 V, step it to full scale just before the sample and sweep the sample time from short to long. The reading approaches the final value exponentially; the time to get within half an LSB is the real acquisition time, which you compare with $9\tau$ for 12 bits.
3. **Jitter test.** Sample a pure high-frequency sine near full scale from a low-jitter generator, and compute SNR from an FFT. Subtract the converter's other noise sources (measured with a DC input) to isolate the jitter contribution, and check it against the formula above at two different input frequencies; jitter noise rises 6 dB per octave of input frequency while other noise stays constant.
4. **Feedthrough test.** With the converter idle (or the sampling switch off), apply a full-scale high-frequency tone and look for a spur in the output at the input frequency.

::: applications
- **Every SAR ADC in a microcontroller**: the sampling capacitor and input switch.
- **Multiplexed data acquisition**: a S/H per channel or simultaneous-sampling S/H arrays to capture several signals at the same instant (for example, three motor phase currents).
- **Oscilloscope front ends**: track-and-hold circuits in front of interleaved ADCs.
- **Analog sample delay**: capturing a peak or a value at a trigger time.
- **Switched-capacitor circuits**: filters and delta-sigma modulators are chains of sampling operations.
- **Audio codecs**: S/H inside the converter before the quantizer.
:::

::: assumptions
- An **ideal switch** during track (resistive) and ideal open circuit during hold; the real parts add leakage, charge injection and feedthrough.
- **Ideal buffer** with no offset or droop. Real op-amps add offset, bias current and finite bandwidth.
- The input is slow enough to be tracked within the acquisition window. At high frequency the track bandwidth $1/(2\pi\tau)$ also limits the input.
- A **unity gain** S/H. Gain-of-two or differential versions exist for different ADC ranges.
- Terminology: some texts reserve "track and hold" for the continuously tracking version. Treat them as equivalent unless told otherwise.
:::

::: followups
### Why not just use a faster ADC and skip the S/H?
For slow signals you can. For a signal changing by more than an LSB during conversion, you'd need the conversion to be shorter than the 78 ns computed above (for 12 bits at 1 kHz), which is impractical for a SAR and doesn't help flash ADCs with comparator timing skew. The S/H decouples the sample instant from the conversion time.

### How big should the hold capacitor be?
Big enough that $\sqrt{kT/C}$ is well under an LSB and droop and pedestal are small; small enough to settle in the sampling window and not overload the source. For 12 bits that typically lands at a few pF up to tens of pF ([[q0816]]).

### What is aperture jitter and when does it matter?
Random variation of the sampling instant. It converts to amplitude noise with the signal's slope: $\text{SNR}=-20\log_{10}(2\pi f\sigma_t)$. At 1 MHz with 1 ps it is 104 dB, but at 100 MHz it is 64 dB, so it limits high-frequency performance ([[q0813]]).

### What happens if the sampling time is too short on a microcontroller?
The sampling capacitor doesn't reach the input. The reading is pulled toward the previous channel's voltage and the error depends on source impedance. A 100 kΩ sensor with 8 pF needs 7 µs for 12 bits.

### Harder: you must sample three phase currents at the same instant. What does that require?
Three S/H circuits triggered by the same clock (simultaneous sampling), or an ADC with multiple S/Hs, followed by sequential conversion. A single multiplexed ADC with one S/H samples the phases at different times, producing a phase error equal to the signal slope times the delay, which matters in field-oriented motor control.
:::
