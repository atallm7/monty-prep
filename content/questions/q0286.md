::: basics
### In plain words
The standard way to draw a charge pump is a voltage doubler with four switches. A flying capacitor sits in the middle, with two switches on each plate. One pair of switches charges it from the input. The other pair stacks it on the input and connects it to the output. A capacitor and the load sit at the output.

### Picture it
Picture a ferry that shuttles between two docks. At the first dock it loads up from the supply. At the second dock, it is lifted onto a platform at the supply level and unloads into the output tank. The two docks are never used at the same moment, which matches how the two switch phases must never overlap. The ferry analogy does not show the voltage stacking, which is the heart of the circuit.

### Words you need
- **Switches S1 to S4**: four transistor switches that connect the flying capacitor's plates.
- **Clock phases ($\phi_1$, $\phi_2$)**: the two alternating settings of the switches.
- **Non-overlapping**: the two phases are never closed together, so the supply is never shorted.
- **Top plate and bottom plate**: the two sides of the flying capacitor.
- **Inverter**: a variant that gives $-V_{in}$ at the output ([[q0284]]).

### The simplest example
Draw the input on the left, the flying capacitor in the middle, and the output capacitor and load on the right. In phase one, close S1 (top plate to $V_{in}$) and S2 (bottom plate to ground), so the capacitor charges to $V_{in}$. In phase two, close S3 (bottom plate to $V_{in}$) and S4 (top plate to the output), so the output is $2V_{in}$. If S2 and S3 closed together, they would short the supply to ground.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page lists every connection, the diode version, the inverter, and how the switches are built.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can sketch a correct charge pump **and label the phases**. The expected drawing is a voltage doubler: input source, flying capacitor, output capacitor, load, and four switches (or two diodes in the passive version), with the switch states marked for $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$. The interviewer checks that the flying capacitor's plates go to the right nodes in each phase, that a ground reference is drawn, and that you can narrate the charge flow while you draw.
:::

::: why
A drawing is the fastest way to expose whether you understand the topology or just remember the phrase "flying capacitor". The same four-switch cell becomes a doubler, an inverter or a 1.5x pump just by re-wiring, so a clean doubler sketch is the base for every follow-up in this section.
:::

::: concepts
- **Flying capacitor**: moves between across-input and series-with-input positions. See [[q0284]].
- **Output (reservoir) capacitor**: connected to the output permanently.
- **Two-phase switching**: $\phi_1$ switches S1, S2 and $\phi_2$ switches S3, S4, never overlapping.
- **Diode version (Dickson)**: diodes replace switches and a clock drives the capacitor. See [[q0285]].
- **Polarity marks** on capacitors, ground symbol, labeled $V_{out}$. Compare the boost drawing in [[q0272]].
:::

"I'll draw a voltage doubler. On the left, the input source $V_{in}$ with ground along the bottom. In the middle, the flying capacitor, drawn vertically. The top plate connects to $V_{in}$ through switch S1 and to the output through switch S4. The bottom plate connects to ground through S2 and to $V_{in}$ through S3. On the right, the output node, with the output capacitor to ground and the load.

Phase one: S1 and S2 closed, so the flying cap is across the input and charges to $V_{in}$. Phase two: S3 and S4 closed, so its bottom plate is at $V_{in}$ and its top plate is on the output, which makes $2V_{in}$.

I'd mark the capacitor polarity, the clock phases, and say that the phases must not overlap or I'd short the supply. If asked for a passive version I'd draw two diodes and a clock: $V_{in}$ through D1 to a node, D2 from that node to the output, the flying capacitor from the node to a square-wave clock, and the output capacitor to ground. And for an inverter I'd keep the same parts but connect the top plate to ground in phase two and take the output from the bottom plate."

::: technical
**Switch-based doubler netlist**

| Element | Between | Phase closed |
|---|---|---|
| S1 | $V_{in}$ and $C_{fly}$ top plate | $\phi_1$ |
| S2 | $C_{fly}$ bottom plate and GND | $\phi_1$ |
| S3 | $C_{fly}$ bottom plate and $V_{in}$ | $\phi_2$ |
| S4 | $C_{fly}$ top plate and $V_{out}$ | $\phi_2$ |
| $C_{out}$ | $V_{out}$ and GND | always |

**Diode doubler:** $V_{in}\to D_1\to X\to D_2\to V_{out}$; $C_{fly}$ from $X$ to CLK (0 to $V_{in}$); $C_{out}$ from $V_{out}$ to GND. $V_{out}\approx2V_{in}-2V_D-I_{out}/(fC)$.

**Inverter:** $\phi_1$: top plate to $V_{in}$, bottom to GND. $\phi_2$: top plate to GND, bottom plate to $V_{out}$, giving $V_{out}=-V_{in}$.

**Switch implementation:** MOSFETs with body connections chosen so body diodes don't conduct; non-overlapping clock generator with dead time; S4/S3 need gate drive above $V_{in}$ (use the output or a bootstrap).
:::

::: long
"I'll draw the switched doubler first. Input $V_{in}$ on the left, ground rail along the bottom.

Flying capacitor in the middle. Its top plate has two switches: S1 back to $V_{in}$, and S4 to the output node. Its bottom plate also has two: S2 to ground, and S3 to $V_{in}$. The output node has the output capacitor to ground and then the load.

Phases: $\phi_1$ closes S1 and S2, putting the capacitor across the input so it charges to $V_{in}$. $\phi_2$ closes S3 and S4: the bottom plate is lifted to $V_{in}$, the top plate goes to the output, and the output is $V_{in}$ plus the capacitor's $V_{in}$. The output capacitor holds the output during $\phi_1$. I mark capacitor polarity and note the clock phases must not overlap, otherwise S2 and S3 together short $V_{in}$ straight to ground.

If they want a simpler circuit, the diode version: $V_{in}$ through a diode to a node, a second diode from that node to the output, a capacitor from the node to a square-wave clock, and an output capacitor. Clock low: the capacitor charges through D1. Clock high: the node jumps up by the clock swing, D1 blocks, D2 conducts. The output is about $2V_{in}$ minus two diode drops.

And the inverter uses the same four switches, but in phase two the top plate is grounded and the output is taken from the bottom plate, so it's $-V_{in}$."
:::

::: terms
- **$\phi_1$ / $\phi_2$**: the two clock phases.
- **Non-overlap (dead) time**: gap between phases.
- **Top / bottom plate**: capacitor terminals; the bottom plate has the larger parasitic capacitance on-chip.
- **Reservoir capacitor**: $C_{out}$.
- **Body diode**: MOSFET parasitic that can conduct when switches are off.
- **Clock driver**: square-wave source of the diode pump.
- **Inverting pump**: produces $-V_{in}$.
- **Dickson stage**: diode, capacitor and clock phase.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws a capacitor and two switches and says "charge, then stack".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws all four switches with correct node connections and labels the phases.
- Marks polarity and ground and the output capacitor.
- States the non-overlap requirement and the consequence of overlapping.
- Offers the diode version and the inverter as variations on the same parts.
- Mentions implementation: MOSFET switches, body diodes, gate-drive above the rail, and that the input current is $2I_{out}$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Connecting both plates to the output in phase 2.** Only the top plate goes to $V_{out}$; the bottom goes to $V_{in}$.
- **Missing the output capacitor.** Then the output collapses during phase 1.
- **Wrong capacitor polarity** when using electrolytics (usually ceramics are used).
- **Diodes backwards** in the passive doubler.
- **No ground reference**.
- **Overlapping phases.**
:::

## The inverter

Same parts, different wiring: in $\phi_1$ the top plate goes to $V_{in}$ and the bottom to ground; in $\phi_2$ the top plate goes to ground and the bottom plate goes to $V_{out}$, so $V_{out}=-V_{in}$ relative to ground, and $C_{out}$ sits between $V_{out}$ and ground. Typical use: $-3.3$ V from 3.3 V for op-amps or bias.

## What the real chip adds

- MOSFET switches, with gate drive: S4 and S3 need gates above their source (bootstrapped or from the output).
- Body diodes oriented so they never forward-bias in normal operation, and a startup path.
- Non-overlap clock logic and an oscillator, and for regulated parts a comparator on $V_{out}$.
- External parts: $C_{fly}$, $C_{in}$, $C_{out}$ ([[q0289]]).

## Node-voltage audit: how to check your drawing in 30 seconds

A drawing can look right and still be wrong. The fastest check is a table of plate voltages per phase, using the rule that **a capacitor's voltage cannot jump**. For the unloaded doubler at $V_{in}=3.3$ V:

| | $\phi_1$ | $\phi_2$ | Capacitor voltage |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top plate | 3.3 V ($V_{in}$ via S1) | 6.6 V ($V_{out}$ via S4) | |
| Bottom plate | 0 V (GND via S2) | 3.3 V ($V_{in}$ via S3) | |
| $V_{top}-V_{bottom}$ | 3.3 V | 3.3 V | the same in both phases |

If your drawing makes the plate-to-plate voltage differ between the two phases, it is wrong, because that would need the capacitor to charge instantly. The same audit catches the classic errors: both plates tied to $V_{out}$ (the capacitor voltage would be forced to 0 V), or $S_3$ landing on ground instead of $V_{in}$ (the output would only reach $V_{in}$).

After the dead time, the plates jump together by the same amount (top 3.3 V to 6.6 V, bottom 0 V to 3.3 V), which is how the capacitor keeps its 3.3 V while its plates move.

## What each switch has to survive

An exam drawing treats the switches as ideal, but the choice of device in a real chip follows from the voltages across them:

| Switch | On-state voltage | Off-state voltage it must block | Off-state polarity |
|---|---|---|---|
| S1 (Vin to top plate) | about $V_{in}$ at both terminals | $2V_{in}-V_{in}=V_{in}$ | top plate higher |
| S2 (bottom plate to GND) | about 0 V at both terminals | $V_{in}$ | bottom plate higher |
| S3 (Vin to bottom plate) | about $V_{in}$ at both terminals | $V_{in}$ | $V_{in}$ higher |
| S4 (top plate to Vout) | about $2V_{in}$ at both terminals | $V_{out}-V_{in}=V_{in}$ | $V_{out}$ higher |

Every switch blocks only $V_{in}$, so a low-voltage device is sufficient; the doubler is not a high-voltage stress problem. The catch is *gate drive*: S1 and S4 sit at up to $2V_{in}$, so their gates must swing relative to the output rail, not the input rail, and their bodies must be tied to the highest node ($V_{out}$) so the body diodes stay reverse-biased. That is why S1 and S4 are the switches that use a level shifter or a bootstrap, while S2 and S3 can be driven from the input logic. The assignment of NMOS or PMOS to each position is a design choice and differs between products.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Which device type, gate drive scheme and body connection a given IC uses for S1 to S4 is vendor-specific. The block-voltage table above is a first-principles statement for an ideal doubler, not a description of any particular part.

## Start-up: the capacitor is empty

At power-up $C_{fly}$ and $C_{out}$ are both at 0 V, and the gate-drive rails that depend on $V_{out}$ are not available yet. The circuit has to bootstrap itself. Usually a body diode or a small start-up diode from $V_{in}$ to $V_{out}$ charges $C_{out}$ to about $V_{in}-V_D$ (3.0 V with a 0.3 V Schottky), which is enough to power the gate drivers, and only then does normal pumping lift the output toward $2V_{in}$. If the diode were missing or reversed, a pump that needs its own output to drive S1 and S4 would never start. In a sketch it is worth drawing that diode in dashed lines and saying why it is there.

## What overlap actually costs

The interview line "overlap would short the supply" can be made quantitative. If S2 and S3 each have 1 Ω on-resistance and are on together, the current through the pair is $3.3/2=1.65$ A. Assume an illustrative 10 ns overlap at each of the two transitions per cycle: the charge wasted per cycle is $2\times1.65\text{ A}\times10\text{ ns}=33$ nC, which at 500 kHz is an extra 16.5 mA of input current. For a 20 mA load the ideal input is 40 mA, so the shoot-through alone adds 41% to the supply current and divides the efficiency by 1.41 (a 29% relative drop). This is why the clock generator inserts a dead time longer than the worst-case switching skew, and why efficiency drops at high frequency: the wasted charge per cycle is fixed, so the wasted current scales with $f$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 10 ns overlap and 1 Ω on-resistance are illustrative numbers for the arithmetic. Real break-before-make timing depends on the gate drivers and process.

## How to check a real doubler with a scope

- Probe the top plate and the bottom plate (use a short ground spring; a ground lead adds ringing in the 10 MHz range): they should step together between the two levels in the audit table, with only a small droop and rise during each phase.
- Probe two gate signals (for example S2 and S3) on the same screen, trigger on one edge, and zoom in on the dead time. You should see a gap where both are low. A missing gap shows as a spike on the supply and a current pulse at every edge.
- Compare the input current with and without the load: the difference divided by the load current should be close to 2 (plus the overhead of the clock). A larger number points at shoot-through, leakage or a switch that does not fully turn on.
- Repeat at start-up with the output shorted for a moment and released, to watch the soft-start behavior and confirm the start-up path.

::: followups
### Why do you need the output capacitor?
During $\phi_1$ the flying capacitor is disconnected from the output, so only $C_{out}$ can supply the load. Without it the output would collapse every half cycle.

### How would you change the drawing to get 1.5x?
Use two flying capacitors: charge them in series across $V_{in}$ (each $V_{in}/2$), then connect them in parallel on top of $V_{in}$ so the output is $V_{in}+V_{in}/2$. It needs more switches and states.

### What happens if the two phases overlap?
All four switches are briefly on. S3 and S2 together connect $V_{in}$ straight to ground, and S1 with S4 ties $V_{in}$ directly to the output capacitor. Both are low-resistance shoot-through paths limited only by switch resistance. A non-overlapping clock with dead time prevents it.

### How do you drive the switches?
MOSFET gates from a non-overlapping clock. S3/S4 need drive above $V_{in}$: use the output rail or a bootstrapped capacitor.

### Scenario: you drew the diode doubler but the output is $V_{in}-V_D$ only. What went wrong?
The clock isn't toggling or isn't coupling through $C_{fly}$: the node never rises above $V_{in}$, so D2 never conducts. Check the clock swing and the capacitor connection; also check diode orientation.
