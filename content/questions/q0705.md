::: testing
Whether you can define ripple precisely (periodic AC on the DC rail at the switching or rectified-line frequency, distinct from noise and spikes) and explain its **physical origin** by charge balance: the output capacitor supplies or absorbs the AC part of the current, so $\Delta V=\int i_C\,dt/C$ plus the $I\cdot ESR$ drop. A strong answer gives the buck formula $\Delta V\approx\Delta I_L/(8f_{sw}C)+\Delta I_L\,ESR$, the rectifier sawtooth $I/(2fC)$, mentions other sources (burst mode, load modulation, input coupling) and notes how it is measured and reduced.
:::

::: why
Ripple is the most commonly quoted supply spec and the first thing a bench engineer looks at on a scope. It feeds directly into ADC accuracy, clock jitter, RF spurs, audio hum and EMI, and it is the main knob in buck design (inductor, capacitor and frequency choices are all made to hit a ripple target, [[q0212]]). Knowing where it comes from lets you predict it, reduce it and tell it apart from noise and spikes ([[q0707]]) when debugging a rail. It also bridges circuit analysis ([[q0194]]) and power integrity.
:::

::: concepts
- **Charge balance**: in steady state the average capacitor current is zero; the AC part of the inductor current flows into $C$ and creates the ripple ([[q0194]], [[q0189]]).
- **Capacitor voltage from current**: $\Delta V=\Delta Q/C$, with $\Delta Q$ the area under the positive half of $i_C$ ([[q0196]]).
- **Capacitor ESR and ESL**: real capacitors add resistive and inductive terms ([[q0030]]).
- **Inductor ripple current**: $\Delta I_L=(V_{in}-V_{out})D/(Lf_{sw})$ ([[q0195]]).
- **Rectification and filtering**: bulk capacitor after a bridge rectifier ([[q0160]]).
- **PSRR of linear regulators**: how much input ripple appears at the output ([[q0704]]).
:::

::: answer
"Ripple is the periodic AC component that sits on top of the DC output of a supply, at the converter's switching frequency or, for a rectified AC supply, at twice the line frequency. It's different from noise, which is random, and spikes, which are brief edges.

It comes from the fact that the supply delivers energy in pulses and the output capacitor smooths them. In a buck converter the inductor current isn't constant: it ramps up and down by $\Delta I_L$ around the load current. The load takes the average, so the capacitor takes the difference: it charges while the inductor current is above the load and discharges when it is below. That charge swings the capacitor voltage by about $\Delta I_L/(8 f_{sw} C)$. On top there's a term from the capacitor's ESR, $\Delta I_L$ times ESR, which is a triangle in phase with the current, and a little from the ESL at the edges.

Example: 12 V to 3.3 V at 500 kHz with 4.7 µH gives about 1 A of inductor ripple, and a 22 µF capacitor with 5 milliohms ESR gives roughly 12 millivolts peak to peak. For a rectified mains supply it's the bulk cap discharging into the load between charging pulses: a sawtooth at 120 Hz of $I/(2fC)$.

Other sources: burst or PFM modes produce low-frequency ripple, a pulsed load modulates the rail, and anything on the input comes through according to the regulator's PSRR."
:::

::: technical
**Buck converter ripple.** $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$, inductor ripple current $\Delta I_L=\dfrac{(V_{in}-V_{out})D}{Lf_{sw}}=\dfrac{V_{out}(1-D)}{Lf_{sw}}$. The output capacitor current is the AC part of $i_L$: a triangle of peak-to-peak $\Delta I_L$ with zero mean. The capacitor charges during the half of the triangle where $i_C>0$: $\Delta Q=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\Delta I_L}{2}\cdot\tfrac{T}{2}=\dfrac{\Delta I_LT}{8}$, so
$$\Delta V_C=\frac{\Delta Q}{C}=\frac{\Delta I_L}{8f_{sw}C}$$
This part is quadratic (parabolic) in time, 90° behind the current. The ESR term $\Delta V_{ESR}=\Delta I_L\cdot ESR$ is a triangle in phase with the current. Because they are in quadrature, the true peak-to-peak lies between $\max(\cdot)$ and the sum; the sum $\Delta V_C+\Delta V_{ESR}$ is a conservative bound. The ESL adds $ESL\cdot di_C/dt$ steps at the switching edges.

**Rectified AC (linear supply).** Full-wave bridge with capacitor $C$ and load current $I$: the capacitor discharges for most of each half-cycle, so $\Delta V\approx I/(f_rC)$ with $f_r=2f_{line}$ (120 Hz for 60 Hz mains), plus an $I_{pulse}\cdot ESR$ step during the short charging pulse.

**Other sources.**
- *PFM/burst mode*: one pulse per burst; ripple frequency varies with load and can be audible, and is larger than in PWM ([[q0233]]).
- *Pulsed or periodic loads* (RF bursts, motors, processors at activity frequency) modulate the rail at their repetition rate.
- *Input coupling*: ripple on $V_{in}$ passes through with $-\text{PSRR}$ (LDO) or via line transient response (converter).
- *Instability or subharmonic oscillation*: a narrowband large-amplitude component at $f_{sw}/2$ or at the loop's crossover.
- *Interleaving or beat frequencies* between two converters.

**Spec practice.** Quoted as mVpp, with measurement bandwidth (often 20 MHz) and test setup stated.
:::

::: short
"Ripple is the periodic AC component on a DC rail at the switching frequency, or twice the line frequency for a rectified supply. It arises because the supply delivers energy in pulses and the output capacitor smooths them. In a buck, the capacitor absorbs the inductor's current ripple, giving $\Delta I_L/(8f_{sw}C)$ plus $\Delta I_L\times ESR$. In a rectifier the capacitor discharges between charging pulses, giving $I/(2fC)$. Other sources are burst mode, pulsed loads and input ripple coming through the regulator."
:::

::: long
"Ripple is the periodic AC component riding on the DC output, at the switching frequency for a switcher and twice the line frequency for a rectified mains supply. It's different from noise, which is random, and from spikes, which are brief edges.

It comes from energy being delivered in pulses. Take a buck: the inductor current ramps up while the high-side switch is on and down while it's off, so it has a triangular ripple of about $(V_{in}-V_{out})D/(L f_{sw})$ around the DC load current. The load draws only the DC part, so the AC part of the inductor current flows into the output capacitor. The capacitor charges during the half of the cycle where the inductor current is above the load and discharges the rest. The area of the positive half of that triangle is $\Delta I T/8$, so the voltage swing is $\Delta I/(8 f C)$. In real capacitors the ESR adds a triangle in phase with the current, $\Delta I$ times ESR, and the ESL adds small steps at the edges.

With numbers: 12 V to 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz gives about 1 A of inductor ripple. A 22 µF capacitor with 5 milliohm ESR gives 11.6 mV from the capacitance and 5 from the ESR, about 12 mV peak to peak when you combine them properly, since they're 90 degrees apart.

For a mains supply, the bridge rectifier charges the bulk capacitor in short pulses at 120 Hz, and between them the capacitor supplies the load, giving a sawtooth of $I/(2fC)$: 1 amp and 2200 µF gives about 3.8 V.

Other sources are burst mode in light-load switchers, which produces larger, lower-frequency ripple, periodic loads that modulate the rail, ripple coming from the input through the regulator's PSRR, and instability. To reduce it you raise $L$, $C$ or the frequency, lower the ESR, interleave phases or add an LC or LDO stage."
:::

::: terms
- **Ripple**: periodic AC component on a DC rail, at the switching or rectified-line frequency (and harmonics).
- **Peak-to-peak ripple (mVpp)**: max minus min of the AC component over a period.
- **Inductor ripple current ($\Delta I_L$)**: the triangular AC current in the inductor of a switcher.
- **ESR / ESL**: equivalent series resistance and inductance of a capacitor.
- **Full-wave rectification**: both half cycles charge the capacitor, so ripple frequency is $2f_{line}$.
- **PFM / burst mode**: light-load operating mode where switching pulses are skipped, increasing ripple.
- **Interleaving (multiphase)**: phase-shifting parallel converters so their ripple currents partially cancel.
- **PSRR**: power supply rejection ratio of a linear regulator against input ripple.
- **Spike (glitch)**: brief edge-related excursion, distinct from ripple ([[q0707]]).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it's the AC on the DC output from switching" and "use bigger capacitors".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives $\Delta V=\Delta I_L/(8f_{sw}C)$ from the area of the capacitor current, and adds the ESR (and ESL) terms.
- Notes the capacitive and ESR parts are 90° apart so the real peak-to-peak is less than their sum (12.3 mV vs 16.7 mV in the example).
- Gives both converter and rectifier cases with numbers ($I/(2fC)$) and says the ripple frequency in each case.
- Separates ripple from noise and spikes and states that the measurement bandwidth and probing method change the number.
- Lists non-obvious sources: burst/PFM mode, pulsed loads, input coupling, instability, beating between converters.
- Gives the levers and their scaling: $\Delta I_L\propto1/(Lf)$, $\Delta V_C\propto1/(Lf^2C)$, interleaving factor, LC filter attenuation.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Ripple is noise."** Ripple is deterministic and periodic; noise is random; spikes are brief. They have different causes and cures.
- **Adding the capacitive and ESR terms as exact.** They're in quadrature; the sum is an upper bound.
- **Using nominal capacitance** for the calculation. A 22 µF X5R MLCC at 3.3 V DC bias may behave like 10 to 15 µF ([[q0036]]).
- **Believing ripple frequency equals the line frequency.** For a full-wave rectifier it is twice the line frequency.
- **Forgetting the load-dependence.** Inductor ripple is nearly independent of load in CCM, but in DCM or burst mode it changes a lot ([[q0233]]).
- **Treating spikes on the scope as ripple.** A long ground lead or edge ringing adds high-frequency artefacts that don't scale with $C$.
:::

::: deep
## Ripple, noise and spikes

A real supply output looks like this on a scope: a DC level, a regular wave at $f_{sw}$ (ripple), a floor of random fuzz (noise) and sharp little spikes at the switching edges. All three are "AC on DC" but they have different origins and the fixes differ:

| | Ripple | Noise | Spikes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frequency content | $f_{sw}$ and low harmonics | broadband | tens to hundreds of MHz |
| Source | inductor current ripple through $C$ | reference, regulator, thermal | switch edges, $L\,dI/dt$, coupling |
| Typical value | 5 to 50 mVpp | 0.1 to 5 mVrms | 20 to 200 mV |
| Fix | $L$, $C$, $f_{sw}$, interleave | filter, quiet reference | layout, snubber, bead |

This page is about the first column. Spikes are treated in [[q0707]] and the overall quality picture in [[q0704]].

## Why a switcher has ripple: charge balance

In a buck converter ([[q0173]], [[q0174]]) the switch node alternates between $V_{in}$ and ground, and the LC filter averages it. The inductor has a triangular current: it rises at $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ for $DT$ and falls at $V_{out}/L$ for $(1-D)T$. The load $I_{out}$ equals the average of $i_L$. The output capacitor current is therefore

$$i_C(t)=i_L(t)-I_{out}$$

a zero-mean triangle with peak-to-peak $\Delta I_L$. Zero mean is essential: in steady state the capacitor can't gain charge cycle after cycle. Looking at one cycle: $i_C>0$ for the middle of the cycle, around the peak of $i_L$, and $<0$ near the valley. The voltage is the integral:

$$v_C(t)=\frac1C\int i_C\,dt$$

Its extremes are where $i_C$ crosses zero. The zero crossings of $i_C$ are always half a period apart (at $u=D/2$ and $u=(1+D)/2$ in units of $T$), and between them $i_C$ is positive: a triangle of base $T/2$ and height $\Delta I_L/2$. The charge delivered is its area,

$$\Delta Q=\tfrac12\cdot\frac T2\cdot\frac{\Delta I_L}{2}=\frac{\Delta I_L T}{8},\qquad\Delta V_C=\frac{\Delta I_L}{8f_{sw}C}$$

Add the voltage drop across the ESR: $v_{ESR}=i_C\cdot ESR$, a triangle with peak-to-peak $\Delta I_L\cdot ESR$.

## Worked example

Buck, $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $D=0.275$, $L=4.7$ µH, $f_{sw}=500$ kHz, $C=22$ µF, ESR = 5 mΩ.

- $\Delta I_L=(12-3.3)(0.275)/(4.7\ \text{µH}\cdot500\ \text{kHz})=2.39/2.35=1.018$ A.
- $\Delta V_C=1.018/(8\cdot500\text{ kHz}\cdot22\text{ µF})=11.6$ mV.
- $\Delta V_{ESR}=1.018\cdot0.005=5.1$ mV.
- Naive sum: 16.7 mV. Exact combination of the two waveforms: 12.3 mVpp.

## What controls it: scaling

| Change | $\Delta I_L$ | $\Delta V_C$ | $\Delta V_{ESR}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| $L\times2$ | /2 | /2 | /2 |
| $C\times2$ | same | /2 | same |
| $f_{sw}\times2$ | /2 | /4 | /2 |
| ESR /2 | same | same | /2 |
| 2-phase interleave ($D=0.275$) | ×0.62 (total) | smaller still | smaller |
| 4-phase interleave | ×0.11 | | |

The interleaving factor for $N$ phases is $\dfrac{(ND-\lfloor ND\rfloor)(\lfloor ND\rfloor+1-ND)}{ND(1-D)}$, which for $N=2$, $D=0.275$ is 0.62 and for $N=4$ is 0.11, and is exactly zero when $ND$ is an integer (for example 2 phases at $D=0.5$). The effective ripple frequency is $Nf_{sw}$ ([[q0236]]).

An LC post-filter gives second-order attenuation. A 1 µH inductor and 10 µF capacitor have $f_c=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})=50$ kHz, so at 500 kHz the attenuation is $(500/50.3)^2\approx99$, about 40 dB: 12 mV becomes about 0.12 mV (ignoring the filter's own damping and parasitics). A linear regulator after the switcher with 40 dB PSRR does the same job.

> [!TIP]
> Capacitor values in a ripple formula must be *effective* values at the operating DC bias and temperature. A 22 µF, 6.3 V X5R in 0603 can have under 10 µF at 5 V bias.

## Rectifier ripple (linear supplies)

A bridge rectifier ([[q0160]]) feeding a bulk capacitor charges it in brief pulses near each peak of the 100 or 120 Hz rectified waveform. Between pulses the capacitor alone supplies the load, so it droops linearly:

$$\Delta V\approx\frac{I_{load}}{f_rC},\quad f_r=2f_{line}$$

For $I=1$ A, $C=2200$ µF and 60 Hz mains: $\Delta V=1/(120\cdot2200\ \text{µF})=3.79$ V. The ripple is a sawtooth at 120 Hz with a sharp charging edge. An LDO following it with 60 dB of PSRR at 120 Hz leaves 3.8 mV, which is why audio and instrument supplies always follow the rectifier with a regulator ([[q0168]]).

## Less obvious sources

1. **Burst or PFM mode.** At light load a converter skips cycles and delivers occasional bursts; ripple is bigger (the cap is charged to a higher level in each burst) and at a lower, load-dependent frequency, sometimes in the audible range ([[q0233]]).
2. **Periodic loads.** A processor, an RF power amplifier (for example the 217 Hz frame rate in GSM, or LTE slot rates) or a PWM-driven load modulates the rail at its repetition rate. This isn't produced by the converter but it appears as ripple.
3. **Input coupling.** Ripple at the converter input appears at the output attenuated by the line transient response, or by the LDO's PSRR ($-30$ to $-60$ dB, falling with frequency).
4. **Subharmonic or unstable operation.** In current-mode control with $D>0.5$ and no slope compensation, alternate cycles differ and you see a large component at $f_{sw}/2$ ([[q0254]]).
5. **Beat frequencies.** Two converters at slightly different frequencies produce a beat in a shared rail, a classic cause of low-frequency "mystery ripple".
6. **Jitter in the switching frequency** (spread spectrum) deliberately spreads the ripple energy but the time-domain amplitude stays about the same.

## Measuring ripple

Use a scope at the load with the probe's tip-and-barrel (no long ground lead), 20 MHz bandwidth limit if you want to compare with the datasheet, AC coupling or offset to zoom, enough timebase to see several periods, and a realistic load current. A second capture at full bandwidth reveals the spikes that the 20 MHz limit hides ([[q0707]], [[q0997]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Sizing the output capacitor of a buck converter** to meet a ripple spec on a processor or FPGA rail ([[q0212]]).
- **Audio and instrumentation power supplies**, where 100/120 Hz ripple becomes hum unless an LDO or filter follows the rectifier.
- **Radio and PLL supplies**, where ripple at $f_{sw}$ shows up as spurs at $f_{carrier}\pm f_{sw}$.
- **ADC supplies and references**, where ripple must stay below one LSB: a 12-bit 3.3 V ADC has 0.8 mV per LSB.
- **LED drivers**, where current ripple at 100/120 Hz produces visible flicker.
- **Multiphase CPU/GPU VRMs**, which use interleaving to cut ripple and the capacitor count.
- **Battery chargers and USB PD adapters**, where input ripple specifications are part of compliance.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Continuous conduction mode (CCM)** and a fixed-frequency PWM buck. In DCM or burst mode the formulae change.
- **Ideal triangular inductor current**, so inductor DC resistance, saturation and switch drops are ignored. Real inductors saturate and increase the ripple ([[q0046]]).
- **Constant capacitance and ESR**, ignoring DC-bias derating, temperature and frequency dependence.
- **The load is a constant current** so $i_C=i_L-I_{out}$ is exact. A dynamic load adds its own components.
- **Peak-to-peak** definitions: some datasheets quote rms or "typical" values; check the definition.
- **Rectifier formula** assumes a long discharge time compared with the charging pulse and a constant-current load.

For a linear regulator without a switching input, the ripple is just what leaks through from the input (PSRR) and the regulator's own noise.
:::

::: followups
### How much does doubling the switching frequency reduce ripple?
$\Delta I_L\propto1/f$ and the capacitor integrates over half as long, so the capacitive ripple falls by 4 ($\propto1/f^2$); the ESR term falls by 2. In the example 11.6 mV becomes 2.9 mV (capacitive) and 5.1 mV becomes 2.5 mV (ESR). The price is higher switching loss ([[q0216]]).

### Why can ripple get worse at light load?
If the converter enters pulse-skipping or burst mode, it delivers a burst of pulses, charges the capacitor above the target and then waits until it falls to a lower threshold. The ripple is therefore the hysteresis band of the comparator, not the PWM ripple, and its frequency drops with the load. In DCM the inductor current also hits zero each cycle, changing the ripple formula. Designers accept a bigger ripple for efficiency at light load, or force PWM mode for noise-sensitive loads.

### How do you reduce ripple without changing the converter?
Add a second-stage LC or ferrite-bead plus capacitor filter at the load, follow the switcher with an LDO (PSRR at $f_{sw}$ matters), add or replace the output capacitor with lower ESR parts or more in parallel, and keep the capacitors close to the load pins. For a 500 kHz ripple, a 1 µH/10 µF stage cuts it by about 40 dB.

### Scenario: your rail has 30 mV of 100 kHz ripple but the converter switches at 1 MHz. Where could it come from?
A component at $f_{sw}/10$ isn't the normal switching ripple. Candidates: light-load burst mode, the loop oscillating near its crossover (low phase margin), a periodic load at 100 kHz, or the converter's input source (another converter or a rectified supply) beating. I'd check by changing the load (burst), probing the input rail and the feedback node, and looking for a peak in the loop response.

### Harder: the capacitor datasheet says 22 µF but the ripple on the board is three times your calculation. What happened?
The effective capacitance at bias is lower (MLCC derating can give 30 to 60% of nominal for small X5R packages at 5 V), the ESR/ESL or layout adds terms, and the probing may include spikes. I'd measure with a 20 MHz limit and a short ground, check the effective capacitance from the manufacturer's DC-bias curves, and then compare the *shape*: a parabolic wave suggests capacitance, a triangle suggests ESR and spikes suggest ESL or layout.
:::
