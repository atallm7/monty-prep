::: basics
### In plain words
Ripple is the small repeating wobble on the output voltage caused by the switching. To reduce it you smooth the output more. You can add inductance, add capacitance, lower the capacitor's ESR, or switch faster. You can also add a second filter stage.

### Picture it
Picture a tank of water being filled in pulses. A bigger tank rises less with each pulse. Pulses that come more often and are smaller also make the level steadier. A second tank behind the first smooths what is left. The analogy has one catch: some ripple is not a slow level change at all but sharp spikes at the switching edges, and a bigger tank does not fix those.

### Words you need
- **Ripple**: the small repeating variation on the output voltage.
- **Ripple current**: how far the inductor current swings each cycle ([[q0194]]).
- **ESR**: the small resistance inside a capacitor, which adds ripple.
- **Switching frequency**: how often the converter cycles each second.
- **Interleaving**: running several small converters (phases) side by side, switching at staggered times, so their ripples partly cancel ([[q0236]]).

### The simplest example
Say the inductor ripple current is 1 A at 500 kHz with a 10 µF capacitor. The ripple is $\Delta I/(8fC) = 1/(8\times500\text{k}\times10\mu) = 25$ mV (ignoring ESR). Double the capacitor to 20 µF and it is 12.5 mV. Instead double the frequency with the same inductor: the ripple current halves to 0.5 A and the ripple is $0.5/(8\times1\text{M}\times10\mu) = 6.25$ mV. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers ESR, interleaving, extra filter stages and the spikes that need layout fixes.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can list the **distinct levers on ripple** and rank them by effectiveness and cost, and whether you understand what ripple you are talking about. The interviewer is looking for:

1. The basic levers from the equations: larger $L$, larger $C$, lower ESR/ESL, higher $f_{sw}$.
2. Architectural levers: multiphase interleaving (and how much it cancels), a second-stage LC or ferrite-bead filter, a post-regulator (LDO).
3. Awareness of what *doesn't* help: spread spectrum does not reduce amplitude, high-frequency spikes are not cured by more bulk capacitance.
4. The costs: efficiency, size, transient response, loop stability, cost, and numbers that show which lever is cheap and which is big.
:::

::: why
Ripple limits what you can power from a switcher: ADCs, PLLs, RF front ends, sensors and anything with tight tolerance ([[q0218]]). Meeting a ripple spec is therefore a routine design task, and the temptation is to throw capacitors at it. A strong answer distinguishes the *fundamental ripple* (set by $\Delta I_L$ and the output capacitor) from *switching spikes* (set by edges and parasitics), and picks the lever that matches. It also connects to the efficiency questions: most levers trade ripple for loss, size or response ([[q0214]], [[q0216]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor ripple current**: $\Delta I_L=V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf)$ ([[q0194]]).
- **Output ripple voltage**: $\Delta V=\dfrac{\Delta I_L}{8fC}+\Delta I_L\,ESR$ ([[q0212]], [[q0196]]).
- **Interleaving**: phase-shifted parallel phases partially cancel ripple current and raise its frequency.
- **LC filtering**: a second-order low-pass attenuates by $(f_c/f)^2$ above its corner.
- **PSRR**: an LDO's attenuation of supply ripple, which falls with frequency.
- **Ripple vs spike**: low-frequency triangle/sawtooth vs high-frequency edge noise.
:::

::: answer
"I'd start from the ripple equation: output ripple is about $\Delta I_L/(8fC)$ plus $\Delta I_L$ times ESR, and $\Delta I_L$ is $V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf)$. So four direct knobs: more inductance, more capacitance, lower ESR, higher frequency. Frequency is the strongest because the ripple falls as $1/f^2$: doubling it quarters the capacitive term.

If those aren't enough, I'd go architectural. Multiphase interleaving: with two phases at 27% duty the ripple current into the capacitor is 62% of one phase and at twice the frequency, so the ripple voltage drops by a factor of about 2.5; with four phases it's almost cancelled. A second-stage LC or ferrite-bead filter after the main output capacitor gives, say, 30 to 40 dB at the switching frequency, at the cost of some DC drop and a loop-interaction risk. And a post LDO cleans the rail, although its PSRR is poor at hundreds of kilohertz.

I'd also check what kind of ripple it is. High-frequency spikes at the edges come from ESL and loop inductance, and are fixed by layout, small ceramic capacitors close to the load, slower edges or a snubber, not by more bulk capacitance. And spread spectrum helps EMI but doesn't lower the amplitude.

Numbers: baseline ripple in my 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A example is 17 mV; doubling $L$ gives 8 mV, doubling frequency 5.4 mV, two phases 6.8 mV, four phases about 1 mV, and an LC stage with a 72 kHz corner takes it under 1 mV."
:::

::: technical
**Baseline.** $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I_{out}=2$ A, $f=500$ kHz, $L=4.7$ µH, $C=22$ µF, ESR 5 mΩ: $\Delta I_L=1.02$ A, $\Delta V=11.6+5.1=16.7$ mV.

| Lever | Result (mV) | Cost |

| $C\times2$ (44 µF) | 10.9 | area, cost |
| ESR 5 to 2 mΩ | 13.6 | cost, more parts |
| $f\times2$ (same $L$) | 5.4 | switching loss ([[q0216]]) |
| 2-phase interleave | 6.8 | 2 inductors, controller |
| 3-phase | 2.2 | |
| 4-phase | 0.9 | |
| Second-stage LC (0.22 µH/22 µF, $f_c=72$ kHz, damped) | about 0.35 | DC drop, loop interaction |
| LDO post-regulator (PSRR 30 dB at 500 kHz, assumed) | about 0.5 | dropout loss, headroom |

**Interleaving.** For $N$ phases, the summed ripple current into the output capacitor (relative to one phase's $\Delta I_L$) is
$$K(N,D)=\frac{(\lceil ND\rceil-ND)(ND-\lfloor ND\rfloor)}{N\,D\,(1-D)},$$
with zeros at $D=k/N$, and the ripple frequency is $Nf$. Check: $N=2$, $D=0.275$: $K=0.62$; $N=3$: 0.24; $N=4$: 0.11 (verified numerically).

**LC filter.** Above $f_c=\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_2C_2}}$ the attenuation is $(f_c/f)^2$ (40 dB/decade), provided the filter is damped ($Q\approx1$) and the capacitor's ESR and ESL don't dominate: 33.6 dB at 500 kHz for $f_c=72$ kHz.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> LDO PSRR at hundreds of kHz varies widely by part (often 20 to 50 dB, sometimes lower); check the datasheet plot. The numbers for the filters assume ideal components, so real attenuation saturates at the parasitic (ESR, ESL, winding capacitance) floor.
:::

"Output ripple is $\Delta I_L/(8fC)+\Delta I_L\cdot ESR$ with $\Delta I_L=V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf)$, so increase $L$, $C$ or $f$, or reduce ESR. Beyond that, interleave multiple phases to cancel ripple current and raise its frequency, add a second-stage LC or ferrite-bead filter, or a post-LDO. High-frequency spikes need layout and local small capacitors, not more bulk capacitance; spread spectrum lowers EMI peaks, not amplitude."
:::

::: long
"I'll start from where ripple comes from. The inductor current has a triangular ripple, $V_{out}(1-D)$ over $L\,f$, and that flows into the output capacitor, giving a voltage ripple of $\Delta I$ over $8\,f\,C$ plus $\Delta I$ times the ESR.

So the direct levers are more inductance, more capacitance, lower ESR and higher frequency. Frequency is the big one: ripple current falls as $1/f$ and the capacitive term as another $1/f$, so doubling $f$ cuts the capacitive ripple by four. In my 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A example, ripple is about 17 millivolts; doubling $L$ gives 8, doubling frequency gives 5.4. Each has a cost: $L$ means size and DCR and slower transients; frequency means switching loss; capacitance means area and cost.

If I need more, I go architectural. Multiphase interleaving: two phases at 27% duty cut the ripple current into the capacitor to about 62% and double its frequency, so voltage ripple falls by about 2.5 times; three phases to 24% and four to 11%, with nulls when $D$ is a multiple of $1/N$. Next, a second-stage filter: a small inductor or ferrite bead plus capacitor after the main output capacitor. A 72 kHz corner gives about 34 dB at 500 kHz, which takes the ripple below a millivolt, at the price of some DC drop and the need to damp it and keep it away from the loop crossover. Or a post-LDO, with the caveat that its PSRR at 500 kHz may only be 30 dB.

I'd also distinguish the kind of ripple. If what I'm seeing is spikes at the switching edges, that's loop inductance and ESL, so I'd fix the layout, add a small ceramic near the load, slow the edge, or use a snubber, because a bigger bulk capacitor doesn't help. And I'd make sure the probe isn't the problem: use a short ground spring, not the long lead. Spread spectrum reduces EMI peaks but not amplitude."
:::

::: terms
- **Ripple current ($\Delta I_L$)**: peak-to-peak inductor current variation.
- **Output ripple ($\Delta V$)**: peak-to-peak output voltage variation at the switching frequency.
- **Interleaving**: phase-shifted operation of parallel phases.
- **Ripple cancellation factor ($K$)**: output ripple current of $N$ phases relative to one phase.
- **Second-stage (post) filter**: LC or ferrite-bead and capacitor following the output capacitor.
- **PSRR**: power-supply rejection ratio.
- **Spread spectrum**: dithering the switching frequency to spread EMI energy.
- **Switching spike / noise**: high-frequency edge-related voltage excursions.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Increase the inductor and capacitor, raise the frequency, use lower-ESR capacitors."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives or quotes the ripple equation and notes the $1/f^2$ dependence of the capacitive term.
- Quantifies each lever for a concrete converter (for example 17 mV to 8 mV, 5 mV, 7 mV, 1 mV).
- Explains interleaving with the cancellation factor, the frequency multiplication and the nulls at $D=k/N$.
- Designs the post filter correctly: corner placement relative to $f_{sw}$ and loop crossover, damping, DC drop and parasitic floor.
- Distinguishes ripple from spikes and from probe artifacts, and notes what doesn't help (spread spectrum, more bulk C for spikes).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Adding bulk capacitance to cure edge spikes.** Spikes are ESL, loop inductance and edge speed; add small, low-ESL ceramics at the load and fix layout.
- **Measuring ripple with a long ground lead.** The loop picks up the switch-node noise; use a tip-and-barrel or spring ground at the capacitor.
- **Ignoring loop interaction with a post filter.** An LC placed inside the feedback loop adds a double pole; keep $f_c\gg$ crossover or take feedback before the filter.
- **Assuming interleaving always cancels.** Cancellation is complete only at $D=k/N$; at $D=0.275$ two phases give 62%, not zero.
- **"Spread spectrum reduces ripple."** It spreads the spectrum; peak-to-peak amplitude is unchanged.
:::

::: deep
## What are we reducing?

The ripple at the output has three components with different causes:

1. **Fundamental ripple** at $f_{sw}$: the triangular inductor current into $C_{out}$ and its ESR (capacitive part in quadrature, ESR part in phase).
2. **Switching spikes**: sharp pulses at each edge from ESL, loop inductance, inductor interwinding capacitance and diode recovery.
3. **Low-frequency components**: loop noise, subharmonic instability, PFM burst frequency and load-induced variation. Not the topic here.

The equations ([[q0212]]):

$$\Delta I_L=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{Lf},\qquad \Delta V\approx\frac{\Delta I_L}{8fC}+\Delta I_L\,ESR\;\propto\;\frac{1}{L\,C\,f^2}\ (\text{capacitive part}).$$

## Lever 1: the passive and frequency knobs

| Change | $\Delta I_L$ | $\Delta V$ (mV) |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline (4.7 µH, 22 µF, 5 mΩ, 500 kHz) | 1.02 A | 16.7 |
| $L$ doubled | 0.51 A | 8.3 |
| $C$ doubled | 1.02 A | 10.9 |
| ESR 2 mΩ | 1.02 A | 13.6 |
| $f$ doubled | 0.51 A | 5.4 |

Because ESR sets a floor ($\Delta I\cdot ESR$ is 5.1 mV of the 16.7), doubling $C$ gives less than expected. Parallel several ceramics for lower ESR as well as more $C$.

## Lever 2: interleave phases

Parallel phases driven $360^\circ/N$ apart sum their inductor currents. Ripple components partially cancel, and the combined ripple is at $N f$. The ratio of summed ripple to one phase's is

$$K(N,D)=\frac{(\lceil ND\rceil-ND)\,(ND-\lfloor ND\rfloor)}{N\,D\,(1-D)}.$$

## Lever 3: a second-stage filter

Put a small inductor (or ferrite bead) $L_2$ and capacitor $C_2$ after the main output capacitor. Above the corner $f_c=1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_2C_2})$ the attenuation grows at 40 dB/decade:

Figure: The magnitude falls by about 34 dB at 500 kHz. Damping is essential: this plot uses a damping resistance giving $Q\approx1$; an undamped LC with the load resistance 1.65 Ω would have $Q\approx16$ and a large peak near 72 kHz, right where the control loop's crossover usually lives. Real filters damp with a parallel RC branch or a lossy ferrite bead.

Design rules:

- Place $f_c$ one decade or more below $f_{sw}$ (to get at least 40 dB) and well above the loop crossover (to avoid loop interaction), or take feedback *before* the filter (and accept DC regulation error equal to $I\,DCR_2$).
- The inductor must carry the full DC current: DCR 5 mΩ gives 10 mV drop and 20 mW loss at 2 A.
- Real attenuation is limited by $C_2$'s ESR and ESL and the inductor's winding capacitance: typically 40 to 60 dB at best in the 1 to 10 MHz range, not infinite.
- A ferrite bead (an impedance that is resistive at tens of MHz) is better against HF spikes than against 500 kHz ripple.

## Lever 4: a post-regulator

A low-noise LDO after the buck attenuates ripple by its PSRR at $f_{sw}$. PSRR typically falls with frequency to 20 to 40 dB at several hundred kHz; read the datasheet. Cost: dropout headroom (so the buck is set 0.2 to 0.5 V above the final rail), LDO loss $(V_{in}-V_{out})I$, and extra parts. Used for ADC, PLL and RF rails.

## Lever 5: reduce HF spikes (a different problem)

For spikes at the edges:

- **Layout**: shorten the switching loop, place the input capacitor against the FETs ([[q0257]]).
- **Local decoupling**: small low-ESL ceramics (100 nF, 10 nF) at the load, plus a ferrite bead for isolation.
- **Edge control**: a gate or boot resistor; an RC snubber ([[q0209]]).
- **Inductor choice**: low interwinding capacitance (shielded or smaller-package) reduces the spike coupled through the inductor.
- **Probing**: measure with a tip-and-barrel, 20 MHz bandwidth limit off or on according to what the spec means, short ground. Spurious ripple from a long ground clip is a common false alarm.

## Lever 6: control and mode choices

- **Avoid PFM/pulse-skipping** for ripple-sensitive loads; use forced PWM, which keeps ripple at a fixed, predictable frequency.
- **Spread spectrum** reduces peak emissions by dithering $f_{sw}$ but leaves amplitude the same.
- **Ripple injection / feed-forward and active cancellation**: some designs inject an anti-phase ripple, giving large reductions at the cost of design complexity.

## Ranking

| Priority | Lever | Typical gain | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Raise $f_{sw}$ | 3 to 4× on capacitive term for 2× $f$ | if losses allow |
| 2 | More/lower-ESR $C$ | 1.5 to 2× | always cheap, with diminishing returns due to ESR |
| 3 | Interleaving | 2 to 20× | high current, many phases |
| 4 | Second-stage LC / bead | 30 to 40 dB | tight analog supplies |
| 5 | Post LDO | 20 to 40 dB | low-noise rails |
| 6 | Bigger $L$ | 2× per doubling | if size and transient allow |
:::

::: applications
- **ADC and sensor supplies**: buck followed by a ferrite bead and LDO to hit sub-millivolt ripple.
- **PLL/VCO and RF**: ripple translates to spurs, so use an LC filter or LDO ([[q0218]]).
- **CPU/GPU multiphase VRMs**: interleaving to cancel ripple and reduce capacitor count.
- **Audio and instrumentation**: choose frequency and filtering to keep ripple out of the band of interest.
- **LED drivers and motor drivers**: allow larger ripple but control ripple current ([[q0219]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM, constant frequency** and ideal triangular currents.
- **Ripple spec at the load** for a defined bandwidth; if the bandwidth limit differs (20 MHz vs full), the measured number changes.
- **Capacitance and ESR** are nominal and frequency independent; real ceramics derate and have an ESR minimum near their self-resonance.
- **Second-stage filter** parameters are ideal and damped; parasitic floors set the real limit.
- **LDO PSRR** is device specific; the 30 dB figure is an example.
- **Interleaving formula** assumes identical phases and perfect phase spacing; current mismatch limits cancellation.
:::

::: followups
### Why does doubling the frequency cut ripple by more than half?
Two effects multiply. The inductor ripple current halves ($\propto1/f$), and each cycle is half as long, so the charge into the capacitor per cycle halves again: $\Delta V_C=\Delta I/(8fC)\propto1/f^2$. Only the ESR part falls by 2: so the total falls by between 2 and 4, depending on the ESR share (here 16.7 to 5.4 mV, about 3.1×).

### How does interleaving work and when does it cancel perfectly?
Phases switch $360^\circ/N$ apart, so one phase's rising ripple current overlaps another's falling ripple. At $D=k/N$ the sum is constant: the cancellation is complete. At other duty cycles, partial cancellation: for $N=2$, $D=0.275$ the ripple current is 62% of a single phase's and appears at $2f$.

### Your post-filter makes the loop oscillate. Why and what do you do?
The LC adds a double pole (and with an undamped $Q$ of 10 or more, a large peak and phase drop) near the loop's crossover. Move the corner well above crossover (one decade or more), damp it with an R-C branch, or take feedback from the converter side of the filter (accepting the DC drop) or use a small ferrite bead plus capacitor (lower $Q$).

### The ripple on the scope is 80 mV but the datasheet says 20 mV. What do you check?
Probe method first (long ground lead, probe tip loops pick up SW noise); then bandwidth limit; then the spike vs fundamental distinction (spikes are layout/ESL); then capacitor derating at the actual DC bias and temperature; then whether the converter is in PFM or DCM at that load; finally the ripple at the load vs at the converter's capacitor.

### Does a larger inductor always reduce ripple without downsides?
It reduces ripple current as $1/L$ but increases DCR (more conduction loss), size and cost, and slows the transient (slew rate of inductor current is $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$). Past about 20 to 30% ripple ratio, the returns diminish. And at light load a larger $L$ means entering DCM at a lower load, changing behavior.
:::
