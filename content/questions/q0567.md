::: basics
### In plain words
Suppose two signal layers sit back to back with no ground plane between them. A clock trace on one layer and an analog trace on the other then form a tiny capacitor wherever they overlap. The fixes, best first, are a ground plane between the layers, crossing at 90 degrees so the overlap is tiny, and keeping the traces apart sideways.

### Picture it
Imagine a drummer upstairs and a reader downstairs, separated only by a thin floor. The more floor they share, the more thumping comes through. If the drummer is over just one corner, little gets through. A thick carpet between them, which is the ground plane, helps most.

### Words you need
- **Adjacent layers**: two inner signal layers separated only by a thin insulating layer.
- **Dielectric**: that insulating layer between conductors.
- **Capacitance**: two conductors that overlap with insulation between them form a capacitor. More overlap area and less spacing mean more capacitance.
- **90-degree crossing**: running one trace straight across the other, so they overlap only in a small square.
- **Return current**: current flowing back to its source through the nearest plane.

### The simplest example
Take two traces 0.2 mm wide, separated by 0.2 mm of insulation. If they run together for 50 mm, the capacitance is about 1.9 pF. The analog node has 10 pF to ground. A 3.3 V clock edge divides by the capacitances, so the analog node jumps by about 3.3 × 1.86 / 11.86 = **0.52 V**. If the traces instead cross at 90 degrees, the overlap is only 0.2 mm × 0.2 mm. That is about 7 fF, and the jump falls to about **2.4 mV**, 250 times smaller.

That is the whole idea: overlap area sets the coupling, so remove the overlap or put a plane in the way. The rest of this page covers stack-up options and return paths.
:::

2. Fix it in order of effectiveness: **put a plane between** (stack-up), **route orthogonally** (minimise overlap), **separate laterally**, then reduce the aggressor and harden the victim.
3. Quantify with parallel-plate capacitance and a divider.
4. Know the side-effect: the clock needs its own return path, too.

::: why
This is a typical layout-review situation on 6- and 8-layer boards: two routing layers back to back (for example layers 3 and 4) where a clock on one can sit directly over an ADC input on the other. Bad choices here are invisible in the schematic, show up as unexplained noise on the analog channel and are costly to fix after fabrication.
:::

First choice is the stack-up: put a ground plane between the two signal layers, so the clock and the analog trace each have their own plane and are shielded from each other. Second, if they have to be on adjacent layers, route them orthogonally so they only overlap at a crossing, where the overlap is a 0.2 mm by 0.2 mm square, about 7 femtofarads, a 250 times reduction. Third, keep them apart laterally and never run the sensitive trace under or over the clock.

:::

::: technical
**Model.** The clock (aggressor) and analog trace (victim) overlap with area $A = w\ell$ across a dielectric of thickness $d$ and relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r$. Parallel-plate estimate (ignoring fringing, so a lower bound):
$$C_m = \frac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r w\ell}{d}.$$
For $w$ = 0.2 mm, $\ell$ = 50 mm, $d$ = 0.2 mm, $\varepsilon_r$ = 4.2: $C_m$ = 1.86 pF. If the victim has $C_g$ = 10 pF to ground, then for edges faster than $R_v(C_m+C_g)$ the injected step is $\Delta V = V_{clk}\,C_m/(C_m+C_g)$ = 3.3 × 1.86/11.86 = 0.52 V. At a 90° crossing, $A = w^2$: $C_m$ = 7.4 fF and $\Delta V$ = 2.4 mV. The ratio is $\ell/w$ = 250.

**Inductive part.** Parallel traces over the same stretch also couple magnetically: mutual inductance is high when the two loops overlap; a crossing contributes very little.

**Remedies in order.**
1. *Stack-up:* add (or reassign) a ground plane between the layers (signal–plane–signal), or put the analog signal on a layer whose neighbours are planes. 
2. *Orthogonal crossing*, with the shortest possible overlap.
3. *Lateral separation:* offset the traces so they don't overlap (keep a distance of several $h$).
4. *Edge rate and impedance:* series resistor on the clock; lower the victim impedance or buffer; RC filter at the ADC.
5. *Shield:* a ground pour or guard on the layer with the victim; stitched ground vias.
6. *Differential routing* for the analog signal.

**Return paths.** Each signal's return current flows on the nearest plane. If two signal layers sit back to back, each one's plane is on its *other* side; the clock's return is then via the plane above, away from the victim.
:::

The fix, in order of effectiveness. Two, if I'm stuck with adjacent signal layers, route them orthogonally, so they only overlap at a crossing. The overlap is then a 0.2 by 0.2 mm square, about 7 femtofarads, 250 times lower. :::

::: terms
- **Broadside coupling**: coupling between traces on adjacent layers, one directly over the other.
- **Edge coupling**: coupling between side-by-side traces on the same layer.
- **Parallel-plate capacitance**: $C = \varepsilon A/d$, ignoring fringing.
- **Electrostatic shield**: a grounded conductor between two circuits, which terminates the electric field.
- **Orthogonal routing**: adjacent routing layers run in perpendicular directions.
- **Prepreg / core**: the dielectric layers between copper layers in a stack-up.
- **Aggressor / victim**: clock / analog trace here.
- **Reference plane**: plane carrying a signal's return current.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "route them orthogonally" or "keep them apart".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Identifies broadside parallel-plate coupling and computes the capacitance and injected voltage.
- Prioritises a stack-up fix (plane between) over routing tricks, explains why (electrostatic shield and own return planes).
- Quantifies the orthogonal crossing benefit (about 250:1 for 0.2 mm traces over 50 mm).
- Includes electrical hardening of the victim and aggressor and checks return paths.
- Mentions that fringing makes the estimate a lower bound.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Routing them in parallel "because they're on different layers".** Different layers are not isolation if there's no plane between them.
- **Adding a plane between them but cutting it** with antipads, slots or splits under the sensitive area.
- **Orthogonal crossing but with a long diagonal run.** Only a true perpendicular crossing minimises overlap.
- **Ignoring the clock's return.** If the clock's reference plane is on the far side, its return still has to be continuous.
- **Fixing it only on the victim** with a bigger capacitor, which also slows the wanted signal.
- **Ignoring fringing.** Field lines extend beyond the plate area, so the parallel-plate number underestimates $C_m$.
:::

::: deep
## What the problem is

On a typical 6-layer stack-up (signal–ground–signal–signal–power–signal) the two inner signal layers face each other across a core or prepreg with no plane between. A clock on one and an analog trace on the other overlap anywhere their paths cross or run together.

## How big is the coupling?

Plate capacitance: $C_m = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_rA/d$. With $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}$ F/m:

| Case | Overlap area | $C_m$ |
|---|---|---|
| 50 mm parallel, 0.2 mm wide, $d$ = 0.2 mm | 10 mm² | 1.86 pF |
| One 90° crossing | 0.04 mm² | 7.4 fF |
| Two crossings | 0.08 mm² | 14.9 fF |

## Fix 1: put a plane between them (stack-up)

Rearrange the layer order so that the signal layers are never adjacent: signal–plane–signal–signal... becomes signal–plane–signal–plane–signal–plane. A 6-layer stack of S–G–S–G–P–S puts a ground between the two inner signals. The plane is an *electrostatic shield*: field lines from the clock terminate on it instead of reaching the analog trace ([[q0490]]). This is why [[q0478]] recommends every signal layer be adjacent to a plane.

Trade-off: more planes mean fewer routing layers and possibly more layers (cost). For a mixed-signal board it is often worth it.

## Fix 2: orthogonal routing and minimal overlap

If the stack-up is fixed (for example S–G–S–S–P–S, where the two inner signal layers are adjacent): 

- Route layer 3 in the X direction and layer 4 in the Y direction. Crossings then overlap by $w^2$.
- Don't allow the clock to run directly over the analog trace even for short diagonal stretches.
- Keep the sensitive analog on the layer whose other neighbour is a plane, and route the clock on the other, so the plane still protects one side.
- Keep both signals *laterally* separated by several $h$ where possible, with the analog trace away from the clock's region entirely.

## Fix 3: electrical measures

- **Slow the clock edge** with a series resistor at the driver ([[q0562]]): coupled voltage is proportional to $dv/dt$ for the ramp case $V\approx R_vC_m\,dv/dt$.
- **Lower the victim impedance**: buffer the analog node near its source; smaller resistors between source and ADC.
- **Filter at the ADC**: RC with corner just above the signal bandwidth.
- **Differential analog routing**: coupling hits both lines equally and cancels.
- **Move the clock**: if the clock can run on an outer layer over a ground plane, it needn't use the inner layers at all.

## Don't forget return paths

Each signal's return current flows on the plane *nearest* to it. For the layer-3 clock in S–G–S–S–P–S the nearest plane is the ground on layer 2; for the layer-4 analog signal it's the power plane on layer 5. If layer 5 is a power plane (not a ground), the analog return needs a decoupling path; check that and stitching capacitors. And when the clock changes layers, give it a ground via beside its via.

## Rule summary

1. Plane between if you can. 
2. Else orthogonal, minimum overlap.
3. Else separate laterally.
4. Always: slow the aggressor, harden the victim, check returns.

## Worked example: when does slowing the clock edge actually help?

For a ramp edge of amplitude $V$ and rise time $t_r$ coupled through $C_m$ into a victim node with resistance $R_v$ and capacitance $C_g$, the node follows a first-order response with $\tau=R_v(C_m+C_g)$. At the end of the ramp the coupled voltage is

$$v_{pk}=R_vC_m\frac{V}{t_r}\left(1-e^{-t_r/\tau}\right).$$

Use the parallel-routing case above ($C_m=1.86$ pF, $C_g=10$ pF, $V=3.3$ V) and three victim resistances. Computing the peak at each edge time gives:

| $R_v$ | $\tau$ | $t_r=1$ ns | $t_r=5$ ns | $t_r=50$ ns |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 Ω | 1.2 ns | 350 mV | 121 mV | 12 mV |
| 1 kΩ | 11.9 ns | 496 mV | 422 mV | 121 mV |
| 10 kΩ | 119 ns | 515 mV | 507 mV | 422 mV |

The result is counterintuitive and important. For the high-impedance victim, slowing the clock from 1 ns to 5 ns barely helps (515 to 507 mV), because the edge is much faster than the victim's own time constant and the node simply sees charge division, the $0.52$ V limit from the figure above. Slowing the edge only reduces coupling once $t_r$ becomes comparable to or longer than $\tau$. The reliable levers for a high-impedance node are therefore removing the coupling capacitance (a plane or orthogonal routing) and lowering $R_v$ by buffering; edge-rate control is an effective lever only for low-impedance victims. For the orthogonal crossing ($C_m=7.4$ fF, $R_v=1$ kΩ) the same equation gives 2.3 mV at 1 ns, 1.5 mV at 10 ns and 0.5 mV at 50 ns. A 12-bit ADC with a 3.3 V reference has a 0.8 mV LSB, so a crossing leaves about 3 LSB of spike at a 1 ns edge, which a following RC filter and averaging can handle, whereas the parallel run at 0.5 V is hundreds of LSB and cannot be filtered back out.

## What sets the conducted error after the spike

The coupled voltage is not a rectangular pulse. It decays with $\tau$ after each edge, and a clock with 50% duty produces one positive and one negative spike per cycle. If the spikes are symmetrical their average is zero, but the ADC samples at a point in time, not an average. If the sample instant is synchronous with the clock (say a sigma-delta or a sampling ADC clocked from the same source), the error is a fixed offset, not noise, and it does not average away. Asynchronous sampling turns it into noise that averaging does reduce. This is one reason mixed-signal designs often deliberately run the ADC from a clock that is not a rational multiple of the digital clock.

## Limiting cases and when the simple model fails

- **Analog trace is terminated or low-impedance (50 Ω to 1 kΩ):** the coupled spike is smaller but the *transmission-line* analysis of [[q0565]] applies if the trace is long enough to be electrically long.
- **Clock on an outer layer over a solid ground plane, analog on an inner layer with its own plane:** coupling is mostly eliminated, and the remaining mechanism is return-current sharing at vias.
- **Power plane between the layers instead of ground:** still an electrostatic shield for field lines at the clock's frequency as long as the plane is well decoupled to ground; an unbypassed power plane is a floating patch at high frequency and shields less.
- **Very long parallel runs:** the lumped $C_m$ model gives way to distributed coupled-line behavior, and near-end and far-end crosstalk arise, with far-end amplitude growing with length.

## Bench verification

1. **Stop the clock, then start it.** Measure the analog node at the ADC input with the clock source enabled and disabled (a gating or enable pin often exists). The difference is the clock-coupled contribution and shows whether it is spike-shaped.
2. **Check the ADC code histogram** at a constant input: a clean input gives a tight histogram; a clock-synchronous offset shows up as a shifted mean, a non-synchronous one as a broadened histogram.
3. **Probe both layers with a near-field probe** at the crossing: a probe over the clock trace and one over the analog trace tell you whether the energy is on the board surface or in the inner layers where a scope probe cannot reach. Use a cross-section drawing of the stack-up to know which layers the hot nets actually sit on.
4. **Move the clock frequency by 5%** (if the source permits): coupled noise that tracks the clock frequency is confirmed as clock coupling.

::: applications
- **Mixed-signal 6/8-layer boards** (ADC front end beneath or beside digital logic): stack-ups with a plane between routing layers.
- **Sensor modules with MCU**: analog on one side, digital on the other, planes in between.
- **Audio codecs and DACs**: analog output traces kept away from the I²S clock lines on adjacent layers.
- **RF modules**: clocks and VCO control lines carefully separated from LNA and filter traces.
- **Precision measurement instruments**: guard layers or planes between digital and analog routing.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Parallel-plate formula** is a lower bound; fringing increases $C_m$.
- **Capacitive coupling dominates** for the high-impedance analog node in this example; for a low-impedance victim, inductive coupling and common returns matter more.
- **$C_g$ = 10 pF** is an illustrative node capacitance (trace, pin, ESD diode).
- **Stack-up details vary**: the dielectric between inner signal layers may be a thick core (0.5–1 mm), which reduces coupling by a factor of several; check your own stack-up.
- **Alternative answers**: put the clock on the outer layer and the analog on an inner stripline; use a guard layer; or physically separate the two circuits.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a plane between the layers help so much?
A grounded plane terminates the electric field lines of the clock before they reach the analog trace, so the mutual capacitance between them collapses (it is replaced by two capacitances to the plane). It also provides each signal with a nearby return path, which shrinks loop areas and inductive coupling. It acts as an electrostatic shield.

### How much does orthogonal crossing really help?
It reduces the overlap area from width × length to width × width. For 0.2 mm traces and 50 mm of parallel run, the ratio is 250, or 48 dB less capacitance. It reduces inductive coupling as well, since orthogonal segments have near-zero mutual inductance. It does not help if the traces also run parallel elsewhere.

### What if the dielectric between the two layers is a thick core (0.8 mm)?
Then $C_m$ falls in proportion: with $d$ = 0.8 mm instead of 0.2 mm, $C_m$ is about four times smaller (0.47 pF for the 50 mm example), and the coupled step is roughly 0.15 V rather than 0.52 V. That is still big for a sensitive input, so I'd still cross orthogonally and filter, but it shows why core thickness in the stack-up matters.

### Does it matter which layer carries the analog signal?
Yes. Prefer the layer whose other neighbour is a ground plane, so at least one side is shielded, and place the clock on the layer facing a different plane. Also consider return currents: the analog signal's reference plane should be a quiet ground, not a noisy power plane.

### Scenario: you inherit a layout with the clock and ADC input crossing in parallel for 30 mm on layers 3 and 4 and can't change the stack-up. What do you do?
Reroute one of them to cross orthogonally or move it to an outer layer over a ground plane. If that's impossible, add a series resistor on the clock driver, an RC filter and a buffer at the ADC, and check whether a ground pour on one layer between the traces' lateral positions helps. Measure the coupled noise with the clock on and off to confirm the improvement.
