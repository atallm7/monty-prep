::: testing
This is a drawing warm-up, but the interviewer is checking whether you will get the polarity right under pressure and whether you know what the symbols **mean**:

1. A clean triangle pointing in the direction of signal flow, with two inputs on the flat side, one output at the tip.
2. The **inverting (−)** and **non-inverting (+)** inputs labeled correctly, and what each does to the output's sign.
3. That the supply pins exist even when the drawing omits them.
4. That the inputs' position on the symbol can be swapped on a schematic, and the signs are what count. Mixing these up is the most common drawing error in a feedback circuit.
:::

::: why
Every op-amp question afterward ("draw an inverting amplifier", "what are the golden rules", "is this feedback negative?") depends on knowing which input is which. A swapped pair silently turns negative feedback into positive feedback, which means a circuit that latches to a rail instead of amplifying. On a real board, mislabeling an input in a schematic symbol or footprint is a respin. The skill is simple, and an interviewer expects it to be instant and correct.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inverting input ($V_-$)**: a signal applied here appears at the output with opposite sign. See [[q0121]].
- **Non-inverting input ($V_+$)**: a signal applied here appears at the output with the same sign.
- **Differential gain**: $V_{out} = A(V_+ - V_-)$, so the two inputs act with opposite signs.
- **Output terminal**: a low-impedance voltage output at the triangle's tip.
- **Supply pins $V_{CC}/V_{EE}$ (or $V_+/V_-$ supply)**: usually omitted from schematics for clarity, but required. See [[q0123]].
- **Negative feedback** means returning the output to the *inverting* input, either directly or through a network. See [[q0124]].
:::

::: answer
"I draw a triangle pointing to the right. The two inputs are on the left side, the flat side, and the output is the point on the right. I put a minus sign on the upper input and a plus sign on the lower input. The minus is the inverting input and the plus is the non-inverting input.

The names tell you what each one does to the output. If I wiggle the plus input up, the output goes up, same sign. If I wiggle the minus input up, the output goes down. The output is the gain times plus minus minus, so the two inputs fight each other. That's also why negative feedback goes back to the minus input: if the output rises, the minus input rises, and that pushes the output back down.

When I'm being careful, I also draw two little supply pins, the positive supply on top and the negative supply or ground on the bottom. Schematics often leave them out, but the part can't work without them. And one gotcha: on a schematic people sometimes flip the input order to make the wiring look neater, so I always trust the plus and minus signs, not whether an input is on top."
:::

::: technical
**Symbol.** A triangle with the apex at the output. The two inputs sit on the vertical side. The input marked "−" is the inverting terminal and the one marked "+" is the non-inverting terminal. Optional power pins: $V_{S+}$ at the top, $V_{S-}$ (or ground in single-supply circuits) at the bottom.

**Meaning.** For small signals about an operating point:

$$V_{out} = A\,(V_+ - V_-)$$

so $\partial V_{out}/\partial V_+ = +A$ and $\partial V_{out}/\partial V_- = -A$. In the closed-loop circuits that follow:

- Signal into $V_+$ (with $V_-$ held by feedback): gain is non-inverting, $1 + R_f/R_g$ ([[q0125]]).
- Signal into $V_-$ via a resistor (with $V_+$ at a fixed voltage): gain is inverting, $-R_f/R_{in}$.

**Feedback polarity.** The loop is *negative* only if increasing $V_{out}$ pushes $V_-$ up more than it pushes $V_+$ up. A resistor from output to the $-$ input is negative feedback. The same resistor to the $+$ input is positive feedback, the basis of a Schmitt trigger ([[q0127]]).

**Pin names in packages.** Many datasheets call them IN− / IN+, or −IN / +IN, OUT, V+ / V−. For the classic 8-pin dual-in-line general-purpose single op-amp (the LM741-style pinout), pin 2 is the inverting input, pin 3 the non-inverting input, pin 6 the output, pin 7 the positive supply and pin 4 the negative supply. Newer small packages (SOT-23-5, SC70) have their own pinout; always check the datasheet.
:::

::: short
"An op-amp is a triangle pointing in the direction of signal flow. Two inputs on the flat side: the minus input is inverting, the plus input is non-inverting. One output at the tip. The output is the gain times plus minus minus. So a signal into plus comes out in phase and a signal into minus comes out inverted, which is why negative feedback is wired back to the minus input. I'd also add the two supply pins, positive on top and negative on the bottom."
:::

::: long
"I'd sketch a triangle with its point to the right. On the left, the flat side, I draw two input wires: the top one marked with a minus and the bottom one with a plus. The wire leaving the tip is the output. If I want a complete drawing I also add a power pin on top for the positive supply and a pin on the bottom for the negative supply, or ground in a single-supply design.

The two inputs are the inverting input, the minus, and the non-inverting input, the plus. The op-amp's output is the open-loop gain times the voltage on the plus minus the voltage on the minus. So raising the plus input raises the output, and raising the minus input lowers it. That's where the names come from.

Why does that matter? Because it tells me where feedback goes. For a linear amplifier I need negative feedback, which means taking a fraction of the output and feeding it back to the *minus* input. If the output rises, the minus input rises, the difference shrinks, and the output gets pulled back. If I did that on the plus input, the loop would reinforce itself and the output would run into a rail. That's a Schmitt trigger or a latch, not an amplifier.

A detail I'd mention: on schematics people often swap the positions of the plus and minus inputs so wires don't cross, so I never rely on 'the top input is the inverting one.' I read the signs. And I'd note that the symbol doesn't show the power pins by default, but the datasheet pinout does."
:::

::: terms
- **Inverting input (−)**: the input whose signal appears inverted at the output.
- **Non-inverting input (+)**: the input whose signal appears in phase at the output.
- **Differential input voltage**: $V_d = V_+ - V_-$.
- **Output**: the terminal that provides $A V_d$, limited by the supply rails.
- **Supply pins ($V_{S+}$, $V_{S-}$)**: power connections; ground for single-supply parts.
- **Pinout**: the mapping of package pins to symbol terminals, specific to each part and package.
- **Negative feedback**: the output (or a fraction of it) returned to the − input.
- **Positive feedback**: returned to the + input; makes comparators with hysteresis.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws the triangle and labels plus and minus.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws a tidy triangle with the signs *inside* the body next to each input and adds supply pins on the top and bottom.
- States what each input does to the output's sign, using $V_{out} = A(V_+ - V_-)$.
- Links the polarity to feedback: negative feedback returns to the minus input, and a swapped pair silently produces positive feedback.
- Mentions that schematics sometimes flip input positions and that the signs are the authority.
- Notes that real part pinouts vary by package, so you check the datasheet.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the signs on the wrong inputs** or reversing them partway through a drawing. Mark them as you draw each input, then use them consistently.
- **Assuming the top input is always the inverting one.** Convention puts the minus on top, but swapped symbols are common in real schematics.
- **Confusing the "−" with the negative supply.** The minus sign on an input is a polarity marker. The supply pins are separate.
- **Pointing the triangle backward** (output on the left) with no reason. Draw signal flow left to right.
- **Dropping the supply connection in a design.** Omitted in a sketch is fine, omitted on a board means a dead circuit. See [[q0123]].
- **Thinking "inverting" means "negative voltage".** It means the *change* at the output is opposite in direction. A 2 V input into a gain of $-1$ gives $-2$ V, but a $-2$ V input gives $+2$ V.
:::

Now the question is why the two inputs have opposite signs, and the answer is simply that the output is a *difference*:

$$V_{out} = A\,(V_+ - V_-)$$

Any change $\Delta V_+$ moves the output by $+A\Delta V_+$ and any change $\Delta V_-$ moves it by $-A\Delta V_-$.

## What "inverting" really means

The best way to feel it is to apply feedback so the gain is a modest number, then drive each input separately. In the first case, drive the plus input and tie the minus input to a divider from the output (non-inverting, gain $+5$). In the second, hold the plus input at ground and drive the minus input through a resistor with a feedback resistor from output to minus (inverting, gain $-5$).

## Why the sign matters for feedback

For a linear amplifier you need the feedback to *oppose* any change at the output. Suppose a disturbance pushes $V_{out}$ up. If a fraction of $V_{out}$ is fed to the minus input, then $V_-$ rises, $V_d = V_+ - V_-$ decreases, and the output is driven back down. That is negative feedback. If you swap the pins, the same disturbance makes $V_d$ increase and the output goes further up until it hits the rail. This is how you can be sure you have drawn a correct amplifier: trace the loop from the output, through the feedback network, to whichever input it ends at, and confirm that it ends at the minus.

## Packages and pinouts

The symbol is abstract. The physical part has pin numbers. For the old-school 8-pin DIP single op-amp (the LM741 pinout), pin 2 is the inverting input, pin 3 is non-inverting, pin 6 is the output, pin 7 is $V_+$ and pin 4 is $V_-$. Pins 1 and 5 are offset null, pin 8 is not connected. Duals and quads have different pin maps, and the SOT-23-5 package of a single amplifier is different again.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Pinouts of small-package op-amps (SOT-23-5, SC70, SOIC dual) differ between manufacturers and even between product families of the same manufacturer. Never assume. Read the pin table of the exact datasheet.

## Drawing it cleanly in an interview

1. Triangle first, pointing right. Roughly equilateral, not a squashed sliver.
2. Inputs on the left. Write − and + *inside* the triangle at each input before you draw anything else.
3. Output wire from the tip. Label it.
4. Add supply pins only if there's space: top for $V_{S+}$, bottom for $V_{S-}$.
5. When you attach resistors, say out loud "feedback goes to the minus".
:::

::: applications
- **Every op-amp schematic** from a unity buffer on a sensor to an active filter relies on getting these two pins right.
- **Schematic capture and library parts**: a wrong pin mapping in a symbol-to-footprint assignment is a classic board-spin cause. A good symbol shows pin numbers and names.
- **Dual and quad op-amp ICs** (for example, parts with two or four amplifiers in one package) repeat this symbol with different pin numbers per amplifier and shared supply pins.
- **Comparator symbols** use the same triangle with the same polarity convention.
- **Instrumentation and difference amplifiers** are built from several op-amp symbols, where tracing inputs and signs is essential for the CMRR analysis.
:::

::: assumptions
I assumed the standard textbook convention of minus on top, plus on the bottom, with the output on the right. Some schematics (especially when the feedback runs along the top) flip them with the signs still marked. I assumed a single-output op-amp. **Fully differential amplifiers** have two outputs ($V_{out+}$ and $V_{out-}$) and a common-mode pin and are drawn differently. I also assumed a voltage-feedback op-amp. A **current-feedback** amplifier uses the same symbol, but the inverting input is a low-impedance input, so the "−" is not high impedance. Some drawings omit the supply pins entirely; in an interview it is worth drawing them or mentioning them.
:::

::: followups
### What does "inverting" mean for a DC input?
It means the output has the opposite sign relative to the input, scaled by the gain. With an inverting amplifier of gain $-10$, an input of $+0.3$ V gives $-3$ V. Because it's linear, it also inverts the *change*: for a sine input, the output is 180° out of phase.

### Which input would you feed the feedback into, and why?
The inverting (−) input. If the output rises, the − input rises, which reduces the difference $V_+ - V_-$ and pulls the output back. That is negative feedback. Feeding back to the + input would reinforce the change and drive the output to a rail.

### I connect the output of an op-amp straight to the inverting input and put my signal on the non-inverting input. What do I get?
A unity-gain **buffer** (voltage follower). The virtual short forces $V_- = V_+$ and the output equals $V_- $, so $V_{out} = V_{in}$ with gain 1, very high input impedance and low output impedance. It is used to isolate a high-impedance source from a load.

### What happens if I accidentally wire the pins backward on a schematic (feedback to the + input)?
It becomes positive feedback. Any tiny offset gets amplified and the output goes to one rail, and stays there until the input crosses the threshold the other way. If you did it with a resistor divider that sets a deliberate threshold, you get a Schmitt trigger. If you did it by accident in an amplifier, the circuit "doesn't amplify, it just sticks."

### On a bench, how would you check which pin is inverting if the markings are worn off?
Look up the datasheet pinout from the part number. If there's no marking, power the op-amp with a known supply, ground the + input, apply a small DC voltage at the other input through a resistor, and use an oscilloscope or meter on the output. Without feedback, positive input voltage would drive the output negative if that pin is the inverting one. Take care that the input stays within the common-mode range and the part is not driven beyond its rated inputs.
:::

::: interpretations
"Draw an opamp" is a symbol question, not a request for the transistor-level schematic. If the interviewer follows up with "draw what's inside it," answer with the three-stage block picture from [[q0121]]: a differential pair, a gain stage with a compensation capacitor, and an output stage. "The two input terminals" means inverting and non-inverting, not "the two supply pins."
:::
