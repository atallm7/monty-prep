::: basics
### In plain words
Silicon carbide (SiC) and gallium nitride (GaN) are semiconductors with a much wider "bandgap" than silicon. That lets a power transistor made from them block high voltage while being thinner. The result is less resistance and faster switching than silicon, so power converters can be smaller and more efficient.

### Picture it
Think of a dam holding back water. A stronger material lets you build a thinner wall for the same height of water. In a power transistor, the "wall" is a layer that blocks voltage. Wide-bandgap materials withstand about ten times the electric field, so that layer can be much thinner, and the current has a shorter path through it when the transistor is on. The dam picture breaks down on cost: the new materials are pricier.

### Words you need
- **Bandgap**: the energy an electron needs to jump into a conducting state. See [[q0055]]. It is measured in electron-volts (eV): silicon is 1.12 eV, and SiC and GaN are about 3.3 eV.
- **Breakdown field**: the most voltage per unit of thickness (electric field) a material can take before it fails.
- **On-resistance**: the resistance of a transistor when it is on. Lower means less heat. See [[q0096]].
- **Switching loss**: energy wasted each time the transistor turns on or off.
- **dv/dt, di/dt**: how fast voltage and current change. Higher with these devices.

### The simplest example
Compare two transistors rated for the same voltage. The silicon one needs a thick blocking layer. The SiC or GaN one needs about a tenth as thick, so it has far lower on-resistance and much less capacitance to charge. Less resistance means less conduction heat, and less capacitance means faster, cheaper switching. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the differences between SiC and GaN, and the new problems: they are fussier about how you drive them, and their very fast edges cause ringing on the board.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know the **materials physics reason** wide-bandgap (WBG) devices are better for power (higher critical field means thinner drift region, so lower $R_{on}$ at a given voltage), and whether you can give **honest trade-offs** instead of "GaN good, silicon bad". Strong answers name SiC and GaN with their voltage sweet spots, mention emerging ultra-wide-bandgap candidates (Ga₂O₃, diamond, AlN), and discuss the practical costs: gate drive, cost, reliability, dynamic $R_{on}$, thermal limits and layout sensitivity at high $dv/dt$.
:::

::: why
Efficiency and power density are the headline metrics in EV traction inverters, chargers, solar inverters and server supplies, and WBG devices are how those targets are being hit. An intern or new-grad engineer will meet SiC MOSFETs and GaN HEMTs in datasheets and eval boards. Understanding why they switch faster and block more voltage with less resistance, and also why they punish sloppy gate drive and layout, is the difference between using them well and blowing them up. The question also tests whether you can reason from material properties to circuit-level consequences.
:::

::: concepts
- **Bandgap and critical field**: a wider gap means electrons need more energy to cross, so the material tolerates a higher electric field before breakdown. See [[q0055]] and [[q0061]].
- **Drift region and on-resistance**: a power MOSFET blocks voltage across a lightly doped drift region. Its resistance scales roughly as $R_{on,sp}\propto V_B^2/(\varepsilon\mu E_c^3)$.
- **Switching losses**: $\approx\tfrac12VI(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}$ plus $C_{oss}$ and reverse-recovery losses; faster edges lower the first term. See [[q0214]].
- **Gate drive**: the threshold, maximum rating and speed differ between Si, SiC and GaN devices. See [[q0206]].
- **MOSFET basics**: planar and trench power MOSFETs, body diode, $R_{DS(on)}$. See [[q0093]] and [[q0096]].
- **Thermal conductivity**: determines how well a device can shed heat, which limits power density.
:::

"The two main ones are silicon carbide and gallium nitride, with gallium oxide and diamond as longer-term research. They're called wide-bandgap because the gap is about 3.3 eV, versus 1.12 for silicon. A wider gap gives a higher critical breakdown field, around ten times silicon's. A power device blocks voltage across a drift region, and with a ten-times higher field that region can be much thinner and more heavily doped, so for the same voltage rating you get a much lower on-resistance and much smaller capacitances. That means lower conduction loss and faster switching, which allows higher frequency and smaller magnetics.

SiC is the high-voltage one. 650 volts to 3.3 kilovolts and beyond, with good thermal conductivity, used in EV inverters and on-board chargers. GaN is lateral and mostly 15 to 650 volts, with very low gate and output charge, so it goes to megahertz switching, used in chargers and totem-pole PFC.

The trade-offs are real. Cost is higher than silicon. SiC MOSFETs need higher gate drive, around 15 to 20 volts, have a lower threshold and a high-Vf body diode. GaN HEMTs have a low maximum gate voltage, around 6 volts, no real body diode, no avalanche rating, and dynamic on-resistance effects. And both switch with very high dv/dt and di/dt, so layout inductance and ringing become the main design problem."

::: technical
**Why wide bandgap helps.** In a unipolar power device the specific on-resistance of the drift region at breakdown voltage $V_B$ is approximately (Baliga's relation)
$$R_{on,sp}\approx\frac{4V_B^2}{\varepsilon\,\mu\,E_c^3}$$
Here $E_c$ is the critical field, $\mu$ the electron mobility and $\varepsilon$ the permittivity. $E_c$ appears cubed, so a 10× higher field gives up to ~1000× lower theoretical resistance. Drift-region thickness is $W\approx2V_B/E_c$: at 1200 V about 80 µm for Si vs about 10 µm for SiC (using the typical $E_c$ values below).

**Typical material properties** (literature values; they vary with polytype, doping and source):

| Property | Si | 4H-SiC | GaN | β-Ga₂O₃ | Diamond |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bandgap (eV) | 1.12 | ~3.3 | ~3.4 | ~4.8 | ~5.5 |
| Critical field (MV/cm) | ~0.3 | ~2.5 | ~3.3 | ~8 (theoretical) | ~10 |
| Electron mobility (cm²/V·s) | ~1350 | ~900 | ~1250 (bulk), ~2000 (2DEG) | ~200-300 | ~2000 |
| Thermal conductivity (W/m·K) | ~150 | ~370 | ~130-250 | ~10-30 | ~2000 |

**Ideal one-dimensional limit at 650 V** using the table values: Si ≈ 4.5 Ω·mm², SiC ≈ 0.014 Ω·mm² (×300 better), GaN ≈ 0.005 Ω·mm² (~950× better). Real devices are far from the limits (channel, contacts, packaging, and Si superjunction narrows the gap), but the ranking holds.

**Device-level consequences.**
- *SiC MOSFET*: vertical, high voltage (650 V to several kV), body diode with a high forward drop (≈3 V typical), recommended gate drive roughly +15 to +20 V on and 0 to -5 V off, lower transconductance and threshold-voltage drift concerns, gate-oxide reliability and limited short-circuit withstand time are known design issues.
- *GaN HEMT*: lateral 2DEG device, mostly ≤650 V, normally-off through a p-GaN gate (e-mode) or a cascode, gate maximum typically around 6 V, no body diode but reverse conduction through the channel at a higher drop, no avalanche capability (design margin needed), dynamic $R_{on}$ from trapped charge, very low $Q_g$, $Q_{oss}$ and zero $Q_{rr}$.
- *Ga₂O₃/diamond/AlN*: ultra-wide-bandgap research. Ga₂O₃ has cheap melt-grown substrates but very poor thermal conductivity and no good p-type; diamond has outstanding thermal and field properties but small wafers and immature doping.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Property values (especially critical field for Ga₂O₃/diamond, GaN thermal conductivity and SiC channel mobility), voltage-class boundaries and gate-drive limits vary with source, vendor and generation. Treat the numbers as typical and check current datasheets.
:::

::: long
"The established wide-bandgap materials are silicon carbide and gallium nitride. Gallium oxide, diamond and aluminium nitride are further out, still being researched.

The key physics is that their bandgap is about three times silicon's, 3.3 to 3.4 electron-volts against 1.12. A wider gap means the material can sustain a higher electric field before avalanche, around ten times higher. Power devices block voltage across a lightly doped drift region, and its resistance goes as $V^2$ over critical field cubed. So with ten times the field you can use a drift region about ten times thinner, with much higher doping, and the theoretical on-resistance for a given voltage is orders of magnitude lower. Smaller die also means smaller capacitances and gate charge, so faster switching and lower switching loss, so you can run at higher frequency and shrink the inductors and capacitors.

SiC is the high-voltage, high-temperature option. It's vertical, 650 volts to several kilovolts, with thermal conductivity about double silicon's, and it dominates EV traction inverters and fast chargers. GaN is a lateral HEMT, mostly up to 650 volts, with extremely low gate and output charge and no reverse recovery, so it's used in compact USB chargers and totem-pole PFC at hundreds of kilohertz to megahertz.

The trade-offs: higher cost per device and per wafer, less mature reliability data. SiC needs higher gate drive voltages and has a low threshold, a high body-diode drop and gate-oxide concerns. GaN has a low maximum gate voltage of around 6 volts, no avalanche rating and dynamic on-resistance. Both switch with dv/dt of tens of volts per nanosecond, so layout inductance, gate ringing and EMI become the dominant design issues. And silicon isn't dead: superjunction MOSFETs and IGBTs are still cheaper where frequency and efficiency demands are moderate."

::: terms
- **Wide bandgap (WBG)**: semiconductors with bandgap well above silicon's 1.12 eV, e.g. SiC and GaN (~3.3-3.4 eV).
- **Critical electric field ($E_c$)**: field at which avalanche breakdown occurs; sets the drift-region thickness for a voltage rating.
- **Specific on-resistance ($R_{on,sp}$)**: on-resistance times die area ($\Omega\cdot\text{cm}^2$), a figure of merit for conduction loss per area.
- **HEMT**: high-electron-mobility transistor; GaN devices use a 2D electron gas at an AlGaN/GaN interface.
- **2DEG**: two-dimensional electron gas, a thin high-mobility conducting layer in a GaN HEMT.
- **Dynamic $R_{on}$ (current collapse)**: temporary rise of on-resistance after high-voltage stress due to charge trapping.
- **$Q_{rr}$ / $Q_{oss}$**: reverse recovery and output-capacitance charge that cause switching loss; very low in GaN.
- **Superjunction MOSFET**: a silicon structure that narrows the gap to WBG at 600-900 V.
:::

- Is honest about uncertainty and maturity ("numbers depend on generation, check the datasheet").
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Wide bandgap means higher mobility."** Not generally; the advantage is the critical field (cubed in $R_{on}$) and thinner drift region. Mobility in SiC is lower than Si's.
- **"GaN is better than SiC."** They target different voltages. GaN excels at ≤650 V and high frequency; SiC wins at higher voltage and where thermal conductivity and ruggedness matter.
- **Driving a GaN gate like a silicon MOSFET.** Over-driving a ~6 V-limit gate with 10-12 V destroys it.
- **Assuming a body diode exists in GaN.** E-mode HEMTs conduct in reverse through the channel at a higher drop, and dead-time losses can be significant.
- **Ignoring dv/dt and layout.** A 50 V/ns edge with 5 nH of loop inductance causes ringing, false turn-on and EMI.
- **Claiming silicon is obsolete.** Superjunction MOSFETs and IGBTs remain cost-effective, especially at low frequency.
:::

::: deep
## Why the bandgap matters

A semiconductor's bandgap $E_g$ is the energy needed to lift an electron from the valence to the conduction band (see [[q0061]]). Impact ionisation, which causes avalanche breakdown, requires carriers to gain energy of that order between collisions, so a larger $E_g$ means a higher field is needed: the **critical field** $E_c$ roughly scales with a power of $E_g$. Silicon breaks down at about 0.3 MV/cm; SiC and GaN at about 2.5-3.3 MV/cm; Ga₂O₃ is predicted near 8 and diamond near 10.

A wide bandgap also means almost no thermally generated carriers: intrinsic carrier concentration falls exponentially with $E_g$. That's why SiC and GaN junctions keep blocking at high temperature where Si leakage would run away (the package, solder and gate oxide usually limit operation first).

## From critical field to on-resistance

A power MOSFET blocks voltage $V_B$ across a drift region of thickness $W$ and doping $N_D$. To avoid breakdown, the field across it must stay below $E_c$, which for a one-sided abrupt junction gives $W\approx2V_B/E_c$. The doping can be as high as $N_D\approx\varepsilon E_c^2/(2qV_B)$. The resistance per area of that region is $R_{on,sp}=W/(q\mu N_D)$. Substituting:

$$R_{on,sp}=\frac{4V_B^2}{\varepsilon\,\mu\,E_c^3}\quad(\text{Baliga's ideal limit})$$

The cubic dependence on $E_c$ is the lever. Using typical values (silicon: $\varepsilon_r=11.7$, $\mu=1350$, $E_c=0.3$ MV/cm; SiC: 9.7, 900, 2.5; GaN: 9.0, 1250, 3.3; Ga₂O₃: 10, 300, 8):

| Material | $W$ at 1200 V | Ideal $R_{on,sp}$ at 650 V | Ideal $R_{on,sp}$ at 1200 V |
|---|---|---|---|
| Si | 80 µm | ≈ 4.5 Ω·mm² | ≈ 15 Ω·mm² |
| 4H-SiC | 9.6 µm | ≈ 14 mΩ·mm² | ≈ 48 mΩ·mm² |
| GaN | 7.3 µm | ≈ 4.7 mΩ·mm² | ≈ 16 mΩ·mm² |
| β-Ga₂O₃ | 3 µm | ≈ 1.2 mΩ·mm² | ≈ 4.2 mΩ·mm² |

The practical meaning: for the same on-resistance, a WBG die is hundreds of times smaller, so it has less gate charge $Q_g$, less output capacitance $C_{oss}$, and less stored charge to sweep out. That's the switching-loss advantage.

## Switching: why faster is useful

Hard-switching loss per transition is roughly $\tfrac12VI\,t_{sw}$, plus $\tfrac12C_{oss}V^2$ and reverse recovery $Q_{rr}V$. For a 400 V, 20 A switch at 100 kHz, transitions of 10 ns rise and 10 ns fall give $\tfrac12\cdot400\cdot20\cdot20\text{ ns}\cdot100\text{ kHz}=8$ W; 50 ns each (100 ns total) gives 40 W. Cutting transition time cuts the loss proportionally, so you can either improve efficiency or raise frequency (smaller inductors and capacitors) for the same loss. See [[q0214]] and [[q0216]] for how frequency trades with efficiency and ripple.

## SiC in detail

SiC power devices are vertical, like silicon power MOSFETs. 4H-SiC has a bandgap near 3.3 eV and thermal conductivity about double silicon's, which helps with cooling. Typical design points:
- **Voltage classes**: 650 V, 900 V, 1200 V, 1700 V and beyond (several kV for grid applications).
- **Gate drive**: to fully enhance the channel the gate needs roughly +15 to +20 V (check the datasheet), while the threshold is relatively low and can drift with use, so many designs use a negative turn-off voltage to avoid false turn-on.
- **Body diode**: forward drop is high (around 3 V), so dead-time conduction is lossy; some designs use an external SiC Schottky diode or synchronous conduction.
- **Reliability topics**: gate-oxide quality, threshold shift and short-circuit withstand time are studied carefully, and typically shorter than in IGBTs.
- **Cost**: wafers are smaller and harder to make than silicon, so cost per amp is higher, though falling.

## GaN in detail

GaN power devices are usually HEMTs built on silicon (or sapphire/SiC) substrates. A thin AlGaN layer over GaN creates a 2D electron gas with high mobility and sheet charge without doping. The device is lateral, which limits voltage scaling and thermal spreading but gives extremely low $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$.
- **Normally-off**: p-GaN gate (e-mode, threshold around 1-2 V and a gate maximum typically about 6 V) or a cascode with a small silicon MOSFET.
- **No body diode, no $Q_{rr}$**: reverse current flows through the channel when the gate is off, with a voltage drop of a few volts, and no reverse recovery, which allows hard-switched totem-pole PFC in CCM.
- **No avalanche rating**: unlike Si MOSFETs, you must keep drain voltage below the rating with margin (often with a derating guide of 80% or so).
- **Dynamic $R_{on}$**: trapped charge can raise $R_{on}$ temporarily after high-voltage switching. Manufacturers specify it.
- **Packaging**: low-inductance packages (chip-scale, PQFN) matter, since the package inductance is comparable to the loop.

## Beyond SiC and GaN

- **β-Ga₂O₃** (≈4.8 eV): high theoretical field and cheap, melt-grown bulk substrates, but thermal conductivity is an order of magnitude below Si (self-heating) and p-type doping is unavailable, so no bipolar devices or good body diodes.
- **Diamond** (≈5.5 eV): best thermal conductivity and field, but wafers are small and expensive and doping is hard.
- **AlN / AlGaN** (>6 eV for AlN): ultra-wide bandgap research for very high voltage and high temperature.
These are active research, so don't claim products or maturity timelines.

## Trade-offs summary

| | Si (SJ MOSFET / IGBT) | SiC MOSFET | GaN HEMT |
|---|---|---|---|
| Typical voltage | up to kV (IGBT) | 650 V to kV+ | 15-650 V |
| Switching speed | moderate | fast | very fast |
| Cost | lowest | highest per amp | falling, moderate |
| Gate drive | easy | higher voltage, negative off often | low max voltage, tight |
| Thermal | good | very good | package/substrate limited |
| Notes | mature, avalanche-rated | body diode drop, oxide reliability | no avalanche, dynamic Ron |

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A great closing line: "It's not that wide-bandgap devices are better everywhere, it's that they move the efficiency-frequency-size trade-off. If a design is cost-driven and runs at 50 kHz, a superjunction silicon part may still win."

::: applications
- **EV traction inverters and on-board chargers**: SiC MOSFETs at 800 V platforms for lower losses and smaller cooling.
- **Fast USB-C chargers and adapters**: GaN HEMTs enabling small high-frequency flyback and PFC stages.
- **Solar and energy-storage inverters**: SiC reduces losses and filter size at high voltage.
- **Server and telecom power supplies**: GaN totem-pole PFC and LLC stages for 80 PLUS Titanium-class efficiency targets.
- **Lidar and RF/pulsed drivers**: GaN's very fast switching and low gate charge produce nanosecond pulses; GaN RF HEMTs are a separate (but related) technology for power amplifiers.

::: assumptions
- **"Novel" = emerging relative to silicon.** SiC and GaN are now commercial, while Ga₂O₃, diamond and AlN are still mainly research.
- **Material values are typical.** They vary with polytype, doping, crystal quality and source, and the Baliga limit is idealised (drift region only, 1-D, no channel or contact resistance).
- **Voltage classes are approximate** and move with each generation of devices.
- **Cost comparisons change quickly** with wafer size and volume. Check current pricing for a real decision.
- **Alternative valid answers.** Some people also mention silicon superjunction MOSFETs, IGBTs (for high voltage and low frequency), and GaN-on-Si vs GaN-on-GaN vertical devices as part of the landscape; all are fair to bring up if you tie them to trade-offs.

::: followups
### Why does a wide bandgap give lower on-resistance for the same voltage rating?
A wider gap supports a higher critical field, so the drift region that holds off the voltage can be thinner and more heavily doped. Resistance of the drift region scales as thickness over doping, so $R_{on,sp}\propto V_B^2/(\varepsilon\mu E_c^3)$. A tenfold higher field gives up to three orders of magnitude less theoretical resistance, which also reduces die area, capacitance and gate charge.

### What are the main drawbacks of SiC MOSFETs?
Higher cost per amp, a high body-diode drop (about 3 V), a gate that wants roughly 15-20 V and often a negative off voltage, threshold-voltage drift and gate-oxide reliability questions, and shorter short-circuit withstand times than IGBTs. The very fast edges also demand good layout, with Kelvin-source connections.

### What are the main drawbacks of GaN HEMTs?
Low maximum gate voltage (around 6 V for e-mode) so drive margin is small, no avalanche capability, no body diode so reverse conduction drops a few volts, dynamic on-resistance, lateral structure limiting voltage to around 650 V today, and very high $dv/dt$ with demanding layout.

### Why can GaN run at MHz where silicon cannot?
Very small gate charge $Q_g$ and output charge $Q_{oss}$ and no reverse-recovery charge, so gate-drive loss ($Q_gV_gf$), capacitive loss ($\tfrac12C_{oss}V^2f$) and recovery loss ($Q_{rr}Vf$) per cycle are small. That lets you raise $f_{sw}$ and shrink the passives.

### When would you still choose a silicon superjunction MOSFET or IGBT?
When cost dominates and the frequency is low (tens of kHz): the silicon device is cheaper and avalanche rated, with simpler gate drive. IGBTs also remain common above a kilovolt at low frequency, and a good SJ MOSFET at 650 V closes part of the efficiency gap.

### Scenario: you swap a silicon MOSFET for a pin-compatible GaN part in an existing buck design and see ringing and failures. What happened?
GaN edges are 5-10 times faster, so the same loop inductance and gate drive now give huge ringing: overshoot above the device rating (no avalanche protection), false turn-on from $dv/dt$ coupling through $C_{gd}$, and gate overstress (6 V limit). I'd shrink the power loop, add a Kelvin source, reduce the gate drive voltage to the datasheet value, use a dedicated GaN driver, and slow the edges with a gate resistor if needed.
:::
