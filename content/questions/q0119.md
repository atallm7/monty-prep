::: testing
Whether you understand that biasing means **setting the DC operating point (Q-point)** so the transistor stays in its active/saturation region for the whole signal swing, and that the real design problem is making that point **stable against $\beta$, $V_t$ and temperature**. The interviewer wants:

1. What biasing is for and where the Q-point should sit (mid-swing, with headroom).
2. A real bias network: voltage divider plus emitter (or source) resistor, with the design rule (divider current about 10x the base current) and why fixed-base bias is bad.
3. The AC side: coupling and bypass capacitors that separate DC bias from the signal, with the resulting low-frequency corner.
4. Numbers for a worked design, and variations for MOSFETs, op-amps (single-supply mid-rail bias) and current-mirror bias.
:::

::: why
An amplifier that isn't biased properly clips, distorts, or runs hot. Bias design determines gain, linearity, headroom, power consumption and temperature behaviour. It is the foundation of every analog stage, and the same ideas (set the DC point, then superimpose the signal) apply to single-supply op-amp circuits, ADC drivers and sensor front-ends. It also directly feeds the BJT vs MOSFET discussion in [[q0120]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Q-point (operating point)**: the DC $I_C$, $V_{CE}$ (or $I_D$, $V_{DS}$) with no signal. See [[q0100]].
- **Load line and headroom**: the signal moves the operating point along the load line; the Q-point sets symmetric swing.
- **BJT active region**: $I_C=\beta I_B$ with $V_{BE}\approx0.65$ V. See [[q0085]], [[q0087]].
- **Negative DC feedback**: an emitter or source resistor makes current self-stabilizing.
- **Coupling and bypass capacitors**: block DC, pass AC; set the low-frequency corner. See [[q0129]].
- **Thermal drift**: $V_{BE}$ falls about 2 mV/°C and $I_{CBO}$ and $\beta$ increase with temperature.
:::

::: answer
"Biasing means setting the DC operating point so that the transistor sits in its linear region and the signal can swing equally up and down without clipping. For a common-emitter BJT stage, I'd pick the collector current from the gain and noise I want, say 1 mA, then place the collector voltage around the middle of the supply so there's equal headroom either way.

The practical way to do it is voltage-divider bias with an emitter resistor. A resistor divider sets the base voltage, the emitter sits one $V_{BE}$ lower, and the emitter resistor converts that voltage into the emitter current: $I_E=(V_B-V_{BE})/R_E$. Because it uses a voltage, not a base current, it barely depends on $\beta$. If the current tries to rise, the emitter voltage rises, which cuts $V_{BE}$ and pulls it back, which is negative feedback. The design rule is to make the divider current about ten times the base current so it's stiff.

For example with a 12 V supply and 1 mA: $R_E=1$ kΩ gives $V_E=1$ V, base at about 1.65 V, a divider of about 94 kΩ and 16.5 kΩ, and a collector resistor of 5 kΩ puts the collector at 7 V. With $\beta$ ranging from 50 to 300 the current only moves from 0.89 to 1.09 mA, whereas a single base resistor would give 0.5 to 3 mA. Then coupling capacitors in and out and an emitter bypass capacitor if I want full gain. For a MOSFET it's the same idea with a gate divider and a source resistor, and for an op-amp on a single supply I bias the input at half the supply."
:::

::: technical
**BJT voltage-divider bias with emitter resistor ($V_{CC}=12$ V, $I_C=1$ mA target, $\beta\approx100$, $V_{BE}\approx0.65$ V).**
1. Choose $V_E\approx1$ V (about 10% of $V_{CC}$, large enough to stabilize against $V_{BE}$ drift of tens of mV): $R_E=V_E/I_E\approx1$ kΩ.
2. $V_B=V_E+V_{BE}=1.65$ V.
3. Divider current $I_1=10I_B=10\times I_C/\beta=100$ µA: $R_2=V_B/I_1=16.5$ kΩ; $R_1=(V_{CC}-V_B)/(I_1+I_B)=10.35\text{ V}/110\text{ µA}=94.1$ kΩ (standard values: 91 kΩ and 16 kΩ).
4. Collector: choose $V_C\approx7$ V (midway between $V_E+V_{sat}$ and $V_{CC}$): $R_C=(12-7)/1\text{ mA}=5$ kΩ.

**Exact analysis (Thevenin):** $V_{th}=V_{CC}\dfrac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}$, $R_{th}=R_1\parallel R_2$:
$$I_C=\frac{\beta\,(V_{th}-V_{BE})}{R_{th}+(\beta+1)R_E}$$
With 91 kΩ / 16 kΩ: $\beta=50$: 0.886 mA; $\beta=100$: 0.999 mA; $\beta=300$: 1.091 mA. For comparison fixed-base bias ($R_B$ from $V_{CC}$, sized for 1 mA at $\beta=100$): $\beta=50$: 0.5 mA; $\beta=300$: 3.0 mA (which would saturate with $R_C=5$ kΩ).

**Stability condition.** For $\beta$-insensitivity, require $(\beta+1)R_E\gg R_{th}$: here $101\times1\text{ k}=101$ kΩ versus $R_{th}=14$ kΩ. For temperature: $V_{BE}$ changes about $-2$ mV/°C; with $V_E=1$ V, $R_E$ turns a 50 mV drop into a $\Delta I_E=50\text{ mV}/1\text{ k}=50$ µA (5%) change over 25 °C, whereas without $R_E$ the collector current would change by $e^{\Delta V/V_T}$, about 7x per 50 mV.

**Small-signal gain.** $r_e=V_T/I_E=25.9$ Ω at 1 mA. With $R_E$ bypassed by $C_E$: $A_v\approx-R_C/r_e=-193$. Unbypassed: $A_v\approx-R_C/(R_E+r_e)=-4.9$ (stable, linear, set by resistor ratios). A partial bypass (a split $R_E$) gives intermediate gain.

**Input impedance and coupling cap.** $R_{in}=R_1\parallel R_2\parallel(\beta+1)r_e\approx2.2$ kΩ. A high-pass corner of 20 Hz needs $C_1=1/(2\pi\times20\times2.2\text{ k})=3.6$ µF.

**MOSFET equivalent.** Gate divider fixes $V_G$; source resistor sets $I_D$: $V_{GS}=V_G-I_DR_S$, $I_D=\tfrac12k(V_{GS}-V_t)^2$. For $k=2$ mA/V², $V_t=1$ V, $V_{DD}=10$ V: target 1 mA means $V_{ov}=1$ V, $V_{GS}=2$ V; $R_S=1$ kΩ gives $V_S=1$ V so $V_G=3$ V (e.g. 7 MΩ / 3 MΩ), $R_D=4$ kΩ puts $V_D=6$ V; $g_m=2I_D/V_{ov}=2$ mS, gain $-g_mR_D=-8$. No gate current, so the divider can be megaohms.

**Op-amp single-supply bias.** Two equal resistors from $V_{CC}$ to ground set a mid-rail reference, decoupled by a capacitor; the non-inverting input (or the reference of the gain network) sits there, with input and output coupled through capacitors. The DC gain is 1 (the AC gain network is isolated by a capacitor), so the output also sits at mid-rail.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $V_{BE}\approx0.65$ V and $-2$ mV/°C are typical small-signal silicon values; they depend on current level and device. The "10x base current" and "$V_E\approx$ 10% of $V_{CC}$" rules are conventions, not laws; many designs use 5x-20x.
:::

::: long
"Biasing is choosing the DC conditions so the transistor sits in its active region with the right current and enough headroom, then letting the AC signal ride on top. The Q-point should be mid-way on the load line so the output can swing equally up and down without clipping.

The problem is stability. If I bias a BJT with one base resistor, the base current is fixed and the collector current is beta times it. Beta varies by 3x or more between parts and with temperature, so the collector current, and the collector voltage, wander all over the place and the stage clips or saturates.

The standard fix is voltage-divider bias with an emitter resistor. A divider sets the base voltage. The emitter sits one $V_{BE}$, about 0.65 V, below it, and the emitter resistor turns that into current: $I_E=(V_B-V_{BE})/R_E$. That depends on resistors and a roughly constant $V_{BE}$, not on beta. If the current tries to increase, the emitter voltage rises, which reduces $V_{BE}$, which cuts the current: negative feedback. To make the base voltage stiff the divider current is about ten times the base current.

Example: 12 V supply, 1 mA. Pick $V_E=1$ V so $R_E$ is 1 kΩ, base at 1.65 V, divider about 94 kΩ over 16.5 kΩ, and a 5 kΩ collector resistor puts the collector at 7 V. Across beta from 50 to 300 the current only varies from 0.89 to 1.09 mA.

Then the AC part: a coupling capacitor at the input, sized so that the corner with the input impedance, about 2 kΩ, is below the lowest frequency, roughly 4 µF for 20 Hz; an output coupling capacitor; and an emitter bypass capacitor if I want the full gain of $R_C/r_e$, about 190, rather than $R_C/R_E$, about 5.

For a MOSFET it's the same: a gate divider and a source resistor, with no base current, so the divider can be megaohms. For an op-amp on a single supply, I bias the non-inverting input at half the supply with two resistors and a decoupling capacitor, and AC-couple the signal."
:::

::: terms
- **Bias / Q-point**: the DC operating current and voltages with no signal.
- **Load line**: the line of allowed $(V_{CE},I_C)$ values from the supply and load resistor.
- **Voltage-divider bias**: base network of $R_1$/$R_2$ that sets $V_B$ nearly independent of $\beta$.
- **Emitter degeneration ($R_E$)**: emitter resistor providing DC negative feedback and, unbypassed, AC gain stabilization.
- **Bypass capacitor ($C_E$)**: capacitor across $R_E$ that shorts it at signal frequencies.
- **Coupling capacitor**: series capacitor that blocks DC and passes AC.
- **Thermal runaway**: rising temperature raises current, which raises dissipation and temperature further.
- **Mid-rail bias**: setting a single-supply stage's DC level at $V_{CC}/2$.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "use a resistor divider to set the base voltage." An exceptional answer:

- States the goal (stable Q-point with headroom) and **why** simple bias fails ($\beta$ and temperature spread: 0.5 to 3 mA for $\beta$ 50 to 300).
- Designs the network with numbers: $R_E$ from $V_E$, divider at 10x $I_B$, $R_C$ for mid-swing; and checks with the Thevenin formula over $\beta$.
- Explains the **negative feedback** mechanism and the condition $(\beta+1)R_E\gg R_{th}$.
- Covers the **AC side** (coupling/bypass capacitors, low-frequency corner, unbypassed vs bypassed gain).
- Extends to **MOSFETs, op-amps on single supply, and current-mirror bias** in ICs, and mentions thermal runaway for power stages.
- Mentions trade-offs: larger $V_E$ gives more stability but less swing; smaller divider resistors give stiffness but load the source and waste current.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Fixed-base bias for a production design.** Collector current tracks $\beta$, which varies widely and with temperature.
- **Making the divider too weak.** If the divider current is comparable to $I_B$, the base voltage sags with $\beta$ and the stabilizing effect is lost.
- **Forgetting the AC consequences of $R_E$.** Without $C_E$ the gain drops from $R_C/r_e$ to about $R_C/R_E$; with $C_E$ the low-frequency response has a corner set by $C_E$ and $r_e$.
- **Biasing at the supply midpoint without checking headroom.** The Q-point should account for $V_E$ and saturation: optimal $V_C$ is about $(V_{CC}+V_E+V_{sat})/2$.
- **Ignoring the input source.** Coupling capacitors and the source's DC path matter; a floating AC-coupled input needs a bias path to ground or mid-rail.
- **Assuming $V_{BE}$ is exactly 0.7 V.** It depends on current and temperature; compute with the real number and check sensitivity.
:::

::: deep
## What bias is for

A transistor amplifier works around a point where the device is on and has gain: for a BJT, the forward-active region; for a MOSFET, saturation. The signal is a small variation on top of DC currents and voltages. If the DC point is too close to the supply the output clips at the top; too close to the emitter/source the transistor saturates at the bottom. So the first step is choosing the Q-point; the second is making it hold.

Try $I_B=30$ µA and see the transistor saturate: this is what a fixed-bias stage does when $\beta$ is 3x higher than expected.

**Choosing the Q-point.** The current sets $g_m=I_C/V_T$ (gain, bandwidth, noise); the collector voltage sets headroom: the output can swing up to $V_{CC}$ and down to about $V_E+V_{CE,sat}$. The midpoint $V_C\approx(V_{CC}+V_E+0.2)/2=(12+1+0.2)/2=6.6$ V, so 7 V is fine.

## Why fixed bias fails

Base resistor $R_B$ from $V_{CC}$: $I_B=(V_{CC}-V_{BE})/R_B$ and $I_C=\beta I_B$. For 1 mA at $\beta=100$: $R_B=(12-0.65)/10\text{ µA}=1.135$ MΩ. At $\beta=300$ you'd get 3 mA, and $V_C=12-15=-3$ V: impossible, so the transistor saturates. At $\beta=50$, 0.5 mA, $V_C=9.5$ V, a far-from-ideal point and a gain half what you designed.

Figure: $R_1/R_2$ fix the base at about 1.65 V; $R_E$ sets the emitter current; $R_C$ puts the collector near 7 V. $C_1$ and $C_2$ couple the signal in and out while blocking DC; $C_E$ bypasses $R_E$ at signal frequencies.

**Why it works.** The base voltage is held (almost) fixed by the divider. If $I_C$ rises, $V_E=I_ER_E$ rises, so $V_{BE}=V_B-V_E$ falls, and since $I_C\propto e^{V_{BE}/V_T}$ it falls back. The loop gain of this DC feedback is large when $R_E$ is large, and the divider must hold $V_B$ against the base current drawn, hence the ten-to-one rule.

**Exact current.** Thevenin equivalent of the divider ($V_{th}$, $R_{th}$) drives the base:
$$I_C=\frac{\beta(V_{th}-V_{BE})}{R_{th}+(\beta+1)R_E}\ \xrightarrow{(\beta+1)R_E\gg R_{th}}\ \frac{V_{th}-V_{BE}}{R_E}$$

**Trade-offs.** Larger $R_E$ and $V_E$ improve stability but reduce output swing and (unbypassed) gain; lower divider resistances improve stiffness but burn current and load the signal source (the bias network is in parallel with the input).

## The AC picture

The coupling capacitors and the bypass capacitor turn the DC network into an AC amplifier:

- **Input corner:** $f_L=1/(2\pi C_1R_{in})$ with $R_{in}=R_1\parallel R_2\parallel(\beta+1)r_e$. With $R_1\parallel R_2=13.6$ kΩ, $(\beta+1)r_e=2.6$ kΩ gives $R_{in}\approx2.2$ kΩ and $C_1=3.6$ µF for 20 Hz.
- **Bypass:** with $C_E$, gain $\approx-R_C/r_e=-193$ (but distortion and gain depend strongly on $I_C$). Without it, $-R_C/(R_E+r_e)=-4.9$, with much lower distortion. A split $R_E$ (bypass only part) sets intermediate gain.
- **Output corner** from $C_2$ with the load.

Design $C_E$ so that its reactance at the lowest frequency is much less than $r_e$ (about 26 Ω): for 20 Hz, $C_E\gg1/(2\pi\times20\times26)=306$ µF; in practice a few hundred µF or accept a higher corner.

## MOSFET and op-amp variants

**MOSFET.** The gate draws no DC current, so the divider can be 1-10 MΩ and simply sets $V_G$. $I_D=\tfrac12k(V_G-I_DR_S-V_t)^2$ (solve as in [[q0100]]). The source resistor stabilizes against $V_t$ variation, which is large from part to part. Example: $k=2$ mA/V², $V_t=1$ V, $V_{DD}=10$ V, 7 MΩ/3 MΩ divider ($V_G=3$ V), $R_S=1$ kΩ: $I_D=\tfrac12(2\text{ m})(3-1-1)^2=1$ mA, $V_D=10-1\text{ mA}\times4\text{ k}=6$ V.

**Op-amp on a single supply.** An op-amp's outputs and inputs can't swing negative, so the signal rides on $V_{CC}/2$: two 100 kΩ resistors and a 1-10 µF capacitor create a mid-rail bias, and AC coupling capacitors pass the signal. The DC gain of the stage is 1 (the gain-setting resistor is AC-coupled), so DC offsets are not amplified.

**Differential and IC amplifiers.** Bias is set by a **current mirror** (reference current copied to tail and load devices) and by feedback (a common-mode feedback loop); see [[q0104]]. This makes bias independent of supply and mostly of temperature, with the cost of mirror mismatch.

## Thermal runaway and Class-AB bias

In a power output stage, $V_{BE}$ falls with temperature, so a fixed $V_B$ makes current rise, which heats the device further, which raises current: thermal runaway. Emitter resistors (and for class-AB, a bias network thermally coupled to the output devices, for example a $V_{BE}$ multiplier mounted on the heatsink) limit it.

## Real hardware

- A general-purpose small-signal NPN with $\beta$ of 100-300 (datasheet spreads of 2-3x) shows exactly the spread above.
- At 1 mA the emitter resistor's voltage should be at least about 1 V for good stability; in low-supply designs (3.3 V) $V_E$ may be 0.3-0.5 V, so the stabilization is weaker.
- For audio coupling use electrolytic or film capacitors with the correct polarity (the DC voltage across $C_1$ is the base bias, +1.65 V in this design).

- **Discrete audio and RF preamplifiers**: common-emitter and common-source stages with divider bias.
- **Single-supply op-amp circuits**: mid-rail biasing for sensors, microphones and ADC drivers.
- **IC analog blocks**: current-mirror biasing of differential pairs and gain stages ([[q0104]]).
- **Power amplifier output stages**: class-AB bias networks with thermal tracking.
- **LNA and RF stages**: bias tees and bias networks that set the Q-point without loading the RF path.
- **Sensor front-ends**: setting a photodiode or microphone's DC level in the amplifier's range.

::: followups
### Why make the divider current ten times the base current?
So the base current draw barely disturbs the base voltage: the divider looks like an ideal voltage source with $R_{th}\ll(\beta+1)R_E$. If the divider current were only equal to $I_B$, $V_B$ would sag and vary with $\beta$. Ten times is a compromise: stiffer needs more current wasted and heavier loading on the input.

### How does the emitter resistor stabilize against temperature?
$V_{BE}$ falls about 2 mV/°C. Without $R_E$, 25 °C of heating shifts $V_{BE}$ by 50 mV and increases $I_C$ about $e^{50/25.9}\approx6.9\times$. With $R_E=1$ kΩ and a fixed $V_B$ the same 50 mV shift raises $I_E$ by only 50 µA (5% of 1 mA).

### What is the gain with and without the bypass capacitor?
Bypassed: $-R_C/r_e\approx-5\text{ k}/25.9=-193$. Unbypassed: $-R_C/(R_E+r_e)\approx-5\text{ k}/1026=-4.9$. Unbypassed gain is set by resistor ratios, so it's stable and has lower distortion; bypassed gain depends on $I_C$ through $r_e$.

### Where should the Q-point be for maximum symmetric swing with $V_{CC}=12$ V, $V_E=1$ V?
$V_{CE,min}\approx0.2$ V so the minimum collector voltage is $V_E+0.2=1.2$ V; the maximum is 12 V. The midpoint is $(12+1.2)/2=6.6$ V, so $V_C=6.6$-7 V: amplitude about ±5.4 V before clipping in the ideal case; in practice distortion limits the swing to less.

### Harder: how would you bias a MOSFET amplifier so the Q-point is insensitive to $V_t$ variation?
Use a source resistor and a stiff gate divider so the bias current depends on $R_S$: with $V_G$ fixed, $I_D\approx(V_G-V_t-V_{ov})/R_S$; the larger $V_G-V_S$ across $R_S$ relative to the $V_t$ spread, the less $I_D$ varies. Or use a current-mirror/current-source bias (the IC approach), or a feedback bias with a drain-to-gate resistor.

### Scenario: your amplifier's output is clipped at the top on cold start but fine after warm-up. What do you check?
Bias drift with temperature or capacitor charge-up: with divider bias, $V_{BE}$ is larger when cold (about +2 mV/°C), so $I_C$ is lower and $V_C$ higher; check that the cold $V_C$ is still in range. Also check the coupling-capacitor settling time: the bias takes about $R\cdot C$ to settle, and a large bypass or coupling capacitor can take seconds. Verify DC voltages at power-up on a scope.
