::: basics
### In plain words
The usual way to measure a motor's rpm is a sensor that gives pulses as the shaft turns, such as an encoder, a Hall sensor reading a magnet, or an optical slot sensor. Pulses per second, divided by pulses per revolution, gives revolutions per second, and you multiply by 60 for rpm. The real skill is in how you count the pulses. You can also get speed from a motor's own back-EMF with no sensor at all.

### Picture it
Think of taking someone's pulse. You can count the beats for 15 seconds and multiply by 4. Or you can time the gap between two beats and divide into 60. When the heart beats fast, counting for a window works well. When it beats very slowly, you only catch one or two beats in the window, so timing one gap is better.

### Words you need
- **PPR**: pulses per revolution of the sensor. A single magnet gives 1.
- **Gate (frequency) method**: count pulses in a fixed time window. It works well at high speed.
- **Period method**: time the interval between two pulses with a fast clock. It works well at low speed.
- **Input capture**: a timer feature that records the clock value at each pulse edge, so software delays do not add error.
- **Back-EMF**: the voltage a spinning motor generates, proportional to speed. See [[q0408]].

### The simplest example
A Hall sensor gives 1 pulse per revolution, and the motor turns at 3000 rpm. That is 50 pulses per second, one every 20 ms. With a 100 ms window you catch only 5 pulses, and one miscount is a 20% error. With a 1 MHz clock timing one pulse period, you count 20,000 ticks, so one tick is only 0.005%.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page derives both methods, shows the M/T hybrid, covers sensor choices and timer details, and weighs back-EMF and current-ripple counting.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can list the real ways to get RPM (tachometer, encoder, Hall or optical pulse, back-EMF, current ripple, contactless lab tools) *and* reason about their trade-offs. The deeper test is the signal-processing step: converting a pulse train to RPM with a counter. Strong candidates know that counting pulses in a window and timing the interval between pulses behave very differently at low and high speed, and can quantify the resolution of each.
:::

::: why
Speed feedback is the input to every speed loop, stall detector and efficiency measurement. In a lab you also need to *characterise* an unknown motor: no-load speed, speed versus load, and so on. The exact same conversion problem (pulses to rpm with finite resolution) appears in wheel speed, flow meters and fans, so interviewers like it as a compact test of measurement thinking.
:::

::: concepts
- **Pulses per revolution (PPR)**: how many electrical pulses the sensor gives per shaft turn. It sets resolution. See [[q0405]].
- **Frequency to RPM**: $\text{rpm} = 60\,f_{pulse}/\text{PPR}$.
- **Back-EMF**: $E = K_e\omega$, so terminal voltage encodes speed. See [[q0341]] and [[q0408]].
- **Timer input capture**: a hardware counter that timestamps an edge with the timer clock. See [[q0402]] for why we want the number.
- **Quantisation error**: a counter only gives integers, so there is always $\pm1$ count of uncertainty.
:::

Each gives me some number of pulses per revolution, and I convert frequency to rpm with $60 f / \text{PPR}$.

The interesting part is how I count. A single-pulse-per-rev sensor at 3,000 rpm only gives me 5 pulses in 100 ms, so the window method is off by one count, which is 20%. Timing the period with a 1 MHz clock gets me down to a fraction of a percent. Good controllers use a hybrid of both.

If I don't want a sensor, I can use the motor's back-EMF, because it's proportional to speed. That works for brushed and sensorless brushless drives. There's also current ripple counting on brushed motors, and a tachogenerator, which is basically a small DC generator whose output voltage is proportional to speed.

For a quick lab measurement without touching the motor, a handheld optical tach with a reflective strip, or a stroboscope, works well."
:::

::: technical
**Pulse-based methods.** For a sensor with PPR pulses per revolution and shaft speed $n$ (rpm), the pulse frequency is $f = n\,\text{PPR}/60$.

*Frequency (gate) method, "M method":* count $N$ pulses during a fixed gate time $T_g$.
$$n = \frac{60\,N}{\text{PPR}\,T_g},\qquad \Delta n = \frac{60}{\text{PPR}\,T_g}$$
Absolute resolution is constant. The *relative* error $\Delta n/n$ is large at low speed.

*Period method, "T method":* count timer ticks $N_c$ (clock $f_{clk}$) between two consecutive pulses.
$$n = \frac{60\,f_{clk}}{\text{PPR}\,N_c},\qquad \Delta n \approx \frac{n}{N_c} = \frac{n^2\,\text{PPR}}{60\,f_{clk}}$$
Relative error is small at low speed and grows as $n$ rises, because fewer clock ticks fit between pulses.

*M/T method:* count pulses *and* clock ticks over a window that ends on a pulse edge, giving roughly constant relative resolution across speed.

**Numbers.** One pulse per rev, $T_g = 0.1$ s: $\Delta n = 600$ rpm, so at 3,000 rpm you see $N = 5$ and the reading flickers by 20%. With the period method at $f_{clk} = 1$ MHz, 3,000 rpm gives $N_c = 20{,}000$, resolution $0.15$ rpm (0.005%).

**Other sensing physics.**
- **Tachogenerator**: output $V = K_{tach}\,\omega$, typically a few V per 1,000 rpm. Analog and simple, but adds a brush or a rotor and costs space.
- **Back-EMF**: $E = V_{term} - IR - L\,di/dt \approx K_e\omega$. See [[q0408]].
- **Current ripple counting**: a brushed motor's current has a ripple at each commutator segment crossing; counting ripples per revolution gives speed and position without a sensor.
- **Optical (laser) tach / stroboscope**: contactless, for lab characterisation.

**Conversion for MCU use.** Use the timer's input-capture feature to timestamp each edge in hardware, so interrupt latency doesn't corrupt the measurement. Filter with a short moving average; deal with timer overflow at very low speed (declare "zero" if no edge arrives within a timeout).
:::

::: long
"My go-to is a pulse sensor on the shaft: an optical encoder, a Hall sensor reading a magnet or gear tooth, or a slotted-disc interrupter. It gives me N pulses per revolution, so rpm is sixty times the pulse frequency over N.

The engineering is in the counting. There are two basic methods. In the gate method, I count pulses over a fixed window like 100 ms. The resolution is constant, sixty over N times the window, but at low speed there are only a few pulses in the window, so the relative error is big. With one pulse per rev at 3,000 rpm, I only count five pulses, so a one-count error is 20%. In the period method, I use a hardware timer to time the interval between two pulses and take the reciprocal. At low speed there are lots of clock ticks in the interval, so relative error is tiny. At high speed the interval shrinks and resolution gets worse. So low speed favours period, high speed favours gate, and in a real drive you'd use an M over T hybrid that counts both pulses and clock ticks.

If I don't want a sensor, the motor itself is a tachometer: back-EMF is Ke times omega, so during the off-time of the PWM I can measure the terminal voltage and divide by Ke. That's cheap but poor at low speed and sensitive to temperature if I'm using the on-time. Brushed motors also have commutation ripple in the current that I can count.

For lab work, a handheld optical tach with a reflective strip or a strobe tells me the true speed without touching the shaft, which is a good independent check on whatever the electronics report."
:::

::: terms
- **PPR / CPR**: pulses per revolution of one channel, versus counts per revolution after quadrature decoding (4x PPR). See [[q0405]].
- **Gate time**: the fixed counting window in the frequency method.
- **Input capture**: a timer feature that latches the counter value on an edge, giving precise timestamps.
- **M/T method**: hybrid measuring both pulse count and elapsed time for nearly constant relative resolution.
- **Back-EMF**: voltage generated by the rotating armature, proportional to speed.
- **Tachogenerator**: a small generator whose output voltage is proportional to speed.
- **Quantisation error**: the $\pm1$ count uncertainty inherent in any counter.
- **Hall sensor**: a sensor that outputs a voltage or logic level when a magnetic field is present.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "use an encoder". An exceptional answer:

- Gives the **formula** $60f/\text{PPR}$ and states which direction resolution goes with each counting method.
- **Computes** the error with real numbers (e.g. 5 counts per window at 3,000 rpm, or 20,000 ticks per period at 1 MHz).
- Mentions a **hybrid** approach and timeout handling for low speed and standstill.
- Offers **sensorless** options (back-EMF, current ripple) with their limits.
- Mentions an **independent lab check** (optical tach, strobe) for calibration.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using the frequency method with one pulse per rev at low speed.** You get a few counts per window and huge relative error. Use the period method or add pulses (more slots, more poles).
- **Software polling a pin to measure period.** Jitter from interrupts and other tasks dominates. Use input capture.
- **Forgetting timer overflow.** At very low speed the counter wraps. Handle the overflow or declare zero speed after a timeout.
- **Mixing PPR and CPR.** A 100-PPR quadrature encoder decoded 4x is 400 counts per rev. Using the wrong one is a 4x error in rpm.
- **Trusting back-EMF at low speed.** The signal ($K_e\omega$) shrinks to millivolts and drowns in noise and $IR$ drop. See [[q0408]].
- **Ignoring gearbox ratios.** The sensor may be on the motor shaft while the load speed is divided by the gear ratio.
:::

::: deep
## From pulses to rpm

The sensor gives a periodic pulse train; everything reduces to measuring its frequency. Suppose a motor spins at $n$ rpm, which is $n/60$ rev/s. With PPR pulses per revolution, the pulse frequency is

$$f = \frac{n\,\text{PPR}}{60}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad n = \frac{60 f}{\text{PPR}}$$

A single Hall sensor looking at one magnet is PPR = 1. At 3,000 rpm that is 50 Hz, a pulse every 20 ms. A 1,024-line encoder decoded 4x gives 4,096 counts per rev, 204.8 kHz at 3,000 rpm.

## Two counting methods, derived

**Gate (frequency) method.** Over a window $T_g$ you count $N = f T_g$ pulses, with $\pm1$ count error. Hence

$$\Delta n = \frac{60}{\text{PPR}\,T_g},\qquad \frac{\Delta n}{n} = \frac{60}{\text{PPR}\,T_g\,n}$$

Take PPR = 1, $T_g = 0.1$ s: $\Delta n = 600$ rpm. At 600 rpm the error is 100%; at 3,000 rpm it is 20%. If you lengthen the window to 1 s, resolution improves 10x but the measurement is 10x slower, which hurts the control loop.

**Period method.** Between two pulses the timer counts $N_c = f_{clk}/f = 60 f_{clk}/(n\,\text{PPR})$ ticks. A $\pm1$ tick error gives

$$\frac{\Delta n}{n} = \frac{1}{N_c} = \frac{n\,\text{PPR}}{60 f_{clk}}$$

With $f_{clk} = 1$ MHz and PPR = 1: at 3,000 rpm $N_c = 20{,}000$ and the error is 0.005%; at 6,000 rpm 0.01%; at 60 rpm it is 0.0001%. The catch is **latency**: you only get a new value once per pulse. At 60 rpm and PPR = 1 that is once per second.

They would only cross near 60,000 rpm for this sensor, so at normal speeds the period method wins here; the gate method becomes attractive when you have many pulses per rev. Practical designs use period for low speed and gate (or more pulses per rev) for high speed.

**Increase PPR.** More pulses per rev improves both methods and reduces latency. A 12-slot disc gives 12 pulses per rev, so at 3,000 rpm a 100 ms window holds 60 pulses (1.7% per count) instead of 5. This is the usual cheap fix.

**M/T method.** Start and stop a window on pulse edges: count the integer number of pulses $M_1$ and the clock ticks $M_2$ elapsed over the window: $n = 60 f_{clk} M_1/(\text{PPR}\,M_2)$. The window can last about the control period, and the resolution is $\pm1$ clock tick in $M_2$, giving nearly constant relative resolution.

## Sensor choices

| Sensor | Typical PPR | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Optical encoder | 100 to thousands | high resolution, direction | dust, cost, temperature |
| Hall on ring magnet | 1 to tens | rugged, cheap | low pulse count |
| Hall/inductive on gear tooth | number of teeth | steel gear, harsh environments | needs target |
| Slotted optical interrupter | slots in disc | cheap, hobby | alignment, dust |
| Tachogenerator | analog | simple loop | brushes, size, noise |
| Back-EMF | analog | no sensor | poor at low speed |

## Back-EMF and current ripple, briefly

If the motor has no sensor, the electrical terminals carry speed information. Back-EMF is $K_e\omega$: a 12 V motor with $K_e = 0.02$ V/(rad/s) shows 6.28 V at 3,000 rpm and only 0.63 V at 300 rpm. Measuring it correctly during the PWM off-time is the subject of [[q0408]]. A brushed motor with $k$ commutator segments produces $k$ current ripples per revolution; counting ripples (after filtering) gives a pulse train from the existing current sense ([[q0395]]), accurate enough for position-ish tasks on window lifts but with missed or extra counts at the edges of the speed range.

## Practical implementation on an MCU

1. Route the sensor to a timer input-capture channel. Add a Schmitt trigger or RC filter if the signal is noisy ([[q0405]] covers conditioning).
2. In the capture ISR, store the timestamp difference. Keep an overflow counter to extend the timer range.
3. Convert to rpm with an integer reciprocal or a lookup; apply a short moving average or first-order filter.
4. If no edge arrives within a timeout (for example, twice the slowest valid period), report zero.
5. For a speed loop above a few hundred Hz, switch to M/T or to higher PPR so each control cycle has a fresh measurement.

> [!TIP]
> If asked "how would you check your electronic reading?", say you would compare it against a handheld optical tachometer or a strobe on a reflective mark. It is contactless and independent of your electronics.
:::

::: applications
- **Fan and pump controllers**: a Hall tach pulse (often 2 pulses per rev) from a PC fan lets the controller hold a speed or detect a stalled fan.
- **Wheel and vehicle speed**: toothed wheel plus Hall or variable-reluctance sensor; period measurement at low speed.
- **Brushless ESCs**: speed from back-EMF zero-crossing timing, no extra sensor.
- **Servo motors**: high-PPR encoders with hardware quadrature counters feed a 10+ kHz speed loop.
- **Lab bench characterisation**: optical handheld tach on a reflective strip to measure no-load speed and verify $K_e$.
- **Printers and scanners**: optical encoder strips or discs for DC motors.
:::

::: assumptions
- The pulses are **clean and evenly spaced**. A real gear or a ring magnet has tooth and pole spacing errors that show up as ripple in the speed estimate, particularly at low PPR.
- The question says "RPM of a motor": I assume a **rotating** motor with shaft access. Contactless methods (optical tach, strobe, even audio frequency analysis) are the alternatives if the shaft is not accessible.
- The back-EMF method assumes the **motor constant $K_e$ is known** and doesn't change much with temperature. Magnet strength drops with temperature, so $K_e$ falls roughly 0.1 to 0.2 % per kelvin for common NdFeB and ferrite magnets (grade-dependent).
- Resolution numbers assume a timer clock of 1 MHz and a **perfect** edge detector; real jitter and sensor hysteresis add to them.
:::

::: followups
### Your motor has a single Hall sensor and runs from 30 rpm to 6,000 rpm. How would you measure its speed?
Use input capture for the period method across the whole range. At 6,000 rpm the period is 10 ms, which at a 1 MHz clock gives 10,000 ticks, so resolution is 0.01%. At 30 rpm the period is 2 s; use a timer with a prescaler or a software overflow counter. The cost is latency: one measurement every 2 s at 30 rpm. If the control loop needs faster updates at low speed, add more pulses per rev (more magnets), or switch to an encoder.

### How do you get direction as well as speed?
You need two sensors 90 electrical degrees apart (quadrature). Which channel leads tells you the direction. See [[q0405]].

### What resolution do you get from a 12-slot disc using the gate method with a 50 ms window?
$\Delta n = 60/(12\times0.05) = 100$ rpm per count. At 3,000 rpm that is 3.3%; at 600 rpm, 17%. If that is too coarse, lengthen the window, add slots, or switch to the period method.

### How can a brushed motor measure its own speed with no extra sensor, and when does that fail?
By measuring back-EMF during the PWM off-time ($E \approx K_e\omega$) or by counting commutation ripple in the current. It fails at low speed, because back-EMF and ripple get too small relative to noise, and at high load transients because $IR$ and inductive effects confuse the estimate ([[q0408]]).

### The reading jumps around by about 2% even though the motor is steady. How do you debug that?
First check mechanical: ring magnet or tooth spacing errors, shaft eccentricity. Then electrical: a noisy edge causing double triggers (add hysteresis or an RC filter), or software jitter if you're not using input capture. Finally, quantisation: at that error the pulse count is only about 50 per window, so try a longer window or the M/T method.
:::

::: interpretations
"RPM of a motor" is rotating-shaft speed in revolutions per minute. For linear motors or solenoids the equivalent is velocity, treated in [[q0406]]. If the interviewer wants the *electrical* rpm of a brushless motor, multiply mechanical rpm by the number of pole pairs.
:::
