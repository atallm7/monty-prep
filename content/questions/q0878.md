::: basics
### In plain words
A level shifter converts a logic signal from one supply voltage to another, such as 3.3 V to 5 V. First ask: which voltages, which direction, how fast, how much load. For a fast bus the default answer is a dual-supply translator chip. For a slow single line, a transistor and a resistor can do it.

### Picture it
Picture a child pressing a small button that opens a large gate. The button needs only a light push, which is the low-voltage signal. The gate then swings as far as its own motor allows, which is the higher supply voltage. A transistor does this job: a small input voltage switches a connection on the higher supply.

### Words you need
- **NMOS transistor**: a voltage-controlled switch. Current flows between its drain and source pins when its gate pin is raised above a threshold.
- **Rail**: a supply voltage line, such as the 5 V rail.
- **Pull-up resistor**: a resistor from the output to the high rail, which pulls the output high when the transistor is off.
- **Rise time**: how long the output takes to climb from 10 to 90 percent. For a pull-up into a load capacitance, it is about 2.2 times R times C.
- **Translator IC**: a chip with a separate supply pin for each side, which handles speed and drive for you. See [[q0877]].

### The simplest example
Shift a 3.3 V signal to 5 V with an NMOS and a pull-up. This stage inverts: input high gives output low. The load is 15 pF, and we want a 100 ns rise. The resistor must be at most 100 ns divided by 2.2 times 15 pF, which is about 3 kΩ. When the transistor is on, current flows through that resistor, 5 V divided by 3 kΩ, which is 1.67 mA. A faster edge needs a smaller resistor and wastes more current, so a fast bus (say 20 MHz) gets a translator IC. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page sizes the transistor stage and covers the version built inside chips.
:::

::: testing
Whether you treat "design a level shifter" as a *requirements* question first: which voltages, which direction, push-pull or open-drain, how fast, how much load. Then, whether you can offer more than one architecture (translator IC, discrete transistor stage, cross-coupled on-chip shifter), size a discrete one with real numbers (pull-up value from rise time and quiescent current), and say when each one breaks. The single-FET shifter is a special case covered in [[q0879]].
:::

::: why
Level shifting is one of the most common small design tasks on a board, and one of the easiest to get subtly wrong: edge rates that don't meet the bus spec, shifters that fail at low temperature when a threshold drifts, contention on bidirectional lines. In an interview it is a good test of design process: asking clarifying questions, picking an architecture, and calculating a component value.
:::

::: answer
"I'd start by asking what I'm shifting between, which direction the signal goes, whether it's push-pull or open-drain, and how fast it is, because the best design changes completely. For a typical case, like a 3.3 V MCU talking SPI at 10 MHz to a 5 V part, the right answer is a dual-supply translator IC, one rail pin for each side and a direction pin. It's tested, fast and cheap.

If I had to build it from discrete parts, for a slow signal, I'd use an NMOS with the gate driven from the low-voltage side, the source to ground, and a pull-up resistor from the drain to the high rail. The drain is the output. When the input is high the FET pulls the output low, so it inverts; a second stage or firmware fixes the polarity. The pull-up sets the speed: rise time is about 2.2 R C, so for 15 pF and a 100 ns edge, R is about 3 kΩ, which burns about 1.7 mA when low.

For a bidirectional open-drain signal like I²C, I'd use the single-FET version with pull-ups on both sides. And inside a chip, the standard shifter is two cross-coupled PMOS pulling up to the high rail, with NMOS pull-downs driven from the low-voltage logic, which is both fast and static-power free. I'd always verify thresholds in both directions and at corners."
:::

::: technical
**Requirements to pin down:** source/destination rails ($V_{L}$, $V_{H}$), direction(s), push-pull vs open-drain, maximum frequency or rise-time budget, load capacitance $C$, power/quiescent limits, supply sequencing, number of channels.

**Option A: translator IC.** Dual-supply buffer: input stage referenced to $V_{CCA}$, output stage to $V_{CCB}$. Unidirectional (fixed or DIR-controlled) parts reach tens to hundreds of MHz; auto-direction parts are slower and have restrictions on pull-ups and loading. Best for buses, multi-bit signals and anything fast.

**Option B: discrete NMOS inverter (up or down).**
- Gate on low-rail logic, source to ground, drain pulled to $V_H$ through $R_p$.
- Output low $\approx I\cdot R_{DS(on)}$, essentially 0 V. High $=V_H$.
- Switching on: fast pull-down (set by $R_{DS(on)}$ and $C$). Switching off: rise set by $R_pC$: $t_r\approx2.2R_pC$.
- Need $V_{L,min}-V_{OL,drv}>V_{th,max}$ with enough overdrive for low $R_{DS(on)}$.
- Quiescent current when low: $I=V_H/R_p$.

**Worked sizing.** 1 MHz signal, $V_H=5$ V, $C=15$ pF, $t_r\le100$ ns: $R_p\le100\text{ ns}/(2.2\cdot15\text{ pF})=3.0$ kΩ. Then $I_{low}=5/3\text{k}=1.67$ mA and $P_{low}=8.3$ mW; at 50% duty that averages 4.2 mW per line. Compare 0.375 mW for dynamic switching $CV^2f$. A smaller $R_p$ buys speed at the cost of static power, which is why discrete shifters stop making sense above a few MHz.

**Option C: cross-coupled CMOS shifter** (on-chip). Two NMOS driven by $V_L$ logic (IN and its complement), two cross-coupled PMOS to $V_H$. No static current in either state. The NMOS must overpower the PMOS to flip the latch; to first order (square law) $\frac{k_n}{k_p}>\left(\frac{V_H-|V_{tp}|}{V_L-V_{tn}}\right)^2$. For $V_L=1.2$ V, $V_H=3.3$ V, $V_{tn}=0.4$ V, $|V_{tp}|=0.7$ V this ratio is about 10.6.

**Option D: single-FET bidirectional** for open-drain buses ([[q0879]]).
:::

::: short
"I'd ask for rails, direction, push-pull versus open-drain and speed first. For a fast or multi-bit push-pull bus I'd use a dual-supply translator IC. For a slow single signal I'd use an NMOS with the gate on the low rail and a pull-up to the high rail on the drain, inverting, with the pull-up sized from 2.2RC and the quiescent current. For open-drain buses like I²C I'd use the single-FET bidirectional shifter. On chip, it's a cross-coupled PMOS pair with NMOS pull-downs."
:::

::: long
"My first move is to pin down the requirements, since 'level shifter' covers a lot. What are the two voltages? Is the signal one-way or bidirectional? Push-pull or open-drain? How fast, and how much capacitance is on the line? Do I need many channels?

For a typical case, a 3.3 volt MCU talking SPI to a 5 volt part, I'd pick a dual-supply translator IC. It has a supply pin per side, so each input is compared against its own rail's thresholds and each output swings to its own rail. It handles tens of megahertz, and I just check the datasheet for data rate and drive strength.

If it has to be discrete, for a slow signal, I'd build an NMOS inverter. The gate is driven by the low-voltage logic, the source is on ground, and a pull-up resistor goes from the drain to the high rail; the drain is the output. When the input is high the FET conducts and pulls the output low, so it inverts. The only speed limit is the pull-up: the rise time is about 2.2 R C. For 15 picofarads and a 100 nanosecond budget, R is 3 kΩ, which costs 1.7 milliamps whenever the output is low. That trade between speed and static power is why discrete shifters are for slow signals.

For bidirectional open-drain lines like I²C, there's the single-FET shifter with pull-ups on both sides. And on-chip, the standard circuit is a cross-coupled PMOS pair to the high rail with two NMOS pull-downs driven from the low-voltage logic, with no static current.

Finally I'd check the corners: threshold margins over temperature and supply tolerance, power sequencing, and how it behaves when one rail is off."
:::

::: terms
- **Level shifter / translator**: circuit that converts logic levels from one supply domain to another.
- **Dual-supply translator**: IC with $V_{CCA}$ and $V_{CCB}$ pins, one per side.
- **Pull-up resistor**: sets the high level and rise time of an open-drain/NMOS output.
- **$R_{DS(on)}$**: on-resistance of the FET, sets the low-level and fall time.
- **Cross-coupled latch**: positive-feedback pair that snaps to a full-swing state.
- **Static power**: current flowing while the output is held (here $V_H^2/R_p$ when low).
- **Overdrive**: $V_{GS}-V_{th}$, controls drive strength.
- **Direction pin (DIR)**: input that selects A-to-B or B-to-A on a bidirectional translator.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use a level shifter chip" or draws a divider.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Asks about voltages, direction, push-pull or open-drain, speed and load before drawing anything.
- Offers 2 to 3 architectures and states when each wins (IC for speed and channels, discrete for one slow line, FET shifter for open-drain, cross-coupled on-chip).
- Sizes the discrete pull-up from a rise-time budget *and* checks the quiescent current ($R_p=3$ kΩ, 1.7 mA).
- Notes that the discrete NMOS stage inverts and how to handle it.
- Verifies margins at corners and mentions sequencing and contention.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using a resistor divider to shift up.** It only attenuates. Up-shifting needs an active element.
- **Forgetting that the NMOS-with-pull-up stage inverts.** The output is the complement of the input.
- **Choosing the pull-up without a rise-time or current budget.** 100 kΩ saves power but gives a 3.3 µs edge into 15 pF; 220 Ω gives 7 ns but burns 23 mA.
- **Driving the gate from a rail below the FET's threshold.** A 1.2 V gate drive on a FET with $V_{th}$ up to 1.5 V never turns on. Choose a logic-level FET.
- **Using a discrete shifter on a fast bus** (SPI at 20 MHz). The RC edge is too slow; use a translator IC.
- **Ignoring what happens when one supply is off** or the line is idle.
:::

::: deep
## Start with the requirements, not the schematic

A good answer to "design X" begins with questions, because the architecture depends on the answers:

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Which rails ($V_L\to V_H$)? | decides whether a threshold fails and by how much |
| One direction or both? | bidirectional push-pull is hard; open-drain bidirectional is easy |
| Push-pull or open-drain? | open-drain allows the FET shifter; push-pull needs a buffer |
| Frequency / rise-time budget? | sets the pull-up value or whether discrete is possible at all |
| Load capacitance and number of channels? | $C$ in $2.2RC$; channel count favors ICs |
| Power budget? | static current of pull-ups |
| Supply sequencing, hot-plug? | back-powering, defined output when a rail is off |

For the rest of this page I will design for one concrete case: a 3.3 V MCU output at up to 1 MHz driving a 5 V input through about 15 pF, and the reverse direction.

Figure: When IN is high (above Vth) Q1 conducts and the output falls to nearly 0 V through R_DS(on). When IN is low Q1 is off and the output rises toward 5 V through R_p, charging C_L. The edge up is slow (set by R_p), the edge down is fast.

**Why it works.** The NMOS only needs its gate-source voltage above threshold. With the source grounded, $V_{GS}=V_{IN}$, so a 3.3 V high (and even 2 V on a 3.3 V logic family's worst case) is enough for a logic-level FET with $V_{th}\approx1$ V. The drain is free to sit at any voltage up to the FET's $V_{DS}$ rating, so it can be pulled to 5 V, 12 V or higher. This also means the circuit shifts *up* and *down*: pull-up to a lower rail also works.

**Sizing the pull-up.** Two constraints oppose each other:

- Edge speed: $t_r\approx2.2R_pC_L\Rightarrow R_p\le t_r/(2.2C_L)$.
- Static power: $I_{low}=V_H/R_p$, $P_{low}=V_H^2/R_p$.

For 15 pF and a 100 ns edge, $R_p\le3.0$ kΩ, $I_{low}=1.67$ mA, $P_{low}=8.3$ mW. If the signal is low half the time, 4.2 mW average per channel: a 16-bit bus would draw 66 mW. Compare the dynamic power of a CMOS translator at 1 MHz: $CV^2f=15\text{ pF}\cdot25\cdot10^6=0.375$ mW.

| $R_p$ | 10-90% rise (15 pF) | Current when low (5 V) |
|---|---|---|
| 220 Ω | 7.3 ns | 22.7 mA |
| 1 kΩ | 33 ns | 5 mA |
| 3 kΩ | 99 ns | 1.67 mA |
| 10 kΩ | 330 ns | 0.5 mA |
| 100 kΩ | 3.3 µs | 50 µA |

The stage **inverts**. If polarity matters, use two stages (the first stage's output is on the low rail's pull-up, i.e. a normal inverter), accept inversion and fix in firmware (idle-low vs idle-high), or use a P-channel/FET variant. Also check that the output low level matters: $V_{OL}=I_{low}R_{DS(on)}$; with $R_{DS(on)}\approx$ a few ohms, this is a few mV.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Small-signal FETs such as the BSS138 are widely used for this job, but gate threshold, $R_{DS(on)}$ at low $V_{GS}$ and capacitances vary by manufacturer and lot. Use the datasheet's maximum $V_{GS(th)}$ and the $R_{DS(on)}$ at your actual gate drive.

## The IC solution (the default answer)

A dual-supply translator splits into a *receiver* referenced to $V_{CCA}$ and a *driver* referenced to $V_{CCB}$. It has real push-pull outputs, so edge rates are controlled by the driver's strength and your load capacitance, not by a resistor. For the SPI case (10 MHz, 3.3 V to 5 V):

- Rise-time budget: about 10% of a 100 ns bit time, so 10 ns or less.
- Required: $f_{max}$ in the datasheet for the $V_{CCA}$/$V_{CCB}$ pair, output drive strong enough for the load.
- Direction: use a fixed-direction part or a DIR-controlled part per signal group (MOSI/SCK/CS go one way, MISO the other).
- Add 22 to 33 Ω series resistors close to the driver if edges ring.

Auto-direction parts are convenient but they sense the first edge, have limited drive and release time, and tolerate neither strong pull-ups nor large capacitive loads on some families; read the notes before using them with I²C or open-drain buses ([[q0877]]).

Figure: Net names tie the cross-coupling together: A drives MP2's gate and OUT drives MP1's gate. IN and INB (its complement) come from the low-voltage domain.

Operation: with IN high, MN1 turns on and pulls node A low. A low turns MP2 on, which pulls OUT up to $V_{DDH}$, which turns MP1 off, so A stays low with no fighting. Flipping IN reverses everything. Each state has zero static current. The only difficult moment is the transition: when IN rises, MN1 must pull A down *against* the still-on MP1, so MN1 must be stronger than MP1. With square-law transistors, MN1 (gate at $V_L$) sources $\frac{k_n}{2}(V_L-V_{tn})^2$ while MP1 (gate at 0, source at $V_H$) sources $\frac{k_p}{2}(V_H-|V_{tp}|)^2$, so

$$\frac{k_n}{k_p}>\left(\frac{V_H-|V_{tp}|}{V_L-V_{tn}}\right)^2.$$

For $V_L=1.2$ V, $V_H=3.3$ V, $V_{tn}=0.4$ V, $|V_{tp}|=0.7$ V: $(2.6/0.8)^2=10.6$. If $V_L$ drops to 0.8 V the ratio balloons to $(2.6/0.4)^2=42$, which is why very low $V_L$ needs current-mirror or multi-stage shifters. This is a first-order estimate: it ignores that MP1 leaves saturation as A falls, which makes the real requirement somewhat easier.

## Verification checklist

1. **Static levels** at min $V_L$ and max $V_{th}$: does the gate overdrive still give low $R_{DS(on)}$?
2. **Edges** into the real capacitance, including connectors and cables: scope the output, not the input.
3. **Corners**: temperature (thresholds drift about $-2$ mV/°C), supply tolerance, FET lot variation.
4. **Power-down behavior**: if the $V_H$ side is off, the output must not back-power it, and when $V_L$ is off, the input must not float into linear mode.
5. **Contention** on bidirectional lines: make sure only one side can drive.
:::

::: applications
- **MCU to peripheral on a different rail**: SPI/UART lines through a dual-supply translator, for example 3.3 V MCU to a 5 V LED driver or display.
- **Single slow control line** (enable, reset, status LED) shifted with one NMOS and a pull-up.
- **Memory interfaces**: 1.8 V DDR-like or flash parts with 3.3 V hosts need multi-bit translators.
- **On-chip power-domain crossings**: every block that moves signals between core and I/O voltage uses latch-type shifters.
- **Sensor modules** on 5 V headers (Arduino shields) interfacing with 3.3 V or 1.8 V sensors.
:::

::: assumptions
- Signals are single-ended CMOS-like logic; differential or analog interfaces need other solutions.
- First-order MOSFET model: square-law, constant $C_L$, rise time $2.2RC$. Real FETs have Miller capacitance and body effect.
- The discrete circuit assumes the source has enough drive for the FET gate ($V_L>V_{th}$ with margin).
- Translator IC capabilities (data rate, drive, open-drain support) differ by family; the numbers quoted here are for orientation only.
- If the interviewer fixes a topology (for example "with a single FET"), switch to that design: see [[q0879]].
:::

::: followups
### How would you make the discrete NMOS shifter non-inverting?
Add a second inverting stage (another NMOS with its own pull-up, or just a logic inverter) after the first, or put a 74-series buffer at the output. For one control line it is often simpler to invert in firmware. Alternatively, use the single-FET bidirectional shifter ([[q0879]]) which is non-inverting.

### Your NMOS shifter works at 100 kHz but is distorted at 5 MHz. Why, and what do you do?
The rising edge is limited by $R_pC_L$: at 3 kΩ and 15 pF, $t_r=99$ ns, which is half the 5 MHz bit time of 200 ns, hence distortion. Lowering $R_p$ burns quiescent current. Switch to a translator IC (push-pull output), or add a push-pull inverter after the NMOS stage.

### How do you shift 1.8 V logic to 3.3 V and keep it low power?
A dual-supply translator is the easiest. At the transistor level, use the cross-coupled PMOS latch (no static current), sized so the NMOS can overpower the PMOS at the low rail. For very low $V_L$ add a current-mirror stage or use native (low $V_t$) NMOS devices.

### What can go wrong with an auto-direction translator on an SPI line?
It detects direction from the first edge, so a slow or ringing edge can be mis-sensed, heavy capacitive load slows its internal one-shots, and a push-pull driver contending with the translator's hold buffer may trip it. For SPI, use fixed-direction buffers with DIR/OE controlled by CS or by design.

### What if the low-side supply is 0.9 V and the FET threshold is 0.7 V?
Overdrive is only 0.2 V, so $R_{DS(on)}$ is very high and the FET barely conducts: the output won't pull low in time. Choose a lower-threshold device (native FET), use a translator IC designed for sub-1 V, or use a comparator-style input stage.
:::
