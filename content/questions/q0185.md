::: basics
### In plain words
A buck has several signals, and each one has a simple shape. The switch node is a square pulse. The inductor current is a triangle riding on the load current. The output voltage is almost flat. Each shape comes from the one before it, so you can draw them all by reasoning in order.

### Picture it
Think of a bank balance. Deposits and withdrawals arrive as steady blocks: a big deposit for a short time, then a smaller withdrawal for a long time. The running balance is then a triangle, rising and falling. If you keep a running total of that triangle, you get a smooth curve with tiny wiggles. Each waveform in the buck is the running total of the one before it.

### Words you need
- **Switch node**: the point whose voltage swings between the input and about zero.
- **Waveform**: how a voltage or current changes over time.
- **Ripple**: the small up-and-down wiggle on a quantity that is mostly steady.
- **Integral**: the running total of something over time.
- **Duty cycle ($D$)**: how long the switch is on in each cycle ([[q0179]]).

### The simplest example
Use 12 V in, 3.3 V out, 4.7 µH and 500 kHz. The period is 2 µs and $D = 0.275$, so the switch node sits at 12 V for 550 ns and near 0 V for 1.45 µs. While it is high, the inductor sees $12 - 3.3 = 8.7$ V, so the current rises at $8.7/4.7 \approx 1.85$ A per µs, about 1 A over 550 ns. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page lines up every waveform, including the capacitor, input and gate signals.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can hold the whole converter in your head at once and keep every waveform consistent with every other one. The interviewer is checking that:

1. You start from the two states (on and off) and the one rule that ties everything together: the inductor current never jumps.
2. Your waveforms agree: the switch node is the only square wave, the inductor voltage is that square wave minus $V_{out}$, the inductor current is its integral, the capacitor current is the inductor current minus the load, and so on down the chain.
3. You get the *details* right: the diode drop in the off state, the dead time with a synchronous bridge, the bootstrapped gate that swings above $V_{in}$, and the pulsed input current that the input capacitor smooths.
:::

::: why
Every other question in this section (ripple, sizing, efficiency, loss, stability) is read off these pictures. If you can sketch the set quickly and correctly you can answer follow-ups by pointing at the drawing instead of reciting formulas. It is also the best debugging skill in power electronics: when a scope shows a switch node that does not look like waveform number one, you know exactly which of the others to probe. Interviewers like this question because it is impossible to bluff: one wrong sign and the diagram stops adding up.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor law** $v_L = L\,di/dt$: constant voltage gives a constant slope. See [[q0041]].
- **Capacitor law** $i_C = C\,dv/dt$: the output ripple is the integral of the capacitor current. See [[q0025]].
- **Continuity**: inductor current cannot jump and capacitor voltage cannot jump, so those two are the smooth waveforms. See [[q0043]].
- **Volt-second balance**: in steady state the average inductor voltage is zero, which gives $V_{out} = D\,V_{in}$. See [[q0178]].
- **KCL at the output node**: $i_L = i_C + i_{load}$.
- **Two switch states**: on (high-side FET conducts) and off (diode or low-side FET conducts). See [[q0174]] and [[q0175]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd pick a concrete example so the numbers are consistent: 12 V in, 3.3 V out, 2 A, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz. That's a duty cycle of 0.275, so the high-side FET is on for 550 ns and off for 1.45 µs.

The switch node is the starting point. It's 12 V when the high-side FET is on, and about minus one diode drop when it's off, so it's a rectangular pulse whose average is 3.3 V. With a synchronous bridge the off level is basically minus the low-side FET's I times R, near zero, with little negative dips during dead time when the body diode conducts.

The inductor voltage is the switch node minus $V_{out}$: plus 8.7 V on, minus 3.3 V off. Integrate that and you get the inductor current, a triangle: rising at 1.85 A per microsecond, falling at 0.7 A per microsecond, about 1 A peak to peak around the 2 A average.

The capacitor current is the inductor current minus the load current, so the same triangle centred on zero. Integrating that gives a small parabolic ripple, about 12 mV with some ESR. The load sees a nearly flat 3.3 V and 2 A.

On the input side the current is only the high-side FET current: pulses of the inductor current for 550 ns and zero otherwise, average 0.55 A. The input capacitor supplies the pulses. The diode or low-side FET carries the inductor current during the off time, and the gate waveforms are the PWM, with the high-side gate bootstrapped above $V_{in}$."
:::

::: technical
**Operating point** (CCM, ideal parts): $D = V_{out}/V_{in} = 0.275$, $T = 2\,\mu s$, $t_{on} = 550$ ns, $t_{off} = 1.45\,\mu s$.

**Switch node.** On: $V_{SW} = V_{in} - I_L R_{ds(on)} \approx V_{in}$. Off, asynchronous: $V_{SW} = -V_f$ (about −0.3 to −0.5 V for a Schottky). Off, synchronous: $V_{SW} = -I_L R_{ds(on),LS}$ (about −15 to −25 mV for 10 mΩ), with $\approx -0.7$ V steps during the dead times when the low-side body diode carries the current. Average over a period: $D V_{in} = V_{out}$.

**Inductor.** $v_L = V_{in}-V_{out} = 8.7$ V on, $-V_{out} = -3.3$ V off (average zero). Slopes: $+(V_{in}-V_{out})/L = +1.85$ A/µs and $-V_{out}/L = -0.70$ A/µs. $\Delta I_L = \frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})D}{L f_{sw}} = 1.02$ A, so the current runs from 1.49 A to 2.51 A.

**Capacitor.** $i_C = i_L - I_{out}$, peak ±0.51 A, rms $\Delta I/\sqrt{12} = 0.29$ A. Capacitive part $\Delta V_C = \frac{\Delta I_L}{8 f_{sw} C} = 11.6$ mV, ESR part $\Delta I_L \cdot ESR = 5.1$ mV. They are 90° apart, so the combined ripple is about 12.3 mV p-p, not 16.7 mV.

**Currents in the switches.** High-side FET: $i_L$ during $t_{on}$, rms 1.06 A. Diode / low-side FET: $i_L$ during $t_{off}$, average $(1-D)I_{out} = 1.45$ A. Source current with a perfect input capacitor: $D\,I_{out} = 0.55$ A, with $P_{in} = 6.6$ W $=P_{out}$ in the ideal case.

**Gates.** High-side gate-to-source about 5 V while on; with a bootstrap its gate sits at $V_{SW}+5$, i.e. about 17 V above ground during the on time. Low-side gate is complementary with dead time.
:::

:::

::: long
"I always start with numbers so everything stays consistent: 12 V to 3.3 V at 2 A, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz, 22 µF. Duty cycle is 3.3 over 12, 0.275, so on for 550 ns and off for 1.45 µs.

First the switch node. High-side FET on, it's 12 V. High-side off, the inductor current is still flowing, so the diode turns on and the node sits about 0.4 V below ground. Average of that pulse train is 3.3 V, which is exactly why the output is 3.3 V. With a synchronous bridge the low level is the low-side FET's drop, a few tens of millivolts, plus short dips to about minus 0.7 V in the dead times when the body diode conducts.

Inductor voltage is switch node minus output: plus 8.7 V on, minus 3.3 V off. The areas balance: 8.7 times 550 ns equals 3.3 times 1.45 µs, about 4.8 volt-microseconds. The current is the integral, a triangle that ramps up at 1.85 amps per microsecond and down at 0.7, about 1 amp peak to peak, centred on the 2 amp load current.

Capacitor current is inductor current minus load current, so the same triangle centred on zero, plus or minus half an amp. Where it's positive the cap charges, where it's negative it discharges. Integrate it and you get 11.6 mV from the capacitance plus 5 mV from ESR, about 12 mV total.

Input side: the high-side FET passes the inductor current only during the on time, so the input current is a train of 1 to 2.5 amp pulses with a 0.55 amp average. The input capacitor supplies the pulses and the source sees roughly DC. The diode or low-side FET carries the complementary pulses with a 1.45 amp average. Gate-wise it's the PWM, with the high-side gate bootstrapped above 12 V."
:::

::: terms
- **Switch node (SW)**: the node joining the high-side FET, the diode or low-side FET, and the inductor. It is the only truly rectangular waveform.
- **Duty cycle $D$**: fraction of the period the high-side FET is on, $D = V_{out}/V_{in}$ in CCM.
- **CCM**: continuous conduction mode; inductor current stays above zero all cycle.
- **Dead time**: short interval in a synchronous bridge when both FETs are off so they cannot shoot through.
- **Body diode**: parasitic diode of the FET; conducts the inductor current during dead time.
- **Bootstrap**: a diode and capacitor that lift the high-side gate drive above the switch node.
- **Peak-to-peak ripple**: max minus min of a waveform, $\Delta I_L$ or $\Delta V_{out}$.
- **Volt-second balance**: the average inductor voltage is zero in steady state.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** sketches a square wave, a triangle and a flat line, with no numbers and no relationships between the sketches.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws all waveforms on one common time axis, aligned to the same edges, and says out loud which waveform is derived from which (switch node → $v_L$ → $i_L$ → $i_C$ → $v_C$).
- Uses real numbers: levels, slopes, ripple, averages.
- Gets the off-state level right (minus $V_f$ or minus $I R_{ds}$) and mentions dead time and the body diode.
- Notes that the capacitor current changes sign at the inductor-current average, not at the switching edges.
- Points out that the input current is *pulsed* (so the input capacitor is mandatory) and that the output current is smooth.
- Shows the bootstrapped gate swinging to $V_{in}+V_{drive}$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing the inductor current as a square wave or sawtooth.** It is a triangle with *two* slopes because both states apply voltage to the inductor. A sawtooth means one edge is instantaneous, which would need infinite voltage.
- **Putting the capacitor current switching at the FET edges.** $i_C$ is continuous (it is $i_L - I_{out}$). It crosses zero mid-ramp, so the capacitor keeps charging after the high-side FET turns off.
- **Drawing the diode off-state voltage as zero.** It is $-V_{in}$ while Q1 is on and about $+V_f$ while it conducts.
- **Showing the input current as smooth.** At the FET it is pulsed; only after the input capacitor does the source current look like DC.
- **Forgetting the gate level.** An NFET high-side gate must reach about $V_{in}+V_{gs}$ above ground.
- **Drawing $V_{out}$ with a visible sawtooth.** It is millivolts; the picture should be a nearly flat line with a tiny ripple.
:::

## 1. Switch node voltage, asynchronous and synchronous

When Q1 is on, SW is tied to $V_{in}$ through $R_{ds(on)}$: $V_{SW} = V_{in} - I_L R_{ds(on)}$, about 11.97 V for 15 mΩ at 2 A. When Q1 turns off, the inductor current must continue, so SW is pulled down until something conducts to ground. With a diode that is $-V_f$.

Figure: The asynchronous switch node. The high level is $V_{in}$, the low level is a diode drop below ground, and the time-average of the pulse train is $D V_{in} = V_{out}$. The inductor and capacitor are a low-pass filter that keeps only that average.

With a synchronous bridge the low-side FET turns on after a dead time. During dead time both FETs are off and the inductor current flows through the low-side body diode, so SW dips to about $-0.7$ V (a body diode is typically worse than a Schottky). Then the channel conducts and SW sits at $-I_L R_{ds(on)}$, a few tens of millivolts. Just before Q1 turns on, the low-side FET switches off a dead time early and the body diode conducts again.

## 2. Inductor voltage and current

The inductor sees $V_{SW} - V_{out}$. During the on time that is $+8.7$ V and during the off time it is about $-3.3$ V (strictly $-(V_{out}+V_f)$).

## 3. Capacitor current and voltage

KCL at the output node: $i_L = i_C + i_{load}$. The load current is almost constant (the capacitor is big), so $i_C = i_L - I_{out}$.

Real hardware: the ESR term often dominates in electrolytic and tantalum capacitors, while for ceramics the capacitive term dominates.

## 4. Output load voltage and current

The load is almost a resistor (here 1.65 Ω). Its voltage is $V_{out}$, and its current is $V_{out}/R$, the same ripple divided by 1.65 Ω.

## 5. Gate waveforms

The high-side NFET needs $V_{GS}$ of roughly 5 V for a logic-level device. Its source is the switch node, which swings between about 0 and 12 V, so the driver must lift the gate to $V_{SW}+5$ = 17 V during the on time. A bootstrap capacitor, charged from a 5 V rail through a diode while SW is low, provides that supply. In a synchronous buck, the low-side gate is the complement with dead time.

## 7. Input supply voltage and current

The high-side FET is in series with the input. It carries $i_L$ for $t_{on}$ and nothing otherwise, so the current drawn from the input node is a train of pulses with an average of $D I_{out} = 0.55$ A. The input capacitor supplies the difference.

Figure: The low-side NFET carries the inductor current during the off time (red, in amps; it is plotted with the sign that matches the diode current). Its drain-source voltage is the switch node itself (blue), which is why $V_{DS}$ sits at about $-I R_{ds(on)}$ in conduction and $-0.7$ V during dead time.

On the current direction: the NFET conducts from source (ground) to drain (SW). The channel is symmetric, so this is fine and is exactly what synchronous rectification means: the current takes the low-resistance channel instead of the body diode. The numbers: rms low-side current $\sqrt{(1-D)(I_{out}^2+\Delta I^2/12)} = 1.72$ A, which with 10 mΩ dissipates 30 mW, compared with 725 mW for a 0.5 V diode. That is the entire efficiency argument for synchronous rectification.

## A drawing checklist

1. Draw four time axes aligned vertically at the same edges: gate, switch node, inductor current, output ripple. Mark $D\,T$ and $(1-D)T$.
2. Switch node first, then derive the rest from it.
3. Label levels and slopes: $+8.7$ V, $-3.3$ V, $+V_{out}$ in the slope for the fall.
4. Mark that the inductor current's average equals the load current.
5. Draw $i_C$ below $i_L$ with zero at the middle of the ramps and the capacitor voltage minimum and maximum at those zero crossings.

> Draw on a whiteboard in this order: gate, switch node, $v_L$, $i_L$, $i_C$, $v_C$. Say one sentence for each ("the inductor current is the integral of this"). That narration shows you are deriving, not remembering.

::: applications
- **Point-of-load regulators** (12 V to 1.8 V or 3.3 V for FPGAs, DDR, SoCs): this is the waveform set you see on the scope when probing SW, the inductor and the output.
- **USB-C / laptop charger buck stages**: the same picture with higher voltages, dead-time control and larger ripple current.
- **Automotive 12 V to 5 V converters** where the pulsed input current drives the input filter and EMI design.
- **Debugging a board**: SW node on the scope tells you duty cycle, ringing, dead time, and whether you are in CCM or DCM at once.
- **Teaching and simulation** (SPICE and the `buck-lab` sim): the same waveforms are the standard checks that a model is wired correctly.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM and steady state**: the inductor current never reaches zero. At light load (DCM) the inductor current has a flat zero segment and the switch node rings; forced-CCM synchronous controllers let the current go negative instead.
- **Ideal switches** with small drops: the high-side on-state drop and the real switching edges are ignored. Real edges take a few ns to tens of ns and ring.
- **Small ripple**: $V_{out}$ is treated as constant when computing the inductor slopes (the error is about $\Delta V/V_{out} \approx 0.4\%$ here).
- **Constant load current**: a resistive load produces a slight ripple current; a processor-like load does not.
- **Ideal capacitor plus ESR only**: ESL is neglected; with ceramics and fast edges it adds spikes at the edges.
- **Voltage-mode versus current-mode control** only changes how the duty cycle is chosen; the waveforms in steady state are the same.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the inductor current have a different slope on and off?
Because the voltage across the inductor is different. On: $V_{in} - V_{out} = 8.7$ V gives +1.85 A/µs for $L = 4.7$ µH. Off: $-V_{out} = -3.3$ V gives −0.70 A/µs. In steady state the up and down changes are equal, which makes the on time shorter than the off time here.

### Where does the capacitor current cross zero, and what does that mean?
It crosses zero when $i_L = I_{out}$, at the middle of each ramp. That is when the capacitor voltage is at its minimum (going from falling to rising) or maximum. This is a common trap: the capacitor voltage extremes are *not* at the FET switching edges. Here, the minimum is 275 ns into the on time and the maximum is 725 ns into the off time.

### What does the input current look like, and what would the source see without an input capacitor?
Pulses of 1.5 to 2.5 A for 550 ns each period, average 0.55 A. Without $C_{in}$ the source and its wiring inductance have to supply these pulses; a 100 nH lead with a 1.5 A step in 10 ns produces a 15 V transient, which ruins the input rail and the FET's rating. See [[q0193]].

### How do these waveforms change in DCM?
The inductor current drops to zero before the end of the period and stays there. The switch node, no longer clamped, rings around $V_{out}$ (asynchronous). The slopes stay the same, but the average current is lower, so the capacitor ripple has a flat portion and the output voltage no longer follows $D V_{in}$; the duty cycle needed for a given $V_{out}$ is smaller.

### Challenge: you probe SW and see a −3 V spike and a 20 V overshoot. What is it?
Parasitic ringing from the loop inductance (a few nH) resonating with the FET output capacitance, excited by the fast edge, plus body-diode reverse recovery. Check the rating of the FET against $V_{in}$ plus overshoot, shorten the hot loop, add a snubber or gate resistor. Measure with a short ground spring rather than a long ground lead or you will inflate the number.
:::

::: interpretations
"Draw the waveforms" leaves open whether the converter is in CCM or DCM, and whether the rectifier is a diode or a synchronous FET. This page uses CCM with a diode first and then gives the synchronous differences, since the list asks for both. "Diode current/voltage" is understood as the current through the diode and the voltage across it (anode minus cathode). The "FET gate voltage" is shown for the high-side (gate to ground and gate to source) and the low-side in the synchronous case.
:::

::: rubric
- Derives every waveform from the switch node and the component laws :: A coherent chain shows understanding instead of a memorized set of pictures.
- Switch node: $V_{in}$ on, $-V_f$ (or $-I R_{ds}$) off, average $D V_{in}$ :: The off level and the average are where most people slip.
- Inductor: $+(V_{in}-V_{out})$ / $-V_{out}$ and a triangular current with average $I_{out}$ :: The current's average is the load current and its slopes come from the two voltages.
- Capacitor current is $i_L - I_{out}$, zero-centred, and the capacitor charges when $i_L > I_{out}$ :: Explains why the ripple voltage is tiny and where its extremes occur.
- Input current is pulsed (average $D I_{out}$) and smoothed by $C_{in}$ :: Pulsed input current drives the input capacitor and EMI design.
- Diode/low-side carries $i_L$ in the off state with average $(1-D)I_{out}$ :: Ties the picture to loss calculations.
- Synchronous differences: dead time, body diode steps, low-side FET drop :: Shows you know the real bridge, not only the textbook one.
- Gate: bootstrapped high-side above $V_{in}$, complementary low-side :: Shows hardware-level awareness.
:::
