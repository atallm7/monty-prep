::: testing
Whether you can strip an LDO down to its **equivalent circuit**. The expected answer is a **resistor**: a series pass element that drops $V_{in}-V_{out}$ at the load current, i.e. a **variable resistor** with $R_{pass}=(V_{in}-V_{out})/I_{out}$ that the feedback loop continuously adjusts. The interviewer is also testing whether you know what that implies: power loss $=I^2R_{pass}=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$, the efficiency $V_{out}/V_{in}$, and that without the loop it is just a lossy resistor divider. Bonus points for the AC picture: the *closed-loop output impedance* looks **inductive** over a band, which is the other passive component an LDO resembles.
:::

::: why
This mental model makes every LDO result immediate: "it's a resistor that changes value so that the output stays constant, so it dissipates the same power as a resistor would". It tells you why an LDO is a bad idea at big headroom ([[q0280]]), where the heat goes ([[q0282]]) and why efficiency can never exceed $V_{out}/V_{in}$ ([[q0283]]). And the AC model explains why the output capacitor matters and why LDO output impedance rises with frequency, a common source of rail noise.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ohm's law and series resistors**: $V=IR$, and a series resistor drops voltage proportional to current. See [[q0001]].
- **Power in a resistor**: $P=I^2R=V\,I$. See [[q0164]].
- **MOSFET in the triode region**: a voltage-controlled resistor $R_{DS(on)}$. See [[q0100]].
- **Feedback adjusts the resistance**: the loop changes $V_{GS}$ so $R_{pass}$ makes $V_{out}$ constant. See [[q0278]].
- **Output impedance**: how much the output voltage changes per unit load current. For a feedback regulator it is the open-loop resistance divided by $1+T$.
- **Inductor impedance** $Z=j\omega L$ rises with frequency. See [[q0040]].
:::

::: answer
"A resistor, specifically a variable series resistor. The pass transistor sits between the input and the load and drops the excess voltage, so at any operating point it looks like a resistor of $R=(V_{in}-V_{out})/I_{out}$. The feedback loop continuously changes that resistance to hold the output constant: if the load draws more current, the loop lowers the resistance so the drop stays the same size relative to what's needed, and if the input rises, it increases the resistance.

That model gives me the key facts straight away. The power lost is $I^2R$, which is $(V_{in}-V_{out})\times I_{out}$, so it's the same heat as a fixed dropper resistor, but the output doesn't sag with load. The efficiency is $V_{out}/V_{in}$ since the same current flows in and out. And at dropout the loop has run out of range, so the resistor is at its minimum, $R_{DS(on)}$, and the output is just $V_{in}-I\,R_{DS(on)}$.

If someone wants the small-signal picture, the closed-loop output impedance is very low at DC and rises with frequency like an inductor until the loop runs out of gain, which is why the output capacitor takes over."
:::

::: technical
**Large-signal (DC) model.** The pass device carries $I_{out}$ and drops $V_{in}-V_{out}$:
$$R_{pass}=\frac{V_{in}-V_{out}}{I_{out}}$$
This is not a fixed resistance: the loop adjusts $V_{GS}$ so that $R_{pass}$ always has the value that gives $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$. Lower bound: $R_{DS(on)}$ (dropout, [[q0277]]). Upper bound: the device off.

| Case | $V_{in}$ | $I_{out}$ | $R_{pass}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 V to 3.3 V | 5 V | 0.5 A | 3.4 Ω |
| same, light load | 5 V | 0.05 A | 34 Ω |
| 3.5 V to 3.3 V | 3.5 V | 0.5 A | 0.4 Ω (at dropout) |

**Power**: $P=I_{out}^2R_{pass}=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$ and $\eta\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$.

**Input side**: the input current equals the load current, independent of $V_{in}$. For small changes the LDO looks like a **current sink** at the input (high incremental resistance), not a resistor.

**Small-signal output impedance.** With loop gain $T(s)=T_0/(1+s/p_1)$ and open-loop output resistance $R_{OL}$:
$$Z_{out}(s)=\frac{R_{OL}}{1+T(s)}=R_{OL}\frac{1+s/p_1}{1+T_0+s/p_1}$$
For $p_1\ll\omega\ll T_0p_1$, $Z_{out}\approx\dfrac{R_{OL}}{T_0p_1}\,s$, i.e. an inductor $L_{eq}=\dfrac{R_{OL}}{T_0p_1}=\dfrac{R_{OL}}{\omega_c}$ where $\omega_c=T_0p_1$ is the loop's unity-gain frequency. Example: $R_{OL}=10\ \Omega$, $T_0=1000$, $p_1=2\pi\times100$ Hz: $L_{eq}=15.9\ \mu$H. Above the loop bandwidth the impedance flattens at $R_{OL}$ and $C_{out}$ takes over.
:::

::: short
"A variable series resistor. The pass transistor drops $V_{in}-V_{out}$ at the load current, so it looks like $R=(V_{in}-V_{out})/I_{out}$, and the feedback loop keeps adjusting that value to hold the output constant. So the loss is $I^2R=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$ and the efficiency is $V_{out}/V_{in}$. In dropout it bottoms out at $R_{DS(on)}$. At AC, the loop makes the output impedance look inductive over its bandwidth."
:::

::: long
"The passive component is a resistor, a variable one in series with the load.

At DC, the pass transistor carries the full load current and drops $V_{in}-V_{out}$. So at any operating point it's equivalent to a series resistor of $(V_{in}-V_{out})/I_{out}$. For 5 V in, 3.3 V out, 500 mA that's 3.4 Ω; if the load drops to 50 mA the loop raises it to 34 Ω. It's the feedback loop's job to change that value so the output stays at 3.3 V no matter what the load or input does.

The model is a good sanity check. The power dissipated is $I^2R$, which equals $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$: 0.85 W in this example. Since input current equals output current, efficiency is $V_{out}/V_{in}=66\%$. If I'd used a fixed 3.4 Ω dropper resistor instead I'd get the same heat at 500 mA, but the output would sag as the load changes: at 100 mA it would be 4.66 V, not 3.3 V. That's what the loop buys. And when the input falls until the resistance would need to be less than the transistor's $R_{DS(on)}$, you're in dropout and it's just a fixed resistor, so the output follows the input.

If the interviewer means AC small-signal, the closed-loop output impedance is the open-loop output resistance divided by one plus the loop gain. It's tiny at low frequency and rises at 20 dB per decade as the loop gain falls, which looks like an inductor, a few microhenries to tens of microhenries, until the loop bandwidth, where the output capacitor takes over."
:::

::: terms
- **Pass resistance ($R_{pass}$)**: $(V_{in}-V_{out})/I_{out}$, the effective series resistance at an operating point.
- **Variable resistor**: a resistance whose value is set by a control voltage ($V_{GS}$).
- **Triode region**: MOSFET operating mode where it behaves like a resistor.
- **Dropper resistor**: a fixed series resistor used to reduce voltage (unregulated).
- **Output impedance ($Z_{out}$)**: $\Delta V_{out}/\Delta I_{out}$ as a function of frequency.
- **Loop bandwidth ($\omega_c$)**: the frequency where loop gain falls to 1.
- **Equivalent inductance**: $L_{eq}\approx R_{OL}/\omega_c$ of the closed-loop output impedance.
- **Incremental input resistance**: $\Delta V_{in}/\Delta I_{in}$ (very large for an LDO).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a resistor" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Says *variable* resistor, gives $R_{pass}=(V_{in}-V_{out})/I_{out}$ with a number, and explains that feedback adjusts it.
- Derives power ($I^2R$) and efficiency ($V_{out}/V_{in}$) from the model in a line each.
- Contrasts with a **fixed** dropper resistor, showing that the same heat is dissipated but the output no longer sags with load.
- Identifies dropout as $R_{pass}$ hitting its minimum $R_{DS(on)}$.
- Adds the small-signal view: output impedance is resistive/inductive/capacitive across frequency, with $L_{eq}\approx R_{OL}/\omega_c$, and flags that "inductor" is a valid alternative answer for *AC output impedance*.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "a fixed resistor".** A fixed resistor would not regulate. The resistance changes with $V_{in}$ and $I_{out}$.
- **"A diode" or "a zener".** A zener regulator is a different circuit. An LDO is feedback-controlled, not a fixed-voltage clamp.
- **Claiming the LDO input looks like a resistor.** The input current equals the load current regardless of $V_{in}$, so for small signals it is a current sink, not $V/R$.
- **Forgetting dropout.** $R_{pass}$ cannot go below $R_{DS(on)}$.
- **Thinking the loss depends on the output current only.** It depends on $I\times(V_{in}-V_{out})$. At a fixed load, raising $V_{in}$ raises the loss linearly.
- **Treating the inductive output impedance as a real inductor.** It is an effective impedance from feedback and has no stored magnetic energy. It disappears above the loop bandwidth, and at very low frequency it is just a few milliohms.
:::

## Regulated vs fixed resistor

Take 5 V in and a 0.5 A nominal load at 3.3 V. A fixed dropper resistor would be $R=1.7/0.5=3.4\ \Omega$. Now vary the load:

```
Figure: Both circuits waste the same power at the 0.5 A design point (0.85 W). The fixed resistor lets $V_{out}$ climb to 5 V at no load and sag to 1.6 V at 1 A. The LDO holds 3.3 V by changing $R_{pass}$ from huge (no load) down to 3.4 Ω (0.5 A) and 1.7 Ω (1 A), until it reaches $R_{DS(on)}$ at $I=(5-3.3)/0.4=4.25$ A. (At 1 A the LDO burns $1.7\times1=1.7$ W, while the fixed resistor, now dropping 3.4 V, would burn 3.4 W.)

The required $R_{pass}(I)=1.7/I$ is a hyperbola: 17 Ω at 100 mA, 3.4 Ω at 500 mA, 1.7 Ω at 1 A, and 0.4 Ω at the dropout limit. The device must change by a factor of 10 across a 10:1 load range, and by a factor of about 40 going from 17 Ω at 100 mA down to the 0.4 Ω floor, which is why LDOs use large pass devices and high loop gain.

## What the model predicts

1. **Power loss** $=I^2R_{pass}=(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$. At 0.5 A: $0.25\times3.4=0.85$ W. See [[q0282]] for the thermal consequence.
2. **Efficiency** $=V_{out}/(V_{out}+IR_{pass})=V_{out}/V_{in}$ (66% here) ([[q0283]]).
3. **Dropout**: the loop wants $R_{pass}$ below $R_{DS(on)}$ and cannot get there, so $V_{out}=V_{in}-IR_{DS(on)}$.
4. **No gain from the resistor itself**: unlike a transformer or switching converter, the *current* is unchanged, only the voltage differs. A switching converter exploits the extra freedom to trade voltage for current.
5. **Line rejection**: raising $V_{in}$ by 1 V makes the loop raise $R_{pass}$ so the output stays put: $\Delta R=1/I$.

## The small-signal view: why "inductor" shows up

The closed-loop output impedance of any regulator whose loop gain falls with frequency is $Z_{out}=R_{OL}/(1+T(s))$. For a single-pole loop this is a zero at the loop's dominant pole and a pole at $T_0p_1$. Between them the impedance rises 20 dB/decade, the signature of an inductance:

```
Figure: With the loop closed, the output impedance is about 10 mΩ (−40 dB re 1 Ω) at DC and rises at +20 dB/decade from 100 Hz up to about 100 kHz, where it reaches the open-loop 10 Ω (+20 dB). Over that band it behaves like $L_{eq}=R_{OL}/(2\pi f_c)=10/(2\pi\times10^5)=15.9\ \mu$H. At 10 kHz it is about 1 Ω.

Consequence: a fast load step at 10 to 100 kHz sees ohms of impedance, not milliohms, so the output capacitor and its ESR matter. And the LDO with $C_{out}$ forms a series $L_{eq}$-$C_{out}$ combination with a resonance (for example $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{15.9\ \mu\text{H}\times1\ \mu\text{F}})\approx40$ kHz), which is the origin of the peaking in many LDO output-impedance plots.

> [!NOTE]
> "Inductor" is the right answer to a *different* question ("what does the closed-loop output impedance look like?"). The generally expected answer for "model an LDO as a single passive component" is **a resistor**. Give the resistor first and mention the inductor as the AC refinement.

## The input side: not a resistor

If you wiggle $V_{in}$ by 100 mV, the input current of a regulating LDO does not change (it equals the load current). The incremental input resistance is therefore essentially infinite, which is why an LDO draws constant current and why an LDO downstream of a switcher does not load it dynamically. A resistor of 3.4 Ω in series with the load would instead draw more current when $V_{in}$ rises. (Switching converters with tight regulation, by contrast, show a *negative* incremental input resistance: input current falls when $V_{in}$ rises, because power is constant. That is a stability concern for cascaded converters.)

## Worked example: the resistor under line and load variation

Hold the load at 0.5 A and let the input vary from 4.5 V to 5.5 V, as a loosely regulated 5 V rail might. The resistor model says what the loop has to do:

| $V_{in}$ | Required $R_{pass}=(V_{in}-3.3)/0.5$ | Pass-device power | A fixed 3.4 Ω dropper would give |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4.5 V | 2.4 Ω | 0.60 W | $V_{out}=4.5-1.7=2.8$ V |
| 5.0 V | 3.4 Ω | 0.85 W | 3.3 V |
| 5.5 V | 4.4 Ω | 1.10 W | $V_{out}=5.5-1.7=3.8$ V |

The LDO holds 3.3 V throughout by sweeping $R_{pass}$ over a 2:1 range, and the heat in the device follows the input. Note that the worst case for the thermal design is high line, not nominal: 1.1 W against 0.85 W. With a junction-to-ambient resistance of 60 °C/W (the value used by the simulator below), 0.85 W is a 51 °C rise and 1.1 W is 66 °C. At 70 °C ambient that is 121 °C at nominal and 136 °C at high line, which can exceed a 125 °C junction limit. The resistor view makes this immediate, which is why it is the right first model.

## Worked example: the loop gain is what makes the resistor adjust

How well the "resistor" tracks the input depends on loop gain $T(f)$. The line-to-output coupling of the regulator is the open-loop coupling divided by $|1+T|$. Use the page's single-pole model, with $T_0=1000$ and a dominant pole at 100 Hz, so $T(f)=1000/(1+jf/100)$:

| Frequency | $\lvert T\rvert$ | Rejection $1/\lvert1+T\rvert$ |
|---|---|---|
| 10 Hz | 995 | 60 dB |
| 1 kHz | 99.5 | 40 dB |
| 10 kHz | 10 | 20 dB |
| 20 kHz | 5 | 14 dB |
| 100 kHz | 1 | 3 dB |

This explains the scenario in the follow-ups. The datasheet's DC rejection is 60 dB, but at 20 kHz the loop has only 14 dB of rejection to offer, so the 40 mV of output ripple means the input has about $40\ \text{mV}\times5.1\approx200$ mV of ripple at that frequency (after the output capacitor's own filtering, if any). The resistor stopped adjusting fast enough. Between the unity-gain frequency and the point where the output capacitor takes over, the output follows the input with only a few dB of attenuation. A fixed resistor would have shown 0 dB at all frequencies, so the loop gain is exactly the improvement over a fixed dropper.

**A load step.** The same loop bandwidth limits the load response. For a step of $\Delta I=0.4$ A (0.1 A to 0.5 A), the output capacitor supplies the difference for about one loop time constant $1/(2\pi f_c)=1.6\ \mu$s with $f_c=100$ kHz, so $\Delta V\approx\Delta I/(2\pi f_cC)$. With $C_{out}=10\ \mu$F that is 64 mV. With 1 µF it would be 640 mV, which is why the datasheet's minimum capacitance and ESR range matter and why a "tiny cap" LDO needs a fast loop. This is the same statement as "the output impedance looks like $L_{eq}=15.9\ \mu$H": a step of 0.4 A into an inductive source develops a voltage until the capacitor catches up.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The single-pole loop with $T_0=1000$ and a 100 kHz crossover is a textbook idealization. Real LDO loops have a second pole from the output capacitor and pass-device capacitance, and the datasheet PSRR curve already contains their effect. Use the datasheet plot for a design.

## Where the resistor picture stops being right

The model ignores several things that matter in hardware. The pass device has finite gain and its resistance has a minimum ($R_{DS(on)}$) and a maximum (it cannot be infinite: leakage and the feedback-divider current set a floor on the current). The control loop and the pass device add their own poles. The pass element draws a ground-pin current (base current for a PNP, bias for the amplifier), so the input current is not exactly the output current. And the heat of the "resistor" is not uniform: the device temperature changes its resistance, so thermal feedback exists inside the pass transistor, although the loop absorbs it. In addition, for a PMOS pass device the resistance range changes with the gate drive available: near dropout $V_{GS}$ is limited by $V_{in}$, which is why dropout is higher at low input voltages for some parts.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Check the resistor.** Measure $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$ and $I_{out}$ at several loads and compute $R_{pass}=(V_{in}-V_{out})/I_{out}$. It should follow $1.7/I$ in the regulating region and flatten at the dropout resistance near the knee.
2. **Measure line rejection.** Inject a 100 mV sine into the input through a bias tee or a series-connected function generator, sweep from 100 Hz to 1 MHz and measure the output with an AC-coupled scope. The ratio should follow the table's shape, with its notch and peaking set by $C_{out}$.
3. **Step the load.** Use a pulsed electronic load from 0.1 A to 0.5 A with a fast edge and compare the undershoot with $\Delta I/(2\pi f_cC)$. If it is much larger, the capacitor is out of the datasheet's stable range or the loop is slower than assumed.
4. **Check the heat.** At high line and full load, measure the case temperature with a thermocouple. The junction estimate from the resistor model should be close.

::: applications
- **Quick thermal estimate**: treat the LDO as a resistor dissipating $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$, then multiply by $\theta_{JA}$ ([[q0282]]).
- **Choosing LDO vs dropper resistor**: for a tiny, constant load (a status LED or bias current) a fixed resistor is cheaper, but an LDO regulates.
- **Power-budget spreadsheets**: model each LDO as a rail with $I_{in}=I_{out}+I_Q$, which also equals the resistor model's current.
- **Noise and PSRR modeling**: the output-impedance model (resistor then inductor then capacitor) explains why decoupling capacitors close to the load are needed for fast steps.
- **Hot-swap and reverse current**: a PMOS pass device's body diode can conduct backward if $V_{out}>V_{in}$, so reverse-current protection is added where needed.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Large-signal answer**: the *variable resistor* model assumes the loop is regulating ($V_{in}>V_{out}+V_{DO}$). At dropout it is a fixed $R_{DS(on)}$.
- I treat "single passive component" as **resistor** (the standard answer). An interviewer thinking of AC output impedance may want **inductor**; I include both, flagged.
- Loop numbers ($R_{OL}=10\ \Omega$, $T_0=1000$, $p_1=100$ Hz) are illustrative values to demonstrate the shape, not a specific part.
- Ignores quiescent current. Including $I_Q$, input current is $I_{out}+I_Q$.
- A **zener/shunt** regulator is modeled differently (as a clamp), and a **buck** converter is not a passive-equivalent at all (it is closer to a "DC transformer").
:::

::: followups
### What would the equivalent be for a buck converter?
A buck has no good single-passive model: at DC it behaves like an ideal **DC transformer** with turns ratio $D$, changing voltage and current inversely and (ideally) dissipating nothing. That is exactly why it is more efficient than a resistor-like LDO at a large step-down.

### If an LDO is just a resistor, why not use a fixed resistor?
Because the voltage across a fixed resistor changes with the load. The loop's whole purpose is to change the "resistance" with $I_{load}$ and $V_{in}$ so the output voltage stays constant. A fixed resistor gives a "load line" while the LDO gives a flat line until dropout.

### What is the resistance at no load?
Very large. At zero external load the pass device only needs to supply the feedback-divider current (and any internal load), typically microamps to tens of microamps, so the device is nearly off: $R_{pass}=1.7\text{ V}/80\ \mu\text{A}\approx21$ kΩ for a 41 kΩ divider at 3.3 V, and megohms for a high-value divider. That is why LDOs without a minimum load can float up slightly, and why some parts have a minimum-load spec.

### How does the equivalent resistance change if input voltage doubles?
At constant load, $R_{pass}=(V_{in}-V_{out})/I$. From 5 V to 10 V at 3.3 V and 0.5 A: $R$ goes from 3.4 Ω to 13.4 Ω, and power from 0.85 W to 3.35 W. The efficiency drops from 66% to 33%. The same result you would get for a fixed resistor changing the input.

### Scenario: you measure 40 mV of output ripple at 20 kHz on an LDO output fed from a ripple-y input. The LDO datasheet shows 70 dB PSRR at DC. Why is the ripple larger than expected?
The loop gain has already rolled off by 20 kHz. PSRR is high at DC because $T_0$ is large, but at 20 kHz the loop (bandwidth maybe 20 to 100 kHz) rejects only a few tens of dB or less. At that frequency the LDO output impedance looks inductive and the output capacitor is doing the filtering. Read the PSRR *vs frequency* curve at your load and headroom, add output capacitance or a ferrite/RC pre-filter.
:::

::: interpretations
"An LDO can often be modeled as a single passive component, what is this passive component?" most plausibly asks for the **resistor** in series with the load. Some engineers answer **inductor** based on the closed-loop output impedance (rising with frequency). I give the resistor as the main answer and the inductor as the AC refinement; I do not think a **capacitor** is intended (that is the external $C_{out}$, not the regulator).
:::
