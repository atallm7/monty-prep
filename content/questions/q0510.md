::: testing
Whether you can itemise **where the inductance comes from** along the whole current loop and estimate each piece. The interviewer wants:

1. A list that goes beyond "ESL": capacitor body, pads, fan-out traces, vias, plane spreading, package leads or balls and bond wires.
2. Rough numbers for each, so you know which dominates.
3. The idea that these add in series around one loop, and that mutual inductance can reduce them.
4. The design lever attached to each source.
:::

::: why
You can't reduce an inductance you can't find. On real boards the capacitor's own ESL is often the *smaller* part of the loop: vias and fan-out can double or triple it. An engineer who can walk the loop and say "0.4 nH here, 0.5 there, plane spreading 0.3, package 1 nH" can decide where layout effort pays off and can read a PDN simulation sensibly.
:::

::: concepts
- **Partial inductance**: each conductor segment has an inductance that adds in series around the loop ([[q0509]]).
- **Loop inductance**: total of all segments minus mutual coupling.
- **Capacitor ESL**: body and termination geometry ([[q0030]]).
- **Via inductance**: scales with length; formula in the deep section ([[q0503]]).
- **Plane spreading inductance**: current spreading between vias in a plane pair ([[q0486]]).
- **Package inductance**: bond wires, lead frames, balls ([[q0511]]).
:::

::: answer
"I walk around the whole current loop and list every piece. Starting at the capacitor: its own ESL from the body and end terminations, about half a nanohenry for an 0402 and more for bigger cases. Then the pads and any fan-out trace between the pad and the via, roughly half a nanohenry per millimetre or less over a close plane. Then the vias, power and ground, each about a nanohenry if they go through a 1.6 millimetre board and a tenth of that if the plane is 0.2 millimetres below. Then the planes: the current spreads between the two vias, which adds a few tenths of a nanohenry, more if they're far apart or the planes are widely spaced. Then, at the chip, the package: bond wires and leads, one to several nanohenries on a QFP, less on a BGA. And the die's own routing.

All of these add in series around the loop, partly offset by mutual inductance where power and ground currents run side by side in opposite directions. The board-level ones are mine to control: shorter fan-out, vias at the pads, planes close to the surface, power and ground vias adjacent, and more capacitors in parallel. The package part I can only choose by picking the package or by using on-package capacitors."
:::

::: technical
**Loop budget.** $L_{loop}=ESL+L_{pad/trace}+L_{via,pwr}+L_{via,gnd}+L_{plane}+L_{pkg}-2M$.

**Typical contributions (orders of magnitude, geometry dependent):**

| Source | Typical value | Lever |
|---|---|---|
| Capacitor ESL: 0201 | 0.2–0.4 nH | smaller case |
| 0402 | 0.4–0.6 nH | |
| 0603 | 0.6–0.8 nH | |
| 0805 / 1206 | 0.9–1.5 nH | |
| Reverse-geometry (0306 etc.) | 0.15–0.3 nH | low-ESL parts |
| Pad + trace (per mm, unreferenced) | 0.5–1 nH/mm | via at pad |
| Pad + trace (close plane) | 0.2–0.4 nH/mm | |
| Via, 0.3 mm drill, 0.2 mm long | ≈ 0.08 nH | plane near surface |
| Via, 0.8 mm | ≈ 0.54 nH | |
| Via, 1.6 mm | ≈ 1.3 nH | |
| Plane spreading between vias | ≈ 0.1–0.5 nH | close vias, thin dielectric |
| Package (QFP leads) | ≈ 3–8 nH | choose package; vendor |
| Package (QFN, BGA) | ≈ 0.3–2 nH | vendor |
| Bond wire (1 mm) | ≈ 1 nH | flip-chip |

**Via formula.** $L_{via}\approx5.08\,h\,[\ln(4h/d)+1]$ nH, $h$ = length and $d$ = diameter in inches (partial inductance, estimate).

**Plane spreading.** For two vias separated by $s$ in a plane pair with spacing $h$ and via radius $r$: $L\approx\dfrac{\mu_0 h}{\pi}\ln\dfrac{s}{r}$. With $h=0.2$ mm, $s=5$ mm, $r=0.15$ mm: $\approx0.28$ nH. Halving $h$ halves it.

**Mutual inductance.** Adjacent conductors carrying opposing currents reduce each other's net inductance; effective $L_{pair}=2(L-M)$, with $M$ approaching $L$ for very close conductors. Placing power and ground vias side by side exploits it.

**Example loop (0402, poor vs good):**
- Poor: ESL 0.5 + trace 2×2 mm×0.7 = 2.8 + vias 2×1.3 = 2.6 + plane 0.5 = 6.4 nH.
- Good: ESL 0.5 + pad 0.2 + vias 2×0.08 = 0.16 + plane 0.15 = 1.0 nH.
:::

::: short
"The inductance comes from every piece of the current loop: the capacitor's own ESL, the pads and fan-out trace, the power and ground vias, current spreading in the planes, and the IC package's bond wires, leads or balls. They add in series, a bit offset by mutual inductance when power and ground run side by side. On a typical board the vias and fan-out often outweigh the capacitor, so I put vias at the pads, keep planes near the surface, and use parallel capacitors."
:::

::: long
"I look at it as a loop, from the capacitor through the chip and back, and every segment has inductance that adds in series.

At the capacitor, the ESL from its body and terminations: about 0.2 to 0.4 nanohenries for an 0201, 0.4 to 0.6 for an 0402, and up to 1 or more for big cases. Then the pads, and any trace between pad and via: roughly half to one nanohenry per millimetre if there's no close return, a third of that over a plane. Then the vias, which are bigger than people expect: a 0.3 millimetre via through a 1.6 millimetre board is about 1.3 nanohenries, and I need two, power and ground. If the plane is only 0.2 millimetres below the surface, each is about 0.08.

Then the planes. The current has to spread through the power and ground pair between the two vias, which adds a few tenths of a nanohenry, scaling with the plane spacing and the log of via separation. Then the chip's package: bond wires and leads add from a nanohenry up to several on a QFP, a lot less for a BGA or flip-chip, and then the die's own routing.

They all add in series, minus mutual inductance where the power and ground currents run next to each other in opposite directions, which is why I put the two vias side by side. A poor layout of an 0402 can easily total six nanohenries, mostly vias and traces, while a good one is around one. So the levers are: vias at the pads, planes near the surface, power and ground vias adjacent, small capacitor packages or low-ESL types, and more parts in parallel to divide the total. The package part I can only influence by choosing the part."
:::

::: terms
- **Partial inductance**: inductance attributed to one segment of a loop.
- **Fan-out**: the trace from a pad to its via.
- **Plane spreading inductance**: inductance of current spreading through a plane pair.
- **Bond wire**: thin wire from die pad to lead frame, about 1 nH/mm.
- **Reverse-geometry capacitor**: MLCC whose long side carries the terminations, giving a short current path and low ESL.
- **Mutual inductance**: magnetic coupling between nearby current paths.
- **Via aspect/length**: via length drives its inductance.
- **Loop inductance**: net inductance of the full current path.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "the capacitor's ESL and the traces". An exceptional one:

- Walks the whole loop (capacitor, pads, fan-out, vias, plane spreading, package, die) and gives a number for each.
- Notes that **vias and fan-out often exceed the capacitor's ESL** and quotes via inductance versus length (0.08 nH at 0.2 mm, 1.3 nH at 1.6 mm).
- Mentions **plane spreading** with its dependence on dielectric thickness and via spacing.
- Explains **mutual inductance** and why power and ground vias go side by side.
- Maps each source to a design lever, and separates what the PCB designer controls from what the package chooses.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Quoting only the capacitor datasheet's ESL.** Mounting typically adds as much or more.
- **Ignoring the ground via.** The return half of the loop is as long as the power half.
- **Treating vias as "free".** A via through a thick board costs more than the capacitor body.
- **Forgetting plane spreading**, especially with thick dielectric between power and ground.
- **Assuming package inductance can be fixed in layout.** It's fixed by the chosen package; only on-package capacitors help.
:::

::: deep
## Walk the loop

When the chip draws a burst, current flows out of the capacitor, along the pad and fan-out, down the power via, through the power plane to the chip's power via, up to the package, through the die, back down a ground ball or lead, through the ground plane and via, and up to the capacitor's ground pad.

## Source 1: the capacitor itself

ESL comes from the current path through the electrode stack and terminations. Longer and wider bodies have longer paths and more loop area. Typical MLCC ESL by case size (vendor and series dependent): 0201 0.2-0.4 nH, 0402 0.4-0.6 nH, 0603 0.6-0.8 nH, 0805 about 1 nH, 1206 and above 1.2-1.5 nH. Reverse-geometry parts (the long dimension is across the terminals) and multi-terminal arrays cut ESL to 0.15-0.3 nH. Electrolytic and tantalum parts reach several nH to tens of nH due to leads and internal structure.

## Source 2: pads and fan-out

The current has to reach the via. A 0.5 mm pad plus a 1 mm fan-out trace over a close plane adds about 0.3-0.5 nH; with no nearby return it can be 1 nH per mm. Solder fillets and pad size add small amounts. Via-in-pad (filled and capped) removes the fan-out altogether.

## Source 3: vias

A via is a short, thin vertical conductor with partial inductance that scales with length. Using the standard estimate

$$L_{via}\approx5.08\,h\left[\ln\frac{4h}{d}+1\right]\ \text{nH}\quad(h,d\text{ in inches})$$

| Via length to plane | 0.3 mm drill | 0.2 mm drill |
|---|---|---|
| 0.2 mm | 0.08 nH | 0.10 nH |
| 0.5 mm | 0.29 nH | 0.33 nH |
| 0.8 mm | 0.54 nH | 0.60 nH |
| 1.6 mm | 1.30 nH | 1.43 nH |

Smaller drills raise inductance only slightly (log dependence); *length* is what matters. Placing the power and ground planes as the second and third layers, close to the capacitor layer, is the single best structural choice. Parallel vias (two per pad) halve the contribution, with a slight loss from mutual coupling.

## Source 4: plane spreading

Between the capacitor's vias and the chip's vias, current spreads out in the power/ground plane pair. For two vias spaced $s$ apart in planes separated by $h$ with via radius $r$:

$$L_{plane}\approx\frac{\mu_0 h}{\pi}\ln\frac{s}{r}$$

For $h=0.2$ mm, $s=5$ mm, $r=0.15$ mm this is about 0.28 nH. It grows with the log of spacing and linearly with $h$. Thin power/ground dielectrics (0.05-0.1 mm) cut this by half or more and add plane capacitance. A formula like this is an approximation; field solvers do better.

## Source 5: the IC package and die

Bond wires are about 1 nH per millimetre; QFP leads add several nH in total on each supply pin; QFN and BGA reduce it, flip-chip bumps more. These values (vendor- and package-dependent) set a floor you cannot lower in layout. On-package or on-die capacitors sit inside this inductance and serve the very fastest current.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> All the numbers above are typical engineering estimates. Package and capacitor values vary by vendor and series; plane spreading depends on via pattern. Use vendor models or a field solver for sign-off.

## Putting a total together

| Piece | Poor 0402 mounting | Good 0402 mounting |
|---|---|---|
| Capacitor ESL | 0.5 nH | 0.5 nH |
| Pads and fan-out | 2 x 2 mm x 0.7 nH/mm = 2.8 nH | 0.2 nH |
| Two vias | 2 x 1.3 nH = 2.6 nH (1.6 mm deep) | 2 x 0.08 nH = 0.16 nH |
| Plane spreading | 0.5 nH | 0.15 nH |
| **Total** | **6.4 nH** | **about 1.0 nH** |

Resonance of 100 nF: 6.3 MHz (poor) vs 15.9 MHz (good). Impedance at 100 MHz: 4.0 Ω vs 0.63 Ω. The capacitor's own ESL is under 10 % of the poor total; layout contributes the rest.

## How to reduce each

| Source | Fix |
|---|---|
| Capacitor ESL | smaller case, reverse-geometry or array parts, more parts in parallel |
| Pads and fan-out | via in pad or adjacent to pad, wide short traces |
| Vias | planes near the surface, two vias per pad, short stubs |
| Mutual inductance | power and ground vias side by side, alternate polarity |
| Plane spreading | thin power/ground dielectric, vias close to the chip's vias |
| Package | choose lower-inductance package, on-package capacitors |

::: applications
- **PDN design for processors**: modelling capacitor, mounting, plane and package inductances to meet a target impedance.
- **Layout reviews**: spotting long fan-outs, deep vias and far-apart power/ground vias.
- **Choosing capacitor packages**: using reverse-geometry or array capacitors for the highest tier.
- **Package selection**: BGA vs QFP, based on supply-pin inductance.
- **EMC debugging**: tracing a resonance to a specific via or fan-out.
:::

::: assumptions
- Values are typical for common MLCCs and FR-4 boards; exact numbers depend on stackup and geometry.
- Partial inductance is additive in a series loop; mutual terms are approximated.
- The via formula is a standard estimate; plane spreading uses a simple two-via model.
- The package contribution is vendor- and package-dependent and is quoted as a range.
- Another valid answer groups the sources as "capacitor, connection, planes, package" which is the same list.

::: followups
### Which source usually dominates on a 4-layer board?
The vias, if the power plane is on layer 3 of a 1.6 mm board: two vias at about 1 nH each outweigh the 0402's 0.5 nH ESL. With planes on layers 2 and 3 close to the top, the capacitor ESL and the fan-out become the larger pieces.

### How does via length affect inductance?
Approximately linearly with a weak logarithmic dependence on diameter. 0.2 mm gives 0.08 nH, 0.8 mm gives 0.54 nH, 1.6 mm gives 1.3 nH for a 0.3 mm drill. Halving the length roughly halves L.

### Why place the power and ground vias side by side?
The currents are opposite, so their magnetic fields partly cancel. Mutual inductance $M$ reduces the pair's effective inductance from $2L$ toward $2(L-M)$. Spacing them far apart loses the benefit and enlarges the loop.

### Can you reduce the package inductance in layout?
No. You can only choose a package with lower pin inductance, or add capacitance inside it. In layout you can make sure *your* part of the loop is small so the package is the only limiting term.

### Scenario: a measured SRF of 100 nF is 7 MHz. Where is the inductance?
$L=1/((2\pi\times7\text{ MHz})^2\times100\text{ nF})=5.2$ nH. The part contributes about 0.5 nH, so about 4.7 nH is mounting. I'd check via length to the planes, fan-out trace length, and the spacing of the power and ground vias, starting with the deepest vias.
:::
