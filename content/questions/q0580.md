::: basics
### In plain words
Most vias fail from heat cycling. The board's resin, the plastic that holds it together, expands along its thickness much more than copper does. Each time the board heats, the resin stretches the copper tube in the via, and after enough cycles it cracks. That gives an intermittent open circuit, a break that comes and goes, which often shows up only when warm. Other causes are manufacturing flaws, copper filaments growing between close vias, and too much current.

### Picture it
Bend a paper clip back and forth. It does not break the first time, but each bend leaves a little damage, and after enough bends it snaps. The via's copper barrel is the paper clip, and each heating cycle is a bend. Bigger stretches break it in fewer cycles.

### Words you need
- **Barrel**: the copper-plated wall of the via, a thin tube.
- **Thermal cycling**: repeated heating and cooling, from use or from soldering.
- **Expansion mismatch**: resin grows faster with heat than copper, so they pull against each other.
- **Fatigue**: damage that builds up from repeated stretching until something breaks.
- **CAF**: conductive anodic filament, a copper filament that grows inside the board between nearby vias in humid conditions, causing leakage.

### The simplest example
Say the resin expands 60 millionths per °C along its thickness and copper expands 17. The difference is 43 millionths per °C. Heat the board from 25 to 125 °C, which is 100 °C. The mismatch is 43 × 100 = 4,300 millionths, about 0.43% stretch on the copper every cycle. A lead-free soldering pass to 260 °C is far worse, about 2.8%, because the resin softens and expands several times faster at high temperature. A cracked via can still pass a room-temperature continuity test.

That is the whole idea: heat cycling slowly fatigues the barrel. The rest of this page covers each failure mode and its prevention.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can reason about a via as a **mechanical and chemical structure**, not just a wire. A good answer groups the failures by cause, says how each one shows up electrically, and says how you would prevent or detect it. You should cover:

1. **Thermal-mechanical**: barrel cracking from Z-axis expansion mismatch.
2. **Manufacturing defects**: plating voids, thin plating, smear and interconnect separation, drill breakout.
3. **Electrochemical**: conductive anodic filament (CAF).
4. **Assembly-related**: solder wicking, via-in-pad voids.
5. **Electrical overstress**: too much current in too few vias.
:::

::: why
Vias are the most common site of **latent** board failures: a board can pass electrical test at the fab, survive assembly and then fail after months of thermal cycling or humidity. Because they are inside the board, you can't see them. Choosing via size, spacing, aspect ratio, material and the number of vias is partly a reliability decision. Interviewers ask this to see whether you know that "it connected on the bench" isn't the same as "it will keep connecting for ten years", and whether you can connect symptoms (an intermittent open that appears when warm) to physical mechanisms.
:::

::: concepts
- **Via anatomy**: barrel, pad, annular ring, antipad. See [[q0574]].
- **CTE (coefficient of thermal expansion)**: how much a material grows per degree. FR-4 grows far more in the thickness direction than copper does.
- **Glass transition temperature ($T_g$)**: above it, the resin expands several times faster.
- **Aspect ratio**: deep narrow holes plate poorly. See [[q0576]].
- **CAF**: copper migration along glass fibres under humidity and bias.
- **Current density and Joule heating**: $P=I^2R$ in a thin copper tube.
- **Via types and fill**: how the via is closed affects trapped chemistry and wicking. See [[q0578]] and [[q0579]].
:::

::: answer
"The big one is barrel cracking from thermal cycling. FR-4 expands a lot along the thickness of the board, roughly 50 to 70 parts per million per degree below its glass transition and several times more above it, and copper only expands about 17. So every time the board heats, the resin stretches the copper barrel in the Z direction. After enough cycles, or after a few 260 degree lead-free reflows, the barrel fatigues and cracks, usually at the corner where the barrel meets the pad or in the middle of the barrel. It shows up as an intermittent open that appears when warm.

Next are manufacturing defects: voids or thin spots in the plating, usually in deep narrow holes with a high aspect ratio, poor desmear so there's resin smear between the inner-layer pad and the barrel, and drill wander that breaks out of the annular ring.

Then there's CAF, conductive anodic filament: in humid conditions with a voltage between two close vias, copper can grow along the glass fibres and create a leakage path or short. Closer hole spacing makes it worse.

At assembly, vias in pads can wick solder and cause voids or opens. And electrically, too much current through too few vias will overheat or fuse them. I prevent these with lower aspect ratio, enough plating thickness, adequate via spacing, high-Tg material, more vias in parallel, and filled vias where wicking matters."
:::

::: technical
**1. Barrel and corner cracking (thermal fatigue).**
Typical FR-4 has $\alpha_z$ roughly 50–70 ppm/°C below $T_g$ and 200–300 ppm/°C above; copper is about 17 ppm/°C. When temperature rises by $\Delta T$ the board stretches the barrel by roughly $(\alpha_z-\alpha_{Cu})\Delta T$ in strain. With $\alpha_z=60$, $T_g=170$ °C and $\alpha_{z,above}=260$ ppm/°C:
- 25 to 125 °C: $(60-17)\cdot100=4300$ ppm = 0.43 %.
- 25 to 260 °C (lead-free reflow): about 2.8 % strain.
Copper is ductile, but cyclic strain of this size fatigues it. The weak points are **barrel corners** (the knee where the plating turns onto the pad) and thin or voided spots. Fatigue life falls sharply with strain amplitude, with larger aspect ratio, thinner plating, lower $T_g$ materials and thicker boards.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The CTE values are typical, not for a specific laminate. Use the laminate datasheet's $T_g$, $\alpha_z$ (below and above $T_g$) and z-axis expansion percentage from 50 to 260 °C.

**2. Plating defects.** Voids, thin plating or "dog-boning" (thicker at the ends of the barrel than the middle) from poor solution flow, especially above 8:1–10:1 aspect ratio. Weak points that crack first.

**3. Interconnect defects (ICD) and post separation.** Resin smear left after drilling, or contamination, prevents a clean metallurgical bond between the barrel and the inner-layer pad. The joint can separate under stress, giving an intermittent open.

**4. Drill breakout and annular-ring failures.** Drill wander or layer misregistration reduces the annular ring until the hole breaks out of the pad (tangency or breakout), leaving a weak or open connection.

**5. CAF (conductive anodic filament).** Under humidity and DC bias, copper ions migrate along the interface between glass fibres and resin from anode to cathode, forming a conductive filament. Drilling damage that creates microcracks along the glass bundles gives the path. Risk rises with smaller hole-wall spacing, higher voltage, higher humidity and temperature. Hole-wall to hole-wall distance is roughly (pitch - drill): 0.35 mm for 0.65 mm pitch and 0.3 mm drills.

**6. Assembly-related failures.** Open vias in pads wick solder and cause voids or opens; moisture trapped in via fill or tents can expand in reflow. See [[q0578]], [[q0579]].

**7. Electrical overstress.** The barrel cross-section is about 0.02 mm². 5 A through one via is a current density of about 23,000 A/cm² and, by the IPC-2221 fit, a predicted temperature rise in the hundreds of degrees, so it will heat strongly or fuse.

**8. Microvia failures.** In HDI: separation at the microvia base (target pad), dimples or voids in the fill, and stacked vias cracking at their interfaces during thermal cycling.

**Detection.** Four-wire resistance of daisy-chained via coupons, thermal-cycle or interconnect-stress testing (IST) on coupons, cross-sections, X-ray (for gross defects), and electrical leakage tests under temperature-humidity-bias for CAF.
:::

Next are plating voids and thin plating in high-aspect-ratio holes, interconnect separation between barrel and inner pad, annular-ring breakout, and CAF, copper migration along glass fibres between close vias in humidity. At assembly, open vias in pads wick solder, and too much current in too few vias overheats them. I prevent these with low aspect ratio, enough plating, spacing, high-Tg material and via arrays."

::: long
"I group via failures by cause. The first and most important is thermal-mechanical. The board's resin expands along the thickness direction much more than copper does, about 60 parts per million per degree below its glass transition, and several times more above it, while copper is around 17. Each time the board heats up, the resin stretches the barrel. Over many thermal cycles, or after a few lead-free reflows at 260 degrees where the strain is around 2.8 percent, the copper fatigues and cracks, usually at the barrel corner where it meets the pad, or in the middle. It shows up as an intermittent open that appears when the board is hot or cold.

The second group is manufacturing defects: voids and thin spots in the plating, especially in deep narrow holes, resin smear that stops the barrel bonding to inner-layer pads, and drill wander that breaks the annular ring.

Third is CAF, conductive anodic filament. In humid conditions, with a voltage between neighbouring vias, copper can migrate along the glass fibres and make a leakage path or a short. Closer wall-to-wall spacing makes it more likely.

Fourth is assembly-related. A via in a pad that's open wicks solder away and causes voids and opens, and trapped moisture or chemistry in a tented via can pop in reflow.

And fifth is electrical overstress: a barrel is only about 0.02 square millimetres of copper, so a few amps through one via can overheat it.

My prevention list: keep aspect ratio moderate, specify plating thickness and a higher-Tg, low-Z-CTE laminate, space vias, use arrays for current, fill or move vias out of pads, and ask for cross-section and thermal-cycle coupons."
:::

::: terms
- **CTE (Z-axis)**: thermal expansion of the board in the thickness direction, in ppm/°C.
- **$T_g$**: glass transition temperature, above which the resin's CTE jumps and it softens.
- **Barrel crack / corner crack**: fatigue fracture of the plated copper, mid-barrel or at the pad corner.
- **ICD / post separation**: separation between barrel and inner-layer pad (interconnect defect).
- **Plating void**: a gap in the barrel copper, often from trapped air or poor solution flow.
- **CAF**: conductive anodic filament; copper migration along glass fibres.
- **Annular ring breakout**: the drilled hole extends past the pad edge.
- **Daisy-chain coupon**: a test structure of many vias in series to measure resistance change during stress.
- **IST**: interconnect stress test, a method that thermally cycles coupons by resistive heating.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Cracks in the barrel, bad plating, and shorts."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names the root cause of cracking (Z-axis CTE mismatch) and quantifies it with numbers (about 0.4 % strain for a 100 °C swing, about 2.8 % for reflow).
- Explains why aspect ratio, plating thickness and $T_g$ matter and where cracks start (the corner).
- Separates latent failures (fatigue, CAF) from workmanship defects (voids, smear).
- Explains CAF with its conditions (humidity, bias, spacing, drilling damage).
- Connects each failure to a symptom (intermittent open when hot, leakage rising with humidity) and a detection method (daisy-chain resistance, IST, cross-section).
- Gives prevention, not just a list.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The via burns out from current" as the main answer.** It can happen, but fatigue from thermal cycling is far more common.
- **Treating a continuity test as proof.** A cracked barrel can touch at room temperature and open when cycled.
- **Mixing up CAF and dendritic growth on the surface.** CAF forms inside the laminate along glass fibres. Surface dendrites form across the board face.
- **Ignoring spacing.** CAF risk is set by hole-wall to hole-wall distance, not by pad size.
- **Blaming the design only.** Many via failures are process escapes (plating, smear), which is why specifying and verifying plating thickness and coupons matters.
- **Forgetting reflow cycles.** Lead-free assembly plus rework counts as several thermal excursions to 250–260 °C before the product ships.
:::

::: deep
## Why vias fail: a thickness-direction story

A multilayer board is a laminate of copper and glass-reinforced epoxy. In the plane of the board the glass fabric holds expansion to about 14–17 ppm/°C, close to copper, so traces and planes don't stress each other much. But in the thickness direction (Z) nothing reinforces the resin, and it expands at roughly 50–70 ppm/°C below $T_g$ and far more above. The plated via barrel is a copper tube running through that Z dimension, bonded to the hole wall and to every inner-layer pad it crosses.

Heat the board, and the resin tries to lengthen the barrel more than copper wants to. The mismatch strain is

$$\varepsilon(T)=\int_{25}^{T}\left[\alpha_{z}(T')-\alpha_{Cu}\right]dT'$$

A normal operating swing (25 to 125 °C) imposes about 0.4 %, but one lead-free reflow imposes about 2.8 %, which is why assembly and rework consume part of the via's fatigue life before the product ships. Values are typical; use your laminate's datasheet.

Copper can plastically stretch, but each cycle leaves a little damage, and fatigue life falls steeply with strain amplitude. A thick, ductile barrel, a low-Z-CTE high-$T_g$ laminate and a short board (low aspect ratio) all lower the strain on the copper.

## Walk through the failure modes

**Barrel and corner cracks.** The corner where the barrel turns outward onto the pad is a stress concentrator. When the barrel is thin there, the crack starts there. Higher aspect ratio makes plating thinner at the mid-barrel and gives a longer barrel to stretch. IPC-6012 sets minimum plating thicknesses (commonly quoted as roughly 20 µm average for Class 2 and 25 µm for Class 3), but the real life depends on laminate, thickness and thermal profile.

**Plating voids.** Trapped air bubbles or poor wetting leave regions with no copper. A void reduces the cross-section locally and concentrates strain, so a void is a pre-made crack. Voids are checked by cross-section and by wicking or backlight tests at the fab.

**Interconnect defects.** After drilling, hot resin can smear over the exposed edge of the inner-layer copper pad. If the desmear step doesn't remove it, the electroplated barrel never makes a metallic bond to the pad. The connection can look fine until thermal stress opens the gap.

**Annular ring breakout.** Drill wander (a few thousandths of an inch is normal) plus layer registration error can push the hole to the edge of the pad. Minimum annular ring rules exist to keep margin. If you shrink the pad to fit a tight area, you spend that margin.

**CAF.** Imagine two vias at 0.65 mm pitch with 0.3 mm drills: the hole walls are only about 0.35 mm apart. If drilling has created microcracks along a glass bundle between them, moisture can wick along it. With a bias between them, copper at the anode oxidises, migrates and re-deposits, growing toward the cathode as a filament. Leakage rises slowly (hours to months), then collapses into a short. Mitigations: more wall-to-wall spacing, CAF-resistant laminates, a good drill process, and avoiding large DC bias differences between adjacent vias in humid products.

**Wicking and solder issues.** See [[q0578]]. An open via-in-pad drains solder, producing voids or opens that only show up on X-ray.

**Overcurrent.** A single 0.3 mm via has a barrel cross-section of about 0.0216 mm². For 5 A the current density is about 23,000 A/cm². With the IPC-2221 internal-layer fit, a 5 A load predicts a rise of hundreds of degrees (the fit is out of range there, but the message is clear), so you need several vias in parallel. Heating also raises the strain in the barrel, so overcurrent and fatigue compound.

**Microvia failures.** Stacked microvias concentrate Z-strain at their interfaces, and a poorly plated base can crack or separate from the target pad. They are a recognised weak point in thermal-cycling reliability, so fabs and standards treat staggered or copper-filled microvias more conservatively.

## Detecting and preventing

| Failure | Typical symptom | Detection | Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| Barrel/corner crack | intermittent open when hot or cold | 4-wire resistance of daisy-chain coupons, IST, cross-section | lower aspect ratio, thicker plating, high-Tg low-CTE laminate, fewer reflows |
| Plating void | open or high resistance after stress | cross-section, backlight test | process control, aspect ratio limit |
| ICD/post separation | open at temperature | cross-section, microsection after stress | good desmear, qualified fab |
| Annular ring breakout | weak, open under stress | microsection, design-rule check | adequate ring, realistic pad size |
| CAF | rising leakage, then short, humid | temperature-humidity-bias test | wall spacing, CAF-resistant material |
| Wicking/voids | opens, voided joints | X-ray | filled/capped or off-pad vias |
| Overcurrent | hot spot, burnt via | thermal camera | via arrays, IPC-2152 sizing |

## What to say in the room

Pick the one failure you can derive from physics (barrel fatigue, with the CTE numbers), mention CAF as the electrochemical one, and finish with how you reduce risk. If asked "how would you know it's a via?", say: "intermittent, temperature-dependent, and the resistance of the path changes when I flex or heat the board; I'd confirm with 4-wire resistance and a cross-section."
:::

::: applications
- **Automotive and aerospace boards**: reliability specs call for thermal-cycling and interconnect-stress testing of via coupons.
- **Thick, high-layer-count server and backplane boards**: aspect ratio and plating thickness controlled to limit barrel fatigue.
- **High-voltage and humid environments**: CAF-resistant laminates and wider via spacing.
- **HDI phones and wearables**: staggered or copper-filled microvias and qualification by thermal shock.
- **Lead-free assembly with rework**: via life budget includes several 260 °C excursions.
- **Power boards**: via arrays and thermal vias sized for current and temperature.
:::

::: assumptions
- Typical FR-4 values: $\alpha_z\approx60$ ppm/°C below $T_g=170$ °C, 260 ppm/°C above, copper 17 ppm/°C. Your laminate will differ.
- The strain model is simplified: it treats the barrel as fully constrained by the resin and ignores plasticity and stress relaxation.
- Plating thickness minimums are quoted as typical values for IPC-6012 classes; check the standard and the fab.
- The IPC-2221 current fit is for flat traces; 5 A in one via is shown for scale only.
- CAF requires moisture, bias and a damaged resin/glass interface; if any is missing, the risk drops sharply.
:::

::: followups
### Why are cracks more likely at the corner of the barrel?
The corner is where the barrel turns 90 degrees onto the pad. Strain concentrates there, and the plating is often thinnest at that knee. Fatigue cracks start at the highest strain with the least copper, so the corner is the classic initiation site.

### How would you tell a via crack from a solder joint failure on a board that fails intermittently when warm?
Measure the resistance of the suspect net with a 4-wire meter at room temperature and while heating locally with a hot-air gun or cold spray. A via crack often appears as a jump in resistance with temperature or on flexing, and the nets run through vias. A solder joint fails at a component pad. Then cross-section or X-ray to confirm. See [[q0589]].

### Why does a higher aspect ratio hurt reliability?
Plating solution has trouble reaching the middle of a deep narrow hole, so the barrel is thinner there, and voids are more likely. The barrel is also longer, so it sees more total Z-expansion for the same temperature swing. Both raise the strain on a weaker barrel.

### How can you reduce CAF risk without changing the laminate?
Increase hole-wall to hole-wall spacing, reduce the DC voltage difference between adjacent vias, avoid routing different-voltage nets through tightly packed via fields, ask for a good desmear and drill process, and keep the product dry or conformally coated if the environment allows.

### Scenario: a product passes factory test but 1 percent fail in the field after a winter cold start. What do you suspect and how do you investigate?
Accumulated thermal-cycling damage: a barrel, inner-pad (ICD) or microvia crack, or a fatigued solder joint, that finally opens under the large, fast temperature swing of a cold start. I'd pull failed units, 4-wire the failing nets at low temperature, and cross-section the via. I'd compare with a coupon from the same lot with IST, and look for correlation with a specific via type, aspect ratio or lot date. The fix might be thicker plating, a higher-Tg laminate, or fewer reflows.
:::

::: interpretations
"Common failure modes of vias" can be read as **board-level reliability failures** (cracking, CAF) or as **design-level failures** (SI or current limits). I have covered both, with the reliability mechanisms first. A related question, [[q0589]], asks about failure modes of the PCB as a whole, which includes vias but also solder joints, delamination and contamination.
:::
