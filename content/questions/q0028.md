::: testing
Whether you can quickly reason about a capacitor at the two moments that matter in any switching problem:

1. **Initially** (the instant a step is applied, $t = 0^+$): an uncharged capacitor behaves like a **short circuit**; more generally like a **voltage source** equal to its initial voltage.
2. **DC steady state** ($t\to\infty$): it behaves like an **open circuit**; no current flows.
3. *Why*: both come from $i = C\,dv/dt$ ([[q0025]]) and voltage continuity ([[q0037]]).
4. That you can use these to solve a circuit's initial and final values in seconds, and connect them to real effects like inrush current.
:::

::: why
These two "cheat models" let you solve most RC transient questions by inspection: draw the circuit with the capacitor shorted to get initial currents, draw it with the capacitor open to get final voltages, and connect them with an exponential of time constant $R_{th}C$. In hardware they explain inrush current when you plug in a board with big input capacitors, why a reset line starts low and rises, why a DC-blocking capacitor eventually passes no DC, and why a decoupling capacitor delivers its charge at the instant of a load step. Interviewers love it because it reveals whether you understand transients or just memorize formulas.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor law** $i = C\,dv/dt$: current flows only while voltage changes. See [[q0025]].
- **Continuity**: capacitor voltage can't change instantaneously, $v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)$. See [[q0037]].
- **DC steady state**: all derivatives are zero once transients have died out.
- **Thevenin equivalent** as seen by the capacitor, which sets $\tau = R_{th}C$ ([[q0008]]).
- **Impedance limits**: $1/(j\omega C)$ is zero at "infinite frequency" (a sudden edge) and infinite at DC. See [[q0024]].
- **Inductor dual**: an inductor is open initially and short at DC steady state ([[q0044]]).
:::

::: answer
"When it's first excited, an uncharged capacitor acts like a short circuit, and at DC steady state it acts like an open circuit.

The reason is $i = C\,dv/dt$ combined with the fact that capacitor voltage can't jump. At the instant a step hits, the capacitor's voltage is still whatever it was before, so if it started discharged it's still at zero volts, and a zero-volt element with current flowing through it is effectively a short. All the current the rest of the circuit can supply flows into it. More generally, if it was precharged, it looks like a voltage source at its initial voltage.

Then as charge builds up its voltage rises, the current tapers off exponentially with time constant $RC$, and after about five time constants the voltage stops changing. With $dv/dt = 0$, the current is zero, so it's an open circuit.

That gives a really quick way to solve problems: short the capacitor to find the initial currents, open it to find the final voltages, and join them with an exponential. And it's why plugging in a board with big input capacitors causes an inrush spike: for that first instant, the capacitors look like a short across the supply."
:::

::: technical
**Initial instant ($t = 0^+$).** Continuity gives $v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-) = V_0$. Model the capacitor as an ideal voltage source $V_0$:
- If $V_0 = 0$: a **short circuit** (0 V source). Current $i_C(0^+)$ is set entirely by the rest of the circuit: for a series $R$ from $V_s$, $i_C(0^+) = V_s/R$.
- Capacitor current *can* jump; capacitor voltage cannot.

**DC steady state ($t\to\infty$).** With constant sources, $dv_C/dt\to0 \Rightarrow i_C = 0$: an **open circuit**. $v_C(\infty)$ is the open-circuit (Thevenin) voltage at the capacitor's terminals.

**Between them.** For any single capacitor with a resistive network and DC sources:
$$v_C(t) = v_C(\infty) + \big[v_C(0^+) - v_C(\infty)\big]e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau = R_{th}C$$
$R_{th}$ is the resistance seen by the capacitor with sources zeroed. After $5\tau$, within 0.7% of final.

**Frequency-domain view.** $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)$: a fast edge is rich in high frequencies, where $|Z_C|\to0$ (short); DC is $\omega = 0$, where $|Z_C|\to\infty$ (open).

**Worked example.** $V_s = 10$ V steps into $R_1 = 1$ kΩ feeding a node with $C = 10$ µF and $R_2 = 4$ kΩ to ground:
- $t = 0^+$: C is a short → $v_C = 0$, $i_{R2} = 0$, $i_{R1} = i_C = 10$ mA.
- $t\to\infty$: C open → $v_C = 10\cdot4/5 = 8$ V, $i = 2$ mA through $R_1$ and $R_2$, $i_C = 0$.
- $\tau = (R_1\parallel R_2)C = 800\,\Omega\times10\,\mu\text{F} = 8$ ms; settled after ≈ 40 ms.

**Real-world limits.** "Short" is limited by ESR, source resistance and wiring inductance (inrush peak $\approx V_s/(R_{src} + \text{ESR})$). "Open" is limited by leakage current (nA for C0G, µA for electrolytics).
:::

::: short
"Initially, an uncharged capacitor acts like a short circuit, because its voltage can't change instantly: it's at 0 V but can take any current. At DC steady state it acts like an open circuit, because once the voltage stops changing, $i = C\,dv/dt$ is zero. In between, it charges exponentially with $\tau = RC$. More generally, at the first instant it looks like a voltage source at whatever voltage it held before."
:::

::: long
"A capacitor behaves very differently at the instant it's excited compared with long afterward.

At the instant of excitation, the key fact is that capacitor voltage is continuous. Whatever voltage it had just before the step, it still has just after. If it started discharged, it's sitting at zero volts while current can rush into it, which is exactly how a short circuit behaves. So at $t = 0^+$ I redraw the circuit with the capacitor replaced by a wire and solve for the initial currents. If it was precharged, I replace it with a voltage source equal to that initial voltage instead.

As current flows in, charge builds up and the voltage rises. That reduces the voltage across whatever resistance is feeding it, so the current drops, and you get the familiar exponential, with time constant equal to the Thevenin resistance seen by the capacitor times $C$.

Once the voltage stops changing, at DC steady state, $i = C\,dv/dt$ is zero. No current flows, so the capacitor is an open circuit. I redraw the circuit with the capacitor removed and find its voltage as the open-circuit voltage at those terminals.

Here's a quick example. Ten volts steps into a 1 kΩ resistor feeding a node with a 10 µF capacitor and a 4 kΩ resistor to ground. At the first instant the capacitor shorts the node, so 10 mA flows through the 1 kΩ and nothing through the 4 kΩ. At steady state the capacitor is open, so it's a divider and the capacitor ends at 8 volts. The time constant uses the parallel combination, 800 ohms, times 10 µF, so 8 ms, and it's basically settled after 40 ms.

The same idea also falls out of impedance: a sudden edge is high frequency, where the capacitor's impedance is near zero, and DC is zero frequency, where it's infinite. In practice this explains inrush current when you plug in a board with big bulk capacitors: for that first moment they look like a short across the supply. An inductor is the exact opposite: open at first, short at DC."
:::

::: terms
- **Initial condition ($v_C(0^-)$)**: the capacitor's voltage just before a switching event.
- **$t = 0^+$ model**: capacitor replaced by a voltage source equal to $v_C(0^-)$; a short if uncharged.
- **DC steady state**: the condition reached after transients decay, where all voltages and currents are constant.
- **Open-circuit model**: at DC steady state a capacitor carries no current and is removed from the circuit.
- **Time constant ($\tau = R_{th}C$)**: sets the exponential transition between initial and final values.
- **Inrush current**: the large initial current drawn by uncharged capacitors at power-up.
- **Settling**: commonly taken as $5\tau$ (99.3% complete).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Short at first, open at steady state."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains *why* using continuity and $i = C\,dv/dt$.
- Refines "short" to "voltage source equal to its initial voltage" for a precharged capacitor.
- Notes that capacitor **current** can jump even though voltage can't.
- Shows how to use the models: short → initial currents, open → final voltage, $\tau = R_{th}C$ → in between, with a worked number.
- Links it to the impedance picture and to real effects (inrush, leakage, ESR limiting the "short").
- Mentions the inductor is the dual (open initially, short at DC).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "a capacitor is always a short at the start."** Only if it's uncharged. A precharged capacitor is a voltage source at $t = 0^+$.
- **Saying the capacitor's voltage jumps to the source voltage.** Its *current* jumps; its voltage starts exactly where it was.
- **Using the wrong resistance for $\tau$.** It's the Thevenin resistance seen by the capacitor with sources zeroed, often a parallel combination, not just the series resistor.
- **Confusing with the inductor.** Inductors are the opposite: open initially (zero current continuity), short at DC steady state.
- **Assuming "open at DC" means zero voltage.** The capacitor is open in current but holds a voltage, often the full Thevenin voltage.
:::

::: deep
## Why "short first, open later"

Two facts do all the work.

**Fact 1: voltage continuity.** Because $i = C\,dv/dt$, an instant jump in voltage would need infinite current, so $v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)$ ([[q0037]]).

**Fact 2: current only flows while voltage is changing.** Once the voltage is constant, $dv/dt = 0$ and the current is zero ([[q0025]]).

At $t = 0^+$, an uncharged capacitor is held at 0 V by Fact 1, but there's no restriction on its current. "Zero volts, any current" is the definition of a short circuit. As current flows, the capacitor charges and its voltage rises toward the source, which reduces the current that can flow. Eventually Fact 2 takes over: at steady state the voltage stops changing, current is zero, and "any voltage, zero current" is the definition of an open circuit.

## Worked example: solve it in three drawings

**1. Initial instant ($t = 0^+$), capacitor → short.** The node $v_C$ is pinned at 0 V (continuity, starting discharged). So:
- $R_2$ has 0 V across it: $i_{R2}(0^+) = 0$.
- All of $V_s$ is across $R_1$: $i_{R1}(0^+) = 10/1000 = 10$ mA, and all of it flows into the capacitor: $i_C(0^+) = 10$ mA.

**2. DC steady state ($t\to\infty$), capacitor → open.** Now it's just a divider:
- $v_C(\infty) = 10\times\frac{4}{1+4} = 8$ V.
- $i_{R1} = i_{R2} = 10/5000 = 2$ mA; $i_C = 0$.

**3. Time constant.** Zero the source (short $V_s$) and look into the capacitor's terminals: $R_1$ and $R_2$ are in parallel.
- $R_{th} = 1\,\text{k}\parallel4\,\text{k} = 800$ Ω, $\tau = 800\times10\,\mu = 8$ ms.

**Assemble:**

$$v_C(t) = 8\left(1 - e^{-t/8\,\text{ms}}\right)\ \text{V},\qquad i_C(t) = 10\,e^{-t/8\,\text{ms}}\ \text{mA}$$

Check: $i_C = C\,dv_C/dt = 10\,\mu\times8/(8\,\text{m})\,e^{-t/\tau} = 10$ mA $e^{-t/\tau}$ ✓.

## The general version: a precharged capacitor

If the capacitor held $V_0$ before the switch moved, then at $t = 0^+$ it's a **voltage source of $V_0$**, not a short. In the example above, if $C$ started at 3 V, the initial current through $R_1$ would be $(10 - 3)/1\,\text{k} = 7$ mA, $R_2$ would carry $3/4\,\text{k} = 0.75$ mA, so $i_C(0^+) = 6.25$ mA. The final value is still 8 V and $\tau$ is still 8 ms; only the starting point changes:

$$v_C(t) = 8 + (3 - 8)e^{-t/\tau}$$

"Short circuit" is just the special case $V_0 = 0$.

## The frequency-domain picture agrees

The impedance $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)$ ([[q0024]]) tells the same story:
- A sudden step contains energy at very high frequencies; there $|Z_C|\to0$, so the capacitor looks like a short to the edge.
- DC is $\omega = 0$; there $|Z_C|\to\infty$, so it's an open.

That's not a coincidence: the initial-value and final-value theorems of the Laplace transform link $t\to0^+$ to $s\to\infty$ and $t\to\infty$ to $s\to0$.

## What the real world does to these models

### The "short" isn't perfect: inrush current

Plug a board with 1000 µF of input capacitance into a 12 V supply. For that first instant the capacitors look like a short across the supply, so the inrush current is limited only by the series resistance of the supply, cable, connector, traces and the capacitors' ESR. With 100 mΩ total, the ideal estimate is $12/0.1 = 120$ A peak, with a time constant of $0.1\times1000\,\mu = 100$ µs. Real wiring inductance softens the edge, but the spike can still pit connector contacts, trip upstream protection or collapse the supply. That's why hot-swap controllers, NTC thermistors and soft-start circuits exist: they ramp the voltage so $i = C\,dv/dt$ stays bounded.

> [!TIP]
> Inrush-limited ramp sizing: to keep inrush below $I_{max}$, ramp the output no faster than $dv/dt = I_{max}/C$. For 1000 µF and a 2 A limit, that's 2000 V/s, so a 12 V rail should ramp over at least 6 ms.

### The "open" isn't perfect: leakage

At DC steady state a real capacitor still passes a small leakage current: nanoamps or less for C0G and film, up to microamps for electrolytics and tantalums. That matters for battery-powered designs, sample-and-hold droop, and high-impedance timing circuits (a 10 MΩ timing resistor and a leaky electrolytic don't mix).

### ESR makes the terminal voltage step

The ideal capacitance's voltage is continuous, but a real capacitor's *terminal* voltage includes $i\cdot\text{ESR}$, which jumps with the current. At $t = 0^+$ in the example, a 0.1 Ω ESR would show a 1 mV step. In a buck converter output, that ESR step is a visible part of the ripple.

## Comparison with the inductor

| | Capacitor | Inductor |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous quantity | Voltage | Current |
| At $t = 0^+$ (unenergized) | Short circuit | Open circuit |
| At $t = 0^+$ (energized) | Voltage source $V_0$ | Current source $I_0$ |
| At DC steady state | Open circuit | Short circuit |
| Time constant | $R_{th}C$ | $L/R_{th}$ |

See [[q0044]] for the inductor version and [[q0027]] for continuity in both.
:::

::: applications
- **Inrush current at power-up**: bulk capacitors act like a short across the input, so hot-swap controllers or NTC limiters control the charging current.
- **Power-on reset**: an RC on a reset pin holds it low (capacitor initially at 0 V) and releases it after a few τ.
- **Decoupling during load steps**: at the instant a load step hits, the decoupling capacitor supplies current while holding its voltage, before the regulator responds ([[q0032]]).
- **DC blocking**: an AC coupling capacitor charges to the DC difference and then passes no DC current ([[q0033]]).
- **Debounce circuits**: the capacitor's initial voltage and RC time constant filter switch bounce.
- **Bootstrap and charge-pump circuits** rely on a capacitor holding its voltage (acting as a source) across a switching event.
:::

::: assumptions
- **DC (constant) sources** after the switching event; with sinusoidal sources the steady state is AC, not an open circuit.
- **Uncharged initial state** for the "short" model; otherwise use a voltage source $V_0$.
- **Ideal capacitor**: no ESR (which limits the short) and no leakage (which spoils the open).
- **Single capacitor or decoupled first-order network**; with multiple storage elements (e.g. RLC) the initial/final models still hold, but the response in between can be second-order and oscillate.
- "Initially excited" is interpreted as "at the instant a step is applied". If an interviewer means "at the start of AC excitation", the same short-at-high-frequency logic applies.
:::

::: followups
### A 5 V step is applied to a 100 Ω resistor in series with an uncharged 1000 µF capacitor. What's the initial current, the final voltage, and how long until it's charged?
$i(0^+) = 5/100 = 50$ mA (capacitor = short). Final $v_C = 5$ V, $i = 0$ (open). $\tau = 100\times1000\,\mu = 0.1$ s, so about 0.5 s to be within 1%.

### What if the capacitor is precharged to 2 V in the same circuit?
At $t = 0^+$ it acts like a 2 V source: $i(0^+) = (5-2)/100 = 30$ mA. Final value and τ are unchanged: $v_C = 5 - 3e^{-t/0.1}$ V.

### Why does the capacitor current jump but not the voltage?
Voltage is tied to stored charge ($v = q/C$), and charge can't move in zero time with finite current. Current has no such restriction: it can switch instantly to whatever the circuit demands. So $i_C$ jumps from 0 to $V_s/R$ at the step while $v_C$ starts rising smoothly.

### How would you limit inrush current into a 2200 µF bulk capacitor on a 24 V input to 1 A?
Control $dv/dt$: $I = C\,dv/dt \Rightarrow dv/dt \le 1/0.0022 = 455$ V/s, so ramp over at least $24/455 \approx 53$ ms. Use a hot-swap controller or a P-FET with an RC on its gate to make the ramp, or an NTC thermistor that limits the initial current and then heats up to low resistance. A plain series resistor would need 24 Ω, wasting power during normal operation.

### In an RLC circuit, do the same initial and final models still work?
Yes for the endpoints: at $0^+$ capacitors are voltage sources (short if uncharged) and inductors are current sources (open if unenergized); at DC steady state capacitors are open and inductors are short. But the trajectory between them is second-order and may overshoot or ring, depending on damping ([[q0009]]).
:::

::: interpretations
"Initially excited" means the instant a step or switching event is applied ($t = 0^+$), assuming the capacitor starts uncharged; this page also covers the precharged case. "DC steady state" means after all transients have decayed with constant (DC) sources. The inductor counterpart is [[q0044]].
:::
