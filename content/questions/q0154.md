::: testing
Whether you see energy conservation as a **working engineering tool**, not a physics slogan. The interviewer is probing two readings of the question: (1) the physical law ("energy is neither created nor destroyed": $P_{in}=P_{out}+P_{loss}$), and how you *use* it to size converters, find unknown currents ([[q0155]]), sanity-check results and understand why a capacitor charged through a resistor loses half the energy; and (2) the practical goal of **saving energy** (efficiency, battery life, heat, cost). A strong answer connects the two with numbers.
:::

::: why
Almost every power-electronics calculation is conservation of energy in disguise: a boost converter draws more current than it delivers, a buck draws less, a capacitor hold-up time comes from $\tfrac12CV^2$, and an LDO's heat is just the power that didn't go to the load. Practically, wasted energy becomes heat that must be removed, shortens battery life and raises cost. Candidates who can say "$P_{in}=P_{out}/\eta$, so the input current is..." and then compute battery runtime show real engineering fluency. It is the principle behind [[q0155]], [[q0162]] and [[q0164]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Conservation of energy**: input power equals output power plus losses, $P_{in}=P_{out}+P_{loss}$. See [[q0006]].
- **Efficiency**: $\eta=P_{out}/P_{in}$. See [[q0162]].
- **Energy storage in components**: $E_C=\tfrac12CV^2$, $E_L=\tfrac12LI^2$. See [[q0022]] and [[q0039]].
- **Where lost power goes**: heat, which must be removed and raises temperature. See [[q0164]].
- **Charge conservation**: $Q=CV$ is conserved when capacitors share charge, but energy is not.
- **Power vs energy**: power is the rate (W), energy is the total (J or Wh).
:::

::: answer
"Energy conservation says energy can't be created or destroyed, only converted, so in any circuit input power equals output power plus whatever is lost, almost always as heat. In power electronics that's $P_{in}=P_{out}+P_{loss}$, and efficiency is output over input.

It's important for two reasons. First, as a tool: it tells me things without solving the whole circuit. If a converter makes 3.3 volts at 2 amps from 12 volts at 90% efficiency, the output power is 6.6 watts, so the input power is 7.3 watts and the input current is 0.61 amps. A buck draws less current than it delivers, a boost draws more. Same idea for hold-up time, where the energy stored in a capacitor is half C V squared, and for checking any answer is physically plausible.

Second, as a goal: wasted energy is heat, and heat is a design problem. An LDO from 12 to 3.3 volts at 2 amps wastes 17 watts and draws 2 amps from the source, while a buck wastes 0.7 watts and draws 0.6. On a 10 amp-hour 12 volt battery that's the difference between 5 hours and about 16 hours. So energy conservation drives battery life, thermal design, cost and even regulations on standby power.

One neat example is charging a capacitor through a resistor: the source supplies $CV^2$, the capacitor stores half, and the resistor dissipates the other half no matter its value."
:::

::: technical
**The law in circuit form.** For a converter at steady state (averaging over a cycle): $P_{in}=V_{in}I_{in}=P_{out}+P_{loss}$, with $\eta=P_{out}/P_{in}$. Hence
$$I_{in}=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{\eta V_{in}},\qquad I_{out}=\frac{\eta V_{in}I_{in}}{V_{out}}$$
Ideal ($\eta=1$): $V_{in}I_{in}=V_{out}I_{out}$, so a step-down converter has $I_{in}<I_{out}$ and a step-up converter has $I_{in}>I_{out}$. For an LDO, $I_{in}\approx I_{out}$ and efficiency is $V_{out}/V_{in}$. Example: 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, 90%: $P_{out}=6.6$ W, $P_{in}=7.33$ W, $I_{in}=0.61$ A, $P_{loss}=0.73$ W.

**Energy storage.** $E_C=\tfrac12CV^2$, $E_L=\tfrac12LI^2$. A 10 µH inductor at 2 A holds 20 µJ; if that all transferred to a 22 µF capacitor initially empty the capacitor voltage would be $\sqrt{2E/C}=1.35$ V (ignoring losses). A hold-up capacitor supplies $\tfrac12C(V_1^2-V_2^2)$.

**Capacitor charging through a resistor.** Source energy $CV^2$, capacitor stores $\tfrac12CV^2$, resistor dissipates $\tfrac12CV^2$, independent of $R$. For 100 µF at 5 V: 1.25 mJ lost. The same applies to charge sharing: two 10 µF capacitors, one at 5 V and one at 0 V, connected together settle at 2.5 V; charge is conserved (50 µC before and after) but energy falls from 125 µJ to 62.5 µJ; the rest is dissipated in the wiring resistance/switch (or radiated).

**Why saving energy matters.** Battery life $=E_{battery}/P_{avg}$; lost power becomes heat that must be removed (temperature rise $=P_{loss}\theta$); reliability falls with temperature; cost, size and regulatory limits (standby power, efficiency standards).

**Limits of the simple form.** Averages assume steady state; transient storage in L and C means instantaneous $P_{in}\ne P_{out}$. Reactive power exchange and non-resistive losses require care.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Efficiency numbers (90%) are illustrative; real values depend on load, input voltage and components. Regulatory efficiency limits vary by region and product class and are updated periodically.
:::

::: long
"Energy conservation means energy is never created or destroyed, just converted. In a circuit that's input power equals output power plus loss, almost always as heat, and efficiency is output over input.

I use it two ways. First, as a calculation tool. If I know three of the four quantities at a converter's terminals, input voltage and current, output voltage and current, the energy balance gives me the fourth: output current equals efficiency times Vin times Iin over Vout. For a 12 volt to 3.3 volt, 2 amp buck at 90% the output power is 6.6 watts, the input power is 7.3 watts and the input current is 0.61 amps. So a buck draws less current than it delivers and a boost draws more. It's also how I size hold-up capacitors, half C V squared, and how I check a result for plausibility. An answer that gives more power out than in is wrong.

Second, as a design goal. Lost power is heat. An LDO dropping 12 volts to 3.3 volts at 2 amps dissipates 17.4 watts and pulls 2 amps from the source. A buck wastes 0.7 watts and pulls 0.6 amps. On a 10 amp-hour, 12 volt battery that's about 5 hours versus about 16. And the heat from the LDO would need a heat sink and would raise the temperature of everything around it. That's why efficiency matters for battery life, thermal design, cost and standby-power regulations.

A classic subtlety: when you charge a capacitor through a resistor, half of the energy from the source is dissipated in the resistor, regardless of its value, and the same happens when two capacitors share charge. Charge is conserved there, but energy isn't stored; it's lost as heat or radiation."
:::

::: terms
- **Conservation of energy**: total energy is constant; electrical energy is converted to heat, field energy or work.
- **Efficiency ($\eta$)**: $P_{out}/P_{in}$.
- **Power loss**: $P_{loss}=P_{in}-P_{out}$, dissipated as heat.
- **Energy (J, Wh)**: capacity to do work; power times time.
- **Stored energy in a capacitor/inductor**: $\tfrac12CV^2$ and $\tfrac12LI^2$.
- **Charge conservation**: charge cannot be created or destroyed; capacitors sharing charge conserve $Q$ but not $E$.
- **Thermal resistance ($\theta$)**: temperature rise per watt dissipated.
- **Battery capacity**: stored energy, $V\times$ Ah, e.g. 3.7 V × 2 Ah = 7.4 Wh.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "energy can't be created or destroyed, so we want high efficiency to save power."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Writes the balance $P_{in}=P_{out}+P_{loss}$ and **uses** it to compute a missing current (0.61 A at 12 V for 6.6 W at 90%).
- Shows the **buck/boost/LDO** current relationships and what each means for sources, wiring and fuses.
- Gives a **battery-life** comparison with numbers (5 h vs 16 h) and links lost energy to heat and temperature rise.
- Explains the **capacitor charging** paradox (half the energy is lost for any $R$) and charge sharing as a conservation-of-energy subtlety.
- Notes the limitations: averages over a cycle, transient storage in L/C, and that efficiency changes with load.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "a boost converter makes power."** Output power cannot exceed input; input current is higher: $I_{in}=I_{out}V_{out}/(\eta V_{in})$.
- **Confusing power and energy.** A 5 W load for 2 h uses 10 Wh; battery capacity is energy, not power.
- **Assuming a capacitor stores all the energy the source supplies when charging through a resistor.** Half is lost in the resistor; making $R$ small doesn't change that (it just raises the current).
- **Forgetting that conservation applies to the average.** Instantaneous input and output power differ because L and C store energy.
- **Using $V_{out}/V_{in}$ efficiency for a switching converter.** That is the LDO result.
- **Ignoring where the loss goes.** The power is heat in specific components, which must be able to dissipate it.
:::

::: deep
## Two meanings, one principle

"Why is energy conservation important?" can be answered in two ways, and the best answers combine them.

1. **Physical principle**: energy is conserved, so energy in equals energy out plus change in stored energy. In circuits this is the basis of power-balance calculations, of the behaviour of L and C, and of every efficiency number.
2. **Engineering goal**: wasted energy is cost: heat, battery drain, bigger heatsinks, lower reliability, regulatory limits.

Figure: Everything that enters must leave as useful output or as heat (plus any change in stored energy, which averages to zero in steady state). $\eta=P_{out}/P_{in}$.

Solving for any missing quantity:

$$I_{in}=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{\eta\,V_{in}},\qquad I_{out}=\frac{\eta\,V_{in}I_{in}}{V_{out}},\qquad V_{out}=\frac{\eta\,V_{in}I_{in}}{I_{out}}$$

The sanity check is the direction: step-down gives $I_{in}<I_{out}$, step-up $I_{in}>I_{out}$, and $P_{out}\le P_{in}$ always. This is the whole content of [[q0155]].

## Why it matters: LDO vs buck on a battery

A 12 V, 10 Ah battery holds $12\times10=120$ Wh. Load: 3.3 V at 2 A (6.6 W).

| Regulator | Input current | Loss | Run time |
|---|---|---|---|
| LDO ($\eta=27.5\%$) | 2.0 A | 17.4 W | $10/2=5$ h |
| Buck ($\eta=90\%$) | 0.61 A | 0.73 W | $10/0.61=16.3$ h |

The LDO draws the *same current* as the load because the pass element simply drops voltage, so the source delivers 24 W for a 6.6 W load. The buck converts voltage and current like a transformer. The extra 17 W of heat from the LDO also needs heat removal. In a multi-stage power chain efficiencies multiply: 90% × 90% = 81%.

## Distribution: current, not voltage, causes the loss

To deliver 100 W through a wire of 0.5 Ω total resistance: at 12 V the current is 8.33 A and wire loss $I^2R=34.7$ W; at 48 V the current is 2.08 A and loss 2.17 W. That's why higher distribution voltages (and local down-conversion) save energy: see [[q0007]] on why losses scale with current, and the sim:

## Energy storage in L and C

Capacitors and inductors store energy, which is why instantaneous power in and out can differ:

$$E_C=\tfrac12CV^2,\qquad E_L=\tfrac12LI^2$$

- **Hold-up**: a 1000 µF capacitor at 12 V stores 72 mJ; powering 1 W it lasts 72 ms if allowed to discharge fully (less in practice as the voltage falls). See [[q0143]].
- **Switching converters**: each cycle the inductor takes energy from the input and gives it to the output; the average over many cycles is $P_{in}$ minus losses.
- **LC resonance**: energy sloshes between L and C. A 10 µH inductor carrying 2 A holds 20 µJ; moved entirely to a 22 µF capacitor, it would reach 1.35 V.

## The capacitor-charging paradox

Charge a capacitor $C$ from a source $V$ through a resistor $R$. The source supplies $\int V i\,dt=CV^2$. The capacitor ends with $\tfrac12CV^2$. The resistor dissipates the other $\tfrac12CV^2$, whatever $R$ is.

Figure: The source gives $CV^2$, but only half ends up in the capacitor. The curves don't depend on $R$ (only on how fast, $\tau=RC$). The "lost" half is why switched-capacitor and adiabatic circuits try to charge capacitors slowly or via inductors, and why charge pumps have an efficiency limit.

**Charge sharing** is the same story: two 10 µF capacitors at 5 V and 0 V joined end at 2.5 V. Charge is conserved (50 µC before, 25 µC on each capacitor after), but energy drops from 125 µJ to 62.5 µJ; half is dissipated in the switch resistance (or radiated) however small it is. If the resistance goes to zero, it becomes a very fast spike of current that is radiated or dissipated in the remaining parasitics.

## What happens to the lost energy?

Almost always **heat**. A device dissipating $P_{loss}$ with thermal resistance $\theta$ rises by $\Delta T=P_{loss}\theta$. For the LDO at 17.4 W even a good 20 °C/W heatsink would put it 350 °C above ambient (impossible), so the LDO would overheat and shut down. For the buck at 0.73 W and 40 °C/W: about 29 °C rise. Heat limits power density, shortens component life (electrolytic capacitor life roughly halves per 10 °C in common rules of thumb), and adds fan noise and cost.

## Energy conservation beyond the converter

- **Battery life** $=E/P_{avg}$: duty-cycling, sleep modes and low quiescent current ([[q0165]]) save energy.
- **Dynamic power** $\alpha CV^2f$ in CMOS: a switching event moves $CV^2$ from the supply, half stored then dissipated each transition, which is the same RC-charging paradox ([[q0140]]).
- **Regulation and standards**: energy-efficiency and standby-power limits exist for external supplies and many products; check the current rules for your market.
- **Regenerative systems**: motors can return kinetic energy to the supply (regenerative braking) so a bulk capacitor or battery must absorb it.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked "when is conservation of energy not enough?", say: "It tells me what the totals must be, not how fast or at what voltage. It doesn't tell me about instantaneous currents, ripple or stability, those come from circuit equations. I use it for sanity checks and first estimates."

::: applications
- **Calculating converter input current** for battery sizing, fuse and wire selection.
- **Hold-up and ride-through capacitors** sized from stored energy.
- **Battery-life estimates** for IoT devices using average power and capacity in Wh.
- **Thermal design**: loss = input minus output power determines heatsinks and airflow.
- **High-voltage distribution** (48 V and higher buses in data centres, EVs) chosen to cut $I^2R$ loss.
- **Regenerative braking and energy recovery** in motor drives.

::: assumptions
- **Steady-state averages.** Over one switching cycle the net change in stored energy is zero, so average input power equals average output power plus loss.
- **"Energy conservation" read both ways**: physics principle and engineering efficiency goal.
- **Efficiency is given or assumed.** If not stated, the ideal case $\eta=100\%$ is used for estimates, then adjusted.
- **Numbers are illustrative** (90% buck, 12 V 10 Ah battery) and not specific products.
- **Alternative valid answers.** Some will emphasise "energy conservation" as saving energy (regulations, sustainability, cost). That's fully valid; the best answers connect it to the physical balance and to thermal and battery design.
:::

::: followups
### A buck converter outputs 5 V at 1.5 A from a 12 V supply with 92% efficiency. What is the input current?
$P_{out}=7.5$ W, $P_{in}=7.5/0.92=8.15$ W, $I_{in}=8.15/12=0.679$ A. A boost making 12 V at 0.5 A from 5 V at 90% would draw $6/(0.9\times5)=1.33$ A.

### Where does the "missing" energy go when charging a capacitor through a resistor?
Half of the energy supplied by the source ($\tfrac12CV^2$) is dissipated as heat in the resistor, independent of $R$. A smaller $R$ just does it faster with a bigger current spike. To avoid the loss you need to charge slowly (adiabatic charging, ramping the source) or through an inductor, as in a switching converter.

### Why can a boost converter draw more current than the load does?
Because $P_{in}=P_{out}/\eta$ and $V_{out}>V_{in}$: $I_{in}=I_{out}V_{out}/(\eta V_{in})$. Making 5 V at 1 A from 3.7 V at 90% needs 1.5 A, which matters for the battery, wiring and current rating of the inductor and switch.

### How does energy conservation help you check a design?
It gives bounds: output power cannot exceed input power; efficiency cannot exceed 100%; efficiency of a linear regulator cannot exceed $V_{out}/V_{in}$. If a simulation or measurement violates these, something is wrong, such as a mis-measured current, bad probe placement or a ground error.

### Two 10 µF capacitors, one at 5 V and one at 0 V, are connected through a switch. What is the final voltage and energy?
Charge is conserved: $50\ \mu C$ shared by 20 µF gives 2.5 V. Energy goes from $\tfrac12\cdot10\ \mu F\cdot25=125$ µJ to $\tfrac12\cdot20\ \mu F\cdot6.25=62.5$ µJ; 62.5 µJ is dissipated or radiated.

### Scenario: your battery-powered sensor lasts half the expected time. How would you use energy conservation to debug?
Measure average input power over a full duty cycle with a proper meter or logger (not just peak or sleep), compare with the estimated budget, and use $P_{in}=P_{out}+P_{loss}$ to find which stage is wasteful: regulator quiescent current in sleep, an LDO dropping a large voltage, leakage paths, or a peripheral left on. Then fix the biggest contributor.
:::
