::: basics
### In plain words
It depends on what the chip is doing. Under heavy work, the biggest users are the compute blocks: the CPU cores, the graphics block (GPU) and, for AI work, the neural engine. Moving data to and from main memory comes close behind. When the chip sits idle, a small leftover current called leakage matters most. Designers save power by keeping unused blocks off.

### Picture it
Picture a workshop. The big machines use the most electricity while they run. Trips to a distant warehouse for materials also burn real fuel, often more than the work itself. At night, nothing runs, but a few lights stay on, and that trickle adds up. Compute is the machines, memory trips are the warehouse runs, and leakage is the lights. The best savings come from switching off whatever you are not using.

### Words you need
- **Dynamic power**: power spent while circuits switch, because each switch fills or empties wires with charge. It is about $C V^2 f$: capacitance (how much charge the wiring holds per volt), voltage squared, and clock frequency.
- **Leakage**: a small current that flows through transistors (the chip's tiny switches) even when nothing switches.
- **DVFS**: lowering voltage and frequency together when less speed is needed.
- **Clock gating**: stopping the clock to an idle block so it does not switch.
- **Power gating**: cutting the supply to an idle block, which also removes its leakage.
- **Workload**: the job the chip is doing right now.

### The simplest example
Take a CPU cluster with an effective switched capacitance (the total capacitance that flips each cycle) of 1.2 nF. At 0.9 V and 2.5 GHz, $P = 1.2\times10^{-9}\times0.9^2\times2.5\times10^9 \approx 2.43$ W. Drop to 0.7 V and 1.5 GHz. Now $P \approx 0.88$ W. Frequency fell to 60%, but power fell to 36%, because voltage counts squared. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page ranks the blocks, shows why data movement costs so much, and compares idle with heavy load.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can reason about **where power goes** in a SoC and why, instead of naming one block. The interviewer wants:

1. The honest answer: it depends on the workload and the SoC class. Under heavy load it is usually the compute blocks (CPU cluster, GPU, NPU), and in many real workloads the **memory subsystem and data movement** are comparable or larger.
2. The physics: dynamic power $\alpha CV^2f$, leakage, and why voltage matters most.
3. Block-by-block reasoning: GPU, CPU, DRAM interface, accelerators, always-on logic, radios.
4. The management toolbox: clock gating, power gating, DVFS, heterogeneity, thermal throttling.
5. A feel for numbers and when leakage dominates (idle).
:::

::: why
Power sets battery life, sustained performance and cooling cost, so SoC architects, power engineers and system designers all ask "where does the power go?" before deciding what to optimize. Knowing the biggest consumers lets you decide which block deserves gating, which workload belongs on a fixed-function engine ([[q0928]]), and how to size regulators for the highest-current rail ([[q0930]]). It is also a natural follow-up to application classes ([[q0927]]) and the cost of integration ([[q0932]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Dynamic power**: $P_{dyn}=\alpha C V^2 f$; switching activity, capacitance, voltage squared, frequency.
- **Leakage (static) power**: grows with transistor count, lower threshold voltage and temperature.
- **DVFS**: lowering voltage and frequency together; power falls roughly with $f^3$. See [[q0923]] for $V^2$ scaling.
- **Clock and power gating**: switch off clocks or supply to idle blocks.
- **Data-movement energy**: DRAM and long on-chip wires cost far more than arithmetic.
- **Heterogeneous design**: fixed-function engines use less energy per task. See [[q0928]].
- **Thermal limit**: sustained power is bounded by cooling. See [[q0927]].
:::

::: answer
"It depends on the workload, but under heavy load the big ones are the compute blocks: the CPU cluster, and in graphics or AI workloads the GPU or NPU, which together can be well over half the SoC's power. The next big consumer is the memory subsystem, the memory controller, the DRAM interface and the large caches, because moving data costs far more energy than computing on it. Display, modem, video engines and the always-on logic are smaller, though in a particular scenario like video playback or a phone call they can dominate.

The physics is dynamic power, alpha C V squared f, plus leakage. Voltage is squared, so the blocks at the highest voltage and frequency, the big CPU cores and the GPU, burn the most. At idle, dynamic power is gated away and leakage is what's left, so the big SRAMs and large always-powered blocks matter.

So the practical answer is: GPU and CPU for peak power, memory and data movement close behind, and the design response is to keep as much as possible off. Clock-gate idle blocks, power-gate unused domains, scale voltage and frequency with DVFS, use small efficient cores and fixed-function engines for steady tasks, and throttle when temperature gets high."
:::

::: technical
**Power model.**
$$P=\underbrace{\alpha\,C\,V^2 f}_{\text{dynamic}}+\underbrace{V\,I_{leak}(V,T,V_t)}_{\text{static}}$$
- $\alpha$: fraction of capacitance switching per cycle (activity), reduced by clock gating.
- $C$: switched capacitance, proportional to block size and wire load.
- Leakage rises with temperature and with lower $V_t$ (faster transistors); it is paid whenever a block is powered, even if idle.

**Typical ranking under load (illustrative, mobile SoC).** GPU (in graphics/compute) and CPU cluster, then the memory subsystem (controller, PHY, system cache), then accelerators in use (NPU, ISP, video), then display pipeline, modem/radio, and always-on logic. In an idle or standby state the ranking changes: leakage in SRAM and powered-on logic, always-on domain, and radio duty-cycling dominate.

**DVFS example.** With effective capacitance $C=1.2$ nF at 0.9 V and 2.5 GHz: $P=1.2\times10^{-9}\times0.9^2\times2.5\times10^9=2.43$ W. At 0.7 V and 1.5 GHz: $1.2\times10^{-9}\times0.49\times1.5\times10^9=0.88$ W. Frequency fell to 60% but power to 36%, and energy per operation to $(0.7/0.9)^2=60\%$.

**Data movement.** At about 10 pJ/bit (illustrative) for a DRAM access, 25 GB/s costs $25\times10^9\times8\times10\times10^{-12}=2$ W. Arithmetic is cheaper than fetching its operands.
:::

::: short
"Under load it's the compute blocks, the CPU cluster and GPU, and NPU if it's running, followed closely by the memory subsystem, because moving data from DRAM costs far more than computing on it. Dynamic power goes as alpha C V squared f, so high-voltage, high-frequency blocks dominate; at idle, leakage in SRAM and powered domains does. Designers manage it with clock and power gating, DVFS, heterogeneous cores and fixed-function engines."
:::

::: long
"The honest answer is that it depends on the workload, but I can say which blocks are usually the biggest and why.

The physics first. Dynamic power is alpha times C times V squared times f, and static power is leakage. So the blocks with lots of switching capacitance running at high voltage and frequency use the most: the CPU cluster, especially the big cores, and the GPU. In a graphics-heavy workload the GPU is often the single largest consumer; in an AI workload it's the NPU or GPU. Together those can be more than half the SoC power.

Second biggest is the memory subsystem. Computing is cheap compared to moving data: a DRAM access costs maybe an order of magnitude more energy per bit than an on-chip cache access. A workload pulling 25 GB/s from LPDDR can easily burn a couple of watts in the controller, PHY and DRAM, which is why SoCs have big system-level caches and compression. Then come the fixed-function engines when they're active, the display pipeline, the modem and radios, and the always-on logic.

When the SoC is idle, the picture changes. Clock gating removes dynamic power, so leakage dominates: large SRAMs, always-on domains, and anything not power-gated. That's why designers use power gating, retention modes and low-leakage cells.

The management story: DVFS, because power drops roughly with the cube of frequency; heterogeneous big and little cores; moving steady tasks like video decode to fixed-function hardware; clock gating everything idle; and thermal throttling, because sustained performance is limited by heat. So I'd answer: CPU and GPU at peak, memory close behind, and in practice the workload decides."
:::

::: terms
- **Dynamic power**: power from charging and discharging capacitance, $\alpha CV^2f$.
- **Leakage / static power**: current through transistors when not switching.
- **Activity factor ($\alpha$)**: fraction of nodes switching per cycle.
- **DVFS**: dynamic voltage and frequency scaling.
- **Clock gating**: stopping the clock to idle logic to remove its dynamic power.
- **Power gating**: cutting the supply to an idle block to remove its leakage.
- **Retention**: keeping state in low-leakage cells while the rest is off.
- **Thermal throttling**: reducing frequency to hold temperature.
- **Dark silicon**: parts of a chip that cannot be powered simultaneously within the power budget.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the CPU and GPU use the most power".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts with "it depends on the workload", then gives a ranking for a stated scenario and a different one for idle.
- Derives $P=\alpha CV^2f$, shows why voltage dominates, and works a DVFS example (power ~ $f^3$).
- Identifies **data movement and memory** as a leading consumer and quantifies it.

:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "the CPU" as a flat answer.** In phones the GPU often exceeds the CPU, and in many scenarios memory or display matter.
- **Forgetting memory and data movement.** DRAM and cache traffic are major costs.
- **Treating power as only dynamic.** At idle and at high temperature, leakage is large.
- **Believing lower frequency alone saves energy per task.** Without lowering voltage, energy per operation stays $CV^2$; the gain comes from voltage reduction.
- **Ignoring duty cycle.** A small block that runs constantly (always-on sensor hub) can matter more than a big block that runs rarely.
- **Mixing up peak and average power.** Regulators and thermals see peaks, battery life sees averages.
:::

::: deep
## First principles: where power goes in CMOS

Every time a CMOS node switches, a capacitance $C$ is charged through the supply and discharged to ground. Per full 0-to-1-to-0 cycle the supply provides $CV^2$ of energy, half stored and later dissipated when charging, half dissipated discharging. Averaged over time, with activity $\alpha$ and clock frequency $f$:

$$P_{dyn}=\alpha\,C\,V^2 f$$

Leakage is the current that flows even when nothing switches: subthreshold conduction (exponential in $V_{GS}-V_t$ and temperature), gate tunneling, and junction leakage. It is paid for the whole time a block is powered.

Three consequences follow:
1. **Voltage is the lever.** Because of $V^2$ and the fact that a lower $V$ also needs a lower $f$, lowering both reduces power roughly with the cube of frequency.
2. **Capacitance tracks area and wires.** Big blocks with long wires (GPU shader arrays, wide CPU cores, large caches) have large $C$.
3. **Activity is controllable.** Clock gating sets $\alpha$ to near zero for idle logic.

## Block by block

**CPU cluster.** Wide out-of-order cores have large switched capacitance (large register files, issue queues, caches) and run at the highest frequency, so high $V$. A phone cluster at peak might draw 2 to 5 W, which is why only one or two big cores can run at full speed at a time (thermally limited). Small in-order cores deliver a fraction of the performance at a much smaller fraction of the power; this is the basis of big.LITTLE-style designs.

**GPU.** Thousands of lanes and a huge register file ([[q0923]]) give a large $C$, but lower clocks and voltage than a CPU. In a gaming workload the GPU is typically the largest consumer, often the largest single block, though the share varies with the game and the SoC. GPU power scales with the number of active shader units and clock, so mobile GPUs rely on tile-based rendering and bandwidth reduction to keep memory power down.

**NPU / ISP / video codec.** Dense MAC arrays or fixed pipelines are very efficient per operation (tens of TOPS/W is advertised for modern NPUs on INT8), but when used on large continuous workloads, such as 4K video recording through the ISP and encoder, they can reach a few watts. Compare with doing the same on CPUs: fixed function is much lower power for the same task.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Advertised TOPS/W figures depend on precision, utilization and what is counted (peak versus end-to-end). Treat them as marketing-grade upper bounds.

**Memory subsystem.** Data movement is the hidden consumer. For an 8-byte (64-bit) access: arithmetic costs a few pJ, a small SRAM read tens of pJ, and a DRAM access hundreds of pJ to nanojoule range. At an illustrative 10 pJ per bit, sustained 25 GB/s from LPDDR costs $25\times10^9\times8\times10\times10^{-12}=2$ W, comparable to a CPU cluster. This is why SoCs have system-level caches, compression for display and GPU surfaces, and bandwidth QoS. Note that the DRAM chips' own power is outside the SoC but inside the system budget.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 10 pJ/bit figure covers PHY plus DRAM I/O and varies strongly by standard (LPDDR4/5, DDR) and operating point. It is an order-of-magnitude illustration.

**Display pipeline.** A fixed-function pipeline reading frame buffers continuously: modest power on the SoC (a few hundred mW), but it touches memory every frame (for example 3840x2160x4 B at 60 Hz is $8.29\times10^6\times4\times60=2.0$ GB/s), and the display panel and backlight, which are not on the SoC, usually use more power than the whole SoC in a light workload.

**Modem and radios.** During sustained cellular or Wi-Fi traffic the baseband and RF can use a large share, and in weak signal conditions the transmit power amplifier (outside the SoC) dominates. Radios are duty-cycled in IoT SoCs: at the same 3 V supply a BLE transmit burst in the range of several mA to a few tens of mA dwarfs sleep current by thousands of times, but is on for only milliseconds.

**Always-on domain.** A small low-leakage domain with a tiny core, RTC and wake logic. Its power is small in absolute terms (µW to mW) but it is on 100% of the time, so in standby it can dominate the battery drain.

## Leakage and temperature

Subthreshold leakage grows exponentially with temperature (often doubling every 10 to 20 °C in a given process). A hot block therefore leaks more, which heats it more. Design responses: high-$V_t$ cells on non-critical paths, power gating (header or footer switches that cut a domain's supply, with retention for state), body biasing, and lower voltage when idle. Large SRAMs are major leakage sources, so caches have drowsy or retention modes.

## Power management in practice

- **Clock gating**: automatic in synthesis for registers whose enable is false; reduces $\alpha$ with minimal overhead.
- **Power gating**: for blocks idle for many microseconds or longer; costs wake-up latency and energy (rail ramp, state restore).
- **DVFS**: a governor picks an operating point from utilization; the PMIC changes the rail voltage and PLL changes frequency. Transition time sets how responsive it can be.
- **Heterogeneity**: small cores, efficiency GPUs and accelerators for steady tasks ([[q0927]]).
- **Thermal management**: sensors and a controller reduce frequency when temperature or power budgets are exceeded. Peak bursts are allowed by thermal mass, sustained loads are limited to the cooling capacity ([[q0927]]).
- **Measurement**: on-board rail monitors or a power analyzer for system power, on-die power estimators and performance counters for block attribution, thermal cameras for hot spots.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Give a short framework: "Power is alpha C V squared f plus leakage. Find the blocks with the most capacitance at the highest voltage and frequency, add data movement, then ask which are on in the workload. That's CPU, GPU and memory under load, and always-on plus leakage at idle."
:::

::: applications
- **Mobile SoC power budgeting**: per-block budgets for gaming, camera, video and standby scenarios.
- **DVFS governors** in Linux and Android: choose cluster operating points for efficiency.
- **Thermal design of fanless devices**: sustained limits set by chassis, not peak silicon capability.
- **Data-center accelerators**: HBM and compute power both significant; power capping sets cluster throughput.
- **Battery IoT design**: duty cycling the radio and gating everything else for multi-year life.
- **Power-aware verification**: UPF/CPF power-intent descriptions checked for isolation, retention and sequencing.

::: assumptions
- Breakdowns are illustrative for a phone-class SoC under three scenarios. Real distributions vary by product, process, workload and firmware governor settings.
- The DVFS model assumes $V$ scales linearly with $f$ and fixed effective capacitance; real curves have a minimum voltage and activity that changes with $f$.
- Leakage's temperature doubling rule of thumb varies by process and library.
- Memory subsystem power includes DRAM I/O energy that physically sits partly off the SoC; accounting conventions differ.
- For MCUs the ranking is different: flash reads, the CPU at its clock, and radios often dominate; for servers, cores and memory dominate.

::: followups
### Why does power fall roughly with the cube of frequency under DVFS?
Dynamic power is proportional to $V^2f$, and voltage must scale roughly with frequency to meet timing. If $V\propto f$ then $P\propto f^3$. In practice there's a minimum voltage, so the exponent is below three at low frequency, but the trend is why running slowly and finishing later can save energy per task.

### If DVFS saves energy, why not always run at the lowest frequency?
Energy per task also includes leakage and platform power (memory, display, other blocks) that continue while the task runs longer. Below an optimal point, the extra time costs more static and system energy than the dynamic saving. Also latency requirements limit how slowly you may run. "Race to idle" followed by deep sleep is often best when leakage and platform power are significant.

### What is the difference between clock gating and power gating?
Clock gating stops the clock to idle registers, eliminating their dynamic power but not leakage; wake-up is nearly instant. Power gating cuts the supply to a block, removing leakage too, but needs isolation cells, state retention or save/restore, and takes microseconds to wake. Use clock gating for short idleness and power gating for long idleness.

### How would you find out which block uses the most power on a real device?
Combine methods: measure rail currents with a power monitor or PMIC telemetry, use workload-specific tests that isolate blocks, read on-die power estimators and counters, and use a thermal camera for hot spots. During design, use RTL or gate-level power simulation with realistic activity (vectors from workloads) rather than average toggle rates.

### A chip meets its power target on paper but throttles after 30 seconds of gaming. What's happening and what could you do?
The peak or average power exceeds what the cooling can dissipate sustainably, so after the thermal mass is heated, temperature hits the limit and the governor reduces frequency. Remedies: lower the sustained operating point on the GPU and CPU, cut memory traffic (compression, caches, tile rendering), improve the thermal path, or move work to efficient engines. Verify with a real thermal profile.
:::
