::: testing
Whether you know the **landscape** well enough to name real parts, say what each is good for, and place them on the MCU-to-MPU spectrum. The interviewer is checking that you:

1. Can name families from several vendors and architectures (AVR, PIC, MSP430, ARM Cortex-M, RISC-V, ESP32) rather than only Arduino.
2. Know rough capability levels (clock, RAM, flash, power) so you can match a part to a job.
3. Can name microprocessors (Cortex-A application processors, x86) and say how they differ.
:::

::: why
"Which MCU would you use?" comes up in almost every embedded interview, and the honest answer starts with knowing what exists. Familiarity with a few platforms also shows you can adapt: the concepts (GPIO, timers, interrupts, DMA, buses) are the same across vendors, and the datasheet and reference manual are the real documentation.
:::

::: concepts
- **Microcontroller (MCU)**: CPU, memory and peripherals on one chip. See [[q0731]].
- **Microprocessor (MPU)**: a CPU (often with caches and MMU) that needs external memory and runs a full OS.
- **Architecture/ISA**: AVR, PIC, 8051, MSP430, ARM Cortex-M/A, RISC-V, Xtensa, x86.
- **Word size**: 8-, 16-, 32-bit data paths and what they imply for performance and code size.
- **SoC and modules**: chips with radios (ESP32, nRF52) or a CPU plus FPGA fabric.
- **Ecosystem**: toolchains, HALs, libraries, community. See [[q0735]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd split it into microcontrollers and microprocessors.

Microcontrollers integrate the CPU, flash, RAM and peripherals. The classic 8-bit ones: Microchip AVR, like the ATmega328P in the Arduino Uno, with 32 KB of flash and 2 KB of RAM at 16 MHz; PIC; and the old 8051. The 16-bit TI MSP430, famous for very low power. The big family today is 32-bit ARM Cortex-M: STMicro's STM32, NXP's LPC and i.MX RT, Nordic's nRF52 with Bluetooth, Microchip SAM, and the Raspberry Pi RP2040. Espressif's ESP32 has Wi-Fi and Bluetooth built in, and RISC-V parts are growing. Range: from a few hundred bytes of RAM and microamps in sleep up to hundreds of MHz with megabytes of memory.

Microprocessors are CPUs that need external DRAM and storage and run Linux or Android: ARM Cortex-A application processors like the Broadcom chip in the Raspberry Pi, NXP i.MX, TI Sitara in the BeagleBone, Qualcomm and MediaTek mobile chips, and x86 from Intel and AMD.

The line is blurry. A Cortex-M7 at 600 MHz is as fast as some older application processors, and many modern chips are SoCs that mix a Cortex-A core with a Cortex-M real-time core."
:::

::: long
"The way I'd organize it is by capability.

At the bottom, 8-bit and 16-bit microcontrollers. Microchip's AVR, such as the ATmega328P in the Arduino Uno: 8-bit, 16 MHz, 32 KB of flash and 2 KB of RAM. PIC controllers, common in cost-sensitive industrial products. The 8051, still around as a core. And the TI MSP430, a 16-bit part known for microamp-level sleep current, with tiny RAM like 512 bytes on some variants.

The workhorse today is 32-bit ARM Cortex-M. STMicro's STM32 is probably the broadest family: the M0 parts for cost, the M3, M4 and M7 for more speed, with an FPU and DSP instructions at the higher end, up to hundreds of MHz and megabytes of memory. Nordic's nRF52 has Bluetooth LE on chip. The Raspberry Pi RP2040 is a dual-core M0+ with programmable I/O. NXP's i.MX RT crossover parts run an M7 at 600 MHz. Espressif's ESP32 uses Xtensa or RISC-V cores with Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, which is why it's common in IoT. RISC-V microcontrollers are becoming common too.

Above that are microprocessors. These are CPUs, typically Cortex-A or x86, which have an MMU and caches and need external DRAM and storage, and they run Linux or Android. Examples: the Broadcom SoC in the Raspberry Pi 4, NXP's i.MX 6 and 8, the TI Sitara in the BeagleBone, mobile SoCs from Qualcomm, and Intel or AMD x86 parts.

I'd stress that the line is blurry. A 600 MHz Cortex-M7 beats older application processors in raw speed, and many SoCs combine an A core running Linux with an M core for real-time control. What matters when choosing is peripherals, power, cost, ecosystem and availability, not the label."
:::

::: terms
- **MCU / MPU**: microcontroller (with on-chip memory and peripherals) / microprocessor (CPU needing external memory).
- **ISA**: instruction set architecture (ARMv7-M, AVR, RISC-V).
- **Cortex-M / Cortex-A**: Arm cores for microcontrollers / for application processors.
- **SoC**: system on chip, with CPU plus many integrated blocks.
- **DMIPS, CoreMark**: benchmark scores for CPU performance.
- **FPU / DSP extension**: floating-point unit / multiply-accumulate instructions.
- **Crossover MCU**: a high-speed MCU, with MPU-like performance (for example i.MX RT).
- **Module**: a small board with MCU, memory, and often radio, soldered as a unit.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "Arduino, Raspberry Pi, STM32".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Organizes by architecture and capability: 8-bit AVR/PIC/8051, 16-bit MSP430, 32-bit Cortex-M, RISC-V/Xtensa, then Cortex-A and x86.
- Gives representative specs (ATmega328P 32 KB/2 KB, RP2040 264 KB, STM32H7 up to 480 MHz).
- Notes the integrated-radio parts (nRF52, ESP32) and heterogeneous SoCs (A + M cores).

- Flags that numbers are datasheet-typical and vary by variant.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Calling the Arduino or Raspberry Pi "a microcontroller".** Arduino is a board and toolchain around an MCU (the ATmega328P); a Raspberry Pi 4 is a microprocessor board running Linux (the RP2040-based Pico is the microcontroller one).
- **Equating clock speed with performance.** A 200 MHz M7 does more per cycle than a 16 MHz AVR, and memory wait states change the picture.
- **Quoting specs from memory as exact.** Variants differ in flash, RAM and package; say "typical" or "for example the F103C8".
- **Thinking 8-bit parts are obsolete.** They remain very common in simple, cost-sensitive and legacy products.
- **Ignoring the ecosystem.** Toolchains, libraries, debuggers and long-term supply matter as much as the core.
- **Forgetting module-level parts.** In practice you often buy a module (ESP32-WROOM, an nRF52 module) and avoid RF design.
:::

## 8-bit and 16-bit: small, simple, deterministic

An ATmega328P executes most instructions in one or two cycles at 16 MHz, so about 10 to 16 million instructions per second. It has no cache, no pipeline surprises, and instruction timing is exactly predictable, which is why it is easy to bit-bang protocols and meet tight timing without an RTOS. It has only 2 KB of RAM, so the programming style is static allocation and no heap. The MSP430 trades more complex instructions for ultra-low-power operation, drawing microamps in standby with a real-time clock running. These parts persist because they're cheap, simple, robust, and well understood.

## 32-bit Cortex-M: the default

Cortex-M cores give a 32-bit data path, a nested vectored interrupt controller (NVIC), single-cycle multiply, and optional FPU and DSP instructions, with an architecture that is the same across vendors. Moving between STM32, nRF52 or LPC is mostly a matter of learning the peripherals and the vendor's HAL; the core and the debug interface (SWD) are the same. The progression is roughly:

- **M0/M0+**: smallest and lowest power, no divide in hardware on some variants, Thumb-1 instruction set. RP2040 uses two M0+ cores.
- **M3/M4**: Thumb-2, hardware divide, M4 adds DSP instructions and optionally a single-precision FPU. Good for motor control and audio.
- **M7**: superscalar, caches and tightly coupled memory, optional double-precision FPU, up to hundreds of MHz.

## Connectivity SoCs and modules

Radios are hard to design. Parts like the nRF52 (BLE) and ESP32 (Wi-Fi + BLE) integrate the radio and a software stack, and pre-certified modules (a small PCB with chip, crystal, flash and antenna) let you skip RF layout and much of regulatory certification. That's why they dominate IoT designs, even when the on-chip CPU is more than the application needs.

## Microprocessors and the heterogeneous middle

A microprocessor like the Broadcom BCM2711 or the NXP i.MX 8 has multiple Cortex-A cores with an MMU (virtual memory, needed by Linux), L1/L2 caches, a DDR controller, and a large collection of high-speed interfaces (USB, PCIe, MIPI camera and display). It needs external DRAM, storage and a power management IC, and boots through a multi-stage bootloader. The cost is board complexity (4 to 8 layers, DDR routing), power (hundreds of mW to watts) and non-deterministic timing.

To get both, vendors integrate an Cortex-M core alongside the A cores (NXP i.MX 8M has an M7, TI Sitara AM62 variants include an M4F). Linux runs on the A cores; the M core handles hard real-time I/O.

## Decoding a part number

Part numbers carry the selection information, and being able to read one is a quick credibility signal. Take the STM32F103C8T6 as an example. STM32 is the family; F is the performance line (a "general-purpose" series, as opposed to L for ultra-low-power or H for high-performance); 103 is the specific device (Cortex-M3 in the "performance line"); C means 48 pins; 8 means 64 KB of flash; T is the LQFP package; 6 is the temperature range, −40 to 85 °C. Changing one character gives a pin-compatible or near-compatible part with different memory, which is why a design can often move up or down a flash size without a PCB respin. The same logic applies to other vendors, though each has its own scheme, so always check the vendor's ordering guide.

## Choosing within a family

Within one vendor family the same peripherals appear with different amounts of memory, pins and speed. A typical process is: start from the peripheral list and I/O count of your design, pick the family that has them, then choose the smallest memory size that leaves headroom (a rule of thumb is to design for at most 50 to 70% of flash and RAM at the first release, so there's room for features, a bootloader and bug fixes). Then check availability at distributors, and the package you can solder and route (QFN and BGA need finer PCB rules than LQFP).

## Why the "what's common" answer changes with the audience

For a hobbyist audience the common list is Arduino AVR, ESP32, RP2040 and Raspberry Pi. In consumer and IoT products it is ESP32, nRF52, and STM32. In automotive it is Infineon AURIX, NXP S32 and Renesas RH850 or RL78 parts with safety features. In industrial control it is STM32, NXP LPC and i.MX RT, TI C2000 for motor and power control. If the interviewer's company makes a particular type of product, aim your examples at that area, and show how you'd evaluate a part you haven't used before: read the product table, the reference manual's peripheral chapters and the errata.

## A selection example

Suppose the job is "read a 3-axis accelerometer at 1 kHz over SPI, run a small filter and send a result over BLE once per second, on a coin cell". A Cortex-M0+/M4 part with BLE (nRF52 class) fits: the CPU can do a filter in tens of microseconds, the radio is integrated, sleep current is a few µA. An ESP32 would work but the Wi-Fi-class radio and higher sleep current would cost battery life. A Raspberry Pi would be wildly oversized. If the same accelerometer data must be processed by a vision algorithm, the answer flips toward a Cortex-A part or an MCU with a hardware accelerator.
:::

::: applications
- **Arduino-class prototyping and simple products**: AVR, SAMD, RP2040.
- **Low-power sensor nodes**: MSP430, nRF52, STM32L series.
- **Motor control and power electronics**: STM32F4/G4, TI C2000 class parts (specialised real-time control).
- **IoT/wireless products**: ESP32, nRF52/53, and other radio SoCs.
- **Industrial HMI and gateways**: i.MX, Sitara, STM32MP-class MPUs running Linux.
- **Consumer media devices and phones**: high-end Cortex-A SoCs.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Specs are representative** headline values for the common variants named. Flash/RAM sizes, speed grades and package options vary.
- **"MCU" vs "MPU"** is a practical, not formal, distinction. See [[q0731]].
- **Vendors and part availability** change; chip shortages and end-of-life notices affect real selection.
- **Performance** depends on workload, memory system and compiler; DMIPS/MHz and CoreMark are indicative only.
- **The answer changes** by market: automotive and industrial often restrict to qualified parts (AEC-Q100) from a few vendors.
:::

::: followups
### What's the difference between Arduino and an MCU?
Arduino is an open-source board and software ecosystem; the MCU is the chip on it. The Uno uses an ATmega328P, other Arduino boards use SAMD, nRF52 or RP2040 chips. The Arduino API and bootloader hide registers and make prototyping fast; for products you'd typically use the MCU directly with a vendor toolchain.

### Why is ARM Cortex-M so popular?
A licensed, consistent architecture across many vendors (compilers, debuggers, RTOSes and libraries work across STM32, nRF, LPC...), a good power-performance balance, and a broad range of cores and parts. The consistency reduces switching cost and software risk.

### What would you pick for a coin-cell BLE sensor?
A low-power part with integrated BLE, such as an nRF52-class SoC: sleep current of a few µA with RTC running, a Cortex-M4 or M0+ with enough speed for simple filtering, and a certified radio stack. The ESP32 is more power-hungry in sleep, while an STM32 would need an external radio.

### When would you move from an MCU to an MPU?
When the product needs Linux or a full OS, large memory (hundreds of MB), a rich GUI, video, multi-camera input, heavy networking stacks or machine learning beyond what a MCU handles. The costs are board complexity, power, boot time and loss of determinism. Often I'd keep a small MCU for real-time control alongside.

### How would you compare two MCUs' performance honestly?
Run the actual workload, or at least use a standardized benchmark such as CoreMark at the real clock and flash wait-state settings, and then measure power on that workload. Datasheet MHz or DMIPS alone ignore memory wait states, peripherals and DMA offload.

### Scenario: your supplier says the STM32 you designed in is out of stock for 40 weeks. What do you do?
Check pin- and peripheral-compatible parts in the same family (often the same package with a different flash size), then alternatives from other vendors. Keep the hardware abstraction layer thin to port faster. For the future, design with footprint compatibility in mind and qualify a second source early. This is a real selection criterion (see [[q0735]]).
:::
