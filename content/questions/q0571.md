::: basics
### In plain words
A shield only works if it is connected to the right place in the right way. A shield can on a board connects to the circuit's ground plane, all the way around its edge. A cable shield or metal enclosure connects to the chassis, the product's metal frame, through the connector shell. A floating shield passes noise on.

### Picture it
Think of a bathtub. Noise current is water pouring in, and the shield's voltage is the water level. A wide, short drain keeps the level low. A narrow, long drain, like a thin wire pigtail, lets the level rise until it overflows into the room, which is the sensitive circuit. A tub with no drain, a floating shield, fills right up.

### Words you need
- **Ground plane**: a solid copper layer at 0 V inside the board.
- **Chassis**: the metal frame or enclosure of the product.
- **Floating**: connected to nothing, so its voltage drifts with whatever couples to it.
- **Pigtail**: a single thin wire used to connect a shield, which has a lot of inductance.
- **Inductance**: how strongly a wire resists fast current changes. At 100 MHz, a 10 nH wire acts like about 6 Ω.

### The simplest example
Say a noisy node swings 12 V at 2.4 V per nanosecond, and a stray 1 pF capacitance (nearby metal passing fast voltage changes) links it to the shield. That pushes a current of 1 pF × 2.4 V/ns = 2.4 mA into the shield. At 100 MHz, a 10 nH pigtail is about 6.3 Ω, so the shield swings about 15 mV. A connection through many short plated holes (vias), about 0.5 nH, is about 0.3 Ω, which gives only about 0.75 mV. A floating shield, with 5 pF to ground, would swing about 2 V.

That is the whole idea: give the noise current a short, wide way home. The rest of this page covers contact spacing, cable bonds, and grounding one end or both.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know a shield only works if it is connected correctly, and can say *to what* and *how*:

1. **Board-level cans** connect to the PCB **ground plane** at many points around the perimeter.
2. **Cable shields and enclosures** connect to **chassis** at the connector with a low-inductance 360° bond; the one-end/both-ends question depends on frequency.
3. **Why**: an electrostatic shield must drain displacement current to a reference; a floating shield picks up and re-radiates; the connection impedance (inductance) sets how well it works at RF.
:::

::: why
A shield that is connected badly can be worse than no shield: a floating can couples noise through itself, and a long pigtail on a cable shield turns it into an antenna. This is a classic review catch and a common cause of "I added a can and nothing changed". It's also the practical side of the theory in [[q0570]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Electrostatic shielding needs a return**: displacement current drains to ground. See [[q0570]].
- **Ground impedance at RF**: $Z=j\omega L$; a few nH is ohms at 100 MHz. See [[q0495]].
- **Chassis vs signal ground**: different references for different jobs. See [[q0496]].
- **Seam spacing**: contacts every $\lambda/20$ or less. See [[q0569]].
- **Shield terminations on cables**: pigtail vs 360° bond. See [[q0564]].
- **Ground loops** at low frequency from shields grounded at both ends.
:::

::: answer
"A board-level shield is connected to the circuit ground, meaning the PCB ground plane, all the way round its perimeter, either soldered to a ring of ground pads or via a series of ground contacts. A cable shield or an enclosure is bonded to chassis ground, with the connector shell making a 360 degree, low-inductance contact to the metal enclosure.

Why? A shield stops electric fields by giving the field lines somewhere to terminate and the displacement current somewhere to go. That current has to return to the source through ground. If the shield floats, it just takes on a potential set by capacitive dividers, it can be at volts, and then it couples that to whatever's inside, or re-radiates. And at high frequency the connection impedance matters: 10 nH of pigtail is 6 ohms at 100 MHz, so the noise current produces a voltage on the shield instead of being drained. So you want many short, wide connections, spaced under a twentieth of a wavelength.

For cables there's a frequency dependence: at low frequency, grounding a shield at both ends can create a ground loop, so audio shields may be grounded at one end. At RF, you ground at both ends, and bond to chassis at the connector, so the shield carries the return current and keeps the field in."
:::

::: technical
**Board-level cans.** Connect to the PCB ground plane through a perimeter ring of exposed pads with via-stitching to the plane; contact spacing at most $\lambda/20$ at the highest frequency of concern (6.25 mm at 2.4 GHz, 3 mm at 5 GHz); the can's only job is to terminate the field on ground, so low impedance is the goal.

**Why ground.** Consider a noise source with stray capacitance $C_s$ to the shield, a shield-to-ground impedance $Z_g$, and shield-to-victim capacitance $C_v$. 
- *Grounded shield:* displacement current $I = j\omega C_sV_n$ flows through $Z_g$, giving shield voltage $V_{sh}=I\,Z_g$. The victim then sees only the fraction $V_{sh}C_v$-coupled. With $C_s$ = 1 pF, $V_n$ swing 12 V at 2.4 V/ns: $I\approx2.4$ mA; with $Z_g=j\omega L$, $L$ = 10 nH at 100 MHz = 6.3 Ω, $V_{sh}\approx15$ mV; with 0.5 nH (a via-stitched connection) 0.3 Ω gives 0.7 mV.
- *Floating shield:* it forms a divider; with $C_s$ = 1 pF and 5 pF shield-to-ground, the shield sits near $12\cdot1/6 = 2$ V swing, and re-couples through $C_v$.

**Cable shields.** Connect 360° to the chassis at the connector (backshell, clamp or spring contacts), not via a pigtail wire to the PCB ground. At RF the shield should be grounded at both ends so it carries the return/CM current in a controlled path. At low frequency (audio, mains hum) a both-ends connection forms a ground loop that can inject hum, so a one-end connection is used, or a capacitor to the other end gives RF grounding while blocking low-frequency loop currents (hybrid grounding).

**Which ground?** A can over RF circuitry goes to the *signal ground plane*. Connector shells and cable shields go to *chassis* (earth), tied to signal ground at one controlled point or via a capacitor/resistor network, depending on system grounding. Plastic enclosures with no chassis: shield to the board ground near the connector with the shortest path.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Whether to ground a cable shield at one end, both ends or via a hybrid depends on the frequency range, the cable length and the system's grounding scheme; there is a long-standing debate among practitioners. The rule here (RF: both ends, low frequency audio: possibly one end) is the common guidance, but check your standard and application.
:::

::: short
"Board-level cans are connected to the PCB ground plane at many points around the perimeter; cable shields and enclosures are bonded to chassis with a 360 degree low-inductance connection at the connector. Shielding an electric field means giving the displacement current a path to return, so the shield has to be grounded, and at RF the connection impedance matters: 10 nanohenries is 6 ohms at 100 MHz. A floating shield just floats to some potential and re-couples noise. For cables, RF wants both ends bonded; low-frequency audio may need one end to avoid ground loops."

::: long
"A board-level can gets connected to the PCB ground plane, and not at one spot but all around its perimeter: a ring of ground pads, stitched to the plane with vias, with contact spacing below a twentieth of a wavelength at the highest frequency I care about. A cable shield or an enclosure gets bonded to chassis, with the connector shell making a full 360 degree low-inductance contact to the metal.

The reason is how an electric-field shield works. Field lines from the noise source terminate on the shield, and the displacement current they represent has to find its way back to the source through ground. If the shield is grounded through a low impedance, that current is drained and the shield potential stays near zero. If it's floating, it forms a capacitive divider: with 1 picofarad from the source and 5 picofarads to ground, a 12 volt swing puts about 2 volts on the shield, and that re-couples into whatever it covers, or radiates.

At RF, 'grounded' means low impedance, not DC continuity. A 10 nanohenry pigtail is 6 ohms at 100 MHz. With a milliamp or two of displacement current, that's 15 millivolts on the shield, and at 0.5 nanohenries from a via fence it's under a millivolt. That's why we use lots of short, wide connections rather than one wire.

For cable shields the one-end versus both-ends debate is about frequency. At RF both ends, so the shield carries the common-mode and return current instead of the signal conductors. At audio or mains frequencies, grounding both ends can create a ground loop with hum, so you ground one end, or use a capacitor at the other end to get RF grounding without the low-frequency loop."
:::

::: terms
- **Chassis ground**: the enclosure's metal reference, usually tied to earth.
- **Signal ground**: the circuit's reference plane.
- **Displacement current**: $C\,dv/dt$ current through a capacitance; what an electric-field shield must drain.
- **Pigtail**: a wire connecting a shield to ground; adds inductance.
- **360° termination**: shield bonded all the way round at the connector shell.
- **Ground loop**: loop formed by multiple ground connections that picks up magnetic fields and carries unwanted current.
- **Hybrid grounding**: one end DC-grounded, other end grounded through a capacitor.
- **Via fence / stitching**: ground vias at regular spacing.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Leaving a can ungrounded or connected at a single point.** A single connection is a long, inductive path at RF.
- **A pigtail for the cable shield.** The pigtail's inductance can cancel the shield's benefit above tens of MHz.
- **Grounding the shield only to the signal ground inside the board**, so the common-mode current flows through the logic ground instead of out to the chassis.
- **Grounding at both ends at audio** without considering the ground loop (or, conversely, one end only at RF).
- **Shield connected to the quiet ground through a long trace**, which just moves the noise.
- **Thinking DC continuity proves a good ground.** Measure impedance at RF or inspect contact spacing.
:::

## What a shield is connected to

| Shield | Connect to | How |
|---|---|---|
| Board-level can/frame | PCB ground plane | Perimeter ring of pads, via-stitched, contacts $\le\lambda/20$ |
| Cable shield (braid/foil) | Chassis (or board ground in plastic products) | 360° backshell/clamp at the connector, no pigtail |
| Enclosure (metal/coated) | Chassis, tied to earth where required for safety | Gaskets, screws, conductive finishes |
| Shielded connector shell | Chassis, plus connection to board ground where needed | Several ground pins/tabs, short and wide |

Figure: The noise source drives a displacement current through $C_s$ into the shield node. A low-impedance $Z_g$ (short, wide ground connection) drains it to ground, so the shield node barely moves and the victim sees almost nothing through $C_v$. | Ground connection | $L$ | $|Z_g|$ at 100 MHz | Shield swing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Via fence + wide pad | 0.5 nH | 0.31 Ω | 0.75 mV |

That roughly 2700:1 range between a floating shield and a stitched one is why "ground it well" is the rule, not an afterthought.

## Board-level shields: ground at many points

A can has to act as a wall at the highest frequency of concern. Every gap longer than a fraction of $\lambda$ leaks ([[q0570]]), and every ground contact has inductance. So:

- Solder the can all round to a continuous ring of ground copper (not discrete tabs, unless the spacing is under $\lambda/20$).
- Stitch that ring to the ground plane with vias, closely spaced, with a ground plane directly under the can's footprint.
- Don't connect it through a thin trace to some ground "island".
- Keep non-ground traces exiting under the wall on inner layers, filtered.

## Cable shields and chassis

A cable shield's job is to keep return and common-mode current on the outside of the signal conductors and keep the fields inside. For that:

- **360° termination** at the connector to the chassis, using a backshell, clamp or spring fingers.
- **No pigtails**: a 2 cm wire is about 20 nH, 12.6 Ω at 100 MHz, which wastes most of the shield's benefit at VHF.
- Where the product has no chassis (plastic housing), connect the shield to the board ground at the connector with a wide, short connection and a ground plane there.
- Decide how chassis connects to the signal ground (single point, capacitor, or direct) in the system grounding scheme and safety requirements.

### One end or both?

- **At RF (above about 100 kHz to 1 MHz):** ground both ends; the shield must carry the return current to be effective; at the far end it also prevents the shield from acting as a monopole.
- **At low frequency (audio, mains hum):** a shield grounded at both ends with different ground potentials carries a loop current that couples into the signal pair; single-end grounding avoids that. 
- **Hybrid:** one end solid, the other end via a small capacitor (for example 10 nF to 100 nF): RF is grounded at both ends, DC and mains-frequency loops are blocked.

## Chassis vs signal ground

Signal ground is the reference of the circuit; chassis is the enclosure. External noise currents (ESD, common-mode from cables) should flow in the chassis and never enter the logic ground. So connector shells land on chassis, and the connection between chassis and signal ground is made at a point (or with a capacitor) near the connector, so the discharge path is short ([[q0564]], [[q0494]]).

## Worked example: GPS can

A 2-piece can over a GPS front end is soldered to a ring of pads with ground vias every 3 mm ($\lambda/20$ at 5 GHz is 3 mm, much more than enough at 1.575 GHz where the limit is 9.5 mm). The can's ground impedance at 1.575 GHz is dominated by via inductance of about 0.3 nH each, 3 Ω per via; with 40 vias in parallel it is about 0.07 Ω. A floating can would see a divider and re-radiate any noise, which is why a can that is not stitched can degrade GPS performance instead of improving it.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Via inductance (about 0.3 to 1 nH depending on length and geometry) is an approximate figure; use your stack-up and a field solver for design-level numbers.
:::

::: applications
- **RF shield cans**: perimeter ground ring with via stitching to the plane; contact spacing to the highest harmonic of concern.
- **USB, HDMI and Ethernet connectors**: shell tied to chassis with several pins and low-inductance contacts; common-mode chokes elsewhere.
- **Coaxial cable connections** (SMA, BNC): shield bonded 360° at the connector.
- **Audio equipment**: hybrid grounding to avoid hum loops while maintaining RF shielding.
- **Medical and instrumentation cables**: shield strategies chosen against hum and RF.
- **Metal enclosures**: bonding of panels and gasketed seams to chassis.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Sources are electric-field dominant** in the displacement-current model; magnetic fields at low frequency need different treatment ([[q0570]]).
- **Lumped-inductance model** for the shield connection valid below the frequency where the connection length approaches $\lambda/10$.
- **Illustrative values**: $C_s$ = 1 pF, 5 pF shield-to-ground, 0.5 to 10 nH connections.
- **A chassis exists.** Without one, the shield is tied to the board ground close to the connector.
- **Safety grounding** requirements (protective earth) can constrain the connection of chassis to signal ground; follow the product standard.
:::

::: followups
### Why is a floating shield sometimes worse than no shield?
It forms a capacitive divider between source and ground, so it takes on a potential (volts in the example), and then couples that potential to the victim through $C_v$ and can resonate as a patch antenna. With no shield the victim sees $C_{sv}$ directly; a floating shield can transfer more voltage than the direct path.

### Why should a cable shield be grounded at both ends at RF but maybe one end at audio?
At RF the shield must carry the return/common-mode currents and prevent monopole behaviour, so it needs a low-impedance connection at both ends. At audio, different ground potentials between the two ends drive a loop current through the shield, which couples hum into the signal pair; one-end grounding or hybrid grounding avoids that.

### What's wrong with a shield pigtail?
Inductance. A 2 cm wire has about 20 nH, which is 12.6 Ω at 100 MHz. The common-mode current flowing in the shield makes a voltage across that inductance, which appears on the connector ground as noise and drives the cable, so shielding effectiveness at VHF can drop by tens of dB compared with a 360° bond (the exact figure depends on pigtail length and cable). Use a full 360° bond.

### How many ground vias should a shield can have?
Enough that the spacing is below $\lambda/20$ at the highest frequency of interest (3 mm at 5 GHz; 6.25 mm at 2.4 GHz), and the combined inductance is low. The vias connect the can's ground ring to the plane under it. More vias, closer spacing, always lower impedance until the pad ring is saturated.

### Scenario: you added a shield can over a PLL, and the phase noise got worse. What might be wrong?
The can may be touching or capacitively coupling to a trace or component, or not properly grounded, so it floats or resonates; the can's cavity might resonate near the PLL frequency; or a trace entering the can carries noise through. I'd check the ground ring and via stitching, verify the contact with a continuity and RF test, check clearances, and add filtering on signals crossing into the can.
:::
