::: basics
### In plain words
A LiPo battery does not last forever. Over time it holds less charge and gets worse at delivering current. People usually call it worn out when it holds about 80% of its original capacity. Both using it and simply leaving it on a shelf cause this wear.

### Picture it
Think of a pair of running shoes. Every run wears the soles a little. Shoes sitting in a hot garage also stiffen with age, even if you never wear them. Run hard, run hot, and they die sooner. A battery ages the same way: cycling wears it, time wears it, and heat speeds up both. Unlike shoes, a worn battery can also swell, which is a safety signal.

### Words you need
- **Capacity**: how much charge the battery can hold, in Ah or mAh.
- **Cycle**: one full charge and discharge worth of use.
- **Calendar aging**: wear that happens with time, even when unused.
- **State of health**: today's capacity as a fraction of the new capacity.
- **Internal resistance**: the battery's own opposition to current. It rises as the cell ages ([[q0303]]).

### The simplest example
A phone battery is rated for 500 full cycles to 80% capacity. If you charge it from empty to full once a day, that is 500 / 365, or about 1.4 years. A new 4000 mAh cell then holds about 0.8 × 4000 = 3200 mAh. Keeping it cooler or avoiding sitting at 100% can stretch the time.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows what is happening inside the cell, how heat speeds it up, and how a design can slow it down.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand that a lithium cell is a *consumable* with predictable wear-out, and can explain the mechanisms and the knobs. The short answer is "no, they age"; the substance is: capacity fades and internal resistance grows, both from **cycling** and from **calendar** aging; the physical causes (SEI growth, lithium plating, cathode degradation, electrolyte decomposition, gas and swelling); the stress factors (high temperature, high state of charge, high C-rate, deep cycling, cold charging); and what you do about it in a design (limit charge voltage, thermal management, derating, state-of-health tracking).
:::

::: why
Every battery product has a lifetime promise, whether a phone that must hold 80% after two years or a medical device that must work for five. Designing without an aging model means either over-sizing the battery and wasting cost and weight, or shipping a product that fails in the field. Aging also changes safety (swelling, internal shorts) and performance (rising resistance causes brown-outs long before the capacity runs out). Interviewers want to see that you think about battery behaviour over time, not just on day one.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacity fade and resistance growth**: the two aging signatures; capacity falls, resistance rises ([[q0303]]).
- **Cycle aging vs calendar aging**: wear from charge/discharge cycles vs wear from simply existing (time, temperature, SoC).
- **SEI (solid electrolyte interphase)**: a passivation layer that grows on graphite and consumes lithium.
- **Lithium plating**: metallic lithium deposition from cold or fast charging.
- **State of health (SoH)**: current capacity (or resistance) relative to new ([[q0312]]).
- **End of life (EOL)**: commonly defined as 80% of initial capacity.
- **Arrhenius temperature dependence**: aging rate roughly doubles for every 10 K or so, vendor-dependent.
:::

::: answer
"No, LiPos aren't forever. They wear out in two ways: from cycling, and just from sitting there, which we call calendar aging. As they age, two things happen: the usable capacity falls, and the internal resistance rises. A typical cell is considered at end of life when it hits about 80 percent of its original capacity, which might be roughly 300 to 1000 full cycles for LCO or NMC, and a few thousand for LiFePO4.

The reasons are electrochemical. On the graphite anode a protective film called the SEI keeps growing slowly, and every bit that grows consumes lithium and electrolyte, so capacity falls and resistance rises. Charging fast or in the cold can plate metallic lithium. The cathode slowly degrades too, especially at high voltage, and the electrolyte decomposes and makes gas, which is why pouch cells swell.

What speeds it up: heat, being stored at high state of charge, high charge and discharge currents, deep cycles, and overcharging. So to make a battery last, keep it cool, avoid sitting at 100 percent, don't charge it cold, limit the charge voltage a little if you can trade capacity for life, and use a fuel gauge to track health. And when it swells, gets hot, or the capacity has fallen too far, you retire it."
:::

::: technical
**Aging metrics.**

- Capacity retention $SoH_Q=Q_{now}/Q_{new}$; end of life typically at 0.8.
- Resistance growth $SoH_R=R_{now}/R_{new}$; end of life for power applications often at 1.5-2× (depends on the application's voltage margin).
- Cycle life: number of full-equivalent cycles to 80%; depends on depth of discharge (DoD), C-rate, temperature and charge voltage.

**Mechanisms (graphite anode, layered oxide cathode).**

| Mechanism | Where | Effect | Accelerated by |
|---|---|---|---|
| SEI growth/thickening | anode | consumes Li⁺ and electrolyte; capacity loss; resistance rise | high temperature, high SoC (low anode potential), time (≈ √t at first) |
| Lithium plating | anode | metallic Li; capacity loss, dendrites, internal short risk | charging below ≈0-10 °C, high C-rate, high SoC |
| Cathode degradation | cathode | structural change, transition-metal dissolution, surface films | high voltage (>4.2 V), high temperature, deep discharge |
| Electrolyte oxidation/decomposition | both | gas generation, swelling, resistance rise | overcharge, heat |
| Mechanical (particle cracking, binder failure, contact loss) | both | capacity and resistance changes | large DoD swings, high rates |
| Current-collector corrosion | collectors | resistance rise, copper dissolution | over-discharge below ≈2.5 V |

**Temperature dependence.** Many aging processes follow an Arrhenius law $k\propto e^{-E_a/(k_BT)}$. With an effective $E_a\approx0.5$ eV (illustrative; real values vary by mechanism), the rate relative to 25 °C is about 0.17 at 0 °C, 1.9 at 35 °C, 3.4 at 45 °C and 7.7 at 60 °C.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Effective activation energies, "doubling per 10 K" rules and cycle-life numbers are strongly cell-, chemistry- and protocol-dependent. Use datasheet and test data for the specific cell.

**Example.** A phone cell rated 500 full cycles to 80% used once per day: $500/365=1.4$ years to 80%. If daily use is only 50% DoD and cycle life scales (illustratively) as $DoD^{-1.5}$, it reaches 80% after about $1414/365=3.9$ years in cycles, but calendar aging then becomes significant (a few %/year).
:::

::: long
"No, a LiPo is a consumable. It ages in two ways. Cycle aging comes from charging and discharging, and calendar aging happens just from time, even if it's sitting in a drawer. In both cases we see two signs: usable capacity falls, and internal resistance rises. The rising resistance means voltage sags more under load, so a device can start brown-outs even when there's still decent capacity. The usual definition of end of life is eighty percent of original capacity, which for typical consumer LCO or NMC cells is a few hundred to about a thousand full cycles, while LiFePO4 can be thousands.

Why does it happen? On the graphite anode, a film called the SEI forms in the first cycles and protects the electrode, but it keeps thickening slowly. That uses up lithium and electrolyte, so you lose capacity, and it adds resistance. If you charge fast or below about zero degrees, lithium can't get into the graphite quickly enough and plates as metal, which permanently loses capacity and can grow dendrites. On the cathode side, high voltage and heat cause structural damage and metal dissolution. And the electrolyte decomposes, producing gas, which is why aged pouch cells swell.

The stress factors are heat, high state of charge, high currents, deep cycles and overvoltage. Aging rate roughly doubles every ten degrees or so, so a cell kept at 45 degrees ages several times faster than one at 25.

So the design levers are: thermal management, keeping charge voltage and top-of-charge limited if life matters, a gentle charge rate, no cold charging, a fuel gauge that tracks resistance and capacity, and sizing the battery with end-of-life margin so it still meets the spec at eighty percent."
:::

::: terms
- **State of health (SoH)**: present capacity (or resistance) relative to new.
- **Cycle life**: full equivalent cycles to a stated capacity retention, usually 80%.
- **Calendar aging**: degradation with time, temperature and state of charge, independent of use.
- **SEI**: solid electrolyte interphase layer on the anode.
- **Lithium plating**: deposition of metallic lithium on the anode surface.
- **Depth of discharge (DoD)**: fraction of capacity used in a cycle.
- **Capacity fade / power fade**: loss of capacity / rise of resistance.
- **Knee point**: the point where fade accelerates sharply near end of life.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "capacity decreases over time with cycles".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Distinguishes **capacity fade** from **resistance growth** and **cycle** from **calendar** aging, and says why resistance often ends the life first.
- Names the mechanisms (SEI growth, lithium plating, cathode degradation, electrolyte decomposition/gas) and the stress that drives each.
- Quantifies: 80% EOL, 500 cycles ≈ 1.4 years of daily full cycles, Arrhenius factor (≈3× at 45 °C vs 25 °C), a typical "knee".
- Gives design actions: temperature limit, reduced charge voltage or SoC window, cold-charge inhibit, SoH tracking, end-of-life margin in sizing.
- Mentions swelling and the safety side of aging (retire swollen cells; aged cells can short).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Only cycles matter."** A fully charged cell stored at 40 °C loses capacity even if never used (calendar aging).
- **Talking about capacity only.** Rising resistance can break a product (brown-out, early cutoff, heat) long before capacity hits 80%.
- **"Aging is linear."** Fade is often fast at first (SEI formation), slower in the middle, then accelerates at a knee when plating or loss of active material takes over.
- **Believing "100%/0% is best for accuracy".** Using the full window is the harshest; limiting to 20-80% can give a large life gain at the price of usable capacity.
- **Ignoring temperature at charge.** Charging cold is far worse than discharging cold ([[q0295]]).
- **Reusing a swollen cell.** Swelling indicates gas from decomposition; it must be retired ([[q0330]], [[q0332]]).
:::

::: deep
## Two kinds of aging, one cell

A cell's remaining life is limited by *what you do to it* (cycling) and *what happens while it waits* (calendar). Both show up in two measurable quantities:

- **Capacity** falls because lithium is permanently trapped (in the SEI, as plated metal, or in dead material) or because active material loses electrical contact.
- **Internal resistance** rises because the SEI thickens, contacts degrade and electrolyte is consumed ([[q0303]]).

Because most products have a voltage floor, resistance growth reduces usable capacity too: with $R$ doubled, the voltage drop $IR$ at a given load doubles, so the cutoff is reached earlier. For high-power products resistance, not capacity, sets end of life.

## Cycle fade versus depth of discharge

Each charge-discharge cycle stresses the electrodes (expansion and contraction of graphite and oxide particles, SEI cracking and re-forming). Smaller swings stress less. An illustrative scaling is that cycles to 80% grow as $N\propto DoD^{-1.5}$: halving the depth multiplies the number of cycles by about 2.8, but each cycle delivers half the energy, so lifetime energy throughput still rises by about 1.4×. Real cells deviate; use the manufacturer's cycle-life curve.

## Calendar aging: time, temperature, state of charge

Lithium cells age even at rest. SEI growth is driven by the anode's low potential, which is lowest at high state of charge, and by temperature. A cell stored full at 40 °C can lose a large fraction of capacity in months, while one stored at 3.8 V at 15-20 °C loses a few percent per year (typical order of magnitude; vendor- and chemistry-dependent). For storage, vendors typically recommend about 40-60% state of charge in a cool place ([[q0324]]).

## What fails first: capacity or resistance?

For a low-power sensor, capacity (runtime) is what matters. For a drone or power tool, resistance matters more: at end of life, resistance may be 1.5-2× the initial value, so a 20 A load drops twice as much voltage and heats twice as much (since $P=I^2R$). A drone pack that is fine at 80% capacity but 2× resistance will brown out on punch-outs. You can see this in the simulator by raising Rint at the same capacity:

## Swelling and the safety side of aging

Electrolyte decomposition produces gas that inflates a pouch. A slight puffiness after years of use is common, but significant swelling signals a cell near end of life or an abuse event, and is a safety concern since the internal layers separate and a short can develop. Aged cells also have a lower thermal-runaway margin in some conditions (e.g. lithium plating makes internal shorts more likely). Retire swollen cells and dispose of them properly ([[q0328]]).

## Design levers to extend life

1. **Temperature**: keep the cell below about 35-40 °C in operation; limit charge to about 0-45 °C.
2. **State-of-charge window**: stop charging at 4.1 V (about 80-90% capacity) instead of 4.2 V and you can gain a large multiple in cycle life (the trade-off is capacity; vendors publish curves).
3. **Charge rate**: 0.5C or lower is gentler than 1-2C; avoid fast charge at the top of the SoC range.
4. **Don't over-discharge**: cutoff at about 3.0 V keeps copper dissolution away.
5. **Storage**: 40-60% SoC, cool, with a protection circuit that draws very little quiescent current.
6. **Margin**: size the cell for 80% capacity and 1.5-2× resistance at end of life.
7. **Measure**: use a fuel gauge to track resistance and capacity and warn the user ([[q0312]]).

## Worked example: how long will my phone battery last?

500 full cycles to 80% means $500/365\approx1.4$ years of one full cycle per day. A typical user does about 0.5-0.7 full-equivalent cycles per day, which gives about 2-3 years to 80% (cycle-wise), consistent with real-world experience that phone batteries are noticeably worse after two to three years. A product that must last five years with a daily full cycle needs either a chemistry with more cycles (LFP or a high-cycle NMC), a reduced charge voltage and rate, or intentionally derated capacity (an 80% window).
:::

::: applications
- **Phone/laptop "battery health" features**: optimised charging that holds at 80% or delays the last 20% until needed, and reports SoH as capacity retention.
- **EV packs**: thermal management, 80-90% typical charge windows and warranties specified at 70-80% capacity after a stated period.
- **Stationary storage**: LFP chosen for thousands of cycles, low-temperature charge inhibit, calendar-life modelling.
- **Medical and industrial devices**: derated capacity and charge voltage for multi-year service.
- **Drones/RC**: retire packs by internal resistance when voltage sag becomes unacceptable.
:::

::: assumptions
The answer assumes graphite-anode lithium-ion/LiPo cells (LCO/NMC) with typical consumer usage. LiFePO4 and LTO have different (generally better) cycle and calendar life, and high-nickel or high-voltage cells have their own failure modes. Cycle counts and rates are indicative; the DoD scaling and the 0.5 eV activation energy are illustrative approximations, not measured laws.

If the cell is used in a protected, temperature-controlled environment at moderate rates (e.g. a device that sits on a charger), calendar aging at high SoC dominates and the "cycle life" number is secondary. A deep-cycle stationary system flips the emphasis to cycle aging.
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between cycle life and calendar life?
Cycle life is the number of charge-discharge cycles until capacity (or power) falls to a threshold, such as 80%. Calendar life is how long the cell lasts at rest or light use, determined by time, temperature and state of charge. A product with one cycle per month is calendar-life limited; a taxi battery is cycle-life limited. A rated "5 year" life comes from combining both.

### Why do cells age faster when they are full?
At high state of charge the graphite anode is at its lowest potential, which drives SEI growth and electrolyte reduction, and the cathode is highly delithiated and more reactive, so side reactions speed up. Storing at 40-60% charge reduces both.

### How would you track battery health in firmware?
Track full-charge capacity (from learning cycles or coulomb counting between known OCV points) and internal resistance (voltage step over a known current step), plus cycle count, temperature history and maximum voltage. Report $SoH_Q$ and $SoH_R$, and adjust the SoC estimator's capacity as the cell ages ([[q0312]]).

### What if the battery is swollen but still works?
Stop using it. Gas generation means decomposition reactions; the stack may be delaminating or a short may be forming. Discharge it safely if possible, remove it without puncturing and take it to a recycling facility. Investigate the cause (charge voltage, heat, defect) before replacing it.

### You need 5 years of daily full cycling from an NMC pouch cell rated for 500 cycles. How do you meet it?
$5\times365=1825$ cycles is 3.6× the rating, so a single full cycle per day can't work as specified. Options: use a chemistry with more cycles (LFP), size the battery larger so each cycle is shallow (with the illustrative $N\propto DoD^{-1.5}$ rule, 40% DoD gives about 2000 cycles and 30% about 3000, against the 1825 needed), lower the top-of-charge voltage and charge current, and control temperature. Verify with accelerated life testing.
:::

::: interpretations
"Can LiPos be used forever?" is a rhetorical opener; the question is really about aging mechanisms and what limits lifetime. Many people also hear "forever" as a charge question: can they be left on a charger indefinitely? The answer is that long-term float at 4.2 V accelerates aging, so chargers terminate and many devices limit charge to about 80-90% to extend life ([[q0296]]). Compare with [[q0312]] (how to determine health) and [[q0306]] (temperature effects).
