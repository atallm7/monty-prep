::: basics
### In plain words
PWM, or pulse-width modulation, makes an in-between level from a pin that can only be fully high or fully low. The pin flips at a fixed, fast rate, and you change the fraction of each cycle it stays high. That fraction is the duty cycle. A slow load, such as an LED seen by your eye, a motor, or a smoothing filter, responds to the average. PWM is efficient because the switch is always fully on or fully off.

### Picture it
Flick a light switch on and off so fast that your eye sees steady, dimmer light. On for 30% of each flick, the light looks about 30% as bright. A dimmer that burns the extra power in a resistor wastes energy. A switch wastes almost none: when on it has almost no voltage across it, and when off it carries no current.

### Words you need
- **Duty cycle**: the fraction of each period the signal is high. See [[q0671]].
- **PWM frequency**: how many cycles per second the signal repeats.
- **Average value**: duty cycle times the high level. A slow load responds to this.
- **RMS value**: the steady voltage that would heat a resistor the same amount. For PWM it is the high level times √duty.
- **Low-pass filter**: a circuit that passes slow changes and blocks fast ones, such as a resistor feeding a capacitor (an RC filter).
- **Ripple**: the small wobble left after filtering.

### The simplest example
A 3.3 V PWM signal at 30% duty averages 0.3 × 3.3 = 0.99 V, about 1 V. Its RMS value is 3.3 × √0.3 ≈ 1.81 V. A heater gets 30% of full power, so use RMS, not average, for heating.

Now filter it. At 20 kHz and 50% duty, through a 10 kΩ resistor and a 100 nF capacitor (together 1 ms, or 0.001 s), the ripple is about 3.3 × 0.5 × 0.5 ÷ (20,000 × 0.001) ≈ 41 mV.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers choosing the frequency, timer resolution, the pulse spectrum, and limits near 0% and 100% duty.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand PWM as **a way to encode an analog value in the duty cycle of a fast digital signal**, and whether you can name and quantify its characteristics: frequency, duty cycle, resolution, average and RMS value, harmonic content, ripple after filtering. The hidden sub-skills are choosing the PWM frequency (what pushes it up, what pushes it down), knowing why PWM is efficient (the switch is either fully on or fully off), and connecting it to filtering and EMI. A strong candidate treats the load (RC filter, motor inductance, LED, the eye) as the thing that does the averaging.
:::

::: why
PWM is how digital hardware controls analog things efficiently: motor speed, LED brightness, heater power, DC-DC converters, class-D amplifiers and servo position. Because the output transistor is either on (low $V_{DS}$) or off (zero current), it dissipates far less than a linear drive. Interviewers like this question because every one of those applications comes back to the same four decisions: duty cycle, frequency, resolution and filtering. It also links directly to the spectrum questions that follow ([[q0673]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Duty cycle** $D = t_{on}/T$: the information carrier in PWM. See [[q0671]] for the waveform basics.
- **Average value** $= D\cdot V_{pk}$: what a slow load or low-pass filter responds to.
- **Fourier spectrum** of a rectangular wave: DC term plus harmonics at $n f_{PWM}$ ([[q0666]], [[q0673]]).
- **Low-pass filtering / RC time constant**: removes the harmonics and leaves the average ([[q0010]]).
- **Timer resolution**: an $N$-bit counter gives $2^N$ duty steps and sets $f_{PWM} = f_{clk}/2^N$.
- **Switching vs linear drive**: a saturated switch has low $V_{DS}\cdot I$ and no loss when off.
:::

::: answer
"PWM, pulse-width modulation, is a fixed-frequency rectangular wave where I change the fraction of each period that the signal is high. That fraction is the duty cycle. The useful property is that the average of the wave is the duty cycle times the amplitude, so a 3.3 V signal at 30% duty averages about 1 V.

The load or a filter does the averaging. An LED just looks like it's at 30% brightness because your eye is slow, a motor's inductance smooths the current, and an RC filter gives you an actual analog voltage. The big advantage is efficiency: the transistor is either fully on or fully off, so it burns very little power compared with a linear regulator or a resistor.

The characteristics I'd list are the frequency, the duty cycle and its resolution, which is set by the timer bit-width, the average and RMS value, and the harmonics. Spectrally there's a DC term equal to the average, then harmonics at multiples of the PWM frequency. Choosing the frequency is a trade-off: high enough that the load can't follow it and the ripple is small, but low enough that switching losses and EMI stay manageable. For a motor I'd go above 20 kHz so it's inaudible, for an LED a few hundred hertz to kilohertz so it doesn't flicker."
:::

::: technical
**Waveform.** Period $T = 1/f_{PWM}$, high for $t_{on} = DT$, amplitude $V_{pk}$ (often the logic supply or bus voltage).

**Averages.**
$$\bar V = D\,V_{pk},\qquad V_{rms} = V_{pk}\sqrt{D}$$
Power in a resistive load: $P = V_{pk}^2 D/R$. This is why a PWM-dimmed heater or LED scales *linearly* with $D$ in power, while RMS voltage scales as $\sqrt D$.

**Spectrum** (0 to $V_{pk}$, duty $D$): DC term $DV_{pk}$ plus harmonics at $nf_{PWM}$ with peak amplitude
$$c_n = \frac{2V_{pk}}{n\pi}\left|\sin(n\pi D)\right|$$
Harmonics vanish when $nD$ is an integer. At $D = 50\%$ only odd harmonics remain ([[q0673]]).

**Resolution.** An $N$-bit counter at $f_{clk}$ gives $f_{PWM} = f_{clk}/2^N$ (edge-aligned, no prescaler) and $2^N$ duty steps. A 16 MHz clock with 8 bits gives 62.5 kHz and 256 steps; with 16 bits only 244 Hz. Frequency and resolution trade against each other for a fixed clock.

**Filtering and ripple.** For a first-order RC with $\tau = RC \gg T$, the peak-to-peak ripple at the filter output is approximately
$$\Delta V \approx \frac{V_{pk}\,D(1-D)}{f_{PWM}\,RC}$$
Example: 3.3 V, 20 kHz, $D = 0.5$, $R = 10\ \text{k}\Omega$, $C = 100\ \text{nF}$ ($\tau = 1$ ms): $\Delta V \approx 41$ mV (the exact expression gives 41.2 mV). The ripple is largest at $D = 50\%$.

**Inductive load.** For an inductor in series with the load, current ripple is $\Delta I \approx \frac{V_s D(1-D)}{L f_{PWM}}$. Example: 12 V, $D=0.5$, $L = 1$ mH, 20 kHz gives 0.15 A peak-to-peak.

**Choosing $f_{PWM}$.** Raise it to shrink ripple and filter size, to stay above human hearing (motors, magnetics, > 20 kHz) and to keep the load's own time constant much longer than $T$. Lower it to cut switching loss ($P_{sw} \propto f$), gate-drive power, EMI and to avoid timer-resolution loss.

> [!WARNING]
> Linearity breaks down near $D \to 0$ and $D \to 1$: the minimum pulse width is limited by gate-drive delay, dead time, and driver rise/fall times, so a real controller clamps the duty range.
:::

::: short
"PWM is a fixed-frequency rectangular wave where the duty cycle, the fraction of time it's high, carries the information. The average is duty times amplitude, so 30% of 3.3 V is about 1 V. A load or a low-pass filter does the averaging. It's efficient because the switch is either fully on or off. The key characteristics are frequency, duty cycle and resolution, average and RMS value, and the harmonics at multiples of the PWM frequency. Frequency is a trade-off between ripple and audible noise on one side and switching loss and EMI on the other."
:::

::: long
"PWM stands for pulse-width modulation. I generate a rectangular wave at a fixed frequency and vary the duty cycle, the fraction of each period that it's high. The key property is that the average of the signal is the duty cycle times the amplitude. So a 5 V signal at 40% duty averages 2 V. A slow load, or a filter, responds to that average.

Why do people use it? Efficiency. If I want to control a motor with a linear stage, the transistor drops the leftover voltage and burns it as heat. In PWM the transistor is either fully on, with a tiny voltage across it, or fully off, with no current, so the loss is mostly switching loss and conduction loss. That's the basis of switching regulators and class-D amplifiers.

The characteristics I'd talk about: first, frequency. It has to be high enough that the load can't follow the individual pulses. Motors are usually above 20 kHz so they're inaudible. LEDs need to be above a few hundred hertz to avoid visible flicker, higher for cameras. Second, resolution: a timer with N bits gives two to the N duty steps, and the PWM frequency is the clock divided by two to the N, so you trade frequency against resolution. Third, the spectrum: a DC term equal to the average plus harmonics at multiples of the PWM frequency, which is what your filter has to remove and what shows up as EMI. And fourth, the ripple after filtering, which for an RC is about V times D times one minus D over f times RC.

Finally, limits: very small or very large duty is limited by minimum pulse width, dead time, and driver delays, so I never count on the full 0 to 100% range."
:::

::: terms
- **Duty cycle ($D$)**: fraction of the period the signal is high, $t_{on}/T$.
- **PWM frequency**: rate of the pulse train; sets ripple, audible noise and switching loss.
- **Resolution**: number of distinct duty steps, $2^N$ for an $N$-bit timer.
- **Average value**: $D\,V_{pk}$, what a filter or a slow load responds to.
- **RMS value**: $V_{pk}\sqrt{D}$, what determines heating in a resistor.
- **Ripple**: residual AC left on the filtered output at $f_{PWM}$ and harmonics.
- **Edge-aligned vs center-aligned**: whether the pulse starts at the counter reset or is centered in the period; center-aligned reduces some harmonics and current ripple in bridges.
- **Dead time**: deliberate gap between complementary switches to prevent shoot-through.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it varies the duty cycle to change the average voltage".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives $\bar V = DV_{pk}$ and distinguishes it from RMS ($V_{pk}\sqrt D$) and says which one matters for which load (average for an RC or motor speed, RMS for heating).
- Explains *who does the averaging* (filter, inductance, eye, thermal mass) and the condition: the load time constant is much longer than $T$.
- Quantifies one trade-off with numbers (e.g. the ripple expression, or $f_{PWM} = f_{clk}/2^N$).
- Names the spectrum: DC plus harmonics at $nf_{PWM}$ with nulls at integer $nD$, and how that drives EMI and filter design.
- Knows the practical limits: minimum pulse width, dead time, non-linearity near 0% and 100%.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"PWM gives an analog voltage."** Not by itself. It is a digital waveform whose *average* is analog. Without a filter or an inherently slow load, the load sees the full-amplitude pulses.
- **Using average voltage to get power.** A 12 V resistive heater at $D = 25\%$ has an average voltage of 3 V, but $3^2/R$ is *not* its power. It dissipates 25% of full power ($V_{pk}^2D/R$), which corresponds to $V_{rms} = 6$ V. Use RMS (or $V_{pk}^2 D/R$) for heating, average for what an RC filter or motor speed responds to.
- **Picking the frequency for ripple only.** Ignoring audible noise from motor windings or switching loss at high $f$.
- **Forgetting resolution scales inversely with frequency** for a fixed timer clock.
- **Assuming the full 0 to 100% range is usable.** Gate-drive delay and dead time clip the ends.
- **Thinking an RC filter eliminates ripple.** A first-order filter only reduces it by $1/(2\pi f RC)$; sub-mV ripple needs higher order or a larger time constant.
:::

## The idea in one picture

A digital pin can only be high or low. To get an in-between average, hold it high for a fraction $D$ of each fast period.

Figure: Same period, same amplitude, different high time. The average of each trace is $D$ times the amplitude: about 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75 of the swing. Nothing about the frequency changes.

Every pulse delivers the same amplitude. What changes is the *volt-seconds* per period: $V_{pk}\cdot DT$. Anything that integrates (a capacitor, an inductor, thermal mass, the retina) responds to the volt-seconds.

With $\tau = RC = 1$ ms and $T = 50\ \mu$s ($f = 20$ kHz), each pulse nudges the capacitor by a tiny amount, and the voltage settles near $DV_{pk}$ with a small sawtooth ripple. During the on time the capacitor charges toward $V_{pk}$; during the off time it discharges toward 0. In the steady state the charge gained equals the charge lost, which fixes the mean at $DV_{pk}$. The ripple is the charge-per-period divided by $C$. In the limit $\tau\gg T$ the current is roughly constant at $I\approx (V_{pk}D - V_{out})/R$ during the on time, and $-V_{out}/R$ during the off time, which gives
$$\Delta V \approx \frac{V_{pk}D(1-D)}{f_{PWM}RC}$$
Plugging in 3.3 V, 20 kHz, 10 kΩ, 100 nF, $D=0.5$ gives 41 mV, about 1.25% of the supply. A 12-bit DAC resolution would need ripple below 0.8 mV, which would need a much larger $\tau$ (slow settling) or a second RC stage. That is the fundamental PWM-DAC trade-off: **ripple versus settling time**.

## The frequency view

The pulse train is the rectangular wave of [[q0671]] with duty $D$. Its Fourier series is
$$x(t) = DV_{pk} + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2V_{pk}}{n\pi}\sin(n\pi D)\cos(2\pi n f_{PWM}t)$$
(This form is for a pulse centred on $t=0$; shifting the pulse only changes phases.) So there is a DC term (the part we want) and harmonics at $f_{PWM}$, $2f_{PWM}$, ... (the part the filter must kill). The first-order RC above attenuates the fundamental at 20 kHz by $|H| = 1/\sqrt{1+(2\pi f RC)^2} = 0.008$ (-42 dB). The fundamental of a 50% 3.3 V wave has amplitude $2.1$ V, so $2.1\times0.008 = 16.7$ mV peak. The ripple is nearly triangular (the capacitor charges and discharges almost linearly), and a triangle whose fundamental has amplitude $a$ has peak-to-peak value $a\pi^2/4$: $16.7\times2.47 = 41$ mV. That matches the time-domain estimate, a good cross-check.

## Resolution and the timer

A timer counts from 0 to $ARR$ and compares with a threshold $CCR$: $D = CCR/(ARR+1)$. The PWM frequency is $f_{clk}/((PSC+1)(ARR+1))$. For a 16 MHz timer clock:

| ARR+1 | Duty steps | $f_{PWM}$ | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 256 | 256 (8 bit) | 62.5 kHz | audio-inaudible, coarse |
| 1024 | 1024 (10 bit) | 15.6 kHz | typical LED dimmer |
| 65536 | 65536 (16 bit) | 244 Hz | fine steps, visible flicker on LEDs |

If you need both high frequency *and* fine resolution, raise the timer clock (some MCU timers run at 100 to 400 MHz or have high-resolution PWM modes), or dither the duty cycle across successive periods so the long-term average resolves finer steps.

## Choosing the frequency

The load's own time constant sets the lower bound; the loss and EMI budget sets the upper bound.

- **LED**: eye flicker fusion is of the order of 100 Hz; for cameras and stroboscopic effects pick hundreds of Hz to kHz. LEDs themselves can switch at MHz.
- **Brushed DC motor**: electrical time constant $L/R$ in the ms range; PWM above about 20 kHz avoids audible whine from windings.
- **Buck converter**: 100 kHz to a few MHz so $L$ and $C$ stay small; the price is switching loss ($\propto f$) and EMI.
- **Class D amplifier**: switching at 300 to 500 kHz, several times the audio bandwidth, so the output filter can reject it.
- **Hobby servo**: *not* a power-averaging use. The pulse width is the command: 1 to 2 ms pulse every 20 ms.

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Edges**: a PWM output has rise and fall times, so every edge carries the harmonic content discussed in [[q0675]] to [[q0678]]. Slower edges cut EMI but lengthen the transition loss in a power stage.
- **Dead time**: in a half-bridge, both switches must never be on together. The dead time (tens of ns to a few hundred ns) shifts the effective duty and adds distortion in class D and motor drives.
- **Min/max duty**: gate-driver propagation delay and bootstrapped high-side drivers limit the maximum duty (bootstrap capacitor must refresh).
- **Spread spectrum**: slightly modulating $f_{PWM}$ spreads the harmonic energy over a wider band and reduces peak EMI readings ([[q0674]]).

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked to "design a PWM-controlled LED dimmer", start with the frequency choice (flicker and camera), then resolution (the eye is roughly logarithmic, so you want fine steps at low duty), then the driver (constant-current vs constant-voltage with series resistor), then EMI. This shows the same four decisions in a real design.
:::

::: applications
- **Switching regulators** (buck, boost): the control loop varies duty to hold $V_{out}$; $V_{out} = DV_{in}$ for a buck in CCM.
- **Motor speed control** with an H-bridge: duty sets average armature voltage and therefore speed.
- **LED dimming**: the LED current is switched at kilohertz rates so the perceived brightness tracks duty without colour shift.
- **PWM DAC**: MCU pin plus RC filter to produce a bias or reference voltage when no DAC is available.
- **Class D audio**: audio signal compared against a triangle carrier to generate a PWM stream at several hundred kHz, filtered by an LC at the speaker.
- **Hobby servos and ESCs**: pulse width encodes position or throttle.
:::

::: assumptions
- **The load averages**: the answer $\bar V = DV_{pk}$ requires that the load or filter responds much more slowly than the PWM period. A load that follows the pulses (a fast logic input, a speaker with no filter at low $f$) sees the raw wave.
- **Ideal switch**: edges are instantaneous and on-resistance is zero. Real switches add a conduction drop ($V_{DS(on)}$) and switching loss.
- **Constant $V_{pk}$**: if the supply droops during the on time or the bus varies, the average no longer equals $DV_{pk}$.
- **First-order filter formulas** assume $\tau\gg T$. For $\tau\sim T$ use the exact exponential expression: $\Delta V = V_{pk}\frac{(1-e^{-DT/\tau})(1-e^{-(1-D)T/\tau})}{1-e^{-T/\tau}}$.
- **Duty polarity**: active-low outputs invert $D$.

Alternative: some interviewers use "PWM" to include variable-frequency modulation (PFM) schemes; this page covers fixed-frequency PWM.
:::

::: followups
### What is the average voltage and RMS voltage of 3.3 V PWM at 30% duty?
Average $= 0.3\times3.3 = 0.99$ V. RMS $= 3.3\sqrt{0.3} = 1.81$ V. A resistive load dissipates $V_{rms}^2/R = 0.3\cdot V_{pk}^2/R$, 30% of full power, not $0.99^2/R$.

### How do you choose the PWM frequency for a brushed DC motor?
Above about 20 kHz so winding whine is inaudible, high enough that the current ripple $V_s D(1-D)/(Lf)$ is a small fraction of the average current, and low enough that switching losses in the bridge are acceptable. With $L = 1$ mH and 12 V at $D = 0.5$, 20 kHz gives about 0.15 A ripple; if the motor draws 2 A that is 7.5% and fine.

### What does the spectrum of a PWM signal look like and why does it matter?
A DC term equal to the average plus harmonics at multiples of $f_{PWM}$, with amplitudes $2V_{pk}|\sin(n\pi D)|/(n\pi)$. It matters because the harmonics are what the filter must remove and what radiates as EMI. See [[q0673]].

### You need 12-bit resolution on a PWM DAC at 20 kHz with a 72 MHz timer clock. Can you?
$f_{clk}/f_{PWM} = 72\,000\,000/20\,000 = 3600$ counts per period, about 11.8 bits, just short of 12 bits (4096). Options: lower the PWM frequency to $72\text{ MHz}/4096 = 17.6$ kHz, use a faster timer or high-resolution PWM, or dither between adjacent duty values and let the filter average them.

### Why does the ripple peak at 50% duty, and what changes with a second filter stage?
Ripple $\propto D(1-D)$, which is maximal at $D=0.5$. A second RC stage multiplies the attenuation at $f_{PWM}$ by another $1/(2\pi f RC)$, giving 40 dB per decade instead of 20, so you can keep the corner frequency (and settling time) while cutting ripple by an order of magnitude or more. The cost is a longer settling time and loading between stages unless you buffer.
