::: basics
### In plain words
An op-amp needs a few connections before it does anything useful: power, two inputs, an output, a feedback path, and a decoupling capacitor. Without feedback it works as a comparator (its output just slams high or low depending on which input is bigger), not as a smooth amplifier. Without a defined steady (DC) voltage at each input, it drifts to a rail, one of the supply limits. And without decoupling, supply noise gets in.

### Picture it
Think of a lamp. It needs a power cord, a switch you control, and a bulb. A lamp with a loose cord flickers. Unplugging one wire leaves it dark. In the same way, each missing op-amp connection gives its own symptom, from a stuck output to oscillation.

### Words you need
- **Supply pins**: V+ and V−. V− is ground in a single-supply design.
- **Floating input**: an input connected to nothing, which will drift.
- **Feedback path**: a resistor from output to the minus input. See [[q0121]].
- **Decoupling capacitor**: usually 100 nF at the supply pins, supplying fast current.
- **Common-mode range**: the input voltages the part can handle.

### The simplest example
Take a single 5 V supply. Connect V+ to 5 V with a 100 nF capacitor right next to it, and V− to ground. Bias the plus input at 2.5 V with two equal resistors. Run a feedback resistor from the output to the minus input. Now every pin has a defined connection, and the output has room to swing both ways around 2.5 V.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the limits to check, and how to tie off unused channels in a dual or quad package.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can take an op-amp from a symbol to a circuit that works on a board. The interviewer wants the full list, not just "two inputs and an output":

1. **Power**: a positive and negative supply (or supply and ground), with local **decoupling capacitors**.
2. **Feedback**: a path from the output back to the inverting input for a linear circuit.
3. **A DC path for each input**: bias currents need somewhere to flow, and inputs must not float.
4. **Operating limits**: input common-mode range, output swing and load.
5. Practical items: unused channels in a dual or quad, offset-null or shutdown pins, single-supply biasing.

Mentioning decoupling and the DC bias path unprompted is what separates people who have built circuits from those who haven't.
:::

::: why
The symbol shows three terminals, and a beginner's circuit "doesn't work" because the other required pieces are invisible. A floating input drifts to a rail. A missing bypass capacitor turns a clean amplifier into an oscillator or a noise source. A single-supply circuit with an input swinging below ground just clips. These are the bugs behind a large share of "why does my op-amp circuit do nothing" debug sessions in the lab. Knowing the full connection list also tells you what to look at first on a misbehaving board: supplies, feedback, bias path.
:::

::: concepts
- **Supply pins**: the op-amp is an active device, the output power comes from the supplies, not the inputs. See [[q0122]].
- **Decoupling / bypass capacitor**: a local capacitor that supplies fast current and shunts supply noise. See [[q0119]] for how amplifiers are biased.
- **Negative feedback path**: from output to the inverting input, which defines the gain. See [[q0124]].
- **Input bias current**: a small DC current into (or out of) each input, so each input needs a DC path to a defined voltage.
- **Common-mode input range / output swing**: the voltages the inputs and output may take relative to the supplies.
- **Single vs dual supply**: with one supply, signals need to be biased to a mid-rail reference.
:::

::: answer
"At minimum you need five things. First, power: a positive supply pin and a negative supply pin, which can be a negative rail or just ground in a single-supply design. Second, the two inputs, which both need a defined DC voltage, so neither is left floating, because the inputs draw a tiny bias current and a floating pin will drift to a rail. Third, the output. For a linear amplifier I also need the fourth thing, a feedback path from the output back to the inverting input, usually a resistor, because without it the op-amp is a comparator.

The fifth thing is the one people forget: a decoupling capacitor, typically 100 nF ceramic, right next to the supply pins, plus a bigger bulk cap nearby. It provides the fast current the output stage needs and keeps supply noise out of the signal.

Then I'd check the limits: the inputs have to stay inside the common-mode range, the output can't go outside the rails, and in a single-supply circuit I bias the signal to mid-supply so it can swing both ways. In a dual or quad package I'd also tie off any unused channels instead of leaving them floating."
:::

::: technical
**Required terminals.** $V_{S+}$, $V_{S-}$ (or GND), IN+, IN−, OUT. Some parts add offset-null pins (for example, trimming a classic precision bipolar part), shutdown or enable, or a reference pin.

**Power.** The total supply $V_{S+} - V_{S-}$ must be within the recommended operating range and below the absolute maximum. A dual supply ($\pm 5$ V, $\pm 15$ V) lets the output swing about 0 V. A single supply requires bias to $V_{S}/2$ (or another reference) for AC signals.

**Decoupling.** Place a 100 nF ceramic capacitor from each supply pin to ground (or between the supplies for dual-supply parts) as close to the pins as possible, plus 1 to 10 µF bulk capacitor on the supply entry. The 100 nF handles tens of MHz while the bulk covers lower frequencies. Without it, the supply inductance and the op-amp's output-stage current transients feed back into the signal and can cause ringing or oscillation.

**Feedback and input paths.** Negative feedback from OUT to IN− (directly for a follower, through a network for gain). Each input needs a DC path. The input bias current flows through the source resistance: $V_{err} = I_B\cdot R_{source}$. For example, 100 nA through 1 MΩ gives 0.1 V.

**Limits.** Inputs must stay within the *common-mode input range* (for non-rail-to-rail parts typically within 1 to 2 V of the rails, per the datasheet). The output swing is $V_{S-} + V_{OL}$ to $V_{S+} - V_{OH}$, and the load current must stay below the datasheet limit. Capacitive loads may need an isolation resistor.

**Unused channels.** Connect as a unity buffer with the non-inverting input tied to a mid-supply or other valid point inside the common-mode range, so the unused amplifier stays in a defined state.
:::

::: short
"Beyond the two inputs and the output, an op-amp needs a positive and negative supply, so power pins, with a 100 nF decoupling capacitor close to them. For a linear amplifier it also needs a feedback path from the output to the inverting input. Both inputs need a DC path to a defined voltage, because bias current flows into them and a floating input drifts to a rail. Then I check the common-mode range and output swing, and in single-supply designs I bias the signal to mid-rail."
:::

::: long
"If I'm wiring one up, I go through it like a checklist. First, power. The op-amp needs a positive supply pin and a negative supply pin. That could be plus and minus 12 volts, or plus 5 and ground in a single-supply design. And right at those pins I put a 100 nanofarad ceramic capacitor to ground, plus a larger capacitor, a few microfarads, somewhere nearby. The output stage draws sharp current pulses, and without local decoupling those pulses go through the supply inductance and show up as noise or ringing, and sometimes oscillation.

Second, the inputs. The op-amp has two, and I need to know the DC voltage on each. They draw a small bias current, anywhere from picoamps to hundreds of nanoamps depending on the part, so I never leave one floating. For example, if I AC-couple a signal in with a capacitor, I need a resistor from that input to a bias voltage to give the bias current a place to go. And they have to stay inside the input common-mode range, which for many parts stops a volt or so short of the rails.

Third, feedback. A linear amplifier needs a resistor network from the output to the inverting input. That sets the gain. If it's missing, I've built a comparator.

Fourth, the output. I make sure the load current is within the part's limit, the signal swing is inside what the output can deliver, and if I drive a cable or other capacitive load, I add a small isolating resistor.

Last, single supply: signals that swing negative need to be level-shifted to mid-supply, usually with a divider and a bypass capacitor, and any unused channels in a dual or quad op-amp get wired as a buffer to a safe voltage rather than left floating."
:::

::: terms
- **Supply rails ($V_{S+}$, $V_{S-}$)**: the power connections; the output cannot exceed them.
- **Decoupling (bypass) capacitor**: a capacitor from the supply pin to ground that provides local charge and shunts noise.
- **Input bias current ($I_B$)**: the small DC current into the inputs; needs a DC return path.
- **Common-mode input range**: the input voltage range over which the op-amp operates correctly.
- **Output swing**: how close the output gets to each rail at a given load.
- **Rail-to-rail (RRIO)**: inputs and/or outputs that operate (almost) all the way to the supplies; "rail-to-rail" claims vary by spec, see the datasheet.
- **Virtual ground / mid-rail reference**: a bias voltage near $V_S/2$ used in single-supply designs.
- **Offset null**: pins on some parts to trim the input offset voltage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "two inputs, an output, and power".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Splits connections into **power (with decoupling), signal (inputs and output), feedback**, and says why each is needed.
- Says that **both inputs need a DC path** and quantifies the error ($I_B R_{source}$).
- Names **100 nF at the pins plus bulk** and explains what fails without it.
- Raises **single-supply biasing** and common-mode range unprompted.
- Mentions unused channels, capacitive load isolation and the datasheet limits.
- Notes that the schematic symbol hides the supply pins, which is why a "complete" schematic still has to show them.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Forgetting the supply.** The most common bring-up bug: "The op-amp is dead" because the supply pins aren't connected, especially the negative one in a dual-supply design.
- **Leaving an input floating**, or AC-coupling an input with no DC return resistor. The output drifts or sticks at a rail. Fix: add a resistor to a bias point.
- **No decoupling**, or placing the capacitor far away or through a long trace. Place it within a few mm of the pin with a short ground return.
- **Single supply with a signal centered on 0 V.** Negative halves clip at ground. Bias to mid-rail and AC-couple, or use a rail-to-rail part that actually reaches the lower rail.
- **Exceeding common-mode input range** with a non-rail-to-rail part. Some older parts can even *phase-reverse* (output flips) when the input exceeds the range. Check the datasheet.
- **Leaving unused amplifiers floating.** An unconnected input in a dual/quad can oscillate and inject noise into the neighboring channel and the supply.
:::

## Power: what the supply pins really do

An op-amp's output power comes from the supply, not the input. The input pair only steers the current. That is also why the output cannot go beyond the rails: the output transistors run out of voltage headroom, so $V_{out}$ typically stops 0.5 to 2 V short for a classic bipolar output and within tens of mV for a rail-to-rail one.

**Decoupling.** When the output slews, the output stage pulls a pulse of current from the supply. The PCB trace to the regulator has inductance. If it's 10 nH, a current edge of 10 mA in 10 ns gives $V = L\,di/dt = 10\text{ nH}\times 10\text{ mA}/10\text{ ns} = 10$ mV of supply disturbance. That disturbance appears at the supply pin, and op-amp PSRR falls with frequency, so it leaks into the output. A 100 nF capacitor placed near the pin supplies that charge locally. Its own series inductance (a few nH including mounting) gives a self-resonance around 11 MHz for 2 nH and 100 nF, above which it behaves inductively. That is why a bulk cap (1 to 10 µF) handles lower frequencies and the ceramic handles higher.

## Inputs need a DC path

An op-amp input is not an open circuit at DC. A bipolar-input part has bias currents of tens of nA to µA. A JFET or CMOS part has pA or fA, but the current is still non-zero and so is leakage. Whatever the source, the input must see a DC path to a point in the common-mode range, or the pin charges up to a rail.

The classic fail case is AC coupling:

The error from bias current is $V_{err} = I_B \times R_{source}$. With $I_B = 100$ nA and $R_b = 1$ MΩ, the offset is 0.1 V. Using a lower value (100 kΩ) cuts it to 10 mV, at the cost of a lower input impedance.

## Single supply versus dual supply

With $\pm 12$ V the signal can swing around 0 V and ground is the natural reference. With a single 5 V rail, ground *is* the negative rail, and a signal centered on 0 V will clip on its negative half. The usual fix is to create a mid-rail reference (two equal resistors and a capacitor, or a buffered reference) and bias the signal around it. In the figure above, that is $V_{REF} = 2.5$ V.

Check the common-mode input range *at that bias point*. For a non-rail-to-rail part, the range might not even include 2.5 V on a 3.3 V supply. And check the output swing: a 5 V single-supply, 10 kΩ-loaded amplifier might reach within a few tens of mV of each rail for a rail-to-rail output, or within 1 to 1.5 V for a classic part.

## Unused channels

A dual or quad op-amp package has shared supply pins. If you use only one channel, tie the unused channels as a unity buffer with the non-inverting input at a valid voltage (for example, mid-supply or ground in a rail-to-rail part's range). A floating input can wander, cause the output to oscillate and couple that noise through the shared supply.

## Debug order

If a new op-amp board does nothing, check in this order:
1. **Supply voltages at the pins** (not just at the connector).
2. **Output stuck at a rail?** Feedback missing or on the wrong input, or an input has no DC path.
3. **Both inputs at DC voltages inside the common-mode range?**
4. **Oscillating?** Add or move the decoupling; check for capacitive load.

## Worked example: how much local charge does the output stage need?

The "100 nF plus bulk" rule is easier to remember once you see what each capacitor is for. Suppose the op-amp on ±12 V drives a 600 Ω load with a 20 kHz sine of 5 V peak.

1. Peak load current: $I_{pk}=5\text{ V}/600\ \Omega=8.33$ mA.
2. A half-cycle of a sine carries charge $Q=I_{pk}/(\pi f)=8.33\text{ mA}/(\pi\cdot20\text{ kHz})=133$ nC out of one supply rail.
3. If a 100 nF capacitor had to supply that whole half-cycle itself, the rail would sag by $\Delta V=Q/C=1.33$ V. That is unacceptable, so the 100 nF is clearly not meant to carry audio-rate load current.
4. A 10 µF bulk capacitor would sag only $133\text{ nC}/10\ \mu\text{F}=13$ mV over the same half-cycle, which is why bulk capacitance sits near the amplifier, and why the regulator and the board's supply loop take over at audio frequencies.

The 100 nF has a different job. It covers the nanosecond-to-microsecond current pulses (edges, slewing, output-stage crossover) that the supply wiring is too inductive to deliver, as computed above. The two values are not redundant; they handle different frequency ranges, and the board needs both. For the small-signal case with a 10 kΩ load, the load current is 0.5 mA, and the stage is dominated by the amplifier's own quiescent current and edge pulses, so a single 100 nF per pin is usually all that is needed.

## Building the mid-rail reference properly

A common single-supply recipe is two equal resistors from a 3.3 V rail with a bypass capacitor. With 10 kΩ each, the node sits at 1.65 V and has a Thevenin resistance of 5 kΩ. With a 10 µF capacitor the time constant is $5\text{ k}\Omega\times10\ \mu\text{F}=50$ ms, so the reference takes about 250 ms (five time constants) to settle after power-up, and the AC-coupled input will show a start-up thump while the bias point charges. The same RC corner, $1/(2\pi\cdot5\text{ k}\Omega\cdot10\ \mu\text{F})=3.2$ Hz, means the reference rejects supply ripple above a few hertz. Two limits to remember: any current drawn from this node, including the bias resistors of every stage that share it, shifts it by $I\times5\text{ k}\Omega$; and its PSRR is only as good as the capacitor, so noise-sensitive designs buffer it with a unity-gain op-amp instead. A buffered reference should also be checked against the buffer's own output-swing and capacitive-load limits.

## Verifying the connections on the bench

Before applying signal, put a multimeter on the actual pins, not the connector: pin $V_{S+}$ to pin $V_{S-}$ should read the full supply, and each input should sit at the expected DC level (for the follower above, 2.5 V on both). For a working negative-feedback stage the two inputs differ by far less than a millivolt; if they differ by volts the loop is open or the output is saturated. Then look at the supply pin with the scope AC-coupled, 20 MHz bandwidth limit off, a short ground spring and the amplifier driving its real load. The ripple should be tens of millivolts or less with no ringing at the output-stage edges. Move the 100 nF from the pin to 2 cm away and repeat: the ringing and noise you see is the supply inductance you were hiding. If the stage oscillates only with a cable attached, add the isolating resistor and re-check.

::: applications
- **Sensor front ends** on a single 3.3 V rail: the signal is biased to mid-rail through a divider and bypass capacitor, with an RRIO op-amp.
- **Audio preamps** powered from ±12 to ±15 V, with large electrolytics at the supply and ceramic capacitors at each op-amp.
- **Photodiode transimpedance amplifiers** where input bias current and leakage must be tiny, so the input has guarded high-impedance routing.
- **Dual and quad op-amp ICs** (for example, general-purpose parts with two or four amplifiers) where unused channels are tied off.
- **Op-amps in battery devices** with RRIO parts and a µA-level quiescent current, where the mid-rail reference must be low-power too.
:::

::: assumptions
The list assumes a voltage-feedback, general-purpose op-amp with two supply pins. **Rail-to-rail** parts remove most of the common-mode and swing concerns but not the need for supplies, decoupling and DC paths. Some **chopper or zero-drift** amplifiers have different noise and clock-injection behavior and may need extra filtering. **Fully differential amplifiers** add common-mode feedback and a $V_{OCM}$ pin. The 100 nF value is a standard starting point: some datasheets require larger or additional values, and for very fast op-amps (hundreds of MHz) you also add small capacitors (1 to 10 nF) and pay attention to layout. A circuit powered by a clean linear supply with the op-amp next to the regulator may need less than a noisy switcher-powered board.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact common-mode input range, output swing and bias current depend on the part and the temperature. Treat the numbers here as typical orders of magnitude and read the datasheet's electrical-characteristics table for your device.
:::

::: followups
### Why 100 nF specifically, and where exactly do you put it?
100 nF is a convenient value that has low enough impedance (about 0.16 Ω at 10 MHz) and small enough size in a 0402 or 0603 package, with a self-resonance often in the 10 to 20 MHz region. Place it within a few millimeters of the supply pin with a short, wide path to ground. A cap far away behaves like an inductor plus the capacitor and loses most of its benefit at high frequency. Many datasheets show the recommended value and layout.

### An op-amp circuit's output sits at the positive rail with no input signal. What do you check?
I check, in order: (1) is feedback connected to the inverting input, not the non-inverting one; (2) does each input have a DC path, so nothing is floating; (3) does the input offset or the DC at the + input exceed what the gain can handle, so the output saturates; (4) are the inputs within the common-mode range. A broken feedback path or a floating input is the usual culprit.

### How would you power an op-amp amplifier from a single 3.3 V rail for an AC sensor signal?
Use a rail-to-rail input/output op-amp, create $V_{REF}\approx 1.65$ V from two equal resistors with a bypass capacitor (buffered if the load on it matters), AC-couple the sensor through a capacitor with a bias resistor to $V_{REF}$, and set gain using resistors referenced to $V_{REF}$ so DC passes at gain 1. Check the output swing at the load.

### What can go wrong if you tie the unused half of a dual op-amp's inputs to ground and its output to nothing?
Depending on the part, inputs at the rail might violate the common-mode range, causing phase reversal or a high supply current, and an open-loop amplifier may saturate and switch, dissipating more power and injecting noise on the supply. The usual practice is to wire it as a follower with the + input at a mid-range voltage.

### Harder: your op-amp circuit works on the bench with a lab supply, but oscillates on the product board powered by a switcher. Why?
The lab supply is quiet and the cables are short. On the product, switching noise and long supply traces with inductance interact with the op-amp's PSRR (which degrades at high frequency) and with the output-stage current pulses. Add or move the 100 nF to the pins, add bulk capacitance, maybe a small RC or ferrite bead filter on the supply for the analog section, and check the loop's phase margin with the actual load capacitance.
:::

"Wire up an opamp" could mean (a) the **bare minimum** to power and use it, (b) the connections to make a **working amplifier**, or (c) the **physical pins** of a package. If the interviewer is thinking of the classic textbook list, the answer is "power supply (±), two inputs, output, and feedback", and the extra details above (decoupling, DC paths) are what make the answer strong.
