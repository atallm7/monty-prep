::: testing
Whether you understand AC coupling as a **high-pass filter** you design deliberately, not just "a cap in series that blocks DC". The interviewer wants:

1. What it does: passes the AC (signal) component and blocks the DC component, so two stages with different DC bias levels can be connected.
2. The hidden resistor: the corner frequency is $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$ where $R$ is the **input resistance seen by the capacitor**, including the bias network.
3. How to choose $C$ from the lowest signal frequency you must pass, and the cost: startup settling time, size, leakage, and distortion from the capacitor's voltage dependence.
4. The need to re-establish a DC bias on the far side (single-supply op-amps, ADC inputs).
:::

::: why
Real signals arrive with DC offsets that the next stage can't tolerate: a microphone output on a 2 V bias, a sensor swinging around ground feeding a single-supply ADC that only accepts 0–3.3 V, an RF amplifier whose bias must not leak back into the previous stage. A coupling capacitor solves that with one part, but if you choose it carelessly you get lost bass response, droopy pulses, slow start-up, or distortion. It's also the simplest practical application of an RC high-pass filter, which makes it a favourite interview topic.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor impedance** ([[q0024]]): $|Z_C|=1/(2\pi fC)$: high at low frequency (blocks), low at high frequency (passes).
- **Differential equation** ([[q0025]]): $i=C\,dv/dt$, so a constant voltage produces no current.
- **RC high-pass filter** ([[q0010]]): $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$, $-3$ dB at $f_c$, $+20$ dB/dec slope below it.
- **Biasing**: a divider or reference sets the DC operating point after the capacitor.
- **Time constant** $\tau=RC$: sets how long the DC level takes to settle at start-up.
- **Capacitor type**: leakage, polarity and voltage coefficient matter for signal fidelity.
:::

::: answer
"An AC coupling capacitor goes in series with a signal path. It passes the changing part of the signal and blocks the DC part. That lets me connect two circuits that sit at different DC levels without one disturbing the other's bias, like a microphone into an amplifier, or a sensor that swings around zero into a single-supply input that biases at half the rail.

Electrically it forms a high-pass filter with the resistance after it, so it isn't an on-off switch. The corner is f equals one over two pi R C, where R is the input resistance the capacitor drives, usually the bias network in parallel. Below that frequency the signal is attenuated, and above it it passes. So I choose C based on the lowest frequency I want to keep: for 20 Hz audio into a 5 kilohm bias network, I want the corner well below 20 Hz, say 3 to 5 hertz, so around 8 to 10 microfarads, and a 2.2 microfarad part would give a corner at 14 Hz and attenuate 20 Hz by almost 2 dB.

Costs: a bigger capacitor means a longer startup, because it charges through R with tau equals R C, so tens of milliseconds, and polarized electrolytics need the right DC polarity, leak a bit, and can add distortion. And I always need to provide a DC bias path after the cap, so the node doesn't float."
:::

::: technical
**Circuit:** source → series $C_c$ → node biased by $R_1$ (to $V_{DD}$) and $R_2$ (to ground) → high-impedance input. The capacitor sees $R_{in}=R_1\parallel R_2$ in series with the source resistance $R_s$ for the corner calculation.

**High-pass transfer:** $\dfrac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}=\dfrac{j\omega\tau}{1+j\omega\tau}$, $\tau=(R_s+R_{in})C_c$, $f_c=\dfrac{1}{2\pi\tau}$.
- Magnitude: $1/\sqrt{1+(f_c/f)^2}$; at $f=f_c$: 0.707 ($-3$ dB), phase $+45^\circ$.
- Phase lead at low frequency: $\arctan(f_c/f)$.

**Design rule:** $C_c\ge\dfrac{1}{2\pi R f_{min}}$ for a $-3$ dB point at $f_{min}$; for flat response (<0.5 dB droop) choose $f_c\le f_{min}/3$ or lower.

**DC behaviour:** at DC no current flows through $C_c$; the output node sits at the bias $V_{bias}=V_{DD}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$. The cap holds $V_C=V_{in,DC}-V_{bias}$. After a DC step of size $\Delta V$ the output decays as $\Delta V\,e^{-t/\tau}$ to the bias; settling to 1 % takes $4.6\tau$.

**Pulses:** for a rectangular pulse of width $T$, droop (tilt) $\approx T/\tau$ for $T\ll\tau$.

**Example:** $R_{in}=10\ \text{k}\Omega\parallel10\ \text{k}\Omega=5$ kΩ. For $f_{min}=20$ Hz and $f_c=4$ Hz: $C=1/(2\pi\cdot5000\cdot4)=7.96\to10$ µF, $\tau=50$ ms, and $f_c=3.2$ Hz.
:::

::: short
"An AC coupling capacitor in series blocks DC and passes AC, so two stages with different DC bias levels can connect. Together with the input resistance after it, it forms a high-pass filter with corner one over two pi R C, so I choose C so that corner is well below the lowest signal frequency. The cost is a startup settling time of a few R C, and I need a bias network after the cap to set the DC level."
:::

::: long
"A coupling capacitor sits in series with the signal. For DC, the capacitor is an open circuit, so no DC current flows and the two sides can sit at different DC voltages. For AC, its impedance is one over two pi f C, which is small at high frequency, so the signal passes through. That lets me connect, for instance, a microphone sitting on a two volt bias into an amplifier biased at half of a single supply without them fighting each other.

But it's not a switch, it's a high-pass filter. The capacitor and the resistance to ground after it, normally the bias network in parallel with the amplifier's input, make an RC high-pass with a corner at one over two pi R C. Below that frequency the signal is attenuated by 20 dB per decade and the phase leads. So I pick C from the lowest frequency I need to pass. If R is 5 kilohms and I want 20 hertz flat, I want a corner at about 4 hertz, which means about 8 microfarads, so I'd use 10. A 2.2 microfarad part gives a 14.5 hertz corner and would knock 20 hertz down by about 1.8 dB.

The trade-offs: a big capacitor charges through R, so the startup settling time is a few tau, 50 milliseconds here. If the input is a pulse train, the pulse tops droop in proportion to width over tau. A big electrolytic may leak, is polarized, and has ESR and voltage dependence. A class-two ceramic changes capacitance with the signal voltage, which causes distortion, so for audio I'd use film or C0G.

And the bias: after the cap, the node has no DC path, so I have to provide one, with a divider or a reference, which also sets the input resistance in the filter equation. The source resistance adds to that R as well."
:::

::: terms
- **AC coupling / DC blocking**: series capacitor that passes AC and blocks DC.
- **High-pass corner $f_c$**: $1/(2\pi RC)$, the −3 dB point.
- **Bias network**: resistors that set the DC operating point on the far side.
- **Time constant $\tau$**: $RC$, the settling time scale after a DC step.
- **Droop / tilt**: sag of a flat pulse top through a high-pass.
- **Single-supply bias (mid-rail)**: $V_{DD}/2$ reference allowing AC signals to swing both ways.
- **DC bias on the capacitor**: DC voltage across the capacitor, set by the difference in DC levels.
- **Voltage coefficient**: change of capacitance with applied voltage, causing distortion.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it blocks DC and passes AC".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Identifies the high-pass filter and writes $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$ with the correct R (bias network ∥ input, plus source resistance).
- Sizes C from a low-frequency requirement with a number and checks attenuation at the lowest frequency.
- Talks about start-up transients and settling, pulse droop, and sizing to avoid long recovery after overload.
- Chooses capacitor type for the application (film/C0G for audio linearity, polarized electrolytic orientation, ceramic bias dependence).
- Mentions the need for a DC bias path and shows the divider; considers leakage current effects on bias.
- Mentions the trade-off with low-frequency response vs. noise/settling, and bigger issues like click/pop on switching.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Forgetting the bias resistor.** With no DC path, the node floats, drifts or charges up to a rail through leakage.
- **Ignoring the filter.** Using a 100 nF coupling cap into a 10 kΩ input gives $f_c=159$ Hz and removes the bass.
- **Using the wrong R in $f_c$.** The capacitor sees source resistance plus the *parallel* bias network, not just one resistor.
- **Putting a polarized electrolytic in backwards** or with near-zero DC across it, in which case the polarity may reverse.
- **Using a high-K ceramic for low-distortion audio.** Its capacitance moves with signal voltage; use C0G or film.
- **Making the cap huge "to be safe".** Start-up time scales with C, and leakage and size grow.
- **Assuming it blocks all low frequencies equally.** It attenuates progressively, $-20$ dB/dec, and shifts phase near and above $f_c$.
:::

## Why it blocks DC and passes AC

$i=C\,dv/dt$ ([[q0025]]). For a constant voltage, $dv/dt=0$ and no current flows: open circuit. For a sinusoid, $|I|=\omega C|V|$: the higher the frequency, the more current for the same voltage, i.e. lower impedance $1/(\omega C)$ ([[q0024]]). Put it in series with a resistance $R$ to ground and you get the voltage divider $Z_R/(Z_R+Z_C)$:

$$\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}=\frac{R}{R+\frac{1}{j\omega C}}=\frac{j\omega RC}{1+j\omega RC}.$$

That's the high-pass response, with $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$.

## Choosing C: a worked example

Requirements: audio, flat from 20 Hz; bias network 10 kΩ/10 kΩ to a 3.3 V rail; source resistance 600 Ω.

- $R_{eff}=600+5000=5.6$ kΩ.
- For $f_c=20/5=4$ Hz: $C=1/(2\pi\cdot5600\cdot4)=7.1$ µF → standard 10 µF gives $f_c=2.84$ Hz.
- Droop at 20 Hz: $20\log_{10}(1/\sqrt{1+(2.84/20)^2})=-0.09$ dB.
- Time constant: $\tau=5.6\ \text{k}\cdot10\ \mu\text{F}=56$ ms; the bias settles to 1 % in 260 ms.
- Capacitor: 10 µF film is large; a 10 µF X7R/X5R is small but its capacitance drops and varies with voltage. For small signals around a fixed bias the distortion may be acceptable; for hi-fi audio, film or a polymer/electrolytic bipolar are used.

## Time-domain behavior: startup and DC steps

When the DC input changes by $\Delta V$ the capacitor can't change its voltage instantly ([[q0037]]), so the output node jumps by the same $\Delta V$ and then relaxes to the bias with time constant $\tau$:

$$v_{out}(t)=V_{bias}+\Delta V\,e^{-t/\tau}.$$

Figure: A DC step on the input appears at first on the output (the capacitor holds its charge) and then decays exponentially back to the bias. This is why powering up or plugging in a source causes a "thump" in audio and why an overloaded stage takes time to recover.

For a **pulse train**, the same time constant causes droop: a pulse of width $T=1$ ms through $\tau=56$ ms droops by about $T/\tau=1.8$ %. And for a unipolar signal with changing duty cycle the *average* DC shifts with the duty cycle: the output baseline wanders. That is the issue behind "DC restoration" circuits in video and digital links (and why encoded links like 8b/10b are DC-balanced).

## Practical issues

1. **Bias point and input leakage:** a bias divider of 5 kΩ and an op-amp with 100 nA bias current gives 0.5 mV shift, fine; with a 10 MΩ bias the same current gives 1 V. Choose the bias R and keep leakage in mind (capacitor leakage plus input bias current).
2. **Source resistance matters:** it adds to $R$ in the corner and attenuates the signal as a divider with $R_{in}$ when large.
3. **Polarity of electrolytics:** the DC across the capacitor is the difference of the two DC levels; orient + toward the more positive node. If the sign can reverse, use two back-to-back capacitors or a bipolar (non-polarized) electrolytic.
4. **Voltage coefficient:** class-II ceramics (X7R, X5R) lose capacitance with DC bias. In a coupling application that is both a change in $f_c$ and a source of distortion because the capacitance varies with the signal.
5. **Switch-on thump:** a large capacitor charging through R produces a transient on the output; mute during start-up or pre-charge the bias.
6. **Series resistance and noise:** a larger R gives more thermal noise; a larger C is not noisy.
7. **RF/digital:** for GHz links, DC-blocking capacitors are chosen for SRF and low insertion loss ([[q0031]]); SERDES lines use 100 nF or similar caps on both lines of a differential pair.

## Alternatives to AC coupling

- **DC-coupled with level shift/offset** (op-amp summing): preserves DC and low frequencies but needs a stable offset source.
- **Transformer coupling**: isolation plus DC blocking at higher cost.
- **AC-coupled with servo/DC restoration**: a slow feedback loop removes offset with a gentler corner than a large capacitor.
- **Digital highpass filter** after the ADC if the front end can tolerate the offset.
:::

::: applications
- **Audio chains**: input and output coupling capacitors on preamps and headphone amplifiers.
- **Single-supply op-amp stages** that bias the signal at mid-supply ([[q0012]]).
- **RF and high-speed links**: DC blocks on transmission lines and SERDES transmitters/receivers.
- **Sensor and oscilloscope inputs**: the scope's "AC coupling" mode is a series capacitor (~1–10 Hz corner).
- **Bias-tee networks** that separate DC power from RF.
- **Transistor amplifier stages** (common-emitter, common-source) coupling stage to stage.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear first-order high-pass** using a single series C and a resistance to a bias level.
- The capacitor is ideal (no leakage, ESR, voltage coefficient). Those effects are discussed above.
- Source resistance is small compared to the input resistance unless stated; otherwise add it to $R$.
- The load after the bias network is high impedance (op-amp or ADC input); otherwise it joins the parallel resistance.
- The answer changes for RF: coupling capacitor value then depends on SRF and impedance matching rather than the audio corner.
:::

::: followups
### What is the corner frequency of a 100 nF coupling capacitor into a 10 kΩ input?
$f_c=1/(2\pi\cdot10^4\cdot10^{-7})=159$ Hz. That's far too high for audio; it removes everything below 159 Hz. Use at least 1 µF (15.9 Hz) or 10 µF.

### Why is the input resistance of the next stage important to the coupling capacitor?
It's the "R" in the RC. A high-impedance input (10 MΩ) allows a small capacitor (1 nF for 16 Hz), but a low-impedance input (50 Ω RF) needs a larger C ($1/(2\pi\cdot50\cdot f)$: 100 nF for 32 kHz).

### How would you avoid a start-up pop in an audio amplifier with a large coupling capacitor?
Pre-charge the bias node or hold the output muted until the DC settles (a few $\tau$), use a soft-start for the bias reference, or reduce the capacitor with a lower R bias. Many headphone amps use a mute relay or a DC servo.

### What if the DC difference across the capacitor is 12 V but the capacitor is rated for 6.3 V?
It will be overstressed; capacitors must be rated for the DC across them, with margin (and for class-II ceramics, an even higher rating because of bias derating). Choose a ≥ 25 V part or re-think the bias levels.

### Does AC coupling work for a signal that is a slowly varying DC voltage?
No. Below the corner frequency it attenuates strongly, so a 0.1 Hz variation through a 3 Hz corner is attenuated by 30× (about 30 dB). For slowly varying signals use DC coupling, a chopper or a precision amplifier with offset cancellation.
:::
