::: basics
### In plain words
During the buck's off state, the inductor current flows through the bottom transistor. But for a short dead time after the top one turns off, neither transistor is on. The current then flows through the bottom transistor's built-in body diode, which pulls the switch node about 0.7 V below ground. Then the channel turns on and the node returns close to 0 V.

### Picture it
Picture a relay baton pass where the first runner drops out before the second takes over. For a moment the baton must go through a narrow side door instead of the open track. The side door is the body diode: it works, but it costs more effort, shown as a deeper dip below zero on the waveform.

### Words you need
- **Body diode**: the diode that is part of every MOSFET, from source to drain.
- **Dead time**: the gap when both transistors are off.
- **Switch node (SW)**: the node whose voltage we draw.
- **Forward drop**: the voltage across a conducting diode, about 0.7 V for a body diode.
- **Reverse recovery**: the brief backward current when the diode switches off.

### The simplest example
Say 2 A flows in the inductor. During the 20 ns dead time, the body diode carries it and the switch node sits at about $-0.7$ V. Then the low-side transistor turns on, and with 10 mΩ it drops $2 \times 0.01 = 20$ mV, so the node sits at $-0.02$ V. Described in words, the waveform is a square wave from about 0 to the input voltage with a small negative shelf at each edge. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page draws it and explains the ringing.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can follow **the inductor current through every sub-interval of a synchronous buck cycle** and draw the switch-node waveform to match, including the short dead-time features that most people omit. The interviewer is looking for:

1. A clear statement of which devices are on or off in each interval, and where the inductor current goes.
2. That the low-side body diode conducts during dead time (SW sits about one diode drop below ground), and is *off* (blocking $V_{in}$) when the high side is on.
3. A correct sketch: $V_{in}$ plateau, small negative steps at both edges, near-zero plateau during low-side conduction, and a ringing overshoot at the rising edge.
4. Awareness of reverse recovery as what the body diode does when it is turned off at the next high-side turn-on.
:::

::: why
The switch-node waveform is the most-probed waveform on any buck: it is what you measure first on the bench, it shows duty cycle, dead time, ringing and conduction mode at a glance. The little negative shelves at the edges are where the body diode lives, and they explain real efficiency, EMI and reliability issues ([[q0209]], [[q0210]]). A candidate who can draw them and explain them shows they have really looked at SW on a scope.
:::

::: concepts
- **Body diode**: the parasitic P-N junction between source and drain; in an NFET its anode is the source, cathode the drain ([[q0201]]).
- **Inductor current continuity**: current must continue through some path when a switch opens ([[q0202]]).
- **Dead time**: both gates off, the body diode carries the current ([[q0207]]).
- **Channel conduction vs diode conduction**: once the channel is on, the lower drop of $I\,R_{DS(on)}$ takes over from the diode.
- **Reverse recovery**: charge stored in the diode must be removed before it blocks again ([[q0209]]).
:::

::: answer
"In the off state of the buck, the high side is off and the inductor current has to continue, so it flows through the low side. There are two sub-intervals. First is the dead time, both gates off, so the current has no channel to flow through and it goes through the low-side FET's body diode. The diode's anode is the source, which is ground, and the cathode is the drain, which is the switch node, so SW gets pulled to about minus 0.7 to minus 1 V, one diode drop below ground. Then the low-side FET turns on and the channel takes over. The channel drop $I\cdot R_{DS(on)}$ is tens of millivolts, so SW comes up to just under zero.

At the end of the off state the low side turns off again, another dead time, and SW goes back to minus one diode drop. Then the high side turns on, SW jumps to $V_{in}$ with some ringing, and the body diode, which was conducting, turns off through reverse recovery, which adds a spike.

So the waveform is a roughly 0 to $V_{in}$ square wave with duty $D$, with a small negative shelf at each transition for the length of the dead time, and ringing at the rising edge. During the on state the body diode is reverse biased by the full input voltage."
:::

::: technical
**States per cycle** (CCM, $I_L>0$, high-side NFET $Q_1$, low-side NFET $Q_2$):

| Interval | $Q_1$ | $Q_2$ | Current path | $V_{SW}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| On | on | off | $V_{in}\to Q_1\to L$; $Q_2$ body diode reverse biased by $V_{in}$ | $\approx V_{in}-I R_{DS(on),1}$ |
| Dead time 1 | off | off | ground $\to Q_2$ body diode $\to$ SW $\to L$ | $\approx -V_{SD}$ |
| Off (sync) | off | on | ground $\to Q_2$ channel $\to$ SW $\to L$ | $\approx -I R_{DS(on),2}$ |
| Dead time 2 | off | off | body diode again | $\approx -V_{SD}$ |

**Body diode drop.** For a silicon power MOSFET $V_{SD}\approx0.7$ to $1.0$ V at rated current, falling by about 2 mV/°C. Compare with the channel drop $2\text{ A}\times10\text{ mΩ}=20$ mV. The channel only takes over when $I R_{DS(on)} < V_{SD}$, true for all practical currents.

**Dead-time loss.** $P_{dt}=V_{SD}\,I\,(t_{d1}+t_{d2})\,f_{sw}$. For 0.75 V, 2 A, 50 ns total, 500 kHz: 37.5 mW (0.6% of 6.6 W).

**Reverse recovery.** When $Q_1$ turns on and the diode is forced off, the stored charge $Q_{rr}$ flows as reverse current through $Q_1$ from $V_{in}$: loss $\approx Q_{rr}V_{in}f_{sw}$ (20 nC: 120 mW) and rings the SW node with the loop inductance.

**Rising edge.** SW overshoots $V_{in}$ and rings at $f_{ring}=1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{loop}C_{oss}})$ (tens to hundreds of MHz). Falling edge: SW undershoots slightly below $-V_{SD}$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $V_{SD}$, $Q_{rr}$ and ring frequency depend on the device, layout and current. The numbers here are representative for a 12 V, 2 A converter with a 20 to 30 V rated FET.
:::

::: short
"In the off state the inductor current keeps flowing through the low side. During the dead time both FETs are off, so it flows through the low-side body diode and SW sits about one diode drop, 0.7 to 1 V, below ground. Then the low-side channel turns on and SW comes up to about minus $I\,R_{DS(on)}$, tens of millivolts. Before the high side turns on there's another dead-time shelf. When the high side turns on the diode is forced off through reverse recovery, SW rises to $V_{in}$ with ringing. During the on state the body diode just blocks $V_{in}$."
:::

::: long
"Let me walk the cycle. I'm assuming CCM and a synchronous bridge.

On state: the high side is on, SW is at about $V_{in}$, the inductor ramps up. The low-side FET is off, and its body diode, with the source at ground and the drain at SW, is reverse-biased by the full input voltage. So it's just blocking.

Then the high side turns off. The inductor current can't change instantly, so it has to find a path. The low side is still off for the dead time, so the only path is its body diode. SW is pulled down until the diode conducts, to about minus 0.7 to minus 1 V for a silicon body diode. That's the first negative shelf, 10 to 50 ns long typically.

Then the low-side gate goes high and the channel turns on. The drop falls from the diode's 0.7 V to $I\,R_{DS(on)}$, say 20 mV at 2 A and 10 mΩ, so SW rises to almost zero. The inductor current ramps down while SW sits just below ground.

Before the next cycle the low side turns off, again for the dead time, so SW dips again to minus one diode drop. Then the high side turns on. SW slews to $V_{in}$ in a few nanoseconds, and rings at some tens or hundreds of megahertz with the loop inductance and the FETs' output capacitance. At the same time the body diode, which was conducting, has to turn off. It needs to remove its stored charge, so a reverse-recovery current flows through the high side for a few nanoseconds, adding to the spike and loss.

If I sketch SW it's a pulse from about zero to $V_{in}$ with width $DT$, little negative shelves at each edge as long as the dead time, and ringing on the rising edge. If the interviewer wants the async version, the Schottky does the same job with about minus 0.4 V and no recovery."
:::

::: terms
- **Body (intrinsic) diode**: the source-to-drain P-N junction of a MOSFET.
- **$V_{SD}$ (forward drop)**: voltage across the body diode when conducting.
- **Dead time**: both devices off so they never overlap.
- **Switch node ringing**: oscillation of SW after a fast edge, from loop inductance and capacitance.
- **Reverse recovery ($Q_{rr}$, $t_{rr}$)**: the time/charge required to turn a conducting diode off.
- **Undershoot / overshoot**: SW below ground / above $V_{in}$.
- **CCM / DCM**: continuous or discontinuous inductor current.
- **Diode conduction vs channel conduction**: two ways the low side carries current.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "It conducts during dead time and SW goes to minus 0.7 V."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Walks all four intervals and states what each device does, including that the body diode is reverse biased (blocking) when the high side is on.
- Explains why the channel takes over (lower drop) and when (once the gate is high enough).
- Gives numbers: $V_{SD}$ about 0.7 to 1 V vs 20 mV, shelf length 10 to 50 ns, dead-time loss $V_{SD}It_df$.
- Mentions reverse recovery at the next high-side turn-on, ringing, and the negative-SW-voltage rating of the controller.
- Contrasts with the async Schottky and notes the DCM variant (diode emulation: SW floats and rings).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The body diode is on during the whole off time."** Only during dead time; after that the channel takes over because its drop is much lower.
- **Drawing SW going to exactly 0 V.** It goes below ground by one diode drop, which is a real negative voltage with design consequences.
- **Forgetting the diode is reverse-biased in the on state.** It blocks $V_{in}$; its leakage and $C_{oss}$ matter, but it conducts no current.
- **Orientation errors.** For an NFET the body diode's anode is the source (ground for $Q_2$) and cathode is the drain (SW). Current flows from ground to SW, so SW goes negative.
- **Treating the high-side body diode as part of the story.** It stays reverse biased unless SW overshoots above $V_{in}$ (ringing), when it clamps.
:::

## Why the body diode conducts

The inductor current $I_L$ is flowing into the inductor from SW. With $Q_1$ off and $Q_2$ off the only device that can supply current into SW is $Q_2$'s body diode (current from ground to SW). The inductor drives SW negative until the diode turns on: $V_{SW}=-V_{SD}$.

After the dead time the controller turns on $Q_2$. The channel is a resistor in parallel with the diode. Both share the same $V_{DS}$ ($=-I R_{ch}$ when the channel takes the current), and the diode current is exponential in voltage: at 20 mV the diode carries essentially nothing. So the channel takes over almost completely. The charge stored in the diode while it conducted decays by recombination (and partly flows out through the channel), so the stored charge that matters for $Q_{rr}$ is what has accumulated in the *second* dead time, just before the high side turns on. That is why shorter dead time reduces recovery loss as well as conduction loss.

## What the diode does during each state

| State | Body diode of $Q_2$ | Body diode of $Q_1$ |
|---|---|---|
| High side on | Reverse biased by $V_{in}$ (blocking) | Reverse biased (drain at $V_{in}$ above source) by a few mV |
| Dead time 1 | Conducting at $-V_{SD}$ | Reverse biased by $V_{in}+V_{SD}$ |
| Low side on | Off (shunted by channel) | Reverse biased by $V_{in}$ |
| Dead time 2 | Conducting at $-V_{SD}$ | Reverse biased |
| Next high side on | Being turned off: $Q_{rr}$ flows | Not involved (unless SW overshoots above $V_{in}$) |

## Losses tied to the body diode

- **Conduction**: $P_{dt}=V_{SD}\,I\,(t_{d1}+t_{d2})\,f_{sw}=0.75\times2\times50\text{ ns}\times500\text{ kHz}=37.5$ mW.
- **Recovery**: $Q_{rr}V_{in}f_{sw}$. With $Q_{rr}=20$ nC: $20\text{ nC}\times12\text{ V}\times500\text{ kHz}=120$ mW. This is bigger than the conduction term; it is the more important one at higher $V_{in}$.
- **$C_{oss}$ energy**: part of the SW transition; not a body-diode loss but related.

> [!TIP]
> If asked "draw the SW waveform", state the assumptions first: CCM, synchronous, dead time $t_d$. Draw the flat tops, then add the dead-time shelves and the ringing, and say what each feature represents.

## DCM variant and diode emulation

If the controller turns $Q_2$ off at zero current (diode emulation), the current stops at zero and SW floats: the node rings at the resonance of the inductor with the SW capacitances before settling at $V_{out}$. That "DCM ring" is not body-diode conduction; the body diode is off since the current is zero. If the controller turns $Q_2$ off *late*, a small negative current flows through the channel and then through $Q_1$'s body diode into $V_{in}$ as SW rises above it ([[q0203]]).

## Worked example: where the ring comes from and what it costs

The rising-edge plot above uses a 150 MHz ring. Here is where a number like that comes from, so you can reproduce it for a real board. When $Q_1$ turns on, the stored charge in $Q_2$'s body diode is swept out and the diode stops conducting abruptly. At that instant the loop formed by $C_{in}$, $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ looks like a small series inductance $L_{loop}$ driving $Q_2$'s output capacitance. Take illustrative values of $L_{loop}=3$ nH and $C_{oss}=400$ pF:

$$f_{ring}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{loop}C_{oss}}}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{3\ \text{nH}\times400\ \text{pF}}}\approx145\ \text{MHz}, \qquad Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{L_{loop}}{C_{oss}}}=2.7\ \Omega$$

When the diode snaps off with a loop current $I_{rr}$ still flowing, the inductor's energy $\tfrac12L_{loop}I_{rr}^2$ transfers into the capacitor, so the node overshoots by

$$\Delta V\approx I_{rr}\sqrt{\frac{L_{loop}}{C_{oss}}}=1.5\ \text{A}\times2.7\ \Omega\approx4.1\ \text{V}$$

That is the 16 V peak on a 12 V rail in the figure. The energy involved is only $\tfrac12\times3\ \text{nH}\times(1.5\ \text{A})^2=3.4$ nJ, but it is dumped every cycle, and it radiates. Because overshoot scales with $\sqrt{L_{loop}}$, halving the loop inductance cuts the overshoot by 30%, not by half, and it moves the ring frequency up by 41% into a band where the radiation is more efficient. Tight layout and a snubber attack the problem from two sides.

**RC snubber design.** A common starting point is to put a resistor equal to $Z_0$ in series with a capacitor of about three to four times $C_{oss}$ from SW to ground. Here $R\approx2.7\ \Omega$ and $C\approx1.4$ nF. It takes the ring and damps it in a cycle or two. The price is that the snubber capacitor is charged to $V_{in}$ and discharged each cycle, dissipating about $C_{sn}V_{in}^2f_{sw}=1.4\ \text{nF}\times144\times500\ \text{kHz}=0.10$ W regardless of load. Compare that with the 37.5 mW dead-time term above: a snubber that cures the ring can cost more than the body diode does, which is why it is added only when EMI or a voltage rating demands it, and why slower gate edges are tried first.

## Why a short dead time helps twice

Return to the loss list. With $V_{SD}=0.75$ V, 2 A and 25 ns per edge, the conduction loss is 37.5 mW. The recovery term depends on how much charge accumulated, which grows with the time the diode conducted. A diode carrying 2 A for 25 ns stores roughly the same order as its rated $Q_{rr}$ (the datasheet number is measured at specific current and $di/dt$). So each nanosecond removed from the second dead time cuts a conduction term and a recovery term. The limit is shoot-through: the margin must cover gate-driver delay variation, FET threshold spread over temperature, and the finite turn-off time of the FET that is going off. Adaptive dead time, which watches SW or the gate voltage, reduces the margin without risking overlap.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $Q_{rr}$, $C_{oss}$ and loop inductance vary widely with part, current, $di/dt$ and layout. The values in this section are illustrative for the exercise, not from a specific device.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Get the probe right first.** A 150 MHz ring has meaningful harmonics out to several hundred MHz, so use at least a 500 MHz to 1 GHz scope and probe with a ground spring or a coaxial tip. A 15 cm ground lead is on the order of 100 nH. It does not change the board's ring, but it resonates with the probe's roughly 10 pF tip capacitance near $1/(2\pi\sqrt{100\ \text{nH}\times10\ \text{pF}})\approx160$ MHz, right on top of the ring you are trying to measure, and adds overshoot and ringing that are not on the board.
2. **Read the shelf.** With the scope offset so ground sits near the top of the screen, measure the negative shelf and confirm it matches $-V_{SD}$ at the load current and temperature you are running. A shelf near $-0.4$ V in a part with a Schottky in parallel, or near $-20$ mV when the shelf is the channel, tells you which path is conducting.
3. **Measure the dead time.** Use the 50% crossing of the falling edge of $G_H-V_{SW}$ and the rising edge of $G_L$. Compare with the gap between the SW step and the point where SW rises to its channel level.
4. **Log the ring.** Capture the rising edge on a single shot. Measure peak overshoot and the ring frequency. Then add a candidate snubber (start with the $Z_0$ rule above) and verify both fall. Check the dissipation with a thermal camera or a power meter at no load, which isolates the load-independent snubber term.
5. **Check a gate resistor trade.** Increase $R_g$ on $Q_1$ in steps and plot overshoot versus efficiency. The best compromise is usually the smallest $R_g$ that keeps overshoot inside the part's rating with margin.
:::

::: applications
- **Scope validation of any synchronous buck**: SW at the right edge looks for dead-time shelves, overshoot and ringing; the shelf depth and width show body-diode conduction.
- **Dead-time optimization in motor drivers and inverters**: measuring the shelf to tune dead time.
- **EMI debugging**: ringing on the rising edge is where much of the radiated energy originates; snubbers target it.
- **Controllers with negative-SW-voltage rating**: datasheets specify SW pin transient minimums like $-1$ V to $-5$ V because of this shelf and ringing.
- **Efficiency characterization**: efficiency vs dead time curves show the body-diode contribution.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Synchronous buck in CCM with silicon NFETs.** For asynchronous, the Schottky replaces the body-diode shelf at about $-0.4$ V and has no recovery.
- **GaN FETs** have no body diode; reverse conduction through the channel gives about 1.5 to 3 V drop during dead time, so SW shows a deeper shelf and dead time matters more.
- **Dead time** of tens of nanoseconds; if it is long enough the shelf is a flat region, otherwise the edges blend.
- **Ring frequency and overshoot** are illustrative; real values come from loop inductance, $C_{oss}$ and edge speed.
- **High-side body diode** is assumed reverse biased; ringing above $V_{in}$ can briefly forward bias it.
- In **DCM** or **PFM**, the shelf may be absent.
:::

::: followups
### Why does SW go negative at all?
Because the inductor current cannot stop and the only available path into SW from the low-side network is ground through the body diode. The inductor forces SW below ground until that diode conducts, at about $-V_{SD}$. Without a diode (or channel) SW would keep falling ([[q0202]]).

### Does the body diode conduct while the low-side FET is on?
Essentially not. The diode and the channel share the same $V_{DS}$. The channel's drop $I R_{DS(on)}$ (20 mV at 2 A and 10 mΩ) is far below the 0.6 V needed to turn the diode on appreciably, and the diode current is exponential in voltage, so it carries negligible current. At very high current $I R_{DS(on)}$ could approach the diode drop, but that is far from typical.

### How would the waveform change at a lower load, around 0.3 A, in diode-emulation mode?
SW would follow the same shape until the inductor current reaches zero. Then $Q_2$ turns off and SW floats: it jumps from near 0 V and rings around $V_{out}$ at the resonance of $L$ with the SW node capacitance (DCM ringing), decaying toward $V_{out}$ until the next high-side turn-on. The body-diode shelf exists only at the start of the off time.

### The controller datasheet limits SW to $-0.3$ V. What do you do?
A body-diode shelf at $-0.75$ V violates that, so either the part is wrong for the application, or you add a Schottky in parallel with $Q_2$ (to clamp at $-0.4$ V; requires a short connection), reduce dead time or add a series resistor in SW's path. In an integrated converter, the datasheet limit is usually transient-rated (for example $-1$ V for a few ns); read the footnotes.

### What if the dead time is much longer than necessary?
The negative shelf is wider, so $V_{SD}It_df_{sw}$ grows, the diode stores more charge (more $Q_{rr}$ loss at the next turn-on) and ripple and EMI content shift. At 1 MHz with 100 ns total dead time and 2 A the dead-time conduction loss alone becomes 150 mW.
:::

::: interpretations
"Off state" in the section note means the high-side FET is off and the low side or diode conducts. The question asks about *the internal body diode* "in the off state", so I treat it as the body diode of the low-side NFET during that off state, with the dead-time subtlety, and also say what it does while the high side is on. If you were asked about the *high-side* FET's body diode, it stays reverse biased in normal operation. The waveform to draw is the switch node under synchronous rectification with dead time (compare Q185).
