::: testing
Whether you know the **common numbers by heart** (9600, 115200 and the family around them) and, better, whether you know where they come from and what limits them. A strong answer ties the list to a calculation: bit time, bytes per second, and the divider error you get from a given clock.

Hidden sub-skills: baud vs bits per second, 8N1 overhead, why the list is 9600 x 2^n (and 57600 = 6 x 9600), why some clocks cannot hit some rates, and how to choose a baud for a given job.
:::

::: why
Baud rate is the one setting both ends of a UART link must get right. You will set it on every console, GPS, modem and bootloader you ever touch, and a mismatch is the most common reason for a dead link. Knowing the list saves you time, and knowing the divider arithmetic saves you from the "my 16 MHz part is 3.5% off at 115200" surprise that causes intermittent failures in production.
:::

::: concepts
- **Baud vs bit rate**: baud is symbols per second; for plain UART each symbol is one bit, so 115200 baud is 115200 bit/s on the wire. Payload is lower because of framing. See [[q0771]].
- **Frame overhead**: 8N1 sends 10 bits per byte, so throughput is baud/10 bytes/s.
- **Baud-rate generator**: divides the peripheral clock by an integer or fractional divisor. See [[q0772]].
- **Divider error**: the actual baud differs from the target by the rounding of the divisor; the budget is a few percent. See [[q0778]].
- **Oversampling**: the receiver clock is 8x or 16x the baud, which sets the divisor granularity. See [[q0779]].
- **Crystal choice**: clocks that are integer multiples of 1.8432 MHz hit all the classic rates exactly.
:::

::: answer
"The classic list is 300, 1200, 2400, 4800, 9600, 19200, 38400, 57600 and 115200 baud, and then the higher ones: 230400, 460800, 921600, and 1 or 3 megabaud on modern parts. The two I use most are 9600, the old default for GPS modules and plenty of sensors, and 115200, the usual default for debug consoles.

Baud is bits per second on the wire, and with the standard 8N1 frame each byte costs ten bits, so 115200 baud gives you about 11.5 kilobytes per second, and 9600 gives you 960 bytes per second.

Where do those numbers come from? They're multiples of 9600 for the most part, and they came from dividing a 1.8432 MHz clock by a power of two. Divide 1.8432 MHz by 16 and you get 115200 exactly. That's why old serial hardware used crystals like 1.8432 or 14.7456 MHz: every classic baud rate comes out with zero error.

On a modern MCU with a 16 MHz or 48 MHz clock you often can't hit the target exactly. A 16 MHz clock with a 16x oversampling divider gets you 111 kbaud for 115200, which is 3.5% low. That's inside the budget but eats margin, so I'd use a fractional divider or a different clock."
:::

| 230,400 | 4.34 us | 43.4 us | 23,040 B/s |
| 460,800 | 2.17 us | 21.7 us | 46,080 B/s |
| 921,600 | 1.085 us | 10.85 us | 92,160 B/s |
| 1,000,000 | 1.0 us | 10 us | 100,000 B/s |

Lower legacy rates (300, 600, 1200, 2400, 4800) still exist. MIDI is a fixed 31,250 baud. DMX512 runs at 250,000 baud (8N2) on an RS-485 line.

**Divider arithmetic.** With oversampling factor $N$ (8 or 16) and peripheral clock $f_{clk}$:

$$\text{baud}_{actual} = \frac{f_{clk}}{N\cdot D},\qquad \text{error} = \frac{\text{baud}_{actual}}{\text{baud}_{target}} - 1$$

where $D$ is an integer (or fixed-point) divisor. At $f_{clk}=16$ MHz and $N=16$, $D = 16\text{ MHz}/(16\times 115200) = 8.68 \to 9$ gives 111,111 baud, error $-3.55\%$. With a 4-bit fractional part ($D = 8 + 11/16 = 8.6875$) the baud is 115,108, error $-0.08\%$.

**Why 1.8432 MHz?** $1.8432\text{ MHz}/16 = 115{,}200$ exactly. All the classic rates are $115200/n$ for integer $n$ (57600 = /2, 38400 = /3, 19200 = /6, 9600 = /12), so 1.8432 MHz or any multiple (3.6864, 7.3728, 11.0592, 14.7456 MHz) yields zero error. 14.7456 MHz is eight times that, and also reaches 921600 exactly.

**Budget.** Sender plus receiver mismatch should stay under roughly 3 to 4% (theoretical limit about 5%): see [[q0778]].

**Throughput.** $\text{bytes/s} = \text{baud}/10$ for 8N1; with parity or two stop bits it is baud/11.
:::

In practice I see 9600 on GPS modules and older sensors, and 115200 as the default for debug consoles and Bluetooth or Wi-Fi modules.

Baud means symbols per second, and for ordinary UART one symbol is one bit. So 115200 baud is 115200 bits a second on the wire. With 8N1 framing, each byte takes ten bits, a start bit, eight data bits and a stop bit, so you only get 11,520 bytes a second of real payload, 80%. That's 87 microseconds per byte. For 9600 it's 960 bytes a second, about a millisecond per byte.

The rates look odd until you know where they come from. They're 115200 divided by a small integer, and 115200 itself is 1.8432 MHz divided by 16. Old serial chips used a 1.8432 MHz crystal so every standard rate came out with zero error. A 14.7456 MHz crystal, which is eight times that, is still popular for the same reason.

On a modern MCU with a 16 MHz or 48 MHz clock, you usually can't hit those exactly. A 16 MHz clock with a simple divider by 16 times 9 gets you 111,111 baud, 3.5% low. Switching to 8x oversampling changes the rounding, sometimes for the better (here it gives plus 2.1%), sometimes for the worse. That error adds to whatever the other side has, and the limit is around 3 to 4%. So I'd check the divider error in the reference manual, use a fractional baud-rate register if the part has one, or pick a clock that divides nicely.

For choosing a rate: go as slow as the application allows for noise margin and cable length, and as fast as the data volume needs."
:::

::: terms
- **Baud**: symbols per second on the line. For UART, equal to bits per second.
- **8N1**: 8 data bits, no parity, 1 stop bit; 10 bits per byte.
- **Baud-rate divisor**: integer or fixed-point number by which the peripheral clock is divided.
- **Divider error**: percentage difference between the achieved and target baud rate.
- **Oversampling factor**: receiver ticks per bit (8 or 16 typically); sets divisor granularity.
- **Fractional baud-rate generator**: divider with a fraction field, giving much smaller error than integer-only.
- **Throughput / goodput**: payload bytes per second after framing overhead.
- **1.8432 MHz crystal**: classic UART clock that yields exact standard baud rates.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Confusing baud with bytes per second.** 115200 baud is not 115200 B/s; it is about 11.5 kB/s at 8N1.
- **Assuming any clock hits any baud rate.** A 12 MHz clock at 115200 with 16x oversampling is 7% off; 8 MHz is worse.
- **Ignoring the other side's error.** If one side is +2.5% and the other -2.5%, the combined 5% is at the theoretical limit.
- **Picking the highest baud "because it's faster".** Long cables, USB bridge limits and RC-oscillator clocks all make high rates fragile.
- **Thinking 57600 is a multiple of 9600 by 5.** It is 6 x 9600, which equals 115200 / 2.
- **Forgetting the idle gap.** Real throughput also depends on software feeding the data register; the table shows the ceiling.
:::

::: deep
## Where the numbers come from

Early UART chips (like the 8250 family) took a fixed crystal and a programmable divisor. The convention of a 1.8432 MHz clock came from this: it makes the 16x sample clock for 115200 baud exactly, and all lower standard rates follow by integer division:

$$\frac{1{,}843{,}200}{16\cdot D} = \frac{115200}{D}\ \Rightarrow\ D = 1, 2, 3, 6, 12, 24, \dots$$

gives 115200, 57600, 38400, 19200, 9600, 4800 baud. That is the whole reason the list looks the way it does. The same logic carries on in modern MCUs: they have a divisor register and a clock.

## Divider error: the real constraint on modern parts

Now suppose your MCU runs from 16 MHz. The receiver wants the baud clock times $N$, so the divisor is $D = f_{clk}/(N\cdot\text{baud})$. Rounding to an integer causes error. Here is what 16x oversampling gives for several clocks (computed by dividing and rounding to the nearest integer):

| Clock | 9600 | 38400 | 57600 | 115200 | 230400 | 921600 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 14.7456 MHz | 0% | 0% | 0% | 0% | 0% | 0% |
| 16 MHz | +0.16% | +0.16% | +2.12% | -3.55% | +8.51% | +8.51% |
| 8 MHz | +0.16% | +0.16% | -3.55% | +8.51% | +8.51% | -45.8% |
| 12 MHz | +0.16% | -2.34% | +0.16% | -6.99% | +8.51% | -18.6% |
| 48 MHz | -0.16% | +0.16% | +0.16% | +0.16% | +0.16% | +8.51% |
| 72 MHz | -0.05% | +0.16% | +0.16% | +0.16% | -2.34% | -2.34% |

(A 14.7456 MHz clock, like any multiple of 1.8432 MHz, hits every rate in the table exactly. For 8 MHz and 16 MHz at the highest rates, the integer divisor is as small as 1 and the error is large.) So a 16 MHz AVR-style part at 115200 is 3.55% off *by arithmetic alone*, before the crystal tolerance, the other side's error and any edge distortion. This is why Arduino-class boards at 115200 can be unreliable with some USB bridges while 9600 is rock solid, and why they offer double-speed (8x) or fractional modes.

**Two ways out.** (1) Pick a "baud-friendly" crystal such as 14.7456 or 11.0592 MHz. (2) Use a fractional baud-rate generator: many 32-bit MCUs have a fraction field (for instance 4 bits), so $D = 8.6875$ for 115200 from 16 MHz, error -0.08%. (3) Run the oversampling at 8x with a different divider and compare errors: at 16 MHz, 8x gives $D = 17$ and +2.12% for 115200, better than the 16x result in this case.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Which oversampling modes, fractional bits and maximum rates are available is vendor-specific, and some parts auto-adjust the divisor for you. Check the reference manual's baud-rate error table, which most vendors include.

## The error budget

Both ends contribute error and the receiver must keep the stop-bit sample within the stop bit, which for 8N1 allows a total relative error of about 5% (see [[q0778]]). With a 3.5% divider error on one side, the other side has only 1.5% to spare. Crystals are typically tens of ppm, negligible. An internal RC oscillator can be several percent off and drifts with temperature and supply, so an RC-clocked UART at 115200 can be fine on the bench and fail in a thermal chamber, or vice versa. Calibrate the RC or run from the crystal for UART.

```sim
{"id":"uart-frame","char":"U","baud":115200,"parity":"none","stop":1,"mismatch":3.5}
```
Figure: 'U' (0x55) is the classic test character because its alternating bit pattern 10101010 gives the most edges, so it is easy to measure a bit time on a scope. The mismatch slider starts at +3.5%, the size of the error in the 16 MHz example (the real case is a receiver 3.55% *slow*; pick the 16 MHz RX clock and press *Use as RX error* to apply it with the right sign). Push the slider either way and watch the receiver's samples slide off the bit centres until frames fail. The failure point is not quite symmetric, because the receiver detects the start edge up to one sample tick late.

## Choosing a baud rate

- **Longer cable or noisy plant**: lower (9600 to 57600), and use RS-485.
- **Console logging**: 115200 is the de facto default; go to 921600 or 1 M for heavy logging if the bridge supports it.
- **Streaming sensor data**: compute the byte rate. 100 samples/s of 16 bytes is 1,600 B/s, which needs at least 16,000 baud; use 115200 for headroom.
- **Bridges and modems**: USB-UART chips typically support up to a few Mbaud; check the datasheet.
- **Latency**: at 9600, a 10-byte message takes 10.4 ms to transmit; at 115200 it is 0.87 ms.

## Special rates

MIDI's 31,250 baud is 1 MHz/32, a number chosen so a plain 1 MHz or 2 MHz clock hits it exactly. DMX512 uses 250 kbaud (4 us per bit) with 8N2 framing over RS-485. LIN buses run at up to 20 kbaud. Many modules support an "auto-baud" mode where the receiver measures the first received character (often 'A', 'U' or 0x55) to infer the baud rate.
:::

::: applications
- **Debug console**: 115200 8N1 on nearly every dev board.
- **GPS/GNSS modules**: 9600 default on many modules, configurable to 115200 or higher.
- **Bluetooth and Wi-Fi modules (AT command)**: 115200 typical default; configurable.
- **MIDI**: 31,250 baud over a current loop.
- **DMX512 stage lighting**: 250,000 baud over RS-485.
- **USB-UART bridges for logging**: 921600 baud to several megabaud.
:::

::: assumptions
- I assume 8N1 framing for the throughput numbers; 8E1 and 8N2 are baud/11, and 9-bit modes differ.
- Divider-error values use a plain "round to nearest integer divisor" and 16x oversampling unless stated; real parts may offer fractional dividers or auto-correct.
- The 1.8432 MHz derivation assumes a 16x sample clock, consistent with classic 16550-type UARTs.
- Maximum usable baud depends on the line standard, cable and drivers, not just the MCU.
- Defaults (9600 for GPS, 115200 for consoles) are conventions, not rules; always check the specific module datasheet.
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between baud rate and bit rate?
Baud is symbols per second; bit rate is bits per second. For UART each symbol carries one bit, so they are numerically equal. They differ in schemes where a symbol carries multiple bits (modems with QAM). Payload rate is lower than either because of start/stop/parity overhead: 80% at 8N1.

### Why 115200 and not 100000?
Because it is 1.8432 MHz / 16, and that crystal gave exact divisions for all the standard rates. Rates like 100000 weren't in the standard list because they don't divide down from that crystal. Modern MCUs can generate 100000 or 1 M with fractional dividers but 115200 is the interoperable default.

### My MCU's UART is 3.5% off at 115200. Is that a problem?
It leaves only about 1.5% for everything else under a roughly 5% theoretical limit, assuming the other end is exact. If the other end is a USB bridge with its own error, you may fail at temperature extremes. Fix: use a fractional divider, change the clock, or run 8x oversampling and compare errors. Measure on a scope: you should see 8.68 us per bit.

### How fast can UART go?
Limited by the peripheral clock divided by the oversampling factor (e.g., 48 MHz / 16 = 3 Mbaud), by the line (cable capacitance, RS-232 slew-rate limits, bridge chip limits), and by software if no DMA. Parts commonly reach several Mbaud. At a given baud, shorter cable and series termination help.

### Harder: how would a receiver automatically detect the baud rate?
Auto-baud: send a known character such as 0x55 ('U') whose bits alternate. The receiver measures the time between edges with a timer and computes the divisor. Some UARTs have this built in. It only works if the first byte is predictable.
:::
