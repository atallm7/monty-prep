::: basics
### In plain words
A capacitor next to a chip is the right default, but not an absolute rule. Some parts, such as regulators and amplifiers, become unstable if you add more capacitance than the datasheet allows. A big capacitor can also cause a new problem: a huge surge of current at power-on. And a capacitor on a signal line is not decoupling at all. It just slows the signal down.

### Picture it
Think of shock absorbers on a car. The right ones smooth a bumpy road. The wrong ones make the car bounce or fight the steering. The same part that helps in one setup hurts in another. A capacitor is a shock absorber for voltage, so you match it to what it is attached to, and you read the datasheet first.

### Words you need
- **Inrush current**: the surge that flows into an empty capacitor when power is first applied.
- **Stability**: whether a regulator or amplifier settles to a steady output instead of ringing or swinging up and down on its own.
- **ESR window**: the range of internal resistance some regulators need in their output capacitor to stay stable.
- **Capacitive load limit**: the most capacitance an amplifier or reference can drive without ringing.
- **Hot-plug**: connecting a live supply to a board whose capacitors are empty.

### The simplest example
Plug a 12 V supply into a board with 100 µF of capacitance, and let the voltage rise over 1 ms. The current needed to fill the capacitor is $i = C\,dV/dt = 100\ \mu\text{F} \times 12\ \text{V} / 1\ \text{ms} = 1.2$ A. That can trip a protection circuit sized for a small load. If the voltage jumps up instantly, the surge is far bigger, limited only by the wiring's resistance. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds hot-plug ringing, regulator stability limits, and what to use instead.
:::

::: testing
Whether you treat decoupling as an engineered choice with limits rather than a reflex. The interviewer wants:

1. Cases where a datasheet forbids or restricts capacitance (stability: LDO ESR window, reference and op-amp load limits).
2. Cases where capacitance causes a new problem: hot-plug overshoot, inrush current, anti-resonance peaks, leakage or short-circuit failure, microphonics.
3. Cases where it is simply the wrong tool: signal lines, high-impedance sense nodes.
4. A numeric example, and what you do instead.
:::

::: why
"Put 100 nF on everything" is a good default and a dangerous absolute. Real boards have been lost to a ceramic capacitor on a hot-plugged 12 V input that rang to twice the supply, to an LDO that oscillated with the "wrong" capacitor, and to a precision reference that couldn't drive its bypass. A candidate who can list the exceptions *and* explain the physics shows they will read datasheets critically.
:::

::: concepts
- **Feedback stability**: regulators and buffers assume a capacitive load within a range ([[q0505]]).
- **Series RLC ringing**: a ceramic with a small series resistance and cable inductance can overshoot ([[q0509]]).
- **Inrush current**: $i=C\,dV/dt$ into an uncharged capacitor.
- **Anti-resonance** between mixed capacitors ([[q0506]]).
- **Leakage and failure modes** of capacitor technologies ([[q0502]]).
- **Signals vs supplies**: decoupling a signal is filtering it.
:::

::: answer
"Almost every IC supply wants decoupling, so the exceptions are specific. First, when the datasheet says not to, or limits it: some LDOs need a minimum ESR for stability, so a big low-ESR ceramic makes them oscillate; precision references and op-amp outputs often have a maximum capacitive load, beyond which they ring or oscillate; and some parts have integrated capacitors or are designed capacitor-free.

Second, when the capacitor creates a new problem. On a hot-plugged input, a low-ESR ceramic with the cable inductance forms an underdamped series RLC, and the capacitor voltage can overshoot to nearly twice the input, so I'd use damping, a bulk electrolytic or a TVS, rather than just more ceramics. Large capacitors on a battery or hot-swap rail cause inrush currents, C dV/dt, that trip protection. Mixing values badly can create an anti-resonance peak that makes noise worse. In ultra-low-power designs, capacitor leakage or tantalum short-circuit failure can matter, and in audio or sensitive analog, high-K ceramics can be microphonic.

Third, when it's the wrong tool: you don't put a capacitor on a data or analog signal to 'decouple' it, because that's a low-pass filter on the signal, and you shouldn't add capacitance on a high-impedance feedback or sense node without checking the loop. So I'd check the datasheet, model the hot-plug or loop if there's any doubt, and use filtering deliberately where I want it."
:::

::: technical
**1. Stability limits (datasheet-driven).** Linear regulators and reference/op-amp outputs form feedback loops with the load capacitance. Older LDOs (PNP/NMOS-follower style) require output ESR in a window (e.g. a few tenths of an ohm to a few ohms) for a zero that stabilises the loop; a low-ESR ceramic removes it. Others are "ceramic-stable" but specify minimum and maximum capacitance. Op-amps driving $C_L$ lose phase margin: the output resistance $R_o$ and $C_L$ form a pole at $1/(2\pi R_oC_L)$ inside the loop. References often specify a maximum capacitive load or a required RC.

**2. Hot-plug overshoot.** An input of $V_{in}$ applied through cable inductance $L$ to a capacitor $C$ with series resistance $R$ is a series RLC with damping ratio $\zeta=\frac R2\sqrt{C/L}$. Peak overshoot: $V_{peak}=V_{in}\left(1+e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\right)$. Example: 12 V, $L=1$ µH, $C=22$ µF ceramic, $R=50$ mΩ: $\zeta=0.117$, $V_{peak}=1.69\times12=20.3$ V, above a 16 V capacitor or a 20 V regulator input.

**3. Inrush.** $i=C\,dV/dt$. A 470 µF capacitor charged from 0 to 12 V in 1 ms draws 5.6 A average; a hard-connected battery limited only by 50 mΩ would see up to 240 A peak. Hot-swap controllers and soft-start limit it.

**4. Anti-resonance.** Mixed values create an impedance peak between SRFs; if a peak sits at a clock harmonic the noise worsens ([[q0506]]). Fix with ESR, intermediate values, or fewer values.

**5. Leakage and failure.** MLCC insulation resistance is large but finite (datasheet-specific); electrolytic leakage is µA; tantalum can fail short. For nA-level sleep currents or fail-safe rails, choose parts accordingly.

**6. Microphonics.** High-K (Class II) ceramics are piezoelectric; mechanical vibration can generate millivolts in a sensitive analog or audio supply. Use C0G or film there.

**7. Signals.** A capacitor $C$ on a line of source resistance $R$ is a low-pass with $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$; 50 Ω and 100 pF gives 32 MHz.
:::

::: short
"Decoupling is the default, so the exceptions are specific. Don't add capacitance beyond what the datasheet allows: LDOs with ESR windows, references and op-amp outputs with capacitive-load limits. Be careful where capacitance causes problems: hot-plugged inputs where ceramic plus cable inductance overshoots toward twice the input, large capacitors that cause inrush, and bad value mixes that create anti-resonance. And never decouple a signal line, because that is just a low-pass filter."
:::

::: long
"My default is to decouple every supply pin, so the exceptions are specific, and they fall into three groups.

First, where the datasheet restricts it. Some LDOs need a minimum output ESR for stability, so a big low-ESR ceramic can make them oscillate; others specify a capacitance range. Precision references and op-amp outputs often have a maximum capacitive load, because the load capacitance and the output impedance form a pole inside the feedback loop and eat phase margin. Some parts have the capacitors built in. In those cases I use exactly what the datasheet says.

Second, where adding capacitance creates a new problem. The classic is hot-plugging a supply through a cable. The cable has a microhenry or so, a ceramic has almost no ESR, so it's a lightly damped series RLC. With 12 volts, 1 microhenry, 22 microfarads and 50 milliohms, the damping ratio is about 0.12 and the capacitor overshoots to roughly 1.7 times, about 20 volts, which can exceed the rating of the capacitor or the regulator behind it. So I add damping, a bulk electrolytic or polymer, a TVS, or a hot-swap controller. Large capacitors also cause inrush, C dV/dt, which can trip protection or overload a battery. A bad mix of values can create an anti-resonance peak that makes noise worse at one frequency. And in ultra-low-power or safety designs, leakage and short-circuit failure modes matter; in audio or sensitive analog, high-K ceramics can be microphonic.

Third, where it's the wrong tool. You don't put a capacitor on a data or analog signal to decouple it, because that's an RC filter on the signal: 50 ohms and 100 picofarads is 32 megahertz. If I need filtering I design it deliberately.

So I treat 100 nF as a default, read the datasheet's capacitor requirements, and simulate or measure when the source has inductance or the loop might be affected."
:::

::: terms
- **ESR window**: allowed range of output-capacitor ESR for a regulator's stability.
- **Capacitive load limit**: maximum $C_L$ an op-amp or reference can drive.
- **Hot-plug (hot-swap)**: connecting a powered source to an uncharged load.
- **Damping ratio $\zeta$**: $\frac R2\sqrt{C/L}$ for a series RLC; $\zeta<1$ means ringing.
- **Inrush current**: charging current into an uncharged capacitor.
- **Microphonic**: converting vibration to voltage (piezoelectric effect).
- **Anti-resonance**: impedance peak between capacitors.
- **TVS**: transient voltage suppressor.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "when the datasheet says not to". An exceptional one:

- Groups exceptions by **mechanism**: loop stability, series RLC ringing, inrush, anti-resonance, leakage/failure, microphonics, wrong tool.
- Quantifies a case: damping ratio 0.117 and 1.69× overshoot (20 V on a 12 V input).
- Knows the **ESR window** history of LDOs and the $R_o C_L$ pole of op-amp outputs.
- Says what to do instead: damping ESR, TVS, hot-swap controller, series isolation resistor, C0G or film, deliberate filtering.
- Notes verification (simulate or scope the hot-plug).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Adding "extra" ceramics to an LDO output** without checking its stable region.
- **Believing more capacitance can't hurt a hot-plugged input.** Low ESR makes ringing worse.
- **Putting capacitance on reference or feedback pins** beyond the datasheet.
- **Decoupling signal lines** to "clean them up".
- **Ignoring inrush**, then wondering why the protection trips or the connector arcs.
:::

::: deep
## Why a capacitor can hurt

A capacitor is energy storage with an impedance that depends on what is around it. Its helpfulness assumes the surrounding circuit is well behaved. The exceptions come from the surrounding circuit: feedback loops that assume a certain load, sources with inductance, and sources with limited current.

## Case 1: stability of regulators and buffers

An LDO is a feedback loop whose output pole depends on the load capacitor and load. Classic PNP-pass LDOs had a low-frequency dominant pole at the output and needed the capacitor's ESR zero to restore phase margin, hence the "ESR must be between 0.3 Ω and 5 Ω" requirement. Modern LDOs move the dominant pole internally and tolerate ceramics, but still specify minimum and sometimes maximum capacitance. Rule: use the datasheet's capacitor table, and check the effective capacitance after DC-bias derating.

An op-amp driving a capacitive load $C_L$ has output resistance $R_o$; the pole $f_p=1/(2\pi R_oC_L)$ adds phase lag near the crossover and can ring or oscillate. The cure is a small isolation resistor in series with the load or a different compensation, not "decoupling". A reference whose output buffer is weak may need an RC with a series resistor, and it is documented in its datasheet.

## Case 3: inrush

Charging a capacitor from a source with resistance $R_s$ gives a peak current $V/R_s$. For $C=470\ \mu$F charging to 12 V in a controlled 1 ms ramp: $i=C\,dV/dt=470\ \mu\text{F}\times12\text{ V}/1\text{ ms}=5.6$ A. With only 50 mΩ of source resistance the unconstrained peak is 240 A. This trips fuses and protection, welds relay contacts, or draws arcs at connectors. Use inrush limiters, soft-start, precharge resistors, or hot-swap controllers.

## Case 4: anti-resonance from bad mixes

An unmanaged mix of values can put a high-impedance peak at a frequency where the load has energy. Decoupling networks need design and verification. The remedy may be fewer, more carefully chosen values, ESR damping, or intermediate values ([[q0506]]).

## Case 5: leakage, failure mode, and microphonics

- **Leakage.** MLCC insulation resistance is high but finite (check the datasheet; it falls with temperature); tantalum and electrolytic leakage is µA-level. For nA sleep currents or high-impedance nodes this matters.
- **Failure mode.** MLCCs can crack from board flex and fail short; tantalum can fail short with high energy. On a rail that can deliver high current, a shorted capacitor can start a fire; use flex terminations, series fuses or current limits where required.
- **Microphonics.** Class II (X7R, X5R, Y5V) ceramics are piezoelectric; vibration or acoustic noise creates millivolts. In sensitive analog or audio, use C0G/NP0 or film.

## Case 6: it's not a supply

A capacitor to ground on a signal line is a low-pass filter: $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$ with $R$ the source resistance. 50 Ω and 100 pF give 32 MHz; 10 kΩ and 100 pF (a typical high-impedance sensor) give 160 kHz. Don't "decouple" signals; either filter deliberately or fix the noise source ([[q0505]]).

## What to do instead

| Situation | Better approach |
|---|---|
| LDO with ESR requirement | use specified capacitor type; add series resistor if needed |
| Op-amp/reference with capacitive-load limit | series isolation resistor; follow datasheet RC |
| Hot-plug input | damping (ESR/electrolytic), TVS, hot-swap controller |
| Large bulk on battery rail | soft-start, inrush limiter |
| Sensitive analog supply | C0G or film, ferrite + small ceramic |
| Noise on a signal | proper return path, termination, deliberate RC |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> ESR windows, capacitive-load limits and leakage figures are part-specific; only the datasheet is authoritative. The overshoot numbers use an idealised series RLC; real cables and sources add more inductance and resistance.
:::

::: applications
- **Automotive and industrial 12/24 V inputs**: hot-plug and load-dump handling with damping and TVS.
- **Precision ADC references**: following the datasheet's capacitor requirements, with series resistors.
- **Battery-powered products**: inrush and leakage control on bulk capacitors.
- **Audio and instrumentation**: C0G or film parts to avoid microphonic pickup.
- **LDO-based power trees**: using only datasheet-approved output capacitors.
:::

::: assumptions
- Ordinary digital boards: decoupling is nearly always correct; this answer concerns the exceptions.
- The hot-plug model is a simple series RLC with constant $L$, $R$, $C$; real ceramic capacitance falls with bias, which changes the numbers.
- ESR-window behaviour applies to older or specific LDOs; modern ceramic-stable parts differ.
- "Don't use" can mean "don't use that value, type, or placement"; in nearly every case a *different* capacitor arrangement is the answer.
- Another valid answer is that you also don't need extra decoupling where the module or IC datasheet states that integrated capacitors suffice.
:::

::: followups
### Why can a low-ESR ceramic make an LDO unstable?
Some LDOs rely on the output capacitor's ESR to create a zero that adds phase lead near the loop crossover. A very low ESR removes that lead and the loop loses phase margin and oscillates. The datasheet specifies the ESR window or the allowed capacitor type.

### How would you protect a 12 V input from hot-plug overshoot?
Damp the RLC: add an electrolytic or polymer capacitor in parallel whose ESR is near $\sqrt{L/C}$, or a series resistor, so $\zeta\approx0.7$-1. Use a TVS or a regulator rated for twice the input, or a hot-swap controller that ramps the input. Verify with a scope on the actual cable.

### Why not just put a capacitor on a noisy signal line?
It becomes a low-pass filter on the signal, rounding edges and reducing bandwidth: $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$. The noise needs a proper source fix (return path, termination, shielding) or a deliberately designed filter.

### What if an op-amp drives a 1 nF load and oscillates?
The output resistance and load capacitance create a pole inside the loop. I'd add an isolation resistor (10–100 Ω) in series with the output, with feedback taken before or after it depending on the DC accuracy needed, or choose an amplifier rated for capacitive loads.

### Scenario: your board's 3.3 V rail has 40 µF of ceramics and the protection trips at power-on. What's happening?
Inrush: $i=C\,dV/dt$. If the input rises in 100 µs, 40 µF × 3.3 V/100 µs = 1.3 A peak, beyond the limiter's threshold. I'd slow the input ramp with soft-start, add an inrush limiter or a load switch with controlled slew rate, or reduce the capacitance behind the switch.
:::

::: interpretations
This is the counterpart to [[q0501]] ("when needed") and [[q0505]] ("what to decouple"). "Should not use" rarely means "no capacitor at all"; it usually means no capacitor *of that type, value or placement* for that node. Where this could be read as "when is decoupling unnecessary", the answer is low-speed, low-current parts with a nearby, well-bypassed rail, where the extra capacitor adds nothing but cost.
:::
