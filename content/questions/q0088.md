::: testing
Whether you understand beta as **a ratio with caveats**, not a constant of nature. The interviewer is looking for:

1. The definition $\beta=I_C/I_B$ and the relationship to the common-base gain $\alpha=I_C/I_E$, with $\beta=\alpha/(1-\alpha)$ and $I_E=(\beta+1)I_B$.
2. Typical values (50-300 for small-signal parts) and that datasheets give a wide spread (e.g. 100-300).
3. Where it comes from physically: emitter injection efficiency and base transport, with doping and base width setting it.
4. Its limits: it varies with $I_C$, $V_{CE}$ and temperature, and it only applies in the forward-active region, so circuits must be designed not to depend on it.
:::

::: why
Beta is the number everyone quotes and nobody can rely on. A bias network that works at $\beta=100$ and fails at $\beta=300$ will fail in production, and a switch designed with typical beta will stay unsaturated on cold parts. Understanding beta, including its spread and temperature behaviour, is the difference between a design that works on one prototype and one that works across lots, temperature and years. It is also what turns up in the input resistance $r_\pi=\beta/g_m$, in current-mirror errors ($2/\beta$) and in emitter-follower output impedance.
:::

::: concepts
- **Common-emitter gain**: $\beta=I_C/I_B$. See [[q0085]].
- **Common-base gain**: $\alpha=I_C/I_E$, close to 1, and $\beta=\alpha/(1-\alpha)$.
- **Forward-active requirement**: $\beta$ is meaningful only in the active region; in saturation use forced $\beta$. See [[q0087]].
- **Origin**: base transport factor times injection efficiency; set by $W_B$, $L_n$ and the doping ratio. See [[q0081]].
- **DC vs AC beta**: $h_{FE}=I_C/I_B$ (large signal) versus $h_{fe}=\Delta I_C/\Delta I_B$ (small signal).
:::

::: answer
"Current gain, or beta, is the ratio of collector current to base current in the forward-active region: $\beta=I_C/I_B$. For a small-signal transistor it's typically 50 to 300, so a base current of 10 µA controls a collector current of about 1 to 3 mA. The related quantity alpha is the collector current over the emitter current, which is just under one, and the two are linked: $\beta=\alpha/(1-\alpha)$. If alpha is 0.99, beta is 99.

It comes from the physics: of all the carriers the emitter injects, almost all reach the collector, and only a small fraction is lost to recombination in the thin base or to holes injected back into the emitter. The base current replaces those losses. A thinner base and a more heavily doped emitter give higher beta.

The key engineering point is that beta isn't a precise or constant number. It varies from part to part by a factor of two or three, it changes with collector current, with $V_{CE}$, and with temperature, rising when hot and dropping when cold. So I never design a circuit that depends on a particular beta. For an amplifier I use emitter degeneration and a stiff base-bias divider so $I_C$ is set by resistors and not by beta. For a switch I use a forced beta of about 10, so it saturates even on the lowest-gain parts."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.**
$$\beta_{DC}=h_{FE}=\frac{I_C}{I_B},\qquad \beta_{AC}=h_{fe}=\left.\frac{\partial I_C}{\partial I_B}\right|_{V_{CE}},\qquad \alpha=\frac{I_C}{I_E}$$
$$\beta=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha},\qquad \alpha=\frac{\beta}{\beta+1},\qquad I_E=(\beta+1)I_B$$
$\alpha=0.99\Rightarrow\beta=99$; $\alpha=0.995\Rightarrow\beta=199$; $\beta=100\Rightarrow\alpha=0.990$.

**Physical origin.** $\alpha=\gamma\,\alpha_T$ (emitter injection efficiency times base transport factor), and
$$\frac1\beta\approx\frac{D_pW_BN_{AB}}{D_nW_EN_{DE}}+\frac{W_B^2}{2L_n^2}+(\text{depletion-region and surface recombination})$$
Thin base, high $N_{DE}/N_{AB}$ and long lifetime raise $\beta$.

**Variation.** $\beta$ rises then falls with $I_C$: low at very small currents (recombination in the BE depletion region dominates $I_B$), peaks in the mid-range, and falls at high current (high-level injection, base-widening/Kirk effect, emitter crowding). It rises with $V_{CE}$ (Early effect, same factor as $I_C$) and increases with temperature (often +0.5 to +1 %/K).

**Spread.** A 2N3904 datasheet specifies $h_{FE}$ of 100 minimum to 300 maximum at $I_C=10$ mA, $V_{CE}=1$ V (check current datasheet), i.e. a 3:1 spread across parts. Other transistors range from 20 (power) to 800+ (super-beta).

**Consequences.**
- Input resistance looking into the base: $r_\pi=\beta/g_m=\beta V_T/I_C$ (e.g. 2.6 kΩ for $\beta=100$ at 1 mA).
- Current mirror error $\approx2/\beta$: 2% for $\beta=100$.
- Emitter-follower output resistance $\approx r_e+R_S/(\beta+1)$.
- Darlington pair: $\beta_{total}\approx\beta_1\beta_2$ (10,000 for two devices of 100) with $V_{BE}\approx1.4$ V.
:::

::: long
"Beta, or current gain, is how much collector current you get per unit of base current: $\beta=I_C/I_B$ when the transistor is in forward-active mode. For a typical small-signal NPN it's between 50 and 300. So if I put 20 µA into the base with a beta of 100, I get 2 mA at the collector.

There's a companion number, alpha, which is the collector current divided by the emitter current. Because the emitter current is the sum of collector and base currents, alpha is just below one, around 0.99. They're related by $\beta=\alpha/(1-\alpha)$, so alpha of 0.99 is beta of 99, and alpha of 0.995 is beta of 199. Another way to see it is $I_E=(\beta+1)I_B$.

Where does beta come from? The emitter injects carriers into the base. Almost all of them cross the thin base and get collected, and only a small fraction is lost: some recombine in the base, and some holes from the base are injected back into the emitter. The base current is the supply of those holes. So beta is basically one over the fractional loss. A thinner base reduces recombination, and a more heavily doped emitter reduces back-injection, so both raise beta.

What matters most in practice is that beta isn't a stable parameter. Across parts of the same type it can vary three to one: the datasheet for a 2N3904 says 100 to 300 at 10 mA. It changes with collector current, peaking at a mid-range current and falling at low and high current. And it increases with temperature, maybe half a percent per degree. So I don't design circuits that depend on it.

For an amplifier I use emitter degeneration and a bias divider whose current is much bigger than the base current, so the collector current is set by resistor values and $V_{BE}$, not by beta. For a switch I use a forced beta of 10, so even a low-gain part saturates. And where I need a bigger effective gain, I'd use a Darlington, where the betas multiply, or just a MOSFET."
:::

::: terms
- **Beta ($\beta$, $h_{FE}$)**: DC current gain $I_C/I_B$ in forward-active mode.
- **$h_{fe}$ (AC beta)**: small-signal current gain $\Delta I_C/\Delta I_B$.
- **Alpha ($\alpha$)**: common-base current gain $I_C/I_E$, close to 1.
- **Injection efficiency ($\gamma$)**: fraction of emitter current carried by carriers injected into the base.
- **Base transport factor ($\alpha_T$)**: fraction of injected carriers reaching the collector.
- **Forced beta**: $I_C/I_B$ imposed in saturation, chosen low (about 10) for margin.
- **Darlington pair**: two cascaded BJTs with $\beta\approx\beta_1\beta_2$.
- **$r_\pi$**: small-signal base-emitter resistance, $\beta/g_m$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating beta as a constant** and designing a fixed-base-current bias, which makes $I_C$ vary 3x across parts.
- **Using beta in saturation.** The actual $I_C/I_B$ there is lower; the datasheet beta doesn't apply.
- **Confusing alpha and beta.** Alpha is $I_C/I_E$ (about 0.99) and beta is $I_C/I_B$ (about 100).
- **Assuming beta is the same for NPN and PNP counterparts, or between small-signal and power devices.** Power BJTs often have $h_{FE}$ of 20-100.
- **Forgetting the temperature effect.** Beta falls in the cold; a switch that saturates at 25 °C may not at -40 °C.
- **Believing a high-beta part is better in every way.** Higher beta usually means a thinner base with a lower Early voltage and breakdown voltage, and higher spread.
:::

::: deep
## Definition and the alpha relation

For an NPN in forward-active mode, KCL gives $I_E=I_C+I_B$. Define
$$\beta=\frac{I_C}{I_B},\qquad\alpha=\frac{I_C}{I_E}$$
Then $\alpha=\frac{I_C}{I_C+I_B}=\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}$ and $\beta=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}$. The mapping is steeply nonlinear: a tiny change in $\alpha$ is a big change in $\beta$.

| $\alpha$ | $\beta$ |
|---|---|
| 0.95 | 19 |
| 0.98 | 49 |
| 0.99 | 99 |
| 0.995 | 199 |
| 0.997 | 332 |

This is why datasheet scatter in $\beta$ (3:1) corresponds to a tiny spread in $\alpha$ (0.990 to 0.997).

## Where beta comes from

$\alpha=\gamma\alpha_T$. With heavy emitter doping and a thin base ([[q0085]]):
$$\frac1\beta\approx\underbrace{\frac{D_pW_BN_{AB}}{D_nW_EN_{DE}}}_{\text{back-injection}}+\underbrace{\frac{W_B^2}{2L_n^2}}_{\text{base recombination}}+\ldots$$
The idealised worked example in [[q0085]] gave $\beta\approx1400$; real small-signal devices are 100-300 because of additional recombination in the BE depletion region (dominant at low current), bandgap narrowing in the heavily doped emitter (extra hole injection) and surface effects. Very high gain "super-beta" transistors (up to a few thousand) use extremely thin bases at the cost of a low breakdown voltage (often under 10 V for $V_{CEO}$).

- **Collector current:** as above.
- **$V_{CE}$:** $\beta$ rises with $V_{CE}$ by the Early factor $(1+V_{CE}/V_A)$. With $V_A=100$ V, going from 1 V to 10 V raises $\beta$ by about 9%.
- **Temperature:** typically $+0.5$ to $+1$ %/K. A part with $\beta=100$ at 25 °C might be 60-70 at -40 °C and 130-150 at 100 °C.
- **Part-to-part:** 3:1 or more within a datasheet range.
- **Aging and radiation:** $\beta$ degrades with surface damage and neutron/ionising radiation (a known effect in space and nuclear applications).

> The 2N3904 $h_{FE}$ range of 100-300 at 10 mA/1 V quoted here is from memory of the standard datasheet specification; different manufacturers' datasheets and revisions vary. Always check the datasheet for the exact part and test conditions, and note that the temperature coefficient of $\beta$ is device dependent.

## Designing so beta doesn't matter

**Fixed bias** (one base resistor from the supply): $I_B=(V_{CC}-V_{BE})/R_B$ fixed, so $I_C=\beta I_B$ is proportional to $\beta$. For $V_{CC}=12$ V and $R_B=1.13$ MΩ ($I_B\approx10\ \mu$A): $\beta=50\to0.50$ mA, $\beta=100\to1.00$ mA, $\beta=300\to3.00$ mA, a 6:1 spread (and the 3 mA case may saturate a load).

**Divider bias with emitter resistor** (as in [[q0082]]): $V_{TH}=1.655$ V, $R_{TH}=13.8$ kΩ, $R_E=1$ kΩ:
$$I_C=\frac{\beta\,(V_{TH}-V_{BE})}{R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E}$$
- $\beta=50$: 0.737 mA
- $\beta=100$: 0.832 mA
- $\beta=300$: 0.910 mA

A 6:1 change in $\beta$ now gives only a 1.23:1 spread in $I_C$, because $(\beta+1)R_E\gg R_{TH}$ makes $I_C\approx(V_{TH}-V_{BE})/R_E$. That's negative feedback in action: if $I_C$ rises, the emitter voltage rises, reducing $V_{BE}$. See [[q0084]].

**Switches.** Use forced beta of about 10 so a part with $\beta_{min}=30$ at cold temperature still saturates with margin.

## Where beta shows up

- **Input resistance:** $r_\pi=\beta/g_m=\beta V_T/I_C$; for $\beta=100$ and $I_C=1$ mA, $r_\pi=2.6$ kΩ.
- **Current mirror error:** $I_{out}/I_{ref}=1/(1+2/\beta)$: 0.96 ($\beta=50$), 0.98 ($\beta=100$), 0.993 ($\beta=300$).
- **Emitter follower:** $R_{out}\approx r_e+R_S/(\beta+1)$: with a 10 kΩ source, $\beta=100$ and $I_C=1$ mA it is about 125 Ω.
- **Voltage gain:** $-g_mR_C$ is independent of $\beta$ (if the source is stiff), but the *current* gain and input impedance depend on it.
- **Darlington pair:** $\beta_{tot}\approx\beta_1\beta_2\approx10^4$ at the cost of $V_{BE}\approx1.4$ V and slower turn-off.

- **Bench:** set $I_B$ via a known resistor, measure $I_C$ with a sense resistor at a stated $V_{CE}$.
- **Curve tracer:** gives the full family and $h_{fe}$ at any point.

## Worked example: sensitivity of the bias point to beta, and why $V_{BE}$ can matter more

The divider-bias formula $I_C=\beta(V_{TH}-V_{BE})/[R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E]$ lets you quantify how well the emitter resistor suppresses beta. Define the sensitivity as the fractional change in $I_C$ per fractional change in $\beta$. Differentiating gives
$$S_\beta=\frac{\beta}{I_C}\frac{\partial I_C}{\partial\beta}=\frac{R_{TH}+R_E}{R_{TH}+(\beta+1)R_E}$$
With $R_{TH}=13.8$ kΩ and $R_E=1$ kΩ this evaluates to 0.23 at $\beta=50$, 0.13 at $\beta=100$ and 0.047 at $\beta=300$. So near $\beta=100$ a 10 % change in beta moves $I_C$ by only 1.3 %. A fixed-bias stage, by contrast, has $S_\beta=1$ at every beta. The design rule that makes this work is to choose $(\beta_{min}+1)R_E\gg R_{TH}$, equivalent to keeping the divider current at least about ten times the base current. Here the divider carries $12\text{ V}/116\text{ k}\Omega=103\ \mu$A against $I_B=8.3\ \mu$A, a ratio of 12.

Now add temperature. Beta rises with temperature, but so does the effect of the $V_{BE}$ tempco of about −2 mV/K, and the second one is not suppressed by $R_E$ in the same way. Take $\beta=65$ and $V_{BE}=0.83$ V at −40 °C, $\beta=100$ and 0.70 V at 25 °C, and $\beta=140$ and 0.55 V at 100 °C (the beta values from the guidance above, the $V_{BE}$ values from −2 mV/K):

| Temperature | $\beta$ | $V_{BE}$ | $I_C$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| −40 °C | 65 | 0.83 V | 0.67 mA |
| 25 °C | 100 | 0.70 V | 0.83 mA |
| 100 °C | 140 | 0.55 V | 1.00 mA |

The collector current changes by 50 % between the extremes, and nearly all of it comes from $V_{BE}$: with $V_{TH}=1.655$ V only 0.955 V stands across the bias network at 25 °C, so a 150 mV drift in $V_{BE}$ is a 15 % change of the numerator. To make the bias more stable, raise $V_{TH}$ and $R_E$ together so that $V_E$ (the voltage across $R_E$) is a volt or two: then a 150 mV drift is a much smaller fraction. This is the standard recommendation that the emitter sits at 1 to 2 V in a discrete amplifier, at the cost of headroom. Beta therefore doesn't just vary; it is one of three drifting quantities (beta, $V_{BE}$ and leakage), and a good bias network is judged against all three.

## The beta you can actually rely on

A datasheet gives a minimum, typical and maximum $h_{FE}$ at stated conditions, and the guaranteed numbers are the minimum and maximum. The practical rules:

1. **Design for the minimum $\beta$ when you need enough drive** (a switch must saturate with the weakest part at the coldest temperature) and for the **maximum $\beta$ when you need to limit current** (a fixed-bias stage must not overheat with the strongest part at the hottest temperature).
2. **Use the current the datasheet specifies.** A part with $h_{FE}=100$ to 300 at 10 mA may have only 30 to 60 at 100 mA; if the curve is not given, ask the manufacturer or measure.
3. **Distinguish DC and small-signal beta.** The datasheet may quote both $h_{FE}$ (DC, $I_C/I_B$) and $h_{fe}$ (small-signal, $\partial I_C/\partial I_B$ at a frequency, often 1 kHz). Near the middle of the bell curve they are similar, while at the ends of the curve $h_{fe}$ and $h_{FE}$ differ. Gain estimates use $h_{fe}$; switch design uses $h_{FE}$.
4. **Beta also falls at high frequency.** The magnitude of the current gain falls as $\beta(f)\approx f_T/f$ above the corner $f_\beta=f_T/\beta_0$. For a transistor with $f_T=300$ MHz and $\beta_0=100$, the corner is 3 MHz; at 30 MHz the current gain is about 10; at 300 MHz it reaches 1 by definition of $f_T$.

## Worked example: Darlington pair numbers

A Darlington pair built from two devices with $\beta_1=\beta_2=100$ has $\beta_{tot}\approx\beta_1\beta_2+\beta_1+\beta_2\approx10{,}200$. Where the single transistor needed a base current of $I_C/100$, the pair needs $I_C/10{,}200$: a 1 A load needs 98 µA of base current. The price is paid in the voltage terms: the input requires $2V_{BE}\approx1.4$ V, and the output cannot saturate below $V_{CE(sat),1}+V_{BE2}\approx0.2+0.7=0.9$ V, so the pair dissipates $1\text{ A}\times0.9\text{ V}=0.9$ W where a single saturated device with $V_{CE(sat)}=0.2$ V would dissipate 0.2 W. In a 5 V system a Darlington driving a 1 A load loses 18 % of the supply; in a 3.3 V system it loses 27 %. Turn-off is also slower, because Q2's stored charge has no direct path to leave its base except through the emitter-base resistor; a 5 to 10 kΩ resistor from Q2's base to ground (included in many integrated Darlingtons) speeds this up at the cost of slightly more base drive.

## Verifying beta and its spread on the bench

- **Measure $\beta$ at your operating point, not on the multimeter's $h_{FE}$ socket.** Build a fixed-bias circuit: a precision resistor from a 5 V supply to the base sets $I_B$ (for 20 µA, a 215 kΩ resistor drops $5-0.65$ V), and a 1 kΩ collector resistor from a second supply lets you read $I_C$ from its voltage drop with a DMM. $\beta=I_C/I_B$. Repeat for 5 to 10 transistors of the same part number from the same reel and look at the spread: you will usually find the 2:1 or 3:1 range the datasheet warns about.
- **Vary temperature.** Warm the transistor with a heat gun or hot plate while holding $I_B$ constant using a current source (not a resistor, so $V_{BE}$ drift doesn't confound the result). Beta should rise roughly 0.5 to 1 % per kelvin.
- **Test the emitter-resistor bias.** Swap three transistors in the divider-biased amplifier and measure $V_E$ each time; with $R_E=1$ kΩ the three readings should agree within 10 % even though the betas differ by 2:1. Then short $R_E$ out (or set it to near zero) and see the spread explode. That single experiment demonstrates the value of feedback better than the algebra.
- **Current sense for the measurement.** Use a small series resistor (10 Ω to 100 Ω) to measure currents, and verify that the added drop doesn't push the transistor out of the active region.

::: applications
- **Choosing bias networks**: stiff dividers and emitter resistors so $I_C$ is insensitive to $\beta$ in amplifiers and references.
- **Switch design**: forced-beta rule for relay, LED and motor drivers.
- **Darlington arrays (ULN2003)**: high effective beta lets a 5 V logic pin drive 500 mA channels.
- **Current mirrors and differential pairs**: matched $\beta$ and the $2/\beta$ error budget in analogue ICs.
- **Super-beta input transistors** in precision op-amps (e.g. very low input bias current types): thin-base devices with $\beta$ of 1000+ and low breakdown voltage.
- **Phototransistors and optocouplers**: effective gain (current transfer ratio) is a function of the transistor's $\beta$.
:::

::: assumptions
- **NPN silicon BJT in forward-active** at room temperature; PNP behaves the same with polarities reversed (usually with a lower $\beta$).
- **Typical values** (50-300) are for small-signal discrete parts; power, RF, super-beta and IC devices differ widely.
- **DC beta used unless stated**; at low frequency $h_{fe}\approx h_{FE}$ is a common approximation but they differ at high current and frequency; $\beta$ also falls above $f_\beta=f_T/\beta$.
- **Ideal bias examples** neglect Early effect and temperature drift of $V_{BE}$.
- **Alternative answer**: a short answer ($\beta=I_C/I_B$, typically 100, with a caution about variation) is acceptable; the design-around-beta discussion is what makes it strong.
:::

::: followups
### Compute beta given alpha = 0.995.
$\beta=\alpha/(1-\alpha)=0.995/0.005=199$. Conversely for $\beta=100$, $\alpha=100/101=0.990$. A 0.5% change in alpha (0.990 to 0.995) doubles beta, which is why beta is such a variable parameter and why alpha is nearly constant.

### Why does beta vary so much between transistors of the same type?
Because it depends on small fractional losses (about 1% of the carriers) set by base width, doping profile, recombination centres and lifetime, all of which vary across wafers and lots. A 30% variation in the loss fraction is a 30% variation in $\beta$, and the datasheet range of 3:1 is typical. This is why designs shouldn't depend on beta.

### How would you bias a transistor so $I_C$ barely depends on beta?
Use a base voltage divider whose current is at least 10x the base current (so the base is a stiff voltage source) and an emitter resistor. Then $I_C\approx(V_B-V_{BE})/R_E$ where $V_B=V_{TH}$; beta only enters through $R_{TH}/(\beta+1)$, which is small compared to $R_E$. In the example, a 6:1 spread in beta yields 1.23:1 in $I_C$. Typical rule: $R_{TH}\le(\beta_{min}+1)R_E/10$.

### Why does a Darlington have such high gain, and what's the downside?
$\beta_{tot}\approx\beta_1\beta_2$, e.g. 100 × 100 = 10,000, because the first transistor's emitter current feeds the second's base. Downsides: $V_{BE}$ total of about 1.4 V, a higher $V_{CE(sat)}$ (about 0.7-1 V or more), slow turn-off (no way to remove Q2's stored charge except through a resistor from its base to ground), and lower bandwidth.

### What happens to beta in saturation?
The ratio $I_C/I_B$ (forced beta) drops below $\beta_{active}$ because the circuit, not the transistor, limits $I_C$ and extra base current just increases stored charge. Datasheets specify $V_{CE(sat)}$ at $I_C/I_B=10$ for this reason. Deep saturation also reduces effective gain because the forward-biased collector junction injects carriers back into the base.

### Scenario: your LED driver works at room temperature but is dim at -20 °C. How might beta explain it?
At low temperature $\beta$ drops (maybe 30-40% lower than at 25 °C), so with a fixed base resistor the transistor may fall out of saturation and enter the active region, where $I_C=\beta I_B$ is smaller than what the LED resistor demands. $V_{CE}$ rises above 0.2 V and the LED current decreases. In addition, the LED forward voltage rises in the cold. The fix is more base current (smaller $R_B$) so forced beta stays at about 10, giving margin over temperature, or use a MOSFET.
:::

::: interpretations
"Current gain/beta" has several levels: the **definition** ($I_C/I_B$), the **related alpha**, the **physical origin** and the **practical implications** (variation and design). I treat all four. "Current gain" in other contexts can mean the *AC current gain* of a stage or amplifier (e.g. the current gain of an emitter follower is $\beta+1$), and $\beta_F$/$\beta_R$ in SPICE (forward and reverse). For related BJT questions see [[q0085]] (operation) and [[q0087]] (modes, forced beta); the MOSFET has no analogue because its gate current is zero.
:::

::: references
- Sedra & Smith, *Microelectronic Circuits*: the BJT chapter, on $\alpha$, $\beta$, the large-signal model and biasing for beta insensitivity.
- Horowitz & Hill, *The Art of Electronics*: transistor chapter, on $h_{FE}$ variation, biasing and Darlington pairs.
- Gray, Hurst, Lewis & Meyer, *Analysis and Design of Analog Integrated Circuits*: the bipolar transistor chapter, on the origin and variation of current gain.
- onsemi/Fairchild 2N3904 datasheet: $h_{FE}$ specification and typical $h_{FE}$ vs $I_C$ and temperature curves.
:::
