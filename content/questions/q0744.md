::: basics
### In plain words
I2C stands for Inter-Integrated Circuit. It is a two-wire bus that lets many chips on one board talk to a controller. One wire carries a clock, SCL, and one carries data, SDA. Every chip has its own address, so the controller says whom it wants and only that chip answers. It is slow, from 100 kHz up to about 1 MHz, so it suits sensors, memories and clocks that need few bytes.

### Picture it
Picture a single street with numbered houses. A delivery truck goes down it, one lane for the schedule beat and one for the packages. The truck calls out a house number, and only that resident answers "got it". Nobody can drive the street gate up. Whoever needs it down pulls it, and a spring lifts it back when everyone lets go. That is why many houses can share the street without fighting. The spring is the pull-up resistor.

### Words you need
- **SCL and SDA**: the clock wire and the data wire of the bus.
- **Open-drain**: an output that can only pull the wire low. A resistor makes it high ([[q0761]]).
- **Pull-up resistor**: the resistor that lifts the wires to high when nobody pulls them low ([[q0755]]).
- **Controller and target**: the chip that starts a transfer, and the chip that answers. Older documents say master and slave.
- **Address**: a 7-bit number that picks one target.
- **ACK**: a reply bit from the receiver meaning "got it."

### The simplest example
A controller writes the value 0x3A to a chip at address 0x48. It sends a START signal, then the address 0x48 plus a "write" bit, and the chip replies with an ACK. Then it sends the byte 0x3A, gets another ACK, and finishes with a STOP signal. The two bytes take 18 clock ticks, about 180 µs at 100 kHz. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the speed modes, why open-drain matters, and related buses.
:::

Whether you know the name and the one-sentence identity of the bus: **I2C is the Inter-Integrated Circuit bus, a two-wire, multi-device, addressed, synchronous serial bus** with open-drain lines and pull-up resistors. Beyond the acronym, interviewers are checking that you can state what makes it distinctive (two wires for any number of devices, in-band addressing, acknowledge bits, multi-master capability), what it is typically used for, and what its limits are (speed ~100 kHz to 1 MHz, bus capacitance, address collisions). ::: why
I2C is the default way to configure and read low-speed chips on a board: sensors, EEPROMs, RTCs, PMICs, fuel gauges, GPIO expanders, display controllers, audio codec control ports. If you work with embedded hardware you will draw an I2C schematic, size pull-ups, and debug missing ACKs within your first few projects. The question is a gateway: once you can say what it is, the interviewer moves to operation ([[q0745]]), frames ([[q0749]]), pull-ups ([[q0755]]) and failure modes. A crisp, correct definition up front sets the tone for those follow-ups.
:::

::: concepts
- **Serial synchronous bus**: bits go one at a time, timed by a clock wire ([[q0737]], [[q0741]]).
- **Open-drain + pull-up**: devices only pull lines low; resistors pull them high ([[q0761]], [[q0755]]).
- **Controller/target addressing**: one device starts transactions; each target responds to a unique address ([[q0746]]).
- **START/STOP conditions**: special SDA edges while SCL is high that frame a transaction ([[q0745]]).
- **Acknowledge**: a 9th-bit handshake after each byte ([[q0751]]).
- **Logic levels**: thresholds at 30% and 70% of $V_{DD}$ ([[q0762]]).

"I2C stands for Inter-Integrated Circuit, usually pronounced 'I squared C'. It's a two-wire serial bus that Philips, now NXP, created in the early 1980s so that chips inside the same product could talk without needing many pins.

The two wires are SCL, the clock, and SDA, the data. Both are open-drain: any device can pull a line low, but nobody drives it high; a pull-up resistor does that. Because of this, many devices can share the same two wires without damaging each other.

Every device on the bus has an address, usually 7 bits. A controller, which used to be called the master, starts a transaction with a START condition, sends the address plus a read-write bit, and the device with that address answers with an ACK. Then bytes flow, each followed by an ACK bit, until the controller sends a STOP.

It's slow, 100 kHz standard, 400 kHz fast, 1 MHz fast-mode plus, but it's perfect for things like temperature sensors, EEPROMs, real-time clocks, power management chips, where you want lots of devices on very few pins. And it supports multiple controllers with arbitration, plus clock stretching, so a slow device can pause the clock."

::: technical
**Name and origin.** Inter-Integrated Circuit (I²C, "I-squared-C", "I2C"). Developed by Philips Semiconductors (now NXP) in the early 1980s for connecting the control ICs inside consumer electronics. Some vendors call it **TWI** (two-wire interface) for trademark or licensing reasons; the protocol is the same. The formal specification is NXP *UM10204*.

**Physical layer.** Two bidirectional open-drain lines: SDA (serial data) and SCL (serial clock), plus a shared ground, each line pulled up to $V_{DD}$ by a resistor $R_p$. A low is driven by a device; a high is the resistor releasing the line. The wired-AND behaviour means the bus is low if *any* device pulls low.

**Addressing.** 7-bit addresses (112 usable out of 128; 16 are reserved) or 10-bit addressing. The 8th bit of the address byte is the read/write bit.

**Protocol elements.** START (SDA falls while SCL high), STOP (SDA rises while SCL high), 8 data bits MSB-first per byte plus a 9th ACK/NACK clock, repeated START, optional clock stretching and multi-master arbitration.

**Speed modes.**

| Mode | Max bit rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | 100 kbit/s | $t_r \le 1000$ ns |
| Fast | 400 kbit/s | $t_r \le 300$ ns |
| Fast-mode Plus | 1 Mbit/s | 20 mA sink, $t_r \le 120$ ns |
| High-speed | 3.4 Mbit/s | special master code and current-source pull-up |
| Ultra Fast | 5 Mbit/s | unidirectional, push-pull, no ACK |

**Limits.** Total bus capacitance $C_b \le 400$ pF in Standard/Fast mode (550 pF for Fm+); address space; rise time $t_r = 0.8473\,R_pC_b$ ([[q0756]], [[q0759]]).
:::

::: long
"I2C stands for Inter-Integrated Circuit, usually written I2C or I-squared-C. Philips developed it in the early 1980s to let the control chips inside a product talk to each other over as few wires as possible, and today NXP maintains the specification.

It uses just two wires, SCL for the clock and SDA for data, plus ground. Both lines are open-drain: a device can pull the line low, but it never drives it high. A pull-up resistor to the supply makes the line high when nobody is pulling. That gives a wired-AND, so if any device pulls the line low, the line is low, and it means devices can never short each other out, which is what makes it safe to share the same two wires across a whole board.

Each target has an address, normally 7 bits. A transaction begins when a controller pulls SDA low while SCL is high, which is the START condition. The controller then clocks out the 7-bit address and a read-write bit. The addressed device pulls SDA low for the ninth clock, the acknowledge. Then data bytes follow in whichever direction, each followed by its own ACK, and the controller ends with a STOP, SDA rising while SCL is high.

Speeds are 100 kHz standard, 400 kHz fast and 1 MHz fast-mode plus, with the limit coming from how quickly the pull-up can charge the bus capacitance. That's why I2C is for housekeeping: sensors, EEPROMs, real-time clocks, PMICs, fuel gauges. Its strengths are pin count and the formal spec; weaknesses are speed and address collisions. It's also the basis of related buses: SMBus and PMBus add timeouts and command sets, and I3C is the newer, faster successor."
:::

::: terms
- **I2C / I²C / IIC**: Inter-Integrated Circuit bus.
- **TWI**: Two-Wire Interface, Atmel/Microchip's name for I2C-compatible hardware.
- **SCL / SDA**: serial clock and serial data lines.
- **Controller / target**: modern names for master / slave: who initiates and who responds.
- **Open-drain**: output that can only pull a line low ([[q0761]]).
- **Pull-up resistor ($R_p$)**: resistor to $V_{DD}$ that sets the high level ([[q0755]]).
- **START / STOP**: SDA falls / rises while SCL is high, framing a transaction.
- **ACK / NACK**: 9th-bit acknowledge (SDA low) or not-acknowledge (SDA high).
- **SMBus / PMBus**: stricter I2C derivatives with timeouts and standard command sets.
- **I3C**: MIPI's successor, backward compatible with many I2C devices.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "Inter-Integrated Circuit, two wires, SDA and SCL, used for sensors."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the **name, origin and spec** (Philips early 1980s, NXP UM10204) and notes **TWI** as an alias.
- States the **electrical idea**: open-drain lines with pull-ups, so many devices can share two wires safely (wired-AND).
- Describes the **protocol skeleton** in one breath: START, 7-bit address + R/W, ACK, data bytes with ACKs, STOP.
- Gives **speed modes** and names what limits them (rise time, bus capacitance).
- Mentions **features and limits** (multi-master, clock stretching, address collisions, 400 pF) and **relatives** (SMBus, PMBus, I3C).
- Ends with **where it is used** and where it is not (not for bulk data).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying I2C stands for "Inter-IC communication" or "Integrated Interconnect".** It is Inter-Integrated Circuit.
- **Calling the pins "TX/RX" or "MOSI/MISO".** They are SCL and SDA; the data line is bidirectional.
- **Believing devices drive both highs and lows.** They only pull low; the resistor does the high.
- **Forgetting the pull-ups.** The bus doesn't work without them; most MCUs do not include adequate ones, and many breakout boards do ([[q0755]]).
- **Treating 100/400 kHz as the transfer rate in bytes.** Each byte costs 9 clocks, so 400 kHz is at most about 44 kB/s ([[q0739]]).
- **Using master/slave as the *only* terms.** The original spec used them and the question list does too, but the current spec says controller/target. Know both.
:::

::: deep
## The problem I2C solved

In the early 1980s a TV set contained a microcontroller and a handful of control chips (tuner, audio processor, display). Connecting them with parallel buses or one chip-select line per chip was costly in pins and traces. Philips' answer was a bus where:

- every chip is on the same two wires,
- selection is done by *address* instead of by wire,
- the protocol includes a handshake so the controller knows whether the target heard it.

That description still defines I2C.

### Why open-drain is the key idea

If two push-pull outputs drive the same wire to opposite values, the transistors fight and a large current flows. Open-drain avoids this by making "high" the *absence* of driving. Any device can pull low; the line is high only when everyone lets go:

| Device A | Device B | Line |
|---|---|---|
| releases | releases | high (pull-up) |
| pulls low | releases | low |
| releases | pulls low | low |
| pulls low | pulls low | low |

That truth table is an AND gate, and it gives I2C three features for free: no bus contention damage, multi-controller arbitration ([[q0748]]) and clock stretching ([[q0752]]).

## Numbers: how fast and how big

- **Throughput**: at 100 kHz each byte takes 9 clocks = 90 µs, so at most 11.1 kB/s of payload; at 400 kHz, 44.4 kB/s. The write above is 2 bytes + START/STOP, about 18 clocks plus framing, 180 µs at 100 kHz and 45 µs at 400 kHz.
- **Rise time**: $t_r = 0.8473\,R_pC_b$. With 4.7 kΩ and 100 pF it is 398 ns, fine in Standard mode (limit 1000 ns), too slow for Fast mode (300 ns).
- **Addresses**: 7 bits give 128 codes; 16 are reserved (0x00 to 0x07 and 0x78 to 0x7F), leaving 112 usable. 10-bit mode extends this ([[q0747]], [[q0749]]).
- **Capacitance**: the spec caps $C_b$ at 400 pF (550 pF for Fm+). With about 10 pF per device and roughly 1 pF per cm of trace this is a few tens of devices at most.

## The family tree

- **TWI**: Atmel/Microchip name; same protocol, sometimes without full feature support.
- **SMBus**: Intel-originated derivative used on PC motherboards and in battery packs. It adds a clock-low timeout (a device that sees SCL held low for more than 25 to 35 ms resets its interface), a minimum clock of 10 kHz, and defined transaction types (Read Byte, Write Word, and so on). Up to version 2.0 the clock range was 10 to 100 kHz; version 3.0 (2014) added 400 kHz and 1 MHz classes. Its input thresholds are fixed voltages rather than fractions of $V_{DD}$ ($V_{IL}=0.8$ V and $V_{IH}=2.1$ V in SMBus 2.0; 3.0 lowered $V_{IH}$ to 1.35 V), which matters when mixing it with I2C at low supply voltages.
- **PMBus**: a power-management command set layered on SMBus, used by regulator controllers and power modules.
- **I3C**: MIPI's successor with push-pull data phases, higher rates, in-band interrupts and dynamic addressing, designed so many I2C targets can coexist.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> SMBus voltage thresholds and timeout figures differ between SMBus versions and between vendor implementations. If a design mixes SMBus and I2C parts, check each datasheet's $V_{IL}$/$V_{IH}$ and timeout behaviour rather than relying on these approximate numbers.

## Strengths and weaknesses in one table

| Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|
| Two pins for many devices | Slow compared with SPI |
| Address-based, no chip-select | Address collisions between parts |
| ACK confirms delivery | Bus capacitance limits devices and speed |
| Multi-controller capable | Needs pull-ups; wrong values cause edge problems |
| Formal spec; widely supported | Half duplex; protocol overhead |
| Clock stretching for slow targets | A stuck target can hang the whole bus |

## Where you meet it in practice

- **Sensors**: temperature, humidity, pressure, light, IMU (low-rate).
- **Memory and time**: EEPROMs (24-series), real-time clocks.
- **Power**: PMICs, battery fuel gauges, chargers, voltage-regulator control (PMBus).
- **I/O and control**: GPIO expanders, LED drivers, DAC/ADC with I2C interface, audio-codec control ports, HDMI/DDC (monitor EDID is read over an I2C-based channel).
- **Debug and bring-up**: scanning the bus with a logic analyzer to find which addresses respond.
:::

::: applications
- **Temperature and environmental sensors** in thermostats and wearables, read once a second over I2C.
- **24-series serial EEPROMs** storing calibration constants and serial numbers.
- **Real-time clock chips** with battery backup holding time while the MCU sleeps.
- **Battery fuel gauges and chargers** (SMBus-compatible) reporting state of charge to a host.
- **GPIO expanders** adding extra I/O pins through two MCU pins.
- **Monitor EDID** (display data channel): your computer reads the display's capabilities over an I2C-style channel in the video cable.
:::

::: followups
### What does the acronym stand for, and why the "squared"?
Inter-Integrated Circuit; the original notation was I²C, a superscript 2 meaning "I-squared-C". In plain text it's written I2C or IIC. Some vendors use TWI (Two-Wire Interface) for compatible hardware, mostly for legal reasons.

### Why does I2C use open-drain instead of push-pull outputs?
So that multiple devices can share a line without contention. If two push-pull outputs drive opposite levels, they short the supply through the transistors. With open-drain a high is just "nobody is pulling", so any device pulling low wins safely, which also enables multi-controller arbitration and clock stretching.

### How many devices can be on an I2C bus?
Address space allows 112 with 7-bit addressing (more with 10-bit). The electrical limit is usually tighter: 400 pF of bus capacitance. At about 10 pF per device plus traces, roughly 20 to 40 devices. See [[q0747]].

### An I2C bus works with one device but fails when you add a second. What do you check?
Address clash (both parts using the same address), bus capacitance and rise time (extra pin and trace capacitance), pull-up values (two sets of pull-ups in parallel), and level compatibility if the new device is a different voltage. A scope on SCL/SDA, and a bus scan, tell you which.

### What is the difference between I2C and SMBus?
SMBus is derived from I2C with extra rules: a 10 kHz minimum clock and (classically) a 100 kHz maximum, raised to 1 MHz in SMBus 3.0, a timeout so a stuck bus recovers, defined message formats (including optional packet error checking) and fixed input thresholds. Most I2C devices work on SMBus and vice versa, but don't assume it: voltage thresholds and timing differ.
:::
