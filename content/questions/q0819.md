::: testing
Whether you separate **resolution** (how finely the ADC divides its range: the LSB) from **accuracy** (how close the code is to the truth) and can calculate both from given data. The interviewer expects $V_{LSB}=V_{FS}/2^N$ immediately, then the error terms that make real converters worse than their bit count (offset, gain error, INL, DNL, noise, reference error), a way to combine them (worst-case sum or root-sum-square), and ENOB from SINAD. "Calculate it given all the required info" means you should say what information you need, then do the arithmetic with units.
:::

::: why
Datasheets sell bits; systems live on accuracy. A 12-bit ADC on a 1% reference gives a 1% reading, no matter how fine the LSB is. Being able to build an error budget decides whether a sensor design meets spec, whether you need a better reference, calibration, oversampling or an external converter, and it avoids reporting digits that are noise. It's also the vocabulary used in every ADC datasheet, and it ties to the DAC version ([[q0822]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Resolution / LSB**: $V_{LSB}=V_{FS}/2^N$. See [[q0808]].
- **Quantization error**: ±½ LSB, rms $V_{LSB}/\sqrt{12}$.
- **Offset and gain error**: shift and slope errors of the whole transfer line.
- **DNL / INL**: code-width and straight-line deviations, in LSB.
- **Noise and ENOB**: analog noise raises the effective LSB; $\text{ENOB}=(\text{SINAD}-1.76)/6.02$.
- **Reference accuracy**: scales every reading ([[q0812]]).
:::

::: answer
"I separate resolution from accuracy. Resolution is the smallest step the ADC can distinguish: one LSB equals full scale over $2^N$. For a 12-bit ADC with a 3.3 V reference that's 3.3 over 4096, 0.806 mV, or 0.024% of full scale. That's the best-case precision, plus or minus half an LSB from quantization.

To get what you can actually trust, I'd add the real errors from the datasheet. Offset error, say 2 LSB. Gain error, which includes the reference tolerance: 0.1% is 3.3 mV, about 4 LSB. INL, the bow of the transfer curve, say 1.5 LSB. And noise: if the code flickers by 1 LSB rms, I'd budget three sigma, 3 LSB. Add them in worst case and I get about 11 LSB, 9 mV; root-sum-square gives about 5.7 LSB, 4.6 mV. So a '12-bit' ADC is accurate to about 9.5 bits unless I calibrate out offset and gain.

For dynamic performance I'd use ENOB: measure SINAD with a sine, subtract 1.76 and divide by 6.02. An SINAD of 66 dB is 10.7 effective bits.

So I'd say the precision is 0.8 mV per code, but the accuracy is about 5 mV before calibration, and I'd improve it with a better reference, a two-point calibration, and oversampling and averaging for noise."
:::

::: technical
**Resolution.** $V_{LSB}=\dfrac{V_{FS}}{2^N}$; relative resolution $=2^{-N}$ of full scale. Examples (3.3 V reference):

| $N$ | $V_{LSB}$ | % of FS | Ideal SNR |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | 12.89 mV | 0.391% | 49.9 dB |
| 10 | 3.223 mV | 0.098% | 62.0 dB |
| 12 | 0.806 mV | 0.0244% | 74.0 dB |
| 14 | 0.201 mV | 0.0061% | 86.0 dB |
| 16 | 50.4 µV | 0.0015% | 98.1 dB |

**Quantization error.** ±½ LSB peak, $V_{LSB}/\sqrt{12}$ rms.

**Static error terms** (datasheet, in LSB or % FS): offset $E_O$, gain error $E_G$ (including reference), INL (max deviation from the best-fit or end-point line), DNL (deviation of each code width from 1 LSB; DNL $>-1$ LSB guarantees no missing codes). *Total unadjusted error (TUE)* combines them.

**Combining errors.** Worst case: sum of magnitudes. Statistical: root-sum-square, valid for independent terms:

$$E_{RSS}=\sqrt{E_O^2+E_G^2+E_{INL}^2+E_Q^2+(3\sigma_n)^2}$$

**Worked example (12-bit, 3.3 V).** Offset 2 LSB, gain/reference 0.1% FS (4.1 LSB), INL 1.5 LSB, quantization 0.5 LSB, noise $\sigma=1$ LSB rms ($3\sigma=3$ LSB):
- Worst case: $2+4.1+1.5+0.5+3=11.1$ LSB $=8.9$ mV.
- RSS: $\sqrt{4+16.8+2.25+0.25+9}=5.68$ LSB $=4.6$ mV.
- Effective accuracy: $12-\log_2(5.68)=9.5$ bits.
- After a two-point calibration removing offset and gain: $\sqrt{2.25+0.25+9}=3.4$ LSB $=2.7$ mV, about 10.2 bits.

**Dynamic: ENOB.** $\text{SINAD}$ from a full-scale sine test, then
$$\text{ENOB}=\frac{\text{SINAD}-1.76}{6.02}$$
For a 12-bit ADC with 1 LSB rms analog noise plus quantization: total noise $=\sqrt{1+1/12}=1.041$ LSB; full-scale sine rms $=4096/(2\sqrt2)=1448$ LSB; $\text{SNR}=62.9$ dB, $\text{ENOB}=10.15$.

**Noise-free resolution.** Peak-to-peak noise $\approx6.6\sigma$; noise-free bits $=N-\log_2(6.6\sigma_{LSB})$: here $12-\log_2(6.6)=9.3$ bits.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Datasheets define INL (best-fit versus end-point), gain error (with or without reference) and ENOB (which sine amplitude, which bandwidth) differently, and sign conventions vary. Check the definitions for the part before combining numbers.
:::

::: long
"I always split this into resolution and accuracy, because people mix them up.

Resolution is the easy part: one LSB is full scale divided by $2^N$. For a 12-bit ADC on 3.3 V that's 3.3 over 4096, about 0.8 millivolts, or 0.024% of full scale. Even a perfect converter has ±half an LSB of quantization error, so that's the best-case precision, plus an ideal SNR of 6.02N plus 1.76, 74 dB for 12 bits.

Real converters do worse. From the datasheet I'd take offset error, gain error, which includes the reference tolerance, INL and DNL. I'd also include noise, which I'd measure by shorting the input and looking at the standard deviation of the codes.

As a worked example: offset of 2 LSB, a 0.1% reference giving about 4 LSB of gain error, INL of 1.5, quantization 0.5, and noise of 1 LSB rms, so 3 LSB at three sigma. Worst case adds to about 11 LSB, 9 millivolts. If the errors are independent, root-sum-square gives 5.7 LSB, or 4.6 millivolts. That corresponds to about 9.5 bits of accuracy out of a 12-bit part.

For dynamic specs I'd use ENOB: put a full-scale sine in, take an FFT, compute SINAD, then ENOB equals SINAD minus 1.76 over 6.02. So 66 dB is 10.7 bits.

Then I'd say how to improve it: calibrate out offset and gain with a two-point measurement, use a better reference, and oversample and average to reduce noise, which gains half a bit for every doubling of samples, as long as there's enough noise to dither the quantizer. Linearity errors like INL I can't average away, but a lookup table calibration can help."
:::

::: terms
- **Resolution**: number of bits $N$, or equivalently $V_{LSB}$.
- **Accuracy**: closeness of the reading to the true value, including all error sources.
- **Precision (repeatability)**: scatter of repeated readings; sets the noise floor.
- **DNL**: deviation of each code's width from 1 LSB.
- **INL**: deviation of the transfer function from an ideal straight line.
- **Offset / gain error**: intercept and slope errors of the transfer line.
- **TUE**: total unadjusted error, combining offset, gain, INL and quantization.
- **SINAD / ENOB**: signal to noise-and-distortion ratio and the effective number of bits derived from it.
- **Missing code**: a code that never appears (DNL = −1 LSB).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** gives $V_{ref}/2^N$ and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- States **resolution versus accuracy versus precision** clearly, and says which one the question is asking for.
- Computes the LSB **with units** and as a percentage, and mentions ±½ LSB quantization.
- Lists the **error terms** (offset, gain, reference, INL/DNL, noise) and **combines them** both worst-case and RSS.
- Converts to **effective bits** ($N-\log_2$ of the error in LSB) and to ENOB from SINAD.
- Says what **calibration can and can't remove** (offset/gain yes; noise and INL mostly no).
- Gives a **practical improvement plan** (reference, calibration, oversampling).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Equating bits with accuracy.** A 16-bit ADC on a 0.5% reference gives 0.5% readings.
- **Quoting more digits than the error allows.** 2.41699 V from a converter accurate to ±5 mV is false precision.
- **Adding RSS to systematic errors.** Offset and gain are systematic, so use worst case or calibrate them out; RSS is for independent random terms.
- **Forgetting reference error** in the gain error.
- **Using 6.02N+1.76 as the real SNR.** That's the ideal ceiling; real SINAD is lower, giving ENOB below $N$.
- **Assuming averaging always helps.** Averaging $M$ samples reduces random noise by $\sqrt M$, but not offset, gain, INL or reference drift, and not quantization if the input is noiseless.
:::

::: deep
## Three words that get confused

| Word | Meaning | Example (12-bit, 3.3 V) |
|---|---|---|
| **Resolution** | Smallest step the converter distinguishes | 0.806 mV per code |
| **Precision** (repeatability) | Scatter of repeated measurements of one input | ±1 LSB rms noise |
| **Accuracy** | Distance from the true value | ±4.6 mV (RSS) before calibration |

The question says "precision" and then "calculate it given all the required info". Give resolution first because it's the unambiguous number, then say accuracy requires the datasheet terms and work through them.

## Resolution from first principles

An $N$-bit ADC has $2^N$ output codes covering a span $V_{FS}$, so each code covers $V_{FS}/2^N$. Ideal quantization error is uniformly distributed over $\pm\tfrac12V_{LSB}$ with rms $V_{LSB}/\sqrt{12}$. For a 12-bit converter with a 3.3 V reference, $V_{LSB}=0.806$ mV and the quantization rms is 233 µV. If you measure a temperature sensor with 10 mV/°C, one code is 0.08 °C, but whether you believe the second decimal depends on the rest of the budget.

## From resolution to accuracy: the error budget

Suppose the datasheet says (12-bit, 3.3 V reference, 25 °C):

| Error | Datasheet or measured | In LSB | In mV |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quantization | ±½ LSB | 0.5 | 0.40 |
| Offset | ±2 LSB | 2.0 | 1.61 |
| Gain error (incl. 0.1% reference) | 0.1% FS | 4.1 | 3.30 |
| INL | ±1.5 LSB | 1.5 | 1.21 |
| Noise (3σ of 1 LSB rms) | 3 LSB | 3.0 | 2.42 |
| **Worst-case sum** | | **11.1** | **8.94** |
| **RSS** | | **5.68** | **4.58** |

(DNL is not added separately; it is already contained in INL, which accumulates it.)

Effective accuracy in bits $=N-\log_2(E_{LSB})$. Using the RSS value: $12-\log_2(5.68)=9.5$ bits. Using the worst case: $12-\log_2(11.1)=8.5$ bits. The real converter gives you somewhere between 8.5 and 9.5 bits of *absolute* accuracy, though its *relative* resolution (differences between close readings) is closer to 12 bits, less noise.

**What calibration buys.** A two-point calibration (measure two known voltages, solve for gain and offset) removes the offset and gain terms, leaving INL, quantization and noise:
$\sqrt{1.5^2+0.5^2+3^2}=3.4$ LSB $=2.7$ mV, about 10.2 bits. Without a better reference *and* temperature compensation, drift quickly erodes this, which is why production systems calibrate once and re-check with a stable reference.

## INL and DNL: linearity, in pictures

**DNL** is the error in each code's *width*: ideal width is 1 LSB, a code of width 1.3 LSB has DNL +0.3, a code of width 0 (missing) has DNL −1. If DNL ≤ −1 LSB the code is missing; if every DNL is greater than −1 the converter is monotonic.

**INL** is the cumulative deviation from a straight line through the transfer curve. It is the running sum of DNL: $\text{INL}_k=\sum_{i\le k}\text{DNL}_i$ (after removing offset and gain). Typical SAR ADCs show INL of ±1 to ±2 LSB at 12 bits; INL appears as harmonic distortion on a sine input.

You measure both with a **code-density (histogram) test**: apply a slow, linear ramp, histogram the codes, and compare each count with the average. A bin with 1.3× the mean count has DNL +0.3.

## Dynamic precision: SINAD and ENOB

Resolution and linearity describe DC behaviour. For a moving signal, the useful metric is SINAD, the ratio of signal power to *everything else* (noise and distortion):

$$\text{ENOB}=\frac{\text{SINAD}_{dB}-1.76}{6.02}$$

where SINAD is measured with a (nearly) full-scale sine. A datasheet SINAD of 66 dB gives $(66-1.76)/6.02=10.67$ bits. ENOB falls with input frequency (S/H aperture jitter, settling), so always check at the frequency you use.

## Averaging: buying back precision

If noise (not quantization) dominates and it is random and uncorrelated, averaging $M$ samples reduces it by $\sqrt M$. Four times as many samples is one extra bit (6 dB). With 1 LSB rms noise, 16 samples give 0.25 LSB rms, bringing ENOB from about 10.2 to about 11.5. This does nothing for offset, gain, INL or reference drift, and with a noiseless input the quantizer's staircase doesn't average away at all (you need a little noise or dither to make the staircase "smooth"). Oversampling is covered with the front end in [[q0818]].

## Calculating "given all the required info"

Here is the checklist of information to ask for or assume, and then compute:

1. $N$ and $V_{ref+},V_{ref-}$: gives $V_{LSB}$, quantization (±½ LSB), ideal SNR.
2. Offset and gain error specs, and reference tolerance and drift: gives systematic error.
3. INL and DNL: gives linearity error.
4. Noise (rms in LSB or µV): gives random error and noise-free bits.
5. SINAD (and the test frequency): gives ENOB.
6. Temperature range: scales drift terms.

If a datasheet gave TUE, you can use that directly as the accuracy: for example, "±5 LSB TUE" means ±4 mV on 3.3 V at 12 bits.
:::

::: applications
- **Choosing between an MCU's internal ADC and an external 16 to 24-bit ADC**, based on an error budget.
- **Battery fuel gauging**: 12-bit resolution gives 1 mV steps but a 0.5% reference limits cell voltage accuracy to about 20 mV.
- **Thermistor and RTD measurement**: precision requirements in °C converted to volts to LSBs.
- **Load cell and strain gauge scales**: using noise-free resolution and ENOB to decide how many counts the display can show.
- **Production test**: two-point or multi-point calibration to remove offset and gain.
- **Audio and instrumentation**: SINAD and ENOB specs for ADCs in data acquisition.
:::

::: assumptions
- A **unipolar, single-ended** ADC. For bipolar converters the LSB is $V_{FS}/2^N$ over $\pm V_{ref}$, and offset/gain definitions change slightly.
- Error terms are **independent and random** when using RSS; systematic terms should be summed or calibrated out.
- The example values (2 LSB offset, 1.5 LSB INL, 1 LSB noise, 0.1% reference) are illustrative, typical of MCU SARs, not any specific part.
- **Full-scale input** for SNR/ENOB. At lower amplitudes SINAD falls proportionally.
- "Precision" is interpreted mainly as resolution plus accuracy, as the wording leaves it open ([[q0822]] asks the DAC equivalent).
:::

::: followups
### What is the resolution of a 16-bit ADC with a 4.096 V reference?
$4.096/65536=62.5$ µV per code (0.0015% of full scale), a convenient number: 4.096 V references are used so that one LSB at 12 bits is exactly 1 mV. Quantization error is ±31 µV.

### An ADC datasheet says INL = ±2 LSB and DNL = ±0.5 LSB. Which sets absolute accuracy?
INL does, because it measures the total deviation from the ideal line, while DNL only describes step-to-step variation. DNL under ±1 LSB means no missing codes; INL of ±2 LSB at 12 bits is ±1.6 mV on 3.3 V.

### How many bits do I really gain from averaging 64 samples?
Up to $\log_4 64=3$ bits if the noise is random, uncorrelated and larger than about 0.5 LSB. In practice you gain fewer because of 1/f noise, reference noise correlated across samples and quantization with too little noise.

### Why does ENOB fall at high input frequency?
Aperture jitter converts timing error into amplitude error proportional to frequency, S/H settling and charge injection become signal-dependent, and distortion rises. A converter specified as 12 ENOB at 1 kHz might give 10.5 at 1 MHz.

### Harder: your 12-bit ADC reads 2.4170 V, 2.4195 V, 2.4162 V and 2.4180 V for a constant 2.4200 V input. What can you say?
The scatter is about 3.3 mV peak-to-peak (σ about 1.4 mV, about 1.8 LSB), so noise is above the quantization floor, and the mean (2.4177 V) is 2.3 mV, about 2.9 LSB, below the truth. A systematic error, probably gain/offset/reference, plus random noise. Calibrate the systematic part and average the noise.
:::

::: interpretations
"Precision" is used loosely. In metrology, precision is repeatability and accuracy is closeness to truth; in ADC marketing, "precision ADC" often means high resolution and low noise. This page gives (a) the resolution ($V_{LSB}$, the number that is always computable), (b) the accuracy via an error budget, and (c) ENOB for dynamic precision, so whichever the interviewer means is covered. The DAC counterpart is [[q0822]].
:::
