::: basics
### In plain words
By convention, ground goes on layer 2, directly under the top signals, and on the layer next to the bottom. On bigger boards you add more ground planes so that every signal layer has one beside it. The reason is that a fast signal's return current runs right under it, and a thin gap between the signal layer and a ground plane gives tight return loops and narrow traces. On a 2-layer board, ground is the bottom layer.

### Picture it
Think of a restaurant where every table must sit next to an aisle so the servers can reach it. A routing layer is a table, and a ground plane is the aisle beside it. The closer the aisle, the easier the service. The analogy is loose: the plane also shields and stores charge.

### Words you need
- **Reference plane**: the plane next to a signal layer that carries its return current ([[q0478]]).
- **Prepreg**: the thin glue layer between outer copper and the first plane ([[q0474]]).
- **Impedance**: the ratio of voltage to current a fast signal sees on a trace, in ohms (Ω). It is set by trace width and distance to the plane.
- **Stripline**: an inner trace sandwiched between two planes ([[q0479]]).
- **Symmetric stackup**: layers mirrored around the center so the board stays flat ([[q0476]]).

### The simplest example
Compare where the plane sits for a 50 Ω trace, using the page's quick formula (IPC-2141). With ground 0.2 mm below on layer 2, the trace is 0.335 mm wide. With the plane 1.6 mm away on the far side of the board, the same impedance needs about 2.99 mm, close to 3 mm. A 0.335 mm trace can squeeze out between the closely spaced pins of a small chip. A 3 mm trace cannot. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page lists the conventions for 2 to 10 layers, why planes are not on the outer layers, and the exceptions.
:::

::: why
Fab houses, reference designs and layout reviewers all assume these conventions, and following them avoids a lot of avoidable problems. Knowing the *why* lets you adapt: a thick-core 4-layer board, an RF board with a thin top laminate, or a 10-layer board with several grounds. This is a standard recall question in hardware interviews, usually followed by "and why not on the top layer?"
:::

::: concepts
- **Reference plane adjacency**: signal layers want an adjacent plane. See [[q0478]].
- **Prepreg vs core thickness**: thin prepreg outside, thick core inside. See [[q0474]].
- **Stripline vs microstrip**: inner layers between two planes. See [[q0479]].
- **Plane pairing**: ground beside power. See [[q0480]].
- **Symmetry**: balanced stack for flatness. See [[q0476]].
- **Plane functions**: return, shield, impedance, heat. See [[q0489]].
:::

::: answer
"The convention is: ground on layer 2, ground on the layer next to the bottom, and more ground planes wherever there's an inner signal layer that needs one.

On a two-layer board it's the bottom layer. On a four-layer board it's layer 2, directly under the top signals, with power on layer 3 and the bottom layer for more signals; sometimes layer 3 is ground too if I want both outer layers to have a ground neighbour. On six layers it's layers 2 and 5, so each outer layer sits next to a ground and the inner signal layer sits between a ground and a power plane. On eight and more I keep adding grounds so every signal layer is next to one.

The reasons: the outer layers are the ones with parts and the fastest, most exposed routing, and the prepreg next to them is thin, so a ground plane there gives a tight return, narrow 50 ohm traces and a small loop. Putting ground right beside power makes a capacitor that helps decoupling. Ground in the middle shields inner signal layers from each other. And the stack stays symmetric so it doesn't warp. I don't normally put the planes on the outer layers because that's where the components and routing are; there I use pours stitched to the planes."
:::

::: technical
**Conventions**

| Layers | Ground planes | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Bottom (L2) | 1.6 mm from the top signals: wide $Z_0$ traces, larger loops |
| 4 | L2 (S/G/P/S) or L2+L3 (S/G/G/S) | Prepreg ~0.1–0.2 mm to L1; bottom references L3 |
| 6 | L2, L5 | Inner signal layers between G and P/G |
| 8 | L2, L5, L7 (e.g., S/G/S/P/G/S/G/S) | Every signal layer has a plane neighbour |
| 10 | L2, L4, L7, L9 (e.g., S/G/S/G/S/P/G/S/G/S) | Several stripline layers between grounds |

**Why L2.** With prepreg $h=0.2$ mm, a 50 Ω microstrip needs $w=0.335$ mm; with the plane 1.6 mm away it needs about 3 mm. Narrow traces enable fine-pitch escape. The loop inductance is about 0.3 nH/mm versus 0.7 nH/mm. 

**Why not on L1/L$n$.** The outer layers hold the parts and exposed routing. A solid plane there would be punctured by every pad and trace, and the parts would sit on the plane's side, destroying the return-path advantage. Outer-layer *pours* tied to the planes by stitching vias add shielding and heat spreading.

**Closed form for width.** Inverting the IPC-2141 microstrip approximation: $w=\frac{5.98h\,e^{-Z_0\sqrt{\varepsilon_r+1.41}/87}-t}{0.8}$. For $Z_0=50$ Ω, $\varepsilon_r=4.3$, $t=35$ µm: $w=0.146$ mm at $h=0.1$ mm; 0.335 mm at 0.2 mm; 1.85 mm at 1.0 mm; 2.99 mm at 1.6 mm.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The IPC-2141 closed form is approximate (valid for moderate $w/h$), and stackups differ per fab. The positions in the table are conventions, not standards; processor and memory vendors specify their own.
:::

::: long
"The convention I follow is ground on layer 2, ground next to the bottom layer, and extra ground planes beside any inner signal layer.

On a two-layer board there's only one choice, the bottom as a ground pour. On a four-layer board it's layer 2, right under the top signals, and power on layer 3; if I want both outer layers to have a ground neighbour, I'll make layer 3 ground as well and distribute power with pours. On six layers it's layers 2 and 5. Then both outside layers have a ground next to them, and inner signal layers are between a ground and a power plane. On eight or ten layers I keep adding grounds so that every routing layer is adjacent to one, with each stripline layer between planes.

The reasons all come back to return current. At high frequency the return runs directly under the trace, so the closer the plane, the smaller the loop. The outer layers have thin prepreg, so a plane next to them gives narrow 50 ohm traces, 0.335 millimetres at 0.2 millimetres of dielectric instead of about 3 millimetres over a 1.6 millimetre board, and a small loop. A ground next to a power plane is a capacitor, so it helps decoupling. A ground in the middle shields inner signal layers from each other. And the pattern is symmetric, so the board stays flat.

I don't put the planes on the outer layers because that's where the parts are and where the fast, exposed routing goes. A plane there would be full of holes, and the signals would be on the wrong side of it. Instead I use ground pours with stitching vias on the outside. The exceptions are RF boards, where the thin RF laminate is on top over a ground, and some thick-core four-layer boards where the vendor's guide says otherwise."
:::

::: terms
- **Reference plane**: plane providing the return path and impedance reference.
- **Prepreg / core**: thin bonding layer / thick rigid layer between copper.
- **Microstrip / stripline**: outer / inner trace geometry.
- **Plane pair**: adjacent power and ground planes.
- **Stitching**: vias tying ground layers and pours together.
- **Symmetry**: balanced layer stack about the centre.
- **Return path**: path of current back to the source.
- **Controlled impedance**: trace geometry chosen to hit a target $Z_0$.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer gives the layer table for 2/4/6/8 layers and the reasons in terms of dielectric thickness and return current, with a number (0.335 mm vs 3 mm width for 50 Ω). It knows the main variants (S/G/G/S, ground-power pairing) and exceptions (RF laminate, vendor stackups) and why outer layers are not planes (components, routing, hole punching). It also mentions stitching of outer pours and symmetry.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Ground is always layer 2 and layer $n-1$."** That's the usual pattern, not a law; the principle is adjacency to signal layers.
- **Putting ground next to the thick core only** so signals are far from their reference.
- **Using outer layers for full planes** and putting signals inside with no plane neighbour.
- **Forgetting that the bottom layer of S/G/P/S references power**, not ground.
- **Ignoring symmetry.**
- **Assuming a ground pour on the outside is equivalent to a plane.**
:::

## The principle, restated as a rule

*Every routing layer should have a continuous ground plane as its nearest neighbour on at least one side, separated by a thin dielectric.* Everything in the table is a consequence of that rule plus stack symmetry and plane pairing.

The same distance sets loop inductance and the spread of the return current (80% within ±3h). See [[q0478]].

## Why the second ground is next to the bottom layer

The bottom layer deserves the same treatment as the top: components and signals live there, and without a close plane its signals have long return loops. A symmetric stack (ground at L2 and L$n$-1) keeps the electrical behaviour and mechanical balance the same top and bottom.

The four-layer S/G/P/S stack is the cost-driven exception: the bottom references the power plane through thin prepreg. It works if the power plane is solid and well decoupled. S/G/G/S fixes it at the cost of distributing power by pours.

## Why ground beside power

A ground plane next to a power plane forms the capacitor and low-inductance spreading path discussed in [[q0480]]. A typical 6-layer stack puts P at L4 and G at L5 for this reason; the 8-layer stack puts P at L4 and G at L5 similarly, with an additional G at L2 and L7.

## Why grounds in the middle for stripline

An inner signal layer between two ground planes (stripline) is fully shielded. In 8- and 10-layer boards each inner signal layer is flanked by planes. The two planes should be tied together (stitching) so that the return current can use both without exciting the cavity. See [[q0487]].

## Why not on the outer layers

1. **Components and exposed routing.** The outer layers are where parts are mounted; pads and traces puncture any plane there.
2. **Return path orientation.** For signals routed on L1 you want the plane *below* them at 0.2 mm. A plane on L1 itself puts the signals on the other side of the dielectric from where the field wants to go.
3. **Assembly and heat.** A large copper area on an outer layer draws heat during soldering (thermal reliefs), so you don't want it everywhere.

Outer-layer pours still help: stitched ground pours provide extra shielding and heat spreading.

## Exceptions

- **RF boards**: a thin low-loss RF laminate on top over a ground plane; the rest FR-4. The ground is still L2.
- **Thick-core 4-layer boards** where the fab can't do thin prepreg: the benefits shrink; check the stack.
- **High-power boards**: heavy copper may be put on outer layers as power/ground pours, and the planes still on inner layers.
- **Vendor stackups** for DDR/SerDes: they may require ground on specific layers.
- **Flex and rigid-flex**: the stack differs; keep the principle.

## A check before sign-off

1. Does every routing layer have a ground neighbour?
2. Is the thickness between each signal layer and its reference known, from the fab?
3. Are the ground planes stitched together (edge, connectors, layer changes)?
4. Is the stack symmetric?
5. Are power planes next to ground wherever possible?

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If you only have one sentence: "Ground goes next to every signal layer: layer 2 and the layer before the bottom, plus more in the middle on bigger stacks, because the return current wants to be right under the trace."

## Worked example: a 90 Ω USB pair on layer 1 of the 4-layer stack

The page's scenario mentions a USB pair; here is the arithmetic for getting a differential trace geometry from the plane position. A common approximation for edge-coupled microstrip pairs is $Z_{diff}\approx2Z_0\left(1-0.48\,e^{-0.96\,s/h}\right)$, where $s$ is the edge-to-edge gap and $h$ is the height above the reference plane. The target is $Z_{diff}=90\ \Omega$. With $h=0.2$ mm and a gap equal to the height ($s/h=1$):

$$1-0.48e^{-0.96}=1-0.48(0.383)=0.816,\qquad Z_0=\frac{90}{2(0.816)}=55.1\ \Omega$$

Using the inverted IPC-2141 microstrip relation ($\varepsilon_r=4.3$, 35 µm copper), a 55.1 Ω single-ended trace at $h=0.2$ mm is $w\approx0.285$ mm. (The more accurate Hammerstad–Jensen microstrip model, used by the site's microstrip calculator and in the width table of [[q0523]], gives 0.30 mm for the same 55 Ω; the simple IPC-2141 formula runs about 5 % narrow at this $w/h$. Both pages use the same coupling factor, so the 0.285 mm and 0.30 mm figures are the same design computed with two single-ended models.) Using the IPC value, the pair occupies $2(0.285)+0.2=0.77$ mm of routing width, a pitch of 0.485 mm between trace centers. Now thin the prepreg to $h=0.1$ mm with $s=0.1$ mm (same $s/h$, same $Z_0$): the width is $w\approx0.12$ mm (0.14 mm by Hammerstad–Jensen) and the pair is 0.34 mm wide, about 2.3 times narrower. If the plane were instead on the far side of a 1.6 mm board, a single 50 Ω trace alone needs about 3 mm of width (2.99 mm IPC, 3.07 mm Hammerstad–Jensen), so a coupled pair would need well over 6 mm and could not be routed to a fine-pitch connector at all. That is the strongest argument for ground on layer 2: the plane's distance, not the stack's name, decides whether controlled-impedance routing is physically possible.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The differential-pair formula is an approximation for loosely to moderately coupled pairs; fabs use field solvers and their real dielectric thickness and etch factor. The single-ended model matters too: IPC-2141 gives 0.285 mm and Hammerstad–Jensen 0.30 mm here. Treat either as a starting point for the fab's calculator, not as a drawing dimension.

## Worked example: which plane does the return current use at a layer change?

Take an 8-layer S/G/S/P/G/S/G/S board and a fast signal that changes from L3 to L6 through a via. L3 sits between the L2 ground and the L4 power plane; L6 sits between the L5 ground and the L7 ground. On L3 the return current prefers whichever neighbor is closer or lower impedance, which for a symmetric build is shared between the ground and the power plane. On L6 both neighbors are ground. So at the via the return current must hop from the L2 ground (and the L4 power plane) to the L5 and L7 grounds. It can do this through a ground stitching via placed next to the signal via. With a 1 mm gap and 0.15 mm via radius across roughly a 1.2 mm via length between L2 and L7 the loop is of order

$$L\approx\frac{\mu_0\ell}{\pi}\ln\frac{s}{r}=\frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(1.2\times10^{-3})}{\pi}\ln\frac{1}{0.15}\approx0.9\ \text{nH}$$

which at 500 MHz is $2\pi(5\times10^{8})(0.9\times10^{-9})=2.8\ \Omega$ in the return: acceptable for a single stitching via but not for a bus of 16 signals that all hop planes with one via between them. Rules that follow: place a ground via beside every signal via that changes reference planes, or keep a signal on layers that share the same plane pair, and where the reference changes from ground to power add a decoupling capacitor within a few millimeters.

## Limiting cases: when the table's conventions bend

- **Fewer layers than signals need.** On a 4-layer board where both outer layers carry fast signals, S/G/G/S is the better convention than S/G/P/S, because the bottom signals gain a ground neighbor. The cost is that power must be routed on the outer layers.
- **Odd planes.** A 5-layer board is possible but asymmetric; the fab usually builds it as a 6-layer anyway, and the sixth layer is best spent as another ground.
- **Vendor-mandated stacks.** Processor and memory vendors specify not just the order of layers but the dielectric thicknesses, which they derived by simulating the interface. If the vendor's reference contradicts a convention in this page, follow the vendor, and flag the difference in your design review.
- **High-power boards.** With 3 oz or 4 oz copper on the power layers, the "plane" is thick enough that its prepreg must be thicker to fill the etched gaps, which raises the plane separation; the rule becomes a balance between power and signal needs.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **Plane thickness and etch.** Inner copper is typically thinner than outer copper (for example 17.5 µm versus 35 µm after plating), and the foil you specify is not the finished thickness on the outer layers because plating adds to it. Ask the fab for the finished numbers when you do impedance calculations.
- **Plane edge pullback.** Many fabs and rules require inner planes to be pulled back from the board edge (for example by 0.25 mm or more) to avoid exposing copper at the routed edge. That pullback affects edge stitching, so check the fab's rule against your edge-via plan.
- **Plane connections at the connector.** Connector shells and mounting holes are where the ground planes should be tied together and to chassis: several vias at the shell, not one.
- **Keep-outs.** Some parts (antennas, wireless modules, inductors, high-voltage isolation barriers) demand no copper on any layer under them. The convention that "L2 is ground" gives way locally, and traces crossing that region need an alternate reference.

## Bench verification

You can read the stackup off a finished board. The fab's stackup report gives the dielectric heights. A cross-section (microsection) of a coupon or a scrap board under a microscope shows the true layer order and thicknesses, which is the definitive check that L2 is ground and the prepreg is the thickness you assumed. Electrically, measure the capacitance between two planes with an LCR meter to confirm which layers are adjacent and how thick the dielectric is between them: for 20 in² overlap, a 0.1 mm core reads near 4.9 nF and a 1 mm core near 0.49 nF. A TDR of a coupon trace confirms the impedance target, and a continuity check between plane layers at the connector shell confirms the stitching was actually connected as intended.

::: followups
### Why isn't ground on the bottom layer in a typical 4-layer board?
Because then the top signals would be 1.6 mm from their reference. Layer 2 is 0.2 mm below the top, giving narrow controlled-impedance traces and small loops. The bottom layer is served by L3, which in S/G/P/S is the power plane.

### How many ground planes in a 6-layer board and where?
Two, at L2 and L5, in S/G/S/P/G/S: each outer layer has a ground neighbour, and the inner signal layer L3 sits between L2 ground and L4 power. An alternative, S/G/S/S/G/S, also has ground at L2 and L5 but puts the two inner signal layers next to each other, so they must be routed orthogonally, and power moves to pours.

### Is it OK to put signals on the layer next to the ground plane on both sides?
Yes: that's a stripline when the signal layer has a ground on both sides, like L3 between grounds in an 8- or 10-layer board. It's the best-shielded arrangement, with the caveat that the two grounds must be stitched together.

### What do you do on a 2-layer board?
The bottom becomes a nearly solid ground plane, with signals on top. Use stitching vias to connect the top pours, ground vias at every ground pad, and keep fast signals over unbroken ground. The distance to the plane is 1.6 mm, so loops are bigger, so slower edges and careful routing help.

### Scenario: your four-layer board is S/P/G/S and the top-layer USB pair is wide and hard to keep 90 Ω. What's wrong?
The top signals reference the power plane at L2, not ground, and the ground is at L3, so the pair sees a noisy plane and, depending on the stack, the thick core instead of thin prepreg to the ground. Swap L2 and L3 (S/G/P/S), so the pair references ground through the thin prepreg. Then recompute the width for 90 Ω differential with the fab's thickness.
