::: basics
### In plain words
In a normal CMOS inverter the PMOS sits on top and the NMOS sits on the bottom. If you swap them, the circuit still conducts, but the output no longer reaches the rails. Each transistor shuts itself off before the output gets all the way to its target. The circuit also stops inverting.

### Picture it
Picture climbing a ladder whose rungs vanish as you climb. Each step up removes a bit of your footing. You get most of the way and then there is nothing left to push against. The swapped NMOS does this near the top. Its gate drive shrinks as its own source rises, so it stalls a threshold short of the supply.

### Words you need
- **Source follower**: a stage whose source copies the gate voltage minus a threshold.
- **Threshold voltage ($V_t$)**: the gate-to-source voltage needed to turn on. See [[q0099]].
- **Rail-to-rail**: an output that swings all the way from ground to supply.
- **Body effect**: the threshold rising when the source is not at body voltage.
- **Non-inverting**: the output follows the input instead of flipping it.

### The simplest example
Use a 5 V supply and give both transistors a 1 V threshold ($V_{tn}=|V_{tp}|=1$ V), with the NMOS now on top. Drive the input to 5 V. The output rises until it is about $5-1=4$ V, and then the NMOS has no gate drive left and stops. Drive the input to 0 V. The PMOS at the bottom pulls the output down only to about 1 V, one threshold above ground. So the output swings from about 1 V to 4 V, and follows the input instead of flipping it.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page explains the slow edges, the dead zone in the middle, and why CMOS is built the other way round. See also [[q0107]].
:::

::: testing
Whether you can **reason through a modified circuit instead of recalling one**. The interviewer wants you to:

1. Redraw the circuit with the NMOS on top (drain at $V_{DD}$) and the PMOS on the bottom (drain at ground) and notice that each device is now a **source follower**.
2. Predict the consequence from the turn-on condition: the NMOS can only pull up to $V_{DD}-V_{tn}$ and the PMOS can only pull down to $|V_{tp}|$, so **the output no longer reaches the rails**, and the circuit is **non-inverting**.
3. Add real-device effects: body effect makes the loss worse, drive weakens as the output approaches its limits, and there's a dead band where neither device conducts.
:::

::: why
It checks that you actually understand *why* CMOS puts PMOS on top and NMOS on the bottom, rather than just remembering the picture. The same insight explains why a high-side NFET needs a gate voltage above the supply (bootstrap), why source followers lose a threshold, why pass-transistor logic degrades levels, and why class-B follower output stages have crossover distortion. It is a good "derive it from the equations" question.
:::

::: concepts
- **Turn-on conditions**: NMOS needs $V_{GS}>V_{tn}$; PMOS needs $V_{SG}>|V_{tp}|$. See [[q0097]].
- **Source follower**: a MOSFET whose source follows the gate minus a threshold; it stops conducting when $V_{GS}$ falls to $V_t$.
- **Source = lower terminal for NMOS, higher for PMOS**: the source moves with the output when the devices are swapped.
- **Body effect**: raising source-to-body voltage raises $V_t$. See [[q0091]].
- **Why PMOS pulls up in CMOS**: its source is at $V_{DD}$, so $V_{SG}=V_{DD}-V_{in}$ is large when the input is low. See [[q0107]].
- **Dead band / crossover**: the interval where neither transistor conducts.
:::

::: answer
"If I swap them, the NMOS goes on top with its drain at $V_{DD}$ and the PMOS goes on the bottom with its drain at ground, gates tied together as before. The problem is that each device's source is now the output node. The NMOS turns on only if the gate is a threshold above its source, so when I drive the input high to $V_{DD}$ the output rises until it reaches $V_{DD}-V_{tn}$, and then the NMOS turns itself off. With the body effect that's worse, maybe $V_{DD}$ minus 1.1 V. Likewise, the PMOS at the bottom needs its gate a threshold below its source, so when the input goes low the output only gets pulled down to about $|V_{tp}|$ above ground.

So the output no longer swings rail to rail: it goes between roughly $|V_{tp}|$ and $V_{DD}-V_{tn}$. It's also non-inverting: input high gives output high. And because each device is acting as a source follower, the current drops as the output approaches its limit, so the edges get slower and slower near the end. There's also a dead zone for mid-range inputs where neither device conducts, so the output just floats.

That's exactly why CMOS is built the other way round: with the PMOS on top, its source is at $V_{DD}$ and the NMOS source is at ground, so each has the full gate drive and the output reaches the rails."
:::

::: technical
**Swapped circuit.** NMOS $M_N$: drain at $V_{DD}$, source = output. PMOS $M_P$: source = output, drain at ground. Gates tied to $V_{in}$. Body connections in a standard bulk process: NMOS body at ground, PMOS n-well at $V_{DD}$ (or each at its own source if isolated).

**Conduction conditions.**
- $M_N$ on if $V_{in}-V_{out}>V_{tn}$, i.e. $V_{out}<V_{in}-V_{tn}$. It *pulls the output up* toward $V_{in}-V_{tn}$.
- $M_P$ on if $V_{out}-V_{in}>|V_{tp}|$, i.e. $V_{out}>V_{in}+|V_{tp}|$. It *pulls the output down* toward $V_{in}+|V_{tp}|$.

**Result.** The output settles anywhere in the band $[\,V_{in}-V_{tn},\ V_{in}+|V_{tp}|\,]$ intersected with $[0,V_{DD}]$:
- $V_{in}=V_{DD}$: $V_{out}\to V_{DD}-V_{tn}$ (NMOS stops; PMOS is off since $V_{out}<V_{in}$).
- $V_{in}=0$: $V_{out}\to|V_{tp}|$ (PMOS stops; NMOS is off since $V_{out}>V_{in}-V_{tn}$).
- Mid-range: both off, output holds its previous value (dead band of width $V_{tn}+|V_{tp}|$).
The gain is non-inverting: $V_{out}$ follows $V_{in}$ within a threshold.

**With $V_{t0}=0.7$ V, $V_{DD}=3.3$ V (ignoring body effect):** $V_{OH}=2.6$ V, $V_{OL}=0.7$ V, swing 1.9 V (58% of $V_{DD}$).

**Body effect.** $V_t=V_{t0}+\gamma\left(\sqrt{2\phi_F+V_{SB}}-\sqrt{2\phi_F}\right)$. Take $\gamma=0.4$ V$^{1/2}$, $2\phi_F=0.7$ V. For the top NMOS, $V_{SB}=V_{out}$, so $V_{OH}=V_{DD}-V_t(V_{out})$, solved iteratively: $V_{OH}\approx2.25$ V (rather than 2.6 V). For the bottom PMOS with the n-well at $V_{DD}$, $V_{SB}=V_{DD}-V_{out}$, so $V_{OL}\approx1.05$ V (rather than 0.7 V). The swing shrinks to about 1.2 V, only 36% of $V_{DD}$.

**Drive vs output level.** As $V_{out}$ approaches its limit, $V_{GS}\to V_t$ and $I_D=\tfrac12k(V_{GS}-V_t)^2\to0$: the edge is a decaying exponential-like approach (and ultimately subthreshold leakage). Static current is essentially zero in the limits, but the logic levels are unusable as is.

**Side effects.** Next-stage inverters see mid-level inputs, so both of *their* transistors conduct and draw DC current. Noise margin is destroyed. It is, however, a legitimate **class-B follower** structure (the same idea as a complementary emitter-follower), with crossover distortion in the dead band and a non-inverting voltage gain just below 1.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The body-effect parameters ($\gamma$, $2\phi_F$) vary with process and doping. The numbers above use typical textbook values, so use them as illustrations, not as predictions for a specific process. In a process that allows the PMOS well to be tied to its source, the PMOS part of the body effect disappears.
:::

::: short
"Swapping them puts the NMOS on top and the PMOS on the bottom, so each source is the output node. The NMOS then can only pull the output up to $V_{DD}-V_{tn}$, since it turns off once its $V_{GS}$ falls to $V_t$, and the PMOS can only pull it down to $|V_{tp}|$. So the output no longer reaches the rails, the body effect makes it worse, the edges get slower near the limits, and the stage becomes non-inverting. That's why CMOS has the PMOS on top: its source sits at $V_{DD}$ so it gets full gate drive."
:::

::: long
"Let me draw it with the swap. NMOS on top: drain to $V_{DD}$, source to the output. PMOS on the bottom: source to the output, drain to ground. Gates still tied together at the input.

Now each transistor is a source follower, because its source is the output node. The NMOS conducts only while the gate is more than $V_{tn}$ above its source. When I take the input to $V_{DD}$, the output rises, but as soon as it reaches $V_{DD}$ minus $V_{tn}$ the gate-source voltage has dropped to the threshold and the NMOS shuts itself off. With a 0.7 V threshold on a 3.3 V supply that's 2.6 V, and with the body effect, since the NMOS source is above the substrate, the threshold rises to about 1.1 V so I'm at about 2.25 V.

The same thing happens at the bottom. With the input at ground, the PMOS pulls the output down until the output is only $|V_{tp}|$ above its gate, and then it turns off. So the low level is about 0.7 V, or about 1 V with the body effect.

So three consequences. The output doesn't reach the rails, so the output swing is only about a third to a half of the supply and the noise margins are gone, and the next gate sees a mid-level input and draws DC current. Second, it's non-inverting: high in, high out. Third, the drive current dies off as the output approaches the limit, and for mid-range inputs there's a dead band where neither device conducts and the output floats, which is crossover distortion.

The right way round, PMOS on top, works because its source is at $V_{DD}$, so with the input at ground it sees the full $V_{DD}$ of gate drive and pulls all the way to the rail, and the NMOS does the same with ground. A high-side NMOS needs a gate higher than $V_{DD}$, which is why we bootstrap it."
:::

::: terms
- **Source follower**: a stage whose output (the source) follows the gate voltage minus about $V_{GS}$.
- **Threshold drop**: the loss of $V_t$ when a MOSFET passes a signal in the "wrong" direction.
- **Body effect**: increase in $V_t$ when the source is above (NMOS) the body.
- **Rail-to-rail output**: output that swings essentially to both supplies.
- **Non-inverting**: output has the same polarity as the input.
- **Dead band**: input range over which neither transistor conducts.
- **Crossover distortion**: waveform distortion at the transition between the two devices' conduction.
- **Bootstrap**: boosting the gate voltage above the supply to turn on a high-side NMOS fully.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "the output won't reach the rails" and stops. An exceptional answer:

- **Derives** the limits from the on-conditions ($V_{out}<V_{in}-V_{tn}$ and $V_{out}>V_{in}+|V_{tp}|$) and gives the output band.
- Notes the stage is **non-inverting** and has a **dead band** of $V_{tn}+|V_{tp}|$, comparing it with a complementary follower/class-B output.
- Adds the **body effect** with numbers and notes the asymmetry (it hits both devices in a bulk process).
- Explains the **consequence for downstream logic**: mid-level inputs cause DC current in the next stage.
- Connects to practice: why CMOS has PMOS on top, why high-side NFETs need bootstrapping ([[q0107]]), pass-gate logic degradation, and where the swapped arrangement *is* used on purpose (source-follower buffers, NMOS-only output drivers with boosted gates).
- Mentions the body-diode angle for power devices ([[q0101]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Nothing happens / it's just inverted."** It is not just inverted: the levels are degraded and the polarity is *non-inverting*.
- **Forgetting the source moves.** The source of the top NMOS is the output, so $V_{GS}$ changes as the output moves; assuming $V_{GS}=V_{DD}$ is the classic error.
- **Ignoring the body effect**, which can double the threshold loss in a bulk process.
- **Claiming both devices conduct at the input extremes.** At $V_{in}=V_{DD}$ the PMOS is off ($V_{SG}<0$), and at $V_{in}=0$ the NMOS is off.
- **Missing the dead band.** For mid-range inputs neither device conducts and the output holds/floats.
- **Treating it as a power-electronics bridge.** In a half-bridge with NMOS on top and PMOS on the bottom the gate drive must differ from CMOS drive; see below.
:::

## Body effect and real levels

$$V_t=V_{t0}+\gamma\left(\sqrt{2\phi_F+V_{SB}}-\sqrt{2\phi_F}\right)$$

For the top NMOS, $V_{SB}=V_{out}$ increases as the output rises. Solve $V_{OH}=V_{DD}-V_t(V_{OH})$ with $\gamma=0.4$ V$^{1/2}$ and $2\phi_F=0.7$ V:

| Quantity | No body effect | With body effect |
|---|---|---|
| $V_{OH}$ (top NMOS) | 2.60 V | 2.25 V |
| $V_{OL}$ (bottom PMOS, n-well at $V_{DD}$) | 0.70 V | 1.05 V |
| Swing | 1.90 V | 1.20 V |

The two levels are now only 1.2 V apart, centred near $V_{DD}/2$, with a downstream inverter's threshold also near $V_{DD}/2$, so both of its transistors conduct to some degree: static current flows through the next stage and the noise margins shrink to a few hundred millivolts (worked out below).

## Where this arrangement is used on purpose

1. **Source-follower output buffers / class-B stages.** A complementary NMOS/PMOS follower (or NPN/PNP emitter follower) gives low output impedance with unity gain. Crossover distortion is removed with biasing (class AB).
2. **NMOS-only drivers** (e.g. the high-side switch of a half-bridge): the NMOS is on top, so its gate must exceed $V_{DD}+V_t$ to pass the full supply; hence the bootstrap or charge pump ([[q0107]]). The swapped CMOS stage is exactly what happens if you drive the high-side NMOS with an ordinary logic level.
3. **Pass-transistor logic.** An NMOS passes a strong 0 but a degraded 1 ($V_{DD}-V_t$); a PMOS passes a strong 1 but a degraded 0. A transmission gate uses both in parallel for full swing.
4. **Regulators with NMOS pass elements**: the pass device is a source follower, so its gate needs a supply above the output (a charge pump or auxiliary rail).

## Power-electronics version

In a half-bridge, high-side NMOS and low-side NMOS is the standard (efficient) arrangement. If someone builds a stage with an NMOS high-side and PMOS low-side driven from one tied gate, the same degradation occurs: the load sees a reduced swing and the devices run in linear mode with high dissipation. The correct complementary power stage is PMOS high-side/NMOS low-side (both with sources at their rails), or NMOS/NMOS with a floating high-side drive. Also note body diodes ([[q0101]]): the top NMOS's body diode (source to drain) conducts when the output exceeds $V_{DD}$, and the bottom PMOS's body diode (drain to source) clamps the output below ground, so overshoots are clamped by diodes.

## Real hardware

- A 74HC-style gate wired incorrectly like this would show about $V_{DD}/2$ levels, high supply current in the following stage, and slow, mushy edges.
- In a layout, the mistake happens by swapping the symbols in a schematic: the simulator shows rails reached only through leakage and long tails.
- Debug approach: measure $V_{out}$ at both extremes; if you see $V_{DD}-V_t$ and $|V_t|$, the polarities are swapped.

## Worked example: what the next stage actually sees

The earlier claim that the degraded levels leave "zero noise margin" is worth checking with numbers. Take a downstream ordinary inverter with $V_{tn}=|V_{tp}|=0.7$ V, equal $\beta=1$ mA/V² for both devices, $V_{DD}=3.3$ V, and a square-law model. Solving its transfer curve gives a switching threshold $V_M=1.65$ V, and the unity-gain points $V_{IL}\approx1.41$ V and $V_{IH}\approx1.89$ V. Now feed it the levels from the swapped stage ($V_{OH}=2.25$ V, $V_{OL}=1.05$ V):

| Input to next stage | Its output | Its supply current |
|---|---|---|
| 0 V (ideal low) | 3.30 V | 0 |
| 1.05 V (swapped-stage low) | 3.26 V | 0.061 mA |
| 1.65 V (midpoint) | about 1.65 V (indeterminate in the ideal square law, which has infinite gain at $V_M$) | 0.45 mA |
| 2.25 V (swapped-stage high) | 0.04 V | 0.061 mA |
| 3.30 V (ideal high) | 0 V | 0 |

Two conclusions. First, the next inverter still restores the signal to nearly full levels, so a single degraded stage does not by itself destroy the logic value. The cost is a static current of about 61 µA through the downstream stage in both logic states, compared with essentially zero for a proper CMOS gate: with a million such gates that is 61 A. Second, the noise margins fall from $V_{IH}$/$V_{IL}$ measured against the rails (1.41 V each way) to $2.25-1.89=0.36$ V and $1.41-1.05=0.36$ V. Margins of 0.36 V are not zero, but with threshold spread, supply noise and ground bounce they leave little room, and each additional swapped stage in series (or a smaller $V_{DD}$) would erase them. So "noise margin is nearly gone and static current flows" is the accurate statement.

## Worked example: the swapped stage as a follower: gain and output resistance

Treat the NMOS at the top as a source follower. With the body effect, its small-signal gain is $g_m/(g_m+g_{mb})=1/(1+\chi)$, where $\chi=\gamma/(2\sqrt{2\phi_F+V_{SB}})$. At $V_{SB}=V_{out}=2.25$ V, $\chi=0.4/(2\sqrt{2.95})=0.116$, so the gain is 0.90; at $V_{out}=1.65$ V it is 0.88. The output resistance at $V_{in}=3.3$ V and $V_{out}=1.65$ V is found from the threshold at that source-body voltage: $V_t=0.7+0.4(\sqrt{2.35}-\sqrt{0.7})=0.98$ V, so $V_{ov}=3.3-1.65-0.98=0.67$ V and $g_m=\beta V_{ov}=0.67$ mS. Then
$$R_{out}=\frac{1}{g_m(1+\chi)}=\frac{1}{0.67\text{ mS}\times1.13}\approx1.3\ \text{k}\Omega$$
A 1.3 kΩ source resistance is large compared with a CMOS inverter's few hundred ohms in the same technology, and it grows as the output approaches its final value because the overdrive shrinks.

## Worked example: why the last few hundred millivolts take so long

As $V_{out}$ approaches $V_{DD}-V_t$ the overdrive of the driving NMOS goes to zero, and with it the current. With the gate at $V_{DD}$, a load of $C=100$ fF and $\beta=1$ mA/V² (ignoring body effect for simplicity) the capacitor equation is
$$C\frac{dV_{out}}{dt}=\frac\beta2V_{ov}^2,\quad V_{ov}=V_{DD}-V_t-V_{out}\ \Rightarrow\ V_{ov}(t)=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{V_{ov0}}+\frac{\beta t}{2C}}$$
This is a $1/t$ decay, not an exponential. Starting from $V_{ov0}=2.6$ V, the time to get to a remaining overdrive of 0.5 V, 0.1 V, 0.05 V and 0.01 V is 0.32 ns, 1.9 ns, 3.9 ns and 20 ns. The output is "within 100 mV of its final value" about six times later than it is "within 500 mV," and it never quite arrives. A real circuit lets subthreshold leakage and other loads take over, but the slow tail explains the mushy edges: the output spends most of its time near the degraded level, and a chip using such a stage would need its clock period budgeted for the tail. Compare with a proper inverter edge, where the PMOS stays in triode at the end and the output approaches the rail exponentially.

## Why transmission gates and pass logic live with this problem on purpose

The same degraded-level mechanism appears whenever a single transistor type is used as a switch in a logic path. An NMOS passes a good 0 but a degraded 1; a PMOS passes a good 1 but a degraded 0. Pairing them in parallel with complementary gate drives gives a transmission gate that passes both, with an on-resistance that is roughly constant over the whole input range because the two devices take over from each other (the NMOS dominates below about $V_{DD}/2$ and the PMOS above). In pass-transistor logic families, a level restorer (a weak PMOS pull-up feedback from the output of the following inverter) pulls the degraded high up to $V_{DD}$ after the fact and eliminates the static current of the following inverter.

## Verifying the claim on the bench

- **Build the swapped stage from discrete parts.** Use a small-signal NMOS and PMOS (for example, a complementary pair in one package) with the NMOS drain on the supply and the PMOS drain to ground, both gates tied to a function generator output through a series resistor, and both sources joined as the output. With $V_{DD}=5$ V and a 0 to 5 V square wave, put a scope on the output. You should see the output reach a level a threshold below the high input and a threshold above ground, with slow tails near the ends. Confirm the levels are $V_{DD}-V_{tn}$ (with body effect) and $|V_{tp}|$.
- **Check the follower gain.** Replace the square wave with a slow triangle and plot output against input: inside the active range the slope should be about 0.9, with a flat dead band of width $V_{tn}+|V_{tp}|$ around the crossover, where neither device conducts.
- **Measure the downstream current.** Feed the degraded levels into a 74HC-family inverter, measure the supply current with a series resistor and compare it with the same inverter driven by rail-to-rail levels. The degraded input should raise it by orders of magnitude, matching the static-current argument above.
- **Debug rule.** In a real circuit, if a node reads $V_{DD}$ minus a threshold or a threshold above ground, suspect a swapped or missing complementary device, or an NMOS-only or PMOS-only pass element.

::: applications
- **Why CMOS gates are PMOS-on-top**: the same reasoning in every logic cell.
- **Bootstrapped high-side NMOS drivers** ([[q0107]]): exploiting an NMOS at the top by raising its gate.
- **Source-follower / push-pull output buffers**: complementary followers for low output impedance.
- **Pass-transistor and transmission-gate design**: strong vs weak levels.
- **Debugging**: recognising degraded levels at about $V_{DD}-V_t$ as a polarity or drive error.
- **LDO and linear regulators with NMOS pass devices**: gate must be above the output by $V_{GS}$.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Bulk CMOS** with enhancement devices, gates tied together, drive from a full-swing input.
- **Body connections**: NMOS substrate at ground, PMOS n-well at $V_{DD}$. If each device's body is tied to its own source (possible in isolated-well or SOI processes, or for discrete devices), the body effect disappears but the threshold drop remains.
- **Square-law and constant thresholds** for the first-order answer; subthreshold conduction lets the output creep towards the limit slowly.
- **Static (DC) analysis**: dynamically, capacitive loads and finite input slew give additional behaviour.
- If the swap is made in a **discrete half-bridge with separate gate drives**, the answer is about gate-drive requirements rather than a tied-gate follower.
:::

::: followups
### What output levels do you get for $V_{DD}=5$ V and $V_{tn}=|V_{tp}|=1$ V with no body effect?
High: $5-1=4$ V; low: 1 V. Swing 3 V (60%). A downstream 5 V CMOS input sees 4 V as high (needs above about 3.5 V for $V_{IH}=0.7V_{DD}$, so just passes) and 1 V as low ($V_{IL}=0.3V_{DD}=1.5$ V passes), but with almost no margin. With body effect it would fail.

### Is the swapped circuit inverting or non-inverting? How do you know?
Non-inverting. At $V_{in}=V_{DD}$ the NMOS pulls the output up (and the PMOS is off), at $V_{in}=0$ the PMOS pulls it down (NMOS off). The output follows the input within a threshold.

### Why does the correct arrangement not suffer from this?
In the correct arrangement the PMOS source is at $V_{DD}$ (fixed) so $V_{SG}=V_{DD}-V_{in}=V_{DD}$ at $V_{in}=0$: the device stays fully on until the output reaches $V_{DD}$ where $V_{SD}=0$ and no current flows, with no loss. The sources are tied to the rails, not to the moving output.

### What gate voltage would the top NMOS need to pull the output to $V_{DD}$?
At least $V_{DD}+V_{tn}$ (and in practice more for low $R_{on}$): $V_{DD}+V_t=4.0$ V for 3.3 V and 0.7 V, ignoring body effect, and about $V_{DD}+1.1$ V with it. Bootstrap or charge-pump supplies provide it.

### Harder: the swapped stage with $V_{in}=1.65$ V drives a 1 kΩ load to ground. What is $V_{out}$ (take $k_n=2$ mA/V², $V_{tn}=0.7$ V, no body effect)?
The NMOS must supply $V_{out}/R$, so it needs overdrive: $V_{out}=V_{in}-V_{tn}-V_{ov}$ with $I=\tfrac12k_nV_{ov}^2=V_{out}/1\text{ k}$. In mA and V: $V_{ov}^2=V_{out}$, so with $s=\sqrt{V_{out}}$: $s^2+s-0.95=0$, $s=0.595$, $V_{out}=0.35$ V. Without a load the output would sit at 0.95 V; the load costs another 0.6 V. A follower's output depends on the load current, unlike an ideal rail-to-rail inverter.

### Scenario: a colleague's schematic shows a "CMOS buffer" feeding a logic input, but the scope shows 0.9 V to 2.3 V levels on a 3.3 V supply. What went wrong?
The high and low levels are $V_{DD}-V_{tn}$ and $|V_{tp}|$ with body effect: the transistors were swapped (NMOS on top, PMOS on bottom) or a pass gate drives the input with only one polarity of device. Check the schematic symbols and device types against the netlist.
:::

::: interpretations
"Swap the high-side PFET and the low-side NFET" assumes the standard CMOS pair with a PFET to $V_{DD}$ and an NFET to ground. The answer assumes the gates remain tied together and driven from one input. If the gates are driven separately (as in a power half-bridge), the same physical limitation (a high-side NFET needs more than $V_{DD}$ at its gate; a low-side PFET needs a gate below ground) applies, just with different drive requirements. Both readings are covered.
:::
