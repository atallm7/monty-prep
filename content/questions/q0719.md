::: testing
Whether you know the **design knobs** of $Z_0$ and which direction each one moves it, not just that "$Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$". The interviewer is looking for:

1. The physical logic: $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$, so anything that raises capacitance lowers $Z_0$, and anything that raises loop inductance raises it.
2. The concrete levers on a PCB: trace width, dielectric height, $\varepsilon_r$, copper thickness, reference plane, and (for coax) the $D/d$ ratio.
3. The practical workflow: pick a stackup, use a field solver or calculator, ask the fab for controlled impedance, and know tolerance (about ±10%).
:::

::: why
Almost every high-speed design step starts with "make the trace 50 Ω (or 90/100 Ω differential)". If you can't predict which way $Z_0$ moves when the stackup changes, you can't fix a board that came back wrong ([[q0528]]) or choose between a 4-layer and a 6-layer stackup with confidence. This is one of the most direct questions linking circuit theory to a layout decision.
:::

::: concepts
- **$Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$** for a low-loss line. See [[q0710]] and [[q0718]].
- **Capacitance per length** of a trace over a plane rises with trace width and $\varepsilon_r$ and falls with dielectric height.
- **Loop inductance per length** falls when the return plane is close, because the signal and return currents are close and their fields cancel.
- **Effective permittivity** $\varepsilon_{eff}$ for microstrip sits between air (1) and the substrate $\varepsilon_r$, because the field is partly in air.
- **Microstrip vs stripline**: outer-layer trace with one plane vs inner-layer trace between two planes. See [[q0715]].
- **Differential impedance** depends on coupling between the pair as well as single-ended $Z_0$.
:::

::: answer
"$Z_0$ is $\sqrt{L'/C'}$, so I control it by changing the capacitance or inductance per length, and on a PCB that comes down to geometry and material.

The big knob is the **trace width relative to the dielectric height**. A wider trace, or a thinner dielectric to the reference plane, increases the capacitance to the plane and also reduces the loop inductance, so $Z_0$ goes down. A narrower trace or thicker dielectric goes the other way. For FR-4 with a 0.2 mm dielectric, about 0.39 mm wide gives roughly 50 Ω on microstrip, and doubling the dielectric height to 0.4 mm would push the same trace up to about 73 Ω.

The second knob is **dielectric constant**: higher $\varepsilon_r$ means more capacitance and lower $Z_0$, and it scales as roughly $1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$. Third, **copper thickness** matters a bit, thicker copper acts like a wider trace. And stripline gives different numbers than microstrip because the field is all in the dielectric.

In practice I'd pick the stackup, calculate it in a field solver or the fab's tool, and specify controlled impedance with a ±10% tolerance. For coax it's the ratio of outer to inner diameter and the dielectric: $Z_0=\frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln(D/d)$."
:::

::: technical
**Principle.** For a low-loss line, $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and the delay per length is $\sqrt{L'C'}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/c$. So $L'=Z_0\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/c$ and $C'=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/(cZ_0)$. Everything that changes $Z_0$ changes the capacitance or the loop inductance.

**Microstrip** (width $w$, dielectric height $h$, $u=w/h$). Hammerstad-type approximation:
$$\varepsilon_{eff}=\frac{\varepsilon_r+1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon_r-1}{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+12/u}}$$
$$Z_0\approx\begin{cases}\dfrac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}}\ln\!\left(\dfrac8u+\dfrac u4\right)& u\le1\\[2ex]\dfrac{120\pi}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}\,[\,u+1.393+0.667\ln(u+1.444)\,]}& u\ge1\end{cases}$$
Copper thickness $t$ acts like extra width: $w_{eff}\approx w+\Delta w$, with $\Delta w$ growing with $t$.

**Stripline** (trace between two planes spaced $b$ apart), IPC-2141 style approximation: $Z_0\approx\frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\frac{4b}{0.67\pi(0.8w+t)}$, valid for narrow traces. All field is in the dielectric, so $\varepsilon_{eff}=\varepsilon_r$.

**Coax:** $Z_0=\frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\frac Dd$. With PE ($\varepsilon_r=2.25$) 50 Ω needs $D/d\approx3.49$ and 75 Ω needs $D/d\approx6.52$.

**Differential pairs** (edge coupled microstrip), approximately $Z_{diff}\approx2Z_0\,[1-0.48e^{-0.96\,s/h}]$ where $s$ is the gap between traces. Closer spacing means more coupling, which lowers $Z_{diff}$ below $2Z_0$.

**Sensitivities (FR-4, $\varepsilon_r=4.3$, $h=0.2$ mm, $w=0.39$ mm, Hammerstad: 50.2 Ω):**

| Change | New $Z_0$ |
|---|---|
| width +10% | 47.3 Ω |
| width −10% | 53.4 Ω |
| height +10% | 53.1 Ω |
| height ×2 (0.4 mm) | 72.7 Ω |
| $\varepsilon_r$ 4.8 (instead of 4.3) | 47.7 Ω |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Closed-form formulas disagree with each other by several percent: for this geometry the Hammerstad form (no thickness) gives 50 Ω while the IPC-2141 microstrip form with $t=35$ µm gives about 45 Ω. Treat them as first-order guides and have the fab confirm with their own field solver and measured $\varepsilon_r$.
:::

::: long
"The starting point is that for a low-loss line, $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$. So controlling $Z_0$ means controlling the inductance and capacitance per unit length, and both are set by cross-section geometry and the dielectric.

On a PCB the geometry is trace width $w$, the dielectric height $h$ between the trace and its reference plane, copper thickness $t$, and the dielectric constant $\varepsilon_r$. The trace and plane form a capacitor, so a wider trace or thinner dielectric raises $C'$. The loop inductance falls as the return current sits closer under the trace. Both move the same direction, so $Z_0$ falls. The reverse for a narrower trace or taller dielectric. As a worked example with FR-4 and 0.2 mm dielectric, 0.39 mm is about 50 Ω; if the fab built it with 0.4 mm dielectric, the same trace would be about 73 Ω.

Dielectric constant is the other lever. Higher $\varepsilon_r$ multiplies the capacitance and cuts $Z_0$ by roughly $1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$. Microstrip has some field in air, so its $\varepsilon_{eff}$ is lower than $\varepsilon_r$ and it's faster than stripline, which is fully inside the dielectric.

For differential pairs there's also the spacing. Pulling the two traces closer increases coupling and reduces differential impedance below twice the single-ended value.

In coax it's the diameter ratio and dielectric: $60/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}\cdot\ln(D/d)$.

The practical flow is: choose stackup with the fab, calculate widths in a solver, specify controlled impedance on the fab notes, and check with a TDR coupon. If it's off, the easiest change is the width, but the stackup thickness is what really sets the baseline."
:::

::: terms
- **Controlled impedance**: a fab service that tunes trace width and dielectric to hit a target $Z_0$ within a tolerance, usually ±10%.
- **Reference plane**: the adjacent copper plane that carries the return current.
- **Dielectric height $h$**: the distance from the trace to the reference plane.
- **Effective dielectric constant** $\varepsilon_{eff}$: the blended permittivity seen by the quasi-TEM wave in microstrip.
- **Microstrip / stripline**: outer-layer / inner-layer trace geometries.
- **Field solver**: software that solves for $L'$ and $C'$ numerically from a cross-section.
- **TDR coupon**: a test trace on the panel measured with a time-domain reflectometer to verify impedance.
- **Coupling spacing** $s$: gap between the traces of a differential pair.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "change the trace width".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Ties it to $\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and says *why* width and height move $Z_0$ the way they do.
- Gives all the levers: $w$, $h$, $\varepsilon_r$, $t$, microstrip vs stripline, differential spacing, and $D/d$ for coax.
- Gives a real example with numbers (about 0.39 mm over 0.2 mm FR-4 for 50 Ω) and a sensitivity: ±10% on width is about ∓3 Ω.
- Mentions that $h$ (stackup) is the dominant lever and width is the fine adjustment, which is also what the fab can tune.
- Knows that closed-form formulas are approximate and that the answer comes from a field solver plus TDR verification.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "add a series resistor to set $Z_0$".** A resistor changes the termination, not the line's $Z_0$. It matches the line, it doesn't set it.
- **Believing a wider trace raises $Z_0$.** It lowers it: more capacitance, less inductance.
- **Ignoring the stackup.** The same trace width gives 50 Ω or 73 Ω depending on dielectric height. Always ask "over which plane, at what height?"
- **Forgetting the reference plane.** If the return plane has a gap or split under the trace, the local $Z_0$ jumps. $Z_0$ is a property of trace plus plane.
- **Using microstrip formulas for stripline** (or vice versa). $\varepsilon_{eff}$ is different.
- **Trusting the formula to 1%.** Real $\varepsilon_r$ varies by lot and frequency, etch leaves a trapezoidal trace, and the solder mask adds capacitance. Plan for ±10% and verify.
:::

::: deep
## The physical picture

Picture a signal edge travelling down a trace over a plane. As the wavefront advances, it must charge the capacitance of the next bit of trace, and the current it carries creates a magnetic field in the loop of trace and plane. The ratio of the voltage to current that the source "sees" is the ratio of how hard the line resists building those fields, and it is $\sqrt{L'/C'}$.

Now ask what each geometry knob does to $C'$ and $L'$:

- **Wider trace**: more plate area, so higher $C'$. The current also spreads out, which reduces loop inductance slightly. $Z_0$ falls.
- **Taller dielectric (larger $h$)**: plates farther apart, so lower $C'$, and the signal/return loop is bigger, so higher $L'$. $Z_0$ rises, strongly.
- **Higher $\varepsilon_r$**: $C'$ scales with $\varepsilon$, $L'$ doesn't change, so $Z_0$ falls like $1/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$.
- **Thicker copper**: the side walls add fringing capacitance, like a wider trace. A small effect: 1 oz to 2 oz copper typically shifts $Z_0$ by a few ohms.

## Worked example: hitting 50 Ω and 100 Ω differential

Design a 4-layer board: signal on top, ground plane on layer 2, 0.2 mm prepreg, FR-4 $\varepsilon_r\approx4.3$.

1. Single-ended 50 Ω: solve $Z_0(w)=50$ → $w\approx0.39$ mm (Hammerstad). Real fabs would typically come back with a slightly narrower number once copper thickness and solder-mask are included, so I'd treat the calc as a first guess.
2. For 100 Ω differential at $s/h=1$ ($s=0.2$ mm): $Z_{diff}\approx2Z_0[1-0.48e^{-0.96}]=2Z_0\times0.816$. Need $Z_0\approx61$ Ω, which means a narrower trace than 50 Ω: about 0.28 mm by the same formula. Check: $Z_0(0.28)\approx 60.5$ Ω.
3. If you widen the gap to $s/h=2$, coupling falls and you need $Z_0\approx54$ Ω, so the traces get wider (about 0.35 mm) but take more board space.

The key trade-off is routing density vs coupling: tight spacing uses less area and rejects common-mode noise better, but needs narrower traces and has tighter etch tolerance.

## Reading the impedance from a measurement

You can check a built board without a field solver. A TDR shows impedance versus time along the trace: step edge in, reflected voltage out, converted to ohms with $Z=Z_{ref}(1+\rho)/(1-\rho)$. A trace that should be 50 Ω but reads 56 Ω has $\rho=(56-50)/(56+50)=0.057$. From the sensitivities above, 56 Ω on this stackup means either the dielectric is about 10% thicker than assumed, the trace is about 10 to 12% narrower than drawn, or $\varepsilon_r$ is lower than the 4.3 used. Etching typically produces a trace narrower at the top than the bottom, so measured widths on a cross-section are quoted at mid-height.

Because several variables move $Z_0$ together, the fab works backward from its own measured stackup. That is why a good controlled-impedance order includes the stackup drawing, the requested impedance with tolerance, which layers are references, and the coupon location. When a board comes back out of tolerance, the report shows which of $h$, $w$, $t$ or $\varepsilon_r$ moved, and the fix is usually adjusting the width on the next panel rather than redesigning.

## What the fab actually does

You specify the target impedance, tolerance (commonly ±10%, ±5% costs more), the reference layers, and the stackup. The fab picks dielectric thicknesses from stock materials, computes widths with their own solver, and adds **test coupons** on the panel edge. They TDR the coupons to confirm. If your design has varying dielectric thickness between prepreg lots or different resin content around copper pours, there's real spread. So you ask for the stackup *and* the impedance report, and you leave margin (e.g. don't squeeze the PHY's allowed budget).

For a quick sanity check without a solver, remember: 50 Ω microstrip on FR-4 is $w/h\approx2$ (slightly under), and stripline 50 Ω is $w\approx b/4$ to $b/3$ for typical stackups. These are rules of thumb, not design values.
:::

::: applications
- **USB 2.0, Ethernet and HDMI** layouts specify 90 Ω or 100 Ω differential pairs, set by trace width and spacing in the stackup.
- **DDR memory routing**: 40–50 Ω single-ended traces with tight length control, set through controlled-impedance stackups.
- **RF boards (antenna feeds, 2.4 GHz radios)**: 50 Ω microstrip or coplanar waveguide chosen by $w/h$ and the gap to the ground pour.
- **Coax cable design**: 50 Ω for RF equipment, 75 Ω for video, different $D/d$ with the same polyethylene dielectric.
- **HDI and flex boards**: thin dielectrics make 50 Ω traces narrow, which is a manufacturing constraint as much as an electrical one.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Reference plane is continuous** under the trace. If not, local $Z_0$ changes.
- **Quasi-static approximations** are used for the formulas, so dispersion at very high frequency is ignored. $\varepsilon_{eff}$ rises with frequency slightly.
- **Solder mask**, if present over the trace, adds a little capacitance and lowers $Z_0$ by about 1–3 Ω, depending on thickness. Exact numbers vary by fab.
- **Rectangular trace cross-section.** Etch produces a trapezoid, narrower at the top, which shifts $Z_0$ up a bit.
- **The answer changes** for stripline (all dielectric, no air), for differential pairs (coupling), and for coplanar waveguide (side ground pours add capacitance).
:::

::: followups
### If I need 50 Ω but my dielectric is 0.1 mm instead of 0.2 mm, what width?
$Z_0$ depends on $w/h$, so keep the same ratio: $w\approx0.196$ mm. That's a fine line, but within normal fab capability (about 0.1 mm min). See the plot: curves for $h=0.1$ mm cross 50 Ω at 0.2 mm.

### Why does a thicker dielectric raise the impedance?
Capacitance to the plane drops as the plates move apart, and the signal/return loop gets bigger so inductance rises. Both push $\sqrt{L'/C'}$ up. For 0.39 mm wide on 0.4 mm dielectric the Hammerstad formula gives about 73 Ω rather than 50 Ω at 0.2 mm.

### How does stripline impedance compare to microstrip for the same geometry?
Stripline's field is entirely in the dielectric, so it sees the full $\varepsilon_r$ rather than $\varepsilon_{eff}$, and it has a plane on both sides. For the same trace-to-plane distance that means more capacitance and a lower $Z_0$, and it's slower ($v=c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$). Also, with two planes, it's better shielded against radiation and crosstalk.

### How tight can I get $Z_0$ on a manufactured board?
The usual quote is ±10% on standard processes, ±5% with tighter process control and extra cost. Sources of variation are dielectric thickness, $\varepsilon_r$, etch width and copper thickness. For a 50 Ω target, ±10% is 45–55 Ω, which gives reflection coefficients up to about 5%.

### Scenario: Your TDR shows 62 Ω on a trace designed for 50 Ω. What would you check?
First the stackup: is the dielectric thicker than assumed (about 0.3 mm instead of 0.2 mm would explain 62 Ω)? Second the etched width: is it narrower than drawn? Third the reference: is there a plane cut or a void under the trace? Fourth $\varepsilon_r$ lower than assumed (cheaper lot). The fix is a respin with revised width or corrected stackup, or a fab re-quote with the actual build.

### Harder: could you tune $Z_0$ without changing width or stackup?
Yes, locally. Add a ground pour beside the trace (coplanar waveguide) to raise capacitance, add a plane cutout under a pad to lower its capacitance and raise local $Z_0$ (used to compensate connector pads), or change the solder-mask. These are standard tricks for BGA and connector breakouts where the pad capacitance would otherwise cause a dip.
