::: basics
### In plain words
At high frequency, a line's characteristic impedance is set by two numbers per meter: its inductance and its capacitance. More inductance raises the impedance, and more capacitance lowers it. Resistance and leakage (current seeping through the insulation) mostly add loss and matter at low frequency. Conductor shape and insulation set both. Coax and circuit-board traces differ in how they do that.

### Picture it
Think of a hose carrying a pulse of water. The hose's capacitance is how stretchy it is. A stretchier hose soaks up more flow for each push, so impedance is lower. The inductance is how heavy and slow to start the moving water is. Heavier water gives less flow for the same push, so impedance is higher. Widen a trace over its ground plane and it gets "stretchier". Raise it higher above the plane and it gets "heavier".

### Words you need
- **Inductance and capacitance per meter ($L'$, $C'$)**: the amounts of each for one meter of line. At high frequency, $Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}$.
- **Dielectric**: the insulating material between the conductors.
- **Coax**: a wire inside a round shield with insulation between them. Its field is entirely in the insulation.
- **Microstrip**: a trace on the surface of a board above a ground plane. Its field is partly in the board and partly in air.
- **Loss**: signal weakening from the metal's resistance and from the insulation.

### The simplest example
A line has 250 nH of inductance and 100 pF of capacitance per meter. Its impedance is $\sqrt{250\text{ nH} / 100\text{ pF}} = \sqrt{2500} = 50\ \Omega$.

Raise the capacitance by 21% to 121 pF and the impedance falls to about 45.5 Ω. Instead raise the inductance by 44% to 360 nH and it rises to $\sqrt{3600} = 60\ \Omega$.

That is the whole idea: more L pushes impedance up, more C pushes it down, and geometry sets both. The rest of this page covers the low-frequency case, the coax formula, and the microstrip formula, which is only approximate.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can use $Z_0=\sqrt{(R+j\omega L)/(G+j\omega C)}$ *qualitatively*: more $L$ raises $Z_0$, more $C$ lowers it, $R$ and $G$ matter only at low frequency or as loss, and then connect that to geometry. For the second half, the interviewer wants to hear that coax has all its field in a uniform dielectric and a closed, shielded geometry (so $Z_0=\frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\frac Dd$ exactly and no dispersion except loss), while microstrip is open with field partly in air, so it has an effective permittivity, only approximate formulas, mild dispersion, radiation and crosstalk.
:::

::: why
Every layout decision about impedance is really a decision about $L'$ and $C'$: widening a trace, moving it closer to the plane, changing the laminate, or choosing a cable. If you know which way each knob moves $Z_0$, you can predict the effect of a stack-up change or a manufacturing variation without opening a solver, and you know how a coax cable's behaviour differs from that of a PCB trace when you connect them (SMA launches, probe cables, backplanes). The comparison also explains why coax is used for instruments and microstrip for boards, a favourite topic for hardware interviews.
:::

::: concepts
- **Telegrapher's parameters** $R',L',G',C'$ per unit length, and $Z_0=\sqrt{(R'+j\omega L')/(G'+j\omega C')}$ ([[q0710]]).
- **Inductance** depends on loop area; **capacitance** on conductor area and dielectric ([[q0039]], [[q0022]]).
- **Skin effect**: $R'$ rises as $\sqrt f$ ([[q0056]]).
- **Dielectric loss**: $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$ ([[q0058]]).
- **Effective permittivity** $\varepsilon_{eff}$: the permittivity seen by a wave when its field is split between dielectric and air.
- **TEM vs quasi-TEM**: coax is truly TEM; microstrip is quasi-TEM ([[q0709]]).
:::

::: answer
"Start from $Z_0=\sqrt{(R+j\omega L)/(G+j\omega C)}$ with everything per unit length. At high frequency, where $\omega L\gg R$ and $\omega C\gg G$, it collapses to $\sqrt{L/C}$. So more inductance raises the impedance, more capacitance lowers it, and I can think of every layout change as changing one of those. A wider trace, or a trace closer to its plane, adds capacitance and removes inductance, so $Z_0$ drops. A thicker dielectric, or a narrower trace, does the opposite. A higher dielectric constant raises $C$ so $Z_0$ falls by one over root epsilon.

R and G are really about loss. At high frequency they add a small imaginary part and attenuate the signal: series resistance, with skin effect, and dielectric loss. At low frequency, below roughly where $R$ equals $\omega L$, the resistance dominates and $Z_0$ gets bigger and complex. On a fine PCB trace that corner is a megahertz or two; on a coax it's tens of kilohertz.

Microstrip versus coax: coax has the field entirely inside a uniform dielectric between the centre conductor and the shield, so $Z_0=(60/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r})\ln(D/d)$ and it is shielded and non-dispersive. Microstrip has its field partly in the board and partly in the air above, so I use an effective dielectric constant, around 3.2 on FR-4, only approximate formulas, and it's open, so it radiates, couples to neighbours and is slightly dispersive."
:::

::: technical
**Lossy line.** $Z_0=\sqrt{\dfrac{R'+j\omega L'}{G'+j\omega C'}}$.
- High frequency ($R'\ll\omega L'$, $G'\ll\omega C'$): $Z_0\approx\sqrt{\dfrac{L'}{C'}}\left[1-j\left(\dfrac{R'}{2\omega L'}-\dfrac{G'}{2\omega C'}\right)\right]$, essentially real; attenuation $\alpha\approx\dfrac{R'}{2Z_0}+\dfrac{G'Z_0}{2}$ Np/m.
- Low frequency ($R'\gg\omega L'$, $G'\approx0$): $Z_0\approx\sqrt{R'/(j\omega C')}$, magnitude $\sqrt{R'/\omega C'}$ at $-45^\circ$ (diffusive RC line).
- Distortionless condition (Heaviside): $R'/L'=G'/C'$; real lines have $R'/L'\gg G'/C'$ at low frequency and the opposite at multi-GHz.

**Lossless sensitivities:** $\dfrac{\Delta Z_0}{Z_0}=\tfrac12\left(\dfrac{\Delta L'}{L'}-\dfrac{\Delta C'}{C'}\right)$. E.g. $C'$ up 21% gives $Z_0$ down to 45.5 Ω from 50 Ω; $L'$ up 44% gives 60 Ω.

**Coax** (inner diameter $d$, outer $D$, dielectric $\varepsilon_r$): $L'=\dfrac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln\dfrac Dd$, $C'=\dfrac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}{\ln(D/d)}$, so $Z_0=\dfrac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\dfrac Dd$ and $v=c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$. Depends only on the ratio $D/d$ and $\varepsilon_r$, not on the size.

**Microstrip** (width $w$, height $h$, thickness $t$): quasi-TEM, $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx\dfrac{\varepsilon_r+1}{2}+\dfrac{\varepsilon_r-1}{2}\left(1+\dfrac{12h}{w}\right)^{-1/2}$ (Hammerstad, $w/h\ge1$) and $Z_0\approx\dfrac{87}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r+1.41}}\ln\dfrac{5.98h}{0.8w+t}$ (IPC-2141, roughly $0.1<w/h<2$). $\varepsilon_{eff}$ rises slowly with frequency (dispersion).

| | Coax | Microstrip |
|---|---|---|
| Field | all in dielectric | split dielectric/air |
| Mode | pure TEM | quasi-TEM |
| $Z_0$ formula | exact | empirical |
| Shielded | yes | no (radiates, crosstalk) |
| $v$ | $c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$ | $c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$ |
| Dispersion | only from loss | $\varepsilon_{eff}(f)$ plus loss |
| Corner $R=\omega L$ | tens of kHz | about 1 to 2 MHz (fine trace) |
:::

::: long
"Everything here is per unit length. The general expression is $Z_0=\sqrt{(R+j\omega L)/(G+j\omega C)}$. At high frequency, where the inductive reactance beats the resistance and the capacitive susceptance beats the conductance, it collapses to $\sqrt{L/C}$. So the rule is: more inductance raises $Z_0$, more capacitance lowers it, and R and G only matter for the impedance at low frequencies.

At low frequency the resistance dominates: $Z_0$ becomes $\sqrt{R/(j\omega C)}$, large, complex, with a phase of minus 45 degrees. The crossover is at $\omega=R/L$. For a fine PCB trace with a few ohms per metre it's about 1 to 2 MHz, so above that the flat 50 ohm number is valid. For a good coax with tens of milliohms per metre it's tens of kilohertz. At high frequency R is the skin-effect resistance and G the dielectric loss, which don't change the magnitude of $Z_0$ much but make it slightly complex and set the attenuation.

Geometry maps onto L and C. A wider trace or a thinner dielectric increases the capacitance to the plane and reduces the loop inductance, so $Z_0$ drops; a narrower trace or taller dielectric raises it. A higher permittivity raises $C$ and lowers $Z_0$ and speed by the square root.

Microstrip versus coax: in coax the field is confined between the centre conductor and the shield, in a uniform dielectric, so the impedance is exactly $(60/\sqrt{\varepsilon_r})\ln(D/d)$, depends only on the ratio, and nothing radiates. Microstrip has some of its field in the board and some in air, so I use an effective permittivity, around 3.2 for FR-4 with a 0.3 mm trace over 0.2 mm, the formulae are empirical, and the effective permittivity changes slowly with frequency, so it has some dispersion. It's open, so it radiates, picks up and couples to neighbours, whereas coax is shielded."
:::

::: terms
- **Per-unit-length parameters**: $R'$ (Ω/m), $L'$ (H/m), $G'$ (S/m), $C'$ (F/m).
- **Heaviside (distortionless) condition**: $R'/L'=G'/C'$, where all frequencies travel with the same attenuation and velocity.
- **Effective dielectric constant ($\varepsilon_{eff}$)**: the equivalent homogeneous permittivity for a mixed air/dielectric structure.
- **Quasi-TEM**: approximately transverse-electromagnetic propagation, true of microstrip at low enough frequency.
- **Dispersion**: frequency dependence of velocity (and so of $\varepsilon_{eff}$ or delay).
- **Skin depth ($\delta$)**: depth at which current density drops to $1/e$; $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu)}$.
- **Loss tangent ($\tan\delta$)**: ratio of the dielectric's loss to its energy storage, $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$.
- **Aspect ratio $w/h$** (microstrip) and **$D/d$** (coax): the dimensionless geometry controlling $Z_0$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "more L raises Z0, more C lowers it" and "coax is shielded, microstrip isn't".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts from the full expression, shows the high-frequency limit, and gives the **low-frequency corner** $R'=\omega L'$ with numbers for a PCB trace (about 1.8 MHz) versus a coax (tens of kHz).
- Treats R and G as *loss* and phase effects on $Z_0$ and gives the attenuation $\alpha=R'/2Z_0+G'Z_0/2$.
- Maps geometry to $L'$ and $C'$ separately (width up: $C$ up, $L$ down), not just "wider means lower".
- Explains the coax result is size-independent (ratio $D/d$) and microstrip's depends on $w/h$ and $\varepsilon_{eff}$.
- Explains why microstrip needs $\varepsilon_{eff}$, is dispersive, radiates and couples, and where coax stops being TEM (higher-order modes).
- Mentions the sensitivities: $\Delta Z/Z=\frac12(\Delta L/L-\Delta C/C)$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"R sets $Z_0$."** At the frequencies of interest the resistive terms are tiny compared with $\omega L$; $R'$ sets the loss, not the impedance.
- **Mixing up the direction.** Widening a trace *lowers* $Z_0$ (more C); thickening the dielectric *raises* it.
- **Using the substrate $\varepsilon_r$ in microstrip formulas for speed.** The wave sees $\varepsilon_{eff}<\varepsilon_r$ (about 3.2 for $\varepsilon_r=4.3$), so delay is about 5.9 ps/mm, not 6.9.
- **Claiming coax impedance depends on its absolute diameter.** It depends on the ratio $D/d$ and $\varepsilon_r$; size sets power handling, loss and cutoff.
- **Treating microstrip formulas as exact.** IPC-2141 and Hammerstad are approximations valid over limited $w/h$ ranges, and fabrication tolerance adds ±10%.
- **Assuming coax has no loss or dispersion.** It has skin-effect and dielectric loss, both frequency dependent; it is non-dispersive only in the lossless limit.
:::

::: deep
## 1. The general expression and what each term does

Per unit length, a line has series impedance $Z'=R'+j\omega L'$ and shunt admittance $Y'=G'+j\omega C'$. The wave equation gives a propagation constant $\gamma=\sqrt{Z'Y'}=\alpha+j\beta$ and a characteristic impedance

$$Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{Z'}{Y'}}=\sqrt{\frac{R'+j\omega L'}{G'+j\omega C'}}$$

Factor out the lossless part:
$$Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}\,\sqrt{\frac{1+R'/(j\omega L')}{1+G'/(j\omega C')}}$$

The second factor is $1$ when $R'\ll\omega L'$ and $G'\ll\omega C'$, which is true over most of the useful band, and it explains each role:

- **L:** increases the magnitude of $Z_0$ as $\sqrt{L'}$. Inductance is the energy stored in the magnetic field around the conductor. More loop area (a conductor farther from its return) means more $L'$.
- **C:** decreases $Z_0$ as $1/\sqrt{C'}$. Capacitance is the energy stored in the electric field between the conductors, which grows with facing area, closeness and permittivity.
- **R:** adds a *negative imaginary* part, $-jR'/(2\omega L')$ times $\sqrt{L'/C'}$, and at low frequency dominates; it also produces attenuation $\alpha_c=R'/(2Z_0)$.
- **G:** adds a positive imaginary part, $+jG'/(2\omega C')$, and attenuation $\alpha_d=G'Z_0/2$.

## 2. The low-frequency crossover

Below the frequency $f_{RL}=R'/(2\pi L')$ the resistance dominates and
$$|Z_0|\approx\sqrt{\frac{R'}{\omega C'}},\qquad\angle Z_0\approx-45^\circ$$
This is an RC "diffusive" line, where signals spread rather than propagate. Take a 0.15 mm wide, 35 µm copper microstrip with $R'=3.3\ \Omega$/m, $L'=289$ nH/m: $f_{RL}\approx1.8$ MHz. A coax like RG-58 (centre conductor 0.9 mm, $R'_{dc}\approx0.03\ \Omega$/m, $L'=250$ nH/m) has $f_{RL}\approx24$ kHz. That is why bench coax is usable for audio-rate to GHz signals at a flat 50 Ω, while a thin PCB trace is only "50 Ω" above a few MHz (see the plot in [[q0710]]).

At high frequency the small corrections are **loss**. At 1 GHz the copper surface resistance is $R_s=\sqrt{\pi f\mu_0\rho}=8.2$ mΩ/sq; for a 0.3 mm wide trace, $R'\approx R_s/w\approx27$ Ω/m. Then $R'/(\omega L')=27/(2\pi\cdot10^9\cdot289\text{ nH})=1.5\%$, so $Z_0$ changes by about 1.5% in its imaginary part, but the attenuation $\alpha_c=R'/(2Z_0)=0.275$ Np/m $=2.4$ dB/m is the thing you notice. Dielectric loss with $\tan\delta=0.02$ (typical order for FR-4, vendor and frequency dependent) gives $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta=14.5$ mS/m and $\alpha_d=G'Z_0/2=0.36$ Np/m $=3.2$ dB/m. Together: roughly 5.5 dB/m at 1 GHz, 1.4 dB for 0.25 m. See [[q0722]] and [[q0723]].

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $\tan\delta$ for FR-4 ranges from about 0.01 to 0.025 depending on laminate and frequency, and surface roughness raises conductor loss further. Treat these numbers as an order-of-magnitude illustration; use the laminate datasheet or a field solver for real channels.

## 3. How geometry sets L' and C'

For a TEM structure, $L'C'=\mu\varepsilon$ (it is fixed by the material and sets the speed), so only the *ratio* $L'/C'$ is a design choice. For the same dielectric:

| Change | $C'$ | $L'$ | $Z_0$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wider trace (microstrip), or larger $d$ (coax) | up | down | down |
| Thicker dielectric $h$ (microstrip), or larger $D$ (coax) | down | up | up |
| Higher $\varepsilon_r$ | up | same | down (as $1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$), $v$ down |
| Thicker copper | up slightly | down slightly | down slightly |

## 4. Coax

The fields are radial: $E$ between the conductors, $H$ circulating around the centre conductor. Integrating gives
$$L'=\frac{\mu_0}{2\pi}\ln\frac Dd,\qquad C'=\frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}{\ln(D/d)},\qquad Z_0=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\mu_0}{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r}}\ln\frac Dd\approx\frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\ln\frac Dd\ \Omega$$

Because the field is completely inside the dielectric, $\varepsilon_{eff}=\varepsilon_r$ and the result is exact for TEM. Examples:
- Polyethylene ($\varepsilon_r=2.25$), 50 Ω: $D/d=3.49$, $L'=250$ nH/m, $C'=100$ pF/m, $v=0.67c$.
- Air dielectric ($\varepsilon_r=1$), 50 Ω: $D/d=2.30$, $L'=167$ nH/m, $C'=67$ pF/m, $v=c$.

Note that in the second example the impedance is the same as the first, but both $L'$ and $C'$ are smaller, because $L'C'=1/v^2$ changed.

## 5. Microstrip

Microstrip is a trace on top of a dielectric over a plane. The field lines between trace and plane pass partly through the board and partly through the air above, so the wave sees an *effective* permittivity between 1 and $\varepsilon_r$.

$$\varepsilon_{eff}\approx\frac{\varepsilon_r+1}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon_r-1}{2}\left(1+\frac{12h}{w}\right)^{-1/2},\qquad Z_0\approx\frac{87}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r+1.41}}\ln\frac{5.98h}{0.8w+t}\ \Omega$$

Microstrip also differs from coax because:
- $\varepsilon_{eff}$ **increases with frequency** (more of the field gets pulled into the dielectric), so the delay creeps up (dispersion), around a few percent over many GHz.
- It is **open**: it radiates at discontinuities, couples to neighbouring traces (crosstalk, [[q0522]]), and has solder-mask and moisture effects. A solder mask coating typically lowers $Z_0$ by a few ohms.
- Return current flows in the **plane** under the trace and spreads laterally, so $L'$ depends on the plane's quality ([[q0713]]).

## 6. Putting it together: when each is used

| Need | Pick | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Board-level interconnect | microstrip/stripline | Manufactured with the board, dense |
| Instruments, test cables, antennas | coax | Shielded, flexible, exact impedance, low loss |
| Very high speed in a board | stripline | Field fully in dielectric, shielded by two planes |
| Board-to-board cable | coax, twinax, flex with controlled impedance | Needs the cable's shielding and repeatable impedance |
:::

::: applications
- **Stack-up design**: choose $h$ and $w$ for 50 Ω single-ended and 90 to 100 Ω differential, knowing which direction each knob moves $Z_0$.
- **Choosing cable**: 50 Ω coax for RF and lab; 75 Ω for video, because attenuation is lowest near 77 Ω for an air-dielectric coax.
- **SMA/edge-launch design**: a coax centre pin must transition to a microstrip with a compensated pad (extra C or cut-outs) to hide the difference in $L'$ and $C'$.
- **TDR calibration**: a precision air line or good coax is used as a reference because its impedance is exactly computable from $D/d$.
- **High-loss laminates vs low-loss**: changing $\tan\delta$ changes $G'$ and loss but barely changes $Z_0$.
- **Probing and fixtures**: very thin or lossy traces have a low-frequency $Z_0$ that rises toward kΩ; low-frequency TDR of such a trace can be misleading.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Per-unit-length, uniform line** with frequency-independent $L'$ and $C'$. Real $L'$ and $R'$ vary with frequency (skin effect, proximity effect) and $C'$ with $\varepsilon_r(f)$.
- **IPC-2141 and Hammerstad formulas** are approximations valid over limited aspect ratios (microstrip about $0.1<w/h<2$) and give a few percent error compared with a solver; trace thickness, solder mask and etch trapezoid are neglected.
- **Typical loss numbers** use $\tan\delta=0.02$ and smooth copper; real FR-4 and copper roughness vary.
- **Copper resistivity** $1.72\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,$m at room temperature; $R'$ rises about 0.4% per °C.
- **Coax** analysed in the TEM regime below the first higher-order mode.

Differential and coupled-line impedances add mode dependence and are covered in [[q0719]].
:::

::: followups
### If I double the dielectric thickness under a microstrip, what happens to $Z_0$ and delay?
$C'$ falls and $L'$ rises, so $Z_0$ goes up (for $w=0.3$ mm, $h$ from 0.2 to 0.4 mm moves it from 53.5 Ω to about 79 Ω in the IPC-2141 approximation). Delay changes only slightly: $\varepsilon_{eff}$ drops a bit for narrower $w/h$ (more field in air), so the line is slightly *faster*.

### Why does a PCB trace look less than 50 Ω at DC on a TDR?
It doesn't have a meaningful $Z_0$ at DC: the line is just a resistor in series with whatever is at the end. A TDR step contains a range of frequencies, but in the first hundreds of picoseconds the edge is above the RL corner (about 2 MHz) so the line looks like $\sqrt{L'/C'}$. The *later* part of the TDR trace (many nanoseconds) rises as the series resistance of a long thin trace adds: for 1 m of trace with 3.3 Ω/m, that's a DC step of about 3 Ω.

### Which is more sensitive to manufacturing tolerance, microstrip or coax?
Microstrip: its $Z_0$ depends on $w$, $h$, $t$ and $\varepsilon_r$, each with tolerance (etch ±10 to 20% of width, dielectric thickness ±10%, $\varepsilon_r$ ±5%). Coax depends on a ratio $D/d$ and $\varepsilon_r$ and is extruded or machined with tight control, so a good cable is typically within ±1 to 2 Ω. That is why a PCB spec is ±10% and instrument cables are better.

### Scenario: your prototype's 50 Ω traces measure 58 Ω on a TDR. What do you suspect?
A thicker dielectric than the stack-up assumed, narrower etched traces (over-etch), lower $\varepsilon_r$ than assumed, or missing solder mask. Each lowers $C'$ or raises $L'$. A check: measure the delay (a higher $Z_0$ from lower $\varepsilon_r$ will also be *faster*) and ask the fab for the as-built stack-up and a coupon cross-section.

### Harder: could $Z_0$ ever be purely resistive and independent of frequency on a real line?
Only if $R'/L'=G'/C'$ (Heaviside condition). Real lines have $R'/L'\gg G'/C'$ at low frequency (R-dominated) and the opposite at high frequency (dielectric loss grows linearly in $\omega$ while $R'$ grows as $\sqrt\omega$). The best you get is a narrow band near where the two cross, though $Z_0$ is close to real across the whole upper band. Heaviside's trick was to add series inductance (loading coils) on telephone lines to approach the condition.
:::

::: interpretations
"How does R, L, and C impact characteristic impedance?" can be read as (a) how the three *per-unit-length* parameters set $Z_0$ (this page), or (b) how *lumped* parasitic R, L, C from vias, pads and packages perturb a line's impedance (see [[q0713]] and [[q0721]]). "R, L and C" is shorthand for $R',L',C'$ (and $G'$). The second part ("microstrip vs coax") compares how the geometry produces $L'$ and $C'$ in the two structures.
:::
