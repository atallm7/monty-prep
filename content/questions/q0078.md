::: basics
### In plain words
A silicon diode needs about 0.6 to 0.7 V across it before it conducts a useful current. That number is not magic. It comes from how the current rises with voltage. The current is exponential in voltage, but it starts from an extremely tiny value, so you must push the voltage fairly high to reach milliamps. Other diodes have different voltages because their barriers differ.

### Picture it
Picture climbing a very long staircase where each step multiplies your reward by ten. You start with almost nothing at the bottom. You need to climb about eleven steps before the reward is large enough to matter. Each step costs about 60 mV. The voltage you climbed is the forward voltage. The analogy is a little loose, since the rise is smooth and not in steps.

### Words you need
- **Forward voltage ($V_F$)**: the voltage across a diode when it conducts normally.
- **Saturation current ($I_S$)**: the tiny current scale in the diode equation, about $10^{-14}$ A for a small silicon diode.
- **Exponential**: here, current rising about ten times for each 60 mV. See [[q0074]].
- **Band gap**: the energy gap of the material, which sets the barrier height. See [[q0064]].
- **Decade**: a factor of ten.

### The simplest example
Start at $10^{-14}$ A and go up to 1 mA, which is $10^{-3}$ A. That is eleven decades. At 60 mV per decade, you need about $11 \times 60 = 660$ mV, or roughly 0.66 V. That is where the 0.6 to 0.7 V number comes from. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers why $I_S$ is so small, and why germanium, Schottky, LED and high-power diodes have different drops.
:::

::: testing
Three layers, matching the three parts of the question:

1. **The number**: ~0.6–0.7 V for a silicon PN diode at typical currents. Easy.
2. **The why**: can you explain it from physics? The forward voltage is the voltage needed to lower the junction barrier enough to get a useful current, and that is set by the **bandgap** (through $n_i$ and $I_S$), the current, the area and the temperature, through the Shockley equation. Strong candidates also say it isn't a fixed threshold: it moves ~60 mV per decade of current and about −2 mV/°C.
3. **The comparison**: why Schottky (~0.2–0.45 V), germanium (~0.2–0.3 V), LEDs (1.8–3.5 V), SiC (higher) and high-voltage rectifiers differ.

The interviewer is checking whether "0.7 V" is a memorized fact or something you understand.
:::

::: why
Forward voltage drives real design decisions: conduction loss ($P = V_FI$) in rectifiers, catch diodes and reverse-polarity protection; headroom in low-voltage supplies (a 0.7 V drop from a 3.3 V rail is 21%); LED resistor sizing; and the temperature sensor and bandgap reference inside nearly every IC, which exploit how $V_F$ (or $V_{BE}$) depends on temperature. It's also the most common "explain why" follow-up in the diode section, and the reasoning carries straight over to $V_{BE} \approx 0.7$ V in BJTs.
:::

::: concepts
- **Shockley equation**: $I = I_S(e^{V/nV_T}-1)$, so $V_F = nV_T\ln(I_F/I_S)$. See [[q0072]].
- **Saturation current $I_S$**: proportional to junction area and to $n_i^2$, so it depends exponentially on bandgap: $I_S \propto e^{-E_g/kT}$.
- **Bandgap $E_g$**: Ge 0.66 eV, Si 1.12 eV, GaAs 1.42 eV, 4H-SiC ~3.26 eV, GaN ~3.4 eV at 300 K. See [[q0064]].
- **Barrier height**: for a Schottky, the metal–semiconductor barrier $\Phi_B$ (≈0.5–0.9 eV) replaces the PN built-in barrier.
- **I–V curve shape**: exponential, ~60 mV/decade, plus $I R_S$ at high current. See [[q0074]].
- **Temperature dependence**: $V_F$ falls ~2 mV/°C at constant current.
:::

::: answer
"For a regular silicon PN diode it's about 0.6 to 0.7 volts at milliamp currents.

The reason is the physics of the junction. The current is exponential in voltage, $I = I_S e^{V/V_T}$, and $I_S$ is tiny for silicon, around $10^{-14}$ amps for a small diode, because it scales with the square of the intrinsic carrier concentration, which depends exponentially on the bandgap. To get from $10^{-14}$ up to a milliamp you need about eleven decades of current, and at 60 millivolts per decade that's roughly 0.65 volts. So the 0.7 volts isn't a hard threshold, it's just where the exponential reaches useful currents. It goes up about 60 millivolts per tenfold increase in current, and down about 2 millivolts per degree as it heats up.

Different diodes have different drops because the barrier is different. Germanium has a smaller bandgap so it's around 0.2 to 0.3 volts. Schottky diodes use a metal–semiconductor barrier that's lower, so 0.2 to 0.45 volts. LEDs use wide-bandgap materials, so a red LED is around 2 volts and blue around 3. Silicon carbide diodes are higher too. And big high-voltage rectifiers end up around 1 volt at rated current because of their series resistance."
:::

::: technical
**Typical values** (at the current where you'd normally use the part):

| Diode | $V_F$ | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Si small-signal (1N4148) | 0.6–0.7 V at 1–5 mA; up to ~1 V at 10s of mA | Datasheet max 1.0 V at 10 mA |
| Si rectifier (1N4007) | ~0.9–1.1 V at 1 A | $IR_S$ of thick drift region |
| Si Schottky (1N5819, BAT54) | 0.2–0.45 V at low/moderate current; ~0.5–0.6 V at rated A | Lower barrier; depends on metal and $V_{RRM}$ |
| Germanium | 0.2–0.3 V | $E_g$ = 0.66 eV |
| SiC Schottky | ~1.3–1.8 V at rated I | Large $\Phi_B$ |
| SiC PN (e.g. MOSFET body diode) | ~2.5–3.5 V | $E_g$ ≈ 3.26 eV |
| LEDs | IR 1.2–1.5, red 1.8–2.2, blue/white 2.8–3.3 V | $qV_F \gtrsim E_{photon}$ |

**Why: derivation.** From Shockley, $V_F = nV_T\ln(I_F/I_S)$. For a PN diode, $I_S = qAn_i^2\left(\frac{D_p}{L_pN_D}+\frac{D_n}{L_nN_A}\right)$ and $n_i^2 = N_CN_Ve^{-E_g/kT}$. Substituting:

$$V_F \approx \frac{E_g}{q} - V_T\ln\!\left(\frac{K\,A}{I_F}\right)$$

where $K$ collects $N_CN_V$, diffusion constants and doping. The forward voltage is the bandgap (in volts) minus a logarithmic term of a few hundred mV. With values representative of a small Si diode, that offset is ≈0.4 V at 10 mA, giving: Ge ≈ 0.25 V, Si ≈ 0.71 V, GaAs ≈ 1.0 V, SiC ≈ 2.9 V. (Same $K$ assumed for all; illustrative only.)

**Schottky:** $I = AA^{**}T^2e^{-q\Phi_B/kT}(e^{qV/nkT}-1)$, so $V_F \approx \Phi_B + nV_T\ln\!\left(\frac{J}{A^{**}T^2}\right)$. With $A^{**} \approx 112$ A/cm²K² (n-Si) and $J = 100$ A/cm² at 300 K, the log term is −0.30 V, so $\Phi_B = 0.7$ eV gives $V_F \approx 0.4$ V.

**Dependencies:**
- Current: $+nV_T\ln 10 \approx 60n$ mV/decade, plus $I_FR_S$.
- Area: doubling area halves current density → $V_F$ falls by $V_T\ln 2 \approx 18$ mV.
- Temperature: $\frac{dV_F}{dT} \approx \frac{V_F - E_{g0}/q - \gamma V_T}{T} \approx \frac{0.65 - 1.21 - 3(0.0259)}{300} \approx -2.1$ mV/K for Si.
- Voltage rating: higher $V_{RRM}$ → thicker, lighter-doped drift region → more $R_S$ → higher $V_F$ at rated current.
:::

::: long
"The number everyone quotes is 0.7 volts for a silicon diode, and at a few milliamps that's about right: realistically 0.6 to 0.7. But I think the more interesting part is why.

The current through a PN junction follows the Shockley equation, $I = I_S e^{V/nV_T}$, where $V_T$ is about 26 millivolts at room temperature. The scale factor $I_S$ is the key. It's proportional to the junction area and to $n_i^2$, the square of the intrinsic carrier concentration, and $n_i^2$ goes like $e^{-E_g/kT}$. Silicon's bandgap is 1.12 electron-volts, which makes $n_i$ only about $10^{10}$ per cubic centimetre, so $I_S$ for a small diode is around $10^{-14}$ amps. To get a milliamp you need the exponential to multiply that by $10^{11}$. At about 60 millivolts per decade, eleven decades is roughly 0.65 volts. So 0.7 volts is really 'bandgap minus a logarithmic term', not a threshold.

That picture explains all the differences. Germanium has a 0.66 eV bandgap, so its drop is around 0.2 to 0.3 volts. A Schottky diode doesn't have a PN barrier at all; it has a metal–semiconductor barrier of maybe 0.6 to 0.8 eV and a much bigger saturation current, so 0.2 to 0.45 volts. LEDs use wide-bandgap materials because the photon energy equals roughly the bandgap: red is around 2 eV so about 2 volts, blue about 2.7 eV so around 3 volts. Silicon carbide has a 3.3 eV bandgap, so a SiC PN junction drops close to 3 volts, and even a SiC Schottky is up around 1.5.

Then there are the second-order effects. Current: every tenfold increase adds about 60 millivolts, and at high current the series resistance adds $IR$, which is why a 1N4007 is about 1 volt at 1 amp. Area: a bigger diode at the same current has a lower drop. And temperature: at constant current the drop falls about 2 millivolts per degree, which is used for temperature sensing and bandgap references."

::: terms
- **Forward voltage $V_F$**: the voltage across a conducting diode at a specified current and temperature.
- **Saturation current $I_S$**: the scale current in the Shockley equation; sets where on the voltage axis the exponential rises.
- **Intrinsic carrier concentration $n_i$**: carriers in undoped material; $n_i^2 \propto T^3 e^{-E_g/kT}$, about $10^{10}$ cm⁻³ in Si at 300 K.
- **Bandgap $E_g$**: energy gap between valence and conduction bands; the dominant material property behind $V_F$.
- **Schottky barrier height $\Phi_B$**: the energy barrier at a metal–semiconductor contact.
- **Series resistance $R_S$**: bulk and contact resistance, dominates $V_F$ at high current.
- **Temperature coefficient**: $dV_F/dT \approx -2$ mV/°C at constant current for silicon.
- **Current density $J$**: current per unit area; $V_F$ depends on $J$, so bigger diodes drop less at the same current.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "0.7 V for silicon, 0.3 V for Schottky" and maybe "because of the depletion region".

**An exceptional answer**:

- Explains the chain **bandgap → $n_i^2$ → $I_S$ → $V_F = nV_T\ln(I/I_S)$**, and ideally does the 11-decades × 60 mV estimate.
- Explains Schottky in terms of a lower metal–semiconductor barrier and larger $I_S$, and LEDs in terms of photon energy ≈ bandgap.
- Quantifies dependencies: +60 mV/decade, +$IR_S$, −2 mV/°C, −18 mV per doubling of area.
- Explains why high-voltage rectifiers sit near 1 V (thick drift region), and why SiC devices drop more.

:::

::: mistakes
- **"The forward voltage equals the built-in potential."** $V_{bi}$ (~0.7–0.9 V) and $V_F$ are related but not the same; $V_F$ depends on current, area and temperature, and is always below $V_{bi}$.
- **"Below 0.7 V no current flows."** Current is ~10× smaller per 60 mV below; at 0.5 V a small silicon diode still carries µA.
- **"V_F is constant."** It rises with current (60 mV/decade plus $IR_S$) and falls with temperature (−2 mV/°C). A 1N4007 at 1 A is ~1 V, not 0.7 V.
- **"Schottky drops less because the depletion region is thinner."** It drops less because the metal–semiconductor barrier is lower and thermionic emission gives a far larger saturation current.
- **"An LED's V_F is just a bigger 0.7 V."** It's set by the bandgap of a different material (photon energy); you can't derive it by scaling silicon.
- **"Hotter diodes have higher V_F."** At constant current, $V_F$ decreases with temperature. (At high current, rising $R_S$ with temperature can partially offset this in some power diodes.)
:::

::: deep
## The number, stated properly

"0.7 V" is shorthand for: **a small silicon PN diode, at room temperature, conducting a few milliamps, drops about 0.6–0.7 V.** Every word in that sentence matters, because $V_F$ depends on the material, the current, the size of the diode and the temperature.

## Why ~0.7 V: from the Shockley equation

Start from the diode equation ([[q0072]]) and solve for voltage:

$$I_F = I_S\left(e^{V_F/nV_T}-1\right)\ \Rightarrow\ V_F = nV_T\ln\!\left(\frac{I_F}{I_S}+1\right)$$

So $V_F$ is decided by **how many decades** separate the operating current from $I_S$. For a small silicon diode, $I_S \sim 10^{-14}$ A. For 1 mA:

$$\frac{I_F}{I_S} = \frac{10^{-3}}{10^{-14}} = 10^{11}\quad\Rightarrow\quad V_F = 0.02585 \times \ln(10^{11}) = 0.02585\times25.3 \approx 0.655\text{ V}$$

Equivalently, eleven decades at 59.5 mV/decade. That's the whole "why 0.7 V" in one line. The next question is: why is $I_S$ so small?

## Why I_S is tiny: the bandgap

$I_S$ is the current carried by minority carriers diffusing across the junction in equilibrium. Minority-carrier density on each side is $n_i^2/N$, so

$$I_S = qA\,n_i^2\left(\frac{D_p}{L_pN_D}+\frac{D_n}{L_nN_A}\right)$$

and the intrinsic carrier density depends exponentially on the bandgap:

$$n_i^2 = N_CN_V\,e^{-E_g/kT}$$

Silicon's $E_g = 1.12$ eV gives $n_i \approx 10^{10}$ cm⁻³ at 300 K, out of ~$5\times10^{22}$ atoms/cm³. Very few thermally generated carriers means a tiny $I_S$, which means you must lower the barrier a lot (most of the way) before significant current flows.

Substituting $n_i^2$ into $V_F = V_T\ln(I_F/I_S)$ gives a neat form:

$$V_F \approx \frac{E_g}{q} - V_T\ln\!\left(\frac{K\,A}{I_F}\right)$$

**$V_F$ is roughly the bandgap voltage minus a logarithmic correction of a few hundred millivolts.** Plugging in constants representative of a small diode at 10 mA (correction ≈ 0.41 V):

| Material | $E_g$ (300 K) | Estimated $V_F$ | Typical real $V_F$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Germanium | 0.66 eV | ≈0.25 V | 0.2–0.3 V |
| Silicon | 1.12 eV | ≈0.71 V | 0.6–0.7 V |
| GaAs | 1.42 eV | ≈1.0 V | ~1.0–1.2 V |
| 4H-SiC | 3.26 eV | ≈2.9 V | ~2.5–3.5 V |

> This table uses the same prefactor $K$ for all materials, which isn't strictly true ($N_C$, $N_V$, mobilities and lifetimes differ by material and process). It shows the trend correctly and lands near real values, but treat the estimates as ±0.1–0.2 V.

## Schottky diodes: a different barrier

A Schottky diode has no PN junction. Current is electrons in N-type silicon thermionically emitted over the metal–semiconductor barrier $\Phi_B$:

$$I = A\,A^{**}T^2\,e^{-q\Phi_B/kT}\left(e^{qV/nkT}-1\right)$$

Here the "saturation current" depends on $\Phi_B$ (typically ~0.5–0.9 eV depending on the metal) instead of the full bandgap, and the Richardson constant prefactor $A^{**}T^2$ is huge (≈112 A/cm²K² × 9×10⁴ K² ≈ 10⁷ A/cm² for n-Si). Solving for voltage:

$$V_F \approx \Phi_B + nV_T\ln\!\left(\frac{J}{A^{**}T^2}\right)$$

At $J = 100$ A/cm²: the log term is $0.02585\times\ln(100/1.0\times10^7) \approx -0.30$ V. A barrier of 0.7 eV gives $V_F \approx 0.40$ V. A lower barrier metal gives lower $V_F$ but higher leakage, since the same large $I_S$ that lowers $V_F$ also flows backwards in reverse bias. **Low $V_F$ and low leakage are the same knob turned opposite ways.**

> [!NOTE]
> Schottky manufacturers trade this off with barrier metal choice: "low-$V_F$" Schottkys exist, but their leakage is correspondingly higher. Higher-voltage Schottkys (100–200 V) use higher barriers and have higher $V_F$.

## LEDs: the forward voltage pays for the photon

In an LED each recombining electron-hole pair emits one photon with energy ≈ $E_g$, so the electrons must arrive with at least that much energy: $qV_F \gtrsim E_g \approx hc/\lambda$. With $E[\text{eV}] \approx 1240/\lambda[\text{nm}]$:

- 940 nm IR → 1.32 eV → $V_F$ ≈ 1.2–1.5 V
- 630 nm red → 1.97 eV → $V_F$ ≈ 1.8–2.2 V
- 470 nm blue → 2.64 eV → $V_F$ ≈ 2.8–3.3 V

Blue and white (blue + phosphor) LEDs often sit noticeably above the photon energy because of series resistance and heterostructure barriers in InGaN devices.

## Second-order effects: current, area, temperature, voltage rating

### Current

Every decade of current adds $nV_T\ln10 \approx 60n$ mV. At high current the bulk and contact resistance $R_S$ adds $I_FR_S$ on top:

$$V_F \approx nV_T\ln\!\left(\frac{I_F}{I_S}\right) + I_FR_S$$

A 1N4007 has a datasheet maximum around 1.1 V at 1 A, well above what the exponential alone would predict, because of $R_S$.

### Area

$I_S \propto A$, so doubling the junction area at fixed current lowers $V_F$ by $V_T\ln 2 \approx 18$ mV (and $R_S$ also drops). Big power diodes run at lower current density, which helps; small-signal diodes pushed to high current have high $V_F$.

### Temperature

At constant current, $V_F$ falls about 2 mV/°C for silicon. The derivation: differentiate $V_F = E_g/q - V_T\ln(K'T^{\gamma}/I_F)$ with respect to $T$ (with $\gamma \approx 3$ from $N_CN_V \propto T^3$, and $E_{g0} \approx 1.21$ V being the bandgap extrapolated to 0 K):

$$\frac{dV_F}{dT} \approx \frac{V_F - E_{g0}/q - \gamma V_T}{T} = \frac{0.65 - 1.21 - 0.078}{300} \approx -2.1\text{ mV/K}$$

That linearity is why junction voltages make good temperature sensors, and the fact that it's negative (complementary to absolute temperature, CTAT) is half of a bandgap reference. The slope varies a bit with current and device.

This matters in practice:
- **Thermal runaway when paralleling diodes**: the hotter diode has lower $V_F$, takes more current, gets hotter. Use ballast resistance or a single larger part.
- **Temperature sensing**: on-die sensors bias a junction at two currents and use $\Delta V = nV_T\ln(I_2/I_1)$, which is proportional to absolute temperature.

### Voltage rating

To block high reverse voltage, a diode needs a thick, lightly doped drift region. That region has resistance, adding to $V_F$ at high current. That's one reason a 1000 V rectifier drops ~1 V at 1 A while a 40 V Schottky drops ~0.5 V, and why SiC (high critical field, thin drift region) is attractive for high-voltage power.

## Why it matters for design

| Situation | Consequence of $V_F$ | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| 5 V, 2 A reverse-polarity diode | Si: ~2 W and 1 V lost | Schottky (≈1 W) or P-FET (≈0.1 W) |
| 3.3 V rail through a diode | 0.7 V is 21% of the rail | Schottky, ideal diode ([[q0079]]) |
| 12 V → 1.2 V async buck | Diode conducts ~90% of the time; $V_F$ ≈ 0.4 V vs 1.2 V output is a huge loss | Synchronous rectification ([[q0203]]) |
| Bridge rectifier, 5 V output | Two diode drops (~1.4–2 V) per half-cycle | Schottky bridge, or active bridge |
| Driving a blue LED from 3.3 V | Only ~0.3 V left for the current-setting resistor | Lower $V_F$ part, higher rail, or a current-source driver |

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A great closing line: "So the 0.7 V is really 'silicon's bandgap minus a log term'. It changes with current, temperature and area, and choosing between PN, Schottky and synchronous FETs is mostly about trading that drop against leakage, voltage rating and cost."
:::

::: applications
- **Reverse-polarity protection**: choosing a Schottky (~0.4 V) over a PN diode (~0.8–1 V) halves the loss; a P-FET reduces it further.
- **OR-ing USB and battery power**: Schottky drops are small enough to keep a 3.7 V battery useful, while PN drops would waste a big fraction of it.
- **CPU/SoC temperature monitoring**: remote-diode temperature sensors use the predictable $V_F$-vs-temperature behaviour of a junction.
- **Bandgap voltage references** (≈1.2 V): add a CTAT junction voltage (−2 mV/°C) to a PTAT term scaled from $\Delta V_{BE}$ to get a temperature-stable reference.
- **LED driver design**: forward voltage by colour sets the minimum supply and the resistor or current-source headroom.
- **Ideal diodes and synchronous rectifiers**: exist specifically because diode $V_F$ losses are unacceptable at low voltage and high current.
:::

::: assumptions
- Values assume room temperature and typical operating currents (a few mA for small-signal diodes, rated current for power diodes). $V_F$ moves with both.
- The bandgap-based estimates assume identical device geometry and constants across materials; they show trends, not datasheet values.
- Ideality factor $n = 1$ in the derivation; real diodes have $n$ between 1 and 2, which increases $V_F$ at a given current ratio.
- Schottky values depend strongly on barrier metal and voltage rating. Low-voltage, low-$V_F$ parts can drop under 0.3 V at modest currents; 100–200 V Schottkys can exceed 0.7 V at rated current.
- An alternative valid explanation of "why 0.7 V" in terms of the built-in potential and band diagrams is fine, as long as you add that the current-dependence comes from the exponential and that $V_F < V_{bi}$.
:::

::: followups
### If 0.7 V isn't a threshold, why does everyone use it?
Because for most circuits the current is in the 0.1–100 mA range, which spans only three decades, so $V_F$ only changes by ~180 mV across it: about 0.6–0.78 V. Using 0.7 V gives errors of ~0.1 V, negligible when the other voltages are several volts. It's an engineering approximation of a logarithm that changes slowly.

### By how much does V_F change if the current goes from 1 mA to 100 mA?
Two decades: about $2 \times 60 = 120$ mV from the exponential (for $n = 1$), plus $I R_S$. For a small-signal diode with $R_S$ of ~1 Ω, that's another ~0.1 V at 100 mA, so roughly 0.65 V → ~0.87 V. This is why datasheets quote $V_F$ at a specific current.

### Why do Schottky diodes leak more?
The same physics that gives them a low forward voltage: a lower barrier and a huge thermionic-emission prefactor give a saturation current many orders of magnitude larger than a silicon PN diode's. In reverse bias, that saturation current (plus barrier lowering with field) is the leakage. Since it's $\propto T^2e^{-q\Phi_B/kT}$, it rises steeply with temperature, often roughly doubling every ~10 °C.

### Why does a SiC MOSFET's body diode have such a high forward voltage (~3 V)?
It's a PN junction in 4H-SiC, whose bandgap is ~3.26 eV, almost three times silicon's. $n_i$ is astronomically smaller, so $I_S$ is tiny and you need ~2.5–3 V to get useful current. That's why designers avoid conduction through SiC body diodes (they use synchronous conduction through the channel or add an external SiC Schottky). See [[q0146]].

### Two identical diodes are in parallel sharing 2 A. Will they share equally?
Not reliably. Small differences in $I_S$ (manufacturing) and temperature mean one diode takes more current, heats up, its $V_F$ drops ~2 mV/°C, and it takes even more: positive feedback toward thermal runaway. A 10 mV mismatch is already a ~1.5× current ratio at 60 mV/decade (more with the $R_S$ contribution diluting it). Fix with small series ballast resistors, a single diode rated for the full current, or a dual diode on one die that shares temperature.
:::
