::: testing
Whether you can separate spikes from ripple and noise (brief, high-frequency, edge-synchronous), name the real physical causes ($L\,di/dt$ in switching loops, switch-node edges coupled through parasitics, reverse recovery, simultaneous switching, inductive loads, hot-plug and ESD/surge events), and describe a **credible capture method**: scope bandwidth and sample rate matched to the spike, the right probe and grounding technique, full bandwidth rather than 20 MHz, triggering on the glitch, and a check that it isn't a probe artefact. The sceptical step ("is it real?") is what impresses most.
:::

::: why
Spikes are what reset microcontrollers, corrupt ADC readings, blow up MOSFETs through $V_{DS}$ overshoot, fail EMC tests and damage ICs rated close to their absolute maximum. They are also the measurement most often done wrong: a 20 MHz bandwidth limit hides real spikes and a long ground lead creates fake ones. Being able to explain where spikes come from, how large they can be and how to capture them reliably is a standard hardware-debugging skill, and it ties together layout (loop inductance), components (ESL, $C_{oss}$, diodes) and instrumentation.
:::

::: concepts
- **$V=L\,di/dt$**: any inductance carrying a fast-changing current produces a voltage ([[q0039]], [[q0041]]).
- **Parasitics**: capacitor ESL, trace and loop inductance, MOSFET $C_{oss}$, inductor winding capacitance ([[q0030]], [[q0047]]).
- **Ringing**: a parasitic LC excited by a fast edge, $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ ([[q0696]]).
- **Inductive kick** in relays and motors ([[q0144]]).
- **Decoupling and PDN** that must supply high-frequency current locally ([[q0032]], [[q0142]]).
- **Measurement bandwidth**: $BW\gtrsim0.35/t_r$, sample rate and probing ([[q0980]], [[q0997]]).
:::

::: answer
"A supply spike is a short, fast excursion on a rail, typically nanoseconds to a microsecond long and tens or hundreds of millivolts, sometimes volts, so unlike ripple it's not at a clean frequency; it's synchronous with edges.

The causes are mostly fast current changes through small inductances, $V=L\,di/dt$. A switching converter's hot loop, the input capacitor, high-side FET and low-side FET, has a few nanohenries; commutating 3 amps in 2 nanoseconds across 5 nH gives 7.5 V, and that inductance rings with the MOSFET's output capacitance at around 160 MHz. The edge of the switch node also couples through parasitic capacitance, such as an inductor's winding capacitance, to the output, and reverse recovery of a diode adds a sharp spike. On digital boards, many outputs switching at once pull a current step through the package and board inductance, ground bounce. Beyond that, inductive loads turning off, hot-plugging a supply through a cable, ESD and line surges.

To capture them I need enough bandwidth and sample rate for the edge, say 500 MHz to 1 GHz for nanosecond spikes, no 20 MHz limit, a short ground connection, tip-and-barrel or a coax with a DC block, and I trigger on the spike with a glitch or width trigger and persistence. Then I check it's real: does it change if I move the probe ground or short the probe tip to ground?"
:::

::: technical
**Causes and typical magnitudes.**
1. *Hot-loop inductance* in a buck: $V=L_{loop}\,di/dt$; example $L_{loop}=5$ nH, $di/dt=3\text{ A}/2\text{ ns}$ gives 7.5 V overshoot at the switch node. Ringing between $L_{loop}$ and the low-side FET's $C_{oss}$: $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})=159$ MHz for 5 nH and 200 pF, characteristic impedance $\sqrt{L/C}=5\ \Omega$.
2. *Coupling of the switch-node edge* ($dv/dt$ of volts per ns) through inductor winding capacitance and board parasitics into the output, as a high-frequency spike each edge.
3. *Diode reverse recovery* ($Q_{rr}$) and MOSFET body-diode recovery: a sharp current spike at turn-off of the diode, producing $L\,di/dt$.
4. *Capacitor ESL*: $V=ESL\cdot di/dt$; a 1 nH ESL with 1 A/ns is 1 V; the capacitor does not hold the rail at high frequency.
5. *Simultaneous switching (SSO / ground bounce)*: $N$ outputs each swinging $C_L$ in $t_r$: $\Delta V\approx N\,L\,C_LV_{DD}/t_r^2$; for $N=8$, $C_L=15$ pF, $V_{DD}=3.3$ V, $t_r=1$ ns, $L=2$ nH: about 0.8 V.
6. *Load steps through PDN inductance*: $L_{PDN}\,\Delta I/\Delta t$.
7. *Inductive load turn-off* (relay, solenoid, motor): $V=L\,di/dt$ limited only by a clamp; hot-plug into a low-ESR capacitor: a cable inductance and ceramic input capacitor ring with up to about $2V_{in}$.
8. *ESD, surge, EFT, load-dump*: external transients; clamped by TVS diodes.

**Capture.**
- Bandwidth: $BW\ge0.35/t_r$ of the spike's edge, more for amplitude fidelity (a rectangular 2 ns, 200 mV spike reads 44 mV at 20 MHz, 184 mV at 200 MHz, 200 mV at 1 GHz with a single-pole response).
- Sample rate $\ge$ 4 to 5× the bandwidth; enough memory depth to span the repetition period at that rate.
- Probe: short ground spring or tip-and-barrel ($150$ nH of ground lead with a 10 pF probe rings at about 130 MHz); 50 Ω coax with DC block or a power-rail probe (1:1, large offset, DC-coupled).
- Trigger: edge at an amplitude above ripple, glitch/pulse-width trigger, or runt; use holdoff, persistence/infinite persistence, peak-detect, segmented memory.
- Validate: probe-grounding test, compare 1× and 10× and ground methods, near-field probe or spectrum analyser.
:::

::: long
"A spike is a brief, fast excursion on the rail: nanoseconds to a microsecond, tens or hundreds of millivolts or more. It isn't periodic at a clean frequency like ripple; it is tied to edges. In a switching converter it appears at every switching transition, so it repeats at $f_{sw}$, but its spectrum extends to hundreds of MHz.

The cause is almost always $V=L\,di/dt$ with a small inductance, a few nanohenries, carrying a fast-changing current. The main example is the hot loop in a buck, input capacitor, high-side FET, low-side FET. If it has 5 nH and 3 amps commutate in 2 ns, that's 7.5 V. That loop inductance resonates with the FET's output capacitance, 200 pF for example, at about 160 MHz, which is the ringing you see on the switch node. The edge of the switch node, several volts per nanosecond, also couples through the inductor's winding capacitance and board parasitics straight onto the output. Reverse recovery in a diode adds another sharp current spike. Capacitor ESL means a decoupling cap doesn't hold the rail at high frequency, so a 1 nH ESL with 1 A per ns is a volt. On digital boards, simultaneous switching of many outputs pulls a current step through the package inductance: ground bounce, about 0.8 V in a typical eight-output example. And then external events: relay or motor turn-off, hot-plugging a supply through a cable into a low-ESR cap, which rings up to twice the supply, and ESD or surge.

To capture them you need a scope fast enough for the edge: the rule is bandwidth at least 0.35 over the edge time, so 350 MHz or more for a 1 ns edge, and ideally more, with a sample rate four to five times that. You turn off the 20 MHz limit, which would show a 2 ns spike at about a quarter of its height. You probe with a short ground spring or tip-and-barrel, or a coax with a DC block, never a long clip lead. You trigger on the glitch, using pulse width or a level above the ripple, with persistence to catch intermittent ones. And you check the spike is real: short the probe tip to ground and see if it's still there, change the ground path, and see if it moves with the board's layout."
:::

::: terms
- **Spike / glitch**: brief high-frequency excursion on a rail, tied to edges.
- **Hot loop**: the high-$di/dt$ current loop (input capacitor, switch and diode/FET) in a switching converter.
- **Loop inductance ($L_{loop}$)**: the inductance of that loop, set by its area; the main driver of spike amplitude.
- **Ringing**: damped oscillation of a parasitic LC after an edge.
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance of a capacitor.
- **Reverse recovery ($Q_{rr}$)**: charge a diode must remove before blocking, producing a current spike.
- **Ground bounce / SSO**: supply or ground disturbance from simultaneous output switching.
- **Snubber**: RC network that damps switch-node ringing.
- **Peak-detect / persistence / glitch trigger**: scope features for capturing rare narrow events.
- **TVS**: transient-voltage-suppression diode that clamps surges.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "spikes come from switching and you see them on a scope".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Uses $V=L\,di/dt$ for each cause and puts **numbers** on it (5 nH and 3 A/2 ns is 7.5 V; ring at 159 MHz).
- Separates spikes from ripple and noise, and lists causes beyond converters: ESL, SSO, inductive loads, hot-plug, ESD/surge.
- Gives capture criteria quantitatively: bandwidth from $0.35/t_r$, sample rate, memory depth, and shows how a bandwidth limit reduces a spike.
- Describes grounding technique and the probe-loop artefact (150 nH ground lead with 10 pF resonates at 130 MHz).
- Knows trigger tools: pulse-width/glitch trigger, runt, persistence, peak-detect, segmented memory.
- Always **validates** that the spike is real (shorted-probe test, probing at the load vs at the source) and gives fixes: smaller loops, snubber, slower edges, local HF decoupling, TVS.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using the 20 MHz bandwidth limit** and concluding "no spikes". It shows ripple but hides ns-wide spikes.
- **Using a long ground lead.** The loop picks up the converter's magnetic field and rings with the probe capacitance, so the "spike" is partly the probe.
- **Measuring at the regulator output** rather than at the load pins, or the opposite: the spike at the pins is what matters, but local to the source it's easier to interpret.
- **Calling every high-frequency fuzz a spike.** Noise and spikes differ: spikes are edge-synchronous; random noise is not.
- **Triggering on the wrong thing.** Edge trigger at the ripple level; a spike 20 ns wide on a rising edge needs trigger level and holdoff tuned, or a glitch trigger.
- **Fixing with bigger bulk capacitors.** Bulk capacitance does nothing at 100 MHz; you need low-inductance local capacitors and smaller loops.
- **Forgetting the sample rate.** A 100 MS/s scope can't capture a 2 ns spike regardless of its analogue bandwidth.
:::

::: deep
## What a spike is (and isn't)

Rails show three kinds of "AC on DC" ([[q0705]]):

- *Ripple*: periodic at $f_{sw}$, mV, understood from capacitor charge balance.
- *Noise*: random, broadband, µV to mV.
- *Spikes*: brief, edge-synchronous, spectrally wide (tens to hundreds of MHz), 10 mV to many volts. They are caused by *fast current changes through parasitic inductance* or by *fast voltage edges through parasitic capacitance*.

A spike is described by its amplitude, width and repetition. In a switching regulator the spike repeats on every switching edge; in digital logic at each clock edge; for ESD or hot-plug it's a one-off.

## Causes and numbers

### 1. The hot loop of a switching converter

When the high-side FET of a buck turns on, the current in the low-side FET (or diode) must commutate to the high-side FET in a few nanoseconds. The current flows around the loop $C_{in}$, $Q_1$, $Q_2$, back to $C_{in}$, whose parasitic inductance $L_{loop}$ (board traces, package leads, capacitor ESL) is typically a few nH. The loop voltage is $V=L_{loop}\,di/dt$.

The loop resonates with $C_{oss}=200$ pF at $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})=159$ MHz with characteristic impedance $\sqrt{L/C}=5\ \Omega$; with only the FET's small $R_{ds(on)}$ as damping, the ring lasts several cycles. On a 12 V input the first peak can approach $V_{in}+7.5$ V or so (limited by FET avalanche and damping), which is why 12 V converters use 30 V parts.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> These are illustrative values. Real loop inductances range from under 1 nH in carefully laid out GaN stages to over 10 nH on a poor layout, and measured overshoot also depends on gate drive speed, damping in the FET and board, and the current at turn-on. Do not treat 7.5 V as a prediction for any specific board.

### 2. Capacitively coupled edges

The switch node has $dv/dt$ of several V/ns. The inductor's winding capacitance (about 5 to 50 pF), board capacitance and the output capacitor's ESL provide a path for that edge to the output: a displacement current $C_p\,dv/dt$, for $C_p=10$ pF and 5 V/ns, 50 mA, flows into the output network and produces a voltage across its (low) impedance. This is why the output of a converter shows a small spike at each edge even with perfect loop layout.

### 3. Diode reverse recovery

A non-synchronous buck's freewheel diode, or the body diode of a synchronous FET during dead time, stores charge $Q_{rr}$ that must be removed when the high-side switch turns on. The recovery current spike adds to the commutation current. Schottky diodes have almost none; silicon body diodes can be substantial, a reason for choosing FETs with low $Q_{rr}$ or adding a parallel Schottky.

### 4. Capacitor ESL: the rail is not held at high frequency

A ceramic decoupling capacitor in 0402 or 0603 has ESL near 0.5 to 1 nH in total (including mounting). A current step of 1 A in 1 ns through 1 nH makes 1 V. Decoupling must be local, with short, wide connections and vias close to the pads ([[q0142]], [[q0032]]).

### 5. Simultaneous switching and ground bounce

When $N$ outputs switch together each charges or discharges its load $C_L$ over $t_r$, drawing $I=C_LV_{DD}/t_r$. The total current change flows through the package and board inductance $L$:
$$\Delta V\approx L\cdot N\frac{C_LV_{DD}}{t_r}\cdot\frac1{t_r}$$
For eight outputs of 15 pF at 3.3 V with 1 ns edges: each draws 50 mA, 0.40 A total in 1 ns, and $L=2$ nH gives about 0.8 V. That is a spike on both the local supply and the ground reference.

### 6. External events

Inductive loads (relays, solenoids, motors) turn off with $V=L\,di/dt$ until clamped ([[q0144]]). Hot-plugging a power supply through a cable into a low-ESR ceramic input capacitor creates an LC circuit: with 1 µH of cable and 10 µF, $Z=\sqrt{L/C}=0.32\ \Omega$ and $f=50$ kHz; the oscillation can peak at nearly twice the supply voltage (about 24 V for a 12 V adapter), limited only by the resistance, which is why a bulk electrolytic with ESR or a TVS is used. ESD, EFT bursts and mains surges are clamped by TVS diodes and filters. In automotive systems, load dump can put 40 V or more on a 12 V line.

## How to capture a spike

### Bandwidth and sample rate

For an edge of rise time $t_r$ the significant bandwidth is $0.35/t_r$. The scope's own rise time adds in quadrature: $t_{meas}=\sqrt{t_{sig}^2+(0.35/BW)^2}$. A 1 ns signal edge on a 200 MHz scope reads as $\sqrt{1+1.75^2}=2.0$ ns; on a 1 GHz scope it reads 1.06 ns. For narrow spikes the *amplitude* also drops, as the following model shows (single-pole response to a 2 ns, 200 mV rectangular spike):

(A real scope is not a perfect single-pole filter, and real spikes are not rectangular, but the trend holds.)

Sample rate must also be sufficient: about 4 to 5 samples per rise time (or 4 to 5 times the bandwidth), so 2 to 5 GS/s for a 500 MHz to 1 GHz measurement. Memory depth then sets how long you can record at that rate: 10 µs at 5 GS/s needs 50 kpoints, enough to cover a full switching period of a 500 kHz converter with its edges resolved.

### Probing

- **Short ground**: a 15 cm ground lead has about 150 nH of inductance; with a 10 pF probe tip capacitance it resonates at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{150\text{ nH}\cdot10\text{ pF}})\approx130$ MHz, which appears as ringing on any fast edge, and the loop area picks up magnetic fields from the converter. Use the spring ground (a few nH) or tip-and-barrel.
- **Best for power rails**: a 50 Ω coax soldered or connected via a MMCX/SMA test point, into the scope's 50 Ω input through a DC block; or a dedicated power-rail probe (1:1, DC-coupled, large offset, low noise, GHz-class bandwidth). Beware that 50 Ω inputs have a low maximum voltage.
- **1× vs 10×**: 10× probes have higher bandwidth and less loading but attenuate the signal and raise the noise floor relative to it. For rails at a few volts with 50 mV spikes, a 10× probe's noise floor can be comparable.
- **Measure where it matters**: at the load pins or the nearest decoupling capacitor.

### Triggering and acquisition

- **Edge trigger** at a level above the ripple; with the rail at 3.3 V and 100 mV ripple, set the trigger at 3.5 V (or −3.1 V for negative spikes) and use holdoff.
- **Pulse-width or glitch trigger**: trigger on pulses narrower than a set width, ideal for rare short spikes.
- **Runt trigger** for partial excursions that don't reach the second threshold.
- **Persistence / infinite persistence** to accumulate many acquisitions and show where spikes occur and how large they get; **peak-detect** acquisition at slow timebases so narrow spikes aren't lost between samples; **segmented memory** or *history* to record many triggered events back-to-back and a high waveform-update rate to catch rare ones.
- **Spectrum view**: an FFT or a spectrum analyser plus a near-field probe locates the frequency of ringing (159 MHz in the example) and which part of the board radiates.

## Is it real? Validating the measurement

1. Short the probe tip to its ground at the measurement point: whatever remains is pickup and setup artefact.
2. Change the ground path: if the spike amplitude changes a lot, the probe loop is part of it.
3. Move the probe to the load pins and compare: a real rail spike changes with position because of PDN impedance.
4. Check that its timing tracks switching edges (trigger on the switch node as a second channel).
5. Check it responds to the fix: a snubber or slower gate drive should change both the switch node and the output.

## Reducing spikes

| Fix | Mechanism | Numbers |
|---|---|---|
| Tighter hot loop, place $C_{in}$ adjacent to the FETs, use a ground plane right under the loop | Lower $L_{loop}$ | halving the loop area roughly halves $L$ |
| RC snubber across the low-side FET | Dampens the LC ring | $R\approx\sqrt{L/C_{oss}}=5\ \Omega$, $C\approx3C_{oss}=600$ pF; dissipation $CV^2f=600\text{ pF}\cdot12^2\cdot500\text{ kHz}=43$ mW |
| Slower turn-on (gate resistor, bootstrap resistor) | Lower $di/dt$, $dv/dt$ | trade-off: more switching loss |
| Low-$Q_{rr}$ FETs or a Schottky across the body diode | Less recovery spike | |
| Small, low-ESL capacitors at the load (0402 100 nF plus 1 to 10 nF), many in parallel with short vias | Supply HF current locally | [[q0142]] |
| Ferrite bead or RC/LC filter between noisy and quiet rails | Attenuate above 10 MHz | [[q0168]] |
| TVS and input damping (electrolytic or RC damper) for hot-plug and surges | Clamp or damp | |
| Slower output edges in digital I/O, fewer simultaneous outputs, more power/ground pins | Reduce SSO $di/dt$ | bounce $\propto1/t_r^2$ |

::: applications
- **Debugging resets and brown-outs** on MCUs that coincide with a relay, a motor, or a radio burst.
- **Validating a buck converter layout**: the switch-node ringing and output spike measured with a short ground spring and a snubber trial.
- **Driving MOSFET and GaN gate stages**, where switch-node overshoot sets the voltage rating needed ([[q0097]]).
- **FPGA/DDR boards**: ground-bounce and PDN HF noise at the supply pins affect eye margin and jitter.
- **EMC pre-compliance**: spikes at the converter edges are the main conducted and radiated emission sources.
- **Hot-swap and USB-powered devices**: surge and ring-up when plugged into a live source.
- **Automotive and industrial power**: load dump, EFT and inductive loads, with TVS protection.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear single-pole scope response** is used to illustrate bandwidth effects; real scopes have approximately Gaussian response and may differ by a few percent in amplitude.
- **Rectangular 2 ns spike** is an idealised shape; real spikes are damped sinusoids.
- **Parasitic values** (5 nH loop, 200 pF $C_{oss}$, 1 nH ESL, 2 nH package) are order-of-magnitude examples that depend on layout and parts.
- **$0.35/t_r$ bandwidth rule** assumes a 10 to 90% rise time and single-pole behaviour.
- **Triggering terminology** (glitch, runt, width) varies slightly between scope vendors.

Other valid views: in EMC work the same events are described in the frequency domain as spectral peaks at the ringing frequency, measured with a spectrum analyser or LISN. In the time domain on slow supplies (linear regulators), what people call "spikes" can be slower overshoots at load release ([[q0151]]).
:::

::: followups
### Why does the 20 MHz bandwidth limit hide spikes?
It is a low-pass at 20 MHz, whose time constant is about 8 ns, so a 2 ns spike barely develops: the peak is $1-e^{-2/7.96}=22\%$ of its true height and it's smeared into a tail. That limit is used for ripple measurements to exclude high-frequency noise, which is why datasheet ripple numbers are quoted with it, but it is the wrong setting for spikes.

### What bandwidth do I need for a 1 ns edge?
At least $0.35/1\text{ ns}=350$ MHz; I'd use 500 MHz to 1 GHz so the amplitude error is below a few percent and 2 to 5 GS/s sample rate. A 200 MHz scope would show the edge as about 2 ns, and amplitude of a narrow spike reduced by a few percent to tens of percent depending on its width.

### How can you tell if a spike is real or a probe artefact?
Short the probe to its ground at the same point and see if the spike remains. Change the ground path (spring vs long clip), probe type, or position. If the spike is real it won't vanish with a shorted tip, it will scale with the circuit (load current, input voltage, gate resistor) and it will appear in the same relation to the switch edge. Also compare with a current probe or the switch-node channel to check timing.

### Scenario: a 12 V to 3.3 V buck shows 300 mV spikes at its output with a 200 MHz scope and a long ground clip. What do you do?
First re-measure with a spring ground or coax and a faster scope, bandwidth at least 500 MHz, to see whether it's partly an artefact. If real, look at the timing relative to the switch node: spikes right at the rising edge point to hot-loop ringing or capacitive coupling, spikes at the falling edge to diode/body-diode recovery. I'd try a snubber (about 5 Ω and 600 pF in the example), slower gate drive, and a small 100 nF/1 nF capacitor pair right at the output, and compare. Layout inspection of the loop area is the permanent fix.

### Harder: can a larger output capacitor remove a spike?
Not at its source. Bulk capacitance has ESL of 5 to 20 nH, so at the spike's frequency (100 MHz and up) its impedance is dominated by $2\pi fL$, which is large (for 5 nH at 100 MHz, 3 Ω). Spikes need small low-ESL capacitors close to the source of the current step, smaller loops, and filters (a ferrite bead and small capacitor) between the noisy and quiet sections. Larger bulk capacitance helps ripple and load steps, not nanosecond spikes.
:::

- Howard Johnson and Martin Graham, *High-Speed Digital Design*: ground bounce, SSO and probing considerations.
- Erickson & Maksimović, *Fundamentals of Power Electronics*: parasitic elements, switching transitions and ringing in converters.
- Oscilloscope vendor application notes on probing and measuring power rails (Tektronix, Keysight, Rohde & Schwarz): check each vendor's current document for probe models and trigger terminology.

::: rubric
- Defines spikes: brief, edge-synchronous, high-frequency, distinct from ripple and noise :: Sets the scope of the answer.
- Gives the $V=L\,di/dt$ mechanism with a number (hot-loop, ESL or SSO) :: Shows quantitative understanding of the root cause.
- Lists several causes: converter edges and ringing, diode recovery, SSO/ground bounce, inductive loads, hot-plug/ESD :: Breadth, beyond one converter example.
- Specifies capture settings: bandwidth $\ge0.35/t_r$, sample rate, no 20 MHz limit, memory depth :: The measurement half of the question.
- Describes probing: short ground, coax/DC block or power-rail probe, measure at the load :: Probing is the main source of error.
- Describes triggering/acquisition: glitch trigger, persistence, peak detect, segmented memory :: How to catch rare events.
- Validates the measurement (shorted-probe test) and names fixes (snubber, smaller loop, slower edge, HF decoupling, TVS) :: Shows engineering rigor and completes the debug loop.
:::
