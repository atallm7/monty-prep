::: basics
### In plain words
Ground stitching means placing vias that tie ground copper on different layers together, in rows or arrays. This makes the ground layers act as one well-connected, low-impedance system. It also gives return current a short path when a signal changes layers, and it keeps floating copper islands from acting as antennas. The key rule is to space the vias no more than about one twentieth of a wavelength apart at the highest frequency you care about.

### Picture it
Think of a quilt. Two layers of fabric lie on top of each other, but unless you stitch them, they bunch up and gap. Stitches every few centimeters hold them flat as one piece. Ground planes work the same way: two planes joined only far away behave like two separate plates with a gap between them. Stitching vias are the stitches. The closer the stitches, the tighter the layers act as one. The analogy breaks in one place: stitch spacing for a board depends on signal frequency, which a quilt does not have.

### Words you need
- **Stitching via**: a via that connects ground copper on two or more layers ([[q0485]]).
- **Via fence**: a row of stitching vias that forms a wall around a trace or the board edge ([[q0490]]).
- **Wavelength in the board**: $\lambda = c/(f\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}})$, shorter than in air ([[q0474]]).
- **Return via**: a ground via beside a signal via so the return current can change planes ([[q0479]]).
- **Ground island**: copper connected to nothing, which can radiate or pick up noise.

### The simplest example
With $\varepsilon_{eff} \approx 3.2$, a wave in an FR-4 board moves at $c/\sqrt{3.2}$, about 1.68 × 10⁸ m/s. At 1 GHz the wavelength is about 168 mm, so $\lambda/20$ is 8.4 mm. At 2.4 GHz the wavelength is about 70 mm, so the spacing must be about 3.5 mm. Higher frequency means closer stitches. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers return vias at layer changes, the shielding of a via fence, edge fences, and mistakes such as rows of vias that slot an inner plane.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know **what stitching is, why it works, and the spacing rule**. Stitching means tying ground layers and pours together with vias at regular intervals. The expected reasons: lower impedance between planes, a nearby return path for signals changing layers, no floating copper islands, a shielding fence around sensitive or RF traces, and suppression of edge radiation and plane cavity resonance. The key number is the spacing: about $\lambda/20$ (or closer) at the highest frequency of concern, with the wavelength measured in the board dielectric.
:::

::: why
Stitching vias are free noise reduction: no parts, and the only cost is some routing area and drill hits. They are also easy to get wrong (spaced too far apart, or placed as a row that actually slots the plane). Interviewers ask this because candidates who know it have usually debugged an EMI failure or laid out an RF section. It connects to the broader topics of return paths ([[q0485]]), shielding ([[q0490]]) and via stitching for heat and current ([[q0539]]).
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** A stitching via connects ground copper (planes and pours) on two or more layers. A "via fence" is a row of stitching vias that forms a wall.

**Spacing.** Wavelength in the board: $\lambda=c/(f\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}})$. With $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3.2$:

| $f$ | $\lambda$ in FR-4 | $\lambda/20$ | $\lambda/10$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 MHz | 1.68 m | 84 mm | 168 mm |
| 500 MHz | 335 mm | 16.8 mm | 33.5 mm |
| 1 GHz | 168 mm | 8.4 mm | 16.8 mm |
| 2.4 GHz | 70 mm | 3.5 mm | 7.0 mm |
| 5 GHz | 33.5 mm | 1.7 mm | 3.4 mm |

**Shielding with a via fence.** A row of vias with centre spacing $s$ behaves like a wall with slot-like apertures of length about $s$. A standard first-order estimate (Ott) for an aperture of length $\ell$ is

$$SE\approx20\log_{10}\frac{\lambda_0}{2\ell}\ \text{dB}$$

with $\lambda_0$ the free-space wavelength. At 1 GHz ($\lambda_0$ = 300 mm) a 5 mm spacing gives about 29.5 dB; 10 mm gives 23.5 dB; 2 mm gives 37.5 dB. At 5 GHz, 5 mm spacing gives only 15.6 dB.

**Return via at layer change.** A through via has about 1.3 nH ($h=1.6$ mm, $d=0.3$ mm): 8 Ω at 1 GHz. Several stitching vias in parallel cut that in proportion. Place them within a few mm of the signal via, closer for faster edges.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The aperture formula is a rule-of-thumb estimate for a thin wall with a single slot; real via fences leak differently depending on via diameter, row count, plane spacing and the field geometry. Use it to compare options. Verify critical shields by measurement or full-wave simulation.
:::

::: long
"Stitching vias are vias that tie ground copper on different layers together. I use them in four main ways.

First, pours. If I put a ground pour on the top layer, any piece that isn't connected to the plane is a floating island, which can pick up noise and radiate. So I stitch the pour to the ground plane with vias, and I delete tiny islands that can't be stitched.

Second, return paths. When a signal changes layers, the return current has to move from one reference plane to another. If both are ground, I put a ground via right beside the signal via, so the return current has a short, low-inductance hop. Without it the current finds its own way, which is a bigger loop.

Third, shielding. A row of vias around an RF trace or along the board edge acts like a wall, which keeps fields in and out. It isn't a solid wall though: the gaps between vias are apertures, and their size sets how much leaks through. Shielding falls off as 20 log of the wavelength over twice the gap, so a 5 millimetre spacing is about 30 dB at 1 GHz and only about 16 dB at 5 GHz.

Fourth, plane resonance and edge radiation: tying planes together at many points, especially near the edge, damps cavity modes.

For spacing, the usual rule is a twentieth of a wavelength in the board dielectric at the highest frequency that matters. That's about 8 millimetres at 1 GHz and 3.5 at 2.4 GHz. Smaller is better, as long as I don't make a perforated slot in the plane: if the vias' anti-pads on an inner layer overlap, they cut the plane, so I leave copper between them."
:::

::: terms
- **Stitching via**: via connecting ground copper on different layers.
- **Via fence**: a row of stitching vias used as a shield or boundary.
- **Return via**: ground via placed beside a signal via to carry the return current.
- **Ground island**: copper tied to nothing or poorly tied.
- **$\lambda/20$ rule**: maximum spacing between stitching vias for good effect.
- **Aperture**: gap in a shield through which fields leak.
- **Anti-pad**: clearance around a via in planes it doesn't connect to.
- **Cavity resonance**: standing waves between two planes.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer gives the wavelength-in-board spacing rule with numbers (8.4 mm at 1 GHz), explains the *aperture* view of a via fence (leakage grows as spacing approaches $\lambda/2$), warns about plane slots from overlapping anti-pads, ties stitching to return-path continuity at layer changes (including ground-to-ground vs ground-to-power transitions), and notes the cost: drill count and routing area. It acknowledges that spacing depends on the highest *harmonic* of concern, not the clock frequency, and mentions that the board edge and connector areas are where it matters most.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Spacing by habit** (every 10 mm) regardless of frequency. The right spacing depends on the highest frequency of concern.
- **Basing the spacing on the clock frequency** rather than the edge's harmonics (a 100 MHz clock with 1 ns edges has meaningful content to 1 GHz or more).
- **A tight row of vias with overlapping anti-pads** that slots an inner plane and breaks return paths.
- **Stitching only the top pour** and leaving the bottom or inner pours floating.
- **Using stitching to "fix" a split plane** with long gaps. It doesn't give a return path where the signal crosses.
- **Using one stitching via for many signals**. A signal via with no nearby ground via on a layer change still has a large loop.
:::

::: deep
## Why stitching vias are needed at all

A ground plane on L2 and a ground plane on L5 are both "ground", but they are only connected at the places where vias join them. In between, they are two plates separated by dielectric: a capacitor, and a cavity. Wherever there is noise or an RF field in that cavity, those two ground planes can differ in potential by a lot, even though both are labelled GND.

A stitching via is a short, low-impedance connection between them. The more vias, and the more evenly spaced, the more tightly the planes act as one conductor at high frequency.

## Wavelength in the board and the spacing rule

The wave in a board travels slower than in air. With $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3.2$ for a microstrip on FR-4, $v=c/\sqrt{3.2}=1.68\times10^8$ m/s. At 1 GHz, $\lambda = 168$ mm. Via spacing $\ell \le\lambda/20$ means the gaps are electrically small; the shield and the planes behave like a continuous sheet. The reason for 20 (instead of 2): the *leakage* through a gap is already significant at much smaller fractions, and a practical shielding requirement is 20–40 dB.

## Shielding effectiveness of a via fence

Treat each gap as a slot of length about $\ell$ = centre spacing (slightly less, minus the via diameter). The aperture estimate is $SE=20\log_{10}(\lambda_0/2\ell)$. It is $\lambda_0$ in air because the leakage field passes through the slot into the surrounding space:

> [!NOTE]
> A via fence shields *in the plane of the board* (sideways). It doesn't shield above and below; that needs the ground planes above and below the trace (a stripline) or a shield can. A "coplanar waveguide with ground" plus via fence is a standard RF structure.

## Return vias at layer changes

When a signal changes layers between two ground-referenced layers, the signal current flows in the via barrel and the return current in plane 1 must get to plane 2. If the planes are tied only far away, the return current spreads through the capacitance between the planes, exciting the cavity. A nearby stitching via gives it a direct path.

How many and how close? For edges with 1 ns rise time (knee 350 MHz), the loop for the return should be small compared with the wavelength; practical guidance is within 1–3 mm of the signal via, one via for slower edges, two to four for gigabit signals. For differential pairs, put the ground vias on both sides or between the signal vias.

## Ground pours and islands

Pours generated by the CAD tool can leave isolated bits (islands) between traces. Such islands are not connected to ground; they are small floating conductors. At RF they couple capacitively and re-radiate. Always (1) remove islands below a size threshold, and (2) stitch every pour region with vias to the plane. On two-layer boards a thin ground pour with stitching vias between top and bottom is the usual method to make a "plane" out of two pours.

## Edge fences and plane cavities

At the board edge, the two planes of a plane pair form an open cavity (like a slot antenna all round the board). A ring of stitching vias around the edge ties the planes together there. Fields in the cavity are then shorted at the edge, which reduces both radiation and the amplitude of cavity resonances. The ring is most effective when it runs within about 2 mm of the board edge and is spaced $\le\lambda/20$ at the highest emission frequency of concern.

## How not to do it

- **A row of through-hole vias with overlapping anti-pads** in an inner plane cuts a slot: the return path breaks. Check by viewing the plane layer, or use smaller anti-pads, staggered rows or blind vias.
- **Hundreds of vias with no reason:** increased drill cost and lowered mechanical strength. Place them where they work.
- **Blind assumption that more vias always help.** The first few matter; beyond that the improvement is small compared with the placement of the vias relative to the signals.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> The one-line formula to remember: "spacing ≤ λ/20 where λ is in the board: about 8 mm at 1 GHz, 3.5 mm at 2.4 GHz." Add that you'd check for slotting the inner planes.
:::

::: applications
- **RF front ends and modules**: via fences around microstrip or coplanar waveguide lines and shield-can footprints.
- **Boards with connectors and cables**: stitching around the connector shield to give common-mode currents a short path to the plane.
- **EMC fixes**: adding a stitched ground ring around the board edge to cut radiated emissions.
- **High-speed layer changes**: ground vias beside USB, PCIe, DDR signal vias.
- **Two-layer boards**: ground pours on both sides stitched with vias to simulate a plane.
:::

::: assumptions
- FR-4 multilayer board with $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3.2$ for microstrip; stripline sees the full $\varepsilon_r\approx4.3$ so its wavelength is shorter ($\lambda/20$ spacing is slightly smaller).
- The highest frequency of concern is set by the edge-rate harmonics or by the radio band, not the clock.
- The aperture formula is a rule-of-thumb for slots in a thin wall.
- Via fences for RF use have additional design rules (via diameter, row count, distance from trace), usually from the RF design guide.
- Stitching affects ground connections only; power plane pairs may need decoupling capacitors to give returns instead.
:::

::: followups
### How do you choose the spacing between stitching vias?
Pick the highest frequency of concern (clock harmonics up to about $0.35/t_r$ for logic, or the radio band), compute the wavelength in the board ($c/(f\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}})$) and use $\lambda/20$ or less. For 1 GHz in FR-4 that's about 8 mm; for 2.4 GHz about 3.5 mm. For stronger shielding I use closer spacing, such as 2 mm.

### Why put a ground via next to a signal via?
When the signal changes layers, its return current has to change planes. The ground via gives it a short path, so the loop through the layer transition is small. Without it, the return current spreads through the capacitance between planes, which creates noise in the cavity and radiation at the board edge.

### Can too many vias hurt?
They can: tightly packed through-holes in a line, with overlapping anti-pads, cut a slot in the inner planes and break the return path. They also add cost and reduce copper area. Keep copper between anti-pads (for example, via pitch at least 1.5× the anti-pad diameter) or use staggered rows.

### Does stitching help between a ground plane and a power plane?
Not directly; they're different nets. For ground–power transitions, the return current has to go through decoupling capacitors, so those placed near the signal via serve the role of a stitching via. That's another reason to prefer ground-referenced routing.

### Scenario: your 2.4 GHz radio module's emissions are 10 dB too high at the board edge harmonics. How does stitching help, and what would you do?
Add a via ring around the board edge at ≤ 3.5 mm spacing (λ/20 in the board at 2.4 GHz), tie all ground planes together there, add return vias beside any signals leaving the RF area, and fence the RF trace and the shield-can footprint. Then re-measure: the estimate says that halving the spacing buys about 6 dB, so I'd expect the 10 dB goal to need a few millimetres' spacing plus fixing any other leakage, such as cables.
:::
