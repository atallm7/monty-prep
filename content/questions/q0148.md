::: testing
Whether you can carry the Thevenin/loading idea from a basic circuit ([[q0008]]) into a real design decision. The interviewer wants the *reasons*, not only "it wastes power": the output depends on **load current** (high output impedance $R_1\parallel R_2$), the output is **not regulated** against input changes, a divider is **inefficient** because the quiescent current flows whether or not there is a load, it can't **sink or hold up** against transients, and you can't make it stiff without wasting a large current. Giving numbers on a concrete 5 V to 3.3 V example is the mark of a strong answer.
:::

::: why
Beginners often reach for two resistors to turn 5 V into 3.3 V because it works on a breadboard with a multimeter. In a real system the load current changes, the input moves, and the supply has to hold within a few percent. If you can't explain why a divider fails, you'll hesitate between an LDO and a buck for the right reasons. The question also bridges to the next one: [[q0149]] (when a divider *is* fine), and to the power-supply decision map in [[q0147]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Voltage divider**: $V_{out}=V_{in}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$, valid only with negligible load current. See [[q0008]].
- **Thevenin equivalent**: the divider's output is $V_{th}=V_{in}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ behind $R_{th}=R_1\parallel R_2$.
- **Output impedance / load regulation**: output droops by $R_{th}I_{load}$.
- **Line regulation**: a divider passes input variations through at the divider ratio, with no rejection.
- **Efficiency**: $P_{out}/P_{in}$. Divider current is wasted continuously. See [[q0162]].
- **Regulator behaviour**: an LDO or buck uses feedback to hold $V_{out}$ against load and line. See [[q0157]].
:::

::: answer
"A divider's output only stays at the ratio if nothing is drawn from it. A power rail has a load, and the load current changes, so the output moves. Formally, the divider is a source of $V_{in}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ behind a resistance of $R_1\parallel R_2$, so the output droops by that resistance times the load current. And the load isn't a fixed resistor. A microcontroller might draw 20 milliamps active and microamps asleep, so the rail would swing wildly.

To make the output stiff I'd have to make $R_1\parallel R_2$ tiny, which means a large current through the divider all the time. Take 5 V to 3.3 V for 20 milliamps. To get the rail within about one percent I'd need roughly a 1.6 ohm Thevenin resistance, so the divider burns about 3.4 watts to deliver 66 milliwatts. And even then it's unregulated: if the input drops 10%, the output drops 10%.

An LDO does the same job, for that same load, with 66% efficiency and a stable output, and a buck even better. So you use a divider only where the current is negligible, like a feedback sense node or an ADC input, and use a regulator for anything that powers something."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** The loaded divider is a Thevenin source:
$$V_{th}=V_{in}\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2},\qquad R_{th}=R_1\parallel R_2,\qquad V_{out}=V_{th}-R_{th}I_{load}$$

**Problems for a rail.**
1. **Load regulation.** $\Delta V_{out}=R_{th}\Delta I_{load}$. With a load that swings from 2 µA to 20 mA, $V_{out}$ moves by $R_{th}\cdot20$ mA. For $R_{th}=31$ Ω that's 0.63 V, which is out of spec for a 3.3 V MCU.
2. **Line regulation.** $\Delta V_{out}/\Delta V_{in}=R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ (here 0.79 for a current-source load, about 0.66 if the load behaves like a 165 Ω resistor), so ripple and drift on $V_{in}$ appear almost unattenuated. A regulator achieves tens of dB of rejection.
3. **Efficiency.** The divider current $I_{div}=V_{in}/(R_1+R_2)$ flows even at zero load. For a rail that must be stiff, $R_{th}$ must be small, so $I_{div}\gg I_{load}$.
4. **No transient response and no protection.** There's no current limit, no soft start, no reverse blocking and no way to hold up for fast steps.
5. **Wrong behaviour at light load.** At zero load the output rises to $V_{th}$, which can exceed the IC's absolute maximum rating.

**Worked example: 5 V to 3.3 V, 20 mA load.** Choose $R_1=40$ Ω and solve $R_2$ so $V_{out}=3.3$ V at 20 mA: $I_{R1}=(5-3.3)/40=42.5$ mA, so $I_{R2}=22.5$ mA and $R_2=146.7$ Ω. Then $V_{th}=3.93$ V and $R_{th}=31.4$ Ω.
- At 2 µA (sleep): $V_{out}\approx3.93$ V, above many 3.6 V maximums.
- At 40 mA burst: $V_{out}=2.67$ V, below brown-out.
- Input power $5\times42.5$ mA $=0.21$ W for 66 mW of output: 31% efficiency at full load, 0% at no load.
- If $V_{in}$ falls to 4.5 V, loaded $V_{out}$ falls to 2.9 V.

**To make it stiff.** For 33 mV (1%) droop over 20 mA you need $R_{th}\approx1.65\ \Omega$: $R_1\approx2.5\ \Omega$, $R_2\approx4.9\ \Omega$, which draws 0.68 A and 3.4 W to deliver 66 mW (about 2% efficiency).

**What the alternatives give for the same load.** An LDO from 5 V dissipates $(5-3.3)\times0.02=34$ mW (66% efficient) with a fixed 3.3 V output. A buck would be about 90%.
:::

::: short
"A divider's output is a Thevenin source with output resistance $R_1\parallel R_2$, so the voltage drops with load current, and a rail's load changes all the time. To make it stiff you'd need tiny resistors that waste far more power than the load uses, and it still isn't regulated against input changes. For 5 V to 3.3 V at 20 mA it would burn watts for 66 milliwatts. Use an LDO or buck, which use feedback to hold the output."
:::

::: long
"The core problem is that a divider only gives you the ratio if nothing is drawn from the middle. As soon as a load connects, it's in parallel with the bottom resistor, and the output falls. A cleaner way to see it is the Thevenin equivalent: the divider is a source of $V_{in}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ in series with $R_1\parallel R_2$. So the output droops by that resistance times the load current.

For a power rail that's a deal-breaker, because the load isn't constant. A microcontroller might draw 20 milliamps while running and microamps in sleep. Take 5 V to 3.3 V. If I size the divider for 3.3 volts at 20 milliamps, with R1 of 40 ohms and R2 of about 147 ohms, then in sleep the output rises to nearly 4 volts, which can damage a 3.3 V part, and at a 40 milliamp burst it falls to 2.7 volts and resets.

I could make it stiff by lowering the resistances. To hold within one percent over 20 milliamps I need a Thevenin resistance of about 1.6 ohms, and that means roughly 2.5 and 5 ohm resistors drawing 0.68 amps, around 3.4 watts, to deliver 66 milliwatts. That's 2% efficiency, and it's a heater.

Even then, it doesn't regulate. If the 5 V input drifts or has ripple, it passes straight through at the divider ratio, and there's no current limit or transient control. A regulator senses the output with feedback and adjusts a pass transistor or a duty cycle. An LDO delivers that same 3.3 V at 20 milliamps wasting 34 milliwatts, and a buck is better still.

So I use dividers where the current is negligible, like feedback networks, ADC scaling into a buffer or comparator thresholds, and regulators for anything that powers a load."
:::

::: terms
- **Thevenin equivalent**: ideal source in series with a resistance representing the circuit's output behaviour.
- **Output resistance**: $R_{th}=R_1\parallel R_2$ for a divider; how much the output sags per amp of load.
- **Load regulation**: change in output voltage as load current changes.
- **Line regulation**: change in output voltage as input voltage changes.
- **Quiescent (divider) current**: $V_{in}/(R_1+R_2)$, flowing with or without a load.
- **Stiff supply**: one with low output impedance, so $V_{out}$ barely moves with load.
- **Regulator**: a feedback circuit that holds the output despite load and line changes.
- **Brown-out**: supply falling below an IC's minimum operating voltage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a divider is inefficient and the output changes with load."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames it with the **Thevenin equivalent**: $V_{out}=V_{th}-R_{th}I$, and explains that the divider has high output impedance relative to a rail.
- Separates **load regulation**, **line regulation** and **efficiency**, and gives each a number for a concrete case.
- Notes that the worst case may be **light load** (output *rises* above the rating) as well as heavy load.
- Shows that making it stiff needs $R_{th}$ tiny, which costs watts: "3.4 W to deliver 66 mW".
- Says what to use instead (LDO/buck) and when a divider is fine (negligible current; see [[q0149]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"It's only inefficient."** Efficiency is just one problem. The output also moves with load and with input, and may overshoot at light load.
- **Testing with a DMM and concluding it works.** A multimeter has about 10 MΩ input resistance, which barely loads the divider; the real load does.
- **Sizing the divider for average current.** The output has to be correct at *all* loads, including minimum (no load) and peak.
- **Believing smaller resistors fix everything.** They lower $R_{th}$ but burn power in proportion to $1/R$, and the output is still unregulated.
- **Adding a capacitor and calling it a supply.** A cap helps transients but doesn't fix DC regulation.
- **Using a Zener across a divider output** as a fix. It clamps the maximum but doesn't regulate for line or load, and adds more waste.
:::

$$V_{out}=V_{th}-R_{th}I_L$$

A regulator tries to make $R_{th}$ as close to zero as possible *and* keep $V_{th}$ fixed. A divider gives you neither: $R_{th}$ is not small unless you accept large current, and $V_{th}$ is proportional to the input.

## A concrete design that fails

Target: a 3.3 V rail from 5 V for a microcontroller that draws 20 mA when active and about 2 µA asleep, with spec 3.0-3.6 V.

Design the divider to give 3.3 V at 20 mA: $R_1=40\ \Omega$ (so it can carry 42.5 mA), $R_2=146.7\ \Omega$. Results:

## Why smaller resistors won't save it

To hold within 1% (33 mV) at 20 mA you need $R_{th}\le0.033/0.02=1.65\ \Omega$. With a ratio $R_2/(R_1+R_2)=0.66$, $R_1=R_{th}/0.66=2.5\ \Omega$ and $R_2=R_{th}/0.34=4.85\ \Omega$. Divider current $5/7.35=0.68$ A, so

$$P_{in}\approx5\times0.68=3.4\ \text{W}\quad\text{vs}\quad P_{out}=3.3\times0.02=66\ \text{mW}\Rightarrow\eta\approx2\%.$$

Both resistors dissipate heat constantly, even when the processor sleeps. In a battery product that's disastrous.

## Line regulation and noise

If $V_{in}$ varies, $V_{th}$ does too: $\Delta V_{out}=\Delta V_{in}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$. With a ratio of roughly 0.66-0.79, a 100 mV ripple on 5 V shows up as about 70-80 mV on the "3.3 V" rail. A regulator reduces input ripple by its PSRR (often 40-70 dB for an LDO at low frequencies; vendor-dependent).

## Why a regulator works

A regulator measures its output (often with a divider as the sensor!), compares it with a reference, and adjusts a pass transistor or switch duty cycle so $V_{out}$ stays put. Feedback drives the output impedance toward milliohms. The sensing divider carries only microamps, so its own $R_{th}$ doesn't matter. That's the right place for a divider: [[q0149]], [[q0223]].

| Option for 5 V to 3.3 V at 20 mA | $V_{out}$ stability | Loss at full load | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|
| Divider (as designed) | poor | 0.15 W | 31% |
| Stiff divider (2.5 Ω / 4.85 Ω) | ±1% at 20 mA only, unregulated line | 3.3 W waste | 2% |
| LDO | regulated | 34 mW | 66% |
| Buck (~90%) | regulated | ~7 mW | ~90% |

## When load current is truly constant

If the load is a fixed resistor and $V_{in}$ is accurate, a divider works. A resistive load *is* just a divider element. But a processor, motor, radio or LED is not a constant resistor, and the supply tolerance matters, so this rarely applies.
:::

::: applications
- **Where dividers are used correctly**: regulator feedback networks, ADC input scaling with a buffer or low source impedance, comparator thresholds, enable-pin UVLO. See [[q0149]].
- **Why every MCU board has an LDO or buck instead**: even a coin-cell-powered sensor uses a regulator or runs directly from the battery.
- **Battery-powered IoT**: a buck or low-Iq LDO keeps rails stable and efficient, with microamps of quiescent current.
- **Automotive 12 V to 3.3 V/5 V**: bucks with wide input range because of load dump and cranking transients, unlike a divider.
- **Laboratory bring-up**: a common mistake is powering a prototype from a divider, which produces mysterious resets as the load changes.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Load current varies** and the rail has a tolerance (e.g. ±5%). With a constant, known load and no tolerance requirement a divider can work.
- **"Power rail" means powering ICs**, not just a bias or reference node.
- **Numbers are for the specific 5 V to 3.3 V, 20 mA example**; the *conclusion* holds for any rail but the numbers scale with current and voltage ratio.
- **Ideal resistors and supply.** Real tolerances (1% resistors) add several percent error to the divider ratio, making it even worse.
- **Alternative valid answers.** One can argue a divider could be used for a rail when the load is a very high-impedance device (current below microamps) or is followed by a regulator or buffer. That's an application of [[q0149]], not a rail supply.
:::

::: followups
### What if I add a big capacitor to the divider output?
It helps with fast load transients (the capacitor supplies the charge), but not with DC load regulation, line regulation or efficiency. The DC output still moves with load current by $R_{th}I_L$, and the capacitor adds an RC time constant $R_{th}C$ that makes the rail slow to respond.

### What if I use a Zener diode to clamp the divider output at 3.3 V?
It limits the maximum (protecting against the light-load rise), but now the Zener must carry the difference between the divider's available current and the load current, so it dissipates a lot at light load, and regulation is still only as good as Zener impedance and the series resistance. That is a shunt regulator, which is a worse version of an LDO for rails.

### Why is a divider fine inside the feedback path of a regulator?
Because the divider only feeds a high-impedance comparator or error-amplifier input that draws nanoamps. Its output impedance and quiescent current are acceptable at that scale (e.g. 100 kΩ total gives 33 µA at 3.3 V). The regulator then supplies the actual load current.

### How does an LDO get a low output impedance?
Negative feedback. An error amplifier compares the divided output with a reference and drives the pass transistor. The loop gain divides the pass element's open-loop output resistance by $(1+T)$, producing milliohm-level closed-loop output impedance at low frequency.

### Compute the output of the divider (R1 = 40 Ω, R2 = 146.7 Ω, 5 V) at a 10 mA load.
$V_{th}=3.929$ V and $R_{th}=31.4\ \Omega$, so $V_{out}=3.929-31.4\times0.01=3.61$ V.

### Scenario: a prototype that powers a sensor from a divider resets whenever the radio transmits. Explain.
The radio draws a large burst, the divider's output impedance makes the rail sag below the brown-out threshold, and the sensor resets. Replace the divider with a regulator sized for the burst current, plus bulk capacitance on the rail.
:::

"Step down voltage for a power rail" means supplying a load that draws real current, which is what makes the divider unsuitable. The question is the opposite side of [[q0149]] ("when is a divider applicable"), and both build on [[q0008]] (derivation and loading). For the general toolbox of conversion methods see [[q0147]].
