::: testing
Whether you know the *procedure* and the *reasons* behind it. For lithium cells: constant-current then constant-voltage (CC/CV) charging with an accurate termination voltage, a gentle pre-charge for deeply discharged cells, a temperature window, a safety timer, and a discharge that respects a cutoff voltage and current limit. The interviewer also wants to hear that you do not do this with a bench supply and a prayer: you use a dedicated charger IC and protection circuit. A strong answer also contrasts chemistries (NiMH and lead-acid terminate differently) and mentions what happens when a step is skipped.
:::

::: why
Charging is the moment a battery is most likely to be abused. A wrong voltage, a missed temperature check or a missing termination can cause plating, swelling or fire, and a consumer product with a charging bug is a recall. Discharge matters too: over-discharge shortens life and can make a cell unsafe to recharge. This question links the physics of [[q0295]] (what to monitor) to the circuits in [[q0304]] (BMS) and the design exercise in [[q0323]].
:::

::: concepts
- **CC/CV charging**: constant current until the cell reaches 4.2 V, then constant voltage while the current tapers ([[q0321]]).
- **Termination**: stop when taper current falls to about C/10 (or a timer expires).
- **Pre-charge / trickle**: low current (about 0.1C) for deeply discharged cells below about 3.0 V.
- **C-rate**: current normalised to capacity; 0.5C on a 2 Ah cell is 1 A ([[q0315]]).
- **Temperature window**: charge only within about 0-45 °C ([[q0295]]).
- **Discharge cutoff and current limit**: do not run below about 3.0 V, do not exceed the continuous rating.
- **Protection layers**: protection IC with MOSFETs, fuses, BMS ([[q0304]]).
:::

::: answer
"For a lithium cell I'd use a dedicated charger IC and follow constant-current, constant-voltage charging. First check the cell is OK: temperature inside roughly zero to forty-five degrees and voltage above about three volts. If it's deeply discharged, I start with a gentle pre-charge at around a tenth of C until it recovers.

Then constant current: charge at something like 0.5C to 1C, so one amp for a 2 amp-hour cell, while the cell voltage rises. When it reaches 4.2 volts, with about one percent accuracy, the charger switches to constant voltage and holds 4.2 while the current tapers off. When it falls to about a tenth of C, I terminate, and I also have a safety timer in case that never happens. Overshooting 4.2 volts is the thing you must never do.

For discharge, I keep the current within the cell's rating and stop at the cutoff, about 3 volts, because going lower damages the cell. I'd put a protection circuit in the pack for over-voltage, under-voltage, over-current and short circuit, with an NTC for temperature. And if it's a multi-cell pack, I'd add balancing, because each cell has to stay within limits. For other chemistries like NiMH or lead-acid, the termination method changes, but the principle is the same: know the chemistry's limits and enforce them in hardware."
:::

::: technical
**Li-ion/LiPo charge profile (single cell, graphite anode, 4.2 V type).**

1. **Qualification**: temperature in window (about 0-45 °C), battery present (NTC), cell voltage $V_{bat}$.
2. **Pre-charge** if $V_{bat}<V_{pre}$ (about 2.8-3.0 V): constant current about 0.05-0.1C until $V_{bat}>V_{pre}$. If it does not recover within a timeout, flag the cell as faulty.
3. **Constant current (CC)**: $I_{CC}$ up to the cell's recommended charge current (commonly 0.5C-1C; fast-charge cells up to 2C or more with datasheet approval). The voltage rises.
4. **Constant voltage (CV)**: when $V_{bat}$ reaches $V_{reg}=4.20$ V (tolerance about ±0.5-1%), regulate voltage; current decays roughly exponentially.
5. **Termination**: when $I<I_{term}$, typically C/10 to C/20, stop charging; do not float at 4.2 V. Re-charge only if voltage falls below a re-charge threshold (e.g. 4.1 V).
6. **Safety timer**: stop after a fixed duration (e.g. 4-6 h) regardless.

**Worked example.** 2 Ah cell, 0.5C: $I_{CC}=1$ A. CC phase reaches 4.2 V at about 75-80% of capacity, so roughly 1.6 h; the CV tail adds the last 20-25% and takes about 0.7 h to reach C/10 = 200 mA, for a total of about 2.3 h in the illustrative profile below.

**Charger topology.** Linear charger: simple, dissipates $(V_{in}-V_{bat})I$: at 5 V in, 3.7 V cell and 1 A it burns 1.3 W (74% efficiency). Switch-mode (buck) charger: about 90% efficiency, better for high current. Programmable charge current often set by a resistor ($I=K/R_{PROG}$, e.g. K = 1000 V for some parts; check the datasheet).

**Discharge.** Keep $I\le I_{cont}$ from the datasheet at the actual temperature; cut off at $V_{cut}$ (about 3.0 V, cell-dependent); under-voltage protection IC trips at about 2.3-2.9 V; allow for sag $I R_{int}$ so the system shuts down cleanly before protection trips.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Pre-charge thresholds, termination current, timers and fast-charge limits are chemistry- and vendor-specific. Use the cell datasheet and the charger IC datasheet as the source of truth.
:::

::: long
"For a lithium-ion or LiPo cell, I wouldn't improvise: I'd use a dedicated charger IC and make sure it implements constant-current, constant-voltage charging.

Step one is qualification. Is a battery present? Is the temperature between about zero and forty-five degrees Celsius, from an NTC? If the cell is deeply discharged, under about three volts, I'd pre-charge with a small current, around a tenth of C, so I don't force high current into a damaged cell. If it doesn't recover within a timeout, I declare it faulty.

Step two is constant current. Charge at the recommended rate, usually 0.5C to 1C. For a 2 amp-hour cell, half a C is one amp. The voltage climbs as the cell fills. This phase gets you to roughly 70 to 80 percent.

Step three is constant voltage. When the cell reaches 4.2 volts, I hold exactly that, with about one percent accuracy, and let the current fall. The last chunk of charge goes in slowly, which is why the final twenty percent takes a long time.

Step four is termination: when the current has dropped to about a tenth of C, I stop. I don't float the cell at full voltage. A safety timer backs this up.

On discharge: stay below the rated current, cut off at around three volts, and design the system to shut down gracefully before the protection IC trips. Over-discharge, over-current and short circuits are handled by a protection IC with back-to-back MOSFETs, and for packs of multiple cells I add balancing. Other chemistries differ in how they terminate, NiMH uses a negative delta-V or temperature slope, lead-acid uses float voltage, but the principle is the same."
:::

::: terms
- **CC/CV**: constant-current then constant-voltage charge algorithm.
- **Termination current**: the taper current at which charging stops (about C/10).
- **Pre-charge**: low-current charging of deeply discharged cells.
- **Charge regulation voltage**: 4.20 V for standard graphite/LCO/NMC cells.
- **Safety timer**: a hard time limit on charging.
- **Re-charge threshold**: voltage below which a new charge cycle starts after termination.
- **NTC**: negative temperature coefficient thermistor for cell temperature.
- **Dropout/headroom**: input-to-battery voltage margin needed by a charger.
:::

**An exceptional answer**:

:::

::: mistakes
- **Charging a Li-ion cell from a plain constant-voltage supply.** Without current limiting, a deeply discharged cell draws large current. Always use a current-limited CC phase.
- **Letting the voltage overshoot 4.2 V.** A 4.3 V regulator error is not "a little" over: the cell ages faster and heats, and above that it can plate and vent. Use 1% or better accuracy.
- **Terminating on time alone.** A timer is a backup, not the primary termination. Terminate on taper current.
- **Floating a Li-ion cell at 4.2 V indefinitely.** Float at full voltage degrades lithium cells; terminate and re-charge only after the voltage falls.
- **Charging cold.** Below about 0 °C use no charge or a heavily reduced current.
- **Using NiMH or lead-acid termination logic on Li-ion** (or vice versa). The chemistries need different algorithms.
:::

::: deep
## Why CC/CV

A lithium cell charges by moving lithium ions into graphite. The cell's open-circuit voltage rises as it fills, and the terminal voltage during charge is $V=V_{oc}+IR_{int}$. If you applied a 4.2 V constant-voltage source to an empty cell (OCV about 3.0 V), the initial current would be $(4.2-3.0)/0.05=24$ A for a 50 mΩ cell: far above anything safe. So the first phase **limits current** (CC).

As the cell fills, $V_{oc}$ rises toward 4.2 V. When the terminal voltage hits the limit, you **hold the voltage** (CV). Now $V_{oc}$ keeps creeping up, the difference $4.2-V_{oc}$ shrinks, and the current falls. The cell is approaching full when the current is small, so termination uses current, not time. Holding the voltage limit at exactly 4.2 V is what keeps the anode potential above lithium-plating territory and the cathode within its stable range ([[q0295]]).

The programming resistor sets the charge current (for some parts $I_{CHG}=1000\,\text{V}/R_{PROG}$, giving 1 A for 1 kΩ; check the datasheet), the NTC on the TS pin lets the charger inhibit charging outside the temperature window, and the BAT pin regulates 4.2 V. **Linear vs switching.** A linear charger drops $(V_{in}-V_{bat})$ across a pass element: at 5 V in, 3.7 V cell and 1 A, loss is 1.3 W and efficiency $3.7/5=74\%$. At 3.0 V pre-charge with 200 mA the loss is $(5-3)\times0.2=0.4$ W. That heat limits how fast a linear charger can charge in a small package, which is why phones use switching chargers (efficiency typically about 90% or more; part-dependent).

## Pre-charge and fault detection

A cell below about 2.0-3.0 V should not receive full current: copper dissolution during deep discharge can leave internal shorts, and high current into a cell with a short is dangerous. A pre-charge at about 0.05-0.1C lifts it past the threshold; if the voltage doesn't rise in a set time, the charger declares a fault. A fully collapsed cell (<2 V, sometimes lower) is generally treated as a reject and not charged ([[q0330]]).

## Temperature and timers

Charge window about 0-45 °C is typical, with reduced current outside the middle band (see the derating profile in [[q0295]]). Always include a **safety timer** (a few hours) that stops charging regardless of termination detection. After termination, the charger waits for the voltage to droop to a re-charge threshold (about 100-150 mV below 4.2 V) before starting again.

## Discharging safely

- **Current**: stay within the continuous rating at your temperature. Sustained high current heats the cell as $I^2R_{int}$ ([[q0292]]).
- **Cutoff**: end discharge at about 3.0 V under load (load-dependent); under-voltage protection at 2.3-2.9 V is the last resort, not the normal end of discharge. System firmware should shut down cleanly before that.
- **Storage**: for long storage leave the cell at about 40-60% charge, roughly 3.7-3.9 V, in a cool place, not full and not empty ([[q0324]]).
- **Multi-cell packs**: charge and discharge limits apply per cell; a balancing circuit keeps cells matched ([[q0314]]).

## Other chemistries (brief)

NiMH/NiCd: constant current with termination by negative delta-V or temperature rise, then a low trickle. Lead-acid: constant current / constant voltage (absorption), then float at a lower voltage, with temperature compensation. LiFePO4: CC/CV at 3.6-3.65 V per cell. The *shape* is similar, but the voltage, termination and float behaviour differ, so never reuse a Li-ion charger on another chemistry.
:::

::: applications
- **Phones and wearables**: switching charger IC with power-path, NTC, USB current negotiation, JEITA-style temperature derating.
- **Power banks**: charge a Li-ion pack from USB and discharge through a boost converter with protection.
- **Hobby LiPo chargers**: balance chargers for 2-6 cells with CC/CV per pack.
- **Solar and energy-harvesting devices**: charger ICs with input power tracking and a low charge-current floor.
- **EV and e-bike chargers**: multi-cell CC/CV with BMS communication limiting current and voltage.
:::

::: assumptions
The answer assumes a single-cell or small-series lithium-ion/LiPo pack with a standard 4.2 V charge voltage and graphite anode; high-voltage (4.35-4.45 V) cells and LiFePO4 (3.6-3.65 V) change the target voltage and sometimes the profile. Charge rates of 0.5C-1C are conservative defaults; fast-charge cells have explicit higher ratings.

The example profile is idealised; actual CC time and tail depend on resistance, temperature and cutoff criteria. A charger IC may add features (power-path management, input current limiting, thermal regulation) not covered here. Always defer to the cell and charger datasheets.
:::

::: followups
### Why does charging slow down at the end?
In CV the voltage is held at 4.2 V, and as the cell's open-circuit voltage rises towards it, the current $(4.2-V_{oc})/R$ shrinks. Also, lithium diffusion in the electrodes limits how fast the last bit of charge can go in. So the final 20% takes a disproportionate amount of time; you could not speed it up by raising the voltage without damaging the cell.

### What termination current would you choose and why?
About C/10 for a conservative design (200 mA for a 2 Ah cell), up to C/20 for slightly more charge and shorter-lived cells; C/5 or more for fast-charge phones that accept slightly less than full charge. A lower termination current gives a bit more capacity but spends more time at high voltage, which ages the cell.

### How would you charge from a bench supply for testing?
Set the supply to constant-current mode at the desired current (e.g. 0.5C), voltage limit 4.20 V, and monitor it. Stop when the current in CV falls to C/10. Use 4-wire sensing if possible, put a thermocouple on the cell, and set the supply's over-voltage and over-current protection. And be in the room: a bench supply doesn't know when to terminate.

### What if one of the series cells reaches 4.2 V before the others?
The charger sees the sum and may keep pushing, so the full cell goes over its limit. Per-cell monitoring either stops the charge or a balancing circuit bleeds the high cell so the others catch up ([[q0314]]). That is why multi-cell packs have a BMS and not just a pack-level charger.

### Your charger IC is getting too hot at 1 A from 5 V. What do you do?
Compute the dissipation $(V_{in}-V_{bat})I$: up to $(5-3.0)\times1=2$ W at low battery voltage. Options: lower the charge current, use a switching charger, reduce input voltage with a pre-regulator, improve the PCB thermal path, or rely on the IC's thermal-regulation feature, which reduces current as temperature rises.
:::
