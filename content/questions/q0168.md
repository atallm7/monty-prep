::: testing
Whether you understand that "3.3 V" is not one thing: a digital rail tolerates hundreds of millivolts of switching ripple, while an ADC reference, PLL or RF stage may need microvolts. The interviewer wants to hear *isolation and attenuation* techniques: separate regulators, post-regulating with a low-noise LDO (with attention to PSRR at the switching frequency and the headroom it needs), passive LC/ferrite filtering with damping, plus layout (separate paths, ground return) and switching-noise management (frequency choice, synchronisation, spread spectrum).
:::

::: why
Mixed-signal boards live or die by supply noise. A buck's ripple at its switching frequency shows up as spurs in an ADC spectrum, jitter in a PLL, or phase noise in an RF transceiver, and the fix is almost never "bigger capacitor". Choosing between separate regulators, an LDO after the buck, or a filter is a daily design decision, and you have to do the attenuation arithmetic to know whether the chosen cure is enough.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ripple and noise**: switching ripple at $f_{sw}$ and harmonics versus broadband noise; both matter for sensitive loads.
- **PSRR**: power-supply rejection ratio, $20\log(\Delta V_{in}/\Delta V_{out})$, falling with frequency ([[q0277]]).
- **LC / ferrite filtering**: second-order low-pass; needs damping to avoid peaking.
- **Post-regulation**: an LDO after a switcher; the LDO needs headroom above its output ([[q0169]]).
- **Decoupling**: local capacitors that supply fast load changes ([[q0143]]).
- **Isolation of return paths**: sensitive circuits should not share current paths with noisy loads.
:::

::: answer
"The idea is to keep the noisy and the quiet loads from sharing a supply path, and to attenuate the switching noise before it reaches the quiet one.

The first option is separate regulators: the digital 3.3 volts comes from a buck, and the analog 3.3 volts from a low-noise LDO. The LDO needs a bit of headroom, so I'd feed it from the input rail or from a slightly higher pre-regulated rail, say 3.8 volts from a buck. That costs a little efficiency, about 3.3 over 3.8, but gives you a clean output. I'd pick an LDO with good PSRR at the buck's switching frequency, because PSRR falls with frequency, and add an LC or ferrite-bead filter in front if it isn't enough.

The second option is passive: tap the digital rail through a ferrite bead and capacitors, an LC pi filter. It's cheap and efficient, but it only filters, doesn't regulate, and needs damping so the filter doesn't ring. It works well for a few milliamps to hundreds of milliamps.

Third, I'd manage the source: pick the buck's switching frequency away from sensitive bands, synchronise multiple converters, sometimes use spread spectrum, and use low-ripple topologies.

And layout matters just as much: separate return paths, keep the filter and LDO close to the load, and keep the buck's hot loop away from the analog area."
:::

::: technical
**Requirement first.** Convert the load's noise limit into a required rejection: e.g. buck ripple 20 mV p-p at 500 kHz and an analog limit of 50 µV p-p needs $20\log(20\text{ mV}/50\ \mu\text{V})=52$ dB. For an ADC, compare with its LSB: a 16-bit, 3.3 V ADC has $3.3/2^{16}=50\ \mu$V per LSB, and a 24-bit one 0.2 µV.

**Option 1: independent regulators.** Digital: buck at 3.3 V. Analog: low-noise LDO from a higher rail (input or pre-regulator), $\eta_{LDO}\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$ and loss $(V_{in}-V_{out})I$. Fully isolates the analog load from the digital load's current steps.

**Option 2: buck then LDO.** Buck at $V_{out}+V_{DO}+$margin (for example 3.8 V for 3.3 V), then LDO. Efficiency of the chain $\approx\eta_{buck}\,V_{out}/V_{pre}$; for $\eta_{buck}=92\%$ and 3.8 V pre-regulator, $0.92\times3.3/3.8=80\%$. Total attenuation at $f_{sw}$ is the sum (in dB) of the LDO PSRR at $f_{sw}$ and any filter. PSRR typically falls from 60–70 dB at low frequency to far less at hundreds of kHz–MHz (device-specific).

**Option 3: passive filter.** An LC low-pass has $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ and rolls off at 40 dB/decade. With $L=1\ \mu$H, $C=10\ \mu$F: $f_0=50.3$ kHz, attenuation at 500 kHz $\approx40$ dB, characteristic impedance $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}=0.32\ \Omega$. Damping resistance should be of the order of $Z_0$; a lightly damped filter has $Q=Z_0/R_s$ (6.3 for $R_s=50$ mΩ, a 16 dB peak). A ferrite bead provides lossy damping, but its impedance is mostly resistive only at high frequencies, and its inductance drops with DC bias current; check the impedance-vs-frequency and DC-bias curves.

**Option 4: source management.** Choose $f_{sw}$ outside sensitive bands (ADC sampling harmonics, IF), synchronise multiple converters to a common clock to avoid beat frequencies, use spread-spectrum modulation to lower peak spurs (at the cost of spreading noise), choose low-ripple or "quiet switcher" parts, or higher-order output filters.

**Layout.** Separate return paths (star or partitioned ground without cutting planes), filter and LDO right at the load, small hot loop for the buck, and no analog traces routed under the inductor.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> LDO PSRR at 100 kHz–1 MHz and ferrite-bead impedance are strongly part-specific. Use the datasheet PSRR-vs-frequency curve at your $V_{in}-V_{out}$ headroom and load current, because PSRR also degrades as the LDO approaches dropout.
:::

::: long
"The core idea is don't let the noisy load and the quiet load share a supply path, and attenuate the switching ripple before it gets to the quiet rail.

First I'd quantify the requirement. Say the buck leaves 20 millivolts of ripple at 500 kilohertz and my analog circuit, maybe an ADC reference, can tolerate 50 microvolts. That's a ratio of 400, or 52 decibels of attenuation at the switching frequency, which tells me what I need.

Option one: separate regulators. The digital rail is a buck at 3.3 volts. The analog rail is a low-noise LDO, which needs headroom, so I'd feed it from the input rail or from a slightly higher buck output like 3.8 volts. The cost is efficiency of about 3.3 over 3.8, so about 87 percent for the LDO stage, which is fine for a small analog load. I'd check the LDO's PSRR at 500 kilohertz, not at DC, since it can fall from 60-plus dB to 30 or 40 dB there, and I'd add an LC or ferrite-bead filter in front if I need more. A one microhenry and 10 microfarad filter gives about 40 dB at 500 kilohertz, but it has to be damped or it peaks near 50 kilohertz.

Option two: the passive path alone, taking the digital rail through a ferrite bead and capacitors. It's cheap and efficient but it doesn't regulate or isolate load steps, so it's for lighter, less critical loads.

Option three: manage the source. Choose the switching frequency away from sensitive bands, synchronise multiple converters, or use spread spectrum. And layout: keep the hot loop tight, filter and LDO near the load, and separate return paths so digital return currents don't flow through the analog ground.

The right choice depends on the sensitivity. For a general sensor, bead plus capacitor may be enough; for a precision ADC or PLL I'd use a buck, an LC filter and a low-noise LDO."
:::

::: terms
- **PSRR**: attenuation of supply ripple to the output, in dB, as a function of frequency.
- **Post-regulator**: LDO or filter after a switching regulator.
- **Ferrite bead**: lossy inductive element; resistive at high frequency.
- **Pi filter**: C-L-C (or C-FB-C) network for supply filtering.
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: $\sqrt{L/C}$ of an LC filter, used to choose damping.
- **Spread-spectrum clocking**: dithering $f_{sw}$ to spread noise energy over a band.
- **Synchronisation**: locking converters to a common clock to avoid beat frequencies.
- **Headroom / dropout**: voltage margin needed by the LDO.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use an LDO for the quiet one."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts from the numeric noise requirement and converts it to dB of attenuation at the switching frequency.
- Notes that you cannot post-regulate a rail to the same voltage without headroom, and chooses the pre-regulator voltage (e.g. 3.8 V for 3.3 V) while pricing the efficiency cost.
- Checks PSRR *at $f_{sw}$* and near dropout, and adds an LC/bead filter if needed, with damping.
- Mentions source-side techniques (frequency choice, sync, spread spectrum) and layout (separate returns, hot loop).
- Gives options in order of cost and effectiveness and chooses by the sensitivity of the load.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Quoting the LDO's DC PSRR.** At 500 kHz–1 MHz, PSRR may be only 20–40 dB; use the datasheet curve at your operating point.
- **Trying to post-regulate with no headroom.** A 3.3 V LDO cannot clean a 3.3 V buck output; it would be in dropout and pass ripple straight through.
- **Undamped LC filters.** The filter can peak at its resonance and amplify noise near $f_0$ or cause instability with a switching regulator's negative input impedance.
- **Ferrite bead alone as "isolation".** It filters but does not regulate; load steps still modulate the rail.
- **Splitting the ground plane.** Cutting planes forces return currents into long loops; use partitioning and careful routing instead.
- **Ignoring the capacitor's ESR/ESL.** A big electrolytic does little against MHz noise; use ceramics near the load.
:::

::: deep
## Why one "3.3 V" isn't enough

A buck converter's output has several noise components:

- **Switching ripple** at $f_{sw}$: from the inductor current ripple through the output capacitor's impedance, $\Delta V\approx\Delta I_L/(8f_{sw}C)+\Delta I_L\,\mathrm{ESR}$ ([[q0157]]), typically 5–50 mV p-p.
- **Switching spikes:** nanosecond edges coupling via ESL and parasitic capacitance, with energy at tens to hundreds of MHz.
- **Load-step transients** from the digital load, hundreds of mV for a large step.
- **Low-frequency noise and reference noise.**

A digital I/O pin tolerates several hundred mV of this. An ADC reference or a VCO does not.

| Load | Typical tolerance | Rail strategy |
|---|---|---|
| Digital logic / I/O | 100–300 mV of ripple (set by noise margin and spec) | buck directly |
| Microcontroller analog supply (ADC) | 10s of mV down to < 1 mV | bead + capacitor, or LDO |
| Precision ADC/DAC | µV to tens of µV | LDO (low noise) + filter |
| PLL/VCO, RF | µV and below at specific offsets | ultra-low-noise LDO, careful filtering |

## The attenuation budget

The requirement converts directly to decibels. Suppose the buck has $20$ mV p-p at 500 kHz and the analog load needs $\le50\ \mu$V p-p:
$$A_{req}=20\log_{10}\frac{20\ \text{mV}}{50\ \mu\text{V}}=52\ \text{dB}.$$
You can build that from stages in series (dB add):

| Stage | Attenuation at 500 kHz |
|---|---|
| LDO (assume 35 dB PSRR at 500 kHz, device dependent) | 35 dB |
| LC filter (1 µH, 10 µF) | ~40 dB (theoretical) |
| Total | ~75 dB, meets 52 dB with 23 dB margin |

Margins are needed because real filters are worse than ideal ones at high frequency (ESL, parasitic coupling), and because noise couples around the filter through the board.

For an ADC, a cross-check with the LSB size: a 16-bit ADC with a 3.3 V reference has an LSB of 50 µV, so supply ripple of 50 µV p-p at $f_{sw}$ is about 1 LSB of spur if PSRR is poor; a 24-bit converter (LSB 0.2 µV) needs the supply and reference to be very quiet, or the ADC's own PSRR to do the work.

The pre-regulator must sit above the LDO's output by at least the dropout plus margin (3.8 V for a 3.3 V output here); the LDO then provides regulation and rejection of whatever the filter let through.

**Efficiency and heat.** At 3.8 V to 3.3 V, 100 mA, the LDO dissipates 50 mW (0.5 V × 0.1 A) and the chain efficiency is $0.92\times3.3/3.8=80\%$ for the analog share of the power; trivial for a small load. If the analog load were 1 A, 0.5 W in the LDO might be a thermal problem, which suggests a bigger headroom trade-off or a quieter buck.

**PSRR vs headroom.** PSRR depends on how much headroom the pass device has; as $V_{in}-V_{out}$ approaches dropout, PSRR collapses. A 300–500 mV headroom is a common starting point, but the datasheet curve at your headroom decides.

## Option B: passive filtering from the same rail

An LC filter between the digital rail and the analog pins attenuates above $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ at 40 dB/decade. For $L=1\ \mu$H and $C=10\ \mu$F, $f_0=50.3$ kHz, and the attenuation at 500 kHz is $-20\log|1-(f/f_0)^2|=39.8$ dB. But the filter is a resonant circuit, and the response peaks at $f_0$ unless damped:

Figure: With only 50 mΩ of series resistance the LC peaks at about +16 dB near 50 kHz, so noise near that frequency is *amplified*, while with 0.3 Ω (close to $Z_0=0.32$ Ω) the peak is under 1 dB. Both give about 40 dB at 500 kHz. Damping is mandatory, either by a lossy element (ferrite bead, series resistor) or a parallel RC damping branch.

**Ferrite beads.** A bead is an inductor whose loss dominates above a frequency set by the material, giving natural damping. But its impedance at 100 kHz–1 MHz can be quite small (the datasheet gives |Z| at 100 MHz, which is misleading), and its inductance falls under DC bias, so measure or read the impedance-vs-frequency and DC-bias curves. A bead plus capacitor is a good cheap filter for lowering hundreds-of-MHz spikes; for the 500 kHz fundamental, a real inductor or an active approach (LDO) is needed.

**Voltage drop.** A series filter element drops $I\times R_{DC}$; 100 mA through 0.2 Ω is 20 mV, so check the load's tolerance. For larger currents use a low-DCR inductor or an LDO.

**RC filter** for tiny currents: $R=10\ \Omega$, $C=10\ \mu$F gives $f_c=1.6$ kHz and roughly 50 dB at 500 kHz, with a 0.1 V drop at 10 mA. Good for reference pins and bias lines, bad for larger loads.

## Option C: reduce noise at the source

- **Choose $f_{sw}$ with the signal chain in mind.** If an ADC samples at 1 MSPS, a buck at exactly 1 MHz folds its ripple to DC (a fixed offset), and near-multiples fold into low-frequency tones. Pick a frequency (and its harmonics) outside the band of interest and away from sampling harmonics.
- **Synchronise converters** to a common clock so beat frequencies do not appear in the spectrum.
- **Spread spectrum** converts a narrow spur into a lower-amplitude broadband hump, helpful for EMI limits and for avoiding a single bad tone, though it adds low-frequency noise energy that might hurt some analog systems.
- **Pulse-skipping vs forced PWM:** at light load, a converter in PFM mode creates low-frequency ripple (kHz range) that LDOs and filters handle less well; forced-PWM keeps ripple at a known frequency.
- **Low-noise switcher parts** with reduced hot-loop radiation and tuned edges.

## Layout and grounding

- Put the **buck's hot loop** (input capacitor, high-side FET, low-side FET) as small as possible, and place the analog area away from the inductor, which radiates a magnetic field.
- Place the **LDO and its capacitors next to the load**, with a short local return.
- **Partition** the board so analog return currents do not share a path with digital return currents; avoid cutting the ground plane under signals that cross. See [[q0143]] for how the capacitors on a rail work.
- Use **Kelvin or remote sense** for precision references to avoid IR drop on the return.
- Check with measurements: a 20 MHz bandwidth-limited probe with a spring ground tip (not a long ground lead) and an FFT or a spectrum analyser ([[q0162]] discusses measurement setup, and the same care applies to noise).
:::

::: applications
- **Data-acquisition boards:** digital 3.3 V buck plus an LDO-fed analog 3.3 V for the ADC and reference buffer.
- **RF transceivers and synthesizers:** buck followed by ultra-low-noise LDO and ferrite-bead filtering for the VCO and PLL supplies.
- **Audio codecs:** separate analog and digital supplies, with the analog rail post-regulated.
- **MCU with built-in ADC:** ferrite bead plus capacitor on the analog supply pin (the usual datasheet recommendation) for modest accuracy requirements.
- **Sensor front ends** (strain gauge, thermocouple, photodiode TIA): low-noise LDO plus RC filtering of the bridge excitation.
- **Camera modules:** analog pixel supply filtered separately from digital I/O.
:::

::: assumptions
- The loads share one input source and one nominal voltage (3.3 V in the example); the answer applies to any common rail voltage.
- The ripple numbers (20 mV at 500 kHz), PSRR (35 dB at 500 kHz) and the filter values are representative; the real design uses datasheet curves and measurement.
- Ideal capacitors and inductor in the Bode calculation; real ESL, ESR, DC-bias effects and board coupling reduce attenuation at high frequency.
- If the analog load is large (amps), an LDO's dissipation is a problem and filter-only or a quieter dedicated converter is preferred.
- If the rail needs *exactly* the same voltage as another but the loads are equally tolerant of noise, the simplest answer is one regulator with separate branch filtering.
:::

::: followups
### Why can't I just add a bigger capacitor to the buck output to clean the rail?
A larger $C$ reduces the ripple from the capacitance term ($\propto1/C$) but ESR and ESL set a floor, and the extra capacitance does not stop switching spikes or load-step transients from the *digital* load coupling into the analog rail via the shared impedance. Isolation (a separate regulator or a filter) is needed. Large capacitance also slows the loop and increases inrush.

### Your LDO PSRR is 70 dB at 1 kHz but your buck switches at 1 MHz. Is that good enough?
Not necessarily. PSRR falls with frequency; at 1 MHz it may be only 20–40 dB (check the curve at your headroom and load). With 20 mV ripple, 30 dB leaves about 0.6 mV, which may be too much for a precision ADC. So I'd add an LC or bead filter before the LDO, increase headroom slightly, or choose an LDO with high-frequency PSRR specified.

### Why damp the LC filter?
A lightly damped LC has $Q=Z_0/R$ and peaks at its resonant frequency by $Q$, amplifying noise near $f_0$ and, together with a regulator's negative input resistance (constant power load), can oscillate. Adding resistance (bead loss, series R, or a parallel RC branch with $R\approx Z_0$) turns the peak into a flat roll-off.

### How would you verify the analog rail is clean enough?
Measure with a 20 MHz bandwidth limit, a short spring-ground probe or a coaxial connection, AC coupling and 1 mV/div or lower, at full digital activity, then use an FFT to look for spurs at $f_{sw}$ and harmonics. Ultimately verify the system metric: ADC noise floor and spurs with and without the switcher running.

### What if both the digital and analog loads need a lot of current?
Use two separate buck converters (one per rail) and add filtering/post-regulation only on the sensitive branch; with synchronised clocks and separate input filtering. A shared buck with a post-regulating LDO for 2 A would dissipate $0.5\times2=1$ W in the LDO at 0.5 V headroom, which is possible with good copper but not free.
:::

::: interpretations
"Multiple output supplies of the same voltage but different noise performance" means the loads differ in noise sensitivity (digital vs analog or RF), not in voltage. The ideal answer lists several isolation and attenuation techniques and says which to pick based on current and sensitivity. It is the same-voltage companion to [[q0167]] (different voltages) and a subset of the clean-rail problem in [[q0169]] (high dropout and high efficiency with a clean output).
