::: testing
Whether you understand a magnetic core as a **flux conductor** and can reason about what it buys you and what it costs. The interviewer wants:

1. The benefit: the core's high permeability concentrates the magnetic flux, so the same turns give far more inductance, $L=N^2/\mathcal R$ with reluctance $\mathcal R=\ell/(\mu A)$.
2. The costs: core **saturation**, **core loss** (hysteresis and eddy currents), temperature and frequency dependence, nonlinearity, cost and size.
3. The air gap: how a gap trades inductance for energy storage and saturation current.
4. Other jobs: shielding and confining the field, coupling windings (transformers, coupled inductors), common-mode chokes, and EMI beads.
:::

::: why
Almost every power converter, filter and EMI choke is a cored inductor. Choosing the core type, size and gap determines the inductance, saturation current, losses, size and cost. The question is the gateway to "what happens when it saturates" ([[q0046]]), "what materials are used" ([[q0049]]) and "where do the losses come from" ([[q0050]]). It tests whether you can think in terms of flux, permeability and reluctance, not just $L$.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductance** ([[q0039]]): $L=\lambda/i=N\Phi/i$; with a core, $L=N^2/\mathcal R$.
- **Magnetic circuit analogy**: MMF $=Ni$ (like voltage), flux $\Phi$ (like current), reluctance $\mathcal R=\ell/(\mu_0\mu_rA)$ (like resistance).
- **Relative permeability $\mu_r$**: ratio of the material's permeability to that of vacuum; 1 for air, 10–10,000+ for cores.
- **Saturation** ([[q0046]]): above $B_{sat}$ the material can't carry more flux; $\mu_r\to1$.
- **Core loss** ([[q0050]]): energy lost per cycle in the core.
- **Air gap**: a high-reluctance section that dominates $\mathcal R$ and stores most of the energy.
:::

::: answer
"A core is a piece of magnetic material, like ferrite or powdered iron, that the winding goes around. Its job is to guide and concentrate the magnetic flux. Inductance depends on the number of turns squared divided by the reluctance of the flux path. Air has a very high reluctance, but a core with a relative permeability of hundreds or thousands has a much lower one, so the same number of turns gives far more inductance. A ferrite toroid with permeability 2000 gives 2000 times the inductance of the same winding in air. That means smaller inductors and fewer turns, so less copper loss.

The core also confines the field, so it reduces coupling to nearby circuits, and it lets multiple windings share the same flux, which is how transformers and coupled inductors work.

The costs: a core can only carry so much flux before it saturates, and then the inductance collapses. It also has losses from the changing flux, hysteresis and eddy currents, which heat it and limit the frequency range. Permeability changes with temperature, frequency and bias.

For power inductors, we often put an air gap in the core. The gap has a high reluctance, so it reduces the inductance but stores the energy and lets the inductor carry much more DC current before saturating. In one example, a one millimeter gap drops the inductance from 1.1 millihenries to 54 microhenries but raises the saturation current from 0.4 to 8.4 amps."
:::

::: technical
**Magnetic circuit:** $Ni=\Phi\,\mathcal R$, $\mathcal R=\dfrac{\ell_e}{\mu_0\mu_rA_e}$, $L=\dfrac{N^2}{\mathcal R}=\dfrac{\mu_0\mu_rN^2A_e}{\ell_e}$. In terms of the core's inductance factor, $L=A_LN^2$.

**With a gap** $\ell_g$: $\mathcal R_{tot}=\dfrac{\ell_e}{\mu_0\mu_rA}+\dfrac{\ell_g}{\mu_0A}$, $\mu_{eff}\approx\dfrac{\mu_r}{1+\mu_r\ell_g/\ell_e}$.

**Flux density:** $B=\dfrac{\Phi}{A}=\dfrac{Li}{NA}$; saturation current $I_{sat}=\dfrac{B_{sat}NA}{L}$.

**Energy:** $E=\tfrac12Li^2$; in a gapped core most of the energy sits in the gap (volume × $B^2/2\mu_0$).

**Worked example** ($N=30$, $A_e=50$ mm², $\ell_e=100$ mm, ferrite $\mu_r=2000$, $B_{sat}=0.3$ T):
- Air core: $L=0.565\ \mu$H.
- Ungapped ferrite: $L=1.13$ mH; $I_{sat}=0.3\cdot30\cdot50\times10^{-6}/1.13\times10^{-3}=0.40$ A.
- 1 mm gap: $\mathcal R_{core}=7.96\times10^5$, $\mathcal R_{gap}=1.59\times10^7$ H⁻¹, $L=900/1.67\times10^7=53.9\ \mu$H, $I_{sat}=8.36$ A. Energy at saturation: 0.09 mJ (ungapped) vs 1.88 mJ (gapped).

**Core functions:** raise $L$ per turn; confine flux (shielding); couple windings (transformer, coupled inductor); store energy (gapped/powder); suppress common-mode noise (choke); provide loss (ferrite bead).
:::

::: long
"A core is a body of magnetic material, such as ferrite, powdered iron or laminated steel, that the winding is wrapped around. What it does is guide and concentrate the magnetic flux.

You can think of it as a magnetic circuit: the current times the turns is the driving force, the flux is the 'current', and the reluctance, length over permeability times area, is the 'resistance'. Inductance equals turns squared divided by the reluctance. Air has permeability one, so the reluctance is huge. A ferrite with relative permeability of 2000 reduces the reluctance by that factor, so the same winding gives 2000 times more inductance. In practice that means fewer turns, less copper, lower DC resistance, and a smaller inductor.

Other benefits: it confines the field, reducing interference with neighbors, and it links several windings with one flux, which is how transformers and coupled inductors and common-mode chokes work.

Now the costs. First, saturation: there's a maximum flux density, say 0.3 tesla for ferrite, above which the permeability falls toward one and the inductance collapses, so the current is no longer limited by the inductor. Second, core losses, from hysteresis and eddy currents, which heat the core and limit the frequency range. Third, permeability varies with temperature, frequency and bias.

For energy storage, as in a buck converter, we add an air gap or use a distributed-gap material like powdered iron. The gap dominates the reluctance and holds most of the energy. With my example numbers, a 30-turn core of 1.1 millihenries saturates at 0.4 amps. Adding a 1 millimeter gap drops the inductance to 54 microhenries but pushes the saturation current to 8.4 amps, so I've traded inductance for current handling. That's the fundamental design trade-off with cores."
:::

::: terms
- **Core**: magnetic material that carries the flux of the winding.
- **Permeability $\mu=\mu_0\mu_r$**: how readily a material supports flux per unit field.
- **Reluctance $\mathcal R$**: opposition to flux, $\ell/(\mu A)$.
- **$B_{sat}$**: flux density where the core saturates.
- **$A_L$ value**: inductance per turn squared (nH/N²) of a core.
- **Air gap**: deliberate break in the magnetic path that lowers $\mu_{eff}$ and raises $I_{sat}$.
- **Hysteresis / eddy-current loss**: loss mechanisms in the core.
- **Toroid, E-core, pot core, drum/rod**: common core geometries.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **"The core stores the energy."** In a gapped inductor most of the energy is in the *gap*; a very high-μ ungapped core stores little energy per volume.
- **Assuming more permeability is always better.** It raises L but lowers $I_{sat}$ for the same size; power inductors want controlled, lower effective permeability.
- **Ignoring saturation** when specifying inductance: the datasheet L is at small signal, no bias.
- **Using ferrite at too high a flux swing or frequency** and overheating from core loss.
- **Forgetting the core also couples fields.** Unshielded cores radiate; shielded types or toroids reduce it.
- **Believing a gap reduces core size.** It increases the size needed for a given L, but allows more current.
:::

::: deep
## The magnetic circuit

Ampère's law around the core gives $\oint H\cdot d\ell=Ni$. In a uniform core with flux density $B=\mu H$ and flux $\Phi=BA$:

$$Ni=\Phi\,\mathcal R,\qquad \mathcal R=\frac{\ell}{\mu A}.$$

This is exactly Ohm's law with MMF $\leftrightarrow$ voltage, flux $\leftrightarrow$ current, reluctance $\leftrightarrow$ resistance. The flux linkage is $\lambda=N\Phi=N^2i/\mathcal R$, so $L=N^2/\mathcal R$ ([[q0039]]).

The physical idea: a magnetic material has atomic magnetic moments (domains) that align with the applied field and strengthen it. That multiplies the flux for a given field by $\mu_r$. A core of $\mu_r=2000$ is like a conductor 2000× more conductive than air for flux.

## The numbers: air vs. ferrite vs. gapped

Take $N=30$, $A_e=50$ mm², $\ell_e=100$ mm, $B_{sat}=0.3$ T.

| Case | $\mu_{eff}$ | $L$ | $I_{sat}=B_{sat}NA/L$ | Energy at $I_{sat}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Air | 1 | 0.565 µH | n/a (never saturates) | — |
| Ungapped ferrite ($\mu_r=2000$) | 2000 | 1.13 mH | 0.40 A | 0.09 mJ |
| Ferrite + 1 mm gap | 95 | 53.9 µH | 8.36 A | 1.88 mJ |

The gap has 95 % of the total reluctance, so it sets $L$ and *linearizes* the inductor: the core's nonlinear permeability matters less. Saturation current rises ~21× and the stored energy at saturation rises by the same factor. That's the key idea behind power inductors: **energy is stored in the gap** (in powdered-iron cores, in the many tiny distributed gaps between particles).

## Other jobs of a core

1. **Shielding / field confinement.** A closed toroid or a shielded drum core keeps stray flux off neighboring circuits. Open rod/drum cores and unshielded bobbin parts radiate more.
2. **Coupling windings.** A shared core links the flux of two or more windings: transformers, flyback converters, coupled inductors, common-mode chokes (where both lines pass through one core so differential-mode flux cancels while common-mode flux adds).
3. **Lossy filtering.** Ferrite beads use a lossy core ($\mu''$ large) so their impedance is mostly resistive at EMI frequencies.
4. **Inductance density.** Cores reduce the number of turns, thus DCR, thus copper loss at a given L.
5. **Core geometry/material tailoring.** Gap, material, shape (toroid, E, pot, planar) tune $L$, saturation, losses, EMI and manufacturability.

## Costs and limits

- **Saturation:** $B\le B_{sat}$ (ferrite ≈ 0.3–0.5 T, powdered iron ≈ 1–1.5 T, silicon steel ≈ 1.5–2 T; see [[q0049]]).
- **Core loss:** hysteresis and eddy currents ($\propto f^\alpha B^\beta$) limit frequency and flux swing ([[q0050]]).
- **Temperature:** permeability and $B_{sat}$ change with temperature; ferrite loses magnetization at the Curie point (~100–300 °C depending on material).
- **Nonlinearity and distortion:** $\mu$ depends on $B$; in audio/RF filters this makes harmonics.
- **Fringing:** flux bulging around a gap induces eddy currents in nearby copper (extra loss).
- **Cost, size, weight, mechanical fragility** (ferrite is brittle).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Saturation flux density and permeability depend on material grade, temperature and frequency. The numbers here are typical values for illustration; use the manufacturer's curves.
:::

::: applications
- **Buck/boost converter inductors**: gapped ferrite or powdered-iron cores to store energy without saturating.
- **Transformers** in flyback, forward and isolated supplies: ferrite E/ETD/planar cores.
- **Common-mode chokes** on USB, Ethernet, mains: nanocrystalline or high-μ ferrite toroids.
- **EMI ferrite beads** on power rails and cables.
- **RF inductors and baluns**: low-permeability ferrite or air core for high Q.
- **Current-sense transformers and wireless-charging coils** using flux-guiding ferrite sheets.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear region** for $L=N^2/\mathcal R$; the gap formulas assume ideal geometry and ignore fringing (which raises $L$ somewhat).
- **Uniform flux** over a single effective length/area; real cores have varying cross-sections.
- **Typical material parameters**; they vary by grade, temperature and frequency.
- Initial permeability is the small-signal value; incremental permeability under DC bias is lower.
- The answer for an *RF* inductor differs: air cores avoid core loss and saturation altogether, accepting lower inductance per size.
:::

::: followups
### How much more inductance does a ferrite core with $\mu_r=1000$ give than air, for the same winding and geometry?
Up to 1000×, because $L\propto\mu_r$ for a closed core. With an air gap the multiplier is much less: $\mu_{eff}=\mu_r/(1+\mu_r\ell_g/\ell_e)$. For $\ell_g/\ell_e=0.01$ it's $1000/11=91$.

### Why do power inductors use gapped cores or powdered iron instead of high-μ ungapped ferrite?
Because the ungapped core saturates at very small current for a given inductance; energy is $\tfrac12Li^2$ and a high-μ core stores little of it in the core volume. The gap (or the distributed gap in powder) stores the energy and linearizes the inductor, raising $I_{sat}$. The cost is more turns or larger size for the same L, and fringing loss.

### What's an $A_L$ value and how do you use it?
$A_L=L/N^2$ in nH/turn², given for a specific core (and gap). To get 10 µH from a core with $A_L=100$ nH/N², you need $N=\sqrt{10\ \mu\text{H}/100\ \text{nH}}=10$ turns. Then check $B_{peak}=LI_{pk}/(NA_e)$ against saturation.

### What happens if you remove the core from a working inductor?
Inductance falls by roughly the effective permeability (down by 10–1000×), so for the same applied voltage and frequency the current ripple rises by the same factor. In a converter the peak current would be many times higher and could destroy the switch.

### Why can an ungapped ferrite common-mode choke still carry a large load current?
Because the load current flows in opposite directions in the two windings, so the net MMF is zero and the core sees no DC flux. Only the common-mode (same-direction) current drives the core, so it can use a very high permeability without saturating.
