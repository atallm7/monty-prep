::: testing
Whether you can explain **distance** in terms of physics rather than folklore. The interviewer is checking:

1. Distance means inductance ($v = L\,di/dt$), and the budget is the allowed supply droop.
2. That it is the *inductance*, not the propagation delay, that sets how close is close enough, and you can show it with numbers.
3. The extra consequences: an anti-resonance with the chip's own capacitance, a bigger radiating loop, and shared-rail coupling.
4. Awareness of diminishing returns: the IC's own package and bond-wire inductance sets a floor.
:::

::: why
"Place it as close as possible" is the most repeated decoupling rule and also the most ignored when a board is crowded. If you can show quantitatively what 5 mm costs, you can defend a layout change, win a placement argument, or decide which capacitors truly must hug the part. Interviewers use the question to separate engineers who memorised a rule from those who know *why* and can judge when a millimetre matters.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductance of a connection**: a conductor of length $\ell$ has partial inductance on the order of 0.3–1 nH/mm ([[q0509]], [[q0510]]).
- **Inductive voltage drop**: $v = L\,di/dt$.
- **Loop**: supply pin to capacitor to ground pin and back through planes ([[q0501]]).
- **Resonance of the decoupling loop**: $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ with the capacitor.
- **Lumped vs distributed**: a short connection behaves as a lumped inductor; a long one begins to behave as a transmission line.
- **Loop area and radiation**: bigger loops radiate and couple more.
:::

::: answer
"Because the capacitor is only useful if it can deliver current faster than the chip changes its demand, and everything between the capacitor and the pin is inductance. Every millimetre of trace or via adds roughly half a nanohenry to a nanohenry, and the voltage across that inductance is L times di/dt. A chip stepping 50 milliamps per nanosecond through 1 nanohenry takes 50 millivolts of droop right there, no matter how big the capacitor is. Put the capacitor 20 millimetres away and that's 10 to 20 nanohenries and a volt of droop.

That inductance also resonates against the chip's own capacitance, which drops the anti-resonance into the chip's active band, and the long loop, from capacitor to pin and back through the ground, radiates and picks up noise. And the part of the loop you can't remove, the package pin and bond wire, already uses up some of the budget, which is why the closest you can get is what counts.

It's inductance rather than delay that matters. The signal delay over 20 millimetres is only about 130 picoseconds, which is short compared to a nanosecond edge, but the inductance is already ruinous. So the rule isn't about waves, it's about L di/dt."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism.** The current burst $\Delta I$ must travel from the capacitor to the pin through a connection with inductance $L_c$, and return through the ground path with $L_g$. The loop inductance $L_{loop}=L_c+L_g+ESL-2M$ (mutual inductance $M$ helps if the paths run side by side). The pin voltage droops by

$$\Delta V \approx L_{loop}\frac{di}{dt}+\frac{I\,t}{C}+I\,R$$

**Numbers.** Suppose the load steps 50 mA in 1 ns ($di/dt=0.05$ A/ns) and the budget is 50 mV of supply droop. Then $L_{loop}\le 50\text{ mV}/0.05\text{ A/ns} = 1$ nH. A 100 nF 0402 capacitor already uses 0.5 nH for its own ESL, leaving 0.5 nH for pads, vias and trace: roughly 1 mm of unreferenced trace plus short vias. At 10 mm of fan-out, $L_{loop}\approx 5$–$10$ nH and the droop is 250–500 mV.

**Anti-resonance.** The loop inductance also forms a tank with the chip's own on-die plus package capacitance $C_d$ (the board capacitor looks like a short at that frequency): $f_{ar}\approx 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{loop}C_d})$, with a peak impedance of roughly $L_{loop}/(R\,C_d)$ for loop resistance $R$. For $C_d=10$ nF and $R=0.1\ \Omega$: 1 nH gives 50 MHz and about 1 Ω; 10 nH gives 16 MHz and about 10 Ω. More distance means a lower, taller peak, moving into the band where the chip has energy. (Simple model; real die damping lowers the peak.)

**Delay vs inductance.** Propagation on FR-4 microstrip is about 6–7 ps/mm, so 20 mm is ~130 ps one-way, small against a 1 ns edge (a common criterion is that a connection is electrically short if its one-way delay is below about $t_r/6$–$t_r/10$, i.e. 100–170 ps for 1 ns). The inductance limit (1–2 mm) is far tighter than the delay limit (15–25 mm), so inductance governs.

**Emissions.** Radiation from a small current loop scales with loop area and $f^2$; a 10× bigger loop is ~20 dB worse at the same current.

**Floor.** The IC's own package (bond wires/leads, 1–5 nH for QFP-type, well under 1 nH for flip-chip/BGA, vendor- and package-dependent) and on-die capacitance set the limit: moving the capacitor closer than the package's own inductance gives diminishing returns.
:::

::: short
"Because everything between the capacitor and the pin is inductance, about half to one nanohenry per millimetre, and the pin voltage drops by L times di/dt. A chip stepping 50 mA per nanosecond through just 1 nH loses 50 mV, regardless of capacitor size. Farther away also means the loop inductance resonates with the chip's own capacitance at a lower frequency, and the bigger loop radiates. It's inductance, not signal delay, that sets the distance, so the capacitor must be within millimetres."
:::

::: long
"Think about what the capacitor is actually supposed to do: deliver a nanosecond burst of current to the chip. The only path from capacitor to chip is copper, and copper has inductance, roughly half to one nanohenry per millimetre for a trace, and about a nanohenry for each millimetre or two of via. The voltage needed to push a changing current through an inductance is L di/dt. So the capacitor can have infinite capacitance, and the chip's pin still sees L di/dt across the connection.

Numbers: say the chip steps 50 milliamps per nanosecond, and I can tolerate 50 millivolts of droop. That budget is 1 nanohenry total, and a 100 nanofarad ceramic already uses half a nanohenry for its own ESL. So I have about half a nanohenry, roughly a millimetre of trace and two short vias, for everything else. If the capacitor is 10 millimetres away, I'm looking at 5 to 10 nanohenries, and 250 to 500 millivolts of droop.

Second, that inductance resonates against the chip's own die and package capacitance, at one over two pi root L C_die. With 10 nanofarads on the die and 10 nanohenries of loop, that's about 16 megahertz with a peak impedance of roughly L over R C, around 10 ohms with 0.1 ohm of loop resistance. With 1 nanohenry it's 50 megahertz and 1 ohm. So distance drops the resonance into the chip's active band and raises the peak. The far capacitor does little at the frequencies the chip cares about.

Third, the current loop is bigger, so it radiates more, and it couples noise into neighbours. A ten times bigger loop is about twenty dB worse.

And it isn't a wave problem: the delay over 20 millimetres of FR-4 is about 130 picoseconds, short next to a nanosecond edge. The inductance is the killer, and that's why the guidance is millimetres. Beyond that, the package's own inductance sets a floor, so it's about getting as close as the package lets me."
:::

::: terms
- **Loop inductance**: total inductance of the capacitor-to-chip current loop.
- **$L\,di/dt$ drop**: voltage developed across an inductance by changing current.
- **Anti-resonance**: parallel resonance of loop inductance with the chip's on-die/package capacitance, $1/(2\pi\sqrt{L C_d})$.
- **Electrically short**: a connection whose delay is small compared to the signal's edge time.
- **Partial inductance**: inductance assigned to a conductor segment of a loop.
- **Mutual inductance**: coupling between nearby conductors; opposing currents reduce net inductance.
- **Package inductance**: inductance of bond wires, leads or balls inside the IC package.
- **Radiated emission**: field energy leaving the loop; increases with area and frequency.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "inductance of the trace". An exceptional one:

- States the **inductance budget** from $\Delta V_{allowed}/(di/dt)$: 50 mV / 0.05 A/ns = 1 nH, then subtracts the capacitor's own ESL.
- Compares the **delay criterion** (tens of mm) with the **inductance criterion** (about a mm) to show inductance governs.
- Adds the **anti-resonance** consequence ($f_{ar}$, peak $\approx L/(RC_d)$) and the **EMI** consequence (loop area).
- Notes the **floor** from package inductance and on-die capacitance, i.e. diminishing returns.
- Says what to do if the part can't be that close: smaller capacitor in a better package, via-in-pad, back side under the BGA.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Close because of signal delay."** The delay over a few cm is far shorter than the edge; inductance is the problem.
- **"A bigger capacitor makes distance less important."** Capacitance doesn't change inductance.
- **Counting only trace length**, forgetting via inductance and the *ground* return, which is half the loop.
- **Trusting a capacitor on the far side of a long plane**: spreading inductance of the plane between the two vias adds to the loop.
- **Chasing the last millimetre when the package inductance dominates**: it's better to improve vias and planes first.
:::

::: deep
## Setting up the inductance budget

A chip's supply current is not constant. Think of a step $\Delta I$ over a time $t_r$, so $di/dt\approx\Delta I/t_r$. The capacitor is going to supply this current. Everything in the loop from capacitor to chip behaves as a series inductance $L_{loop}$. The supply voltage at the chip pin dips by

$$\Delta V_L = L_{loop}\,\frac{\Delta I}{t_r}$$

Rearranging gives the **maximum loop inductance** for a given droop budget:

$$L_{loop,max} = \Delta V_{allowed}\,\frac{t_r}{\Delta I}$$

For a processor core step of 1 A in 10 ns with a 30 mV budget: 0.3 nH. For a small MCU step of 20 mA in 2 ns with 100 mV allowed: 10 nH. The faster and bigger the step, the closer the capacitor must be.

## Why not "it's the delay"?

An electrical signal moves at about $c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$. On FR-4 microstrip, $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx 3$, so $v\approx 17$ cm/ns and the delay is about 6 ps/mm. A common rule says a connection is "short" if the one-way delay is below $t_r/6$ or so. For $t_r = 1$ ns that allows ~170 ps, about 28 mm. The capacitor could be that far away and still be *electrically near*, yet at 28 mm it contributes 14–28 nH, which is hopeless for the droop budget. So the two limits differ by more than an order of magnitude and the inductance limit is the binding one. Wave effects only enter when you go beyond a few cm.

## The anti-resonance penalty

The board capacitor is a short at these frequencies, so the loop inductance $L_{loop}$ resonates against the chip's own on-die and package capacitance $C_d$. That is a parallel (anti-)resonance of the PDN seen from the die:

$$f_{ar}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{loop}C_d}},\qquad |Z|_{peak}\approx\frac{L_{loop}}{R\,C_d}$$

Take $C_d = 10$ nF and loop resistance $R = 0.1\ \Omega$ (trace plus die):

| $L_{loop}$ | $f_{ar}$ | $|Z|_{peak}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 nH | 50.3 MHz | 1 Ω |
| 3 nH | 29.1 MHz | 3 Ω |
| 10 nH | 15.9 MHz | 10 Ω |
| 30 nH | 9.2 MHz | 30 Ω |

The peak impedance grows in proportion to $L_{loop}$ and the resonance slides down into the band where digital activity (clock harmonics, memory bursts) lives. Current at that frequency then produces a large voltage ripple that other chips on the rail also see. Loop resistance (ESR, trace) damps it.

## Radiation and coupling

The magnetic dipole radiation of a small loop of area $A$ carrying current $I$ at frequency $f$ scales as $f^2 A I$. A capacitor 10 mm away has a loop area on the order of 10× that of one 1 mm away, giving roughly +20 dB in the radiated field at the same current. The larger loop also couples magnetic flux into nearby traces, so noise in the supply path appears in signals.

## The floor: package and die

The IC's own supply path (bond wire or lead frame, die pad, on-die routing) adds inductance and on-die capacitance. Typical numbers (vendor- and package-dependent): QFP leads 3–8 nH, QFN 1–2 nH, BGA balls 0.3–1 nH, flip-chip bumps less. If the package alone has 2 nH, shaving the PCB loop from 0.8 nH to 0.5 nH moves the total from 2.8 to 2.5 nH, a modest 10 % gain; going from 8 nH to 1 nH on the PCB side is a 3× gain. So the first priority is cutting the long connections and big vias, and the last mm only matters for the fastest, lowest-package-inductance parts.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Package inductances and the "electrically short" criterion ($t_r/6$ to $t_r/10$) are rules of thumb that vary by source and package. Use vendor models or measure where it matters.

## Practical consequences for layout

- If a chip has several supply pins, each gets its own small capacitor within a few mm ([[q0503]]).
- The ground connection counts as much as the supply connection; if the ground via is far away, the loop is big ([[q0510]]).
- Capacitors that must be far (bulk, mid-value) are for slower events, where a few nH doesn't matter. At 100 kHz, 10 nH is 6 mΩ.
- When the chip is too small or crowded, place the capacitor on the other side beneath it, accept two via lengths, and choose a smaller package (0201) to reduce ESL.
:::

::: applications
- **BGA processors and FPGAs**: capacitors on the back directly below the power balls.
- **Small MCU packages (QFN/QFP)**: 100 nF capacitor placed against each VDD/VSS pair.
- **RF modules and PLLs**: tiny capacitors within 1–2 mm of the supply pin.
- **Switching-regulator ICs**: input capacitor within a few mm of VIN and PGND.
- **Memory (DDR) interfaces**: per-pin decoupling placed beneath or immediately beside the device.
:::

::: assumptions
- Fast-edge digital or mixed-signal loads (nanosecond-scale steps); for slow loads distance matters less.
- Partial-inductance figures 0.3–1 nH/mm are typical for unreferenced to well-referenced traces; real values depend on geometry.
- The 50 mV budget and 50 mA/ns step are illustrative. Use your device's real $\Delta I$, $t_r$ and tolerance.
- FR-4 propagation of roughly 6–7 ps/mm.
- Another valid answer: "so the capacitor's impedance at the frequencies of interest is below the target impedance"; it is the same statement in the frequency domain.
:::

::: followups
### How close is close enough?
Close enough that the total loop inductance fits the budget $L_{max}=\Delta V\,t_r/\Delta I$. For a 1 A / 10 ns core step and 30 mV that's 0.3 nH, essentially the capacitor sitting under the ball. For a 20 mA / 2 ns MCU with 100 mV it's 10 nH, a few mm of trace is acceptable.

### Does a bigger capacitor let you place it farther away?
Only for slow events. For nanosecond events the limit is the inductance, which doesn't depend on capacitance. A larger capacitor helps with microsecond-scale demands and can be farther, which is the basis of the hierarchy.

### Where does the PDN resonate if the loop is 10 nH and the die has 10 nF?
$f_{ar}=1/(2\pi\sqrt{10\text{ nH}\times10\text{ nF}})=15.9$ MHz, with a peak around $L/(RC_d)\approx 10\ \Omega$ for 0.1 Ω of loop resistance. At 1 nH it moves to 50 MHz and about 1 Ω. Simple model; real dies are better damped.

### Why does ground via placement matter as much as the power connection?
Because the current loop includes both. A short power connection with a ground via 5 mm away still has a big loop. Place the ground via right beside the power via/pad so opposite currents are close and their fields cancel.

### Scenario: you can't place the capacitor within 5 mm because of a heatsink keep-out. What do you do?
Move it to the opposite side of the board directly under the pin, use smaller 0201/0402 capacitors with vias at the pads, add more capacitors in parallel to reduce $L$ (N capacitors give ~$L/N$), and verify the PDN impedance or measure the supply at the pin. If it still fails, reduce $di/dt$ with slower edges or add on-package capacitance.
:::

::: interpretations
"Source package" most likely means the IC package that *sources* (draws) the transient current, i.e. the chip being decoupled. "As close as possible" is about electrical distance (loop inductance), not just physical separation. For rules of thumb see [[q0503]]; for consequences if the capacitors are too far, [[q0508]].
