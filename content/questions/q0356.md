::: testing
Whether you understand arcing as a physical process (gas breaking down into plasma between conductors) and can name the two ways it happens in motor and switching circuits: **dielectric breakdown** of a gap by high voltage, and a **sustained arc when you interrupt an inductive current** ($v = L\,di/dt$ drives the gap voltage up until the air conducts). Beyond the definition they want consequences (contact erosion, EMI, brush wear, fire/ignition hazard) and the standard cures (flyback diode, snubber, varistor/TVS, solid-state switching, DC-rated contacts, magnetic blow-out).
:::

::: why
Arcing is why relays weld shut, why brushed motors wear out and jam radios, why DC contacts have lower voltage ratings than AC ones, and why a 12 V circuit with an inductor can produce hundreds of volts. It is also a safety topic: arcs ignite flammable atmospheres, and arc faults start fires. If you drive a motor, solenoid or relay with a mechanical contact, or you work with brushed motors, you need to be able to recognise and suppress arcing.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductive kick**: interrupting inductor current forces a large voltage, $v = L\,di/dt$. See [[q0340]].
- **Dielectric breakdown (Paschen)**: air breaks down at a voltage that depends on gap × pressure.
- **Plasma and arc voltage**: once ionised, an arc sustains with a low voltage (tens of volts) and any current above a minimum.
- **Contact materials and erosion**: arcs vaporise metal; material transfers between contacts.
- **Motor commutation**: brush-segment transitions interrupt coil current. See [[q0357]] and [[q0355]].
- **Inrush and switching**: closing contacts can also arc (contact bounce with capacitive/lamp loads), see [[q0343]].
:::

::: answer
"Arcing is an electrical discharge through a gas, usually air, between two conductors that are close but not touching. The gas becomes ionised into a plasma, which conducts, and it glows and gets very hot, thousands of degrees at the core.

It happens in two main ways. One is breakdown: if the electric field in a gap is high enough, roughly 3 million volts per metre in air for larger gaps, or around 300 volts minimum for the right gap and pressure, the gas breaks down. The other, and the one that matters for motors, is breaking an inductive current. If a switch contact or a brush opens while current flows through an inductor, the inductor won't let the current stop instantly: $v = L\,di/dt$ pushes the voltage across the opening gap up until the gap breaks down. The current then continues as an arc. Once it's lit, an arc is sustained by a fairly low voltage, tens of volts and a few hundred milliamps, and DC has no zero-crossing to extinguish it.

In a brushed motor, this happens at the brush-commutator contact all the time, which causes brush and commutator wear, electrical noise and sometimes visible sparks. In relays and switches, it erodes or welds the contacts.

To prevent it you give the inductor current somewhere else to go: a flyback diode across the load, an RC snubber across the contact, a varistor or TVS, or you use a solid-state switch. For motors, you add capacitors across the terminals and use the right brush material."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** An arc is a self-sustaining gas discharge with a low-voltage, high-current plasma column, as opposed to a glow discharge (high voltage, low current) or a spark (brief breakdown).

**Initiation.**
1. *Breakdown of a gap.* In uniform field and air at 1 atm, breakdown field is about 3 MV/m (3 kV/mm) for gaps of millimetres and larger. For small $pd$ the Paschen curve applies: minimum breakdown voltage about 300–330 V (at $pd \approx 0.6$–$0.8$ Torr·cm in air). A $pd$ of 0.76 Torr·cm is a 10 µm gap at atmospheric pressure, where breakdown needs ~300 V. Below ~5–10 µm other mechanisms (field emission) lower the needed voltage.
2. *Interruption of inductive current.* Contacts opening with current $I$ in inductance $L$: the gap's capacitance $C$ charges at $dv/dt = I/C$ until it reaches the gap's breakdown voltage $V_{bd}(g)$, with a peak $\sim I\sqrt{L/C}$ if nothing breaks down first. As the gap widens the breakdown voltage rises, so the arc forms and persists as long as the circuit supplies energy.
3. *Contact make.* Bounce and pre-strike also arc at closing, particularly with capacitive or lamp (cold-filament) loads: high inrush, molten contact spots, welding.

**Sustaining.** A DC arc persists if the supply voltage and current exceed the **minimum arc voltage and current** of the contact material, roughly 10–20 V and 0.3–0.5 A for common metals (silver, copper, palladium). These values vary with material and conditions; they are order-of-magnitude figures. Because arc voltage rises with arc length, you can extinguish an arc by lengthening it (magnetic blow-out, arc chutes, splitter plates).

**Energy.** Inductor energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ goes into the arc: for 10 mH at 1 A it is 5 mJ per opening, which erodes contacts a little each time; over $10^5$ cycles, this adds up to failure.

**DC vs AC.** AC arcs self-extinguish at the next zero crossing (every 8.3 ms at 60 Hz), DC arcs do not. Hence switches and relays carry a lower DC voltage rating than AC rating.

**Consequences.** Contact erosion and welding, brush/commutator wear ([[q0360]]), EMI ([[q0355]]), ozone and nitrogen oxide formation (corrosion), ignition of flammable vapours, and in high-power DC systems arc flash.

**Suppression.** Flyback diode (low voltage clamp, slow release), diode + zener, RC snubber across the contact or load, varistor/TVS, series resistance, solid-state switches (MOSFET/SSR) with a clamp, contacts rated for DC (wider gap, magnetic blow-out), hermetic or gas-filled contacts, and for motors, terminal capacitors.
:::

::: long
"Arcing is an electrical discharge through a gas, normally air, between two conductors that are close together. The gas gets ionised into a plasma that conducts, and it is very hot and bright.

There are two routes to an arc. The first is simple dielectric breakdown: if the electric field is high enough, around 3 megavolts per metre in air for gaps of a millimetre or more, the air ionises. For very small gaps the voltage needed follows the Paschen curve, which has a minimum around 300 volts for air at atmospheric pressure, around a 10 micrometre gap.

The second route is the important one for motors and relays: interrupting an inductive current. When a switch or brush opens while current flows through an inductor, the inductor demands the current continue. $v = L\,di/dt$ becomes a big number, so the voltage across the opening contacts rises until the gap breaks down, and the current carries on through an arc. A 10 millihenry coil at 1 amp stores 5 millijoules, and that all goes into the arc. Once it's established, an arc can be sustained at only 10 to 20 volts and a few hundred milliamps, depending on the material, which is why even a 12 volt or 24 volt DC circuit can arc, and why DC arcs are worse than AC ones: there is no zero crossing to extinguish them.

The consequences: contact erosion and welding in relays and switches, brush and commutator wear in motors, broadband EMI, and ignition hazards in flammable atmospheres.

For prevention, I'd give the inductor an alternative path: a flyback diode across the coil, an RC snubber across the contacts, or a varistor or TVS. Or I'd avoid mechanical contacts, using a MOSFET or solid-state relay with a clamp. For brushed motors, put capacitors on the terminals and pick the right brush grade. And use contacts rated for DC at the voltage and current you have, with magnetic blow-out for high power."
:::

::: terms
- **Arc**: self-sustaining, low-voltage, high-current plasma discharge through a gas.
- **Spark**: a brief breakdown discharge that does not necessarily sustain.
- **Dielectric breakdown**: failure of an insulating gap under high field, leading to conduction.
- **Paschen curve**: breakdown voltage vs $pd$ (pressure × gap distance).
- **Minimum arc voltage/current**: lowest voltage and current at which an arc can be sustained for a given contact metal.
- **Inductive kick (flyback)**: voltage generated by an inductor when its current is interrupted. See [[q0340]].
- **Snubber**: RC network absorbing the transient.
- **Contact erosion/welding**: material loss or fusion from arc heating.
- **Magnetic blow-out**: a magnet that deflects and stretches an arc to extinguish it.
- **Arc flash**: very high-energy arc event in power systems.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Arcing needs thousands of volts."** An inductive circuit at 12 V can arc: the kick provides the voltage, and the sustained arc needs only ~10–20 V and a few hundred mA.
- **Thinking AC and DC arcs behave the same.** DC lacks a zero crossing, so it sustains; switch DC ratings are lower than AC.
- **Calling every spark an arc.** A brief breakdown spark differs from a sustained arc, though both come from ionisation.
- **Putting the clamp across the wrong element.** A flyback diode belongs across the inductive load, not across the contact. Across the contact it would short the supply or do nothing for the kick; across the contact use an RC snubber or varistor.
- **Putting a snubber capacitor with no series resistor across contacts.** At closing it discharges through the contacts (inrush), which welds them.
- **Assuming the arc is harmless because it is small.** Repeated micro-arcs erode brushes, contacts and commutators, and emit broadband noise.
:::

::: deep
## What an arc is

A gas is normally an insulator because it has almost no free charge. If enough energy enters a gap, free electrons accelerate, collide with gas atoms and knock out more electrons (an avalanche). The gas becomes a **plasma** with ions and electrons that conducts. Three regimes appear as you increase current:

1. **Townsend/dark discharge** (tiny currents).
2. **Glow discharge**: hundreds of volts, mA of current, a diffuse glow (neon lamps).
3. **Arc**: tens of volts, amps, a very hot, bright, concentrated channel. At the electrodes there is a tiny, intensely hot "cathode spot" that emits electrons by thermionic and field emission and vaporises metal.

The transition happens when the electrodes themselves become hot enough to supply the current. This is why the arc can then run at low voltage (the **arc voltage** is mostly the near-electrode fall of ~10–15 V plus a column drop of a few V/mm). A circuit with a supply under ~10–15 V can't sustain an arc in air, whereas 24 V, 48 V or more can. (These figures are approximate and material-dependent.)

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Minimum arc voltage and current depend on the electrode material, surface condition, gap and atmosphere. Typical quoted values for relay metals are around 10–20 V and 0.3–0.5 A; always check the contact vendor's data for the load type (resistive, inductive, lamp).

## Breakdown: the Paschen curve

For a uniform gap in air, breakdown voltage depends on the product $pd$ of pressure and distance. A classic fit is
$$V_{bd} = \frac{B\,pd}{\ln(A\,pd) - \ln\!\big(\ln(1 + 1/\gamma)\big)},$$
with $A \approx 15$ (cm·Torr)$^{-1}$, $B \approx 365$ V/(cm·Torr) and secondary emission coefficient $\gamma\approx0.01$ for air. The curve has a minimum of roughly 300 V at $pd \approx 0.8$ Torr·cm.

The take-away: when contacts open, the gap goes through every $pd$. At first it's tiny, so even modest voltage (few hundred V) can break it down. That is why a 12 V relay circuit with a kick of a few hundred volts still arcs on opening.

## Interrupting an inductor

Consider a contact opening in series with a load of inductance $L$ and current $I$ (the dominant arcing mechanism for motors and solenoids).

1. Before opening: $I$ flows through the closed contact, with a negligible drop.
2. As the contacts separate, the current must continue. The inductor's voltage rises to whatever is needed: the gap capacitance $C$ (~ pF to tens of pF) charges at $dv/dt = I/C$. For $I = 1$ A and $C = 10$ pF that is $10^{11}$ V/s, 100 V per nanosecond.
3. The gap breakdown voltage at that instant (from the Paschen curve for the gap) is reached within nanoseconds; then the gap conducts and the voltage collapses to the arc voltage (~15–20 V).
4. The current continues through the arc, decaying at $di/dt = (V_{supply} - V_{arc})/L$ plus resistive terms. If $V_{supply} < V_{arc}$ the current decays and the arc extinguishes, with all the stored energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ dissipated in the arc.
5. If the supply (plus the inductor's kick) can sustain the arc, it persists until the contacts are far enough apart that the arc voltage exceeds what the circuit provides.

Energy example: $L = 10$ mH, $I = 1$ A: $E = 5$ mJ per opening. If current falls with a 12 V supply and 15 V arc: $di/dt = (12 - 15)/0.01 = -300$ A/s, so the arc lasts at most $1/300$ s $\approx 3.3$ ms (a resistive load in the loop shortens it). Over $10^5$ operations that is 500 J of arc energy in the contact metal.

## Arcing in brushed motors

At the brush-commutator interface there are always two types of arcing:

- **Commutation arcing**: as a brush leaves a segment, the coil current must reverse; the reactance voltage $L_c\,di/dt$ can drive a spark if the commutation isn't completed before the brush leaves (poor commutation, mis-set neutral, high current).
- **Contact bounce and dry-circuit arcing**: the brush bounces on a rough commutator and breaks and remakes contact.

The result is visible sparks at the brush edge, a blackened or pitted commutator, brush wear and noise ([[q0355]]), and ozone that attacks rubber and plastics. Large motors are designed to limit it (interpoles and compensating windings, brush grade, brush position), small motors use capacitors across terminals.

## Contacts and relays

Datasheets list ratings by load type: "10 A 250 V AC resistive; 10 A 30 V DC resistive; 3 A 30 V DC inductive (L/R = 7 ms)". Observe that the DC rating is far lower than AC and inductive loads are derated. Why: DC arcs don't self-extinguish; contacts need larger gaps and blow-out magnets for high-voltage DC. For example, contactors for 400 V+ DC (EVs, battery systems) use magnetic blow-out or hydrogen-filled sealed chambers.

- **Flyback diode** across the load: contact sees $V_{supply} + 0.7$ V; current decays with time constant $L/R$ (slow release, e.g. 10 mH, 24 Ω gives 0.42 ms, but with a relay it delays drop-out).
- **Diode + zener** in series across the load: faster decay, at higher clamp voltage (e.g. 12 V supply + 24 V zener).
- **TVS or varistor** across the load or contacts: clamps both polarities.
- **RC snubber** across the contacts (or the load): absorbs energy, damps ringing, typical $R$ = 10–100 Ω and $C$ = 10–100 nF. The resistor limits the capacitor discharge current at closing.
- **Solid-state switching** with a clamp: no contacts, no arcs, but the MOSFET sees the clamp voltage and avalanche energy.
- **Contact choice**: silver-nickel, silver–cadmium-oxide alternatives (cadmium is being phased out), tungsten for heavy arcing; gold flash for dry circuits.
- **Wider gap and magnetic blow-out** for high-power DC.

## Safety notes

Arcs ignite flammable gases and dust, so motors in hazardous locations use sealed or explosion-proof construction or brushless motors. Arc faults in wiring (loose connection, damaged insulation) cause fires, which is why arc-fault circuit interrupters exist. Battery systems with large DC currents can produce arc flash with severe injury risk. Treat any switching of a DC inductive load with respect.
:::

::: applications
- **Relay and contactor design**: contact materials and DC/AC ratings, snubbers across relay coils and contacts.
- **Brushed motors (power tools, automotive)**: commutation sparks; brush grade and suppression capacitors.
- **Automotive**: ignition coils (deliberate arcing at the spark plug), contactors in EV battery packs with magnetic blow-out.
- **Welding and plasma cutting**: deliberate use of a sustained arc.
- **Switch-mode supplies**: MOSFET avalanche and snubbers manage the equivalent of arcing in a semiconductor, so no contact erosion.
- **Safety devices**: arc-fault circuit interrupters and fuses with arc-quenching (sand-filled) bodies.
- **Hot-plugging connectors**: pre-charge and staggered pins to avoid arcing on live battery connections.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Air at atmospheric pressure** and clean electrodes. Pressure, humidity, contamination, altitude and gas type (hydrogen, SF6, vacuum) change breakdown and arc behaviour considerably.
- **Paschen fit** is for uniform fields and gaps larger than a few micrometres; actual contacts have non-uniform fields and surface roughness.
- **Minimum arc voltage/current** values are indicative and material dependent.
- **Lumped inductive load** with constant $L$; real loads have iron saturation and mutual coupling.
- **DC supplies** are assumed ideal. Real supplies have impedance that helps or hurts.
- Alternative framings: "arcing" in power systems refers to arc faults and arc flash, a different regime, but the same plasma physics; the answer here is for switching of inductive loads and brushes.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a 12 V relay circuit produce hundreds of volts when it opens?
The relay coil is an inductor. Opening the switch forces the coil current to continue, so $v = L\,di/dt$ rises until something conducts: the parasitic capacitance charges, then the switch or driver breaks down. A 12 V coil of 100 mH at 0.1 A can spike hundreds of volts with no clamp. A flyback diode across the coil limits this to about 12.7 V.

### Why are DC switch ratings lower than AC?
AC current passes through zero 100/120 times per second, which extinguishes the arc at each zero crossing. DC never does, so an arc, once established, persists until the contacts are far enough apart that arc voltage exceeds the supply. DC-rated contacts need larger gaps, blow-out magnets, or arc chutes.

### Why does a diode across a *contact* not solve the problem?
A diode must be across the *inductive load*, oriented to conduct the inductor current when the contact opens. Across the contact it would conduct in normal operation or be reverse biased, doing nothing for the kick (or shorting the supply). The protective element across a contact is an RC snubber or varistor.

### How do you estimate how bad the spike is?
The energy is $\tfrac12LI^2$, the voltage $I\sqrt{L/C}$ if a capacitance $C$ absorbs it. For $L = 10$ mH, $I = 1$ A and a 100 pF load: $\sqrt{10^{-2}/10^{-10}} = 10$ kV (the gap would break down long before). With a 100 nF snubber it is only 316 V and the energy is stored in the capacitor instead of the arc. In practice measure with a scope and a high-voltage probe.

### How would you tell if a brushed motor is arcing too much?
Visible sparks at the brushes in the dark, burnt or blackened commutator segments, brush dust, a smell of ozone, radio interference, and rapid brush wear. Measuring the noise on the supply with a scope shows spiky bursts at the commutation rate. Causes include worn or wrong-grade brushes, dirty or out-of-round commutator, high mica, overload or misplaced brushes ([[q0359]], [[q0360]]).

### Is arcing ever useful?
Yes: arc welding, plasma cutting, arc lamps, spark plugs, arc furnaces, and sparkgap and ESD protection devices (gas discharge tubes). The same physics. In signal and power electronics we usually want to avoid it.
:::

::: interpretations
"What is arcing?" in the brushed DC motor section most likely refers to **brush arcing/sparking at the commutator** and to contact arcing when switching inductive loads. Power-system arc flash and arc-fault circuit interrupters are further meanings you can mention in passing.
