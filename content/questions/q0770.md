::: basics
### In plain words
SPI has no official speed grades. Each chip has its own maximum clock speed, and the board and the master chip can lower it further. Typical clocks are a few MHz for simple sensors, 10 to 50 MHz for displays, SD cards and flash, and 100 MHz or more for fast flash. The usual limit is the time budget for data coming back.

### Picture it
Imagine two people playing catch to a drum beat. One throws on each beat, and the other must catch before the next beat. The gap between the throw and the catch has to cover three things: the thrower's reaction time, the ball's flight, and the catcher getting set. Beat faster and that gap shrinks until the catch fails.

### Words you need
- **Clock frequency**: ticks per second. 10 MHz means 10 million ticks per second.
- **Datasheet maximum**: the highest clock the maker guarantees. Reads are sometimes limited lower than writes.
- **Clock-to-output delay**: how long a slave takes, after a clock edge, to put its next bit on the wire.
- **Setup time**: how long the master needs the bit to be steady before it samples it.
- **Level translator**: a chip that links two supply voltages. It adds a few nanoseconds of delay. See [[q0769]].

### The simplest example
In the common clock mode, the slave sends a new bit on one clock edge, and the master reads it on the next edge, half a period later. Say the slave takes 12 ns to respond, the board adds 4 ns for the round trip, and the master needs 5 ns of setup. That is 21 ns, so half a period must be at least 21 ns. The period is then 42 ns, or about 24 MHz. Two level translators adding 10 ns push it to about 16 MHz. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page gives typical speeds by application, signal-quality limits, and how SPI can go faster.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can give typical **numbers** and, more importantly, explain **what limits** SPI's clock, since there is no standard speed grade. The interviewer is listening for:

1. Typical ranges: about 1 to 10 MHz for sensors and ADCs, 10 to 50 MHz for displays, SD cards and flash, and 100+ MHz for fast flash (including dual/quad modes).
2. The limiters: the slave's datasheet maximum, the master's peripheral clock divider, the MISO round-trip timing budget, and signal integrity on the board.
3. A worked timing-budget calculation.
4. How SPI compares to I2C in throughput, and what quad SPI changes.
:::

::: why
"How fast can I run this SPI?" is a daily question, and the answer is rarely the datasheet maximum. A bus that works at 4 MHz and fails at 20 MHz usually fails on the MISO path or on ringing, not on the protocol. Being able to compute the limit on paper, and to say why SPI is an order of magnitude faster than I2C ([[q0739]]), is exactly the skill being tested.
:::

::: concepts
- **No speed grades**: each device specifies its own maximum SCLK. See [[q0764]].
- **MISO timing budget**: clock-to-data-valid delay plus propagation and setup must fit in a half period. See [[q0766]].
- **Push-pull edges**: fast, symmetric, no pull-up RC. See [[q0761]].
- **Signal integrity**: ringing and reflections at tens of MHz. See [[q0767]].
- **Level translators**: add delay to the budget. See [[q0769]].
- **I2C comparison**: [[q0763]] and [[q0739]].

::: answer
"SPI has no standard speed grades, so it depends on the devices, but typical numbers are a few MHz for simple sensors and ADCs, 10 to 50 MHz for things like SD cards, displays and flash, and up to 100 MHz or more for fast flash, especially in dual or quad mode, where several data lines carry bits at once. MCU SPI peripherals are usually limited to a fraction of their peripheral clock, often half of it, so tens of MHz on typical parts.

What actually limits it: first the slave's datasheet maximum, which is often lower for reads than for writes. Second, the MISO timing. After the clock edge, the slave takes some time to output the bit, it has to travel back to the master, and the master needs setup time before it samples. In mode 0 all of that has to fit in half a clock period. So the maximum clock is roughly one over twice the sum of the slave's clock-to-output delay, the propagation delay out and back, and the master's setup. For example 12 ns plus 4 ns plus 5 ns gives about 24 MHz, and a pair of level translators costing 10 ns drops that to about 16 MHz. Third, signal integrity: at tens of MHz edges are a few nanoseconds, so traces need to be short and I'd use series resistors.

In throughput terms, SPI sends one bit per clock with almost no overhead, so 10 MHz is 1.25 MB/s, about ten times what I2C does at 1 MHz, and quad SPI at 100 MHz is about 50 MB/s."
:::

::: technical
**Typical ranges (device-dependent).**

| Application | Typical SCLK |
|---|---|
| Sensors, IMUs, low-speed ADCs/DACs | 1 to 10 MHz |
| SD card (SPI mode) | up to 25 MHz default; 50 MHz in high-speed (card-dependent) |
| SPI NOR flash (single I/O) | 20 to 133 MHz |
| Dual/Quad/Octal SPI flash | often 100 to 200+ MHz, with 2/4/8 data bits per clock |
| TFT/OLED displays | 10 to 80 MHz |
| MCU SPI peripheral | often $f_{periph}/2$ at best: tens of MHz |

**Throughput.** One bit per clock: byte time $=8/f_{SCLK}$. 10 MHz: 0.8 µs per byte, 1.25 MB/s. Quad SPI at 100 MHz moves 4 bits per clock: 50 MB/s. Overhead is the command, address and any dummy cycles, e.g. a 256-byte flash read at 50 MHz is $(8+24+256\times8)/50\text{ MHz}=41.6$ µs.

**Limit 1: device maximum.** From the datasheet, often separate maxima for read and write, and sometimes dependent on $V_{DD}$.

**Limit 2: MISO timing budget (mode 0).** MISO changes after the *falling* edge of SCLK (the slave shifts out) and is sampled by the master at the next rising edge, half a period later. So

$$\frac{1}{2f_{SCLK}}\ge t_V + t_{prop,SCLK} + t_{prop,MISO} + t_{su,master}$$

$$f_{max}\approx\frac{1}{2\,(t_V+t_{prop}+t_{su})}$$

where $t_V$ is the slave's clock-to-output valid delay, $t_{prop}$ the round-trip propagation (traces, translators), and $t_{su}$ the master's input setup. Example: $t_V=12$ ns, $t_{prop}=4$ ns, $t_{su}=5$ ns gives $f_{max}\approx23.8$ MHz; adding 10 ns for two translators gives 16.1 MHz. Writes (MOSI) have a more relaxed path since master and slave see the same edge; many masters can also sample MISO late (a "delay" or a mode where data is read on the next edge) to extend this.

**Limit 3: master capability.** SCLK = $f_{periph}/N$ with $N\ge2$ in many MCUs; DMA and firmware overhead may limit sustained throughput below the raw clock.

**Limit 4: signal integrity.** Edge rates of 1 to 5 ns give a knee frequency near $0.5/t_r$ (100 to 500 MHz), so ringing and reflections matter above about 20 MHz: short traces, a ground plane, series damping, no long stubs.

**Limit 5: daisy-chain/multi-drop loading** on SCLK ([[q0768]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The speeds in the table are typical families, not guarantees. SD card, flash and display speeds depend on the specific part and mode; check the datasheet.
:::

::: long
"SPI doesn't have defined speed grades the way I2C does, so the clock rate is whatever the slowest limit allows. Typical numbers: simple sensors and ADCs run at 1 to 10 MHz, SD cards, displays and flash at 10 to 50 MHz, and fast SPI flash at 100 MHz or beyond, particularly in dual, quad or octal modes where two, four or eight data lines carry bits on each clock. Throughput is easy: one bit per clock, so 10 MHz gives 1.25 megabytes per second, about ten times what I2C manages at 1 MHz, with almost no framing overhead.

What actually sets the limit is a handful of things. First, the slave's datasheet maximum, which can be lower for reads than writes. Second, the master: SCLK comes from a divider off the peripheral clock, often at best half of it. Third, signal integrity: SPI edges are fast, a few nanoseconds, so at tens of MHz I need short traces, a ground plane, and often a 22 to 47 ohm series resistor at the source.

The one I like to calculate is the MISO timing budget, because it's the one people forget. In mode 0 the slave drives a new bit after the falling edge and the master samples it at the next rising edge, which is half a period later. In that half period the slave needs its clock-to-output time, the clock has to travel to the slave and the data back, and the master needs setup time. Add those up, say 12 ns for the slave, 4 ns for the board, 5 ns of setup: 21 ns. Half a period has to be at least 21 ns, so the maximum clock is one over 42 ns, about 24 MHz. If I add two level translators at 5 ns each, that becomes 31 ns and about 16 MHz. Writes on MOSI don't have this round trip, so a device can often be written faster than read.

So in practice I start at the datasheet maximum, check the MISO budget, check the board, and back off until the signal looks clean on a scope."
:::

::: terms
- **SCLK frequency**: bit rate, one bit per clock.
- **$t_V$ (clock to output valid)**: delay from clock edge to valid MISO.
- **Setup time ($t_{su}$)**: time data must be stable before the sampling edge.
- **Half-period budget**: in mode 0, the MISO path has half a clock period.
- **Dual/Quad/Octal SPI**: 2, 4 or 8 data lines, multiplying bits per clock.
- **Dummy cycles**: extra clocks in fast flash reads to let the device fetch data.
- **Knee frequency**: $\approx0.35/t_r$, the bandwidth needed for an edge.
- **Peripheral clock divider**: sets SCLK from the MCU's SPI clock.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "SPI is fast, tens of MHz".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives ranges by application and notes that there are no speed grades.
- Works the MISO half-period budget with numbers and shows the effect of translators or long traces.
- Notes read-versus-write asymmetry and MCU divider limits.
- Compares throughput to I2C and mentions quad/octal SPI and dummy cycles.
- Mentions signal integrity (edge rate, knee frequency, series termination) as a practical limit.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Setting the clock to the datasheet maximum without checking the board.** The path delay and layout may not support it.
- **Using the write maximum for reads.** Read maxima are often lower.
- **Forgetting translators' delay** in the MISO budget.
- **Confusing clock rate with throughput** for flash: command, address and dummy cycles take time.
- **Assuming the MCU can do $f_{periph}$.** The divider often limits to half or a quarter.
- **Ignoring ringing.** A 50 MHz clock with a 1 ns edge and unterminated stub can double-clock.
:::

::: deep
## SPI speeds in context

I2C is bounded by $R_pC_b$: its rising edge is a resistor charging a capacitor ([[q0763]]). SPI's drivers are push-pull, so edges are driven both ways in nanoseconds, and the clock is limited by timing budgets rather than the RC edge. That's why SPI runs 10 to 100 times faster.

| Bus | Typical clock | Peak payload |
|---|---|---|
| I2C Fast mode | 400 kHz | ≈44 kB/s |
| I2C Fm+ | 1 MHz | ≈111 kB/s |
| SPI (sensor) | 10 MHz | 1.25 MB/s |
| SPI (flash/display) | 50 MHz | 6.25 MB/s |
| Quad SPI | 100 MHz × 4 bits | 50 MB/s |

## Where the half-period goes (mode 0, reading MISO)

In mode 0, MOSI and MISO change on SCLK's falling edge and are sampled on the rising edge. For the master reading MISO:

1. The master's falling edge leaves the master and travels to the slave ($t_{prop}$).
2. The slave shifts out the next bit, which becomes valid $t_V$ after that edge.
3. The bit travels back ($t_{prop}$) and must be stable $t_{su}$ before the master's *next rising edge*, half a period after the falling edge.

Summing the contributions:

$$\frac{T}{2}=\frac{1}{2f}\ \ge\ t_V + t_{SCLK,prop}+t_{MISO,prop}+t_{su}$$

For board delays, $t_{prop}\approx6.7$ ps/mm on FR-4 microstrip (about 0.17 ns per inch), so even 15 cm each way is only about 1 ns. The delay that hurts is usually the slave's $t_V$ and any translators or isolators. Digital isolators, for example, can add 10 to 25 ns each way, which is why isolated SPI is often limited to a few MHz.

## Worked examples

**Example A: IMU at 3.3 V.** Datasheet: SCLK max 10 MHz, $t_V$ = 40 ns max. Budget: $1/(2\times(40+5+4))=10.2$ MHz. So 10 MHz is right at the edge; running at 8 MHz gives margin: half-period 62.5 ns versus 49 ns needed.

**Example B: fast NOR flash.** Datasheet: 133 MHz for fast read with dummy cycles, $t_V$ about 6 ns, but only 50 MHz for the basic read command (no dummy cycles). Two separate limits are at work. The 50 MHz cap on the basic read is about internal access time: with no dummy cycles the first data bit must be ready right after the last address bit, and dummy cycles buy the array that time. The per-bit MISO budget is the other limit, and it is tight even at 50 MHz: half period 10 ns versus $6+5+2=13$ ns, a fail by the simple mode-0 budget. At 133 MHz the half period (3.75 ns) is shorter than $t_V$ alone, so masters that run flash this fast sample MISO late, with a programmable sample delay or by sampling on the following edge, which gives nearly a full period. Consequence: the maximum clock depends on the command type and on how the master samples, not just on the chip.

**Example C: with level translators.** The same IMU through two translators of 5 ns each way: $40+5+4+10=59$ ns, half-period $\ge59$ ns, $f_{max}=8.5$ MHz.

## Beyond 1 bit per clock

Single SPI moves 1 bit per clock. Memory devices extend it:

| Mode | Data lines | Bits per clock |
|---|---|---|
| Standard | MOSI, MISO | 1 each way (full-duplex) |
| Dual | IO0, IO1 | 2 (half-duplex) |
| Quad | IO0 to IO3 | 4 |
| Octal | IO0 to IO7 | 8 (often DDR: 16 per clock cycle) |

For a 256-byte read from quad SPI flash at 100 MHz: command (8 clocks on one line) + 24-bit address (6 clocks on four lines) + 8 dummy clocks + data ($256\times8/4=512$ clocks) = 534 clocks = 5.3 µs, versus 41.6 µs for single SPI at 50 MHz. That's the numbers behind memory-mapped quad SPI flash on microcontrollers.

## Signal integrity at speed

Edge rates of 1 to 5 ns put significant energy up to 100 to 500 MHz (the knee frequency, $0.5/t_r$), so a 15 cm trace is not necessarily electrically short. Practical rules: keep SCLK short and direct, use a ground plane under it, use source series resistors (22 to 47 Ω, see [[q0767]]), avoid star topologies and long stubs on SCLK, and keep SCLK away from MISO to limit crosstalk. At 50+ MHz consider controlled impedance and a length-matched SCLK/MOSI/MISO group.

## How to choose a speed in practice

1. Start with the lowest of: slave max (read), master max, system need.
2. Compute the MISO half-period budget with real delays.
3. Run at that speed with a margin of 20 to 30% and check the waveforms on a scope.
4. Increase until reads fail, then back off.
5. For high-throughput needs, use dual/quad SPI or DMA instead of pushing the clock.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Give three tiers of numbers, then do the MISO budget on the whiteboard. A one-line formula with real delays is what separates "SPI is fast" from an engineering answer.
:::

- **SPI NOR flash with memory-mapped quad SPI** for code execution on MCUs.
- **TFT displays** where pixel data streams at 20 to 80 MHz.
- **High-speed ADCs and DACs** where SCLK sets sample rate and resolution.
- **SD/microSD cards** in SPI mode at 25 MHz.
- **IMU and pressure sensors** sampled at kHz rates over 1 to 10 MHz SPI.

::: assumptions
- **Mode 0, push-pull, single master.** Other modes shift where the half-period budget applies.
- **Typical delays** (a few ns for boards and translators); isolators and long cables add much more.
- **MCU can generate the clock**: real peripherals have divider steps.
- **Device maxima vary** with supply voltage, temperature and command type.

If the interviewer states a specific device, use its datasheet's $f_{SCK}$, $t_V$ and setup numbers in the same formula.

::: followups
### Why is SPI faster than I2C?
SPI uses push-pull drivers, so both edges are actively driven in nanoseconds, with no pull-up RC to wait for; it has a dedicated clock and no address/ACK overhead per byte. I2C is limited by $R_pC_b$ rise times to 100 kHz to 1 MHz, with nine clocks per byte.

### What limits SPI speed in a real design?
The slave's maximum, the master's divider, the MISO round-trip timing (slave $t_V$ plus propagation plus setup within half a period), translators and isolators, and signal integrity (ringing, crosstalk). Usually the MISO budget and the board are the surprises.

### Why can you often write faster than you can read?
On writes, MOSI is launched and sampled by the same clock edge pair with one-way delay, whereas for reads the master must receive data that was launched after the slave saw the clock, adding the slave's $t_V$ and two propagation delays. Datasheets often list a higher maximum for write or for commands with dummy cycles.

### What is quad SPI and why does it help?
It uses four data lines (IO0 to IO3) so four bits move per clock instead of one, quadrupling throughput at the same frequency. Flash chips use it for fast reads and memory-mapped execution. Dummy cycles after the address give the device time to prepare data.

### Your SPI works at 5 MHz but fails at 20 MHz. What do you check?
First the slave's read maximum and $t_V$; then compute the half-period budget with translator/isolator delays; then look at the signals with a scope for ringing or double edges on SCLK; then try a series resistor, shorter traces, or a lower mode-compatible sampling option (a MISO delay setting).
:::
