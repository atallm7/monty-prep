::: basics
### In plain words
Any repeating wave can be built by adding sine waves. An ideal square wave has instant edges, and an instant jump takes sine waves of ever higher frequency, without end. So it has no highest frequency. A real square wave's edges take some time, so its high-frequency content fades quickly, and the edge speed sets where it stops mattering.

### Picture it
Imagine drawing a shape with marker pens. A rounded corner can be drawn with one thick marker. A perfectly sharp corner needs thinner and thinner pens, forever. The sine waves are the pens: the thickest draws the basic shape, finer ones add detail. Even a rounded real edge has a little finer detail left. It just stops mattering.

### Words you need
- **Square wave**: a signal that switches between two levels at a steady rate ([[q0671]]).
- **Harmonic**: a sine wave at a whole-number multiple of the square wave's frequency. A square wave uses the 1st, 3rd, 5th and so on.
- **Rise time**: how long an edge takes to go from 10% to 90% of its full height.
- **Highest significant frequency**: where the content becomes too weak to matter. It is a choice, not a fixed fact.
- **Rule of thumb**: about 0.35 divided by the rise time gives that frequency ([[q0676]]).

### The simplest example
Take a 100 MHz clock with 1 ns edges. The rule gives 0.35 / 1 ns = 350 MHz, so the important content stops around the 3rd harmonic (300 MHz) or the 5th (500 MHz). Harmonics beyond that are weak.

Now slow the clock to 10 MHz but keep the same 1 ns edges. The important content still reaches about 350 MHz, which is roughly the 35th harmonic. The clock rate changed tenfold. The reach did not.

That is the whole idea: the edge, not the clock rate, sets the highest frequency that matters. The rest of this page shows the math and the real-world limits.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand that an **ideal** square wave needs infinite bandwidth (instant edges, harmonics falling only as $1/n$ forever) and that a **real** one has a *practically* bounded spectrum set by its rise time. The subtle part is being honest about "highest component": a trapezoid still has mathematically infinite harmonics, but they fall off much faster beyond a knee near $1/(\pi t_r)$, and real physical systems low-pass the edge further. A strong answer says "ideal: infinite; real: effectively about $0.35/t_r$ to $0.5/t_r$ with rapidly vanishing content above that", and explains why.
:::

::: why
This is the question behind "what bandwidth do I need?" Choosing a scope, probe, cable, connector or filter, estimating EMI risk, deciding whether a trace is a transmission line, and setting a simulation's maximum frequency all depend on the highest frequency that actually carries energy. If you say "the clock frequency" you will under-design everything by a large factor; if you say "infinite" with no qualification you cannot size anything. The honest answer sits in between and is the foundation for [[q0676]] to [[q0678]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Fourier series of a square wave**: odd harmonics with amplitude $\propto 1/n$ ([[q0671]]).
- **Convergence and energy**: with $1/n$ amplitudes, power per harmonic goes as $1/n^2$, the sum converges, but the series never terminates.
- **Rise time $t_r$** sets how smooth the edge is; smoother means faster spectral decay ([[q0673]]).
- **Knee frequency**: roughly where a trapezoid's spectrum steepens from $-20$ to $-40$ dB/decade.
- **Physical bandwidth limits**: output impedance with load capacitance, package inductance, skin effect and dielectric loss all low-pass real edges.
- **Noise floor / measurement limit**: harmonics lost below the noise or emissions limit are "not there" in practice.
:::

::: answer
"For an ideal square wave the answer is: there isn't one. The edges are instantaneous, so the harmonics at 3f, 5f, 7f and so on keep going with amplitude one over n, forever. You'd need infinite bandwidth to reproduce it.

A real square wave has a finite rise time, and that changes the story. Mathematically a trapezoid still has infinitely many harmonics, but above a knee frequency of roughly one over pi times the rise time the amplitudes fall like one over n squared, so 40 dB per decade instead of 20. Add the fact that real drivers, traces and packages are low-pass, and the content above a few times that knee is negligible, usually well below the noise floor or the emissions limit.

So I'd say the highest significant frequency component of a real square wave is set by the edge, not the clock: roughly 0.35 to 0.5 divided by the rise time. A 100 MHz clock with a 1 ns edge has important content out to about 350 to 500 MHz, which is the 3rd to 5th harmonic. And a 10 MHz clock with a 100 ps edge has content out to several GHz, which is why a slow clock can still wreck your EMI or need a fast scope."
:::

::: technical
**Ideal.** $x(t) = \frac{4A}{\pi}\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac{\sin(n\omega_0t)}{n}$. No upper limit on $n$. The fraction of the total power in harmonics above $N$ is about $4/(\pi^2N)$, which falls only as $1/N$: truncating always leaves residual energy (9.9% of the power beyond the 3rd harmonic of a $\pm A$ wave, 2.0% beyond the 19th, 1.0% beyond the 39th). Truncation shows up as Gibbs ringing of about 9% of the step.

**Real (trapezoid, linear edges, rise time $t_r$ from 0 to 100%).** Harmonic $n$ has amplitude
$$A_n = \frac{2V}{n\pi}\left|\text{sinc}(nf_0t_r)\right|$$
For $f \gg 1/(\pi t_r)$ the envelope falls as $1/f^2$. Still infinitely many harmonics, but their sum is dominated by the first few. A truly smooth edge (Gaussian, or any infinitely differentiable edge) has a spectrum that falls faster than any power of $1/n$, so the series is effectively finite.

**Where the content ends in practice**
- Knee: $f_{knee}\approx 1/(\pi t_r)$ (linear 0-100% ramp) or about $0.5/t_{r,10\text{-}90}$ (a common conservative convention, [[q0676]]).
- Everything above is attenuated at $-40$ dB/dec; two decades above the knee the envelope is 80 dB below the flat level. A 1 ns edge on a 100 MHz clock has harmonics below $-50$ dB relative to the fundamental by about the 19th.
- A reasonable engineering definition of "highest frequency component" is the frequency where the envelope drops below the noise floor or the regulatory limit. That is a *system* choice.

**Example.** 100 MHz, 3.3 V, 50% duty, $t_r = 1$ ns (0-100%): amplitudes of odd harmonics are 2.07 V (100 MHz), 0.60 V (300 MHz), 0.27 V (500 MHz), 0.11 V (700 MHz), 0.026 V (900 MHz; near the null at 1/$t_r$ = 1 GHz). By the 9th harmonic (900 MHz) the amplitude is under 30 mV, 38 dB below the fundamental, and the envelope keeps falling at 40 dB/decade (with dips at the nulls).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> "Highest frequency component" has no single accepted threshold. Conventions (0.35/$t_r$, 0.5/$t_r$, $1/(\pi t_r)$, or "5th harmonic") differ by up to a factor of 2 to 3 because they answer slightly different questions (bandwidth to preserve the edge, vs. where emissions fall below a limit).
:::

::: short
"An ideal square wave has instant edges, so it contains harmonics out to infinity, falling only as one over n. A real one has a finite rise time. It still has infinitely many harmonics mathematically, but above a knee of about one over pi times the rise time they fall off at 40 dB per decade, and real drivers and traces low-pass it further. So the highest significant frequency is set by the edge, roughly 0.35 to 0.5 over the rise time, not by the clock frequency."
:::

::: long
"Ideal first. A perfect square wave has instantaneous edges. In the Fourier series that means odd harmonics at 3f, 5f, 7f, and so on, with amplitude one over n, forever. There's no highest component. In practice that means you'd need infinite bandwidth to reproduce it, and if you truncate the series you get Gibbs ringing of about nine percent at each edge.

A real square wave has a finite rise time, and the edge is a smooth ramp. A trapezoid's spectrum is the ideal one multiplied by a sinc of the rise time. For harmonics below about one over pi times the rise time, nothing changes: still one over n. Above that knee the amplitudes fall like one over n squared, so 40 dB per decade. Strictly there are still infinitely many harmonics, but they shrink very quickly, and real hardware adds more low-pass filtering: output impedance with load capacitance, package inductance, skin effect and dielectric loss on the trace.

So the practical answer is that the highest significant frequency is set by the edge, not by the clock rate. The rules of thumb are about 0.35 to 0.5 divided by the 10 to 90 percent rise time. For a 100 MHz clock with a 1 ns edge, that's 350 to 500 MHz, which is only the third to fifth harmonic, and the content is gone by about a gigahertz. But a 10 MHz clock with a 100 ps edge has content out to a few GHz, which is why it can still fail emissions and why you need a fast scope to see its real shape.

Practically, I'd define 'highest frequency' as where the envelope drops below the noise floor or the regulatory limit for the system I'm designing."
:::

::: terms
- **Ideal square wave**: instantaneous edges, infinite bandwidth, odd harmonics $\propto 1/n$.
- **Trapezoidal wave**: square wave with finite linear rise and fall times; the standard model of a real clock.
- **Rise time ($t_r$)**: time to go from 10% to 90% of the swing (or 0 to 100% for a linear ramp; state which).
- **Knee frequency**: where the spectral envelope changes from $-20$ to $-40$ dB/decade.
- **Bandwidth**: the frequency range a system passes; for a single pole, $0.35/t_r$.
- **Gibbs phenomenon**: about 9% overshoot caused by truncating the harmonic series.
- **Noise floor**: level below which spectral content cannot be distinguished or does not matter.
- **Band-limited**: containing no (or negligible) content above some frequency.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the ideal one has infinite frequency and the real one has a limit related to the rise time."

**An exceptional answer**:
- States clearly that the clock frequency is *not* the answer; the edge is.
- Admits the mathematical subtlety: a trapezoid still has infinite harmonics, but they fall at 40 dB/dec past the knee, and real smooth edges decay faster still.
- Gives the numbers: $0.35/t_r$ (bandwidth), $0.5/t_r$ (conservative knee), and works a concrete example (100 MHz, 1 ns).
- Notes the physical causes of the bound (driver and load RC, package L, skin effect, dielectric loss) and that "significant" depends on the noise floor or regulatory limit.
- Connects to consequences: scope and probe bandwidth, EMI, transmission-line criteria.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "the highest frequency is the clock frequency".** That is only the fundamental. A 100 MHz clock has content at several hundred MHz.
- **Saying "a real square wave has no harmonics above X".** A trapezoid has infinitely many; they are just small. Say "negligible above".
- **Mixing definitions of rise time.** 10-90%, 20-80% and 0-100% differ by factors of 1.25 or more. State the definition.
- **Assuming the scope shows the truth.** A scope with bandwidth $B$ shows a rise time of $\sqrt{t_r^2 + (0.35/B)^2}$; a slow scope hides the real edge.
- **Forgetting the source.** The driver's own output stage and the load capacitance often set $t_r$ more than the silicon's capability.
- **Treating the ideal as free.** Anything that approaches an ideal edge costs EMI, ringing and loss ([[q0677]]).
:::

::: deep
## The ideal case: why infinite

The ideal $\pm A$ square wave has Fourier coefficients $b_n = 4A/(n\pi)$ for odd $n$. Is there a largest $n$? No: every term is non-zero. Is the signal's energy finite? Yes: Parseval gives average power $A^2$, and the harmonic powers are $\tfrac12(4A/n\pi)^2 = 8A^2/(\pi^2n^2)$ which sum (over odd $n$) to $A^2$. The tail beyond the $N$th harmonic carries $\approx 4A^2/(\pi^2N)$. Cumulatively:

| Harmonics included (up to) | Fraction of total power |
|---|---|
| 1st | 81.1% |
| 3rd | 90.1% |
| 9th | 96.0% |
| 19th | 98.0% |
| 39th | 99.0% |

You can pass nearly all the *power* with a modest bandwidth, but reproducing the *shape* (steepness of the edge) needs far more. Edge steepness lives in the high harmonics, and each harmonic is a $1/n$ sliver of energy. This is why bandwidth requirements are set by the edge, not the power ([[q0676]]).

## Switching to a real edge

Model a real clock as a trapezoid with rise time $t_r$. A trapezoid is the ideal wave convolved with a rectangular smoothing kernel of width $t_r$. Convolution in time is multiplication in frequency, so every harmonic gets multiplied by $\text{sinc}(f t_r)$:
$$A_n = A_n^{ideal}\cdot\left|\frac{\sin(\pi n f_0t_r)}{\pi nf_0t_r}\right|$$
Below $f \approx 1/(\pi t_r)$ the sinc is near 1. Above it, the sinc falls like $1/(\pi ft_r)$, adding a second $-20$ dB/decade. Because the sinc oscillates, the envelope has nulls at $f = k/t_r$ (the first at $1/t_r$).

## Smoother edges decay faster

The trapezoid's edge has a corner at each end of the ramp (the slope jumps). A corner gives $1/n^2$ decay. An edge that is smooth (continuous slope, such as a raised cosine or the Gaussian-like edge of a band-limited driver) decays faster still, and an edge with many continuous derivatives decays faster than any power. Real edges are closer to the second case than to a perfect linear ramp, because every real driver is an RC or LC low-pass with a smooth step response. For an ideal Gaussian-shaped edge there are no nulls and the spectral content falls off exponentially in $f^2$. So the trapezoid model is a *conservative* estimate of high-frequency content.

## What physically limits the real edge

- **Driver and load**: output resistance $R_o$ and load capacitance $C_L$ give $t_r\approx 2.2R_oC_L$.
- **Package and via inductance** with capacitance: second-order low-pass, often with a ring.
- **Skin effect and dielectric loss** in traces and cables attenuate the highest frequencies proportionally more ([[q0722]]).
- **Transistor $f_T$**: for most digital logic this is a much higher limit than the above.

## Seeing it on the bench

An instrument has its own bandwidth. The edge you measure is approximately $t_{meas}=\sqrt{t_{signal}^2 + t_{scope}^2}$. A 350 MHz scope has $t_{scope}\approx 1$ ns, so a 1 ns real edge reads as 1.41 ns, a 41% error, and the spectrum you compute from it would show *less* high-frequency content than the truth.

## Putting a number on "highest significant"

One workable definition: the highest frequency at which the envelope is within some loss of the noise floor or the limit that matters. For emissions, plot the envelope against the limit line. For signal reproduction, require the passband to include the knee plus margin, so the edge distortion is small ([[q0676]]). For simulation, set the maximum frequency to 3 to 5 times the knee.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A crisp way to answer: "Ideal is infinite. Real is limited by rise time. For a trapezoid the spectrum falls at 20 dB/decade up to roughly $1/(\pi t_r)$ and 40 dB/decade after. In practice I'd use about 0.35 to 0.5 divided by $t_r$ as the highest frequency I need to care about." Then give a number.
:::

::: applications
- **Choosing scope and probe bandwidth** so edges are not slowed by the instrument.
- **Setting the frequency range of an SI or EM simulation** (S-parameter extraction, field solver) from the edge rate rather than the clock rate.
- **EMC planning**: deciding up to which frequency to expect and test for emissions from a given clock.
- **Cable and connector selection** for fast edges (loss and cut-off at the knee).
- **Specifying a driver's slew-rate limit** so the real content stays within what the board can handle ([[q0674]]).
- **Sampling-rate selection** for capturing or simulating the waveform ([[q0668]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear (trapezoidal) edges.** Real edges are smoother; the sinc nulls are shallower and high-frequency content is usually lower than the trapezoid estimate.
- **Equal rise and fall times.** If they differ, the envelope involves both and nulls move.
- **Rise time definition** (0-100% linear in the formulas; 10-90% in the rules of thumb). The conversion for a linear ramp is $t_{10\text{-}90} = 0.8t_{0\text{-}100}$.
- **Linear, time-invariant channel** between source and observer.
- **50% duty** unless noted. Other duties change which lines exist but not the edge-driven envelope.

Alternative valid answer: "ideal: infinite; real: the clock's harmonics up to the bandwidth of the system, typically the 5th (or tenth) harmonic". That is a pragmatic digital-design rule; it matches the knee estimate when $t_r\approx T/10$.
:::

::: followups
### What is the highest significant frequency for a 10 MHz clock with a 2 ns rise time?
Using $0.35/t_r$: 175 MHz; using $0.5/t_r$: 250 MHz. That is the 17th to 25th harmonic of the 10 MHz fundamental. The 10 MHz fundamental is irrelevant to the bandwidth needed.

### A 1 GHz scope views a signal with a 500 ps edge. What rise time does it show?
Scope rise time $\approx 0.35/1\text{ GHz}= 0.35$ ns. Measured $\sqrt{0.5^2+0.35^2} = 0.61$ ns, 22% slower than the real edge. For under 3% error you need a scope about $4\times$ the $0.35/t_r$ figure, i.e. about 2.8 GHz.

### Does a Gaussian-shaped edge have a highest frequency?
Technically no (the Fourier transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian, never exactly zero), but it falls so quickly that content is negligible within a few multiples of the 3-dB frequency. That is the practical sense in which real signals are band-limited.

### Why do people say "5th harmonic" for digital bandwidth?
For a 50% square wave with $t_r \approx T/10$, the knee falls near the 3rd to 5th harmonic. Passing through the 5th harmonic keeps the fundamental plus 3rd and 5th (about 93% of the power) and gives a recognisable, slightly rounded square wave. It is a minimum for waveform fidelity, not a safe number for EMI.

### What if the edge is faster than the clock frequency suggests, e.g. a 1 MHz clock with a 1 ns edge?
The knee is near $1/(\pi\cdot1\text{ ns}) = 318$ MHz, 318 times the fundamental. The 1 MHz clock still radiates at hundreds of MHz and needs fast-edge layout practice: continuous return plane, controlled impedance for long runs, filters at the connectors. Slowing the edge (series resistor or slew control) costs nothing in timing at 1 MHz and cuts the high-frequency energy dramatically.
:::

::: interpretations
"Highest frequency component of a real vs ideal square wave" can be read two ways. The strict mathematical reading: both have infinitely many harmonics, ideal falls as $1/n$, real as $1/n^2$ beyond a knee. The engineering reading: ideal needs infinite bandwidth, real needs about $0.35/t_r$ to $0.5/t_r$. The answer above gives both. [[q0676]] asks specifically for the rule of thumb.
:::
