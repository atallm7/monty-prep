::: testing
Whether you can place and connect the inductor with its two roles in mind: one terminal is the high-dV/dt switch node and the other is a quiet, smooth-current output node; and the body is a magnetic-field source. The interviewer wants: put it right next to the SW pins, keep the SW pad small, take the output pad to the output capacitors with wide copper, keep sensitive traces and other inductors out of its field, treat what is under it with care, and give it copper for heat. Understanding that its wiring is about resistance, not nanohenries, is the insight.
:::

::: why
The inductor is the biggest component, the biggest source of magnetic field and the one with a terminal on the loudest node. Routing it wrongly gives noisy feedback, bad ripple measurements, extra EMI and sometimes a thermal problem. Because its current is smooth, many people over-optimise the wrong thing (inductance) and under-optimise the right ones (the SW stub and the field). A clean answer here shows you understand each node's electrical nature.
:::

::: concepts
- **Smooth vs pulsed currents**: the inductor current is a ripple triangle; the hot loop is a pulse train. See [[q0546]].
- **Switch node routing**: the inductor's SW terminal is part of it. See [[q0550]].
- **Magnetic coupling**: mutual inductance $M$ induces $v = M\,di/dt$ in nearby loops.
- **Shielded vs unshielded inductors**: the field footprint differs. See [[q0259]].
- **DCR and heat**: $I^2R_{DCR}$ in the winding and pads.
- **Output capacitor loop**: the ripple current path to ground. See [[q0553]].
:::

::: answer
"The inductor has two very different ends. One end is the switch node, which swings the full input voltage in nanoseconds, so I put the inductor right next to the SW pins and keep that copper short and small. The other end is the output node, carrying smooth ripple current, so I take it to the output capacitors with wide, short copper, because there the concern is resistance, not inductance.

The inductor body is also a magnetic field source. I keep the feedback trace, the divider and any sensitive analog circuitry away from it, and I orient it so its field isn't pointing at them; with several inductors I rotate them 90 degrees apart and space them. A shielded inductor helps a lot when space is tight. I don't route signals under an unshielded inductor, and I check the datasheet about copper pours or planes underneath, since some vendors recommend a keep-out for the best inductance.

Last, heat: the winding dissipates I squared DCR, so I give its pads copper, and I keep temperature-sensitive parts away. For the numbers: with 12 V in, 3.3 V out and 4.7 µH, the ripple slope is about 1.85 A per microsecond, so a 10 nH mutual coupling into a feedback loop gives about 18 millivolts of pickup."
:::

::: technical
**Two terminals, two natures.** SW terminal: dV/dt of $V_{in}/t_r$ (4×10⁹ V/s for 12 V, 3 ns); keep the pad and stub small ([[q0550]]). Output terminal: smooth triangle, here $\Delta I_L = 1.02$ A p-p on 5 A DC (RMS 5.0 A), so wiring is judged by resistance: IPC about 1.8 mm for 5 A on 1 oz outer; 2 mm×8 mm is 1.9 mΩ.

**Placement.**
1. Inductor adjacent to the SW pins; SW pad faces the IC.
2. Output pad → output ceramics (and sense point) via wide copper; the loop L → $C_{out}$ → PGND → IC's low-side return should be compact so ripple doesn't ground-bounce.
3. Orientation: unshielded drum or open core spills flux along its axis; point the axis away from FB, ADC and sensors. Rotate adjacent inductors by 90°.
4. Under the inductor: shielded/molded inductors tolerate signals and planes below; for unshielded cores avoid signal traces underneath (flux couples and the plane eddy currents can reduce $L$ slightly); follow the vendor's land-pattern advice.
5. Heat: copper on pads (not on SW beyond the stub), consider that DCR heating plus core loss raise the inductor temperature, which lowers $I_{sat}$ (ferrites) and raises DCR (+0.39%/°C).
6. Keep the inductor away from heat-sensitive parts.

**Magnetic coupling numbers.** During the on-time $di/dt = (V_{in}-V_{out})/L = 8.7/4.7\ \mu\text{H} = 1.85\times10^6$ A/s; off-time $0.70\times10^6$ A/s. A victim loop with mutual inductance $M$ sees $v = M\,di/dt$: 1 nH → 1.9 mV; 10 nH → 18.5 mV; as a step at $f_{sw}$ against an 0.8 V reference, 18 mV is about 2%. $M$ falls rapidly with distance and with orientation (zero when axes are orthogonal in the ideal case).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Whether a ground plane or pour under the inductor helps or hurts depends on the inductor (shielded or not) and its datasheet. Some vendors specify a copper keep-out under the body for rated inductance and thermal performance. Follow your inductor's recommended land pattern.
:::

::: long
"The inductor is the one component with a very noisy end and a very quiet end, plus a body that radiates a magnetic field, so I treat those three things separately.

The switch-node end swings the full input voltage in a few nanoseconds, so I put the inductor right next to the IC's SW pins and keep that stub short and small, no pour. That's the same rule as for the switch node.

The other end carries the smooth ripple current, a triangle of about an amp peak to peak on top of the DC. For that, inductance in the wiring isn't a concern; resistance and heat are, so I use wide, short copper to the output capacitors, around 2 millimetres for 5 amps, and I keep the loop through the output capacitor and back to the IC's ground compact so the ripple doesn't bounce the ground.

The body: it's a magnetic source. During the on-time the current ramps at about 1.85 amps per microsecond for 12 volts in, 3.3 out and 4.7 microhenries, so a feedback loop with 10 nanohenries of mutual coupling picks up about 18 millivolts. So I keep feedback, the divider and analog circuitry away from it, orient the axis away from them, rotate nearby inductors by 90 degrees, and use a shielded inductor if I can't give it distance. I don't run signals under an unshielded one, and I check the vendor's land pattern about copper underneath.

Finally heat: the winding dissipates I squared DCR, so give the pads copper and keep sensitive parts away."
:::

::: terms
- **DCR**: DC resistance of the inductor winding.
- **Shielded inductor**: an inductor with a magnetic enclosure that confines its field.
- **Mutual inductance ($M$)**: coupling between two loops; $v = M\,di/dt$.
- **Ripple current ($\Delta I_L$)**: the peak-to-peak triangular current in the inductor.
- **Saturation current ($I_{sat}$)**: the current at which inductance falls substantially.
- **Land pattern**: the footprint and copper recommended for a component.
- **Axis orientation**: the direction of the inductor's dominant flux.
- **Core loss**: hysteresis and eddy losses in the core.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "put it close to the IC and away from sensitive traces."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Treats the two terminals differently (SW: small and short; output: wide and resistive).
- Quantifies the ripple current and slope, and the $M\,di/dt$ pickup number.
- Mentions shielded vs unshielded, orientation, rotating neighbouring inductors.
- Raises the "what's under the inductor" question and defers to the vendor's land pattern.
- Includes heat and the effect of temperature on $I_{sat}$ and DCR.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating the inductor wiring as inductance-critical.** The current is smooth; the wiring is about resistance. The hot loop is the inductance-critical one.
- **Routing feedback right beside the inductor.** Magnetic and capacitive pickup. See [[q0552]].
- **Placing two inductors side by side with the same orientation.** They couple (beat noise, synchronisation problems).
- **Putting the sense point on the inductor side of the output capacitors.** The sensed voltage includes ripple and drop; sense at the load side of $C_{out}$.
- **Ignoring heat.** A hot inductor loses $I_{sat}$ and may saturate at a current that was fine at room temperature.
- **Ignoring the land pattern.** Some inductors want a keep-out or specific pad geometry.
:::

::: deep
## Two ends, three roles

The inductor sits between the switch node and the output node:

Figure: The inductor current is a triangle of 1.02 A peak to peak at 500 kHz around the 5 A DC. Its slope is 1.85 A/µs while the high-side switch is on and 0.70 A/µs while it is off. These are gentle compared with the hot loop's 1.7 A/ns pulses, so wiring inductance in the inductor path is unimportant, while resistance and field matter.

**Role 1: SW terminal.** The terminal toggles between $V_{in}$ and ground in nanoseconds. Treat it exactly like the switch node ([[q0550]]): short, small, one layer, plane below. A low-profile inductor with terminals that face the IC and a footprint with a small SW pad is ideal. Avoid footprints in which the SW terminal has a long internal trace or a large heat-spreading pad.

**Role 2: output terminal.** The output carries $I_{L}$ with ripple. Route it as wide as the IPC chart says (1.8 mm for 5 A on 1 oz, plus margin), directly to the output ceramic capacitors. The loop $L \to C_{out} \to$ PGND $\to$ low-side switch $\to$ SW has smooth current and a ripple of 1.02 A p-p; if the capacitor ground and IC ground are separated by an impedance $Z$, the ripple generates $\Delta I\cdot Z$ of ground bounce (1 A × 5 mΩ = 5 mV). Place $C_{out}$ close to the inductor's output pad with ground vias into the plane, and put the voltage sense point after the capacitors ([[q0552]], [[q0553]]).

**Role 3: the body.** Windings with a ferrite or powder core produce a flux that depends on construction:

| Type | Stray field | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Open drum core / unshielded | Largest, axis-directed | Cheap, high $I_{sat}$; keep sensitive loops away; orient axis away |
| Semi-shielded | Moderate | Compromise |
| Fully shielded / molded | Small | Slightly higher DCR or lower $I_{sat}$ for a given size; allows closer neighbours |

The field induces voltage in any nearby loop: $v = M\,di/dt$ with $di/dt = 1.85\times10^6$ A/s on the up-slope. A feedback loop (the path from the divider to the pin and back through ground) of area a few mm² with $M$ = 1 nH sees 1.9 mV, with $M$ = 10 nH sees 18.5 mV; against an 0.8 V reference the second is a 2% disturbance every cycle, which appears as jitter or ripple on the output (or, if it aliases, low-frequency noise). Because $M$ falls steeply with distance (and is ideally zero for orthogonal axes), keep sensitive loops a few mm to a few cm away, and make their loop area small by routing signal and return together.

## What is under the inductor

Copper under an unshielded inductor interacts with its flux: eddy currents in a plane under the body oppose the flux and reduce the effective inductance slightly and add a bit of loss. With a shielded inductor, the effect is smaller. Meanwhile a solid ground plane on L2 beneath the part's footprint is usually fine and desirable for the SW terminal's return. Some vendors' datasheets specify a "keep-out" of copper on the top layer under the body (apart from pads) and some recommend no signal routing underneath. Follow the part's land pattern; in the absence of guidance, keep signal traces out from under the part and leave the L2 plane solid.

## Multiple inductors

Two inductors close together couple magnetically: mutual inductance causes beat-frequency noise if their switching frequencies are slightly different, and can cause interaction between loops. Rotate them 90°, space them apart, or use synchronous operation with a controlled phase. For multiphase converters, the coupling is sometimes deliberate and useful (coupled inductors), but for independent converters it is not.

## Heat and temperature

The winding dissipates $I_{rms}^2R_{DCR}$. With 5 A RMS and a 10 mΩ DCR it's 250 mW in addition to core losses; the temperature rise of a small inductor can be 20 to 40 °C, which (a) increases DCR by 0.39%/°C, (b) reduces ferrite $I_{sat}$ at higher temperature (check the vendor's curve), and (c) heats neighbouring parts. Give the pads copper to spread heat (not onto SW), maintain airflow and keep heat-sensitive parts away.

## A placement recipe

1. Put the IC; put the inductor immediately on the SW-pin side with the SW terminal toward the pins.
2. Run SW stub: 2 mm wide, under 8 mm, one layer, plane beneath.
3. Run the output terminal straight to the output capacitors; ground vias at the capacitors.
4. Place the FB divider on the far side of the IC; route FB from the output capacitor's load side, away from the inductor.
5. Check orientation: the axis of an unshielded part points away from sensitive loops; adjacent inductors rotated.
6. Check the land pattern and copper/keep-out requirements.
7. Add thermal copper on pads, away from SW.

::: applications
- **Compact buck regulators** where the inductor sits touching the IC's SW pins and the output ceramics are right behind it.
- **Low-noise supplies for ADCs/sensors**, using shielded inductors and keep-out zones for analog.
- **Dual- or multi-rail PMICs** with several inductors rotated and spaced to avoid beat noise.
- **High-current POL converters**, where inductor pads are wide copper tied to output pours.
- **Boost converters and LED drivers**, where the inductor's switch-side terminal is treated like SW.
:::

::: assumptions
The answer assumes a conventional buck at hundreds of kHz with a 1 to 10 µH power inductor in ferrite or powder-iron construction. The slope and pickup numbers are for 12 V → 3.3 V, 4.7 µH; other designs scale ($di/dt = V/L$). Mutual inductance depends on geometry and cannot be computed here; the 1 to 10 nH values are illustrative of small to moderate coupling. For integrated-inductor modules the guidance is largely handled by the vendor's layout. Whether a plane or pour under the part helps or hurts is vendor-specific. If the inductor is a coupled inductor in a multiphase design, the neighbouring coupling is intended.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the SW end of the inductor need to be short but the output end only wide?
At the SW end the voltage swings the full input voltage in nanoseconds, so any extra copper is capacitance injecting noise ($C\,dv/dt$). At the output end the voltage is nearly DC and the current is a smooth triangle, so the wiring's inductance barely matters ($L\,di/dt$ with 1.85 A/µs is tiny) and what counts is resistance and heat, so wide copper is good.

### How would you reduce magnetic pickup into the feedback network?
Distance (the coupling falls quickly), orientation (axes orthogonal), a shielded inductor, small feedback loop area (route the divider and return tightly), a lower-impedance divider if quiescent current allows, and a solid plane between the inductor and the sensitive trace where the construction allows. Then confirm with the scope on the FB pin.

### Does a ground plane under the inductor help?
For the SW terminal's return, a solid plane on L2 is good. Directly under an unshielded body the plane can suppress inductance slightly through eddy currents, so some vendors recommend keep-outs in the top-layer copper beneath the body. I follow the vendor's land pattern and keep L2 solid unless told otherwise.

### Why rotate two inductors by 90 degrees?
Their magnetic axes then are orthogonal, which in the ideal case gives zero mutual coupling. If they're aligned and close, the field from one induces voltage in the other, so changing currents in one disturb the other, and if their frequencies are slightly different you get beat-frequency noise.

### What goes wrong if the inductor runs hot?
The DCR increases with temperature (about 0.39%/°C for copper), efficiency falls, and ferrite saturation current drops at high temperature, so a part that is fine at 25 °C can saturate at the peak current in a hot enclosure. Saturation makes the current spike and can trip protection or damage the FETs. So I check the inductor's temperature rise and the vendor's saturation curves at the maximum ambient.
:::
