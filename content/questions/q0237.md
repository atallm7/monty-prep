::: testing
Whether you can give a **balanced engineering comparison**, not a sales pitch. A strong candidate:

1. Lists the real benefits (current, ripple, transient, thermal spreading, smaller passives) and ties each to a mechanism.
2. Lists the real costs (parts, area, cost, controller, current balancing, fixed losses times N, layout).
3. Knows the benefit is not free in efficiency terms: at the same total silicon, conduction loss is the same.
4. Ends with a decision rule, such as "it depends on current, voltage ratio and transient spec".
:::

::: why
"Should this rail be multiphase?" is a daily power-architecture decision. Choosing wrong either gives you a hot, noisy, slow single phase or a board full of inductors and a controller you did not need. Interviewers like this question because a memorized list of advantages is easy; explaining *why* each advantage appears and when it disappears is the sign of someone who has actually looked at a design.
:::

::: concepts
- **Interleaving and ripple cancellation**: shifted triangles partly cancel, and the summed ripple is at $N f_{sw}$ ([[q0236]]).
- **Effective inductance $L/N$**: all phases slew together in a load step.
- **Conduction loss**: $I^2R$. Splitting current among $N$ stages with the same per-stage $R$ divides the loss by $N$, but you also bought $N$ times the silicon.
- **Fixed per-phase losses**: gate drive, driver quiescent current, inductor core loss all scale with the number of active phases.
- **Current sharing**: tiny mismatches become big imbalances ([[q0238]]).
- **Thermal resistance and spreading**: power dissipated over more area gives lower temperature rise ([[q0242]]).
:::

::: answer
"The big advantages are that you can deliver much more current, the output ripple is lower and at a higher frequency so you need less capacitance, the transient response is faster because the effective inductance is L over N, and the heat is spread over several smaller stages instead of one hot spot. You can also shed phases at light load to keep efficiency up, and the input capacitor sees a smaller RMS ripple because the input current pulses are interleaved.

The disadvantages are cost and complexity. You need N inductors and N pairs of FETs or power stages, a multiphase controller, per-phase current sensing and balancing, and a careful symmetrical layout. Fixed losses like gate drive and core loss scale with the number of phases, so at light load a many-phase converter is worse unless it sheds phases. And imbalance is a real risk: if one phase carries more current it heats first.

One subtlety I like to mention: if you compare against a single phase using the same total silicon, the conduction loss is basically the same. The efficiency win comes from being able to use smaller, faster parts per phase, lower AC losses and phase shedding, not from the I-squared-R magically going down.

So for a 5 A rail I stay with one phase. For a 1 V, 60 A processor rail with fast steps I go multiphase."
:::

::: technical
**Benefits and mechanisms**

| Aspect | Multiphase effect | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Max current | $\approx N\,I_{phase}$ (derated) | Each inductor and FET pair carries $I_{out}/N$ |
| Output ripple | smaller, at $N f_{sw}$ | Shifted triangles cancel; zero at $ND\in\mathbb Z$ |
| Output capacitance | down by about the cancellation and $N$ factors | Lower ripple charge and higher ripple frequency |
| Transient | faster | $L_{eq}=L/N$, slew $\propto N$ |
| Thermal | lower hot-spot density | Loss divided among $N$ stages |
| Input capacitor | lower RMS current | Input pulses interleaved |

**Costs**

- **BOM and area**: $N$ inductors, $N$ drivers/FET sets, extra sense parts, a bigger controller.
- **Fixed losses**: gate drive $Q_gV_{dr}f_{sw}$ per phase, driver quiescent current, core loss. All scale with $N$ unless phases are shed.
- **Balance**: a duty-cycle error $\Delta D$ between phases gives $\Delta I\approx \Delta D\,V_{in}/R_{loop}$. For $\Delta D=0.1\%$, $V_{in}=12$ V and $R_{loop}=4$ mΩ that is 3 A, which is 20% of a 15 A phase.
- **Layout**: symmetrical power loops, equal sense routing, thermal coupling.

**Worked comparison (12 V to 1 V, 60 A, 30 A load step, 500 kHz per phase).** Four phases of 300 nH give $L_{eq}=75$ nH, summed ripple 4.4 A p-p at 2 MHz, slew up 147 A/µs. A single phase that gives the same 4.4 A ripple needs $L=V_{out}(1-D)/(f_{sw}\Delta I)=412$ nH and slews up only 27 A/µs, 5.5 times slower. In the ideal slew-limited limit the sag is $\Delta V=L_{eq}\Delta I^2/(2(V_{in}-V_{out})C)$, so for 20 mV you need about 153 µF with four phases and 845 µF with one. Real designs are limited by the control loop and ESR/ESL as well, so use these as the *relative* effect.

**Efficiency**: with equal per-phase hardware, conduction loss is $I^2R/N$ in total, but $N$ times the silicon. With the same total silicon, it is unchanged. Switching loss $\tfrac12V_{in}I t_{sw}f_{sw}$ is the same in total for the same edge times, because each phase carries $1/N$ of the current.
:::

::: short
"Multiphase gives you more current, lower and higher-frequency output ripple, a faster transient response because the effective inductance is L over N, and heat spread over several stages. The costs are more parts and area, a more complex controller with current balancing, fixed losses that multiply with the phase count, and a harder layout. At the same total silicon the conduction loss doesn't change, so I'd pick multiphase for high-current, low-voltage rails with tough transients, and stay single-phase for low-current rails."
:::

::: long
"I'd split it into what you gain and what you pay.

What you gain: first, current capability. Each phase carries a fraction of the load, so you can use ordinary FETs and inductors instead of one monster. Second, ripple. The inductor ripples are interleaved, so they partly cancel, and the summed ripple is at N times the switching frequency. That means less output capacitance, and the input capacitor sees lower RMS current too. Third, transient response. In a load step all phases ramp together, so the effective inductance is L over N, and the inductor current can catch up N times faster. In my 60 A example with a 30 A step, the ideal slew-limited estimate is about 150 microfarads with four phases versus about 850 with one phase of the same ripple. Fourth, thermal: the same total loss is spread over several packages and inductors, so peak temperatures are lower. And fifth, efficiency flexibility: at light load you can shut phases off so you do not pay their gate drive and core losses.

What you pay: more components, more board area and cost. A multiphase controller with per-phase current sensing and a balancing loop. Fixed losses multiply with the phase count, so if you cannot shed phases, light-load efficiency suffers. Mismatch is a real problem: a small duty-cycle or resistance difference can push several amps into one phase. And layout needs to be symmetric.

One caveat on efficiency: if I compare with a single phase built from the same total silicon, the conduction loss is the same. So I would not claim multiphase is inherently more efficient. It is mainly about current, ripple, transient and heat.

Rule of thumb: below about 10 to 20 amps with relaxed transients, single phase. Above that, or when the transient spec demands it, multiphase."
:::

::: terms
- **Interleaving**: staggered phase clocks, $T/N$ apart.
- **Effective inductance**: $L/N$ seen during a load step.
- **Ripple cancellation factor**: $\Delta I_\Sigma/\Delta I_{ph}$, zero when $ND$ is an integer.
- **Current balancing**: control action that equalizes phase currents.
- **Phase shedding**: turning phases off at light load.
- **Fixed loss**: loss that does not depend on load current, such as gate drive.
- **Input RMS current**: the AC current the input capacitors carry, lower with interleaving.
- **Derating**: using only a fraction of the nominal $N\,I_{phase}$ capability for margin.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** reads off a list of pros and cons.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Ties every pro to a mechanism ($L/N$, shifted triangles, loss divided among stages).
- Quantifies at least one effect, for example ripple 0.73 of one phase and slew 5.5 times faster for four phases.
- Notes that at the same total silicon the conduction loss is unchanged and that fixed losses grow with $N$.
- Brings up current-sharing sensitivity with a number (a 0.1% duty error giving amps).
- Gives a decision rule in terms of current, voltage ratio and transient demand.
- Mentions that the input capacitor RMS current also improves.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Multiphase is always more efficient."** Not at the same silicon, and not at light load unless phases are shed.
- **Ignoring light-load behavior.** Four active phases at 2 A waste gate-drive and core power.
- **Claiming zero ripple.** Cancellation is complete only when $ND$ is an integer.
- **Saying you need N times fewer capacitors.** Output capacitance requirements fall, but ESR/ESL and the loop bandwidth also matter.
- **Forgetting the input side.** Interleaving reduces the input capacitor RMS current, which is one of its less-talked-about benefits.
- **Ignoring balance and layout.** Mismatched sense, routing or thermal coupling erodes the benefits.
:::

::: deep
## The comparison, mechanism by mechanism

### 1. Current capability
A single phase is limited by three things: FET conduction and thermal limits, the inductor's saturation and heating current, and the package's thermal path. Dividing the current by $N$ moves all three away from their limits. The practical capability is $N I_{phase}$ times a derating factor for imbalance. With a $\pm5\%$ error in current sensing, one phase can sit 5% above the average, so the usable total is $\approx N I_{lim}/1.05$.

### 2. Ripple and capacitance
For a given inductance and frequency each phase has $\Delta I_{ph}=V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf_{sw})$. With the interleaving, $\Delta I_\Sigma=\kappa\,\Delta I_{ph}$ where $\kappa$ is the cancellation factor from [[q0236]]. In the VRM example $\kappa=0.73$ and the ripple frequency is $4f_{sw}$, so the capacitor ripple voltage (capacitive part, $\Delta V\approx\Delta I/(8Cf)$) drops by $\kappa/N=0.18$. The ESR part scales with $\Delta I_\Sigma$, so it drops by 0.73.

### 3. Transient and capacitance
During a load step the phases can all ramp, so the energy and charge the capacitors must supply during the catch-up time is smaller. In the slew-limited ideal case

$$\Delta V_{sag}=\frac{L_{eq}\,\Delta I^2}{2\,(V_{in}-V_{out})\,C},\qquad L_{eq}=\frac{L}{N}$$

Doubling the phases halves the capacitance, with diminishing absolute returns beyond four to six phases. A real design also needs enough ESR/ESL budget and a fast loop, so this is a trend rather than a BOM.

### 4. Heat and efficiency
Per-phase loss is $I_{ph}^2R_{ph}+\tfrac12V_{in}I_{ph}t_{sw}f_{sw}+Q_gV_{dr}f_{sw}$. The first two terms scale with $I_{ph}$, so for a fixed *total* current the sum over phases of conduction loss is $I_{out}^2R_{ph}/N$ and the switching term is constant. The gate term grows with $N$. The conclusion depends on what you hold fixed:

- **Same per-phase parts** (e.g. adding a phase to an existing design): conduction falls as $1/N$. This is the "I²R/N" curve in the sim.
- **Same total silicon** (splitting the same die area): conduction is unchanged, gate-drive loss rises.

So the honest efficiency benefits are: freedom to choose parts for the per-phase current, lower AC inductor loss because of lower ripple per amp, and shedding at light load ([[q0241]]).

### 5. The cost side
- **Parts**: for four phases, four inductors (the biggest and most expensive parts), four power stages, per-phase decoupling and sense.
- **Control**: the controller has to hold currents equal. A 0.1% duty mismatch ($\Delta D V_{in}/R_{loop}$) is 3 A with 4 mΩ of loop resistance, which is 20% of a 15 A phase.
- **Board**: symmetrical inductor placement, equal-length sense traces, thermal coupling. See [[q0238]].

## A decision framework

| Situation | Choose |
|---|---|
| $I_{out}<10$ A, relaxed transient, cost-sensitive | single phase |
| 15 to 30 A, moderate transient, height limited | 2 phases |
| 30 A and above at about 1 V, fast steps (CPU/GPU) | 4 or more phases |
| Very wide load range with light-load efficiency target | multiphase with phase shedding |
| Low $V_{out}/V_{in}$ ratio, very high current, and no way to meet the slew with one inductor | multiphase, possibly coupled inductors |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The current thresholds in this table are rules of thumb and vary with package, cooling, voltage ratio and vendor power-stage ratings. Check the actual FET, inductor and thermal limits for your design.

## Deriving the ripple cancellation instead of memorizing it

Every phase has the same inductor ripple $\Delta I_{ph}=V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf_{sw})$, with a rising slope $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ for time $DT$ and a falling slope $-V_{out}/L$ for $(1-D)T$. Shift phase $k$ by $kT/N$ and add them. Between switching events the summed slope is the sum of the individual slopes, so it takes only two values. Let $m=\lfloor ND\rfloor$ be the number of phases that are always on at any instant (the rest of the time $m+1$ are on):

- With $m+1$ phases in their on state: slope $=\big[(m+1)(V_{in}-V_{out})-(N-m-1)V_{out}\big]/L=\big[(m+1)V_{in}-NV_{out}\big]/L$.
- With $m$ phases on: slope $=\big[mV_{in}-NV_{out}\big]/L$.

Using $V_{out}=DV_{in}$, the first is $V_{in}(m+1-ND)/L>0$ and the second is $-V_{in}(ND-m)/L<0$. The fraction of each sub-interval of length $T/N$ spent in the "$m+1$ on" state is $ND-m$, so the rise lasts $(ND-m)T/N$. Therefore

$$\Delta I_\Sigma=\frac{V_{in}(m+1-ND)}{L}\cdot\frac{(ND-m)T}{N}$$

and dividing by $\Delta I_{ph}$ gives the cancellation factor

$$\kappa=\frac{\Delta I_\Sigma}{\Delta I_{ph}}=\frac{\big(1-\tfrac{m}{ND}\big)\big(m+1-ND\big)}{1-D}.$$

A brute-force sum of shifted triangles agrees with it to four digits for any $(N,D)$ you try. For the 12 V to 1 V rail ($N=4$, $D=1/12$, so $m=0$) it gives $\kappa=(1)(1-1/3)/(11/12)=0.727$, so the summed ripple is $0.727\times6.11\ \text{A}=4.44$ A, the "4.4 A" used above.

Figure: The cancellation is perfect (κ = 0) when $ND$ is an integer: $D=0.5$ for two phases, and $D=0.25,0.5,0.75$ for four. Away from those duty cycles the ripple climbs back toward the single-phase value, and at very low duty (like our 1 V from 12 V rail, $D=0.083$) four phases only get to $\kappa=0.73$. The cancellation is a property of the operating point, not of the topology.

> [!TIP]
> This is why a two-phase design making 3.3 V from 6.6 V ($D=0.5$) has almost no summed ripple, while the same two phases making 3.3 V from 24 V ($D=0.14$) only reach $\kappa\approx0.84$.

## Worked example: the input capacitor

Interleaving helps the input side even when the output ripple barely improves. Idealize each phase's current as a flat $I_{ph}=I_{out}/N$ while its high-side FET is on. Total input current then equals $kI_{ph}$ with $k$ phases on, taking only the values $m$ and $m+1$. The input capacitor must carry the AC part of that current, and a two-valued waveform with the upper value occupying fraction $f=ND-m$ of the time has RMS (about its mean)

$$I_{Cin,rms}=I_{ph}\sqrt{f(1-f)}.$$

For one phase ($N=1$, $f=D$) this reduces to the familiar $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$. For the 60 A, $D=1/12$ rail:

| Phases | $I_{ph}$ | $f$ | $I_{Cin,rms}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 60 A | 0.083 | 16.6 A |
| 4 | 15 A | 0.333 | 7.07 A |

The RMS drops to 43% and the heating in the input capacitors ($I^2 R_{ESR}$) to 18%. The input-capacitor count that the one-phase design would need at 16.6 A RMS is one of the large, often overlooked savings. These are idealized flat-top currents; real phase currents have ripple, which adds a little to the RMS figure.

## Where the story breaks down

- **Imbalance destroys cancellation.** If one phase has 10% more current or a slightly different duty cycle, the shifted triangles no longer cancel exactly and a residual at $f_{sw}$ appears. Ripple at the fundamental is a sign of mismatch you can see on a scope.
- **Phase shedding moves the cancellation point.** If four phases drop to two at light load, the ripple frequency halves and the "sweet spot" duty cycles change from $0.25/0.5/0.75$ to $0.5$. The output capacitor was sized for four phases and the ripple at light load is different, so verify the shed condition too.
- **Switching-frequency choice interacts.** Raising $f_{sw}$ per phase and adding phases are two ways to cut ripple. Phases cost board area and parts, frequency costs switching loss. The cheaper lever depends on the FETs.
- **Transient advantage requires the controller to use it.** The $L/N$ slew advantage only appears if the control loop can turn on all phases at once. Some controllers add phases sequentially, which gives up part of the benefit.
- **Layout parasitics scale with $N$ too.** Each power stage needs its own tight input-decoupling loop. A shared input capacitor that is far from one stage degrades its switching edges, raising its loss and noise relative to the others.

## What to measure on the board

1. **SW-node phase offset.** Probe the SW nodes of two phases. At 500 kHz with four phases they should be 500 ns apart (90°). A wrong offset means mis-programmed interleaving, and the summed ripple will be wrong.
2. **Current sharing.** Measure each phase current with a current probe on each inductor (or read the controller's DCR-sense telemetry) at 25%, 50% and 100% load. A healthy design stays within a few percent of the mean. Large drifts that grow with temperature suggest a thermal layout mismatch, since copper DCR rises about 0.39% per °C.
3. **Output ripple versus phase count.** With the controller allowing manual phase count (or by disabling phases), compare ripple at the same load for $N=1,2,4$ and check against $\kappa$.
4. **Efficiency vs load with shedding on and off.** The curves cross at the load where the saved fixed losses of the shed phases equal the extra conduction loss of fewer phases. That crossover is the design target for the shedding thresholds.
5. **Load-step sag.** Use an electronic load with a fast slew and measure the output droop. Compare to the ideal $L_{eq}\Delta I^2/(2(V_{in}-V_{out})C)$ figure to see how much of it the real loop and ESR/ESL add.

::: applications
- **Server and desktop CPU/GPU VRMs**: four to sixteen phases at about 1 V and 100 A or more.
- **FPGA/ASIC cores** at 20 to 60 A: two to four phases.
- **Laptop SoC rails** where board height forbids one big inductor, so two thin inductors are used.
- **AI accelerator boards**: many phases to track steep, repeating load steps.
- **Single-phase wins**: a 3.3 V, 3 A auxiliary rail, where a single buck IC is cheaper and smaller.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Comparison basis**: same $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$, $f_{sw}$ per phase, and CCM.
- **Matched phases**: balanced currents, equal L and R.
- **Ideal slew-limited transient** for the capacitance numbers. The real loop bandwidth, ESR and ESL change the absolute values.
- **Efficiency claim** depends on whether you compare equal per-phase parts or equal total silicon. The page states both.
- If the question comes with a specific rail (e.g. "5 A, 3.3 V"), the honest answer is that single phase wins.
:::

::: followups
### Does multiphase make the converter more efficient?
Not by itself. With equal total silicon the conduction loss is the same, and gate-drive and core losses grow with $N$. It becomes more efficient in practice because you can tune the per-phase parts, reduce AC loss with lower ripple per phase and shed phases at light load. At full load with extra phases of the same size, the conduction loss falls as $1/N$.

### What is the worst-case failure mode of a multiphase buck?
A current-sharing failure. If one phase has a bad sense, a drifting duty cycle or a degraded inductor, it carries more than its share, heats up, and can trip the overcurrent limit early or be damaged. Hence per-phase current limits, thermal monitoring and a balance loop.

### How does the input capacitor change?
The input current of each phase is a pulse train. Interleaving staggers the pulses, so the sum has a smaller RMS value. For $N$ phases at $D=1/N$ the input current is nearly constant. This reduces the input capacitor count and heating.

### When would you not use multiphase?
For low-current rails (under about 10 A), when cost and area dominate, when the load is steady, and when the input-to-output ratio is gentle (so a single inductor is not hard to slew). The controller and parts overhead then outweigh the gains.

### Scenario: your four-phase 1 V rail is 5% less efficient at 5 A than at 40 A. What do you check?
At light load fixed losses dominate: gate drive, driver quiescent current and inductor core loss for four phases, plus the controller. First check whether phase shedding is enabled and the thresholds. Then check DCM/pulse-skipping behavior, driver supply current, and per-phase core loss at the ripple current. I would also compare efficiency with 1, 2 and 4 phases enabled to see where the crossover is.
