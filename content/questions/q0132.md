::: testing
Whether you understand bandwidth as more than "the cutoff frequency". A strong answer connects three views:

1. **Frequency domain**: the range of frequencies a system passes within 3 dB of its passband (low-pass: 0 to $f_c$; band-pass: $f_H - f_L$).
2. **Time domain**: bandwidth sets **speed**, i.e. rise time $t_r\approx 0.35/BW$ and settling.
3. **Information and noise**: how fast data can be sent, how much noise gets in (noise bandwidth), and what the signal needs.

The interviewer is also checking that you apply it: scopes, op-amps, ADC front-ends and links all have a bandwidth that must match the signal.
:::

::: why
"Is this fast enough?" is the central question in analog and mixed-signal design. Bandwidth answers it for a filter, an amplifier, a probe, a scope, a cable or a channel. If the bandwidth is too low, edges are smeared and amplitudes are wrong; too high, you integrate more noise and let in interference. A good grasp of the bandwidth-rise time relationship also lets you spot measurement errors: a scope with insufficient bandwidth will lie about your signal.
:::

::: concepts
- **−3 dB bandwidth**: the frequency range over which the gain stays within 3 dB (0.707) of the passband gain. See [[q0133]].
- **Corner frequency**: for a low-pass, the bandwidth equals $f_c$. See [[q0129]].
- **Rise time**: 10–90% time of a step response; $t_r\approx 0.35/BW$ for a first-order system.
- **Gain-bandwidth product**: closed-loop bandwidth $\approx GBW/\text{gain}$. See [[q0116]].
- **Noise bandwidth**: the effective bandwidth for noise integration; $\frac{\pi}{2}f_c$ for a first-order filter.
- **Fourier content**: a fast edge contains energy up to roughly $0.35/t_r$.
:::

::: answer
"Bandwidth characterizes the range of frequencies a circuit or system can pass without significant attenuation, usually measured between the points where the gain is 3 dB below its passband value. For a low-pass filter that's from DC to the corner frequency. For a band-pass it's the upper minus the lower frequency.

What it really tells you is how fast the system can respond. A system with a bandwidth of $B$ has a rise time of about 0.35 over $B$, so a 100 MHz oscilloscope can't show you an edge faster than about 3.5 nanoseconds, and a 1 MHz amplifier can't follow a signal that changes in much less than a microsecond.

It also tells you how much information you can push through. A wider channel can carry more data, and Shannon's formula has bandwidth right in it. And it tells you how much noise you let in, because noise power is proportional to bandwidth. That's why you don't make the bandwidth wider than the signal needs.

For op-amps, the closed-loop bandwidth is about the gain-bandwidth product divided by the gain, so a 1 MHz GBW part with a gain of 10 has about 100 kHz of bandwidth."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** For a transfer function $H(f)$ with passband gain $H_0$, the −3 dB bandwidth is $BW = f_H - f_L$ where $|H(f_{L,H})| = |H_0|/\sqrt2$. Low-pass: $f_L = 0$, $BW = f_c$. Band-pass: $BW = f_H - f_L = f_0/Q$ with $f_0=\sqrt{f_Lf_H}$.

**Rise time.** For a first-order low-pass, the step response is $1-e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = \frac{1}{2\pi f_c}$. The 10–90% rise time is
$$t_r = \tau\ln 9 = 2.197\,\tau = \frac{0.350}{f_c}.$$
For higher-order, well-damped systems the product $t_r\cdot BW$ is about 0.35 to 0.45. In the case of cascaded systems: $t_{r,total}\approx\sqrt{\sum t_{r,i}^2}$.

**Signal bandwidth.** A digital edge of rise time $t_r$ has significant spectral content up to about $0.35/t_r$ to $0.5/t_r$ (the "knee" frequency; conventions differ). The fundamental of a clock is much lower.

**Op-amp closed-loop bandwidth.** $f_{-3dB}\approx GBW/(1/\beta)$ for a single-pole amplifier.

**Noise bandwidth.** Integrated noise: $v_n = e_n\sqrt{ENBW}$ for white noise density $e_n$. For a first-order filter $ENBW=\frac{\pi}{2}f_c$ (1.57$f_c$); for higher orders the ratio gets closer to 1.

**Channel capacity.** $C = B\log_2(1+SNR)$. A 1 MHz channel at 30 dB SNR supports about 10 Mb/s in the ideal limit.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The "0.35/t_r" and "0.5/t_r" rules are approximations with different definitions of "significant" content and of rise time (10–90% vs 20–80%). They are the standard rules of thumb, but check which one your team or the spec uses.
:::

::: short
"Bandwidth is the range of frequencies a system passes within 3 dB of its passband gain: DC to the corner for a low-pass, upper minus lower edge for a band-pass. It characterizes speed: rise time is about 0.35 over the bandwidth. It also sets how much noise gets in and, through Shannon, how much information you can send. For an op-amp, closed-loop bandwidth is about GBW over gain."
:::

::: long
"Bandwidth characterizes how much of the frequency spectrum a system passes. Formally, it's the span of frequencies between the two points where the gain has dropped 3 dB from its passband value. For a low-pass filter or an amplifier that starts at DC, the bandwidth is simply the upper cutoff, say 100 kHz. For a band-pass filter it's the upper edge minus the lower edge, which equals the center frequency over the Q.

But the reason it matters is what it implies. First, speed. A first-order system with bandwidth $B$ has a 10 to 90 percent rise time of 0.35 over $B$. So a 100 kHz amplifier takes about 3.5 microseconds to rise, and a 350 MHz oscilloscope has about a one nanosecond rise time. That's why you need a scope with several times the bandwidth of the signal you're looking at: if the edge is 1 nanosecond and the scope's rise time is also 1 nanosecond, you'll see about 1.4 nanoseconds.

Second, signal content. A fast edge contains frequencies up to about 0.35 over its rise time, so the circuit that carries it needs to pass those frequencies or the edge gets rounded. A square wave isn't just its fundamental.

Third, noise. For white noise, the noise you integrate is proportional to the square root of the bandwidth, so there's no reason to be wider than the signal needs. A first-order filter's noise bandwidth is about 1.57 times its corner frequency.

And fourth, capacity. Shannon's formula says capacity is bandwidth times log of one plus SNR, so bandwidth is the main way to get more data through a channel.

For amplifiers I'd add that, because of the gain-bandwidth product, bandwidth and gain trade off: a 1 MHz GBW op-amp at a gain of 10 gives about 100 kHz."
:::

::: terms
- **Bandwidth ($BW$)**: the width of the passband between the −3 dB points.
- **Cutoff / corner frequency**: the −3 dB edge of the passband.
- **Rise time ($t_r$)**: 10–90% time of the step response; $\approx 0.35/BW$ (first-order).
- **Gain-bandwidth product (GBW)**: gain × bandwidth, constant for a single-pole amplifier.
- **Quality factor ($Q$)**: $f_0/BW$ for a band-pass.
- **Noise bandwidth (ENBW)**: the width of an ideal rectangular filter that passes the same noise power.
- **Knee frequency**: $\approx 0.35/t_r$ to $0.5/t_r$, above which an edge has little energy.
- **Channel capacity**: maximum error-free data rate of a bandwidth-limited noisy channel.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the range of frequencies the circuit can pass, between the −3 dB points".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the definition **with its edge cases**: low-pass BW = $f_c$, band-pass BW = $f_H - f_L = f_0/Q$.
- Links it to **time-domain speed** with the formula $t_r\approx 0.35/BW$ and a worked example (350 MHz scope, 1 ns edge).
- Mentions **noise bandwidth** ($\pi/2$ factor) and the principle "don't be wider than needed".
- Mentions **gain-bandwidth** trade-offs for amplifiers with a number.
- Notes that signals have bandwidth too: a **fast edge** needs $0.35/t_r$ from the circuit, and measurement systems combine as the root-sum-square of rise times.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Bandwidth is the cutoff frequency" without the lower edge.** For band-pass or AC-coupled systems, bandwidth is the *span*.
- **Confusing bandwidth with data rate** (bits/s). They are related by the modulation and SNR, not equal.
- **Applying $t_r = 0.35/BW$ blindly.** It is exact for a first-order system and an approximation for others (0.35 to 0.45).
- **Using a scope with bandwidth equal to the signal frequency.** At the −3 dB point the amplitude reads 30% low and edges are smeared. A common rule of thumb is 3 to 5 times the highest frequency of interest.
- **Forgetting that a wider bandwidth lets in more noise** ($\propto\sqrt{BW}$). Faster isn't free.
- **Calling a −3 dB point "where the signal stops".** The signal is attenuated by 30%; it doesn't vanish.
- **Mixing up GBW and bandwidth**: GBW is a constant; bandwidth depends on the closed-loop gain.
:::

::: deep
## Bandwidth in the frequency domain

For a low-pass system the response is flat out to a corner, then falls. The bandwidth is how far the flat region extends: where $|H|$ falls to 0.707 of its passband value.

For a band-pass, there are two edges.

Because the two stages overlap, the passband peaks about 0.8 dB below 0 dB at 3.2 kHz, and the −3 dB points (measured from that peak) fall at about 0.84 kHz and 11.8 kHz. The bandwidth is therefore about 11 kHz, the centre is $f_0=\sqrt{f_Lf_H}\approx3.2$ kHz and $Q=f_0/BW\approx 0.3$. A low Q means a wide, shallow band-pass; a narrow-band filter has a high $Q$.

For a system that goes down to DC, such as a low-pass filter or a DC-coupled amplifier, $f_L = 0$ and $BW = f_H$. An AC-coupled amplifier also has a lower edge set by the coupling capacitor.

## Bandwidth means speed: the rise time connection

A first-order low-pass with corner $f_c$ has step response $v(t) = 1-e^{-t/\tau}$, $\tau=1/(2\pi f_c)$. The 10% point is at $t_{10} = -\tau\ln0.9 = 0.105\tau$ and the 90% point at $t_{90}=-\tau\ln 0.1=2.303\tau$, so

$$t_r = t_{90}-t_{10} = \tau\ln 9 = 2.197\,\tau=\frac{0.350}{f_c}.$$

Figure: Between the 10% and 90% points the output takes 3.5 µs. Double the bandwidth and the rise time halves. This is why bandwidth and speed are the same question.

**Cascading.** The rise times of cascaded stages combine as the root of the sum of squares, $t_{r,total}=\sqrt{t_{r1}^2+t_{r2}^2+\dots}$. For example: a signal with a 1 ns edge measured by a 350 MHz scope ($t_r = 0.35/350\text{ MHz}=1$ ns) shows $\sqrt{1^2+1^2}=1.41$ ns, a 41% error. A scope with 3 times the bandwidth (1050 MHz, $t_r=0.33$ ns) would show 1.05 ns, a 5% error. That's the reason for the "scope bandwidth should be at least 3 to 5 times the signal's bandwidth" rule of thumb.

## Signals have bandwidth too

Think of a square wave as a sum of odd harmonics. A clock at $f_0$ with a 50% duty cycle has harmonics at $3f_0, 5f_0, \dots$ with amplitudes falling as $1/n$. A real edge has finite rise time $t_r$ and its spectrum rolls off faster above roughly $0.35/t_r$ (or $0.5/t_r$ depending on the convention). So:

- A 100 MHz clock with 1 ns edges has significant content up to 350 to 500 MHz. The circuit, PCB and probes must have the bandwidth for *that*, not 100 MHz.
- Slowing the edge (say to 5 ns) lowers the needed bandwidth to about 70 MHz and cuts EMI, at the cost of timing margin.

This links bandwidth to signal integrity and EMC: higher-bandwidth edges excite more resonances and radiate more.

## Bandwidth and noise

For white noise with density $e_n$ (V/√Hz) the rms noise is $e_n\sqrt{ENBW}$. A real first-order filter isn't a brick wall; its noise-equivalent bandwidth is $\frac{\pi}{2}f_c = 1.57f_c$. So 10 nV/√Hz of white noise through a 100 kHz first-order filter gives $10\text{ nV}\sqrt{157\text{ kHz}} = 3.96$ µV rms. Cutting the bandwidth to 10 kHz cuts that by √10 to 1.25 µV. That's the benefit of limiting the bandwidth to what the signal needs.

## Amplifier bandwidth: gain trade-off

For a single-pole amplifier, $GBW$ is constant, and bandwidth $=GBW/\text{gain}$.

| GBW | Gain | Bandwidth | Rise time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 MHz | 1 | 1 MHz | 350 ns |
| 1 MHz | 10 | 100 kHz | 3.5 µs |
| 1 MHz | 100 | 10 kHz | 35 µs |
| 10 MHz | 100 | 100 kHz | 3.5 µs |

## Capacity: bandwidth carries information

Shannon–Hartley: $C = B\log_2(1+SNR)$. With $B=1$ MHz and SNR = 30 dB (1000), $C=1\text{ MHz}\times\log_2 1001\approx 9.97$ Mb/s. Doubling the bandwidth doubles capacity; doubling the SNR adds only one bit/s/Hz. That is why high-speed links push to wider bandwidth first, and why filters, cables and PCB traces that cut bandwidth cause inter-symbol interference (see eye diagrams).

## Choosing the bandwidth

A rule of thumb: pick the bandwidth just wide enough to get the signal through with the allowed amplitude and rise-time error, and no wider. Too narrow → distortion and slow response; too wide → noise and interference. That is the heart of every filter design.

## Worked example: choosing a bandwidth for a pulse measurement

A sensor produces a flat-topped 20 µs pulse and you want the amplifier to report its height to within 2%. The amplifier is first-order and has white input-referred noise of 10 nV/√Hz (an assumed figure). How much bandwidth is enough, and what does extra bandwidth cost?

1. **Accuracy requirement.** The output reaches 98% of the final value at $t=\tau\ln(1/0.02)=3.91\tau$. That must be at most 20 µs, so $\tau\le5.11$ µs and $f_c=1/(2\pi\tau)\ge31.1$ kHz. The corresponding 10-90% rise time is $0.35/31.1\text{ kHz}=11.2$ µs.
2. **Margin.** Real components drift, and the pulse top is rarely perfectly flat, so choose $f_c=40$ kHz. Then $\tau=3.98$ µs and the output reaches $1-e^{-20/3.98}=99.3\%$ by the end of the pulse.
3. **Noise cost.** The noise bandwidth is $\frac{\pi}{2}f_c=62.8$ kHz, so the rms noise is $10\text{ nV}\sqrt{62.8\text{ kHz}}=2.5$ µV. Had you left the amplifier at 1 MHz "to be safe", the noise would be $10\text{ nV}\sqrt{1.57\text{ MHz}}=12.5$ µV, five times larger, a 14 dB penalty in signal-to-noise ratio for no gain in accuracy.

This is the whole logic of bandwidth selection in one calculation: the time-domain requirement sets the minimum, the noise sets the maximum, and the sensible choice sits just above the minimum. Note that the answer would change if the pulse's *shape* mattered (for example if you wanted the 10-90% edge timing of the sensor to within 1 µs, you would need about 0.35/1 µs = 350 kHz and accept the extra noise).

## Limiting cases of $t_r\cdot BW=0.35$

The constant 0.35 belongs to a single-pole response, and it moves with the shape of the roll-off:

- **Single pole.** Exactly $\ln 9/2\pi=0.350$, with no overshoot.
- **Smoother, Gaussian-like responses** (many scopes and Bessel filters) give a similar product of roughly 0.34 to 0.4, with little or no overshoot, which is why scope vendors quote 0.35 to 0.4 depending on the model.
- **Sharp-cutoff filters** (high-order Chebyshev or elliptic) give a higher product, closer to 0.45 or more, and overshoot and ring on a step, because the abrupt gain change is accompanied by a rapid phase change.
- **Brick-wall limit.** An ideal rectangular passband has an unbounded, ringing step response whose rise time is set by the width of the band, a reminder that bandwidth alone never fully determines the time response.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The exact product for a specific scope or filter depends on its response shape and on whether the vendor quotes 10-90% or 20-80% rise time. Use the number from the instrument's datasheet when precision matters.

## Verifying bandwidth on the bench

There are two independent ways to measure the same quantity, and the best practice is to do both and compare. In the frequency domain, sweep a small sine through the circuit and find the frequency where the output has fallen to 0.707 of its low-frequency value, keeping amplitude small enough to avoid slew limiting. In the time domain, apply a small step or a square wave much slower than the expected time constant and measure the 10-90% rise time, then compute $0.35/t_r$. For a single-pole system the two answers agree within a few percent; if the time-domain bandwidth is clearly lower, the circuit is slew-limited or has an overshoot-producing second pole.

Do not forget the instrument. Measure the scope's own rise time with a fast pulse source (or use the vendor's figure), subtract it in quadrature as in the follow-up on a 5 ns measurement, and check that the generator's edge is at least three times faster than the circuit under test. A probe's input capacitance and its ground lead belong to the circuit during the measurement, so use the shortest ground connection available.

:::

::: applications
- **Oscilloscope and probe selection**: bandwidth of at least 3 to 5 times the fastest signal content, to keep amplitude and rise-time error small.
- **Op-amp selection**: the required closed-loop bandwidth sets the GBW (GBW ≥ gain × bandwidth, with margin).
- **ADC front ends**: the anti-aliasing filter bandwidth is chosen relative to the sample rate and the signal band.
- **High-speed links and clocks**: channel bandwidth versus data rate and edge rate.
- **Sensor signal conditioning**: limiting noise bandwidth to the sensor's actual signal band.
- **Audio and RF**: band-pass bandwidth sets channel width (a voice channel versus a wideband receiver).
:::

::: assumptions
I defined bandwidth by the **−3 dB** criterion, which is the most common but not the only one. Some specs use a flatness criterion (e.g. ±0.1 dB for audio and instrumentation), a −6 dB point or a noise bandwidth. The $0.35/BW$ relation is exact for a first-order system and approximate otherwise: Gaussian-response scopes give about 0.34 to 0.4, and a system with peaking or sharp cutoffs differs. The "0.35/t_r" knee frequency is an engineering approximation. For an **ideal square wave** (zero rise time) the bandwidth is infinite. For **band-pass** filters the fractional bandwidth is quoted. For an **op-amp**, I assumed single-pole behavior (the GBW relation is not exact for multi-pole amplifiers or those with peaking).
:::

::: followups
### What bandwidth do I need to pass a 1 ns rise-time edge with minimal distortion?
About $0.35/1\text{ ns} = 350$ MHz for a first-order system, and conservatively 500 MHz to 1 GHz if you want the rise time preserved to within 10% or so (since a measurement system adds rise times in quadrature: a system with bandwidth 3× the edge's knee adds about 5%).

### What bandwidth does a first-order filter with $\tau = 1$ µs have, and what is its rise time?
$f_c = 1/(2\pi\tau) = 159$ kHz, and $t_r = 2.2\tau = 2.2$ µs (equivalently $0.35/159\text{ kHz}$).

### How is the bandwidth of a band-pass related to its Q?
$BW = f_0/Q$. A resonant band-pass at 10.7 MHz with a bandwidth of 200 kHz has $Q=53.5$. Higher Q means narrower bandwidth and a longer ringing time (the time constant of the resonance is $Q/(\pi f_0)$).

### An op-amp circuit needs a gain of 50 and a bandwidth of 200 kHz. What GBW is needed?
At least $50\times200\text{ kHz}=10$ MHz; practically choose 20 to 30 MHz so that the −3 dB point isn't at the specification limit and the gain error at 200 kHz is reasonable. Check slew rate as well for large signals.

### Harder: you measure an amplifier's rise time as 5 ns with a scope whose own rise time is 3 ns. What is the amplifier's real rise time?
The measured value is the root-sum-square: $t_{meas}^2 = t_{amp}^2+t_{scope}^2$ so $t_{amp}=\sqrt{25-9}=4$ ns. Then the bandwidth is about $0.35/4\text{ ns}=87.5$ MHz. If the scope were much faster than the amplifier, the correction would be negligible; here it matters. (This assumes both are approximately first-order or Gaussian, and the probe adds its own contribution.)
:::

::: interpretations
"What does the bandwidth characterize?" is deliberately open. The expected core answer is "the range of frequencies the circuit passes (between −3 dB points), which characterizes how fast the circuit can respond." The extra context (rise time, noise, information) is what makes the answer strong. In this section the question follows the roll-off question ([[q0131]]) and precedes the −3 dB question ([[q0133]]), so it's likely set in a filter context, but the answer extends naturally to op-amps ([[q0116]]) and measurement equipment.
:::
