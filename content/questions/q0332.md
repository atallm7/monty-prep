::: basics
### In plain words
Heat is the big enemy of a lithium battery for three reasons. Current flowing through its internal resistance makes heat inside the cell. Heat speeds up aging. And at high enough temperatures the cell can start heating itself in a runaway, which ends in fire. So designs keep cells inside a safe temperature window and get the heat out.

### Picture it
Think of a crowded room. Every person gives off a little heat, and the vents carry it away. With a few people, the room stays comfortable. Pack in more people, or block the vents, and the room keeps warming up. A cell works the same way, with the current as the crowd and the surface as the vents. If heat is made faster than it leaves, the temperature keeps rising.

### Words you need
- **Joule heating**: heat made when current flows through resistance, equal to $I^2R$ ([[q0303]]).
- **Internal resistance**: the cell's own opposition to current.
- **Thermal runaway**: self-feeding heating that ends in venting and possible fire ([[q0327]]).
- **Aging**: the slow loss of capacity, which speeds up as temperature rises ([[q0301]]).
- **NTC thermistor**: a temperature sensor that the charger or BMS watches ([[q0306]]).

### The simplest example
Take a 2 Ah cell with 50 mΩ of resistance, discharged at 3C, which is 6 A. The heat is $6^2 \times 0.05 = 1.8$ W. Say a small sealed pack sheds about 0.1 W for each kelvin of warming. Then it settles about $1.8 / 0.1 = 18$ K above the surrounding air. At 20C, which is 40 A, the heat would be 80 W, and the cell would warm by several degrees per second without strong cooling.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the heat sources, how cooling is estimated, the effect on aging and performance and the runaway threshold.
:::

::: testing
Whether you see heat as both a **cause and a consequence** of nearly every lithium failure. The hidden sub-skills:

1. You know where the heat comes from (I²R in internal resistance, reaction and entropy heat, ambient) and where it goes (conduction, convection).
2. You understand the two consequences: **gradual ageing** (side reactions speed up with temperature) and **thermal runaway** (positive feedback beyond a critical point).
3. You can describe design responses: temperature limits for charge and discharge, NTC sensing, derating, thermal design and propagation barriers.
:::

::: why
Temperature is the one variable that touches lifetime, performance, and safety at once. A product that runs its cell 15 °C hotter than planned can lose a large fraction of its life; a product that cannot shed heat can reach runaway. Thermal limits drive charge-rate decisions, enclosure design, cell spacing in packs and the thresholds in every charger IC. In an interview, "why is heat a concern" is a way to see whether you can give a mechanism and numbers rather than just "it gets hot and explodes".
:::

- **Internal resistance and Joule heating**: $P = I^2R_{int}$ inside the cell. See [[q0303]].
- **Temperature dependence of performance**: cold raises resistance, heat speeds reactions. See [[q0306]].
- **Arrhenius behaviour**: reaction rates grow roughly exponentially with temperature.
- **Thermal runaway**: self-heating beyond what the cell can shed. See [[q0327]].
- **Thermal resistance and heat paths**: conduction from the cell core to the surface and out to ambient.

::: answer
"Heat matters for three reasons. First, it's generated inside the cell any time current flows, mainly I squared R in the internal resistance. A 2 amp-hour cell at 5C is 10 amps through maybe 50 milliohms, so 5 watts inside a small package. If that can't escape, the cell warms up.

Second, heat accelerates ageing. Side reactions that grow the SEI layer, consume lithium and degrade the electrolyte all speed up roughly exponentially with temperature, and a common rule of thumb is that degradation rates roughly double for every 10 degrees, though the exact figure varies. So a pack that lives at 45 degrees ages much faster than one at 25.

Third, and most important for safety, there's thermal runaway. Above roughly 80 to 130 degrees C the decomposition reactions inside the cell become exothermic, so heat makes more heat. If the heat generation outpaces cooling, it's a positive feedback loop: the cell vents, burns, and can set its neighbours off.

So we put limits on it: charge only between about 0 and 45 degrees, discharge within about minus 20 to 60, NTC sensors that the charger and BMS watch, derating at high temperature, and thermal design: spacing, heat spreading, airflow, and barriers between cells."
:::

::: technical
**Heat sources.**

- **Joule heating**: $P_{joule} = I^2 R_{int}$ (dominant at high current).
- **Reversible (entropic) heat**: $P_{rev} = -I\,T\,\frac{\partial V_{oc}}{\partial T}$, small, sign depends on SoC.
- **Side-reaction heat** during abuse (SEI decomposition, electrolyte reactions, cathode breakdown).
- **Ambient and neighbours**: sun, adjacent hot components, other cells.

**Heat removal.** Conduction through cell layers, then convection/radiation from the surface: $P_{loss} = hA(T - T_{amb})$. Steady-state rise: $\Delta T = P/(hA)$. Example: a 2 Ah cell at 3 C (6 A) with $R_{int}=50\ \text{m}\Omega$ dissipates $36\times0.05 = 1.8$ W; with $hA \approx 0.1$ W/K (small sealed pack), $\Delta T = 18$ K. At 40 A (20 C), $P = 80$ W and the cell would heat by several degrees per second unless forced cooling is used.

**Temperature and ageing.** Reaction rates follow Arrhenius: $k = A\,e^{-E_a/(k_BT)}$. A common rule of thumb is roughly 2x per 10 °C for ageing rates; the true factor depends on mechanism and temperature range.

**Temperature and performance.** Cold: internal resistance rises (the sim uses an Arrhenius factor about 2.5x at 0 °C versus 25 °C), capacity at a given rate drops, and charging below 0 °C plates lithium. Hot: resistance falls (more power available) but ageing, gas generation and safety margin worsen. Typical limits: charge 0 to 45 °C, discharge -20 to 60 °C (cell-specific).

**Thermal runaway as a stability problem.** Heat generation grows exponentially with temperature while cooling is roughly linear. If the cooling line is tangent to or below the generation curve, there is no stable operating point above a critical temperature (Semenov criterion).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 2x-per-10 °C rule is a rough heuristic; actual activation energies, onset temperatures (about 80 to 130 °C for the first exotherm) and temperature limits are chemistry- and cell-specific. Use the datasheet.
:::

::: long
"Heat touches performance, lifetime and safety all at once. Start with where it comes from. When current flows, the internal resistance dissipates I squared R, inside the cell where it's hard to get out. A 2 amp-hour cell with 50 milliohms at 5C, 10 amps, dissipates 5 watts. Add the ambient temperature, a hot neighbouring component, or sunlight.

Where it goes is conduction through the cell and then convection from its surface: roughly h times A times delta T. For a small sealed pack with maybe a tenth of a watt per kelvin, 1.8 watts gives about an 18 degree rise. At high-rate drone discharge, tens of watts, the pack needs airflow.

Now the consequences. First, ageing. The side reactions that thicken the SEI, consume lithium and degrade electrolyte follow Arrhenius, so they speed up exponentially with temperature. As a rule of thumb, rates double per 10 degrees, so living at 45 instead of 25 can be several times worse. Second, the temperature extremes limit use: cold raises resistance and causes lithium plating when charging; hot reduces safety margin. Third, and most serious, thermal runaway. Above roughly 80 to 130 degrees, decomposition reactions turn exothermic. Heat generation grows exponentially with temperature while cooling grows only linearly, so beyond a critical point the cell can't shed heat as fast as it makes it, and you get venting, fire, and propagation to neighbours.

So design responses: charge only between about 0 and 45 degrees, discharge within the rated range, NTC sensing into the charger and BMS, current derating when hot, thermal paths and airflow, spacing and barriers between cells, and a thermal fuse or cutoff as a last resort."
:::

::: terms
- **Joule (I²R) heating**: heat from current flowing through the cell's internal resistance.
- **Entropic (reversible) heat**: heat absorbed or released by the cell chemistry, proportional to current and $\partial V/\partial T$.
- **Arrhenius law**: reaction rate $\propto e^{-E_a/k_BT}$; rates grow exponentially with temperature.
- **Thermal runaway**: self-accelerating heating when generation exceeds removal.
- **Thermal resistance**: $\Delta T/P$ between cell and ambient (K/W).
- **NTC thermistor**: temperature sensor used by chargers and BMS.
- **Derating**: reducing allowed current or voltage at temperature extremes.
- **Propagation**: spread of runaway from one cell to its neighbours.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it can explode and shortens battery life".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Splits heat into **generation** (I²R, entropic, reactions) and **removal** (conduction/convection) and gives a worked number.
- Separates **gradual** effects (Arrhenius ageing, rule of thumb with caveat) from **abrupt** ones (runaway), and notes cold is also a problem (plating, high resistance).
- Frames runaway as a **stability problem**: exponential generation versus linear cooling, so a critical temperature exists.
- Describes **design levers**: temperature windows, NTC placement, derating, thermal path, spacing and barriers, thermal fuse.
- Notes that internal temperature differs from surface temperature, so an NTC on the surface under-reads during high-rate pulses.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Heat is only a safety issue."** It is also the main driver of calendar and cycle ageing.
- **"Cold is safe."** Cold increases resistance and, if charging, causes lithium plating; both are damaging.
- **"Surface temperature equals cell temperature."** Core temperature can be many degrees above the surface during high-rate pulses.
- **"Low internal resistance at high temperature means hot cells are better."** More power available, but at the cost of faster ageing and less margin.
- **Treating the 2x per 10 °C rule as exact.** It is a heuristic and varies by mechanism.
- **Placing the NTC on the PCB far from the cell.** It reads the board, not the cell.
:::

::: deep
## Heat in, heat out

The energy balance for a cell is

$$m c\,\frac{dT}{dt} = P_{gen}(T, I) - hA\,(T - T_{amb})$$

with $P_{gen} = I^2R_{int}(T) + P_{rev} + P_{side}(T)$. For ordinary operation $P_{side}\approx 0$, and the steady state is $\Delta T = I^2R_{int}/(hA)$.

Worked examples with $R_{int} = 50\ \text{m}\Omega$, $hA = 0.1$ W/K:

| Current | Power | Steady rise |
|---|---|---|
| 2 A (1 C) | 0.2 W | 2 K |
| 6 A (3 C) | 1.8 W | 18 K |
| 10 A (5 C) | 5 W | 50 K |
| 40 A (20 C) | 80 W | 800 K (impossible: needs forced cooling or the cell fails) |

The last row is why hobby packs for 20C use have low-resistance cells (5 mΩ or lower, giving 8 W) and airflow. Heat grows with the *square* of current.

## Ageing: the slow effect of heat

Side reactions that consume cyclable lithium and thicken the SEI follow $k \propto e^{-E_a/(k_BT)}$. Take an activation energy of 0.5 eV (a typical-order value; actual varies) between 25 °C (298 K) and 45 °C (318 K):

$$\frac{k_{45}}{k_{25}} = \exp\!\left[\frac{E_a}{k_B}\left(\frac1{298}-\frac1{318}\right)\right] = \exp\!\left[5802\times 2.11\times10^{-4}\right]\approx 3.4$$

## Runaway: when heat outruns cooling

The decomposition reactions are also Arrhenius, so the generation term grows exponentially, while cooling grows linearly. Plot both and the picture of stability appears:

Figure: With good cooling the blue line meets the red curve twice. The first crossing (about 32 °C) is stable: a small rise gives more cooling than heating, so it settles back. The second crossing (about 78 °C) is a point of no return: above it generation beats cooling and the temperature runs away. With poor cooling (orange) there is no crossing at all, so the cell runs away from any starting temperature. This is the Semenov picture: improving heat removal moves the critical point up (more headroom), while weakening it below a threshold removes the stable point altogether, and a hotter ambient (shifting the lines) pulls the critical temperature down.

The numbers here are schematic (not a real cell), but the shape is general. Engineering consequences: better thermal conductivity, larger surface area, airflow and heat spreaders move you toward the "good cooling" line. Cells packed tightly in an insulating foam move you toward the "poor cooling" line.

## Why cold is a heat problem too

At 0 °C the internal resistance may be about 2 to 3 times its 25 °C value, so the same current gives 2 to 3 times the $I^2R$ heating, and the cell reaches its voltage cutoff early. The heat is useful (it warms the cell from inside) but charging at low temperature plates lithium, which is a *latent* safety hazard rather than a thermal one ([[q0330]]). That is why chargers refuse to charge a cold cell, and some products pre-heat the pack.

## Design checklist

- **Sense**: NTC bonded to the cell (not the PCB); place near the hottest cell; use more than one sensor in large packs. Surface temperature lags the core during pulses, so apply a margin.
- **Limit**: charge window about 0 to 45 °C; discharge about -20 to 60 °C; derate current linearly near the edges; trip a thermal cutoff well below the runaway onset.
- **Remove heat**: thermal pads to a case or heatsink, airflow for high-C use, avoid stacking pouch cells in insulating foam.
- **Contain**: spacing, thermal barriers and vent paths between cells so one cell failing does not heat its neighbours past their critical point ([[q0327]]).
- **Choose**: lower-$R_{int}$ cells for high-rate use; LFP chemistry for better thermal margin.

## A scenario number

A drone pack is 4S 5000 mAh with $R_{int}=20\ \text{m}\Omega$ drawing 100 A (20 C): $P = 10^4 \times 0.02 = 200$ W. With mass about 0.5 kg and $c\approx 1$ kJ/kg·K, the rise is 0.4 K/s with *no* cooling, 24 K per minute, which is why a hard-flying pack is hot to touch (the airflow and short flight time keep it from running away). Hovering at 20 A: 8 W, 0.016 K/s, only about 1 K per minute and harmless.

::: applications
- **Phone fast charging**: current tapers with temperature, with multiple NTCs and charge-rate derating when hot.
- **EV packs**: liquid cooling plates, preconditioning for cold, and propagation barriers between cells.
- **Drones/RC**: airflow design, post-flight cool-down before charging, rule of not charging hot packs.
- **Laptops**: charge termination or reduction at high temperature, and cell spacing from CPU heat.
- **Energy storage and e-bikes**: temperature-compensated charge voltage and thermal cutoffs.
:::

::: followups
### How much heat does a 2 Ah cell make at 2 C if R_int = 60 mΩ?
$I = 4$ A, $P = 16\times0.06 = 0.96$ W. With 0.1 W/K, the steady rise is about 10 K above ambient. Not dangerous, but at 45 °C ambient the cell reaches about 55 °C, which is above the preferred range.

### Why does charging a hot cell cause more damage than discharging it?
Charging adds lithium to the graphite at high potential, where plating and electrolyte reduction are favoured, and with heat both accelerate; at high SoC the cathode is also at its most reactive. Hence charge-temperature limits are tighter than discharge limits, and chargers derate at high temperature.

### What is the difference between a PTC and an NTC in battery safety?
An NTC thermistor is a sensor read by electronics to decide whether to charge. A PTC is a resettable fuse whose resistance jumps when hot, limiting current passively. Both appear in packs; one informs the controller, the other acts on its own.

### How can an NTC on the surface mislead you?
During a high-rate pulse the core can be many kelvin hotter than the surface, and the surface sensor lags. Compensate with a margin or a thermal model, place the sensor in good thermal contact, and use fault detection (open/short NTC means stop).

### Scenario: field returns show pack capacity fading fast in a hot-climate market. What do you investigate?
Look at the temperature history (does the product charge in a hot car or direct sun?), whether charge-temperature derating exists, whether the pack sits next to a heat source, and the average state of charge during storage. Use the Arrhenius numbers: if packs run at 45 °C instead of 25 °C, ageing is about 3x faster. Fixes: derate charge above about 35 to 40 °C, lower the top-of-charge voltage (for example 4.1 V), improve heat paths, and relocate the cell.

### Why does a cell heat more when it is cold at the start of a discharge?
Internal resistance is higher, so $I^2R$ is larger; this self-heats the cell and resistance falls (a negative feedback), so the voltage recovers during the discharge. It is a stable effect, unlike runaway.
