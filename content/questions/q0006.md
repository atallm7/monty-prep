::: basics
### In plain words
Power is how fast energy is being used or turned into heat. In an electrical part, it equals voltage times current, $P = VI$, measured in watts. Knowing the power tells you how hot a part gets and how fast a battery drains.

### Picture it
Think of a waterfall driving a water wheel. The power depends on how far the water falls (voltage) and how much water flows each second (current). A tall waterfall with a trickle and a short one with a huge flow can deliver the same power. Add Ohm's law and the same power can be written using only current and resistance, or only voltage and resistance.

### Words you need
- **Power (P)**: energy used per second, measured in watts (W).
- **Energy**: the capacity to do work or make heat, measured in joules (J). One watt is one joule per second.
- **Dissipation**: power turned into heat in a part.
- **Power rating**: the most power a part can safely dissipate.
- **Ohm's law**: $V = IR$. See [[q0001]].

### The simplest example
Put 12 V across a 1 kΩ resistor. The current is $12 / 1000 = 12$ mA. The power is $12 \times 0.012 = 0.144$ W, or **144 mW**. Using the resistor form gives the same answer: $V^2/R = 144/1000 = 0.144$ W. Many small resistors are rated well below that, so this one would overheat. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the $I^2R$ form, AC power where the timing of voltage and current matters, and everyday examples like regulator heat.
:::

::: testing
"What's the equation for power?" sounds like a one-liner, but the interviewer is checking:

1. You know $P = VI$ and the two Ohm's-law substitutions $P = I^2R = V^2/R$, and **when each is convenient**.
2. You know what power *is*: the rate of energy transfer (W = J/s), and the sign convention for absorbed vs delivered power.
3. You can handle **AC**: average power uses RMS values and the power factor, $P = V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\varphi$.
4. You instinctively apply it: resistor ratings, regulator dissipation, battery life, efficiency.
:::

::: why
Power is where electronics meets heat, batteries and cost. Every part has a power rating; every regulator has to get rid of $(V_{in} - V_{out})\cdot I$ or its switching losses; every battery product lives or dies on average power. Interviewers ask this to see whether you go further than $P = VI$: do you check resistor wattage, think about the thermal consequence, or know that an LDO dropping 1.7 V at half an amp burns almost a watt? It also sets up the next question, why losses are dominated by current ([[q0007]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Energy and power**: power is the rate of energy transfer, $P = dW/dt$; 1 W = 1 J/s.
- **Voltage as energy per charge, current as charge per time**: multiply them and you get energy per time. See [[q0001]].
- **Ohm's law substitution**: for a resistor, $P = I^2R = V^2/R$.
- **Passive sign convention**: $P = VI > 0$ means the element absorbs power; $< 0$ means it delivers.
- **RMS and power factor**: for AC, average power uses RMS values and the phase between $V$ and $I$.
- **Conservation of energy**: $P_{in} = P_{out} + P_{loss}$, the basis of efficiency. See [[q0154]].
:::

::: answer
"The basic equation is $P = VI$: power equals voltage times current. It makes sense dimensionally: voltage is energy per unit charge and current is charge per second, so their product is energy per second, which is watts.

For a resistor I can substitute Ohm's law to get $P = I^2R$ or $P = V^2/R$. I pick whichever uses what I know. If I know the current through a sense resistor, $I^2R$ is easiest; if I know the voltage across a resistor, $V^2/R$. So 12 volts across 1 kΩ is 144 milliwatts, which is already more than an 0603 resistor is rated for.

For AC, the instantaneous power is still $v(t)\cdot i(t)$, but what matters is the average, which is $V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the phase between voltage and current. For a pure resistor $\cos\varphi = 1$; for a pure capacitor or inductor the average is zero.

In practice I use it constantly: checking resistor wattage, working out that an LDO going from 5 to 3.3 volts at half an amp burns 0.85 watts, or estimating battery life from average power."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** $p(t) = v(t)\,i(t)$ (instantaneous). With the passive sign convention (current enters the + terminal), $p > 0$ means the element absorbs power.

**Resistor forms.** Substitute $V = IR$:
$$P = VI = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}$$

**Energy.** $W = \int p\,dt$. Constant power: $W = Pt$. Battery energy: $E \approx V_{nom}\times Q$, e.g. 3.7 V × 2 Ah = 7.4 Wh. Stored energy: capacitor $\tfrac12CV^2$, inductor $\tfrac12LI^2$ (power into them averages to zero in periodic steady state).

**AC (sinusoidal steady state).** With $v = V_p\sin\omega t$ and $i = I_p\sin(\omega t - \varphi)$:
$$p(t) = \tfrac12V_pI_p\left[\cos\varphi - \cos(2\omega t - \varphi)\right]$$
$$P_{avg} = \tfrac12V_pI_p\cos\varphi = V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\varphi$$
- **Apparent power** $S = V_{rms}I_{rms}$ (VA); **reactive power** $Q = V_{rms}I_{rms}\sin\varphi$ (var); complex power $\mathbf S = \mathbf V\mathbf I^* = P + jQ$.
- **Power factor** $= P/S$ (equals $\cos\varphi$ only for sinusoids; with harmonic distortion it's lower).
- RMS is defined so that $P = V_{rms}^2/R$ for a resistor with *any* waveform: $V_{rms} = \sqrt{\tfrac1T\int v^2\,dt}$.

**Common engineering forms.**
- Linear regulator dissipation: $P = (V_{in} - V_{out})I_{out} + V_{in}I_q$.
- Efficiency: $\eta = P_{out}/P_{in}$, losses $= P_{in} - P_{out}$.
- CMOS dynamic power: $P = \alpha CV^2f$ ([[q0110]]).
- Thermal: $T_J = T_A + P\,\theta_{JA}$.
:::

::: long
"The fundamental equation is $P = VI$. Voltage is energy per coulomb and current is coulombs per second, so the product is joules per second, which is watts. I use the passive sign convention: if current flows into the positive terminal, the element is absorbing power; if it flows out of the positive terminal, it's delivering power, like a battery discharging.

For resistors, I substitute Ohm's law. $P = I^2R$ is handy when I know the current, like a 10 milliohm sense resistor carrying 5 amps: that's 0.25 watts. $P = V^2/R$ is handy when I know the voltage: 12 volts across 1 kΩ is 144 milliwatts. Both are the same physics; it's just which variable you have.

For AC, instantaneous power is still $v$ times $i$, but it fluctuates at twice the line frequency, so what I care about is the average. For sinusoids that's $V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\varphi$, where $\varphi$ is the phase between voltage and current. A resistor has $\cos\varphi = 1$, so it's just $V_{rms}I_{rms}$, which is exactly why RMS is defined the way it is. A pure capacitor or inductor has 90 degrees of phase shift, so it stores energy and gives it back each cycle, averaging zero. That leads to apparent power in volt-amps and power factor.

In design, power is mostly about heat and efficiency. An LDO going from 5 volts to 3.3 at half an amp drops 1.7 volts at 0.5 amps, so 0.85 watts of heat, which means checking the package's thermal resistance. A buck converter at 90% efficiency delivering 6.6 watts takes 7.3 from the input and dissipates about 0.73. And for battery products, average power times time is energy: a 7.4 watt-hour cell at half a watt lasts about 15 hours, ignoring conversion losses."
:::

::: terms
- **Power (P)**: rate of energy transfer, in watts (W = J/s = V·A).
- **Energy (W or E)**: integral of power over time; joules, or watt-hours (1 Wh = 3600 J).
- **Instantaneous power**: $p(t) = v(t)i(t)$ at each moment.
- **Average (real) power**: the time average of $p(t)$; what heats things and what the utility bills you for.
- **RMS value**: the DC-equivalent value for heating a resistor, $\sqrt{\overline{v^2}}$; $V_p/\sqrt2$ for a sine.
- **Apparent power (S)**: $V_{rms}I_{rms}$ in volt-amperes (VA); sizes wiring and transformers.
- **Reactive power (Q)**: energy sloshing in and out of L and C each cycle, in var; averages to zero real power.
- **Power factor (PF)**: $P/S$; 1 for a resistor, 0 for a pure reactance.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "$P = VI$" and maybe "$I^2R$".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives all three forms and explains *why* $P = VI$ (energy/charge × charge/time).
- Mentions the **sign convention** (absorbed vs delivered).
- Handles **AC correctly**: average power, RMS, power factor, and the fact that reactive elements average zero.
- Immediately **applies** it: resistor wattage/derating, LDO dissipation $(V_{in} - V_{out})I$, efficiency, $T_J = T_A + P\theta_{JA}$.
- Knows the trap: $V^2/R$ looks like "higher voltage = more loss", which leads straight into [[q0007]].
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using peak values for AC power.** $P = V_pI_p$ overestimates by 2× for a sinusoid. Use RMS: $P = V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\varphi = \tfrac12V_pI_p\cos\varphi$.
- **Plugging the wrong $V$ into $V^2/R$.** $V$ must be the voltage *across that resistor*, not the supply voltage. A resistor in series with a load doesn't see the full supply.
- **Ignoring power ratings.** 12 V across a 1 kΩ 0603 resistor (rated ≈0.1 W) burns 144 mW. Always check $P$ against the rating, with margin and temperature derating.
- **Thinking capacitors and inductors dissipate power.** Ideal ones only store and return energy; their average power is zero. Real ones dissipate through ESR, core loss and so on.
- **Multiplying volt-amps by efficiency without power factor** for AC loads. A motor drawing 10 A at 120 V isn't necessarily using 1200 W.
- **Confusing energy and power.** mAh is charge, Wh is energy, W is power. Battery life is energy ÷ average power.
:::

::: deep
## Why $P = VI$

Voltage is energy per unit charge: 1 V = 1 J/C. Current is charge per unit time: 1 A = 1 C/s. Multiply them:

$$P = V\cdot I\quad\left[\frac{\text{J}}{\text{C}}\cdot\frac{\text{C}}{\text{s}} = \frac{\text{J}}{\text{s}} = \text{W}\right]$$

Every coulomb that falls through a potential difference $V$ gives up $V$ joules. If $I$ coulombs do so each second, the energy transfer rate is $VI$. This holds for **any** element: resistor, LED, motor, battery, transistor, capacitor (instantaneously).

**Sign convention.** Define $V$ with + at the terminal where the reference current $I$ enters. Then $P = VI$ is the power *absorbed*. A resistor always gives $P > 0$. A battery being discharged has current leaving its + terminal, so $P < 0$ in this convention: it's delivering power. A battery being charged absorbs power. This is how you check a circuit solution: the sum of absorbed powers is zero (Tellegen's theorem, see the worked example in [[q0003]]).

## The resistor forms, and which to pick

For a resistor, substitute $V = IR$:

$$P = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}$$

| You know | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Current through it | $I^2R$ | 5 A through a 10 mΩ shunt → 0.25 W |
| Voltage across it | $V^2/R$ | 12 V across 1 kΩ → 144 mW |
| Both | $VI$ | 3 V × 10 mA in an LED resistor → 30 mW |

The forms seem to disagree about whether power rises or falls with $R$. At fixed *current*, more $R$ means more power ($I^2R$). At fixed *voltage*, more $R$ means less power ($V^2/R$). Both are right; the question is which quantity your circuit holds fixed. Power transmission holds the delivered power fixed and lets you choose voltage, which is the setup for [[q0007]].

## Power ratings, heat and temperature

Power becomes heat, and heat raises temperature. For a component with junction-to-ambient thermal resistance $\theta_{JA}$:

$$T_J = T_A + P\cdot\theta_{JA}$$

**Example: LDO.** A 5 V → 3.3 V LDO supplying 0.5 A dissipates $(5 - 3.3)\times0.5 = 0.85$ W (plus a negligible $V_{in}I_q$). In a small package with $\theta_{JA}$ of, say, 150 °C/W, the junction rises 128 °C above ambient, which is far too hot. You'd need a package with better thermal resistance and copper area, or a buck converter.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $\theta_{JA}$ depends heavily on package, PCB copper area and airflow; datasheet values (measured on a JEDEC standard test board) commonly range from roughly 30 °C/W for exposed-pad packages to over 200 °C/W for tiny leaded packages. Use the datasheet and treat it as an estimate for your board.

**Resistor ratings.** Typical chip resistor ratings: 0402 ≈ 1/16 W, 0603 ≈ 1/10 W, 0805 ≈ 1/8 W, 1206 ≈ 1/4 W ([[q0018]]). Those ratings apply at up to about 70 °C ambient and derate above, so design for roughly half the rating.

## Energy

Power integrated over time is energy:
$$W = \int_0^T p(t)\,dt\quad(\text{constant }P: W = PT)$$

- **Battery life**: a 3.7 V, 2000 mAh Li-ion cell holds about $3.7\times2 = 7.4$ Wh. At an average draw of 0.5 W that's ≈14.8 h, before regulator losses and the cell's usable-capacity limits.
- **Stored energy**: capacitor $\tfrac12CV^2$ (100 µF at 12 V: 7.2 mJ), inductor $\tfrac12LI^2$ (10 µH at 3 A: 45 µJ). They store and return energy; in periodic steady state their average power is zero.

## AC power: instantaneous, average, RMS

With sinusoidal voltage and a current lagging by $\varphi$:

$$v = V_p\sin\omega t,\quad i = I_p\sin(\omega t - \varphi)$$
$$p(t) = vi = \tfrac12V_pI_p\cos\varphi - \tfrac12V_pI_p\cos(2\omega t - \varphi)$$

The first term is constant: that's the **average (real) power**. The second oscillates at **twice** the frequency and averages to zero.

Figure: Power pulses at twice the source frequency. For a resistor it never goes negative and averages V_pI_p/2 = 10 W. With a 60° phase shift it dips negative (energy flows back to the source) and averages half as much. A pure reactance swings symmetrically and averages zero. So

$$P_{avg} = \tfrac12V_pI_p\cos\varphi = V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\varphi,\qquad V_{rms} = \frac{V_p}{\sqrt2}$$

**Why RMS?** RMS is defined so a resistor dissipates the same power as with a DC voltage of that value: $P = V_{rms}^2/R$ for *any* waveform. 120 V RMS mains has a peak of ≈170 V; a 144 Ω heater on it dissipates $120^2/144 = 100$ W.

**Apparent and reactive power.** $S = V_{rms}I_{rms}$ (VA) is what the wiring has to carry; $Q = V_{rms}I_{rms}\sin\varphi$ (var) is the energy that sloshes back and forth. Power factor $\text{PF} = P/S$. Utilities care because low-PF loads draw current (and cause $I^2R$ losses in the grid) without delivering real power; that's why power supplies above certain power levels include power-factor correction.

## Power in the rest of electronics

| Context | Formula | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Linear regulator | $(V_{in} - V_{out})I_{out}$ | efficiency ≈ $V_{out}/V_{in}$ |
| Switching converter | $P_{in} = P_{out}/\eta$ | 12 V → 3.3 V, 2 A at 90%: $P_{in}$ = 7.33 W, loss 0.73 W, $I_{in}$ ≈ 0.61 A |
| CMOS logic | $\alpha CV^2f$ | 10 pF, 3.3 V, 100 MHz, α = 1: ≈10.9 mW |
| MOSFET conduction | $I_{rms}^2R_{DS(on)}$ | use RMS current, not average |
| Diode | $V_F\cdot I_{avg}$ | why synchronous rectification wins at low voltage |
| Motor | $\tau\omega$ mechanical, $I^2R$ copper loss | |

> [!TIP]
> The quickest efficiency sanity check is power in = power out + losses. If someone gives you three of $V_{in}$, $I_{in}$, $V_{out}$, $I_{out}$ and an efficiency, the fourth falls out ([[q0155]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Resistor selection**: a 10 mΩ shunt at 5 A dissipates 0.25 W, so you'd choose a 0.5 W or larger part; an LED's 330 Ω resistor at 9 mA dissipates only ≈27 mW.
- **Thermal design of regulators**: LDO dissipation $(V_{in} - V_{out})I$ plus $\theta_{JA}$ predicts junction temperature; above a few hundred mW in a small package, consider a buck.
- **Battery runtime**: energy (Wh) ÷ average power (W), including converter efficiency and sleep current.
- **Power-supply sizing**: summing load powers with margin, then dividing by efficiency to size the input source and fuse.
- **Mains appliances**: RMS voltage and power factor determine real power and the current the wiring must carry.
- **Digital power**: $\alpha CV^2f$ explains why lowering core voltage is the strongest lever on processor power ([[q0110]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **DC or periodic steady state** for the average-power formulas. During transients, compute $\int vi\,dt$ directly.
- **Sinusoidal waveforms** for $P = V_{rms}I_{rms}\cos\varphi$. With distorted current (e.g. a rectifier with capacitor input), the power factor includes distortion and $\cos\varphi$ of the fundamental isn't the whole story.
- **Ideal reactive elements** average zero power. Real capacitors and inductors have ESR, core and dielectric losses.
- **Constant resistance** for $I^2R$ and $V^2/R$. Hot resistors and conductors change value.

All three resistor forms are valid; there isn't a "right" one, only the one that uses what you know. For semiconductors, $P = VI$ is still exact, but the $I^2R$ and $V^2/R$ forms only apply to the ohmic part (e.g. $R_{DS(on)}$).
:::

::: followups
### Which is "correct" for resistor power, $I^2R$ or $V^2/R$? They seem to contradict each other.
Both are correct. They're the same equation with Ohm's law substituted. The apparent contradiction (power rises with $R$ in one, falls in the other) is because each holds a different quantity fixed. With a fixed current source, a bigger resistor drops more voltage and dissipates more. With a fixed voltage source, a bigger resistor draws less current and dissipates less.

### What's the average power of a 50% duty cycle, 5 V square wave into a 10 Ω resistor?
When high, $P = 25/10 = 2.5$ W; when low, 0. Average: 1.25 W. Equivalently, $V_{rms} = 5\sqrt{0.5} = 3.54$ V and $V_{rms}^2/R = 12.5/10 = 1.25$ W. Note that using the *average* voltage (2.5 V) gives $6.25/10 = 0.625$ W, which is wrong: heating depends on RMS, not average.

### Why does a pure capacitor dissipate no average power?
Its current leads its voltage by 90°, so $\cos\varphi = 0$. Physically, it stores $\tfrac12CV^2$ as the voltage rises and returns it as the voltage falls. Instantaneous power swings positive and negative equally. A real capacitor dissipates $I_{rms}^2\cdot\text{ESR}$, which is why ripple-current ratings exist.

### A 12 V supply delivers 2 A to a buck converter whose output is 5 V at 4.2 A. What's the efficiency and loss?
$P_{in} = 24$ W, $P_{out} = 21$ W, $\eta = 87.5\%$, loss = 3 W. The loss turns into heat in the MOSFETs, inductor, capacitors and controller.

### If you double the voltage across a resistor, what happens to its power, and what does that mean for derating? (harder probe)
Power quadruples ($V^2/R$). A resistor at 50% of its rating at 5 V is at 200% at 10 V. Worse, its temperature rises, which (for a positive-TCR part) changes its resistance slightly and, more importantly, accelerates drift and failure. Resistors also have a maximum working voltage independent of power: a high-value resistor can hit its voltage limit (arcing or breakdown across the film) before its power limit ([[q0019]]).
