::: testing
Whether you can state the definition ($I = C \times$ capacity in Ah) and then **use it**: convert a C rating to amps, to a time to empty, and to a design requirement for a load. The deeper test is whether you know a C rating is a *thermal and voltage-sag limit set by internal resistance*, not a free guarantee: continuous versus burst ratings, the heat $I^2R$ that comes with high rates, and the fact that manufacturer ratings are not standardized and are often optimistic. Strong answers also mention charge C rating and that a "1C" label is relative to capacity.
:::

::: why
Anyone choosing a battery for a drone, RC car, e-bike, power tool or robot is really matching the load's peak current to the cell's C rating. Choose too low a rating and the pack sags, overheats and swells; choose too high and you pay for weight and cost you don't need. Interviewers ask this because it is a short question that exposes whether you can connect a spec to physics (internal resistance, heat) and to a sizing calculation.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ampere-hour capacity**: the charge the cell stores ([[q0311]]).
- **C-rate**: a current normalized to capacity: $I = C \times Q$; $1C$ would empty the cell in one hour.
- **Internal resistance and sag**: $V_t = V_{OCV} - IR$; heat $= I^2R$ ([[q0303]]).
- **Continuous vs burst rating**: sustained current limit vs a short-duration (seconds) limit.
- **Charge C rating**: the maximum recommended charging current, usually much lower than discharge ([[q0318]]).
- **Thermal limit**: the maximum safe temperature determines the real current limit ([[q0306]]).
:::

::: answer
"The C rating tells you how much current a battery can deliver, as a multiple of its capacity. One C is the current that would drain the battery in one hour, so for a 2200 milliamp-hour pack, 1C is 2.2 amps, and a 30C rating means 30 times that, or 66 amps. Time to empty is one over C, so 30C is two minutes.

So the maximum current is just $I_{max} = C \times$ capacity in amp-hours. Most hobby packs list two numbers: a continuous rating, and a higher burst rating that is valid only for a few seconds.

The important thing is what limits it. It's the internal resistance and the heat. At 66 amps through, say, 10 milliohms, the cell drops 0.66 volts and dissipates $66^2 \times 0.01 = 44$ watts inside a small pouch. That heats it up fast, and above about 60 degrees you're damaging the cell. So the C rating is really a statement that the manufacturer thinks the cell survives that current within a temperature and sag limit.

Also, those ratings aren't standardized, so I treat them as a rough guide, and I'd measure the voltage sag and the temperature at my actual load. When picking a battery I take my peak current, divide by capacity to get the required C, and add margin, maybe 30 to 50 percent. And charge rates are separate and much lower, typically 1C."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** $I = C_{rate} \times Q$ where $Q$ is the rated capacity in Ah (or mAh/1000). $t_{empty} \approx 60/C_{rate}$ minutes (ignoring rate-dependent capacity loss, [[q0311]]).

**Examples.** 2200 mAh: 1C = 2.2 A; 10C = 22 A; 30C = 66 A (continuous), 60C = 132 A (burst).

**Required rating for a load.** $C_{req} = I_{peak}/Q$. For a 40 A peak with a 2.2 Ah pack: $40/2.2 = 18.2$C minimum; with 50 % margin, 27C, so choose 30C.

**What really limits it.** Voltage sag $\Delta V = I R$, heat $P = I^2R$, and efficiency $\eta \approx 1 - IR/V_{OCV}$. For a 2.2 Ah cell with $R = 10$ mΩ and $V = 3.7$ V:

| C | I (A) | Sag (V) | Heat (W) | Efficiency | Time to empty |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2.2 | 0.02 | 0.05 | 99.4 % | 60 min |
| 5 | 11 | 0.11 | 1.2 | 97 % | 12 min |
| 10 | 22 | 0.22 | 4.8 | 94 % | 6 min |
| 20 | 44 | 0.44 | 19.4 | 88 % | 3 min |
| 30 | 66 | 0.66 | 43.6 | 82 % | 2 min |
| 60 | 132 | 1.32 | 174 | 64 % | 1 min |

**Thermal consequence.** If the cell has mass 45 g and specific heat about 1 J/(g·K) (assumed), 43.6 W with no cooling heats it by about 1 K/s, so 30C is only possible for short bursts or with airflow. For a given cell, the ratings assume a particular cooling and a maximum temperature.

**Charge rating.** Typically 1C for common LiPo (some high-rate cells allow 2C to 5C), see [[q0318]]. Charge and discharge C are independent specs.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> There is no universal test standard for hobby-LiPo C ratings. Different vendors define "continuous" with different temperature rises and voltage cutoffs, and some labels are inflated. Treat the rating as an upper bound to be verified by measurement.
:::

::: long
"The C rating is how a battery's maximum current is expressed relative to its capacity. A one-C current is the current that would discharge the battery in one hour. So with a 2200 milliamp-hour pack, 1C is 2.2 amps, 10C is 22 amps, and a 30C rating means about 66 amps. Time to empty is about sixty over C minutes, so at 30C it's about two minutes.

Hobby packs usually list a continuous rating and a higher burst rating that's valid for only a few seconds, around 10 seconds typically.

What does the rating physically mean? It's about internal resistance and heat. With 10 milliohms of internal resistance, 66 amps gives a 0.66 volt sag and 44 watts of heat inside a pouch weighing maybe 45 grams, which would heat it by roughly a degree per second without cooling. So the C rating is the vendor's claim for the current at which temperature and voltage stay acceptable. That also means the efficiency at 30C is only about 82 percent.

A practical caveat is that C ratings aren't standardized and some are inflated, so I'd verify with a test: load the pack at my real current and watch the voltage under load and the temperature.

For selection I divide the peak current by capacity. A 40 amp peak on a 2.2 amp-hour pack is 18C; with a 50 percent margin I want about 27C, so I'd choose a 30C pack. And separately, the charge rating is much lower, typically 1C."
:::

::: terms
- **C-rate**: current expressed as a multiple of capacity; $I = C \times Q$.
- **Continuous discharge rating**: the sustained current for which temperature and voltage stay within limits.
- **Burst rating**: higher current allowed for a short time (seconds).
- **Voltage sag**: the drop of terminal voltage below OCV under load, $IR$.
- **Internal resistance (DCIR)**: resistance seen at the terminals; sets sag and heat ([[q0303]]).
- **Charge C rating**: maximum recommended charging current as a multiple of capacity.
- **Peak current**: the highest current the load demands.
- **Derating**: using only a fraction of the rating for margin.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "C rating is how fast you can discharge, 1C is one hour".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Computes with the definition: 2200 mAh 30C → 66 A → 2 min.
- Explains the **physics limit**: $IR$ sag, $I^2R$ heating, efficiency $\approx 1 - IR/V$, with numbers.
- Distinguishes **continuous vs burst** and **charge vs discharge** ratings.
- Warns that **ratings are not standardized**, may be inflated, and should be verified by measuring sag and temperature.
- Shows the **selection method**: $C_{req} = I_{peak}/Q$, margin, plus pack-level considerations (series/parallel scaling, wiring and connector limits).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"C rating is the capacity."** C is a rate multiplier. Capacity is in Ah; the rating times capacity gives amps.
- **Forgetting that the same C gives more amps for bigger packs.** 30C on 5 Ah is 150 A, on 1 Ah only 30 A. Compare in amps for your load.
- **Taking the burst number as continuous.** Burst is typically only for a few seconds.
- **Believing the label.** Inflated ratings are common; a cell that sags 1.5 V at its "rated" current is not a 30C cell in practice.
- **Ignoring everything downstream.** Wires, connectors and FETs must also carry the current; a 100 A battery behind a 30 A connector is a 30 A system.
- **Using the discharge C for charging.** Charging at 30C is dangerous; charge limits are about 1C unless the datasheet says otherwise ([[q0318]]).
:::

::: deep
## Definition and the arithmetic

The "C" in C-rate is the capacity number in ampere-hours turned into a current. A cell of capacity $Q$ Ah has a $1C$ current of $Q$ A. Discharging at $nC$ means $I = nQ$:

$$I = n\,Q,\qquad t_{empty} \approx \frac{1}{n}\ \text{h} = \frac{60}{n}\ \text{min}$$

The time is "approximately" because at high rate the capacity delivered shrinks ([[q0311]]); at 5C the simulator's model of a 2 Ah cell with 50 mΩ delivers only 0.9 Ah, not 2 Ah.

C-rate is convenient because it scales with cell size: cells of the same chemistry and design behave similarly at the same C-rate (current density), regardless of capacity. That makes the C rating a design-independent quality indicator for the cell's resistance ($R \propto 1/Q$ at fixed design).

## What actually limits the rating

Three effects increase with current:

1. **Voltage sag.** $V_t = V_{OCV} - IR$. A higher current drops the voltage, which the load may not tolerate (regulator dropout, motor speed, brownout).
2. **Heat.** $P = I^2R$. This scales with the *square* of the current. Doubling the rate quadruples the heat.
3. **Reduced accessible capacity**, from the sag reaching the cutoff earlier.

The rating is then roughly the current at which a given cell, with a given cooling, reaches an allowed temperature rise or voltage drop.

## Thermal picture

With heat $P$ and cell thermal mass $m c_p$, the adiabatic temperature rise rate is $dT/dt = P/(m c_p)$. With $m = 45$ g and $c_p \approx 1$ J/(g·K): $43.6/45 \approx 0.97$ K/s. Starting at 25 °C, you reach 60 °C in about 36 s with no cooling. In practice heat flows to the surroundings and the cell's temperature is limited by airflow. This is why drone packs are mounted in airflow and why a pack that handles 30C for a few seconds can be damaged at 30C for a minute.

## Continuous vs burst

- **Continuous**: the current the pack can sustain until empty or to a steady-state temperature within limits.
- **Burst**: a higher figure, normally good for about 10 s or less, with the pack otherwise cool. It exists because heating takes time while sag is immediate.

A pack labelled "25C continuous / 50C burst" at 3 Ah is 75 A continuous and 150 A burst.

## Choosing a battery from the load

Given a motor ESC with continuous current 25 A and peaks of 40 A for 5 s:

1. Required C at peak: $40/2.2 = 18.2$C. With 50 % margin, 27C.
2. Required C at continuous: $25/2.2 = 11.4$C, so a 20C continuous rating covers it with margin.
3. Check sag: at 40 A and 10 mΩ (per cell), the sag is 0.4 V per cell. For a 4S pack that is 1.6 V total; the ESC must tolerate it.
4. Check temperature at the continuous current with your airflow.
5. Check wiring and connectors for 40 A.

If the load is fixed, a larger-capacity pack at lower C may be better: a 4 Ah 15C pack gives 60 A and has half the resistance of a 2 Ah of the same design, so less sag and heat at the same amps.

## Charge C rating

For charging, the C rating is much more modest: 1C is standard for LiPo, with some cells allowing 2C or higher, and the limit depends on temperature and lithium plating risk ([[q0318]]). A pack that can discharge at 50C cannot charge at 50C. Charge and discharge specifications are separate lines on the datasheet.

## Series and parallel scaling

For $S$ cells in series and $P$ in parallel, capacity is $P \times Q_{cell}$ and the C rating of the pack equals the cell's. Amps scale with $P$: a 3S2P pack of 2.2 Ah 30C cells has 4.4 Ah and 132 A continuous capability (at the same temperature rise). Voltage scales with $S$.

## Verifying a claimed rating

1. Fully charge the pack and let it rest.
2. Apply the claimed continuous current from an electronic load.
3. Record voltage, current and cell surface temperature every second for 30 s or until a temperature limit.
4. Compute the sag (OCV minus loaded voltage), the temperature rise and the effective resistance $R = \Delta V/I$.
5. Compare with the minimum voltage your load needs and the temperature limit.

If sag is large (a 3.7 V cell dropping to 3.0 V at the first second of the "rated" current), the real rating is lower than claimed.
:::

::: applications
- **Drones and FPV racing**: 4S to 6S packs rated 75C or higher for punch-outs, where the current is several times capacity.
- **RC cars and boats**: high-C packs sized to ESC and motor peak current.
- **Power tools and e-bikes**: cylindrical cells chosen by continuous current rating with careful thermal design.
- **Robotics and battle bots**: burst-heavy loads where sag determines performance.
- **Smartphones and wearables**: low C rates (about 0.2C to 1C) so the rating hardly matters, but fast-charge C rates matter.
:::

::: assumptions
- Capacity-based definition: $1C = Q$ in A, using the **rated capacity**; as the cell ages, the same current is a higher fraction of the true capacity.
- Heat and efficiency numbers use **R = 10 mΩ for a 2.2 Ah high-discharge cell**, a constant resistance (it changes with SoC, temperature and age), and a nominal 3.7 V.
- The adiabatic heating example assumes **45 g and 1 J/(g·K)**; real values depend on the cell design.
- The rating is **vendor-defined** and may refer to different temperature rises and cutoffs; verify by measurement.
- For **cylindrical cells** (18650 etc.) datasheets usually give a maximum continuous current in amps (and a temperature condition) rather than a C rating; convert with the capacity.
:::

::: followups
### A pack is rated 3000 mAh, 25C continuous. What current can it supply and for how long at that current?
$I = 25 \times 3 = 75$ A. Time to empty at that rate is about $60/25 = 2.4$ min, less in practice because capacity falls at high rate and the pack may hit its temperature limit first. A drone drawing 75 A would only fly briefly; the rating is about capability, not endurance.

### Two packs: 5000 mAh 20C and 2000 mAh 50C. Which delivers more current?
$5 \times 20 = 100$ A versus $2 \times 50 = 100$ A: equal on paper. The 5 Ah pack has twice the energy, but the 2 Ah 50C pack is lighter. In practice, the larger cell has lower resistance in absolute terms ($R \propto 1/Q$), so it sags less at 100 A if both ratings are honest. Check the real sag.

### Why is heat proportional to $I^2$ and not $I$?
Power in a resistor is $VI = I^2R$: doubling the current doubles the voltage drop $IR$ *and* the current, so the product quadruples. That's why a modest increase in C-rate has a big thermal effect and why short bursts are tolerated but sustained high rates are not.

### What if the battery sags below the ESC/regulator's minimum at peak current?
The load will brown out or shut down (a drone may lose power mid-flight). Options: use a pack with lower resistance (higher C or bigger Ah), add capacitance to handle brief peaks, increase the pack voltage (more series cells) so the same power needs less current, or limit the peak current in firmware.

### How would you test a vendor's 30C claim?
Discharge at the claimed continuous current with an electronic load from a full, rested state. Log voltage and surface temperature; extract the DCIR from the first-second step; confirm the voltage stays above the load's minimum and the temperature stays under about 60 °C (cell-dependent). Repeat with increasing current to find the real limit. Compare several lots if you plan production.
:::

::: interpretations
In the hobby world "C rating" nearly always means the **discharge** rating. In datasheets you may also see a charge C rating, and for cylindrical cells a max continuous current in amps. The answer covers discharge (what was asked) and notes the charge rating briefly. See [[q0316]] for how the voltage curve changes with C-rate and [[q0318]] for charge rate.
