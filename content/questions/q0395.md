::: basics
### In plain words
To measure the current in a motor or solenoid, the usual method is a shunt: a very small resistor in the current path. The current makes a small voltage across it, $V = I \times R$. An amplifier scales that voltage up so the microcontroller's ADC (its voltage-to-number converter) can read it. The catch is that "current drawn" depends on where you measure, because a PWM drive chops the current into pulses.

### Picture it
Picture a ring road with a toll booth at the entrance. While the entrance gate is open, cars flow in and keep circling the ring. When it shuts, the cars already on the ring keep circling. An entrance counter sees only cars coming through the gate. A counter on the ring sees them all. The entrance counter is the supply shunt, and the ring counter is a shunt in series with the motor.

### Words you need
- **Shunt resistor**: a precision resistor of a few milliohms. Its voltage drop measures current.
- **Current-sense amplifier (CSA)**: an amplifier that scales up that small drop.
- **PWM and duty cycle ($D$)**: PWM switches the supply fully on and off very fast. The duty cycle is the fraction of each period it is on.
- **Free-wheel current**: the current that keeps circulating through a diode across the motor (the flyback diode) when the switch is off.

### The simplest example
Say the current is up to 20 A and the shunt is 2 mΩ. At full current it drops $20 \times 0.002 = 0.04$ V, or 40 mV. An amplifier with a gain of 75 turns 40 mV into 3.0 V. On a 12-bit (4096-step) ADC each count is about 5.4 mA. Now a separate trap: if the motor current is a smooth 4 A at 50% duty, a bench supply shows only about 2 A.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page compares shunt placements, magnetic and other methods, PWM ripple and when to sample, and the amplifier errors that matter.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can pick and justify a way to measure current in a switched inductive load, and whether you know that "current drawn" depends on *where* you measure: supply current is pulsed and smaller than motor current, free-wheel current only exists in the motor loop, and PWM makes sampling timing matter. The interviewer wants the standard methods (shunt plus amplifier, Hall sensor, $R_{DS(on)}$ sensing, model-based estimate), a worked number, and awareness of errors (offset, tempco, common mode, layout).
:::

::: why
Current is the most informative signal in motor control: torque, stall, shorts, efficiency and heating all come from it ([[q0392]]). It is also the signal that is easiest to measure badly. Candidates who have only used a bench meter often miss that a chopped current needs averaging or synchronised sampling, and that a high-side shunt needs a rated amplifier. This question tests whether you can choose a method and quantify its accuracy.
:::

Alternatives: a Hall-effect sensor when I need isolation or no insertion loss; using the MOSFET's on-resistance as the sense element, which saves a part but is temperature-dependent and sloppy; or estimating from the motor model, voltage minus back-EMF over resistance, which is rough.

::: technical
**Shunt + amplifier.** $V_s = IR_s$, $V_{out} = G V_s + V_{ref}$ (bidirectional sensing adds a mid-rail reference). Design example: $I_{max} = 20$ A, $R_s = 2$ mΩ → $V_s = 40$ mV, $P = I^2R_s = 0.8$ W. Gain 75 → 3.0 V at full scale. Resolution on a 12-bit, 3.3 V ADC: $3.3/4096/(R_sG) = 5.4$ mA/LSB.

**Error sources.** Amplifier offset (µV, multiplied by $G$; matters most at small currents), gain error, shunt tolerance and tempco (a 50 ppm/K shunt over 100 K changes 0.5%), self-heating, thermal EMF and trace resistance in non-Kelvin layouts, common-mode rejection, ADC noise, aliasing of PWM ripple.

**Placement.**
- *Low-side (between load driver and ground)*: common-mode near 0 V. Simple. Doesn't see shorts of the load terminal to ground; ground shifts by $IR_s$.
- *High-side (between supply and driver)*: sees supply current and faults to ground; needs high common-mode CSA. Current is pulsed in a PWM system.
- *In-line (in series with the motor)*: sees the true motor current including free-wheeling, but the common-mode voltage switches between rail and ground each PWM cycle. Needs a CSA with high CMRR and fast settling (PWM-rejection input stage).

**Supply versus motor current.** Under PWM with duty $D$ and smooth motor current $I_m$, the average supply current is $\approx D\,I_m$ (the converter acts like a buck). 4 A motor current at $D = 0.5$ means 2 A average from the supply, in 4 A pulses. A bench supply's display shows the average, not the motor current.

**Ripple.** For a motor with large time constant, ripple is triangular with $\Delta I_{pp} \approx V D(1-D)/(L f_{sw})$. 12 V, $D=0.5$, $L = 1$ mH, 20 kHz: 0.15 A peak-to-peak.

**Hall / magnetic.** Isolated, low loss, ±1–2% typical accuracy range for open-loop parts and better for closed-loop (vendor dependent), bandwidth tens to hundreds of kHz for good parts, offset drift. See [[q0441]].

**$R_{DS(on)}$ sensing.** $I \approx V_{DS}/R_{DS(on)}$; $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature (often ×1.5–1.8 from 25 to 125 °C, check the datasheet) and varies part to part, so accuracy is poor; fine for overcurrent detection, bad for torque control. See [[q0448]].

**Model estimate.** $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$, e.g. 12 V, $K_e = 0.02$, $\omega = 300$ rad/s, $R = 1.2\,\Omega$: 5 A. Needs accurate $R$ (temperature drift) and $\omega$.

**Solenoid.** Steady-state current is $V/R$ (plunger position doesn't change it); the transient shows the plunger signature ([[q0392]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The ratio by which $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature, and the accuracy of Hall sensors, differ by part. Use the datasheet curve for your device.
:::

:::

::: terms
- **Shunt resistor**: precision low-value resistor used as a current sensor.
- **Kelvin (four-terminal) sensing**: sense wires connected at the resistor's pads.
- **CMRR**: common-mode rejection ratio of the amplifier.
- **Insertion loss**: voltage drop and power the sensor adds to the circuit.
- **Free-wheeling current**: current circulating through the flyback path when the switch is off.
- **LSB**: least significant bit of the ADC.
- **Burden voltage**: voltage dropped across an ammeter (meter shunt).
- **Offset**: output error at zero input.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer distinguishes supply current from motor current under PWM, gives a worked design (20 A, 2 mΩ, 75×, 5.4 mA/LSB, 0.8 W), compares placements and alternatives, describes error sources with numbers (tempco, offset), and states sampling strategy for chopped current. It also mentions meters for the bench (clamp meters with DC Hall sensing, scope current probes) and how to handle solenoid current.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Reading the bench supply's current as motor current.** It shows average supply current, which under PWM is $D\,I_m$.
- **Putting a regular op-amp on a high-side shunt.** Common-mode exceeds its input range.
- **Ignoring free-wheel current.** A low-side shunt misses it.
- **No Kelvin connection.** Trace resistance and solder joints become part of the "shunt".
- **Skipping the zero-current calibration.** Amplifier offset times gain is big at small currents.
- **Using $R_{DS(on)}$ for torque control.** Its temperature drift is too large.
:::

::: deep
## What "current drawn" means

A motor in a PWM drive has three currents that are all legitimate answers to "how much is it drawing":

1. **Motor (load) current $I_m$**: smooth because of motor inductance, with a small ripple.
2. **Supply current $I_s$**: pulsed; flows only when the high-side switch conducts. Average $\approx D\,I_m$.
3. **Free-wheel current $I_{fw}$**: flows in the diode loop when the switch is off, average $\approx (1-D)I_m$.

## Shunt design: a worked example

Requirements: 0 to 20 A, 12-bit ADC, 3.3 V reference, 100 mA resolution acceptable.

1. Shunt power budget: 1 W at most → $R_s \le 1/20^2 = 2.5$ mΩ. Choose 2 mΩ: $P = 0.8$ W at 20 A.
2. Full-scale shunt voltage: $20\times0.002 = 40$ mV.
3. Gain to use the ADC: $3.3/0.040 = 82$ max; choose 75 (standard value, leaves headroom) → 3.0 V full scale.
4. Resolution: $3.3/4096 = 0.806$ mV per LSB, divided by $R_sG = 0.15$ V/A → 5.4 mA per LSB.
5. Offset check: a CSA with $\pm100$ µV input offset gives ±50 mA error ($100\,\mu\text{V}/2\,\text{m}\Omega$). Small compared with 100 mA, but significant at 100 mA load; calibrate or choose a zero-drift CSA.
6. Tempco: a 50 ppm/K shunt over a 100 K rise shifts 0.5%, or 100 mA at 20 A.

Sampling in the center of the on-time (or off-time) catches the cycle average for a triangular ripple, which is why center-aligned PWM with a timer-triggered ADC is the standard technique. Drag the PWM frequency up to 100 kHz and the ripple shrinks fivefold; drag the inductance down to 0.3 mH and the ripple more than triples, to 0.5 A.

## Other methods

**Hall-effect sensors.** Open-loop sensors offer isolation with some temperature drift; closed-loop (compensated) sensors are more accurate and faster but costlier. Choose by current range, bandwidth and isolation requirement ([[q0441]]).

**$R_{DS(on)}$ sensing.** Zero extra loss and part count, but $R_{DS(on)}$ varies part to part and rises with temperature; use for overcurrent protection with a generous margin ([[q0448]]).

**Model-based estimate.** $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$. With 12 V, $K_e = 0.02$ V·s/rad, 300 rad/s and 1.2 Ω: $(12-6)/1.2 = 5$ A. Sensitive to $R$ (copper +0.39%/K) and $K_e$; fine for coarse stall detection.

**Bench measurement.** A DMM in series adds burden voltage and doesn't handle PWM well. A DC-capable clamp meter or a scope with a current probe shows the waveform. For solenoids, a small shunt and a scope reveal the pull-in signature.

## Solenoids

For a DC solenoid the steady current is $V/R$, independent of plunger position, e.g. 12 V / 24 Ω = 0.5 A. The transient has the characteristic dip when the plunger moves. Peak-and-hold drivers measure current with a low-side shunt and regulate it, so a shunt plus comparator is the standard solution.

## Layout

Route the two sense lines as a differential pair from the shunt pads (Kelvin), keep them away from switching nodes, add a small differential RC filter at the CSA input if needed, and place the amplifier close to the shunt. See [[q0443]] for the resistor itself.

## Worked example: what an in-line shunt does to the amplifier

Take the earlier 20 A, 2 mΩ shunt (40 mV full scale) but move it in series with the motor, so it sees the true motor current including the free-wheel period. Its terminals now swing between roughly 0 V and the 12 V bus at the PWM frequency, with edges tens of nanoseconds long. The amplifier must reject a 12 V common-mode step to resolve 40 mV. With a CMRR of 80 dB (a factor of $10^4$), the input-referred error is $12/10^4 = 1.2$ mV, which is 3 % of full scale and equivalent to $1.2\text{ mV}/2\text{ m}\Omega = 0.6$ A. Worse, CMRR typically falls with frequency, and the fast edges contain content well above where the datasheet quotes it. That is why in-line sensing uses amplifiers that are specified for PWM rejection or sampled during the quiet part of the cycle, and why low-side shunts are cheaper and more common even though they miss the free-wheel current.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> CMRR versus frequency and PWM-rejection performance differ greatly between current-sense amplifiers. Read the exact device's specification for the frequency and edge rate of your drive.

## Worked example: model-based estimate and its failure mode

The estimate $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$ with 12 V, $K_e = 0.02$ V·s/rad, 300 rad/s and 1.2 Ω gives 5 A. Test its sensitivity. A 10 % error in $R$ gives $(12-6)/1.32 = 4.55$ A, 9 % low. A 10 % error in $K_e$ gives $(12-6.6)/1.2 = 4.5$ A, 10 % low. Those are tolerable for a stall detector. The problem is the subtraction. Near no-load, say $K_e\omega = 11.4$ V, the true current is $0.6/1.2 = 0.5$ A. A mere 5 % $K_e$ error moves the back-EMF to 11.97 V and the estimate to 0.025 A, a 95 % error. The method amplifies uncertainty in exactly the region where you want a small current, so it is a coarse high-current tool only.

## Filtering and its price

A tempting way to read the average current is an RC filter on the amplifier output. To bring 20 kHz ripple down by 20 dB with a single pole, the corner must be at 2 kHz, so $RC = 80$ µs (for example 1 kΩ and 80 nF). The delay is the same 80 µs, and a hardware overcurrent trip can't wait that long: a short in the winding could add several amps in that time. The standard architecture therefore has two paths. A fast, unfiltered comparator path guards against faults, while a filtered or synchronously sampled path feeds the control loop and the display. Sampling at the PWM midpoint avoids the delay penalty and needs no analog filter at all.

## Verifying on the bench

- **Compare three instruments:** measure the same drive with a DC-capable current probe on the motor lead, a DMM on the supply line, and your shunt-amplifier output. At $D=0.5$ and $I_m = 4$ A, the DMM should read about 2 A while the probe's average reads 4 A. The shunt should agree with whichever location it is in. If it doesn't, you've found a gain or offset error.
- **Look at the ripple:** with the probe on a scope, confirm that the triangle's peak-to-peak value agrees with $VD(1-D)/(Lf_{sw})$ (0.15 A at 12 V, 1 mH, 20 kHz, D = 0.5). A larger ripple means the effective inductance is lower than expected, for example because the motor inductance falls at higher current.
- **Zero offset:** with the driver off, record the ADC counts and compute offset in mA (at 5.4 mA per LSB for the example chain).
- **Kelvin check:** pass 20 A and compare the voltage measured at the amplifier pins with a DMM across the shunt's pads. A difference means the sense traces are picking up copper drop from the high-current path.

- **Battery-powered tools**: high-side shunt on the battery for pack current and state-of-charge.

::: assumptions
The example assumes a brushed DC motor driven by a low-side switch with a flyback diode; half-bridge and H-bridge arrangements have different current paths but the same principles. $I_s \approx D\,I_m$ assumes smooth motor current and steady state. Ripple formula assumes constant back-EMF and a motor dominated by its inductance at the PWM frequency. Accuracy numbers (offset, tempco) are example values. For AC and three-phase drives, current transformers, Rogowski coils and phase shunts are used; the principles of isolation and common-mode handling carry over.

::: followups
### Why does my bench supply show half the current my motor controller reports?
The supply shows average supply current. With PWM duty $D$ the supply provides pulses and the motor inductance with the flyback diode circulates the rest, so $I_s \approx D\,I_m$. At low duty the motor can draw several times what the supply delivers.

### How do you pick the shunt value?
Balance power loss ($I^2R_s$), signal level ($IR_s$ versus offset and noise) and ADC range. Larger $R_s$ gives better resolution and lower relative offset error but burns more power and shifts ground. Pick the largest value whose dissipation and voltage drop are acceptable at maximum current, then set the gain so full-scale current fills the ADC. See [[q0445]] and [[q0446]].

### Can you measure current without any added component?
Yes, indirectly: with a driver IC that has current-mirror sensing, with $R_{DS(on)}$ sensing, or with a model using voltage and speed. Each gives a rough value. For torque control you want a real shunt or Hall sensor.

### What happens if the shunt is too hot?
Its resistance drifts with temperature (tempco), changing the gain, and heat flows into the PCB. If the shunt power approaches its rating, the resistor fails. Use a low-tempco alloy shunt, enough copper area, or a four-terminal part, and derate.

### Harder: your low-side current reading is garbage at low duty cycles but fine at high duty. Why?
The shunt only carries current while the FET is on, and at low duty that on-time can be shorter than the switching ringing, the CSA's settling time and the ADC's acquisition time, so there is no clean window to sample. At high duty the on-time is long and the window is easy. Fixes: enforce a minimum on-time (and accept the loss of very low duty), sample on the off-time with an in-line shunt, use a CSA with fast settling, or reconstruct the current from a model and the last good sample.
:::
