::: basics
### In plain words
The inductor equation is $v = L\,di/dt$. The voltage across an inductor equals its inductance times how fast the current is changing. Turn it around and it says the current is the running total of the voltage you have applied. A constant voltage makes the current ramp up steadily. A zero voltage makes the current hold steady.

### Picture it
Think of driving a car. The gas pedal is the voltage, and your speed is the current. Holding the pedal steady does not hold your speed steady. It makes your speed rise at a steady rate. Take your foot off and you keep your speed. The inductance is like the car's mass: a heavier car needs more push for the same change in speed. An ideal inductor has no friction, which real cars do.

### Words you need
- **Rate of change ($di/dt$)**: how many amps per second the current is rising or falling.
- **Inductance (L)**: the constant that links voltage and the rate of change of current. See [[q0039]].
- **Running total (integral)**: the current is the sum of all the voltage applied so far, divided by $L$.
- **Ramp**: a straight-line rise, which is what a constant voltage gives.
- **Volt-second balance**: in steady state, the up-ramps and down-ramps of current cancel out.

### The simplest example
Put 10 V across a 1 mH inductor. The current rises at $di/dt = V/L = 10/0.001 = 10{,}000$ A/s, which is 1 A every 100 µs. Now set the voltage to 0 V and the current stays where it is, because $di/dt = 0$. At DC an ideal inductor is just a wire. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the integral form and shows how a buck converter (a circuit that efficiently steps a voltage down) uses these ramps. See also [[q0004]].
:::

Whether you know the inductor's defining relationship **in both directions** and can actually use it:

2. Integral form: $i_L(t) = i_L(t_0) + \dfrac{1}{L}\displaystyle\int_{t_0}^{t} v_L\,d\tau$, and why the initial-condition term matters.
3. What it predicts: constant voltage gives a linear current ramp, the current can't jump, and at DC steady state the inductor voltage is zero.
4. Can you write and solve the circuit ODE for a simple RL circuit, and recognise volt-second balance in a switching converter?
:::

::: why
This one equation explains how every switching power supply works (a fixed voltage across the inductor makes the current ramp linearly, [[q0188]]), why turning off an inductive load creates a voltage spike ([[q0144]]), and how RL and RLC circuits respond to steps ([[q0009]]). The integral form is what you actually use when reading converter waveforms on a scope: inductor current is the running integral of inductor voltage. Interviewers ask it to see whether you can go beyond phasors and reason in the time domain, which is where power electronics lives.
:::

::: concepts
- **Faraday's law**: $v = d\lambda/dt$, with flux linkage $\lambda = Li$ for a linear inductor ([[q0039]]).
- **Passive sign convention**: current enters the terminal marked +; then $v = +L\,di/dt$.
- **Integration and initial conditions**: an inductor's current depends on its whole voltage history, summarized by $i(t_0)$.
- **KVL**: combining the element laws around a loop gives the circuit's differential equation ([[q0004]]).
- **First-order response**: an RL circuit's solution is an exponential with $\tau = L/R$ ([[q0044]]).
- **Duality with the capacitor**: $i_C = C\,dv_C/dt$ ([[q0025]]) is the same equation with $v$ and $i$ swapped.
:::

::: answer
"The differential equation for an inductor is $v = L\,di/dt$: the voltage across it is the inductance times the rate of change of current through it, with current flowing into the plus terminal.

You can also flip it around into integral form: the current is the initial current plus one over L times the integral of the voltage, $i(t) = i(t_0) + \frac1L\int v\,dt$. I find that form more intuitive. It says the inductor current is the running total of the voltage you've applied, so if you put a constant voltage across it, the current ramps linearly at $V/L$ amps per second.

A few things fall out of it. If the current is constant, the voltage is zero, so at DC an ideal inductor is a short. The current can't jump instantly, because that would need infinite di/dt and therefore infinite voltage; that's the continuity condition. And if you try to interrupt the current fast, you get a big voltage spike.

A practical example is a buck converter. When the switch is on, the inductor sees $V_{in} - V_{out}$ and the current ramps up; when it's off, it sees $-V_{out}$ and ramps down. In steady state the up-ramp and down-ramp must cancel, so the average inductor voltage is zero. That's volt-second balance, and it's how you derive $V_{out} = D\cdot V_{in}$."
:::

::: technical
**Derivative form** (passive sign convention, linear inductor):
$$v_L(t) = L\,\frac{di_L(t)}{dt}$$

**Integral form:**
$$i_L(t) = i_L(t_0) + \frac{1}{L}\int_{t_0}^{t} v_L(\tau)\,d\tau$$

**General (nonlinear) form:** $v_L = \dfrac{d\lambda}{dt}$ where $\lambda = N\Phi$. For a saturating core $\lambda(i)$ is nonlinear and $v = L_{inc}(i)\,di/dt$ with incremental inductance $L_{inc} = d\lambda/di$, which falls in saturation ([[q0046]]).

**Consequences.**
- Constant $V$ → linear ramp: $\Delta i = V\,\Delta t / L$ (5 V for 2 µs on 10 µH → 1 A).
- Constant $i$ → $v_L = 0$: short at DC steady state.
- Finite $v$ → $i_L$ continuous ([[q0043]]).
- Energy: $p = vi = L i\,di/dt$ → $E = \tfrac12 Li^2$.

**Series RL with a step $V_s$ at $t=0$:** KVL gives
$$L\frac{di}{dt} + Ri = V_s,\qquad i(t) = \frac{V_s}{R} + \left(i(0) - \frac{V_s}{R}\right)e^{-t/\tau},\quad \tau = \frac{L}{R}$$
Initial slope is $V_s/L$ (from $i(0)=0$); final current is $V_s/R$.

**Series RLC:** $L\,\dfrac{di}{dt} + Ri + \dfrac{1}{C}\displaystyle\int i\,dt = v_s$, i.e. $\ddot q + \dfrac{R}{L}\dot q + \dfrac{1}{LC}q = \dfrac{v_s}{L}$, with $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, $\zeta = \dfrac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$.

**Volt-second balance.** In periodic steady state, $i_L(T) = i_L(0)$, so $\int_0^T v_L\,dt = 0$: the average inductor voltage is zero. For a buck in CCM: $(V_{in}-V_{out})DT = V_{out}(1-D)T \Rightarrow V_{out} = DV_{in}$, and ripple $\Delta I_L = \dfrac{(V_{in}-V_{out})D}{Lf_{sw}}$. Example: 12 V → 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz gives $D = 0.275$ and $\Delta I_L \approx 1.02$ A peak-to-peak.
:::

::: long
"The defining equation is $v = L\,di/dt$. It comes from Faraday's law: voltage is the rate of change of flux linkage, and for a linear inductor flux linkage is $L$ times current. The sign assumes current flows into the plus terminal.

I like to also write the integral form, because it's the one I actually reason with: $i(t)$ equals the initial current plus one over L times the integral of the voltage from then to now. The current is like a bucket that integrates voltage. The initial-current term is important; it's the energy already stored in the field, and you need it to solve any transient.

Three consequences come out immediately. One, if the voltage is constant, the current ramps linearly at $V/L$. Five volts across 10 microhenries for two microseconds gives you one amp. Two, if the current is constant, the voltage is zero, so at DC steady state an ideal inductor is a short circuit. Three, the current can't change instantaneously, because a step in current would need infinite voltage. That's why opening a switch on a relay coil gives you a huge spike.

To analyse a circuit, I combine it with KVL. For a resistor and inductor in series with a step $V_s$: $L\,di/dt + Ri = V_s$. The solution is $i = (V_s/R)(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ with $\tau = L/R$. The current starts at zero with slope $V_s/L$ and settles at $V_s/R$.

The place this really pays off is switching converters. In a buck, the inductor sees $V_{in} - V_{out}$ when the switch is on and minus $V_{out}$ when it's off, so the current is a triangle wave. In steady state the current at the end of each cycle equals the start, so the integral of inductor voltage over a period has to be zero. That's volt-second balance, and solving it gives $V_{out} = D\cdot V_{in}$ and the ripple current, which for 12 to 3.3 volts with 4.7 microhenries at 500 kilohertz is about one amp peak to peak."
:::

::: terms
- **Inductor law**: $v_L = L\,di_L/dt$, the element's constitutive relation.
- **Integral form**: $i_L(t) = i_L(t_0) + \frac1L\int v_L\,d\tau$; current as the accumulated volt-seconds.
- **Initial condition**: $i_L(t_0)$, the current (stored energy) at the start of the analysis.
- **Passive sign convention**: current enters the + terminal of the element; makes absorbed power $vi$ positive.
- **Time constant (τ)**: $L/R$ for an RL circuit; time to cover 63.2% of the change.
- **Volt-second balance**: in periodic steady state, the average voltage across an inductor is zero.
- **Incremental inductance**: $d\lambda/di$, the local slope of flux linkage vs. current; drops in saturation.
- **Flux linkage (λ)**: $N\Phi$; its time derivative is the inductor voltage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** writes $v = L\,di/dt$.

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the sign convention and gives **both** the derivative and the integral forms, explaining the initial-condition term.
- Draws out the three consequences: linear ramp under constant voltage, short at DC, current continuity.
- Writes the KVL ODE for a series RL and gives its solution with $\tau = L/R$ (and maybe the RLC form).
- Applies it to a **real circuit**: buck converter ripple, $\Delta I = (V_{in}-V_{out})DT/L$, and volt-second balance.
- Mentions the general form $v = d\lambda/dt$ and that $L$ is not constant when the core saturates.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Writing $i = L\,dv/dt$.** That's the capacitor equation with L substituted. For an inductor, *voltage* depends on the derivative of *current*.
- **Dropping the initial condition.** $i(t) = \frac1L\int v\,dt$ without $i(t_0)$ is only right if the inductor started at zero current. In a converter the current never starts at zero.
- **Sign errors.** If you define current flowing *out* of the + terminal, the equation becomes $v = -L\,di/dt$. Pick a convention, mark it on the drawing, and stick to it.
- **Assuming the voltage must be continuous.** It's the current that's continuous; the voltage across an inductor can jump instantly (e.g. from 0 to $V_s$ at a step).
- **Applying $v = L\,di/dt$ with a fixed L in saturation.** In saturation $L_{inc}$ drops and di/dt rises sharply for the same voltage.
:::

::: deep
## Where the equation comes from

Faraday's law says the voltage induced around a loop equals the rate of change of the flux linkage through it. For an inductor's terminals (passive sign convention):

$$v_L = \frac{d\lambda}{dt}$$

For a linear inductor, $\lambda = L\,i_L$ with constant $L$, so

$$\boxed{v_L = L\,\frac{di_L}{dt}}$$

Figure: Current i_L enters the terminal marked +. With that choice, v_L = +L di_L/dt and the power vi is the power absorbed by the inductor.

Integrating both sides from a starting time $t_0$:

$$\boxed{i_L(t) = i_L(t_0) + \frac{1}{L}\int_{t_0}^{t} v_L(\tau)\,d\tau}$$

The integral form says the inductor current is the **accumulated volt-seconds** divided by $L$. The initial-condition term is the memory of everything that happened before $t_0$. That memory is energy, $\tfrac12 L\,i_L(t_0)^2$, sitting in the magnetic field.

## Three consequences you should be able to say instantly

**1. Constant voltage → linear current ramp.** If $v_L = V$ is constant, $i_L(t) = i_L(t_0) + \dfrac{V}{L}(t - t_0)$. Five volts across 10 µH for 2 µs adds exactly 1 A.

**2. Constant current → zero voltage.** If $di_L/dt = 0$, $v_L = 0$. At DC steady state an ideal inductor is a short circuit ([[q0044]]).

**3. Current is continuous.** A jump in $i_L$ means $di/dt \to \infty$ and thus infinite voltage. With any finite voltage available, $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$ ([[q0043]]). The voltage, however, can jump.

### Volt-second balance

In periodic steady state the current returns to the same value each cycle, $i_L(T) = i_L(0)$. Plug that into the integral form:

$$0 = \frac{1}{L}\int_0^T v_L\,dt \quad\Rightarrow\quad \langle v_L \rangle = 0$$

For the buck: $(V_{in}-V_{out})\,DT = V_{out}(1-D)\,T$, so $V_{out} = D\,V_{in}$. The ripple is the up-ramp:

$$\Delta I_L = \frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})\,D}{L\,f_{sw}} = \frac{8.7 \times 0.275}{4.7\,\mu\text{H}\times 500\text{ kHz}} \approx 1.02\text{ A}$$

That's the inductor ODE doing real design work. See [[q0188]] and [[q0212]].

KVL: $V_s = Ri + L\,\dfrac{di}{dt}$. Rearranged into standard form:

$$\frac{di}{dt} + \frac{R}{L}\,i = \frac{V_s}{L}$$

With $i(0) = 0$ (continuity: it was zero before the switch closed), the solution is

$$i(t) = \frac{V_s}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R}$$

With 12 V, 6 Ω and 3 mH: $\tau = 0.5$ ms, final current 2 A, current at $t = \tau$ is 1.26 A, and the initial slope is $V_s/L = 4000$ A/s. The inductor voltage $v_L = V_s e^{-t/\tau}$ jumps to 12 V at $t = 0^+$ and decays to zero. Note which quantity jumped and which didn't.

## The second-order case

Add a capacitor and KVL becomes

$$L\frac{di}{dt} + Ri + \frac{1}{C}\int i\,dt = v_s(t)$$

Differentiating once (or writing it in charge $q$, with $i = \dot q$) gives the classic second-order ODE

$$\ddot q + \frac{R}{L}\dot q + \frac{1}{LC}q = \frac{v_s}{L}$$

with natural frequency $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$ and damping ratio $\zeta = \tfrac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$. The inductor's ODE contributes the "inertia" term. See [[q0009]].

## Worked example: the inductor equation sets the load-step response

In the buck converter from the page (12 V to 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz, with a 47 µF output capacitor), what happens when the load jumps from 2 A to 4 A? The inductor current cannot jump, so for the controller's best effort the current has to ramp, and the slope is limited by the voltage you can put across the inductor. The equation $di/dt=v_L/L$ gives two different limits:

- **Load increase.** Even with the high-side switch on 100 % of the time, the inductor sees $V_{in}-V_{out}=8.7$ V, so the maximum slew is $8.7/4.7\ \mu\text{H}=1.85$ A/µs. Catching up 2 A takes $2/1.85=1.08$ µs.
- **Load decrease.** With the high-side switch fully off, the inductor sees only $-V_{out}=-3.3$ V, so the slew is $-0.70$ A/µs. Falling by 2 A takes $2/0.70=2.85$ µs.

While the inductor current lags the load, the output capacitor supplies (or absorbs) the difference. The charge is the triangle $\tfrac12\Delta I\,t$: 1.08 µC on the step up and 2.85 µC on the step down. The voltage excursion is $\Delta V=Q/C$:

- Step up: $1.08\ \mu\text{C}/47\ \mu\text{F}=23$ mV droop.
- Step down: $2.85\ \mu\text{C}/47\ \mu\text{F}=61$ mV overshoot.

So the buck converter responds to a load release more slowly and with a larger excursion than to a load step, because there is less voltage available to unwind the current than to build it. The asymmetry grows as the output voltage gets small relative to the input: at 1.0 V out from 12 V the up-slew is more than ten times the down-slew. This is the model that sits behind the output-capacitor sizing in a power stage. It is an upper bound on how well any control loop can do, since it assumes the controller saturates the duty cycle at once and ignores the capacitor's ESR and ESL.

## Worked example: the DCM boundary from the ripple equation

The ripple $\Delta I_L=1.02$ A tells you when the converter stops behaving as a continuous-conduction converter. The inductor current never touches zero as long as the average current exceeds half the ripple: $I_{load}>\Delta I_L/2=0.51$ A. Below that, the current ramps down to zero before the cycle ends, the inductor current is discontinuous, and $V_{out}=DV_{in}$ no longer holds. For a fixed load you can express this as a critical inductance $L_{crit}=(1-D)V_{out}/(2f_{sw}I_{load})$. At 0.5 A that is 4.79 µH, almost exactly the chosen 4.7 µH, so this design is right on the edge at half an amp. That is why ripple current is often chosen at 20 to 40 % of the maximum load current: it keeps the converter in CCM down to a light load of 10 to 20 % of maximum.

## What the DCR does to volt-second balance

Real inductors have winding resistance, so the volt-second equation has an extra term, $v_L=L\,di/dt+R_{DC}i$ across the part. In the buck example a 20 mΩ DCR at 2 A drops $0.02\times2=40$ mV. Averaging over a cycle, $\langle v_L\rangle=0$ applies to the ideal inductance, so the duty cycle must rise to cover the drop: $D=(3.3+0.04)/12=0.278$ instead of 0.275. The change is small, but it is a real loss ($I^2R=80$ mW), and it is why a tiny difference between calculated and measured duty cycle is normal.

## Solving the ODE numerically: why the time step matters

Circuit simulators solve $L\,di/dt=V-Ri$ step by step. The simplest method (forward Euler) is $i_{n+1}=i_n+\Delta t\,(V-Ri_n)/L$. Try it on the RL example above ($\tau=0.5$ ms, exact $i(2\text{ ms})=1.963$ A):

| Time step $\Delta t$ | Euler result at 2 ms | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 ms ($\tau/5$) | 1.977 A | within 0.7 % |
| 0.25 ms ($\tau/2$) | 1.992 A | decent |
| 0.5 ms ($\tau$) | 2.000 A | jumps to the final value in the first step |
| 1.0 ms ($2\tau$) | 0 A | flips between 4 A and 0 A forever: the stability limit |

The numerical method is stable only for $\Delta t<2\tau$ (at exactly $2\tau$ the error oscillates without decaying, and beyond it it grows), and its accuracy is acceptable only when the step is a small fraction of the time constant. Real simulators use implicit or trapezoidal methods that stay stable for large steps, and variable step sizes that shrink at switching edges. If a simulation of a switching converter shows ringing at exactly the step frequency, suspect the solver before you suspect the circuit.

## Verifying on the bench

1. **Ramp slope test.** Apply a known voltage across the inductor through a MOSFET for a few microseconds, with a small sense resistor (for example 50 mΩ, which gives 250 mV at 5 A) in series. The scope trace of the sense voltage should be a straight line with slope $V/L$. For a 10 µH part at 5 V that is 0.5 A/µs, so 2.5 A after 5 µs.
2. **Find saturation.** Lengthen the pulse. When the slope of the current starts to increase, the incremental inductance $L_{inc}=V/(di/dt)$ is falling. The current at which the slope begins to rise by about 10 to 30 % (the definition varies between vendors) is the practical saturation current.
3. **Check volt-second balance.** On a running converter, measure the inductor voltage $v_L = v_{SW} - v_{out}$ (two probes and a math subtraction, or a differential probe) and integrate it with the scope's math channel; over each period the integral should return to the same value. Integrating the switch node alone gives $V_{out}T$ per cycle, not zero. A drift in the integral means the converter is not in steady state or that the measurement has a DC offset.
4. **Watch for probe errors.** The ground lead of a standard probe adds inductance and ringing at the switch node. Use a short ground spring when you want to see the actual $v_L$ edge.

## When L is not a constant

The general law is $v = d\lambda/dt$. If the core saturates, $\lambda(i)$ flattens and

$$v = \frac{d\lambda}{di}\,\frac{di}{dt} = L_{inc}(i)\,\frac{di}{dt}$$

As $L_{inc}$ collapses, the same applied voltage produces a much steeper current slope. That's the "runaway" current spike of a saturated inductor ([[q0046]]). Similarly, if the inductance changes mechanically (a solenoid plunger moving), $v = L\,di/dt + i\,dL/dt$, and the second term is a back-EMF used in sensorless position detection.

> Say the integral form out loud. "Inductor current is the integral of inductor voltage, so a square-wave voltage makes a triangle-wave current" is the sentence that makes buck converter questions easy.

::: applications
- **Buck/boost ripple calculation**: $\Delta I_L = V_L\,\Delta t/L$ during each switching interval sets inductor selection ([[q0212]]).
- **Volt-second balance**: deriving conversion ratios of buck ($D$), boost ($1/(1-D)$) and buck-boost converters.
- **Inductive kick**: interrupting current fast gives $v = L\,di/dt$ spikes in relay and solenoid drivers; flyback diodes limit them ([[q0144]]).
- **Current-mode control**: the controller senses the inductor current ramp, whose slope is literally $v_L/L$ ([[q0195]]).
- **Motor current dynamics**: a DC motor winding follows $V = Ri + L\,di/dt + k\omega$, setting how fast torque responds.
- **Inductive sensors**: a moving plunger changes $L$, and the $i\,dL/dt$ term reveals position.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear inductor**: constant $L$, independent of current, frequency and temperature. False near saturation; use $v = d\lambda/dt$.
- **Passive sign convention**: current enters the + terminal. With the opposite reference direction the equation picks up a minus sign.
- **Lumped element**: the inductor is small compared with the wavelength, so its current is the same at both terminals.
- **Ideal inductor**: no winding resistance or capacitance. A real one adds $Ri$ (DCR) in series, which is why a real inductor isn't a perfect short at DC.
- **Steady state for volt-second balance**: it only applies once the circuit is periodic; during startup or load transients the average inductor voltage is not zero, which is exactly what changes the current.
:::

### What happens to the current if you apply 5 V across a 10 µH inductor for 3 µs, then 0 V?
It ramps at $5/10\ \mu\text{H} = 0.5$ A/µs, reaching 1.5 A at 3 µs (from zero). Then with 0 V across it, $di/dt = 0$ and an ideal inductor keeps carrying 1.5 A forever (in practice it decays through DCR with $\tau = L/R_{DC}$).

### How does the inductor equation relate to the capacitor equation?
They're duals: $v_L = L\,di_L/dt$ and $i_C = C\,dv_C/dt$. Swap $v \leftrightarrow i$ and $L \leftrightarrow C$. An inductor integrates voltage into current; a capacitor integrates current into voltage. Continuity follows the integrated variable: current for L, voltage for C. See [[q0025]].

### Derive the boost converter's conversion ratio using the inductor equation.
On-time: the inductor sees $V_{in}$ for $DT$. Off-time: it sees $V_{in} - V_{out}$ (negative) for $(1-D)T$. Volt-second balance: $V_{in}DT + (V_{in}-V_{out})(1-D)T = 0$, so $V_{in} = V_{out}(1-D)$ and $V_{out} = V_{in}/(1-D)$.

### Why does a real inductor's current eventually stop changing at DC instead of ramping forever?
Its winding resistance. With $V$ applied, the real equation is $V = R_{DC}\,i + L\,di/dt$, so the current approaches $V/R_{DC}$ with $\tau = L/R_{DC}$. For a 10 µH, 20 mΩ inductor across 5 V that would be 250 A after a few 0.5 ms time constants, so in practice the core saturates or something else limits it first. That's why you never put a raw inductor directly across a supply.

### What if L depends on current? How does the equation change and what does it predict near saturation?
Use $v = d\lambda/dt = L_{inc}(i)\,di/dt$ with $L_{inc} = d\lambda/di$. Near saturation $L_{inc}$ might fall from 10 µH to 2 µH, so under the same 8.7 V the slope jumps from 0.87 A/µs to 4.35 A/µs. The current waveform bends upward into a sharp spike at the end of the on-time, which is the tell-tale scope signature of a saturating inductor.
