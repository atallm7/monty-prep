::: basics
### In plain words
A spinning motor is also a generator. The voltage it generates, called back-EMF, is proportional to its speed. So if you can read that voltage by itself, you have a speed sensor with no extra part. The trouble is that while current flows, other voltage drops hide it. The usual fix is to measure when the current has stopped. For a solenoid the matching effect is weaker and depends on the plunger's position.

### Picture it
Think of a bicycle dynamo that lights a lamp. Pedal faster and the lamp gets brighter, because the dynamo makes more voltage at higher speed. A motor shaft acts like that dynamo. To read it cleanly, you disconnect the power for an instant and measure what the spinning shaft produces on its own.

### Words you need
- **Back-EMF**: the voltage a spinning motor generates, $E = K_e \omega$. It is proportional to speed.
- **$K_e$**: the back-EMF constant, in volts per rad/s of speed (radians per second; one turn is 2π radians).
- **Off-time sampling**: reading the terminal voltage while a PWM drive (one that switches on and off rapidly) is off and the current has died away.
- **Freewheel (flyback) diode**: carries the winding's current after the switch opens. See [[q0340]].

### The simplest example
A motor has $K_e = 0.02$ V per rad/s. At 3000 rpm, which is 314 rad/s, it generates $0.02 \times 314 = 6.28$ V. Turn the PWM switch off. After the freewheel current dies, about 0.13 ms for a small example motor, the open terminal voltage is that 6.28 V. Two resistors scale it down for the microcontroller's ADC (its voltage-to-number converter), and you divide by $K_e$ to get speed. At 300 rpm it is only 0.63 V, which is why the method struggles at low speed.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the on-time alternative, which subtracts the winding's own voltage drop, hardware details, copper's temperature drift, sensorless brushless motors, and the solenoid version.
:::

Whether you understand back-EMF as a *measurement*, not just a nuisance. The interviewer wants the equation ($V_{term} = IR + L\,di/dt + K_e\omega$), the idea that you must make the other terms vanish or subtract them, and the practical solution for PWM drives: sample the terminal voltage during the off-time once the current has stopped. They are also probing hardware awareness (dividers, ADC sampling time, blanking after the flyback transient) and honesty about limits (low speed, temperature, noise).
:::

::: why
Speed feedback without a tachometer or encoder saves cost, wires and a failure point, and is the basis of sensorless BLDC drives and of cheap brushed-motor speed control in drills, fans and toys. For solenoids the same physical coupling gives plunger-motion detection. Because it needs no extra hardware, it appears in a huge number of products, so interviewers use it to see whether you can reason about an electrical signal that mixes several effects.
:::

::: concepts
- **Back-EMF**: voltage generated by the rotating armature, $E = K_e\omega$. See [[q0341]].
- **Armature equation**: $V = IR + L\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t + E$.
- **PWM off-time sampling**: with the drive switched off the terminals reflect the back-EMF if current is zero.
- **Flyback/freewheel**: after turn-off the inductive current continues through a diode and clamps the terminal voltage until it decays. See [[q0340]].
- **IR compensation**: use measured current and a known $R$ to subtract the resistive drop during on-time.
- **Solenoid motional EMF**: $i\,\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x\,v$. See [[q0406]].
:::

::: answer
"A motor is also a generator, so its back-EMF is proportional to speed: E equals Ke times omega. The terminal voltage is IR plus L di/dt plus E, so if I can make the first two terms disappear, the terminal voltage is the speed.

With a PWM drive the trick is to measure during the off-time. When the switch turns off, the inductive current freewheels through the diode and the terminal is clamped for a bit. The back-EMF itself helps kill that current, so it's short, a fraction of a millisecond for a small motor. Once that current has decayed to zero, the winding carries no current, there's no IR or L di/dt, and the open terminal voltage is just the back-EMF. I put a resistive divider into an ADC, wait out the blanking time, sample, and divide by Ke. For a 12 V motor with Ke of 0.02 V per rad per second, 3,000 rpm gives 6.3 volts.

If the current doesn't reach zero at high duty, I can measure during the on-time instead and subtract: E equals V minus I times R, with a shunt for I. That depends on knowing R, which rises with temperature, about 0.4% per kelvin for copper.

The limits are low speed, where E is tiny and noisy, and temperature. In a solenoid the equivalent term is i times dL/dx times velocity, and it shows up as a dip in the current. Same principle, harder to extract because L depends on position."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** Brushed PM motor with armature resistance $R$, inductance $L$, constant $K_e$ (V·s/rad):
$$v_t = iR + L\frac{\mathrm{d}i}{\mathrm{d}t} + K_e\omega$$

**Method A: open-circuit sampling during PWM off-time (preferred).** With the bridge or switch off and the motor current decayed to zero, $i = 0$ and $\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t = 0$, so $v_t = K_e\omega$ exactly (brush voltage drop, about 0.5 to 1 V per brush pair on carbon brushes, is also present when current flows but vanishes at $i=0$). The motor terminal must be high-impedance (both switches open, "coast"). Required: wait for the freewheel current to reach zero. While the diode conducts, $L\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t = -(V_f + iR + E)$: the back-EMF *adds* to the decay, so the time from $I_0$ to zero is $t_{dec} = \frac{L}{R}\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{I_0R}{V_f + E}\right)$. For $L = 1$ mH, $R = 1\ \Omega$, $I_0 = 1$ A, $V_f = 0.7$ V and $E = 6.28$ V this is 0.13 ms; at low speed ($E = 0.63$ V) it grows to 0.56 ms. Add margin for ringing. A 20 kHz PWM has only 50 µs per period, so there may be no usable window inside one off-time: use a **lower PWM frequency**, an **occasional extended off window** (skip pulses), or Method B.

**Method B: on-time subtraction.** $E = V_{supply}\,-\,i\,(R + R_{on}) - L\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$. Using the average over the PWM period gives $\bar{E} \approx D\,V_{bus} - \bar{I}R$ (the $L\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$ term averages to zero in steady state). So $\omega = (D V_{bus} - \bar I R)/K_e$. Needs $\bar I$ and $R$. Resistance changes with temperature: copper has $\alpha \approx 0.0039$ /K, so a rise from 25 °C to 105 °C increases $R$ by 31%, and the $IR$ term (e.g. 2 A × 1 Ω = 2 V) by about 0.63 V, which is 10% of 6.3 V.

**Method C: Wheatstone bridge compensation.** Build a bridge from motor ($R$, $L$) and a matched network ($R_x$, $L_x$) so that the bridge output cancels the $IR$ term; historically used in analogue drives.

**Sampling hardware.** Divider from the motor terminal (say 12 V max) to the ADC range (3.3 V): ratio about 3.8:1 (for example 10 kΩ over 3.6 kΩ), with a filter capacitor (the divider's Thevenin resistance times ADC sampling capacitor sets accuracy; see [[q0008]]). Resolution: with a 12-bit ADC and a 3.78:1 divider each count is 3.04 mV at the motor, i.e. 0.152 rad/s per count or 1.45 rpm per count with $K_e = 0.02$.

**Brushless (sensorless) motors.** In a 6-step drive one phase floats each sector; its voltage crosses the half-bus level at the back-EMF zero crossing, 30 electrical degrees before the next commutation. Timing the zero crossings gives speed and position, but the signal vanishes at zero speed so the motor must be started open-loop ([[q0364]]).
:::

"A motor is also a generator, so when the shaft turns the armature produces a back-EMF equal to Ke times omega. The terminal voltage is I times R, plus L times di/dt, plus that back-EMF. If I can get the current to zero, the first two terms vanish and the terminal voltage is the speed.

With a PWM drive, the natural way is to measure during the off-time. I turn off the switch and the inductive current freewheels through the diode briefly (the back-EMF helps drive it to zero), clamping the terminal to about a diode drop below ground. When it has decayed, the winding is open-circuit and what I see is the back-EMF. So I take a resistive divider from the motor terminal to an ADC pin, wait out a blanking time, sample, and divide by Ke. For a 12 volt motor with Ke of 0.02, 3,000 rpm gives about 6.3 volts. The practical issue is that the decay plus ringing takes a few hundred microseconds, which may not fit inside the off-time of a 20 kHz PWM, so I'd skip a PWM pulse now and then to open a measurement window, or use a lower PWM frequency.

The alternative is to use the on-time: E equals the applied voltage minus I times R. That needs a current sense and a known resistance, and the resistance rises about 0.4% per kelvin for copper, so a hot motor would read the wrong speed by around 10 percent unless I compensate.

The main limitations are low speed, where the back-EMF is millivolts and swamped by noise and brush noise, and temperature drift of both R and the magnet strength.

For a solenoid the same term exists as i times dL/dx times velocity, but L depends on position and the signal appears as a dip in the current, so it's used for arrival detection rather than a precise speed reading."
:::

::: terms
- **Back-EMF ($E = K_e\omega$)**: voltage generated by motion in the field; proportional to speed.
- **Armature resistance $R$**: winding resistance; its drop must be removed in on-time sensing.
- **Blanking time**: delay after switching before sampling, to let the flyback transient die out.
- **Freewheel (flyback) diode**: carries inductive current after turn-off. See [[q0340]].
- **Coast / high-Z**: both switches open so the terminal voltage can be measured.
- **Motional EMF**: the solenoid equivalent, $i\,\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x\,v$ ([[q0406]]).
- **Zero-crossing detection**: sensorless BLDC technique using the floating phase's back-EMF.
- **$K_e$**: back-EMF constant, V per rad/s; equal to $K_t$ in SI units.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **Reading the terminal voltage right after turn-off.** The inductive current is still flowing through the freewheel diode and clamps the node near ground. You are measuring the diode, not speed.
- **Ignoring the $IR$ drop in on-time measurements.** At 2 A and 1 Ω you are off by 2 V of a 6 V signal.
- **Assuming $R$ is constant.** Copper resistance rises about 0.4% per kelvin; a hot motor reads wrong unless compensated.
- **Feeding 12 V directly to an ADC pin** or using a divider with high impedance and no sampling capacitor. See [[q0008]] for loading and source impedance.
- **Trusting back-EMF near stall.** The signal is tiny; use it for speed above roughly a tenth of rated speed, or combine with another sensor.
- **Forgetting that the motor must be high-impedance during sensing.** If the bridge is braking or driving, the terminals are forced and there is nothing to measure.
:::

::: deep
## The physics: a motor is a generator

A conductor of the armature moving through the magnetic field of the stator magnets has an EMF induced in it. Summed over the winding it is proportional to speed: $E = K_e\omega$, where in SI units $K_e$ (V·s/rad) equals the torque constant $K_t$ (N·m/A). For a motor with $K_e = 0.02$ V·s/rad, 3,000 rpm is $\omega = 314$ rad/s and $E = 6.28$ V.

The terminal equation is the electrical side of the motor model:

$$v_t = i R + L\frac{\mathrm{d}i}{\mathrm{d}t} + K_e\omega$$

To measure $\omega$ you need $v_t$ and the first two terms, or a moment when they are zero. That is the whole art.

Figure: Switch Q₁ drives the motor from the battery; D₁ freewheels the motor current when Q₁ turns off. A divider from the motor terminal to the ADC lets the MCU read the terminal voltage. During Q₁-off, once the diode current has stopped, that voltage equals the back-EMF.

What the terminal voltage does around one PWM cycle:

- **On-time**: $v_t \approx V_{bat}$ (minus the switch drop).
- **Just after turn-off**: the inductor current continues through $D_1$, so $v_t \approx -V_f$ (about −0.7 V; the divider will read zero or clip).
- **When the current reaches zero** (discontinuous conduction): the diode turns off, the node floats and the voltage jumps to $E$ with some ringing from the motor's inductance and the node capacitance.
- **If the current never reaches zero before the next pulse** (continuous conduction, typical at high duty), you never see $E$ at the terminal.

Figure: The window to sample (right) only exists after the freewheel current has decayed. With L/R = 1 ms and E = 6.28 V the wait is about 0.13 ms, so the off-time must be longer than this wait plus the ringing, with time left over to sample. Real waveforms have ringing at the transition and a gradual (not instantaneous) approach to E.

**Timing numbers.** After turn-off, with the diode conducting, $L\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t = -(V_f + iR + E)$. Note the sign: the back-EMF opposes the current that was driving the motor, so it *helps* the current fall. Integrating, the time to reach zero is

$$t_{dec} = \frac{L}{R}\ln\!\left(1 + \frac{I_0 R}{V_f + E}\right)$$

With $L = 1$ mH, $R = 1\ \Omega$, $I_0 = 1$ A, $V_f = 0.7$ V: at 3,000 rpm ($E = 6.28$ V) $t_{dec} = 0.13$ ms; at 300 rpm ($E = 0.63$ V) $t_{dec} = 0.56$ ms. If the motor were stalled ($E = 0$) the current would follow the plain $L/R$ decay. So the blanking time is set by the *slow* case, and you should still check the waveform on a scope, since ringing at the moment the diode turns off can last longer.

## On-time measurement

During the on-time (or averaged over the PWM period) with duty $D$ and bus voltage $V_{bus}$:
$$\bar v_t = D V_{bus} = \bar I R + K_e\omega\ \Rightarrow\ \omega = \frac{D V_{bus} - \bar I R}{K_e}$$
Pros: always available, no blanking, works at high PWM frequency. Cons: needs a current sense ([[q0395]]), $R$ estimate and switch drop; at light load $\bar I R$ is small so errors in $R$ matter little, at heavy load they dominate.

Example: $V_{bus} = 12$ V, $D = 0.6$, $\bar I = 0.5$ A, $R = 1\ \Omega$: $\omega = (7.2 - 0.5)/0.02 = 335$ rad/s = 3,199 rpm. If the winding is 80 K hotter, $R$ is 31% bigger: the actual $IR$ drop is 0.66 V instead of 0.5 V, so you would over-estimate speed by $0.16/0.02 = 8$ rad/s (2.4%) without compensation. At 2 A the same effect is 4x larger.

## Practical hardware notes

- **Divider and ADC**: choose a ratio to place 12 V at 3.3 V. Keep $R_a\parallel R_b$ small enough for the ADC's source impedance limit; add a capacitor if needed ([[q0008]]). The 10 kΩ / 3.6 kΩ pair gives a ratio of 3.78:1, so 12 V becomes 3.17 V, leaving headroom for a bus up to about 12.5 V.
- **Sampling time**: the motor terminal is noisy (brush arcing, ringing). Average several samples and discard outliers.
- **Resolution**: with a 12-bit ADC and the 3.78:1 divider, one LSB at the terminal is 3.04 mV, or 1.45 rpm for $K_e = 0.02$. The accuracy is far worse than that: brush noise, $K_e$ tolerance (±5% is common for cheap motors) and temperature dominate.
- **Brush drop**: when the motor current is not zero, brush contact adds 0.5 to 1 V (vendor-dependent). At this $K_e$ every 0.1 V of unaccounted drop is $0.1/0.02 = 5$ rad/s, about 48 rpm, so an uncompensated brush drop in on-time sensing is a 240 to 480 rpm error. Off-time sampling at zero current avoids it.

## Sensorless BLDC

In a six-step drive each phase pair conducts for 60° while the third is floating. The floating phase voltage equals the neutral-point voltage plus its own back-EMF, so it crosses half the bus voltage when its back-EMF crosses zero. The zero crossing marks a rotor angle, and the commutation happens 30 electrical degrees later. This both commutates the motor and gives speed. At zero or very low speed the back-EMF is too small to detect, so the drive starts open-loop (forced commutation) until the signal is large enough ([[q0364]]).

## The solenoid version

For a solenoid the voltage equation is $v_c = iR + L\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t + i\,(\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x)\,v$. The speed voltage $e_m = i\,(\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x)\,v$ is the analogue of $K_e\omega$, but three differences make it harder:

1. $\mathrm{d}L/\mathrm{d}x$ depends on position, not a constant.
2. It is proportional to $i$, so it vanishes when the coil is off. You can't "read it with the coil open" as for a motor, since with zero current there is no field from the coil (you'd need a permanent magnet or a bias current).
3. Motion lasts only milliseconds.

So one typically watches the coil current under voltage drive and looks for the dip ([[q0406]]) rather than computing $v$ from a formula. In solenoids with a permanent magnet or bias magnet in the plunger (latching solenoids), an open-circuit coil does produce a voltage proportional to velocity, which is a true back-EMF measurement.

::: applications
- **Cordless drill and appliance speed control**: universal and DC motors regulated from their own back-EMF (no tachometer).
- **Sensorless BLDC ESCs for drones and fans**: floating-phase zero-crossing detection.
- **Motor stall/jam detection**: back-EMF collapsing while current is high.
- **Small robotics H-bridge drivers**: coast-and-sample windows for crude speed estimates.
- **Latching solenoid valves with a permanent magnet**: open-circuit coil voltage proportional to plunger velocity.
- **Fuel injector and valve drivers**: plunger arrival detection from the current signature.
:::

::: assumptions
- A **brushed permanent-magnet DC motor** with constant $K_e$; with a wound-field or universal motor $K_e$ depends on field current.
- The drive can put the terminals in **high impedance** for the measurement window.
- Armature **inductance and resistance are known** (or measured) for blanking and compensation.
- For solenoids, the signal appears as **motional EMF** under bias current, not as a clean open-circuit voltage unless there is a permanent magnet in the plunger.
- Ring-down and ADC timing details depend on the specific drive and motor; verify with an oscilloscope.
:::

::: followups
### Why can't you just read the motor terminal voltage while the PWM is on?
Because then $v_t = iR + L\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t + E$ and the first two terms are large and unknown unless you also measure current and know $R$. During the on-time $v_t$ is clamped to the supply by the switch, so you'd read the bus voltage, not the back-EMF. You get information from the *average* voltage and current, but not from a single sample.

### How long do you have to wait after turn-off before sampling?
Until the freewheel current has reached zero and the ringing has died. The back-EMF helps drive that current down, so $t_{dec} = (L/R)\ln(1 + I_0R/(V_f+E))$: 0.13 ms for the example motor at 3,000 rpm, 0.56 ms at 300 rpm. Use the slow case plus margin and verify on a scope.

### How would you measure speed from BEMF with a 20 kHz PWM?
A 50 µs period leaves no room for a 0.2 to 0.6 ms blanking window. Options: periodically skip PWM pulses (say a 1 ms coast window every 10 ms) and sample then; use the on-time method with current sensing and $R$ compensation; or drop the PWM frequency if audible noise and ripple allow.

### Your BEMF speed reading drifts as the motor warms up. What changes?
Two things. The winding resistance rises about 0.39% per kelvin, which corrupts on-time compensation. And the magnets weaken with temperature, so $K_e$ falls a little: the same speed produces slightly less voltage, so a fixed-$K_e$ estimate reads low. Compensate with a temperature estimate (from $R$ or a sensor) or recalibrate.

### Can you use BEMF to detect a stalled motor?
Yes: during commanded drive, if BEMF stays near zero while the current is high, the shaft is not turning. It is faster than waiting for the temperature to rise, but at start-up BEMF is also zero, so combine with a timeout.
:::
