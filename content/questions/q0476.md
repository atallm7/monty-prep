::: basics
### In plain words
Choosing a stackup means deciding the order of the board's layers and how thick the insulation between them is. A good order puts a solid copper plane (usually ground) right next to every signal layer, so each fast signal has a short, well-defined path home. It also keeps the stack symmetric and uses thicknesses the fab can build at a sensible cost.

### Picture it
Hold your hand just above a table under a ceiling lamp. Its shadow is small and sharp, right under your hand. Lift your hand high and the shadow spreads out and overlaps the shadows of things nearby. A signal's return current behaves the same way: it flows in the plane under the trace, tight when the plane is close and spread wide when it is far. The analogy breaks in one place: the return current is a real current, so the plane under it must be unbroken.

### Words you need
- **Reference plane**: the solid ground or power layer next to a signal layer, where its return current flows ([[q0478]]).
- **Return current**: the current flowing back to the source, completing the circuit.
- **Crosstalk**: a signal leaking into a neighboring trace.
- **Plane pair**: a power plane right next to a ground plane, which acts as a small built-in capacitor ([[q0480]]).
- **Symmetric stackup**: the same layer thicknesses mirrored around the center, so the board stays flat.

### The simplest example
The page uses a rule for how far the return current spreads: about 80% of it flows within three times the trace's height on either side. For a trace 0.2 mm above its plane, that is within 0.6 mm either side. For a trace 1.6 mm above its plane, it spreads over 4.8 mm either side, so neighbors share return current and crosstalk grows. That is why a 4-layer board uses signal, ground, power, signal, with thin insulation between each outer layer and its plane. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers common 4-layer and 6-layer orders, their weak spots and the thickness budget.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can **arrange** a given number of layers on purpose, using a few principles (every signal next to a plane, planes paired, symmetry, impedance reachable, cost) instead of copying a template without knowing why. [[q0475]] asks *how many* layers; this one asks *in what order, and what thicknesses*. A strong answer gives a concrete 4-layer and 6-layer example, explains the trade-offs between alternatives, and says that the fab's real stackup numbers feed the impedance calculation.
:::

::: why
The stackup decides signal return paths, achievable trace impedance, plane capacitance, crosstalk, EMI and cost, and it is baked into every layout decision that follows. It's also the thing you coordinate with the fab *before* routing, because changing it later means re-doing impedance-controlled traces. In design reviews and interviews it shows whether you understand return current and coupling rather than just layer names.
:::

::: concepts
- **Reference plane for every signal layer**: fast signals want a solid plane directly next to them for a short, defined return path. See [[q0478]], [[q0486]].
- **Microstrip vs. stripline**: outer-layer traces over one plane vs. inner traces between two planes. See [[q0479]].
- **Plane pairing**: a power plane next to a ground plane provides low-inductance, high-frequency decoupling. See [[q0480]].
- **Symmetry**: keeps the board flat through lamination and reflow. See [[q0474]].
- **Impedance and thickness**: the dielectric height to the reference plane sets $Z_0$ for a given width.
- **Cost**: standard fab stackups and thicknesses are cheaper than custom ones.
:::

::: answer
"I choose the stackup by working from a few rules. First, every signal layer should sit right next to a solid plane, because that's where its return current wants to flow, and that keeps loops small and impedance defined. Second, I want ground and power planes adjacent to each other with a thin dielectric between them, since that pair acts like a high-frequency decoupling capacitor. Third, the stack should be symmetric about the centre so the board doesn't warp, and fourth, the dielectric thickness to each reference plane has to be something the fab can actually build while giving me a reasonable trace width for 50 ohms.

In practice, for four layers that gives signal, ground, power, signal, with a thin prepreg on the outsides and a thick core in the middle. For six layers I'd go signal, ground, signal, power, ground, signal, so the inner signal layer has planes close by. For eight or more I'd add more ground planes so every signal layer is sandwiched or adjacent to one.

Then I send the fab my intent, get their stackup with the real thicknesses and dielectric constants, and run the impedance calculation against that, not against assumptions."
:::

::: technical
**Principles, in priority order:**

1. **Adjacent reference plane per signal layer.** Return current at high frequency follows the path of minimum inductance, directly under the trace. The current density spreads as $J(x) \propto \frac{1}{1+(x/h)^2}$, so about 80% flows within $\pm 3h$ of the trace. Smaller $h$ means a tighter return and less crosstalk.
2. **No two adjacent routing layers without a plane in between**, unless their routes are orthogonal and short. Broadside coupling between parallel traces on adjacent layers is strong because the spacing is the thin dielectric.
3. **Power/ground pairs tightly coupled**, with $h \le 0.1$–0.2 mm if possible: $C \approx 0.12$–0.25 nF/in² and an interplane inductance of $\mu_0 h \approx 0.13$–0.25 nH per square.
4. **Symmetry** about the centre for dielectric thickness and copper balance.
5. **Controlled impedance reachable**: microstrip with $h=0.2$ mm needs $w \approx 0.33$ mm for 50 Ω ($\varepsilon_r=4.3$, 35 µm Cu). Symmetric stripline between planes 0.4 mm apart needs $w \approx 0.15$ mm for 50 Ω (IPC-2141 approximation).
6. **Ground on layer 2** to shield the top, and a ground (or at least a stable plane) next to the bottom signal layer.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The impedance numbers use IPC-2141 approximations with $\varepsilon_r=4.3$. Field-solver results and the real fab $\varepsilon_r$ and thickness shift them by several percent. Use the fab's calculator and stackup.

**Typical arrangements** (S = signal, G = ground, P = power):

| Layers | Stack | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | S / G / P / S | Default. Bottom signals reference the power plane, so keep that rail clean and avoid fast traces over splits. |
| 4 | S / G / G / S | Best SI/EMI; power distributed by wide traces or pours on S layers. |
| 6 | S / G / S / P / G / S | Two outer microstrip layers, one stripline layer, one P–G pair. |
| 6 | S / G / S / S / G / S | Two inner signal layers adjacent to each other: route them orthogonally. Power then lives as pours on signal layers. |
| 8 | S / G / S / P / G / S / G / S | Two stripline layers plus pairing. |
:::

::: short
"I'd start with a few rules: every signal layer next to a solid reference plane, power and ground planes adjacent and tightly coupled, a symmetric stack so the board stays flat, and dielectric thicknesses that give sensible trace widths for 50 ohms. For four layers that's signal, ground, power, signal. For six, signal, ground, signal, power, ground, signal. Then I get the fab's real thicknesses and permittivity and run the impedance calculation on those."
:::

::: long
"I treat the stackup as a way to give every signal a good return path and every rail a low impedance, within what the fab can build.

The rules I start from: first, every routing layer should be adjacent to a continuous plane, ideally ground. High-frequency return current flows right under the trace, so a thin dielectric between them makes a small loop, low inductance and defined impedance. Second, avoid two signal layers back to back with no plane between them, because they couple strongly through the thin dielectric. If I can't avoid it I route them orthogonally. Third, put power and ground planes next to each other with a thin dielectric, because that pair is itself a capacitor with almost no inductance, which helps decoupling at high frequency. Fourth, keep the stack symmetric so it doesn't warp. And fifth, make sure the thickness to the reference plane gives me a manageable trace width: 0.2 mm of prepreg gives me roughly 0.33 mm for 50 ohms on the outside layer.

Concretely, for four layers I'd do signal, ground, power, signal with thin prepreg outside and a thick core in the middle. For six I'd do signal, ground, signal, power, ground, signal so the inner signal layer is a stripline-like route between planes. For eight, more ground planes so each signal layer is next to one.

Then I'd confirm with the fab. I send my target stackup, they give me the real thicknesses and Dk, I calculate trace widths for each impedance-controlled net, and I put that table on the fab drawing."
:::

::: terms
- **Microstrip**: trace on an outer layer with one reference plane beneath it, air (and mask) above.
- **Stripline**: trace on an inner layer between two reference planes.
- **Reference plane**: the solid ground or power plane a signal's return current flows on.
- **Broadside coupling**: coupling between traces on adjacent layers, directly above one another.
- **Dk / $\varepsilon_r$**: dielectric constant of the laminate.
- **Controlled impedance**: traces whose width and dielectric height are specified so $Z_0$ meets a target within a tolerance (commonly ±10%).
- **Plane pair**: power and ground planes close together for built-in capacitance.
- **Stackup sheet**: the fab's table of layers, materials and thicknesses.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer gives the **why** behind each rule (return current under the trace, coupling falling with plane spacing, plane capacitance and inductance) and a **number** or two, mentions both the outer microstrip and inner stripline cases, and admits the real compromises: the 4-layer S/G/P/S stack has bottom signals referencing power; 6-layer stacks trade shielded stripline against cost. They also mention talking to the fab early, impedance coupons, and respecting the processor vendor's reference stackup for DDR or high-speed SerDes.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting power on layer 2 and ground on layer 3 by default**, so the top-layer signals reference a power plane. It can work if that plane is solid and well decoupled, but ground next to the top layer is the conventional, safer choice.
- **Thick dielectric between the signal and its plane.** You want the thin prepreg on the outside, next to the outer signal layers, and the thick core in the middle of the board.
- **Adjacent signal layers with no plane between them** routed in parallel, causing broadside crosstalk.
- **Asymmetric stackups** that warp.
- **Using calculated widths without checking fab thicknesses.** Impedance can be off by 10–20%.
- **Reusing a template from a different design** without checking that its dielectric thickness yields the needed widths for its smaller pitch parts.
:::

::: deep
## What we are optimizing

Choose the layer order to get five things at once:

1. **Short, defined return paths** for every signal.
2. **Controlled impedance** with a trace width that routes at the part's pin pitch.
3. **Low-impedance power delivery.**
4. **Low crosstalk and emissions.**
5. **Mechanical balance and cost.**

The physical lever is the dielectric thickness between each pair of adjacent copper layers. A thin dielectric between a trace and its plane tightens the field and the return current. A thin dielectric between power and ground gives capacitance and low inductance. But a thin dielectric between two *signal* layers is bad: it couples them strongly.

## Return current distribution

For a trace at height $h$ over a plane, the high-frequency return current density across the plane is

$$J(x) = \frac{I}{\pi h}\,\frac{1}{1+(x/h)^2}$$

and the fraction within $\pm k h$ is $\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan k$.

Putting P and G tight together needs a thin core in the middle, which means a thinner overall board or a different build.

## Why S/G/P/S gets chosen anyway

Because it's the best compromise at four layers: both signal layers are reference to a plane over thin prepreg (so traces are narrow and well-coupled), and a plane for power is available. Where EMI or SI is critical, S/G/G/S gives two ground planes (better shielding and a second return path for the bottom layer) and the power is routed as wide traces or pours on the outer layers.

## Six layers: the usual story

With six layers you can have two surface microstrip layers, one or two inner routing layers, and a P–G pair. In S/G/S/P/G/S:

- L1 is microstrip over G (L2).
- L3 sits between G (L2) and P (L4). It sees a ground plane on one side and a power plane (which is an AC ground, if decoupled well) on the other, so it behaves like a stripline.
- L4/L5 is the P–G pair. If the core between them is 0.1 mm thick, it gives $C \approx 0.25$ nF/in².
- L6 is microstrip over G (L5).

Impedance numbers (IPC-2141 approximation, $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$, 35 µm outer, 17.5 µm inner): a 0.11 mm prepreg from L1 to L2 gives about 0.16 mm for 50 Ω; a symmetric stripline with 0.4 mm plane spacing needs about 0.15 mm.

## Practical constraints

- **Thin prepreg isn't free.** 0.1 mm or less usually requires specifying glass style and may cost more or have limited availability. Many shops list standard builds; use them.
- **Resin fill.** Prepreg must flow around heavy copper. If you use 2 oz inner copper, you need thicker prepreg.
- **Vias.** Blind/buried vias restrict which layers can be skipped. Through vias from L1 to L6 leave stubs that matter above a few GHz.
- **Impedance coupons.** Boards for controlled impedance include test coupons that the fab measures with TDR.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> After you give the stack, say one caveat out loud: "On the 4-layer, the bottom layer references the power plane, so I'd keep fast signals on top and make sure the bottom isn't routed over split planes." That shows you know the weakness, not just the template.

## Worked example: building a thickness budget and checking tolerances

A stackup is not finished until the thicknesses add up to a real board. Take a 4-layer S/G/P/S build ordered at 1.6 mm total, with 35 µm outer copper, 17.5 µm inner copper, and 0.2 mm prepreg between each outer layer and its plane:

$$t_{core} \approx 1.6 - \underbrace{2(0.035)}_{\text{outer Cu}} - \underbrace{2(0.2)}_{\text{prepreg}} - \underbrace{0.035}_{\text{inner Cu}} = 1.095\ \text{mm}$$

(In practice you specify a standard core, such as 1.0 mm, and the fab's pressed thicknesses land near these numbers; the fab's stackup report is the authority.) Two consequences follow immediately. First, the L2-L3 plane capacitance per square inch is $0.246\ \text{nF}\times(0.1/1.1)\approx 0.022$ nF, ten times lower than a 0.1 mm pair, so decoupling in a 4-layer board must be discrete. Second, the 50 Ω microstrip width is set entirely by the 0.2 mm prepreg: 0.335 mm from the IPC-2141 approximation.

Now check sensitivity, because the fab will not deliver 0.200 mm exactly. Using $Z_0 = \frac{87}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r+1.41}}\ln\frac{5.98h}{0.8w+t}$ with $w = 0.335$ mm:

| Prepreg height $h$ | $Z_0$ | Error from 50 Ω |
|---|---|---|
| 0.18 mm (−10 %) | 46.2 Ω | −7.7 % |
| 0.20 mm | 50.0 Ω | 0 |
| 0.22 mm (+10 %) | 53.5 Ω | +6.9 % |

And etch tolerance acts independently: with the nominal $h$, a trace etched 0.03 mm narrow (0.305 mm) reads 53.0 Ω, and 0.03 mm wide (0.365 mm) reads 47.2 Ω. A ±10 % impedance spec therefore consumes most of the budget from dielectric thickness and etch variation together, which is why fabs ask for a target impedance and adjust widths to their real build rather than accepting your width verbatim.

## The thickness-surplus problem in six layers

Doing the same arithmetic for the 6-layer S/G/S/P/G/S build in the figure shows a trap. Using the heights discussed above (0.11 mm L1-L2, 0.2 mm each side of the inner signal layer so the stripline plane spacing is 0.4 mm, a 0.1 mm core for the P-G pair, 0.11 mm L5-L6), the dielectric is $0.11+0.2+0.2+0.1+0.11 = 0.72$ mm and copper adds $2(0.035)+4(0.0175) = 0.14$ mm, for **0.86 mm total**. If the product needs 1.6 mm (a card-edge connector, a mechanical standard), 0.74 mm is missing. You can order a thinner board, or you must put that 0.74 mm of dielectric somewhere, and wherever it goes it separates two layers. The least damaging place is a gap whose coupling you do not need, typically between the inner signal layer and one of its neighboring planes, accepting an asymmetric stripline, or split between two cores. Each choice moves the impedance calculation, so recompute. This is the main reason experienced designers ask for the board thickness requirement before choosing the stackup.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **Symmetry and warp.** Unequal copper distribution or an asymmetric dielectric stack bows the board during reflow. Keep the copper fraction on mirrored layers within a similar range and avoid a large pour on one outer layer with an empty opposite outer layer.
- **Dielectric constant is not constant.** FR-4 $\varepsilon_r$ falls with frequency and varies with resin content; the 4.3 used here is a low-frequency textbook value. Vendors list values at one or two frequencies, and the number the fab's field solver uses may differ by 5-10 %. Flag this when a design is marginal.
- **Glass weave and skew.** At multi-gigabit rates, a differential pair can see different local $\varepsilon_r$ on its two traces depending on whether each lies over glass or resin. Fabs offer spread-glass laminates for this; whether you need them depends on the data rate and length, so check the interface specification.
- **Reference-plane changes.** Moving a signal between layers that reference different planes needs a nearby stitching via or decoupling capacitor so the return current can change planes. This is the practical cost of S/G/S/P/G/S: L1 references G but L3 sits between G and P.

## Bench verification

Order impedance coupons on the panel and ask for the fab's TDR report; it lists the measured impedance of each coupon line against your target. For a cheaper check on a prototype, a TDR or a fast pulse generator with a scope reveals impedance steps along the trace, and a plane-pair capacitance measured with an LCR meter at 1 kHz (between power and ground with all parts removed) confirms the core thickness: a 1.1 mm core over 20 in² should read about 0.45 nF, and a 0.1 mm core over the same area about 4.9 nF. If the measured capacitance disagrees by more than the dielectric tolerance, the build was not what you specified.

::: applications
- **USB/Ethernet MCU boards**: S/G/P/S, USB differential pair on top over ground at 0.2 mm prepreg.
- **DDR3/DDR4 processor boards**: 6–8 layers, ground-referenced signal layers, power plane routing in between, per the SoC vendor's stackup.
- **Mixed-signal data acquisition**: S/G/G/S or S/G/S/S/G/S with analog routed on one outer layer over solid ground.
- **High-power motor driver**: 4-layer with 2 oz outer layers and a ground plane just below the control signals.
- **RF modules**: a thin RF-grade top dielectric over a solid ground plane, with the rest of the stack standard FR-4.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Digital/mixed-signal boards** in FR-4 with mostly single-ended 50 Ω and 90–100 Ω differential interfaces.
- **No special flexible or hybrid** materials. Hybrids change thicknesses and costs, but the logic is the same.
- **Standard fab capabilities.** 0.1 mm prepreg and 17.5 µm inner copper are normally available, but check.
- **Different choices** are right for RF (laminate-specific), power (heavy copper), or backplanes. High-speed backplanes often prioritize stripline layers surrounded by ground planes, with back-drilled vias.
- **Processor vendors** often publish a recommended stackup that overrides generic advice.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the thick core in the middle and thin prepreg outside?
Because the outer signal layers need a short distance to their reference plane (thin dielectric) to get narrow, well-controlled 50 Ω traces and tight return paths. The middle of the board is just structural: a thick core provides stiffness and total thickness (1.6 mm), and the power and ground planes in a 4-layer board sit on opposite sides of it.

### In a 4-layer S/G/P/S board, what's the weakness?
The bottom signal layer references the power plane, which has splits and more noise, and the P–G separation is large (the thick core), so planes contribute little capacitance. Mitigations: route fast signals on top; keep bottom traces off split boundaries; use S/G/G/S if power can be distributed by pours; stitch decoupling capacitors to both planes.

### Do you ever put signals on layers with no adjacent ground plane?
Only slow, non-critical ones, or pairs of layers routed orthogonally and kept short. Even then, I'd check that the reference is a power plane that is itself well-decoupled at that frequency: a power plane is an AC ground only if there's a decoupling cap nearby to bridge to ground.

### What if your fab can't deliver the 0.1 mm prepreg you wanted?
Use their standard build: say 0.2 mm. Then recompute trace widths (0.33 mm for 50 Ω instead of 0.16 mm), check the BGA breakout: a wider trace may not fit between pads. If it doesn't, either ask for a more expensive thin build, use via-in-pad, or accept higher impedance on the short breakout section only. The decision is guided by the numbers, not guesswork.

### Scenario: your DDR interface passes simulation but crosstalk is worse on the prototype. What in the stackup could cause that?
Likely the real dielectric between the signal layer and its plane is thicker than modelled, so return currents spread wider and coupling increases (a crude coupling proxy $1/(1+(s/h)^2)$ at $s = 0.2$ mm rises from 0.5 to 0.8 when $h$ doubles from 0.2 mm to 0.4 mm). I'd check the fab's final stackup report, compare coupon TDR data against the model, and then increase spacing (3W or more) on the worst nets.
:::
