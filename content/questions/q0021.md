::: testing
On the surface this is a five-second drawing question, so the interviewer is really listening for what you say *while* you draw:

1. Can you draw the basic non-polarized symbol (two parallel plates with a gap) cleanly and label it with a reference designator like $C_1$ and a value?
2. Do you know there is a **polarized** version, which plate is positive, and *why* the distinction exists (electrolytic and tantalum parts fail if reverse-biased)?
3. Do you connect the symbol to physical reality: the two lines *are* the two plates, the gap *is* the dielectric?
4. Bonus signals: variable/trimmer symbols, IEC vs ANSI styles, and how polarity is marked on the actual part and the PCB silkscreen.
:::

::: why
Schematics are the language of hardware teams. A capacitor drawn with the wrong polarity on a schematic becomes a reversed electrolytic on a PCB, and reversed electrolytics and tantalums can short, overheat, vent or burn. Reviewing schematics, reading reference designs and datasheets, and building footprints all depend on reading these symbols instantly and correctly. Interviewers ask symbol questions early as a warm-up, but a candidate who adds "and the polarized one has a curved or marked negative plate, which matters because…" shows the habit of thinking about what the drawing does in the real world, which is exactly what a design review needs.
:::

::: concepts
- **A capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator.** The symbol is a cartoon of that structure. See [[q0022]] for what capacitance is.
- **Dielectric**: the insulating gap between the plates, which sets how much charge is stored per volt. See [[q0023]].
- **Polarized vs non-polarized**: aluminium electrolytic and tantalum capacitors rely on an oxide layer that only works with one voltage polarity, so their symbol marks the positive terminal.
- **Reference designators**: capacitors are labelled `C` plus a number ($C_1$, $C_{12}$), just as resistors use `R` ([[q0013]]) and inductors use `L` ([[q0038]]).
- **Schematic conventions (ANSI/IEEE vs IEC)**: two drawing standards exist; you should recognise both.
- **Failure consequences**: reversing a polarized part is one of the classic capacitor failure modes ([[q0036]]).
:::

::: answer
"Sure. The basic capacitor symbol is two short parallel lines with a gap between them, and a lead coming off the middle of each line. That's literally a cartoon of the physical part: two conducting plates separated by an insulating dielectric. I'd label it with a reference designator like $C_1$ and a value, say 100 nF, and for a power-rail part I'd also note the voltage rating.

Then there's the polarized version, for electrolytic or tantalum capacitors. In the ANSI style one plate is curved, and the curved plate is the negative side; usually there's also a small plus sign next to the straight plate. In the IEC style you'll see one plate drawn as a filled or hollow rectangle with a plus sign. The polarity marking matters because those parts use a thin oxide dielectric that only holds up in one direction. If you put one in backwards it can leak heavily, heat up and fail, sometimes violently with tantalums.

There are also variants like a variable capacitor, which has a diagonal arrow through it, and a trimmer, which has a little T-shaped bar instead of an arrow."
:::

::: technical
**Non-polarized capacitor** (ceramic MLCC, film, C0G, mica): two equal-length parallel lines (plates) separated by a gap, with a lead perpendicular to the centre of each plate. Both terminals are electrically interchangeable. Used for any capacitor whose dielectric is symmetric with respect to voltage polarity.

**Polarized capacitor** (aluminium electrolytic, tantalum, aluminium/tantalum polymer, supercapacitors marked as polarized):
- ANSI/IEEE 315 style: one straight plate and one **curved** plate. The curved plate is the **negative** (cathode) terminal; a "+" is drawn beside the straight plate.
- IEC 60617 style: two straight plates, one drawn as a hollow or filled rectangle, with a "+" next to the positive plate.
- Physical part markings differ by technology: aluminium electrolytics usually have a stripe (often with "−" signs) on the **negative** lead and a shorter negative lead on radial parts, while **SMD tantalums usually mark the positive (anode) end** with a bar. That inversion is a well-known assembly trap.

**Other variants**:
- **Variable capacitor**: the basic symbol with a diagonal arrow through it (user-adjustable, e.g. an old radio tuning capacitor or a varactor-tuned network drawn as variable).
- **Trimmer (preset)**: a diagonal line ending in a short perpendicular bar ("T") instead of an arrowhead, meaning set once during calibration.
- **Varactor diode**: drawn as a diode with a capacitor on the cathode bar, because it's a reverse-biased junction used as a voltage-controlled capacitor.
- **Feed-through capacitor**: a straight line passing through with a curved/parallel plate to ground, used in EMI filters.

**Annotation**: designator $C_n$, capacitance (pF, nF, µF), and when relevant voltage rating (e.g. 25 V), dielectric (X7R, C0G), tolerance and package (0402). A typical BOM-ready label: "C12 10 µF 16 V X5R 0603".

**Orientation convention**: in power schematics, polarized caps are drawn with "+" toward the higher-potential rail and the curved plate toward ground, so a reviewer can verify polarity at a glance.
:::

For electrolytic or tantalum parts there's a polarized symbol: one plate is curved, or drawn as a box in IEC style, and there's a plus sign on the positive side. That matters because those parts are damaged if you reverse-bias them."

::: long
"I'll start with the plain, non-polarized symbol: two short parallel lines of equal length, a small gap between them, and a lead coming off the centre of each. Those two lines represent the two conducting plates, and the gap represents the dielectric, the insulator that stores the electric field. I'd label it with a designator, $C_1$, and a value like 100 nF. For real schematics I'd also add the voltage rating and dielectric, like 16 V X7R, because that's what actually ends up on the BOM.

Next is the polarized symbol, used for aluminium electrolytics and tantalums. In the American style, one plate is straight and one is curved. The curved one is the negative terminal, and there's usually a plus sign next to the straight plate. In the IEC style, which you see a lot in European datasheets, one plate is drawn as a rectangle with a plus sign beside it.

The reason polarity gets its own symbol is physical. Those capacitors use a very thin oxide layer as the dielectric, grown on the positive electrode. With the right polarity it insulates well; reversed, it starts conducting, the part heats up, and electrolytics can vent while tantalums can short and even catch fire. So I always draw the plus side toward the higher rail and the curved plate toward ground, so anyone reviewing it can check polarity at a glance.

One practical gotcha: the marking on the physical part isn't consistent. Aluminium electrolytics mark the negative lead with a stripe, but SMD tantalums mark the positive end with a bar. So you match the footprint silkscreen to the part, not just to the schematic.

Finally, there are a couple of variants worth knowing: a variable capacitor has a diagonal arrow through it, and a trimmer has a diagonal line with a small flat bar on the end."
:::

::: terms
- **Plate**: one of the two conductors of a capacitor; each line in the symbol represents one.
- **Dielectric**: the insulator between the plates, represented by the gap in the symbol.
- **Polarized capacitor**: a capacitor that must be operated with one terminal positive with respect to the other (electrolytic, tantalum).
- **Anode / cathode**: the positive / negative terminals of a polarized capacitor.
- **Reference designator**: the unique label on the schematic and silkscreen, `C` followed by a number for capacitors.
- **ANSI/IEEE 315 and IEC 60617**: the two common standards for schematic graphic symbols (US-style and international-style).
- **Trimmer capacitor**: an adjustable capacitor set once during calibration, drawn with a T-ended diagonal.
- **Silkscreen polarity mark**: the printed marking on the PCB showing which pad gets the positive terminal.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws two parallel lines and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws both the non-polarized and polarized symbols, and states clearly that the **curved plate is negative** (ANSI) or the boxed/"+" plate is positive (IEC).
- Explains *why* polarity matters physically: the oxide dielectric of electrolytic/tantalum parts only blocks in one direction, and reverse bias leads to leakage, heating and failure.
- Ties the drawing to physical structure: plates plus dielectric.
- Mentions annotation discipline: designator, value, voltage rating, dielectric.
- Knows the real-world trap that **aluminium electrolytics mark the negative lead while SMD tantalums mark the positive end**, and that footprints and silkscreen must agree.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking the curved plate is positive.** In the ANSI symbol, the curved plate is the negative terminal. The "+" goes next to the straight plate.
- **Drawing plates of very different length without meaning it.** Unequal-length parallel lines look like a battery cell symbol (long = positive). Keep capacitor plates equal.
- **Using the polarized symbol for a ceramic capacitor** (or vice versa). Reviewers and assemblers read the symbol literally; the BOM and footprint should match the symbol.
- **Assuming the stripe always means negative.** True for aluminium electrolytics, but SMD tantalum bars mark the *positive* end. Always check the datasheet's marking drawing.
- **Leaving out the voltage rating on power-rail capacitors.** Capacitance alone isn't a usable spec, especially for MLCCs whose effective capacitance drops with DC bias.
:::

::: deep
## Why the symbol looks the way it does

A capacitor, at its most basic, is two conductors separated by an insulator. Put a voltage across them and charge $+Q$ collects on one conductor and $-Q$ on the other; the insulator keeps them from meeting and holds the electric field. The schematic symbol is a deliberately literal sketch of that parallel-plate structure: each line is a plate, the gap is the dielectric, and the leads are the terminals.

The physical meaning of the symbol also tells you what *not* to read into it. Unlike a battery cell symbol (long line = positive, short line = negative), the two plates of a capacitor are equal length, because the capacitor itself is symmetric. Accidentally drawing unequal plates makes a capacitor look like a battery.

## The polarized symbol, and why it exists

Aluminium electrolytic and tantalum capacitors reach huge capacitance in small packages because their dielectric is an oxide layer only nanometres to a few micrometres thick, grown electrochemically on the positive electrode (aluminium oxide on aluminium foil, or tantalum pentoxide on sintered tantalum). Since $C = \varepsilon A/d$ ([[q0022]]), tiny $d$ and enormous etched surface area give large $C$.

That oxide is formed by anodising, and it only acts as a good insulator when the metal it was grown on is the **positive** terminal. Reverse the voltage and the oxide starts conducting; leakage current rises sharply, the part heats, and:

- aluminium electrolytics can generate gas, bulge and vent through the scored top;
- MnO₂ tantalums can go short circuit and, with enough available current, ignite.

That's why the schematic must tell everyone downstream which way round the part goes.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Symbol styles vary between companies and CAD libraries. Some libraries draw the IEC polarized plate as a filled box, others as a hollow box, and some draw an ANSI polarized cap with two straight plates plus a "+" only. When in doubt, the "+" sign is the unambiguous marker. Always confirm against the footprint and the part's datasheet.

## From symbol to physical part

The symbol is only useful if it maps correctly to the part and to the PCB footprint. This is where most real-world polarity errors happen:

| Technology | Polarized? | Physical marking | What the marking indicates |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ceramic MLCC (C0G, X7R, X5R) | No | Usually no marking at all | — |
| Film (polyester, polypropylene) | No (an outer-foil band is sometimes printed) | Optional band | Outer foil (for noise shielding), not polarity |
| Aluminium electrolytic (radial) | Yes | Stripe with "−" signs; shorter lead | **Negative** lead |
| Aluminium electrolytic (SMD can) | Yes | Black/coloured half-moon on top; bevelled base corners | **Negative** side |
| Tantalum (SMD moulded) | Yes | Bar/band at one end | **Positive** (anode) end |
| Polymer aluminium / polymer tantalum | Yes | Bar or stripe (check datasheet) | Varies by vendor |

> [!WARNING]
> The tantalum/electrolytic inversion catches people regularly: a bar on an aluminium electrolytic is negative, a bar on a moulded SMD tantalum is positive. Put a clear "+" on the PCB silkscreen next to the positive pad, and keep the polarity mark visible after the part is placed so assembly inspection can check it.

## Annotation: what goes next to the symbol

A symbol with only "10 µF" next to it is incomplete for a real design. For a power-rail part you want enough information that the BOM is unambiguous:

- **Designator**: $C_{12}$.
- **Capacitance**: 10 µF (and tolerance if it matters, e.g. ±10%, or ±1% for a C0G timing cap).
- **Voltage rating**: 16 V. MLCCs in X5R/X7R lose a large fraction of their capacitance under DC bias, so voltage rating and package size change the *effective* capacitance (covered in [[q0023]] and [[q0030]]).
- **Dielectric/technology**: X7R, C0G, tantalum polymer, aluminium electrolytic.
- **Package**: 0402, 0603, 1206, or can size.

For example, "C12, 10 µF, 16 V, X5R, 0603" tells a reviewer that at a 12 V bias the part may deliver well under half its nameplate capacitance. That sort of comment is what makes a schematic review productive.

## Drawing conventions that make schematics readable

- Draw polarized parts with "+" toward the more positive rail and the curved plate toward ground or the more negative rail.
- Put decoupling capacitors near the IC pin they serve on the schematic, and label which pin, since layout engineers use that grouping.
- Keep the plates horizontal (leads vertical) for capacitors between a rail and ground, so current visually "flows down" to ground.
- Use a junction dot wherever a capacitor lead tees into a net.

## Related symbols you might be asked to compare

- **Inductor** ([[q0038]]): a series of loops or bumps; a core is shown by parallel lines alongside.
- **Resistor** ([[q0013]]): zigzag (ANSI) or rectangle (IEC). Note that IEC uses a rectangle for a resistor, so don't confuse it with the IEC polarized capacitor's box plate.
- **Battery cell**: unequal parallel lines (long = positive). Easy to confuse with a capacitor if your plates aren't equal.
- **Varactor**: diode symbol with a capacitor-plate-like double bar at the cathode, meaning a junction used as a voltage-controlled capacitor.
:::

::: applications
- **Power-rail bulk capacitors**: a 220 µF aluminium electrolytic at a 12 V input connector is drawn with the polarized symbol, "+" toward the 12 V rail. Getting this wrong on the schematic produces a reversed part on every board.
- **Decoupling MLCCs**: 100 nF and 1 µF ceramics next to every IC supply pin use the non-polarized symbol and need no orientation on the board.
- **Audio coupling**: electrolytic coupling capacitors between stages must be oriented according to which side sits at the higher DC bias; the schematic symbol communicates that.
- **RF tuning**: trimmer capacitors in crystal oscillator or antenna matching circuits are drawn with the trimmer symbol so the test procedure knows they need adjustment.
- **Footprint and silkscreen design**: CAD library symbols are linked to footprints, and the "+" pin on the symbol must map to the "+" pad on the footprint.
:::

::: assumptions
- **ANSI vs IEC**: the answer above assumes US-style (IEEE 315) drawing with an IEC alternative. Companies may follow one standard exclusively; both are correct if used consistently.
- **"Polarized" means electrolytic or tantalum**. Non-polarized ("bipolar") electrolytics exist (two anodes back to back, used in crossovers) and are drawn with the plain symbol or with "NP".
- **Library variation**: CAD tools (KiCad, Altium, OrCAD) ship different default artwork. The "+" sign is the universal clue.
- If the interviewer asks you to "draw a capacitor" in the context of a model, they may actually want the realistic model with ESR and ESL ([[q0030]]). Ask, or offer both.
:::

::: followups
### Which plate is negative in the polarized symbol, and how do you remember it?
In the ANSI symbol the **curved** plate is negative. My memory trick: the "+" is always drawn next to the straight plate, so the odd-shaped one is the minus. If a symbol has a "+" anywhere, trust the "+" over the plate shape, because libraries vary. In IEC style, the boxed plate is positive.

### What happens if you install an electrolytic capacitor backwards?
The anodic oxide dielectric only blocks current when its metal is positive. Reversed, leakage current climbs steeply, the part self-heats, and electrolyte breaks down into gas. Aluminium electrolytics bulge and vent through the scored top; MnO₂ tantalums can short and burn if the supply can deliver enough current. Small reverse voltages (manufacturers often tolerate on the order of 1 V or a small percentage of rated voltage briefly) may not cause instant failure but degrade the part. See [[q0036]].

### How do you tell polarity on an SMD tantalum versus an SMD aluminium electrolytic?
On a moulded SMD tantalum, the bar marks the **positive** end. On an SMD aluminium electrolytic can, the coloured half-moon or stripe on top marks the **negative** side, and the plastic base has bevelled corners on the positive side. Because these conventions are opposite, check the datasheet and make the PCB silkscreen show "+" explicitly.

### Why is there no polarity on a ceramic capacitor?
Ceramic dielectrics like C0G or X7R are symmetric: the material polarizes the same way whichever plate is positive, and there's no electrochemically formed layer that depends on direction. The electrodes are identical interleaved metal layers. So either terminal can be the positive one, and the symbol has two identical straight plates.

### How would you draw a capacitor that's controlled by a voltage?
Use a varactor (varicap) diode: a reverse-biased PN junction whose depletion capacitance decreases as reverse voltage increases. The symbol is a diode with a second bar at the cathode. You'd show it reverse-biased through a large resistor or RF choke from a tuning voltage, with DC-blocking capacitors isolating the tuning voltage from the RF circuit. This is how VCOs and electronically tuned filters work.
:::

::: interpretations
"Draw the symbol for a capacitor" is usually satisfied by the non-polarized symbol, but a strong answer draws **both** the non-polarized and polarized versions and briefly mentions variable/trimmer variants. If the interviewer meant a *realistic model* (capacitor plus ESR, ESL and leakage), that is Q30 ([[q0030]]); you can offer to extend the drawing.
:::
