::: basics
### In plain words
Kirchhoff's laws are two bookkeeping rules for circuits. The current law (KCL) says that at any junction, the current going in equals the current coming out. The voltage law (KVL) says that if you go around any closed loop, the voltage rises and drops add up to zero. Together with Ohm's law, they let you solve circuits.

### Picture it
For the current law, picture water pipes meeting at a junction. Pipes can't store water there, so whatever flows in must flow out. For the voltage law, picture a hiking loop that ends where it began. You climb some and descend some, but your total height change is zero. Voltage works like height, and the battery is the climb.

### Words you need
- **Node**: a junction where wires or parts meet.
- **Loop**: any path through the circuit that returns to its starting point.
- **KCL**: the currents into a node sum to zero.
- **KVL**: the voltages around a loop sum to zero.
- **Voltage drop**: the voltage lost across a part as current passes through it.

### The simplest example
KCL: 5 mA flows into a node, and one branch takes 2 mA. The other branches must carry the remaining 3 mA. KVL: a 9 V battery drives two resistors in series, and the resistors drop 3 V and 6 V. Going around the loop, $9 - 3 - 6 = 0$. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the sign rules, where the laws come from, and when they stop being exactly true.
:::

::: testing
The interviewer wants three things:

1. **Correct statements** with a consistent sign convention: currents into a node sum to zero; voltages around a closed loop sum to zero.
2. **The physics behind them**: KCL is conservation of charge, KVL is conservation of energy (voltage is a potential). Knowing *why* lets you reason about when they break.
3. **Fluency in using them**: you can write the equations for a small circuit without tripping over signs, and you know the generalisations (supernodes, closed surfaces) and the limits (high frequency, changing magnetic flux).
:::

::: why
KCL and KVL are the two equations behind every circuit solution, by hand or in SPICE. In the lab they turn into debugging instincts: "the regulator's output current must equal the sum of all loads, so 200 mA is going somewhere I didn't expect" (KCL), or "the 5 V rail measures 4.6 V at the far connector, so 0.4 V is dropping across the cable and connector" (KVL). They're also the starting point for nodal analysis, filter derivations and power-integrity budgets, so interviewers use them to check that your foundations are solid before going deeper.
:::

::: concepts
- **Node**: a connection point; all ideal wire between elements is a single node.
- **Loop**: any closed path through the circuit that returns to its starting node.
- **Charge conservation**: charge can't be created, destroyed or (at an ideal node) stored. See [[q0059]].
- **Electric potential**: voltage is a difference in potential energy per charge, so it depends only on the endpoints, not the path (in the lumped, quasi-static case).
- **Ohm's law and element laws** turn KCL/KVL into solvable equations. See [[q0001]].
- **Sign conventions**: pick reference directions first; a negative answer just means the actual direction is opposite. See [[q0003]].
:::

::: answer
"KCL and KVL are Kirchhoff's two laws, and they're really conservation laws.

KCL, the current law, says the sum of currents entering any node equals the sum leaving, or equivalently, the algebraic sum is zero. That's conservation of charge: a node is just a junction of wires, so it can't store charge, and whatever comes in has to go out. If 5 mA comes into a node and one branch takes 2 mA, the other branches together take 3 mA.

KVL, the voltage law, says the sum of the voltages around any closed loop is zero. That's conservation of energy: voltage is potential, so if I walk around a loop and come back to the same node, my net change in potential has to be zero. So in a series loop, the source voltage equals the sum of the drops across the resistors.

Together with Ohm's law they let you solve any circuit: KCL at the nodes, KVL around loops. The caveat is they assume a lumped circuit. At high frequencies, or if a loop links a changing magnetic field, you get induced voltages, which is why ground loops and long scope ground leads pick up noise."
:::

::: technical
**KCL.** For any node (more generally any closed surface),
$$\sum_{k} i_k = 0\quad\text{(currents leaving positive)}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \sum i_{in} = \sum i_{out}$$
Derivation: continuity equation $\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\partial\rho/\partial t$. An ideal node has negligible capacitance, so it holds no net charge and $\oint \vec J\cdot d\vec A = 0$.

**KVL.** Around any closed loop,
$$\sum_k v_k = 0$$
Derivation: for static or slowly varying fields, $\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec l = 0$ (the field is conservative), and the voltage across each element is the line integral of $E$ through it. In general, Faraday's law gives $\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec l = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$, so KVL is exact only when the loop links no changing magnetic flux *outside* the modelled inductors.

**Sign conventions.** Choose a direction for every branch current and polarity for every voltage. For KVL, walk the loop: add a voltage when you go from $-$ to $+$ through an element (a rise) and subtract when you go from $+$ to $-$ (a drop), or the reverse consistently. A negative result means the true direction is opposite.

**Generalisations.**
- **Supernode / closed surface**: KCL applies to any region, e.g. the total current into an IC through all its pins sums to zero.
- **KVL across open terminals**: the loop can include an open circuit; the voltage across the gap is then whatever balances the loop.

**Independent equations.** For $N$ nodes and $B$ branches, KCL gives $N-1$ independent equations and KVL gives $B-N+1$ (the number of meshes in a planar circuit). With $B$ element laws, that's exactly enough to solve for all $2B$ unknowns.

**Example.** 12 V → $R_1$ = 1 kΩ → node B → ($R_2$ = 3 kΩ ∥ $R_3$ = 6 kΩ) → ground. $R_2\parallel R_3 = 2$ kΩ, so $I_1 = 4$ mA and $V_B = 8$ V; $I_2 = 2.67$ mA, $I_3 = 1.33$ mA. KCL at B: $4 = 2.67 + 1.33$ ✓. KVL: $12 - 4 - 8 = 0$ ✓.
:::

::: long
"KCL and KVL are Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws, and each one is a conservation law in disguise.

KCL first. At any node, the sum of currents flowing in equals the sum flowing out, or if I call currents leaving positive, they all add to zero. The reason is conservation of charge. A node is just a junction of ideal wires, it has no capacitance, so it can't store charge. Every coulomb that arrives has to leave. It also works for any closed surface, not just a single node: the total current into a chip summed over all its pins is zero, which is a nice debugging check.

KVL: around any closed loop, the voltages add to zero. Voltage is a difference in electric potential, which depends only on where you start and end. If I walk all the way around a loop and get back to my starting node, the net change has to be zero. In practice that means in a series circuit the source voltage equals the sum of all the drops. If a 12 volt source drives 1 kΩ in series with a 2 kΩ combination, I get 4 volts and 8 volts, and they add back to 12.

The tricky part is signs, so I always pick current directions and voltage polarities first and stick to them. If an answer comes out negative, the real current just flows the other way.

Together with each element's law, like Ohm's law, KCL and KVL give exactly enough equations to solve any circuit. Nodal analysis is just KCL written in terms of node voltages.

The limits are worth knowing. KVL assumes there's no changing magnetic flux through the loop. That's fine for most circuits, but in a fast switching circuit, a big loop picks up an induced voltage, which is exactly why ground loops and long scope ground leads cause noise. And at high frequency, where wires are a significant fraction of a wavelength, you need transmission-line models instead."
:::

::: terms
- **Kirchhoff's current law (KCL)**: algebraic sum of currents at a node (or through a closed surface) is zero.
- **Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)**: algebraic sum of voltages around a closed loop is zero.
- **Node**: a junction where elements connect; connected ideal wire is one node.
- **Loop / mesh**: a closed path; a mesh is a loop that doesn't enclose other loops.
- **Supernode**: a region enclosing several nodes (often around a voltage source) treated as one for KCL.
- **Reference direction / polarity**: the assumed direction of a current or polarity of a voltage before solving.
- **Lumped-element assumption**: circuit dimensions are small compared with the signal wavelength, and fields are confined to the elements.
- **Induced EMF**: a voltage $-d\Phi/dt$ from changing magnetic flux through a loop; the thing that "breaks" naive KVL.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "KCL: current in equals current out. KVL: voltages around a loop add to zero."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Ties each to its **physical principle**: charge conservation (no charge storage at an ideal node) and the conservative electric field (voltage is path-independent).
- Shows **sign discipline**: assume directions, write consistently, interpret negative results.
- Mentions the **generalisations**: KCL for any closed surface (an IC, a whole sub-circuit), supernodes.
- Knows the **limits**: changing magnetic flux (Faraday) and distributed/high-frequency effects; connects this to a practical symptom like ground-loop noise or scope ground-lead pickup.
- Gives a 10-second worked example with numbers, or a debug use ("the currents don't add up, so there's a leakage path").
:::

::: mistakes
- **Mixing sign conventions halfway through.** Decide "leaving = positive" (or entering) for KCL and "rise = positive" (or drop) for KVL once, and apply it to every term.
- **Treating a negative result as an error.** It just means the real current or voltage is opposite your assumed reference. Don't flip signs to "fix" it.
- **Thinking current is consumed by components.** KCL holds through every element: the current out of a resistor's bottom equals the current into its top.
- **Forgetting that a loop can pass through a current source or an open circuit.** KVL still applies; the voltage across that element is just an unknown to solve for.
- **Believing KVL is "violated" by inductors.** An inductor's $L\,di/dt$ is a voltage term in the loop. KVL only needs extra care when flux from *outside* the modelled elements threads the loop.
- **Writing KCL at a node that's really two nodes** (or vice versa). Trace the ideal wires: everything connected without an element in between is one node.
:::

::: deep
## KCL: what goes in comes out

Think of a node as a junction of pipes with no tank. If water flows in through one pipe, it must flow out through the others at the same total rate, because there's nowhere to store it. Charge works the same way. Formally, the continuity equation says

$$\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}$$

Integrate over a small volume around the node: the net current out equals the rate at which charge inside decreases. An ideal node has no capacitance, so it can't hold net charge, and the right side is zero:

$$\sum_{\text{node}} i_k = 0$$

The same argument works for **any closed surface**. Draw a bubble around an entire microcontroller and the sum of currents through all its pins (VDD, GND, I/O) is zero. That's why the current into VDD comes back out through GND plus any I/O pins that are sourcing current.

## KVL: walking around a loop

Voltage between two points is the work per unit charge to move between them, $V_{ab} = -\int_b^a \vec E\cdot d\vec l$. In a static (or slowly varying) field, $\vec E$ is **conservative**: the work only depends on the endpoints. Start at a node, walk around any closed loop through any elements, return to the start: zero net work, so

$$\sum_{\text{loop}} v_k = 0$$

The mountain-hiking analogy fits: altitude is potential. Whatever path you take around a mountain, if you return to the trailhead your net altitude change is zero. The climbs (sources) equal the descents (drops).

Figure: I₁ enters node B and splits into I₂ and I₃ (KCL). Loop L1 goes through the source, R₁ and R₂; loop L2 goes through R₂ and R₃. Each gives one KVL equation.

**Unknowns:** $I_1, I_2, I_3$.

**KCL at B:** $I_1 = I_2 + I_3$.

**KVL around L1** (clockwise, rises positive): up through the source $+12$, down across $R_1$ $-1\text{k}\cdot I_1$, down across $R_2$ $-3\text{k}\cdot I_2$:
$$12 - 1000\,I_1 - 3000\,I_2 = 0$$

**KVL around L2** (clockwise): going clockwise around L2 we travel *down* through $R_3$ (with $I_3$, so a drop) and *up* through $R_2$ (against $I_2$, so a rise):
$$3000\,I_2 - 6000\,I_3 = 0\;\Rightarrow\; I_2 = 2I_3$$

**Solve:** $I_1 = 3I_3$, and $12 = 1000(3I_3) + 3000(2I_3) = 9000\,I_3$, so $I_3 = 1.333$ mA, $I_2 = 2.667$ mA, $I_1 = 4$ mA, $V_B = 3000 \times 2.667\text{ mA} = 8$ V.

**Check** with the shortcut: $R_2 \parallel R_3 = 2$ kΩ, total 3 kΩ, $I_1 = 12/3\text{k} = 4$ mA ✓. And $R_2$ (smaller) takes twice the current of $R_3$, as the current divider predicts.

> [!TIP]
> L2 shows why KVL implies the parallel rule: two elements across the same pair of nodes must have the same voltage. That's all "parallel" means.

## Nodal analysis is just KCL in disguise

Instead of branch currents, write the single unknown $V_B$ and express each current with Ohm's law:

$$\frac{V_B - 12}{1\text{k}} + \frac{V_B}{3\text{k}} + \frac{V_B}{6\text{k}} = 0\;\Rightarrow\; V_B\left(1 + \tfrac13 + \tfrac16\right) = 12\;\Rightarrow\; V_B = 8\text{ V}$$

One equation instead of three. KVL is satisfied automatically, because node voltages are single-valued (that's the potential idea again). This is why nodal analysis is the standard method and why SPICE uses it. More practice in [[q0005]].

## When KVL needs care: changing magnetic flux

Maxwell's equations give, in general,

$$\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec l = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

If a changing magnetic field threads your loop, the "sum of voltages" around it equals the induced EMF, not zero. Inside an inductor this is intended: we model it as the element voltage $L\,di/dt$, so KVL holds for the circuit model. The trouble is flux from *outside* the modelled elements:

- **Scope ground leads.** A 15 cm ground clip forms a loop with the probe tip. A nearby switching converter's magnetic field induces voltage in that loop, which shows up as "ringing" on the trace that isn't really on the board. Use a short ground spring.
- **Ground loops.** Two pieces of equipment grounded at different points form a large loop with the signal cable; mains-frequency flux through it induces hum.
- **PCB hot loops.** The fast-switching loop in a buck converter radiates; neighbouring loops pick it up. Small loop area is the fix.

In every case the cure is the same: make the loop physically small or add the mutual inductance to the model, at which point KVL works again.

## When KCL needs care: hidden capacitance

KCL assumes nodes store no charge. Real nodes have capacitance to everything nearby. At high frequency, current "leaks" through parasitic capacitance into places you didn't draw. KCL still holds for the real circuit if you include those capacitors as elements, but your schematic is incomplete. Displacement current through stray capacitance is exactly how common-mode noise escapes a switching supply.

## Where else you'll use them

| Where | KCL | KVL |
|---|---|---|
| Power budget | Regulator output current = sum of load currents | Supply voltage − IR drops ≥ load minimum |
| Op-amp analysis | No current into ideal inputs, so feedback current = input current | Virtual short: $V_+ = V_-$ |
| Buck converter | Inductor current = capacitor current + load current | $V_L = V_{in} - V_{out}$ when the switch is on |
| Debugging | Missing current → leakage or short | Missing voltage → bad connector or trace |
:::

::: applications
- **Power budgeting**: the sum of every load's current (KCL) sizes the regulator and its input fuse; KVL tracks IR drop from supply to load.
- **Op-amp circuits**: with no current into the inputs, KCL at the inverting node gives the inverting gain $-R_f/R_{in}$. See [[q0012]].
- **Current-sense placement**: low-side vs high-side sense resistors both rely on KCL (the load current must pass through the shunt).
- **Switch-mode converters**: KVL across the inductor in each switch state gives the buck duty-cycle relation $D = V_{out}/V_{in}$ through volt-second balance.
- **Ground-loop and probing problems**: understanding KVL's flux caveat explains hum and false ringing, and how to fix them with smaller loops.
- **SPICE**: the simulator assembles KCL equations at every node (modified nodal analysis).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Lumped-element circuit**: wires are equipotential and elements are small compared with the wavelength. A 1 GHz signal has a free-space wavelength of 30 cm (roughly 15 cm on FR-4), so board-level traces at those speeds need transmission-line treatment.
- **No external changing flux through loops** (or it's included as mutual inductance).
- **Ideal nodes with no capacitance** (or parasitic capacitances are drawn as elements).

These conditions hold for almost all circuit-level analysis you'll do in an interview. If an interviewer asks "is KVL always true?", the strong answer is: it's exact for the lumped model, and the cases where it "fails" are really cases where the lumped model left out an inductance or a capacitance.
:::

::: followups
### Why does KCL hold even inside a capacitor, where charge does accumulate on the plates?
Charge accumulates on each plate, but equal and opposite, so the capacitor as a whole stays neutral. Current flowing into the top plate equals current flowing out of the bottom plate. In field terms, the conduction current in the wires is continued by displacement current $\varepsilon\,\partial E/\partial t$ between the plates. So KCL around the whole capacitor still holds.

### Three branches meet at a node: 10 mA flows in through one, 4 mA flows out through another. What about the third?
KCL: $10 = 4 + I_3$, so $I_3 = 6$ mA flowing out. If you'd assumed it was flowing in, you'd get $-6$ mA, the same answer with the opposite reference direction.

### Can KVL be applied to a loop that includes an open circuit?
Yes. The voltage across the open is simply an unknown. For example, a 9 V source with a 1 kΩ resistor in series and an open switch: no current flows, so the resistor drops 0 V, and KVL says the full 9 V appears across the open switch. That's how you find the voltage stress on an off switch.

### What happens to KVL in a circuit with a long scope ground lead near a switching regulator? (harder probe)
The probe tip and ground lead form a loop of a few square centimetres. The regulator's switching current produces a changing magnetic field, and Faraday's law puts an EMF $-d\Phi/dt$ around that loop. The scope sees the real node voltage plus this induced voltage, so you measure ringing and spikes that aren't on the board. KVL "fails" only because the loop's induced EMF wasn't in your model. Shrinking the loop with a ground spring removes most of it.

### How many independent KCL and KVL equations does a circuit have?
With $N$ nodes and $B$ branches: $N-1$ independent KCL equations (the last node's equation is implied by the others) and $B - N + 1$ independent KVL equations (the meshes of a planar circuit). In the example on this page, if you treat the source and R₁ as one series branch, there are $N = 2$ nodes (B and ground) and $B = 3$ branches: 1 KCL equation and 2 KVL equations, exactly what we wrote. Counting the source–R₁ junction as its own node gives $N=3$, $B=4$: 2 KCL and 2 KVL, with the extra KCL equation just saying the source current equals $I_1$.
