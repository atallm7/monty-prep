::: basics
### In plain words
A reflection happens anywhere the impedance a wave sees changes. On a circuit board, that means the two ends of the line (a driver or load that does not equal the line's impedance), and anything along the way that changes the line's inductance or capacitance per meter. Examples are vias, stubs, T-junctions, width changes, ground-plane gaps and connectors. Whether one matters depends on its size compared with the edge.

### Picture it
Picture a wave traveling down a canal. If the canal suddenly narrows, or the water gets shallower, part of the wave bounces back. A side dock sticking out also sends back a little echo. A smooth, uniform canal gives no echo. A board trace is the canal, and each width change or side branch is a place where some signal turns around.

### Words you need
- **Impedance discontinuity**: any point where the impedance seen by the wave changes ([[q0711]]).
- **Stub**: a dangling or branching bit of line, such as a T-branch or the unused tail of a via.
- **Via**: a plated hole that carries a signal from one board layer to another.
- **Return path**: the route the return current takes, usually through the ground plane under the trace.
- **Termination**: how a line ends. A mismatched end reflects.

### The simplest example
Start at the end of the line. A typical chip input looks almost like an open circuit, so the reflection coefficient there is about +1 and almost the whole wave comes back.

Now look along the way. At a T-junction, three 50 Ω lines meet. The wave sees the two branches side by side, which is 25 Ω. The reflection coefficient is (25 - 50) / (25 + 50) = -1/3, and each branch gets 2/3 of the voltage.

That is the whole idea: find every place the impedance changes. The rest of this page goes through each cause, with numbers for how large each reflection is.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can turn "impedance mismatch" into a **list of real physical causes on a board**, ranked by how much they matter. The interviewer expects the load and source mismatch first, then the discontinuities you can actually create in layout: vias, stubs and T-junctions, connectors, package leads, width or layer changes, plane gaps, pad and pin capacitance. The strong answer also explains *why* each one has a different impedance (extra C or L per unit length, or a changed reference) and gives rough magnitudes, including that small discontinuities only matter if their delay is comparable to the edge.
:::

::: why
Layout engineers are not given reflections, they create them. Every time a trace changes layer, goes through a connector, branches to a second receiver or loses its reference plane, it introduces a local $Z$ that differs from $Z_0$. Knowing the usual suspects lets you route a high-speed net so it doesn't need a fix later, and lets you read a TDR plot and say "that dip at 38 mm is the via". It is the diagnostic half of the reflections story: [[q0711]] says what a reflection is, this page says where they come from, and [[q0714]] to [[q0717]] say how to stop them.
:::

::: concepts
- **Reflection coefficient** $\Gamma=(Z_2-Z_1)/(Z_2+Z_1)$ at any boundary between local impedances ([[q0711]]).
- **Local impedance** $Z=\sqrt{L'/C'}$: any change in the local $L'$ or $C'$ (width, height, dielectric, reference) changes $Z$ ([[q0710]], [[q0715]]).
- **Lumped discontinuity**: a small feature acts as a series L or shunt C when its delay is below the edge's rise time.
- **Return path**: the return current's route sets the loop inductance; breaking it changes $Z$ ([[q0522]]).
- **Rise time vs feature delay** decides the size of the reflection ([[q0709]]).
- **Parasitics of components and pads** ([[q0011]], [[q0721]]).
:::

::: answer
"A reflection happens anywhere the impedance the wave sees changes. So I'd split the causes into two groups.

The first is the terminations. If the source or the load isn't equal to $Z_0$, you get a reflection at that end. A CMOS receiver is nearly an open circuit, and a driver might be 10 ohms or 40, so the typical unterminated net has mismatches at both ends.

The second group is everything in between that changes the local inductance or capacitance per unit length. Vias add pad capacitance and barrel inductance. A stub or a T-junction hangs an extra length of line, which looks like capacitance or splits the impedance in half. Connectors and package leads have their own geometry. A trace that changes width, layer, or dielectric changes its characteristic impedance, and a gap or split in the reference plane forces the return current to detour and adds inductance. Even the pad of a component, an ESD diode or a test point adds a picofarad or so. Fabrication tolerance means a nominal 50 ohm line is only 50 ohms plus or minus about 10 percent.

Whether they matter depends on size: a 1 pF pad is fine for a 10 MHz clock and a serious reflection at 100 picosecond edges. I'd minimise them in layout and use a TDR to find the ones that remain."
:::

::: technical
**General rule.** A boundary between local impedances $Z_1$ and $Z_2$ reflects $\Gamma=(Z_2-Z_1)/(Z_2+Z_1)$. Anything that changes $L'$ or $C'$ over a length comparable to $v\,t_r$ creates $Z\neq Z_0$ there.

**Catalogue of causes**
1. *Termination mismatch*: $R_L\ne Z_0$ (CMOS input $\approx$ open, $\Gamma_L\approx+1$); $R_s\ne Z_0$ (re-reflection, $\Gamma_S$).
2. *Stubs and T-junctions*: at a T where three equal lines meet, the incident wave sees $Z_0\parallel Z_0=Z_0/2$, so $\Gamma=-1/3$ and each branch gets $2/3$ of the voltage. A short open stub of length $\ell$ acts as a shunt capacitor $C=\ell C'$ when $2t_d\ll t_r$.
3. *Vias*: shunt capacitance from pad and antipad ($\sim0.3$ to $1$ pF) and series barrel inductance, $L\approx5.08\,h[\ln(4h/d)+1]$ nH with $h,d$ in inches (about 1.3 nH for a 1.6 mm board and 0.3 mm barrel). Unused via barrel sections form stubs.
4. *Geometry changes*: width neck-downs, pad-sized widenings under components, changes of dielectric height, layer changes between different reference planes.
5. *Return-path discontinuities*: plane slots, splits, layer changes without nearby stitching vias; extra loop inductance raises local $Z$.
6. *Connectors, sockets, package leads, bond wires, BGA balls*: 3D structures of mixed L and C.
7. *Capacitive loading*: receivers, ESD structures and test pads. Distributed loading of $C_d$ per length lowers the impedance to $Z=Z_0/\sqrt{1+C_d/C'}$ and slows propagation by the same factor.
8. *Process variation*: etch width, copper and dielectric thickness, $\varepsilon_r$ and glass-weave effects (typically ±10% in $Z_0$).

**Size of small discontinuities** (linear ramp of duration $t_r$): shunt $C$: $\Gamma_{pk}\approx\frac{\tau}{t_r}(1-e^{-t_r/\tau})$, $\tau=Z_0C/2$. Series $L$: same form with $\tau=L/(2Z_0)$. E.g. 1 pF with $t_r=100$ ps gives about 24%; with $t_r=1$ ns about 2.5%.
:::

::: long
"A reflection forms wherever the impedance the wave sees changes, so I think of the causes in two groups.

The first is the ends. A receiver is a high-impedance input with a bit of capacitance, essentially an open, so the load end reflects close to plus one. The driver has some output impedance, often 10 to 40 ohms, which isn't 50, so the wave returning to it also reflects. That's the classic unterminated-net problem.

The second group is the things in between that change the inductance or capacitance per unit length of the path. Vias are the big one: the pad and antipad add roughly half a picofarad, the barrel adds around a nanohenry, and an unused barrel section is a stub. Stubs and T-junctions are another: a T splits the wave into two lines, so the impedance it sees is half of $Z_0$, reflecting minus a third; a short open stub looks like a small capacitor, about one picofarad for 10 millimetres of FR-4. Connectors, sockets, package leads and bond wires are three-dimensional structures with their own L and C. Any change of trace width, dielectric thickness or reference layer changes $Z_0$ directly. A split or gap in the plane under the trace forces the return current to detour, which adds loop inductance and raises the local impedance. Then there's loading: every receiver, ESD diode and test pad adds a few picofarads, and spread along a line that can pull a 50 ohm line down to 30. Finally, fabrication tolerance means 50 ohms is really plus or minus ten percent.

How much each one matters depends on its size relative to the edge. For a shunt capacitor the reflection is about the capacitor's time constant, $Z_0C/2$, divided by the rise time, so 1 pF is 2 to 3% for a 1 ns edge and about 25% for a 100 ps edge. So I'd keep stubs short, avoid plane gaps, use stitching vias at layer changes, and put a TDR on the first board to find what's left."
:::

::: terms
- **Impedance discontinuity**: any point where the local impedance seen by the wave changes.
- **Stub**: a dangling or branching section of line, often unintentional (via barrel remainder, test point, T-branch).
- **T-junction / branch**: a point where one line splits into two or more.
- **Via stub**: the unused portion of a via barrel beyond the layer where the signal exits.
- **Antipad**: the clearance hole in a plane around a via, which sets its capacitance.
- **Neck-down**: a local narrowing of a trace, which raises its impedance.
- **Return-path discontinuity**: a break in the conductor carrying the return current (slot, split, missing stitching via).
- **Lumped discontinuity**: a feature much shorter than the edge's spatial extent, modelled as a single L or C.
- **TDR**: time-domain reflectometer; shows $Z$ versus distance by observing reflections of a fast step.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "impedance mismatches, like at the end of the line or a connector".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Says "any change in local $L'$ or $C'$" and shows what physical change in layout creates it.
- Quantifies at least two: the T-junction ($\Gamma=-1/3$) and a small capacitor (reflection $\approx Z_0C/(2t_r)$), or a via (about 1 nH and 0.5 pF).
- Says size must be compared with the edge: the same pad matters at 100 ps and not at 1 ns.
- Points to the often-forgotten return path (split planes, layer changes) and to unused via stubs.
- Mentions the source end (re-reflection), distributed receiver loading and process tolerance.
- Knows the tools: TDR to find them, stitching vias, back-drilling, short stubs, series termination to absorb what's left.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Only naming the load end.** The source mismatch re-reflects the returning wave, and in-line discontinuities create reflections even when both ends are matched.
- **"Right-angle bends cause big reflections."** They add a small excess capacitance (a fraction of a picofarad on fine traces), which usually matters only at multi-GHz. Prefer 45° or arcs, but don't let it dominate your answer.
- **Forgetting the reference plane.** A perfectly uniform trace over a plane with a slot is not uniform: $L'$ changes.
- **Treating all discontinuities as equal.** Their effect scales with size over rise time; a 0.1 pF pad is negligible, a 5 pF connector is not.
- **Believing a T-junction splits "evenly" without a reflection.** The incident sees $Z_0/2$ and reflects $-1/3$; the branches get $2/3$ each.
- **Assuming matched impedance means no reflections.** A trace with ±10% tolerance still has a few percent reflection at each change, and these accumulate over many segments.
:::

::: deep
## One principle

A wave can only travel unchanged where $L'$ and $C'$ stay the same. Everywhere else $Z=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ changes and the boundary conditions force a reflection of $\Gamma=(Z_2-Z_1)/(Z_2+Z_1)$. So every cause of reflection is either:

1. **A termination**: the line ends in something other than $Z_0$.
2. **A change in capacitance per length**: closer or wider conductors, higher $\varepsilon$, or a capacitive load. $C'$ up means $Z$ down.
3. **A change in inductance per length**: narrower conductor, larger loop area, a detouring return current. $L'$ up means $Z$ up.
4. **A branch**: more lines in parallel, which divides the impedance.

An *impedance profile* along a real channel shows this clearly. It is what a TDR measures, and the following figure is a sketch of one for a typical board-to-board link:

## 1. Terminations

The load: a CMOS input is a few picofarads and a leakage, effectively open at the frequencies of interest, so $\Gamma_L\approx+1$ (its pin capacitance rounds the reflected edge with a time constant $Z_0C$). The source: a driver's output resistance ranges from about 5 to 50 Ω (and is not constant), so the returning wave sees $\Gamma_S=(R_s-Z_0)/(R_s+Z_0)$. A mismatch at either end is enough to make an echo; mismatch at both is what makes a ring ([[q0711]]).

## 2. Vias, pads and small capacitors

A small shunt capacitance $C$ on a $Z_0$ line adds a time constant $\tau=Z_0C/2$ (the two half-lines on either side act as $Z_0/2$ in parallel with $C$). With a ramped edge of duration $t_r$ the peak reflection is $\Gamma_{pk}=\frac{\tau}{t_r}(1-e^{-t_r/\tau})$. A via is more than a capacitor. A through-hole via in a 1.6 mm board has about 0.3 to 1 pF of pad capacitance and, from $L\approx5.08\,h[\ln(4h/d)+1]$ nH (dimensions in inches), about 1.3 nH of barrel inductance for $h=0.063$ in and $d=0.012$ in. The series L reflects $\Gamma_{pk}=\frac{\tau}{t_r}(1-e^{-t_r/\tau})$ with $\tau=L/(2Z_0)$, giving about 10% at 100 ps edges for 1 nH and about 2% at 500 ps. If the signal leaves on an inner layer, the remaining barrel stub (the part past the exit layer) adds extra capacitance and, for very fast channels, a resonance: a stub of length $\ell$ resonates at $f=\dfrac{v}{4\ell}$, for a 1.2 mm stub in FR-4 stripline ($v\approx1.45\times10^8$ m/s) that is about 30 GHz.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The via inductance formula is a widely used first-order approximation that ignores the return vias; real values depend on the return-path distance, antipad size and layer stack-up and can differ by a factor of two. For critical channels, use a 3D field solver or the fabricator's via models.

## 3. Stubs and T-junctions

At a three-way T-junction where all lines have impedance $Z_0$, the wave sees the other two lines in parallel: $Z_0/2=25\ \Omega$. So $\Gamma=(25-50)/(25+50)=-1/3$ and the voltage on each branch is $1+\Gamma=2/3$ of the incident. If both branches are terminated in $Z_0$, they are fine afterwards but the junction itself reflects a negative echo of one third.

A short *open stub* of length $\ell$ looks like capacitance $C=\ell C'$ if the round trip $2\ell/v$ is much less than $t_r$. With $C'=115$ pF/m a 10 mm stub is about 1.15 pF; the earlier plot says that is about a 28% reflection at 100 ps edges and about 5% at 500 ps. A longer stub produces its own delayed echo (the wave goes down the stub, reflects from its open end and arrives back, in phase, after $2t_{stub}$).

Anything that changes $L'$ or $C'$ over a distance comparable to the edge's length creates a reflection. Use the trace-impedance calculator to see how sensitive 50 Ω microstrip is to geometry:

A neck-down where a trace squeezes between pads can raise $Z$ by 10 to 15 Ω for a few millimetres, with a reflection of about $+9\%$ to $+13\%$ at its start and the opposite sign at its end (so the two partially cancel when the section is electrically short).

Two other important cases:
- *Layer change with different reference planes*: if the signal moves from a microstrip over plane A to a stripline between planes B and C, impedance changes and the return current must hop between planes, ideally through a nearby stitching via.
- *Plane gaps and slots*: the return current follows the minimum-impedance path under the trace; a slot forces it around, so the loop inductance and local $Z$ jump. This can be the single biggest uncontrolled reflection on a bad layout ([[q0522]]).

## 5. Distributed loading

If receivers or test pads of capacitance $C_d$ are spread along the line at spacing $s$, the average added capacitance per length is $C_d/s$, and
$$Z'=\frac{Z_0}{\sqrt{1+\dfrac{C_d/s}{C'}}},\qquad t_{pd}'=t_{pd}\sqrt{1+\frac{C_d/s}{C'}}$$
Example: 5 pF loads every 25 mm on a 50 Ω FR-4 line ($C'=115.5$ pF/m): added $200$ pF/m, so $Z'=50/\sqrt{1+200/115.5}=30\ \Omega$ and the delay per mm rises from 5.8 ps to 9.6 ps. A wave entering the loaded section from a 50 Ω section reflects $(30-50)/(30+50)=-25\%$. This is why multi-drop buses (traditional DRAM, backplanes) are slow and must be designed with the loaded impedance in mind.

## 6. Manufacturing variation

Etched width, copper thickness, dielectric thickness and $\varepsilon_r$ vary, so a 50 Ω design is typically 50 Ω ±10% (some fabs offer ±5% at extra cost). Between two 'nominal' sections, say 45 Ω and 55 Ω, the reflection is $(55-45)/(55+45)=10\%$. Glass-weave periodicity can also create periodic impedance variation. These are small individually but add up over a long channel.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Offer a ranking: "In my experience the biggest are the unterminated ends, then stubs, then vias and connectors, then geometry changes, then the rest." Then say how you would find them: TDR, simulation of the extracted channel, and layout review rules (no stubs over 2 mm, stitching vias at layer changes, no plane crossings).
:::

::: applications
- **High-speed routing rules**: keep stubs short, avoid plane splits under fast nets, add stitching vias beside layer changes, and back-drill long via stubs on thick backplanes.
- **Multi-drop memory/backplanes**: derate the loaded impedance, use fly-by topology instead of star T-junctions in DDR3 and later.
- **Connector selection**: choose connectors specified for the data rate (impedance-controlled, low return-loss pins), and place ground pins near each signal.
- **TDR/VNA impedance testing** of a new stack-up and its via transitions during bring-up.
- **Test point design**: use small pads on the net, or probe points on a short stub (< 1 to 2 mm), because a 5 mm test stub is a reflection at GHz.
- **Package/board co-design**: model bond wires, balls and board escape routing together.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Small discontinuity approximations** ($\tau$-based formulae) assume linear behaviour and that the feature is electrically short; for longer features treat them as short transmission-line sections with their own $Z$.
- **Typical values** (via about 0.3 to 1 pF and about 1 nH, FR-4 $C'$ of 115 pF/m, ±10% tolerance) depend on stack-up, drill and antipad sizes, and fab; they are order-of-magnitude figures.
- **Single-ended 50 Ω line** assumed; differential pairs add mode conversion from skew and asymmetric discontinuities ([[q0719]]).
- **Lossless line**; with loss the reflections attenuate and disperse.
- **Linear receivers**: clamp diodes and driver nonlinearity modify the late-time waveform.

Different sources rank the causes differently by data rate: below about 100 MHz terminations dominate; above a few GHz, connectors, vias and packages are usually the limiting reflections.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a T-junction reflect? Is it a problem if both branches are terminated properly?
At the T the incident wave sees the two branches in parallel, $Z_0/2$, so it reflects $\Gamma=-1/3$; each branch gets only $2/3$ of the incident voltage. Even with both branches terminated in $Z_0$ there is that one reflection at the junction. It matters for a driver with a long line before the T because the negative echo returns to the source after $2t_d$ and can corrupt the edge. Fixes: terminate the source, use a three-resistor star splitter ($Z_0/3\approx16.7\ \Omega$ in each leg: matched at all three ports, at the cost of 6 dB of signal), or use a point-to-point or fly-by topology.

### How does a via cause a reflection?
Two things: shunt capacitance from the via pad and antipad to the planes, and series inductance in the barrel. In a first-order lumped model it's a "C–L–C" or "π" network; in a 1.6 mm board that's roughly 0.3 to 1 pF and 1 nH. Together they make a short section whose impedance is dip, rise, dip relative to 50 Ω; the reflection depends on the edge: about 10% for the inductance at 100 ps and less at 500 ps. Reduce it with a smaller pad and larger antipad (less C), a shorter barrel and ground vias nearby (less L), and back-drilling stubs.

### My trace is perfectly 50 Ω everywhere but I still see ringing. Why?
Check the ends: the receiver is essentially open and the driver is not 50 Ω, so the net needs termination. Also check the return path (plane slot or reference layer change), pads and test points, the probing (long ground lead) and the connector or cable between the board and the scope. A TDR shows which one.

### What happens at a trace that changes from 50 Ω to 100 Ω, and then back?
At the first change $\Gamma=(100-50)/150=+1/3$. At the second $\Gamma=-1/3$ going from 100 to 50 Ω. If the 100 Ω section is electrically short compared with the edge, the two reflections nearly cancel (a bump-shaped echo). If it is long (a delay greater than the rise time), you see both separately, plus multiple internal reflections inside the 100 Ω section.

### Harder: how would you find a single bad via in a 200 mm channel without cutting the board?
A TDR with an edge faster than the feature (tens of picoseconds in a sampling TDR). The reflection location is at $x=vt/2$; the polarity says whether it is capacitive (negative dip) or inductive (positive bump). I'd compare with a reference trace without the via, de-embed the fixture and probes, and, as a cross-check, run a field-solver model of the via. If I only had a scope and pulse generator, I'd use the fastest edge available and look at the first reflection from the driver end.
:::
