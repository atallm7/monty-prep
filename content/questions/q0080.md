::: testing
Whether you can draw the symbol **correctly and label it with meaning**, not just reproduce a shape. The interviewer is checking:

1. The three terminals in the right places: base (the control lead), collector, emitter.
2. The **emitter arrow**: present, on the emitter, and pointing *out* for an NPN (into the device for a PNP). This is the single most common error.
3. Current directions: for an NPN, $I_C$ and $I_B$ flow in, $I_E$ flows out, with $I_E=I_C+I_B$.
4. The link between the drawing and the physics: the arrow is the base-emitter diode, pointing in the direction of forward conduction.
:::

::: why
A BJT symbol is the first thing you draw in any analogue or interface circuit interview, and getting the arrow wrong tells the interviewer immediately that you haven't built anything with one. Reading schematics fluently depends on knowing which way the arrow points and what that implies about polarity: whether the emitter goes to ground (NPN low-side switch) or to the supply (PNP high-side switch). It also leads straight into "what are the terminals?" ([[q0081]]) and "NPN vs PNP" ([[q0086]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Three terminals**: base (B), collector (C), emitter (E). See [[q0081]].
- **Arrow convention**: the emitter arrow shows the direction of conventional current through the base-emitter junction when it is forward biased.
- **NPN vs PNP**: NPN has electrons as the main carriers; PNP has holes. See [[q0086]].
- **Current relation**: $I_E=I_C+I_B$ (KCL on the transistor). See [[q0004]].
- **Diode symbol**: the arrow of a diode points from anode (P) to cathode (N). See [[q0072]].
:::

::: answer
"I draw a vertical line for the base region, with the base lead coming in horizontally from the left. On the right side of that bar I draw two slanted lines, one going up to the collector and one going down to the emitter. The emitter line gets an arrowhead. For an NPN, the arrow points *away* from the base, out of the transistor, toward the emitter terminal. A PNP is the mirror image where the arrow points *into* the base.

The way I remember it is that the arrow is the base-emitter diode: it points from P to N, which is the direction conventional current flows when that junction conducts. In an NPN the base is P and the emitter is N, so the arrow goes from base to emitter, outward. Another mnemonic is NPN, 'Not Pointing iN'.

Then I'd label the terminals B, C and E, and add the currents: for an NPN, collector current flows into the collector, base current flows into the base, and both leave through the emitter, so $I_E=I_C+I_B$. Typically the collector goes up to the load and supply and the emitter goes down toward ground."
:::

::: technical
**Symbol (NPN).**
- A short thick line (the base region) with the base lead attached at its midpoint.
- Two slanted lines from the thick bar: upper to the **collector**, lower to the **emitter**.
- **Arrowhead on the emitter**, pointing outward from the bar (toward the emitter terminal). Optionally, a circle around the symbol for discrete parts.

**PNP symbol.** Same drawing but the arrowhead on the emitter points inward toward the base. The emitter is usually drawn at the top.

**Why the arrow points that way.** The base-emitter junction is a PN diode. In an NPN the base is P-type and the emitter is N-type, so the diode's anode is the base and its cathode is the emitter. A diode symbol's arrow points anode to cathode, which is base to emitter, i.e. outward. In a PNP the base is N and the emitter is P, so the arrow goes emitter to base, i.e. inward.

**Current directions (conventional).** NPN in forward-active operation: $I_B$ into the base, $I_C$ into the collector, $I_E$ out of the emitter, $I_E=I_C+I_B$. PNP: all three reversed ($I_E$ in, $I_C$ and $I_B$ out).

**Voltage polarities (forward-active).** NPN: $V_C>V_B>V_E$ with $V_{BE}\approx0.65$-$0.7$ V. PNP: $V_E>V_B>V_C$ with $V_{EB}\approx0.65$-$0.7$ V.

**Labels.** Use $Q_1$ for the reference designator and a part number (e.g. 2N3904 NPN, 2N3906 PNP) next to it.
:::

::: short
"The BJT symbol is a base bar with the base lead on one side, and two angled lines on the other: collector at the top and emitter at the bottom. The emitter has an arrow. For an NPN the arrow points out, away from the base, because it's the base-emitter diode direction, P to N. For a PNP the arrow points in. Current goes into the collector and base and out of the emitter for an NPN, so $I_E=I_C+I_B$."
:::

::: long
"Let me draw it. I start with a short vertical bar. That represents the base. From the middle of the bar I run a horizontal line to the left, and that's the base terminal. On the right-hand side of the bar I draw two lines at an angle: one going up and out, which is the collector, and one going down and out, which is the emitter. Then I put an arrowhead on the emitter line.

The arrow is the important bit. On an NPN it points outward, away from the bar. On a PNP it points inward, toward the bar. A memory trick is 'NPN: Not Pointing iN'. But I prefer to remember why: the arrow is the base-emitter diode. In an NPN the base is P-type and the emitter N-type, so the diode conducts from base to emitter, and the symbol arrow follows the direction of forward conventional current. In a PNP the base is N and the emitter P, so the diode conducts from emitter to base and the arrow points inward.

Then I label the terminals B, C and E and mark the currents. In an NPN in normal operation, current flows into the base and the collector, and both leave through the emitter, so $I_E=I_C+I_B$. The base current is small, around one percent of the collector current for a gain of 100. For voltages, the collector is the most positive, the base is about 0.7 V above the emitter, and the emitter is typically at the lowest potential. For a PNP, everything is flipped.

Finally I'd show it in a quick circuit to make it concrete: an NPN low-side switch with the emitter at ground, the load between the supply and the collector, and a resistor from the control signal into the base to set the base current. That's the classic use of the symbol."
:::

::: terms
- **Base (B)**: the thin, lightly doped control region; current into it (NPN) controls the larger collector current.
- **Collector (C)**: the terminal that collects the carriers injected by the emitter; usually the higher-voltage side for an NPN.
- **Emitter (E)**: the heavily doped terminal that emits carriers into the base; carries the arrow on the symbol.
- **Emitter arrow**: direction of conventional current through the forward-biased base-emitter junction.
- **NPN / PNP**: the doping sequence of the three regions, which sets polarity.
- **$I_B,\ I_C,\ I_E$**: terminal currents with $I_E=I_C+I_B$ (for an NPN, $I_E$ leaves).
- **$V_{BE}$**: base-emitter voltage, about 0.65-0.7 V when conducting (silicon).
- **Reference designator**: label such as Q1 identifying a transistor on a schematic.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws the symbol and guesses which way the arrow points.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws it cleanly with B, C, E labelled and the arrow on the emitter.
- Derives the arrow direction from the diode picture (P to N across the base-emitter junction) rather than relying on a mnemonic.
- Draws the PNP and notes the mirror relationship, including supply polarity.
- Marks $I_B$, $I_C$, $I_E$ with directions and writes $I_E=I_C+I_B$ and $I_C=\beta I_B$.
- Adds typical voltages ($V_{BE}\approx0.7$ V, $V_C>V_B>V_E$) and puts the symbol in a small circuit (a switch or a common-emitter stage).
- Warns that two back-to-back diodes are *not* a transistor: the thin shared base is what creates gain.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Arrow pointing the wrong way.** NPN: arrow out (base to emitter). PNP: arrow in (emitter to base).
- **Putting the arrow on the collector.** The arrow is on the emitter only.
- **Mixing up collector and emitter** when the symbol is rotated or flipped. The arrowed terminal is always the emitter.
- **Writing $I_C=I_E+I_B$.** KCL gives $I_E=I_C+I_B$ for the NPN's currents with $I_E$ out.
- **"The arrow shows electron flow."** It shows conventional current. Electrons move the opposite way, from emitter into the base (in an NPN).
- **Treating the BJT as two diodes.** The diode model gives the junction polarities but not the gain; the shared thin base matters.
:::

## Why the arrow points that way

The silicon structure of an NPN is N-P-N: an N emitter, a thin P base and an N collector. The two junctions are the base-emitter junction and the base-collector junction. Each is a PN diode:

- Base-emitter: P (base) to N (emitter), so the diode arrow points from base to emitter.
- Base-collector: P (base) to N (collector), so its diode arrow points from base to collector.

The BJT symbol uses only the emitter arrow, because the emitter junction is the one that's normally forward biased and the one whose direction tells you about polarity. For the PNP structure (P emitter, N base, P collector), the base-emitter diode points from emitter (P) to base (N), so the arrow goes inward.

## What the symbol's currents mean quantitatively

The three currents on the symbol are tied together by two exact facts and one approximate one. KCL gives $I_E=I_C+I_B$ exactly. The collector current in forward-active operation follows the exponential diode law of the base-emitter junction (the Ebers-Moll relation):

$$I_C\approx I_S\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T},\qquad V_T=\frac{kT}{q}=25.85\ \text{mV at }300\ \text{K}$$

and the current gain $\beta=I_C/I_B$ is approximately constant, so $I_B=I_C/\beta$. A convenient companion is $\alpha=I_C/I_E=\beta/(\beta+1)$, which is 0.990 for $\beta=100$ and 0.997 for $\beta=300$.

This is where the "0.7 V" in the drawing's labels comes from. For a small-signal silicon device with an assumed saturation current of $I_S=10^{-14}$ A (a typical order of magnitude; real values vary with the part and temperature), the 10.4 mA collector current of the LED example needs

$$V_{BE}=V_T\ln\frac{I_C}{I_S}=0.02585\times\ln\frac{10.4\times10^{-3}}{10^{-14}}=0.715\ \text{V}$$

At 1 mA the same part needs only 0.655 V. Every factor of 2 in collector current costs $V_T\ln2=17.9$ mV, so $V_{BE}$ sits in a narrow 0.6 to 0.75 V window over a wide current range, which justifies using one number. If $I_S$ is 10 times larger or smaller, $V_{BE}$ moves by only $\pm60$ mV. The junction also has a negative temperature coefficient of about −2 mV/K, so the same 10.4 mA at 75 °C needs roughly 0.1 V less (0.715 − 0.002 × 50 = 0.615 V). This temperature behavior matters for bias design and is why bandgap references can use two BJTs as a thermometer.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $I_S$, $\beta$ and the temperature coefficient depend on the specific transistor type and its operating current. Datasheets list $\beta$ (often called $h_{FE}$) as a wide range, not a single number, so never design for a typical $\beta$ in a switch.

## Worked example: sizing the base resistor for a saturated switch

A transistor used as a switch should not rely on $\beta$ at all. The usual method is a **forced beta** of about 10, meaning you supply a base current of $I_C/10$ regardless of how high the part's $\beta$ really is. For the LED example, $I_C=10.4$ mA, so

$$I_B=\frac{10.4\ \text{mA}}{10}=1.04\ \text{mA},\qquad R_B=\frac{3.3\ \text{V}-0.7\ \text{V}}{1.04\ \text{mA}}=2.5\ \text{k}\Omega$$

The 2.2 kΩ resistor in the figure is the next standard value below, giving 1.18 mA and a forced beta of 8.8. In saturation $V_{CE(sat)}$ is typically 0.1 to 0.3 V for small-signal parts at this current; with 0.2 V the transistor dissipates $0.2\times10.4=2.1$ mW, negligible. A 10 kΩ resistor from base to ground is often added so the base cannot float high from leakage or noise when the driver is high-impedance; it draws only $0.7/10\ \text{k}\Omega=70\ \mu$A from the base node while the transistor is on.

## Worked example: why a PNP high-side switch needs a level shifter

The PNP symbol hides an interfacing trap. Suppose a PNP switch has its emitter on a 12 V rail and the load (120 Ω, so 100 mA) between the collector and ground. To turn it on, the base must be about 0.7 V below the emitter, at roughly 11.3 V. To turn it off the base must be pulled up to nearly the rail. A 3.3 V microcontroller pin connected directly to the base would leave $V_{EB}=12-3.3=8.7$ V, which does not turn the transistor off. The junction is forward biased by far more than 0.7 V, so the transistor is on, with a large uncontrolled base current limited only by whatever resistance is in the path. The standard fix is a small NPN stage: the NPN's collector pulls the PNP's base resistor toward ground when the logic input is high, while a resistor from the PNP's base to its emitter holds it off otherwise.

For 100 mA with a forced beta of 10, the PNP needs $I_B=10$ mA. The base resistor to the NPN's collector, with the NPN saturated at about 0.2 V, is $(12-0.7-0.2)/10\ \text{mA}=1.1\ \text{k}\Omega$. This is also why many designers choose a P-channel MOSFET or an N-channel device with a bootstrap driver for high-side loads instead.

## Variants you may be asked to draw

- **Darlington pair:** two NPN transistors with the first emitter feeding the second base; collectors tied together.
- **Schottky-clamped BJT** (e.g. 74S/74LS logic): a Schottky diode from base to collector to prevent deep saturation.
- **Phototransistor and opto-isolator output:** the same symbol with the base replaced by incoming light arrows.
- **BJT in IC schematics:** often drawn without the circle, and with the emitter arrow proportionally larger when the emitter area is a ratio (e.g. current mirrors).

## How to verify the symbol against a real part on the bench

1. **Diode-test mode identifies type and base.** In diode-test mode a meter's red lead is positive. Put red on one pin and touch black to each of the other two. If one pin reads about 0.6 to 0.7 V to both others, it is the base, and because red was on the P-type material, the device is an NPN. If you need black on the common pin to get the same readings, it is a PNP. Between the two remaining pins, the reading should be open or very high. Two diodes that both read in both directions mean a damaged junction.
2. **Separate collector from emitter.** For a small-signal NPN the base-collector junction usually reads a few tens of millivolts lower than the base-emitter junction, but the difference is small and part-dependent, so confirm against the datasheet pinout rather than relying on it. A part with the pinout reversed behaves as a poor, low-gain transistor.
3. **Check $V_{BE}$ and $V_{CE}$ in the circuit.** With the LED example conducting, a scope or meter should show $V_{BE}\approx0.65$ to 0.75 V and $V_{CE}\approx0.1$ to 0.3 V. A $V_{BE}$ near 0.7 V with a $V_{CE}$ that is still high means the collector path is open (an LED reversed or missing); a $V_{BE}$ near zero means the drive or base resistor is the problem.
4. **Measure the gain.** A meter with an $h_{FE}$ socket, or a curve tracer, gives the current gain at one set current. Compare with the datasheet's range and note how much it varies with current and temperature.

::: applications
- **Low-side switch for LEDs, relays and buzzers**: NPN with emitter to ground and the load to the supply.
- **High-side switch**: PNP with emitter to the supply and the load to ground.
- **Common-emitter amplifier**: input to base, output from collector, emitter degeneration resistor for stability.
- **Emitter follower (buffer)**: output taken from the emitter for low output impedance.
- **Differential pair in an op-amp input stage**: two matched NPN or PNP transistors sharing a tail current.
- **Current mirrors and bandgap references**: matched BJTs whose $V_{BE}$ and area ratio set a precise current or voltage.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Discrete, circled symbol for a standard junction BJT.** IC schematics may drop the circle; the arrow convention is the same.
- **Conventional current** is used for arrow directions. Electron flow is opposite.
- **Standard orientation**: collector up, emitter down for NPN; reversed vertically for PNP when drawn with the supply on top. Drawings can be flipped or rotated; always identify the emitter by the arrow.
- **Normal forward-active operation** for the voltage and current directions quoted.
- **Alternative accepted answer**: some interviewers just want the arrow direction and terminals; the diode-based derivation is extra credit.
:::

::: followups
### How do you remember which way the arrow points?
Two ways. "NPN: Not Pointing iN," or physically: the arrow is the base-emitter diode, pointing from P to N. In an NPN the base is P and the emitter is N, so the arrow goes base to emitter, which is outward. In a PNP the base is N and the emitter is P, so the arrow goes emitter to base, inward. The physical rule never fails, even if you forget the mnemonic.

### If I give you a BJT with the arrow pointing down and the emitter at the bottom, is it NPN or PNP?
Arrow pointing away from the base bar means NPN. Down and out of the device at the emitter terminal, so it's an NPN with the emitter at the bottom, the usual orientation for a low-side switch. If the arrow pointed up toward the base bar it would be a PNP drawn upside down.

### Draw the currents and write the relationships for an NPN in the active region.
$I_B$ in, $I_C$ in, $I_E$ out. KCL: $I_E=I_C+I_B$. With current gain $\beta$: $I_C=\beta I_B$ and $I_E=(\beta+1)I_B$. For $\beta=100$ and $I_B=50$ µA, $I_C=5$ mA and $I_E=5.05$ mA. The base current is about 1% of the collector current.

### Can I build a transistor by connecting two diodes back-to-back?
No. The diode model gets the junction polarities right but it can't amplify, because in a discrete pair the "base" between them is thick, so carriers injected from the emitter recombine before they reach the collector. A real BJT shares one very thin base (about 0.1-1 µm in small-signal devices) so most injected carriers diffuse across to the collector. See [[q0085]].

### What changes in a circuit if I swap an NPN for a PNP at the same position?
All polarities flip. The emitter must be the most positive terminal, the collector goes to a lower potential, and base current must be drawn *out* of the base (pulled low) rather than pushed in. In a switch, the NPN conducts when the base is about 0.7 V above the emitter; a PNP conducts when the base is about 0.7 V below the emitter. A drop-in swap without re-biasing won't work.

### Scenario: your NPN LED driver doesn't light even though the base has 3.3 V. What would you check?
First that the emitter really is at ground (a floating or wrongly connected emitter looks like no $V_{BE}$). Then the pinout: a TO-92 2N3904 is E-B-C looking at the flat face, while some other parts (e.g. BC547) are C-B-E, and a swapped pin gives a reversed device with poor gain. Then check the base resistor (not open, correct value), and measure $V_{BE}\approx0.65$-$0.7$ V and $V_{CE}$. If $V_{BE}$ is right and $V_{CE}$ is high, the collector load path may be open (LED backwards).
