::: testing
Whether you ask **"what kind of sensor?"** before drawing, and then draw the right interface with the supporting parts a real design needs. The question is deliberately generic, so the interviewer is checking that you separate analog sensors (divider or amplifier, filter, ADC reference, ratiometric thinking), digital bus sensors (I2C or SPI with pull-ups, addressing, decoupling), and level compatibility (5 V sensor into a 3.3 V MCU). Details like the RC anti-alias filter, bypass capacitor, pull-up values and the ADC's reference are what separate a sketch from a working circuit.
:::

::: why
Sensors are how a microcontroller learns about the world, and the interface circuit often determines accuracy more than the sensor does: a missing bypass capacitor adds noise, a wrong pull-up breaks an I2C bus, an unfiltered ADC input aliases, a 5 V output can damage a 3.3 V pin. This question tests whether you can turn a block diagram ("sensor to MCU") into a circuit you would actually build ([[q0008]], [[q0783]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Voltage divider and sensor excitation**: resistive sensors (thermistor, LDR) become voltages via a divider ([[q0008]]).
- **ADC input, reference and filtering**: sampling capacitor, source impedance, anti-alias RC.
- **Ratiometric measurement**: powering the sensor and ADC reference from the same rail cancels supply error.
- **I2C bus**: open-drain lines with pull-ups, addresses, bus capacitance ([[q0755]], [[q0786]]).
- **Decoupling**: a 100 nF capacitor close to each IC's supply pin.
- **Level compatibility**: a 5 V output into a 3.3 V pin needs a divider or level shifter ([[q0762]]).
- **GPIO configuration**: analog mode, alternate function, open-drain ([[q0783]]).
:::

::: answer
"I'd start by asking what kind of sensor, because the circuit is different. I'll draw the two most common cases.

For an analog sensor like an NTC thermistor, I'd make a voltage divider from the MCU's supply: a 10 kΩ fixed resistor on top and the thermistor to ground, with the midpoint going to an ADC pin. Because the divider and the ADC reference both come from the same 3.3 V, the measurement is ratiometric and supply error cancels. Between the divider and the pin I'd add a small RC filter, say 1 kΩ and 100 nF, which cuts noise and gives the ADC's sampling capacitor a charge reservoir.

For a digital sensor, like an I2C temperature or IMU chip, it's four wires: power, ground, SDA and SCL. SDA and SCL are open-drain, so each needs a pull-up to 3.3 V, typically 4.7 kΩ for 100 to 400 kHz with a short bus. The sensor gets a 100 nF decoupling capacitor right at its VDD pin, and I set the address pin if it has one.

If the sensor runs at 5 V and outputs 5 V, I have to protect a 3.3 V MCU: a resistor divider for a slow analog signal, say 12 k and 22 k, or a level shifter for a digital bus.

And on every design I'd add the basics: common ground, a clean supply, short traces, and in firmware configure the pin as analog or as the right peripheral function."
:::

::: technical
**Case 1: resistive sensor to ADC.** Divider: $V_{ADC}=V_{DD}\dfrac{R_{NTC}}{R_{1}+R_{NTC}}$. With a 10 kΩ NTC (B = 3950 K, $R_{NTC}=R_0\,e^{B(1/T-1/T_0)}$) and $R_1=10$ kΩ: at 25 C it is 10 kΩ, giving 1.650 V; at 0 C, 33.6 kΩ, 2.543 V; at 50 C, 3.59 kΩ, 0.871 V; at 100 C, 698 Ω, 0.215 V. Sensitivity at 25 C about $-36.7$ mV/C, which on a 12-bit, 3.3 V ADC (0.806 mV/LSB) is about 45 LSB/C, so resolution is much finer than 0.1 C before noise and tolerance. Choose $R_1$ equal to the thermistor's resistance at the temperature where you want the most sensitivity.

**Ratiometric.** If the ADC reference is $V_{DD}$ and the divider is also fed by $V_{DD}$, the code is $4095\cdot R_{NTC}/(R_1+R_{NTC})$ independent of $V_{DD}$. If $V_{DD}$ sags 3.3 to 3.0 V, the code is unchanged (2048 at 25 C) whereas an absolute sensor against a fixed reference would drift.

**Self-heating.** Current at 25 C is $3.3/20\text{ k}=165\ \mu A$; power in the NTC $=I^2R=0.27$ mW. With a typical dissipation constant of order 1-2 mW per C (check the datasheet), the error is roughly 0.14-0.27 C. Use a larger $R_1$ or pulse the divider only when sampling.

**Anti-alias/charge reservoir.** RC low-pass $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$: 1 kΩ and 100 nF gives 1.59 kHz. The SAR ADC's sampling capacitor draws a charge kick at every sample; the 100 nF cap supplies it so the divider (source impedance $R_1\parallel R_{NTC}=5$ kΩ at 25 C) does not need to settle within the acquisition time. Check the ADC datasheet's maximum source impedance.

**Case 2: I2C sensor.** SDA and SCL are open-drain. Pull-ups $R_p$ to $V_{DD}$: lower bound $R_{p,min}=(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$ (e.g. $(3.3-0.4)/3\text{ mA}\approx0.97$ kΩ), upper bound from rise time $R_{p,max}=t_r/(0.8473\,C_b)$ (100 pF bus capacitance, 300 ns fast-mode limit: 3.5 kΩ; 1 µs standard mode: 11.8 kΩ) per the NXP I2C-bus specification. 4.7 kΩ is a common choice for short 100 kHz buses and 2.2 kΩ for 400 kHz. Address select pin tied high/low; decouple VDD with 100 nF; keep traces short.

**Case 3: 5 V output to a 3.3 V ADC.** Divider $R_t=12$ kΩ, $R_b=22$ kΩ: $5\times22/34=3.24$ V; source impedance $12\parallel22=7.8$ kΩ so add the 100 nF cap, or buffer. Make sure the ADC is never above $V_{DD}+0.3$ V (absolute maximum on many pins; check the part). For digital 5 V signals use a level shifter ([[q0762]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Thermistor B values, dissipation constants, ADC maximum source impedance and pin voltage limits are part-specific. The values above are typical/example numbers; check the datasheets.
:::

::: long
"Since the question says 'a sensor', I'd first ask what it is, and then draw the two cases that cover most things.

Case one, an analog resistive sensor, say a 10 kΩ NTC thermistor. I put it in a voltage divider with a 10 kΩ fixed resistor from the 3.3 V rail, thermistor to ground, and take the midpoint to an ADC pin. At 25 degrees it's half the supply, 1.65 V, and it changes about 37 millivolts per degree, which is roughly 45 counts per degree on a 12-bit ADC. The key trick is making it ratiometric: the ADC reference is the same 3.3 V rail that feeds the divider, so if the rail moves, the reading doesn't. Between the divider and the pin I'd add a 1 kΩ and 100 nF RC. That low-pass filters noise at about 1.6 kHz, and the capacitor supplies the charge for the ADC's sampling capacitor, so the divider's several-kilohm source impedance doesn't hurt accuracy. I'd also watch self-heating, since the thermistor dissipates a fraction of a milliwatt.

Case two, a digital sensor on I2C. Four connections: 3.3 V, ground, SDA and SCL. Both signal lines are open-drain, so each needs a pull-up resistor to 3.3 V, 4.7 kΩ is typical for a short bus at 100 or 400 kHz, and I'd check that against the rise-time limit for the bus capacitance. A 100 nF capacitor sits right at the sensor's supply pin, and I tie its address pin to set the I2C address. If I'd need an interrupt or data-ready, I'd also route its INT pin to a GPIO with interrupt capability.

If the sensor works at 5 V, I need to protect the 3.3 V MCU. For a slow analog output, a divider like 12 k over 22 k gives about 3.2 V; for digital buses, a level shifter. And in all cases a common ground and a clean supply, with the MCU pin configured in analog or the right alternate-function mode."
:::

::: terms
- **Ratiometric**: output proportional to supply, so a measurement referenced to the same supply is supply-independent.
- **NTC thermistor**: resistor whose resistance falls as temperature rises.
- **Anti-alias filter**: low-pass filter before an ADC to remove frequencies above half the sample rate.
- **Open-drain**: output that can only pull low; needs a pull-up for the high level.
- **Pull-up resistor**: connects a line to VDD so it idles high.
- **Decoupling (bypass) capacitor**: local charge reservoir at an IC's supply pin.
- **Level shifter**: circuit translating logic levels between voltage domains.
- **Self-heating**: temperature rise of a sensor from its own excitation power.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer draws a box labelled "sensor" with a line to the MCU. An exceptional answer:

- Asks **what kind of sensor** and gives analog and digital circuits.
- Draws the **divider, RC filter and ADC reference** and explains **ratiometric** measurement.
- Computes **sensitivity and resolution** (mV/C, LSB/C) and notes **self-heating**.
- For I2C: **pull-up sizing** from rise time and sink current, **decoupling**, **address**, and interrupt pin.
- Handles **voltage level mismatch** (5 V sensor into 3.3 V MCU) and mentions **ESD/overvoltage protection** for off-board sensors.
- Mentions **sampling strategy**: averaging, oversampling, calibration.
:::

::: mistakes
- **No pull-ups on I2C.** Open-drain lines never go high, so the bus is stuck low; the first thing to check.
- **Feeding the ADC directly from a high-impedance divider without a capacitor**, so the readings depend on the sampling timing and read low.
- **Using a different supply for the sensor and ADC reference** and calling the result ratiometric, then seeing supply noise in the readings.
- **Driving a 3.3 V MCU pin from a 5 V sensor output** without a divider or level shifter, exceeding the absolute maximum.
- **Omitting the bypass capacitor** or placing it far from the pin, so supply noise appears in the sensor output.
- **Forgetting a common ground** between the sensor and the MCU.
:::

> [!TIP]
> If the sensor and ADC could ever see different references (an external 2.5 V reference on the ADC, but the divider off 3.3 V), the measurement is no longer ratiometric. Either power the divider from the reference (through a buffer) or accept the supply error and calibrate.

## ADC input details that bite

An SAR ADC input is a sampling switch and capacitor $C_S$ (a few pF) with an internal resistance. Each sample pulls a small charge, which the source must supply within the acquisition time. For a source impedance $R_s=5\text{ k}\Omega+1\text{ k}\Omega=6$ kΩ and settling to 12-bit accuracy takes $\ln(2^{13})\approx9$ time constants of $R_sC_S$. With $C_S$ only 5 pF that is about 0.27 µs, fine; but the charge kick from $C_S$ on a high-impedance node causes errors unless a larger external cap (100 nF) holds the node steady. Rule: make the external cap large enough that the charge-sharing step, about $V\cdot C_S/(C_S+C_{ext})$, is under half an LSB. For 12 bits that means $C_{ext}\gtrsim2^{13}C_S\approx41$ nF for a 5 pF $C_S$, hence 100 nF. Keep the resistor small enough that the filter time constant ($R_fC_f=100\ \mu s$ here) is much shorter than the signal's variation.

## Putting it into firmware

1. Configure the pin as analog (disables the digital input buffer) or as the I2C alternate function (open-drain, speed) ([[q0783]]).
2. Set the ADC sample time to satisfy $R_s$ and $C_S$; calibrate the ADC if the chip offers it.
3. Average or oversample N readings; each factor of 4 oversampling adds about one bit if noise is white and above about 1 LSB rms.
4. Convert: counts to volts, volts to resistance, resistance to temperature (beta equation: $1/T=1/T_0+\frac{1}{B}\ln(R/R_0)$).
5. For I2C, check ACK and CRC, use timeouts to avoid a hung bus, and handle bus recovery (clocking SCL until SDA releases).

::: applications
- **Temperature measurement** with NTC thermistors in battery packs and appliances.
- **I2C/SPI environmental and motion sensors** (temperature, humidity, pressure, IMUs) on IoT boards.
- **Potentiometer position sensing** (joysticks, knobs) via the same divider into an ADC.
- **Light sensing** with photodiodes/phototransistors and ambient light ICs.
- **Industrial 0-10 V and 4-20 mA sensors** conditioned by dividers or sense resistors.
- **Hall-effect and ultrasonic sensors** read through timer capture ([[q0790]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes a 3.3 V MCU with a 12-bit SAR ADC and a typical I2C sensor; a 1.8 V or 5 V system changes the numbers.
- Assumes a short on-board bus. Long cables need series resistance, filtering, protection and possibly a differential interface (RS-485, 4-20 mA).
- Thermistor numbers use the beta model with B = 3950 K, which is accurate to a fraction of a degree near 25 C only; Steinhart-Hart gives better accuracy over a wide range.
- Pull-up values depend on bus capacitance and speed mode; the NXP I2C-bus specification gives the limits.
- If the sensor is a bridge (strain gauge, pressure) it needs an instrumentation amplifier or a bridge ADC; if it is a photodiode it needs a transimpedance stage. The "divider" answer is only one of several valid analog front ends.
:::

::: followups
### Why make the measurement ratiometric?
Because supply variation then cancels: the sensor output and the ADC reference scale together, so the code depends only on the sensor's resistance ratio. It removes the need for a precise reference and makes battery-powered systems read correctly as the battery sags. It fails if the sensor and the ADC use different references.

### How do you choose pull-up resistors for I2C?
Between the minimum set by the driver's sink capability $(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$ and the maximum set by rise time $t_r/(0.8473C_b)$. With 100 pF at 400 kHz that is roughly 1 kΩ to 3.5 kΩ; at 100 kHz and 100 pF up to about 11.8 kΩ. Pick near the lower-middle: 2.2 kΩ to 4.7 kΩ. More devices or longer wires increase $C_b$ and force a smaller resistor ([[q0755]]).

### The ADC reading jumps around by several counts. What do you check?
Noise on the supply or reference (add bypass, separate analog and digital grounds properly), source impedance too high for the sample time (add the capacitor or lower R, lengthen the sample time), no anti-alias filter, digital switching near the analog trace, and then average or oversample. Also check that the pin is in analog mode and no pull-up is enabled.

### How would you read a sensor that outputs a PWM signal?
Use a timer input capture: measure the high time and the period between edges, and compute duty $=t_{high}/T$. The sensor's output level must match the MCU's logic level, with a pull-up if open-drain ([[q0790]]).

### What protection would you add if the sensor is on a 2 m cable?
A series resistor (a few hundred ohms to 1 kΩ) at the MCU pin, a clamp or TVS to the rails or ground at the connector, an RC filter, and for noise a shielded or twisted pair. For I2C over distance use a buffer/extender or switch to a differential bus.
:::

::: interpretations
The question names no sensor and no MCU voltage, so it is open-ended by design. I answer with the two standard cases (analog and I2C) plus the level-compatibility case, and state the assumption of a 3.3 V MCU. If the interviewer names a sensor (an accelerometer, a thermistor, an IR proximity sensor), pick that circuit and go deeper. Related interface drawings are the button ([[q0805]]), LED ([[q0806]]) and motor ([[q0807]]).
