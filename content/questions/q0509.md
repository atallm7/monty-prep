::: testing
Whether you can explain, with equations, how a small series inductance turns a capacitor into something else at high frequency, and what that does to the supply. The interviewer wants:

1. The series-RLC model and the three regions (capacitive, resonant minimum, inductive).
2. That above the self-resonance the impedance is $2\pi f L$, set by inductance, not capacitance.
3. The consequences for supply droop ($L\,di/dt$), for the useful bandwidth, and for mixed-value networks.
4. Numbers: how 0.5 nH vs 3 nH changes the capacitor's impedance and resonance.
:::

::: why
Parasitic inductance is *the* limit of decoupling performance above a few MHz. Once you see that a capacitor is a series RLC and that the inductance is usually mounting-dominated, most layout rules follow. It also explains why a 10 µF capacitor can be worse than a 10 nF one at 50 MHz, and why replacing a capacitor with a bigger one doesn't fix a high-frequency problem.
:::

::: concepts
- **Series RLC capacitor model**: $C$ with ESR and ESL ([[q0030]]).
- **Impedance magnitude**: $|Z|=\sqrt{ESR^2+(\omega L-1/\omega C)^2}$ ([[q0024]]).
- **Self-resonant frequency**: $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ ([[q0031]], [[q0512]]).
- **Inductive voltage**: $v=L\,di/dt$ ([[q0504]]).
- **Mounting inductance**: pads, vias and traces add to the part's ESL ([[q0510]]).
- **Anti-resonance** between capacitors of different values.
:::

::: answer
"A real capacitor is a series chain: the capacitance, a little resistance, ESR, and a little inductance, ESL, from its leads, body and the connections to it. At low frequency the capacitive reactance dominates and the part looks like a capacitor, impedance falling as one over omega C. At the self-resonant frequency, one over two pi root LC, the capacitive and inductive reactances cancel and the impedance bottoms out at the ESR. Above that, the inductance dominates and the impedance climbs as omega L, so the capacitor has turned into an inductor.

That matters because the fast current a chip needs lives above resonance. A 100 nanofarad capacitor with half a nanohenry resonates around 22 megahertz, and at 100 megahertz it's 0.31 ohms, whatever its capacitance. Increase the inductance to 3 nanohenries, with long traces and far vias, and resonance drops to 9 megahertz and the 100 megahertz impedance rises to 1.9 ohms.

So parasitic inductance sets how fast the capacitor can respond, through L di/dt, limits its useful bandwidth, and, with other capacitors in parallel, creates anti-resonance peaks. The total inductance includes the part's ESL, pads, vias and plane spreading, and the mounting is usually the bigger part. That's why layout, not the capacitor value, decides performance at high frequency."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** $Z(\omega)=ESR+j\omega L+\dfrac{1}{j\omega C}$, $L=ESL+L_{mount}$.

**Regions.** Below $f_0$: $|Z|\approx 1/(2\pi f C)$ (capacitive). At $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$: $|Z|=ESR$. Above: $|Z|\approx 2\pi f L$ (inductive), phase $\to+90^\circ$.

**Numbers (100 nF, ESR 20 mΩ).**

| Total $L$ | $f_0$ | $|Z|$ at 100 MHz |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 nH | 22.5 MHz | 0.31 Ω |
| 1.5 nH | 13.0 MHz | 0.94 Ω |
| 3 nH | 9.2 MHz | 1.9 Ω |
| 10 nH | 5.0 MHz | 6.3 Ω |

**Transient limit.** A capacitor can supply di/dt at most $\Delta V/L$: with 50 mV allowed, $L=0.5$ nH supports 100 mA/ns, $L=5$ nH only 10 mA/ns.

**Characteristic impedance.** $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$ is the impedance at which reactances are equal: 0.07 Ω for 100 nF and 0.5 nH, 0.32 Ω for 10 nH. It also governs anti-resonance peaks between capacitors: a parallel tank of characteristic impedance $Z_0$ and series loss $R$ peaks at roughly $Z_0^2/R$; for two paralleled capacitors this is $X^2/(ESR_1+ESR_2)$, with $X$ each branch's reactance at the peak (worked in the deep-dive).

**Parallel networks.** Capacitors of different values have opposing reactances between their resonances; the parallel combination has an anti-resonance peak limited by ESR. $N$ identical capacitors reduce $L$ to $L/N$ ([[q0506]]).

**Effective capacitance.** Near resonance the apparent capacitance is $C/(1-(f/f_0)^2)$: it appears larger just below $f_0$ and negative (inductive) above it.
:::

::: short
"A real capacitor is a series RLC. Below its self-resonant frequency it's a capacitor; at resonance the impedance dips to the ESR; above it the inductance dominates and the impedance rises as 2 pi f L, so it's effectively an inductor. That sets the useful bandwidth: a 100 nF with 0.5 nH resonates near 22 MHz, but with 3 nH of mounting it drops to 9 MHz and is six times worse at 100 MHz. It also limits di/dt and creates anti-resonance with other capacitors."
:::

::: long
"Start with the model: a capacitor in series with a small resistor, ESR, and a small inductor, ESL. The inductance comes from the part's body and terminations, and then the pads, traces and vias you connect it with add more. Add them up and call it L.

Impedance is the resistance plus j omega L minus j over omega C. At low frequency the capacitor term dominates, so the impedance falls with frequency. At the self-resonant frequency, one over two pi root LC, the two reactances cancel and you get a dip down to just the ESR. That's the capacitor's sweet spot. Above that, the inductor wins, and impedance rises at 2 pi f L, so the capacitor is really an inductor.

Why that matters: decoupling is about fast current, and fast current lives above resonance. A 100 nanofarad cap with half a nanohenry resonates at 22 megahertz and at 100 megahertz is 0.31 ohms, whatever its capacitance. With 3 nanohenries it resonates near 9 megahertz and is 1.9 ohms at 100. And it limits transients: the most di/dt you can supply within a 50 millivolt budget is 50 millivolts divided by L, so half a nanohenry gives 100 milliamps per nanosecond, five nanohenries only 10.

Two more effects. When you parallel capacitors of different values, one is inductive while the other is still capacitive between their resonances, so you get an anti-resonance peak, which ESR damps. And the biggest part of L is usually mounting, so layout, with short vias, planes close to the surface and parallel capacitors, matters more than picking a different capacitor."
:::

::: terms
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance of the capacitor itself.
- **Mounting inductance**: inductance of pads, traces and vias connecting it.
- **SRF ($f_0$)**: frequency where reactances cancel.
- **Characteristic impedance**: $\sqrt{L/C}$.
- **Inductive region**: frequencies above $f_0$ where $|Z|\approx 2\pi fL$.
- **Anti-resonance**: parallel-resonance peak between capacitors.
- **Phase**: −90° (capacitive) to +90° (inductive); 0° at resonance.
- **Slew capability**: maximum $di/dt$ supportable, $\Delta V/L$.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "inductance makes the capacitor stop working at high frequency". An exceptional one:

- Writes $Z$ and identifies the three regions with $f_0$ and $|Z|=ESR$ at resonance.
- States that above $f_0$ the impedance is set by $L$, independent of $C$, and gives numbers (0.31 vs 1.9 Ω at 100 MHz).
- Converts L into a transient limit: $di/dt\le\Delta V/L$.
- Separates **ESL** (part) from **mounting inductance** (layout) and says mounting usually dominates.
- Notes anti-resonance in parallel networks and the $L/N$ benefit of parallel parts.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking a bigger capacitor always lowers impedance.** Above $f_0$, impedance is $2\pi fL$ and bigger parts often have more ESL.
- **Quoting the datasheet ESL as the whole story.** Mounting adds as much or more.
- **Believing the SRF printed by the vendor applies on your board.** It is for a test fixture; yours is lower.
- **Forgetting the phase.** Above $f_0$ the part is inductive, so it interacts with other capacitors' capacitance (anti-resonance).
- **Ignoring ESR.** It sets the resonant dip and damps anti-resonance.
:::

## What the inductance does to the supply

**1. Bandwidth.** The capacitor only lowers the supply impedance up to roughly $f_0$. Beyond it the impedance rises at $2\pi fL$, so the useful bandwidth is set by L, not by C.

**2. Transient capability.** Whatever the capacitance, the capacitor's terminals can change current no faster than $\Delta V/L$. For a 50 mV droop budget:

| L | Max $di/dt$ |
|---|---|
| 0.25 nH | 200 mA/ns |
| 0.5 nH | 100 mA/ns |
| 1.5 nH | 33 mA/ns |
| 5 nH | 10 mA/ns |

A processor that steps 5 A in 10 ns ($0.5$ A/ns) needs $L_{eq}\le 0.1$ nH at 50 mV, hence the parallel arrays ([[q0506]], [[q0507]]).

**3. Anti-resonance.** Two capacitors in parallel: below both resonances both are capacitive; above both, both are inductive; between them, the larger capacitor (already inductive) and the smaller (still capacitive) form a parallel resonant circuit, which shows as an impedance peak:

**4. Loop with the chip.** The inductance also forms an anti-resonance with the die capacitance ([[q0504]], [[q0508]]).

## Where the inductance comes from

The part itself contributes ESL (roughly 0.3 nH for 0201 up to over 1 nH for 1206 MLCCs; vendor and geometry dependent). On top of that you add pad and trace inductance, two vias with their lengths, and current spreading in the planes. A 0402 with a short fan-out and 0.2 mm vias totals 0.5-0.8 nH; with 1.6 mm vias and a few mm of trace it can exceed 3-5 nH. Details in [[q0510]]; package effects in [[q0511]].

## Worked example: how much capacitance do you "lose"?

A designer wants 50 mV droop or less for a 200 mA step in 2 ns from a 100 nF 0402. Capacitive droop for 5 ns: $0.2\times5\text{ ns}/100\text{ nF}=10$ mV. Inductive droop with $di/dt=0.2\text{ A}/2\text{ ns}=10^8$ A/s: 0.5 nH → 50 mV (just meets), 3 nH → 300 mV (fails). Same capacitor, same capacitance, six times the spike. Capacitance isn't the limiting parameter; inductance is.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> ESL values by case size and mounting are typical orders of magnitude (vendor-, series- and layout-dependent). Use vendor S-parameter models or measure for critical designs.

## Q and the shape of the dip

The series RLC model has a quality factor $Q = Z_0/ESR$ with $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$. For the 100 nF, 20 mΩ part:

| Total $L$ | $Z_0$ | $Q$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5 nH | 0.071 Ω | 3.5 |
| 3 nH | 0.173 Ω | 8.7 |

A high $Q$ means a deep, narrow notch at $f_0$ and a steep climb either side of it; a low $Q$ (more ESR) gives a shallow, broad bottom. This is a nuance for decoupling: a capacitor with very low ESR and low $L$ has a deep notch but is also the one that creates the highest anti-resonance peaks when paralleled with another, as the next example shows. Phase tells you the same thing: the phase swing from $-90^\circ$ to $+90^\circ$ is faster for higher $Q$.

The "effective capacitance" statement in the technical section deserves a number. The apparent capacitance is $C/(1-(f/f_0)^2)$, so at half the resonant frequency the capacitor already looks 33% larger than its nominal value ($1/(1-0.25)=1.33$), and at 90% of $f_0$ it looks 5.3 times larger. It is not magic: the inductive part is subtracting from the capacitive reactance, so the net reactance looks like a bigger capacitor. This is why measuring a capacitor on a bridge near its self-resonance gives nonsense capacitance values.

## Worked example: when paralleling makes it worse

Put a 100 nF and a 10 nF capacitor in parallel, each with 1.5 nH of total inductance and 20 mΩ of ESR (assumed, for a short mounted 0402 each). Their self-resonances are at 13.0 MHz and 41.1 MHz. Between those two frequencies the 100 nF part is already inductive and the 10 nF part is still capacitive, so they form a parallel resonant tank. Computing the complex parallel impedance numerically across the range gives a peak of about **1.4 Ω at 30 MHz**, even though each capacitor alone is only about 0.24 Ω at that frequency. So the pair is nearly six times *worse* than either part at that frequency. The peak frequency follows from the tank's loop: current circulating between the two capacitors passes through *both* inductances and both capacitances in series, so $f_{ar}\approx 1/(2\pi\sqrt{(L_1+L_2)\,C_{series}})$ with $C_{series}=C_1C_2/(C_1+C_2)=9.1$ nF and $L_1+L_2=3$ nH, which gives 30.5 MHz. At that frequency the two branch reactances are equal and opposite ($X\approx0.23\ \Omega$), and the peak height is about $X^2/(ESR_1+ESR_2)=0.234^2/0.04\approx1.4\ \Omega$. The ESRs set the height much more than the frequency, so halving them roughly doubles the peak.

How do you fix an anti-resonance? Lower the loop inductance (it moves the peak up and lowers it), use several parts of the same value instead of widely separated values, or add damping. A part with a deliberately higher ESR reduces $Q$ and flattens the peak, which is why some designers add a small series resistor or a lossy capacitor for broadband damping. A power-plane pair also adds capacitance that fills the gaps between discrete values (see [[q0507]] and [[q0508]]).

## Estimating mounting inductance from geometry

The inductance of a via is the largest term you control. A widely used approximation (for a single via through a dielectric of height $h$ and diameter $d$, both in millimeters) is

$$L_{via}\approx 0.2\,h\left[\ln\frac{4h}{d}+1\right]\ \text{nH}.$$

For a 0.3 mm via through 1.6 mm of board: $0.2\times1.6\times[\ln(21.3)+1] = 1.3$ nH. For the same via only 0.2 mm long (the planes are the next layer down): $0.2\times0.2\times[\ln(2.67)+1] = 0.08$ nH. A decoupling capacitor needs two vias (one to power, one to ground), so ignoring mutual inductance between them (which reduces the sum, since the currents run in opposite directions and the two vias are close), the via contribution is about 2.6 nH with long vias and 0.16 nH with short ones. That is 16 times, easily more than the 0.3 to 1 nH of the capacitor body itself.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> This formula is an engineering approximation. Real inductance depends on via spacing, nearby return vias, the plane geometry and the current spreading, and can be 30 to 50% lower than the self-inductance sum once mutual coupling is included. For critical designs use a field solver or a measured S-parameter model.

The practical rules that follow are not mysterious: put the capacitor on the same side as the planes that feed it, shorten the via to the plane (planes close to the surface, or a small buried pair near the top), place the power and ground vias right next to each other so that their fields cancel, and use via-in-pad or short stubs rather than a long trace between pad and via. See [[q0510]].

## Measuring it on the bench

A vector network analyzer connected as a **shunt-through** measurement gives you the impedance of a low-impedance part directly. With a 50 Ω system and the DUT shunted between the signal and ground, $S_{21}=2Z/(2Z+50)$, which rearranges to $Z = 25\,S_{21}/(1-S_{21})\approx25\,S_{21}$ for small $Z$. For example, a 0.3 Ω impedance gives $S_{21} = -38.5$ dB, and the 20 mΩ ESR at resonance gives $-62$ dB, which is close to the noise floor of many instruments and needs careful calibration and low-impedance fixturing. Practical points:

- Keep the fixture's ground path short and use the same footprint the product will use, or the fixture's own inductance will dominate the measurement.
- Below a few milliohms, ground-loop currents in the coax shields add errors; a common-mode choke or a ground-loop suppression technique is standard practice.
- Compare against a vendor S-parameter or SPICE model of the part. If the measured $f_0$ is lower than the model's, the difference is your mounting inductance: $L_{mount} = 1/((2\pi f_{0,meas})^2 C) - ESL$. For example, a 100 nF part measured at 9.2 MHz implies a total of 3.0 nH.

A rough check without a VNA: a fast current step (a switched load) with a differential probe on the rail shows the droop; compare $\Delta V$ against $L\,di/dt$ to extract the inductance. It is crude but fast.

::: applications
- **Choosing capacitor sizes and values** for PDN networks: smaller packages (lower ESL) for the highest-frequency tier.
- **Layout reviews**: judging via and fan-out inductance for each decoupling capacitor.
- **Simulation**: using vendor S-parameter or SPICE models that include ESL and ESR.
- **EMC fixes**: adding small capacitors where bypassing at 100 MHz+ is needed, with attention to mounting.
- **Measuring**: VNA shunt-through measurements to find the real SRF of a mounted capacitor.

::: assumptions
- Series RLC model, valid up to the frequency where the capacitor's own distributed behaviour matters (hundreds of MHz to GHz for small parts).
- ESR is constant; in reality ESR varies with frequency and dielectric.
- Capacitance is constant; ceramic capacitance varies with DC bias and temperature.
- Mounting inductance values are examples; board stackup changes them substantially.
- Another valid answer is to describe it as "a capacitor in series with a resonant circuit" in the time domain: the response to a current step has an $L\,di/dt$ spike.
:::

::: followups
### What's the impedance of a 100 nF capacitor with 1 nH at 100 MHz?
Above resonance: $2\pi f L=2\pi\times10^8\times10^{-9}=0.63\ \Omega$ (the capacitive term $1/\omega C=16$ mΩ barely matters). SRF is 15.9 MHz.

### Why does a bigger capacitor not help at high frequency?
Because the impedance is $2\pi f L$ there, independent of $C$. A bigger-value part is in a bigger package with more ESL, so it's often worse above its own resonance.

### How does parasitic inductance create the anti-resonance peak?
Between two capacitors' self-resonances the larger one is inductive and the smaller one is still capacitive, so the inductance of one resonates with the capacitance of the other in parallel. The peak is limited by ESR; adding ESR or an intermediate value flattens it.

### How would you measure the capacitor's real inductance on your board?
Two-port shunt-through measurement with a VNA across the mounted capacitor, or a simpler impedance sweep; the SRF gives $L=1/((2\pi f_0)^2C)$. For example, SRF 10 MHz and 100 nF gives $L=2.5$ nH.

### Scenario: your 100 nF capacitors show resonance at 8 MHz instead of the datasheet's 20 MHz. What happened?
Mounting inductance. $L=1/((2\pi\times8\text{ MHz})^2\times100\text{ nF})=3.96$ nH versus the part's 0.63 nH. I'd look at via lengths, fan-out traces, the plane depth, and the return path, and fix the biggest contributor first.
:::
