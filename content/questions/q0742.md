::: basics
### In plain words
In real hardware, 0 and 1 are voltage ranges, not exact voltages. A sending chip promises to keep its 1 above one voltage and its 0 below another. A receiver accepts anything above a threshold as a 1 and anything below a lower threshold as a 0. The receiver's rules are looser than the sender's promises, which leaves room for noise (small unwanted wiggles).

### Picture it
Imagine judging a room cold or warm by a thermometer. You call it cold below 15 °C and warm above 25 °C, and between them you are unsure. A heating company promises its warm rooms are at least 28 °C and its cold ones at most 12 °C. Their promises beat your rules by 3 degrees, so small thermometer errors can't fool you.

### Words you need
- **Logic level**: the voltage range that counts as a 1 or a 0.
- **$V_{OH}$ and $V_{OL}$**: the lowest voltage a sender guarantees for a 1, and the highest it guarantees for a 0.
- **$V_{IH}$ and $V_{IL}$**: the lowest voltage a receiver requires to read a 1, and the highest it accepts as a 0.
- **Noise margin**: the gap between the sender's promise and the receiver's need.
- **Undefined region**: input voltages between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$, where the reading is not guaranteed.
- **Floating input**: a pin connected to nothing, so its voltage drifts.

### The simplest example
In a common 3.3 V chip family (chips built to the same rules), a sender guarantees at least 2.4 V for a 1 and at most 0.4 V for a 0. The receiver needs at least 2.0 V for a 1 and at most 0.8 V for a 0. That is 0.4 V of noise margin on each side. Now connect a 1.8 V sensor to it. Its high of 1.8 V is below 2.0 V, so it may not read as a 1. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers how thresholds vary and why mixed voltages need checking.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand that "0" and "1" in real hardware are **voltage ranges, not exact voltages**, and that the ranges promised by a driver are deliberately tighter than the ranges demanded by a receiver. The interviewer wants to hear the four numbers ($V_{OL}$, $V_{OH}$, $V_{IL}$, $V_{IH}$), the idea of noise margin, the forbidden or undefined region in the middle, and the fact that these numbers depend on the logic family and supply voltage. A strong candidate connects it to practice: why a 3.3 V part cannot be trusted to read a 1.8 V signal, and why a floating input sits in the undefined region.
:::

::: why
Logic levels are the contract between two chips. Almost every "it doesn't work" digital bring-up problem involves a level somewhere: a signal that doesn't reach $V_{IH}$, a low that doesn't get below $V_{IL}$ because of a pull-up that is too strong, an I2C bus whose low level is raised by too much current, or two voltage domains connected without translation. Interviewers use this basic question to see if you think of a digital signal as an analog voltage with thresholds, which is the mindset that lets you debug with a scope instead of guessing.
:::

::: concepts
- **Digital abstraction**: map a continuous voltage into two states with a gap between them so noise can be tolerated ([[q0733]]).
- **Output levels** $V_{OL}$ and $V_{OH}$: the worst-case voltages a driver guarantees for a 0 and a 1 under a specified load.
- **Input thresholds** $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$: the voltages a receiver is guaranteed to interpret as 0 and as 1.
- **Noise margin**: the gap between what a driver guarantees and what a receiver requires ([[q0743]]).
- **Push-pull vs open-drain outputs**: determine where the high level comes from ([[q0761]]).
- **Floating inputs and pull resistors**: why unconnected inputs are undefined ([[q0780]]).
:::

::: answer
"Logic levels are the voltage ranges that a digital circuit treats as a 0 and as a 1. They're ranges, not single values, because real signals have noise and loading.

Each pin has four numbers on its datasheet. On the output side, $V_{OL}$ is the highest voltage a driver might produce for a 0, and $V_{OH}$ is the lowest it might produce for a 1. On the input side, $V_{IL}$ is the highest voltage the receiver will definitely read as 0, and $V_{IH}$ is the lowest voltage it will definitely read as 1. Between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ is an undefined region where the receiver might read either, or oscillate.

For a link to work, the driver's worst case must clear the receiver's requirement: $V_{OH}$ above $V_{IH}$, and $V_{OL}$ below $V_{IL}$. The gaps are the noise margins. For 3.3 V LVTTL, for example, $V_{OH}$ is at least 2.4 V and $V_{IH}$ is 2.0 V, giving 0.4 V of noise margin on the high side, and the same on the low side.

The numbers depend on the family and supply. CMOS thresholds are fractions of the supply, around 30% and 70%, while old TTL used fixed values of 0.8 V and 2.0 V. That's why connecting chips at different voltages needs checking or level shifting."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions (all worst-case, over temperature, supply and specified load current):**

- $V_{OL,max}$: maximum output voltage for a logic 0 (at sink current $I_{OL}$).
- $V_{OH,min}$: minimum output voltage for a logic 1 (at source current $I_{OH}$).
- $V_{IL,max}$: maximum input voltage guaranteed to be read as 0.
- $V_{IH,min}$: minimum input voltage guaranteed to be read as 1.

**Compatibility conditions** for driver $A$ driving receiver $B$:
$$V_{OH,min}(A) > V_{IH,min}(B), \qquad V_{OL,max}(A) < V_{IL,max}(B)$$

**Noise margins:**
$$NM_H = V_{OH,min} - V_{IH,min}, \qquad NM_L = V_{IL,max} - V_{OL,max}$$

A signal with noise within $NM_H$ or $NM_L$ is still read correctly. Example, 3.3 V LVTTL-style levels ($V_{OH}\ge2.4$ V, $V_{OL}\le0.4$ V, $V_{IH}\ge2.0$ V, $V_{IL}\le0.8$ V): $NM_H = 0.4$ V and $NM_L = 0.4$ V. For 5 V CMOS with $V_{IH} = 0.7V_{DD} = 3.5$ V, $V_{IL} = 0.3V_{DD} = 1.5$ V, $V_{OH} \approx 4.4$ V and $V_{OL} \approx 0.1$ V under light load: $NM_H = 0.9$ V, $NM_L = 1.4$ V.

**Thresholds relate to the transfer curve.** On a CMOS gate's voltage-transfer curve, $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ are conventionally the points where the small-signal gain is $-1$, and a symmetric inverter switches at about $V_{DD}/2$. Because the gate has gain greater than 1 in between, a degraded input is *restored* toward the rail at the output, which is what lets digital systems cascade many gates without accumulating noise.

**Dependence on load.** $V_{OH}$ falls as the source current rises, and $V_{OL}$ rises as the sink current rises, which is why datasheets quote them "at $I_{OH} = -x$ mA". Driving an LED directly from a GPIO pulls $V_{OH}$ far below the lightly loaded value.

**Undefined region.** Between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ the receiver may read either value, draw excess shoot-through current (both transistors partly on), or oscillate. Schmitt-trigger inputs add hysteresis ($V_{T+} > V_{T-}$) so slow or noisy edges don't cause multiple transitions.
:::

A driver guarantees $V_{OL}$ at most and $V_{OH}$ at least; a receiver requires $V_{IL}$ at most for a 0 and $V_{IH}$ at least for a 1, with an undefined gap in between. The link works if $V_{OH}$ is above $V_{IH}$ and $V_{OL}$ is below $V_{IL}$; the leftover is noise margin. CMOS thresholds scale with the supply, roughly 30% and 70% of $V_{DD}$, so mixed-voltage systems need level checks."
:::

::: long
"Logic levels are how a digital circuit turns a continuous voltage into a 0 or a 1. They're voltage ranges rather than exact values, because real signals have noise, loading and variation.

On a datasheet there are four numbers per pin. On the output: $V_{OL}$, the highest voltage the driver might give for a 0, and $V_{OH}$, the lowest voltage it might give for a 1, both specified at a given load current. On the input: $V_{IL}$, the highest voltage that's guaranteed to read as a 0, and $V_{IH}$, the lowest voltage that's guaranteed to read as a 1. In between, the receiver's behaviour is undefined: it might see 0, or 1, or burn extra current as both transistors in its input stage conduct.

The design rule is that the driver's worst case must clear the receiver's requirement. $V_{OH}$ from the driver must be above $V_{IH}$ of the receiver, and $V_{OL}$ must be below $V_{IL}$. The margin left over is the noise margin, $NM_H = V_{OH} - V_{IH}$ and $NM_L = V_{IL} - V_{OL}$. For classic LVTTL 3.3 V that's 2.4 minus 2.0, so 0.4 V high, and 0.8 minus 0.4, so 0.4 V low. A noise spike smaller than that won't flip the bit.

The numbers depend on the logic family and the supply. TTL used fixed thresholds, 0.8 V and 2.0 V. CMOS thresholds scale with the supply, around 30% and 70% of $V_{DD}$. That's why you can't just wire a 1.8 V output to a 3.3 V input: 1.8 V is below the roughly 2.0 V the 3.3 V part needs for a 1, so you add a level shifter or choose parts that share a voltage.

Also, logic levels depend on load. $V_{OH}$ droops as the source current rises, so a GPIO driving an LED isn't giving you the datasheet's headline number. And a floating input sits in the undefined region, which is why you add pull-ups or pull-downs."
:::

::: terms
- **Logic level**: a voltage range interpreted as logic 0 or logic 1.
- **$V_{OH}$ / $V_{OL}$**: guaranteed output high (minimum) and output low (maximum) voltages.
- **$V_{IH}$ / $V_{IL}$**: required input high (minimum) and input low (maximum) thresholds.
- **Noise margin**: how much noise a signal can tolerate before it is misread ($NM_H$, $NM_L$).
- **Undefined (forbidden) region**: input voltages between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ where the result is not guaranteed.
- **Hysteresis / Schmitt trigger**: an input with two thresholds that rejects slow or noisy edges.
- **Logic family**: a set of parts with matched levels, such as TTL, HC CMOS, LVCMOS.
- **Rail-to-rail**: an output that swings (almost) the full supply range.
- **Regeneration**: a gate's gain restores a degraded input to a clean level at its output.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a voltage range for a 1 and a voltage range for a 0, like 0 V and 3.3 V."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Says **ranges, not values**, and names the four parameters ($V_{OL}$, $V_{OH}$, $V_{IL}$, $V_{IH}$) with the *direction* of each (max/min).
- Explains why **output specs are tighter than input specs** and defines **noise margin** with a number.
- Notes the **undefined region** and what happens in it (indeterminate reading, shoot-through current), tying it to floating inputs.
- Points out **supply and family dependence** (TTL fixed; CMOS ratiometric) and the practical consequence for mixing voltage domains.
- Mentions **load dependence** of $V_{OH}$/$V_{OL}$, and Schmitt triggers for slow or noisy inputs.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Logic high is 3.3 V and low is 0 V."** Those are nominal rail voltages. Actual levels are ranges with thresholds, and an output may only reach $V_{OH}$ = 2.4 V with a loaded pin.
- **Mixing up the output and input numbers.** $V_{OH,min}$ is a *guarantee* from the driver; $V_{IH,min}$ is a *requirement* of the receiver. They are not the same value.
- **Assuming thresholds are always at $V_{DD}/2$.** That is the switching point of a symmetric inverter. The guaranteed $V_{IL}$/$V_{IH}$ are further apart.
- **Using the 5 V TTL numbers for a 3.3 V CMOS part (or vice versa).** Family and supply determine the thresholds.
- **Ignoring $V_{OL}$ on open-drain buses.** On I2C, a $V_{OL}$ above $V_{IL}$ of the receiver ($0.3V_{DD}$) breaks communication ([[q0760]]).
- **Leaving inputs floating.** A floating CMOS input drifts through the undefined region, draws extra current and can oscillate ([[q0780]]).
:::

::: deep
## From voltage to bit

Inside a chip, every signal is a voltage on a wire. A **logic level** is the convention that turns voltage ranges into bits. The idea is the **digital abstraction**: a range of voltages near the low rail means 0, a range near the high rail means 1, and a gap between them is not used. Because any gate output lands inside its "valid" band, and any gate input accepts a wider band, small amounts of noise or voltage drop are absorbed and can't accumulate.

The receiver's requirements are looser than the driver's guarantees on purpose. That is what leaves room for the noise margin, which absorbs ground bounce, crosstalk, IR drop on a long trace, and supply noise between the two chips.

### Noise margin as a budget

Anything that moves the signal at the receiver eats margin. For a 1:

$$V_{rx} = V_{OH} - V_{IR\ drop} - V_{crosstalk} - V_{ground\ shift} \ge V_{IH}$$

With $NM_H = 0.4$ V in the LVTTL example, 0.4 V of combined disturbance is the whole budget. Illustrative contributors: 50 mV of crosstalk, 100 mV of ground bounce, 100 mV of ground or supply offset between the chips. Which is why 3.3 V LVTTL on a noisy board can fail even when the "voltages look fine" on a quiet scope capture.

## Why CMOS levels scale with the supply

A CMOS inverter is a PMOS pull-up and an NMOS pull-down driven by the same gate voltage. Its switching threshold sits where both transistors conduct equally, near $V_{DD}/2$ for a balanced design. The guaranteed $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ are placed on either side, commonly $0.3V_{DD}$ and $0.7V_{DD}$. Because everything is ratio-metric, a 1.8 V CMOS part has $V_{IH} \approx 1.26$ V and a 5 V part has 3.5 V. Plug the same 2.4 V signal into both: the 1.8 V part may be damaged by over-voltage, and the 5 V part sees it inside its undefined band (1.5 V to 3.5 V), so it is not guaranteed to read a 1.

## Where the high level comes from

For **push-pull** outputs (CMOS GPIO, SPI, UART) the PMOS actively pulls the pin to near $V_{DD}$, so $V_{OH}$ follows the chip's supply minus a drop $I_{OH}R_{on}$. For **open-drain** outputs (I2C, interrupt lines) the chip only pulls low; the high level is whatever voltage the pull-up resistor connects to. That is why an open-drain bus can mix voltage domains simply by choosing the pull-up rail, within the pin's tolerance ([[q0761]]).

## Load dependence: a worked example

A GPIO has $R_{on,P} = 25\ \Omega$ in the high state and $V_{DD} = 3.3$ V. Driving a 10 mA LED load (the datasheet may not even allow it) gives

$$V_{OH} = 3.3 - 0.010\times25 = 3.05\text{ V}$$

but a specified $V_{OH,min}$ of 2.4 V at 8 mA is much worse than the typical value. Always size loads from the spec's guaranteed numbers, not from "3.3 V". For the low side with $R_{on,N} = 20\ \Omega$ at 10 mA: $V_{OL} = 0.2$ V, fine for a CMOS input ($V_{IL} \approx 1$ V).

## The undefined region in practice

Leave a CMOS input floating and it drifts into the band between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ through leakage and coupled noise. In that zone both the input stage's PMOS and NMOS conduct, so the gate draws "crowbar" current, and the output may toggle, ring or oscillate. Three practical fixes:

- **Pull resistors** (10 kΩ external, or a 20 to 50 kΩ internal) hold unused inputs at a defined level ([[q0780]]).
- **Schmitt-trigger inputs** use two thresholds (a higher one for rising edges, a lower one for falling edges, set per part), so a slow ramp can't make the output chatter.
- **Tie off unused inputs** of logic gates; never leave them open.

## Beyond single-ended CMOS

Not all links use thresholds relative to ground. RS-485, CAN, LVDS and USB look at the **difference** between two wires, so the "level" is a differential voltage (hundreds of millivolts to a few volts) and common-mode noise is rejected. Their level specs are covered in [[q0743]].

## Quick sanity check you can do on the bench

Scope the signal at the *receiver's pin* (not the driver's), with a short ground spring. Check that highs exceed $V_{IH}$ and lows are below $V_{IL}$ with margin, including during switching activity of neighbouring signals. If you only have a multimeter, you can check static levels, but not the dynamic dips and spikes where margin is usually lost.

::: applications
- **Mixed-voltage boards**: a 1.8 V sensor, a 3.3 V MCU and a 5 V peripheral need level checks or translators.
- **I2C bring-up**: $V_{OL}$ and the 0.3/0.7 $V_{DD}$ thresholds decide pull-up sizing ([[q0760]]).
- **Reading switches and buttons**: a pull-up plus switch gives a defined high and a $V_{OL}$ of 0 V; add debounce.
- **GPIO driving LEDs or transistors**: use the datasheet's $V_{OH}$/$V_{OL}$ at the specified current, not rail values.
- **Legacy interfaces**: 5 V TTL-compatible peripherals (displays, old logic) feeding 3.3 V MCUs.
- **Differential links** (LVDS camera/display links, RS-485 fieldbuses) where "level" means a differential swing.
:::

::: assumptions
- Numbers quoted are the common **LVTTL (3.3 V)** values ($V_{IH}=2.0$ V, $V_{IL}=0.8$ V, $V_{OH}=2.4$ V, $V_{OL}=0.4$ V) and generic CMOS ratios (0.7/0.3 $V_{DD}$); the specific part's datasheet always rules.
- Output levels are quoted **at specified load currents**; at lighter loads $V_{OH}$ is closer to the rail and $V_{OL}$ closer to ground.
- The treatment is for **single-ended logic**. Differential and analog-signalling standards use different definitions.
- "Logic level" is also used casually to mean the **I/O supply voltage** ("3.3 V logic"). That is how the term is used in the next question ([[q0743]]).
- Thresholds also vary with **temperature and process**, so datasheet min/max values already include margin for those.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 5 V CMOS output values ($V_{OH}\approx4.4$ V, $V_{OL}\approx0.1$ V) are typical of HC-family parts at light load. Other 5 V CMOS families (AHC, LVC at 5 V, MCUs) differ; confirm against the datasheet.
:::

::: followups
### What happens if a signal sits at 1.4 V on a 3.3 V LVTTL input?
It's in the undefined region (between 0.8 V and 2.0 V). The gate might read 0, read 1, or produce a slow, noisy output transition; the input stage conducts crowbar current while both transistors are partly on, so it also wastes power. This is typical of a floating input or a signal that is too slow. Fix with a pull-up/pull-down, a faster edge, or a Schmitt-trigger input.

### A 1.8 V sensor drives a 3.3 V MCU input. Why might this fail even though 1.8 V is "above half"?
The MCU's $V_{IH}$ may be 2.0 V (LVTTL) or $0.7\times3.3 = 2.31$ V (CMOS). The 1.8 V driver tops out below both, so the margin is negative: reads would be marginal and temperature-dependent. Use a level shifter, a part with a separate I/O supply, or an MCU pin bank configured for 1.8 V.

### Why do output specs differ from input specs? Why not one set of numbers?
Because the driver must be *guaranteed* to give at least $V_{OH}$ and the receiver must be *guaranteed* to accept at least $V_{IH}$. If the two numbers were equal there would be no margin: any noise would flip the bit. The difference is the noise margin, which is the safety budget for all the disturbances between the two chips.

### An I2C target pulls SDA low only to 0.5 V on a 3.3 V bus. Is that a problem?
The receiver side is fine: $V_{IL} = 0.3\times3.3 = 0.99$ V, so a 0.5 V low has about 0.49 V of margin. But the I2C driver spec is $V_{OL} \le 0.4$ V at 3 mA, so 0.5 V means the driver is out of spec: the pull-up may be sinking too much current (resistor too small), or the driver is weak or hot. Check the pull-up value against $R_{p,min}=(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$. See [[q0760]].

### How does a Schmitt trigger change the logic-level story?
It has two thresholds, rising $V_{T+}$ and falling $V_{T-}$, with hysteresis $V_{T+}-V_{T-}$. The output flips only when the input crosses the far threshold, so a slow or noisy edge produces one clean transition. It effectively widens noise margin for that input, at the price of making the thresholds asymmetric.
:::

::: interpretations
"What are logic levels?" can mean (1) the **voltage thresholds** for 0 and 1 ($V_{IL}$, $V_{IH}$, $V_{OL}$, $V_{OH}$), which is the technical definition used here, or (2) the **voltage standard** of a part ("3.3 V logic"), covered in the next question [[q0743]]. A complete answer defines the first and mentions the second. It is also asked in the I2C context in [[q0762]] and in the SPI context in [[q0769]]; those pages apply these ideas to each bus.

::: rubric
- Defines logic levels as voltage ranges, not exact values :: The central idea; "3.3 V = 1" is a common oversimplification.
- Names the four parameters and states which are guarantees (outputs) and which are requirements (inputs) :: Shows you can read a datasheet.
- States the compatibility condition: $V_{OH} > V_{IH}$ and $V_{OL} < V_{IL}$ :: This is the practical rule for connecting chips.
- Defines noise margin and computes one for a real family :: Quantitative understanding.
- Describes the undefined region and floating inputs :: Connects theory to a classic bug.
- Notes family/supply dependence (TTL fixed vs CMOS ratiometric) and load dependence :: Shows breadth and real-world awareness.
- Mentions level shifting or mixed-voltage implications :: Leads naturally into the next questions.
