::: basics
### In plain words
Capacitors do not all fail the same way. The type decides the failure. Ceramic ones are brittle and crack, usually shorting. Aluminum electrolytics slowly dry out, losing capacitance and gaining resistance. Tantalum ones can fail short suddenly under a surge and may burn. Film capacitors are the gentlest. Knowing the type tells you what to prevent.

### Picture it
Think of three household items. A ceramic capacitor is a dinner plate: strong until it is bent, and then it cracks. An electrolytic is a wet sponge: it dries out slowly, faster when hot. A tantalum is like a fuse that can flare when overloaded: fine most of the time, then sudden when pushed. The comparison is rough, but it matches how each type tends to fail.

### Words you need
- **MLCC**: multilayer ceramic capacitor, the small ceramic chip type.
- **Dry-out**: the electrolyte in an electrolytic evaporating, which raises ESR and lowers capacitance.
- **Derating**: using a part well below its voltage or ripple rating for margin. See [[q0019]].
- **Inrush current**: a surge into an uncharged capacitor when power is applied.
- **Thermal runaway**: a part heating, conducting more, and heating even more until it fails.

### The simplest example
A board flexes during assembly and a 100 nF ceramic on the 5 V rail cracks. The crack lets the inner plates touch, so the rail shorts to ground and the part heats up. For an electrolytic, a rule of thumb is that life roughly doubles for every 10 °C cooler, so a part run 20 °C cooler lasts about four times as long. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page lists each type's modes, causes and prevention. See also [[q0030]].
:::

::: testing
Whether you know that **capacitor families fail in very different ways**, and which ones are dangerous. The interviewer wants you to:

1. Separate the types: MLCC (ceramic), aluminum electrolytic, tantalum/polymer, film.
2. Give the dominant mechanism for each: ceramic cracks (short), electrolytic dry-out and ESR rise (drift/open), tantalum short and thermal runaway, film self-healing.
3. Name the stresses: overvoltage, reverse polarity, ripple current/self-heating, temperature, board flex, thermal shock, inrush, humidity.
4. Tie them to design practice: voltage derating, temperature and life calculation (Arrhenius ~2× per 10 °C), part orientation, series/flex-term parts, fusing, and what a failed short does to the rail.
:::

::: why
Capacitors are the most common cause of field failures on many boards, and their failure modes are often *dangerous*: a cracked MLCC on a power rail is a dead short that can overheat and burn the board; a tantalum shorted by an inrush can ignite; an electrolytic that dries out slowly raises ripple and breaks regulator loops long before it opens. Understanding the mechanisms lets you derate correctly, pick the right type for each rail, and diagnose "the power supply died after two years".
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor types and dielectrics** ([[q0023]]): ceramic, electrolytic (wet), tantalum (MnO₂ or polymer), film.
- **Parasitics** ([[q0030]]): ESR sets heating $P=I^2R_{esr}$; leakage and DA are related to dielectric quality.
- **Voltage derating**: stress and failure rate rise steeply with the electric field in the dielectric.
- **Temperature and life**: electrolyte evaporation follows an Arrhenius-type law; a common rule is life doubles per 10 °C drop.
- **Mechanical stress**: ceramic is brittle; board flex and thermal shock crack it.
- **Failure mode analysis** ([[q0019]]): what the failure does to the circuit (short vs open vs drift).
:::

::: answer
"It depends on the type, and that's the point. A ceramic capacitor, an MLCC, is brittle: if the board flexes or there's a thermal shock, it can crack, and a crack through the electrodes usually causes a short, or a leaky part, which on a power rail can overheat. Ceramics are also derated by DC bias and fail from overvoltage.

An aluminum electrolytic wears out. The electrolyte slowly evaporates, especially when hot, so the capacitance falls and the ESR rises, which means more ripple and more heating, and it eventually fails open or dries out. Life roughly doubles for every 10 degrees cooler. Overvoltage, reverse polarity, or too much ripple current can make it vent or burst.

A tantalum capacitor fails short, and it can do that suddenly with a surge or inrush current, and then go into thermal runaway and burn, which is why you derate the voltage by about 50 percent and limit inrush. Polymer versions are better. Film capacitors are the most benign; they can self-heal small breakdowns, and mainly lose capacitance slowly.

To prevent it, I derate voltage, check the ripple current rating and temperature, avoid placing ceramics near board edges or mounting holes, use flex-term or series arrangements on critical rails, limit inrush into tantalums, and choose the type according to the failure mode I can live with."
:::

| MLCC (ceramic) | Cracking (flex/thermal shock), dielectric breakdown, IR degradation | Short or low-resistance leakage; sometimes open | Board flex, depanelization, thermal gradient during soldering, over-voltage | Orientation parallel to bend, keep off edges, flex-termination or soft-term parts, derate, series pair |

| Tantalum/aluminium polymer | Reduced but similar breakdown, moisture sensitivity | Short or ESR drift | Over-voltage, humidity, reflow | Follow MSL handling, derate |
| Film | Self-healing breakdown; capacitance loss over life | Gradual C loss; rarely short | Over-voltage, humidity, high dv/dt | Rating margin; metallized types for self-healing |
| Supercapacitor | Voltage balance, electrolyte aging | C↓, ESR↑ | Over-voltage, heat | Balancing, derating |

**Electrolytic life (rule of thumb):** $L=L_0\cdot2^{(T_{max}-T)/10}$, with $T$ the *core* temperature including ripple self-heating, $L_0$ the rated life at $T_{max}$ (e.g. 2000 h at 105 °C). Example: 2000 h at 105 °C → 8000 h at 85 °C → 32,000 h at 65 °C.

**Ripple heating:** $P=I_{rms}^2R_{esr}$; core temperature rise $\Delta T=P\,\theta$ (K/W).

**MLCC energy:** a short in a charged capacitor can release $\tfrac12CV^2$; 3 mJ for 10 µF at 25 V is small, but the sustained supply current through the short is the hazard.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Derating factors (50 % for tantalum, 80 % or less for MLCC), the "×2 per 10 °C" rule, and reliability figures depend on the manufacturer and series. Use the vendor's life and derating guidelines.
:::

::: long
"The answer depends on the technology, so let me go through the main ones.

Ceramic capacitors, MLCCs, are hard and brittle. The most common field failure is a crack from board flexing, depanelization, a screw tightened near it, or thermal shock during soldering. A crack can short the electrodes or let leakage increase, and on a power rail a shorted ceramic is a direct short which can overheat and char the board. They also fail from overvoltage and from degradation of insulation resistance at high temperature and voltage. So I keep them away from board edges and mounting holes, orient them parallel to the bend axis, derate the voltage, and use flex-termination parts or two in series where a short would be catastrophic.

Aluminum electrolytics are wear-out parts. The electrolyte evaporates through the seal, faster when hot, so capacitance drops and ESR climbs. That means more ripple, more heating, and a positive feedback toward failure, ultimately open or dried out. Rated life is something like 2000 hours at 105 degrees, and it roughly doubles per 10 degrees cooler, so running at 65 degrees gives about 32,000 hours. Overvoltage and reverse polarity cause gas, venting or bursting. They also stop working in the cold when ESR rises.

Tantalum capacitors, with manganese dioxide, fail short. A surge or inrush can cause a local breakdown, which heats, and then thermal runaway can ignite. So derate to about 50 percent of rated voltage, limit inrush, and consider polymer types, which are safer.

Film capacitors are the most benign: metallized film self-heals small breakdowns by vaporizing the metal near the fault, so they slowly lose capacitance.

Beyond the part itself, I think about what the failure does to the system: a short needs protection like a fuse or current limit, and an open needs the circuit to remain safe."
:::

::: terms
- **MLCC**: multilayer ceramic chip capacitor.
- **Flex cracking**: mechanical cracks due to PCB bending.
- **Dry-out**: electrolyte evaporation in an aluminum electrolytic.
- **ESR drift**: rise of ESR as a capacitor ages.
- **Thermal runaway**: self-reinforcing heating (tantalum short → heat → more current).
- **Self-healing**: metallized film clears a local breakdown by vaporizing the electrode.
- **Derating**: operating below the rated voltage/temperature/ripple.
- **Ripple current rating**: maximum rms AC current for the stated life.
- **Insulation resistance (IR)**: DC resistance of the dielectric, degrades with failure.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "capacitors fail short or open, from overvoltage or heat".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Organizes by technology and gives the dominant mode for each (crack/short, dry-out/drift, short/ignition, self-heal).
- Quantifies life with the Arrhenius rule (2× per 10 °C) and ripple self-heating ($I^2R_{esr}$).
- Mentions derating rules with numbers (50 % tantalum, 50–80 % MLCC and electrolytic) and the reason (field strength).
- Describes mitigation by design: layout (orientation, board flex), part choice (flex-term, polymer), protection (fuse, inrush limit), series MLCC.
- Considers the consequence: a shorted bypass cap kills the rail; an open decoupling cap raises noise; an ESR increase destabilizes a regulator.
- Knows to diagnose: ESR meter, IR/leakage test, thermal camera, visual (bulging, vent), X-ray or cross-section for cracks.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating all capacitors alike.** Ceramics, electrolytics and tantalums have different dominant failure modes and different rules.
- **Using tantalum at full rated voltage on a low-impedance rail.** The inrush and surge cause failure; derate and limit inrush.
- **Ignoring ripple current.** $I^2R_{esr}$ heats the core, shortening electrolytic life dramatically.
- **Placing MLCCs near the board edge or V-score.** Flex cracks are a manufacturing and assembly issue.
- **Thinking electrolytics fail suddenly only.** They drift first (ESR, C), often causing a regulator or filter to misbehave long before an open.
- **Reversing polarity** of a polarized capacitor, even briefly.
- **Using the nominal capacitance after DC-bias derating** and concluding the part has "failed" when it is just a class-II effect.
:::

::: deep
## Ceramic (MLCC): brittle, and a short is the danger

An MLCC is a stack of thin ceramic layers (micrometers) between electrodes, sintered into a hard block. It has a high Young's modulus and almost no ductility. When the PCB bends (during depanelization, press-fit connector insertion, mounting-screw tightening, or thermal expansion mismatch), strain is transferred through the solder joints into the ceramic, concentrated near the terminations, where cracks start and propagate diagonally through the stack. A crack through multiple electrodes can short the plates directly or create a leakage path that heats and shorts later, particularly as moisture enters. The failure is then a **low-resistance short** across whatever the capacitor decouples. A cracked 10 µF capacitor on a 3.3 V rail connected to a supply that can deliver amps will dissipate watts and char the board.

Other ceramic stresses:
- **Thermal shock:** fast soldering or rework (hot air) creates gradients; larger parts (1206 and up) crack more readily.
- **DC bias and temperature:** class-II capacitance falls with bias; not a failure, but apparent loss.
- **Overvoltage / high field:** dielectric breakdown, insulation-resistance degradation at high temperature, **highly accelerated life test (HALT)** type stresses reveal infant mortality.
- **Aging and piezo noise:** not failures, but a source of microphonics.

Mitigation: orient the part's long axis parallel to the likely bend; keep it ≥ 5 mm away from board edges, V-groove, screw holes, connectors; **soft/flexible termination** (polymer-epoxy layer in the end cap) increases strain tolerance; use smaller case sizes (less lever arm); for safety-critical rails, **two capacitors in series** (so one short doesn't short the rail) or use a fused/limited supply; check with strain-gauge tests on the assembly line.

## Aluminum electrolytic: wear-out

Liquid electrolyte slowly evaporates through the seal and degrades the oxide: capacitance drops (typical end-of-life criterion: −20 % C, ESR ×2 to ×3, leakage above spec). Life follows an Arrhenius-like law, approximated as doubling for each 10 °C reduction in core temperature:

Ripple heating: with $R_{esr}=0.15$ Ω and $I_{rms}=1$ A, $P=0.15$ W; at 40 K/W the core is 6 K above ambient, which cuts the life by a factor of $2^{0.6}\approx1.5$. Failure signs: bulging or vented top, brown electrolyte residue, ESR increase, noisy rails, or a switching regulator that becomes unstable or overheats. Reverse polarity or overvoltage causes gas generation and venting.

Cold: ESR rises sharply at low temperature (−20 °C and below), which can break a regulator loop that worked at room temperature.

## Tantalum: fail short, can ignite

MnO₂ tantalum capacitors have a thin Ta₂O₅ dielectric with MnO₂ as the cathode. A local defect can conduct, heating the surrounding MnO₂; this can convert it to a poor conductor (self-healing in small events) or, if the energy is high, run away: the short draws more current, which heats more, and the tantalum can burn. Typical triggers: **inrush** into an uncharged capacitor from a low-impedance supply (e.g. 5 V across 0.1 Ω gives 50 A), voltage transients, reverse voltage, and operation near rated voltage. Standard practice: derate to 50 % of rated voltage (vendors recommend deeper derating for low-impedance sources), add series resistance or soft-start, and use a fused or polymer type. Polymer tantalums and polymer aluminums have different (generally benign) failure behavior and lower ESR but are moisture sensitive and have their own voltage limits.

## Film and supercaps

Metallized film capacitors **self-heal**: a local breakdown vaporizes the thin metallization around the fault, isolating it; capacitance falls slowly with each event. Failure comes from over-voltage, high dv/dt, humidity, or heat, and is generally gentle. Supercapacitors fail from overvoltage on a cell (series strings need balancing), heat, and electrolyte aging.

## Diagnosing a suspect capacitor

1. **Visual:** bulged or vented top, discoloration, cracks, tombstoned or lifted parts.
2. **Thermal:** a hot capacitor under power or a shorted rail with a thermal camera or injecting a limited current ("smoke test" with a current-limited supply).
3. **Electrical:** ESR meter (in-circuit for electrolytics), LCR meter, insulation resistance/leakage test; compare with a known-good unit.
4. **Cross-section/X-ray** for cracks in MLCCs after a rework.
5. **Root-cause:** correlate with process steps (depanelization, soldering), environment (temperature, humidity), and electrical stress (ripple current, inrush).
:::

::: applications
- **Switching power supplies**: input/output electrolytics and polymer caps sized by ripple current and life.
- **Automotive and industrial boards**: AEC-Q200 MLCCs with flex termination, series configurations for safety.
- **Consumer electronics**: replacing electrolytics that dry out (capacitor plague in older boards).
- **Power-sequencing/inrush circuits** limiting surge into tantalum or polymer capacitors.
- **Mains filter and snubber capacitors** using X/Y safety-rated, self-healing film types.
- **Reliability engineering**: HALT/ALT testing, FMEA for decoupling networks.
:::

::: assumptions
- Failure descriptions cover mainstream types; exotic or newer parts (organic polymer hybrids, ceramic with special terminations) have different details.
- The "life halves per 10 °C" rule is an approximation (a pure Arrhenius model with an activation energy around 0.5 eV would give only about 6× for a 40 K drop, versus 16× from the rule); vendors publish their own formulas.
- Derating percentages are common practice, not universal.
- The question assumes a typical board environment; space, high-voltage and RF applications have more mechanisms (radiation, corona, microphonics).
- Failure rates (FIT) are not quoted because they depend on standards and quality levels.
:::

::: followups
### Why do cracked MLCCs often fail short and not open?
The crack propagates across electrode layers of alternating polarity; the cracked surface becomes conductive through metal smearing, contamination or moisture, forming a path between the plates. It may start as a leakage path that heats and carbonizes the ceramic, eventually becoming a low-resistance short. An open can occur if the crack separates the terminations from the stack, but a short is the dangerous and more common outcome.

### Why derate tantalum capacitors by 50 %?
The dielectric is very thin, and failures are driven by field strength and surge current; derating reduces the field and the failure probability, which rises steeply with voltage. Recommendations vary by manufacturer and by the source impedance of the circuit (lower impedance, more derating).

### An electrolytic rated 2000 h at 105 °C runs at a 75 °C core temperature. What is its expected life?
$2000\cdot2^{(105-75)/10}=2000\cdot8=16{,}000$ h, about 1.8 years of continuous operation. At a core of 65 °C it would be 32,000 h (3.7 years); for a 10-year (≈88,000 h) life you'd want the core near 50 °C (90,000 h), or a long-life series.

### What if a switching regulator suddenly oscillates after two years in service?
Aging electrolytic output capacitors have lost capacitance and gained ESR (maybe 2–3×), changing the loop's phase margin (ESR zero moves), and increasing ripple. Check ESR with a meter, compare with the original, replace with long-life or polymer, and re-verify loop stability with the aged values.

### How would you design a rail so that a single ceramic capacitor short does not take it down?
Use two capacitors in series for the critical part (halves capacitance, but a single short leaves the other), or put a fuse/current limit or a series resistor/ferrite so the short is current-limited. Reduce case size and use flex-termination. For truly critical systems, protect the rail with a load switch or eFuse.
