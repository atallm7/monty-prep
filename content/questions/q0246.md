::: testing
Whether you can classify the filter in control-theory terms and say what that implies. A strong answer has:

1. **Second-order, linear, low-pass** system with two poles (a complex pair) and no finite zero (apart from the ESR zero).
2. **Underdamped/resonant**: quality factor $Q$ (or damping $\zeta=1/2Q$) set by load, ESR and inductor DCR.
3. The frequency response: flat to $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, a resonant peak, then $-40$ dB/decade and a phase drop of $180^\circ$.
4. Why that matters for the buck: this is the plant the compensator must tame, and its Q changes with load.
:::

::: why
The LC filter is the heart of the control problem. Everything in buck compensation (type II, type III, crossover choice, ESR-zero tricks) is about the properties of this one second-order system. Interviewers often ask "what kind of system is it?" as an opening, then follow with "so what would the Bode plot look like?" and "how does load change it?". Saying "second-order underdamped low-pass with Q set by the load" and then showing consequences is a strong start.
:::

::: concepts
- **Second-order system**: two energy-storage elements ($L$ and $C$) give a second-order differential equation and two poles.
- **Natural frequency and damping**: $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$, $\zeta=1/(2Q)$ ([[q0010]], [[q0186]]).
- **Quality factor $Q$**: how sharply the filter resonates. For a parallel load, $Q\approx R/Z_0$ with $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$.
- **Low-pass**: passes DC (the average of the switch node) and attenuates the switching ripple.
- **ESR zero**: $f_z=1/(2\pi\,ESR\,C)$ adds phase lead.
- **Type 0 plant**: no integrator, finite DC gain.
:::

::: answer
"The output filter is a second-order low-pass system: it has an inductor and a capacitor, so two energy-storing elements, a second-order differential equation, and two poles. It's linear and time-invariant for small signals. The poles are at the LC resonant frequency, 1 over 2 pi root LC, and they're a complex pair, so it's an underdamped, resonant system with a quality factor Q.

The Q is set by the damping: the load resistance, the capacitor ESR and the inductor DCR. A light load means little damping and a high Q, so a large resonant peak and a steep phase drop. The frequency response is flat at DC, peaks near the resonance, and then falls at minus 40 dB per decade with the phase going from 0 to minus 180 degrees. The capacitor's ESR adds a zero that flattens the gain and gives back some phase at high frequency.

That's why the buck's control problem is hard: the compensator has to deal with a resonant double pole and 180 degrees of phase lag. In current-mode control the inner loop effectively turns the inductor into a current source, which collapses the filter to a single-pole system, which is a big reason people use it."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** With the switch node averaged to $d\,V_{in}$ driving $L$ (DCR $R_L$), into $C$ (ESR $r$) in parallel with the load $R$ (small-signal duty-to-output):

$$G_{vd}(s)=V_{in}\frac{1+sCr}{1+s\left(\dfrac{L}{R}+Cr+CR_L\right)+s^2LC\left(1+\dfrac{r+R_L}{R}\right)}$$

(a good approximation when $r,R_L\ll R$.)

**Parameters.** $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$, $f_0=\omega_0/2\pi$, characteristic impedance $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$. Damping comes from the load and from series resistance:

$$\frac1Q\approx\frac{Z_0}{R}+\frac{r+R_L}{Z_0},\qquad\zeta=\frac1{2Q}$$

**Example.** $L=4.7$ µH, $C=47$ µF, $r=10$ mΩ, $R_L=20$ mΩ: $f_0=10.7$ kHz, $Z_0=0.316$ Ω.

| Load | $R=V_{out}/I$ | $Z_0/R$ | $(r+R_L)/Z_0$ | $Q$ | $\zeta$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.1 A | 33 Ω | 0.0096 | 0.095 | 9.6 | 0.052 |
| 1 A | 3.3 Ω | 0.096 | 0.095 | 5.2 | 0.095 |
| 2 A | 1.65 Ω | 0.19 | 0.095 | 3.5 | 0.143 |

The peak magnitude is about $Q$ (14.4 dB at 1 A) at $f_0$. Open-loop, a step excites ringing at 10.7 kHz with envelope time constant $\tau=2Q/\omega_0=156$ µs at 1 A, an overshoot near 74%.

**Asymptotes.** Gain: DC value $V_{in}$ (21.6 dB for 12 V), $-40$ dB/decade above $f_0$, flattening to $-20$ dB/decade above the ESR zero $f_z=1/(2\pi rC)=339$ kHz. Phase: $0^\circ\to-180^\circ$ around $f_0$ (sharper for higher $Q$), rising back toward $-90^\circ$ above $f_z$.

**In control language** this plant is **type 0** (finite DC gain, no integrator), second order, and in voltage mode minimum-phase for the buck in CCM (no right-half-plane zero, unlike the boost).
:::

::: long
"In control terms the output LC filter is a second-order low-pass system. It has two energy storage elements, the inductor and the capacitor, so the dynamics are described by a second-order differential equation, and the transfer function has two poles and, from the capacitor's ESR, one zero.

The poles are a complex-conjugate pair at the natural frequency, f zero, which is 1 over two pi root LC. So it's underdamped: when you excite it with a step it rings at that frequency. How strongly depends on the damping ratio, which is one over two Q. The only things that damp it are resistive: the load resistance, the capacitor's ESR and the inductor's winding resistance. For an example with 4.7 microhenries and 47 microfarads, the corner is about 10.7 kHz. At a 1 amp, 3.3 volt load the Q is about 5, so the resonant peak is around 14 dB and the open-loop step response overshoots by nearly 75 percent. At 100 milliamps the Q is nearly 10, at 2 amps it drops to about 3.5.

On the Bode plot: the gain is flat at the DC value, which is V in for voltage-mode, rises to the Q peak at f zero, then rolls off at minus 40 dB per decade. The phase goes from 0 to minus 180 degrees, steeper with higher Q. The ESR zero, which is at 1 over two pi ESR C, changes the slope back to minus 20 dB per decade and brings phase back toward minus 90.

That matters because the compensator has to cross over beyond or around this resonance while keeping phase margin, so it adds two zeros to cancel the double pole. It also matters that the plant changes with load. And with current-mode control the inner loop forces the inductor current, so the LC turns into a first-order, single-pole plant, which is much easier to compensate."
:::

::: terms
- **Second-order system**: a system with two poles; described by a second-order ODE.
- **Natural frequency $\omega_0$**: $1/\sqrt{LC}$, where the filter resonates.
- **Quality factor $Q$**: peak gain at resonance relative to DC; $Q=1/2\zeta$.
- **Damping ratio $\zeta$**: 0 undamped, 1 critically damped.
- **Characteristic impedance $Z_0$**: $\sqrt{L/C}$.
- **ESR zero**: zero at $1/(2\pi\,ESR\,C)$.
- **Double pole**: a pole pair at the same frequency (for high Q approximately so).
- **Minimum-phase**: no right-half-plane zero; phase is determined by gain slope.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "second-order low-pass filter".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Calls it second-order, linear, low-pass, *underdamped* with a complex-pole pair and gives $f_0$ and $Q$.
- Explains where the damping comes from (load, ESR, DCR) and gives $Q$ formulas and example numbers at several loads.
- Describes the Bode shape: peak, $-40$ dB/dec, $-180^\circ$, ESR zero effect.
- Notes it is type 0, minimum-phase in CCM (buck), and that load changes the Q.
- Contrasts it with current-mode control where the plant becomes first-order.
- Links to compensation: the compensator zeros cancel the double pole.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Calling it a first-order filter** because "it's just an RC-like smoothing". An LC with two energy stores is second-order.
- **Quoting Q without the load.** The damping comes from the load and series resistances; the lossless LC has infinite Q.
- **Ignoring that Q depends on load.** Light load gives higher Q, so the worst case for margin is often light load.
- **Forgetting the ESR zero.** With electrolytic capacitors the ESR zero is in the loop; with ceramics it is far above crossover.
- **Mixing up the corner with the switching frequency.** $f_0$ (10 kHz range) is far below $f_{sw}$ (hundreds of kHz).
- **Assuming the same plant applies in DCM or PFM.** In DCM the plant is first-order-like with a different pole.
:::

## Deriving the transfer function

Let the series source be $v_s=d\,V_{in}$, the inductor with DCR $R_L$, and the output node $v_o$ with the load $R$ in parallel with the capacitor branch $r+1/(sC)$. The output impedance of the parallel combination is

$$Z_o=\frac{R(1+sCr)}{1+sC(R+r)}$$

and the divider with the inductor branch gives

$$\frac{v_o}{v_s}=\frac{Z_o}{Z_o+sL+R_L}=\frac{R(1+sCr)}{R(1+sCr)+(sL+R_L)(1+sC(R+r))}$$

Expand the denominator and divide by $R+R_L$:

$$\frac{v_o}{v_s}=\frac{R}{R+R_L}\cdot\frac{1+sCr}{1+s\,a_1+s^2a_2},\;a_2=\frac{LC(R+r)}{R+R_L},\;a_1=\frac{L+CR_L(R+r)+CrR}{R+R_L}$$

For $R_L,r\ll R$ this reduces to the expression in the technical section with $\omega_0\approx1/\sqrt{LC}$ and the damping terms $L/R$ and $C(r+R_L)$. Check with $R_L=r=0$: $1/(1+sL/R+s^2LC)$, the classic parallel-RLC low-pass, with $Q=R\sqrt{C/L}$.

## Time domain: why it rings

An underdamped second-order step response is

$$y(t)=1-e^{-\zeta\omega_0t}\left[\cos\omega_dt+\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\sin\omega_dt\right],\quad\omega_d=\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$$

## Why the load matters

The load resistor is in parallel with the capacitor, so it damps the resonance: a bigger $R$ means less damping. At light load $R\to\infty$ and only ESR and DCR damp the filter, so Q is highest there, and the loop has the least margin ([[q0245]]). In DCM the inductor current is zero part of the cycle, so the plant becomes a single-pole system and the second pole disappears.

## Current mode changes the plant

If an inner loop forces the inductor current to follow a command, the inductor behaves as a controlled current source into $C\parallel R$, so the duty-to-output transfer function becomes a single pole at $1/(2\pi RC)$ (plus the ESR zero) ([[q0252]]). That is the reason for the popularity of peak-current-mode control: the large resonance and the $-180^\circ$ phase drop go away.

::: applications
- **Compensator design**: place the two type III zeros near $f_0$ to cancel the double pole.
- **Choosing output capacitor type**: ESR zero position decides whether you need extra phase lead.
- **Feedforward capacitor and internal compensation** designs that depend on a window of allowed $L$ and $C$ ([[q0261]]).
- **Active damping** in digital controllers.
- **EMI/ripple filtering**: the same LC reduces the switching ripple by $(f_{sw}/f_0)^2$.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Small-signal, averaged model, CCM.** In DCM or PFM the plant has a single dominant pole.
- **Linear, time-invariant** capacitor and inductor. Real MLCCs lose capacitance with DC bias and inductors lose inductance near saturation.
- **Resistive load.** A constant-power or current-sink load has different damping (negative incremental resistance).
- **$Q$ formula** is the approximation for $r,R_L\ll R$ and $Z_0$ comparable to them. For high-ESR capacitors use the full expression.
- Voltage-mode plant; with current-mode control the effective system is first-order at low frequency.
:::

::: followups
### What is the Q of the filter if the load is removed entirely?
Then only the series resistances damp it: $Q\approx Z_0/(r+R_L)=0.316/0.03=10.5$ in the example. It cannot become infinite because of the winding and ESR losses. In practice the converter would also enter DCM or PFM at no load, which changes the plant.

### Why is the buck's LC filter "minimum phase" while a boost's is not?
In CCM a boost has a right-half-plane zero in the control-to-output transfer function because increasing duty first reduces the energy delivered to the output, so the output initially moves the wrong way. A buck's output current path is not interrupted that way, so its plant has no RHP zero and its phase is determined by its magnitude slopes.

### How does the ESR zero help or hurt?
It gives up to 90° phase lead and raises the gain at high frequency. For electrolytic or polymer capacitors it often falls below crossover and helps. For ceramics it sits at MHz so it is irrelevant at crossover. At the same time it makes the switching ripple at the output larger (the ESR step $\Delta I\cdot ESR$).

### Compute the resonant frequency if $C$ derates to 50% by DC bias.
$f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ rises by $\sqrt2$: from 10.7 kHz to 15.1 kHz. Also $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$ rises by $\sqrt2$, so $Q$ rises at light loads. A compensator tuned to the nominal capacitance can lose margin, which is why capacitance derating is part of loop validation.

### Scenario: an interviewer asks you to damp the resonance passively. What would you do?
Add damping resistance: a series resistor with a dedicated bulk capacitor (an RC damping branch with $C_d\ge4C$ and $R_d\approx Z_0$) in parallel with the output capacitor, or use capacitors with higher ESR. These cost loss or ripple, so active damping through the compensator or current-mode control is usually preferred.
:::
