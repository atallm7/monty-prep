::: testing
Whether you can predict what actually happens to a microcontroller-based system when its supply leaves the legal range, and not just say "it breaks". Interviewers want the **different failure modes** (undervoltage gives misbehaviour, overvoltage gives damage or latch-up, over/undervoltage on a *pin* is different from on the *supply*), the **hardware defences** (brown-out reset, supervisors, series resistors and clamps, sequencing) and the **firmware defences** (reset on brown-out, flash-write guards, watchdog, safe state). Good candidates also mention subtle cases: back-powering through an ESD diode and the "zombie" state.
:::

::: why
Embedded systems are the place where a supply glitch turns into a bug report instead of smoke: the device "freezes sometimes", "loses its settings", "resets when the relay clicks". Many field failures that look like firmware bugs are brown-outs. Conversely, an overvoltage on one I/O pin can quietly damage a microcontroller that works fine on the bench for weeks. Knowing exactly what happens inside the chip lets you debug these problems and design protection that matches ([[q0454]] gives the general picture; this page is the embedded view).
:::

::: concepts
- **Supply range and absolute maximum ratings**: the recommended operating $V_{DD}$ range versus the stress limit ([[q0454]]).
- **Brown-out reset (BOR) and power-on reset (POR)**: on-chip circuits that hold the CPU in reset when $V_{DD}$ is too low.
- **ESD / clamp diodes on I/O pins**: they conduct when a pin goes above $V_{DD}$ or below ground ([[q0112]]).
- **Latch-up**: a parasitic thyristor in CMOS that, once triggered by overvoltage or injection current, shorts $V_{DD}$ to ground ([[q0112]]).
- **Power sequencing**: the order and timing in which rails come up and down ([[q0171]]).
- **Flash and SRAM retention**: non-volatile writes and memory contents need a minimum supply.
:::

::: answer
"It depends on which way and how far, but they're very different problems.

Undervoltage is a malfunction problem. Below the minimum $V_{DD}$, the logic slows down, timing margins vanish and the CPU can execute garbage: wrong branches, corrupted RAM, or a flash write that's cut halfway so settings are lost. Peripherals misbehave: the ADC reference moves, I/O drive weakens, the clock can stop or glitch. Many MCUs have brown-out reset, which holds the chip in reset below a threshold, say 2.7 V, and a good design adds a supervisor and avoids flash writes when the rail is weak.

Overvoltage is a damage problem. If the supply exceeds the absolute maximum, oxides and junctions break down, sometimes immediately, sometimes as latent damage that shortens life. If a pin goes above $V_{DD}$ plus a diode drop, the ESD clamp conducts, pumping current into the supply, and can trigger latch-up, where the chip shorts the rail and heats until it fails.

So I'd use brown-out detection and a supervisor, series resistors and clamps on exposed inputs, correct power sequencing so no pin is driven before its chip is powered, and fuses or an OVP stage on the rail. In firmware: reset on brown-out, a watchdog, and guards on flash writes."
:::

::: technical
**Undervoltage.** Gate delay rises as $V_{DD}$ falls toward $V_{th}$, so the maximum clock frequency falls; a core running at full speed at a lower $V_{DD}$ violates setup times and flips wrong bits. Flash program/erase needs a minimum voltage (often higher than the logic minimum) and the charge pump needs headroom. SRAM retention has its own minimum. An MCU in the voltage range between "logic fails" and "POR asserts" can be in an undefined state: this is why BOR thresholds are set above the minimum safe operating voltage. A POR circuit alone only resets on full power-up, not on a dip that stays above its low trip.

**Overvoltage on the supply.** Absolute maximum for a 3.3 V part is typically in the range 3.6 to 4.0 V (check the datasheet). Above it, oxide breakdown and electromigration risk rise; destructive latch-up becomes possible.

**Overvoltage or undervoltage on a pin.** If $V_{pin} > V_{DD}+V_F$, the upper clamp diode conducts: current flows into $V_{DD}$ (which can back-power the chip through its own pin) and the injection current is usually limited to a few mA by the datasheet. Series resistor: $R = (V_{in,max}-V_{clamp})/I_{max}$. For a 12 V signal into a pin clamped near 3.6 V with 10 mA: $R=(12-3.6)/0.010=840\ \Omega$, so use 1 kΩ (8.4 mA, 70 mW).

**Mitigation:** BOR/supervisor, UVLO on downstream loads, hold-up capacitance, series resistors and clamps on I/O, level shifters for 5 V to 3.3 V ([[q0878]]), power sequencing, watchdog and safe-state firmware.
:::

::: short
"Undervoltage makes the system misbehave: slower logic, corrupted RAM, half-written flash, weak I/O, so you add brown-out reset and a supervisor. Overvoltage damages it: breakdown above the absolute maximum, and a pin above Vdd forward-biases the ESD diodes and can trigger latch-up. So I use brown-out detection, series resistors and clamps on exposed inputs, proper power sequencing, and a fuse or OVP on the rail, plus firmware that resets on brown-out and avoids flash writes when the supply is weak."
:::

::: long
"They're two different failure types, so I'd treat them separately.

Undervoltage is about function. As Vdd falls, transistors have less overdrive, gates slow down and the maximum clock speed drops, so a CPU running at full speed starts violating setup times and executes garbage. Flash needs a minimum voltage to program, so a write cut off halfway leaves corrupted settings. SRAM can lose its contents, the ADC reference moves, and the I/O drive weakens. The scary part is the band between 'logic fails' and 'power-on reset trips', where the chip is alive but wrong. That's why microcontrollers have a brown-out reset set above the minimum safe voltage, and why I'd add an external supervisor with a reset delay for slow-ramping or noisy rails, and avoid flash writes unless the rail is verified good.

Overvoltage is about damage. On the supply, exceeding the absolute maximum breaks down the oxide or junctions, either instantly or as latent damage. On a pin, anything above Vdd plus a diode drop forward-biases the ESD clamp. That pumps current into the supply, can back-power the chip when its rail is off, and can trigger latch-up, a parasitic thyristor that shorts Vdd to ground until something burns. So I'd put a series resistor and clamp on exposed inputs, for example 1 kilohm limiting a 12 volt input to about 8 milliamps, level-shift between voltage domains, and sequence rails so no pin is driven before its chip is up.

In firmware I'd enable brown-out reset, use a watchdog, guard flash writes, and use a power-fail interrupt to enter a safe state."
:::

::: terms
- **Brown-out reset (BOR)**: an on-chip comparator that holds the MCU in reset while $V_{DD}$ is below a threshold.
- **Power-on reset (POR)**: a circuit that resets the chip when power is first applied.
- **Supervisor (reset IC)**: an external monitor with a precise threshold and reset delay after the rail recovers.
- **Latch-up**: a parasitic PNPN structure that, once triggered, shorts supply to ground until power is removed.
- **Injection current**: current forced into a pin through its clamp diodes when the pin voltage is outside the rails.
- **Back-powering**: powering a chip through an I/O pin's ESD diode while its supply is off.
- **Zombie state**: an MCU alive but behaving unpredictably in a marginal supply region.
- **Absolute maximum rating**: the stress limit beyond which permanent damage may occur.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "overvoltage damages the chip, undervoltage resets it".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains **why** undervoltage causes misbehaviour (slower gates, timing violations, flash write cut-off, SRAM retention) and the **"between logic failure and POR" band**.
- Distinguishes **supply** overvoltage from **pin** over/undervoltage, including **ESD diode injection**, **latch-up** and **back-powering**.
- Gives a **series resistor calculation** for a pin and a real **clamp** circuit.
- Lists **hardware** (BOR, supervisor, sequencing, OVP) **and firmware** (flash-write guards, watchdog, power-fail interrupt) defences.
- Mentions debugging: scope the rail during the event, check reset cause registers.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Assuming "the chip works, so the supply is fine".** A rail that dips to 2.5 V for 50 µs can corrupt state without the CPU ever visibly crashing.
- **Relying only on POR.** POR only triggers on power-up (a low-going crossing near the bottom of the range), while a brown-out can stay well above that level.
- **Setting BOR below the safe operating voltage.** The chip is then allowed to run in the range where it is already unreliable.
- **Driving an input before the chip is powered.** The ESD diode back-powers the chip through the pin and leaves it in a half-alive state.
- **Ignoring pin voltage limits when mixing 5 V and 3.3 V parts.** A 5 V output into a 3.3 V input exceeds $V_{DD}+0.3$ V on many devices ([[q0878]]).
- **No series resistance on exposed inputs.** The clamp diodes can only take a few mA; an unlimited source overheats or latches them.
:::

::: deep
## Two directions, two failure families

When the question says "over/undervoltage on an embedded system" there are really two places the voltage can be wrong (the supply pin $V_{DD}$, or an I/O pin) and two directions (high or low). The effects differ.

| Where | Too low | Too high |
|---|---|---|
| **Supply ($V_{DD}$)** | Brown-out: slow logic, bad flash writes, lost RAM, weak I/O | Oxide/junction breakdown, latch-up, reduced lifetime |
| **I/O pin** | Below ground: lower clamp diode conducts (injection current) | Above $V_{DD}$: upper clamp diode conducts, back-powering, latch-up |

## Undervoltage on the supply: what actually breaks

### Timing: the CPU gets slower than the clock

A CMOS gate's delay grows rapidly as $V_{DD}$ approaches the threshold voltage, roughly as $t_d\propto V_{DD}/(V_{DD}-V_{th})^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha$ between 1 and 2. A chip that meets timing at 3.3 V and 48 MHz may have to run slower below 2.7 V. When the clock does not slow with it, setup-time violations occur and flip-flops capture wrong values. Instructions are decoded incorrectly, branches go the wrong way, and the symptom is random crashes, not a clean stop.

### Memory: flash writes and RAM

Flash program and erase need a minimum supply (often specified separately from the logic minimum) and use internal charge pumps. A write interrupted by a dip leaves the page half-programmed: settings or even the bootloader become corrupted. SRAM has a data-retention voltage; below it, bits flip. EEPROM-emulation and wear-levelling schemes are designed assuming the supply holds long enough to complete a write, which is exactly what brown-outs break.

### Analog and I/O

The ADC reference follows $V_{DD}$ if it is not separate, so readings drift. I/O drive current falls with $V_{DD}$, so signals have slower edges and weaker pull. Oscillators may stop or glitch. Peripherals reset independently of the core, so you can end up with a CPU in one state and a peripheral in another.

### The marginal band

The dangerous region is between the lowest voltage where the logic still works and the voltage where POR asserts. In that band the chip is alive but unreliable. Designers close it with the **brown-out reset** threshold placed above the lowest safe operating voltage, and sometimes a higher-accuracy external supervisor.

### Reset has to last long enough and clean up

A reset signal that releases the instant $V_{DD}$ crosses back above the threshold can release while the supply is still ringing and the oscillator is unstable. A supervisor adds a **reset delay** (tens to hundreds of milliseconds) after the rail is good.

## Overvoltage on the supply

Above the absolute maximum rating the failures are physical: gate oxide breakdown (a thin insulator with a limited field), junction breakdown, electromigration at high current density. Damage can be immediate or **latent**: the chip still works but its lifetime is reduced, a nasty failure mode because it passes the production test. The usual causes are an adapter of the wrong voltage, a regulator failure that passes the input through ([[q0457]]), or a transient. Defences: a regulator with margin to the supply, an OVP stage or clamp, and rated components. A 3.3 V MCU powered from a 5 V rail through a failed LDO will exceed its maximum immediately; a TVS or crowbar plus fuse upstream is the safety net.

## Over/undervoltage on a pin

Every CMOS input pin has ESD protection diodes to $V_{DD}$ and to ground ([[q0112]]). When $V_{pin}>V_{DD}+0.3$ V (roughly) the upper diode conducts and current flows into the supply. Three consequences:

1. **Injection current** into $V_{DD}$ raises the rail if the chip's own consumption is smaller than the injected current, and it may exceed the pin's allowed injection current (typically a few mA; check the datasheet).
2. **Back-powering**: with $V_{DD}$ off, a pin driven high through the diode powers the chip partly. It enters a half-alive state in which POR may not trigger and the chip draws unexpected current. This is why sequencing matters ([[q0171]], [[q0172]]).
3. **Latch-up**: the parasitic PNPN thyristor between pin, substrate and supply can trigger from injection current, shorting $V_{DD}$ to ground until power is removed ([[q0112]]).

The external diodes divert most of the current away from the chip's internal ESD diodes. With a 12 V input, the current is about $(12-3.6)/1\ \text{k}\Omega=8.4$ mA. Check that the 3.3 V rail can absorb it, or the clamp pushes the rail up.

**Sizing the resistor.** $R=(V_{in,max}-V_{clamp})/I_{max}$ with $V_{clamp}\approx V_{DD}+V_F$. For 12 V, $V_{clamp}=3.6$ V, $I_{max}=10$ mA: $R=840\ \Omega$; a 1 kΩ resistor gives 8.4 mA and dissipates $I^2R=71$ mW. The cost: the resistor with pin and trace capacitance forms an RC (1 kΩ times 5 pF is 5 ns, negligible; 100 kΩ gives 500 ns, which matters at high speed).

## What the firmware should do

- **Enable brown-out reset** at a threshold above the minimum flash/logic voltage and read the reset-cause register at boot to log brown-outs.
- **Guard flash/EEPROM writes**: check the supply (BOR flag or an ADC reading of $V_{DD}$) before writing, and keep write sequences short.
- **Watchdog timer**: recovers from a hung CPU but doesn't fix corrupt memory; combine with checksums or CRC on stored settings.
- **Power-fail interrupt** from a comparator on the input rail, to stop actuators, save state and reset cleanly ([[q0456]]).
- **Safe default states** for outputs on reset (motors off, relays open), using pull-downs on gate-drive lines so they stay off while the MCU's pins are high-Z during reset.

## Debugging checklist

1. Scope $V_{DD}$ at the chip's pins (not at the regulator) during the failure, with a single-shot trigger on a drop.
2. Read the reset-cause register: a BOR flag confirms brown-out.
3. Look at what happens around the event: motor start, radio transmit burst, relay actuation ([[q0343]]).
4. Check the supply impedance: a long cable or a small bulk capacitor sags under load steps.
5. Check sequencing: is any pin driven while the chip is unpowered?
:::

::: applications
- **IoT sensors on coin cells**: as the cell ages and internal resistance rises, radio bursts cause brown-outs, so BOR and bulk capacitance are essential ([[q0303]]).
- **Automotive ECUs**: cold-crank dips and load dumps demand supervisors, reset delays and input clamps.
- **Industrial I/O modules**: 24 V field signals into 3.3 V MCU inputs through resistors, clamps and isolation.
- **Medical and metering devices**: power-fail interrupts and hold-up so calibration and logs are saved reliably.
- **Hobbyist boards (Arduino-class)**: BOR fuse bits and 5 V versus 3.3 V sensor connections.
- **Multi-rail SoC and FPGA boards**: sequencing and power-good monitors to keep I/O from leading the core.
:::

::: assumptions
I assumed a standard 3.3 V or 1.8 V CMOS microcontroller with on-chip BOR and clamp diodes. Behaviour varies by vendor: BOR thresholds, flash minimum voltages, allowed injection current, latch-up immunity and whether pins are 5 V tolerant (some pins have no clamp to $V_{DD}$ and tolerate higher voltages) all differ, so the datasheet is the final authority. For battery-powered devices the dominant issue is usually undervoltage (a sagging cell); for industrial and automotive devices it is overvoltage and transients. The answer would also change for very low-power designs, where a lower BOR threshold may be chosen deliberately to extend battery life at the cost of margin. Valid alternative framings are by cause (battery, noise, sequencing) or by layer (hardware, firmware).
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between POR and BOR?
POR (power-on reset) holds the chip in reset while $V_{DD}$ ramps up from zero and releases when it passes a threshold. BOR (brown-out reset) monitors $V_{DD}$ continuously while running and resets the chip if it falls below its threshold, even if it never reached zero. A supply that dips from 3.3 V to 2.0 V and recovers may never trigger POR, so BOR is what catches it.

### An input pin is driven by a sensor that is powered before the MCU. What can go wrong?
The sensor's high output pushes current through the MCU pin's upper ESD diode into the MCU's supply rail, partly powering it ("back-powering"). The MCU's rail then sits at a few hundred millivolts to about 2 V, below POR release, so it may not reset properly and may draw current in a strange state, or latch up. Fix by sequencing the sensor after the MCU, adding series resistance, or using a load switch on the sensor supply.

### Why can a 5 V device driving a 3.3 V MCU input damage it even though it "works"?
The pin may exceed $V_{DD}+0.3$ V, so the upper clamp diode conducts continuously, heating the pin structure and raising the 3.3 V rail slightly, which can stress the regulator. It may work for months but shorten lifetime. Use a level shifter, a resistor divider (for slow signals), or a 5 V tolerant input as specified in the datasheet.

### What if the supply drops very slowly, like a dying battery? Does BOR still help?
Yes, BOR is a level comparator, not a rate detector, so it triggers when the threshold is crossed. But the system will reset repeatedly if the load pulls the battery below the threshold at each turn-on (battery recovery then re-trigger). Add hysteresis, a supervisor with a delay, or a deliberate low-battery shutdown at a higher voltage so the system powers down cleanly before BOR becomes a problem.

### Scenario: a product resets whenever a relay clicks. How do you find the cause?
Scope $V_{DD}$ at the MCU pins with a single-shot trigger on the relay coil. If the rail dips, it's the supply: coil inrush and switching noise on a shared rail or a long ground return. If $V_{DD}$ is clean but the MCU resets, suspect an inductive kick coupling into reset or an input pin (see [[q0144]]), and check the reset-cause register. Fixes: flyback diode across the coil, separate supply or LC filter for the MCU, more bulk capacitance, and a supervisor with a proper threshold.
:::
