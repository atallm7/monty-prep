::: basics
### In plain words
A reflection is part of a signal bouncing back when it meets a change in impedance. A wave on a line sees the line's characteristic impedance. If it reaches something different, such as the end of the line or a connector, part goes on and part comes back. This causes ringing, overshoot and false edges.

### Picture it
Think of an ocean wave arriving at a shore. A gently sloping beach absorbs the wave and almost nothing returns. A solid sea wall throws the wave straight back. The beach is a load matched to the line. The wall is an open end. In between you get a partial reflection. On a real line the bounced wave can bounce again at the driver, so it keeps going back and forth.

### Words you need
- **Impedance**: how strongly a part opposes current when a signal is applied.
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: the impedance a wave sees as it travels along the line ([[q0710]]).
- **Reflection coefficient ($\Gamma$)**: the fraction of the arriving wave that bounces back, $(Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)$.
- **Ringing**: the repeated up-and-down wiggle caused by waves bouncing back and forth ([[q0696]]).

### The simplest example
A 50 Ω line ends in a 100 Ω load. The reflection coefficient is (100 - 50) / (100 + 50) = 1/3, so one third of the arriving wave comes back. A matched 50 Ω load gives zero reflection. An open end gives +1, so all of it comes back.

Now drive that line from 3.3 V through 10 Ω, with the far end open. The first wave is 2.75 V. At the open end it reflects completely and adds to itself, so the far end jumps to 5.5 V. The reflected wave then returns to the driver and bounces again.

That is the whole idea: a change in impedance sends part of the wave back. The rest of this page derives the formula and follows the repeated bounces.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can explain a reflection physically (a wave meets a different impedance, the boundary conditions force part of it back), write $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$, evaluate it for open, short and matched loads, and then say *why a hardware engineer cares*: overshoot beyond the rails, ringing and false edges, delayed or non-monotonic transitions, eye closure and EMI. The strongest answers also describe the back-and-forth bouncing between a mismatched source and load, and give a number (for example 5.5 V on a 3.3 V line).
:::

::: why
Reflections are the main way "digital" signals fail in real hardware. They turn a clean clock into a ringing, double-edged one, corrupt data at high bit rates, overstress receiver input protection with overshoot, and radiate EMI. They are also the main thing you look for on a scope or TDR when a board "works on the bench but not at speed". Understanding them is what lets you decide where to terminate, how long a stub may be and why a layout change like a via or a connector can break a link.
:::

::: concepts
- **Characteristic impedance $Z_0$**: the impedance a wave sees while travelling. See [[q0710]].
- **Boundary conditions**: voltage and current must be continuous at the junction of two impedances.
- **Reflection coefficient $\Gamma$**: reflected over incident voltage. Transmitted is $1+\Gamma$.
- **Propagation delay** $t_d$ and **round-trip time** $2t_d$ ([[q0708]]).
- **Source reflection coefficient** $\Gamma_S=(R_S-Z_0)/(R_S+Z_0)$: the wave coming back also reflects at the driver.
- **Rise time versus delay** decides how visible reflections are ([[q0709]]).
:::

::: answer
"A reflection is part of a signal bouncing back when it hits a change in impedance. A wave travelling on a transmission line sees the line's characteristic impedance. If it reaches something with a different impedance, like the end of the line, a connector or a stub, the voltage and current on both sides of the junction have to match up, and the only way to do that is to send some of the wave back. The fraction that comes back is $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$. Open end: $\Gamma=+1$, the whole wave comes back with the same sign and the voltage doubles. Short: $-1$, it comes back inverted. Load equal to $Z_0$: zero, everything is absorbed.

I care because the reflection travels back, and if the driver isn't matched it bounces again. You get ringing and overshoot on every edge. For example, a 3.3 V driver with a 10 ohm output impedance into an unterminated 50 ohm trace launches about 2.75 V and the open end doubles it to 5.5 V, well past the supply rail and enough to hit the input clamp diodes. It can also cause false clock edges when the ring crosses a threshold, delay the edge through a staircase, close the eye at high data rates and radiate EMI. The fix is to match impedances and terminate: a series resistor at the source or a resistor at the load."
:::

::: technical
**Derivation.** At the junction between line 1 ($Z_0$) and load $Z_L$: incident wave $V_i$, reflected $V_r=\Gamma V_i$, current $I_i=V_i/Z_0$, $I_r=-V_r/Z_0$ (it travels the other way). Continuity at the load requires $V_L=V_i+V_r$ and $I_L=I_i+I_r$, with $V_L=I_LZ_L$:
$$V_i(1+\Gamma)=Z_L\frac{V_i}{Z_0}(1-\Gamma)\;\Rightarrow\;\Gamma=\frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0}$$
The transmitted voltage is $V_L=(1+\Gamma)V_i$.

| Load | $\Gamma$ | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Open | +1 | $V$ doubles, $I\to0$ |
| Short | −1 | $V\to0$, current doubles |
| $Z_L=Z_0$ | 0 | fully absorbed |
| $Z_L=100\ \Omega$ on 50 Ω | +1/3 | +33% reflection |
| $Z_L=25\ \Omega$ on 50 Ω | −1/3 | −33% reflection |

**Multiple reflections.** The reflected wave reaches the source after $2t_d$ and reflects with $\Gamma_S=(R_S-Z_0)/(R_S+Z_0)$. Each round trip scales the bouncing wave by $\Gamma_S\Gamma_L$. The voltage converges to the DC divider value $V_sR_L/(R_s+R_L)$ if $|\Gamma_S\Gamma_L|<1$, with alternating over/undershoot when $\Gamma_S\Gamma_L<0$.

**Example.** $V_s=3.3$ V, $R_s=10\ \Omega$, $Z_0=50\ \Omega$, open load: $V_1=2.75$ V, $\Gamma_S=-0.667$, $\Gamma_L=+1$. Load voltage: 5.50, 1.83, 4.28, 2.65, 3.73, 3.01 V… (arrivals at $t_d$, $3t_d$, $5t_d$…). Ring period $4t_d$.

**Why it matters:** overshoot/undershoot beyond rails (reliability, clamp current), non-monotonic edges through the input threshold (double clocking), added delay or jitter (data-dependent: ISI), eye closure, EMI from ringing, and extra power in clamps.
:::

::: short
"A reflection is the part of a wave that bounces back at an impedance change. The reflected fraction is $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$: +1 for an open, −1 for a short, zero when the load equals $Z_0$. If the source isn't matched either, the wave bounces back and forth and you see ringing and overshoot. I care because it can push a 3.3 V line to 5.5 V, cause false clock edges, close the eye and radiate EMI. You fix it by matching impedances and terminating."
:::

::: long
"A signal on a PCB trace is a wave moving along a line with a characteristic impedance, say 50 ohms. As long as the line looks uniform, the wave keeps going. When it hits a change, say the end of the line, a stub or a connector, the voltage and current on both sides have to be continuous. The only way to satisfy that when the impedances differ is for part of the wave to reflect. The fraction is the reflection coefficient, $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$. An open gives +1, so the voltage doubles; a short gives −1, so the wave comes back inverted; a load equal to $Z_0$ gives zero, so everything is absorbed.

The reflected wave travels back to the source. If the driver's output impedance isn't equal to $Z_0$, it reflects again with its own coefficient, and you get a series of echoes. A concrete example: a 3.3 volt driver with a 10 ohm output into an open 50 ohm line launches 2.75 V, which doubles to 5.5 V at the open end. Then it goes back, reflects at the source with $-0.67$, and the receiver sees 1.8 V, then 4.3 V, and so on, ringing with a period of four line delays.

That matters for several reasons. Overshoot beyond the supply stresses the input protection diodes. Ringing through the input threshold makes a clean edge look like two or three, which on a clock is a functional bug. Reflections add data-dependent timing errors that close the eye at high speed, and ringing radiates EMI.

The fixes all aim to make the reflection coefficient small at one end or both: series termination at the source so the returning wave is absorbed, parallel termination at the load, or avoiding the mismatch in the first place by controlled impedance, short stubs and clean transitions."
:::

::: terms
- **Reflection coefficient ($\Gamma$)**: $V_{reflected}/V_{incident}$, $(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$.
- **Incident / reflected / transmitted wave**: the wave arriving at a junction, the part returned, and the part continuing ($1+\Gamma$ of the incident).
- **Overshoot / undershoot**: excursions above the high level or below the low level of the signal.
- **Ringing**: damped oscillation after an edge, caused by repeated reflections.
- **Bounce (lattice) diagram**: time-versus-position chart that tracks each reflection.
- **Return loss / VSWR**: frequency-domain measures of reflection magnitude ($-20\log_{10}|\Gamma|$; $(1+|\Gamma|)/(1-|\Gamma|)$).
- **Round-trip delay ($2t_d$)**: the time for a wave to go to the far end and back.
- **Non-monotonic edge**: a transition that goes up, dips, then goes up again, risking multiple triggers.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "reflections happen when impedances don't match and cause ringing".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives or at least states $\Gamma$ from continuity of $V$ and $I$ and evaluates open, short and matched cases with signs.
- Describes the *multiple* bounces between a mismatched source and load and shows $\Gamma_S\Gamma_L$ controls the decay and the ring direction.
- Gives a numerical example (2.75 V launched, 5.5 V at an open end, ring period $4t_d$).
- Lists concrete harms: overshoot past the supply, clamp current, false clocking, ISI/eye closure, EMI.
- Notes that a *small* reflection from a via or connector is still important at multi-GHz because errors accumulate.
- Knows the fix list and when each applies ([[q0716]], [[q0717]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Reflection means the signal is lost."** The reflected energy returns and may re-reflect; in the end the line settles to its DC value. The damage is during the transient.
- **Forgetting the source end.** A wave that returns to a mismatched driver reflects again. With $R_s=Z_0$ the first reflection is absorbed and there is no ringing.
- **Sign errors.** A load lower than $Z_0$ gives a *negative* $\Gamma$ (voltage dips), a load higher than $Z_0$ gives a positive one.
- **Treating "open" as "no reflection because no current".** The current is zero because the reflected current cancels the incident, and the voltage doubles.
- **Ignoring rise time.** If the line is very short compared with the edge, reflections merge into the edge and are not visible ([[q0709]]).
- **Believing a probe's picture without checking the probe.** A long ground lead can create ringing that isn't on the board ([[q0997]]).
:::

::: deep
## Why a wave reflects

Imagine a wave with voltage $V_i$ and current $I_i=V_i/Z_0$ running down a line and arriving at a termination $Z_L$. At the termination, Ohm's law must hold for the *total* voltage and *total* current: $V_L=I_LZ_L$. But a single forward wave carries $V/I=Z_0$. If $Z_L\ne Z_0$ the forward wave alone violates Ohm's law at the end, so the system generates a second wave to fix it: a backward wave $V_r$ with current $I_r=-V_r/Z_0$ (the minus sign because it flows the other way).

Continuity of voltage and current gives the reflection coefficient:

$$V_L=V_i+V_r,\qquad I_L=\frac{V_i-V_r}{Z_0},\qquad V_L=Z_LI_L\;\Rightarrow\;\Gamma=\frac{V_r}{V_i}=\frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0}$$

Intuition by limits:
- **Open**: no current can flow, so the reflected current must cancel the incident current. The reflected voltage has the same sign as the incident, and the voltage doubles.
- **Short**: voltage must be zero, so the reflected voltage cancels the incident ($\Gamma=-1$), and current doubles.
- **Capacitor load**: initially a short (the capacitor has no voltage), then an open: the reflection starts at $-1$ and decays to $+1$ with time constant $Z_0C$.
- **Inductor load**: initially an open, then a short.

## Bouncing between source and load

If instead both coefficients had the same sign (for example $R_s=10\ \Omega$ and $R_L=25\ \Omega$, both $<Z_0$) the product is positive and the response creeps monotonically to its final value in a staircase.

## What the numbers mean in hardware

With the 3.3 V, 10 Ω, open-end example and $t_d=0.88$ ns (150 mm of FR-4 microstrip):

- Peak: **5.5 V**, well over a 3.3 V part's absolute maximum input rating (often $V_{DD}+0.3$ V). In practice the clamp diode conducts, but the stress and noise injected into the supply are real.
- Ring frequency $1/(4t_d)=284$ MHz. That's inside the bandwidth of many sensitive analog and RF circuits and is a classic EMI source.
- Settling: each round trip multiplies the error by 0.667. To get below 5% of the step, $0.667^k<0.05$ gives $k\approx7.4$ round trips, so about $7.4\cdot2t_d\approx13$ ns. At 100 MHz (10 ns period) the line is still ringing when the next edge comes.
- Threshold crossings: if the receiver switches at 1.65 V and the ring dips to 1.83 V, it is only 0.18 V from a false edge. With a bit more undershoot (e.g., a longer or faster-edged line) it crosses and creates a runt or extra pulse.

## Why care at high data rates

At a multi-Gb/s data rate the symbols are only a few $t_d$ apart. Reflections from a prior symbol arrive during later symbols and add or subtract, depending on the pattern: this is *inter-symbol interference* (ISI). A small $\Gamma=5\%$ at a via, a connector and a package each can add up to significant eye closure ([[q0703]]). That is why high-speed channels are specified with return-loss limits, not just insertion-loss limits.

## Reading reflections on a TDR trace

A TDR is a reflection experiment in an instrument: it launches a fast step into a 50 Ω system and records what comes back. Because the reflected voltage is $\Gamma$ times the incident step, you can invert it: $Z=Z_0\dfrac{1+\Gamma}{1-\Gamma}$. A reflection of $+0.2$ times the step means $Z=50\cdot1.2/0.8=75\ \Omega$; $-0.2$ means $50\cdot0.8/1.2=33\ \Omega$; $+1$ is an open and $-1$ a short. The *time* of the echo gives the distance, $x=v\,t/2$: an echo arriving 1.2 ns after the step on a 1.73 $\times10^8$ m/s line is $0.5\cdot1.2\text{ ns}\cdot1.73\times10^8=104$ mm away. The sign tells you whether the feature is more inductive/high-impedance (up) or more capacitive/low-impedance (down), which is why a TDR plot is the standard tool for finding bad vias and connectors ([[q0713]]).

## The causes and cures

The causes (discontinuities, mismatches, stubs) are in [[q0713]], the termination choices in [[q0716]] and [[q0717]], the drawn waveforms in [[q0712]], and the broader concept of matching in [[q0714]].

> [!TIP]
> A neat sanity check: at the instant the wave first hits the load, the load voltage is $(1+\Gamma_L)V_1$. For an open it is $2V_1$, for a short $0$. If your scope shows something very different, suspect the probe, the layout or an unexpected discontinuity.
:::

::: applications
- **Clock distribution**: series resistors near the clock driver (22 to 33 Ω) remove ringing and false edges on 50 to 200 MHz clock traces.
- **High-speed serial links (PCIe, USB, SATA)**: return-loss specifications limit reflections from connectors, vias and packages.
- **TDR cable testing**: reflection polarity and time-of-arrival locate opens (+), shorts (−) and impedance changes.
- **DDR memory**: on-die termination and trace impedance control to avoid reflections on multi-drop buses.
- **RF**: VSWR and return loss specifications on antennas and amplifiers.
- **Pulse generators and fast logic edges in test equipment**: 50 Ω terminated cables to avoid ghost pulses.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear, uniform, lossless line** and resistive terminations. Real loads include capacitance (pin, pad, ESD) and inductance (bond wires), which reflect in a frequency-dependent way.
- **Step incident wave.** For a finite rise time $t_r$ the reflection amplitude is reduced if the reflecting feature is electrically small ($t_r\gg$ its delay).
- **Driver output impedance constant.** Real CMOS drivers have non-linear, state-dependent output resistance (IBIS models describe it); the high and low states can differ.
- **Single line.** Coupled lines have mode-dependent impedances and extra reflections from skew.

Alternatively, the same phenomenon may be described in the frequency domain via return loss and S11: $|S_{11}|=|\Gamma|$.
:::

::: followups
### What happens at a termination equal to $Z_0$?
$\Gamma=0$. All the incident power goes into the load, there's no reflected wave and the line voltage is a clean delayed copy of the source. For a 3.3 V source with $R_s=10\ \Omega$ and a 50 Ω termination the load sees 2.75 V, a steady DC value set by the divider, with no ringing.

### Why does an open at the end make the voltage double?
Because current can't flow at an open, the reflected current wave must be equal and opposite to the incident one. Voltage and current waves are tied by $V=\pm Z_0I$, so the reflected voltage has the *same* sign as the incident voltage: $V=V_i+V_r=2V_i$. Energy-wise, the incident energy stored in $C'$ and $L'$ is all converted to electric energy at the end.

### My 3.3 V clock shows 1 V undershoot and a double edge at the receiver. What do you check?
First rule out probe artifacts (short ground spring, bandwidth). Then check the driver strength and trace length: a strong driver into a 15 cm unterminated trace is the classic case. Add a series resistor of about $Z_0-R_{drv}$ (typically 22 to 33 Ω) near the driver, then look for stubs or T-junctions (a clock fanned out to two loads), and check the return path at layer changes. I'd measure again with the same probe and compare.

### How big is a reflection from a small capacitive discontinuity, like a 2 pF pad on a 50 Ω line?
A shunt capacitor on a line looks like $\tau=Z_0C/2=50$ ps in series with the incident edge, and for a linear ramp of duration $t_r$ the peak reflection is $(\tau/t_r)(1-e^{-t_r/\tau})$ of the step. With a 200 ps ramp that is about 24%; with a 1 ns ramp only about 5%. So the same pad is a problem for DDR and not for a 10 MHz clock.

### Harder: could reflections ever be good?
Yes: in an RF resonator, open and short stubs are used deliberately, and in "incident-wave switching" you rely on the open-end doubling (series-terminated lines launch half the voltage and let the open load double it, so receivers at the end see a full-swing edge). Even a tiny controlled reflection is used in TDR measurements. In digital buses, we usually only want the *desired* reflection at the end of a series-terminated line.
:::
