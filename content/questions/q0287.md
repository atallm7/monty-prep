::: testing
Whether you can match the **charge pump's strengths** (no inductor, small, low EMI, fixed ratios, low current) to real jobs, and know when it is the wrong tool. A list is the floor. The interviewer wants the reason each application picks a pump over an inductive converter and a sense of the numbers (milliamps to a few hundred milliamps, ratios 2x, 1.5x, -1x), plus the disambiguation from the PLL charge pump.
:::

::: why
Most boards contain several charge pumps without the designer drawing them: inside RS-232 and RF-switch ICs, LED drivers, load switches, flash memories and display drivers. Choosing between a pump and an inductive boost ([[q0273]]) is a standard decision, and the answer depends on current, noise, size and cost.
:::

::: concepts
- **Strengths**: no magnetics, small footprint, low radiated EMI, simple.
- **Limits**: fixed ratios, output resistance $R_{out}$, input current $n\times I_{out}$. See [[q0284]].
- **Efficiency** $V_{out}/(nV_{in})$. See [[q0288]].
- **Boost comparison**: inductors handle amps and arbitrary ratios. See [[q0270]].
- **Negative rails and high-side gate drive** both need a voltage outside the supply range.
- **Disambiguation**: a PLL charge pump steers current into a loop filter ([[q0071]]); it is a different circuit.
:::

::: answer
"Charge pumps are used when I need a voltage outside the supply range at fairly low current and I don't want an inductor.

The big ones: a negative rail from a positive supply, for op-amp or ADC front ends or LCD contrast. RS-232 transceivers, which make about plus and minus 10 V from 5 V or 3.3 V. White-LED backlight and flash drivers, where a 1x, 1.5x or 2x pump keeps the LEDs lit as a Li-ion cell falls from 4.2 to 3 V. Gate-drive supplies, to turn on a high-side N-FET or a load switch above the rail. And on-chip high voltages: flash and EEPROM programming, DRAM word-line boost, and bias for RF switches or MEMS.

I'd choose a pump over an inductive boost for small loads, up to a couple of hundred milliamps: it takes less board area, there's no inductor to pick, and EMI is easier. I'd go inductive when I need amps, a continuously adjustable output, or high efficiency across a wide input range, because a pump's efficiency is capped by $V_{out}/(nV_{in})$. And I'd note that a PLL's charge pump is something else entirely."
:::

::: technical
| Application | Ratio / output | Typical current | Why a pump |
|---|---|---|---|
| Negative bias from $+V$ | $-1\times$ | 1 to 100 mA | no inductor, quiet |
| RS-232 driver | about $+2\times$ then $-1\times$ ($\pm$10 V ideal from 5 V; real output lower) | few mA per line | small, low EMI |
| White-LED backlight/flash | 1x/1.5x/2x | 20 mA to a few hundred mA | wide battery range, small |
| Gate drive / load switch | about $V_{in}+V_{gs}$ | microamps | tiny gate current |
| Flash/EEPROM program | 10 to 20+ V on-chip | microamps | integrated, Dickson |
| LCD/OLED bias | $\pm$ rails | milliamps | quiet, small |
| RF switch / MEMS bias | negative or high voltage | nA to $\mu$A | tiny current |

**Worked LED example.** 4 white LEDs in parallel ($V_F=3.2$ V, 20 mA each, 80 mA total, current regulators need 0.2 V headroom): need $nV_{in}\ge3.4$ V. Use 1x for $V_{bat}\ge3.4$ V ($\eta\approx3.2/V_{bat}$: 94% at 3.4 V, 76% at 4.2 V) and 1.5x below ($\eta=3.2/(1.5V_{bat})=71\%$ at 3.0 V). Input current at 3.0 V in 1.5x: $1.5\times80=120$ mA.
:::

::: short
"Anywhere I need a voltage outside the supply at low current without an inductor: negative rails from a positive supply, RS-232 levels, white-LED backlight and flash drivers with 1x/1.5x/2x modes, gate drive above the rail, and on-chip high voltage for flash programming. They're small and quiet but limited to a few hundred milliamps with efficiency capped by the ratio. For amps or flexible ratios I'd use an inductive converter."
:::

::: long
"A charge pump is the right answer when I need a rail outside the supply, the current is small, and I'd rather not use an inductor.

Negative supplies are probably the most common: I have 3.3 V and need minus 3.3 V for an op-amp, ADC front end or LCD contrast. An inverting pump does it with two capacitors and a chip.

RS-232 is the classic: the standard wants plus and minus several volts on the line, and the MAX232-style parts make that from 5 V or 3.3 V with a doubler then an inverter.

LED drivers are another big one. A phone backlight or camera flash has white LEDs at about 3.2 V from a Li-ion cell that runs from 4.2 V down to 3 V. A 1x/1.5x/2x pump picks the smallest ratio that keeps the current source in regulation. For four LEDs at 80 mA, from 3.0 V it runs 1.5x with 120 mA input, about 71% efficient.

Then gate drive: a high-side N-FET or load switch needs a gate above the rail, and a tiny pump supplies that because the gate draws almost no current. And inside memory chips, Dickson pumps make the 10 to 20 V for programming flash from 1.8 or 3.3 V.

Against an inductive boost, a pump wins on size, EMI and simplicity at low current, loses on efficiency flexibility and power. And I'd mention the PLL 'charge pump' is a different thing: a current source pair that drives the loop filter."
:::

::: terms
- **Inverting charge pump**: produces $-V_{in}$.
- **Fractional pump**: 1.5x or 1/2x ratios.
- **Mode hopping**: switching between 1x, 1.5x, 2x as $V_{in}$ changes.
- **Current sink / headroom**: LED regulator needs minimum voltage.
- **Gate-drive supply**: rail above $V_{in}$ for N-FET gates.
- **Dickson pump**: on-chip diode/capacitor multiplier.
- **RS-232**: serial standard with $\pm$ voltage levels.
- **Soft start**: limits inrush when the pump begins.
:::

::: exceptional

:::

::: mistakes
- **Using a pump for amps.** $R_{out}$ and switch current limits make it impractical.
- **Quoting 100% efficiency.** Capped at $V_{out}/(nV_{in})$.
- **Forgetting the input current $n\times I_{out}$.**
- **Confusing with the PLL charge pump.**
- **Ignoring mode hopping glitches** in LED drivers (brightness steps, noise).
- **Assuming RS-232 gets the full $\pm$10 V.** Loaded outputs are lower.
:::

::: deep
## Negative rail

An inverter pump ($-1\times$) from 3.3 V gives about $-3.3$ V minus $I_{out}R_{out}$. For a 10 mA op-amp load with $R_{out}=8\ \Omega$ that's $-3.22$ V. The ripple (a few mV at the clock) is easier to filter than an inductive inverter's spikes, and there is no magnetic field near sensitive analog circuits. Often an LDO follows for a quiet rail.

## RS-232

A doubler turns 5 V into about 10 V, then an inverter makes about $-10$ V from that. Loaded by receivers (3 to 7 k$\Omega$), real outputs sit around $\pm$5 to $\pm$9 V depending on the part and supply. The 1 µF-class capacitors are the visible external parts.

## Gate drive and load switches

A high-side N-FET at $V_{in}=5$ V needs about 5 V more at the gate for full enhancement. A tiny pump (microamps) makes $\approx2V_{in}=10$ V. Because $I_{out}$ is microamps, $R_{out}$ is irrelevant and a diode pump is enough. The same trick is used inside bootstrap-less load-switch ICs.

## On-chip high voltage

Flash cells need 10 to 20+ V to program or erase, from a 1.8 to 3.3 V core. A multi-stage Dickson pump on die generates it at microamp levels ([[q0285]]). Efficiency is not the point: area and integration are.

## When not to use a pump

- Current above a few hundred mA at a significant ratio: use an inductive boost ([[q0273]]).
- Precise arbitrary voltage: use a boost or buck-boost with an LDO.
- Wide $V_{in}$ range with fixed ratio: efficiency collapses ($V_{out}/(nV_{in})$).
- Quiet analog rail: pump ripple at the clock still needs an LDO.

::: followups
### Why do LED drivers use fractional ratios?
Because the ratio sets efficiency $V_F/(nV_{bat})$ and a fixed 2x would waste half the power at 4.2 V. 1.5x and 1x modes keep the loss lower across the battery range.

### Why can a gate driver use a very small charge pump?
A MOSFET gate draws only charge during switching and negligible DC current, so microamps suffice and $R_{out}$ does not matter.

### Pump or boost for 5 V at 500 mA from 3.3 V?
A boost. A doubler would need $I_{in}\approx1$ A and a very low $R_{out}$ ($6.6-5=1.6$ V over 0.5 A means 3.2 Ω max) with large switches and capacitors, and efficiency is 76% at best. An inductive boost does 90%+.

### Can a charge pump make a variable output?
Only with regulation: linear post-regulation, burst control, or ratio hopping. The pump itself offers fixed ratios.

### Scenario: LED brightness flickers when the battery crosses 3.4 V. Why?
The driver hops between 1x and 1.5x. The transition can glitch the supply and change headroom briefly. Add hysteresis and check output capacitor value and soft-transition behavior in the datasheet.
:::
