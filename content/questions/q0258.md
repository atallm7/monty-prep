::: testing
Whether you can size $L$, $C_{OUT}$ and $C_{IN}$ from requirements with the actual equations, and whether you know that the number on the BOM is not the number the circuit sees. The hidden sub-skills: picking an inductor ripple ratio, using worst-case $V_{IN}$, separating ripple-driven and transient-driven capacitance, remembering ESR, ESL and DC-bias derating, and recognizing that the **input** capacitor is stressed by RMS pulsed current rather than by voltage ripple alone. Good candidates also name voltage, current and temperature ratings, not just values.
:::

::: why
The power stage passives decide ripple, transient response, stability, efficiency, board area and cost. Most field failures of buck converters (ringing, overheating inductors, ceramic caps cracking or being too small once biased, input spikes killing the IC) trace back to a passive that was specified for the value only. In an interview, this question separates people who recall the ripple formula from those who have built a regulator.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor ripple current**: $\Delta I_L = \dfrac{V_{OUT}(1-D)}{L f_{SW}}$, largest at maximum $V_{IN}$. See [[q0211]].
- **Output ripple**: capacitive part $\Delta I/(8 f_{SW}C)$, ESR part $\Delta I\cdot ESR$, ESL part during edges ([[q0212]]).
- **Load-transient sizing**: during a step the output capacitor supplies the difference until the inductor slews ([[q0248]]).
- **Input capacitor RMS current**: $I_{CIN,RMS} = I_{OUT}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$, peaking at $D=0.5$.
- **Real capacitors**: ESR, ESL, DC-bias derating, temperature, voltage rating ([[q0213]]).
- **Real inductors**: saturation, DCR, self-resonance ([[q0259]]).
- **Stability window**: $L$ and $C$ must suit the compensation ([[q0261]]).
:::

::: answer
"I do the inductor first. I pick a ripple ratio, typically 20 to 40% of the maximum load current, and size L at the worst case, which is maximum input voltage, using $L = V_{OUT}(1-D)/(f_{SW}\Delta I)$. Then I check the peak current $I_{OUT}+\Delta I/2$ against the IC's current limit and pick an inductor whose saturation current is above the limit, with DCR low enough for efficiency.

For the output capacitor there are two requirements. Ripple: $\Delta V \approx \Delta I/(8 f C) + \Delta I\cdot ESR$, which for ceramics is usually easy. And the load transient: the capacitor has to hold the output up while the inductor current slews, which usually demands more capacitance than ripple does. Then I derate: a 22 µF ceramic at 3.3 V on a 6.3 V rating might only be 10 to 15 µF, so I count effective capacitance, and check it against the range the compensation needs.

For the input capacitor the key number is RMS current, $I_{OUT}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$, at most half of $I_{OUT}$, plus the voltage ripple at the pins. I'd put ceramics right at the pins, add a bulk or damping cap if there's a cable, and rate the voltage with margin for transients."
:::

::: technical
**Inductor.** Choose a ripple ratio $r = \Delta I_L / I_{OUT(max)}$ of 0.2 to 0.4. Then at $V_{IN(max)}$:
$$L = \frac{V_{OUT}\,(1 - V_{OUT}/V_{IN(max)})}{f_{SW}\, r\, I_{OUT(max)}}$$
Peak current $I_{pk} = I_{OUT} + \Delta I/2$. Require $I_{sat} \geq I_{LIM(max)}$ (the IC's maximum current limit), and RMS rating $I_{RMS} \approx \sqrt{I_{OUT}^2 + \Delta I^2/12}$ with a temperature-rise rating above it. Lower L gives faster transient but more ripple and RMS loss; higher L gives the opposite ([[q0260]]).

**Output capacitor.** Three constraints, take the max:

1. Ripple: $\Delta V_{pp} \approx \dfrac{\Delta I}{8 f_{SW} C_{OUT}} + \Delta I\cdot ESR$ (plus ESL spikes at edges).
2. Load step $\Delta I_{step}$ with allowed deviation $\Delta V$: for a crossover frequency $f_c$, $C \approx \dfrac{\Delta I_{step}}{2\pi f_c \Delta V}$; energy-based checks give $C \geq \dfrac{L\,\Delta I_{step}^2}{2\,\Delta V\,(V_{IN}-V_{OUT})}$ on a load step-up, and $C \geq \dfrac{L\,\Delta I_{step}^2}{(V_{OUT}+\Delta V)^2 - V_{OUT}^2}$ on a step-down.
3. Stability/compensation window and soft-start/inrush ([[q0260]], [[q0261]]).

Use **effective** capacitance (derated for DC bias and temperature). Rate voltage at roughly 1.5 to 2× the output; ceramic capacitance loss with bias rises as the voltage rating margin shrinks.

**Input capacitor.** It supplies the pulsed switch current. RMS current is $I_{CIN,RMS} = I_{OUT}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$ (at most $I_{OUT}/2$ at $D=0.5$), and the input voltage ripple is $\Delta V_{IN} \approx \dfrac{I_{OUT} D (1-D)}{f_{SW} C_{IN}} + I_{OUT}\,ESR$. Place ceramics adjacent to the VIN/GND pins, rate for $V_{IN(max)}$ plus transient margin (at least 1.5 to 2×) and Irms; if the source is a cable or has high impedance, add a damping bulk capacitor ([[q0265]]).
:::

::: short
"I size the inductor at maximum input for a 20 to 40% ripple current, $L = V_{OUT}(1-D)/(f_{SW}\Delta I)$, and make sure its saturation current clears the IC's current limit. The output cap has to satisfy ripple and the load transient, using effective capacitance after DC-bias derating, and fit the loop's allowed range. The input cap is sized for RMS current, $I_{OUT}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$, and voltage ripple, with ceramics at the pins and a damping bulk cap if there is a cable. I rate everything for voltage, current and temperature with margin."
:::

::: long
"I treat it as three small design problems that share one set of requirements. For my example take 12 V in, 3.3 V out, 3 A, 500 kHz.

Inductor first. I choose a ripple current of about 30% of the load current, so about 0.9 A. At maximum input, say 18 V, duty is 0.18, and L is $V_{OUT}(1-D)/(f\Delta I)$, about 6 µH, so I'd take the next standard value, 6.8 µH. Peak current is about 3.4 A, but the real constraint is the IC's current limit; the inductor's saturation current needs to be above the maximum limit, and its DCR decides conduction loss.

Output capacitor next. Ripple is the easy one: with 0.8 A ripple at 500 kHz, a 50 µF ceramic gives only about 4 mV. The load step is what sizes the capacitor. If I want under 5% deviation on a 2.5 A step and a crossover around 50 kHz, I need roughly 50 µF effective. And that means effective: a 22 µF ceramic can lose more than half its capacitance at 3.3 V bias, so I'd put several in parallel and check against the datasheet's allowed range of C so the compensation still works.

Input capacitor last. The switch pulls a pulsed current from the input, so the cap sees RMS current of up to half the load current, and that's a rating I check as well as capacitance. I place ceramics at the pins to give the pulsed current a tiny loop, and if the supply comes through a cable I add a bulk, lossy capacitor to damp the resonance.

Finally I check everything against ratings: voltage, current, temperature, and then I'd measure ripple and a load step on the board."
:::

::: terms
- **Ripple ratio ($r$)**: $\Delta I_L / I_{OUT}$, typically 0.2 to 0.4.
- **Effective capacitance**: actual capacitance at the operating DC bias and temperature, lower than nominal for ceramics.
- **ESR / ESL**: equivalent series resistance and inductance of a capacitor; ESR sets ripple and a loop zero, ESL sets high-frequency spikes.
- **$I_{sat}$ and $I_{RMS}$ (inductor)**: saturation current (inductance drops) and thermal (rise) current rating.
- **Input RMS current**: $I_{OUT}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$, the AC current $C_{IN}$ must carry.
- **DC-bias derating**: ceramic capacitance falling as DC voltage approaches the rated voltage.
- **Hot loop**: $C_{IN}$ and both FETs; its inductance causes switch-node ringing.
- **Damping capacitor**: lossy bulk capacitor that suppresses LC ringing at the input.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** gives $L = V_{OUT}(1-D)/(f\Delta I)$ and says "use enough capacitance for ripple".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Sizes L at **maximum $V_{IN}$** and justifies the 20 to 40% ripple choice as an efficiency, size and transient trade-off.
- Checks inductor saturation against the **current limit**, not just the load, and separates $I_{sat}$ from $I_{RMS}$.
- Sizes $C_{OUT}$ from **both ripple and load transient**, and notes that the transient usually dominates for ceramics.
- Uses **effective** capacitance after DC-bias derating and checks the compensation window.
- Describes the input cap's role as the AC current source with an **RMS** rating, and mentions damping for cable-fed inputs.
- Runs numbers and then plans bench measurement.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Sizing L at nominal $V_{IN}$.** Ripple is largest at maximum input; a value that is fine at 12 V may produce 50% ripple at 18 V.
- **Ignoring DC-bias derating.** A 22 µF 6.3 V X5R ceramic at 3.3 V may be 10 to 15 µF (part dependent), invalidating your ripple and loop numbers.
- **Only checking ripple for $C_{OUT}$.** Ripple needs a few µF; the load transient needs tens.
- **Choosing inductor by $I_{sat}$ vs load current.** Saturation must be above the IC's current limit, or short-circuit and transient peaks will saturate it.
- **Sizing $C_{IN}$ only by capacitance.** RMS current heating and the $V_{IN}$ rating (with transients) are what kill input capacitors.
- **Forgetting the loop.** Too little (or too much) $C_{OUT}$ relative to the compensation breaks stability ([[q0261]]).
:::

::: deep
## Where the formulas come from

In steady-state CCM the inductor sees $V_{IN}-V_{OUT}$ for $DT$ and $-V_{OUT}$ for $(1-D)T$. Volt-seconds balance:
$$\Delta I_L = \frac{(V_{IN}-V_{OUT})\,D}{L f_{SW}} = \frac{V_{OUT}(1-D)}{L f_{SW}}$$
Substituting $D = V_{OUT}/V_{IN}$ shows that $\Delta I_L$ grows with $V_{IN}$ (the off-time slope $V_{OUT}/L$ is fixed, but the off-time grows as $D$ falls). So worst-case ripple is at **maximum input**. The output capacitor integrates the ripple current: the AC part of $i_L$ charges and discharges $C$, a triangle current into a capacitor gives a parabolic voltage with peak-to-peak $\Delta I/(8 f_{SW} C)$.

## Running example

Requirement: $V_{IN} = 9$ to 18 V (12 V nominal), $V_{OUT} = 3.3$ V, $I_{OUT} = 3$ A, $f_{SW} = 500$ kHz, ripple $\leq 33$ mV, 2.5 A load step with $\leq 165$ mV deviation.

**Inductor.** Choose $r = 0.3$, so $\Delta I = 0.9$ A. At 18 V, $D = 0.183$:
$$L = \frac{3.3\,(1-0.183)}{500\text{k}\times0.9} = 6.0\ \mu\text{H} \Rightarrow 6.8\ \mu\text{H}$$
Then ripple at 18 V is $3.3\times0.817/(500\text{k}\times6.8\mu) = 0.79$ A (26%), at 12 V 0.70 A, at 9 V 0.61 A. Peak current $3 + 0.4 = 3.4$ A, $I_{RMS} \approx 3.01$ A. An inductor with $I_{sat}$ above the IC's maximum current limit (say 5 to 6 A) and a $\geq$ 4 A rms rating, with DCR of order 15 mΩ, fits.

Figure: Ripple falls as $1/L$ and is always greatest at maximum input. The orange line is the 30% target; at 18 V you need about 6 µH to meet it, so 6.8 µH is the next standard value up. Halving L doubles the ripple and the RMS stress.

**Output capacitor.** Ripple: with an effective 55 µF and 5 mΩ ESR, $0.79/(8\times500\text{k}\times55\mu) + 0.79\times0.005 = 3.6 + 4.0 \approx 7.6$ mV (the two are not in phase for ceramics, so the true value is a bit lower, but it is far below 33 mV). Transient: with a crossover at $f_c = 50$ kHz,
$$C \approx \frac{2.5}{2\pi\,(50\text{k})(0.165)} = 48\ \mu\text{F effective}$$
The energy approach gives 15 µF for the load step-up (at $V_{IN} = 12$ V, $L = 6.8$ µH) and 38 µF for the step-down (unloading), where the inductor current has to decay through $V_{OUT}$ alone. So the **load transient** needs about 48 µF effective, six times what ripple needs.

Hardware: ceramic capacitance falls with DC bias. If 22 µF 6.3 V X5R 0805s are only about half their rating at 3.3 V, you want about five of them (110 µF nominal, 55 µF effective).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The amount of capacitance loss at a given DC bias depends strongly on the dielectric, case size and voltage rating; 40 to 60% loss is typical for small-case X5R at 50 to 60% of rated voltage, but you must read the manufacturer's DC-bias curve (or their simulation tool) for the exact part.

**Input capacitor.** At 9 V in, $D = 0.367$, $I_{CIN,RMS} = 3\sqrt{0.367\times0.633} = 1.45$ A; at 12 V, 1.34 A. For a 100 mV input ripple at $D=0.367$: $C_{eff} = \dfrac{3\times0.367\times0.633}{500\text{k}\times0.1} = 13.9\ \mu\text{F}$. With 25 V rated parts that lose half their capacitance at 12 V, you need about 30 µF nominal, say three 10 µF in parallel, which also splits the RMS current.

## Input network and ringing

The input supply, cable and $C_{IN}$ form an LC. With 1 µH of lead inductance and 20 µF of ceramic, $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC}) = 36$ kHz and characteristic impedance $Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C} = 0.22$ Ω. Ceramic ESR is a few mΩ, so $Q$ is very high and a hot-plug can overshoot to about twice the supply. Adding a bulk capacitor with ESR near $Z_0$ (say 100 µF electrolytic or polymer with 0.2 Ω), with capacitance several times the ceramic, damps this. Also, a buck with constant-power-like behaviour presents negative incremental input resistance; that is another reason to damp. See [[q0265]].

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Placement.** The ceramic $C_{IN}$ must be closest to the VIN and PGND pins: the loop it closes with the FETs carries the full switching current, and nanohenrys there create switch-node overshoot.
- **ESL.** At edge transitions the ESL of $C_{OUT}$ adds spikes of $L_{ESL}\,di/dt$; many small capacitors in parallel reduce ESL and ESR.
- **Inductor choice.** Shielded, powdered-iron or composite inductors saturate softly; ferrite cores saturate abruptly ([[q0259]]). The self-resonant frequency should be well above $f_{SW}$.
- **Temperature.** Capacitance and inductance vary with temperature; for capacitors, X7R is more stable than X5R and much more than Y5V/Z5U.

::: applications
- **POL converters on FPGA boards**: several 22 µF ceramics plus a bulk, 1 to 4.7 µH inductors at 0.5 to 2 MHz.
- **Automotive buck**: input ripple current rating and hot-plug/load-dump damping for $C_{IN}$.
- **Battery devices**: very small inductors (1 to 2.2 µH) at 2 to 3 MHz with 10 to 22 µF effective output capacitance.
- **Industrial 24 V supplies**: polymer or electrolytic bulk input capacitors for ripple current and surge damping.
- **High-current CPU core rails**: low-ESR polymer/ceramic stacks and small L (hundreds of nH) for fast load transients ([[q0243]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- Continuous conduction mode, constant $f_{SW}$, and an ideal capacitor model (ESR, ESL as lumped parasitics). DCM and PFM change ripple and frequency ([[q0229]]).
- Ripple ratio of 0.2 to 0.4 is a rule of thumb. Ratio can be higher for small, fast, low-current converters (0.5 or more) and lower where efficiency or noise is critical.
- The load transient sizing treats the loop as well-compensated with the stated crossover; a poorly compensated loop needs more capacitance.
- Capacitor derating numbers are illustrative; read vendor curves.
- Peak-current-mode controllers may restrict $L$ via slope compensation; check the datasheet's allowed range.
:::

::: followups
### Why size the inductor at maximum input voltage?
Ripple $\Delta I = V_{OUT}(1-D)/(L f_{SW})$ increases as $D$ falls (higher $V_{IN}$). If you size at 12 V and the supply goes to 18 V, ripple rises from 0.70 to 0.79 A in my example (6.8 µH), and peak current, saturation margin and output ripple move with it. You also want the converter to stay in CCM at light load at the other extreme, so check minimum load vs $\Delta I/2$.

### Why do ceramic capacitors lose capacitance on the output rail?
Class II dielectrics (X5R/X7R) are ferroelectric; applied DC field aligns domains and reduces permittivity, so capacitance falls with bias and with temperature extremes. Smaller cases and lower voltage ratings fall more. A 22 µF 0805 6.3 V part might read 10 µF at 3.3 V. This lowers real $C_{OUT}$ and raises the loop crossover. Fix by paralleling more parts or using a higher voltage rating; read the DC-bias curve.

### How do you decide between ceramic and polymer/electrolytic output capacitors?
Ceramics: very low ESR (good ripple, but small ESR zero frequency high, so the loop may lack the phase boost it needs). Polymer/electrolytic: higher ESR gives a zero that boosts phase but increases ripple, and has more capacitance density for large transients. Many designs combine ceramic bulk with a small polymer to supply ESR damping. The selection depends on compensation type; see [[q0261]].

### What happens to the input capacitor's life if you undersize it?
RMS current of $I_{OUT}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$ heats the capacitor through ESR. For electrolytics, life roughly doubles for each 10 °C lower core temperature, so a small increase in ripple current rating shortfall can cut life drastically. Ceramics don't wear this way, but undersized parts see larger input ripple and EMI.

### Scenario: output voltage ripple measures 80 mV but your calculation gave 8 mV. Where do you look?
First, measurement: use a short ground spring and no 10 cm ground lead, which picks up switching noise. If the ripple is real, check effective capacitance (DC bias), ESL spikes at the edges (spike width ~ rise time), the inductor entering saturation (ripple current much larger than calculated), and the layout of $C_{OUT}$ relative to the load. Look at the shape: triangle (capacitive), sawtooth with spikes (ESL), or unstable subharmonic ([[q0253]]).
:::

::: rubric
- Sizes L from ripple ratio at maximum $V_{IN}$ with $L = V_{OUT}(1-D)/(f_{SW}\Delta I)$ :: The formula is the basic requirement; maximum input is the hidden condition.
- Checks peak current against current limit and picks $I_{sat}$ accordingly :: Prevents saturation in transients and faults.
- Distinguishes $I_{sat}$ (magnetic) from $I_{RMS}$ (thermal) and mentions DCR :: Shows real inductor selection experience.
- Sizes $C_{OUT}$ for ripple and load transient, noting transient dominates :: Shows you know why bigger caps are needed than ripple suggests.
- Accounts for ESR/ESL and ceramic DC-bias derating :: Real capacitors differ from their nominal values.
- Gives input capacitor RMS current $I_{OUT}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$ and mentions voltage rating margin :: The input cap's stress is different from the output cap's.
- Mentions hot-plug ringing/damping and placement close to the pins :: Practical failure mode and layout.
- Verifies the choice against loop stability window :: Passives are part of the compensation.
