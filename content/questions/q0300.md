::: testing
Whether you know that "lithium-ion" is a *family* defined mostly by the **cathode** (and sometimes the anode), and can name the main members with their characteristic voltage, energy density, life and safety: LCO, NMC, NCA, LMO, LFP and LTO, plus lithium-metal primary cells. The interviewer also wants the trade-offs in a form you can use: which to pick for a phone, a power tool, a stationary store, or a fast-charging bus, and what the voltage differences mean for the electronics (series counts, charger voltage, state-of-charge estimation).
:::

::: why
The chemistry sets nearly everything about a battery design: the charge voltage your charger must regulate, the number of cells in series for a given rail, how you estimate state of charge, and how much safety margin you have. A designer who treats all "lithium" cells as 3.7 V/4.2 V will build a charger that destroys LiFePO4 cells or undercharges LTO. This question is a quick check that you understand the range and can choose, and it leads into voltage ranges ([[q0305]]) and SoC estimation difficulties ([[q0308]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Cathode material defines the family**: layered oxides (LCO, NMC, NCA), spinel (LMO), olivine (LFP). Anode is usually graphite, or lithium titanate (LTO).
- **Nominal and limit voltages**: each chemistry has its own charge limit ([[q0305]]).
- **Energy vs life vs safety**: the main trade triangle ([[q0292]]).
- **Flat vs sloped OCV curve**: affects SoC estimation ([[q0308]]).
- **Thermal stability**: oxygen release from layered oxides at high temperature vs the strongly bonded phosphate in LFP ([[q0327]]).
- **Primary lithium cells** (lithium metal anode) are a separate category ([[q0290]]).
:::

::: answer
"Lithium-ion is really a family named by the cathode. The common ones: lithium cobalt oxide, LCO, in phones and laptops, highest energy for its size, 3.7 volts nominal, but shorter life and the least thermally stable. Lithium nickel manganese cobalt, NMC, is the workhorse for e-bikes, tools and EVs: a good balance of energy, power and life. NCA is similar with even higher energy, used in some EVs and cylindrical cells. Lithium manganese oxide, LMO, is cheaper and good for high current but has lower energy and shorter life. Lithium iron phosphate, LiFePO4 or LFP, is 3.2 volts per cell, much safer and lasts thousands of cycles, but has lower energy density, and its voltage curve is very flat. And lithium titanate, LTO, uses a titanate anode: only about 2.4 volts, low energy, but very fast charging, extreme cycle life and good cold performance.

So the trade-offs are always energy versus life versus safety versus cost. For a phone, energy and size win, so LCO or NMC. For a power tool, NMC with high current capability. For home energy storage or a lead-acid replacement, LFP. For something that fast-charges constantly, like a bus, LTO. And I'd remember that each has a different charge voltage, so a charger has to match the chemistry."
:::

::: technical
**Typical characteristics (cell level, indicative)**

| Chemistry | Cathode / anode | Nominal V | Max charge V | Energy (Wh/kg) | Cycle life (to 80%) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LCO | LiCoO₂ / graphite | 3.6-3.7 | 4.2 (4.35-4.45 for high-voltage types) | 150-200 | 500-1000 | phones, laptops; cobalt cost; least thermally stable |
| NMC | LiNiMnCoO₂ / graphite | 3.6-3.7 | 4.2 | 150-250 | 1000-2000 | EVs, tools, e-bikes; most versatile |
| NCA | LiNiCoAlO₂ / graphite | 3.6 | 4.2 | 200-260 | 500-1500 | highest energy; cost and safety care |
| LMO | LiMn₂O₄ / graphite | 3.7-3.8 | 4.2 | 100-150 | 300-700 | high current, cheap; short life, often blended with NMC |
| LFP | LiFePO₄ / graphite | 3.2 | 3.6-3.65 | 90-160 | 2000-5000+ | safest of the common types, flat curve, no cobalt |
| LTO | various / Li₄Ti₅O₁₂ | 2.3-2.4 | 2.7-2.8 | 50-110 | 10,000+ | very fast charge, low-temperature capable, expensive, low energy |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> All figures are broad typical ranges that vary with cell design (energy vs power cell), manufacturer, test method and year. Cell voltage limits vary per datasheet. Never design a charger from this table: use the cell's datasheet.

**Why voltages differ.** Cell voltage is the difference between cathode and anode potentials versus Li/Li⁺. Graphite is at about 0.1 V vs Li; layered oxides sit near 3.7-3.9 V (average discharge); LFP at about 3.4 V (so 3.2-3.3 V cell); LTO anode at about 1.55 V vs Li, hence a ~2.3-2.4 V cell with an NMC/LMO cathode.

**Why safety differs.** Layered oxide cathodes can release oxygen on heating or overcharge, feeding thermal runaway. The strong P-O bonds in the LFP olivine hold oxygen far more tightly, so thermal onset is higher and runaway is less violent. LTO avoids the lithium-plating and SEI issues of graphite, so it charges fast and safely.

**Primary lithium (metal) cells.** Li-MnO₂ (3 V coin cells), Li-SOCl₂ (3.6 V), Li-FeS₂ (1.5 V AA). Not rechargeable; extreme shelf life.

**Design consequences.** Series count for a given bus: 3S NMC is 11.1 V nominal (12.6 V full, 9.0 V at 3.0 V/cell); 4S LFP is 12.8 V nominal (14.6 V full, 10.0 V at 2.5 V/cell), the reason 4S LFP is a drop-in for 12 V lead-acid.

::: long
"Lithium-ion isn't one chemistry, it's a family, mostly defined by the cathode material.

Lithium cobalt oxide, LCO, is the classic phone and laptop cell: 3.7 volts nominal, 4.2 full, highest energy density in a small package, but it only lasts a few hundred to a thousand cycles, it uses expensive cobalt, and it is the least thermally stable.

NMC, nickel manganese cobalt, is the workhorse. Tune the ratio and you can favour energy or power. It's used in e-bikes, power tools and many EVs. Good energy, decent cycle life, safer than LCO. NCA, nickel cobalt aluminium, is similar with even more energy, used in some EVs and high-capacity cylindrical cells, but it needs careful thermal management.

LMO, manganese oxide, has a spinel structure that handles current well and is cheaper and safer, but has lower energy and shorter life, so it's often blended with NMC.

Then two that differ more. Lithium iron phosphate, LFP, is 3.2 volts per cell and charges to about 3.65. The phosphate bond holds oxygen tightly, so it's much safer, lasts thousands of cycles, and uses no cobalt. The price is lower energy density and a very flat voltage curve, which makes state of charge hard to read from voltage. Lithium titanate, LTO, swaps the graphite anode for titanate: only about 2.4 volts and low energy, but it charges in minutes, lasts ten thousand cycles, and works in the cold.

So I choose by what's scarce: size and energy, go LCO or NMC; balanced power and life, NMC; safety and cycle life in stationary storage, LFP; extreme fast charge or cold, LTO. And whatever I choose, the charger voltage and the number of cells in series have to match."
:::

::: terms
- **Cathode chemistry**: LCO, NMC, NCA, LMO, LFP; sets voltage, energy and safety.
- **LFP / LiFePO₄**: lithium iron phosphate; olivine cathode, 3.2 V nominal.
- **LTO**: lithium titanate anode; about 2.3-2.4 V cell, very fast charge and long life.
- **Energy cell vs power cell**: thick-electrode high-energy design vs thin-electrode high-current design.
- **Nominal vs maximum charge voltage**: e.g. 3.7/4.2 V vs 3.2/3.65 V.
- **Spinel / layered / olivine**: crystal structures of LMO / LCO,NMC,NCA / LFP.
- **Thermal runaway onset**: temperature at which self-heating becomes uncontrolled; higher for LFP.
- **Specific energy**: Wh/kg.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Charging every lithium cell to 4.2 V.** LFP must stop at about 3.6-3.65 V; LTO at about 2.7-2.8 V; high-voltage LCO may go to 4.35-4.45 V. Wrong voltage destroys the cell or gives almost no capacity.
- **Equating LiPo with a chemistry.** LiPo is a format/electrolyte term; a pouch can be LCO, NMC or LFP ([[q0298]]).
- **Saying LFP is "better".** It is better on life and safety, worse on energy density and voltage; for a phone it's a poor choice.
- **Assuming LTO is just a safer NMC.** Its low voltage (2.4 V) means about a third less energy per Ah and many more cells in series.
- **Using voltage-based SoC for LFP.** The flat curve makes voltage a poor indicator in the mid-range ([[q0308]]).
- **Quoting cycle life without conditions.** It depends on depth of discharge, rate and temperature.
:::

::: deep
## A family tree, organised by what changes

Every lithium-ion cell has the same architecture: an anode and cathode that can host lithium, a separator and an electrolyte ([[q0302]]). What changes between members is which host materials are used:

- **Cathodes**: layered oxides (LCO, NMC, NCA), spinel oxide (LMO), olivine phosphate (LFP).
- **Anodes**: graphite (most), lithium titanate (LTO), graphite with silicon blended in (some high-energy cells).
- **Format**: cylindrical, prismatic, pouch ([[q0298]]).

The cathode determines the voltage window and the energy per Ah. The anode determines how fast and how safely the cell can charge.

## Voltage and the shape of the OCV curve

Cell voltage is the cathode potential minus the anode potential, both against Li/Li⁺. Because LFP and LTO store lithium in two-phase reactions, their potentials barely change with state of charge; that gives a nearly flat OCV curve. Layered oxides change continuously, giving a sloped curve.

## Safety: why LFP is different

Layered oxides such as LCO and NMC contain oxygen in structures that become unstable at high state of charge and temperature, releasing oxygen that reacts with the flammable electrolyte. That is the engine of thermal runaway ([[q0332]]). In LFP, oxygen is covalently bonded in the PO₄ phosphate group, which is far more stable, so the onset temperature of self-heating is higher and the energy released is lower. LTO's higher anode potential avoids lithium plating and the SEI problems of graphite, which is why it tolerates fast charging and low temperatures.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Thermal-runaway onset temperatures, severity and "safer" claims depend on cell design, size, state of charge, additives and test method. LFP is generally more tolerant, not immune.

## Choosing by application

| Need | Good choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Thin phone with maximum runtime | LCO / high-voltage LCO or NMC pouch | highest volumetric energy |
| E-bike, power tool, general EV | NMC (or NCA) | energy + power + life balance |
| Stationary storage, RV, 12 V replacement | LFP | thousands of cycles, thermal stability, no cobalt |
| Cheap high-current pack | LMO (often blended with NMC) | spinel conducts well; cheap |
| Fast-charge fleets, cold climates | LTO | minutes to charge, 10,000+ cycles, low-temperature capable |
| 10-year sensor, not rechargeable | Li-SOCl₂ / Li-MnO₂ primary | self-discharge below ~1%/year (typical) |

## Series counts and charger voltage

Because voltages differ, the pack arithmetic changes:

- 3S NMC: nominal 11.1 V, full 12.6 V, empty about 9.0 V (3.0 V/cell).
- 4S LFP: nominal 12.8 V, full 14.6 V, empty about 10.0 V (2.5 V/cell). It fits a 12 V lead-acid slot, but charge voltage is 14.6 V, which some lead-acid chargers can reach but must not float.
- A 12.8 V, 100 Ah LFP pack stores $12.8\times100/1000=1.28$ kWh; usable about 90% (1.15 kWh) versus a lead-acid 12 V 100 Ah that offers about 50% usable (0.6 kWh), which is the stationary-storage argument.

A charger must match the cell chemistry exactly: an LFP charger regulating 14.6 V for 4S would put 3.65 V per cell, while using that on a 3S NMC pack would give 4.87 V per cell. Sub-systems such as a gauge ([[q0308]]), protection IC thresholds and balancing ([[q0314]]) all need chemistry-specific settings.

## Trends and caveats

Silicon-graphite anodes increase energy density at the expense of expansion and aging; high-nickel NMC (e.g. 811) increases energy and lowers cobalt but is thermally more sensitive; manganese-rich, sodium-ion and solid-state technologies are active research and early product areas. Treat these as emerging and defer to current datasheets.

## Where the numbers come from: Faraday and Gibbs

Two relations from electrochemistry explain most of the table. The cell voltage comes from the free-energy change of the reaction, $E_{cell}=-\Delta G/(nF)$ with $n=1$ electron per lithium and $F=96485$ C/mol, so a 3.3 V cell has $\Delta G\approx-318$ kJ per mole of lithium and a 3.7 V cell about $-357$ kJ. The capacity per gram of host material follows from counting how many lithium ions it can store per formula unit:

$$q=\frac{xF}{3.6\,M}\ \text{mAh/g},\qquad F/3.6=26{,}801\ \text{mAh/mol}$$

where $x$ is the number of lithium ions cycled per formula unit and $M$ is the molar mass. Working through the common cathodes:

| Material | $M$ (g/mol) | $x$ cycled | Capacity $q$ (mAh/g) | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| LiFePO₄ | 157.8 | 1.0 | 170 | practical value is close to theoretical |
| LiCoO₂ | 97.9 | 0.5 | 137 | only about half the lithium is removed in practice; going further wrecks the structure |
| Li₄Ti₅O₁₂ (anode) | 459.1 | 3 | 175 | the LTO anode, with a flat 1.55 V plateau |

LiCoO₂ could in principle deliver 274 mAh/g if all the lithium came out ($x=1$), but the structure collapses beyond roughly $x=0.5$ to 0.6 in standard cells. That is the whole story of "high-voltage LCO" (4.35 to 4.45 V): it pushes $x$ a bit higher using dopants and coatings to get a few percent more capacity, at a cost in cycle life and safety margin. Multiplying by voltage gives cathode energy of about 580 Wh/kg of LFP active material (at 3.4 V) versus about 530 Wh/kg for LCO (at 3.9 V). The materials are surprisingly close. The reason LFP cells end up around 160 Wh/kg against 250 Wh/kg for NMC cells is not the cathode alone: it is the lower average voltage, the lower electrode density (LFP's particles are small and need carbon coating), and the thicker inactive structure the cell needs to hold the electrode together. Cell-level energy density is a system property.

## A design example: pick a chemistry for a 1 kWh pack

Compare 1 kWh of nominal energy in an LFP pack at 160 Wh/kg and an NMC pack at 250 Wh/kg, cycled to a stated depth with 3000 and 1000 cycles to 80% capacity respectively (illustrative values from the table ranges):

- **Mass:** $1000/160=6.25$ kg for LFP, $1000/250=4.0$ kg for NMC, a 56% penalty for LFP.
- **Lifetime energy throughput** (taking the average capacity over life as 90% of nominal): LFP $3000\times0.9=2.7$ MWh, NMC $1000\times0.9=0.9$ MWh. That is three times as much energy delivered per kWh of installed cell.

So if the cost per kWh were equal, LFP would be three times cheaper per delivered kWh, whereas NMC would be better for anything where each kg carried costs flight time, range or a user's comfort. For a drone or phone the mass term wins; for a home battery that cycles daily for ten years (about 3650 cycles) the throughput term wins. That single comparison is a good way to say what "the best chemistry" means in an interview: you give the criteria and weigh them for the application.

## Limiting cases for state-of-charge from voltage

With the OCV slopes in the plot, a given voltage error maps into SoC error as $\Delta\text{SoC}=\Delta V/(dV/d\text{SoC})$. For 10 mV: NMC at 8 mV per 1% gives 1.25%; LFP at 1 mV per 1% gives 10%; LTO is similar to LFP at about 10%. There is a second problem, hysteresis: LFP's OCV after charging differs from after discharging at the same SoC by several tens of millivolts at the same state of charge (cell-dependent), which exceeds the entire mid-range voltage swing. A gauge that uses voltage alone on LFP cannot do better than tens of percent in the middle of the curve, which is why LFP gauges integrate current and only use voltage at the steep ends. For LFP, relaxation after a load also takes longer, so "rest and read" needs longer waits.

## Fast charge and the anode

The anode sets charge acceptance. Graphite sits at about 0.1 V versus lithium, only about 100 mV above the potential where lithium metal plates. Under high charge current, the anode surface potential is pushed lower by the $IR$ drop and diffusion limits, and at the surface it can fall below 0 V versus Li/Li⁺, where lithium plates as metal instead of inserting. This is why graphite cells have a charge-rate limit, why cold charging is prohibited, and why the CV phase shortens the safe fast-charge window. LTO sits at about 1.55 V versus lithium, more than a volt of margin above plating, so it can be charged at 6C to 10C (10 to 6 minutes for a full charge, ignoring the CV tail) at room temperature and even in the cold. The price is that the 1.55 V anode subtracts about 1.5 V from the cell voltage, so energy per cell falls by a third or more: a 20 Ah LTO cell at 2.4 V stores 48 Wh against about 74 Wh for the same Ah at 3.7 V.

## What to check when you inherit a pack

1. Read the cell part number and get the datasheet; do not infer the chemistry from the pack label.
2. Measure OCV of an unloaded cell and see if it is consistent with the claimed chemistry and SoC (a "3.3 V" cell at rest could be LFP at mid charge or a deeply discharged NMC).
3. Check that the charger target, protection thresholds and gauge OCV table all match the chemistry. Pack failures from the wrong charge target are a classic.
4. Run a slow capacity test at 0.2C and compare Wh with the nominal to confirm the chemistry's energy density.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Cycle life, energy density and the claim that LFP hysteresis exceeds the mid-curve swing depend on the cell and the test. Treat the numbers as illustrative and check the cell datasheet or cell-level characterization for the part you use.

::: followups
### Why does LiFePO₄ have such a flat discharge curve and why is that a problem?
Lithium moves between two coexisting phases (LiFePO₄ and FePO₄) at nearly constant potential, so the voltage barely moves from 20% to 80% SoC: about 1 mV per 1% SoC versus around 8 mV per 1% for NMC. Voltage-based gauging then has huge error from tiny measurement offset or sag, so the BMS needs coulomb counting and uses the steep ends for re-calibration.

### Why would you choose NMC over LFP for a drone?
Energy per kg and per cell voltage. NMC delivers roughly 1.5-2× the Wh/kg of LFP at similar rate capability, so flight time is longer. LFP's cycle life and safety are valuable in stationary storage, but weight counts for more in aircraft.

### What do you give up with LTO?
Energy density and cost: a 2.4 V cell with roughly half to a third of the Wh/kg of NMC, so more cells and mass for the same energy, and a higher price per kWh. In exchange you get charge in minutes, 10,000+ cycles, very good cold behaviour and low plating risk.

### Can you charge an LFP battery with a Li-ion charger?
Not safely as-is. A 4.2 V per cell charger would put the LFP cell far above its 3.65 V limit, damaging it. For a single cell the CC/CV target must be about 3.6-3.65 V. Use a chemistry-specific charger or a configurable charger IC.

### How would a cell's cathode choice change your protection IC thresholds?
Overvoltage and undervoltage trip points shift: for LFP maybe about 3.7-3.8 V OV and about 2.0-2.5 V UV; for NMC about 4.25-4.35 V OV and about 2.5-3.0 V UV; for LTO about 2.8 V OV and about 1.5-2.0 V UV. These are indicative; always use the datasheet.
:::
