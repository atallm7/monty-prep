::: testing
Whether you can define a wafer precisely and say why it exists: a thin, round slice of ultra-pure single-crystal silicon on which hundreds or thousands of identical dies are built in parallel. The interviewer wants the vocabulary (ingot, Czochralski, 300 mm, notch, die) and some feel for numbers: diameter, thickness, dies per wafer, why wafers are round, and why bigger wafers lower cost per die. Hidden sub-skills: area and edge-loss estimation, linking wafer size to cost, and knowing a wafer is not the same as a die or a package ([[q0645]]).
:::

::: why
Every chip's cost starts with how many good dies you get per wafer, and wafer cost, size and defects drive pricing, capacity and supply discussions. For a hardware engineer, understanding the wafer explains die size trade-offs, yield, why big dies cost disproportionately more ([[q0651]]), why reticle and wafer geometry produce partial dies at the edge, and what "300 mm fab" means in the news. It is the base for the rest of the fabrication questions.
:::

::: concepts
- **Single-crystal silicon**: atoms arranged in one continuous lattice, needed so transistors behave uniformly. See [[q0064]].
- **Czochralski growth**: pulling a crystal from a silicon melt to make a cylindrical ingot.
- **Wafer diameter and thickness**: standard sizes are 150, 200 and 300 mm, with thickness around 0.7 mm for the larger ones.
- **Doping and resistivity**: wafers are lightly doped (typically with boron for p-type), which sets the substrate resistivity. See [[q0065]].
- **Die**: a rectangular chip site; the wafer is a grid of dies separated by scribe lines. See [[q0645]].
- **Yield**: the fraction of dies that work, which depends on defects and die area. See [[q0650]].
:::

::: answer
"A wafer is a thin, round disc of extremely pure single-crystal silicon that serves as the base on which chips are built. Think of it as the canvas for manufacturing: instead of making chips one at a time, a fab builds hundreds or thousands of identical dies across the wafer at once, then cuts them apart.

It's made by melting very pure silicon and slowly pulling a seed crystal out of the melt, the Czochralski process, which grows a long cylindrical single crystal called an ingot. The ingot is sliced into discs, which are ground, etched and polished until they're flat to nanometre-scale roughness. The standard size for modern leading-edge fabs is 300 millimetres across, about 775 micrometres thick, with a small notch that marks the crystal orientation. Older and specialty fabs use 200 or 150 millimetre wafers.

Wafers are round because of how the crystal is grown, and bigger wafers are better economically: a 300 millimetre wafer has 2.25 times the area of a 200 millimetre one, so the processing cost is spread over more dies. For example, a 100 square millimetre die gives roughly 640 candidate dies on a 300 mm wafer, minus some loss at the edges, and then yield determines how many are good.

So the wafer is the substrate, a die is one chip cut from it, and the package is what the die ends up in."
:::

::: technical
**Material.** Electronic-grade silicon, purity better than parts per billion for impurities. Single crystal grown by Czochralski (CZ): a seed is dipped in molten silicon (melting point 1414 °C) and pulled and rotated slowly, forming an ingot up to ~300 mm in diameter and 1–2 m long. Float-zone silicon (purer, used for power devices) is an alternative. Dopants (boron for p-type; phosphorus or arsenic for n-type) are added to set substrate resistivity, typically around 1–20 Ω·cm for CMOS wafers.

**Standard sizes (SEMI).** 150 mm (≈675 µm thick), 200 mm (≈725 µm), 300 mm (≈775 µm). 300 mm wafers have a notch rather than a flat to mark orientation (so less area is lost). 450 mm wafers were developed but the industry did not adopt them.

**Processing steps to make a wafer.** Slicing with a wire saw, lapping, edge rounding, chemical etching and chemical-mechanical polishing to a mirror finish with nanometre-scale roughness and very tight flatness. Optionally an epitaxial silicon layer is grown on top, or an oxide layer is buried inside to make silicon-on-insulator (SOI).

**Area and dies per wafer.** A good approximation:

$$\text{DPW}\approx\frac{\pi (d/2)^2}{A}-\frac{\pi d}{\sqrt{2A}}$$

where $d$ is wafer diameter and $A$ the die area including scribe lanes. For $d=300$ mm and $A=100\ \text{mm}^2$: $70{,}686/100 - 942.5/14.14 = 706.9 - 66.6 \approx 640$. For $d = 200$ mm: 270. The ratio is 2.37, slightly better than the area ratio of 2.25, because a smaller fraction is lost at the edge.

**Edge exclusion.** The outer few millimetres are unusable because of non-uniform films and handling damage. Partial dies at the edge are discarded.
:::

::: short
"A wafer is a thin, polished disc of ultra-pure single-crystal silicon, usually 300 mm across and about 0.775 mm thick, on which hundreds or thousands of identical chips are built at the same time. It's grown as a crystal ingot by the Czochralski method, sliced and polished. Dies are cut from it later. Bigger wafers mean more dies per processing step, which lowers cost per chip."
:::

::: long
"A wafer is the silicon base that all integrated circuits are built on. Chemically it's about as pure as any manufactured material, single-crystal silicon where the atoms form one continuous lattice, because uniform crystal properties are essential for transistors to behave identically across the chip.

To make one, you start with polysilicon, melt it at about 1414 degrees Celsius, dip in a small seed crystal, and slowly pull and rotate it, so a big cylindrical crystal, the ingot, grows. This is the Czochralski method. A bit of dopant, usually boron, goes in to make the wafer lightly p-type. The ingot is ground to a precise diameter, sliced with a wire saw into thin discs, and then each disc is lapped, etched and chemically-mechanically polished until it's mirror-flat. Leading-edge fabs use 300 millimetre wafers, about 775 micrometres thick, with a notch to indicate crystal orientation. Older analog and power fabs use 200 or 150 millimetre.

The reason we use wafers is parallelism. A whole wafer goes through each process step together, so you build hundreds or thousands of chips simultaneously. If a die is 100 square millimetres, a 300 millimetre wafer fits around 640 of them, after subtracting edge loss. Each die is surrounded by narrow scribe lanes where the wafer will later be sawn apart.

Bigger wafers are cheaper per die because a 300 millimetre wafer has 2.25 times the area of a 200 millimetre one and processing costs are mostly per wafer.

So the wafer is the substrate, a die is one chip sitting on it, and the package is what wraps a single die for use on a board."
:::

::: terms
- **Wafer**: thin disc of semiconductor material on which dies are fabricated.
- **Ingot / boule**: the single-crystal cylinder from which wafers are sliced.
- **Czochralski (CZ) process**: crystal growth by pulling from a melt with a seed.
- **Notch / flat**: marker on the wafer edge indicating crystal orientation.
- **Die (plural dice or dies)**: one chip site on the wafer.
- **Scribe lane (street)**: the strip between dies where the wafer is cut.
- **Epitaxial layer**: a thin crystalline silicon layer grown on the wafer surface.
- **SOI (silicon on insulator)**: wafer with a buried oxide layer under the active silicon.
- **Dies per wafer (DPW)**: number of complete dies that fit on a wafer.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "a thin slice of silicon that chips are made on." An exceptional answer:
- States single crystal and ultra-pure, and says how it is made (Czochralski, slice, polish).
- Gives size/thickness numbers (300 mm, ~775 µm, notch) and mentions 200/150 mm for specialty processes.
- Explains the economics: parallel processing, cost per wafer, and area scaling with $d^2$.
- Calculates dies per wafer with the edge-loss term and notes that yield is separate.
- Distinguishes wafer vs die vs package, and mentions variants (epi, SOI, compound wafers).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying the wafer is "the chip".** The wafer holds many dies; each die becomes one chip ([[q0645]]).
- **Thinking wafers are made from sand directly.** Sand is purified through several chemical steps to polysilicon first.
- **Quoting dies per wafer as area ratio only.** Edge loss and scribe lanes reduce it, especially for big dies.
- **Assuming all wafers are silicon.** Power and RF often use SiC, GaN or GaAs wafers, smaller in diameter.
- **Calling the 300 mm size a node.** Wafer size and process node (transistor technology) are unrelated.
:::

::: deep
## What a wafer is made of and why

Transistors rely on carefully controlled doping and a defect-free lattice. A single crystal keeps the electrical properties uniform across the chip: no grain boundaries cutting through channels, uniform carrier mobility, predictable oxide growth. Silicon is used because it is abundant, forms a stable native oxide (SiO₂) with excellent interface properties, and can be purified and grown into large crystals cheaply.

### Making the crystal

1. **Polysilicon**: sand is reduced to metallurgical-grade silicon, converted to a chlorosilane gas, distilled and deposited as high-purity polysilicon.
2. **Czochralski pull**: polysilicon is melted in a quartz crucible at about 1414 °C. A seed crystal with the desired orientation (usually (100) for CMOS) is dipped in the melt and slowly pulled upward while rotating. Pull speed and temperature control the diameter. Boron or phosphorus is added to the melt to set resistivity.
3. **Shaping**: the ingot is ground to an exact diameter and a notch is cut.
4. **Slicing**: a wire saw cuts the ingot into discs, around 775 µm thick for 300 mm wafers.
5. **Lap, etch, polish**: grinding removes saw damage, etching removes the damaged layer, and CMP gives a surface with nanometre-level roughness and global flatness of tens of nanometres across the wafer.

Wafers are round because a rotating crystal pulled from a melt grows as a cylinder. A round wafer also spins uniformly in photoresist coating and furnaces.

## Wafer sizes and why they matter

| Diameter | Typical thickness | Area | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| 150 mm | ≈675 µm | 17,700 mm² | Legacy, MEMS, some power devices |
| 200 mm | ≈725 µm | 31,400 mm² | Analog, mixed signal, power, MEMS |
| 300 mm | ≈775 µm | 70,700 mm² | Leading-edge logic and memory |

The area scales as $d^2$, so a 300 mm wafer has 2.25× the area of a 200 mm wafer, but most per-wafer process costs (tool time, resist, gases) are roughly per wafer, so cost per die falls. The industry looked at 450 mm wafers but did not adopt them, because the cost of re-tooling the whole equipment industry was too high for the benefit.

## Dies per wafer

For a die of area $A$ (including scribe lanes) on a wafer of diameter $d$:

$$\text{DPW}\approx\frac{\pi (d/2)^2}{A}-\frac{\pi d}{\sqrt{2A}}$$

The first term is the area ratio; the second subtracts the partial dies lost around the edge (perimeter times about half the die diagonal).

### Worked example

How many *good* dies? Take $A=100\ \text{mm}^2 = 1\ \text{cm}^2$, $D_0=0.1\ \text{cm}^{-2}$: Poisson yield $Y=e^{-0.1}=0.905$. Good dies ≈ $640\times0.905=579$. For an 800 mm² die, DPW $\approx 65$, $Y=e^{-0.8}=0.449$, giving about 29 good dies. Same wafer cost, 20× fewer good chips, which is why big dies are so expensive ([[q0650]]).

## Variants

- **Epitaxial wafers**: a thin, cleaner silicon layer grown on the wafer, used to control resistivity and reduce latch-up.
- **SOI**: a buried oxide layer isolates the thin top silicon from the bulk, reducing parasitic capacitance.
- **Compound wafers** (GaAs, InP, SiC, GaN): smaller (typically 100–200 mm), used for RF, optoelectronics and power. GaN is often grown on silicon.
- **Reclaimed or test wafers**: used for process monitoring.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Wafer thicknesses and resistivity ranges vary by supplier and product; the SEMI standard values are nominal (about 675/725/775 µm). Typical CMOS substrate resistivity of 1–20 Ω·cm is a representative range, not a standard.
:::

::: applications
- **300 mm wafers**: CPUs, GPUs, DRAM, NAND and other leading-edge products.
- **200 mm wafers**: analog ICs, power management, MEMS sensors, automotive microcontrollers.
- **150 mm and smaller**: SiC and GaN power devices, compound semiconductors, LEDs.
- **SOI wafers**: RF switches and low-power logic.
- **Test wafers**: used by fabs to qualify tools; "dummy" wafers keep furnace loading constant.
:::

::: assumptions
- Numbers are for standard silicon wafers; thickness values are nominal SEMI sizes.
- The DPW formula ignores scribe lane width unless included in $A$, edge exclusion, and reticle-shot grid effects (dies are placed in rectangular fields, not independently), so real counts differ by a few percent.
- Poisson yield is a simple model; real yield includes parametric and systematic loss.
- "Wafer" without qualifier means a silicon wafer; compound semiconductor wafers have different sizes and processes.
:::

::: followups
### Why are wafers round and not square?
The crystal is grown as a cylinder by pulling from a melt while rotating, so slices are round. Round wafers also spin uniformly in coating and polishing steps and heat evenly in furnaces. Dies are rectangular, which wastes the area at the edge, but square wafers would be hard to grow from a round ingot without waste.

### Why use 300 mm instead of 200 mm wafers?
More area per wafer processed, so the fixed per-wafer cost spreads across more dies. 300 mm has 2.25× the area and for a 100 mm² die about 2.4× the dies. Leading-edge fabs adopted it in the 2000s; analog and power fabs keep 200 mm because their processes and tools are mature and depreciated.

### What does the notch do?
It marks crystal orientation so tools can align every wafer the same way. This matters because transistor performance and cleave directions depend on the crystal direction, and the dies must be aligned with the crystal planes.

### What limits the maximum die size on a wafer?
The lithography field (about 26 × 33 mm, or 858 mm²), yield (large dies catch more defects) and edge loss. See [[q0651]].

### Scenario: your foundry says the next lot has "lower yield at the wafer edge." What could be causing it, and what can you do as a customer?
Edge effects include non-uniform film thickness, etch rate and temperature, handling damage, and particles from the carrier. As a customer you can check the wafer map for edge-ring clustering, ask whether edge exclusion or edge die handling changed, and, if you must reduce risk, move critical blocks away from the edge dies or use redundancy. The foundry can adjust process uniformity tuning.
:::
