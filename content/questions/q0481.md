::: testing
Whether you can connect a **part's properties** (pitch, pin count, speed, number of supply rails, RF content) to the **layer count it forces**. Anyone can say "FPGAs and BGAs"; a strong answer says *why* each one pushes the count: escape routing, reference planes for fast interfaces, power domains and decoupling, isolation and thermal. It also gives rough numbers for how many layers each class typically needs, and says where those numbers come from.
:::

::: why
In a real project the component list is the strongest predictor of the stackup and therefore of cost. The chip you pick on day one can decide whether you build a 4-layer or an 8-layer board. Reading a datasheet's package and interface section with an eye to board layers is a skill that separates people who have laid out boards from people who haven't. This also feeds directly into [[q0475]] (how many layers) and [[q0476]] (which stackup).
:::

::: concepts
- **Escape routing**: getting signals out from under dense packages, limited by pitch and trace/space rules. See [[q0475]].
- **Controlled-impedance interfaces**: USB, DDR, PCIe, Ethernet need reference planes. See [[q0479]].
- **Power domains**: multiple rails and decoupling placement. See [[q0480]].
- **Return paths and isolation**: RF and mixed-signal sections need clean planes. See [[q0493]].
- **Thermal and current**: heavy copper and thermal vias add layers or weight.
- **Package types**: QFP, QFN, BGA, WLCSP, PoP. See [[q0462]].
:::

::: answer
"The components that drive the layer count up are the ones that are dense, fast, or have lots of supplies.

Fine-pitch BGAs are the main one. With a BGA you have to bring every signal out from under the chip, and once the pitch is 0.8 mm or less you can only fit one trace between pads, so you get roughly two rows of balls per layer. A few hundred balls can need four or more signal layers just for escape. FPGAs and application processors are the standard examples, and that's why they're usually six to ten layers.

Then there are fast interfaces: DDR memory, PCIe, USB 3, HDMI, gigabit Ethernet. They need controlled impedance, a solid reference plane beside every routing layer, and tight length matching, so you add layers to give each signal layer its own plane.

Third is power: a processor with a core rail, an I/O rail, DDR, analog and so on needs several planes or islands and lots of decoupling, and that adds power layers.

And RF or mixed-signal parts need isolation and clean ground planes, so you put those on dedicated layers. Big connectors or backplanes with hundreds of pins also drive layers. Simple parts like small QFP microcontrollers, op-amps and power regulators don't, which is why a hobby board can be two layers."
:::

::: technical
**Why a BGA forces layers.** With via-pad diameter $d_v$, pitch $p$, trace width $w$ and spacing $s$ (assume $w = s$), the number of traces that fit in a channel is

$$n=\left\lfloor\frac{p-d_v-w}{2w}\right\rfloor$$

| Pitch | Via pad | Trace/space | Gap | Traces per channel $n$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.27 mm | 0.65 mm | 0.10 mm | 0.62 mm | 2 |
| 1.0 mm | 0.55 mm | 0.10 mm | 0.45 mm | 1 |
| 0.8 mm | 0.45 mm | 0.10 mm | 0.35 mm | 1 |
| 0.65 mm | 0.35 mm | 0.075 mm | 0.30 mm | 1 |
| 0.5 mm | 0.30 mm | 0.075 mm | 0.20 mm | 0 (needs via-in-pad / microvias) |

Heuristic: each signal layer escapes about $n+1$ rows of balls (the outer row leaves directly, and each inner row uses one of the $n$ channel slots). For an array of $N\times N$ balls with a $K\times K$ power/ground core, signal rows $=(N-K)/2$ and

$$\text{signal layers}\approx\left\lceil\frac{(N-K)/2}{n+1}\right\rceil$$

Examples: 16×16 with 6×6 core and $n=1$: 3 layers; 22×22 (484 balls, 1 mm pitch) with 8×8 core and $n=1$: 4 layers; 32×32 with 12×12 core and $n=1$: 5 layers. Add two to four plane layers.

**Other drivers (typical, not rules):**

| Component class | Why it adds layers | Typical total |
|---|---|---|
| Small MCU in QFP/QFN | Low pin count, escapes on top | 2–4 |
| BGA MCU/SoC, 0.8–1.0 mm | Escape + planes | 6–8 |
| DDR3/4/LPDDR to a processor | Matched, referenced byte lanes; many rails | 6–10 |
| Large FPGA/SoC FPGA | Hundreds of I/O, many power rails | 8–14+ |
| PCIe/10 GbE/HDMI/SerDes | Plane next to each routing layer; low-loss | 6–12 |
| RF transceivers + digital | Isolation, ground under RF | 4–8 |
| Backplane/edge connectors, FMC | Many pins, many return pins | 10–30+ |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The "typical total" numbers are experience-based ranges that vary by package, cost target and vendor reference design. Always check the processor or FPGA vendor's layout guide, which often gives a minimum layer count and a reference stackup.
:::

::: short
"Dense, fast or power-hungry parts push the layer count up. Fine-pitch BGAs need extra layers to escape their signals, roughly two rows of balls per layer at 0.8 mm pitch. High-speed interfaces like DDR, PCIe and USB 3 need a reference plane next to each routing layer. Processors and FPGAs with many rails need power planes and decoupling space. RF and mixed-signal sections need isolation, and big connectors need layers for sheer pin count. Simple QFP microcontrollers and regulators don't."
:::

::: long
"I'd group the answer into four things that drive layer count: density, speed, power and isolation.

Density first. Any fine-pitch BGA. Once the pitch gets to 0.8 mm or finer, with normal fab rules you can only fit one trace between adjacent via pads. So each signal layer lets you escape about two rows of balls: the outer row leaves directly and the next one goes through the channels. For a 484-ball FPGA at one millimetre pitch with a power and ground core in the middle, that's around four signal layers just to get out, and then you need planes on top of that. That's why FPGAs and applications processors tend to be eight layers or more. With a 0.5 mm package you can't even fit a trace between pads, so you need via-in-pad and microvias, which is HDI.

Speed second. DDR, PCIe, USB 3, HDMI, and gigabit Ethernet need controlled impedance and a continuous reference next to every signal layer, so more signal layers means more plane layers.

Power third. A modern SoC has a core rail, I/O rails, DDR, PLL and analog supplies. Each wants a low impedance network and decoupling, so you end up with split planes or multiple power layers.

Last, isolation. RF transceivers and sensitive analog front ends want a solid ground directly under them and separation from digital noise. And things like big connectors or backplane interfaces need many layers just because there are hundreds of pins. In contrast, a small MCU in a QFP, a few op-amps, and a buck converter are all fine on two or four layers."
:::

::: terms
- **BGA (ball grid array)**: package with a grid of solder balls under the body.
- **Pitch**: centre-to-centre distance between pins or balls.
- **Escape routing / breakout**: routing signals out of a dense footprint.
- **Via-in-pad**: via placed in the pad (filled and plated) to save space.
- **HDI**: high-density interconnect, using microvias and sequential lamination.
- **Reference plane**: solid plane adjacent to a signal layer.
- **Power domain**: a group of circuits on a separate supply rail.
- **Channel**: the space between adjacent pads/vias available for traces.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer *derives* the BGA argument with the channel arithmetic, quantifies it for a real array, and extends it with the other drivers (interfaces needing reference planes, power domains, RF isolation). It also says that the vendor's reference design or layout guide is the final authority, mentions HDI when pitch drops to 0.5 mm, and notes the flip side: that a part can be moved to a smaller package or a lower-speed configuration to avoid more layers.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"More pins always means more layers."** A 144-pin LQFP routes on 2–4 layers; a 144-ball 0.8 mm BGA may not.
- **Counting only signal layers for BGA escape** and forgetting the plane layers and the reference needs.
- **Forgetting that pitch matters more than ball count.** A 0.5 mm package needs via-in-pad even with fewer balls.
- **Ignoring power.** A rail-heavy part with few signals can still need extra layers for planes.
- **Ignoring fab capability.** 0.075 mm trace/space costs more or isn't offered by cheap fabs.
- **Treating the table of typical layers as a rule.** Always check the vendor's layout guide.
:::

## Fast interfaces

DDR, PCIe, USB 3 and HDMI all demand:
- controlled impedance (single-ended 40–60 Ω, differential 85–100 Ω);
- solid reference planes on at least one side of each routing layer (see [[q0479]]);
- length matching, which costs routing area (serpentines);
- crosstalk spacing, which costs even more area.

A DDR3 interface with two chips needs about 40 matched signals (data, strobes, address, command, clocks). The routing can eat two or three layers plus plane layers.

## Power domains and decoupling

A modern SoC may have 6–10 supply rails, with decoupling capacitors in a ring around or under the package. Each rail needs a low-impedance distribution. See [[q0480]]. Split planes help, but each island is a gap that signals shouldn't cross, so complex parts drive a second power layer.

## RF and mixed-signal

An RF transceiver wants unbroken ground directly under the RF layer, with isolation from digital switching noise. A common pattern is a four-layer board with RF on top, ground L2, digital below. Sensitive analog front ends (24-bit ADCs, small-signal sensors) benefit from a dedicated ground layer and careful partitioning. See [[q0493]].

## High-density connectors and backplanes

A 400-pin connector brings 400 signals to a small area. Even with 1 mm pitch, you need several layers to fan out. Backplane and mezzanine connectors (hundreds of differential pairs) can drive boards of 12–30+ layers; each pair needs a signal layer with adjacent grounds.

## Heat and current

Power stages with tens of amps often use 2–4 oz copper on several layers in parallel, tied with many vias, instead of a thin plane. That increases layer count or copper weight and thermal-via arrays under the package. An example: a 30 A buck stage might use 4 layers of 2 oz copper carrying the switch node and ground returns.

## A second, independent check: routing capacity

Escape counting tells you the layers needed under the package. A second check asks whether the rest of the board has enough *track length*. Each layer can hold a certain total length of trace, and the nets need a certain total length.

**Worked example.** An 80 mm × 60 mm board (4,800 mm²) with 0.1 mm trace and 0.1 mm space has a track pitch of 0.2 mm, so one fully packed layer holds $4800/0.2 = 24{,}000$ mm of trace. Real layers cannot be filled: components, vias, keep-outs and the need to leave room for return paths reduce the achievable utilization, and 30 to 50% is a plausible planning range (an assumed rule of thumb). So one layer holds about 7,200 to 12,000 mm. If the design has 600 nets with an average routed length of 40 mm, it needs $600\times40 = 24{,}000$ mm. That is 2 to 3.3 signal layers' worth.

Now compare the two checks. Escape needs perhaps 3 or 4 layers under the BGA for the 22×22 array, routing capacity says 2 to 4 signal layers, and the plane layers come on top. The two estimates agree to within a layer or so, and the larger one wins. If they disagree wildly, one of your assumptions (average net length, pitch, utilization) is off. It is a sanity check, not a replacement for placing the part and routing it: a hand-drawn or CAD escape of the single densest component takes an hour and gives the real answer.

## Why planes cost layers: the pairing rule

On a high-speed board every routing layer wants an adjacent solid reference plane, and every pair of planes (power and ground) is best kept tightly coupled to give decoupling capacitance and a low-inductance return. A typical eight-layer stack, for example, is arranged signal, ground, signal, power, ground, signal, ground, signal (S-G-S-P-G-S-G-S). Here each signal layer sits next to a plane. Note, though, that S-G-S has two signal layers sharing one plane between them, which saves a layer but means the returns for both layers use the same plane and traces on adjacent layers must be routed orthogonally to limit crosstalk. A stripline signal layer between two planes is better shielded but needs two plane layers. Layer count therefore grows with *signal layers plus planes plus an allowance for power*, not signal layers alone. See [[q0475]] and [[q0479]] for the stack-up and return-path reasoning.

## Vias, stubs and high-speed layer changes

On a thick board, a signal that enters on the top layer and exits on L3 uses a via whose unused lower part is an open-ended **stub**. The stub is a quarter-wave resonator at $f = c/(4L\sqrt{\varepsilon_r})$. For $\varepsilon_r = 4.3$:

| Stub length | Resonance |
|---|---|
| 0.5 mm | 72 GHz |
| 1.2 mm | 30 GHz |
| 3.0 mm | 12 GHz |
| 6.0 mm | 6 GHz |

A 1.2 mm stub is harmless for a 5 Gb/s link (Nyquist 2.5 GHz) but a 3 mm stub resonates at 12 GHz, uncomfortably close to a 16 Gb/s link's 8 GHz Nyquist frequency, and a 6 mm stub would sit right inside it. So fast interfaces push you to route on layers that minimize stub length, use back-drilling, or choose blind and buried vias (HDI). All of these raise cost, and all tend to increase the layer count or the process complexity.

## Via-in-pad and HDI: when the pitch gets small

For a 0.5 mm-pitch BGA, the channel formula in the technical section returns $n=0$: no trace fits between via pads. There is no escape route on the surface, so you must put the via in the pad (filled and capped, with a smaller laser-drilled microvia from one layer to the next). Each microvia layer pair is a lamination step, so cost rises with the number of build-up levels and the process is a vendor-specific capability, not a drawing option. This is the point where changing the part (a larger pitch, a module, or a package variant) is typically cheaper than designing the board. In an interview, saying "I'd check the fab's HDI capabilities and cost before committing to that package" is stronger than a bare layer count.

## Checking the choice before committing

1. Place the dense component in your CAD tool, fan out every ball with dog-bone vias (a short stub from the pad to a via diagonally between neighboring balls, with center offset $p\sqrt2/2$ = 0.57 mm for a 0.8 mm pitch), then try to route the escape on the planned layers with the fab's real minimum rules. If the router runs out of room at ball row 4, you need another layer or a finer rule.
2. Ask the fab for its capability sheet and a stack-up with real dielectric thicknesses. Re-run the impedance calculation on that stack-up, because dielectric height drives trace width and a thicker prepreg widens traces and uses up routing channels.
3. Check the vendor reference design and layout guide: for a processor or FPGA they typically state a minimum layer count and sometimes give a reference stack-up.
4. Run the design-rule check with the fab's rules loaded, before ordering.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The track utilization (30 to 50%), the average net length, and the cost impact of HDI features vary by design and by fab. Use them as planning numbers and replace them with measured values from a trial route.

## What to do about it

You can reduce layers by changing the component choice: a smaller-pin-count BGA, a package with 1 mm pitch instead of 0.5 mm, a memory with fewer chips, a lower-speed interface, or an SoM (system-on-module) that already embeds the dense part.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked "what would make you pick a 6-layer board?", say a specific part and a specific number: "A 0.8 mm-pitch BGA with 300 balls, one trace per channel, so about three or four signal layers for escape. I'd rather buy a module than lay that out on four layers."
:::

::: applications
- **Application processors (i.MX, Zynq, Raspberry Pi-class)**: 6–10 layers because of BGA escape and DDR.
- **FPGA dev boards**: 8–14+ layers with many rails and transceivers.
- **Smartphones**: 10–14+ layer HDI with microvias for 0.4 mm-pitch packages.
- **Network switches and servers**: 12–30+ layers with 25–100 Gb/s SerDes and backplane connectors.
- **RF modules**: 4–6 layers with RF on a thin top laminate and solid ground beneath.
:::

::: assumptions
- Standard fab capability: 0.1 mm (4 mil) trace/space, 0.2 mm mechanical drill, unless noted; finer rules or microvias change the count.
- A uniform ball map with a power/ground centre; real maps are irregular, and often a good design can escape on fewer layers than the simple formula suggests, or need more.
- "Typical layer counts" are experience ranges for commercial products, not standards.
- For SoM or module-based designs, the carrier board can have far fewer layers.
- Power-dominated boards can drive layer count through copper weight and current rather than signal density.
:::

::: followups
### How would you route a 0.5 mm-pitch BGA?
A trace can't fit between 0.3 mm pads at normal rules, so I'd use via-in-pad with filled and capped vias, laser-drilled microvias on the outer layers (HDI), and 0.075 mm trace/space if the fab supports it. The layer count is higher or the stack is a build-up (e.g. 1+N+1). Often the cheaper answer is to buy the part in a coarser-pitch package if one exists.

### Why does DDR need so many layers even though it's "just" 40 signals?
Because every byte lane needs controlled impedance, a reference plane beside each routing layer, and spacing for crosstalk, plus length matching with serpentines that takes area. Add multiple power rails and decoupling, and you end up with signal layers sandwiched between planes: typically 6–8 layers.

### Can you reduce layers by using a system-on-module?
Yes: the module integrates the processor and DDR and gives you a carrier connector at coarse pitch, so the carrier can be 2–4 layers. The cost is module price and size. This is a common trade for low-volume products.

### What if the layout guide says 8 layers and your budget is 6?
Check what the guide's minimum is based on. If it assumes the full DDR channel or all rails, perhaps a smaller configuration works: fewer memory chips, lower speed grade, or removing unused interfaces. Otherwise push for HDI features or the vendor's cost-reduced reference. I'd show the SI simulation or vendor sign-off, not guess.

### Scenario: your 4-layer prototype with a 0.8 mm BGA can't be routed. What are your options?
Count rows: 0.8 mm pitch allows one trace per channel, so four layers (two signal layers + two planes) escape only about four rows. If the array is deeper than that, either depopulate signals (use fewer pins), move some of the I/O to different pins in the FPGA/MCU pin assignment, add two layers, or use via-in-pad/microvias. I'd re-run the pin assignment first since it is free.
:::
