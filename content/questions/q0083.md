::: testing
Whether you can compare two devices **along the axes an engineer actually chooses on**, with numbers, and not just recite "BJT is current-controlled, MOSFET is voltage-controlled". The interviewer wants:

1. The control mechanism: base current into a forward-biased junction versus gate voltage across an insulator.
2. The transfer law and its consequence: exponential and high $g_m$ for the BJT, square-law (or exponential only in weak inversion) and lower $g_m$ for the MOSFET.
3. Switching trade-offs: $V_{CE(sat)}$ and base drive versus $R_{DS(on)}$ and gate charge.
4. Thermal behaviour (runaway versus self-limiting), matching, noise, input impedance, and which domain each wins in.
:::

::: why
Picking the device is the first real design decision in a switch, amplifier or driver. Most digital logic and power switching has gone to MOSFETs, but BJTs remain better for precise, low-noise, high-gain analogue stages, references and some RF. Interviewers use this question to see if you can reason from the physics ($I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$ versus $I_D\propto(V_{GS}-V_T)^2$) to design consequences. This question appears twice in the list ([[q0094]] is the MOSFET-section version).
:::

::: concepts
- **BJT operation**: base current controls collector current via an exponential $V_{BE}$ law. See [[q0085]].
- **MOSFET operation**: gate voltage creates an inversion channel; the gate draws no DC current. See [[q0096]].
- **Transconductance**: $g_m=I_C/V_T$ (BJT) versus $g_m=2I_D/V_{ov}$ (MOSFET, strong inversion). See [[q0088]].
- **On-state loss**: $V_{CE(sat)}$ (fixed voltage drop) versus $R_{DS(on)}$ (resistive drop).
- **Gate charge and base drive**: what it costs to switch each device. See [[q0093]].
:::

::: answer
"The biggest difference is how they're controlled. A BJT is current-controlled: a small base current into a forward-biased junction controls a larger collector current, with a gain beta around 100. A MOSFET is voltage-controlled: the gate voltage sets up a channel across an insulating oxide, so the gate draws essentially no DC current, just a pulse of charge when you switch it.

That leads to the practical differences. The BJT has a much higher transconductance for the same current, $I_C/V_T$, about 39 mS at 1 mA, because of its exponential law. It also matches well and has low noise, so it's better for precision analogue, references and low-noise amps. But it needs continuous base current, has a saturation voltage of around 0.2 V, and it has negative thermal feedback issues, because $V_{BE}$ drops as it heats, so parallel BJTs can run away.

The MOSFET has no gate current, a very high input impedance, and in the on-state it's a resistor, $R_{DS(on)}$, as low as a few milliohms, so for switching currents of amps its conduction loss is much lower. $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature, so MOSFETs share current in parallel. And it's the basis of CMOS, so it scales to billions of transistors. So: MOSFET for switching and digital, BJT for precision and high-gain analogue, and an IGBT if you need both for high voltage."
:::

::: technical
**Control and transfer law.**
- BJT: $I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$ (forward-active), $I_B=I_C/\beta$. $g_m=I_C/V_T$ (38.7 mS at 1 mA).
- MOSFET (long channel, saturation): $I_D=\tfrac12k\,(V_{GS}-V_T)^2$, $g_m=\sqrt{2kI_D}=2I_D/V_{ov}$. In weak inversion it is exponential with $g_m\approx I_D/(nV_T)$, $n\approx1.2$-$1.5$.

**Input.** BJT: $r_\pi=\beta/g_m$, base current continuous (about 10 µA for 1 mA at $\beta=100$). MOSFET: DC gate current ≈ 0 (nA or less); switching needs charge $Q_g$ and a gate driver.

**On-state loss.** BJT: $P=V_{CE(sat)}I_C+V_{BE}I_B$ (e.g. 0.3 V × 5 A = 1.5 W plus about 0.35 W base drive for forced $\beta=10$). MOSFET: $P=I^2R_{DS(on)}$ (10 mΩ at 5 A = 0.25 W). At low current ($<$ ~100 mA) a BJT's fixed drop can be competitive; at high current the MOSFET wins.

**Thermal.** BJT: $V_{BE}$ falls about 2 mV/K, so for a fixed $V_{BE}$ the current rises with temperature (positive feedback, runaway risk). MOSFET: above its zero-temperature-coefficient point, mobility decline raises $R_{DS(on)}$ with temperature, a negative feedback that helps paralleling (low-current, near-threshold operation can have the opposite sign).

**Speed.** BJT: limited by charge storage in saturation (storage time of order 100 ns-µs), $f_T=g_m/(2\pi(C_\pi+C_\mu))$. MOSFET: majority-carrier device with no minority storage, switching limited by gate charge and the Miller effect; typically faster in power switching.

**Matching/noise.** BJT $V_{BE}$ matching is excellent (sub-mV to mV for matched pairs) and flicker noise is low. MOSFETs have worse threshold matching (several mV) and higher 1/f noise (surface states), though modern designs mitigate this.

**Integration.** MOSFETs dominate VLSI: tiny, no DC input current, complementary pairs. BJTs appear in BiCMOS and analogue processes.
:::

::: short
"A BJT is current-controlled: base current into a forward-biased junction controls collector current, with a gain around 100. A MOSFET is voltage-controlled, with an insulated gate that draws essentially no DC current. The BJT has higher transconductance per milliamp, better matching and lower noise, so it suits precision analogue. The MOSFET has very low on-resistance, no base drive, positive temperature coefficient for current sharing, and scales to VLSI, so it dominates switching and digital."
:::

::: long
"The first difference is the control mechanism. In a BJT, a base current into the forward-biased base-emitter junction injects carriers across a thin base, and about beta times that base current flows from collector to emitter. The relationship is exponential, $I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$. In a MOSFET, a voltage on the gate sets up an electric field across the oxide that forms an inversion channel between source and drain. The gate is insulated, so there's no DC gate current, and the drain current follows roughly a square law above threshold.

The practical effects of that follow. For the same current, the BJT's transconductance is higher: $I_C/V_T$ gives about 39 mS at 1 mA, while a MOSFET at the same current with an overdrive of 0.3 V might be 6 mS. So the BJT gives more gain per milliamp, matches very well on-chip, and is quiet, which is why it's used in op-amp inputs, current mirrors and bandgap references. The cost is that the base takes current, which loads the source and burns power.

For switching, the on-state is different. A saturated BJT drops about 0.2 to 0.3 V, so at 5 A it dissipates around 1.5 watts plus the base drive. A MOSFET is a resistor: at 10 milliohms, 5 A is 0.25 watts. So MOSFETs win at higher currents. They also don't suffer from storage time, so they switch faster in power converters, and their resistance rises with temperature, so paralleling is easy, while BJTs can run away.

The MOSFET also integrates better: a CMOS pair draws no static current, so you can put billions of them on a chip. That's why digital is entirely MOSFET. Bipolar still wins where you need exponential precision, low noise or very high $g_m$, and IGBTs combine a MOSFET gate with a bipolar output for high-voltage, high-current switching."
:::

::: terms
- **Current-controlled vs voltage-controlled**: BJT input is a junction needing base current; MOSFET input is a capacitor needing no DC current.
- **Transconductance ($g_m$)**: output current change per input voltage change; $I_C/V_T$ for BJT.
- **$R_{DS(on)}$**: on-resistance of a MOSFET channel.
- **$V_{CE(sat)}$**: collector-emitter voltage of a saturated BJT.
- **Gate charge ($Q_g$)**: charge needed to turn on a MOSFET; sets drive loss and switching time.
- **Storage time**: delay to remove excess minority carriers when a saturated BJT turns off.
- **Thermal runaway**: positive feedback between temperature and current (BJT).
- **Zero-temperature-coefficient (ZTC) point**: gate bias where MOSFET current does not change with temperature.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "BJT is current-controlled, MOSFET is voltage-controlled."

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the equations ($I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$ vs $I_D=\tfrac12k(V_{GS}-V_T)^2$) and what they imply for $g_m$ with numbers (38.7 mS vs about 6 mS at 1 mA).
- Compares conduction loss with a worked example (BJT $V_{CE(sat)}\cdot I$ + base drive vs MOSFET $I^2R_{DS(on)}$).
- Covers thermal behaviour: BJT runaway vs MOSFET self-limiting current sharing, including the ZTC caveat.
- Mentions speed (storage time vs gate charge), noise, matching, input impedance and integration.
- States where each wins: MOSFET for digital and power switching, BJT for precision analogue, references, low noise, RF SiGe, and mentions IGBT as a hybrid.
- Notes the regimes where the rule breaks (MOSFET in weak inversion looks exponential; tiny loads favour a BJT).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"MOSFETs have infinite input impedance."** DC gate current is near zero, but the gate is a capacitance (nF) that needs charge on every switching edge; AC input impedance is finite.
- **"BJTs are obsolete."** They are still the best choice for many analogue functions: precision references, low-noise amplifiers, matched pairs, RF SiGe, and cheap small switches.
- **"A MOSFET is always the better switch."** At low currents or for tiny loads, a BJT with a small base resistor may be cheaper and simpler, and at very high voltage the IGBT (bipolar output) wins.
- **Ignoring base-current power.** At forced $\beta=10$ the base drive is 10% of load current and costs $V_{BE}\cdot I_B$.
- **Claiming MOSFETs never run away.** Below the ZTC point (near threshold) a MOSFET's current rises with temperature, and large paralleled devices in linear mode can show hot spots.
- **Using the square law for tiny currents.** In weak inversion, MOSFET current is exponential; the quadratic approximation overstates or understates $g_m$.
:::

::: deep
## Two different control mechanisms

**BJT.** A forward-biased base-emitter junction injects carriers into a thin base; most reach the collector. The collector current depends exponentially on $V_{BE}$:
$$I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T},\qquad I_B=\frac{I_C}{\beta}$$
Control is by $V_{BE}$ or equivalently $I_B$; the input looks like a diode, drawing a base current continuously.

**MOSFET.** A gate voltage above threshold inverts the surface under the oxide, forming a conducting channel. In saturation:
$$I_D=\tfrac12\mu C_{ox}\frac WL(V_{GS}-V_T)^2(1+\lambda V_{DS})$$
The gate is insulated by oxide so the DC gate current is ideally zero.

See [[q0085]] (BJT operation) and [[q0096]] (MOSFET operation).

The BJT line is a straight line of slope 1 ($g_m\propto I_C$); the MOSFET follows the same slope at very low current (weak inversion, with a ratio $1/n$ lower) then bends over to a square-root law ($g_m\propto\sqrt{I_D}$) in strong inversion. ## Thermal behaviour

- **BJT:** $V_{BE}$ falls by about 2 mV/K at constant current, so at fixed base voltage $I_C$ rises with temperature and the device heats more: positive feedback. Paralleled BJTs need emitter resistors (ballasting). A single BJT in an amplifier needs emitter degeneration and thermally stable bias.
- **MOSFET:** in strong inversion mobility decreases with temperature so $R_{DS(on)}$ rises roughly 0.4-0.7 %/K for silicon. The hotter device carries less current: self-balancing. Below the ZTC point (roughly where the gate overdrive is small), the threshold decrease dominates and current rises with temperature, so linear-mode operation at low $V_{GS}$ can create hot spots (a known issue for MOSFETs in linear/hot-swap applications).

## Noise, matching and integration

| Axis | BJT | MOSFET |
|---|---|---|
| Input current | Base current (µA), shot noise | ~0 DC; gate leakage pA-nA |
| $1/f$ noise | Low | Higher (surface states), improved by large area |
| Matching | $V_{BE}$ mismatch ~sub-mV | $V_T$ mismatch several mV (shrinks with area) |
| Input impedance | $r_\pi=\beta/g_m$ (kΩ-100 kΩ) | Very high (MΩ+), capacitive |
| Speed (analogue) | $f_T$ up to hundreds of GHz (SiGe) | $f_T$ hundreds of GHz in advanced CMOS |
| Static power in logic | High (BJT logic burns current) | Near zero in CMOS |
| Integration | Larger, analogue/BiCMOS | Dominant in VLSI |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Mismatch, noise and $R_{DS(on)}$ figures vary by process, device size and bias; the values given are typical orders of magnitude. For a real design use the datasheet or PDK numbers.

## Where each wins

- **MOSFET:** logic and memory (CMOS), power switching above about 1 A, load switches, motor drives, DC-DC converters, anything needing near-zero DC input current.
- **BJT:** precision references, low-noise preamplifiers, current mirrors and matched pairs, logarithmic converters, RF/SiGe, cheap small low-side switches.
- **IGBT:** combines a MOSFET input with a bipolar output; used at hundreds of volts and amps where MOSFET $R_{DS(on)}$ grows too large.

::: applications
- **CMOS microprocessors and memories**: billions of MOSFETs, essentially no static input current.
- **Buck/boost converters and motor drives**: low-$R_{DS(on)}$ power MOSFETs for switching.
- **Precision references and sensors**: BJT $V_{BE}$-based bandgap and temperature sensors in PMICs and MCUs.
- **Low-noise audio and sensor preamplifiers**: BJT input stages, though JFET/CMOS inputs are used for very high source impedance.
- **Op-amp design**: BJT input pairs for low voltage noise (e.g. precision op-amps), MOS or JFET input for low bias current.
- **High-voltage inverters**: IGBTs in EV drives and industrial inverters.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Silicon devices at room temperature**, discrete or IC, long-channel textbook models for the equations; short-channel MOSFETs deviate (velocity saturation, linear $I_D$ vs $V_{ov}$).
- **Forced $\beta=10$** for the BJT switch comparison, a conservative convention; a lower $\beta_{forced}$ requirement (e.g. 20) reduces drive power but risks soft saturation.
- **$R_{DS(on)}=10$ mΩ** is representative of a modern low-voltage power MOSFET; higher-voltage devices have much higher resistance (e.g. 100 mΩ-1 Ω at 600 V in silicon).
- **"Better" depends on the criterion**: the answer changes between low current, high current, high voltage, low noise, and integration.
- **Alternative answers**: a minimal answer (current- vs voltage-controlled, input impedance, switching loss) is acceptable; the numbers and thermal arguments show depth.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a BJT have higher transconductance than a MOSFET at the same current?
Because the BJT's current is exponential in $V_{BE}$ with a fixed slope $I_C/V_T$, set only by temperature. A MOSFET in strong inversion needs a gate overdrive $V_{ov}$ to carry the current, and its $g_m=2I_D/V_{ov}$; with $V_{ov}\approx0.2$-$0.5$ V that gives 4-10x $I_D$ in S versus the BJT's $39\ I_C$. Only in weak inversion does the MOSFET approach the BJT, with $g_m=I_D/(nV_T)$ and $n>1$.

### When would you pick a BJT over a MOSFET to switch a load?
When the load is small (say below 100-200 mA), the drive voltage is awkward, cost and simplicity matter, and the extra base current is acceptable. For example, a 20 mA LED from a 5 V logic pin: a single NPN and a base resistor is cheap and robust. Above an amp, or when the drive current budget is tight, I'd choose a MOSFET.

### Can MOSFETs be paralleled more easily than BJTs? Why?
Yes, in switching mode. $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature, so a device that gets hotter carries less current, equalising sharing. A BJT's $V_{BE}$ drops with temperature, so the hotter transistor hogs current and heats more (runaway). BJTs need emitter ballast resistors. Caveat: MOSFETs in linear mode near their ZTC point can also hot-spot.

### How does the gate drive requirement differ?
A BJT needs a continuous base current (about $I_C/10$ for saturation), so the driver burns power in the base resistor. A MOSFET needs charge $Q_g$ delivered quickly at each switching edge, with a gate-drive average power $Q_gV_{GS}f_{sw}$: for $Q_g=20$ nC, $V_{GS}=10$ V and $f_{sw}=500$ kHz that is 0.1 W, from a driver that can source an amp or so peak for fast edges. A MOSFET draws zero DC.

### What is an IGBT and why does it exist?
It's a MOSFET gate driving a bipolar (PNP) output transistor: you get the voltage-controlled, easily-driven input of a MOSFET and the low on-state voltage drop of a bipolar at high voltage (carrier injection reduces resistance). Hence it dominates above ~600 V and tens to hundreds of amps, with a fixed ~1-2 V drop and a slower turn-off from stored charge ("current tail").

### Scenario: you need a low-noise preamp for a 1 kΩ-source microphone. BJT or MOSFET input, and why?
For a 1 kΩ source, a BJT input is typically the better choice. Its voltage noise $e_n\approx\sqrt{4kT(r_b+r_e/2)}$ can be below 1 nV/√Hz at a few mA, and the base current noise is irrelevant at 1 kΩ. A MOSFET or JFET would be preferred for source impedances of hundreds of kΩ to MΩ, where the BJT's base current noise ($\sqrt{2qI_B}\cdot R_S$) dominates. Choose the device so that its noise matches the source impedance.
