::: testing
Whether you can explain **transistor action** physically, not just say "a small current controls a big current". The interviewer wants the chain of cause and effect:

1. The base-emitter junction is forward biased, so the emitter injects electrons into the base (NPN).
2. The base is so thin that most of those electrons diffuse across without recombining.
3. The reverse-biased base-collector junction's field sweeps them into the collector, so collector current is set by the *injection*, not by the collector voltage.
4. The small base current supplies the carriers that are lost (recombination and hole back-injection), which is why $I_C=\beta I_B$ with $\beta\approx100$.
5. The exponential $I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$ follows from the diode physics.
:::

::: why
Everything in BJT circuits (biasing, gain, saturation, Early effect, temperature drift, $f_T$, thermal runaway) falls out of the carrier-flow picture. A candidate who understands it can answer follow-ups about why the base is thin, why the emitter is heavily doped, why the collector current barely depends on $V_{CE}$, and why $V_{BE}$ falls with temperature, instead of memorising rules. It's also the gateway to MOSFETs, where the same "inject, transport, collect" story is replaced by a gate-controlled channel ([[q0096]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Forward-biased PN junction**: majority carriers cross the lowered barrier and become minority carriers on the other side. See [[q0072]].
- **Depletion region and field**: a reverse-biased junction has a strong field that sweeps minority carriers across. See [[q0063]].
- **Minority-carrier diffusion**: injected carriers diffuse down a concentration gradient, with a diffusion length set by recombination. See [[q0069]].
- **Terminals and doping asymmetry**: heavy emitter, thin base, light collector. See [[q0081]].
- **Current gain**: base current replaces the carriers lost, so $I_C=\beta I_B$. See [[q0088]].
:::

::: answer
"Take an NPN. It's an N-type emitter, a very thin P-type base, and an N-type collector. In normal operation I forward-bias the base-emitter junction, about 0.7 V, and reverse-bias the base-collector junction.

The forward bias lowers the barrier at the emitter junction, so electrons from the heavily doped emitter flow into the base. In the base they're minority carriers. Because the base is so thin, around a tenth to a micron, most of them diffuse across it before they recombine with holes. When they reach the edge of the base-collector depletion region, the strong field there, which comes from the reverse bias, sweeps them into the collector. That electron flow from emitter to collector is the collector current, and it depends on how many electrons the emitter injects, which is set by $V_{BE}$, not on $V_{CE}$. That's why $I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$ and why the output curves are flat.

A small fraction of the carriers don't make it. A few electrons recombine with holes in the base, and some holes from the base are injected back into the emitter. The base current is what resupplies those holes. Since the base is thin and the emitter heavily doped, both losses are small, around one percent, so $I_B$ is about $I_C/100$. That's the current gain. So the transistor is basically a controlled flow of carriers from emitter to collector, with the base as a thin valve whose control signal is the forward bias on the junction."
:::

::: technical
**Biasing (NPN, forward-active).** $V_{BE}\approx0.65$-$0.7$ V (forward), $V_{BC}<0$ (reverse).

**Injection.** Electrons injected into the base: $n_p(0)=n_{p0}e^{V_{BE}/V_T}$ with $n_{p0}=n_i^2/N_{AB}$ the equilibrium minority density. At the collector edge of the base, the reverse-biased junction holds $n_p(W_B)\approx0$.

**Diffusion across the base.** For $W_B\ll L_n$ the profile is linear: $n_p(x)=n_p(0)(1-x/W_B)$, so the diffusion current density is $J_n=qD_n\,\frac{n_p(0)}{W_B}$. Hence
$$I_C=\frac{qAD_nn_i^2}{N_{AB}W_B}\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T}=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$$
$I_S\propto A\,n_i^2/(N_{AB}W_B)$ is independent of $V_{CE}$ (ideal), which explains flat output curves; $n_i^2$ is why $I_S$ and $V_{BE}$ depend strongly on temperature.

**Base current components.** (1) Holes injected from base into emitter: $I_{B1}/I_C\approx\frac{D_pW_BN_{AB}}{D_nW_EN_{DE}}$. (2) Recombination in the base: $I_{B2}/I_C\approx\frac{W_B^2}{2L_n^2}$. Then
$$\frac1\beta\approx\frac{D_pW_BN_{AB}}{D_nW_EN_{DE}}+\frac{W_B^2}{2L_n^2}$$
Also recombination in the base-emitter depletion region (important at low current) and surface recombination.

**Worked example (idealised).** $A=10^{-4}$ cm², $N_{AB}=10^{17}$, $N_{DE}=10^{19}$ cm⁻³, $W_B=0.5\ \mu$m, $W_E=1\ \mu$m, $D_n=20$, $D_p=2.6$ cm²/s, $\tau_n=1\ \mu$s: $I_S=6.4\times10^{-15}$ A; back-injection term $6.5\times10^{-4}$, base-recombination term $6.3\times10^{-5}$, giving $\beta\approx1400$. Real small-signal parts are 100-300 because of bandgap narrowing in the emitter, depletion-region recombination, and shorter lifetimes.

**Transit time.** $\tau_B=\frac{W_B^2}{2D_n}=62$ ps for the example, bounding $f_T\lesssim1/(2\pi\tau_B)\approx2.5$ GHz from the base alone.
:::

::: short
"In an NPN the forward-biased base-emitter junction injects electrons from the heavily doped emitter into the thin base. Most diffuse across before recombining, and the field of the reverse-biased base-collector junction sweeps them into the collector. That gives a collector current set by $V_{BE}$, $I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$, nearly independent of $V_{CE}$. A small base current supplies the holes lost to recombination and back-injection, so $I_B\approx I_C/\beta$ with $\beta\sim100$."
:::

::: long
"Let me walk through an NPN: N-type emitter, a thin P-type base, N-type collector. For normal amplifying operation, the base-emitter junction is forward biased, around 0.7 V, and the base-collector junction is reverse biased.

Start at the emitter. The forward bias lowers the barrier, so electrons from the emitter, which is heavily doped so there are a lot of them, flow into the base. In the base they're minority carriers, so there's a high concentration at the emitter edge and almost none at the collector edge, because the reverse-biased collector junction sucks them away. That concentration gradient makes them diffuse across the base. The base is very thin, about a tenth of a micron to a micron, much shorter than the diffusion length, so almost all of them get across.

When they reach the base-collector depletion region, they meet a strong electric field in the direction that carries electrons into the collector, so they're swept in. That's the collector current. Notice that the collector current is set by how many electrons are injected at the emitter. That's why it follows the diode law, $I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$, and why raising the collector voltage barely changes it. That's the flat part of the output curves.

Now the base current. Two things need current to flow into the base. A few electrons recombine with holes while crossing the base, and the emitter junction also injects a few holes backwards into the emitter. Each of those needs a hole supplied from the base terminal. Because the emitter is heavily doped, there are very few holes injected backward, and because the base is thin there's very little recombination, so the base current is only about a hundredth of the collector current. That ratio is beta.

So the whole picture is a controlled flow of electrons from emitter to collector, where the base voltage sets the injection and the base current is just the leakage in that process. The same picture explains the other properties: $V_{BE}$ falls about 2 mV per degree because $n_i^2$ rises, the Early effect is a narrower base at higher $V_{CB}$, and the speed limit is the base transit time."
:::

::: terms
- **Injection**: flow of carriers across a forward-biased junction into a region where they are minority carriers.
- **Minority-carrier diffusion**: movement down a concentration gradient, with the profile set by recombination.
- **Base transit time ($\tau_B$)**: average time for carriers to cross the base, $W_B^2/(2D_n)$.
- **Injection efficiency ($\gamma$)**: fraction of emitter current carried by carriers injected into the base.
- **Base transport factor ($\alpha_T$)**: fraction of injected carriers that reach the collector.
- **Common-base gain ($\alpha$)**: $I_C/I_E=\gamma\alpha_T$, close to 1; $\beta=\alpha/(1-\alpha)$.
- **Saturation current ($I_S$)**: scale current in $I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$; $\propto n_i^2$.
- **Early effect**: base narrowing as $V_{CB}$ rises, increasing $I_C$ slightly.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a small base current controls a larger collector current through the junctions."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Tells the carrier-flow story (inject, diffuse, collect) with the right bias polarities.
- Explains *why* the collector current is independent of $V_{CE}$ and exponential in $V_{BE}$, and derives $I_S$ from the linear base profile.
- Identifies the two base-current components (hole back-injection, base recombination) and ties $\beta$ to doping ratio and $W_B/L_n$.
- Explains the Early effect, $V_{BE}$ temperature coefficient and $f_T$ limit from the same picture.
- Gives numbers (e.g. $I_S\sim10^{-15}$-$10^{-14}$ A, $W_B\sim0.1$-$1\ \mu$m, $\beta\sim100$, 60 mV/decade).
- Notes the PNP analogue (holes) and that the BJT is both a majority and minority carrier device only in terms of how carriers are injected and collected.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The base current is amplified."** The base current isn't "converted"; it supplies the carriers lost in recombination. The collector current is set by $V_{BE}$ through injection, and $\beta$ is just the ratio.
- **"Electrons pass through the base by drift in a field."** In a uniformly doped base they cross by *diffusion*; the collector field only acts at the edge of the base.
- **"The collector voltage controls the collector current."** In forward-active it hardly does (flat curves); only the Early effect gives a weak dependence.
- **Treating a BJT as two diodes.** The thin shared base is what enables transport; separate diodes recombine the carriers.
- **Thinking the base-collector junction conducts in normal operation.** It's reverse biased; carriers flow through it by being swept in, not injected.
- **Forgetting the PNP uses holes.** The same analysis applies with polarities and carriers reversed, and the lower hole mobility makes PNPs slower.
:::

## Setting the stage: three layers, two junctions

An NPN: N+ emitter (about $10^{19}$ cm⁻³), thin P base ($10^{17}$, 0.1-1 µm), N- collector ($10^{15}$-$10^{16}$). Bias in forward-active: emitter grounded, $V_{BE}=0.7$ V, collector at a few volts positive.

## Step 1: injection

The forward-biased emitter junction obeys the diode relation. The excess minority electron density at the base edge of the junction is
$$n_p(0)=n_{p0}\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T},\qquad n_{p0}=\frac{n_i^2}{N_{AB}}$$
With $n_i=10^{10}$, $N_{AB}=10^{17}$: $n_{p0}=10^{3}$ cm⁻³. At $V_{BE}=0.7$ V, $e^{0.7/0.02585}=5.75\times10^{11}$, so $n_p(0)=5.75\times10^{14}$ cm⁻³. That's over eleven orders of magnitude above equilibrium, but still 200x below the base doping ($10^{17}$): low-level injection holds.

## Step 2: diffusion through the thin base

At the collector edge the reverse bias removes electrons as soon as they arrive: $n_p(W_B)\approx0$. Between the two edges the concentration falls almost linearly, since $W_B\ll L_n$ (little recombination):

Collector current:
$$I_C=qAD_n\frac{n_p(0)}{W_B}=\underbrace{\frac{qAD_nn_i^2}{N_{AB}W_B}}_{I_S}e^{V_{BE}/V_T}$$
Plugging in $A=10^{-4}$ cm², $D_n=20$ cm²/s, $W_B=0.5\ \mu$m: $I_S=6.4\times10^{-15}$ A and $I_C(0.7\text{ V})=3.7$ mA. This agrees with the measured order of magnitude for small-signal BJTs ($I_S\sim10^{-15}$-$10^{-14}$ A).

## Step 3: collection

At the base-collector junction the depletion region has a strong field pointing from collector to base, which carries electrons toward the collector. Any electron that reaches the base edge of this region is swept across. The rate of arrival is set by the diffusion current, not by the field magnitude, which is why the collector voltage has almost no effect (hence a good current source). Raising $V_{CB}$ widens the depletion region and reduces $W_B$, which gives the Early effect, a slight increase in $I_C$.

## Step 4: where the base current goes

For each electron that crosses to the collector, the base terminal must supply a little extra charge:

1. **Hole back-injection into the emitter.** The forward bias also injects holes from base to emitter. Their density is much smaller because the emitter is $10^{19}$ versus $10^{17}$:
$$\frac{I_{B1}}{I_C}=\frac{D_pW_BN_{AB}}{D_nW_EN_{DE}}=\frac{2.6\times5\times10^{-5}\times10^{17}}{20\times10^{-4}\times10^{19}}=6.5\times10^{-4}$$
2. **Recombination in the base.** Fraction recombining: $\frac{W_B^2}{2L_n^2}$ with $L_n=\sqrt{D_n\tau_n}=45\ \mu$m: $=\frac{(0.5)^2}{2\times45^2}=6.2\times10^{-5}$.

So $1/\beta\approx7.1\times10^{-4}$ and $\beta\approx1400$ in this idealised design. The real $\beta$ of a 2N3904-type part is 100-300 because heavy emitter doping narrows the bandgap (more hole injection), recombination in the depletion regions and at the surface adds base current, and lifetimes are shorter. The design lesson is clear: raise $\beta$ by a heavier emitter, thinner base and longer lifetime; lower it by the reverse.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The numbers in this worked example are idealised and ignore bandgap narrowing, base grading, depletion-region recombination and high-injection effects. They show how the pieces scale, not what a specific device does.

## Step 5: consequences you can now derive

- **Exponential law and 60 mV/decade**: from the Boltzmann factor in step 1.
- **$V_{BE}$ temperature coefficient**: $I_S\propto n_i^2\propto T^{3}e^{-E_g/kT}$, so at constant $I_C$ $V_{BE}$ falls about 2 mV/K.
- **Early effect**: collector-base depletion widening reduces $W_B$, raising $I_S$.
- **Saturation**: when $V_{CE}$ falls below $V_{BE}$ the collector junction becomes forward biased and stops collecting all carriers; excess charge builds up in the base. See [[q0087]].
- **Speed**: base transit time $\tau_B=W_B^2/(2D_n)=62$ ps here (about 2.5 GHz bound from the base alone); real RF transistors reduce $W_B$ to tens of nm and use graded or SiGe bases to reach $f_T$ of hundreds of GHz.
- **PNP**: the same with holes injected into an N base; hole mobility is lower so transit time is longer. See [[q0086]].

::: applications
- **Amplifiers (common-emitter, differential pairs)**: transistor action converts a small $V_{BE}$ change into a large collector current change.
- **Switches and drivers**: saturation and cutoff operation of the same mechanism.
- **Bandgap references**: $V_{BE}$ and $\Delta V_{BE}$ derived directly from the exponential law and $I_S\propto n_i^2$.
- **Current mirrors**: matched $I_S$ gives matched currents.
- **Phototransistors**: photons generating carriers in the base region act like base current.
- **High-speed SiGe HBTs**: thinner, graded bases to cut the base transit time.
:::

::: assumptions
- **NPN, forward-active, low-level injection, uniform base doping and short base** ($W_B\ll L_n$) for the linear profile.
- **Idealised $\beta$ formula** from emitter injection efficiency and base recombination only; real devices have additional recombination paths, bandgap narrowing and graded bases.
- **1-D, steady state, room temperature**; no high-frequency or transient behaviour, no Kirk effect or high-level injection.
- **Silicon**; a SiGe or GaAs HBT uses a wider-gap emitter or a graded-gap base to improve injection efficiency.
- **Alternative answer**: a short answer is "forward-biased emitter injects carriers into a thin base which are swept into the collector, with a small base current for recombination." Give the equations if the interviewer wants depth.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the collector current barely depend on $V_{CE}$?
Because collection is nearly 100% efficient: any electron that reaches the edge of the collector depletion region is swept in, whatever the field magnitude. The current is limited by how many carriers the emitter injects, set by $V_{BE}$. $V_{CE}$ only slightly widens the depletion region and shortens the base (Early effect). That's why the BJT is a good current source and why $r_o=V_A/I_C$ is large.

### Why does making the base thinner increase beta?
Both base-current terms shrink. The recombination loss fraction $W_B^2/2L_n^2$ falls with the square of the width, and the hole back-injection ratio $\frac{D_pW_BN_{AB}}{D_nW_EN_{DE}}$ is proportional to $W_B$, so it falls too. Halving $W_B$ halves the second term and quarters the first, so $\beta$ roughly doubles or more. The price is higher base resistance, a lower punch-through voltage (the collector depletion region can reach the emitter) and a stronger Early effect.

### Why is the emitter more heavily doped than the base?
To make the emitter injection efficiency close to 1. The ratio of hole to electron injection current across the emitter-base junction scales as $N_{AB}/N_{DE}$ (with widths and diffusivities). With $N_{DE}/N_{AB}=100$, hole injection is about 1% of the electron current if other factors are equal; with a thin base it is smaller still. Without it, a large part of the base current would go to useless hole injection and $\beta$ would collapse.

### What happens to the picture in saturation?
When $V_{CE}$ drops below $V_{BE}$ the base-collector junction also becomes forward biased. The collector now injects electrons back into the base, so the concentration at the collector edge of the base stops being zero. The gradient, and therefore $I_C$, falls, and excess minority charge piles up in the base. That stored charge must be removed on turn-off, causing the storage delay. See [[q0087]].

### Scenario: a transistor's beta is 300 at 25 °C but only 120 at -40 °C. Why does the temperature matter physically?
Beta depends on injection efficiency and on recombination. A major contributor to the temperature dependence is bandgap narrowing in the heavily doped emitter, which raises the hole back-injection by a factor of about $e^{\Delta E_g/kT}$; this factor grows as temperature falls, so injection efficiency and $\beta$ drop in the cold. Recombination in the emitter-base depletion region (a current with a weaker dependence on $V_{BE}$) also matters more at low temperature. The typical result is a $\beta$ that rises with temperature, often quoted at roughly +0.5 to +1 %/K for small-signal parts. Design with margin using the datasheet's $h_{FE}$ vs temperature curves.

### How does a PNP work?
Same structure with doping types reversed. The emitter is P+, the base N, the collector P. Forward bias injects holes from the emitter into the base; they diffuse through and are swept into the collector, so conventional current enters the emitter and exits the collector and base. Because hole mobility is lower than electron mobility, the transit time is longer, so PNPs are slower with lower $f_T$. See [[q0086]].
:::
