::: basics
### In plain words
A via is a small plated hole through a circuit board that connects copper on one layer to copper on another. A board has several layers, and a trace can only run flat along one of them. A via lets a signal, a power rail or heat move up or down between layers. It is not a perfect wire, though. It has a little resistance, inductance and capacitance.

### Picture it
Think of an elevator in an office building. Each floor is a layer, and the hallways are traces. To get from one floor to another you need the elevator shaft, which is the via. The shaft runs through every floor, even if you only stop at two, so it takes space on all of them. And the elevator is not instant: at high speeds it holds up traffic.

### Words you need
- **Layer**: one flat sheet of copper in the board's stack. Boards often have four or more.
- **Trace**: a copper line that carries a signal along one layer.
- **Barrel**: the copper-plated wall of the hole, like a thin copper tube.
- **Pad**: a copper ring on a layer that the barrel connects to.
- **Inductance**: how strongly a wire resists fast changes in current. 1 nH is a billionth of a henry.

### The simplest example
Take a common via: a 0.3 mm hole through a 1.6 mm board. Its resistance is only about 1.3 mΩ, so 1 A through it loses about 1.3 mV. Its inductance is about 1.3 nH. At slow speeds you can ignore it. At 1 GHz the same 1.3 nH acts like about 8 Ω, which is large. That is why fast designs count their vias and put a ground via next to each signal via.

That is the whole idea: a via connects layers, but it is a small circuit element, not a free wire. The rest of this page covers a via's parts, uses, and current limits.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand a PCB as a **3-D stack of copper layers**, not a flat drawing. A via is the one structure that moves a signal, a current or heat through that stack. The interviewer wants to hear four things:

1. A clear definition: a **plated hole** that electrically connects copper on different layers.
2. Why a board needs them (routing in 3-D, reaching planes, connecting a component's pins to inner layers, moving heat).
3. That you know a via is **not an ideal wire**. It has resistance, inductance, capacitance and a current limit.
4. That you know the basic anatomy: barrel, pad, annular ring, antipad.
:::

::: why
Almost every board with more than one layer lives or dies by its vias. Layer changes set how a power rail reaches a chip, how a return current finds its way home, how well a thermal pad sheds heat, and how many boards the fab ships without cracked barrels. A candidate who says "a via is a hole that connects layers" gets half credit. The other half is knowing that every via is a small inductor, a small capacitor and a small resistor, and that this starts to matter once edges get fast or currents get large. It's also the vocabulary for the next ten questions in this section: types, failure modes, via-in-pad, plane perforation.
:::

::: concepts
- **PCB stack-up**: alternating copper and dielectric (core and prepreg) layers, laminated together. See [[q0474]].
- **Plated through hole (PTH)**: a drilled hole whose wall is copper-plated so it conducts between layers.
- **Pad, annular ring, antipad**: the copper landing ring around the hole, the copper that remains around the drilled hole, and the clearance cut in planes the barrel must not touch.
- **Via as a parasitic element**: resistance, inductance and capacitance that scale with barrel length and size. See [[q0463]].
- **Current and thermal capacity**: a barrel is a thin copper tube, and its cross-section limits both current and heat flow.
:::

::: answer
"A via is a small plated hole through the board that connects copper on one layer to copper on another. The fab drills the hole, then plates copper onto the wall, so the wall becomes a tiny copper tube, called the barrel, that joins pads on the layers it passes through.

We need them because a multilayer board is a 3-D structure and traces can only run in 2-D on each layer. When two traces would have to cross, or when a chip pin on the top has to get to a ground plane on layer two, or a power rail has to reach a pin from an inner plane, the signal drops through a via. They also carry heat from a hot component to a copper plane on the other side.

The thing I'd add is that a via isn't a perfect wire. A standard 0.3 mm via through a 1.6 mm board is about a milliohm of resistance, but around 1 nH of inductance and a few tenths of a picofarad of capacitance. At low frequency you ignore it. At a gigahertz that inductance is several ohms, and you have to think about it. That's why high-speed designers count vias and put a ground via next to every signal via."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** A via (vertical interconnect access) is a hole drilled through one or more dielectric layers and plated with copper so that it conducts between copper features on different layers.

**Anatomy.**
- **Barrel**: the plated wall, typically on the order of 20–25 µm of copper (the exact minimum depends on the IPC class and fab; see the uncertainty note below).
- **Pads (lands)**: copper rings on each connected layer that the barrel lands on.
- **Annular ring**: copper left around the drilled hole on a pad, $(D_{pad}-D_{drill})/2$. It absorbs drill wander.
- **Antipad (clearance)**: the circular void cut in planes that the via passes through but must not connect to.
- **Aspect ratio**: board thickness divided by drill diameter. A 1.6 mm board with a 0.3 mm drill is about 5.3:1. Fabs limit this because plating solution has to circulate inside the hole.

**Why vias exist.** (1) Routing: crossing traces need a second layer. (2) Distribution: power and ground planes are inner layers, so components reach them through vias. (3) Component access: BGAs and fine-pitch parts are escape-routed by fanning out through vias. (4) Thermal: vias carry heat from a pad to planes or the opposite side. (5) Return current: ground vias give return current a short path from one reference plane to the next.

**Electrical model.** Barrel DC resistance is that of a thin tube, $R = \rho L / A$ with $A=\frac{\pi}{4}\left[D^2-(D-2t)^2\right]$. For $D=0.3$ mm, $t=25$ µm, $L=1.6$ mm, $\rho=1.72\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\text{m}$: $A\approx 0.0216\ \text{mm}^2$ and $R\approx 1.3\ \text{m}\Omega$. Inductance from the commonly used Johnson & Graham approximation is $L \approx 5.08\,h\left[\ln(4h/d)+1\right]$ nH, with $h$ the via length and $d$ the diameter in inches. For the same via that is about 1.3 nH. Capacitance to the surrounding planes is $C\approx \frac{1.41\,\varepsilon_r T D_1}{D_2-D_1}$ pF (dimensions in inches), which gives about 0.5 pF for a 0.6 mm pad in a 1.1 mm antipad.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The inductance and capacitance formulas are first-order approximations and the exact results depend on the return-path geometry. Plating thickness requirements depend on the IPC class and the fab's process (commonly quoted as an average of roughly 20 µm for Class 2 and 25 µm for Class 3). Check your fab's capability sheet.

**Current and heat.** The barrel cross-section is only about 0.02 mm², so a single 0.3 mm via is roughly a 0.8 A conductor for a 10 °C rise using the IPC-2221 internal-layer curve, which is conservative. Heat is limited the same way: one via is about 190 K/W along its length.
:::

::: short
"A via is a plated hole that electrically connects copper on different layers of a PCB. We need them because traces only run in 2-D on each layer, so signals have to drop through the stack to cross each other, reach power and ground planes, escape from BGAs, and move heat. A via isn't ideal: a typical one is about a milliohm, around 1 nH and a few tenths of a picofarad, so at high frequency I treat it as a small inductor and keep return paths close."
:::

::: long
"A via is a hole that's drilled through the board and then plated with copper on the inside wall, so the wall becomes a thin copper tube, the barrel, that connects the copper pads on every layer it touches. Around the hole there's a pad with an annular ring, the copper that survives drill wander, and on any plane the via passes through but shouldn't touch, there's a clearance void called an antipad.

Why do we need them? A multilayer board is a stack of 2-D layers. Within one layer traces can't cross, so a via lets one trace dive to another layer and cross underneath. Power and ground are mostly on inner planes, and the only way a component pin on the top gets there is a via. Fine-pitch parts like BGAs are escaped by dropping vias beside each ball. Vias also conduct heat: under a hot QFN pad you put an array of them to carry heat to a ground plane. And for high-speed signals, ground vias next to signal vias give return current a short route when the signal changes layers.

The important engineering point is that a via is a real component. A 0.3 mm via through a 1.6 mm board is about 1.3 milliohms, about 1.3 nanohenries, and about half a picofarad. At DC that's nothing, but at 1 GHz 1.3 nH is about 8 ohms of reactance, so it disturbs a 50 ohm line. Current is limited too: one such via carries on the order of an amp, so a 5 A rail needs several in parallel.

So my rule is: use vias freely for slow signals and power, but for fast signals, minimise layer changes, pair every signal via with a nearby ground via, and for power use arrays."
:::

::: terms
- **Via**: a plated hole connecting copper on different layers.
- **Barrel**: the copper-plated wall inside the hole. It carries the current.
- **Pad / land**: the copper ring on a layer that the barrel connects to.
- **Annular ring**: the copper remaining between the drilled hole edge and the pad edge, $(D_{pad}-D_{drill})/2$.
- **Antipad**: the clearance hole cut in a plane so a via passes through without shorting to it.
- **Aspect ratio**: board (or dielectric) thickness divided by drill diameter. It limits how small a hole the fab can plate reliably.
- **Drill size vs finished hole size**: the finished hole is smaller than the drill bit by about twice the plating thickness.
- **Stub**: the unused part of a barrel beyond the layer where the signal exits.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a hole that connects layers" and "so we can route on multiple layers".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names the anatomy (barrel, pad, annular ring, antipad) and says the barrel is a thin plated tube, not a solid wire.
- Gives the reasons in a clear list: crossing, planes, escape routing, thermal, return path.
- Quotes rough numbers for one via (about 1 mΩ, about 1 nH, about 0.5 pF, about 1 A, about 190 K/W) and says when each one matters.
- Mentions the aspect ratio limit and that smaller vias cost more.
- Treats the via as a design decision: every via costs routing space, adds a discontinuity, and is a reliability site, so you count them.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"A via is just a wire."** It has L and C that matter above a few hundred MHz, and a finite current rating. Treat it as an element.
- **Assuming a via carries unlimited current.** A 0.3 mm barrel is a copper tube of about 0.02 mm². Parallel several for power.
- **Confusing the drilled hole with the pad.** Drill 0.3 mm and pad 0.6 mm are different numbers. Quote both.
- **Forgetting that a through via touches every layer.** Even if it doesn't connect on layer 3, it needs an antipad in a plane on layer 3 and takes routing room.
- **Calling a via a "through hole" component pin.** Component through-holes are also plated holes, but they accept a lead. A via is for interconnect only (though the fab drills and plates both with the same process).
- **Ignoring the return path.** A signal that changes layers also changes its reference plane. See [[q0575]].
:::

::: deep
## What a via physically is

A PCB is a sandwich of copper and glass-epoxy. If you could slice one, you would see layers of copper foil (typically 17.5, 35 or 70 µm thick, written as 0.5, 1 or 2 oz) separated by dielectric. A via starts life as a drilled hole. The fab then runs the board through a chemical sequence: remove smear from the drilled wall (desmear), deposit a very thin conductive seed of copper chemically (electroless copper), and then electroplate copper on top until the barrel has the required thickness. The pads on each layer come from the same copper sheets the traces are etched from, so when the barrel touches a pad, you get a good connection.

> In a plain through via, the barrel physically passes through every layer. It only electrically connects to the layers that have a pad on it. On a layer with a plane, the CAD tool cuts an antipad so the barrel doesn't touch the plane.

## Why a board needs vias

**1. Crossing traces.** Two traces on one layer can't cross without shorting. One of them dives through a via to another layer, runs past, and comes back.

**2. Reaching planes.** Typical stack-ups put ground and power in inner layers ([[q0478]]). A decoupling capacitor on the top layer connects to a ground plane through one via, and to its power pin through another trace and via.

**3. Escape routing.** A BGA with 0.8 mm pitch has no room for traces between all the balls on the outer layer. The pads are fanned out with a via per ball, and traces then leave on inner layers.

**4. Thermal paths.** Dielectric is a poor heat conductor (FR-4 is roughly 0.3 W/m·K through-plane) and copper is excellent (roughly 385 W/m·K). A group of vias under a hot pad gives heat a copper route through the board.

**5. Return current.** A high-frequency signal's return current flows in the reference plane directly under the trace. When the trace changes layers, the return current has to hop planes too, and a nearby ground (or power) via gives it a path. See [[q0515]].

## The via as a parasitic element

Take the standard via: 0.3 mm drill, 25 µm plating, 1.6 mm board, 0.6 mm pad, 1.1 mm antipad, FR-4 with $\varepsilon_r=4.3$.

**Resistance.** The barrel is a tube with annular area $A=\frac{\pi}{4}(D^2-(D-2t)^2)\approx 0.0216\ \text{mm}^2$:

$$R=\frac{\rho L}{A}=\frac{1.72\times10^{-8}\cdot 1.6\times10^{-3}}{2.16\times10^{-8}}\approx 1.3\ \text{m}\Omega$$

That's negligible next to a typical power trace (a 10 mil, 50 mm trace on 1 oz copper is about 97 mΩ), but it adds up in high-current paths (50 A shared by an array of 20 vias is 2.5 A each, about 3 mV and 8 mW per via) and it matters in 4-wire measurements of low-value shunts.

**Inductance.** The widely used Johnson & Graham approximation for a via of length $h$ and diameter $d$ (both in inches) gives

$$L\approx 5.08\,h\left[\ln\left(\frac{4h}{d}\right)+1\right]\ \text{nH}$$

For $h=62.99$ mil and $d=11.8$ mil, $L\approx 1.3$ nH. Reactance $X_L=2\pi f L$ is 0.8 Ω at 100 MHz, 8.2 Ω at 1 GHz and 41 Ω at 5 GHz.

Figure: The resistance is flat at about 1.3 mΩ, but the inductive reactance rises linearly and overtakes it near 160 kHz. By 1 GHz it is about six thousand times larger than the DC resistance. That's why via DC resistance almost never matters for signals, while via inductance does.

**Capacitance.** The pad and its antipad act like a small capacitor to the planes: $C\approx\frac{1.41\,\varepsilon_r T D_1}{D_2-D_1}$ pF with dimensions in inches. For $T=63$ mil, $D_1=23.6$ mil, $D_2=43.3$ mil, this comes to about 0.46 pF. The via's characteristic impedance is $\sqrt{L/C}\approx 53\ \Omega$, so a well-designed via is not far from a 50 Ω line, but its delay is only about 24 ps.

## Current and thermal capacity

The barrel cross-section is about 33 mil². Using the IPC-2221 conductor fit $I = k\,\Delta T^{0.44}A^{0.725}$ with $k=0.024$ for internal layers, a single via carries about 0.84 A at a 10 °C rise. Five amps need about six vias, and in practice you add margin, because the formula was fitted to flat traces, not tubes (IPC-2152 is the better reference).

Thermally, $R_{th}=L/(kA)=\frac{1.6\times10^{-3}}{385\cdot 2.16\times10^{-8}}\approx 190$ K/W per via. Nine vias in parallel are about 21 K/W, and twelve about 16 K/W.

## Real hardware

- **Drill size limits.** Mechanical drills are commonly 0.2 mm and up on standard processes; smaller vias use lasers (microvias, see [[q0576]]). Limits depend on the fab.
- **Aspect ratio.** The ratio of board thickness to drill diameter is limited because plating solution must flow in the hole. A 3.2 mm backplane with 0.3 mm drills is 10.7:1, which many standard fabs refuse or charge extra for.
- **Cost.** More holes means more drill time and bits. Smaller holes mean more bit breakage. This is why via count is a cost and yield lever.
- **Where it shows up.** The next questions ([[q0575]] to [[q0585]]) all build on this: how to connect layers, which type, how to fill, how it fails, and when to avoid it.
:::

::: applications
- **BGA escape routing**: a 0.8 mm-pitch BGA is fanned out with via-per-ball, and traces leave on inner layers.
- **Decoupling capacitors**: a short top-layer trace plus a via to the ground plane under the cap. The loop inductance of that via is a large part of why caps placed far away don't work.
- **Thermal pads**: an array of vias under a QFN or power MOSFET exposed pad moves heat to an inner or bottom copper plane.
- **Power distribution**: a buck converter's output reaches the load through via arrays between the top-layer inductor pad and an inner power plane.
- **Via stitching**: ground vias around a board edge or between a ground pour on two layers keep the planes at the same potential and reduce radiation.
- **Test points and rework**: a via can serve as a probe point on a prototype, though this is not good practice on production designs.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Standard FR-4 build**, 1.6 mm, 4–6 layers, mechanical drill 0.3 mm, copper plating about 25 µm. A 0.8 mm board has about half the L and R, and a 3 mm backplane roughly doubles them.
- The inductance and capacitance numbers are **first-order approximations**. A 3-D field solver gives the right answer, and the return-path geometry matters (inductance goes down sharply when ground vias are nearby).
- **Current rating** is derated from flat-trace charts, so it is indicative only. It also depends on the surrounding copper because adjacent planes carry heat away.
- "Via" here means interconnect via. Some people say "via" for any plated hole, including component through-holes. The process is the same, but the design intent is different.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a via have inductance?
Current flowing up the barrel creates a magnetic field loop around it, and the energy stored in that field is the inductance. The loop is closed by the return current, so the inductance depends on the geometry of the whole loop, not just the barrel. Longer vias (thicker boards) have more. A ground via close to a signal via cancels part of the field, which is why paired vias help.

### Why not just use a thicker barrel to improve current capacity?
You can, but plating thickness is process-limited and barrel thickness is controlled by the fab. The easier lever is parallel vias or a larger drill: increasing the drill from 0.3 mm to 0.5 mm raises the cross-section from 0.0216 mm² to roughly 0.037 mm² at the same plating, about 1.7 times as much. Four 0.3 mm vias give four times the current for less board area than one huge one.

### How many vias would you use to carry 10 A between layers?
Using about 0.84 A per 0.3 mm via from the internal-layer fit, 10 A needs about 12 vias. In practice I'd use 12 to 16, spread out so the plane current isn't crowded into one spot. IPC-2152 and the fab's limits are the real basis, and thermal and surrounding copper change the answer.

### What's the difference between a via and a through-hole component pad?
Both are plated holes made by the same process. A via is meant only for interconnect and often has a smaller drill and can be covered with solder mask. A through-hole pad is sized for a component lead, with a hole sized to the lead diameter plus clearance, and it is left uncovered for soldering.

### Scenario: your 1 GHz signal looks distorted after crossing from L1 to L6. What could be wrong with the via?
Three suspects. First, the via's inductance and capacitance create a small impedance bump (the 1.3 nH is about 8 Ω at 1 GHz). Second, the leftover barrel beyond L6, if there is any, is a stub. Third, the return path has been broken because the reference plane changed and there is no nearby ground via. I'd check the stack-up, add ground vias beside the signal via, and consider back-drilling or a shorter layer pair.
:::

::: interpretations
"Why are they needed?" can be answered at two levels. The basic level is "to connect layers so we can route in 3-D". A stronger answer adds the less obvious uses (thermal, return paths, plane connections, escape routing). Both are covered here. If the interviewer's next question is "how do you connect two layers", that is [[q0575]]. "Types of vias" is [[q0576]].
