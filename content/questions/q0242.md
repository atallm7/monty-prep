::: basics
### In plain words
Heat density is how much power is wasted in each small area. It decides how hot a part gets. A multiphase buck splits the loss among several stages, so each package and inductor makes much less heat, and at full load the total heat goes down too. The heat is also spread over more board area, so the hottest spot is cooler.

### Picture it
Picture one big campfire versus four small fires. The campfire is intense in one spot and you must stand far away. The small fires are each gentle, so you can put them close to things. In a multiphase buck each stage is a small fire. The analogy has a limit: the stages sit near each other, so they warm each other a little, and the inner ones run somewhat hotter than the outer ones.

### Words you need
- **Heat density**: power dissipated per unit area.
- **Thermal resistance ($\theta$)**: how many degrees a part heats per watt dissipated.
- **Temperature rise**: power times thermal resistance, $\Delta T = P\,\theta$.
- **Phase**: one complete buck stage ([[q0236]]).
- **Conduction loss**: $I^2R$ heat, which grows with the square of the current ([[q0241]]).

### The simplest example
Take the page's 60 A load and build it from the same parts two ways. Split into four phases of 15 A, each stage wastes about 1.27 W, so 5.08 W in total. Put all 60 A through one stage of those parts and it wastes about 8.75 W, in one package: the resistance heat grows with the square of the current. Say each package heats 25 °C per watt. One stage would rise $8.75\times25 \approx 219$ °C, far too hot. Each of the four rises only $1.27\times25 \approx 32$ °C. That is the whole idea: about seven times less heat per part, and less in total too. The rest of this page shows why the drop per part is bigger than four, how neighboring phases warm each other, and why a cooler board also lowers conduction loss.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can connect **power dissipation, area and temperature**. The interviewer wants to hear:

1. Heat density (W/mm² or W/cm²) is what limits a component, not just total watts.
2. Multiphase splits the loss over $N$ sources, so each source and each local hot spot is smaller (and at full load the total falls too, because conduction loss per device goes as $1/N^2$).
3. A number: temperature rise $\Delta T=P\theta$, and why the benefit is real but not exactly $1/N$ (thermal coupling, spreading, extra losses).
4. Second-order effects: $R_{DS(on)}$ and DCR rise with temperature, so cooler running improves efficiency, and reliability depends on temperature.
:::

::: why
At low voltage and high current, most of the loss is in a small number of parts. A 60 A, 1 V rail loses about 5 W even with four phases; one pair of FETs carrying all of it on the same parts would dissipate nearly 9 W by itself, and that die would be far too hot, and heat sinks and airflow around a processor socket are scarce. Thermal design often decides the phase count, so interviewers ask it to see if you can estimate a junction temperature and understand that heat is a local problem.
:::

::: concepts
- **Power dissipation $P$** from loss in FETs, inductors and board copper ([[q0241]]).
- **Heat (power) density**: watts per unit area of the heat source.
- **Thermal resistance $\theta$ (°C/W)**: temperature rise per watt from junction to ambient or board.
- **Thermal coupling**: neighbouring heat sources raise each other's temperature.
- **Temperature coefficient of resistance**: $R_{DS(on)}$ and copper resistance rise with temperature.
- **Thermal derating**: current rating falls as board or ambient temperature rises ([[q0240]]).
:::

::: answer
"Heat density is the power dissipated per unit area, and it is what sets how hot a part gets. With a single phase at 60 amps, everything is concentrated in one pair of FETs and one inductor, and on the same parts that one stage would lose almost 9 watts in my example, because conduction loss goes as current squared. With four phases the total is about 5 watts, divided into four sources of about 1.3 watts each, spread over four packages and four inductors. Temperature rise is power times thermal resistance, so each package runs several times cooler than one package carrying everything would.

That spread also gives the board more copper and surface area to take the heat away, and you can place the phases around the load or in a row with airflow.

It isn't a clean one over N. Per package the loss actually falls faster than that, since conduction drops as one over N squared, but the phases are close together, so they warm each other, and the inner ones run hotter. And at light load the total can go up, because gate drive and core loss grow with the number of phases. Either way the peak temperature and the heat density come down, and there is a second benefit, because FET on-resistance and copper resistance rise with temperature, so running cooler also reduces conduction loss a bit.

So at full load multiphase makes somewhat less heat, but the bigger win is that it makes the heat manageable."
:::

::: technical
**Basic relations.** Heat density $q=P/A$. Junction temperature $T_j=T_a+P\,\theta_{JA}$, or $T_j=T_{board}+P\,\theta_{JB}$.

**Worked example (illustrative).** 12 V to 1 V at 60 A with the loss model of [[q0241]] ($R_{eff}=1.417$ mΩ per phase, 3.6 W total switching loss, 50 mW gate drive per phase). Assume each power stage is a 5 mm × 6 mm package (30 mm²) with $\theta_{JA}=25$ °C/W.

- Four phases, 15 A each: $0.90$ W switching $+\,15^2\cdot1.417\text{ mΩ}=0.32$ W conduction $+\,0.05$ W gate $=1.27$ W per phase (5.08 W total). $q=1.27/30=0.042$ W/mm², $\Delta T=1.27\cdot25=32$ °C.
- One stage of the same parts carrying all 60 A: $3.6$ W switching $+\,60^2\cdot1.417\text{ mΩ}=5.10$ W conduction $+\,0.05$ W gate $=8.75$ W, all in one package. $q=8.75/30=0.29$ W/mm², $\Delta T=8.75\cdot25=219$ °C, far beyond any junction limit.

The loss per package falls by $8.75/1.27=6.9$, more than the factor of 4 you might expect, because per device the switching loss falls as $1/N$ but the conduction loss falls as $1/N^2$. The total also falls at this load, from 8.75 W to 5.08 W. (A real single-phase design would use much bigger silicon and a package with lower $\theta$, so the comparison shows the *trend*, not an actual single-phase design.)

**Thermal coupling.** With mutual resistance $\theta_{12}$ between adjacent stages, $\Delta T_i=\theta_{self}P_i+\sum_{j\ne i}\theta_{ij}P_j$. With $\theta_{self}=25$ °C/W and 3 °C/W to each adjacent neighbour: outer phase $31.8+3.8=35.6$ °C, inner phase $31.8+7.6=39.4$ °C. So the hottest (inner) stage runs about 24% above its "isolated" 31.8 °C figure.

**Temperature feedback.** $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature; a typical silicon MOSFET increases by tens of percent from 25 °C to 125 °C, and copper by $0.39\%/°C$. A 32 °C rise gives about 12% more copper resistance; the single stage's 219 °C rise would add about 85% on paper (it would fail first), and that extra resistance feeds more loss into the same part. The electro-thermal section of the deep dive puts numbers on this loop.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The $\theta_{JA}$, coupling values and the FET temperature coefficient depend on package, board copper, airflow and the specific device. They are illustrative, so use datasheet and thermal-simulation values for real designs.

**Not just FETs.** Inductor DCR loss $I_{ph}^2R_{DCR}$ (about 56 mW at 15 A and 0.25 mΩ) and board copper loss also scale as $1/N$ per phase, but inductor core loss does not fall with the phase count.
:::

::: long
"Heat density is how many watts you dump per unit area, and it is the thing that actually kills components, because junction temperature is power times thermal resistance. So the question is how multiphase changes where the watts are dissipated.

Take a 12 V to 1 V, 60 amp converter. With four phases my estimate of the total loss is a bit over 5 watts. With one phase on the same parts it would be closer to 9 watts, because conduction loss goes as current squared, and all of it sits in one pair of FETs and one inductor, so a very high power density in a tiny area, and a junction temperature that can easily go past its limit unless you use a large package and a heatsink. With four phases the loss is divided over four power stages, each about 1.3 watts. If each stage has a thermal resistance of 25 degrees per watt, that's about a 32 degree rise. A single stage of the same size carrying everything would see over 200 degrees, which simply isn't survivable.

A couple of caveats. The phases are neighbours, so they warm each other, and the middle ones run a few degrees hotter than the outer ones. At full load the total loss is lower with four phases, but fixed losses like gate drive and core loss increase with the number of phases, so at light load the total can be higher. And hot spots can shift: the inductors and the board copper carry heat too.

There's also a feedback effect. Resistance in the FETs and copper increases with temperature, so a hot converter loses more power, which makes it hotter. Cooler phases lose less. That's why distributing the heat improves efficiency a little as well.

Practically, I would lay out the phases evenly around the load with airflow, balance current so one phase isn't always the hottest, and use per-phase temperature monitoring in smart power stages."
:::

::: terms
- **Heat (power) density**: dissipated power per unit area (W/mm² or W/cm²).
- **Thermal resistance ($\theta_{JA}$, $\theta_{JB}$, $\theta_{JC}$)**: °C per watt from junction to ambient, board or case.
- **Junction temperature $T_j$**: temperature of the silicon, limited by the datasheet (often 125 to 150 °C).
- **Thermal coupling**: heat from one source raising the temperature of nearby ones.
- **Hot spot**: the locally hottest point, which limits the rating.
- **Thermal derating**: lowering the current limit as temperature increases.
- **Temperature coefficient of resistance**: fractional change of resistance per degree.
- **Smart power stage**: power stage with an on-chip temperature indicator.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the heat is spread out so it is easier to cool".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Defines heat density and uses $\Delta T=P\theta$ with numbers.
- Notes that total loss does not necessarily fall (gate drive and core loss grow with $N$), but each source is smaller.
- Includes thermal coupling and that the inner phases run hottest.
- Describes the positive feedback of resistance with temperature.
- Mentions layout, airflow, copper area and per-phase temperature sensing.
- Notes that thermal limits often decide the phase count.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Multiphase reduces total heat."** It spreads it. Total loss may be lower at high load and higher at light load.
- **Dividing temperature by N.** Neighbour coupling, board spreading and package effects mean the benefit is not exactly $1/N$.
- **Ignoring the inductors.** They dissipate DCR and core loss and can be the hot spot.
- **Forgetting temperature feedback** on $R_{DS(on)}$, DCR and sense accuracy.
- **Assuming equal temperature across phases.** Position on the board and airflow differ, so one phase runs hotter.
- **Using ambient in place of board temperature.** Near a hot processor the board temperature can be much higher than the room.
:::

::: deep
## Why density, not just watts

A silicon junction fails because of its *temperature*, which is set by how fast heat can leave the chip. For a heat source of area $A$ on a spreading substrate, the thermal resistance falls with area. A 5 W source on 30 mm² is much more demanding than five 1 W sources on separate 30 mm² packages, because each one has its own conduction path and the surrounding copper has a larger total area to spread into.

## Temperature rise: the numbers

With $\theta_{JA}=25$ °C/W per package (illustrative), $\Delta T=P\theta$ gives 219 °C for one stage (8.75 W) and 32 °C per stage for four (1.27 W). Including 3 °C/W neighbour coupling, the inner stages reach about 39 °C. ## Second-order effects

1. **Temperature feedback on resistance.** Copper rises by 0.39%/°C. A part running 90 °C hotter has about 35% more copper resistance ($90\times0.39\%$), so keeping each phase cool directly reduces $I^2R$. Silicon FET $R_{DS(on)}$ also rises with temperature (vendor-dependent, commonly several tenths of a percent per °C).
2. **Reliability.** Failure rates of many mechanisms rise exponentially with junction temperature (an Arrhenius dependence). A common rule of thumb says failure rate roughly doubles every 10 °C, but the activation energy depends on the mechanism.
3. **Derating.** A power stage rated 40 A at a 25 °C board may carry only 25 A at 100 °C ([[q0240]]). Lower temperature means more usable current.
4. **Inductors.** Inductor temperature raises DCR and lowers saturation current. Spreading the inductors reduces local heating of the board.

## Layout and cooling

- Place phases evenly around the load (processor socket) so each has its own copper and path.
- Give each power stage thermal vias into inner ground/power planes.
- Check airflow direction: downstream phases see preheated air.
- Measure with a thermal camera or the power stage's temperature output and compare phases. A consistent hot phase indicates a layout or current-balance issue ([[q0238]]).

## Why spreading helps more than the package number says

The package figure of 25 °C/W hides what happens in the board. Heat leaves the power stage into the copper planes and spreads sideways. For a circular source of radius $a$ spreading radially through a copper layer of thickness $t$ to a radius $b$, the thermal resistance is

$$R_{spread}=\frac{\ln(b/a)}{2\pi k\,t}$$

with $k\approx385$ W/(m·K) for copper. For 1 oz copper ($t=35$ µm), $kt=0.0135$ W/K, so $R_{spread}=11.8\,\ln(b/a)$ K/W per layer.

The important feature is that $R_{spread}$ depends only on the *ratio* $b/a$. To isolate the geometry, hold the total heat fixed at the four-phase figure of 5.08 W. Suppose one source of radius 5 mm spreads out to 20 mm: $\ln4=1.39$ and $R_{spread}=16.4$ K/W. Now divide the heat among four sources, each of radius 2.5 mm (a quarter of the area) and each with a 10 mm spreading region (a quarter of the area): the ratio is still 4 and the resistance is still 16.4 K/W per source. So each source's temperature rise from board spreading is

$$\Delta T=\frac{P}{N}\,R_{spread}=\frac{5.08\text{ W}}{4}\times16.4=21\ ^\circ\text{C}\quad\text{versus}\quad5.08\times16.4=83\ ^\circ\text{C for one source.}$$

(A single stage on the same parts would also make more heat, 8.75 W, which widens the gap further: $8.75\times16.4=144$ °C.)

Scaling the layout down by $\sqrt N$ keeps the thermal resistance fixed, so the temperature falls linearly with the power per source: that is the first-principles reason that distributing heat helps, independent of any package. In practice you add parallel layers connected by vias, which divides $R_{spread}$ by the number of effective layers (four 1 oz layers give about 4 K/W for the same geometry), and the package and via resistances add in series. This model ignores heat lost through the top of the board and the finite size of the plane.

## Electro-thermal feedback: a stability margin

Resistance rises with temperature, which raises loss, which raises temperature. This is a loop, and it has a gain. If the conduction loss at 25 °C is $P_c$, the temperature coefficient is $\alpha$ and the thermal resistance is $\theta$, the steady state is

$$\Delta T=\theta\,\big[P_f+P_c(1+\alpha\Delta T)\big]\ \Rightarrow\ \Delta T=\frac{\theta(P_f+P_c)}{1-\theta P_c\alpha}$$

where $P_f$ is the loss that does not depend on temperature (taken here as switching plus gate drive) and the loop gain is $\theta P_c\alpha$. Take $\theta=25$ °C/W and an effective $\alpha=0.4\%$/°C:

| Case | $P_f$ | $P_c$ at 25 °C | Loop gain | $\Delta T$ without feedback | $\Delta T$ with feedback |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| One of four phases | 0.95 W | 0.32 W | 0.032 | 31.7 °C | 32.8 °C |
| Single phase, all 60 A | 3.65 W | 5.10 W | 0.51 | 219 °C | 446 °C |

For four phases the feedback adds 3%, which you can ignore. For the single stage the loop gain is 0.51 and the temperature doubles; if it reached 1 the equation has no steady state, which is thermal runaway. (The 446 °C figure is not physical, since the part would long since have failed; it shows how fast the margin vanishes.) Distributing the loss keeps each device far from that cliff. The 0.4%/°C is an effective value for the mix of FET and copper resistance; silicon MOSFET $R_{DS(on)}$ commonly has a higher coefficient than copper, so check the datasheet.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 25 °C/W package resistance and the 0.4%/°C coefficient are illustrative. Use the datasheet's $\theta_{JB}$ or $\theta_{JC}$ with your actual board for real designs, and note that effective $\theta$ depends on copper area, vias and airflow.

## Measuring it

1. **Steady-state wait.** The board's thermal time constant is tens of seconds to minutes, so hold the load for several time constants before reading temperatures. A reading taken 20 seconds after load application understates the final value.
2. **Thermal imaging needs emissivity care.** Bare shiny copper reads far too cool at the default emissivity; mold compound and solder mask read close to the true temperature. A tiny piece of high-emissivity tape or a thermocouple on the power stage case gives a trustworthy reference. Alternatively use the power stage's own temperature report, if it has one.
3. **Extract $\theta$ from the data.** Measure the temperature rise $\Delta T$ at a known dissipation (total input power minus output power divided by $N$, if currents are balanced). $\theta=\Delta T/P$ for each stage gives the effective figure to use in your own calculations, and it shows which phase has the poorest thermal path.
4. **Check the sensitivity.** Compare phases at 50% and 100% load. If one phase's temperature rises faster than $I^2$ would predict, current imbalance or the feedback loop above is at work.

::: applications
- **CPU/GPU VRMs**, where power stages are laid around the socket and monitored with per-phase temperature outputs.
- **Server boards with front-to-back airflow**, where downstream phases get warmer air.
- **Thin laptops**, where there is no heat sink and spreading the loss over many small parts is the only option.
- **Automotive compute modules** operating at high ambient temperature with derating.
- **Telecom modules** that use multiphase to stay within a baseplate-temperature limit.

::: assumptions
- **Same parts in every phase** for the one-stage versus four-stage comparison, using the loss model of [[q0241]]: 8.75 W in one stage versus 1.27 W in each of four. The board-spreading estimate instead holds the total at 5.08 W to isolate geometry.
- **Illustrative thermal resistances** ($\theta_{JA}=25$ °C/W, 3 °C/W coupling). They vary strongly with copper area, vias and airflow.
- **Equal current in each phase.** Imbalance shifts the hot spot.
- **Steady state.** Transient thermal response (thermal capacitance) allows short high-power bursts.
- A real single-phase design would use larger devices with lower $\theta$, so the extreme 219 °C case is a trend, not a prediction.
:::

::: followups
### If the total loss is the same, why does heat density matter?
Because temperature rise depends on local power density and the thermal path, not total watts alone. Five watts in one 30 mm² package gives a far higher junction temperature than 1.27 W in each of four packages, since each has its own path to the board and a larger combined area.

### Which phase runs hottest and why?
Typically an inner phase, because it has neighbours on both sides, or one downstream of the airflow, or one with a worse thermal path (less copper, fewer vias). Current imbalance can also make a phase hotter.

### Does lower temperature improve efficiency?
Yes, a little. Copper and silicon on-resistance have positive temperature coefficients. For copper, 0.39%/°C means 60 °C cooler is roughly 23% lower DCR. This reduces conduction loss and also helps the sense accuracy.

### What do you do if a power stage reaches its thermal limit at full load?
Options: add a phase (reduce current per stage), improve the thermal path (more vias, copper, heat sink, airflow), lower the switching frequency to reduce switching loss, reduce the load, or choose a lower $R_{DS(on)}$/better package. The controller should have per-phase thermal protection and throttle before damage.

### Scenario: IR imaging shows phase 3 is 20 °C hotter than the others. What next?
Check whether it carries more current (telemetry or sense voltage), then its inductor DCR and sense network. If current is equal, look at thermal path: missing vias, less copper, voids in solder under the thermal pad, or airflow blockage. Swap the power stage with a neighbour to see whether the problem follows the part.
