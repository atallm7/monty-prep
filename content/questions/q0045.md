::: basics
### In plain words
Impedance is how strongly a part opposes alternating current at a given frequency. For an inductor, that opposition grows as the frequency goes up. At DC the inductor opposes nothing and acts like a wire. At very high frequency it opposes almost everything and acts like a gap. This page asks you to describe and sketch that rise.

### Picture it
Think of pushing a heavy swing. Push slowly and it follows along easily, so it feels no resistance. Push back and forth faster and faster, and the heavy swing fights every reversal harder and harder. The inductor works the same way: each time the current tries to reverse, the coil pushes back, so more reversals per second means more pushback. The analogy breaks for real parts, which behave differently at very high frequency.

### Words you need
- **Impedance ($Z$)**: the opposition to AC current, in ohms. It is the AC version of resistance.
- **Reactance**: the part of impedance that comes from storing energy. For an inductor it is $\omega L$.
- **Frequency ($f$)**: how many cycles per second the signal makes. Here $\omega = 2\pi f$.
- **Inductor impedance**: $Z_L = j\omega L$. See [[q0040]].
- **Self-resonant frequency**: where a real inductor's own tiny capacitance takes over.
- **Decade**: a factor of 10 in frequency.

### The simplest example
Take a 1 mH inductor. At 0 Hz its impedance is 0 Ω, a short. At 1 kHz, $\omega L = 2\pi \times 1000 \times 0.001 \approx 6.3$ Ω. At 10 kHz it is ten times more, about 63 Ω. At 100 kHz it is about 630 Ω. Each ten-fold step in frequency gives a ten-fold step in impedance, which is a straight line going up on a log-log plot. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows how to sketch it, and how a real inductor bends away from that line at both ends.
:::

::: testing
Two answers and a sketch:

1. **DC**: $Z_L = j\cdot0\cdot L = 0$, a short (a real part shows its DCR).
2. **Infinitely high frequency**: $|Z_L| = \omega L \to \infty$, an open.
3. **The plot**: $|Z|$ vs. $f$ is a straight line through the origin on linear axes, and a line with slope +1 (+20 dB/decade) on log–log axes, with phase fixed at +90°.

The hidden test is whether you know the ideal plot is a lie at both ends: a real inductor flattens out at its DCR at low frequency, peaks at its self-resonant frequency, and turns **capacitive** above it, so "open at infinite frequency" is never what a real part does.
:::

::: why
This picture is how you choose inductors for filtering and decide where they stop working. EMI chokes, ferrite beads, bias tees and LC filters all depend on the inductor's impedance being high at the noise frequency and low at DC, and every one of them fails if the noise is above the part's self-resonant frequency. Being able to sketch $|Z|$ vs. $f$ for both the ideal and real part, and read a datasheet impedance curve, is a daily skill in power and RF design. The same question appears under the capacitor section ([[q0029]]), which makes it worth knowing both curves cold.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor impedance**: $Z_L = j\omega L$, magnitude $2\pi fL$ ([[q0040]]).
- **DC and high-frequency limits**: short at DC, open at high frequency, matching the time-domain shortcuts ([[q0044]]).
- **Log–log plots**: a quantity proportional to $f$ is a straight line of slope +1 (+20 dB/decade).
- **Real-part parasitics**: DCR in series, winding capacitance in parallel ([[q0047]]).
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: $f_{SRF} = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_p})$, where the part stops being inductive.
- **Duality with capacitors**: a capacitor's impedance falls as $1/f$ ([[q0024]]).
:::

::: answer
"For an ideal inductor the impedance is $j\omega L$. At DC, omega is zero, so the impedance is zero: it's a short circuit. As frequency goes to infinity, the magnitude $\omega L$ goes to infinity, so it's an open circuit.

If I plot magnitude against frequency on linear axes, it's a straight line through the origin with slope $2\pi L$. On log–log axes, which is how you'd normally draw it, it's a straight line going up at 20 dB per decade: ten times the frequency, ten times the impedance. The phase is a constant plus 90 degrees.

Then I'd add what a real inductor does, because the ideal plot is wrong at both ends. At low frequency it doesn't go to zero; it flattens out at the DC resistance of the winding, maybe tens of milliohms. And at high frequency the capacitance between turns resonates with the inductance. The impedance peaks at the self-resonant frequency, and above that it actually falls, because the part behaves like a capacitor. So a 10 µH power inductor might peak in the tens of megahertz and be useless as an inductor above that.

So in practice: short at DC, rising impedance through the useful band, and you design to stay well below SRF."
:::

::: technical
**Ideal inductor.** $Z_L = j\omega L$, $|Z_L| = 2\pi fL$, $\angle Z_L = +90^\circ$.

| Frequency | $|Z_L|$ | Behaves as |
|---|---|---|
| $f = 0$ (DC) | 0 | Short circuit |
| $f \to \infty$ | $\infty$ | Open circuit |

On linear axes: a line through the origin, slope $2\pi L$ (Ω/Hz). On log–log: $\log|Z| = \log f + \log(2\pi L)$, a line of slope +1 (+20 dB/decade). Ten times the inductance shifts it up one decade.

**Real inductor model:** $(R_{DC} + j\omega L)$ in parallel with winding capacitance $C_p$ (and often a parallel resistance $R_p$ representing core loss):
$$Z(\omega) = \left[\frac{1}{R_{DC} + j\omega L} + j\omega C_p + \frac{1}{R_p}\right]^{-1}$$

Regions:
1. **Resistive floor**, $f \ll R_{DC}/(2\pi L)$: $|Z| \approx R_{DC}$. For 10 µH and 50 mΩ, the corner is about 800 Hz.
2. **Inductive**, between the corner and roughly $f_{SRF}/3$: $|Z| \approx 2\pi fL$, phase near +90°.
3. **Self-resonance**: $f_{SRF} \approx 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_p})$. For 10 µH and 5 pF, 22.5 MHz. Impedance peaks and the phase passes through 0°. The peak height is limited by losses ($R_p$, AC resistance), typically kΩ to tens of kΩ.
4. **Capacitive**, above SRF: $|Z| \approx 1/(2\pi fC_p)$, falling at −20 dB/decade, phase near −90°.

At still higher frequencies the lumped model fails altogether: lead/pad inductance and distributed winding effects create further resonances.

**Datasheet reading.** Vendors give the SRF and often an $|Z|$ vs. $f$ curve. Rule of thumb: use an inductor as an inductor only up to a fraction of its SRF (often quoted as ~1/3 to 1/10, depending on how much the effective $L$ is allowed to rise near resonance).
:::

::: short
"At DC an ideal inductor's impedance $j\omega L$ is zero, a short; as frequency goes to infinity it goes to infinity, an open. Plotted, $|Z|$ is a straight line through the origin, or on log–log axes a line rising at 20 dB per decade with a constant +90° phase. A real inductor bottoms out at its DCR at low frequency, peaks at its self-resonant frequency because of winding capacitance, and turns capacitive above it."
:::

::: long
"Starting from the impedance, $Z_L = j\omega L$. At DC, omega is zero, so the impedance is zero and the inductor is just a short circuit. That matches the time-domain picture: in DC steady state, di/dt is zero so there's no voltage across it.

As frequency increases, the impedance grows linearly. At infinitely high frequency it's infinite: an open circuit. That matches the other time-domain picture, where an inductor blocks the instantaneous edge of a step.

For the plot: on linear axes it's a straight line through the origin with slope $2\pi L$. Usually you'd draw it log–log, and then it's a straight line with slope one, rising at 20 dB per decade. A 10 microhenry inductor is about 6 ohms at 100 kilohertz and 63 ohms at 1 megahertz. The phase is flat at plus 90 degrees, meaning voltage leads current everywhere.

Now the real part, which is where the interesting stuff is. At very low frequency, a real inductor's impedance doesn't go to zero; it flattens out at the winding's DC resistance. For a 10 microhenry, 50 milliohm part, the curve flattens below about 800 hertz.

At high frequency, the capacitance between turns, a few picofarads, sits in parallel with the inductance. At the self-resonant frequency, one over two pi root LC, those resonate, and the impedance peaks. For 10 microhenries and 5 picofarads, that's around 22 megahertz. Above that, the capacitance wins, the impedance falls at 20 dB per decade, and the phase flips to minus 90. So it never becomes an open circuit; it becomes a small capacitor.

The practical takeaway is to check SRF before using an inductor or choke to filter noise. If your noise is at 100 megahertz and the choke's SRF is 20 megahertz, it's letting the noise straight through its own capacitance."
:::

::: terms
- **Impedance magnitude |Z|**: $2\pi fL$ for an ideal inductor, in ohms.
- **Short circuit**: zero impedance; an ideal inductor at DC.
- **Open circuit**: infinite impedance; the ideal inductor's high-frequency limit.
- **+20 dB/decade**: the slope of $|Z| \propto f$ on a log–log (Bode-style) plot.
- **DCR**: winding DC resistance; the low-frequency floor of a real inductor's impedance.
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: where winding capacitance resonates with $L$; impedance peaks there.
- **Winding (parasitic) capacitance**: capacitance between turns and layers, in parallel with $L$.
- **Capacitive region**: above SRF, where a real inductor's impedance falls and its phase is near −90°.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "zero at DC, infinite at infinite frequency" and draws a rising line.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws it properly on **log–log** axes with the slope labelled (+20 dB/decade) and the phase (+90°).
- Puts a number on it (10 µH: 6.3 Ω at 100 kHz, 63 Ω at 1 MHz).
- Overlays the **real** curve: DCR floor, the low-frequency corner $R_{DC}/(2\pi L)$, the SRF peak and the capacitive roll-off above it.
- Explains what that means for design: inductors and chokes stop filtering above SRF, so check the datasheet curve against your noise frequency.
- Mentions the dual curve for capacitors ([[q0029]]) and that the time-domain shortcuts (short at DC, open at the edge) say the same thing.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing it on log–log axes as a curve.** $|Z| \propto f$ is a straight line of slope +1 on log–log axes. On linear axes it's also a straight line, through the origin.
- **Swapping with the capacitor.** Capacitor impedance falls with frequency (open at DC, short at high frequency); inductor rises.
- **Saying a real inductor is an open circuit at very high frequency.** Above SRF its impedance falls; it's capacitive.
- **Saying a real inductor is exactly 0 Ω at DC.** It's the DCR, which matters for power loss and current-sense accuracy.
- **Forgetting the phase.** The plot request usually wants magnitude, but a complete answer notes +90° (and the flip through 0° to −90° at SRF in a real part).
:::

::: deep
## The ideal curve

$$Z_L(f) = j2\pi fL, \qquad |Z_L| = 2\pi fL, \qquad \angle Z_L = +90^\circ$$

**At DC** ($f = 0$): $|Z_L| = 0$. With no change in current there's no induced voltage ([[q0044]]), so the inductor is a wire.

**As $f \to \infty$**: $|Z_L| \to \infty$. The current would have to reverse infinitely fast, which needs infinite voltage per amp, so the inductor blocks it: an open circuit.

On linear axes it's simply a ramp:

Notice the 10 MHz entry: approaching SRF, the *effective* inductance rises, because $X = \omega L/(1-\omega^2LC_p)$. At $f = 0.45\,f_{SRF}$ the apparent inductance is already about 25% high. That's why designers stay well below SRF if they need a precise $L$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The guidance on how far below SRF to operate varies (you'll see 1/3, 1/5 or 1/10 of SRF quoted) and depends on how much inductance error your circuit tolerates. The 20 kΩ peak here comes from an assumed loss resistance; real peaks depend on core material and construction, so read the vendor's |Z| curve.

## Why the ends match the time-domain intuition

- DC ↔ "inductor is a short at DC steady state" ([[q0044]]).
- Very high frequency ↔ "inductor is an open at the instant of a step", because a step's edge is all high-frequency content.

And the real-part corrections have time-domain counterparts too: DCR means the current settles at $V/R_{DC}$ instead of ramping forever; $C_p$ means a very fast edge sees a capacitive current spike through the "open" inductor.

## Design consequences

**Filtering.** An LC or choke filter works only in the band where the inductor is inductive. A common-mode choke with SRF at 30 MHz does little against 300 MHz noise: its own capacitance bypasses it. That's why EMI filters often combine a choke (for MHz) with ferrite beads (for 100 MHz+).

**Bias tees.** An inductor feeding DC to an RF amplifier must have high impedance at the RF frequency, so its SRF must be above the operating band (or deliberately placed at it, since the impedance peaks there).

**Power inductors.** At a 500 kHz switching frequency, a 10 µH inductor's SRF of tens of MHz is far away, but switching edges contain harmonics up into the 100 MHz range, where the inductor is capacitive. Ringing on the switch node couples straight through the winding capacitance to the output, one reason for post-filters.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> When asked to "plot this behaviour", draw log–log axes, label the slope (+20 dB/dec) and then sketch the real curve on top with the DCR floor and SRF peak. Drawing both shows you know the ideal model and its limits.
:::

::: applications
- **EMI chokes**: chosen for high impedance in the conducted-emissions band (150 kHz–30 MHz for many standards), which requires SRF above that band.
- **Ferrite beads**: their impedance curve (e.g. "600 Ω at 100 MHz") is used directly in place of an inductance value.
- **Bias tees**: an inductor passes DC to an RF device while presenting high impedance at RF.
- **LC output filters**: in a buck, the inductor's high impedance at the switching frequency (and low impedance at DC) is what separates ripple from the DC output ([[q0052]]).
- **RF choke selection**: a choke is often picked with SRF near the operating frequency to maximize blocking impedance.
- **Current sensing via DCR**: at low frequency the inductor is its DCR, which some converters use to sense current ([[q0226]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal model** for the headline answer: $Z = j\omega L$ with constant $L$. The real-part curve assumes the simple $R_{DC}$/$L$/$C_p$/$R_p$ model, which is good up to a little beyond SRF.
- **Small-signal, unbiased**: $L$ is measured at small AC current with no DC bias. With DC current in a cored inductor, $L$ drops ([[q0046]]), so the inductive line shifts down.
- **Frequency-independent L**: ferrite permeability rolls off at high frequency, so the "inductive" line also bends.
- **"Infinitely high frequency"** is a mathematical limit; physically the lumped model breaks down and the part becomes a distributed structure with multiple resonances.
:::

::: followups
### What's the self-resonant frequency of a 4.7 µH inductor with 3 pF of winding capacitance?
$f_{SRF} = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{4.7\times10^{-6}\cdot3\times10^{-12}}) \approx 42.4$ MHz. Above that it behaves capacitively.

### How does the capacitor's impedance plot compare?
It's the mirror image: $|Z_C| = 1/(2\pi fC)$ falls at −20 dB/decade, infinite at DC (open) and zero at infinite frequency (short), with −90° phase. A real capacitor bottoms out at its ESR at its SRF and then rises because of ESL. See [[q0029]] and [[q0024]].

### Why does the apparent inductance increase as you approach the SRF?
For a parallel $L$–$C_p$, the net reactance is $X = \omega L/(1-\omega^2LC_p)$. Dividing by $\omega$ gives an effective inductance $L/(1-(f/f_{SRF})^2)$, which grows toward infinity as $f \to f_{SRF}$. At half the SRF it's already 33% higher than the nominal $L$.

### At what frequency does a 100 µH, 0.2 Ω inductor stop looking resistive and start looking inductive?
Where $2\pi fL = R$: $f = 0.2/(2\pi\cdot10^{-4}) \approx 318$ Hz. Below that, DCR dominates the impedance; above it, the inductance does.

### What if the noise you're trying to filter is at 150 MHz and your choke's SRF is 25 MHz? What would you do?
At 150 MHz the choke is capacitive, so it passes noise through its parasitic capacitance. Options: add a ferrite bead rated for high impedance around 100–300 MHz in series; use a smaller inductor with higher SRF for the high-frequency stage (a two-stage filter); or reduce the noise at its source with layout and edge-rate control.
:::
