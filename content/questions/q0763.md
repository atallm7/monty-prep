::: testing
Whether you know the **standard speed grades by name and number**, and, better, that the number on the label is a ceiling set by the physical layer and by the slowest device, not a promise of throughput. The interviewer wants:

1. The modes: Standard 100 kHz, Fast 400 kHz, Fast-mode Plus 1 MHz, High-speed 3.4 MHz (and Ultra-fast 5 MHz as the odd one).
2. What actually limits the clock: rise time ($R_pC_b$), device support, clock stretching.
3. A feel for real throughput after protocol overhead (9 clocks per byte, address, START/STOP).
4. Which speed you'd choose for a given job.
:::

::: why
Picking an I2C speed is a daily decision: the sensor datasheet says "up to 400 kHz", the MCU can do 1 MHz, and the bus has 150 pF of capacitance. You need the numbers to answer "will it work, and how fast does it really read?" It also leads directly into the I2C vs SPI speed comparison ([[q0739]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Speed modes**: Standard, Fast, Fast-mode Plus, High-speed, Ultra-fast.
- **Rise-time limits**: 1000/300/120 ns for Sm/Fm/Fm+. See [[q0755]].
- **Bus capacitance**: ties the mode to the allowed $R_p$ and $C_b$. See [[q0756]].
- **Frame overhead**: each byte costs 9 clocks (8 data bits + ACK). See [[q0749]].
- **Clock stretching**: slaves can slow the effective rate. See [[q0752]].
- **I2C vs SPI**: SPI is much faster because it is push-pull. See [[q0738]] and [[q0739]].
:::

::: answer
"The standard I2C speeds are 100 kHz for Standard mode, 400 kHz for Fast mode, 1 MHz for Fast-mode Plus, and 3.4 MHz for High-speed mode, and there's a 5 MHz Ultra-fast mode that is write-only and push-pull. In practice most sensors and EEPROMs run at 100 or 400 kHz, and newer parts support 1 MHz.

Those numbers are ceilings, not guarantees. The limit is the physical layer: the rising edge is an RC charge through the pull-up, and the spec gives rise-time limits of 1000 ns, 300 ns and 120 ns for the first three modes. So whether I can use 400 kHz depends on my pull-up and my bus capacitance. And the bus only runs as fast as the slowest device on it, plus any clock stretching.

Also, the clock rate isn't the data rate. Every byte takes nine clocks, eight bits plus the ACK, and there's an address byte and START and STOP around each transaction. So 400 kHz gives at most about 44 kilobytes per second, and a short register read gets maybe half of that.

If I'm choosing, I start at 100 kHz because it's the most forgiving, move to 400 kHz when I need the throughput and the bus capacitance allows, and go to SPI if I really need megabits."
:::

::: technical
**Speed modes (NXP UM10204).**

| Mode | Max SCL | Max rise time ($t_r$) | Max $C_b$ | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Standard (Sm) | 100 kHz | 1000 ns | 400 pF | default, most forgiving |
| Fast (Fm) | 400 kHz | 300 ns | 400 pF | common for sensors; 50 ns spike filter |
| Fast-mode Plus (Fm+) | 1 MHz | 120 ns | 550 pF listed in recent revisions | 20 mA sink drivers; smaller $R_p$ |
| High-speed (Hs) | 3.4 MHz | very short; current-source pull-up | 100 pF (or 400 pF at 1.7 MHz) | master code, current-source pull-up in master |
| Ultra-fast (UFm) | 5 MHz | push-pull | n/a | unidirectional, write-only, no ACK, no pull-ups |

**Timing minima (Fast mode as an example).** $t_{LOW}\ge1.3$ µs and $t_{HIGH}\ge0.6$ µs, so even at the 400 kHz ceiling the 2.5 µs period leaves only 0.6 µs for edges; Fm+ has $t_{LOW}\ge0.5$ µs and $t_{HIGH}\ge0.26$ µs in a 1 µs period.

**Throughput.** One byte = 8 data bits + ACK = 9 clocks. Peak byte rate $=f_{SCL}/9$: 11.1 kB/s (100 kHz), 44.4 kB/s (400 kHz), 111 kB/s (1 MHz). A real transaction adds START/STOP and the address byte (and a register address and repeated START for reads).

**Example: reading 6 bytes of accelerometer data** (write addr+W, reg, repeated START, addr+R, 6 data bytes): $9+9+9+6\times9=81$ clocks plus START/STOP, so about 84 clocks: 840 µs at 100 kHz, 210 µs at 400 kHz, 84 µs at 1 MHz.

**What limits the speed.**
- $R_pC_b$: $t_r=0.8473R_pC_b$ must be below the limit.
- The slowest device on the bus.
- MCU peripheral limits and software overhead (bit-banged I2C is much slower).
- Clock stretching by slaves.
- Cable length and edge integrity.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Maximum $C_b$ for Fast-mode Plus and High-speed mode differs between spec revisions and device classes; check the revision and the part's datasheet. Also, "typical" real SCL frequencies depend on the MCU's clock divider, so a "400 kHz" setting often produces 380 or 390 kHz.
:::

::: long
"The I2C speed grades are Standard mode at 100 kHz, Fast mode at 400 kHz, Fast-mode Plus at 1 MHz, High-speed mode at 3.4 MHz, and Ultra-fast mode at 5 MHz, which is a special unidirectional, write-only variant with push-pull drivers. In real boards, almost everything is 100 or 400 kHz; 1 MHz shows up on newer sensors, memories and PMICs; and 3.4 MHz is rare.

The speed is limited by physics before it's limited by the protocol. The rising edge is the pull-up resistor charging the bus capacitance, so the rise time is about 0.85 times R times C, and each mode has a maximum: a microsecond for Standard, 300 ns for Fast and 120 ns for Fast-mode Plus. If my bus has 200 pF and a 2.2 kΩ pull-up, that's about 370 ns, so 100 kHz works and 400 kHz doesn't. To go faster I'd shrink the capacitance or the pull-up, or use drivers that can sink more current. And the bus only runs as fast as its slowest device, so a single 100 kHz-only part limits everything unless I put it behind a switch.

Then there's the difference between clock rate and data rate. Each byte takes nine clocks with the ACK, and every transaction has an address byte, a START and a STOP. So at 400 kHz the absolute peak is about 44 kilobytes per second, and reading a six-byte sample takes around 200 microseconds, which is fine for a sensor sampled at a kilohertz but not for streaming.

In practice I start at 100 kHz because it's forgiving, move to 400 kHz if I need the speed and my capacitance and pull-ups allow, and if I'm moving real data, I use SPI instead."
:::

::: terms
- **Standard mode (Sm)**: 100 kbit/s.
- **Fast mode (Fm)**: 400 kbit/s.
- **Fast-mode Plus (Fm+)**: 1 Mbit/s, 20 mA sink drivers.
- **High-speed mode (Hs)**: 3.4 Mbit/s, master-code handshake and current-source pull-up.
- **Ultra-fast mode (UFm)**: 5 Mbit/s, unidirectional, push-pull, no ACK.
- **SCL frequency**: the clock rate; one bit per clock.
- **Rise-time limit**: maximum 30–70% time allowed in each mode.
- **Protocol overhead**: START, address, ACK bits and STOP that carry no payload.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "100 kHz, 400 kHz" and maybe 1 MHz.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names all the modes with numbers, including Fm+ and Hs, and mentions UFm as a special case.
- Ties each mode to its rise-time limit and shows how $R_pC_b$ decides which mode a given bus can use.
- Distinguishes clock rate from throughput with the nine-clock byte and a worked read example.
- Mentions slave-limited speed, clock stretching and MCU peripheral limits.
- Gives a selection heuristic: 100 kHz default, 400 kHz for throughput, SPI for bulk data.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating the bus clock as the data rate.** With ACKs and addressing, payload throughput is well below $f_{SCL}\times1$ bit.
- **Believing 400 kHz always works if both chips support it.** The pull-up and capacitance must meet 300 ns.
- **Forgetting the slowest device.** One 100 kHz-only part drags the bus unless isolated.
- **Calling 3.4 MHz "just a faster clock".** Hs-mode needs a master code, current-source pull-up and compatible devices.
- **Confusing kHz with kbit/s only at the sloppy level.** In I2C they're equal (one bit per clock), but throughput isn't.
- **Not budgeting for clock stretching** when computing sample times.
:::

::: deep
## The speed grades and what each requires

I2C has five speed grades. Each trades speed against how much the bus can be loaded:

| Mode | SCL | $t_r$ max | What changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | 100 kHz | 1000 ns | baseline |
| Fast | 400 kHz | 300 ns | tighter rise, 50 ns input spike filter, Schmitt inputs |
| Fast-mode Plus | 1 MHz | 120 ns | 20 mA drivers, so small pull-ups are allowed |
| High-speed | 3.4 MHz | very short | master code, switched current-source pull-up in master, separate timing |
| Ultra-fast | 5 MHz | push-pull | unidirectional write-only, no ACK, no pull-up needed |

**Timing at the limit.** Because $t_{LOW}$ and $t_{HIGH}$ have fixed minima, the period at the ceiling is mostly *required* low/high time with little room for edges. At 400 kHz: 2.5 µs period, 1.3 µs low and 0.6 µs high required, leaving 0.6 µs, spent largely on the rise and fall.

## Which bus can run which mode? Back from $R_pC_b$

For a given $R_p$ and $C_b$, the rise time is $t_r=0.8473R_pC_b$. Rearranged, the largest capacitance a mode permits with that resistor is:

$$C_{b,max}=\frac{t_{r,max}}{0.8473R_p}$$

## Throughput: what the clock really buys

Every byte takes nine clocks (8 bits + ACK). Peak byte rate is $f_{SCL}/9$:

| Mode | Peak bytes/s | Time per byte |
|---|---|---|
| 100 kHz | 11.1 kB/s | 90 µs |
| 400 kHz | 44.4 kB/s | 22.5 µs |
| 1 MHz | 111 kB/s | 9 µs |
| 3.4 MHz | 378 kB/s | 2.6 µs |

A short transaction is worse because the fixed overhead (START, address byte, STOP, register pointer) is amortised over few payload bytes. With one address byte, $n$ payload bytes and about 2 clocks of START/STOP, payload bits per second are $f\cdot 8n/(9(n+1)+2)$:

```plot

 "series":[{"label":"100 kHz","fn":"100*8*x/(9*(x+1)+2)","color":"blue"},{"label":"400 kHz","fn":"400*8*x/(9*(x+1)+2)"},{"label":"1 MHz","fn":"1000*8*x/(9*(x+1)+2)","color":"red"}]}
```
Figure: Even a 64-byte burst delivers only about 87% of the clock rate as payload (the ACK alone costs 1/9). A single-byte transaction gets 40% of the clock rate in payload: a 400 kHz bus moves just 160 kbit/s of data when you read one byte at a time.

**Worked example.** An accelerometer read: write the register address, repeated START, read 6 bytes. Clocks: address+W (9) + register (9) + address+R (9) + 6×9 (54) = 81, plus START/STOP/repeated START ≈ 3 clocks: 84 clocks. At 400 kHz that's 210 µs; at 100 kHz 840 µs; at 1 MHz 84 µs. If you need 1 kHz sampling of one such sensor, 100 kHz uses 84% of the bus time. That's a design decision driven by the speed grade.

## What limits the clock in practice

1. **Rise time** ($R_pC_b$), covered above.
2. **Slowest device.** Mixed buses drop to the lowest common mode unless the slow part is behind a switch.
3. **Clock stretching.** A slave that holds SCL low for a few µs per byte can halve the effective rate ([[q0752]]).
4. **MCU peripheral and software.** Hardware I2C blocks hit the target; bit-banged or interrupt-heavy implementations can't. The clock divider often produces something slightly under the nominal.
5. **Signal integrity**: long cables and bad edges.
6. **SMBus compatibility**: SMBus targets 10–100 kHz with a timeout, so parts on an SMBus-compatible bus may reject very low or very high clock.

> [!NOTE]
> I2C has no minimum clock frequency in the I2C spec (it is a static interface), but SMBus does impose a minimum of about 10 kHz via timeouts.

## Choosing a speed

| Situation | Choice |
|---|---|
| Config registers, slow sensors, long wires | 100 kHz |
| Typical sensor at tens to hundreds of Hz | 400 kHz |
| Many sensors or larger reads (IMU, display config) | 1 MHz if all parts support it and the bus is short |
| Streaming data, displays, cameras, memories | Not I2C: SPI, or a parallel/MIPI interface |
| Bus capacitance near 400 pF | 100 kHz, or add a buffer |

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Give the numbers (100k, 400k, 1M, 3.4M), then add: "and what actually sets it is $R_pC_b$ against the rise-time limit." It shows you know why the bus has those grades.
:::

::: applications
- **Environmental and motion sensors**: temperature, humidity, pressure and IMUs commonly run at 400 kHz.
- **EEPROMs and RTCs**: 100 or 400 kHz, with 1 MHz on newer EEPROMs.
- **Power-management ICs**: configuration and telemetry at 400 kHz or 1 MHz.
- **Fast-mode Plus**: sensor hubs and camera control buses where 1 MHz reduces latency.
- **High-speed mode**: rare; some touch controllers and audio codecs.
:::

::: assumptions
- **I2C spec limits** per NXP UM10204; individual devices may be rated lower or higher.
- **Typical resistive pull-up with lumped $C_b$**; with active pull-ups or buffers the capacity is higher.
- **Hardware I2C peripheral**; bit-banging typically manages 50 to 100 kHz.
- **Throughput numbers** ignore clock stretching, software latency and bus idle time.

If the interviewer asks "how fast can I read my sensor?", compute clocks per transaction, divide by $f_{SCL}$ and add software overhead.
:::

::: followups
### Why doesn't I2C go faster than a few MHz?
The open-drain bus has slow rising edges because a pull-up resistor has to charge the bus capacitance. The rise time must fit inside the clock phase, so higher speeds need smaller resistors (bounded by sink current), smaller capacitance, or active pull-ups. Hs-mode uses a current-source pull-up to get 3.4 MHz. Beyond that, push-pull buses like SPI are more efficient.

### My MCU supports 1 MHz and my sensor supports 400 kHz. What speed do I use?
400 kHz, the slowest common value. Or if the other devices on the bus support 1 MHz, put the sensor behind a switch or on a separate bus so they can run fast.

### How long to read 16 bytes at 400 kHz?
Address+W (9), register (9), address+R (9), 16×9 (144) = 171 clocks plus START/STOP overhead (≈3): about 174 clocks, 435 µs at 400 kHz. So about 37 bytes/ms, or 37 kB/s of payload.

### Is 400 kHz really the same as 400 kbit/s?
In I2C, one bit is transferred per clock, so the bit rate equals the clock frequency. But payload throughput is lower: 1 bit in 9 is the ACK, and addresses and framing are overhead.

### What happens if I run the clock above the mode's limit?
Rise times, setup/hold and the minimum high/low times fail first. You'll see missing ACKs and corrupted bytes, often temperature- and bus-dependent. Some slaves have no problem (they only need minimum pulse widths), others reject it; datasheets list the maximum SCL.
:::
