::: basics
### In plain words
Pre-emphasis changes a bit stream in a way you can see in two pictures. In the time picture, the first bit after a change stays at full height, but repeated bits are drawn lower. In the frequency picture, the slow part of the signal is turned down while the fast part is left alone. The two pictures describe the same change. A run of identical bits is slow, so lowering it lowers the slow part.

### Picture it
Think of the bass and treble knobs on a stereo. Slow, deep sounds are low frequency, and quick, sharp sounds are high frequency. Pre-emphasis turns the bass down and leaves the treble alone. The song sounds brighter. Here, bass is the long runs of identical bits and treble is the edges where bits change. A real driver has no knob, only weighted copies of the data.

### Words you need
- **Frequency**: how fast something repeats. A long run of identical bits is slow, so it is low frequency.
- **Spectrum**: a chart of how much of the signal sits at each frequency.
- **NRZ**: a bit stream with one fixed level per bit, high for 1 and low for 0.
- **Transition**: a bit that differs from the one before it.
- **De-emphasis**: lowering repeated bits relative to transition bits ([[q0726]]).

### The simplest example
Send the bits 0, 1, 1, 1, 0 as plain NRZ, with levels of −1 and +1. Now apply 6 dB of de-emphasis. The first 1 follows a change, so it stays at 1. The next two 1s are repeats, so they drop to 0.5. The final 0 is a change, so it is back at −1. The spectrum shows the same thing: the slow, low-frequency part falls by half in height, while an alternating 1010 pattern, which is all changes, is untouched. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page draws both pictures in full and shows how the received signal improves.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can **draw and explain** what preemphasis does in both domains, and keep them consistent. The interviewer wants to see:

1. Time domain: a data waveform where each transition gets full swing and the repeated bits are lower (de-emphasis), and the received waveform with fewer long tails.
2. Frequency domain: the NRZ spectrum (a $\mathrm{sinc}^2$ envelope) multiplied by a high-pass shaped filter, so the low frequencies are reduced relative to the high frequencies.
3. The link between them: a repeated bit is low-frequency content, a transition is high-frequency content, so lowering repeated bits *is* lowering the low-frequency spectrum.
:::

::: why
This is the "show me" version of [[q0726]]. A candidate who can sketch both pictures quickly, and tie each feature in one picture to the other, shows genuine understanding of signals and filters, not just vocabulary. It's also exactly how you'd explain an eye diagram improvement on a whiteboard to a colleague.
:::

::: concepts
- **NRZ spectrum**: random NRZ data has power spectral density proportional to $\mathrm{sinc}^2(fT)$, with nulls at multiples of the bit rate. See [[q0726]].
- **FIR filter frequency response**: $|H(f)|^2=(1-a)^2+a^2-2a(1-a)\cos(2\pi fT)$ for the 2-tap case.
- **Time-frequency duality**: slow, repeated levels are low frequency, edges are high frequency.
- **Channel low-pass**: here a single pole at 1.5 GHz as a simple stand-in for a lossy channel. See [[q0722]].
- **Eye height**: the vertical opening at the best sampling point.
- **ISI**: tails from earlier bits landing on later ones.
:::

::: answer
"I'd draw four little pictures.

Time domain at the transmitter: without preemphasis a plain NRZ waveform, levels at plus and minus full swing, flat between transitions. With de-emphasis it looks the same on the first bit after every transition, full height, but if the data stays the same the next bits sit lower, at about half height for a 6 dB setting. The result is a waveform with a 'shoulder' after each edge.

Time domain at the receiver: the lossy channel rounds everything. Without preemphasis a single isolated bit barely reaches the threshold because it's too short, while a run of ones reaches full height, so the eye height is set by the worst isolated bit. With de-emphasis the run of ones is lower and the single bit is relatively higher, so all the received levels are closer to uniform and the eye is bigger.

Frequency domain: the NRZ spectrum is a sinc-squared lobe, flat near DC, falling toward the first null at the bit rate. The preemphasis filter is a high-pass shelf, down 6 dB at DC and 0 dB around the Nyquist frequency. So the transmitted spectrum has its low-frequency part pushed down and its high end relatively raised. After the channel, whose response falls with frequency, the product is flatter without preemphasis being more tilted."
:::

::: technical
**Signal.** NRZ data with bit time $T$ and levels $\pm1$. Without preemphasis $y[n]=x[n]$. With 2-tap de-emphasis $y[n]=(1-a)x[n]-a\,x[n-1]$, $a=0.25$.

**Time domain, TX.** A transition ($x[n]\ne x[n-1]$): $|y|=(1-a)+a=1$. A repeated bit: $|y|=(1-a)-a=1-2a=0.5$. So the TX waveform alternates between amplitude 1 on the first bit after a change and 0.5 after.

**Spectrum.** For random NRZ data the power spectral density is $S(f)\propto T\,\mathrm{sinc}^2(fT)$, with $\mathrm{sinc}(u)=\sin(\pi u)/(\pi u)$, first null at $f=1/T$ (10 GHz for 10 Gb/s). The FIR multiplies it by $|H(f)|^2$:
$$S_{pre}(f)=S(f)\left[(1-a)^2+a^2-2a(1-a)\cos(2\pi fT)\right]$$
At DC the bracket is $(1-2a)^2=0.25$ (−6 dB). At Nyquist ($fT=0.5$) it is 1 (0 dB).

**Channel and result.** A single-pole channel with $f_c=1.5$ GHz: $|H_{ch}|^2=1/(1+(f/f_c)^2)$ (−3 dB at 1.5 GHz, about −10.5 dB at 5 GHz).

**Eye height at the best sampling phase** (127-bit PRBS, nominal ±1 levels, so a full 2.0 span; values from a simulation I ran for this single-pole channel):

| $a$ | 0 | 0.10 | 0.15 | 0.20 | 0.25 | 0.30 | 0.35 | 0.40 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Eye height | 0.45 | 0.60 | 0.68 | 0.76 | 0.83 | 0.80 | 0.60 | 0.41 |

The optimum is near $a=0.25$ (6 dB), giving 0.83 vs 0.45: an eye 1.84 times taller (5.3 dB) than without. Past $a\approx0.3$ over-equalization eats the eye again.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The numbers apply to this specific idealized channel (single pole, 1.5 GHz, 10 Gb/s, no noise or jitter). A real channel has a different shape, so the optimum tap weight differs. The qualitative picture, an optimum at moderate boost and degradation beyond it, carries over.
:::

::: short
"Time domain: preemphasis keeps full swing on the first bit after a transition and lowers the following repeated bits, a shoulder after each edge. Frequency domain: the NRZ sinc-squared spectrum is multiplied by a high-pass shape, so DC drops by 6 dB while the Nyquist region stays put. Since the channel is a low-pass, the two combine to a flatter overall response, and at the receiver the isolated bits and long runs reach more similar levels, which opens the eye."
:::

::: long
"I'd sketch it as two columns, time on the left and frequency on the right, and two rows: before the channel and after.

Top left, transmitter without preemphasis: an NRZ bit stream, plus and minus levels with flat tops. Top right: the spectrum, a sinc-squared envelope, flat up to a fraction of the bit rate, falling toward the first null at the bit rate.

Now add preemphasis. In the time domain I keep the first bit after each transition at full amplitude and lower the bits that repeat, so each edge has a small shoulder. For 6 dB of de-emphasis the repeated bits are at half amplitude. In the spectrum, all the repeated bits are low-frequency energy, since a long run of ones is nearly DC, so the low-frequency part of the spectrum drops 6 dB while the Nyquist region, where edges alternate, stays. So the spectrum is the original multiplied by a shelf that rises from minus 6 dB at DC to 0 dB at Nyquist.

Second row, after the channel. The channel is low-pass: in the spectrum its roll-off multiplies the TX spectrum; in the time domain it rounds every edge. Without preemphasis the received spectrum is heavily tilted, strong at DC, weak near Nyquist, and in time a lone bit never reaches the level of a long run, so the eye is only about 23% of full swing in my example. With preemphasis the TX shelf and the channel roll-off cancel, so the received spectrum is flatter, and in the time domain repeated bits are lower and isolated bits are higher, so all the levels are close together. The eye opens to about 42% in that example, roughly 5 dB better. And if I push the boost further than that, the first bit overshoots and the eye shrinks again, so there's an optimum."
:::

::: terms
- **NRZ**: non-return-to-zero signalling, one level per bit.
- **PSD**: power spectral density.
- **Sinc envelope**: $|\sin(\pi fT)/(\pi fT)|$, the shape of random NRZ spectrum.
- **De-emphasis**: lowering repeated bits after a transition.
- **Pre-shoot / post-cursor**: tap positions before and after the main bit.
- **Eye height**: vertical opening at the best sample point.
- **Shelf filter**: response that steps between two gain levels.
- **Over-equalization**: too much boost, which overshoots and closes the eye.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws a square wave with a bump.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws the TX time waveform with exact levels (1 after a transition, $1-2a$ for repeated bits) and explains why.
- Draws the spectrum as a $\mathrm{sinc}^2$ envelope multiplied by a shelf: $-6$ dB at DC to 0 dB at Nyquist for $a=0.25$.
- Connects both pictures: repeated bits are low-frequency content, so reducing them reduces the low-frequency spectrum.
- Shows the received picture: with preemphasis, the tail and plateau levels are better balanced and the eye is taller.
- Notes the optimum and the over-equalization penalty, and that 1010 clock-like patterns are unaffected while long runs are reduced.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing the boost on a long run of bits.** Preemphasis acts on transitions. A long run of identical bits settles to the *lowered* level.
- **Drawing the spectrum as growing without limit.** The filter is periodic in $1/T$ and the NRZ envelope still has a sinc roll-off and nulls at the bit rate.
- **Forgetting the DC drop.** Preemphasis in a fixed-swing driver *lowers* the low-frequency level, it doesn't raise the high end.
- **Showing the received eye bigger in amplitude.** The boost improves *relative* levels (less ISI). Absolute amplitude at the receiver is lower at low frequency.
- **Mixing dB conventions.** Amplitude ratio $0.5$ is $-6$ dB; power ratio $0.25$ is also $-6$ dB. Make sure axes are labelled.
- **Ignoring nulls.** The NRZ spectrum has nulls at multiples of the bit rate; the FIR doesn't remove them.
:::

## Tying it together

Consider pure patterns, where the spectrum is a line spectrum, not a continuum:

- **1010… (clock-like, period 2T)**: a fundamental at the Nyquist frequency (5 GHz) with odd harmonics. The FIR's gain at Nyquist is 1, so these patterns pass unchanged.
- **1111… (a long run)**: pure DC. The FIR gain is $1-2a=0.5$, so the level halves.
- **11001100…** (period 4T, fundamental at 2.5 GHz): gain $|H|$ at $fT=0.25$ is $\sqrt{(0.75)^2+(0.25)^2}=0.79$, so about −2 dB relative to the Nyquist pattern.

So the filter treats the data pattern differently depending on its frequency content, which is exactly why a complicated spectrum (random data) is best explained by the envelope picture. It also explains why the worst ISI patterns are a single 1 in a long run of 0s (energy at many frequencies, with the DC component only partially delivered), and why a PRBS test pattern is used for link testing: it contains all of them.

## Sketching it on a whiteboard in a minute

If you only have a minute and a marker, this is the sequence that works:

1. **Draw the bit pattern first** (for example 0 0 1 0 1 1 1 0 0 1) as a row of cells, and mark which bits are transitions. Everything else follows from that row.
2. **Plain NRZ on one line**: every cell is a full-height level.
3. **De-emphasized NRZ on the next line**: cells that follow a change are full height, cells that repeat are drawn at half height (for 6 dB). Label the two levels $1$ and $1-2a$.
4. **Received waveforms**: draw exponentials between levels. In the plain case, show the isolated 1 failing to reach the threshold margin; in the de-emphasized case, show the levels bunched together.
5. **Spectrum**: draw a sinc-squared bump with its first null at the bit rate. Overlay the same bump with the left side lowered by 6 dB, and say "DC down, Nyquist unchanged".
6. **Say the sentence that ties them together**: a run of identical bits is low frequency, and de-emphasis lowers it; an edge is high frequency, and de-emphasis keeps it.

The interviewer is usually checking one thing: that the spectrum sketch and the time sketch agree. If your time-domain picture has the repeated bits lowered, your spectrum must have the low frequencies lowered. If you draw boosted edges in time but a lowered DC in frequency, you should be able to say why that's the same thing (the same filter, referenced to a different level).

::: applications
- **Explaining eye-diagram improvements** during link bring-up: lowering the DC level while keeping edges.
- **SerDes TX tuning** (PCIe, USB, SATA, Ethernet): choose presets and verify with the scope.
- **Spectrum analyzer checks of EMI**: a boosted TX raises the spectrum above Nyquist, which matters for emissions compliance.
- **Audio and FM**: the same spectral reshaping at the transmitter, undone at the receiver.
- **Test patterns**: PRBS patterns and clock patterns reveal different ISI because they stress different parts of the spectrum.

::: assumptions
- **Random NRZ data**, so the continuous $\mathrm{sinc}^2$ envelope applies. Real data with encoding (8b/10b, 128b/130b) has similar envelopes but different low-frequency content (DC balance).
- **Single-pole channel** at 1.5 GHz is an illustration. Real channels have steeper loss, reflections, and group-delay variation.
- **2-tap post-cursor filter** with no pre-cursor tap.
- **No noise or jitter** in the time-domain figures. With noise, the optimum boost is lower.
- **Eye heights** are for ideal sampling at the best phase and an exhaustive PRBS-7 pattern.
- **The answer changes** with PAM4 signalling (3 eyes, different weights) or return-to-zero formats.

::: followups
### Why does preemphasis not change the peak amplitude of the waveform?
The transition bits are driven at $(1-a)+a=1$, the same peak as the plain waveform. Only the repeated bits are lowered. So the peak-to-peak swing and the driver's headroom are unchanged. That's why the filter is implemented as de-emphasis within a fixed-swing driver.

### What does the spectrum of 1010... look like with and without preemphasis?
A line at the Nyquist frequency plus odd harmonics, in both cases. The filter gain at Nyquist is 1, so amplitudes are unchanged. The difference only shows for patterns with lower-frequency content.

### Why does the TX spectrum with preemphasis still have a null at the bit rate?
The nulls come from the NRZ pulse shape ($\mathrm{sinc}$ of a rectangular bit), and the FIR is just a multiplier. At $f=1/T$, $\mathrm{sinc}=0$, so the product is zero whatever the filter does.

### How would you verify the effect on a bench?
Capture the TX output at the pins with a high-bandwidth scope in a PRBS pattern: you should see the shoulder. On a spectrum analyzer or the scope's FFT you'd see the low-frequency part reduced relative to the Nyquist region. Then probe at the far end of the channel and compare eye diagrams with preemphasis off and on.

### Harder: why would the optimum boost with noise present be less than the no-noise optimum?
The received noise is mostly added after the channel and isn't changed by TX boost, but the *signal* at low frequency is reduced by $1-2a$, so SNR on low-frequency content falls. At higher boost the ISI gains are smaller than the SNR losses, shifting the optimum to lower $a$. This is why training chooses a setting based on the actual BER or eye, not the zero-noise ISI minimum.

### Scenario: your received eye looks good with preemphasis on a short test cable but terrible on the real long cable. Same setting. Why?
The test cable has little loss, so the preemphasis over-equalizes it: you're adding more boost than the channel takes away, which causes overshoot on the first bit and hurts the eye. Interesting point: the "good" short cable result may just be that the receiver tolerates it. The right setting depends on the channel, which is why standards use link training.
:::
