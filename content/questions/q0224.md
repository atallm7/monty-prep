::: testing
Whether you can take the basic divider ([[q0223]]) and reason about its **non-idealities** instead of treating resistors as magic. The interviewer wants a two-sided answer:

1. Too high: the FB pin's bias current, leakage, noise pickup and the pole formed with pin capacitance all start to matter, hurting accuracy, noise and phase margin.
2. Too low: the divider itself wastes current, hurting light-load efficiency and battery life.
3. A sensible design range and how you'd pick within it, using numbers.
:::

::: why
Feedback divider values look arbitrary until you have debugged a rail that is 7% high because someone used 4.5 MΩ for a "low power" design, or a coin-cell device that dies early because the divider draws 600 µA. Datasheets often print a recommended maximum for $R_2$ or for $R_1\parallel R_2$ for exactly these reasons. This is the same loading logic as [[q0008]], applied to a real IC pin.
:::

::: concepts
- **Divider with a load**: the FB pin draws bias current $I_{FB}$ and has capacitance; it is not an ideal infinite-impedance input. See [[q0008]].
- **Thevenin resistance of the divider**: $R_{th}=R_1\parallel R_2$, the impedance the FB pin sees. See [[q0149]].
- **Quiescent current of the divider**: $V_{out}/(R_1+R_2)$, drawn forever. See [[q0230]].
- **RC pole**: $R_{th}$ with the FB-pin and stray capacitance gives $f_p=1/(2\pi R_{th}C_{par})$ inside the loop.
- **Feed-forward capacitor interaction**: $C_{ff}$ with $R_1$ sets a zero that moves with resistor scale. See [[q0250]].
- **Thermal and noise**: resistor noise voltage $\sqrt{4kTR}$ grows with $\sqrt{R}$.
:::

::: answer
"The divider has to balance two things. If the resistance is too high, the feedback pin is no longer ideal. The pin draws a little bias current, and that flows through the top resistor and shifts the output, so the error is $I_{FB}\cdot R_1$. Leakage on the board or flux residue across the bottom resistor does the same. The node is also high impedance, so it picks up noise from the switch node, and the resistance forms a low-pass pole with the pin and stray capacitance. That pole can land near the loop crossover and eat phase margin, so the converter can ring or oscillate.

If the resistance is too low, the divider itself becomes a load. At 3.3 V, a 5.5 kΩ divider draws 600 microamps all the time. That barely matters at an amp of load, but at light load or on a battery it can be a big fraction of the current and ruins efficiency in PFM or sleep.

So I'd pick the divider in the tens to a few hundred kΩ range, 10 k to 100 k for $R_2$ is typical, check the datasheet's recommended maximum, and use 1% or better resistors, kept close to the FB pin."
:::

::: technical
**Too high.** Let $R_{th}=R_1\parallel R_2$.
- *Bias-current error.* The FB pin sinks $I_{FB}$, which flows through $R_1$: $\Delta V_{out}\approx I_{FB}R_1$. With $I_{FB}=50$ nA (assumed; parts vary from pA to hundreds of nA), $R_1=45.3$ kΩ gives 2.3 mV (0.07%), but $R_1=4.53$ MΩ gives 227 mV (6.8%).
- *Leakage.* A parasitic path across $R_2$, for example 10 MΩ of contamination, scales the error roughly as $R_2/R_{leak}$: 0.1% for $R_2=10$ kΩ, 10% for $R_2=1$ MΩ.
- *Pole.* With $C_{par}=5$ pF: $R_{th}=8.2$ kΩ gives 3.9 MHz; $R_{th}=819$ kΩ gives 39 kHz, inside a typical loop bandwidth, adding phase lag.
- *Noise.* Resistor noise $\sqrt{4kTR_{th}}$: 11.6 nV/√Hz at 8.2 kΩ, 116 nV/√Hz at 819 kΩ, plus more pickup from the switch node.

**Too low.** Divider current $I_{div}=V_{out}/(R_1+R_2)$. For $R_2=1$ kΩ, $R_1=4.53$ kΩ at 3.3 V: 597 µA, 2 mW. At a 1 mA load that is 60% of the output current (or a 37% efficiency penalty); for $R_2=100$ kΩ it is 6 µA.

**Good range.** $R_2$ of about 10 to 100 kΩ for most ICs, subject to the datasheet's recommended maximum. Use the lower end for noise or accuracy, the upper end for light-load efficiency.

**Check list.** Compare $I_{FB}R_1$ with the accuracy budget, $V_{out}/(R_1+R_2)$ with the sleep budget, and $1/(2\pi R_{th}C_{par})$ with the crossover frequency.
:::

::: short
"Too high, and the feedback pin's bias current flows through the top resistor and shifts the output, leakage and noise pickup get worse, and the resistance plus pin capacitance makes a pole that can eat phase margin. Too low, and the divider itself draws current all the time, which hurts efficiency at light load and battery life. So you pick something in the tens to hundreds of kΩ, within the datasheet's recommendation."
:::

::: long
"There are two failure directions, so let me take them in turn.

If the divider resistance is too high, the FB pin stops looking like an ideal voltmeter. The pin has a small bias current, say tens of nanoamps. That current has to come through the top resistor, so the output shifts by $I_{FB}\times R_1$. For a 45 k top resistor that's a couple of millivolts, nothing. For 4.5 MΩ it's a couple of hundred millivolts, which is several percent. Leakage across the board, from flux residue or humidity, acts like a resistor in parallel with the bottom resistor and has the same effect. The node is also very sensitive, so noise from the inductor and switch node couples in, and the thermal noise of the resistor goes up as the square root of its value. Finally the resistance combines with pin and trace capacitance to make a pole. With a few pF and 800 kΩ that pole is near 40 kHz, which is right where a loop might cross over, and it costs phase margin and can make the supply ring or oscillate.

If it's too low, the divider is simply a load. At 3.3 V with about 5 kΩ total, it draws 600 microamps constantly. At several amps nobody notices, but at light load, in sleep mode or on a battery, that can be most of the current and it wrecks efficiency.

So I'd choose a bottom resistor in the 10 to 100 kΩ range, check the recommended maximum in the datasheet, use 1 percent or better resistors, and place them next to the FB pin with a short trace."
:::

::: terms
- **FB-pin bias current ($I_{FB}$)**: small current the error amp input draws or leaks. Typically nA-level, part dependent.
- **Thevenin resistance ($R_{th}$)**: $R_1\parallel R_2$, the source impedance seen by the FB pin.
- **Divider quiescent current**: $V_{out}/(R_1+R_2)$, a permanent load.
- **Leakage path**: unintended resistance on the PCB (contamination, humidity, solder flux) shunting a divider resistor.
- **Parasitic pole**: low-pass from $R_{th}$ and stray capacitance on the FB node.
- **Johnson (thermal) noise**: $v_n=\sqrt{4kTR}$ in V/√Hz.
- **Phase margin**: distance from -180° at crossover; extra lag in the loop reduces it.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "too high is noisy, too low wastes power".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the **error formula** $I_{FB}R_1$ and evaluates it for a low and high resistance.
- Explains the **loop-stability** effect with a number: the pole $1/(2\pi R_{th}C_{par})$ approaching crossover.
- Quantifies divider current against light-load current.
- Mentions leakage, contamination and why guard/cleanliness matters for high-value dividers.
- Names a sensible range, says to check the datasheet maximum, and points out the $C_{ff}$ interaction (zero position scales with $R_1$).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Believing higher resistance is always better because it saves power.** At the megaohm level, bias current, leakage and the pole dominate.
- **Believing lower is always better because it is more accurate and quieter.** It is, but it burns power continuously.
- **Treating the FB pin as ideal.** Even a 10 nA bias current through 4.5 MΩ is 45 mV.
- **Scaling the divider but not $C_{ff}$.** If you multiply $R_1$ by 10, the feed-forward zero moves 10× lower unless you reduce $C_{ff}$ by 10×.
- **Forgetting the divider is a *constant* load.** In a PFM/sleep design that is a first-order term in the efficiency number.
:::

::: deep
## The ideal divider and where it stops being ideal

An ideal FB pin draws no current, so $V_{out}=V_{ref}(1+R_1/R_2)$ exactly, regardless of the absolute resistor values. The ratio fixes the output, and any scale of resistors works. The two failure modes come from the real pin and the real board.

Take the running example: $V_{ref}=0.6$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, so $R_1/R_2\approx4.53$. I will scale the pair across five decades.

| $R_2$ | $R_1$ | $R_{th}$ | Divider current | $I_{FB}$ error (50 nA) | Pole with 5 pF |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 kΩ | 4.53 kΩ | 819 Ω | 597 µA | 0.01% | 38.9 MHz |
| 10 kΩ | 45.3 kΩ | 8.19 kΩ | 59.7 µA | 0.07% | 3.9 MHz |
| 100 kΩ | 453 kΩ | 81.9 kΩ | 6.0 µA | 0.68% | 389 kHz |
| 1 MΩ | 4.53 MΩ | 819 kΩ | 0.6 µA | 6.8% | 38.9 kHz |
| 10 MΩ | 45.3 MΩ | 8.19 MΩ | 0.06 µA | 68% | 3.9 kHz |

(The 1 kΩ row's pole is listed in MHz, which means it is far above anything the loop cares about. The pole column uses $C_{par}=5$ pF, an assumed value, and $I_{FB}=50$ nA, also assumed.)

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> FB-pin bias current and pin capacitance depend entirely on the IC. Some parts guarantee tens of nA, others pA. Read "FB pin current" in the electrical characteristics table and use that number, not mine.

## Too high: four separate problems

**1. Bias-current error.** If the FB pin sinks $I_{FB}$ into the IC, the current through $R_1$ is the current through $R_2$ plus $I_{FB}$:
$$V_{out}=V_{FB}+R_1\left(\frac{V_{FB}}{R_2}+I_{FB}\right)=V_{ref}\left(1+\frac{R_1}{R_2}\right)+I_{FB}R_1$$
The error is $I_{FB}R_1$ and does not care about the ratio at all. For a 1% error budget on 3.3 V (33 mV) at 50 nA, $R_1$ must be under 660 kΩ.

**2. Leakage.** Flux residue, humidity, and surface contamination create a leakage resistance in parallel with the board's resistors. With $R_{leak}=10$ MΩ across $R_2$:

| $R_2$ | Effective $R_2$ | Error in $V_{out}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 10 kΩ | 9.99 kΩ | 0.1% |
| 100 kΩ | 99.0 kΩ | 1% |
| 1 MΩ | 909 kΩ | 10% |

This is why high-impedance dividers are sensitive to cleaning, coating and moisture, and why an engineer gets a rail that "drifts when I breathe on it".

**3. Noise.** Thermal noise of the Thevenin resistance is $v_n=\sqrt{4kTR_{th}}$: 3.7 nV/√Hz at 819 Ω, 11.6 nV/√Hz at 8.2 kΩ, 116 nV/√Hz at 819 kΩ. At the FB pin this noise is *amplified* by the loop (the output follows the FB-pin noise times $1+R_1/R_2$), so a noisy FB node means a noisy rail. Added to that, switching noise coupling from the SW node through parasitic capacitance goes up in proportion to the node impedance.

**4. Phase margin.** $R_{th}$ and the capacitance at the FB pin (pin capacitance plus a few pF of trace) produce a pole $f_p=1/(2\pi R_{th}C_{par})$ that adds lag in the feedback path. With $R_{th}=819$ kΩ and 5 pF, $f_p=39$ kHz. If the loop crosses over at 50 to 100 kHz, that pole alone contributes about 52° to 69° of phase lag at crossover (arctan of $f_c/f_p$: $\arctan(100/39)=69^\circ$) and the supply may ring or oscillate. The same thing happens with $C_{ff}$: its zero $1/(2\pi R_1C_{ff})$ slides down as $R_1$ grows. With $R_1=4.53$ MΩ and 22 pF, $f_z=1.6$ kHz instead of 160 kHz, so the compensation designed around the original zero no longer works.

## Too low: the divider is a load

The divider draws $I_{div}=V_{out}/(R_1+R_2)$ continuously. At a 3.3 V output and 5.53 kΩ total, 597 µA, which is 2 mW. At a 2 A load that's 0.03% of the current, invisible. At a 1 mA light load it is 60% of the output current: input power goes up by about 60% for the same useful output. In a PFM design or in sleep mode ([[q0230]], [[q0231]]) where the whole point is single-digit µA of quiescent current, a 600 µA divider defeats the purpose. A 1 MΩ-class divider draws 0.6 µA and does not.

Low values do have benefits: they are very stiff against leakage, noise pickup and bias current, and the pole is far from any loop frequency. That is why high-current converters (where light-load efficiency doesn't matter) commonly use 1 to 10 kΩ at the bottom, and ultra-low-power parts push to hundreds of kΩ.

## Choosing in practice

1. Read the datasheet for the recommended range of $R_2$ (or maximum $R_{th}$). Many ICs say something like "keep $R_2$ below 100 kΩ" or give a table of values with the matching $C_{ff}$.
2. Compute the divider current against the minimum load current (or the sleep-mode budget). Aim for the divider to be at most a few percent of it.
3. Compute $I_{FB}R_1$ against the accuracy budget.
4. Place the resistors within a few mm of the FB pin with the top of the divider routed as a quiet Kelvin trace; keep the FB node away from the SW node and inductor, and put ground under, not signals.
5. Choose 0.1% to 1% resistors to meet your accuracy.
6. If you change the scale, rescale $C_{ff}$ inversely to keep the same zero frequency ($R_1C_{ff}$ constant), then verify phase margin on the bench.

::: applications
- **Coin-cell/IoT regulators**: 1 to 10 MΩ dividers (or integrated dividers inside the IC) to keep the quiescent current in the sub-µA range, with careful PCB cleanliness and conformal coating.
- **High-current POL converters** for FPGAs/CPUs: 1 to 10 kΩ dividers, with very precise 0.1% resistors, since efficiency at light load is not a goal.
- **Fixed-output buck ICs**: internal trimmed dividers (in the MΩ range) avoid external leakage and accuracy issues.
- **Programmable outputs**: a DAC or digital potentiometer switched into the FB divider, which has to stay within the pin's allowed source impedance.
- **Battery chargers** with an FB divider setting the termination voltage, where 0.5% accuracy of the divider matters more than a few µA.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Single FB pin** feeding a high-impedance error amp. Transconductance-type error amps and low-offset input stages have different $I_{FB}$ behaviour.
- **Example numbers** ($I_{FB}=50$ nA, $C_{par}=5$ pF) are assumptions to show magnitudes. Use the actual datasheet values.
- **Constant reference**, 0.6 V. A different $V_{ref}$ changes the ratio but not the logic.
- **Light-load figure**: I used a 1 mA load as an example threshold. In a design the relevant number is your own minimum or sleep current.
- **DC only** for the accuracy points. The noise/pole points are AC.

Alternative valid answer: an interviewer may also expect you to say a high-value divider is more susceptible to *noise pickup* (coupling), and some answers also mention that a low-value divider is more robust to everything above but, in a very small converter, can load a weak reference or bias pin in ICs that use the FB divider to also set a bias point (rare).
:::

::: followups
### A 3.3 V rail is 150 mV high on the bench but correct in simulation. The divider is 4.7 MΩ and 1 MΩ. What would you check first?
The FB-pin bias current through $R_1$: $150\text{ mV}/4.7\text{ MΩ}\approx32$ nA, a plausible $I_{FB}$ for many ICs. Then board leakage (flux residue). The simulation used an ideal FB pin. Fix by scaling the divider down by 10 to 100×.

### How would you set the divider for a coin-cell device that sleeps at 5 µA?
The divider must be well below 5 µA, say 0.3 µA, so $R_1+R_2\approx3.3\text{ V}/0.3\text{ µA}=11$ MΩ. At that size I'd check $I_{FB}$ vs $R_1$ error, add guard/clean layout, accept lower bandwidth (pole), and probably prefer a part with an internal divider.

### How does $C_{ff}$ change if I scale both resistors up by 10×?
The zero is $1/(2\pi R_1C_{ff})$, so to keep the same zero frequency $C_{ff}$ must go down by 10×. Keeping 22 pF would shift the zero down 10×, from 160 kHz to 16 kHz, where it can even lower the phase margin through the shifted pole.

### What if the interviewer says "isn't the divider current a loss in efficiency at full load too?"
Yes but negligible: 60 µA at 3.3 V is 0.2 mW against 6.6 W (3.3 V × 2 A), which is 0.003%. It only matters when the load is within a couple of orders of magnitude of the divider current.

### Could a too-low divider cause stability trouble?
Rarely directly. It moves the FB-pole very high and makes the node stiff. Indirectly, if the datasheet specifies compensation around a given $R_2$, large changes in the divider scale the feed-forward zero. And in a current-limited or very-low-power source it can change start-up behaviour.
:::

::: interpretations
"Too high resistance" and "too low resistance" refer to the **absolute size** of the divider (given the same ratio, since the ratio sets $V_{out}$). This is the same logic as loading a general divider ([[q0008]]), applied to the specific features of a regulator FB pin: bias current, capacitance, leakage, and permanent quiescent draw.
:::

::: rubric
- Says the divider ratio sets $V_{out}$ but the absolute value affects non-idealities :: Frames the question correctly.
- Too high: FB bias current through $R_1$ gives output error $I_{FB}R_1$ :: The most common concrete failure.
- Too high: leakage/noise pickup and thermal noise :: Shows board-level awareness.
- Too high: pole with pin capacitance reduces phase margin / affects stability :: Connects to the loop, the "advanced" insight.
- Too low: divider draws continuous current, hurting light-load efficiency and battery life :: The other half of the question.
- Quantifies with numbers (e.g., $V_{out}/(R_1+R_2)$ or $I_{FB}R_1$) :: Turns qualitative statements into engineering.
- Gives a practical range and mentions checking the datasheet maximum :: Design judgment.
:::
