::: testing
Whether you know **more than one way** to turn a current into a voltage and can compare them honestly. The interviewer wants to hear:

1. The standard approach: a small series sense resistor plus a current-sense amplifier.
2. The "free" approaches used in converters: inductor DCR sensing with an RC filter, MOSFET $R_{DS(on)}$ sensing, and SenseFET/mirror sensing.
3. Non-resistive methods (Hall, current transformer) and when they are used.
4. The trade-offs: accuracy, power loss, temperature drift, bandwidth, common-mode range and cost, with a number or two.
:::

::: why
Current is the second thing a power converter has to know about: for over-current protection, for current-mode control, for load telemetry and battery gauging, and for multiphase current sharing. Each sensing method has a failure mode (drift with temperature, noise from the switch node, wasted watts), and a good engineer chooses by the requirement. This question leads to [[q0226]] (where to sense) and [[q0227]] (what the loop uses it for), and connects to the sense-resistor questions elsewhere in the list.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ohm's law as a transducer**: $V=IR$ turns current into a measurable voltage across a known resistance. See [[q0001]].
- **Inductor model**: a real inductor is an ideal $L$ in series with its winding resistance DCR. See [[q0043]].
- **Differential amplification**: a current-sense amplifier rejects the large common-mode voltage and gains up the small $V_{sense}$.
- **Temperature coefficients**: copper (about +0.39%/°C) and MOSFET $R_{DS(on)}$ (roughly +0.4%/°C or more) drift; a precision shunt does not.
- **Switching noise**: a sense signal that sits on the switch node needs blanking and filtering.
- **Power vs signal trade-off**: more sense voltage means better SNR but more loss ($P=I^2R_s$).
:::

::: answer
"There are a handful of ways. The most direct is a small sense resistor in series with the current path and a current-sense amplifier that reads the voltage across it. It's accurate and temperature-stable, but it costs some power, $I^2R$, and a bit of voltage drop. If I want to avoid that, converters often use the inductor's own DC resistance: you put an RC network across the inductor, and if the time constant $RC$ equals $L/DCR$, the capacitor voltage is proportional to the inductor current. It's free in terms of loss, but DCR is only a few milliohms, it drifts with temperature by about 0.4 percent per degree, so accuracy is typically a few to 10 percent unless you compensate.

Another approach is to measure the voltage across a MOSFET while it's on, since that's $I\times R_{DS(on)}$, again free but with large temperature drift and part-to-part spread. Some ICs use a SenseFET, a small slice of the power device that carries a fixed fraction of the current, for better ratio accuracy. For isolation or very high currents you can use a Hall sensor, or a current transformer for AC and switching currents.

I'd choose by what the number is for: a precision gauge gets a shunt, a cycle-by-cycle limit in a cheap converter gets DCR or $R_{DS(on)}$ sensing."
:::

::: technical
**1. Shunt + current-sense amplifier (CSA).** $V_s=IR_s$, amplified by gain $G$: $V_{out}=G I R_s$. Loss $P=I^2R_s$. Example: $R_s=10$ mΩ at 3 A gives 30 mV, 90 mW (0.9% of a 9.9 W output). Accuracy depends on the resistor tolerance (0.1 to 1%), TCR (tens to hundreds of ppm/°C for metal-alloy parts), CSA offset (tens to hundreds of µV: 100 µV across 10 mΩ is 10 mA of error) and gain error.

**2. Inductor DCR sensing.** Place $R_s$ in series with $C_s$ across the inductor. The capacitor voltage is
$$V_C(s)=I_L(s)\,DCR\,\frac{1+sL/DCR}{1+sR_sC_s}$$
which reduces to $V_C=I_L\,DCR$ when $R_sC_s=L/DCR$. For $L=4.7$ µH and DCR = 10 mΩ, $\tau=470$ µs: for example 10 kΩ and 47 nF. Copper has a TC of about +0.39%/°C, so across +75 °C the signal grows by about 29%; an NTC in the network can compensate.

**3. $R_{DS(on)}$ sensing.** Measure $V_{DS}$ of the high-side or low-side FET during its on-time: $V=I R_{DS(on)}$. Free but $R_{DS(on)}$ rises by a large factor with temperature (see the FET's curve) and spreads part to part; needs blanking after the switching edge.

**4. SenseFET / mirror.** A small parallel cell carries $I/N$ (for example $N=5000$: 3 A gives 0.6 mA). Better ratio matching than $R_{DS(on)}$, no extra drop.

**5. Hall, fluxgate, current transformer.** Isolated and low-loss; Hall has offset and bandwidth limits, CTs only measure AC/pulsed current.
:::

::: short
"The simplest is a series sense resistor with a current-sense amplifier: accurate, but it costs $I^2R$ loss. To avoid the loss, converters sense the inductor's DCR with an RC network (time constant $L/DCR$) or the MOSFET's $R_{DS(on)}$ while it's on, or use a SenseFET mirror, but those drift with temperature. For isolation or high current you use a Hall sensor or a current transformer."
:::

::: long
"I think of it as a menu with a cost for each item.

First option is a shunt: a low-value resistor, a few milliohms, in the current path, and a current-sense amplifier that picks off the voltage across it and rejects the common-mode. It's accurate and stable with temperature if you use a good resistor, but it dissipates $I^2R$ and its drop eats into your rail. For 3 A and 10 mΩ that's 30 mV and 90 mW, about a percent of a 3.3 V output, and the amplifier's offset sets the minimum current you can resolve.

Second, inductor DCR sensing. The inductor has a few milliohms of winding resistance. If I put a resistor and capacitor in series across the inductor and choose $RC = L/DCR$, the capacitor voltage is an exact replica of the inductor current times DCR. No extra loss and it sees the whole inductor current including ripple. The catch is that DCR is a copper resistance, so it climbs by around 0.4 percent per degree, roughly 30 percent over a 75 degree rise, and the inductor tolerance matters. A thermistor in the network can correct it.

Third, FET sensing, where I measure the voltage across the switch during its on-time. That's $I\cdot R_{DS(on)}$, again no extra loss but it drifts a lot with temperature and part variation, and it has to be blanked after the switching edge. A SenseFET, which is a small mirror of the power device carrying a fixed fraction of the current, improves the matching.

For isolated measurements or very large currents there are Hall-effect sensors and current transformers. The choice depends on whether I need a precise number, as in a fuel gauge, or just a protection threshold or current-mode ramp."
:::

::: terms
- **Shunt (sense) resistor**: a precision low-value resistor, often 1 to 100 mΩ, in series with the current.
- **Current-sense amplifier (CSA)**: a differential amplifier with high common-mode range and fixed gain that reads the shunt voltage.
- **DCR**: the DC winding resistance of the inductor.
- **Kelvin (4-wire) connection**: separate force and sense traces, so lead and solder resistance do not corrupt the reading.
- **SenseFET**: a small replica of the power MOSFET that conducts a known fraction $1/N$ of its current.
- **Temperature coefficient (TCR)**: relative resistance change per °C. Copper ≈ +3900 ppm/°C.
- **Common-mode voltage**: the average voltage of the two sense terminals; for high-side sensing it can be $V_{in}$ or a switching node.
- **Blanking**: ignoring the sense signal briefly after a switching edge, while ringing settles.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "sense resistor, Hall sensor" without trade-offs.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Covers shunt + CSA, **DCR sensing with the $RC=L/DCR$ condition**, $R_{DS(on)}$ and SenseFET, and Hall/CT.
- Quantifies a shunt: $V=IR$, $P=I^2R$, and offset-limited resolution (100 µV across 10 mΩ is 10 mA).
- Knows DCR drifts about +0.39%/°C and how to compensate (NTC) and what happens if $RC\neq L/DCR$.
- Says which method suits which purpose: precision telemetry vs protection vs current-mode control.
- Mentions blanking and Kelvin connection, the practical things that make sensing actually work.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using a bigger sense resistor "for better accuracy" without checking power and drop.** At 10 A, 50 mΩ would burn 5 W.
- **Forgetting the $RC=L/DCR$ matching condition.** If $RC$ is too small, the sensed signal overshoots on steps; too large, it lags. Only at equality is it a faithful current replica.
- **Assuming $R_{DS(on)}$ sensing is accurate.** It drifts with temperature by tens of percent and varies part to part. Fine for protection, poor for metering.
- **Ignoring Kelvin routing.** A few mm of copper in the sense path can add several percent error on a 5 mΩ shunt.
- **Calling a Hall sensor "lossless and exact".** It has offset, gain drift and limited bandwidth, and costs more.
- **Ignoring common-mode range.** A CSA rated for 3.3 V cannot sit across a shunt at 24 V.
:::

::: deep
## What every method is doing

Every method converts current into a voltage: $V=I\times R_{something}$, or $V=I\times$ (a transfer ratio). The differences are *which* resistance, how it changes with temperature, how much power it wastes, and where the signal sits relative to ground and the switch node.

| Method | Resistance used | Added loss | Accuracy (typical) | Drift | Bandwidth | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Shunt + CSA | Precision resistor | $I^2R_s$ | 0.5 to 2% | Low (tens of ppm/°C, alloy) | 100s of kHz to MHz | Best accuracy; CSA offset limits low-current resolution |
| Inductor DCR | Winding copper | None | 3 to 10% (uncompensated) | +0.39%/°C | Full if $RC=L/DCR$ | Needs an RC network; sees inductor current incl. ripple |
| $R_{DS(on)}$ | FET channel | None | 10 to 20% or worse | Large (FET-dependent) | Limited by blanking | Cheap; for protection |
| SenseFET | Replica cell | Tiny | 3 to 10% | Matched with main FET | Limited by blanking | Used in peak current-mode ICs |
| Hall | Magnetic | Tiny (primary) | 1 to 3% | Offset/gain drift | 100 kHz to MHz | Isolated; costs more |
| Current transformer | Magnetic | Tiny | 1 to 3% | Core-dependent | AC only | Used in resonant and isolated converters |

(Accuracy figures are typical ranges, not guaranteed values. They depend heavily on the specific part and calibration.)

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The accuracy and drift ranges in the table are order-of-magnitude guides collected from general practice, not from a specific datasheet. Check the actual part, especially for $R_{DS(on)}$ and DCR vs temperature curves.

## Shunt resistor and amplifier

Ohm's law, plus a bit of design. Choose $R_s$ so that $V_s$ at full scale uses the amplifier input range but wastes little power.

For $I_{max}=3$ A:

| $R_s$ | $V_s$ at 3 A | Loss | Drop vs 3.3 V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 mΩ | 15 mV | 45 mW | 0.45% |
| 10 mΩ | 30 mV | 90 mW | 0.91% |
| 20 mΩ | 60 mV | 180 mW | 1.8% |

With a 50 V/V amplifier and a 10 mΩ shunt: 0.5 V per amp, so a 12-bit, 3.3 V ADC sees full scale at 6.6 A and has 1.6 mA/LSB. An amplifier input offset of 100 µV is $100\text{ µV}/10\text{ mΩ}=10$ mA, so the lowest reliable reading is tens of mA. Increasing $R_s$ to 20 mΩ halves both offset error and LSB size but doubles loss.

## Inductor DCR sensing

The inductor is really $L$ in series with $R_L=DCR$. Across it, a series $R_s$-$C_s$ network forms a low-pass for the inductor's voltage $v_L+i\,DCR$. The transfer function from inductor current to $V_C$ is

$$\frac{V_C(s)}{I_L(s)}=DCR\;\frac{1+sL/DCR}{1+sR_sC_s}$$

The zero at $L/DCR$ and pole at $R_sC_s$ cancel when $R_sC_s=L/DCR$, leaving $V_C=DCR\cdot I_L$ at all frequencies of interest.

Figure: The sensing network is in parallel with the inductor and its winding resistance. When $R_sC_s=L/DCR$, the voltage across $C_s$ (relative to $V_{out}$) tracks $DCR\cdot i_L$. The sensed voltage is referenced to $V_{out}$, so the amplifier needs a common-mode range at the output voltage.

Worked example: $L=4.7$ µH, DCR = 10 mΩ gives $\tau=470$ µs, so $R_s=10$ kΩ and $C_s=47$ nF. At 3 A the signal is 30 mV; hot (+75 °C) the same current gives $30\cdot(1+0.00393\cdot75)=38.8$ mV, a 29.5% error. A thermistor in the $R_s$ branch is the standard fix. What if the matching is off? With $R_sC_s=400$ µs instead of 470 µs, the high-frequency gain is 470/400 = 1.18× the DC gain, so a load step shows an overshoot of the sense signal. Too large an $RC$ gives a lag, which for current-mode control looks like extra delay.

## FET and SenseFET sensing

The on-state voltage $V_{DS}=I\,R_{DS(on)}$. At 3 A and 15 mΩ this is 45 mV, which is easy to measure; but the same FET at 125 °C may have 1.4 to 1.7× the resistance of the 25 °C value (read the datasheet's normalised curve), and the voltage is only valid after the switching edge settles, so the controller blanks the first 50 to 100 ns or more. A SenseFET keeps a small group of cells ($1/N$ of the area) at the same source/drain potential through an internal amplifier, so the same temperature behaviour applies to both parts of the ratio and the result is within a few percent. For $N=5000$, 3 A corresponds to 0.6 mA through a sense resistor.

## Hall effect and transformers

A Hall-effect sensor measures the magnetic field around a conductor, providing isolation and negligible insertion loss (the primary conductor may be tens of µΩ), with limited accuracy (offset and gain drift) and bandwidth. A current transformer scales an AC or pulsed current by the turns ratio, which is why they're popular in isolated and resonant converters, but they cannot measure DC (the core would saturate). With a 100:1 CT and a 10 Ω burden resistor, 3 A pulses give 0.3 V.

## Why the Kelvin connection matters

Copper is not a conductor at the milliohm level. One ounce copper has a sheet resistance of about 0.5 mΩ per square, so a 5 mm stretch of 0.3 mm trace (17 squares) is about 8 mΩ, nearly as much as the 10 mΩ shunt itself. If the amplifier reads the voltage across the shunt *plus* that trace, the error is close to 80%. The fix is to bring two thin sense traces straight from the shunt's own pads, carrying no load current, to the amplifier inputs, and to keep the pair routed together so they pick up the same noise. The same argument applies to DCR sensing: the RC network should connect directly at the inductor pads.

::: applications
- **Battery and fuel gauges**: shunt + dedicated amplifier/ADC (coulomb counting needs low offset). See [[q0226]].
- **Peak current-mode buck ICs**: SenseFET or $R_{DS(on)}$ sensing on the high-side switch, with leading-edge blanking, for cycle-by-cycle limiting.
- **CPU/GPU VRMs**: inductor DCR sensing with an NTC for temperature compensation, for load-line and phase-current balancing across many phases.
- **Motor drives and e-bikes**: low-side shunts in the phase legs read by an amplifier + ADC, or Hall sensors where isolation is needed.
- **Server hot-swap and power-path controllers**: high-side shunt with a high common-mode CSA for current limit and telemetry.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Buck converter context.** The current of interest is the inductor current or the load current. Other topologies and isolated converters shift which method is common.
- **Typical numbers**: DCR ≈ 10 mΩ, $R_s$ ≈ 10 mΩ, copper TC +0.39%/°C. Real inductors range from sub-mΩ to tens of mΩ.
- **Linear inductor.** DCR sensing assumes $L$ is constant; if the inductor saturates, $L/DCR$ changes and the replica distorts at high current.
- **Accuracy figures** are generic. A calibrated, temperature-compensated DCR scheme can reach about 1 to 3%, while an uncompensated one can be much worse.
:::

::: followups
### Why does DCR sensing need $R_sC_s = L/DCR$?
The inductor voltage is $L\,di/dt + DCR\cdot i$. The RC filter integrates it: $V_C=\frac{1}{1+sR_sC_s}\left(sL+DCR\right)I$. If $R_sC_s=L/DCR$, the factor $(sL+DCR)=DCR(1+sL/DCR)$ cancels the denominator, so $V_C=DCR\cdot I$. Otherwise the $L\,di/dt$ part is not exactly removed and the response has a spike or lag.

### How would you sense a 30 A rail with a shunt?
At 30 A a 10 mΩ shunt dissipates 9 W, too much. A 1 mΩ shunt gives 30 mV and 0.9 W, which is workable with a good Kelvin layout and a low-offset CSA (gain ~100). Or use Hall/DCR sensing. I'd also watch the temperature rise and TCR of the shunt itself.

### What if the sense signal is noisy right after the high-side switches on?
That's the leading-edge spike from the gate and $C_{oss}$/reverse-recovery current. Add leading-edge blanking (the controller ignores the sense for the first 50 to 100 ns) and/or a small RC filter. It's a classic problem with peak current-mode control.

### Why not just use a really large sense resistor to get a big signal?
Because the loss $I^2R_s$ and the voltage drop scale with $R_s$. At 3 A, 100 mΩ gives 300 mV (9% of 3.3 V) and 0.9 W. The right size trades off SNR against loss; and with a low-offset CSA, you can afford a small shunt.

### Your DCR-sensed current reads 25% high after the board warms up. Why?
Copper's resistance rises about 0.39% per °C, so a 65 °C rise gives about +25%. The controller thinks the current is higher. Add an NTC thermistor in the sense network, or use a controller with built-in DCR temperature compensation.
:::

::: interpretations
"Output current" can mean either the **inductor current** (used inside the converter, ripple and all) or the **load current** (average, delivered to the load). Most methods above sense inductor current; averaging it or placing a shunt in series with the load gives the load current. [[q0226]] discusses which location is right for which purpose.
:::
