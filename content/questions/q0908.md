::: basics
### In plain words
Program run time equals the number of instructions, times the average clock cycles per instruction (CPI), times the length of one clock cycle. Every way of making a CPU faster attacks one of those three. You can shorten the cycle, keep the hardware busy so each instruction needs fewer cycles, or make each instruction do more work.

### Picture it
Picture a bakery making 1,000 rolls. Total time is rolls times minutes per roll. A bigger tray lets one action handle several rolls, which is fewer instructions. Keeping every oven and worker busy is a lower CPI. A hotter oven is a faster clock, but it burns much more fuel. The slowest part is often waiting for flour to arrive, like a CPU waiting on memory.

### Words you need
- **Instruction count**: how many instructions the program needs.
- **CPI**: average clock cycles per instruction. Its inverse is IPC, instructions per cycle ([[q0918]]).
- **Clock period**: the time of one cycle, which is 1 divided by the clock frequency.
- **Cache miss**: the data wanted isn't in the small fast memory, so the CPU waits for slow main memory.

### The simplest example
Say the ideal CPI is 1, but 30% of instructions access memory, 5% of those accesses miss the cache, and each miss costs 100 cycles. Then CPI = $1 + 0.3 \times 0.05 \times 100 = 2.5$. The CPU is idle more than half the time, waiting on memory. Cut the miss rate to 2% and CPI becomes $1 + 0.3 \times 0.02 \times 100 = 1.6$. That is 2.5 / 1.6, about 1.56 times faster, with no change to the clock or the ALU.

That is the whole idea: find which factor is the bottleneck. The rest of this page covers each lever, its power and area cost, how more cores are limited by the serial part of a program, and why to measure first.
:::

::: testing
Whether you have a **framework** for performance, not a random list. The best answer starts from the iron law, $T=\text{IC}\times\text{CPI}\times t_{clk}$, and shows which technique attacks which term:

1. Raise clock frequency (shorter stages, better process) at a power cost.
2. Lower CPI: pipelining, superscalar issue, out-of-order execution, branch prediction, caches and prefetching.
3. Lower instruction count: better ISA, compiler and SIMD.
4. Add parallelism: SIMD, multithreading, multicore, accelerators.
Then the trade-offs: power ($CV^2f$), area, memory bandwidth, and Amdahl's law.
:::

::: why
CPU architects, SoC designers and firmware engineers all have to answer "how do we make this faster?" and the right answer depends on where time actually goes. Clock speed stopped scaling around the mid-2000s because of power, so most modern gains come from parallelism and memory-system improvements. Showing that you understand why (and can quantify it) separates someone who has read a spec sheet from someone who can reason about a bottleneck. This is also a bridge to the memory and power/performance/thermal questions later in the list.
:::

::: answer
"I'd start from the performance equation: execution time equals instruction count times cycles per instruction times clock period. Every technique attacks one of those.

To reduce the clock period you shorten the logic per stage, mainly by deeper pipelining, plus better process technology, but frequency costs power roughly cubically because voltage rises with frequency, so it's mostly capped.

To reduce CPI you keep the pipeline busy. Pipelining gets you toward one instruction per cycle; superscalar designs issue several per cycle; out-of-order execution reorders around dependencies and cache misses; branch prediction avoids flushing the pipeline; and caches, prefetchers and bigger buffers cut the time waiting for memory, which is often the biggest part of CPI.

To reduce instruction count you use a good compiler and ISA features like SIMD or fused instructions, so one instruction does more work.

Then there's parallelism beyond one instruction stream: SIMD or vector units, multithreading like SMT, multiple cores, and specialized accelerators.

The trade-offs are power and area, since wider cores and bigger predictors cost energy, memory bandwidth, which doesn't scale as fast as compute, and Amdahl's law, since only the parallel portion speeds up. So I'd profile first to see whether the workload is compute-bound or memory-bound, then pick the technique."
:::

::: technical
**Iron law.** $T=\frac{\text{IC}\times\text{CPI}}{f}$. Throughput in instructions per second $=\text{IPC}\times f$.

**Frequency.** Limited by $T\ge t_{clk\to Q}+t_{logic}+t_{su}$; deeper pipelines shorten $t_{logic}$ but raise register overhead, branch-misprediction penalty and power. Dynamic power $P=\alpha CV^2f$ and $V$ must rise with $f$, so power grows roughly as $f^3$ (e.g. raising frequency by 20% costs about $1.2^3=1.73\times$ power if voltage scales proportionally).

**CPI reduction.**
- Pipelining: CPI from $\sim5$ (multi-cycle) toward 1 ([[q0906]]).
- Superscalar issue width $W$: ideal CPI $=1/W$ (real IPC much lower: dependencies, misses and branches limit it).
- Out-of-order + register renaming + larger window: hides latency of cache misses and dependences.
- Branch prediction: CPI penalty $b\cdot m\cdot P$ (e.g. $0.2\times0.05\times15=0.15$).
- Memory: CPI $=\text{CPI}_{ideal}+f_{mem}\cdot m\cdot P_{miss}$. With $f_{mem}=0.3$, miss rate $m=5\%$, penalty 100 cycles: $\text{CPI}=1+0.3\times0.05\times100=2.5$. Improving to 2% misses gives 1.6 (a 36% reduction in time); AMAT goes from 6 to 3 cycles. A two-level hierarchy (L1 miss 5%, L2 hit 10 cycles, local L2 miss rate 20%, DRAM 100) gives $\text{AMAT}=1+0.05(10+0.2\times100)=2.5$ cycles.

**Instruction count.** SIMD: a 128-bit vector op processes four 32-bit floats in one instruction; compilers vectorize loops; fused multiply-add and addressing modes reduce instruction count.

**Parallelism.** SMT shares execution units between threads to fill idle slots; multicore adds cores. Amdahl: with parallel fraction $p$ and $N$ cores $S=1/((1-p)+p/N)$; $p=0.95$, $N=8$ gives 5.93; $p=0.8$ gives only 3.33.

**Specialization.** Fixed-function or programmable accelerators raise performance per watt by orders of magnitude on specific workloads ([[q0907]]).
:::

Beyond that, add parallelism with multithreading, multiple cores and accelerators. The limits are power, memory bandwidth and Amdahl's law."

::: long
"I'd organize the answer around the performance equation: execution time equals instruction count times cycles per instruction times the clock period. Each family of techniques attacks one term.

For the clock period, you can shorten the logic in each pipeline stage by pipelining deeper, and use a faster process. But frequency isn't free: higher frequency needs higher voltage, and power goes roughly as frequency cubed, which is why clocks flattened out near 3 to 5 GHz.

For CPI, the idea is to keep every part of the machine busy. A basic pipeline gets CPI close to one. A superscalar core issues several instructions per cycle, and out-of-order execution lets later independent instructions run while an earlier one waits on a cache miss. Branch prediction avoids flushing the pipeline, which matters a lot in a deep pipeline: with 20% branches, a 5% mispredict rate and a 15-cycle penalty you lose 0.15 CPI. And the memory system is usually the biggest piece, so caches, prefetchers and more memory-level parallelism: with 30% memory instructions, a 5% miss rate and 100-cycle miss penalty, CPI is 2.5 instead of 1; getting to 2% misses brings it to 1.6.

For instruction count, better compilers and ISA features help, particularly SIMD, where one instruction does four or eight operations.

Then there's thread- and data-level parallelism: SIMD and vector units, simultaneous multithreading to fill idle execution slots, multiple cores, and accelerators for specific tasks like graphics or ML.

All of this runs into limits. Power and thermal limits cap frequency and width. Memory bandwidth grows more slowly than compute. And Amdahl's law says that if only 80% of the program is parallel, eight cores give you 3.3 times, not eight. So the first step is always to profile and find the real bottleneck, whether that's compute, branches, memory latency or bandwidth, and then choose the technique that addresses it."
:::

::: terms
- **Iron law of performance**: $T=\text{IC}\times\text{CPI}\times t_{clk}$.
- **IPC / CPI**: instructions per cycle / cycles per instruction.
- **Superscalar**: issues multiple instructions per cycle.
- **Out-of-order (OoO) execution**: executes instructions as operands become ready, not in program order.
- **ILP / DLP / TLP**: instruction-, data-, and thread-level parallelism.
- **SIMD**: single instruction operating on a vector of data elements.
- **SMT (simultaneous multithreading)**: multiple threads sharing one core's execution units.
- **AMAT**: average memory access time $=t_{hit}+m\cdot t_{miss}$.
- **Amdahl's law**: speed-up limited by the non-parallel fraction.
- **Memory wall**: growing gap between CPU speed and memory latency/bandwidth.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "higher clock, more cores, more cache".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames everything with the iron law and maps each technique to IC, CPI or clock.
- Quantifies at least two: the memory CPI example, branch penalty, cubic power, or Amdahl.
- Explains why frequency scaling stalled (power wall) and why wide, out-of-order, multicore designs arrived.
- Distinguishes latency-oriented (single-thread) from throughput-oriented (many-thread) designs, e.g. CPU vs GPU.
- Recommends profiling first and identifies compute-bound vs memory-bound code.
- Mentions energy: the best throughput per watt, not just raw speed.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Just raise the clock."** Power rises about with $f^3$ and the memory system does not speed up, so performance gains are sub-linear.
- **"More cores = proportionally faster."** Only the parallel fraction scales (Amdahl), plus synchronization and bandwidth limits.
- **Ignoring memory.** Most real-world CPI is dominated by cache misses; a bigger ALU doesn't help a memory-bound workload.
- **Confusing throughput with latency.** Pipelining, multithreading and GPUs improve throughput; single-task latency may not improve.
- **Treating IPC as the metric.** Performance is IPC times frequency; a higher-IPC core at lower clock can lose.
- **Believing superscalar width scales indefinitely.** Dependencies, branches and register-file/bypass complexity limit useful width; returns diminish past 4 to 6 wide for typical code.
:::

::: deep
## The equation and where each term lives

$$T=\text{IC}\times\text{CPI}\times t_{clk}$$

| Term | Determined by | Techniques |
|---|---|---|
| Instruction count (IC) | Algorithm, compiler, ISA | Better algorithms, compiler optimization, SIMD, fused ops, complex addressing modes |
| CPI | Microarchitecture, memory system | Pipelining, superscalar, out-of-order, branch prediction, caches, prefetch, forwarding |
| Clock period $t_{clk}$ | Circuit design, process, pipeline depth | Deeper pipelines, faster transistors, better circuit design, lower wire delay |

The techniques interact: deeper pipelines raise frequency but increase the branch penalty (CPI), and wider issue lowers CPI but lengthens critical paths in the scheduler and bypass network (clock period).

## The memory term usually dominates

$$\text{CPI}=\text{CPI}_{core}+f_{mem}\cdot m\cdot P_{miss}$$

## Pipelining and the frequency lever

Pipelining shortens $t_{clk}$: with $n$ equal stages and register overhead $t_o$, $t_{clk}\approx t_{logic}/n+t_o$. Doubling the stages doesn't double the frequency because $t_o$ stays: with $t_{logic}=900$ ps and $t_o=50$ ps, $n=5$ gives 230 ps, $n=10$ gives 140 ps, a 1.64x frequency gain for 2x the stages. Branch penalty grows with depth, so CPI rises. This is the trade that ended the clock-speed race: power at 4+ GHz with high voltage and deep pipelines grew faster than performance.

Frequency and power: with $V\propto f$ in the DVFS range, $P\propto f^3$. 20% more frequency means $1.2^3=1.73\times$ the power for $\le1.2\times$ performance.

## Parallelism within one thread: superscalar and out-of-order

A $W$-wide superscalar core can retire up to $W$ instructions per cycle if enough independent instructions are available. Real code has dependencies, branches every five or six instructions and cache misses, so average IPC is far below $W$: roughly 1 to 3 on a 4 to 6 wide core for typical integer code (workload dependent). Out-of-order execution with a large instruction window (reorder buffer of a few hundred entries) extracts more parallelism and overlaps cache misses (memory-level parallelism). The cost: the scheduling, renaming and bypass hardware scale superlinearly with width and window size, so area and power rise much faster than performance.

## Data and thread parallelism

- **SIMD/vector** (NEON, AVX, RISC-V V): one instruction operates on 4 to 16 elements. Great on regular loops (signal processing, graphics, ML) with a modest area cost.
- **SMT**: two or more threads share a core. When one stalls on a miss the other fills the execution slots; typical throughput gains are modest, on the order of tens of percent (workload dependent).
- **Multicore**: independent threads on independent cores. Amdahl: parallel fraction $p=0.95$ on 8 cores gives $1/(0.05+0.95/8)=5.93\times$; $p=0.8$ gives $3.33\times$; and no number of cores gets beyond $1/(1-p)=20\times$ or $5\times$ respectively.
- **Accelerators and GPUs**: thousands of simple lanes for throughput-oriented work ([[q0907]], [[q0899]]).

## Instruction count: compilers and ISA

A compiler optimization like loop unrolling or vectorization removes instructions executed per useful operation. An ISA with a fused multiply-add executes in one instruction what would take two. Counter-intuitively, a lower instruction count can raise CPI (complex instructions take more cycles), which is the RISC versus CISC discussion ([[q0917]]). The product matters, not either factor alone ([[q0918]]).

## The physical limits

- **Power wall**: $P=\alpha CV^2f$ plus leakage; chips must stay within a thermal budget (tens to over a hundred watts for desktop parts, a few watts sustained in a phone), so frequency and active core count are limited (dark silicon).
- **Memory wall**: DRAM latency has improved little compared with core speed; a miss costs on the order of 100 or more cycles at GHz clocks. Bandwidth per core is limited by pins and channels.
- **ILP wall**: limited independent instructions in typical code.
- **Wire delay**: at advanced nodes, wire resistance and capacitance limit how far a signal travels in a cycle, so large monolithic structures (huge register files, wide bypass) get slower.

## Choosing the lever: a decision recipe

1. **Measure.** Use performance counters: IPC, cache miss rates, branch mispredict rates, stalled cycles.
2. If **memory-bound** (low IPC, high miss rate): improve locality, add cache or prefetch, restructure data, raise bandwidth.
3. If **branch-bound**: better predictor, restructure code to avoid unpredictable branches, use conditional moves.
4. If **compute-bound and parallel**: vectorize, add cores or move to an accelerator.
5. If **serial and compute-bound**: higher frequency or a wider core, accepting the power cost.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> End with "it depends on the bottleneck, so I'd profile first" and give the recipe above. Interviewers like candidates who match the technique to the measured problem rather than naming the biggest hammer.

::: applications
- **Desktop/server CPUs**: wide out-of-order cores, big caches, SMT and many cores.
- **Phone SoCs**: big.LITTLE with DVFS and accelerators, tuned for performance per watt.
- **GPUs and AI accelerators**: throughput-first designs with thousands of lanes and high-bandwidth memory.
- **Embedded MCUs**: instruction caches and flash prefetch buffers (ART-style accelerators) to hide slow flash.
- **DSPs**: VLIW and SIMD datapaths with zero-overhead loops for high throughput on regular loops.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Throughput is read as instructions per second (or work per second) of a CPU**, not only single-thread latency.
- **Typical parameter values** (30% memory ops, 100-cycle penalty, 20% branches) are textbook-style examples, not measurements.
- **Power scaling $\propto f^3$** assumes voltage scales with frequency in the DVFS range; near the voltage floor power scales closer to linearly with $f$, and leakage adds a constant.
- **IPC ranges** for superscalar cores are workload dependent. Specific numbers vary a lot by program.
- **Amdahl** ignores synchronization overhead and assumes perfectly divisible parallel work.
:::

::: followups
### Why did clock frequencies stop increasing around 4 to 5 GHz?
Power density. Dynamic power grows roughly as $f^3$ when voltage scales with frequency, and leakage became significant at small feature sizes; chips hit cooling limits. Designers shifted to multicore, wider cores, better caches and specialization.

### What is the difference between throughput and latency optimizations?
Latency optimizations make a single task finish sooner (higher clock, out-of-order, prediction, caches). Throughput optimizations complete more tasks per second (pipelines, multicore, SMT, GPUs), possibly with longer individual latency. CPUs emphasize latency; GPUs emphasize throughput.

### How would you tell whether a program is memory-bound or compute-bound?
Look at performance counters: low IPC with a high last-level cache miss rate and many stalled cycles points to memory-bound. High IPC near the issue width and execution units saturated points to compute-bound. A roofline analysis compares arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte) to the machine's balance.

### Does SIMD change the instruction count or the CPI?
It reduces instruction count (one instruction processes several elements) while CPI per instruction may be the same or slightly higher; the overall time drops when the loop is vectorizable and not memory-bound.

### If you could double only one thing (cores, cache size or clock frequency) for a mixed workload, what would you choose and why?
It depends on a profile. For a memory-bound, mostly serial workload I'd double cache size or improve the memory system, since the cores are idle. For a highly parallel workload I'd add cores (subject to Amdahl). Frequency scaling gets the least return per watt. I'd also check the power budget before committing.
