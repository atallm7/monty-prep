::: testing
Whether you understand that **a measurement is part of the circuit**, and that the probe's ground connection is a real inductor. The interviewer wants: a long ground lead plus the probe's input capacitance forms an LC circuit that rings and distorts fast edges and ripple measurements; a short ground (spring tip or a nearby ground pad) removes it; ground points also make bring-up, automated test and ESD/EMC debugging possible. Good answers include a number for the ringing frequency and mention design-for-test practices (ground test points beside every important test point).
:::

::: why
Many engineers have chased a "noisy" rail or a "ringing" switch node that was mostly their probe. Putting a ground point within a few millimetres of each important test point is a cheap layout step that makes every later measurement trustworthy and fast. It is also a very common practical interview question ("how would you measure the ripple on that rail?"), and it ties grounding theory to real debugging. See also [[q0508]] for decoupling-related measurements.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ground lead inductance**: roughly 1 nH per mm of wire or trace. See [[q0485]], [[q0509]].
- **Probe input capacitance**: a passive 10× probe is typically on the order of 10 pF at the tip. See [[q0022]].
- **Series RLC ringing**: $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, $\zeta=\tfrac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$. See [[q0014]].
- **Loop area pickup**: the probe tip and its ground lead form a loop that picks up magnetic fields. See [[q0484]].
- **Design for test**: test points, ground pads, access for probes and fixtures. See [[q0508]].
- **Ground reference of the instrument**: the scope ground is tied to earth, which creates ground loops. See [[q0493]].
:::

::: answer
"Ground probe points matter because the probe's ground connection is part of the circuit I'm measuring, and a long one gives wrong answers.

A normal oscilloscope probe has a ground clip lead about 15 centimetres long, and that's roughly 100 to 200 nanohenries. The probe tip has about 10 picofarads of capacitance. Together they form a series LC circuit: with 150 nanohenries and 10 picofarads the resonant frequency is about 130 MHz. A fast edge, like a switching node or a clock, excites it, and I see ringing and overshoot on the screen that isn't really on the board. The same loop also picks up magnetic fields from nearby inductors and switch nodes, so a ripple measurement shows spikes that aren't real.

So I put a ground pad or loop right next to every test point I care about, ideally with a via to the plane, and I use the probe's tip-and-barrel with a short spring ground. With 15 millimetres of ground path the resonance moves to about 400 MHz and the overshoot falls from about 50 percent to about 7. Ground points also help production test and bring-up: fixtures and bed-of-nails need ground contacts, and you want one near each rail so you can measure ripple and noise quickly. And I make sure the points are on the right ground, so I'm not measuring a floating or noisy section."
:::

::: technical
**Probe model.** Source resistance $R_s$ (e.g., 50 Ω), probe tip capacitance $C_p\approx10$ pF, ground lead $L_g\approx1$ nH/mm:

$$f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_gC_p}},\qquad Q=\frac{1}{R_s}\sqrt{\frac{L_g}{C_p}},\qquad \zeta=\frac{1}{2Q}$$

| Ground path | $L_g$ | $f_0$ | $Q$ | $\zeta$ | Overshoot |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alligator lead, ~150 mm | 150 nH | 130 MHz | 2.45 | 0.20 | 52% |
| Short lead, ~50 mm | 50 nH | 225 MHz | 1.41 | 0.35 | 31% |
| Spring ground, ~15 mm | 15 nH | 411 MHz | 0.77 | 0.65 | 7% |
| Ground pad adjacent, ~5 mm | 5 nH | 712 MHz | 0.45 | 1.1 | 0% (overdamped) |

(Overshoot for a step into the series RLC: $\exp(-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2})$ for $\zeta<1$; overdamped at $\zeta\ge1$.)

**Rise-time check.** A 1 ns edge has a knee near 350 MHz. A 130 MHz ground-lead resonance sits well inside its spectrum, so it rings. With 411 MHz it is near the edge; with 712 MHz it's above.

**Pickup.** The loop area (lead length × tip-to-ground spacing) couples to $dB/dt$ from nearby switching currents. Reduce the area (short lead, tip close to ground) and the pickup drops proportionally.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> 1 nH/mm and 10 pF are typical order-of-magnitude values; real probes differ (tip capacitance of about 6–15 pF for common 10× passive probes, and the probe's cable and compensation add effects). Check the probe's datasheet. Source impedance of 50 Ω is an assumption for the example.
:::

A 150 mm clip lead resonates near 130 MHz and shows about 50% overshoot on a fast edge that isn't really there; it also picks up magnetic fields through its loop. A ground pad right next to the test point, with a spring ground, brings the resonance up to hundreds of MHz and cleans up ripple and edge measurements. Ground points also serve production test and debug."

::: long
"A ground probe point is a ground pad or loop placed right beside a test point so the scope probe can connect its ground very close to where it measures. It matters because the ground lead is part of the circuit.

A typical probe's ground clip lead is about 15 centimetres long, which is on the order of 150 nanohenries, call it a nanohenry per millimetre. The probe tip has about 10 picofarads. Put them together and you have a series LC with a resonance at about 130 MHz. When I probe a fast edge, like a switch node or a clock with a nanosecond rise time, that LC gets excited and I see ringing and overshoot on the scope that isn't on the board. With a source impedance of 50 ohms the damping ratio is only about 0.2, so I'd see roughly 50% overshoot.

If I shorten the ground path to a spring tip about 15 millimetres long, that's 15 nanohenries, the resonance goes up to about 410 MHz, the damping goes to about 0.65 and overshoot drops to around 7%. A ground pad right next to the test point can do better still.

The ground lead is also half of a loop antenna: the loop made by the tip and the ground lead picks up the magnetic field from nearby inductors and switch nodes. For something like a ripple measurement on a buck converter's output, a long ground lead can add spikes that look like ripple but aren't.

So on a board I put a ground pad, or a ground test loop, within a few millimetres of every test point I care about, ideally stitched to the plane. That also helps production test, logic analyzer connections and bed-of-nails fixtures. And I label them so I know which ground they connect to."
:::

::: terms
- **Test point**: pad or loop provided for probing a net.
- **Ground probe point**: a ground pad or loop located beside a test point.
- **Ground lead / ground clip**: the wire that connects the probe's ground to the circuit.
- **Spring ground / tip-and-barrel**: short ground contact on the probe barrel.
- **Probe input capacitance**: capacitance seen at the tip, typically ~10 pF for passive 10× probes.
- **Damping ratio ($\zeta$)**: measures how quickly ringing decays.
- **Ground loop**: closed path through grounds, here via the scope's earth.
- **Bed-of-nails fixture**: spring pins contacting test pads for automated test.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer works the number (130 MHz, ~50% overshoot, versus 410 MHz and ~7%), explains both ringing and magnetic pickup, describes the right technique (tip-and-barrel/spring ground, close ground pad, bandwidth limit for ripple), and extends to layout practice (a ground pad beside every important test point, stitched to the plane, labelled, with spacing that fits the probe's spring, plus ICT and logic-analyzer access). It also mentions that the scope ground is earthed, so you must not clip it to a hot or floating ground without isolation.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Blaming the circuit for ringing that's from the probe.** Always re-measure with a short ground first.
- **Using the long clip lead to measure switch nodes or fast edges.**
- **Putting the only ground point on the far side of the board.**
- **Probing a mains-referenced ground with an earth-grounded scope.** The scope's ground clip shorts it; use isolation or a differential probe.
- **Ignoring the probe's bandwidth and the board's return path.** Check the probe's rating and use the 20 MHz limit for ripple.
- **Thinking ground points are only for scopes.** They're also for fixtures, meters and logic analyzers.
:::

::: deep
## The probe is a circuit element

A passive 10× probe is a 9 MΩ resistor at the tip in parallel with a compensation capacitor, a coax cable, then the scope's 1 MΩ input with about 15 pF. At the tip, what the circuit sees is about 10 MΩ in parallel with roughly 10 pF. That capacitance loads high-impedance, high-frequency nodes. But the more important parasitic for fast edges is the **ground return**: the current that charges that 10 pF has to come back to the circuit through the ground lead.

## Step response with a long and a short ground

For a series RLC driven by a step, the capacitor voltage is

$$v_C(t)=1-e^{-\alpha t}\left[\cos\omega_dt+\frac{\alpha}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\right],\quad \alpha=\frac{R}{2L},\ \omega_d=\sqrt{\frac{1}{LC}-\alpha^2}$$

## Magnetic pickup

The tip-and-ground loop has area $A=\ell\times d$. A nearby switching current creates a flux density $B$ that induces $V=A\,dB/dt$ in the loop. A 150 mm lead with a 25 mm tip separation is 37.5 cm²; a spring ground 15 mm long and 5 mm from the tip is 0.75 cm², 50× smaller, so 50× less pickup for the same field. This is why a buck converter's output ripple looks spiky with a long ground clip.

## What to put on the board

1. **A ground pad next to every important test point**, ideally within 5 mm, stitched to the plane with one or two vias.
2. **Test points for each supply rail**, with ground adjacent, spaced to fit a spring-ground probe tip (many layouts use 2.54 mm pitch pairs; some probes have 2 mm spacing options).
3. **Ground loops or posts** (wire loops, test hooks) for quick clip-on measurements.
4. **Access for a logic analyzer or a fast-edge measurement**: a header with a ground pin beside every signal or every other pin.
5. **ICT/bed-of-nails**: pads of 0.9–1 mm or more with ground pads distributed.
6. **Labels** (silkscreen) for ground points, and which ground (digital/analog/chassis).
7. **Keep them clear** of tall parts so the probe fits.

## Measurement technique

- **Ripple measurement:** use the spring ground, set the scope to AC coupling and a 20 MHz bandwidth limit if you want just the ripple (or use full bandwidth if you care about spikes), and measure across the output capacitor directly. Short, large-area? No, small loop.
- **Fast edges:** use a short ground and a low-capacitance (or active) probe; check that the probe and scope bandwidth are at least 3–5× the signal's knee frequency ($0.35/t_r$).
- **Mains-referenced circuits:** never clip a grounded scope to a hot node; use an isolated or differential probe.
- **Verify the measurement:** change the ground lead (long vs short) and see whether the waveform changes. If it does, you were measuring the probe.

## Ground probe points as a diagnostic for the board itself

A ground point also allows measuring ground noise: put the probe tip and ground at two ground points (e.g., near the regulator and near the MCU) and look at the voltage between them while the board operates. A few millivolts of high-frequency difference is normal; tens or hundreds of millivolts means a poor return path or a shared-impedance problem. See [[q0497]] and [[q0498]].

> [!INTERVIEW]
> When asked how you'd measure the ripple on a 3.3 V rail, say: "Spring ground or tip-and-barrel right at the output capacitor, short loop, bandwidth limit on if I want ripple only, and compare against a long lead to see how much of what I see is the probe." That answer shows measurement and grounding together.
:::

::: applications
- **Buck/boost converters**: switch-node and output-ripple measurements with a spring ground pad next to the capacitor.
- **High-speed digital bring-up**: clock edges and eye measurements need short ground paths.
- **Production test**: ICT fixtures need distributed ground pads.
- **Mixed-signal debugging**: measuring ground noise between analog and digital regions with two ground points.
- **RF boards**: ground pads beside coax connectors or probe pads for microprobes.
:::

::: assumptions
- Passive 10× scope probe with ~10 pF tip capacitance and a clip lead of ~1 nH/mm; real values vary.
- Source impedance 50 Ω as a representative low-impedance node; high-impedance nodes see more loading than ringing.
- Edge rates in the nanosecond range and frequencies of tens to hundreds of MHz.
- Active probes, differential probes and coax-adapter probes change the numbers but not the principle.
- Safety rules for mains-referenced circuits take precedence over measurement convenience.
:::

::: followups
### How would you measure output ripple on a switching regulator?
Use a short ground path directly across the output capacitor, either the probe's spring ground or tip-and-barrel on test points, AC coupled with the right bandwidth limit. Compare with a long lead to confirm the spikes are real. Check the ripple against the capacitor's ESR/ESL contribution, and repeat at full load.

### Why does the ground clip pick up noise?
The tip and ground lead form a loop, and any changing magnetic field through it induces a voltage $V=A\,dB/dt$. Near a switching inductor the field is strong, so a large loop sees spikes. Reduce the area with a short ground lead and the tip close to the ground contact.

### Can you probe a switch node on a mains-powered off-line supply with a normal scope?
Not safely: the scope's ground is tied to earth through the mains lead, so clipping it to the primary side can short the supply to earth. Use an isolated scope, a differential or high-voltage differential probe, or an isolation transformer for the DUT. Probe-point layout on the primary side should account for this.

### How many ground pads should a board have?
Enough that you can always get a short ground for the important nets: one per supply rail test point, one beside each fast signal test point, plus a few ground hooks around the board edge. A typical layout has 4–10 for a small board and more for complex ones. They cost nothing but a little area.

### Scenario: your buck converter's output shows 300 mV spikes at the switching frequency with a clip lead and 20 mV with a spring ground. Which is real?
The 20 mV is much closer to the truth. The 300 mV was mostly the clip lead's loop picking up the switch node's dV/dt and dI/dt and ringing with the probe capacitance. I'd confirm by checking the spring-ground measurement against the expected ripple from $\Delta I_L\times ESR$ and the capacitor's ESL times the current ramp, and by probing right across the output capacitor pads.
:::
