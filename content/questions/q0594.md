::: testing
Whether you understand that boards are not built one at a time. The interviewer wants the definition (several copies of a board, or different boards, arranged in one larger "panel" for fabrication and assembly), the two main ways to separate them (V-scoring and tab-routing with mousebites), and the reasons: throughput, handling of small boards, material utilisation and machine requirements like conveyor rails and fiducials. A strong answer also shows you can *design* one: rails, tooling holes, spacing, depanelization stress near components.
:::

::: why
Assembly lines are built for boards in a certain size range. A 20 mm x 30 mm sensor board won't ride on an SMT conveyor reliably and would need a pick-and-place cycle per board for fiducial alignment. Put 12 of them in a panel and one paste print, one placement program run and one reflow pass builds them all. Panel design also decides how the boards come apart, which affects cracked ceramic capacitors, edge-component clearance and yield. It is a classic DFM topic ([[q0613]]) that interns are expected to handle.
:::

::: concepts
- **Fabrication panel vs assembly panel**: the fab ships a large production panel (about 450 x 600 mm class); you often also define a smaller *assembly array* that holds several boards for SMT.
- **Rails**: sacrificial strips (often 5-10 mm) at the panel edge for conveyor grip, tooling holes and fiducials.
- **V-score**: a V-shaped groove cut partway into both sides, leaving a thin web to snap.
- **Tab routing with mousebites**: boards are routed out but held by small tabs perforated with drilled holes. See [[q0595]].
- **Fiducials**: optical alignment marks on the panel and boards. See [[q0596]].
- **Depanelization stress**: bending or shearing the panel to break boards out flexes the PCB and the components on it.
:::

::: answer
"Panelization means arranging multiple copies of a board, or sometimes different boards, into one bigger panel that's manufactured and assembled together. The fab does it anyway on its production panel, but I also design an assembly array so the shop can run it through the SMT line as one unit.

It helps for a few reasons. Small boards are hard to handle on conveyors, so you add rails with tooling holes and fiducials. Paste printing, placement and reflow then process N boards in one pass, which cuts cost per board. And you use laminate more efficiently.

The boards are held together either by V-scores, which are grooves cut from both sides leaving a thin web you snap at the end, or by routed tabs with perforations, which people call mousebites. V-score is cheap and fast but only works for straight lines and boards that touch, and it needs component keep-out near the cut. Tabs allow odd shapes and gaps between boards, but you get small stubs on the edge to clean up. I also keep sensitive parts like ceramic capacitors away from the breakout area, because snapping a panel flexes the board."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** A *panel* (or array) is a rigid frame containing N board outlines connected by break-away features. A step-and-repeat of one design is the common case; a *mixed* or *combined* panel holds different designs, useful for prototypes.

**Panel structure.**
- **Rails** along two (sometimes four) edges, commonly 5-10 mm wide, carrying 3 global fiducials (so the machine can compute X, Y offset, rotation and scale), tooling holes for fixtures, and sometimes a barcode label.
- **Board spacing**: V-score boards butt together (kerf of a fraction of a mm); routed-tab boards need a route channel typically 2-3 mm wide, set by the router bit.
- **Break-aways**: either V-score (leaving a web, often about one-third of the thickness, i.e. ~0.5 mm in a 1.6 mm board), or tabs of about 2-3 mm perforated with 3-5 small holes.
- **Panel size** is limited by the SMT line's conveyor and stencil frame, commonly something like 250-350 mm per side for small shops (vendor dependent); too large a panel also sags in reflow.

**Throughput example.** An 80 mm x 50 mm board on a 250 mm x 200 mm assembly area: with 10 mm of rail/margin on all sides and a 2 mm gap, you can fit 2 x 3 = 6 boards (computed; rotating the board 90° gives 4 x 2 = 8, see the deep dive). One print, one placement program and one reflow pass build all six, so the per-board handling time is roughly 1/6.

**Depanelization methods.** Hand snapping (cheapest, highest stress), pizza-cutter/ guillotine for V-scores, **router** (precise, dust, stress-free when done properly), laser (no mechanical stress, expensive). Strain on the board near the break is the issue; MLCC cracking thresholds are commonly quoted somewhere from several hundred to around a thousand microstrain, depending on size and termination, so place them parallel to, and away from, the break line.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Rail width, tab dimensions, V-score web thickness and maximum panel sizes vary with the assembly house and equipment. Ask for their panelization guideline sheet, and treat the numbers here as typical starting points.
:::

::: short
"Panelization is arranging several boards in one panel so they're fabricated and assembled together. Rails add conveyor grip, tooling holes and fiducials, which makes small or odd-shaped boards easy to run through paste print, pick-and-place and reflow, and it cuts cost per board. The boards are held with V-scores or with tabs and mousebites, and then broken apart at the end. I keep sensitive parts like ceramic caps away from the break line because depaneling flexes the board."
:::

::: long
"Panelization is putting multiple boards into one larger panel for manufacturing. There are really two panels to think about. The fab has its big production panel, and they nest your outline into it to waste as little laminate as possible. Then there's the assembly panel, or array, which I design so the SMT line can handle the board.

Why bother? First, handling. Conveyors, stencil printers and reflow ovens have minimum widths, and tiny boards can fall through or tilt. A panel with rails gives the machine something to grip and gives me a place for tooling holes and fiducials. Second, throughput. One paste print, one placement program and one reflow profile build every board in the panel, so a six-up panel is roughly six times faster per board than single boards. Third, cost and material utilization.

Then there's how the boards separate. V-scoring cuts a groove from both sides and leaves a thin web, say a third of the thickness, and you snap it. It's cheap and fast, but only for straight cuts with boards edge to edge. For non-rectangular boards or gaps you use tab routing: the board is routed out except for a few small tabs, and those tabs are perforated with a row of tiny holes, the mousebites, so they snap cleanly.

The design concerns are keep-outs near the break, because snapping a panel flexes the board and cracks ceramic capacitors, components overhanging the edge, and making sure the panel is rigid enough so it doesn't sag in reflow. I would also check the assembly house's size limits before finalizing."
:::

::: terms
- **Panel / array**: a frame of multiple boards processed as one.
- **Step-and-repeat**: the same board copied N times in a grid.
- **Rail / breakaway rail**: sacrificial edge strip for conveyor handling.
- **V-score / V-groove**: partial-depth groove used to snap boards apart.
- **Tab routing**: routed outline with small connecting tabs (often perforated: mousebites).
- **Depanelization (depaneling)**: separating individual boards from the panel.
- **Tooling holes**: unplated holes for fixture pins.
- **Global fiducial**: alignment mark on the panel rail used by the placement machine.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it means putting many boards on one panel to save money."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Distinguishes the fab's production panel from your assembly array and says why each exists.
- Names the physical reasons: conveyor width and rails, fiducials/tooling, one paste/place/reflow pass for N boards.
- Compares V-score and tab-route with mousebites on cost, shape freedom and edge quality.
- Raises depanelization stress and keeps ceramic capacitors, connectors and BGAs away from break lines.
- Mentions panel rigidity and standard sizes, and that the assembly house's guideline should drive the final dimensions.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking panelization is only about saving money on the fab.** The big win is assembly handling and process throughput.
- **Using V-score for a board with overhanging parts or irregular outline.** V-score only cuts straight lines edge to edge; parts overhanging the edge get sheared.
- **Putting capacitors across the break line.** Snapping flexes the board and cracks MLCCs, sometimes as latent shorts.
- **Forgetting fiducials on the rails.** The placement machine then has to rely on edge sensors or per-board marks only.
- **Making the panel too floppy.** Thin boards with big cut-outs sag in reflow; add rails, support or a carrier.
- **No keep-out around tab locations.** The stub left by a broken tab protrudes; if your enclosure or connector touches that edge, you lose clearance.
:::

::: deep
## Why single boards are awkward

SMT assembly equipment is designed around a *conveyed flat carrier*. The stencil printer clamps the board, the placer's vision system finds fiducials, the oven chain edges grip the board along its long sides. A board below roughly 50 mm in a dimension, or with an odd outline, doesn't clamp or rest flat. Panelization wraps those awkward boards inside a nicely rectangular frame.

## Sizing an array (worked example)

Take a 80 mm x 50 mm board, a 2 mm gap between boards (router channel) and 10 mm rails on all sides, in a 250 mm x 200 mm assembly area. Usable width: 250 - 2x10 = 230 mm, so boards across = floor((230 + 2)/(80 + 2)) = 2; usable height 180 mm, so floor((180 + 2)/(50 + 2)) = 3. Result: 6 boards per array.

You can rotate the board to see whether 50 mm x 80 mm packs better, and compare the **utilisation** $= N A_{board}/A_{panel}$. The same arithmetic at the fab's scale (a 600 x 450 mm production panel, 3 mm gaps, 10 mm rails) fits 7 x 8 = 56 boards at about 83% utilisation (computed), which is why a fab's price per board falls with volume: the unused fraction goes into rails and spacing.

## V-score vs tab-route

| | V-score | Tab-route with mousebites |
|---|---|---|
| Board shape | Rectangular, edge-to-edge | Any outline, gaps allowed |
| Material usage | Best, no gap | Router channel wasted |
| Edge quality | Smooth, slightly rough at the web | Small stubs from broken tabs |
| Component keep-out | Larger (blade shear, snapping stress) | Moderate; tabs fewer than full line |
| Minimum thickness | Not for thin boards (< ~0.8 mm, vendor dependent) | OK for thin boards |
| Cost | Cheap, fast | Router time |

V-score leaves a web. For a 1.6 mm board with a one-third web, the web is about 0.53 mm, with the groove on each side about 0.53 mm deep (computed). Because the blade shears copper near the line, fabs ask you to keep copper and components some distance away from the score line, often on the order of 0.5 - 1 mm for copper and more (a few mm) for components.

## Stress during depaneling

Bending a panel to snap it produces strain in the board. A ceramic capacitor mounted with its long axis perpendicular to the bend bridges between two solder joints that move relative to each other, and ceramic is brittle. A cracked MLCC can show up later as a leakage short. Mitigations:

- Orient MLCCs parallel to the break line, and keep them away from it.
- Use a depaneling router or laser for sensitive designs.
- Avoid placing connectors, BGAs and large ceramics near scored edges.
- Support the panel and snap on a fixture, not by hand.

## Panel design checklist

1. Rails on the two long sides, 5-10 mm.
2. Three global fiducials on rails (in an asymmetric pattern), plus local fiducials near fine-pitch parts ([[q0596]]).
3. Tooling holes in the rails.
4. Break-away features: V-score for rectangles; tabs and mousebites ([[q0595]]) otherwise.
5. Bad-board marks, if the assembler uses them.
6. Panel stiffness, and a way to support the middle during reflow.

> [!TIP]
> The question "who designs the panel?" usually has the answer "whoever builds it". Many assembly houses send a panel drawing for you to approve, and many fabs offer panelization as part of the order. Either way you own the board-level requirements: keep-out zones, edge clearance, mechanical fit.

## Worked example: rotating the board, and what it does to throughput

Return to the 80 mm × 50 mm board in the 250 mm × 200 mm assembly area with 2 mm gaps and 10 mm rails. In the first orientation the array is 2 across by 3 down, 6 boards, with utilization $6\times4000/50000=48\%$. Rotate the board to 50 mm × 80 mm: the usable width of 230 mm now holds $\lfloor(230+2)/(50+2)\rfloor=4$ boards and the usable height of 180 mm holds $\lfloor(180+2)/(80+2)\rfloor=2$, so 8 boards, utilization $8\times4000/50000=64\%$. The same panel carries a third more boards.

That matters because most of the per-panel cost in SMT assembly is fixed per pass: loading, stencil print, placement-head cycle to find fiducials, reflow dwell and unloading. If the printer-plus-placer cycle takes the same time for a panel of 6 as for a panel of 8, per-board machine time falls by $1-6/8=25\%$. Part placement time itself scales with the number of components, but the fixed overhead (fiducial vision, conveyor transfer, board clamp) does not. This is why assemblers push for panels that fill the machine's maximum board size, within limits of weight and warpage. A caveat: the *orientation* is also set by the conveyor direction and by how the fab's routing and scoring pass through the panel, and the longer dimension of the panel normally runs along the conveyor to keep the sag across the rails small, so the best layout is a compromise and the assembler should confirm it.

## Worked example: bending strain when the panel is snapped

A 1.6 mm FR-4 panel is supported at two points 100 mm apart and pushed down at the middle (a common snapping pose). For a simply supported beam with a central load, the maximum curvature is $\kappa=12\delta/L^2$ for midspan deflection $\delta$, and the surface strain is $\varepsilon=\kappa t/2=6\delta t/L^2$. For $\delta=2$ mm:

$$\varepsilon=\frac{6\times2\times1.6}{100^2}=1.9\times10^{-3}=1900\ \mu\varepsilon$$

At $\delta=3$ mm it is 2,900 µε, and at 1 mm only 960 µε. The strain is proportional to the deflection, so a modest extra push has a large effect, and it scales as $1/L^2$: halving the span quadruples the strain. Such numbers are in the range where large ceramic capacitors in rigid solder joints start to be at risk, although the cracking threshold depends on the capacitor size, termination design and solder fillet, and is vendor dependent.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Whether a given MLCC cracks at 1,900 µε depends on case size, dielectric, termination construction, solder volume and board stiffness. Use the capacitor manufacturer's flex-crack guidance and the assembler's depaneling limits, not this figure, as the design criterion.

The local strain near a V-score is higher than the beam estimate, because the web is a stress concentrator and the board bends at a sharp crease. That is why the keep-out zone beside the score line is stated in millimeters and why a router or laser depaneling, which applies essentially no bending, is the safest choice for boards with 1206 or larger ceramics near the edge.

## Limiting cases and when the rules change

- **Very small boards** (under about 20 mm) can only be built in arrays, and the array's frame, not the board, determines how it is handled; plan test fixtures and programming around the panel.
- **Thin boards** (below roughly 0.8 mm) do not have enough material for a reliable V-score web, so tab-routing is used, and the panel may need a stiffener or carrier during reflow because it sags in the oven.
- **Non-rectangular or projecting boards** (connectors hanging off an edge) need routing with tabs, and the tab positions must avoid parts and traces: copper traces crossing a tab get broken or exposed when the tab is cut.
- **Mixed arrays** of different boards are allowed for small quantities, but they complicate the stencil, the pick-and-place program and the test fixtures, so they usually cost more setup than they save.

## Bench and line verification

Check the first-article panel physically: measure the board dimension and the rail clearance against the machine limits; look at the fiducials under a loupe for the asymmetric pattern; and break a sacrificial board out by the method production will use. Inspect the edge for burrs and delamination, and then check a few capacitors nearest the cut with a microscope and, for critical lots, with an LCR meter for a drop in capacitance or leakage current. In production an in-line strain-gauge test (a gauge glued near the suspect part while depaneling) shows the actual microstrain, which should stay under the component manufacturer's limit with margin.
:::

::: applications
- **Small IoT or sensor boards** (20 x 30 mm): 10-20 per panel so the SMT line can run them.
- **Prototype arrays**: a "combined" panel holding board variants A, B and C in one fab order.
- **LED strip/ light-bar boards**: many long thin boards panelised, often V-scored.
- **Round or irregular boards (wearables)**: tab-routed with mousebites since V-score can't cut curves.
- **Rails as test carriers**: tooling holes and fiducials double as alignment features for ICT/flying probe fixtures ([[q0609]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- Describes **rigid** board panelization for SMT. Flex circuits use carriers/pallets rather than break-away rails.
- Dimensions are typical; line capacity and conveyor width vary by assembly house.
- Assumes tab and V-score depaneling are mechanical; laser depaneling removes most of the stress concern but costs more.
- If the board is large (say over 200 mm in each direction), the "panel" may be a single board with its own rails, or none, if the line can clamp it.
:::

::: followups
### What are the downsides of V-scoring?
It only cuts straight lines through the whole panel, boards must be edge-to-edge, and it needs keep-out for copper and components near the line. The web leaves a slightly rough edge, and snapping stresses nearby parts. Thin boards (below roughly 0.8 mm) don't have enough material for a good web.

### How would you panelize a round board?
Tab routing: route the circular outline, leave 2-3 tabs with mousebite perforations, and add rails. You'd place the tabs where no components or traces are, and could add small notch features so the stubs don't protrude beyond the final diameter.

### Why are there fiducials on the panel rails when there are already fiducials on each board?
Global fiducials let the machine find the panel's position and scale in one go, and they survive even if individual board fiducials are covered or damaged. Local fiducials near fine-pitch parts refine the placement for that one part. See [[q0596]].

### Each board in your array is 80 x 50 mm. How many fit in a 250 x 200 mm area with 10 mm rails and 2 mm gaps?
Across: floor((230+2)/82) = 2; down: floor((180+2)/52) = 3. So 6 boards. Rotating the board 90 degrees gives floor(232/52) = 4 across by floor(182/82) = 2 down = 8 boards, which is better utilisation, so always try both orientations (as long as the conveyor direction and rail placement still work for the line).

### The assembly house reports intermittent shorts on boards from the corner of the panel and not the center. What could be wrong?
Likely mechanical: corner boards see more flex during depaneling, or sag in reflow if the panel has no support, cracking MLCCs or joints. Check capacitor orientation near the break, the break method, and whether the panel needs a carrier or more rail material.
:::

::: interpretations
The question has two readings. Fab-side: "how does the fab arrange my boards on its manufacturing panel". Assembly-side: "how do I design an array for SMT." Interviewers mean the second most of the time, and both are covered above. Don't confuse it with *depanelization*, which is the separation step. Mousebites are covered in [[q0595]].
:::
