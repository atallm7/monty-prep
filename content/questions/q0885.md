::: basics
### In plain words
Every clock tick gives the logic a fixed amount of time to finish its work. If you speed the clock up so the next tick arrives before the slowest logic is done, the circuit captures an unfinished answer. You get wrong results, often only for certain inputs.

### Picture it
A baker pulls a tray out of the oven on a fixed timer. Most items bake in 10 minutes, but the thick loaf needs 20. Set the timer to 15 minutes and the rolls come out fine while the loaf comes out raw. A chip behaves the same way: the slowest path fails first, and conditions shift the line.

### Words you need
- **Clock period**: the time between ticks, which is 1 divided by the clock frequency.
- **Critical path**: the slowest chain of logic between two flip-flops (one-bit memories). It sets the fastest safe clock.
- **Slack**: spare time on a path, equal to the period minus the time the path needs. Negative slack means failure.
- **Metastability**: when data changes right at the capture instant, the output can hover in between before settling ([[q0887]]).

### The simplest example
An 8-bit adder passes its carry (the "1" carried to the next column) through 8 stages of 0.25 ns each, plus 0.5 ns of flip-flop overhead. The slowest case needs $8 \times 0.25 + 0.5 = 2.5$ ns, so the safe maximum clock is 400 MHz. At 2.0 ns (500 MHz) the slack is $-0.5$ ns. Adding 1 + 1 still works, since its carry moves only one stage. Adding 255 + 1 sends the carry through all 8 bits, so the top bit gets a stale carry and the answer is wrong.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds how voltage and temperature move the failure point, the power cost of a faster clock, and how to debug and fix it.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can connect a clock frequency to the **timing budget** and predict failure modes: setup violations on the critical path first, wrong or metastable captures, and dependence on temperature, voltage and the particular chip. A strong answer also covers the non-timing effects (power and heat rise linearly with frequency, minimum pulse width, I/O and memory interfaces that cannot keep up) and how you'd debug or fix it.
:::

::: why
Overclocking, bring-up at target frequency and "works on my bench, fails in production" are all instances of this question. Real failures are intermittent: they depend on data patterns, temperature and supply voltage, which is why they are hard to debug. The question tests whether you understand the mechanism (setup slack goes negative) rather than just saying "it breaks".
:::

::: concepts
- **Setup constraint**: $T_{clk}\ge t_{cq}+t_{logic}+t_{su}+t_{skew}$ ([[q0880]]).
- **Critical path**: the slowest register-to-register path sets $f_{max}$ ([[q0884]]).
- **Metastability** when data arrives inside the setup/hold window ([[q0887]]).
- **PVT dependence**: gate delay grows at low voltage, high temperature and slow process.
- **Dynamic power** $P=\alpha CV^2f$ rises with frequency.
- **Latches and flip-flops** capture whatever is at their input at the edge ([[q0882]]).
:::

::: answer
"If the clock is faster than the slowest path can handle, the data doesn't arrive in time for the next edge, so flip-flops capture stale or half-settled values. That's a setup violation. The first symptom is usually that the longest paths fail, the carry chain of an adder, say, so you get wrong results for specific data patterns while simple operations still work. Because delay depends on temperature, voltage and the individual chip, the failure can be intermittent: it works cold and fails hot, or works on one board and not another.

Worse, if the data changes right at the sampling edge, the flip-flop can go metastable, so its output sits at an in-between level for a while and then resolves randomly, which can spread into other logic as inconsistent values and show up as crashes.

Other things go wrong too. Power and heat scale with frequency, so the chip heats up and gets slower, which can make it worse. The clock itself might not meet minimum pulse width. And interfaces have their own limits: memory access time, flash wait states, an SPI slave's max clock.

Note that hold violations don't depend on frequency, so going faster doesn't create them.

To fix it, I'd find the failing path from timing analysis, then pipeline it, restructure the logic, raise the supply or cool the chip, or just lower the clock to what the critical path supports."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism.** With period $T$ shorter than $t_{cq}+t_{logic,max}+t_{su}+t_{skew}$, the capturing flip-flop samples before the new value has propagated: Q gets the previous (stale) value, a partially switched value, or goes metastable. Slack $=T-(t_{cq}+t_{logic}+t_{su}+t_{skew})<0$.

**Which paths fail first.** The longest: carry chains, multipliers, wide comparators, high-fanout nets, long wires. Data-dependent: only inputs that sensitize the long path (such as a carry rippling through all bits) fail, so errors look pattern-dependent and intermittent.

**Worked example (8-bit ripple-carry adder).** Per-stage carry delay 0.25 ns, register overhead 0.5 ns. Full ripple needs $8\times0.25+0.5=2.5$ ns. At $T=2.0$ ns (500 MHz), the carry reaches only stage $(2.0-0.5)/0.25=6$ in time. For $0xFF+0x01$ the correct result is $0x00$ with carry-out 1; the captured result is $0x80$ with carry-out 0: bit 7 uses a stale carry. $0x01+0x01$ is correct at the same clock, since its carry doesn't ripple.

**PVT.** Delay follows roughly $t_d\propto\frac{CV}{(V-V_t)^\alpha}$ with $\alpha\approx1.3$ for short-channel devices. Lower $V$, higher temperature, slower process all increase delay and lower $f_{max}$. For $V_t=0.35$ V, going from 1.0 V to 0.8 V reduces $f_{max}$ to about 77% (and to 64% at 0.7 V).

**Power.** $P=\alpha CV^2f$ so doubling $f$ doubles dynamic power; if the voltage must also rise to close timing, power scales roughly as $f\cdot V^2$, about $f^3$ overall.

**Other limits.** Minimum clock pulse width (flip-flop and memory macros), clock tree slew, PLL jitter, memory and flash access times (wait states), external-device max clock.

**Hold unaffected.** Hold depends on same-edge races; frequency does not appear.
:::

::: short
"If the clock period is shorter than clock-to-Q plus the longest logic delay plus setup, flip-flops sample before data has settled: setup violations. The longest paths fail first, so you get pattern-dependent wrong results, worse when hot or at low voltage, and possibly metastability. Power and heat rise with frequency too, and interfaces like memory or SPI slaves have their own limits. Hold violations aren't affected by frequency. The fix is to pipeline or optimize the critical path, raise the voltage, or lower the clock."
:::

::: long
"A clock that's too fast breaks the setup condition. Between two flip-flops, the data has to get out of the first one, through the logic, and settle at the second one before its next clock edge, minus the setup time. If the period is shorter than clock-to-Q plus the longest logic delay plus setup, the second flop samples before the right answer has arrived, and it captures a stale or half-switched value.

What you see in practice is that the slowest paths go first, such as the carry chain in an adder or a long routing path, and only for data patterns that exercise them. Say I have an 8-bit ripple adder with a quarter nanosecond per bit, and I clock it so that only six stages of carry have time to propagate. Adding 1 and 1 still works, but 0xFF plus 1 gives 0x80 instead of 0 with a carry, because the top bit used an old carry. So it looks random: sometimes right, sometimes wrong.

Delay depends on process, voltage and temperature, so the failure frequency moves around. It works cold and fails hot, passes at 1.0 volts and fails at 0.8, or works on this chip and not the next one. If the data lands right at the sampling edge, the flop can go metastable, with the output hovering in between and resolving randomly, so different bits of downstream logic can disagree.

Other things scale with frequency: dynamic power is C V squared f, so heat goes up, silicon gets slower, and it can run away. The clock might also violate minimum pulse width, and interfaces such as memory, flash wait states or an SPI device's max clock can limit you.

One thing frequency does not do is create hold violations, since hold compares data against the same edge.

To fix it, I'd look at the timing report for the failing path, pipeline or restructure it, upsize cells, raise the voltage within limits, or run slower."
:::

::: terms
- **Setup violation**: data arrives too late for the capturing edge (negative setup slack).
- **Slack**: period minus the required path delay (positive is margin).
- **Critical path**: longest path that limits $f_{max}$.
- **$f_{max}$**: highest clock frequency at which all paths meet timing.
- **Guardband**: margin kept for PVT and aging so parts work in the field.
- **Metastability**: indeterminate output after violating setup/hold ([[q0887]]).
- **Minimum pulse width**: shortest high or low clock time a flop or memory can tolerate.
- **Wait state**: extra cycles inserted so a slow memory can respond.
- **Overclocking**: running above the rated frequency, consuming margin.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "setup violations happen and the circuit gives wrong outputs".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Writes the timing inequality and says which side shrinks as the clock speeds up.
- Describes failures as *data-dependent and PVT-dependent*, with a concrete example such as the ripple-carry adder.
- Mentions metastability as the possible nasty outcome of marginal captures.
- Adds non-timing effects: dynamic power $\propto f$ (and $\approx f^3$ with voltage scaling), thermal feedback, minimum pulse width, memory/IO limits.
- Notes that hold is unaffected and gives practical fixes and debugging steps (STA report, voltage/temperature sweep, shmoo plot).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The chip just stops."** Usually it doesn't fail gracefully: it produces wrong results for some data patterns, intermittently.
- **Believing hold violations increase with frequency.** They are independent of period.
- **Forgetting temperature and voltage.** A design that passes at room temperature may fail hot or at low supply: delay changes with PVT.
- **Ignoring the rest of the system.** Flash, RAM and peripherals have their own access times and clock limits.
- **Assuming a faster clock always means proportionally faster performance.** Wait states, stalls and memory bandwidth limit real speed-up.
- **Forgetting power.** Higher $f$ raises dissipation, which can raise temperature and slow the logic further.
:::

::: deep
## The timing budget, shrunk

For a path between two flip-flops, the data has $T-t_{cq}-t_{su}-t_{skew}$ of time to cross the logic. Cutting $T$ cuts that time one-for-one. Take the example from [[q0880]]: $t_{cq}=0.4$ ns, $t_{su}=0.1$ ns, skew penalty 0.1 ns, logic 2.0 ns. The minimum period is 2.6 ns (385 MHz). At 500 MHz ($T=2.0$ ns) the path has $2.0-0.6=1.4$ ns available for 2.0 ns of logic: slack $=-0.6$ ns.

Not every path is as long. A real chip has millions of paths with delays spread over a range. As the clock speeds up, the available time falls; paths whose delay exceeds it begin to fail.

The first failing paths are usually few, long and data-dependent, so a test suite may pass until the right pattern appears. This is why "it works most of the time" is the signature of a timing margin problem.

## What a failing capture looks like

Take an 8-bit ripple-carry adder: each full-adder stage passes the carry in 0.25 ns, the register adds 0.5 ns of overhead. The worst case ($0xFF+0x01$) needs the carry to ripple across all 8 stages: $8\times0.25+0.5=2.5$ ns.

At a 2.0 ns clock, there are $2.0-0.5=1.5$ ns for carry propagation, or 6 stages:

| Operation | Longest carry ripple | Result at 2.0 ns clock | Correct? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0x01 + 0x01 | 1 stage | 0x02 | yes |
| 0x0F + 0x01 | 4 stages | 0x10 | yes |
| 0x7F + 0x01 | 7 stages | bit 7 uses a stale carry: 0x00 if the old carry was 0, 0x80 if it was 1 | wrong or right by luck (expected 0x80) |
| 0xFF + 0x01 | 8 stages | 0x80, no carry-out | no (expected 0x00 with carry) |

The failing case depends on the *data*, and also on what the registers held the cycle before (a "stale" carry of 0 or 1 gives different wrong answers). A processor with this fault might compute most arithmetic correctly and silently corrupt a few percent, which is the worst kind of bug.

## Why it depends on temperature and voltage

Gate delay is set by how fast a transistor can charge a load: $t_d\approx\frac{CV}{I_{on}}$ with $I_{on}\propto(V-V_t)^\alpha$ (the alpha-power law, $\alpha\approx1.3$ for modern short-channel devices; $\alpha=2$ for long-channel). So $t_d\propto\frac{V}{(V-V_t)^\alpha}$. Normalized to $V=1.0$ V with $V_t=0.35$ V:

Temperature raises delay in most nodes (carrier mobility drops as temperature rises), so hot silicon is slower. In some advanced nodes at low voltage the effect reverses ("temperature inversion", where threshold voltage falls with temperature enough to speed devices up), so the worst-case corner for setup can be *cold*. Check which corner your process uses.

Because manufacturing varies, each die has its own $f_{max}$. Vendors **bin** parts: test each at several frequencies and sell the fast ones at a higher grade. A shmoo plot (pass/fail on a grid of frequency versus voltage) shows the boundary, and it slopes: higher voltage buys higher frequency.

## Power and thermal feedback

Dynamic power is $P=\alpha CV^2f$. If you also need to raise $V$ to hold timing at a higher $f$, and $V$ rises roughly in proportion to $f$ in the operating range, power grows approximately as $f^3$: pushing 20% more frequency costs roughly 70% more power ($1.2^3=1.73$). More power means more heat; if cooling can't keep up, temperature rises, delay rises, and $f_{max}$ falls: thermal throttling exists to break this loop. Leakage also grows exponentially with temperature, adding to the heat.

## Non-timing limits

- **Minimum pulse width.** A flip-flop or SRAM macro needs a minimum high and low time; with a 50% duty cycle at very high frequency each phase is half the period, and distribution through the clock tree degrades the pulse (duty-cycle distortion).
- **Clock slew and PLL.** Weak clock buffers can't toggle fast enough; a PLL can lose lock or have higher jitter at its range limit.
- **Memory.** On-chip flash typically needs *wait states* above a certain core frequency, since its access time is fixed in nanoseconds; run faster and the instruction fetch returns garbage unless the wait-state setting is raised. SRAM and DRAM have access times and refresh limits.
- **I/O interfaces.** An SPI flash or sensor specifies a maximum SCK; above it, data is not valid at the sample edge, the same setup problem at the board level.
- **EMI.** Faster edges and higher frequencies raise emissions.

## Debugging and fixes

1. **Reproduce and characterize**: sweep frequency, voltage and temperature; find the pass/fail boundary (shmoo).
2. **Find the path**: use the STA report (negative slack, startpoint and endpoint). Failing bit positions often point to the path (e.g. the top bits of an adder).
3. **Fix by design**: pipeline the long path, restructure or retime logic, reduce fan-out, upsize drivers, floorplan to shorten wires.
4. **Fix by operating point**: lower the clock, raise voltage within the rated range, improve cooling.
5. **Check the margins**: leave guardband (typically 5 to 10 %, depending on the product) for aging and corners.

> A good one-liner: "A too-fast clock turns the setup inequality negative on the critical path, so I'd get wrong, pattern-dependent and temperature-dependent results, maybe metastability. Hold is unaffected, and I'd fix it by finding the failing path in STA and pipelining or by lowering the clock."

::: applications
- **Processor binning and DVFS**: vendors test each die and set frequency/voltage pairs; the OS scales them to save power.
- **FPGA timing closure**: tools report negative slack for the paths that limit the achievable clock.
- **Board bring-up**: finding the maximum SPI or memory clock before data errors appear.
- **Overclocking and stress tests**: pushing frequency until intermittent errors reveal the margin.
- **Safety-critical designs**: adding timing margin or monitors (canary paths, razor-style detectors) so the system backs off before failing.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the chip fail only when hot?
Gate delay grows with temperature in most operating regimes (lower carrier mobility), shrinking setup slack. The critical path that had 50 ps of margin at 25°C can go negative at 100°C. Worst-case (slow, low V, hot) is the setup corner; designers sign off timing there.

### Can reducing the clock speed fix a hold violation?
No. Hold compares the new data arrival with the hold window of the *same* clock edge, and the period doesn't appear in the inequality. You fix it by adding delay on the short path or fixing the skew ([[q0880]]).

### If you double the clock frequency, what happens to power?
Dynamic power doubles (at fixed voltage). If you must raise the voltage to meet timing too, power rises faster, roughly cubic: 20% more frequency needs about 20% more voltage and costs about 73% more power ($1.2\times1.2^2$).

### Your MCU runs at 80 MHz but crashes at 168 MHz even though the logic is fine. What else could be wrong?
Flash wait states not set (the flash cannot return instructions in one cycle at that clock), peripheral bus clocks beyond their limits, a PLL setting out of range, or supply droop under the faster load. Check the reference manual's wait-state table and voltage-range limits.

### How can a system detect that it is running too fast?
Add timing monitors (canary flip-flops with a deliberately shortened margin or replica critical paths) that fail slightly before the real paths do, and throttle frequency or raise voltage when they trigger; add ECC or parity to catch corrupted values, and watch thermal sensors.
:::

::: interpretations
"Too fast" can refer to the *core clock* of a processor or logic block (the main reading here) or to a *communication clock* such as SPI or I²C. For the latter, the same logic applies at board level: setup and hold of the receiver, plus signal integrity and rise-time limits on the bus. The embedded duplicate of the timing background is in [[q0880]].
