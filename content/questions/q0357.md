::: basics
### In plain words
Brushes are the stationary contacts of a brushed motor. They are small blocks, usually carbon, pressed by springs against a spinning ring of copper segments called the commutator. They do two jobs: they carry current from the fixed terminals into the spinning coils, and they help flip the current in each coil at the right moment.

### Picture it
Picture a pencil lead pressed against a spinning bicycle rim. The lead stays still, the rim slides past it, and the lead stays in contact. In the motor, the rim is the commutator and the pencil lead is the brush, carrying electricity instead of marking paper. The analogy has one gap: the brush also has to switch from one copper segment to the next, which a pencil never does.

### Words you need
- **Brush**: a stationary conductor, often carbon, that slides on the commutator.
- **Commutator**: copper segments on the rotor, each wired to a coil.
- **Rotor**: the spinning part of the motor.
- **Contact voltage drop**: the voltage a carbon brush loses as current passes through it, roughly 0.5–1 V per brush.
- **Brushless motor**: a motor that switches current electronically instead. See [[q0363]].

### The simplest example
Take one wire loop spinning between magnets, with its ends on two half-rings and two fixed brushes touching the half-rings. Half a turn later, each brush has slid onto the other half-ring, so the current in the loop reverses. That reversal keeps the push in the same direction. The brushes also waste a little voltage. Take the top of the usual range, 1 V per brush: the current passes through two brushes, so 2 V is lost. At 2 A, that is 2 V × 2 A = **4 W**.

That is the whole idea: brushes bring in power and flip the current. The rest of this page covers the downsides (wear, voltage drop, noise, friction) and why brushless motors avoid them.
:::

Whether you understand the brush as a **sliding electrical contact** and can place it in the commutation mechanism: brushes are the stationary conductors that carry current onto the rotating commutator, and together with the commutator they reverse the current in the armature coils at the right moments so torque stays in one direction. A strong answer also names what the brush must do (conduct, slide with low friction and wear, tolerate arcing), what it costs you (voltage drop, wear, noise) and why brushless motors exist.
:::

::: why
The brush and commutator are the defining feature, and the main limitation, of the brushed DC motor. They set the motor's life, its electrical noise, its maximum speed and current, and part of its efficiency. Nearly every other brush question (materials, wear, replacement, spring force) builds on this one, and so does the choice between a brushed and a brushless motor.
:::

::: concepts
- **Rotor winding and commutator**: coils on the rotor connected to a segmented ring on the shaft. See [[q0348]].
- **Lorentz torque and the need to reverse current**: torque keeps its sign only if the coil current reverses as the coil passes the neutral plane. See [[q0334]].
- **Sliding contact**: an electrical connection between a static and a moving surface, with friction and a contact voltage drop.
- **Contact voltage drop**: roughly constant volts per brush for carbon; a fixed loss $V_bI$.
- **Arcing at commutation**: inductive current reversal at the brush edge. See [[q0356]].
- **Slip rings**: continuous rings (no reversal) used to feed current to a rotor; contrast with a commutator.
:::

::: answer
"Brushes are the stationary contacts that deliver current to the spinning part of a brushed motor. They're blocks of conductive material, usually carbon or graphite, in small motors sometimes thin metal fingers, held by springs against the commutator, which is a ring of copper segments on the rotor. The motor's terminals connect to the brushes.

They do two jobs. First, they're the power connection: they let current flow from the fixed terminals into the windings of a rotating armature without a wire to twist. Second, together with the commutator they do the switching. As the rotor turns, each coil's segments pass under the brushes and the current in the coil reverses at the right angle. That keeps the torque pointing the same way, so the motor keeps spinning in one direction instead of oscillating.

The downsides come from the fact that they slide on a moving surface. They wear, which limits life. They have a contact voltage drop of roughly a volt for carbon brushes, so there's an $I\times V_{brush}$ loss. They make friction, and they spark a little at commutation, which makes electrical noise. That's why brushless motors, which switch the current electronically and have no sliding contact, last longer and are quieter, at the cost of needing a controller."
:::

::: technical
**Function.**
1. *Current transfer* between stationary terminals and the rotating armature through a sliding contact at the commutator surface.
2. *Commutation*: the pair of brushes sits at the magnetic neutral axis. Each armature coil moves between the two parallel paths of the winding once per half revolution; as its segments pass under a brush, the coil is briefly short-circuited and its current reverses from $+I_a/2$ to $-I_a/2$ (in a 2-parallel-path winding). The torque of each coil is thus $K\,|\sin\theta|$-like rather than alternating.

**Contact physics.** The contact area is a few asperities. A **film** (patina) of copper oxide and graphite forms on the commutator and is essential for low friction and wear. Typical electrical behaviour: the contact voltage drop is roughly constant (about 0.5–1 V per brush for carbon–graphite grades, less for metal–graphite; see [[q0358]]), so the power loss is $P_b = 2V_bI$. For 3 A with 2×0.5 V: 3 W. At 12 V and 3 A input (36 W) that is 8% of the input.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Brush voltage drop depends on grade, current density, speed, film and temperature. Values of about 0.5–1.5 V per brush for carbon types are typical in the literature; always check the manufacturer's data for your grade.

**Commutation limit.** The reactance voltage $e_r = L_c\,\Delta i/t_c$ (self-inductance of the commutated coil times the current reversal over the commutation time) must be small, otherwise there is arcing ([[q0359]]). Large machines add interpoles to cancel it.

**Brushes in different motors.**
- Small PM motors (toys, pagers): precious-metal or copper finger brushes; low-current, low-noise grades.
- Automotive/industrial: carbon–graphite or metal–graphite blocks in holders, with springs and braided leads ([[q0362]]).
- Universal motors: carbon blocks, sized for high speed and AC.

**Rotation direction and polarity.** Because brushes are fixed, reversing the motor means reversing the supply polarity; the neutral axis is symmetric so the brushes work in both directions.
:::

::: long
"Brushes are the stationary electrical contacts in a brushed motor. They're usually blocks of carbon or graphite, in small motors sometimes thin metal fingers, pressed by springs against the commutator, which is the segmented copper ring on the rotor. The motor's terminals connect to the brushes.

They do two jobs. The first is simply power transfer: the rotor windings are spinning, so you need a sliding contact to get current into them from the fixed terminals. The second is commutation. Each rotor coil has its ends connected to commutator segments. As the rotor turns, the segments slide past the brushes, and when a coil passes the neutral position, the brushes move onto the next segments and the current in that coil reverses. That's what keeps torque the same sign. If the current in a coil didn't reverse, its torque would flip sign every half turn and the motor would just rock back and forth. With several coils and segments, the sum is a fairly smooth torque, ripple of only a few percent.

Now the costs. Because the brush slides on a moving surface it wears, so brush life limits motor life, often a few thousand hours. There's a contact voltage drop, roughly half to one volt per brush for carbon, so I lose around a couple of volts times the current: for 3 amps that's several watts, maybe 8% of a 12 volt motor's input. There's friction. And at the moment the brush leaves a segment, the coil is inductive, so you get arcing and noise: that's why you add capacitors across the terminals.

All of that is the reason brushless motors exist. They move the windings to the stator and do the switching electronically, so there's no sliding contact to wear or spark, in exchange for a controller. Brushed motors stay popular because they're cheap and simple to drive: just apply a voltage."
:::

::: terms
- **Brush**: stationary conductive contact (carbon, graphite or metal) that rides on the commutator.
- **Commutator**: rotating ring of insulated copper segments connected to the armature coils.
- **Commutation**: reversal of current in a coil as it passes the neutral axis.
- **Neutral axis**: angular position where coil sides are not cutting flux; the brushes sit near here.
- **Contact voltage drop**: voltage lost across the brush-commutator interface.
- **Film (patina)**: thin oxide/graphite layer on the commutator that lubricates and stabilises contact.
- **Brush holder and spring**: guide and pressure mechanism ([[q0362]]).
- **Pigtail (shunt)**: flexible lead carrying current from brush to terminal.
- **Reactance voltage**: $L\,di/dt$ of the coil under commutation.
- **Slip ring**: continuous conductive ring for current transfer without reversal.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "brushes are contacts that carry current to the rotor". An exceptional answer:

- Gives both functions: power transfer *and* current reversal, and explains why reversal is needed (torque sign).
- Explains the arrangement: stationary brushes at the neutral axis, rotating segments, the coil being shorted briefly during commutation.
- Quantifies the costs: volt-level contact drop and its loss $2V_bI$, wear, friction, arcing, EMI.
- Distinguishes a commutator from slip rings, and brushed from brushless in one sentence.
- Mentions brush materials and springs and that a film on the commutator is part of the system ([[q0358]], [[q0362]]).
- Mentions practical fixes: capacitors across terminals, brush grade, neutral position.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying brushes rotate.** They are fixed; the commutator rotates under them ([[q0348]]).
- **Saying brushes only conduct current.** They also, with the commutator, perform the switching that makes a DC motor work.
- **Calling the commutator and brushes "slip rings".** Slip rings are continuous rings; the commutator is segmented and switches.
- **Believing brush resistance is ohmic.** The drop is roughly constant volts (a film/contact phenomenon), not proportional to current, over much of the range.
- **Forgetting the film.** Brushes working on a clean, polished commutator can wear fast; the patina is part of the design.
- **Treating brush wear as a defect.** It's inevitable in sliding contacts; design for life, don't expect none.
:::

::: deep
## The problem brushes solve

A motor's rotor windings need current, but the rotor spins. You can't hard-wire a battery to a spinning coil; the wires would twist. A sliding contact solves this: a stationary conductor (the brush) rubs against a conducting surface moving with the rotor (the commutator or a slip ring).

If that were all, you'd use slip rings. But there is a second problem: torque in a coil is $T = NBIA\cos\theta$-like, reversing every half turn with a fixed current direction. For continuous rotation the current direction in each coil must flip every half turn. The commutator does this mechanically: it's a **rotating polarity switch**.

Figure: End view of a 6-segment commutator. Brushes (green, static, spring-loaded) touch segments 2 and 5. Each armature coil is connected between two adjacent segments, so the coils form two parallel paths from the + brush to the − brush (one round each side of the ring). As the ring turns, each brush briefly bridges two adjacent segments, shorting the coil between them while it passes the neutral axis; after 60° the brushes sit on the next pair of segments and that coil has moved into the other path, so its current has reversed.

In a simple 2-pole winding, the rotor winding forms **two parallel paths** between the brushes. At any instant, half of the coils carry $+I/2$ and the other half $-I/2$ (in terms of direction along the rotor). When a coil crosses the neutral axis, it moves from one path to the other, which means its current changes from $+I/2$ to $-I/2$. The brushes are placed so this happens when the coil sides are not under a pole (little torque at that moment), so reversing costs nothing in torque.

## Torque ripple and the number of segments

One coil only gives $|\cos\theta|$ torque with zeros twice a revolution, so a 2-segment motor can stall at the null. More coils and segments overlap, so the sum has little ripple ([[q0348]]: 3 coils give about ±7%; 12 coils about ±0.4%). Cheap toy motors use 3 segments; better ones use 7–13 or more.

## What the brush must do

Material and mechanical requirements (details in [[q0358]] and [[q0362]]):
- **Conduct** the full armature current with low resistance (current density limit).
- **Slide** on the commutator with low friction and low wear, using a stable film.
- **Commutate**: a high enough resistivity/contact drop to damp the short-circuit current in the commutated coil (carbon brushes are deliberately resistive for this reason; low-resistance copper brushes would let large circulating currents flow).
- **Follow** the commutator surface despite runout and vibration.
- **Be soft** enough not to wear the commutator excessively, hard enough to last.

## The losses

1. **Electrical:** $P_b = 2V_bI$. With a 0.5 V drop per brush and 3 A: 3 W. In a small, low-voltage motor (a 3 V toy or 1.5 V) a 1 V total drop is a third of the supply, so such motors use precious-metal brushes (lower drop and resistance).
2. **Friction:** $P_f = \mu F_n v$: with $\mu = 0.2$, force 0.5 N per brush, and surface speed 3.8 m/s (a 12 mm commutator at 6000 rpm): $0.2\times0.5\times3.8\times2 = 0.76$ W for two brushes.
3. **Arcing/commutation losses and noise** ([[q0355]]).

For 3 A at 12 V (36 W in) the brush-related loss is about $3 + 0.8 = 3.8$ W, about 10%, a significant part of a small motor's total loss, and one reason brushless motors are more efficient.

## Brushes and the commutation process

When a brush straddles two segments, it short-circuits the coil between them. The coil has inductance $L_c$ and sits in the neutral zone, so little EMF is induced, and the brush's resistance limits the circulating current. The current must go from $+I/2$ to $-I/2$ within the commutation time $t_c$ (the time the brush bridges the segments). If the current hasn't finished reversing when the brush leaves the leaving segment, the remaining inductive current has to jump the gap: a spark ([[q0356]]). Ways to help it: carbon (resistive) brushes, brush shifted slightly past the neutral axis, interpoles or compensation in large machines, and in small motors a low $L_c$, more segments.

## Alternatives

- **Brushless**: the stator carries the windings and an electronic bridge switches them based on rotor position ([[q0363]]).
- **Coreless/ironless brushed**: lower $L_c$ so easier commutation; precious-metal brushes.
- **Slip rings** (wound-rotor induction motors, generators, rotating joints): continuous rings with brushes, no switching.
- **Inductive or capacitive coupling**: no contact at all for signals or small power across a rotating interface.

::: applications
- **Toys, small appliances, pumps and fans**: tiny PM motors with sheet-metal or precious-metal brushes.
- **Automotive**: window, seat, wiper and blower motors with carbon brushes in holders.
- **Power tools and vacuum cleaners**: universal motors with carbon brushes sized for speed and AC.
- **Industrial DC drives and elevators (older)**: large carbon-brush DC motors with replaceable brush sets.
- **Generators/alternators**: slip rings and brushes feeding the rotor field winding.
- **Rotating electrical joints, slip-ring assemblies in wind turbines and radar**: brushes on continuous rings.

::: assumptions
- **Brushed PM DC motor** with carbon or metal brushes on a segmented copper commutator at the neutral axis.
- **2-pole, simple lap/wave winding** picture for the commutation explanation. Multi-pole motors repeat the pattern with several brush pairs or equalising connections.
- **Brush drop and friction** values are typical, not specific to a grade; they depend on current density, speed, humidity and film.
- Whether brushes are "the contact" or "the contact plus switch" depends on viewpoint: electrically, a brush is a sliding contact and the commutator is the switch; mechanically they function as a pair, so answers often describe them together.
- At very low voltage and current (signal-level), dry-circuit contact behaviour dominates and precious metals are used.
:::

::: followups
### Why do brushes have a voltage drop and why is it roughly constant?
The contact interface is a film plus tiny asperity contacts and a thin layer of oxide/graphite; electrons cross by tunnelling/thermionic mechanisms and through the film. This gives a drop that depends weakly on current (a logarithmic-like characteristic), roughly 0.5–1 V per carbon brush at normal current densities. At low current the drop is smaller, and at very high current density it grows; consult the grade data. The practical effect is that small low-voltage motors lose a larger fraction.

### Why are carbon brushes deliberately resistive?
During commutation the brush shorts a coil. A low-resistance brush (copper) allows a large circulating current in that coil, causing heating and sparking. A resistive carbon brush limits it and helps the coil's current reverse smoothly, at the cost of a higher contact drop. Metal–graphite grades trade some commutation behaviour for lower drop and higher current capacity ([[q0358]]).

### Why do reversible brushed motors work equally well both ways?
The brushes sit on the neutral axis, which is symmetric. Reversing the supply reverses the current in every coil, so torque reverses; commutation happens at the same geometrical points in both directions. If the brushes had been advanced (shifted) for one direction, performance would degrade in reverse. Many motors use symmetrical brush positions for this reason.

### What replaces brushes in a brushless motor?
An electronic inverter and a position sensor (Hall sensors, back-EMF sensing or an encoder). The inverter switches the stator phase currents at the right rotor angles, which does what the commutator did mechanically ([[q0363]]).

### What if the brush loses contact momentarily at high speed?
The armature current is interrupted, arcing occurs (inductive kick), the motor torque drops, noise appears, and the brush surface may burn. This is "brush bounce", and it limits the motor's maximum speed. The cures are higher spring force, lighter brushes, better commutator roundness and smaller TIR, or a different brush design (multi-finger brushes).

### How would you tell from the outside that the brushes are worn?
Increased noise and sparking, reduced torque or erratic running at low speed, higher no-load current or higher voltage drop, burnt smell, and carbon dust. Direct inspection: brush length below the minimum mark, or a spring-loaded brush showing little remaining compression ([[q0361]]).
:::
