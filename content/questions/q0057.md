::: basics
### In plain words
The photoelectric effect is when light hitting a material knocks electrons out of it. Light arrives in tiny packets called photons. One photon gives its energy to one electron. If a photon carries enough energy, the electron escapes. If it does not, nothing happens, no matter how bright the light is. Solar cells and camera sensors use a version of this.

### Picture it
Picture a coconut shy at a fair. Each ball is a photon, and each coconut is an electron held on its stand. A fast ball knocks a coconut off. A slow ball just bounces off, and throwing a hundred slow balls does not help, because each hits on its own. Brighter light is more balls per second, not faster balls. The analogy is loose: a photon's "speed" is really its energy, set by its frequency.

### Words you need
- **Photon**: one packet of light. Its energy is $hf$, where $f$ is the frequency and $h$ is a fixed number of nature (Planck's constant).
- **Frequency**: how many wave cycles per second. Blue light has a higher frequency than red.
- **Work function ($\phi$)**: the energy needed to free an electron from a metal's surface.
- **Band gap**: in a semiconductor, the energy needed to free an electron. See [[q0055]].
- **Electron-hole pair**: a freed electron and the empty spot it left behind. See [[q0061]].

### The simplest example
Energies here are in eV (electron-volts, a tiny unit of energy). Take a metal with a work function of 2 eV. A photon of 3 eV frees an electron and leaves 3 - 2 = 1 eV as energy of motion (kinetic energy). A photon of 1.5 eV frees nothing, even if you shine a million of them. A brighter 3 eV beam gives more electrons, each still with 1 eV. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers Einstein's equation, and the version inside semiconductors that powers photodiodes and solar cells.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand the experiment that proved light comes in **quanta** and can state Einstein's equation $KE_{max} = hf - \phi$ with its consequences:

- There is a **threshold frequency**: below it no electrons come out, however bright the light.
- **Intensity** sets *how many* electrons come out; **frequency** sets *how energetic* they are.
- You can turn photon energy into numbers fast ($E[\text{eV}] \approx 1240/\lambda[\text{nm}]$).

For a hardware role, the hidden test is whether you can connect it to devices: the **internal** photoelectric effect in semiconductors is how photodiodes, solar cells, image sensors and optocouplers work, with a band-gap cutoff wavelength playing the role of the work function.
:::

::: why
Every light sensor you'll put on a board is a photoelectric device: a photodiode in a pulse-oximeter or LiDAR receiver, a CMOS image sensor, the phototransistor in an optocoupler, a solar cell. The same equation sets what they can detect (silicon is blind beyond about 1.1 µm, which is why 1550 nm telecom uses InGaAs) and how much current they give per watt of light. It's also a classic physics question used to see if you can explain a non-obvious experimental result clearly and quantitatively.
:::

::: concepts
- **Photon energy**: light of frequency $f$ comes in packets of energy $E = hf = hc/\lambda$; handy form $E[\text{eV}] = 1240/\lambda[\text{nm}]$.
- **Work function $\phi$**: the minimum energy to pull an electron out of a metal surface into vacuum (≈2–6 eV depending on the metal).
- **Electron-volt**: the energy an electron gains crossing 1 V, $1.602\times10^{-19}$ J; makes stopping voltage and kinetic energy the same number.
- **Band gap**: in a semiconductor, the energy to promote an electron from the valence to the conduction band; the internal-photoeffect analogue of $\phi$. See [[q0055]], [[q0061]].
- **Energy conservation**: one photon gives all its energy to one electron; whatever isn't used escaping becomes kinetic energy.
- **Quantum efficiency and responsivity**: what fraction of photons produce a collected electron, and how many amps you get per watt.
:::

::: answer
"The photoelectric effect is when light hitting a material, classically a metal surface, knocks electrons out of it.

What makes it famous is how it behaves. If the light's frequency is below a certain threshold, no electrons come out, no matter how bright you make it. Above the threshold, electrons come out immediately, and their maximum kinetic energy depends only on the frequency, not the intensity. Brighter light just gives you more electrons.

Einstein explained this in 1905 by treating light as photons, each with energy $hf$. One photon gives all its energy to one electron. Part of that pays the work function $\phi$, the energy to escape the surface, and the rest is kinetic energy: $KE_{max} = hf - \phi$. So if $hf$ is less than $\phi$, nothing escapes. You can measure $KE_{max}$ as a stopping voltage, and plotting it against frequency gives a straight line with slope $h/e$.

For hardware, the version that matters is the internal photoelectric effect. In a semiconductor, a photon with energy above the band gap creates an electron-hole pair. That's how photodiodes, solar cells and image sensors work, and it's why silicon can't see past about 1.1 µm."
:::

::: technical
**Observations (Hertz 1887, Lenard ~1902) that classical waves couldn't explain:**
1. A **threshold frequency** $f_0$ below which no emission occurs at any intensity.
2. $KE_{max}$ of emitted electrons depends linearly on $f$ and **not at all** on intensity.
3. Photocurrent (number of electrons per second) is proportional to intensity.
4. No measurable time delay, even in very dim light.

**Einstein (1905):** light energy is quantized, $E = hf$. One photon is absorbed by one electron:
$$KE_{max} = hf - \phi,\qquad f_0 = \phi/h,\qquad \lambda_0 = hc/\phi$$

**Stopping voltage.** A retarding potential $V_s$ stops the most energetic electrons when $eV_s = KE_{max}$, so
$$V_s = \frac{h}{e}f - \frac{\phi}{e}$$
A plot of $V_s$ vs $f$ is a line with slope $h/e = 4.14\times10^{-15}$ V·s for every metal; only the intercept ($\phi$) changes. Millikan's careful measurements of this slope confirmed Einstein's equation and gave an accurate value of $h$.

**Numbers.** $E[\text{eV}] = 1239.8/\lambda[\text{nm}]$. Sodium ($\phi \approx 2.28$ eV) under 400 nm light (3.10 eV): $KE_{max} = 0.82$ eV, $V_s = 0.82$ V, threshold $\lambda_0 \approx 544$ nm. Typical work functions: Cs ≈ 2.1 eV, Na ≈ 2.3, Zn ≈ 4.3, Cu ≈ 4.7, Pt ≈ 5.6 eV.

**Internal photoelectric effect (semiconductors).** A photon with $hf > E_g$ creates an electron-hole pair. Cutoff wavelength $\lambda_c = 1240/E_g$: Si (1.12 eV) ≈ 1.1 µm, Ge ≈ 1.9 µm, GaAs ≈ 870 nm, InGaAs (≈0.75 eV) ≈ 1.65 µm. A photodiode's responsivity is
$$\mathcal R = \eta\frac{q\lambda}{hc} \approx \eta\,\frac{\lambda[\text{nm}]}{1240}\ \text{A/W}$$
so an ideal ($\eta = 1$) detector gives 0.69 A/W at 850 nm; real Si photodiodes reach roughly 0.5–0.6 A/W there.
:::

::: long
"The photoelectric effect is the emission of electrons when light hits a material. The classic setup is a metal plate in a vacuum tube, a collector plate opposite it, and you measure the current as you shine light on the metal.

What people found didn't fit the wave picture of light. If light were just a wave, a brighter beam should deliver more energy and kick electrons out harder, and even dim red light should eventually work if you wait long enough to build up energy. Instead, there's a sharp threshold frequency. Below it, zero electrons, even with an intense beam. Above it, electrons appear instantly even with dim light. And the maximum electron energy depends only on frequency. Intensity just changes how many electrons you get.

Einstein's explanation in 1905 was that light comes in photons with energy $hf$. A single photon is absorbed by a single electron. The electron spends the work function $\phi$ escaping the surface, and keeps the rest as kinetic energy, so $KE_{max} = hf - \phi$. You measure it by applying a reverse voltage until the current stops: that stopping voltage in volts equals $KE_{max}$ in electron-volts. For example, sodium has a work function of about 2.3 eV, and 400 nm light is about 3.1 eV per photon, so the stopping voltage is about 0.8 V. Red light at 650 nm is only 1.9 eV, so sodium gives nothing no matter how bright.

For hardware, the version that matters is the internal photoelectric effect. In a semiconductor, a photon with energy above the band gap promotes an electron into the conduction band and leaves a hole. In a photodiode the junction field sweeps them apart and you get a current proportional to light power, roughly half an amp per watt for silicon in the near-infrared. The band gap sets the cutoff: silicon stops absorbing around 1.1 µm, which is why 1550 nm fibre receivers use InGaAs."
:::

::: terms
- **Photon**: a quantum of light with energy $E = hf = hc/\lambda$.
- **Work function ($\phi$)**: minimum energy to remove an electron from a solid's surface to vacuum.
- **Threshold frequency / wavelength**: $f_0 = \phi/h$, $\lambda_0 = hc/\phi$; below $f_0$ (above $\lambda_0$) there is no emission.
- **Stopping voltage ($V_s$)**: the retarding voltage that reduces photocurrent to zero; $eV_s = KE_{max}$.
- **Planck's constant ($h$)**: $6.626\times10^{-34}$ J·s; $h/e = 4.14\times10^{-15}$ V·s.
- **External vs internal photoelectric effect**: electrons leave the material (photoemission) vs electrons are promoted inside it (photoconduction, photovoltaic effect).
- **Quantum efficiency ($\eta$)**: collected electrons per incident photon.
- **Responsivity ($\mathcal R$)**: photocurrent per optical power, A/W.
:::

::: exceptional
**Average:** "Light hits metal and electrons come off. Einstein won the Nobel Prize for it."

**Exceptional:**
- Lists the **experimental facts that broke the wave model** (threshold, intensity-independent energy, no time delay) and explains each with photons.
- Writes $KE_{max} = hf - \phi$ and explains the **stopping-voltage measurement** and the universal slope $h/e$.
- Does a **quick calculation** with $E = 1240/\lambda$ (e.g. sodium under 400 nm gives ~0.8 V stopping voltage).
- Bridges to **semiconductors**: band gap as the threshold, cutoff wavelength $1240/E_g$, responsivity $\eta\lambda/1240$ A/W, and real devices (photodiodes, image sensors, solar cells, optocouplers, photomultipliers).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Brighter light gives faster electrons."** Intensity changes the *number* of photons (and electrons), not the energy per photon. $KE_{max}$ depends on frequency only.
- **Confusing $KE_{max}$ with every electron's energy.** Electrons from deeper states lose more energy getting out, so there's a spread; the equation gives the maximum.
- **Mixing up wavelength and frequency direction.** Higher frequency means *shorter* wavelength and *more* energy per photon. Red (650 nm, 1.9 eV) is less energetic than UV (254 nm, 4.9 eV).
- **Thinking a solar cell or photodiode "uses the photoelectric effect" exactly like a phototube.** Electrons don't leave the material. It's the internal effect: the band gap, not the work function, sets the threshold, and a junction field separates the carriers.
- **Assuming photons above the threshold are fully used.** Excess energy above $E_g$ is lost as heat in a semiconductor (thermalization), one reason single-junction solar cells have a theoretical efficiency limit around 33%.
:::

## Why classical waves fail

Treat light as a continuous wave. Energy arrives uniformly over the surface, and an electron should absorb energy at a rate set by intensity. Then:

| Classical prediction | What actually happens | Photon explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Brighter light → faster electrons | $KE_{max}$ independent of intensity | Each photon still has $hf$; more photons, more electrons |
| Any frequency works with enough intensity | Hard threshold $f_0$ | One photon must supply at least $\phi$ by itself |
| Dim light needs time to accumulate energy | No measurable delay | The energy arrives in one packet |

How long would the classical delay be? Give one atom a cross-section of about $10^{-19}$ m² (radius ~0.2 nm) in dim light of 1 mW/m². It collects $10^{-22}$ W and needs $2.28\text{ eV} = 3.65\times10^{-19}$ J, which takes about **3,700 s, an hour**. Real phototubes respond with no measurable delay, at any intensity.

## Einstein's equation, with numbers

Energy conservation for one photon and one electron:

$$hf = \phi + KE_{max}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad KE_{max} = hf - \phi$$

The most useful conversion in optoelectronics:

$$E[\text{eV}] = \frac{hc}{q\lambda} = \frac{1239.8}{\lambda[\text{nm}]}$$

**Worked example (sodium, $\phi \approx 2.28$ eV):**
- 400 nm: $E = 3.10$ eV, $KE_{max} = 0.82$ eV, $V_s = 0.82$ V, maximum electron speed $\sqrt{2KE/m} \approx 5.4\times10^5$ m/s.
- Threshold: $\lambda_0 = 1239.8/2.28 \approx 544$ nm (green), $f_0 \approx 551$ THz.
- 650 nm red laser: $E = 1.91$ eV < 2.28 eV, so **no emission** at any power.

> Work functions depend on crystal face, surface cleanliness and oxidation, so published values vary by a few tenths of an eV (e.g. sodium is quoted from about 2.3 to 2.75 eV). The values here are typical textbook figures used for illustration.

## The version engineers use: the internal photoelectric effect

In a semiconductor, a photon doesn't need to eject an electron into vacuum. It only needs to lift one from the valence band to the conduction band, so the threshold is the **band gap** $E_g$ rather than the work function ([[q0061]]). The result is an electron-hole pair:

- In a **photoconductor** (e.g. a CdS light-dependent resistor), the extra carriers simply lower the resistance.
- In a **photodiode or solar cell**, the junction's built-in field ([[q0063]]) sweeps the electron and hole apart, producing a current proportional to optical power.

**Cutoff wavelength** $\lambda_c = 1240/E_g$:

| Material | $E_g$ (eV) | $\lambda_c$ | Typical use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Si | 1.12 | ≈1.1 µm | visible/NIR photodiodes, image sensors, 850/905 nm receivers |
| GaAs | 1.42 | ≈870 nm | high-speed detectors, solar cells (space) |
| Ge | 0.66 | ≈1.9 µm | older NIR detectors |
| InGaAs (lattice-matched to InP) | ≈0.75 | ≈1.65 µm | 1310/1550 nm fibre receivers |

**Responsivity**: each collected photon gives one electron, so with quantum efficiency $\eta$,

$$\mathcal R = \frac{I}{P} = \eta\frac{q}{hf} = \eta\,\frac{\lambda[\text{nm}]}{1240}\ \text{A/W}$$

An ideal detector gives 0.69 A/W at 850 nm and 1.06 A/W at 1310 nm. Longer wavelength means more photons per watt, so more current, right up to the cutoff, where it collapses. A real silicon photodiode at 850 nm with $\eta \approx 0.8$ gives about 0.55 A/W. With 1 mW of 650 nm light (about $3.3\times10^{15}$ photons/s) an ideal detector would deliver 0.52 mA.

## Where you'll meet it in hardware

- **Photodiodes and transimpedance amplifiers**: optical receivers, pulse oximeters, LiDAR, ambient-light sensors. The TIA converts $I = \mathcal R P$ into a voltage.
- **Image sensors (CMOS/CCD)**: each pixel's photodiode integrates photo-generated electrons; the "full-well capacity" is literally a count of electrons.
- **Optocouplers**: an LED's photons generate current in a phototransistor across an isolation barrier.
- **Photomultiplier tubes and image intensifiers**: the *external* effect, followed by electron multiplication, for single-photon sensitivity.
- **UV-erasable EPROMs**: UV light gives electrons on the floating gate enough energy to cross the oxide barrier (internal photoemission), erasing the cell.
- **Solar cells**: photovoltaic conversion; excess photon energy above $E_g$ is lost as heat, which limits single-junction efficiency.
:::

::: applications
- **Photodiode receivers**: fibre-optic links (InGaAs at 1310/1550 nm), 850 nm multimode links (Si or GaAs), LiDAR (Si avalanche photodiodes at 905 nm).
- **CMOS image sensors** in phones and machine-vision cameras: per-pixel photodiodes convert photons to electrons.
- **Solar panels**: silicon photovoltaic cells, with multi-junction III-V cells on spacecraft.
- **Optocouplers and optical encoders**: LED plus photodetector for isolation or position sensing.
- **Photomultiplier tubes**: external photoemission from alkali photocathodes for scintillation detectors and low-light measurement.
- **Ambient light and proximity sensors** in phones, which use filtered photodiodes.
:::

::: assumptions
- **One photon, one electron.** True for normal light intensities. Very intense pulsed lasers can cause multi-photon absorption, where two photons below threshold together eject an electron.
- **Clean, well-defined surface** for the metal case. Work function depends on surface condition, so thresholds shift.
- **$KE_{max}$ refers to the most energetic electrons**, those from the top of the occupied states (the Fermi level). Others come out slower. In a real tube the measured stopping voltage is also offset by the contact potential between emitter and collector, which shifts the line but not its slope.
- **Room-temperature photon energies (visible/UV).** At X-ray energies, photoelectric absorption by inner-shell electrons competes with Compton scattering; the "photoelectric effect" in radiation physics means that inner-shell process.
- **Semiconductor responsivity values** assume the photon is absorbed in the depletion region or within a diffusion length of it. Near the cutoff, absorption length gets very long (silicon is an indirect-gap material), so efficiency falls before $\lambda_c$.
:::

::: followups
### What stopping voltage would you measure for zinc illuminated by 254 nm UV?
Photon energy $= 1239.8/254 \approx 4.88$ eV. With $\phi_{Zn} \approx 4.3$ eV, $KE_{max} \approx 0.58$ eV, so $V_s \approx 0.58$ V. Visible light wouldn't work at all: zinc's threshold is about 288 nm.

### Why can't a silicon photodiode detect 1550 nm light?
1550 nm photons carry $1240/1550 = 0.80$ eV, less than silicon's 1.12 eV gap, so they can't create electron-hole pairs and pass straight through. Silicon's cutoff is around 1.1 µm. Telecom receivers at 1550 nm use InGaAs (≈0.75 eV gap, cutoff ≈1.65 µm) or germanium.

### If you double the light intensity, what happens to the photocurrent and to the stopping voltage?
Photocurrent doubles because twice as many photons arrive per second, each releasing at most one electron. The stopping voltage is unchanged because each photon still has the same energy $hf$. This is the cleanest experimental argument for photons.

### How much current does 10 µW of 850 nm light produce in a silicon photodiode with 80% quantum efficiency?
$\mathcal R = 0.8\times850/1240 \approx 0.55$ A/W, so $I \approx 0.55\times10\ \mu\text{W} = 5.5$ µA. Through a 100 kΩ transimpedance resistor that's 0.55 V.

### What if you used a very intense infrared laser below the threshold on a metal?
In ordinary conditions nothing is emitted, since no single photon has enough energy. At extreme intensities (focused femtosecond lasers), two or more photons can be absorbed by the same electron almost simultaneously (multiphoton photoemission), and electrons do come out. That's a nonlinear effect and doesn't contradict Einstein's picture: energy is still delivered in units of $hf$.
:::
