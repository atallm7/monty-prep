::: basics
### In plain words
A half-bridge is one pair of switches that connects its output to the supply or to ground. "Asymmetrical" here means the two halves do unequal jobs: either the two switches are sized differently, or the load only ever sees one polarity. A symmetrical H-bridge has four equal switches and drives a load in both directions. The question asks when lopsided is smart and when you need equal halves.

### Picture it
Imagine two workers sharing a shift. One works 90% of the day and the other 10%. You would give the busy one the better tools, not split the budget evenly. A buck converter at low duty cycle is like that: the low-side switch carries current most of the time, so it deserves more silicon. But if both workers must do the same job in turn, as in a reversing motor or a transformer, you want identical workers. The analogy leaves out switching losses, which also differ between the two sides.

### Words you need
- **Duty cycle ($D$)**: the fraction of each cycle the high-side switch is on. See [[q0179]].
- **On-resistance ($R_{DS(on)}$)**: the small resistance of a switch that is on; current through it makes heat.
- **Conduction loss**: heat from current in that resistance, $P = I^2 R$ times the fraction of time it conducts.
- **Unipolar vs bipolar**: the load sees one voltage polarity, or both.
- **Volt-seconds**: voltage multiplied by time; a transformer needs these to cancel to zero on average.

### The simplest example
Take a buck converter with $D = 0.1$ and a 10 A load. Two equal 10 mΩ switches give $10^2 \times (0.1 \times 0.01 + 0.9 \times 0.01) = 1.0$ W. Now keep the same total silicon but make the high side 20 mΩ and the low side 6.67 mΩ. The loss becomes $100 \times (0.1 \times 0.02 + 0.9 \times 0.00667) = 0.8$ W, a 20% saving. At $D = 0.5$ equal switches are already the best. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page derives the best split, covers one-way loads, and explains why bipolar loads and transformers need a symmetrical bridge.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can interpret a loosely worded design question and then answer it with engineering reasons. "Asymmetrical" and "symmetrical" can refer to the **devices** (unequal vs equal FETs in a leg), to the **load voltage** (one polarity vs both), or to the **topology** (a leg with diodes, a bridge with four equal switches). The interviewer is looking for:

1. A stated interpretation, then a clear rule for each.
2. The quantitative reason an asymmetrical leg wins at low or high duty cycle (conduction loss, switching loss).
3. The reason you need a symmetrical bridge: bipolar voltage, transformer volt-second balance, four-quadrant motor operation, equal stress.
4. Awareness that asymmetry is a **design optimization** (area allocation), not a different circuit.
:::

::: why
Real power stages are not drawn from identical parts. A 12 V to 1.2 V buck runs at 10% duty: the low-side FET conducts 90% of the time, so giving both FETs the same $R_{DS(on)}$ wastes silicon. A motor that reverses, or a transformer that must see no net DC, needs a symmetrical bridge. Knowing when "cheap and optimized" ends and "symmetrical and robust" begins is a core design-judgement skill, and it echoes the buck-converter power-bridge questions ([[q0201]], [[q0205]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Duty cycle and conduction time**: the high side conducts $D$, the low side $1-D$ ([[q0179]]).
- **Conduction loss**: $I^2R_{DS(on)}$ times conduction fraction; sets the optimum device split ([[q0205]]).
- **Switching loss asymmetry**: the high side switches with full $V_{in}$ across it; the low side turns on near zero volts ([[q0207]], [[q0209]]).
- **Unipolar vs bipolar drive**: a ground-referenced half-bridge produces one polarity ([[q0423]]).
- **Volt-second balance**: a transformer or inductor needs zero DC average voltage ([[q0041]]).
- **Four-quadrant operation**: drive and brake in both directions needs an H-bridge ([[q0424]]).
:::

::: answer
"I'd read asymmetrical half-bridge two ways, and the answer is similar for both. One is a half-bridge whose two FETs are deliberately different: the high-side and low-side have different on-resistance and gate charge. The other is a half-bridge driving a one-way load, where the output only ever swings between zero and the supply.

You can use that whenever the load only needs one polarity and the duty cycle is lopsided. A buck converter stepping 12 V down to 1.2 V is on for 10% of the time, so the low-side FET carries the current 90% of the time. If I split the same silicon area so the low side is three times more conductive than the high side, I cut conduction loss by about 20%. Solenoids, fans, heaters and LEDs are the same story: they need one polarity, so one leg is enough.

I need a symmetrical H-bridge when the load needs both polarities with equal strength: a motor that reverses and brakes, a voice coil, a class-D amplifier driving a speaker, or a transformer that needs zero DC volt-seconds. There the duty cycle averages around 50% and both directions carry the same current, so equal FETs are the right choice, and a single leg can't make a negative voltage anyway."

::: technical
**Device asymmetry (buck-type leg).** Let total conductance be fixed: $G_H+G_L=G_{tot}$ (equal die area). Conduction loss:
$$P_{cond}=I^2\left(\frac{D}{G_H}+\frac{1-D}{G_L}\right)$$
Minimum at $G_H/G_L=\sqrt{D/(1-D)}$, with improvement ratio
$$\frac{P_{opt}}{P_{sym}}=\frac{(\sqrt D+\sqrt{1-D})^2}{2}.$$
Example: $D=0.1$, $I=10$ A, symmetric pair of 10 mΩ ($G=100$ S each): $P_{sym}=100\,(0.1\times0.01+0.9\times0.01)=1.0$ W. Optimal: $G_H=50$ S (20 mΩ), $G_L=150$ S (6.67 mΩ): $P=100\,(0.1\times0.02+0.9\times0.00667)=0.8$ W. At $D=0.5$ the optimum is symmetric. Switching loss and $Q_{gd}$ also differ between sides, which pushes the high side toward a low-$Q_{gd}$ device ([[q0205]]).

**Load asymmetry (unipolar).** A single leg with the load to a rail produces $0\le v\le V_M$. It is "asymmetric" in that the load sees only one polarity and one current direction (plus a freewheel). Suitable if no reversal, no negative voltage and no active reverse braking are needed.

**Asymmetric bridge for switched-reluctance (SRM) phases.** Each phase has one high-side switch, one low-side switch and two diodes. Both on: $+V_M$; one off: zero voltage freewheel; both off: $-V_M$ through the diodes as energy returns to the supply. Torque in an SRM does not depend on current polarity, so the phase never needs the reverse current an H-bridge provides. A diode-clamped asymmetrical leg is therefore enough.

**When you need a symmetrical H-bridge:**
- Bipolar load voltage: reversing motor, voice coil, piezo, bipolar stepper winding.
- Zero DC average: transformers, wireless-power coils and resonant tanks, where any DC volt-second imbalance walks the flux into saturation (full or half bridge with symmetrical 50% drive and a DC-blocking capacitor).
- Four-quadrant torque control with active regeneration and plugging ([[q0424]]).
- Equal stress on all four devices at $D\approx0.5$, so one part number and one gate-drive design.
- Bidirectional power flow with a battery on each side (four-switch buck-boost).
:::

I need a symmetrical H-bridge when the load must see both polarities or zero DC average: reversing motors, voice coils, class-D speakers and transformers, where equal FETs and symmetrical drive are the right choice."

::: long
"First I want to say how I'm reading the question. 'Asymmetrical half-bridge' could mean the FETs in the leg are different sizes, or it could mean a leg driving a load that only ever sees one polarity. The engineering is related, so I'll cover both.

Take the different-FET case. In a leg, the high side conducts for a fraction D of the cycle, the low side for one minus D. If D is 10%, as in a 12 volt to 1.2 volt buck, the low side is on 90% of the time. If I have a fixed budget of silicon, the best allocation puts more of it in the low side, in proportion to the square root of one minus D over D. For D equals 0.1 that makes the low side three times more conductive than the high side, and the loss drops from 1.0 watt to 0.8 watt in a 10 amp example. The high side also switches with the full input voltage across it, so I'd choose a low gate-charge device there, which is another asymmetry.

For the one-polarity case: if the load only needs zero to the supply, a fan, a solenoid, a heater, a buck converter, one half-bridge is all I need. Even switched-reluctance motors, which need no reverse current, use an asymmetric bridge with two switches and two diodes per phase.

Now when do I need a symmetrical H-bridge? When the load must see both polarities: a reversing brushed motor, a voice coil, a bipolar stepper winding, a speaker in a class-D amplifier. Also when the load must have zero DC average, like a transformer: any asymmetry builds up a DC volt-second and walks the core into saturation. In those cases the operating point is around 50% duty and current flows in both directions, so equal FETs are the natural choice, and having four identical devices simplifies gate drive, thermal design and sourcing. Four-quadrant motor control, with braking and regeneration, needs that full bridge."
:::

::: terms
- **Asymmetrical half-bridge**: a leg with unequal devices or a one-polarity load; also the name of specific topologies (SRM asymmetric bridge, asymmetrical half-bridge converter).
- **Symmetrical H-bridge**: four equal switches with a floating load, bipolar output.
- **Unipolar / bipolar drive**: output of one polarity vs both polarities.
- **Optimal split**: $G_H/G_L=\sqrt{D/(1-D)}$ for minimum conduction loss at fixed area.
- **Volt-second balance**: the average voltage across an inductor or transformer winding must be zero.
- **Four-quadrant operation**: both signs of voltage and current across the load.
- **Figure of merit ($R_{DS(on)}Q_{gd}$)**: trades conduction against switching loss.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "asymmetrical = different FETs, symmetrical = same FETs".

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the interpretation and the second reading explicitly.
- Derives the optimum device split $\sqrt{D/(1-D)}$ and gives a numeric payoff (0.8 W vs 1.0 W at $D=0.1$).
- Notes that switching loss also differs between sides ($Q_{gd}$ for the high side, $R_{DS(on)}$ for the low side).
- Gives load-based reasons for symmetry: bipolar voltage, zero DC volt-second, four-quadrant, one part number.
- Mentions asymmetric bridges in SRM drives as a legitimate use of an "incomplete" bridge.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using a symmetrical pair out of habit in a low-duty buck.** The high side sits idle 90% of the time; its area would conduct better in the low side.
- **Claiming a half-bridge can never reverse a motor.** With a split rail or a bipolar supply it can; it's just a poor choice ([[q0423]]).
- **Believing asymmetry is free.** Different parts mean different gate charges, different drivers, different thermal behaviour, and different failure modes if the duty cycle moves.
- **Driving a transformer from a leg with uneven drive.** A DC component in the volt-seconds saturates the core; use symmetric 50% drive and a series blocking capacitor.
- **Ignoring the high-side switching loss** when making the high side bigger (lower $R_{DS(on)}$): the larger part has higher $Q_{gd}$ and $C_{oss}$.
:::

::: deep
## Two meanings, one principle

The words "asymmetrical" and "symmetrical" refer to **how the two halves of the stage are treated**. The reasons to differ are all about *how long* and *under what conditions* each half works. So the design question is: do the two halves do equal jobs?

- If the high side and low side conduct for very different fractions of the cycle, or switch under different conditions, **asymmetry is efficient**.
- If the load needs equal treatment in both directions (equal voltages, equal currents, equal volt-seconds), **symmetry is necessary**.

## Asymmetric device sizing in a buck-type leg

For a given die area, $R_{DS(on)}\propto1/\text{area}$, so $G=1/R_{DS(on)}$ is proportional to area. The conduction loss with total conductance $G_{tot}$ is

$$P=I^2\left(\frac{D}{G_H}+\frac{1-D}{G_L}\right),\qquad G_H+G_L=G_{tot}.$$

Setting $\partial P/\partial G_H=0$ gives $G_H/G_L=\sqrt{D/(1-D)}$. The optimum gain over a symmetric pair is

| $D$ | $G_H/G_L$ | $R_H$ vs symmetric | $R_L$ vs symmetric | $P_{opt}/P_{sym}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 1 | 1.00 | 1.00 | 1.00 |
| 0.275 (12 V to 3.3 V) | 0.62 | 1.31 | 0.81 | 0.95 |
| 0.1 (12 V to 1.2 V) | 0.33 | 2.00 | 0.67 | 0.80 |
| 0.05 | 0.23 | 2.68 | 0.62 | 0.72 |

Figure: The gain from asymmetry grows as the duty cycle moves away from 50% and is symmetric about it (a low-side-dominated converter and a high-side-dominated one both gain). At 50% duty there is nothing to gain.

Why not go all the way? Because switching loss also matters. The high side turns on and off with the full input voltage across it and has to charge the low-side body diode's reverse recovery, so its figure of merit is $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{gd}$; the low side turns on near zero volts, so its figure of merit is $R_{DS(on)}$ alone. A "high-side" part is therefore usually optimized for low gate charge and a "low-side" part for low resistance, and they end up different even when the duty cycle is near 50% ([[q0209]]).

(The FET body diodes are not drawn; the external diodes carry the freewheel current.)

This is **asymmetric in the bridge sense**: one switch and one diode per side, a non-reversible current, and a deliberate choice that costs less than a full H-bridge. Shoot-through is impossible by construction because each switch is in series with the winding, not with the other switch.

## When symmetry is required

**1. Bipolar load voltage.** A motor that reverses, a voice coil, a piezo, a bipolar stepper winding need $v_{load}$ of both signs from a single rail. Only an H-bridge (or a half-bridge from a split rail with half the voltage) does this ([[q0423]]).

**2. Zero DC volt-seconds.** A transformer's core flux follows $\Phi=\frac1N\int v\,dt$. In a half- or full-bridge drive with a transformer, any asymmetry in the on-times, or a threshold difference between the FETs, gives a net DC volt-second per cycle and the flux ratchets up until the core saturates. A symmetrical bridge at close to 50% drive, plus a series capacitor in the primary to block the DC, solves it. For example, a 1% on-time asymmetry on 48 V at 100 kHz gives $48\times100\text{ ns}=4.8\ \mu\text{V·s}$ each cycle; without the blocking capacitor this keeps adding.

**3. Four-quadrant operation.** Driving and braking in both directions needs the freedom to apply either polarity to the load at either current sign ([[q0424]]).

**4. Equal thermal stress and parts.** At $D\approx0.5$ all four devices carry equal RMS current; one part number, one gate driver and the same heat sinking work for each. An asymmetrical choice would run one device hot.

## A decision procedure

1. Does the load need both voltage polarities or current directions? Yes: **H-bridge**. No: go on.
2. Is the duty cycle far from 50% (buck, boost-like cases)? Yes: **resize the devices** (smaller high side, larger low side) or use an integrated stage that does it for you.
3. Is a transformer or AC-only element involved? Yes: **symmetrical** drive and a blocking capacitor.
4. Is cost or area dominant and the current unidirectional? Consider a **diode-clamped asymmetric bridge** or a **single low-side switch**.

::: applications
- **Synchronous buck converters at low duty cycles**: smaller high-side, larger low-side FET (for example 12 V to 1 V rails).
- **Switched-reluctance motor drives**: the asymmetric bridge, one leg per phase.
- **Solenoid, valve and heater drivers**: unipolar single leg.
- **Reversing brushed motors and voice-coil actuators**: symmetrical H-bridge.
- **Class-D amplifiers (BTL) and ultrasonic drivers**: symmetrical bridge on a single rail.
- **Wireless power and resonant converters**: symmetrical bridge with a series blocking capacitor.
:::

::: assumptions
- Equal total die area for the comparison, constant $R_{DS(on)}\propto1/\text{area}$, and negligible ripple.
- Conduction loss only for the sizing result; switching loss modifies the optimum, usually shifting it toward a smaller high side with lower $Q_{gd}$.
- "Symmetrical" means four equal switches. A symmetrical bridge can still run with unequal duty (sign-magnitude), but its hardware is equal.
- Current direction positive out of the switch node for the half-bridge example.

If the interviewer means the **asymmetrical half-bridge converter** (a topology with a transformer and complementary duty $D$ and $1-D$), say so and explain that it is chosen for its zero-voltage switching potential and that its transformer sees different volt-seconds on each half, which is why it needs a DC-blocking capacitor and an air gap.
:::

::: followups
### At what duty cycle does the asymmetrical sizing stop mattering?
Around $D=0.5$: the optimum ratio is 1 and the loss ratio is 1. At $D=0.275$ the gain is only 5%, which is why a 12 V to 3.3 V buck usually uses similar devices, while a 12 V to 1 V buck (8%) gains about 20% with an asymmetric pair.

### Why can't you use an asymmetrical half-bridge to drive a transformer directly?
Any difference between the two half-cycles puts a DC component in the volt-seconds. The magnetizing current has no DC path, so it grows each cycle until the core saturates. A symmetrical drive and a series capacitor (which charges to the DC offset and blocks it) fix it.

### In a high-side/low-side split, why does the high side get the lower-$Q_{gd}$ part?
The high side turns on with the full input voltage across it, and the voltage and current overlap time during the Miller plateau sets the switching loss. $R_{DS(on)}Q_{gd}$ is the figure of merit. The low side turns on at zero volts (its body diode is conducting), so only its conduction loss matters.

### What if the load is bidirectional but I only have a half-bridge IC?
Use two of them as an H-bridge, or split the supply (a ±V rail or two capacitors) and return the load to the middle. The split-rail version halves the load voltage and loads the capacitors with the full load current, so check ripple current and balance.

### The half-bridge in a motor drive runs hot on the low side. Do you change the FET sizes?
First check the duty cycle and current. If the low side conducts most of the time (low duty on the high side), a larger low-side device or two in parallel reduces conduction loss, and synchronous rectification should be active. If the heat comes from dead-time diode conduction or ringing, resizing won't help.
:::
