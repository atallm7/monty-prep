::: basics
### In plain words
A fast signal travels down a wire as a wave. At an end that doesn't absorb it, part of the wave bounces back and garbles the signal. A termination is a resistor that soaks up the wave so it can't bounce. Series termination puts that resistor at the sending end. Parallel termination puts it at the receiving end.

### Picture it
Push a wave down a long water trough. At a solid end wall, the wave bounces back. Parallel termination is a sponge beach at the far end: the wave dies on arrival. Series termination is a soft pad at the pushing end: the wave bounces off the far wall, returns, and the pad soaks it up. The gap in the analogy: a resistor at the far end also wastes steady power as heat while the signal is high.

### Words you need
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: the resistance a wire "looks like" to a wave traveling down it ([[q0710]]).
- **Reflection**: the part of a wave that bounces back from a mismatched end ([[q0711]]).
- **Driver output resistance**: the small resistance inside the chip pin that sends the signal.
- **Receiver**: the chip input that reads the signal. It draws almost no current, so to a wave it looks like an open end.
- **Multi-drop**: one wire with several receivers tapped along it.

### The simplest example
Take a 3.3 V driver with 15 Ω of its own resistance, a 35 Ω series resistor, and a 50 Ω wire. The driver sees 15 + 35 = 50 Ω, matching the wire. Those 50 Ω and the wire's 50 Ω split the voltage evenly, so the wave leaves at 1.65 V. The open receiver end doubles it to 3.3 V and bounces it back. The sending end absorbs the bounce. A receiver tapped mid-wire would see only 1.65 V for a while, so this suits one receiver at the end. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page compares the two styles on power, speed and layout.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can compare the two basic termination styles with the physics, not just a list: series termination absorbs the *returning* reflection at the source and relies on the open end doubling the half-amplitude wave; parallel termination absorbs the wave on *arrival* at the load. The interviewer wants the trade-offs that follow: power, amplitude, edge rate, topology (point-to-point vs multi-drop), component count and tolerance, plus the variants (Thevenin, AC, diode) and a rule for which to pick. Numbers (launch voltage, DC power, the shelf at mid-line receivers) are what make an answer convincing.
:::

::: why
Termination is the standard fix for reflections and the choice affects power, EMI, timing margin and layout. Series resistors cost almost nothing and are in nearly every board's clock and fast-GPIO routing. Parallel and on-die termination are the norm in DDR and high-speed serial links. A good engineer picks the right one for the topology: a series resistor on a multi-drop bus is a bug, a 50 Ω pull-down on every net of a battery-powered board is a power disaster. This is a high-probability interview question and it comes with a follow-up scenario every time.
:::

::: concepts
- **Reflection coefficient and matching**: $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$ ([[q0711]], [[q0714]]).
- **Launch voltage**: $V_1=V_sZ_0/(R_{tot}+Z_0)$ for source resistance $R_{tot}$ ([[q0710]]).
- **Incident-wave vs reflected-wave switching**: whether a receiver switches on the first arriving wave or must wait for the reflection ([[q0712]]).
- **Driver output impedance**: sets how large the series resistor is, and is not constant.
- **Power dissipation** $V^2/R$ in a DC-coupled parallel termination ([[q0006]]).
- **Topology**: point-to-point, daisy-chain, star, multi-drop.
:::

::: answer
"Series termination puts a resistor at the driver so that driver plus resistor equals the line impedance. The line sees half the swing, the wave goes down the line, hits the open end of the receiver, doubles to full swing, and the reflection travels back and is absorbed at the source, so there's no ringing. It uses essentially no DC power, it's one small resistor, and the driver only sees the AC load. The downside is that the signal only reaches full amplitude after the reflection returns, so it only works with receivers at the end of the line: anything tapped off the middle sees a half-amplitude shelf, and the edge is a bit slower.

Parallel termination puts a resistor equal to $Z_0$ at the receiver, to ground or to a bias voltage. The wave is absorbed on arrival with no reflection at all, so every receiver along the way sees a full, clean edge. That's best for multi-drop buses and the fastest links. The costs: DC power, since a 3.3 V signal into 50 ohms to ground burns about 200 milliwatts whenever it's high; a reduced high level because of the divider with the driver; and the driver has to supply the current.

So: point-to-point, low power, moderate speed, I'd use series. Multi-drop, bidirectional or very fast, and power isn't a constraint, I'd use parallel, usually as Thevenin, AC or on-die termination."
:::

::: technical
**Series (source) termination.** Choose $R_s=Z_0-R_{drv}$. Launch $V_1=V_s\dfrac{Z_0}{R_s+R_{drv}+Z_0}=V_s/2$. At an open/high-Z load $\Gamma_L\approx+1$ so $V_{load}=V_s$ (full swing) at $t_d$; the reflection returns at $2t_d$ and is absorbed ($\Gamma_S=0$). Points along the line see $V_s/2$ for $2(t_d-t_x)$ then $V_s$.
- *Pros:* no DC load; low power ($\sim CV^2f$ only); one component; reduces edge rate and EMI; works with strong drivers.
- *Cons:* delayed full level (waits for reflection), unsuitable for mid-line receivers or multi-drop; rise time slowed by $R_{tot}C_{load}$; sensitive to driver-impedance tolerance ($\pm10\ \Omega$ error leaves $|\Gamma_S|\approx0.1$); cannot damp a *bidirectional* bus alone.

**Parallel termination.** Choose $R_t=Z_0$ at the far end, to ground (or $V_{DD}$ or $V_{TT}$). Load swing $V_s\dfrac{R_t}{R_{drv}+R_t}$ and $\Gamma_L=0$. Static current $I=V/(R_{drv}+R_t)$.
- *Pros:* incident-wave switching everywhere on the line; cleaner and faster edges; supports multi-drop with the termination at the end; best SI.
- *Cons:* DC power; reduced noise margin (the high level drops by the divider and the low level rises for a pull-up variant); heavier driver load; extra component at the receiver (area, via, routing).

**Variants.**
- *Thevenin:* $R_1\parallel R_2=Z_0$ to $V_{DD}$/ground, biasing the line at $V_{th}=V_{DD}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$; halves driver peak current but dissipates constant power.
- *Terminate to $V_{TT}$* (such as $V_{DD}/2$): symmetric, less driver current, used by SSTL (DDR).
- *AC (RC):* $R=Z_0$ in series with $C$ ($RC\gtrsim3\times$ round-trip delay); no DC power, but consumes $\sim CV^2f$ and has a startup/data-pattern dependence.
- *Diode clamp:* Schottky diodes to the rails clip overshoot; not a match, a safety net.
:::

::: short
"Series termination puts a resistor at the driver so driver plus resistor equals $Z_0$: the line launches half the swing, the open end doubles it, and the reflection is absorbed at the source. It's cheap, uses no DC power, but only works with receivers at the end, and the full level arrives after a round trip. Parallel termination puts $Z_0$ at the receiver and absorbs the wave on arrival, so it suits multi-drop and fast links, but burns DC power, lowers the high level and loads the driver."
:::

::: long
"They solve the same reflection problem in different places. Series termination goes at the driver. I size it so driver impedance plus resistor equals the line's $Z_0$, say 15 plus 35 equals 50 ohms. Then the line sees a divider, so the incident wave is half the swing. It travels to the receiver, which is basically an open circuit, so it doubles and the receiver sees full swing. The reflection goes back to the source, where the source impedance equals $Z_0$ so it's absorbed completely. No ringing, no DC current, one resistor.

The trade-offs are that the full amplitude only shows up at the end, after the signal has gone out and the reflection has come back to the source. So any receiver in the middle of the line sees a half-height shelf for a while, which on a half-supply threshold is a disaster. That means series termination is really for point-to-point, one receiver at the end. It also slows the edge a bit with the resistor and the load capacitance, and it depends on knowing the driver's output impedance, which varies.

Parallel termination goes at the receiver, a resistor equal to $Z_0$ to ground or to a bias voltage. The wave is absorbed on arrival, so there's no reflection and every receiver sees the full-amplitude edge, which is what you want for multi-drop buses or the fastest links. But the DC current is real: 3.3 V into 50 ohms is about 66 milliamps, 218 milliwatts when the line is high. It also reduces the output swing through the divider with the driver, and the driver must source that current. So we use Thevenin or termination to a mid-rail $V_{TT}$ to halve the driver current, AC termination with a series capacitor to avoid DC power, or on-die termination in DDR.

My rule: point-to-point, low power, single receiver at the end: series. Multi-drop, bidirectional, or very fast and power available: parallel."
:::

::: terms
- **Source (series) termination**: resistor near the driver making $R_{drv}+R_s=Z_0$.
- **Parallel (shunt) termination**: resistor equal to $Z_0$ at the receiver end, to ground or a reference.
- **Thevenin termination**: resistor divider ($R_1$ to $V_{DD}$, $R_2$ to ground) with $R_1\parallel R_2=Z_0$.
- **$V_{TT}$ termination**: resistor to a mid-rail supply, for example $V_{DD}/2$.
- **AC termination**: $R=Z_0$ in series with a capacitor, so no DC path.
- **ODT**: on-die termination, switchable resistors inside the receiver or driver chip.
- **Incident-wave switching**: receiver switches on the first arriving wave (parallel termination).
- **Shelf**: the half-amplitude plateau seen by mid-line receivers on a series-terminated line.
- **Noise margin**: the gap between actual signal levels and the receiver thresholds.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "series goes at the source, parallel at the load, parallel burns power".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the *mechanism* of series termination: half-amplitude launch, doubling at the open end, absorption at the source.
- Quantifies: launch $V_s/2$, a mid-line shelf of $2(t_d-t_x)$, DC power $V^2/R$ (218 mW for 3.3 V into 50 Ω), the reduced swing $V_sR_t/(R_{drv}+R_t)$.
- Picks by **topology and constraints**: point-to-point or multi-drop, power budget, speed, bidirectional, driver strength.
- Knows the variants (Thevenin, $V_{TT}$, AC, diode, ODT) and their trade-offs: power, driver current, level shift, time constant.
- Mentions that driver impedance varies with PVT and drive strength, and recommends measuring or using IBIS to size the resistor.
- Places the resistor correctly: series close to the driver, parallel close to the receiver (short stub).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using series termination with receivers in the middle of the line.** They see only $V_s/2$ for a while and may not switch, or switch late.
- **Ignoring the driver's output impedance** and picking a 50 Ω series resistor for a 20 Ω driver (total 70 Ω gives $\Gamma_S=+0.17$ and a slow, over-damped edge).
- **Placing the series resistor far from the driver.** Any trace between driver and resistor is unterminated and can ring; keep it within a few millimetres.
- **Placing the parallel resistor on a long stub.** The stub is a reflection source; put the resistor at the very end of the line or in a fly-by topology.
- **Forgetting the DC cost** of parallel termination to ground on many nets (about 50 to 130 mW each at 3.3 V into 50 Ω).
- **Assuming terminating "always helps".** It lowers swing and slows edges, so on short lines it can only hurt.
- **Misreading AC termination as free.** The capacitor passes the edges, so it dissipates about $CV^2f$ at high toggle rates.
:::

## Why series termination works: edge counting

The resistor makes $\Gamma_S=0$. At the source, a wave returning from the open end sees $R_{drv}+R_s=Z_0$ to the (stiff) source, so it is absorbed. At the receiver the wave is doubled: $V_{load}=(1+\Gamma_L)V_1=2V_1=V_s$. So the receiver sees a *full-swing* edge at $t_d$, with no ringing. Anything tapped midway at distance $x$ sees $V_s/2$ from $t_x=x/v$ until $2t_d-t_x$, then $V_s$ (the plot above). Series termination is therefore the correct answer for a single receiver at the end, and incorrect for a star or bus.

Tolerance: if the driver is 5 Ω stronger than assumed (total 45 Ω), $\Gamma_S=-0.053$; the residual echo is small. If the total is 10 Ω low (40 Ω): launch 1.83 V, doubled to 3.67 V at the load, about 11% overshoot (0.37 V) and a small echo. Series termination degrades gracefully, but it needs the driver impedance to be known: IBIS models or datasheet output-impedance settings tell you.

## Why parallel termination costs

A resistor to ground draws $V/R$ whenever the line is high. For 3.3 V into 50 Ω that is 66 mA (218 mW) with an ideal source, and 50.8 mA (129 mW in the resistor) with a 15 Ω driver. On a bus of 32 lines at 50% duty cycle that is about 2 W. Reduce it:

| Scheme | Static power (3.3 V, 15 Ω driver, $Z_0=50\ \Omega$) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Series | about 0 (dynamic $CV^2f$ only) | Point-to-point only |
| Parallel to ground | 129 mW when high, 0 when low (average 64 mW at 50% duty) | High level 2.54 V, low 0 V |
| Thevenin 100 Ω/100 Ω | 87 mW constant | Levels 2.92 V and 0.38 V, centred on 1.65 V; driver current 25 mA each way, half the peak of the ground termination |
| Parallel to $V_{TT}=1.65$ V (50 Ω) | 32 mW in the resistor in either state (25.4 mA) | Levels 2.92 V and 0.38 V, same as Thevenin without the divider's rail current; needs a $V_{TT}$ regulator that can source and sink (SSTL/DDR) |
| AC: 50 Ω + 100 pF | about $CV^2f$ (54 mW at 50 MHz toggling) | No DC; the capacitor charges each edge, so the pattern matters |

The numbers show a subtle point: Thevenin does *not* save average power versus a ground termination at 50% duty. It halves the driver's peak current, centres the levels around the receiver threshold, and makes the power independent of the data pattern.

For AC termination the capacitor must not charge significantly during one round trip, so $RC\gtrsim3\cdot2t_d$. With $2t_d=2$ ns (the 1 ns line above) and $R=50\ \Omega$ that gives $C\gtrsim120$ pF; the 100 pF in the table ($RC=5$ ns, 2.5 round trips) sits at the low edge of that rule. A larger $C$ behaves more like DC termination (and more power); a smaller $C$ lets the line reflect at the edge's start and droops on long runs of zeros or ones.

## Edge rate

Any termination affects the edge. A receiver with $C_{in}=5$ pF at the end of a series-terminated line forms an RC with the line: the time constant is about $Z_0C_{in}=250$ ps (10 to 90% rise about 0.55 ns, added in quadrature with the driver's own edge). With parallel termination the receiver capacitance sees $Z_0\parallel R_t=25\ \Omega$: about 125 ps, so parallel is the faster option, which is another reason it dominates at very high speeds.

## Topologies

- **Point-to-point, one receiver:** series, or on-die termination.
- **Multi-drop bus** (several receivers along the line): parallel at the far end (and often at the near end too if bidirectional), with short stubs.
- **Star / tree:** series resistors at each branch (and a clock buffer per branch), or a proper splitter.
- **Bidirectional bus** (each end drives and receives): parallel termination at *both* ends, or ODT in each chip, since each end is both a source and a sink. See [[q0717]].
- **Fly-by (DDR3 and later)**: a line passing by each memory chip, ending in a $V_{TT}$ parallel termination.

## Where to place the resistor

Series: right next to the driver pin, because the trace between the driver and the resistor sees the full unattenuated amplitude and an unmatched impedance. Parallel: at the very end of the line, after the last receiver, with the shortest possible stub. If the termination resistor has its own via and a 10 mm stub, the stub reflects.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A crisp comparison line: "Series is a one-resistor, zero-DC-power fix that only works when the receiver is at the end; parallel is the bus-friendly, fastest option that costs DC power and noise margin."
:::

::: applications
- **Fast GPIO and clock outputs to a single receiver**: 22 to 33 Ω series resistors next to the driver, common on boards with FPGAs and MCUs.
- **DDR3/DDR4/DDR5 memory**: on-die termination (a controllable parallel termination to a $V_{TT}$-like level) at the receiver, plus tuned drive strength.
- **Backplane and multi-drop buses (older RS-485, multi-point LVDS)**: parallel termination at the ends of the line.
- **Coaxial cables and RF**: 50 Ω terminators at the end (parallel) and a 50 Ω source (double termination).
- **High-speed differential links (USB, PCIe, HDMI)**: parallel (differential) termination on-chip at the receiver.
- **Battery-powered designs**: AC or series termination (or no termination, with short, slow nets) to avoid DC burn.
- **Test and probing**: scope inputs set to 50 Ω are parallel terminations; use them only with low-swing signals or the terminator will overheat.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear driver with a constant $R_{drv}$.** Real CMOS drivers are nonlinear and change with PVT and drive-strength setting; use IBIS data for real work.
- **Single point-to-point line, lossless**; loss reduces the amplitude of the returning wave and slightly helps.
- **Receiver treated as open plus a capacitor.** Input protection diodes will clamp overshoot to about $V_{DD}$ plus a diode drop.
- **Thresholds** $V_{DD}/2$ and $V_{IH}=2.0$ V are generic; use the datasheet values.
- **50 Ω** is assumed; with 28 Ω or 60 Ω lines all the numbers shift.

A reasonable alternative answer: some engineers prefer no termination at all on short lines and slow edges ([[q0709]]), or use damping resistors only at the receiver end. And for differential links, the termination is across the pair (100 Ω) rather than to ground ([[q0719]]).
:::

::: followups
### Why does series termination only work with the receiver at the end?
Because the line only reaches full voltage after the reflection from the open end returns. A receiver in the middle sees the half-amplitude wave on its way out, then the full-amplitude wave when the reflection passes it on the way back. In the example, a receiver 0.5 ns from the driver sees 1.65 V for a full nanosecond, exactly at the threshold of a 3.3 V part. So you'd put receivers only at the end, or use a star with a series resistor per branch, or switch to parallel.

### How do you size the series resistor?
$R_s=Z_0-R_{drv}$, with $R_{drv}$ from the IBIS model or datasheet at the chosen drive strength (often 10 to 40 Ω). With a 50 Ω line and a 22 Ω driver: 28 Ω, so use 27 or 30 Ω. A useful check by simulation or measurement is that the load edge is clean with no overshoot and no shelf, and that the rise time is acceptable. If $R_{drv}$ is unknown, 22 to 33 Ω is a common starting value.

### Compare the DC power of a ground termination and a Thevenin termination.
For 3.3 V, 50 Ω, 15 Ω driver: the ground termination dissipates 129 mW when high and 0 when low (64 mW average at 50% duty). A 100 Ω/100 Ω Thevenin dissipates a constant 87 mW. The Thevenin termination is no cheaper on average, but the driver current halves (25 mA instead of 51 mA) and the levels are centred on the receiver threshold. $V_{TT}$ termination is similar and can recycle current if the $V_{TT}$ regulator can sink.

### Scenario: a clock goes to four receivers along a 12 cm trace and one of them intermittently misses edges. What's the issue and fix?
Likely a series-terminated line with mid-line receivers: they see the half-amplitude shelf. Fix by: terminating at the far end with parallel/Thevenin termination and keeping stubs short; or by using a clock buffer with one output per receiver, each with its own series resistor (a star of point-to-point lines). I'd also check the placement of the resistor and the driver strength.

### Harder: why can a series resistor *not* fix a bidirectional bus?
On a bidirectional bus any chip can drive, so each chip needs a source impedance matching $Z_0$ and also needs to absorb waves arriving at its (now receiving) pins. A single series resistor at one end terminates waves only from that end; waves launched from the other end see an open (no termination) at the first end and reflect. You need parallel termination (or ODT) at both ends, or series at each driver with the line short enough not to matter. This is why multi-point buses and DDR use ODT.
:::
