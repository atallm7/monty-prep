::: testing
Whether you can define duty cycle precisely and **connect it to what it does in a buck converter**. The interviewer is checking:

1. A clean definition: the fraction of each switching period during which the switch is on, $D=t_{on}/T$, between 0 and 1 (or 0 to 100%).
2. That you separate duty cycle (a *ratio*) from switching frequency (a *rate*), and know that the on-time is $D/f_{sw}$.
3. Its role: $D$ is the control knob of a switching converter, and the average of the pulse train is $D\,V_{in}$.
4. Practical awareness: how it is generated, how it is measured, and how dead time, minimum on-time and PWM resolution complicate the clean picture.
:::

::: why
Duty cycle is the single most-used word in power electronics. Every control scheme ultimately sets it, every ripple and ratio equation includes it, and every scope capture of a switch node shows it. Interviewers ask it early because a muddled definition (confusing it with frequency, or with percent-on of a *different* signal) predicts a muddled understanding of the rest of the buck. It also appears outside power: PWM for LED dimming and motor speed ([[q0145]]) and clock duty cycle in digital design. A crisp definition plus one example is enough to pass, and a note on resolution or dead time is enough to stand out.
:::

::: concepts
- **PWM** (pulse-width modulation): a periodic signal whose width varies while frequency stays fixed. See [[q0145]].
- **Period and frequency**: $T=1/f_{sw}$; duty cycle is independent of $f_{sw}$.
- **Average value of a pulse train**: $\bar v = D\,V_{pulse}$, which is why an LC filter turns PWM into a DC voltage.
- **On-time and off-time**: $t_{on}=D\,T$ and $t_{off}=(1-D)T$.
- **Complementary duty** $D'=1-D$: the fraction the freewheel path conducts.
- **Control variable**: in a buck, the loop adjusts $D$ to regulate $V_{out}$ ([[q0178]]).
:::

::: answer
"Duty cycle is the fraction of each switching period that the signal, or the switch, is on. If the period is $T$ and the switch is on for $t_{on}$, then $D=t_{on}/T$. It's a number between 0 and 1, or 0 to 100 percent. At 25 percent duty the switch is on a quarter of each cycle and off for three quarters.

It's separate from frequency. At 500 kHz the period is 2 µs, so a 27.5 percent duty cycle means a 550 ns on-time. If I went to 1 MHz at the same duty cycle, the on-time would be 275 ns, but the ratio hasn't changed.

In a buck converter, the duty cycle is the control variable. The switch node is a pulse train between $V_{in}$ and about zero, and its average is $D\,V_{in}$, which the LC filter passes to the output. So 27.5 percent from 12 V gives 3.3 V. The feedback loop varies $D$ to hold the output.

Practically, it's generated by comparing a control voltage to a ramp, or with a counter in a microcontroller timer, so the resolution is limited. And at the switch node the effective duty cycle can differ a little from the gate signal because of dead time and finite edges."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** For a periodic switching waveform with period $T=1/f_{sw}$ and on-time $t_{on}$:
$$D=\frac{t_{on}}{T}=t_{on}\,f_{sw},\qquad 0\le D\le1,\qquad D'=1-D=\frac{t_{off}}{T}$$

**Average of a pulse train.** For a waveform that is $V_{pk}$ for $t_{on}$ and $0$ otherwise: $\bar v=D\,V_{pk}$. The RMS value is $V_{pk}\sqrt D$.

**In a buck.** The switch-node waveform swings between $V_{in}$ (high-side on) and about $0$ (freewheel on), so $\bar v_{SW}=D\,V_{in}$ and $V_{out}\approx D\,V_{in}$ in CCM ([[q0178]]). Input current is $D\,I_{out}$ on average.

**On-time vs frequency** (for $D=0.275$): 2.75 µs at 100 kHz, 1.1 µs at 250 kHz, 550 ns at 500 kHz, 275 ns at 1 MHz, 137.5 ns at 2 MHz. Higher frequency squeezes the on-time, which runs into the controller's minimum on-time ([[q0181]]).

**Generation.** Analog PWM: compare a control voltage $V_c$ to a sawtooth of amplitude $V_{ramp}$: $D=V_c/V_{ramp}$. Digital PWM: a timer counts to $N$ (period register) and a compare register $C$ sets the edge, so $D=C/N$ with resolution $1/N$.

**Resolution and output step.** With $b$-bit duty resolution the smallest output step is $\Delta V_{out}=V_{in}/2^b$: 47 mV for 8 bits and 2.9 mV for 12 bits at $V_{in}=12$ V.

**Effective duty.** Dead time $t_{dt}$ at each edge in a synchronous buck reduces the high-side's effective on-time and shifts the SW average slightly; at 500 kHz two 10 ns dead times are 1% of the period.
:::

::: short

In a buck the switch node is a pulse train whose average is $D\,V_{in}$, so duty cycle sets the output voltage, and the feedback loop adjusts it to regulate."
:::

::: long
"Duty cycle describes a repeating on-off signal: the fraction of each period that it's on. If the period is $T$ and the on-time is $t_{on}$, then $D=t_{on}/T$, so it runs from zero to one, or zero to a hundred percent. A clock with equal high and low times has a fifty percent duty cycle.

It's a ratio, so it's separate from frequency. At 500 kHz the period is 2 microseconds, and a 27.5 percent duty cycle means the switch is on for 550 nanoseconds. At 1 MHz, same duty cycle, it's on for 275 nanoseconds. Frequency tells you how often; duty cycle tells you how long each time, as a share of the period.

Why does it matter in a buck? The switch node toggles between the input voltage and roughly ground, so its average is $D\,V_{in}$. The inductor and capacitor are a low-pass filter that keeps that average, so $V_{out}\approx D\,V_{in}$. For 12 V in and 3.3 V out, $D$ is 27.5 percent. The controller's job is to adjust $D$ to hold $V_{out}$. The remaining fraction, $1-D$, is when the freewheel path conducts.

The duty cycle comes from a modulator. In analog controllers a comparator compares an error voltage to a ramp, so $D$ equals the control voltage over the ramp amplitude. In a microcontroller a timer compares a counter against a register, so the duty cycle moves in steps of one count. With 8 bits and a 12 V input, that's 47 millivolts of output per step, which is too coarse for tight regulation, so digital power designs use high-resolution PWM.

In real hardware the effective duty cycle at the switch node differs slightly from the gate signal, because of dead time and finite edges. And practical limits on minimum on-time and off-time mean you can't use the full 0 to 100 percent."
:::

::: terms
- **Duty cycle ($D$)**: on-time divided by the period, $t_{on}/T$.
- **Period ($T$) and frequency ($f_{sw}$)**: $T=1/f_{sw}$.
- **On-time ($t_{on}$) / off-time ($t_{off}$)**: durations of the on and off portions of a cycle.
- **PWM**: pulse-width modulation; varying $D$ at a fixed frequency to encode a value or control power ([[q0145]]).
- **Complementary duty ($D'$)**: $1-D$.
- **Minimum on-time**: the shortest pulse the controller and gate drive can produce reliably.
- **Duty resolution**: the smallest increment in $D$ a digital modulator can produce.
- **Dead time**: the deliberate gap between the high-side turning off and the low-side turning on.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the percentage of time the switch is on" and gives $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Defines $D=t_{on}/T=t_{on}f_{sw}$ and immediately separates it from frequency with a numeric example (550 ns at 500 kHz).
- Explains *why* $D$ controls voltage: the average of the pulse train is $D\,V_{pk}$, and the LC filter keeps the average.
- Mentions the complement $1-D$ (freewheel conduction) and the relation $I_{in}=D\,I_{out}$.
- Describes how $D$ is generated (comparator with ramp, or timer compare) and the digital resolution consequence.
- Notes real-world limits: minimum on-time, dead time and effective vs. commanded duty.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Confusing duty cycle with frequency.** Changing $f_{sw}$ doesn't change $D$; changing $D$ doesn't change $f_{sw}$.
- **Quoting it as a time instead of a ratio.** "The duty cycle is 550 ns" is wrong; the *on-time* is 550 ns and the duty cycle is 27.5%.
- **Forgetting it's defined for the high-side switch in a buck.** The low-side or diode conducts for $1-D$ (in CCM), so don't mix them up.
- **Assuming duty cycle can go anywhere from 0 to 100%.** Minimum on-time, minimum off-time and bootstrap refresh restrict the usable range ([[q0181]]).
- **Treating the commanded duty as equal to the measured SW duty.** Dead time, ringing and the threshold you choose to measure at all shift it.
- **Using RMS and average interchangeably.** Average is $D\,V_{pk}$; RMS is $V_{pk}\sqrt D$.
:::

::: deep
## The definition, from a picture

A switch turns on and off repeatedly with period $T$. During each period it is on for $t_{on}$ and off for the remaining $t_{off}=T-t_{on}$.

```
Figure: Three periods' worth of PWM at the same frequency but different duty cycles. The period is eight divisions; the high portion is 2, 4 or 6 divisions, giving 25%, 50% and 75%. The *frequency* is identical in all three; only the *width* changes.

$$D=\frac{t_{on}}{T}=t_{on}\,f_{sw}$$

Because $D$ is dimensionless, it tells you nothing about the actual time. For that you need $f_{sw}$:

| $f_{sw}$ | Period $T$ | On-time at $D=0.275$ |
|---|---|---|
| 100 kHz | 10 µs | 2.75 µs |
| 250 kHz | 4 µs | 1.10 µs |
| 500 kHz | 2 µs | 550 ns |
| 1 MHz | 1 µs | 275 ns |
| 2 MHz | 500 ns | 137.5 ns |

## Why a duty cycle sets a voltage

Take a waveform that equals $V_{pk}$ for a fraction $D$ of the time and $0$ otherwise. Its average over one period is

$$\bar v=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T v(t)\,dt=\frac{1}{T}\,V_{pk}\,t_{on}=D\,V_{pk}$$

 "points":[{"x":0.275,"y":3.3,"label":"D = 27.5% gives 3.3 V"},{"x":0.5,"y":6,"label":"D = 50% gives 6 V"}],
 "hlines":[{"at":"vin","label":"V_in","color":"muted"}]}
```
Figure: The average is a straight line in $D$, so a buck's ideal transfer function is perfectly linear: every 1% of duty is 0.12 V of output from 12 V. That linearity is one reason PWM is a convenient control knob.

If the pulse train passes through a low-pass filter whose corner frequency is far below $f_{sw}$, only this average survives. In a buck the "filter" is the LC output stage; with a microcontroller PWM pin an RC filter does the same thing (e.g. 3.3 V at 30% gives about 0.99 V after filtering).

The RMS value is different: $V_{rms}=V_{pk}\sqrt D$. Power in a resistive load driven directly by PWM goes with $D$ (since $P=D\,V_{pk}^2/R$), which is how PWM dimming and heater control work.

## Where the duty cycle comes from

### Analog modulator

A comparator compares the error amplifier's output $V_c$ with a ramp of amplitude $V_{ramp}$. The switch is on while the ramp is below $V_c$:

```plot

 "vars":{"vc":0.275},
 "series":[{"label":"ramp","fn":"saw(x)","color":"muted"},{"label":"PWM output (high while ramp < V_c)","fn":"step(vc-saw(x)-0.000001)","step":true,"color":"accent"}],
 "hlines":[{"at":"vc","label":"V_c = 0.275 × V_ramp","color":"orange"}]}
```
Figure: The PWM output goes high at the start of each ramp and drops when the ramp crosses the control voltage. Raise $V_c$ and the pulse widens, so $D=V_c/V_{ramp}$. The feedback loop moves $V_c$ to regulate the output.

### Digital modulator

A timer counts from 0 to $N$ (set by the period register) and toggles the output when the counter matches a compare value $C$. The duty cycle is $C/N$, so it moves in steps of $1/N$.

At $f_{sw}=500$ kHz with a 100 MHz timer clock, $N=200$, so only about 7.6 bits of duty resolution: each step is 0.5% of 12 V, or 60 mV of output, too coarse for a tight rail. This is why digital power controllers use high-resolution PWM (delay-line or dithering techniques) to get below a few mV per step. More generally

$$\Delta V_{out}=\frac{V_{in}}{2^b}\ \ (b\ \text{bits of duty resolution})$$

| Bits | Output step at $V_{in}=12$ V |
|---|---|
| 8 | 46.9 mV |
| 10 | 11.7 mV |
| 12 | 2.9 mV |
| 16 | 0.18 mV |

If the resolution of the PWM is coarser than the resolution of the voltage measurement, the loop can't settle on a single duty value and can "hunt" between two neighbouring values, producing a low-frequency output wobble.

## Duty cycle as seen at the switch node

The commanded gate duty and the real switch-node duty are not exactly equal.

- **Dead time**: in a synchronous buck, after the high-side turns off both FETs are off for a few ns before the low-side turns on, and vice versa. During that gap the body diode conducts. At 500 kHz, two 10 ns gaps are $20/2000=1\%$ of the period.
- **Edge rates and ringing**: the SW node does not jump instantly, so "on-time" depends on where you measure. Convention: measure at 50% of the swing.
- **Minimum on-time**: controllers need a minimum high-side pulse (set by blanking, current-sense settling and gate drive), typically on the order of tens to a hundred or more ns. Below it, the controller skips pulses. This vendor-dependent figure sets the lowest usable $D$ at high $V_{in}$ and frequency.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Minimum on-time and dead time vary widely between devices and are strongly vendor- and process-dependent. Check the datasheet of the specific part; the numbers here are illustrative.

## Where else duty cycle appears

- **Clocks**: a 50% duty clock is normal; duty-cycle distortion eats into timing margins in half-rate DDR interfaces and clock trees.
- **PWM dimming**: LED brightness proportional to $D$ at a frequency high enough to avoid visible flicker.
- **Motor speed control**: average voltage (and thus speed) $\propto D$ ([[q0145]]).
- **Heater and servo control**: servos encode angle in pulse width (not strictly duty cycle) at a fixed rate.
- **Boost and buck-boost converters**: duty cycle is the control variable too, but the transfer functions differ ($1/(1-D)$ for boost).

::: applications
- **Setting a buck's output**: $D\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$; the loop's control variable.
- **Analysing a scope capture**: read $t_{on}$ and $T$ off the SW node to compute $D$ and compare with $V_{out}/V_{in}$ ([[q0180]]).
- **LED brightness control and fan/heater control** with PWM at a fixed frequency.
- **Motor drivers and H-bridges**: average motor voltage is $D\,V_{supply}$ ([[q0145]]).
- **Clock distribution**: checking that a clock keeps a 50% duty cycle across buffers and levels.
- **Digital power**: choosing timer resolution so the voltage step per count is small enough.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Fixed-frequency PWM.** In constant-on-time, hysteretic or PFM controllers the on-time and frequency both vary, and "duty cycle" is an emergent average.
- **$D$ refers to the high-side switch in a buck.** Some texts define the duty cycle of the "main" switch generally; for boost it is the low-side switch.
- **Ideal edges.** Rise and fall times are neglected; with real edges $t_{on}$ is measured between 50% crossings.
- **Average is meaningful only over many cycles**, with a low-pass filter that rejects the switching frequency.
- **Percent or fraction.** Both conventions are used; say which you mean.
:::

::: followups
### What's the duty cycle if the switch is on for 800 ns at 250 kHz?
The period is 4 µs, so $D=0.8/4=0.2$, or 20%. The off-time is 3.2 µs. A 20% duty cycle at 12 V in gives an ideal 2.4 V output.

### Does changing the switching frequency change the duty cycle?
Not by itself. The duty cycle is set by the ratio $V_{out}/V_{in}$ (plus loss terms). Raising $f_{sw}$ shortens both the on- and off-times proportionally, so $D$ stays the same, but ripple falls ($\Delta I_L\propto1/f_{sw}$) and switching loss rises. In practice, minimum on-time may limit how high you can go at small $D$ ([[q0181]], [[q0216]]).

### How do you measure the duty cycle of a buck converter?
Probe the switch node with a short ground spring (minimizing ringing), set the trigger on the rising edge, and read $t_{on}$ (50% to 50%) and $T$ with the scope's cursors or automatic measurements: $D=t_{on}/T$. Compare it with $V_{out}/V_{in}$ to check for losses or dead-time effects. A DMM average on SW (through an RC filter) should read $D\,V_{in}$ too.

### What duty cycle gives 5 V from 12 V and what's the RMS value of the SW waveform?
$D=5/12=0.417$. The RMS value of the pulse train is $12\sqrt{0.417}=7.75$ V, quite different from the 5 V average. The RMS matters for resistive heating in a load driven directly by the PWM, not for the buck's LC-filtered output.

### Scenario: your microcontroller PWM has 8-bit resolution at 12 V in. The output wanders by ±50 mV at idle. Why?
A single duty step is $12/256=46.9$ mV at the output. A loop that needs a value between two steps will toggle between neighbours, producing a limit-cycle of about one step. Fixes: more duty resolution (higher-resolution timer, dithering or a delay-line PWM), a lower input voltage or a different loop structure that tolerates quantization.
