::: basics
### In plain words
To find which region a MOSFET is in, you guess one region, calculate with it, and check whether the answer agrees with the guess. The load line is the picture of the circuit's side of the problem. The operating point is where the circuit's line crosses the transistor's curve.

### Picture it
Picture a tug of war between two rules. The transistor says "my current is this much for this gate voltage". The resistor says "my current depends on how much voltage is left over for me". The two rules only agree at one point, like two roads crossing at one intersection. That intersection is where the circuit settles.

### Words you need
- **Cutoff, triode, saturation**: the three regions. See [[q0099]].
- **Overdrive ($V_{ov}$)**: $V_{GS}-V_t$.
- **Load line**: the straight line $I_D=(V_{DD}-V_{DS})/R_D$ set by the circuit.
- **Operating point (Q-point)**: the crossing of the load line and the transistor's curve.
- **Check step**: after guessing saturation, confirm that $V_{DS}\ge V_{ov}$.

### The simplest example
Take $V_{DD}=5$ V, $R_D=1$ kΩ, $V_t=1$ V and $k=2$ mA/V². Set $V_{GS}=2$ V. The overdrive is 1 V, and guessing saturation gives $I_D=\tfrac12\times2\times1^2=1$ mA. Then $V_{DS}=5-1=4$ V, which is at least 1 V, so the guess was right. If instead $V_{GS}=5$ V, saturation would want 16 mA, but 1 kΩ from 5 V can supply at most 5 mA. So the guess fails, and the FET is in triode.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page lays out the procedure step by step, handles the triode case, and shows the load line on the curves.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can turn "given $V_{GS}$ and $V_t$" into a **repeatable procedure** and actually solve for the operating point, not just name a region. The interviewer is watching for:

1. The first check ($V_{GS}$ vs $V_t$) before any equation. If it's below threshold you're done: cutoff.
2. The **assume-and-verify** habit: assume saturation, compute $I_D$, compute $V_{DS}$ from the circuit, and check that $V_{DS}\ge V_{ov}$. If not, redo it in triode.
3. The **load line**: the circuit gives a straight line $I_D=(V_{DD}-V_{DS})/R_D$, the transistor gives a curve, and the operating point is their intersection. You should be able to draw both and explain what moves when $V_{GS}$ changes.
:::

::: why
This is exactly the kind of problem you meet in a first analog course and in an interview whiteboard: "NMOS, gate at 3 V, $V_t$ is 1 V, drain resistor 1 kΩ, supply 10 V: where does it bias?" It's also the foundation of amplifier biasing ([[q0119]]) and of checking whether a switch is really "on" (triode) or being starved of drive (saturation). The same method works for a diode ([[q0076]]) and a BJT.
:::

::: concepts
- **Region conditions**: cutoff $V_{GS}<V_t$; triode $V_{DS}<V_{ov}$; saturation $V_{DS}\ge V_{ov}$. See [[q0099]].
- **Square-law equations** for each region. See [[q0095]].
- **KVL on the drain circuit**: $V_{DS}=V_{DD}-I_DR_D$, a straight line in the I-V plane.
- **Load line and operating (Q) point**: the intersection of the device curve and the circuit line.
- **Assume-and-verify**: guess the region, solve, check the inequality, fix if violated.
- **Overdrive voltage**: $V_{ov}=V_{GS}-V_t$.
:::

::: answer
"I'd work it as a short procedure. First, compare $V_{GS}$ with $V_t$. If $V_{GS}$ is below threshold there's no channel, the FET is in cutoff, $I_D\approx0$, and the drain sits at $V_{DD}$.

If it's above threshold, I compute the overdrive, $V_{ov}=V_{GS}-V_t$, and assume saturation because it's the easier equation: $I_D=\tfrac12k\,V_{ov}^2$. Then I use the circuit to get $V_{DS}=V_{DD}-I_DR_D$ and check: is $V_{DS}$ at least $V_{ov}$? If yes, I'm done, it's in saturation. If $V_{DS}$ comes out smaller, or even negative, the assumption was wrong and the FET is in triode, so I redo it with the triode current equation and solve the resulting quadratic with the load-line equation.

The load line is the picture of this. The circuit says $I_D=(V_{DD}-V_{DS})/R_D$, a straight line from $V_{DD}$ on the voltage axis to $V_{DD}/R_D$ on the current axis. The transistor says $I_D$ is on one of the curves for the given $V_{GS}$. The operating point is where they cross. Low gate drive crosses in the flat saturation part, higher drive crosses in the steep triode part, and zero drive crosses at the $V_{DD}$ end."
:::

::: technical
**Procedure (N-channel, circuit: $V_{DD}$, drain resistor $R_D$, source grounded, $V_{GS}$ set by the gate network).**

1. If $V_{GS}\le V_t$: **cutoff**, $I_D\approx0$, $V_{DS}=V_{DD}$.
2. Else $V_{ov}=V_{GS}-V_t$. Assume saturation: $I_D=\tfrac12kV_{ov}^2$ (ignore $\lambda$ for first pass), $V_{DS}=V_{DD}-I_DR_D$.
3. If $V_{DS}\ge V_{ov}$: **saturation** confirmed.
4. Else **triode**. Solve simultaneously
$$I_D=k\left[V_{ov}V_{DS}-\tfrac12V_{DS}^2\right],\qquad V_{DS}=V_{DD}-I_DR_D$$
which gives $\tfrac12kR_D\,V_{DS}^2-(1+kR_DV_{ov})V_{DS}+V_{DD}=0$. Take the smaller root (the larger one lies beyond $V_{ov}$, outside triode).

**Worked example.** $V_{DD}=10$ V, $R_D=1$ kΩ, $k=2$ mA/V², $V_t=1$ V.

| $V_{GS}$ | $V_{ov}$ | Test | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 V | -0.5 V | below threshold | cutoff, $I_D=0$, $V_{DS}=10$ V |
| 3 V | 2 V | sat guess: $I_D=4$ mA, $V_{DS}=6$ V $\ge2$ V | **saturation**, 4 mA, 6 V |
| 6 V | 5 V | sat guess: $I_D=25$ mA, $V_{DS}=-15$ V, invalid | **triode**: $V_{DS}=1.00$ V, $I_D=9.00$ mA |

For the last row: $\tfrac12(2\text{ m})(1\text{ k})=1$, so $V_{DS}^2-(1+2\cdot5)V_{DS}+10=0$ i.e. $V_{DS}^2-11V_{DS}+10=0$, giving $V_{DS}=(11-\sqrt{121-40})/2=1.00$ V. $I_D=(10-1)/1\text{ k}=9.00$ mA. Check with the triode equation: $2\text{ m}(5\cdot1-0.5)=9$ mA ✓, and $V_{DS}<V_{ov}=5$ V ✓ triode. Deep-triode $R_{on}=1/(kV_{ov})=100$ Ω predicts roughly the divider value $10\cdot100/1100=0.91$ V; the exact value is slightly higher because the channel resistance rises as $V_{DS}$ grows.

**Boundary.** The device leaves saturation when $V_{DD}-\tfrac12kV_{ov}^2R_D=V_{ov}$, i.e. $V_{ov}^2+V_{ov}-10=0$, so $V_{ov}=2.70$ V, $V_{GS}=3.70$ V. Below 3.7 V it amplifies; above it it saturates as a switch.

**PMOS version.** Source at $V_{DD}=5$ V, $|V_t|=1$ V, $k_p=1$ mA/V², load $R=2$ kΩ to ground, gate at 2 V: $V_{SG}=3$ V, $V_{ov}=2$ V. Saturation guess gives $I_D=2$ mA, so $V_{SD}=5-4=1$ V $<2$ V: invalid, triode. Solving $V_{SD}^2-5V_{SD}+5=0$ gives $V_{SD}=1.38$ V and $I_D=1.81$ mA.

Including channel-length modulation, replace $I_D\to\tfrac12kV_{ov}^2(1+\lambda V_{DS})$; because $V_{DS}$ depends on $I_D$, iterate once or twice.
:::

::: long
"I'd treat it as assume-and-verify. Step one is the cheapest test: is $V_{GS}$ above $V_t$? If not, there's no channel, it's in cutoff, $I_D$ is basically zero and the drain sits at the supply.

If it is above, I compute the overdrive, $V_{GS}$ minus $V_t$, and assume saturation. Then $I_D$ is half k times overdrive squared. With a drain resistor, KVL gives me the drain voltage: $V_{DS}=V_{DD}-I_DR_D$. Now I verify. If $V_{DS}$ is at least the overdrive, the assumption holds and I'm done. For example, 10 V supply, 1 kΩ, k of 2 mA per volt squared, $V_t$ of 1 V and a 3 V gate: overdrive 2 V, current 4 mA, $V_{DS}$ is 6 V, bigger than 2, so it's saturated.

If I raise the gate to 6 V, the saturation guess gives 25 mA, which would put $V_{DS}$ at minus 15 volts. That's impossible, so I know the FET is in triode. I redo it with the triode equation and the load-line equation together, which is a quadratic: I get $V_{DS}$ of about 1 V and 9 mA, so the FET looks like roughly a 100 ohm resistor, which is what a switch should look like.

The load line is the graphical version. The circuit contributes a straight line from $V_{DD}$ on the $V_{DS}$ axis to $V_{DD}/R_D$ on the current axis. The transistor contributes a family of curves, one per gate voltage. The intersection is the Q-point. As $V_{GS}$ increases it slides up the line from the cutoff end, through saturation where it's an amplifier, into triode where it's a closed switch. Channel-length modulation just tilts the saturation curves slightly."
:::

::: terms
- **Load line**: the straight line of allowed $(V_{DS},I_D)$ pairs from the external circuit, $I_D=(V_{DD}-V_{DS})/R_D$.
- **Operating point (Q-point)**: the DC intersection of the load line and the device curve.
- **Overdrive ($V_{ov}$)**: $V_{GS}-V_t$.
- **Assume-and-verify**: the standard method of guessing a region, solving, then checking the region inequality.
- **Cutoff / triode / saturation**: the three regions ([[q0099]]).
- **Transconductance ($g_m$)**: $kV_{ov}$; sets the small-signal slope at the Q-point.
- **Headroom**: the margin $V_{DS}-V_{ov}$ before the device falls out of saturation.
- **Channel-length modulation ($\lambda$)**: a small positive slope in the saturation curves.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer states the region conditions and plugs numbers in. An exceptional answer:

- **Verifies** the assumed region instead of trusting it, and shows what happens when the check fails (negative $V_{DS}$ is a giveaway).
- Draws the load line with both intercepts ($V_{DD}$ and $V_{DD}/R_D$) and the Q-point on the correct curve.
- Finds the **boundary gate voltage** where saturation turns into triode (3.70 V in the example).
- Relates the answer to use: "in saturation it's an amplifier with gain $-g_mR_D$; in triode it's a closed switch with $R_{on}\approx100$ Ω".
- Mentions the effect of $\lambda$ and body effect and says when they matter.
- Handles the PMOS version with source-referenced voltages.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Skipping the verification.** If you get $V_{DS}$ negative or less than $V_{ov}$ the saturation answer is wrong. Never report 25 mA from a 10 V supply through 1 kΩ.
- **Testing $V_{DS}$ against $V_t$ rather than $V_{ov}$.** The boundary is $V_{DS}=V_{GS}-V_t$.
- **Using the saturation equation for a switch.** An on-state FET with a resistive load is in triode.
- **Drawing the load line with the wrong intercepts**: it goes from $(V_{DD},0)$ to $(0,V_{DD}/R_D)$, regardless of the transistor.
- **Forgetting the source resistor.** If there is $R_S$, then $V_{GS}=V_G-I_DR_S$ and the problem couples to the load line: solve it simultaneously.
- **Dropping the PMOS signs.** Use $V_{SG}$, $V_{SD}$ and check magnitudes.
:::

Read the picture three ways.

- **Which region?** Flat part = saturation. Steep rising part = triode.
- **How much gain?** The slope of the curves at the Q-point sets $g_m=kV_{ov}$ and small signal gain $A_v\approx-g_mR_D$. At $V_{GS}=3$ V, $g_m=4$ mS and $A_v=-4$. This is also the case for a switch: gain is near zero when it saturates into triode.
- **What swings are allowed?** Output can swing downwards only until the load line meets the knee (the dashed parabola, $V_{DS}=V_{ov}$). The headroom is $V_{DS}-V_{ov}=4$ V at $V_{GS}=3$ V.

## Adding a source resistor and channel-length modulation

With a source resistor, $V_{GS}=V_G-I_DR_S$, so $I_D=\tfrac12k(V_G-V_t-I_DR_S)^2$. This is a quadratic in $I_D$ that is solved by taking the root with $V_{GS}>V_t$. It is also the stable bias network for amplifiers ([[q0119]]) because larger $I_D$ lowers $V_{GS}$ (negative feedback).

With $\lambda$: $I_D=\tfrac12kV_{ov}^2(1+\lambda V_{DS})$ and $V_{DS}=V_{DD}-I_DR_D$. At $V_{GS}=3$ V and $\lambda=0.02$ /V (current in mA, $R_D=1$ kΩ): $I_D=4(1+0.02V_{DS})$ and $V_{DS}=10-I_D$, so $I_D=4.8-0.08I_D$, giving $I_D=4.44$ mA and $V_{DS}=5.56$ V. That is about 11% more current than the $\lambda=0$ answer. The output resistance is $r_o\approx1/(\lambda I_D)\approx11$ kΩ, which matters for gain, and the shift in bias is modest.

## Real hardware

- **Switch check.** For a power MOSFET, design so the load line ends deep in triode: check $V_{GS}$ is well above $V_{th}$ (datasheets give $R_{DS(on)}$ at specific $V_{GS}$ like 4.5 V or 10 V) and that $I_D\cdot R_{DS(on)}$ is a small fraction of the supply.
- **Linear-mode operation.** In hot-swap or load-switch circuits the FET is *intentionally* held in saturation during inrush, where it dissipates $V_{DS}I_D$; the safe-operating-area graph limits how long.
- **Temperature.** $V_t$ falls and mobility falls with temperature, so the Q-point drifts; the source-resistor bias reduces this.

## Worked example: the source-resistor bias point, solved and stress-tested

Add a 500 Ω source resistor to the same stage and set the gate at $V_G=5$ V from a divider. Keep $k=2$ mA/V² (so $\tfrac12k=1$ mA/V²), $V_t=1$ V, $R_D=1$ kΩ, $V_{DD}=10$ V. With $I_D$ in milliamps, $V_{GS}=V_G-0.5I_D$ and
$$I_D=(5-1-0.5I_D)^2=(4-0.5I_D)^2\ \Rightarrow\ 0.25I_D^2-5I_D+16=0\ \Rightarrow\ I_D^2-20I_D+64=0$$
The roots are $I_D=4$ mA and 16 mA. The 16 mA root would need $V_{GS}=5-8=-3$ V, below threshold, so it is spurious; this is the "take the root with $V_{GS}>V_t$" step. Then $V_S=2$ V, $V_{GS}=3$ V, $V_{ov}=2$ V, $V_D=10-4=6$ V, $V_{DS}=4$ V. Check: $V_{DS}=4>V_{ov}=2$, so the transistor is in saturation with 2 V of margin.

Now vary the threshold voltage by ±0.3 V, as process spread and temperature would. Without $R_S$ (gate set to give $V_{GS}=3$ V directly), $I_D=(3-V_t)^2$ mA gives 5.29, 4.00 and 2.89 mA for $V_t=0.7$, 1.0, 1.3 V: a spread of +32 % and −28 %. With the source resistor the answers are 4.40, 4.00 and 3.60 mA, a spread of ±10 %. The resistor has reduced the sensitivity to $V_t$ by roughly a factor of three, and the same mechanism reduces sensitivity to $k$ (mobility, which falls roughly 0.5 %/K near room temperature): if $k$ doubles from 2 to 4 mA/V², the unresisted stage doubles its current, but the resistor stage goes from 4 mA to 4.88 mA (+22 %). This is the fundamental reason discrete MOSFET amplifiers include a source resistor: $V_{GS}$ self-adjusts with current, like the emitter resistor of a BJT stage.

## Worked example: the AC load line and how much swing you get

The DC load line has slope $-1/R_D$. If the output is capacitively coupled into a 1 kΩ load, then for signals the drain sees $R_D\parallel R_L=500\ \Omega$, so the signal moves along a steeper **AC load line** of slope $-1/500\ \Omega$ through the same Q-point. For the $V_{GS}=3$ V Q-point ($V_{DS}=6$ V, $I_D=4$ mA, source resistor ignored for simplicity):

- **Upward swing** is limited by cutoff, where $I_D$ falls by 4 mA: the drain rises by $4\text{ mA}\times500\ \Omega=2$ V, to 8 V.
- **Downward swing** is limited by the knee. On the AC line $V_{DS}=6-0.5(I_D-4)$, with $I_D$ in mA, and the knee is $V_{DS}=V_{ov}=\sqrt{I_D}$ (in volts, for $I_D$ in mA, since $I_D=V_{ov}^2$). Setting $8-0.5I_D=\sqrt{I_D}$ gives $\sqrt{I_D}=3.12$, so $I_D=9.75$ mA and $V_{DS}=3.12$ V. The drain can fall by 2.88 V.

So the output can swing about +2 V and −2.9 V around the Q-point; clipping occurs on the upward half first, so the largest undistorted symmetric swing is about ±2 V (4 V peak-to-peak), against the ±4 V suggested by the DC load line alone. To get more symmetric swing, move the Q-point up in current (a higher gate bias), or increase $R_L$ relative to $R_D$. This is a ubiquitous exam question: it tests whether you see that the DC and AC load lines differ.

## Power along the load line

The power dissipated in the transistor at a point on the load line is $P=V_{DS}I_D=V_{DS}(V_{DD}-V_{DS})/R_D$, a downward parabola with its maximum at $V_{DS}=V_{DD}/2$:
$$P_{max}=\frac{V_{DD}^2}{4R_D}=\frac{100}{4000}=25\ \text{mW}$$
reached at $V_{DS}=5$ V and $I_D=5$ mA. That point is the worst case for a linear-mode device. The saturation-edge point ($V_{GS}=3.7$ V, 7.29 mA at 2.71 V) dissipates 19.8 mW, the amplifier Q-point at $V_{GS}=3$ V dissipates 24 mW (6 V × 4 mA), and the hard-on switch point ($V_{GS}=6$ V) dissipates only 9 mW (1 V × 9 mA). The deep-triode switch operates at the bottom of the parabola. During a switching transition the operating point sweeps across the middle of the load line, which is where the switching loss is concentrated. At 25 mW this is harmless, but scale the same picture to a 100 V, 20 A power switch and the mid-line point is 50 V × 10 A = 500 W instantaneously, which is why gate-drive speed and transition time matter so much in power stages.

## Why the load line does not stay straight: reactive and inductive loads

The resistive load line is the simplest case. With an inductive load such as a relay coil or motor winding, the drain current cannot change instantly, so when the transistor turns off the drain voltage flies up until the inductor's current finds a path (a flyback diode, or avalanche in the MOSFET itself). The trajectory in the $I_D$–$V_{DS}$ plane is no longer a straight line but a path that can overshoot beyond $V_{DD}$ at high current, and it must stay within the datasheet's safe operating area. With a capacitive load, the trajectory is an ellipse-like loop. A straight resistive load line is therefore a good first-order tool, but check it against the real load when designing a power stage.

## Verifying the load line on the bench

- **Measure the Q-point.** Build the circuit with a variable gate-bias source and a DMM on the drain. Step $V_{GS}$ through 2, 3, 4 and 6 V and compare the measured $V_D$ and $I_D$ (from the drop across a small series resistor, or from $(V_{DD}-V_D)/R_D$) with the table. The 3.7 V edge is found by watching where $V_D$ stops falling like $V_{DD}-I_DR_D$ with $I_D\propto V_{ov}^2$.
- **Check the AC swing.** Inject a sine at the gate on top of the bias and watch the drain on an oscilloscope while increasing the amplitude. The top should flatten first (cutoff) if the swing estimate is right; with a different load resistor the clip levels move as predicted by the AC load line.
- **Test bias robustness.** Swap three MOSFETs of the same type and measure $I_D$ each time with and without the source resistor, as in the worked example, or warm the part with a heat gun and watch the drift. The source resistor should cut the spread to roughly a third.
- **Estimate junction temperature in a switch.** Measure $V_{DS}$ at rated current with a short pulse, compute $P=V_{DS}I_D$ and multiply by the package's junction-to-ambient thermal resistance (for example, a small SOT-23 package is often in the hundreds of °C/W, so check the datasheet). A load switch that is hot to the touch is probably operating partly in saturation because the gate drive is too low.
:::

::: applications
- **Common-source amplifier bias**: pick $V_{GS}$ to land the Q-point mid-line in saturation for maximum swing ([[q0119]]).
- **Load switch design**: ensure $V_{GS}$ is enough for deep triode at max load current.
- **LED/relay driver check**: a logic-level NMOS with a 3.3 V gate driving a 100 mA load must be verified to be in triode with a small $V_{DS}$.
- **Pull-down / current-limiter circuits**: finding whether the FET saturates and acts as a current source.
- **Troubleshooting**: a measured $V_{DS}$ of a few volts with a gate well above threshold tells you the device is in saturation, unexpectedly dissipating power.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Square-law, long-channel, $\lambda=0$** for the hand calculation; real devices differ (velocity saturation, body effect). Use a simulator for final design.
- **Source at ground, body at source** (no body effect). With a source above ground, $V_t$ increases.
- **DC operating point only.** Small-signal gain, frequency response and gate capacitance are separate.
- **Resistor load.** With an active load (current source, mirror) the "load line" is a nearly horizontal curve instead of a straight line with slope $-1/R_D$.
- **Enhancement NMOS.** For depletion mode the same procedure applies with a negative $V_t$ ([[q0098]]); for PMOS use magnitudes.
:::

::: followups
### How would you bias the FET in the example to be a linear amplifier with maximum output swing?
Put the Q-point at the middle of the usable part of the load line: about $V_{DS}\approx(V_{DD}+V_{ov})/2$. With $V_{DD}=10$ V this means $V_{DS}\approx6$ V for $V_{ov}=2$ V, which is $V_{GS}=3$ V, $I_D=4$ mA, giving 4 V of headroom downwards and 4 V upwards. Small-signal gain is $-g_mR_D=-4$.

### What if there's a 500 Ω resistor in the source?
Now $V_{GS}=V_G-I_D\cdot500$. With $V_G=4$ V: $I_D=\tfrac12(2\text{ m})(3-500I_D)^2$. In mA, $x=(3-0.5x)^2=9-3x+0.25x^2$ so $0.25x^2-4x+9=0$, $x=(4-\sqrt{16-9})/0.5=2.71$ mA ($V_{GS}=2.65$ V). The other root, 13.3 mA, would make $V_{GS}<V_t$. $V_{DS}=10-2.71(1.5)=5.9$ V $>V_{ov}=1.65$ V, saturation ✓.

### Why does the saturation guess go wrong at high $V_{GS}$?
The square-law saturation current rises as $V_{ov}^2$ without limit, but the resistor limits real current to at most $V_{DD}/R_D$. Once the guess implies $V_{DS}<V_{ov}$ (or negative), the model is out of its validity range and triode is the only consistent region.

### How does the load line change with $R_D$?
$R_D$ changes the slope ($-1/R_D$) and the current-axis intercept ($V_{DD}/R_D$), but not the $V_{DD}$ intercept. A bigger $R_D$ gives a shallower line, pushing the device toward triode at lower $V_{GS}$ and giving more gain ($g_mR_D$) at the cost of headroom.

### Harder: what changes if $R_D$ is replaced by a PMOS current source?
The load line becomes almost horizontal (a high-resistance curve with slope $1/r_{o}$), so the intersection with the NMOS saturation curve is very sensitive: tiny shifts in $V_{GS}$ slide $V_{DS}$ across the supply. Gain becomes $-g_m(r_{o,n}\parallel r_{o,p})$, tens to hundreds. But the bias point is hard to set precisely without feedback.

### Scenario: your logic-level FET's $V_{DS}$ measures 2 V at a 1 A load even though the gate is at 3.3 V, and it gets hot. What do you check?
Compute the actual overdrive. If the FET has $V_{th}\approx2$ V and only 3.3 V drive, $V_{ov}\approx1.3$ V, so $R_{on}$ is far above the datasheet 4.5 V or 10 V figure, and it may be sitting in saturation. Use a part specified at 3.3 V (or a driver at higher voltage), and recompute $P=I^2R_{on}$.
:::

::: interpretations
"Given $V_{GS}$ and $V_t$, how will the FET behave?" is incomplete without $V_{DS}$ or a circuit. The usual intended answer is the **procedure** above: $V_{GS}<V_t$ means cutoff; otherwise the region depends on $V_{DS}$ vs $V_{GS}-V_t$, and with a given load the region is found by the load-line analysis. If no load is given, state your assumption (for instance a resistor to $V_{DD}$) and say so.
:::
