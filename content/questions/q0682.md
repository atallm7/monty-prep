::: testing
Whether you can explain noise immunity as a **mechanism** rather than a slogan: noise couples nearly equally onto two closely routed wires (common-mode), the receiver amplifies the difference and rejects the common part (CMRR), and several secondary effects add up (doubled signal, no dependence on the ground reference, built-in threshold, cancelling return currents, supply rejection). The interviewer also wants to see you state the limits: the rejection is finite, requires symmetry, and is wrecked by skew and imbalance, which convert common-mode into differential.
:::

::: why
Noise immunity is the main reason differential signalling exists, and "how does it improve noise immunity?" is the question where interviewers separate memorized answers from understood ones. Being able to quantify it (CMRR, mode conversion from 1% imbalance) and to say what breaks it is how you debug a link that "should be immune" but is not: a bad connector, an unequal via, a ground-return asymmetry, a cable shield treated badly.
:::

::: concepts
- **Common-mode vs differential**: $V_{cm}=(V_++V_-)/2$, $V_d=V_+-V_-$ ([[q0683]], [[q0684]]).
- **Common-mode rejection ratio**: $CMRR=|A_d/A_{cm}|$, in dB.
- **Coupling mechanisms**: capacitive, inductive, shared-impedance (ground), and radiated fields ([[q0565]]).
- **Symmetry**: equal coupling and loading on each leg keeps noise common-mode.
- **Mode conversion**: asymmetry turns common-mode noise into differential noise.
- **Reference independence**: threshold is the leg crossing, not a fixed level ([[q0680]]).
:::

::: answer
"The core idea is that noise gets onto both wires at the same time and by about the same amount, and the receiver only looks at the difference. So the noise is common-mode and it cancels, while the signal is opposite on the two wires, so it adds.

Why is the noise the same on both wires? Because the two wires are routed or twisted together. A neighbouring trace couples capacitively into both, a magnetic field induces about the same voltage in both loops, and a ground shift moves both wires together. In the receiver, the difference amplifier has a high common-mode rejection ratio, say 60 dB, so half a volt of common-mode noise leaves about half a millivolt.

There are some extra bonuses. The signal is twice as big, so you get 6 dB better signal-to-noise for the same swing per wire. The threshold is the crossing point of the two wires, so it doesn't depend on a ground or supply reference that's moving around. The equal and opposite currents mean it doesn't disturb its own ground or radiate much.

The caveat is that the immunity is only as good as the symmetry. If one wire sees more coupling, or the legs have skew, part of the common-mode noise turns into differential. A 1 percent imbalance limits the rejection to about 40 dB."
:::

::: technical
**Receiver model.** $V_{out}=A_dV_d+A_{cm}V_{cm}$. Input-referred error from common-mode: $V_{err}=V_{cm}/CMRR$.

**Examples.** 0.5 V common-mode noise: CMRR 40 dB (100): 5 mV; 60 dB (1000): 0.5 mV; 80 dB (10 000): 50 µV.

**Coupling cancels.**
- *Capacitive*: aggressor $v_a$ through $C_{c1}$ and $C_{c2}$ gives noise $\propto C_c$ on each wire; equal $C_c$ gives equal noise.
- *Inductive/magnetic*: induced EMF $=-d\Phi/dt$ in each loop. In a twisted pair, adjacent twists have opposite orientation, so induced voltages alternate sign and cancel along the cable.
- *Ground shift / supply noise*: moves both wires together.

**Mode conversion (imbalance).** If one wire couples with relative imbalance $\varepsilon$ (e.g. $C_{c1}=C_{c2}(1+\varepsilon)$ or unequal terminating impedances), a fraction of the common-mode noise appears as differential: $v_{d,noise}\approx\varepsilon\,v_{cm}/2$ to $\varepsilon v_{cm}$ depending on the mechanism. With $\varepsilon=1\%$ and 1 V noise: about 5 to 10 mV, a 40 to 46 dB limit.

**Resistor-network receiver.** For a 4-resistor difference amplifier with resistor tolerance $t$, worst-case $CMRR\approx(1+G)/(4t)$: unity gain, 1% resistors give about 50 (34 dB); 0.1% give 500 (54 dB). Dedicated instrumentation amplifiers and RS-485/LVDS receivers reach 60 to 100 dB at low frequency, with CMRR falling at high frequency.

**Doubled signal**: legs $\pm V_s$ give $V_d=\pm2V_s$ (+6 dB SNR for equal noise per wire, which for correlated noise is much better).

**Skew.** Leg skew $t_s$ yields a common-mode glitch $\approx\Delta V_{leg}t_s/(2t_r)$ and shifts the differential crossing, causing jitter and eye closure ([[q0679]]).

**Common-mode range.** Even with perfect CMRR, a common-mode voltage outside the input range (LVDS about 0 to 2.4 V, RS-485 $-7$ to $+12$ V) breaks the receiver.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> CMRR values quoted are typical at low frequency. Datasheet CMRR is specified at particular frequencies and drops (often 20 dB per decade) as frequency rises; read the curve for your noise frequency.
:::

::: short
"Noise couples about equally onto both wires of a closely routed pair, so it's common-mode, and the differential receiver subtracts it away. The receiver's common-mode rejection ratio sets how well: 60 dB turns half a volt of common-mode noise into half a millivolt. Beyond that, the signal is twice as large, the threshold is the crossing of the two wires so it doesn't depend on ground or supply, and the equal and opposite currents don't disturb their own ground. The limits are symmetry, skew and the receiver's common-mode range."
:::

::: long
"The mechanism is subtraction. A differential receiver outputs gain times V-plus minus V-minus. If noise adds the same voltage to both wires, that voltage drops out of the difference. The question is why the noise is the same on both wires, and the answer is geometry. The two wires are routed or twisted close together, so a nearby aggressor couples to both through nearly equal capacitance, a magnetic field links both loops with nearly equal flux, and a ground shift moves both together. In a twisted pair the induced voltages from adjacent twists have opposite signs, so they also cancel along the length.

Then the receiver. Its common-mode rejection ratio is the differential gain divided by the common-mode gain. At 60 dB, half a volt of common-mode noise becomes half a millivolt at the input. A 100 mV receiver threshold has plenty of room.

There are secondary benefits. The signal doubles, because the wires move in opposite directions, so for a given swing per wire you gain 6 dB. The threshold is the crossing point of the two signals, so it does not depend on a ground or supply reference that moves with noise. And because the currents are equal and opposite, the driver does not inject much switching current into its own ground, so it's less of a noise source too.

The honest caveat is that the immunity only holds if the pair is balanced. If one wire picks up more coupling, or has a different load or via, some common-mode converts to differential. A 1 percent imbalance caps rejection at roughly 40 dB. Skew between the legs creates a common-mode glitch and shifts the crossing, which shows up as jitter. And the receiver has a common-mode input range, so a large ground offset can still break it. So I'd say differential gives you 40 to 80 dB of rejection when it's done well, and a good layout is what protects that."
:::

::: terms
- **Common-mode noise**: interference with the same voltage on both wires.
- **CMRR**: $|A_d/A_{cm}|$; how many dB the receiver favours the difference.
- **Mode conversion**: transformation of common-mode noise into differential by asymmetry.
- **Twisted pair**: two conductors twisted together so induced noise alternates and cancels.
- **Balanced line**: both conductors have equal impedance to ground.
- **Crossover threshold**: point where the two legs cross, $V_d=0$.
- **Skew**: arrival-time difference between legs.
- **Common-mode input range**: allowed range of $V_{cm}$ at the receiver.
- **PSRR**: ability to reject supply noise; differential circuits have better PSRR.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "noise appears on both wires and cancels when you subtract".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains why noise is equal on both wires (proximity, twisting, symmetric loading) for each coupling mechanism (capacitive, inductive, ground).
- Quantifies the rejection with CMRR and a worked example, and the limit from a 1% imbalance.
- Lists secondary benefits (6 dB signal doubling, reference-free threshold, cancelling return currents, lower EMI emitted, supply rejection).
- Identifies what breaks it: skew, asymmetric layout/loading, limited common-mode range, CMRR roll-off with frequency.
- Gives a debugging heuristic: if a "differential" link is noisy, look for common-mode current and mode conversion.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Differential eliminates noise."** It rejects *common-mode* noise only. Differential-mode noise (e.g. crosstalk from a trace that couples to only one leg) passes through.
- **"CMRR is a fixed number."** It depends on frequency, source impedance imbalance and component matching.
- **Ignoring the common-mode range.** A receiver that is out of range does not reject anything; it clips.
- **Routing the legs apart or through different environments.** Unequal coupling destroys the cancellation.
- **Terminating unequally.** Asymmetric terminations or loads convert common-mode to differential.
- **Forgetting the generated noise.** Skew in your own driver creates common-mode, which radiates and then comes back as noise elsewhere.
:::

::: deep
## Noise sources and how they couple

Four routes put unwanted voltage on a signal:

1. **Capacitive coupling**: an aggressor with $dV/dt$ drives displacement current $C_c\,dV_a/dt$ into the victim.
2. **Inductive (magnetic) coupling**: changing current in a nearby loop induces $M\,dI/dt$ or $-d\Phi/dt$ in the victim loop.
3. **Common impedance (ground and supply)**: shared return or supply impedance converts someone else's current into a voltage that you see ($L\,di/dt$, $IR$).
4. **Radiated fields**: far-field E or H fields induce voltages in loops and wires acting as antennas.

In every case, the coupled voltage depends on the *geometry between aggressor and victim wire*. Two wires that run side by side, much closer to each other than to the aggressor, see geometry that is almost identical for each. The aggressor is "far" relative to the pair spacing. The result is nearly equal noise on both.

## How good is the cancellation: CMRR

The receiver has differential gain $A_d$ and common-mode gain $A_{cm}$. Define
$$CMRR=\frac{|A_d|}{|A_{cm}|},\qquad CMRR_{dB}=20\log_{10}\frac{|A_d|}{|A_{cm}|}.$$
Referred to the input, a common-mode voltage $V_{cm}$ looks like a differential error $V_{cm}/CMRR$:

| CMRR | 0.5 V common-mode becomes |
|---|---|
| 40 dB | 5 mV |
| 60 dB | 0.5 mV |
| 80 dB | 50 µV |
| 100 dB | 5 µV |

In a resistor-based difference amplifier the CMRR comes from resistor matching: with four resistors of tolerance $t$ and gain $G$, worst-case CMRR is about $(1+G)/(4t)$. For a unity-gain stage with 1% resistors that is 50 (34 dB); 0.1% resistors give 500 (54 dB). Integrated instrumentation amplifiers use laser-trimmed networks and reach 90 to 120 dB at low frequency. Digital receivers (LVDS, RS-485) specify a threshold and common-mode range rather than CMRR; at high speed their effective rejection falls with frequency because of input capacitance mismatch.

## Twisting and the magnetic field

A loop of area $A$ in a changing field $B(t)$ has induced EMF $-A\,dB/dt$. Two wires of a straight, untwisted pair form one big loop between them and the termination, so the voltage induced in that loop is $A\,dB/dt$, which appears as differential-mode noise and is not rejected. Twisting breaks the loop into many small loops of alternating orientation: induced voltages in adjacent loops have opposite signs and cancel. That's why twisted pair is important for magnetic interference, and why balanced signalling plus twisting is better than either alone: balancing handles electric (capacitive) coupling; twisting handles magnetic coupling.

## Imbalance and mode conversion

Suppose a noisy trace couples to wire P through $C_1$ and to wire N through $C_2$. The induced noise voltages are $\propto C_1$ and $C_2$. The common-mode part is $(C_1+C_2)/2$ and the differential part is $C_1-C_2$. If $C_2=0.99C_1$, the differential part is 1% of the coupled noise: 40 dB of rejection, no matter how good the receiver's CMRR. Unequal terminations or loading convert in the same way: a common-mode voltage $v_{cm}$ drives current through unequal impedances $Z_1\ne Z_2$ and creates a difference voltage of roughly $v_{cm}(Z_1-Z_2)/(Z_1+Z_2)$, i.e. about 0.5% of $v_{cm}$ for a 1% mismatch.

This is why layout rules insist on symmetry: equal lengths, equal spacing to neighbours, symmetric vias and connector pins, equal load capacitance ([[q0519]], [[q0520]]).

## Signal doubling and the threshold

Relative to a single wire against ground with the same swing, the pair gives twice the signal for the same noise per wire: +6 dB. For *uncorrelated* noise on each wire (thermal or driver noise) the benefit is exactly that 6 dB minus 3 dB from adding two noise sources, a net 3 dB; for *correlated* noise it is the CMRR. The decision threshold is at $V_d=0$ (the crossing) and does not depend on a fixed voltage that supply and ground noise can move.

## Lower noise generation

Immunity has an inverse: the pair emits less. The equal and opposite currents mean the far fields cancel. Because current-steering differential drivers draw constant supply current, there is little $L\,di/dt$ noise on the supply and ground, so the driver is not a noise source for the rest of the board ([[q0688]]). The net effect is that differential signalling improves both sides of EMC: susceptibility and emissions.

## Limits and failure modes

- **Differential-mode noise** is not rejected: crosstalk from a trace that couples to one leg only; magnetic fields linking the loop between the legs.
- **Common-mode range exceeded**: the input stage saturates.
- **CMRR roll-off at high frequency**: input capacitance mismatch and finite amplifier bandwidth.
- **Skew and mode conversion** in the driver, connector, cable or layout.
- **Cable common-mode currents** radiate even with a perfectly good differential receiver; use chokes and shields.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Say the mechanism in one line ("noise is common-mode, the receiver subtracts"), give one number ("60 dB turns 0.5 V into 0.5 mV") and one limit ("a 1% imbalance caps it near 40 dB"). That structure covers what interviewers listen for.
:::

::: applications
- **Balanced microphone cables**: hum from mains wiring appears equally on both conductors; the preamp's differential input removes it.
- **Industrial fieldbuses** (RS-485, CAN): motor and relay noise coupled onto the cable is mostly common-mode.
- **Instrumentation front-ends**: bridge and thermocouple signals measured with an in-amp rejecting the excitation common-mode level.
- **High-speed serial links**: supply and ground noise rejected by differential receivers; small swing signalling.
- **Differential ADC drive and clocks**: rejection of supply noise that would otherwise become jitter or spurs.
- **Touch and capacitive sensing** with differential measurement to reject environmental noise.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Noise is mostly common-mode**, which needs close, symmetric routing; if noise couples to one wire only, the benefit disappears.
- **Receiver within its common-mode range** and with adequate CMRR at the noise frequency.
- **Linear receiver**: large common-mode signals can generate differential error via nonlinearity.
- **Balanced source and load impedances**; real systems have some imbalance.
- Numbers (CMRR examples, 1% imbalance) are illustrative.

Alternative viewpoint: from a systems perspective differential signalling "moves the reference from an unreliable global ground to a local reference (the other wire)". Both explanations are valid; the CMRR explanation is the quantitative one.
:::

::: followups
### What CMRR do you need to receive a 100 mV differential signal with 2 V of common-mode noise and no more than 1% error?
Allowed error 1 mV at the input: $2\text{ V}/CMRR\le1\text{ mV}\Rightarrow CMRR\ge2000$, about 66 dB. In practice also account for common-mode range and for the frequency at which the noise occurs (CMRR is lower at higher frequency).

### Why is a twisted pair better than two parallel wires for noise immunity?
Twisting makes the pair's loops alternate in orientation so magnetically induced voltages cancel along the length, and it keeps both wires equally close to any noise source so capacitive coupling is equal. Two parallel straight wires enclose a loop that picks up magnetic fields as a differential voltage.

### Does a differential receiver reject crosstalk?
Only the common-mode part. If an aggressor is closer to one leg than the other (as it usually is in a PCB), the coupling is unequal and part of it is differential. Spacing the pair tightly, keeping aggressors far (the 3W rule) and symmetric helps ([[q0694]]).

### How much common-mode noise results from 20 ps of skew on a 100 ps, 350 mV-per-leg edge, and does the receiver reject it?
About $0.35\times20/(2\times100)=35$ mV peak. The receiver's CMRR rejects it from the output's perspective, but the differential crossing also moves and the eye closes; the common-mode glitch also radiates. So skew costs timing and EMI beyond just noise.

### What if the noise frequency is above the receiver's CMRR corner?
CMRR typically decreases with frequency (e.g. an amplifier with 100 dB at DC might have 60 dB at 100 kHz and 40 dB at 1 MHz), so high-frequency noise is rejected less. Use a common-mode choke or filter in front of the receiver, shielding, and make the input network as symmetric as possible; select a receiver whose CMRR is specified at your noise frequency.
:::
