::: testing
Whether you can name the dopants **and explain the rule behind them**: a donor has one more valence electron than the host, an acceptor has one fewer. The interviewer wants to see:

1. The silicon answer: phosphorus, arsenic, antimony for N-type; boron (and gallium, aluminium, indium) for P-type.
2. The periodic-table logic (Group V for N, Group III for P, in a Group IV host).
3. Awareness that the choice among them is a *process* decision (diffusivity, solubility, ionization energy, mass), not arbitrary.
4. Bonus: that compound semiconductors use different dopants (Si and Mg in GaN, Si and Zn in GaAs).
:::

::: why
Dopant choice shows up in real process and device decisions: arsenic for shallow, abrupt N+ source/drain junctions because it diffuses slowly; boron for P-type everywhere because it is the only element that is soluble and well behaved enough; phosphorus for deep wells and for the very high activation in N+ emitters. In a hardware interview it tests whether you connect the periodic table to carrier type, and in a fab or device interview it separates people who know the process from those who only know the theory.
:::

::: concepts
- **Valence electrons and bonding**: silicon (Group IV) forms four covalent bonds. See [[q0062]].
- **Donors (Group V)**: one extra valence electron; becomes a fixed $+$ ion and a free electron.
- **Acceptors (Group III)**: one fewer valence electron; becomes a fixed $-$ ion and a free hole.
- **Substitutional site and atomic size**: a good dopant has a size close to silicon so it fits the lattice without much strain.
- **Solid solubility and diffusivity**: set the maximum active concentration and how far a dopant moves in a hot process. See [[q0065]].
:::

::: answer
"For silicon, it comes straight from the periodic table. Silicon is Group IV with four valence electrons. If I substitute a Group V atom, which has five, there's one extra electron, so it's a donor and gives N-type. The usual ones are phosphorus, arsenic and sometimes antimony. If I substitute a Group III atom with three valence electrons, there's one fewer, so it's an acceptor and gives P-type. The standard one is boron, and gallium, aluminium and indium are used in special cases.

Which one you pick is about the process. Arsenic is heavy and diffuses slowly, so it makes shallow, abrupt junctions, which is why it's used for source and drain. Phosphorus diffuses faster and is used for deeper wells and for heavily doped layers. Boron is small, so it diffuses quickly and is the default P-type dopant.

For compound semiconductors it changes. In gallium arsenide, silicon sits on the gallium site and acts as a donor, and zinc or carbon give P-type. In gallium nitride, silicon is the donor and magnesium is the acceptor."
:::

::: technical
**Rule.** Substitutional atoms with valence one more than the host donate an electron; one fewer accept one.

**Silicon dopants** (approximate energy levels from the nearest band edge, from Sze-type tables; sources differ by a few meV):

| Type | Element | Group | Ionization energy | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Donor | Phosphorus (P) | V | ~45 meV | Fast diffuser, high solubility; wells, N+ layers, phosphorus glass gettering |
| Donor | Arsenic (As) | V | ~54 meV | Slow diffuser, high solubility; shallow N+ source/drain, buried layers |
| Donor | Antimony (Sb) | V | ~40 meV | Slowest; buried layers, high-voltage epi substrates |
| Acceptor | Boron (B) | III | ~45 meV | Light, fast diffuser; standard P-type dopant |
| Acceptor | Aluminium (Al) | III | ~57 meV | Rarely used in silicon ICs (unwanted fast diffusion in oxide) |
| Acceptor | Gallium (Ga) | III | ~65 meV | Used in solar-grade wafers (less light-induced degradation than boron) |
| Acceptor | Indium (In) | III | ~160 meV | Deep acceptor; used for retrograde channel profiles |

**Why ~45 meV?** The hydrogenic model gives $E_d\approx13.6\ \text{eV}\times\frac{m^*/m_0}{\varepsilon_r^2}\approx13.6\times\frac{0.26}{11.7^2}\approx26$ meV. Real shallow donors in silicon are 40-65 meV due to central-cell corrections, still comparable to $kT=26$ meV and fully ionized at room temperature at moderate doping.

**Other hosts.**
- **Germanium:** same groups (P, As, Sb; B, Ga, In).
- **GaAs:** Si, Te, Se, S as donors (Si on the Ga site; amphoteric at high density); Zn, Be, C, Mg as acceptors.
- **GaN:** Si as donor; Mg as acceptor (deep, about 150-200 meV, so only a few percent are ionized at room temperature).
- **SiC:** N and P as donors; Al and B as acceptors.

**Solid solubility** (maximum concentration in silicon at process temperatures): about $10^{21}$ cm⁻³ for As and P and a few $10^{20}$ for B (typical values). Active carrier density is lower than total dopant above about $2\times10^{20}$ due to clustering.
:::

::: short
"In silicon, donors are Group V atoms, with an extra valence electron: phosphorus, arsenic and antimony give N-type. Acceptors are Group III atoms with one fewer: boron mainly, and gallium, aluminium or indium in special cases, give P-type. Arsenic diffuses slowly so it's used for shallow source and drain; phosphorus diffuses faster and goes in wells; boron is the standard P-type dopant. In compound semiconductors the dopants differ, such as silicon and magnesium in GaN."
:::

::: long
"The logic is the periodic table. Silicon is in Group IV and has four valence electrons, all used in bonds. If I swap in a Group V atom, it has five valence electrons, so one is extra and gets released into the conduction band. That atom is a donor and the silicon becomes N-type. Phosphorus, arsenic and antimony are the common ones. If I swap in a Group III atom, it has three valence electrons, so one bond is incomplete, and it captures an electron from the lattice and leaves a hole. That's an acceptor and the silicon becomes P-type. Boron is the main one, and gallium, aluminium and indium show up less.

In silicon they all ionize easily, around 45 to 65 meV from the band edge, except indium, which is deep at roughly 160 meV, so room temperature thermal energy of 26 meV frees most of the shallow ones.

Which I'd pick comes from the process. Arsenic is a heavy atom that diffuses slowly, so after anneals the junction stays shallow and abrupt, and that's what you want for modern MOSFET source and drain. Phosphorus diffuses faster, so it's used for wells, for heavily doped emitters and for getting the highest active density. Antimony is the slowest and sits in buried layers that need to stay put during later epitaxy. For P-type, boron is the standard: its size fits the lattice and it dissolves to high concentrations, though it diffuses fast and out-diffuses into oxide, which is something designers account for.

I'd also mention it's not universal. In gallium arsenide the donors are silicon, selenium or tellurium and the acceptors zinc, beryllium or carbon. In gallium nitride the donor is silicon and the acceptor is magnesium, which is a deep level so only a small fraction of it is ionized. So the rule of thumb about valence still holds, but you have to think about which lattice site the atom sits on."
:::

::: terms
- **Donor**: dopant with one more valence electron than the host (P, As, Sb in Si).
- **Acceptor**: dopant with one fewer valence electron (B, Ga, Al, In in Si).
- **Shallow level**: a dopant energy level within a few $kT$ of a band edge, so it ionizes at room temperature.
- **Deep level**: a level far from the band edge (such as In in Si, Mg in GaN); poorly ionized at room temperature.
- **Amphoteric dopant**: one that can act as donor or acceptor depending on lattice site (Si in GaAs).
- **Solid solubility**: maximum dopant concentration the crystal can dissolve at a given temperature.
- **Diffusivity**: rate at which dopants migrate at high temperature; sets junction depth after thermal steps.
- **Transmutation doping**: converting a silicon-30 atom to phosphorus by neutron capture, giving uniform N-type doping in large wafers.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "phosphorus and boron."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the rule (Group V donor, Group III acceptor in a Group IV host) with the valence-electron reason.
- Lists the full set for silicon: P, As, Sb versus B, Ga, Al, In, and notes boron and phosphorus/arsenic dominate.
- Explains the *process* reason for choosing each: As for shallow junctions, B as the standard P-type, Sb for buried layers.
- Mentions ionization energy ($\sim$45 meV, shallow) and the odd one out (In, deep).
- Mentions that compound semiconductors differ (Si/Mg in GaN, Si/Zn in GaAs, N/Al in SiC) and the amphoteric nature of Si in GaAs.
- Notes practical issues: solubility limits, out-diffusion, and that dopants also come in unintentionally (e.g. boron from some glassware or ambient).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Swapping the groups.** Boron is Group III and makes P-type; phosphorus is Group V and makes N-type. Mnemonic: **P**hosphorus makes **N**, **B**oron makes **P**.
- **Saying "silicon is doped with silicon".** Silicon only acts as a dopant in compound hosts such as GaAs and GaN, where it sits on the Ga site as a donor.
- **Naming Group IV or VI atoms as the answer for silicon.** Carbon and germanium are isoelectronic (same valence) and do not dope; Group VI atoms such as sulfur or selenium are deep, double donors in silicon and not used as normal dopants.
- **Confusing dopants with contaminants.** Gold, iron, copper and nickel create deep levels in silicon that act as recombination centres and kill lifetime. Gold doping is used deliberately only to shorten carrier lifetime in some fast diodes and thyristors.
- **Assuming every dopant atom contributes a carrier.** Only the substitutional, ionized fraction does. Mg in GaN is only a few percent ionized at room temperature; very high doping can cluster and deactivate.
- **Thinking the choice of dopant doesn't matter.** Diffusivity and solubility differences determine junction depth and achievable resistance.
:::

## Silicon donors: P, As, Sb

All three have five valence electrons, are small enough to sit substitutionally, and ionize at roughly 40-55 meV. The differences are practical.

- **Phosphorus** diffuses fairly fast, which is useful for deep N-wells and drive-in steps, and it gives very high active concentration. It also forms a phosphosilicate glass that getters metallic contaminants. It is also made *inside* silicon by **neutron transmutation doping**: neutron irradiation turns $^{30}$Si into $^{31}$P, giving extremely uniform N-type wafers used for power devices.
- **Arsenic** is heavy and slow, so after a thermal anneal it stays near where it was implanted: this is the choice for shallow, abrupt N+ source and drain regions and for the emitter of NPN transistors. Solubility is high (about $2\times10^{21}$ cm⁻³ at high temperature).
- **Antimony** diffuses even more slowly, so it is used for buried N+ layers beneath an epitaxial layer (the layer has to survive many later hot steps).

## Silicon acceptors: boron and the rest

**Boron** is the dominant acceptor because it is small, soluble (a few $10^{20}$ cm⁻³) and stable in silicon. Its drawbacks: a high diffusivity (transient enhanced diffusion during anneal after implantation) and a tendency to segregate into oxide, which depletes the silicon surface. **Gallium** is used in some solar wafers because boron-oxygen complexes cause light-induced degradation in boron-doped cells. **Aluminium** diffuses quickly through oxide and is used mainly in deep diffusions for power devices and as a back-surface field in solar cells. **Indium** is a deep acceptor (about 160 meV) so is only partly ionized at room temperature; it is used for retrograde channel profiles in some MOSFET processes because it diffuses so little.

Quick physical check using the hydrogenic model: a donor electron orbits the ion like an electron around a proton but screened by $\varepsilon_r=11.7$ and with a lighter effective mass $m^*$:
$$E_d=13.6\ \text{eV}\cdot\frac{m^*/m_0}{\varepsilon_r^2}$$
With $m^*/m_0\approx0.26$: $E_d\approx26$ meV, and the Bohr radius is $a=0.53\ \text{Å}\times\varepsilon_r/(m^*/m_0)\approx24$ Å, spanning many lattice sites. That explains why all Group V donors look similar (45-ish meV): the far-field behaviour dominates, and only the core ("central cell") differs. Real values are somewhat larger because of the central-cell correction.

## Choosing between them: junction depth

Diffusion length after an anneal is of order $\sqrt{Dt}$, with $D=D_0e^{-E_a/kT}$. Since the diffusivity of As and Sb is far lower than P and B at the same temperature, As can hold a junction at tens of nanometres through anneals that would drive phosphorus or boron much deeper.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Quoted ionization energies, solubility limits and relative diffusivities depend on source, doping level and temperature. Treat the table as a guide to ordering (As slower than P; B fast; In deep), not as design data.

## Beyond silicon

- **GaAs:** silicon (on Ga sites) is the usual donor; tellurium, selenium and sulfur are also used. Acceptors are zinc, beryllium, magnesium and carbon. Silicon is *amphoteric* in GaAs: at very high concentrations some atoms land on As sites and act as acceptors, compensating the donors.
- **GaN:** silicon is the donor. Magnesium is the usual acceptor but its level is deep (about 150-200 meV), so at room temperature only a few percent of the atoms are ionized, which is why p-GaN is resistive and why GaN HEMTs avoid it for conduction. Magnesium also needs an activation anneal to drive off hydrogen.
- **SiC:** nitrogen and phosphorus are donors (nitrogen is the standard); aluminium and boron are acceptors. Dopants diffuse extremely slowly in SiC, so doping is done by implantation at high temperature or by epitaxy.
- **Germanium:** the same as silicon (P, As, Sb donors; Ga, B, In acceptors).

## Unwanted dopants

Any contaminant with a level in the gap matters. Gold, iron, copper and nickel create deep traps that increase leakage and cut minority-carrier lifetime, which is why fabs are so strict about metal contamination. Oxygen and carbon in silicon are normally electrically neutral but can form donor complexes ("thermal donors") when annealed near 450 °C. Even trace boron in an N-type source gas can compensate a lightly doped layer.

## Worked example: not every dopant atom becomes a carrier

The statement "$p\approx N_A$" assumes every acceptor is ionized. For a shallow acceptor that is nearly true, but for a deep one it is not, and you can see the difference with a short calculation. For a p-type sample with negligible donors, charge neutrality plus the Boltzmann occupation of the acceptor level gives

$$\frac{p^2}{N_A-p}=\frac{N_V}{g}\,e^{-E_a/kT}$$

where $E_a$ is the acceptor level above the valence band, $g=4$ is the usual acceptor degeneracy factor, and $N_V\approx1.04\times10^{19}$ cm⁻³ at 300 K (a commonly quoted value; others use $1.8\times10^{19}$). Solving this quadratic for $N_A=10^{16}$ cm⁻³ at 300 K, with representative ionization energies of 45, 67, 72 and 160 meV for B, Al, Ga and In:

| Acceptor | $E_a$ (meV) | Fraction ionized at 300 K | At 200 K |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boron | 45 | 98 % | 95 % |
| Aluminium | 67 | 95 % | 86 % |
| Gallium | 72 | 94 % | 83 % |
| Indium | 160 | 51 % | 14 % |

So an indium-doped wafer with $10^{16}$ cm⁻³ atoms has only about $5\times10^{15}$ holes at room temperature, and the hole density is now temperature dependent, which is the very behavior doping was supposed to remove. Boron is nearly flat. The same method for phosphorus ($E_d\approx45$ meV, $g=2$, $N_C\approx2.8\times10^{19}$ cm⁻³) gives 99.6 % ionization at 300 K, but only 89 % at 100 K and 18 % at 50 K: that is freeze-out. At higher doping the fractions fall because the Fermi level moves toward the level.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Ionization energies and effective density-of-states values vary by a few meV or tens of percent between references, and at doping above about $10^{17}$ cm⁻³ the levels broaden into an impurity band, so the numbers above are for the dilute limit only. Treat the table as showing the trend: deep acceptor, partial ionization.

## Worked example: why arsenic wins for shallow junctions

Take representative intrinsic diffusion parameters, $D=D_0e^{-E_a/kT}$, as commonly tabulated in process textbooks: boron $D_0=0.76$ cm²/s, $E_a=3.46$ eV; phosphorus 3.85 and 3.66; arsenic 22.9 and 4.1; antimony 0.214 and 3.65. At 1000 °C (1273 K) and a 30 minute furnace step:

| Dopant | $D$ (cm²/s) | $\sqrt{Dt}$ |
|---|---|---|
| Boron | $1.5\times10^{-14}$ | 52 nm |
| Phosphorus | $1.2\times10^{-14}$ | 47 nm |
| Arsenic | $1.3\times10^{-15}$ | 16 nm |
| Antimony | $7.6\times10^{-16}$ | 12 nm |

Arsenic moves about a third as far as phosphorus in the same step, and the effect compounds through every later hot step in the flow. For a 20 nm deep extension, a 47 nm diffusion length from phosphorus would erase the structure, while arsenic keeps it. Those are intrinsic-diffusion numbers: at high concentration, and with implant damage present, real diffusion of boron and phosphorus is faster than this and can transiently be orders of magnitude faster (transient enhanced diffusion), so the real gap between arsenic and the others is usually larger than the table suggests.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The diffusion parameters are the commonly tabulated intrinsic values quoted from memory of textbook tables; different references differ, and at modern thermal budgets diffusion is dominated by point-defect effects. Use them for ranking, not for process design.

## Practical dopant selection checklist

When choosing a dopant for a region, run through the properties in this order:

1. **Type needed.** N or P decides the column.
2. **Required depth and abruptness.** Shallow and abrupt favors arsenic (N) or low-energy boron or indium retrograde profiles (P); deep wells favor phosphorus (N) or boron (P).
3. **Concentration.** The active density cannot exceed solid solubility, and ionization must be near complete, which excludes deep levels for room-temperature devices.
4. **Thermal budget downstream.** Anything placed early sees every later hot step.
5. **Contamination and compatibility.** Fabs segregate tools by dopant because cross-contamination changes lightly doped layers. Arsine, phosphine and diborane sources are highly toxic, which is a real handling constraint on how and where doping happens.

## Verifying the dopant and its activation

- **Type and resistivity:** a hot-probe test for type and a four-point probe for sheet resistance, as in [[q0065]].
- **Hall effect:** gives carrier density and mobility; comparing the carrier density against the intended dopant dose shows the activated fraction. A deep acceptor or a freeze-out shows up as a Hall density that changes with temperature.
- **Temperature-dependent Hall or resistivity:** sweeping 50 to 400 K and plotting carrier density against $1/T$ shows three regimes: freeze-out (steep slope set by the dopant ionization energy), the flat extrinsic plateau, and intrinsic rise. The slope in the freeze-out region is a direct measurement of the dopant's identity.
- **SIMS:** gives the chemical species and concentration versus depth, so you can check that the right element went in and see how far it diffused.
- **Deep-level transient spectroscopy (DLTS)** detects unwanted deep-level contaminants such as gold or iron at very low concentration.

::: applications
- **Phosphorus**: N-wells, phosphorus-doped polysilicon gates, power-device substrates made by neutron transmutation doping.
- **Arsenic**: shallow N+ source/drain and NPN emitters in CMOS and BiCMOS.
- **Antimony**: buried layers in bipolar and smart-power ICs.
- **Boron**: P-wells, P+ source/drain in PMOS, base of NPN transistors, resistors, and the dominant dopant in most solar wafers.
- **Gallium**: boron-free P-type solar wafers, and Ga in GaAs/GaN device layers.
- **Si and Mg in GaN, Si in GaAs, N and Al in SiC**: LEDs, laser diodes, RF HEMTs and SiC power devices.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Silicon host** unless stated. Compound semiconductors have different dopants because each element can occupy more than one sublattice.
- **Shallow, substitutional dopants** with room-temperature ionization. Deep dopants (In in Si, Mg in GaN) behave differently and give poor activation.
- **"Typically used"** means the standard production dopants, not every element that can dope silicon. Rarely used ones (Al, Ga, In, Sb) are listed for completeness.
- **Energy and solubility figures** are approximate and vary with source; the qualitative ordering is reliable.
- **Alternate answer**: if the interviewer only wants "boron and phosphorus", say that first, then add arsenic and the compound-semiconductor cases if time allows.

::: followups
### Why is arsenic preferred over phosphorus for shallow source/drain junctions?
Arsenic is much heavier and diffuses far more slowly, so after the anneal that activates it, the junction stays shallow and abrupt. Phosphorus diffuses further, which would lengthen the source/drain junctions into the channel, causing short-channel effects. Arsenic also reaches high active concentration (about $2\times10^{21}$ cm⁻³ solid solubility). Phosphorus is still used where deeper profiles are needed, such as lightly doped drain extensions or wells.

### Why is boron the standard P-type dopant even though it diffuses fast?
It's small enough to fit the lattice substitutionally, dissolves to high concentrations (a few $10^{20}$ cm⁻³), and is cheap and well characterised. Larger Group III atoms (Ga, In) either have poor solubility or deep levels, and aluminium diffuses through oxide. Engineers manage boron's fast diffusion with rapid-thermal and laser anneals and with low-energy implants.

### What happens if I dope silicon with a Group IV element like germanium or carbon?
Nothing electrically: they have the same four valence electrons, so they fit into the bonding without adding or removing a carrier. They do change other properties. Germanium alloyed into silicon strains the lattice and narrows the bandgap (SiGe), and carbon is used to limit boron diffusion and to create strain.

### Why do solar wafers sometimes use gallium instead of boron?
Boron-doped silicon wafers grown with oxygen can form boron-oxygen complexes under illumination, which lower carrier lifetime and cell efficiency (light-induced degradation). Gallium doping avoids this, at the cost of a harder crystal-growth process because gallium segregates strongly so resistivity varies along the ingot.

### Why is p-type GaN hard to make?
Magnesium is a deep acceptor (about 150-200 meV), so only a few percent of the atoms are ionized at room temperature even though you may have $10^{19}$-$10^{20}$ cm⁻³ Mg. Hydrogen also passivates the Mg, so an activation anneal is required. The result is a resistive p-layer, which is why GaN LEDs use thin p-layers and why many GaN power devices avoid p-type conduction altogether.

### Scenario: your N-type wafer measures far higher resistivity than the spec. What dopant-related things might have happened?
Possible causes: unintended compensation by an acceptor such as boron contamination; incomplete activation (anneal temperature too low or too short, so dopants are not substitutional); dopant lost by out-diffusion or segregation into oxide; or mis-set implant dose. I'd check sheet resistance across the wafer with a four-point probe, then use SIMS to compare total dopant to active carrier density from spreading resistance or Hall measurements: a gap between SIMS and carriers points to activation or compensation.
:::
