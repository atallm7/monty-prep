::: basics
### In plain words
Balance charging means charging a multi-cell pack so that every cell ends at the same voltage, not just so the total looks right. In a series pack, the same current flows through every cell, but the cells are not identical. One cell reaches 4.2 V first. A charger that only watches the total keeps pushing, and that cell goes over its limit.

### Picture it
Think of four friends splitting a restaurant bill. The total on the receipt can be exactly right while one person quietly paid far too much and another far too little. Checking only the total hides the problem. Balance charging is looking at what each person paid, and adjusting until each share is fair. For a battery, an unfair share means a cell pushed past its safe voltage.

### Words you need
- **Series pack**: cells connected end to end, so their voltages add ([[q0305]]).
- **Balance lead**: an extra connector with a wire to each cell junction, so the charger can read every cell.
- **Passive balancing**: bleeding extra charge from the fullest cells through a resistor ([[q0314]]).
- **Active balancing**: moving charge from fuller cells to emptier ones.
- **Cell spread**: the voltage difference between the highest and lowest cell.

### The simplest example
Take a 4S pack charged to 16.80 V, which is 4 times 4.20 V. The total can be right even when the cells are uneven: $4.35 + 4.20 + 4.15 + 4.10 = 16.80$ V. The first cell is 150 mV over its 4.20 V limit, although the pack voltage looks perfect. With a balance lead, the charger sees that cell and bleeds it. A 47 Ω bleed resistor at 4.2 V draws about 89 mA, so removing a 110 mAh excess (5% of a 2200 mAh cell) takes about 1.2 hours.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers weakest-cell capacity limits, the two balancing methods and the hardware a balance connector needs.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand **why a series pack cannot be treated as one big cell**. The hidden sub-skills:

1. You know series cells carry the *same current* but not the same *state of charge*, so the pack voltage hides individual cell voltages.
2. You can explain what balance charging physically does (passive bleeding or active charge transfer) and what hardware it needs (per-cell sense taps).
3. You know **when** it matters: 2S and larger series packs, mismatched or aged cells, and near the full-charge end, where it also protects against over-voltage on one cell.
:::

::: why
Every drone, e-bike, laptop and power-tool pack is several lithium cells in series, and the most common field failure is one weak or drifted cell. Without balance charging that cell hits 4.2 V first and ends the charge early, or worse, goes over-voltage while the pack total looks fine. For hobby LiPos it is also the reason chargers have a second "balance" plug. In an interview this question checks that you think at the *cell* level and not just the pack level.
:::

::: concepts
- **Series connection**: the same current flows through every cell, so charge added to each cell is identical, but their capacities and starting SoC differ. See [[q0001]].
- **Li-ion voltage limits**: each cell must stay at or below about 4.2 V (and above about 3.0 V). See [[q0305]].
- **CC/CV charging**: charging ends when the pack reaches its CV limit and current tapers. See [[q0321]].
- **Cell mismatch**: capacity, internal resistance and self-discharge vary cell to cell and drift with age. See [[q0301]].
- **Passive vs active balancing**: burn off the surplus in a resistor, or move it to another cell. See [[q0314]].
:::

"Balance charging means charging a series pack so that every cell ends up at the same voltage, not just the pack total at the right voltage. In a series string all cells get the same charge current, but they don't start at the same state of charge and they don't have exactly the same capacity. So one cell reaches 4.2 V before the others. If the charger only watches the pack voltage, it keeps going and pushes that cell past 4.2 V while the others are still lower. That damages the cell and is a safety risk.

With balance charging there's an extra connector with a tap to every cell node, so the charger or BMS can see each cell. When a cell gets to 4.2 V, it bleeds that cell through a small resistor, or in active schemes moves the energy to weaker cells, while the others keep charging until all are about 4.2 V.

You need it for any series pack, 2S and up, and especially for old or mismatched cells and for any pack you fast charge. A single cell, or cells that are only in parallel, don't need it because parallel cells equalize themselves."

::: technical
**The problem.** For $N$ series cells with open-circuit voltages $V_i$, the pack voltage is $\sum V_i$. A charger regulating the pack to $N \times 4.2$ V is satisfied when the *sum* is right. Individual cells can then sit anywhere, for example in a 4S pack at $16.80$ V:

$$4.35 + 4.20 + 4.15 + 4.10 = 16.80\ \text{V}$$

The first cell is 150 mV over the 4.2 V limit although the pack voltage looks perfect.

**Capacity limit.** In series, the usable capacity is that of the weakest cell: charge stops when it is full and discharge stops when it is empty. If one cell in a 2200 mAh pack is 5% weaker, usable capacity is about $2200 \times 0.95 = 2090$ mAh, and that cell cycles deeper than the others and ages faster. That is positive feedback in the wrong direction.

**Passive balancing.** A bleed resistor $R_b$ is switched across a cell when its voltage exceeds the balance threshold. Bleed current $I_b = V_{cell}/R_b$ and the resistor dissipates $V^2/R_b$. With $R_b = 47\ \Omega$ at 4.2 V: $I_b \approx 89$ mA, $P \approx 0.38$ W. To remove a 110 mAh excess takes $110/89 \approx 1.2$ h.

**Active balancing.** Energy is moved cell to cell with capacitors, inductors or transformers. It is more efficient and can balance during discharge but costs more parts and complexity.

**Hardware needed.** An $N$-cell pack needs $N+1$ sense nodes. Hobby LiPos bring this out as a JST-XH balance lead (for example 5 pins for a 4S pack) next to the high-current discharge lead.

**When it is needed.** For series packs, always at some level. In practice: at assembly (cells matched and brought to the same SoC first), periodically through life, and whenever cell-to-cell spread exceeds a threshold (typically tens of mV). Cells in *parallel* share one voltage and do not need balancing among themselves, only the series groups do.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Balance current, start threshold (often a few tens of mV below full) and recommended spread limits are charger- and BMS-specific. The numbers here are illustrative.
:::

Series cells get the same current but differ in capacity and starting charge, so one hits 4.2 V first. The charger uses a tap on each cell to see that, then bleeds the full cell through a resistor, or moves charge to weaker cells, while the rest catch up. ::: long
"Think of a 4S LiPo as four cells in a row. The same current goes through all four, but the cells aren't identical: slightly different capacity, internal resistance, self-discharge and starting charge. So when I charge the pack, one cell gets to 4.2 V before the others. If the charger only measures the total pack voltage, 16.8 V, it can't tell. I could have one cell at 4.35 V, which is over-voltage, and three others at 4.2, 4.15 and 4.1, and the total still adds up to 16.8. That one cell is stressed, can plate lithium or gas, and in the worst case goes into thermal runaway.

Balance charging solves this by monitoring each cell. That's why a LiPo has a balance connector with one more pin than cells. When the charger sees a cell reach about 4.2 V, it takes charge away from that cell. The simple way is passive: switch a bleed resistor across the cell, so the energy is burned as heat, maybe 50 to 500 milliamps. The fancy way is active: move charge to the weakest cells with an inductor or capacitor, which is more efficient.

The result is that all cells end the charge at the same voltage, you can use the full pack capacity, and no cell sees more than its limit. 

When do you need it? For any series pack, 2S and up, and more so when the cells are mismatched or old, or when you fast charge. A single cell, or cells in parallel, don't need it, because parallel cells share one voltage. And I'd add that balancing mostly works near the top of charge, because the voltage curve in the middle is too flat to tell cells apart."
:::

::: terms
- **Balance charging**: charging a series pack while equalizing individual cell voltages, using per-cell sense taps.
- **Balance lead / tap**: the extra connector wires to every cell node (N+1 wires for N cells).
- **Passive balancing**: dissipating the excess of the higher cells in a bleed resistor.
- **Active balancing**: transferring charge between cells with capacitors, inductors or transformers.
- **Cell mismatch**: differences in capacity, internal resistance, SoC or self-discharge across cells.
- **Top balancing / bottom balancing**: aligning cells at full (4.2 V) or at empty; top balancing is the usual approach for charging.
- **Over-voltage protection (OVP)**: per-cell cutoff when any cell exceeds its limit, a safety net independent of balancing.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it makes all the cells the same voltage" and "you need it for LiPos".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Shows the **failure case with numbers**: pack total 16.8 V yet one cell at 4.35 V.
- Explains *why* mismatch arises (capacity, resistance, self-discharge, temperature gradients, ageing) and why it grows without balancing.
- Distinguishes **passive vs active** balancing with typical currents and the thermal limit of passive bleeding.
- States the **hardware requirement** (N+1 taps) and where balancing is effective (the high-SoC knee of the curve, because mid-range voltage is flat).
- Notes that balancing is **not protection**: OVP/UVP per cell is separate, and that parallel cells need no balancing.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The charger just needs to hit 16.8 V."** For a 4S pack that only guarantees the *sum*. Individual cells can exceed 4.2 V.
- **"Balancing adds capacity."** It does not create energy. It lets you use the capacity of the weakest cell fully and keeps the others within limits.
- **Balancing from the pack terminals.** Without per-cell taps nothing can be balanced.
- **Thinking passive balancing fixes a bad cell.** A cell with much lower capacity or high internal resistance will keep drifting. Bleeding only trims small differences.
- **Balancing during the flat mid-curve region.** At 50% SoC the Li-ion voltage slope is so small that a few mV error means big SoC error, so voltage-based balancing is done near full.
- **Applying it to cells in parallel.** They are already forced to one voltage.
:::

::: deep
## Why the cells drift apart

In a series string, current continuity ($I_1 = I_2 = \dots = I_N$) means every cell receives the same number of coulombs during charge. That sounds like it keeps cells in step, but there are three drivers that move them apart:

1. **Different capacity** $Q_i$. The same charge $\Delta Q$ moves SoC by $\Delta Q/Q_i$, so the smaller cell swings further. If two cells with a 2% capacity gap start aligned at full, after a full-depth discharge the smaller one sits about 2% lower in SoC. This spread does not accumulate cycle after cycle on its own, but it means the cells only line up at one point (top or bottom) and the small cell always reaches the limits first.
2. **Different self-discharge.** A cell with higher leakage (or sitting next to a warm component) loses more charge between uses. This is the driver that genuinely *accumulates*: a 1 mAh/day difference becomes 30 mAh of offset after a month.
3. **Different internal resistance.** During CC charge, the terminal voltage is $V_{OCV} + I R_i$. A higher $R_i$ cell *reaches 4.2 V terminal earlier* even if its SoC is lower. Charge ends sooner for it.

Over time, cells with less capacity are cycled deeper and age faster, which makes the mismatch worse. That is the self-reinforcing part.

Figure: Each cell has its own resistor and switch (a MOSFET inside the balancer IC). Cell 1 is at the threshold, so S1 is closed and burns its surplus. Cell 2 is lower, so S2 stays open and keeps charging.

Each cell's bleed circuit must handle the heat. For $R_b = 47\ \Omega$: $I_b = 4.2/47 = 89$ mA and $P = V^2/R = 0.375$ W per cell, a lot for a pack with several cells on a small PCB. This is why passive balancing currents in integrated BMS ICs are often modest (tens of mA, with an external FET and resistor for more), and why passive balancing is slow:

$$t_{bal} = \frac{\Delta Q}{I_b} = \frac{110\ \text{mAh}}{89\ \text{mA}} \approx 1.2\ \text{h}$$

for a 110 mAh surplus (5% of 2200 mAh). A pack that is badly unbalanced can take several cycles to recover, which is why a balance charger often spends a long time at "4.2 V, current near zero" doing the last bit.

## How a balance charger sequences it

1. **CC phase.** Charge at constant current, e.g. 1C, monitoring every cell tap. Many chargers *start* balancing only when any cell reaches about 4.1 to 4.15 V.
2. **Balance + CV phase.** As the highest cell reaches 4.2 V, pack current is reduced (the CV phase effectively regulates the highest cell), and bleeders turn on for the high cells while the others continue charging.
3. **Termination.** When all cells are within a few mV (commonly 10 to 20 mV) and the current has tapered, stop.

Because Li-ion's OCV curve is steep only near full and near empty, voltage-based balancing is effective in the top 10 to 20% of charge and cannot resolve SoC in the mid range. Gauge ICs that balance by SoC use more sophisticated models.

## Where active balancing is better

For large, high-current packs (EVs, energy storage) passive bleeding at tens of mA cannot keep up with the mismatch from aged cells. Active schemes use a capacitor or inductor to ferry charge from the highest cell to the lowest, at efficiencies often above 80 to 90% depending on topology. They can also work during discharge, extending runtime when the weakest cell would otherwise cut off the pack. The cost is parts, board area and design complexity.

## When balance charging is *not* needed

- **1S packs.** No other cell to be unbalanced against.
- **Parallel groups.** Cells wired in parallel share the same voltage, so they equalize directly (watch for large inrush when connecting cells at different voltages).
- **Short-life or disposable series packs** with matched new cells, where cost trumps lifetime. Even there, an over-voltage per-cell cutoff is wise.
:::

::: applications
- **Hobby LiPo chargers (drones, RC cars)**: balance lead plus a multi-cell charger that bleeds the high cells while charging.
- **Laptop and e-bike packs**: a BMS IC with integrated passive balance FETs and sometimes external bleed resistors.
- **Power-tool packs**: series cells of the same chemistry with balance at the BMS board.
- **EVs and stationary storage**: active or hybrid balancing across hundreds of cells per pack, because passive bleed alone is too slow.
- **Pack assembly**: new cells are matched by capacity and brought to the same voltage before wiring in series.
:::

::: assumptions
- The pack is made of **identical Li-ion/LiPo cells in series** with a 4.2 V full-charge limit; LiFePO₄ cells have a flatter curve (about 3.65 V max) and make voltage-based balancing harder.
- The balance current, resistor value and thresholds are **illustrative**; real BMS ICs differ.
- Cells are assumed healthy. If one cell has failed (high resistance, very low capacity), balancing will not rescue it; replace the pack.
- If an interviewer says "we use a *parallel* pack of cells", answer that balance charging between them is automatic; you may still need series group balancing if there are series stages.
:::

::: followups
### Why can't you just charge each cell separately?
You can, and some systems do (per-cell chargers, or isolated charging channels), but it needs N independent chargers and N separate connections to the pack. A balance charger is a compromise: one main charger delivers the pack current, and a small bleeder per cell trims differences. It is cheaper but slower and wastes the bled energy.

### How do you know if a pack needs balancing?
Measure each cell at rest after a charge. A spread of more than 50 mV between cells at full charge is a red flag, and 100 mV or more suggests a weak cell. Also look at how the pack voltage behaves under load: if it sags to the low-voltage cutoff early, one cell may be reaching empty first.

### Why does balancing mostly happen near full charge?
The Li-ion OCV curve is nearly flat between about 20% and 80% SoC (a few tens of mV over a big charge range), so a 10 mV measurement error is a large SoC error. Near full the slope rises sharply, so a 20 mV difference clearly means a charge difference. So balancing is triggered near the top.

### A 4S pack of 5000 mAh cells has a 200 mAh imbalance. The BMS bleeds at 50 mA. How long will it take?
$t = 200\ \text{mAh}/50\ \text{mA} = 4$ h of balancing at full voltage. Since the bleed happens only while the cell sits above the threshold, it might take several charge cycles. A larger bleed current produces more heat in the PCB, so a faster balancer needs thermal design or an active scheme.

### What if you connect a fully charged cell in parallel with a depleted one?
Large equalizing current flows: $I = \Delta V / (R_1 + R_2)$. For 4.2 V vs 3.0 V and $R_{int} = 50\ \text{m}\Omega$ each, $I = 1.2/0.1 = 12$ A. That can damage connectors and cells, so parallel connections are made only after matching voltages (within about 0.1 V) or through current limiting.

### Does the balance connector carry power?
No. The balance taps are thin sense wires with small currents (the bleed current is typically hundreds of milliamps at most), so never use them as the main charge or discharge path. Connecting the balance plug in the wrong order or orientation can short cells through the thin wires.
:::
