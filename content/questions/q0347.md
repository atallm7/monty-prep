::: testing
Whether you can compare two inductive electromechanical loads on the right axes: what kind of motion each produces (continuous rotation vs a short, one-shot stroke), how force or torque depends on current and position, what the electrical behaviour looks like (back-EMF vs changing inductance), and what that means for drive circuits (flyback diode, peak-and-hold, duty rating). The sign of a strong answer is that you give the *common* ground (both are coils making a magnetic force and both need flyback protection) before the differences.
:::

::: why
Hardware interns routinely drive both: a fan or pump motor on one pin and a valve, lock or latch solenoid on the next. They need different drive strategies. A solenoid held on at full voltage can overheat in minutes; a motor held stalled will too, but a motor naturally reduces its current as it spins. Picking the wrong actuator (a solenoid where you need long travel, a motor where you need a fast, simple, one-shot push) or the wrong drive (no flyback diode, no peak-and-hold) is a common design error.
:::

::: concepts
- **Electromagnet / coil**: current in a winding produces a magnetic field, $\mathcal{F} = NI$ (ampere-turns). See [[q0335]].
- **Lorentz force vs reluctance force**: a motor's torque comes from current-carrying conductors in a magnet's field ($F = BIL$); a solenoid pulls a ferromagnetic plunger into a gap to minimise reluctance ($F = \tfrac12 i^2\,dL/dx$).
- **Back-EMF**: a motor generates $K_e\omega$; a solenoid generates a motional EMF $i\,dL/dx\cdot v$ only while the plunger moves. See [[q0341]].
- **RL load and flyback**: both are inductors and kick when switched off. See [[q0340]].
- **Duty cycle and holding current**: a solenoid held energised dissipates $I^2R$ continuously.
- **Mechanical transmission**: rotary-to-linear conversion (lead screws, cams) versus direct linear force.
:::

::: answer
"Both are coils that turn electrical energy into mechanical motion using magnetic force, and both are inductive loads, so both kick back when you switch them off and need a flyback diode. The difference is the kind of motion and how they behave electrically.

A motor makes continuous rotation. The torque comes from current in the windings sitting in a magnetic field, so torque is proportional to current, and as it spins it generates a back-EMF that limits the current. You get a smooth torque-speed curve and can run it forever.

A solenoid is a coil with a movable iron plunger, and it gives a short linear stroke. Energise it and the plunger gets pulled in to close the magnetic gap. The force is roughly proportional to current squared and it's strongest when the gap is smallest, falling off quickly as the gap grows. There's no continuous rotation or speed regulation: it's basically on or off, a push or a latch.

Electrically, a motor draws a big inrush that decays as it speeds up. A solenoid draws a pull-in current, then once the plunger seats, its inductance jumps and the current settles to $V/R$. If you hold it on, it just burns $I^2R$ as heat, so you often use peak-and-hold PWM to drop the holding current. So I'd use a motor for continuous motion and a solenoid for short, fast, simple on/off actuation like valves, locks and latches."
:::

::: technical
**Motion and force law.**
- Brushed DC motor: torque $T = K_t I$ (independent of rotor angle on average), speed set by $V - IR = K_e\omega$. Continuous rotation, speed controllable.
- Solenoid (reluctance actuator): force $F = \tfrac12 i^2\,\frac{dL}{dx}$. In the unsaturated regime with air-gap $g$ dominating, $L \approx \mu_0N^2A/g$ and
$$F \approx \frac{\mu_0 (Ni)^2 A}{2g^2}.$$
Force rises steeply as the gap closes until the iron saturates ($F_{max} \approx B_{sat}^2A/2\mu_0$ per gap). Stroke is typically millimetres to a few centimetres.

**Electrical behaviour.**
- Motor: $L\,di/dt = V - iR - K_e\omega$; inrush $V/R$ at standstill, running current $\approx (T_{load}/K_t)$.
- Solenoid: $L(x)$ changes with plunger position; $v = \frac{d(L i)}{dt} + iR$. During motion the term $i\,\frac{dL}{dx}v$ acts like back-EMF and causes a current dip; once seated $L$ is at its maximum and the current rises to $V/R$.

**Example solenoid** ($N=500$, plunger area 1 cm², 12 V coil of 24 Ω): $I = 0.5$ A, power 6 W. At a 1 mm gap, $F = 3.9$ N; at 5 mm it is 0.16 N (25 times lower since $F\propto 1/g^2$). Seated, $L \approx 31$ mH at 1 mm of residual gap in this idealisation.

**Drive.** Both need a flyback path. A solenoid is often driven by **peak-and-hold**: full voltage for ~tens of ms until it pulls in, then PWM at ~20–40% to hold. With 25% duty, current falls to 0.125 A and power to 0.375 W (16× less) because $P = I^2R$.

**Heat and duty.** A motor's winding is cooled by rotation (fan, airflow) and its current falls when running; a continuously-energised solenoid has no such relief, so "duty cycle" ratings are standard for solenoids.
:::

::: short
"Both are coils that turn current into mechanical force and both are inductive loads that need flyback protection. A solenoid pulls an iron plunger in over a short linear stroke: force goes roughly as current squared and rises sharply as the gap closes. Motors for continuous motion, solenoids for short on/off pushes like valves and latches. Holding a solenoid on costs $I^2R$ continuously, so we use peak-and-hold PWM."
:::

::: long
"They share a lot, so I'll start there. Each is a coil that turns electrical energy into mechanical force through a magnetic field. Each is an inductive load, which means current can't change instantly, the inrush is set by $V/R$ and the time constant $L/R$, and when you switch it off the inductor kicks, so you need a flyback diode or other clamp.

The differences are the kind of motion and the way force depends on position and current.

A motor makes continuous rotation. In a brushed DC motor the windings carry current in a permanent magnet's field, torque is $K_t I$, and as it spins it generates a back-EMF $K_e\omega$ that opposes the supply. So the current is high at start and settles to whatever the load torque needs. Speed is controlled with voltage or PWM, and you can run it as long as the thermal limits allow.

A solenoid is a coil with a plunger. Energise it and the plunger is pulled into the coil to close the magnetic gap. The force is a reluctance force, $\tfrac12 i^2\,dL/dx$, so it goes as current squared and rises sharply as the gap closes, maybe 25 times larger at 1 millimetre than at 5. The stroke is short, there's no speed control and it is basically on or off. While it moves, the changing inductance makes the current dip, and once it's seated the current settles at $V/R$.

That has a drive consequence. A solenoid left on is just a heater, 6 watts in a 12 volt, 24 ohm coil. Since holding needs far less magnetic force than pulling in, I'd use peak-and-hold: full voltage for tens of milliseconds, then 25% PWM, which cuts holding power 16-fold.

So: motors for continuous or controlled motion, solenoids for short strokes, latches, valves and locks."
:::

::: terms
- **Solenoid**: a coil with a movable ferromagnetic plunger (armature) that produces a linear pull or push.
- **Reluctance force**: force that moves an iron part to reduce the magnetic circuit's reluctance, $F = \tfrac12 i^2\,dL/dx$.
- **Lorentz force**: force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field, $F = BIL$; the basis of motor torque.
- **Stroke**: the distance a solenoid plunger travels.
- **Pull-in / holding current**: current needed to start the plunger moving vs to keep it seated.
- **Peak-and-hold**: a drive scheme with high initial current then reduced holding current.
- **Duty cycle (solenoid rating)**: fraction of time the coil may be on without overheating.
- **Back-EMF**: voltage opposing the supply from motion in the field. See [[q0341]].
- **Flyback diode**: clamp across an inductive load to carry its current when the switch opens. See [[q0340]].
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "a motor rotates, a solenoid moves in a straight line". An exceptional answer:

- Opens with the **commonalities**: both coils, both inductive, both need flyback protection, both have inrush.
- Distinguishes the force laws: $T = K_tI$ (linear in $I$, flat over angle) vs $F\propto I^2/g^2$ (position dependent).
- Mentions the mechanical consequence: no speed control, short stroke, bang-bang behaviour; motors need commutation or an electronic driver.
- Explains the thermal difference and peak-and-hold with numbers.
- Notes that the electrical signature differs (back-EMF vs a plunger-motion current dip) and how to detect plunger arrival or a stall.
- Acknowledges overlap: voice coils and linear motors blur the line ([[q0350]]), and a relay is a solenoid with contacts.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"A solenoid is just a linear motor."** It's a limited-stroke, force-versus-position reluctance actuator with a strongly nonlinear force curve; linear motors have roughly constant force along the travel ([[q0350]]).
- **Assuming force is proportional to current.** For a solenoid it goes as $i^2$ (unsaturated) and as $1/g^2$.
- **Leaving a solenoid energised at full voltage** and cooking it. Check the duty rating or use peak-and-hold.
- **Believing a solenoid has no back-EMF.** It has no *speed-proportional* EMF like a motor, but moving the plunger changes $L$ and generates a motional EMF, visible as the current dip.
- **Skipping the flyback diode on a "simple" solenoid.** It kicks just like a motor; a 12 V, 31 mH coil at 0.5 A stores $\tfrac12 LI^2 = 3.9$ mJ and will spike the switch.
- **Using a solenoid for long travel or precise positioning.** Force collapses with distance, and there's no proportional control without extra effort.
:::

::: deep
## What they share

Strip away the iron and moving parts and both devices are **a coil of wire**. Current in the coil makes a magnetic field, the field interacts with a ferromagnetic or magnetic part, and the resulting force moves something. Electrically each looks like a resistor in series with an inductor (plus a motion-dependent voltage):
$$v = iR + \frac{d(L i)}{dt} + e_{motion}.$$

That has four consequences common to both:

1. **Inrush**: current starts at 0 and rises with $L/R$ toward $V/R$ ([[q0345]]).
2. **Inductive kick**: opening the switch forces $v = L\,di/dt$ to large values; a clamp is needed ([[q0340]]).
3. **Heating**: the copper dissipates $I^2R$ whenever current flows.
4. **Low-side or high-side switch** driving a coil with a flyback diode is the standard circuit.

## Force law 1: motor torque from Lorentz force

Take a rotor conductor of length $L_c$ carrying current $I$ in magnetic flux density $B$. The force is $F = BIL_c$, perpendicular to both. Summed over all conductors at radius $r$ with the commutator keeping the current direction right, torque becomes $T = K_t I$ with $K_t$ constant over a revolution (apart from small ripple, see [[q0357]]). Power: $P_m = T\omega$. The same $BL$ gives back-EMF $K_e\omega$ ([[q0346]]).

Torque is **linear** in current, **independent of position** (on average) and available at **any speed**, with the speed regulated by back-EMF.

## Force law 2: solenoid force from reluctance

A solenoid's magnetic circuit is iron (yoke and plunger) plus an air gap $g$. The reluctance is dominated by the gap: $\mathcal{R} \approx g/(\mu_0A)$, so inductance is $L = N^2/\mathcal{R} = \mu_0N^2A/g$. The magnetic co-energy is $\tfrac12 L i^2$; the force along $x$ (gap decreasing) is
$$F = \tfrac12 i^2\frac{dL}{dx} \approx \frac{\mu_0 (Ni)^2A}{2g^2}.$$

For $N = 500$, $A = 1\ \text{cm}^2$, $i = 0.5$ A: $F = 15.7$ N at 0.5 mm, 3.9 N at 1 mm, 0.98 N at 2 mm, 0.16 N at 5 mm. The iron saturates when $B = \mu_0Ni/g$ reaches ≈1.5 T (here at $g \approx 0.2$ mm), which caps the force at roughly $B_{sat}^2A/2\mu_0 \approx 90$ N per gap in the idealisation.

This is an idealised model; real force-stroke curves (cone-shaped pole pieces flatten them) come from the datasheet.

Contrast with the motor: its torque is flat vs angle, so you can drive a lead-screw through many metres of travel.

## Electrical signature: back-EMF vs inductance step

In a motor the opposing voltage is $K_e\omega$: smooth, speed proportional. In a solenoid, starting with the plunger far out ($L$ small), the current rises quickly. When the plunger accelerates inwards, $\frac{dL}{dx}v\,i$ opposes the supply and the current dips. When the plunger seats, $L$ is large and constant, so the current rises again toward $V/R$ with a slower time constant $L_{seated}/R$.

For our example, $L_{seated} \approx 31$ mH and $R = 24\ \Omega$ gives $\tau = 1.3$ ms. The current-dip timing is a handy sensor: some drivers detect plunger arrival from the waveform.

## Thermal and duty-cycle differences

A running motor's current falls to $T_{load}/K_t$ and the rotor often moves air. A solenoid at full voltage dissipates $V^2/R = 6$ W continuously in a small coil, so its temperature rises until the insulation fails. Solenoid datasheets give **duty cycle** limits (e.g. "intermittent 25%, ED") and a maximum on-time at rated voltage; continuous-duty solenoids use more copper or lower voltage.

**Peak-and-hold** exploits the fact that once the gap is closed, the force from the same current is much larger (because $1/g^2$), so you can cut the current drastically. Example: 12 V, 24 Ω coil. Pull-in current 0.5 A, 6 W. Hold at 25% PWM duty: $I = 0.25\times12/24 = 0.125$ A, $P = I^2R = 0.375$ W, 16 times less. The magnetic force is $\propto(Ni)^2$ so the hold force at the closed gap should still be adequate: check the datasheet's minimum holding ampere-turns, and leave a margin so vibration doesn't release the plunger.

## When to use which

| Need | Choose |
|---|---|
| Continuous rotation, speed control | Motor |
| Short, fast linear push/pull, latch, valve | Solenoid |
| Proportional force over a long stroke | Linear motor / voice coil ([[q0350]]) |
| Holding without power | Latching solenoid or a motor with a worm gear |
| Smooth precise positioning | Servo/stepper |

Hybrid devices blur the line: a rotary solenoid, a relay (solenoid + contacts), a voice coil (Lorentz force with limited stroke) and a stepper (which is a motor stopped at defined positions).
:::

::: applications
- **Solenoid valves** in washing machines, irrigation, pneumatics and fuel injectors: short stroke, on/off, often with peak-and-hold drive.
- **Door locks and latches** (electronic locks, car door actuators, vending machines).
- **Relays and contactors**: a solenoid moving contacts.
- **Printers and old dot-matrix heads, pinball flippers**: fast short-stroke actuators.
- **Brushed DC motors** in toys, fans, pumps, power-window motors and robot wheels, usually through a gearbox.
- **Camera shutters and autofocus**: voice coils or small solenoids for linear motion; motors with lead screws for longer travel.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Brushed DC motor** as "the motor" since the section is about brushed motors. AC induction, BLDC and stepper motors are covered in later questions, but the rotary-vs-linear and force-law contrast still holds.
- **Unsaturated, ideal magnetic circuit** for the solenoid force formula: real solenoids have iron reluctance, fringing, leakage flux and shaped pole pieces that flatten the curve.
- **Linear motor model** for the electrical behaviour; real motors have brush drops and nonlinearities.
- **Constant supply voltage** and room-temperature copper; resistance increases about 0.4% per °C, so a warm solenoid pulls with less current and force.
- Alternative framings: some people compare *"motor vs. solenoid"* as *rotary vs. linear actuators* or *proportional vs. on/off*; these are equivalent views of the same difference.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a solenoid's current dip as it pulls in?
Because moving the plunger changes the inductance. Voltage across the coil is $d(\lambda)/dt = L\,di/dt + i\,\frac{dL}{dx}v$. While the plunger moves inward ($v>0$, $dL/dx>0$) the second term eats part of the supply voltage, so $di/dt$ reduces and can go negative. When the plunger stops, the term vanishes and the current rises again. It's the solenoid analogue of motor back-EMF.

### How would you drive a 12 V, 24 Ω solenoid from a 3.3 V MCU?
A logic-level low-side N-MOSFET (rated for at least 20–30 V and 1 A at $V_{GS}$ = 3.3 V), a gate resistor, a pulldown on the gate, and a flyback diode across the coil. For peak-and-hold, drive the gate with a PWM output: 100% for 50 ms, then ~25%. Add a bulk capacitor near the load for the pull-in surge.

### What is peak-and-hold and how do you pick the numbers?
Full voltage until the plunger seats (measure or use the datasheet's pull-in time, with margin), then PWM at a duty that gives holding ampere-turns with 1.5–2× margin above the datasheet's hold requirement. Make the PWM frequency above audible range (>20 kHz) or accept a buzz, and fast enough that the ripple doesn't release the plunger.

### Can I use a solenoid as a position-proportional actuator?
Roughly, with a spring and closed-loop control, as in proportional valves. The force-position curve is nonlinear and current-dependent, so they are driven with a current loop plus a dither signal to overcome stiction. It's much more awkward than a voice coil or linear motor, where force is proportional to current over the stroke.

### What if you hold a motor stalled? Is that like holding a solenoid on?
Electrically yes, it's the $V/R$ case. A stalled motor draws $V/R$ with zero mechanical output, all $I^2R$ heat. A solenoid at the end of its stroke also has zero mechanical output. The difference is the design intent: solenoids are specified for it (duty cycle, hold current), while most motors are not.

### Why is there a "kick" when a solenoid turns off, and how big is it?
$v = L\,di/dt$. Without a clamp the switch opens, current has to go somewhere and the voltage across the coil rises until a path forms (the MOSFET's avalanche or arc). With a flyback diode the clamp is about 0.7 V above the supply, and the current decays with time constant $L/R_{loop}$.
:::

::: interpretations
"Solenoid" in this list is used for the generic linear electromagnetic actuator (coil with plunger), as in neighbouring questions [[q0333]] and [[q0334]]. Some people use "solenoid" for any coil, so the interviewer's focus is on the actuator, not the bare inductor. Relay coils, rotary solenoids and voice coils are close relatives; mention them briefly.
:::
