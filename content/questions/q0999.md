::: basics
### In plain words
A probe is part of the circuit while you measure. Its input impedance, how strongly it resists the signal at each frequency, decides how much it disturbs the node. For general measurements you want the probe's impedance much higher than the node's own source impedance, about ten times or more, at every frequency that matters. Capacitance makes that harder as frequency rises.

### Picture it
A huge thermometer would cool a cup of tea and read falsely. A probe must be electrically "small". At low frequency, resistance counts. At high frequency, capacitance takes over, and a probe that looked tiny becomes a big load. For 50 Ω cables the rule flips, as the example shows.

### Words you need
- **Impedance**: the opposition a circuit gives to a signal at a given frequency, in ohms.
- **Source impedance**: the impedance the node being measured drives through. Higher means easier to disturb.
- **Capacitive reactance**: a capacitor's opposition, $1/(2\pi f C)$. It falls as frequency rises.
- **10x probe**: about 10 MΩ in parallel with 10 to 15 pF. See [[q0997]].

### The simplest example
A node has a 1 kΩ source impedance. A 10x probe's 10 MΩ is far higher, so at DC the probe is harmless. At 100 MHz, 12 pF has a reactance of $1/(2\pi \times 10^8 \times 12\times10^{-12}) \approx 133\ \Omega$. That is lower than the node's 1 kΩ, so the probe loads it badly.

The 1 kΩ source and the probe capacitance form a filter that starts cutting signals above about 13 MHz, so fast edges look rounded. An active probe with about 1 pF moves that point to about 160 MHz.

For a 50 Ω cable carrying fast signals (a transmission line), the goal is different: you want a 50 Ω load at the end so no echo, or reflection, bounces back.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the impedance-versus-frequency picture and a bias node that reads wrong.
:::

::: testing
Whether you treat the probe as **part of the circuit**, not as a perfect observer. The expected answer: the probe's input impedance should be much higher than the source impedance at every frequency of interest, so it does not load the node; a typical scope and 10x probe are about 1 MΩ/10 MΩ in parallel with 10-15 pF; and at high frequency the capacitance, not the resistance, sets the impedance. The twist is "should it be high or low?" For general measurement high; for 50 Ω transmission-line signals the scope's 50 Ω input is the right one. The interviewer also looks for a rule of thumb (10x or more) and numbers.
:::

::: why
Every measurement changes what it measures. A probe that loads the node can lower the amplitude, slow an edge, shift an oscillator, even stop a circuit working. If you do not think of probe impedance you will trust a bad waveform, or worse, "fix" a problem that only exists because of the probe. The same loading ideas ([[q0148]], [[q0008]]) apply to voltage dividers, ADC inputs and sensors, so it is also a good test of general engineering reasoning.
:::

::: concepts
- **Loading as a voltage divider**: source impedance and probe impedance form a divider, $V_{meas}=V_{src}\dfrac{Z_{in}}{Z_s+Z_{in}}$. See [[q0008]] and [[q0148]].
- **Capacitor impedance**: $|Z_C|=1/(2\pi fC)$ falls with frequency. See [[q0022]].
- **10x probe**: 10 MΩ and about 10-15 pF. See [[q0997]].
- **1 MΩ vs 50 Ω scope inputs**: two input modes for different source environments ([[q1000]]).
- **Source impedance**: the Thevenin impedance looking back into the node you measure.
:::

::: answer
"The probe input impedance should be as high as possible compared with the impedance of the node I'm measuring, so it doesn't load it. A good rule of thumb is at least ten times higher than the source impedance, at every frequency that matters.

For a standard 10x passive probe, that's about 10 megaohms in parallel with 10 to 15 picofarads. A 1x probe or the scope's raw input is 1 megaohm and a bit more capacitance, perhaps 100 pF with the cable. The key is that the capacitor wins at high frequency: at 100 MHz, 12 picofarads is only 130 ohms, so the probe isn't a high impedance at all any more. That's why for a node with 1 kilohm source impedance, a 10x probe starts to attenuate and round the signal above roughly 10 MHz.

So for general measurements I want high resistance and low capacitance, which is what 10x and active probes give. The exception is a 50 ohm system, like a transmission line or RF measurement. There the scope's 50 ohm input is correct, because it terminates the line properly and doesn't create a reflection, though it loads any high-impedance node badly. In practice I pick the probe by comparing its impedance at the signal's highest frequency with the source impedance."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** $Z_{in}(j\omega)=R_{in}\parallel\dfrac{1}{j\omega C_{in}}$. Typical values: scope input 1 MΩ ∥ about 10-20 pF; 1x probe 1 MΩ ∥ ~100 pF (cable included); 10x passive 10 MΩ ∥ ~10-15 pF; active probe about 1 MΩ ∥ ~1 pF or less; 50 Ω input is purely about 50 Ω (resistive, with small parasitics), with limited maximum input voltage (typically about 5 V rms, check the scope's rating).

**Loaded signal.** With source $Z_s=R_s$ and a probe $R_{in}\parallel C_{in}$:
$$\frac{V_{meas}}{V_{src}}=\frac{1}{1+R_s/R_{in}+j\omega R_sC_{in}}$$
Corner (for $R_{in}\gg R_s$): $f_{3dB}=\dfrac{1}{2\pi R_sC_{in}}$. DC error: $R_s/(R_s+R_{in})$ (e.g. 100 kΩ source into 10 MΩ: 0.99%).

**Rule of thumb.** $|Z_{in}(f_{max})|\ge 10\,|Z_s|$ keeps the amplitude error under about 10% (about 0.8 dB) even for a purely resistive load, and much less when the probe is mainly capacitive. Use $f_{max}\approx0.35/t_r$ for edges.

**Numbers for a 1 kΩ source.** 10x (10 MΩ ∥ 12 pF): −3 dB near 13 MHz; 1x (1 MΩ ∥ 115 pF): near 1.4 MHz; 1 pF active: about 160 MHz. For a 100 kΩ source the 10x corner falls to 130 kHz.

**Why not always make it as high as possible?** High-impedance probes pick up noise and have limited bandwidth because of capacitance; a 50 Ω path gives the widest bandwidth and clean reflections for transmission lines, and an active probe's high impedance comes with limited range and noise ([[q0996]]).
:::

::: long
"The probe's input impedance should be high compared with the impedance of the circuit node I'm measuring, so that it doesn't draw significant current or change the waveform. As a rule of thumb, ten times higher at the highest frequency that matters.

The numbers: a standard 10x passive probe is about 10 megaohms in parallel with 10 to 15 picofarads. A 1x probe, or the scope's input with the cable, is about 1 megaohm with something like 100 picofarads. Those look like big resistances, but the capacitance dominates beyond a few kilohertz. At 10 MHz, 100 picofarads is 160 ohms; at 100 MHz, 12 picofarads is 130 ohms.

So the question to ask is what is the node's source impedance? For a 1 kilohm node, the probe's 12 picofarads makes an RC pole near 13 megahertz, so the displayed signal rolls off above that, and fast edges are rounded. A 1x probe on the same node would be down at about 1.4 megahertz. A 100 kilohm node is much worse: even the 10x is only good up to about 130 kilohertz. For high-impedance nodes I'd use an active probe with around a picofarad, or probe a buffered point.

On the other hand, when the circuit is a 50 ohm transmission line, like a coax or controlled-impedance trace, I want the instrument to be 50 ohms. A 1 megaohm input would leave the end of the line unterminated and cause reflections. So I switch the scope's input to 50 ohms, or use a probe designed for 50 ohm systems. Just remember its drawbacks: it loads high-impedance circuits heavily and has a maximum voltage rating of only about 5 volts rms."
:::

::: terms
- **Input impedance ($Z_{in}$)**: the impedance the probe plus scope presents at the tip, as a function of frequency.
- **Input resistance / capacitance ($R_{in}$, $C_{in}$)**: the low-frequency and high-frequency components of the load.
- **Source impedance ($Z_s$)**: the Thevenin impedance of the node being measured.
- **Loading**: the error caused by the probe drawing current from the source.
- **1 MΩ input**: the default high-impedance scope input mode.
- **50 Ω input**: scope input mode terminating a 50 Ω line; low voltage limits.
- **Pole frequency**: $f=1/(2\pi R_sC_{in})$ where the probe starts to attenuate.
- **Reflection**: wave bounced back from an impedance mismatch at the end of a line.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "high, so it doesn't load the circuit."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives a quantitative rule (at least 10x the source impedance at the highest frequency of interest) and the divider formula.
- States typical values (10 MΩ ∥ 10-15 pF for 10x; 1 MΩ ∥ ~100 pF for 1x) and shows capacitance dominates: 12 pF = 133 Ω at 100 MHz.
- Works an example: 1 kΩ source, 12 pF gives a pole near 13 MHz.
- Brings up the exception: the 50 Ω input for transmission lines, and why it's right there and wrong elsewhere ([[q1000]]).
- Mentions that you can make the probe *seem* to disappear with an active probe or by probing a buffered point.
- Notes that probe loading can change circuit behavior, not just the displayed waveform.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Quoting only the resistance** ("it's 10 MΩ, so it's fine"). At high frequency the capacitance sets the impedance.
- **Probing a high-impedance node (hundreds of kΩ or MΩ) with a 1x probe.** The 1 MΩ forms a divider with the node's resistance and halves the signal at DC, and the 100 pF kills bandwidth.
- **Using the 50 Ω input on a high-impedance circuit.** A 1 kΩ source into 50 Ω gives a 21x attenuation (about −26 dB), may overload the driver and the input has a low voltage limit.
- **Using a 1 MΩ input on a long 50 Ω line** and seeing reflections, ringing or wrong amplitudes because the line is not terminated.
- **Forgetting the DC divider error** with very high source impedance: 1 MΩ source into 1 MΩ halves the voltage.
- **Thinking probing is passive and harmless.** A probe can shift a crystal oscillator, stop a PLL or change a comparator's timing.
:::

::: deep
## A probe is a load, so use the divider

Whatever the probe does, from the circuit's point of view it is a two-terminal impedance placed on the node. The node has a Thevenin source $V_{src}$ with impedance $Z_s$. With the probe attached:

$$V_{meas}=V_{src}\frac{Z_{in}}{Z_s+Z_{in}}$$

This is exactly the loaded divider of [[q0008]]: the error is $Z_s/(Z_s+Z_{in})$ when $Z_{in}\gg Z_s$. With both impedances complex, you must compute the magnitude and phase, because a capacitive $Z_{in}$ also makes the measurement a low-pass filter.

For a probe of $R_{in}\parallel C_{in}$ driven from a resistive $R_s$:

$$\frac{V_{meas}}{V_{src}}=\frac{1}{1+R_s/R_{in}+j\omega R_sC_{in}}$$

At low frequency this reduces to $R_{in}/(R_s+R_{in})$, a DC loading error. At higher frequency it becomes a low-pass with corner $1/(2\pi R_sC_{in})$.

## Impedance of the probe versus frequency

The probe impedance magnitude is flat at $R_{in}$ until $f=1/(2\pi R_{in}C_{in})$ (about 1.3 kHz for 10 MΩ ∥ 12 pF) and then falls at 20 dB/decade, reaching $1/(2\pi fC)$: 13 kΩ at 1 MHz, 1.3 kΩ at 10 MHz, 133 Ω at 100 MHz. That frequency dependence is why the same probe can be "fine" for a 1 MHz signal and a bad choice for the 100 MHz harmonic content of its fast edges. If the edge has $t_r=1$ ns, the relevant frequency is $0.35/t_r=350$ MHz, where 12 pF is about 38 Ω.

## Worked example: a bias node that "reads wrong"

A transistor base is biased from two 470 kΩ resistors (one to the supply, one to ground), so the Thevenin source impedance at the base is $470\text{ k}\Omega\parallel470\text{ k}\Omega=235$ kΩ. You probe it with three options:

- **1x probe** (1 MΩ ∥ 115 pF): the DC divider error is $235/(235+1000)=19\%$. The voltage drops by almost a fifth, and the base current may change enough to shift the bias point. The AC corner is $1/(2\pi\cdot235\text{k}\cdot115\text{ pF})\approx5.9$ kHz.
- **10x probe** (10 MΩ ∥ 12 pF): the DC error is $235/(235+10\,000)=2.3\%$. The AC corner is $1/(2\pi\cdot235\text{k}\cdot12\text{ pF})\approx56$ kHz. A DC bias reading is fine; a 100 kHz signal at the same node is not.
- **Active probe** (1 MΩ ∥ 1 pF): DC error 19% again because of its 1 MΩ resistance, but a corner of about 680 kHz.

So no single probe is "best". The 10x is best for the DC reading, the active probe for the AC. The better engineering answer is to measure the AC signal at a lower-impedance point (the collector or emitter) and the DC bias with a DMM that has 10 MΩ input, and always to ask what the probe is doing to the base before trusting what you see.

## The opposite case: 50 Ω input

A scope input can be switched to 50 Ω. The input becomes a resistive termination: 50 Ω to ground, with very little shunt capacitance. This is correct for signals on a 50 Ω transmission line (coax from a generator, a controlled-impedance trace with a test connector), because the line sees a matched load. There are no reflections, the bandwidth is the scope's full bandwidth, and the loading on the line is by design.

But it is the **wrong** choice for most circuit nodes. A 1 kΩ source feeding 50 Ω is attenuated by $50/1050=0.048$ (−26 dB) and the driver may be overloaded. Also the 50 Ω input has a limited maximum voltage, often around 5 V rms; applying 12 V DC or a spike can damage the input resistor. See [[q1000]] for when to deliberately choose different impedances.

## Choosing the probe by impedance: a recipe

1. Estimate the node impedance $Z_s$ (driver output resistance, or the parallel of pull-up and pull-down, or the series resistance of the trace).
2. Find the highest frequency of interest, $f_{max}\approx0.35/t_r$ for edges, or the top of the signal's spectrum.
3. Calculate $|Z_{in}(f_{max})|$ for each candidate probe, and check that it is at least 10 times $Z_s$.
4. If it isn't: pick a probe with lower capacitance, or move the probe point to a lower-impedance node (a driver output rather than a far-end high-Z input), or use an active probe.
5. If the node is a 50 Ω line: use a 50 Ω input or a Z0 probe.

## Does it also change the circuit?

Yes. A probe's capacitance is added to the node whether or not the display shows it. A crystal oscillator loaded with 12 pF of extra capacitance shifts its frequency; a 100 kΩ-pulled-up open-drain line gets a slower edge; a high-impedance comparator input might stop switching. If the circuit behaves differently when you probe it, believe the circuit: the probe has changed it, and you should find a lower-capacitance way of looking.
:::

::: applications
- **Probing a digital bus or clock** with a few hundred ohms of driver impedance: an active or low-capacitance probe.
- **Power rail debugging** where rail impedance is milliohms: a 10x passive or a 50 Ω rail probe is fine.
- **High-impedance sensor outputs** (piezo, photodiode, pH) where even 10 MΩ matters: buffered output or electrometer-grade input.
- **RF and transmission lines**: 50 Ω input, matched cables.
- **I²C and open-drain lines** with 4.7 kΩ pull-ups: probe capacitance adds to bus capacitance (see [[q0759]]).
- **Oscillators**: probing a crystal oscillator pin with a 10x probe shifts the frequency; probe the buffered output.
:::

::: assumptions
- Linear, small-signal impedance behavior; probe $R_{in}$ and $C_{in}$ are the typical values quoted, which vary by model.
- The source is modeled as a resistive Thevenin impedance. A real source may be reactive or nonlinear (a driver whose output impedance changes with the state).
- The ground connection is short; a long ground lead adds inductance and resonance that aren't part of this model.
- Cable effects (transmission-line behavior, losses) are not modeled; above a few hundred MHz the probe tip's response is more complicated than a simple $RC$.

Alternative valid answer: "as high as practical, and for transmission lines 50 Ω because the aim is matching, not non-loading." Both are right in their context.
:::

::: followups
### What's the input impedance of a typical scope, and why 1 MΩ?
Typically 1 MΩ in parallel with about 10-20 pF in the default mode. 1 MΩ is high enough to load most circuits lightly, low enough to be practical in the input amplifier's bias and noise design, and it forms a defined divider with a 9 MΩ probe resistor for the 10x probe. The 50 Ω mode is the alternative for RF and transmission lines.

### My 100 kΩ-impedance node reads half what it should on a 1x probe. Why?
The 1x probe's 1 MΩ is only 10 times the source, so the DC error is about 9%, but with cable capacitance the AC amplitude falls much more. At 14 kHz the 115 pF already gives a corner for a 100 kΩ source. Use a 10x probe (10 MΩ, 12 pF) or an active one; or probe a buffered point.

### How could you tell the probe is loading the circuit?
Change the probe (1x vs 10x vs active) and see if the waveform changes. Also look for changes in circuit behavior when the probe is attached (frequency shifts, resets, bus errors). A low-capacitance probe that gives a different, faster waveform than a standard one indicates loading on the first.

### Why not always use the 50 Ω input for best bandwidth?
Because it loads any circuit that isn't a 50 Ω source or line. It draws current ($V/50$ Ω: 20 mA per volt), attenuates a high-impedance source heavily, and has a low maximum voltage rating. It's correct for 50 Ω systems only.

### What is the impedance of a 10x probe at 500 MHz (12 pF)?
$1/(2\pi\cdot5\times10^8\cdot12\times10^{-12})=26.5$ Ω. So at 500 MHz the 10x probe is not 10 MΩ at all but a 26 Ω load, which would badly disturb anything but a low-impedance node. At that frequency use an active or Z0 probe.
:::

::: interpretations
The wording "any why" is a typo for "and why". The question can be read as "what impedance should the probe present to the circuit (high, to avoid loading)", or as "what impedance should the probe input be set to (1 MΩ or 50 Ω)". Both are covered: high and capacitance-limited in general, 50 Ω for transmission lines, with the extension in [[q1000]].
