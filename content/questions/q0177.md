::: basics
### In plain words
SIMO usually stands for single-inductor multiple-output (the question's "single-input" wording describes the same converter from the supply side). It makes several different output voltages from one input using just one inductor. A normal buck needs its own inductor for every output, and inductors are big and costly. A SIMO shares the single inductor between the outputs by serving them one at a time.

### Picture it
Imagine one person with a watering can and three thirsty plants. They fill the can once, pour it into plant A, refill, pour it into plant B, and so on. One can serves every plant, and the schedule decides who gets water. The catch is that the plants share a limited supply: if one drinks a lot, the others may wait, which is called cross-regulation.

### Words you need
- **Output rail**: one regulated voltage the converter must supply.
- **Time-multiplexing**: taking turns, one output per time slot.
- **Inductor**: the coil that stores and moves energy in each slot ([[q0041]]).
- **Discontinuous mode**: each slot ends with the inductor current back at zero.
- **Cross-regulation**: one output's load affecting another output's voltage.

### The simplest example
Say a device needs three rails. Three ordinary bucks need three inductors, three high-side switches and three low-side switches. A SIMO keeps one pair of main switches and one inductor, and adds one small steering switch per output. In slot one the steering switch connects the inductor to output A. The main switch lets the inductor current build up from the input, then lets it run down into output A until it reaches zero. Slot two does the same for output B, and slot three for C, then it repeats. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the savings in area and cost, and the trade-offs in peak current, power limit and complexity.
:::

Whether you understand a buck well enough to **reuse its most expensive part**. The interviewer wants to see that you can:

2. Explain *why* anyone would do it (one inductor instead of N saves area and cost) and *how it works* (each output gets a time slot in the switching period).
3. Name the costs honestly: cross-regulation between outputs, higher peak currents, a shared current budget, and a more complex controller.
4. Handle the wording: the question says "single-input", whereas the usual term is **single-inductor** multiple-output. Strong candidates notice and address both meanings.
:::

::: why
Modern SoC and IoT designs need many rails (core, memory, I/O, analog) from one battery, and the inductor is the biggest, most expensive component of each buck. Power-management IC designers therefore look for ways to share it. The question is a test of whether you can extend the basic buck story to an architecture trade-off: what do you gain, what do you give up, and when is it the right call? It connects the single-output buck ([[q0173]]), the "multiple outputs from one input" system question ([[q0167]]), and DCM operation ([[q0229]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Buck basics**: the inductor is charged from $V_{in}$ and discharged into an output ([[q0174]]).
- **Time-multiplexing**: one inductor serves one output at a time, in sequence.
- **Discontinuous conduction mode (DCM)**: inductor current returns to zero at the end of each slot, so each output starts from a clean state ([[q0229]]).
- **Energy delivered per slot**: the charge delivered to an output per cycle must equal $I_{out}\,T$ for that output to hold its voltage.
- **Cross-regulation**: a load change on one output disturbs the others because they share one energy source.
- **Power architecture alternatives**: separate bucks, a buck followed by LDOs, or SIMO ([[q0166]], [[q0167]]).
:::

::: answer
"SIMO usually stands for single-inductor multiple-output. It's a buck-style converter that makes several different regulated rails from one input using just one inductor. The question says 'single-input', which describes the same thing from the supply side: one input rail in, several output rails out. I'll cover the single-inductor version, because that's what's different.

In a normal buck every output needs its own inductor, high-side switch and low-side switch. In a SIMO, one inductor is shared and each output gets a switch connecting the inductor to its output capacitor. The controller time-multiplexes the inductor. In a given slot it charges the inductor from the input, then steers the current into whichever output needs energy, and when the current has returned to zero, it moves on to the next output.

The big benefit is area and cost, because one inductor replaces three or four, and inductors are the biggest and most expensive parts in a power stage. The costs are cross-regulation between outputs, since they share the energy, much higher peak inductor currents because it runs in discontinuous mode, a limit on total output power, and a more complicated controller. So I'd use it where board space is tight and the loads are modest, like wearables or IoT, and use separate bucks for high power rails."
:::

::: technical
**Structure (buck-type SIMO).** High-side switch $Q_H$ from $V_{in}$ to SW; low-side switch $Q_L$ from SW to ground; one inductor $L$ from SW to an internal node X; output switches $S_1\dots S_N$ from X to each output $V_{o,k}$, each with its own $C_{o,k}$.

**Time-multiplexed DCM operation.** In slot $k$: $Q_H$ and $S_k$ on, so $L$ sees $V_{in}-V_{o,k}$ and current rises to $I_{pk,k}$; then $Q_L$ and $S_k$ on, so $L$ sees $-V_{o,k}$ and current falls to zero. Then slot $k+1$ begins. Each slot is a complete DCM buck cycle for one output.

**Per-slot relations.** Charge delivered to output $k$ in a period $T_c$ is $I_{o,k}T_c=\tfrac12 I_{pk,k}(t_{c}+t_{d})$ with $t_c=\dfrac{L I_{pk,k}}{V_{in}-V_{o,k}}$ and $t_d=\dfrac{L I_{pk,k}}{V_{o,k}}$. Solving:
$$I_{pk,k}=\sqrt{\frac{2\,I_{o,k}\,T_c\,V_{o,k}(V_{in}-V_{o,k})}{L\,V_{in}}},\qquad t_{slot,k}=\sqrt{\frac{2\,L\,T_c\,V_{in}\,I_{o,k}}{V_{o,k}(V_{in}-V_{o,k})}}$$

**Capacity constraint.** All slots must fit in one period: $\sum_k t_{slot,k}\le T_c$. That sets the maximum total load for a given $L$ and $T_c$.

**Example** ($V_{in}=5$ V, $L=1$ µH, $T_c=5$ µs): 1.8 V at 150 mA, 1.2 V at 100 mA, 3.3 V at 50 mA gives slot times of 1.14, 1.05 and 0.67 µs (total 2.86 µs, 57% of the period) and peak currents of 1.32, 0.96 and 0.75 A, so 9 to 15 times the average output currents.

**Variations.** Pseudo-continuous (PCCM) schemes add a freewheeling interval at a non-zero current to raise capability. Buck-boost-type SIMO converters (outputs above and below the input) also exist. Terminology differs between papers.
:::

::: short
"SIMO means single-inductor multiple-output: one input, one shared inductor, several regulated rails. The inductor is time-multiplexed: it's charged from the input and then dumped into one output at a time, usually in discontinuous mode so each slot starts at zero current. The win is area and cost, because one inductor replaces several. The costs are cross-regulation between outputs, high peak inductor currents, a shared power limit, and a complex controller."
:::

::: long
"A SIMO buck is a switching regulator that generates several regulated output rails from one input using a single shared inductor. The acronym normally means single-inductor multiple-output; the 'single-input' wording in the question describes the same thing from the supply side, one input rail in, many rails out.

The motivation is that in a regular multi-rail design each buck needs its own inductor, and the inductor is the biggest, tallest and most expensive part. In a phone-sized or wearable device with maybe four or five rails, that's a lot of board area. A SIMO shares one.

How does it work? The inductor can only serve one output at a time, so the controller time-multiplexes it. In each switching period, there is a slot for each output. In a slot, the high-side switch connects the input to the inductor, and the output switch for that rail connects the inductor to that rail's capacitor. The current ramps up, then the high-side switch turns off, the low-side switch conducts, and the inductor current ramps down into the same rail until it hits zero. Then the next slot starts for the next output.

Running in discontinuous mode, so the current returns to zero at the end of each slot, means every slot starts from the same state, which keeps the outputs from interacting through a leftover inductor current. In my example, 5 volts in, a 1 microhenry inductor and 5 microsecond period, three rails use about 57 percent of the period, and the peak inductor currents are about ten times the average load currents.

The trade-offs follow from that. Cross-regulation: a load step on one output steals time from the others. Higher peak currents mean more conduction loss and larger saturation ratings. There's a ceiling on total power, because the slots must all fit in the period. And the controller is more complex. So SIMO fits low-power, space-constrained devices; heavy rails get their own buck."
:::

::: terms
- **SIMO**: single-inductor multiple-output converter; one input, a shared inductor, and several regulated outputs.
- **Time-multiplexing**: giving each output a turn at the inductor in sequence.
- **Slot**: the part of the switching period during which the inductor serves a single output.
- **DCM (discontinuous conduction mode)**: inductor current returns to zero each slot ([[q0229]]).
- **Cross-regulation**: how much one output's voltage moves when another output's load changes.
- **Ordered power distribution**: a control scheme that serves the outputs in a fixed sequence.
- **Pseudo-continuous mode (PCCM)**: a variation with a freewheel interval at non-zero current, to support higher load.
- **Output switch**: the switch from the shared inductor node to one output.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking the outputs are served simultaneously.** One inductor carries one current; outputs are served one at a time.
- **Ignoring cross-regulation.** Since the outputs share a single energy source, a load transient on one rail can disturb others unless the control scheme is designed for it.
- **Claiming it saves *all* the area.** It saves inductors, but each output still needs a capacitor and a switch, and the controller is more complex.
- **Forgetting the peak current penalty.** Running each slot in DCM means peaks of many times the average load current, so the inductor needs a higher saturation rating and conduction loss goes up.
- **Using SIMO for high-power rails.** The shared-inductor timing budget caps total output power.
- **Mixing it up with a multiphase buck.** Multiphase uses *several* inductors in parallel for *one* output ([[q0236]]); SIMO uses *one* inductor for *several* outputs.
:::

::: deep
## Two possible meanings

The wording "single-input multiple-output" is slightly unusual. In the power-management literature, SIMO stands for **single-inductor multiple-output**. A candidate who says "I'd read SIMO as single-inductor multiple-output, which is the usual meaning; if you meant a single input feeding several outputs, that's a multi-output regulator, and I can describe both" has handled the ambiguity well.

| Reading | What it is | Inductors | Outputs interact? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single-**inductor** multiple-output (standard) | One shared inductor, time-multiplexed | 1 | Yes (cross-regulation) |
| Single-**input** multiple-output, independent | One input rail, several independent bucks (e.g. a multi-channel PMIC) | N | No |

The rest of this page covers the shared-inductor version, because that is what makes a SIMO interesting. The independent version is simply several of the converters from [[q0175]] fed from the same input, with the usual input-capacitor and sequencing considerations ([[q0167]], [[q0171]]).

## Why share the inductor?

In a conventional design with $N$ rails you need $N$ inductors, each a chunky component. In a power budget of a few hundred milliwatts per rail (wearables, hearing aids, IoT sensors, small SoCs), each inductor may dominate the board area and cost. Sharing one inductor among $N$ outputs removes $N-1$ of them.

## How a slot works

Take slot $k$ with $S_k$ closed:

1. **Charge phase** ($t_c$): $Q_H$ on. $L$ sees $V_{in}-V_{o,k}$ and the current ramps up to $I_{pk}$. Current already flows into output $k$ during this phase, exactly as in a DCM buck.
2. **Discharge phase** ($t_d$): $Q_H$ off, $Q_L$ on. $L$ sees $-V_{o,k}$ and the current ramps down to zero. Output $k$ receives the rest of its charge.
3. **Hand-off:** at zero current $S_k$ opens and $S_{k+1}$ closes. The next slot starts from zero current. No energy is left in $L$ for the next output to inherit.

This reveals the key design point: each slot is *a DCM buck pulse*. The total charge delivered to output $k$ per period must equal $I_{o,k}T_c$, which fixes the peak current needed.

Notice how tall the peaks are compared with the load currents of 50 to 150 mA: that is the cost of DCM. The flat idle time at the end of each period is the headroom left for more load.

The slot-length formula is
$$t_{slot,k}=\sqrt{\frac{2LT_cV_{in}I_{o,k}}{V_{o,k}(V_{in}-V_{o,k})}}$$
so slot time grows with the *square root* of the load current. Doubling every load in the example multiplies the total by $\sqrt2$, taking 2.86 µs to 4.04 µs (81% of the period). Doubling again would not fit, so the converter runs out of capacity. A smaller $L$ or a longer $T_c$ helps, though a longer period means more output ripple.

## Cross-regulation: the central problem

All outputs draw from the same inductor, so they are coupled:

- If output 1's load jumps, its slot must grow, which pushes the other slots later or shorter, and their outputs can sag.
- A controller needs to measure each output and decide in each period which output needs how much energy. This is the control scheme: it must be fast enough and arbitrate fairly.
- In DCM with each slot starting from zero, the coupling is limited to *time sharing*, not leftover energy, which is why DCM with ordered slots has good cross-regulation. Pseudo-continuous schemes support more power but have a leftover-current coupling, which needs extra control effort.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Names for SIMO control schemes (ordered power distribution, pseudo-continuous conduction, freewheeling or "dual-state" variants) differ between authors and vendors. The descriptions above are the general ideas; check a specific paper or datasheet for exact definitions and cross-regulation figures.

## Trade-offs against the alternatives

| Approach | Inductors | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| Separate bucks | $N$ | Independent, simple, high power per rail | Largest area and cost |
| One buck plus LDOs | 1 | Quiet rails, simple | LDO losses when ratios are large ([[q0157]]) |
| SIMO | 1 | One inductor and high efficiency on all rails | Cross-regulation, high peaks, limited power, complex control |

## Where the idea sits in the bigger picture

A SIMO buck is one of several ways to build a multi-rail power tree ([[q0166]], [[q0167]]). When someone asks you for the architecture for a wearable with five rails, SIMO is a candidate for the lowest area; whether it wins depends on the power budget, the noise tolerance of each rail, and how much risk the team will take on a more complex controller.
:::

::: applications
- **Wearables, hearing aids and IoT sensor nodes**: several rails (core, I/O, analog) from one small battery where board area is tight.
- **Power-management ICs for small SoCs** that integrate a SIMO stage to serve multiple low-power domains with a single external inductor.
- **Display drivers**: multiple output voltages (positive and negative supplies) for panels from a single inductor, often in a buck-boost-type SIMO arrangement.
- **Energy-harvesting and ultra-low-power systems**, where the converter must serve several rails efficiently at small currents.
- **Academic and IC-design work**: SIMO controllers are an active research area for time-multiplexing control and cross-regulation reduction.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a SIMO run in discontinuous mode?
Because it lets each slot start from zero inductor current, so no energy carries over from one output to the next and the outputs are decoupled in energy terms. The price is peak current many times the average (here 9 to 15 times), which raises conduction loss and the inductor's saturation rating. Continuous-style operation can serve more power, but at the cost of stronger cross-regulation and a harder control problem.

### What limits the total output power of a SIMO converter?
All the slots must fit into one switching period: $\sum_k t_{slot,k}\le T_c$. Since $t_{slot}\propto\sqrt{L\,T_c\,I_o}$, a smaller $L$ and a longer $T_c$ increase capacity, but a longer $T_c$ increases ripple, and a smaller $L$ increases peak current. Additionally, the inductor's saturation current and the switches' ratings limit the usable peak.

### How would you reduce cross-regulation?
Use fast, per-output feedback and a scheduling scheme that gives each output a guaranteed slot, with DCM so no inductor current carries over. Also use adequate output capacitance, and priority or "skip" logic so a heavily loaded rail can't starve lightly loaded rails. Add a freewheel or recirculation interval to absorb unused energy.

### Compare SIMO to three separate bucks for three 100 mA rails. When would you choose each?
Separate bucks give independence, lower peaks (each can run in CCM at higher load) and simpler control, at the cost of three inductors, which is a lot of area at 100 mA. A SIMO wins when area or cost dominates and total power is small. If any rail is noise-critical or high current, give it its own buck or follow it with an LDO.

### Scenario: with a SIMO, you see the 1.2 V rail sag every time the 1.8 V rail's load switches on. What do you check?
That's cross-regulation: the 1.8 V rail's slot grew, so the other slots were delayed or shortened. Check the total slot utilization against the period (if it's near 100% there's no headroom), the controller's slot priority, and the output capacitance on the 1.2 V rail. Possible fixes include increasing $C_{1.2V}$, raising the switching frequency or reducing $L$ to increase capacity, or giving the 1.2 V rail priority.
:::
