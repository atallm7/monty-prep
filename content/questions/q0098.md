::: testing
Whether you can say what "mode" means in terms of a single number, the **sign of the threshold voltage**, and then use it. The interviewer wants to hear:

1. Enhancement-mode is **normally off** ($I_D=0$ at $V_{GS}=0$) and the gate must *create* a channel.
2. Depletion-mode is **normally on** (a channel exists at $V_{GS}=0$) and the gate must *remove* it.
3. A real use for each: enhancement for switches and logic (almost everything you buy), depletion for start-up circuits, current sources and normally-on protection.

Bonus points for connecting it to JFETs, to the symbol difference (broken vs solid channel line), and to GaN/SiC devices where "normally-on" is a safety issue.
:::

::: why
Nearly every MOSFET on a board is enhancement-mode, so the question is really "do you know what the rare case is and when you would reach for it?" Depletion devices show up in off-line start-up circuits, constant-current sources, and some GaN power stages. Understanding why a normally-on device is dangerous in a power converter (a gate-drive failure means a short from rail to rail) is exactly the kind of system-level thinking interviewers look for.
:::

::: concepts
- **Threshold voltage $V_t$**: the gate-source voltage at which an inversion channel forms. Its sign defines the mode. See [[q0096]].
- **Inversion vs built-in channel**: enhancement devices have no channel until the gate field makes one; depletion devices have a channel implanted at fabrication.
- **Square-law saturation current**: $I_D=\tfrac12k(V_{GS}-V_t)^2$ applies to both. Only $V_t$ changes sign. See [[q0095]].
- **Symbols**: broken channel line = enhancement, continuous channel line = depletion. See [[q0089]].
- **N vs P polarity**: the sign conventions flip for P-channel. See [[q0097]].
- **JFET analogy**: a JFET is a depletion-mode device; the gate pinches off an existing channel.
:::

::: answer
"The two modes differ in whether a channel exists with zero gate voltage. An enhancement-mode MOSFET has no channel at $V_{GS}=0$. The gate voltage has to exceed the threshold, say +1 V for an NMOS, to pull enough electrons to the surface to make one. So it's normally off, and you *enhance* the conductivity by turning the gate on. That is what almost every MOSFET you buy is, and it's what CMOS logic and power switches use.

A depletion-mode MOSFET has a channel built in during fabrication, with a doping implant under the gate, so it conducts at $V_{GS}=0$. To turn it off you apply a gate voltage of the opposite sign, for an N-channel a negative $V_{GS}$ below its threshold, which depletes the channel of carriers. In other words, an N-channel depletion device has a *negative* $V_t$.

I'd use enhancement devices as switches because failing off is safe. I'd use a depletion device where I want something that works before anything else is powered: a start-up current source, a constant-current regulator, or a normally-on pass element. The saturation equation is the same for both. Only the sign of $V_t$ changes."
:::

::: technical
**Definition by threshold sign (N-channel).**
- Enhancement: $V_{tn}>0$. At $V_{GS}=0$, $I_D\approx0$.
- Depletion: $V_{tn}<0$ (typically -1 V to -4 V). At $V_{GS}=0$, a channel exists and the device sits in saturation with
$$I_{DSS}=\tfrac12\,k\,V_{t}^2 .$$
Applying $V_{GS}<0$ depletes the channel; at $V_{GS}=V_t$ (negative) $I_D\to0$.

**For P-channel** both signs flip: enhancement $V_{tp}<0$, depletion $V_{tp}>0$.

**Equations.** The same square-law model holds in each region:
$$I_D=\begin{cases}0 & V_{GS}\le V_t\\ k\left[(V_{GS}-V_t)V_{DS}-\tfrac12V_{DS}^2\right] & V_{DS}<V_{GS}-V_t\\ \tfrac12k(V_{GS}-V_t)^2 & V_{DS}\ge V_{GS}-V_t\end{cases}$$

**Example.** With $k=2$ mA/V² and $V_t=-2$ V (depletion NMOS): $I_{DSS}=\tfrac12(2\text{ mA/V}^2)(4\text{ V}^2)=4$ mA at $V_{GS}=0$. Adding a source resistor $R_S=1$ kΩ with the gate grounded gives a self-biased current source: $V_{GS}=-I_DR_S$ and $I_D=\tfrac12k(-I_DR_S+2)^2$, which solves to $I_D=1$ mA, $V_{GS}=-1$ V (check: $\tfrac12(2\text{ mA})(1)^2=1$ mA).

**Fabrication.** Depletion devices get a channel implant of the same type as the source/drain under the gate oxide. Enhancement devices are tuned to $V_t>0$ by threshold-adjust implants. In a CMOS process a depletion option is an extra mask and implant, which is why it is rare.

**Power devices.** Silicon power MOSFETs are enhancement. GaN HEMTs come as e-mode (a p-GaN gate, normally off) or d-mode (normally on, usually paired in a cascode with a low-voltage silicon enhancement MOSFET so the combination is normally off). SiC JFETs are naturally normally-on.
:::

::: short
"The mode tells you whether a channel exists at zero gate voltage. Enhancement mode has no channel at $V_{GS}=0$, so it's normally off and you apply a gate voltage above $V_t$ to create one. That's nearly every MOSFET. Depletion mode has a channel implanted at fabrication, so it conducts at zero gate voltage and you apply a gate voltage of the opposite polarity to cut it off. For an N-channel that means a negative threshold. The equations are identical, just with a negative $V_t$, and depletion parts are used for start-up circuits and current sources."
:::

::: long
"The way I think about it is: does a channel exist when the gate is at the same voltage as the source? In an enhancement-mode MOSFET, no. The body is plain p-type for an NMOS, and until the gate is above the threshold, around 1 V, there's no inversion layer, so no current. Positive gate voltage *enhances* the conductivity. That's what we use for switches and logic because the safe default state is off. If the gate drive dies, the transistor stops conducting.

A depletion-mode device has a thin n-type channel implanted under the gate during fabrication, so there's already a conductive path from source to drain at $V_{GS}=0$. To turn it off, you apply a negative gate voltage, which pushes electrons out and *depletes* the channel. The threshold voltage is negative, for example -2 V. The current equation is the same square law, $\tfrac12k(V_{GS}-V_t)^2$, so at $V_{GS}=0$ you get $I_{DSS}=\tfrac12kV_t^2$.

Where do you use a depletion device? A classic one is a start-up circuit in an off-line power supply: a normally-on device supplies a trickle of current from the high-voltage rail to wake the controller, then it's shut off. Another is a constant-current source: put a resistor in the source, tie the gate to the other end, and the device self-biases to a fixed current. A JFET works the same way.

The danger is in power electronics. A normally-on device in a bridge conducts if the gate driver isn't powered yet, which can shoot through the half-bridge. That's why GaN power devices are either enhancement mode or cascoded with a small silicon enhancement FET so the stack is normally off."
:::

::: terms
- **Enhancement-mode**: no channel at $V_{GS}=0$; gate must exceed $V_t$ in the turn-on direction to create one.
- **Depletion-mode**: a channel exists at $V_{GS}=0$; the gate removes it.
- **Threshold voltage ($V_t$)**: gate-source voltage at which conduction starts or stops; sign defines the mode.
- **$I_{DSS}$**: drain current at $V_{GS}=0$ in saturation, usually quoted for depletion devices and JFETs.
- **Normally-on / normally-off**: behaviour at zero gate drive. Normally-off is safer in power stages.
- **Pinch-off voltage ($V_P$)**: JFET/depletion-device term for the $V_{GS}$ that fully closes the channel; equal to $V_t$ here.
- **Cascode (GaN)**: a depletion GaN HEMT in series with a low-voltage silicon enhancement MOSFET to make a normally-off switch.
- **Self-biased current source**: a depletion FET with a source resistor, where $V_{GS}=-I_DR_S$ sets the current.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "enhancement is off at zero gate voltage, depletion is on." An exceptional answer:

- Reduces the difference to the **sign of $V_t$** and shows the equations don't change.
- Computes $I_{DSS}=\tfrac12kV_t^2$ for an example and uses it.
- Gives the self-biased current source $I_D$ with a source resistor, with numbers.
- Names concrete uses: start-up regulators, constant-current sources, normally-on pass devices, analog JFET input stages.
- Brings in the **power-electronics safety angle**: normally-on GaN/SiC JFETs, why they are cascoded, and why a normally-off device is preferred for fail-safe behaviour.
- Draws the symbols correctly (broken vs solid channel line).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Depletion mode means it depletes at zero bias."** It is the opposite: depletion-mode devices are *on* at zero bias; the gate *depletes* the channel to turn them off.
- **Assuming the equations differ.** Same square-law model, different sign of $V_t$. Don't invent separate formulas.
- **Calling every negative-threshold device "PMOS".** An N-channel depletion device has a negative $V_t$ but is still N-channel. Polarity (N/P) and mode (E/D) are independent.
- **Using a depletion device as a switch without checking the off gate voltage.** A 3.3 V logic signal can't turn off a -3 V threshold part; you need a negative gate supply.
- **Forgetting that "normally on" is a safety issue** in a half-bridge: with the driver unpowered both devices conduct.
- **Saying the symbol difference is the arrow.** The arrow shows N vs P; the broken vs continuous channel line shows enhancement vs depletion.
:::

::: deep
## What the gate is actually doing

A MOSFET is controlled by how much charge the gate puts in the silicon surface under it. There are two ways to end up with a conductive channel at zero gate bias.

**Enhancement.** The surface under the gate is the same type as the body. For an NMOS: p-type under the oxide, between two n+ regions. That is two back-to-back diodes, no conduction. The gate must pull electrons up against the doping to *invert* the surface, which takes a threshold voltage of order 0.5-1 V.

**Depletion.** During fabrication a shallow n-type implant is added under the gate. At $V_{GS}=0$ there is already a conducting n-type path. A negative $V_{GS}$ repels electrons, shrinking the path (depletion), until at $V_{GS}=V_t<0$ nothing is left. A positive $V_{GS}$ in this device *enhances* it above $I_{DSS}$, so a depletion MOSFET can run in both directions, unlike a JFET, whose gate junction forward-biases.

## Worked example: depletion current source

Take the depletion NMOS above ($k=2$ mA/V², $V_t=-2$ V), gate to ground, resistor $R_S$ from source to ground. $V_{GS}=-I_DR_S$ gives $I_D=\tfrac12k(2-I_DR_S)^2$. With $R_S=1$ kΩ:

$$I_D=1\text{ mA},\quad V_{GS}=-1\text{ V}$$

With $R_S=500$ Ω, solving numerically gives $I_D\approx1.53$ mA. A larger $R_S$ gives a smaller current. As long as $V_{DS}$ stays above $V_{GS}-V_t=1$ V, the current barely depends on supply voltage, and the source resistor makes it less sensitive to variations in $k$ and $V_t$ (the larger the resistor, the more the current is set by $R_S$ alone).

## Real hardware

- **Start-up circuits.** Off-line flyback controllers need a few hundred µA to start from a 300 V rail. A depletion MOSFET passes it with no resistor dissipating heat all the time, and turns off once the auxiliary winding takes over.
- **Constant-current regulators.** Two- and three-terminal current limiters are depletion FETs with an internal source resistor; used for LED strings and as the bias source for analog stages.
- **JFET input stages.** Low-bias-current op-amp inputs rely on JFETs, a depletion-mode structure.
- **Normally-on power devices.** SiC JFETs and d-mode GaN HEMTs are normally on. Cascoding with a 30 V silicon enhancement NMOS gives a package that behaves as normally-off from the gate pin, and the low-voltage device's body diode and reverse-recovery behaviour become part of the stack's behaviour.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Whether a given product is "depletion MOSFET", "JFET" or "HEMT" varies by vendor. Check the datasheet for $V_{GS(th)}$ (negative means normally on) and for the recommended off-state gate voltage.

## Symbols

In the standard schematic symbol, the channel is drawn as three segments for enhancement (broken: no channel until the gate makes one) and as a single continuous line for depletion (channel exists). Arrow direction (pointing into the channel for N, out for P) tells you the polarity. See [[q0089]].
:::

::: applications
- **Off-line start-up**: a normally-on high-voltage depletion NMOS feeds the controller's supply pin before the bias winding is alive.
- **Constant-current source / LED current limiter**: a depletion FET with a source resistor (or an integrated version) gives a supply-independent current for LED strings.
- **Analog front ends**: JFET (depletion) input stages for electrometer and audio amplifiers with very low input bias current.
- **Cascoded GaN and SiC switches**: depletion HEMT/JFET plus low-voltage silicon MOSFET to make a normally-off switch.
- **Fail-on or "always conducting" pass elements**, for instance bypass switches that must conduct if the control circuit is unpowered.
- **Enhancement-mode** everywhere else: logic gates, load switches, synchronous rectifiers, and every discrete power MOSFET.
:::

::: assumptions
- The discussion is about **MOSFETs in strong inversion with a long-channel square-law model**; real devices have channel-length modulation and subthreshold conduction, so "zero" current at $V_{GS}<V_t$ is really a small leakage.
- "Enhancement" and "depletion" are defined relative to the gate-source voltage of **zero**. Some depletion-mode parts are specified with a very small negative $V_t$ (near $-0.1$ V) and behave almost like enhancement devices at the edge of conduction.
- For **JFETs**, the gate junction must stay reverse-biased; a depletion MOSFET (insulated gate) tolerates a small positive $V_{GS}$ while a JFET does not.
- For **P-channel**, flip every sign.
:::

::: followups
### Can a depletion-mode NMOS be turned on harder than $I_{DSS}$?
Yes. Unlike a JFET, the gate of a MOS device is insulated, so a positive $V_{GS}$ is allowed and enhances the channel above $I_{DSS}$. With $k=2$ mA/V² and $V_t=-2$ V, $V_{GS}=+1$ V gives $\tfrac12(2\text{ m})(3)^2=9$ mA. The limit is the oxide rating, not a junction turning on.

### How would you turn a depletion NMOS completely off using a 3.3 V logic supply?
You can't directly: the gate must go to roughly $V_t$ *below* the source. Either use a negative supply for the gate, or raise the source with a resistor/regulated node until $V_{GS}=V_G-V_S\le V_t$, which is exactly the self-bias trick. Or pair it with a normally-off enhancement FET in series, so the enhancement device does the switching.

### Why is normally-on dangerous in a half-bridge?
During power-up or driver failure both gates sit at 0 V. If both devices are normally-on, both conduct and short the bus through the bridge. A normally-off device fails safe. That's why GaN and SiC JFET devices are cascoded or use gate structures that make them normally-off.

### How does the symbol tell you the mode?
Enhancement: broken channel line (three dashes), no channel at zero bias. Depletion: continuous channel line. The arrow direction gives N vs P.

### What is the self-bias point of a depletion NMOS with $R_S=2$ kΩ, $k=2$ mA/V², $V_t=-2$ V?
$V_{GS}=-I_D(2\text{ k})$ and $I_D=\tfrac12(2\text{ m})(2-2000I_D)^2$. Let $x=I_D$ in mA: $x=(2-2x)^2=4-8x+4x^2$, so $4x^2-9x+4=0$ and $x=(9-\sqrt{81-64})/8=(9-4.123)/8=0.610$ mA (the other root, 1.64 mA, makes $V_{GS}<V_t$ and is invalid). So $I_D\approx0.61$ mA and $V_{GS}\approx-1.22$ V.

### Scenario: you replace a depletion start-up FET with an enhancement FET and the supply won't start. Why?
The depletion part conducted at $V_{GS}=0$ with no control voltage. An enhancement FET needs a positive $V_{GS}$ above $V_t$, but at start-up nothing is powered to generate it. You'd need a resistor from drain to gate (giving $V_{GS}$ from the high-voltage rail, clamped by a Zener) or a different start-up scheme.
:::
