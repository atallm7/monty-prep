::: testing
Whether you can follow a fault or overload through the whole chain instead of stopping at "the voltage drops". The interviewer wants the sequence: the trace's $IR$ drop starves the load (brown-out, resets), the trace heats as $I^2R$ and can discolour, delaminate or fuse, the supply may hit its current limit and hiccup or fold back, the regulator cannot see the sag because it senses locally, and constant-power loads can collapse. Then the design response: size copper for the fault, protect at the source, add remote sense and local capacitance.
:::

::: why
Real products get overloaded: a motor stalls, a short appears on a connector, a downstream converter starts up into a heavy load. If the wiring between supply and load is long and thin, the failure mode is decided by layout: either the system resets cleanly, the supply trips cleanly, or a trace burns. Interviewers like this because it needs electrical, thermal and protection reasoning in one answer, and it separates "the voltage sags" from a real failure analysis.
:::

::: concepts
- **IR drop**: $\Delta V = IR_{path}$ grows linearly with current and with length. See [[q0543]].
- **$I^2R$ heating and fusing**: copper has a finite current-time capacity (Onderdonk). See [[q0534]].
- **Current limit behaviours**: constant-current, foldback, hiccup, latch-off.
- **Local feedback**: the regulator cannot see drop beyond its sense point.
- **Constant-power load collapse**: more current as voltage falls.
- **Protection placement**: fuses, eFuses and limits belong at the source of the weak conductor.
:::

::: answer
"Several things happen at once, and which one wins depends on the layout and the supply.

First, the voltage at the load falls because of the drop in the trace: current times the path resistance. A 1 mm, one ounce trace that's 50 mm long is about 24 milliohms, so 10 A drops 240 mV each way, and the load may brown out or reset. The regulator doesn't know, because its feedback senses at its own output.

Second, the trace heats as I squared R. That's 2.4 W in a tiny copper strip, which can discolour the board, lift the copper, and if the current is high and lasts long enough it fuses. For that 1 mm trace the adiabatic fusing current is roughly 10 A for a second, 32 A for a tenth of a second.

Third, the supply reacts: if it has a current limit it either clamps, folds back, or goes into hiccup, which can make the load repeatedly restart. If the load is itself a converter, lower voltage means more current, which means more drop, and past a limit it collapses.

So the design answer is to size the copper for the maximum fault the supply can deliver, or set a current limit or fuse at the source below the trace's rating, use remote sense, and keep the run short."
:::

::: technical
**DC sag.** $V_{load} = V_{reg} - I(R_{sup}+R_{ret})$. For $R = 48$ mΩ round trip (1 mm, 50 mm, both paths) and $I = 10$ A: 0.48 V.

**Heating.** $P = I^2R$: 4.8 W for the round trip. Steady-state rise follows IPC-2221, $I = 0.048\,\Delta T^{0.44}A^{0.725}$: 1 mm, 1 oz allows about 3.2 A for 20 °C on an outer layer, so 10 A is far beyond it.

**Fusing (adiabatic).** Onderdonk: $I = A_{cmil}\sqrt{\dfrac{\log_{10}\left(\frac{T_m-T_a}{234+T_a}+1\right)}{33\,s}}$, $T_m=1083$ °C, $T_a=25$ °C. For 1 mm × 35 µm (69 circular mils): 3.2 A at 10 s, 10.1 A at 1 s, 32 A at 0.1 s. Real traces lose heat, so the true values are higher, but the board may be damaged well before copper melts (FR-4 chars, copper lifts above its glass-transition temperature, typically 130 to 170 °C).

**Supply behaviour.** Options: constant-current limiting (the voltage collapses to whatever the load resistance gives), foldback (limit current reduces as $V_{out}$ falls), hiccup (periodic retry, duty cycle low), latch-off. The trace must survive the supply's *fault* current, not its rated current.

**Constant-power load.** $V = \tfrac12(V_s + \sqrt{V_s^2-4RP})$ exists only for $P \le V_s^2/4R$; beyond, voltage collapses. With $V_s = 3.3$ V and $R = 100$ mΩ: 27 W.

**Regulation and loop.** Local feedback does not compensate; long paths add inductance and can reduce phase margin.

**Mitigations.** Wider/shorter conductors, planes; fuse or eFuse at the source sized below the trace rating; current limit set below trace capability; remote sense; local capacitance and undervoltage lockout (UVLO) at the load so it resets cleanly; thermal monitoring.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Fusing times depend on the board, layer and surrounding copper; Onderdonk gives the adiabatic upper bound on time (lower bound on current). Board damage (delamination, charring) can occur at far lower currents than copper melting.
:::

The supply may hit current limit, fold back or hiccup, and a constant-power load can collapse. The fix is copper sized for fault current, a fuse or limit at the source, remote sense, and a clean UVLO."
:::

::: long
"A few things happen, and the order depends on the numbers.

First, the voltage at the load falls. A one ounce, 1 millimetre trace 50 millimetres long is about 24 milliohms, so at 10 amps you lose 240 millivolts each way. The regulator's feedback senses at its own output, so it doesn't see this; it happily reports a good rail while the load is browning out or resetting.

Second, thermal. That trace dissipates I squared R, about 2.4 W per path, in a tiny area. The IPC chart says 1 mm of one ounce copper carries around 3 amps for a 20 degree rise, so 10 amps is way beyond that. If it continues, the copper heats until the board chars or the copper lifts, and eventually it fuses. By the Onderdonk equation it's about 10 amps for a second, about 32 amps for a tenth of a second, adiabatically. Real boards usually fail before that, from delamination.

Third, how the supply responds matters. If it has a current limit it might clamp, fold back, or hiccup, and hiccup can make the load restart over and over. If the load is a converter drawing constant power, falling voltage means rising current, so the drop grows, and beyond V squared over four R, it collapses.

So I design for it: size the copper for the fault current the supply can deliver, or put a fuse, eFuse or current limit at the source set below the trace rating. I add remote sense, local capacitance, and an undervoltage lockout so the load resets cleanly instead of limping. And I keep the run short to begin with."
:::

::: terms
- **Brown-out**: supply voltage falling below the load's minimum, causing resets or errors.
- **Onderdonk equation**: the adiabatic relation between conductor size, current and time to melting.
- **Foldback limiting**: current limit that reduces as output voltage falls.
- **Hiccup mode**: a fault response that shuts down and retries periodically.
- **eFuse**: an electronic fuse (a switch with current limiting and protection).
- **UVLO**: undervoltage lockout; holds the load off below a threshold.
- **Constant-power load**: draws $I = P/V$, so its current rises as voltage falls.
- **Delamination**: separation of copper from the laminate when overheated.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the voltage drops and the trace might heat up."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Lays out distinct failure paths: drop, heat/fusing, supply limit behaviour, collapse of constant-power loads, loop effects.
- Gives numbers (240 mV per path at 10 A, 3.2 A IPC rating, 10 A fusing in 1 s).
- Notes the regulator is blind to the drop without remote sense.
- Distinguishes the supply's *fault* current from its rated current.
- Offers the protection architecture: fuse/limit at the source, UVLO at the load, adequate copper.
- Mentions damage can occur below the copper melting point.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Assuming the regulator keeps the rail up.** Its feedback is local; the load sees the sag.
- **Sizing the trace for the rated current only.** The supply may deliver much more in a fault.
- **Forgetting UVLO.** A load that half-works at low voltage can misbehave or corrupt memory.
- **Thinking a trace fuse is a protection strategy.** A fusing trace damages the board, can start a fire, and is unpredictable; use a real fuse.
- **Ignoring constant-power behaviour.** Converters downstream make the overload self-reinforcing.
- **Ignoring hiccup.** Repeated restart can overheat the trace or the load over time.
:::

::: deep
## The scenario

A regulator feeds a load 60 mm away over a 1 mm wide, 1 oz trace (and a similar return). Normal load: 2 A. Now the load tries to draw 10 A: a motor stalls, a downstream converter starts into a short, or a capacitor bank charges.

## Step 1: voltage sag

$V_{load} = V_{reg} - I R_{path}$ with $R_{path} = 2R_\square l/w$. For the 60 mm, 1 mm trace in each direction that is $2\times60\times0.48 = 57.6$ mΩ. At 10 A it is 0.58 V; at 2 A it is 0.12 V. A 3.3 V rail drops to 2.7 V, below the typical 3.0 V minimum for a 3.3 V part with a 10% tolerance. The regulator sees none of it. Result: resets, logic errors, brown-out loops (a restart draws inrush, which resets again).

## Step 2: heat

$P = I^2R_{path}$ = 5.8 W at 10 A, dissipated in about 120 mm of narrow copper, roughly 48 mW per mm. The IPC-2221 rating for 1 mm, 1 oz outer layer is about 3.2 A at 20 °C rise; at 10 A the formula gives a rise above 200 °C, outside its valid range, so the trace simply overheats, and copper resistance increases about 0.39% per °C, making things worse. FR-4 typically starts to degrade near its glass-transition temperature, so the board can char or the copper can lift well below copper melting.

### Fusing

Onderdonk's adiabatic equation (no heat loss) gives the minimum time to melt, or the maximum current for a given time. For 1 mm × 35 µm:

| Time | Fusing current |
|---|---|
| 0.1 s | 32 A |
| 1 s | 10 A |
| 10 s | 3.2 A |

A short trace will not fuse cleanly: it chars, vaporises and can arc. That is why you do not rely on a trace as a fuse. (Deliberate fusible links are designed with controlled geometry and are used only in low-cost, low-energy products.)

## Step 3: what the supply does

Typical current-limit behaviours:

- **Constant current**: output current clamps; the voltage falls to what the load permits. The trace may carry the limit current indefinitely: if that exceeds the trace rating, the trace overheats.
- **Foldback**: the limit decreases as $V_{out}$ falls, reducing fault dissipation but possibly preventing a heavy load from starting.
- **Hiccup**: the converter shuts down for a long time and retries at a low duty cycle; average fault dissipation is small, but the load sees repeated restarts.
- **Latch-off**: the output stays off until power cycles.

The supply's *limit* must be below what the weakest conductor can survive. The rated current is not the point; the fault limit is.

## Step 4: constant-power loads

A load that is a converter draws $I = P/V$. With path resistance $R$:

$$V = V_s - \frac{P}{V}R \;\Rightarrow\; V^2 - V_sV + PR = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; V = \frac{V_s + \sqrt{V_s^2 - 4PR}}{2}$$

Solutions exist only for $P \le V_s^2/(4R)$: for $V_s = 3.3$ V and $R = 58$ mΩ that's 47 W, but for 100 mΩ it's 27 W. Approaching the limit the voltage falls steeply, the current rises, and the load can fall off a cliff. At the point of collapse $V = V_s/2$ and $I = V_s/(2R)$. This is the same effect as negative incremental resistance in input filters.

## Step 5: loop and dynamics

The long path adds inductance. When the overload arrives suddenly, $L\,di/dt$ makes an instantaneous dip, and the resonance with local capacitance may ring. If the load then browns out and releases (reset), the current suddenly drops and the inductance kicks the voltage up, a surge that can stress the load. See [[q0543]].

## Putting it together: design responses

1. **Know the fault current.** What can the supply deliver? What can a downstream short pull? Size the copper for that, or limit the supply.
2. **Protect at the source.** Put a fuse, PTC, or eFuse at the start of the weak conductor, rated below the trace rating and above normal load plus inrush. An eFuse offers adjustable current limit and a defined shutdown.
3. **Right-size the conductor.** Use pours/planes; 10 A in a 6 mm trace is 4 mΩ per 50 mm instead of 24.
4. **Add remote sense** if drop matters, and local bulk capacitance to carry transients.
5. **Add UVLO** at the load or its supervisor so it resets cleanly instead of running in a damaged state.
6. **Keep it short**, and test: apply a worst-case overload and measure with a thermal camera and a scope for droop and restart behaviour.

::: applications
- **Motor drives**, where a stalled motor draws many times the running current through long supply runs.
- **Battery packs** with fuses or MOSFET protection at the pack, sized below the wiring ratings.
- **Hot-swap and eFuse designs** in servers and telecom, limiting inrush and faults at the connector.
- **USB-powered products**, where the port's current limit and the cable resistance combine to determine droop at the device.
- **Multi-rail processors**, where core regulators far from the load use remote sense and POL conversion.
:::

::: assumptions
The numbers assume 1 oz copper on FR-4, 1 mm width, an outer layer, and a resistive path. The Onderdonk values are adiabatic limits; real boards conduct heat away so fusing currents are higher, but board damage can occur sooner. The supply's behaviour depends heavily on the specific regulator (limit type and value). Constant-power collapse applies to converter loads; resistive and motor loads behave differently (a stalled motor is closer to a resistive load with high current and an inductive kick). For very short pulses the copper's thermal mass matters and it can survive much more. Alternative approach: choose to make the trace the weak point on purpose, with a controlled fusible link, only when cost forbids a real fuse and the energy is low.
:::

::: followups
### Why doesn't the regulator fix the voltage drop in the trace?
Because its feedback is connected to its own output capacitor, and it regulates that node to the set point. The drop is downstream of that node and invisible to it. Only remote sense (moving the feedback to the load) lets it compensate, and then only for DC and slow drop.

### How would you protect a long supply trace from a downstream short?
A fuse, PTC or eFuse at the start of the trace, sized below the trace's rating (including a margin on the fusing curve) and above the normal and inrush current. If the supply has its own limit I set it below the trace rating too. I'd also check what the supply delivers in a hard short: foldback or hiccup supplies are friendlier.

### What happens with hiccup mode if the load restarts into the overload?
The supply shuts down, waits, then retries. Average dissipation is low, but each retry gives a burst of high current and a restart of the load, which may also be a brown-out loop. It protects the wiring thermally if the duty cycle is low, but the system looks like it's resetting. The load's UVLO and a proper fault indication help diagnose it.

### Why can a long trace make a constant-power load collapse?
The load draws $I = P/V$, so as the drop lowers $V$, the current rises, increasing the drop. Solving gives a maximum deliverable power $V_s^2/4R$; above it there's no solution and the voltage collapses. With a 3.3 V source and 100 mΩ path, that is 27 W. Lower path resistance moves the cliff far away.

### Could you rely on the trace itself as a fuse?
I wouldn't. Its fusing time and temperature depend on layout, board and surroundings, it chars the laminate, can arc, and might not open at all if the current is just high enough to heat without melting. A real fuse or eFuse gives a defined, certified response.
:::

::: interpretations
"Draw too much current" means the load demands more than the normal operating current, either through a fault or a transient. The question could be asking about a *transient* demand, which brings in inductance and the local capacitor ([[q0543]], [[q0553]]), or a *sustained* overload, which brings in heating and protection; the answer covers both. Related: failure modes of bucks ([[q0265]]).
