::: testing
Whether you can define a differential pair precisely (two complementary signals, information carried by the *difference*), explain why it's used (common-mode noise rejection, lower EMI, immunity to ground offset, lower swing) and **balance it with the costs** (twice the traces and pins, tight routing and matching rules, skew-to-common-mode conversion, termination and AC-coupling complexity). The board-layout angle is: it works only if the two halves stay balanced and coupled, which is why layout rules exist.
:::

::: why
Almost every modern high-speed interface is differential: USB, Ethernet, PCIe, SATA, HDMI/DisplayPort, MIPI, LVDS, CAN, RS-485, DDR clocks. As an engineer you will be asked to route them, so you need to know what the pair is doing electrically in order to judge which layout rules are essential and which are folklore. The interviewer also uses it to see whether you think in trade-offs rather than "differential is always better". The signals-level view of the same topic is [[q0679]]; this page is the board-design view.
:::

::: concepts
- **Differential and common-mode voltage**: $V_d=V_P-V_N$, $V_{cm}=(V_P+V_N)/2$. See [[q0683]], [[q0684]].
- **Noise rejection**: noise that couples equally to both lines is common-mode and cancels in the receiver's subtraction. See [[q0682]].
- **Odd/even modes and coupling**: a pair has differential ($Z_{odd}$) and common ($Z_{even}$) impedances; $Z_{diff}=2Z_{odd}$. See [[q0710]].
- **Field cancellation**: equal and opposite currents make fields cancel at a distance, reducing EMI.
- **Skew and mode conversion**: any imbalance turns differential signal into common-mode. See [[q0520]].
- **Termination**: the pair is terminated with a differential resistor (about $Z_{diff}$) at the receiver. See [[q0529]].
:::

::: answer
"A differential pair is two traces that carry complementary versions of the same signal: when one goes up by a bit, the other goes down by the same amount, and the receiver only looks at the difference between them. So the information is $V_P - V_N$ and the average of the two, the common-mode level, doesn't carry data.

That's useful for three reasons. Noise that couples into the pair, like crosstalk, ground bounce or EMI pickup, hits both lines about equally, so it's common-mode and the receiver subtracts it out. Second, the currents in the two lines are equal and opposite so their fields cancel and the pair radiates less. And third, you get twice the voltage difference for a given swing per line, so you can run lower swings, which also helps with speed and power.

The trade-offs are cost and discipline. You need twice the pins and twice the traces, and the pair has to be routed together with matched lengths and constant spacing; if the two halves arrive at different times, some of the differential signal turns into common-mode noise, and that wrecks the benefit. It also needs a differential receiver and usually a 100-ohm termination, and sometimes AC coupling with its own placement issues. So I use differential where speed, noise or EMI demand it, and single-ended for slow, short, cheap nets."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.** For line voltages $V_P, V_N$:
$$V_d=V_P-V_N,\qquad V_{cm}=\frac{V_P+V_N}{2}$$
Ideal differential driving has $V_P=V_{cm}+V_d/2$, $V_N=V_{cm}-V_d/2$.

**Impedances.** A coupled pair has two modes: *odd* (differential) with impedance $Z_{odd}$ per line and *even* (common) with $Z_{even}$. The differential impedance seen across the pair is $Z_{diff}=2Z_{odd}$ and the common-mode impedance (both lines driven together) is $Z_{cm}=Z_{even}/2$. Typical targets: 100 Ω diff for Ethernet, PCIe, SATA, LVDS, HDMI; 90 Ω for USB. Because coupling lowers $Z_{odd}$, a tightly coupled pair needs narrower traces to reach 100 Ω than two uncoupled 50 Ω traces ([[q0524]]).

**Noise rejection.** A common-mode disturbance $V_n$ appears on both lines; the receiver output is $A_d(V_d)+A_{cm}V_n$, where the ratio $A_d/A_{cm}$ is the CMRR (typically 30–60+ dB in a good receiver at low frequency, falling at high frequency). The rejection depends on the *balance* of the line: skew or length mismatch converts part of $V_d$ to $V_{cm}$ and vice versa (mode conversion, see [[q0520]]).

**Pros.** Noise immunity, tolerance to ground offset between boards, lower EMI (cancellation of equal and opposite currents), a more constant current draw (less supply noise), a switching threshold set by the other line rather than a fixed reference, precise timing at the crossing point, and lower swing for the same noise margin.

**Cons/trade-offs.** 2× pins and routing area; strict matching and symmetry; more complex termination (differential resistor, AC coupling, bias); less tolerance of interruptions in the pair; power consumption of constant-current drivers (LVDS: nominally ~3.5 mA into 100 Ω, ≈350 mV swing around ~1.2 V common-mode); mode conversion from skew and asymmetry; test points and probing are harder.

**Coupling trade-off.** *Tight coupling* gives more noise rejection, better immunity to plane disturbances and narrower, denser routing (but narrower traces → more copper loss and tighter fab tolerance). *Loose coupling* allows wider traces (lower loss), is more tolerant of fab variations, but each trace behaves more like a single-ended line and is more sensitive to the reference plane.
:::

::: short
"A differential pair is two complementary traces where the data is the voltage difference between them. Noise that hits both lines equally is common-mode and gets subtracted out, the opposite currents make the pair radiate less, and you get a bigger signal difference for a given swing. The costs are double the pins and traces, strict length matching and constant spacing, differential termination, and the fact that any skew converts signal into common-mode noise, so it must be routed carefully."
:::

::: long
"A differential pair is two traces carrying equal and opposite versions of a signal. The information is the difference between the two voltages, and the average of the two, the common-mode voltage, carries nothing. The receiver subtracts one from the other.

That gives several benefits. First, noise immunity. Crosstalk, ground shifts and EMI pickup usually couple equally into both traces, so they're common-mode and the subtraction cancels them. Second, EMI. The two currents are equal and opposite, so their magnetic and electric fields cancel in the far field, and the return current is carried by the partner trace rather than through the plane. Third, signal level. If each line swings plus or minus 200 millivolts, the receiver sees 400 millivolts of difference, so I can use lower swings, which helps speed, power and radiation. And since the receiver compares the two lines against each other, it isn't sensitive to a ground offset between the transmitter and receiver.

Now the trade-offs. It costs twice the pins, twice the traces and more routing area. Both traces need to be the same length, the same shape and the same environment: if there's skew, the edges don't line up and a chunk of the difference turns into common-mode, which both degrades the eye and radiates. It needs a differential receiver and a proper 100-ohm differential termination. I have to decide how tightly to couple them: tight coupling gives better rejection but narrower, lossier traces; loose coupling lets me use wider traces but makes each half more sensitive to the plane. And there are practical issues like AC-coupling capacitors, vias, and probing.

So I use differential for fast or noisy links, anywhere EMI or ground shifts matter, and single-ended for slow, short, local signals."
:::

::: terms
- **Differential voltage ($V_d$)**: $V_P-V_N$; the signal.
- **Common-mode voltage ($V_{cm}$)**: $(V_P+V_N)/2$; the average, which should carry no information.
- **CMRR**: common-mode rejection ratio $A_d/A_{cm}$, how well the receiver ignores common-mode.
- **Odd / even mode**: the two modes of a coupled pair (driven in opposition / together).
- **Differential impedance ($Z_{diff}$)**: impedance between the two lines, $2Z_{odd}$.
- **Skew**: timing difference between the two lines of a pair (intra-pair skew).
- **Mode conversion**: transformation of differential signal into common-mode (or vice versa) because of imbalance.
- **Tight / loose coupling**: small / large spacing relative to trace width and height.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "two wires carrying opposite signals, good for noise." An exceptional answer:
- Defines $V_d$ and $V_{cm}$ and states that the **receiver subtracts**, so common-mode noise cancels.
- Mentions **EMI cancellation and return current** in the partner trace, and **tolerance to ground offset**.
- Gives balanced **trade-offs** with specifics: 2× pins/area, skew → mode conversion, termination, AC coupling, tight vs loose coupling.
- Gives typical numbers: 100 Ω / 90 Ω diff impedances, LVDS 350 mV swing, CMRR in dB.
- Knows the practical conclusion: **balance is what matters**; the benefits vanish if the pair is asymmetric.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Differential signals have twice the voltage so need more power."** The swing per line is usually *smaller*; and a constant-current driver draws constant current.
- **"The receiver compares the signal with ground."** It compares the two lines against each other; ground offset only needs to stay within the receiver's common-mode range.
- **Thinking the pair needs a plane at all.** At high frequencies tightly coupled pairs return current through each other, but a nearby plane still sets impedance and shields. Don't route pairs over gaps.
- **Treating the two traces as independent 50 Ω lines.** Coupling changes impedance: $Z_{diff}\ne 2\times50$ Ω unless the pair is loosely coupled.
- **Assuming differential is always better.** For a slow GPIO or I²C line it adds pins and cost without benefit.
- **Ignoring skew.** A few ps of mismatch at 10 Gb/s makes real common-mode noise.
:::

## What "differential" means electrically

## Why noise cancels, and why layout decides whether it does

A nearby aggressor couples a voltage $v_{n,P}$ onto line P and $v_{n,N}$ onto line N. The receiver sees $v_{n,P}-v_{n,N}$ as differential noise and $(v_{n,P}+v_{n,N})/2$ as common-mode. If the pair is closely spaced and symmetric, the aggressor sees both lines at nearly the same distance and $v_{n,P}\approx v_{n,N}$, so the differential noise is small. If the pair is loose or one line is nearer the aggressor, the noise is unequal and the benefit is reduced. The same logic applies to ground bounce: both lines share the same reference, so a shift of the reference moves both.

A real receiver has finite CMRR. Suppose a 0.5 V common-mode disturbance and a 40 dB (100×) CMRR: the equivalent differential error is 5 mV, small compared with a 400 mV signal. A single-ended receiver with a 0.5 V noise would see the whole disturbance against a ~200 mV noise margin. Try it:

## EMI and return current

In a single-ended microstrip, the return current is in the plane under the trace; any plane gap or via transition breaks it ([[q0514]]). In a tightly coupled pair, the signal on P and the signal on N are equal and opposite: much of the return current for P flows in N and vice versa (odd mode). Far-field radiation from two equal and opposite currents separated by a small spacing $s$ scales with $s$ (dipole-like cancellation), so smaller spacing gives lower radiation. Even so, a continuous plane helps: it holds the impedance and shields, and the real path of odd-mode current is not entirely in the partner.

## What you give up

- **Area and pins.** Every differential channel costs two pins and two traces (plus spacing). A 16-bit single-ended bus becomes 32 pins as LVDS.
- **Discipline.** Constant spacing, symmetric route, matched length, matched vias, symmetric AC capacitors and connectors. See [[q0519]] and [[q0521]].
- **Mode conversion.** If P and N are skewed by $\Delta t$ on an edge of rise time $t_r$, a pulse of common-mode amplitude up to about $\tfrac{V_s}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\Delta t}{t_r}$ appears (for $\Delta t<t_r$ and single-ended swing $V_s$), and the differential eye closes by roughly $\Delta t$. See [[q0520]].
- **Power.** Current-mode drivers draw constant current regardless of data; LVDS-like standards use ~3.5 mA for a nominal 350 mV into 100 Ω, i.e. ~1.2 mW per line pair plus the receiver's bias.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> LVDS levels quoted here (≈350 mV swing, ≈1.2 V common-mode, ≈3.5 mA) are the nominal values from the TIA/EIA-644 family; individual parts and variants (sub-LVDS, SLVS, CML, LVPECL) use different swings and common-mode levels. Check the part's datasheet.

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Receiver common-mode range:** a differential receiver has an allowed range of $V_{cm}$ (for example 0–2.4 V for LVDS); outside it, CMRR collapses. That's why large ground offsets still break differential links.
- **AC-coupling** (series capacitors, e.g. 100 nF in PCIe/USB 3 channels) removes DC offset between transmitter and receiver, but the pads add capacitance, so the footprint and cut-outs need optimising.
- **Cables** for differential signals (twisted pair, shielded) preserve the balance the same way a layout does.
:::

::: applications
- **USB 2.0/3.x, Ethernet (100/1000BASE-T magnetics), PCIe, SATA, HDMI/DisplayPort**: all differential high-speed serial links.
- **LVDS camera/display links and ADC/DAC interfaces** (e.g. JESD204, LVDS parallel).
- **CAN and RS-485** in noisy industrial and automotive environments: differential for ground offset and EMI immunity.
- **Differential clocks** (LVPECL/LVDS clocks, DDR CK/CK#) for cleaner timing and reduced EMI.
- **Audio and instrumentation**: balanced lines (XLR microphones) and instrumentation amps use the same principle with common-mode rejection.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes the pair is **balanced**: equal lengths, identical environment, and a receiver with a decent CMRR. The benefits shrink with imbalance and at high frequency.
- Impedance numbers use an **FR-4 microstrip** model (IPC-2141 style approximation); the fab's field solver is the final authority.
- "Differential pair" here means **two-wire balanced signalling** on a PCB; the same word appears in analog contexts (differential amplifier pairs) with a different meaning.
- Some links (e.g. I²C, SPI at low speed) use single-ended signalling simply because it is adequate and cheaper.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a differential pair radiate less than two single-ended traces?
The two currents are equal and opposite and spaced closely, so their far-field contributions cancel, like a small dipole whose moment scales with spacing. Single-ended traces have return current that is spread in the plane, so the loop is bigger. Mismatch destroys the cancellation: common-mode current is what radiates.

### A pair is routed 3 mm longer on one side. What changes?
The longer trace's edges arrive about 18 ps later in microstrip ($3\times5.9$ ps). For a 5 Gb/s link (UI = 200 ps) that is 9% of a UI of skew, which reduces eye width and creates a common-mode pulse of up to about $\tfrac{V_s}{2}\tfrac{18\text{ ps}}{t_r}$. Compensate with a small meander on the shorter trace where the mismatch arises ([[q0521]]).

### Why 100 Ω? Isn't it two 50 Ω lines?
If the traces were far apart, the differential impedance would be about 2×50 = 100 Ω. When they are coupled the odd-mode impedance drops (coupling adds capacitance between the lines), so the pair has to be built with smaller geometry to hold $Z_{diff}=100$ Ω. Each trace's single-ended impedance to the plane is then about 55–60 Ω in a typical design.

### When would you choose single-ended over differential?
When the net is slow, short, on the same board, and noise margins are healthy (GPIO, SPI < 20 MHz, I²C). Differential costs pins, area and routing time. It starts to pay off when edges are fast, the link is long or leaves the board, or EMI/ground shifts are a concern.

### What if the ground potential differs by 3 V between two boards?
A differential receiver only needs $V_{cm}$ within its input range. A 3 V offset exceeds LVDS's range (≈0–2.4 V). I'd use a transceiver rated for it (e.g. RS-485 allows about −7 to +12 V) or isolate the link galvanically, with digital isolators or transformers/capacitors.
:::
