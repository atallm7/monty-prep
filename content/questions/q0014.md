::: testing
Whether you can define resistance **physically**, not just as "V over I". The interviewer wants a layered answer: the operational definition ($R = V/I$), the material picture (electrons scattering as they drift), the geometric formula ($R=\rho L/A$), and the energy picture (resistance converts electrical energy to heat). They are also listening for the difference between **resistance** (a property of an object) and **resistivity** (a property of a material), and for whether you know that $R=V/I$ is a definition that always works, while "$V=IR$ with constant $R$" is only true for ohmic materials.
:::

::: why
Resistance is the first concept in circuits, and nearly everything builds on it: Ohm's law, power loss, voltage dividers, wire sizing, PCB trace design, thermal design, and the impedance concept. When you can explain it physically, the other topics (why copper traces get hot, why a wire's resistance rises with temperature, why a semiconductor behaves differently) become consequences rather than facts to memorize. It also shows up as a quick check on whether a candidate understands electrical and material fundamentals or just formulas.
:::

::: concepts
- **Charge, current and voltage**: current is charge flow, $I = dQ/dt$; voltage is energy per unit charge. See [[q0001]].
- **Ohm's law** ([[q0001]], [[q0002]]): for many materials the current is proportional to the voltage.
- **Resistivity $\rho$ and conductivity $\sigma = 1/\rho$**: intrinsic material properties.
- **Geometry**: longer conductors resist more, thicker ones less: $R=\rho L/A$.
- **Joule heating**: power dissipated is $P = I^2R = V^2/R$ ([[q0006]]).
- **Conductors, insulators, semiconductors** ([[q0055]]): they differ by many orders of magnitude in resistivity.
:::

::: answer
"Resistance is how much a piece of material opposes current flow. Operationally it's the ratio of the voltage across it to the current through it, R equals V over I, in ohms. One ohm is one volt per amp.

Physically, when you apply a voltage you create an electric field that accelerates the free electrons, but they keep scattering off lattice vibrations, impurities and defects, so instead of accelerating forever they reach a steady average drift speed. The more they scatter, the more voltage you need for the same current, so the higher the resistance. All that scattering turns electrical energy into heat, which is why current through a resistance dissipates I squared R.

For a uniform conductor, resistance is rho times length over area, where rho is the material's resistivity. Doubling the length doubles it, doubling the cross section halves it. Copper is about 1.7 times ten to the minus eight ohm-meters, glass is more than ten orders of magnitude higher.

And it's worth saying that V over I is a definition, while Ohm's law is the observation that for many materials this ratio is constant. A diode or a lamp filament has a V over I that changes with the operating point."
:::

::: technical
**Definition:** $R = V/I$ (ohms, $1\ \Omega = 1\ \text{V/A}$). Conductance $G = 1/R$ (siemens).

**Geometry:** for a uniform bar of length $L$ and cross-section $A$,
$$R = \rho\,\frac{L}{A},\qquad \sigma = \frac{1}{\rho} = n q \mu,$$
with carrier density $n$, charge $q$ and mobility $\mu$.

**Microscopic (Drude) picture:** $\rho = \dfrac{m}{n e^2 \tau}$, where $\tau$ is the mean time between scattering events. For copper, $n\approx 8.5\times10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3}$ and $\tau\approx 2.5\times 10^{-14}$ s give $\rho\approx 1.7\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,$m. The drift velocity in 1 A through 1 mm² of copper is $v_d = I/(nqA)\approx 0.07$ mm/s.

**Energy:** $P = VI = I^2 R = V^2/R$, converted to heat (Joule heating).

**Ohmic vs. non-ohmic:** a device is *ohmic* if $V$–$I$ is a straight line through the origin. For a non-ohmic device we distinguish **static resistance** $V/I$ at an operating point and **dynamic (small-signal) resistance** $r = dV/dI$.

Typical resistivity (20 °C): silver $1.6\times10^{-8}$, copper $1.7\times10^{-8}$, aluminum $2.7\times10^{-8}$, nichrome about $1.1\times10^{-6}\ \Omega$m; doped silicon varies over many decades with doping; glass and ceramics can exceed $10^{10}\ \Omega$m.
:::

::: short
"Resistance is the opposition to current: R equals V over I, measured in ohms. Physically, electrons drift through a material and keep scattering off the lattice and impurities, which limits the current for a given voltage and turns energy into heat. For a uniform wire it's rho times length over area. V over I is a definition; Ohm's law says it's constant for ohmic materials like metals, but not for devices like diodes."
:::

::: long
"I think of resistance at three levels. At the circuit level, it's the ratio of voltage across a component to the current through it. R equals V over I, in ohms. One ohm is a volt per amp.

At the material level, it comes from how electrons move. When there's a voltage across a conductor, there's an electric field that pushes the free electrons. But they constantly collide with lattice vibrations, impurities and defects, so they don't accelerate forever. They settle at an average drift velocity that's proportional to the field, and that proportionality is mobility. More collisions, lower mobility, higher resistivity. Those collisions are where electrical energy turns into heat, which is the I squared R power loss.

At the geometry level, a uniform conductor has R equals rho L over A. Resistivity rho belongs to the material, resistance R belongs to the specific piece. So a meter of 1 square millimeter copper is about 17 milliohms, and the same cross-section in nichrome is about 1.1 ohms.

Two refinements. First, resistance changes: copper's resistivity rises about 0.39 percent per degree, so a hot wire has higher resistance. Second, V over I is always a valid definition, but Ohm's law, meaning that the ratio is constant, only holds for ohmic devices. For a diode I'd talk about dynamic resistance, which is the slope dV over dI at the operating point."
:::

::: terms
- **Resistance $R$**: opposition of an object to current, $V/I$, in ohms.
- **Resistivity $\rho$**: intrinsic material property, in Ω·m.
- **Conductance $G$ and conductivity $\sigma$**: reciprocals of $R$ and $\rho$.
- **Mobility $\mu$**: drift velocity per unit electric field, $v_d=\mu E$.
- **Drift velocity**: the net average velocity of carriers in a field (typically mm/s in copper).
- **Scattering time $\tau$**: mean time between carrier collisions.
- **Joule heating**: conversion of electrical energy to heat in a resistance.
- **Ohmic / non-ohmic**: linear / nonlinear $V$–$I$ relationship.
- **Dynamic resistance**: $dV/dI$ at an operating point.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "resistance is opposition to current, R = V/I".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the definition *and* the physical mechanism: scattering that limits drift velocity and produces heat.
- Separates resistance (object) from resistivity (material) and gives $R=\rho L/A$ with a number (copper ~17 mΩ per meter-mm²).
- Notes that $V/I$ is a definition and Ohm's law is an empirical property of ohmic materials; mentions static vs. dynamic resistance.
- Mentions that metals' resistance rises with temperature while semiconductors' falls, linking to [[q0015]].
- Connects to power ($I^2R$) and why it matters for design.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Resistance is the same as resistivity."** The first is for an object, the second for a material. A copper wire's $R$ depends on its length and area; copper's $\rho$ does not.
- **"Resistance stops current."** It limits current for a given voltage; with zero resistance (a short) current is limited by the source and wiring instead.
- **"The electrons travel at the speed of light."** Drift velocity is ~0.07 mm/s for 1 A in 1 mm² of copper. The *signal* (field) propagates near the speed of light; individual carriers move slowly.
- **"R = V/I means R depends on V and I."** For an ohmic resistor $R$ is fixed; V and I are linked through it. R is changed by temperature, geometry and material, not by the applied voltage.
- **Applying $V=IR$ with constant $R$ to a diode or lamp.** Use the operating point and dynamic resistance instead.
:::

::: deep
## From electrons to the ohm

Consider a conductor with free carriers of charge $q$ and density $n$. In an electric field $E$, a carrier is accelerated, $a = qE/m$, but after a mean time $\tau$ it scatters and its drift is reset. The average drift velocity is then
$$v_d = \frac{qE\tau}{m}\equiv \mu E,\qquad \mu = \frac{q\tau}{m}.$$
Current density is $J = nqv_d = (nq\mu)E = \sigma E$. For a bar of length $L$ and area $A$, $E = V/L$ and $I = JA$, so
$$I = \sigma\frac{A}{L}V\;\Longrightarrow\;R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{L}{\sigma A} = \rho\frac{L}{A}.$$
This is Ohm's law derived from a model, and it shows exactly what sets $\rho$: carrier density $n$, and scattering time $\tau$ (through mobility). Copper has a huge $n\approx8.5\times10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3}$. Doped silicon has $n$ perhaps $10^{21}$–$10^{25}\ \text{m}^{-3}$, so it is a much worse conductor but with *tunable* resistivity ([[q0055]]).

> [!NOTE]
> Drift is slow: in 1 A through a 1 mm² copper wire $v_d = I/(nqA) = 1/(8.5\times10^{28}\cdot1.6\times10^{-19}\cdot10^{-6}) = 0.073$ mm/s. A change in the field propagates at a good fraction of $c$, which is why a light turns on instantly.

## Where the energy goes

Each scattering event transfers the kinetic energy the carrier gained from the field to the lattice (heat). Power per volume is $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} = \rho J^2$; integrating over the bar yields $P = I^2R = VI$. This explains why a resistor warms up and why wiring has a current rating based on temperature rise ([[q0007]]).

## Worked example: how much is a wire's resistance?

1 m of copper wire with area 0.5 mm² has
$$R = \frac{1.68\times10^{-8}\cdot 1}{0.5\times10^{-6}} = 33.6\ \text{m}\Omega.$$
Carrying 10 A: $V = 0.336$ V and $P = I^2R = 3.4$ W. For a 12 V system that is a 2.8 % drop and 3.4 W of heat in the wire. A PCB trace of 5 mil width and 2 in length in 1 oz copper (35 µm) is $R=\rho/t\cdot L/w = (1.68\times10^{-8}/35\times10^{-6})\cdot(2/0.005) = 0.19\ \Omega$. Here $\rho/t = 0.48\ \text{m}\Omega/\square$ is the **sheet resistance**.

## Why temperature changes $\rho$: the mechanism and a worked number

Scattering has two sources. Static defects and impurities scatter electrons by a roughly temperature-independent amount; lattice vibrations (phonons) scatter more as the lattice gets hotter. Because the scattering rates add, so do the resistivities, which is **Matthiessen's rule**:

$$\rho(T) \approx \rho_{defects} + \rho_{phonon}(T)$$

Near room temperature the phonon term is close to linear in $T$ for metals, so engineers use the linearized form

$$\rho(T) = \rho_0\,[1 + \alpha\,(T - T_0)],\qquad \alpha_{Cu}\approx 0.0039\ \text{K}^{-1}$$

You can turn this around and use a winding as its own thermometer. A motor winding measures 2.00 Ω at 20 °C. After running it reads 2.50 Ω. Then $R/R_0 = 1.25$ and

$$T = 20 + \frac{1.25 - 1}{0.00393} = 83.6\ \text{°C}$$

That is how many standards measure average winding temperature without a sensor buried in the coil. A full 80 K rise (20 °C to 100 °C) multiplies copper resistance by $1 + 0.00393\times 80 = 1.31$, so a 2 Ω winding becomes 2.63 Ω. For a motor driven from a constant voltage this means about 24% less current and less torque when hot. For a motor driven from a constant current it means 31% more $I^2R$ heating, which is a positive feedback you design around.

> [!NOTE]
> The linear model is an approximation. It is fine over a few tens of kelvin around the reference temperature, but resistivity curves flatten at cryogenic temperatures, where defect scattering dominates, and the numbers for $\alpha$ depend on purity and alloy.

## A sense of scale: how far does an electron go between collisions?

Drift speed is millimeters per second, but electrons are not sitting still between collisions. The relevant speed for scattering is the Fermi velocity, which for copper is about $1.57\times10^{6}$ m/s (a standard textbook value from the free-electron model). With $\tau = 2.5\times10^{-14}$ s the mean free path is

$$\ell = v_F\tau \approx 1.57\times10^{6}\times 2.5\times10^{-14} \approx 39\ \text{nm}$$

and each electron scatters about $4\times10^{13}$ times per second. Two lessons follow. First, a net drift of 0.07 mm/s is a tiny bias on top of a very fast random motion, so $R$ stays constant over enormous current ranges until heating intervenes. Second, once a conductor's cross-section is only a few tens of nanometers (on-chip interconnect), surfaces and grain boundaries scatter electrons as much as the bulk does, and the effective resistivity rises above the bulk value. That is one reason the smallest wires on a chip are worse than $\rho L/A$ predicts.

## Semiconductors: same law, different knobs

For a semiconductor $\sigma = q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$, so you can change $\rho$ by orders of magnitude by changing $n$ with doping. As a rough illustration, silicon doped with $10^{16}\ \text{cm}^{-3}$ donors and a mobility of order $10^3\ \text{cm}^2/\text{V·s}$ has $\rho = 1/(q n \mu) \approx 0.6\ \Omega\,$cm, roughly seven orders of magnitude above copper in the same units ($1.7\times10^{-6}\ \Omega\,$cm).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Mobility depends on doping level, temperature and carrier type, so the 0.6 Ω·cm figure is an order-of-magnitude example. Use a measured resistivity-versus-doping curve for a real design.

At moderate temperatures the competition between more carriers and lower mobility goes the other way from a metal: the carrier count rises exponentially and wins, hence the falling resistance of an NTC thermistor and the risk of thermal runaway ([[q0015]]).

## Worked example: when "1 mΩ" is mostly your leads

You want to measure a 1 mΩ shunt carrying 10 A. The correct signal is $10\ \text{A}\times 1\ \text{m}\Omega = 10$ mV. A two-wire ohmmeter that forces a test current through the same two leads sees the shunt plus 0.2 Ω of leads and contacts and displays 201 mΩ, which is 200 times too high. The Kelvin (four-wire) connection uses one pair to force the current and a separate pair of high-impedance sense leads on the resistor's body. Almost no current flows in the sense leads, so their resistance produces no drop and the reading is 1.000 mΩ. On a PCB the same idea means routing the amplifier's sense traces to the inner edges of the shunt's pads, not to the thick copper that carries the load current ([[q0225]]).

Remember that at these levels thermal EMFs at dissimilar-metal junctions (of order microvolts) matter. A DMM at a 1 mA test current produces only 1 µV across 1 mΩ, so bench meters with offset compensation reverse the current and average the two readings.

## How to verify resistance behavior on the bench

1. **Sweep and plot**: apply a few voltages with a bench supply, record $I$, and plot $V$ versus $I$. A straight line through the origin means ohmic. A line that curves upward in resistance at higher current is self-heating, which is why you keep the test power small.
2. **Check geometry**: measure the length and width of a long trace or wire and compare with $\rho L/A$. A big mismatch usually points to plating thickness, a narrower etched width, or contact resistance.
3. **Check temperature dependence**: measure a copper coil cold, warm it with a hair dryer or a heat gun at a low setting, and confirm $R$ rises by about 0.39% per kelvin. The same experiment on an NTC thermistor shows the opposite sign.
4. **Look for the offset**: a line that does not pass through the origin means a series source or a threshold, as in the last follow-up question.

## The analogy and where it fails

The water-pipe analogy (pressure = voltage, flow = current, narrow pipe = resistor) is good for series and parallel intuition and for power. It fails for inductors, capacitors in AC circuits, and the fact that heat in a resistor arises from scattering, not from friction in a visible fluid. Use it to build intuition, and leave it behind when analyzing frequency-dependent behavior.

## Resistance is not always "R"

In AC and high-frequency circuits the general quantity is impedance, $Z = R + jX$; $R$ is the real (dissipative) part. Real resistors have parasitic inductance and capacitance ([[q0016]]), and the AC resistance of a wire is higher than its DC resistance because of skin effect ([[q0056]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Wire and PCB trace sizing**: voltage drop and heating from $R=\rho L/A$.
- **Heating elements**: toasters, hair dryers and 3D-printer hotends use nichrome for its high $\rho$ and stability.
- **Sense resistors**: milliohm resistors convert current into a measurable voltage ([[q0225]]).
- **Strain gauges**: stretching a conductor changes $L$ and $A$, hence $R$.
- **Sensors**: thermistors, photoresistors and RTDs (platinum) all use $R$'s dependence on a physical variable.
- **Current limiting and dividers**: LED resistors and bias networks.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Uniform, homogeneous conductor** at a fixed temperature for $R=\rho L/A$.
- **DC or low frequency**, so the current distribution is uniform. At high frequency, skin effect raises resistance.
- **Ohmic behavior** assumed for a "resistor"; the definition $R=V/I$ still holds for nonlinear parts as a static resistance.
- **Classical (Drude) picture** is good for metals at room temperature; semiconductors need band theory and have both electrons and holes ($\sigma = q(n\mu_n + p\mu_p)$).
- Another valid answer: define resistance purely operationally, as in a lab measurement with a four-wire method.
:::

::: followups
### What is the resistance of 100 m of 24 AWG copper wire?
24 AWG has diameter 0.511 mm, area $2.05\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2$. $R = 1.68\times10^{-8}\cdot100/2.05\times10^{-7} = 8.2\ \Omega$ (about 82 mΩ/m; wire tables list roughly 84 mΩ/m at 20 °C, and the small difference is just the resistivity value used). For a 1 A load this drops about 8 V, which is why long thin cables are bad for low-voltage power.

### Why does the resistance of a metal increase with temperature while a semiconductor's decreases?
In metals the carrier density is essentially fixed, but lattice vibrations increase with temperature and scatter electrons more (lower $\tau$), so $\rho$ rises. In semiconductors, heat generates more carriers (higher $n$), which outweighs the extra scattering, so $\rho$ falls. That is why NTC thermistors exist and why semiconductors can show thermal runaway.

### What is the difference between resistance and impedance?
Resistance is the real, dissipative part, independent of frequency to first order. Impedance $Z=R+jX$ includes reactance from capacitors and inductors, which stores and returns energy and shifts phase. A pure resistor has $X=0$.

### How do you measure a very small resistance (like 1 mΩ) accurately?
Use a four-wire (Kelvin) measurement: force a known current through two leads and sense the voltage with two other leads, so lead and contact resistance don't contribute. A DMM's two-wire measurement would include perhaps 0.1–0.5 Ω of lead resistance. See [[q0225]] for current sensing.

### What if the I–V curve is a straight line but doesn't pass through the origin?
Then it's not a pure resistor: there's an offset, such as a battery in series or a diode threshold. The slope gives the dynamic resistance, but $V/I$ is not constant and Ohm's law in its simple form does not apply.
:::

"What is resistance?" can be answered at the circuit level (V/I), physical level (scattering), or materials level ($\rho L/A$). The best answer gives all three briefly. A follow-up usually follows ([[q0015]]: what does it depend on?). The 24 AWG number above is a worked example, not a table value; use a wire table for exact values.
