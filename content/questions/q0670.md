::: testing
Whether you understand aliasing as an **irreversible** consequence of undersampling: frequencies above $f_s/2$ fold back and masquerade as lower ones. The interviewer wants the formula for the apparent frequency, a physical example, the prevention (analog anti-alias filter before sampling, plus oversampling), and a feel for where it bites in real hardware: ADC readings contaminated by switching ripple, noise folding, scope displays, and decimation. Hidden sub-skills: reading a spectrum with images, diagnosing aliasing by changing $f_s$, and noise-bandwidth thinking.
:::

::: why
Aliasing is a classic source of "impossible" measurements: a 500 kHz switching ripple that shows up as a slow drift on a 100 kS/s ADC, a scope showing a stable but wrong frequency, a sensor reading that changes when you change the sampling interval. Because it cannot be removed after sampling, you must prevent it in the analog domain. It follows directly from the Nyquist criterion ([[q0669]]) and is the main reason every real ADC system has a filter in front of it ([[q0668]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Sampling replicates the spectrum**: copies appear at multiples of $f_s$; overlap means aliasing. See [[q0669]].
- **Nyquist frequency**: $f_s/2$, the folding point; frequencies above it fold back.
- **Apparent (alias) frequency**: $f_a=|f-kf_s|$ for the integer $k$ that places $f_a$ in $[0,f_s/2]$.
- **Anti-alias filter**: analog low-pass before the sampler; cannot be replaced by digital filtering afterwards.
- **Noise folding**: all broadband noise above $f_s/2$ also folds into the band.
- **Decimation**: reducing sample rate digitally needs a low-pass filter first, or you alias again.
:::

::: answer
"Aliasing is what happens when you sample a signal too slowly: frequencies above half the sample rate don't just disappear, they fold back and show up as lower frequencies in your data, and once that happens you can't tell the alias from a real signal at that lower frequency.

The simplest example: sample a 900 hertz sine at 1000 samples per second. The samples are exactly the same as those of a 100 hertz sine, so the data says 100 hertz. In general the apparent frequency is the absolute difference between the signal frequency and the nearest multiple of the sample rate. A 1200 hertz tone sampled at 1000 comes out at 200; 2300 comes out at 300.

You see it in everyday life as the wagon wheel effect in films, where the wheel seems to spin slowly or backwards, and in moiré patterns in images. In hardware it shows up when, say, a 500 kilohertz buck converter ripple is sampled by a 100 kilosample-per-second ADC: it aliases to DC and looks like a constant offset error. And also all the broadband noise above the Nyquist frequency folds into your band and raises the noise floor.

The fix is to prevent it before sampling: put an analog low-pass anti-alias filter ahead of the ADC with a corner below half the sample rate, and often oversample so that the filter can be gentle, then decimate digitally. A quick test for aliasing is to change the sample rate: a real signal stays put, an alias moves."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism.** Sampling at $f_s$ cannot distinguish $e^{j2\pi ft}$ from $e^{j2\pi(f+kf_s)t}$ for integer $k$, since at $t=n/f_s$ they are identical. Therefore any frequency maps to a unique value in $[0,f_s/2]$:

$$f_a=\left|f-f_s\cdot\mathrm{round}\!\left(\frac{f}{f_s}\right)\right|.$$

Examples at $f_s=1000$ Hz: 900 → 100; 1200 → 200; 2300 → 300; 500 (exactly $f_s/2$) → 500, with phase-dependent amplitude.

**Phase.** The alias can be sign-inverted: a 900 Hz sine appears as $-\sin(2\pi\cdot100\,t)$.

**Noise folding.** For white noise density $e_n$ (V/√Hz) over bandwidth $B_n\gg f_s/2$ with no filter, all of it folds into $0\ldots f_s/2$ so the in-band noise is $e_n\sqrt{B_n}$ rather than $e_n\sqrt{f_s/2}$. With $e_n=10$ nV/√Hz, $B_n=10$ MHz, $f_s=100$ kS/s: 31.6 µV rms unfiltered vs 2.24 µV if band-limited to 50 kHz, a factor of $\sqrt{10^7/5\times10^4}=14$.

**Interference.** A tone at $f_i$ lands at $|f_i-kf_s|$: a 500 kHz ripple sampled at 100 kS/s aliases to 0 Hz; at 480 kHz it aliases to 20 kHz.

**Prevention.**
1. Analog anti-alias filter with attenuation $\ge A$ dB at $f_s-f_{max}$ and above ([[q0669]]).
2. Oversample so the transition band is wide; then decimate with a digital filter.
3. Synchronise sampling with the interferer (e.g., sample once per PWM period at a fixed point).
4. Choose $f_s$ so known interferers alias to harmless frequencies.

**Detection.** Change $f_s$ slightly: true components remain at the same frequency; aliases move (by $k\,\Delta f_s$).

**Decimation.** Reducing the rate by $M$ requires a low-pass at $f_s/(2M)$ before discarding samples.
:::

::: long
"Aliasing is the distortion you get when you sample a signal at less than twice its highest frequency. Instead of being lost, frequency components above half the sample rate fold back down and appear at a lower frequency in your sampled data, indistinguishable from a real signal at that frequency.

The math is simple: sampling at fs can't tell a frequency f from f plus any multiple of fs, because at the sample instants they take identical values. So the apparent frequency is the absolute difference between f and the nearest multiple of fs. If I sample a 900 hertz sine at 1000 samples per second, I get exactly the same numbers as a 100 hertz sine, and the 100 hertz is what shows up in the data. A 1200 hertz tone shows as 200, a 2300 hertz tone as 300.

You've seen it before: the wagon wheel that appears to rotate backwards in a film, because the frame rate is too low for the spoke rate, and moiré patterns in photos of fine patterns. In electronics, a classic one is switching ripple. A buck converter at 500 kilohertz creates ripple on a rail; if my ADC samples that rail at 100 kilosamples per second, the 500 kilohertz aliases to exactly zero and looks like a DC offset in my reading; at 480 kilohertz it appears as a 20 kilohertz tone. Likewise broadband noise above the Nyquist frequency folds into the band: ten nanovolts per root hertz of noise out to 10 megahertz, folded into a 50 kilohertz band, gives about 14 times more noise than if I'd filtered first.

You can't fix it in software after the fact, so you prevent it. Put an analog anti-alias low-pass filter in front of the ADC with its corner below half the sample rate. Often oversample, say 4 times or more, so that a simple filter is enough, then filter and decimate digitally. For known interferers like PWM, you can synchronise the sampling to them. And to check if something is an alias, change the sample rate: real signals stay at the same frequency, aliases move."
:::

::: terms
- **Aliasing**: false lower frequency created when a signal above $f_s/2$ is sampled.
- **Alias / apparent frequency**: $f_a=|f-kf_s|$.
- **Folding frequency**: the Nyquist frequency $f_s/2$.
- **Anti-alias filter**: analog low-pass placed before the ADC.
- **Noise folding**: broadband noise above $f_s/2$ aliasing into the band.
- **Decimation**: lowering the sample rate after digital filtering.
- **Oversampling**: sampling faster than needed to ease filtering.
- **Image (reconstruction)**: replica of the spectrum at multiples of $f_s$ produced by a DAC.
- **Moiré / wagon-wheel effect**: aliasing in images and video.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "when you sample too slowly you get false frequencies." An exceptional answer:
- Gives the folding formula and a worked example (900 Hz at 1 kHz → 100 Hz; also the sign inversion).
- Explains why it's irreversible: all $f+kf_s$ produce identical samples.
- Gives a hardware example with numbers (500 kHz ripple at 100 kS/s → DC; noise folding factor of 14).
- Lists prevention: analog filter, oversampling and decimation, synchronous sampling, choosing $f_s$.
- Offers a diagnostic: change $f_s$ and see which components move.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"I'll just filter it digitally afterwards."** After sampling the alias is indistinguishable from signal.
- **Forgetting noise.** Noise above $f_s/2$ aliases too; a "clean" signal can have a polluted baseband.
- **Only worrying about the signal's frequency.** Interferers (switching ripple, clock harmonics) set the real requirement.
- **Decimating without filtering.** Dropping samples (taking every $M$th) aliases the content above the new Nyquist.
- **Confusing aliasing with imaging.** Aliasing happens at the sampler; imaging is the replica spectrum at a DAC output.
:::

::: deep
## The folding picture

For a real signal sampled at $f_s$, the input frequency axis folds like an accordion at $f_s/2$, $f_s$, $3f_s/2$, and so on. Every input frequency maps onto the baseband $[0,f_s/2]$:

$$f_a(f)=\left|f-f_s\,\mathrm{round}(f/f_s)\right|.$$

## Seeing it in the time domain

Why are 900 Hz and 100 Hz indistinguishable at fs = 1000 Hz? Sample instants are $t_n=n\cdot1$ ms. A 900 Hz sine advances $0.9$ cycles per sample, which is the same as stepping back $0.1$ cycle each sample:

$$\sin(2\pi\cdot900\cdot n/1000)=\sin(2\pi\,0.9\,n)=\sin(2\pi n-2\pi\,0.1\,n)=-\sin(2\pi\,0.1\,n).$$

## Where it bites in hardware

**1. Switching ripple and clock noise into an ADC.** A buck converter's ripple at $f_{sw}$ appears at $|f_{sw}-kf_s|$. With $f_{sw}=500$ kHz and $f_s=100$ kS/s, $k=5$ gives $|500-500|=0$: a DC offset. If the converter drifts to 480 kHz the alias is 20 kHz, and if it jitters (spread-spectrum), the alias wanders. A designer might think the sensor is noisy when in fact the supply ripple is aliasing. Fixes: RC filter at the ADC input, synchronise the ADC sampling to the converter's clock, or choose $f_s$ so the alias lands out of band.

**2. Noise folding.** Resistor and amplifier noise is broadband. Suppose a front end has $e_n=10$ nV/√Hz and 10 MHz of bandwidth, and the ADC samples at 100 kS/s. Without an anti-alias filter, every Nyquist zone folds into the first: the noise is $10\ \text{nV}\sqrt{10^7}=31.6$ µV rms. With a filter limiting the bandwidth to 50 kHz it is $10\ \text{nV}\sqrt{5\times10^4}=2.24$ µV rms, 14× lower, almost 4 bits of resolution.

**3. Oscilloscope displays.** If the scope's sample rate is too low for the timebase (or in a deep memory/decimating mode), a fast signal can appear as a stable slow waveform. The trigger and the frequency counter read wrongly. If the displayed frequency changes when you change the time/div, suspect aliasing. Modern scopes warn or use peak-detect mode.

**4. Digital downsampling.** Taking every 4th sample of 100 kS/s data without a low-pass first folds content from 12.5–50 kHz into 0–12.5 kHz. Always filter, then decimate.

**5. Video, images and mechanical systems.** Frame rate 24 fps filming a wheel whose spokes pass at 23 Hz appears to turn at $|23-24|=1$ Hz, slowly (backward, since the folding inverts direction). Fine patterns on a camera sensor create moiré; cameras use optical low-pass filters for the same reason an ADC has an electrical one.

**6. Control systems.** A digital controller sampling a sensor with vibration at 700 Hz at a 1 kHz loop rate sees a 300 Hz disturbance that is not really there, and the controller may respond to it.

## A worked anti-alias filter sizing

Suppose a 12-bit ADC samples a 5 kHz-bandwidth sensor signal at $f_s=100$ kS/s. Interference at the alias frequencies must be pushed below one LSB, i.e. about $6.02\times12+1.76=74$ dB down. The first frequency that folds onto the top of the band is $f_s-B=95$ kHz, so the filter needs 74 dB attenuation at 95 kHz with a corner near 5 kHz. The ratio is $95/5=19$, and each pole of a Butterworth filter gives roughly $20\log_{10}19=25.6$ dB at that ratio, so:

- one pole (a single RC): 25.6 dB, nowhere near enough;
- two poles: about 51 dB, still short;
- three poles: about 77 dB, enough. A 3rd-order active or passive filter, or a 2nd-order filter plus the ADC's own digital decimation, meets the target.

Raising the sample rate to 400 kS/s moves the first alias to 395 kHz (ratio 79, 38 dB per pole), so two poles (76 dB) suffice: that is the quantitative reason oversampling lets a simpler analog filter do the job. In both cases the filter must sit right at the ADC input so that noise picked up on the cable or traces is also removed.

## Preventing aliasing: the design recipe

1. Decide the signal band $B$ you care about.
2. Pick $f_s$ well above $2B$ (typically 2.5–10×).
3. Place an analog low-pass with $f_c\approx B$–$1.2B$ and enough attenuation (set by the ADC's resolution: ~6 dB per bit) at $f_s-B$.
4. If the ADC and filter are separated by a long cable, filter at the ADC, not at the source, to catch interference picked up en route.
5. If decimating digitally, use a decimation filter with the same stopband requirement.

> [!TIP]
> A handy rule: to get an $N$-bit-accurate measurement, out-of-band interferers should be attenuated by at least $6.02N+1.76$ dB at their alias frequencies. For a 12-bit ADC, that's about 74 dB.

## Aliasing is not always bad

Intentional aliasing is a tool. Bandpass sampling ([[q0669]]) deliberately aliases a narrow high-frequency band down to baseband, replacing a mixer. Sampling scopes use equivalent-time sampling of repetitive signals. And a stroboscope uses aliasing to make fast rotation look slow so you can inspect it.
:::

::: applications
- **Data acquisition and sensor systems**: input RC or active filters ahead of the ADC to stop switching noise from aliasing.
- **Audio converters**: analog filters plus oversampling and digital decimation filters.
- **Oscilloscopes and spectrum analyzers**: anti-alias filtering in the front end and warnings when sample rate is too low.
- **Motor control and power electronics**: synchronous current sampling at the PWM midpoint to avoid sampling switching noise.
- **Imaging**: optical low-pass filters (or sensor blur) to avoid moiré.
:::

::: assumptions
- The folding formula assumes uniform sampling of a real signal. Non-uniform or randomised sampling spreads aliases into noise instead.
- Noise folding computation assumes white noise and an ideal sampler; real front ends have 1/f noise and finite bandwidth.
- The anti-alias attenuation rule ($6.02N+1.76$ dB) is a design heuristic for quantization-limited systems.
- "Aliasing" here refers to temporal sampling; the same mathematics applies to spatial sampling (pixels, array sensors).
- Valid alternative framing: aliasing as a consequence of the periodicity of the discrete-time frequency axis ($\omega$ and $\omega+2\pi$ are identical).
:::

::: followups
### How can you tell whether a low-frequency component in your data is real or an alias?
Change the sampling rate by a small amount (or the timebase on a scope). A real component stays at the same frequency; an alias shifts, because its apparent frequency is $|f-kf_s|$. You can also add a (temporary) analog low-pass filter and see whether the component disappears, or compute the possible alias frequencies from suspected sources such as switching regulators and clocks.

### What does a 60 kHz tone look like when sampled at 100 kS/s?
Nyquist frequency is 50 kHz, so 60 kHz folds to $|60-100|=40$ kHz. The data shows a 40 kHz tone. If an unfiltered 40 kHz tone is also present, the two are indistinguishable and add.

### Why can't you remove aliasing with a digital filter after the ADC?
Because the alias lies in the same frequency range as legitimate signals. The ADC output contains no information about what the original frequency was, so no processing can separate them. Only an analog filter before sampling, or knowledge of the system's spectrum, can.

### What is the difference between aliasing and imaging?
Aliasing occurs at the *sampler*: out-of-band input folds into the band. Imaging occurs at the *DAC output*: the sampled spectrum has replicas at multiples of $f_s$, which a reconstruction low-pass filter must remove. Both are manifestations of spectrum replication.

### Scenario: your 12-bit ADC at 200 kS/s reads a slow temperature sensor, but readings have a ±5-count wobble that changes when you adjust the board's switching regulator frequency. What do you do?
That is a classic alias: the regulator's ripple (hundreds of kHz) folds into the ADC band, and changing its frequency moves the alias. I'd add an RC anti-alias filter at the ADC input (for a slow sensor, a corner of a few kHz), put the sensor ground return away from the switcher, and if possible synchronise ADC sampling to the regulator clock or average over an integer number of switching periods. Then verify by checking that the wobble no longer depends on the regulator frequency.
:::
