::: basics
### In plain words
The voltage across the inductor in a buck flips sign every cycle. While the switch is on, it is positive and equals the input minus the output, so the current rises. While the switch is off, it is negative and equals minus the output, so the current falls. In steady state the two balance out.

### Picture it
Think of climbing stairs. You take big steps up for a short time, then small steps down for a long time, and end each cycle at the same height. The step size is the voltage, the time is the duration, and the height is the current. Big-short up must cancel small-long down, or you would drift upward forever.

### Words you need
- **Polarity**: which end of the inductor is more positive.
- **Switch node**: the inductor's input-side end, which swings with the switch.
- **Inductor voltage**: the voltage across the coil, which sets the current's slope ([[q0041]]).
- **Volt-seconds**: voltage multiplied by time, the quantity that must balance.
- **Steady state**: the cycle repeats exactly, with no drift.

### The simplest example
Use 12 V in, 3 V out and 25 percent duty. During the on-time the inductor sees $12 - 3 = +9$ V for 25 percent of the cycle, which is $9 \times 0.25 = 2.25$ volt-fractions. During the off-time it sees $-3$ V for 75 percent, which is $-3 \times 0.75 = -2.25$. They cancel. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page explains why the voltage flips, with the inductor trying to keep its current going, and how the node swings to do it.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can assign the voltage polarity across the inductor in each state from first principles ($v=L\,di/dt$ and KVL), and explain *why it flips* in the off state: the inductor is trying to keep its current constant. The common weak answers are "the inductor is positive on the left" (without saying when) or "the polarity reverses because the diode turns on" (confusing cause and effect). A strong answer names the two ends (switch-node side and output side), gives the voltages, and explains that the reversal is how an inductor releases energy.
:::

::: why
Polarity is the key to understanding the switch node going below ground (and the diode turning on), the inductive kick, volt-second balance, and which direction power flows. It also matters for layout and measurement: you must know which end of the inductor is the quiet node ($V_{out}$) and which is the noisy one (SW) when placing probes, shields and sense circuits, and for dot conventions when inductors are coupled (SEPIC, flyback, multiphase coupled inductors).
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor equation** $v_L=L\,di/dt$ with passive sign convention: positive $v_L$ at the end the current enters. See [[q0041]].
- **Continuity of current**: the inductor opposes changes in current. See [[q0043]].
- **KVL**: $v_L=V_{SW}-V_{out}$. See [[q0004]].
- **Lenz's law**: the induced voltage opposes the change in flux/current.
- **Volt-second balance**: average $v_L$ is zero. See [[q0178]].
:::

::: answer
"I define the inductor current flowing left to right, from the switch node to the output, and the voltage positive at the switch-node end when it's pushing that current up.

In the on state the high-side FET ties the switch node to the input. The output is at 3.3 V, so the switch-node side of the inductor is at 12 V and the output side at 3.3 V. The voltage is plus 8.7 V on the switch-node end, which is positive across the inductor in the direction of the current, so the current ramps up and the inductor is absorbing energy.

In the off state the high-side FET opens, but the inductor current can't stop. Its voltage flips: it tries to keep the current going, and the switch node swings down until the diode or low-side FET conducts and clamps it at about zero. Now the output side is at 3.3 V and the switch-node side is at zero or slightly below, so the polarity is reversed: negative 3.3 V on the switch-node end relative to the output. The current is still flowing the same way, but now it's falling and the inductor is giving energy to the output.

So the polarity flips every cycle between plus $V_{in}-V_{out}$ and minus $V_{out}$, with the two areas equal in steady state."
:::

::: technical
**Convention.** $i_L$ positive from SW to $V_{out}$. Passive sign convention: $v_L=V_{SW}-V_{out}$ (SW-side end taken as the "+" terminal); then $v_L=L\,di_L/dt$.

**On state:** $V_{SW}=V_{in}-I_L R_{ds(on)}$. $v_L=V_{in}-V_{out}-I_L R_{ds}=+8.7$ V (slightly less with drops). $di/dt>0$. Power into the inductor $p_L=v_L i_L>0$: energy stored.

**Off state:** inductor current continues, so the SW end must be pulled until a path conducts: $V_{SW}=-V_f$ (diode, about −0.4 V) or $-I_L R_{ds}$ (synchronous). $v_L=V_{SW}-V_{out}=-V_{out}-V_f\approx-3.7$ V (diode) or $-3.3$ V ideal. $di/dt<0$; $p_L<0$: energy released.

**Steady state:** $\int_0^T v_L\,dt=0\Rightarrow (V_{in}-V_{out})DT=V_{out}(1-D)T$ giving $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$. With $V_f$ and $R_{ds}$ included: $D\approx\frac{V_{out}+V_f}{V_{in}+V_f-V_{sw,on}}$.

**What does not flip.** The current direction in the inductor, and the polarity of the output voltage. Only $v_L$ reverses.

**Absolute node potentials.** The output end of the inductor is at about +3.3 V (with mV ripple) in both states, so the polarity flip is entirely due to the SW end swinging between +12 V and about −0.4 V.

**Dot convention.** For a single inductor, "polarity" means the voltage sign above. In coupled inductors (e.g. coupled-inductor multiphase bucks, flyback) the dots mark ends that have the same instantaneous polarity.
:::

::: short
"With the current flowing from the switch node to the output, the voltage across the inductor is positive on the switch-node end during the on state, plus 8.7 V in my example, and the current ramps up as it stores energy. In the off state the high-side FET opens, the inductor keeps its current, so its polarity reverses: the switch-node end goes negative relative to the output, minus 3.3 V, and the current ramps down as it delivers energy. Those two areas balance in steady state."
:::

::: long
"Let me set the conventions first. The inductor current positive direction goes from the switch node on the left, through the inductor, to the output on the right. I define the inductor's voltage with plus on the left end. With that convention the equation is just $v = L\,di/dt$.

On state: the high-side FET connects the left end to the 12 V input. The right end is the output at 3.3 V. So the left end is 8.7 V above the right end, plus on the left, and since the current enters at the plus end, the inductor is absorbing energy and the current ramps up.

Off state: the high-side FET opens. The inductor current can't stop instantly, so the inductor does what inductors do: its voltage flips sign to try to keep the current going. That pulls the left end down until the diode, or the low-side FET's body diode, turns on and clamps the switch node to about zero or just below. Now the left end is about minus a diode drop and the right end is still 3.3 V. So the voltage across the inductor is about minus 3.3 V, plus on the right side. The current is still going left to right but the inductor is now delivering energy: it has plus at the output end like a source.

It also helps to remember that the output end of the inductor stays at about 3.3 V the whole time. Only the switch-node end moves: 12 V in the on state, about 0 V in the off state. The reversal in polarity is the switch node dropping below the output voltage. In steady state the positive and negative volt-second areas are equal, which gives the duty cycle relationship."
:::

::: terms
- **Polarity**: which terminal of the inductor is more positive; defined here with + at the SW end.
- **Passive sign convention**: current enters the + terminal, so $p=vi>0$ means absorbing.
- **Lenz's law**: induced voltage opposes the change in current (flux).
- **Volt-second balance**: net volt-seconds across the inductor over a cycle are zero.
- **Freewheeling / flyback**: the off-state conduction through the diode that follows the polarity reversal.
- **Inductive kick**: large reverse voltage when the current path is interrupted without a clamp. See [[q0144]].
- **Dot convention**: marks the terminals with the same polarity in coupled inductors.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Positive on the input side in the on state and reversed in the off state."

**An exceptional answer**:
- States a clear convention (current direction, which end is +) before answering.
- Gives both voltages with numbers ($+V_{in}-V_{out}$, $-V_{out}-V_f$).
- Explains *why* it flips: current continuity forces the SW node down until the diode or low-side FET conducts.
- Notes that the output end is fixed at $V_{out}$ and only SW moves.
- Ties it to energy: absorbing (on) vs delivering (off), and to volt-second balance.
- Mentions what would happen without a clamp path (see [[q0197]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The inductor current reverses."** The *voltage* reverses; the current direction is the same in both states.
- **"The polarity flips because the diode turns on."** It's the other way round: the polarity flip drags the switch node down until the diode turns on.
- **Using the wrong sign for the off-state voltage across L.** It's $-V_{out}$ (SW below $V_{out}$), not $+V_{out}$ nor $-V_{in}$.
- **Forgetting the diode drop.** The off-state inductor voltage is $-(V_{out}+V_f)$, which affects the duty cycle slightly.
- **Confusing electrical polarity with the dot convention.** Dots are only meaningful for coupled windings.
:::

## Setting conventions

## Why it flips: continuity

The inductor stores $\tfrac12Li^2$. If the current dropped to zero instantly, $di/dt\to-\infty$ and $v_L\to-\infty$. So when $Q_1$ opens, the SW node swings negative *as far as it must* to keep $i_L$ flowing:

- With a diode: until $V_{SW}=-V_f\approx-0.4$ V and the diode turns on.
- With a synchronous low-side FET: first the body diode (about −0.7 V), then the channel clamps it near $-I_LR_{ds(on)}$.
- With nothing: until something breaks down (see [[q0197]]).

With real drops: using $V_{sw,on}=0.03$ V, $V_f=0.4$ V, the balance is $(V_{in}-V_{sw,on}-V_{out})D=(V_{out}+V_f)(1-D)$, giving $D=\frac{3.3+0.4}{12-0.03+0.4}=0.299$. The duty cycle is 9% higher than the ideal 0.275 to make up for the diode drop. That is a measurable effect.

Quick check: $(12-0.03-3.3)\times0.299=2.59$ and $(3.3+0.4)\times0.701=2.59$. ✓.

## Energy view

During the on state $p_L=v_Li_L>0$: the input is feeding the inductor. During the off state $p_L<0$: the inductor is feeding the output, and the power to the load is $V_{out}\,i_L$. The net energy in one cycle is zero in steady state, so the on-state energy in equals the off-state energy out. That is the content of [[q0192]].

## Where polarity matters in real hardware

- **Probing**: the SW end sees 12 V to −0.4 V with fast edges; the output end sees 3.3 V with mV ripple. Place a differential probe across $L$ and you will see the $+8.7/-3.7$ V rectangle.
- **Inductor shielding and layout**: the SW end is the noisy terminal; in a shielded inductor the start of the winding is conventionally the end connected to the noisy node, so the outer layer is at the quiet voltage (check the datasheet's dot marking).
- **Coupled inductors**: in a two-phase coupled buck, the dots mark ends that go positive together; swapping them changes ripple cancellation.

## What the polarity buys you: slopes, ripple and stored energy

Because $v_L=L\,di_L/dt$, the two voltages are really two slopes. With $L=4.7$ µH, $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $f_{sw}=500$ kHz ($T=2$ µs, $D=0.275$):

$$\left.\frac{di_L}{dt}\right|_{on}=\frac{+8.7}{4.7\,\mu\text{H}}=+1.85\ \text{A/µs},\qquad \left.\frac{di_L}{dt}\right|_{off}=\frac{-3.3}{4.7\,\mu\text{H}}=-0.70\ \text{A/µs}$$

The current climbs for $DT=0.55$ µs and falls for $(1-D)T=1.45$ µs, so the peak-to-peak ripple is

$$\Delta I_L=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})\,DT}{L}=\frac{8.7\times0.55\ \mu\text{s}}{4.7\ \mu\text{H}}=1.02\ \text{A}$$

and, riding on a 2 A average load, the current swings between 1.49 A and 2.51 A. Check the off side: $0.70\ \text{A/µs}\times1.45\ \mu\text{s}=1.02$ A. The rise and fall match, which is volt-second balance restated as "the current ends the cycle where it began".

The energy bookkeeping uses the same numbers. At the valley the inductor holds $\tfrac12(4.7\,\mu\text{H})(1.49\,\text{A})^2=5.2$ µJ and at the peak $\tfrac12(4.7\,\mu\text{H})(2.51\,\text{A})^2=14.8$ µJ. During the on state it gains 9.57 µJ (equal to $v_L\,i_L\,DT$ with $i_L\approx2$ A: $8.7\times2\times0.55\ \mu\text{s}=9.57$ µJ) and during the off state it gives back $3.3\times2\times1.45\ \mu\text{s}=9.57$ µJ. So the positive-polarity phase charges the inductor with exactly the energy the negative-polarity phase delivers. Nothing in the inductor is consumed; it is a bucket moving energy from the input side to the output side. (The energy delivered to the load per cycle is $V_{out}I_{out}T = 13.2$ µJ; the inductor only carries the part of it that is not already supplied directly from the input during the on state.)

## Limiting cases: when "current never reverses" is not true

The claim that the current direction stays the same is true in a diode-rectified converter and in any converter at a load above half the ripple. From the numbers above, the valley current is $I_{out}-\Delta I_L/2$, which reaches zero at $I_{out}=0.51$ A. Below that:

- **Asynchronous (diode) buck:** the diode stops conducting when $i_L$ hits zero. The converter enters DCM, the inductor voltage goes to about zero for part of the period, and the SW node rings (see the followup on DCM).
- **Synchronous buck in forced continuous mode:** the low-side FET can conduct backward. At $I_{out}=0.3$ A the valley is $0.3-0.51=-0.21$ A, so the inductor current **does** reverse for the last part of the off time, flowing from the output back through the FET. The voltage polarity is still $v_L=-V_{out}$ during that interval; only the current sign changed, so power flow reverses for that fraction of the cycle (the inductor is now pulling energy back from the output capacitor). This costs efficiency at light load, which is why many parts offer diode emulation or pulse skipping.
- **Shorted output:** $V_{out}=0$ gives $v_L=V_{in}=12$ V in the on state and nearly 0 V in the off state, so the current ramps up at $12/4.7\ \mu\text{H}=2.55$ A/µs and barely decays, which is why cycle-by-cycle current limiting is needed.

## Real inductors are not ideal $L$

A real inductor is $L$ in series with its winding resistance $R_{DCR}$ (and in parallel with a small winding capacitance). The voltage across the terminals is $v_{term}=L\,di/dt+i\,R_{DCR}$. The circuit fixes the terminal voltage at $+8.7$ V (on) or $-3.3$ V (off); with $R_{DCR}=10$ mΩ and $i=2$ A, 20 mV of that falls across the resistance, so the ideal inductance sees $+8.68$ V when on and $-3.32$ V when off (the drop always opposes the sign of the current, so it subtracts in both states). The DCR drop is tiny compared with the volts-level swings, but it is exactly what DCR current sensing measures. Put a resistor and a capacitor across the inductor with $R_{s}C_{s}=L/R_{DCR}$, here $4.7\ \mu\text{H}/10\ \text{m}\Omega=470$ µs (for example $R_s=4.7\ \text{k}\Omega$, $C_s=100$ nF). The capacitor voltage then equals $i_L R_{DCR}$ with no polarity flip: 20 mV at 2 A, ranging 14.9 to 25.1 mV across the ripple. If the time constants are mismatched, the sensed waveform shows the rectangular $v_L$ edges bleeding through, a useful clue when debugging.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Inductor DCR rises with temperature (copper is roughly +0.39% per °C), and the $L/R_{DCR}$ match drifts with it and with the inductance's roll-off under DC bias. Datasheet inductance specs vary by manufacturer and measurement condition, so treat the 4.7 µH here as nominal.

## Reading the real waveform on a scope

Real $v_L$ is not a clean rectangle. Expect:

1. **Rising-edge ringing** on SW when Q1 turns on, from the loop formed by input capacitor, FET and SW-node parasitics, at tens to a few hundred MHz. It shows up on $v_L$ too, because the output end is quiet.
2. **A dead-time notch** at the falling edge on a synchronous buck: for a few tens of ns the body diode conducts and SW sits near −0.7 V, so $v_L\approx-4.0$ V instead of −3.3 V. At 4.0 V across 4.7 µH, a 20 ns dead time changes the current by only $4.0/4.7\ \mu\text{H}\times20\ \text{ns}=17$ mA, but at high frequency the loss adds up.
3. **Probe polarity check:** connect probe A to SW and probe B to $V_{out}$ and display A−B. If you see the positive pulse first and the long negative portion after it, with equal areas, the polarity and the volt-second balance are right. If the pulse is positive for most of the cycle, swap your channels or check which end is SW.

Measure with both ground leads as short as you can; a long ground lead adds inductance in the common-mode path and shows up as fake spikes at the edges.
:::

::: applications
- **Understanding the switch node in debugging**: a negative excursion below ground is not a fault; it's the diode or body diode conducting.
- **Choosing the diode/low-side FET** voltage rating: the inductor's polarity flip is what forward-biases it.
- **Placing sense circuits** (inductor DCR current sensing): the R-C across L measures $i_L R_{DCR}$ regardless of polarity.
- **Coupled-inductor and multiphase designs** where dot placement matters.
- **Flyback/inductive load protection** ([[q0144]]): the same polarity reversal is the voltage spike when a relay or motor current is interrupted.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Reference direction:** $i_L$ from SW to output; the sign of $v_L$ would swap if you chose the opposite reference.
- **CCM, steady state.** In DCM the inductor voltage is zero for part of the cycle (after the current reaches zero) and the SW end rings at $V_{out}$.
- **Ideal switch and diode** with small drops.
- **Constant $V_{out}$** (small ripple).
- **Single (uncoupled) inductor.** Dots matter only in coupled magnetics.
:::

::: followups
### What is the voltage at each end of the inductor in the off state?
Output end: about +3.3 V. SW end: about −0.4 V with a Schottky, about −0.7 V during dead time on a body diode, or a few tens of mV below ground with a conducting low-side FET. So the voltage across the inductor is about −3.7 V, −4.0 V or −3.3 V respectively.

### Does the polarity change if the load current is heavier?
The polarity doesn't; the magnitudes change very slightly because of the resistive drops ($I R_{ds}$ and $I R_{DCR}$). The reversal is determined by the switch node dropping below $V_{out}$.

### What happens to the polarity in DCM?
After the current reaches zero, the diode blocks and the inductor voltage drops to near zero, with the SW node ringing around $V_{out}$. The inductor voltage then oscillates in sign at the ring frequency determined by $L$ and the SW-node capacitance, a decaying oscillation around zero.

### What if the off-state clamp had a lower drop, say −0.2 V instead of −0.4 V?
The inductor voltage in the off state would be $-3.5$ V instead of $-3.7$ V, so the current falls slightly slower, and for the same volt-second balance the duty cycle falls: a lower forward drop gives higher efficiency, which is the motive for synchronous rectification.

### Harder: how would you measure $v_L$ safely with a scope?
Use two single-ended probes on SW and $V_{out}$ with the math function A−B, or a high-voltage differential probe across the inductor. Keep probe grounds short, because the SW edge is fast (ns), and watch common-mode error: both ends have large common-mode swings relative to ground.
:::

::: interpretations
"Inductor polarity" is read as the sign of the voltage across the inductor in each state (which end is positive), not the dot convention of coupled inductors, and not the direction of current (covered in [[q0187]]). If the interviewer means "what are the dots for," explain that a single buck inductor has no dots but coupled-inductor and flyback designs do.

- Notes only the SW end moves; output end stays near $V_{out}$ :: Useful for measurement and layout.
