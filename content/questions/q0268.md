::: testing
Whether you can connect **wear-out mechanisms** in each component to **measurable performance drift** in the converter. The interviewer wants you to identify the weakest links (electrolytic capacitors by far, then solder joints and thermally stressed magnetics, then semiconductors), explain the physics (electrolyte evaporation, Arrhenius acceleration, ceramic dielectric aging, bond-wire and solder fatigue, oxide/hot-carrier damage), say what the converter does as it ages (ripple up, transient worse, efficiency down, margin down) and how you design and test for it (derating, temperature, ripple-current ratings, accelerated life tests, telemetry).
:::

::: why
Power supplies are among the first things to fail in long-lived products, and the failure is usually gradual before it is sudden. Knowing *what ages* lets you choose parts (polymer or ceramic instead of wet electrolytic), set derating rules (voltage, temperature, ripple current), predict life from temperature, and decide what to monitor. It also ties back to validation ([[q0267]]): you test at the corners an aged converter will drift to.
:::

::: concepts
- **Arrhenius acceleration**: reaction rates and wear-out accelerate exponentially with temperature: $AF = \exp\!\left[\tfrac{E_a}{k}\left(\tfrac1{T_1}-\tfrac1{T_2}\right)\right]$.
- **Electrolytic capacitor wear-out**: electrolyte loss raises ESR, lowers capacitance; life roughly doubles per 10 °C drop.
- **Ceramic aging**: class II dielectrics lose capacitance logarithmically with time; cracks from flex.
- **Inductor aging**: winding insulation and core material thermal aging; saturation margin changes ([[q0259]]).
- **Semiconductor degradation**: bond wires, die attach, gate oxide, hot-carrier, electromigration.
- **Bathtub curve**: infant mortality, random failures, wear-out.
- **Performance impact**: ripple, transient, loop margin, efficiency, thermal runaway ([[q0261]], [[q0258]]).
:::

::: answer
"The weakest link in most buck converters is the electrolytic capacitor, so I'd start there. The electrolyte slowly evaporates, especially when hot and with high ripple current, so capacitance drops and ESR rises. A common rule is that life doubles for every 10 °C cooler. As ESR climbs, output ripple goes up in proportion, transient droop gets worse, the capacitor runs hotter from its own ripple heating, and eventually the loop margin changes because the ESR zero moves. If it's all ceramic, there's little wear-out, but class II ceramics lose a few percent capacitance per decade of time, and board flex can crack them into shorts.

The inductor is usually robust, but heat ages the winding insulation and some powdered cores increase their loss over time, and a saturation margin that was thin gets thinner when hot. The silicon and the package can degrade through thermal cycling: bond wires and solder joints fatigue, die-attach thermal resistance rises, so the part runs hotter, and $R_{DS(on)}$ can drift up. Gate oxide and hot-carrier effects are small in well-derated parts.

Net result: ripple rises, efficiency drops slightly, the loop moves, and eventually there's a start-up failure or a short. I'd design for it by derating voltage and temperature, using polymer or ceramic capacitors where possible, keeping ripple current under the rating, and testing with accelerated life tests."
:::

::: technical
**Acceleration models.** Arrhenius acceleration factor between a use temperature $T_{use}$ and a stress temperature $T_{stress}$ (kelvin): $AF = \exp\left[\frac{E_a}{k_B}\left(\frac{1}{T_{use}}-\frac{1}{T_{stress}}\right)\right]$, $k_B = 8.617\times10^{-5}$ eV/K. With $E_a = 0.7$ eV (a common assumption for silicon failure mechanisms; mechanism-dependent), stressing at 105 °C versus 55 °C gives $AF \approx 26$. For aluminium electrolytics manufacturers publish a **10 °C rule**: $L = L_0\cdot2^{(T_0-T)/10}$ (datasheet rated life $L_0$ at rated temperature $T_0$, e.g. 2000 h at 105 °C), plus ripple-current heating.

**Per-component mechanisms and symptoms.**

| Component | Mechanism | Parameter drift | Converter effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aluminium electrolytic | Electrolyte evaporation, oxide degradation | ESR up (often to 2× at end-of-life), C down (~20% typical end-of-life criterion) | Ripple up, droop up, heating, loop margin shift, eventual failure |
| Polymer / hybrid | Slower; oxidation, humidity | ESR up slowly | Same, slower |
| MLCC (X5R/X7R) | Dielectric aging (logarithmic), cracking | C down a few %/decade-hour; cracks → short/open | Loop shift, short = no output |
| Inductor | Insulation aging, powder core thermal aging, solder/terminal fatigue | DCR slightly up, core loss up, $I_{sat}$ margin down | Efficiency down, saturation sooner |
| Power FETs / IC | Bond-wire/solder/die-attach fatigue, hot carriers, TDDB, electromigration | $R_{th}$ up, $R_{DS(on)}$ up, $V_{th}$ shift, leakage | Hotter, less efficient, eventual overheating |
| Solder joints / PCB | Thermal-cycle fatigue, vibration, corrosion/whiskers | Intermittent contacts, resistance up | Intermittent regulation, dropouts |
| Reference / divider | Long-term drift, resistor drift with humidity/heat | $V_{OUT}$ drifts slowly | Accuracy loss |
:::

Ceramics lose a few percent capacitance per decade of time and can crack. Inductors age through insulation and core heating; silicon and solder suffer thermal-cycling fatigue that raises thermal resistance and $R_{DS(on)}$. ::: long
"I'd structure it by component, ordered by how likely it is to be the first to wear out.

First, aluminium electrolytic capacitors. The electrolyte slowly evaporates, faster at high temperature and with ripple-current heating. Capacitance falls and ESR rises, and a typical end-of-life criterion is ESR doubled or capacitance down 20%. The rated life is something like 2000 hours at 105 °C and doubles with each 10 °C reduction, so at 65 °C the same part might last 32,000 hours, about 3.7 years of continuous operation, and at 55 °C 64,000 hours. When ESR doubles, output ripple, which in an electrolytic design is mostly ESR times the inductor ripple current, doubles, the capacitor dissipates more, so it gets hotter and ages faster. The loop also moves because the ESR zero shifts lower.

Second, ceramics: no wear-out in the electrolytic sense, but class II dielectrics lose capacitance logarithmically with time, a few percent per decade of hours, and flexing the board can crack them, causing shorts or opens.

Third, the inductor: wire insulation and some powdered cores age with heat, DCR and core loss creep up, and a part with thin saturation margin may saturate when hot.

Fourth, semiconductors and solder: repeated thermal cycles fatigue bond wires, die attach and solder joints, which increases thermal resistance and $R_{DS(on)}$, so the IC runs hotter at the same load. Gate oxide and hot-carrier effects matter at very high stress.

Combined, the converter shows more ripple, worse load-step response, slightly lower efficiency, higher temperature and lower margin, until it fails to start or shorts. Design response: derate voltage and temperature, use polymer or ceramic capacitors, respect ripple-current ratings, test at the aged corner, and consider monitoring."
:::

::: terms
- **Wear-out**: failure due to cumulative degradation, rising late in life.
- **Arrhenius model**: temperature acceleration of degradation with activation energy $E_a$.
- **10 °C rule**: electrolytic life halves per 10 °C increase.
- **End-of-life criteria**: parameter drift (e.g. ESR 2×, C −20%) defining failure for a capacitor.
- **Bathtub curve**: failure rate vs time: early failures, constant random rate, wear-out.
- **Capacitance aging**: logarithmic capacitance loss in class II ceramics, resettable by heating above the Curie point.
- **Thermal cycling fatigue**: damage to solder/bond wires from repeated expansion/contraction.
- **Derating**: using parts below their ratings (voltage, temperature, current) to extend life.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "capacitors dry out and the inductor gets old".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Ranks the **weakest links** and explains each mechanism (electrolyte loss, ceramic dielectric aging and cracking, solder fatigue).
- Uses **numbers**: the 10 °C rule (2000 h at 105 °C becomes 32,000 h at 65 °C), Arrhenius acceleration factors, ESR doubling.
- Connects drift to **converter performance**: ripple ≈ $\Delta I\cdot ESR$ doubles, loop zero moves, efficiency drops, thermal feedback.
- Mentions that heating accelerates aging: **ripple-current self-heating** is a positive feedback.
- Describes **design mitigations** (derating, polymer/ceramics, margins, conformal coat) and **test/monitoring** (HALT, burn-in, telemetry, periodic ripple checks).
- Flags uncertainty: model parameters are material and vendor specific.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "it just gets less efficient".** Efficiency changes are small; ripple, transient and margin shifts, and sudden failures matter more.
- **Ignoring ripple-current heating.** An electrolytic running at its ripple limit is hotter than ambient and ages much faster.
- **Treating ceramic caps as ageless.** They lose capacitance over time (class II) and crack from board flex.
- **Applying the 10 °C rule blindly.** It's a manufacturer's design rule for a given family; its validity range is limited (datasheets cap the extrapolated life and humidity and voltage stress also matter).
- **Testing only at the new-part values.** A validated design should still pass with aged C and ESR ([[q0267]]).
- **Assuming silicon is immortal.** Package and solder fatigue and thermal runaway are real wear-out modes.
:::

::: deep
## What "aging" means for a power converter

A converter ages through two kinds of processes. **Parametric drift**: slow changes in component values (C, ESR, DCR, $R_{DS(on)}$, reference) that move the converter's performance. **Catastrophic wear-out**: a component finally fails (open, short, high resistance). The first kind is predictable and can be tested for; the second is statistical and follows the bathtub curve.

## The electrolytic capacitor, in numbers

The rated life $L_0$ (say 2000 h at 105 °C, as is common for general purpose parts) scales with the "10 °C rule":
$$L = L_0\cdot 2^{\frac{T_0 - T_{core}}{10}}$$
where $T_{core}$ includes ripple self-heating, $T_{core} = T_{amb} + I_{ripple}^2 ESR\cdot R_{th}$.

Figure: Each 10 °C reduction doubles life; 40 °C cooler is 16×. A core at 85 °C gives under a year of continuous life for a 2000 h part, so long-life designs use 5000 to 10,000 h parts, run the capacitor much cooler, or use polymer parts. The rule is a manufacturer's design guide; actual life depends on the family, voltage derating and humidity, and usually has an upper cap in the datasheet.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> End-of-life criteria (commonly ESR up to 2 to 4× initial and capacitance down 20 to 30%), the 10 °C rule's validity range and any upper cap on predicted life are vendor and series specific. Treat the numbers as an illustration and read the manufacturer's life calculation notes.

**What it does to the converter.** Take a 12 V to 3.3 V, 3 A design with one electrolytic of 470 µF and 50 mΩ as the output capacitor (inductor ripple 0.79 A at 18 V, 500 kHz):

- New: ripple $\approx \Delta I\cdot ESR + \Delta I/(8fC) = 39.5 + 0.4 = 39.9$ mV; ESR zero at $1/(2\pi\cdot0.05\cdot470\mu) = 6.8$ kHz.
- End-of-life (ESR 2×, C −20%: 100 mΩ, 376 µF): ripple $= 79 + 0.5 = 79.5$ mV; ESR zero at 4.2 kHz.

The extra heating compounds: the capacitor's own dissipation $I_{rms}^2 ESR$ doubles when ESR doubles, raising $T_{core}$, shortening life further (a positive feedback that makes end-of-life accelerate).

## Ceramic capacitors

Class II ceramics (X5R/X7R) lose capacitance with the logarithm of time since they were last above the Curie temperature (about 125 °C for barium titanate), $C(t) = C_0[1 - k\log_{10}(t/t_0)]$ with $k$ of order 1 to 3%/decade for X7R (higher for Y5V/Z5U; C0G has none; the actual number is vendor and dielectric specific). With $k = 2.5$%: 97.5% after 10 h, 92.5% after 1000 h and 90% after 10,000 h. Heating above the Curie point resets it (soldering "de-ages" the part). Compared with DC-bias derating (up to 50% or more) this is a modest effect, but it adds to the effective-capacitance budget ([[q0258]]).

More important is **mechanical**: flex cracking from board bending, depanelling or screw torque can create a latent crack that later turns into a low-resistance short as moisture or silver migration builds, which kills the rail. Mitigations: flex-termination (soft-termination) parts, orientation, keep out of the bending zone, series fuse/monitor for critical rails, use of multiple smaller caps.

## Inductor aging

Copper winding insulation (enamel) has a temperature class; sustained operation above rating shortens life and can lead to inter-turn shorts. Powdered-iron cores with organic binders can show thermal aging in which core loss rises substantially after prolonged exposure to high temperature (a known effect in some iron-powder materials), and ferrite shows only small permeability drift over time. The converter-level effect: efficiency creeps down a little, the inductor runs warmer, saturation margin shrinks. Choose cores/insulation rated well above peak operating temperature, and verify $I_{sat}$ at hot ([[q0259]]). Mechanical: solder fatigue at the terminals under vibration; audible-noise mechanical wear is cosmetic.

## Semiconductors and packaging

- **Thermal-cycle fatigue:** each power cycle heats the die; differences in thermal expansion between silicon, solder, copper and the board stress the bond wires and die attach. Cracks raise thermal resistance, so $T_J$ increases at the same load; the chip runs hotter, accelerating further degradation. Lifetime follows a Coffin–Manson-type relationship with temperature swing $\Delta T$: bigger swings, fewer cycles to failure.
- **$R_{DS(on)}$ drift:** package and bond-wire degradation raises on-resistance. A 10% increase in FET resistance in the 3 A design adds only about 0.03 W, but raises the IC temperature by about 1 °C, so on its own it isn't dramatic; the thermal-resistance increase is the bigger risk.
- **Oxide and hot-carrier effects:** gate-oxide time-dependent breakdown, bias-temperature instability (shifting threshold voltage), and hot-carrier injection degrade parameters at high field and temperature. For well-derated parts they're minor, but gate-drive overshoot (excess $V_{GS}$ from ringing) accelerates them.
- **Electromigration** in aluminium or copper interconnects: current density and temperature raise open-circuit risk; relevant to IC sense and power metal at the limit.
- **Reference and divider drift:** slow shifts of $V_{REF}$ and resistor values with temperature and humidity change $V_{OUT}$; with 0.5 or 1% parts this is usually small compared with initial tolerance.

## Solder and board

Solder joints fatigue under thermal cycling (large ceramic capacitors, QFNs and inductors are prone); vibration loosens big parts. Symptoms: intermittent output, resistance rising in the power path, hot spots. Under-fills, appropriate pad design, avoiding large mismatch of CTE and conformal coating address these.

## Putting it together: what a converter does as it ages

| Observable | Direction | Dominant cause |
|---|---|---|
| Output ripple | up (maybe 2×) | Electrolytic ESR increase |
| Load-step droop and ringing | worse | Capacitance loss, ESR/loop zero shift |
| Phase margin | changes (up or down) | Crossover and ESR zero move ([[q0261]]) |
| Efficiency | slightly down | $R_{DS(on)}$, DCR, core loss up |
| Component temperature | up | ESR, thermal-resistance increase, positive feedback |
| Start-up reliability | down | Capacitor ESR, weak input, current limit |
| Eventual failure | open/short, no start | Capacitor crack/short, solder open, thermal runaway |

## Designing and testing for lifetime

- **Derate:** capacitor voltage to 50 to 80% of rating (depending on type); ripple current to 50 to 70% of rated at operating temperature; junction temperature below 100 to 110 °C for long life.
- **Choose parts for the life model:** polymer or ceramic output caps; electrolytics only for bulk with life calculated from the real core temperature.
- **Margin:** design the loop and ripple with end-of-life C and ESR (C −20%, ESR 2×), using validation tests ([[q0267]]).
- **Accelerated testing:** HALT/HASS, high-temperature operating life (HTOL), thermal cycling, power cycling; scale by an Arrhenius or Coffin–Manson factor to field conditions, knowing activation energy is an assumption.
- **Monitoring:** log output ripple, efficiency or temperature in the field (PMBus telemetry on digital supplies), and schedule replacement for supplies with wet capacitors.

::: applications
- **Industrial and telecom power supplies** with 10 to 15 year life targets: electrolytic life calculations dominate the design.
- **Automotive converters** rated for 15 years with large temperature cycling: solder fatigue and capacitor choice.
- **LED lighting drivers** where the electrolytic capacitor is the life-limiting part.
- **Space and avionics:** derating and screening for radiation and thermal cycling.
- **Server power:** monitoring capacitor health and scheduled maintenance.

::: assumptions
- Numbers (2000 h at 105 °C, ESR 2×, C −20%, $E_a$ = 0.7 eV, 2.5%/decade) are typical illustrations, not data for a specific part.
- Aging is modelled as monotonic drift; real lives scatter statistically (Weibull-like) and depend on humidity, vibration and electrical stress.
- The ripple example assumes ESR-dominated ripple for an electrolytic; designs with ceramics see much less ripple drift.
- Polymer and hybrid capacitors have slower ESR drift and different (often voltage-dependent) life models.
- The Curie-point reset of ceramic aging and iron-powder-core aging are material specific.
:::

::: followups
### How long will an electrolytic capacitor last if it's rated 2000 h at 105 °C and runs at a core temperature of 65 °C?
By the 10 °C rule, $L = 2000\times2^{(105-65)/10} = 2000\times16 = 32{,}000$ h, about 3.7 years of continuous operation. At 55 °C it's 64,000 h (7.3 yr). If the product runs 8 h/day and the capacitor sits near room temperature when off, the calendar life is roughly three times longer. Verify with the manufacturer's life formula, including ripple-current heating, which is often the dominant contribution.

### Why does an aged converter often get worse ripple and a different loop response?
ESR rises and capacitance falls. Ripple, mostly ESR times inductor ripple for electrolytics, grows with ESR. The ESR zero $1/(2\pi\,ESR\,C)$ moves down and the capacitance changes the LC pole and the crossover (in current mode $f_c\propto 1/C$), so phase margin changes. Validate the design at end-of-life values.

### Why do thermal cycles hurt a buck's IC even if the average temperature is fine?
Differences in thermal expansion (silicon, copper, solder, board) create cyclic mechanical stress at each power or ambient cycle. Bond wires, die attach and solder joints fatigue: cracks grow, thermal resistance rises, and local temperature increases. Life follows a Coffin–Manson-type law in temperature swing, so a large $\Delta T$ cycle can matter more than the mean temperature.

### Scenario: field units fail after 3 years with output ripple much higher than new. How do you diagnose and fix the design?
Measure ripple and ESR/capacitance of the output capacitors on failed units; if ESR is high and capacitance down, it's electrolytic wear-out. Compute core temperature from ambient and ripple current; check against the life rating. Fix by switching to polymer/ceramic capacitors, adding parallel capacitors to reduce ripple current per part, lowering temperature (airflow, placement away from hot parts), or choosing a higher-life-rated series.

### Do MLCC capacitors age in the same way as electrolytics?
No. They don't dry out, but class II MLCCs lose capacitance logarithmically with time (a few percent per decade of hours for X7R, reset by heating above the Curie temperature) and are vulnerable to mechanical cracking. C0G/NP0 dielectrics don't age this way. Typically their effect is far smaller than electrolytic wear-out, unless a crack shorts them.
:::
