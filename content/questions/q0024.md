::: basics
### In plain words
Impedance is how much a part opposes AC current. For a capacitor, the opposition gets smaller as the frequency goes up. At DC it is infinite, so it blocks current. At very high frequency it is nearly zero, so it passes current easily. The equation is $Z = 1/(j\omega C)$, with a magnitude of $1/(2\pi f C)$.

### Picture it
Imagine a rubber sheet stretched across a water pipe. A steady push stretches the sheet and then no more water flows, so DC is blocked. If you push back and forth quickly, the sheet flexes with each push and water moves on both sides. The faster you push, the more easily it passes. A bigger sheet, meaning more capacitance, also passes more easily. The analogy ignores the 90 degree phase shift that real capacitors add.

### Words you need
- **Impedance (Z)**: the AC opposition to current, measured in ohms.
- **Reactance**: the part of impedance from a capacitor or inductor. It depends on frequency.
- **Frequency ($f$)**: cycles per second. The symbol $\omega$ is $2\pi f$.
- **Phase**: the timing offset between voltage and current. For a capacitor, current leads by 90°.
- **Open / short**: no current at all, or no voltage drop at all.

### The simplest example
Take a 100 nF capacitor. At 1 kHz, $|Z| = 1/(2\pi \times 1000 \times 100\times10^{-9})$, about 1.6 kΩ. Raise the frequency by 1,000 times to 1 MHz and the impedance falls by 1,000 times, to about 1.6 Ω. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page derives the formula from $i = C\,dv/dt$ and shows what happens above the capacitor's self-resonant frequency. See also [[q0025]].
:::

::: testing
Whether you can write $Z_C = \dfrac{1}{j\omega C}$ *and* use it. Underneath, the interviewer is checking:

1. Correct form, including the $j$ and that $\omega = 2\pi f$ (not $f$).
2. That you can read off the meaning: magnitude $1/(2\pi fC)$ falls with frequency; phase is −90° (current leads voltage).
3. That you can derive it from $i = C\,dv/dt$ ([[q0025]]) rather than just recall it.
4. Limits: open circuit at DC, short circuit at high frequency, and the realization that a *real* capacitor stops following this curve above its self-resonant frequency ([[q0031]]).
:::

::: why
This one equation drives most analog and power design decisions involving capacitors: choosing decoupling values to hit a target impedance, setting filter corners ($R = 1/(2\pi f_c C)$), sizing AC-coupling capacitors so the signal band passes, estimating ripple current through a bulk capacitor, and computing how much current a capacitor draws from the AC mains. If you can quickly turn "100 nF at 1 MHz" into "about 1.6 Ω", you can sanity-check designs in your head, and interviewers notice that fluency immediately.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor I–V law**: $i = C\,dv/dt$, the starting point for the derivation. See [[q0025]].
- **Capacitance**: charge per volt, set by geometry and dielectric. See [[q0022]].
- **Phasors**: represent a sinusoid $V_m\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ by the complex number $V_m e^{j\phi}$; differentiation becomes multiplication by $j\omega$.
- **Impedance**: the complex ratio $Z = V/I$ for sinusoids; resistance generalized to include phase.
- **Reactance**: the imaginary part of impedance; negative for capacitors ([[q0026]]).
- **Angular frequency**: $\omega = 2\pi f$ in rad/s.
:::

::: answer
"The impedance of a capacitor is $Z_C = \dfrac{1}{j\omega C}$, where $\omega = 2\pi f$. You can also write it as $-\dfrac{j}{\omega C}$, or in the Laplace domain as $\dfrac{1}{sC}$.

It comes straight from $i = C\,dv/dt$. If the voltage is a sinusoid, $V e^{j\omega t}$, taking the derivative just multiplies by $j\omega$, so the current is $j\omega C$ times the voltage, and $V/I = 1/(j\omega C)$.

Two things fall out of it. The magnitude is $1/(2\pi fC)$, so it's inversely proportional to frequency and capacitance: at DC it's infinite, so a capacitor is an open circuit, and at high frequency it goes toward zero, so it looks like a short. And the $-j$ means the phase is −90°: the current leads the voltage by a quarter cycle.

As a quick number, a 100 nF capacitor at 1 MHz is about 1.6 Ω, and at 1 kHz it's about 1.6 kΩ. The one caveat is that a real capacitor has series inductance, so above its self-resonant frequency the impedance starts rising again instead of continuing to fall."
:::

::: technical
**Derivation.** Let $v(t) = \text{Re}\{\mathbf V e^{j\omega t}\}$. With $i = C\,dv/dt$:
$$i(t) = \text{Re}\{j\omega C\,\mathbf V e^{j\omega t}\} \;\Rightarrow\; \mathbf I = j\omega C\,\mathbf V$$
$$\boxed{Z_C = \frac{\mathbf V}{\mathbf I} = \frac{1}{j\omega C} = -\frac{j}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{\omega C}\angle{-90^\circ}}$$
Laplace form (zero initial conditions): $Z_C(s) = 1/(sC)$. With initial voltage $v(0^-)$, the s-domain model is $1/(sC)$ in series with a source $v(0^-)/s$.

**Magnitude and phase.** $|Z_C| = X_{C,mag} = \dfrac{1}{2\pi fC}$; $\angle Z_C = -90^\circ$ at every frequency. On a log–log plot $|Z_C|$ is a straight line of slope −1 (−20 dB/decade).

| C | 120 Hz | 1 kHz | 1 MHz | 100 MHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 nF | 13.3 kΩ | 1.59 kΩ | 1.59 Ω | 15.9 mΩ (ideal) |
| 10 µF | 133 Ω | 15.9 Ω | 15.9 mΩ | 0.16 mΩ (ideal) |

**Admittance**: $Y_C = j\omega C$ (susceptance $B_C = \omega C$, positive). Parallel capacitors add admittance, which is why $C$ adds in parallel.

**Limits**: $\omega\to0$: $|Z|\to\infty$ (open at DC). $\omega\to\infty$: $|Z|\to0$ (short).

**Real capacitor** (series ESR, ESL): $Z = \text{ESR} + j\omega L_{ESL} + \dfrac{1}{j\omega C}$, minimum $|Z| = \text{ESR}$ at $f_{SRF} = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{ESL}C})$, inductive above ([[q0030]], [[q0031]]). The 100 MHz entries above are therefore unphysical for real parts.

**Worked example.** 1 µF across 120 V, 60 Hz mains: $|Z| = 1/(2\pi\cdot60\cdot10^{-6}) = 2.65$ kΩ, so $I_{rms} = 120/2652.6 = 45.2$ mA, leading the voltage by 90°, with zero average power (purely reactive).
:::

::: long
"The impedance of an ideal capacitor is $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)$, where $\omega$ is the angular frequency, $2\pi f$. Equivalently it's $-j/(\omega C)$, and in Laplace terms $1/(sC)$.

I'd derive it from the capacitor's defining equation, $i = C\,dv/dt$. If I drive the capacitor with a sinusoid and use phasors, so the voltage is $V e^{j\omega t}$, then differentiating just pulls out a factor of $j\omega$. So the current phasor is $j\omega C V$, and dividing voltage by current gives $1/(j\omega C)$.

There are two pieces of information in that expression. The magnitude, $1/(2\pi fC)$, tells me the capacitor's opposition to current goes down as frequency goes up, and also as capacitance goes up. Intuitively, at higher frequency the voltage is changing faster, and since current is proportional to $dv/dt$, more current flows for the same voltage amplitude. At DC nothing changes, so no current flows: it's an open circuit. At very high frequency it looks like a short.

The $-j$ is the phase. It says the voltage lags the current by 90 degrees, or equivalently current leads voltage. That's also why an ideal capacitor dissipates no average power: energy goes in during one quarter cycle and comes back out in the next.

Some numbers I keep in my head: 100 nF at 1 kHz is about 1.6 kΩ, at 1 MHz about 1.6 Ω. A 10 µF at 120 Hz is about 133 Ω, which matters for rectifier ripple.

The caveat is that real capacitors have series inductance and resistance, so the impedance only follows this falling line up to the self-resonant frequency. There it bottoms out at the ESR, and above it the part behaves like an inductor. That's why decoupling design isn't just 'add more capacitance'."
:::

::: terms
- **Impedance ($Z$)**: complex ratio of voltage phasor to current phasor, $Z = R + jX$, in ohms.
- **Capacitive reactance**: the imaginary part of $Z_C$, $X_C = -1/(\omega C)$; often quoted as a positive magnitude $1/(2\pi fC)$.
- **Angular frequency ($\omega$)**: $2\pi f$, in rad/s.
- **Phasor**: complex amplitude representing a sinusoid at a known frequency.
- **Admittance / susceptance**: $Y = 1/Z$; for a capacitor $Y = j\omega C$, susceptance $B = \omega C$.
- **Laplace impedance**: $1/(sC)$, used for transient and transfer-function analysis.
- **Self-resonant frequency**: where a real capacitor's ESL cancels its capacitive reactance; above it the part is inductive.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** writes $X_C = 1/(2\pi fC)$ and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Writes the complex form $1/(j\omega C)$, explains the $-j$ (−90°, current leads), and also gives $1/(sC)$.
- Derives it in two lines from $i = C\,dv/dt$.
- States the DC and high-frequency limits and *why* physically.
- Does a quick number from memory (100 nF at 1 MHz ≈ 1.6 Ω).
- Notes that real parts deviate above SRF because of ESL, and that ESR sets the floor.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using $f$ instead of $\omega$**: $1/(fC)$ is off by 2π (a factor of 6.28). Write $\omega = 2\pi f$ explicitly.
- **Dropping the $j$**: $1/(\omega C)$ is only the magnitude. Adding a capacitor's "1.6 Ω" to a resistor's 1 Ω does **not** give 2.6 Ω; it gives $|1 - 1.6j| \approx 1.89$ Ω.
- **Sign confusion**: $1/(j\omega C) = -j/(\omega C)$ because $1/j = -j$. The reactance is negative ([[q0026]]).
- **Believing it keeps falling forever**: real capacitors become inductive above SRF; a 100 nF 0402 is not 16 mΩ at 100 MHz.
- **Saying "capacitors pass AC"** without the frequency: a 100 pF capacitor at 50 Hz is about 32 MΩ, effectively open. Impedance depends on both $f$ and $C$.
:::

::: deep
## From the capacitor law to impedance

Everything starts with the capacitor's defining relationship ([[q0025]]):

$$i(t) = C\frac{dv(t)}{dt}$$

Drive the capacitor with a sinusoid, $v(t) = V_m\cos(\omega t)$. Then:

$$i(t) = -\omega C V_m\sin(\omega t) = \omega C V_m\cos(\omega t + 90^\circ)$$

Read that carefully. The current is a sinusoid of the *same frequency*, with amplitude $\omega C V_m$, shifted **ahead** of the voltage by 90°. So the ratio of amplitudes is $V_m / I_m = 1/(\omega C)$, and the phase of voltage relative to current is −90°.

Phasors package both facts into a single complex number. Representing $v(t) = \text{Re}\{\mathbf V e^{j\omega t}\}$, a time derivative becomes multiplication by $j\omega$:

$$\mathbf I = j\omega C\,\mathbf V \quad\Rightarrow\quad Z_C = \frac{\mathbf V}{\mathbf I} = \frac{1}{j\omega C}$$

Multiply top and bottom by $-j$ to see the sign: $\frac{1}{j} = -j$, so

$$Z_C = -\frac{j}{\omega C} = \frac{1}{\omega C}\,e^{-j\pi/2}$$

In the Laplace domain ($s = \sigma + j\omega$), $Z_C(s) = 1/(sC)$, which reduces to the phasor form when $s = j\omega$.

## Magnitude: why it falls with frequency

$$|Z_C| = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}$$

Physical picture: for a fixed voltage amplitude, a higher frequency means the voltage swings back and forth faster. Since current is proportional to the *rate* of change, more charge must flow per second to keep up, so the current is larger and the impedance smaller. A bigger capacitor needs more charge per volt, so it also draws more current at the same frequency.

Two limits are worth saying out loud:
- **DC** ($f = 0$): $|Z_C| \to \infty$. No change, no current. The capacitor is an **open circuit**.
- **Very high frequency**: $|Z_C| \to 0$. The capacitor acts as a **short circuit** for fast changes, which is why a capacitor "shorts" noise to ground.

## Using impedance in circuits

Because impedances combine exactly like resistances (series add, parallel combine reciprocally, divider formula applies), you can analyse any linear AC circuit with Ohm's law in complex form.

**Worked example: RC divider at a single frequency.** A 1 kΩ resistor in series with a 159 nF capacitor, driven at 1 kHz, output across C. $Z_C = -j\,1000.6$ Ω ≈ $-j$1 kΩ. The divider:

$$\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} = \frac{Z_C}{R + Z_C} = \frac{-j1000}{1000 - j1000} = \frac{1}{1+j} = 0.707\angle{-45^\circ}$$

That's exactly the −3 dB corner of an RC low-pass filter ([[q0035]]). Notice you can't just add 1 kΩ + 1 kΩ: the magnitudes add as vectors, $|1000 - j1000| = 1414$ Ω.

## The real capacitor: where the equation stops being true

A physical capacitor has series resistance (ESR) and series inductance (ESL) from its electrodes, terminations and mounting ([[q0030]]):

$$Z_{real} = \text{ESR} + j\left(\omega L_{ESL} - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right)$$

- Well below resonance, the $-1/(\omega C)$ term dominates: the ideal formula is accurate.
- At $f_{SRF} = \dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{ESL}C}}$ the reactances cancel and $|Z| = \text{ESR}$, the minimum.
- Above SRF, $\omega L$ dominates and the impedance *rises* with frequency: the "capacitor" behaves like an inductor ([[q0031]]).

Example: 100 nF with 1 nH total ESL (part plus vias) resonates at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{10^{-9}\cdot10^{-7}}) \approx 15.9$ MHz. At 100 MHz its impedance is about $2\pi\cdot10^8\cdot10^{-9} - 15.9\,\text{m} \approx 0.61$ Ω, not the 16 mΩ the ideal formula predicts.

## Power: why an ideal capacitor dissipates nothing

With $v$ and $i$ 90° apart, instantaneous power $p = vi$ is positive for a quarter cycle (charging) and negative for the next (returning energy). The average is $\frac12 V_mI_m\cos(90^\circ) = 0$. A capacitor on the mains draws *reactive* power $Q = V_{rms}^2/|X_C|$ in VAR, not real watts, which is why capacitors are used for power-factor correction in inductive loads. Real capacitors dissipate $I_{rms}^2\cdot\text{ESR}$, which is the ripple-current heating that limits electrolytic lifetime.
:::

::: applications
- **Decoupling design**: picking capacitors so that $|Z|$ stays below a target impedance across the noise band (e.g. 100 nF ≈ 1.6 Ω at 1 MHz, helped by 10 µF at lower frequencies). See [[q0032]].
- **RC filter corners**: setting $R = |Z_C|$ at the desired cutoff, $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$. See [[q0035]].
- **AC coupling**: choosing $C$ so $|Z_C| \ll R_{in}$ at the lowest signal frequency ([[q0033]]).
- **Capacitive dropper power supplies**: a mains-rated X2 capacitor's reactance limits current to tens of mA for low-power appliances (with serious safety considerations).
- **Rectifier ripple current**: a bulk capacitor's impedance at 100/120 Hz determines ripple voltage and heating.
- **Power-factor correction**: capacitor banks supply reactive current to cancel inductive loads.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Sinusoidal steady state**: phasor impedance applies to sinusoids after transients have died out. For transients, use $1/(sC)$ with initial conditions, or the differential equation.
- **Ideal, linear capacitor**: constant $C$, no ESR, ESL or leakage. Real parts follow the formula only well below SRF; Class II ceramics' $C$ also changes with DC bias.
- **Engineering sign convention** ($e^{j\omega t}$): physics texts using $e^{-i\omega t}$ write $Z_C = i/(\omega C)$; the physics is identical. See [[q0026]].
- An interviewer might accept $X_C = 1/(2\pi fC)$ as "the equation"; adding the complex form and phase makes the answer complete.
:::

::: followups
### What is the impedance of a 4.7 µF capacitor at 100 kHz?
$|Z| = 1/(2\pi\cdot10^5\cdot4.7\times10^{-6}) = 0.339$ Ω, so $Z = -j0.339$ Ω. That's typical of a buck converter output capacitor at its switching frequency, which is why its ripple is often dominated by ESR and ESL rather than the capacitive term.

### A 1 kΩ resistor is in series with a capacitor whose reactance is 1 kΩ. What's the total impedance magnitude and the current from 10 V rms?
$Z = 1000 - j1000$, $|Z| = 1414$ Ω, phase −45°. Current is $10/1414 = 7.07$ mA rms, leading the source voltage by 45°. Adding magnitudes (2 kΩ) would give the wrong 5 mA.

### Why is a capacitor's impedance infinite at DC? Is that exactly true for a real capacitor?
At DC, $dv/dt = 0$ in steady state, so $i = C\,dv/dt = 0$: open circuit. A real capacitor has finite leakage (insulation) resistance, typically megaohms to gigaohms for ceramics and microamps of leakage for electrolytics, so its DC impedance is large but finite.

### What happens to the impedance of a real 100 nF capacitor above its self-resonant frequency?
It becomes inductive: $|Z| \approx \omega\cdot\text{ESL}$, rising at +20 dB/decade. With 1 nH of mounted ESL, SRF ≈ 15.9 MHz; at 200 MHz the part looks like about 1.2 Ω of inductive reactance. That's why high-frequency decoupling relies on low-inductance packages, short via paths and the PCB's plane capacitance rather than on bigger capacitors ([[q0031]]).

### How would you write the impedance of a capacitor with an initial voltage in the Laplace domain?
Since $i = C\,dv/dt$ transforms to $I(s) = sCV(s) - Cv(0^-)$, the capacitor is equivalent to $1/(sC)$ in series with a voltage source $v(0^-)/s$, or $1/(sC)$ in parallel with a current source $Cv(0^-)$. That's how you include stored energy in transient circuit analysis.
