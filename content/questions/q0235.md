::: basics
### In plain words
In PFM, one cycle has three parts. First the converter sleeps while the output capacitor alone feeds the load and the output sags. Then a pulse of inductor current ramps up and down and refills the capacitor. Then the converter sleeps again. Over a full period, the charge put in equals the charge the load takes out.

### Picture it
Think of a bank account that pays your bills continuously. Money leaves at a steady rate, so the balance slowly falls. When it hits a floor, you make one deposit of a fixed size, and the balance jumps back up. The account is the capacitor, the bills are the load, and the deposit is one pulse from the inductor. The analogy falls short in that a deposit does not appear instantly: it rises and falls over about a microsecond.

### Words you need
- **Output capacitor ($C$)**: holds charge and feeds the load between pulses ([[q0189]]).
- **Droop rate**: how fast the capacitor voltage falls, $I_{load}/C$.
- **Lower threshold**: the output voltage that starts a new pulse.
- **Charge balance**: over one period, charge in equals charge out, so the average output stays steady.
- **Pulse**: one triangle of inductor current ([[q0231]]).

### The simplest example
Take a 22 µF capacitor and a 20 mA load. While asleep, the output falls at $0.02/22\mu \approx 0.9$ mV per µs. After about 17 µs it has sagged 15 mV, and the next pulse starts. That pulse delivers about 0.33 µC (microcoulombs) of charge, and $0.33\mu/22\mu = 15$ mV puts the voltage right back. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page walks through the on-time, the off-time and the exact moments the capacitor charges and discharges.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can follow **energy and charge** through one PFM cycle, not just recite "the inductor charges then discharges". A strong answer walks through the phases in order and says, for each, what the inductor is doing (storing or releasing energy), what the capacitor is doing (charging or discharging, and why), and where the load current comes from. Hidden sub-skills:

1. $i_C=i_L-I_{load}$: the capacitor charges only when the inductor current is above the load current.
2. Inductor current rises during the on-time and falls during the off-time, then idles at zero.
3. Charge balance: the charge the capacitor gains during the pulse equals what the load drains during the sleep interval.
:::

::: why
This is the "explain it with a pencil" question: draw the inductor current, the capacitor current and the output voltage on three aligned axes. If you can do that, you understand why the output sags between pulses, why it overshoots at the end of a pulse, how the load is supplied while the converter sleeps, and where the ripple comes from. It is also the cleanest way to see why PFM is efficient: all the energy movement happens in short bursts. See [[q0231]] for the control and [[q0234]] for the inductor waveform.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor energy storage**: $E=\tfrac12LI^2$; current rises when $v_L>0$ and falls when $v_L<0$. See [[q0192]].
- **Capacitor charge**: $C\,dV/dt=i_C$; the capacitor voltage rises when $i_C>0$ and falls when $i_C<0$. See [[q0190]].
- **KCL at the output node**: $i_L=i_C+I_{load}$. See [[q0189]].
- **Charge balance**: the average capacitor current over a full period is zero.
- **Zero-current detect**: ends the off-time at $i_L=0$. See [[q0229]].
- **Hysteretic trigger**: a new pulse starts when $V_{out}$ falls to the lower threshold. See [[q0231]].
:::

::: answer
"Let me walk through one cycle, with 12 V in, 3.3 V out, a 20 mA load and 22 µF.

Start asleep: both FETs are off, the inductor current is zero, and the load is fed entirely by the capacitor, so the capacitor is discharging at $I_{load}/C$, about 0.9 millivolts per microsecond, and the output sags. When it hits the lower threshold, a pulse starts.

On-time: the high-side FET closes, the inductor has $V_{in}-V_{out}$ across it and its current ramps up, storing energy. For the first few nanoseconds the inductor current is still below the 20 mA load, so the capacitor keeps discharging, but almost immediately it exceeds the load, the extra goes into the capacitor, and the output starts rising. The inductor is charging and the capacitor is charging.

Off-time: the high side opens, the low side or diode takes over and the inductor, now with $-V_{out}$ across it, releases its energy into the output. The current falls linearly. While it's still above the load the capacitor keeps charging; once it falls below 20 mA the capacitor discharges again. At zero, the low side turns off.

Then back to sleep. Over the whole period the charge the capacitor gains during the pulse, about 0.33 microcoulombs, equals what the load pulls out during the sleep interval, so the net is zero. The output swings about 15 mV."
:::

::: technical
**Variables.** $i_L(t)$ pulse of height $I_{pk}=0.6$ A; $i_C=i_L-I_{load}$; $C\,dv/dt=i_C$ (ignoring ESR).

**Phase table (12 V to 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 22 µF, 20 mA, period 17.7 µs).**

| Phase | Duration | $i_L$ | $i_C$ | $v_{out}$ | Inductor |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sleep | ≈16.5 µs | 0 | $-20$ mA | falls 0.91 mV/µs (≈15 mV) | idle, energy 0 |
| On-time (high side on) | 324 ns | 0 → 0.6 A, slope +1.85 A/µs | $-20$ mA → +580 mA | falls for 11 ns, then rises | charging: stores 0.85 µJ at the end |
| Off-time (low side on) | 855 ns | 0.6 A → 0, slope −0.70 A/µs | +580 mA → −20 mA (crosses zero at 1.15 µs from pulse start) | peaks when $i_L=I_{load}$, then falls | discharging: 0.85 µJ into the output |

**Charge.** Surplus charge $Q_{ex}=\tfrac12(I_{pk}-I_{load})^2\left(\frac{L}{V_{in}-V_{out}}+\frac{L}{V_{out}}\right)=0.330$ µC, so $\Delta V=Q_{ex}/C=15.0$ mV. Of this, 0.091 µC is added during the on-time and 0.240 µC during the off-time. Over the full period $\int i_C\,dt=0$.

**Energy.** The load takes $V_{out}I_{load}T_p=1.17$ µJ per period. Each pulse supplies $QV_{out}=1.17$ µJ: 0.85 µJ released from the inductor, 0.32 µJ passed directly to the output from the source during the on-time. The capacitor's stored energy rises by about $CV\Delta V=1.09$ µJ during the pulse and is drained by the load during sleep.

The capacitor is the energy buffer across the sleep interval.
:::

::: short
"Asleep, the capacitor feeds the load and the output droops. When it hits the threshold, the high-side FET turns on, the inductor current ramps up and stores energy; once it exceeds the load current the capacitor charges. Then the low side takes over, the inductor releases its energy into the output as the current ramps down, and the capacitor keeps charging until the inductor current drops below the load. At zero current everything turns off and the capacitor discharges into the load until the next pulse. Net charge per period is zero."
:::

::: long
"Let me use the usual numbers: 12 V in, 3.3 V out, 4.7 µH, 22 µF, a 0.6 A peak, and a 20 mA load.

I'll go around the loop starting at the end of the last pulse. The inductor current is zero, both FETs are off, and the output capacitor is the only thing supplying the load. So it discharges at the load current over the capacitance: 20 mA over 22 µF, about 0.9 millivolts per microsecond. About 16 µs later the output is 15 mV lower and hits the lower threshold.

Now the pulse. The high-side FET turns on, so the inductor sees 8.7 V across it, and the current ramps up at 1.85 amps per microsecond. In about 11 nanoseconds it passes the 20 mA load; before that the capacitor was still supplying the load, after that the inductor current exceeds the load, the surplus goes into the capacitor and the output starts to rise. The inductor is storing energy, up to half L I squared, 0.85 microjoules at the 0.6 A peak, after 324 ns.

Then the high side opens and the low-side FET or diode conducts. The inductor now has minus 3.3 V across it, so the current ramps down at 0.7 amps per microsecond, releasing its energy into the output. While the inductor current is above 20 mA the surplus keeps charging the capacitor, so the output keeps rising, and it peaks when the inductor current falls through 20 mA, about 1.15 µs into the pulse. In the last little bit, the inductor current is below the load and the capacitor starts discharging again. At zero, the zero-current detector turns the low side off.

Charge accounting: the capacitor gains about 0.33 microcoulombs during the pulse, which is 15 mV on 22 µF, and the load removes the same amount during the following 16 µs of sleep. Energy accounting: each pulse delivers about 1.17 microjoules to the output, equal to what the load uses in a period, and the capacitor acts as the buffer in between."
:::

::: terms
- **Surplus charge ($Q_{ex}$)**: charge delivered while $i_L>I_{load}$, which charges the capacitor.
- **Capacitor current ($i_C$)**: $i_L-I_{load}$ (ignoring ESR); positive means charging.
- **Sleep interval**: time with both FETs off; the capacitor alone supplies the load.
- **Charge balance**: zero net capacitor charge over a period in steady state.
- **Energy per pulse**: $QV_{out}$, delivered to the output (equals $\tfrac12LI_{pk}^2$ plus the on-time part).
- **Inductor charging / discharging**: building up / releasing magnetic energy as current rises / falls.
- **Droop rate**: $I_{load}/C$, the rate at which the output falls in sleep.
- **Lower threshold**: the output voltage that triggers the next pulse.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the inductor charges and discharges while the capacitor smooths the output".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Walks through the **phases in order** with the sign of each current and the direction of $v_{out}$.
- States $i_C=i_L-I_{load}$ and notes that the capacitor **keeps charging into the off-time** until $i_L$ falls to the load current.
- Gives the **droop rate** $I_{load}/C$ and **step per pulse** $Q_{ex}/C$ with numbers.
- Does the **charge balance** and the **energy accounting** (inductor energy, source energy, load energy).
- Adds the observation that the load is supplied *only* by the capacitor for about 93% of the time.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying the capacitor only charges during the on-time.** It charges whenever $i_L>I_{load}$, which includes most of the off-time.
- **Saying the inductor supplies the load directly during sleep.** The inductor current is zero then. The capacitor supplies everything.
- **Forgetting the load current is subtracted.** $i_C\neq i_L$; the capacitor sees $i_L-I_{load}$.
- **Saying "the inductor discharges into the capacitor".** It discharges into the output node, which splits between the capacitor and the load; the capacitor gets only the part above the load current.
- **Ignoring the end-of-pulse state.** At zero current both FETs are off; nothing pushes current back in.
- **Treating the capacitor as just a filter.** In PFM it is the energy reservoir that carries the load through the sleep interval.
:::

::: deep
## Setup

Take the standard converter: $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $L=4.7$ µH, $C=22$ µF (ESR ignored for clarity), $I_{pk}=0.6$ A and a steady load of 20 mA. The output node obeys KCL: whatever the inductor delivers either goes to the load or charges the capacitor,
$$i_L=I_{load}+C\frac{dv_{out}}{dt}\quad\Rightarrow\quad C\frac{dv_{out}}{dt}=i_L-I_{load}$$

So the capacitor voltage rises whenever the inductor current is above 20 mA and falls whenever it is below.

## Reading the cycle: charge and energy

**Charge.** Gain during the pulse $=\int_{i_L>I_{load}}(i_L-I_{load})dt=0.330$ µC. Loss during the rest of the period, whenever $i_L<I_{load}$ (the sleep plus the first 11 ns and last 29 ns of the pulse), $=I_{load}\times(T_p-1.14\text{ µs})=0.02\times16.54\text{ µs}\approx0.330$ µC. Total over the period: zero. (The load's full draw per period, $I_{load}T_p=0.354$ µC, is larger by the 0.023 µC the load takes directly from the inductor during the 1.14 µs when $i_L>I_{load}$.)

**Energy.** Per period the load consumes $V_{out}I_{load}T_p=3.3\cdot0.02\cdot17.68\text{ µs}=1.17$ µJ. The pulse delivers $QV_{out}=1.17$ µJ. The inductor is a short-term buffer (0.85 µJ at its peak, all returned within 855 ns); the capacitor is the medium-term buffer (storing about 1.09 µJ extra at the top of the ripple, and releasing it over 16.5 µs). Because the active part is only 1.18 µs out of 17.68 µs, the converter's gate drive and switching losses are paid once every 17.7 µs rather than 35 times in that interval, which is the efficiency benefit.

## Where ESR and the load type change the picture

**ESR.** The capacitor current $i_C$ flows through the ESR, so the terminal voltage is $v_C+ESR\cdot i_C$. With 5 mΩ and a +580 mA peak, the output jumps by about 2.9 mV when the pulse starts and drops by the same amount when it ends, riding on the capacitive sawtooth. This is the quick step a scope shows on top of the slow droop. With an electrolytic or polymer capacitor of 50 mΩ the steps would be 29 mV and dominate, and ESL adds a spike on each edge.

**Resistive load.** I used a constant 20 mA sink, which makes the sleep droop exactly linear. A real 165 Ω resistor ($3.3\text{ V}/20\text{ mA}$) gives an exponential decay with $\tau=RC=3.6$ ms. Over a 16.5 µs sleep this is $3.3\cdot16.5\text{ µs}/3.63\text{ ms}=15$ mV, the same to within a fraction of a percent, so the linear picture is a good approximation whenever the sleep is much shorter than $RC$.

The same ESR step exists in PWM operation, but there it is a small fraction of an already small ripple, so only in PFM does it show up as a visible edge on the scope trace.

::: applications
- **Output-capacitor sizing in PFM designs**: the step per pulse $Q_{ex}/C$ and droop $I/C$ fix the ripple budget.
- **Debugging a noisy light-load rail**: a scope with the inductor current on one channel and $v_{out}$ on another shows the sawtooth and which phase is responsible.
- **Load-step analysis**: after a step the droop rate $I_{load}/C$ is much larger than at idle, and the pulses must catch up.
- **Energy-harvesting converters**: the capacitor stores energy between pulses and the inductor pumps packets of charge into it.
- **Teaching and simulation**: SPICE of a PFM buck, plotted with $i_L$, $i_C$ and $v_{out}$, mirrors this cycle.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Peak-current PFM** with ideal ZCD, no comparator delay, no ESR; real converters add overshoot and ESR steps at the pulse edges.
- **Constant 20 mA load** (a current sink). For a resistive load the droop is exponential rather than linear, but over 15 mV on 3.3 V it is essentially linear.
- **Lossless pulses.** Efficiency losses are a few percent of the pulse energy and do not change the picture.
- **One pulse per trigger.** In burst mode the inductor cycle repeats several times before the sleep.
- **Component values** are illustrative.
:::

::: followups
### During the off-time, is the capacitor charging or discharging?
Both, in sequence. While $i_L>I_{load}$ the capacitor charges (about 99% of the 855 ns off-time here); in the last ~29 ns $i_L<I_{load}$ and the capacitor starts discharging. The output voltage peaks at the instant $i_L=I_{load}$.

### Where does the load current come from during sleep?
Entirely from the output capacitor. The inductor current is zero and both FETs are off, so the capacitor discharges at $I_{load}/C$; for 20 mA and 22 µF that's 0.91 mV/µs, or 15 mV over 16.5 µs.

### How much energy does each pulse deliver, and where does it come from?
Each pulse delivers $QV_{out}=0.354\text{ µC}\times3.3\text{ V}=1.17$ µJ to the output. Of this, 0.85 µJ is the energy stored in the inductor at the end of the on-time ($\tfrac12LI_{pk}^2$) and released in the off-time, and 0.32 µJ flows directly from the source through the inductor to the output during the on-time. The source supplies all of it: $V_{in}\cdot\tfrac12I_{pk}t_{on}=1.17$ µJ.

### What would change if the load were 100 mA instead of 20 mA?
Same pulse and same step per pulse, but the sleep is shorter: $T_p=3.54$ µs, so the droop between pulses is 100 mA × 2.36 µs / 22 µF ≈ 11 mV and the pulses recur at 283 kHz. The capacitor spends less time supplying the load alone, and the inductor is active 33% of the time.

### How would a larger output capacitor change this cycle?
The step per pulse $Q_{ex}/C$ and droop rate $I/C$ both fall in proportion (47 µF halves them), so ripple halves. The pulse rate and the inductor waveform do not change at all; only the capacitor sees different voltages. The output also takes longer to respond to a load step.
:::

::: interpretations
"Describe the charge/discharge cycle of inductor and capacitor" in the PFM section means the sequence of storing and releasing energy during one burst, plus the sleep interval. I describe the inductor as charging (current rising, energy stored) and discharging (current falling, energy released), and the capacitor in terms of the sign of $i_C=i_L-I_{load}$.
:::

::: rubric
- Describes the sleep phase: inductor idle, capacitor feeds load, output droops at $I/C$ :: Often forgotten, and the largest fraction of the cycle.
- On-time: inductor current ramps up, energy stored, capacitor charges once $i_L>I_{load}$ :: The first active phase with the right condition.
- Off-time: inductor releases energy, capacitor charges until $i_L$ falls to the load current :: Common misconception that the capacitor only charges in the on-time.
- States $i_C=i_L-I_{load}$ (KCL at the output node) :: The relationship that explains everything.
- Charge balance over the period (charge gained in the pulse equals charge drawn in sleep) :: Shows steady-state understanding.
- Energy accounting (inductor stores $\tfrac12LI^2$ and releases it; per-pulse energy equals load energy per period) :: The efficiency link.
- Gives numbers (droop rate, step per pulse, durations) :: Quantification.
:::
