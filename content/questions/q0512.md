::: testing
Whether you can define self-resonant frequency (SRF), derive it, and say **why a decoupling designer cares**. The interviewer wants:

1. The series-RLC picture and $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{L\,C})$.
2. What happens below, at and above $f_0$ (capacitive, minimum $|Z|=ESR$, inductive).
3. Why it matters for decoupling: it sets the useful band, drives value selection, and shifts with mounting and DC bias.
4. How to find the real SRF of a mounted part (datasheet vs board vs measurement).
:::

::: why
SRF is the single number that tells you where a capacitor stops being a capacitor. In decoupling, picking a value without looking at SRF is how you end up with a 10 µF capacitor that does nothing at 50 MHz. Interviewers ask it in the decoupling context to see whether you connect the definition (already covered in [[q0031]]) to design decisions: values, placement, anti-resonance and measurement.
:::

::: concepts
- **Series RLC model** of a capacitor ([[q0030]]).
- **Reactance cancellation**: $\omega L=1/\omega C$ at $f_0$.
- **Minimum impedance** equals ESR at $f_0$.
- **Inductive region** above $f_0$ ([[q0509]]).
- **Mounting inductance** lowers the SRF seen on the board ([[q0510]]).
- **Capacitance derating** shifts SRF up (less $C$) ([[q0502]]).
:::

::: answer
"A capacitor's self-resonant frequency is the frequency at which its parasitic inductance and its capacitance resonate, one over two pi root LC. Below it the part acts like a capacitor, its impedance falling with frequency. At resonance the capacitive and inductive reactances cancel, and the impedance drops to just the ESR, the lowest it ever gets. Above it, the inductance dominates and the part behaves like an inductor with impedance rising as 2 pi f L.

For decoupling that makes it the edge of the capacitor's useful band. A 100 nanofarad ceramic with half a nanohenry resonates at about 22 megahertz, a 10 microfarad at about 1.6 or 2, a 10 nanofarad at about 70. So I pick values so their SRFs tile the frequency range, with the smallest, highest-SRF capacitor closest to the chip.

Two practical points: the SRF on my board is lower than the datasheet number, because vias, pads and traces add inductance, so 100 nF with 3 nH of mounting resonates near 9 megahertz, not 22. And capacitors still help somewhat above SRF, they just look like inductors, so the real fix at high frequency is lower inductance rather than a bigger capacitor. I'd verify with a VNA shunt measurement or a PDN simulation."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** $f_0=\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{tot}C}}$, with $L_{tot}=ESL+L_{mount}$. At $f_0$: $|Z|=ESR$, phase $0^\circ$.

**Impedance.** $|Z|=\sqrt{ESR^2+(2\pi fL-1/(2\pi fC))^2}$.
- $f\ll f_0$: $|Z|\approx1/(2\pi fC)$, phase $-90^\circ$.
- $f\gg f_0$: $|Z|\approx2\pi fL$, phase $+90^\circ$.

**Examples ($ESL=0.5$ nH).**

| $C$ | $f_0$ | $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 10 nF | 71 MHz | 0.22 Ω |
| 100 nF | 22.5 MHz | 0.07 Ω |
| 1 µF | 7.1 MHz | 0.022 Ω |
| 10 µF | 2.3 MHz | 0.007 Ω |

(For bigger parts the ESL is also bigger; the 10 µF row with 1 nH is 1.6 MHz.)

**On the board.** $L_{tot}$ includes mounting. 100 nF with 3 nH: 9.2 MHz. With 6 nH: 6.5 MHz. SRF from a measurement: $L_{tot}=1/((2\pi f_0)^2C)$.

**DC bias.** If bias reduces the effective capacitance to $C'=\alpha C$, $f_0$ rises by $1/\sqrt\alpha$: halving $C$ raises $f_0$ by 41 %.

**Usable range.** For a target impedance $Z_t$, a capacitor is useful from where $1/(2\pi fC)=Z_t$ to where $2\pi fL=Z_t$. For 100 nF with 0.5 nH and $Z_t=0.3\ \Omega$: from 5.3 MHz to 95 MHz, extending well above SRF because $Z_t$ is above $Z_0$.

**Parallel capacitors.** Different SRFs give an anti-resonance peak in between; identical ones keep the same $f_0$ and lower $|Z|$ ([[q0506]]).
:::

::: long
"The self-resonant frequency is where a real capacitor stops acting like a capacitor. A real part is the capacitance in series with a small ESR and a small inductance, the ESL. At low frequency the capacitive reactance, one over omega C, dominates and the impedance drops with frequency. As frequency rises, the inductive reactance, omega L, grows. Where the two are equal, one over two pi root LC, they cancel, and the part's impedance is just its ESR, the lowest it ever gets. Above that, the inductance wins and the impedance rises at 2 pi f L, so the capacitor is effectively an inductor.

Numbers: with half a nanohenry, a 100 nanofarad capacitor resonates at about 22 megahertz, 10 nanofarads at 70, one microfarad at 7, and 10 microfarads around 2.

Why it matters for decoupling: that frequency is the edge of the useful band. Fast current lives well above the SRF of big capacitors, so you need smaller parts with higher SRF, closest to the chip, and a spread of values so the SRFs tile the range. The caveat, between two capacitors' SRFs you get an anti-resonance peak, which ESR damps.

And I need to remember that the datasheet SRF is for the bare part. On the board, pads, vias and traces add inductance: 100 nF with 3 nanohenries total resonates near 9 megahertz instead of 22. So I judge SRF on the mounted part, from a VNA measurement or a PDN simulation, and I can back out the inductance as one over two pi f squared times C. DC bias that cuts capacitance by half pushes the SRF up by 40 percent. Above SRF the part still helps somewhat, but the only way to improve it is less inductance, not more capacitance."
:::

::: terms
- **SRF ($f_0$)**: frequency where capacitive and inductive reactances cancel.
- **ESR**: series resistance; sets $|Z|$ at resonance.
- **ESL**: series inductance of the part.
- **Characteristic impedance $Z_0$**: $\sqrt{L/C}$.
- **Mounting inductance**: board contribution to $L_{tot}$.
- **Anti-resonance**: parallel-resonance peak between different capacitors.
- **VNA**: vector network analyser used to measure impedance.
- **Useful band**: frequency range where $|Z|$ is below a target.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer defines SRF and says "above it the capacitor is an inductor". An exceptional one:

- Gives $f_0$, the three regions, $|Z|=ESR$ at $f_0$, and numbers for typical values.
- Distinguishes **datasheet SRF** from **mounted SRF**, with an example (22 → 9 MHz) and how to extract $L$ from a measured $f_0$.
- Notes that DC bias lowers $C$ and **raises** SRF.
- Connects SRF to **value selection**, anti-resonance and the point that above SRF the fix is less inductance.
- Knows a capacitor stays useful somewhat above SRF if the target impedance is above $Z_0$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Above SRF the capacitor is useless."** It's inductive, not absent; it can still meet a target impedance above $Z_0$.
- **Using the datasheet SRF for the board.** Mounting inductance can halve it.
- **Thinking SRF depends only on capacitance.** It depends on $LC$; bigger capacitors usually come with more $L$ too.
- **Forgetting DC-bias.** Effective capacitance, hence SRF, changes with voltage.
- **Choosing values at decade steps without checking anti-resonance** between them.
:::

::: deep
## The numbers behind $f_0$

$$f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$

For a fixed inductance, SRF falls as $1/\sqrt C$: a hundred times more capacitance drops SRF tenfold.

## Impedance and phase

Magnitude and phase were plotted in [[q0509]]. In short: slope $-20$ dB/decade below $f_0$, a dip to ESR at $f_0$, $+20$ dB/decade above. Phase swings from $-90^\circ$ to $+90^\circ$ and crosses $0^\circ$ exactly at $f_0$, which is how you can find it on a VNA.

## Why SRF drives decoupling design

**1. Value selection.** The goal is $|Z|\le Z_{target}$ across the band. Each capacitor covers roughly from where its capacitive reactance equals $Z_{target}$ to where its inductive reactance equals $Z_{target}$. For $Z_{target}=0.1\ \Omega$ and $L=0.5$ nH:

| $C$ | Lower edge $1/(2\pi C Z_t)$ | Upper edge $Z_t/(2\pi L)$ | Exact band (ESR 20 mΩ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 µF | 0.16 MHz | 31.8 MHz | 0.16–31 MHz |
| 1 µF | 1.6 MHz | 31.8 MHz | 1.6–33 MHz |
| 100 nF | 15.9 MHz | 31.8 MHz | 12–43 MHz |
| 10 nF | (159 MHz) | (31.8 MHz) | 57–88 MHz only |

The asymptotic upper edge depends only on $L$, so for the large values (where $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}\ll Z_t$) every capacitor stops at about the same frequency, roughly 32 MHz at 0.1 Ω with 0.5 nH. Smaller values push the exact edge up a little (the capacitive term still cancels some of $\omega L$), and once $Z_0$ exceeds $Z_t$ (10 nF here, $Z_0=0.22\ \Omega$) the part dips below $Z_t$ only in a narrow notch around its SRF, which needs a low ESR. To push broad coverage higher you need lower $L$ (more parallel parts), not just smaller capacitors with the same mounting. This is the point many candidates miss: **smaller values with the same inductance give a narrow notch, not a wider band**.

**2. Where small values help.** Smaller parts in smaller cases have less ESL and a higher SRF. A 10 nF 0402 at 0.4 nH reaches $Z_t/(2\pi L)=40$ MHz at 0.1 Ω; a reverse-geometry 0.2 nH part reaches 80 MHz.

**3. Anti-resonance.** Between $f_{0,big}$ and $f_{0,small}$ the larger part is inductive and the smaller is capacitive. They resonate in parallel and the combined $|Z|$ peaks at roughly $f\approx\dfrac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{big}C_{small}}}$ (when $C_{big}\gg C_{small}$), with peak height governed by ESRs. Damping reduces it.

**4. Mounted vs datasheet SRF.** Vendors quote SRF measured in a low-inductance fixture. A real board with vias, pads and traces has more. Back out the loop inductance with $L=1/((2\pi f_0)^2C)$:

- Measured 100 nF resonance 8 MHz: $L=3.96$ nH.
- Datasheet 22 MHz: $L=0.52$ nH.
- Mounting contribution: about 3.4 nH, so fix vias and fan-out, not the part.

**5. DC bias and temperature.** Ceramic capacitance drops with bias; if $C$ is halved, SRF rises by $\sqrt2$ (41 %), and the low-frequency impedance doubles. Always evaluate at operating conditions.

## Measuring SRF

- **VNA two-port shunt-through**: connect the mounted capacitor between port 1 and 2 in a shunt configuration on the board; $Z=25\ \Omega\cdot S_{21}/(1-S_{21})$ for 50 Ω systems; SRF is where $S_{21}$ is minimum (shunt thru) / phase crosses zero.
- **Impedance analyser** for the bare part (less representative of the board).
- **Simulation** with vendor S-parameter or SPICE models, plus extracted mounting inductance.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 25 Ω shunt-through conversion applies to a 50 Ω two-port measurement in the usual low-impedance approximation; details depend on the fixture and calibration. Vendor-quoted SRF values depend on the test fixture.

## What if you operate above SRF?

The part is an inductor of value $L$, in series with its ESR. It still contributes: it provides a path of impedance $2\pi fL$. That may be fine if it is below the target impedance at that frequency or if other capacitors dominate. It can also resonate with *other* capacitors' capacitance. So SRF is a boundary, not a cliff.
:::

::: applications
- **Selecting a value family** for a rail so the SRFs cover the band the chip's current spectrum demands.
- **Debugging**: noticing a supply resonance around 8 MHz and tracing it to 4 nH of mounting for the 100 nF parts.
- **RF bypassing**: choosing a capacitor whose SRF sits at the operating frequency (e.g. 100 pF near 1 GHz) for low impedance there.
- **PDN simulation**: entering ESL, ESR and mounting inductance so peaks are predicted.
- **Filter design**: avoiding capacitors used above SRF in EMI filters where they behave as inductors.
:::

::: assumptions
- Series RLC model with constant ESR and $C$; real ceramics change with bias and temperature.
- ESL values are typical; mounted inductance depends on the layout.
- The usable-band calculation assumes a flat target impedance across frequency.
- The VNA conversion is the common low-impedance approximation.
- Another valid answer is to define SRF through the impedance minimum or the zero-phase crossing; they coincide for small ESR.
:::

::: followups
### What's the SRF of a 1 µF capacitor with 0.7 nH?
$f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{0.7\text{ nH}\times1\ \mu\text{F}})=6.0$ MHz. With 3 nH mounted it drops to 2.9 MHz.

### Is a capacitor useless above its SRF?
No. It becomes an inductor of value $L$ with impedance $2\pi fL$. If that is below the target impedance (or lower than what other parts provide) it still helps. It becomes a problem if it forms an anti-resonance with other capacitors.

### How would you find the real SRF of a capacitor on your board?
Measure it with a VNA in a shunt-through configuration (or an impedance analyser on a test coupon) and read where the impedance dips and the phase crosses zero. From it, $L_{tot}=1/((2\pi f_0)^2C)$ tells you the total mounting inductance.

### Why do we use several capacitor values if the upper edge depends mostly on L?
Because each value covers a different lower edge, and small packages usually have lower $L$. Parallel parts divide $L$. In practice you use several values to cover the band and many parts to lower inductance.

### Scenario: DC bias reduces a 10 µF capacitor to 4 µF at 5 V. How does the SRF change?
It rises by $\sqrt{10/4}=1.58$ times, e.g. from 1.6 MHz to 2.5 MHz for 1 nH, but the low-frequency impedance is 2.5 times higher than expected. The part supplies less charge and resonates higher, so the mid-band may have a gap I need to fill with extra capacitance.
:::
