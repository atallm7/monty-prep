::: testing
Whether you understand that a voltage measurement is always **between two points**, and that on a fast signal the *ground connection* of the probe is half the measurement. The question can be read two ways, and strong candidates cover both:

1. **Ground test points / ground probe points on the board**: pads tied to the ground plane placed next to signal test points so a scope probe's ground can attach with a very short return.
2. **Ground probes in a test fixture**: spring probes ("pogo pins") that contact ground pads in ICT/FCT fixtures, giving the test equipment a low-impedance reference.

The interviewer wants to hear: loop area and inductance, ringing, noise pickup, and a placement rule.
:::

::: why
Every scope measurement uses a signal tip and a ground return. A 15 cm ground lead looks like roughly 100+ nH of inductance, which rings with the probe's input capacitance at around 100 MHz and distorts fast edges. If the board gives you no nearby ground pad, you are forced to clip the ground far away, and you end up debugging your probe rather than your circuit. In production fixtures, too few or poorly placed ground contacts give noisy or false test results. So where and how you provide grounds affects both lab accuracy and factory yield.
:::

::: concepts
- **Voltage is a difference**: a probe measures tip minus ground clip; the return path is part of the circuit. See [[q0627]].
- **Loop inductance and area**: a larger loop means more inductance and more magnetic pickup. See [[q0629]] and [[q0630]].
- **Ringing**: parasitic L with probe C forms a resonance. See the `ringing-lc` sim in [[q0629]].
- **Ground plane**: a low-impedance reference that ground pads connect to with vias.
- **Test points**: signal pads that ground pads accompany. See [[q0619]], [[q0621]].
- **Fixture ground probes**: bed-of-nails pins dedicated to ground/return. See [[q0612]].
:::

::: answer
"I read 'ground probe' two ways, and they're the same idea.

On the board, a ground probe point is a pad connected straight to the ground plane, with several vias, placed right next to a signal test point. The reason is that a scope measures the difference between the tip and its ground clip, and on a fast signal that return path matters. A long alligator lead adds around a hundred nanohenries or more, which rings with the probe capacitance and picks up noise, so a 1 ns edge looks like it has overshoot that isn't really there. If I give the engineer a ground pad a couple of millimetres from the signal pad, they can use a spring tip or a short wire and the loop is tiny.

In a production fixture, ground probes are the pins that contact ground pads to give the tester a solid reference. You want several, spread around the board, so each region has a nearby return.

So I'd use them whenever a signal is fast or sensitive, essentially every test point on a fast or analog net, and for power rails when measuring ripple, where I'd want a ground pad next to each. For slow DC signals a single ground point is fine, and a good rule is about one ground pad for every one or two signal test points."
:::

::: technical
**Why the ground connection matters.** A voltage probe measures $V_{tip} - V_{gnd}$. The probe's ground lead has inductance $L$ and, together with the probe's input capacitance $C_{in}$, forms a series resonance at $f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$. Using typical numbers:

| Ground connection | L (approx.) | C | $f_0$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long clip lead (about 15 cm) | ≈ 150 nH (≈ 1 nH/mm rule of thumb) | 10 pF | ≈ 130 MHz |
| Short spring/ground blade (about 1 cm) | ≈ 10 nH | 10 pF | ≈ 500 MHz |

The characteristic impedance of the resonator is $\sqrt{L/C}$: 122 Ω for 150 nH/10 pF. The loop is lightly damped (critical damping would need a series R of $2\sqrt{L/C} \approx 245$ Ω), so a fast edge rings, and the ringing is a measurement artifact, not circuit behaviour.

**Pickup.** The loop also acts as an antenna: induced emf $v = \frac{d\Phi}{dt} = A\frac{dB}{dt}$, proportional to loop area. Cutting area by 10× cuts pickup by 10×. See [[q0630]].

**Board-level design of ground probe points.**

- Place a ground pad within a few mm (about 2.5 mm / 100 mil is a common spacing for a scope's spring ground) of every signal test point that is fast or sensitive.
- Connect the pad to the **ground plane with 1–2 vias**, not a thin trace; thin trace adds inductance.
- Size: about 1 mm round pad, or a through-hole loop/ pin for a hook.
- Use ground pads next to power-rail test points to measure ripple and noise (tip-and-barrel technique).
- Label them (GND) on silkscreen and make them visually obvious.
- For multiple domains, ground pads should connect to the **same reference** the signal uses (analog ground near analog test points). See [[q0621]].

**In fixtures (ICT/FCT).** Ground probes are spring pins on ground pads. Use several, distributed, so no signal probe is far from a ground return, because each long return adds inductance and resistance. Power and ground probes often use larger, higher-current probes. Guidance depends on the fixture vendor.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The "1 nH/mm" and "about 2.5 mm spacing" figures are common rules of thumb. Real values depend on lead geometry and the return path; check the probe's datasheet for its ground-lead inductance.
:::

It's needed because a probe measures tip minus ground, and a long ground lead adds inductance that rings with the probe capacitance and picks up noise. ::: long
"A voltage measurement is always between two points, so the probe's ground is half the measurement. On the board, a ground probe point is a pad connected to the ground plane with a couple of vias, placed a few millimetres from a signal test point.

It's needed because of loop inductance. A scope probe with a 15 cm ground clip has something like 150 nanohenries of inductance. With about 10 picofarads of probe capacitance that resonates near 130 MHz, so a fast edge makes it ring, and what you see on the scope isn't the real signal. That loop is also an antenna: its area picks up noise from switching nodes. If the board gives me a ground pad right beside the test point, I can use a spring ground tip, which is around 10 nanohenries, and the ring moves up near 500 MHz where it matters much less.

When should they be used? On every test point for fast edges, clocks or switching nodes, on sensitive analog nodes, and next to power rails when I'm measuring ripple and noise. For slow DC signals one shared ground point is enough, but I'd still try to put one near every cluster of test points, roughly one ground pad per one or two signals.

In production, the equivalent is a ground probe in the bed-of-nails fixture: spring pins that contact ground pads, and I'd place several of them around the board so each signal probe has a nearby return.

Details: connect the pad to the plane with vias, not a thin trace, label it, and make sure it's the right ground: analog ground near analog test points."
:::

::: terms
- **Ground probe point / ground test point**: a pad connected to the ground plane, used as the return for a probe.
- **Ground lead inductance**: inductance of the probe's return path; about 1 nH/mm rule of thumb.
- **Spring ground / ground blade**: a short probe tip attachment that minimises loop size.
- **Loop area**: area enclosed by the signal and return path; sets magnetic pickup.
- **Ringing**: damped oscillation from L and C after a fast edge.
- **Pogo pin / spring probe**: spring-loaded contact used in fixtures.
- **Return path**: the route current takes back to the source; for probes, via the ground lead.
- **Tip-and-barrel**: probing technique that uses the probe's barrel against a ground point with the tip on the signal, for lowest loop.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "ground pads are for connecting the scope ground".

**An exceptional answer**:

- Notes both meanings (on-board ground points, fixture ground pins) and answers both.
- Explains that voltage is a difference and the ground lead is part of the circuit, with numbers: about 150 nH and 130 MHz for a long lead, about 10 nH and 500 MHz for a spring tip.
- Gives placement rules: within a few mm of the signal, via to the plane, same ground domain.
- Mentions when it matters (fast edges, ripple, small signals) and when it doesn't (slow DC).
- Links to loop area and pickup and to probe types (spring tips, tip-and-barrel).
:::

::: mistakes
- **One ground pad for the whole board.** A long ground lead for a distant test point defeats its purpose.
- **Ground pad on a thin trace.** The trace adds inductance and the pad isn't a good reference. Use vias to the plane.
- **Wrong ground.** Using a noisy digital ground pad as the reference for a tiny analog signal measurement gives wrong readings.
- **No labels.** A pad nobody recognises as ground gets a probe shorted onto a rail.
- **Thinking the clip lead is harmless at low speed.** For DC and slow signals it is, but for edges below a few ns or mV-level noise it isn't.
- **Placing the ground pad on the other side of the board from the signal pad.** The loop crosses the board.
:::

::: deep
## A measurement is a loop

The scope's channel is a voltmeter with a high-impedance input in parallel with a small capacitance, connected to the circuit through two conductors: the **tip** and the **ground lead**. Together with the DUT's signal and return path they form a loop. Everything you know about loops applies: inductance ($L \propto$ length and area), pickup ($\propto$ area), and resonance with any capacitance.

## How bad is it? Numbers

- Lead L = 150 nH, C = 10 pF: $f_0 = 130$ MHz, $Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C} = 122$ Ω, so with a 50 Ω source the damping is light (critical damping needs about 245 Ω total series resistance in this simple model). Overshoot with 50 Ω series R in the model: about 52%.
- Spring tip L = 10 nH, C = 10 pF: $f_0 = 503$ MHz, $Z_0 = 32$ Ω; critical damping needs about 63 Ω.

## What a good ground probe point looks like

1. **Placement**: a pad, about 1 mm in diameter, 2–3 mm from the signal pad. The scope's spring ground tip can span both; for a hook probe there's a through-hole loop.
2. **Connection**: directly to the nearest ground plane through one or two vias adjacent to the pad (or vias in pad if the process allows, see [[q0613]]). The connection should not be a long trace.
3. **Same reference**: tie to the ground plane that the signal returns on. For mixed boards, put the ground pad over the same plane region the signal references.
4. **Labelling**: silkscreen "GND" or a ground symbol.
5. **Density**: at least one ground pad per cluster of test points; about one ground per one or two signal pads is a common rule of thumb for fast nets.

## Probing techniques that use the ground point

- **Spring ground tip** or **ground blade**: pushes onto the ground pad while the tip touches the signal.
- **Tip-and-barrel**: remove the probe's alligator lead; touch the tip to the signal and the barrel (outer ground sleeve) to the ground pad.
- **Coax/ SMA test points**: for RF; the connector's shield is the ground.
- **Active probes** with small ground leads for high-bandwidth measurement.

## Power rails: ripple measurement

To measure the ripple of a rail with millivolt-level switching noise, the ground loop dominates. A big loop near a fast switch node picks up magnetically coupled spikes that are not on the rail at all. Use the tip-and-barrel at a rail test point with ground pad adjacent, and set the scope bandwidth limit (20 MHz) as standards for ripple measurement often do.

## Fixture ground probes

A bed-of-nails fixture ([[q0612]]) uses dedicated ground pins. Reasons for many: (1) keep return paths short for every signal pin; (2) reduce the voltage drop in the ground when currents flow during functional test (copper resistance of 0.49 mΩ per square for 1 oz means 1 A over 50 squares drops 25 mV); (3) provide redundancy so one dirty pad doesn't fail the test. Higher-current pins are used for power and ground when test currents are large.

## Production cost of adding them

A ground pad is a pad and 1–2 vias: negligible cost on a 4-layer board. It consumes area (about 2–3 mm²) and may need a keep-out if fixture pins are present. The cost of omitting it shows up in debugging time.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If you only remember one sentence: "A ground probe point turns a 15 cm loop into a 2 mm loop." Then back it with the f₀ = 1/(2π√LC) numbers.
:::

::: applications
- **Dev boards and evaluation modules**: nearly every test point has an adjacent ground post or pad.
- **Switching regulator boards**: ground pads next to VOUT and switch-node test points for ripple and edge measurements.
- **High-speed digital boards**: ground pads adjacent to clock and data probing pads (with minimal stubs).
- **Production fixtures**: several ground spring pins around the board for the in-circuit and functional test stations.
- **RF boards**: SMA or u.FL test connectors where the connector shield is the ground.
:::

::: assumptions
- Applies to scope probes and similar measurements on boards with a ground plane.
- For DC and slow signals (below about 1 MHz edges) the ground lead is a minor effect.
- Values like 1 nH/mm and probe capacitance (about 10 pF for passive 10× probes, which may be less for active probes) are typical.
- "Ground probe" may also mean a physical probe or "ground clip" for a scope; the answer is the same: minimise the return loop.
- In differential measurements (differential or isolated probes) the ground lead matters less, but common-mode behaviour does matter.
:::

::: followups
### Why not just use the scope probe's alligator ground clip everywhere?
It's fine for slow signals, but at fast edges the ~150 nH lead adds ringing near 130 MHz and a big loop for pickup. You can't tell real overshoot from probe artifacts. A spring tip or tip-and-barrel with a nearby ground pad cuts L by about 15× and loop area similarly.

### How far from the signal pad should the ground pad be?
As close as the layout permits: about 2–3 mm center to center is a common compromise (the spacing of many spring ground tips is near 2.54 mm). Closer is better for loop area but must keep fixture and soldering clearances.

### Should the ground pad connect through a trace or vias?
Vias straight to the ground plane. A thin trace adds inductance (about 1 nH/mm) and defeats the purpose, and a pad over a split or noisy plane gives the wrong reference.

### Do differential probes need ground pads?
They need less, because the measurement is between two signal points and the common-mode ground path matters less. But they have a ground/common connection and still benefit from proximity, especially for common-mode noise measurements.

### Scenario: your switching regulator output shows 200 mV spikes with the clip lead, but 30 mV with the spring tip on a ground pad. What is real?
The 30 mV is closer to the truth. The clip lead's 150 nH loop is picking up the switch node's dI/dt and ringing with probe C. Confirm by shortening further (tip-and-barrel) and limiting the bandwidth to 20 MHz; if the number drops further, the first reading was largely artifact. See [[q0629]] to [[q0631]].
:::

::: interpretations
"Ground probes" can mean (1) the **ground test points/pads** on the board for scope grounds, (2) the **ground pins in a test fixture**, or (3) the physical ground lead of a scope probe. The page covers all three: the physics (loop inductance) is the same in each.
:::
