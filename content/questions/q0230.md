::: testing
Whether you can **diagnose before prescribing**. At light load the output power is tiny, so any loss that does not shrink with the load wrecks the efficiency percentage. The interviewer wants you to:

1. Identify which losses are fixed (gate drive, $C_{oss}$, controller quiescent current, feedback divider, core loss) versus load-dependent ($I^2R$).
2. Name the standard fixes: PFM/burst mode, diode emulation (DCM), lower or foldback frequency, low-$I_q$ design, phase shedding, and, at the very lowest loads, an LDO or load switch.
3. Quantify at least one, and know the trade-offs (output ripple, transient response, EMI, audible noise).
:::

::: why
Battery life is set by how the device behaves most of the time, which is usually idle. A phone or sensor spends 99% of its life at microamps to milliamps, where a PWM buck that peaks at 97% can drop below 50%. Interviewers use this question to test if you understand that "efficiency" is a ratio and that the numerator collapses at light load while the fixed denominators do not. It ties directly to [[q0228]] (modes), [[q0229]] (DCM) and [[q0231]] (PFM).
:::

::: concepts
- **Efficiency as a ratio**: $\eta=\frac{P_{out}}{P_{out}+P_{loss}}$; a fixed loss of 40 mW is invisible at 10 W and fatal at 30 mW. See [[q0217]].
- **Per-cycle (switching) losses**: gate charge $Q_GV_{drv}$, $\tfrac12C_{oss}V^2$, overlap, dead time. Power = energy per cycle × $f_{sw}$.
- **Quiescent current ($I_q$)**: what the controller draws with no load, plus the feedback divider.
- **Diode emulation / DCM**: stops reverse current and circulating ripple. See [[q0229]].
- **Pulse-frequency modulation**: fires pulses only when needed, so switching losses scale with load. See [[q0231]].
- **Conduction loss**: $I^2R$ falls as the square of the load, so it is not the problem at light load.
:::

::: answer
"At light load the output power is tiny, so efficiency is dominated by the losses that don't scale down with the load: gate drive and switch-node capacitance, which are energy-per-cycle times switching frequency, plus the controller's quiescent current and the feedback divider. The conduction loss is the thing that shrinks with load, so it's not the culprit.

So the fixes attack those fixed costs. The biggest is to stop switching at a fixed frequency: use a pulse-frequency or burst mode, where the converter fires fixed-size pulses only when the output needs them, so the switching rate and the losses scale with the load. Next, use diode emulation, so the low-side turns off at zero current and you don't circulate negative ripple current in a forced-PWM converter. Then cut the quiescent current: a controller that powers down most of its blocks between bursts, a larger feedback divider, and bias taken from the output instead of the input. In a multiphase converter I'd shed phases. A bigger inductor also helps in PFM, because each pulse carries more charge, so you need fewer of them.

At extremely low loads, microamps, a nano-amp quiescent LDO or a load switch can beat any buck. The costs of all this are more output ripple, a slower first response to a load step, and a variable switching frequency, so I'd keep a forced-PWM option for noise-sensitive rails."
:::

::: technical
**Where the power goes at 10 mA** (12 V to 3.3 V, 500 kHz, model values): output power 33 mW. Fixed per-cycle loss (gate $12\text{ nC}\times5\text{ V}\times500\text{ kHz}=30$ mW plus $\tfrac12C_{oss}V^2f=10.8$ mW) = 40.8 mW; controller bias 5 mW; ripple conduction $\frac{\Delta I^2}{12}R=1.7$ mW; load-dependent conduction and switching about 0.4 mW. Total about 48 mW, so $\eta=41\%$.

**Fixes in order of impact.**
1. *PFM/burst*: loss per pulse $E_p\approx107$ nJ for a 0.6 A peak; each pulse delivers $Q=0.354$ µC. Loss power $=E_p\,I_{out}/Q=0.30\,I_{out}$ W, i.e. 3 mW at 10 mA, plus sleep $I_q$. $\eta\approx91\%$.
2. *Diode emulation*: removes the 1.7 mW of ripple conduction and prevents negative inductor current. Alone it does little because the 40.8 mW is switching-related.
3. *Lower $I_q$*: sleep current of 20 µA from 12 V is 0.24 mW; at 1 mA load (3.3 mW) that is 7% of the output power; cutting it to 2 µA gives about 5 points of efficiency at 1 mA (86% to 91% in the model).
4. *Larger $L$*: $Q$ per pulse $\propto L$ for a fixed $I_{pk}$, so overhead per coulomb falls: 4.7 µH to 10 µH halves the pulse rate and lifts 10 mA efficiency from 91% to 95.1% in the model.
5. *Phase shedding, segmented FETs, lower $f_{sw}$, bias from $V_{out}$.*
6. *Different device*: nA-$I_q$ LDO or load switch when $V_{in}-V_{out}$ is small and the load is in the µA range.
:::

::: short
"At light load the losses that don't scale with load, gate drive, switch-node capacitance and quiescent current, dominate the efficiency number. So you cut them: use PFM or burst mode so pulses and switching losses scale with load, diode emulation so you don't circulate reverse current, a low-quiescent-current controller and a high-value feedback divider, and in multiphase designs shed phases. At microamp loads an ultra-low-$I_q$ LDO can win. The trade-offs are more ripple and slower first response."
:::

::: long
"First I'd work out where the power is going, because efficiency at light load is a ratio with a tiny numerator. Take a 12 V to 3.3 V converter at 500 kHz delivering 10 mA, so 33 mW. In forced PWM mode, the gate drive and switch-node capacitance cost about 40 mW, because that's energy per cycle times 500 thousand cycles a second, whether or not any load is there. The controller bias is another few milliwatts, and ripple conduction maybe 2. So efficiency is around 40 percent, even though the same converter hits 97 percent at 2 A. The conduction loss that dominates at heavy load is almost irrelevant here.

So the fixes are about fixed costs. First and biggest: pulse-frequency or burst mode. The converter stops switching at a fixed rate and instead fires fixed-size pulses only when the output droops. A 0.6 A pulse delivers about 0.35 microcoulombs, so 10 mA needs about 28 thousand pulses a second, not 500 thousand, and the switching loss falls by the same factor of 17. Efficiency goes from 41 to about 91 percent in my model.

Second, diode emulation: turn off the low-side FET at zero current. That removes the negative current of forced PWM, but by itself it only saves the ripple conduction, a couple of milliwatts. Third, quiescent current: the controller should shut down most of its blocks between pulses, and a higher-value feedback divider and bias from the output help. Fourth, a bigger inductor helps in PFM since each pulse carries more charge, so fewer pulses. In multiphase supplies I'd shed phases, and some ICs switch in smaller FET segments.

Finally, for microamp loads a nano-amp LDO or a load switch can beat any buck if the input is close to the output. The costs: PFM has more output ripple at lower frequencies, a slower first response to a load step, and a variable spectrum, so noise-sensitive rails use forced PWM."
:::

::: terms
- **Fixed (load-independent) loss**: loss present at zero load: gate drive, $C_{oss}$ charging, controller $I_q$, divider current.
- **Quiescent current ($I_q$)**: the current the converter draws from the input with no load.
- **PFM / burst mode**: regulation by varying the rate of fixed-size pulses. See [[q0231]].
- **Diode emulation**: low-side FET turned off at zero inductor current.
- **Phase shedding**: disabling phases of a multiphase converter at light load.
- **Pulse-skipping**: omitting switching cycles when the output is above target.
- **Forced PWM (FCCM)**: constant frequency at all loads, noise-friendly but inefficient at light load.
- **Ultrasonic mode**: PFM with a minimum frequency (about 20 to 25 kHz) so it stays inaudible.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use PFM mode" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts with a **loss breakdown** at a specific light load, showing that gate/$C_{oss}$ dominates.
- Explains **why** PFM works: energy per pulse is fixed, so loss is $E_p\times$ pulse rate, and the rate is proportional to the load.
- Separates the contributions: diode emulation (small), $I_q$ reduction (matters at µA), larger $L$ and phase shedding (medium).
- Brings up the trade-offs: output ripple/noise, transient latency, audio-band frequencies, EMI.
- Mentions an LDO or load switch as the answer at the lowest currents, with the headroom caveat.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Blaming conduction loss.** $I^2R$ falls with the *square* of the load; at 10 mA it is microwatts.
- **Saying "just lower the switching frequency" without the trade-off.** A fixed lower frequency increases ripple at all loads and may push the converter to a larger inductor/capacitor. PFM lowers frequency only when the load is light.
- **Treating diode emulation as the main fix.** In the model above it saves 1.7 mW of 48 mW. It prevents negative-current ripple but doesn't stop gate and $C_{oss}$ loss.
- **Ignoring the feedback divider.** A 5 kΩ divider draws 600 µA at 3.3 V, 2 mW, which is large against a 100 µA sleep budget ([[q0224]]).
- **Forgetting the drawbacks.** PFM gives more output ripple, a variable-frequency spectrum, and a slower first response to a load step.
- **Using an LDO when headroom is large.** A 12 V to 3.3 V LDO is 27% efficient at any load.
:::

::: deep
## Step 1: find where the power goes

Efficiency is $\eta=P_{out}/(P_{out}+P_{loss})$. At 2 A and 3.3 V ($P_{out}=6.6$ W) a loss of 0.2 W is a 3% problem. At 10 mA ($P_{out}=33$ mW), the same 0.2 W would make the efficiency 14%. What matters is which losses persist at light load.

Take the usual 12 V to 3.3 V converter at 500 kHz, with $R_{HS}=15$ mΩ, $R_{LS}=8$ mΩ, DCR 10 mΩ, 12 nC total gate charge at 5 V drive, 300 pF of switch-node $C_{oss}$, 10 ns edges and 5 mW of controller bias in PWM mode (a model, not a datasheet):

| Loss at 10 mA, forced PWM | Mechanism | Power |
|---|---|---|
| Gate drive | $Q_GV_{drv}f = 12\text{ nC}\cdot5\text{ V}\cdot500\text{ kHz}$ | 30.0 mW |
| $C_{oss}$ | $\tfrac12\cdot300\text{ pF}\cdot12^2\cdot500\text{ kHz}$ | 10.8 mW |
| Controller bias | fixed | 5.0 mW |
| Ripple conduction | $\frac{\Delta I^2}{12}R_{tot}$ with $\Delta I=1.02$ A | 1.7 mW |
| Edge overlap and dead time | $\propto I_{out}f$ | 0.4 mW |
| Load conduction | $I^2R$ | 0.002 mW |
| **Total** | | **47.9 mW** |

With $P_{out}=33$ mW, $\eta=33/(33+47.9)=41\%$. About 85% of the loss is switching per cycle, 10% is bias, 4% is ripple conduction. **That ranking tells you the order of the fixes.**

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Real devices vary. Integrated converters often have smaller gate charge at light load because they use segmented FETs, and actual quiescent currents range from nA to mA. Treat the proportions as illustrative; the method of listing the fixed per-cycle losses is what generalises.

## Step 2: stop paying per-cycle costs when there is nothing to deliver (PFM)

Per-cycle losses cost energy per pulse. If the converter only pulses as often as the load requires, loss power is *energy per pulse × pulse rate*, and the pulse rate is proportional to the load:

$$f_{pulse}=\frac{I_{out}}{Q_{pulse}},\qquad Q_{pulse}=\tfrac12 I_{pk}(t_{on}+t_{off})=\frac{L I_{pk}^2}{2}\frac{V_{in}}{(V_{in}-V_{out})V_{out}}$$

For $I_{pk}=0.6$ A, $L=4.7$ µH: $t_{on}=324$ ns, $t_{off}=855$ ns, $Q=0.354$ µC. Energy lost per pulse: conduction about 3 nJ, switching/gate/$C_{oss}$/dead-time about 104 nJ, so $E_p\approx107$ nJ and the loss power is
$$P_{loss}=\frac{E_p}{Q}I_{out}+I_{q,sleep}V_{in}=0.30\,I_{out}+I_{q,sleep}V_{in}$$
At 10 mA: 3.0 mW plus 0.24 mW of sleep bias (20 µA from 12 V) = 3.3 mW, so $\eta=33/36.3=91\%$, a 2.2× improvement over forced PWM, with a pulse rate of 28 kHz instead of 500 kHz. The efficiency of PFM is roughly constant at $1/(1+0.30/3.3)=91.6\%$ across the load range until the sleep term takes over at the bottom. See [[q0231]].

## Step 3: remove circulating current (diode emulation)

A synchronous converter in forced CCM carries the full 1.02 A p-p ripple at any load. The valley goes negative below 0.5 A, so energy is pumped into the inductor from the output and back every cycle. That is a pure loss of about $\frac{\Delta I^2}{12}R_{tot}=1.7$ mW here, plus extra switching loss. Diode emulation (turning the low-side FET off at zero current, [[q0229]]) removes it. In the table it is a small part of the total (about 4%), so it helps, but only after the switching rate has been addressed.

## Step 4: attack quiescent current

Everything the controller leaves running between pulses is a fixed drain: reference, error amplifier, comparators, bias regulators, and the feedback divider.
- Controllers designed for light load put most of the IC to sleep between bursts and wake on a low-power comparator, achieving sleep currents in the nA to µA range (check the datasheet's quiescent current in PFM mode; values vary widely by part).
- A 1 MΩ-class divider draws 0.6 µA instead of 60 µA ([[q0224]]).
- Take the controller's bias supply from the output (bootstrapped) when $V_{out}$ is lower than $V_{in}$, so the bias current is not multiplied up the step-down ratio: 20 µA from 12 V is 0.24 mW, while 20 µA from the 3.3 V output is 0.066 mW.

Quantified: at a 1 mA load, 3.3 mW is delivered; a 0.24 mW sleep loss is 7% of that, so cutting sleep $I_q$ to a tenth gains about 5 points (86% to 91% with the 4.7 µH inductor).

## Step 5: choose components and architecture for light load

- **Larger inductor.** At a fixed peak current the charge per pulse scales with $L$: 4.7 µH gives 0.354 µC, 10 µH gives 0.752 µC. The pulse rate for a given load halves, so the overhead per coulomb halves: PFM loss coefficient 0.30 W/A falls to 0.146 W/A, and 10 mA efficiency rises from 91% to 95.1%. The cost is a bigger, higher-DCR inductor and a longer first pulse.
- **Lower peak current setting.** Smaller pulses mean less ripple but more of them, raising the per-coulomb overhead. This is a ripple versus efficiency knob.
- **Phase shedding** (multiphase): at light load turn off all but one phase, so fixed per-phase gate and $I_q$ losses are not multiplied ([[q0236]]).
- **Segmented or smaller FETs at light load**: some integrated converters switch off part of the power FET, cutting gate charge.
- **Lower or foldback frequency** in non-PFM controllers.
- **Different input rail**: with $V_{in}=5$ V instead of 12 V the $C_{oss}$ term falls by 5.8× ($V^2$), since the overhead scales with $V_{in}^2$.

## Step 6: don't use a buck at all

For loads of tens of µA, an LDO with $I_q$ of about 1 µA beats nearly any buck when headroom is small: 3.6 V in, 3.3 V out at 100 µA is 90.8% efficient, 1 mA gives 91.6%. With 12 V in, it is 27.5%, so the headroom caveat is critical ([[q0211]], [[q0222]]). Another technique is a dual-path design: a buck for the active state and an LDO or load switch for the sleep state, which designers use in always-on MCU rails.

## The costs of light-load modes

- **Output ripple** is larger and lower in frequency ([[q0233]]).
- **First response to a load step** is delayed by the sleep interval and the wake-up comparator.
- **Variable frequency spectrum**: harder EMI filtering; may fall in audio band (ultrasonic mode keeps $f\gtrsim20$ kHz).
- **Mode transition glitches** at the threshold; vendors add hysteresis.

That is why most ICs expose a pin or register to force PWM when the application cannot tolerate these.

::: applications
- **Phones, earbuds and wearables**: auto PFM/PWM bucks with a few µA of sleep current, since devices idle most of the time.
- **Coin-cell and energy-harvesting IoT nodes**: nano-amp quiescent-current buck or buck-boost with PFM, wake on a comparator.
- **Always-on MCU rails**: dual path, with a buck for active mode and a tiny LDO or load-switch bypass during deep sleep.
- **Laptop/server rails**: phase shedding and diode emulation at light load, so idle power meets energy-efficiency regulations.
- **Automotive keep-alive rails**: low-$I_q$ converters so the battery is not drained when the car is parked (specs are often in the tens of µA).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Loss numbers are a model.** Gate charge, $C_{oss}$, bias and $I_q$ values were chosen to be representative of a discrete 12 V to 3.3 V converter. Integrated parts differ.
- **Same load profile**: efficiency here is $P_{out}/(P_{out}+P_{loss})$ at a given steady load; battery life depends on the *weighted* load profile.
- **Light load** means below about 5 to 10% of full load, where fixed losses begin to dominate. The exact threshold depends on the design.
- **PFM pulses** are assumed to be at a fixed 0.6 A peak, and pulse energy is constant. Real parts adapt peak current with $V_{in}$ and $V_{out}$.
- **LDO alternative** requires small $V_{in}-V_{out}$; otherwise it fails on efficiency.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a forced-PWM buck waste so much at 10 mA?
Because gate drive and $C_{oss}$ charging cost the same energy every cycle regardless of load. At 500 kHz with 12 nC of gate charge at 5 V and 300 pF at 12 V, that is about 41 mW. Against 33 mW of output power, it means efficiency near 40% before anything else is counted.

### Does diode emulation by itself fix light-load efficiency?
No. It removes negative-current ripple (here about 1.7 mW) and avoids pushing energy backwards, but the converter still switches at 500 kHz, so the dominant 41 mW remains. Diode emulation is a necessary condition for PFM and a useful refinement, not the main fix.

### What are the downsides of PFM?
Larger, lower-frequency output ripple; a variable-frequency spectrum that can reach the audio band; and a delay of tens of µs in responding to a large load step because the converter is asleep. Mitigation: bigger output cap, a minimum frequency (ultrasonic mode), or forcing PWM for sensitive loads. See [[q0233]].

### The system draws 20 µA in sleep from 3.3 V. The buck's sleep $I_q$ is 20 µA from 12 V. What is the efficiency?
Output power is $3.3\cdot20\text{ µA}=66$ µW. The converter draws at least 20 µA × 12 V = 240 µW. $\eta\le66/(66+240)=22\%$ before any pulse loss. Here the controller's own quiescent current dominates. Choose a part with a few µA or less, or bypass the buck in sleep with an LDO.

### What if the interviewer asks about increasing efficiency across the whole load range, not just light load?
Light load: fix per-cycle and quiescent losses. Mid load: lower switching frequency, better FETs ($Q_G$, $R_{DS(on)}$), synchronous rectification. Heavy load: lower DCR and $R_{DS(on)}$, multiphase to spread $I^2R$, and thermal design. See [[q0214]].
