::: testing
Whether you know the literal expansion (**Li**thium **Po**lymer) and, more importantly, what it *actually* means: the polymer refers to the **electrolyte and packaging**, not to a new battery electrochemistry. A strong candidate can say that a typical LiPo is a lithium-ion cell with a gel or polymer electrolyte in a flexible laminated pouch, can contrast it with liquid-electrolyte cylindrical Li-ion cells, and can mention the nuance that truly solid-polymer lithium cells exist but are a different (and rarer) product. This is a terminology question with a hidden test of whether you understand battery construction.
:::

::: why
"LiPo" is the word everyone uses (phones, drones, wearables, hobby packs), and it is routinely misunderstood: people think it is a distinct, more energetic chemistry or that it has no liquid inside. Knowing what it really means lets you reason about its behaviour: why it can be thin and flexible, why it swells instead of bursting a can, why it needs a rigid housing in the product, and why its safety and aging are the same as other lithium-ion cells. It also keeps you from being tripped up on questions like [[q0299]] (trade-offs) and [[q0302]] (materials).
:::

::: concepts
- **Lithium-ion chemistry**: lithium shuttles between a graphite anode and a metal-oxide cathode ([[q0297]]).
- **Electrolyte**: the ion-conducting medium; liquid organic carbonate with lithium salt in cylindrical cells, a gel/polymer matrix in LiPo.
- **Separator**: porous membrane that prevents electrode contact but passes ions ([[q0302]]).
- **Pouch cell**: stack or winding sealed in aluminum-laminate foil rather than a metal can.
- **Gel polymer electrolyte**: a polymer network swollen with liquid electrolyte, so it holds its shape.
- **Solid polymer electrolyte**: no free liquid; ion conduction through a polymer (often needs elevated temperature).
:::

::: answer
"LiPo stands for lithium polymer, sometimes lithium-ion polymer. It's easy to hear that as a different chemistry, but really it describes the electrolyte and the packaging. A lithium polymer cell is essentially a lithium-ion cell, graphite anode, lithium metal-oxide cathode, where the electrolyte is held in a polymer gel instead of being a free-flowing liquid in a steel can. Because the electrolyte is gelled, the cell doesn't need a rigid case, so it's built as a flat pouch in aluminum-laminate foil, which can be very thin and any shape. That's why phones and drones use them.

One caveat: the 'polymer' in consumer LiPo is generally a gel that still contains liquid electrolyte, so it's not a solid battery. Truly solid-polymer lithium cells exist, but they're a different technology and typically need to run warm. And the voltage, 3.7 nominal and 4.2 full, and the safety concerns, overcharge, puncture, heat, are the same as for lithium-ion."
:::

::: technical
**Expansion.** LiPo = lithium polymer; also written Li-Po, Li-poly, lithium-ion polymer (LIP) or polymer lithium-ion.

**What "polymer" refers to.** In the original concept the liquid electrolyte of a lithium-ion cell is replaced by a polymer membrane. There are two families:

| Type | Electrolyte | Typical status |
|---|---|---|
| Gel polymer (the usual "LiPo") | polymer matrix (e.g. PVDF-based) swollen with liquid carbonate electrolyte + lithium salt | mass-market, room-temperature operation |
| Solid polymer | lithium salt dissolved in a solid polymer (e.g. PEO-based) | specialised, conduction poor at room temperature (often needs about 60-80 °C; cell-dependent) |

**What stays the same as Li-ion.** Graphite (or similar) anode, lithium metal-oxide cathode (LCO, NMC...), separator, lithium hexafluorophosphate salt in organic carbonates, copper/aluminium current collectors. Nominal 3.6-3.7 V, charge limit 4.2 V (standard types), same CC/CV charging ([[q0296]]), same protection needs ([[q0295]]).

**What changes.** (1) *Packaging*: laminated aluminum pouch (typically polymer/aluminum/polymer layers) instead of a steel or aluminum can. (2) *Form factor*: flat stacked or wound electrodes, thin down to well below 3 mm in some cells, arbitrary outline. (3) *Mechanics*: lower pressure on the stack than a can provides, so cells can swell when gas is generated.

**Size naming example.** A common pouch naming convention encodes dimensions: 603450 means about 6.0 mm thick × 34 mm wide × 50 mm long. That volume is about 10.2 cm³; if the cell holds 3.7 Wh (about 1000 mAh at 3.7 V, a common rating for this size, though vendor-dependent), its volumetric energy is about 360 Wh/L.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The term "LiPo" is used loosely in industry; some vendors call any pouch cell "LiPo" even when the electrolyte is a conventional liquid, and some call any lithium cell with a gelled electrolyte "polymer". The meaning in a datasheet is whatever the vendor means; check the cell construction notes.
:::

That gives thin, custom shapes. :::

::: long
"LiPo stands for lithium polymer, or lithium-ion polymer. The name is slightly misleading because it sounds like a brand new chemistry. It isn't. A typical LiPo is a lithium-ion cell: a graphite anode, a lithium metal-oxide cathode like cobalt oxide or NMC, a separator in between, and a lithium salt electrolyte. The difference is that the electrolyte is held in a polymer matrix, a gel, instead of being a free liquid soaking a wound electrode in a steel can.

Why does that matter? Because the gelled electrolyte doesn't slosh and doesn't need the mechanical containment of a can. So manufacturers stack or fold the electrodes and seal them in a thin aluminum-laminate pouch. That makes cells that are flat, light and can be custom-sized, three millimetres thin or an odd L shape for a phone. It also gets rid of the metal can weight, which helps energy per kilogram a bit.

There's a nuance I'd mention. The gel in mass-market LiPo still contains liquid electrolyte, so it's not a solid-state battery. A true solid polymer electrolyte exists, but ion conduction is poor at room temperature, so those cells are niche and often need heating.

Electrically, a LiPo behaves like any lithium-ion cell: 3.7 volts nominal, 4.2 volts when full, charged with constant current then constant voltage, and it needs the same protection against overcharge, over-discharge, short circuit and heat. The practical differences are mechanical: the pouch has no rigid case, so it can swell as it ages and it's more fragile when punctured or bent, and the product has to give it room and protect it."
:::

::: terms
- **Lithium polymer (LiPo)**: lithium-ion cell with a polymer/gel electrolyte, usually in a laminated pouch.
- **Gel polymer electrolyte**: polymer network holding liquid electrolyte so it behaves as a gel.
- **Solid polymer electrolyte**: lithium salt in a solid polymer with no free liquid.
- **Pouch cell**: electrodes sealed in a flexible aluminium-polymer laminate.
- **Separator**: porous insulating membrane between electrodes.
- **Tab**: metal strip that brings the electrode current out of the pouch.
- **Swelling**: expansion of the pouch from gas generated by electrolyte decomposition.
- **Cylindrical / prismatic cell**: rigid-can formats (e.g. 18650).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "lithium polymer" and "it's a type of lithium battery".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Expands the acronym, then immediately says it is **not a different chemistry**: the polymer is the electrolyte/packaging.
- Explains the cause-effect chain: gelled electrolyte, no rigid can, laminated pouch, thin and custom shapes.
- Knows the nuance (gel vs fully solid polymer; consumer LiPo still contains liquid electrolyte) and the marketing looseness of the term.
- Mentions what it implies for the designer: swelling margin, fragility, same charge and protection as Li-ion.
- Draws or describes the layer stack (collector, electrode, separator, electrode, collector).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"LiPo is a completely different, more powerful chemistry."** It's the same lithium-ion electrochemistry; any difference in power comes from cell design (thin electrodes, low resistance) not the word "polymer".
- **"There is no liquid in a LiPo."** In mainstream cells the polymer is a gel holding liquid electrolyte; it still burns and can leak or swell.
- **"LiPo is solid-state."** Solid-state batteries use solid inorganic or polymer electrolytes with no liquid, a separate and still-emerging category.
- **"LiPo is safer than Li-ion."** Pouch cells are often *less* mechanically robust, with no hard case to resist puncture or pressure, so safety depends on the same protection circuitry and handling ([[q0327]]).
- **"The polymer is the separator/pouch plastic."** The pouch does contain polymer layers, but the name refers to the electrolyte.
- **Treating LiPo and LiFePO4 as the same.** LiFePO4 names the cathode material; LiPo names the electrolyte/format. A pouch cell can use either cathode.
:::

::: deep
## Name versus meaning

The battery community uses three different naming systems, and confusion comes from mixing them:

| Naming axis | Examples | What it describes |
|---|---|---|
| Electrode chemistry | LCO, NMC, NCA, LFP, LTO | cathode (and sometimes anode) material |
| Electrolyte type | liquid, gel polymer, solid polymer, solid ceramic | the ion conductor |
| Cell format | cylindrical (18650), prismatic, pouch, coin | the packaging |

"LiPo" lives mostly on the electrolyte axis (polymer) but in practice also implies the pouch format. A "LiPo" can be LCO/graphite (a typical phone), NMC/graphite (a drone), or even LiFePO4. So when someone says LiPo they have told you about the electrolyte and shape but nothing about the cathode, which is what sets voltage and life ([[q0300]]).

## Why a polymer (gel) electrolyte allows a pouch

In a conventional cylindrical cell the electrodes are long strips wound with a separator into a jelly-roll and stuffed into a steel can with liquid electrolyte. The can contains the pressure, keeps the stack tightly compressed (good ionic contact) and seals the liquid. If the electrolyte is gelled into a polymer matrix, it stays in the pores of the electrodes and separator without being free-flowing, and in some designs the polymer bonds the layers together. That means the mechanical compression and containment from a can are less essential, and the cell can be sealed with a light foil pouch.

## What you gain and give up

**Gains.** Thin and arbitrary shapes (3 mm or less, custom outlines). Lower non-active mass because there is no steel can, so energy per kg and per litre of the *pack* can be a bit higher. Flexible cell design: stacked pouch cells can have very low resistance for high-C hobby/drone packs.

**Costs.** The pouch is mechanically weak, so the product must support it and keep it away from sharp edges; pressure from gas is not contained, so aging shows up as swelling ([[q0301]]); tab seals and laminate edges can leak or corrode if damaged; and the lack of a hard shell means a puncture is more severe ([[q0329]]). Cost per kWh is typically not lower than cylindrical cells, which are produced in the largest volumes.

## What about "solid-state"?

True solid-state cells replace the liquid with a solid electrolyte (polymer, sulfide, oxide). Polymer-only electrolytes like PEO-lithium salt have low ionic conductivity at room temperature, so early solid-polymer cells were designed to operate at elevated temperature (on the order of 60-80 °C; specifics vary). They are not what a phone contains. Hybrid "gel polymer" cells are the practical compromise: some of the mechanical and safety benefits of a polymer without the conductivity penalty.

## Worked sizing example

A pouch cell labelled 603450 is about 6.0 × 34 × 50 mm = 10.2 cm³. If it stores 3.7 Wh (1000 mAh × 3.7 V), the volumetric energy density is $3.7/0.0102 = 363$ Wh/L. A 400 Wh/L high-end phone cell would fit 4.1 Wh into the same volume, which is about 10% more energy for the same space. Real products must also leave about 5-10% extra thickness for aging swelling (vendor- and product-dependent), which is why the datasheet's "max thickness" matters more than the nominal.

## How little gas it takes to make a pouch swell

A can resists swelling with steel. A pouch does not, so it is worth knowing how much gas turns a flat cell into a pillow. Take the 603450 cell from the example: 6.0 × 34 × 50 mm. A swelling of 10% of the thickness, 0.6 mm, adds a volume of $34\times50\times0.6=1020\ \text{mm}^3\approx1.02\ \text{cm}^3$. At atmospheric pressure and 25 °C the ideal-gas law gives

$$n=\frac{PV}{RT}=\frac{101325\times1.02\times10^{-6}}{8.314\times298}=4.2\times10^{-5}\ \text{mol}=42\ \mu\text{mol}$$

which is only about 1.8 mg of CO₂. That is a minute fraction of the several grams of electrolyte in a cell of this size, so a swollen pouch does not need a dramatic failure; slow decomposition of 0.1% of the electrolyte is enough to add a visible 10% to the thickness. A 20% bulge (1.2 mm) takes only 83 µmol. This is why swelling is such a sensitive early-warning indicator, and why a phone with a swollen cell can lift its screen even though the battery still tests "fine". It also shows why the thickness tolerance in the mechanical design (the extra space the cell is allowed to grow into) has to be set from the datasheet's end-of-life thickness and not the new-cell thickness.

## Ionic resistance: why gel works and solid polymer is hard

The electrolyte layer is a resistor: $R_{ion}=\dfrac{d}{\sigma A}$, with thickness $d$, ionic conductivity $\sigma$ and area $A$. For one layer of a 20 µm separator over a 34 × 50 mm electrode ($A=1.7\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$), and an effective conductivity of about 1 mS/cm (0.1 S/m, a representative figure for a liquid or gel electrolyte inside a porous separator, once porosity and tortuosity are accounted for), the ionic resistance is

$$R_{ion}=\frac{20\times10^{-6}}{0.1\times1.7\times10^{-3}}\approx0.12\ \Omega$$

per layer. A cell contains many layers (or one long wound strip) effectively in parallel, so the ionic contribution to the total is a small fraction of the cell resistance. Now suppose a solid polymer electrolyte that is 100 times less conductive at room temperature: the same geometry gives about 12 Ω per layer, and the same cell would have resistance too high to use at room temperature. Raising the temperature to 60 to 80 °C increases conductivity (the reason early solid-polymer cells were hot), but that is not a pocket device. The gel route avoids the penalty by keeping a liquid inside the polymer.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The conductivity values are representative orders of magnitude, not data for any product. Gel and solid polymer electrolytes vary widely in composition and conductivity, and published figures depend on temperature and measurement method.

## Current and the tabs: the limit in high-C packs

A high-power pouch for a drone may carry 30 A or more through one tab. A typical aluminum tab might be 6 mm wide and 0.2 mm thick (assumed), so the cross-section is 1.2 mm². Its current density at 30 A is 25 A/mm². Over a 10 mm length, resistance is $\rho L/A=2.8\times10^{-8}\times0.01/(1.2\times10^{-6})=0.23\ \text{m}\Omega$, and the heat is $30^2\times0.23\times10^{-3}=0.21$ W, concentrated at the seal where the pouch is least able to shed heat. Compared with the cell's own internal resistance (a few milliohms for a high-power cell) the tab is small, but its heat comes out at one spot, so it is where overload shows up first as discoloration or seal softening. This is why the connector and the soldered or welded tab joint (not the cell) are often the real current limit of an RC pack, and why thick tabs, multiple parallel tabs, and short, thick leads matter.

## Mechanical design rules for pouch cells

- **Never fold or bend the tab sharply at the seal**: the polymer-coated tab section is the sealing interface, and metal fatigue or a cracked sealant lets moisture in.
- **Give the cell a flat support and a swelling gap.** Constrain it with soft foam so it is held without point loads. Hard edges or screws that press on the pouch create local pressure that can cause internal shorts.
- **Keep sharp edges and adhesive-backed parts away** from the laminate. A scratch through the polymer layer to the aluminum can create a path for corrosion or a short to the case.
- **Never solder directly to a cell's tabs with a hot iron** unless the vendor allows it; the heat can reach the seal and the internal stack. Use the vendor's lead wires or weld-ready tabs.

## Verifying a pouch cell on the bench

1. Measure thickness at the four corners and center with calipers (or a flat plate and gauge) at fixed SoC, and track the number over cycles. A thickness increase of more than the vendor's allowance is a rejection criterion.
2. Weigh the cell: gas does not change mass, but electrolyte loss through a leaking seal does.
3. Measure 1 kHz AC resistance and DC resistance from a pulse ([[q0291]]): a rise with swelling points to degradation.
4. Check the tab temperature with a thermocouple or thermal camera at maximum current, since that is where the heat is concentrated.
5. For a design review, check the swelling gap numerically: end-of-life thickness (say 8% above the datasheet's initial value) plus the stack tolerance has to fit the enclosure with margin.

::: followups
### Is LiPo different from Li-ion electrochemically?
Not fundamentally. Both shuttle lithium ions between a graphite-type anode and a lithium metal-oxide cathode. The differences are the electrolyte form (gel vs liquid) and packaging (pouch vs can). Differences in performance (power, energy) come from cell design: electrode thickness, resistance, and the cathode chemistry chosen.

### Why do LiPo cells swell?
Aging and abuse decompose the electrolyte, generating gas (CO₂, CO, hydrocarbons) inside the sealed pouch. There's no rigid can to contain the pressure, so the pouch bulges. Swelling is a sign of degradation or abuse (overcharge, heat) and a swollen cell should be retired ([[q0301]]).

### Why do drones use LiPo and not 18650s?
Low resistance and light weight. Stacked pouch cells with many parallel layers can deliver very high currents (tens of C) with small sag, and no steel can saves mass. Cylindrical cells are more robust and cheaper per Wh, so some larger or long-range drones do use 18650/21700 packs where energy matters more than burst power.

### Is a LiPo safer than a cylindrical cell?
Not inherently. Both can go into thermal runaway if abused. The pouch gives no mechanical containment, so it is more vulnerable to puncture and swelling, but its flat shape spreads heat well. Safety comes from cell quality, protection electronics and proper housing rather than the format.

### If a vendor says "solid polymer", what should you ask?
Ask about the operating temperature range and ionic conductivity, internal resistance across temperature, whether it is a gel or truly solid, and what safety and transport certifications it has. A solid polymer cell that needs 60-80 °C operation would be unsuitable for a pocket device.
:::
