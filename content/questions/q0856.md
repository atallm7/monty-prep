::: basics
### In plain words
A camera is a lens that projects a picture of the scene onto a chip covered in a grid of tiny light meters. Each light meter, called a pixel, collects light for a short time and turns it into a number. Put all the numbers together and you have the image.

### Picture it
Hold a magnifying glass near a sheet of paper and a small, sharp picture of the window appears on it. Now replace the paper with a grid of millions of tiny light meters, each reading one dot of that picture. The lens decides where each bit of the scene lands. The picture breaks down in one way: the meters cannot tell colors apart, so each one gets a tiny red, green or blue filter, and the missing colors are guessed afterward.

### Words you need
- **Pixel**: one light meter on the chip. It turns light into electrons, tiny packets of electric charge.
- **Exposure**: the time the pixel spends collecting light, together with how wide the lens opening is.
- **Color filter array (Bayer)**: a mosaic of red, green and blue filters, one per pixel. See [[q0862]].
- **ADC**: a circuit that turns a voltage into a number. See [[q0808]].
- **Image signal processor (ISP)**: the chip block that turns raw numbers into a finished picture.

### The simplest example
Say 400 photons land on one pixel during the exposure, and 60 percent of them free an electron. The pixel collects 240 electrons. The ADC turns that into a number. Light arrives randomly, so the count wobbles by about its square root: about 15 electrons. Now dim the scene to a quarter of the light. The pixel gets 60 electrons, with noise of about 8. The signal fell four times, but the noise only halved, so the dim image looks grainier. The rest of this page follows the chain from lens to finished picture, and where noise and blur come from.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can follow a photon all the way to a pixel value and name each block on the way: optics, color filter, photodiode, charge-to-voltage conversion, ADC, and the digital processing. The interviewer wants to hear that an image sensor is a **2-D array of light meters** and that every later artifact (noise, blur, color error) traces back to a specific stage. Strong candidates connect it to hardware numbers: pixel pitch, exposure time, f-number, full-well capacity, bit depth. They also show they know a pixel only counts photons and has no idea what color they were.
:::

::: why
Cameras are the highest-volume sensor in consumer hardware. A phone has three to five of them, cars have eight or more, and almost every robot or AR headset depends on one. As a hardware intern you may be asked to bring up a sensor over MIPI, debug a noisy image, or pick a sensor for a product, and all of that starts with a correct mental model of the signal chain. This is also the opening question for the whole camera block ([[q0857]] to [[q0863]]), so a clean, structured answer here sets up everything else.
:::

::: concepts
- **Image formation**: a lens bends light from each scene point to one point on the sensor, so the sensor sees a sharp 2-D image (thin-lens equation $1/f = 1/u + 1/v$).
- **Exposure**: the amount of light collected is set by aperture (f-number), exposure time and the scene's brightness. More photons means a better signal-to-noise ratio ([[q0860]]).
- **Photodiode and photoelectric effect**: each pixel converts incoming photons into electrons, roughly $N_e = \text{QE}\cdot N_{photons}$, with quantum efficiency (QE) typically 50 to 80 percent in visible light for modern silicon.
- **Color filter array (Bayer)**: each pixel sits under one red, green or blue filter, so full color has to be reconstructed later (demosaicing, [[q0862]]).
- **Readout and ADC**: collected charge is converted to a voltage, amplified (analog gain, which sets ISO), and digitized to 10 to 14 bits ([[q0808]] for ADC basics).
- **Image signal processor (ISP)**: turns raw Bayer data into a viewable image ([[q0862]]).
:::

::: answer
"A camera is basically a lens that projects the scene onto a chip full of tiny light meters. The lens focuses light so every point in the scene lands on one spot on the sensor. Each pixel is a photodiode: photons hit silicon, knock electrons loose by the photoelectric effect, and the pixel collects those electrons during the exposure time. More light or a longer exposure means more electrons.

On top of each pixel there's a microlens to funnel light in, and a red, green or blue color filter in the Bayer pattern, because a photodiode only counts photons and can't tell colors apart. When the exposure ends, the charge in each pixel is converted to a voltage, amplified by an analog gain, which is what ISO really is, and digitized by an ADC, usually 10 to 12 bits on a phone. That gives a raw mosaic.

Then the image signal processor does the rest: it fixes bad pixels and lens shading, demosaics to get full color at every pixel, applies white balance and a color matrix, denoises, tone-maps, sharpens, and compresses to JPEG or HEIC. The autofocus, auto exposure and white-balance loop runs in the background to set the lens position, exposure time and gain for the next frame. So the chain is optics, sensor, ADC, ISP, with a control loop wrapped around it."
:::

::: technical
**Optics.** For a lens of focal length $f$ and an object at distance $u$, the image forms at $v = \frac{fu}{u-f}$. For a phone main camera with $f \approx 5.6$ mm focused at 1 m, $v = 5.63$ mm, so focusing from infinity to 1 m only needs about 31 µm of lens travel (a voice-coil actuator does this). Light gathering scales as $1/N^2$ where $N = f/D$ is the f-number, and diffraction limits the spot size to about $2.44\,\lambda N$ (2.4 µm at f/1.8, 550 nm).

**Sensor.** A CMOS image sensor (CIS) is an array of 4T pixels: photodiode, transfer gate, reset transistor, source follower and row-select. Exposure collects charge on the photodiode; the transfer gate dumps it onto a floating diffusion with conversion gain of roughly 50 to 150 µV/e⁻ (varies by design); a source follower buffers the voltage; column-parallel ADCs (often single-slope) digitize each row. Correlated double sampling (CDS) subtracts the reset level and removes reset (kTC) noise.

**Signal model.** Digital number $\approx g\,(N_e + N_{dark}) + \text{offset}$, with noise variance $\sigma^2 = \sigma_{shot}^2 + \sigma_{read}^2 + \sigma_{dark}^2$, where shot noise $=\sqrt{N_e}$ (Poisson). SNR $= N_e/\sqrt{N_e + \sigma_{read}^2}$.

**Dynamic range.** DR = full-well capacity / read noise. 6000 e⁻ over 2.5 e⁻ is 67.6 dB, or 11.2 stops, for a single exposure.

**Shutter.** Most phone sensors use a rolling shutter: rows are exposed and read out sequentially (a readout of a few to tens of ms per frame), so fast motion skews. A global shutter exposes all pixels at once and needs extra storage in each pixel.

**Color and processing.** Raw Bayer data is 1 sample per pixel; demosaic interpolates the missing two colors. The ISP chain is covered in [[q0862]], and the sequence of events around a shutter press in [[q0863]].
:::

"A camera focuses the scene with a lens onto a sensor made of millions of photodiodes. Each pixel converts photons to electrons during the exposure, under a Bayer color filter. The charge is converted to a voltage, amplified, which is the ISO gain, and digitized by an ADC into a raw mosaic. An image signal processor then demosaics, white-balances, denoises, tone-maps and compresses it, while an autofocus, auto-exposure and white-balance loop controls the lens, exposure time and gain for the next frame."
:::

::: long
"I like to follow a photon through the system. First is the optics. A lens, really a stack of several plastic elements in a phone, focuses light so each scene point lands on one point on the sensor. The f-number sets how much light gets in, and a voice-coil motor moves the lens a few tens of microns to focus. An infrared cut filter blocks light the silicon can see but we don't want.

Second is the sensor. Each pixel has a microlens, a color filter, red, green or blue in a Bayer pattern, and a photodiode. Photons generate electrons by the photoelectric effect, and about half to three quarters of the photons make an electron, that's the quantum efficiency. The pixel integrates those electrons for the exposure time. The statistics matter: photons arrive randomly, so the noise on N electrons is the square root of N, which is why dark scenes are noisy.

Third is readout. The charge moves to a floating diffusion, a source follower converts it to a voltage, correlated double sampling removes reset noise, an analog gain stage sets ISO, and column ADCs digitize it, typically 10 to 12 bits, sometimes 14. A rolling shutter does this row by row.

Fourth is the ISP. It subtracts black level, fixes bad pixels, corrects lens shading, demosaics, applies white balance and a color matrix, denoises, tone-maps and sharpens, then compresses.

And wrapped around all of it is a control loop. Auto exposure, autofocus and auto white balance measure the last frames and set exposure time, gain and lens position for the next. So: optics, photodiode, ADC, ISP, and a feedback loop that keeps all of it tuned to the scene."
:::

::: terms
- **Pixel pitch**: center-to-center spacing of pixels, around 0.6 to 2.4 µm on phones. Larger pixels collect more light.
- **Quantum efficiency (QE)**: fraction of incident photons that become collected electrons.
- **Full-well capacity**: maximum electrons a pixel can hold before saturating (clipping).
- **Read noise**: noise added by the readout electronics, in electrons rms.
- **Conversion gain**: volts per electron at the floating diffusion.
- **Bayer pattern**: RGGB mosaic of color filters, with twice as many green pixels as red or blue.
- **Demosaicing**: interpolating the two missing color values at each pixel.
- **Rolling vs global shutter**: row-by-row exposure versus all pixels exposed simultaneously.
- **f-number**: focal length divided by aperture diameter, light collected scales as $1/N^2$.
- **ISO**: the analog (and digital) gain applied before digitization.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "light goes through a lens, hits the sensor and becomes a picture." An exceptional answer:

- Names the block chain (lens, IR filter, microlens, CFA, photodiode, readout and ADC, ISP) and says what each block adds or loses.
- Explains that a pixel measures only intensity, so color is reconstructed from a Bayer mosaic.
- States that the fundamental limit is photon shot noise, $\text{SNR}\approx\sqrt{N_e}$, and ties it to pixel size, aperture and exposure time.
- Mentions ISO is a gain, not extra light.
- Gives a number or two: pixel pitch, bit depth, dynamic range or lens travel.
- Notes the control loop (3A) around the pipeline.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The sensor sees color."** The photodiode is color-blind; the Bayer filter plus demosaicing creates color. A third of a pixel's color information is measured, and two thirds are interpolated.
- **"Higher ISO makes the sensor more sensitive."** ISO is amplification after the photons are collected. It brings the signal above ADC and downstream noise but does not add photons or improve shot-noise SNR.
- **"More megapixels is a better camera."** Packing more pixels in the same sensor area shrinks each one and lowers its light collection; the lens may also not resolve that detail (diffraction at 2.4 µm for f/1.8).
- **Mixing CCD and CMOS.** CCDs shift charge out to a single converter; modern phone sensors are CMOS with a converter in every column.
- **Forgetting the lens and the infrared filter.** Silicon responds out to about 1000 nm, so without an IR cut filter colors look wrong.
:::

::: deep
## From scene to photons

The scene reflects light with luminance $L$ (cd/m²). An ideal lens with f-number $N$ and transmission $T$ produces an image-plane illuminance of roughly

$$E_{img} \approx \frac{\pi\,L\,T}{4N^2}$$

so doubling $N$ (one "stop" is a factor of $\sqrt2$ in $N$) cuts the light by four. For a mid-gray patch lit by 500 lux (typical office), $L = E\rho/\pi \approx 29$ cd/m² for 18 percent reflectance. With a rough conversion of $4.1\times10^{15}$ photons per m² per second per lux at 555 nm (an approximation for broadband light, treat as order-of-magnitude), a 1.0 µm pixel behind an f/1.8 lens with T = 0.9 receives about 850 photons in 1/30 s. At QE = 0.6 that is about 510 electrons, so shot-noise-limited SNR $\approx\sqrt{510}\approx 22.6$ (27 dB). For a 1.4 µm pixel it is about 1000 electrons (31.6, or 30 dB). Dim a scene to 5 lux and the 1.0 µm pixel gets about 5 electrons in 1/30 s: SNR about 2. That is the physics behind [[q0858]] and [[q0860]].

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The photons-per-lux figure depends on the spectrum (555 nm is the peak of the eye's response; white LED light has a different photon count per lux). Treat the electron counts here as order-of-magnitude, not as sensor specifications. Real pixel QE, microlens efficiency and lens transmission differ by vendor.

Figure: In a back-side-illuminated (BSI) sensor the wiring sits under the photodiodes, so light reaches the photodiode without passing through the metal layers. The microlens focuses light onto the photodiode (raising effective fill factor) and the filter above each pixel lets only red, green or blue through.

Inside the silicon, a photon of wavelength $\lambda$ with energy above the bandgap (1.12 eV, i.e. below about 1100 nm) can create an electron-hole pair. Blue light is absorbed within a fraction of a micron, red light penetrates several microns, which is why deep photodiodes help red and near-IR sensitivity.

**4T pixel operation** (one frame, one row):

1. **Reset**: reset transistor clears the floating diffusion (FD) to $V_{DD}$; the source follower output is sampled (reset level).
2. **Integrate**: photodiode accumulates electrons for the exposure time $T_{exp}$.
3. **Transfer**: transfer gate pulses, moving charge to the FD, whose small capacitance gives the conversion gain $q/C_{FD}$. With $C_{FD}$ = 1.5 fF, $q/C_{FD} = 1.6\times10^{-19}/1.5\times10^{-15} = 107$ µV per electron.
4. **Sample and digitize**: signal level minus reset level (correlated double sampling) goes to the column ADC.

CDS matters because resetting a capacitor leaves a random charge of rms $\sqrt{kTC}$, which is tens of electrons on a plain capacitor. Subtracting the pre-transfer reset sample cancels it, leaving a read noise of only 1 to 3 electrons rms in a good modern CMOS sensor (varies by sensor and gain).

## Noise, SNR and dynamic range

The pixel signal $S$ (electrons) has these noise terms:

| Source | Behavior | Depends on |
|---|---|---|
| Photon shot noise | $\sqrt{S}$ | scene brightness |
| Read noise | constant, $\sigma_r$ e⁻ | readout circuit |
| Dark current shot noise | $\sqrt{I_{d}T_{exp}}$ | temperature, exposure time |
| Fixed pattern noise | proportional or fixed | pixel mismatch (corrected in calibration) |
| Quantization | $\text{LSB}/\sqrt{12}$ | ADC bits |

**Dynamic range.** The ratio of full-well to read noise: $20\log_{10}(6000/2.5) = 67.6$ dB, 11.2 stops. Real scenes (sun and shade) can exceed 14 to 20 stops, which is why phones fuse several exposures (HDR) and why [[q0861]] lists dynamic range as a key figure of merit.

## Reading the sensor

Rolling-shutter timing: with 3000 rows and a row time of 10 µs, the readout takes 30 ms, so the top and bottom rows are exposed 30 ms apart. A vertical edge moving across the frame in that time becomes slanted (skew); a fast camera pan produces "jello" wobble ([[q0857]] explains how EIS must undo this).

Raw data volume: a 12 MP frame at 10 bits is 15 MB, and at 30 fps it is 3.6 Gbit/s. That is why phone sensors use MIPI CSI-2 lanes at multiple Gbit/s each and often bin pixels (for example 2x2 or 4x4) to cut the data rate, which also lifts SNR.

## What the control loop does

The camera subsystem is a closed loop. Auto exposure picks $T_{exp}$ and gain so the average brightness hits a target (often around an 18 percent mid-gray, depending on tuning). Autofocus moves the VCM using contrast, phase-detect pixels or a ToF/laser ranger. Auto white balance estimates the illuminant so the ISP can scale R, G, B correctly. All three use statistics produced by the ISP every frame and update the sensor registers over I²C or CCI ([[q0863]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Smartphone main cameras**: 50 MP stacked BSI sensors at about 1.0 µm pitch, binned 4-to-1 to 12 MP / 2.0 µm effective pixels in low light.
- **Automotive surround-view and ADAS cameras**: HDR sensors with 120 dB single-frame dynamic range using split-pixel or multi-exposure readout.
- **Machine vision and robotics**: global-shutter sensors to avoid skew on conveyors and moving arms.
- **Webcams and laptops**: small low-cost sensors where the ISP may be inside the SoC.
- **Scientific / astronomy cameras**: large pixels, deep cooling, read noise below 2 e⁻ for photon-starved imaging.
- **Machine-learning sensors**: always-on low-power image sensors that output processed data rather than a full-resolution stream.
:::

::: assumptions
- I describe a **CMOS image sensor with a Bayer filter, rolling shutter and a lens-based camera**, which covers nearly all phones. A CCD, a monochrome sensor, a Foveon-style stacked sensor or a global-shutter sensor differ in readout details but share the same photon-to-electron physics.
- The thin-lens equation is an approximation; real lenses have 5 to 7 elements, distortion and aberrations.
- The light-level numbers are order-of-magnitude. Actual QE, transmission, microlens efficiency and conversion gain vary by vendor.
- If the interviewer asks "how does a *pinhole* camera work", the same geometry applies without the lens: light rays go through one point and the image is inverted, but the aperture is so small that light gathering is poor and diffraction blurs the image.
- Event cameras, time-of-flight cameras, and LiDAR ([[q0867]]) are different sensors and are not covered here.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a camera need a lens at all? Why not just put a sensor in front of the scene?
Without a lens every pixel receives light from every point in the scene, so you get an average, not an image. The lens maps each direction to a location, so each pixel sees a narrow cone of the scene. A pinhole does the same but gathers almost no light, which is why we use a lens.

### What does ISO actually do?
ISO is the analog gain (plus some digital gain) applied before or during digitization. Doubling ISO doubles the signal and noise from earlier stages, so it keeps the signal above the ADC's quantization and read noise from later stages. It does not add photons. In low-light, higher ISO looks noisy because the shot noise from few photons is amplified too. If shot noise dominates, SNR is the same at any ISO.

### Why is the Bayer pattern 50 percent green?
The human eye is most sensitive to green and gets most of its luminance detail from it, so the sensor samples green twice as densely as red or blue. A 12 MP sensor therefore has 6 million green, 3 million red and 3 million blue samples, and demosaicing interpolates the rest.

### How would the image change if you cut the pixel pitch from 1.4 µm to 0.7 µm at the same sensor size and f-number?
You have four times as many pixels, and each collects one quarter of the light. Per-pixel SNR drops by about half (shot-noise SNR scales with the square root of light, so $\sqrt{1/4} = 1/2$, or 6 dB). If you bin 2x2 back down, total light per output pixel is the same, but the sensor now handles more read noise (unless binned in charge domain) and lens diffraction (2.4 µm Airy diameter at f/1.8) limits any real resolution gain.

### Scenario: your prototype's image is fine outdoors but very noisy and tinted green indoors under LED light. Where do you look?
Indoors the light level is 50 to 100 times lower, so shot noise rises; first check exposure time and gain limits. Then white balance: the AWB statistics may be failing under narrow-band LED spectra, so check the color correction matrix for that illuminant. Finally check flicker: if exposure is not a multiple of the light's flicker period, you get banding ([[q0860]]). I'd capture a raw frame with a color chart to separate sensor, ISP tuning and lighting problems.
:::
