::: basics
### In plain words
Transmission line theory is circuit theory for wires that are "electrically long". Normally we treat a wire as one point with the same voltage everywhere. That works only while signals change slowly compared with their travel time along it. With fast edges, a signal travels as a wave, and the wire can reflect part of it back.

### Picture it
Hold one end of a long rope and flick it. A wave runs down the rope. The far end does not move until the wave arrives, and there the wave can bounce back. A short rope flicked slowly moves as one piece. A fast signal on a long trace is the flick. The electrical wave also needs a nearby return conductor.

### Words you need
- **Propagation delay**: the time a signal takes to travel along a wire.
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: the ratio of voltage to current in a wave travelling along the line ([[q0710]]).
- **Reflection**: part of a wave bouncing back where the line changes ([[q0711]]).
- **Electrically long**: long enough that its delay matters compared with the signal's edge time ([[q0709]]).
- **Return path**: the second conductor, usually a ground plane, that carries the current back.

### The simplest example
On a typical circuit board, signals move about 5.9 ps per mm, so a 15 cm trace has a delay of about 0.9 ns. Send a 1 ns edge down it. The driver end is still rising when the front reaches the far end, so the two ends see different voltages and "one voltage everywhere" fails.

A common rule of thumb treats a trace as a transmission line when its delay exceeds about one sixth of the edge time. For 1 ns edges, that is any trace longer than about 3 cm.

That is the whole idea: fast edges make wires behave as waves. The rest of this page builds the model from tiny inductors and capacitors and shows where $Z_0$ comes from.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand that "a wire is a wire" is only true when signals are slow, and whether you can say *why* the wire stops behaving like a node. The interviewer wants to hear four ideas in your own words: signals take finite time to travel, so a trace has to be modelled as distributed $L$ and $C$ rather than one lumped element; that model gives a **characteristic impedance** and a **delay**; mismatched impedances cause **reflections**; and that is why high-speed boards use controlled-impedance traces and termination. Bonus points for knowing the rule of thumb for when it starts to matter, and that the same theory covers coax, cables, connectors and package leads.
:::

::: why
Almost every real interface that isn't slow (DDR, USB, PCIe, Ethernet, HDMI, SPI at tens of MHz, fast clocks, even a 1 ns edge on a 10 cm trace) is a transmission line problem in disguise. If you treat the trace as an ideal wire you get ringing, overshoot that stresses input pins, false clock edges, closed eyes and EMI that appears "out of nowhere". Knowing TL theory lets you predict that before the board is built, choose trace widths and stack-up for a target impedance, decide where termination goes, and read a scope or TDR trace correctly when something is off. It is the foundation of the whole signal-integrity section of the interview.
:::

::: concepts
- **Finite propagation speed**: signals travel at $v = c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$, roughly half the speed of light on FR-4. A 15 cm trace is about 0.9 ns long. See [[q0023]] for permittivity.
- **Distributed parasitics**: every length of trace has series inductance and shunt capacitance to its return path. See [[q0039]] and [[q0022]].
- **Rise time vs delay**: what matters is how the *edge* compares to the trip time, not the clock frequency. See [[q0117]].
- **Characteristic impedance $Z_0$**: the ratio of voltage to current in a wave travelling on the line. Covered in [[q0710]].
- **Reflection coefficient $\Gamma$**: how much of a wave bounces off an impedance change ([[q0711]]).
- **Lumped vs distributed**: lumped analysis (KVL/KCL, [[q0004]]) assumes the whole circuit sees the same voltage at the same instant.
:::

::: answer
"Transmission line theory is circuit theory for wires that are electrically long. Normally I treat a wire as a node: the voltage is the same everywhere along it at the same instant. That assumption is really saying the signal arrives instantly. But a signal on a PCB trace travels at roughly half the speed of light, so a 15 cm trace takes about a nanosecond. If my edge rate is also around a nanosecond, the far end hasn't even heard about the edge when the near end is already half way through it.

So instead I model the trace as a chain of tiny series inductors and shunt capacitors, a distributed model. That gives me a wave equation instead of a lumped equation. Out of it comes two numbers that describe the line, the characteristic impedance $Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}$ and the delay, and one rule: wherever the impedance changes, part of the wave reflects, with coefficient $\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L + Z_0)$.

We need it because reflections turn into ringing, overshoot and false edges. With TL theory I can pick trace geometry for a controlled impedance, add termination, and avoid stubs. When the trace is short compared with the edge, it collapses back to lumped analysis, so it's the more general theory rather than a competing one."
:::

::: technical
**Distributed model.** Divide the signal conductor and its return into slices of length $\Delta z$. Each slice has series resistance $R'\Delta z$, series inductance $L'\Delta z$, shunt conductance $G'\Delta z$ (dielectric loss) and shunt capacitance $C'\Delta z$, where the primed quantities are *per unit length*.

**Telegrapher's equations** (from KVL and KCL on one slice as $\Delta z\to 0$):

$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial z} = -R' i - L'\frac{\partial i}{\partial t},\qquad \frac{\partial i}{\partial z} = -G' v - C'\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}$$

**Lossless case** ($R'=G'=0$): combining gives the wave equation $\partial^2 v/\partial z^2 = L'C'\,\partial^2 v/\partial t^2$ with solution
$$v(z,t) = f(t - z/v_p) + g(t + z/v_p),\quad v_p = \frac{1}{\sqrt{L'C'}}$$
a forward wave $f$ and a backward wave $g$. For each wave $v/i = \pm Z_0$ with $Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}$.

**What you get from it:**
- Delay per length $t_{pd} = \sqrt{L'C'} = \sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/c$ (about 5.9 ps/mm in FR-4 microstrip, about 6.9 ps/mm in stripline with $\varepsilon_r=4.3$).
- Reflection coefficient at a boundary: $\Gamma = \dfrac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}$.
- Input impedance of a line of length $\ell$: $Z_{in} = Z_0\dfrac{Z_L + jZ_0\tan\beta\ell}{Z_0 + jZ_L\tan\beta\ell}$.
- Loss and dispersion once $R'$ (skin effect) and $G'$ (dielectric) are included ([[q0056]], [[q0058]]).

**When you need it.** A common rule of thumb: treat the interconnect as a transmission line when the one-way delay exceeds about one sixth (some use one quarter or one tenth) of the signal's rise time, or equivalently when its length exceeds roughly $\lambda/10$ at the highest significant frequency, about $0.35/t_r$. Below that, a lumped model is fine. See [[q0709]].
:::

::: short
"Transmission line theory models a wire as distributed series inductance and shunt capacitance, because signals take real time to travel along it. That gives a characteristic impedance $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$, a propagation delay, and reflections wherever impedance changes, with $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$. We need it when the trace delay is a meaningful fraction of the signal's rise time, because then ordinary node-voltage analysis fails and you get ringing and overshoot unless you control impedance and terminate."
:::

::: long
"Ordinary circuit analysis assumes a wire is a single node: every point has the same voltage at the same time. That's the same as saying signals travel infinitely fast. In reality they travel at about half the speed of light on a PCB, so a 15 cm trace has about 0.9 ns of delay. If my edge takes a nanosecond or less to rise, the two ends of the trace see different voltages during the edge, and the lumped picture breaks.

Transmission line theory fixes that by modelling the trace as a long ladder of tiny series inductors and shunt capacitors, each slice with inductance and capacitance per unit length. Writing KVL and KCL on one slice and shrinking it gives the telegrapher's equations, and in the lossless case they reduce to a wave equation: a forward wave and a backward wave moving at $1/\sqrt{L'C'}$. The ratio of voltage to current in each wave is the characteristic impedance, $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$, typically 50 ohms single-ended and 100 ohms differential.

The practical result is the reflection rule: when a wave hits a change in impedance, a fraction $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$ goes back. An open end reflects everything with the same sign, a short reflects with the opposite sign, and a load equal to $Z_0$ reflects nothing. Those bounces are the ringing and overshoot you see on a scope.

So I need the theory to decide trace width and stack-up so the impedance is controlled, to place termination, to avoid stubs and to know how long a trace can be before it matters. A rule of thumb is that if the one-way delay is more than about a sixth of the rise time, I treat it as a transmission line. Otherwise lumped is fine, since the theory contains lumped as a special case."
:::

::: terms
- **Transmission line**: two conductors (signal plus return) carrying a wave whose behaviour is described by per-unit-length parameters instead of single lumped values.
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: $\sqrt{L'/C'}$ for a lossless line, the voltage-to-current ratio of a single travelling wave ([[q0710]]).
- **Propagation delay ($t_{pd}$, $t_d$)**: time per unit length ($\sqrt{L'C'}$) or total one-way time of the line.
- **Telegrapher's equations**: the coupled differential equations for $v(z,t)$ and $i(z,t)$ on a line.
- **Reflection coefficient ($\Gamma$)**: ratio of the reflected to incident wave at an impedance discontinuity.
- **Electrically long**: a line whose delay is a significant fraction of the signal's rise time (or whose length is a significant fraction of a wavelength).
- **Return path**: the second conductor (usually a ground plane) that carries the return current. The line impedance is defined with respect to it.
- **Controlled impedance**: a PCB trace designed and fabricated so its $Z_0$ is a specified value within a tolerance (commonly ±10%).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it's about reflections at high frequency" and gives $Z_0=50\ \Omega$.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts from the assumption being broken (instant propagation) and frames lumped analysis as the limiting case.
- Ties the threshold to **rise time**, not clock frequency, and puts numbers on it ("1 ns edge, about 28 mm per sixth-of-rise-time rule in FR-4").
- Writes $v_p=1/\sqrt{L'C'}$, $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and $\Gamma$ without being asked, and explains that $Z_0$ is not something an ohmmeter can measure.
- Names the consequences (ringing, overshoot beyond the supply, non-monotonic edges, eye closure, EMI) and the cures (controlled impedance, termination, shorter stubs, return-path continuity).
- Mentions that the theory also applies to coax, cables, connectors and bond wires, and that loss and dispersion are the next layer ([[q0722]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Transmission lines only matter at high clock frequency."** What matters is edge rate. A 1 MHz clock with 1 ns edges on a 30 cm cable reflects badly. A 100 MHz sine wave on a 5 cm trace does not.
- **"$Z_0$ is the resistance of the trace."** It is not the DC resistance and an ohmmeter cannot read it. It comes from the geometry through $\sqrt{L'/C'}$.
- **"Terminate everything at 50 ohms."** Only when the line is electrically long; terminating a short trace wastes power and slows edges.
- **Forgetting the return path.** A signal trace has no impedance by itself. It is defined with respect to its reference plane, so a split plane or missing via ground changes $Z_0$ locally.
- **Believing the theory replaces circuit theory.** It reduces to lumped analysis for short lines, and loads and drivers are still analysed with ordinary circuit models at the ends.
- **Assuming the delay depends on the load.** Delay is set by $L'C'$ and length; the load only sets the reflections.
:::

::: deep
## The assumption that breaks

KVL and KCL ([[q0004]]) quietly assume that when a node changes voltage, *every* point connected to it by a wire changes at the same instant. Maxwell's equations say otherwise: the change propagates as an electromagnetic wave at $v_p=c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$. In FR-4 microstrip, $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx 3.1$, so $v_p\approx 0.57c \approx 1.7\times10^8$ m/s and each millimetre costs about 5.9 ps.

A 170 mm trace therefore has about 1 ns of delay. If a driver launches a 0.2 ns edge into it, the far end doesn't see anything for a full nanosecond, and when the wave arrives, the receiver's input (high impedance) reflects it back. The near end then sees a second step 2 ns after the first. A lumped model cannot produce either event.

## The distributed model

Because a trace is a pair of conductors, it has inductance (current loop area) and capacitance to its return plane everywhere along its length. Chop it into slices, each with $L'\Delta z$ in series and $C'\Delta z$ to the return:

The key insight is that a step applied at the input can't charge all the capacitors at once. The first slice charges through the first inductor, which then lets current flow to the second, and so on. The disturbance propagates down the ladder at a finite speed, $1/\sqrt{L'C'}$.

## From ladder to wave equation

KVL on a slice: $v(z) - v(z+\Delta z) = L'\Delta z\,\partial i/\partial t$. KCL at the node: $i(z) - i(z+\Delta z) = C'\Delta z\,\partial v/\partial t$. Dividing by $\Delta z$ and taking the limit gives the lossless telegrapher's equations:

$$\frac{\partial v}{\partial z}=-L'\frac{\partial i}{\partial t},\qquad \frac{\partial i}{\partial z}=-C'\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}$$

Differentiating one with respect to $z$ and substituting the other yields the wave equation $\partial_z^2 v = L'C'\,\partial_t^2 v$. Any waveform $f(t-z/v_p)$ satisfies it, which is a pulse that moves unchanged to the right at speed $v_p=1/\sqrt{L'C'}$, and $g(t+z/v_p)$ moves left. Plugging a forward wave back into the first equation gives $v = Z_0 i$ with $Z_0 = \sqrt{L'/C'}$ (derived physically in [[q0710]]).

Worked numbers, from a typical 50 Ω FR-4 microstrip: $v_p\approx1.73\times10^8$ m/s, so $C'=1/(Z_0v_p)\approx 115$ pF/m and $L'=Z_0/v_p\approx 289$ nH/m. Check: $\sqrt{289\text{ nH}/115.5\text{ pF}}=50\ \Omega$ and $1/\sqrt{289\text{ nH}\cdot115.5\text{ pF}}=1.73\times10^8$ m/s.

## Same trace in the frequency domain

Look into the near end of the 150 mm trace with the far end open. A lumped model says the trace is a single capacitor, $C=\ell C'=0.15\cdot115.5\text{ pF/m}=17.3$ pF, whose impedance falls forever as $1/\omega C$. The transmission line says $Z_{in}=-jZ_0\cot(\beta\ell)$: it looks like that capacitor at low frequency, but at $f=v_p/(4\ell)=1.73\times10^8/(4\cdot0.15)=288$ MHz the input impedance falls to nearly zero (a quarter-wave resonance), then turns inductive and repeats. That 288 MHz is $1/(4t_d)$ for this 0.87 ns line, the same relationship that set the 4 ns ring period of the 1 ns (170 mm) line in the time-domain plot above. The lumped capacitor is right only far below it: at 10 MHz the two agree within 0.1%, at 100 MHz the capacitor is already off by about 10%, and at 288 MHz it is completely wrong.

## What the theory gives you

| Result | Formula | Why you care |
|---|---|---|
| Delay | $t_d = \ell\sqrt{L'C'}$ | Timing budgets, length matching |
| Impedance | $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ | Trace geometry, termination value |
| Reflection | $\Gamma=\dfrac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0}$ | Predict overshoot and ringing |
| Input impedance | $Z_{in}(\ell)$ | Stubs, quarter-wave effects, TDR |
| Loss | $R'$, $G'$ terms | Long or very fast channels ([[q0722]]) |

## Real hardware

In practice, TL theory shows up as a set of habits: pick a stack-up and trace width for 50 Ω single-ended or 100 Ω differential; keep the return plane unbroken under the trace; keep stubs short; terminate where needed; and watch every transition (via, connector, package) as a possible discontinuity. A TDR (time-domain reflectometer) is literally the theory in an instrument: launch a fast step, record the reflections, and read impedance versus distance.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The "one-way delay greater than $t_r/6$" threshold is a rule of thumb. Different authors use $t_r/4$ to $t_r/10$, depending on how much ringing is acceptable, and "rise time" may mean 10-90% or 20-80%. Treat it as an order-of-magnitude trigger, then simulate.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked "when does it matter?", answer with a number: "My edge is 1 ns, FR-4 delay is about 6 ps/mm, so anything longer than roughly 30 mm deserves TL thinking." That one sentence shows you can apply it, not just define it.
:::

::: applications
- **DDR memory buses**: tens of traces length-matched and impedance-controlled, with on-die termination at the receiver.
- **USB, PCIe, HDMI and Ethernet**: differential pairs designed at 90 to 100 Ω, with connectors and vias optimised to limit discontinuities.
- **Coaxial cables** in lab and RF work: 50 Ω cables between instruments, 75 Ω cable for video.
- **Fast clock distribution**: a 100 MHz clock with 0.5 ns edges routed over 10 cm needs series termination to avoid double-clocking.
- **TDR measurements** of PCB impedance and cable faults: the reflection arrival time and polarity locate an open or short.
- **Antenna feeds and RF front ends**, where matching networks use exactly the same $\Gamma$ and $Z_{in}$ equations.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Uniform line**: $L'$ and $C'$ constant along the length. Real lines have discontinuities that you treat as separate elements.
- **Two-conductor (TEM or quasi-TEM) propagation**: field energy travels between signal and return. This breaks at very high frequency where higher-order modes appear ([[q0709]]).
- **Lossless for the basic result**: $R'$ and $G'$ matter for long or multi-GHz channels, adding attenuation and dispersion.
- **Linear, time-invariant** drivers and loads. Real drivers have nonlinear output impedance, which IBIS models capture.
- **Threshold choice**: the "one sixth of rise time" rule is a heuristic; some references use $t_r/4$ or $t_r/10$ depending on how much ringing you will tolerate. Always check against your noise margin.

The answer changes if the interviewer means frequency-domain RF design: then the same theory is expressed with $\beta\ell$, Smith charts and S-parameters.
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between lumped and distributed circuits?
In a lumped circuit, elements are points: all the inductance of a coil is in one place and wires are ideal nodes with no delay. In a distributed circuit, L and C are spread along the length, so voltage and current vary with position and time. The dividing line is size relative to the signal's shortest wavelength (or edge length). Below about $\lambda/10$, lumped is accurate.

### How long can a trace be before I need to worry, for a 1 ns rise time on FR-4?
Using one-way delay $<t_r/6$: $t_r/6=167$ ps, and at about 5.9 ps/mm that is about 28 mm. So under roughly 3 cm I can ignore it; over 5 cm I should check, and over 15 cm I should expect ringing unless terminated. If my rise time drops to 200 ps, the limit shrinks to about 6 mm.

### Why 50 ohms?
For air-dielectric coax at a fixed outer diameter, power handling peaks near 30 Ω and conductor loss is lowest near 77 Ω; 50 Ω is a compromise between the two, and it also gives practical trace widths on common PCB stack-ups. Fifty ohms became the RF standard and carried over into digital, where 50 Ω microstrip is a convenient width on a typical stack-up. See [[q0720]].

### A scope shows 6 V overshoot on a 3.3 V logic line. What do you suspect?
A low source impedance driving an unterminated, electrically long line: the first incident wave is about 0.83 of the swing (if $R_s=10\ \Omega$, $Z_0=50\ \Omega$: $2.75$ V of 3.3 V), the open end doubles it to 5.5 V. I'd confirm by checking ring period, which is $4t_d$ for a low-impedance source and open load. Fix with a series resistor near the driver. I'd also check the probe ground lead before trusting the number.

### Harder: does a lumped capacitor on the end of a transmission line change the delay?
A load capacitor doesn't change the line's own delay, but it changes the reflected waveform: the capacitor looks like a short at the edge's start ($\Gamma\to-1$) and an open at DC ($\Gamma\to+1$), so the reflection has an exponential tail with $\tau=Z_0C_L$. The receiver sees a slowed, rounded edge with delay added roughly proportional to $Z_0C_L$ (for 5 pF at 50 Ω, 250 ps time constant). So the effective timing shifts even though the line is unchanged.
:::
