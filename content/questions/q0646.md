::: basics
### In plain words
A process node such as "3 nm" names a generation of chip manufacturing technology. Today the number is a marketing label. It is not the size of any part of a transistor. A smaller number means a newer generation, which packs more transistors into the same area and generally uses power more efficiently.

### Picture it
Think of a "half-ton" pickup truck. The name once meant it carried about half a ton. Today it is just a size class. A chip node name works the same way: it once matched a real length, and now it only says "this generation". Unlike the truck name, a newer node does give a real gain, just not the one the number suggests.

### Words you need
- **Transistor**: a tiny electronic switch. A modern chip has billions.
- **Nanometer (nm)**: one billionth of a meter.
- **Process node**: a named generation of chip manufacturing, such as "5 nm" or "3 nm".
- **Transistor density**: how many transistors fit in one square millimeter.
- **Pitch**: the center-to-center spacing of repeated features, such as neighboring wires.
- **Die**: one chip's worth of silicon cut from a wafer. See [[q0645]].

### The simplest example
If the names were literal lengths, shrinking from 5 nm to 3 nm would shrink each feature to 3/5 of its size. Area would shrink to (3/5)², so density would rise by (5/3)² ≈ 2.8×. Real gains claimed by chip factories (foundries) per generation are closer to 1.6 to 1.8×. Real dimensions are also larger than the name: roughly 45 to 50 nm between neighboring transistors' gates (the part that switches it on and off), and about 20 to 30 nm between the tightest wires. These are estimates. Some thin layers inside a transistor really are only a few nanometers thick, but the spacings that set density are ten times larger.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds how the name drifted away from real sizes, the shift from flat to 3D transistors, and how light prints such small patterns.
:::

The interviewer wants the history (node once meant gate length or half-pitch, then diverged), the actual metrics now used (contacted gate pitch, metal pitch, fin or nanosheet dimensions, standard-cell height, transistor density in MTr/mm²), the device evolution (planar → FinFET → gate-all-around), and why finer features are even printable (EUV, multi-patterning). ::: why
Node names show up in every news article, product launch and job description. If you take "3 nm" literally you will mis-reason about density, performance and cost: you might expect a 5.4× density gain from 7 nm to 3 nm when real gains are about 1.6 to 1.8× per full node. Understanding what the number does and doesn't mean helps you read foundry roadmaps critically, estimate the die area or power of a design on a given node, and talk sensibly about why scaling is getting harder and more expensive. It also links the physics (short-channel effects, leakage) to the manufacturing (lithography) and to packaging (chiplets).
:::

::: technical
**Origin of the label.** Through the 1990s, a node was the **half-pitch of the densest metal or DRAM** (ITRS convention) or approximately the **gate length**. Scaling by 0.7× per generation halved area: $0.7^2 = 0.49$. After roughly the 90 to 45 nm era, gate length stopped tracking the node (it was bounded by leakage and electrostatics) and metal pitch scaled more slowly than the name. After the planar-to-FinFET transition (about 22/16/14 nm), names were chosen by each foundry to follow the 0.7× cadence in marketing terms.

**What is measured now.** Typical metrics for a leading-edge logic process:

| Metric | Meaning | Rough value at "3 nm" class (estimates) |
|---|---|---|
| Contacted gate pitch (CPP) | spacing between adjacent gates including contact | about 45 to 50 nm |
| Minimum metal pitch (MMP) | tightest wiring pitch (lowest metal layers) | about 20 to 30 nm |
| Fin pitch / nanosheet stack | spacing of fins or stacked sheets | tens of nm lateral; sheet thickness a few nm |
| Standard cell height | track count × metal pitch | in the 100-200 nm range for high-density cells |
| Logic transistor density | transistors per mm² | on the order of 100 to 300 million/mm² |
| Gate length | channel length | roughly 12 to 20 nm for gate (varies, often unpublished) |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Foundries publish few exact dimensions, and node-to-node comparisons come from teardown and conference analyses. The values above are order-of-magnitude estimates for "3 nm"-class processes; they differ between foundries and between variants (N3, N3E, SF3, etc.). For example, one commonly reported figure is about 19 billion transistors in a die of roughly 104 mm² (≈180 million/mm²) for a 2023 phone SoC on a 3 nm-class process, from press and teardown reports.

**Scale comparisons.** Silicon's lattice constant is 0.543 nm: 3 nm ≈ 5.5 unit cells, or about 13 nearest-neighbour atomic spacings (0.235 nm). So a 3 nm feature would be around a dozen atoms wide, far too small for a transistor channel with reliable doping and control. That is why the number can't be a literal dimension.

**Density gains.** If names were literal, density would scale as $(n_1/n_2)^2$: 7 → 5 nm gives 1.96×, 5 → 3 nm gives 2.78×, 7 → 3 nm gives 5.4×. Reported foundry logic-density improvements per full node recently have been about 1.5× to 1.8×, with SRAM and analog scaling much less. So the name "over-promises" by a large factor.

**Transistor architecture.**
- *Planar MOSFET*: channel under a flat gate; short-channel effects and leakage limit scaling below ~28 nm.
- *FinFET*: channel is a tall thin fin wrapped on three sides by the gate; better electrostatic control (introduced commercially around 22/16/14 nm).
- *Gate-all-around (GAA) nanosheet / nanowire*: horizontal sheets fully surrounded by the gate; introduced in leading 3 and 2 nm-class processes. Fin or sheet thickness of a few nm sets the channel control.

- **Dennard scaling**: the (now ended) observation that scaled-down transistors keep constant power density.

::: mistakes
- **"3 nm is the gate length."** It isn't; gate lengths at that node are several times larger.
- **"Smaller node always means faster and lower power."** Gains per node have shrunk; SRAM and analog scale poorly; wiring resistance and capacitance increasingly dominate.
- **Comparing nodes across foundries by the number.** Each foundry defines its own names (and Intel renamed its nodes in 2021), so compare measured density instead.
- **Assuming density doubles each node.** Recently it's about 1.5 to 1.8× for logic, less for SRAM.
- **Believing light can't print features smaller than its wavelength.** It can, with resolution enhancement (immersion, multipatterning, phase-shift masks) and with EUV's 13.5 nm.
- **Treating "angstrom" nodes (2 nm, 18A) as different in kind.** They're just continued naming.
:::

::: deep
## Where the numbers came from

The classic definition: Gordon Moore's observation and the ITRS roadmap set a cadence of a new node every two to three years with linear dimensions scaled by about 0.7. That makes area scale by $0.7^2 \approx 0.5$, which halves cost per transistor if wafer cost stays flat. The "node" was the half-pitch of the densest pattern (DRAM or metal-1), and the transistor gate length was typically slightly smaller than the node through the 1990s.

Dennard scaling said that if you scale all dimensions and the voltage by $1/\kappa$, the power density stays constant, so you could scale frequency "for free." That ended around the mid-2000s when leakage and voltage floors stopped $V_{DD}$ scaling; since then scaling has been about density and about transistor architecture changes that keep leakage in check.

## What the name is now

Foundries tie the name to a generation with an expected density or performance step. Names have jumped (Intel's re-naming to "Intel 7", "Intel 4", "Intel 3", "20A", "18A") partly to line up with competitors. The bottom line is that the number is a label, so compare actual metrics: CPP × MMP (a proxy for logic density), standard-cell height, SRAM bitcell area, and measured transistor density.

## What a transistor looks like at the "3 nm" node

Imagine the layout of a standard cell: horizontal rows of gates at the contacted gate pitch (about 45 to 50 nm), crossing a set of fins or nanosheet stacks, with metal wiring above at 20 to 30 nm pitch. In a **FinFET**, the channel is a fin a few nanometres wide (on the order of 5 to 7 nm) and tens of nanometres tall; the gate wraps three sides. In a **GAA nanosheet**, several horizontal sheets (a few nm thick, tens of nm wide) are stacked and the gate surrounds each fully.

Why do these shapes exist? For a MOSFET with channel length $L$, the gate must keep control of the channel potential. The scale length $\lambda$ over which the drain field penetrates is set by the geometry (body thickness, oxide thickness); for good control you need $L \gtrsim 5$ to $10\,\lambda$. Thin bodies and wrap-around gates reduce $\lambda$, so the channel can be shorter without leakage or drain-induced barrier lowering. That's the physics reason FinFETs and GAAs exist, and the reason a planar transistor at these lengths would leak too much.

**Physical picture of the scale.** Silicon's lattice constant is 0.543 nm. A 5 nm fin is about 9 unit cells wide; a 3 nm sheet about 5.5. At that scale a few atoms of dopant or a single atomic-layer roughness changes behaviour: variability is a major design issue. That is also why channels at leading nodes are often undoped (to avoid random dopant fluctuation) and the threshold voltage is set by gate metals and workfunction.

**Density figures.** A reported 19 billion transistors in about 104 mm² gives $19\times10^9/104 = 183$ million transistors/mm². Compare with a 28 nm-class process at roughly 10 to 15 million per mm² for logic: an order of magnitude more. A literal 3 nm pitch would imply far more than that; a CPP near 48 nm and a cell height of a couple of hundred nanometres give densities of order $10^8$ per mm², consistent with the reports.

## How can features be smaller than the light wavelength?

Printing resolution follows the Rayleigh criterion for half-pitch:

$$HP = k_1\,\frac{\lambda}{NA}.$$

- 193 nm ArF immersion: $\lambda = 193$ nm, $NA \approx 1.35$, $k_1 \ge 0.25$ for a single exposure: $HP \approx 0.25\times193/1.35 = 35.7$ nm, i.e. a pitch of about 70 to 80 nm. To print tighter lines, use **multipatterning** (litho-etch-litho-etch, self-aligned spacers), which adds steps and cost.
- EUV: $\lambda = 13.5$ nm, $NA = 0.33$: at $k_1 = 0.3$, $HP \approx 12.3$ nm in principle; practical single-exposure metal pitches are around 28 to 30 nm today with stochastic effects limiting.
- High-NA EUV: $NA = 0.55$: $HP = 0.3\times13.5/0.55 = 7.4$ nm in principle.

So "3 nm" features come from a 13.5 nm wavelength and clever optics and process, not from light the size of the feature. [[q0664]] covers how features are patterned in more detail, and [[q0661]] covers photolithography.

## Why scaling is slowing, and what that means

- **Cost per transistor** no longer falls steadily; wafer costs rise with each node (EUV tools, more masks).
- **SRAM scaling** has slowed: bitcell area doesn't shrink as fast as logic.
- **Wiring**: resistance of thin copper lines rises, so interconnect delay and power increase; new metals (ruthenium, cobalt) are being introduced for some layers.
- **Power density and thermals** limit how many transistors can switch at once ([[q0110]]).
- **Design response**: chiplets and advanced packaging ([[q0651]], [[q0652]]), specialisation (accelerators), backside power delivery, 3D stacking ([[q0653]]).

## Practical use of the numbers

When someone quotes a node, ask for transistor density, SRAM bitcell size and power/performance claims at fixed conditions. When estimating die area for a design on a new node, scale the old area by the claimed logic density gain (about 1.6 to 1.8× for a full-node step), but scale SRAM and analog blocks by smaller factors, and add area for I/O, which doesn't scale much. A chip with 50% SRAM and 25% analog sees far less than a logic-only gain.

Example: a die of 100 mm² on node A: 50 mm² logic, 40 mm² SRAM, 10 mm² analog/IO. Moving to a node with 1.7× logic, 1.2× SRAM and 1.0× analog/IO density: new area $50/1.7 + 40/1.2 + 10/1.0 = 29.4 + 33.3 + 10 = 72.7$ mm², i.e. a 27% reduction, not the 41% the logic figure alone would suggest.

::: assumptions
The dimensions quoted are order-of-magnitude estimates from public analyses and vary by foundry and variant; foundries do not publish all numbers. Density gains per node are approximate and depend on the logic/SRAM mix, standard-cell libraries and design rules. The statement that node names became marketing labels is widely accepted, but the exact year of divergence is debated; I've said "roughly the late 1990s and 2000s". The Rayleigh numbers are theoretical minima, and practical resolution depends on resist, stochastics and mask effects. Different readers use "node" for the process family (e.g. N3E), a specific variant, or the entire generation. Roadmap information changes quickly; check current announcements.

::: followups
### If it's not the gate length, what is a "3 nm" gate length?
Roughly in the low tens of nanometres (around 12 to 20 nm is often quoted for the physical gate), and it varies by foundry and variant; foundries rarely publish it. The point is that the gate length stopped tracking the node name because of leakage and electrostatics. The contacted gate pitch (about 45 to 50 nm) is a more meaningful dimension for density.

### How can a 193 nm laser print 30 nm features?
By using immersion (water between lens and wafer raises NA to about 1.35), resolution enhancement (phase-shift masks, off-axis illumination, OPC) and multipatterning, where one layer is split into multiple exposures and etches, or self-aligned spacer techniques that effectively halve the pitch. EUV at 13.5 nm reduces the need for multipatterning. The Rayleigh half-pitch $k_1\lambda/NA$ with $k_1 = 0.25$ gives about 36 nm for a single immersion exposure, so smaller pitches need these tricks.

### What changes between FinFET and GAA?
In a FinFET the gate wraps three sides of a vertical fin, and the drive current is set in discrete steps by the number of fins. In GAA nanosheets, the gate surrounds each horizontal sheet on all four sides, so electrostatic control is better and you can tune the sheet width continuously for more or less current. GAA allows shorter channels with less leakage at the same size. It's more complex to manufacture, since stacks of sheets must be etched and the gate formed around them.

### Is smaller always better for a product?
No. Smaller nodes cost much more per wafer, have higher leakage and variability, and need more design effort. Analog, RF, power and many MCU and sensor products use mature nodes where cost, voltage tolerance and device characteristics fit. A leading-edge node pays off when density, performance or power efficiency are the product's main values (phone SoCs, GPUs, servers).

### How would you estimate the area savings of moving a design to a newer node?
Split the design into logic, SRAM, analog/IO and scale each by the node's density gain for that block type. For example 50 mm² logic at 1.7×, 40 mm² SRAM at 1.2× and 10 mm² analog at 1.0× gives 29.4 + 33.3 + 10 = 72.7 mm² from 100 mm², a 27% saving rather than the 41% from logic alone. Then check power, cost per wafer and yield.

### Scenario: marketing claims "our 2 nm chip has transistors twice as small as your 3 nm chip." How do you respond?
I'd point out that the labels aren't physical dimensions. The honest comparison uses measured data: transistor density per mm², SRAM bitcell area, CPP and MMP, and power and performance at fixed conditions. A move from "3 nm" to "2 nm" is a generation step with a claimed density gain of perhaps 1.1 to 1.3× (check the foundry's own claims) and some power improvement, not a halving in linear size, which would be 4× density. I'd ask for their density numbers and benchmark results.
