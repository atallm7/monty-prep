::: testing
Whether you can draw a bridge rectifier correctly (four diodes in a diamond, AC across one diagonal, the load across the other), trace the current path for **both** half-cycles, and then say what it actually outputs: pulsating DC at twice the line frequency, one level lower by **two** diode drops, which needs a filter capacitor. Strong candidates volunteer the practical numbers (ripple, peak diode current, diode voltage rating).
:::

::: why
Almost every mains-powered product starts with a rectifier, and low-voltage designs use the same bridge to make a polarity-independent DC input (so a user can plug a barrel jack in either way round). The bridge also teaches diode behaviour in a real circuit: forward drop, reverse voltage, surge current from a capacitor-input filter, and why efficient designs replace diodes with MOSFETs. It is also the AC-DC box from [[q0159]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Ideal vs real diode**: conducts one way with about 0.7 V drop for silicon (about 0.3 V for Schottky). See [[q0072]] and [[q0078]].
- **Full-wave rectification**: both half-cycles are used, so the output frequency is $2f_{line}$.
- **Peak vs RMS**: $V_p=\sqrt2V_{rms}$; the filter capacitor charges to near $V_p$ minus the diode drops.
- **Ripple**: capacitor discharge between peaks, $\Delta V\approx \dfrac{I}{2fC}$ for full-wave.
- **Peak inverse voltage (PIV)**: the reverse voltage a diode must block, about $V_p$ for a bridge.
- **Surge/peak current**: with a capacitor-input filter the diodes conduct in short, large pulses.
:::

::: answer
"A bridge rectifier is four diodes arranged in a diamond that turns AC into DC of one polarity. The AC source connects across two opposite corners, and the load connects across the other two.

On the positive half-cycle, the top AC terminal is positive, so current goes through one diode to the positive output rail, through the load, and returns through the diode on the opposite side to the other AC terminal. On the negative half-cycle the roles swap: the other pair of diodes conducts, and current still flows through the load in the *same* direction. So the load sees the absolute value of the sine, a pulsating DC at twice the line frequency.

Two practical things: at any moment two diodes are in series with the source, so the output is the input peak minus about 1.4 volts for silicon diodes. And the output isn't smooth, so you add a reservoir capacitor, which charges at the peaks and supplies the load in between, leaving ripple of about I over 2 f C. I'd choose diodes rated for the peak reverse voltage and for the repetitive current pulses, and for low-voltage supplies I might use Schottkys or an ideal-diode controller to save the drop."
:::

::: technical
**Topology.** Anode of $D_1$ and cathode of $D_3$ join at AC terminal A; anode of $D_2$ and cathode of $D_4$ join at AC terminal B. $D_1$ and $D_2$ cathodes join the positive output; $D_3$ and $D_4$ anodes join the negative (ground) output.

**Operation.**
- $v_A>v_B$: $D_1$ and $D_4$ conduct; $D_2$ and $D_3$ are reverse-biased.
- $v_B>v_A$: $D_2$ and $D_3$ conduct; $D_1$ and $D_4$ are reverse-biased.

**Output (resistive load, no filter).** $v_{out}=|v_{ac}|-2V_f$ when $|v_{ac}|>2V_f$, else 0. Average (ideal) $V_{avg}=2V_p/\pi\approx0.637V_p$; ripple frequency $=2f_{line}$ (120 Hz for 60 Hz).

**With a reservoir capacitor.** Output peak $\approx V_p-2V_f$ (minus winding resistance drops). Ripple, assuming a constant load current $I$ discharging the capacitor for nearly a half-period:
$$\Delta V_{pp}\approx\frac{I}{2f_{line}C}.$$
This is an upper estimate because the diodes conduct for part of the cycle.

**Ratings.**
- PIV: each diode blocks about $V_p$ (versus $2V_p$ for a center-tapped full-wave rectifier).
- Average diode current: $I_{load}/2$. Repetitive peak current is several times $I_{load}$ and the first-cycle inrush can be far higher, so check the surge rating and consider a series resistor or NTC thermistor.
- Dissipation: about $V_f\,I_{load}$ total (two diodes in series each half cycle).

**Worked numbers.** A 12 V RMS transformer gives $V_p=16.97$ V; the bridge output peaks near $16.97-1.4=15.6$ V. At 0.5 A, 60 Hz and $C=2200\ \mu$F, $\Delta V\approx 0.5/(120\times2200\times10^{-6})=1.89$ V (upper bound).
:::

::: short

Two diodes are in the path, so you lose about 1.4 volts, and you add a capacitor to smooth it, with ripple of about I over 2fC."
:::

::: long
"A bridge rectifier is four diodes in a diamond. The AC source goes across one diagonal, the top and bottom corners, and the load goes across the other diagonal. Two of the diodes have their cathodes joined at the positive output, and the other two have their anodes joined at the negative output or ground.

When the top AC terminal is positive, current goes from it up through one diode to the positive rail, through the load, back through the ground-side diode on the other leg, and into the lower AC terminal. The other two diodes are reverse-biased. When the AC reverses, the lower terminal is the positive one, so the other pair conducts, and current again enters the load at the positive rail and leaves at ground. So the load current never reverses. The output is the absolute value of the sine wave, with pulses at twice the line frequency.

Real-world details: because two diodes are always in series, the output peak is the input peak minus about 1.4 volts for silicon diodes. For a 12 volt RMS transformer, the peak is about 17 volts, and the output peaks near 15.6.

On its own that's lumpy DC, so you put a reservoir capacitor across the output. The diodes only conduct when the input exceeds the capacitor voltage plus two drops, so they pass short, high-current pulses at the peaks, and the capacitor feeds the load in between. Ripple is about the load current over two times line frequency times C, so half an amp at 60 hertz with 2200 microfarads is up to roughly 1.9 volts.

For ratings, each diode must block about the peak voltage, and handle the repetitive peak current, which is several times the average, plus the first-cycle inrush. And if the drop matters, like at 5 volts and an amp, I'd use Schottky diodes or a MOSFET-based ideal diode bridge."
:::

::: terms
- **Bridge rectifier**: four-diode full-wave rectifier that accepts either AC polarity.
- **Full-wave rectification**: using both half-cycles of the AC input.
- **Forward drop ($V_f$)**: voltage across a conducting diode, about 0.7 V for Si, 0.2–0.4 V for Schottky.
- **Reservoir (filter) capacitor**: smooths the rectified output.
- **Ripple voltage**: peak-to-peak variation on the filtered output, at $2f_{line}$.
- **PIV / $V_{RRM}$**: maximum repetitive reverse voltage rating of a diode.
- **Surge current ($I_{FSM}$)**: single-cycle peak forward current rating.
- **Center-tap rectifier**: two-diode full-wave alternative with a center-tapped transformer.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws a diamond of diodes and says "it converts AC to DC."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws it so both diagonals are clear, and traces current for both half-cycles with the direction through the load unchanged.
- States that the output is the absolute value at twice the line frequency, and that **two diodes** conduct each half-cycle, so the drop is $2V_f$.
- Adds the filter capacitor and gives ripple $\approx I/(2fC)$ with a worked number.
- Mentions the **pulsed** diode current, the PIV rating (about $V_p$), and the surge rating.
- Knows alternatives: center-tap (one drop, $2V_p$ PIV), Schottky or active (MOSFET) bridges for low voltage, and the lack of isolation without a transformer.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing the AC source across the wrong diagonal.** The source must connect to the two mid-points of the diamond, not to the output rails.
- **Saying only two diodes ever matter.** All four are used, in pairs, one pair per half-cycle.
- **Forgetting both diode drops.** The output is $V_p-2V_f$, which matters at low voltage (1.4 V is 28% of a 5 V rail).
- **Calling the output DC without a filter.** Unfiltered it's pulsating, and it reaches zero twice per line cycle.
- **Using the average current for diode selection.** Capacitor-input filters give peaks of several times the average; check $I_{FSM}$ and repetitive peak ratings.
- **Quoting ripple at 60 Hz.** Full-wave ripple is at $2f$ (120 Hz); half-wave is at $f$.
:::

::: deep
## Building it from first principles

A single diode passes current only when its anode is above its cathode. With an AC source and a load, one diode gives half-wave rectification: you throw away half the cycle. The idea of the bridge is to *steer* the current so that, whichever terminal is positive, the load always sees the same polarity.

Label the two AC terminals A and B and the output terminals $+$ and $-$ (ground). We need a diode path from A to $+$ and from B to $+$ (so whichever is high feeds the positive rail), and a diode path from $-$ to A and from $-$ to B (so whichever is low can return current from ground). That is four diodes: $D_1$ (A to $+$), $D_2$ (B to $+$), $D_3$ ($-$ to A), $D_4$ ($-$ to B).

## What the output looks like

If we ignore the diode drops, the output is $|v_{ac}|$, which has average $2V_p/\pi$ and repeats at $2f$. Including the two forward drops, conduction starts only when $|v_{ac}|>2V_f\approx1.4$ V, leaving a short dead zone around each zero crossing.

## Adding the reservoir capacitor

With $C$ across the load, the capacitor charges toward the peak whenever the rectified input exceeds the capacitor voltage plus two drops, then discharges into the load until the next peak. The diodes therefore conduct only for a short conduction angle near each peak. In the discharge interval (approximately a half period $T/2=1/(2f)$), constant load current $I$ removes charge $Q=I/(2f)$:

$$\Delta V_{pp}\approx\frac{I}{2fC}.$$

Example: $V_{rms}=12$ V, $V_p=16.97$ V, $I=0.5$ A, $f=60$ Hz, $C=2200\ \mu$F.

$$\Delta V_{pp}\approx\frac{0.5}{120\times2200\times10^{-6}}=1.89\ \text{V (upper bound)},\qquad V_{max}\approx16.97-1.4=15.6\ \text{V}.$$

A quick numerical simulation of this circuit with 0.5 Ω of transformer winding resistance, a 31 Ω load and the diode drops gave a peak around 14.7 V, ripple about 1.3 V p-p (less than the 1.9 V bound, since the capacitor is recharged for part of the cycle) and diode current peaks near 2.6 A against a 0.5 A load. The pulses are about five times the average, which is why average-current ratings alone are not enough.

The capacitor for a ripple target: $C=I/(2f\,\Delta V)$. For 1 V ripple at 0.5 A, $C=0.5/(120\times1)=4170\ \mu$F. Half-wave rectification would need twice the capacitance for the same ripple, because the recharge interval is $T$ not $T/2$.

## Diode ratings and selection

- **PIV.** When $D_1$ and $D_4$ conduct, $D_2$ has its anode at B (near the ground rail potential through $D_4$) and cathode at the positive rail (near $+V_p$ after charging): about $V_p$ reverse bias, plus any transformer overshoot. Choose a $V_{RRM}$ with a healthy margin (for example, 1N4007-type parts rate 1000 V and are common for 120/230 V mains bridges; for 17 V peak, any 50 V diode would do).
- **Current.** Average per diode is $I_{load}/2$; peak repetitive currents several times $I_{load}$; startup surge $\approx (V_p-2V_f)/R_{source}$ into an empty capacitor, limited by transformer and wiring resistance. Specify the surge rating $I_{FSM}$ or add a thermistor.
- **Dissipation.** About $2V_fI_{load}$ in total, since two diodes are in the path at any instant: $1.4\times0.5=0.7$ W across the pair at 0.5 A.
- **Efficiency.** Dropping 1.4 V from a 5 V output at 1 A wastes about 1.4 W; so low-voltage rectification uses Schottky diodes (about 0.3–0.4 V drop at rated current) or MOSFET-based ideal-diode controllers.

## Bridge vs center-tap vs half-wave

| | Half-wave | Center-tap full-wave | Bridge |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diodes | 1 | 2 | 4 |
| Transformer | plain | center-tapped secondary | plain secondary |
| Diode drops in path | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| Diode PIV | $V_p$ (with capacitor, $2V_p$) | $2V_p$ | $V_p$ |
| Ripple frequency | $f$ | $2f$ | $2f$ |
| Transformer utilisation | poor | good | best |

A center-tap rectifier needs a center-tapped secondary (more copper, usually half the voltage per winding) but only one diode drop, so it was preferred at low voltage. Bridges dominate because they need only a plain winding, can run straight from mains without a transformer, and accept any polarity of AC input.

## Rectification without a transformer

A bridge connected straight to mains gives a DC bus of about $\sqrt2\times120=170$ V (or 325 V from 230 V) that is **not isolated** from the line. Isolation must come later (an isolated DC-DC converter). Charging the bulk capacitor produces a large inrush current, which is why mains supplies use NTC thermistors or soft-start circuits, and why larger supplies need PFC ([[q0159]]).

> [!WARNING]
> Mains-connected bridges and bulk capacitors are lethal and can hold charge after unplugging. Do not probe them with an earth-referenced scope without an isolation transformer.
:::

::: applications
- **Linear wall adapters** (transformer + bridge + capacitor, then an LDO) for small appliances.
- **Offline switching supplies:** a bridge on the 120/230 V input feeding a bulk capacitor and a flyback or PFC stage.
- **Polarity-protection bridge on a DC jack:** a Schottky bridge so the board works with either centre-pin polarity (costs 2 drops).
- **Alternator output rectification** in cars (three-phase bridge).
- **Power over Ethernet powered devices:** a bridge on each pair makes the PoE input polarity-insensitive.
- **Bridge-rectifier modules** (single-package, 1–35 A) for bench supplies and battery chargers.
:::

::: assumptions
- Silicon diodes with about 0.7 V forward drop at moderate current; Schottky diodes about 0.3–0.4 V; real drops rise with current.
- Sinusoidal source with negligible source impedance for the ideal formulas; the numerical simulation used 0.5 Ω of winding resistance.
- Ripple formula assumes constant load current, which overestimates ripple slightly for capacitor-input filters; for resistive loads the discharge is exponential.
- Full-wave ripple at $2f$; for a three-phase bridge, six pulses per cycle and much lower ripple.
- If the interviewer wants "how does it work" for a DC input (polarity protection), the same analysis applies with DC replacing AC: the diagonal diodes conduct and the output polarity is fixed.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the output of a bridge rectifier about 1.4 V lower than the input peak?
Two silicon diodes are in series with the source at every instant: one connecting the positive terminal to the positive rail and one connecting the other terminal to ground. Each drops about 0.7 V, so $V_{out,peak}=V_p-2(0.7)=V_p-1.4$ V. With Schottky diodes at 0.3 V each it is $V_p-0.6$ V.

### What peak reverse voltage must the diodes withstand?
About the peak input $V_p$ for a bridge, since the conducting pair clamps the other pair's anode and cathode across the AC peak. For 230 V RMS mains, $V_p=325$ V, so you use at least 600 V diodes (with margin for transients, 1000 V parts are common). A center-tap design would need $2V_p$.

### What happens if one diode in the bridge fails open? Shorted?
Open: the bridge degrades to a half-wave rectifier on one polarity, so ripple frequency halves to $f$ and ripple roughly doubles, and the diode count that carries current halves, raising stress. Shorted: on one half-cycle the source is shorted through the failed diode and its neighbour, so the transformer secondary or fuse sees a huge current. This is why a fuse or current limit in front of a bridge matters.

### How would you size the capacitor for 5% ripple on a 12 V rail at 1 A with 60 Hz mains?
$\Delta V=0.05\times12=0.6$ V, so $C\approx I/(2f\Delta V)=1/(120\times0.6)=13{,}900\ \mu$F. That is large, which is why low-ripple supplies add a regulator afterwards that tolerates ripple (the regulator needs headroom at the ripple minimum) rather than relying on a huge capacitor.

### Why might you replace the diodes with MOSFETs?
At 12 V and 10 A, two diodes at 0.8 V each dissipate 16 W. A MOSFET bridge with 10 mΩ devices dissipates $2\times I^2R=2\times100\times0.01=2$ W. This needs control circuitry, so it's used in efficiency-critical designs (PoE, telecom, high-current rectification).

### Scenario: your bench supply makes 12 V but buzzes at 120 Hz and the output sags under load. What do you check?
The 120 Hz hum is the ripple of the reservoir capacitor. Check the capacitor value and its health (ESR rises as electrolytics age), measure ripple with the scope AC-coupled, verify the diodes are all conducting (a failed-open diode gives 60 Hz ripple), and check that the minimum of the ripple stays above the regulator's dropout.
:::
