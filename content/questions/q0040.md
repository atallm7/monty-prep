::: testing
Whether you can state $Z_L = j\omega L = j2\pi fL$ **and know what each piece means**:

1. The magnitude grows linearly with frequency, so inductors pass low frequencies and block high ones.
2. The $j$ means a +90° phase shift: voltage leads current by a quarter cycle. It's purely reactive, so an ideal inductor dissipates no average power.
3. You can derive it from $v = L\,di/dt$ rather than reciting it, and you can plug in numbers with correct units (ω in rad/s, not Hz).
4. Bonus: you know the Laplace form $sL$ and the real-part additions (DCR) for a practical inductor.
:::

::: why
Every AC analysis with an inductor starts from this equation: LC filters, EMI chokes, RF matching, buck converter output stages, motor windings at PWM frequency. It lets you treat an inductor like a frequency-dependent resistor and use Ohm's law and voltage dividers in phasor form. Getting the $2\pi$ wrong (using $f$ where you need $\omega$) is a classic 6.28× error that shows up in real designs. Interviewers ask it early because it gates the rest: reactance sign ([[q0042]]), DC vs. high-frequency behaviour ([[q0045]]) and filter design ([[q0052]], [[q0053]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor law**: $v = L\,di/dt$ ([[q0041]]); the impedance is what this becomes for sinusoids.
- **Phasors**: represent $A\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ as the complex number $Ae^{j\phi}$; differentiation becomes multiplication by $j\omega$.
- **Angular frequency**: $\omega = 2\pi f$ in rad/s. Mixing up $\omega$ and $f$ is a 2π error.
- **Impedance**: $Z = V/I$ in phasor form, $Z = R + jX$; resistance is the real part, reactance the imaginary part.
- **Reactance**: $X_L = \omega L$, positive for inductors ([[q0042]]); compare $X_C = -1/(\omega C)$ for capacitors ([[q0024]]).
- **Laplace domain**: $Z_L(s) = sL$, used for transfer functions and transients.
:::

::: answer
"The impedance of an ideal inductor is $Z_L = j\omega L$, which is $j2\pi fL$.

That comes straight from the inductor equation, $v = L\,di/dt$. If the current is a sinusoid, $I e^{j\omega t}$, then taking the derivative just multiplies it by $j\omega$, so the voltage is $j\omega L$ times the current. The ratio is the impedance.

There are two things to read off it. The magnitude is $\omega L$, so it grows linearly with frequency: zero at DC, where the inductor is just a wire, and very large at high frequency, where it looks like an open. And the $j$ means it's purely reactive with a +90° phase: the voltage leads the current by a quarter cycle, and on average it stores and returns energy without dissipating any.

To give a number: a 10 µH inductor at 1 MHz is $2\pi\times10^6\times10\times10^{-6}$, about 63 ohms. At 100 kHz it's 6.3 ohms.

In the Laplace domain it's just $sL$. And for a real part I'd add the winding resistance in series, so $Z \approx R_{DC} + j\omega L$ at moderate frequencies, plus the winding capacitance that makes it self-resonate at high frequency."
:::

::: technical
**Derivation.** Let $i(t) = \text{Re}\{I e^{j\omega t}\}$ with phasor $I$. Then
$$v = L\frac{di}{dt} = \text{Re}\{j\omega L\, I e^{j\omega t}\} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V = j\omega L\, I$$
$$\boxed{Z_L = \frac{V}{I} = j\omega L = j2\pi f L = \omega L\,\angle{+90^\circ}}$$

**Reading it.**
- $|Z_L| = \omega L$: linear in frequency (+20 dB/decade on a log plot).
- $\angle Z_L = +90^\circ$: voltage leads current; the reactance $X_L = \omega L$ is positive.
- $\text{Re}\{Z_L\} = 0$: average power $P = \tfrac12 |I|^2\text{Re}\{Z\} = 0$. Energy sloshes in and out of the field every half-cycle.
- Limits: $f \to 0$, $Z \to 0$ (short); $f \to \infty$, $|Z| \to \infty$ (open) ([[q0045]]).

**Laplace form.** $Z_L(s) = sL$, and with an initial current $i(0^-)$ the s-domain model adds a source: $V(s) = sL\,I(s) - L\,i(0^-)$.

**Admittance.** $Y_L = 1/(j\omega L) = -j/(\omega L)$: susceptance is negative.

**Numbers.**

| Part | Frequency | $X_L = 2\pi fL$ |
|---|---|---|
| 100 nH | 2.4 GHz | 1.51 kΩ |
| 10 µH | 1 MHz | 62.8 Ω |
| 10 µH | 100 kHz | 6.28 Ω |
| 1 mH | 1 kHz | 6.28 Ω |
| 2.2 µH | 120 Hz | 1.66 mΩ |

**Real inductor.** A first-order practical model is $Z = R_{DC} + j\omega L$. Its quality factor is $Q = \omega L / R$ (e.g. 10 µH with 50 mΩ at 100 kHz: $Q \approx 126$). At higher frequencies, AC resistance rises (skin and proximity effects, core loss, [[q0051]]) and the inter-winding capacitance $C_p$ makes $Z = (R + j\omega L)\parallel\frac{1}{j\omega C_p}$, which peaks at the self-resonant frequency $f_{SRF} \approx 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_p})$ and turns capacitive above it ([[q0047]]).
:::

::: long
"The impedance of an ideal inductor is $Z_L = j\omega L$, or written with ordinary frequency, $j2\pi fL$.

I'd derive it from the defining equation, $v = L\,di/dt$. If I drive the inductor with a sinusoidal current and write it as a phasor, $Ie^{j\omega t}$, then the time derivative just pulls out a factor of $j\omega$. So the voltage phasor is $j\omega L$ times the current phasor, and impedance is that ratio. In the Laplace domain the same idea gives $sL$.

There are two parts to interpret. First, the magnitude, $\omega L$. It's proportional to frequency. At DC, omega is zero, so an ideal inductor is a short circuit. As frequency goes up the impedance grows without bound, so at very high frequency it looks like an open. That's why inductors are used to block high-frequency noise while passing DC, like in a power-line filter or a ferrite choke.

Second, the $j$. It means the impedance is purely imaginary, with a phase of plus 90 degrees. The voltage leads the current by a quarter cycle, or equivalently the current lags. Because there's no real part, an ideal inductor dissipates no average power: it stores energy in its magnetic field for half a cycle and gives it back the next.

For a feel for the numbers: 10 µH at 100 kHz is about 6.3 ohms, and at 1 MHz it's about 63 ohms. A common slip is forgetting the $2\pi$, which gives you an answer 6.28 times too small.

In a real inductor I'd add the winding resistance in series, so it's $R + j\omega L$ and the ratio $\omega L/R$ is the quality factor. At high enough frequency the capacitance between turns takes over: the impedance peaks at the self-resonant frequency and then actually falls, so above that the part behaves like a capacitor."
:::

::: terms
- **Impedance (Z)**: the complex ratio of voltage phasor to current phasor, $Z = R + jX$, in ohms.
- **Inductive reactance ($X_L$)**: $\omega L$, the imaginary part of an inductor's impedance; positive.
- **Angular frequency (ω)**: $2\pi f$, in rad/s.
- **Phasor**: a complex number encoding a sinusoid's amplitude and phase at a single frequency.
- **Quality factor (Q)**: $\omega L/R_s$, the ratio of reactance to series loss resistance; higher is a "purer" inductor.
- **Susceptance (B)**: the imaginary part of admittance; for an inductor $-1/(\omega L)$.
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: where winding capacitance resonates with $L$; above it the part is capacitive.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** writes "$Z = j\omega L$".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives it in one line from $v = L\,di/dt$ with phasors ($d/dt \to j\omega$).
- Interprets both magnitude (linear in $f$, short at DC, open at high $f$) and phase (+90°, voltage leads, zero average power).
- Plugs in a number correctly with the $2\pi$, e.g. 10 µH at 1 MHz ≈ 63 Ω, and maybe a quick contrast with a capacitor.
- Mentions $sL$ for Laplace analysis.
- Adds the practical model $R_{DC} + j\omega L$, defines $Q$, and notes the self-resonant frequency where the ideal equation stops applying.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Forgetting 2π.** $X_L = 2\pi fL$, not $fL$. Using $f$ directly underestimates impedance by 6.28×.
- **Dropping the j.** Writing $Z_L = \omega L$ loses the phase. You can't add it to a resistance directly: 10 Ω of R in series with 10 Ω of $X_L$ is $|Z| = 14.1$ Ω, not 20 Ω.
- **Sign confusion with capacitors.** $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C) = -j/(\omega C)$, negative reactance. $Z_L = +j\omega L$. See [[q0042]].
- **Saying an inductor "dissipates power" because it has impedance.** An ideal inductor's impedance is purely reactive: zero average power. Real losses come from DCR, AC resistance and core loss ([[q0050]]).
- **Applying $j\omega L$ above the self-resonant frequency.** There the part is capacitive; check the datasheet's |Z| curve.
:::

::: deep
## Deriving it two ways

**Phasor method.** Write a sinusoidal current as the real part of a rotating complex number: $i(t) = \text{Re}\{Ie^{j\omega t}\}$. The inductor law gives

$$v(t) = L\frac{d}{dt}\text{Re}\{Ie^{j\omega t}\} = \text{Re}\{j\omega L\,I e^{j\omega t}\}$$

so the voltage phasor is $V = j\omega L\, I$ and

$$Z_L = \frac{V}{I} = j\omega L$$

**Time-domain check.** Let $i(t) = I_p\sin(\omega t)$. Then $v(t) = L\,\frac{di}{dt} = \omega L I_p\cos(\omega t) = \omega L I_p\sin(\omega t + 90^\circ)$. The amplitude ratio is $\omega L$ and the voltage leads by 90°. Same answer, no complex numbers needed, which is a nice sanity check if an interviewer asks "why does the voltage lead?"

**Laplace method.** $\mathcal{L}\{L\,di/dt\} = L[sI(s) - i(0^-)]$. With zero initial current, $V(s) = sL\,I(s)$, so $Z_L(s) = sL$. Evaluate on the imaginary axis, $s = j\omega$, to get back $j\omega L$. The $-L\,i(0^-)$ term matters for transients: it's an initial-condition source representing energy already stored.

## Magnitude: linear in frequency

$$|Z_L| = \omega L = 2\pi f L$$

On log–log axes this is a straight line with slope +1 (+20 dB/decade). Two consequences:

- **DC (f = 0):** $Z_L = 0$. An ideal inductor is a short. A real one is its DC resistance.
- **High f:** $|Z_L| \to \infty$. An ideal inductor is an open. A real one stops being inductive at its self-resonant frequency.

## Phase: why "+j" means no power

Average power into any impedance is $P = \tfrac12|I|^2\,\text{Re}\{Z\}$. For $Z_L = j\omega L$ the real part is zero, so $P = 0$. Instantaneously, power $p = vi$ alternates sign every quarter cycle: the inductor absorbs energy while $|i|$ grows and returns it while $|i|$ falls. Reactive power is $Q = \tfrac12|I|^2\omega L$ (in VAR), which is real current your source and wiring must carry even though no net work is done. That's why utilities penalize poor power factor from inductive motor loads.

## Using it: phasor Ohm's law

Because impedance is just a complex resistance, all DC techniques carry over. Example: 10 Ω in series with 1 mH at 1 kHz.

$$X_L = 2\pi(1000)(10^{-3}) = 6.28\ \Omega,\qquad Z = 10 + j6.28\ \Omega$$
$$|Z| = \sqrt{10^2 + 6.28^2} = 11.8\ \Omega,\qquad \angle Z = \tan^{-1}(6.28/10) = 32.1^\circ$$

With 1 V peak applied, the current is 84.7 mA peak, lagging the voltage by 32.1°. The **corner frequency** where $X_L = R$ is $f = R/(2\pi L) = 1.59$ kHz. Below it the resistor dominates; above it the inductor does. That one idea becomes the RL filter in [[q0053]].

## Worked example: impedance of a real 10 µH part across frequency

Take the model from above, $Z=(R+j\omega L)\parallel\frac{1}{j\omega C_p}$, with $L=10\ \mu$H, $R=50$ mΩ and $C_p=5$ pF. These are illustrative values for an SMD power inductor (read your part's datasheet and its impedance-versus-frequency curve). Evaluating the complex impedance at several frequencies gives:

| Frequency | Ideal $\omega L$ | Real $\lvert Z\rvert$ | Real phase | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 kHz | 63 mΩ | 80 mΩ | +51.5° | DCR is comparable to $X_L$ |
| 10 kHz | 0.628 Ω | 0.630 Ω | +85.5° | nearly ideal |
| 100 kHz | 6.28 Ω | 6.28 Ω | +89.5° | ideal |
| 1 MHz | 62.8 Ω | 63.0 Ω | +90° | ideal |
| 10 MHz | 628 Ω | 783 Ω | +90° | $C_p$ starts to help the impedance rise |
| 22.5 MHz | 1.41 kΩ | very large | near +90° | self-resonance (peak) |
| 100 MHz | 6.28 kΩ | 335 Ω | −90° | capacitive: $1/(\omega C_p)$ |

Two things stand out. First, below roughly $f=R/(2\pi L)=800$ Hz the DCR, not the inductance, sets the impedance, so the +90° phase is only reached a decade or more above that. Second, the ideal $\omega L$ keeps growing while the real part peaks and then falls: at 100 MHz, the 6.3 kΩ that the equation promises is really 335 Ω, set entirely by the 5 pF.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The peak at SRF is only huge here because the model holds $R$ at 50 mΩ. A real part has an $R_{ac}$ that rises with frequency (skin, proximity and core loss), which damps the peak to a finite value, often in the kΩ range. Use the manufacturer's measured curve or S-parameters for the actual peak.

## Why an LCR meter reads the wrong inductance near SRF

A meter that assumes a pure series $L$ interprets the capacitive parallel path as extra inductance. Using only $L$ and $C_p$, the apparent inductance is

$$L_{app}=\frac{L}{1-\omega^2LC_p}.$$

For the same part the apparent value is 10.02 µH at 1 MHz, 10.5 µH at 5 MHz, 12.5 µH at 10 MHz, 18.0 µH at 15 MHz and 47.5 µH at 20 MHz, and it heads to infinity at the SRF. So an inductor that "increases" with frequency in a datasheet plot, or on your own measurement, is not magical: you are watching $C_p$. The honest value of $L$ is the low-frequency plateau, measured well below SRF (a rule of thumb is a tenth of it or lower, a figure that depends on how accurate you need to be).

## Worked example: what $X_L$ does to ripple in a buck converter

The impedance idea is not only for sinusoids. In a buck converter from 12 V to 3.3 V at 500 kHz with a 4.7 µH inductor, the duty cycle is $D=3.3/12=0.275$, and the peak-to-peak ripple current is

$$\Delta I=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})D}{Lf_s}=\frac{(12-3.3)(0.275)}{4.7\ \mu\text{H}\times500\text{ kHz}}=1.02\ \text{A}.$$

The inductor presents $X_L=2\pi(500\text{ kHz})(4.7\ \mu\text{H})=14.8\ \Omega$ to the switching fundamental, which is why the current ripple is much smaller than the $V/R$ of a resistor would suggest. The impedance view shows why a bigger $L$ means smaller ripple. Now introduce saturation: if the inductance falls 30 % at the peak current ([[q0046]]), the same equation gives $\Delta I=1.02/0.7=1.45$ A, a 43 % higher ripple, which also raises the peak current that further saturates the core. Inductance is not a constant in a real design, and a quick impedance estimate at the operating point must use the inductance at that bias, not the datasheet's zero-bias value.

## Measuring $L$ and $Z$ with ordinary bench gear

1. **Series-resistor method.** Put a known resistor, for example 10 Ω, in series with the inductor and drive the pair with a sine at 100 kHz. Read the phase between the source voltage and the voltage across the resistor on a scope. For a 10 µH part the phase should be $\arctan(6.28/10)=32.1^\circ$ with the current lagging. Solve for $L$ from the phase measurement with $L=R\tan\phi/\omega$. Choose $R$ comparable to $\omega L$ so the phase lands well between 0° and 90°, where it is easiest to read accurately, and remember the source's own output resistance adds to $R$.
2. **Amplitude method.** Measure the voltage ratio $|V_L|/|V_R|=\omega L/R$ for the same circuit. A scope's amplitude is easier than its phase at high frequency, since probe delays corrupt phase measurements.
3. **Impedance analyzer or LCR meter.** Use a 4-terminal fixture, short leads, and a test signal level and bias appropriate for the part. A power inductor measured at a small signal reads the zero-bias inductance, which can be well above its inductance at rated DC current. If the part is a ferrite bead, expect the resistive and reactive parts to be comparable over a wide band, so the reading should be $|Z|$, not $L$.
4. **Find SRF.** Sweep upward until the phase crosses zero. The frequency where the phase passes through zero going from +90° to −90° is the self-resonance. It should match $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_p})$ for the $C_p$ you can back out of it: $C_p=1/(\omega_{SRF}^2L)$.

## Where the ideal equation breaks

A real inductor's impedance is closer to

$$Z(\omega) = \left(R_{ac}(\omega) + j\omega L\right)\ \Big\|\ \frac{1}{j\omega C_p}$$

- At low frequency, $R_{DC}$ dominates: a 10 µH power inductor might have 20–50 mΩ, so below a few hundred hertz its impedance is basically resistive.
- $R_{ac}$ rises with frequency from skin effect, proximity effect and core loss ([[q0051]]).
- $C_p$ (a few pF for typical SMD power inductors) resonates with $L$ at $f_{SRF} = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_p})$. For 10 µH and 5 pF that's 22.5 MHz. Above it, $|Z|$ falls like a capacitor's.
- With a magnetic core, $L$ itself falls with DC bias ([[q0046]]) and, for ferrites, with frequency as permeability rolls off.

**Quality factor.** $Q = \omega L/R_s$ measures how close the part is to ideal. A 10 µH, 50 mΩ inductor at 100 kHz has $Q = 6.28/0.05 \approx 126$. RF inductors are chosen for high Q at the operating frequency; ferrite beads have deliberately low Q.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Always say the number out loud with the $2\pi$: "10 microhenries at 1 megahertz is 2π times 10 to the 6 times 10 to the minus 5, about 63 ohms." It shows you can actually use the equation.
:::

::: applications
- **LC output filter of a buck converter**: at the 500 kHz switching frequency a 4.7 µH inductor has $X_L \approx 14.8$ Ω, which together with the output capacitor knocks the switching ripple down.
- **EMI chokes**: a 100 µH choke is 6.3 Ω at 10 kHz but 628 Ω at 1 MHz, blocking conducted noise while passing DC.
- **RF matching**: a few nH chip inductors provide specific reactances at 2.4 GHz (e.g. 3.3 nH ≈ 50 Ω) to match an antenna to 50 Ω.
- **Motor windings**: at PWM frequencies (e.g. 20 kHz) the winding's $X_L$ dominates its resistance, which smooths current ripple in the motor ([[q0144]]).
- **Power factor**: inductive loads draw lagging current; utilities and PFC circuits deal with the reactive power $\tfrac12|I|^2\omega L$.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Sinusoidal steady state** at a single frequency. For transients use $v = L\,di/dt$ or the Laplace form with initial conditions.
- **Ideal, linear inductor**: constant $L$, no resistance, no capacitance. Real parts add DCR, AC resistance, core loss and winding capacitance.
- **Below the self-resonant frequency.** Above SRF the measured impedance is capacitive.
- **Passive sign convention** and the engineering $j$ (some physics texts use $e^{-i\omega t}$, which flips the sign of $i$ in impedances: $Z_L = -i\omega L$). The physics is the same.
- If the interviewer wants a magnitude only, "$X_L = 2\pi fL$" is acceptable, but mention the phase.
:::

::: followups
### What's the impedance of 10 Ω in series with 2 mH at 500 Hz?
$X_L = 2\pi(500)(0.002) = 6.28$ Ω, so $Z = 10 + j6.28$ Ω, $|Z| = 11.8$ Ω at +32.1°. The current lags the voltage by 32.1°.

### At what frequency does a 4.7 µH inductor have 100 Ω of reactance?
$f = X_L/(2\pi L) = 100/(2\pi\cdot4.7\times10^{-6}) \approx 3.39$ MHz.

### How does the impedance of an inductor compare to a capacitor?
$Z_L = j\omega L$ grows with frequency and has +90° phase; $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C) = -j/(\omega C)$ falls with frequency and has −90° phase. They're duals: an inductor is a short at DC and open at high frequency, a capacitor the opposite. In series they resonate where $\omega L = 1/(\omega C)$, i.e. $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, and the reactances cancel. See [[q0024]].

### Why is the average power in an ideal inductor zero even though current flows?
Because voltage and current are 90° apart, the instantaneous power $vi$ is positive for a quarter cycle (energy into the field) and negative for the next quarter (energy back to the circuit). Over a full cycle it averages to zero: $P = \tfrac12 V_pI_p\cos 90^\circ = 0$.

### What if you measure an inductor with an LCR meter at 100 kHz and then at 30 MHz and get a capacitive reading at 30 MHz?
You've passed the self-resonant frequency. The winding capacitance resonates with $L$ at $f_{SRF}$, and above it the capacitive path dominates. For a 10 µH part with ~3 pF of winding capacitance, SRF is about 29 MHz. The part isn't broken; it just isn't an inductor at that frequency. Datasheets give SRF so you can stay well below it.
