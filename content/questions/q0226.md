::: basics
### In plain words
You can measure a buck's current at different places, and the place depends on the purpose. Near the inductor or switch, you get the fast, rippling current that control and protection need. At the output, in series with the load, you get the smooth, slower current that metering and accuracy need.

### Picture it
Think of a river feeding a reservoir, with a steady stream leaving it. Upstream of the reservoir the flow surges and dips with each pulse of the pump. Downstream, the reservoir has smoothed it into a steady flow. If you want to react to each surge, measure upstream. If you want the real consumption, measure downstream. The picture leaves out one cost: a sensor in the outflow sits in the load's path and drops a little voltage itself.

### Words you need
- **Inductor current**: the actual current in the power stage; it ramps up and down ([[q0188]]).
- **Ripple**: the up-and-down swing of that current each cycle.
- **Peak current-mode control**: a control scheme that turns the switch off when the inductor current reaches a limit ([[q0252]]).
- **Cycle-by-cycle limit**: a protection that checks the current every switching cycle.
- **Common-mode voltage**: the average voltage level the sense signal sits on.

### The simplest example
A buck feeds a steady 3 A load, and the inductor ripple is 1 A peak-to-peak. At the inductor, the current ramps between 2.5 A and 3.5 A each cycle, so a protection circuit sees the 3.5 A peak immediately. In series with the load, after the capacitor, the current is a smooth 3 A with almost no wobble. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows how to pick the sense point, and the costs of each: a series drop at the output and extra delay behind the capacitor ([[q0225]]).
:::

::: testing
Whether you treat sensing location as a **design decision tied to purpose**, not a matter of convenience. A strong answer says "it depends on what the measurement is for" and then maps each purpose to a place:

1. Cycle-by-cycle control or protection: at the switch or inductor, where you see the current *before* it is averaged by the output capacitor.
2. Accurate load current or telemetry: in series with the load, after the output capacitor (or an averaged inductor current).
3. The trade-offs: ripple and spikes, common-mode voltage, power loss, bandwidth and what the capacitor does to the signal.
:::

::: why
Where you tap the current decides how fast you can react to a short circuit, whether you can do current-mode control, whether your fuel-gauge number is correct, and how much noise you fight. A sense resistor at the wrong place can inject switching noise, add a drop in the output, or give you a signal that the capacitor has already averaged away. Interviewers like this question because it forces the candidate to connect the power stage waveforms ([[q0185]]) to the control and protection requirements ([[q0227]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor current waveform**: a triangle of average $I_{out}$ with ripple $\Delta I_L$. See [[q0188]].
- **Switch currents**: the high-side FET carries $i_L$ during the on-time only; the low-side FET carries it during the off-time.
- **Output capacitor current**: $i_C=i_L-i_{load}$, zero average. The capacitor filters the ripple, so the load sees a smooth DC. See [[q0189]].
- **Peak, valley and average current**: three different numbers from the same waveform, needed for different jobs.
- **Common-mode voltage**: the DC level of the sense terminals; at the switch node it swings from 0 to $V_{in}$ every cycle, at the output it is $V_{out}$.
- **Blanking**: ignoring the sense signal briefly after a switching edge.
:::

::: answer
"It depends on what the measurement is for. If I need it for control or fast protection, like peak current-mode control or cycle-by-cycle limiting, I sense the inductor or switch current, before the output capacitor. The inductor current is the real state of the power stage, it responds immediately, and the capacitor hasn't filtered it. I'd typically use the high-side FET, the low-side FET, or the inductor's DCR.

If I want the load current, for telemetry, a fuel gauge or a precise output limit, I sense after the inductor and output capacitor, in series with the load. There the current is smooth DC, the common-mode is just the output voltage, and I can use a precision shunt and filter freely. The cost is a small series drop in the output, and the signal is slower because the capacitor sits in front of it.

Sensing right after the inductor but before the capacitor still sees the ripple, because that's still the inductor current. And somewhere else, like the input side, is useful for input current limit and inrush. So the answer is: fast and inside for control, slow and at the output for accuracy."
:::

::: technical
**Available current signals** (CCM, steady state; $i_L$ has average $I_{out}$ and ripple $\Delta I_L$):
- High-side FET: $i_{HS}=i_L$ for $0<t<DT$, else 0. Average $D\,I_{out}$. Pulsed, with a turn-on spike. Common mode swings 0 to $V_{in}$ for a high-side shunt.
- Low-side FET (or diode): $i_{LS}=i_L$ for $DT<t<T$. Average $(1-D)I_{out}$.
- Inductor: $i_L$, continuous. Peak $I_{out}+\Delta I_L/2$, valley $I_{out}-\Delta I_L/2$, average $I_{out}$.
- Output capacitor: $i_C=i_L-i_{load}$, average zero.
- Load: $i_{load}$ = $I_{out}$ plus whatever the capacitor does not filter.

**Purpose to location.**
- *Peak current-mode control / cycle-by-cycle limit*: high-side switch or inductor, sensed on the rising ramp, with leading-edge blanking.
- *Valley current limit, zero-crossing*: low-side FET.
- *Current sharing across phases, droop/load-line*: inductor (DCR) with an averaging filter.
- *Output current limit/telemetry*: after $C_{out}$, in series with the load, or averaged inductor current.
- *Input limit, inrush, power metering*: at $V_{in}$.

**Example.** $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I_{out}=2$ A, $\Delta I_L=1.02$ A: peak 2.51 A, valley 1.49 A. The high-side current averages only 0.55 A.

**Trade-offs.** Switch and inductor locations are fast but noisy and need blanking. The post-capacitor location is accurate and quiet but slow, and costs $I^2R_s$ in the output path. Input sensing is the cheapest but is pulsed, so it must sit upstream of $C_{in}$.
:::

::: short
"For control and fast protection I sense the inductor or switch current, before the output capacitor, because that's the real state of the power stage and it isn't filtered yet. For accurate load current or telemetry I sense after the capacitor in series with the load, where it's smooth DC and the common-mode is just $V_{out}$. Sensing right after the inductor still shows the ripple. And the input side is for input current limit. So the location follows the purpose."
:::

::: long
"I'd start by asking what the measurement is for, because the same converter has several different 'output currents'.

Inside the power stage, the inductor current is a triangle with an average equal to the load current plus a ripple. The high-side FET carries it only during the on-time, the low-side FET during the off-time, and the output capacitor takes the difference between the inductor current and the load current, so its average is zero.

For control and fast protection, I want the inductor current itself, before the capacitor. Peak current-mode control and cycle-by-cycle limiting need to know when the ramp hits a threshold, within a few hundred nanoseconds. So I'd use the high-side switch current, or a SenseFET, or the inductor's DCR. A short circuit is seen immediately that way, whereas the output capacitor would hide it for several cycles. The price is noise: the signal has a turn-on spike and common mode that swings with the switch node, so I'd use leading-edge blanking and careful filtering.

For the load current as a number, say telemetry, battery gauging or a precise output current limit, I'd put a shunt after the output capacitor in series with the load. There the current is smooth DC, the common mode is just $V_{out}$, and I can filter heavily. The cost is a small drop in the output, a few millivolts, which I can include in the feedback loop with remote sense, and the signal is slower since the capacitor sits in between.

Another location that's useful is the input, for inrush and input current limiting. And sensing right after the inductor but before the capacitor isn't really the load current: it's still the inductor current with all its ripple.

So: before the capacitor for speed and control, after the capacitor for accuracy and smoothness."
:::

::: terms
- **Peak current sensing**: reading $i_L$ at the end of the on-time. Used for peak current-mode control and short-circuit limits.
- **Valley current sensing**: reading $i_L$ at the end of the off-time, typically via the low-side FET.
- **Average current sensing**: filtering $i_L$ or measuring in series with the load.
- **Cycle-by-cycle limit**: terminating the on-time as soon as the sensed current hits a threshold.
- **Leading-edge blanking**: masking the turn-on spike for a fixed time.
- **High-side vs low-side sensing**: shunt on the supply side of the load versus the ground side.
- **Common-mode range**: allowable DC voltage on the sense inputs.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "after the inductor, in series with the load."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts with "it depends on the purpose" and lists the signals: switch current, inductor current, capacitor current, load current.
- Notes that **sensing between the inductor and capacitor sees the ripple**, because it is still $i_L$.
- Explains why control wants the inductor/switch current (speed, it is the state variable) and telemetry wants the post-capacitor DC.
- Uses numbers: peak 2.51 A / valley 1.49 A / average high-side 0.55 A for a 2 A load.
- Lists the trade-offs: blanking, common mode, series drop, response time, and mentions the low-side/valley and input options.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Sensing only the high-side current and calling it the load current.** It averages $D\cdot I_{out}$, and is zero during the off-time. To recover $I_{out}$ you'd divide by $D$, which is inaccurate.
- **Believing the inductor current equals the load current at every instant.** They differ by the capacitor current; only the *averages* match.
- **Placing a sense resistor in the output path and ignoring the voltage drop.** At 3 A and 50 mΩ you lose 150 mV (4.5% of a 3.3 V rail) unless the sense is inside the feedback loop.
- **Forgetting blanking.** The turn-on spike can trip a limit falsely.
- **Believing "after the inductor" means "after the capacitor".** The node between $L$ and $C_{out}$ still carries the triangular inductor current.
- **Using the output location for short-circuit protection.** The post-capacitor shunt measures load current, which during a fault is mostly the capacitor discharging. The inductor and switch current, which is what actually needs limiting, is not measured, and a fault upstream of the shunt is invisible.
:::

::: deep
## Which current is "the output current"?

A buck has four candidate currents. With $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V ($D=0.275$), $f_{sw}=500$ kHz, $L=4.7$ µH and a 2 A load, the inductor ripple is $\Delta I_L=1.02$ A. Then:

| Signal | Shape | Average | Peak | Where it lives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| High-side FET current | Pulses during on-time (550 ns) | 0.55 A ($D I_{out}$) | 2.51 A | between $V_{in}$ and SW |
| Low-side FET current | Pulses during off-time (1.45 µs) | 1.45 A | 2.51 A | between SW and ground |
| Inductor current | Continuous triangle | 2.00 A | 2.51 A (valley 1.49 A) | between SW and $V_{out}$ |
| Capacitor current | Triangle around zero | 0 A | +0.51 A / -0.51 A | across $C_{out}$ |
| Load current | Nearly DC | 2.00 A | ~2.00 A | after $C_{out}$ |

## Case 1: control and fast protection means before the capacitor

**Peak current-mode control** compares the rising inductor current to the error-amplifier output. It needs the current while it ramps, so it measures the high-side FET current (or inductor current) during the on-time and terminates the pulse at the threshold ([[q0252]]). **Cycle-by-cycle current limit** does the same with a fixed threshold. **Valley current limit** samples the low-side current near the end of the off-time and delays the next turn-on if it is above the limit; it is popular for high step-down ratios with very short on-times ([[q0221]]).

Why before the capacitor? Because the inductor current is the *state variable* of the power stage. A short at the output makes $v_L=V_{in}-V_{out}\approx V_{in}$ for the whole on-time: with $L=4.7$ µH and $V_{in}=12$ V, $di/dt=2.55$ A/µs, so in 400 ns the current climbs by 1 A. Sensing it at the switch catches this inside one cycle. A shunt in series with the load does see a sudden load-side surge, but most of that first surge is the output capacitor dumping its charge, not the switch current. It tells you nothing directly about the inductor and FET current, which is what saturates the inductor and overstresses the switches, and it is blind to faults upstream of it (a shorted $C_{out}$ or switch node).

The downsides, visible in the sense signal:
- **Turn-on spike.** Charging $C_{oss}$ and reverse recovery produce a current spike in the high-side FET for tens of ns. Use blanking, e.g. 50 to 100 ns, and a small RC filter.
- **Common mode.** A high-side shunt sits at the switch node (0 to 12 V swing). Use the FET's $R_{DS(on)}$/SenseFET, or a DCR network referenced to $V_{out}$.
- **Peak-vs-average error.** The peak current is $I_{out}+\Delta I_L/2$, not $I_{out}$, so a 3 A peak-limit threshold trips at a load of only $3\text{ A}-\Delta I_L/2$ (about 2.5 A here), and that point moves with $V_{in}$ and $L$.

## Case 2: the node between L and C_out is still the inductor current

It is tempting to say "sense after the inductor, before the capacitor, since that's the output". At that node the sense element still carries $i_L$: the full triangle. That is useful for averaging (sense and low-pass filter) or for current-mode control (the inductor current feeds the ramp), and indeed this is what an inductor-series shunt or DCR does ([[q0225]]). But the DC value is the load current only after averaging. The raw signal has ripple of $\Delta I_L$: with a 10 mΩ shunt that is 10 mV p-p riding on a 20 mV DC, and the amplifier must tolerate the common mode of $V_{out}$.

## Case 3: after C_out for accuracy and telemetry

Put the shunt in series with the load, after the capacitor. The current is smooth (ripple filtered by $C_{out}$), the common mode is $V_{out}$ (a constant), and a precision CSA can average and filter freely. This is the right place for:
- Fuel gauging and coulomb counting
- A precise "output current limit" or constant-current mode (for example in a USB charger)
- Load monitoring and current-aware power management

Costs: a series drop $I\cdot R_s$ (30 mV at 3 A and 10 mΩ, about 0.9% of 3.3 V), the $I^2R_s$ loss (90 mW), and a degradation of load regulation unless the sense point is inside the feedback loop (use remote sense after the shunt). There is also a *dynamic* cost: during transients and faults the load current and the inductor current differ by the capacitor current, so the load-side signal does not tell you what the switches are carrying. It is the wrong signal for protecting the power stage. A common design combines both: fast switch-current sensing for cycle-by-cycle protection, plus a slower precision shunt for telemetry or a programmable limit.

## Case 4: other places

- **Input side**: a shunt in series with $V_{in}$ measures input current for inrush limiting, battery-draw limiting and input-power calculation. The current is pulsed ($D\cdot i_L$ chopped) and filtered by $C_{in}$, so sense it upstream of $C_{in}$. Output power estimates require efficiency knowledge.
- **Low-side FET / valley**: see above; low common mode (the FET source is ground), and the on-time of the low-side is long, so the spike is less of an issue.
- **Multiphase**: each phase's inductor (DCR) is sensed for balancing, with the sum for the total load-line ([[q0236]]).

## Decision summary

| Purpose | Best location | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Peak current-mode control, short-circuit trip | High-side FET / inductor | Fast, the state variable |
| Valley limit, zero-cross detect | Low-side FET | Ground-referenced, long on-time |
| Phase current sharing, droop | Inductor DCR | Continuous, averaged, lossless |
| Accurate load current, fuel gauge | Series with load after $C_{out}$ | Smooth DC, low CM, precise |
| Inrush / input power | Input side | Protects the source |

::: applications
- **Peak current-mode buck ICs**: sense the high-side FET current internally (SenseFET), with blanking, for control and cycle-by-cycle limit.
- **USB-C chargers/power-path controllers**: a precision shunt after the output for a programmable constant-current limit.
- **CPU VRMs**: DCR sensing of each phase for balancing and a summed signal for the load-line (droop).
- **Battery fuel gauges**: a low-side shunt in the battery return path, read by a coulomb-counting gauge IC.
- **Hot-swap/eFuse**: a high-side shunt at the input with a fast comparator for short-circuit trip.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM buck with a synchronous low side** and a stiff output capacitor. In DCM, the inductor current returns to zero and the average-vs-peak relationships change.
- **Control is current-mode or voltage-mode with an added limit.** Pure voltage-mode controllers often only use a protection comparator.
- **"Output current" as the load current.** If the question means "inductor current", the inductor/switch location is the direct answer.
- **Ideal sensing elements.** Real ones add delay and noise; blanking time and filter RC introduce lag.
- Typical values (10 mΩ shunt, 50 to 100 ns blanking) are examples, not rules.
:::

::: followups
### Why not just sense the load current for peak current-mode control?
Peak current-mode needs the inductor current ramp inside each cycle. The load current is already filtered by $C_{out}$: it has no ramp and lags by many cycles, so using it would give a slow, effectively voltage-mode behaviour with no cycle-by-cycle limit. You need the inductor/switch current.

### Which location do you choose for a fast short-circuit trip?
The high-side FET or inductor, with a fast comparator. At $V_{in}=12$ V into a dead short with 4.7 µH, the current rises 2.55 A/µs, so with a 100 ns detection and gate delay it overshoots by about 0.3 A. A shunt after the capacitor would see the capacitor's discharge into the short, but not the switch current that needs limiting, and not a fault upstream of the shunt.

### What changes if the sense resistor sits after the output capacitor and the load jumps from 0 to 3 A?
The shunt sees the step immediately, because the load itself draws the current through it. But the inductor current has not caught up: it can only slew at $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L=1.85$ A/µs upward, and the controller needs several cycles to react. The capacitor supplies the difference, and the output droops. So the post-capacitor shunt tracks the *load* quickly, but it says nothing about what the power stage is doing yet. For loop control you want the inductor view.

### How do you get average load current from a high-side FET sense?
Filter the pulsed signal, which gives $D\cdot I_{out}$ (0.55 A for a 2 A load), then divide by $D$. This is inaccurate because $D$ changes with $V_{in}$ and losses, and the filtered value mixes spike and blanking errors. So only the inductor or load-side sense gives a trustworthy average.

### Where would you sense the current in a 6-phase CPU VRM?
At each inductor with DCR sensing (lossless, high current) and an NTC for compensation, giving per-phase currents for balancing, and a sum for the total load current and droop. A precision shunt at 100 A would waste a lot of power.
:::

::: interpretations
"Before or after the output inductor" is ambiguous about the capacitor. I treat "before the inductor" as the switch side (high-side/low-side FET current), "after the inductor" as the inductor current node and the node after the capacitor as the load side, and I note the subtlety that the node between $L$ and $C_{out}$ still carries the ripple.
:::
