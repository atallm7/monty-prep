::: basics
### In plain words
BJTs are used in two main ways. As a switch, a small base current turns a bigger load on and off, such as a relay or an LED. As an amplifier, a small signal at the base becomes a larger signal at the collector. Inside chips, BJTs also make references and temperature sensors, because their base-emitter voltage is very predictable.

### Picture it
Picture a light switch with a remote helper. The microcontroller pin is too weak to power a relay directly, so it works the switch of a stronger helper, which does the heavy lifting. That is the switch use. For the amplifier use, picture turning a small volume knob and hearing a much bigger sound. The analogy leaves out the base current limit and the small voltage the helper itself needs.

### Words you need
- **Switch**: a use where the transistor is fully on or fully off.
- **Amplifier**: a use where the output is a larger copy of a small input. See [[q0081]].
- **Saturation**: fully on, with a very small voltage across it.
- **Base resistor**: a resistor that sets how much base current flows.
- **Current gain ($\beta$)**: collector current divided by base current.

### The simplest example
Say you drive a 70 mA relay from a 3.3 V microcontroller pin. To make sure the transistor is fully on, use a forced gain of 10. That needs 7 mA of base current. The base resistor sees about $3.3 - 0.7 = 2.6$ V, so $2.6\,\text{V} / 7\,\text{mA} \approx 370$ Ω. A 330 Ω resistor is a good choice. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the amplifier uses, such as the common-emitter stage, and where BJTs appear inside chips.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know what BJTs are actually **good at** and can give specific, concrete circuits, not a vague "amplifier and switch". The interviewer is looking for:

1. The two classic roles: switch (cutoff/saturation) and amplifier (forward-active), with a number or two for each.
2. Where a BJT beats a MOSFET: high transconductance per unit current, low noise at low source impedance, matching of $V_{BE}$, predictable exponential law.
3. Analogue building blocks: current mirrors, differential pairs, emitter followers, bandgap references and temperature sensors.
4. Awareness of practical limits: base current, saturation, thermal runaway, and why many switching jobs have moved to MOSFETs.
:::

::: why
Even though CMOS dominates digital design, BJTs are still everywhere in analogue and power: inside op-amps and references, as driver and level-shift transistors, in RF and high-speed (SiGe), as IGBT outputs and as the cheapest way to switch a relay or LED from a microcontroller pin. A good answer shows you can pick the right device and size a base resistor, not just recite "amplifies current".
:::

::: concepts
- **Switching (cutoff and saturation)**: with a base drive, the BJT acts as a closed switch with $V_{CE(sat)}\approx0.1$-$0.3$ V. See [[q0087]].
- **Amplification (forward-active)**: $I_C=\beta I_B=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$ and $g_m=I_C/V_T$. See [[q0088]].
- **Exponential $V_{BE}$ law**: gives high gain per unit current and a predictable, temperature-dependent voltage.
- **Base current as input**: BJTs need drive current, unlike MOSFETs. See [[q0083]].
- **Diode behaviour of junctions**: the base-emitter junction is a diode. See [[q0072]].
:::

::: answer
"BJTs get used in two broad ways, as switches and as amplifiers, plus a set of analogue building blocks that exploit their exponential current-voltage law.

As a switch, I'd use an NPN to drive a relay, LED or buzzer from a microcontroller pin: emitter to ground, load in the collector, and a base resistor sized so the transistor saturates. For example, a 70 mA relay from a 3.3 V pin needs about 7 mA of base current with a forced gain of 10, so a base resistor around 330 ohms.

As an amplifier, the common-emitter stage gives voltage gain $-g_mR_C$ with $g_m=I_C/V_T$, so at 1 mA that's about 39 mS, and with a 5.6 kΩ collector resistor a gain of a couple of hundred. The emitter follower is a buffer with a low output impedance, handy for driving a load from a high-impedance source.

Inside ICs, BJTs show up in differential pairs, current mirrors, bandgap voltage references and temperature sensors, because $V_{BE}$ is so predictable and matches well between transistors. And at higher power and RF, you see them as IGBTs, power BJTs, and SiGe heterojunction transistors for GHz radio. The main limits to remember are the base current, the saturation voltage and thermal runaway, which is why MOSFETs have taken over most power switching."
:::

::: technical
**1. Switch.** Cutoff ($V_{BE}<0.5$ V): $I_C\approx0$. Saturation: $I_B\ge I_{C(sat)}/\beta_{forced}$ with $\beta_{forced}\approx10$ (a design rule since datasheet $\beta$ varies by 3x or more) giving $V_{CE(sat)}\approx0.1$-$0.3$ V. Base resistor: $R_B=(V_{drive}-V_{BE})/I_B$. Power in the switch: $P\approx V_{CE(sat)}I_C+V_{BE}I_B$.

**2. Amplifier.** In forward-active: $I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$, $g_m=I_C/V_T$ ($V_T=25.85$ mV at 300 K), $r_\pi=\beta/g_m$, $r_e=V_T/I_E$. Common-emitter (emitter bypassed): $A_v\approx-g_mR_C$; with unbypassed emitter resistor $R_E$: $A_v\approx-R_C/(r_e+R_E)$. Emitter follower: $A_v\approx1$, $R_{out}\approx r_e+R_S/(\beta+1)$.

**3. Analogue ICs.**
- Differential pair: input stage of op-amps and comparators; $g_m=I_C/V_T$ per side.
- Current mirror: $I_{out}=I_{ref}/(1+2/\beta)$ (matched pair), within 2% for $\beta=100$.
- Bandgap reference: PTAT voltage $\Delta V_{BE}=V_T\ln N$ (53.8 mV for $N=8$) added to the CTAT $V_{BE}$ (about $-2$ mV/K).
- Temperature sensor: $V_{BE}$ or $\Delta V_{BE}$ versus T.

**4. Logic and power.** TTL/ECL families (historic, still seen); IGBTs (a MOSFET driving a PNP) for kilovolt/hundreds-of-amps switching; power BJTs in linear regulators and audio output stages; RF SiGe HBTs.

**5. Why BJTs rather than MOSFETs.** Higher $g_m$ per unit current ($I_C/V_T$ versus roughly $2I_D/V_{ov}$), low $1/f$ noise and voltage noise for low source impedances, excellent $V_{BE}$ matching and predictability, and very good small-signal gain from tiny devices. Drawbacks: base current, limited speed at saturation, positive thermal feedback and a larger saturation voltage than a modern low-$R_{DS(on)}$ MOSFET.
:::

::: long
"I'd group BJT uses into four buckets.

First, switching. A BJT is a cheap way to drive something that a microcontroller pin can't. Emitter to ground, the load between the supply and the collector, and a base resistor from the pin. I design for saturation with a forced beta of about 10, because real beta varies a lot. For a 70 mA relay from 3.3 V, that's 7 mA of base current and a base resistor of about 330 ohms. I'd add a flyback diode across the coil, since the coil kicks back when the transistor turns off.

Second, amplification. In forward-active the collector current is $I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$, so the transconductance is $I_C/V_T$, about 39 mS per milliamp. A common-emitter stage with a bypassed emitter has a voltage gain of $-g_mR_C$, so at 1 mA and 5.6 kΩ you get roughly minus 200. Without the bypass, the gain is about $-R_C/(r_e+R_E)$, which is lower but more stable and linear. An emitter follower gives unity gain and low output impedance, so I'd use it as a buffer.

Third, analogue ICs. The exponential law and excellent matching make BJTs ideal for differential pairs in op-amp input stages, current mirrors where the output current tracks the reference to within a couple of percent, and bandgap references, where a PTAT voltage from two transistors at different current densities cancels the negative temperature coefficient of $V_{BE}$. The same trick gives temperature sensors.

Fourth, higher power and RF. IGBTs combine a MOSFET gate with a bipolar output stage for kilovolt, hundreds-of-amps drives, and SiGe heterojunction BJTs run at tens to hundreds of GHz.

The limits I'd mention are base current, which loads the driver, the saturation voltage around 0.2 V, which wastes power versus a low-$R_{DS(on)}$ MOSFET, and thermal runaway, because $V_{BE}$ falls with temperature. That's why MOSFETs have taken over most power switching but BJTs still win in analogue."
:::

::: terms
- **Saturation**: both junctions forward biased; transistor acts as a closed switch with $V_{CE(sat)}\approx0.2$ V.
- **Forced beta**: the conservative ratio $I_C/I_B$ used for saturated switch design (about 10).
- **Transconductance ($g_m$)**: $I_C/V_T$, change in collector current per volt of $V_{BE}$.
- **Common-emitter / common-collector**: amplifier configurations (gain stage; emitter follower buffer).
- **Current mirror**: two matched transistors copying a reference current.
- **PTAT / CTAT**: voltages proportional / complementary to absolute temperature; combined in bandgap references.
- **Flyback diode**: diode across an inductive load to clamp turn-off voltage spikes.
- **Thermal runaway**: rising temperature lowers $V_{BE}$ and raises current, which heats the device further.
:::

- Mentions power and RF uses (IGBT, power BJT, SiGe HBT) and notes that most power switching moved to MOSFETs.
- Flags limitations: base drive, saturation voltage and storage time, thermal runaway, beta variation.
- Mentions that PNP and NPN choices depend on high-side versus low-side loads.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Driving the load directly from the base with no resistor.** The base-emitter junction is a diode; without a series resistor the pin sources enormous current. Always size $R_B$.
- **Designing a switch using the datasheet's maximum $\beta$.** Beta varies 3x or more across parts and temperature, so use a forced beta of about 10 to guarantee saturation.
- **Forgetting the flyback diode** on relays, solenoids and motors, which can destroy the transistor on turn-off.
- **Using a BJT as a voltage-controlled device** and ignoring that the input draws current, so a high-impedance source gets loaded.
- **Assuming gain is constant.** $g_m$ scales with $I_C$ and $\beta$ with current and temperature.
- **Ignoring thermal runaway** in parallel BJTs without emitter resistors; one device hogs current.
:::

## Use 2: the BJT as an amplifier

In forward-active the collector current is exponential in $V_{BE}$:
$$I_C=I_S\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T},\qquad g_m=\frac{dI_C}{dV_{BE}}=\frac{I_C}{V_T}$$
At $I_C=1$ mA: $g_m=0.001/0.02585=38.7$ mS, a number that is independent of device size and process, which is a major advantage over MOSFETs.

**DC bias.** Thevenin base source: $V_{TH}=12\times16/116=1.655$ V, $R_{TH}=100\|16=13.8$ kΩ. With $\beta=100$: $I_B=\frac{1.655-0.7}{13.8\text{k}+101\times1\text{k}}=8.3\ \mu$A, so $I_C=0.83$ mA, $V_C=12-0.83\text{ mA}\times5.6\text{ k}=7.34$ V and $V_E=0.83$ V. The collector sits well above the base so the device is in forward-active.

**AC gain (C_E bypassed).** $g_m=0.83\text{ mA}/25.85\text{ mV}=32.2$ mS, $A_v=-g_mR_C=-180$ (unloaded, ignoring $r_o$). **Without** $C_E$: $r_e=25.85/0.83=31\ \Omega$, $A_v\approx-5600/(31+1000)=-5.4$. Much lower gain but much more stable and linear; designers often leave part of $R_E$ unbypassed.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Actual gain depends on $\beta$, early voltage ($r_o$), load and temperature. A $\beta$ of 100 is a typical datasheet value for a small-signal NPN but real parts range widely; treat the numbers as design estimates and verify in simulation or on the bench.

## Use 3: analogue building blocks

**Differential pair.** Two matched transistors sharing a tail current $I_{EE}$ amplify the difference of their base voltages with $g_m=I_{EE}/(2V_T)$ per side: at $I_{EE}=1$ mA, $g_m=19.3$ mS. This is the input stage of most op-amps and comparators.

**Current mirror.** A diode-connected transistor sets the reference current and a second matched transistor copies it. Base currents steal a bit: $I_{out}=I_{ref}\,\frac{1}{1+2/\beta}$, i.e. 0.980 for $\beta=100$ and 0.990 for $\beta=200$.

**Bandgap reference and temperature sensor.** Two BJTs with emitter-area ratio $N=8$ at equal current give $\Delta V_{BE}=V_T\ln N=53.8$ mV, proportional to absolute temperature (PTAT, about +0.18 mV/K). $V_{BE}$ itself falls about 2 mV/K (CTAT). Summing them with the right weights gives a reference near 1.2 V with nearly zero first-order temperature drift.

## Use 4: other places BJTs appear

- **Emitter follower (common-collector):** unity-gain buffer; $R_{out}\approx r_e+R_S/(\beta+1)$. With $R_S=10$ kΩ, $\beta=100$ and $I_C=1$ mA: $R_{out}\approx25.9+99\approx125\ \Omega$, so a 10 kΩ source can drive much heavier loads.
- **Linear regulators and Darlington output stages:** NPN pass devices.
- **IGBT:** a MOSFET gate controlling a PNP bipolar output stage; used at hundreds of volts and amps.
- **RF/microwave:** SiGe HBTs and InP HBTs as low-noise and power amplifiers.
- **Logic:** historic TTL and ECL; bipolar still used in some high-speed logic and BiCMOS drivers.
- **Phototransistors and optocouplers:** base driven by light.

## When to use a MOSFET instead

| Need | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Switching more than about 1 A with low loss | MOSFET (low $R_{DS(on)}$, no DC gate current) |
| Very high input impedance | MOSFET |
| Highest $g_m$ per mA, low voltage noise | BJT |
| Matched pairs, bandgap, temperature sense | BJT |
| Parallel devices sharing current | MOSFET (positive $R_{DS(on)}$ TC) |
| Cheap, simple, low-side load switch | Either; BJT is common for tiny loads |

## Worked example: what the common-emitter amplifier really delivers

The bias calculation above gave $I_C=0.83$ mA, $g_m=32.1$ mS and an unloaded gain of $-g_mR_C\approx-180$. A real stage has a source and a load, and each takes some of that gain away. Add a 10 kΩ load on the output and a 1 kΩ source resistance on the input.

1. **Loaded gain.** The signal sees $R_C\parallel R_L=5.6\text{ k}\parallel10\text{ k}=3.59$ kΩ, so $A_v=-g_m(3.59\text{ k})=-115$.
2. **Input resistance.** The base looks like $r_\pi=\beta/g_m=100/32.1\text{ mS}=3.11$ kΩ in parallel with the bias divider's $100\text{ k}\parallel16\text{ k}=13.8$ kΩ, giving $R_{in}=2.54$ kΩ.
3. **Source loss.** The input divider with the 1 kΩ source passes $2.54/(1+2.54)=0.72$ of the source voltage, so the gain from the source to the output is $-115\times0.72=-83$.

So the textbook $-180$ becomes $-83$, a 54 % loss, before a single capacitor has been considered. The lesson is the standard one: the BJT's gain is high but its input resistance is low, and bias networks and loads both shave it down. If you need to hold the gain, you drive the stage from a lower source resistance or add an emitter follower in front.

## Worked example: where the bandwidth goes

The base-collector capacitance $C_\mu$ (a reverse-biased junction, around 2 pF for a small-signal part, an assumed round figure here) sees the full inverting gain across it, so it is multiplied by the Miller effect to $C_\mu(1+|A_v|)=2\text{ pF}\times116=232$ pF. Suppose the transistor's transition frequency is $f_T=300$ MHz (a representative small-signal value; always read the datasheet). Then $C_\pi=g_m/(2\pi f_T)-C_\mu=17.0-2=15$ pF. The input pole is formed by the source resistance in parallel with $R_{in}$, which is $1\text{ k}\parallel2.54\text{ k}=718\ \Omega$, with the total capacitance of about $15+232=247$ pF:
$$f_{-3dB}=\frac{1}{2\pi(718)(247\text{ pF})}\approx0.9\ \text{MHz}$$
A transistor that is good to 300 MHz as a follower or current source gives only about 1 MHz when used as a gain stage with a 1 kΩ source. This is the gain-bandwidth trade-off in practice, and it is why wideband amplifiers use a cascode (which pins the collector of the input device so the Miller multiplier is about 1 instead of 116) or a lower-gain stage.

The low-frequency end is set by the coupling and bypass capacitors. With a 1 µF input capacitor the corner is $1/(2\pi(1\text{ k}+2.54\text{ k})(1\ \mu\text{F}))=45$ Hz; with a 1 µF output capacitor into 10 kΩ it is $1/(2\pi(5.6\text{ k}+10\text{ k})(1\ \mu\text{F}))=10$ Hz. The emitter bypass sees the much lower resistance looking back into the emitter, $R_E\parallel\left(r_e+\frac{R_S\parallel R_{th}}{\beta+1}\right)=1\text{ k}\parallel\left(31+\frac{1\text{ k}\parallel13.8\text{ k}}{101}\right)=1\text{ k}\parallel(31+9.2)\approx39\ \Omega$ (the base is driven through the 1 kΩ source, which is in parallel with the divider), so a 100 µF bypass capacitor puts that corner near 41 Hz. The bypass is typically the highest low-frequency corner and the one that most often needs enlarging.

## Headroom: how big a signal fits

The quiescent collector is at $V_C=7.35$ V. It can swing up to 12 V (when the transistor cuts off) and down to about $V_E+V_{CE(sat)}=0.83+0.2\approx1.0$ V, so the available room is +4.65 V upward but −6.3 V downward. The output clips on the positive side first; the largest symmetric swing is about 4.65 V peak before loading. A designer who needs more headroom moves the bias point or lowers $R_C$. With the 10 kΩ load the AC load line is steeper than the DC load line, so the usable swing shrinks further and the optimum bias current rises.

## BJT as a switch: what the simple design hides

The relay driver above forces $\beta=10$, so the transistor is deep in saturation. That has a cost. Saturation means both junctions are forward biased and the base region is flooded with stored minority charge; when the base drive is removed that charge must be pulled out or recombine before the collector can rise, so the turn-off is delayed by a **storage time**. For general-purpose small-signal switching transistors this is typically tens to a few hundred nanoseconds, and it grows with how hard the device was overdriven. For a relay it is irrelevant, but at 1 MHz PWM it would dominate the waveform. Remedies are to reduce the overdrive (forced $\beta$ of 20 instead of 10), add a small speed-up capacitor across the base resistor, use a Schottky clamp from base to collector (a Baker clamp), or switch to a MOSFET.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Storage and fall times depend strongly on the specific part, its test conditions and the base drive. The ranges quoted are typical of small-signal general-purpose devices; use the datasheet's switching times at conditions matching your circuit.

Two other real-hardware habits belong in the driver. Add a 10 kΩ or so resistor from base to emitter so that the transistor stays off when the microcontroller pin is high-impedance during reset, instead of floating and chattering the relay. And remember that the flyback diode increases the relay's release time because the coil current decays through a diode drop instead of a high voltage; if release time matters, a diode in series with a Zener (or just a TVS) clamps higher and releases faster.

## Verifying a BJT stage on the bench

- **DC operating point first.** Power the circuit with no signal and measure $V_B$, $V_E$ and $V_C$ with a DMM. For the amplifier above you should read about 1.5 V at the base, 0.83 V at the emitter and 7.3 V at the collector. $V_B-V_E$ far from 0.65 to 0.75 V, or $V_C$ pinned near $V_E$, means something is wrong with the bias or the transistor is in saturation.
- **Gain and clipping.** Inject a small sine (tens of millivolts, 1 kHz) and view input and output on an oscilloscope. Measure the gain and compare with the loaded estimate. Raise the amplitude until the output clips; the top should flatten first, matching the headroom calculation.
- **Frequency response.** Sweep the generator from 10 Hz to a few MHz and mark the −3 dB points; the upper corner should land near the Miller estimate. A probe adds 10 to 15 pF of capacitance, so measure with a 10x probe at a low-impedance node where possible, since probing the high-impedance collector can itself change the response.
- **Switch behavior.** Drive the relay-driver base with a square wave and look at the collector on the scope at the relay-off edge: you should see a clean clamp at about 5.7 V from the flyback diode. A spike far above that means the diode is missing or reversed.

::: applications
- **Relay, LED, solenoid and buzzer drivers**: NPN low-side switches such as 2N3904, BC547, or Darlington arrays (ULN2003).
- **Audio and small-signal amplifiers**: common-emitter and differential stages in preamps and op-amp input stages.
- **Bandgap references and temperature sensors**: on-chip $V_{BE}$-based sensors in microcontrollers and PMICs.
- **Op-amp and comparator input stages** (e.g. LM358, LM741, LM393): BJT differential pairs.
- **Linear regulator pass devices**: classic 7805-style regulators use bipolar pass transistors.
- **IGBT motor drives and induction heaters**; **SiGe RF front-ends** in Wi-Fi/5G transceivers.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Silicon small-signal NPN**, room temperature, $\beta\approx100$ and $V_{BE}\approx0.7$ V unless stated.
- **Quasi-static (low-frequency) analysis**; at high frequency $C_\pi$, $C_\mu$ and $f_T$ limit gain and speed.
- **Forced beta of 10** for saturated switches is a conservative design convention, not a physical constant; sometimes 20 is used with tight margins.
- **Design numbers are estimates**; verify with the datasheet's $h_{FE}$ at the actual $I_C$ and $V_{CE}$ and over temperature.
- **Alternative answer**: "switch and amplifier" with one example each is a good short answer; extend with analogue ICs only if asked.

::: followups
### How would you size the base resistor for an NPN driving a 20 mA LED from 5 V?
Choose the LED resistor first: with $V_F=2$ V and $V_{CE(sat)}=0.2$ V, $R_C=(5-2-0.2)/20\text{ mA}=140\ \Omega$. For saturation use forced $\beta=10$: $I_B=2$ mA. With a 3.3 V drive, $R_B=(3.3-0.7)/2\text{ mA}=1.3$ kΩ, so a 1.2 kΩ standard value gives 2.2 mA. If the drive is 5 V, $R_B=(5-0.7)/2\text{ mA}=2.15$ kΩ, so 2.2 kΩ. I'd also check the pin's current limit.

### Why use a BJT rather than a MOSFET in an amplifier?
For the same bias current a BJT has higher transconductance, $I_C/V_T$ (38.7 mS at 1 mA), versus a MOSFET's $2I_D/V_{ov}$, which is typically 5-10 mS for an overdrive of 0.2-0.4 V in strong inversion. That gives more gain per mA, and BJTs also have lower input-referred voltage noise and better $V_{BE}$ matching. The price is base current (noise and loading) and the need for a bias that is stable in temperature.

### What's the difference between operating a BJT as a switch and as an amplifier?
A switch uses only cutoff and saturation, with a large base drive and a fast transition across the active region. The amplifier lives in forward-active, biased at a quiescent point with small signals riding on top, where $V_{CE}$ is well above saturation. Switch design uses forced beta; amplifier design uses $g_m$, bias stability and linearity. See [[q0087]].

### Two BJTs in parallel for more current: any problems?
Yes, thermal runaway and current hogging. $V_{BE}$ falls about 2 mV/K, so the hotter transistor takes more current, gets hotter and takes more. Add emitter ballast resistors (for example 0.1-0.5 Ω so the drop is 50-100 mV at full current) to provide negative feedback and equalise sharing. MOSFETs share better because $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature.

### Explain how two BJTs can measure temperature.
At equal collector current in transistors with emitter-area ratio $N$, $\Delta V_{BE}=V_T\ln N=\frac{kT}{q}\ln N$, which is proportional to absolute temperature and independent of $I_S$. With $N=8$: 53.8 mV at 300 K, and 0.179 mV/K. It's amplified and digitised in a temperature sensor or combined with $V_{BE}$ for a bandgap reference. It works well because the matching of on-chip junctions is excellent.

### Scenario: your relay driver works on the bench but the transistor dies after a few weeks in the field. What do you suspect?
Turn-off voltage spikes from the coil: either the flyback diode is missing or too slow or its path inductance is high. Also check for a rated-current margin, temperature derating (beta drops cold, so there may be insufficient drive at low temperature, leading to partial saturation, heating and failure), and supply transients. Measure the collector voltage on a scope at turn-off; it should clamp near $V_{CC}+0.7$ V.
