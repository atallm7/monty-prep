::: basics
### In plain words
An inductor lets slow signals through and holds back fast ones. At DC, which never changes, it acts like a plain wire. As the signal changes faster, the inductor fights it more. That is why inductors are used to smooth out power and to filter out noise. It is the opposite of a capacitor, which blocks DC and passes fast signals.

### Picture it
Picture a heavy door. If you push it slowly and steadily, it moves along with you and hardly resists. If you try to shake it back and forth quickly, its weight fights every change of direction, and it barely moves. A slowly changing current is like the slow push, so it passes. A quickly changing signal is like the shaking, and the inductor blocks it.

### Words you need
- **Inductor**: a coil that resists changes in current. See [[q0039]].
- **Impedance**: opposition to AC, in ohms. For an inductor its size is $2\pi f L$, so it grows with frequency $f$. See [[q0040]].
- **Low-pass filter**: passes low frequencies and blocks high ones. See [[q0010]].
- **Corner frequency**: where the filter has started to cut the signal. For an inductor into a load, $R/(2\pi L)$.
- **Choke**: an inductor used to block high-frequency noise.

### The simplest example
Put a 100 µH inductor in series with a 50 Ω load. The corner frequency is $R/(2\pi L) = 50 / (2\pi \times 0.0001) \approx 80$ kHz. A 1 kHz signal passes almost untouched. A 1 MHz signal is cut down a lot, since the inductor's impedance is about 630 Ω, far more than the load. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the inductor plus capacitor filter used in power supplies, and the real-world limits of winding capacitance and saturation.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can read an inductor's behaviour from $Z_L=j\omega L$ and $v=L\,di/dt$ and turn it into a filter statement. The interviewer wants:

1. The headline: an inductor **passes DC and low frequencies and blocks high frequencies**, the opposite of a capacitor, so a series inductor is a **low-pass** filter (a "choke").
2. The topology caveat: a *shunt* inductor to ground shorts low frequencies, giving a **high-pass** response.
3. The time-domain meaning: it passes slow changes and steady current, but resists fast changes in current (edges, spikes).
4. Practical numbers (corner $R/2\pi L$, LC resonance) and limits: the real inductor's self-resonance and saturation.
:::

::: why
Inductors keep switching noise out of power rails, smooth the current in buck converters, keep RF out of DC bias lines, and form the L in every LC filter and EMI filter. Understanding what they pass and block is the quick way to analyze circuits ("at DC the inductor is a wire, at high frequency it's an open"), and it contrasts neatly with capacitors ([[q0034]]), which is how interviewers often pair these two questions.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor impedance** ([[q0040]]): $|Z_L|=2\pi fL$, zero at DC, rising with frequency.
- **Differential equation** ([[q0041]]): $v=L\,di/dt$; a fast current change needs a big voltage.
- **First-order RL filters** ([[q0010]]): corner $f_c=R/(2\pi L)$.
- **Second-order LC filters**: $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, $-40$ dB/dec, with a $Q$ set by the damping.
- **Duality with capacitors** ([[q0034]]): series C is high-pass; series L is low-pass.
- **Real inductor limits** ([[q0045]]): SRF, DCR, saturation ([[q0046]]).
:::

::: answer
"An inductor's impedance is j omega L, so it's zero at DC and grows with frequency. That means it passes DC and low frequencies and blocks high frequencies, the opposite of a capacitor. In the time domain, v equals L di/dt: the inductor resists changes in current, so it lets a steady current through but opposes fast edges.

As a filter, a series inductor feeding a load is a low-pass filter. With a resistive load R, the corner is R over two pi L. A 100 microhenry choke into 50 ohms has its corner at about 80 kilohertz. Above that it blocks more and more, at 20 dB per decade. If you put the inductor from the signal to ground instead, it shorts low frequencies and passes high ones, so that's a high-pass filter.

Combine an inductor in series with a capacitor to ground, and you get a second-order low-pass with 40 dB per decade and a corner at one over two pi root L C. That's how you smooth the output of a buck converter or build an EMI filter: a 4.7 microhenry and 22 microfarad pair has a corner near 15.7 kilohertz and attenuates a 500 kilohertz switching ripple by about 60 dB.

The caveats: a real inductor has winding capacitance, so above its self-resonant frequency it stops blocking, and DC current can saturate it, so a choke for a power line has to be rated for the current."
:::

::: technical
**Impedance:** $Z_L=j\omega L$; $|Z_L|=2\pi fL$; phase $+90^\circ$.

**Series L, load $R$ (low-pass):** $H(s)=\dfrac{R}{R+sL}=\dfrac{1}{1+sL/R}$, $f_c=\dfrac{R}{2\pi L}$, $\tau=L/R$; $-20$ dB/dec above $f_c$.

**Shunt L, series $R$ (high-pass):** $H(s)=\dfrac{sL}{R+sL}$, same $f_c=R/2\pi L$; $+20$ dB/dec below.

**Series L, shunt C, load $R$ (LC low-pass):**
$$H(s)=\frac{1}{s^2LC+sL/R+1},\quad f_0=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}},\quad Q=R\sqrt{C/L}.$$
Slope $-40$ dB/dec above $f_0$; attenuation at $f\gg f_0$ is $(f/f_0)^2$, i.e. $40\log_{10}(f/f_0)$ dB. Peaking near $f_0$ if $Q>0.707$.

**Time domain:** $i(t)=\frac1L\int v\,dt$. A step $V$ across $L$ in series with $R$ gives $i=\frac{V}{R}(1-e^{-t/\tau})$, $\tau=L/R$; an ideal inductor with a fixed voltage ramps current at $V/L$ (12 V across 10 µH: 1.2 A/µs).

**Passes / blocks (series L):** passes DC, slow variations, low-frequency AC; blocks fast edges, switching noise, RF.

**Numbers:** 100 µH into 50 Ω: $f_c=79.6$ kHz; 4.7 µH + 22 µF: $f_0=15.65$ kHz, $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}=0.46\ \Omega$, attenuation at 500 kHz $=40\log_{10}(500/15.65)=60$ dB.
:::

::: long
"The inductor's impedance is j omega L, so at DC it's zero, a short, and at high frequency it's large. So an inductor passes DC and low frequencies and blocks high frequencies, which is the mirror image of the capacitor. The time-domain version is v equals L di/dt: to change the current quickly, an inductor needs a large voltage, so it resists fast changes in current but lets steady current go through.

Filter behavior depends on where it sits. In series with the signal path, it's a low-pass filter. With a resistive load R, the output is R over R plus j omega L, which gives a corner of R over two pi L, and 20 dB per decade roll-off above it. For example a 100 microhenry choke into 50 ohms has a corner of about 80 kilohertz. Put the inductor from the line to ground, with a series resistor, and it shorts out low frequencies and passes high ones, so it's a high-pass filter.

More commonly, we pair it with a capacitor: a series L and a shunt C is a second-order low-pass, with a corner at one over two pi root LC and a roll-off of 40 dB per decade. A buck converter's output filter is the classic: 4.7 microhenries and 22 microfarads, resonant at about 15.7 kilohertz, so a 500 kilohertz switching ripple is attenuated by about 60 dB in the ideal case. The Q depends on damping from the load: R times root C over L.

And the limits. A real inductor has winding capacitance, so above its self-resonant frequency it stops blocking, and DC current saturates the core, so inductance falls at high current. For EMI chokes, I pick a part whose impedance is high at the noise frequency, not just a large nominal inductance, and for a power line I check the current rating and DCR."
:::

::: terms
- **Choke**: an inductor used to block high-frequency current while passing DC.
- **RL corner**: $f_c=R/(2\pi L)$.
- **LC resonance**: $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$.
- **Quality factor $Q$**: peaking/damping measure; $Q=R\sqrt{C/L}$ for a parallel-loaded LC.
- **Characteristic impedance**: $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}$.
- **Inductive kick / spike**: the voltage $L\,di/dt$ when current is interrupted.
- **Ripple filtering**: attenuating the switching-frequency component of a rail or current.
- **Common-mode vs differential-mode choke**: a choke acting on noise common to both lines vs. between them.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "inductors block AC and pass DC".

**An exceptional answer**:

- Connects $Z=j\omega L$ and $v=L\,di/dt$, and notes the duality with the capacitor ([[q0034]]).
- Gives the RL corner $R/2\pi L$ and the LC resonance with numbers; knows the second-order slope and what $Q$ means.
- Mentions real limits: SRF, saturation, DCR drop, and the need for damping (resonance peaking).
- Uses the buck output filter or an EMI choke as the example, with the attenuation at the switching frequency.
- Notes the safe-handling point: interrupting inductor current causes a voltage spike (flyback).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Inductors block DC."** They block *fast changes*; DC passes (with a $I^2R$ drop in DCR). Capacitors block DC.
- **Reversing the filter type.** Series L ⇒ low-pass; series C ⇒ high-pass.
- **Forgetting the load/source resistance.** The RL corner depends on $R$: $R/2\pi L$; with no resistance there's no corner, just a divider with the load.
- **Ignoring resonance peaking in LC filters.** A high-Q LC filter amplifies near $f_0$ and can interact with a converter's control loop.
- **Choosing the inductor by nominal µH only.** Above its SRF it's capacitive; check the impedance curve at the noise frequency.
- **Forgetting saturation.** A choke carrying large DC current can lose most of its inductance.
- **Using the 20 dB/dec number for an LC filter.** LC is 40 dB/dec.
:::

::: deep
## Two views

**Frequency domain.** $|Z_L|=2\pi fL$: for 100 µH, 0.063 Ω at 100 Hz, 6.3 Ω at 10 kHz, 63 Ω at 100 kHz, 628 Ω at 1 MHz. In a divider with a resistance $R$ the inductor "wins" at high frequency, so the output across the resistor is attenuated; the crossover $|Z_L|=R$ is $f_c=R/(2\pi L)$.

**Time domain.** $v=L\,di/dt$, so $i(t)=i(0)+\frac1L\int v\,dt$. Apply a step $V$ in series with $R$: the current rises as $\frac VR(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ with $\tau=L/R$ and settles to $V/R$ (inductor acting as a wire). Fast edges of voltage can't produce fast edges of current, so the inductor smooths the current, which is exactly what a switching converter needs.

## The RL low-pass

$H=\dfrac{R}{R+j\omega L}$, $|H|=1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}$, $f_c=R/2\pi L$. A 100 µH choke into 50 Ω: $f_c=79.6$ kHz, $\tau=2$ µs. At 10× the corner (796 kHz) the output is down 20 dB. Note: a *higher* load resistance means a *higher* corner (the opposite of an RC low-pass, where larger R lowers the corner): a lightly loaded series inductor barely filters, and the corner moves whenever the load current changes.

## The LC low-pass

Adding a shunt capacitor makes a second-order low-pass:
$$H(s)=\frac{1}{s^2LC+sL/R+1},\qquad f_0=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt{LC}},\quad Q=R\sqrt{\frac CL}.$$

## Reading the LC response: where peaking comes from

The denominator $s^2LC + sL/R + 1$ has the standard second-order form with natural frequency $\omega_0=1/\sqrt{LC}$ and damping ratio $\zeta=1/(2Q)$. At $f_0$ the real part $1-\omega^2LC$ vanishes and the gain magnitude is exactly $Q$:

$$|H(j\omega_0)|=\frac{1}{\omega_0L/R}=\frac{R}{\omega_0L}=Q$$

For $R=10\ \Omega$, $Q=21.6$ and the peak is $20\log_{10}21.6=26.7$ dB. A filter that attenuates 60 dB at 500 kHz also *amplifies* by 27 dB at 15.65 kHz when it is lightly loaded. The two facts do not conflict, since the high-frequency asymptote is $(f_0/f)^2$ regardless of $Q$. The $Q$ governs only what happens near $f_0$, and that is where a converter's load transients and control-loop crossover live.

## Worked example: how a buck inductor "blocks" the switching edge

A 12 V to 3.3 V buck converter runs at 500 kHz with $L=4.7\ \mu$H and $C=22\ \mu$F. The duty cycle is $D=3.3/12=0.275$. The switch node is a 12 V pulse train, and the inductor must pass the 3.3 V average (DC) and block the rest. Two independent ways to find the output ripple should agree:

1. **Time domain.** The inductor current ripple is $\Delta I=\dfrac{V_{out}(1-D)}{Lf_{sw}}=\dfrac{3.3\times0.725}{4.7\ \mu\text{H}\times500\ \text{kHz}}=1.02$ A peak-to-peak. If the capacitor is ideal and absorbs all of that ripple, the voltage ripple is $\Delta V=\dfrac{\Delta I}{8f_{sw}C}=\dfrac{1.02}{8\times5\times10^{5}\times22\times10^{-6}}=11.6$ mV peak-to-peak.
2. **Frequency domain.** The fundamental of the pulse train has peak amplitude $\dfrac{2V_{in}}{\pi}\sin(\pi D)=\dfrac{2\times12}{\pi}\sin(0.864)=5.81$ V. The LC filter attenuates it by $(f_{sw}/f_0)^2=1020$, leaving 5.7 mV peak, or 11.4 mV peak-to-peak, from that fundamental.

The two numbers (11.6 mV and 11.4 mV) agree to within 2%, which is a good sanity check: the "blocking" of the inductor is the same physics seen once in the time domain ($di/dt=v/L$) and once in the frequency domain ($|Z_L|=\omega L$). In a real design, the capacitor's ESR adds a term $\Delta I\times R_{esr}$; for a 5 mΩ ESR that is another 5.1 mV peak-to-peak, which is why the real ripple is higher than the ideal filter says ([[q0030]]).

## What changes when the inductor is not ideal

- **Saturation shifts the corner and the attenuation.** If the inductance falls by 30% at peak current, the attenuation at a given frequency, which scales as $LC$, falls by $20\log_{10}(0.7)=-3.1$ dB, and the current ripple rises to $\Delta I\propto1/L$: 1.45 A instead of 1.02 A in the example. Past saturation the inductance collapses and the current spikes, which is the failure mode of undersized chokes ([[q0046]]).
- **DCR is the price of passing DC.** At 3 A through a 20 mΩ winding the drop is 60 mV and the loss is $3^2\times0.02=0.18$ W, a real part of the efficiency budget in a small converter.
- **Self-resonance caps the blocking.** Above the SRF the winding capacitance takes over, so the filter attenuation stops improving and can even decrease at very high frequency. This is the reason a switching converter often needs a small ceramic capacitor or ferrite bead for the high-frequency spikes that the main inductor passes through its own capacitance.
- **Core loss and AC resistance** add loss that rises with frequency, which tends to damp the resonance and lowers the real $Q$ below what the ideal formula says.

## How to verify it on the bench

1. **Measure the inductor, not the label.** Use an LCR meter for $L$ at a frequency below the SRF, and a four-wire meter for DCR. Compare with the datasheet, including its tolerance (commonly ±20% for power inductors) and the stated saturation current definition, which is vendor-specific.
2. **Check saturation under DC bias.** Use an LCR meter or analyzer with DC-bias capability, or inspect the converter's inductor current with a current probe at maximum load. A triangular current ramp that turns upward at the end of the on-time is a saturating core.
3. **Look at the real ripple correctly.** Measure the output ripple with a short ground spring on the probe tip (a long ground lead picks up the switch-node edge and adds fake spikes). Use the scope's bandwidth limit only to separate fundamental ripple from spikes, not to hide them.
4. **Test the filter.** For an input or EMI filter, inject a swept sine through a network analyzer's injection transformer, or measure $S_{21}$ with the filter between two 50 Ω ports. Compare the peak and the slope with the calculation; a peak larger than predicted usually means low damping.
5. **Check interaction.** Step the load and watch the output for ringing at $f_0$. If it appears, add damping (a series resistor with a bulk capacitor, or an electrolytic's ESR) and repeat.

## Where chokes are used

1. **Ripple smoothing in converters**: the output inductor turns pulsed voltage into a smooth current.
2. **EMI filters**: an inductor (or ferrite bead) in series with a supply line, plus capacitors, attenuates conducted noise; common-mode chokes block noise that is the same on both lines but pass the differential signal.
3. **RF chokes** in bias lines: block the RF signal from leaking into the DC supply while passing DC.
4. **Current limiting in AC circuits**: reactance limits current without dissipating power (ballasts, motor starting).
5. **Anti-inrush and di/dt limiting** in power circuits.

## Real inductor limits

- **SRF.** Above the self-resonant frequency, the winding capacitance dominates and the part no longer blocks ([[q0045]], [[q0047]]). For EMI use, check impedance at the noise frequency from the vendor's curve.
- **DCR.** The "pass DC" statement is not free: the voltage drop is $I\cdot R_{dc}$ and heat $I^2R_{dc}$.
- **Saturation.** DC current in a choke reduces its inductance and thus its filtering ([[q0046]]).
- **Inductive kick.** Interrupting current through an inductor generates $L\,di/dt$ spikes, so choke circuits need clamping or snubbing ([[q0144]]).
- **Interaction.** A high-Q LC filter on a converter input/output can interact with the converter's negative input resistance or control loop, requiring damping.
:::

- **Buck/boost converter output and input filtering.**
- **EMI filters** on AC/DC supplies (differential and common-mode chokes).
- **RF chokes and bias tees** for amplifiers.
- **Ferrite beads** on cables and rails to attenuate high-frequency noise.
- **Audio speaker crossover networks** (series L for woofers: low-pass).
- **Motor drive output (dv/dt) filters** and sine filters.

::: assumptions
- Ideal inductor (no DCR, no winding capacitance) and linear (no saturation); real parts as discussed.
- First-order and second-order formulas assume a resistive load $R$ and an ideal source.
- Series inductor "low-pass" assumes a load that is not an open circuit; with no load the output equals the input at every frequency except at resonance with any parasitic capacitance.
- Capacitor ESR/ESL limit the LC filter's high-frequency performance.
- A duality statement ("capacitor passes AC, blocks DC; inductor passes DC, blocks high-frequency AC") is the standard quick answer; the topology-dependent nuance is the follow-up.
:::

::: followups
### What's the corner frequency of a 10 mH inductor in series with a 1 kΩ load?
$f_c=R/(2\pi L)=1000/(2\pi\cdot0.01)=15.9$ kHz. Above that the output falls at 20 dB/decade.

### Why would you choose an LC filter rather than an RC filter for a power rail?
An RC filter has a resistor in series with the load current, so it wastes $I^2R$ power and drops voltage; an inductor passes DC with only its small DCR. For the same attenuation an LC achieves 40 dB/decade, and it dissipates almost nothing. The cost is size and the possibility of resonant peaking.

### What if the LC filter on a converter output has a very high Q?
It peaks near $f_0$ (a 21× gain at resonance in the lightly loaded example) and can ring on load steps or interact with the loop. Damping: a series R with the capacitor (or an electrolytic's ESR), a parallel RC damping branch (a blocking capacitor several times larger than $C$ in series with a resistor of the order of $\sqrt{L/C}$), or a lossy inductor/ferrite.

### A 10 µH inductor must filter 100 MHz noise. What would you check?
The impedance at 100 MHz from the vendor curve, not the nominal L. An ideal 10 µH is 6.3 kΩ there, but a real one with an SRF of 20 MHz is capacitive at 100 MHz, perhaps a few hundred ohms. A smaller inductor or a ferrite bead rated for 100 MHz is better ([[q0045]]).

### How do you block a high-frequency signal on a DC bias line without affecting the DC?
Place an RF choke in series with the bias path (its impedance at the RF frequency is ≫ the source's), plus a bypass capacitor to ground. Choose the choke so its SRF is above the RF frequency, and the DC current rating and DCR keep the bias voltage drop acceptable. This is the bias-tee idea.
