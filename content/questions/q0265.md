::: basics
### In plain words
A buck converter can fail in a handful of predictable ways, and you can sort them by part. The switches can overheat, overlap, or see too much voltage. The inductor can saturate. The input voltage can bounce up (ring) or dip (sag), the output can overshoot, and the capacitors can wear out. Good designs add protections and margins to prevent each one, and ways to detect it.

### Picture it
Think of inspecting a house room by room. In the plumbing you look for leaks, in the wiring for overload, and in the roof for storm damage. Each room has its own typical problem and its own safeguard. A buck is the same: each block has its usual failure and its usual guard. The house picture does not show one buck-specific danger: both switches on at once, which is a short from the input to ground.

### Words you need
- **Shoot-through**: both switches on at the same time, shorting the input to ground.
- **Dead time**: a short gap when both switches are off, to prevent shoot-through.
- **Saturation**: the inductor's core is full, so its current runs away ([[q0259]]).
- **Current limit**: a protection that turns the switch off when the current gets too high.
- **UVLO / OVP**: under-voltage lockout and over-voltage protection.

### The simplest example
Say a 12 V input drives two switches, each with 10 mΩ of resistance. If both turn on together, the input sees about 20 mΩ. In principle that is $12/0.02=600$ A, limited in practice by the capacitor and the wiring, but it can still destroy the part in moments. The fix is dead time: turn one off, wait a few nanoseconds, then turn the other on.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page goes through each failure by block, how to detect it on the bench, and how to prevent it.
:::

::: testing
Whether you think like a reliability engineer: for each part of the buck (switches, inductor, capacitors, control, input source, load) you can name how it fails, how that shows up on the bench or in the IC's protection, and what design choice prevents it. The interviewer is also checking that you know the **protection features** a buck IC provides (UVLO, current limit, hiccup, thermal shutdown, OVP, soft-start) and the failures those *cannot* protect against (a shorted high-side FET puts $V_{IN}$ on the output). Structure and prioritization matter more than a long list.
:::

::: why
Power supplies are the part of a product most likely to fail dramatically, and failures propagate: a buck that dies often takes the load with it. Real design work is mostly anticipating what the world will do to the converter (hot-plug spikes, shorts, cold starts, dropouts, saturation) and making sure it fails safe. Validation plans ([[q0267]]) and debug skills ([[q0266]]) are both built from this list.
:::

::: concepts
- **Shoot-through**: both FETs on at once, a direct $V_{IN}$ to ground path ([[q0207]]).
- **Inductor saturation and current runaway** ([[q0259]]).
- **Over-current and short-circuit protection**: cycle-by-cycle limit, foldback, hiccup.
- **Over-voltage**: input surges and ringing, switch-node spikes, output overshoot.
- **Thermal limits**: junction temperature and shutdown.
- **Start-up and UVLO**: soft-start, brown-outs, pre-bias.
- **Loop instability** ([[q0261]]).
- **Component wear-out and mechanical failures**: electrolytic ESR, ceramic cracks, solder fatigue ([[q0268]]).
:::

::: answer
"I'd go through the converter part by part. In the switches: shoot-through if dead time is wrong, switch-node overshoot beyond the FET's voltage rating from loop inductance, and thermal overload. Prevention is built-in dead time or adaptive gate drive, a small hot loop and snubber if needed, and a thermal shutdown. In the inductor: saturation, which makes the current runaway; prevention is choosing a saturation current above the IC's current limit. For overcurrent and shorts, the IC has cycle-by-cycle current limiting, and on a hard short the inductor current can ratchet up, so there's a minimum on-time, frequency foldback and hiccup mode.

On the input side: hot-plugging a supply with ceramic capacitors can ring to twice the input and exceed the IC's rating, so I damp it or add a TVS; and brown-outs are handled by UVLO with hysteresis. On the output: overshoot at start-up or on load release, handled by soft-start and OVP, and the one thing a buck can't protect against is a high-side FET failing short, which puts $V_{IN}$ on the output, so downstream I'd use a crowbar or clamp if that matters.

Then control and passives: loop instability from capacitor derating, and wear-out of electrolytic caps. Detection is a combination of IC flags such as power-good and fault pins, and on the bench, scope the switch node, current and output during corner tests."
:::

::: long
"I like to walk through the converter from input to output, because every block has a failure.

At the input, the classic one is hot-plugging. The supply cable's inductance and the low-ESR ceramics form an LC that rings to nearly twice the supply voltage, which can exceed the IC's absolute maximum. I'd detect it with a scope at the pins during plug-in, and prevent it with a damping electrolytic or polymer, a TVS, or a higher voltage rating. Also brown-outs: UVLO with hysteresis stops the converter from chattering as the input sags.

In the power stage, the first failure is shoot-through, where both FETs conduct. At 18 V and a few tens of milliohms, that's hundreds of amps for a few nanoseconds, so the IC uses dead time or adaptive gate drive, and I check the gate waveforms and switch node. The second is switch-node overshoot: loop inductance and the FET's output capacitance ring on every edge, so I keep the hot loop tiny, add a snubber if needed and use FETs with margin.

For the inductor, saturation makes the current runaway, so I pick a saturation current above the IC's maximum current limit. Related is a short-circuit: with the output at zero, the inductor has almost no volts to discharge it, so current ratchets up each cycle even with a minimum on-time. ICs use frequency foldback, valley current limit and hiccup mode to survive.

On the output, soft-start limits inrush, and OVP covers load release and loop faults. The one thing the IC can't fix is a high-side FET that fails shorted: $V_{IN}$ goes straight to the load, so if it matters I add a crowbar or fuse.

Finally instability and aging: capacitor derating changes the loop, and electrolytics dry out. I'd detect these with load-step and loop measurements across corners."
:::

::: terms
- **Shoot-through**: simultaneous conduction of high-side and low-side FETs.
- **Dead time**: gap between one FET turning off and the other turning on.
- **Hiccup mode**: protection that shuts down on a fault and retries periodically.
- **Frequency foldback**: reducing $f_{SW}$ when $V_{OUT}$ is low so the off-time can discharge the inductor.
- **UVLO**: under-voltage lockout, with hysteresis.
- **OVP / OTP / OCP**: over-voltage, over-temperature, over-current protection.
- **Crowbar**: a clamp or SCR that shorts the rail to ground on overvoltage.
- **Pre-bias**: output already charged at start-up.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "overcurrent, overtemperature and overvoltage".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Organizes by **block** and gives mechanism, detection and prevention for each.
- Explains **short-circuit current runaway** with a number: during a short the per-cycle current gain (e.g. 0.21 A up vs 0.03 A down per cycle for an 80 ns on-time) ratchets the inductor current unless foldback or hiccup intervenes.
- Quantifies **hot-plug overshoot** (ζ ≈ 0.02 gives ~1.9× $V_{IN}$) and the damping fix.
- Notes what protection **cannot** cover (shorted high-side FET) and what the system must add.
- Mentions **negative-current/FPWM boost-back** and **bootstrap refresh** edge cases.
- Connects to validation: corner tests, scope probes, thermal imaging.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking current limit is enough.** Cycle-by-cycle limit doesn't stop runaway if the off-time can't reset the inductor current in a short ([[q0259]]).
- **Ignoring input transients.** Ceramics make hot-plug ringing worse, not better.
- **Assuming the IC protects against a shorted high-side FET.** It can't; $V_{OUT} = V_{IN}$.
- **Checking only DC conditions.** Failures show on edges: switch-node overshoot, dead time, gate coupling.
- **Using a 20 cm ground lead on the scope probe.** It creates fake spikes and hides real ones.
- **Not testing pre-bias and cold start.** A sync buck can sink current from a pre-biased rail and fail the start-up.
:::

::: deep
## Think in terms of stress: voltage, current, temperature, time

Every failure is something exceeding a rating: voltage (junctions and oxides), current (bond wires, saturation), temperature (silicon, solder, magnetics) or accumulated stress over time (aging). Organizing by "which stress does this threaten?" makes the list systematic and shows which ones are fast (nanoseconds) versus slow (years).

## 1. Shoot-through

When the high-side turns on, the switch node rises quickly; $dv/dt$ across the low-side's $C_{gd}$ injects current $C_{gd}\,dv/dt$ into its gate. If the gate-drive impedance is high enough that this lifts the gate above $V_{th}$ the low-side turns on spuriously. Or the controller's dead time is too short and both conduct. With $V_{IN}$ = 18 V and a combined path of 70 mΩ the ideal current is $18/0.07 \approx 257$ A, limited in practice by driver capability and wiring inductance: a brief, very large spike that heats the FETs, sags $V_{IN}$ and loses efficiency.

*Detect:* efficiency lower than expected, unusually hot FETs, a "foot" on the switch-node edge, $V_{GS}$ of the off FET rising above 1 V at the transition. *Prevent:* adequate dead time (a trade-off against body-diode loss), adaptive gate drive, a low-impedance pull-down on the low-side gate, FETs with $Q_{gd}/Q_{gs}$ small ([[q0207]]).

## 2. Switch-node overshoot and ringing

At turn-off of the high-side FET the loop inductance (2 to 5 nH typical for a good layout, more for bad) and the output capacitance of the FETs ring at $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{loop}C_{oss}})$: for 2 nH and 300 pF, about 205 MHz. The peak overshoot is bounded by $I\sqrt{L_{loop}/C_{oss}}$: for 3 A, $L_{loop} = 2$ nH and $C_{oss} = 300$ pF, up to about 7.7 V on top of $V_{IN}$ (an upper bound, real values are lower due to damping and finite edge rate). With $V_{IN} = 18$ V that's 26 V against, say, a 30 V rating: little margin. At 4 A and 5 nH it would be 16 V extra: a 34 V spike that breaks a 30 V FET.

*Detect:* scope the SW node with a short-ground spring probe (with a long ground lead you cannot see it). *Prevent:* minimize hot-loop area (ceramics right at the pins), RC snubber, gate resistor to slow the edge, FETs/ICs with avalanche rating and margin.

## 3. Saturation and short-circuit runaway

Per cycle the inductor gains $\Delta I_{on} = (V_{IN}-V_{OUT})t_{on}/L$ and loses $\Delta I_{off} = V_{OUT}t_{off}/L$. In regulation they cancel. In a hard short $V_{OUT} \approx 0$ so $\Delta I_{off}$ is tiny while the controller can't make $t_{on}$ shorter than the minimum on-time (blanking plus delays):

- $V_{IN} = 18$ V, $t_{on,min} = 80$ ns, $L = 6.8$ µH: $\Delta I_{on} = 0.21$ A per cycle.
- $V_{OUT} = 0.1$ V (short with some resistance), $t_{off} = 2\ \mu s - 0.08\ \mu s$: $\Delta I_{off} = 0.1\times1.92\mu/6.8\mu = 0.028$ A.

Net about +0.18 A each cycle, so the current climbs from 3 A to 6 A in about 16 cycles (33 µs at 500 kHz), well past the current limit's intended setpoint, even though each pulse is "limited". Saturation then makes it worse (slope $V/L$ grows).

## 4. Input overvoltage and hot-plug

An LC step response with a tiny damping factor overshoots by $1 + e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$. For $L_{cable} = 1$ µH, $C_{in} = 20$ µF and $R = 10$ mΩ (ceramic ESR plus cable), $\zeta = \tfrac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L} = 0.022$ and the overshoot is $1.93\times V_{IN}$.

## 5. Output overvoltage

Causes: the high-side FET fails short (output = $V_{IN}$), the feedback divider's top resistor drifts or $R_2$ opens (FB reads low and the loop drives duty to maximum), a load release with a slow loop (inductor energy dumped into $C_{OUT}$: $\Delta V \approx \sqrt{V_{OUT}^2 + L I^2/C} - V_{OUT}$; for $I=3$ A, 6.8 µH, 55 µF: $\sqrt{10.89 + 1.11} - 3.3 = 0.16$ V), or a loop fault.

*Detect:* OVP comparator on FB or $V_{OUT}$, power-good dropout. *Prevent:* OVP that turns off the high side and turns on the low side, careful FB layout, series fuse, crowbar or clamp (TVS or SCR) downstream if the load can't take $V_{IN}$. A buck IC alone **cannot** protect against its own high-side FET shorting.

## 6. Thermal

Junction temperature $T_J = T_A + P\theta_{JA}$. With a $\theta_{JA}$ of 40 °C/W a 0.5 W dissipation adds 20 °C, but in a hot, enclosed system or with poor copper the real $\theta_{JA}$ is much higher. Hitting thermal shutdown (typically ~150 to 170 °C, vendor-dependent) is a protective failure; operating at the limit continuously is a reliability issue ([[q0268]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Thermal shutdown thresholds and hysteresis are product-specific. Use the datasheet values; the 150 to 170 °C range is typical but not universal.

## 7. Start-up, pre-bias and brown-out

Inrush $C\,V/t_{ss}$ must stay below the current limit; a UVLO without hysteresis can chatter as the input sags under load; a **pre-biased** output (output already charged by another rail) can be pulled down by a synchronous low-side FET at start-up, sinking large current. ICs with pre-bias start-up logic keep the low-side off until the soft-start reference crosses the output voltage.

## 8. Loop instability and component aging

If the capacitor's effective value drops (bias, temperature, aging), crossover and phase margin move ([[q0261]]). Symptoms: audible whine, irregular pulse widths, ringing on load steps. Long-term, electrolytic capacitor ESR rises and capacitance falls with heat ([[q0268]]), ceramics can crack from board flex (shorting to ground) and solder joints fatigue with thermal cycling.

## 9. Corner cases: bootstrap and negative current

High-side NFET gate drive relies on a bootstrap capacitor refreshed when the low side is on. At 100% duty (dropout) or very light load in skip mode the capacitor can discharge: with $C_{BST}$ = 100 nF and 50 µA of high-side quiescent current, a 0.5 V droop takes $100\text{ nF}\times0.5/50\mu\text{A} = 1$ ms; ICs insert a refresh pulse every so often. In forced-PWM at light load the inductor current goes negative and pumps energy back into $V_{IN}$; if the source can't sink it (a diode-isolated input, a battery charger), $V_{IN}$ rises ([[q0230]]).

## 10. A systematic detection kit

- **Switch node** (short ground spring): overshoot, ringing, dead time, jitter.
- **Inductor current** (current probe or sense resistor): saturation knee, limit behaviour, DCM/CCM.
- **$V_{IN}$ at the pins** during plug-in and load steps.
- **Thermal imaging** and thermocouples on the IC, inductor and diode.
- **Power-good/fault pins** for logging protection events.
- **Protection tests**: short on output, over-load ramp, input brown-out sweep, hot-plug.

::: followups
### What's the difference between cycle-by-cycle current limit and hiccup mode?
Cycle-by-cycle limit terminates each on-time when the peak current hits the threshold, keeping $V_{OUT}$ regulated as best it can under overload. Hiccup mode counts consecutive limit events, then shuts down for a long period and retries; the average power in a persistent short falls to a few percent. Many ICs use both: limit for transients and hiccup for sustained shorts.

### Why can the inductor current "run away" in a short circuit even with a current limit?
The limit trips on a high-side comparator after a minimum on-time, but the inductor current only falls at $V_{OUT}/L$. If $V_{OUT} \approx 0$ the off-time cannot discharge what the minimum on-time added, so current rises cycle by cycle. A valley current limit (don't start the next pulse until current falls below the limit) or frequency foldback fixes it.

### How do you protect against a shorted high-side FET?
You can't inside the IC, because the switch is the failing part. Use a fuse or hot-swap element upstream, an OVP clamp or crowbar (TVS/SCR) on the output that blows the fuse or clamps below the load's maximum rating, and a downstream part with margin if allowed. Some module designs use a series disconnect FET.

### Scenario: your buck dies only when the board is plugged into a bench supply, never from the battery. Why?
Likely hot-plug ringing: bench supply leads are long (high inductance), ceramics resonate, the input spikes above the IC's abs-max. Battery cables are short with high ESR. Confirm with a scope at the pins; fix by adding bulk damping capacitor or TVS, or soft-start the supply before connecting, or use a higher voltage-rated IC. See [[q0258]].

### What happens if the low-side FET turns on before the high side is fully off, and how would you see it on the bench?
Shoot-through: current spike from $V_{IN}$ to ground through both FETs. On the bench you'd see a notch or flat spot on the switch-node rising edge, input current rising and sagging $V_{IN}$ at the pins, extra heat in the IC, and lower efficiency at high $V_{IN}$ than predicted. A current probe in the input shows narrow spikes at every edge.
:::

::: interpretations
"Failure modes" could mean catastrophic hardware failures, protection-triggering conditions, or performance failures such as instability. I covered all three, organized by block, because a good answer addresses how they differ: some are destructive (shoot-through, overvoltage), some are protected (overcurrent), and some are gradual (aging).
:::
