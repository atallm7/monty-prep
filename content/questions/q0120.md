::: basics
### In plain words
BJTs are popular for amplifiers because they turn a small change in input voltage into a large change in output current. That ratio is called transconductance. At the same current, a BJT has several times more of it than a MOSFET. That means more gain, and often less noise, per milliamp.

### Picture it
Think of two gas pedals. One is very sensitive: a light touch changes the speed a lot. The other needs a big push for the same change. The BJT is the sensitive pedal. The catch is that the sensitive pedal is a bit jumpy and nonlinear, and the BJT also draws a little current at its input.

### Words you need
- **Transconductance ($g_m$)**: change in output current per volt of input change.
- **BJT $g_m$**: $I_C/V_T$, where $V_T\approx 26$ mV is the "thermal voltage", a fixed value at room temperature. See [[q0094]].
- **Overdrive ($V_{ov}$)**: how far a MOSFET gate is above threshold.
- **Input-referred noise**: noise expressed as if it were added at the input. It falls as $g_m$ rises.
- **Bias current**: the small DC current drawn at the input of the BJT.

### The simplest example
Run both at 1 mA. The BJT gives $g_m=I_C/V_T=1\text{ mA}/26\text{ mV}\approx39$ mS (millisiemens, meaning milliamps per volt). A MOSFET with half a volt of overdrive gives $g_m=2I_D/V_{ov}=2\text{ mA}/0.5\text{ V}=4$ mS. That is about ten times less. So for gain and low noise at the same current, the BJT wins. For no input current and easy integration, the MOSFET wins.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers why ICs mostly use MOSFETs anyway, and the accuracy and drift trade-offs.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can give an honest, quantitative comparison and **challenge the premise where it's partly wrong**. The interviewer wants to hear:

1. The BJT's genuine amplifier advantages: **higher transconductance per unit current** ($g_m=I_C/V_T$ vs $2I_D/V_{ov}$), higher intrinsic gain, better matching of $V_{BE}$ (low offset), and lower low-frequency (1/f) noise in many processes.
2. The BJT's drawbacks: base current (finite input resistance), exponential nonlinearity, $V_{BE}$ and $\beta$ temperature dependence, and lower integration density in CMOS processes.
3. The context: "BJTs more often" is true for **discrete, precision and low-noise analog**, but in integrated circuits MOSFETs dominate, and weak-inversion MOS closes much of the $g_m/I_D$ gap.
:::

::: why
Choosing a transistor for the front-end of an amplifier (a microphone preamp, a low-noise RF stage, an op-amp input pair) is a real design decision. The answer follows from device physics: exponential vs quadratic current law. If you can state it with numbers, you show you understand both devices rather than repeating a rule of thumb, and you can also say when a MOSFET or JFET is the better choice (high impedance sources, integration, low power).
:::

::: concepts
- **Exponential vs square law**: $I_C=I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}$ vs $I_D=\tfrac12k(V_{GS}-V_t)^2$. See [[q0085]], [[q0096]].
- **Transconductance ($g_m$)**: BJT $I_C/V_T$; MOS $2I_D/V_{ov}$ (strong inversion).
- **Intrinsic gain ($g_mr_o$)**: BJT $V_A/V_T$; MOS $2/(\lambda V_{ov})$.
- **Input resistance**: BJT $r_\pi=\beta/g_m$; MOS gate is insulated.
- **Bias dependence**: $\beta$, $V_{BE}$, $V_t$ variation. See [[q0119]].
- **BJT vs MOSFET comparison in general**: [[q0094]], [[q0093]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd say it's true in a particular context, and the reason is transconductance. A BJT's collector current is exponential in $V_{BE}$, so its transconductance is $g_m=I_C/V_T$, about 39 mS at 1 mA, and it depends only on current and temperature. A MOSFET in strong inversion has $g_m=2I_D/V_{ov}$; at the same 1 mA with a half-volt overdrive that's 4 mS, ten times less. More $g_m$ per milliamp means more gain per stage, more gain-bandwidth per unit power, and lower voltage noise, because the input-referred thermal noise goes as one over $g_m$. The BJT's intrinsic gain, $V_A/V_T$, is also several thousand, versus a few hundred for a MOSFET. And two BJTs' $V_{BE}$ match better than two MOSFETs' thresholds, which gives lower offset in differential pairs.

The downsides are that it draws base current, so the input resistance is finite, which matters with high-impedance sources, its exponential law is more nonlinear, and $V_{BE}$ and beta drift with temperature. MOSFETs have an insulated gate, zero DC input current, better linearity per input volt, and they are what you get for free in a CMOS process.

So in discrete analog and precision or low-noise stages, BJTs are common; in integrated circuits MOSFETs dominate because of integration and power, and designers run them in weak inversion or use BiCMOS or JFET inputs when they need the best of both."
:::

::: technical
**Transconductance.**
- BJT: $g_m=\dfrac{I_C}{V_T}$, $V_T=kT/q=25.85$ mV at 300 K: $g_m=38.7$ mS at 1 mA ($g_m/I_C=38.7$ V$^{-1}$).
- MOSFET (strong inversion): $g_m=\dfrac{2I_D}{V_{ov}}=\sqrt{2kI_D}$: 10 mS at $V_{ov}=0.2$ V, 4 mS at 0.5 V (1 mA); $g_m/I_D=10$ or 4 V$^{-1}$.
- MOSFET (weak inversion): $g_m/I_D\approx\dfrac{1}{nV_T}\approx28$ V$^{-1}$ ($n\approx1.4$), approaching but not matching the BJT.

**Intrinsic gain** ($g_mr_o$, the maximum single-stage voltage gain):
- BJT: $\dfrac{V_A}{V_T}$; with $V_A=100$ V: 3870 (independent of current).
- MOS: $\dfrac{2}{\lambda V_{ov}}$; with $\lambda=0.02$ /V: 500 at $V_{ov}=0.2$ V, 200 at 0.5 V. Longer $L$ gives a lower $\lambda$, so higher gain at the cost of area and speed.

**Input resistance.** BJT: $r_\pi=\beta/g_m=2.6$ kΩ at 1 mA, $\beta=100$ (so base bias current of 10 µA flows into the source). MOSFET: gate current is essentially zero (leakage pA to fA), a clear advantage for high-impedance sensors and sample-and-hold circuits.

**Noise.** Voltage noise referred to the input: BJT (collector shot noise) $e_n\approx\sqrt{4kT/(2g_m)}=0.46$ nV/√Hz at 1 mA, plus base-resistance thermal noise $\sqrt{4kTr_b}$ (for $r_b\sim100\ \Omega$ this is 1.3 nV/√Hz and usually dominates); MOS channel noise $e_n\approx\sqrt{4kT\gamma/g_m}$ with $\gamma\approx2/3$: 1.05 nV/√Hz at 10 mS (4 mS: 1.7 nV/√Hz). Low-frequency 1/f noise is generally lower in bipolar devices because the current doesn't flow along a surface with traps, but it depends on the process. Current noise is the reverse: BJTs have base-current shot noise ($\sqrt{2qI_B}$) while MOSFET gate current noise is negligible.

**Matching and offset.** $V_{BE}$ mismatch of a well-made BJT pair is of order 0.1-1 mV and depends on the log of the saturation-current mismatch: $\Delta V_{BE}=V_T\,\Delta I_S/I_S$ (1% mismatch gives 0.26 mV). MOS threshold mismatch is typically a few mV for small devices, falling as $1/\sqrt{WL}$.

**Linearity.** For an exponential law the second-harmonic distortion for a peak input $v$ is about $v/(4V_T)$: 1 mV gives roughly 1% HD2. For the MOS square law it is about $v/(4V_{ov})$: 1 mV at $V_{ov}=0.5$ V gives about 0.05%. The BJT's high $g_m$ is bought with a narrow linear range, so BJT stages usually use emitter degeneration.

**Temperature.** $V_{BE}\approx-2$ mV/°C; MOS thresholds also drift (about $-1$ to $-4$ mV/°C), plus mobility changes $g_m$ with temperature. BJT $g_m=I_C/V_T$ varies as $1/T$ at fixed current, which is simple to compensate (PTAT biasing).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Noise and matching numbers depend heavily on the device, geometry and process. The values here are illustrative for textbook-grade devices; check datasheets for actual performance. Also, "more often" is a generalization: it holds for discrete and bipolar-process analog, not for CMOS ICs.
:::

::: short
"Mostly because of transconductance. A BJT's $g_m$ is $I_C/V_T$, about 39 mS per milliamp, whereas a MOSFET's is $2I_D/V_{ov}$, around 4 to 10 mS. So a BJT gives more gain per stage, more bandwidth per mA, lower input-referred voltage noise, a higher intrinsic gain ($V_A/V_T$ in the thousands) and better offset matching. The costs are base current, a more nonlinear exponential law and temperature drift. MOSFETs win on input resistance, integration and power, so in CMOS ICs they dominate, and in weak inversion they approach BJT efficiency."
:::

::: long
"The core reason is the shape of the current-voltage law. A BJT's collector current is exponential in $V_{BE}$, so its transconductance is just $I_C$ over the thermal voltage, 25.85 millivolts at room temperature. At 1 mA that's 39 mS, and it doesn't depend on the device geometry. A MOSFET in strong inversion has a quadratic law, so $g_m=2I_D/V_{ov}$. At the same 1 mA and a 0.2 V overdrive that's 10 mS, with 0.5 V overdrive 4 mS: 4 to 10 times lower.

That higher $g_m$ per milliamp shows up everywhere. Voltage gain of a stage is $g_m$ times the load; gain-bandwidth is $g_m$ over the capacitance; and the equivalent input voltage noise goes as one over root $g_m$, so a BJT front-end at 1 mA has under a nanovolt per root hertz of collector noise. The intrinsic gain, $g_mr_o$, is the Early voltage over $V_T$, around 3,000 to 4,000, versus a couple of hundred for a MOSFET. And in differential pairs, $V_{BE}$ matching is good, so offsets are sub-millivolt, which is why precision op-amp input stages and instrumentation amplifiers use bipolar pairs.

Where BJTs lose: they draw base current, so the input resistance is only $\beta/g_m$, a couple of kilohms, which loads high-impedance sources and adds current noise. The exponential law is more nonlinear, about 1% second harmonic for 1 mV in, so we add emitter degeneration. And $V_{BE}$ and beta drift with temperature.

MOSFETs have an insulated gate, so zero input current, better linearity per input volt, and they're what's cheap and dense in CMOS. That's why in integrated circuits MOSFETs dominate; designers bias them in weak inversion to approach BJT efficiency, or use BiCMOS or JFET inputs when they want both. So the 'BJTs for amplifiers' rule is really about discrete and precision analog."
:::

::: terms
- **Transconductance ($g_m$)**: change in output current per change in input voltage, $\partial I/\partial V$.
- **Thermal voltage ($V_T$)**: $kT/q\approx25.85$ mV at 300 K.
- **Early voltage ($V_A$)**: parameter modelling the output-resistance slope of a BJT, $r_o=V_A/I_C$.
- **Intrinsic gain**: the maximum single-device voltage gain $g_mr_o$.
- **Overdrive ($V_{ov}$)**: $V_{GS}-V_t$ for a MOSFET.
- **Weak inversion (subthreshold)**: MOS regime with exponential current law and high $g_m/I_D$.
- **$g_m/I_D$ ratio**: efficiency metric for converting current into transconductance.
- **BiCMOS**: process combining bipolar and CMOS devices.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "BJTs have higher gain." An exceptional answer:

- Derives the difference from the **current laws** and gives $g_m=I_C/V_T$ vs $2I_D/V_{ov}$ with numbers at the same bias current.
- Lists the **secondary advantages**: intrinsic gain, offset matching ($V_{BE}$ vs $V_t$), noise, and states the **disadvantages** (base current, nonlinearity, drift).
- **Qualifies the premise**: true for discrete, precision and low-noise analog; MOSFETs dominate in ICs, and weak inversion plus BiCMOS narrow the gap.
- Mentions application cases: precision op-amp and instrumentation-amp inputs, RF/low-noise preamplifiers, bandgap references, vs. CMOS op-amps, high-impedance sensor interfaces, switched-capacitor circuits.
- Includes linearity vs transconductance as a trade-off, and degeneration as the standard fix.
- Notes JFETs as the low-noise, high-impedance alternative.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying BJTs have "better gain" without stating $g_m$.** The meaningful metric is $g_m$ per unit current, and intrinsic gain.
- **Claiming MOSFETs are always worse.** They win on input resistance, linearity per volt, integration, and low-power weak-inversion design.
- **Forgetting base current** in a BJT front end: it matters with high source impedance (current noise and bias error).
- **Ignoring the premise.** In IC design MOSFETs, not BJTs, are used "more often" for amplifiers.
- **Comparing at different currents.** Fix $I_C=I_D$ and compare $g_m$, or fix $g_m$ and compare power.
- **Ignoring base-resistance noise.** A BJT's low collector shot noise is often swamped by $r_b$ thermal noise unless $r_b$ is small.
:::

## Why higher $g_m$ is good

1. **Voltage gain.** A common-emitter stage gives $A_v=-g_mR_C=-I_CR_C/V_T=-(V_{R_C}/V_T)$: with 5 V across $R_C$, the gain is $-193$ regardless of the current. A MOS stage gives $-g_mR_D=-2V_{R_D}/V_{ov}$: with 5 V across the load and 0.5 V overdrive, $-20$.
2. **Bandwidth.** The unity-gain frequency of the input stage is $g_m/(2\pi C)$; more $g_m$ per mA means more GBW per mA (see [[q0116]]).
3. **Noise.** Input-referred thermal noise voltage goes as $\sqrt{1/g_m}$: BJT collector noise 0.46 nV/√Hz at 1 mA vs 1.05 nV/√Hz for a MOSFET at 10 mS. The BJT's base resistance $r_b$ adds $\sqrt{4kTr_b}$ (1.3 nV/√Hz for 100 Ω), so the advantage shrinks unless $r_b$ is small (large-geometry, low-noise transistors).
4. **Intrinsic gain.** $g_mr_o=V_A/V_T=3870$ vs 200-500: one transistor with an ideal current-source load can provide gains an order of magnitude higher.

## Why BJTs match better

$V_{BE}=V_T\ln(I_C/I_S)$. Two adjacent transistors sharing a die differ only in $I_S$; a 1% mismatch gives $\Delta V_{BE}=V_T\times0.01=0.26$ mV. A MOS pair's mismatch depends on threshold and mobility variations, scaling as $1/\sqrt{WL}$; a small pair might have 3-10 mV mismatch, a large one (area $\sim1000\ \mu\text{m}^2$) a few mV. In the same area, the BJT's offset is usually lower. That's the reason precision op-amps, instrumentation amplifiers and bandgap references use bipolar devices for the input pair or core.

## The price: base current and non-linearity

**Input current.** $I_B=I_C/\beta$: 10 µA at 1 mA and $\beta=100$, which creates a voltage drop across source resistance and adds current noise $\sqrt{2qI_B}$. For a 1 MΩ sensor that is a large error; bias current compensation, super-beta devices or JFET/CMOS inputs are used instead.

**Linearity.** Expanding the exponential: $i_c=I_C[1+v/V_T+\tfrac12(v/V_T)^2+\dots]$, giving a second-harmonic ratio $\approx v/(4V_T)$: 1 mV peak gives 0.97% HD2, 5 mV gives 4.8%. A MOSFET (square law) has HD2 $\approx v/(4V_{ov})$: for $V_{ov}=0.5$ V and 1 mV, 0.05%. So BJTs need emitter degeneration $R_E$ (which divides the signal by $1+g_mR_E$, trading gain for linearity) or feedback; the high intrinsic $g_m$ is spent on loop gain.

**Temperature.** $V_{BE}$ drifts about $-2$ mV/°C and $\beta$ increases with temperature; biasing ([[q0119]]) handles it with degeneration. $g_m=I_C/V_T$ is directly proportional to $1/T$, which can be cancelled by PTAT bias currents.

## Weak inversion and BiCMOS

Below threshold, a MOSFET's drain current is also exponential: $I_D\propto e^{V_{GS}/(nV_T)}$, so $g_m/I_D=1/(nV_T)\approx28$ V$^{-1}$ ($n\approx1.4$), about 70% of the BJT's. The price is that it requires a very large W/L (hundreds to thousands) for useful current and is slow because of the large capacitance. Low-power IC designers (hearing aids, implantables, sensor nodes) live there.

BiCMOS processes add bipolar devices to CMOS for the few places they matter: precision input pairs, low-noise RF, bandgap references, output stages. JFETs offer a third option: very high input resistance with lower noise than CMOS at modest cost, which is why audio and electrometer input stages use them.

## What to choose

| Need | Better choice |
|---|---|
| Lowest voltage noise, low-impedance source (microphone, RF LNA) | BJT (or large JFET/MOS) |
| Lowest offset in a discrete or precision differential pair | matched BJTs |
| High-impedance source (pH electrode, photodiode, piezo) | JFET or MOSFET input |
| Highest single-stage gain with a simple load | BJT |
| Lowest standby/bias current, CMOS integration | MOSFET (weak inversion for low power) |
| Large signals, linearity per volt, analog switches | MOSFET |
| Highest speed (gain-bandwidth per amp) | BJT/HBT, or MOS with very high current density |

## Real hardware

- A classic precision op-amp (bipolar input) has an offset of tens of µV with matched pairs, input bias of tens of nA; a CMOS op-amp may have mV offsets (unless trimmed or auto-zeroed) but pA bias currents.
- Audio preamplifiers and RF low-noise amplifiers (SiGe HBTs) are bipolar at heart; their MOS counterparts run at much higher current for similar noise.
- Bandgap voltage references exploit $V_{BE}$'s predictable temperature behaviour; CMOS processes provide parasitic substrate BJTs for exactly this.
:::

::: applications
- **Precision op-amp and instrumentation-amplifier inputs**: matched bipolar pairs for low offset and noise ([[q0115]]).
- **Low-noise audio and RF preamplifiers**: bipolar (or SiGe HBT) front ends.
- **Bandgap references and temperature sensors** using $V_{BE}$ and $\Delta V_{BE}$.
- **CMOS op-amps and ADCs**: MOSFET inputs for integration and zero input current.
- **High-impedance sensor interfaces** (electrometers, photodiode TIAs): JFET or CMOS inputs.
- **Switched-capacitor circuits** where the MOSFET's insulated gate and switch behaviour are essential.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Comparison at equal bias current** with textbook device parameters ($V_A=100$ V, $\lambda=0.02$ V$^{-1}$, $\beta=100$).
- **Long-channel strong-inversion MOS** model; short-channel devices have lower $g_m/I_D$ and gain, and weak inversion changes it again.
- **Noise numbers** are for thermal/shot noise only; flicker noise and base resistance depend on process, geometry and bias.
- **"More often"** reflects discrete/precision analog practice; in integrated CMOS systems the statement is reversed.
- The conclusion changes at **very high frequency** (HBTs and advanced MOS can reach similar $f_T$) and at **low power** (weak-inversion MOS).
:::

::: followups
### Compare $g_m$ of a BJT and a MOSFET at 1 mA with $V_{ov}=0.25$ V.
BJT: $I_C/V_T=1\text{ mA}/25.85\text{ mV}=38.7$ mS. MOS: $2I_D/V_{ov}=2\text{ mA}/0.25\text{ V}=8$ mS. The BJT has about 4.8x more $g_m$ at the same current.

### What's the voltage gain limit of a common-emitter stage with an ideal current-source load?
$A_v=-g_mr_o=-V_A/V_T=-100/0.02585=-3870$, independent of bias current. A MOS stage with the same ideal load gives $-2/(\lambda V_{ov})$, about $-500$ at $V_{ov}=0.2$ V and $\lambda=0.02$/V.

### Why does a high-impedance source favour a MOSFET or JFET input?
A BJT draws base current $I_B=I_C/\beta$; through a 1 MΩ source resistance 10 µA gives 10 V of error, and the base current shot noise $\sqrt{2qI_B}$ times the source resistance adds noise. A MOSFET gate draws essentially zero current, so its noise and error are determined by leakage (fA to pA) and thermal noise of the source.

### How does degeneration trade gain for linearity?
An emitter resistor $R_E$ reduces the gain to $-R_C/(R_E+r_e)$ and divides the input-referred nonlinearity by $(1+g_mR_E)$ in the same ratio. With $g_mR_E=38.7$ (1 kΩ at 1 mA) the distortion falls about 40x; the stage becomes a resistor-defined gain of about $-5$ with the BJT providing the loop gain.

### Harder: could a MOSFET front-end match a BJT's collector-noise floor?
Yes, at a price. MOS channel noise is $e_n^2=4kT\gamma/g_m$ with $\gamma\approx2/3$. To reach $e_n=0.46$ nV/√Hz you need $g_m=4kT\gamma/e_n^2=(1.66\times10^{-20}\times0.667)/(0.46\times10^{-9})^2\approx52$ mS. In strong inversion that means tens of mA and a very large W/L, or a huge device in weak inversion, with correspondingly large input capacitance. Low-noise IC designs do exactly this with big input transistors.

### Scenario: you're picking a part for a photodiode transimpedance amplifier with 1 MΩ feedback and a 100 pF photodiode. Bipolar or CMOS/JFET input op-amp?
A CMOS or JFET input. The 1 MΩ feedback resistor means bias current in nA would produce millivolts of output error, and bipolar bias current noise multiplied by 1 MΩ is large. The input-capacitance and noise-gain stability (a feedback capacitor, adequate GBW) also matter; the op-amp's voltage noise is less critical than its current noise here.
