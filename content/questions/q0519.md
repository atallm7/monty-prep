::: testing
Whether you know the **short list of rules that actually matter for a differential pair** and can say what is *different* from a single-ended route: the two traces are routed as one object (constant spacing, same layer, same length, same vias, symmetric bends), the length matching is done *within* the pair and locally, and impedance is set by width *and* spacing. Candidates who connect each rule to a failure mode (mode conversion, reflections, crosstalk, EMI) stand out.
:::

::: why
Differential interfaces are where layout skill shows up. A PCIe or USB 3 lane with a sloppy breakout can fail compliance even if the schematic is perfect. Reviewers look at symmetry, skew, via transitions and spacing, and a candidate who can list the rules and the reasoning has clearly done it before. This question follows [[q0518]] (what a pair is) and leads to the skew question [[q0520]] and the meander question [[q0521]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Coupled-line impedance**: $Z_{diff}=2Z_{odd}$ depends on width $w$, spacing $s$, height $h$, $\varepsilon_r$. See [[q0524]].
- **Intra-pair skew**: timing difference between the two traces; the key matching requirement. See [[q0520]].
- **Mode conversion**: asymmetry turns differential signal into common-mode.
- **Return path**: the pair needs a continuous reference plane; at layer changes, ground vias carry the return. See [[q0515]].
- **Crosstalk and spacing to other nets**: a pair-to-aggressor spacing greater than the intra-pair spacing. See [[q0694]].
- **Termination and AC coupling**: differential termination and series capacitors must be placed symmetrically. See [[q0529]].
:::

::: answer
"The main idea is that I route the two traces as a single object. Same layer, parallel the whole way, constant spacing, same length, same number of vias, same neighbours. Anything that makes one trace see a different environment than the other turns differential signal into common-mode.

In detail: I set the width and spacing from the stackup so the differential impedance hits its target, 100 ohms for most standards or 90 for USB, and I keep that spacing constant along the whole run, only opening it up briefly at the pins. I route over a solid reference plane and avoid anything under the pair that could differ between the two traces, like a plane edge or a via field. Bends are symmetric 45 degrees or arcs, and the outer trace of a bend is longer, so I add a little jog on the inner trace right after the bend instead of waiting till the end. At layer changes I use two identical vias, with ground vias next to them for the return current. AC coupling caps get placed symmetric, side by side, at the same place along both traces.

What's different from single-ended is that the matching is within the pair, in picoseconds, and that I care about keeping the pair together; the clearance to other signals should be several times the gap between the two traces, and I don't want stubs, test points, or one side detouring around an obstacle."
:::

::: technical
**Impedance.** For edge-coupled microstrip the IPC-2141 style approximation is $Z_{diff}\approx 2Z_0\left(1-0.48\,e^{-0.96\,s/h}\right)$, so $Z_{diff}$ depends on $s$ as well as $w$. Targets: 100 Ω (Ethernet, PCIe, SATA, HDMI/DP, LVDS), 90 Ω (USB 2.0/3.x), 120 Ω (CAN/RS-485 buses, wire-level). Example: FR-4, $h=0.2$ mm, 1 oz copper: $s=0.2$ mm requires $w\approx 0.24$ mm for 100 Ω; $s=0.1$ mm requires $w\approx 0.17$ mm.

**Routing rules.**
1. *Same layer, parallel, constant gap* along the entire route; relax the gap only in the fan-out region and keep that short.
2. *Intra-pair length match* within a few mils or a few ps, by delay not length; typical budgets are a small fraction of a UI (e.g. 5% of UI or the standard's limit).
3. *Compensate locally*: at each bend or mismatch, add the missing length right there ([[q0521]]).
4. *Symmetric bends* (45° or arc). The outer trace is longer by roughly $s_c\theta$ per bend, where $s_c$ is the centre-to-centre pitch.
5. *Vias in pairs*: identical, adjacent, with matched ground-via placement; minimise count; no unmatched layer changes.
6. *Continuous reference plane* under both traces; don't cross splits; a plane edge should be ≥ a few $h$ away from both traces.
7. *Spacing to other signals*: typically ≥ 2–3× the intra-pair spacing (and ≥ 3 W); more for noisy aggressors (clocks, switching regulators). Exact figures come from the interface's layout guide.
8. *No stubs or asymmetric loads*: test points, ESD parts and AC caps are placed symmetrically in line with the pair; keep stubs out entirely at multi-Gb/s.
9. *Don't route one trace around an obstacle* while the other goes the other way: put both on the same side.
10. *Termination* and AC caps: at the specified end, symmetric, with plane cut-outs under the large pads if the guide says so.
11. *Polarity swap*: some standards (PCIe, USB 3) allow P/N inversion at the receiver; this can ease routing but should be consistent with the standard.
:::

::: short
"I route the two traces as one object: same layer, constant spacing set by the stackup for the target impedance, matched length, identical vias and symmetric bends, over a continuous reference plane. The big differences from single-ended routing are that matching is within the pair and done locally at each bend or via, spacing sets the impedance, and there should be no stubs or one-sided obstacles. Anything asymmetric converts differential signal into common-mode."
:::

::: long
"I think of a pair as a single object, so almost every rule is about keeping the two halves identical.

First, impedance. I get the stackup from the fab and pick width and spacing together for 100 ohms, or 90 for USB. For example on a 0.2 millimetre FR-4 dielectric, 0.24 millimetre traces with 0.2 millimetre gap hit about 100 ohms. Then I keep that gap constant along the entire route, opening it only at the pins and re-closing it as soon as I can.

Second, symmetry and length. Both traces on the same layer, same length, same number of vias, same neighbours. I match in time not millimetres, within a few picoseconds or whatever the standard gives. At a bend, the outer trace is longer, so I add a small jog to the inner trace right after the bend rather than fixing it at the end, because the common-mode signal is created where the skew is.

Third, return path and vias. A continuous plane underneath, no gaps. If the pair has to change layers I use two identical vias side by side, with ground vias next to them so the return current can hop planes. I keep stubs short, with back-drilling if the speed requires.

Fourth, spacing to everything else. The pair gets a keep-out of several times the intra-pair gap, because a nearby aggressor that couples more to one trace than the other creates common-mode noise. Connectors, AC-coupling capacitors, ESD diodes and termination are placed symmetrically, with no test points hanging off.

Compared with single-ended, the key differences are that I'm matching within the pair rather than to a reference, spacing is part of the impedance, and I deliberately keep the two traces coupled so that they carry each other's return current."
:::

::: terms
- **Edge-coupled pair**: two traces side by side on one layer; the usual PCB differential structure.
- **Broadside-coupled pair**: traces on adjacent layers, one above the other (less common).
- **Intra-pair skew**: arrival-time difference between P and N.
- **Inter-pair skew**: difference between pairs (lanes); handled separately by the receiver for serial links.
- **Pitch ($s_c$)**: centre-to-centre distance; equals $w+s$.
- **Breakout**: the region where the pair exits a BGA or connector, usually forced to deviate from the nominal spacing.
- **Polarity swap**: routing P to N at the receiver where the standard allows inversion.
- **Fibre-weave effect**: local dielectric variation from the glass weave that causes skew between the traces.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "keep them close and the same length." An exceptional answer:
- Treats the pair as **one object** and explains each rule via **mode conversion**.
- Gives **numbers**: 100/90 Ω, example $w/s$ for a stackup, 6 ps/mm for converting skew to length, spacing ≥ 2–3× intra-pair gap.
- Says to **compensate where the skew is introduced**, with the outer-trace length ($s_c\theta$) calculation.
- Covers **vias, plane references, AC caps, ESD and connectors**, and symmetric treatment.
- Mentions **fibre-weave skew** and **polarity swap** as advanced practical points and defers to the interface's layout guide for exact limits.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Fixing length at the end of the route.** The skew has already generated common-mode along the way; add compensation locally.
- **Splitting the pair around a via or component.** Each trace then has a different environment and length.
- **Fanning out with wide gaps and never re-coupling.** The impedance rises and crosstalk to neighbours changes; keep the loose section short.
- **Matching length but ignoring vias/layers.** A layer change adds delay and a stub that is different for each trace if the vias aren't identical.
- **Placing AC-coupling caps far apart or on different sides.** Adds skew and a mode-conversion source; put them side by side.
- **Ignoring other nets.** An aggressor close to one trace and far from the other is a common-mode noise source.
- **Treating 100 Ω as 2×50 Ω.** If the pair is coupled, the traces must be narrower than 50 Ω singles.
:::

::: deep
## What's different from a single-ended route

A single-ended line is characterised by $Z_0$ to the plane, and its quality depends on the plane below it. A differential pair has the same dependence on the plane but adds *a second degree of freedom*: the coupling between the traces. That produces three new requirements:

1. **Impedance depends on spacing**, so width and gap must be designed together.
2. **The two halves must be balanced**, since any difference converts differential to common-mode ([[q0520]]).
3. **The two halves share a return path** (the odd-mode current), so keeping them together is more important than keeping them near ground.

## Setting width and spacing

For edge-coupled microstrip, increasing the spacing raises $Z_{diff}$ toward $2Z_0$; to keep 100 Ω with tighter coupling the traces must be narrower:

## Bends and the length you need to add

At a bend of angle $\theta$ the outer trace is longer than the inner by about $\Delta\ell=s_c\,\theta$ where $s_c=w+s$ is the pitch and $\theta$ is in radians. For $w=0.24$ mm, $s=0.2$ mm: $s_c=0.44$ mm; a 90° bend gives $0.44\times\pi/2=0.69$ mm, i.e. 4.1 ps in microstrip. A route with four 90° bends in the same direction accumulates 16 ps (a big chunk of a 10 Gb/s UI of 100 ps). Bends that alternate left and right cancel each other out. Compensation jogs and alternating bends handle this.

## Via transitions and references

If the pair changes layers: use a via pair with the same barrel length for P and N, and put ground vias next to them. If the new layer references a different plane, the ground vias carry the return between planes. Do not leave one trace's via with ground vias and the other with none. Note the compensation for the different path lengths in the via (layer exit points differ if the pair swaps order).

## Spacing to other nets

An aggressor trace at distance $d$ from the pair couples to the two traces in proportion to something like $(h/d)^2$ for each; the near trace gets more noise than the far one. The difference is converted to differential noise, and the common part is rejected. The rule "≥ 2–3 × the pair spacing, and ≥ 3 W" ensures that the difference is small. Interface guides give explicit numbers (e.g. clearance to other signals, to the board edge, to power switches).

## Fibre-weave and material

Within a pair on a coarse glass weave, one trace can lie over a glass bundle ($\varepsilon_r\approx 6$) while its partner lies over resin ($\varepsilon_r\approx 3.5$). The velocity difference leads to periodic skew. Fixes: use spread-glass or flat-weave laminates, route at a small angle (≈10°) relative to the weave, or use loosely coupled pairs and zig-zag routing. It becomes relevant above about 10 Gb/s.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Standard-specific clearance, via and skew limits (for example the allowed intra-pair skew for USB, PCIe or HDMI) differ by standard and revision. The values above are typical practice, not quotes from a specification. Use the interface's layout guide and the SoC vendor's design checklist.

## Worked example: a 90 Ω USB 3.x pair breakout

1. FR-4, $h=0.2$ mm, 1 oz copper; target 90 Ω (USB 3.x). Using the model, $s=0.2$ mm needs $w\approx 0.30$ mm for 90 Ω.
2. The connector pad pitch is 0.5 mm; the pair must neck down to $w=0.2$ mm, $s=0.15$ mm over 1.5 mm, then re-widen. The neck-down section is about 103 Ω instead of 90 Ω; over 1.5 mm (about 9 ps) it is a small, acceptable discontinuity.
3. At the 90° bend the pitch is $0.30+0.20=0.50$ mm: $\Delta\ell=0.5\times\pi/2=0.79$ mm = 4.6 ps. Add a 0.8 mm jog on the inner trace right after the bend.
4. At the 5 Gb/s data rate (UI = 200 ps) the remaining skew is within about 1 ps, good; verify by simulation or a TDR measurement.
:::

::: applications
- **USB 2.0/3.x, PCIe, SATA, HDMI/DisplayPort, Ethernet PHY lines**: pair routing with the rules above and the specific guide of each standard.
- **DDR clock (CK/CK#) and DQS pairs**: matched pairs routed with tighter skew control than data lines.
- **LVDS camera/display and MIPI D-PHY lanes**: pair routing with inter-lane matching in addition to intra-pair.
- **CAN/RS-485 buses**: twisted or closely routed pairs with terminations and common-mode chokes.
- **High-speed ADC clocks and sample lines**: symmetric routing where jitter and balance matter.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Edge-coupled microstrip on FR-4** is assumed; stripline pairs have similar rules but different widths (e.g. $w\approx 0.11$ mm, $s=0.2$ mm for 100 Ω with 0.2 mm dielectric above and below in the model) and no fringing to air.
- Impedance values come from an IPC-2141-style approximation and should be checked with the fab's solver; ±10% is the typical manufacturing tolerance.
- Spacing multiples (2–3× pair gap) and skew limits vary by standard and speed; defer to the interface's layout guide.
- Slow differential links (RS-485 at 100 kbaud) do not need controlled impedance or tight matching: a twisted pair or simple parallel routing is sufficient.
:::

::: followups
### Should differential pairs be tightly or loosely coupled?
Tight coupling gives better noise rejection and less sensitivity to plane disturbances, and it allows denser routing, but needs narrower traces (more loss) and tighter fab control. Loose coupling uses wider traces and is easier to build, but each trace behaves more like a single-ended line and is more sensitive to the plane and to noise. A moderate choice, spacing of about one to two trace widths, is common; the standard's guide often decides.

### Where do you do the length matching?
Locally, at the place where the mismatch is created, e.g. immediately after a bend or at the breakout, not at the end. A skewed section carries common-mode noise over its whole length. Compensating at the end leaves the common-mode content and may create a new asymmetric structure.

### The pair must pass between two BGA balls. What do you do?
Neck down to a narrower width and perhaps a smaller gap for the shortest possible distance, keep it symmetric, and re-establish the nominal geometry immediately. The short neck is a small discontinuity; the model for the stackup should include it, and for multi-gigabit rates I'd simulate the breakout.

### Why shouldn't I put a test point on one trace of a pair?
It adds a stub and capacitance to only one line, so the pair becomes unbalanced: reflection plus mode conversion. If a probe point is really needed, add symmetric footprints on both lines, or use a probe at the termination or a connector.

### What if the two traces must be on different layers?
That's a broadside-coupled pair or a swap in layer; the traces then see different dielectrics, speeds and vias, so lengths must be matched in time and the via transitions must be replicated. It's best avoided; if forced, keep it short and simulate.
:::
