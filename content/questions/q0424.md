::: testing
Whether you understand a motor as an electromechanical system with a **back-EMF source**, not as a resistor. Braking questions test energy flow: where does the kinetic energy go, how big is the current, and what does the hardware and the motor feel? The hidden sub-skills:

1. Short-circuit (dynamic) braking: terminals tied together, current $=E/R$, torque $\propto\omega$.
2. Reverse-voltage braking (plugging): current $=(V+E)/R$, up to twice the stall current, much faster.
3. Which topology can do which: the half-bridge can only short, the H-bridge can short or plug (and regenerate).
4. The stresses: peak current, winding heating, mechanical shock, brush arcing, supply pumping.
:::

::: why
Anything that moves and has to stop on command needs a braking strategy: robot wheels, gimbals, power tools (the saw blade must stop), disk drives, elevator doors. Choosing between coast, short brake and plugging is a classic trade-off between stopping distance, motor and driver stress, and energy flow. Interview answers that are numeric ("12 A versus 24 A, 115 ms versus 35 ms") stand out from "the H-bridge brakes faster".
:::

::: concepts
- **Back-EMF**: $E=K_e\omega$ opposes the drive and reverses roles when braking ([[q0334]], [[q0346]]).
- **Motor as generator**: with the terminals shorted, the rotor drives current through its own winding.
- **Torque constant**: $T=K_t i$, so braking torque tracks current ([[q0345]]).
- **Mechanical time constant**: $\tau_m=\dfrac{J R_a}{K_e K_t}$ sets how fast speed decays.
- **Freewheel and flyback paths**: the bridge must carry the reverse current ([[q0144]], [[q0422]]).
- **Energy accounting**: $\tfrac12J\omega^2$ goes into winding heat (and, if plugging, supply energy too).
:::

::: answer
"There are three ways to stop a motor. Coast: switch everything off and let friction slow it. Short-circuit brake: tie both motor terminals together, so the back-EMF drives current through the winding resistance and the rotor decelerates. And reverse-voltage braking, or plugging: apply the opposite polarity while it's still spinning.

A half-bridge, with the motor returned to ground, can do the short brake: turn on the low-side FET and both terminals sit at ground. It can't plug, because it can't apply reverse voltage. An H-bridge can short brake by turning on both low sides, and can also plug by reversing the legs, so it can stop faster.

Numbers make it concrete. Take a 12 V motor with 1 ohm winding resistance spinning at no-load speed, so the back-EMF is about 12 V. Shorting it gives about 12 A and the current decays with speed. Plugging gives 12 plus 12 over 1 ohm, so 24 A, twice the stall current, and roughly three times as much torque early on. With my example parameters the plug stop takes about 35 milliseconds while the short brake takes about 115 ms to get down to ten percent. The cost is stress: higher peak current, more winding heating, a mechanical jolt, brush arcing, and energy pumped from the supply, and if I don't cut the drive at zero speed the motor just reverses."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** Brushed DC motor with $R_a$, negligible $L_a$ for the mechanical timescale, $K_e=K_t=K$ (SI), rotor inertia $J$, no friction or load torque. Drive $V$ at no-load speed $\omega_0=V/K$ so $E_0=V$.

**Short-circuit brake** (terminals tied, $v=0$): $i=-K\omega/R_a$ (sign opposes rotation), and
$$J\dot\omega=-\frac{K^2}{R_a}\omega\;\Rightarrow\;\omega(t)=\omega_0e^{-t/\tau_m},\quad \tau_m=\frac{JR_a}{K^2}.$$
Peak current $K\omega_0/R_a=V/R_a$ (equal to the stall current at the original drive voltage). Never reaches zero exactly (friction finishes the job). Total angle turned $=\omega_0\tau_m$. All the kinetic energy $\tfrac12J\omega_0^2$ becomes heat in $R_a$.

**Plugging** (apply $-V$): $J\dot\omega=-\dfrac{K}{R_a}(V+K\omega)$, so
$$\omega(t)=\omega_0\bigl(2e^{-t/\tau_m}-1\bigr),$$
zero at $t_0=\tau_m\ln2$. Initial current $(V+K\omega_0)/R_a=2V/R_a$. If the drive is not removed at $t_0$, the motor accelerates in reverse toward $-\omega_0$. Heat in $R_a$ until stop is $3\times$ the kinetic energy for $E_0=V$; the supply supplies the extra $2\times$.

**Example.** $V=12$ V, $R_a=1$ Ω, $K=0.02$ N·m/A, $J=2\times10^{-5}$ kg·m²: $\omega_0=600$ rad/s ($\approx5730$ rpm), $\tau_m=0.05$ s, kinetic energy $3.6$ J.

| | Short brake | Plug (until $\omega=0$) |
|---|---|---|
| Peak current | 12 A | 24 A |
| Peak torque | 0.24 N·m | 0.48 N·m |
| Time to stop | 115 ms to 10%, never exactly 0 | 34.7 ms |
| Winding energy | 3.6 J | 10.8 J |
| Supply energy | 0 | 7.2 J |
| Angle turned | 30 rad (4.8 rev) | 9.2 rad (1.5 rev) |

**Half-bridge with a split-rail return** can plug at half voltage: $V'=6$ V gives $\omega=-\omega_{ss}+(\omega_0+\omega_{ss})e^{-t/\tau_m}$ with $\omega_{ss}=300$ rad/s, zero at $\tau_m\ln3=54.9$ ms and 18 A peak.

**Regenerative braking** with a bridge: turn on the low side to build current in $L_a$ from $E$, then release to push it through the high-side diode into $V_M$: a boost converter, so the motor brakes while returning energy to the battery (if it accepts charge) with a controlled current limit.
:::

An H-bridge can do that with both low sides, or can plug by applying reverse voltage, which makes current $(V+E)/R$, about twice as much, and stops several times faster. The stress is peak current, winding heating, a torque jolt on the gears and brushes, and with plugging you must cut the drive at zero speed or it reverses."

::: long
"A motor spinning is also a generator. So braking is really about giving that generated voltage somewhere to push current. I see three options.

First, coast. Everything off, the motor slows down due to friction and the current flows through the body diodes at most. It's gentle but slow.

Second, short-circuit or dynamic braking: tie the two motor terminals together. In a half-bridge with the motor returned to ground, that's just turning on the low-side FET. In an H-bridge it's both low sides, or both high sides. The back-EMF E pushes current E over R through the winding, and the torque is proportional to that current, so the braking torque is highest at high speed and fades as the motor slows. The speed decays exponentially with the mechanical time constant J times R over K squared.

Third, plugging, which needs an H-bridge. I reverse the drive polarity while the motor is still spinning, so the applied voltage and the back-EMF add and the current is V plus E over R. At full speed that's twice the stall current. The torque is bigger the whole time and the motor reaches zero in about 0.69 time constants, whereas the short brake takes more than two time constants just to get to ten percent.

With a 12 V, 1 ohm motor at no-load speed, shorting gives 12 A and about 115 ms to 10 percent speed; plugging gives 24 A and stops in about 35 ms. So the H-bridge brakes faster.

The stress is the flip side. Plugging doubles the peak current, triples the heating in the winding, and the torque shock is bigger on gears and couplings. Brushes arc more at high current, magnets can be demagnetized in extreme cases, and the supply sees 24 amps at the start. And if I don't turn off the drive at zero speed, it just spins up backwards. So I'd use current limiting and a speed or back-EMF sense to cut the drive at zero."
:::

::: terms
- **Coast (freewheel)**: all switches off; slow decay by friction, current only through body diodes.
- **Dynamic / short-circuit brake**: motor terminals tied together; braking current $=E/R$.
- **Plugging (reverse-voltage brake)**: reversing the applied voltage while spinning; current $=(V+E)/R$.
- **Regenerative brake**: using the bridge as a boost stage to return braking energy to the supply.
- **Back-EMF ($E=K_e\omega$)**: generated voltage that drives the braking current.
- **Mechanical time constant ($\tau_m$)**: $JR_a/(K_eK_t)$, how fast the speed responds.
- **Stall current**: $V/R_a$ at zero speed; plugging starts at twice this.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "short the motor to brake; reverse it to brake faster".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames braking as **energy flow** from $\tfrac12J\omega^2$ into $R_a$ (and the supply when plugging).
- Gives the formulas and a numeric example: 12 A vs 24 A, exponential decay vs a 0.69$\tau_m$ stop.
- States which topology can do which: half-bridge short-brake only (or half-voltage plug with a split rail), H-bridge both.
- Lists the stresses: peak current vs. FET rating, winding $I^2R$ heating, torque jolt, brush arcing, supply pumping, bus overvoltage.
- Warns that plugging needs a zero-speed cutoff, and mentions regenerative braking and current limiting as the engineered answer.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Braking is the same as turning the motor off."** Off is coast; braking actively removes energy.
- **"Shorting stops the motor completely."** Torque is proportional to speed, so it fades out. Friction and cogging finish the job; the rotor can drift.
- **Treating current as $V/R$ during plugging.** It is $(V+E)/R$, which at full speed is twice the stall current.
- **Forgetting the zero-speed cutoff** after plugging; the motor reverses.
- **Ignoring where the energy goes.** In a plug, the supply and the rotor both pour energy into the winding; in regeneration the bus must be able to absorb it.
:::

::: deep
## Where does the kinetic energy go?

A rotor spinning at $\omega$ stores $\tfrac12J\omega^2$. To stop it you must take that energy out, and the options differ by where it goes:

1. **Friction and windage** (coast): slow and unpredictable.
2. **The winding and brushes** (short brake, plug): converted to heat in $R_a$.
3. **Back into the supply or a brake resistor** (regenerative): recovered or dumped elsewhere.

## Short brake: a decaying exponential

Take $L_a\approx0$ (electrical time constant of 1 ms is much shorter than $\tau_m=50$ ms here). Then
$$i=\frac{-K\omega}{R_a},\qquad J\dot\omega=-Ki=-\frac{K^2}{R_a}\omega.$$
The braking torque $K i$ is **proportional to speed**: strong early, weak as the rotor slows, hence the exponential.

## Plugging: applying the opposite voltage

Reversing the legs applies $-V$. The winding sees $v=-V$ and the back-EMF $+K\omega$ (opposite polarity), so
$$i=\frac{-V-K\omega}{R_a}.$$
Torque is constant-ish at the start ($2V/R_a$) and reduces linearly to $V/R_a$ at zero speed. The speed is
$$\omega(t)=\omega_0(2e^{-t/\tau_m}-1),$$
which crosses zero at $t=\tau_m\ln2\approx0.69\,\tau_m$.

## Energy bookkeeping

For the example: $E_k=\tfrac12\times2\times10^{-5}\times600^2=3.6$ J.

- **Short brake**: 3.6 J into the winding and FETs. Average power over the first $\tau_m$ is large, but spread over 115 ms.
- **Plug**: $\int i^2R\,dt=\dfrac{4V^2}{R}\cdot\dfrac{\tau_m}{2}(1-e^{-2\ln2})=10.8$ J. The supply provides $V\int i\,dt=7.2$ J, which is the extra, and the winding dissipates $3\times$ the stored kinetic energy. The *peak* winding power is $24^2\times1=576$ W for an instant, which only a brief event can tolerate.

## How it stresses the motor and the bridge

| Stress | Short brake | Plug |
|---|---|---|
| Peak current | $V/R$ (12 A) | $2V/R$ (24 A), twice stall |
| Peak winding power | 144 W | 576 W |
| Torque shock | 0.24 N·m | 0.48 N·m, a jolt to gears and couplings |
| Commutator and brushes | Arcing at 12 A | Heavier arcing at 24 A |
| Magnets | Armature field opposes magnet flux | Higher risk of demagnetization at the peak |
| Supply and bridge | FET current 12 A, no supply current | 24 A from the supply, bridge FETs rated for 24 A, bus droop |
| Control | Self-limiting (decays) | Needs a zero-speed cutoff or it reverses |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Demagnetization limits depend on the magnet material (ferrite vs NdFeB), temperature and motor construction; check the motor datasheet's peak-current or demagnetization rating rather than relying on the stall current alone.

The motor's **peak current rating** (often given as a stall or "peak" value for a short time) and the **bridge's pulse rating** are the limits. A real driver adds **current limiting** in the bridge (a sense resistor and comparator) so that the braking current is capped at a safe level, say 15 A: the torque then becomes roughly constant, and the stopping time is $J\omega_0/(K i_{max})=2\times10^{-5}\times600/(0.02\times15)=40$ ms, only a bit slower than the uncontrolled plug but with about 40% less peak current.

## Regenerative braking: controlled current, energy returned

With the low side on the motor current builds from $E$ (the motor as a generator). Releasing the low side forces the current through the high-side diode (or synchronous FET) into $V_M$. That is a **boost converter** with the motor as the source, so $V_M$ rises relative to $E$ and the energy goes into the supply or battery. This brakes with a controlled current (set by duty) and returns energy, so it is gentler and efficient, but requires an H-bridge, a bus that can absorb the energy and, at low speed where $E<V_M$ is too small, falls back on short braking.

## Practical advice

- Choose coast for gentle stops, short brake for a quick stop that is gentle on the hardware, plugging only when stopping time matters, and always add a zero-speed cutoff.
- Short brake is "free": no supply energy and low stress. But a brake held on also acts as a *holding* torque only while moving; at standstill there is no braking torque, so a load can back-drive the motor.
- Check the **bridge**: during a short brake through both low sides, the current flows through the low FETs and the ground path, so the FET current rating, not just the supply current, sets the limit.
:::

::: applications
- **Power tools**: short brake to stop a saw blade or drill chuck quickly when the trigger is released.
- **Robot drive wheels**: plugging or controlled regeneration to stop a mobile robot, with current limit.
- **Elevator/door and conveyor drives**: dynamic braking with a brake resistor.
- **Electric bikes and vehicles**: regenerative braking into the battery.
- **Camera gimbals and printers**: short brake to hold or stop precisely.
- **Disk drives and spindle motors**: reverse-voltage braking to park the heads quickly (historical example).
:::

::: assumptions
- Brushed DC motor, $K_e=K_t$ in SI units, constant inertia, no friction or load torque.
- Electrical time constant $L_a/R_a$ much smaller than $\tau_m$ (here 1 ms vs 50 ms). If not, the current builds more slowly and the peaks are lower.
- Ideal bridge: $R_{DS(on)}$, diode drops and dead time ignored. Including 2 FETs of 20 mΩ lowers the peak by about 4%.
- Speed starts at the no-load speed for the drive voltage ($E_0=V$). At lower speed all the currents scale down.
- Supply can source plug current and accept regeneration as needed.

For a loaded motor with friction and load torque, braking torque is aided by friction, so stopping times are shorter than computed. For a **half-bridge with the motor to $V_M$** (high-side load), short brake uses the high-side FET instead.
:::

::: followups
### Can a half-bridge brake faster than coasting?
Yes, if the motor's return makes shorting possible: turn on the FET that ties the driven terminal to the return rail. The shorted motor brakes with torque proportional to speed. It can't plug, so it can't beat an H-bridge's plug.

### What's the peak current when plugging a motor at half speed?
$E=6$ V, so $i=(12+6)/1=18$ A, versus 6 A for a short brake. Braking current scales with speed in both cases, but plugging always includes the full $V$.

### How would you avoid reversing the motor at zero speed?
Sense speed (encoder, back-EMF during a brief off-window, or a time-out estimate from the mechanical model) and turn the bridge off or to brake when the speed crosses zero. With a current limit you can also limit plugging to a fixed time $\tau_m\ln2$ if you know the speed at the start.

### What if the supply is a bench supply or a diode-protected rail?
Plugging draws a lot of current from the supply, and regenerative braking pushes current into it. A supply with a series diode or a regulator that can't sink current sees its output voltage rise. Add bulk capacitance, a TVS or a brake chopper, or limit the deceleration rate.

### The FETs overheat when braking. What are the possible reasons?
Peak current is above rating (plugging 24 A vs a 12 A rating), or repeated braking cycles heat the FETs ($I^2R_{DS(on)}$ at 12 A with 2 FETs: 5.8 W), or the dead time or shoot-through during reversal adds loss. Fix with current limiting, thermal design and a controlled reversal sequence.
:::

::: interpretations
"Which can brake the motor faster?" can be answered at two levels: the H-bridge brakes faster because it can **plug** (the half-bridge can only short-brake), or "equally fast if both just short the terminals". The page answers the first and notes the second. The phrase "half-bridge" assumes the motor is returned to a fixed rail; with a split rail a half-bridge can also plug at half voltage.
:::
