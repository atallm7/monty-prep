::: testing
Whether you can define a differential pair precisely (two conductors carrying equal and opposite signals, with the information in the **difference**, not in either voltage alone) and explain the mechanism end to end: driver, coupled lines, termination, and a differential receiver that subtracts. The key insight the interviewer wants is *why* subtraction helps: noise that hits both wires equally (common-mode) cancels, while the wanted signal (opposite on each wire) doubles. Strong answers also name the weak points: skew, imbalance, and finite common-mode range. This question is from the signals perspective; board layout rules are covered in [[q0518]].
:::

::: why
Almost every fast or noisy interface uses differential pairs: USB, Ethernet, PCIe, HDMI, SATA, CAN, RS-485, LVDS, DDR clocks, camera and display links, and balanced audio. If you do not understand how the receiver treats the pair, you cannot reason about its noise immunity, its EMI behaviour, why termination and matching matter, or why a "ground offset" that kills a single-ended link barely affects a differential one. It is a gateway question for the rest of the section: [[q0680]] to [[q0684]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Differential voltage** $V_d=V_+-V_-$ carries the information ([[q0684]]).
- **Common-mode voltage** $V_{cm}=(V_++V_-)/2$ carries offset and noise ([[q0683]]).
- **Subtraction (differential receiver)**: a difference amplifier or comparator responds to $V_d$ and rejects $V_{cm}$ (CMRR).
- **Complementary drive**: when one wire goes up, the other goes down by the same amount, so return currents cancel.
- **Coupling**: closely spaced wires pick up the same interference, so noise is mostly common-mode ([[q0682]]).
- **Termination**: a resistor across the pair at the receiver, typically 100 Ω (some standards 90 or 120 Ω), absorbs the signal and prevents reflections ([[q0710]]).
:::

::: answer
"A differential pair is two conductors that carry the same signal with opposite polarity. The driver pushes one wire up while it pulls the other down by the same amount, around some common-mode level. The receiver looks only at the difference between the two wires, V-plus minus V-minus, and ignores what they have in common.

That's what makes it work well. Any noise that couples onto the pair, from a neighbouring trace, a ground shift, or a magnetic field, hits both wires about equally, because they run side by side. So it shows up as common-mode, and the differential receiver subtracts it away. The signal, in contrast, is opposite on the two wires, so the subtraction doubles it.

Other benefits: the two currents are equal and opposite, so the return path is the other wire, which means less loop area, less EMI and less ground bounce. The receiver's threshold is built in, the crossing point between the two wires, so it doesn't depend on a ground reference. In practice it's terminated across the pair, about 100 ohms, and the two wires need to stay matched in length and symmetric, because any skew or imbalance turns common-mode into differential and eats the benefit. The cost is two pins and two traces per signal."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.** For wire voltages $V_+$ and $V_-$ referenced to a common ground:
$$V_d=V_+-V_-,\qquad V_{cm}=\frac{V_++V_-}{2},\qquad V_\pm=V_{cm}\pm\frac{V_d}{2}.$$

**Driver.** Complementary outputs (e.g. LVDS steers a current $\approx3.5$ mA through the termination: 3.5 mA $\times$ 100 Ω = 350 mV). Ideal: $V_{cm}$ constant, $V_d$ = data.

**Line.** Two coupled transmission lines support two modes: **odd mode** (differential, $V_+=-V_-$) with impedance $Z_{odd}$, and **even mode** (common, $V_+=V_-$) with $Z_{even}$. Differential impedance $Z_{diff}=2Z_{odd}$; common-mode impedance $Z_{cm}=Z_{even}/2$. With weak coupling $Z_{odd}\approx Z_0$, so a 50 Ω single-ended trace pair gives about 100 Ω differential; stronger coupling lowers $Z_{odd}$ and therefore $Z_{diff}$.

**Receiver.** Output $=A_dV_d+A_{cm}V_{cm}$. The common-mode rejection ratio $CMRR=|A_d/A_{cm}|$ (often in dB). Residual error referred to the input is $V_{cm}/CMRR$: for 0.5 V of common-mode noise, 40 dB (100) leaves 5 mV, 60 dB (1000) leaves 0.5 mV.

**Why signal doubles.** If each wire swings $\pm V_s$ around $V_{cm}$, then $V_d$ swings $\pm2V_s$. Compared with a single wire swinging $V_s$ against ground, a pair gives +6 dB of signal for the same per-wire amplitude; equivalently, the same differential swing needs half the per-wire swing.

**Termination.** Terminate with $R_T=Z_{diff}$ across the pair at the receiver (a single resistor handles the differential mode; common-mode termination, if needed, uses split resistors with a capacitor to ground).

**Weak points.**
- **Skew** $t_s$ between the legs converts to common-mode: peak $V_{cm}\approx\Delta V_{leg}\,t_s/(2t_r)$. Example: 0.35 V leg swing, $t_r=100$ ps, $t_s=20$ ps gives about 35 mV of common-mode glitch (and a shortened differential edge).
- **Imbalance** (different capacitance or loading on one leg) converts common-mode noise into differential.
- **Input common-mode range**: the receiver stops working if $V_{cm}$ leaves its allowed window (LVDS roughly 0 to 2.4 V; RS-485 $-7$ to $+12$ V).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Standard values (LVDS swing and common-mode, RS-485 range, USB impedance) are quoted from memory of the commonly cited specs. Check the actual standard or the driver's datasheet for exact limits.
:::

::: long
"A differential pair is two conductors that carry one signal as a complementary pair. When one wire goes high, the other goes low by the same amount, around some common-mode voltage. The information is the difference between the two, V-plus minus V-minus, so a receiver with a differential input only looks at that.

Let me describe how it works end to end. The driver produces the two complementary outputs. They travel on two closely coupled traces or a twisted pair. At the far end there's a termination resistor across the pair, usually 100 ohms, to absorb the signal. The receiver is a difference amplifier or comparator, and its output is gain times V-plus minus V-minus, plus a small term for the common-mode.

The reason this is robust is that noise couples on both wires about equally, because they're routed together. So a ground shift, a nearby trace switching, or an interference field shows up as common-mode, and the subtraction cancels it. The receiver's common-mode rejection ratio tells you how well: with 60 dB of CMRR, half a volt of common-mode noise leaves about half a millivolt. The wanted signal is opposite on the two wires, so the subtraction doubles it, which gives about 6 dB better signal-to-noise for the same swing per wire.

There's also a benefit on the transmit side. The currents in the two wires are equal and opposite, so each is the other's return. The loop area is small and the fields cancel, so you get lower emissions and less ground bounce. The threshold is the crossing point of the two lines, so it doesn't depend on a ground reference.

The catches are that the pair has to be matched. If the legs have skew or different loading, some common-mode converts into differential, and the benefit shrinks. And the receiver has a limited common-mode range. I'd also note the cost: two pins and two traces per signal, and routing and termination discipline."
:::

::: terms
- **Differential pair**: two conductors carrying complementary signals, received as a difference.
- **Differential voltage ($V_d$)**: $V_+-V_-$, the wanted signal.
- **Common-mode voltage ($V_{cm}$)**: average of the two wires; offset and noise.
- **CMRR**: ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain, in dB.
- **Differential impedance ($Z_{diff}$)**: impedance seen between the two wires, $2Z_{odd}$.
- **Odd / even mode**: opposite-phase and in-phase propagation modes of a coupled pair.
- **Termination**: resistor matching $Z_{diff}$ at the receiver.
- **Skew**: timing difference between the two legs.
- **Mode conversion**: transformation of common-mode into differential (or vice versa) due to imbalance.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "two wires with opposite signals, the receiver takes the difference, so noise cancels".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Defines $V_d$ and $V_{cm}$ with the equations and states that the receiver output is $A_dV_d+A_{cm}V_{cm}$ with a CMRR.
- Explains *why* noise is common-mode (same field, same coupling, because the wires are close and symmetric) and gives a numerical example of the rejection.
- Mentions the doubling of signal (+6 dB), the built-in threshold, and the cancellation of return currents (EMI, ground bounce).
- Names the failure modes: skew, imbalance, limited input common-mode range, and mode conversion.
- Gives typical practical details: 100 Ω termination, coupled pairs, an example standard (LVDS 350 mV, 1.2 V common-mode).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying "each wire carries half the information".** Each wire carries the complete signal (inverted on the other). The *difference* is the signal.
- **Saying noise is "cancelled" before it reaches the receiver.** It is present on both wires; the *receiver* rejects it, within its CMRR and common-mode range.
- **Forgetting the receiver's common-mode range.** A large ground offset can push both wires outside it.
- **Treating differential as immune to skew.** Skew creates common-mode and eye closure.
- **Thinking you can ignore the return path.** In an ideal balanced pair the other wire is the return, but real pairs also have common-mode currents that need a path.
- **Quoting single-ended impedance for a differential pair.** Use $Z_{diff}=2Z_{odd}$, which depends on spacing.
:::

::: deep
## Information in the difference

A single-ended signal is a voltage on one wire measured against ground. If the ground at the receiver differs from the ground at the driver by 0.5 V, every logic level is shifted by 0.5 V, and a threshold designed for 1.65 V might now see 1.15 V. In a differential link the information is defined purely between the wires:
$$V_d=V_+-V_-.$$
A 0.5 V ground shift moves both $V_+$ and $V_-$ by 0.5 V and leaves $V_d$ unchanged. That is the whole idea. Everything else is making it hold in practice.

## Why subtraction rejects noise

Suppose interference couples a voltage $v_n$ onto both wires, e.g. from a neighbouring trace or a shifting ground. Then
$$V_+'=V_++v_n,\quad V_-'=V_-+v_n\;\Rightarrow\; V_d'=V_d,\quad V_{cm}'=V_{cm}+v_n.$$
The differential voltage is unaffected; only the common-mode moves. A real receiver has finite CMRR, so a residual $v_n/CMRR$ leaks through.

The effect needs the noise to be *equal* on both wires. Anything that couples differently to the two wires (a trace running closer to one leg, a via on one leg only, a different component) converts common-mode to differential. That is why layout rules insist on symmetry ([[q0519]], [[q0520]]).

## Doubling, and the built-in threshold

If each wire swings by $\pm V_s$, the difference swings $\pm2V_s$. With the same per-wire swing, you get 6 dB more signal than a single wire against ground, or you can halve the per-wire swing for the same signal. Low swing means lower power, less EMI and faster edges for a given $C\,dV/dt$.

The decision threshold is $V_d=0$, the point where the two wires cross. A single-ended receiver compares against a fixed voltage ($V_{DD}/2$) which moves with supply and ground noise; a differential receiver compares the signal with its own complement, so it tracks.

## The return path and EMI

In a single-ended link the return current flows in the ground plane under the trace. It has to find a path and any plane discontinuity forces a larger loop. In a differential pair with equal and opposite currents, the two wires carry each other's return. The fields of the two currents cancel at a distance, so radiation falls. At the same time, because the supply current drawn by the driver is nearly constant (one leg turns on as the other turns off, as in LVDS current steering), the supply and ground see little switching current and ground bounce falls ([[q0688]]).

## Imperfections: skew and mode conversion

If the two legs arrive at different times by $t_s$, then during the overlap the legs do not cancel. The resulting common-mode glitch has amplitude about
$$V_{cm,pk}\approx\frac{\Delta V_{leg}\,t_s}{2\,t_r}.$$
For a leg swing of 0.35 V, a 100 ps edge and 20 ps skew, that is 35 mV of common-mode. It also removes eye width, because the differential crossing shifts. Matching to a small fraction of the edge time is the usual layout target ([[q0520]]). Skew also causes common-mode current to radiate, so it hurts EMI as well.

## Receiver limits

The receiver has an input common-mode range. LVDS receivers accept roughly 0 to 2.4 V common-mode; RS-485 is designed for about $-7$ to $+12$ V because the grounds of two nodes in a building can differ by many volts. Outside the range the input stage saturates and the output is unreliable regardless of $V_d$. The receiver also has a differential threshold (often $\pm100$ mV for LVDS, $\pm200$ mV for RS-485), with hysteresis in some designs.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Offer the one-line mental model: "The signal is the difference, the noise is the average." Then give a CMRR example (0.5 V common-mode at 60 dB leaves 0.5 mV), which shows you can quantify the benefit.

## Where the hardware differs

Real pairs also have a common-mode path. The driver's common-mode output, the receiver's common-mode input and any common-mode choke or cable shield define how stray common-mode current flows. Many EMI problems with "differential" links come from common-mode current on the cable, which is why Ethernet and USB connectors include common-mode chokes and shielding.
:::

::: applications
- **USB**: D+/D- pair, 90 Ω differential, receiver sees the difference.
- **Ethernet** over twisted pair with magnetics (transformers) that also block DC and reject common-mode.
- **LVDS** displays and camera links, clock distribution to ADCs and FPGAs.
- **RS-485 / CAN** industrial buses over long cables with large ground offsets.
- **PCIe, SATA, HDMI (TMDS), DisplayPort**: multi-gigabit serial links.
- **Balanced audio (XLR microphones)**: the same idea, with a differential input stage rejecting hum.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear receiver** with finite CMRR and a common-mode range; the numbers assume the signal stays inside that range.
- **Balanced, symmetric pair**: equal coupling to interference and equal loading on each leg. Real imbalance converts some common-mode.
- **Ideal complementary drive** with no skew; real drivers have some.
- **Terminated at the receiver** with $R_T=Z_{diff}$; standards differ (100 Ω for LVDS and Ethernet, about 85 Ω for most PCIe board routing, 90 Ω for USB, 120 Ω for CAN and RS-485 buses).
- Quoted standard numbers are typical; check the standard or datasheet.

An alternative framing from an analog perspective: a differential pair is a "balanced line", which can be driven by a transformer instead of an active driver (Ethernet, audio).
:::

::: followups
### A pair swings between 1.375 V and 1.025 V on opposite legs. What are $V_d$ and $V_{cm}$?
$V_d=1.375-1.025=0.350$ V (and $-0.350$ V when the legs swap). $V_{cm}=(1.375+1.025)/2=1.2$ V. These are typical LVDS numbers.

### A 60 dB CMRR receiver sees 0.8 V of common-mode noise. What is the error at the input?
$0.8\text{ V}/1000=0.8$ mV differential-equivalent. With 40 dB it would be 8 mV, which starts to matter against a 100 mV receiver threshold.

### How does a differential pair keep EMI low?
The two currents are equal and opposite, so their magnetic and electric fields cancel in the far field. The effect is limited by symmetry: any residual common-mode current (from skew or imbalance) on the pair or cable acts like a single-ended antenna and radiates. That's why EMI issues on differential cables usually trace back to common-mode.

### Why is a differential pair terminated with 100 Ω across it rather than 50 Ω to ground on each leg?
For the differential mode, the two 50 Ω-to-ground terminations are equivalent to 100 Ω across the pair, and the single resistor does the same job with one part and no DC path to ground. A single 100 Ω across the pair does *not* terminate the common-mode, so any common-mode wave will reflect; where that matters, a split termination plus capacitor to ground is used.

### What if the two traces differ in length by a half inch?
FR4 delay is about 170 ps/inch, so a 0.5 inch mismatch gives about 85 ps skew. For a 1 Gb/s link (1 ns bit) that is 8.5% of the bit and converts a fraction of the edge to common-mode (using the earlier formula with $t_r=200$ ps, $\Delta V=0.35$ V gives about 75 mV). For a 10 Gb/s link (100 ps bit) it is nearly a whole bit, which is unacceptable. This is why length matching is specified in fractions of a unit interval ([[q0521]]).
:::
