::: basics
### In plain words
Saturation is when an inductor's magnetic core is full and cannot hold any more magnetic flux. Up to that point, the core multiplies the inductance. Past it, the inductance collapses, and the coil behaves like a much smaller inductor. The current then shoots up fast, which can damage other parts in the circuit.

### Picture it
Think of a sponge soaking up water. A dry sponge soaks up a lot, so it does its job well. Once it is completely soaked, more water just runs off. A core is the sponge, and the magnetic flux is the water. Past the point of fullness, extra current gets almost no help from the core. The analogy is loose about one thing: some cores fill up gradually, and others hit their limit suddenly.

### Words you need
- **Magnetic flux**: the amount of magnetic field passing through the coil and core.
- **Core**: the magnetic material, such as ferrite, that the wire wraps around. See [[q0048]].
- **Saturation current ($I_{sat}$)**: the current at which the inductance has dropped by a set amount.
- **Peak current**: the highest current the inductor sees, which is what you must compare to $I_{sat}$.
- **Slope of current**: $di/dt = V/L$, so a smaller $L$ means a faster current rise.

### The simplest example
Apply 10 V across a 10 µH inductor. The current rises at $V/L = 10 / 0.00001 = 1$ A per µs. Now suppose the core saturates and the inductance falls to 2 µH. The same 10 V now makes the current rise at 5 A per µs, five times faster. In a buck converter (a circuit that switches a coil on and off to step a voltage down), the switch would see a sharp current spike. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows what saturation looks like on a scope, what it causes, and how to choose an inductor rated for the peak current.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand saturation as a **core material** phenomenon with a specific, dangerous circuit consequence:

1. Mechanism: the core's flux density $B$ approaches its saturation limit $B_{sat}$; magnetic domains are fully aligned and the core's permeability collapses toward that of air.
2. Consequence: inductance drops sharply, so for the same applied voltage $di/dt = V/L$ shoots up and current spikes.
3. Effects in a real circuit: overcurrent in switches, tripped current limits, extra loss and heat, noise, and control-loop problems.
4. Design: rate inductors by **peak** current against $I_{sat}$ (with margin and at temperature), know hard vs. soft saturation, and know that gapping trades inductance for saturation headroom.
:::

::: why
Saturated inductors are one of the most common ways switching power supplies fail. A buck converter that works on the bench at room temperature and light load can blow its MOSFET at full load on a hot day because the inductor's peak current crept past $I_{sat}$ while $I_{sat}$ itself fell with temperature. The same physics limits transformers (inrush, DC offset), current transformers and common-mode chokes. Interviewers love this question because it tests whether you've actually picked an inductor from a datasheet: inductance alone means nothing without saturation current. It feeds directly into [[q0259]] (why $I_{sat}$ matters in buck design).
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductance from the core**: $L = \mu N^2A_e/\ell_e$; a high-permeability core multiplies $L$ ([[q0039]], [[q0048]]).
- **B–H curve**: flux density $B$ vs. field strength $H \propto NI$; its slope is the permeability, and it flattens at $B_{sat}$.
- **Magnetic domains**: regions of aligned magnetization that rotate into line with the applied field; once all are aligned, the material can't add more flux.
- **Inductor law**: $di/dt = v/L$ ([[q0041]]); if $L$ falls, current slope rises.
- **Peak vs. average current**: in a converter, peak = DC + half the ripple, and that's what saturates the core.
- **Saturation current $I_{sat}$**: vendor-defined current where $L$ has dropped by a stated percentage.
:::

::: answer
"Saturation is when the inductor's magnetic core can't carry any more flux. A core works by having high permeability: the magnetic domains in the material line up with the field and multiply the flux, which is why you get lots of inductance from a few turns. But there's a limit. Once the flux density reaches the material's saturation level, basically all the domains are already aligned, and extra current adds flux only as if the core were air.

So the inductance collapses. A 10 µH inductor might drop to 2 or 3 µH or even lower. And since $di/dt = V/L$, the same voltage now ramps the current much faster. On a scope, you see the inductor current ramp in a buck converter suddenly bend upward into a sharp spike at the end of the on-time.

That causes real problems: the switch sees a big current spike, you can trip the current limit or destroy the MOSFET, losses and heating go up, and since a hotter ferrite saturates at lower current it can run away thermally.

So when I pick an inductor I check its saturation current against the peak current, which is the DC load plus half the ripple, plus margin for transients and temperature. Ferrite saturates hard and abruptly, while powdered-iron cores saturate softly, losing inductance gradually."
:::

::: technical
**Material picture.** In a ferromagnetic or ferrimagnetic core, $B = \mu_0(H + M)$. Magnetization $M$ grows as domains align with $H$, giving a large permeability $\mu = dB/dH$. When $M$ approaches its maximum, $B$ approaches $B_{sat}$ and the incremental permeability falls toward $\mu_0$.

**Inductor picture.** With $\lambda = N\Phi = NBA_e$, the incremental inductance is
$$L_{inc} = \frac{d\lambda}{di} = N A_e\frac{dB}{di}$$
which collapses when $B \to B_{sat}$. Peak flux density in a given inductor: $B_{pk} = \dfrac{L\,I_{pk}}{N A_e}$. Example: 10 µH, 15 turns, $A_e = 10$ mm² at 3 A gives 0.2 T, comfortably below a power ferrite's ≈0.35–0.5 T at room temperature. At 4.5 A it's 0.3 T, getting close at high temperature.

**Circuit consequence.** $\dfrac{di}{dt} = \dfrac{v_L}{L_{inc}(i)}$. With 12 V across a 10 µH part, the slope is 1.2 A/µs; if $L$ falls to 1 µH it's 12 A/µs. Current overshoots, the switch and diode see excess peak current, $I^2R$ and core losses rise, and the radiated/conducted noise of the spike increases. In peak-current-mode control the loop may hit current limit early; in voltage-mode it may not notice until something fails.

**Hard vs. soft.** Ungapped/lightly gapped ferrite: $L$ is flat then drops abruptly. Powdered iron, sendust (e.g. Kool Mµ), MPP and other distributed-gap cores: $L$ rolls off gradually with DC bias ([[q0049]]).

**Datasheet $I_{sat}$.** Defined by the vendor as the DC current where $L$ drops by a stated amount, commonly 10%, 20% or 30% depending on the vendor and product family. Distinct from the thermal rating $I_{rms}$ (e.g. 40 °C temperature rise).

**Temperature.** Ferrite $B_{sat}$ falls with temperature, so $I_{sat}$ at 100 °C can be noticeably lower than at 25 °C. Saturation raises current and losses, raising temperature, lowering $I_{sat}$: a positive feedback loop.

**Design rule.** $I_{pk} = I_{out} + \Delta I_L/2$, then choose $I_{sat} > I_{pk,max}$ including transients, startup, current-limit level and temperature, with margin (often 20–30%). For a 12 V → 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz buck at 2 A, $\Delta I_L = 1.02$ A, $I_{pk} = 2.51$ A. At 4 A, $I_{pk} = 4.51$ A, which saturates a 3 A part.

**Gapping.** Adding an air gap $\ell_g$ raises the reluctance, lowering $\mu_e \approx \mu_r/(1+\mu_r\ell_g/\ell_e)$ and $L$ per turn, but the core reaches $B_{sat}$ at a much higher current, and most energy is stored in the gap.
:::

::: long
"An inductor with a core gets its inductance from the core's high permeability. The material is made of magnetic domains, and when you drive current through the winding, those domains rotate to line up with the field and multiply the flux. That's why a ferrite core can give you thousands of times more inductance than the same winding in air.

Saturation is what happens when you run out of domains to align. The flux density approaches the material's saturation level, $B_{sat}$, around 0.3 to 0.5 tesla for typical power ferrite. Beyond that, extra current only adds flux at the rate air would, so the incremental permeability, and with it the inductance, collapses.

The circuit consequence follows from $di/dt = V/L$. In a buck converter, the inductor sees a fixed voltage during the on-time. Normally the current ramps at a steady slope. If the peak current gets into saturation, L drops and the slope suddenly gets much steeper, so you see the triangle wave bend up into a spike. The switch, the diode or synchronous FET, and the input caps all see that spike. You can trip current limit, kill the FET, or cause a lot of noise.

It also has a nasty thermal side. Saturation increases RMS current and core loss, so the inductor heats up, and ferrite's saturation flux density drops as it gets hotter, so it saturates at even lower current. That can run away.

There's also a material difference. Ferrite saturates hard: inductance is flat and then falls off a cliff. Powdered-iron and similar distributed-gap cores saturate softly: they lose inductance gradually, which is more forgiving.

For design, I'd calculate peak current as load current plus half the ripple, consider transients, startup and the controller's current limit, and pick an inductor whose saturation current is above that with 20 to 30 percent margin. I'd also check how the vendor defines $I_{sat}$, since it might mean a 10, 20 or 30 percent drop in inductance, and look at the inductance-versus-current curve at high temperature."
:::

::: terms
- **Saturation**: the state where a magnetic core's flux density nears $B_{sat}$ and its permeability collapses.
- **$B_{sat}$**: saturation flux density of a core material, in tesla.
- **$I_{sat}$**: the DC current at which a specific inductor's $L$ has fallen by the vendor-defined percentage.
- **$I_{rms}$ (thermal rating)**: the current that causes a specified temperature rise; separate from $I_{sat}$.
- **Incremental inductance**: $d\lambda/di$, the local slope that sets $di/dt$; it's what collapses in saturation.
- **Hard saturation**: abrupt loss of inductance (ferrite).
- **Soft saturation**: gradual roll-off of inductance with bias (powdered iron, MPP, sendust).
- **Air gap**: a deliberate break in the core that lowers $L$ per turn but raises the saturation current.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Comparing $I_{sat}$ to average load current.** The core sees the **peak** current, DC plus half the ripple, plus any transient or current-limit overshoot.
- **Confusing $I_{sat}$ with $I_{rms}$.** $I_{rms}$ is a heating limit; $I_{sat}$ is a magnetic limit. A part can be cool and saturated, or unsaturated and overheating.
- **Assuming the inductance drops to zero.** It drops toward the air-core value, which might be a few percent of nominal. That's still a dramatic increase in di/dt.
- **Thinking saturation is permanent damage.** Saturation itself is reversible: remove the excess current and the inductance returns. The damage is to the *other* parts (the MOSFET) or from overheating.
- **Ignoring temperature.** Ferrite $B_{sat}$ drops noticeably from 25 °C to 100 °C; check the $L$ vs. $I$ curve at the hot temperature.
- **Thinking an air-core inductor saturates.** Air has no domains and doesn't saturate; it just gives little inductance per turn.
:::

::: deep
## The material picture: why a core can run out

A magnetic core multiplies flux. Inside ferromagnetic (iron) or ferrimagnetic (ferrite) materials are **domains**: microscopic regions where atomic magnetic moments are already aligned with each other, but different domains point in different directions so the net field is zero. Apply a field $H$ (from $NI$ in the winding) and the domains aligned with it grow, and others rotate. The material's magnetization $M$ adds to the applied field:

$$B = \mu_0(H + M) = \mu_0\mu_r H\ \ (\text{in the linear region})$$

With $\mu_r$ in the thousands, a small $H$ produces a large $B$. But $M$ has a ceiling: once every domain is aligned, there is nothing left to rotate. $B$ approaches $B_{sat}$ and the slope $dB/dH$ (the incremental permeability) falls toward $\mu_0$.

Use the vendor's material data.

## The inductor picture: L collapses

Flux linkage $\lambda = NBA_e$. The incremental inductance, which is what sets $di/dt$, is

$$L_{inc}(i) = \frac{d\lambda}{di} = NA_e\frac{dB}{dH}\frac{dH}{di} = \frac{\mu_{inc}N^2A_e}{\ell_e}$$

So $L$ tracks the slope of the B–H curve. Datasheets show this as **inductance vs. DC bias current**:

Under an applied voltage the inductor obeys $di/dt = v/L_{inc}(i)$ ([[q0041]]). While $L$ is constant the current ramps linearly. ## What saturation breaks in a real converter

Take a buck converter, 12 V → 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 500 kHz. The ripple is $\Delta I_L = (V_{in}-V_{out})D/(Lf) = 1.02$ A, so

$$I_{pk} = I_{out} + \frac{\Delta I_L}{2}$$

| Load | Peak current | With a 3 A $I_{sat}$ part |
|---|---|---|
| 2 A | 2.51 A | Fine, 16% margin |
| 2.5 A | 3.01 A | At the edge; worse when hot |
| 4 A | 4.51 A | Saturated: current spikes every cycle |

Consequences in order of how often they bite:

1. **Switch overcurrent.** The high-side FET's peak current jumps. If the controller has cycle-by-cycle current limit, it trips and the output droops. If not, the FET can exceed its pulsed rating.
2. **Losses and heat.** Higher peak and RMS current raise conduction loss in the FET and inductor DCR; core loss rises too.
3. **Thermal runaway.** Ferrite $B_{sat}$ (and so $I_{sat}$) falls with temperature. More heat, earlier saturation, more current, more heat.
4. **Noise.** Sharp current spikes raise conducted and radiated EMI.
5. **Control problems.** The inductor is part of the LC filter and the loop's double pole; a collapsing $L$ moves it, and in current-mode control the steep slope changes the modulator gain.

When does a converter hit peak current it wasn't designed for? **Startup** (charging output capacitors), **load transients**, **output short circuits** (current rises until current limit acts, so $I_{sat}$ should exceed the current-limit threshold) and **input transients**.

> [!WARNING]
> Many controllers' current-limit threshold is well above the nominal load. If the inductor saturates below the current limit, a short circuit drives it into saturation before the limit acts, and the fast di/dt can outrun the limit's response time. Choose $I_{sat}$ above the controller's maximum current-limit setting.

## Defining $I_{sat}$: read the fine print

Vendors define saturation current differently:

- the DC current at which $L$ falls by **10%, 20% or 30%** from its zero-bias value (it varies by manufacturer and product line),
- sometimes at 25 °C, sometimes at elevated temperature.

There's also a separate **$I_{rms}$** (or $I_{temp}$) rating: the current that raises the part's temperature by e.g. 40 °C, set by DCR and losses. You need both: $I_{sat} > I_{pk}$ and $I_{rms} > I_{L,rms}$ where $I_{L,rms} = \sqrt{I_{out}^2 + \Delta I^2/12}$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The percentage drop used to define $I_{sat}$ and the temperature at which it's specified vary by manufacturer and product family. Compare inductors from different vendors using their $L$ vs. $I$ curves, not the headline $I_{sat}$ number.

## Gapping: buying saturation headroom

An air gap $\ell_g$ in the magnetic path adds reluctance. The effective permeability becomes

$$\mu_e \approx \frac{\mu_r}{1 + \mu_r\,\ell_g/\ell_e}$$

For $\mu_r = 2500$ and a gap of 1% of the path length, $\mu_e \approx 2500/26 \approx 96$. Inductance per turn drops 26×, so you need ~5× more turns for the same $L$, but the flux density per amp-turn in the core drops so much that the saturation current rises substantially. And because the gap dominates the reluctance, $L$ becomes much less sensitive to the ferrite's temperature-dependent $\mu_r$. Powdered-iron and alloy powder cores achieve the same thing with a "distributed gap" of insulating binder between particles, which is why they saturate softly ([[q0048]], [[q0049]]).

## Saturation outside converters

- **Transformers:** a DC offset or an asymmetric drive walks the flux up each cycle until the core saturates and the primary current spikes. Mains transformers also see **inrush** saturation at switch-on if energized at the wrong phase of the AC cycle.
- **Common-mode chokes:** designed to cancel differential flux, but a large imbalance or common-mode surge can saturate them, killing their filtering.
- **Current transformers:** a DC component saturates the core and the reading goes wrong.
- **Magnetic amplifiers and saturable reactors** use saturation deliberately as a switch.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> The killer sentence: "saturation means the core can't hold more flux, L collapses, and since di/dt = V/L the current spikes, so I rate $I_{sat}$ against peak current, not average, with margin at temperature."
:::

::: applications
- **Buck/boost inductor selection**: compare $I_{sat}$ against peak current including ripple, transients and the controller's current limit ([[q0259]], [[q0263]]).
- **Flyback transformers**: gapped cores avoid saturation while storing energy each cycle.
- **Mains transformers**: designed with flux density margin for high line voltage; inrush saturation is why big transformers need soft-start or NTC limiting.
- **Common-mode chokes**: rely on differential flux cancelling; imbalance can saturate them.
- **Current transformers**: must avoid DC bias that saturates the core.
- **Saturable reactors / mag amps**: deliberately use saturation to switch or regulate in some rugged power supplies.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Cored inductors only.** Air-core inductors don't saturate; their limit is heating and mechanical forces.
- The plots use **illustrative models** (tanh B–H, rational $L(i)$ fits), not a specific part. Real curves come from the vendor.
- **Temperature**: the discussion assumes ferrite's $B_{sat}$ falls with temperature, which is typical for MnZn power ferrites; other materials behave differently.
- **Hysteresis is ignored** in the B–H plot. Real cores have a loop whose area is energy lost per cycle ([[q0050]]).
- "What happens" is answered for a converter context. In an AC context (transformer), saturation shows up as distorted magnetizing current with large peaks.

::: followups
### How do you tell from a scope capture that an inductor is saturating?
Probe the inductor current (current probe, or across a sense resistor). During the on-time a healthy inductor's current is a straight ramp. In saturation, the ramp curves upward near the peak, often turning into a sharp spike. You may also see current-limit pulses skipping, the inductor running hot, and audible noise.

### Why does adding an air gap increase the saturation current if it reduces the inductance?
The gap adds reluctance, so for a given current the flux density in the core is much lower. You need more turns to restore the inductance, but $B = L I/(N A_e)$, and at the same $L$ with more turns, $B$ per amp is lower. The gap also stores most of the energy, so you can store $\tfrac12 LI^2$ at higher $I$ without the ferrite reaching $B_{sat}$.

### An inductor is rated 10 µH, Isat 3 A, Irms 4 A. Your design has 2.5 A DC with 1.5 A peak-to-peak ripple. Is it okay?
Peak current is $2.5 + 0.75 = 3.25$ A, above $I_{sat}$, so no: the inductance will have dropped more than the rated percentage at the peak. RMS current is $\sqrt{2.5^2 + 1.5^2/12} \approx 2.54$ A, fine for heating. This is exactly the case where the thermal rating hides a saturation problem.

### What's the difference between soft and hard saturation, and which would you choose?
Hard (ferrite): $L$ stays nearly flat then collapses; you get a well-defined $L$ up to $I_{sat}$ but no forgiveness beyond it. Soft (powdered iron, MPP, sendust): $L$ rolls off gradually, so overload is graceful, but $L$ at full load may be 60–80% of nominal and core losses are higher at high frequency. For high-frequency, tight-ripple designs ferrite is common; for high-current or overload-prone designs (e.g. PFC chokes, output chokes that see short circuits) soft-saturating materials are popular.

### What if your inductor only saturates at 85 °C ambient but not on the bench? What's going on and how do you fix it?
Ferrite's $B_{sat}$ falls with temperature, so $I_{sat}$ at 85 °C ambient plus self-heating is lower than the 25 °C datasheet number. Fixes: pick a part whose $L$ vs. $I$ curve at high temperature still covers your peak current, increase $L$'s size class or use a soft-saturating material, reduce ripple (higher $L$ or $f_{sw}$) to lower peak current, or lower the current limit.
