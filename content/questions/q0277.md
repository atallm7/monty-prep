::: basics
### In plain words
An LDO is a low-dropout regulator. It is a linear regulator, which means it holds a steady output voltage by acting like an adjustable valve in series with the load. "Dropout" is the smallest gap between input and output voltage at which it can still regulate. A low dropout lets you run from an input close to the output, with little wasted power.

### Picture it
Think of a tap that reduces the water pressure on its way to a faucet. The tap needs some pressure to spare: if the incoming pressure is barely above what you want out, the tap cannot hold the output steady, because it is already fully open. An LDO is a tap that needs very little spare pressure. The excess pressure it removes is lost as heat.

### Words you need
- **Linear regulator**: a regulator that uses a transistor in series with the load and burns off the extra voltage as heat.
- **Pass transistor**: the series transistor that does the regulating.
- **Dropout voltage**: the minimum $V_{in}-V_{out}$ for the regulator to still regulate. It grows with load current.
- **Headroom**: the actual difference between input and output voltage in your circuit.
- **Efficiency**: output power divided by input power, about $V_{out}/V_{in}$ for an LDO.

### The simplest example
A 3.3 V LDO has a 200 mV dropout at 500 mA. So it needs at least $3.3+0.2=3.5$ V in. From 3.5 V the efficiency is about $3.3/3.5\approx94\%$. From a 12 V supply it is only $3.3/12\approx27\%$, since the rest is burned as heat.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers why dropout depends on the pass device and current, and why a small headroom is such a big advantage.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can define the acronym **and** explain the specification behind it. "Low-dropout" is a number on the datasheet with units of volts, and the interviewer wants to see you understand what it limits (the minimum headroom $V_{in}-V_{out}$ for the regulator to keep regulating) and why a small one helps (efficiency, battery life, working from a nearly empty cell). Look for: Low-DropOut regulator, a linear regulator; dropout voltage as $V_{in}-V_{out}$ at which regulation is lost for a given load; the dependence on load current ($V_{DO}\approx I_{out}R_{DS(on)}$); and the efficiency advantage $\eta\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$.
:::

::: why
LDOs are the most common regulator on a board. Every mixed-signal chip that needs a quiet analog supply, every battery device that needs a clean 1.8 V or 3.3 V rail, and many microcontroller boards use one. Choosing the wrong dropout spec means the rail collapses when the battery is still half full, or the part runs hot because you gave it more headroom than it needs. It is also the first step into the buck vs LDO trade-off ([[q0157]], [[q0158]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Linear regulator**: a pass element (transistor) in series with the load that drops the excess voltage, controlled by feedback. See [[q0278]].
- **Dropout voltage ($V_{DO}$)**: the minimum $V_{in}-V_{out}$ for the output to stay within regulation (typically defined as the point where $V_{out}$ falls 100 mV or so below nominal, or by a fixed percentage, depending on the datasheet).
- **Pass-element types**: NPN Darlington (about 1.5 to 2 V dropout), PNP (about 0.3 to 0.6 V), P-channel MOSFET or N-channel MOSFET (tens to hundreds of mV, set by $R_{DS(on)}$).
- **Efficiency** $\eta\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$ (ignoring $I_Q$). See [[q0162]].
- **Headroom**: the actual $V_{in}-V_{out}$ in the application. It must exceed $V_{DO}$ at the worst-case load.
- **Battery discharge curve**: the cell voltage falls over time, so headroom shrinks. See [[q0297]].
:::

::: technical
**LDO**: Low-DropOut linear regulator. The pass device is a PNP transistor, a P-channel MOSFET (source to $V_{in}$) or an N-channel MOSFET (with a charge-pumped gate drive in some parts).

**Dropout voltage**: $V_{DO}=V_{in}-V_{out}$ at the point the regulator can no longer hold $V_{out}$ (datasheets define it as $V_{in}-V_{out}$ when $V_{out}$ has dropped by a specified amount, or at a specified load current, so always read the definition). For a MOSFET pass element in the triode region:
$$V_{DO}\approx I_{out}R_{DS(on)}$$
Example: $V_{DO}=0.2$ V at 0.5 A implies $R_{DS(on)}=0.4\ \Omega$.

**Efficiency (ignoring $I_Q$)**:
$$\eta=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_Q)}\approx\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}$$
At minimum headroom, $\eta_{max}=\dfrac{V_{out}}{V_{out}+V_{DO}}$. For 3.3 V with 0.2 V dropout: 94.3%.

**Typical dropout by pass element** (order-of-magnitude, vendor dependent):

| Pass element | Typical dropout | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| NPN Darlington (classic regulator) | 1.5 to 2.5 V | 2 $V_{BE}$ plus saturation, not an LDO |
| PNP | 0.3 to 0.7 V | high ground-pin current ($I_{out}/\beta$) |
| P-channel MOSFET | 50 to 300 mV | low $I_Q$, dropout $\propto I_{out}R_{DS(on)}$ |
| N-channel MOSFET (with bias/charge pump) | 50 to 200 mV or less | needs auxiliary supply, very low dropout |

**Advantage**: allows regulating closer to the input, extends battery use, and raises efficiency when $V_{in}\approx V_{out}$.
:::

::: long
"LDO stands for low-dropout regulator. It's a linear regulator: a pass transistor in series between the input and the load, with an error amplifier comparing the output, through a feedback divider, to a reference and adjusting the transistor to hold the output voltage.

The 'dropout' is the voltage you lose across that pass transistor when it's wide open. Specifically it's the minimum input-to-output difference at which the regulator still holds its output. If I need 3.3 V and the dropout is 200 mV at 500 mA, I need at least 3.5 V at the input. Below that the transistor is fully on, the output just tracks the input minus the drop, and the regulator has lost control. The dropout number depends on load: for a MOSFET it's basically load current times $R_{DS(on)}$, so 0.2 V at 0.5 A means 0.4 Ω.

'Low' is relative to older linear regulators with a Darlington pass transistor, which need 1.5 to 2 V or more. An LDO is typically 100 to 300 mV, sometimes tens of millivolts.

That's an advantage in two ways. First, battery operation. A single Li-ion cell goes from 4.2 V down to about 3.0 V, and with an LDO I can keep a 3.3 V rail regulated until the cell is near 3.5 V instead of cutting out at 5 V or so. Second, efficiency. An LDO's input current equals its output current, so $\eta\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$. With 3.5 V in and 3.3 V out that's 94%, which can match a buck converter at light load with none of the noise and size. The same part with 12 V in is 27% efficient, so LDOs are for small headroom, or for low-noise rails."
:::

::: terms
- **LDO**: low-dropout linear regulator.
- **Dropout voltage ($V_{DO}$)**: minimum $V_{in}-V_{out}$ for regulation at a stated load.
- **Pass element**: the series transistor that drops the excess voltage.
- **Headroom**: actual $V_{in}-V_{out}$ in the circuit.
- **Linear regulator**: regulator that dissipates the excess voltage as heat in the pass device.
- **Quiescent (ground) current ($I_Q$)**: current drawn by the regulator itself. See [[q0165]].
- **Regulation**: output held to within a specified tolerance across line, load and temperature.
- **Triode (ohmic) region**: where a fully-on MOSFET behaves like a resistor $R_{DS(on)}$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "low dropout means it works with a small voltage difference" and gives one example.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Spells out the acronym and distinguishes **dropout voltage** (a spec at a specified load) from **headroom** (what the circuit gives).
- Gives the physical origin: pass-FET $R_{DS(on)}$, so $V_{DO}=I\,R_{DS(on)}$ and rises with load and temperature.
- Compares pass elements (Darlington vs PNP vs MOSFET).
- Quantifies the advantage: efficiency $V_{out}/V_{in}$ ($94\%$ at 3.5 V to 3.3 V) and battery runtime.
- Notes the caveat: low dropout helps *only if* the headroom is actually small; with 12 V in, it is a poor regulator.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Dropout is how much voltage the LDO drops."** It is the *minimum* drop at which it still regulates. The actual drop is whatever $V_{in}-V_{out}$ is.
- **Quoting one dropout number for all loads.** $V_{DO}$ scales with load current and rises with temperature. A 100 mV figure at 100 mA could be 500 mV at 1 A.
- **"Low dropout means more efficient."** Only when headroom is small. At a large $V_{in}-V_{out}$ an LDO is as inefficient as any linear regulator.
- **Ignoring dropout in battery designs.** A "3.3 V LDO" on a 3.3 V coin cell regulates for ~0 V of headroom, so it just passes the battery voltage.
- **Confusing LDO with any linear regulator.** A 7805 is linear but not low-dropout.
- **Thinking the output stays regulated in dropout.** In dropout the output equals $V_{in}-I\,R_{DS(on)}$ and follows the input including noise.
:::

::: deep
## Where "dropout" comes from

An LDO's loop turns the pass transistor on harder when $V_{out}$ sags. The loop keeps $V_{out}$ regulated until the transistor can't turn on any harder: the PMOS gate is pulled to ground (or the PNP base is saturated), and the pass device is as low-resistance as it can be. At that point the device is just a resistor $R_{DS(on)}$ (or a saturated transistor $V_{CE(sat)}$) and

$$V_{out}=V_{in}-I_{out}R_{DS(on)},\qquad V_{DO}=I_{out}R_{DS(on)}.$$

Different pass elements have different floors:

- **NPN follower / Darlington (classic 78xx-style):** the emitter-follower output sits one or two $V_{BE}$ below its drive, so $V_{DO}\approx2V_{BE}+V_{CE(sat)}\approx1.5$ to 2.5 V.
- **PNP:** the output is the collector, and it can saturate to roughly $V_{CE(sat)}\approx0.3$ to 0.7 V, but the base current (about $I_{out}/\beta$) goes to ground and is wasted: ground-pin current rises with load.
- **P-MOSFET:** drain is the output. No base current, dropout set by $R_{DS(on)}$, typical 50 to 300 mV, down to tens of mV for good parts.
- **N-MOSFET:** a source follower normally needs gate drive above $V_{in}$ (so a separate bias rail or an internal charge pump), but lets the *input* voltage get very close to the output with fast transient response.

## The efficiency advantage

An LDO's input current equals its output current plus its own $I_Q$. Power in is $V_{in}I_{in}$, power out is $V_{out}I_{out}$, so

$$\eta=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_Q)}.$$

For a 3.3 V rail from different inputs at 500 mA (neglecting $I_Q$):

| $V_{in}$ | Headroom | $\eta$ | Pass-device heat |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.5 V | 0.2 V | 94.3% | 0.10 W |
| 3.7 V | 0.4 V | 89.2% | 0.20 W |
| 4.2 V | 0.9 V | 78.6% | 0.45 W |
| 5.0 V | 1.7 V | 66.0% | 0.85 W |
| 12 V | 8.7 V | 27.5% | 4.35 W |

The pattern: efficiency is just the voltage ratio, so the advantage is *conditional*: low dropout only wins when the actual headroom is small. A good rule of thumb: if $V_{in}/V_{out}$ is less than about 1.3 to 1.5, an LDO is competitive with a buck, especially when you include the buck's quiescent current, switching noise and cost. For larger ratios see [[q0158]] and [[q0222]].

## The battery argument in a different light

An LDO's input current equals its output current, so the battery supplies exactly the load's charge, no more. If the load draws a constant 100 mA from a 1500 mAh cell, runtime is $1500/100=15$ h whether the cell is at 4.2 V or 3.5 V. The cost is energy: $\Delta V\cdot I$ is thrown away as heat. If the cell runs from 4.2 V down to about 3.5 V (where this LDO drops out) with an average near 3.8 V, the average efficiency is about $3.3/3.8=87\%$. At 100 mA that is competitive with a buck, whose own quiescent current and switching losses eat several percent, and the LDO is quieter and smaller. The price is the last stretch of battery capacity below $V_{out}+V_{DO}$, which the LDO cannot use, and a boost or buck-boost could.

## Real specs to read

Datasheets give dropout in a table "at $I_{out}=$ X mA, $V_{out}$ dropped by 2% (or 100 mV)" and sometimes a curve vs load and temperature. Pay attention to: which condition defines dropout, whether the figure is typical or maximum, the worst-case temperature (dropout rises with $R_{DS(on)}$ tempco, which for silicon is roughly +0.3% to +0.7% per °C), and the minimum headroom for *good PSRR*, which can be much higher than the dropout voltage ([[q0278]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Typical dropout ranges by pass-element type are order-of-magnitude figures. Individual parts vary widely, and "LDO" in marketing is sometimes used loosely. Always read the dropout definition and conditions in the datasheet.

## Worked example: when does the Li-ion cell stop being enough?

The plot above treats the source as ideal. A real cell has internal resistance, and that moves the dropout knee in exactly the wrong direction when the load is a radio burst. Take a 3.3 V LDO with a pass-device resistance of 0.4 Ω at 25 °C, a cell with 150 mΩ of internal resistance plus 50 mΩ of connector and trace (assumed values), and an ambient hot enough to put the junction near 85 °C. Silicon $R_{DS(on)}$ rises about 0.3% to 0.7% per °C, so take 0.5%/°C: $0.4\times(1+0.005\times60)=0.52\ \Omega$. The input pin sees the cell's open-circuit voltage less the drop in the source resistance, so the cell needs to be at least

$$V_{cell,min}=V_{out}+I_{out}\,(R_{DS(on),hot}+R_{source})=3.3+I_{out}(0.52+0.20).$$

| Load | Dropout of LDO | Sag in cell and wiring | Minimum open-circuit cell voltage |
|---|---|---|---|
| 100 mA (idle) | 52 mV | 20 mV | 3.37 V |
| 500 mA (radio burst) | 260 mV | 100 mV | 3.66 V |
| 1 A | 520 mV | 200 mV | 4.02 V |

At the 500 mA burst the cell must be above 3.66 V for the LDO to keep regulating. A lithium-ion cell passes through 3.7 V roughly in the middle of its discharge, so a rail that holds up at idle can still dip during every transmit burst once the cell is about half empty. Where exactly that point falls on the capacity scale depends on the chemistry, the temperature and the discharge rate, so it should be read from the cell's discharge curve and not assumed. The fix is not necessarily a different regulator: lower the pass-device resistance (a larger or lower-$R_{DS(on)}$ part), shorten and thicken the wiring, add bulk capacitance so the burst is shared with the battery, or move to a buck-boost for the last part of the discharge.

## Worked example: quiescent current at the other end

Dropout is about headroom at heavy load. The other half of the LDO story is quiescent current at light load, which is why "the LDO is efficient when headroom is small" is not enough. A sleeping sensor node pulls 10 µA at 3.3 V from a 3.7 V cell. The LDO's own ground-pin current is $I_Q$. Efficiency is $\eta=V_{out}I_{out}/[V_{in}(I_{out}+I_Q)]$:

| LDO $I_Q$ | $\eta$ at 10 µA load | Total drain | 1500 mAh runtime (ignoring self-discharge) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 µA | 14.9% | 60 µA | about 2.9 years |
| 1 µA | 81% | 11 µA | about 15.6 years |

A part with a 50 µA quiescent current wastes five times the load in the regulator itself. The runtime figure ignores cell self-discharge and aging, which will cap it far below 15 years in practice, but the ratio of 5.5 is what matters. This is why micro-power LDOs advertise $I_Q$ in the hundreds of nanoamps, and why a 0.1 V dropout at 3.5 V is no use if the regulator's own consumption dominates. A buck with a low-power mode may beat both, but the comparison has to be at the actual load profile, not at one number.

## Limiting cases and the dropout definition

Taking $R_{DS(on)}$ as a constant resistor is good only deep in dropout. Near the knee the loop is just running out of gate drive, so the transition from regulation to follow-the-input is gradual, and "dropout" is defined as the headroom at which the output has fallen by a stated amount, commonly 2% or 100 mV, at a stated current. Two parts with the same quoted dropout can differ by tens of millivolts in how soon the output starts to sag. PSRR also degrades well before the knee, because the loop has less gain available. In the extreme limit of very small $I_{out}$ the dropout approaches zero and the minimum headroom is set by the loop's own requirements, such as the minimum $V_{GS}$ the error amplifier can drive. At the other extreme, a high-current LDO runs hot first: at 500 mA and 260 mV the pass device dissipates only 0.13 W, but at 5 V in it would dissipate 0.85 W, so the dropout advantage is also a thermal advantage.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 0.5%/°C temperature coefficient, cell resistance and wiring resistance are assumed for the exercise. Dropout versus temperature and load is usually given as a curve in the LDO datasheet, which should replace the estimate.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Measure dropout directly.** Set a fixed load current. With a bench supply, start at a comfortable $V_{in}$ and lower it in 10 mV steps while measuring $V_{out}$ at the pins. Dropout is the point where $V_{out}$ has fallen by the datasheet criterion. Repeat at three load currents and plot the line; its slope is the effective $R_{DS(on)}$.
2. **Repeat hot.** Warm the part (a heat gun on the lowest setting, watched with a thermocouple) and re-measure. The shift in the knee is your temperature coefficient.
3. **Use a real source.** Replace the bench supply with the actual battery, or add a series resistor equal to its internal resistance. Trigger the scope on the radio burst and watch whether the rail dips.
4. **Measure quiescent current.** With the load disconnected or at a known tiny current, measure the supply current with a nanoamp-capable meter or a shunt and amplifier. Many bench meters have burden voltages and ranges that perturb the measurement at microamps.

::: applications
- **Battery-powered MCU rails**: a 3.3 V or 1.8 V LDO from a single Li-ion cell or two AAs.
- **Low-noise analog supplies**: LDOs after a switching regulator for ADC, PLL, RF and sensor rails.
- **Post-regulation**: a small 100 to 300 mV headroom LDO after a buck to clean ripple and set the rail accurately.
- **Always-on standby rails**: ultra-low-$I_Q$ LDOs with microamps of quiescent current.
- **Core supplies**: 0.9 V to 1.2 V digital core rails from 1.8 V or 3.3 V rails, using the low voltage difference.
:::

::: assumptions
- "Dropout" is defined at a **specified load current and a specified output-voltage droop** (often 2% or 100 mV). Different vendors define it differently.
- I assumed a **MOSFET pass element** for the $I\,R_{DS(on)}$ model. PNP-based LDOs saturate with $V_{CE(sat)}$ and behave less like a resistor.
- Efficiency ignores $I_Q$ unless stated. At very light load $I_Q$ dominates ([[q0283]]).
- A valid alternative viewpoint: some engineers say any linear regulator needing less than about 1 V of headroom is "low-dropout"; others reserve it for sub-500 mV. State the number rather than the label.
- Temperature and aging raise $R_{DS(on)}$ and hence dropout. A design with exactly zero margin will fail at hot.
:::

::: followups
### Why does dropout voltage increase with load current?
For a MOSFET pass device $V_{DO}=I_{out}R_{DS(on)}$, so doubling the current doubles the dropout. A part with 0.4 Ω has 0.2 V at 0.5 A and 0.4 V at 1 A. For a PNP the effect is milder because $V_{CE(sat)}$ grows slowly with current, but there is a base-drive limit.

### What happens to the output when $V_{in}$ falls below $V_{out}+V_{DO}$?
The pass device is fully on and the loop has no more drive. The output becomes $V_{in}-I_{out}R_{DS(on)}$, follows the input including any ripple or noise (PSRR drops to about 0 dB), and the ground-pin current may rise for PNP or bipolar types. Some parts flag this with a power-good pin going low.

### Is a lower dropout always better?
Not always. Very-low-dropout parts (N-channel or large P-channel) can cost more, need bias supplies, or have different stability requirements (output capacitor range). And what matters for your design is the dropout *at your load and worst-case temperature*, with margin. Also, if your headroom is larger than a volt, dropout is irrelevant and PSRR, noise and thermal behavior matter more.

### Can you get 100% efficiency from an LDO?
No. Efficiency is bounded by $V_{out}/V_{in}$, and $V_{in}>V_{out}$ by at least the dropout, plus $I_Q$. If $V_{in}=V_{out}+V_{DO}$ exactly, $\eta_{max}=V_{out}/(V_{out}+V_{DO})$. Even at 0.1 V dropout and 3.3 V out that is 97%.

### Scenario: your board's 3.3 V LDO rail reads 3.15 V when the battery is at 3.4 V and the radio transmits. Why?
The battery is within about 100 to 250 mV of the LDO's dropout and the radio's burst current (hundreds of mA) raises both the dropout ($I\,R_{DS(on)}$) and the battery sag (internal resistance). The LDO is in dropout during bursts, so the rail follows $V_{bat}-IR$. Fix with a lower-dropout part, a larger pass device, a boost or buck-boost stage, or reduce peak current with bulk capacitance.
:::
