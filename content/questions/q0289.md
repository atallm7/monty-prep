::: testing
Whether you can turn a requirement (output current, allowed droop, ripple) into **capacitor specifications**, and know why a capacitor's datasheet value is not what the circuit sees. Expected: flying capacitor from $R_{SSL}=1/(fC)$ against the switch limit; output capacitor from ripple $I/(2fC)$ plus ESR; voltage rating from the node it sees; dielectric and DC-bias derating; ESR/ESL and ripple-current (RMS) ratings; startup inrush; placement. Strong answers put numbers on each and pick the capacitor that *still* works at bias, temperature and tolerance.
:::

::: why
In a charge pump the capacitors *are* the power path. A bad choice does not just add ripple, it raises $R_{out}$, collapses the output or overheats the part. The most common field failure is a small-case ceramic that loses most of its capacitance at the DC bias it sees. This is also where all the earlier capacitor knowledge ([[q0030]], [[q0032]]) gets applied.
:::

::: concepts
- **Flying capacitor sets $R_{SSL}=1/(fC)$.** See [[q0284]].
- **Output ripple** $\Delta V\approx I/(2fC_{out})+2I\,R_{ESR}$ for a doubler.
- **DC-bias derating** of class-II ceramics. See [[q0030]].
- **ESR and ESL** shape the current edges. See [[q0030]].
- **RMS ripple current**: $2I_{out}$ in $C_{fly}$ for a doubler, $I_{out}$ in $C_{out}$.
- **Decoupling** of the input. See [[q0032]].
:::

::: answer
"I work backwards from the allowed output droop and ripple.

The flying capacitor sets the slow-switching output resistance, $R_{SSL}=1/(fC_{fly})$. For a doubler at 500 kHz and 1 µF that's 2 Ω, against about 8 Ω from four switch resistances, so making $C_{fly}$ bigger past a point buys almost nothing. A good rule is to choose it so $R_{SSL}$ is no more than the switch-limited resistance: here 0.25 µF is the floor and 1 µF is comfortable.

The output capacitor sets ripple. It supplies the load alone for half a cycle, so $\Delta V=I/(2fC_{out})$; for 20 mA, 500 kHz and 10 µF that's 2 mV, plus ESR times the current step.

Then the part I'd actually check is what the capacitor really is in the circuit. Ceramics lose capacitance with DC bias: a 1 µF 0402 X5R at 3.3 V may hold only half of that. So I'd specify the effective value at the working voltage and temperature, with a rating at least 1.5 to 2 times the voltage across it, X7R or X5R in a decent case size, low ESR, and enough RMS current rating: $2I_{out}$ in the flying cap. And put them right at the pins to keep loop inductance down."
:::

::: technical
**Flying capacitor ($C_{fly}$).**
- Value: $R_{SSL}=\dfrac{1}{fC_{fly}}$; choose so $R_{SSL}\lesssim R_{FSL}=8R_{sw}+4R_{ESR}$, i.e. $C_{fly}\gtrsim\dfrac{1}{8R_{sw}f}$ (0.25 µF at 500 kHz, 1 Ω), then margin for derating and tolerance.
- Voltage rating: doubler $V_{C,fly}=V_{in}$ in steady state (peaks can exceed during startup/transients); rate for $\ge1.5V_{in,max}$ before bias derating.
- RMS current: $\approx2I_{out}$ (square-wave $\pm2I_{out}$, fast-switching case; higher if spiky in the slow-switching region), plus startup peak.

**Output capacitor ($C_{out}$).**
- Ripple: $\Delta V_{pp}\approx\dfrac{I_{out}}{2fC_{out}}+2I_{out}R_{ESR}$.
- Voltage rating: $\ge1.25$ to 1.5 times $V_{out,max}$ (at 6.6 V, a 10 V part is common) before derating.
- RMS current: $\approx I_{out}$.

**Input capacitor ($C_{in}$).** Input current is $2I_{out}$ with edges: $C_{in}\ge C_{fly}$ typically, close to the pin.

**Effective capacitance:** $C_{eff}=C_{nom}\cdot k_{bias}k_{temp}k_{tol}$ (vendor curves). Class-II ceramics (X5R/X7R) at a large fraction of rated voltage can retain well under 50% (small case, vendor-dependent). C0G/NP0 is stable but only practical at small values.

**Example** ($V_{in}=3.3$ V, 500 kHz, $R_{sw}=1\ \Omega$, 20 mA, $C_{out}=10\ \mu$F):

| $C_{fly}$ effective | $R_{SSL}$ | $R_{out}$ | $V_{out}$ | $\eta$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.25 µF | 8.0 Ω | 11.3 Ω | 6.374 V | 96.6% |
| 0.5 µF | 4.0 Ω | 8.94 Ω | 6.421 V | 97.3% |
| 1 µF | 2.0 Ω | 8.25 Ω | 6.435 V | 97.5% |
| 2.2 µF | 0.91 Ω | 8.05 Ω | 6.439 V | 97.6% |
:::

::: short
"Flying cap from the slow-switching resistance $1/(fC)$: pick it so that's no more than the switch-limited resistance, about 1 µF at 500 kHz. Output cap from ripple $I/(2fC)$ plus ESR. Then spec effective capacitance after DC-bias derating, voltage rating 1.5x or more, low ESR, and RMS current: $2I_{out}$ in the flying cap. Place close to the pins."
:::

::: long
"I'd work from the requirement: output current, allowed droop and ripple, and the clock frequency.

For the flying capacitor, the slow-switching resistance is $1/(fC)$. Combined with the switch-limited $8R_{sw}$, the output resistance is the root sum of squares. At 500 kHz with 1 Ω switches the switch limit is 8 Ω, so a 0.25 µF capacitor already matches it and 1 µF is comfortable: going from 1 µF to 2.2 µF changes $R_{out}$ from 8.25 to 8.05 Ω, 2%. Going down to 0.25 µF costs about 3 Ω more, 0.06 V at 20 mA. So I'd pick about 1 µF and let the datasheet's recommended value override.

The output capacitor sets ripple: it carries the load alone for half a cycle, so $\Delta V=I/(2fC)$, 2 mV for 10 µF at 20 mA, plus ESR times the 2I current step, 0.4 mV for 10 mΩ. Usually it's sized for the load transient or for the datasheet minimum rather than ripple.

Then the checks that matter in practice. DC bias: a 6.3 V X5R 0402 at 3.3 V may have half its capacitance, so I use the vendor's curve and specify effective values. Voltage rating: the flying capacitor sees $V_{in}$ and the output sees 6.6 V, so I'd use 10 V parts, bigger case for less derating. RMS current: the flying capacitor carries plus and minus $2I_{out}$, so 40 mA RMS at 20 mA out, easy for ceramics but worth checking at 200 mA. Startup: an empty flying capacitor charged through two 1 Ω switches draws about 1.6 A, and the first transfer into an empty output capacitor can be over 3 A, so soft start matters. And everything sits right at the pins with short ground returns."
:::

::: terms
- **Effective capacitance**: capacitance after bias, temperature and tolerance.
- **DC-bias derating**: loss of capacitance of class-II ceramics under voltage.
- **ESR / ESL**: series resistance and inductance.
- **RMS ripple current**: heating-relevant current in the capacitor.
- **C0G/NP0, X5R, X7R**: ceramic dielectric classes.
- **Inrush**: startup charging current.
- **Rated voltage**: maximum working voltage, with margin.
- **Piezo/microphonics**: audible noise from ceramics at clock harmonics.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "use 1 µF ceramic capacitors".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives $C_{fly}$ from $R_{SSL}$ vs $R_{FSL}$, and says when more capacitance stops helping.
- Calculates ripple for $C_{out}$ including ESR step.
- Specifies *effective* capacitance with bias derating and gives the voltage rating logic.
- Gives RMS currents ($2I_{out}$ and $I_{out}$) and startup inrush.
- Covers layout and the frequency trade-off.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using the nominal capacitance** from the label.
- **Rating the capacitor at exactly the working voltage.**
- **Oversizing $C_{fly}$** past the point of benefit (inrush and size).
- **Ignoring ESR in $R_{FSL}$:** $4R_{ESR}$ adds to the switch term.
- **Using Y5V/Z5U** with huge derating.
- **Long traces to the pins**, adding loop inductance and spikes.
:::

::: deep
## Sizing the flying capacitor

$R_{out}\approx\sqrt{(1/fC)^2+(8R_{sw}+4R_{ESR})^2}$. The returns diminish quickly:

Figure: $R_{out}$ is within about 10% of the switch limit once $C_{fly}\ge$ about 0.5 µF and climbs quickly below 0.25 µF. Because of DC-bias loss, a capacitor that is 1 µF nominal and 0.4 µF effective sits in the steep part. Lower frequency moves the knee to larger $C$ ($C\propto1/f$).

At 20 mA, 0.25 µF costs $0.02\times(11.3-8.25)=61$ mV more droop than 1 µF. Spending more than 1 µF gains at most about 5 mV ($0.02\times(8.25-8)$). So the design target is "effective $C\ge1/(8R_{sw}f)$ times margin of 2 to 4".

## Output capacitor and the transient

Steady ripple: $I/(2fC)=2$ mV for 20 mA and 10 µF. The load-step side is larger: a 50 mA step recovers on the pump's time scale (a few clock cycles), so the droop is roughly $\Delta I\cdot n/(f C_{out})$ over a few cycles. Many datasheets ask for $C_{out}\ge C_{fly}$ (often 2x to 10x), vendor-dependent.

## Derating, voltage and dielectric

For each capacitor: nominal, bias derating, temperature and tolerance. For a 1 µF, 6.3 V, 0402 X5R at 3.3 V illustrate a 50% retention: 0.5 µF effective. A 10 V 0603 part might keep 75%. Rating: the flying capacitor sees $V_{in}$ (3.3 V) and spikes at startup; $C_{out}$ sees 6.6 V (or more if unregulated and unloaded: up to $2V_{in,max}$). So 10 V minimum, 16 V for margin on a 5 V supply doubler (10 V output).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The retained fraction (50% to 75%) is illustrative. It depends strongly on case size, voltage rating, dielectric and vendor. Always use the manufacturer's DC-bias curve. Datasheet "recommended capacitor" tables usually assume ceramic at rated voltage; follow them.

## ESR, ESL and current ratings

- Flying capacitor current: $\pm2I_{out}$ square-wave: RMS $2I_{out}$ (40 mA at 20 mA). This is the fast-switching (flat-current) case; if the design sits in the slow-switching region the current comes in exponential spikes and the RMS is higher. ESR loss $4I_{out}^2R_{ESR}$: 16 µW for 10 mΩ.
- Output capacitor: $\pm I_{out}$: RMS $I_{out}$.
- At higher currents (200 mA), RMS becomes 0.4 A: still small for MLCCs but relevant for tiny 0201 parts.
- ESL (about 0.5 to 1 nH in small MLCCs) with the current edge $2I/t_r$ gives spikes: 40 mA in 5 ns with 1 nH is 8 mV.

## Startup inrush

With empty capacitors, the first phase 1 charges $C_{fly}$ from $V_{in}$ through two switches plus ESR: $I_{pk}=V_{in}/(2R_{sw}+R_{ESR})\approx1.6$ A for 1 Ω switches. If $C_{out}$ also starts empty, the first phase 2 drives about $2V_{in}/(2R_{sw})=3.3$ A into it (less if body diodes have pre-charged it to about $V_{in}$). Parts use soft-start (ramped switch strength or frequency) and need input capacitance and source impedance that can support it. Select $C_{in}\ge C_{fly}$ (about 2x for margin) close to the pin; with a long supply lead the input rings.

## Placement and layout

Put $C_{fly}$ directly across its pins (shortest loop), $C_{out}$ and $C_{in}$ at their pins with a solid ground return. Large $di/dt$ loops radiate and add ESL spikes.

## A derated worked example, start to finish

Take the doubler from the example above: 3.3 V in, 500 kHz, 1 Ω switches, 20 mA load, and a 1 µF, 6.3 V, 0402 X5R flying capacitor. The label says 1 µF. What does the circuit see? Stack the three derating factors. Say the bias curve at 3.3 V on a 6.3 V part keeps 50%, X5R at 85 °C is down about 15%, and the −10% side of a ±10% tolerance is possible. The product is $0.5\times0.85\times0.9=0.38$, so the effective value is about 0.38 µF, not 1 µF.

Now $R_{SSL}=1/(fC)=1/(5\times10^5\times3.83\times10^{-7})=5.2\ \Omega$, and $R_{out}=\sqrt{5.2^2+8^2}=9.56\ \Omega$ instead of the 8.25 Ω you computed from the nameplate value. At 20 mA the output is $6.6-0.02\times9.56=6.409$ V instead of 6.435 V, a 26 mV shift. That is small at 20 mA, but the error scales with load: at 200 mA it is 260 mV, and the part may drop out of regulation or fail a downstream threshold. The lesson is that the worst-case corner is the one you specify against, so write the requirement as "at least 1 µF effective at 3.3 V and 85 °C", and let the purchasing team find a part that meets it (a 10 V 0603, or a 0805, usually does).

The output capacitor gets the same treatment. A 10 µF, 10 V, 0603 X5R at 6.6 V might keep 60%, with the same temperature and tolerance factors that gives about 4 µF at the worst corner (here I took 0.6 × 0.85 × 0.8, assuming a ±20% part). The capacitive ripple is then $0.02/(2\times5\times10^5\times4.1\times10^{-6})=4.9$ mV, 2.5 times the nameplate figure. Still tiny, but notice that the trend (a factor of 2 to 3) is the same one that bites the flying capacitor.

## Limiting cases

- **$C_{fly}\to\infty$:** $R_{SSL}\to0$ and $R_{out}\to8R_{sw}$. The flying capacitor then behaves as an ideal battery of $V_{in}$ and only the switches limit you. Inrush gets worse and size grows, with no benefit.
- **$C_{fly}\to0$:** $R_{out}\to1/(fC)$ and the pump becomes a capacitor-limited current source. The output sags in proportion to load until it drops out of the datasheet window.
- **Frequency up:** $R_{SSL}$ falls as $1/f$, so you can shrink $C_{fly}$ by the same factor, but switching and gate-drive losses rise linearly and the ESL edges get faster. The knee capacitance $1/(8R_{sw}f)$ is 1.25 µF at 100 kHz, 0.25 µF at 500 kHz and 62 nF at 2 MHz, so a faster clock is how pumps get into tiny cases.
- **ESR-dominated ripple:** if the ceramic is replaced by a high-ESR electrolytic or tantalum, the $2IR_{ESR}$ step overtakes $I/(2fC)$. At 20 mA, 500 mΩ gives 20 mV of spike, ten times the capacitive term.

## Bench verification

You can check all of this with a scope, an electronic load and a DMM.

1. **Effective capacitance:** do not trust the LCR meter alone, because most LCR meters measure with 0.5 or 1 V AC and no DC bias. Use a bias-capable meter, or infer $C_{eff}$ by measuring $R_{out}$ at two frequencies (if the part has a settable clock) and solving $R_{SSL}$ out of the root-sum-square.
2. **Output resistance:** measure $V_{out}$ at two loads, say 10 mA and 50 mA, and take the slope $R_{out}=-\Delta V/\Delta I$. The model above predicts 6.504 V and 6.122 V, a slope of 9.56 Ω. A much steeper slope says the flying capacitor is derated more than you assumed.
3. **Ripple:** probe $C_{out}$ with the ground spring (no long ground lead, or you will measure your own loop inductance), 20 MHz bandwidth limit off for the edge spikes and on for the clean ripple number. Compare the sawtooth amplitude to $I/(2fC_{eff})$ and the spikes to $2I\,R_{ESR}$ plus ESL terms.
4. **Inrush:** put a current probe on the input and power-cycle with the output discharged. Compare against the roughly 1.6 A estimate, and confirm soft start limits it.
5. **Audible noise:** if a pump in a burst mode whines, the ceramics are acting as piezo transducers at the burst frequency. Change the case size or switch to a soft-terminated part.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 50% and 60% bias retention figures and the −15% X5R temperature figure are illustrative round numbers, not vendor data. Real values depend on case size, rated voltage and manufacturer, so read the DC-bias and temperature curves for the exact part number.

::: applications
- **Inverter ICs for negative rails**: 1 µF flying plus output capacitors specified in the datasheet table.
- **LED driver pumps**: larger flying capacitors at higher current (hundreds of mA), tighter ESR.
- **RS-232 transceivers**: 0.1 to 1 µF capacitors, polarity and rating as per datasheet.
- **High-voltage on-chip pumps**: small stage capacitors in the pF to nF range.
- **Automotive**: X7R/X8R with tight derating.
:::

::: assumptions
- Doubler, 50% duty, equal switch resistance, $R_{out}$ by root-sum-square (within about 9% of the exact result for this topology, slightly conservative; see [[q0284]]).
- Frequency is the full cycle rate; datasheets may quote oscillator at $2f$.
- Ceramic derating numbers are illustrative.
- Switch resistance and gate charge are generic values.
- Regulated or burst-mode parts have extra ripple considerations not covered here.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a bigger flying capacitor stop helping?
$R_{SSL}=1/(fC)$ falls, but $R_{FSL}=8R_{sw}$ does not. Once $R_{SSL}\ll R_{FSL}$, $R_{out}$ is limited by the switches.

### What dielectric would you pick?
X7R or X5R in a case large enough for low derating; C0G only for small values. Avoid Y5V/Z5U due to huge bias and temperature loss.

### How does lower frequency change capacitor choice?
$R_{SSL}$ grows as $1/f$, so $C_{fly}$ must grow proportionally to keep the same $R_{out}$, and ripple $I/(2fC)$ grows too.

### What happens if the flying capacitor is half the datasheet value after derating?
$R_{SSL}$ doubles. In the example from 2 Ω to 4 Ω, $R_{out}$ from 8.25 to 8.94 Ω, about 14 mV more at 20 mA. Close to the datasheet minimum the output may sag more and ripple rises.

### Scenario: output is 5.5 V instead of 6.4 V at 50 mA with a new capacitor lot. Why?
Check effective capacitance (a different case size or voltage rating can derate far more), ESR, and the clock. At 50 mA each lost 1 Ω of $R_{out}$ costs 50 mV; if $C_{fly}$ fell to 0.1 µF, $R_{SSL}=20\ \Omega$ and $R_{out}=21.5\ \Omega$, giving $6.6-1.08=5.5$ V.
:::
