::: testing
Whether you recognise the feedback divider as the most noise-sensitive node in the regulator and know how to protect it: divider at the FB pin with the high-impedance tap kept tiny, the longer run placed on the low-impedance side (the sense line from the output), kept away from the switch node and inductor, referenced to a quiet ground, and sensed at the right point (after the output capacitors, at the load). The interviewer also wants the quantitative reason (a femtofarad-scale coupling matters) and the trade-offs (lower divider resistance versus quiescent current).
:::

::: why
The feedback node decides the output voltage and the loop behaviour, and it is the node where microvolts of noise become millivolts of output error, jitter or instability. Many buck boards that "almost work" have a feedback trace running beside the inductor. It is also an elegant example of how impedance level controls sensitivity, a principle that applies to every high-impedance node (comparators, ADC inputs, oscillator pins).
:::

::: concepts
- **Voltage divider**: $V_{FB}=V_{out}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ and Thevenin impedance $R_1\parallel R_2$. See [[q0008]].
- **Capacitive coupling**: $\Delta V = V\,C_c/(C_c+C_p)$ for a coupling into a high-Z node.
- **Magnetic coupling**: $v = M\,di/dt$. See [[q0551]].
- **Switch node and inductor as aggressors**: [[q0550]], [[q0551]].
- **Point of regulation**: the regulator controls the voltage where it senses. See [[q0543]].
- **Grounds**: AGND vs PGND. See [[q0546]].
:::

::: answer
"The feedback network is the most sensitive part of a buck, so I treat it like a victim. The divider goes right at the FB pin, so the high-impedance tap, the junction of the two resistors and the pin, is only a few millimetres of trace. Its bottom resistor goes to the quiet analog ground at the IC, not into the noisy power return.

I keep the whole thing away from the switch node and the inductor, on the opposite side of the IC if I can, because both couple into it. A femtofarad-scale capacitance from the switch node is enough: with 0.1 pF to the switch node and 5 pF at the pin, a 12 V edge puts about a quarter of a volt on a pin that's regulating to 0.8 V. For the same reason I never run the FB tap trace under or beside the inductor, and I cross anything else at right angles.

If a long run is needed, I make it the sense line from the output, which is low impedance and driven by the output capacitors, and keep the tap short. I take that sense line from the load side of the output capacitors, ideally at the load, so the regulator corrects for drop, and route it as a quiet trace, ideally over the ground plane. Compensation and feed-forward parts hug their pins the same way. If the layout is cramped, I lower the divider resistance a bit, trading quiescent current for noise immunity."
:::

::: technical
**Divider.** $V_{FB} = V_{out}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$. Example: $V_{ref}=0.8$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V: $R_1 = 31.6$ kΩ, $R_2 = 10$ kΩ gives 3.33 V. Thevenin impedance at the tap: $R_1\parallel R_2 = 7.6$ kΩ.

**Capacitive pickup.** With coupling $C_c$ from SW and total pin/stray capacitance $C_p$ at the tap, a SW edge of $\Delta V_{SW}=12$ V produces $\Delta V_{FB}=\Delta V_{SW}C_c/(C_c+C_p)$:

| $C_c$ | $C_p = 5$ pF |
|---|---|
| 20 fF | 48 mV |
| 100 fF | 0.235 V |
| 500 fF | 1.09 V |

The step then decays with $\tau = R_{th}(C_c+C_p) = 7.6\ \text{k}\Omega\times5.1\ \text{pF} = 39$ ns. A lower-impedance divider (e.g. 0.76 kΩ) shortens that to 4 ns: the peak, set by the charge ratio, is unchanged, but the error amplifier and the modulator see far less of it.

**Magnetic pickup.** $v = M\,di/dt$: a loop with $M=10$ nH near a 4.7 µH inductor ($1.85$ A/µs) picks up 18.5 mV ([[q0551]]). Keep the loop area (divider, trace, return) small and away.

**Layout rules.**
1. Divider at the FB pin; $R_2$ return to AGND/quiet ground near the IC; $R_1\to R_2\to$ pin adjacent, tap trace < 5 mm.
2. Long run, if any, on the low-impedance side: the sense line from $V_{out}$ to $R_1$, routed away from SW/L and over a plane; consider a ground guard trace beside it.
3. Sense point at the load side of $C_{out}$ (or at the load with remote sense), not at the inductor side where ripple is largest.
4. Keep FB on the opposite side of the IC from SW/BOOT; cross other nets at 90° on a different layer.
5. Feed-forward capacitor across $R_1$ and compensation parts hug their pins.
6. No vias from the tap if avoidable; if used, a plane must be below.
7. Keep the area under the FB trace solid plane; keep SW copper off the layer adjacent.

**Accuracy.** Resistor tolerance (1% each) and reference tolerance set the output error; layout adds drop between the sense point and the load and noise-induced error.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Coupling capacitances depend on geometry; the 0.1 pF case is illustrative. Some controllers include internal filtering or specify limits on divider resistance; follow the datasheet.
:::

::: long
"The feedback network is the most sensitive part of a buck, so I route it as a victim.

The divider goes right at the FB pin, so the high-impedance part, the tap between the resistors and the pin, is just a few millimetres. High impedance is the key: with 31.6 kilohms over 10 kilohms the tap sits at about 7.6 kilohms, and the pin has a few picofarads. If I get even 0.1 picofarad of coupling from the switch node, a 12 volt edge puts 12 times 0.1 over 5.1, about a quarter of a volt, on a pin regulating to 0.8 volts. That's why feedback is kept far from the switch node, on the opposite side of the IC, and why I never run it under or beside the inductor, which couples magnetically too: 10 nanohenries of mutual inductance with 1.85 amps per microsecond is 18 millivolts.

Grounding: the bottom resistor returns to the analog ground at the IC, which joins the power ground at one point, so switching current doesn't flow in it.

If a long run is unavoidable, I make it the sense line from the output to the top of the divider. That side is low impedance, driven by the output capacitors, and the divider attenuates what it picks up. I route it over a plane, away from the noisy parts, maybe with a guard trace.

Where I sense matters too: after the output capacitors, at the load if I can, so the regulator corrects for the drop in the wiring, rather than at the inductor, where the ripple is.

If I'm cramped, I lower the divider resistance, accepting a bit more quiescent current, and I leave a pad for a feed-forward capacitor across the top resistor."
:::

::: terms
- **FB node (tap)**: the junction of the divider resistors and the FB pin; high impedance.
- **Thevenin impedance**: $R_1\parallel R_2$ seen at the tap.
- **Coupling capacitance ($C_c$)**: parasitic capacitance between an aggressor and the tap.
- **Guard trace**: a grounded trace beside a sensitive line to intercept coupled fields.
- **Remote sense**: sensing the output voltage at the load.
- **Feed-forward capacitor**: a capacitor across $R_1$ that adds phase lead to the loop.
- **AGND**: the quiet analog ground reference.
- **Mutual inductance ($M$)**: magnetic coupling between two loops.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "keep the feedback trace short and away from the inductor."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the impedance reason, with the capacitive-divider pickup number (0.1 pF → 0.24 V) and the magnetic number.
- Distinguishes the high-impedance tap (keep tiny) from the low-impedance sense line (can run longer).
- Specifies sense point (after $C_{out}$, ideally at the load) and why.
- Mentions grounding (AGND), crossing at 90°, planes/guards, feed-forward and compensation parts.
- Knows the trade-off of divider resistance versus noise and quiescent current.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the divider near the load and running a long trace to the FB pin.** That long trace is the high-impedance tap. Put the divider at the pin and run the sense line.
- **Routing FB under or next to the inductor or switch node.**
- **Sensing at the inductor's output pad.** Ripple and drop distort the measurement; sense after the capacitors.
- **Returning $R_2$ to a noisy ground** (power return near the hot loop).
- **Using very large divider resistors** (hundreds of kΩ to MΩ) to save current: leakage, bias current and noise pickup then dominate.
- **Forgetting leakage and flux.** Board contamination across a high-Z node causes offsets; clean the board or guard.
:::

::: deep
## A node defined by impedance

The FB pin looks at a divider, and what it senses is the voltage at the tap, so the question is how easy it is to disturb that voltage. A noise current $i_n$ injected into the tap produces $v_n = i_n R_{th}$ (for slow noise) where $R_{th}=R_1\parallel R_2$. For fast edges the capacitive divider formed by $C_c$ and $C_p$ sets the initial step:

$$\Delta V_{FB} = \Delta V_{SW}\frac{C_c}{C_c + C_p}$$

Figure: A tiny capacitance $C_c$ from the switch node injects a step into the tap, divided against the pin capacitance $C_p$. The divider's Thevenin resistance (7.6 kΩ) then sets how fast the step decays: $\tau = 7.6\ \text{k}\Omega\times5.1\ \text{pF}\approx39$ ns.

Numbers for a 12 V edge:

- $C_c=20$ fF → 48 mV
- $C_c=100$ fF → 0.235 V
- $C_c=500$ fF → 1.09 V

For comparison 100 fF is about the capacitance between two 0.2 mm traces running parallel for a few millimetres at a 0.2 mm gap. On an 0.8 V reference with ±1% tolerance, a 0.235 V glitch is not a small error; though it is brief and the error amplifier's bandwidth filters it, it shows up as modulator jitter and sometimes subharmonic-like behaviour or output ripple at harmonics of $f_{sw}$.

Lower impedance helps duration, not peak: the peak is the charge ratio $C_c/(C_c+C_p)$. With R divided by 10 (0.76 kΩ), $\tau$ becomes 3.9 ns. The glitch's area, roughly $0.235\ \text{V}\times 39\ \text{ns} = 9.1$ nV·s, falls ten-fold, and the error amplifier's response to it falls accordingly.

## Magnetic pickup

The divider, its trace and its return form a loop. Flux from the inductor through that loop induces $v = M\,di/dt$. With $M = 10$ nH and the inductor slope $1.85$ A/µs: 18.5 mV; $M = 1$ nH gives 1.9 mV ([[q0551]]). The remedies are distance, orthogonal orientation, small loop area (route the tap and the ground return together, using the plane), and shielded inductors.

## Which part is long?

A divider has two kinds of wire:

1. **The tap** (junction of $R_1$, $R_2$ and the FB pin): impedance 7.6 kΩ, sensitive, to be kept as small as possible, a few millimetres.
2. **The sense line** from $V_{out}$ to the top of $R_1$: driven by the output capacitor's low impedance (milliohms at the switching frequency), so noise injected onto it is a small fraction of $V_{out}$ and is further divided by $R_2/(R_1+R_2)=0.24$ before the pin.

So if the sense point must be far (at the load), put the divider at the IC and run the long wire as the sense line; do not put the divider at the load and run a long tap wire back to the pin.

## Where to sense

The regulator sets the voltage at its sense point, so the sense point should be where you want the correct voltage. Options:

- **At the output capacitor (load side)**: simple; excludes inductor ripple; but not the drop to the load.
- **At the load (remote sense)**: corrects drop in the supply and return wiring. Add a small RC or follow the controller's recommendation to keep the loop stable ([[q0543]]); route the sense pair with its own ground reference.
- **At the inductor output pad**: avoid; ripple and the ESR drop are in the sense signal.

For output ripple measurement, use the capacitor's pads and a short ground spring, not the sense trace.

## Ground, feed-forward and compensation

$R_2$'s ground end should go to AGND, near the IC's AGND pin or exposed pad, so the tap's reference is the same quiet reference as the IC. If $R_2$ returns to a noisy plane region, the switching return current creates an offset that appears in the tap. A feed-forward capacitor across $R_1$ (a few tens of pF) adds a high-frequency path from $V_{out}$ to FB, which helps the loop but also pipes ripple in; keep the same short routing. Compensation components (R, C at the COMP pin) are high-impedance nodes too: put them right at the pin, with returns to AGND.

## Practical routing recipe

1. Place $R_1$/$R_2$ within 3 to 5 mm of the FB pin on the side of the IC away from SW and BOOT.
2. Draw the tap trace as a short, narrow (0.15 to 0.25 mm) line, with solid ground plane below and no other nets close.
3. $R_2$'s other end to AGND with two vias to the plane, or direct to the IC's ground pad.
4. Route the sense line from the load-side of the output capacitors (or the load) to $R_1$'s top, keeping at least a few mm from the inductor and SW; use a guard trace or place the line between ground pours stitched to the plane.
5. Check crossings: cross SW or inductor-related nets only at 90° on a different layer.
6. Probe FB on a bench with a low-capacitance probe and a spring ground, and check for edges at SW's frequency.

## When divider resistance should change

Large resistors (hundreds of kΩ) minimise quiescent current in a battery system but make the tap very sensitive: pickup duration scales with $R_{th}$, and the FB pin's own bias current (tens of nA) adds an offset $I_{bias}R_{th}$ (50 nA × 8 kΩ = 0.4 mV is negligible; 50 nA × 800 kΩ = 40 mV is not). Small resistors (a few kΩ) are robust but waste current: a 3.33 V output across 4.16 kΩ total draws 0.8 mA (versus 80 µA across 41.6 kΩ), which at light load is a significant fraction. Datasheets give a recommended range for the divider resistors; follow it.

::: applications
- **Buck and boost regulators** with external dividers on the feedback pin (most adjustable-output parts).
- **LED drivers** that regulate a current-sense voltage on a high-impedance FB.
- **Low-noise supplies for ADC/RF** where the feedback is kept clean to avoid spurs.
- **Remote-sense supplies** where the sense line runs to the load.
- **Battery-powered designs** that use high-value dividers and must manage leakage and pickup.
:::

::: assumptions
The numbers assume a buck with an 0.8 V reference, a 31.6 kΩ/10 kΩ divider, a 12 V switch node with 3 ns edges, 5 pF pin capacitance, and a 4.7 µH inductor; other parts differ. Some controllers (current-mode, constant-on-time) have different noise sensitivity, and some have internal feedback dividers (fixed-output parts) where only the sense line matters. For remote sense, the regulator's recommendations for series resistance and filtering override the generic guidance. Hysteretic or ripple-based regulators sense the output ripple deliberately, so their feedback layout follows different rules. The coupling capacitance values are illustrative.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the feedback trace more sensitive than the output trace?
Impedance and precision. The output node is driven by capacitors with milliohm impedance, so injected current barely moves it. The tap is several kΩ and is compared with a reference to about a percent; a few femtofarads from the switch node move it by tens to hundreds of mV on every edge. Sensitivity scales with the node's impedance and the precision required.

### Where exactly should you take the feedback sense from?
From the point where you want the voltage to be right. That's at the load, or at least after the output capacitors, so the regulator corrects for drop between its inductor and that point, and ignores inductor ripple. Sensing at the inductor output pad includes ripple, and sensing at the IC ignores wiring drop to the load.

### The tap trace must be 20 mm because the layout is cramped. What do you do?
Reduce the impedance (smaller divider resistors), add a ground guard or route it on an inner layer over a plane, keep it perpendicular to anything noisy, add a small capacitor from the tap to ground (a few pF to tens of pF) if the datasheet permits, and verify on the bench. Better: rearrange so the divider sits at the pin and the long run is the sense line.

### How does a feed-forward capacitor change the layout?
It connects $V_{out}$ to the FB tap at high frequency, so it brings the output ripple into the tap on purpose; it must be placed across $R_1$ at the pin so the extra connection doesn't lengthen the high-Z node. Noise on the output is then also coupled in, so keep the output node quiet by sensing after the capacitors.

### What do you do if the FB pin shows a spike every switching edge on the scope?
First confirm it's not probe artefact (short ground spring). Then identify the aggressor by moving or shielding: check where the FB trace runs relative to SW, BOOT and the inductor; shorten the tap; move the divider; add a ground pour or guard; lower the divider resistance; make the inductor shielded. Re-measure after each change.
:::
