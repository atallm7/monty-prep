::: basics
### In plain words
SPI does not define any voltage levels. The voltages are whatever the chips on the bus use. Each output drives its own supply voltage for a high and ground for a low. When they differ, you must check each wire in its own direction, and you may need a level translator.

### Picture it
Imagine two people talking, each at their own volume. The supply voltage is how loud a person speaks. A listener has a quietest sound they will still hear as a "yes", and a loudest sound they can take without being hurt. If a loud speaker talks to a listener who can only handle quiet sounds, the listener is damaged. If a quiet speaker talks to a listener who needs loud sounds, the message is missed. A level translator is a go-between who repeats each message at the right volume, in both directions.

### Words you need
- **I/O supply**: the voltage a chip's input and output pins run from. It sets that chip's high level.
- **Input high threshold**: the lowest voltage a receiver reliably reads as high. It is often about 70% of its supply.
- **Absolute maximum rating**: the highest voltage a pin can see without damage. For many pins it is the supply plus about 0.3 V.
- **Level translator**: a chip that converts signals between two supply voltages. See [[q0877]].

### The simplest example
A 3.3 V microcontroller talks to a 1.8 V flash chip. The microcontroller's 3.3 V high goes into a flash pin that tolerates only about 2.1 V. Going the other way, the flash's 1.8 V high must be read by the microcontroller, which needs $0.7 \times 3.3 = 2.31$ V. So the flash's high may not be recognized. Both directions fail, so you add a fast level translator. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers more cases, translators, and timing cost.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know that **SPI defines no logic levels at all**, and can still answer usefully. The interviewer wants:

1. SPI is a convention; levels follow each device's I/O supply: typically 1.8 V, 2.5 V, 3.3 V or 5 V CMOS push-pull.
2. Thresholds are the devices' own $V_{IL}/V_{IH}/V_{OL}/V_{OH}$ (roughly 0.3 and 0.7 of $V_{DD}$ for CMOS, or TTL-style fixed values on older 5 V parts).
3. Contrast with I2C: no pull-up rail defines the high; every output drives its own rail.
4. What to do when master and slave have different supplies: level translators with the right direction per line (MISO reversed), fast enough for the clock.
:::

::: why
Mixed-voltage SPI is everywhere: a 3.3 V MCU talking to a 1.8 V flash or display, or a 5 V legacy part. A wrong assumption either overstresses a pin or gives a high that isn't recognised. Interviewers like this question because it checks whether you read datasheets for $V_{IH}$ rather than assuming "logic is logic".
:::

::: answer
"SPI doesn't specify logic levels. It's just a convention for the four signals, so the levels are whatever the devices' I/O supplies are. In practice it's push-pull CMOS at 3.3 V, 1.8 V, 2.5 V or 5 V, with a driver pulling to its own rail for a high and to ground for a low. So at 3.3 V a high is around 3.3 V and a low is near 0, and a receiver typically calls a high anything over about 70% of its supply and a low anything under about 30%, though the exact numbers, and whether 3.3 V is enough for a 5 V part, come from each device's datasheet.

That's different from I2C, where the high is set by the pull-up rail. In SPI each output drives its own rail, so if master and slave are at different voltages you have to check both directions: the master's high has to satisfy the slave's input threshold and not exceed its maximum rating, and the slave's output has to satisfy the master's.

When they don't match, I'd use a level translator, which for SPI means a push-pull, directional one: three lines go master to slave and MISO goes back the other way. I'd choose one fast enough for the clock, since their propagation delay eats into the timing budget. The single-FET shifter that works for I2C isn't a good fit at SPI speeds."
:::

::: technical
**No standard.** SPI has no electrical specification; each device's datasheet defines $V_{IL}$, $V_{IH}$, $V_{OL}$, $V_{OH}$ relative to its I/O supply $V_{DDIO}$.

**Typical CMOS numbers** (device-dependent; check the datasheet):

| $V_{DDIO}$ | $V_{IL}$ (max) | $V_{IH}$ (min) | $V_{OH}$ (typ) | $V_{OL}$ (typ) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1.8 V | ≈0.54–0.63 V | ≈1.17–1.26 V | ≈1.8 V | ≈0 V |
| 2.5 V | ≈0.75–0.8 V | ≈1.7–1.75 V | ≈2.5 V | ≈0 V |
| 3.3 V | ≈0.8–0.99 V | ≈2.0–2.31 V | ≈3.3 V | ≈0 V |
| 5 V (CMOS) | ≈1.5 V | ≈3.5 V | ≈5 V | ≈0 V |
| 5 V (TTL-compatible inputs) | 0.8 V | 2.0 V | ≈3.5–4.5 V | ≈0.4 V |

**Receiver rule.** For each link, $V_{OH,drv}\ge V_{IH,rcv}$, $V_{OL,drv}\le V_{IL,rcv}$ with margin, and $V_{OH,drv}\le V_{max,rcv}$.

**Mixed voltages.** Example: 3.3 V MCU, 1.8 V flash. MCU high 3.3 V exceeds the flash's maximum input (typically $V_{DDIO}+0.3$ V = 2.1 V): fail. Flash high 1.8 V into the MCU: $V_{IH}=0.7\times3.3=2.31$ V > 1.8 V: fail. A translator is required in both directions.

**Translators.** Use push-pull buffers: unidirectional (e.g. dedicated multi-channel translators with fixed direction, some with a direction pin) or auto-direction types sized for push-pull speeds; check the data rate rating (often 20 to 100+ MHz) and the propagation delay (a few ns). Open-drain/single-FET shifters ([[q0879]]) rely on pull-up edges and are too slow for most SPI clocks.

**Edge rates and noise.** Push-pull drivers give fast edges (1 to 5 ns), so SPI is more sensitive to ringing than I2C; use short traces and source resistors ([[q0767]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Threshold ranges in the table are typical families; individual parts can differ (for example LVCMOS vs LVTTL inputs). Use the device datasheet's $V_{IL}/V_{IH}$ rather than the ranges above.
:::

::: long
"SPI is a convention, not a standard, so it doesn't define logic levels. In practice it's push-pull CMOS signals at whatever I/O voltage the chips run from: 3.3 V is the most common today, then 1.8 V and 2.5 V on low-power parts, and 5 V on older ones. A driver pulls to its own supply rail for a high and to ground for a low, and a receiver treats anything above roughly 70% of its supply as high and below roughly 30% as low, though the exact numbers are in each datasheet. Older 5 V TTL-compatible parts used 0.8 V and 2.0 V thresholds instead.

The contrast with I2C is worth saying: in I2C the pull-up rail defines the high. In SPI each output drives its own rail. So the compatibility check is per signal and per direction. The master's SCLK, MOSI and CS have to be valid highs for the slave and below its maximum rating, and the slave's MISO has to be a valid high for the master.

Take a 3.3 V MCU with a 1.8 V flash. The MCU's 3.3 V high exceeds the flash's input rating of around 2.1 V, so it could damage it. The flash's 1.8 V high is below the MCU's 2.3 V threshold, so it might not be read. So I'd put a level translator in. For SPI it needs to be push-pull and fast, so a directional buffer for SCLK, MOSI and CS in one direction and MISO in the other, or an auto-direction translator rated for the data rate. I'd also add its propagation delay, a few nanoseconds each way, to the timing budget, because the MISO round trip includes two translator delays. I wouldn't use the single-transistor shifter from I2C; it's built for open-drain lines with pull-ups and is too slow.

Finally, because the edges are fast, I'd check signal integrity: short traces, source resistors and a ground reference."
:::

::: terms
- **Logic level**: voltage range interpreted as high or low.
- **$V_{IH}$ / $V_{IL}$**: input thresholds.
- **$V_{OH}$ / $V_{OL}$**: output levels when driving high/low.
- **$V_{DDIO}$**: I/O supply of a device, which sets its logic levels.
- **LVCMOS / LVTTL**: low-voltage CMOS and TTL-compatible input families.
- **5 V tolerant**: input can safely see 5 V while powered at a lower supply.
- **Level translator**: circuit linking two voltage domains. See [[q0877]].
- **Propagation delay ($t_{pd}$)**: delay through a translator, part of the timing budget.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **"SPI is 3.3 V logic."** It's whatever the devices use; check each.
- **Checking only one direction.** The slave's MISO must also be valid for the master.
- **Using an I2C-style FET shifter** on SPI. It needs open-drain edges and is too slow for most SPI clocks.
- **Relying on "5 V tolerant" without reading the spec.** Tolerance may apply only when the device is powered.
- **Forgetting the translator's delay** in the MISO timing budget ([[q0770]]).
- **Leaving a translator's enable/direction pin floating**, which can leave CS undefined at boot.
:::

::: deep
## Why SPI has no defined levels

I2C is a specification (NXP UM10204), so its thresholds are given ([[q0762]]). SPI began as a block in Motorola microcontrollers; nobody standardised the electrical side. So levels depend on what the silicon is built from: a modern MCU or flash uses CMOS I/O at a chosen $V_{DDIO}$, and a legacy part may use 5 V TTL-compatible I/O.

CMOS push-pull output: with the PMOS on, the pin is within a few tens of millivolts to a couple hundred millivolts of $V_{DD}$ (depending on load); with the NMOS on it's near 0 V. So $V_{OH}\approx V_{DD}$ and $V_{OL}\approx0$. A typical CMOS input switches around $V_{DD}/2$ and datasheets guarantee $V_{IL}\approx0.3V_{DD}$ and $V_{IH}\approx0.7V_{DD}$ (families vary).

## The per-link check

For each signal, with driver $d$ and receiver $r$:

1. $V_{OH,d}\ge V_{IH,r}$ (a valid high arrives).
2. $V_{OL,d}\le V_{IL,r}$ (a valid low arrives).
3. $V_{OH,d}\le V_{max,r}$ (the receiver survives it).

The four SPI links:

| Signal | Driver | Receiver |
|---|---|---|
| SCLK | master | slave |
| MOSI | master | slave |
| CS | master | slave |
| MISO | slave | master |

## Worked mixed-voltage checks

**Case 1: 3.3 V master, 1.8 V flash, no translator.**
- SCLK/MOSI/CS: $V_{OH}=3.3$ V into a 1.8 V pin whose maximum is typically $V_{DDIO}+0.3=2.1$ V: **overstress**.
- MISO: flash $V_{OH}=1.8$ V into a 3.3 V master with $V_{IH}=0.7\times3.3=2.31$ V: **not recognised** as a high.

**Case 2: 1.8 V master, 3.3 V slave.**
- Master 1.8 V into a 3.3 V slave with $V_{IH}=2.31$ V: **not recognised**.
- Slave 3.3 V into a 1.8 V master: **overstress** unless 3.3 V tolerant.

**Case 3: 3.3 V master, 5 V slave with TTL-compatible inputs ($V_{IH}=2.0$ V).** Master's 3.3 V is recognised. Slave's MISO ($V_{OH}\approx3.5$ to 5 V) into a 3.3 V master: needs 5 V tolerance or a resistor divider/translator.

**Case 4: 3.3 V master, 5 V CMOS slave ($V_{IH}=3.5$ V).** Master's 3.3 V is below 3.5 V: **not recognised**. Needs a translator.

These are the same checks as I2C's ([[q0762]]), but per direction rather than via one pull-up rail.

## Worked example: reading a 1.8 V part from a 3.3 V master, both ways

Suppose the slave is a 1.8 V sensor with inputs that tolerate $V_{DDIO}+0.3$ V = 2.1 V, $V_{IH}=0.7\times1.8=1.26$ V, $V_{IL}=0.54$ V, and the master is a 3.3 V MCU.

- **Master to slave**: a 3.3 V high is 1.2 V above the slave's absolute limit. Fix by translating, or (for slow, one-way lines like CS) a divider such as 1 kΩ over 1.2 kΩ: $3.3\times1.2/2.2=1.8$ V. Its output impedance is about 545 Ω, which with 5 pF is a 2.7 ns time constant (about 6 ns 10–90%). That is fine for CS and for clocks of a few MHz, but at 20 MHz it eats a noticeable slice of the 25 ns half-period, and with more pin and trace capacitance it gets worse quickly.
- **Slave to master**: a 1.8 V high is 0.51 V short of the master's 2.31 V threshold. A divider can't fix this; you need active translation (or an MCU input that supports a lower $V_{IH}$ setting, found on some parts).

This asymmetry (you can sometimes divide down, but never up) is why a proper translator is the default answer for mixed-voltage SPI.

## Choosing a translator for SPI

| Option | Fit |
|---|---|
| Dedicated fixed-direction push-pull translators (e.g. dual-supply buffer families) | Good: fast (tens of MHz+), lowest delay; direction fixed per channel (MISO reversed) |
| Auto-direction push-pull translators | Good if rated for the data rate; slightly more delay/ edge sensitivity |
| Simple buffers in a 74LVC-style family powered at the slave's rail | Works when inputs are 5 V tolerant; check the input voltage |
| Single-FET (BSS138-type) shifter with pull-ups | Poor for SPI; edges limited by pull-up RC ([[q0879]]) |
| Resistor divider on master-to-slave lines | Only for slow signals, down-conversion only (not MISO) |

Check: supply ranges on both rails, **max data rate** (it must exceed your SCLK), propagation delay (a few ns), output enable/boot behaviour (so CS is not floating when the translator is disabled), and drive strength into the trace.

## Where the delay goes

A translator adds $t_{pd}$ on each crossing. For MISO the round trip is: SCLK passes through the translator to the slave, the slave's $t_V$ later data comes back through it again. With 5 ns each way, that is 10 ns extra, which at 20 MHz (half-period 25 ns) already consumes 40% of the half-period. See [[q0770]] for the full budget.

::: followups
### Is SPI 3.3 V or 5 V?
Neither is mandated. It's whatever the devices use. 3.3 V is the most common today; 1.8 V appears on low-power devices (flash, sensors, displays); 5 V on legacy parts and Arduino-class boards. The compatibility check is per link and per direction.

### Why can't I use an I2C level shifter for SPI?
The single-FET shifter works with open-drain signals: one side pulls low through the FET, and each side's pull-up creates the high. SPI lines are push-pull and fast; the shifter's pull-up RC (kΩ with tens of pF) limits edge speed to a few MHz, and it adds loading. Use a push-pull translator rated for the clock.

### What does "5 V tolerant" mean for an SPI input?
That the input can withstand up to 5 V even when its own supply is lower (often 3.3 V), usually when powered (some parts also when unpowered). It doesn't mean the input will recognise a 3.3 V high as such: that depends on $V_{IH}$. Check both.

### How do you level-shift MISO?
Opposite direction to the others: a translator channel configured slave-to-master, with the slave's rail on the input side. Also, in a multi-slave bus where MISO is tri-stated, the translator must handle the high-Z state (use a part with a bus-hold or add a pull resistor).

### How would the translator affect the maximum SCLK?
Its propagation delay (a few ns each way) adds directly to the MISO round-trip path and eats into the half-period. With 10 ns of round-trip translator delay, a slave $t_V$ of 12 ns and 5 ns of master setup, the half-period must be at least 27 ns, so about 18 MHz at best in mode 0. Choose a fast translator, or use the MCU's MISO sample-delay feature if it has one.
