::: basics
### In plain words
TTL and CMOS are two families of digital logic chips. TTL is built from bipolar transistors and draws current all the time. CMOS is built from paired MOSFETs and draws almost nothing when idle. Their voltage thresholds for "high" and "low" differ, so connecting them needs care.

### Picture it
Imagine two languages that use the same words but draw the line between "loud" and "quiet" at different volumes. A listener from one may miss the other's "loud" if it stops just short of their line. That is what can happen at a TTL-to-CMOS connection. The fix is a translator, a chip designed to accept one language and speak the other.

### Words you need
- **TTL**: transistor-transistor logic, a bipolar family such as 74LS.
- **CMOS**: logic built from complementary MOSFETs. See [[q0105]].
- **Input thresholds**: the lowest voltage an input is sure to read as "high", and the highest it is sure to read as "low". In between, the reading is not guaranteed. See [[q0742]].
- **Noise margin**: the gap between what an output guarantees and what an input needs.
- **74HCT**: CMOS with TTL-compatible input thresholds.

### The simplest example
A 5 V TTL output only guarantees at least 2.4 V for a high. A 74HC input on 5 V needs about 3.5 V, which is 70% of its supply, before it is sure to read a high (datasheets often quote 3.15 V, measured at a 4.5 V supply). So the TTL signal might not be read as high at all. A 74HCT input accepts anything above about 2.0 V as high, so it works. The usual choice today is CMOS, since it is faster and uses less power.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page compares power, input currents and noise margins in more detail.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know the two families well enough to **interface them safely**, not just recite "CMOS is lower power". The interviewer is probing:

1. Device level: TTL is built from bipolar transistors (multi-emitter input, totem-pole output); CMOS from complementary MOSFETs.
2. Logic levels and noise margins: TTL's fixed 0.8 V / 2.0 V input thresholds vs CMOS thresholds that scale with $V_{DD}$.
3. Power: TTL draws static current; CMOS draws mostly dynamic power.
4. Input behaviour: TTL inputs source real current and float high; CMOS inputs are high impedance and must never float.
5. The practical punchline: a TTL output may not drive a 5 V CMOS input reliably, which is why 74HCT exists.
:::

::: why
You still meet "TTL" everywhere: datasheets say "TTL-compatible inputs", USB-serial cables are called "TTL serial", and lots of 3.3 V parts are specified with LVTTL thresholds. When you hook a 5 V sensor to a 3.3 V MCU, or a legacy board to a modern one, you're doing exactly the $V_{OH}$-vs-$V_{IH}$ comparison this question asks for. Getting it wrong gives intermittent bugs that depend on temperature and part lot. It also leads into logic levels ([[q0742]], [[q0743]]) and level shifting ([[q0877]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **BJT switching**: TTL uses saturating (or Schottky-clamped) bipolar transistors, which need base current. See [[q0085]] and [[q0087]].
- **CMOS inverter**: complementary MOSFETs with insulated gates and near-zero static current. See [[q0105]] and [[q0106]].
- **Logic thresholds**: $V_{IH}$/$V_{IL}$ are what a receiver needs; $V_{OH}$/$V_{OL}$ are what a driver guarantees. See [[q0742]].
- **Noise margin**: $NM_H = V_{OH,min} - V_{IH,min}$, $NM_L = V_{IL,max} - V_{OL,max}$.
- **Fan-out**: how many inputs one output can drive; set by DC current in TTL, by capacitance in CMOS.
- **Static vs dynamic power**: TTL burns power continuously; CMOS power is $\propto C V^2 f$. See [[q0110]].
:::

::: answer
"TTL is transistor-transistor logic, built from bipolar transistors: a multi-emitter input transistor and a totem-pole output with an NPN pulling up and an NPN pulling down. CMOS is built from complementary PMOS and NMOS transistors.

The differences fall out of that. Power: TTL draws current all the time, a couple of milliwatts per gate for 74LS, while CMOS draws almost nothing static and only pays $CV^2f$ when it switches. Inputs: a TTL input has to source current out of the pin when it's low, up to 1.6 mA for standard TTL, and floats high if left open. A CMOS input is just a gate capacitor, basically zero DC current, but it must never be left floating.

Logic levels are the big practical one. TTL runs at 5 V with fixed thresholds: inputs see anything above 2.0 V as high and below 0.8 V as low, and outputs only guarantee 2.4 V high, because the totem pole loses a couple of junction drops. CMOS outputs swing rail to rail and the input thresholds scale with supply, roughly 70% and 30% of $V_{DD}$ for 74HC. So CMOS has much bigger noise margins, but a TTL output's 2.4 V doesn't meet a 5 V 74HC input's 3.15–3.5 V $V_{IH}$. That's why 74HCT exists: CMOS internals with TTL-compatible input thresholds.

For anything new I'd use CMOS; modern CMOS is faster and lower power. TTL mostly survives as a set of logic-level conventions."
:::

| Input current (low) | −1.6 mA | −0.4 mA | ±1 µA max | ±1 µA max |
| Static power / gate | ~10 mW | ~2 mW | µW-level (leakage) | µW-level + extra $\Delta I_{CC}$ for TTL-level inputs |
| Propagation delay | ~10 ns | ~10 ns | ~10 ns at 5 V | ~10–15 ns |

**Noise margins.** Standard TTL: $NM_H = 2.4-2.0 = 0.4$ V, $NM_L = 0.8-0.4 = 0.4$ V. 74HC at 4.5 V (4 mA load): $NM_H = 3.98-3.15 = 0.83$ V, $NM_L = 1.35-0.26 = 1.09$ V; at light load, about 1.25 V each.

**Why TTL levels are what they are.** The input threshold is set by junction drops: the multi-emitter transistor's base sits at about three $V_{BE}$ (≈2.1 V) when switching, so the input threshold is about $2.1 - 0.7 \approx 1.4$ V. The totem-pole output high is $V_{CC} - V_{BE(Q3)} - V_D - I R$, typically ~3.4 V, guaranteed only to 2.4 V.

**Interfacing at 5 V.**
- CMOS (HC) → TTL: $V_{OH} = 3.98 \ge 2.0$, $V_{OL} = 0.26 \le 0.8$. Works. Fan-out limited by current: 4 mA / 0.4 mA = 10 LS loads, 4 / 1.6 = 2 standard TTL loads.
- TTL → HC: $V_{OH,min} = 2.4$ V < $V_{IH} = 3.15$ V. **Not guaranteed.** Use 74HCT/ACT inputs, a pull-up resistor to 5 V, or a level translator.

**Speed.** Early 4000-series CMOS was slow (tens to over 100 ns). 74HC matched 74LS; 74AC/LVC/AUP and on-chip CMOS are far faster. TTL gates also have saturation storage delay unless Schottky-clamped.
:::

::: long
"TTL stands for transistor-transistor logic. It's built from bipolar transistors: the input is a multi-emitter NPN, there's a phase-splitter transistor, and the output is a totem pole, one NPN pulling up through a diode and one NPN pulling down. CMOS builds the same gates out of a PMOS pull-up and an NMOS pull-down.

I'd compare them on four things. First, power. TTL transistors need base current and the internal resistors are always conducting, so a 74LS gate burns around 2 mW even sitting still, and standard TTL about 10. CMOS only draws leakage when static, and pays $CV^2f$ when it switches. At high frequency the gap narrows, but for most logic CMOS wins by orders of magnitude.

Second, inputs. A TTL input low has to sink current out of the input transistor's emitter, 1.6 mA for standard TTL, 0.4 for LS, which is why TTL fan-out is quoted as a number of loads. An open TTL input floats high. A CMOS input is a gate capacitor with microamp-level leakage at worst, so fan-out is limited by capacitance, but an open CMOS input floats anywhere and can cause shoot-through current.

Third, logic levels, which is where real bugs come from. TTL is a 5 V family with fixed thresholds set by diode drops: above 2.0 V is high, below 0.8 V is low, and the output only guarantees 2.4 V high because the totem pole loses a couple of junction drops. CMOS outputs swing to the rails, and the input thresholds scale with supply, for 74HC about 70% and 30% of $V_{DD}$. So CMOS has bigger noise margins, but a TTL output's 2.4 volts doesn't meet a 5 V HC input's 3.15 to 3.5 volt requirement. You fix that with an HCT part, which is CMOS with TTL-level inputs, or a level shifter.

Fourth, speed and flexibility. Modern CMOS is as fast or faster and runs from about 1 V to 5 V. So for new designs I'd pick CMOS, and treat 'TTL' as a logic-level standard you check compatibility against."
:::

::: terms
- **TTL**: transistor-transistor logic, a bipolar logic family (7400 series and subfamilies like 74LS, 74S, 74F).
- **Totem-pole output**: TTL's push-pull stage, an upper NPN plus diode and a lower NPN, with a 130 Ω current limit.
- **Multi-emitter transistor**: TTL's input device; each emitter is one gate input.
- **74HC / 74HCT**: high-speed CMOS; the T variant has TTL-compatible input thresholds.
- **$V_{IH}$, $V_{IL}$, $V_{OH}$, $V_{OL}$**: the four logic-level specs that determine compatibility.
- **Noise margin**: how much noise a valid output can tolerate before the receiver misreads it.
- **Fan-out**: number of inputs one output can drive within spec.
- **LVTTL / LVCMOS**: 3.3 V (and lower) standards; LVTTL keeps the 0.8/2.0 V input thresholds.
- **Schottky clamping**: a Schottky diode across a BJT's base-collector junction to prevent deep saturation and speed up turn-off.
:::

::: exceptional
**Average answer:** "TTL uses BJTs, CMOS uses MOSFETs, CMOS uses less power."

**Exceptional answer:**
- States actual numbers: TTL $V_{IL}/V_{IH}$ = 0.8/2.0 V, $V_{OL}/V_{OH}$ = 0.4/2.4 V; 74HC thresholds ≈ 30%/70% of $V_{DD}$.
- Computes noise margins and shows CMOS's are larger.
- Explains *why* TTL levels are odd (junction drops in the input and totem-pole) rather than just listing them.
- Spots the interfacing trap: TTL $V_{OH}$ 2.4 V < HC $V_{IH}$ 3.15 V, and gives fixes (HCT, pull-up, translator).
- Covers input behaviour: TTL inputs float high and source current; CMOS inputs must be tied.
- Notes that modern CMOS also beats TTL on speed, and that "TTL levels" live on as LVTTL in 3.3 V parts.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"TTL is 5 V and CMOS is 3.3 V."** Those are supply conventions, not the families. 74HC runs at 5 V; LVTTL is a 3.3 V standard with TTL-style thresholds.
- **Assuming any 5 V logic output can drive any 5 V input.** TTL's guaranteed 2.4 V high doesn't meet 74HC's 3.15 V $V_{IH}$ at 4.5 V. Compare $V_{OH}$ vs $V_{IH}$ and $V_{OL}$ vs $V_{IL}$ every time.
- **Leaving CMOS inputs floating "because TTL inputs float high".** A floating CMOS input can sit mid-rail, draw crowbar current and oscillate. Tie it.
- **"CMOS is always lower power."** At high frequency and high load capacitance, CMOS dynamic power can approach TTL's per-gate power. CMOS still wins overall, but power depends on $f$.
- **"CMOS is slower."** True for the 4000 series in the 1970s, false for 74HC onward and certainly for modern CMOS.
:::

::: deep
## Two ways to build a gate

**TTL** came first as an integrated family: the 7400 series from the 1960s. Its building block is the bipolar junction transistor. **CMOS** (4000 series, then 74HC) uses complementary MOSFETs. Most differences between the families follow directly from the device physics: a BJT needs base *current* and has fixed junction drops of about 0.7 V; a MOSFET gate is an insulated *capacitor* and its output can reach the rails.

Figure: Q1 is the input transistor (its emitter is the input; NAND gates use a multi-emitter Q1). Q2 is a phase splitter that drives the totem pole: Q3 + diode pull the output up, Q4 pulls it down. The diode makes sure Q3 is off when Q4 is on. Every DC path here draws current, which is where TTL's static power comes from.

Walk through it:

- **Input low (A ≈ 0.2 V).** Current flows out of the input pin: from $V_{CC}$ through the 4 kΩ resistor and Q1's base-emitter junction into whatever is driving A. That's the input-low current, about $(5 - 0.7 - 0.2)/4\text{k} \approx 1$ mA (spec max 1.6 mA). Q1 steals base drive from Q2, so Q2 and Q4 are off; Q3 is driven through the 1.6 kΩ resistor and pulls the output high.
- **Input high (A > 2 V).** Q1's base-collector junction forward-biases and pushes current into Q2's base. Q2 and Q4 turn on, the output is pulled low to $V_{CE(sat)} \approx 0.2$ V, and Q3 is held off because its base sits only one $V_{BE}$ + $V_{CE(sat)}$ above ground, too low to also forward-bias Q3's junction *and* the diode.

**Why the threshold is ≈1.4 V.** For Q2 and Q4 to conduct, Q1's base must reach about $V_{BC(Q1)} + V_{BE(Q2)} + V_{BE(Q4)} \approx 3\times0.7 = 2.1$ V. The input is one $V_{BE}$ below that: $2.1 - 0.7 = 1.4$ V. The 0.8 V and 2.0 V specs bracket this threshold with margin for temperature and process.

**Why $V_{OH}$ is only ~3.4 V.** With the output high, Q3 is an emitter follower: $V_{OH} \approx V_{CC} - V_{R(1.6k)} - V_{BE(Q3)} - V_D \approx 5 - 0.1 - 0.7 - 0.7 \approx 3.4$ V typical, and under load and worst-case conditions it's guaranteed only to 2.4 V.

## Noise margins, worked

| Family | $NM_H = V_{OH} - V_{IH}$ | $NM_L = V_{IL} - V_{OL}$ |
|---|---|---|
| Standard TTL | $2.4 - 2.0 = 0.40$ V | $0.8 - 0.4 = 0.40$ V |
| 74LS | $2.7 - 2.0 = 0.70$ V | $0.8 - 0.5 = 0.30$ V |
| 74HC @ 4.5 V, 4 mA | $3.98 - 3.15 = 0.83$ V | $1.35 - 0.26 = 1.09$ V |
| 74HC @ 4.5 V, 20 µA | $4.4 - 3.15 = 1.25$ V | $1.35 - 0.1 = 1.25$ V |

CMOS margins are two to three times larger, and because the thresholds track $V_{DD}$, they stay proportionally large at 3.3 V or 2.5 V.

## Interfacing: the classic trap

**HC output → TTL input (both at 5 V):** fine. $V_{OH} = 3.98$ V clears 2.0 V, and $V_{OL} = 0.26$ V is below 0.8 V. The limit is current: a 74HC output specified at 4 mA can sink $4/0.4 = 10$ LS inputs but only $4/1.6 = 2.5$, so 2 standard TTL inputs.

**TTL output → HC input (both at 5 V):** *not guaranteed.* The TTL output's 2.4 V minimum high is below the 3.15 V that a 74HC input needs at 4.5 V (3.5 V at 5 V). It may work on the bench with a typical 3.4 V output, then fail hot or with a different lot. Fixes:

1. Use a **74HCT** (or 74ACT) receiver: CMOS with TTL thresholds (≈1.4 V switching point).
2. Add a **pull-up** (e.g. 1–10 kΩ to 5 V) on the TTL output to pull the high level toward 5 V.
3. Use a **level translator**, e.g. a 74LVC1T45-style translator for different supplies. See [[q0877]].

The same check applies to modern 3.3 V ↔ 5 V interfacing. A 3.3 V LVCMOS output (~3.1 V high) drives a 5 V HCT or TTL-level input fine, but not a 5 V HC input. And a 5 V output into a 3.3 V input needs a 5 V-tolerant pin or a translator, or you'll forward-bias the input's ESD diodes ([[q0112]]).

## Power and speed

TTL's power is mostly static. Its resistors and base currents conduct all the time, so a 74LS gate burns about 2 mW whether it toggles or not, and standard TTL about 10 mW. CMOS power is dynamic: $P = (C_{PD} + C_L)V_{CC}^2 f$.

**Worked example.** A 74HC gate with power-dissipation capacitance $C_{PD} \approx 20$ pF driving $C_L = 15$ pF at 5 V and 1 MHz: $P = 35\text{ pF}\times 25\times 10^6 = 0.875$ mW. It would match a 74LS gate's ~2 mW only at about 2.3 MHz, and at a typical glue-logic toggle rate of kHz it uses thousands of times less. Scaled down to on-chip capacitances of fF, the CMOS advantage becomes overwhelming.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> $C_{PD}$ is specified per part (typically tens of pF per gate for 74HC). The 2 mW and 10 mW per-gate TTL figures are the commonly quoted typical values for 74LS and standard 74xx.

On speed, TTL's weakness is saturation: a saturated BJT stores base charge that must be removed before it turns off. Schottky TTL (74S, 74LS) clamps the base-collector junction with a Schottky diode to avoid saturation, which is how 74LS achieved 74xx speed at a fifth of the power. Modern CMOS families (74LVC, 74AUP and on-chip logic) are far faster than any TTL.

## Where "TTL" survives

- **Datasheet language**: "TTL-compatible inputs" means $V_{IH} \le 2.0$ V and $V_{IL} \ge 0.8$ V.
- **LVTTL**: 3.3 V I/O standard keeping the 0.8/2.0 V input thresholds, common in FPGA I/O settings.
- **"TTL serial"**: a UART at logic levels (0 / 3.3 V or 5 V) as opposed to RS-232's ±V levels. The chips are CMOS; only the voltage convention is "TTL".
:::

::: applications
- **5 V legacy interface**: reading a 74LS-era bus or an old peripheral with a 74HCT245 buffer so the TTL-level highs are recognized.
- **3.3 V MCU driving a 5 V system**: a 74HCT or 74AHCT1G-style gate powered at 5 V accepts the 3.3 V output (≥2.0 V is high) and outputs full 5 V levels.
- **FPGA I/O standards**: choosing LVTTL vs LVCMOS33 in the constraints file changes drive and threshold settings.
- **USB-to-"TTL" serial adapters**: UART at 3.3 V or 5 V logic levels; check the target's $V_{IH}$ before connecting a 5 V adapter to a 3.3 V pin.
- **Unused-input handling**: on legacy TTL boards open inputs float high; on CMOS boards every unused input is tied to a rail.
- **Low-power products**: 74LVC/74AUP CMOS glue logic with µA quiescent current in battery devices.
:::

::: assumptions
- Numbers are for classic 5 V families (standard 74xx, 74LS) and 74HC/74HCT at $V_{CC} = 4.5$ V, the condition many datasheets use. At $V_{CC} = 5$ V, 74HC's $V_{IH}$ is 3.5 V, making the TTL → HC problem worse.
- "CMOS" here means standard CMOS logic families. On-chip CMOS logic and modern low-voltage families (LVC, AUP) have different thresholds that still scale with $V_{DD}$.
- Power comparisons assume typical toggle rates. At very high frequency with large loads, CMOS dynamic power rises and can rival TTL per gate.
- If the interviewer means "TTL levels vs CMOS levels" rather than the device families, focus on the threshold/noise-margin comparison and the interfacing rules; that's often what they really want.
- BiCMOS families (74ABT, 74BCT) blend both: bipolar outputs for drive, CMOS for low power.
:::

::: followups
### Can a 3.3 V CMOS output drive a 5 V TTL input?
Yes. A 3.3 V LVCMOS output gives roughly 2.4–3.3 V high depending on load, which clears TTL's 2.0 V $V_{IH}$, and its low (≤0.4 V) is well under 0.8 V. The receiving TTL input sources current when low (0.4 mA for LS), which a CMOS output can easily sink. It's the reverse direction, 5 V into a 3.3 V input, that needs care.

### Why does a floating TTL input read as high?
With nothing driving it, no current can flow out of Q1's emitter, so the 4 kΩ base resistor drives Q1's base-collector junction and turns Q2 and Q4 on, exactly as if the input were high. It's still bad practice: an open input is noise-sensitive and slower. Tie unused TTL inputs high through a resistor.

### What's the difference between 74HC and 74HCT?
Same CMOS process and outputs, different input thresholds. HC switches near $V_{CC}/2$ with $V_{IH} = 0.7V_{CC}$; HCT is designed to switch near 1.4 V with $V_{IH} = 2.0$ V and $V_{IL} = 0.8$ V, so it can accept TTL or 3.3 V CMOS outputs. HCT only operates at 4.5–5.5 V and draws a little extra supply current when its input sits at a TTL-level high like 2.4 V.

### Compute the noise margins of standard TTL.
$NM_H = V_{OH,min} - V_{IH,min} = 2.4 - 2.0 = 0.4$ V. $NM_L = V_{IL,max} - V_{OL,max} = 0.8 - 0.4 = 0.4$ V. So any noise larger than 0.4 V on a worst-case TTL signal can cause an error. 74HC at 4.5 V has about 0.8–1.25 V depending on load.

### What happens if a 5 V TTL output drives a 3.3 V CMOS input that isn't 5 V tolerant?
The TTL output's high (typically ~3.4 V, up to ~5 V with a pull-up) can exceed the 3.3 V input's absolute maximum ($V_{DD} + 0.3$ V or so). The input's upper ESD diode forward-biases and injects current into the 3.3 V rail. That can cause latch-up, back-power the 3.3 V rail, or degrade the part. Use a series resistor sized for the injection-current spec, a translator, or a 5 V-tolerant input. See [[q0112]].
:::
