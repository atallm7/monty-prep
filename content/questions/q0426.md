::: basics
### In plain words
A high-side switch sits between the supply and the load. An N-channel FET is the better switch, with lower resistance and cost than a P-channel one of the same size. The catch is that an N-channel FET turns on when its gate is a few volts above its source. On the high side the source is the output node, which rises to the supply when the FET is on. So the gate needs a voltage higher than the supply, which takes extra circuitry.

### Picture it
Picture a person on a platform that rises with the water level, trying to stay 10 steps above the platform. When the platform is at 24 meters, the person must be at 34 meters. A ladder fixed to the ground tops out at 24 and never gets them there. You need a ladder that rides up with the platform. The analogy leaves out that the extra height is not free: you pay in parts, board area and limits on how long the switch can stay on.

### Words you need
- **High-side switch**: the switch between the supply and the load. See [[q0421]].
- **Gate-source voltage ($V_{GS}$)**: the gate voltage measured against the source, not against ground.
- **On-resistance ($R_{DS(on)}$)**: the resistance of the FET when it is on. Lower means less heat.
- **Bootstrap**: a capacitor that is charged at low voltage, then lifted with the source to give the gate its extra voltage. See [[q0206]].
- **Charge pump**: a small circuit that makes a voltage above the supply, so the FET can stay on indefinitely.

### The simplest example
Take a 24 V supply and an NFET that needs 10 V of $V_{GS}$ to be fully on. When it conducts, its source is at 24 V, so the gate must be at $24 + 10 = 34$ V. A 10 V ground-referenced driver gives $V_{GS} = 10 - 24 = -14$ V, so the FET stays off. For the payoff, at 10 A a 10 mΩ NFET wastes $10^2 \times 0.01 = 1$ W, while a 25 mΩ PFET wastes 2.5 W. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page explains why NFETs conduct better, how bootstrap and charge pumps solve the gate problem, and the side effects: duty limit, start-up, droop, undershoot and dv/dt.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand **why the high side is the hard side**: an N-channel FET is the better switch, but its source is the switch node, so its gate must be driven above the supply. The interviewer wants the advantage with physics behind it (electron mobility) and the challenges as a list of consequences that you can solve:

1. Lower $R_{DS(on)}$ and gate charge for the same die area, and wider choice and lower cost than PFETs.
2. $V_{GS}=V_G-V_S$ with $V_S$ moving, so you need a gate supply that floats with the source: bootstrap, charge pump or isolated supply.
3. The side effects: duty-cycle limits, start-up, dv/dt immunity, negative undershoot, extra parts and layout.
:::

::: why
Every motor bridge, buck converter and inverter above a few watts has to decide between the "easy" PFET high side and the "efficient" NFET high side. At 10 A the difference between a 10 mΩ NFET and a 25 mΩ PFET of similar cost is 1.5 W of heat. But choosing the NFET means designing a floating gate drive, and that part is where bridges fail in practice: undershoot, dv/dt, bootstrap droop, start-up. This question tests whether you can weigh that trade.
:::

::: concepts
- **N vs P channel mobility**: electrons are 2 to 3 times more mobile than holes in silicon ([[q0070]], [[q0097]]).
- **$V_{GS}$ is relative to the source**: with the NFET source on the switch node, the gate must go above the supply ([[q0090]]).
- **Bootstrap capacitor**: a floating gate supply charged while the low side is on ([[q0206]]).
- **Charge pump**: generates a gate supply above the rail, enabling 100% duty ([[q0285]]).
- **Miller capacitance and dv/dt**: fast switch-node edges couple into gates ([[q0207]]).
- **PFET high side**: gate drive is simple but the part is larger and slower ([[q0204]], [[q0107]]).
:::

::: answer
"The big advantage is that an N-channel FET is simply a better switch. Electrons are two to three times more mobile than holes, so for the same die size an NFET has two to three times lower on-resistance and less gate charge. That means less conduction loss, faster switching, smaller and cheaper parts, and many more to choose from. At 10 amps, a 10 milliohm NFET dissipates 1 watt where a comparable-cost PFET at 25 milliohm dissipates 2.5.

The challenge is the gate drive. An NFET turns on when its gate is a few volts above its *source*. On the high side the source is the switch node, and when the FET is on that node is at the supply. So to turn on a high-side NFET in a 24 volt bridge the gate has to go to about 34 volts, higher than anything I have in the system. I need a floating gate supply that follows the switch node: usually a bootstrap capacitor charged through a diode while the low side is on, or a charge pump.

That brings the side effects. Bootstrap needs the low side to turn on periodically, which limits maximum duty cycle, and it can't hold the FET on forever because the capacitor droops, so motor drivers add a charge pump for 100% duty. Start-up is trickier because the capacitor starts empty. The switch node undershoots below ground and has fast dv/dt that can upset the level shifter. So it's more parts and more layout care, in exchange for efficiency."
:::

::: technical
**Why NFET is better.** For a MOSFET in triode, $R_{DS(on)}\approx\dfrac{L}{\mu C_{ox}W\,(V_{GS}-V_{th})}$. Silicon: $\mu_n\approx2$ to $3\,\mu_p$, so a PFET needs 2 to 3 times the width for the same $R_{DS(on)}$. Gate charge, $C_{iss}$ and $C_{oss}$ scale with area, so at equal $R_{DS(on)}$ the PFET has 2 to 3 times the capacitance and gate loss, and switches more slowly ([[q0204]]).

**Gate requirement.** High-side NFET: $V_{GS}=V_G-V_{SW}$. On: $V_{SW}\approx V_M$, so $V_G\approx V_M+V_{GS(on)}$ (10 V gate drive, 24 V rail: 34 V). Off: $V_G\approx V_{SW}$.

**Bootstrap.** $C_{boot}$ charges to $V_{DRV}-V_f$ when SW is low. Required: $C_{boot}\ge\dfrac{Q_g}{\Delta V}$ plus leakage. For $Q_g=40$ nC and 0.5 V droop: 80 nF minimum; use 10× margin (about 0.47 to 1 µF) and account for ceramic DC-bias derating.

**Duty-cycle limits.** Bootstrap needs a minimum off-time $t_{off,min}$ each cycle (to recharge): $D_{max}=1-t_{off,min}f_{sw}$. For 1 µs at 20 kHz: 98%. For 100% duty, a charge pump holds the gate supply, with only leakage ($\mu$A) to feed.

**Droop.** Holding on at $D=100\%$ with quiescent plus leakage of $I_q=100$ µA and a 1 µF bootstrap cap: $\Delta V=I_q t/C$, so 0.5 V droop in $5$ ms. The FET stays on only while the voltage is above $V_{th}$ plus margin.

**Other challenges.**
- Start-up: the bootstrap capacitor is empty until the low side has been on; pre-biased or high-side-only operation needs a charge pump or a precharge pulse.
- SW undershoot (to $-5$ V or lower from parasitic inductance) violates driver pin ratings and can over-charge the boot cap.
- dv/dt (5 to 50 V/ns) can shift the level-shifter or cause false turn-on through $C_{gd}$.
- High-side gate drive adds a diode, a capacitor, a level shifter, and layout constraints (small loop).
:::

::: long
"The advantage is mostly physics and economics. Electrons are about two to three times more mobile than holes in silicon, so a given area of silicon makes a much better N-channel FET than a P-channel FET: lower on-resistance, lower gate charge, lower output capacitance. In practice that means NFETs are available with 5 to 10 milliohms where a PFET at the same cost is 20 to 30. At 10 amps in a bridge leg that's the difference between 1 watt and 2.5 watts of heat, so the NFET also helps with thermal design and efficiency. They also switch faster, so you can run a higher frequency, and there's a much bigger catalogue to choose from.

The challenge is that an N-channel FET turns on when its gate is a few volts above its source. On the low side the source is ground and that's easy. On the high side the source is the switch node, which swings from ground to the supply voltage. When the FET is fully on, the source is at the supply, so the gate must be at the supply plus 5 to 10 volts. In a 24 volt system that's 34 volts, and I don't have that rail.

So I need a gate supply that floats with the switch node. The classic solution is a bootstrap: a diode and a capacitor. While the low side is on, the switch node is near ground and the capacitor charges to the driver supply through the diode. When the high side turns on, the node rises and the capacitor, still holding that voltage, lifts the gate with it.

The cost is a list of side effects. The capacitor needs refreshing, so there's a maximum duty cycle, and it can't hold the FET on forever because it droops. For motor drives that need 100% duty I use a charge pump instead or as well. At start-up the capacitor is empty. The switch node can undershoot below ground and has big dv/dt, so the driver needs to handle negative voltages and noise immunity. And the layout has to keep the bootstrap loop tight. Integrated half-bridge or motor drivers hide most of this."
:::

::: terms
- **High-side switch**: the device between the supply and the load; its source (NFET) is at the switch node.
- **Bootstrap**: a diode-capacitor circuit that creates a floating gate supply referenced to the switch node.
- **Charge pump**: a switched-capacitor circuit that generates a voltage above the rail; allows 100% duty.
- **$V_{GS}$ overdrive**: $V_{GS}-V_{th}$, setting $R_{DS(on)}$.
- **Maximum duty cycle**: limit from the bootstrap refresh interval.
- **Undershoot**: the switch node going below ground due to parasitic inductance.
- **dv/dt immunity (CMTI)**: how well the driver tolerates fast switch-node edges.
- **Figure of merit ($R_{DS(on)}Q_g$)**: a measure of switching-conduction efficiency.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "NFETs have lower resistance but need a bootstrap".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the advantage from $\mu_n/\mu_p$ and area, then puts numbers on it (10 mΩ vs 25 mΩ, 1 W vs 2.5 W).
- Derives the gate voltage requirement for a specific rail (34 V for a 24 V rail).
- Lists the challenges as consequences: duty limit, start-up, droop, undershoot, dv/dt, parts and layout.
- Names fixes: bootstrap, charge pump, isolated supply, integrated drivers.
- States when a PFET is the right choice anyway (low voltage, low frequency, 100% duty without a charge pump, simplicity).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The NFET is better because it needs less gate voltage."** A PFET high side needs about the same drive; the issue is where the gate is referenced.
- **Driving the high-side gate from a ground-referenced 5 V.** With the source at the rail, $V_{GS}$ is negative.
- **Ignoring max duty cycle.** Bootstrap requires periodic off-times; "100% duty" needs a charge pump.
- **Using a tiny bootstrap capacitor.** It must hold $Q_g$ with small droop plus leakage; 100 nF is a minimum for many FETs, and ceramics lose capacitance under bias.
- **Forgetting start-up.** Until the low side has conducted, the bootstrap cap is empty and the high side cannot turn on.
:::

::: deep
## Why an NFET is the better switch

The on-resistance of a MOSFET is set by how many carriers you can pack into how much channel. In the triode region

$$R_{DS(on)}\approx\frac{L}{\mu C_{ox}\,W\,(V_{GS}-V_{th})}.$$

For the same $W/L$ and overdrive, the device made of the more mobile carrier wins. In silicon the electron mobility is roughly 2 to 3 times the hole mobility, so for the same $R_{DS(on)}$ a PFET must be 2 to 3 times as wide. A bigger device has more gate area: more $C_{iss}$, $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$, more switching loss and more silicon cost ([[q0070]], [[q0097]]).

| For a 20 mΩ-class part | NFET | PFET |
|---|---|---|
| Relative die area | 1 | about 2 to 3 |
| $Q_g$ (typical) | 10 nC | about 25 nC |
| $C_{oss}$ | 1 | about 2.5 |
| Gate-drive loss at 10 V, 20 kHz | $10\text{ nC}\times10\text{ V}\times20\text{ kHz}=2$ mW | $25\text{ nC}\times10\text{ V}\times20\text{ kHz}=5$ mW |
| Selection | very wide | narrow |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The mobility ratio and the die-area penalty vary with process and voltage rating; "2 to 3 times" is typical for silicon power MOSFETs, not a guaranteed number. Check the datasheets for the actual parts.

Example in a 24 V, 10 A motor leg: an NFET at 10 mΩ conducts $I^2R=1.0$ W (at 100% on time); a PFET with similar cost might be 25 mΩ, so 2.5 W. For gate drive, the difference is small at 20 kHz; at 500 kHz in a buck converter the gate-charge difference becomes significant.

## The problem: the source moves

An NFET turns on when $V_{GS}=V_G-V_S$ exceeds the threshold plus overdrive. Look at what the source does on each side of a half-bridge:

- **Low side**: the source is ground. Drive $V_G$ from a 5 to 12 V ground-referenced supply.
- **High side**: the source is the switch node. It is at 0 V when the low side is on, and at $V_M$ when the high side is on.

A gate at 12 V with the source at 24 V gives $V_{GS}=-12$ V: firmly off. This is why the high-side drive needs its own supply: one referenced to the switch node.

## The solution families

1. **Bootstrap** ([[q0206]], [[q0427]]): $C_{boot}$ charged to $V_{DRV}$ while SW is low. When SW rises the cap's bottom plate rises with it, and the driver (powered by the cap) lifts the gate to $V_{SW}+V_{boot}$.
2. **Charge pump**: a small oscillator-driven capacitor stack generates $V_M+V_{DRV}$ continuously. Works at 100% duty, also during start-up and at low $f_{sw}$.
3. **Isolated gate-drive supply**: a small isolated DC-DC per high-side switch (common in high-voltage inverters).
4. **Pulse-transformer or opto/isolated gate drivers** ([[q0432]], [[q0433]]).
5. **Avoid the problem**: use a PFET high side ([[q0438]]) or a low-side-only design.

## The challenges, one by one

| Challenge | What happens | Typical fix |
|---|---|---|
| Gate above rail | $V_{GS}<0$ with ground-referenced drive | Bootstrap / charge pump |
| Max duty cycle | The cap needs refresh time each cycle; $D_{max}=1-t_{off,min}f_{sw}$ | Add a charge pump; or limit to 95 to 98% |
| Holding on (100% duty) | Droop $\Delta V=I_qt/C$ | Charge pump |
| Start-up | Cap empty, low side not yet on | Pre-charge pulse; use low-side on before first high-side pulse |
| SW undershoot | Parasitic L pulls SW to $-5$ V or less; may over-charge the cap or violate the driver's rating | Driver rated for negative $V_{SW}$; series resistor; tight layout |
| dv/dt on SW | False level-shifter triggering; Miller turn-on | High CMTI driver; slower edges via $R_g$ |
| More parts, layout | Diode, capacitor, driver, loop area | Integrated half-bridge driver |

Sizing example for the 24 V motor leg: $Q_g=40$ nC, 10 V drive. $C_{boot}\ge40\text{ nC}/0.5\text{ V}=80$ nF; choose 470 nF. Droop at 100% duty with 100 µA total leakage and quiescent: $0.5\text{ V}\times0.47\text{ µF}/100\text{ µA}=2.35$ ms before $V_{GS}$ falls by 0.5 V. A motor that sits at 100% duty for seconds therefore needs a charge pump.

Gate-drive power is small: $P=Q_gV_{DRV}f_{sw}=40\text{ nC}\times10\text{ V}\times20\text{ kHz}=8$ mW.

## When a PFET is still the right answer

- Low supply (3 to 12 V) where the gate can be pulled from the supply to ground: a PFET needs only a level-shifter ([[q0438]]).
- Low frequency, low current, 100% duty without a charge pump.
- Cost- or space-sensitive designs where the PFET's extra $R_{DS(on)}$ is acceptable.
- Simple load switches.

Above a few amps, above about 20 V, or at high frequency, the NFET plus bootstrap or integrated driver wins.

## Integrated drivers

Most designs use a half-bridge driver IC (bootstrap diode, level shifter, dead time, UVLO) or a motor-driver IC with an internal charge pump. The designer chooses $C_{boot}$ and $R_g$, keeps the loop small, and checks the driver's negative-$V_{SW}$ and dv/dt ratings. See [[q0427]] for a full design, and [[q0204]] for the buck-converter version of this trade-off.
:::

::: applications
- **Motor-driver ICs and BLDC inverters**: integrated charge pumps hold high-side NFETs on at 100% duty.
- **Synchronous buck converters**: bootstrapped NFET high side, usually with an internal boot diode.
- **Server and PC VRMs**: bootstrap drivers with minimum off-time.
- **Class-D amplifiers**: NFET half-bridges with bootstrap supplies.
- **Electric-vehicle and industrial inverters**: isolated gate drivers with isolated supplies.
- **Load switches and hot-swap controllers**: charge-pumped high-side NFET gate drive (to avoid the PFET's resistance).
:::

::: assumptions
- Silicon power MOSFETs with $\mu_n/\mu_p\approx2$ to 3.
- Logic-level or standard-gate NFET with 10 V drive for the example; the same reasoning holds for 5 V drive.
- Bootstrap droop calculated with constant leakage and quiescent current.
- Supply voltage large enough that the gate (referenced to ground) cannot reach $V_M+V_{GS(on)}$ without a boost.

For very low supply voltages (a 3.3 V rail with a logic-level NFET), the driver supply can exceed the rail, and a simple ground-referenced 5 V can drive the gate at $V_M=3.3$ V ($V_{GS}\approx1.7$ V, usually not enough for good $R_{DS(on)}$). The argument still applies, just at a smaller scale. For wide-bandgap GaN FETs the gate-drive limit ($\pm6$ V) makes bootstrap overcharge more serious.
:::

::: followups
### Why not just give the NFET a 30 V gate supply from a boost converter?
You can (a charge pump is exactly that kind of above-rail supply), but a fixed, ground-referenced 34 V rail is only half the job. When the FET is off its source sits near 0 V, so connecting the gate to 34 V then would give $V_{GS}=34$ V, beyond the typical $\pm20$ V rating. You still need a level shifter that switches the gate between the source and the high rail, plus a Zener to clamp $V_{GS}$. A bootstrap avoids all that: its supply moves with the source, so $V_{GS}$ never exceeds $V_{DRV}$.

### How does a charge pump get 100% duty?
It continuously generates $V_M+V_{DRV}$ using an oscillator and switched capacitors, with enough current to cover leakage and gate charge. It doesn't depend on the switch node moving, so the high side can stay on indefinitely.

### What if the switch node undershoots to −8 V in the bootstrap circuit?
The bootstrap diode then charges the cap to $V_{DRV}+8$ V (minus the diode drop), which may exceed the gate rating. Add a series resistor in the boot path or a Zener across the cap, use a driver with a rated negative-SW tolerance, and reduce loop inductance.

### Which has more shoot-through risk, a PFET/NFET pair or an NFET/NFET pair?
Both need dead time, but the NFET/NFET pair has faster edges and larger dv/dt, so Miller-induced turn-on is more likely. The PFET/NFET pair has a mismatched delay. Either way you design the dead time from the datasheet delays.

### When would you pick a PFET high side anyway?
Low voltage (a 12 V rail), modest current, no charge pump available, and simple level shifting. The PFET's higher $R_{DS(on)}$ is acceptable and the gate drive is two discrete parts instead of a driver IC.
:::
