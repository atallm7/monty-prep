::: testing
Whether you can predict current from the **circuit model** instead of memorizing "motors draw a lot at startup". The hidden sub-skills:

1. You know the motor equation $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$ and see that the maximum is at $\omega = 0$: stall or the first instant of startup.
2. You know the current at turn-on is limited by $L/R$ for a short time, then by $R$ alone, so the peak is $V/R$ (or a bit less).
3. You can say what is different for a solenoid (steady state $V/R$ with the plunger seated, dip while moving) and for AC coils (inrush with the plunger open).
:::

::: why
The maximum current is the number you size everything with: the supply, the wiring, the MOSFET or driver, the fuse, the current limit and the heat. Getting it wrong means a resetting microcontroller, a blown driver, or a burned winding. This question (and its sibling [[q0344]]) is asked because it tests whether you can connect the back-EMF idea to a design number.
:::

::: concepts
- **Back-EMF**: $e = K_e\omega$ opposes the supply and reduces current as speed rises. See [[q0341]].
- **RL step response**: $i(t) = \frac{V}{R}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ with $\tau = L/R$. See [[q0010]].
- **Stall**: shaft held, $\omega = 0$, no back-EMF, current $V/R$. See [[q0338]].
- **Solenoid inductance changes with position**: low when the plunger is out, high when it is in. See [[q0334]].
- **Torque proportional to current**: peak current means peak torque. See [[q0334]].
:::

::: answer
"A motor draws the most current when it's not turning: at startup or when it's stalled. A spinning motor generates a back-EMF proportional to speed, and that subtracts from the supply. So the current is V minus K_e omega over R. At omega equals zero there's no back-EMF, so the current is V over R, limited only by the coil resistance. With a 12 V supply and a 1 ohm winding, that's 12 amps, versus half an amp when it's spinning freely. The same applies when the shaft is jammed or heavily loaded, since a higher load means a lower speed and more current to make the torque.

Strictly, the inductance delays the current for a few milliseconds, with a time constant L over R, so the peak is a bit below V over R and happens a few milliseconds after turn-on, before the speed picks up.

A solenoid is simpler: it's an inductor with resistance, and in DC its current rises to V over R and stays there, so the most current is at steady state, after the plunger has closed. During the stroke the inductance changes, which makes the current dip, but then it rises to V over R. AC solenoids are different: with the plunger open the inductance is low, so inrush current can be several times the holding current."
:::

::: technical
**Motor.** Steady-state: $I = \dfrac{V - K_e\omega}{R}$, and with load torque $\tau_L$, $I = (\tau_L + \tau_f)/K_t$.

- Maximum at $\omega = 0$: $I_{stall} = V/R$, $\tau_{stall} = K_tV/R$.
- Transient: with the rotor still, $i(t) = \dfrac{V}{R}(1 - e^{-t/\tau_e})$, $\tau_e = L/R$. The rotor accelerates on the mechanical timescale $\tau_m = JR/(K_tK_e)$. When $\tau_m \gg \tau_e$ the current gets near $V/R$ before back-EMF builds, so the peak is slightly below $V/R$.
- Example: $V = 12$ V, $R = 1\ \Omega$, $L = 1$ mH, $K = 0.02$, $J = 10^{-5}$ kg·m²: $\tau_e = 1$ ms, $\tau_m = 25$ ms, peak $\approx 10.9$ A at about 3.4 ms (calculated from the second-order response), then decay to the load value.
- Anything that lowers speed raises current: heavy load, jam, low supply voltage with load, a brake or reversal ([[q0344]]).

**Solenoid (DC).** $i(t) = \dfrac{V}{R}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, with $\tau = L(x)/R$. Plunger motion adds a term $v\,dL/dx\cdot i$ that appears as a back-EMF during the stroke, so the current rises, dips as the plunger moves, then rises to $V/R$. Maximum current is at the final steady state. Example: 24 V, 48 Ω solenoid: $V/R = 0.5$ A and the coil dissipates $V^2/R = 12$ W continuously.

**Solenoid (AC) and motors on AC.** Current is $V/|Z|$; with the plunger out $L$ is small so $|Z|$ is small and the inrush is several times the sealed-in holding current (the ratio depends on design).

**PWM drive.** The average current depends on duty $D$: $I_{avg} \approx (DV - K_e\omega)/R$; the peak includes ripple $\Delta I \approx V D(1-D)/(Lf)$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> AC inrush ratios (often quoted as roughly 5 to 10 times holding current) depend on the specific solenoid; check the datasheet.
:::

::: short
"A motor draws the most current when it's stalled or just starting, because there's no back-EMF to oppose the supply: I equals V minus K_e omega over R, so at zero speed it's V over R, say 12 amps for 12 volts and 1 ohm, versus half an amp at free-running speed. Inductance only delays it by a few milliseconds. The same goes for a jammed or heavily loaded motor. A DC solenoid draws its maximum, V over R, at steady state once the plunger is seated."
:::

::: long
"For a motor, the answer comes straight from the circuit model. The coil has resistance R and inductance L, and the spinning rotor generates a back-EMF equal to K_e times omega that opposes the supply. So the steady-state current is V minus K_e omega over R. That is largest when omega is zero. At the instant I apply power, the rotor is at rest, there's no back-EMF, and only the winding resistance limits the current: V over R. For a 12 volt supply and 1 ohm winding that's 12 amps, compared with maybe half an amp once it's at full speed. The inductance sets a small delay: the current rises with a time constant L over R, around a millisecond, while the rotor speed rises on a much slower mechanical time scale, tens of milliseconds, so the current peaks near V over R, typically a little below, a few milliseconds after switching on, and then decays as the back-EMF builds.

The same logic says the motor draws large current any time the speed is held low: a jammed shaft, a heavy load, a stall. And since torque is K_t times I, maximum current is also maximum torque.

For a solenoid, there's no back-EMF to speak of. A DC solenoid is just an inductor with resistance, so its current rises exponentially to V over R. There's a small dip while the plunger moves because the inductance jumps, but the largest current is the final steady state with the plunger seated, V over R, and it keeps dissipating V squared over R unless I reduce it with PWM. AC solenoids are the exception: with the plunger open the impedance is low, so the inrush can be several times the holding current."
:::

::: terms
- **Stall current**: current with the shaft held, $V/R$.
- **Back-EMF**: speed-proportional voltage that opposes the supply.
- **Electrical time constant $\tau_e$**: $L/R$, how fast the current rises.
- **Mechanical time constant $\tau_m$**: $JR/(K_tK_e)$, how fast the speed rises.
- **Inrush current**: initial peak current at turn-on.
- **Holding current**: current needed to keep a solenoid closed.
- **Pull-in**: the stroke phase when the plunger closes.
- **Locked rotor**: another name for the stalled condition.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "at startup, because of the inrush".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Writes $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$ and **derives** startup, stall and heavy load as the same condition (low speed).
- Distinguishes the **electrical** (L/R) and **mechanical** time constants and notes the peak is a bit below $V/R$ and a few ms after switch-on.
- Gives a **number** ($V/R = 12$ A vs 0.5 A free-running) and relates current to torque.
- Handles the **solenoid** correctly (max at steady state for DC, dip during stroke, AC inrush is different).
- Mentions the **design consequences**: supply and driver sized for stall, current limit, thermal time at stall, soft start.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Inductors oppose current, so the peak must be small."** Inductance only delays the rise by $L/R$ (milliseconds); the steady stall current is still $V/R$.
- **"A motor draws most current at full speed."** The opposite: full speed gives the *least* current.
- **"Solenoids have inrush like motors."** For DC, the max is the steady state; the transient is a smooth rise with a dip in the middle.
- **Using the running current to size the driver.** Size for stall or the current limit you enforce.
- **Ignoring supply and wiring resistance.** They add to $R$ and reduce the true peak (and sag the rail).
- **Ignoring reversal.** Reversing a spinning motor can draw about *twice* stall current ([[q0344]]).
:::

KVL gives $V = Ri + L\,\dot i + K_e\omega$ and the mechanical equation is $J\dot\omega = K_t i - \tau_L$. With $\omega=0$ held, $i(t) = (V/R)(1 - e^{-t/\tau_e})$.

## The startup current waveform

For the example motor ($R = 1\ \Omega$, $L = 1$ mH, $K_t = K_e = 0.02$, $J = 10^{-5}$ kg·m², friction ignored) the roots of $s^2 + (R/L)s + K_tK_e/(JL) = 0$ are $-42$ and $-958\ \text{s}^{-1}$ (overdamped), and the current is

$$i(t) = \frac{V}{L\,(p_1 - p_2)}\left(e^{p_1t} - e^{p_2t}\right),\quad p_1 = -42,\ p_2 = -958$$

## Why heavy load = high current

At any speed, current is set by the torque the motor must make: $I = (\tau_L+\tau_f)/K_t$. If the load rises (steeper hill, jammed gear), the motor slows until the extra current from the reduced back-EMF supplies the torque. At stall the load torque exceeds $K_tV/R$ and the shaft stops: $I = V/R$. So *maximum current* is when the motor is stalled or held below the speed where torque matches the load.

| Condition | Speed | Current (12 V, 1 Ω, $K$=0.02) |
|---|---|---|
| No load | 575 rad/s | 0.5 A |
| 0.1 N·m load | 350 rad/s | 5 A |
| 0.2 N·m load | 100 rad/s | 10 A |
| Stall | 0 | 12 A |

Figure: Schematic only (the dip shape is illustrative). The current rises with $\tau = L/R$; when the plunger starts to move, $L$ increases and the motion-induced term pulls the current down briefly, after which it recovers to $V/R = 0.5$ A. The dashed curve is what a stationary plunger would give. The dip is the electrical signature of plunger travel.

The maximum is the final value $V/R$, which is also what the coil dissipates continuously: $V^2/R = 12$ W. That is why many solenoids use a pull-in current (full voltage) then a lower hold current by PWM or an economizer resistor: only the pull-in needs the full force, since once closed the gap is small and the holding force is large for little current ([[q0334]]).

## Design consequences

- **Supply and wiring:** choose a supply that can provide $V/R$ (or the enforced current limit) without sagging, or use soft start ([[q0343]]).
- **Driver/transistor:** rated for peak current and with SOA for the transient; the current limit protects the driver at stall.
- **Fuse/thermal:** a stalled motor dissipates $I^2R = 144$ W; coil copper (say 20 g, $c = 385$ J/kg·K) heats by $144/(0.02\times385) \approx 19$ K/s, so burnout takes seconds ([[q0338]]).
- **Sensing:** a shunt sized for stall current; set the overcurrent trip above the startup peak but below the damage level, with a blanking time.
:::

::: applications
- **Motor drivers** with current limiting set from stall current (for example H-bridge ICs with a cycle-by-cycle limit).
- **Robot wheels**: stall detection by watching current to detect obstacles or jams.
- **Battery sizing**: peak stall draw vs a pack's C-rating and internal resistance.
- **Solenoid valves**: economizer circuits to cut hold power and heat.
- **Power tools**: electronic soft start to limit inrush current and mechanical shock.
:::

::: assumptions
- Brushed DC motor with constant $K_t = K_e$, linear electrical model, supply with negligible internal resistance (a real one lowers the peak).
- No saturation: a real solenoid or motor core can saturate at high current, increasing the peak.
- Friction and load only shift the final current; they do not change $V/R$ at stall.
- PWM drives change the peak (ripple) but not the average-current logic.
- Hot windings have higher resistance ($+0.39\%/K$), which lowers $V/R$.
:::

::: followups
### Why isn't the peak exactly V/R?
Because the back-EMF starts building as soon as the rotor moves, while the current is still rising with $\tau_e = L/R$. If $\tau_m \gg \tau_e$ the peak is only slightly below $V/R$ (10.9 A vs 12 A in the example); with a very small rotor inertia the speed rises as fast as the current and the peak is much lower.

### How would you limit the startup current?
Ramp the voltage (soft start by PWM duty), set a cycle-by-cycle current limit in the driver, add a series resistor or NTC (wasteful), or use a controller with torque limiting. The limit must still allow enough current for the required starting torque.

### Why is stall dangerous for the motor?
At stall all the input power is heat in the winding: $I^2R = V^2/R$, 144 W for 12 V and 1 Ω. With a few tens of grams of copper the winding heats by tens of K per second until the insulation fails. Motors are rated for a stall time or protected by thermal cutoffs and current limits.

### A solenoid's current has a notch part-way through the rise. What does it mean?
Plunger movement changes $L$ and creates an extra back-EMF-like term; the notch is the moment the plunger moves. If it is missing, the plunger may be stuck or the voltage too low. Some drivers use this to confirm actuation.

### Scenario: your robot's battery voltage sags and resets the MCU when the wheels start. What do you do?
Peak stall current from several motors drawn through the battery's internal resistance ($\Delta V = I R_{batt}$) causes the sag. Stagger motor starts, ramp the PWM, limit current, add local bulk capacitance for the logic rail, or use a higher-C battery with lower internal resistance.
:::
