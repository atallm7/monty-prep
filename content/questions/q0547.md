::: testing
Whether you can name the actual noisy nodes of a switching supply, say what is noisy about each (dV/dt or di/dt), give the routing rule that follows, and then go beyond the node itself to board-level hygiene: partitioning, planes, keep-outs, shielding, filtering and connector placement. A strong answer links every rule to a mechanism and notes the compromise between current capacity and area.
:::

::: why
The switching supply is usually the noisiest thing on a board, and it shares the board with ADCs, sensors, radios and clocks. Getting noisy-node routing right is the difference between passing EMC at the first attempt and months of ferrites and shields. In interviews it separates candidates who can recite "short and wide" from those who can explain the trade-offs and the second-order board-level effects.
:::

::: concepts
- **High di/dt loop**: the hot loop radiates a magnetic field. See [[q0548]].
- **High dV/dt node**: the switch node couples electric fields and common-mode current. See [[q0550]].
- **Common-mode current**: displacement current through capacitance to the plane returns via cables. See [[q0562]].
- **Magnetic coupling**: the inductor's stray field. See [[q0551]].
- **Conducted noise**: pulsed input current on the supply line.
- **Coupling mechanisms and prevention**: [[q0565]].
:::

::: answer
"The noisy nodes of a buck are the switch node, the hot loop, and the inductor with its stray field. The switch node swings the full input voltage in a few nanoseconds, so any copper area on it couples through capacitance. The hot loop, input capacitor plus both switches, carries pulses of the load current with nanosecond edges, so it makes a magnetic field and voltage spikes. The inductor has a magnetic field, and the bootstrap and gate-drive loops are smaller versions of the same thing. Then there's the pulsed current on the input line, which is conducted noise.

For routing: keep each of these as short and small as possible, on one layer, with no vias, and with a solid ground plane right beneath so the fields terminate there rather than somewhere else. The switch node should be just wide enough for the inductor current, no pours, no test pad bigger than needed. Keep sensitive nets, like feedback, away from it and don't route anything underneath or alongside it.

Other board-level things: put the converter in its own corner of the board, away from analog, RF and crystals; use a shielded inductor; filter the input with a small LC or a ferrite so noise doesn't leave on the supply line; keep the supply's connectors and cables away from the noisy nodes because cables make great antennas; and if needed add a snubber across the low-side switch to damp the ring."
:::

::: technical
**Noisy nodes and mechanisms.**
- **SW**: $dv/dt \approx 12\ \text{V}/3\ \text{ns} = 4\times10^9$ V/s; displacement current $i = C\,dv/dt$ (1 pF → 4 mA); rings with the hot loop at $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L C_{oss}})$ (168 MHz for 3 nH, 300 pF).
- **Hot loop** ($C_{in}$, $Q_H$, $Q_L$): $di/dt \approx I_{out}/t_r = 5\ \text{A}/3\ \text{ns} = 1.7\times10^9$ A/s; $V = L\,di/dt$; radiated H-field falls roughly as $1/r^3$ near the loop.
- **Inductor**: stray flux; shielded vs unshielded; its winding capacitance couples SW's dV/dt to the board.
- **Gate drive and bootstrap**: about 1 A, 10 ns pulses for a 10 nC gate.
- **Input line**: pulsed current of RMS $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$ unless smoothed by $C_{in}$ and an input filter.
- **Output**: ripple at $f_{sw}$ and ringing spikes.

**Routing rules.**
1. Minimise copper area and length of SW; one layer; no vias; plane beneath; no other nets underneath on the *adjacent* layer within the SW region except the plane.
2. Minimise hot-loop area: $C_{in}$ at the pins; layer 2 solid ground directly below (dielectric $h \le 0.2$ mm).
3. Keep both away from FB, COMP, sense lines, ADC and RF.
4. Do not route a signal across SW or the hot loop; if it must cross, cross at right angles on another layer with a plane between.
5. Shielded inductor and orientation away from sensitive loops.
6. Damp ringing: gate resistor/BOOT resistor, snubber (R and C across $Q_L$), proper layout first.

**Other board-level considerations.** Partition and floor-plan; plane integrity (no slots under SW or the hot loop); input filtering (ferrite, LC); connector and cable placement away from the converter; stitching vias around the converter's ground; enclosure/shield cans for the worst cases; test with near-field probe and pre-compliance scans.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The ring frequency and amplitude depend on the actual loop inductance, FET output capacitance and damping. The numbers shown are illustrative, not predictions for a specific part.
:::

::: long
"There are really two kinds of noise in a buck: fast voltage edges and fast current edges, and each has its own nodes.

For voltage edges, the switch node is the big one. It swings the full input voltage in a few nanoseconds, so four billion volts per second for 12 volts in 3 nanoseconds. Any copper area on it couples through capacitance: one picofarad gives 4 milliamps. That current goes through the plane and out via cables, which is common-mode emission.

For current edges, the hot loop, input capacitor plus both switches, carries pulses of the full load current with nanosecond edges. Inductance in it gives overshoot, and the loop is a magnetic antenna. The gate-drive and bootstrap loops are smaller versions of the same thing. The inductor has a stray field and winding capacitance. And the input line carries pulsed current unless it's filtered.

So for routing: keep each as short and small as possible, on one layer, no vias, and a solid ground plane right underneath so fields terminate there. Switch node just wide enough for the inductor current, no pours. Nothing sensitive routed near or under them.

Then the board level: put the converter in its own region away from analog, RF and crystals; use a shielded inductor; filter the input with a ferrite or LC; keep connectors and cables away because they're antennas; keep the plane unbroken; and add a snubber across the low-side switch if it rings. I'd check with a near-field probe."
:::

::: terms
- **Hot loop**: input capacitor and both switches; the high-di/dt loop.
- **Switch node**: SW; high dV/dt.
- **Common-mode current**: current on the signal and return in the same direction, flowing through capacitance to the plane and out through cables.
- **Snubber**: an RC network that damps ringing, usually across the low-side switch.
- **Shielded inductor**: an inductor with a magnetic enclosure that confines its field.
- **Keep-out**: a region where no sensitive routing is allowed.
- **Near-field probe**: a small loop or stub probe used to find emission hot spots.
- **Conducted noise**: noise travelling on supply or signal wires.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the switch node is noisy, keep it short."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Distinguishes dV/dt nodes (electric coupling, common-mode) from di/dt loops (magnetic) and lists both, plus the inductor, gate loops and input line.
- Quantifies: $4\times10^9$ V/s, 1 pF → 4 mA, 5 A in 3 ns.
- Gives routing rules with reasons (area equals coupling, plane terminates fields, no vias).
- Names the compromise: SW wide enough for the current but small in area.
- Covers board-level measures: partitioning, plane integrity, shielded inductor, input filter, cables, snubber, near-field probing.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Pouring copper on SW to "lower the resistance".** The current is not the problem; the area is. See [[q0537]].
- **Routing a signal underneath the inductor or SW.** Even with a plane between, vias and edges leak; it's the worst place for a victim.
- **Splitting the ground plane under the power stage.** Return currents detour and the loop grows.
- **Ignoring the cables.** Common-mode current exits via cables; a noisy converter next to a connector raises emissions.
- **Relying on a snubber instead of fixing the loop.** A snubber damps ringing but costs loss and does not fix a big loop.
- **Believing the shielded inductor solves everything.** It reduces the field, not the SW dV/dt or the hot loop.
:::

::: deep
## Two kinds of noise, two kinds of nodes

Switching converters create noise because they chop currents and voltages quickly. The Fourier content of a trapezoidal edge with rise time $t_r$ extends to roughly $1/(\pi t_r)$: 100 MHz for $t_r = 3$ ns. The converter's switching frequency is only 500 kHz, but its harmonics reach hundreds of MHz, the range where small structures radiate and couple.

### Voltage edges: SW

The switch node swings $V_{in}$ in $t_r$: with 12 V and 3 ns, $dv/dt = 4\times10^9$ V/s. A conductor with capacitance $C$ to another node injects $C\,dv/dt$. Three destinations matter:

1. **Plane below** (common-mode): current flows into the plane and must return to the converter. If its return is through the supply cable or a chassis, that loop radiates. A larger SW area $A$ raises $C = \varepsilon A/h$ proportionally ([[q0537]]).
2. **Neighbouring traces** (crosstalk, [[q0565]]): a trace within a mm or two with mutual capacitance of 0.1 pF picks up 0.4 mA, which across a 10 kΩ node is 4 V (limited by the node's own capacitance).
3. **Heat sinks and shields**, which become radiating plates.

### Current edges: hot loop and friends

The hot loop carries pulses of $\approx I_{out}$ with edges of a few ns: $5\ \text{A}/3\ \text{ns} = 1.7\times10^9$ A/s. A loop of inductance $L$ develops $L\,di/dt$: 3 nH gives 5 V. The loop is also a small magnetic dipole; its near field falls off like $1/r^3$. Reduce the area (and the inductance) and both effects shrink. Gate-drive and bootstrap loops carry about 1 A for 10 ns and behave likewise at a smaller scale. The input line carries the residual pulsed current not absorbed by $C_{in}$; unfiltered, it is conducted emissions and also feeds noise into other loads on the same bus.

### The inductor

An inductor stores energy in a magnetic field; unshielded drum cores spill flux, and the winding's parasitic capacitance (and its terminal geometry) couple SW's dV/dt to the board. A shielded (or molded) inductor greatly reduces the stray field; orientation matters for unshielded parts (keep the winding axis pointing away from sensitive loops and FB).

## Routing rules, with reasons

| Rule | Reason |
|---|---|
| SW: shortest path pin → inductor; one layer; no vias | Each via and each mm adds area and a path for common-mode current |
| SW width: set by the inductor current (IPC), not larger | Area equals capacitance equals noise |
| Solid plane directly beneath SW and the hot loop | Terminates the electric field locally; reduces loop inductance |
| No other nets on the adjacent layer under SW | Avoid coupling through the thin dielectric |
| Hot loop: $C_{in}$ at the pins, plane at $h \le 0.2$ mm | Loop inductance $\approx \mu_0 h\,l/w$: lower for thinner $h$ |
| Keep FB, COMP, sense, ADC, RF at least a few mm away; orthogonal crossings only | Mutual capacitance and mutual inductance both fall with distance |
| Keep gate-drive/BOOT loops tight | Same reason on a smaller scale |

A quick comparison of loop inductance with the plane distance: over a plane with $h = 0.1$ mm a 5 mm × 2 mm loop has a plane-coupled inductance of about $\mu_0 h\,(l/w) = 0.126\times2.5 = 0.31$ nH; with $h = 1$ mm it is ten times that (vias and the vertical parts of the loop add more). That is why the dielectric between the top layer and layer 2 is chosen thin on power boards.

## Board-level considerations

1. **Floor-plan and partition.** Put the converter at one edge or corner, with a keep-out (a few cm if possible) from analog front ends, RF, clocks and crystals. Group by noise class.
2. **Plane integrity.** No slots under SW or the hot loop; stitching vias around the converter's ground, but not through the hot loop ([[q0539]]).
3. **Filtering.** Input LC or ferrite bead plus a bulk/damping capacitor, so pulsed current does not reach the connector; output LC or post-regulation LDO for quiet loads.
4. **Cables and connectors.** Common-mode current finds cables. Put connectors away from the converter or filter them at the entry point; avoid routing the input line past the SW node.
5. **Shielding and magnetic choices.** Shielded inductor, and for tough cases a can or a shield over the converter, grounded around its perimeter.
6. **Damping.** Gate/boot resistor to slow the edge, RC snubber across $Q_L$ (typical R of a few ohms, C a few times $C_{oss}$) or ferrite bead in series; the better first step is always a smaller loop.
7. **Synchronisation.** Several converters can be synchronised or spaced in frequency so beat tones fall outside the band of interest.
8. **Test.** Probe SW with a short ground spring; scan the board with a near-field probe; compare before/after changes; use a LISN for conducted emission pre-compliance.

## An example priority list

For the 12 V → 3.3 V, 5 A buck: (1) $C_{in}$ at pins, plane below; (2) SW ≤ 10 mm long, 1.5 to 2 mm wide (roughly 4 to 5 A by the IPC chart, see [[q0534]]) rather than a pour; (3) the inductor next to SW with its field away from FB; (4) FB divider at the pin on the opposite side; (5) input ferrite and 10 µF bulk at the connector; (6) a spot for an RC snubber footprint on $Q_L$ (populate if the ring is large); (7) pre-compliance scan.

::: applications
- **Automotive and industrial buck converters** designed to pass CISPR-type conducted and radiated limits.
- **Mixed-signal boards**: a switcher on one side, a 24-bit ADC on the other, joined by an LDO and a ferrite.
- **Wireless products**: converters kept away from antennas and oscillators with shield cans.
- **Battery-powered IoT**, where efficiency and EMI both matter and small layouts must be tight.
- **Multi-converter systems** where spacing and synchronisation avoid beat-frequency noise.
:::

::: assumptions
The explanation assumes a buck converter with hundreds of kHz to MHz switching and nanosecond edges, mounted on a four-layer board with a ground plane on layer 2. The same ideas apply to boost, buck-boost and isolated converters, with different noisy nodes (for example the transformer and rectifier in a flyback). Dielectric thickness, edge rate and parts determine the actual emissions; the numbers shown are order-of-magnitude. On a two-layer board, the plane is a bottom pour and the loops are larger. Another valid emphasis is conducted emissions (filters, LISN) over radiated; both are covered above.
:::

::: followups
### Why shouldn't you route a signal under the switch node, even on another layer with a plane between?
A plane reduces coupling, but vias, plane edges, and cut-outs let fields leak, and the SW voltage edge is large. Any signal there is exposed to the highest dV/dt on the board. If a route is unavoidable, cross at 90° on a layer separated by a solid plane and keep it low impedance.

### What does a snubber do and where do you place it?
It's an RC network, typically across the low-side switch (SW to ground), that damps the ringing between the loop inductance and the FET's output capacitance. The capacitor is typically a few times $C_{oss}$ and the resistor near $\sqrt{L/C_{oss}}$ (about 3 Ω for 3 nH, 300 pF). It dissipates about $C V^2 f_{sw}$ (half on charging, half on discharging each cycle), so it's a trade of efficiency for noise. It must be placed very close to the switch to be effective.

### Which is more important for EMI, the hot loop or the SW node?
Usually the hot loop dominates the high-frequency (above ~100 MHz) emissions, because its inductance sets the ring, while SW's area drives common-mode current. Layouts need both, but I'd start with the hot loop and then the SW area, then edge-rate control.

### How does a shielded inductor change the layout?
It reduces stray magnetic field so FB and other sensitive loops can sit closer, and it makes orientation less critical. It doesn't remove the SW dV/dt or the hot loop, and shielded parts have higher DCR/cost or lower saturation for a given size. Still, the sensible layout keeps sensitive nets away.

### Your product fails radiated emissions around 150 MHz. What do you check?
The ring frequency of the hot loop (likely near 100 to 200 MHz) with a near-field probe, the SW area and trace, cable positions, and the input filter. Then I would reduce the loop (move $C_{in}$, add a second smaller ceramic), slow the edge (gate resistor), add an RC snubber, and shield or filter the cable.
:::
