::: testing
Whether you know the through-hole counterpart to reflow: how it works (flux, preheat, solder wave, cooling), what it is used for, and what it demands from the design (bottom-side SMT glued and wave-compatible, component orientation, pad shape and spacing to avoid bridges and shadowing, keep-outs, thermal reliefs). The interviewer is also checking whether you can name the defects (bridging, icicles, insufficient hole fill, skips) and the process variables (contact time, temperature, conveyor angle and speed).
:::

::: why
Plenty of real products still have through-hole connectors, transformers and capacitors, and wave soldering is the fast, cheap way to solder many leads at once. But it constrains layout. A designer who puts a fine-pitch part on the bottom of a wave-soldered board, or who orients an SO-16 across the wave direction, will see bridges that no one can fix cheaply. Knowing the process lets you apply the design rules rather than just follow them.
:::

::: concepts
- **Solder wave**: a pump forces molten solder through a nozzle, creating a standing wave that the board's underside touches.
- **Flux application**: spray or foam flux cleans oxide so solder wets the leads and barrels.
- **Preheat**: warms the board and activates flux; avoids thermal shock and helps the barrels fill.
- **Capillary action**: pulls solder up the hole, filling the barrel ([[q0598]]).
- **Contact time**: seconds the board is in the solder; typically a few seconds.
- **Wave-compatible SMT**: chip passives and small packages glued to the bottom side ([[q0599]]).
- **Selective soldering**: a localised, programmable alternative.
:::

::: answer
"Wave soldering is how through-hole boards get soldered in bulk. After the through-hole parts are inserted from the top, the board rides a conveyor across a fluxer that sprays flux on the underside, then a preheat section that warms it and activates the flux, and then over a pump-driven wave of molten solder, around 250 °C for lead-free. The underside touches the wave for a few seconds, and capillary action pulls solder up the holes, filling the plated barrels and forming fillets on the leads. Then the board cools.

It's very fast and cheap for boards with lots of through-hole joints. The catch is that the whole underside sees molten solder, so any surface-mount parts on the bottom must be glued on beforehand and be wave-compatible, meaning simple chip parts and small packages with good spacing. Fine-pitch ICs would bridge. Common defects are bridging between pins, icicles, and insufficient hole fill, and I'd prevent them with design choices like orienting multi-pin parts along the wave direction, adding solder thieves, and keeping spacing between pads.

If there are only a few through-hole parts, or SMT on both sides, selective soldering or pin-in-paste is usually the better route."
:::

::: technical
**Process flow.**
1. **Flux** (spray, or foam): no-clean or water-soluble. Applied to the underside.
2. **Preheat** (convection or IR): board topside reaches about 90-130 °C (vendor dependent), activating flux, evaporating solvent, reducing thermal shock.
3. **Wave**: often two waves. A turbulent *chip wave* first (fills hard-to-reach joints, wets glued SMD), then a smooth *laminar wave* (removes bridges, forms clean fillets). Solder pot about 250-260 °C for SAC alloys (tin-lead pots run cooler; vendor dependent).
4. **Cooling**.

**Contact time.** The time any point of the board is in contact with solder is $t = L_c / v$ where $L_c$ is the contact length along the travel direction and $v$ is the belt speed. For $L_c = 40$ mm and $v = 1.2$ m/min: $t = 0.04/(1.2/60) = 2.0$ s; at 0.9 m/min it is 2.7 s (computed). Typical contact times are in the range of about 2-5 s.

**Hole fill.** Capillary rise requires wettable surfaces and enough heat. Industry acceptability criteria (IPC-A-610) specify a minimum percentage of barrel fill by class; high-thermal-mass joints (ground-connected pins) fill poorly, which is why they get thermal relief spokes.

**Constraints on the design.**
- Bottom-side SMD: glued, chip passives and SOT/SOIC-type parts with specific orientation and spacing; no fine-pitch, QFN, BGA.
- Orient multi-pin SMD parts so their long axis is parallel to the travel direction (so the wave leaves each pin pair cleanly), and add "thieving" pads at the trailing end of dense rows to drain solder.
- Spacing between pads and between a wave-side part and nearby parts to avoid shadowing: tall parts shield the following pad from the wave.
- Keep heat-sensitive parts away or use pallets/masks.

**Other.** Lead-free alloys cause more dross, copper dissolution in the pot and need nitrogen or controlled atmospheres in some cases.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Temperatures, preheat targets, wave heights and contact times differ by alloy, flux, board thickness and machine. Use the flux and alloy suppliers' recommended settings and verify with test boards.
:::

::: short
"Wave soldering solders through-hole boards in bulk. The board's underside is fluxed, preheated, and then passes over a standing wave of molten solder, around 250 °C for lead-free, for a few seconds. Capillary action fills the holes and forms the joints. Bottom-side SMT parts have to be glued and wave-compatible, and designs need spacing, orientation and thermal reliefs to avoid bridging and poor hole fill. Few through-hole parts or SMT on both sides usually mean selective soldering instead."
:::

::: long
"Wave soldering is the classic way to solder through-hole parts in volume. The through-hole components are inserted from the top, and the board goes onto a conveyor that runs in this order: a fluxer that sprays flux onto the underside, a preheat zone that warms the board and activates the flux so it doesn't shock the board, and then the solder pot, where a pump pushes molten solder up through a nozzle to form a standing wave. The underside of the board skims across that wave at a slight angle for two to four seconds. The solder wets the exposed leads and pads, and capillary action pulls it up into the plated holes, filling the barrel. After the wave there's a cooling section.

Often there are two waves: a turbulent one first, to get solder into tight spots and around glued SMT parts, and a smooth laminar one to wipe off excess and clear bridges.

What it means for design is the main point. The whole bottom surface goes through the wave, so SMT parts on the bottom have to be glued first and be the kind that tolerate it, like chip resistors and capacitors or SOT packages, not fine-pitch ICs or BGAs. You orient multi-pin parts along the travel direction, add dummy thieving pads at the end of pin rows to carry off excess solder, leave spacing so tall parts don't shadow neighbours, and use thermal reliefs on pins tied to planes so they heat enough to fill.

Typical defects are bridges, icicles, insufficient fill and skipped joints. For boards with just a few through-hole parts, or SMT on both sides, I'd go to selective soldering or pin-in-paste."
:::

::: terms
- **Wave soldering**: soldering by passing the board over a standing wave of molten solder.
- **Flux**: cleaning agent that lets solder wet metal.
- **Preheat**: pre-wave warming of the board.
- **Chip wave / lambda wave**: turbulent first wave and laminar second wave.
- **Dross**: oxidised solder skimmed off the pot.
- **Shadowing**: tall parts blocking solder from reaching a following pad.
- **Solder thief**: extra pad at the end of a pin row that drains excess solder.
- **Hole fill**: percentage of the barrel filled with solder.
- **Pallet / wave carrier**: fixture masking areas from the wave.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the board goes over a wave of molten solder."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the sequence: flux, preheat, wave(s), cooling, and says why preheat and flux exist.
- Explains capillary hole fill and contact time with a number ($t=L_c/v$).
- Describes the two-wave arrangement (turbulent then laminar).
- States design implications: glued wave-compatible bottom SMT, orientation, thieves, spacing, thermal reliefs, no fine-pitch bottom parts.
- Lists defects and root causes, and names alternatives (selective soldering, pin-in-paste) with when each is better.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Wave-soldering a board with fine-pitch SMD on the bottom.** Bridging and shadowing.
- **Forgetting adhesive.** Bottom SMD parts without glue float off in the wave.
- **Orienting SOICs across the wave.** The trailing pins bridge. Align the long axis with travel.
- **Direct connection of through-hole pins to large planes.** The plane sinks heat and the barrel fills poorly; use thermal reliefs.
- **Mixing up wave and reflow.** Wave contacts only the bottom and the solder comes from a pot, not from paste.
- **Skipping preheat.** The cold board chills the solder (poor fill) and risks thermal shock.
:::

::: deep
## The physics of a joint in the wave

A through-hole joint has a pin in a plated barrel with a small clearance (the annular gap). When the board touches the wave:

1. Flux has already removed oxide from the pin and barrel.
2. Molten solder wets the pin and barrel wall; surface tension and the narrow gap create **capillary rise** up the barrel, towards the top pad.
3. When the board leaves the wave, solder in the gap solidifies, forming the hole-fill and a bottom fillet.

Capillary rise in a narrow gap of width $g$ is roughly $h \approx 2\gamma\cos\theta/(\rho g\, g)$ for a parallel-plate gap, with surface tension $\gamma$ (about 0.5 N/m for molten solder), density $\rho$ (about 7,400 kg/m³ for SAC) and contact angle $\theta$. For a gap of 0.2 mm and a good contact angle near 30°, that is $h \approx 2\cdot0.5\cdot0.87/(7400\cdot9.81\cdot0.0002)=0.06$ m, so 60 mm of ideal rise. That is far above a 1.6 mm board thickness, which is why capillary action fills the holes well *if* the metal is hot and clean, and why poor fill almost always means a thermal or wetting problem rather than a geometry limit (idealised estimate; real flow is limited by solidification).

## Process variables and defects

| Variable | Too low | Too high |
|---|---|---|
| Solder temperature | Poor wetting, insufficient fill, icicles | Dross, board/parts damage |
| Preheat | Thermal shock, poor fill | Flux burn-off, no cleaning action |
| Contact time | Skips, insufficient fill | Parts overheated, copper dissolution |
| Conveyor angle (a few degrees) | Solder does not peel cleanly at exit: bridges, icicles | Poor contact, skips |
| Flux amount | Bridging, poor wetting | Residue, corrosion risk |

(The angle row is typical practice: a few degrees of conveyor tilt helps solder peel away cleanly at the exit.)

Defects and fixes:
- **Bridging** between pins: orientation, thieves, laminar wave, smaller pad-to-pad exposure, more flux.
- **Icicles/spikes**: too cold a pot, long lead stick-out, insufficient flux.
- **Insufficient hole fill**: thermal mass of planes; use reliefs, more preheat, longer contact.
- **Skips/shadowing**: tall parts blocking the wave, mask openings, poor wetting.
- **Part loss**: unglued bottom SMD.

## Design rules (typical)

1. Keep lead protrusion below the board to about 1-2 mm.
2. Orient SOT/SOIC with pins parallel to the wave direction; add a thief pad at the trailing end.
3. Use thermal relief spokes on through-hole pins connected to planes.
4. Keep bottom-side SMD to chip components and small packages, glued, with spacing for shadowing (often 1-2 mm or more in the direction of travel; ask the assembler).
5. Provide at least 2-3 mm of keep-out around the board edges for conveyor fingers.
6. Use a mask opening only where solder is wanted.

## When to choose something else

- **Selective soldering**: few through-hole joints, SMT parts on both sides, tight spacing; slower but flexible.
- **Pin-in-paste**: connectors rated for reflow; avoids a separate soldering process.
- **Hand soldering**: prototypes and rework.

See [[q0599]] for the full decision flow and [[q0598]] for why through-hole persists.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked to compare reflow and wave, give the one-liner: "In reflow the solder is already on the board as paste and the heat comes to it; in wave, the heat and solder come as a flowing molten bath to a bare board." Then list the bottom-side constraint.
:::

::: applications
- **Power supply boards** with big electrolytic capacitors, transformers and terminal blocks.
- **Appliance and industrial controls** with relays and connectors.
- **Older or low-cost consumer products** that are mostly through-hole.
- **Mixed boards** with glued bottom-side chip parts and through-hole connectors on top.
- **Educational kits** assembled by wave to keep costs low.
:::

::: assumptions
- Lead-free alloys (SAC) assumed; tin-lead pots run cooler.
- Settings quoted are typical; each machine, flux and board needs tuning.
- Assumes a conventional single or dual wave. Lambda, chip and nitrogen-inerted waves are variants.
- The capillary-rise estimate is an idealisation of two smooth plates; in reality, flux, oxide, temperature and freezing limit fill.
- Boards with heavy thermal mass need pallets, longer preheat or selective soldering.
:::

::: followups
### Why does wave soldering require glue for bottom-side SMD parts?
The wave would wash them off, since there is no paste to hold them and the molten solder flows past. The adhesive is dispensed before placement, the part is placed, and the glue is cured in an oven. That holds the part through the wave.

### Why orient SOIC packages parallel to the board's travel?
When the board exits the wave, solder peels off the trailing edge of the pins. If the pin row is parallel to travel, the solder drains off the end; if it's perpendicular, the pins act as a comb that bridges. A thief pad at the trailing end absorbs excess.

### How can a through-hole pin tied to a ground plane fill poorly?
The plane conducts heat away, so the barrel doesn't reach solder temperature, and the capillary front freezes. Thermal relief spokes limit the heat sink, and increased preheat or contact time helps.

### What's the difference between a chip wave and a laminar wave?
The chip (turbulent) wave is a jet that forces solder into gaps and around glued SMD parts, avoiding shadowed joints. The laminar wave is smooth and quiet; it wipes off excess solder and helps clear bridges.

### Why not wave solder a board with a fine-pitch QFP on the bottom?
At 0.5 mm pitch, solder bridges the leads easily, flux and shadowing cause skips, and the part would be thermally stressed. Fine-pitch parts are reflowed; the bottom through-hole joints use selective soldering instead.
:::

::: interpretations
This is the companion to reflow ([[q0602]]): in conversation, "wave" refers to through-hole soldering by a molten solder wave. It isn't related to wave soldering of other materials. The question is usually asked right after SMT vs through-hole ([[q0598]]).
:::
