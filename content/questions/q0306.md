::: basics
### In plain words
Temperature changes how well a lithium cell works. In the cold it gives less power and less usable capacity, because its internal resistance goes up. Charging it below about 0 °C can permanently damage it. In the heat it works well but wears out faster and gets less safe. So devices measure the temperature and limit charging to a safe window.

### Picture it
Think of honey. At room temperature it pours easily. Put it in the fridge and it flows slowly and resists the spoon. Warm it up and it pours again. A cold cell is like cold honey: the lithium ions move slowly, so it is harder to pull current out. The picture breaks in one place: cold honey takes no harm, but charging a cold cell can do lasting damage. A cold cell that is only discharged recovers when it warms up.

### Words you need
- **Internal resistance**: the cell's own opposition to current, which grows when cold ([[q0303]]).
- **Voltage sag**: the drop in terminal voltage when current flows through that resistance.
- **Lithium plating**: lithium turning into metal on the anode during cold or fast charging. It is permanent and can cause shorts.
- **NTC thermistor**: a small sensor whose resistance falls as it warms. The charger reads it to get temperature.
- **Aging**: the slow loss of capacity over time, faster when hot ([[q0301]]).

### The simplest example
Take a 2 Ah cell with 50 mΩ internal resistance at 25 °C, discharged at 1C, which is 2 A. The sag is $2 \times 0.05 = 0.10$ V. At 0 °C the resistance is roughly 2.5 times higher, or about 125 mΩ, so the sag is about 0.25 V. The cell hits its cutoff voltage sooner, so you get less capacity out. The same cell would be blocked from charging at that temperature.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page puts numbers on resistance and capacity versus temperature and explains plating, aging and the heat limits.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand that a battery is an electrochemical device whose internal resistance and chemistry are strongly temperature dependent, and that **cold hurts performance while heat hurts life and safety**. The interviewer wants the asymmetry: cold gives voltage sag and lost capacity (reversible), forbidden charging (lithium plating), while heat gives accelerated aging and the thermal-runaway risk. The hidden sub-skills are linking this to $I^2R$ self-heating, to charger and BMS design (NTC, temperature windows) and to what you would actually do in a product.
:::

::: why
Real products live outside the lab: drones in winter, phones on a dashboard, wearables on skin, e-bikes in the sun. Most "my battery died at 20 % in the cold" and "my battery swelled" tickets are temperature stories. Charger and BMS thresholds, derating curves, heater circuits and thermal design all come from this one topic, so it is a good interview question for checking whether you think about the system and not just the cell.
:::

::: concepts
- **Internal resistance rises in the cold.** Ionic conduction in the electrolyte and charge-transfer at the electrodes are thermally activated (Arrhenius). See [[q0303]].
- **Terminal voltage and the $IR$ drop.** A bigger $R$ means more sag at the same current, so the cutoff is reached earlier ([[q0305]]).
- **Self-heating.** Heat generated is $P = I^2 R$, so a cold cell heats itself more, which partially helps it recover.
- **Lithium plating.** At low temperature (and high SoC or high current) lithium intercalation is slow, so lithium deposits on the graphite surface as metal instead.
- **Calendar and cycle aging.** Parasitic reactions (SEI growth, electrolyte decomposition) speed up with temperature, so heat shortens life ([[q0301]]).
- **Thermal runaway.** Above a critical temperature exothermic decomposition reactions feed on themselves ([[q0332]]).
:::

::: answer
"Temperature affects a LiPo in pretty much every way, and it's asymmetric. In the cold, the electrolyte gets more viscous and the reactions slow down, so the internal resistance goes up a lot. As a rough model, it goes up about two and a half times at 0 degrees C and maybe five to six times at minus 20, compared to room temperature. The same current then gives a bigger $I R$ drop, so the voltage sags more, the cell hits the cutoff early, and you only get a fraction of the rated capacity. For a 2 amp-hour cell at 1C my model says about 1.95 amp-hours at 25 degrees but only about 1.6 at 0 and well under half at minus 20. That is mostly reversible; warm it up and it comes back.

The bigger deal is charging in the cold. Below about 0 degrees, lithium can't intercalate into the graphite fast enough, so it plates as metal, and that is permanent and can cause shorts. So chargers use an NTC to block charging outside roughly 0 to 45 degrees.

In the heat it's the opposite: resistance drops so performance looks great, but aging accelerates, gas generation increases, and above about 60 degrees you're approaching the region where safety margins vanish. So in practice you monitor temperature, limit charge current outside the sweet spot, and design thermally."
:::

::: technical
**Resistance vs temperature.** Both the ohmic and charge-transfer parts of the impedance follow roughly Arrhenius behaviour:

$$R(T) = R_{25}\,\exp\!\left[\frac{E_a}{R_g}\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_{25}}\right)\right]$$

with $T$ in kelvin and $E_a/R_g \approx 3000$ K (about 25 kJ/mol) as a representative value (it varies by cell). This gives multipliers of 1.7× at 10 °C, 2.5× at 0 °C, 3.8× at −10 °C and 6× at −20 °C relative to 25 °C. At 45 °C it is about 0.53×.

**Voltage sag.** At current $I$, $\Delta V = I R(T)$. For a 2 Ah cell with $R_{25} = 50$ mΩ at 1C (2 A): 0.10 V at 25 °C, 0.25 V at 0 °C and 0.60 V at −20 °C.

**Capacity.** Slower diffusion and the early cutoff reduce *accessible* capacity at a given current. In the model I use here, 1C delivers 1.95 Ah (25 °C), 1.63 Ah (0 °C, 82 % of rated), and 0.41 Ah (−20 °C, 21 % of rated). The heavy loss at −20 °C is largely the voltage hitting 3.0 V early because of the $IR$ drop. At a low current the same cell would deliver much more. Energy shrinks even more: 7.08 Wh, 5.57 Wh and 1.30 Wh respectively.

**Charging.** Safe charge range is typically about 0 to 45 °C (many datasheets), with reduced current in the 0 to 10 °C region and a ban below 0 °C. Discharge range is typically about −20 to 60 °C. Always take these from the cell's datasheet.

**Heat.** Heat generation is roughly $P = I^2 R + I\,T\,(dV_{OCV}/dT)$ (the second term is reversible entropic heat, small). With $R$ larger in the cold, $I^2R$ is larger, so cold cells self-warm under load.

**Aging.** Parasitic reactions roughly double in rate for every 10 °C or so near room temperature (a rule of thumb that varies by chemistry), so a pack living at 45 °C ages much faster than one at 25 °C. Storage at high SoC and high temperature is the worst combination.

**Safety.** Separator shutdown (polyethylene melts at about 130 °C) and exothermic decomposition reactions at higher temperatures define the thermal-runaway threshold. Chargers and BMS enforce temperature cutoffs well below this.
:::

::: long
"Temperature touches almost every parameter of a LiPo, and the effects are asymmetric.

In the cold, the electrolyte is more viscous and the electrochemical reactions are slower. Mathematically, internal resistance follows an Arrhenius law, so it climbs steeply. In a typical model it's about 2.5 times the room-temperature value at 0 degrees and about 6 times at minus 20. At a fixed current, the voltage sag is $I R$, so the terminal voltage is lower and the cell reaches its cutoff early. A 2 amp-hour cell at 1C delivers about 1.95 amp-hours at 25 degrees, around 1.6 at 0 and well under half an amp-hour at minus 20, in my model. The energy drop is worse than the capacity drop, because the average voltage is lower too. The good news is that the cell warms itself, since the heat is $I^2 R$, and the loss is mostly reversible.

Charging in the cold is the real hazard. Below about 0 degrees, lithium ions can't diffuse into the graphite fast enough, so they deposit as lithium metal on the surface. That's permanent capacity loss and it can grow dendrites that short the cell. So the charger watches an NTC thermistor and refuses to charge outside about 0 to 45 degrees, and usually reduces current near the edges.

At high temperature the cell performs well, because resistance is lower, but side reactions speed up. You lose life faster, especially if the cell is stored full, and gas generation gets worse. Past about 60 degrees you're approaching unsafe territory, and eventually thermal runaway.

So for design: put an NTC on the cell, set a charge window, derate current in the cold, size for the sag at the lowest temperature, and manage heat in the enclosure."
:::

::: terms
- **Arrhenius behaviour**: reaction and transport rates, and so resistance, depend exponentially on $1/T$.
- **Internal resistance ($R_{int}$)**: ohmic plus charge-transfer plus diffusion resistance; rises in the cold and with age.
- **Voltage sag**: the drop of terminal voltage below OCV under load, $I R$.
- **Lithium plating**: metallic lithium deposition on the anode, from cold, fast or overcharging.
- **NTC thermistor**: negative-temperature-coefficient resistor used to sense cell temperature.
- **Derating**: reducing allowed current or charge voltage when temperature is outside the ideal window.
- **Thermal runaway**: self-accelerating heating from exothermic decomposition reactions.
- **Calendar aging**: capacity loss over time, even at rest, strongly accelerated by temperature and high SoC.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "cold reduces capacity, heat is dangerous".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates **reversible** loss (cold sag, accessible capacity) from **irreversible** damage (plating when charging cold, accelerated aging when hot).
- Gives an Arrhenius model with numbers (about 2.5× at 0 °C, about 6× at −20 °C) and works out $IR$ sag for a real current.
- Explains why charging and discharging have *different* temperature windows (0 to 45 °C vs −20 to 60 °C, typically).
- Mentions self-heating, and parallel-cell current hogging as hot cells get lower resistance.
- Describes the design response: NTC on the cell, charger JEITA-style current/voltage derating, pre-heating, and sizing for the cold-case sag.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The cold battery lost its capacity."** The charge is still there. It cannot be extracted at that current because of the extra $IR$ drop. Warm it or lower the current and it returns.
- **Charging a cold battery because "it's low".** The cell is worst at accepting charge when cold. Plating damage is not reversible.
- **Using the room-temperature internal resistance for sizing.** A converter that works at 25 °C may brown out in the cold because the 3.3 V rail input drops below minimum.
- **Believing heat is "good performance".** The lower resistance is real, but each 10 °C costs life, and a hot, high-SoC cell ages the fastest.
- **Measuring temperature far from the cell.** An NTC on the PCB lags the cell by many minutes and reads the electronics, not the cell.
:::

::: deep
## Why cold is bad: the physics

Three things slow down as temperature falls:

1. **Ionic conductivity of the electrolyte** falls because the carbonate solvent gets more viscous and the salt dissociates less.
2. **Charge-transfer kinetics** at each electrode (the actual intercalation reaction) slow down. This usually dominates at low temperature.
3. **Solid-state diffusion** of lithium in the graphite and in the cathode particles slows, so lithium builds up near the surface.

All three are thermally activated, so resistance follows an Arrhenius law:

$$R(T) = R_{25}\,\exp\!\left[\frac{E_a}{R_g}\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{298.15\ \text{K}}\right)\right]$$

## Self-heating: the cold cell fights back

At 1C with $R = 0.3$ Ω (−20 °C for this cell) the generated power is $2^2 \times 0.3 = 1.2$ W, versus $2^2 \times 0.05 = 0.2$ W at 25 °C. A small pouch cell has a heat capacity of maybe 1 to 2 J/(g·K) and a mass of tens of grams, so 1.2 W warms it by a few kelvin per minute if it is insulated. That's why a cold battery often "wakes up" after a few minutes of load, and why some designs insulate the pack or use a heater.

## Why cold charging is the dangerous one

During charge, lithium must enter the graphite lattice. In the cold, the intercalation rate drops and the diffusion into the graphite is slow, so the anode surface potential can fall below 0 V versus Li/Li$^+$. At that point the thermodynamically easier reaction is plating metal. The plated lithium:

- is partly irreversible (it becomes "dead lithium" and loses capacity);
- can form needle-like dendrites that pierce the separator;
- reacts with the electrolyte and generates heat and gas.

Fast charging at low SoC and high current also pushes the anode potential down, which is why the allowed charge current is reduced in the 0 to 10 °C region and why a hot-weather charge is faster than a cold one. See [[q0317]] and [[q0318]]. A typical charge IC reads an NTC, and charging is suspended below about 0 °C and above about 45 °C (many parts also reduce current or voltage in a warm band, in the style of JEITA guidelines).

## Why heat is bad: aging and safety

Unwanted side reactions (growth of the solid-electrolyte interphase on the anode, electrolyte decomposition, cathode dissolution) are thermally activated. A common rule of thumb says that the rate roughly doubles every 10 °C, though the factor depends on chemistry and SoC. So a pack that sits at 45 °C and 100 % SoC loses calendar life much faster than one at 20 °C and 50 %.

At very high temperature the separator can shrink or melt (polyethylene around 130 °C) and exothermic cathode-electrolyte reactions begin, which can lead to thermal runaway ([[q0332]]). Practical protection thresholds are far below that, typically cutting off discharge around 60 to 70 °C and charge around 45 to 50 °C (cell and product dependent).

A further trap in parallel packs: a hot cell has lower resistance, so it takes a bigger share of the current, which heats it more. Good thermal design keeps parallel cells at similar temperatures.

## Design responses

- **Sense the right thing**: put an NTC in thermal contact with the cell, not just the PCB. A 10 kΩ NTC with a 10 kΩ pull-up gives about 1.65 V at 25 °C on a 3.3 V reference, 2.44 V at 0 °C and 1.09 V at 45 °C (B ≈ 3380 K) ([[q0307]]).
- **Charge window and derating** in the charger IC or BMS.
- **Size for cold**: calculate the sag at the lowest temperature and make sure the downstream regulator still works. $V_{min} = V_{OCV} - I R(T_{min})$.
- **Insulate or heat** the pack for outdoor use; accept lower power in the cold.
- **Cool**: heat spreading, airflow, or limiting charge current in hot environments.
:::

::: applications
- **Drones and RC**: pilots warm packs before flying in winter, because a 5C hover current on a cold pack gives a large sag and a low-voltage alarm early.
- **Smartphones**: charge current is throttled when the phone is hot, and charging is blocked when it is cold. The battery gauge corrects its capacity for temperature.
- **Electric vehicles and e-bikes**: pre-heating the pack before fast charging; liquid or air cooling to hold the pack near 25 to 35 °C.
- **Wearables and IoT in cold climates**: a coin-cell-like internal resistance rise can reset a radio burst; engineers add bulk capacitance to carry the peak.
- **Medical and military portable gear**: specified operating windows, often with heater circuits.
:::

::: assumptions
- The multipliers and capacity numbers come from a **representative single-activation-energy model** and a generic 2 Ah, 50 mΩ cell. Real cells differ by chemistry, format and design (high-power cells are less sensitive).
- Window values (0 to 45 °C charge, −20 to 60 °C discharge) are **typical** for consumer LiPo. Always use the cell datasheet; some specialised cells charge down to −10 °C or discharge to −40 °C.
- The loss in cold capacity assumes a **fixed current** and a **fixed voltage cutoff**. With a lower current, or by lowering the cutoff (not recommended), you get more energy out.
- At moderate temperature changes the loss is reversible; below about −20 °C or after cold charging the damage may not be.
- A different answer applies if the question is about **calendar life**: the high-temperature, high-SoC storage condition dominates.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a cold battery "recover" after you warm it up?
The lithium is still in the electrodes. The resistance was high and the diffusion slow, so at that current the voltage dropped to cutoff early. When the temperature rises, the resistance returns to normal, the voltage under load rises again, and the remaining capacity becomes accessible. If you had also charged it cold and plated lithium, that part does not come back.

### A 2 Ah cell with 50 mΩ at 25 °C powers a 3.3 V device through a regulator that needs 3.5 V minimum. At 0 °C, 1 A load, is the sag a problem?
At 0 °C the resistance is about $2.5 \times 50 = 125$ mΩ, so the drop is $1 \times 0.125 = 0.125$ V and the OCV must be at least 3.625 V, which is roughly 25 % SoC on the typical curve. At 25 °C the drop is 50 mV, so the OCV can go down to 3.55 V (about 14 %). So at 1 A the cold costs you roughly 10 % of the usable range, annoying but survivable. At 5 A the cold drop is 0.625 V, so you would need an OCV of 4.125 V, meaning the pack is only usable when it is nearly full. That is the brownout-in-winter failure.

### Why is it more dangerous to charge cold than to discharge cold?
Discharge in the cold just causes sag and an early cutoff, which is reversible. Charge in the cold pushes lithium into graphite that cannot accept it, so lithium metal deposits. That damage is irreversible, reduces capacity, and can create internal shorts.

### How would you measure cell temperature, and where?
A 10 kΩ NTC thermistor (B around 3380 K) on a divider read by the ADC or the charger's TS pin. It should be glued or taped to the cell surface (ideally near the center, where it is hottest) and not only on the PCB. For packs, multiple NTCs at the hottest and coldest locations. You also check slope (dT/dt) to detect a runaway earlier than an absolute threshold.

### What if the pack is in a fast charger at 40 °C ambient? What does the charger do?
A good charger limits current or the CV setpoint as the cell temperature rises (derating in a warm band, often in the style of JEITA guidelines), because high temperature plus high voltage accelerates side reactions and gas. It might terminate the charge at a reduced voltage (such as 4.1 V) and stops completely when the NTC reaches the upper limit, typically about 45 to 50 °C.
:::

::: interpretations
"Performance" could mean capacity, voltage sag and power capability (cold), or lifetime and safety (hot). The answer covers both sides and separates reversible from irreversible effects. If the interviewer's focus is a specific product (drone, phone, wearable), tie the cold sag and the charge window to that product.
