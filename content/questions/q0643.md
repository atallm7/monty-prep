::: basics
### In plain words
Through-hole parts have metal legs that go through holes in the board and are soldered on the far side. Surface-mount parts sit flat on pads on the board's surface and are soldered there. Surface-mount parts are smaller and easy for machines to place. Through-hole parts hold on much more strongly. Most boards use both.

### Picture it
Think of hanging things on a wall. Sticky tape is quick and works well for light things, and you can put tape on both sides of a thin panel. A heavy mirror needs screws that go through the wall. Surface-mount is the tape and through-hole is the screw. Tape is not weak in every way, though: for fast electrical signals the short, tidy connection is better.

### Words you need
- **Lead**: a metal leg on a part.
- **Pad**: a small flat copper patch on the board's surface that a part is soldered to.
- **Plated hole**: a drilled hole lined with copper, which a through-hole lead is soldered into.
- **Reflow**: melting printed solder paste in an oven to solder surface-mount parts. See [[q0602]].
- **Inductance**: how strongly a wire resists fast changes in current. Longer leads have more. See [[q0039]].

### The simplest example
Consider a 100 nF capacitor (a part that stores a little charge) used to steady a power supply. With long through-hole leads it adds about 10 nH of inductance. Mounted flat on pads it adds about 1 nH. At 100 MHz, 10 nH acts like about 6.3 Ω of opposition, and 1 nH like about 0.63 Ω. The flat mount is ten times better at high speed, so supply-steadying capacitors are almost always surface-mount.

Now a connector that people plug in a thousand times. Its small solder joints on surface pads could crack or peel off, so connectors often keep through-hole pins.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds how each type is assembled, board density and cost, failure modes such as cracked ceramic capacitors, and how to choose.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can compare two mounting technologies on engineering axes instead of slogans. The interviewer wants density, cost and automation, parasitics and high-frequency behaviour, mechanical strength and vibration, thermal and current handling, prototyping and rework, and how each is assembled (reflow vs wave or selective soldering). A mature answer also says that real boards are mixed technology, that connectors and heavy parts often stay through-hole for strength, and that surface-mount dominates because of automation and density, not because through-hole is "bad".
:::

::: why
Choosing the mounting technology affects board area, assembly cost, reliability and even how you can debug. Putting a heavy connector on two small SMD pads will rip it off the board; putting all passives through-hole will make the board enormous and expensive to build. Knowing the trade-offs also helps in practical situations: breadboard-friendly prototypes, parts that must withstand repeated plugging, power parts that need a heat sink, and high-speed parts where lead inductance matters. This is a common warm-up question for DFM and layout interviews.
:::

::: concepts
- **Through-hole technology (THT)**: leads pass through plated holes and are soldered on the opposite side.
- **Surface-mount technology (SMT)**: parts sit on pads on the surface and are reflow soldered. See [[q0602]].
- **Wave and selective soldering**: how through-hole joints are made in volume. See [[q0603]].
- **Parasitic inductance of leads**: longer leads mean higher ESL and lead inductance. See [[q0039]].
- **Mechanical strength**: through-hole joints carry load through the barrel; SMD pads rely on small solder fillets.
- **Component size and density**: chip passives are much smaller than axial/radial parts. See [[q0018]].
- **Rework and prototyping**: hand-solderability, breadboard compatibility.
:::

::: answer
"The big picture is that surface-mount wins on density, automation and high-frequency behaviour, and through-hole wins on mechanical strength and power handling. With surface-mount, parts sit on pads and a pick-and-place machine puts them down and a reflow oven solders them all at once, both sides of the board if needed. The parts are smaller, so the board shrinks, and the leads are short, so inductance is lower, which matters for decoupling and high-speed signals. Through-hole parts have leads that go through plated holes. They're bigger and need drilling, which uses board space on every layer, and assembly is by wave or selective soldering or by hand, so it's slower and costs more in volume.

But through-hole is stronger. A connector that gets plugged in a thousand times, a big electrolytic capacitor, a transformer, or a TO-220 bolted to a heat sink gets its mechanical strength from the leads and barrel, not from a small solder fillet. Through-hole is also easier to prototype, because you can hand-solder it and use it on a breadboard.

In practice most boards are mixed: SMT for the bulk of the parts, through-hole for connectors and heavy or high-power parts, sometimes with through-hole tabs on SMD connectors for strength. I'd pick based on size, frequency, mechanical load and who will assemble it."
:::

::: technical
**Comparison.**

| Aspect | Surface-mount (SMT) | Through-hole (THT) |
|---|---|---|
| Size / density | Small parts; components on both sides; roughly an order of magnitude less area for passives | Large bodies; holes occupy every layer, blocking routing |
| Assembly | Paste printing, pick-and-place, reflow (automated, fast) | Insertion (manual or machine), wave or selective soldering, or hand |
| Cost at volume | Low per-placement cost | Higher; insertion and extra process steps |
| Parasitics | Short leads, lower L and C; better at high frequency | Longer leads, higher inductance; poor for high speed |
| Mechanical strength | Fillets only; weak for heavy or plugged parts | Strong; barrel and clinch carry load |
| Power and heat | Pad and copper area limit current and heat; good for exposed-pad parts | Better for large currents, heat sinks, tab mounting |
| Prototyping | Hard by hand at fine pitch; no breadboard | Easy hand soldering and breadboard use |
| Rework | Hot air, small pads; risk of lifting | Easy for simple joints; hard if plane-connected ([[q0637]]) |
| Reliability | Susceptible to board flex (ceramic caps crack), thermal cycling at large parts | Robust to vibration and shock |

**Illustrative numbers.**
- *Parasitic inductance*: a leaded part with about 10 mm of total lead length has roughly 10 nH using the 1 nH/mm rule of thumb; an SMD 0603 mount may be about 1 nH including vias. For a 100 nF capacitor, self-resonant frequency is $f = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$: 5.0 MHz at 10 nH versus 15.9 MHz at 1 nH. At 100 MHz the leaded version looks like $2\pi f L = 6.3\ \Omega$ of inductive reactance.
- *Plated through-hole barrel*: a 1.0 mm hole with 25 µm plating in a 1.6 mm board has cross-section $\pi\cdot1.0\text{ mm}\cdot0.025\text{ mm} = 0.079\ \text{mm}^2$ (as much copper as a 2.2 mm wide, 1 oz trace) and resistance $\rho L/A = 1.72\times10^{-8}\times1.6\times10^{-3}/7.85\times10^{-8} = 0.35$ mΩ.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 1 nH/mm lead inductance and the mounting inductance figure are order-of-magnitude rules of thumb; real values depend on lead geometry, height above the return plane and the via layout. Plating thickness (commonly around 20 to 25 µm average) varies by fabricator and class.

**Assembly flows.**
1. *SMT*: print paste (stencil) → place → reflow → inspect (AOI, X-ray) ([[q0602]]).
2. *THT*: insert leads → wave solder (whole board bottom passes over a solder wave) or selective solder (nozzle per joint) or hand solder ([[q0603]]).
3. *Mixed*: SMT top first (reflow), glue and wave solder SMD on bottom with THT on top, or reflow-then-selective-solder the through-hole parts. Each extra process adds cost and thermal cycles.
:::

::: short
"Surface-mount parts sit on pads and are placed by machine and reflowed, so they're small, cheap to assemble in volume, usable on both sides and have short leads and low inductance. Through-hole parts go through plated holes and are wave or selectively soldered; they're bigger and costlier to assemble but mechanically strong and good for power, so connectors and heavy parts often stay through-hole. Most boards are mixed. Prototypes favour through-hole for hand soldering."
:::

::: long
"The core trade-off is density and automation against strength and ease of hand assembly. Surface-mount parts sit on pads. A stencil prints paste, a pick-and-place machine drops parts, and a reflow oven solders everything at once, on both sides if needed. The parts are small, so the board shrinks, and the leads are short, so the parasitic inductance is low. A 100 nanofarad capacitor with 10 nanohenries of lead inductance resonates around 5 megahertz and looks like 6 ohms at 100 megahertz, while a mounted SMD capacitor with about 1 nanohenry resonates near 16 megahertz and stays capacitive much higher. That's why decoupling is always SMD.

Through-hole parts have leads through plated holes, and they're soldered by wave, selective soldering or by hand. They need a drilled hole on every layer, which blocks routing, and the process is slower and costs more at volume. What you get in return is mechanical strength: a plated barrel with a clinched or flanged lead carries real load, so connectors, large electrolytic capacitors, transformers, switches and TO-220 power devices bolted to a heat sink stay through-hole. The barrel is also a robust conductor: a 1 millimetre hole has about 0.35 milliohm of barrel resistance and as much copper as a 2.2 millimetre wide trace.

Rework and prototyping matter too. Through-hole is easy to hand-solder and put on a breadboard; fine-pitch SMD isn't. But a through-hole pin connected to a plane can be harder to rework than an SMD part because of heat sinking.

So most real boards are mixed: SMT for the bulk, through-hole for the parts that need strength or power, sometimes SMD connectors with through-hole tabs for retention. I'd choose by size, frequency, mechanical load, power, and who is assembling it."
:::

::: terms
- **SMT / SMD**: surface-mount technology / device.
- **THT**: through-hole technology.
- **Wave soldering**: board passes over a standing wave of molten solder; used for through-hole (and glued SMD on the bottom).
- **Selective soldering**: miniature solder fountain applied to individual through-hole joints.
- **Plated through-hole (PTH)**: drilled hole with copper plating on its barrel, connecting layers and holding a lead.
- **Pick-and-place**: machine that places SMD components on pasted pads.
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance, including leads.
- **Mixed technology**: boards with both SMT and THT.
- **Clinch**: bending a through-hole lead on the underside to hold the part before soldering.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "SMD is smaller and through-hole is stronger".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Compares along **explicit axes** (density, assembly, cost, parasitics, strength, power, prototyping, rework).
- Gives **numbers**: lead inductance and SRF (5 vs 16 MHz for 100 nF), barrel resistance and copper area.
- Explains **assembly flows** (reflow vs wave/selective) and the complexity of **mixed** boards.
- Says why **connectors and heavy parts** stay through-hole, and mentions hybrid options (SMD connectors with through-hole tabs).
- Notes **surprises**: through-hole pins on planes can be harder to rework; ceramic SMD caps crack with board flex.

:::

::: mistakes
- **"Through-hole is obsolete."** It's still the right choice for connectors, power and mechanical load.
- **Believing SMD is always cheaper.** For a few hand-built boards, through-hole parts and hand soldering may be cheaper.
- **Ignoring board flex.** Large ceramic capacitors near board edges or screw holes crack; through-hole parts are less prone.
- **Overlooking the hole cost.** Every through-hole pin consumes routing area on every layer.
- **Assuming SMD can't handle current.** Power SMD packages with exposed pads and wide copper handle large currents; it's about the pad and copper design.
- **Forgetting the mixed-technology process cost.** Wave or selective soldering a few through-hole parts adds a process step and thermal exposure.
:::

## What actually differs

At the circuit level a resistor is a resistor either way. The differences are physical: how the part touches the board, and how that touch is made in manufacturing.

## Density and cost

A 1/4 W axial resistor has a body about 6 mm long and leads spaced about 10 mm apart, so its footprint is on the order of 25 mm². A 0603 chip resistor's land pattern is about 2.5 mm × 1 mm = 2.5 mm². That's roughly a ten-fold area reduction, and the chip can go on both sides. Each through-hole pin also takes up a hole, a pad, and routing blockage on every layer. In a dense board, through-hole pins act like vias through your whole stackup.

Assembly cost follows automation: pick-and-place machines place tens of thousands of chip components per hour and reflow solders them all at once. Through-hole insertion is slower; wave soldering is quick for the board but needs fixtures, pallets and separate process control. For a few boards or a single prototype, hand soldering is feasible either way, and through-hole is far easier for beginners.

## Electrical behaviour at frequency

Leads are inductors. Take a 100 nF capacitor with a 20 mΩ ESR:

$$|Z| = \sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2},\qquad f_{SRF} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}.$$

Through-hole parts also couple differently: longer loop areas pick up more noise and radiate more. This is why RF and high-speed circuits are almost entirely SMD.

## Mechanical and thermal behaviour

- **Vibration and shock**: a heavy through-hole part supported by its barrel survives vibration better; an SMD tall electrolytic needs adhesive or underfill support.
- **Insertion forces**: connectors take plug and unplug forces of many newtons; through-hole or SMD with through-hole mounting tabs absorb them.
- **Board flex**: large ceramic SMD capacitors can crack if the board bends (screwing down, depanelising); orient them and place them away from flexed regions. Through-hole parts are less sensitive.
- **Thermal cycling**: large SMD parts with a CTE mismatch to the board (ceramic packages) put strain on small fillets; through-hole leads act as compliant springs.
- **Heat and current**: through-hole power parts bolt to heat sinks via a tab; SMD power parts use exposed pads soldered to copper and vias. Both can handle amps; the SMD design depends on copper and vias ([[q0540]]).

A through-hole barrel is also a high-current conductor: 1 mm drill with 25 µm plating is 0.35 mΩ and carries as much copper as a trace 2.2 mm wide at 1 oz.

## Assembly processes

**SMT**: stencil-print paste, place parts, reflow ([[q0602]]). Double-sided boards reflow the first side, flip, and reflow the second; heavy parts on the second side may need adhesive ([[q0636]] covers the weight limit).

**THT**: insert, then wave solder: flux, preheat, and a pass over a molten solder wave. SMD parts on the underside need adhesive to stay on during the wave. In selective soldering a programmable nozzle solders chosen joints, avoiding already-soldered SMD.

**Mixed**: a common sequence is SMT reflow on top, then bottom SMT (if any) reflow, then through-hole insertion and selective soldering. Each process step adds time, cost and thermal exposure, which is why designers try to avoid through-hole parts when SMD alternatives exist, and why many SMD connectors exist.

## Design implications

- Through-hole pins on plane layers use thermal reliefs to allow soldering ([[q0538]], [[q0637]]).
- Hole sizes follow lead diameter plus clearance; annular ring and aspect ratio come from the fab ([[q0641]]).
- Keep through-hole parts on one side where possible to simplify wave/selective soldering.
- For SMD connectors that see mechanical loads, add through-hole tabs or large pads, and consider adhesives or strain relief.
- For prototype boards intended for hand assembly, prefer larger SMD sizes (0805, SOIC) or through-hole where space allows.

## When to choose which

| Situation | Suggested |
|---|---|
| High-volume consumer product | SMT everywhere possible; through-hole only for connectors/heavy parts |
| Breadboard or perfboard prototype | Through-hole or breakout modules |
| High-frequency or fast digital | SMT, small parts, short loops |
| Plug-in connectors, switches, heavy transformers | Through-hole (or SMD with through-hole tabs) |
| High-power device on a heat sink | TO-220 / TO-247 style through-hole, or SMD with exposed pad plus copper |
| Harsh vibration | Through-hole or reinforced SMD (adhesive, underfill) |
:::

::: applications
- **Phones, laptops, wearables**: almost entirely SMT, with tiny parts on both sides.
- **Industrial and automotive controls**: SMT with through-hole connectors and power parts for ruggedness.
- **Power supplies**: through-hole transformers and bulk capacitors, SMT controllers and passives.
- **Hobby and education**: through-hole kits and breadboards for easy soldering.
- **Test fixtures and prototype boards**: through-hole headers and connectors for frequent plugging.
- **RF and high-speed digital**: SMT for short loops and controlled impedance.
:::

::: assumptions
The comparison is for typical rigid-board electronics and standard parts. Numbers such as area ratios and lead inductance are order-of-magnitude, depending on component and layout. Exceptions abound: SMD connectors can be very strong with proper design; some through-hole parts exist for RF (feed-through capacitors); and in very low volume or with simple boards through-hole can be cheaper. Cost comparisons depend on volume: at low volume setup costs (stencils, programming) dominate. "Through-hole" in this page means plated through-hole leads, not press-fit pins, which are a separate technique used for heavy connectors without solder. A different valid framing contrasts "automated vs manual" assembly rather than "SMT vs THT".
:::

::: followups
### Why are decoupling capacitors always SMD?
Because of lead inductance. A leaded 100 nF capacitor with 10 nH has a self-resonant frequency of about 5 MHz and looks like 6.3 Ω at 100 MHz, while an SMD mount at about 1 nH resonates near 16 MHz and is 0.6 Ω at 100 MHz. Fast IC supply currents contain content well above 10 MHz, so the lower-inductance mount supplies it. Placement and via inductance matter too ([[q0503]]).

### What are the problems of mixing SMT and through-hole on one board?
Extra process steps (wave or selective soldering after reflow), thermal exposure for already-soldered parts, adhesives for bottom-side SMD, fixtures and pallets, and constraints on component placement (keepouts near through-hole pins for the solder wave or nozzle). Designers reduce mixed assembly by using SMD alternatives or placing all through-hole parts on one side. Cost and complexity rise with each extra process.

### Why do connectors often stay through-hole?
Mechanical load. Plugging and unplugging puts tens of newtons on the housing, and an SMD fillet on tiny pads would peel off. A through-hole pin or a mounting tab inside a plated hole spreads the load into the board. SMD connectors often add through-hole tabs or large anchor pads for the same reason. Through-hole also handles higher current on power connectors.

### Can SMD parts carry high current?
Yes, if the pad and copper are designed for it. Power MOSFETs, inductors and regulators in SMD packages carry tens of amps with wide copper pours, multiple vias, and exposed pads. The limit is thermal: heat spreads into the board copper. Through-hole parts with heat sinks scale to higher power more easily, but SMD with proper layout covers most board-level needs.

### What happens to ceramic SMD capacitors if the board flexes?
Ceramic is brittle. Board bending puts strain on the solder fillet and the capacitor body, which can crack it. The crack may cause a short or leakage that shows up later. Mitigations: keep large ceramics away from board edges, screw holes and connectors; orient them with the long axis parallel to the bend or break line, so the bending strain does not pull the two terminations apart; use flexible terminations (soft termination) or smaller sizes; and control depanelising.

### Scenario: you need to rework a through-hole connector on a 6-layer board and it won't release. How do you decide between continued rework and a redesign?
Short-term: preheat the board, use a desoldering station or hot air from the bottom, add leaded solder to lower the melting point, or cut the plastic body off and remove pins individually; it's a one-off. If this will recur (lots of boards, service replacement), the layout should change: add thermal reliefs on the shield and ground pins ([[q0637]]) or switch to a press-fit or SMD connector with through-hole tabs. I'd weigh volume and risk: if one board in a prototype run, rework; if every board in production, redesign.
:::
