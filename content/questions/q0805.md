::: basics
### In plain words
A pushbutton connects a microcontroller pin to ground when you press it. A pin left unconnected reads random values, so a resistor pulls it up to the supply voltage when the button is open. The pin reads high when released and low when pressed. The only other trick is that real buttons chatter for a few milliseconds when pressed.

### Picture it
A rubber band holds a flag up. That is the pull-up: with nobody touching it, the flag is up (high). Pressing the button yanks the flag to the floor (low). The band is weak, so the button easily wins. The chatter is like dropping a ball: it bounces several times before it settles, and each bounce looks like a new press.

### Words you need
- **Pull-up resistor** ([[q0786]]): a resistor from the pin to the supply that holds it high when nothing else drives it.
- **Floating input**: a pin connected to nothing, so it reads unpredictable values.
- **Active-low**: "pressed" is shown by a logic 0 (low), not a 1.
- **Contact bounce**: the rapid on-off chatter of switch contacts when pressed.
- **Debounce**: filtering out the bounce so one press counts once.

### The simplest example
Wire the button from the pin to ground, with a 10 kΩ pull-up to 3.3 V. Released: the pin sits at 3.3 V and reads 1, with no current flowing. Pressed: the pin is shorted to ground and reads 0, and 3.3 V / 10 kΩ = 0.33 mA flows through the resistor. The bounce may produce ten edges in a few milliseconds. A simple software fix is to accept a new state only after it has stayed the same for a short time, such as 20 ms.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers hardware debouncing with a capacitor, polling versus interrupts, and protecting a pin that people can touch.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know that a button is **a switch to ground with a pull-up** (a floating input is undefined), and whether you go on to the three real-world issues: contact bounce (hardware RC and Schmitt trigger or software debounce), protection (series resistor, ESD) and detection method (polling versus interrupt, wake from sleep). Choosing active-low with a pull-up and justifying it (internal pull-ups, power draw only while pressed, noise immunity) is the expected default, and sizing the resistor with a number shows you can design it.
:::

::: why
Buttons look trivial and are a classic source of bugs: random triggers from a floating pin, double-counting from bounce, a stuck MCU after ESD from a front-panel button. The same pattern (pull resistor, filter, input threshold, debounce) applies to every digital input from the outside world, so interviewers use it to test GPIO fundamentals ([[q0786]], [[q0783]]) quickly.
:::

::: concepts
- **Floating input**: an undriven CMOS pin picks up noise and reads randomly; it needs a defined level ([[q0786]]).
- **Pull-up/pull-down resistor**: sets the idle level; the switch overrides it when closed.
- **Active-low**: pressed reads 0; usual default because pull-ups are common and the switch ties to ground.
- **Contact bounce**: mechanical contacts chatter for milliseconds on make and break.
- **Schmitt trigger**: input with hysteresis, so a slowly changing RC voltage gives one clean transition.
- **Polling vs interrupt**: how firmware notices the press ([[q0794]], [[q0799]]).
- **ESD/series protection**: resistor and clamp for user-accessible inputs.
:::

::: answer
"The standard circuit is a pushbutton from the GPIO pin to ground, with a pull-up resistor from the pin to 3.3 V. When the button is open the pull-up holds the pin high, a logic 1. When it's pressed, the pin is shorted to ground, a logic 0. So the button is active-low. A 10 kΩ pull-up is typical, which draws only 0.33 mA, and only while the button is held. Many MCUs have an internal pull-up I can enable, so the external resistor can be dropped.

The reason for the pull-up is that without it the pin would float when the button is open and read random values.

Then the real-world issue is bounce. A mechanical button chatters for a few milliseconds when pressed, so the MCU might see ten edges. I can fix that in hardware with a capacitor, say 100 nF to 1 µF, across the switch, forming an RC with the pull-up, into a Schmitt-trigger input; or in software by sampling every millisecond and accepting a state only after it's been stable for about 20 milliseconds.

For firmware, either poll it from a timer tick, or use a pin interrupt on the falling edge if I want to wake from sleep, with a debounce timer so I don't get repeated interrupts. If the button is on a front panel I'd add a series resistor, maybe 1 kΩ, for ESD and current limiting."
:::

::: technical
**Basic circuit (active-low).** $V_{DD}\rightarrow R_{pu}\rightarrow$ node $\rightarrow$ MCU pin; node $\rightarrow$ switch $\rightarrow$ GND. Open: pin = $V_{DD}$ minus input leakage drop (negligible: 1 µA × 10 kΩ = 10 mV). Closed: pin $\approx0$ V, current $V_{DD}/R_{pu}=0.33$ mA at 10 kΩ and 3.3 V ($P=1.1$ mW only while pressed). Internal pull-ups are typically in the range of tens of kΩ (vendor dependent, often 20-50 kΩ; check the datasheet): at 40 kΩ the pressed current is 82 µA.

**Choice of $R_{pu}$.** Larger values: lower power but weaker against noise and leakage coupling and slower edges with pin capacitance (RC). Smaller: stiffer but more current. 4.7-10 kΩ for on-board; 1-4.7 kΩ in noisy or long-wire situations; internal 20-50 kΩ is acceptable for short traces on a quiet board. With a long cable (capacitance 100 pF/m) the edge is $\tau=RC$ - e.g. 10 kΩ × 1 nF = 10 µs, still fast compared with debounce times.

**Pull-down alternative (active-high).** Pull-down to GND, switch to $V_{DD}$. Works but pressed current flows from VDD, a short on the switch wiring to a supply is more hazardous than a short to ground, and some MCUs lack pull-downs. Active-low is conventional.

**Bounce.** Contacts bounce for typically about 1 to 10 ms (varies widely by switch and age; some tactile switches are within 1-2 ms, worn ones longer), producing a burst of edges.

**Hardware debounce.** Pull-up $R_{pu}$ to the node with capacitor $C$ from node to ground and the switch in series with $R_s$ (limits capacitor discharge current: $V_{DD}/R_s=10$ mA with 330 Ω). Release: the node charges as $V=V_{DD}(1-e^{-t/R_{pu}C})$; reaching $0.7V_{DD}$ takes $1.2\,R_{pu}C$. With 10 kΩ and 1 µF that is 12.0 ms. A bounce lasting 1 ms re-charges only to $3.3(1-e^{-0.1})=0.31$ V, well below the Schmitt threshold, so bounce is rejected. Press: discharge through $R_s$: to $0.3V_{DD}$ in $1.2R_sC=0.40$ ms. The MCU pin must have a Schmitt-trigger input (many GPIOs do; check the datasheet) or the slow edge can cause multiple transitions or extra current.

**Software debounce.** Sample every 1-10 ms; accept a new state after $N$ consecutive equal samples ($N\cdot T_s\approx20$ ms), or a shift-register method. Costs no hardware.

**Protection.** Series resistor 330 Ω-1 kΩ at the pin limits current into the ESD diodes during an overvoltage or ESD event; for user-accessible buttons add a TVS diode or a low-capacitance ESD array to ground at the connector.

**Detection.** Poll from a tick (1 kHz) with debounce; or GPIO interrupt on the falling edge (wake-up) plus a debounce timer.
:::

::: long
"I'll draw it active-low, which is the conventional way. The pushbutton goes between the GPIO pin and ground. A pull-up resistor goes from the pin to 3.3 V, about 10 kΩ. When the button is open, the resistor pulls the pin up to 3.3 V so the MCU reads a clean logic high. When I press the button, it shorts the pin to ground, so it reads low, and the resistor just drops the 3.3 V, which is 0.33 milliamps, only while the button is held. Most MCUs have a software-enabled internal pull-up, tens of kilohms, so for a simple board I can leave the external resistor off.

Why the pull-up at all? A CMOS input draws almost no current, so if it's left floating it picks up noise and reads random values. The pull-up gives it a defined idle state.

The second thing to deal with is bounce. When contacts close they chatter for maybe 1 to 10 milliseconds, so the MCU sees a burst of edges. The hardware fix is a capacitor, for example 1 µF from the pin to ground, with the pull-up forming an RC of 10 milliseconds, plus a small series resistor to limit the capacitor's discharge current through the switch. The slow edge goes into a Schmitt-trigger input, so it makes one clean transition. The software fix is cheaper: sample the pin every millisecond and only change the debounced state after about 20 consecutive identical samples.

For detection, I can poll the debounced value from a timer tick, or use a falling-edge interrupt, especially if the button needs to wake the MCU from sleep. With an interrupt I'd start a debounce timer in the ISR and ignore further edges until it expires.

And if the button is user-facing, I'd put a series resistor, around a kilohm, between the switch and the pin, plus an ESD protection diode, since people can zap it."
:::

::: terms
- **Pull-up resistor**: connects the pin to VDD so it idles high.
- **Active-low**: asserted state is logic 0.
- **Contact bounce**: rapid make/break of switch contacts upon actuation.
- **Debounce**: ignoring bounce to produce one clean event.
- **Schmitt trigger**: input stage with hysteresis between rising and falling thresholds.
- **Floating input**: an input not driven to a defined level.
- **Series (current-limit) resistor**: limits current into pin protection diodes.
- **TVS / ESD diode**: clamp that shunts transient energy away from the pin.
:::

::: mistakes
- **No pull resistor**, relying on the pin to be "low by default". A floating CMOS input is undefined.
- **Putting a large capacitor directly across the switch with no series resistor**: each press shorts the charged capacitor, giving a big current spike that wears contacts.
- **Using an RC debounce into a non-Schmitt input.** The slow edge sits in the linear region, causing multiple transitions and extra current.
- **Triggering an interrupt on both edges without debounce**, producing dozens of ISR calls per press ([[q0799]]).
- **Using blocking `delay(20)` for debounce**, freezing the system.
- **Ignoring ESD** on user-accessible buttons.
:::

Figure: With the default 10 kΩ and 1 µF, a clean release crosses the 70% threshold after 12 ms (1.2 RC). A short bounce lifts the node only to about 0.31 V and is pulled back down by the next contact closure, so the MCU never sees a high. The two thresholds are only reliable with a Schmitt input, whose hysteresis prevents multiple transitions in the slow ramp. Drag C down to 0.1 µF (τ = 1 ms): the same 1 ms bounce now lifts the node to about 2.1 V, close to the 2.31 V threshold, and a slightly longer bounce would cross it, giving the MCU a spurious extra edge.

The cost of this approach is a 12 ms release delay, a bigger bill of materials and the dependence on the input threshold; for many designs the software method is preferred.

## Software debounce

Sample every $T_s=1$ ms (from a timer tick, [[q0793]]) and use a counter:

```c
static uint8_t cnt, stable = 1;          // 1 = released (active low)
void tick_1ms(void) {
    uint8_t raw = gpio_read();
    if (raw == stable) { cnt = 0; }
    else if (++cnt >= 20) { stable = raw; cnt = 0; on_change(stable); }
}
```
Detects a press after 20 ms of consistent readings. Noise shorter than 20 ms cannot flip it. The ms cost is trivial: a handful of instructions per tick (about 0.1% CPU).

Alternative, a shift-register debounce: `hist = (hist << 1) | raw; if (hist == 0x00) pressed;` with a sample period of 2 ms and an 8-bit history gives a 16 ms window.

## Interrupt-driven buttons

If the MCU must sleep and wake on a press, configure the pin as an external interrupt (falling edge) with the internal pull-up, as in [[q0799]]. The ISR should:
1. Disable (or ignore) that interrupt line, or just set a flag and record the time.
2. Start a debounce timer (10-20 ms).
3. When the timer fires, re-read the pin; if it is still low, register the press and re-enable the interrupt.

Without step 1 each bounce would trigger the ISR again, about 10 to 20 interrupts per press, wasting energy and possibly overloading the system ([[q0800]]).

## Protection for user-accessible inputs

A panel button with a cable can bring in ESD of kilovolts. Typical protection: a TVS or low-capacitance ESD diode at the connector to take the bulk of the surge, a series resistor of 330 Ω to 1 kΩ at the pin to limit what reaches the MCU's internal clamp diodes, and optionally a small capacitor (10-100 nF) to ground. Example: if the TVS holds the connector at about 5 V during a transient, the 1 kΩ resistor passes roughly $(5-3.3-0.6)/1\text{ k}\approx1.1$ mA into the pin's clamp diode to VDD, which is harmless.

## Many buttons

A matrix keypad scans rows and reads columns: $R\times C$ keys with $R+C$ pins, at the cost of ghosting (add diodes) and scan firmware; or an I2C GPIO expander or capacitive-touch controller ([[q0782]]). Resistor ladder on one ADC pin is another option: each button selects a different divider ratio.

## Choosing active-high or active-low

| | Active-low (pull-up) | Active-high (pull-down) |
|---|---|---|
| Idle | pin = VDD | pin = GND |
| Pressed | pin = GND | pin = VDD |
| Current while pressed | VDD/R | VDD/R |
| Internal resistor availability | pull-ups nearly universal | pull-downs less common |
| Wire short to ground | looks like a press | looks like idle |
| Convention | default | used when logic is positive |

Active-low is the safe default.
:::

::: applications
- **User interface buttons** on consumer products, with software debounce on a 1 kHz tick.
- **Wake-up buttons** on battery devices using a falling-edge interrupt and internal pull-up.
- **Limit switches and door sensors** (same circuit with a longer cable, a lower pull-up and ESD protection).
- **Emergency-stop and safety inputs**, often normally-closed switches so a cut wire reads as a press.
- **Keypads** (matrix scan) and rotary encoder push-buttons.
- **Reset and boot-mode buttons** on development boards, usually with RC debounce.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes a 3.3 V logic MCU with GPIO input thresholds around 0.3 and 0.7 VDD (typical CMOS); some inputs have Schmitt triggers built in, others don't, check the datasheet.
- Bounce duration varies by switch from under a millisecond to over 10 ms; software debounce times of 10-50 ms are common, and I pick 20 ms as an example.
- The internal pull-up value (tens of kΩ) is a typical figure; some parts have programmable or weak (e.g. 30-50 kΩ) pull-ups, others none.
- For safety-critical inputs, use redundant or normally-closed contacts, supervised inputs, and diagnostics; the simple circuit here is not enough.
- If the button signal is long-distance or industrial, use optocoupled or 24 V inputs with proper conditioning.
:::

::: followups
### Why active-low rather than active-high?
Pull-ups are available on nearly every MCU, the switch ties the pin to ground (a common reference), current flows only while pressed, and a failure that shorts the wire to ground looks like a press (fail-safe in many designs). It is also the convention in most reference designs and libraries.

### How do you size the pull-up?
Balance power and noise immunity: 10 kΩ at 3.3 V draws 0.33 mA only while pressed, adequate for on-board traces. Use 4.7 kΩ or lower for long wires or noisy environments, 100 kΩ or the internal 20-50 kΩ for ultra-low power on short traces. Check that the resulting RC with pin and wire capacitance is fast compared with the debounce time, and that leakage times R is far below the logic threshold.

### How does the RC debounce time constant relate to the bounce time?
Choose $1.2\,R_{pu}C$ longer than the longest bounce (the time to reach 70% VDD): for 5 ms bounce choose at least about 5 ms, i.e. $R_{pu}C\approx4$-5 ms (10 kΩ and 470 nF). Too long a constant makes the button sluggish; too short misses bounce at release.

### How would you detect a long press versus a short press?
Record a timestamp on the debounced press edge and compare at release: below about 500 ms is a short press, above is a long press. Or start a timeout at press and fire the long-press action when it expires while the button is still held. Use unsigned subtraction for wrap safety ([[q0791]]).

### What happens if you use both an interrupt and debounce incorrectly?
A bouncing edge interrupt fires repeatedly; if the ISR does heavy work or restarts a timer each time, the debounce window never closes (or you handle the same press ten times). The fix: first edge disables the interrupt and starts a one-shot timer, then re-read the pin and re-enable.
:::
