::: testing
Whether you can build the efficiency from charge and energy bookkeeping instead of quoting a number. Expected: (1) ideal efficiency $\eta=V_{out}/(nV_{in})$ because input current is $nI_{out}$; (2) the loaded output $V_{out}=nV_{in}-I_{out}R_{out}$ with $R_{out}$ from the slow- and fast-switching limits; (3) overheads (gate drive, quiescent current); (4) for regulated pumps, efficiency drops to $V_{reg}/(nV_{in})$, so the choice of ratio matters; (5) light-load and heavy-load behaviour.
:::

::: why
Charge-pump efficiency is not obvious: a pump can be 97% efficient at one operating point and 75% at another with the same parts. Knowing the structure tells you how to choose frequency, capacitor and switch size, when a pump beats an LDO, and when a boost is needed ([[q0283]], [[q0274]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Charge conservation**: $I_{in}=nI_{out}$ for an $n\times$ pump. See [[q0285]].
- **Output resistance**: $R_{out}=\sqrt{R_{SSL}^2+R_{FSL}^2}$. See [[q0284]].
- **Efficiency**: $P_{out}/P_{in}$. See [[q0162]].
- **Quiescent current** and gate-drive power. See [[q0165]].
- **Regulated output** wastes the excess ratio like an LDO.
- **Switching loss** $\propto f$, conduction loss $\propto R_{out}$.
:::

::: answer
"For an ideal $n$-times pump, the input current is $n$ times the output current, because charge is conserved. So power in is $V_{in}\cdot nI_{out}$ and power out is $V_{out}I_{out}$, which gives $\eta=V_{out}/(nV_{in})$. Under load the output droops, $V_{out}=nV_{in}-I_{out}R_{out}$, so efficiency is $1-I_{out}R_{out}/(nV_{in})$. That's the whole conduction story: all the loss is $I_{out}^2R_{out}$.

$R_{out}$ has two parts: $1/(fC_{fly})$ from charge sharing, and about $8R_{sw}$ for a doubler. Raising frequency lowers the first until the switches limit it. Then on top there's overhead: gate-drive power scales with frequency and $I_Q$ matters at light load.

For a doubler at 3.3 V, 500 kHz, 1 µF and 1 Ω switches, $R_{out}$ is about 8 Ω. At 20 mA that's 6.435 V and about 97% efficiency; at 100 mA it's 5.78 V and about 87%.

If the pump is regulated to a fixed voltage, say 5 V, efficiency becomes $5/(nV_{in})$: with a 2x ratio and 3.3 V in that's 76%, whatever the load. That's why good parts hop between 1x, 1.5x and 2x. So a pump is efficient only when its ratio matches the need."
:::

::: technical
**Ideal:** $\eta=\dfrac{V_{out}}{nV_{in}}$.

**Loaded (open loop):** $V_{out}=nV_{in}-I_{out}R_{out}$,
$$\eta=\frac{nV_{in}-I_{out}R_{out}}{nV_{in}}=1-\frac{I_{out}R_{out}}{nV_{in}}$$
Doubler: $R_{SSL}=\dfrac1{fC_{fly}}$, $R_{FSL}=8R_{sw}+4R_{ESR}$, $R_{out}\approx\sqrt{R_{SSL}^2+R_{FSL}^2}$ (approximation). For this two-phase doubler with equal switch resistances and negligible ESR the exact steady-state result is $R_{out}=\dfrac{1}{fC_{fly}}\coth\!\left(\dfrac{1}{8fR_{sw}C_{fly}}\right)$, which has the same two limits; the root-sum-square overestimates it by at most about 9% (worst near the crossover), so it is slightly conservative. Here it gives 8.25 Ω against 8.17 Ω exact.

**Including overhead:** $P_{in}=V_{in}(nI_{out}+I_Q)+P_{gate}$, $P_{gate}\approx N_{sw}Q_gV_{drv}f$.

**Regulated pump:** $\eta\approx\dfrac{V_{reg}}{nV_{in}}\cdot\dfrac{I_{out}}{I_{out}+I_Q/n}$, with $n$ the smallest ratio such that $nV_{in}\ge V_{reg}$ plus drops.

**Example** ($V_{in}=3.3$ V, 500 kHz, $C_{fly}=1\ \mu$F, $R_{sw}=1\ \Omega$, $R_{out}=8.25\ \Omega$):

| $I_{out}$ | $V_{out}$ | $\eta$ (conduction) | with overhead* |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 mA | 6.435 V | 97.5% | 96.8% |
| 50 mA | 6.188 V | 93.8% | 93.5% |
| 100 mA | 5.775 V | 87.5% | 87.4% |

*$I_Q=100\ \mu$A and four switches with 100 pC gate charge at 3.3 V, 500 kHz (0.66 mW), assumed values.
:::

::: short
"Input current is $n$ times output current, so ideal efficiency is $V_{out}/(nV_{in})$. Under load $V_{out}=nV_{in}-I_{out}R_{out}$, so conduction efficiency is $1-I_{out}R_{out}/(nV_{in})$, with $R_{out}$ from $1/(fC)$ and the switch resistance. Add gate-drive and quiescent overhead. A regulated pump wastes the excess ratio, so efficiency is $V_{reg}/(nV_{in})$, which is why parts hop between 1x, 1.5x, 2x."
:::

::: long
"I'd do it as charge and energy bookkeeping.

Charge: an $n$-times pump draws $n$ times the output current from the input. So power in is $V_{in}\cdot nI_{out}$, power out is $V_{out}I_{out}$, and the efficiency is $V_{out}/(nV_{in})$. It's the voltage ratio of what you got to what you ideally would.

Loaded: the output droops by $I_{out}R_{out}$, where $R_{out}$ has two limits. In the slow-switching limit the flying capacitor droops by $I/(fC)$ each cycle, so $R_{SSL}=1/(fC)$. In the fast-switching limit the switches' resistance dominates, about $8R_{sw}$ for a doubler. They combine roughly as a root sum of squares. All the conduction loss is $I_{out}^2R_{out}$, so $\eta=1-I_{out}R_{out}/(nV_{in})$.

Numbers: 3.3 V in, 500 kHz, 1 µF, 1 Ω switches: $R_{SSL}=2\ \Omega$, $R_{FSL}=8\ \Omega$, $R_{out}=8.25\ \Omega$. At 20 mA the output is 6.435 V and efficiency 97.5%. At 100 mA it's 5.775 V and 87.5%. Overhead like gate drive and a 100 µA quiescent current trims about 0.5% at 20 mA and matters more at light load.

Finally the regulated case, which is what most ICs are. If the output is held at 5 V from a doubler with 3.3 V in, the excess is wasted: $5/6.6=76\%$. Mode-hopping parts choose 1.5x when $V_{in}$ is high enough, but efficiency still saws between roughly 75% and 100% across the battery range. So I'd check the average over the real input range, not a single peak number."
:::

::: terms
- **Conversion ratio ($n$)**: ideal $V_{out}/V_{in}$.
- **Ideal efficiency**: $V_{out}/(nV_{in})$.
- **SSL**: slow-switching limit, $R=1/(fC)$.
- **FSL**: fast-switching limit, switch-resistance dominated.
- **Regulation loss**: excess ratio discarded like an LDO.
- **Gate-drive loss**: $Q_gV_{drv}f$ per switch.
- **Quiescent current**: bias drawn from the input.
- **Mode hopping**: automatically changing $n$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "charge pumps are about 90% efficient".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives $\eta=V_{out}/(nV_{in})$ from $I_{in}=nI_{out}$.
- Uses $R_{out}$ with SSL/FSL and gets numbers.
- Splits losses: conduction, gate drive, quiescent.
- Explains regulated efficiency $V_{reg}/(nV_{in})$ and mode hopping.
- Notes the optimum frequency trade-off and the crossover $f=1/(8R_{sw}C)$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Quoting efficiency without the operating point.**
- **Forgetting $I_{in}=nI_{out}$** and computing efficiency from output voltage only.
- **Ignoring $R_{out}$ at high load.**
- **Assuming regulated pumps keep their unregulated efficiency.**
- **Adding $R_{SSL}$ and $R_{FSL}$ linearly** without saying it's an approximation.
- **Raising frequency indefinitely**: gate-drive loss grows with $f$.
:::

::: deep
## Loss budget at 20 mA

Example: doubler, 3.3 V, 500 kHz, 1 µF, $R_{sw}=1\ \Omega$, 20 mA.

| Loss | Formula | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Conduction (switches + charge sharing) | $I^2R_{out}=0.02^2\times8.25$ | 3.3 mW |
| Gate drive | $4\times100\text{ pC}\times3.3\times5\times10^5$ | 0.66 mW |
| Quiescent | $3.3\times100\ \mu$ | 0.33 mW |
| Output power | $6.435\times0.02$ | 128.7 mW |
| Input power | $3.3\times0.0401+0.66$ mW | 133.0 mW |
| Efficiency | | 96.8% |

(The conduction loss cannot be split into a separate $I^2R_{SSL}$ plus $I^2R_{FSL}$: adding them, $0.8+3.2=4.0$ mW, is the linear-sum bound and is pessimistic. The exact $R_{out}=8.17\ \Omega$ gives 3.27 mW, so the root-sum-square 3.3 mW is within about 1% here.)

## Optimizing frequency and capacitor

$R_{out}$ falls with $f$ until $R_{SSL}=R_{FSL}$ at $f_c=1/(8R_{sw}C_{fly})=125$ kHz here. Above that, more frequency only adds gate-drive loss. So the best frequency is near or somewhat above $f_c$; below it, capacitor size sets $R_{out}$. A larger switch lowers $R_{sw}$ but raises gate charge. Details in [[q0289]].

## Regulated pumps: the ratio sawtooth

If the output is regulated to $V_{reg}=5$ V (by burst or a linear stage), the pump runs at the smallest ratio with $nV_{in}\ge V_{reg}$+drops, and $\eta=V_{reg}/(nV_{in})$:

Figure: Efficiency is $5/(nV_{bat})$. It climbs to nearly 100% at 3.33 V in 1.5x mode (ideal, before $R_{out}$), then falls to 79% at 4.2 V, and the real part would lose a few points more to $R_{out}$ and overhead. A 2x-only pump would be 60% at 4.2 V. In practice ratio hops need headroom for $R_{out}$ drop, so the hop point moves up.

Comparison: an LDO from 4.2 V to 5 V is impossible (it can't step up), so a pump or boost is required; a boost at 4.2 V to 5 V would be 90%+ ([[q0274]]).

## Light-load behavior

At 100 µA load the overhead (100 µA quiescent plus $f$-proportional switching) dominates: $P_{out}\approx0.66$ mW against $P_{in}\approx3.3\times(0.2+0.1)\text{ mA}+0.66\text{ mW}=1.65$ mW, so $\eta\approx40\%$. Many parts use burst mode (reduced frequency) at light load to cut gate losses, at the cost of larger ripple.

::: applications
- **Backlight and flash LED drivers**: mode-hopping efficiency curves are the key spec.
- **Negative-rail ICs**: efficiency matters less than noise at mA loads.
- **Battery-powered RS-232 and sensor interfaces**: quiescent current dominates.
- **Load-switch gate drivers**: microamp pumps, efficiency irrelevant.
- **System power budgeting**: pump efficiency multiplies with upstream stages.
:::

::: assumptions
- $R_{out}$ via root-sum-square (Seeman-style approximation). For the simple doubler it is within about 9% of the exact $\coth$ result (conservative); other topologies and unequal switches need the full charge-multiplier analysis.
- Gate charge (100 pC) and $I_Q$ (100 µA) are assumed illustrative values.
- Doubler with equal $R_{sw}$ and negligible ESR (add $4R_{ESR}$ otherwise).
- Frequency $f$ is the full cycle rate; datasheet oscillators may be quoted at $2f$.
- Regulated efficiency ignores $R_{out}$ drops, which move the hop points.
:::

::: followups
### Why does charge pump efficiency fall as load increases?
$R_{out}$ drops $I_{out}R_{out}$ from the output while input current scales as $nI_{out}$, so $\eta=1-I_{out}R_{out}/(nV_{in})$ falls linearly (unlike an LDO, where it is flat).

### Why can't a doubler from 3.3 V regulated to 5 V exceed 76%?
Its input current is $2I_{out}$ and $V_{out}=5$ V, so $\eta\le5/(2\times3.3)=75.8\%$. The pump makes 6.6 V and the regulator discards 1.6 V.

### Should you raise the switching frequency to improve efficiency?
Only up to a few times the crossover where $R_{SSL}=R_{FSL}$ (125 kHz in the example). At 125 kHz $R_{out}$ is about 10.5 Ω (exact), at 500 kHz about 8.2 Ω, and beyond that it flattens toward $8R_{sw}=8\ \Omega$ while gate-drive loss keeps growing linearly with $f$.

### How does efficiency compare to an LDO making the same voltage drop?
For a step-up an LDO cannot do it. For step-down, a 1/2x pump (followed by regulation if needed) has $\eta\approx V_{out}/(V_{in}/2)$, exactly twice the LDO's $V_{out}/V_{in}$, for any $V_{out}\le V_{in}/2$ minus the $R_{out}$ drop. Near $V_{out}\approx V_{in}/2$ the pump approaches 100% while the LDO is at 50%. Above $V_{in}/2$ the 1/2x ratio cannot reach the output, so you need a 2/3x or 1x mode, or an LDO.

### Scenario: measured efficiency at 50 mA is 85% but you computed 93%. What next?
Check real capacitance (ceramic derating raises $R_{SSL}$), the actual clock frequency, switch $R_{on}$ at the real gate drive and temperature, ESR, and the input current including quiescent and gate drive. Measure input and output power with the same meter and compare $V_{out}$ droop with $I_{out}R_{out}$.
:::
