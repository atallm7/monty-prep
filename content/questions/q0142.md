::: testing
Whether you understand that a real capacitor is an **RLC** device, and that the whole reason for a *bank* of capacitors is frequency coverage and inductance, not capacity. The interviewer wants to hear: they are decoupling (bypass) capacitors that act as local charge reservoirs; each one is only a low impedance over a limited frequency band; one massive capacitor is inductive (and slow) at the frequencies where the load actually demands current. Bonus points for target impedance, anti-resonance and placement.
:::

::: why
Every board with a processor, FPGA or fast converter has a sea of 0402 and 0603 capacitors. If you do not understand why, you will either delete "redundant" ones during cost-down and break the board, or you will add 47 µF caps everywhere and still have a noisy rail. This question also sits behind real debugging: a rail that looks fine on a DMM but has 100 mV of 50 MHz noise is a *high-frequency impedance* problem, and the fix is the right mix and placement of capacitors. This is the power-rail framing; [[q0506]] asks the same thing from the PCB-layout side.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor impedance** $Z = 1/(j\omega C)$: lower impedance at higher frequency and larger $C$. See [[q0024]].
- **Real capacitor model**: series $R_{ESR}$, $L_{ESL}$ and $C$, with a self-resonant frequency (SRF). See [[q0030]] and [[q0031]].
- **Decoupling vs bulk**: small ceramics near each pin handle fast current; bulk capacitors handle slower, larger demands. See [[q0032]] and [[q0143]].
- **Target impedance**: $Z_{target}=\Delta V/\Delta I$; the capacitor bank must keep the rail impedance below it over a wide band.
- **Anti-resonance**: parallel combinations of different capacitors can create impedance peaks between their resonances.
- **Loop inductance**: mounting pads, traces and vias add inductance in series with each capacitor. See [[q0509]].
:::

::: answer
"Those are decoupling or bypass capacitors. A digital chip pulls current in sharp bursts, and the regulator is too far away and too slow to supply it, so the capacitors act as tiny local batteries that supply the burst and keep the rail from dipping.

Why so many? A real capacitor isn't just a capacitance. It has ESR and a small series inductance, so above its self-resonant frequency it stops acting like a capacitor and acts like an inductor. A big electrolytic might be inductive above a few tens of kilohertz to a megahertz, while a 100 nF 0402 stays capacitive up to roughly 20 megahertz. The chip's current demand extends to hundreds of megahertz, so you need a mix of values, each covering its own band, and you need lots of small ones in parallel because paralleling divides the inductance.

Why not one massive capacitor? A bigger capacitor has a bigger package, which means more inductance, and it sits farther from the pins, adding more. So its impedance at 100 MHz is just $2\pi f L$, which is huge. A big cap solves low frequency, but not the high frequency. Ten 100 nF in parallel have a tenth of the inductance of one, and putting them at the pins keeps the loop small. It also costs less than one very large low-ESL part."
:::

::: technical
**Real capacitor.** $Z(f)=R_{ESR}+j\left(2\pi fL_{ESL}-\dfrac{1}{2\pi fC}\right)$, with $|Z|$ minimum at the SRF
$$f_{SRF}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{ESL}C}}$$
Below the SRF it is capacitive; above it, inductive ($|Z|\approx2\pi fL_{ESL}$). At the SRF $|Z|=R_{ESR}$.

**Representative values** (illustrative; vendors differ):

| Part | $C$ | ESL (incl. mounting) | ESR | SRF |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electrolytic / polymer bulk | 1000 µF | ~10 nH | ~30 mΩ | ~50 kHz |
| MLCC 0805 | 10 µF | ~0.8 nH | ~5 mΩ | ~1.8 MHz |
| MLCC 0402 | 100 nF | ~0.5 nH | ~20 mΩ | ~22 MHz |
| 10 × 0402 in parallel | 1 µF | ~50 pH | ~2 mΩ | ~22 MHz |

**Why parallel small parts win.** $N$ identical capacitors in parallel have $C_{tot}=NC$, $L_{tot}=L/N$ and $R_{tot}=R/N$. The SRF is unchanged but the high-frequency impedance $2\pi fL/N$ falls $N$-fold. At 100 MHz, one 1000 µF/10 nH part is $2\pi\cdot10^8\cdot10^{-8}=6.3$ Ω; ten 100 nF/0.5 nH parts are about 0.03 Ω.

**Why different values.** Each part's $|Z|$ is a "V" on a log-log plot with its minimum at its SRF. Several values cover the band from kHz to hundreds of MHz. Between two adjacent values, the larger (inductive) capacitor and the smaller (still capacitive) one form a parallel LC and give an **anti-resonance** peak, e.g. at $\approx1/(2\pi\sqrt{0.8\text{ nH}\cdot100\text{ nF}})\approx17.8$ MHz for the 10 µF and 100 nF pair. Peak height is limited by ESR.

**Design target.** For a 1.0 V rail with ±5% tolerance and a 10 A step, $Z_{target}=0.05/10=5$ mΩ. Compute the combined $|Z|$ and keep it below $Z_{target}$ from the regulator's bandwidth up to where on-package capacitance takes over.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> ESR and ESL depend on package, vendor, DC bias (ceramic capacitance falls with bias), temperature and the PCB pad/via layout. Use vendor S-parameter or SPICE models for real designs; the values above are typical orders of magnitude.
:::

::: short
"They're decoupling capacitors, acting as local charge reservoirs so fast current bursts don't pull the rail down. There are many because a real capacitor has ESR and inductance, so each one is only a low impedance over a limited band, above its self-resonant frequency it looks like an inductor. Paralleling several small parts divides the inductance and gives low impedance out to hundreds of megahertz. One massive capacitor has a big package and long path, so it's inductive exactly where the chip needs help."
:::

::: long
"Those capacitors are decoupling capacitors. A digital IC draws current in short bursts, with edges of nanoseconds. The regulator is centimetres away, with inductance and a control loop that responds in microseconds, so something local has to supply that charge. That's the capacitors: little reservoirs next to the pins.

The reason it takes many is that a real capacitor isn't ideal. It's a capacitance in series with its ESR and its ESL, the equivalent series inductance from the package, pads and vias. Impedance goes down with frequency until the self-resonant frequency, then it goes back up as an inductor. For a 100 nF 0402, with about half a nanohenry, that resonance is around 20 megahertz. Above that it's an inductor. A 1000 microfarad electrolytic is inductive above only tens of kilohertz.

So why not one massive capacitor? Two reasons. First, big capacitors are physically bigger with more ESL, so at 100 MHz a 10 nanohenry part is over 6 ohms, completely useless. Second, even if it had low ESL, it can't be right at every power pin, and the trace and via inductance to get to it adds up.

What works instead is several values in parallel: bulk capacitors for the low end, a few microfarads for the mid range, and many 100 nF or 10 nF parts at the pins. Parallel inductances divide, so ten capacitors have a tenth of the inductance. Different values widen the band, but I have to watch for anti-resonance peaks between them. The goal is to keep the impedance below a target, voltage ripple allowed divided by current step, across the whole frequency range. And placement matters as much as value: short, wide connections and vias right next to the pad."
:::

::: terms
- **Decoupling (bypass) capacitor**: a capacitor close to a load that supplies its fast transient current locally.
- **ESR**: equivalent series resistance of a capacitor; sets the impedance at resonance and damps peaks.
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance from package, pads, traces and vias; makes the capacitor inductive at high frequency.
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, where reactances cancel and $|Z|=R_{ESR}$.
- **Target impedance**: $\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I_{step}$; the PDN impedance ceiling.
- **Anti-resonance**: a parallel resonance between two capacitor values, creating an impedance peak.
- **PDN**: power delivery network, from regulator through board, package and die.
- **Bulk capacitor**: a large-value capacitor that handles low-frequency demand. See [[q0143]].
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "decoupling capacitors filter noise, and different values filter different frequencies."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames the cap as a local **charge reservoir** whose job is to supply di/dt, not just "filter noise".
- Shows numerically that one 1000 µF/10 nH part is 6.3 Ω at 100 MHz while ten small parts are ~0.03 Ω.
- Explains that paralleling **divides inductance**, so many small parts beat one big one even at the same total capacitance.
- Mentions **target impedance** ($\Delta V/\Delta I$), anti-resonance peaks between value families, and ESR damping.
- Says that **placement and loop inductance** (pad, via, plane distance) often matter more than the capacitor's own ESL.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Different values filter different frequencies, like notch filters."** They are each a low-impedance path to ground near their SRF, with a broad V-shaped response, not a sharp filter.
- **"A bigger capacitor is always better."** Above its SRF it behaves like an inductor, and its package ESL is larger.
- **Assuming "10 µF in 0805" is 10 µF at 5 V.** Class 2 ceramics lose capacitance with DC bias, sometimes 50% or more. Check the DC-bias curve.
- **Ignoring the mounting inductance.** Long traces or a via far from the pad can add more inductance than the capacitor itself. Correction: pad to via to plane as short and as wide as possible.
- **Mixing many values without checking anti-resonance.** Adding a mid value can create a peak, and sometimes more of the *same* value in parallel with sensible ESR is better than many different ones.
- **Treating decoupling as a DC problem.** The regulator sets DC; the capacitors handle nanosecond-to-microsecond transients.
:::

::: deep
## What is the capacitor actually doing?

A processor or FPGA switches millions of gates in a few hundred picoseconds. Each transition pulls charge from the rail, so the current is a spiky waveform with content from DC up to several hundred MHz. The regulator and the wiring back to it (see [[q0140]]) cannot provide those spikes: the loop responds in microseconds and the path has nanohenries of inductance.

The voltage across an inductor in the path is $V = L\,di/dt$. If $L = 5$ nH and the current changes 1 A in 1 ns, the rail dips by 5 V. That is impossible, so something close to the load must supply the current: a **local charge reservoir**. A capacitor next to the pin does exactly this. When the chip asks for a burst of charge $Q=I\Delta t$, the capacitor gives it up and its voltage falls by $\Delta V=Q/C$. For $\Delta t=1$ ns and $I=1$ A, $Q=1$ nC, and a 100 nF capacitor droops by only 10 mV (while it later has to be recharged slowly from the supply).

$$Z(f)=R_{ESR}+j\left(2\pi fL_{ESL}-\frac{1}{2\pi fC}\right),\qquad f_{SRF}=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{ESL}C}}$$

Below the SRF, the part is a capacitor: $|Z|\approx 1/(2\pi fC)$. Above it, $|Z|\approx 2\pi fL_{ESL}$. At the SRF it is a resistor equal to the ESR. A plot of $|Z|$ on log axes is therefore a V (or U) shape: falling at -20 dB/dec, flat bottom at ESR, rising at +20 dB/dec.

Three effects combine against the single big capacitor:

1. **ESL scales with size.** Bigger packages have longer current loops. Large electrolytics, even polymer types, are tens of nH for leaded or large SMD cans.
2. **Distance.** One part cannot sit at every power pin. Each extra centimetre of trace is about 5-10 nH, far more than the part's ESL.
3. **Parallelism divides inductance.** $N$ capacitors with $L$ each give $L/N$. This is the same reason why a wide plane has lower inductance than a thin trace.

## Anti-resonance: the price of mixing values

Between the SRFs of two different capacitors, the larger one is inductive and the smaller one is still capacitive. That's a parallel LC, which has a *high* impedance peak. A 10 µF (ESL 0.8 nH) next to a 100 nF (SRF 22 MHz) peaks around $1/(2\pi\sqrt{0.8\text{ nH}\times100\text{ nF}})\approx17.8$ MHz. The peak is limited by ESR: higher ESR damps it at the expense of a higher floor. This is why a PDN is designed with a model, not by rule of thumb alone.

## Choosing the bank in practice

1. **Compute $Z_{target}$** from the rail's tolerance and the worst-case current step: 5% of 1 V over a 10 A step is 5 mΩ.
2. **Bulk** at the regulator output (and board entry) covers the low-frequency range until the regulator loop takes over ([[q0143]]).
3. **Mid-value ceramics** (1-10 µF) cover 100 kHz to a few MHz.
4. **Many 100 nF (and sometimes 10 nF)** at the pins cover tens to hundreds of MHz. Modern guidance often prefers *more of fewer values* with controlled ESR and tight placement over a long ladder of values.
5. **On-package and on-die capacitance** takes over above a few hundred MHz.
6. **Layout**: short, wide connections; vias right at the pad (or via-in-pad); place the smallest capacitors closest to the pin; the loop (pad, via, power plane, ground plane) sets the inductance. See [[q0507]] and [[q0508]].
:::

::: applications
- **FPGA and SoC boards**: BGA parts have dozens of 0402 capacitors on the underside close to the power balls, plus larger parts around the edge, and bulk near the regulator.
- **Microcontroller boards**: a 100 nF at every VDD pin, plus 4.7-10 µF per rail, is the standard pattern.
- **Switching regulator inputs**: a few 10 µF ceramics next to the high-side FET and a small 100 nF at the switch to handle fast edges.
- **High-speed memory (DDR)**: many small capacitors near the DRAM VDDQ pins to support simultaneous switching.
- **Op-amp and ADC supplies**: a local 100 nF plus 1-10 µF at each supply pin to keep the supply impedance low across the band the part's PSRR falls off.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Digital load with fast current edges.** For a slowly varying load (an LED), one bulk capacitor is fine.
- **Parameters are illustrative.** ESR/ESL depend on package, vendor, bias and layout, so use manufacturer models for real designs.
- **The regulator behaves as a low impedance only up to its loop bandwidth.** Above that, capacitors carry the load.
- **Ceramic capacitance derates** with DC bias and temperature, so a "10 µF" 0805 may be only 3-5 µF at 5 V.
- **Alternative valid answers.** Many modern designs use many *identical* mid-value capacitors rather than a ladder of values, because identical parts avoid anti-resonance peaks between different families. Both approaches are correct when verified with an impedance model.
:::

::: followups
### What is the purpose of each of the different values?
Roughly, bulk (tens to hundreds of µF) supplies slow, large demand until the regulator reacts. Mid values (1-10 µF) cover from hundreds of kHz to a few MHz. Small values (10-100 nF) at the pins cover tens to hundreds of MHz. Each is only effective around and below its own SRF, so you combine them to keep $|Z|$ below target across the band.

### Why does adding another capacitor sometimes make noise worse?
Anti-resonance. The new capacitor, in parallel with the existing network at a frequency where the old network is inductive, can form a parallel resonance with a high impedance peak. If the load has energy at that frequency, noise gets *worse*. The fix is to damp it (slightly higher ESR) or choose values so the peak falls where the load has little energy.

### Which matters more, the capacitor value or where you put it?
Usually placement. A 100 nF with a 5 nH loop (long trace, via far away) has an SRF of about 7 MHz and is inductive beyond, so it is already inductive above that. With a 0.3 nH mounting loop added to the 0.5 nH ESL, the same part stays capacitive out to about 20 MHz.

### How many capacitors do you need?
Compute $Z_{target}$ and the impedance of the candidate bank, then add capacitors until $|Z|<Z_{target}$ across the frequency band. For a rough check at high frequency: with $L_{eff}$ per capacitor and $N$ in parallel, $Z\approx2\pi fL_{eff}/N$. To get under 5 mΩ at 50 MHz with 0.5 nH each: $N\ge\dfrac{2\pi\cdot5\times10^{7}\cdot0.5\times10^{-9}}{0.005}\approx31.4$, so 32 parts. That is why big BGAs have so many.

### What if I use a 100 µF ceramic instead of many small ones?
It reduces the low-frequency impedance and takes less board area, but these large ceramics still have more ESL (and are limited by DC-bias derating, microphonics and cost). They cover the mid band well, but the highest-frequency band still needs small parts close to the pins.

### Scenario: the scope shows 80 mV of 100 MHz ripple on a rail with plenty of capacitors. What do you check?
I'd check loop inductance first: pad geometry, via distance, distance from the capacitors to the supply pins, and whether the return plane is directly under the power plane. Then I'd measure with a short ground spring (not a long ground lead, which fakes noise), look at anti-resonance peaks, and check whether the noise correlates with a clock or switching activity.
:::
