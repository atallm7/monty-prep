::: basics
### In plain words
An optocoupler is an LED and a light sensor sealed in one package with an insulating gap between them. Current through the LED makes light, the sensor on the other side sees it and conducts, and no electrical connection crosses the gap. The main number to know is the current transfer ratio, how much output current you get per unit of LED current. It is cheap and robust, but it is slow, and it weakens with age.

### Picture it
Imagine signaling across a canyon with a flashlight. A friend on the far side has a light sensor and reacts when the beam arrives. The two of you share no wire, only light. Over the years the flashlight bulb dims, so the beam gets weaker, and the sensor eventually misses it. The sensor is also slow compared with a wire. Heat makes the bulb dim faster.

### Words you need
- **Optocoupler**: an LED plus a photodetector in one insulating package.
- **Phototransistor**: a transistor switched by light instead of by an input current.
- **Current transfer ratio (CTR)**: output current divided by LED current, $I_C / I_F$.
- **Pulse-width distortion**: unequal rise and fall delays that stretch or shrink a pulse.
- **CMTI**: how fast a common-mode voltage jump the part tolerates without a false output. See [[q0432]].

### The simplest example
Drive the LED with 5 mA from a 3.3 V pin and a resistor: $(3.3 - 1.2) / 5\text{ mA} = 420$ Ω. A new part with 50% CTR can then sink $0.5 \times 5 = 2.5$ mA. After years of aging and heat, CTR may fall to 20%, and the same 5 mA gives only 1 mA. If the output's pull-up resistor (which holds the line high) needs 2.5 mA to pull the output low, the circuit now fails. So you design for the worst-case CTR, not the nominal one. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers sizing the pull-up, why these couplers are slow, why fast voltage edges can trigger them, and when a digital isolator is better.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand an optocoupler as a **component with a transfer characteristic and wear-out**, not as a magic gap. The interviewer wants:

1. The mechanism: input LED, light across an insulating gap, a photodetector (phototransistor, photodiode with amplifier) on the output.
2. The key spec, current transfer ratio $CTR=I_C/I_F$, and why it varies with current, temperature and age.
3. The trade-offs, with real consequences: speed and pulse-width distortion, aging, input current, limited CMTI, noise, cost, and how they compare with digital isolators.
4. Design consequences: bias the LED with margin, design for worst-case CTR, choose the pull-up as a speed-versus-saturation trade.
:::

::: why
Optocouplers are the oldest and most familiar isolators and are still everywhere: PLC inputs, AC-line sensing, relay and solid-state-relay drivers, SMPS feedback. They are cheap and have high isolation ratings. They are also the source of classic failures: a product that works for a year, then the optocoupler's LED has aged, the CTR has dropped, and the output no longer saturates; or a PWM signal that is distorted by microseconds. The question separates people who have used them from those who read the pinout.
:::

::: concepts
- **LED and photoelectric effect**: forward-biased LED emits light; the detector converts light to current ([[q0057]], [[q0072]]).
- **Phototransistor**: a BJT whose base current is the photocurrent, so $I_C=\beta I_{photo}$ ([[q0086]]).
- **Current transfer ratio (CTR)**: $I_C/I_F$ at specified $I_F$ and $V_{CE}$.
- **Saturation and pull-up design**: the output must saturate for a defined low level ([[q0435]]).
- **Galvanic isolation**: light crosses, current doesn't ([[q0436]]).
- **CMTI and barrier capacitance**: fast common-mode edges inject current ([[q0432]]).
:::

::: answer
"An optocoupler is an LED and a light detector in one package, separated by an insulating barrier. You drive current through the LED on the input side, it emits light, and the detector on the output side, usually a phototransistor, conducts in proportion. Electrically the two sides are isolated: only light crosses.

The key spec is the current transfer ratio, the output collector current divided by the LED current. A typical part might have 50% or more at 5 milliamps, so 5 milliamps in gives at least 2.5 milliamps out. The catch is that it varies a lot: from part to part, with temperature, with LED current, and it decreases over the life of the part as the LED ages.

The trade-offs follow from that. It's slow: a basic phototransistor optocoupler has microsecond-scale delays and unequal rise and fall times, so it's good for a status line or a 1 kilohertz enable, not for fast PWM or SPI, unless I choose a high-speed type. It draws milliamps on the input side. The gain aging means I have to design for the end-of-life CTR with margin. Common-mode transient immunity is limited because of the small capacitance across the barrier and the phototransistor's collector-base capacitance, so a fast dv/dt can trigger it falsely. In exchange it's cheap, simple, robust, has a high isolation rating, and has no switching noise of its own. If I need speed or long life I'd go to a digital isolator."
:::

::: technical
**Structure.** Infrared LED (GaAs/AlGaAs), a transparent insulating compound, and a photodetector: a phototransistor in basic parts, a photodiode plus transimpedance amplifier and logic output in high-speed parts, or a photo-triac or photo-MOSFET array in solid-state relays.

**Transfer.** $I_C=CTR\cdot I_F$. Typical basic phototransistor optocouplers are specified with a minimum and maximum CTR (for example 50 to 600% at $I_F=5$ mA, divided into rank bins) *(typical; check the datasheet)*. CTR depends on $I_F$ (peaks at a few mA, falls at very low and very high current), temperature (falls when hot), and age.

**Aging.** LED light output degrades with operating time and with current and temperature, so CTR falls. Datasheets or application notes give degradation guidance; the usual design rule is to design for an end-of-life CTR of a fraction (for example 50% or lower) of the minimum initial value, plus a temperature derating. *Quantities vary by vendor.*

**Speed.** Phototransistor response is limited by the base-collector capacitance (Miller effect) and carrier storage: delay and rise/fall times are in the µs range and depend strongly on load resistance: $t_r\approx2.2R_LC_{out}$ plus an intrinsic phototransistor lag of microseconds *(approximate, device-specific)*. A larger pull-up is slower. High-speed optocouplers (integrated photodiode with an amplifier and logic output) reach 10 Mbaud or more *(vendor-specific)*.

**Isolation.** Typical isolation test voltages are 3.75 to 5 kV$_{rms}$ for basic optocouplers and higher for reinforced-rated parts; the continuous working voltage is much lower and listed separately ([[q0433]]).

**CMTI.** The barrier capacitance (about 0.5 to 2 pF) injects $C\,dv/dt$ into the output. Basic phototransistor types have low CMTI (order of 1 to 10 kV/µs *typical*) while advanced and digital types reach 25 to 100+ kV/µs.

**Input requirement.** The LED needs $I_F$ of 1 to 20 mA, so a 3.3 V GPIO and a series resistor $R_{LED}=(V_{OH}-V_F)/I_F$: $(3.3-1.2)/5\text{ mA}=420$ Ω.

**Comparison with digital isolators.**

| | Phototransistor opto | High-speed opto | Digital isolator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speed | µs | 10 Mbaud+ | 100+ Mbaud |
| Propagation delay | µs | 20 to 100 ns | 5 to 20 ns |
| Pulse-width distortion | large | modest | small |
| Aging | LED wears, CTR drops | LED wears | no LED, long life |
| Input power | mA per channel | mA | low |
| CMTI | low | medium to high | high |
| Cost | lowest | higher | moderate |

*All numbers are typical orders of magnitude, not guarantees.*
:::

::: long
"An optocoupler puts an LED and a light detector in the same package with an insulating gap between them. When current flows in the LED, it emits infrared light, the detector on the other side sees it, and the output conducts. The simplest detector is a phototransistor: the light acts as the base current. Nothing electrical crosses the gap, just light, so the two sides are galvanically isolated and can sit at completely different potentials, within the part's rated voltage.

The number you design around is the current transfer ratio, CTR: the output collector current divided by the input LED current. A typical part has a minimum of 50 percent at 5 milliamps, so 5 milliamps in is at least 2.5 milliamps out. But CTR isn't constant. It varies from part to part by a factor of several, it changes with LED current, it drops at high temperature, and it degrades over the life of the product because the LED loses efficiency with time. So I design with the worst case: the minimum CTR at the temperature extreme and at end of life, which can be a quarter of the datasheet nominal or less.

The trade-offs follow. Speed: a phototransistor optocoupler has microsecond delays, with different rise and fall times, so a PWM's pulse widths get distorted. That's fine for a slow enable or status signal and not fine for 20 kilohertz PWM with dead time or for SPI. High-speed optos exist but cost more. Input power: each channel needs a few milliamps, and the output needs a pull-up that I size for saturation versus speed. Aging, as I said. Common-mode transient immunity: the capacitance across the barrier, about a picofarad, lets fast common-mode edges couple through, and the phototransistor's collector-base capacitance amplifies it, so a fast bridge can trigger false pulses. And the isolation rating: high test voltage, but check the working voltage for continuous use.

On the plus side they're cheap, simple, available in every flavour, from logic to triac and MOSFET-output types, and robust against high voltage. For anything fast, or for a long-life product, I'd go with a digital isolator, but for status inputs and slow control, an optocoupler is hard to beat."
:::

::: terms
- **Optocoupler (opto-isolator, photocoupler)**: LED plus photodetector in one insulating package.
- **Current transfer ratio (CTR)**: $I_C/I_F$, the output-to-input current gain.
- **Phototransistor**: BJT whose base is excited by light.
- **LED forward current ($I_F$)**: current through the input LED.
- **Saturation ($V_{CE(sat)}$)**: output transistor fully on; defines the logic-low level.
- **Pulse-width distortion**: asymmetry between rising and falling delays.
- **CMTI**: tolerance to fast common-mode transients ([[q0432]]).
- **LED aging / CTR degradation**: reduction of light output, and thus CTR, over time.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "LED and phototransistor, isolates, slow".

**An exceptional answer**:
- States CTR and its dependences (current, temperature, age, part-to-part) and **designs to worst-case CTR**.
- Explains why it is slow: phototransistor Miller capacitance and storage, with the pull-up resistor setting rise time.
- Names the saturation-versus-speed trade-off in choosing the pull-up.
- Lists CMTI and barrier capacitance as the limit in noisy bridges.
- Compares with digital isolators and high-speed optos and says when to choose each.
- Distinguishes test voltage from working voltage and mentions safety ratings.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Designing at nominal CTR.** Part-to-part spread, temperature and aging can cut it by a factor of 4 or more.
- **Driving the LED with a voltage source and no resistor.** The LED current is exponential in voltage; it burns or overdrives.
- **Using a phototransistor optocoupler for fast PWM.** The delay mismatch can exceed the dead time.
- **Ignoring the output saturation check.** If $CTR\cdot I_F$ is less than $(V_{CC}-V_{CE})/R_{pull}$, the output sits in the linear region with a poor logic-low level.
- **Assuming isolation voltage equals working voltage.** The test rating is not the continuous rating.
:::

When $I_F$ flows, photons generate electron-hole pairs in the phototransistor's base region. That photocurrent is the base current, so $I_C\approx\beta I_{photo}$. The ratio $CTR=I_C/I_F$ folds together the LED efficiency, the optical coupling and $\beta$.

## CTR is not a constant

| Dependence | Direction | Typical consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Part-to-part | Min to max may differ by 10× or more (bins are sold) | Design to the minimum |
| $I_F$ | Peaks at a few mA, falls at very low (below about 1 mA) and very high current | Bias in the datasheet's specified range |
| Temperature | Falls when hot | Check at maximum operating temperature |
| Aging | Falls with hours of operation, faster at high $I_F$ and temperature | Allow for end-of-life CTR |
| $V_{CE}$ | Mostly flat once the transistor is out of saturation | Saturated operation reduces effective CTR |

An illustrative worst case: initial minimum CTR 50% at 25 °C, a 0.6× factor for temperature, a 0.5× factor for end-of-life aging: $0.5\times0.6\times0.5=15\%$. At $I_F=4.9$ mA, the worst-case collector current is $0.15\times4.9=0.73$ mA. *These derating factors are examples; use the vendor's aging curves and temperature data.*

## Output design: saturation versus speed

For a logic output with pull-up $R_{pull}$ to $V_{CC2}$, the transistor must pass $(V_{CC2}-V_{CE(sat)})/R_{pull}$ to pull the output low. It can supply only $CTR\cdot I_F$. So for a solid logic low:

$$R_{pull}\ge\frac{V_{CC2}-V_{CE(sat)}}{CTR_{min,EOL}\cdot I_F}.$$

For $V_{CC2}=5$ V, $V_{CE(sat)}=0.3$ V, $I_F=4.88$ mA and $CTR_{min,EOL}=20\%$: $R_{pull}\ge4.7/0.976\text{ mA}=4.8$ kΩ. But speed wants the opposite: the output rises through $R_{pull}$ into the load capacitance: $t_r\approx2.2R_{pull}C_{out}$. With $C_{out}=20$ pF and 6.8 kΩ: 0.33 µs plus the phototransistor's own lag of microseconds *(approximate)*.

## The speed of an optocoupler

- **Phototransistor optocouplers**: input to output delay of the order of a few to tens of µs with a typical resistive pull-up, and *unequal* rise/fall delays, so pulse widths shift by microseconds. For a 20 kHz PWM (50 µs period) a 5 µs distortion is 10% of the period.
- **Faster basic types** use a Darlington (slower) or a photodiode with a transistor and an access to the base to speed up.
- **High-speed logic-gate optocouplers** (photodiode + amplifier + totem-pole output) reach about 10 Mbaud and 20 to 100 ns propagation delay *(vendor-specific)*, but need a supply on the output side and cost more.

## CMTI: why fast bridges false-trigger optos

The barrier capacitance $C_{io}\approx0.5$ to 2 pF carries $C_{io}\,dv/dt$. At 1 V/ns that is about 1 mA for 1 pF, comparable to the LED's output current at the low end of the signal range. A bridge switching with 10 to 50 V/ns injects 10 to 50 mA pulses, enough to false-trigger a phototransistor with a high-impedance pull-up. Mitigations: a lower pull-up value (reduces the voltage swing for a given current), a part with an internal Faraday shield (a specific CMTI rating), an extra shield between LED and detector, or a different isolator technology.

## Why people still use them

Cheap, simple, available with logic, Darlington, triac and photo-MOSFET outputs, high isolation voltage and creepage, no radiation of switching noise, and well understood: for AC mains sensing, relay and SSR driving, PLC inputs, SMPS feedback loops (where the CTR variation is part of the loop design), and slow control lines.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A concise answer that stands out: "LED and phototransistor, isolated by light, key spec is CTR, which falls with temperature and age, so I design to end-of-life CTR. They're slow, a few microseconds with distorted pulse widths, and have limited CMTI, so for PWM or gate drive I'd use a digital isolator or an isolated gate driver; for status and slow control they're great."

## Worked example: closing the loop on a full design

Put the earlier pieces into one chain. A 3.3 V GPIO drives the LED through 430 Ω. Nominal numbers give $I_F = (3.3 - 1.2)/430 = 4.88$ mA, which is what the plot above assumed. But two inputs to that equation vary: the GPIO's high level can be below the supply under load (say 3.0 V) and the LED's forward voltage can be higher at low temperature (say 1.4 V). The worst-case current is $(3.0-1.4)/430 = 3.72$ mA, 24 % lower than nominal. Now apply the illustrative 15 % end-of-life CTR from the table: collector current available is $0.15\times3.72 = 0.56$ mA. The pull-up for a guaranteed logic low is then

$$R_{pull}\ge\frac{5-0.3}{0.56\ \text{mA}} = 8.4\ \text{k}\Omega$$

compared with 1.9 kΩ from the nominal 50 % case: a 4.4× spread coming only from tolerances. With 8.4 kΩ and 20 pF of load, the rise time is $2.2\times8400\times20\text{ pF} = 0.37$ µs in the capacitive part, which is small beside the phototransistor's microsecond-level lag, so the pull-up's RC is not the limiting factor here. Designing for the worst case therefore costs a larger pull-up (a slower, weaker high level), not LED power. The alternative, raising $I_F$ to keep the small pull-up, does cost power (each milliamp is about 1.2 mW in the LED, 5.9 mW at the nominal 4.88 mA) and speeds up LED aging.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The derating factors (0.6 for temperature, 0.5 for aging) are illustrative. Real values come from the specific device's CTR-versus-temperature curve and its aging characterization. Some vendors specify a minimum CTR at the end of the guaranteed lifetime and some do not.

## Worked example: pulse-width distortion and the maximum PWM rate

A phototransistor coupler has different delays for rising and falling edges, because turn-off needs the base charge to be removed and turn-on does not. Suppose turn-on delay is 3 µs and turn-off delay 8 µs. Every pulse is then lengthened or shortened by their difference, 5 µs. At 20 kHz (50 µs period) that is 10 percentage points of duty cycle: a commanded 50 % arrives as 40 % or 60 % depending on polarity. A closed loop will correct a static offset, but the error is not constant, since it depends on temperature, current and part. If you want the distortion to stay within 2 % of the period, the maximum PWM frequency is $0.02/5\ \mu\text{s} = 4$ kHz. That is the practical argument for digital isolators (with propagation-delay skew in the single-digit nanoseconds range, vendor-specific) for anything switching faster than a few kilohertz.

## Worked example: why CMTI kills a slow pull-up

Suppose a half-bridge node swings 400 V in 20 ns, so $dv/dt = 20$ V/ns, across a coupler whose input-to-output capacitance is 1 pF. The displacement current is $C\,dv/dt = 1\text{ pF}\times20\text{ V/ns} = 20$ mA, and the total charge pushed across the barrier is $1\text{ pF}\times400\text{ V} = 400$ pC. If that charge lands on a 20 pF output node, it would raise the node by 20 V, which obviously the supply and clamps limit, but it is enough to disturb the phototransistor and the output threshold. The pull-up drains it with the time constant $R_{pull}C_{out}$: 94 ns for 4.7 kΩ and 20 pF, but only 9.4 ns for 470 Ω. A lower pull-up shortens the glitch, but a 470 Ω pull-up needs 10 mA of collector current to hold a low, far beyond a 1-mA-class coupler, so it is not an option. That tension is why a coupler rated in kV/µs for CMTI, or an isolator without a photodetector at all, is the real fix.

## Verifying on the bench

- **Measure CTR at your bias:** apply the intended $I_F$ with a current-set resistor, tie the collector to a fixed supply through a small sense resistor and measure the collector current. Repeat at the temperature extremes in a chamber or with a heat gun and thermocouple. Compare the lowest value with the design assumption.
- **Delay and distortion:** put the input and output on a two-channel scope with a 50 % duty square wave and measure both edge delays at the actual pull-up and load. The difference between them is the pulse-width distortion.
- **CMTI in situ:** with the high-voltage side running in its real switching conditions, put the scope on the isolated output with the input held static. Any output pulses coincident with the switching node edges are CMTI failures. Use a short ground spring and an isolated or differential probe where the reference is the switching node.
- **Aging margin:** stress a few sample units at elevated temperature and LED current for a stated number of hours and re-measure CTR, to confirm that the end-of-life factor you used is not optimistic.

::: applications
- **PLC and industrial digital inputs**: isolated 24 V sensing with an optocoupler per channel.
- **AC mains zero-cross and presence detection** with an LED and series resistor.
- **Switch-mode power-supply feedback**: an optocoupler carries the error signal across the isolation barrier (CTR is part of the loop gain).
- **Relay/solid-state-relay drivers**: photo-triac or photo-MOSFET output optocouplers.
- **UART and RS-232-style slow serial links** across isolated grounds.
- **Gate-drive optocouplers** in older IGBT inverters (higher-current output stage, rated for higher CMTI).

::: assumptions
- A phototransistor-type optocoupler with typical-class parameters ($V_F\approx1.2$ V, $V_{CE(sat)}\approx0.2$ to 0.3 V, CTR 50% nominal at 5 mA); real parts and bins differ.
- Derating factors for temperature and aging are illustrative; check the datasheet curves and the application's life requirement.
- 3.3 V GPIO drive for the LED; 5 V pull-up on the isolated side.
- Speed numbers are orders of magnitude; the load resistor and capacitance dominate.
- Isolation ratings are as per the datasheet and applicable standard; they are not generic.

For high-speed or long-life requirements, digital isolators are a better fit; for analog signals, linear optocouplers (with a feedback photodiode) or isolated amplifiers are used instead of a plain phototransistor.

::: followups
### What happens to an optocoupler circuit as the product ages?
The LED output falls, so CTR falls. The output's saturation margin shrinks, the logic-low level rises toward the linear region, and the switching speeds change. If you designed for nominal CTR, the circuit may fail only after months or years. Design to end-of-life CTR with extra margin, or run the LED at a lower current to age more slowly.

### Why are the rise and fall times different?
Turn-on is driven by the photocurrent charging the base and collector capacitance (fast with strong light), but turn-off depends on removing stored charge from a saturated transistor, which takes longer. A heavily saturated output switches off more slowly, so reducing saturation depth (not over-driving $I_F$) improves turn-off.

### Optocoupler or digital isolator for an SPI link at 5 MHz?
Digital isolator: an optocoupler of the phototransistor type can't pass 5 MHz, and even a high-speed opto adds delay and skew that limits the SPI clock. For slow I²C-like or UART links an opto is fine.

### How do you improve CMTI in an optocoupler circuit?
Choose a part with a rated CMTI (an internal shield), use a lower pull-up resistance so a given injected current creates less voltage, reduce the barrier capacitance (a wider package, no copper under it), add a small filter or Schmitt trigger on the output, and keep the common-mode dv/dt down with a slower edge.

### Can an optocoupler pass an analog signal?
Not accurately: the CTR is nonlinear and varies. A linear optocoupler uses two matched photodiodes (one for feedback) to cancel the non-linearity and drift; otherwise use an isolated amplifier or a delta-sigma modulator.
