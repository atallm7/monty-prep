::: basics
### In plain words
AC resistance, or ACR, is the resistance an inductor's winding shows at its working frequency. It is higher than the DC resistance you measure with a basic meter. At AC, the current does not use the whole cross-section of the wire. It crowds toward the surface, so less of the wire carries current, and resistance goes up.

### Picture it
Picture a wide highway at rush hour where every driver crowds into the outer lanes and the middle lanes sit empty. The road is just as wide, but it carries less traffic than it could. A wire at high frequency is that highway: the middle copper carries little current, so the usable area shrinks and the wire acts like a thinner one. The analogy is loose, since the current falls off smoothly toward the center.

### Words you need
- **DC resistance (DCR)**: the resistance with steady current, using the whole wire.
- **Skin effect**: AC current crowds toward the wire surface. See [[q0056]].
- **Skin depth ($\delta$)**: how deep the current reaches. For copper it is about 66 µm at 1 MHz.
- **Proximity effect**: neighboring turns' magnetic fields push current into crowded patches.
- **Litz wire**: many thin insulated strands that give the current more surface to use.

### The simplest example
Suppose a winding has 10 mΩ of DC resistance, and at 500 kHz its ACR is three times higher, so 30 mΩ. Carry 3 A of ripple current rms. The loss is $3^2 \times 0.01 = 0.09$ W if you wrongly use DCR, but $3^2 \times 0.03 = 0.27$ W with the real ACR. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page explains skin and proximity effects in detail, shows why multi-layer windings are worse, and lists ways to cut ACR, such as litz wire and foil.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can define ACR and explain its **physical origin** and **frequency dependence**. The interviewer expects:

1. Definition: ACR is the effective series resistance of the winding at the operating frequency, $R_{ac}=P_{loss}/I_{rms}^2$, which is larger than the DC resistance (DCR).
2. Origins: **skin effect** (a conductor's own changing field pushes current to the surface) and **proximity effect** (neighbouring conductors' fields induce eddy currents), and in practice also core loss that appears as extra series resistance.
3. Scaling: skin-limited $R_{ac}\propto\sqrt f$; proximity can be much worse and grows with the number of layers.
4. What you do about it: thinner conductors/strands (Litz), foil sized near the skin depth, fewer layers, interleaving, planar windings.
:::

::: why
At switching and RF frequencies the winding's AC resistance, not its DC resistance, sets the inductor's Q and a large share of converter loss. A power inductor with 10 mΩ DCR can have 100 mΩ ACR at 1 MHz; a coil with a Q of 300 at 1 MHz might have a Q of 30 at 30 MHz. Datasheets quote DCR prominently and ACR rarely, which is why many designs run hotter than predicted. Knowing the mechanism tells you how to fix it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Skin effect** ([[q0056]]): current density decays exponentially into a conductor with the skin depth $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu)}$.
- **Proximity effect**: a conductor in the field of its neighbours carries induced eddy currents, redistributing current and adding loss.
- **Inductor loss budget** ([[q0050]]): ACR combines with the ripple current to give AC copper loss.
- **Equivalent series resistance and Q** ([[q0047]]): $Q=\omega L/R_{ac}$.
- **Eddy-current physics**: Faraday's law in a conductor.
- **Litz wire**: many insulated, transposed strands, each thinner than $\delta$.
:::

::: answer
"ACR is the effective resistance of an inductor's winding at the operating frequency. It's higher than the DC resistance because at AC the current doesn't spread evenly through the wire.

There are two reasons. The first is skin effect: the changing magnetic field inside the conductor induces eddy currents that oppose the current in the center and reinforce it near the surface, so the current crowds into a skin whose depth is delta equals root of rho over pi f mu. For copper that's 66 micrometers at 1 megahertz and 0.2 millimeters at 100 kilohertz. When the wire is thicker than a couple of skin depths, only the outer layer carries current, so the resistance goes up as the square root of frequency.

The second is the proximity effect: in a winding, each turn sits in the magnetic field of its neighbors, which induces eddy currents that further crowd the current. In multi-layer windings, this is usually worse than skin effect alone, and it rises quickly with the number of layers.

Practically, a 1 millimeter wire at 500 kilohertz has an AC resistance about three times its DC resistance from skin effect alone, and in a multi-layer coil, many times more. So, to reduce it, you use Litz wire, which has many thin insulated strands, or foil, fewer layers, and interleaving. Core loss also shows up as an effective series resistance in measurements, so measured ACR of a cored inductor includes it."
:::

::: technical
**Definition:** $R_{ac}(f)=P_{cu}(f)/I_{rms}^2$, with $R_{ac}/R_{dc}\equiv F_R(f)\ge1$.

**Skin depth:** $\delta=\sqrt{\dfrac{\rho}{\pi f\mu_0}}$. Copper at 20 °C: 9.2 mm (50 Hz), 2.06 mm (1 kHz), 0.65 mm (10 kHz), 0.21 mm (100 kHz), 92 µm (500 kHz), 65 µm (1 MHz), 20.6 µm (10 MHz).

**Isolated round wire** (radius $r$): $F_R\to1$ for $r\ll\delta$; for $r\gg\delta$, $R_{ac}\approx R_{dc}\dfrac{r}{2\delta}$ (current in an annulus of thickness δ) with a +0.25 correction in the common approximation $F_R\approx r/(2\delta)+1/4$ for $r\gtrsim\delta$. Example: 0.5 mm wire at 500 kHz: $r/\delta=2.7$, $F_R\approx1.6$; 1 mm wire at 500 kHz: $r/\delta=5.4$, $F_R\approx3.0$.

**Winding with $m$ layers of thickness $h$** (Dowell, $\Delta=h/\delta$):
$$F_R=\Delta\left[\frac{\sinh2\Delta+\sin2\Delta}{\cosh2\Delta-\cos2\Delta}+\frac{2(m^2-1)}{3}\cdot\frac{\sinh\Delta-\sin\Delta}{\cosh\Delta+\cos\Delta}\right]$$
Skin-only ($m=1$): $F_R\to\Delta$ for large $\Delta$. Proximity (the $m^2$ term) dominates for many layers.

**Litz:** strand diameter $d_s\lesssim2\delta$ at the highest frequency of interest; the number of strands sets the copper area; the strands must be properly twisted/transposed so each carries equal current.

**Measured ACR** also includes core loss reflected as a series resistance: $R_{ac,eff}=R_{cu,ac}+R_{core,eq}$ with $R_{core,eq}=P_{core}/I_{rms}^2$.
:::

::: long
"ACR, AC resistance, is the effective resistance of the inductor winding at the frequency it's operating at, defined by the loss divided by the rms current squared. It's greater than the DC resistance because at AC the current is not uniformly distributed over the conductor cross-section.

There are two mechanisms. Skin effect: a changing current creates a changing magnetic field inside the conductor, which by Faraday's law induces eddy currents that oppose the current in the middle and add to it near the surface. The current density falls off exponentially from the surface with the skin depth, delta equals the square root of resistivity over pi f mu. For copper that's about 2 millimeters at 1 kilohertz, 0.2 millimeters at 100 kilohertz, 65 micrometers at 1 megahertz. If the wire is much thicker than that, the effective area is a ring of thickness delta, so the resistance scales as the square root of frequency.

Proximity effect: each turn in a winding sits in the field produced by the other turns. That field induces eddy currents in the conductor which push the current to one side, and they get larger the more layers you have, because the field builds up through the winding. With m layers the proximity loss is roughly proportional to m squared, so a four-layer winding can have many times the loss of an isolated wire with the same skin depth.

So a 1 millimeter wire at 500 kilohertz has about 3 times its DC resistance just from skin effect, and a multilayer coil might have 10 times or more. To reduce it you want conductors thinner than about a skin depth, which means Litz wire, or foil about one skin depth thick, fewer layers, interleaved windings or planar traces. A caveat: measured ACR on a cored inductor also includes the core loss as an equivalent series resistance."
:::

::: terms
- **DCR**: winding resistance at DC.
- **ACR**: effective series resistance at frequency, $P/I_{rms}^2$.
- **Skin depth $\delta$**: depth at which current density falls to $1/e$.
- **Proximity effect**: loss from eddy currents induced by neighbouring conductors' fields.
- **Dowell's equation**: analytic AC-resistance factor for layered windings.
- **Litz wire**: bundle of thin insulated, twisted strands.
- **Interleaving**: alternating primary and secondary layers to lower field in the winding.
- **Q factor**: $\omega L/R_{ac}$.
:::

**An average answer** says "ACR is higher than DCR because of skin effect".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Defines ACR by loss, and gives $\delta$ with numbers for copper at several frequencies.
- Separates skin from proximity effect and says which usually dominates in a multilayer winding ($m^2$ growth).
- Gives $F_R$ estimates: $r/(2\delta)$ for round wire, Dowell for layers.
- Explains Litz wire design (strand diameter < ~δ, transposition) and its limits at very high frequency.
- Mentions that measured inductor ESR also contains core loss and the winding capacitance effects near the SRF.
- Links to Q and converter loss, and to practical mitigations (foil, planar, interleaving, fewer layers, gap placement).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Skin effect makes the resistance proportional to frequency."** For a thick wire the skin-limited resistance rises as $\sqrt f$; proximity can grow faster ($f^2$ for small Δ, saturating later).
- **Using a bigger-diameter wire to cut AC resistance.** Beyond ~2δ extra radius adds almost no conducting area at AC; use multiple thin strands.
- **Using Litz at far too high a frequency** with strands thicker than δ, or with poor strand insulation/transposition, giving no benefit.
- **Ignoring proximity** and using only isolated-wire skin-effect formulas for a multilayer winding.
- **Attributing all of an inductor's measured ESR to copper.** Core loss (and, near SRF, capacitive effects) contribute.
- **Comparing DCR across parts to choose a high-frequency inductor.** At 1 MHz the part with the lower DCR can have higher ACR.
:::

## The physics in one picture

A conductor carrying AC has a magnetic field both outside and inside it. Inside, the field changes with time, and Faraday's law says it induces an electric field that drives eddy currents. For a round wire, these loops oppose the main current near the axis and add to it near the surface. The net current density solves a diffusion equation, $\nabla^2\mathbf J=j\omega\mu\sigma\,\mathbf J$, whose solution decays as $e^{-x/\delta}$ with
$$\delta=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}}=\sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\pi f\mu}}.$$

## Proximity effect: why layers hurt

Consider a winding with $m$ layers of conductors. The magnetic field at layer $k$ is proportional to the ampere-turns of all inner layers, $(k-1)NI$ (for the usual one-turn-per-layer-thickness picture), so the field grows through the winding. Each layer is bathed in an external field that drives eddy currents in it, and the loss goes as $H^2$, summed over layers: $\sum(k-1)^2\sim m^3/3$ over $m$ layers, i.e. the average per-layer factor grows as $\propto m^2$. That's the $(m^2-1)$ term in Dowell's formula.

## Litz wire

Litz wire is many strands, each individually insulated, twisted and transposed so each strand occupies all positions in the bundle equally (avoiding strand-to-strand proximity loss). With strand diameter $d_s$ small relative to δ, each strand has $F_R\approx1$ (plus a small proximity term). Example: replace a 1 mm solid wire (area 0.785 mm²) with 100 strands of 0.1 mm diameter. At 500 kHz, δ = 92 µm so each strand has $r/\delta=0.54$ and is close to DC resistance, versus about 3× for the solid wire at the same area. Limits: Litz has a lower copper fill factor (more insulation) so its DC resistance is higher in a given window; it's expensive; and at several MHz even 0.05 mm strands and the proximity effects of the strand bundle lose effectiveness, so foil or planar is used.

## Core loss appears as ACR

Because a measurement just sees voltage and current, any loss, including core loss, shows up as an effective series resistance. For a cored inductor, $R_{ac,measured}=R_{cu,ac}+P_{core}/I_{rms}^2$. Near the self-resonant frequency the winding capacitance also changes the apparent loss. So datasheet Q curves and ESR curves combine all of these ([[q0047]]).

## Numbers for a design

Take a 10 µH inductor wound with 0.5 mm wire, DCR 20 mΩ, at 500 kHz (δ = 92 µm). Skin effect alone gives $F_R\approx1.6$ (ACR ≈ 32 mΩ). In a multilayer winding proximity adds more; as a planning number, 2–4× DCR is common for a two-layer round-wire winding at this frequency (check with the vendor's ACR curve; round wire with gaps between turns does better than the equivalent-foil Dowell numbers). The DC load current only sees DCR, so with 3 A DC the loss is still 0.18 W, but the 1 A p-p ripple (0.29 A rms) sees ACR, for example $0.29^2\cdot0.06=5$ mW. In this design AC copper loss is small *because the ripple is small*. In high-ripple designs (small L, DCM, resonant converters, RF coils carrying only AC) ACR is the number that sets efficiency and Q.

## What to do about it

1. **Choose the conductor to match the frequency:** wire diameter ≲ 2δ, Litz for 50 kHz–1 MHz, foil/planar traces ~δ thick above that.
2. **Reduce layers:** wider, single-layer windings (use wide core window, flat wire or foil).
3. **Interleave** primary and secondary in transformers so the MMF per layer drops.
4. **Manage the gap:** keep windings away from the fringing field of the gap (extra eddy loss).
5. **Select by Q or ACR vs frequency curves** (vendor's Q/ESR/ACR graphs), not DCR alone.
6. **Verify by measurement:** an impedance analyzer or LCR meter at the operating frequency, and thermal rise at full load.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact $F_R$ for a real winding depends on conductor shape, porosity, layer count and gap position. The formulas give estimates; use FEA or vendor ACR data for design.
:::

::: applications
- **High-frequency power inductors and transformers** (100 kHz–MHz): Litz or foil windings and interleaving.
- **Wireless power and induction heating coils**: Litz wire for Q at 100 kHz–6.78 MHz.
- **RF coils and matching inductors**: silver-plated, thick, large-diameter conductors where skin effect dominates.
- **PCB planar magnetics**: copper traces thickness chosen near δ.
- **Audio crossover inductors** (small effect, but air-core coils are chosen for low DCR).
- **High-speed interconnect loss**: the same skin effect raises trace resistance and loss at GHz.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear conductor** and **sinusoidal current**; non-sinusoidal (PWM) currents are decomposed into harmonics, each with its own $R_{ac}(f_k)$.
- **Isolated round wire or layered foil approximations** for $F_R$; real windings differ.
- **Copper at 20 °C** ($\rho=1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega$m); $\delta$ changes with temperature (+0.2 % per K in δ for copper's $\rho$ change).
- **Core loss separate** in the model; in measurement they merge.
- The interviewer might want only the skin effect; mention proximity to show depth.

::: followups
### What is the skin depth of copper at 100 kHz and what does it mean for a 1 mm wire?
$\delta=\sqrt{1.68\times10^{-8}/(\pi\cdot10^5\cdot4\pi\times10^{-7})}=0.206$ mm. The 1 mm wire has radius 0.5 mm = 2.4δ, so $F_R\approx r/(2\delta)+0.25=1.21+0.25\approx1.46$: the AC resistance is about 46 % above DC.

### Why does a multilayer winding have a much higher ACR than a single-layer one?
Because the field produced by inner layers passes through outer layers, inducing eddy currents (proximity effect) that scale with the square of the number of layers. A single-layer winding sees only the field of its own turns, so only skin and a small proximity term apply. Interleaving or fewer layers cut the field.

### How would you specify Litz wire for a 200 kHz inductor?
Skin depth at 200 kHz is 146 µm in copper; strands should be below about 2δ, so ≲0.2–0.25 mm; 0.1 mm (38 AWG) is comfortable and also good to ~1 MHz. Choose the number of strands to match the copper area for the current density (e.g. 100 strands of 0.1 mm = 0.785 mm² for ~4–5 A at 6 A/mm²), check the window fill factor, and confirm with a measurement of ACR.

### What if your inductor's Q is much lower than the datasheet at the operating frequency?
Check what frequency and conditions the datasheet Q was measured at, whether the part is near its SRF (Q collapses), whether DC bias reduces inductance and raises core loss, and whether the PCB adds resistance (thin traces/vias). Also check temperature (copper's R rises 0.39 %/K).

### How does temperature change ACR?
Copper resistivity rises about 0.39 %/K, so DCR rises at that rate. In the skin-limited regime $R_{ac}\approx\rho/(2\pi r\delta)$ and $\delta\propto\sqrt\rho$, so $R_{ac}\propto\sqrt\rho$: ACR rises only about half as fast, roughly 0.2 %/K. Hot windings therefore lose less of their ACR advantage than their DC loss suggests, but both rise with temperature, and core loss shifts too.
