::: testing
Whether you can go from a picture to an equation **from first principles** rather than reciting a formula. The interviewer is checking three things at once:

1. You can draw the circuit correctly (series resistors, output taken across the *bottom* resistor, a ground reference).
2. You can derive $V_{out} = V_{in}\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}$ from Ohm's law + KVL/series current, not just state it.
3. You know the **assumption hiding inside the formula**: no current is drawn from the output node. Strong candidates bring up loading before being asked.
:::

::: why
Voltage dividers are everywhere in real hardware: feedback networks that set a regulator's output voltage, scaling a battery voltage into an ADC's range, biasing a transistor, setting comparator thresholds, and pull-up/pull-down networks. It's also the gateway to Thevenin equivalents and to the question "why can't I power something from a divider?" ([[q0148]]). Interviewers use it as a fast signal: candidates who derive it cleanly and mention loading usually have real lab experience.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ohm's law** ($V = IR$): each resistor's voltage is proportional to the current through it. See [[q0001]].
- **Series connection**: with nothing else attached to the middle node, the *same* current flows through $R_1$ and $R_2$ (KCL at the midpoint).
- **KVL**: the source voltage equals the sum of the drops around the loop: $V_{in} = V_{R1} + V_{R2}$. See [[q0004]].
- **Loading / Thevenin equivalent**: any load on the output is in parallel with $R_2$, which changes the ratio.
:::

::: answer
"Sure. I'd draw a source $V_{in}$ feeding two resistors in series, $R_1$ on top and $R_2$ on the bottom going to ground, and I take $V_{out}$ at the node between them, so it's the voltage across $R_2$.

The equation is $V_{out} = V_{in}\cdot\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}$.

To derive it: if nothing is connected to the output, the same current flows through both resistors. The total resistance is $R_1 + R_2$, so by Ohm's law $I = V_{in}/(R_1+R_2)$. The output is just the drop across $R_2$, which is $I R_2$. Substituting gives $V_{in}\,R_2/(R_1+R_2)$.

The intuition is that the voltage splits in proportion to resistance: whichever resistor is bigger takes the bigger share of the drop. One thing I'd flag: this assumes the output is unloaded. If you hang a load $R_L$ on it, $R_L$ is in parallel with $R_2$, the effective bottom resistance drops, and $V_{out}$ sags. So dividers are great for references and sensing, like feeding an ADC or a regulator's feedback pin, but not for powering anything."
:::

::: technical
**Circuit.** $V_{in}$ drives series resistors $R_1$ (top) and $R_2$ (bottom, to ground). $V_{out}$ is the midpoint node voltage referenced to ground.

**Derivation (unloaded).**
1. KCL at the midpoint with no load: $I_{R1} = I_{R2} = I$.
2. KVL around the loop: $V_{in} = IR_1 + IR_2 = I(R_1+R_2) \Rightarrow I = \frac{V_{in}}{R_1+R_2}$.
3. Output: $V_{out} = IR_2 = V_{in}\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}$.

The ratio $\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}$ is always between 0 and 1. A resistive divider can only attenuate.

**Loaded divider.** With load $R_L$: $V_{out} = V_{in}\frac{R_2\parallel R_L}{R_1 + R_2\parallel R_L}$. Equivalently, the divider looks like a Thevenin source with $V_{th} = V_{in}\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}$ and $R_{th} = R_1\parallel R_2$. The loading error is $\frac{R_{th}}{R_{th}+R_L}$, so $R_L = 10R_{th}$ gives ≈9.1% error and $R_L = 100R_{th}$ gives ≈1%.

**Design trade-off.** Small resistors give a stiff (low-$R_{th}$) output but burn quiescent power $V_{in}^2/(R_1+R_2)$. Large resistors save power but become sensitive to loading, input bias/leakage currents and noise pickup, and form slow RC time constants with input capacitance. Typical feedback dividers on regulators sit in the 10 kΩ–1 MΩ total range. The datasheet usually recommends a range.

**Accuracy.** With resistor tolerances, the worst-case ratio error is roughly the sum of both tolerances scaled by $(1-\text{ratio})$: $\frac{\Delta V_{out}}{V_{out}} \approx (1 - \frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2})(\varepsilon_{R2} - \varepsilon_{R1})$. Matched or ratio-trimmed networks are used when accuracy matters.
:::

::: short
"A voltage divider is two resistors in series across $V_{in}$, with the output taken across the bottom one: $V_{out} = V_{in}\,R_2/(R_1+R_2)$. It comes straight from series current: $I = V_{in}/(R_1+R_2)$, and $V_{out} = IR_2$. The key caveat is that it assumes no load current. A load appears in parallel with $R_2$ and pulls the output down, so dividers are for setting references and scaling signals, not for powering things."
:::

::: long
"Let me draw it first: a source $V_{in}$, then $R_1$ from the source down to a middle node, then $R_2$ from that node to ground. $V_{out}$ is the voltage at the middle node, so it's the voltage across $R_2$.

For the derivation I'll assume nothing is connected to the output. Then KCL at the middle node says the current through $R_1$ equals the current through $R_2$; call it $I$. KVL around the loop says $V_{in} = IR_1 + IR_2$, so $I = V_{in}/(R_1 + R_2)$. The output voltage is the drop across $R_2$, $V_{out} = IR_2 = V_{in}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$.

Physically, the same current flows through both resistors, so each one drops voltage in proportion to its resistance. If $R_1 = R_2$, you get exactly half. If $R_2$ is nine times $R_1$, you get 90%.

Now the part that matters in practice: loading. If I connect a load $R_L$, it's in parallel with $R_2$, so the bottom of the divider becomes $R_2 \parallel R_L$ and the output drops. A clean way to think about it is the Thevenin equivalent: the divider looks like an ideal source of $V_{in}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ behind a resistance $R_1 \parallel R_2$. As long as the load is much bigger than that Thevenin resistance, say 10 to 100 times bigger, the error stays small.

That leads to the design trade-off. Low resistor values make the output stiffer but waste current all the time. High values save power but make the node sensitive to leakage, bias currents and noise. So in practice I'd use dividers for things like a regulator feedback network, scaling a battery voltage into an ADC, or biasing. For powering a load I'd use a regulator, because a divider's output isn't regulated and it wastes power in $R_1$."
:::

::: terms
- **Voltage division**: the voltage across one of several series resistors is the total voltage times that resistor's fraction of the total resistance.
- **Unloaded (open-circuit) output**: the output with no current drawn. The textbook formula only holds here.
- **Loading**: a load in parallel with $R_2$ reduces the effective bottom resistance and pulls $V_{out}$ down.
- **Thevenin equivalent**: $V_{th} = V_{in}\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}$, $R_{th} = R_1\parallel R_2$. This is the cleanest way to reason about loading.
- **Source impedance / output impedance**: for a divider this is $R_1\parallel R_2$. Lower means "stiffer".
- **Quiescent (bias) current**: $V_{in}/(R_1+R_2)$, which flows whether or not anything useful is happening.
- **Attenuation ratio**: $\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}$ is always ≤ 1. A passive divider cannot amplify.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** writes the formula, maybe draws it, and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws it cleanly *with a ground symbol* and labels which node is $V_{out}$.
- Derives it in three lines from KCL/KVL and Ohm's law, saying *why* the currents are equal (no current leaves the middle node).
- Gives the one-sentence intuition: drop is proportional to resistance.
- **Volunteers the loading assumption** and quantifies it with the Thevenin resistance ($R_1\parallel R_2$) and a rule of thumb ($R_L \gg R_{th}$).
- Mentions one real use (regulator feedback divider, ADC scaling) and the power-vs-stiffness trade-off when choosing resistor values.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting $R_1$ in the numerator.** The output is across the resistor you measure across. If you take $V_{out}$ across $R_2$ (the bottom resistor), $R_2$ goes in the numerator. Re-derive it rather than memorize.
- **Forgetting the unloaded assumption** and then claiming a divider can power a 3.3 V microcontroller from 5 V. The MCU's current draw changes, so its effective resistance changes, and so does $V_{out}$. See [[q0148]].
- **Saying "the current splits"** between $R_1$ and $R_2$. They're in *series*, so the same current flows through both. The *voltage* splits.
- **Ignoring power.** 10 Ω + 10 Ω across 12 V burns 7.2 W. Always sanity-check $V^2/R$.
- **Choosing huge resistors blindly.** 10 MΩ dividers feeding an ADC with a sample-and-hold capacitor or input leakage give wrong readings.
:::

::: deep
## First principles

Start from the two laws every circuit obeys:

- **KCL** (conservation of charge): current into a node equals current out.
- **KVL** (conservation of energy): the sum of voltage rises and drops around any closed loop is zero.

Figure: The output is the voltage across **R₂**, measured from the middle node to ground. With nothing attached at V_out, the same current I flows through both resistors.

**Step 1: equal currents.** The middle node has only two connections, $R_1$ and $R_2$. By KCL, whatever flows in through $R_1$ must flow out through $R_2$:
$$I_{R1} = I_{R2} \equiv I$$

**Step 2: KVL around the loop.**
$$V_{in} - IR_1 - IR_2 = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; I = \frac{V_{in}}{R_1 + R_2}$$

**Step 3: output voltage.** $V_{out}$ is the drop across $R_2$:
$$\boxed{V_{out} = I R_2 = V_{in}\,\frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}}$$

Equivalently, $\frac{V_{R1}}{V_{R2}} = \frac{R_1}{R_2}$: series resistors share the voltage in proportion to their resistance. That one sentence is the physical intuition. The current is fixed by the total resistance, and each resistor "uses up" voltage according to how much it opposes that same current.

> [!TIP]
> Sanity-check every divider with the extremes: if $R_2 \to \infty$ (open), $V_{out} \to V_{in}$; if $R_2 \to 0$ (short), $V_{out} \to 0$; if $R_1 = R_2$, $V_{out} = V_{in}/2$. If your formula fails any of these, you've swapped the resistors.

## What changes when you add a load

Connect a load $R_L$ from $V_{out}$ to ground. Now three things meet at the middle node, and $R_L$ is in **parallel** with $R_2$:

$$V_{out} = V_{in}\,\frac{R_2\parallel R_L}{R_1 + R_2\parallel R_L},\qquad R_2\parallel R_L = \frac{R_2R_L}{R_2+R_L}$$

Because $R_2\parallel R_L < R_2$ always, the loaded output is always **lower** than the unloaded value.

The cleanest way to reason about this is the **Thevenin equivalent** of the divider as seen from the output: kill $V_{in}$ (short it) and $R_1$ and $R_2$ are in parallel, so

$$V_{th} = V_{in}\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2},\qquad R_{th} = R_1\parallel R_2$$

$$\text{error} = \frac{R_{th}}{R_{th}+R_L}$$

## Choosing resistor values

Picking the ratio is easy. Picking the *magnitude* is the engineering:

- **Power / quiescent current:** $I_q = V_{in}/(R_1+R_2)$ flows all the time. A 10 kΩ total across 12 V is 1.2 mA (14 mW). That matters for a battery product, where you might use 1 MΩ total (12 µA) instead.
- **Stiffness:** $R_{th} = R_1\parallel R_2$ must be small compared with whatever loads the node: an ADC input, a comparator's bias current, an op-amp input, PCB leakage.
- **Dynamic loading:** ADCs with a sample-and-hold capacitor pull a charge spike at each sample. A high-impedance divider can't recharge the cap in time, so you add a small capacitor at the node or a buffer op-amp.
- **Noise:** resistors generate thermal noise $\sqrt{4kTR\,\Delta f}$, and high-impedance nodes pick up coupled noise more easily.
- **Tolerance:** with 1% resistors the ratio can be off by up to about 2% in the worst case. Use 0.1% or matched networks for precision references.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked "what values would you use?", don't just give numbers. Say what drives the choice: "Depends on how much current I can afford and what's loading the output. For an ADC input I'd keep $R_{th}$ low enough for the ADC's sampling time, or add a buffer. For a battery monitor I'd go high-value to save power and add a filter cap."

## Where this shows up

- **Regulator feedback:** an LDO or buck compares $V_{FB}$ (e.g., 0.8 V) to an internal reference. $V_{out} = V_{ref}(1 + R_1/R_2)$ is the same divider equation solved for $V_{in}$. See [[q0223]].
- **Battery/ADC scaling:** a 4.2 V Li-ion cell into a 3.3 V ADC.
- **Bias networks** for BJT amplifiers, and comparator thresholds.
- **Potentiometers** are adjustable dividers: the wiper position sets the ratio.
:::

::: applications
- **Setting a regulator's output voltage**: a buck converter with $V_{FB}=0.6$ V producing 3.3 V uses a divider with $R_1/R_2 = 3.3/0.6 - 1 = 4.5$, e.g., 45.3 kΩ / 10 kΩ.
- **Measuring battery voltage**: two 100 kΩ resistors halve a 4.2 V cell to 2.1 V for a 3.3 V ADC. The draw is only 21 µA, and a 100 nF capacitor at the ADC pin helps the sample-and-hold.
- **Level-translation of slow signals**: a 5 V → 3.3 V divider on a UART RX line works for slow signals but rounds edges because of the RC formed with pin capacitance.
- **Sensor bridges**: thermistors and strain gauges in a divider or Wheatstone bridge turn resistance changes into voltage changes.
- **Biasing**: setting the base voltage of a BJT common-emitter stage.
:::

::: assumptions
- **No load current** on the output. This is the core assumption. With a load, use $R_2\parallel R_L$ or the Thevenin form.
- **Ideal source**: $V_{in}$ has zero internal resistance. A real source's resistance adds in series with $R_1$.
- **DC / resistive**. At high frequency, parasitic capacitance across the resistors and the input capacitance of whatever reads $V_{out}$ turn it into an RC network. Scope probe attenuators use a *compensated* divider (matching $R_1C_1 = R_2C_2$) for exactly this reason.
- **Linear resistors**: no self-heating or voltage coefficient. That's true for normal operating ranges.

If the interviewer says "the output is fed to an op-amp buffer", the unloaded formula is essentially exact, because the op-amp input draws only nA–pA.
:::

::: followups
### What happens to Vout if you connect a 10 kΩ load to a 10 kΩ / 10 kΩ divider from 10 V?
$R_2\parallel R_L = 5$ kΩ, so $V_{out} = 10\cdot\frac{5}{10+5} = 3.33$ V instead of 5 V. A 33% drop. Equivalently: $V_{th} = 5$ V and $R_{th} = 5$ kΩ, so $V_{out} = 5\cdot\frac{10}{15} = 3.33$ V.

### Why not use a divider to make 3.3 V for a microcontroller from 5 V?
The MCU's current draw varies (sleep vs. active vs. radio bursts), so its effective resistance varies and the divider output moves with it. To keep the output stiff you'd need tiny resistors, which waste far more power than the load uses. And the output isn't regulated against $V_{in}$ changes either. Use an LDO or buck. See [[q0148]].

### How would you pick resistor values for a divider feeding an ADC?
Set the ratio to map the max input to just under the ADC full scale. Then pick the magnitude: low enough that $R_{th}$ can recharge the ADC's sampling capacitor within the acquisition time (check the datasheet's max source impedance; for many MCU SAR ADCs it's on the order of 10 kΩ or lower at full speed), and high enough that the quiescent current is acceptable. If those conflict, add a capacitor at the ADC pin (it supplies the sampling charge) or an op-amp buffer.

### Draw a divider that outputs 1/3 of the input. Now make it adjustable.
$R_1 = 2R$, $R_2 = R$ gives $V_{in}/3$. For adjustable, use a potentiometer as the divider (wiper is $V_{out}$), or put a pot in series with fixed resistors to limit the range and improve resolution.

### What's the output impedance of a voltage divider and why does it matter?
$R_1\parallel R_2$, found by shorting $V_{in}$ and looking back into the output. It sets how much the output sags per unit load current, and with any capacitance at the node it sets the RC time constant (bandwidth) of the divider.

### Your divider reads correctly on the bench with a DMM but wrong in the system. Why?
The DMM has ~10 MΩ input impedance and barely loads it. In the system, something is loading the node: an ADC input, a pull-up/pull-down inside an MCU pin, leakage from flux residue on a high-impedance node, or a dynamic load from the ADC's sampling. Check $R_{th}$ against the real load.
:::

::: interpretations
"Draw a voltage divider" almost always means the **resistive** divider. Some interviewers will extend it to a **capacitive divider** ($V_{out} = V_{in}\frac{C_1}{C_1+C_2}$ for $V_{out}$ across $C_2$, which is inverted relative to resistors because impedance ∝ 1/C) or a general **impedance divider** $V_{out} = V_{in}\frac{Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}$, which is the basis of RC/RL filters ([[q0010]]). If they ask for the "derivation", they want the KCL/KVL steps, not the formula.
:::
