::: testing
Whether you can turn a **conversion ratio into a duty cycle** and know when that is trivial and when it isn't. The interviewer wants to see:

1. The one-liner: for a buck in CCM, $D=M=V_{out}/V_{in}$.
2. The reasoning behind it (volt-second balance), so you can re-derive the relation for other cases.
3. Awareness that "just the ratio" is only enough in CCM. In DCM the answer also depends on the load, inductance and frequency, through $D=M\sqrt{K/(1-M)}$.
4. Sanity checks: converting to an on-time, and checking it against minimum on-time and maximum duty limits.
:::

::: why
In practice you are usually handed a ratio ("12 V down to 1.2 V", "about a tenth") and asked what the controller needs to do. The duty cycle then drives the on-time, the ripple, the controller choice and whether the converter is feasible at the chosen frequency. The question also tests whether you can generalize: a candidate who knows $D=M$ for a buck can often be pushed to give the boost relation $D=1-1/M$ and the DCM relation, which shows you derive rather than memorize. This is the "given a ratio" companion to [[q0180]] (how to calculate $D$ in general) and [[q0183]] (the reverse direction).
:::

::: concepts
- **Conversion ratio** $M=V_{out}/V_{in}$: the DC gain of the power stage ([[q0178]]).
- **Volt-second balance**: $(V_{in}-V_{out})D=V_{out}(1-D)$, which gives $M=D$ for a buck in CCM ([[q0174]]).
- **On-time from ratio**: $t_{on}=M/f_{sw}$ ([[q0179]]).
- **CCM vs DCM**: determined by $K=2L/(RT)$ against $1-M$ ([[q0229]]).
- **Other topologies**: boost $M=1/(1-D)$, inverting buck-boost $|M|=D/(1-D)$.
- **Duty limits**: $D_{min}=t_{on,min}f_{sw}$ and $D_{max}=1-t_{off,min}f_{sw}$ ([[q0181]]).
:::

::: answer
"For a buck in continuous conduction it's simply the ratio: $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$. If I'm told the ratio is 0.275, the duty cycle is 27.5 percent. The reason is volt-second balance on the inductor: the average of the switch node, $D\,V_{in}$, must equal the output.

Then I'd turn it into something physical. At 500 kHz the period is 2 µs, so 0.275 means a 550 ns on-time. And I'd sanity-check it: is that on-time above the controller's minimum, and is the off-time long enough? A ratio of 0.08, like 12 V to 1 V, gives under 170 ns at 500 kHz, which might be too short for some controllers.

I'd also say what I'm assuming. If the converter has meaningful drops, like a diode or a lot of series resistance, the duty cycle is a bit higher than the ratio. And if the load is light enough that the converter runs in discontinuous mode, the ratio alone doesn't determine $D$. There the relation is $D=M\sqrt{K/(1-M)}$ with $K=2L/(RT)$. For example, 3.3 V from 12 V at 100 mA with 4.7 µH and 500 kHz needs only about 12 percent duty, not 27.5."
:::

::: technical
**CCM, ideal buck.**
$$M=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}=D\quad\Rightarrow\quad D=M,\qquad t_{on}=\frac{M}{f_{sw}},\qquad t_{off}=\frac{1-M}{f_{sw}}$$

**With drops** (sync, using $V_{in}$ and $I$): $D=\dfrac{M+I(R_{ls}+R_{DCR})/V_{in}}{1-I(R_{hs}-R_{ls})/V_{in}}$ ([[q0180]]).

**DCM.** Define $K=\dfrac{2L}{R\,T}$ ($R=V_{out}/I_{out}$). The converter is in CCM when $K>1-M$ and in DCM when $K<1-M$. In DCM the inductor current peaks at $I_{pk}=(V_{in}-V_{out})DT/L$, falls to zero in $D_2T$ with $D_2=D(1-M)/M$, and the average output current is $\tfrac12I_{pk}(D+D_2)=V_{out}/R$. Solving:
$$D=M\sqrt{\frac{K}{1-M}}\quad(K<1-M),\qquad \text{equivalently}\quad M=\frac{2}{1+\sqrt{1+4K/D^2}}$$

**Other topologies (CCM).**

| Topology | Ratio $M$ | Duty cycle from $M$ |
|---|---|---|
| Buck | $D$ | $D=M$ |
| Boost | $1/(1-D)$ | $D=1-1/M$ |
| Inverting buck-boost | $\lvert M\rvert=D/(1-D)$ | $D=\lvert M\rvert/(1+\lvert M\rvert)$ |

**Worked table** (500 kHz, $T=2$ µs):

| $V_{in}\to V_{out}$ | $M=D$ | $t_{on}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 12 V to 1.2 V | 0.100 | 200 ns |
| 12 V to 3.3 V | 0.275 | 550 ns |
| 24 V to 5 V | 0.208 | 417 ns |
| 5 V to 1.8 V | 0.360 | 720 ns |
| 3.7 V to 1.8 V | 0.486 | 973 ns |
| 5 V to 3.3 V | 0.660 | 1320 ns |
:::

"For a buck in continuous conduction, duty cycle equals the ratio: $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$, from volt-second balance. A ratio of 0.275 is 27.5 percent, which at 500 kHz is a 550 ns on-time. I'd check that against the minimum on-time. It's a bit higher with real drops, and in discontinuous mode at light load the ratio alone isn't enough: $D=M\sqrt{K/(1-M)}$ with $K=2L/(RT)$."
:::

::: long
"If I'm given the ratio $V_{out}/V_{in}$ for a buck, the answer is straightforward in continuous conduction: the duty cycle equals the ratio. I can justify that quickly. In steady state the inductor has zero average voltage. While the switch is on it sees $V_{in}-V_{out}$ for a fraction $D$ of the period, and while it's off it sees $-V_{out}$ for the rest. Setting those volt-seconds equal gives $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$, so $D=M$.

I'd then put numbers on it. If the ratio is 0.275, $D$ is 27.5 percent. At 500 kHz that's 550 ns on out of 2 µs. For 12 V to 1.2 V, the ratio is 0.1, which is 200 ns. Then I check feasibility: the on-time has to be above the controller's minimum, and the off-time long enough for the bootstrap refresh and current sensing. At 2 MHz, 12 V to 1 V gives about 42 ns, which many parts can't produce.

I'd mention what I've assumed. First, losses. If the drops are comparable to the output, say a diode drop at 1.8 V, the duty cycle must be a bit higher than the ratio. Second, conduction mode. If the load is light enough that the inductor current reaches zero, the duty cycle needed for a given ratio is smaller, and it depends on load, inductance and frequency: $D=M\sqrt{K/(1-M)}$ where $K=2L/(RT)$. For 3.3 V from 12 V at 100 mA, 4.7 µH and 500 kHz, $K$ is 0.142 against $1-M=0.725$, so it's in DCM and needs only 12.2 percent duty.

And if the interviewer changes the topology, I'd re-derive with the same volt-second balance. For a boost the ratio is $1/(1-D)$, so $D=1-1/M$; for an inverting buck-boost it's $M=D/(1-D)$, so $D=M/(1+M)$."
:::

::: terms
- **Conversion ratio ($M$)**: $V_{out}/V_{in}$; the converter's DC gain.
- **Duty ratio ($D$)**: $t_{on}/T$.
- **CCM / DCM**: inductor current is positive throughout the period / drops to zero for part of it.
- **Normalized inductor time constant ($K$)**: $2L/(RT)$; compares the inductor's current-slewing ability with the load.
- **Critical conduction**: the boundary $K=1-M$.
- **$D_2$**: the fraction of the period during which the inductor current falls to zero in DCM.
- **Volt-second balance**: zero average inductor voltage in steady state.
- **Minimum on-time**: the controller's shortest pulse, which limits low $D$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "$D=V_{out}/V_{in}$."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives $D=M$ with a one-line derivation (volt-second balance) and a number with units of time: 550 ns at 500 kHz.
- Checks feasibility against minimum on-time and maximum duty, for the whole input range.
- States the assumption (CCM, ideal) up front and says how the answer changes with losses and in DCM, quoting $D=M\sqrt{K/(1-M)}$.
- Shows a DCM numerical example (12.2% rather than 27.5%) to prove the point.
- Can switch topology: $D=1-1/M$ for boost, $D=M/(1+M)$ for inverting buck-boost.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using $D=V_{in}/V_{out}$** (inverted). A buck has $M\le1$, so $D\le1$.
- **Forgetting that the ratio gives only the ideal CCM duty cycle.** Losses push $D$ up; DCM brings it down.
- **Quoting a duty cycle without converting to time.** The interviewer wants to know if it's 20 ns or 2 µs, since that decides feasibility.
- **Using the buck formula for a boost.** Boost: $M=1/(1-D)$, so $D=1-1/M$; $D=M$ would be wrong.
- **Ignoring the CCM/DCM check** when the load current is small or $L$ is small ([[q0229]]).
- **Not checking the corner cases**: highest $V_{in}$ (smallest $D$) vs minimum on-time, lowest $V_{in}$ (largest $D$) vs maximum duty ([[q0181]]).
:::

::: deep
## The CCM answer

The buck's DC transfer function is $M=D$ (derivation in [[q0174]]). Reading it backwards gives $D$ from $M$ with no further work:

$$D=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}$$

Because it's a ratio, only $M$ matters, not the absolute voltages. A 24 V to 4.8 V converter and a 5 V to 1 V converter both have $D=0.2$. What differs is everything else (switch voltage rating, losses, controller choice).

### Turn it into time

The duty cycle alone doesn't tell you whether the design is buildable; the on-time does:

$$t_{on}=\frac{M}{f_{sw}},\qquad t_{off}=\frac{1-M}{f_{sw}}$$

| $V_{in}\to V_{out}$ | $M=D$ | $t_{on}$ at 500 kHz | $t_{on}$ at 2 MHz |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 V to 1.2 V | 0.100 | 200 ns | 50 ns |
| 12 V to 1 V | 0.083 | 167 ns | 42 ns |
| 24 V to 5 V | 0.208 | 417 ns | 104 ns |
| 12 V to 3.3 V | 0.275 | 550 ns | 138 ns |
| 5 V to 1.8 V | 0.360 | 720 ns | 180 ns |
| 3.7 V to 1.8 V | 0.486 | 973 ns | 243 ns |
| 5 V to 3.3 V | 0.660 | 1320 ns | 330 ns |

Look at the 12 V to 1 V row at 2 MHz: 42 ns. Many controllers have a minimum on-time of a few tens to over a hundred ns (vendor-dependent), so this combination may force pulse skipping ([[q0181]]).

## When the ratio isn't enough: DCM

In CCM the inductor current never reaches zero. At light load, or with a small inductor, it does: the current ramps up during $D$, falls to zero during $D_2$, and sits at zero for the remaining $1-D-D_2$. The volt-second balance changes: the inductor sees $V_{in}-V_{out}$ for $D$, $-V_{out}$ for $D_2$, and zero for the rest:

$$(V_{in}-V_{out})D=V_{out}D_2\ \Rightarrow\ D_2=D\frac{1-M}{M}$$

The output current equals the average inductor current, which is the area of the triangle over the period:

$$I_{out}=\frac{1}{2}I_{pk}(D+D_2)=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})DT}{2L}\cdot\frac{D}{M}$$

Using $I_{out}=V_{out}/R=M\,V_{in}/R$ and $V_{in}-V_{out}=V_{in}(1-M)$:

$$\frac{V_{in}(1-M)D^2T}{2LM}=\frac{M\,V_{in}}{R}\ \Rightarrow\ D^2=\frac{2L}{RT}\cdot\frac{M^2}{1-M}$$

With $K=2L/(RT)$:

$$\boxed{D=M\sqrt{\frac{K}{1-M}}}\qquad\text{valid when } K<1-M\ \ (\text{DCM})$$

At the boundary $K=1-M$, this equals $D=M$, so the two regimes join continuously. The DCM condition is also $D+D_2<1$, which with $D_2=D(1-M)/M$ reads $D<M$: in DCM the duty cycle needed is *always smaller* than the ratio.

> [!TIP]
> To decide which formula to use, compute $K=2L/(RT)$ with $R=V_{out}/I_{out}$ and compare with $1-M$. If $K>1-M$, use $D=M$. If $K<1-M$, use the DCM formula.

### Worked example

$V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V ($M=0.275$, $1-M=0.725$), $L=4.7$ µH, $f_{sw}=500$ kHz ($T=2$ µs):

| $I_{out}$ | $R=V_{out}/I_{out}$ | $K=2L/(RT)$ | Mode | Duty cycle needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 A | 1.65 Ω | 2.85 | CCM | 0.275 |
| 1 A | 3.3 Ω | 1.42 | CCM | 0.275 |
| 0.5 A | 6.6 Ω | 0.712 | DCM (barely) | 0.2725 |
| 0.3 A | 11 Ω | 0.427 | DCM | 0.211 |
| 0.1 A | 33 Ω | 0.142 | DCM | 0.122 |
| 0.05 A | 66 Ω | 0.071 | DCM | 0.086 |

At 100 mA the required duty is $0.275\sqrt{0.1424/0.725}=0.275\times0.443=0.1219$, an on-time of 244 ns. I checked this against a cycle-by-cycle numerical simulation of the same circuit (ideal switches, diode-emulating freewheel): at $D=0.1219$ the output settles at 3.30 V; at the CCM duty of 0.275 the same load would give 6.12 V.

The CCM/DCM boundary current here is $I_{crit}=V_{in}M(1-M)/(2Lf_{sw})=0.509$ A, matching the table.

## Other topologies, same method

If the question is asked of a different converter, use the same volt-second balance on the inductor. In CCM:

| Topology | Inductor sees ($D$) | Inductor sees ($1-D$) | $M$ | $D$ from $M$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buck | $V_{in}-V_{out}$ | $-V_{out}$ | $D$ | $M$ |
| Boost | $V_{in}$ | $V_{in}-V_{out}$ | $\dfrac{1}{1-D}$ | $1-\dfrac1M$ |
| Inverting buck-boost | $V_{in}$ | $V_{out}$ (negative) | $\dfrac{-D}{1-D}$ | $\dfrac{\lvert M\rvert}{1+\lvert M\rvert}$ |

For example, boosting 3.7 V to 5 V needs $D=1-3.7/5=0.26$. A buck with that same 0.26 would give $0.26\times3.7=0.96$ V. See [[q0272]] for the boost.

## With losses: ratio plus an offset

If the drops matter, the ratio alone doesn't give $D$; you also need the operating point. For a synchronous buck:

$$D=\frac{M+\dfrac{I(R_{ls}+R_{DCR})}{V_{in}}}{1-\dfrac{I(R_{hs}-R_{ls})}{V_{in}}}$$

For 12 V to 3.3 V at 2 A with 20/10/15 mΩ, this is $(0.275+0.00417)/(1-0.00167)=0.2796$ ([[q0180]]). The correction is small at 3.3 V but grows at low $V_{out}$: at 1 V out and 20 A with the same resistances (illustrative), the drops would be $20\times0.025=0.5$ V, half the output, so the duty cycle would be about 50% higher than the ratio. That is why high-current low-voltage rails use very low-resistance FETs and inductors.

## Feasibility check: the whole input range

For a design specified over a range of $V_{in}$, compute $D$ at both ends:

- **Highest $V_{in}$** gives the smallest $D$: compare $t_{on}=D/f_{sw}$ with the controller's minimum on-time.
- **Lowest $V_{in}$** gives the largest $D$: compare $t_{off}=(1-D)/f_{sw}$ with the minimum off-time and check dropout.

Example: 3.3 V out from 9 to 15 V in. $D$ ranges from 0.367 down to 0.22. At 1 MHz: $t_{on}$ from 367 ns down to 220 ns, comfortable for typical controllers. For 1 V out from 24 V at 1 MHz: $D=0.042$, $t_{on}=42$ ns, probably not feasible without lowering the frequency.

::: applications
- **First-pass design**: from the spec ($V_{in}$ range, $V_{out}$) compute $D$ range, then check $t_{on}$, $t_{off}$ and ripple ([[q0212]]).
- **Choosing the switching frequency**: the highest frequency is often set by $D_{min}t_{on,min}$ at maximum $V_{in}$.
- **Selecting a controller**: matching its minimum on-time and maximum duty to the ratio range of the application.
- **Reading a scope capture**: comparing the measured duty with the expected $M$ to spot dropout, DCM or loss problems.
- **Boost and buck-boost design**: the same procedure with their own ratio equations.
- **Test and validation**: choosing the load points for characterisation, including the CCM/DCM boundary ([[q0267]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal, lossless components** for $D=M$. Real drops raise $D$.
- **CCM** for $D=M$. In DCM, $D=M\sqrt{K/(1-M)}$ for a resistive load.
- **Resistive load** $R=V_{out}/I_{out}$ in the DCM relation; a constant-current load gives the same formula using $I_{out}$.
- **Fixed-frequency, voltage-mode style PWM.** In hysteretic, constant-on-time or PFM converters the duty cycle isn't fixed, but the average still follows the same ratio.
- **No dead time or edge effects**, which perturb the effective duty by about 1% in typical designs.
- **Steady state.** During transients $D$ moves away from these values.
:::

::: followups
### What duty cycle gives 1.8 V from a 3.7 V cell?
$D=1.8/3.7=0.486$. At 1 MHz that is an on-time of 486 ns and an off-time of 514 ns. As the cell falls to 3.0 V, $D$ rises to 0.6. If the cell drops further toward 2.5 V the ratio is 0.72, still fine; the problem would be for a 3.3 V rail, where a plain buck runs out of headroom near 3.5 V.

### If the ratio is 0.1 and the controller's minimum on-time is 100 ns, what's the maximum switching frequency?
$f_{sw,max}=D/t_{on,min}=0.1/100\text{ ns}=1$ MHz. Above that, the required on-time is below the minimum and the converter skips pulses. Use a lower frequency or a controller with a shorter minimum on-time.

### What duty cycle does a boost need for a ratio of 2.5 (e.g., 3.7 V to 9.25 V)?
$D=1-1/M=1-1/2.5=0.6$. In a boost the inductor charges for $DT$ from $V_{in}$ and releases for $(1-D)T$ onto the output, giving $M=1/(1-D)$ ([[q0272]]).

### A 12 V to 3.3 V buck draws only 50 mA. What duty cycle should the controller use in steady state (4.7 µH, 500 kHz, ideal)?
It's in DCM: $R=66$ Ω, $K=0.0712$, $1-M=0.725$, so $D=0.275\sqrt{0.0712/0.725}=0.0862$. That's an 86 ns... no, $0.0862\times2$ µs $=172$ ns on-time. So at light load the controller uses less than a third of the CCM duty. In practice it will usually switch to pulse skipping or PFM ([[q0231]]).

### Scenario: a 5 V rail from a 5.4 V input at 1 MHz needs ratio 0.926. Is it a problem?
$t_{off}=(1-0.926)\times1\text{ µs}=74$ ns, probably shorter than the minimum off-time of many controllers (and drops make it worse: the required duty is higher than 0.926). The converter will run in dropout or skip pulses. Lower the frequency (at 300 kHz, $t_{off}=247$ ns), or use a part specified for 100% duty, or raise $V_{in}$ ([[q0181]]).
:::
