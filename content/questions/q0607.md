::: testing
Whether you can name the physical stations you would walk past on an electronics line, say what each one does in a sentence and give a sensible order. This is the "show me you've seen a factory" question: SPI, AOI, X-ray, ICT or flying probe, programming, functional test, RF or calibration, hipot, burn-in, final QA, plus the repair/rework bench. A strong answer gives the *purpose* of each station, not just its name, and notes the bottleneck concept.
:::

::: why
Interns often tour or work with a contract manufacturer. Recognising stations and what they measure lets you ask good questions ("what is the false-fail rate at ICT?", "is programming done in the fixture?") and helps you design a board that fits them: fiducials for AOI, test points for ICT, programming pads for the flash station. [[q0606]] gives the ordered sequence; here the focus is on the stations themselves.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inspection stations**: SPI, AOI, X-ray ([[q0608]]).
- **Electrical test stations**: ICT ([[q0609]]), flying probe ([[q0611]]), boundary scan.
- **Programming and calibration station**: firmware flash, serial numbers, trims.
- **Functional test station**: powers and exercises the board ([[q0610]]).
- **Reliability / safety stations**: burn-in chamber, hipot tester.
- **Repair station**: diagnoses and reworks failures.
- **ESD protection and handling**: grounded benches, wrist straps, ionisers.
:::

::: answer
"On a typical line I'd see a handful of stations in this order. Right after the printer there's a solder paste inspection machine, a 3D scanner checking paste volume. After the reflow oven there's automated optical inspection, a camera gantry looking for missing, skewed or bridged parts. For boards with BGAs there may be an X-ray machine. Then the electrical stations: an in-circuit test fixture, or a flying probe machine with a few little probes darting around the board, which checks shorts, opens and component values with the board unpowered.

After that comes a programming station that flashes firmware and writes the serial number, then a functional test station where the board is powered and exercised, typically in a fixture with pogo pins and instruments or a PC controlling a test sequence. Some products also have an RF or calibration station, a hipot tester for mains isolation, and a burn-in chamber. At the end there's final visual inspection, labelling and packing, and off to the side a repair bench where technicians fix failed boards using the test logs.

All of these are linked by conveyors and a database that logs each result by serial number, and the slowest station sets the line's output."
:::

::: technical
**Station list with purpose.**

| Station | What it is | Checks |
|---|---|---|
| SPI | 3D laser or structured-light scanner after the printer | Paste height, area, volume, offset |
| Pre/post-reflow AOI | Camera gantry with lighting (multi-angle) | Presence, polarity, position, bridges, fillet shape ([[q0608]]) |
| X-ray (2D/3D) | X-ray source and detector | Hidden joints: BGA/QFN voids, bridges, head-in-pillow |
| ICT | Bed-of-nails fixture and measurement hardware ([[q0612]]) | Shorts, opens, component values, orientation |
| Flying probe | Moving probes, no fixture ([[q0611]]) | Same as ICT with lower throughput, high flexibility |
| Boundary-scan station | JTAG controller | Interconnect between compliant ICs |
| Programming | Flash programmer, fixture with pogo pins | Firmware, calibration, serial number |
| FCT | Fixture, power supplies, loads, DMM, scope, PC | Product-level behaviour ([[q0610]]) |
| RF / calibration | Shielded box, VNA/spectrum analyser | Output power, sensitivity, trims |
| Hipot / safety | High-voltage tester | Insulation, ground continuity |
| Burn-in | Temperature chamber with powered racks | Early-life failures |
| Final QA / pack | Visual inspection, labels, barcode scan | Cosmetics, labelling, traceability |
| Repair | Rework bench: iron, hot air, microscope | Fix failures, record the cause |

**Line balancing at the test end.** The line rate equals the slowest station's rate (after parallel stations are accounted for). Suppose AOI 30 s, ICT 40 s, programming 60 s, FCT 45 s. Programming limits to $3600/60 = 60$ boards/h. Duplicating the programming station (2 x 30 s effective) moves the bottleneck to FCT at 45 s, giving $3600/45 = 80$ boards/h (computed).

**ESD and handling.** All stations are ESD-protected: grounded work surfaces, wrist straps, conductive trays, ioniser bars, humidity control, following ANSI/ESD S20.20 or IEC 61340-5-1 practice. Static discharge can silently damage ICs and cause latent failures.

**Information flow.** Barcode or QR scanners read the board serial at each station; the manufacturing execution system routes boards, blocks any board that skipped a mandatory step, and stores results.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The set and order of stations vary widely by product and factory. The table is a representative layout, not a universal one.
:::

::: short
"After reflow I'd expect AOI with cameras checking part presence and solder, X-ray for BGAs, then an electrical test station: in-circuit test with a bed-of-nails fixture, or a flying probe machine. Then a programming station for firmware and serial numbers and a functional test station that powers and exercises the board. Depending on the product there's also RF calibration, hipot, and a burn-in chamber, with final inspection and a repair bench. Everything logs to a database by serial number."
:::

::: long
"Walking down a line, there are really three groups of stations. First, inspection stations. After the stencil printer there's often solder paste inspection, which is a 3D scanner measuring each paste deposit. After the reflow oven there's automated optical inspection, where a camera head with different lighting angles scans the board and compares it with a reference to catch missing parts, wrong rotation, bridges and bad fillets. If there are BGAs there's an X-ray machine looking through the package at the joints.

Second, electrical test stations. An in-circuit tester has a fixture with hundreds of spring-loaded pins that press on test pads, and the machine measures shorts, opens and component values very quickly. If the volume is lower there's a flying probe machine, where a few probes on moving arms touch pads one after another, which needs no fixture but takes minutes. Then a programming station loads firmware and the serial number, and a functional test station powers the board up in a fixture and runs the product through its paces with supplies, loads and instruments. RF products add a shielded RF test station, mains products add hipot, and some get a burn-in chamber.

Third, the human and information side: a repair bench with microscopes and rework tools, final inspection and packing, and barcode scanners that tie every result to a serial number so the system can enforce the route.

And behind all of it is line balancing: the slowest station sets the output. If programming takes 60 seconds and everything else is 45, I'd add a second programming station or flash in the ICT fixture."
:::

::: terms
- **SPI**: solder paste inspection.
- **AOI**: automated optical inspection.
- **ICT**: in-circuit test.
- **Pogo pin**: spring-loaded contact probe.
- **FCT**: functional test.
- **Hipot**: high-potential insulation test.
- **MES**: manufacturing execution system.
- **ESD**: electrostatic discharge protection practices.
- **Bottleneck**: slowest station limiting line rate.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "AOI, ICT, functional test."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names at least eight stations and gives each a one-line purpose and what it measures.
- Orders them sensibly and explains bottlenecks with a number.
- Mentions programming, calibration, repair and ESD handling, which are easy to forget.
- Explains how serialisation and the MES route boards and block skipped steps.
- Connects stations to board design (fiducials, test points, programming pads).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Forgetting the programming station.** Many failures at FCT are just unflashed boards.
- **Treating AOI as an electrical test.** It sees appearance only.
- **Listing stations without purpose.** The interviewer wants what each one detects.
- **Ignoring ESD.** A line without ESD control causes latent damage.
- **Forgetting the repair loop.** Failed boards need diagnosis, repair and full retest.
- **Overlooking the bottleneck.** More fast stations don't help if one is slow.
:::

## Station details

**SPI.** A structured-light or laser triangulation system scans the board and reports volume, height and area per pad against limits (typically as a percentage of the stencil aperture volume, with tolerance windows such as 50-150%, set by process capability). Feeds data back to the printer for automatic adjustment.

**AOI.** Multi-angle lighting (top, side, coloured rings) and 2D or 3D imaging. It compares with a programmed library, e.g. polarity marks for diodes. Cannot see hidden joints or check values. Programming the library needs a good set of golden boards and care to limit false calls.

**X-ray.** 2D transmission shows voids and bridges as density differences; 3D (laminography or CT) separates layers. It is slow, so used for sampling or critical boards. Void percentage limits (often cited for thermal pads or BGAs by customer spec) are not universal.

**ICT / flying probe.** See [[q0609]], [[q0611]], [[q0612]] for how they work. ICT measures each part via guarded measurements: for example a 10 kΩ resistor in parallel with another 10 kΩ path would read 5 kΩ unless guarding isolates the other path, which is why ICT software uses guard probes.

**Programming.** A flash station may be the line's slowest step, because flash write time scales with image size. Parallel programming of a panel, or in-fixture programming, is common.

**FCT.** A fixture with pogo pins, programmable supplies and loads, DMM/scope or custom instruments, and a PC running a sequence (often LabVIEW, Python, or a vendor test executive). Tests rails, I/O loopbacks and sensors, comms and current consumption.

**RF / calibration.** Shielded fixture to avoid interference; measures output power, frequency error, receiver sensitivity; stores trims.

**Hipot and safety.** Applies high voltage between isolated circuits and checks leakage; for mains-powered products.

**Burn-in.** Racks in an oven, powered and monitored for hours; typically sample-based or for high-reliability products.

## The bottleneck calculation

Station cycle times: AOI 30 s, ICT 40 s, programming 60 s, FCT 45 s. Rate is limited by the maximum, 60 s, so 60 boards/h. Add a second programming station: effective 30 s, new maximum is 45 s (FCT), so 80 boards/h, a 33% gain (computed). Adding a third programming station would change nothing, because FCT now limits.

## Design implications

| Station | What your board should have |
|---|---|
| AOI | Clear polarity marks, fiducials, silkscreen not on pads |
| ICT / FP | Test points on nets, fixture-friendly spacing ([[q0621]]) |
| Programming | Programming pads/header, boot-mode access |
| FCT | Accessible rails, test modes, UART/log output |
| Hipot | Creepage and clearance consistent with the voltage |
| Repair | Space around parts for tools, reworkable footprints |

> [!TIP]
> If you can, visit a line and notice the three-minute tour version: where do boards queue? That's the bottleneck. In an interview, the story "I noticed programming was the slow step" shows systems thinking.
:::

::: applications
- **Contract manufacturers** running many products over shared AOI/X-ray and product-specific test fixtures.
- **Automotive tier-1 plants** with full traceability and 100% end-of-line test.
- **Consumer electronics** with high-speed parallel functional test cells.
- **Medical device lines** with documented test limits and calibration records.
- **Low-volume labs** with a flying probe and a bench FCT jig.
:::

::: followups
### Which station would you add first to improve quality on a line with frequent BGA problems?
X-ray (2D or 3D) to see voids, bridges and head-in-pillow joints that AOI and ICT can't see well, plus boundary scan for interconnect. And look upstream: paste volume (SPI), profile and warpage.

### What do you do if programming is the slowest station?
Parallelise: program a panel at once, add a second programming station, or program inside the ICT fixture, speed up the programming interface or image size, or program earlier in the flow (pre-programmed devices).

### Why do all stations scan the board serial?
To route boards, enforce that no required step was skipped, store results against the serial, and trace failures later. The MES can block a board from advancing if it failed or missed a station.

### What's the difference between AOI and X-ray stations?
AOI uses visible light to check surface features: placement, polarity, fillets. X-ray sees through packages for hidden joints (BGA, QFN) and internal voids. AOI is fast and in line; X-ray is slower and is often sampled or used on critical boards.

### Where do ESD controls matter most on the line?
Anywhere boards are handled unprotected: loading/unloading, test fixtures, repair benches, final QA. Grounded surfaces, wrist straps, ionisers and proper packaging prevent latent damage that functional test may not catch.
:::
