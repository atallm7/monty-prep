::: basics
### In plain words
An inductor is drawn as a coil: a wire comes in, goes through three or four humps or loops, and goes out. You label it with a reference designator such as L1 and its value in henries, like 10 µH. Extra lines or dots added to the coil tell you about a core or about coupled windings.

### Picture it
The symbol is a cartoon of the part itself. An inductor really is a wire wound into turns, like a spring or an old phone cord seen from the side. Each hump stands for one or more turns. Two parallel lines next to the coil stand for a core inside it, such as iron or ferrite. The drawing does not show how many turns there are, so the label carries the value.

### Words you need
- **Henry (H)**: the unit of inductance. Real parts are nH to mH.
- **Core**: the magnetic material the coil is wound around. Two lines in the symbol show it.
- **Saturation current**: the current at which a cored inductor stops working properly. It matters as much as the inductance in power circuits.
- **Coupled inductors**: two coils sharing a core, drawn with dots to show matching ends. This is a transformer.
- **Ferrite bead**: a part drawn like an inductor that acts as a lossy resistor at high frequency.

### The simplest example
Draw a short wire, four humps in a row, and another short wire. Write L1 above it and 10 µH, 3 A below it. That is a complete inductor on a schematic. Add two straight lines alongside the humps and it becomes a cored inductor. Add an arrow through it and it is variable. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page lists the other variants and what each one communicates. See also [[q0021]].
:::

::: testing
On the surface it's a ten-second drawing check: can you sketch a coil (a row of humps or loops) with two leads and label it **L** with a value in henries? Underneath, the interviewer is listening for whether you know the *variants* and what each one tells the reader of a schematic:

1. The plain coil (air core) vs. the coil with parallel lines (iron or ferrite core), and that some schematics draw a ferrite core with dashed lines.
2. Coupled inductors and transformers with **dot convention**, and the **ferrite bead** symbol, which looks different because it behaves differently.
3. That the symbol is a picture of the physical object: a wire wound into turns, which is exactly where inductance comes from.
:::

::: why
Schematics are the main language of hardware teams. You'll read inductor symbols in every buck converter, EMI filter, RF matching network and motor driver you touch, and the variant matters: a cored power inductor saturates, an air-core RF coil doesn't; a ferrite bead is lossy by design and is *not* a substitute for an inductor in a filter that needs high Q. Getting the symbol right, labelling the reference designator (L1) and value (10 µH, 3 A), and knowing dot convention for coupled windings is the baseline that lets a reviewer trust your drawings. It's also the opener for the rest of the inductor section ([[q0039]] to [[q0054]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor**: a two-terminal part that stores energy in a magnetic field and opposes changes in current, $v = L\,di/dt$. See [[q0039]] and [[q0041]].
- **Coil = turns of wire**: the humps in the symbol represent turns. More turns means more flux linkage and more inductance ($L \propto N^2$).
- **Magnetic core**: a high-permeability material (iron, ferrite) inside the coil that multiplies inductance; shown by lines next to the coil. See [[q0048]].
- **Reference designator**: inductors are labelled L1, L2…; ferrite beads are often FB1 or also L-prefixed, depending on the company's convention.
- **Dot convention**: for magnetically coupled windings, dots mark the terminals that have the same voltage polarity at the same instant.
- **Symbol vs. model**: the symbol stands for an ideal inductor; the real part has parasitics (DCR, winding capacitance) that you draw explicitly when they matter. See [[q0047]].
:::

::: technical
**Basic symbol.** A series of 3–4 semicircular humps (US/ANSI style, IEEE 315) or loops between two leads. Reference designator **L**, value in henries (typically nH for RF, µH for power conversion, mH for audio/line filters, H for mains chokes). Inductors are non-polarized as single parts; the only "polarity" is the dot on coupled windings.

**Variants and what they communicate:**

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Coil only | Air core or unspecified; linear, no saturation (RF coils, PCB spirals) |
| Coil + two solid lines | Laminated iron / magnetic core: higher $L$ per turn, can saturate |
| Coil + dashed lines | Ferrite or powdered core (convention varies by organization) |
| Coil + diagonal arrow | Variable / adjustable inductor (tuning slug) |
| Two coils + core lines + dots | Coupled inductor or transformer; dots mark like-polarity terminals |
| Filled rectangle, or "FB" box | Ferrite bead: designed to be resistive (lossy) at its target frequency |

**Annotations that matter.** For a power inductor, the value alone is incomplete. A good schematic or BOM line gives $L$, tolerance (often ±20%), saturation current $I_{sat}$, rated (thermal) current $I_{rms}$, and DCR. For RF, it gives $L$, Q at frequency and self-resonant frequency.

**Dot convention.** If current enters the dotted terminal of one winding, the mutual-inductance voltage induced in the other winding is positive at its dotted terminal: $v_2 = M\,di_1/dt$ with + at the dot. Flyback converters deliberately wind the secondary "anti-dot" so energy transfers when the primary switch turns off.

**Physical origin.** The symbol is a cartoon of the part: $N$ turns of wire, each linking flux $\Phi$, giving flux linkage $\lambda = N\Phi = Li$. For a long solenoid $L = \mu_0\mu_r N^2 A / \ell$, so the "humps" are the turns, and the core lines are $\mu_r$.
:::

::: short

Two parallel lines next to the coil mean it has a magnetic core, an arrow through it means it's adjustable, and two coils with dots are coupled windings or a transformer. A ferrite bead usually gets its own rectangle symbol because it acts more like a frequency-dependent resistor. For power parts I'd also annotate saturation current."
:::

::: long
"The standard inductor symbol is a coil. You draw a lead coming in, then a row of three or four humps, then a lead going out. I'd label it with a designator, L1, and a value. Inductance is in henries, but in practice you see nanohenries in RF circuits, microhenries in DC-DC converters, and millihenries in audio or mains filters.

The coil shape isn't arbitrary. An inductor really is a wire wound into turns. Current in each turn makes magnetic flux, and that flux links all the other turns. The total flux linkage per amp is the inductance, and because both the flux and the number of turns it links scale with N, inductance goes as N squared.

Then there are the variants. If the coil has a magnetic core, like iron or ferrite, I draw two parallel lines next to it. That tells the reader the inductance is boosted by the core's permeability, but also that it can saturate if the current gets too high. Some schematics use dashed lines for ferrite or powdered cores. A diagonal arrow through the coil means it's variable, like an old tuning coil with an adjustable slug.

If two coils share a core, that's a coupled inductor or transformer, and the dots matter. Current entering one dot induces a voltage that's positive at the other dot. Get the dots wrong in a flyback converter and it simply won't transfer energy the way you intended.

Last one: ferrite beads. They're often drawn as a filled rectangle or a box labelled FB. They're made of lossy ferrite and act mostly like a resistor at high frequency, which is what you want for soaking up noise. So it's worth drawing them differently from a real inductor.

In a power schematic I'd always annotate more than the value: something like 4.7 µH, 5 A saturation, 20 milliohm DCR, since that's what decides whether the circuit actually works."
:::

::: terms
- **Inductor (L)**: a component that stores energy in a magnetic field and develops a voltage proportional to the rate of change of current through it.
- **Henry (H)**: the unit of inductance; 1 H produces 1 V when current changes at 1 A/s.
- **Air-core inductor**: a coil with no magnetic material; linear and saturation-free, but low inductance per turn.
- **Cored inductor**: a coil wound on iron, ferrite or powdered metal, shown with parallel lines; much higher inductance per turn, but it can saturate.
- **Coupled inductor / transformer**: two or more windings sharing magnetic flux, drawn with dots for polarity.
- **Dot convention**: dotted terminals have the same instantaneous polarity of induced voltage.
- **Ferrite bead**: a lossy ferrite sleeve or chip used to absorb high-frequency noise; rated by impedance at 100 MHz rather than inductance.
- **Reference designator**: the label prefix, L for inductors, T for transformers, FB (or L) for ferrite beads.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws some loops and writes "L".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws clean humps with leads and labels both **designator and value with units** (L1, 10 µH), and for power parts adds $I_{sat}$.
- Explains that the humps represent **turns**, connecting the symbol to $L \propto N^2$.
- Distinguishes air-core vs. cored symbols and says what the core implies (more $L$ per turn, saturation risk).
- Mentions **dot convention** for coupled windings, with an example of why it matters (flyback polarity).
- Knows the **ferrite bead** is drawn differently because it's lossy by design, and says *when* you'd use one instead of an inductor.
- Notes conventions vary (company libraries, IEEE vs IEC) and the key is to be consistent and unambiguous.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing a resistor-like zigzag.** Zigzag is a resistor. Inductors are rounded humps or loops. On a whiteboard, exaggerate the curves.
- **Forgetting units or using "L = 10".** 10 what? Inductance spans nH to H, so always write the prefix: 10 nH and 10 µH are wildly different parts.
- **Treating a ferrite bead as an inductor.** A bead's impedance is mostly resistive at its rated frequency and its inductance collapses with DC bias. Putting one in an LC filter where you need a clean inductor can create a lossy, poorly-defined filter or even a resonance with the capacitor.
- **Ignoring dots on coupled windings.** Two coils without dots are ambiguous. With a transformer, reversing one winding flips the output polarity.
- **Thinking an inductor has polarity like an electrolytic capacitor.** A single inductor is non-polar. (Very fussy designers sometimes orient the start of the winding toward the quiet node to reduce radiated noise, which is why some parts have a polarity mark, but it's an EMI detail, not an electrical requirement.)
:::

::: deep
## Why the symbol is a coil

The symbol is a stylized drawing of the physical part. Take a wire and wind it into $N$ turns. Current $i$ in the wire produces a magnetic field; the field lines pass through the middle of the coil and loop around the outside. Each turn "links" the flux $\Phi$ produced by the whole coil, so the total flux linkage is

$$\lambda = N\Phi = L\,i$$

and inductance is simply flux linkage per amp, $L = \lambda / i$ (developed fully in [[q0039]]).

For a long, thin coil (a solenoid) of cross-section $A$, length $\ell$ and core permeability $\mu = \mu_0\mu_r$, the field inside is nearly uniform and

$$L = \frac{\mu_0 \mu_r N^2 A}{\ell}$$

So the two things the symbol encodes, the turns and the core lines, are exactly the two biggest knobs on inductance. Worked example: 100 turns, $A = 1\ \text{cm}^2$, $\ell = 5$ cm, air ($\mu_r = 1$):

$$L = \frac{4\pi\times10^{-7}\cdot 100^2 \cdot 10^{-4}}{0.05} \approx 25\ \mu\text{H}$$

Put the same winding on a ferrite rod with an effective permeability of, say, 100 and you get roughly 2.5 mH. That's why the core lines are worth drawing.

**Air core (plain coil).** No magnetic material, so the B–H relation is linear forever: no saturation, no core loss, very stable with temperature. The cost is low inductance per turn, so air coils live in RF (nH range) where you need a stable, high-Q part, and in a few specialised high-current applications.

**Cored (coil + lines).** The lines represent a magnetic core. Historically two solid lines meant laminated iron (mains transformers, chokes). Many people draw dashed lines for ferrite or powdered cores, though plenty of company libraries use solid lines for everything. The core lines quietly warn the reader: this part can saturate ([[q0046]]), has core loss ([[q0050]]), and its inductance drifts with temperature and DC bias.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The solid-vs-dashed core convention is not applied consistently across companies, CAD libraries and textbooks. IEEE 315 (the US graphic-symbols standard) and IEC 60617 both define coil symbols with core variants, but in practice most schematics just use whatever is in the team's library. Some older European schematics draw an inductor as a filled or open rectangle. If you're unsure what a symbol means on a real schematic, read the BOM part number.

**Variable (arrow).** An arrow through the coil means adjustable: classic examples are slug-tuned IF transformers in radios, or a variometer. Rare in modern designs, where you'd tune with a varactor or switch capacitors instead.

**Coupled inductors and transformers.** Two windings drawn on either side of a shared core. The **dots** carry the polarity. If current $i_1$ enters the dotted end of winding 1, the induced voltage in winding 2 is $v_2 = M\,\frac{di_1}{dt}$, positive at winding 2's dot. Reference designator is usually **T** for transformers, sometimes L for coupled inductors (e.g. a SEPIC coupled inductor or a common-mode choke).

**Ferrite bead.** Usually a filled rectangle or a box marked FB. A bead is ferrite material chosen to be *lossy* at high frequency: at its rated frequency (datasheets quote impedance at 100 MHz, e.g. "600 Ω at 100 MHz"), its impedance is mostly resistive. That turns noise into heat instead of reflecting it, which is good for EMI but means it's a very different part from an inductor. Drawing it differently stops someone "optimizing" it into an inductor or vice versa.

## What the symbol promises electrically

The ideal symbol means one equation: $v = L\,\frac{di}{dt}$ ([[q0041]]). Current can't change instantly ([[q0043]]), the impedance is $j\omega L$ ([[q0040]]), so the part is a short at DC and an open at very high frequency ([[q0045]]), and the voltage leads the current by 90°. Try it below: set the element to L and watch the current waveform lag the voltage by a quarter cycle.

A real inductor is not just that symbol: it also has winding resistance (DCR), inter-winding capacitance and core loss. When those matter, you draw them explicitly around the ideal symbol, which is the realistic model in [[q0047]].

> [!INTERVIEW]
> When asked to draw a symbol, draw it, label it, then add one sentence that shows you've used the part: "and for a power inductor I'd always note the saturation current next to the value." It turns a trivia question into a signal of real experience.
:::

::: applications
- **Buck/boost converters**: the power inductor (e.g. 4.7 µH, 5 A) is drawn with core lines or plain, labelled with $I_{sat}$; its value sets ripple current ([[q0212]]).
- **RF matching networks**: tiny air-core or multilayer chip inductors (0402, a few nH) drawn as plain coils, with Q and SRF on the BOM.
- **EMI filtering on power entry**: a common-mode choke drawn as two coupled windings with dots on the same end, so differential currents cancel in the core.
- **Ferrite beads on supply rails**: e.g. a "600 Ω at 100 MHz" bead feeding an analog supply pin, drawn with the bead symbol.
- **Flyback converters and isolated supplies**: a transformer symbol where the dot placement shows that the secondary conducts when the primary switch is off.
- **Relay coils and solenoids**: often drawn as a box or coil; they are inductive loads that need flyback protection ([[q0144]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- The answer uses the common **US/IEEE 315-style** humps symbol. IEC-style drawings use similar loops; some older European drawings use rectangles. Any clear, consistent convention is acceptable.
- The core-line conventions (solid for iron, dashed for ferrite) are **common but not universal**. Many company libraries use one cored symbol for all core types.
- "Inductor" here means a discrete two-terminal part. If the interviewer is asking about **parasitic inductance** (trace or via inductance), you'd still draw a coil, often labelled $L_{trace}$ or ESL, and sometimes in a dashed "parasitics" box.
- A ferrite bead is drawn separately here because it's functionally different. Some libraries do draw beads as an inductor with an "FB" label, which is also acceptable as long as the part number makes it clear.
:::

::: followups
### Why is inductance proportional to the number of turns squared?
Each turn adds to the magnetomotive force ($NI$), so flux grows with $N$ for a fixed magnetic path. That flux then links all $N$ turns, so flux linkage $\lambda = N\Phi$ picks up another factor of $N$. For a toroid or solenoid, $L = \mu N^2 A/\ell$. Doubling turns roughly quadruples inductance, which is also why core vendors publish an $A_L$ value in nH/turn²: $L = A_L N^2$, so an $A_L$ of 100 nH/turn² with 10 turns gives 10 µH.

### How would you draw a common-mode choke, and why do the dots matter?
Two windings on the same core, with the dots on the same side (both input ends dotted). Differential-mode current flows in one dot and out the other, so the fluxes cancel and the choke looks like almost nothing. Common-mode noise flows the same direction in both windings, the fluxes add and the choke presents high impedance. If you wire one winding backwards, you've built a differential-mode inductor that saturates on the load current.

### What's the difference between a ferrite bead and an inductor, and when would you use each?
A bead is lossy ferrite: its impedance at high frequency is mostly resistive, so it dissipates noise. It's rated by impedance at 100 MHz, not inductance, and its impedance drops a lot with DC bias current. An inductor is designed for low loss and well-defined $L$. Use a bead to keep tens-to-hundreds-of-MHz hash off an analog rail; use an inductor for energy storage (converters) or a well-defined LC filter at lower frequency.

### What does a polarity mark on a single inductor mean if inductors are non-polar?
Electrically nothing for a single winding. Some shielded and unshielded inductors mark the winding start so you can connect the outer layer of the winding to the quiet node (e.g. $V_{out}$) and the inner start to the noisy switch node, or vice versa, which reduces capacitive coupling of the switch-node $dv/dt$ to nearby circuits. It's an EMI optimization, not a functional requirement.

### What if the interviewer hands you a schematic with a coil next to two solid lines and asks what could go wrong at high current?
The lines say there's a magnetic core, so the part has a saturation current. Above it, the core's permeability collapses, inductance drops sharply, $di/dt = V/L$ rises and current spikes. In a buck converter that can trip current limit or kill the switch. I'd check the BOM for $I_{sat}$ against peak (not average) inductor current, including ripple and transients, with margin for temperature, since ferrite $I_{sat}$ drops as it gets hot. See [[q0046]].
:::

- Draws a coil (rounded humps or loops) with two leads, not a zigzag :: The zigzag is a resistor; confusing them on a whiteboard is an instant credibility hit.
- Labels designator and value with units (L1, 10 µH) :: Inductance spans nH to H; a value without a prefix is meaningless on a real schematic.
- Shows the cored variant and explains what the core implies :: Core lines warn of saturation and core loss, which are central to power design.
- Mentions dot convention for coupled windings or transformers :: Polarity errors in transformers and chokes are real, common bugs.
- Distinguishes ferrite beads from inductors :: Beads are lossy by design; mixing them up causes poorly-behaved filters.
- Connects the symbol to the physics (turns linking flux, L ∝ N²) :: Shows understanding, not just memorization of a picture.
- Mentions practical annotations like I_sat or DCR for power inductors :: Signals hands-on experience with real parts and BOMs.
