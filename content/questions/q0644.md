::: basics
### In plain words
A ball grid array, or BGA, is a chip package whose connections are small solder balls laid out in a grid on its underside, instead of legs around the edge. The grid fits many connections into a small area and keeps each path short. The price is hidden joints and a board that needs careful design.

### Picture it
Picture a crowd standing in a grid on a floor, each person needing a path to the exit. The people at the edge walk out easily. People in the middle need gaps to squeeze through. Pack them tighter and the gaps vanish. A BGA board is the same: every ball needs a path, and tighter spacing leaves less room.

### Words you need
- **Pitch**: the center-to-center spacing between neighboring balls.
- **Reflow**: heating the board so the balls and solder paste melt and form joints. See [[q0602]].
- **Escape routing**: getting a copper path from each ball out to the edge of the grid or down to another board layer.
- **Via**: a tiny plated hole that connects copper on different layers of the board.
- **X-ray inspection**: the way to check hidden joints, since nothing is visible from above.

### The simplest example
Take a 10 mm square package with balls at 0.5 mm pitch. The grid is 20 × 20, so it holds 400 balls. An edge-pin package of the same size and pitch holds only about 80 pins. That is the density win. Now the routing. At 0.8 mm pitch with pads half that size, the gap between pads is 0.4 mm. A 0.1 mm copper path (a trace) needs 0.1 mm of clearance on each side, so 0.3 mm total, and it fits. At 0.5 mm pitch the gap is only 0.25 mm, so it does not fit. You then put a via inside the pad itself, which costs more.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds fan-out methods, defects, why corner balls fail first in temperature cycling, and rework.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand a BGA as a system: the package (balls in a grid), the board (pads, escape routing, vias) and the process (reflow, inspection, rework). The interviewer wants the definition, the advantages (pin density, short electrical paths, good thermal path, self-alignment during reflow), and the real costs (hidden joints that need X-ray, rework stations, escape routing with more layers or via-in-pad, warpage, head-in-pillow, fatigue from thermal-expansion mismatch). A strong candidate quantifies at least one of these, for example whether a trace fits between pads at a given pitch.
:::

::: why
Almost every modern processor, FPGA, DDR memory and many SoCs come only as a BGA. If you design boards you will have to fan out a BGA, choose a via strategy, plan inspection and debug without being able to touch the pins, and deal with a rework request. Understanding why BGAs behave as they do (why corner balls fail, why a 0.5 mm pitch changes the board technology) turns a vague "BGAs are hard" into specific design decisions. It connects directly to routing, DFM and rework questions in this section.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ball grid array**: package with solder balls in a matrix on the underside, replacing perimeter leads. See [[q0642]].
- **Pitch, ball and pad size**: centre-to-centre spacing (0.4 to 1.27 mm common) sets how much room there is between pads.
- **Escape routing**: getting signals out of the grid, using dogbone vias, via-in-pad, and extra layers. See [[q0576]], [[q0582]].
- **Pad definitions**: solder-mask-defined (SMD) vs non-solder-mask-defined (NSMD) pads.
- **Reflow and self-alignment**: surface tension pulls balls onto pads. See [[q0602]].
- **CTE mismatch and fatigue**: package, board and balls expand differently with temperature.
- **Inspection and rework**: X-ray, reballing, rework stations. See [[q0632]].
:::

::: answer
"A ball grid array is a package where the electrical connections are solder balls arranged in a grid on the bottom, instead of leads around the edge. The chip sits on a small substrate, and the balls attach to pads on the PCB during reflow.

The advantages come from the grid. You can fit a lot of connections in a small area: a 10 millimetre package can have around 400 balls at 0.5 millimetre pitch, versus about 80 pins on a 0.5 millimetre QFP. The connections are very short, so lead inductance is low, and you can put power and ground balls in the middle close to the die, which helps power integrity. Heat can go down through the balls into the board's planes. And BGAs self-align during reflow because surface tension centres the balls on the pads, so they're tolerant of small placement errors.

The costs are mainly in manufacturing and board design. The joints are hidden, so you need X-ray to inspect them, and rework needs a dedicated station and often reballing. Routing is hard: at 0.5 millimetre pitch there isn't room to run a trace between pads at standard rules, so you need via-in-pad or HDI, and more layers. And there are reliability issues: BGAs warp in reflow, which can cause head-in-pillow defects, and thermal cycling stresses the corner balls the most because they're farthest from the centre, so large BGAs sometimes get underfill. In practice you use a BGA when you need the pin count or performance and plan the board, assembly and inspection for it from the start."
:::

::: technical
**Structure.** A BGA package has a die attached to an organic or ceramic substrate (or directly to a leadframe-like carrier in some types). Wire bonds or flip-chip bumps connect the die to the substrate; the substrate routes to balls on the bottom. Typical ball alloys are SAC (tin-silver-copper) for lead-free. Ball pitches commonly range from 1.27 mm (older, coarse) through 1.0, 0.8, 0.65, 0.5 to 0.4 mm for fine-pitch types.

**Pin count.** Fully populated $N\times N$ array: $N_{balls} = (L/p)^2$. A 15 mm body at 0.8 mm pitch has $\lfloor15/0.8\rfloor^2 = 18^2 = 324$ balls, many packages remove centre or corner balls.

**Escape-routing arithmetic.** With pad diameter $d_p$ and pitch $p$, the gap between adjacent pads is $g = p - d_p$. A trace of width $w$ with clearance $c$ to each pad needs $w + 2c \le g$. Assuming $d_p \approx 0.5p$ and standard 0.1 mm trace and 0.1 mm clearance ($w + 2c = 0.3$ mm):

| Pitch | Pad (assumed 0.5 p) | Gap | Fits trace + clearance (0.3 mm)? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.0 mm | 0.50 | 0.50 | Yes (even 2 traces if finer) |
| 0.8 mm | 0.40 | 0.40 | Yes, one trace |
| 0.65 mm | 0.33 | 0.33 | Barely |
| 0.5 mm | 0.25 | 0.25 | No at 0.1/0.1; needs 75 µm rules or via-in-pad |
| 0.4 mm | 0.20 | 0.20 | No; via-in-pad/microvias (HDI) |

With the actual pad sizes recommended by the package manufacturer (typically a little smaller than the ball), the gap is somewhat larger than this simple model, but the trend holds: below about 0.65 to 0.5 mm pitch, standard design rules stop working.

**Rings and layers.** For an $N\times N$ array, ring $k$ (outermost is $k=1$) has $4(N-2k+1)$ balls. For $N=20$ (400 balls at 1.0 mm in a 20 mm package): ring 1 has 76, ring 2 has 68, and so on down to 4 in ring 10, which sums to 400. Outer rings can be routed out on the top layer; inner rings need vias to inner layers. As a rule of thumb, with one trace between pads each signal layer clears roughly two rings, so a full 20×20 array might need on the order of five signal layers if all balls were signals; real devices have many power and ground balls, which connect to planes through vias and need no escape routing.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> "Two rings per layer" is a rough rule of thumb that assumes one trace channel between pads and dogbone vias; it varies with pitch, via technology and the share of power and ground balls. Always follow the chip vendor's PCB layout guide for the particular package.

**Thermal-expansion fatigue.** Package substrate and silicon expand differently (around 3 to 7 ppm/K for the package/die assembly) from FR-4 (about 14 to 17 ppm/K in-plane). The relative displacement at a ball at distance $L_{DNP}$ from the centre ("distance to neutral point") for a temperature swing $\Delta T$ is $\delta = L_{DNP}\,\Delta\alpha\,\Delta T$, and the approximate shear strain in a ball of height $h$ is $\gamma \approx \delta/h$. For a 15 mm package, the corner ball is at $L_{DNP} = 7.5\sqrt2 = 10.6$ mm; with $\Delta\alpha = 11$ ppm/K, $\Delta T = 100$ K, $h = 0.4$ mm: $\delta = 11.7$ µm and $\gamma \approx 2.9\%$. A 7 mm package would see 1.8% with $h = 0.3$ mm. Strain is highest at the corners, so corner balls fail first, and larger packages are worse.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> This is a simplified geometric strain estimate; real fatigue life depends on solder alloy, ball height, pad design, board stiffness, dwell times and underfill. Use manufacturer reliability data and board-level reliability testing for qualification.
:::

::: long
"A ball grid array is a package where, instead of leads around the edge, the connections are solder balls in a matrix on the underside. During reflow the balls melt and form joints to matching pads on the PCB.

The benefits come from the grid. Pin density scales with area rather than perimeter: a 10 millimetre package fits about 80 pins as a QFP at 0.5 millimetre pitch but around 400 balls as a 0.5 millimetre BGA. The electrical path is short, so inductance is under a nanohenry, ground bounce is lower, and you can put power and ground balls near the middle of the package, close to the die. Heat can flow through the balls into planes. And surface tension self-aligns the package during reflow, so the placement tolerance is forgiving.

The trade-offs are about process and board design. The joints are hidden, so you need X-ray to check for opens, bridges and voids, and rework needs a dedicated station with bottom preheat and often reballing. Escape routing gets hard: at 0.8 millimetre pitch with 0.4 millimetre pads, there's 0.4 millimetre between pads, enough for a trace and clearance at 0.1 millimetre rules. At 0.5 millimetre pitch the gap is about 0.25, which doesn't fit, so you go to via-in-pad with filled and capped vias or microvias, and the board gets more expensive. There are also reliability issues. The package and the board expand differently, so for a 15 millimetre package with a 100 degree swing the corner ball sees roughly 12 microns of shear displacement, a few percent strain on a 0.4 millimetre ball, which is why corner balls fail first and why big BGAs may be underfilled. And warpage during reflow can lift a ball off the paste, giving head-in-pillow.

So I use a BGA when I need the pin count or performance, and I involve the assembler early to plan inspection, stencil, and rework."
:::

::: terms
- **Pitch**: centre-to-centre distance between balls.
- **Dogbone**: short trace connecting a BGA pad to a nearby via, shaped like a dog bone.
- **Via-in-pad (VIP)**: via placed in the pad itself, usually filled and capped.
- **NSMD / SMD pad**: pad defined by copper (mask opening larger than pad) / defined by the solder mask opening.
- **Head-in-pillow (HiP)**: defect where ball and paste fail to coalesce, forming a visible pillow shape in X-ray.
- **Reballing**: replacing a BGA's solder balls.
- **Underfill**: epoxy filling the gap under the package to redistribute stress.
- **Distance to neutral point (DNP)**: distance from package centre to a ball, which sets thermal-mismatch displacement.
- **HDI**: high-density interconnect, using microvias and fine lines.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "pins are balls under the chip; it's good for high pin count but hard to inspect".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies **density** ($(L/p)^2$ vs $4L/p$) and names the **electrical advantage** (short, low-inductance, central power/ground).
- Works through **escape routing**: gap $= p - d_p$ vs $w + 2c$, and says what pitch forces via-in-pad or HDI.
- Explains **assembly and inspection**: X-ray, head-in-pillow, voids, reballing, rework stations.
- Explains **reliability**: CTE mismatch, corner-ball failure, underfill, DNP argument.
- Mentions **pad design** (NSMD vs SMD) and following the manufacturer's land pattern.
- Names **sources** (IPC-7095, vendor layout guides) instead of inventing numbers.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"BGAs have no inductance."** They have much less than leaded packages, not zero; the substrate routing and vias still contribute.
- **Ignoring the vendor's recommended footprint.** Pad size, mask definition and via placement come from the package maker.
- **Via-in-pad without fill and cap.** Solder wicks into the via and starves the joint.
- **Assuming you can probe BGA pins.** You can't; plan test points on the escape vias or use boundary scan ([[q0614]]).
- **Reusing a removed BGA without reballing.** The old balls are deformed; reliability suffers.
- **Overlooking warpage.** Thin, large BGAs warp at reflow temperature; profile and board support matter.
- **Believing X-ray finds everything.** It misses poor wetting that looks aligned, and hairline cracks.
:::

## Why the grid wins on density and electrical performance

Perimeter packages are limited by the circumference; the grid uses the whole area. At $p = 0.5$ mm and $L = 10$ mm, $N_{QFP} \approx 4L/p = 80$ and $N_{BGA} = (L/p)^2 = 400$. The electrical path from die to board is a bond wire or bump, a few millimetres of substrate trace and a ball, usually on the order of a nanohenry or less. Compare that with several nanohenries for a QFP lead plus bond wire: for the 8-output ground-bounce example of [[q0642]], 5 nH gives 0.8 V and 1 nH gives 0.16 V.

Grids also let the vendor interleave signal and power/ground balls: a typical pattern surrounds signal balls with ground to give a local return path. Many BGAs place a block of ground balls under the die, which doubles as a thermal path.

## Pad design and assembly

**NSMD (copper-defined)**: the mask opening is larger than the pad, so the solder wraps the pad's edge and the pad geometry is controlled by etching, which is more precise than mask registration. Recommended for most fine-pitch BGAs because mask registration error is larger than etch error, and solder wrap-around improves fatigue life in many studies. **SMD (mask-defined)**: the mask overlaps the pad edge, which anchors the copper (better pad adhesion, helpful for large pads or tracks that might lift) but concentrates stress at the mask edge. Many package makers specify which they want; follow the vendor's land pattern ([[q0613]]).

During reflow, the molten balls pull the package toward alignment ("self-centring"), so placement accuracy of tens of microns is acceptable; the package floats on the liquid solder. This is a major reason BGA assembly yields are good despite hidden joints.

**Defects.**
- *Head-in-pillow*: the package or board warps during reflow so the ball and paste briefly separate; oxide on the ball then prevents them from merging on cooling. X-ray shows a "pillow" shape.
- *Voids*: gas trapped in the joint (flux outgassing) reduces the cross-section; acceptance criteria are in IPC standards (check IPC-A-610 and IPC-7095 for the allowed void area).
- *Opens and bridges*: from insufficient paste, coplanarity, or too much paste at fine pitch.
- *Cracked joints and pad cratering*: after thermal cycling or mechanical shock (drop, board flexing), cracks form at the interface or in the laminate under the pad.

A 0.1/0.1/0.1 mm rule set needs 0.30 mm, so it works only above about 0.6 mm pitch; 75 µm rules ($3\times0.075 = 0.225$ mm) work down to 0.45 mm. Below that you need via-in-pad and HDI. Real pad sizes differ, but the break points show why 0.5 mm and finer pitch is a different board technology.

**Dogbone fan-out** (pitches of about 0.8 mm and above): each outer pad gets a short stub to a via placed in the space between pads diagonally; inner balls go down vias to inner layers where traces run in the channels between via pads. This keeps solder from wicking into the via because the via is not in the pad.

**Via-in-pad** (pitches of about 0.5 mm and below): the via is in the pad; it must be filled with epoxy or copper and capped (planarised and plated over) so solder doesn't wick away ([[q0578]], [[q0582]]). Cost goes up. Microvias (laser-drilled, from one layer to the next) further reduce space; stacked or staggered microvias form HDI.

**Layer count.** For a 20 × 20 array, rings 1 to 10 contain 76, 68, 60, ..., 4 balls (sum 400). If the rule of thumb of about two rings per signal layer holds, you'd need around five signal layers for the signal balls; because many centre balls are power/ground (which go to planes with a via each, often with several per ball group), the real count is lower. Vendor layout guidelines give layer counts for the particular device.

**Power delivery.** Place decoupling capacitors close to the power balls, often on the opposite side under the BGA, with short vias ([[q0503]], [[q0504]]). With via-in-pad, a capacitor can connect directly beneath its ball, minimising loop inductance.

## Thermal-cycling fatigue

Silicon expands about 2.6 ppm/K and organic substrates and boards more; FR-4 in-plane around 14 to 17 ppm/K. When the temperature changes, the package and the board try to expand by different amounts, and the balls are the compliant link. Using the neutral-point model: the relative displacement of a ball at distance $L_{DNP}$ is $\delta = L_{DNP}\Delta\alpha\Delta T$ and the shear strain $\gamma\approx\delta/h$.

- Corner ball of a 15 mm package: $L_{DNP} = 10.6$ mm, $\Delta\alpha = 11$ ppm/K, $\Delta T = 100$ K: $\delta = 11.7$ µm, $h = 0.4$ mm gives $\gamma \approx 2.9\%$.
- Corner ball of a 7 mm package, $h = 0.3$ mm: $L_{DNP} = 4.95$ mm, $\delta = 5.4$ µm, $\gamma \approx 1.8\%$.

The larger the package, the larger the strain; fatigue life follows a power law in strain range (Coffin-Manson style), so a modest increase in strain shortens life substantially. Mitigations: underfill or corner glue (adds rework difficulty), smaller packages, taller balls, copper-core or compliant balls, matched substrates, and board stiffness. Drop and shock events cause a different failure mode (brittle intermetallic or pad cratering), not thermal fatigue.

## Inspection, testing and rework

- **X-ray** finds bridges, opens, voids and misalignment. 2D X-ray is routine; 3D (laminography or CT) distinguishes layers in double-sided boards.
- **Boundary scan (JTAG)** tests interconnect continuity through the pins without physical access ([[q0614]]).
- **Functional and stress tests** (thermal cycling, vibration) verify reliability.
- **Rework**: remove with a rework station (bottom preheat, top hot air or IR, a profile with TAL), clean pads, then use a new part or reball the old one, print paste or flux, reflow with a similar profile, and X-ray ([[q0632]]).

**Design for debug.** Because pins are inaccessible, route critical signals so they surface at test points or pass vias that can be probed, and use strap and debug pins accessible near the BGA's perimeter. Leave a keepout for the rework nozzle ([[q0636]]).

## When BGAs are the right choice

When pin count, bandwidth or area demands it, and you have the assembly and board capability. If the chip is also available in a QFN or QFP at suitable pin count, prefer the simpler one for prototypes. If the BGA is the only option, bring the assembler into the design review early: stencil design, profile, inspection method and rework plan.
:::

::: applications
- **Application processors, SoCs and FPGAs**: hundreds to thousands of balls in dense arrays.
- **DDR and LPDDR memory**: FBGA packages with specific ball maps for short, matched routes.
- **Network and server ASICs**: large BGAs with power/ground-heavy ball maps.
- **Phones and wearables**: fine-pitch BGAs, WLCSPs and PoP stacks ([[q0647]]).
- **Automotive and industrial controllers**: BGAs with underfill or corner glue for vibration and thermal cycling.
- **Prototype and eval boards**: breakout boards that fan a BGA out to hand-solderable headers.
:::

::: assumptions
The escape-routing and rule-of-thumb numbers use simplified geometry (pad = half pitch, uniform grid, standard rule sets), and real parts specify pad sizes, depopulation and via recommendations in vendor layout guides. The fatigue estimate is a geometric strain model, not a life prediction. Ball heights and alloys vary; lead-free SAC balls are assumed. For ceramic BGAs (CBGA) and tape BGAs (TBGA) the thermal-mismatch behaviour differs. For wafer-level and flip-chip packages (WLCSP, FCBGA) the die is closer to the board and underfill is common. A valid alternative viewpoint is that BGAs are more robust than leaded packages in vibration (no fragile leads) and in assembly yield (self-alignment), so "BGAs are hard" is true mainly for inspection, rework and routing.
:::

::: followups
### Why can't you just route traces between pads at 0.5 mm pitch?
With pads at about 0.25 to 0.3 mm the gap is only 0.2 to 0.25 mm. A 0.1 mm trace needs 0.1 mm clearance on each side, so 0.3 mm total, which doesn't fit. You can use finer rules (75 µm) if the fab supports them, which narrows the gap needed to 0.225 mm, or you use via-in-pad with filled and capped vias or microvias to take signals straight down to inner layers, which means HDI and a higher board cost.

### Why do corner balls fail first in thermal cycling?
They are farthest from the neutral point at the package centre, and the relative displacement between package and board grows with that distance: $\delta = L_{DNP}\Delta\alpha\Delta T$. For a 15 mm package, a corner ball sees about 11.7 µm of displacement across a 100 K swing, about 2.9% shear strain on a 0.4 mm ball. Centre balls see almost none. Underfill or corner staking spreads the strain to counter this.

### What is head-in-pillow and how do you prevent it?
It's a defect where the BGA ball and the solder paste don't merge: warpage lifts the ball off the paste during reflow, oxide forms on the surfaces, and on cooling they touch without wetting, so the joint looks like a head resting on a pillow in X-ray. Prevention: reduce warpage (profile, board support, thicker substrates), use adequate flux activity, fresh paste, a good stencil aperture volume, nitrogen reflow, and appropriate pad design. Test with X-ray and electrical tests since it can pass initial inspection.

### Why use NSMD pads instead of SMD pads?
NSMD pads are defined by copper etching, which is more accurate than mask registration, and solder wraps around the pad edge, improving fatigue life and allowing tight pitch. SMD pads are defined by the mask opening and anchor the copper better, but concentrate stress at the mask edge. Most fine-pitch BGAs recommend NSMD; I follow the vendor's land pattern.

### How do you debug a BGA board when you can't probe the balls?
Plan for it in layout: bring critical signals to accessible vias or test points, put strap and debug pins near the edge, use boundary scan to test connectivity, and use the chip's built-in debug and trace interfaces. For failures, use X-ray, thermal imaging and, if needed, cross-sectioning. During bring-up, power-rail measurements near the decoupling capacitors help a lot.

### Scenario: 3% of boards fail an intermittent open on one corner ball after temperature cycling. What do you do?
Take failures to X-ray and then cross-section: check whether the crack is at the ball-to-pad or ball-to-package interface (fatigue, or brittle fracture, pad cratering). Corner-ball failure points to thermal mismatch, so I'd consider underfill or corner staking, a smaller or lower-CTE package option, bigger or NSMD pads, or reducing temperature swing. Check profile (peak and TAL), warpage, and board stiffness; compare with the package maker's board-level reliability data. Fix the root cause rather than rework each board.
:::
