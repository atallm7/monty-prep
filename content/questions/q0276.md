::: testing
Whether you see the flyback voltage as both **the engine of the boost** and **the hazard you must control**. The interviewer is looking for:

1. A precise statement of what happens: when the switch opens, the inductor current continues, $v_L$ reverses, and the switch node rises until the diode conducts, **clamping it at $V_{out}+V_F$**.
2. Why that is useful: the flyback energy is delivered to the output instead of wasted, which is how the voltage steps up.
3. Why it is dangerous: if the current has no path (open load, missing or failed diode, controller fault), the voltage rises until something breaks down, $V\approx I\sqrt{L/C_{par}}$.
4. Real-world refinements: ringing from parasitic $L$ and $C$, snubbers, switch voltage rating, overvoltage protection.
:::

::: why
Voltage spikes at the switch node are one of the most common ways power converters die, and the question is how a good engineer thinks about them: where does the energy go, what clamps it, what is the worst case? In a boost the clamp is the output itself, which is why the topology is relatively forgiving, but the worst cases (open-circuit load, start-up overshoot, fault conditions, parasitic ringing) are what sizing the MOSFET's $V_{DS}$ rating and the OVP threshold are about. The same effect shows up with relays and motors ([[q0187]], [[q0265]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor continuity**: $i_L$ cannot jump, so interrupting it creates $v_L=L\,di/dt$ of large magnitude and reversed polarity. See [[q0043]].
- **Energy in the inductor**: $E=\tfrac12LI^2$. When current is interrupted that energy must go somewhere. See [[q0039]].
- **Clamping**: a diode (or the output capacitor) limits the switch node voltage to a safe value. See [[q0202]] for the freewheel diode in a buck.
- **Parasitic LC**: switch $C_{oss}$, diode junction capacitance, loop inductance. These cause ringing and overshoot.
- **Voltage rating / avalanche**: the MOSFET's $BV_{DSS}$ and repetitive avalanche energy rating.
- **Overvoltage protection (OVP)**: a comparator that stops switching when $V_{out}$ is too high.
:::

::: answer
"When the switch opens, the inductor current can't stop instantly, so the inductor voltage reverses and the switch node flies up. In a boost the diode catches it: once the node gets to the output voltage plus a diode drop, the diode turns on, and the inductor current flows into the output capacitor and load. So the flyback voltage is clamped at $V_{out}+V_F$, and instead of being a spike it becomes the normal off-state voltage on the switch.

That's why it's important in two ways. It's the working principle: the inductor's flyback energy is what gets delivered to the output at a higher voltage. And it sets the stress on the switch, which has to withstand at least $V_{out}$ plus some ringing margin.

The dangerous case is when the current has nowhere to go. If the diode is open, or the load is removed and nothing limits the output voltage, then the energy goes into whatever capacitance is there and the voltage rises as $I\sqrt{L/C}$. With 1.5 A, 4.7 µH and 100 pF on the node, that's over 300 V, far above a 20 V FET. That's why boost controllers have overvoltage protection and why you look at the switch node on a scope for overshoot and ringing."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism.** At turn-off, $i_L=I_{pk}$. The switch current must go to zero, so the switch node rises. Neglecting parasitics, the diode turns on when $V_{SW}=V_{out}+V_F$, and
$$v_L=V_{in}-V_{SW}=V_{in}-V_{out}-V_F<0.$$
The inductor's energy flows to $C_{out}$ and the load. The switch node sees $V_{out}+V_F$ during the entire off-time.

**Unclamped case.** If the current is interrupted with no diode path, energy $\tfrac12LI_{pk}^2$ goes into the node's parasitic capacitance $C_{par}$ (switch $C_{oss}$ plus layout and winding capacitance). Equating energy: $\tfrac12LI_{pk}^2=\tfrac12C_{par}V_{pk}^2$, so
$$V_{pk}\approx I_{pk}\sqrt{\frac{L}{C_{par}}}$$
With $I_{pk}=1.56$ A, $L=4.7\ \mu$H: 107 V for 1 nF, 337 V for 100 pF, and 477 V for 50 pF. The MOSFET would avalanche (if rated) or fail.

**Clamped but parasitic ringing.** Loop inductance $L_{par}$ between switch, diode and capacitor, and the switch's $C_{oss}$, form a resonant circuit. With $L_{par}=5$ nH and $C_{oss}=200$ pF the ring frequency is $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})=159$ MHz and the characteristic impedance is $\sqrt{L/C}=5\ \Omega$. Typical overshoot is on the order of $I_{pk}Z_0$ before damping (7.8 V extra for 1.56 A and 5 Ω, which is a worst-case upper bound if the current commutated instantly), hence the practice of choosing $V_{DSS}$ well above $V_{out}$ and adding an RC snubber with $R\approx Z_0$.

**Open-circuit load.** With no load and the controller still switching at minimum duty, each cycle injects net energy into $C_{out}$, so $V_{out}$ climbs until OVP, the switch rating, or the capacitor breaks down. Hence OVP (often set 5 to 20% above nominal, vendor-dependent) is standard.
:::

::: short
"When the boost switch opens, the inductor current has to continue, so its voltage reverses and the switch node rises until the diode conducts. That clamps the node at $V_{out}+V_F$, so the flyback energy goes into the output instead of a spike. That is both how the boost steps up and what sets the switch voltage stress. If the current has no path, say an open diode or open load with no OVP, the voltage runs up as $I\sqrt{L/C}$ and kills the switch, so you add OVP, snubbers and voltage margin."
:::

::: long
"The flyback voltage is what you get when you interrupt current in an inductor. It can't change instantly, so $v=L\,di/dt$ becomes large and negative, and whatever it is connected to rises until the current has a path.

In a boost that path is built in. When the switch opens, the switch node starts to rise, and as soon as it passes $V_{out}$ plus a diode drop, the diode conducts and the inductor current flows into the output capacitor and load. So the flyback voltage is clamped at about $V_{out}+V_F$, and the inductor voltage is $V_{in}-V_{out}-V_F$, a normal negative voltage that ramps the current down. Nothing gets wasted. That energy is the mechanism that moves charge to a higher voltage, and it's why the switch sees $V_{out}$ in the off-state, which sets the MOSFET rating.

Where it goes wrong is when there's no path. If the diode fails open, or if the load is disconnected and the controller keeps switching with no overvoltage protection, the energy $\tfrac12LI^2$ dumps into small capacitances. For 1.5 A in 4.7 µH with 100 pF at the node the voltage is $I\sqrt{L/C}$, about 340 V, which exceeds the 20 V of the FET.

Even in normal operation the parasitics matter: layout inductance and the switch's output capacitance ring at tens to hundreds of MHz at turn-off and can overshoot the clamp by a few volts, so I'd choose the MOSFET with margin, keep the switch-diode-capacitor loop tiny, and add an RC snubber if the scope shows ringing. I'd also look at start-up: when power is first applied, the inductor and output capacitor can ring and overshoot through the diode, and a soft-start and input-current limit control that."
:::

::: terms
- **Flyback (inductive kick) voltage**: the voltage an inductor generates when its current is interrupted.
- **Clamp**: a path (diode to a rail) that limits a node's voltage.
- **Switch-node overshoot / ringing**: the extra voltage beyond $V_{out}+V_F$ caused by loop inductance and $C_{oss}$.
- **Snubber**: an RC (or RCD) network that damps ringing, with $R\approx\sqrt{L_{par}/C_{par}}$.
- **Avalanche**: breakdown mode of a MOSFET at $BV_{DSS}$. Repetitive avalanche energy is limited.
- **OVP**: overvoltage protection that stops switching above a threshold.
- **Characteristic impedance ($Z_0$)**: $\sqrt{L/C}$ of the resonant parasitic tank.
- **Soft-start**: ramping the reference or current limit at startup to limit inrush and overshoot.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the inductor produces a voltage spike when the switch opens, and the diode protects the switch".

**An exceptional answer**:
- States that the node is clamped at $V_{out}+V_F$ and that this clamped voltage *is* the boost mechanism, not just protection.
- Computes the unclamped worst case, $V\approx I\sqrt{L/C}$, with real numbers.
- Distinguishes **steady-state** stress ($V_{out}$) from **transient** ringing (loop inductance and $C_{oss}$) and fault conditions (open load, missing diode, start-up).
- Names mitigations: MOSFET rating margin, tight layout, snubber, OVP, soft-start, input current limit.
- Points out that in a boost, unlike a buck, an open load is dangerous because the converter *adds* energy to the output with nowhere for it to go.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The flyback spike is always bad."** In a boost it is the normal working voltage. The unwanted part is the overshoot and ringing *above* the clamp.
- **"The diode protects the switch from a spike" and nothing more.** It also transfers the energy. Say it conducts the inductor current to the output.
- **Choosing a FET rated at $V_{out}$ exactly.** Ringing and overshoot need margin, usually 20 to 50% for low-voltage designs and more for high-$dI/dt$ layouts.
- **Assuming a boost is safe with no load.** Without OVP or load, the output rises each cycle until something fails. Unlike a buck, the boost does not self-limit at no load.
- **Forgetting startup.** The output capacitor charges through $L$ and $D$ before switching begins, causing inrush current and an LC overshoot. See below.
- **Ignoring the diode's own parasitics.** Forward-recovery voltage and reverse-recovery current add overshoot and loss, especially in PFC-type high-voltage boosts.
:::

::: deep
## The physics in one line

$v_L=L\,di/dt$. If the current is forced to change by $\Delta I$ in time $\Delta t$ the voltage is $L\Delta I/\Delta t$. For an ideal switch $\Delta t\to0$, so the voltage rises as high as needed until *something* conducts. In a boost that something is the diode to the output.

Walk through a real turn-off. Before: $i_L=i_Q=1.56$ A, diode off, $V_{SW}\approx0.1$ V. As $Q$ turns off over a few ns, $i_Q$ falls and the same current charges $C_{oss}$ of the switch and diode junction, so $V_{SW}$ rises with slope $i_L/C_{par}$. When $V_{SW}$ reaches $V_{out}+V_F=5.4$ V the diode conducts and takes over the current. The node voltage then holds at 5.4 V. Total energy that passed through the parasitic capacitance is small: with $C_{par}=200$ pF it is $\tfrac12C_{par}V^2\approx3$ nJ, lost once per cycle. At 500 kHz: $\tfrac12\times200\text{ pF}\times5.4^2\times5\times10^5=1.5$ mW. Negligible here, but it scales as $V^2f$ and matters in high-voltage boosts.

This is exactly the mechanism of the relay-coil and motor kick ([[q0187]], [[q0265]]), where a flyback diode or TVS gives the current a path. In a boost the diode and output capacitor are that path *by design*.

## Ringing on top of the clamp

Real layouts have loop inductance $L_{par}$ (a few nH) around switch, diode and capacitor, and the switch's $C_{oss}$. When the diode takes over the current, $L_{par}$ and $C_{oss}$ form an underdamped tank. Characteristic impedance: $\sqrt{5\text{ nH}/200\text{ pF}}=5\ \Omega$. Ring frequency: $1/(2\pi\sqrt{5\text{ nH}\times200\text{ pF}})=159$ MHz. The overshoot above the clamp is bounded by about $I_{pk}Z_0=1.56\times5\approx7.8$ V in the worst case where the current commutates instantly, though in practice the diode's capacitance and the damping reduce it. That is why a 5 V boost still uses 20 V (or higher) FETs, and why designers watch the SW node on a bandwidth-limited probe (a 20 MHz limit hides the ringing, so use short ground springs and full bandwidth).

Cures: tight layout (small hot loop), a small gate resistor to slow the edge, an RC snubber across the switch with $R\approx Z_0$ and $C\approx3$ to 4 times $C_{oss}$, a ferrite bead or resistor in the diode path, or a snubbed or soft-recovery diode.

## Startup and inrush: flyback's quieter cousin

Before the controller switches, the output capacitor charges from the input through $L$ and $D$. Treating them as an LC circuit with $C=22\ \mu$F and $L=4.7\ \mu$H: $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})=15.7$ kHz and peak current $V_{in}\sqrt{C/L}=3.7\times2.16=8.0$ A if undamped. DCR, the diode drop and the source impedance reduce this a lot, but a few amps of inrush is typical, and the output can overshoot slightly above $V_{in}$ before the controller takes over. Parts add a soft-start ramp, a lower start-up current limit, or an input load switch. For large output capacitance (hundreds of µF) the inrush can trip a small battery's protection circuit.

## Open-circuit load and OVP

With the controller commanding minimum on-time at no load, each cycle still transfers a little energy. Say 1 µJ per cycle (an assumed value for illustration) into $C_{out}=22\ \mu$F at 5 V: $\Delta V=\Delta E/(CV)=1\ \mu\text{J}/(22\ \mu\text{F}\times5\text{ V})=9$ mV per cycle, which is about 4.5 V per millisecond at 500 kHz. Without OVP, $V_{out}$ climbs through the FET rating in under 4 ms. Real controllers skip pulses at light load (PFM/burst), and an OVP comparator (on a divided feedback or dedicated pin) terminates switching, but the OVP threshold must sit below the lowest-rated component on the rail.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 1 µJ per cycle at no load is illustrative. Actual minimum energy per cycle depends on the controller's minimum on-time, inductor and current-limit settings. Many controllers go into burst mode, which reduces energy per second substantially.

## Boost vs buck: where the flyback energy goes

In a buck the freewheel diode clamps the SW node at about $-V_F$ when the high-side turns off, so it is protected the same way, but there is always a load path through the inductor. In a boost the inductor current is delivered to the output node, a node that must be able to accept it. That is why a boost has the open-circuit hazard and a buck does not ([[q0202]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Choosing the MOSFET voltage rating** for a boost (e.g. 20 V for a 5 V rail, 30 V for 12 V) based on $V_{out}$ plus ringing margin.
- **OVP threshold design** for LED drivers, where an open LED string (open load) is the classic fault and the boost must stop switching.
- **Snubber design** in PFC boosts, where 400 V and fast SiC edges produce large ringing.
- **Relay and solenoid drivers** share the same flyback physics with a freewheel diode or TVS clamp ([[q0187]]).
- **EMI debugging**: the SW-node ringing frequency often shows up as a spike in radiated or conducted spectra and guides snubber and layout changes.
:::

::: assumptions
- Ideal inductor and switch unless parasitics are explicitly included. The energy-balance formula $V=I\sqrt{L/C}$ assumes all energy goes into $C_{par}$ and ignores avalanche clamping and winding capacitance paths.
- **Non-synchronous** boost with a Schottky diode. In a synchronous boost the high-side FET (or its body diode during dead time) provides the clamp path.
- Component values ($L_{par}$ 5 nH, $C_{oss}$ 200 pF) are representative for a small portable boost. Real values depend on package, layout and part.
- **Interpretation**: I took "flyback voltage" to mean the inductive voltage reversal and clamp at the switch node. In a *flyback converter* (a different topology, with a transformer) the term means something else, see below.
:::

::: followups
### What is the voltage across the switch during the off-state of a boost?
Ideally $V_{out}+V_F$. With a 5 V output and Schottky, about 5.4 V. Add ringing and overshoot margin: it is common to see 7 to 9 V on the scope for a small low-voltage design. The FET's $BV_{DSS}$ should be at least 1.5x or more above that for reliability.

### Why can't you just ignore the diode in a boost with a MOSFET that has a body diode?
The body diode points from source (ground) to drain (SW), which is the wrong way to carry current from SW to the output. It conducts only when SW goes below ground. It would not help the flyback path: the inductor current has to go through the *external* diode (or a high-side MOSFET) to $V_{out}$.

### What does an RC snubber do, and how do you size it?
It absorbs the ring energy. Measure the ring frequency $f_r$ on the SW node, add a small capacitor to double or triple the ring period, then calculate $C_{par}$ and $L_{par}$ and choose $R\approx\sqrt{L_{par}/C_{par}}$ with a snubber capacitor 3 to 4 times $C_{par}$. Loss is $C_{snub}V^2f$, so keep it small. An alternative is a slower gate drive or a better layout.

### How is "flyback voltage" in a boost different from a flyback converter?
A flyback converter uses a coupled inductor (transformer) and stores energy in the core during the on-time and delivers it to a secondary winding in the off-time, so the "flyback voltage" is the reflected output voltage and the leakage-inductance spike on the primary, which needs a clamp or snubber. A boost uses the single inductor and the output is the clamp.

### Scenario: your boost converter works until you disconnect the load, then the IC dies. What happened and how do you fix it?
At no load the converter keeps transferring energy with nowhere to go, the output voltage climbs past the IC's rating (or the SW pin's absolute maximum), and the part breaks down. Check whether the IC has OVP and whether it is enabled and referenced correctly (some parts need an external divider on an OVP pin). Add a minimum load, a bleeder, or an OVP crowbar, and verify with a scope that the output stops rising at the OVP threshold. Also confirm no part on the rail is rated below the OVP level.
:::

::: interpretations
"Flyback voltage" here refers to the **inductive kick** at the switch node when the switch opens, not the **flyback converter** topology. In a boost the flyback voltage is clamped by the diode at $V_{out}+V_F$. Some interviewers use the phrase to mean the overshoot above that clamp. Both are covered. The buck equivalent is [[q0202]], and the general inductive-load version is [[q0187]].
:::
