::: testing
Whether you have a clean mental model of an **actuator**: a device that turns electrical energy into motion using magnetic force. The hidden sub-skills:

1. You can define a motor (rotary) and a solenoid (linear, usually on/off) and what they share: a coil of wire that makes a magnetic field.
2. You can list realistic uses and match each device to a job (continuous rotation vs short stroke).
3. You can say what each looks like *electrically* (resistance, inductance, and for a motor a back-EMF), which is what a hardware engineer has to drive.
:::

::: why
Almost every product that moves has a motor or solenoid: fans, pumps, drones, door locks, valves, printers, robots. For a hardware engineer the question is really "what do I have to build to drive this thing?" Both are inductive loads that draw big currents at startup, need protection from voltage spikes when switched off, and have very different behaviour from a resistor. Interviewers (this question is the entry point of the motors section) check that you can name the basics before going into current, flyback and drive circuits.
:::

::: concepts
- **Electromagnetism**: current in a wire creates a magnetic field, and a wire carrying current in a field feels a force. See [[q0334]].
- **Coil/inductance**: both devices are wound coils, so they are inductors with series resistance. See [[q0022]].
- **Ohm's law and power**: coil resistance sets current and heating. See [[q0001]].
- **Back-EMF**: a spinning motor also acts as a generator. See [[q0341]].
- **Energy conversion**: electrical in, mechanical out, plus heat from losses.
:::

::: answer
"A motor is an actuator that converts electrical energy into rotation, and a solenoid converts it into a short linear push or pull. Both work using a coil of wire: current through the coil makes a magnetic field, and that field creates force.

A solenoid is basically a coil with a movable iron plunger inside. When you energize the coil, the magnetic field pulls the plunger in. When you turn it off, a spring pushes it back. So it's mostly on/off, a few millimetres of stroke with decent force. Typical uses are door locks, valves, relays, fuel injectors and latches.

A motor, for example a brushed DC motor, has a rotor with coils and a stator with magnets or coils. Current through the rotor coils in the stator's magnetic field creates torque, and the commutator or electronics keep switching the current so the rotation continues. You use motors for anything that spins or drives: fans, wheels, pumps, drone propellers, hard drives, power tools.

From the electronics side, both are inductive loads: a resistance in series with an inductance, and a motor also generates a voltage as it spins. That's why you need to think about startup current and a flyback diode or other protection when switching them."
:::

::: technical
**Solenoid.** A coil of $N$ turns wound on a bobbin with a ferromagnetic plunger and a return path (frame). Energizing it with current $I$ produces magnetomotive force $NI$ and a flux that pulls the plunger to close the air gap. Force (for a small gap $g$, ignoring saturation) is approximately

$$F \approx \frac{\mu_0 A (NI)^2}{2 g^2}$$

so force rises steeply as the gap closes; a spring returns the plunger when current stops. Electrical model: $R_{coil}$ in series with $L(x)$ that increases as the plunger enters. Types: linear (push/pull), rotary, latching.

**Brushed DC motor.** Rotor coils carry current in the stator's field. Torque and back-EMF:

$$\tau = K_t\, I, \qquad e = K_e\,\omega, \qquad K_t = K_e \ (\text{SI units})$$

Electrical model: $V = IR + L\,\frac{dI}{dt} + K_e\omega$. Mechanical: $J\,\dot\omega = K_t I - \tau_{load} - \tau_{friction}$.

**Other motors.** Brushless DC (BLDC) and stepper motors use the same physics with electronic commutation; AC induction motors use a rotating field. Voice-coil actuators are solenoid-like linear motors with a proportional force-current relation.

**Uses.**

| Device | Motion | Typical uses |
|---|---|---|
| Solenoid | Short linear, on/off | Door locks, valves, relays/contactors, fuel injectors, printer hammers, latches |
| Brushed DC motor | Continuous rotary | Toys, fans, pumps, power tools, wheels |
| BLDC motor | Continuous rotary, efficient | Drones, EV traction, hard drives, cooling fans |
| Stepper motor | Incremental rotary | 3D printers, CNC, camera focus |
| Servo | Position-controlled rotary | RC, robot joints |
| Voice coil | Short linear, proportional | Speaker, HDD head, camera autofocus |
:::

::: long
"Both a motor and a solenoid are electromagnetic actuators: they turn electrical power into mechanical motion using the force a magnetic field puts on a current-carrying coil.

A solenoid is a coil wound around a tube with a movable ferromagnetic plunger. Energize it, the coil makes a magnetic field, the field pulls the plunger toward the centre to close the air gap. Turn it off and a spring returns it. So it gives you a short stroke, mostly binary on or off, with force that gets much stronger as the gap closes. Use cases: door locks, water and pneumatic valves, relays and contactors, fuel injectors, printer hammers and latches.

A motor produces continuous rotation. In a brushed DC motor, a rotor with several coils sits in a magnetic field from permanent magnets. Current in the rotor coils creates a force on each side of the loop, which together make torque. The commutator and brushes flip the current direction as the rotor turns so the torque keeps pushing the same way. Torque is proportional to current, and the spinning rotor also generates a back-EMF proportional to speed. You find motors in fans, pumps, power tools, wheels and propellers; brushless and stepper versions replace the brushes with electronics.

For a hardware engineer, the important electrical picture is that both are an inductor in series with a resistor, and the motor adds that back-EMF source. So they draw a high current when they start, store energy in their magnetic field, and kick back a voltage spike when you switch them off, which means a driver, a flyback path, and current sensing."
:::

::: terms
- **Actuator**: device that converts energy (here electrical) into mechanical motion.
- **Solenoid**: coil with a movable ferromagnetic plunger giving linear motion.
- **Plunger / armature**: the moving iron part pulled by the magnetic field.
- **Rotor / stator**: the rotating and stationary parts of a motor.
- **Commutator and brushes**: mechanical switch reversing rotor current as it turns in a brushed motor.
- **Torque constant $K_t$**: N·m of torque per amp.
- **Back-EMF constant $K_e$**: volts per rad/s of generated voltage.
- **Inductive load**: load whose current cannot change instantly, storing energy in a magnetic field.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Calling a solenoid a "relay".** A relay is a switch operated by a solenoid; a solenoid itself is the actuator.
- **Treating a motor as a resistor.** Its current depends on speed and load because of back-EMF.
- **Forgetting that a solenoid's force depends strongly on the air gap.** It pulls much harder when closed than open.
- **"Motors only draw current when moving."** They draw the most current when stalled or starting.
- **Ignoring the stored energy.** A coil has $\frac12LI^2$ of energy that must go somewhere when switched off.
- **Confusing motor and solenoid coil resistance with their operating current.** The rated current depends on mechanical load and duty.
:::

::: deep
## One principle, two shapes

Both devices rely on the same two facts from electromagnetism:

1. **Current makes a field.** A coil with $N$ turns carrying $I$ produces magnetomotive force $NI$. Winding it on a ferromagnetic core concentrates the flux ([[q0335]]).
2. **A field acts on current (and on iron).** A wire of length $\ell$ carrying $I$ in flux density $B$ feels a force $F = B I \ell$; a piece of iron in a field is pulled toward the stronger field region.

In a **solenoid**, the movable piece is iron and the pull is the "reluctance force": the plunger moves to minimize the magnetic energy, closing the gap. In a **motor**, the force acts on the current-carrying rotor conductors, and the geometry turns that into torque about the shaft. The motor adds a commutation scheme (brushes or electronics) to keep switching current so the torque stays in one direction as the rotor moves. [[q0334]] goes through each mechanism in detail.

## Choosing between them

| Need | Choose | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous rotation | Motor | Solenoid has a limited stroke |
| Short, strong, binary push/pull | Solenoid | Simple, fast, no gearing |
| Precise position | Stepper/servo/voice coil | Closed-loop or step counting |
| Holding force without power | Latching solenoid or gear-reduced motor | Permanent magnet or friction holds |

A solenoid's force is $F \propto (NI)^2/g^2$, so at $g=2$ mm, $N = 500$, $I = 0.5$ A and a 1 cm² pole area the force is about $\frac{4\pi\times10^{-7}\times10^{-4}\times250^2}{2\times(0.002)^2} \approx 0.98$ N: enough to move a lock bolt, not enough to lift a kilogram. Doubling current (if the coil survives) gives four times the force. That square law and the heat from $I^2R$ are why solenoids are usually pulsed.

## Why electronics engineers care

- **Startup current**: both look like a short at the instant of turn-on (motor stalled, plunger open): $V/R$.
- **Inductive kick**: switching the current off makes a large voltage spike across the switch ([[q0340]]).
- **Back-EMF**: a motor's current falls as it speeds up, and it can generate power when driven ([[q0341]]).
- **Noise**: brush arcing and PWM edges create EMI.
- **Heat**: stalled or held-on coils dissipate $V^2/R$.
:::

::: applications
- **Door locks and latches**: latching solenoids that need power only to change state.
- **Valves**: 12 V and 24 V solenoid valves for water, fuel injection and pneumatics.
- **Drones/RC**: BLDC motors for propellers, servos for control surfaces.
- **Consumer electronics**: vibration motors in phones, fans, camera autofocus voice coils.
- **Automation**: stepper motors in 3D printers, DC gearmotors in robot drivetrains.
:::

- "Motor" is taken to mean a **brushed DC motor** first, as in the surrounding questions; BLDC, stepper and AC motors share the physics but need electronic or AC commutation.
- "Solenoid" is the **linear DC solenoid** (coil with plunger). Rotary and latching versions follow the same principle.
- Component values quoted are **typical ranges**; real devices vary by orders of magnitude.
- The answer treats them as DC-driven; AC solenoids and motors have additional behaviour (shading rings, inrush from impedance changes).

::: followups
### What is the difference between a solenoid and a relay?
A relay is a switch (contacts) operated by a solenoid coil; its job is switching a circuit electrically isolated from the coil. A solenoid is an actuator whose job is mechanical motion. The same coil physics, different purpose ([[q0347]]).

### Why does a solenoid produce more force when the plunger is closed?
The magnetic circuit reluctance is dominated by the air gap, so a small gap gives much more flux for the same $NI$. Force scales roughly as $1/g^2$ at a fixed current, so a 2 mm gap that closes to 0.5 mm raises the force by about 16 times.

### How would you drive a 12 V, 1 Ω motor from a microcontroller pin?
Not directly: a GPIO supplies milliamps and the motor can draw up to 12 A. Use a logic-level N-MOSFET or an H-bridge driver, a flyback diode (or the bridge's body diodes), and PWM for speed control, with a current limit sized to the stall current.

### What happens if you hold a solenoid energized for a long time?
It dissipates $V^2/R$ continuously and heats until something fails if it is not rated for continuous duty. Many solenoids are rated for a duty cycle (for example 25%), or use a high pull-in current followed by a lower holding current.

### Scenario: your motor works on the bench supply but resets the microcontroller when connected to the battery. Why?
The startup inrush from the stalled motor draws up to $V/R$ from the battery, whose internal resistance and wiring make the rail sag, and back-EMF/switching noise couples into the logic supply. Check the rail with a scope during start, add bulk and local decoupling, separate power and logic grounds correctly, and soft-start the motor ([[q0343]]).

### How does a voice coil differ from a solenoid?
A voice coil moves a coil in a constant permanent-magnet field, so force is proportional to current ($F = BIl$) and can be positive or negative; a solenoid pulls an iron plunger and its force depends on the gap and goes with $I^2$, and only in one direction.
:::
