::: testing
Whether you know the **landscape** of semiconductor materials and, more importantly, *why* each is used. A list alone is a weak answer. The interviewer wants to see you connect the material property (bandgap, mobility, critical field, thermal conductivity, direct versus indirect gap, manufacturability) to the application:

1. Silicon is the default because of its oxide, cost, wafer quality and a decent bandgap.
2. Germanium is historical (low bandgap, leaky); SiGe is an alloy used for fast BJTs and strained channels.
3. III-V compounds (GaAs, InP, GaN) are used where speed or light emission matters.
4. Wide-bandgap SiC and GaN are used for high voltage, high temperature and high frequency power.
:::

::: why
Choosing a material is a system-level decision: it sets the forward voltage of a diode, the on-resistance of a power switch, the maximum junction temperature, the color of an LED and the frequency of an RF amplifier. When an interviewer for a power, RF or photonics team asks about GaN versus silicon MOSFETs, this is the foundation. Even for general hardware roles, knowing why a Schottky diode drops 0.3 V or why a blue LED needs 3 V traces back to the bandgap.
:::

::: concepts
- **Bandgap ($E_g$)**: the energy needed to lift an electron from the valence band to the conduction band; sets forward voltage, leakage, maximum temperature and the colour of emitted light. See [[q0061]].
- **Intrinsic carrier density**: $n_i\propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/2kT}$; a larger gap means exponentially fewer thermally generated carriers.
- **Mobility and saturation velocity**: set the speed and on-resistance. See [[q0068]].
- **Direct vs indirect bandgap**: direct-gap materials (GaAs, GaN) emit light efficiently; indirect ones (Si, Ge) do not.
- **Critical field**: the field at which avalanche breakdown occurs; wide-gap materials tolerate roughly 10x more, enabling thin, low-resistance high-voltage devices.
- **Doping ability**: the material must be dopable both N and P (or at least one type reliably). See [[q0062]], [[q0067]].
:::

::: answer
"Silicon is the main one by a huge margin: it's cheap, you can grow giant, nearly perfect single crystals, it has a native oxide, SiO2, which is why MOSFETs and CMOS work so well, and a bandgap of 1.12 eV that's good for room temperature. Almost every IC is silicon.

After that, the main families are: germanium, the original transistor material, with a small bandgap of about 0.66 eV that makes it leaky, so now it's mostly used as silicon-germanium in fast RF and in strained channels. Then III-V compounds like gallium arsenide and indium phosphide, which have much higher electron mobility and a direct bandgap, so they're used in RF, high-speed electronics and LEDs and laser diodes. And then wide-bandgap materials, silicon carbide and gallium nitride, with gaps around 3.3 eV, so they handle higher voltage, higher temperature and faster switching, which is why they're taking over in EV chargers, inverters and fast chargers.

So the way I think about it is that silicon wins on cost and manufacturability, and everything else is chosen when a specific property, like speed, light, or voltage, matters more than cost."
:::

::: technical
**Elemental (Group IV).** Silicon ($E_g=1.12$ eV, indirect) dominates. Germanium ($E_g=0.66$ eV, indirect) has higher mobility but too high a leakage and no good native oxide. Diamond ($E_g\approx5.5$ eV) is a research and niche material.

**Alloys and IV-IV compounds.** SiGe is used as a base material in heterojunction bipolar transistors and in strained-silicon PMOS channels. SiC (4H polytype, $E_g\approx3.2$ eV) is used for power devices.

**III-V compounds.** GaAs ($E_g=1.42$ eV, direct, electron mobility about 8000 cm²/V·s), InP ($E_g\approx1.34$ eV, direct), GaN ($E_g\approx3.4$ eV, direct), InGaAs, AlGaAs, and others for LEDs, laser diodes, RF amplifiers and photodetectors.

**Typical properties at 300 K** (approximate; vary with doping, crystal quality and source):

| Material | $E_g$ (eV) | $\mu_n$ (cm²/V·s) | Critical field (MV/cm) | Thermal cond. (W/m·K) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Si | 1.12 | ~1350 | ~0.3 | ~150 |
| Ge | 0.66 | ~3900 | ~0.1 | ~60 |
| GaAs | 1.42 | ~8500 | ~0.4 | ~55 |
| 4H-SiC | ~3.2 | ~900-1000 | ~2-3 | ~370 |
| GaN | ~3.4 | ~1000-1500 (bulk), ~2000 (2DEG) | ~3 | ~130-250 |

**Why the gap matters.** $n_i^2\propto e^{-E_g/kT}$. Going from silicon's 1.12 eV to 4H-SiC's 3.2 eV lowers $n_i$ from about $10^{10}$ to about $10^{-8}$ cm⁻³ (computed from the density-of-states formula; sources differ), so intrinsic leakage is negligible even at 300 °C. In power devices the **Baliga figure of merit** $\varepsilon\mu E_c^3$ shows why wide-gap materials give much lower $R_{on}$ for a given blocking voltage. Because $E_c$ is cubed, a 10x higher critical field is a factor of roughly 1000 in this figure of merit.
:::

::: short
"Silicon is the workhorse because it's cheap, forms a great oxide, and has a good 1.12 eV gap. Germanium is the older, leakier, 0.66 eV material, now mostly used in SiGe. III-V compounds like GaAs, InP and GaN are used for RF and optoelectronics because they have high electron mobility and direct bandgaps that emit light. Silicon carbide and gallium nitride are wide-bandgap materials around 3.3 eV used for high-voltage, high-temperature and fast power switching. The choice is a trade between cost and a specific property."
:::

::: long
"If I had to list them by family, I'd start with silicon, because the vast majority of chips are made from it. Silicon is cheap, you can pull huge wafers with very low defect density, and it has a stable native oxide, which is what made the MOSFET and CMOS possible. Its bandgap is 1.12 eV, which is wide enough to keep leakage low at room temperature but narrow enough to switch at about 0.7 V.

Germanium has a 0.66 eV gap and higher mobility, which is why early transistors used it, but its leakage current is orders of magnitude higher and its oxide is unstable, so it lost to silicon. You still see it as silicon-germanium alloys in fast RF bipolar transistors and strained transistor channels.

The next family is the III-V compounds, like gallium arsenide, indium phosphide and gallium nitride. Their electrons are much faster than in silicon, so GaAs is used in RF amplifiers and cell-phone power amplifiers, and because they have direct bandgaps they emit light efficiently, which is what LEDs and laser diodes are made of. A blue LED is GaN, and red or infrared ones are GaAs-based.

The last group is wide-bandgap: silicon carbide and gallium nitride at about 3.2 to 3.4 eV. That wide gap gives about ten times higher breakdown field, so for the same voltage rating you can make the device thinner and lower resistance. They also tolerate higher temperatures and switch faster. That's why SiC MOSFETs are in EV traction inverters and GaN transistors are in compact fast chargers.

So my summary: silicon for everything where cost and integration win, SiGe or GaAs when speed matters, GaAs, InP and GaN when you need light, and SiC and GaN when you need high voltage and efficiency."
:::

::: terms
- **Bandgap ($E_g$)**: energy separation between valence and conduction bands, in eV.
- **Direct bandgap**: the band extrema line up in momentum, so electron-hole recombination emits a photon efficiently (GaAs, GaN).
- **Indirect bandgap**: recombination needs a phonon too, so light emission is inefficient (Si, Ge).
- **Wide-bandgap (WBG) semiconductor**: usually $E_g>2$ eV, e.g. SiC and GaN.
- **III-V compound**: a binary or ternary semiconductor combining Group III and Group V elements (GaAs, InP, GaN).
- **Heterojunction**: a junction between two different semiconductors, as in HBTs and HEMTs.
- **Critical (breakdown) field**: the electric field at which avalanche begins; sets the thickness and resistance needed for a given voltage rating.
- **Epitaxy**: growing a thin crystalline layer of controlled doping on a substrate, used for SiC, GaN and III-V devices.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "silicon, germanium, gallium arsenide" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives *why* silicon dominates (native oxide, wafer quality, cost, bandgap) instead of just naming it.
- Groups the materials by family and ties each to a property: GaAs and InP for speed and light, SiC and GaN for voltage and temperature.
- Uses numbers: 1.12 vs 0.66 vs 1.42 vs ~3.3 eV, and the roughly 10x higher breakdown field in wide-gap materials.
- Connects bandgap to something the interviewer cares about: LED colour, forward drop, leakage versus temperature, GaN/SiC switching.
- Mentions practical limits such as cost, defect density, p-type doping difficulty in GaN, and that GaN-on-silicon exists to cut cost.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Silicon is used because it conducts best."** It doesn't: GaAs and Ge have higher mobility. Silicon wins on oxide, cost and manufacturability.
- **"Germanium is obsolete."** It still appears in SiGe devices, in some infrared detectors and optics. It is not a mainstream discrete-device material.
- **Calling carbon (graphite) or copper semiconductors.** Diamond is a wide-gap semiconductor but not a practical one for circuits yet; the bandgap, not resistivity, defines a semiconductor.
- **Confusing GaN LED and GaN power transistor.** Same material, entirely different device structures: LEDs use quantum wells; power GaN uses HEMTs with a 2D electron gas.
- **Claiming all wide-gap parts are strictly better.** They cost more, have more crystal defects, and SiC MOSFET gate oxides and GaN HEMT threshold stability need care.
- **Saying the diode forward drop is a property of "the diode" and not the material.** It scales with bandgap: about 0.3 V for Ge or Schottky, 0.7 V for Si, 1.8-3+ V for LEDs, and 2.5+ V for SiC PN diodes.
:::

::: deep
## What makes a good semiconductor material?

A material is useful for devices if you can:

1. **Control its conductivity by doping** (N and P ideally), over several decades.
2. **Grow it as a near-perfect single crystal**, because grain boundaries and defects trap carriers.
3. **Make an insulator, an ohmic contact and a rectifying junction** on it reliably.
4. **Afford it** at the volume required.

Silicon is not the best at any one electrical property, but it is the only material that checks all four boxes with a native oxide (SiO₂) so good it can serve as a gate dielectric with interface trap density low enough for CMOS. That is the engineering reason it dominates.

## The bandgap controls so much

The bandgap $E_g$ shows up in everything:

- **Intrinsic carriers:** $n_i\propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/2kT}$. Wider gap means exponentially fewer thermally generated carriers, so lower leakage and a higher maximum operating temperature.
- **Diode forward voltage:** roughly the built-in potential, which scales with $E_g/q$.
- **Light emission:** photon energy $\approx E_g$, so wavelength $\lambda(\mu m)\approx1.24/E_g(eV)$. GaAs at 1.42 eV emits near 0.87 µm (infrared), and GaN at 3.4 eV is at about 365 nm (UV), which with InGaN alloys gives blue and green.
- **Breakdown field:** a wider gap needs more energy to impact-ionize, so $E_c$ is higher.

## Silicon: why the default

- The wafer technology is the most mature in history: 300 mm wafers with defect densities low enough for billion-transistor dies.
- Thermally grown SiO₂ gives a stable, low-defect insulator.
- Bandgap 1.12 eV is wide enough for low leakage at 85 °C and narrow enough to give moderate voltages.
- Its weaknesses: indirect gap (no efficient light emission), electron mobility far below GaAs, critical field only about 0.3 MV/cm which limits power device performance.

## Germanium and SiGe

Early transistors were germanium, but with $n_i$ roughly $2\times10^{13}$ cm⁻³ they leaked heavily when warm and Ge has no stable native oxide. Today, SiGe is grown epitaxially on silicon. In SiGe heterojunction bipolar transistors (HBTs) the narrower-gap base boosts gain while keeping the base thin and heavily doped, giving cutoff frequencies well into the hundreds of GHz. In CMOS, SiGe in PMOS source/drain regions applies compressive strain to raise hole mobility.

## III-V compounds: speed and light

GaAs has electron mobility about 6x that of silicon and a direct gap. That means fast transistors (MESFETs, HEMTs, HBTs) for microwave amplifiers and photonic devices (LEDs, laser diodes, solar cells). It also has a semi-insulating substrate, which reduces parasitic capacitance. The disadvantages: poor hole mobility, no good native oxide, brittle and expensive wafers, and low thermal conductivity (about 55 W/m·K versus 150 for silicon).

InP supports high-speed optical-fibre receivers and transmitters at 1.3 and 1.55 µm (using InGaAsP and InGaAs alloys).

## Wide-bandgap: SiC and GaN

The Baliga figure of merit for unipolar power switches, $\text{BFOM}=\varepsilon\mu E_c^3$, ranks materials by the theoretical $R_{on,sp}$ they can reach at a given blocking voltage. Because $E_c$ appears cubed, a roughly 10x higher critical field gives a very large advantage. To see this, take a 1000 V device with a drift region where $E_c$ sets the doping and thickness: silicon needs a drift layer on the order of 100 µm thick and light doping, while SiC needs roughly a tenth of that thickness and 100x higher doping, so its drift resistance is orders of magnitude smaller.

- **SiC** (mostly 4H polytype): excellent thermal conductivity (about 370 W/m·K), good for 650 V-3.3 kV MOSFETs and Schottky diodes. SiC has a native oxide (SiO₂), but the interface has more traps than on silicon, so channel mobility is lower and threshold stability needs care.
- **GaN**: typically grown on silicon, SiC or sapphire. Power GaN uses HEMTs, in which an AlGaN/GaN interface creates a 2D electron gas with a high density and mobility without any doping. They are normally on; enhancement-mode versions use a p-GaN gate or a cascode. GaN switches very fast (low $Q_g$, $Q_{oss}$), good for 100-650 V.

## Design relevance

The same bandgap logic explains device numbers you've probably seen:

| Device | Material | Typical forward drop | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ge point-contact diode | Ge | ~0.2-0.3 V | small gap |
| Schottky diode | metal-Si | ~0.2-0.4 V | metal-semiconductor barrier, lower than PN |
| 1N4148 / 1N4007 | Si | ~0.6-0.8 V | Si gap |
| Red / blue LED | AlGaInP / InGaN | ~1.8 V / ~3 V | photon energy ≈ gap |
| SiC Schottky diode | SiC | ~1.2-1.5 V | wide gap barrier |
| SiC PN body diode | SiC | ~2.5-3 V | gap about 3.2 eV |

(Forward drops are typical and current-dependent.) See [[q0078]].
:::

::: applications
- **Silicon**: microprocessors, memories, MOSFETs, op-amps, regulators; essentially every standard IC.
- **SiGe BiCMOS**: RF front-ends, 5G/mmWave transceivers and high-speed optical drivers where fast bipolar is combined with CMOS.
- **GaAs / GaAs-based**: power amplifiers in some handset front-ends, red and infrared LEDs, VCSELs in face-ID, and high-efficiency solar cells.
- **InP / InGaAs**: fibre-optic lasers and photodetectors at 1310/1550 nm.
- **GaN**: blue/white LEDs (InGaN), and GaN HEMT power stages in compact USB-C chargers and RF power amplifiers.
- **SiC**: MOSFETs and Schottky diodes in EV traction inverters, on-board chargers, solar inverters and industrial drives.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Room-temperature, bulk properties.** Bandgap shrinks with temperature (silicon about 1.17 eV at 0 K, 1.12 at 300 K) and mobility drops with doping and temperature; table values are typical and vary by source and crystal quality.
- **"Typically used" means commercially dominant.** Research materials (graphene, MoS₂, diamond, Ga₂O₃, organic semiconductors) exist but aren't what the question expects as the main answer. Mention them briefly if asked about the future.
- **SiC and GaN numbers** depend on polytype and orientation; I use 4H-SiC and wurtzite GaN.
- **Different answer for different interviewers**: a digital IC interviewer expects "silicon, with SiGe in advanced nodes", a power-electronics interviewer wants SiC and GaN detail, an optics interviewer wants direct-gap III-V materials.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Mobility, critical field and thermal conductivity figures in the table vary by 20-50% between textbooks and datasheets depending on doping, polytype and measurement method. They are order-of-magnitude comparisons, not design values.
:::

::: followups
### Why is silicon used instead of germanium, which has higher mobility?
Germanium's 0.66 eV gap gives an intrinsic density roughly $2\times10^{13}$ cm⁻³, about 2000x silicon's, so reverse leakage is far higher and gets worse quickly with temperature. It also lacks a stable native oxide (GeO₂ is water-soluble), which is critical for MOS devices and passivation. Silicon gives manageable leakage up to ~150 °C, a robust oxide, and abundant raw material.

### Why do LEDs use GaAs/GaN and not silicon?
Silicon has an indirect bandgap: an electron and hole recombining must also conserve momentum, which needs a phonon, so most recombinations are non-radiative (heat) rather than emitting a photon. Direct-gap materials like GaAs and GaN allow efficient photon emission, and alloying (AlGaInP, InGaN) tunes the gap to select the colour: $\lambda\approx1.24/E_g$ µm.

### Why does a SiC MOSFET have a higher breakdown voltage than a silicon one of the same size?
SiC's critical field is about ten times silicon's. With the same blocking voltage, the SiC drift layer can be about a tenth as thick and doped roughly 100 times heavier, so its specific on-resistance is orders of magnitude lower (the Baliga figure of merit scales as $E_c^3$). The consequence is smaller die area, lower capacitances and faster switching for the same voltage and resistance.

### What is the downside of wide-bandgap devices?
Cost and maturity: SiC wafers are small (150-200 mm), expensive and defect-prone. SiC MOSFETs have a high gate-oxide interface-trap density, so channel mobility is low and the threshold voltage can shift. GaN HEMTs have dynamic on-resistance (current collapse) and low gate-voltage margin (often only about 6 V maximum for enhancement-mode devices, so check the datasheet). Their fast edges (tens of V/ns) also require careful layout and gate drive to avoid ringing and EMI.

### Scenario: your design must operate in an engine bay at 200 °C ambient. Which material and why?
Silicon junction leakage roughly doubles every 10 °C, and at a 200+ °C junction temperature the intrinsic carrier density approaches the doping level for lightly doped regions, so the device loses control. A wide-gap material like SiC has negligible intrinsic carriers at that temperature, so it works. I'd also check packaging (solder and wire-bond limits), the gate-oxide reliability, and whether silicon-on-insulator (SOI), which reduces leakage, is sufficient for lower-power circuits.

### What would a 'perfect' semiconductor material look like for a power switch?
High critical field (high voltage with a thin drift region), high mobility (low resistance), high thermal conductivity (spreads heat), a high-quality native oxide or other gate insulator, both N and P doping, large cheap wafers and low defect density. Nothing has all of them; SiC has the best balance for 650 V+ today, GaN excels at lower voltage and high frequency, and silicon wins on cost and maturity.
:::

::: interpretations
"What materials are typically used for semiconductors?" can be read in two ways: **semiconductor host materials** (silicon, germanium, GaAs, SiC, GaN), which this page answers, or **dopant materials** (boron, phosphorus, arsenic), which is the subject of [[q0067]]. If the interviewer hints at "what do you add to silicon", answer with the dopants. A third reading, "what materials are used in chip fabrication" (silicon, SiO₂, copper, aluminium, tungsten, photoresist), can be mentioned briefly at the end.
:::
