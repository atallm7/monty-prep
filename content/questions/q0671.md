::: basics
### In plain words
A square wave is a repeating signal that flips between two levels and spends half of each cycle at each. It matters because the clock signals that keep digital chips in step, and many power and motor controls, are square or rectangular waves. A square wave also has a hidden side: it is made of many sine waves added together. The sharper its edges, the more high frequencies it needs.

### Picture it
Picture flipping a light switch up and down at a steady rhythm: on for a second, off for a second. That is the time view. Now picture building a sharp-cornered shape out of smooth round pebbles. A few pebbles give rounded corners, and many small pebbles make the corners sharp. The pebbles are sine waves, and sharp edges need many of them.

### Words you need
- **Duty cycle**: the fraction of each period the signal is high. A square wave has 50%.
- **Average (DC) value**: the mean level over one period.
- **RMS value**: the steady voltage that would heat a resistor by the same amount.
- **Harmonic**: a sine wave at a whole-number multiple of the signal's main frequency.
- **Rise time**: how long an edge takes to go from 10% to 90% of the swing.

### The simplest example
Take a 0 to 3.3 V clock at 100 MHz. The period is 10 ns, with 5 ns high. The average is 1.65 V. The RMS is 3.3 ÷ √2 ≈ 2.33 V, because you average the square of the voltage, not the voltage. Its sine content is at 100 MHz, 300 MHz, 500 MHz and so on, only odd multiples. The peak sizes are 2 × 3.3 ÷ (nπ), so about 2.1 V, 0.70 V and 0.42 V. Real edges are not instant. A 1 ns rise time still puts meaningful content out to roughly 0.35 ÷ 1 ns = 350 MHz.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers why even harmonics cancel, the size of the main sine wave, the overshoot at sharp edges, and how rise time drives ringing and radio interference (EMI).
:::

::: testing
Whether you can go beyond "it goes high, then low" and describe a square wave the way an engineer does: a **periodic two-level signal** with a specific amplitude, period, duty cycle (50% for a true square wave), DC average and RMS value, and a **spectrum of odd harmonics falling as 1/n**. The interviewer also wants to see whether you know the ideal one does not exist. Real edges take time, and that single fact drives most of signal integrity and EMI. Strong candidates move from the time-domain picture to the frequency-domain picture without being pushed, and mention what separates a square wave from a rectangular wave or pulse train.
:::

::: why
Every digital clock, every PWM output and every switching regulator node is a square or rectangular wave. Almost all the "mysterious" problems on a board (ringing, crosstalk, EMI failures, a scope showing 2 V overshoot) come from the fact that a square wave is secretly a very wide stack of sinusoids. Interviewers ask this as an opener because the answer shows whether you think in both domains. It is also the gateway to the rest of the Signals section: spectrum ([[q0673]]), rise time ([[q0676]]) and edge-rate trade-offs ([[q0677]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Periodic waveform**: repeats every period $T$; frequency $f_0 = 1/T$. See [[q0665]] for analog vs digital signals.
- **Duty cycle**: fraction of the period spent high, $D = t_{high}/T$. A *square* wave is the special case $D = 50\%$; other values are rectangular waves or pulse trains ([[q0672]]).
- **Average and RMS**: for a 0-to-$V$ wave, average $= DV$ and RMS $= V\sqrt{D}$.
- **Fourier series**: any periodic wave is a sum of harmonics at $nf_0$ ([[q0666]], [[q0667]]).
- **Rise/fall time**: real transitions take $t_r$, defined between 10% and 90% of the swing.
- **Gibbs phenomenon**: truncating the harmonic series leaves about 9% overshoot at each edge.
:::

::: answer
"A square wave is a periodic signal that sits at one of two voltage levels and flips between them, spending exactly half of each period at each level. So it has an amplitude, a period or frequency, and a 50% duty cycle. If the duty cycle isn't 50% I'd call it a rectangular wave or a pulse train.

The numbers I care about: for a wave swinging between 0 and V, the average is V/2 and the RMS is V over root two, about 0.707 V. If it's symmetric around zero, plus and minus A, the average is zero and the RMS is just A, because it's always at magnitude A.

The part I find most useful is the frequency view. A square wave contains only odd harmonics, the fundamental plus 3f, 5f, 7f and so on, with amplitudes falling as one over n. The fundamental of a plus-minus A wave has amplitude 4A over pi, about 1.27 A. An ideal square wave has instant edges, which means infinite harmonics. Real ones have a finite rise time, which limits the content and sets how much high-frequency energy actually shows up as ringing, crosstalk and EMI. So I think of a square wave as a time-domain shape with an edge-rate knob, and that knob is what I'd manage on a board."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** A square wave alternates between $V_{low}$ and $V_{high}$ with equal time at each level: $D = 50\%$, period $T$, frequency $f_0 = 1/T$, peak-to-peak amplitude $V_{pp} = V_{high} - V_{low}$.

**Time-domain numbers** (0 to $V$, $D = 0.5$): mean $= V/2$, RMS $= V/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707V$. For symmetric $\pm A$: mean $= 0$, RMS $= A$.

**Fourier series** for the symmetric $\pm A$ wave with zero phase at a rising zero crossing:
$$x(t) = \frac{4A}{\pi}\sum_{n=1,3,5,\dots}\frac{\sin(2\pi n f_0 t)}{n}$$
- Only **odd** harmonics exist. Half-wave symmetry, $x(t + T/2) = -x(t)$, kills every even term.
- Amplitude of harmonic $n$ is $4A/(n\pi)$. The 3rd is $-9.5$ dB below the fundamental, the 5th $-14$ dB, the 9th $-19$ dB.
- A 0-to-$V$ wave adds a DC term $V/2$, and its harmonic amplitudes are $2V/(n\pi)$.

**Power.** The fundamental carries 81.1% of the total power, harmonics up to the 3rd carry 90.1%, up to the 9th 96.0%. The tail never ends in the ideal case.

**Ideal vs real.** The ideal wave has zero rise time, so harmonics never stop (they fall only as $1/n$). A real wave has rise time $t_r$ (10 to 90%), which adds a second roll-off beyond a knee at roughly $0.35/t_r$ to $0.5/t_r$, steepening the decay to $1/n^2$ ([[q0675]], [[q0676]]).

**Gibbs.** If you truncate the series, a partial sum overshoots each edge by about 8.95% of the full step, and the overshoot stays at that height as you add terms; it just gets narrower.

> [!NOTE]
> A square wave is not the same as a clock. A clock can have any duty cycle. A PWM signal deliberately varies it. Say "50% duty" when you mean square.
:::

::: long
"A square wave is a periodic signal that alternates between two levels and spends equal time at each, so the duty cycle is 50%. The basic properties are amplitude, period, frequency and duty cycle. If the duty isn't 50% it's a rectangular wave or a pulse train, which is what PWM is.

For the time-domain numbers: take a wave going from 0 to V. The average is V over two. The RMS is V over root two because it spends half its time at V squared and half at zero. If it's symmetric around zero, plus and minus A, then the average is zero and the RMS is A.

In the frequency domain it's a fundamental plus odd harmonics only: 3f, 5f, 7f and so on. They fall as one over n, so the third harmonic is a third as big, about 9.5 dB down. The fundamental amplitude for a plus-minus A wave is 4A over pi, so a bit bigger than A itself. The even harmonics cancel because of half-wave symmetry: the second half of the cycle is the first half flipped.

There are two things I'd add. First, the ideal wave has instant edges, which needs infinite bandwidth. A real wave has a rise time, and I'd estimate its meaningful bandwidth as roughly 0.35 to 0.5 divided by the rise time. That's why a slow 10 MHz clock with 1 ns edges still radiates and rings like a few-hundred-megahertz signal. Second, if you build the wave out of a finite number of harmonics you see Gibbs ringing, about 9% overshoot at each edge. That's a nice reminder that sharp corners mean high frequency content. So practically I treat a square wave as a clean two-level shape plus an edge rate I have to manage."
:::

::: terms
- **Square wave**: periodic two-level waveform with 50% duty cycle and (ideally) instant transitions.
- **Duty cycle**: $D = t_{high}/T$; sets average value and harmonic content.
- **Fundamental**: the sinusoid at $f_0$; here $4A/\pi$ for a $\pm A$ wave.
- **Odd harmonics**: components at $3f_0, 5f_0, \dots$; the only ones a 50% square wave has.
- **Rise time $t_r$**: time for the edge to go from 10% to 90% of the swing.
- **RMS value**: $\sqrt{\text{mean of } x^2}$; $V/\sqrt2$ for a 0-to-$V$ square wave.
- **Gibbs phenomenon**: roughly 9% overshoot at a discontinuity when a Fourier series is truncated.
- **Half-wave symmetry**: $x(t+T/2) = -x(t)$ about the mean; the reason even harmonics vanish.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it goes high and low periodically, 50% duty cycle" and perhaps adds the frequency formula.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the numbers: average $V/2$, RMS $0.707V$ for 0-to-$V$, or $A$ for $\pm A$, and the $4A/\pi$ fundamental.
- States *why* only odd harmonics exist (half-wave symmetry) and that amplitudes go as $1/n$.
- Separates a square wave from a rectangular wave or pulse train, with the one-line effect on the spectrum (nulls at multiples of $f_0/D$).
- Says unprompted that the ideal edge is non-physical, and that rise time sets the effective bandwidth, linking to ringing, crosstalk and EMI.
- Mentions Gibbs overshoot as the frequency-domain view of a sharp edge.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Calling any two-level signal a square wave.** A 10% duty wave is a pulse train and has a different spectrum, including even harmonics. Say "rectangular" or "pulse train".
- **Saying the RMS of a 0-to-$V$ square wave is $V/2$.** That is the average. RMS is $V/\sqrt2$ because you average the *square*.
- **Believing a square wave has only a fundamental.** The 3rd harmonic is only 9.5 dB down. Filtering a square wave to a sine takes a real filter.
- **Thinking even harmonics exist.** At exact 50% duty they cancel. Duty-cycle error brings them back, which is a handy clock-quality check.
- **Treating real edges as vertical.** Every real edge has finite $t_r$, ringing and overshoot. That is where the engineering is.
- **Thinking adding more harmonics removes the overshoot.** Gibbs overshoot stays near 9% of the step; it only narrows.
:::

::: deep
## The shape in the time domain

Define the wave by three numbers: the levels, the period and the duty cycle. A 3.3 V logic clock at 100 MHz has $V_{low}\approx 0$, $V_{high}\approx 3.3$ V, $T = 10$ ns and $D \approx 50\%$, so $t_{high} = 5$ ns.

The averages follow from integrating over a period. For a 0-to-$V$ wave:
$$\bar{x} = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x\,dt = DV,\qquad x_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T x^2\,dt} = V\sqrt{D}$$
With $D = 0.5$ that gives $0.5V$ and $0.707V$. A 3.3 V square wave therefore has mean 1.65 V and RMS 2.33 V. The gap between the two is the AC power in the wave: $x_{rms}^2 = \bar{x}^2 + x_{ac,rms}^2$, so the AC part has RMS $V/2$ (= 1.65 V). That matters when you ask how much heat a square-wave-driven resistor produces: use RMS, not average.

## The same wave as a stack of sinusoids

The Fourier series ([[q0666]], [[q0667]]) says a periodic signal is a sum of sinusoids at multiples of $f_0$. For the symmetric $\pm A$ wave, the coefficient of harmonic $n$ is $\frac{2}{T}\int_0^T x(t)\sin(2\pi n f_0 t)\,dt$. Integrate: the wave is $+A$ for half the cycle and $-A$ for the other half, so
$$b_n = \frac{2A}{n\pi}\left(1-\cos n\pi\right) = \begin{cases}\dfrac{4A}{n\pi} & n\ \text{odd}\\[4pt] 0 & n\ \text{even}\end{cases}$$
Even terms vanish because $\cos n\pi = 1$ for even $n$. Physically, the two half-cycles are mirror images, so any component that does not flip sign over half a period (every even harmonic) cancels.

## Why harmonics fall as $1/n$

The amplitude pattern $1/n$ is a statement about the *jump*. A function with a discontinuity has Fourier coefficients that fall as $1/n$. A function that is continuous but has a corner (a triangle wave) falls as $1/n^2$. Smoother still falls faster. That is the core reason sharp edges are expensive: each extra order of smoothness buys you another factor of $1/n$ of high-frequency attenuation. A real trapezoid has a *continuous* edge, so it behaves like $1/n$ at low harmonics and bends to $1/n^2$ above the knee ([[q0676]]).

| Harmonic $n$ | Frequency (for $f_0 = 10$ MHz) | Amplitude vs fundamental | dB |

| 1 | 10 MHz | 1 | 0 |
| 3 | 30 MHz | 1/3 | -9.5 |
| 5 | 50 MHz | 1/5 | -14.0 |
| 7 | 70 MHz | 1/7 | -16.9 |
| 9 | 90 MHz | 1/9 | -19.1 |

## Real edges

Nothing physical can change voltage instantly: the driver has finite output impedance, the line has capacitance, and the package has inductance. Typical logic edges are 100 ps to a few ns. A first-order RC edge has $t_r = 2.2RC$ and a corresponding bandwidth $f_{3dB} = 0.35/t_r$.

Once an edge has time $t_r$, you can ask which harmonics matter. The harmonic amplitudes stay near $1/n$ until $f \approx 1/(\pi t_r)$ and then fall faster. For 10 MHz with $t_r = 1$ ns, that knee is around 300 MHz, harmonic 30. So a 10 MHz clock can still be a 300 MHz problem. The width of the pulse then tells you where the spectrum lobes sit ([[q0673]]).

## Square vs rectangular vs pulse train

- **Square**: $D = 50\%$; odd harmonics only.
- **Rectangular**: any duty; harmonics at all multiples except those that are multiples of $1/D$ (nulls), e.g. at $D = 25\%$ every 4th harmonic is missing.
- **Pulse train**: informally a narrow rectangular wave (small $D$); a broad spectrum, with many harmonics of nearly equal height.
- **PWM**: a rectangular wave whose duty cycle carries information ([[q0672]]).

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If the interviewer asks "what are the properties of a square wave", offer a quick list (amplitude, period, duty, average, RMS, odd-harmonic spectrum, finite rise time in practice) and then pick the spectrum to expand on. That's the point where candidates usually separate.

## Practical checks

- On a scope, set the FFT on a 50% clock: you should see odd harmonics clearly and even ones 30 to 40 dB lower. Strong even harmonics mean the duty cycle is off.
- A square wave into a low-pass filter with cut-off between $f_0$ and $3f_0$ comes out as a sine wave of amplitude $\approx 4A/\pi$ times the filter gain.
- When you need a clean sine from a logic clock, remember the 3rd harmonic is only 9.5 dB down and sits close to the fundamental. A 4th-order Butterworth filter with $f_c = 1.5f_0$ gives only $1/\sqrt{1+2^8} \approx -24$ dB at $3f_0$, so you need real filter order or a higher ratio.
:::

::: applications
- **Digital clocks** (CPU, SPI, I2C SCL): square or near-square waves with controlled edges; duty-cycle error shows up as even harmonics and timing margin loss.
- **PWM drive** in motor controllers and LED dimmers: rectangular waves whose average sets the output ([[q0672]]).
- **Switch-node waveform** in a buck converter: a rectangular wave of amplitude $V_{in}$ with duty $D = V_{out}/V_{in}$, the main EMI source on the board.
- **Test signal for amplifiers and scope probes**: a square wave reveals bandwidth (edge rise time), droop (low-frequency response) and ringing (phase margin) in one picture. Scope probe compensation is adjusted with a square wave.
- **Frequency synthesis and mixing**: a square wave as an LO in a switching mixer multiplies by $\pm1$, and the odd-harmonic content gives well-known harmonic mixing responses.
:::

::: assumptions
- "Square" is taken to mean **exactly 50% duty**. With other duty cycles the even harmonics return and the amplitudes follow $|\sin(n\pi D)|$.
- **Ideal edges** for the series coefficients. Real edges add a second roll-off beyond the knee.
- Harmonic amplitudes are quoted **per sinusoid peak**. RMS values divide by $\sqrt2$.
- The $\pm A$ symmetric form assumes zero DC. Real logic waves have a DC offset equal to the mean.
- Phase: the series quoted uses a rising zero crossing at $t = 0$. Shifting the time origin changes phases, not amplitudes.

Valid alternative answers: some interviewers want just "50% duty, two levels, instant transitions", which is fine as a one-line definition. The spectral properties are the extra credit.
:::

::: followups
### What is the RMS value of a 0 to 5 V square wave?
$V_{rms} = V\sqrt{D} = 5\sqrt{0.5} = 3.54$ V. The mean is 2.5 V. For the AC part alone it is 2.5 V RMS, since $3.54^2 = 2.5^2 + 2.5^2$.

### Why are there no even harmonics?
Half-wave symmetry: $x(t+T/2) = -x(t)$ once the mean is removed. Even harmonics $\sin(2\pi\, 2k f_0 t)$ repeat every $T/2$ without a sign flip, so they cannot be part of a function that flips sign every $T/2$. They cancel exactly. If the duty cycle drifts from 50%, the symmetry breaks and even harmonics appear.

### What is the amplitude of the 5th harmonic of a 0 to 3.3 V, 100 MHz square wave?
Harmonic amplitude is $2V/(n\pi) = 2\cdot3.3/(5\pi) = 0.42$ V (peak), at 500 MHz. That is 14 dB below the fundamental ($2.1$ V peak). In practice the edge rate trims this: with a 1 ns linear edge the 5th harmonic is already about 4 dB lower, and with a 3 ns edge it falls to about 21% of the ideal value (-13.5 dB).

### A square wave is applied to an RC low-pass with $f_c = f_0$. What does the output look like?
The fundamental is attenuated 3 dB and phase-shifted 45 degrees, the 3rd harmonic is down by about 10 dB (a factor $1/\sqrt{10}$) with phase near -72 degrees. The output looks like a rounded triangle-ish wave with exponential charging segments. Time-domain view: each half period is an RC charge with $\tau = 1/(2\pi f_0)$, which is about 16% of the period, so the output swings most of the way to the rail but with visible curvature.

### What if the square wave's edges are slowed so $t_r = T/2$? What is the resulting waveform and spectrum?
With 50% duty and rise time $T/2$, the trapezoid becomes a triangle. The spectrum changes from $1/n$ to $1/n^2$ for odd harmonics, e.g. the 3rd harmonic is $1/9$ of the fundamental instead of $1/3$. Sharp edges cost high-frequency energy; slow edges give it back.
:::

Questions in this group ([[q0675]] to [[q0678]]) all build on the same observation: edges set bandwidth.
