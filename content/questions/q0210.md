::: testing
Whether you can propose **targeted fixes for specific body-diode loss mechanisms** and say what each one costs. The interviewer is looking for:

1. A mapping from problem to remedy: conduction time in dead time, $V_{SD}$ drop, stored charge $Q_{rr}$, snap-off ringing.
2. Real options: adaptive dead time, a parallel Schottky (and why its inductance matters), low-$Q_{rr}$ FET selection, edge-rate control or snubbers, and wide-bandgap devices.
3. Numbers showing which fix matters most and what trade-off it creates (for example slower edges versus switching loss).
4. Judgment about when not to bother.
:::

::: why
After [[q0209]] has told you what the body diode does to the design, this is the engineering response. In a 12 V, 500 kHz buck the body diode can cost 2% of the output power; at 48 V and 1 MHz it can be the dominant loss. Fixes range from free (controller dead-time setting) to expensive (different devices or package). Interviewers like this question because it has no single answer; a strong response ranks the options by cost and benefit.
:::

::: concepts
- **Conduction in dead time**: $V_{SD}It_df$ ([[q0208]]).
- **Reverse recovery**: $Q_{rr}V_{in}f$ and the snap-off spike ([[q0209]]).
- **Schottky diode**: majority-carrier diode with lower $V_f$ (0.3 to 0.5 V) and negligible $Q_{rr}$ ([[q0202]]).
- **Loop inductance**: $L\,di/dt$ determines whether a parallel diode actually takes over the current.
- **Adaptive dead time**: gate timing from sensed voltages instead of a fixed delay ([[q0207]]).
- **Soft vs hard switching**: techniques that avoid the problem by switching at zero voltage.
:::

::: answer
"I'd attack it in order of cheapness. First, minimize the time the body diode conducts: use adaptive or predictive dead time and a strong gate driver so the gap is just enough to avoid shoot-through, say 10 to 20 ns instead of 50. That cuts conduction loss directly and also cuts the stored charge, so it reduces recovery loss too.

Second, put a Schottky in parallel with the low-side FET. At about 0.4 V it conducts instead of the 0.75 V body diode, and it has almost no recovery charge. The catch is the loop inductance: the current has to commutate into the Schottky in a nanosecond or so, and a nanohenry of lead inductance costs a volt, so the body diode would conduct first. That's why it's done inside the package or on the die, as in monolithic Schottky-integrated FETs, not as a separate part a centimeter away.

Third, pick the FET for low $Q_{rr}$ and soft recovery, not just low $R_{DS(on)}$. A few nC saved at 12 V and 1 MHz can beat a milliohm of resistance.

Fourth, tame the spike: a gate or boot resistor on the high-side turn-on to slow $di/dt$, or an RC snubber, and tight layout. Those fix ringing and EMI but cost some efficiency.

And finally, device choices: GaN has no recovery charge but a higher reverse drop, so dead time must be very short."
:::

::: technical
**Baseline** (12 V, 2 A, 500 kHz, $V_{SD}=0.75$ V, 50 ns total dead time, $Q_{rr}=20$ nC): $P_{dt}=37.5$ mW, $P_{rr}=120$ mW, total 157.5 mW (2.4% of 6.6 W).

**1. Shorten the dead time.** $P_{dt}\propto t_d$; $Q_{rr}$ shrinks with the time the diode conducts (for times short compared with carrier lifetime). With the illustrative model $Q_{rr}(t_d)=Q_{max}(1-e^{-t_d/\tau})$, $Q_{max}=30$ nC, $\tau=30$ ns: at 15 ns, $P_{dt}=11$ mW and $P_{rr}=71$ mW. Total 82 mW: a 48% reduction.

**2. Parallel Schottky ($V_f=0.45$ V, $C_j$ charge about 1 nC).** At 50 ns: $22.5+6=28.5$ mW. At 15 ns: 12.8 mW. Condition: commutation $di/dt\approx I/t_{fall}=2\text{ A}/3\text{ ns}=0.67$ A/ns, so $L_{loop}\,di/dt<V_{SD}-V_f=0.3$ V requires $L_{loop}<0.45$ nH, which only a co-packaged or monolithic Schottky achieves.

**3. Choose the FET on $Q_{rr}$ as well.** Example at 12 V, 1 MHz, 10 A, $D=0.1$: FET A 4 mΩ, 60 nC; FET B 5 mΩ, 20 nC. Recovery: 0.72 W vs 0.24 W; conduction: 0.36 W vs 0.45 W. Net saving with B: 0.39 W.

**4. Soften the edge.** Gate or boot resistor on $Q_1$ turn-on lowers $di/dt$, reducing the snap-off spike and $Q_{rr}$-induced ringing; costs overlap loss $\tfrac12 V_{in}It\,f$. RC snubber: $R\approx\sqrt{L/C_{oss}}=2.6$ Ω (for 2 nH, 300 pF), $C_{snub}\approx3$ to $4\,C_{oss}$; loss $C_{snub}V^2f=72$ mW for 1 nF at 12 V, 500 kHz. It cuts ringing, not $Q_{rr}$.

**5. Remove or sidestep the diode.** GaN HEMTs: no $Q_{rr}$, but reverse conduction at $V_{SD}\approx V_{th}+|V_{GS,off}|\approx1.5$ to 3 V: 3 V, 2 A, 10 ns total, 500 kHz gives 30 mW. Soft-switching (triangular-current mode) achieves ZVS and eliminates recovery.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The recovery-charge model, $V_f$ and $V_{SD}$ values, and GaN reverse drop are illustrative and vendor-specific.
:::

::: short
"Attack each mechanism. Shorten the dead time with adaptive control so the diode conducts as little as possible, which cuts both conduction and stored charge. Add a Schottky in parallel, but only co-packaged or monolithic, since nanohenries of loop inductance stop it taking the current. Choose a FET with low $Q_{rr}$ and soft recovery. Slow the high-side turn-on or add a snubber and tighten the layout to tame the recovery spike. Or move to GaN, which has no recovery charge but needs very short dead time."
:::

::: long
"I'd go from cheapest to most expensive and match each fix to the loss it attacks.

The body diode loses power in two ways: conduction during the dead time, about the diode drop times current times dead time times frequency, and reverse recovery, the stored charge times $V_{in}$ times frequency. In a 12 V, 2 A, 500 kHz converter with 20 nC and 50 ns dead time, that's about 40 milliwatts plus 120 milliwatts.

Step one is dead time. If the controller has adaptive or predictive dead time, use it and tune it down to 10 to 20 ns. That cuts the conduction term proportionally, and the diode has less time to build up charge, so the recovery term drops too. In my estimate that roughly halves the total. It's free, but I have to validate at hot temperature and light load for shoot-through margin.

Step two is a Schottky in parallel. A Schottky drops 0.4 V instead of 0.75 and has almost no recovery charge, so it removes most of both terms. The catch is inductance. The current has to move into the Schottky in a nanosecond or so, and $L\,di/dt$ across even a nanohenry or two is more than the 0.3 V advantage, so the body diode conducts anyway. The Schottky works when it's inside the package or on the same die, as in monolithic Schottky-integrated FETs. A separate part on the board mostly helps only slow converters.

Step three is device selection. Compare $R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{rr}$ and soft recovery, not just on-resistance. At high $V_{in}$ and frequency the recovery term can outweigh a milliohm of conduction loss.

Step four is the edge. A gate or boot resistor slows the high-side turn-on, lowering the snap-off spike and ringing at the price of some overlap loss, and an RC snubber damps the ring.

Finally GaN: no recovery charge at all but a 2 to 3 V reverse drop, so the dead time has to be a few nanoseconds."
:::

::: terms
- **Adaptive / predictive dead time**: gap timing from sensed gate/SW signals rather than a fixed delay.
- **Parallel (co-packaged) Schottky**: a Schottky diode connected across the synchronous FET.
- **Monolithic Schottky-integrated MOSFET**: a power FET with a Schottky built into the same die.
- **Soft recovery**: reverse current that decays gradually, giving less ringing.
- **Snubber**: RC network that damps SW ringing.
- **$R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{rr}$ FOM**: figure of merit for synchronous FETs.
- **ZVS (zero-voltage switching)**: turning on when the device voltage is already near zero.
- **Reverse conduction (GaN)**: channel conduction in the third quadrant with a higher drop.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Add a Schottky in parallel and reduce dead time."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Maps each remedy to a mechanism and quantifies the gain on a common baseline (157 mW to 13 to 80 mW).
- Explains *why* a discrete parallel Schottky often fails: $L_{loop}\,di/dt\gg V_{SD}-V_f$, giving a limit near 0.45 nH.
- Notes the dead-time safeguard: adaptive control needs validation hot and at light load.
- Offers device-level and system-level options: low-$Q_{rr}$ FETs, monolithic Schottky, GaN, soft switching/TCM, lower $V_{in}$ or $f_{sw}$.
- States the trade-offs: slower edges versus switching loss, snubber loss $CV^2f$, GaN needing nanosecond dead time.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Just add a Schottky anywhere."** A distant Schottky doesn't take current during the fast commutation; the body diode conducts first.
- **Cutting dead time without checking shoot-through margin.** The cure is adaptive control, validated at temperature, not blindly shorter timers ([[q0207]]).
- **Believing a snubber removes recovery loss.** It damps ringing and overshoot, and adds its own $CV^2f$ loss.
- **Choosing the lowest-$R_{DS(on)}$ FET only.** At high $V_{in}$ and frequency, $Q_{rr}$ and $Q_{oss}$ can dominate.
- **Assuming GaN removes the problem.** It removes recovery charge but increases the reverse-conduction drop and demands very tight dead time.
:::

## Match remedies to mechanisms

| Mechanism | Formula | Baseline (12 V, 2 A, 500 kHz) | Attack by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conduction in dead time | $V_{SD}It_df$ | 37.5 mW | shorter $t_d$; lower $V_f$ (Schottky) |
| Reverse recovery | $Q_{rr}V_{in}f$ | 120 mW | shorter $t_d$; no minority carriers (Schottky, GaN); low-$Q_{rr}$ FET |
| Snap-off ringing / overshoot | $L\,di/dt$, $f_{ring}$ | spike to about 16 V | slower $di/dt$; snubber; layout |
| Negative SW voltage | $-V_{SD}$ | $-0.75$ V | Schottky clamp; short dead time |

## 1. Adaptive dead time

The dead time must cover turn-off delay and fall time of the device that is turning off, and temperature variation. A fixed value therefore carries maybe 2 to 3 times the minimum needed. Adaptive schemes sense:

- the **gate voltage** of the turning-off FET (enable the other when $V_{GS}<$ threshold), or
- the **SW node** (enable the low side when SW crosses a level near zero, enable the high side when the low-side gate falls),

and add a small fixed delay. Predictive schemes learn the delay from the previous cycle. A well-implemented scheme gets to 5 to 20 ns.

## 2. The parallel Schottky, and why inductance decides

When $Q_1$ turns off, the inductor current must commutate from the high-side channel into the low-side branch within the SW fall time $t_f\approx3$ ns. The commutating $di/dt\approx I/t_f=0.67$ A/ns. In a branch of loop inductance $L_x$ the voltage needed is $L_x\,di/dt$. The Schottky only wins if

$$L_x\frac{di}{dt}<V_{SD}-V_f=0.3\text{ V}\ \Rightarrow\ L_x<\frac{0.3\text{ V}}{0.67\text{ A/ns}}=0.45\text{ nH}.$$

The body diode sits on the same die as the channel with a few tenths of a nH. A discrete SO-8 or SOD-123 Schottky even a few millimetres away, with its own pad and vias, has 2 to 5 nH, so the body diode conducts first, the Schottky picks up only after the current ramps, and the benefit is small (it still helps in the *long* dead-time regime, where current has time to move). The solutions:

- **Co-packaged Schottky** inside the power-stage package (for example DrMOS-style modules).
- **Monolithic Schottky-integrated FET**: the Schottky is built into the die cells.
- **Very short, wide connection** if discrete.

At 50 ns the parallel Schottky model gives 22.5 mW conduction plus about 6 mW charge, 28.5 mW, versus 157.5 mW baseline.

## 3. Choose the FET on $Q_{rr}$ and recovery softness

For a 12 V to about 1.2 V converter at 10 A and 1 MHz ($D=0.1$):

| FET | $R_{DS(on)}$ | $Q_{rr}$ | Low-side conduction | Recovery $Q_{rr}V_{in}f$ | Sum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 4 mΩ | 60 nC | $0.9\times100\times0.004=0.36$ W | 0.72 W | 1.08 W |
| B | 5 mΩ | 20 nC | $0.9\times100\times0.005=0.45$ W | 0.24 W | 0.69 W |

FET B saves 0.39 W. The lesson: compare totals at your $V_{in}$, current and frequency. The datasheet's $Q_{rr}$ is measured at a specified $I_F$ and $di/dt$ (often much larger than your actual commutation), so treat it as a ranking, not an absolute.

Soft recovery matters too: a snappy diode (abrupt end of reverse current) generates higher $L\,di/dt$ spikes and EMI even with the same $Q_{rr}$.

## 4. Control the edge, or damp it

**Gate or boot resistor on $Q_1$ turn-on**: slows the SW rise so the diode recovery current rises and decays more gently. Costs overlap loss:

$$P_{sw}\approx\tfrac12 V_{in}I\,t_r f_{sw}$$

Doubling $t_r$ from 3 to 6 ns at 2 A and 12 V, 500 kHz adds 36 mW. A boot resistor (series with the bootstrap diode) slows only turn-on, not turn-off.

**RC snubber**: across SW to ground. Choose $R\approx\sqrt{L_{loop}/C_{oss}}$ (2.6 Ω for 2 nH, 300 pF) and $C_{snub}$ about 3 to 4 times $C_{oss}$ (about 1 nF). Loss $C_{snub}V_{in}^2f=1\text{ nF}\times144\times500\text{ kHz}=72$ mW. It damps the ringing but doesn't reduce $Q_{rr}$ loss; use it when EMI or overshoot is the issue ([[q0209]]).

**Layout**: input capacitor adjacent, minimal power loop, so $L_{loop}$ is 1 to 2 nH, not 10.

## 5. Sidestep the diode

- **GaN HEMTs**: no minority carriers, hence no $Q_{rr}$; the third-quadrant conduction is through the channel with $V_{SD}\approx V_{th}+|V_{GS,off}|$ (typically 1.5 to 3 V; check the datasheet). With 10 ns total dead time that is 30 mW, comparable to a silicon FET with 40 ns. So GaN designs need dead times of a few ns, and layout for fast, precise gate timing.
- **Soft-switching / ZVS operation**: e.g. triangular-current mode or quasi-resonant control, where the inductor current is allowed to reverse so that SW swings to the opposite rail resonantly before the next device turns on. The body diode of the *incoming* device conducts at zero voltage, so there is no hard recovery. Costs: variable frequency, higher peak and RMS currents.
- **Operate with less $V_{in}$ or $f_{sw}$** where the system allows: both $P_{dt}$ and $P_{rr}$ scale with $f_{sw}$, and $P_{rr}$ also with $V_{in}$.

| Option | Total dead-time loss (model) | Cost / catch |
|---|---|---|

| Schottky and 15 ns | 12.8 mW | both |
| Snubber only | 157.5 mW plus 72 mW | cleaner SW, lower efficiency |

The combined best is about 145 mW less than baseline, or 2.2 percentage points of efficiency on a 6.6 W output.

## When not to bother

At 3 V to 5 V input, 1 A and a few hundred kilohertz, the body diode costs under 1% of the output. Check the loss budget first: if conduction and gate losses dominate, buying a monolithic-Schottky FET is not worth it.
:::

::: applications
- **CPU/GPU multiphase VRMs**: DrMOS power stages with integrated Schottky or Schottky-like synchronous FETs and adaptive dead time at 500 kHz to 1 MHz.
- **High-$V_{in}$ (24 to 60 V) bucks**: low-$Q_{rr}$ FETs and snubbers; recovery loss scales with $V_{in}$.
- **GaN-based point-of-load and USB-PD converters**: nanosecond dead time to cut reverse-conduction loss.
- **Server and telecom bricks**: soft switching or TCM to remove hard recovery.
- **Automotive buck regulators**: EMI-driven edge-rate control (boot resistors, spread spectrum) alongside body-diode management.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Baseline** is a silicon synchronous buck at 12 V, 2 A, 500 kHz with 50 ns total dead time; the loss numbers come from the simple models in this page (including an assumed saturating $Q_{rr}(t_d)$).
- **Schottky** $V_f=0.45$ V and 1 nC junction charge; real values depend on current and temperature.
- **GaN reverse drop** of 3 V is at the high end of typical; devices with different gate-off levels show 1.5 to 3 V.
- **Commutation $di/dt$** is set by the SW fall time; slower edges relax the inductance limit proportionally.
- **Soft switching** changes other loss terms (higher RMS current); it is not free.
- For **low-current, low-frequency designs** the achievable gain is under 1% and the simple fix is a controller with decent dead-time management.
:::

::: followups
### Why doesn't a Schottky placed a few millimetres away from the FET help much?
The current has to switch into it in about a nanosecond. A few nH of path inductance requires $L\,di/dt\approx2\text{ nH}\times0.67\text{ A/ns}=1.3$ V, more than the 0.3 V advantage, so the body diode, with its lower inductance, conducts first. The Schottky only carries current once it has ramped up through that inductance, usually after several nanoseconds, so it only helps in long dead times.

### How does reducing dead time reduce $Q_{rr}$?
The stored charge in the diode builds up the longer it conducts (until saturating at the carrier lifetime). A shorter dead time means less charge stored by the time the high side turns on, so less must be removed. In the model, going from 50 ns to 15 ns cuts $Q_{rr}$ from about 24 nC to 12 nC.

### What does a boot resistor do and what does it cost?
It's a resistor in series with the bootstrap diode, which limits the current that charges the high-side gate drive's supply path and thereby the turn-on speed of $Q_1$. Slower turn-on lowers $di/dt$ and the snap-off spike and ringing. The cost is more overlap loss in $Q_1$ and a slower reaction time.

### Why is GaN not a free fix?
It has no $Q_{rr}$, but conducting backwards through the channel costs 1.5 to 3 V (vs 0.7 for silicon), so loss per nanosecond of dead time is 2 to 4 times worse. GaN works because the dead time can be shortened to a few nanoseconds with a fast, precise driver; if the controller can't do that, GaN loses its advantage. Gate-drive voltage limits also tighten.

### You can only change one thing on an existing board. What do you try first?
Dead time, if the controller exposes it: run efficiency versus dead time at the hottest corner and pick the point just on the long side of the shoot-through knee. It is free and typically captures the largest share of the potential gain. Next: a different FET if the layout allows.
:::
