::: testing
Whether you understand **why** a digital node needs a defined default state and can size the resistor from real constraints instead of saying "10k, always". The hidden sub-skills:

1. Floating inputs: a high-impedance CMOS input has no defined voltage, picks up noise, and can draw shoot-through current.
2. Open-drain/open-collector outputs need a pull-up because they can only pull low.
3. The two-sided sizing trade-off: small $R$ gives speed and noise immunity but wastes current and loads the driver; large $R$ saves power but is slow (RC) and sensitive to leakage.
4. Using real numbers: logic thresholds, leakage, sink current, bus capacitance ([[q0008]] for the divider logic when an input is also driven).
:::

::: why
Almost every board has dozens of pull-ups and pull-downs: reset and enable pins, chip selects, I²C buses, buttons, boot straps, open-drain interrupts, gate pull-downs for FETs. A missing or badly sized one causes the classic "works on the bench, flaky in the field" bugs: a floating enable pin that turns a supply on randomly, an I²C bus whose rise time is too slow at 400 kHz, a MOSFET that turns on at power-up because its gate floated. This is also a standard "design a small circuit" interview question.
:::

::: concepts
- **Digital input thresholds**: $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ define what counts as low/high; between them behavior is undefined.
- **Voltage divider and Thevenin** ([[q0008]]): a pull-up plus a switch or driver sets the node voltage; leakage current times $R$ is an error voltage.
- **Open-drain / open-collector output**: the driver can only sink; the resistor provides the high level.
- **RC time constant**: the pull-up resistance and the node capacitance set the rise time.
- **Power**: current flows through the resistor whenever the node is driven to the opposite level, $I=V/R$.
- **Standard values** ([[q0017]]): 4.7 kΩ, 10 kΩ and 100 kΩ are the typical choices.
:::

::: answer
"A pull-up or pull-down resistor ties a signal to the supply or to ground through a resistor, so that the line has a known default state when nothing is driving it. If a CMOS input is left floating, it's high impedance, so it picks up noise, can sit at a mid-level, and can oscillate or even draw extra current. A pull-up gives it a default high; a pull-down gives it a default low. When something does drive it, a switch, or a driver, that overrides the resistor, because the resistor is deliberately weak.

They're also needed for open-drain outputs, like I2C, which can only pull the line low. The resistor is what pulls it high again.

To spec one, I look at the two sides. The resistor has to be small enough that the node rises fast enough, which is the RC of the resistor and the node capacitance, and that leakage doesn't pull the level down, so leakage times R stays small compared to the noise margin. It has to be big enough that when the line is pulled low, the current V over R is acceptable for the driver and the power budget, so for I2C it can't exceed the sink rating, about 3 mA, which gives about a kilohm at 3.3 volts. A typical default is 10 kilohms for slow control signals, 4.7 kilohms for I2C at 100 or 400 kilohertz, and 100 kilohms or more for low-power or gate pull-downs."
:::

::: technical
**Why needed.** A CMOS input has $\sim$pA to nA leakage and tens of pF capacitance; with nothing driving it the node voltage is set by leakage/coupling, may sit between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$ and cause the input stage to conduct both transistors (extra supply current), and is vulnerable to noise.

**Pull-up sizing constraints** (supply $V_{DD}$, node capacitance $C$):

1. **Low-state current / sink capability:** $R\ge (V_{DD}-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$. For I²C: $V_{OL(max)}=0.4$ V at $I_{OL}=3$ mA, so at 3.3 V: $R_{min}=(3.3-0.4)/3\ \text{mA}=967\ \Omega$.
2. **Rise time:** the 30 %–70 % rise time of an RC is $0.8473\,RC$, so $R_{max}=t_r/(0.8473\,C_b)$. For I²C standard mode $t_r\le1000$ ns with $C_b=200$ pF: $R_{max}=5.9$ kΩ; fast mode (300 ns): 1.77 kΩ.
3. **Leakage and noise margin:** $I_{leak}R\ll V_{DD}-V_{IH}$. 1 µA across 100 kΩ is 0.1 V.
4. **Power:** $P=V_{DD}^2/R$ while held low. 10 kΩ at 3.3 V: 1.1 mW (330 µA). For battery sleep modes this can be significant; use 100 kΩ+ or internal pull-ups enabled only when needed.

**Pull-down sizing:** same rules mirrored. The gate of a MOSFET needs a pull-down (e.g. 10–100 kΩ) to keep it off while the driver is high-Z at power-up; the resistor forms a divider with any series gate resistor when driven.

**Internal pulls:** many MCUs have programmable internal pull-ups/pull-downs of roughly 20–100 kΩ, wide-tolerance and often weak; noise coupled onto a long trace or cable can overcome them. External resistors give controlled values.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Internal pull resistor values and leakage currents vary widely by device and temperature; use the specific datasheet's electrical characteristics.
:::

::: short
"A pull-up or pull-down gives a node a known default level when nothing is driving it, so a CMOS input doesn't float and pick up noise, and so open-drain outputs can go high. I size it between two limits: small enough for rise time (RC) and to beat leakage, big enough that the current when pulled low is acceptable, V over R, and within the driver's sink rating. Typical values are 10 k for control lines, 4.7 k for I2C, and 100 k for low-power or gate pull-downs."
:::

::: long
"Pull-ups and pull-downs exist because a digital input that nothing is driving is not at zero or at the supply, it's floating. A CMOS gate is basically a capacitor, so the voltage is set by leakage and noise coupling. It can sit at a mid-level where both transistors conduct, or toggle randomly. A pull-up resistor to Vdd gives the line a default high, a pull-down gives a default low, and I can still override it by driving the pin or closing a switch, because the resistor is much weaker than the driver.

The other main use is open-drain or open-collector outputs, like an I2C line, an interrupt output, or a reset line with several sources. The transistor can only pull low, so the resistor has to pull it high when everything lets go. That also allows wire-OR behavior with many devices sharing a line.

Choosing the value is a trade-off. If R is too big, the rise time is too slow: the time constant is R times the node capacitance, and the 30 to 70 percent rise time is 0.85 R C. With 200 picofarads of bus and 100 kilohertz I2C, that caps R at about 5.9 kilohms. Also leakage times R shifts the high level, so 1 microamp across 100 kilohms is 100 millivolts of lost margin. If R is too small, then when the line is pulled low, you get a current of V over R, which wastes power and can exceed the driver's sink capability. For I2C at 3.3 volts with 3 milliamps, that puts the minimum at about 970 ohms.

So the usual defaults are 10 kilohms for reset, enable and straps, 2.2 to 4.7 kilohms for I2C, and larger values like 100 kilohms in low-power designs or for gate pull-downs. I'd calculate, not guess, when the bus is fast, long, or battery-powered."
:::

::: terms
- **Floating input**: a high-impedance input with no defined voltage.
- **Pull-up / pull-down**: resistor to supply / ground that sets a default level.
- **Open-drain / open-collector**: an output stage that can only pull low; needs an external pull-up.
- **$V_{IL}$, $V_{IH}$**: input low and high thresholds.
- **$V_{OL}$, $I_{OL}$**: output low voltage and the sink current at which it is specified.
- **Wire-OR / wired-AND**: multiple open-drain drivers share a line; any one pulling low pulls it low.
- **Weak vs strong pull**: resistors are weak (high impedance) compared to drivers, so drivers override them.
- **Internal pull**: configurable resistors inside a microcontroller pin.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Pull-ups are optional if the pin has internal ones."** Internal pulls are weak and varied, and don't exist on all pins or during reset.
- **Too-low resistance.** 100 Ω on a push-pull signal wastes power and may exceed driver sink current when contending.
- **Too-high resistance on a fast bus.** 100 kΩ on a 400 kHz I²C line gives a rise time many times the spec.
- **Pulling to the wrong rail.** A 3.3 V device with a pull-up to a 5 V rail can be overvoltaged through its ESD diodes.
- **Using a pull-up where a pull-down is the safe default.** A power-enable pin that defaults high may power the system before the MCU boots; decide the safe state first.
- **Forgetting the pull-up when using an open-drain output.** The line never goes high.
:::

::: deep
## What "floating" really means

A CMOS input is the gate of a pair of transistors, a capacitor with an insulator in the way. No DC current flows into it, so no current path fixes the voltage. Leakage of a few nA and noise coupling at a few pF of stray capacitance can move the node. If the node sits between $V_{IL}$ and $V_{IH}$, both PMOS and NMOS of the input buffer partially conduct, drawing "shoot-through" current from the supply. In the worst case a floating line oscillates.

A pull-up or pull-down supplies a **DC path** to a rail. Its resistance has to be low enough to dominate the leakage and noise, and high enough that other drivers can overrule it comfortably.

## Open-drain outputs and I²C

An open-drain output has a MOSFET to ground and nothing to the supply. Pull-up resistor $R_p$ restores the high level. On I²C, each line (SDA, SCL) needs one pull-up (not one per device), and its value is bounded on both sides:

$$R_{p(min)}=\frac{V_{DD}-V_{OL(max)}}{I_{OL}},\qquad R_{p(max)}=\frac{t_r}{0.8473\,C_b}.$$

With $V_{DD}=3.3$ V, $V_{OL}=0.4$ V, $I_{OL}=3$ mA: $R_{p(min)}=967\ \Omega$. For standard mode ($t_r=1000$ ns) and $C_b=200$ pF: $R_{p(max)}=5.9$ kΩ. For fast mode ($t_r=300$ ns) at $C_b=100$ pF: 3.5 kΩ. So the window is roughly 1 kΩ–3.5 kΩ, and 2.2 kΩ is a common pick. The simulation checks it:

> Capacitance on a bus is the sum of pins, traces and connectors. 10 pF per device and ~1 pF per cm of trace is a rough guide. The I²C-bus specification (NXP UM10204) gives the limits; confirm against the current revision.

Other typical cases:

- **Reset / enable pins:** pull-up (or pull-down) chosen so the IC is in a safe state until the controller takes over.
- **Boot/strap pins:** the level at reset selects the boot mode; use a resistor to the default and override by a jumper or driver (the strap must be stronger than any internal pull).
- **Buttons:** pull-up plus switch to ground gives active-low; add 10–100 nF for debounce (RC = 10 kΩ × 100 nF = 1 ms) or a Schmitt input.
- **Unused inputs:** tie to a rail directly or through a resistor, never leave floating.
- **Open-drain interrupts / alert lines:** one shared pull-up to the logic rail of the receiver.

## Pull-ups, speed and RC

A pull-up charges the node's capacitance $C$ through $R$ while the discharge is through the (low-impedance) driver, so edges are asymmetric: a fast fall, a slower RC rise with $\tau=RC$. For $R=4.7$ kΩ and $C=100$ pF, the 30–70 % rise time is $0.8473\cdot4.7\ \text{k}\Omega\cdot100\ \text{pF}=398$ ns. That's why fast open-drain buses use smaller $R$, active pull-ups (current sources) or reduce $C$.

## Worked example: an open-drain alert line with six devices

Here is a sizing exercise in the shape an interviewer might pose. Six sensors share one active-low, open-drain ALERT line to an MCU on 3.3 V. The datasheets give an output-high leakage of up to 1 µA per device at high temperature (an assumed worst case; read the real datasheets), the MCU input threshold is $V_{IH} = 0.7\,V_{DD} = 2.31$ V, and the bus capacitance is about 60 pF.

**Upper bound on R (leakage).** When all outputs are off, the leakage of six devices, 6 µA, flows through the pull-up and drops $6\ \mu\text{A}\times R$. The line must stay above $V_{IH}$, which allows 0.99 V in total, but I want a noise margin, so I budget 0.1 V of drop: $R_{max} = 0.1\ \text{V}/6\ \mu\text{A} = 16.7$ kΩ. Even a 100 kΩ pull-up here would drop 0.6 V and still pass, but it would leave only 0.39 V of margin before the logic level fails.

**Lower bound on R (sink current).** If a device guarantees $V_{OL} \le 0.4$ V at 3 mA, then $R_{min} = (3.3 - 0.4)/3\ \text{mA} = 967\ \Omega$. In practice the output sits well below 0.4 V at a smaller current; with a 10 kΩ pull-up, a driver with 25 Ω on-resistance pulls the line to $3.3\times25/10025 = 8$ mV. So the lower bound is only about the driver rating and power.

**Speed.** With 16.7 kΩ and 60 pF the 30–70 % rise time is $0.8473\times16.7\ \text{k}\Omega\times60\ \text{pF} = 0.85\ \mu$s, which is irrelevant for an interrupt line the MCU handles in firmware. The same resistor on a 400 kHz I²C line would blow the 300 ns limit, so the answer depends on what the bus is for.

**Power.** Here the choice between 4.7 kΩ and 10 kΩ and 100 kΩ matters for a battery product. While the line is held low, a 10 kΩ pull-up draws $3.3/10\ \text{k}\Omega = 330\ \mu$A and dissipates 1.09 mW; a 100 kΩ pull-up draws 33 µA. If the line is asserted half the time, the 10 kΩ choice averages 165 µA, which can dwarf a microcontroller that sleeps at a few µA. That is the argument for the largest resistor that meets the speed and leakage limits, not the smallest.

I would pick 10 kΩ for this alert line, comfortably between 967 Ω and 16.7 kΩ, and note the assumptions: worst-case leakage at the highest temperature and the capacitance estimate.

## What a pull-up cannot do

It is tempting to think a stiffer pull-up fixes noise. Consider a neighboring trace with a 2 pF coupling capacitance switching 3.3 V in 2 ns while your line has 10 pF to ground. For a pulse this fast, the line is held by its own capacitance, not by the resistor, so the step is set by the capacitive divider: $3.3\times2/(2+10) = 0.55$ V no matter what $R$ is. What $R$ controls is how long the glitch lasts, with $\tau = R(C + C_c)$: 120 ns for 10 kΩ against 1.2 µs for 100 kΩ. A 0.55 V excursion leaves a 3.3 V logic line on the correct side of both thresholds (a dip from 3.3 V to 2.75 V is still above $V_{IH} = 2.31$ V, and a bump from 0 V to 0.55 V is below $V_{IL} \approx 0.99$ V), but add 0.5 V of ground bounce or a longer coupled edge and a clock-like or edge-triggered input will see a false transition. The right fix for those is layout (spacing, a guard trace or a ground reference) and a Schmitt input or small RC filter.

## Weak versus strong: internal pulls and overrides

Microcontroller internal pull-ups are typically in the range of a few tens of kΩ (20–50 kΩ is common, but vendor- and pin-dependent, and the value varies a lot over temperature and process). They are fine for a button, but on a bus they are too weak: with 45 kΩ and 50 pF the rise time would be $0.8473\times45\ \text{k}\Omega\times50\ \text{pF} = 1.9\ \mu$s, six times beyond the fast-mode limit. In parallel with a 4.7 kΩ external pull-up, the internal one changes the total by only about 10 % (4.26 kΩ), so you can leave it enabled without harm, but don't count on it.

For strap pins the question is which side wins at reset. If you add an external 10 kΩ pull-up and the chip has a 45 kΩ internal pull-down, the pin sits at $3.3\times45/55 = 2.7$ V, which is a valid high in 3.3 V logic, but only because the resistor ratio is adequate. If the internal pull were 10 kΩ too, the pin would sit at half the supply, in the forbidden zone. Always check the datasheet for the internal pull value, and note that driving the pin during reset needs a driver whose output resistance is far below the pull-up: a 50 Ω driver against 10 kΩ moves the node by only 16 mV off its rail, so the external driver has no trouble overriding.

## Partial power and backfeed

When two chips are powered from different rails and one is off, a pull-up to the live rail can push current into the dead chip's input through its ESD protection diode to its (collapsed) supply. With a 10 kΩ pull-up the current is about $(3.3 - 0.6)/10\ \text{k}\Omega = 270\ \mu$A, and 575 µA for 4.7 kΩ (the 0.6 V is a typical diode drop). That current can partly power the unpowered chip through its protection structures, causing odd half-alive behavior. Cures are a pull-up that comes from the same rail as the receiver, a bus switch or level translator with power-down isolation, or open-drain parts specified as "failsafe" or "power-off protected".

## Bench verification

1. **Power off, ohms mode.** Measure the node to the rail; the reading should be the resistor value in parallel with whatever else leaks. A reading that is much lower points to a damaged pin or a contaminated board.
2. **Rise time.** Use a scope with a low-capacitance (10x) probe. A 10 pF probe adds to the bus capacitance, so a measured rise slower than predicted may be partly the probe; check with the probe off by comparing the system's behavior.
3. **Low level.** Probe the line while a device holds it low and read $V_{OL}$; it should be below 0.4 V at the specified current, with margin to $V_{IL}$.
4. **Static current.** Hold the line low and measure the supply current in sleep mode with and without the pull-up fitted, to confirm the budget from the power estimate.

## Design checklist

1. Decide the **default (safe) state** at power-up and during reset.
2. Pick pull-up to the rail of the receiving logic, not a different voltage.
3. Compute $R_{min}$ (sink current, power) and $R_{max}$ (rise time, leakage, noise margin).
4. Pick a standard value (4.7 k, 10 k, 100 k) inside the window.
5. Check the idle current in low-power modes ($V/R$ times the duty cycle held low).
6. Check what happens when the system is partially powered (backfeeding through the pull-up).

::: followups
### What pull-up would you choose for a 3.3 V I²C bus at 400 kHz with 100 pF of bus capacitance?
Fast mode requires $t_r\le300$ ns, so $R_{max}=300\ \text{ns}/(0.8473\cdot100\ \text{pF})=3.54$ kΩ, and $R_{min}=967\ \Omega$ for 3 mA sink. Pick about 2.2 kΩ (about 1.5 mA when low, rise time ≈ 186 ns). With 4.7 kΩ the rise time would be 398 ns, too slow.

### Why is a pull-down often used on a MOSFET gate?
At power-up or reset the driver is high-impedance, so the gate floats and could turn the FET on from noise or leakage. A pull-down of 10–100 kΩ keeps it off. The value must be low enough to beat leakage but large enough not to load the gate driver.

### Can two devices on one open-drain line each have their own pull-up?
Yes, the pull-ups add in parallel: two 10 kΩ resistors give 5 kΩ. This reduces the effective $R_p$, speeding the rise but increasing the sink current, so check $R_{min}$. Usually you use one set of pull-ups for the whole bus.

### What if the pull-up is connected to 5 V but the MCU is 3.3 V?
Current flows through the MCU's input protection diode to the 3.3 V rail, raising it and risking damage unless the pin is 5 V tolerant (and then only for specific configurations). Use a pull-up to 3.3 V, or a level shifter with the 5 V device on the other side ([[q0008]] explains why a divider is not a clean fix).

### What if a pull-up on an unpowered subsystem back-feeds a powered one?
Current can flow from the powered rail through the resistor and the unpowered chip's ESD diode, partially powering it (phantom power) and causing odd behavior. Use isolators or switches, pull up to the rail that's always powered when the bus is active, or choose parts that have partial-power-down protection.
