::: basics
### In plain words
A microcontroller (MCU) cannot drive a BLDC motor directly. Its pins give only milliamps, and the motor needs amps at 12 V or more. So you place a power stage between them: gate drivers and six MOSFET switches. The MCU sends small timing signals; the power stage does the heavy lifting.

### Picture it
Think of the power steering in a car. Your hands turn the wheel with little force, but the hydraulic booster does the real work of moving the heavy wheels. The MCU is your hands, and the gate driver and MOSFETs are the booster. The analogy has one gap: the MCU also listens for feedback, such as rotor position and current, to know what the motor actually did.

### Words you need
- **MOSFET**: a transistor (a part a small signal can turn on and off) used as a fast, powerful switch.
- **Half-bridge**: two MOSFETs stacked between the supply and ground, with one motor phase (coil group) connected between them.
- **PWM**: switching on and off very fast, so the fraction of on-time sets the average voltage. See [[q0145]].
- **Gate driver**: a chip that delivers the burst of current needed to switch MOSFETs fast.
- **Dead-time**: a short pause so the two switches of a half-bridge are never both on.
- **Shunt resistor**: a tiny resistor used to measure current. See [[q0443]].

### The simplest example
Say a motor draws 3 A from a 12 V supply, and an MCU pin can supply about 20 mA (a typical figure). The motor needs 3 / 0.02 = **150 times** more current than the pin can give. The chain is: MCU makes six PWM signals, the gate driver boosts them, three half-bridges (six MOSFETs) switch the supply, and the motor turns. Hall sensors and a current shunt report back to the MCU.

That is the whole idea: the MCU commands, the power stage delivers. The rest of this page covers the right MCU timers, bootstrap supplies, current sensing, and a hardware fault path for overcurrent.
:::

Whether you know that a microcontroller **cannot drive a motor directly** and can name the complete chain: MCU → gate driver → power stage → motor, plus the feedback signals coming back. The interviewer is checking for:

1. The right components: three-phase bridge (six MOSFETs), gate driver, power supply for the logic, position/current/voltage sensing.
2. MCU peripheral knowledge: complementary PWM timers with dead-time insertion, ADC triggered by the timer, hardware fault input, capture/Hall interface.
3. Practical design points: high-side drive (bootstrap), shoot-through protection, decoupling, flyback and regeneration, ground and current-sense layout.
4. Whether you also know the shortcut: integrated BLDC driver ICs.
:::

::: why
This is the "how would you actually build it" question. Most motor problems in the lab are not algorithm problems; they are a missing dead-time, a gate driver that cannot supply the current, a bus that pumps up on braking, or a noisy current-sense line. An engineer who can list the chain and the failure points is someone you can hand a motor board to. It is also the most direct bridge between this section and the microcontroller topics in the rest of the list ([[q0145]] PWM, timers, ADC).
:::

::: concepts
- **GPIO limits**: an MCU pin sources a few mA at 3.3 V; a motor needs amps at tens of volts, so a power stage is mandatory. See [[q0363]].
- **Half-bridge / inverter**: three half-bridges drive three phases. See [[q0423]].
- **Gate driver**: provides the gate charge current and the level shift for high-side N-MOSFETs.
- **Complementary PWM with dead-time**: advanced MCU timers generate high/low gate signals with a programmable blanking time.
- **Current and voltage sensing**: shunt + amplifier into the ADC; divider for the bus.
- **Position feedback**: Hall inputs, back-EMF comparators or an encoder interface.
:::

The chain is: microcontroller, gate driver, three half-bridges made of six N-channel MOSFETs, then the motor. The MCU's job is to generate PWM signals. ::: technical
**Signal chain.**

$$\text{MCU timer PWM (3.3 V)} \rightarrow \text{gate driver (10 to 15 V)} \rightarrow \text{6 N-MOSFETs} \rightarrow \text{phases A, B, C}$$

**MCU requirements.**
- Advanced timer: 3 complementary outputs, dead-time generator (tens of ns to µs), break input that forces outputs inactive in hardware.
- ADC with simultaneous or timer-triggered sampling so currents are sampled at the PWM midpoint (when the low-side is on and the switching noise is low).
- Timer capture for Hall edges (speed and sector) or comparators for back-EMF.
- Enough CPU for the loop rate: six-step needs little, FOC at 20 kHz typically needs a Cortex-M4-class core with hardware multiply and floating point, or a dedicated motor-control MCU.

**Gate driver sizing.** Peak gate current $I_g \approx Q_g/t_{sw}$. A MOSFET with $Q_g = 40$ nC switching in 50 ns needs about 0.8 A of peak gate current, far beyond a GPIO. High-side drive: bootstrap capacitor $C_{boot} \gg Q_g/\Delta V$; with 40 nC and 0.5 V allowed droop, $C_{boot} \geq 80$ nF, usually 10 to 100× larger in practice (e.g. 1 µF). Bootstrap needs the low side to turn on regularly to recharge, so 100% duty on the high side is not possible without a charge pump.

**Dead-time.** Must exceed the driver delay mismatch and the gate turn-off time, typically 50 to 500 ns. Too long, and body-diode conduction adds loss and distortion; too short, and shoot-through.

**Current sensing.** Shunt $R_{sh}$ with an amplifier of gain $G$: $V_{ADC} = V_{ref}/2 + G\,I\,R_{sh}$ for bidirectional sensing. Example: 5 mΩ, 20 A full scale → 100 mV, with $G=16$ gives 1.6 V swing around mid-scale of a 3.3 V ADC. Power in the shunt: $I^2R = 2$ W at 20 A.

**Power and protection.** DC-link capacitor bank for ripple current, TVS or brake chopper for regeneration, UVLO, over-temperature sensing, reverse-battery protection if applicable.
:::

::: long
"The microcontroller can't drive the motor directly. A pin gives a few milliamps at 3.3 V, and a BLDC motor needs amps at 12 or 24 V and above, plus it's an inductive load that kicks back. So the chain is: MCU, gate driver, power stage, motor, and then feedback going the other way.

The power stage is a three-phase bridge: six N-channel MOSFETs, a high-side and low-side per phase. The MCU generates PWM for each phase. I'd pick an MCU with an advanced motor-control timer: three complementary pairs with programmable dead-time, so the high and low switch in a leg are never on together, and a break input that kills all outputs in hardware on a fault.

Those six logic-level signals go to a three-phase gate driver. It does two jobs. It supplies the amps of gate current to switch the MOSFETs fast, and it level-shifts the high-side gates above the bus, generally with bootstrap capacitors. I'd check the gate charge, so I get the switching speed I want without excessive ringing, and add small gate resistors.

For feedback, I'd read the Hall sensors on timer capture pins, or for sensorless, divide each phase down into an ADC or comparator. For the current loop and protection I'd use low-side shunts and amplifiers into the ADC, with the sampling triggered from the timer in the middle of the PWM period. A divider on the bus gives voltage for undervoltage and regeneration monitoring.

Supporting hardware: a bulk electrolytic plus ceramics on the bus, a buck or LDO for the logic and gate supply, an overcurrent comparator wired to the break input, and a thermistor.

And if I don't need to design the whole thing, there are integrated three-phase driver ICs and even single-chip BLDC controllers, where the MCU only sends a PWM or speed command."
:::

::: terms
- **Gate driver**: IC that converts logic-level inputs into high-current, level-shifted MOSFET gate drive.
- **Bootstrap**: diode-capacitor circuit that creates a floating supply for the high-side driver, refreshed when the low side turns on.
- **Dead-time**: forced delay between one switch turning off and its partner turning on.
- **Shoot-through**: both switches in a leg conducting, shorting the bus.
- **Break input**: a hardware pin on an advanced timer that disables PWM outputs immediately.
- **Shunt resistor**: low-value resistor used to convert current into a small voltage.
- **DC link**: the DC bus between the supply and the inverter, usually with a bulk capacitor.
- **Complementary PWM**: pair of outputs where the high-side is the inverse of the low-side (with dead-time).
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "MCU plus motor driver plus MOSFETs". An exceptional answer:

- Explains why: pin current (mA) vs motor current (A), and the need for level shifting for high-side N-FETs.
- Names MCU features by function (complementary PWM, dead-time, break input, ADC triggered from the timer) instead of a part number.
- Computes at least one number: gate current from $Q_g$, or shunt power and ADC swing.
- Brings up shoot-through, bootstrap limits (no 100% duty), regeneration bus pump-up, and noisy current sensing.
- Closes with the trade-off between discrete design and an integrated driver or pre-built ESC.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Connect the MCU pins to the MOSFET gates."** For a small logic-level N-FET on a single low-side it can work, but for a bridge, you need a driver for high-side level shifting and gate current.
- **Leaving out dead-time.** A software-generated PWM pair without hardware dead-time will shoot through the first time a timing glitch occurs.
- **Using a P-MOSFET high-side to avoid the bootstrap.** It works at low power but P-FETs have 2 to 3× the on-resistance for the same die area and are slower.
- **Sampling current at a random time.** Sampling at a switching edge reads noise; trigger the ADC at the PWM centre.
- **No hardware overcurrent protection.** Software at kHz rates is too slow to save a MOSFET at 10 µs of short circuit.
- **Forgetting the supply rail's regeneration.** Decelerating a spinning motor can push the bus above its rating if nothing absorbs the energy.
:::

::: deep
## Why the MCU cannot do it alone

An MCU GPIO sources maybe 4 to 25 mA. A motor drawing 10 A at 24 V is 240 W. Even forgetting power, there are three more reasons:

1. **Level.** A high-side N-MOSFET's source swings to the bus voltage whenever it conducts, so its gate has to go to $V_{bus} + V_{gs}$, higher than any supply the MCU has.
2. **Speed.** A MOSFET gate is a capacitor with $Q_g$ of tens of nC. Switching it in 50 ns means amps of peak current.
3. **Protection.** The motor generates inductive kick and back-EMF ([[q0340]], [[q0341]]) that would reach the MCU pin.

## MCU peripherals that matter

**Complementary PWM with dead-time.** A motor-control timer takes one duty value per phase and outputs a high-side and a low-side signal that are inversions of each other, each shortened by the dead-time. With 24 kHz PWM, the period is 41.7 µs; a 100 ns dead-time is 0.24% of the period, an acceptable loss in range, and long enough to cover typical gate-driver and MOSFET delay mismatches.

**Break / fault input.** The over-current comparator output feeds a pin that forces all PWM outputs inactive with no CPU involvement. Reaction time is tens of nanoseconds to a microsecond, versus tens of microseconds for an interrupt.

**ADC trigger.** In centre-aligned PWM, the timer's update or compare event triggers the ADC at the middle of the low-side-on window, where the shunt current equals the phase current and switching noise is quiet.

**Hall inputs.** Three digital inputs with edge interrupts, or the timer's Hall interface mode, which captures the time between edges to produce speed.

## Gate drive and bootstrap

Figure: Change the gate resistor and gate charge. The sim starts with a 25 Ω GPIO-like source resistance, which is exactly why a bare MCU pin is too weak: the "Peak gate current" readout is only about 0.3 A. Click "Gate-driver IC" (1.5 Ω source) and the peak current rises to roughly 1 A with $R_g$ = 10 Ω, and the edges get much faster. Then lower $R_g$ below the $R_{crit}$ in the "Gate loop ζ" readout and the gate starts to ring; larger $R_g$ slows the edges and raises switching loss. Peak gate current is roughly $V_{drive}/(R_{src}+R_g)$.

Bootstrap: a diode charges $C_{boot}$ from the 12 V supply while the low-side is on (phase node at ground). When the high-side turns on, the phase node rises and the capacitor, now floating on top of it, supplies the driver. The capacitor droop per turn-on is $\Delta V = Q_g/C_{boot}$; with 40 nC and 1 µF, that is 40 mV. Long high-side on-times with a leaky bootstrap can discharge it, so drivers have a maximum duty (e.g., 98%) or a charge pump option.

## Current sense and sizing

Take 20 A full-scale bidirectional, a 5 mΩ shunt: $V_{sh} = 100$ mV, power $I^2R = 2$ W (use a power shunt). With amplifier gain 16: swing is ±1.6 V around 1.65 V mid-supply on a 3.3 V ADC. A 12-bit ADC gives 0.8 mV/LSB at the pin, which is 0.8 mV / (16 × 5 mΩ) = 10 mA per LSB. Leave headroom for transients above full-scale.

## Layout and noise

Switching currents of tens of amps with 10 ns edges radiate. Keep the high-current loop (bus cap, high-side, low-side) as small as possible, with ceramic capacitors adjacent to the MOSFETs. Route the shunt sense lines as a differential Kelvin pair, away from the switching nodes, and put the analogue ground return at the shunt. See the grounding questions elsewhere in this list for the broader picture.

## Integrated options

1. **Three-phase driver + external MCU**: e.g., a driver IC that does gate drive, current-sense amps and protections, leaving PWM, ADC and algorithm to the MCU.
2. **Integrated driver with FETs**: smaller motors (a few amps) where the MOSFETs are inside the IC.
3. **BLDC controller IC with built-in commutation logic**: you give it Hall inputs, it handles six-step; the MCU only sends a speed PWM or analogue command.
4. **Complete ESC module**: the MCU speaks a protocol to it (see [[q0373]]).

> [!NOTE]
> Part families (for example TI DRV8xxx motor drivers, ST motor-control MCUs and drivers, Allegro and Infineon gate drivers) exist in each category, but check the current datasheets for specific features, since lineups change.
:::

::: applications
- **Hobby and drone controllers**: MCU + three-phase driver + 6 FETs on a small board.
- **Robot actuators**: FOC board with encoder, current sensing and CAN interface.
- **E-bike controllers**: MCU, driver, large FET array, Hall and throttle inputs.
- **Appliances**: sensorless MCU drive with a driver IC for fans and pumps.
- **Dev kits**: evaluation boards from MCU vendors combine all of the above.

::: assumptions
- Three-phase BLDC, N-channel inverter, MCU with an advanced timer. If the motor is tiny (fan), a single integrated driver with Hall inputs replaces all of this.
- Numbers (gate charge, shunt value, PWM frequency, dead-time) are examples, not universal values; use the actual MOSFET and driver datasheets.
- I assumed a battery-like supply that can absorb regenerated energy; a bench supply may not, and then you need a brake chopper or clamp.
- Sensorless needs back-EMF sensing hardware; with Halls you can skip it.
:::

::: followups
### Why not drive the MOSFET gates straight from the MCU?
For the high-side, the gate must exceed the bus by $V_{gs}$, which an MCU cannot produce. Even for the low-side, a GPIO supplies a few mA, so a 40 nC gate takes microseconds to charge, which means huge switching loss. A gate driver provides amps of gate current and level shifting.

### What dead-time would you choose?
Longer than the worst-case turn-off delay of the MOSFET and driver mismatch, typically 100 to 500 ns, then verify on a scope by looking at the phase node and the gate signals. At 24 kHz and 200 ns dead-time, the dead fraction is about 0.5% of the period.

### What do you do about regeneration when the motor decelerates?
Regenerated energy raises the bus voltage if the supply can't sink it. With a battery it flows back and the voltage rises a little. With a diode-fed supply it pumps the bus up. I'd monitor bus voltage, limit the deceleration rate or add a brake chopper (a MOSFET and resistor across the bus), and size the MOSFETs' voltage rating with margin.

### How do you read phase current while the PWM is switching?
Use low-side shunts and sample at the centre of the low-side-on window, where the current in the shunt equals the phase current and the edges are far away. Or use an in-line sensor. A single DC-link shunt works too, but reconstructing three currents from it needs careful timing.

### Scenario: the MOSFETs get hot and one dies at startup, but the logic is fine. What do you suspect?
Shoot-through is first: check dead-time and gate waveforms with a scope; the high and low gates must never overlap. Next, a missing overcurrent trip so stall current is unbounded, a bootstrap that droops and leaves the high-side half-on, or a gate loop ringing above $V_{gs,max}$. I would probe with a current probe on the bus at the first startup with a current-limited supply.
:::
