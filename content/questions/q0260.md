::: basics
### In plain words
Both the inductor and the output capacitor have a window of good values. Too small and the ripple is large, the current limit may trip, or the loop gets unstable. Too large and the converter responds slowly, starts up with a big surge, or the part gets big and costly. You pick values in the middle of the window.

### Picture it
Think of a bathtub as the capacitor. A tiny tub overflows and sloshes with every change. A huge tub takes forever to fill when you first turn on the tap. The inductor is like a heavy flywheel: too light and it swings wildly, too heavy and it is slow to change speed. The analogy fits ripple and start-up well, but it does not show the loop stability limit.

### Words you need
- **Ripple**: the small repeating variation in current or voltage.
- **Slew rate**: how fast the inductor current can change, $V/L$.
- **Load step**: a sudden jump in the load current.
- **Soft-start**: a slow ramp of the output voltage at power-up.
- **Effective capacitance**: the real capacitance after derating ([[q0258]]).

### The simplest example
Take $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V and $L=8$ µH. When the load steps up, the current can rise at $(12-3.3)/8\approx1.1$ A/µs. When it steps down, it falls at only $3.3/8\approx0.41$ A/µs. For a 1 A step that is about 0.9 µs up and 2.4 µs down, and the capacitor covers the gap. For start-up, a 100 µF capacitor charged to 3.3 V in 1 ms needs $100\,\mu F\times3.3/1\,ms=0.33$ A.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page lists each limit for the inductor and the capacitor, and which one usually wins.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can reason about a design window instead of a single "right" value. L and C each have a lower bound *and* an upper bound, set by different physical effects: ripple, current stress, DCM boundary, slope compensation, transient slew, inrush and loop stability. The interviewer wants a list of constraints for each end, with one or two quantified, and the insight that **the reasons for the minimum and the maximum are different mechanisms**, not "too big is bad and too small is bad".
:::

::: why
In practice the datasheet gives you a recommended range for L and a minimum and maximum $C_{OUT}$, and ignoring it gives a converter that oscillates, hiccups at start-up or can't survive a load step. Understanding *why* those limits exist lets you push them safely (for example using more capacitance for a big load step) and explain what you'd check when the range is violated. It also connects ripple, transient response, soft-start and stability into one coherent picture.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ripple current**: $\Delta I = V_{OUT}(1-D)/(Lf_{SW})$ falls with L ([[q0258]]).
- **CCM/DCM boundary**: CCM requires $I_{OUT} > \Delta I/2$ ([[q0229]]).
- **Slew limit**: inductor current changes at $(V_{IN}-V_{OUT})/L$ up and $V_{OUT}/L$ down, which bounds transient response ([[q0248]]).
- **Slope compensation**: peak-current-mode needs ramp slope greater than half the inductor down-slope $V_{OUT}/L$ ([[q0255]]).
- **Inrush**: $I = C\,dV/dt$ during soft-start; must fit under the current limit.
- **LC double pole**: $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ with the compensation designed around it ([[q0261]]).
- **Saturation and DCR**: bigger L in the same package means lower $I_{sat}$ or more DCR ([[q0259]]).
:::

::: answer
"For the inductor, the minimum comes from ripple and from the controller. If L is too small, ripple current gets large, so peak current hits the current limit, RMS current and core loss rise, output ripple grows, and at light load the converter drops into DCM. On peak-current-mode parts there's also a minimum from slope compensation, because the inductor down-slope $V_{OUT}/L$ must be below what the ramp can handle.

The maximum comes from transient response and the physical part. A big L slows the current slew, $V/L$, so on a load step the output capacitor has to carry the load for longer and the droop gets worse. It also pushes the LC corner down which squeezes loop bandwidth, and a bigger inductor at a given size has a lower saturation current or higher DCR.

For the capacitor, the minimum is set by ripple, by the load-transient droop, and by what the compensation needs for phase margin. The maximum is mostly start-up and loop. The charging current $C\,dV/dt$ during soft-start has to stay under the current limit or you trip into hiccup, large C slows the control loop, and the datasheet usually lists a max. So L and C each have a window, and I pick values in the middle."
:::

::: technical
**Inductor, lower bound ($L_{min}$)**

- Peak current: $I_{OUT(max)} + \Delta I/2 < I_{LIM}$, with $\Delta I = V_{OUT}(1-D_{min})/(Lf_{SW})$ at $V_{IN(max)}$.
- RMS and loss: $I_{RMS}^2 = I_{OUT}^2+\Delta I^2/12$; AC and core loss rise with ripple.
- Continuous mode at minimum load: $L > \dfrac{V_{OUT}(1-D)}{2 f_{SW} I_{OUT(min)}}$ if CCM is required (otherwise the converter enters DCM/PFM and the loop's plant changes, [[q0229]]).
- Peak-current-mode stability: compensating ramp slope $S_e > \tfrac12 S_{f}$, where $S_f = V_{OUT}/L$ is the falling slope; with a fixed internal ramp this gives $L > V_{OUT}/(2 S_e)$ ([[q0255]]).
- Current-sense signal-to-noise: very small ripple makes jitter; very large ripple pushes current sensing blanking issues.

**Inductor, upper bound ($L_{max}$)**

- Slew rate in a transient: step-up $di/dt = (V_{IN}-V_{OUT})/L$, step-down $V_{OUT}/L$. Droop/overshoot $\propto L\,\Delta I^2/C$.
- Loop: LC corner $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ and, in voltage mode, a lower $f_0$ forces a lower crossover.
- Physical: a larger L in a given package has more DCR or lower $I_{sat}$; cost and height.
- Light-load (ripple too low) is rarely a real issue, but low ripple weakens the current-mode ramp signal-to-noise.

**Capacitor, lower bound ($C_{min}$)**: ripple ($\Delta I/8f C$ + ESR term), load-step droop $C \gtrsim \Delta I/(2\pi f_c \Delta V)$ or the energy equations, and the manufacturer's minimum for loop stability, using *effective* capacitance.

**Capacitor, upper bound ($C_{max}$)**: start-up current $I_{inrush} = C\,V_{OUT}/t_{ss}$ plus load must be below $I_{LIM}$; short-circuit and pre-bias behaviour; loop crossover (in current mode, $f_c \propto 1/C$) too low means slow response; energy dumped at over-voltage faults; cost and size.
:::

::: short
"Minimum L is set by ripple, peak current against the current limit, staying in CCM at light load, and slope compensation on peak-current-mode parts. Maximum L is set by transient slew, since current can only change at $V/L$, by the LC corner limiting loop bandwidth, and by saturation and DCR in a real part. Minimum C comes from ripple, load-step droop and the compensation's required range. Maximum C comes from soft-start inrush against the current limit and from a slow loop."
:::

::: long
"I think of each component as having a window. For the inductor, the lower edge is about ripple. Ripple current is $V_{OUT}(1-D)/(L f)$, so a small L means a big ripple. That pushes the peak current into the IC's current limit, raises RMS and core loss, increases output ripple, and when the load drops below half the ripple the converter falls into DCM. On a peak-current-mode part there's an extra lower bound from slope compensation: the ramp inside the IC has a fixed slope, and the inductor's falling slope is $V_{OUT}/L$, so a very small L makes the loop prone to subharmonic oscillation.

The upper edge is the transient. Inductor current can only change at $(V_{IN}-V_{OUT})/L$ going up. In my 12 V to 3.3 V, 6.8 µH example that's 1.3 A per µs. If I made L 47 µH, I'd get 0.19 A/µs, so a 2.5 A load step would take 13 µs to deliver instead of 2 µs, and the output cap droops seven times more. A bigger L also drags the LC corner down, and the real part gets bigger and lossier.

For the capacitor, the minimum comes from the ripple, the load-step droop and the compensation. Typically the transient is the sizing case. The maximum is largely start-up: during soft-start the capacitor needs $C\,dV/dt$, which has to stay below the current limit minus the load, or the part trips into hiccup. A very large C also lowers the loop's crossover so response slows, and datasheets list a maximum.

So I pick L for about 30% ripple, check it against slew and slope-comp limits, pick C for the step, and check soft-start and stability."
:::

::: terms
- **CCM / DCM**: continuous vs discontinuous conduction; DCM when inductor current reaches zero each cycle.
- **Ripple ratio ($r$)**: $\Delta I_L / I_{OUT}$.
- **Slew rate ($di/dt$)**: $V/L$ across the inductor, limits how fast current can follow the load.
- **Slope compensation ($S_e$)**: artificial ramp added to the current signal to stabilize peak-current-mode above 50% duty and for sub-harmonic damping.
- **Soft start ($t_{ss}$)**: reference ramp time that limits inrush into $C_{OUT}$.
- **Inrush current**: $C\,dV/dt$ drawn to charge the output.
- **LC corner ($f_0$)**: $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, the double pole of the output filter.
- **Hiccup**: periodic restart after current-limit fault.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "L too small gives high ripple; too big is slow and large. C too small gives ripple; too big is expensive."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates the mechanisms at each bound: ripple/peak/DCM/slope-comp for $L_{min}$; slew, loop and saturation for $L_{max}$; ripple/droop/loop for $C_{min}$; inrush/loop for $C_{max}$.
- Quantifies at least two: for example $V/L$ slew and $C\,V/t_{ss}$ inrush.
- States the CCM/DCM criterion $L > V_{OUT}(1-D)/(2fI_{min})$ and the peak-current-mode condition $S_e > S_f/2$.
- Notes the datasheet's allowed $L$/$C$ ranges for internally compensated parts.
- Notes that **effective** C matters and that the window shifts with temperature and tolerance.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating "bigger is always safer" for L or C.** Big L slows transients and big C triggers hiccup at start-up.
- **Forgetting slope compensation** as a lower bound on L for peak-current-mode converters.
- **Sizing L only at nominal $V_{IN}$.** The ripple and slope limits are at maximum $V_{IN}$; the slew limit is at minimum $V_{IN}$ for load step-up.
- **Ignoring soft-start.** A 1 mF output cap with a 1 ms soft-start needs 3.3 A of inrush at 3.3 V.
- **Assuming the DCM boundary doesn't matter.** In DCM the plant changes and a loop designed for CCM can behave differently ([[q0229]]).
- **Using nominal capacitance** rather than effective capacitance over bias, temperature and tolerance.
:::

::: deep
## Setting up: the design window

Everything here follows from three first-order facts about the inductor and one about the capacitor:

1. $v_L = L\,di/dt$, so ripple $\propto 1/L$ and slew rate $\propto 1/L$.
2. Energy in the inductor is $\tfrac12LI^2$; the capacitor supplies the difference between load and inductor current.
3. Capacitor current is $C\,dV/dt$.
4. The output LC filter has a resonant double pole at $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$.

Running example: $V_{IN} = 12$ V (range 9 to 18 V), $V_{OUT} = 3.3$ V, $I_{OUT} = 0.3$ to 3 A, 500 kHz, $I_{LIM}\approx 4.5$ A.

## Lower bound on L

**1. Peak current.** At 18 V, $\Delta I = 3.3\times0.817/(500\text{k}\,L)$. Setting $3 + \Delta I/2 < 4.5$ A leaves $\Delta I < 3$ A, so $L > 1.8$ µH just to stay under the limit, but you'd also want $I_{sat}$ margin, so the practical minimum is higher.

**2. CCM at light load.** For $I_{min} = 0.3$ A at 18 V:
$$L > \frac{V_{OUT}(1-D)}{2 f I_{min}} = \frac{3.3\times0.817}{2\times500\text{k}\times0.3} = 9.0\ \mu\text{H}$$
So a 6.8 µH inductor operates in DCM below $\Delta I/2 = 0.40$ A at 18 V. That might be fine (PFM mode saves power) but changes the loop plant, so the compensation must work in both.

**3. Slope compensation.** In peak-current mode with a fixed internal compensation ramp of slope $S_e$, subharmonic stability needs the ramp slope to exceed half the inductor's falling slope, $S_e > \tfrac12 V_{OUT}/L$ (the classic condition that makes the loop stable at every duty cycle), so $L > V_{OUT}/(2S_e)$. As an illustration with an effective ramp of 0.5 A/µs: $L > 3.3/(2\times0.5\times10^6) = 3.3$ µH. Real parts state a minimum L or a recommended L range for this reason. See [[q0255]] and the `subharmonic` sim linked there.

**4. Loss and noise.** RMS current and core loss rise with ripple ([[q0259]]). On the other extreme, ripple that is too small (large L) leaves the current-mode controller with little ramp signal, so jitter can appear.

## Upper bound on L

**Slew-limited response.** On a load step-up of $\Delta I = 2.5$ A the inductor current can only climb at $(V_{IN}-V_{OUT})/L$. The capacitor makes up the shortfall until the inductor catches up. With the controller saturated at maximum duty, the output droop is about
$$\Delta V \approx \frac{L\,\Delta I^2}{2\,C\,(V_{IN}-V_{OUT})}$$
(which is the energy-based estimate used in [[q0258]]). With $C = 55$ µF and $V_{IN} = 12$ V:

| L | slew up | time to supply 2.5 A | droop estimate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3.3 µH | 2.6 A/µs | 0.95 µs | 21 mV |
| 6.8 µH | 1.28 A/µs | 1.95 µs | 44 mV |
| 15 µH | 0.58 A/µs | 4.3 µs | 98 mV |
| 47 µH | 0.19 A/µs | 13.5 µs | 307 mV |

Droop is proportional to L, so a 47 µH inductor would give a 307 mV droop, which is nearly 10% of the 3.3 V rail: exceeding a typical 5% budget. And that's *with* an ideal controller; the loop bandwidth is a separate limit.

Figure: The slew time increases linearly with L, from 1.95 µs at 6.8 µH to 13.5 µs at 47 µH. This is a hard physical limit that no control loop can beat: even an infinitely fast controller cannot move the inductor current faster than $V/L$.

**Loop.** The LC double pole at $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ is 8.2 kHz for 6.8 µH and 55 µF but 3.1 kHz for 47 µH. A voltage-mode loop must cross over above $f_0$ and below about $f_{SW}/5$; a lower $f_0$ isn't fatal, but combined with a larger L the plant's phase drops earlier and the usable crossover shrinks ([[q0261]]).

**Physical part.** In a fixed footprint, more inductance means more turns: higher DCR and lower $I_{sat}$; so the practical upper bound on L is often the largest part that still has an $I_{sat}$ above the current limit and an acceptable DCR.

## Lower bound on C

Three constraints (see [[q0258]]): ripple (~4 to 8 mV for 55 µF), load step droop (about 48 µF effective for a 165 mV, 50 kHz-crossover design), and the datasheet's minimum for a compensated loop. If effective C falls below the minimum, a current-mode loop's crossover (which scales as $1/C$ for a fixed compensation) rises, closer to $f_{SW}/2$ where sampling delay kills phase margin.

## Upper bound on C

**Inrush at soft-start.** With a linear ramp of $V_{OUT}$ over $t_{ss}$, the capacitor takes $I_C = C\,V_{OUT}/t_{ss}$. With $C = 470$ µF, 3.3 V and $t_{ss} = 2$ ms:
$$I_C = \frac{470\mu\times3.3}{2\text{ ms}} = 0.78\ \text{A}$$
The converter must deliver this plus the load current at that voltage, and the sum must stay under the current limit (use the **minimum** limit). If it doesn't, the part enters current limit/hiccup and never starts. Maximum C given a current budget $I_{budget}$: $C_{max} = I_{budget}\,t_{ss}/V_{OUT}$; for $I_{budget} = 2$ A and 2 ms, $C_{max} = 1.2$ mF.

**Loop.** In current mode the plant has a low-frequency pole at $1/(2\pi R_L C)$ and crossover $f_c \propto g_m/C$; a bigger C lowers $f_c$ and slows the transient *response* even though the *droop* is smaller. For voltage mode the double pole drops.

**Others.** Pre-biased outputs, OVP behaviour when a huge C holds charge, cost and board area, and datasheet limits (e.g. "maximum $C_{OUT}$ 470 µF" for compensation reasons) round out the list.

## Putting it together: a window summary

| Parameter | Lower bound from | Upper bound from |
|---|---|---|
| L | ripple/peak vs $I_{LIM}$, DCM boundary, slope comp, core/RMS loss | transient slew, LC corner/loop, size, DCR/$I_{sat}$ |
| C | ripple, load-step droop, loop stability window | soft-start inrush, slow loop, cost/area, OVP energy |

::: applications
- **Datasheet "recommended L and C range" tables** for internally compensated converters: now you know the reasons.
- **Large-C systems** (e.g. FPGA rails with 1 mF of bulk) where soft-start time must be extended.
- **Fast-transient CPU rails**: small L (hundreds of nH) with multiphase to maximize slew ([[q0243]]).
- **Light-load battery products**: deliberately small L with PFM, or larger L to stay in CCM for noise.
- **Automotive buck** where cold-crank minimum $V_{IN}$ makes step-up slew ($V_{IN}-V_{OUT}$) tiny, so $L_{max}$ tightens.
:::

::: assumptions
- Continuous-mode, fixed-frequency analysis; DCM and PFM give different relationships.
- Slope-compensation formulas assume the textbook current-mode model; real ICs hide the ramp slope in internal parameters and give you an $L$ range instead.
- The droop equation is the energy-balance estimate for a saturated-duty step; a well-compensated loop is usually close to it, but a slower loop will droop more.
- Numeric current limits and $t_{ss}$ are example values.
- Capacitance values are *effective* values; derating shifts the window.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a small inductor cause problems in peak-current-mode control?
The compensation ramp slope is fixed by the IC; the inductor's down-slope $V_{OUT}/L$ rises as L falls. If it exceeds twice the compensation ramp, the loop can't damp the cycle-to-cycle perturbation, so sub-harmonic oscillation appears at higher duty cycles (and damping is poor below that). Datasheets express this as a minimum L or a minimum $L\times f_{SW}$ for the given $V_{OUT}$.

### What limits the maximum output capacitance of a buck?
Start-up: the charging current $C\,V/t_{ss}$ plus load current must be less than the minimum current limit, or the controller will go into hiccup. Then there's loop behaviour: crossover frequency drops, slowing transient recovery, and compensation designed for a smaller C may produce too little phase margin. Many datasheets list a maximum for these reasons; to use more C, extend the soft-start time.

### Does a larger inductor always reduce output ripple?
Inductor ripple current falls, so the capacitive and ESR parts of output ripple fall as $1/L$, but at some point other contributions dominate: ESL spikes at the switching edges and coupled noise, which don't depend on L. Beyond that you only pay in slew rate and size.

### Scenario: your converter starts fine at 20 °C but never starts when cold with a big electrolytic on the output. Why?
The root cause is usually a start-up current budget that was already marginal: charge current $C\,V/t_{ss}$ plus the load at start-up sits just under the minimum current limit at room temperature. Cold pushes it over. The load may draw more when cold (motors, heaters, inrush of downstream rails), the IC's current-limit threshold drifts with temperature, and a cold electrolytic's ESR rises sharply, which disturbs a loop tuned for its room-temperature ESR. The part then hits current limit and hiccups instead of finishing soft-start. Fix by extending $t_{ss}$, reducing C or using a polymer/ceramic bulk part, or delaying the load until power-good.

### How would you pick L if the load needs very fast transients?
Go smaller L, accept more ripple, and compensate by increasing $f_{SW}$ or using multiphase to cancel ripple ([[q0236]]). Slew rate $V/L$ is the fundamental limit. Then raise $C_{OUT}$ or add a feed-forward path, and check the minimum-L limits (peak current, slope comp, $I_{rms}$).
:::
