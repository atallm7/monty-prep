::: basics
### In plain words
A converter's output power equals its input power times its efficiency. Power is voltage times current, so $V_{out}I_{out} = \eta\,V_{in}I_{in}$. If you know three of the four numbers (and the efficiency), you can solve for the missing one. A step-down switching converter takes less current than it delivers, and a step-up one takes more.

### Picture it
Picture a seesaw. Voltage on one side, current on the other. If one side goes up, the other has to come down to keep the balance. A switching converter trades voltage for current like this, losing only a little to friction. The seesaw breaks down for a linear regulator, which does not trade: its input current is about the same as its output current.

### Words you need
- **Power**: $P = VI$. See [[q0006]].
- **Efficiency** ($\eta$): the fraction of input power that reaches the output.
- **Step-down / step-up**: output voltage lower or higher than input.
- **Ideal converter**: a 100% efficient one, useful as a first estimate.
- **LDO**: a linear regulator. See [[q0147]].

### The simplest example
A buck takes 12 V at 0.5 A and makes 3.3 V. Input power is 6 W. If ideal, the output current is $6/3.3 \approx 1.82$ A. At 90% efficiency, the output power is 5.4 W, so the output current is $5.4/3.3 \approx 1.64$ A. A quick check: it is a step-down, so output current is larger than input current, as it should be. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds all four rearrangements and the LDO caveat.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can do the single most common power-supply calculation quickly and *sensibly*: given three of $V_{in}$, $I_{in}$, $V_{out}$, $I_{out}$, find the fourth using $P_{in}=P_{out}/\eta$. The interviewer is also checking that you ask or state the **efficiency assumption** (ideal 100% unless told), that you check direction (buck: $I_{in}<I_{out}$; boost: $I_{in}>I_{out}$; LDO: $I_{in}\approx I_{out}$), and that you notice when the numbers imply an impossible efficiency above 100%.
:::

::: why
This is bread-and-butter arithmetic for sizing a battery, a fuse, wire gauge, an adapter, an inductor or the input-current limit of a converter. It also doubles as a sanity check on lab measurements: if your measured $V_{in}I_{in}$ is less than $V_{out}I_{out}$ you have a measurement error or a missing source. The question is a direct application of conservation of energy ([[q0154]]) and the efficiency definition ([[q0162]], [[q0163]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Power**: $P=VI$. See [[q0006]].
- **Conservation of energy and efficiency**: $P_{out}=\eta P_{in}$. See [[q0154]] and [[q0162]].
- **Buck/boost/LDO current relationships**: a buck lowers voltage and raises current, a boost the reverse, an LDO passes the same current. See [[q0147]] and [[q0157]].
- **Ideal vs real**: ideal means $\eta=100\%$; real converters are typically 80-95% (switching) or $V_{out}/V_{in}$ (linear).
- **Quiescent current**: a small extra input current at no load. See [[q0165]].
- **Sanity check**: $\eta$ must be between 0 and 1.
:::

::: answer
"The relationship is conservation of power. Input power is $V_{in}I_{in}$, output power is $V_{out}I_{out}$, and they're related by the efficiency: $V_{out}I_{out}=\eta\,V_{in}I_{in}$. So whichever one is missing, I solve for it. For example, the output current is $\eta V_{in}I_{in}/V_{out}$, the input current is $V_{out}I_{out}/(\eta V_{in})$, the output voltage is $\eta V_{in}I_{in}/I_{out}$ and the input voltage is $V_{out}I_{out}/(\eta I_{in})$.

If the efficiency isn't given, I'd say so: I'll assume an ideal 100% first, and then mention that a real switching converter would be around 85 to 95 percent, so the input current would be about 10 percent higher.

Quick example: 12 volts in at half an amp, 3.3 volts out. Ideal output current is $12\times0.5/3.3=1.82$ amps; with 90% efficiency it's 1.64 amps. And I'd sanity check: it's a step-down, so output current is bigger than input current, which it is.

One caveat is that it depends on the type of converter. A linear regulator doesn't trade voltage for current. Its input current is essentially its output current, so $I_{in}\approx I_{out}$ and the efficiency is $V_{out}/V_{in}$. So I'd make sure I know whether it's an LDO or a switcher."
:::

::: technical
**Core relation.** Averaged over a cycle at steady state:
$$V_{out}I_{out}=\eta\,V_{in}I_{in}$$
Solving for each unknown:

| Unknown | Formula (ideal: $\eta=1$) |
|---|---|
| $I_{out}$ | $\dfrac{\eta V_{in}I_{in}}{V_{out}}$ |
| $I_{in}$ | $\dfrac{V_{out}I_{out}}{\eta V_{in}}$ |
| $V_{out}$ | $\dfrac{\eta V_{in}I_{in}}{I_{out}}$ |
| $V_{in}$ | $\dfrac{V_{out}I_{out}}{\eta I_{in}}$ |
| $\eta$ (if all four given) | $\dfrac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}I_{in}}$ |

**Worked examples** (computed):
1. *Buck, find $I_{out}$.* $V_{in}=12$ V, $I_{in}=0.5$ A, $V_{out}=3.3$ V. Ideal: $12\times0.5/3.3=1.82$ A. At 90%: 1.64 A.
2. *Boost, find $V_{out}$.* $V_{in}=3.7$ V, $I_{in}=1.5$ A, $I_{out}=1$ A. Ideal: 5.55 V. At 90%: $0.9\times3.7\times1.5/1=4.995\approx5.0$ V.
3. *Find $V_{in}$.* $V_{out}=5$ V, $I_{out}=2$ A, $I_{in}=0.5$ A, $\eta=92\%$: $V_{in}=10/(0.92\times0.5)=21.7$ V.
4. *Find $\eta$ from all four.* $V_{in}=5$ V, $I_{in}=1$ A, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I_{out}=1.4$ A: $\eta=4.62/5=92.4\%$ (loss 0.38 W). This is probably a switching converter.
5. *Linear regulator.* $V_{in}=5$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I_{out}=0.2$ A: $I_{in}\approx0.2$ A (plus $I_q$), $\eta\approx0.66$.

**Direction checks.** Step-down switching: $I_{in}<I_{out}$ (by $V_{out}/(\eta V_{in})$). Step-up: $I_{in}>I_{out}$. LDO: $I_{in}\approx I_{out}$. Isolated/transformer ideal: $V_1I_1=V_2I_2$, so $I_2/I_1=N_1/N_2$ (turns ratio).

**Efficiency sanity.** If your numbers give $\eta>100\%$, the data are inconsistent (measurement error, wrong units, missing source). If $\eta$ is very low for a switcher (e.g. 40%), either it is not a switcher or there is a fault.

**AC input.** Use $P_{in}=V_{rms}I_{rms}\cdot PF$ (e.g. 230 V × 0.3 A × 0.6 = 41.4 W).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> If the efficiency is not provided, the "right" assumption depends on the converter type and operating point; 90% is only a typical figure for a switching converter near mid-load. State the assumption explicitly.
:::

::: short
"Output power equals efficiency times input power: $V_{out}I_{out}=\eta V_{in}I_{in}$. Solve for the missing one. With 12 V in at 0.5 A and 3.3 V out, the ideal output current is $12\times0.5/3.3=1.82$ A, or 1.64 A at 90% efficiency. I'd state the efficiency assumption, check direction (buck raises current, boost lowers it, LDO keeps it the same) and flag any result that implies more than 100% efficiency."
:::

::: long
"This is just conservation of power at the converter terminals. Input power is Vin times Iin, output power is Vout times Iout, and they differ by the losses: Vout times Iout equals efficiency times Vin times Iin. So whichever of the four I'm not given, I rearrange. The output current is efficiency times Vin times Iin over Vout. The input current is Vout times Iout over efficiency times Vin. Output voltage is efficiency Vin Iin over Iout, and input voltage is Vout Iout over efficiency times Iin.

First question I'd ask is what the efficiency is. If they don't give it, I'd say, assuming an ideal converter, and then note the real answer shifts by 5 to 15 percent for a switching converter.

An example. Twelve volts in at half an amp is 6 watts. If the output is 3.3 volts at ideal efficiency, the output current is 6 over 3.3, 1.82 amps. At 90 percent efficiency it's 5.4 watts, so 1.64 amps. Another: a boost converter takes 1.5 amps from a 3.7 volt cell and delivers 1 amp. Ideal output voltage is 5.55 volts, and at 90 percent it's 5.0 volts, a typical 5 volt rail.

Then I sanity check. A buck has more current out than in, a boost the reverse. A linear regulator has essentially the same current in and out, so the input current is the output current plus its quiescent current, and efficiency is just Vout over Vin. And if the numbers I'm given produce an efficiency over 100%, something's off, so I'd flag it. If all four values are given, I can compute the efficiency and the loss, which helps spot whether it's a linear or switching regulator."
:::

::: terms
- **Input power**: $P_{in}=V_{in}I_{in}$.
- **Output power**: $P_{out}=V_{out}I_{out}$.
- **Efficiency ($\eta$)**: $P_{out}/P_{in}$, between 0 and 1.
- **Ideal converter**: $\eta=1$; input and output power are equal.
- **Power loss**: $P_{in}-P_{out}$, dissipated as heat.
- **Quiescent current ($I_q$)**: the regulator's own current draw.
- **Step-down / step-up**: output voltage lower/higher than input.
- **Power factor (PF)**: ratio of real to apparent power on an AC input.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** writes $P=VI$ on both sides and rearranges.

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the **efficiency assumption** (or asks), and shows both the ideal and a realistic (90%) result.
- Gives all four rearranged formulas and picks the right one without hesitation, with units.
- Does a **direction sanity check** (buck $I_{in}<I_{out}$, boost $I_{in}>I_{out}$, LDO $I_{in}\approx I_{out}$) and flags $\eta>100\%$ as inconsistent data.
- Notes where the formula doesn't apply as written: **linear regulators** (efficiency fixed by $V_{out}/V_{in}$), **AC input** (power factor), no-load/quiescent draw, transient behaviour.
- Mentions why it matters: battery sizing, fuse/wire rating, thermal loss $P_{in}-P_{out}$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Assuming input and output current are equal.** That's only true for a linear regulator; a switching converter changes the current by the voltage ratio.
- **Forgetting efficiency.** The ideal answer underestimates the input current of a real converter by roughly 5-15%.
- **Using voltage ratio for current in the wrong direction.** For a buck, $I_{in}=I_{out}V_{out}/(\eta V_{in})$, which is *less* than $I_{out}$.
- **Unit slips.** mA vs A, or W vs mW, particularly in multi-step problems.
- **Accepting an efficiency above 100%.** It means an input is wrong, or an additional source is present.
- **Applying it to an instantaneous waveform.** The relation holds for averages in steady state, because inductors and capacitors store energy.
:::

::: deep
## The one equation

At steady state, averaged over a switching cycle, the energy flowing into a converter equals the energy going out plus the heat it makes (see [[q0154]]). In power form:

$$P_{in}=P_{out}+P_{loss}\quad\Rightarrow\quad V_{in}I_{in}\,\eta=V_{out}I_{out}$$

The calculation is one line; the skill is in setting up the units, picking $\eta$, and judging whether the answer makes sense.

## A procedure that always works

1. **Identify the converter type** (buck, boost, LDO, transformer) if you can, because it determines the sanity check and the likely efficiency.
2. **Decide $\eta$.** If given, use it. If not, state "ideal 100%" and, if useful, repeat with a typical value (switching 85-95%; LDO $=V_{out}/V_{in}$).
3. **Write the balance** with units: $V_{out}[\text{V}]\,I_{out}[\text{A}]=\eta\,V_{in}\,I_{in}$.
4. **Solve** for the missing variable and compute with a calculator (or `node`).
5. **Sanity-check**: direction ($I_{in}$ vs $I_{out}$), plausibility (e.g. 1.82 A out of a converter rated for 1 A?), efficiency in range, loss $P_{in}-P_{out}$ and the resulting heat.

## Worked examples

**Example 1: buck, find $I_{out}$.** Input 12 V at 0.5 A (6 W). Output 3.3 V.
- Ideal: $I_{out}=6/3.3=1.818$ A.
- 90%: $I_{out}=0.9\times6/3.3=1.636$ A.

**Example 2: boost, find $V_{out}$.** A 3.7 V cell supplies 1.5 A (5.55 W); the load draws 1 A.
- Ideal: $V_{out}=5.55$ V.
- 90%: $V_{out}=0.9\times5.55/1=4.995$ V, which is a 5 V rail.

**Example 3: find $V_{in}$.** The output is 5 V at 2 A (10 W); the input draws 0.5 A at 92% efficiency. $V_{in}=10/(0.92\times0.5)=21.7$ V, plausible for a 24 V-class bus (with margin).

**Example 4: all four given, find $\eta$.** In: 5 V, 1 A (5 W). Out: 3.3 V, 1.4 A (4.62 W). $\eta=92.4\%$, loss 0.38 W. Efficiency this high with $I_{out}>I_{in}$ shows it must be a switching step-down converter, not an LDO (an LDO could not have $I_{out}>I_{in}$).

**Example 5: linear regulator.** $V_{in}=5$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I_{out}=0.2$ A. $I_{in}\approx I_{out}+I_q\approx0.2$ A, so $P_{in}=1$ W, $P_{out}=0.66$ W, $\eta=66\%$, loss 0.34 W. The relation $V_{out}I_{out}=\eta V_{in}I_{in}$ still holds, but $\eta$ is not a free parameter: it is fixed by $V_{out}/V_{in}$.

## Other cases where the same logic applies

- **Transformers (ideal)**: $V_1I_1=V_2I_2$. A 230 V to 12 V transformer carrying 1 A on the secondary draws 0.052 A on the primary (ratio 19.2:1), i.e. 12 W/230 V. With losses divide by $\eta$.
- **AC-DC adapters**: input real power $=V_{rms}I_{rms}\times PF$; e.g. 230 V × 0.3 A × 0.6 = 41.4 W of real power, even though the apparent power is 69 VA.
- **Multi-stage chains**: efficiencies multiply: 90% × 90% = 81%. Work backwards from the load: the input of the last stage is the output of the previous.
- **Batteries**: runtime $=E_{batt}/P_{in}$, with $P_{in}$ from this calculation. A 2.0 Ah, 3.7 V cell holds 7.4 Wh; a 5 W load behind a 90% boost needs 5.56 W, giving about 1.3 h.
- **Wire and fuse sizing**: use the *input* current at minimum input voltage (the worst case), not the output current.

## Why the input voltage minimum matters

Because $I_{in}\propto1/V_{in}$ for a fixed output power, the maximum input current occurs at the **minimum** input voltage. A buck from a 9-15 V supply delivering 6.6 W at 90% draws $6.6/(0.9\times9)=0.81$ A at 9 V and 0.49 A at 15 V. Size the fuse, connector and wiring for the first number.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> If asked this live, say the efficiency assumption aloud, do the arithmetic slowly, and finish with a one-line sanity check ("it's a step-down so current goes up, 1.8 A out of 0.5 A in, which matches 12 to 3.3"). That one line is what interviewers remember.

## Efficiency is not a constant: it depends on the load

Every example above used a single $\eta$, which is correct only at one operating point. A switching converter's loss is roughly a fixed part (gate drive, controller quiescent current, core loss), a part proportional to current (switching), and a part proportional to current squared (conduction):

$$P_{loss}(I)\approx P_0+k\,I+R_{eff}I^2$$

Take an illustrative 12 V to 3.3 V buck with assumed values $P_0=30$ mW, $k=12$ mW/A and $R_{eff}=0.1\ \Omega$. Then $\eta=\dfrac{3.3I}{3.3I+P_{loss}}$ gives:

| $I_{out}$ | $P_{out}$ | $P_{loss}$ | $\eta$ | $I_{in}$ at the true $\eta$ | $I_{in}$ if you assume 90% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 mA | 33 mW | 30 mW | 52% | 5.3 mA | 3.1 mA |
| 100 mA | 330 mW | 32 mW | 91% | 30.2 mA | 30.6 mA |
| 500 mA | 1.65 W | 61 mW | 96% | 142.6 mA | 152.8 mA |
| 2 A | 6.6 W | 454 mW | 93.6% | 587.8 mA | 611.1 mA |

The "assume 90%" shortcut is fine in the middle of the range, and wrong by 70% at 10 mA. This matters for battery life in standby, where the fixed loss dominates, and for any claim about "average efficiency". Efficiency peaks where the fixed and conduction terms match, $I=\sqrt{P_0/R_{eff}}=0.55$ A, at about 96.4%.

> The loss coefficients above are assumed for illustration, not taken from a datasheet. Real converters differ, and many have a light-load mode that lifts the 10 mA number substantially. Always use the manufacturer's efficiency-versus-load curve at your operating point.

## Which side to trust when the numbers disagree

Suppose a bench measurement gives 12.0 V at 0.62 A in and 3.30 V at 2.00 A out. Then $\eta=6.6/7.44=88.7\%$, and the loss is 0.84 W. If the datasheet promises 93.6% at 2 A, you have a discrepancy, and it is worth knowing how big the measurement error can be before blaming the part. With four independent readings each good to 0.5%, the efficiency uncertainty is the root-sum-square, $\sqrt{4}\times0.5\%=1\%$ relative, or about $\pm0.9$ points. A 5-point gap is therefore real: look for a missing term such as a diode on the input, a lossy cable, or a meter that is measuring after a drop you did not mean to include.

## Where the measurement goes wrong

- **Burden voltage**: a DMM on its current range drops tens to hundreds of millivolts, which is a large error at 3.3 V and a modest one at 12 V. Use a low-burden shunt, or measure the drop on a known sense resistor with a separate meter.
- **Measure at the converter pins**: wire and connector resistance between the supply clips and the board steals voltage, so the voltage you set on the bench supply is not the voltage on the board. Use a four-wire (remote-sense) supply or measure at the input capacitor.
- **Average vs RMS**: a buck's input current is pulsed, so a DMM sees only the average, which is what you want for $P_{in}$, but a clamp meter with poor bandwidth can read low.
- **Thermal drift**: efficiency drops as the converter warms, so wait for steady state and note the temperature.
:::

::: applications
- **Battery sizing and runtime** for portable devices: convert load power to battery current through the regulator efficiency.
- **Fuse, connector and cable selection**: use worst-case input current at minimum input voltage.
- **Power budgets in system design**: add rail powers and divide by each converter's efficiency to find the input power.
- **Measurement cross-checks** in the lab: $V_{out}I_{out}$ must be below $V_{in}I_{in}$, and the difference is the heat to remove.
- **Choosing converter ratings**: a boost from 3 V to 5 V at 1 A needs an input and switch rated for about 1.9 A (at 88%).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Steady state, averaged values.** Inductors and capacitors store energy, so instantaneous power differs.
- **Efficiency is given, or assumed ideal.** The explicit assumption matters; real switching converters are about 85-95% near mid-load and lower at light load.
- **DC quantities.** For AC inputs use RMS values and power factor.
- **Quiescent current is small** relative to the load; at light loads it matters ($I_{in}$ includes $I_q$).
- **Alternative valid answers.** The "fourth value" may be found with the converter's voltage-conversion ratio if the duty cycle is known (e.g. buck $V_{out}=DV_{in}$), but the power-balance route requires no knowledge of topology.
:::

::: followups
### What if efficiency isn't given?
State that assumption: "I'll take it as ideal, 100%." Then give the realistic adjustment: for a switching converter use around 90% (input current 11% higher); for an LDO, $\eta=V_{out}/V_{in}$ and $I_{in}\approx I_{out}$. The interviewer wants to see that you know efficiency matters, not a specific number.

### 24 V in at 0.25 A; 5 V out at 1.1 A. What's the efficiency, and is it plausible?
$P_{in}=6.0$ W and $P_{out}=5.5$ W, so $\eta=91.7\%$. It's plausible for a buck converter (output current is larger than input current by a factor 4.4, consistent with $V_{in}/V_{out}=4.8$). An LDO couldn't do this.

### A boost makes 5 V at 1 A from 3 V. What input current is needed at 88% efficiency?
$I_{in}=\dfrac{5\times1}{0.88\times3}=1.89$ A. Size the inductor, switch and wiring for at least that (plus ripple and margin), and check whether the battery can supply it at the end of its discharge.

### How does the calculation change for an LDO?
$I_{in}\approx I_{out}+I_q$, and the efficiency is automatically $\approx V_{out}I_{out}/(V_{in}I_{in})=V_{out}/V_{in}$. The relation still holds, but the input current is not scaled by the voltage ratio. For 5 V to 3.3 V at 200 mA the LDO draws 200 mA and dissipates 0.34 W.

### Is the relation valid instantaneously?
No. It holds on average. In a buck the input current is pulsed (high-side on-time only), while the output current is smooth, because the capacitor and inductor store energy within the cycle. Using averages avoids the problem. For brief events (load steps) the capacitors supply the energy difference.

### Scenario: you measure 12 V × 0.3 A at the input and 3.3 V × 1.2 A at the output of a "buck" board. What does that tell you?
$P_{in}=3.6$ W and $P_{out}=3.96$ W: efficiency 110%, which is impossible. Either the current measurement is wrong (a meter burden or range issue), the input voltage is read at the wrong node, an additional source feeds the output, or the readings were not simultaneous with steady-state conditions. I'd re-measure with a calibrated meter at the converter terminals.
:::

If they specify a converter (buck/boost/LDO), use the direction checks. It applies directly the principle in [[q0154]] and the efficiency definition in [[q0163]].
