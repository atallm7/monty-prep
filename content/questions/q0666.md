::: testing
Whether you understand the Fourier Transform as a change of viewpoint, from time to frequency, and not just a formula. The interviewer wants the definition, the physical meaning (any signal as a sum of sinusoids, with amplitude and phase at each frequency), a few transform pairs (rectangle/sinc, exponential/low-pass), and the key properties (linearity, convolution theorem, time-bandwidth trade-off). Hidden sub-skills: connecting it to filters and Bode plots, to rise time and bandwidth, and to the FFT used on instruments.
:::

::: why
Hardware engineers use the Fourier Transform constantly without writing the integral: a filter's Bode plot is the transform of its impulse response, EMI standards are specified in the frequency domain, a spectrum analyzer or FFT view on a scope shows a signal's harmonics, and the rule that a 1 ns edge needs about 350 MHz of bandwidth comes straight from it. It is the foundation for square-wave spectra ([[q0673]]), sampling ([[q0668]]), noise analysis and signal integrity.
:::

::: concepts
- **Sinusoids as building blocks**: a linear time-invariant system treats each frequency independently, so decomposing signals into sinusoids simplifies analysis. See [[q0034]] for how capacitors treat different frequencies.
- **Complex exponentials**: $e^{j2\pi ft}=\cos2\pi ft+j\sin2\pi ft$; they carry amplitude and phase compactly.
- **Spectrum**: the transform $X(f)$; its magnitude is the amplitude content at each frequency, its angle the phase.
- **Convolution theorem**: convolution in time is multiplication in frequency; this is why filters are described by $H(f)$.
- **Time-bandwidth trade-off**: narrow in time means wide in frequency, and vice versa.
- **DFT/FFT**: the sampled, finite version computed by instruments and software. See [[q0665]].
:::

::: answer
"The Fourier Transform is a way of rewriting a signal in terms of frequency instead of time. The idea is that almost any signal can be built as a sum, or integral, of sine waves of different frequencies, amplitudes and phases, and the transform tells you how much of each frequency is in the signal.

Mathematically, $X(f)=\int x(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}\,dt$. You multiply the signal by a complex sinusoid at frequency f and integrate, which measures how well the signal correlates with that frequency. The magnitude of $X(f)$ is the amplitude at that frequency, and the angle is the phase. The inverse transform sums those sinusoids back up to reconstruct the signal, so no information is lost.

Why it matters to a hardware engineer: for linear circuits, each frequency passes through independently, so the output spectrum is just the input spectrum times the circuit's frequency response, which is exactly what a Bode plot shows. Convolution in the time domain becomes simple multiplication in the frequency domain. It also explains things like why a sharp edge has a lot of high-frequency content, so a one nanosecond rise time needs about 350 megahertz of bandwidth. And in practice we compute it with the FFT, which is how scopes and spectrum analyzers show you the harmonics and noise of a signal."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** For $x(t)$ with $\int|x|\,dt<\infty$ (or as a distribution):

$$X(f)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x(t)\,e^{-j2\pi ft}\,dt,\qquad x(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(f)\,e^{j2\pi ft}\,df.$$

With angular frequency $\omega=2\pi f$ the pair has factors of $2\pi$ distributed differently by convention.

**Interpretation.** $|X(f)|$ is the amplitude density at $f$; $\angle X(f)$ is the phase. For real $x(t)$, $X(-f)=X^*(f)$, so the magnitude is even and phase is odd and only $f\ge0$ needs plotting.

**Properties.**
- Linearity.
- Time shift $x(t-t_0)\leftrightarrow X(f)e^{-j2\pi ft_0}$: delay changes phase only.
- Convolution $x*h\leftrightarrow X\cdot H$; product $\leftrightarrow$ convolution.
- Differentiation: $dx/dt\leftrightarrow j2\pi f\,X(f)$.
- Scaling: $x(at)\leftrightarrow\frac1{|a|}X(f/a)$: compressing in time stretches the spectrum.
- Parseval: $\int|x|^2dt=\int|X|^2df$ (energy is conserved).

**Key pairs.**
| $x(t)$ | $X(f)$ |
|---|---|
| Rectangle, width $T$, height 1 | $T\,\mathrm{sinc}(fT)$ (first null at $1/T$) |
| $e^{-at}u(t)$ | $1/(a+j2\pi f)$ |
| Impulse $\delta(t)$ | 1 (flat spectrum) |
| Constant 1 | $\delta(f)$ |
| $\cos 2\pi f_0t$ | $\tfrac12[\delta(f-f_0)+\delta(f+f_0)]$ |
| Gaussian | Gaussian |

**Linking to circuits.** For an RC low-pass, $h(t)=\frac1{RC}e^{-t/RC}u(t)$, so $H(f)=\frac1{1+j2\pi fRC}$ with $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$. With $R=1$ kΩ, $C=159$ nF: $f_c=1.0$ kHz.

**Rise time and bandwidth.** A signal with 10–90% rise time $t_r$ has significant spectral content up to about $0.35/t_r$: 1 ns gives 350 MHz.

**DFT/FFT.** $X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]e^{-j2\pi kn/N}$ with bin spacing $\Delta f=f_s/N$; the FFT computes it in $O(N\log N)$ rather than $O(N^2)$. Finite records cause spectral leakage unless windowed.
:::

::: long
"The Fourier Transform is the mathematical tool that changes a signal from the time domain to the frequency domain. The core idea is that you can express almost any signal as a combination of sine waves, and the transform tells you the amplitude and phase of each frequency component.

The definition is $X(f)$ equals the integral of $x(t)$ times $e^{-j2\pi ft}$ over all time. Intuitively, you correlate the signal with a complex sinusoid of frequency f; if the signal contains that frequency, the integral is large. The magnitude gives you how much of that frequency is present, and the angle gives its phase. The inverse transform adds all those sinusoids back and gets the original signal, so it's a lossless change of representation.

A few pairs are worth knowing. A rectangular pulse of width T transforms to a sinc, with its first null at one over T, so a short pulse has a wide spectrum. A decaying exponential becomes a one-pole low-pass response. An impulse has a flat spectrum, which is why impulse responses reveal frequency responses. A sine is just two spikes.

For hardware, the key property is that convolution in time becomes multiplication in frequency. A linear circuit's output is the input convolved with its impulse response, so the output spectrum equals the input spectrum times the transfer function, which is what a Bode plot displays. That's why we analyse filters at each frequency separately.

Another consequence is the time-bandwidth trade-off: the narrower something is in time, the wider it is in frequency. That's where the rule of thumb for edges comes from: bandwidth is about 0.35 over the rise time, so a one nanosecond edge has content out to roughly 350 megahertz.

In practice, scopes and software compute the discrete version with the FFT, using $N$ samples at sample rate $f_s$ gives bins spaced $f_s/N$ apart, and you use windows to reduce leakage."
:::

::: terms
- **Fourier Transform (FT)**: maps a time-domain signal to its frequency-domain representation $X(f)$.
- **Spectrum**: the magnitude (and phase) of the transform versus frequency.
- **Inverse FT**: reconstructs the time signal from its spectrum.
- **Impulse response $h(t)$**: a system's output to an impulse; its FT is the frequency response $H(f)$.
- **Convolution theorem**: $x*h\leftrightarrow X\cdot H$.
- **sinc function**: $\sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)$, the transform of a rectangle.
- **Parseval's theorem**: energy in time equals energy in frequency.
- **DFT / FFT**: discrete transform and its fast algorithm.
- **Spectral leakage**: smearing of energy due to finite observation windows.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "it converts time to frequency." An exceptional answer:
- Writes the definition and interprets it as correlation with complex exponentials, with magnitude and phase.
- Links to circuits: $H(f)$ is the FT of $h(t)$, convolution becomes multiplication, Bode plots are slices of $H$.
- Gives pairs with numbers (rectangle width 1 ms gives a first null at 1 kHz) and states the time-bandwidth trade-off.
- Connects to rise time and bandwidth ($0.35/t_r$) and the practical DFT/FFT with resolution $f_s/N$ and leakage.
- Distinguishes FT from Fourier series ([[q0667]]) and notes the FT of a periodic signal is impulses.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating the spectrum as just an amplitude plot.** Phase matters: delay and waveform shape are in the phase.
- **Confusing $f$ and $\omega$.** Mixing Hz and rad/s gives factors of $2\pi$ errors.
- **Believing a square wave only has its fundamental.** A square wave's spectrum has odd harmonics at $1/n$ ([[q0673]]).
- **Forgetting that the FFT assumes periodicity.** A record that is not an integer number of cycles leaks without a window.
- **Thinking narrow pulses have narrow spectra.** The opposite: short in time means wide in frequency.
:::

::: deep
## The idea in one picture

Take a stereo equaliser: the audio is a complicated waveform in time, but the sliders show how much energy sits in bass, mid and treble. The Fourier Transform gives you those sliders, continuously and with phase. Each frequency component is a sinusoid; the signal is their sum.

Why sinusoids? Because they are the **eigenfunctions of linear time-invariant systems**. If you feed $e^{j2\pi ft}$ into an LTI system, you get out the same $e^{j2\pi ft}$ multiplied by a complex number $H(f)$. So a circuit that is a nightmare in the time domain (differential equations, convolution) reduces to multiplication at each frequency.

### Where the definition comes from

Start with a Fourier series for a periodic signal with period $T$ ([[q0667]]): $x(t)=\sum_n c_n e^{j2\pi nt/T}$ with $c_n=\frac1T\int_0^Tx(t)e^{-j2\pi nt/T}dt$. Let $T\to\infty$: the spacing between harmonics $1/T$ shrinks to zero, the sum becomes an integral, and $Tc_n$ becomes the continuous function $X(f)$. That limit is the Fourier Transform.

### A first transform: the rectangular pulse

A pulse of height 1 and width $T$ (from $-T/2$ to $T/2$):

$$X(f)=\int_{-T/2}^{T/2}e^{-j2\pi ft}\,dt=\frac{\sin(\pi fT)}{\pi f}=T\,\mathrm{sinc}(fT).$$

Most of the energy lies below $1/T$, which is why $\approx1/T$ is the "bandwidth" of a pulse of width $T$. The sidelobes decay as $1/f$ because the pulse has sharp edges; smoothing the edges (a trapezoid or Gaussian) makes the sidelobes fall faster, which is how EMI is reduced by slowing edges.

### The key properties, with their circuit meaning

| Property | Math | Hardware meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Linearity | $ax+by\leftrightarrow aX+bY$ | Superposition of signals and noise sources |
| Time shift | $x(t-t_0)\leftrightarrow Xe^{-j2\pi ft_0}$ | A delay (e.g., trace propagation) only changes phase |
| Convolution | $x*h\leftrightarrow XH$ | Output spectrum = input spectrum × transfer function |
| Differentiation | $\dot x\leftrightarrow j2\pi fX$ | Differentiators boost high frequencies (noise!) |
| Scaling | $x(at)\leftrightarrow X(f/a)/|a|$ | Faster signals have proportionally wider spectra |
| Parseval | $\int|x|^2dt=\int|X|^2df$ | Total energy/power is the same in either domain |

### From impulse response to Bode plot

The impulse response of an RC low-pass is $h(t)=\frac{1}{RC}e^{-t/RC}u(t)$. Its transform is

$$H(f)=\int_0^\infty\frac1{RC}e^{-t/RC}e^{-j2\pi ft}dt=\frac{1}{1+j2\pi fRC}.$$

So when you push a signal through this filter, each frequency component is scaled by $|H(f)|$ and phase-shifted by $\angle H(f)$. A 5 kHz sine is attenuated by $1/\sqrt{1+25}=0.196$ (−14 dB). That calculation, repeated across a spectrum, is a complete analysis of the filter.

### Time-bandwidth: why sharp edges are broadband

An ideal step is $u(t)$, whose transform goes as $1/(j2\pi f)$: content at all frequencies, falling as $1/f$. A real edge with rise time $t_r$ is smoothed, and the spectrum rolls off faster above a knee near $f\approx0.35/t_r$ (for a first-order system with 10–90% rise time $t_r=0.35/f_{3dB}$). For $t_r=1$ ns this is 350 MHz; for 100 ps, 3.5 GHz. This is why probe and scope bandwidth, PCB trace design, EMI and connectors depend on rise time rather than the clock frequency. See [[q0676]].

## The practical version: DFT and FFT

Instruments don't have infinite continuous signals; they have $N$ samples at $f_s$. The discrete Fourier transform is

$$X[k]=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}x[n]\,e^{-j2\pi kn/N},\qquad f_k=k\frac{f_s}{N}.$$

- **Resolution**: bins are $\Delta f=f_s/N$ apart. With $f_s=1$ MHz and $N=1024$: $\Delta f=976.6$ Hz. To resolve closer tones you need a longer record, not a faster rate.
- **Range**: only $0\ldots f_s/2$ is meaningful for real input, and frequencies above $f_s/2$ alias back ([[q0670]]).
- **Speed**: the FFT computes the DFT in $\sim N\log_2N$ operations instead of $N^2$: for $N=1024$, about 10,240 vs 1,048,576, roughly 100× fewer.
- **Leakage**: the DFT treats the record as one period of a periodic signal. If the record doesn't contain an integer number of cycles, the discontinuity at the wrap-around smears energy across bins. A window such as Hann tapers the ends to reduce leakage at the cost of a slightly wider main lobe.

> [!TIP]
> To measure a sine's amplitude accurately in an FFT, either use *coherent sampling* (an integer number of cycles in the record) or use a window with a flat-top response and correct for its gain.

## Common conventions

Textbooks differ on whether to use $f$ (Hz) or $\omega$ (rad/s) and where to put $2\pi$ or $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$. Using $f$ makes the transform pair symmetric with no stray factors, which is why it is used here. When someone quotes a transform, check which variable and normalization they use before comparing results.
:::

::: applications
- **Spectrum analyzers and scope FFT**: view harmonics, noise and spurs of a signal.
- **EMI/EMC**: regulatory limits are specified vs frequency; edge rate controls spectral content.
- **Filter design**: frequency response is the FT of the impulse response; designers shape $H(f)$.
- **Communications**: OFDM uses FFTs to divide a channel into many orthogonal subcarriers.
- **Audio and image compression**: transforms (MDCT, DCT) concentrate information in few coefficients.
- **Medical imaging**: MRI data is acquired in frequency space and reconstructed by an inverse FT.
:::

::: assumptions
- The transform as written requires the signal to be absolutely integrable or treated with distributions (impulses) for sinusoids and constants.
- The $0.35/t_r$ rule is exact for a single-pole (first-order) response with 10–90% rise time; a Gaussian response gives about 0.34, and other shapes use slightly different constants.
- FFT statements assume uniform sampling, a real input, and no aliasing.
- The conventions use $e^{-j2\pi ft}$ in the forward transform (engineering convention); some fields use the opposite sign.
- Valid alternative explanations: emphasise the geometry (projection onto orthogonal basis functions) or the physics (spectrum analyzer sweeps a narrow filter).
:::

::: followups
### What is the Fourier Transform of an impulse and why is that useful?
It is 1 at all frequencies: a flat spectrum. That's useful because the response of a system to an impulse, $h(t)$, then has a transform equal to the frequency response, $H(f)$, so a single impulse test reveals the system's response at every frequency. It is also why narrow pulses are used to excite resonators and why very short edges generate broadband noise.

### What's the relationship between the Fourier Transform and the Laplace transform?
The Laplace transform uses a complex frequency $s=\sigma+j\omega$ and handles growing/decaying signals and initial conditions; the Fourier Transform is the Laplace transform evaluated along the imaginary axis $s=j\omega$ (for stable, causal signals). Bode plots are $H(s)$ evaluated at $s=j\omega$.

### Why does the FFT of a sine on a scope show a spread of energy instead of one line?
Because the record contains a non-integer number of cycles, which acts as a rectangular window: the transform of the window (a sinc) convolves with the sine's two spikes, leaking energy into adjacent bins. Using a window function like Hann reduces the leakage, or you can adjust the sample rate or record length to capture an integer number of cycles.

### How would you use the FT to estimate the bandwidth needed for a pulse train with 2 ns edges?
Use $BW\approx0.35/t_r=0.35/2\ \text{ns}=175$ MHz as the knee frequency of the spectrum. A scope should have at least 3–5× this bandwidth to measure the edge accurately; a probe and scope chain at 500 MHz–1 GHz is adequate. The clock frequency doesn't set it; the edge does.

### Scenario: your scope FFT of a 10 MHz clock shows peaks at 10 MHz, 30 MHz, 50 MHz, but nothing at 20 or 40 MHz. What does that tell you?
A 50% duty-cycle square wave has only odd harmonics. The missing even harmonics indicate close to 50% duty cycle. If the duty cycle drifts from 50%, even harmonics appear, which is a quick way to check duty cycle in the frequency domain. The harmonic amplitudes also fall roughly as $1/n$, characteristic of sharp edges.
:::
