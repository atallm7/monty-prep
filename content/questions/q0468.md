::: basics
### In plain words
Copper carries the current on a circuit board. Gold appears only as a very thin coat on the pads, the bare spots where parts are soldered. Gold is not a better conductor than copper (it resists current about 1.45 times more), and it costs thousands of times more per gram. What gold does well is resist corrosion, so the pads stay clean, flat and easy to solder.

### Picture it
Picture a steel beam in a bridge with a thin coat of rust-proof paint. The beam does the real work, and the paint just protects it. On a board, copper is the beam and a gold layer about 0.1 µm thick is the paint. Underneath sits a thin layer of nickel that stops the copper from creeping into the gold. The analogy is loose on one point: the gold dissolves into the solder during assembly, so it protects the pad only until then.

### Words you need
- **Resistivity**: how much a material resists current, whatever its shape. Gold is 2.44 and copper 1.68, in units of $10^{-8}$ Ω·m ([[q0467]]).
- **Noble metal**: a metal such as gold that does not rust or tarnish.
- **ENIG**: electroless nickel, immersion gold. A common pad finish with a very thin gold layer over nickel ([[q0466]]).
- **Embrittlement**: weakening of a solder joint when too much gold dissolves into it.

### The simplest example
Make the same trace out of both metals. Gold resists 2.44 / 1.68 = 1.45 times more, so to match 35 µm of copper you would need about 51 µm of gold. Even a gold layer only as thick as the copper, 35 µm over 100 cm², weighs about 6.8 g, against 3.1 g of copper. At roughly USD 100 per gram, that gold costs hundreds of dollars. The ENIG finish uses only about 15 mg over the same area. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the nickel barrier, hard and soft gold, the embrittlement limit, and the loss that nickel adds at very high frequencies.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can weigh two conductors on more than "gold is better because it's gold". The interviewer wants the **electrical** comparison (gold is *worse* than copper in resistivity), the **chemical** advantage (gold doesn't oxidise or tarnish), the **cost** (orders of magnitude), the **mechanical and process** differences (hardness, adhesion, solder embrittlement) and where gold *is* used: thin finishes (ENIG), edge connectors, bond pads, contacts. Naming the nickel barrier and explaining why the finish is only about 0.1 µm thick shows real board experience.
:::

::: why
Most boards you see have gold-coloured pads, and many engineers assume the traces are gold. Knowing why they are not, and what the gold is for, is a quick test of PCB literacy. It also touches reliability (corrosion, contact resistance, solder joint embrittlement), high-frequency behaviour (skin depth and nickel loss) and cost, which are all things a hardware engineer is expected to reason about ([[q0467]] covers why copper is the base conductor).
:::

::: concepts
- **Resistivity**: gold 2.44×10⁻⁸ Ω·m versus copper 1.68×10⁻⁸ Ω·m ([[q0467]]).
- **Noble metal**: gold does not form oxide or sulfide films, so contact surfaces stay conductive.
- **Surface finish (ENIG)**: electroless nickel under a very thin immersion gold layer, protecting pads ([[q0466]]).
- **Skin depth**: at GHz the current uses only the surface micron or so ([[q0056]]).
- **Intermetallics and embrittlement**: dissolved gold in solder can weaken joints.
- **Hard versus soft gold**: cobalt-hardened gold for wear surfaces, pure soft gold for wire bonding.
:::

::: answer
"Copper wins for the conductors; gold wins as a thin protective finish. Electrically gold is actually worse: its resistivity is about 2.4 times ten to the minus eight ohm-metres against 1.7 for copper, so a gold trace of the same size has about 45% more resistance. It's also vastly more expensive, thousands of times per unit mass and about twice as dense, so a board built with gold traces would cost hundreds of dollars in metal alone.

What gold does well is that it's noble: it doesn't oxidise or tarnish, so it stays solderable and keeps low, stable contact resistance. That's why pads get a finish like ENIG, electroless nickel with an immersion gold layer only about 0.05 to 0.1 microns thick, and why edge connectors and contacts use gold. The nickel underneath is a barrier that stops copper diffusing through the gold.

Gold has downsides too: it's soft, it dissolves into solder, and too much gold in a solder joint, a few percent by weight, makes it brittle. And at GHz the nickel under the gold adds loss.

So: copper for the current path, a thin gold finish where oxidation, flatness or contact life matter."
:::

For a 0.5×0.5 mm pad with 0.1 mm of solder, 0.1 µm of ENIG gold is about 0.26 wt%, well below; 1 µm of hard gold would be about 2.6 wt%, near the limit.

**High frequency.** Skin depth in nickel (ρ about 7×10⁻⁸ Ω·m, and magnetic) is smaller than in copper, and the ENIG nickel thickness (3 to 6 µm) is comparable or larger than it above a few GHz, so surface current flows through lossy nickel and attenuation rises.

::: long
"For the traces, copper wins clearly, and gold's role is as a thin finish and a contact material.

Start with electrical. Gold's resistivity is about 2.4 times ten to the minus eight ohm-metres against 1.7 for copper, so for the same geometry a gold trace has about 45% more resistance. Skin depth is also slightly larger, 2.5 microns versus 2.1 at 1 gigahertz, so that doesn't rescue it. Gold's thermal conductivity is also lower.

Then cost. Gold is thousands of times more expensive per kilogram and about twice as dense, so a 35 micron gold layer on a 100 square centimetre board would be nearly 7 grams of gold, hundreds of dollars, compared with a few cents of copper.

So why do boards have gold-coloured pads? Because gold is noble: it doesn't oxidise, so a very thin layer keeps copper pads from tarnishing before assembly, gives a flat solderable surface for fine-pitch parts, and has a low, stable contact resistance. The standard finish is ENIG, electroless nickel, 3 to 6 microns, with immersion gold only 0.05 to 0.1 microns. The nickel is a barrier that stops copper from diffusing up through the gold and oxidising the surface. Edge connectors use thicker hard gold, hardened with cobalt, so they survive many insertions. Wire-bond pads use soft gold.

The downsides: gold is soft, it dissolves into solder, and above roughly 3% by weight in a joint it makes the joint brittle, which is not a problem with a 0.1 micron finish but can be with thick gold. ENIG also has a 'black pad' failure risk, and at GHz frequencies the magnetic, resistive nickel layer under the gold adds loss.

So: copper for the current path, gold as a skin where corrosion resistance and contact quality matter."
:::

::: terms
- **ENIG**: electroless nickel immersion gold, a standard flat PCB finish.
- **Immersion gold**: a very thin gold layer deposited by displacement on nickel.
- **Hard gold**: electroplated gold with cobalt or nickel for wear resistance (edge connectors, contacts).
- **Soft gold**: pure, soft gold used for wire bonding.
- **Black pad**: a corrosion defect in the ENIG nickel layer that weakens solder joints.
- **Gold embrittlement**: weakening of solder joints when excess gold dissolves into the solder.
- **Noble metal**: a metal that resists oxidation and corrosion.
- **Contact resistance**: resistance at the interface of two touching conductors.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "gold doesn't corrode but is expensive; copper is cheap".

**An exceptional answer**:
- States that **gold is a worse conductor** (about 1.45× resistivity) and quantifies **cost** with an example.
- Explains **why gold is still used**: noble, stable contact resistance, flat solderable finish.
- Describes **ENIG structure** (copper, nickel barrier, thin gold) and thicknesses, and **hard versus soft gold** uses.
- Knows the **downsides**: solder embrittlement, black pad, softness, cost, nickel loss at GHz.
- Ties the choice to **application**: pads and contacts versus traces.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying gold is a better conductor.** It is not: copper and silver beat it.
- **Thinking the traces are gold because the pads look gold.** The finish is tens of nanometres; the conductor underneath is copper.
- **Ignoring the nickel.** The gold sits on a nickel barrier; skipping that explains neither the cost nor the high-frequency loss.
- **Specifying thick gold on solder pads.** Excess gold embrittles joints; thick gold is for contacts, not solder pads.
- **Assuming gold means no corrosion issues.** Porosity and underlying nickel or copper corrosion can still cause problems (black pad, creep corrosion).
- **Treating hard and soft gold as interchangeable.** Hard gold is not suitable for wire bonding; soft gold wears quickly.
:::

## Two metals, two jobs

Think of the board as a copper structure with a thin "skin" for protection and contact. The copper is the engineered conductor; the finish is chemistry that keeps the pads solderable for months on a shelf and through reflow.

## Electrical comparison

Resistance of a trace is $R=\rho l/(wt)$. For the same geometry gold's resistance is $2.44/1.68=1.45$ times copper's. To match a 35 µm copper trace with gold, you would need $35\times1.45=51$ µm of gold. Thermal conductivity (about 315 versus 400 W/mK) is also lower. Skin depth $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu_0)}$ is larger for gold because $\rho$ is larger:

Both cross the 3 to 6 µm nickel thickness at a few hundred MHz and are only about 2 to 2.5 µm at 1 GHz, so in the GHz range a growing share of the surface current flows in the lossy nickel layer. The 0.08 µm gold is far thinner than the skin depth, so it does not set the loss itself. Skin depth in magnetic nickel is smaller still.

## Cost and weight

Gold's density is 19.3 g/cm³ (2.2× copper's) and its price per mass is thousands of times copper's. For 100 cm² of 35 µm foil the mass is 6.8 g gold versus 3.1 g copper. At roughly USD 100 per gram gold would add several hundred dollars per board; copper is a few cents. The ENIG finish uses only about 0.15 mg per cm² of gold, which is why it is affordable.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Metal prices vary with the market. The figures here use rounded orders of magnitude (gold on the order of USD 100 per gram) to show scale, not a quote.

## Why gold's chemistry is attractive

Copper reacts with oxygen and sulfur in air, forming oxide and sulfide films that resist soldering and add contact resistance. Gold doesn't. A thin gold film therefore:

- Keeps pads solderable during storage (long shelf life compared with OSP or HASL).
- Gives a **flat** surface (unlike HASL's domed solder), which matters for fine-pitch BGA and QFN placement.
- Gives low and stable **contact resistance** for switches, edge connectors and test points, which is important for low-level signals (mV and µA) where an oxide film would add noise or open the contact.

## Why a nickel barrier

Copper diffuses through gold at elevated temperature, reaching the surface and oxidising it, so a diffusion barrier is needed: electroless nickel, 3 to 6 µm. Nickel is also a solderable surface in its own right: during reflow the thin gold dissolves into the solder and the solder wets and bonds to the nickel, forming a nickel-tin intermetallic.

## Hard gold, soft gold

- **Immersion gold (ENIG)**: 0.05 to 0.1 µm, for solderable pads.
- **Hard gold (electroplated, cobalt-hardened)**: about 0.8 to 1.3 µm or more over nickel, for edge connectors and switch contacts that see repeated mating.
- **Soft gold**: pure, soft, for ultrasonic or thermosonic wire bonding.

## Failure modes and downsides

- **Gold embrittlement**: dissolved gold forms brittle Au-Sn intermetallics in the joint. A commonly cited guideline is to keep gold below about 3 wt% of the joint; ENIG at 0.1 µm is about 0.26 wt% for a 0.5×0.5 mm pad with 0.1 mm of solder, but 1 µm hard gold would be about 2.6 wt%, near the guideline.
- **Black pad**: corrosion of the nickel during immersion gold plating creates a phosphorus-rich brittle layer, leading to joint failure. Controlled by process quality.
- **Cost**: even thin gold adds cost relative to HASL or OSP.
- **High-frequency loss**: nickel under the gold has higher resistivity and magnetic permeability, so above several GHz it adds attenuation. ENEPIG (with a palladium layer), immersion silver or thin-nickel variants are used in RF.
- **Softness and porosity**: thin gold can be porous and soft; hard gold is not suited to bonding.

## Putting it together

| Role | Metal | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Traces, planes, via barrels | Copper | Conductivity, cost, processability |
| Solder pad finish | Thin immersion gold over nickel | Flat, corrosion-proof, long shelf life |
| Edge connector fingers | Hard gold over nickel | Wear and contact resistance |
| Wire-bond pads | Soft gold (or ENEPIG) | Bondability |
| RF finish | Immersion silver, ENEPIG, or thin-nickel ENIG | Lower loss |

So the right framing is not "gold or copper": it is "copper plus a finish chosen for the job".
:::

::: applications
- **ENIG finishes**: fine-pitch BGAs and QFNs on phones, laptops and industrial boards.
- **Edge connectors**: PCIe, memory modules and backplane cards use hard-gold fingers.
- **Test points and switch contacts**: gold pads for repeated probing or pushbutton contacts.
- **Wire-bonded modules and chip-on-board**: soft gold bond pads.
- **Medical and aerospace**: gold finishes for corrosion resistance and reliability.
- **RF boards**: ENEPIG or immersion silver to reduce nickel loss.
:::

::: assumptions
I assumed "gold versus copper on the conduction layers" includes both the idea of making conductors from gold and the practical role of gold plating. Thicknesses (0.05 to 0.1 µm ENIG gold, 3 to 6 µm nickel), the 3 wt% embrittlement guideline and gold price scale are typical values or rules of thumb, and they vary by specification (IPC finish standards) and fabricator. If the interviewer is thinking of gold in semiconductors (bond wires, IC metallisation), the logic is similar: gold's corrosion resistance and ductility justify its cost at tiny volumes. Other noble finishes (palladium, silver) are alternatives with their own trade-offs.

::: followups
### Why is the gold on ENIG so thin?
Because it only needs to protect the nickel from oxidising until soldering, and because gold is expensive. Thicker gold adds cost, can embrittle joints and does not improve the solder bond; 0.05 to 0.1 µm is enough to be continuous and protective. Contacts that wear (edge connectors) use thicker hard gold because the layer is worn away over time.

### Does a gold-plated copper trace have lower resistance than plain copper?
No. The gold layer is much thinner than the skin depth at low frequency and adds a resistor in parallel with the copper that is negligible. At DC the current flows through the copper; at GHz it flows in a thin surface layer, which includes the gold and nickel, so a finish can add loss, not remove it.

### What is gold embrittlement and how do you avoid it?
When molten solder dissolves a lot of gold, brittle gold-tin intermetallics form and the joint weakens, particularly after thermal cycling. Avoid it by using thin gold finishes (ENIG's 0.1 µm is fine), limiting hard gold on solder areas (strip it or avoid soldering to it), and following the 3 wt% guideline as a check.

### What if your design needs both solderable pads and an edge connector?
Specify different finishes for the two areas (selective hard gold on the fingers plus ENIG or HASL on the solder pads), or use ENIG for everything and accept a shorter connector life, or use a connector with its own contacts. Hard-gold fingers are plated before the final finish and require bus bars and bevelling, so tell the fab early.

### Scenario: your 10 GHz board has more loss than simulated and uses ENIG. What do you suspect?
The nickel layer: at 10 GHz the copper skin depth is 0.65 µm, and the nickel (3 to 6 µm, magnetic, higher resistivity) carries part of the current, adding loss and phase shift, often a few tenths of a dB per inch more than a bare copper trace. Also check copper roughness and the laminate loss. Fixes: ENEPIG with thin nickel, immersion silver, or OSP on RF traces, with a soldermask opening only where required.
:::
