::: testing
The interviewer wants to watch you build the **output characteristic** ($I_D$ vs $V_{DS}$ for several $V_{GS}$) from understanding, and label it. Hidden checks:

1. Correct **axes and parameter**: $I_D$ on y, $V_{DS}$ on x, one curve per $V_{GS}$, with $V_{GS} \le V_t$ lying on the axis.
2. The **two regions** and the boundary: triode (curves rising, roughly linear near the origin) and saturation (curves flattening), separated by the parabola $V_{DS} = V_{GS} - V_t$.
3. **Square-law spacing**: equal $V_{GS}$ steps give *increasing* gaps between curves, unlike a BJT's evenly spaced $I_B$ curves.
4. Real-device details: slight upward slope in saturation (channel-length modulation), and optionally the **transfer curve** ($I_D$ vs $V_{GS}$) with $V_t$ marked and subthreshold conduction.
:::

::: why
The I-V curves are the MOSFET's personality on one page. They tell you whether a FET in a circuit is acting as a switch (triode, near the y-axis, where the slope is $1/R_{DS(on)}$) or as a current source/amplifier (flat saturation region). Load-line analysis ([[q0100]]) is done on top of exactly these curves. Power MOSFET datasheets publish "output characteristics" and "transfer characteristics" plots, and reading them is how you check whether a part will really be fully on at your gate voltage.
:::

::: concepts
- **Threshold voltage $V_t$**: the $V_{GS}$ needed to form the channel. Below it, $I_D \approx 0$. See [[q0096]].
- **Overdrive $V_{ov} = V_{GS} - V_t$**: sets channel charge and therefore current.
- **Triode region**: $V_{DS} < V_{ov}$, channel continuous source to drain, acts as a voltage-controlled resistor. See [[q0099]].
- **Saturation / pinch-off**: $V_{DS} \ge V_{ov}$, channel pinches off at the drain end, current set by $V_{GS}$. See [[q0099]].
- **Channel-length modulation ($\lambda$)**: the pinch-off point moves toward the source as $V_{DS}$ rises, giving a slight slope.
- **Comparison to BJT and diode curves**: same idea of a family of curves. See [[q0084]] and [[q0074]].
:::

::: answer
"I'd draw the output characteristics: drain current on the y-axis, drain-source voltage on the x-axis, and a family of curves, one for each gate-source voltage.

For $V_{GS}$ below threshold, the curve sits on the x-axis: no channel, no current. Above threshold, each curve starts at the origin and rises roughly linearly at first. That's the triode region, where the MOSFET acts like a resistor whose value is set by $V_{GS}$, so a higher $V_{GS}$ gives a steeper line. As $V_{DS}$ increases, the curve bends over and flattens. That's saturation, where the current is set by $V_{GS}$ and barely depends on $V_{DS}$: $I_D = \frac{1}{2}k_n(V_{GS}-V_t)^2$.

I'd draw a dashed parabola through the knees of the curves. That's the boundary $V_{DS} = V_{GS} - V_t$. Left of it is triode, right of it is saturation.

Two details I'd point out. Because current is square-law in overdrive, equal steps in $V_{GS}$ give increasingly spaced curves. And in a real device the saturation curves tilt up slightly because of channel-length modulation.

If they want the transfer curve too, I'd draw $I_D$ versus $V_{GS}$: zero until $V_t$, then a parabola rising."
:::

::: technical
**Long-channel NMOS model** with $k_n = \mu_nC_{ox}W/L$, $V_{ov} = V_{GS} - V_t$:

- **Cutoff** ($V_{GS} \le V_t$): $I_D \approx 0$ (subthreshold: $I_D \propto e^{(V_{GS}-V_t)/(nV_T)}$, $n \approx 1.2$–1.5).
- **Triode** ($V_{DS} < V_{ov}$): $I_D = k_n\left[V_{ov}V_{DS} - \tfrac12V_{DS}^2\right]$. Near the origin $I_D \approx k_nV_{ov}V_{DS}$, so $r_{DS} = 1/(k_nV_{ov})$.
- **Saturation** ($V_{DS} \ge V_{ov}$): $I_D = \tfrac12 k_nV_{ov}^2(1+\lambda V_{DS})$, $r_o \approx 1/(\lambda I_D)$.
- **Boundary**: substitute $V_{DS} = V_{ov}$: $I_D = \tfrac12 k_n V_{DS}^2$, a parabola through the origin.

**Example** ($k_n = 2$ mA/V², $V_t = 1$ V, $\lambda = 0.02$ V⁻¹):

| $V_{GS}$ | $V_{ov}$ | $I_{D,sat}$ (at the knee) | $r_{DS}$ near origin |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1.5 V | 0.5 V | 0.25 mA | 1 kΩ |
| 2 V | 1 V | 1 mA | 500 Ω |
| 2.5 V | 1.5 V | 2.25 mA | 333 Ω |
| 3 V | 2 V | 4 mA | 250 Ω |

Gaps grow 0.75, 1.25, 1.75 mA for equal 0.5 V steps (square law).

**Transfer curve** (in saturation): $\sqrt{I_D} = \sqrt{k_n/2}\,(V_{GS} - V_t)$ is a straight line whose x-intercept is $V_t$: a standard extraction method.

**PMOS**: same shapes in the third quadrant ($I_D$, $V_{DS}$, $V_{GS}$ all negative), or plotted as $|I_D|$ vs $V_{SD}$ for $V_{SG}$ steps.

**Short-channel devices**: velocity saturation makes $I_{D,sat}$ closer to linear in $V_{ov}$, so curves become more evenly spaced, and saturation occurs at lower $V_{DS}$.
:::

::: short
"I'd plot $I_D$ against $V_{DS}$ with a curve for each $V_{GS}$. Below threshold there's no current. Above it, each curve rises roughly linearly from the origin, that's triode where the FET is a voltage-controlled resistor, then flattens into saturation where $I_D = \frac12 k_n(V_{GS}-V_t)^2$. A dashed parabola $V_{DS} = V_{GS} - V_t$ separates the regions. Curves get further apart for equal $V_{GS}$ steps because it's square-law, and they tilt up slightly from channel-length modulation."
:::

::: long
"I'll draw the output characteristics first, since that's the standard 'MOSFET I-V curve.' The y-axis is drain current, the x-axis is drain-to-source voltage, and I draw a family of curves, each for a fixed gate-to-source voltage.

If $V_{GS}$ is below the threshold voltage, there's no channel, so that curve is just the x-axis. Ideally zero current, really a tiny subthreshold leakage.

Once $V_{GS}$ is above threshold, each curve starts at the origin. At small $V_{DS}$ the channel is uniform from source to drain, so the device is basically a resistor, and the curve is a straight line whose slope is $k_n(V_{GS} - V_t)$. Higher gate voltage, more channel charge, steeper line. That's the triode region, and it's where a MOSFET sits when it's used as a switch: that slope is one over $R_{DS(on)}$.

As $V_{DS}$ grows, the voltage between gate and channel at the drain end shrinks, so the channel thins out there and the curve bends over. When $V_{DS}$ reaches $V_{GS} - V_t$, the channel pinches off at the drain, and the current stops increasing. That's saturation, with $I_D = \frac12 k_n(V_{GS}-V_t)^2$. I'd draw a dashed parabola through all the knees, $I_D = \frac12 k_n V_{DS}^2$, and label left as triode and right as saturation.

Two things I'd point out on the drawing. One, the spacing: with a square law, equal steps in $V_{GS}$ give growing gaps between curves. With $k_n$ of 2 milliamps per volt squared and $V_t$ of 1 volt, $V_{GS}$ of 2, 2.5 and 3 volts give 1, 2.25 and 4 milliamps. Two, real saturation curves aren't perfectly flat; they slope up because of channel-length modulation, and extending them backward they meet near $-1/\lambda$ on the x-axis.

I'd also sketch the transfer curve: $I_D$ versus $V_{GS}$, zero until $V_t$ and then a parabola. On a log scale you'd see the exponential subthreshold tail below $V_t$."
:::

::: terms
- **Output characteristics**: $I_D$ vs $V_{DS}$ for stepped $V_{GS}$.
- **Transfer characteristic**: $I_D$ vs $V_{GS}$ at fixed $V_{DS}$ (usually in saturation).
- **Triode / linear / ohmic region**: $V_{DS} < V_{GS} - V_t$; resistor-like behaviour.
- **Saturation / active region**: $V_{DS} \ge V_{GS} - V_t$; current nearly independent of $V_{DS}$.
- **Pinch-off**: the channel's disappearance at the drain end when $V_{GD} = V_t$.
- **Overdrive voltage $V_{ov}$**: $V_{GS} - V_t$.
- **Channel-length modulation $\lambda$**: the slope of saturation curves; $r_o \approx 1/(\lambda I_D)$.
- **Subthreshold conduction**: exponential weak-inversion current below $V_t$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws some curves that rise and flatten.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Labels axes, the $V_{GS}$ parameter, $V_t$ (the curve that's on the axis), triode and saturation, and draws the **boundary parabola**.
- Draws **growing spacing** between curves and explains it (square law).
- Writes the triode and saturation equations and relates the triode slope to $R_{DS(on)} = 1/(k_nV_{ov})$.
- Adds the **slope from channel-length modulation** and where its extrapolation meets ($-1/\lambda$).
- Offers the **transfer curve**, the $\sqrt{I_D}$ trick for finding $V_t$, and the log-scale subthreshold view.
- Notes the **naming trap** (MOSFET saturation is the amplifier region) and that power MOSFET datasheets show the same plots.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Evenly spaced curves.** That's BJT-like. Long-channel MOSFET curves spread out as $V_{GS}$ rises.
- **Curves not starting at the origin.** With $V_{DS} = 0$ there's no lateral field, so $I_D = 0$ for every $V_{GS}$. There's no "turn-on knee" like a diode's in the $V_{DS}$ direction.
- **Labelling the flat region "linear."** The *flat* region is saturation. "Linear" is another name for triode, the rising region near the origin.
- **Putting the boundary at a fixed $V_{DS}$.** The knee moves right as $V_{GS}$ increases: $V_{DS,sat} = V_{GS} - V_t$.
- **Drawing the transfer curve as linear.** It's a parabola above $V_t$ (in long-channel devices), exponential below.
- **Thinking threshold means "fully on."** At $V_{GS} = V_t$ the current is tiny. Datasheets define $V_{GS(th)}$ at something like 250 µA.
:::

::: deep
## Building the curve from physics

A MOSFET's drain current is "channel charge × how fast it moves." Gradual-channel analysis ([[q0096]]) gives, for an NMOS with $k_n = \mu_nC_{ox}W/L$:

$$I_D = k_n\left[(V_{GS}-V_t)V_{DS} - \tfrac12V_{DS}^2\right]\quad (0 \le V_{DS} \le V_{GS}-V_t)$$

Read this as a function of $V_{DS}$ at fixed $V_{GS}$:

1. **Small $V_{DS}$**: the $V_{DS}^2$ term is negligible, so $I_D \approx k_n V_{ov}V_{DS}$. A straight line through the origin: a resistor $r_{DS} = 1/(k_nV_{ov})$, controlled by $V_{GS}$.
2. **Larger $V_{DS}$**: the $-\tfrac12V_{DS}^2$ term bends the curve over. Physically, the gate-to-channel voltage at the drain end is $V_{GS} - V_{DS}$, so the channel thins toward the drain.
3. **$V_{DS} = V_{ov}$**: the parabola peaks ($dI_D/dV_{DS} = 0$). The gate-to-channel voltage at the drain equals $V_t$, so the channel just disappears there: **pinch-off**.
4. **$V_{DS} > V_{ov}$**: extra drain voltage drops across the short depleted pinch-off region; the channel itself sees roughly $V_{ov}$, so current stays at $I_{D,sat} = \tfrac12 k_nV_{ov}^2$.

The boundary curve comes from substituting $V_{DS} = V_{ov}$ into the saturation current: $I_D = \tfrac12 k_nV_{DS}^2$.

How to draw it on a whiteboard in 30 seconds:

1. Axes: $I_D$ up, $V_{DS}$ right. Write "$V_{GS}$ ↑" with an arrow pointing up across the family.
2. Dashed parabola from the origin.
3. Three or four curves: each rises from the origin, meets the parabola, then runs almost flat to the right with a slight upward tilt. Make the gaps grow.
4. Label "triode" left of the parabola, "saturation" right of it, "cutoff: $V_{GS} < V_t$" along the x-axis.

```
Figure: Zero until V_t, then a parabola. The slope of this curve at the operating point is g_m = k_n(V_GS − V_t) = 2I_D/V_ov. Doubling the overdrive quadruples the current. Drag V_t down from 1 V to 0.5 V: the same V_GS = 2 V now delivers 2.25 times the current (1.5 V of overdrive instead of 1 V).

**Extracting $V_t$**: plot $\sqrt{I_D}$ vs $V_{GS}$. In saturation it's a straight line, $\sqrt{I_D} = \sqrt{k_n/2}(V_{GS}-V_t)$, so the x-intercept is $V_t$ and the slope gives $k_n$.

### Below threshold: the log view

On a linear axis the transfer curve looks dead below $V_t$. On a log axis, it's an exponential: weak inversion, where the MOSFET behaves like a BJT with a worse slope.

```
Figure: Below V_t the current falls exponentially, about one decade per n·V_T·ln(10) ≈ 83 mV here (n = 1.4). This "subthreshold slope" is why a MOSFET at V_GS = 0 still leaks, and why lowering V_t in advanced chips raises leakage dramatically. Above V_t the curve transitions to the square law.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The subthreshold slope factor $n$ (typically 1.2–1.5 for bulk silicon at room temperature, giving roughly 70–90 mV/decade; the ideal limit is 60 mV/decade at 300 K) and the exact shape of the transition region depend on the process and model. This plot uses a simple interpolation for illustration, which also shifts the strong-inversion current by a factor of order $n$.

## Real-device deviations from the textbook curves

| Effect | What it does to the curves | Matters when |
|---|---|---|
| Channel-length modulation ($\lambda$) | Saturation curves slope up; extrapolations meet near $V_{DS} = -1/\lambda$ | Amplifier gain ($r_o$), current-source accuracy |
| Velocity saturation (short channel) | $I_{D,sat}$ ≈ linear in $V_{ov}$, curves more evenly spaced, earlier saturation | Modern digital and RF transistors |
| Mobility degradation | Curves compress at high $V_{GS}$ | High gate drive |
| Self-heating | Current droops at high power | Power MOSFETs, SOI |
| Breakdown / avalanche | Current shoots up at high $V_{DS}$ | Exceeding $V_{(BR)DSS}$ |

## Power MOSFET datasheet curves

Power MOSFET datasheets show the same "output characteristics" plot, but scaled: tens to hundreds of amps on the y-axis and only a few volts on the x-axis, with $V_{GS}$ curves like 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 10 V. Two things to read off:

- **The triode slope at your $V_{GS}$** is $1/R_{DS(on)}$. If your gate drive is 3.3 V and the 3.5 V curve already saturates at 5 A, the part won't be a good switch at 10 A.
- **Many datasheets plot the third quadrant** (negative $V_{DS}$) for the body diode: $I_S$ vs $V_{SD}$ for diode conduction.

## PMOS curves

Everything mirrors. With the source at the top, $V_{GS}$, $V_{DS}$ and $I_D$ (defined into the drain) are all negative. Either draw the family in the third quadrant, or plot $|I_D|$ vs $V_{SD}$ for stepped $V_{SG}$, which looks exactly like the NMOS plot with a smaller $k_p$ (holes have lower mobility, [[q0097]]).

::: applications
- **Choosing a switch**: reading a power MOSFET's output characteristics at your actual gate drive to confirm it's deep in triode at the load current.
- **Amplifier design**: biasing in saturation, with $g_m$ and $r_o$ read from the transfer slope and the saturation tilt.
- **Current sources and mirrors**: rely on flat saturation curves; $\lambda$ sets their output resistance ([[q0104]]).
- **Process characterization**: fabs extract $V_t$ and $k_n$ from $\sqrt{I_D}$–$V_{GS}$ plots, and subthreshold slope from log plots.
- **Load-line analysis**: overlaying a resistor's line on these curves to find the operating point ([[q0100]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Long-channel square-law** model with constant mobility. Modern short-channel devices deviate (velocity saturation, DIBL), though the qualitative picture survives.
- **Enhancement NMOS** with body tied to source (no body effect). For a depletion device, curves exist at $V_{GS} = 0$ and below ([[q0098]]).
- I applied $(1+\lambda V_{DS})$ across both regions so the curves join smoothly at the boundary, as SPICE level-1 does. Textbooks sometimes apply it only in saturation; both are approximations.
- Room temperature. $V_t$ falls and mobility falls with temperature, so curves shift.
- A valid alternative "IV curve" is the **transfer curve** alone. Draw both if you can.
:::

::: followups
### Where on the curves is a MOSFET used as a switch, and where as an amplifier?
Switch: near the y-axis in triode at high $V_{GS}$ (on), or on the x-axis (off). The triode slope is $1/R_{DS(on)}$. Amplifier: in saturation, where $I_D$ is set by $V_{GS}$ and $V_{DS}$ can swing without changing $I_D$ much, giving voltage gain $-g_m R_D$.

### What is the slope of a saturation curve, and what does it tell you?
$\partial I_D/\partial V_{DS} \approx \lambda I_{D}$, so the small-signal output resistance is $r_o \approx 1/(\lambda I_D)$. With $\lambda = 0.02$ V⁻¹ and 4 mA, $r_o \approx 12.5$ kΩ. It caps the intrinsic gain $g_m r_o$. Longer channels give smaller $\lambda$ and higher $r_o$.

### Why do the curves spread out as V_GS increases?
Saturation current is proportional to $(V_{GS} - V_t)^2$. Equal steps in $V_{GS}$ give $1, 4, 9, 16$ units of current: gaps of 3, 5, 7. In short-channel devices velocity saturation makes it closer to linear, so the curves become more evenly spaced.

### How would you find V_t from measured data?
Bias in saturation (tie gate to drain, which guarantees saturation), sweep $V_{GS}$, plot $\sqrt{I_D}$ vs $V_{GS}$ and extrapolate the straight portion to zero current. The intercept is $V_t$ and the slope squared times 2 gives $k_n$. Datasheets instead define $V_{GS(th)}$ as the $V_{GS}$ giving a specified small current (e.g. 250 µA) with $V_{DS} = V_{GS}$.

### Harder: what happens to these curves for negative V_DS on a discrete NFET?
The channel conducts in reverse if $V_{GS}$ is above threshold (the roles of source and drain swap), giving a resistive line through the origin into the third quadrant. With the gate off, current is near zero until $V_{DS} \approx -0.7$ V, where the body diode turns on and current rises exponentially. That's the "third-quadrant" plot on datasheets, and it's how synchronous rectifiers operate.
:::

::: interpretations
"Draw IV curve for MOSFET" most often means the **output characteristics** ($I_D$ vs $V_{DS}$ family), mirroring the BJT version [[q0084]]. Some interviewers mean the **transfer curve** ($I_D$ vs $V_{GS}$). Draw the output family first and offer the transfer curve as a second sketch. Unless told otherwise, draw an N-channel enhancement device.
:::
