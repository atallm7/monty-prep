::: basics
### In plain words
The usual decoupling value is 100 nF at every supply pin, with a family of other values around it: a few nF or smaller for very high frequencies, 1 µF and 10 µF for middle frequencies, and large bulk capacitors near the regulator. You need a family because every real capacitor also has a little inductance. Above a certain frequency, its self-resonant frequency, it behaves like an inductor instead of a capacitor. Each value works best in its own frequency band.

### Picture it
Think of the gears on a bicycle. A low gear is great for climbing but useless at speed, and a high gear is the reverse. Capacitor values are gears: big values work at low frequencies, small ones reach higher. Together they cover the range. The analogy breaks in one place: above about 50 to 100 MHz all the capacitors behave alike, so you need less inductance, not another value.

### Words you need
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance, the small inductance built into every capacitor and its connections ([[q0030]]).
- **Self-resonant frequency**: $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{ESL\cdot C})$. Below it the part acts as a capacitor, above it as an inductor ([[q0031]]).
- **Impedance**: how much the part opposes a changing current at a given frequency, in ohms. Lower is better here.
- **DC-bias derating**: ceramic capacitors lose capacitance as the steady voltage across them rises.
- **Bulk capacitor**: a large capacitor near the regulator for slow changes in load ([[q0143]]).

### The simplest example
Assume a typical 0.5 nH of ESL. A 100 nF capacitor resonates at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{0.5\ \text{nH} \times 100\ \text{nF}}) \approx 22.5$ MHz. A 1 µF part resonates at about 7.1 MHz, and a 10 nF part at about 71 MHz. Ten times more capacitance moves the resonance down by about three times. Above resonance the impedance is $2\pi f \times ESL$, about 0.31 Ω at 100 MHz whatever the capacitance. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers each standard value's role, material and DC-bias traps, and how to choose when the datasheet gives no guidance.
:::

::: testing
Whether you know the *standard values* (100 nF, 10 nF, 1 µF, 10 µF, bulk) **and why they are those values**: each covers a different frequency band, limited by the capacitor's ESL. The hidden sub-skills:

1. Connecting value to self-resonant frequency ([[q0031]]), not just reciting a list.
2. Knowing the dielectric choices (C0G, X7R, X5R) and why you avoid Y5V.
3. Knowing real capacitance is less than the label at DC bias.
4. Being able to say how you'd choose when the datasheet gives no guidance.
:::

::: why
Every schematic review has someone asking "why 100 nF and 10 µF?" Choosing values by habit works for simple boards, but fast digital, RF and precision analog parts have specific needs: a PLL supply pin may want 10 nF plus 1 nF, a DDR rail a calculated network, an LDO a minimum output capacitance. The candidate who can justify values from frequency range and parasitics, and who mentions DC-bias derating, is clearly someone who has debugged real power rails.
:::

::: concepts
- **Self-resonant frequency (SRF)**: $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{ESL\cdot C})$; the capacitor is only capacitive below it ([[q0031]]).
- **ESR and ESL**: the parasitic series resistance and inductance ([[q0030]]).
- **Frequency bands**: larger values cover lower frequencies; smaller ones, with lower ESL, reach higher.
- **Dielectric type**: C0G/NP0 (stable), X7R/X5R (higher density, voltage- and temperature-dependent), Y5V/Z5U (poor) ([[q0023]]).
- **DC-bias derating**: ceramic capacitance falls as DC voltage approaches its rating.
- **Hierarchy**: pin-level, mid-value, bulk ([[q0032]], [[q0143]]).
:::

::: answer
"The standard answer is a hundred nanofarads, 0.1 µF, one at every supply pin. That's a ceramic, usually X7R or X5R in 0402 or 0603, and it's popular because with sub-nanohenry ESL it stays capacitive up to roughly 15 to 25 MHz, and it holds enough charge for the first tens of nanoseconds of a burst. Around that I use a family of values: 10 nF, sometimes 1 nF or 100 pF, in front for higher frequencies, up to a few hundred MHz, usually for fast or RF parts; 1 µF and 4.7 or 10 µF ceramics in the middle for the 100 kHz to a few MHz range; and larger bulk, 47 to 470 µF polymer or electrolytic, near the regulator or connector for slow load steps.

The reason you can't just use one value is that every capacitor has parasitic inductance, so above its self-resonant frequency it acts like an inductor. Big capacitors resonate low, small ones high. Two things I'd check besides the label: the dielectric, X7R or C0G rather than Y5V, and the DC-bias derating, because a 10 µF X5R in a small case can lose half its capacitance at 5 volts. And I always check the datasheet, since many parts, PLLs, FPGAs, ADCs, give their own recommended values."
:::

::: technical
**Typical roles and values.**

| Value | Typical package | Typical ESL | Typical SRF | Role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 pF–1 nF (often C0G) | 0402/0201 | 0.3–0.5 nH | 250–800 MHz | RF/very high-frequency bypass, near RF or PLL pins |
| 10 nF | 0402 | 0.4–0.5 nH | ~70–80 MHz | extends high-frequency range |
| 100 nF | 0402/0603 | 0.5–0.8 nH | ~18–22 MHz | default per pin |
| 1 µF | 0402/0603 | 0.6–0.8 nH | ~5–6 MHz | mid-frequency |
| 4.7–10 µF | 0603/0805 | 0.8–1 nH | ~1.5–2.5 MHz | per IC or group |
| 22–100 µF ceramic | 1206/1210 | 1–1.5 nH | ~0.5–1 MHz | local bulk |
| 100–470 µF polymer/electrolytic | SMD can | 2–10 nH | ~70–350 kHz | bulk at regulator/connector |

(ESL values are typical for the packages; mounting inductance is added on top. Treat them as ±50 %.)

**Why the SRF falls with C.** $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{ESL\,C})$. ESL is set mostly by body size and mounting, not by $C$, so a 100× larger capacitance gives roughly a 10× lower resonance. With $ESL = 0.5$ nH, 100 nF resonates at 22.5 MHz, 1 µF at 7.1 MHz, 10 nF at 71 MHz.

**Minimum impedance.** At resonance $|Z| = ESR$. Above it $|Z|\approx 2\pi f\,ESL$. For ESL 0.5 nH, $|Z| = 0.31\ \Omega$ at 100 MHz regardless of capacitance. So pushing past ~50–100 MHz requires lower mounted inductance (more parallel caps, better via geometry), not a different value.

**Dielectric.** C0G/NP0: ±30 ppm/°C, no DC-bias loss, but low density (≤ ~100 nF in 0603). X7R: ±15 % over −55…125 °C, moderate DC-bias loss. X5R: −55…85 °C. Y5V/Z5U: +22/−82 % and strong bias loss; avoid for decoupling.

**DC-bias derating.** For small-case high-value X5R/X7R, the effective capacitance at rated voltage can fall to 30–60 % of nominal (vendor-dependent; use manufacturer curves). Choose a higher voltage rating or larger case.
:::

::: long
"If I had to name one value, it's 100 nanofarads, one at each supply pin. It's a ceramic, X7R or X5R, in 0402 or 0603. With about half a nanohenry of ESL its self-resonant frequency is around 20 megahertz, so it behaves as a capacitor through the range where a lot of digital switching energy sits, and it stores enough charge to cover the first tens of nanoseconds of a burst.

But I rarely use only that. Around it there's a family of values, each for a frequency band. Smaller ones, 10 nF, 1 nF or even 100 pF, usually C0G, go closest for the hundred-megahertz range and are common near PLLs and RF parts. In the middle, 1 µF and 4.7 or 10 µF ceramics cover from a few hundred kilohertz to a few megahertz, one per IC or per group. And at the bottom, 47 to 470 µF polymer or electrolytic bulk near the regulator or the connector handles slow load steps while the regulator catches up.

The reason for the family is parasitic inductance. ESL is mostly set by the package, so bigger values resonate lower: a 10 µF might resonate at 2 megahertz, and above that it's an inductor. So I need smaller capacitors to cover higher frequencies.

Two practical things beyond the label: I use C0G or X7R, never Y5V, and I look at DC-bias derating, since a small-case 10 µF X5R at 5 volts can drop to half its nominal capacitance, so I pick a higher voltage rating or bigger case. And when the datasheet gives a recommendation, like for an FPGA, a DDR interface, or an ADC reference, I follow that over my habits, and may run a PDN impedance check against a target."
:::

::: terms
- **0.1 µF / 100 nF**: the default per-pin decoupling value.
- **X7R / X5R**: Class II ceramic dielectrics, ±15 % over their temperature ranges; moderate DC-bias loss.
- **C0G (NP0)**: Class I ceramic, very stable, no DC-bias loss, limited capacitance.
- **Y5V / Z5U**: Class II dielectrics with large capacitance swings; avoided for decoupling.
- **DC-bias derating**: loss of capacitance with applied voltage.
- **SRF**: self-resonant frequency $1/(2\pi\sqrt{ESL\,C})$.
- **Bulk capacitor**: large capacitor (≥ tens of µF) for low-frequency load steps.
- **Package size (case code)**: 0201, 0402, 0603, 0805, 1206; affects ESL and capacitance density.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer lists "100 nF, 1 µF, 10 µF". An exceptional one:

- Ties each value to a *frequency band* through $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{ESL\,C})$ and gives SRFs (≈22 MHz for 100 nF at 0.5 nH).
- Notes that above ~50–100 MHz the answer is *less inductance* (smaller package, parallel caps, better vias), not a different value.
- Mentions dielectric (C0G/X7R/X5R vs Y5V) and DC-bias derating with a number.
- Says that datasheet and vendor guidance override habit, with examples (PLL, ADC reference, FPGA, DDR).
- Is honest that the "decade spacing" is a convention and that a target-impedance calculation is better.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Bigger is always better."** A 100 µF capacitor has a lower SRF and higher ESL than a 100 nF one; at 50 MHz it's the worse choice.
- **Believing the label.** A 10 µF 6.3 V X5R in 0402 at 5 V may be 3–4 µF.
- **Using Y5V** because it's cheap: large temperature and bias swings.
- **One value only.** A single 100 nF isn't enough for high-current parts: they need bulk too.
- **Adding 1 pF "for RF" without checking.** Tiny values only help if the layout inductance is lower than the capacitor's own.
:::

::: deep
## Why these values exist

Real capacitors are series RLC circuits: $C$, plus ESR and ESL. Their impedance is

$$|Z(f)| = \sqrt{ESR^2 + \left(2\pi f\,ESL - \frac{1}{2\pi f\,C}\right)^2}$$

At low frequency the $1/(2\pi f C)$ term dominates and $|Z|$ falls at −20 dB/decade. At the self-resonant frequency $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{ESL\,C})$ the reactive terms cancel and $|Z|=ESR$. Above $f_0$ the ESL term dominates and $|Z|$ rises at +20 dB/decade ([[q0031]]).

## The standard family, explained

**100 nF (the default).** Cheap, available in 0402 in X7R at ≥ 16 V (little DC-bias loss), SRF around 15–25 MHz. Good for the 1–20 MHz band and for first-burst charge: $It/C = 5$ mV for 100 mA over 5 ns ([[q0501]]).

**10 nF, 1 nF, 100 pF (high-frequency).** Their SRF is 70–800 MHz. They are useful only if the mounting inductance is small enough. A 1 nF capacitor with 1 nH of total mounting inductance resonates at $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{1\text{ nH}\times1\text{ nF}})=159$ MHz. Typically used for RF modules, PLL supply pins, clock chips, and wherever a datasheet asks.

**1 µF and 4.7–10 µF (mid).** Cover 300 kHz–5 MHz where the 100 nF capacitors have run out of charge and the bulk is still too inductive. One per IC or per two to four supply pins is typical.

**22–100 µF ceramic and 47–470 µF bulk.** Supply energy for microsecond to millisecond load steps and give the regulator a stable load. Aluminium polymer or tantalum-polymer types have ESR of 10–50 mΩ, which also damps the anti-resonance with the ceramics.

## Dielectrics and what the label doesn't tell you

| Class | Examples | Behaviour | Use in decoupling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Class I | C0G (NP0) | ±30 ppm/°C, no bias loss, no piezo, low C density | RF, PLL, analog supply bypass where stability matters |
| Class II (stable) | X7R, X5R | ±15 % over temp; bias loss; some microphonics | The workhorse |
| Class II (general) | Y5V, Z5U | +22/−82 % over temp; large bias loss | Avoid |

**DC-bias derating.** Ferroelectric ceramics lose capacitance as the DC field in the dielectric grows. For a 10 µF, 6.3 V, X5R 0402 at 5 V, the effective value is often below 4 µF (vendor curves; check the part). The same value in 0805 at 25 V rating loses far less. When quoting a design, use the effective capacitance at the operating voltage and temperature.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Quoted ESL and ESR values are typical orders of magnitude for common package sizes. Exact figures depend on manufacturer, series and mounting. DC-bias loss is highly part-specific, so use the manufacturer's capacitance-vs-voltage curve.

## How to choose when there is no recommendation

1. **Start from the datasheet's recommended network**, if given (FPGA, processor, DDR, ADC reference, RF module). Many give a table of values and counts per supply pin or rail.
2. **Else use the default:** 100 nF per supply pin pair, 1–10 µF per IC (or per rail section), bulk per rail.
3. **For fast, power-hungry parts**, compute a target impedance $Z_{target}=\Delta V/\Delta I$ and choose values and counts so that the combined impedance (including mounting inductance) stays below it over the band. The simulation below shows the effect of mixing values and mounting inductance:

4. **Verify** with an impedance measurement or a PDN simulation, and with a scope probe at the pin.

## Ratio between values

A common design rule is to space values by about a factor of 10 so that each capacitor's inductive rise meets the next one's capacitive fall. In practice the spacing is limited by anti-resonance peaks between capacitors, whose height depends on ESR. Using capacitors with more ESR (or adding a small series resistance or a polymer bulk capacitor) damps the peaks. Mixed-value designs should be simulated rather than assumed.

## Real-world examples

- **Typical microcontroller board:** one 100 nF per VDD pin, one 4.7–10 µF per rail section, plus bulk at the regulator. Manufacturers' hardware design guides generally follow this pattern.
- **PLL or oscillator supply:** 10 nF + 100 nF (and sometimes a ferrite bead) at the pin.
- **ADC reference pin:** the datasheet often requires 1–10 µF low-ESR with a particular dielectric.
- **FPGA core rail:** dozens of 100 nF–1 µF capacitors under the BGA, 10–47 µF nearby, bulk at the regulator, sized from a target impedance.
:::

::: applications
- **MCU and logic boards**: 100 nF per VDD pin plus a few µF per rail.
- **PLL and clock chips**: 10 nF / 1 nF in parallel with 100 nF near the supply pins, often with a ferrite.
- **RF front-ends**: 100 pF–1 nF C0G close to the amplifier, bigger values further out.
- **Precision references and ADCs**: 1–10 µF low-ESR ceramics or specified values at the reference pin.
- **FPGAs and processors**: arrays of 100 nF–10 µF capacitors designed against a target impedance.
:::

::: assumptions
- Ceramic MLCCs in common SMD packages, with mounting inductance small enough for the values to matter.
- ESL and ESR are typical values; real parts differ by vendor and series.
- Digital loads with fast edges; for slow loads a few µF is often sufficient.
- If the datasheet recommends specific values, they take precedence over rules of thumb.
- Another valid answer is "use values from the manufacturer's decoupling guide and verify the PDN impedance against a target", which is the right approach for high-performance devices.
:::

::: followups
### Why 100 nF and not 1 µF at every pin?
A 1 µF has a lower SRF (about 5–7 MHz at 0.5–0.7 nH), so above that it is inductive, while 100 nF stays capacitive to ~20 MHz and its 0402 package has less ESL. For fast edges the pin-level capacitor needs low inductance and enough charge for tens of ns; 100 nF delivers both. The 1 µF covers the lower band behind it.

### What happens to a 10 µF X5R capacitor at its rated voltage?
It can lose a large fraction of its capacitance, often 50 % or more in small cases at high bias. So if I need 10 µF effective at 5 V, I'd choose a higher voltage rating, a larger case, or several in parallel, checking the manufacturer's DC-bias curve.

### Would you ever use a 100 pF capacitor for decoupling?
Yes, for RF or multi-hundred-MHz parts where it is placed extremely close to the pin and the datasheet recommends it. Its SRF is several hundred MHz only if the mounting inductance is well below a nanohenry. Otherwise it contributes little.

### What if you can't find a recommended value for a part?
Start with 100 nF per supply pin and a few µF per IC, put bulk per rail, then check supply noise with a short-lead probe. If the part is fast and hungry, compute a target impedance and size a network. Don't skip the check: it takes 10 minutes on the bench.

### Scenario: your 3.3 V rail has 80 mV spikes at 40 MHz even with 100 nF at each pin. What would you try?
Above ~20 MHz, 100 nF is inductive, so I'd look at mounting inductance first: vias, trace length, plane distance. Then add smaller capacitors (10 nF or 1 nF) with the lowest inductance next to the pins or parallel more 100 nF capacitors to halve the ESL. I'd also check for a resonance with a nearby bulk capacitor and damp it with ESR if present.
:::
