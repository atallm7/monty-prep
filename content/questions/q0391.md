::: testing
Whether you think about safety as an engineer rather than a list of warnings. The interviewer is looking for dangers to *people* (crushing and entanglement, shock, burns, fire, flying parts, unexpected restart), to *equipment* (voltage spikes, regeneration, overheating) and to the *system* (EMI, brown-out), and for the mitigation that goes with each: hierarchy of controls, hardware interlocks, fail-safe defaults, energy limits. Quantifying a hazard (stored energy, pinch force, short-circuit current) is the mark of someone who has actually worked with hardware.
:::

::: why
Actuators are the part of an electronic product that can physically hurt someone. A sensor board that fails just stops working; a motor that fails can start unexpectedly, trap a finger, or start a fire. Product safety standards and design reviews therefore centre on actuators. Interviewers for robotics, automotive, medical and industrial roles care that you design for failure: what does the system do when firmware crashes, the supply sags or the user reaches in?
:::

::: concepts
- **Stored energy**: electrical ($\tfrac12LI^2$, $\tfrac12CV^2$), mechanical ($\tfrac12J\omega^2$, springs, gravity), chemical (battery).
- **Inductive kick**: high-voltage spikes when current is interrupted. See [[q0340]].
- **Stall and overcurrent heating**: see [[q0390]] and [[q0336]].
- **Back-EMF and regeneration**: a spinning motor is a generator. See [[q0341]].
- **Fail-safe design**: the default state on loss of power or control should be safe.
- **Hierarchy of controls**: eliminate, substitute, engineer, administrative, PPE.
:::

::: answer
"I split it into dangers to people, to the equipment, and to the system around it.

For people, the main one is mechanical: a motor or solenoid can pinch, crush or entangle, and a modest servo with 20 kg·cm at a 2 cm lever pushes with nearly 100 newtons, plenty to hurt a finger. Then unexpected motion, such as the MCU resetting or restarting after a fault. Heat is another: a stalled motor gets hot enough to burn. Electrical shock matters for mains solenoids or high-voltage motors, and the inductive kick can be hundreds of volts even from a 12 V coil. A short in a battery-driven system can mean a fire. Strong magnets in motors can pinch and interfere with implants.

For equipment, inductive spikes kill MOSFETs, braking motors pump the supply voltage up, and stalls burn windings.

For the system, brushes arcing and PWM edges create EMI, and inrush makes the supply sag and resets controllers.

Mitigation: use the lowest voltage and power that works, guards and torque limits, fuses and current limits, a hardware e-stop that cuts power independent of firmware, flyback protection, gate pulldowns so the motor is off when the MCU is in reset, and fail-safe defaults so power loss leaves the load safe."
:::

::: technical
**To people.**
- *Crush, pinch, entanglement*: force $F = \tau/r$. A 20 kg·cm servo (1.96 N·m) at 20 mm gives 98 N. Gearing multiplies it.
- *Stored mechanical energy*: $E = \tfrac12J\omega^2$; a rotor with $J = 2\times10^{-4}$ kg·m² at 3000 rpm holds about 9.9 J, and at 10,000 rpm about 110 J. Springs and raised loads hold energy too, and can move when power is removed.
- *Unexpected start*: MCU reset or watchdog restart, fault auto-restart, stuck switch.
- *Burns*: stalled motor housings, driver heatsinks.
- *Shock*: mains-powered solenoids, motors above about 50 V DC (touch-safe limits differ by standard and conditions), bus capacitors that remain charged after power-off.
- *Fire*: battery short-circuit current is large (a 3S LiPo at 11.1 V into 10 mΩ is over 1 kA by Ohm's law, limited by cell capability), overheated wiring, a stalled motor, a stuck-on solenoid.
- *Magnetic fields*: neodymium magnets in motors can pinch fingers; fields can affect pacemakers and magnetic media.
- *Flying parts, noise*: rotor failure, thrown objects (propellers), loud motors.

**To equipment.** Inductive spikes ($v = L\,di/dt$), regeneration raising the bus, shoot-through, overheating, EMI into other electronics, mechanical jamming.

**Unexpected generation.** A motor back-driven by its load produces $V = K_e\omega$ on its terminals. A brushless motor with $K_e = 0.02$ V·s/rad at 20,000 rpm produces about 42 V peak per phase with the drive off, which can damage electronics or shock someone.

**Controls (hierarchy).** Eliminate the hazard (lower voltage, lower force), engineer it (guards, torque limits, interlocks, e-stop, safe-torque-off), then procedure and PPE. Standards worth knowing by name: IEC 60204-1 (electrical equipment of machines), ISO 13849-1 (safety-related control system performance), IEC 61800-5-2 (safe torque off in drives), IEC 62368-1 (product safety for electronics). Do not quote clause numbers you have not checked.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Touch-voltage limits and required safety functions depend on the standard, the product category and environment (dry, wet, medical). Check the standard that applies to your product rather than relying on a single number.
:::

::: long
"I think about three groups: people, the equipment itself, and the rest of the system.

People first. The most common injury is mechanical. A motor's torque times a short lever is a lot of force: a 20 kg·cm hobby servo at a 2 cm lever pushes with about 100 newtons, easily enough to pinch a finger, and gears multiply it. Rotating shafts entangle hair and clothing. Rotors and flywheels store energy: a small rotor at 3000 rpm holds about 10 joules, and at 10,000 rpm over 100. Then unexpected motion: the MCU resets, the system auto-restarts after a fault, or a spring or gravity load moves when power is removed. Heat is another; a stalled motor reaches burn temperatures. Electrically, 12 V coil spikes can reach hundreds of volts, mains solenoids and high-voltage buses can shock, and large capacitors stay charged. Batteries add fire risk: a short on a LiPo pack carries over a kiloamp.

For the equipment: inductive spikes destroy MOSFETs, braking pumps energy back into the supply and raises voltage, and stall burns windings.

For the system: arcing brushes and fast PWM edges are EMI sources, and inrush current sags the rail and resets controllers.

Mitigation is layered. First reduce the hazard, using lower voltage and limited force. Then engineer controls: guards, current and torque limits, flyback clamps, gate pulldowns so the load is off during reset, a hardware e-stop or safe-torque-off that doesn't depend on firmware, and fail-safe defaults so loss of power is safe. Procedures and labels come last."
:::

::: terms
- **Hierarchy of controls**: ordered list of hazard mitigation, from elimination to PPE.
- **Safe torque off (STO)**: drive function that removes torque-producing power by hardware means.
- **E-stop**: emergency stop that removes power independent of the controller.
- **Interlock**: switch that prevents operation when a guard is open.
- **Fail-safe**: failure leaves the system in a safe state.
- **Stored energy**: energy held in inductors, capacitors, rotating masses, springs or batteries.
- **Regeneration**: energy flowing from the motor back to the supply during deceleration.
- **Lockout/tagout**: procedure to keep equipment de-energised during service.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer quantifies at least two hazards (pinch force, stored rotor energy, battery short-circuit current, inductive spike), distinguishes people/equipment/system dangers, and describes concrete mitigations that do not depend on firmware (hardware e-stop, gate pulldown, fuse, flyback). It mentions failure behaviour on loss of power, uncontrolled generation from back-driving, standards by name, and avoids false precision on legal limits.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating low voltage as harmless.** 12 V can still deliver a kiloamp from a battery or crush a finger with a geared motor.
- **Relying on software for safety.** A hung MCU cannot stop a motor; the hardware must.
- **Forgetting stored energy.** Capacitors, rotors and springs remain hazardous after power-off.
- **Missing the back-drive case.** A turning motor produces voltage even with the drive disconnected.
- **No gate pulldown.** MCU pins float during reset, so the MOSFET may turn on at random.
:::

## Think in terms of energy

Every hazard here is some form of stored or delivered energy or force. Listing the energy forms makes the dangers systematic.

| Capacitor | $\tfrac12CV^2$ | 4700 µF at 48 V: 5.4 J | Shock, welding, spark |

| Battery | $V\cdot Q$ | 3S 5 Ah: 55 Wh | Fire, short circuit |

## Pinch and force

A rotary actuator's output force at a radius is $F = \tau/r$. A hobby servo at 1.96 N·m (20 kg·cm) with a 20 mm horn gives 98 N, about 10 kgf. A mere 10 N is painful at a pinch point. In collaborative robots, standards talk about permissible forces and pressures on body parts; those numbers vary by body region, so cite the standard rather than quoting from memory. The design response is torque limiting, compliant joints, speed limits near humans, and padding.

## Unexpected start: the MCU reset problem

## Inductive kick as a safety issue

Beyond killing drivers, a spike can arc across switch contacts, ignite flammable vapour, or shock someone touching an exposed node. An unprotected 10 mH coil interrupting 1 A in 10 µs would ideally produce about a kilovolt; in practice it is limited by parasitic capacitance, avalanche and arcing.

## Regeneration and back-drive

A motor is a generator. $V = K_e\omega$ with the drive off. A BLDC with $K_e = 0.02$ V·s/rad turning at 20,000 rpm (2094 rad/s) generates about 42 V peak per phase. With the phases shorted it brakes hard; with the phases open it is a hazard to anything connected. During regenerative braking, current returns to the supply and raises the bus unless a battery or brake resistor takes it. Overvoltage damages capacitors and drivers and, with a lithium battery, can be a charging-fault hazard.

## Fire and battery

A lithium pack's short-circuit current is limited by cell internal resistance and wiring, and a short can reach hundreds of amps to over a kiloamp for a large pack. Stalled motors on a battery without fuse or current limit can overheat wiring. Protection: fuse sized for wire and cell, BMS with overcurrent and short-circuit cut-off, proper wire gauge, and thermal sensing ([[q0396]]).

## Fail-safe thinking

For each fault ask: what is the state of the load? Examples:

1. Power lost: spring-return valve closes; a vertical axis needs a brake that engages on power loss (normally-closed brake).
2. MCU hangs: watchdog resets, gate pulldown defaults off, hardware interlock opens.
3. Sensor lost: encoder fault triggers the drive to shut down, since runaway is worse than stopping.
4. E-stop pressed: power removed from the actuator (STO), not just a software command.

## Mitigation summary

1. **Eliminate**: lower voltage, lower force, lower speed.
2. **Engineer**: guards, torque limiters, current limits, interlocks, E-stop, STO, thermal cutouts, flyback.
3. **Administrate**: labels, procedures, training.
4. **PPE**: last resort.

## Worked example: coasting versus braking

Use the rotor from the energy table: $J = 2\times10^{-4}$ kg·m² at 3000 rpm, so $\omega = 314$ rad/s and $E = 9.87$ J. Suppose bearing and windage friction is a steady 0.01 N·m. Then power-off coasting takes $t = J\omega/\tau_f = 2\times10^{-4}\times314/0.01 = 6.3$ s. For six seconds after someone presses stop, a spinning shaft can still catch a sleeve or a hand. Coasting is a hazard in its own right.

Now short the armature of a brushed motor with $K_t = K_e = 0.05$ N·m/A and $R = 1$ Ω (dynamic braking). The back-EMF at 314 rad/s is $0.05\times314 = 15.7$ V, so the initial braking current is 15.7 A, the braking torque is $K^2\omega/R = 0.79$ N·m, and the first instant dissipates 247 W in the winding. The speed decays exponentially with $\tau_m = JR/K^2 = 0.08$ s, falling to a tenth in $0.08\ln10 = 0.18$ s. That is more than thirty times faster than coasting, but the 15.7 A peak must be survivable by the low-side transistors or the brake contacts. All 9.87 J ends up as heat in the winding, which is harmless once: a repeated emergency-stop cycle every few seconds would not be.

Two cautions follow. Braking torque falls to zero with speed, so dynamic braking never holds a load; a vertical axis still needs a mechanical brake. And a PM motor spun by its load with shorted terminals is a safe state, but with open terminals it is a generator charging whatever is connected, which is the BLDC hazard described above.

## Worked example: how bad is a battery short

Take a 3S lithium pack at 12.6 V full with about 15 mΩ per cell and 15 mΩ of wiring and connector resistance, so 60 mΩ total. A hard short draws $12.6/0.06 = 210$ A. If 15 mΩ of that resistance is a thin wire, it dissipates $210^2\times0.015 = 660$ W as heat in a few centimeters of insulation, which will soften PVC in seconds. A fuse placed at the battery terminal must open before the wire does, and must have enough interrupt rating for the available current. A 30 A automotive blade fuse is plausible for this pack, but its DC interrupt rating and voltage rating must be checked. Cell internal resistance varies widely with chemistry, temperature and state of charge, so treat 15 mΩ as an illustrative number, not a datasheet value.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Short-circuit current depends on the exact cells, cabling and temperature. Always take the cell maximum continuous and pulse discharge ratings from the actual datasheet, and use a pack-level protection IC or fuse rated for the real prospective current.

## Verifying a safe default on the bench

- **Reset behavior:** with the motor unloaded and a current-limited supply, hold the MCU in reset with its debug probe and ramp the supply from zero. Probe the gate. It should sit at 0 V thanks to the pulldown, never drift toward the threshold. Repeat while pulling the reset line with a function generator to mimic brownouts.
- **Brake-out test:** kill the supply with the shaft spinning and time the stop with a tachometer or encoder log. Compare against the $J\omega/\tau_f$ and $JR/K^2$ estimates. A large mismatch means friction or $K_t$ is different from the datasheet.
- **E-stop proof:** with the system running, press the E-stop and confirm with a scope that the motor current goes to zero without any firmware involvement. Then inject a stuck MCU (halt in the debugger) and confirm the same.
- **Flyback margin:** probe the switch node while turning off the highest load current the system allows, and verify the peak is well within the transistor's rating.

::: assumptions
The page treats motors and solenoids in a general engineering context (hobby up to light industrial). Numeric hazard values (pinch force, energies) are illustrative. Legal and standards requirements depend on product type and region; safety-critical designs need risk assessment and the applicable standards, not just this checklist. For brushless motors, the back-driving hazard is greater than for brushed motors with a high-resistance winding; for high-voltage drives (hundreds of volts) the shock and capacitor hazards dominate. Solenoid hazards differ slightly: sudden impact and pinch at the plunger, coil heating, mains shock for AC types.

::: followups
### What stops a motor from starting by itself when the MCU resets?
A pulldown on the gate (or an enable pin default state) so the driver is off while pins float; a driver with enable that requires an active signal; and a power-up sequence in which the MCU enables the power stage only after initialisation. A watchdog should also reset the output stage if firmware hangs.

### How would you add an emergency stop?
Put a hard-wired contact that removes power to the power stage (or disables the driver's safe-torque-off input) independent of software. For a drive, the E-stop opens STO inputs and a contactor. The controller is told the E-stop happened, but does not need to command the stop. Add a brake if the load can coast or fall.

### How much energy is in a small rotor and why does it matter?
$E = \tfrac12J\omega^2$. For $J = 2\times10^{-4}$ kg·m² at 3000 rpm ($\omega = 314$ rad/s) that is 9.9 J, comparable to dropping a 1 kg mass by 1 m. It matters because it keeps spinning after power is cut, and can eject debris if a part fails.

### What are the dangers of a solenoid specifically?
Fast plunger impact (pinch, noise, wear), coil heating if held on, inductive kick at release, mains shock for AC types, and force that can be greater than expected when energised. Use peak-and-hold, clamp the kick, enclose mains parts and keep guards over pinch points.

### Harder: your robot arm drops when the E-stop is pressed. How do you fix it?
The E-stop removed motor power, so nothing holds the load. Add a spring-applied, power-off-to-brake holding brake on each vertical axis, or a counterbalance, and make the drive's stopping mode controlled (dynamic braking) so it comes to rest instead of free-falling. The design must be validated against the risk assessment, since a falling arm may itself be the hazard.
:::
