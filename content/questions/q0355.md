::: testing
Whether you can separate the *kinds* of motor noise (electrical/EMI, supply-rail and ground disturbance, acoustic and vibration) and tie each to its physical cause and its fix. A weak answer says "add a capacitor". A strong one explains that brush commutation interrupts inductive current ($L\,di/dt$, arcing), that the broadband noise couples conductively and by radiation into your MCU, ADC and radios, and then gives layered remedies: at the source (caps, chokes, snubbers), in the path (filtering, routing, grounding, shielding) and at the victim (decoupling, filtering, firmware), plus the acoustic side (PWM frequency, bearings, mounting).
:::

::: why
Motors are the loudest things on most boards. They reset microcontrollers, corrupt ADC readings and I²C buses, jam GPS and Bluetooth, and cause EMC test failures. They are also audibly noisy. Interviewers ask this because it needs real-world experience: it separates people who have wired a motor next to a sensor from those who have only simulated it. The same ideas (source, path, victim; local decoupling; return current) apply to every noisy load.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductive switching**: interrupting current through $L$ produces $v = L\,di/dt$. See [[q0340]].
- **Arcing at brushes**: commutation breaks inductive current across a tiny gap. See [[q0356]].
- **Capacitor impedance and decoupling**: local capacitors supply high-frequency current. See [[q0032]].
- **Parasitics**: real capacitors and wires have ESL/ESR; ferrite beads are lossy inductors at high frequency.
- **Common-mode vs differential noise**: noise on both leads relative to ground vs between them.
- **Ground and return paths**: noise currents return through the shared ground; layout decides who sees it.
:::

::: answer
"There are two kinds of motor noise: electrical and acoustic, and the electrical one is usually what causes trouble.

For a brushed motor, the electrical noise comes mostly from commutation. As the brushes slide from one commutator segment to the next, they interrupt the current in a coil that has inductance, and an inductor resists a change in current, so $v = L\,di/dt$ spikes and the brushes arc. That makes broadband, fast, spiky noise, from kilohertz up into hundreds of megahertz, on the motor leads, and the leads act like antennas. On top of that you get the large current steps on the supply when the motor starts, PWM edges and ripple, and ground bounce if the motor current shares a return with sensitive circuitry.

To reduce it: at the source, I'd put a ceramic capacitor, about 100 nanofarads, across the motor terminals, and often two smaller ones from each terminal to the motor case, soldered right on the motor. A series ferrite bead or inductor on each lead makes an LC filter. In the path: twisted or shielded leads, short wires, a star ground, separate motor and logic returns, and a bulk capacitor at the driver. At the victim: local decoupling and filtering on sensitive analog lines, and maybe software debounce.

Acoustic noise is different: bearings, cogging, brush friction and PWM whine. I'd raise PWM above 20 kHz, use a better or skewed motor and soft-mount it."
:::

::: technical
**Sources (brushed DC motor).**
1. **Commutation arcing and ringing.** When a brush leaves a segment, coil current of the shorted coil must reverse in the commutation time $t_c$; the induced voltage $L_c\,di/dt$ (plus the broken-contact transient) creates sparks. The energy $\tfrac12L_cI^2$ is dumped into a tiny gap and the parasitic capacitance: with $L_c = 50$ µH and $I = 0.5$ A, $E = 6.25$ µJ. The voltage swing is $\Delta V = I\sqrt{L/C}$: 1.1 kV if only 10 pF of parasitic capacitance absorbs it (so the gap breaks down and arcs), 112 V with 1 nF, 11 V with 100 nF. The spectrum extends into the VHF range.
2. **Commutation frequency.** Segments × speed: 6 segments at 6000 rpm gives 600 Hz fundamental of the repeated events, with harmonics.
3. **PWM drive**: voltage edges with kV/µs-class $dv/dt$ couple capacitively; current ripple $\Delta I = V D(1-D)/(Lf)$ (0.15 A p-p for 12 V, 50%, 1 mH, 20 kHz).
4. **Supply disturbances**: start-up and load steps; $\Delta V = I\cdot ESR + L\,di/dt$ (e.g. 5 A step: 0.1 V across 20 mΩ, plus 2.5 V across 500 nH at 5 A/µs).
5. **Ground bounce** when the motor return shares impedance with logic or sensor return.

**Acoustic/vibration sources.** Bearing noise, brush friction/squeal, cogging and torque ripple (slot/pole interaction), commutation ripple at (segments × rpm/60), rotor imbalance, PWM whine at the switching frequency (audible below ~20 kHz) and housing resonances.

**Suppression network (motor side).**
- $C_1$ across terminals (typically 10 nF–100 nF ceramic X7R/X5R rated above the supply) shunts the brush noise (differential mode).
- $C_2, C_3$ (1–10 nF each, from each terminal to the motor case) shunt common-mode noise; the can is bonded to chassis/ground.
- Series ferrite beads or a small inductor (a few µH to tens of µH) in each lead with $C_1$ forms an LC low-pass: $f_c = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$; 10 µH and 100 nF give 159 kHz and $Z_0 = 10\ \Omega$.
- Optional: varistor or TVS across the terminals for the large spikes; RC snubber (e.g. 10–47 Ω with 10–100 nF) damps resonances.

**System-side.** Bulk capacitor (100–1000 µF) plus 100 nF ceramics at the driver, short loops, twisted pair motor leads, star ground, shield the motor cable if needed, keep sensor traces away from motor wires, and filter ADC inputs.
:::

::: long
"I'd split it into electrical noise and acoustic noise, since they have different causes and different fixes.

Electrical first, because it breaks things. In a brushed motor, as the brushes slide from one commutator segment to the next, they interrupt the current in a coil, and that coil has inductance. An inductor won't let current change instantly, so you get $v = L\,di/dt$ spikes, and the brush and commutator arc. The energy in a 50 microhenry coil at half an amp is only about 6 microjoules, but dumped into a tiny gap in nanoseconds it makes volts-per-nanosecond edges. That's broadband noise from kilohertz to hundreds of megahertz, and the motor leads radiate it like an antenna and conduct it onto the supply. On top of this there's PWM switching noise, current steps at start-up, which dip the supply, and ground bounce if the motor current returns through the same copper as my sensors.

The cure is to attack it at the source, the path and the victim. At the source, I solder a 100 nanofarad ceramic right across the motor terminals, and two small ones, a few nanofarads, from each terminal to the case, which is the standard three-capacitor fix. Add a ferrite bead or a few microhenries in each lead and the capacitor makes an LC low-pass, with a corner around 150 kilohertz for 10 microhenries and 100 nanofarads. In the path: short, twisted leads, maybe a shield, a bulk capacitor at the driver, and a star ground where motor and logic grounds meet at one point. At the victim: local decoupling and RC filters on sensitive analog inputs, and firmware checks.

For acoustic noise: PWM whine goes away if I switch above 20 kilohertz, though that raises switching losses. Cogging and bearing noise are fixed by a better motor, such as skewed slots or more segments, and by soft-mounting. And of course going brushless removes the brushes entirely."
:::

::: terms
- **Commutation noise**: broadband noise from the brush-commutator contact breaking and making current.
- **Arcing**: gas ionisation across a small gap when interrupting current or on breakdown. See [[q0356]].
- **Differential-mode noise**: noise between the two motor leads.
- **Common-mode noise**: noise appearing equally on both leads relative to ground/case.
- **Ferrite bead**: a lossy inductor that becomes resistive at tens to hundreds of MHz.
- **Y-capacitor**: capacitor from a line to the chassis/ground, used for common-mode filtering.
- **Snubber**: RC network damping ringing or spikes.
- **Cogging**: torque ripple from magnet-slot interaction.
- **Star ground**: a single point where separate returns join.
- **PWM whine**: audible noise at the switching frequency or its sub-harmonics.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting the capacitor far from the motor.** The leads add inductance and radiate; solder it directly across the motor terminals.
- **Using only one 100 nF cap and ignoring common mode.** Case-to-terminal capacitors handle the common-mode currents.
- **Adding a huge electrolytic and thinking it filters RF.** Electrolytics are inductive and slow; use ceramics for high frequency.
- **Forgetting capacitor voltage rating and DC bias.** X5R/X7R capacitance falls with DC bias; rate ≥ 2× the supply.
- **Sharing the ground return** between the motor and sensors, then blaming the sensor.
- **Raising PWM frequency without checking losses and EMI.** Higher frequency cuts audible noise but raises switching loss and EMI harmonics.
- **Believing a flyback diode cures commutator noise.** It protects the driver from the motor winding's kick, not the brush arcing inside the motor.
:::

::: deep
## Where the noise comes from

A brushed motor is an array of inductors, switched by a mechanical commutator. Every time a brush bridges from segment to segment, the coil attached to those segments is short-circuited for a moment and its current must **reverse** (from $+I$ to $-I$) in the commutation time. For example, with 6 segments at 6000 rpm the commutator turns 100 rev/s and a brush passes a segment every $1/600$ s $\approx 1.7$ ms; the brush bridges for part of that.

The coil has an inductance (tens of µH in small motors) so reversing the current in a short time produces a voltage $L\,di/dt$; the *reactance voltage* is what brushes must overcome, and when the contact breaks before the current has reversed (poor commutation) the current continues through an arc.

**Why arcs make wideband noise.** When a contact opens, the inductor keeps its current; this charges whatever capacitance is across the gap until the voltage reaches the breakdown threshold. With small parasitic $C$ the voltage rises quickly:
$$\Delta V = I\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}}.$$
For $L = 50$ µH, $I = 0.5$ A:

| Capacitance across gap | Peak voltage swing |
|---|---|
| 10 pF (parasitic) | 1118 V (so the gap breaks down and arcs instead) |
| 1 nF | 112 V |
| 100 nF | 11 V |

The arc ignites in nanoseconds and extinguishes, repeated thousands of times per second. A nanosecond edge has spectral content to hundreds of MHz, and a few-cm lead radiates efficiently there. This is the reason a 100 nF cap **at** the motor is so effective: it provides a low-impedance place for the inductive current to go that never lets the voltage reach breakdown. See [[q0356]] for arcing and [[q0340]] for flyback voltage.

## The noise paths and victims

1. **Conducted differential**: noise current in the motor leads and supply. Victim: logic rails, ADC reference, other loads on the supply.
2. **Conducted common-mode**: noise current flowing in both leads in phase and returning through the ground/chassis. Victim: sensitive ground-referenced signals.
3. **Radiated**: motor leads act as an antenna (electric field) and current loops as magnetic antennas; victim: radios (Bluetooth, GPS, FM), nearby high-impedance sensor lines, EMC test limits.
4. **Supply droop**: the start-up and load-step current $\Delta I$ into the supply impedance: $\Delta V = \Delta I\cdot ESR + L\,di/dt$, for example 5 A across 20 mΩ is 0.1 V, but 5 A/µs through 500 nH is 2.5 V. See [[q0345]] for the inrush.
5. **Ground bounce**: motor current through a shared return trace's impedance moves the local ground of the sensor.

## The LC filter on the motor lead

A ferrite bead or small inductor $L$ in series, with $C_1$ across the motor, forms a second-order low-pass. With $L = 10$ µH, $C = 100$ nF: $f_c = 159$ kHz, $Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C} = 10\ \Omega$. Above $f_c$ the noise is attenuated at 40 dB/decade (the motor's own inductance is in series too).

Figure: Above 159 kHz the filter rolls off at 40 dB/decade, so 10 MHz commutation noise is attenuated by roughly 70 dB in theory ($(10\text{ MHz}/159\text{ kHz})^2 \approx 4000$). In practice stray capacitance and inductance of the real components flatten the curve at high frequency, which is why you also add the small case capacitors and keep leads short. With only 1 Ω of damping the filter has $Q = Z_0/R = 10$, so it *amplifies* noise near $f_c$ by about 20 dB (the peak above); the bead's own resistance or an RC snubber across $C_1$ lowers that peak.

> [!NOTE]
> Ferrite beads are lossy: at high frequency they look resistive (absorbing noise) rather than purely reactive, making them well damped. At DC they must carry the full motor current without saturating, so check the bead's rated current (and its DC-bias derating).

## Component choices

- **C1**: 10–100 nF ceramic, X7R, ≥ 50 V for a 12 V system, leads as short as possible (even a 5 mm lead has about 5 nH, which matters at 100 MHz).
- **C2/C3**: 1–10 nF ceramic from each terminal to the case. Safety-rated Y capacitors if the motor is mains-connected or the case can be touched; low value to limit leakage.
- **Series L or bead**: sized for rated current and low DC resistance.
- **TVS or varistor** across the motor for supply transients on automotive systems.
- **RC snubber** (e.g. 22 Ω + 47 nF) across the terminals if a resonance rings.
- **Ground the motor case** to chassis (or to a quiet ground) to give common-mode currents a defined return.

## PWM and supply-side noise

The H-bridge adds its own noise: fast edges (tens of ns) couple through the parasitic capacitance of the motor leads, and the current ripple is $\Delta I = V D(1-D)/(Lf_{sw})$: for 12 V at 50% with 1 mH it is 0.15 A p-p at 20 kHz and 0.75 A p-p at 4 kHz. A larger ripple means more heating (extra $I_{rms}^2R$) and audible singing. Slowing the edges with a gate resistor reduces EMI at a modest switching loss.

At the supply: a 100–1000 µF bulk capacitor (low ESR) next to the bridge plus 100 nF ceramics at each bridge half, and a **star ground** (motor return and logic return meet at a single point at the supply). Run motor wires as a **twisted pair** so the field from outgoing and returning current cancels; shielded cable with the shield bonded at the motor end (or both ends for RF) if radiated emissions fail.

## Acoustic and mechanical noise

| Source | Typical frequency | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Brush friction/squeal | broadband, kHz | softer grade, correct spring force ([[q0362]]) |
| Commutation ripple | segments × rev/s (e.g. 600 Hz) | more segments, skewed slots |
| Cogging | slots × rev/s | skewed magnets/slots, coreless motor |
| Bearings | kHz, grows with wear | ball bearings, preload, lubrication |
| PWM whine | $f_{sw}$ or sub-harmonics | $f_{sw}$ > 20 kHz, higher $L$ for lower ripple |
| Housing resonance | 100 Hz–several kHz | soft mount, stiffen or damp the chassis |
| Gearbox | tooth-mesh frequency | helical/plastic gears, grease |

Changing PWM from 4 kHz to 20+ kHz removes the whine but multiplies switching loss by 5× for the same transition time, and EMI harmonics move into a band where filters are less effective. It is a trade-off, usually resolved at 20–30 kHz for small motors.

## The structural fix: brushless

A BLDC has no brush arcing at all; its noise is PWM switching, which is easier to control. It also avoids brush wear ([[q0360]]) and provides smooth torque. If a design cannot tolerate brush noise (a radio or sensitive analog measurement nearby), moving to BLDC is the clean solution ([[q0366]]). For brushed motors, precious-metal-brush motors and more segments reduce, but never eliminate, noise.

## Debugging approach

1. **Look at the symptom with a scope**: probe the supply at the driver with a short ground spring; motor off, then on. Look for spikes (brush noise), ripple (PWM), sags (inrush).
2. **Disable pieces**: run the motor from a battery to see if the noise is conducted through the supply or radiated.
3. **Add the suppression network** at the motor, re-measure.
4. **Check grounds**: measure voltage between sensor ground and motor ground while the motor runs (a few mV is fine, volts are not).
5. **Pre-compliance scan** with a near-field probe and a spectrum analyser.
:::

::: applications
- **Robotics and drones with brushed micro-motors**: three-capacitor fix to keep IMU and radio readings clean.
- **Automotive motors (windows, wipers, fans)**: filtering to meet radiated-emissions limits and keep the AM/FM radio quiet.
- **Consumer appliances and tools**: mains-side Y-capacitors and chokes in universal-motor products (hair dryers, drills) to pass conducted emissions.
- **Medical and lab instruments**: motors near sensitive analog inputs need shielding, filtering and separate grounds.
- **3D printers and CNC controllers**: stepper/brushed spindle noise coupling into endstop and thermistor lines.
- **PWM fan and pump controllers**: frequency above audible and edge control to avoid whine and EMI.
:::

- **Brushed DC motor** as in the section, so commutation arcing is the dominant electrical noise. For BLDC and stepper motors the dominant noise is PWM switching, not arcing.
- **Typical component values** (100 nF, 4.7 nF, 10 µH) are starting points; the final values come from EMC measurement.

- **Audible threshold** ~20 kHz for most adults, but some people hear above that, and subharmonics of higher PWM frequencies can be audible.
- Alternative answer: focus on a specific symptom (e.g. MCU resets) and fix that path first (supply decoupling, ground).

::: followups
### Why across the terminals *and* to the case?
Across the terminals is differential mode: the arc current circulates in the loop of the two terminals. Case-to-terminal capacitors handle common-mode noise that uses the case and chassis as the return. A single capacitor can't do both, and with only the differential one, common-mode currents still radiate from the leads.

### Why a ceramic and not a big electrolytic?
Electrolytics have high ESL/ESR, so at 10–100 MHz they are inductive and useless. A 100 nF ceramic's self-resonance is around 10–20 MHz with short leads; paralleling it with a smaller (e.g. 1–10 nF) one extends coverage. Keep leads short because 10 nH of lead inductance forms the filter's limit.

### Your ADC readings jump when the motor starts. What do you check?
First the supply and reference: capture the rail with a scope at the ADC. If it dips or spikes at start-up, add bulk and decoupling at the driver, soften the start with PWM ramping ([[q0345]]), and separate the analog supply with an LC or ferrite filter. Then ground: a shared return trace carrying motor current moves the ADC ground. Finally, the radiated path: shield the sensor line, add an RC filter near the ADC pin, and average in firmware.

### How does raising the PWM frequency to 25 kHz change noise and losses?
It removes the audible whine, reduces current ripple ($\propto1/f$), and allows smaller filter components. But switching loss scales with $f$ (proportional), and EMI harmonics increase in the MHz range; slower edges (gate resistor) and filtering compensate. So you trade quiet operation for higher loss and EMC effort.

### What if the problem is mostly acoustic hum, not electrical?
Identify the frequency with a microphone or phone spectrum app: PWM whine at the switching frequency; cogging at slots × rev/s; bearing noise at bearing defect frequencies; gearbox at tooth-mesh frequency. Then address it directly: move the PWM frequency, soft-mount the motor, add damping, change the gear type or the motor.

### Would a flyback diode across the motor reduce brush noise?
Not meaningfully. A flyback diode clamps the whole motor's current when the *driver switch* turns off, protecting the driver. The brush arcing happens inside the motor between segments, and the diode's lead inductance and forward recovery are too slow for nanosecond events. Capacitors and filters at the terminals are the fix.
:::

::: interpretations
"Why are motors so noisy?" is ambiguous between *electrical noise* (EMI, supply noise) and *acoustic noise*. In a hardware-engineering interview the electrical meaning is the main target, but the safest answer covers both briefly and says which you are emphasising. "Motors" here means brushed DC motors, as in the surrounding section. See also [[q0356]] (arcing), [[q0359]] (brush concerns) and [[q0357]] (what brushes do).
