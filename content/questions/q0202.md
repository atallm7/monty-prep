::: testing
Whether you understand **inductor current continuity** well enough to predict what the circuit does when you take its safety valve away. The interviewer wants:

1. The physics: an inductor's current cannot change instantly, so when the high-side switch opens the current must find a new path.
2. The role of the diode: provide that path, clamping the switch node near ground instead of letting it fly.
3. The consequence of removal in numbers: $v_L = L\,di/dt$ becomes huge, so SW slams negative until something avalanches, forward-biases or breaks.
4. Awareness of the sync case: if the "diode" is really the low-side FET's body diode, removing an *external* Schottky is a very different (mild) story.
:::

::: why
This is the same physics as a relay coil kicking back when you open the switch, which makes it one of the most transferable ideas in hardware: motor drivers, solenoids, relay drivers and every switching regulator. In the Power Bridge section the diode question shows up again because the answer depends on the bridge: in an asynchronous buck the diode is mandatory; in a synchronous buck the low-side FET (and its body diode) plays that role. Interviewers like it because a candidate who says "the diode is a freewheel diode" but cannot say *what goes wrong without it* has only memorized the name. The related question [[q0197]] in the circuit-analysis section asks the same thing without the bridge context.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor current continuity**: $v_L = L\,di/dt$. An instant change in current would need infinite voltage, so the current persists ([[q0044]]).
- **Freewheeling**: after the high-side switch turns off, the inductor current continues through a ground-referenced path to SW.
- **Switch node voltage**: with a diode, SW clamps to about $-V_f$; without it, SW is free to swing.
- **Avalanche / clamp**: devices rated for breakdown absorb the inductor energy at the expense of heat and wear; see [[q0199]].
- **Body diode**: every MOSFET has one; in a sync bridge it can take over the diode job in dead time ([[q0208]]).
:::

::: answer
"The diode gives the inductor current somewhere to go. When the high-side switch turns off, the inductor current is still flowing, say 2 A, because an inductor won't let its current jump. The inductor's voltage is $L\,di/dt$, so it will drive its input end, the switch node, to whatever voltage it takes to keep that current going. The diode, with its anode at ground and cathode at the switch node, turns on as soon as SW falls about 0.4 V below ground, and it carries the inductor current while the energy ramps down into the output.

If you remove it, nothing on the board provides that path. The switch node capacitance, a few hundred picofarads, gets the full 2 A, so SW falls at something like 10 V per nanosecond until a device breaks over. That's the FET avalanching, an IC pin's ESD structure conducting, or something failing. Each cycle you dump a big chunk of the inductor energy, $\tfrac12 L I^2$, into that device. In my 12 V to 3.3 V example that's about 15 microjoules a cycle, which at 500 kHz is several watts in a part that was never sized for it. The converter will stop regulating and probably die.

In a synchronous bridge the body diode of the low-side FET is already there, so removing an extra Schottky just costs a little efficiency."
:::

::: technical
**Cause.** For an ideal inductor $v_L = v_{SW} - V_{out} = L\,\dfrac{di_L}{dt}$. At the instant $Q_1$ opens, $i_L = I_{pk}$ and must remain continuous.

**With the diode.** $D_1$ conducts when $v_{SW} < -V_f$. Then $v_L = -(V_{out}+V_f)$, so $i_L$ ramps down at $\dfrac{V_{out}+V_f}{L}$. For $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $V_f=0.45$ V, $L=4.7$ µH the slope is $0.80$ A/µs. Loss: $P_D \approx (1-D)\,I_{out}V_f = 0.65$ W.

**Without the diode (no low-side FET either).** Current has no conducting path. The only elements connected to SW are capacitances ($C_{oss}$ of $Q_1$, pad and trace, inductor interwinding), totalling $C_{sw}$. The slew rate is $\dfrac{dv_{SW}}{dt} = -\dfrac{I_L}{C_{sw}}$. With $I_L = 2.5$ A and $C_{sw}=200$ pF: $-12.5$ V/ns. SW falls until some clamp conducts:
- avalanche of the high-side FET $Q_1$: its drain sits at $V_{in}$ and its source at SW, so $V_{DS} = V_{in} + |v_{SW}|$ and it breaks down when SW reaches about $V_{in} - BV_{DSS}$ (a 40 V part at 12 V in clamps SW near $-30$ V),
- substrate or ESD diodes in the controller IC (parasitic, not designed for amps),
- or $Q_1$ itself if its gate is driven relative to ground (as $v_{SW}$ falls, $V_{GS}$ rises, turning it on in a linear region).

**Energy.** The inductor stores $E = \tfrac12 L I_{pk}^2 = 14.8$ µJ. With SW clamped at $-V_c$, the inductor current ramps down at $(V_c+V_{out})/L$. A ground-referenced clamp (a TVS from SW to ground, say) absorbs the fraction $V_c/(V_c + V_{out})$: for 30 V that is 90%, about 13 µJ per cycle, about 6.7 W average at 500 kHz. If the clamp is $Q_1$ avalanching, it is worse: the input supply also pushes the current through $Q_1$, which sees $V_{in}+V_c = 42$ V, so it absorbs $14.8\times 42/33.3 \approx 19$ µJ per cycle, about 9 W.

> [!WARNING]
> Even one avalanche event per cycle exceeds typical single-pulse avalanche ratings over a lifetime, and the 10 V/ns-class slew also couples into everything nearby. The outcome is not a graceful degradation.

**Synchronous bridge.** The low-side FET's body diode ($V_f \approx 0.7$ to 1.0 V for silicon) plus the FET channel itself provide the path. An external parallel Schottky only reduces dead-time loss ([[q0210]]).
:::

::: short
"The diode is the freewheel path. When the high-side switch opens, the inductor current can't jump, so the switch node swings negative until the diode turns on at about minus 0.4 V and carries the current while it decays. Remove it and the inductor generates whatever voltage it needs, $L\,di/dt$, so SW slams negative at around 10 V per nanosecond until some FET or pin avalanches. That dumps the inductor's stored energy into something that wasn't sized for it, and the converter fails. In a synchronous design, the low-side FET and its body diode do this job."
:::

::: long
"The diode exists because an inductor's current can't change instantly. In a buck, the high-side switch is on, the inductor is carrying, say, 2.5 A, and then the switch opens. The inductor voltage equals $L$ times $di/dt$, so if the current is forced to stop, you'd need infinite voltage. Instead the inductor flips its polarity and drives the switch node negative until something gives the current a place to flow.

The diode is that place. It sits from ground to the switch node. As soon as SW falls about 0.4 V below ground, the diode turns on and carries the inductor current. Now the inductor sees minus $V_{out}$ minus $V_f$ across it, so the current ramps down gently, and that energy goes into the output.

If you remove it, the only thing connected to SW is capacitance, a couple of hundred picofarads, and a few amps flowing into that gives you something like 10 V per nanosecond. SW falls until a clamp conducts: the FET avalanches, the IC's pin diodes conduct, or the gate coupling turns the high-side FET on in the linear region. In my example the inductor holds about 15 microjoules. If a 30 V clamp to ground absorbs 90% of it every cycle at 500 kHz, that's roughly 6 to 7 W in a device that can't take it.

The synchronous case is different: the low-side FET has a body diode, so there is always a path. An external Schottky across it only trims dead-time losses.

And don't confuse it with a load protection diode: this one is part of normal operation every cycle, not a rare fault clamp."
:::

::: terms
- **Freewheel (flyback) diode**: the diode that carries inductor current when the switch is off.
- **Inductive kick**: the voltage spike when current through an inductor is interrupted ([[q0044]]).
- **Current continuity**: an inductor's current cannot change in zero time.
- **Switch node (SW)**: the node connecting the switches, diode and inductor.
- **Clamp**: a component (diode, avalanche, TVS) that limits a voltage.
- **Avalanche energy ($E_{AS}$)**: the energy a MOSFET can absorb once in breakdown.
- **Forward voltage ($V_f$)**: the diode drop when conducting.
- **Dead time**: the gap between switch off and low-side on during which the diode conducts.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "the diode lets the inductor current continue and prevents a voltage spike."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Starts from $v_L = L\,di/dt$ and says what is forced: the current stays, the voltage is free.
- Gives polarity (diode anode at ground, cathode at SW, SW goes to $-V_f$) and the resulting ramp-down slope $(V_{out}+V_f)/L$.
- Estimates the failure mode with numbers: dv/dt $= I/C_{sw}$, energy $\tfrac12 LI^2$, average power at the switching frequency.
- Names what actually clamps in a real board (FET avalanche, pin ESD/substrate diode, or gate-coupling turn-on).
- Distinguishes the async buck (diode essential) from the sync buck (body diode and low-side FET) and the DCM case.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The diode protects against back-EMF" and stops there.** True but vague; say that the inductor current continues and the diode supplies the path.
- **Getting the orientation wrong.** In a buck the diode is from ground (anode) to SW (cathode): reverse-biased during on-time, forward-biased during off-time. A reversed diode shorts $V_{in}$ to ground each time $Q_1$ turns on.
- **"The spike is limited by the load."** The load's voltage does not clamp SW; only a conducting device does.
- **Thinking removal merely lowers efficiency.** Without any path (no diode, no low-side FET) the failure is dramatic, not gradual.
- **Treating a sync buck like an async one.** If the low-side FET exists, removing a Schottky does not cause a spike: the body diode conducts instead.
:::

::: deep
## The physics

An inductor stores energy $\tfrac12 LI^2$ in its magnetic field. The field cannot vanish in zero time without a spike of voltage:

$$v_L(t) = L\frac{di_L}{dt}$$

In a buck, $v_L = v_{SW} - V_{out}$. During the on-state, $v_{SW}\approx V_{in}$ and the current ramps up. At turn-off, $i_L$ is still positive (current into the output). The inductor will drive $v_{SW}$ as low as needed to keep that current. If nothing conducts, that "as low as needed" is limited only by parasitics.

## What the diode does, quantitatively

While $D_1$ conducts, SW sits at $-V_f$, so the inductor sees $-(V_{out}+V_f)$:

$$\frac{di_L}{dt} = -\frac{V_{out}+V_f}{L}$$

For $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $V_f = 0.45$ V, $L = 4.7$ µH: slope $= -0.80$ A/µs. Over the off time $(1-D)T = 1.45$ µs the current falls by about 1.16 A (ignoring $V_f$'s small effect on the average ripple of 1.02 A computed with an ideal diode). The diode loss is about $(1-D)I_{out}V_f = 0.65$ W.

## What happens with no path

Think of SW as a tiny node with capacitance $C_{sw}$ (the sum of $C_{oss}$ of the FETs, bond pad and inductor winding capacitance). With no conduction path:

$$\frac{dv_{SW}}{dt} = -\frac{i_L}{C_{sw}}$$

With 2.5 A into 200 pF: $-12.5$ V/ns. In about 3 ns SW is 40 V below the input rail. What happens next depends on the actual silicon.

Without it, SW races down until a breakdown path conducts and sits at that breakdown voltage as long as the inductor still has current to push, about 0.35 µs in this example before the current reaches zero.

Typical outcomes, in rough order of likelihood on a real board:

1. **Drain-source avalanche of the high-side FET.** $Q_1$ sees $V_{DS} = V_{in} + |v_{SW}|$, so with a 40 V part at 12 V in it breaks down with SW near $-30$ V. It survives a few single events per its $E_{AS}$ rating, but not 500 000 per second. (A low-side FET, if present, would not avalanche here: its body diode is *forward* biased by a negative SW, which is exactly the synchronous case below.)
2. **Parasitic diodes in a monolithic controller**. Substrate injection can cause latch-up and corrupt the control logic.
3. **Gate coupling**. If the high-side gate driver holds the gate at ground rather than SW, the falling source raises $V_{GS}$ and turns $Q_1$ on as a source follower, burning $(V_{in}+|v_{SW}|)\cdot I$ as heat.

Energy per cycle in the example:

$$E = \tfrac12 L I_{pk}^2 = \tfrac12(4.7\,\mu\text{H})(2.51\,\text{A})^2 = 14.8\,\mu\text{J}$$

If a ground-referenced 30 V clamp conducts, it absorbs $30/(30+3.3) = 90\%$ of that, or 13 µJ, and at 500 kHz that is 6.7 W. If the clamp is $Q_1$ avalanching at about 42 V, the supply adds energy on top and $Q_1$ absorbs about $14.8\times42/33.3 \approx 19$ µJ, about 9 W. A small MOSFET die cannot dissipate either.

## What about the synchronous case?

In a synchronous bridge the low-side FET is driven on during $(1-D)T$ and carries the current with $I\cdot R_{DS(on)}$ drop. Between the high-side turn-off and low-side turn-on (dead time), the inductor current flows through the low-side FET's **body diode**. So you cannot get the dramatic failure by removing an external Schottky; you only lose its benefit. If you remove the low-side FET entirely, you are back to an asynchronous buck with no diode, with the same failure.

> [!TIP]
> When the interviewer says "the diode", ask or state whether you are assuming asynchronous or synchronous. It shows you see the bridge section as one family of designs.

## The reversed diode and the DCM cousin

Two practical variations:

- **Diode reversed:** when $Q_1$ turns on, $V_{in}$ appears across a forward-biased diode to ground. That is a direct short, limited only by $Q_1$ and the supply.
- **DCM:** once the inductor current reaches zero in the off phase, the diode turns off and SW rings at the inductor/parasitic capacitance resonance. See the simulation below.

::: applications
- **Asynchronous buck regulators** (for example, low-cost automotive or industrial modules): external Schottky from ground to SW, rated for the peak inductor current and the input voltage.
- **Relay and solenoid drivers**: the same freewheel diode across the coil prevents the kick that would kill the driver transistor ([[q0044]]).
- **Motor H-bridges**: body diodes or external diodes freewheel the winding current when a switch turns off.
- **Boost and flyback converters**: the output diode (or synchronous FET) plays the equivalent role for the other half of the cycle.
- **Sync bucks with a Schottky "booster"**: some designs add an external Schottky across the low-side FET to cut dead-time and reverse-recovery losses.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Asynchronous buck** with an ideal inductor and a conducting diode path of $V_f \approx 0.3$ to 0.5 V (Schottky). A PN-junction diode is 0.7 V or more and slower.
- **$C_{sw}$ of 200 pF** is typical for an amp-class buck, but varies by orders of magnitude with device size and layout.
- **Clamp voltages** (30 V avalanche) are illustrative; the real value depends on the transistor's breakdown rating.
- **Gate-drive reference**: the "gate coupling" outcome only applies to a ground-referenced gate drive, not the usual bootstrapped driver referenced to SW.
- The energy fraction $V_c/(V_c+V_{out})$ assumes the clamp voltage is constant during the decay.
- If the interviewer means a **sync bridge**, the correct answer is that the body diode and low-side FET take over and removal of a separate diode is a minor efficiency hit.
:::

::: followups
### Why a Schottky diode and not an ordinary silicon diode?
A Schottky has a lower forward drop (about 0.3 to 0.5 V vs 0.7 to 1 V) and essentially no reverse-recovery charge, because it is a majority-carrier device. At 2 A and $1-D = 0.725$, 0.45 V vs 0.8 V saves roughly 0.5 W in the example, and removing recovery spikes cuts EMI. The cost is higher leakage, especially hot, and lower voltage ratings.

### How would you rate the diode?
Reverse voltage rating above $V_{in}$ plus ringing margin (for 12 V in, a 30 V or 40 V part). Average current rating above $(1-D)\,I_{out}$ and peak-current rating above $I_{pk}$. Check thermal dissipation $\approx (1-D)I_{out}V_f$ and the package's thermal resistance.

### What if the diode is removed but there is a low-side FET driven complementary?
Then it is a synchronous buck and works. During dead time the body diode conducts, so the loss is a bit higher than with a Schottky across it. The extreme case is dead time much longer than needed, where the body-diode loss $V_{SD}\,I\,t_{dead} f_{sw}$ becomes noticeable.

### What if the diode is installed backwards?
When $Q_1$ turns on, SW goes to $V_{in}$ and the reversed diode (anode at SW) conducts straight to ground. A short through $Q_1$ and the supply: expect a tripped current limit, a blown FET, or a hot board. It is a classic assembly-fault symptom and why polarity markings matter.

### A prototype buck loses its high-side FET every time you power it. The diode is present. What else could it be?
Look at SW with a high-bandwidth, short-ground probe. If SW undershoots well below $-V_f$ or overshoots $V_{in}$ by a lot, the diode's loop inductance and reverse recovery are the issue: a Schottky with a long lead has $L\,di/dt$ drops of volts. Also check that the diode's reverse voltage rating and the FET's $V_{DS}$ margin cover the ringing.
:::
