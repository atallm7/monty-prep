::: testing
Whether you can **draw and explain the inductor current waveform** in PFM and connect it to the control action. Interviewers look for:

1. The shape: isolated triangular pulses that start and end at zero, with long idle intervals of zero current.
2. The numbers: slopes $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ and $-V_{out}/L$, a fixed peak that does not depend on load, and a pulse rate that does.
3. What happens as load changes: pulses move closer together, become back to back (boundary conduction) near $I_{pk}/2$, and the converter hands over to PWM/CCM.
4. The side-effects: high peak-to-average ratio, RMS current, saturation margin, overshoot from delay, ringing at zero current.
:::

::: why
The inductor current *is* the converter. Efficiency, ripple, EMI and component stress are all read from that waveform. In PFM the waveform looks nothing like the CCM triangle most people draw, and getting it right is a quick signal that you actually understand the mode. It also determines inductor selection: saturation current has to cover the PFM peak plus overshoot, and the RMS and peak numbers differ a lot from the average. See [[q0231]] for the control cycle and [[q0235]] for the capacitor side.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor volt-second law**: $di/dt=v_L/L$, so the current ramps linearly with slopes set by $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$ and $L$. See [[q0188]].
- **Diode emulation / zero-current detection**: the low-side turns off at $i_L=0$ so the current cannot go negative. See [[q0229]].
- **Charge per pulse and pulse rate**: $I_{out}=Q f_{pulse}$ with $Q=\frac12I_{pk}(t_{on}+t_{off})$.
- **Peak vs average vs RMS current**: for a narrow triangle, peak ≫ average; RMS lies between.
- **Boundary (critical) conduction**: pulses run back to back, so the current is a sawtooth from 0 to $I_{pk}$ with average $I_{pk}/2$.
- **Saturation current**: the DC bias at which $L$ drops significantly. See [[q0259]].
:::

::: answer
"In PFM the inductor current isn't the continuous triangle you see in CCM. It's a train of separate triangular pulses with the current sitting at zero in between.

Each pulse starts at zero. The high-side FET turns on and the current ramps up at $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ until it hits the fixed peak, say 0.6 A for my 12 to 3.3 V, 4.7 µH example, which takes about 324 nanoseconds. Then the high side turns off, the low side or diode takes over, and the current ramps down at $V_{out}/L$ back to zero, about 855 nanoseconds. A zero-current detector turns the low side off at zero, so the current doesn't reverse. Then nothing happens, the inductor current is zero and the output capacitor feeds the load, until the output droops and the next pulse starts.

What changes with load is how often the pulses come, not what they look like. The peak is fixed. At 20 mA there's a pulse every 17 µs. As the load rises the gaps shrink, and around half the peak current, 0.3 A here, the pulses run back to back, a sawtooth between zero and the peak. Above that the converter should hand over to PWM.

Consequences: the peak-to-average ratio is huge, the average over time is the load current but the pulse peak is 30 times higher at 20 mA, so the inductor's saturation current has to cover the peak, not the load."
:::

::: technical
**Waveform.** Rise: $\frac{di}{dt}=\frac{V_{in}-V_{out}}{L}=1.85$ A/µs for $t_{on}=\frac{LI_{pk}}{V_{in}-V_{out}}=324$ ns. Fall: $-\frac{V_{out}}{L}=-0.70$ A/µs for $t_{off}=\frac{LI_{pk}}{V_{out}}=855$ ns. Idle at 0 A for the remainder of $T_p=Q/I_{out}$.

**Charge and rate.** $Q=\tfrac12I_{pk}(t_{on}+t_{off})=0.354$ µC, so $f_{pulse}=I_{out}/Q$: 2.8 kHz at 1 mA, 28 kHz at 10 mA, 57 kHz at 20 mA, 283 kHz at 100 mA, and the limit $1/(t_{on}+t_{off})=848$ kHz at $I_{out}\approx I_{pk}/2=0.3$ A.

**RMS.** $I_{rms}=I_{pk}\sqrt{\frac{t_{on}+t_{off}}{3T_p}}$: 0.089 A at 20 mA (pulse duty 6.7%); 0.063 A at 10 mA.

**Energy.** Peak stored energy $\tfrac12LI_{pk}^2=0.85$ µJ, delivered to the output in the off-time; during the on-time another $V_{out}\cdot\tfrac12I_{pk}t_{on}=0.32$ µJ goes directly to the output; total $QV_{out}=1.17$ µJ per pulse (lossless).

**Delay overshoot.** With comparator/driver delay $t_d=50$ ns, the actual peak is $I_{pk}+t_d(V_{in}-V_{out})/L=0.69$ A, and $Q$ rises 33% to 0.47 µC.

**Constant on-time variant.** $I_{pk}=(V_{in}-V_{out})t_{on}/L$ scales with $V_{in}$: 0.12 A at 5 V, 0.60 A at 12 V, 1.43 A at 24 V for a fixed 324 ns.
:::

::: short
"In PFM the inductor current is a train of triangular pulses with zero current in between. Each pulse ramps up at $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ to a fixed peak, ramps back down at $V_{out}/L$ to zero, where the low side shuts off. Then the inductor sits idle while the capacitor feeds the load. The pulse shape doesn't change with load, only how often they occur. Peak current is much higher than the average, and near half the peak they run back to back and the converter hands over to PWM."
:::

::: long
"I'll use 12 V in, 3.3 V out, 4.7 µH, and a 0.6 A peak.

One pulse starts from zero current. The high-side FET turns on and the inductor sees $V_{in}-V_{out}=8.7$ V, so the current ramps at 1.85 amps per microsecond. It reaches the 0.6 A peak in about 324 ns. Then the high side turns off and the low side, or a diode, takes over. Now the inductor sees minus $V_{out}$, so the current falls at 0.70 amps per microsecond, which takes about 855 ns to reach zero. A zero-current detector turns the low side off at that point so the current can't go negative. The whole pulse lasts 1.18 µs.

After that the inductor current is zero and the converter sleeps, with the output capacitor feeding the load, until the output falls below the threshold and the next pulse starts.

What changes with load is the pulse spacing. Each pulse delivers 0.354 microcoulombs, so a 20 mA load needs one every 17.7 µs, 57 kHz. At 100 mA, one every 3.5 µs. At about 0.3 A, half the peak, the gap closes completely: the current is a sawtooth between zero and 0.6 A, with no idle time, boundary conduction. Beyond that, PFM can't deliver more, and the converter should be in PWM.

A few practical consequences. The peak is 30 times the average at 20 mA, so the inductor's saturation rating must cover the peak plus overshoot from comparator delay, which adds about 0.09 A per 50 ns, not just the load. The RMS current is about 90 mA at 20 mA load, not 20 mA. And if the zero-current detector were missing, the current would go negative and the mode wouldn't be efficient. In a constant on-time version the peak scales with the input voltage, so a 24 V input would give a 1.4 A peak for the same on-time."
:::

::: terms
- **Pulse**: one triangle of inductor current from zero up to $I_{pk}$ and back.
- **Idle (sleep) interval**: the time with $i_L=0$ and both FETs off.
- **Peak current ($I_{pk}$)**: the fixed threshold at which the on-time ends (peak-current PFM).
- **Zero-current detection (ZCD)**: stops the off-time at $i_L=0$.
- **Boundary conduction (BCM/CRM)**: pulses back to back, with no idle time.
- **Peak-to-average ratio**: $I_{pk}/I_{out}$, large in PFM at light load.
- **Delay overshoot**: extra peak current from comparator and gate-driver propagation delay.
- **Saturation current ($I_{sat}$)**: the DC current at which inductance drops by a specified percentage (often 20 to 30%).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it's discontinuous, goes to zero between pulses".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Describes **one pulse with numbers**: slopes, $t_{on}$ and $t_{off}$, the fixed peak, the zero-current stop.
- Shows that **pulse shape is load-independent** and only the pulse spacing varies, giving $f=I_{out}/Q$.
- Describes the **progression with load**: isolated pulses to back-to-back at $I_{pk}/2$ to PWM/CCM.
- Quotes **RMS and peak-to-average** and what they mean for inductor selection (saturation, heating).
- Mentions delay overshoot, ZCD, constant-on-time vs peak-current differences, and the audible-noise consequence.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Drawing the CCM triangle on a DC offset.** In PFM there is no DC offset; the current returns to zero each pulse.
- **Choosing the inductor saturation rating from the load current.** It must exceed the *peak* (0.6 A, plus overshoot), not the 20 mA average.
- **Thinking the peak varies with load.** In peak-current PFM it is fixed. (In a constant on-time scheme it varies with $V_{in}$, not load.)
- **Forgetting zero-current detection.** Without it the current would reverse and discharge the output.
- **Using $I_{rms}=I_{avg}$.** RMS for a narrow pulse is much larger (0.09 A vs 0.02 A at 20 mA), which sets the copper loss.
- **Ignoring that pulses run back to back at $I_{pk}/2$.** That is the upper limit of PFM output current.
:::

## One pulse

With $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $L=4.7$ µH:

- On-state: $v_L=V_{in}-V_{out}=8.7$ V, so $\frac{di}{dt}=1.851$ A/µs.
- Off-state: $v_L=-V_{out}=-3.3$ V, so $\frac{di}{dt}=-0.702$ A/µs.

## How the waveform changes with load

The pulse shape is fixed; the interval between pulses is $T_p=Q/I_{out}$, which gives the pulse rate and the idle fraction $1-\frac{1.179\text{ µs}}{T_p}$:

| Load | Pulse spacing $T_p$ | Pulse rate | Idle fraction | $I_{rms}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 mA | 354 µs | 2.8 kHz | 99.7% | 0.020 A |
| 10 mA | 35.4 µs | 28 kHz | 96.7% | 0.063 A |
| 20 mA | 17.7 µs | 57 kHz | 93.3% | 0.089 A |
| 100 mA | 3.54 µs | 283 kHz | 66.7% | 0.200 A |
| 0.3 A | 1.18 µs | 848 kHz | 0% (back to back) | 0.346 A |

(The rms values come from $I_{pk}\sqrt{(t_{on}+t_{off})/(3T_p)}$.)

Figure: In a 10 µs window there is one pulse at 20 mA (bottom, blue), three at 100 mA (green, offset by 0.8 A) and a continuous sawtooth at 300 mA (red, offset by 1.6 A) where pulses run back to back and the inductor never rests. Every pulse has the same 0.6 A height and the same 1.18 µs width. Only the spacing changes.

At the back-to-back point the converter is in boundary conduction mode with average current $I_{pk}/2=0.3$ A and its frequency, 848 kHz, is set by $L$, $I_{pk}$, $V_{in}$ and $V_{out}$. Real controllers hand over to fixed-frequency PWM well before this, using a load or frequency threshold with hysteresis, because the pulse rate would exceed the PWM frequency (500 kHz here) at about 177 mA.

## Peak, RMS and energy

At 20 mA the pulse covers 6.7% of the time. The time-average of the current is the load (20 mA), but the peak is 0.6 A (30×), and the RMS is $0.6\sqrt{0.0667/3}=0.089$ A, so copper loss is $I_{rms}^2R_{DCR}$: with 10 mΩ, $0.089^2\cdot0.010=0.08$ mW. Fine, but it is 20× what you would compute from the average current.

Energy per pulse (lossless): the inductor stores $\tfrac12LI_{pk}^2=0.85$ µJ, all of which goes to the output during the off-time. During the on-time the source also supplies $V_{out}\cdot\tfrac12I_{pk}t_{on}=0.32$ µJ directly to the output, so the output receives $1.17$ µJ$=Q\cdot V_{out}$, and the input supplies the same amount $V_{in}\cdot\tfrac12I_{pk}t_{on}=1.17$ µJ. The efficiency of a pulse is high; the loss is overhead (gate charge and so on), not the energy conversion.

## Saturation, tolerance and overshoot

- **Saturation.** $I_{sat}$ must exceed $I_{pk}$ plus overshoot with margin. For the 0.6 A design, a part with $I_{sat}\ge1.5$ A is ample; the converter's full-load CCM peak (2.5 A in this example) will normally dominate selection ([[q0259]]). If $L$ droops 30% at the peak (soft saturation), the on-time to reach 0.6 A shortens to 227 ns, charge per pulse falls and the pulse rate rises.
- **Delay overshoot.** A 50 ns comparator + driver delay adds $50\text{ ns}\times1.85\text{ A/µs}=0.09$ A, so the true peak is 0.69 A and the charge per pulse rises from 0.354 to 0.47 µC (+33%). Pulse rate falls accordingly, and ripple rises ($\propto Q$). The overshoot grows with $V_{in}$, which is why PFM peak current varies with input voltage in real parts.
- **Inductance tolerance.** ±20% in $L$ moves $t_{on}$ in a peak-current scheme (current slope changes), but the peak stays fixed; in a constant on-time scheme the peak changes by ∓20%.

## Constant on-time vs peak-current PFM

If the on-time is fixed ($t_{on}=324$ ns) rather than the peak, $I_{pk}=(V_{in}-V_{out})t_{on}/L$ follows $V_{in}$:

| $V_{in}$ | $I_{pk}$ | $t_{off}$ | $Q$ per pulse |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 V | 0.12 A | 167 ns | 0.029 µC |
| 8 V | 0.32 A | 462 ns | 0.127 µC |
| 12 V | 0.60 A | 855 ns | 0.354 µC |
| 24 V | 1.43 A | 2.03 µs | 1.68 µC |

At 24 V in a constant-on-time design, the peak is 2.4× larger and the charge per pulse nearly 5× larger, so the ripple jumps ($\propto Q$) and the inductor must be rated for it. Peak-current PFM avoids that, which is why many ICs use it. See [[q0233]].

## What if there is no zero-current detection?

The low-side FET stays on after $i_L$ reaches zero and the current continues negative, discharging the output into the input. The waveform becomes a triangle centred on the load current, as in forced CCM ([[q0228]]), with the full ripple at every cycle. The converter is no longer PFM; it just wastes power.

::: applications
- **Phone/wearable PMICs in standby**: the inductor current is mostly zero with occasional sub-µs pulses; designers pick inductors with low DCR and a modest $I_{sat}$ for the peak.
- **IoT buck-boost and buck converters**: peak-current PFM with a programmable peak (for example 0.3 to 1 A) trading ripple against efficiency.
- **Energy harvesting and solar trickle chargers**: boundary/pulsed conduction with the same pulse waveform.
- **Inductor selection for battery devices**: the PFM peak is often what sizes the part, with the inductor audible noise considered when the rate falls into the audio band.
- **Debugging**: a current probe on the inductor shows PFM at a glance: isolated triangles with gaps and a fixed height.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Peak-current PFM** with ZCD, ideal zero delay for the base numbers, lossless pulses, and constant $V_{in}$ and $V_{out}$.
- **Linear inductor** (no saturation) in the main numbers; saturation is discussed separately.
- **Single pulse per trigger** (not burst grouping, in which several pulses are chained).
- **Example values**: 12 V, 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 0.6 A; real ICs set $I_{pk}$ as a fraction of their current limit and may adapt it.
- **RMS formula** assumes isolated, identical pulses.
:::

::: followups
### What is the average inductor current in PFM?
The load current. Over a long time the capacitor's average current is zero, so the average inductor current equals $I_{out}$. For isolated pulses it is $\tfrac12I_{pk}(t_{on}+t_{off})/T_p=Q/T_p$. At 20 mA that is 0.354 µC / 17.7 µs = 20 mA.

### What happens to the inductor current if the load increases to about 0.3 A?
The pulses run back to back: the idle interval disappears and the current becomes a sawtooth from 0 to 0.6 A at 848 kHz, with average 0.3 A. That is the PFM limit; above it the converter must switch to PWM, where the current no longer returns to zero (CCM) for loads above about 0.5 A in this example.

### How would you choose the inductor saturation rating for a PFM design?
Take the larger of the PFM peak (plus overshoot: 0.69 A here) and the full-load PWM peak ($I_{out}+\Delta I_L/2=2.5$ A), then add margin (20 to 30%) and consider the temperature derating of $I_{sat}$. In most converters the full-load PWM peak dominates, but a converter designed for a small load and PFM (a 0.3 A part) is sized by the PFM peak.

### Why is the falling slope shallower than the rising slope?
The slope is $v_L/L$. Rising: $v_L=V_{in}-V_{out}=8.7$ V. Falling: $v_L=-V_{out}=-3.3$ V. The ratio of slopes equals $(V_{in}-V_{out})/V_{out}=2.64$, so $t_{off}/t_{on}=2.64$ (855/324). At higher $V_{in}$ the asymmetry grows.

### What if the inductor current in a PFM converter looks like the CCM triangle at light load?
Then the converter is not really in PFM: the zero-current detection is disabled (forced PWM), or the mode threshold is set too high, or the low-side FET is shorted on. Check the mode-select pin and the ZCD threshold. Efficiency at light load will also be poor.
:::
