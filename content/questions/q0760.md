::: testing
Whether you can debug a **level** problem, not just a timing problem, and reason from a single measured number to a short list of causes. The signal is "low", but not low enough, so something is putting the line above $V_{IL}$ while the device is pulling it down. The interviewer is looking for:

1. A model of the low state as a **divider**: pull-up resistor against the driver's on-resistance (plus anything in series).
2. Causes: pull-up too strong, too many pull-ups in parallel, weak or damaged driver, series resistance, ground offset, contention, mixed-voltage thresholds.
3. How to measure it properly (at the receiver pin, with a good ground, while the line is held low).
4. Fixes that match each cause.
:::

::: why
A bus that is "low but not low enough" fails in the most annoying way: intermittently, with temperature, with cable length, and with how many modules are plugged in. Reading the low level and finding it at 0.9 V instead of 0.2 V is a classic "aha" in bring-up. It also ties together pull-up sizing, MOSFET on-resistance, grounding and logic thresholds in one question.
:::

::: concepts
- **Open-drain output low state**: the device's NMOS has a finite on-resistance $R_{on}$ ($V_{OL}=I\,R_{on}$). See [[q0761]] and [[q0785]].
- **Pull-up as a divider**: $V_{OL}=V_{DD}\,\frac{R_{on}}{R_p+R_{on}}$. See [[q0008]].
- **Logic thresholds**: $V_{IL}=0.3V_{DD}$ on I2C; mixed rails move it. See [[q0742]] and [[q0762]].
- **Ground**: every milliamp flows back through a real, resistive ground path. See [[q0001]].
- **Pull-up sizing**: $R_{p,min}$ from $I_{OL}$. See [[q0755]].
:::

::: answer
"If the low level isn't getting under the input's $V_{IL}$, I think of the low state as a voltage divider: the pull-up resistor on top, and the pulling-down device's on-resistance plus anything in series at the bottom. So the low voltage is about $V_{DD}\cdot R_{on}/(R_p+R_{on})$, and it goes up whenever the pull-up gets stronger or the path to ground gets more resistive.

The usual causes are: pull-ups too small, which is often several breakout boards each adding their own in parallel, so the device has to sink more than its rating; a weak or damaged driver, or one running at a low supply so its $R_{on}$ is higher; extra series resistance, like a protection resistor, a long ground return, or a level shifter; a ground offset between boards, so the 'low' is low relative to the wrong ground; and contention, where something else on the bus, maybe a pin configured as push-pull, is driving high against it.

It can also be the thresholds: if the receiver is on a lower supply, $V_{IL}$ is 0.3 times that supply, so 0.54 V at 1.8 V, which a driver's 0.4 V plus a little ground bounce can exceed.

To debug, I'd measure the low level right at the receiving pin, with the line held low, compare it with the datasheet $V_{OL}$, calculate the effective pull-up, and then remove devices or pull-ups one at a time to see what moves."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** With the driver on, current flows from $V_{DD}$ through $R_p$ and the driver's on-resistance (plus any series resistance $R_s$ and ground-return resistance $R_g$):

$$V_{OL}=V_{DD}\frac{R_{on}+R_s+R_g}{R_p+R_{on}+R_s+R_g}\approx I\,(R_{on}+R_s+R_g),\quad I=\frac{V_{DD}-V_{OL}}{R_p}$$

**Condition for correct reception:** $V_{OL,at\ receiver}<V_{IL}=0.3V_{DD}$ (or the receiver's own spec, noting it may be on a different rail) with margin.

**Causes and checks.**

| Cause | Why $V_{OL}$ rises | Check |
|---|---|---|
| $R_p$ too small, or many pull-ups in parallel | $I$ exceeds $I_{OL}$, so $V_{OL}=I R_{on}$ is above spec | Measure $R_{eq}$ with bus off; compute $I$ |
| Weak driver ($R_{on}$ large) | low supply, hot, damaged, small output stage | Compare with datasheet $V_{OL}$ at that $I_{OL}$ |
| Series resistance | $R_s$ adds directly: $V_{rx}=I(R_{on}+R_s)$ | Look for ESD/EMI resistors, switches, shifters in path |
| Ground offset/bounce | receiver's ground differs from the driver's by $\Delta V_g$ | Probe ground to ground between boards under load |
| Contention | another device driving high (push-pull pin, shorted pull-up rail) | Disconnect devices one at a time |
| Mixed voltage | $V_{IL}=0.3V_{DD,rx}$ is lower than the divider output | Check rails, add translator |
| Leaky/clamped device | partially powered part forces a mid-level | Power sequencing, "Ioff" specs |
| Measurement | long probe ground lead adds apparent offset | Use a short ground spring |

**Numbers.** 3.3 V, driver $R_{on}=100$ Ω: $R_p=330$ Ω gives $V_{OL}=3.3\times100/430=0.77$ V (below $V_{IL}=0.99$ V but with a thin margin: 0.22 V), and with $R_{on}=150$ Ω it is 1.03 V, above $V_{IL}$. The spec's 0.4 V at 3 mA corresponds to $R_{on}\approx133$ Ω at worst case.
:::

::: short
"The low level is a divider between the pull-up and the pulling-down device's on-resistance plus anything in series, so I look for what raises it: pull-ups too strong or stacked in parallel so the device sinks more than its rating, a weak driver, series resistance in the path, a ground offset between boards, contention from a push-pull pin, or a receiver on a lower supply with a lower $V_{IL}$. I measure the low level at the receiver pin and compare it with the datasheet $V_{OL}$."
:::

::: long
"I'd treat the low state as a divider. When a device pulls the line down, its output transistor has some on-resistance, and the pull-up resistor is pushing current into it from the supply. So the low voltage is $V_{DD}$ times $R_{on}$ over $R_p$ plus $R_{on}$, or more simply the sink current times $R_{on}$. If that's coming out above the receiver's $V_{IL}$, which is 0.3 times its supply, the receiver sees an undefined or even high level.

The causes follow from that formula. Number one is too much pull-up: either the resistor is just too small, or, very common, several modules each have their own pull-ups and they add in parallel, so the device has to sink more than the 3 mA it's rated for and its output rises. Number two is a weak driver: a part running at a low supply voltage, hot, or damaged by ESD has a higher $R_{on}$.

Then there's extra resistance in the low path. That could be a series protection resistor, an analog switch, a level shifter, or a poor ground connection between boards, so the 'zero' at the driver isn't zero at the receiver. That ground offset is why I'd probe ground-to-ground under load.

Another case is contention: if a pin that should be open-drain is configured as push-pull and driving high, it fights the pulling-down device. Or a rail is mismatched, say the pull-up rail is 5 V while the receiver's $V_{IL}$ scales with a 3.3 V supply.

To debug I'd hold the line low, measure at the receiver with a short ground lead, compare against the datasheet $V_{OL}$, then compute the actual sink current from the effective pull-up and supply. Then remove modules or pull-ups one at a time and see where the level drops."
:::

::: terms
- **$V_{OL}$**: output low voltage; 0.4 V max at 3 mA in standard I2C devices.
- **$V_{IL}$**: input low threshold; $0.3V_{DD}$ of the receiving device.
- **$I_{OL}$**: the sink current at which $V_{OL}$ is guaranteed.
- **On-resistance ($R_{on}$)**: channel resistance of the pull-down transistor.
- **Noise margin (low)**: $V_{IL}-V_{OL}$ (plus any offset).
- **Ground bounce / offset**: voltage difference between "grounds" due to current and inductance.
- **Contention**: two drivers driving opposite levels on one net.
- **Level translator**: circuit linking two supply domains.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "the pull-up is too small, or something is pulling it high".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Writes the divider formula and uses it to classify the causes ($R_p$, $R_{on}$, series, ground).
- Spots the breakout-board trap (parallel pull-ups) and computes the equivalent resistance and sink current.
- Explains that thresholds scale with the *receiver's* supply and gives a mixed-voltage example.
- Describes a proper measurement (at the receiver, short ground, line held low) and the "remove one thing at a time" bisection.
- Gives fixes matched to causes: raise $R_p$, translator, fix ground, remove contention, replace a damaged part.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Measuring at the pull-up end** and concluding the bus is fine. The level at the far receiver is what matters.
- **Using a long probe ground lead.** It makes a 0.2 V low look like 0.5 V, or hides a real offset.
- **Assuming $V_{OL}$ is always 0.4 V.** That's the worst-case limit at 3 mA; it scales with current and $R_{on}$.
- **Raising $R_p$ without checking rise time.** You may fix the low level and break the edge ([[q0759]]).
- **Forgetting parallel pull-ups on modules.** Four 4.7 kΩ gives 1.2 kΩ and 2.4 mA, close to the limit.
- **Ignoring that thresholds follow the receiver's supply.** A 1.8 V device has $V_{IL}=0.54$ V, not 0.99 V.
:::

When any device on the bus pulls a line low, the picture is:

Figure: The receiver sees $V_{rx}=V_{DD}(R_s+R_{on}+R_g)/(R_p+R_s+R_{on}+R_g)$. Anything that adds resistance to ground, or shrinks $R_p$, lifts the low level. The datasheet's $V_{OL}$ counts only $R_{on}$ at the pin; $R_s$ and $R_g$ come from your board.

The key point: the standard guarantees $V_{OL}\le0.4$ V **at the device pin** while sinking up to 3 mA. Everything else is up to the board designer.

## Numbers, worked

3.3 V bus, $V_{IL}=0.3\times3.3=0.99$ V.

| Case | $R_p$ | $R_{on}$ / extra | $V_{OL}$ at receiver | Margin to 0.99 V |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Healthy | 2.2 kΩ | 40 Ω | $3.3\times40/2240=0.06$ V | 0.93 V |
| Spec-limit driver | 1 kΩ | 133 Ω | $3.3\times133/1133=0.39$ V | 0.60 V |
| Four 4.7 kΩ + 2.2 kΩ stacked: $R_{eq}=0.78$ kΩ | 0.78 kΩ | 133 Ω | $3.3\times133/913=0.48$ V | 0.51 V |
| Overly strong pull-up | 330 Ω | 100 Ω | 0.77 V | 0.22 V |
| Same, weaker/hot driver | 330 Ω | 150 Ω | 1.03 V | **fails** |
| 100 Ω series + 1 kΩ pull-up | 1 kΩ | 50 + 100 Ω | $3.3\times150/1150=0.43$ V | 0.56 V |

The last two lines show the same mechanism: any extra ohm between the pull-up node and ground lifts the receiver's low.

## Cause by cause

**1. Pull-up too small or stacked.** The classic. Compute the effective resistance with the bus unpowered, then $I=(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/R_{eq}$ and compare with the weakest device's $I_{OL}$. Fix: remove pull-ups until only one set remains, or buffer/split the bus.

**2. Weak or damaged driver.** $R_{on}$ rises when the output stage is small (a low-power part), when the supply is low (an NMOS with less gate overdrive), when the device is hot, or after ESD damage. Compare measured $V_{OL}$ at known current to the datasheet; if only one unit fails, swap it.

**3. Series elements.** A 100 Ω ESD resistor at a connector, an analog mux channel (5 to 50 Ω), or a level shifter all add to the path. A FET-based I2C level shifter is the *mild* case, because $R_{on}$ is single-digit ohms but a resistive shifter built from diodes is not. Check the part's "low-level pass-through offset" if specified.

**4. Ground resistance/offset.** The sink current returns through the ground path. A 0.25 Ω ground trace at 3 mA is only 0.75 mV, which is negligible, but the I2C line's ground may also carry large unrelated currents (a motor or radio on the same ground wire). Two boards joined by a thin cable can have 100 mV or more between "grounds" at different currents, and that appears one-for-one in the low level. Always check ground-to-ground under load.

**5. Contention.** A pin accidentally configured as push-pull output (driving high), a pull-up to a different rail, or two masters driving incorrectly. Look at whether the "low" level depends on which device is selected, or if a particular device state (reset, boot) cures it.

**6. Mixed-voltage thresholds.** $V_{IL}=0.3V_{DD,rx}$. A driver whose low is 0.4 V plus 0.15 V of ground offset is 0.55 V, which exceeds a 1.8 V receiver's 0.54 V even though it's far below 0.99 V on the 3.3 V domain. Use a translator ([[q0877]]) rather than a shared bus.

**7. Partially powered devices.** If a device is unpowered, its ESD diodes can clamp the line near a diode drop *above* its own floating or grounded supply. Pulling the bus to ground is fine; the problem is when it holds the line at a mid-level because its rail is partially up. Look for Ioff specs, and sequence supplies.

## How to measure it properly

1. Hold the line low with a known state: write to a device and trigger on the address bit, or run a test that holds SDA low by sending 0s.
2. Probe **at the receiver's pin** with a spring ground (the long lead rings and adds offset).
3. Measure ground-to-ground between boards while the bus is active.
4. Compare with datasheet $V_{OL}$ at the measured current, current from $(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/R_{eq}$.
5. Remove one module/pull-up at a time and watch the level move.

## Fixes matched to causes

| Cause | Fix |
|---|---|
| Parallel/small pull-ups | Remove extras; $R_{eq}\ge R_{p,min}$ |
| Weak driver | Use a stronger part or the Fm+ variant, replace damaged part |
| Series resistance | Reduce or remove; use a low-$R_{on}$ switch/shifter |
| Ground offset | Common ground wire close to the signals, star ground, connector ground pins |
| Contention | Fix the pin mode; check the second master or pull-up rail |
| Mixed voltages | Level translator with a pull-up on each side |
| Margin generally | Raise $R_p$ within the window, accepting slower rise ([[q0758]]) |

::: applications
- **Bring-up of multi-module systems** where each module has its own 4.7 kΩ and the combination overloads the weakest device.
- **Mixed-voltage designs** (3.3 V MCU, 1.8 V sensor) where $V_{IL}$ differs by receiver.
- **Cabled sensors** with ground offset between the main board and a remote module.
- **Hot-plug and partial-power scenarios**: a module with power removed holding the bus low.
- **Production test**: a measured low-level limit catches damaged drivers and assembly errors.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear on-resistance model** for the pull-down; for large currents the MOSFET leaves the linear region and $V_{OL}$ rises faster.
- **DC conditions**: the line is held low long enough to settle; ringing and ground bounce at edges are separate effects.
- **Standard thresholds**: $V_{IL}=0.3V_{DD}$ of the receiving device; other parts (SMBus, PMBus) differ.
- **Single pull-up rail** equals the device supply; with different rails the formulas use the pull-up rail for $R_p$ and the receiver rail for $V_{IL}$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact $V_{OL}$ vs $I_{OL}$ behaviour varies by device. The standard's 0.4 V at 3 mA is a worst-case limit, not a typical value; typical $R_{on}$ may be 10 to 60 Ω.
:::

::: followups
### How can you tell between a weak driver and too-strong pull-ups?
Measure the pull-up (bus unpowered), then compute the sink current $I=(V_{DD}-V_{OL})/R_{eq}$. If $I$ is above the part's $I_{OL}$, the pull-ups are the problem. If $I$ is within spec and $V_{OL}$ is still high, the driver's $R_{on}$ or the path (series, ground) is the problem.

### The bus is fine alone but not when a second board is connected. What do you check?
Ground offset and extra pull-ups. The other board's pull-ups are in parallel, so $R_{eq}$ drops; and its ground path may differ, so measure the ground difference between the boards while the bus is active. Also check whether its rail is up when the bus is being used.

### Can a too-high low level damage anything?
Not directly, but it can cause the receiver's input stage to sit in its linear region (shoot-through current in the CMOS input buffer) and cause metastable reads. The real damage is functional: misread bits, false START conditions, stuck bus.

### Why do I2C specs allow $V_{OL}$ up to 0.4 V when $V_{IL}$ is 0.99 V?
Because the difference is the noise margin: 0.59 V at 3.3 V, enough for ground offset, coupled noise and series resistance. With a 1.8 V supply the margin shrinks ($0.54-0.36=0.18$ V using the 0.2$V_{DD}$ rule), which is why low-voltage buses are less forgiving.

### What if the low level is fine in DC but spikes high during edges?
That's coupling or ground bounce: simultaneous switching on the ground path, or crosstalk from a neighbouring line. Add ground, increase spacing, check the ground return near the connector and the series-resistor placement.
:::

::: interpretations
"If the logic low state of signal doesn't reach logic-low threshold of device" means the line's low voltage stays above the receiver's $V_{IL}$ (the signal fails to get "low enough"). I read it for open-drain I2C, where the pull-down is a finite $R_{on}$ fighting a pull-up. The mirror-image problem (high not reaching $V_{IH}$) is usually a slow rise or missing pull-up ([[q0759]]).
:::
