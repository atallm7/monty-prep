::: testing
Whether you can state clearly when each tool applies: Fourier series for **periodic** signals (discrete, line spectrum at multiples of the fundamental), Fourier transform for **aperiodic** signals (continuous spectrum). The stronger answer also connects them: series coefficients are samples of the transform of one period, and the transform of a periodic signal is a train of impulses. Hidden sub-skills: harmonics, pulse-train spectra, what the FFT is really doing, and why leakage arises.
:::

::: why
Real hardware signals are both. A clock or PWM waveform is periodic, so its spectrum is a set of harmonics that you care about for EMI and filtering ([[q0673]]); a single event, such as a glitch, a step or a one-shot pulse, is aperiodic and has a continuous spectrum. Instruments blur the line: the FFT of a finite record behaves like a Fourier series of its periodic extension. Knowing the connection prevents mistakes like misreading leakage as real content.
:::

::: concepts
- **Periodic signal**: $x(t+T)=x(t)$; fundamental $f_0=1/T$ and harmonics $nf_0$.
- **Fourier series**: a periodic signal as a sum of harmonics with coefficients $c_n$. See [[q0666]] for the transform.
- **Line spectrum**: energy only at discrete frequencies for periodic signals.
- **Fourier transform**: for aperiodic (finite-energy) signals, giving a continuous spectrum.
- **Limit $T\to\infty$**: a single pulse is a periodic pulse train whose period goes to infinity, and its lines merge into a continuous curve.
- **Gibbs phenomenon**: truncating the series near a discontinuity leaves an overshoot of about 9% of the jump.
:::

::: answer
"Both decompose a signal into sinusoids, but they apply to different kinds of signals.

The Fourier series is for periodic signals. A periodic signal with period T can be written as a sum of sinusoids at the fundamental frequency, $1/T$, and its integer multiples, the harmonics. So the spectrum is discrete: lines at specific frequencies, each with a coefficient. A square wave, for instance, has only odd harmonics with amplitudes that fall as one over n.

The Fourier transform is for aperiodic signals, like a single pulse or a decaying transient, which don't repeat. Because there's no fundamental, the spectrum is continuous: every frequency can have content, and you get a spectral density rather than discrete lines.

They're two sides of the same idea. If you take a pulse and repeat it every T seconds, you get a series whose coefficients are samples of the pulse's Fourier transform, spaced $1/T$ apart. As the period goes to infinity, the lines get closer and merge into the continuous transform. And the transform of a periodic signal is itself a train of impulses at the harmonics.

In practice, instruments compute the discrete transform of a finite record, which implicitly treats it as one period of a periodic signal. That's why if the record isn't an integer number of cycles you get leakage."
:::

::: technical
**Fourier series** (period $T$, $f_0=1/T$):

$$x(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_n e^{j2\pi nf_0t},\qquad c_n=\frac1T\int_{T}x(t)\,e^{-j2\pi nf_0t}\,dt.$$

Trigonometric form: $x(t)=a_0+\sum_{n\ge1}[a_n\cos(2\pi nf_0t)+b_n\sin(2\pi nf_0t)]$ with $|c_n|=\tfrac12\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2}$ (single-sided amplitude $2|c_n|$ for $n\ge1$). Parseval: average power $=\sum|c_n|^2$.

**Fourier transform** (aperiodic $x$): $X(f)=\int x(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}dt$; energy $\int|x|^2dt=\int|X|^2df$.

**Relation.** If $x_T(t)$ is one period and $X_T(f)$ its transform, then $c_n=\frac1TX_T(nf_0)$. The transform of the periodic signal is $X(f)=\sum_nc_n\,\delta(f-nf_0)$.

**Examples.**
- Square wave $\pm A$, 50% duty: $x(t)=\frac{4A}{\pi}\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac{1}{n}\sin(2\pi nf_0t)$; fundamental amplitude $4A/\pi=1.273A$.
- Pulse train, 0 to 1 V, duty $D$: $c_0=D$, and $|c_n|=D\,|\mathrm{sinc}(nD)|$ for $n\ne0$ (single-sided amplitude $2D|\mathrm{sinc}(nD)|$). With $D=0.25$ and $f_0=1$ kHz: $c_0=0.25$, harmonics 0.450, 0.318, 0.150, 0 (the 4th), 0.090, ... V. The envelope is the transform of one pulse, with first null at $1/\tau=4$ kHz.

**Gibbs phenomenon.** Truncating the series at a jump leaves an overshoot of about 8.95% of the jump height, which does not vanish as more terms are added; it only narrows.

**DFT link.** The DFT of $N$ samples equals the Fourier series coefficients of the periodic extension of the record; if the record isn't an integer number of periods, leakage occurs.
:::

::: short
"Fourier series is for periodic signals: a sum of sinusoids at the fundamental and its harmonics, so the spectrum is discrete lines. The Fourier transform is for aperiodic signals and gives a continuous spectrum. They're connected: a periodic signal's series coefficients are samples of one period's transform, spaced $1/T$ apart, and as the period goes to infinity the lines merge into the continuous transform. The FFT treats a finite record as one period."
:::

::: long
"Both the Fourier series and Fourier transform express a signal as sinusoids, but they handle different signal classes.

The Fourier series applies to periodic signals, ones that repeat every T. Any reasonable periodic signal is a sum of sinusoids at the fundamental frequency, one over T, and its integer multiples, the harmonics, plus a DC term. The spectrum is therefore a set of discrete lines. For a 50 percent duty cycle square wave with amplitude plus and minus A, the series has only odd harmonics, with amplitudes four A over n pi, so the fundamental is about 1.27 times the amplitude and the third harmonic is a third of that.

The Fourier transform applies to non-periodic signals like a single pulse or a decaying ringing, with finite energy. There's no fundamental, so the spectrum is continuous: you get a spectral density as a function of frequency.

The link is that the transform is the limit of the series as the period goes to infinity. Imagine a pulse repeated every T. Its series coefficients are samples of the single pulse's transform, scaled by one over T, at multiples of one over T. If I increase T, the lines get closer together and their amplitudes shrink; in the limit they merge into the continuous transform. In the other direction, the transform of a periodic signal is a train of impulses at the harmonics, with weights equal to the series coefficients.

For hardware, that means a clock or PWM signal has harmonics, whose envelope is set by the shape of one pulse: for a pulse train with duty cycle D, a sinc envelope with first null at one over the pulse width. And when you take an FFT of a finite record, you're computing the series of the periodic extension, so you need an integer number of cycles or a window to avoid leakage."
:::

::: terms
- **Fourier series**: representation of a periodic signal as a sum of harmonics.
- **Fundamental ($f_0$)**: the lowest frequency, $1/T$.
- **Harmonic**: an integer multiple of the fundamental, $nf_0$.
- **Fourier coefficient ($c_n$)**: complex amplitude of the $n$th harmonic.
- **Line spectrum**: spectrum consisting of discrete lines, characteristic of periodic signals.
- **Spectral density**: amplitude per unit frequency for aperiodic signals.
- **Gibbs phenomenon**: persistent ~9% overshoot of a truncated series at a discontinuity.
- **Periodic extension**: repeating a finite record forever, as the DFT implicitly does.
- **Duty cycle ($D$)**: fraction of the period a pulse is high.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "series is for periodic, transform is for non-periodic." An exceptional answer:

- Uses an example with numbers (square wave $4/\pi$; pulse train $2D|\mathrm{sinc}(nD)|$ with nulls at multiples of $1/\tau$).
- Mentions Gibbs and the DFT's implicit periodicity with leakage.
- Notes practical impact: harmonics of a clock for EMI, and choosing a window or coherent sampling.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying the transform "can't be used" for periodic signals.** It can: the result is impulses at the harmonics.
- **Forgetting the DC term.** Signals with nonzero average have $c_0\ne0$.
- **Mixing single- and double-sided amplitudes.** $|c_n|$ for $\pm n$ combine into amplitude $2|c_n|$ at $+f$.
- **Thinking a periodic signal has a continuous spectrum.** Its spectrum is lines; smearing seen on an instrument comes from windowing or non-integer cycles.
- **Expecting Gibbs ringing to vanish with more harmonics.** The overshoot stays near 9% but gets narrower.
:::

::: deep
## Same idea, two tools

Both decompose a signal into complex exponentials $e^{j2\pi ft}$. The difference is the set of frequencies:

| | Fourier series | Fourier transform |
|---|---|---|
| Signal | Periodic, period $T$ | Aperiodic, finite energy |
| Frequencies | Discrete: $nf_0$, $f_0=1/T$ | Continuous: all $f$ |
| Output | Coefficients $c_n$ (a sequence) | Function $X(f)$ |
| Units | Same as the signal (V) | Signal × time (V·s, i.e. V/Hz) |
| Energy/power | Average power $\sum|c_n|^2$ | Energy $\int|X|^2df$ |

### Building a periodic signal from one pulse

Let $p(t)$ be a single pulse (height 1 V, width $\tau$) and make a periodic signal by repeating it with period $T$. The coefficients are

$$c_n=\frac1T\int_{-\tau/2}^{\tau/2}e^{-j2\pi nf_0t}dt=\frac{\tau}{T}\,\mathrm{sinc}(nf_0\tau)=D\,\mathrm{sinc}(nD),\qquad D=\frac{\tau}{T}.$$

This is exactly $\frac1T\times$ (the transform of one pulse, $\tau\,\mathrm{sinc}(f\tau)$) evaluated at $f=nf_0$. The envelope is therefore the single pulse's transform; the repetition just *samples* it every $f_0$.

Taking $T=1$ ms, $\tau=0.25$ ms (so $D=0.25$, $f_0=1$ kHz):

That's the physical meaning of "the transform is the limit of the series".

### The transform of a periodic signal

Formally, the FT of a periodic signal is

$$X(f)=\sum_{n}c_n\,\delta(f-nf_0).$$

The impulse (Dirac delta) is how we show "all the energy at exactly these frequencies". The area of each impulse is the series coefficient.

## Square wave: the classic series

For a $\pm A$ square wave with 50% duty cycle and period $T$:

$$x(t)=\frac{4A}{\pi}\left[\sin\omega_0t+\frac13\sin3\omega_0t+\frac15\sin5\omega_0t+\cdots\right].$$

The fundamental has amplitude $4A/\pi=1.273A$, the 3rd is $1.273A/3=0.424A$, the 5th $0.255A$. Even harmonics vanish due to half-wave symmetry. Power check: mean square of the wave is $A^2$; the sum of $(\text{amplitude}^2/2)$ for the first few harmonics: $0.81A^2+0.09+0.032+0.017+\ldots\to A^2$, with 81% of the power in the fundamental.

## Gibbs phenomenon

A sum of continuous sinusoids can never reproduce a jump exactly. Truncating the square-wave series to $N$ terms makes ripples near each edge, with the first overshoot of about 8.95% of the total jump, regardless of $N$. For a $\pm1$ wave (jump of 2) the peak is about $1.18$. Increasing $N$ moves the overshoot closer to the edge and narrows it, but doesn't reduce its height. It matters in practice: a bandwidth-limited square wave through a filter shows overshoot and ringing that are intrinsic to the truncated spectrum, not necessarily a circuit fault.

## What the FFT is doing

An FFT computes the DFT of $N$ samples, which is equivalent to the Fourier series of the signal made by repeating the record forever. Therefore:

- If the record contains exactly an integer number of cycles of a periodic input, the DFT bins coincide with the series harmonics. A coherent measurement of 1 kHz sampled at 64 kHz with $N=1024$ includes 16 whole cycles, and the harmonics land exactly on bins ($\Delta f=62.5$ Hz; 1 kHz is bin 16).
- If not (say 16.5 cycles), the periodic extension has a jump at the join; energy leaks across bins (the spectrum of a sine multiplied by a rectangular window). A Hann window tapers the ends to zero to remove the jump, at the cost of a slightly wider main lobe.
- For a truly aperiodic signal (a single pulse), the DFT approximates samples of the continuous transform at spacing $f_s/N$, provided the record contains the entire pulse.

> [!TIP]
> When you see a "smeared" harmonic on a spectrum analyzer or FFT, ask: is the signal really not periodic (jitter, modulation) or is the record just not an integer number of cycles? Try a window or adjust the record length.

## Using the connection for design

For EMI you care about the *envelope*: harmonics of a clock or PWM waveform sit under a sinc envelope that falls at $-20$ dB/decade above $1/(\pi\tau)$ and $-40$ dB/decade above $1/(\pi t_r)$, where $t_r$ is the rise time ([[q0675]]). Slowing the edges lowers the high-frequency harmonics without changing the pulse train's fundamental. For measurement you care about the lines: their frequencies are fixed at multiples of $f_0$, so you can use narrow filters or notch them.
:::

::: applications
- **Clock and PWM harmonics**: predicting EMI spurs from switching regulators and digital clocks.
- **Power electronics**: harmonic analysis of rectifier currents, THD calculations.
- **Communications**: periodic pilots and carriers are lines; data modulation spreads energy continuously.
- **Audio**: harmonics of a note define timbre; a transient (drum hit) has a continuous spectrum.
- **Instruments**: FFT analyzers use windows and coherent sampling to measure harmonics and distortion.
:::

::: assumptions
- The series assumes a periodic signal satisfying the Dirichlet conditions (bounded variation, finitely many discontinuities per period), which covers all physical waveforms.
- The $T\to\infty$ limit argument assumes the single pulse has finite energy.
- Single-sided amplitude is defined as $2|c_n|$ for $n\ge1$ for real signals; some instruments report RMS instead (divide by $\sqrt2$).
- DFT statements assume uniform sampling above the Nyquist rate and no aliasing ([[q0669]]).
- A valid alternative view: treat the series as a special case of the transform, with impulse spectra.
:::

::: followups
### What is the spectrum of a 50% duty cycle square wave from 0 to 3.3 V?
DC component 1.65 V, and odd harmonics with single-sided amplitudes $\frac{2\times3.3}{n\pi}$: the fundamental is $2\times3.3/\pi=2.10$ V (this is a $\pm1.65$ V square wave plus DC, and $4A/\pi$ with $A=1.65$ gives 2.10 V), the 3rd is 0.70 V, the 5th 0.42 V. No even harmonics.

### Why do the harmonics of a pulse train have nulls?
The envelope is the transform of one pulse, a sinc with nulls at multiples of $1/\tau$. Harmonics that land on a null integrate to zero because the pulse contains a whole number of cycles of that harmonic. For $D=25\%$, the 4th, 8th, ... harmonics vanish; for $D=50\%$ the even harmonics vanish.

### How is the DFT related to these two?
The DFT is the sampled-in-time and sampled-in-frequency version. It equals the Fourier series of the periodic extension of the record, and for a short isolated event it approximates samples of the continuous transform. Both views are right; which applies depends on whether the record contains whole periods or the whole transient.

### What happens to the spectrum as you lengthen a pulse train's period while keeping the pulse the same?
The lines get closer ($1/T$ spacing) and each gets smaller ($c_n\propto\tau/T$), but the shape of the envelope is unchanged. In the limit $T\to\infty$ you have a single pulse, and the lines merge into the continuous spectrum $X(f)=\tau\,\mathrm{sinc}(f\tau)$.

### Scenario: you measure a 10 MHz clock with an FFT and see a broad hump around 10 MHz instead of a narrow line. What are the possible explanations?
Spectral leakage from a record that isn't an integer number of cycles (fix with coherent sampling or a window), true frequency modulation or jitter (spread spectrum clocking is deliberate), or too few samples so the bin width is large relative to the real linewidth. To distinguish them, change the window or record length: leakage changes shape with the window, while real modulation stays.
:::
