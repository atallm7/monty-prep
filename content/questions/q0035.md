::: basics
### In plain words
One resistor and one capacitor can make either a low-pass or a high-pass filter. Only the connection changes. For a low-pass, put the resistor in series with the signal and the capacitor from the output to ground. For a high-pass, swap them. In both cases the corner frequency is $1/(2\pi RC)$.

### Picture it
Imagine a pond fed by a hose through a narrow pipe. The pond level follows slow changes in the inflow but smooths out quick ripples. That is a low-pass filter. Now turn the idea around: pay attention only to how the level just changed, and ignore where it sits. That is the high-pass. Same two parts, different roles. The analogy is loose, but the swap matches the circuit.

### Words you need
- **Low-pass filter**: passes low frequencies and drops high ones.
- **High-pass filter**: passes high frequencies and drops low ones. See [[q0010]].
- **Corner frequency**: the frequency where the output is down 3 dB, $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$.
- **Shunt**: connected from the signal line to ground.
- **Roll-off**: the slope past the corner, 20 dB per decade for one RC.

### The simplest example
You want a 1 kHz low-pass. Pick C = 100 nF. Then $R = 1/(2\pi f C) = 1/(2\pi \times 1000 \times 10^{-7})$, about 1.59 kΩ. Wire R in series with the signal and C to ground. Swap the two parts and the same values give a 1 kHz high-pass. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows how to choose the impedance level against the source and load, and how to get a steeper filter by cascading stages.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can **design**, not just identify, a first-order filter on the spot. The interviewer wants to see you:

1. Draw both circuits correctly: LPF is series R, shunt C (output across C); HPF is series C, shunt R (output across R).
2. Note that the "single capacitor" still needs a resistor (possibly the source or load impedance).
3. Pick a corner frequency, choose R and C values using $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$ with real, standard values, and say how you chose the impedance level.
4. Mention the caveats: loading, source impedance, roll-off of only 20 dB/dec, and when to use higher order or an active stage.
:::

::: why
First-order RC filters are everywhere: anti-aliasing at an ADC input, button debounce, supply noise filters, sensor smoothing, AC coupling, edge shaping. Being able to design one in 30 seconds on a whiteboard, with a sane resistor value rather than "R = 1 Ω, C = 159 mF", is a standard hardware interview skill and shows you think about loading and practical component values.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor impedance** ([[q0024]]): $1/(j\omega C)$, which sets the divider ratio.
- **Pass/block behavior** ([[q0034]]): series C passes high frequencies, shunt C shorts them.
- **First-order corner**: $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$, $-3$ dB and $\pm45^\circ$.
- **Roll-off**: $-20$ dB/decade (LPF) or $+20$ dB/decade (HPF below the corner).
- **Loading and source impedance**: the filter's $R$ is only part of the resistance the capacitor really sees.
- **Standard values** ([[q0017]]): choose C from E6/E12 capacitor values and compute R, or vice versa.
:::

::: answer
"With one capacitor and one resistor I can build either one, depending on how I connect them.

For a low-pass filter, I put the resistor in series with the signal and the capacitor from the output node to ground. At low frequency the capacitor is open, so the signal passes; at high frequency it shorts the output to ground, so the signal is attenuated. For a high-pass, I swap them: the capacitor goes in series with the signal and the resistor goes from the output to ground. Now the capacitor blocks low frequencies and passes high ones.

Either way, the corner frequency is one over two pi R C. So to design one, say a 1 kilohertz low-pass, I pick a capacitor, like 100 nanofarads, and compute R as 1 over two pi f C, which is 1.59 kilohms, and I'd use 1.6 kilohms or 1.58 from E96. I choose the impedance level so that R is much larger than the source's output resistance and much smaller than the load's input resistance, otherwise they shift the corner or the gain. Roll-off is 20 dB per decade, so it's a gentle filter; for a steeper one I'd cascade stages with buffering or use an active filter or an LC filter.

For a high-pass at 100 hertz with a 10 kilohm resistor, C is 159 nanofarads, so a 150 nanofarad part gives 106 hertz."
:::

::: technical
**Low-pass (series R, shunt C):** $H(s)=\dfrac{1}{1+sRC}$, $f_c=\dfrac{1}{2\pi RC}$.
- $|H|=1/\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}$; phase $-\arctan(f/f_c)$; −20 dB/dec above $f_c$.
- Step response: $v_{out}=V(1-e^{-t/RC})$; 10–90 % rise time $=2.2RC$.

**High-pass (series C, shunt R):** $H(s)=\dfrac{sRC}{1+sRC}$, same $f_c$.
- $|H|=\dfrac{f/f_c}{\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}}$; phase $+90^\circ-\arctan(f/f_c)$.

**Design procedure**
1. Choose $f_c$ from the requirement (signal bandwidth, noise to reject).
2. Choose the impedance level: $R\gg R_{source}$ and $R\ll R_{load}$ (rule of thumb 10×).
3. $C=1/(2\pi Rf_c)$; round to a standard value, recompute $f_c$.
4. Check loading: for the HPF $f_c'=1/(2\pi(R_s+R)\,C)$ (high-impedance load); for the LPF $f_c'=1/(2\pi\,[(R_s+R)\parallel R_L]\,C)$.

**Examples:** LPF 1 kHz: $C=100$ nF → $R=1.59$ kΩ. HPF 100 Hz with $R=10$ kΩ → $C=159$ nF (150 nF gives 106 Hz).
:::

The corner is one over two pi R C. I pick C from a standard value, compute R, keep R well above the source resistance and well below the load, and remember it's only 20 dB per decade. For a 1 kHz low-pass, 100 nF and 1.6 kΩ."
:::

::: long
"The capacitor needs a resistor to make a filter, because a filter is a divider, and I need two impedances. So one capacitor and one resistor, connected in one of two ways.

For a low-pass: the resistor goes in series with the signal, and the capacitor goes from the output to ground. At low frequencies the capacitor's impedance is huge, so the output follows the input. At high frequencies the capacitor's impedance gets small, so it shorts the output to ground and attenuates. For a high-pass, I swap the two: capacitor in series, resistor to ground. Now low frequencies get blocked by the capacitor, and high frequencies pass.

In both cases the corner, the minus 3 dB point, is one over two pi R C. The roll-off is 20 dB per decade, and the phase is 45 degrees at the corner.

Design: say I want a low-pass at 1 kilohertz. I pick the capacitor first because capacitors come in fewer standard values, say 100 nanofarads. Then R equals one over two pi times 1000 times 100 nanofarads, about 1.59 kilohms; I'd use 1.6 kilohms and get 995 hertz. The impedance level matters: it should be much larger than the source's output impedance so the source doesn't change R, and much smaller than the load's input impedance, so the load doesn't steal signal or change the corner. For example if this drives a 10 kilohm load, the divider takes 1.6 over 11.6, so a 14 percent drop in gain, which is why I'd buffer or use a bigger load impedance.

For the high-pass I'd do the same but starting from the input impedance after the filter: 100 hertz with 10 kilohms gives 159 nanofarads, 150 nanofarad standard, 106 hertz.

If I need more than 20 dB per decade, I'd cascade sections with a buffer between them, or switch to an active filter like Sallen-Key, or an LC network."
:::

::: terms
- **First-order filter**: one reactive element; −20 dB/dec roll-off.
- **Corner / cutoff frequency**: $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$, −3 dB.
- **Time constant**: $\tau=RC=1/(2\pi f_c)$.
- **Loading**: a load's input impedance changing the filter's gain and corner.
- **Source impedance**: output resistance of the driving circuit, adds to $R$.
- **Anti-aliasing filter**: low-pass before an ADC to limit bandwidth.
- **Buffer**: op-amp follower that isolates stages.
- **Rise time**: $t_r=2.2RC$ for 10–90 % of a first-order response.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws the two circuits and quotes $1/(2\pi RC)$.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws both with the output node explicitly marked, and states the single-capacitor-plus-resistor requirement.
- Designs with real numbers: picks standard C, computes R, rounds, and recomputes the corner.
- Chooses the impedance level on purpose: noise (thermal, $\sqrt{4kTR}$), source/load loading, ADC input requirements, power.
- Corrects the corner for source resistance and load, giving the loaded gain.
- Notes the limits: 20 dB/dec, phase shift, no gain; and gives alternatives (cascaded sections with buffers, active, LC).
- Mentions time-domain equivalents: $\tau=RC$, rise time $2.2RC$, and what that means for step response.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Taking the output across the wrong component.** For the LPF the output is across C; for the HPF it's across R.
- **Ignoring source and load.** A 1 kΩ source adds directly to the filter R; a 10 kΩ load takes 14 % off the gain of a 1.6 kΩ section.
- **Choosing silly values** (1 Ω with 160 mF, or 10 MΩ with 16 pF). Tiny R loads the source; giant R is noisy and picks up leakage.
- **Cascading two identical RC sections and assuming the corner doesn't move.** Two identical *unbuffered* RC stages have a −3 dB point at only about 0.37× the single-stage corner (the second stage loads the first); with a buffer between them it is 0.64×.
- **Expecting a sharp cutoff.** First order only attenuates 20 dB per decade.
- **Forgetting capacitor type** (X7R voltage dependence, tolerance 10–20 %): the corner varies accordingly.
:::

## Picking the impedance level

For a given $f_c$, any $R$–$C$ pair with the same product works; the engineering is choosing a good one:

| $R$ | $C$ for 1 kHz | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| 100 Ω | 1.6 µF | heavy load on the source; large capacitor |
| 1.6 kΩ | 100 nF | typical, convenient |
| 16 kΩ | 10 nF | quieter caps, more noise, more loading sensitivity |
| 1.6 MΩ | 100 pF | easily upset by leakage, input capacitance, probe loading |

Factors: (1) **source impedance** $R_s$ must be $\ll R$ or you add it: $f_c'=1/(2\pi(R+R_s)C)$. (2) **load** $R_L$ must be $\gg R$ or the DC gain drops to $R_L/(R+R_L)$ and the corner moves up to $1/(2\pi(R\parallel R_L)C)$ (for the LPF). (3) **Noise:** thermal noise of $R$ is $\sqrt{4kTR}$ (4 nV/√Hz for 1 kΩ, 12.9 nV/√Hz for 10 kΩ); the capacitor itself adds no thermal noise. (4) **Capacitor quality:** use C0G/film for accurate corners and low distortion; X7R can change by ±15 % over temperature and more with bias ([[q0030]]).

### Worked example with loading

A 20 kHz anti-aliasing LPF into a 10 kΩ ADC input: choose $R=1$ kΩ, $C=8.2$ nF → $f_c=19.4$ kHz. The load takes $R_L/(R+R_L)=10/11$, a gain of 0.909 (−0.83 dB), and the loaded corner is $1/(2\pi(1\text{ k}\Omega\parallel10\text{ k}\Omega)\,C)=21.4$ kHz. If the ADC input has its own sampling capacitor, the real filter is a bit more complicated (the input RC with the ADC's charge kick), so a bigger C or a buffer may be used; see the ADC datasheet's recommended RC.

### Worked example: debounce

A button with 10 ms bounce: LPF with $R=10$ kΩ (pull-up), $C=100$ nF, $\tau=1$ ms gives a 63 % rise in 1 ms and about 99 % in 5 ms, so bounce is smoothed; follow it with a Schmitt-trigger input to get clean edges ([[q0020]]).

## Time-domain view

The LPF step response is $V(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ with $\tau=RC=0.16$ ms for the 1 kHz design, so the 10–90 % rise time is $2.2\tau=0.35$ ms. The relation between bandwidth and rise time, $t_r\approx0.35/f_c$, is the same as for any first-order system.

## Derivation from first principles

Both filters are the impedance divider from the voltage-divider question ([[q0008]]) with one resistor and one capacitor. With phasors the capacitor has $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)$, and the divider ratio is output impedance over total impedance.

**Low-pass** (output across $C$, $R$ in series):

$$H(j\omega)=\frac{Z_C}{R+Z_C}=\frac{1/(j\omega C)}{R+1/(j\omega C)}=\frac{1}{1+j\omega RC}.$$

At very low frequency $Z_C\to\infty$, so $H\to1$. At high frequency $Z_C\to0$ and $H\to0$. The magnitude is $1/\sqrt{1+(\omega RC)^2}$, which equals $1/\sqrt2$ when $\omega RC=1$: that defines $f_c=1/(2\pi RC)$. The phase is $-\arctan(\omega RC)$, so −45° at $f_c$ and approaching −90° well above it.

**High-pass** (output across $R$, $C$ in series):

$$H(j\omega)=\frac{R}{R+Z_C}=\frac{j\omega RC}{1+j\omega RC}.$$

Notice that LPF + HPF $= \frac{1+j\omega RC}{1+j\omega RC}=1$. The two outputs of one divider always add up to the input, which is the physical reason the two curves are mirror images that cross at −3 dB. The HPF phase is $90^\circ - \arctan(\omega RC)$, so +45° at $f_c$.

In the time domain the same story is $RC\,dv_C/dt + v_C = v_{in}$, a first-order differential equation with time constant $\tau=RC$. The capacitor voltage can't jump, so the LPF output follows $V(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ and the HPF output, being $v_{in}-v_C$, jumps with the input and then decays as $Ve^{-t/\tau}$.

## Worked example: integrator and differentiator accuracy

The follow-up says the LPF approximates an integrator well above $f_c$. How good is that? For the 995 Hz design, drive the LPF at a frequency $r = f/f_c$ times the corner. The true gain is $1/\sqrt{1+r^2}$ and the ideal integrator gain is $1/r$:

| $r=f/f_c$ | True gain | Ideal $1/r$ | Gain error | Phase (ideal −90°) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 0.316 | 0.333 | 5.4 % | −71.6° |
| 10 | 0.0995 | 0.100 | 0.5 % | −84.3° |
| 30 | 0.0333 | 0.0333 | 0.06 % | −88.1° |

At ten times the corner you have a 0.5 % gain error and 5.7° of phase error, which is a fine integrator for a signal at 10 kHz, but the output is then only 10 % of the input, so the signal is small and noise matters. That trade-off, accuracy against output amplitude, is the reason a real integrator uses an op-amp to keep the virtual ground ([[q0012]]).

## Noise: the surprise that R drops out

The resistor's thermal noise is $\sqrt{4kTR}$ per root-hertz, which for 1.6 kΩ at 300 K is 5.15 nV/√Hz. The LPF shapes that noise, and the equivalent noise bandwidth of a first-order low-pass is $\tfrac{\pi}{2}f_c$, here $1.57\times995 = 1.56$ kHz. The output noise is therefore $5.15\ \text{nV}/\sqrt{\text{Hz}}\times\sqrt{1562\ \text{Hz}}=0.204\ \mu$V rms. Now compute $\sqrt{kT/C}$ for the 100 nF capacitor: $\sqrt{1.38\times10^{-23}\times300/10^{-7}}=0.204\ \mu$V. They match. The reason is that a larger $R$ gives more noise density but also a lower corner for the same $C$, and the two cancel exactly. So the noise of a single RC low-pass depends only on the capacitor: halve the noise voltage by quadrupling $C$ (and cutting $R$ by the same factor to keep $f_c$). That is a useful design rule when someone asks you to filter a noisy signal further.

## Real hardware: tolerance and capacitor choice

The corner is only as accurate as the parts. With a ±1 % resistor and a ±10 % X7R capacitor that also moves ±15 % over temperature (typical class-II numbers, vendor-dependent), the worst case corner for the 995 Hz design is roughly:

- Capacitor low by 25 % in total: $f_c \approx 995/0.75 = 1.3$ kHz (with resistor tolerance this is slightly more).
- Capacitor high by 25 %: $f_c \approx 995/1.25 = 0.8$ kHz.

That is a ±30 % corner swing from the capacitor alone. For a 20 kHz anti-aliasing filter where the corner determines aliasing, or a precision band edge, use C0G/NP0 or film, where the capacitor contributes well under a percent. For a debounce filter or a noise filter on a slow line, X7R is fine because only the order of magnitude of $\tau$ matters. Voltage bias derating also matters: a 100 nF X7R at a large DC bias may have lost a third of its value ([[q0030]]).

Finally, the driving source is never ideal. An op-amp output has a closed-loop output impedance that rises with frequency (it is inductive), so a "buffered" filter can see an output resistance of tens of ohms at its own corner. That is negligible against 1.6 kΩ but would matter for a 50 Ω filter.

## Cascading: seeing the 0.37 and 0.64 numbers

The follow-up states that two identical sections give −3 dB at $0.37f_c$ unbuffered and $0.64f_c$ buffered. The reason is the transfer function. Buffered stages multiply, $H=1/(1+sRC)^2=1/(s^2R^2C^2+2sRC+1)$. Without a buffer the second section loads the first, and the algebra adds one extra cross term: $H=1/(s^2R^2C^2+3sRC+1)$. The 3 versus 2 is the loading. Both reach −40 dB/decade at high frequency, but the pairs start bending earlier than a single stage: at $f_c$ a single section is 3.01 dB down, the buffered pair 6.02 dB down and the unbuffered pair 9.54 dB down.

## Verifying on the bench

1. **Measure the corner directly.** Drive the filter with a sine generator at a constant amplitude and sweep upward from one tenth of the predicted $f_c$, reading both channels on a scope. The point where the output amplitude equals 0.707 of the input is $f_c$. The phase at that frequency should be ±45°.
2. **Check the source and load.** Repeat the measurement with the real source and the real load connected. If the corner moved, the difference is exactly the loading effects in the design procedure.
3. **Use the step response as a cross-check.** A square wave at a frequency well below $f_c$ gives a rise time of $2.2RC$ for the LPF; a rise time of 0.35 ms for the 1.6 kΩ and 100 nF design confirms $\tau=0.16$ ms without a frequency sweep.
4. **Mind the probe.** A 10× probe adds roughly 10 to 15 pF and 10 MΩ at the output node. That is nothing against 100 nF but is significant for a 1 MΩ, 100 pF design, where the probe capacitance changes the corner it is measuring.

## When one stage is not enough

- **Steeper roll-off:** cascade stages with buffers (each stage 20 dB/dec), or use second-order Sallen–Key or an LC filter. Two identical *unbuffered* RC sections have a −3 dB frequency at about $0.37f_c$, because the second stage loads the first; with a buffer between them it is $0.64f_c$.
- **Gain:** passive RC filters can't amplify; use an op-amp stage ([[q0012]]).
- **Source/load isolation:** a follower before and/or after ensures the calculated corner is the actual one.
- **Sharp transition or flat passband:** use higher-order (Butterworth, Chebyshev) active or LC designs.

::: applications
- **Anti-aliasing and reconstruction filters** at ADC and DAC (PWM output smoothing).
- **Button and switch debouncing** (RC plus Schmitt trigger).
- **Power-supply noise filtering** (RC or LC on a rail feeding an analog block).

- **Sensor signal smoothing** (thermistor, battery voltage monitor).

::: assumptions
- **Ideal capacitor**, no ESR/ESL/leakage, and resistors ideal; real parts shift the corner and limit high-frequency attenuation (the stopband flattens at ESL/pole limits).
- **Linear small-signal** analysis; large signals can reveal capacitor nonlinearity.
- **Source and load** assumed ideal (zero and infinite) in the basic formula; corrections given above.
- **First-order** only: one pole, 20 dB/dec. "Single capacitor" in the question implies first-order.
- Alternate valid interpretation: the "resistor" can be the source or load resistance (e.g. a sensor's output impedance forming the LPF with a capacitor at the ADC).
:::

::: followups
### Design a low-pass with a 10 kHz corner using 10 nF.
$R=1/(2\pi\cdot10^4\cdot10^{-8})=1.59$ kΩ. Standard 1.6 kΩ gives 9.95 kHz; 1.58 kΩ (E96) gives 10.07 kHz. Check that the source resistance is below ~160 Ω and the load is above ~16 kΩ, or buffer.

### Why use a larger R and smaller C rather than the reverse?
Smaller capacitors are cheaper, smaller and often better quality (C0G), and a bigger R loads the source less. But a bigger R adds thermal noise ($\sqrt{4kTR}$), makes the node sensitive to leakage and stray capacitance (parasitic C of a few pF becomes significant vs. 100 pF), and increases loading by the next stage. The sweet spot is typically 1–100 kΩ for general circuits.

### What happens to the corner if the source resistance is 500 Ω in your 1.6 kΩ / 100 nF LPF?
The effective resistance is 2.1 kΩ, so $f_c=1/(2\pi\cdot2100\cdot10^{-7})=758$ Hz instead of 995 Hz, a 24 % shift. To avoid this, drive the filter from a low-impedance source (op-amp output) or include $R_s$ in the design.

### What if you need 40 dB/decade roll-off with only passive parts?
Cascade two RC sections with the second at about 10× higher impedance (to reduce loading), or use an LC section: $f_0=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ with 40 dB/dec, but with possible peaking if $Q$ is high (damp it). A buffered two-stage RC gives 40 dB/dec with no peaking, at a −3 dB corner shifted to $0.64f_c$ (about $0.37f_c$ if unbuffered).

### How would you use a single capacitor to make an integrator or differentiator?
If $f\gg f_c$ the LPF acts as an integrator ($v_{out}\approx\frac{1}{RC}\int v_{in}\,dt$); if $f\ll f_c$ the HPF acts as a differentiator ($v_{out}\approx RC\,dv_{in}/dt$). These are approximations valid when $v_{out}\ll v_{in}$; an op-amp circuit gives exact versions ([[q0012]]).
