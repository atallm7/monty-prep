::: basics
### In plain words
Logic chips come in standard voltage families: 5 V, 3.3 V, 2.5 V, 1.8 V and 1.2 V. Each defines what voltage counts as a 1 and a 0. Two chips can be wired together only if the sender's high is high enough for the receiver, and the receiver's pin can survive the sender's voltage. The first problem makes a link unreliable, and the second can destroy a pin. A level shifter translates.

### Picture it
Think of traveling with electrical appliances. A 230 V appliance in a 120 V socket gets too little voltage and doesn't work properly. A 120 V appliance in a 230 V socket burns out. Logic voltages behave the same way. One quirk: a 3.3 V output can still drive some 5 V inputs, since they need only 2.0 V.

### Words you need
- **TTL levels**: fixed thresholds of 0.8 V for a 0 and 2.0 V for a 1, whatever the supply. Many 3.3 V parts use the same numbers.
- **CMOS levels**: thresholds set as fractions of the supply, about 30% and 70%.
- **Absolute maximum rating**: the highest voltage a pin can take without damage, often about 0.3 V above its supply.
- **5 V-tolerant**: a pin on a lower-voltage chip that is rated to accept 5 V.
- **Level shifter**: a part that translates signals between two voltage families.
- **Supply voltage**: the voltage that powers a chip. A family's name, such as 3.3 V, is its supply.

### The simplest example
A 3.3 V output reaches about 3.1 V under load. Feed it to a 5 V CMOS input that needs 70% of 5 V, which is 3.5 V. It falls 0.4 V short, so the link fails. Feed it to a 5 V TTL-compatible input that needs only 2.0 V, and it passes with 1.1 V to spare. A 5 V output into a 3.3 V pin that is not 5 V-tolerant can damage it. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers other standards and ways to translate.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can rattle off the **standard voltage families** (5 V TTL/CMOS, 3.3 V, 2.5 V, 1.8 V, 1.2 V) and the common non-logic-supply standards (RS-232, RS-485, LVDS, CAN), and then use them: can you say which pairs are compatible, which need a translator, and which can destroy a pin? The strongest answers give a table of thresholds, point out that TTL used fixed thresholds while CMOS thresholds scale with $V_{DD}$, and mention how to cross between domains (divider, series resistor, open-drain with different pull-up rail, dedicated translator).
:::

::: why
Real boards mix voltage domains constantly: a 1.8 V sensor, a 3.3 V MCU, a 5 V display or legacy peripheral, and a 12 V or differential field interface. Picking parts, setting I/O banks and adding the right translators requires knowing these levels cold. The mistakes are expensive: a 5 V signal into a 3.3 V-only pin can latch up or kill the pin, and a 1.8 V output into a 3.3 V input produces a marginal link that works on the bench and fails in the field. Interviewers like this question because it takes five minutes of study to answer and shows immediately whether you have wired real hardware.
:::

::: concepts
- **Threshold definitions** $V_{IL}$, $V_{IH}$, $V_{OL}$, $V_{OH}$ and noise margin ([[q0742]]).
- **TTL vs CMOS**: fixed thresholds vs thresholds proportional to $V_{DD}$.
- **I/O supply (VDDIO)**: many ICs have a separate pin that sets the logic-level voltage for their pins.
- **Open-drain with a pull-up**: the high level is whatever rail the resistor goes to ([[q0761]]).
- **Absolute maximum input voltage**: typically $V_{DD}+0.3$ V; exceeding it forces current through protection diodes ([[q0740]]).
- **Differential signalling**: levels are voltage *differences* between two wires (RS-485, LVDS, CAN).
:::

::: answer
"The common ones are 5 V TTL and CMOS, 3.3 V LVTTL or LVCMOS, 2.5 V, 1.8 V, and 1.2 V for modern low-power parts.

Old 5 V TTL used fixed thresholds: a 0 is below 0.8 V and a 1 is above 2.0 V, with outputs guaranteed below 0.4 V and above 2.4 V. 3.3 V LVTTL uses the same thresholds, which is why 3.3 V and 5 V TTL-compatible parts can talk. CMOS thresholds are proportional to the supply, roughly 30% and 70% of $V_{DD}$. So 5 V CMOS needs about 3.5 V for a 1, 2.5 V CMOS about 1.7 V, and 1.8 V CMOS about 1.2 V.

That's why a 3.3 V output, which only reaches about 3.1 V at light load, can't reliably drive a 5 V CMOS input needing 3.5 V, but works fine into a 5 V TTL-compatible input that needs only 2.0 V.

Beyond single-ended logic, there are standards that define different levels: RS-232 swings between about plus and minus 5 to 15 V with inverted logic, RS-485 and CAN are differential, and LVDS uses a roughly 350 mV differential swing.

To cross domains I'd use a dedicated level translator, or for I2C a MOSFET shifter, or for a slow one-way signal a resistor divider. And I'd always check the input's absolute maximum rating before connecting anything."
:::

**Single-ended families (typical datasheet/JEDEC-style values, volts):**

| Family | $V_{DD}$ | $V_{IL,max}$ | $V_{IH,min}$ | $V_{OL,max}$ | $V_{OH,min}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 V TTL | 5 | 0.8 | 2.0 | 0.4 | 2.4 |
| 5 V CMOS (HC-type, generic 0.3/0.7) | 5 | 1.5 | 3.5 | ~0.1 | ~4.4 |
| 3.3 V LVTTL | 3.3 | 0.8 | 2.0 | 0.4 | 2.4 |
| 3.3 V LVCMOS (light load) | 3.3 | 0.8 | 2.0 | 0.2 | $V_{DD}-0.2$ |
| 2.5 V LVCMOS | 2.5 | 0.7 | 1.7 | 0.4 | 2.0 |
| 1.8 V LVCMOS | 1.8 | $0.35V_{DD}$ = 0.63 | $0.65V_{DD}$ = 1.17 | 0.45 | $V_{DD}-0.45$ = 1.35 |
| 1.2 V LVCMOS | 1.2 | $0.35V_{DD}$ = 0.42 | $0.65V_{DD}$ = 0.78 | check datasheet | check datasheet |
| I2C (any $V_{DD}$) | — | $0.3V_{DD}$ | $0.7V_{DD}$ | 0.4 ($\le$3 mA) | pull-up to $V_{DD}$ |

**Differential and other standards (nominal):**

| Standard | Signal | Levels |
|---|---|---|
| RS-232 | single-ended, bipolar, inverted | driver about ±5 to ±15 V; receiver threshold about ±3 V; mark (idle, logic 1) is negative |
| RS-485 / RS-422 | differential pair | receiver threshold ±200 mV; driver at least 1.5 V differential into the specified load |
| LVDS | differential, current-mode | about 350 mV swing on a 1.2 V common mode; receiver threshold about ±100 mV |
| CAN (ISO 11898-2) | differential | recessive about 0 V differential; dominant about 2 V (CANH ≈ 3.5 V, CANL ≈ 1.5 V) |
| USB 2.0 full/low speed | differential with single-ended levels | D+/D− swing 0 to ≈3.3 V |

**Compatibility check, using $V_{OH,min}(A) > V_{IH,min}(B)$ and $V_{OL,max}(A) < V_{IL,max}(B)$:**

- 5 V TTL → 3.3 V LVTTL (levels only): 2.4 vs 2.0 passes with 0.4 V margin, **but** the 5 V high exceeds a 3.3 V part's absolute maximum unless the pin is 5 V-tolerant.
- 3.3 V LVCMOS → 5 V TTL-compatible input: $V_{OH}\approx3.1$ V vs $V_{IH}=2.0$ V passes with 1.1 V margin.
- 3.3 V LVCMOS → 5 V CMOS: 3.1 V vs 3.5 V fails by 0.4 V.
- 1.8 V → 3.3 V LVTTL: 1.35 V vs 2.0 V fails by 0.65 V; need translator.
- 3.3 V → 1.8 V (not 1.8 V tolerant): over-voltage risk, not just margin.
:::

So 3.3 V can drive 5 V TTL-compatible inputs but not 5 V CMOS inputs, and anything lower than the receiver's supply needs a translator. Then there are non-logic-supply standards: RS-232 at plus and minus several volts, and differential RS-485, CAN and LVDS."

::: long
"I group them into two sets: single-ended logic families and interface standards.

The single-ended ones are named by supply voltage. 5 V TTL uses fixed thresholds: a 0 is below 0.8 V and a 1 is above 2.0 V, with outputs guaranteed at under 0.4 V for a 0 and above 2.4 V for a 1. 3.3 V LVTTL uses the same numbers, which is why a lot of 3.3 V and 5 V TTL-compatible parts interoperate. CMOS thresholds scale with the supply instead: about 30% of $V_{DD}$ for a 0 and 70% for a 1. So a 5 V CMOS part needs 3.5 V for a high, a 2.5 V part needs 1.7 V, and a 1.8 V part needs about 1.2 V. 1.2 V and lower are used on modern low-power processors.

The compatibility check is just comparing the driver's $V_{OH}$ against the receiver's $V_{IH}$, and the same for the low side. A 3.3 V CMOS output at around 3.1 V clears a 5 V TTL input at 2.0 V easily, but falls short of 5 V CMOS's 3.5 V. And a 1.8 V output at 1.35 V doesn't meet 3.3 V LVTTL's 2.0 V. Going the other way, 5 V into a 3.3 V pin isn't just a margin issue, it exceeds the absolute maximum rating unless the pin is 5 V-tolerant.

Then there are the interface standards. RS-232 swings roughly plus and minus 5 to 15 V with inverted logic. RS-485 and CAN are differential with thresholds of a couple hundred millivolts. LVDS is a roughly 350 mV differential swing on a 1.2 V common mode.

To translate, I'd use a dedicated translator IC, a MOSFET bidirectional shifter for open-drain buses like I2C, a resistor divider only for slow one-way signals, or just parts with a separate I/O supply pin."
:::

::: terms
- **TTL**: transistor-transistor logic, with fixed thresholds ($V_{IL}$ = 0.8 V, $V_{IH}$ = 2.0 V); the thresholds survive in 3.3 V "LVTTL".
- **CMOS levels**: thresholds proportional to supply, about $0.3V_{DD}$ and $0.7V_{DD}$.
- **LVTTL / LVCMOS**: low-voltage (3.3 V and below) variants standardised by JEDEC.
- **VDDIO**: separate supply pin that sets a chip's I/O voltage domain.
- **5 V-tolerant**: an input rated to accept 5 V even when the chip runs from 3.3 V.
- **Level shifter / translator**: IC or circuit that converts between voltage domains.
- **Differential signalling**: information carried in the voltage between two wires (RS-485, CAN, LVDS, USB).
- **Common mode**: the average voltage of a differential pair; receivers reject it within a limited range.
- **Mark / space**: RS-232 terms for logic 1 (negative voltage) and logic 0 (positive voltage).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "3.3 V, 5 V, 1.8 V logic".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives **thresholds, not just supply voltages**: for example 0.8 V/2.0 V for TTL and 0.3/0.7 $V_{DD}$ for CMOS.
- Explains the **TTL vs CMOS** distinction and why 3.3 V LVTTL is compatible with 5 V TTL.
- Does at least one **compatibility check with numbers** and names the failing combinations (3.3 V → 5 V CMOS, 1.8 V → 3.3 V).
- Distinguishes **margin problems** (signal not recognised) from **damage problems** (input over its absolute maximum).
- Names the **translation options** and when each is appropriate: divider (slow one-way), MOSFET (I2C), IC translator, VDDIO.
- Mentions **non-logic standards** (RS-232, RS-485, CAN, LVDS) with their characteristic levels.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"3.3 V and 5 V are interchangeable."** 5 V into a non-tolerant 3.3 V pin exceeds the absolute maximum; 3.3 V into a 5 V CMOS input may not reach $V_{IH}$.
- **Using the same thresholds for all CMOS.** They scale with $V_{DD}$; 1.8 V CMOS needs only about 1.2 V for a high.
- **Calling RS-232 "TTL serial".** RS-232 levels are bipolar and inverted; connecting an MCU UART pin directly to an RS-232 port can destroy the pin. Use a transceiver (MAX232-style).
- **Thinking a resistor divider is a general level shifter.** It is one-way, slow (RC with pin capacitance) and wastes current.
- **Forgetting open-drain pull-up voltage.** An I2C bus's high level is whichever rail the pull-ups connect to; mixing parts at different voltages needs care at each device's absolute maximum.
- **Ignoring VDDIO.** Many chips have a separate pin that sets their I/O voltage; it must be powered and in the right sequence ([[q0736]]).
:::

::: deep
## Why there are so many levels

Each step down in supply voltage (5 V → 3.3 V → 2.5 V → 1.8 V → 1.2 V → below 1 V) came from shrinking transistors. Smaller gates cannot withstand 5 V across their thin oxide, and dynamic power scales as $CV^2f$, so lower voltage means much lower power. Old and new parts must coexist on boards, and that coexistence is the source of level-shifting.

### TTL and CMOS: the historical wrinkle

Original TTL (bipolar) had asymmetric outputs: a 0 near 0.2 V and a 1 near 3.4 V, with fixed receiver thresholds around 1.4 V. When CMOS arrived it was ratio-metric. To mix them, "TTL-compatible" CMOS families (74HCT, 74ACT) were built with TTL-like input thresholds (0.8/2.0 V) on a 5 V supply so they could be driven directly by TTL, whereas 74HC/74AC have 0.3/0.7 $V_{DD}$ thresholds. When 3.3 V logic appeared, JEDEC standardised LVTTL with the same 0.8/2.0 V thresholds so 5 V TTL parts could drive 3.3 V parts with no thresholds problem. The remaining hazard is **over-voltage**, not margin.

Using the table, check each pair with $NM_H = V_{OH,min}(A) - V_{IH,min}(B)$:

| Driver → Receiver | $NM_H$ | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| 5 V TTL → 3.3 V LVTTL | 2.4 − 2.0 = +0.4 V | Levels fine; check 5 V tolerance |
| 3.3 V LVCMOS (3.1 V) → 5 V TTL-compat | 3.1 − 2.0 = +1.1 V | Works |
| 3.3 V LVCMOS (3.1 V) → 5 V CMOS | 3.1 − 3.5 = −0.4 V | Fails; use HCT-type input or translator |
| 1.8 V (1.35 V) → 3.3 V LVTTL | 1.35 − 2.0 = −0.65 V | Fails; translator |
| 3.3 V → 1.8 V | n/a | Over-voltage; may damage unless tolerant |

### Crossing the domains

#### Down-translating slowly: a resistor divider

For a slow one-way 5 V → 3.3 V signal, a divider from 5 V logic to a 3.3 V input is cheap:

Figure: With R₁ = 1 kΩ and R₂ = 2 kΩ, $V_{out} = 5\times\frac{2}{3} = 3.33$ V. The pin and trace capacitance C_in forms an RC with the Thevenin resistance $R_1\parallel R_2 = 667\ \Omega$.

Time constant: $\tau = 667\ \Omega\times15\text{ pF} = 10$ ns, so a 10% to 90% rise time of $2.2\tau = 22$ ns, fine up to a few MHz. The divider draws $5/3\text{ kΩ} = 1.7$ mA from the driver when it is high, which is acceptable for a CMOS output but is a continuous cost. Disadvantages: one direction only, edge rounding, and the receiving pin's input leakage and clamp diodes interact with the divider.

#### Series resistor into a clamped (not 5 V-tolerant) input

Many MCU pins have ESD clamp diodes to $V_{DD}$. A series resistor limits the clamp current when a 5 V output drives them: with 1 kΩ and a 3.3 V supply, the current is about $(5-3.3-0.3)/1\text{ kΩ} = 1.4$ mA, within many pins' injection-current ratings. That is a last resort: **check the datasheet's injection-current limit**, since it also pumps current into the supply rail.

#### Dedicated translators

- **Direction-controlled buffers** (for example 74LVC-family translators with separate $V_{CCA}$ and $V_{CCB}$): fast, push-pull, but need a direction control or fixed direction.
- **Auto-direction translators** (for open-drain buses): sense which side is pulling low; fine for I2C, slower than push-pull parts.
- **Parts with a separate VDDIO**: simplest, if the chip offers it.

### Non-logic-supply standards

- **RS-232**: a driver swings between about +5 V and +15 V (space, logic 0) and −5 V and −15 V (mark, logic 1); a receiver reads above +3 V as space and below −3 V as mark. The line idles at mark (negative). A UART's TTL-level TX needs a transceiver chip (the MAX232 family) to make these levels.
- **RS-485 / RS-422**: differential; a receiver decides based on whether $V_A - V_B$ is above +200 mV or below −200 mV; a typical driver gives at least 1.5 V differential into a 54 Ω load. Common-mode range is about −7 V to +12 V, which lets devices with different grounds talk over long cables.
- **CAN**: differential bus; the recessive state has CANH ≈ CANL ≈ 2.5 V (0 V differential), the dominant state has CANH ≈ 3.5 V, CANL ≈ 1.5 V (about 2 V differential). Dominant overrides recessive, similar in spirit to I2C's wired-AND.
- **LVDS**: current-mode driver of about 3.5 mA into a 100 Ω termination gives about 350 mV swing, centred around 1.2 V. A low swing makes it fast and low-EMI. Receiver threshold is about ±100 mV.
- **USB 2.0 (full/low speed)**: D+ and D− swing from ≈0 V to ≈3.3 V and are read differentially.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 1.8 V and 1.2 V thresholds (0.35/0.65 of $V_{DD}$) and the 2.5 V and 1.8 V output levels follow the JEDEC JESD8-series definitions as commonly quoted; confirm them in the standard or the part's datasheet, since vendors often specify their own I/O levels. Exact thresholds for RS-232, RS-485, LVDS and CAN depend on the specific revision (TIA/EIA-232-F, TIA/EIA-485-A, ANSI/TIA/EIA-644-A, ISO 11898-2) and the transceiver you use. The values here are the commonly quoted nominal numbers; confirm in the standard or transceiver datasheet for design work.

### How to be sure on a real board

1. Read each pin's **absolute maximum input voltage** relative to its own supply (often $V_{DD}$ + 0.3 V) and its **5 V-tolerant** note.
2. Read $V_{IH}$/$V_{IL}$ (receiver) and $V_{OH}$/$V_{OL}$ (driver) **at the load current you will actually have**.
3. Check **power sequencing**: a driver powered while the receiver is off will back-power it through the clamp diodes ([[q0736]]).
4. Choose translation by direction, speed and bus type from the list above.
5. Scope the receiving pin to confirm the real levels and edges.

::: applications
- **3.3 V MCU talking to a 5 V character LCD**: LCD inputs are TTL-compatible; the MCU can drive them, but the MCU must not *read* the LCD's 5 V outputs without protection.
- **Mixed I2C bus** (1.8 V sensor + 3.3 V MCU): a MOSFET or auto-direction I2C translator with pull-ups on each side.
- **MCU to RS-232 console**: UART TX/RX through a transceiver (MAX232 family) to ±V levels.
- **Industrial sensor networks**: RS-485 transceivers carry UART frames over differential pairs for hundreds of metres.
- **Automotive**: CAN for body and powertrain; MCU talks to a CAN transceiver using ordinary 3.3 V/5 V logic.
- **Camera and display links**: LVDS (and its descendants) carry high-rate pixel data with low EMI.
:::

::: followups
### Can a 3.3 V MCU read a signal from a 5 V TTL-compatible output directly?
On levels, yes: a 5 V TTL output guarantees 2.4 V, above LVTTL's 2.0 V requirement. On voltage, only if the MCU pin is rated 5 V-tolerant; otherwise the 5 V high exceeds the absolute maximum ($V_{DD}$ + 0.3 V ≈ 3.6 V). Without a tolerant pin use a divider (for slow signals), a translator, or an HCT-type buffer powered from 3.3 V.

### How would you connect a 1.8 V I2C sensor to a 3.3 V MCU on the same bus?
Use a bidirectional open-drain translator: a MOSFET shifter per line (gate to 1.8 V, pull-ups on both sides to their own rails) or an auto-direction I2C translator IC. Keep pull-up values appropriate for each side and check the rise time on each. Alternatively, power the sensor's I/O at 3.3 V if it has VDDIO.

### Why is LVDS able to run so fast with such a small swing?
It's differential and current-mode: a 3.5 mA current source into a 100 Ω termination makes about 350 mV, and the receiver only needs ±100 mV. The small swing means short edge times for a given slew, little EMI (the two wires carry equal and opposite currents), and noise rejection because the common-mode shift is ignored.

### The 5 V device's output is only reaching 4.2 V in a 3.3 V system with a 5 V CMOS input, and bit errors appear at high temperature. Why?
A 5 V CMOS input needs $0.7\times5=3.5$ V (HC-type thresholds can be 3.15 V at 4.5 V). The 4.2 V gives 0.7 V of margin, but a long trace, ground shift or heavy load can eat that, and thresholds shift with temperature. Measure at the receiver pin and consider an HCT or Schmitt input, or reduce loading.

### What if you must drive a 5 V CMOS input from a 3.3 V MCU at 20 MHz?
A divider and series-resistor tricks don't work for up-translation. Use a push-pull translator or a buffer such as a 74HCT-type gate powered from 5 V, whose TTL-compatible input threshold (about 2.0 V) is met by the 3.3 V pin directly. Check the buffer's propagation delay and the load capacitance against the 50 ns period.
:::
