::: testing
Whether you can **size a measurement from the signal**, not just quote Nyquist. A weak answer is "twice the signal frequency". A strong answer asks what you want to learn (amplitude? edge time? timing? spectrum?), derives the needed bandwidth from the signal's rise time or harmonics, converts that into a sample rate with margin, checks memory depth, and notes probe and front-end limits. The interviewer is checking the link between **rise time and bandwidth** ($BW\approx 0.35/t_r$), the Nyquist criterion, and a feel for the rules of thumb (5× bandwidth, 4 to 5 samples per edge).
:::

::: why
Under-sizing the scope is the most common measurement error in a lab. A 100 MHz scope will happily display a 1 ns edge as a 3.5 ns ramp with the overshoot gone, and the engineer will conclude the design is fine. Over-sizing wastes money and shows more noise. Sizing correctly is also what decides whether a signal-integrity problem is visible at all, and whether a data-acquisition system for a sensor is adequate. It ties together Fourier content ([[q0132]]), rise time ([[q0117]]) and sampling theory ([[q0669]]). The conceptual distinction between the two specs is in [[q0983]]; this page is the sizing procedure.
:::

::: concepts
- **Rise time and bandwidth**: a first-order (single-pole) system has $t_r(10\text{-}90\%)\approx 0.35/BW$. See [[q0117]].
- **Signal knee frequency**: a digital edge's spectrum rolls off beyond $f_{knee}\approx 0.5/t_r$, so that's the highest frequency that matters.
- **Harmonics**: a periodic non-sinusoidal signal at $f_0$ has harmonics at $nf_0$; capturing shape needs several.
- **Nyquist**: sample rate must exceed twice the highest frequency present, or aliasing occurs. See [[q0669]], [[q0670]].
- **Root-sum-square rise times**: $t_{r,meas}=\sqrt{t_{r,sig}^2+t_{r,scope}^2+t_{r,probe}^2}$.
- **Memory depth**: samples stored; capture time $=\text{depth}/f_s$.
:::

::: answer
"I'd start from what I'm trying to measure, because bandwidth and sample rate answer different questions.

For bandwidth, I look at the fastest feature of the signal. For a sine, I need bandwidth well above the frequency, about 3 to 5 times for amplitude accuracy a few percent. For a digital edge or a switching waveform, I use the rise time: the edge's content goes up to about 0.5 over the rise time, and a scope with bandwidth BW has its own rise time around 0.35 over BW. For under about 2 percent error in rise time, the scope should be about five times faster than the edge, so BW is roughly 1.75 over the edge's rise time. A 2 ns edge means around 900 MHz, not 100.

For sample rate, Nyquist says more than twice the highest frequency, but for a useful picture I'd want about 4 to 5 samples across the fastest edge, and in practice 2.5 to 5 times the scope's bandwidth. I also check memory depth so I can capture the whole event at that rate, and that the probe isn't the limiting factor.

If I just want the spectrum, I choose the sample rate above twice the highest frequency of interest, with an anti-alias filter, and the record length from the frequency resolution I need."
:::

::: technical
**Step 1: Identify the fastest feature.**

- *Sinusoid at $f$*: for single-pole roll-off, amplitude error $=1-1/\sqrt{1+(f/BW)^2}$. At $f/BW=0.25$ (BW = 4f) error is 3.0 %; at $f/BW=0.2$ (BW = 5f) it is 1.9 %.
- *Edge with rise time $t_r$*: $f_{knee}=0.5/t_r$ (the frequency beyond which the spectrum falls off at −40 dB/decade for a trapezoid), and for a Gaussian-like instrument response $t_{r,scope}\approx 0.35/BW$ (about 0.4/BW for flatter, maximally-flat-passband scopes above 1 GHz, check the datasheet).
- *Periodic non-sinusoidal signal*: include harmonics up to the point that matters; a square wave needs about the 5th to 10th harmonic for a recognisable shape, so BW ≥ 5 to 10 times the fundamental.

**Step 2: Required bandwidth for rise-time error.** Measured rise time is $t_{r,meas}=\sqrt{t_{r,sig}^2+t_{r,sys}^2}$ with $t_{r,sys}=0.35/BW_{sys}$.

| $t_{r,sig}/t_{r,sys}$ | Rise-time error | Required $BW$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 41 % | $0.35/t_{r,sig}$ |
| 3 | 5.4 % | $1.05/t_{r,sig}$ |
| 5 | 2.0 % | $1.75/t_{r,sig}$ |
| 10 | 0.5 % | $3.5/t_{r,sig}$ |

For a 2 ns edge: 525 MHz for 5 % error, 875 MHz for 2 %.

**Step 3: System bandwidth, not scope bandwidth.** Probe and scope add: $1/BW_{sys}^2\approx1/BW_{scope}^2+1/BW_{probe}^2$. A 500 MHz scope with a 500 MHz probe is 354 MHz.

**Step 4: Sample rate.** Nyquist: $f_s>2f_{max}$. Practical: $f_s\ge 2.5\text{ to }5\times BW$ so that the display is accurate with sin(x)/x interpolation, and ≥ 4 to 5 samples across the fastest edge: $f_s\ge 5/t_{r,sig}$. For a 2 ns edge, $f_s\ge 2.5$ GS/s.

**Step 5: Record length.** $\text{memory}=f_s\times T_{window}$: 10 ms at 1 GS/s needs 10 Mpts. For FFT, frequency resolution $\Delta f=f_s/N$: resolving 10 Hz at 100 kS/s needs $N=10{,}000$ points.

**Step 6: Vertical.** Match voltage scale and resolution to amplitude (8-bit ≈ 256 levels), and check the noise floor at the chosen bandwidth: noise grows with $\sqrt{BW}$.
:::

::: short
"I size bandwidth from the fastest feature and sample rate from the bandwidth. For a sine I want bandwidth 3 to 5 times the frequency. For an edge, content extends to about 0.5 over the rise time, and a scope's own rise time is 0.35 over its bandwidth, so to keep rise-time error near 2 percent I need roughly 1.75 over the rise time: about 900 MHz for a 2 ns edge. Sample rate is then at least 2.5 to 5 times that, giving 4 to 5 samples per edge, and I check memory depth and the probe bandwidth."
:::

::: long
"Bandwidth and sample rate answer different questions, so I size them in order.

First the signal. What do I need to learn: amplitude, edge time, timing, or spectrum? For a plain sine, the scope's bandwidth should be a few times the frequency: a single-pole response at one fifth of its bandwidth has only about 2 percent amplitude error. For a digital edge or a switch node I use rise time, because that sets the highest frequency that matters. The spectrum of an edge extends to about 0.5 over the rise time, and a scope's own rise time is about 0.35 over its bandwidth. The measured rise time is the root-sum-square of the signal's and the scope's, so to get within about 2 percent, the scope needs to be five times faster than the edge. That gives about 1.75 over the rise time. For a 2 ns edge, that's around 900 MHz, not the 100 MHz scope someone grabs by default.

Second, the sample rate. Nyquist says above twice the highest frequency, but with real interpolation I want a sample rate of 2.5 to 5 times the bandwidth. A sanity check is at least 4 or 5 samples across the edge. For the 2 ns edge that's about 2.5 gigasamples a second.

Third, I check memory depth: sample rate times the window I need. Ten milliseconds at 1 GS/s is 10 million points. And finally the probe: scope and probe bandwidths combine, so a 500 MHz probe on a 500 MHz scope gives about 350. If I only care about spectrum, I'd choose the sample rate above twice the top frequency, with anti-aliasing, and the record length from the resolution I need."
:::

::: terms
- **Analog bandwidth**: frequency at which the instrument's response is 3 dB down (70.7 % amplitude).
- **Sample rate ($f_s$)**: how many samples per second the ADC takes. See [[q0983]].
- **Nyquist frequency**: $f_s/2$, the highest frequency a sampled system can represent unaliased.
- **Rise time (10–90 %)**: time for a signal to go from 10 % to 90 % of its final value.
- **Knee frequency**: $\approx 0.5/t_r$, the highest significant frequency in a digital edge.
- **Aliasing**: components above Nyquist folding to lower, false frequencies.
- **Interpolation (sin(x)/x)**: reconstruction between samples; works when the signal is band-limited under Nyquist.
- **Memory depth / record length**: number of samples captured per acquisition.
- **Samples per cycle / per edge**: a practical measure of whether the display is faithful.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "at least twice the signal frequency (Nyquist)".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates **bandwidth** (analog front end) from **sample rate** (ADC) and sizes each for a stated goal.
- Uses **rise time** as the driver for digital signals: $f_{knee}=0.5/t_r$, $BW=0.35/t_r$, and the RSS rise-time formula.
- Gives a **number with a tolerance** (5× → about 2 % error) and a worked example.
- Mentions **probe and system bandwidth**, **memory depth** and **vertical resolution/noise**.
- Notes that the answer depends on **what you want to measure**: amplitude, edge time, jitter, spectrum.
- Warns about **aliasing** and anti-alias filtering for data acquisition.
- Chooses **sensible, not maximal** bandwidth: more bandwidth admits more noise.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Twice the signal frequency is enough."** That is the Nyquist limit for reconstruction, not a recipe for an accurate waveform. For amplitude within a few percent you need 3 to 5 times in bandwidth, and 2.5 to 5 times in sample rate beyond that.
- **Using the clock frequency as the signal frequency.** A 100 MHz clock with a 1 ns edge has content to 500 MHz and beyond.
- **Forgetting the probe.** The probe's bandwidth and tip capacitance (and a long ground lead) lower the system's.
- **Equating sample rate with bandwidth.** A scope can have 10 GS/s and 1 GHz bandwidth; the two are not interchangeable. See [[q0983]].
- **Overlooking interleaving.** A scope's headline sample rate may apply on one channel only; with several channels active the rate per channel can drop.
- **Ignoring memory depth.** At full sample rate a short memory fills quickly and the scope drops its rate when you zoom out.
:::

::: deep
## First principles: what limits a measurement?

A scope has an analog front end (attenuator, amplifier, filter), an ADC, memory and display. Three independent limits:

1. **Analog bandwidth** limits which frequencies reach the ADC. It sets how fast an edge can look.
2. **Sample rate** limits the time resolution and, through Nyquist, the highest frequency the ADC can represent without aliasing.
3. **Record length** limits how long you can look at that sample rate.

Sizing a measurement means checking all three against the signal and the question.

## Bandwidth from the signal

### Sine waves

A scope's front end behaves roughly like a first-order low-pass near its −3 dB point (many high-end scopes use a flatter, steeper response). For a single pole, the amplitude gain at $f$ is $1/\sqrt{1+(f/BW)^2}$. The fraction of amplitude lost is:

| $BW/f$ | Amplitude error |
|---|---|
| 1 | 29.3 % (−3 dB) |
| 3 | 5.1 % |
| 4 | 3.0 % |
| 5 | 1.9 % |
| 10 | 0.5 % |

That is the origin of the "5× rule": with BW = 5 f the amplitude error is under 2 %. If you only need frequency or timing (period measurement) and not amplitude, you can use much less, because the zero-crossing doesn't need full amplitude.

### Edges and digital signals

A 2 ns rise time is not a 500 MHz signal. Its spectrum is broad. For a trapezoidal pulse the envelope falls at −20 dB/decade beyond $1/(\pi\tau)$ and −40 dB/decade beyond $1/(\pi t_r)$, giving the knee estimate $f_{knee}\approx 0.5/t_r$. For a 2 ns edge:

$$ f_{knee}=\frac{0.5}{2\ \text{ns}}=250\ \text{MHz}. $$

A single-pole system with bandwidth $BW$ has 10–90 % rise time $t_{r}=0.35/BW$ (derived from $t_r=2.2\tau$ and $BW=1/2\pi\tau$: $2.2/(2\pi)=0.35$). Rise times of cascaded stages add in quadrature, so the displayed rise time is

$$ t_{r,meas}=\sqrt{t_{r,sig}^2+\left(\frac{0.35}{BW}\right)^2}. $$

Worked example. A switching converter's switch node rises in 5 ns and rings at about 100 MHz. For 5 % rise-time error you need $t_{r,sys}=5/3=1.67$ ns, so $BW\ge0.35/1.67\ \text{ns}=210$ MHz. Because the ring is at 100 MHz, a 250 MHz or 350 MHz scope (and probe!) is a reasonable choice, but a 20 MHz bandwidth limit (as the "noise filter" button does) would remove the ringing completely. ### System bandwidth with a probe

The probe, cable and scope form a cascade. With first-order behaviour the *rise times* add in quadrature, and approximately $1/BW_{sys}^2=1/BW_{scope}^2+1/BW_{probe}^2$. A 500 MHz scope with a 500 MHz probe gives $1/\sqrt{2/500^2}=354$ MHz. High-end scopes specify bandwidth *at the probe tip* with their matched probes, which avoids this. Long ground leads add inductance that creates resonances well below the probe's rated bandwidth ([[q0990]]).

## Sample rate from the bandwidth

Nyquist says a band-limited signal with highest frequency $f_{max}$ can be reconstructed if $f_s>2f_{max}$. Practical scopes need margin:

- A Gaussian-like front-end rolls off gently, so some energy above BW still reaches the ADC. To avoid aliasing, $f_s\ge 2.5\times BW$ (many scopes use 4 to 5×).
- To *display* edges faithfully with interpolation, 4 to 5 samples across the rise time: $f_s\ge 5/t_r$. For a 2 ns edge that is 2.5 GS/s. At 1 GS/s only 2 samples span the edge, and its apparent shape depends on where the samples fall relative to it.
- For repetitive signals, equivalent-time sampling can build up a high-resolution picture from slow samples, but it can't capture one-shot events.

## Record length and time window

Memory depth and sample rate set the window: $T=\text{depth}/f_s$. Capturing a 10 ms power-up transient with 2 ns details at 1 GS/s needs 10 Mpts. If the memory is only 1 Mpts, the scope will either lower the sample rate (losing the fast detail) or shorten the window. Segmented memory captures repeating bursts without recording the dead time. Spectrum resolution is $\Delta f=f_s/N=1/T$, so to resolve 10 Hz you need a 100 ms record.

## Vertical resolution and noise

Bandwidth also gates noise: the scope's front-end noise voltage scales like $\sqrt{BW}$. Opening the bandwidth to 1 GHz when the signal only needs 100 MHz makes a noisier trace. Most scopes provide bandwidth limits (20 MHz, 200 MHz) for that reason. With an 8-bit ADC and 8 divisions, each LSB is 1/32 of a division: at 10 mV/div the resolution is about 0.31 mV; at 1 V/div it is 31 mV. Pick the smallest vertical scale that keeps the signal on screen. Hi-res or averaging modes trade bandwidth or repetition for resolution.

## Other instruments

- **DMM**: bandwidth in DC mode is effectively "reading rate". See [[q0988]].
- **Data acquisition / ADC on a board**: choose $f_s$ above twice the highest frequency, and add an anti-alias filter below $f_s/2$. For a sensor with content to 1 kHz, 10 kS/s with a 2nd-order filter at 1.5 kHz is typical. See [[q0668]] for the sine-wave sampling question.
- **Spectrum analyzers** use a swept or FFT receiver with resolution bandwidth (RBW) instead of a sample rate; the trade-off is sweep time vs. RBW.

## A procedure you can say out loud

1. What do I need to learn: amplitude, edge, timing, spectrum?
2. Find the fastest feature: $f_0$ and harmonics, or $t_r$.
3. $BW\ge 5f$ (sine), or $BW\ge 1.75/t_r$ (2 % edge error), or $1.05/t_r$ (5 %).
4. Subtract probe: pick a probe and scope whose combined BW meets it.
5. $f_s\ge 2.5\text{ to }5\times BW$ and ≥ $5/t_r$.
6. Memory = $f_s\times T$; check the spectral resolution if using FFT.
7. Vertical scale and bandwidth limit for noise.
8. Verify with a known reference edge or sine.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Put a number on it: "For a 2 ns edge I'd want about a gigahertz of bandwidth and 2.5 to 5 gigasamples." That single sentence shows you've done it.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 0.35 constant assumes a single-pole (Gaussian-like) response. Flatter, high-order "brick-wall" scope responses have constants nearer 0.4 to 0.45, so check the instrument's stated rise time. The 0.5/t_r knee rule is an approximation for trapezoids; real edges with ringing or a slower tail shift it.
:::

::: applications
- **Switching converters**: capturing switch-node edges and ringing needs several hundred MHz of system bandwidth.
- **High-speed digital debug**: DDR, USB and PCIe edges demand GHz-class scopes and probes ([[q0711]]).
- **Power rail ripple**: bandwidth limited to 20 MHz on purpose to ignore switching spikes you'll check separately.
- **Audio and sensor logging**: 44.1 or 48 kS/s for 20 kHz audio; 1 to 10 kS/s for vibration sensors with an anti-alias filter.
- **Motor and battery measurements**: kHz-range bandwidths with long records, so memory depth matters more than GS/s.
- **Embedded ADCs**: choose sampling rate and anti-alias filter from the sensor's bandwidth ([[q0808]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- The 0.35/BW constant assumes a **single-pole** front end; Gaussian is also close. Flatter brick-wall filters differ somewhat.
- The 0.5/$t_r$ knee estimate is for a **trapezoidal** pulse; signals with overshoot or very slow tails shift it.
- "5× rule" is a **rule of thumb** for a few-percent error; tighter accuracy needs more, looser needs less.
- Assumes you need **amplitude and edge fidelity**; for frequency or timing alone you can use less bandwidth.
- Sample rate rules assume a **real-time** scope. Equivalent-time (sampling) scopes reach very high bandwidth on repetitive signals with slow ADCs.
- The answer changes for non-scope measurements: a DMM, a DAQ card, or a spectrum analyzer each have their own bandwidth/rate definitions.
:::

::: followups
### Why is 0.35 the constant in BW = 0.35/t_r?
For a single-pole low-pass, the step response is $1-e^{-t/\tau}$. The 10 % and 90 % points occur at $t=0.105\tau$ and $2.303\tau$, so $t_r=2.2\tau$. The −3 dB bandwidth is $1/(2\pi\tau)$. So $t_r\cdot BW=2.2/(2\pi)=0.35$.

### Can I measure a 1 ns edge with a 100 MHz scope?
You can see that there is an edge, but the display will show about 3.5 ns rise time, because $\sqrt{1^2+3.5^2}=3.64$ ns, and overshoot and ringing will be suppressed. For a 1 ns edge, aim for about 1.75 GHz for 2 % error, or about 1 GHz if 5 % is acceptable.

### What if I need to see a 1 µs glitch at 2 ms intervals over one second?
Bandwidth is modest (about 3 to 5 MHz for a 1 µs pulse with a 100 ns edge, depending on shape), but the record is long, so memory depth dominates: 1 s at, say, 50 MS/s needs 50 Mpts. A triggered capture with segmented memory (only record around the glitch) or a smaller sample rate with a width trigger is more efficient.

### Your scope says 1 GHz and 5 GS/s. With four channels on, what changes?
On many scopes the ADCs are shared, so with more channels active the per-channel sample rate halves (e.g., 2.5 GS/s). Bandwidth stays the same. Check the datasheet. With 2.5 GS/s and 1 GHz you're at 2.5×, the minimum comfortable ratio.

### Scenario: a clock looks clean on a 200 MHz scope but the receiver fails. What would you check?
The clock probably has fast edges or ringing beyond the scope's bandwidth. Re-measure with a scope and probe of several times the knee frequency (for a 500 ps edge, $f_{knee}=1$ GHz), use a short ground spring, and look for overshoot, non-monotonic edges and reflections ([[q0711]]).
:::

::: interpretations
"What sample rate/bandwidth will I need to measure a signal?" reads as a sizing question, so the answer is a procedure plus a worked number. The signal isn't specified; a good answer asks, or states assumptions (for example "a digital signal with 2 ns edges at 50 MHz"). Also note that for a **DMM** or a **DAQ** the words mean something different. This page is about oscilloscope-style measurements. The conceptual difference between the two terms is covered in [[q0983]].
:::

- States bandwidth and sample rate are different and sizes both :: The question asks for both; conflating them is a red flag.
- Uses rise time to drive bandwidth ($0.35/t_r$, knee $0.5/t_r$) :: The right model for digital edges and the core of the answer.
- Quotes a margin rule with a tolerance (3–5× for a few percent error) :: Shows practical, quantified judgment.
- Gives a sample-rate rule (Nyquist plus margin, 2.5–5× BW, 4–5 samples per edge) :: Nyquist alone is not enough for a faithful display.
- Mentions probe/system bandwidth and RSS addition :: The probe often limits the measurement.
- Considers memory depth and time window :: A fast sample rate is useless if the memory runs out.
- Works a numeric example (e.g., 2 ns edge → about 900 MHz) :: Numbers prove you can apply the rule.
