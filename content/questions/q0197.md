::: basics
### In plain words
The diode in a buck gives the inductor current a path when the switch turns off. An inductor cannot stop its current instantly. If there is nowhere for the current to go, the voltage at the switch node swings wildly until something conducts or breaks. The diode catches it safely.

### Picture it
Think of a long pipe full of fast-moving water with a valve at the end. If you slam the valve shut, the water's momentum causes a violent pressure spike, called water hammer. A bypass pipe lets the water keep flowing and slow down gently. The diode is that bypass for the inductor's current.

### Words you need
- **Freewheel diode**: the diode that carries the inductor current while the switch is off.
- **Switch node**: where the switch, inductor and diode meet.
- **Schottky diode**: a diode with a low forward drop and fast switching.
- **Forward drop**: the small voltage across a diode while it conducts, about 0.4 V for a Schottky.
- **Inductor energy**: $\tfrac12 L I^2$, the energy that must go somewhere.

### The simplest example
The inductor carries 2 A when the switch opens. With the diode present, the switch node drops to about 0.4 V below ground, the diode turns on, and 2 A circulates through inductor, load and diode while the current ramps down. Without the diode, nothing can carry that 2 A, so the node plunges until a transistor or chip pin breaks down. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page puts numbers on the energy involved and on the damage it does.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand the diode as the **path for the inductor current when the switch opens**, not just "a rectifier". The key chain of reasoning is: inductor current cannot stop instantly, so if the high-side FET opens and no path exists, $v=L\,di/dt$ drives the switch node as negative as it takes, until something breaks down. A strong answer gives that mechanism, quantifies the energy ($\tfrac12LI^2$) and the likely failure (FET avalanche or IC pin damage), and also covers what else the diode does: it makes the node clamp near 0 V, and it blocks reverse current.
:::

::: why
This is the same physics as flyback/inductive-kick protection ([[q0144]]) in a different outfit, and it is the reason every non-isolated buck has a freewheeling path. It also leads directly into synchronous rectification: you can only understand why a low-side FET can replace the diode if you know exactly what job the diode does. Being able to estimate what happens without it (voltage and energy) is a quick test of engineering judgement.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor continuity**: $i_L$ cannot change instantly; $v_L=L\,di/dt$. See [[q0043]].
- **Inductive kick / flyback**: opening the current path of an inductor generates a large voltage. See [[q0144]].
- **Energy** $E=\tfrac12LI^2$ stored in the inductor must go somewhere. See [[q0041]].
- **Diode**: conducts when forward-biased, blocks when reverse-biased; forward drop $V_f$. See [[q0038]].
- **Avalanche**: breakdown of a FET or diode that can absorb limited energy.
:::

::: answer
"The diode gives the inductor current somewhere to go when the high-side FET turns off. The inductor current can't stop instantly, because $v = L\,di/dt$ would be infinite. So when the FET opens, the inductor forces its current to keep flowing by driving the switch node negative. With a diode from ground to the switch node, that happens until the node is about a diode drop below ground, then the diode conducts and the current freewheels through the inductor, load and diode, ramping down. That also makes the switch-node average work out to $D V_{in}$.

If you remove the diode, there's no path. The switch node would fly negative until something else conducts or breaks down. In my 12 V to 3.3 V example the inductor has about 15 microjoules of energy at the peak current. With nothing to clamp it, the node swings down until the high-side FET's drain-source avalanches, which for a 30 V part means the node going to about minus 18 V, and then dumps that energy into the FET each cycle: tens of microjoules at 500 kHz, which is watts in a tiny device. Realistically the FET or the controller's switch pin dies, or the circuit rings with hundreds of volts on a few picofarads.

So the diode is the freewheeling path, and a Schottky is used so the clamp is low and fast, which also helps efficiency."
:::

::: technical
**Without a clamp.** At turn-off, $i_L=I_{pk}=2.51$ A, $E=\tfrac12LI_{pk}^2=14.8\ \mu J$.

If the only capacitance is the node capacitance $C_{sw}$ (say 100 pF to 200 pF: FET $C_{oss}$, inductor winding capacitance, layout), the node swings to $V\approx I\sqrt{L/C_{sw}}$: for 200 pF, $2.5\sqrt{4.7\ \mu H/200\ pF}=383$ V (ideal, LC resonance). Nothing in the circuit tolerates it, so something breaks down first.

**Which device conducts first (discrete NFET high-side).** Source (SW) falls below ground: $V_{ds}=V_{in}-V_{SW}$ rises. When it reaches $BV_{DSS}$ (30 V device), the FET avalanches: $V_{SW}=V_{in}-BV_{DSS}=12-30=-18$ V. The inductor now sees $V_{SW}-V_{out}=-21.3$ V and the current decays in $t=LI_{pk}/21.3=554$ ns, flowing from $V_{in}$ through the avalanching FET into SW.

Energy per event: charge $Q=\tfrac12I_{pk}t=0.69\ \mu C$; the FET dissipates $BV\cdot Q=20.8\ \mu J$; the source gives $V_{in}Q=8.3\ \mu J$; the inductor gives 14.8 µJ; the load receives $V_{out}Q=2.3\ \mu J$. At 500 kHz that is up to about 10 W in the FET (if it repeated each cycle at this current), far above any avalanche rating for repetitive events.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exactly what breaks first (FET, driver pin, an ESD structure, or a parasitic diode) depends on the specific device, and on an integrated converter the SW pin ESD diode would clamp near $-0.7$ V and carry amps, usually destroying the IC (latch-up). The numbers above use an idealised 30 V avalanche to show scale, not a prediction for a particular part.

**With a diode.** The SW node clamps at $-V_f$ (0.3 to 0.5 V for a Schottky); the inductor current freewheels with slope $-(V_{out}+V_f)/L$. Diode current: $i_L$ during $(1-D)T$, average $(1-D)I_{out}=1.45$ A, rms 1.72 A, power $V_f\times1.45=0.73$ W (for 0.5 V), reverse voltage $V_{in}+$ringing.

**Also:** the diode blocks reverse current, so $i_L$ cannot go negative (DCM at light load).
:::

::: short
"The diode is the freewheeling path. When the high-side FET opens, the inductor current can't stop, so the inductor forces the switch node negative until something conducts. The diode does that at about minus 0.4 V and lets the current ramp down through the load. Without it the node would fly down until the FET avalanches or the controller's switch pin breaks: in my example the inductor holds about 15 µJ at turn-off, and the FET would absorb roughly 20 µJ per cycle, which it can't take. It also sets the average switch-node voltage to $D\,V_{in}$."
:::

::: long
"The diode in a buck is there for the inductor. During the on time the inductor stores energy: at the end of the on time the current is about 2.5 amps in my example, so about 15 microjoules. When the high-side FET turns off, that current has to continue, because an inductor can't change its current instantly. If there's no path, the inductor does what it always does with a broken circuit: $v = L\,di/dt$ gets large, and the switch node swings negative as far as needed to force the current through.

With the diode, the node only swings to a diode drop below ground, about minus 0.4 volts for a Schottky, and then the diode conducts the full inductor current: the current freewheels from ground through the diode, the inductor and the load, ramping down at $V_{out}$ plus the diode drop over $L$. The cycle repeats.

Without the diode the node would go as low as something breaks. The first candidate is the high-side FET itself: its drain is at the input, so as the source goes negative its drain-source voltage rises, and a 30 V FET avalanches when the node reaches about minus 18 volts. Then you'd be dumping about 20 microjoules into the FET's avalanche every cycle, which at 500 kHz is around 10 watts of repetitive avalanche in a small device. It won't survive. In an IC with an integrated switch, the pin's ESD structure conducts instead and the IC latches or burns.

That's also what makes the circuit regulate: the diode makes the switch node a clean square wave between $V_{in}$ and about zero, so its average is $D V_{in}$. And it blocks reverse current, so at light load the inductor current stops at zero rather than going negative. A synchronous FET replaces the diode to cut the 0.7 watt drop loss."
:::

::: terms
- **Freewheeling (flyback) diode**: diode that carries the inductor current when the switch opens.
- **Catch diode**: another name for the buck's diode.
- **Inductive kick**: large voltage from interrupting inductor current.
- **Avalanche**: breakdown where the FET conducts and dissipates energy at $BV_{DSS}$.
- **$V_f$**: forward voltage drop; Schottky 0.3 to 0.5 V, silicon 0.7 V.
- **Reverse voltage rating $V_R$**: must exceed $V_{in}$ plus ringing.
- **Reverse recovery**: charge that must be removed when a diode switches off; negligible for Schottky.
- **Synchronous rectification**: low-side FET replacing the diode.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The diode rectifies the output."** There is no AC; it is a freewheeling path for the inductor.
- **"Without the diode the output would just be lower."** Without a path the converter fails destructively; the output isn't merely lower.
- **"The diode conducts during the on state."** It is reverse-biased with $-V_{in}$ across it; it conducts only in the off state.
- **Choosing a diode on average current only.** Peak current (2.5 A), reverse voltage (with ringing margin), and thermal limits matter too.
- **Using a slow silicon rectifier.** Reverse recovery causes shoot-through-like spikes and loss; use a Schottky or a synchronous FET.
- **Saying the body diode of the high-side FET is enough.** Its body diode points from SW to $V_{in}$ (anode at the source), so it would conduct only if SW rose above $V_{in}$, not when SW goes negative.
:::

::: deep
## The problem: opening a switch in series with an inductor

During the on time $Q_1$ carries $i_L$ from the input into the inductor. At the end of the on time the controller opens $Q_1$. For an ideal inductor, $i_L$ cannot change instantly. Before the switch opens the current path was input → $Q_1$ → $L$ → output. After it opens, that path is gone. The inductor still *has* 2.51 A, so something has to carry it.

Energy: $E=\tfrac12LI^2=\tfrac12\times4.7\ \mu H\times2.51^2=14.8\ \mu J$.

Where could the current go?
1. **Through parasitic capacitance** (FET $C_{oss}$, winding capacitance): the node then rings up to $I\sqrt{L/C}$, hundreds of volts.
2. **Through the high-side FET in avalanche**: the node reaches $V_{in}-BV_{DSS}$.
3. **Through the controller's SW pin ESD structure** (integrated converters): the node goes to about −0.7 V and the structure carries amps until it fails or latches.
4. **Through the low-side device**, if it exists: that is the diode or synchronous FET.

For a discrete 30 V NFET the avalanche scenario gives the numbers in the technical section: the inductor sees $-21.3$ V, the current decays over 554 ns, and the FET absorbs 20.8 µJ per event.

Figure: Off state with the diode: ground → diode → SW → inductor → load/capacitor → ground. The diode clamps the node at $-V_f$ and conducts the full inductor current.

Three functions:
1. **Freewheeling path.** Conducts $i_L$ for $(1-D)T$.
2. **Defines the switch-node waveform** as a rectangle between $V_{in}$ and $-V_f$, so that the average is $D V_{in}$.
3. **Blocks reverse current**, so the inductor current cannot go negative: that gives DCM at light load.

### Ratings

| Parameter | Requirement | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Reverse voltage $V_R$ | $>V_{in}$ + ringing margin | 12 V in: 30 V Schottky |
| Average forward current | $(1-D)I_{out}$ | 1.45 A |
| Peak forward current | $I_{pk}$ | 2.51 A |
| Power | $V_f\times(1-D)I_{out}$ | 0.5 V → 0.73 W |
| Reverse recovery / capacitance | small | Schottky: negligible $Q_{rr}$ |
| Leakage (hot) | acceptable at $V_{in}$ | µA to mA depending on part |

The 0.73 W is 11% of the 6.6 W output; that single loss is what makes synchronous rectification worthwhile at low output voltages ([[q0203]]).

## Failure modes of the diode itself

- **Open**: same as having no diode: the destructive scenario above.
- **Short**: $Q_1$ then shorts $V_{in}$ to ground each on-time; the input collapses or a fuse/current limit trips.
- **Slow diode**: reverse-recovery current spikes through $Q_1$ at turn-on: an extra current of $Q_{rr}$-related amps for tens of ns, causing loss, ringing and EMI.

## Choosing the diode: a worked rating check

Take the 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A example and ask what the diode must survive.

- **Forward current.** Average $(1-D)I_{out}=0.725\times2=1.45$ A and a peak of 2.51 A. A 3 A part gives margin, and the datasheet's forward-voltage curve at the peak current, not the average, tells you the real drop.
- **Reverse voltage.** The DC value is 12 V, but when $Q_1$ turns on the diode is slammed from forward conduction to reverse bias in a few nanoseconds. The diode's junction capacitance and the hot-loop inductance then ring. Assume 200 pF of junction capacitance and 10 nH of loop inductance (illustrative): the ring is at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})=113$ MHz with a characteristic impedance $Z_0=\sqrt{L/C}=7.1\ \Omega$. As an upper bound, a current of 1.49 A (the valley current being commutated) into 7.1 Ω gives an overshoot of up to $1.49\times7.1=10.5$ V, so the diode may see 22.5 V. With a common 80% derating guideline that calls for at least 28 V, which makes a 30 V part marginal and a 40 V part the sensible choice.
- **Power and heat.** A 0.5 V drop at 1.45 A average is 0.725 W. With an assumed 50 °C/W junction-to-ambient thermal resistance, that is a 36 °C rise, which matters because Schottky leakage grows steeply with temperature.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The ringing estimate is an upper bound from an abrupt current step, not a prediction; the real amplitude depends on edge rate, damping and layout. Schottky leakage versus temperature is part-specific and sometimes rises by an order of magnitude over a few tens of degrees; read the leakage curve at your maximum junction temperature.

## Four ways to provide the freewheel path

| Option | Drop at 2 A | Loss at $(1-D)=0.725$ | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silicon fast-recovery diode | about 0.9 V (typical) | 1.31 W | Slow recovery, rarely used at this voltage |
| Schottky diode | about 0.5 V | 0.73 W | Negligible recovery charge, leakage with temperature |
| Synchronous NFET, $R_{DS(on)}=10$ mΩ | $I R=20$ mV | 29 mW | Needs a gate driver and dead time |
| Body diode only, during dead time | about 0.7 V | 28 mW at 20 ns dead time | Carries current only briefly; recovery charge matters |

The Schottky loses 0.73 W of a 6.6 W output, roughly 11% of the output power; the synchronous FET loses 29 mW, about 0.4%, a 25 times improvement. The extra cost is a second FET, a high-side/low-side driver and dead-time logic, and a potential shoot-through failure mode if the timing is wrong ([[q0203]]).

## What the diode removes from the circuit's behavior

Besides carrying current, the diode defines the shape of the switch-node waveform and hence the transfer function. Without a second switch, the diode also decides what happens at light load: because it blocks reverse current, the inductor current stops at zero and the converter enters DCM, in which $V_{out}$ depends on load as well as duty. A synchronous stage that is allowed to conduct in reverse (forced CCM) behaves like a mathematically cleaner converter at the cost of circulating current.

## Limiting cases for the diode's role

- $D\to1$: the diode conducts only for a tiny fraction of the cycle and its loss $V_f(1-D)I$ vanishes. Dropout-region bucks barely need it.
- $D\to0$ (a large step-down, say 48 V to 1 V): the diode conducts almost all the time. At 1 V out with a 0.4 V Schottky, the diode drop is 40% of the output voltage, which drives efficiency down and justifies synchronous rectification.
- $V_{out}$ near zero (start-up, short circuit): the diode's forward drop is then the only downward force on the inductor current, which is how the current staircase builds up in a fault ([[q0195]]).

## Bench verification

1. Probe SW with a tip-and-barrel connection. During the off-state the flat level should sit at $-V_f$ (about $-0.4$ to $-0.5$ V for a Schottky at 2 A). If it is far more negative the diode is undersized, hot or has a bad connection.
2. Look at the turn-on edge for the ringing amplitude and frequency. A ring of tens of volts at 100 MHz or more means the hot loop is too large or the diode capacitance is too high; a small RC snubber across the diode is the usual cure.
3. Measure the diode case temperature after 10 minutes at full load in the worst ambient. Compare with the datasheet's maximum junction temperature, accounting for the leakage rising with heat.
4. Never test the "no diode" case with a hard supply on a real board. If you need to see it, simulate it, or use a current-limited supply and a sacrificial board.

::: applications
- **Every asynchronous buck** (low-cost regulators like TO-220 style modules, LED drivers) uses a Schottky catch diode.
- **Boost and buck-boost converters** have the same freewheeling/output diode logic ([[q0144]]).
- **Relay and solenoid drivers** use a flyback diode in the same way.
- **Motor H-bridges** rely on body diodes or diodes for freewheeling current.
- **Synchronous converters** keep the body diode as the backup path during dead time.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM**, peak inductor current 2.51 A, $V_{in}=12$ V, 30 V FET for the illustrative avalanche numbers.
- **Discrete NFET high-side** and parasitic capacitance of 100 to 200 pF; values are illustrative.
- **Schottky diode** with $V_f$ around 0.4 to 0.5 V; silicon diodes are 0.7 V and slower.
- **No other clamp** (no TVS, no synchronous FET, no snubber). A TVS or RC snubber could limit the voltage but would dissipate the energy or reduce efficiency.
- **Integrated converters** differ: the SW pin typically has ESD diodes and limited negative-voltage tolerance.
:::

::: followups
### What happens if the diode is slow (a 1N400x rectifier)?
It has reverse-recovery charge and a slower forward turn-on, so at turn-on of $Q_1$ the diode conducts in reverse for tens to hundreds of ns, shorting the input through $Q_1$ with an amp-level spike, causing loss, ringing and EMI. Use a Schottky or synchronous rectification.

### Why a Schottky and not a normal silicon diode?
Lower forward drop (0.3 to 0.5 V vs 0.7 V or more at amps) and no minority-carrier stored charge, so negligible reverse recovery. The downsides are higher reverse leakage (rising with temperature) and lower voltage ratings in the common parts.

### Could a TVS or RC snubber replace the diode?
They can limit the voltage spike but they do not provide a conduction path for the load current: the inductor energy would be dumped into the clamp every cycle (15 µJ at 500 kHz is 7 W), which is hugely lossy and the output would collapse. The diode is the only element that returns that energy to the load.

### What is the reverse voltage across the diode and why does the rating need margin?
$V_{in}$ (12 V) during the on state, plus ringing at the turn-on edge that can overshoot by 30 to 100% depending on layout. A 30 V Schottky is typical for a 12 V input.

### Harder: could the high-side body diode serve as the freewheel path?
No. The high-side NFET's body diode has its anode at the source (SW) and cathode at the drain ($V_{in}$): it conducts when SW exceeds $V_{in}$, not when SW goes below ground. It would only help if the inductor current direction were reversed. With a high-side PFET the body diode also points to $V_{in}$. So the clamp must be a separate low-side element.
:::

- Schottky vs silicon, and the link to synchronous rectification :: Connects to design alternatives.
