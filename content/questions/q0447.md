::: basics
### In plain words
Low-side sensing puts the sense resistor between the load and ground. High-side puts it between the supply and the load. Low-side is simple and cheap, but it lifts the load's ground a little and cannot see a short from the load to ground. High-side keeps the load grounded and sees every fault, but the amplifier must handle the bus voltage on both its inputs. The choice depends on whether the load must be grounded and which faults you must catch.

### Picture it
Imagine a water meter on a building's pipes. A meter at the tap (high side) counts all the water that leaves the supply, even if a pipe leaks straight into the ground. A meter at the drain (low side) only counts water that reaches the drain, so a leak that bypasses the drain goes unseen. But a meter at the tap sits under full water pressure, so it must be a sturdier meter. That sturdier meter is the high-side amplifier. The analogy leaves out the small ground lift that the drain-side meter causes.

### Words you need
- **High-side sensing**: shunt in the supply path, between source and load.
- **Low-side sensing**: shunt in the return path, between load and ground.
- **Common-mode voltage**: the voltage both amplifier inputs share relative to ground.
- **CMRR**: the amplifier's ability to ignore common-mode voltage, in dB. See [[q0115]].
- **Ground lift**: the voltage $I R_s$ by which a low-side shunt raises the load's ground.

### The simplest example
A low-side 10 mΩ shunt carrying 5 A lifts the load's ground by $5 \times 0.01 = 50$ mV. That is the price for a simple circuit, plus it misses a load wire shorted to ground. Now take a high-side shunt on a 48 V bus with a 10 mV signal. To keep the error under 100 µV, the amplifier's CMRR must satisfy $48 / 10^{\text{CMRR}/20} \le 0.0001$, which needs about 114 dB. An amplifier with 80 dB would add $48 / 10^4 = 4.8$ mV of error, half the signal. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers fault coverage, PWM edges on the bus, inline motor sensing and a set of rules for choosing.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand **where the sense element sits relative to ground and the load** and the consequences of that placement: common-mode voltage on the amplifier inputs, ground shift seen by the load, and which faults the sensor can detect.

The question has three parts: define the two, give advantages and disadvantages, and say *when each is required*. Strong answers tie the choice to concrete constraints (grounded load, short-to-ground detection, bus voltage, cost) instead of "high side is better".
:::

::: why
This is one of the most common real design decisions in power electronics: a hot-swap or battery monitor wants high-side sensing; a motor driver half-bridge often uses low-side shunts; USB and automotive loads must keep a solid chassis ground. Choosing wrong either lifts the ground of your system, misses a dangerous fault, or forces an expensive high-voltage amplifier. The common-mode rejection angle also connects to [[q0451]] (CSA vs op-amp) and [[q0452]] (how to spec a CSA).
:::

::: concepts
- **Ground reference and ground shift**: current through a low-side shunt lifts the load's ground by $IR_s$. See [[q0443]].
- **Common-mode voltage**: both amplifier inputs sit near the bus voltage in high-side sensing; the amplifier needs that input range and high CMRR. See [[q0115]].
- **Fault detection**: a short from load to ground in a low-side-sensed system bypasses the shunt.
- **KCL on the ground return**: all return current paths must pass through the shunt for low-side sensing to see them.
- **Differential amplifier**: the circuit that extracts the small signal on a large common-mode voltage. See [[q0449]].
- **Half-bridge phase sensing**: an in-line variant used in motor drives.
:::

::: answer
"Low-side sensing puts the sense resistor between the load and ground. High-side puts it between the supply and the load.

Low-side is simple and cheap: the sense voltage sits near ground, so almost any amplifier or even an ADC can read it, and the common-mode problem disappears. The downsides are that the load's ground is lifted by $I R_s$, which can disturb other circuits, and it can't see a fault where the load or its wiring shorts to ground, because that current never goes through the shunt.

High-side keeps the load solidly grounded and does see a short to ground, because all the supply current goes through the shunt. The cost is that the sense voltage rides on the bus voltage, so I need a current sense amplifier with high common-mode range and good CMRR. That's more expensive and, for high voltages, needs level shifting or isolation.

So I use high-side when the load must be grounded, when I need short-to-ground detection, like battery or hot-swap protection, and for common-ground systems with multiple loads. I use low-side when the system tolerates a small ground shift, the bus voltage is high or noisy, and I want the cheapest, simplest sense, like motor-driver legs or small embedded boards."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.**
- Low-side: $V_{bus}\to$ load $\to R_s \to$ GND. Sense voltage common-mode $\approx 0$ V.
- High-side: $V_{bus}\to R_s \to$ load $\to$ GND. Common-mode $\approx V_{bus} - IR_s/2$.

**Common-mode error.** A difference amplifier with CMRR (dB) turns a common-mode voltage $V_{cm}$ into an input-referred error $V_{err} = V_{cm}/10^{CMRR/20}$.
- 48 V bus with 80 dB: $48/10^4 = 4.8$ mV, which is half of a 10 mV signal.
- To keep error ≤ 100 µV (1 percent of 10 mV) on 48 V requires $48/10^{-4} = 4.8\times10^5$, which is **113.6 dB**.
- Note: monolithic CSAs usually specify 90 to 120 dB; PWM edges on the bus also stress CMRR at high frequency.

**Ground shift (low-side).** $\Delta V_{gnd} = IR_s$. At 5 A and 10 mΩ, 50 mV. That shift adds to every ground-referenced signal sensed relative to the "true" ground and reduces logic noise margin.

**Fault coverage.** A short from load to chassis ground diverts current around the shunt in the low-side case; in the high-side case the shunt carries the full fault current (if the short is downstream of it).

**Headroom.** High-side shunt drop reduces the voltage the load sees by $IR_s$; low-side reduces the load's ground-to-supply voltage by the same amount. Both are equal to first order.

**Amplifier requirements.**

| | Low-side | High-side |
|---|---|---|
| Input common-mode | ≈ 0 V (may be slightly below ground for bidirectional) | $V_{bus}$ |
| Amplifier | op-amp or CSA with input range to ground | CSA rated for bus voltage |
| Output | already ground-referenced | ground-referenced (CSA level-shifts) |
| Cost | lower | higher |
:::

::: short
"Low-side puts the shunt between load and ground: simple, cheap, near-zero common-mode, but it lifts the load's ground by $IR_s$ and can't see a short-to-ground fault. High-side puts it between supply and load: load stays grounded and short-to-ground is detected, but the amplifier must handle bus-level common-mode voltage and have good CMRR. I use high-side when the load must be grounded or for protection, and low-side when a small ground shift is fine and I want simplicity."
:::

::: long
"The names say where the shunt goes. Low-side is between the load and ground; high-side is between the supply and the load.

Low-side first. Because the shunt's bottom end is ground, the sense signal is a few millivolts above ground, so I don't need any exotic common-mode range. A plain op-amp that works down to ground, or even a single-supply CSA, is enough, which keeps the cost and complexity low. The downsides are that the load's ground is no longer true ground, it floats up by $I R_s$, say 50 millivolts at 5 amps and 10 milliohms. That can matter for other signals sharing the load's ground. And it can't see a fault where the load shorts to ground, because that current returns by another path and skips the shunt.

High-side is the opposite. The load stays solidly grounded, and if anything downstream shorts to ground, all that current flows through the shunt, so I can detect it. This is why battery monitors, hot-swap controllers, and protection circuits use high-side. The price is that both amplifier inputs sit near the bus voltage. On a 48 volt bus with a 10 millivolt signal, I need a CSA rated for 48 volts with something like 110 dB of CMRR to keep the error below one percent. Those parts exist but cost more, and for hundreds of volts you need level shifting or isolation.

So: high-side when the load must be grounded, when short-to-ground has to be caught, or when multiple loads share a ground. Low-side when the ground shift is acceptable, voltages are high, or I'm sensing a motor leg or a small embedded rail and want the simplest circuit."
:::

::: terms
- **High-side sensing**: shunt in the supply path between source and load.
- **Low-side sensing**: shunt in the return path between load and ground.
- **Common-mode voltage**: the average voltage of the amplifier's two inputs relative to ground.
- **CMRR**: ability of an amplifier to reject common-mode, in dB; error = $V_{cm}/10^{CMRR/20}$.
- **Ground lift / ground shift**: the voltage $IR_s$ by which a low-side shunt raises the load's ground.
- **Short-to-ground detection**: the ability to see a fault where the load path bypasses the shunt.
- **Inline (phase) sensing**: shunt in series with a motor phase; common-mode swings with the PWM.
- **Level shifting**: translating a signal on a high common-mode voltage to a ground-referenced one.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "high-side is between the supply and load; low-side is between load and ground; high-side needs a special amplifier".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies both costs: ground shift $IR_s$ for low-side and the CMRR required for high-side (for example 114 dB for 48 V, 10 mV, 1 percent).
- Names the fault-coverage difference (short-to-ground) and why that decides protection designs.
- Gives rules for when each is needed: grounded load, shared ground, chassis-grounded systems, motor legs, high bus voltages.
- Mentions PWM common-mode edges (inline motor phase sensing) as a harder case, with dV/dt immunity and CMRR at frequency.
- Notes isolation as the third option when the bus is too high for any non-isolated CSA.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"High-side is always better."** It costs more, and for many boards a low-side shunt is perfectly adequate.
- **Ignoring that a low-side shunt can't detect a short-to-ground.** Fault current returns through the short, not the shunt.
- **Putting a standard op-amp on a high-side shunt** without checking the input common-mode range.
- **Forgetting ground shift.** The mV of lifted ground can corrupt ADC readings, sensor outputs, or communication thresholds sharing that ground.
- **Quoting DC CMRR only.** On switching buses the CMRR at the PWM edge frequency is lower, and dV/dt spikes can saturate the amplifier.
- **Mixing up which node is "high".** High-side means on the supply side of the load, not "at the top of the schematic".
:::

## What changes

### Common-mode voltage and CMRR

An ideal difference amplifier ignores anything common to both inputs. A real one converts a fraction $1/\text{CMRR}$ of the common-mode voltage into a differential error:

$$V_{err} = \frac{V_{cm}}{\text{CMRR}}$$

Figure: Error (referred to the input) falls 10x per 20 dB of CMRR. For a 48 V bus to stay below 100 µV needs about 114 dB; 400 V needs about 132 dB, beyond most standard CSAs, which is why very high voltages use isolation or low-side sensing.

Low-side sensing has $V_{cm} \approx 0$, so the entire problem vanishes.

### Ground shift

For a low-side shunt, the load's ground sits at $IR_s$ above system ground. At 5 A, 10 mΩ: 50 mV. If the load includes an ADC or a sensor, its reference moves relative to the controller's ground by that amount, and a 5 V logic line driven from the controller sees 50 mV less noise margin at the load. For a motor driver's gate-drive reference, the lifted ground is also in the high-current return path, so the sense node bounces with $L\,dI/dt$.

For high-side sensing, the load keeps true ground, but its supply is reduced by $IR_s$, the same voltage drop, shifted to the other rail.

### Fault detection

The question "can the sensor see every dangerous fault?" often decides it:

| Fault | Low-side sees it? | High-side sees it? |
|---|---|---|
| Overload in the load | Yes | Yes |
| Short across the load's terminals | Yes (current flows through R_s) | Yes |
| Load or its wire shorts to ground (bypasses R_s) | **No** | Yes |
| Supply shorted to ground upstream of the shunt | N/A | N/A (no sense) |

For battery packs and automotive loads, the chassis is ground and a wire can short to it, so high-side (or both) is required.

### When PWM is on the bus

In a motor-drive half-bridge, a shunt in series with the phase (inline sensing) sees a common-mode voltage swinging between 0 and the bus at the PWM frequency: high dV/dt. The CSA must have good CMRR at that frequency and fast recovery. This is the hardest case, and the one for which special CSAs ("PWM-rejection" parts) exist. Low-leg shunts in the half-bridge avoid it at the cost of seeing current only while the low FET is on.

## Choosing: the rules

**Use high-side when:**
- the load must be tied directly to ground (chassis ground, multiple loads sharing a ground, a USB device, sensors referencing ground);
- you must detect a short to ground (battery protection, hot-swap, automotive harnesses);
- the same shunt serves charge and discharge or a bidirectional bus.

**Use low-side when:**
- a small ground shift is tolerable (isolated motor driver, embedded rail with one controller);
- the bus voltage is high or noisy, making a high-side CSA expensive;
- you want the simplest and cheapest circuit.

**Use inline (phase) or isolated sensing when** the load itself is switched (motor phase currents) or the bus is at hundreds of volts relative to the controller.

> [!TIP]
> In an interview, mention that "high side" and "low side" is a **system choice, not an amplifier choice**. The CSA only follows from where the shunt sits and what the common-mode range must be ([[q0452]]).

## Worked example: CMRR of a discrete difference amplifier

The CMRR plot assumes an integrated amplifier. If you build the high-side stage from an op-amp and four resistors, the CMRR is set by the resistor matching, not the op-amp. For a difference amplifier with gain $G$ and resistors each off by up to a fraction $t$ in the worst direction, the worst-case result is approximately

$$\text{CMRR}\approx\frac{1+G}{4t}$$

With unity gain and 1 % resistors this is $2/0.04 = 50$ (34 dB). With gain 50 and 0.1 % resistors it is $51/0.004 = 12{,}750$ (82 dB), and with 1 % resistors at gain 50 it is $51/0.04 = 1275$ (62 dB). Apply the 1 % version to a 12 V bus with a 10 mΩ shunt. The common-mode error is $12/1275 = 9.4$ mV referred to the input. On a 10 mΩ shunt that is a phantom $9.4\text{ mV}/10\text{ m}\Omega = 0.94$ A, which could be more than the whole load. With the 82 dB version the error falls to 0.94 mV or 94 mA. Compare the integrated parts: a CSA with 100 dB CMRR on a 48 V bus gives $48/10^5 = 480$ µV, or 48 mA on the same 10 mΩ shunt. Hitting the 100 µV goal at 48 V needs 114 dB, and at 400 V it needs 132 dB. These are just the arithmetic behind "buy an integrated, laser-trimmed CSA for the high side".

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The $(1+G)/(4t)$ expression is the standard worst-case result for a four-resistor difference stage; typical, statistically combined values are better. Real op-amp CMRR and the common-mode range also limit the result, and datasheet CMRR is often specified only at DC or low frequency.

## Worked example: how much does the ground move?

A low-side shunt of 5 mΩ carrying 20 A develops 100 mV, and the load's "ground" is now 100 mV above the controller's ground. A 3.3 V sensor on that load that outputs a ratiometric voltage, read by an ADC referenced to the controller ground, picks up the whole 100 mV as a current-dependent offset: 3 % of the 3.3 V span, or about 124 counts on a 12-bit converter. The error is not noise; it varies with load current, so it looks like a gain error elsewhere in the system. Two fixes: keep sensors on the controller side of the shunt, so they never see the lifted node, or sense the shunt voltage differentially and refer all other measurements to the same ground point. The high-side placement has no such shift, but the load now loses 100 mV of supply, which for a 3.3 V rail is a 3 % droop that the load may or may not tolerate.

## Worked example: the PWM time budget for an inline shunt

Suppose an inline phase shunt in a 48 V drive sees a common-mode step of 48 V in about 20 ns, which is 2.4 V/ns. After each edge the amplifier needs time to recover from that overload before its output is valid. With a 1 µs recovery and a 20 kHz PWM at 10 % duty (5 µs of on-time), 20 % of the on-time is lost to settling, leaving 4 µs for the ADC to sample. At 5 % duty it would leave only 1.5 µs, and below some duty the measurement window disappears entirely. Real drives deal with that by shifting the sampling instants, by using two shunts and choosing the better one, or by estimating the third phase from the other two. The point to take away is that the high-dV/dt case is a timing problem as much as a CMRR problem.

## Verifying on the bench

- **Measure CMRR directly:** tie both amplifier inputs together to a variable supply (no shunt current), and plot the output as the supply sweeps from 0 V to the bus voltage. The output change divided by the gain gives the common-mode error in volts at the input; the applied common-mode change divided by that error is the CMRR at DC. Repeat with a square wave at the PWM frequency to see how it falls with frequency.
- **Measure the ground shift:** with the load running at full current, use a DMM to read the voltage between the load's ground pin and the controller ground. It should equal $IR_s$ plus any trace drop.
- **Fault injection:** with a current-limited supply, short the load's ground terminal to chassis. A low-side sensor will read little or nothing while the supply current climbs; a high-side sensor will rise. This is the quick way to show a reviewer why the placement matters for a given product.
- **Dynamic check:** probe the amplifier output next to the gate drive at the switching edges, and measure the time from the edge until the output is within 1 % of its final value.
:::

::: applications
- **Hot-swap and eFuse controllers**: high-side shunt with a comparator, detecting any overload including shorts to ground.
- **USB-C power monitors, server rail monitors**: high-side shunts with digital power monitors.
- **Battery packs**: low-side shunts (before the BMS ground) for coulomb counting, plus high-side protection FETs.
- **BLDC motor drives**: low-leg shunts for phase current at moderate cost; inline sensing or isolated amplifiers for high-performance drives.
- **Automotive 12 V loads**: high-side sensing, since the chassis is ground and shorts to chassis must be seen.
- **Small battery embedded boards**: low-side shunt with a simple op-amp or CSA.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes a single shunt on a DC or slowly varying bus. For switched nodes, edges and CMRR at frequency add to the common-mode problem.
- Assumes a non-isolated measurement. With an isolated amplifier or Hall sensor, the high/low distinction mostly disappears.
- "Detects short-to-ground" assumes the short is downstream of the shunt and returns to the same supply; other fault topologies may still be hidden.
- Alternative answer: some designs use both (a low-side shunt for control accuracy plus a high-side comparator for protection).
- CMRR figures quoted are typical for monolithic CSAs and shift by vendor and frequency.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Real CSAs specify CMRR at DC and sometimes at specific frequencies; the number that matters in a PWM bus is the high-frequency CMRR and the dV/dt rating, which should be read from the datasheet.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a low-side shunt lift the load's ground, and does it matter?
Current returns to the supply through the shunt, so the load's ground node sits at $IR_s$ above the system ground. For 5 A and 10 mΩ that's 50 mV. It matters if the load shares signals with the controller (logic levels, analog references, communication), or if the shunt value is large. For a self-contained motor or LED driver it often doesn't.

### How can a low-side shunt miss a fault?
If the load, its cable or a connector shorts to chassis ground, the current returns through the short rather than through the shunt in the return path. The shunt then reads normal or low current while a very high current flows. High-side sensing in the supply path sees all current drawn from the source.

### What CMRR do you need for a 48 V high-side shunt with a 20 mV full-scale signal and 0.5 percent error?
Allowed error is $0.005\times0.02 = 100\ \mu$V. Required rejection is $48/10^{-4} = 4.8\times10^5$ = 113.6 dB. A typical monolithic CSA is rated 90 to 120 dB, so you'd choose a high-CMRR part, or increase the shunt to raise the signal.

### Scenario: your high-side current readings spike when the load PWM turns on. What's going on?
The common-mode voltage is jumping with each PWM edge and the amplifier's finite CMRR (especially at the edge frequency) or its recovery time turns it into a differential spike. Fixes: a CSA rated for PWM common-mode rejection, an RC input filter matched on both inputs, sampling away from the edges, or moving to low-side or inline-with-blanking sensing.

### What if the bus is 400 V and the controller is at 3.3 V?
A standard high-side CSA is out of range. Use low-side sensing if the architecture allows, a high-voltage level-shifting CSA if available, or an isolated amplifier/isolated delta-sigma modulator, or a magnetic sensor ([[q0441]]).
