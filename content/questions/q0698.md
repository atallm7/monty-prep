::: basics
### In plain words
An eye diagram is a picture made by laying every bit of a long data stream on top of each other, all lined up to the same one-bit time slot. The traces cross and bunch into a shape like an eye. The open space in the middle is where the receiver can safely read a 1 or a 0. A big, clear opening means plenty of margin.

### Picture it
Take a long strip of paper with the data waveform drawn on it, cut it into pieces exactly one bit long, and stack them on a light table. Where the pieces agree, the lines are sharp. Where they differ, they spread. The clear gap left in the middle is the eye. It shows the worst case among all the bits, not the average.

### Words you need
- **Bit time (unit interval, UI)**: how long one bit lasts. At 10 Gb/s that is 100 ps.
- **Eye height**: how much voltage separates a 1 from a 0 at the best sampling moment.
- **Eye width**: how much time the receiver has to sample, around that moment.
- **Inter-symbol interference (ISI)**: each bit's waveform getting smeared by the bits before it, because the channel has memory.
- **Jitter**: edges arriving early or late, which blurs the crossings sideways ([[q0695]]).

### The simplest example
At 10 Gb/s each bit lasts 100 ps. Suppose a perfect signal swings 800 mV between its 0 and 1 levels. The ideal eye is 800 mV tall and 100 ps wide.

Now a lossy cable smears the bits. ISI closes the eye by 200 mV in total, part from the top and part from the bottom, leaving 600 mV of height. Jitter makes the crossings fuzzy and takes 20 ps from the width, leaving 80 ps.

That is the whole idea: one picture of all the bits, with the opening showing the margin. The rest of this page shows how a scope builds it and how each impairment changes its shape.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can explain the eye as **all bits overlaid on one unit interval** and read it as a margin picture: the opening is where the receiver can safely sample. Strong candidates say how it's made (clock-triggered or recovered-clock persistence overlay of a long pseudo-random pattern), what the two axes mean (time within a UI, voltage), what each feature tells you (eye height, eye width, crossing, rise/fall, thickness of the traces), and why it combines ISI, noise, jitter and crosstalk into one picture. Mentioning that the PRBS pattern exercises every bit-history is a plus.
:::

::: why
The eye diagram is the standard way to judge a high-speed serial link at a glance, and compliance tests for USB, PCIe, Ethernet, HDMI and many others are defined as masks on an eye. A designer can look at one picture and tell whether the problem is loss (closed from the top and bottom), reflections (ripple), jitter (blurred crossings) or noise (thick rails). The following questions in this section ([[q0699]]–[[q0703]]) all build on it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Unit interval (UI)**: one bit time, $1/\text{bit rate}$. See [[q0695]].
- **NRZ data and pseudo-random patterns (PRBS)**: sequences that contain every bit history. 
- **Inter-symbol interference (ISI)**: each bit's waveform depends on previous bits because the channel has memory. See [[q0687]].
- **Persistence display / triggering**: a scope overlays many acquisitions triggered by a clock.
- **Differential signalling**: eye is usually measured on $V_+ - V_-$. See [[q0697]] and [[q0680]].
- **Jitter and noise**: the horizontal and vertical blur of the eye. See [[q0703]].
:::

::: answer
"An eye diagram is an overlay of every bit of a data stream, folded onto a single bit period, so that all the transitions and levels are drawn on top of each other. You make it on a scope by triggering from the clock, or a recovered clock, and displaying many bits with persistence. The result looks like an eye: the open area in the middle is where the receiver can sample the data and get the right answer.

What it represents is the margin of the link. The vertical opening, the eye height, shows how much voltage separates a 1 from a 0 at the sampling point after ISI and noise. The horizontal opening, eye width, shows how much time you have around the sampling instant, which is the unit interval minus jitter. The crossing points show timing and duty cycle, and the thickness of the traces tells you about noise and jitter.

It's useful because a single picture combines all the impairments: loss and reflections show up as ISI, closing the eye from above and below; jitter blurs the crossings; noise thickens the rails. A wide open, clean eye means low error rate; a closed eye means the receiver will make errors. And you compare it to a mask for compliance."
:::

::: technical
**Construction.** Take a long serial waveform $v(t)$ of NRZ data at bit period $T = 1/R$ (the UI). Choose a clock reference $t_k = kT$ (the transmit clock, or a clock recovered from the data). Overlay all segments $v(t_k + \tau)$ for $\tau \in [-T/2, 3T/2)$ (one-and-a-half UI wide, or $[0, 2T)$ for a two-UI view), accumulating a persistence/density histogram in the $(\tau, v)$ plane. Each trace is the response to one specific bit history, so the eye is the union over all histories. Using a PRBS-$n$ pattern, which contains every $n$-bit sequence except all-zeros, guarantees the worst-case ISI patterns of up to $n-1$ preceding bits appear.

**Features.**
- *Logic levels*: the settled 1 and 0 rails; eye amplitude is their difference.
- *Eye height* $H_{eye}$: vertical opening at the sampling point (often at the centre, or at a specified BER contour). Roughly $H_{eye} \approx A_{pp} - ISI_{pp} - 2Q\sigma_v$.
- *Eye width* $W_{eye}$: horizontal opening at the decision threshold, $\approx UI - TJ$.
- *Crossing points*: where rising and falling edges cross, ideally at 50% amplitude; the *crossing percentage* and *duty-cycle distortion* are read here.
- *Rise/fall time*, *overshoot/undershoot*, *jitter* (width of crossings), *noise* (thickness of rails).

**Numbers.** At 10 Gb/s the UI is 100 ps. If the swing is 800 mV pk-pk, ISI closes 200 mV, and Gaussian noise of 12 mV rms is taken at $\pm7.03\sigma$ for BER $10^{-12}$ ($2\cdot7.03\cdot12 = 169$ mV), the eye height is $800 - 200 - 169 = 431$ mV. With total jitter 24 ps at that BER the eye width is $100 - 24 = 76$ ps.

**Why it works as a link summary.** Each bit is shaped by the channel's pulse response: $y(t) = \sum_k a_k\,h(t - kT) + n(t)$. The eye overlays the sum for every pattern $\{a_k\}$, so it shows the worst combination. A receiver sampling at the centre sees the signal-to-impairment margin directly.
:::

::: short
"An eye diagram overlays all the bits of a serial data stream onto one unit interval, usually by triggering a scope from the clock with persistence. The open area in the middle is where the receiver can sample safely. Eye height shows voltage margin after ISI and noise, eye width shows timing margin, UI minus jitter, and the crossings and trace thickness show duty cycle, jitter and noise. A big clean opening means a low bit error rate; a closed eye means errors."
:::

::: long
"An eye diagram is a way of looking at a high-speed data stream by folding it. You take the waveform, chop it into pieces one bit long, the unit interval, and draw all the pieces on top of each other. On a scope you do that by triggering from the clock, or a recovered clock if there isn't one, with the display set to persistence so millions of bits accumulate. A random pattern like a PRBS makes sure every possible sequence of bits shows up.

The picture ends up looking like an eye. At the left and right edges the transitions cross over. In the middle, if the signal is healthy, there's an open region with no traces in it. That open region is the margin of the link: the receiver should sample in the middle of it.

Reading it: the vertical opening, the eye height, is how much voltage separates a 1 from a 0 at the sampling instant after losing some to inter-symbol interference and noise. The horizontal opening, the eye width, is how much time you have; it's the unit interval minus the jitter. The crossing point tells you about duty-cycle distortion. The thickness of the traces tells you about noise and jitter.

The reason it's useful is that it combines everything. A lossy channel makes each bit smear into the next, which pulls the high rail down and the low rail up, closing the eye from top and bottom. Reflections add ripple. Jitter blurs the crossings horizontally. Noise and crosstalk thicken the traces vertically. A wide, clean eye means a low bit error rate; a closed eye means errors. For compliance, standards define a mask in the middle that the eye must not touch."
:::

::: terms
- **Unit interval (UI)**: one bit time.
- **Eye diagram**: all unit intervals of a data stream overlaid on one display.
- **Eye height / eye width**: vertical and horizontal opening of the eye.
- **Crossing point**: where rising and falling transitions intersect, ideally at mid-level.
- **ISI (inter-symbol interference)**: each bit's waveform depends on neighbouring bits because of the channel's memory.
- **PRBS**: pseudo-random binary sequence; PRBS7 repeats every 127 bits.
- **Persistence / color grading**: display where frequently-hit pixels are brighter.
- **Recovered clock (CDR)**: a clock derived from the data used to trigger or sample.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "it's an overlay of bits that shows signal quality". An exceptional answer explains **how** it's built (clock or recovered-clock trigger, persistence, PRBS to cover all bit histories), defines **eye height, eye width and crossing** and what each tells you, ties every feature to a **cause** (loss to vertical closure, jitter to crossing blur, noise to thick rails, reflections to ripples), and gives numbers (UI at a bit rate, eye width $= UI - TJ$, eye height budget). It also notes that the scope eye is **not** what the receiver sees after equalization, and that a visible eye on a limited sample is not proof of a low BER.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking the eye is a single bit's waveform.** It's many bits overlaid; each trace is a specific bit history.
- **Using a repeating short pattern.** A pattern like 1010 hides ISI; use PRBS (or the standard's test pattern) so all histories appear.
- **Reading eye width in time without the UI.** The same 60 ps opening is a generous 0.6 UI at 10 Gb/s but only 0.24 UI at 25 Gb/s.
- **Triggering on the data itself.** A data trigger hides low-frequency jitter; trigger from the clock or a recovered clock.
- **Believing a clean-looking eye guarantees BER.** A finite number of UIs displays only down to roughly one error per number of bits captured; $10^{-12}$ needs extrapolation or a BERT.
- **Ignoring receiver equalization.** A closed eye at the receiver pin can still be read by a receiver with CTLE/DFE; specs often define the eye after a reference equalizer.
:::

## Folding time

Imagine writing a long stream of bits on a ribbon, then cutting the ribbon every bit period and stacking the strips. That's an eye diagram. Where bits don't change, the strips overlap into a flat rail at the 1 level or the 0 level. Where a bit changes, the transition passes from one rail to the other, and different strips have transitions at slightly different times and shapes depending on the neighbouring bits.

## What happens as a channel degrades

A real channel has a **pulse response** $h(t)$ that spreads a one-UI pulse over several UIs. The received waveform is $y(t)=\sum_k a_kh(t-kT)$, where $a_k$ is $\pm1$. At the sampling instant the "cursor" $h_0$ contributes the wanted signal, and the other samples $h_k$ ($k\neq0$) add pattern-dependent error, the ISI. Worst case, all neighbouring bits oppose the current one:

$$H_{eye} \approx h_0 - \sum_{k\ne0}|h_k|$$

A channel with $h_0 = 0.79$ and ISI terms summing to 0.21 leaves an eye of 0.58 of the single-bit amplitude (the $BW = 0.25/UI$ single-pole channel worked through in [[q0701]]). The eye closes from the top and bottom first, then also in width, because the edges slow down and cross at varying times.

| Impairment | How it appears in the eye |
|---|---|
| Channel loss / bandwidth limit | rails droop, edges slow, eye closes vertically and the crossings widen |
| Reflections | ripples and bumps on rails and edges, multiple "bands" |
| Random jitter | crossings blur horizontally, tails unbounded |
| Deterministic jitter (ISI, DCD) | crossings split into distinct bands, bounded width |
| Random noise | rails get thick vertically |
| Crosstalk | similar to noise, but pattern-dependent when the aggressor is synchronous |
| Duty-cycle distortion | crossings above or below mid-level |
| Skew in a differential pair | extra crossings, common-mode bumps |

## Making one

1. **Trigger**: use the clock (source-synchronous) or a *clock recovery* circuit/software (CDR) that tracks the data. A raw data trigger would put the first transition at a fixed place and hide jitter.
2. **Pattern**: PRBS7, PRBS15 or PRBS31 (or the standard's pattern). PRBS$n$ has $2^n - 1$ bits and contains every $n$-bit sequence except all-zeros; PRBS7 repeats after 127 bits.
3. **Accumulate**: persistence with density colour grading shows where most traces pass (the eye's "core") versus rare outliers.
4. **Bandwidth**: the scope's bandwidth and the fixture must be higher than the signal's (a rule of thumb is about 1.5 to 2 times the bit rate for NRZ); a slow scope closes the eye itself ([[q0687]]).
5. **Number of UIs**: more UIs reveal rarer events. A million UIs at 5 Gb/s takes 0.2 ms, but shows only events with probability around $10^{-6}$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Scope bandwidth recommendations differ between standards and instrument vendors (some compliance tests specify a fixed bandwidth and filter). Use the test specification for the interface instead of the generic rule of thumb.

## Numbers to remember

| Bit rate | UI |
|---|---|
| 1 Gb/s | 1 ns |
| 2.5 Gb/s | 400 ps |
| 5 Gb/s | 200 ps |
| 10 Gb/s | 100 ps |
| 25 Gb/s | 40 ps |

A single-pole channel with $-3$ dB bandwidth equal to half the bit rate (2.5 GHz at 5 Gb/s) has a 10–90% rise time $0.35/BW = 140$ ps, about 0.7 UI. So a "half the bit rate" bandwidth channel already spends most of the UI in transition.

## Differential eyes and what the scope measures

For differential links the eye is formed from $V_d = V_+ - V_-$, typically with a differential probe or two matched single-ended channels and a math subtraction. The eye amplitude is then the differential swing; the common mode appears separately. A receiver with a differential input sees exactly this eye ([[q0697]]), perhaps after its own CTLE/DFE. The scope eye is a measurement of the signal at the probe point, and the *receiver's* eye after equalization can be bigger.
:::

::: applications
- **Compliance testing** for PCIe, USB, SATA, HDMI, DisplayPort and Ethernet: transmitter eye masks at a specified test point.
- **Board bring-up**: a quick check that a new serial link works, before running a BERT.
- **Channel design**: comparing laminate, via and connector choices by eye height at the receiver.
- **Equalizer tuning**: adjusting TX de-emphasis and RX CTLE/DFE settings while watching the eye open.
- **Optical transceivers**: eye masks at the module's electrical and optical interfaces.
- **Debugging intermittent errors**: persistence eye reveals a rare outlier trace that a single acquisition would miss.
:::

::: assumptions
- **NRZ (two-level) signalling**; PAM4 eyes have three stacked openings, each about one-third the height.
- **Clock aligned to data** via a recovered or transmitted clock; asynchronous or badly triggered displays give meaningless eyes.
- **Linear, time-invariant channel** for the $y(t)=\sum a_kh(t-kT)$ view; drivers and receivers add nonlinearity.
- **Eye measured at a defined reference plane** (probe point or after de-embedding); different points give different eyes.
- **Eye height/width definitions vary** (at a BER contour, at the mean, at a histogram percentage); always check the standard or instrument definition.
:::

::: followups
### Why use a PRBS rather than a repeating 1010 pattern?
1010 has only two transitions' histories, so it hides ISI: every bit has the same neighbours. A PRBS covers every pattern of $n$ consecutive bits, including long runs of the same bit (where the channel settles fully) followed by a transition (which sees maximum ISI). The worst-case traces appear in the eye only if they're in the stimulus.

### What does the sampling point look like in an eye? Why not sample anywhere?
Ideally at the horizontal centre and the vertical middle of the opening, where the distance to the nearest trace in both directions is largest. A clock-and-data-recovery loop places the sampling clock there, often with an offset set by training. Sampling nearer the crossings means the sample time falls in the zone where traces from 0 and 1 are close or overlapping.

### How is an eye diagram different for a PAM4 signal?
There are four levels and three eyes stacked vertically, each about one-third of the NRZ height for the same peak swing, so noise and ISI that NRZ tolerates close PAM4 eyes much sooner; this is why PAM4 links rely on strong equalization and forward error correction.

### The eye on my scope looks great but the link has bit errors. What could explain that?
The scope eye displays a limited number of UIs, so rare events (probability $10^{-9}$ or below) don't appear; the probe loads the line and changes the waveform; or the problem is something the eye doesn't show, such as a clock-recovery jitter-tolerance issue or a protocol/coding error. I'd extend the capture with a bathtub or BERT measurement and check the receiver's own error counters.

### How do you read crossing percentage?
It's where the rising and falling edges intersect, as a percentage of the eye amplitude between the 0 and 1 levels. 50% is ideal. A crossing at 40% or 60% indicates asymmetric rise and fall times or duty-cycle distortion, which narrows the eye on one side.
:::
