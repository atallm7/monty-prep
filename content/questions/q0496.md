::: basics
### In plain words
Should analog, digital and power circuits get separate grounds or share one solid ground plane? Splitting keeps noisy return currents out of the quiet analog copper. But every signal that crosses the split loses its return path right beneath it, which makes a big loop. The usual default is one solid plane, with the board arranged so noisy and quiet currents stay apart.

### Picture it
Imagine a building with a quiet library wing and a noisy workshop wing. A solid wall stops the noise, but anyone carrying things between wings must detour to the single door. Or you can leave the building open and put the workshop and library at opposite ends, so people rarely cross. A solid plane with careful placement is the open building. The analogy is loose: the detour matters because current must always complete a loop.

### Words you need
- **Return current**: the current flowing back to its source, right under the signal at high frequency ([[q0486]]).
- **Split ground**: a plane cut into separate analog and digital areas joined at a narrow bridge.
- **Inductance**: resistance to sudden changes in current. A bigger loop has more, about 1 nH per millimeter of detour.
- **Partitioning by placement**: putting analog, digital and power circuits in separate regions of one plane ([[q0493]]).
- **AGND and DGND pins**: a converter chip's analog and digital ground pins. Normally both go to the same plane.

### The simplest example
A 3.3 V clock edge draws 8 mA in 1 ns. It crosses a split, and its return current must detour 80 mm to the bridge. At about 1 nH per millimeter that adds about 80 nH, so the spike is $L\,di/dt = 80\ \text{nH} \times 8\ \text{mA}/1\ \text{ns} = 0.64$ V. Over an unbroken plane the loop is only a few nH, giving about 16 to 40 mV. The split made it 16 to 40 times worse. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers when separation really helps, what the converter's pins mean, and how to make a single plane work.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can weigh a real design trade-off instead of reciting "separate analog and digital grounds" or "always use one plane". The interviewer wants to hear:

1. What each approach is trying to protect (quiet analog from digital switching and power-stage currents).
2. What each approach risks (a single plane can let noisy return currents pass the analog; a split plane breaks return paths and adds loop area).
3. That the real decision is about **return current paths**, not labels on the schematic.
4. A defensible default and the cases where you deviate.
:::

::: why
Almost every mixed-signal board forces this choice, and old guidance ("keep AGND and DGND separate") conflicts with modern guidance ("use one solid plane"). Candidates who can explain both, and what actually decides, stand out. In practice the wrong choice is costly: a split plane that a clock crosses can fail EMC, while a single plane with a motor driver placed next to a 24-bit ADC can ruin the noise floor.
:::

::: concepts
- **Return current paths**: at high frequency the return follows directly beneath the signal; at DC it spreads by resistance. See [[q0486]].
- **Common-impedance coupling**: noisy return currents flowing through a segment shared with a quiet circuit. See [[q0494]], [[q0495]].
- **Split (moated) ground**: the plane is cut so analog and digital copper is separate, joined at a bridge.
- **Loop area and inductance**: a signal that crosses a gap loses its nearby return and gains loop inductance.
- **Partitioning by placement**: a continuous plane with analog, digital and power zones, so return currents do not mix ([[q0493]]).
- **Mixed-signal IC pins**: AGND and DGND are separate pins to limit *on-chip* coupling, not a request for separate board grounds.
:::

::: answer
"The honest answer is that it's a trade-off between isolating noise and keeping return paths short. If you split analog, digital and power grounds, you stop noisy digital or motor return currents from flowing through the quiet analog copper, which can help in low-frequency, high-resolution systems. But every signal that crosses between the sections needs a return path, and across a split that path has to go around to the bridge, which makes a big loop, adds inductance and crosstalk, and radiates. A single solid plane gives every signal its own low-inductance return right underneath it, and is simpler and more robust, but if you place things carelessly the noisy return currents can mix with the quiet ones.

So my default is one solid ground plane, with the board partitioned by placement: power stage and digital in one area, the analog front end in another, and the ADC at the boundary so digital signals don't have to travel through the analog area. I'd only separate grounds physically when there's a real reason, like very high power currents, a precision low-frequency sensor, or galvanic isolation. Then I'd join them at one deliberate point, under the converter or at the power entry, and route no signals across the gap except over that bridge."
:::

::: technical
**Combined (single plane).**
- *Pros:* every trace has an adjacent return, so loop inductance and emissions are minimised; $Z_{plane}$ is tiny (about 0.5 mΩ per square for 1 oz, tens of pH-nH); no signal crossings to manage; simpler stackup and fewer ways to build mistakes.
- *Cons:* shared copper means any high-current return that flows through the analog region can raise analog reference noise; needs disciplined placement.

**Separated (split planes or separate pours).**
- *Pros:* a physical barrier keeps digital/power return currents out of the analog region if every signal respects it; useful for large current steps (motors, relays), for galvanic isolation, and for very-low-frequency precision (≤ kHz).
- *Cons:* a signal crossing a gap loses its image current: loop area $A$ grows, so inductance $L$ and radiation (∝ $A$) grow, crosstalk increases; if the planes meet only at one point, anything that crosses the split is a risk; difference in potential between sections can appear as common-mode noise; the bridge itself adds impedance; EMC testing often exposes it.

**Rule of thumb that holds in most designs:** one solid ground plane under mixed-signal components, partition by placement and by routing, and only separate grounds where isolation or very large current justifies it. This is consistent with Analog Devices' guidance that data converter AGND and DGND pins are normally both connected to the same low-impedance plane, with the converter placed so digital return currents do not flow under analog circuits.

**Quantitative example.** A clock edge of 3.3 V driving 8 mA with 1 ns edges that crosses a split forces a 80 mm detour. At roughly 1 nH/mm that loop adds about 80 nH: $v = L\,di/dt = 80\text{ nH}\times 8\text{ mA}/1\text{ ns} \approx 0.64$ V of induced disturbance in the return, a significant fraction of the logic swing. With a continuous plane the loop is perhaps a few nH, so about 30 mV.
:::

::: short
"Combining grounds gives every signal a short, low-inductance return right beneath it, which is simple and robust, but noisy return currents can pass through quiet areas if placement is sloppy. Separating them keeps noisy currents out of the analog region, but any signal crossing the split loses its return path, which creates loops, crosstalk and EMI. My default is one solid plane partitioned by placement, with separate grounds only for isolation, very high currents or precision low-frequency work, joined at one deliberate point."
:::

::: long
"Think of it as two goals pulling against each other. One goal is keeping noisy return currents away from quiet circuits. The other is giving every signal a short, tight return path. Combining and separating grounds trade those off differently.

With a single solid plane, every trace has its return current flowing directly underneath it, so loops are tiny, inductance is low, crosstalk and radiated emissions are low. It's also simple, there are no crossings to police. The cost is that all the return currents live in the same copper. If I put a switching regulator or motor driver next to a precision ADC and let them share the plane carelessly, the currents mix and I get noise. But the cure for that is placement and routing, not cutting the plane.

With separate grounds, say an analog plane and a digital plane joined at one point, I get a physical barrier. That can help in low-frequency high-resolution systems, or when there are big currents or isolation requirements. The risk is any signal that crosses the gap: its return can't follow it, so it detours to the bridge, and a loop that was a few nanohenries becomes tens. You get ringing, crosstalk, radiation, and a board that may fail EMC. It also leaves the two sections at slightly different potentials.

So my default is one continuous plane under everything, with partitioning by placement: power and digital in one region, analog in another, the converter at the boundary, and the analog sections never routed over by digital traces. When I do need separation, because of isolation, motors, or a very low-level sensor, I separate with a purpose: join at one place, route nothing across the gap except over the bridge, and keep return currents local. And the AGND and DGND pins on a converter datasheet just mean those pins connect to different places on the chip, so on the board I tie them to the same plane."
:::

::: terms
- **Split plane / moat**: a ground layer divided into separate regions by a gap.
- **Bridge**: the deliberate connection between split regions, usually a narrow copper strip.
- **Return current**: current flowing back to the source, which follows the lowest-impedance path.
- **Image current**: the return current induced in a plane directly under a signal trace.
- **Loop area**: the area enclosed by signal and return; radiation and inductance scale with it.
- **AGND / DGND**: separate ground pins on a mixed-signal IC, which isolate on-chip analog from digital switching.
- **Galvanic isolation**: no conductive path between two sections, using transformers, optocouplers or capacitive isolators.
- **Partitioning**: arranging circuit blocks so return currents do not overlap.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "separate grounds reduce noise, but join them at one point". An exceptional one:

- Frames both options around **return current paths**, and says the label (analog/digital/power) matters less than where current flows.
- Shows the cost of a split with numbers: a detour of 80 mm adding about 80 nH and hundreds of mV at a 1 ns edge.
- Explains that **AGND/DGND pins** are about the chip, and that most converter vendors advise tying them to one plane.
- Gives the default (solid plane, partitioned by placement) *and* the exceptions (isolation, heavy motor currents, precision low-frequency).
- Mentions what to do if you split: single bridge, nothing crosses except over it, filtered or isolated crossings.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"AGND and DGND must be separate nets all the way."** The pins are separate on the chip, but on the board the datasheet usually wants them on the same low-impedance plane.
- **Splitting the plane and then routing a clock across the split.** The return detours and the loop radiates. See [[q0497]].
- **Believing a continuous plane means nothing can interfere.** Noisy and quiet circuits placed next to each other still share return paths.
- **Joining the split grounds at several places.** That gives loops between the pieces and defeats the purpose.
- **Forgetting that isolation is different.** A truly isolated ground (opto/transformer) has no shared conductor, which is a deliberate separation with its own rules.
:::

::: deep
## What each option is trying to do

The ground "problem" is always the same: a quiet circuit and a noisy one both need a return path, and wherever those paths overlap the noisy current appears as a voltage in the quiet circuit's reference. There are two ways to attack it:

1. **Separate the copper** so there is nothing shared (split grounds).
2. **Separate the currents** by placement so there is little shared current even in shared copper (single plane, partitioned).

Both can work. Which works *better* depends on frequency, on what crosses the boundary, and on how large the noisy currents are.

## Return currents at high frequency

At DC, return current spreads out across the plane according to resistance. As frequency rises the return concentrates beneath the signal trace because that path has the least loop inductance. Above a few hundred kHz the return is already "following" the signal. This is the key fact: **high-frequency return currents don't care about your ground labels, they care about geometry**.

## Loop area, inductance and what it costs

For a roughly rectangular loop the inductance scales with the perimeter and the log of the aspect ratio, but a good first estimate when there is no nearby plane is about 1 nH per millimetre of loop path. A detour of 80 mm adds about 80 nH.

An edge of 8 mA in 1 ns has $di/dt = 8\times 10^{6}$ A/s, so the voltage across the extra loop is

$$v = L\frac{di}{dt} = 80\text{ nH}\times 8\ \text{mA/ns} = 0.64\text{ V}$$

That voltage appears as noise on the return, in series with the signal, and the same loop acts as a magnetic antenna. With an unbroken plane the loop is a tight strip, perhaps 2–5 nH, so $v$ is only 16–40 mV. The split increased the disturbance by an order of magnitude.

Radiation grows with loop area and the square of frequency, so a split plane crossed by a 100 MHz clock is a far worse EMC risk than a 1 kHz sensor line crossing it.

## When separation really does help

| Situation | Why separation (or isolation) helps |
|---|---|
| Motor, solenoid or relay drivers drawing amps | Large, spiky return currents; keep a heavy power-ground path and join logic ground at one point near the supply |
| Precision low-frequency (≥ 20-bit, microvolt-level) front ends | Reference shifts of microvolts matter; star-like returns and careful partitioning |
| Galvanic isolation (medical, mains-referenced sensing, industrial buses) | No conductive link at all, a safety requirement |
| Very different ground references (e.g. measuring on a high-side shunt) | The "ground" is genuinely different; use isolation or a differential measurement |

For fast digital with moderate analog (say a 12-bit ADC at a few MSPS beside an MCU), a single plane almost always wins.

## What the AGND/DGND pins mean

Many converter datasheets label separate AGND and DGND pins. They exist because inside the package the analog and digital circuits have separate bond wires so digital switching current does not flow through the analog bond-wire inductance. Analog Devices' tutorial on grounding data converters (MT-031) explains that for most boards the right move is to connect both pins to the **same** low-impedance analog ground plane under the converter, and to make sure digital return currents flow in the plane away from analog circuitry. The device itself becomes the "star point".

## Making a single plane work

1. **Partition by placement.** Group analog on one side, digital on the other, power stages near the connector.
2. **Put the converter at the boundary.** Analog pins face the analog area, digital pins face the digital area, so digital traces never travel through analog space.
3. **Keep the plane unbroken** under all signals, especially clocks and high-speed buses ([[q0486]]).
4. **Route high-current loops compactly** and keep their return directly under them.
5. **Decouple locally** so supply-current spikes close their loops near the part.
6. **Stitch layers** with vias near layer changes ([[q0487]]).

## When you must split

If a split is required, follow the safe pattern:

- A **single** narrow bridge, located under the mixed-signal part.
- Signals between the regions cross **only over the bridge**.
- Power feeds with a ferrite bead or a small inductor and capacitor filter between the regions.
- No traces on adjacent layers crossing the gap away from the bridge.

Even then, test it. If EMC or noise results are bad, the first fix is to try merging the planes.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Opinions differ among experienced designers. Some recommend never splitting; others use a split on low-frequency precision boards. The 1 nH/mm figure is a rough estimate for a trace without a nearby plane; real values depend on geometry.
:::

::: applications
- **Data acquisition boards**: one solid plane, ADC placed at the boundary between analog and digital regions, as ADI's converter guidance recommends.
- **Motor controllers**: heavy power ground for the half bridges and shunt, tied to logic ground near the current-sense amplifier.
- **Isolated industrial I/O**: a deliberate galvanic split, with an isolator bridging the gap and the plane cleared underneath it.
- **Audio codecs**: analog output stage kept away from digital switching, usually on one plane with careful placement.
- **Medical sensors (ECG front ends)**: isolation barrier for patient safety, separate floating grounds on each side.
:::

::: assumptions
- Typical mixed-signal board with a 2–4+ layer stack and modest-to-fast digital edges.
- Return current follows the path of least impedance (inductance at high frequency).
- Numbers use ≈1 nH/mm for loop path inductance without a nearby plane, and ≈2–5 nH for a tight strip loop over a plane. They are order-of-magnitude.
- The answer changes for galvanic isolation (separation is mandatory), for very large currents, and for sub-kHz microvolt measurements, where star-like separation can be worth it.
- Another valid view is that "it depends on the board": some teams prefer split grounds for historical or precision reasons, and well-designed splits do work.
:::

::: followups
### Why do converter datasheets have separate AGND and DGND pins if I'm supposed to connect them together?
The pins separate the analog and digital return currents *inside the package*, so digital switching doesn't flow through the same bond wire as the analog section. On the board you connect both to the same low-impedance plane, close to the part, so there's no potential difference between them. The chip is effectively the star point.

### What goes wrong if a clock crosses a split in the ground plane?
Its return can't follow it, so it detours to the nearest bridge. The loop area and inductance grow, you see ringing and crosstalk, and the loop radiates. An 80 mm detour is roughly 80 nH. Fix by routing over the bridge, stitching across the split with a capacitor, or removing the split.

### When would you separate power ground from logic ground?
When the power stage draws large pulsed currents, like a motor or a switching converter, through a shunt. I'd give the power return its own heavy path back to the supply, and tie logic ground at one point near the shunt or driver, so amps don't flow through logic copper.

### What if the interviewer says "my analog performance got worse after I merged the planes"?
I'd check placement and return paths first, not the plane. Where does the digital return current flow? Is a switching supply or clock return running under the analog front end? I'd move the noisy block, give the analog its own quiet region of the same plane, and verify with a spectrum or a ground-to-ground probe measurement.

### Scenario: you have an isolated RS-485 transceiver. How do you treat the grounds?
Two separate grounds, one per side of the isolator, with no copper under the isolation gap on any layer. The bus side's ground ties to the cable shield through an RC if needed; the logic side uses the main plane. Respect creepage and clearance for the working voltage.
:::
