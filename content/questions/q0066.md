::: basics
### In plain words
Doping improves silicon in four ways. It raises conductivity, since it adds many free carriers. It makes the behavior steady, since the carrier count is fixed by the dopants and not by temperature. It lets you choose electrons or holes, which is needed for diodes and transistors. And it lets you tune each region of a device for its own job.

### Picture it
Think of a river and a dam with adjustable gates. Pure silicon is like a trickle, whose flow changes a lot with weather. Doping is like building controlled channels: more flow, a steady flow, and different channels for different jobs. You can open one wide for low resistance and keep another narrow for high voltage. The analogy fails on one point: too much doping also makes things worse.

### Words you need
- **Conductivity**: how easily a material carries current. It rises with the number of carriers.
- **Intrinsic carriers**: free carriers in pure silicon, about $10^{10}$ per cm³ at room temperature.
- **Doping level**: dopant atoms per cm³, which sets the carrier count. See [[q0065]].
- **Junction**: where N-type meets P-type. See [[q0062]].
- **Breakdown voltage**: the reverse voltage at which a junction starts to conduct heavily.

### The simplest example
Pure silicon has about $10^{10}$ carriers per cm³. Dope it with $10^{16}$ phosphorus atoms per cm³. That is a million times more carriers. The resistivity (the resistance of a 1 cm cube, in ohm-centimeters) falls from hundreds of thousands of ohm-centimeters to about half an ohm-centimeter. The count now depends on the dopants, so it barely moves with temperature. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the other benefits, such as tuning a device, and why heavy doping can hurt mobility and breakdown voltage.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can say **what "improve" means** in concrete engineering terms and not just "makes it conduct better". There are several distinct benefits, and a strong candidate separates them:

1. Conductivity: orders of magnitude more carriers, so low resistance.
2. **Controllability and stability**: carrier density set by design and nearly independent of temperature, instead of by thermal generation.
3. **Carrier type**: N and P regions are what make junctions, and therefore diodes, BJTs and MOSFETs, possible at all.
4. Tunability of device parameters: threshold voltage, breakdown voltage, contact resistance, gain.
5. The trade-offs: too much doping hurts mobility, breakdown and leakage.
:::

::: why
Without doping, silicon is not a useful electronic material: its resistivity is about $3\times10^{5}\ \Omega\cdot$cm, it changes by a factor of two every 10 °C and it cannot make a junction. Doping is the knob engineers turn to pick resistance, threshold voltage, gain, breakdown voltage and contact quality. In interviews the follow-up is usually "so why not dope as heavily as possible?", where the trade-offs matter: that is the difference between memorising "doping increases conductivity" and understanding device design.
:::

::: concepts
- **Conductivity**: $\sigma=q(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$. Doping raises $n$ or $p$ by many orders of magnitude. See [[q0065]].
- **Intrinsic vs extrinsic**: in intrinsic material temperature sets the carriers; in extrinsic material dopants do. See [[q0062]].
- **Mobility**: carriers scatter off ionized dopants, so mobility drops as doping rises. See [[q0068]].
- **PN junction**: doped regions of opposite type create the barrier behind diodes and transistors. See [[q0063]].
- **Depletion width and breakdown**: lighter doping means wider depletion and higher breakdown voltage; heavier means narrower and lower breakdown.
:::

::: answer
"Doping improves silicon in a few separate ways. The first and most obvious is conductivity. Pure silicon has about $10^{10}$ carriers per cm³, and doping with $10^{16}$ per cm³ boosts that by a factor of a million, so resistivity drops from hundreds of kilohm-centimetres to around half an ohm-centimetre.

The second is control. Intrinsic carrier density doubles about every 10 degrees, so undoped silicon would drift wildly with temperature. With doping, the carrier density is fixed by the dopant count, and it stays put from below room temperature to a few hundred degrees C.

The third is that I get to choose the carrier type. Without N and P regions I can't make a junction, so no diode, no BJT, no MOSFET.

And fourth, I can tune a device. Light doping in a drift region gives high breakdown voltage; heavy doping in the source, drain, emitter and contact regions gives low resistance; channel doping sets the MOSFET threshold.

The caveat is that more isn't always better. Heavy doping lowers mobility, narrows the depletion region so breakdown voltage and leakage get worse, and past solid solubility extra dopant doesn't even add carriers."
:::

::: technical
**1. Conductivity.** $\sigma=q\mu_nn$ for N-type with $n\approx N_D$. For $N_D=10^{16}$ cm⁻³, $\mu_n\approx1140$ cm²/V·s: $\sigma\approx1.8$ S/cm, $\rho\approx0.55\ \Omega\cdot$cm, against $3.4\times10^{5}\ \Omega\cdot$cm intrinsic (about $6\times10^{5}$ times lower).

**2. Stability.** In the extrinsic range $n\approx N_D$ is independent of temperature, because dopants are fully ionized above about 100 K and thermally generated pairs are negligible while $n_i\ll N_D$. For $N_D=10^{16}$, $n_i$ reaches 10 % of the doping near 600 K (about 330 °C). A lightly doped $10^{14}$ region goes intrinsic much earlier, near 450-500 K. Resistance then has a small positive temperature coefficient because mobility falls with $T$, which is benign and predictable.

**3. Junctions and carrier type.** Adjacent N and P regions create the built-in potential $V_{bi}=\frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}$. Without them there is no rectification and no gain. The doping on each side sets $V_{bi}$ and the depletion width.

**4. Device-parameter tuning.**
- Breakdown: lighter doping on the drift side gives a wider depletion layer and lower peak field, so higher $V_{BR}$.
- MOSFET threshold: $V_T$ increases with body doping through the depletion charge term $\sqrt{2q\varepsilon N_A\,2\phi_F}/C_{ox}$.
- BJT gain: heavily doped emitter and lighter base raise emitter injection efficiency, so higher $\beta$.
- Contact resistance: doping above $10^{19}$ cm⁻³ thins the metal-semiconductor barrier so carriers tunnel, giving an ohmic contact.

**5. Costs.** Mobility falls (ionized-impurity scattering), junction capacitance rises, breakdown drops, leakage by band-to-band tunnelling rises, and above solid solubility (about $10^{20}$-$10^{21}$ cm⁻³) extra atoms are inactive.
:::

::: long
"I'd split the answer into four benefits and one caveat.

First, conductivity. Intrinsic silicon has around $10^{10}$ carriers per cm³ so its resistivity is hundreds of kilohm-centimetres. Dope with $10^{16}$ per cm³ and the electron density goes up a million times, so resistivity falls to roughly half an ohm-centimetre. Going to $10^{19}$ or $10^{20}$, as in a source or drain, gets you into the milliohm-centimetre range.

Second, predictability. Intrinsic carriers come from thermal generation, which roughly doubles every 10 °C, so an undoped resistor would be almost useless. In doped silicon the dopants supply the carriers, so the carrier density is fixed by the process and is flat over a wide temperature range. The mobility does drop with temperature, so resistance rises slowly, but that's gentle and well-behaved.

Third, I get to choose the carrier type, which is what lets me build junctions. A diode is a P region touching an N region. A BJT is N-P-N or P-N-P. A MOSFET is N+ source and drain in a P body.

Fourth, I can tune each region to its job. A power diode has a lightly doped drift layer so the depletion region is wide and the breakdown voltage is high. An emitter or a contact region is very heavily doped so it has low resistance and an ohmic contact. A MOSFET channel is lightly doped so the threshold voltage is set.

The caveat is that more isn't better everywhere. Heavy doping lowers mobility because carriers scatter off the ions, narrows the depletion region so breakdown voltage falls and tunnelling leakage rises, and beyond roughly $10^{20}$ cm⁻³ the extra atoms stop being electrically active. So doping is a trade-off chosen per region."
:::

::: terms
- **Extrinsic semiconductor**: carrier population set by dopants, not by thermal generation.
- **Intrinsic carrier density ($n_i$)**: thermal electron-hole density; rises exponentially with temperature.
- **Ionized-impurity scattering**: collisions of carriers with charged dopant ions; lowers mobility as doping rises.
- **Ohmic contact**: a non-rectifying metal-semiconductor contact, achieved by heavy doping that lets carriers tunnel.
- **Threshold voltage ($V_T$)**: gate voltage that turns on a MOSFET channel; depends on body doping.
- **Drift region**: lightly doped region that supports voltage in a power device.
- **Injection efficiency**: fraction of emitter current carried by the desired carrier in a BJT; improved by heavy emitter doping.
- **Solid solubility**: maximum dopant concentration a crystal can hold in substitutional sites.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it increases conductivity" and perhaps "it makes N and P material."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Separates conductivity, temperature stability, junction formation and parameter tuning, each with a reason.
- Quantifies the conductivity gain: $10^{16}$ doping cuts resistivity by about $6\times10^{5}$.
- Explains that doping makes resistance a design choice rather than a function of temperature, and gives the range where it stays extrinsic.
- Describes how different regions of one device are doped differently (N+ source, P body, N- drift) and why.
- Volunteers the costs: mobility reduction, lower breakdown, higher junction capacitance and leakage, solubility limit.
- Notes that "improve" depends on the metric: for a high-voltage diode, less doping improves breakdown.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"More doping is always better."** Mobility, breakdown voltage, junction capacitance and leakage all get worse as doping rises. It is a trade-off per region.
- **"Doping improves conductivity by increasing mobility."** It increases the *number* of carriers. Mobility actually decreases with doping.
- **"Doping makes the material charged."** Net charge stays zero: mobile carriers are balanced by fixed ions.
- **"Undoped silicon would be a better resistor because it is purer."** Its resistance is huge and drifts exponentially with temperature.
- **Forgetting the junction.** Many people only mention conductivity and miss that carrier type and the PN junction are the actual enabling benefit.
- **Claiming doped silicon is insensitive to temperature.** Its carrier density is, but mobility and so resistance change moderately, and above roughly 150-175 °C leakage and intrinsic carriers become significant for lightly doped regions.
:::

::: deep
## Benefit 1: orders of magnitude more conductivity

The conductivity of a semiconductor is
$$\sigma=q\,(n\mu_n+p\mu_p)$$
Intrinsic: $n=p=n_i=10^{10}$ cm⁻³, $\sigma\approx2.9\times10^{-6}$ S/cm, or $\rho=3.4\times10^{5}\ \Omega\cdot$cm. For N-type at $10^{16}$ cm⁻³: $\sigma\approx1.8$ S/cm. The ratio is $6\times10^{5}$, which comes almost entirely from carrier number (mobility actually decreased a little).

Worked example: a 100 µm × 1 µm × 1 µm resistor.
- Intrinsic silicon: $R=\rho L/A$ with $L=10^{-2}$ cm and $A=10^{-8}$ cm² gives $R=3.4\times10^{5}\times10^{6}=3.4\times10^{11}\ \Omega$ (340 GΩ).
- $10^{16}$ N-type: $0.55\times10^{6}=550\ \text{k}\Omega$.
- $10^{19}$ N+ ($\rho\approx0.0056\ \Omega\cdot$cm): $5.6\ \text{k}\Omega$.

That range, from 340 GΩ to 5.6 kΩ in the same geometry, is the dial a designer gets from doping alone.

## Benefit 2: control and temperature stability

In intrinsic material $n_i\propto T^{3/2}e^{-E_g/2kT}$, roughly doubling every 10 K near room temperature. In a doped sample the total electron density is
$$n=\frac{N_D}{2}+\sqrt{\frac{N_D^2}{4}+n_i^2}$$
which equals $N_D$ as long as $n_i\ll N_D$.

## Benefit 3: choosing carrier type to make junctions

The bigger reason isn't resistance at all. Two regions of opposite type next to each other create a built-in potential
$$V_{bi}=\frac{kT}{q}\ln\frac{N_AN_D}{n_i^2}$$
and a depletion region ([[q0063]]). Everything with gain or rectification rests on junctions: forward bias lowers the barrier and diffusion current rises as $e^{V/V_T}$ ([[q0072]]). With no doping, no junction, no diode and no transistor.

## Benefit 4: tuning device parameters

A single transistor has regions with very different doping because each has a different job.

| Region | Typical doping | Why |
|---|---|---|
| MOSFET source/drain (N+) | $10^{19}$-$10^{20}$ | Low resistance, ohmic contact |
| MOSFET body/channel (P) | $10^{16}$-$10^{18}$ | Sets $V_T$, controls short-channel effects |
| Power MOSFET drift region (N-) | $10^{14}$-$10^{16}$ | Wide depletion for high blocking voltage |
| BJT emitter (N+) | $10^{19}$-$10^{20}$ | High injection efficiency, high $\beta$ |
| BJT base (P) | $10^{17}$-$10^{18}$ | Thin and moderately doped to limit base resistance and recombination |
| BJT collector (N-) | $10^{15}$-$10^{16}$ | Sustains $V_{CE}$ without breakdown |

Two worked examples:

**Breakdown voltage.** For a one-sided junction the maximum field is $E_{max}=qN_Dx_n/\varepsilon$ with $x_n=\sqrt{2\varepsilon (V_{bi}+V_R)/(qN_D)}$. At breakdown $E_{max}\approx3\times10^5$ V/cm. Solving $V_{BR}\approx\varepsilon E_{c}^2/(2qN_D)$ with a constant $E_c=3\times10^5$ V/cm gives about 290 V for $N_D=10^{15}$, 29 V for $10^{16}$ and 2.9 V for $10^{17}$. Published abrupt-junction curves are somewhat higher at high doping (roughly 300 V, 50 V and 10 V) because the critical field rises with doping, but the trend is the same: ten times lighter doping buys several times more voltage. Lighter doping means higher breakdown, so power devices have wide, lightly doped drift layers at the price of higher on-resistance.

**Threshold voltage.** Raising body doping $N_A$ widens the charge that must be depleted before inversion, which raises $V_T$. Threshold-adjust implants into the channel are a standard way to set $V_T$ to a target.

## Benefit 5: ohmic contacts

A metal on lightly doped silicon forms a Schottky barrier (rectifying). Heavy doping thins the barrier to a few nanometres so electrons tunnel through, producing a low-resistance non-rectifying contact. That's why source, drain, emitter and substrate-tap regions are N+ or P+.

## The costs of doping

- **Mobility falls.** Ionized impurities scatter carriers. At $10^{18}$, $\mu_n$ is roughly a fifth of its lightly doped value. See [[q0068]].
- **Breakdown falls** and **tunnelling leakage rises** as the depletion region narrows.
- **Junction capacitance rises** ($C_j\propto\sqrt{N}$ for a one-sided junction), slowing switching.
- **Bandgap narrowing** at very high doping (tens of meV) reduces emitter efficiency in BJTs.
- **Solubility limit**: extra atoms cluster or precipitate and stop adding carriers.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The breakdown-voltage figures above come from a simple parallel-plane estimate with a constant critical field, and the published figures I quote alongside it are from memory of typical curves; real values depend on doping profile, junction curvature and impact-ionization coefficients, so quote them as ballpark numbers.

## Worked example: where does the flat region end, and what does the resistor do inside it?

The flat plateau in the temperature plot has a definite end, and you can find it by setting the thermal carrier density equal to a fraction of the doping. Using the same $n_i(T)$ model as the plot (anchored at $10^{10}$ cm⁻³ at 300 K, $E_g=1.12$ eV) and solving numerically:

| Net doping | $n_i=0.1N_D$ at | $n_i=N_D$ at |
|---|---|---|
| $10^{14}$ cm⁻³ | 425 K (152 °C) | 492 K (219 °C) |
| $10^{16}$ cm⁻³ | 583 K (310 °C) | 711 K (438 °C) |

So a $10^{16}$ region is safely extrinsic across the whole commercial and automotive range, while a $10^{14}$ drift region is already 10 % intrinsic at about 150 °C, which is part of why leakage and on-resistance trends in lightly doped power devices change character near the top of their rated temperature. At 125 °C (398 K) the $10^{16}$ region has $n_i\approx3\times10^{12}$ cm⁻³, only 0.03 % of the doping, so the carrier count has not moved at all.

What does move is mobility. Lattice scattering in lightly doped silicon makes $\mu\propto T^{-2.4}$ roughly (an approximate textbook exponent that varies with doping and source). From 25 °C to 125 °C:

$$\frac{R(398\ \text{K})}{R(298\ \text{K})}\approx\left(\frac{398.15}{298.15}\right)^{2.4}=2.0$$

A lightly doped silicon resistor therefore roughly doubles over that range, an average temperature coefficient near +1 %/°C or 10,000 ppm/°C. That is stable and monotonic but large, which is why a moderately doped diffused resistor is a poor choice where a precision ratio against temperature matters. Heavily doped (degenerate) regions and polysilicon or thin-film resistors show far smaller coefficients, because mobility there is dominated by impurity scattering, which depends only weakly on temperature.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The $T^{-2.4}$ exponent and the resulting coefficient are approximate and depend on doping level; foundry resistor models give the actual coefficients for each layer. Treat the 2x figure as a lightly-doped ballpark, not a process specification.

## Real-hardware effects: the resistor is also a junction

An integrated N-well or diffused resistor is not an isolated bar of silicon. Its body sits in a reverse-biased PN junction with the substrate, and that junction does three things the bar model ignores.

**Voltage coefficient.** As the reverse bias rises, the depletion layer eats into the resistor's conducting thickness. Take a 2 µm deep N-type layer at $10^{16}$ cm⁻³ capped by a P+ region at $10^{19}$ cm⁻³ (the pinch-resistor geometry). The one-sided depletion depth is $x_d=\sqrt{2\varepsilon(V_{bi}+V_R)/(qN_D)}$ with $V_{bi}\approx0.89$ V, which gives 0.34 µm at 0 V, 0.61 µm at 2 V and 0.87 µm at 5 V. The conducting thickness shrinks from 1.66 µm to 1.13 µm, so the resistance rises by a factor of about 1.5 between 0 V and 5 V of bias. A resistor whose value depends on the voltage across it distorts signals, which is why analog layouts use polysilicon or tie the well to the most positive node the resistor sees.

**Leakage and parasitic capacitance.** The isolation junction leaks, and the leakage roughly doubles every several degrees. Its depletion capacitance (the $C_j\propto\sqrt{N}$ cost from above) loads the resistor's far end and limits bandwidth.

**Self-heating and tolerance.** Absolute resistance varies by tens of percent across process lots because implant dose and anneal vary, but two resistors drawn next to each other match to well under 1 %. That is why analog design uses ratios of identical resistors rather than absolute values.

## How you would verify this on the bench

- **Resistance versus temperature:** put a test chip (or a discrete silicon bar) on a hot plate with a thermocouple on it, force a small current (microamps, to avoid self-heating) with a source-measure unit, and log $V/I$ from 25 to 150 °C. A lightly doped region should rise smoothly, close to the power-law estimate above. A sudden bend toward lower resistance at high temperature means you've entered the intrinsic range or the isolation junction is leaking in parallel.
- **Voltage coefficient:** measure the resistor at a fixed small test current while sweeping the well bias with a second supply. A rising $R$ with reverse bias confirms the depletion-pinch effect.
- **Doping and breakdown:** sweep reverse voltage on a test diode with a curve tracer and a current limit. The knee where current rises sharply is $V_{BR}$; comparing two diodes with different drift doping shows the doping-versus-breakdown trade directly. Always limit current, or the junction is destroyed.

::: applications
- **Power MOSFET / diode drift layers**: lightly doped epitaxial layer sets blocking voltage; heavily doped substrate keeps resistance low.
- **CMOS threshold control**: channel and well implants set $V_T$ for low-power or high-speed variants of the same process.
- **Ohmic contacts on any IC**: N+ or P+ landing regions beneath the metal vias.
- **IC resistors**: implanted or polysilicon resistors with sheet resistances chosen by dose.
- **BJTs in analogue ICs**: heavy emitter, moderate base, light collector to hit target gain and breakdown.
- **Zener diodes**: heavy doping on both sides of a junction makes a thin depletion region that breaks down by tunnelling at a few volts.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Silicon, 300 K, full ionization** of dopants unless stated. At cryogenic temperature dopants freeze out; at high temperature intrinsic carriers dominate.
- **Uniform doping** within a region; real profiles are graded by implantation and diffusion.
- **"Performance" is context-dependent.** For a resistor or contact it means low and stable resistance; for a power diode it means high breakdown voltage; for a switching transistor it means speed. The answer changes with the metric, and the best answer states which one it optimises.
- **Mobility and breakdown estimates** use textbook empirical models. Values from other sources can differ by 10-30%.
- **Alternative reading**: some interviewers use "improve performance" to mean "improve conductivity" only. Give that first, then the broader answer.

::: followups
### Why not just dope as heavily as possible for the lowest resistance?
Three reasons. Mobility falls (at $10^{19}$ the electron mobility is around 110 cm²/V·s versus about 1250 lightly doped, so conductivity grows slower than doping). Breakdown voltage drops and tunnelling leakage rises as the depletion layer gets thinner. And above solid solubility (about $10^{20}$-$10^{21}$ cm⁻³) extra dopant doesn't become active. So you dope each region only as much as its job needs.

### How does doping make resistance stable against temperature?
In the extrinsic range, carrier density equals the doping, which doesn't change with temperature. Only mobility varies (it falls as lattice scattering increases), so resistance rises slowly with temperature, a small positive TC, instead of the exponential fall of intrinsic material. This holds until $n_i$ approaches the doping level, around 600 K (a bit over 300 °C) for $10^{16}$ cm⁻³ silicon.

### Why is the drift region of a power MOSFET lightly doped?
Because it must hold off the blocking voltage. Light doping makes the depletion layer wide and the peak field low, so breakdown voltage is high. The cost is resistance: $R_{drift}\propto V_{BR}^{2.5}$ roughly for silicon, which is why high-voltage MOSFETs have high $R_{DS(on)}$ and why super-junction and wide-bandgap devices exist.

### How does doping help a BJT's current gain?
Gain depends on emitter injection efficiency. If the emitter is much more heavily doped than the base, nearly all of the base-emitter current is carriers injected from emitter to base, not the reverse. A thin, moderately doped base also gives fewer recombinations. Typical emitter doping is $10^{19}$-$10^{20}$ against $10^{17}$ in the base. Too-heavy emitter doping brings bandgap narrowing and Auger recombination, which limit the gain.

### What is an ohmic contact and how does doping create one?
An ohmic contact has a linear, low-resistance I-V without rectification. A metal on silicon forms a Schottky barrier, but if the semiconductor surface is doped above about $10^{19}$ cm⁻³ the barrier is only a few nanometres thick and carriers tunnel through it. Contact resistivity then falls to the order of $10^{-7}$ to $10^{-8}\ \Omega\cdot$cm².

### Scenario: a lightly doped resistor on your chip drifts badly at 125 °C. What's going on and how would you fix it?
Likely the doping is low enough that $n_i$ and leakage start to matter, or its resistance has a large temperature coefficient from mobility and from leakage through the isolating junction. Increase the doping (lower resistivity, more extrinsic), widen the resistor to lower the voltage across any leaky junction, bias the isolation well properly, or switch to polysilicon or thin-film resistors, which have less coupling to the substrate.
:::
