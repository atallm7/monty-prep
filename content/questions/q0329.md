::: testing
Whether you can explain the failure at the **physical level**: what a puncture does inside the cell, and why electronics cannot stop it. The hidden sub-skills:

1. You know the cell's layer structure (collectors, electrodes, separator, electrolyte) and what the separator is for.
2. You understand an **internal short** as a localized, uncontrolled discharge, and why it differs from an external short.
3. You can argue why protection circuits, fuses and chargers are powerless once the damage is inside, and why state of charge changes the severity.
:::

::: why
Puncture is the canonical "abuse" test for lithium cells (nail penetration), and it is the best illustration of why battery safety is about *containing stored energy* rather than just controlling charge and discharge. It matters for product design (enclosure strength, screw placement, thin pouch cells in phones) and for handling rules (do not use sharp tools near packs). Interviewers like it because it checks whether you reason from first principles: what is physically between the electrodes, and what happens when that is breached?
:::

::: concepts
- **Cell stack**: anode, separator, cathode, with metal collector foils and electrolyte. See [[q0302]].
- **Separator**: thin porous polymer that blocks electrons but passes ions; the only barrier between full-charge electrodes.
- **Internal vs external short circuit**: current path inside the cell versus through external wires. See [[q0331]].
- **Joule heating and thermal runaway**: local heating of a small volume. See [[q0332]], [[q0327]].
- **Electrolyte chemistry**: flammable carbonate solvents and a lithium salt that reacts with moisture. See [[q0327]].
:::

::: answer
"A LiPo is basically a thin sandwich: a cathode and an anode separated by a separator that is only some tens of microns thick, wetted with a flammable electrolyte, all in a soft foil pouch. If you puncture it, the sharp object pushes through those layers and connects the electrodes, or the foil layers, directly. That's an internal short circuit: the energy in the cell dumps through a tiny spot in the cell itself.

Because the short is localized and inside the cell, all the heat is released right there. Tens of amps through a spot the size of a pinhead heats it very fast, melts the separator around it, and makes the short bigger. That heat starts the exothermic breakdown reactions, so you get thermal runaway: venting of flammable gas, fire, toxic fumes. The pouch has no rigid can, and air and moisture get in, which makes it worse.

The scary part is that no electronics can help. A protection circuit or fuse sits in the external path. The short is inside the cell, so it can't be interrupted. And the damage may be partial and delayed: a pack that was only dented can ignite hours later. A lower state of charge reduces the severity, but it doesn't make it safe."
:::

::: technical
**Cell structure.** A pouch cell is a stack: aluminium foil (cathode current collector) coated with a cathode oxide (for example LiCoO₂ or NMC), a porous separator about 10 to 25 µm thick, a graphite-coated copper foil (anode), repeated and soaked in electrolyte, sealed in aluminium-polymer laminate. At full charge the electrodes are at a potential difference of about 4.2 V with a large stored charge, and only the separator keeps them apart.

**What puncture does.**

1. A conductive intruder (nail, screw) bridges the electrodes through the separator, or bridges the collector foils directly.
2. The cell discharges through this *internal short* with resistance $R_{sc}$. With $R_{int} \approx 50\ \text{m}\Omega$ and $R_{sc} = 100\ \text{m}\Omega$: $I = 4.2/(0.05 + 0.1) = 28$ A and the heat in the short is $I^2R_{sc} \approx 78$ W, concentrated in a spot around a millimetre across.
3. Local heating melts or shrinks the separator around the spot, enlarging the short; heat triggers decomposition reactions that release more heat ([[q0332]]).
4. The pouch is breached, so air and moisture enter. The salt LiPF₆ reacts with moisture to form hydrofluoric acid and other irritants, and flammable gas can ignite.

**Why electronics cannot stop it.** Protection circuits and fuses sit in the external current path. They can disconnect the pack terminals, but the internal short current never passes through them. The only safeguards are the cell's own design: separator quality, shutdown behaviour, safe venting, and mechanical protection.

**Severity depends on:** state of charge (higher SoC means higher voltage, more lithiated anode and more energetic cathode), the location and conductivity of the short (the worst is aluminium-to-copper foil contact because of its very low resistance), puncture speed, cell energy density and size, ambient temperature.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The relative severity of different internal-short types is documented in research literature on nail-penetration modelling; exact outcomes vary with cell design, so treat the ranking as qualitative.
:::

::: long
"To understand it, picture what's inside a LiPo: very thin layers of cathode and anode on metal foils, separated by a porous separator around 10 to 25 microns thick, all soaked in a flammable electrolyte, in a soft aluminium foil pouch. The separator is the only thing keeping a fully charged anode and cathode, about 4.2 volts apart, from touching.

If you puncture the pouch with a nail, the metal pushes through the layers and bridges the electrodes or the foils. That creates an internal short circuit. The cell now discharges through a tiny spot inside itself. With cell resistance of tens of milliohms, you can easily get tens of amps through a spot a millimetre wide, and that is tens of watts in a few milligrams of material, so it heats up extremely fast.

That heat melts and shrinks the separator around the spot, which enlarges the short, and it pushes the cell into thermal runaway: the electrolyte vaporizes and ignites, the cathode may release oxygen, and the cell vents flames and toxic gas. Because the pouch is now open, air and moisture get in, and the lithium salt can form hydrofluoric acid.

What makes this uniquely nasty is that none of the usual protection helps. The BMS, the fuse, the charger are all in the external path. The short current never flows through them, so they can't interrupt it. Also, the failure can be delayed: a partial puncture or crush may create a high-resistance short that heats slowly, so the cell can ignite hours later. Lower state of charge helps a lot, but it's no guarantee, so punctured or crushed cells should be isolated and recycled."
:::

::: terms
- **Internal short circuit**: unintended conductive path between anode and cathode inside the cell.
- **Separator**: porous polymer film that electrically isolates the electrodes while allowing ion flow.
- **Nail penetration test**: standard abuse test where a nail is driven through a charged cell.
- **Current collector**: metal foil (Al for the cathode, Cu for the anode) that carries current from the electrodes.
- **Joule heating**: heat $I^2R$ generated in a resistance.
- **Venting**: release of internal gas and vapour through a rupture or designed weak point.
- **Hot spot**: small region of intense local heating, the origin of runaway.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it can short out and catch fire".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Describes the **layer structure** and identifies the separator as the single barrier.
- Distinguishes **internal from external short circuits** and explains why protection electronics cannot interrupt an internal one.
- Gives a **quantitative feel** (28 A through a 100 mΩ spot gives about 78 W in a millimetre-scale volume).
- Mentions the **worst-case path** (foil-to-foil contact), the role of **state of charge**, and **delayed failure**.
- Adds pouch-specific points: no rigid can, **air/moisture ingress** and HF formation.
- Links to design practice: protect from sharp objects and compression, keep screws away from the pack, derate thin cells.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The fuse or BMS will blow."** They cannot; the internal short bypasses the external circuit.
- **"It only matters if it's fully charged."** SoC changes severity but a partially charged, damaged cell can still short and heat.
- **"If nothing happens immediately it's fine."** Partial damage can cause delayed thermal runaway.
- **"The danger is the lithium reacting with air."** The cell contains lithium ions in electrodes, not bare metal; the main hazard is internal short heating plus flammable electrolyte.
- **"Pouch cells are safer because they can swell and vent."** Venting helps, but the soft pouch offers far less mechanical protection than a metal can.
- **"A tiny dent is cosmetic."** Pressure can damage the separator without breaking the pouch.
:::

## Internal vs external short: the key difference

In an external short ([[q0331]]) the current flows through the cell, the wires and whatever connects the terminals. Heat is shared between the cell and the wiring, and a fuse or protector in the path can open. In an internal short, the entire energy release happens inside the cell where it cannot be interrupted, and the heat is concentrated in the small volume around the short path.

Numerically, with $R_{int} = 50\ \text{m}\Omega$ and an internal short resistance $R_{sc}$:

| $R_{sc}$ | Current | Power in the short | Power in the rest of the cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Ω | 4.0 A | 16 W | 0.8 W |
| 100 mΩ | 28 A | 78 W | 39 W |
| 20 mΩ | 60 A | 72 W | 180 W |
| 5 mΩ | 76 A | 29 W | 290 W |

## Why the hot spot is so brutal

Consider the short spot as a volume of about $1\ \text{mm} \times 1\ \text{mm} \times 0.1\ \text{mm} = 10^{-10}\ \text{m}^3$ of electrode material with density about 3000 kg/m³ and specific heat about 900 J/(kg·K). The mass is $3\times10^{-7}$ kg. If 78 W is dissipated there and none escapes:

$$\frac{dT}{dt} = \frac{P}{m c} = \frac{78}{3\times10^{-7} \times 900} \approx 2.9\times10^{5}\ \text{K/s}$$

Heat conduction limits this enormously, but even a tiny fraction of that rate takes the neighbouring separator past its melting range (roughly 130 to 165 °C for polyolefin separators) in milliseconds to seconds. This is order-of-magnitude reasoning, not a prediction, but it shows why a pinhole short is a thermal event and not just a current event.

## What happens next

1. **Separator melts** around the spot: short grows, current grows.
2. **Electrolyte heats**: solvents vaporize (flammable gas), pressure builds, the pouch bulges.
3. **Electrode reactions**: anode-electrolyte exothermic reactions and, at higher temperature, cathode decomposition releasing oxygen.
4. **Vent/fire**: pouch ruptures, gas ignites, flames and hot particles are ejected.
5. **Neighbours**: in a multi-cell pack, adjacent cells heat and may propagate.

## State of charge and speed

- **High SoC** (4.2 V): highest voltage and most reactive materials, worst outcome.
- **Low SoC** (about 3.0 V): less energy and lower voltage; heating is less and runaway may not occur, although the cell can still short and vent.
- **Puncture speed and size**: a fast, large nail causes immediate shorting; a slow or small intrusion can cause a high-resistance, slowly heating short that evolves for minutes to hours.
- **Cell format**: large-capacity cells release more energy; a rigid can resists puncture better, but a punctured can fails similarly.

## Practical consequences

- **Enclosure design**: keep screw bosses, PCB edges and sharp components away from the cell; add a rigid backplate or isolation film; avoid compression of pouch cells (and leave room for swelling).
- **Handling**: never use sharp tools near a pack, never disassemble, do not stack heavy items on packs.
- **After damage**: a dented or punctured pack is retired and handed to a recycler ([[q0328]]); do not "test" it by charging.
- **Qualification**: nail penetration and crush tests in standards (for example UL 1642) check cell behaviour. Some cell designs pass at lower SoC or pass because of additional separator features.
:::

::: applications
- **Cell qualification**: nail-penetration and crush tests in standards such as UL 1642 and IEC 62133 to compare cell designs.
- **Smartphone and laptop design**: stiffening plates and spacing around pouch cells; avoiding screws that can reach the cell stack.
- **Drone/RC**: crash damage is the commonest cause of punctured packs; crash inspection rules before reuse.
- **EV packs**: underbody armour and crash structure to prevent puncture and crush during collisions.
- **Recycling**: dismantling uses controlled discharge or cryogenic/inert conditions to prevent punctured-cell fires.
:::

::: assumptions
- Written for **pouch Li-ion/LiPo** cells with organic electrolyte; cylindrical and prismatic cans respond similarly but have more mechanical protection.
- Numbers (separator thickness, short resistances, heat rates) are **order-of-magnitude** and vary by cell design.
- The heating model ignores conduction and the temperature dependence of resistance; it is for intuition only.
- Solid-state or non-flammable-electrolyte cells reduce, but do not remove, the hazard.
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between puncturing a cell and shorting its terminals?
An external short sends current through the cell and external conductors, spreading heat and allowing a fuse or protector to interrupt it. A puncture creates an *internal* short that releases energy inside the cell at the damage site, and no external device sees the current. Both are dangerous, but puncture is not interruptible.

### Why do manufacturers test with a nail?
It is a repeatable way to create a hard internal short. The test shows whether the cell fails safely (vent without flame) or goes into runaway, and it is used to compare separators, coatings and cell designs. It is not a perfect replica of field failures (which often involve slower, partial shorts).

### Can a cell be safe to puncture at low state of charge?
It is much less dangerous because there is less energy and a lower voltage, and many cells pass nail tests at low SoC. But it is still not safe: the cell can still heat and vent, electrolyte may leak, and recharging a damaged cell later can trigger a delayed failure. Treat punctured cells as hazardous.

### Why can a dent without a visible hole be a problem?
Compressive stress can crack electrodes or damage the separator without breaking the pouch, creating a latent internal short or a region with high local current density. The cell may work for days and then fail during charging. This is why dropped or crushed packs are retired.

### How would you design a phone to resist battery puncture?
Keep the cell away from screws and sharp edges, add a stiff plate or frame between the cell and the board or enclosure, avoid compressing the cell with the back cover, include a thermal fuse or PTC for pack-level faults, qualify the design with crush/impact tests, and choose cells with robust separators (ceramic-coated for instance).

### Scenario: a drone crashed and the pack looks fine with a small dent. The pilot wants to fly again. What do you say?
No: retire it. A dent can create a latent internal short that heats during the next high-current discharge. Check voltage balance and the cell for swelling or warmth only to decide on safe storage and disposal ([[q0328]]), not to put it back in service. Replace the pack, and consider impact protection on the airframe.
:::

::: interpretations
"Puncture" is read here as any mechanical breach of the cell (nail, screw, tool, crash shard). A related but different case is *crush* or heavy compression, which causes a similar internal short without a hole. The question is in the LiPo (pouch) section, so pouch-specific points (no rigid case, laminate foil) are emphasized. See [[q0327]] for the general hazards and [[q0331]] for the external short circuit.
:::
