::: testing
Whether you can define the Nyquist rate precisely (twice the signal's highest frequency, or twice its bandwidth for a baseband signal), separate it from the **Nyquist frequency** ($f_s/2$), and explain *why* it matters: it is the sampling-theorem condition for lossless reconstruction, it determines aliasing, and it drives the anti-alias filter and the data rate of every ADC system. Hidden sub-skills: spectrum replicas, practical guard bands, oversampling, bandpass sampling, and recognising signals that aren't band-limited.
:::

::: why
Every digital measurement or media system hinges on this one rule. If you sample below it, information is destroyed irreversibly (aliasing, [[q0670]]); above it, no information is lost in principle. In practice it determines ADC speed and power, memory and data bandwidth, anti-alias filter complexity, and why audio is 44.1 or 48 kHz. Interviewers use it to test whether you understand sampling as a spectral operation, not just "twice the frequency".
:::

::: concepts
- **Band-limited signal**: a signal whose spectrum is zero above some frequency $B$ (or $f_{max}$).
- **Nyquist rate**: $2B$ (samples per second), the minimum sampling rate that preserves a baseband signal of bandwidth $B$.
- **Nyquist frequency**: half the actual sample rate, $f_s/2$: the highest frequency a given sampler can represent.
- **Spectral replicas**: sampling creates copies of the spectrum centered at multiples of $f_s$; they must not overlap. See [[q0666]].
- **Anti-alias filter**: removes content above $f_s/2$ before sampling.
- **Oversampling**: using $f_s$ much larger than $2B$ to relax filters and reduce noise.
:::

::: answer
"The Nyquist rate is the minimum sample rate you need to capture a signal without losing information: twice the signal's highest frequency, or twice its bandwidth for a baseband signal. If a signal contains frequencies up to 20 kilohertz, its Nyquist rate is 40 kilosamples per second. That's different from the Nyquist frequency, which is half of whatever sample rate you're actually using, the highest frequency your sampler can represent.

It matters because of what sampling does in the frequency domain: it makes copies of the signal's spectrum centred at multiples of the sample rate. If you sample faster than twice the highest frequency, the copies don't overlap, and you can recover the original signal exactly with a low-pass filter; that's the Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem. If you sample slower, the copies overlap, high frequencies fold down onto low ones, and that's aliasing, which can't be undone after sampling.

In practice it drives design decisions: you put an anti-alias filter in front of the ADC, you don't sample exactly at the Nyquist rate because real filters can't cut off instantly, so audio goes to 44.1 or 48 kilohertz for 20 kilohertz content, and many converters oversample so the filter can be simple. It also sets data rate and power: double the bandwidth and you double the samples you have to move and store."
:::

::: technical
**Sampling theorem.** If $x(t)$ is band-limited to $|f|\le B$, it is uniquely determined by samples $x[n]=x(nT_s)$ with $f_s=1/T_s>2B$, and

$$x(t)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}x[n]\,\mathrm{sinc}\!\left(\frac{t-nT_s}{T_s}\right).$$

**Definitions.** Nyquist *rate* $=2B$ (a property of the signal). Nyquist *frequency* $=f_s/2$ (a property of the sampler). Don't mix them.

**Spectrum picture.** Ideal sampling replicates the spectrum: $X_s(f)=f_s\sum_kX(f-kf_s)$. Replicas centered at $kf_s$ with half-width $B$ don't overlap iff $f_s-B>B$, i.e. $f_s>2B$.

**Practical rate.** The anti-alias filter must pass up to $B$ and reject by $\ge A$ dB from $f_s-B$ onward. The transition band ratio is $(f_s-B)/B$. For a Butterworth filter, the order is roughly $n\approx A/(20\log_{10}[(f_s-B)/B])$. For 20 kHz audio and 80 dB: at $f_s=44.1$ kHz the ratio is 1.205 and $n\approx49$ (impractical; real converters use sharper elliptic filters plus oversampling); at $176.4$ kHz (4× oversampling) the ratio is 7.82 and $n\approx4.5$, so a 5th-order filter suffices.

**Oversampling.** Quantization noise is spread across $0$ to $f_s/2$; filtering to $B$ reduces it by $10\log_{10}(f_s/2B)$ dB: 4× oversampling gives 6 dB (one bit).

**Bandpass sampling.** For a signal in $[f_L,f_H]$ with $B=f_H-f_L$, any $f_s$ with $\frac{2f_H}{n}\le f_s\le\frac{2f_L}{n-1}$ for integer $n\le\lfloor f_H/B\rfloor$ works. Example: a 20 MHz wide IF at 60–80 MHz can be sampled at 56 MS/s ($n=3$ allows 53.3–60 MS/s), aliasing it to 4–24 MHz.

**Not band-limited.** A square wave or pulse has infinite bandwidth; in practice the useful bandwidth is set by the rise time, $\approx0.35/t_r$ ([[q0666]]).

**Nonlinearity widens bandwidth.** Squaring a signal of bandwidth $B$ gives $2B$; multiplying two signals adds bandwidths.
:::

::: long
"The Nyquist rate is the minimum sampling rate that lets you capture a signal without losing information: twice the highest frequency in the signal, or twice its bandwidth if it's a baseband signal. For audio with content up to 20 kilohertz, the Nyquist rate is 40 kilosamples per second. It's worth keeping a second term separate: the Nyquist frequency is half the sample rate you're actually using, so at 48 kilosamples per second it's 24 kilohertz. The first is a property of the signal; the second is a property of the sampler.

Why does it matter? Think in the frequency domain. Sampling in time makes copies of the spectrum centred on every multiple of the sample rate. If the sample rate is more than twice the signal bandwidth, the copies sit side by side without touching, and an ideal low-pass filter picks out the original, which reconstructs the signal exactly. That's the sampling theorem. If the sample rate is lower, the copies overlap, so high-frequency content lands on top of lower frequencies, and once they're mixed you can't separate them. That's aliasing.

The consequences show up in real design. You always need an analog anti-alias filter before the ADC, to remove anything above half the sample rate, including noise. Since the filter has a transition band, you sample somewhat above the Nyquist rate: CD audio uses 44.1 kilohertz for 20 kilohertz content, which leaves only a couple of kilohertz of transition band, so the filter has to be very sharp. Many converters therefore oversample, say 4 times, so a simple analog filter works and the sharp filtering is done digitally, which also improves SNR by 3 dB per doubling of oversampling.

It also sets cost: the sample rate sets ADC speed and power, and the number of samples to store or send. And it breaks down for signals that aren't band-limited, like square waves, where you use the edge rate to decide the effective bandwidth, or in bandpass sampling where you deliberately alias a narrow high-frequency band down."
:::

::: terms
- **Nyquist rate**: $2B$; the minimum sample rate to preserve a band-limited signal.
- **Nyquist frequency**: $f_s/2$; the highest representable frequency.
- **Nyquist-Shannon theorem**: band-limited signals are perfectly reconstructable from samples above $2B$.
- **Aliasing**: spectral overlap when $f_s<2B$.
- **Anti-alias filter**: low-pass filter before the sampler.
- **Guard band / transition band**: the frequency gap between the signal band and the alias band that the filter uses to roll off.
- **Oversampling ratio (OSR)**: $f_s/(2B)$.
- **Bandpass (under)sampling**: sampling a band at a high frequency intentionally below $2f_H$.
- **Reconstruction filter**: low-pass filter after the DAC (or ideal sinc interpolation) that removes images.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "twice the highest frequency" and "to avoid aliasing." An exceptional answer:
- Separates Nyquist rate (signal property) from Nyquist frequency (sampler property).
- Explains it via spectrum replicas at multiples of $f_s$ and non-overlap condition.
- Gives real numbers: 20 kHz audio, 44.1 kS/s, 2.05 kHz transition band, and the resulting filter order versus oversampling.
- Mentions oversampling's 3 dB/octave noise benefit and bandpass sampling.
- Notes non-band-limited signals (square waves) and nonlinear widening (squaring doubles bandwidth).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using "Nyquist rate" and "Nyquist frequency" interchangeably.** The first is $2B$, the second is $f_s/2$.
- **Sampling at exactly the Nyquist rate in practice.** Real filters can't have zero transition width, and phase can hide amplitude ([[q0668]]).
- **Thinking you can remove aliases digitally afterwards.** Once folded, they are indistinguishable from real in-band signals.
- **Forgetting about noise.** Broadband noise above $f_s/2$ folds into the band just like signals.
- **Assuming the Nyquist rate depends on the signal's *fundamental*.** It depends on the highest significant component, including harmonics.
:::

::: deep
## What sampling does to the spectrum

Sampling multiplies $x(t)$ by an impulse train at spacing $T_s$. Multiplication in time is convolution in frequency, and the transform of an impulse train is another impulse train at multiples of $f_s$. The result: the spectrum of the sampled signal is the original spectrum **repeated at every multiple of $f_s$**.

## Nyquist rate versus Nyquist frequency

| Term | Symbol | Depends on | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nyquist rate | $2B$ | The signal | 20 kHz audio → 40 kS/s |
| Nyquist frequency | $f_s/2$ | The sampler | $f_s=48$ kS/s → 24 kHz |

Condition: Nyquist frequency of the sampler must exceed the highest frequency of the signal.

## Why real systems sample above the Nyquist rate

An ideal brick-wall filter doesn't exist. The anti-alias filter must pass the signal band (up to $B=20$ kHz) and attenuate everything that would alias *into* that band: frequencies above $f_s-B$. The gap from $B$ to $f_s-B$ is the transition band.

For CD audio ($f_s=44.1$ kHz, $B=20$ kHz) the stopband starts at $44.1-20=24.1$ kHz: a transition band of just 4.1 kHz. Requiring 80 dB attenuation there, the Butterworth order estimate $n\approx80/(20\log_{10}(24.1/20))=49$ shows why the analog filter alone is impractical.

> [!NOTE]
> The Butterworth estimate ignores passband droop and phase issues and uses the 3 dB point at 20 kHz. It is meant to show the *scale* of the burden, not as a design.

## Oversampling and noise

Quantization noise power is fixed by the converter's step size and spread uniformly over $0\ldots f_s/2$. After digital low-pass filtering to the signal band $B$, the noise in-band is reduced by $10\log_{10}\left(\frac{f_s/2}{B}\right)$ dB: 3 dB per doubling. For $f_s=4\times2B$: $10\log_{10}4=6.02$ dB, which is one extra bit. Sigma-delta converters go further with noise shaping.

## Bandpass sampling: deliberately breaking the rule

The theorem is really about *bandwidth*, not highest frequency. A signal confined to $[f_L,f_H]$ can be sampled at $f_s\ge2B$ if $f_s$ is chosen so the replicas don't overlap:

$$\frac{2f_H}{n}\le f_s\le\frac{2f_L}{n-1},\quad n=1,2,\ldots,\left\lfloor\frac{f_H}{B}\right\rfloor.$$

For a 70 MHz IF with 20 MHz bandwidth ($f_L=60$, $f_H=80$ MHz, $B=20$ MHz): $n=3$ gives $53.3\le f_s\le60$ MS/s; choose 56 MS/s, and the band lands at $60-56=4$ MHz to $80-56=24$ MHz, inside $0\ldots f_s/2=28$ MHz without overlap. This is how software radios digitize IF signals with converters running well below the carrier frequency, though the ADC's analog input bandwidth must still reach 80 MHz and clock jitter matters more at high input frequency.

## Worked example: choosing a sample rate for a vibration sensor

An accelerometer monitors bearing faults with content up to $B=3$ kHz, but the electronics also pick up noise out to 20 kHz. You choose a 3-pole Butterworth RC-style anti-alias filter with corner at 3 kHz. Its attenuation at frequency $f$ is $10\log_{10}[1+(f/f_c)^{2n}]$.

- At $f_s=10$ kS/s the frequencies that alias onto 3 kHz begin at $10-3=7$ kHz. Attenuation there: $10\log_{10}[1+(7/3)^6]=22$ dB. That leaves out-of-band noise only 22 dB down, which is poor for a measurement with a 60 dB dynamic range goal.
- At $f_s=40$ kS/s the aliasing region starts at $37$ kHz: attenuation $10\log_{10}[1+(37/3)^6]=65$ dB. The same simple filter now suffices, and the extra samples are decimated in software.

The lesson is general: a modest oversampling ratio buys a lot of anti-alias filtering for free, and the cost is only ADC speed and memory (here 4× more samples). When the ADC or memory budget is tight, you instead raise the filter order or switch to an active elliptic design.

## Signals that aren't band-limited

A truly band-limited signal is also infinite in time; real signals are neither. Practical engineering sets an *effective* bandwidth: the frequency above which content is negligible for your purpose. For a digital edge, that's about $0.35/t_r$ (1 ns gives 350 MHz) so a scope or ADC needs to sample well above 700 MS/s to preserve it. And operations that widen bandwidth must be counted: squaring a signal with $B=5$ kHz creates content to 10 kHz, so the Nyquist rate becomes 20 kS/s; a mixer's output includes sum frequencies $f_1+f_2$.

## A related but different Nyquist idea

The phrase "Nyquist criterion" is also used in data communications (no inter-symbol interference requires symbol rate $\le2B$ for bandwidth $B$) and in control (Nyquist stability criterion). They share a name but are different ideas; context tells you which is meant.
:::

::: applications
- **Audio**: 44.1 kHz (CD) and 48 kHz (video) sample rates for 20 kHz content; higher rates for production.
- **Oscilloscopes and DAQ**: sample rate sized to bandwidth; interpolation and anti-alias filtering built in.
- **Software-defined radio**: bandpass sampling of IF signals and oversampled sigma-delta converters.
- **Industrial sensing**: vibration analysis requires sample rates above 2× the highest machine-fault frequency, with an anti-alias filter.
- **Control loops**: sample rate chosen well above the loop bandwidth (often 10–20×) to limit phase lag.
:::

::: assumptions
- The sampling theorem assumes a strictly band-limited signal and ideal sinc reconstruction; real systems approximate both.
- "Nyquist rate" is defined for baseband signals as $2B$; for bandpass signals the minimum is still $2B$ with a carefully chosen $f_s$.
- The filter-order numbers use Butterworth magnitude response and 80 dB rejection as an illustration.
- Oversampling gains assume the quantization noise is white and the post-filter is ideal.
- Some texts define the Nyquist rate as $f_s/2$; the common definition (and the one used here) is $2f_{max}$.
:::

::: followups
### What's the difference between the Nyquist rate and the Nyquist frequency?
The Nyquist rate is $2f_{max}$ and describes the minimum sample rate for a given signal. The Nyquist frequency is $f_s/2$ and describes the highest frequency representable at a given sample rate. For 20 kHz audio the Nyquist rate is 40 kS/s; at 48 kS/s the Nyquist frequency is 24 kHz.

### Why is CD audio 44.1 kS/s instead of 40?
Because the anti-alias filter needs a transition band. With 20 kHz content the stopband must start by $44.1-20=24.1$ kHz, giving about 4 kHz of transition band. At exactly 40 kS/s there would be none. Higher rates (88.2, 96, 176.4, 192 kS/s) widen the guard band further so the filter can be gentle.

### Does oversampling improve resolution?
Yes, by averaging quantization noise: each doubling of sample rate (filtering to the same band) gives 3 dB, or half a bit. A 4× oversampled ADC with decimation gains one bit. This works if the noise is white and uncorrelated with the signal (dither helps). Sigma-delta converters use noise shaping for much larger gains.

### Can you sample a 100 MHz signal with a 50 MS/s ADC?
Only if the signal is a narrow band and $f_s$ is chosen so the band does not overlap its own alias. For a 95–105 MHz band (B = 10 MHz), 50 MS/s fails: 100 MHz is a multiple of $f_s$, so the band folds around DC and its two halves overlap. Choosing 45 MS/s works ($n=5$ allows 42–47.5 MS/s): 95 MHz maps to 5 MHz and 105 MHz to 15 MHz, inside the 22.5 MHz Nyquist zone. The ADC's input bandwidth and clock jitter must still support 105 MHz. For a wideband signal, no.

### Scenario: your 100 kS/s ADC log shows an unexpected 2 kHz tone, while the scope shows only a 98 kHz switching spur on the ADC input. What happened?
The 98 kHz spur is above the Nyquist frequency of 50 kHz, so it aliases to $|98-100|=2$ kHz: the "tone" is the spur. Without an anti-alias filter the ADC cannot tell them apart. Fix: add an analog low-pass before the ADC (corner well below 50 kHz), or increase the sample rate or remove the noise source. You can confirm by changing $f_s$: the aliased component will move.
:::

"Nyquist rate of a signal" most commonly means $2f_{max}$. Sometimes interviewers mean the *Nyquist frequency* (half the sample rate); the answer above distinguishes them.
