::: basics
### In plain words
The receiver of a differential pair expects the two wires to be exact mirror images at the same instant. If one wire arrives late, for a moment the two wires do not mirror each other, and part of the signal turns into common-mode, which is the same-direction motion on both wires. That hurts the eye, adds jitter and makes the pair radiate more noise. You fix it by making both traces arrive together.

### Picture it
Picture two rowers in a boat who must pull their oars together. If one is late, the boat does not just slow a little. It also wobbles sideways, because the pulls no longer cancel. The sideways wobble is the common-mode noise. The forward motion barely changes, which is why the damage is easy to miss.

### Words you need
- **Skew**: the time difference between the two lines of a pair ([[q0517]]).
- **Common-mode**: voltage or current that moves both lines the same way. It radiates well ([[q0683]]).
- **Mode conversion**: signal turning from differential into common-mode because of imbalance.
- **CMRR**: how well a receiver rejects common-mode noise that arrives from outside.
- **Eye diagram**: an overlay of many bits that shows timing and voltage margin ([[q0698]]).

### The simplest example
A 3 mm length difference on FR-4 is about $3 \times 5.9 = 17.7$ ps of skew. The common-mode pulse at each edge peaks near $(V_s/2)(\Delta t/t_r)$. For a larger 25 ps skew, with a 0.4 V swing and a 100 ps edge, that is $0.2 \times 0.25 = 50$ mV on every edge. The receiver's CMRR cannot remove it, because the pair made it inside the channel. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds the frequency-domain numbers, all the sources of skew, and how to measure and fix it.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand **mode conversion**: when the two halves of a pair are not simultaneous, a piece of the differential signal turns into common-mode, which the receiver can't reject and which radiates. A strong answer quantifies it (6 ps per mm; the conversion grows with frequency and skew), lists the visible symptoms (smaller eye, common-mode noise, EMI, jitter), and gives the full cure list: length-match in delay, locally at each asymmetry, with symmetric vias, connectors, AC caps and package, plus awareness of fibre-weave skew. The word "source" in the question is probably meant as "receiver"; see interpretations.
:::

::: why
Intra-pair skew is a classic cause of EMC failures and of links that train at room temperature but not on the production line. Because it shows up as common-mode current on cables, a board with perfect-looking eye diagrams can still fail radiated emissions. Interviewers like this question because the answer ties together layout, timing, EMI and the receiver's CMRR. It also sets up the meander technique in [[q0521]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Differential and common-mode components**: $V_d=V_P-V_N$, $V_{cm}=(V_P+V_N)/2$. See [[q0683]], [[q0684]].
- **Skew**: arrival-time difference $\Delta t$ between P and N; 1 mm of microstrip is about 5.9 ps. See [[q0517]].
- **Mode conversion**: skew creates $V_{cm}$ from $V_d$; its size grows with frequency.
- **CMRR**: rejects *incoming* common-mode noise, but cannot remove common-mode that is generated in the line.
- **Eye diagram and jitter**: skew and its conversion reduce eye opening; see [[q0698]], [[q0703]].
- **EMI**: common-mode current on traces and cables radiates much more than differential current. See [[q0518]].
:::

::: answer
"The receiver wants P and N to be exact mirror images at the same instant. If one line arrives late, then for a short time both lines are moving in the same direction, or one is moving and the other isn't, and that interval looks like common-mode instead of differential.

So skew does three bad things. First, it converts some of your signal into common-mode, which is a voltage the receiver is designed to ignore, so you lose a bit of signal, though the loss is small. Second, that common-mode is real current on the traces and the cable, and common-mode current radiates far better than differential current, so skew shows up as an EMI problem. Third, it eats timing margin: the differential edge gets slower and the eye gets narrower, which means jitter at the receiver.

The quantity that matters is the ratio of skew to rise time and bit time. At 5 GHz, 20 picoseconds of skew is about 3 millimetres of mismatch and gives a mode-conversion ratio of roughly minus 10 dB in S-parameter terms, which is a common-mode voltage about a sixth of the differential amplitude. 5 picoseconds drops that to about minus 22 dB.

To ensure they arrive together I length-match inside the pair, in time rather than millimetres, and I do it locally where the asymmetry appears: after each bend, at the breakout, around vias. I use identical vias, symmetric AC-coupling caps and connectors, and for the fastest links I think about glass-weave skew. Then I verify with a TDR or a differential-to-common-mode S-parameter."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** Let the ideal pair be $v_P(t)=A\,s(t)$ and $v_N(t)=-A\,s(t)$ about the common-mode level. If N is delayed by $\Delta t$:
$$V_d(t)=A[s(t)+s(t-\Delta t)],\qquad V_{cm}(t)=\tfrac{A}{2}[s(t)-s(t-\Delta t)]$$
For an edge of rise time $t_r$ and $\Delta t<t_r$ the common-mode pulse peaks at $\approx \tfrac{V_s}{2}\tfrac{\Delta t}{t_r}$ for a single-ended swing $V_s$ (peak-to-peak), and the differential edge takes $t_r+\Delta t$ to complete.

**Frequency domain.** For a sinusoidal component at $f$: $V_d=2A\cos(\pi f\Delta t)\,\sin(\ldots)$, $V_{cm}=A\sin(\pi f\Delta t)\cos(\ldots)$. The raw voltage ratio is $V_{cm}/V_d=\tfrac12\tan(\pi f\Delta t)$. In mixed-mode S-parameter terms, where each mode is normalised to its own impedance ($Z_{diff}=2Z_0$, $Z_{cm}=Z_0/2$), the wave ratio is 6 dB higher:
$$\left|\frac{S_{cd21}}{S_{dd21}}\right|=\tan(\pi f\,\Delta t)$$
(for a lossless skewed pair $|S_{dd21}|=\cos(\pi f\Delta t)$ and $|S_{cd21}|=\sin(\pi f\Delta t)$). At 5 GHz and $\Delta t=20$ ps: $\tan(0.314)=0.325$ (−9.8 dB; voltage ratio 0.16). At $\Delta t=5$ ps: 0.079 (−22 dB; voltage ratio 0.039). The differential loss is only $\cos(\pi f\Delta t)$: 0.951 (−0.44 dB). Conclusion: skew mostly hurts as *common-mode generation* (EMI, receiver common-mode range, crosstalk), with a smaller effect on the differential amplitude.

**Consequences.**
- Smaller differential amplitude at the transition and slower differential edge: reduced eye width/height at the receiver (jitter, deterministic jitter).
- Common-mode current: radiation and noise on connected cables; the receiver's CMRR can't remove it because it appears *in the signal*.
- Receiver errors if the receiver's input common-mode range is exceeded, or duty-cycle distortion on clock pairs.

**Ensuring simultaneity.** (1) Match delay (ps), not length, within the pair; (2) compensate at the place of the asymmetry ([[q0521]]); (3) identical via structures and symmetric components; (4) account for package/connector internal lengths; (5) use fibre-weave mitigation for ≥ 10 Gb/s; (6) verify by TDR/TDT, mode-conversion S-parameters (differential to common, $S_{cd21}$) or an eye diagram. A typical target is a few percent of UI or the limit specified by the interface standard.
:::

::: short
"If the two lines of a pair don't arrive together, part of the differential signal turns into common-mode. That's a voltage the receiver ignores, but it's also current that radiates and a signal-dependent disturbance, so skew costs eye width, adds jitter and creates EMI. The mode conversion, in S-parameter terms, goes as tan of pi times frequency times skew, so 20 ps at 5 GHz is about minus 10 dB. To avoid it I match the pair in delay, locally at each bend and via, keep everything symmetric, and verify with TDR or an eye diagram."
:::

::: long
"A differential receiver assumes P and N are mirror images in time. If one trace is longer, its edge arrives late. During the overlap, one line has started moving and the other hasn't, so the average of the two, the common-mode voltage, wobbles. That wobble is a piece of your signal that has been converted into common-mode.

The consequences are, first, a degraded differential signal: the edge takes rise time plus skew to complete, the eye gets narrower, and you get deterministic jitter. That part is usually modest. Second, and more serious, the common-mode content is current flowing in the same direction in both traces and the cable. Common-mode current radiates much more efficiently than differential current, so skew shows up in EMC testing. The receiver's common-mode rejection doesn't help, because the common-mode was created inside the line rather than coming from outside.

I can quantify it. For a sine component the mode-conversion ratio, in S-parameter terms, is tan of pi times frequency times skew. For 20 picoseconds at 5 gigahertz, which is 3.4 millimetres of microstrip, that's 0.32, or minus 10 dB, which means a common-mode voltage of about a sixth of the differential swing. For 5 picoseconds it's minus 22 dB. So skew matters more the faster the link.

To make them arrive together I do several things. I length-match within the pair, in time rather than length, and at the place where the asymmetry arises: right after a bend, at the breakout, at vias. I use identical vias and symmetric placement of caps, connectors and ESD parts. I check the package and connector internal lengths. On fast links I worry about glass weave, so I use spread-glass laminate or route at a slight angle. And I verify it with TDR, a mode-conversion S-parameter, or an eye diagram."
:::

::: terms
- **Intra-pair skew**: difference in arrival time between the two lines of one pair.
- **Mode conversion**: conversion of differential-mode signal into common-mode (or vice versa); measured by $S_{cd21}$ (differential-to-common transmission).
- **Common-mode current**: current flowing in the same direction in both conductors; the main radiator.
- **CMRR**: receiver's ability to reject common-mode at its input.
- **Deterministic jitter (DJ)**: bounded, repeatable timing error, e.g. from skew or ISI.
- **Eye width / height**: horizontal / vertical opening of the eye diagram.
- **Fibre-weave skew**: skew caused by differing local dielectric constant under the two traces.
- **Differential TDR**: reflectometry using a pair of complementary steps to examine pair symmetry and impedance.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "skew causes timing errors and noise." An exceptional answer:
- Explains **mode conversion** with $V_{cm}=\tfrac{A}{2}[s(t)-s(t-\Delta t)]$ and gives the **tan($\pi f\Delta t$)** estimate with numbers.
- Distinguishes the **small differential loss** from the **large common-mode/EMI** effect, and notes the receiver's CMRR cannot cancel internally generated common-mode.
- Converts skew to length (**5.9 ps/mm**) and to a fraction of UI.
- Lists **all** sources of skew: bends, vias, package, connector, AC caps, fibre weave, driver output skew.
- Includes **verification**: TDR, mode-conversion S-parameters, eye diagram.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Skew just shifts the timing, so it doesn't matter."** It also generates common-mode current and widens the edge; for EMI it matters even when the eye looks fine.
- **Believing CMRR will fix it.** CMRR attenuates common-mode at the input; the mode-converted energy is already part of the line current and radiates before the receiver.
- **Matching only the total length at the connector.** Internal skew at bends and vias accumulates and isn't undone later.
- **Ignoring package and connector lengths.** Their internal delays can be tens of ps.
- **Matching with unequal vias or layers** and then meandering length to compensate.
- **Confusing intra-pair skew with lane-to-lane skew.** The latter is deskewed by the receiver in most serial standards; the former is not.
:::

## Why the receiver can't fix it

CMRR is defined at the receiver for noise that *arrives* common-mode. The mode-converted common-mode is produced inside the channel from the signal itself, so it travels as real current in both conductors toward the receiver and into any cable. Three consequences:

1. **Radiation.** A common-mode current $I_{cm}$ in a cable of length $\ell$ radiates in proportion to $I_{cm}\ell f$ (the standard short-dipole estimate), and it radiates much more efficiently than the equal-and-opposite differential current. Even a small converted fraction can dominate emissions.
2. **Receiver headroom.** The receiver's input common-mode range can be exceeded when common-mode pulses ride on a high common-mode offset or on noise.
3. **Susceptibility and reconversion.** Common-mode can convert back to differential at the next asymmetry (connector, cable, receiver), degrading the eye later.

## What causes skew

| Source | Typical size | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Length difference at a bend | $s_c\theta$ per bend: 0.69 mm = 4 ps for a 90° bend at pitch 0.44 mm | Compensation jog after the bend |
| Unequal vias / layer changes | ~10 ps per 1.6 mm barrel if only one trace uses it | Identical via pair |
| Package and connector internal lengths | 10s of ps, from datasheet/IBIS | Add board length to compensate |
| Fibre weave | Up to several ps/inch on coarse weave | Spread glass, angled routing, loose coupling |
| Driver output skew / unequal rise-fall | few ps | Choose a better driver; can't fix on the board |
| Asymmetric parts (caps, ESD, test points) | Variable | Symmetric placement |
| Different trace environments (nearby metal, solder mask) | ~1–3% of delay | Keep the pair together |

## How much is acceptable?

A common rule of thumb is to keep intra-pair skew below about 5% of UI, or the interface's published limit. At 10 Gb/s (UI = 100 ps), 5% is 5 ps = 0.85 mm = 33 mil of microstrip. At 5 Gb/s (UI = 200 ps), 10 ps = 1.7 mm. Many designers aim for tighter, 1–2 ps, in multi-gigabit designs and check the mode conversion directly.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The "5% of UI" figure is a design heuristic, not a standard; USB, PCIe, HDMI and others specify their own intra-pair limits, sometimes in ps, sometimes in mils, and these change with generations. Use the standard's design guide.

## Worked example: a USB-class pair with a 3 mm mismatch

A pair carrying 5 Gb/s (UI = 200 ps, Nyquist 2.5 GHz) has a 3 mm length difference in microstrip, so $\Delta t=17.7$ ps. At 2.5 GHz: $|S_{cd21}/S_{dd21}|=\tan(\pi\times2.5\times10^9\times17.7\times10^{-12})=\tan(0.139)=0.14$ (−17 dB; voltage ratio 0.07); the differential loss is $\cos(0.139)=0.990$. Edge-rate content is higher, so at the 5th harmonic (12.5 GHz) the conversion is $\tan(0.695)=0.83$ (−1.6 dB; voltage ratio 0.42): most of that harmonic's energy is now common-mode, which is what radiates. If the standard's limit is 5% of UI = 10 ps, this exceeds it by 77%. A single compensation jog of 3 mm on the short trace brings it back to near zero.

## Real hardware behaviour

- **EMC pre-compliance:** a pair with skew often shows a spectral line at the symbol rate and its odd harmonics in common-mode on a current probe around the cable.
- **Receiver equalisation** (CTLE/DFE) works on the differential signal; it can't remove the common-mode.
- **Temperature and humidity** change the dielectric constant of the glass and resin differently, so weave-induced skew can vary with environment.
:::

::: applications
- **USB 3.x / PCIe / SATA / DisplayPort lanes**: tight intra-pair skew limits, verified in compliance testing.
- **Gigabit Ethernet and automotive Ethernet**: balanced pair behaviour to pass radiated and conducted emissions.
- **DDR clock pairs (CK/CK#) and DQS**: crossing-point and duty-cycle sensitivity to skew.
- **LVDS camera/display links** where cable and board common-mode radiation is regulated.
- **Backplane and cable assemblies** specified by mode-conversion limits ($S_{cd21}$, $S_{dc21}$).
:::

::: assumptions
- Analysis assumes **linear, time-invariant** channel and an ideal complementary driver; real drivers have their own output skew and slew mismatch.
- The $\tan(\pi f\Delta t)$ relation assumes a pure delay of one line; other asymmetries (loss, impedance differences) also convert modes.
- "Arrive at the same time" refers to **intra-pair** timing; parallel pairs (lanes) can arrive at different times if the receiver deskews them.
- Numbers use microstrip at 5.9 ps/mm; stripline is 6.9 ps/mm.
- For slow differential links (RS-485 at 115 kbaud), skew of ns is irrelevant.
:::

::: followups
### What if the receiver is a differential-to-single-ended logic input?
Then skew also produces a glitch or a delayed/narrowed pulse at the output: if the common-mode pulse and the differential level briefly cross the receiver's threshold in the wrong order, the output can chatter. For clocks the crossing point moves, causing duty-cycle distortion. The cure is the same, but the symptoms appear as logic errors rather than as EMI.

### Does intra-pair skew matter more than inter-pair (lane-to-lane) skew?
For serial links with per-lane clock recovery, lane-to-lane skew is usually absorbed by deskew buffers (tens of ns allowed in PCIe lanes, for example), while intra-pair skew can't be undone after the line, so it dominates board design. For source-synchronous parallel buses, lane skew matters just as much, as in [[q0517]].

### How do you measure skew in a finished board?
With a TDR/TDT scope: launch matched steps into the pair and compare arrival times (a few ps resolution), or use a VNA to measure the mode-conversion S-parameters ($S_{cd21}$ should be below, say, −20 to −30 dB over the band, depending on the standard). On a live link, an eye diagram with a differential probe shows crossing shifts and a current probe on the cable shows common-mode.

### What if I can't match exactly because of the connector pinout?
I compensate in the board: measure the connector's internal lengths from the data sheet or a model (they can be ±5 ps or more), add the opposite amount of board length, and note it in the constraint sheet. If pinout forces large crossings, pair polarity swap (where allowed) or re-routing helps.

### Is a small amount of skew ever useful?
Deliberately skewed edges are used for pre-emphasis and in some multiphase clocking schemes, but in a pair on a PCB the skew is a defect. Don't confuse that with intentional delay between lanes or clock phases in the system, which is handled in silicon.
:::

::: interpretations
The question says "arrive to the source", which can't be right: signals *leave* the source and arrive at the **sink/receiver**. I interpret it as "arrive at the receiver at the same time", i.e. intra-pair skew at the load. A second reading, that both halves must also be **launched** together, is also true: driver output skew adds to the PCB skew, and the budget includes both. The matching rules are expanded in [[q0519]] and meander mechanics in [[q0521]].
:::
