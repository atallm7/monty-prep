::: basics
### In plain words
A Faraday cage is a closed metal box, solid or a fine mesh. It stops outside electric fields and radio waves from reaching the inside, and stops inside ones from getting out. Charges in the metal shift until they cancel the field inside. It leaks through holes that are large compared with the wavelength, and misses static magnetic fields and wires passing through.

### Picture it
Look at the door of a microwave oven. You can see your food through its little holes, because light waves are tiny compared with the holes. But the microwaves, about 12 cm long, cannot fit through holes only a millimeter or two wide. A mesh stops a wave when the holes are much smaller than the wavelength.

### Words you need
- **Free charges**: electrons in metal that move freely when pushed by a field.
- **Wavelength**: the distance between two peaks of a wave. For the 2.45 GHz microwave oven it is about 122 mm.
- **Aperture**: any hole, slot or seam in the cage.
- **Grounded cage**: a cage connected to ground (the circuit's 0 V reference), so charge from sources inside can drain away.
- **Penetration**: a wire or cable passing through the cage, which carries outside fields in unless it is filtered.

### The simplest example
The leakage through an opening of size $l$ is about $20\log_{10}(\lambda/2l)$ dB. That is the decibel scale, where 20 dB means a field 10 times weaker. The oven's wavelength is 122 mm. For a single 2 mm hole, the formula gives about 30 dB, which is roughly 30 times weaker. A big 20 mm opening gives only about 10 dB, about 3 times weaker.

That is the whole idea: a closed conductive shell blocks fields, limited by its openings. The rest of this page covers the physics, the limits, and examples.
:::

::: why
The Faraday cage is the conceptual foundation of shielding. It explains why a shield can works, why a car is a reasonable place to sit in a thunderstorm, why microwave oven doors have a mesh, and why a hole bigger than a fraction of a wavelength spoils everything. Being able to say *why* the interior field is zero, and where the idea fails, is exactly the depth interviewers look for.

"A Faraday cage is a closed conductive enclosure, solid or a fine mesh, that blocks external electric fields and electromagnetic waves from reaching the inside, and equally stops fields from inside getting out.

The physics: in a conductor the free charges move in response to any field until the field inside the metal is zero. When an external electric field hits the cage, charges shift on the outer surface to cancel it within the conductor, and for a closed cavity with no charge inside, the field in the cavity is zero too, by Gauss's law and uniqueness of the electrostatic solution. For time-varying fields, the same thing happens dynamically: the surface currents and eddy currents oppose the incident field, which is the reflection-plus-absorption picture, with the field decaying over a skin depth.

It isn't perfect. Holes leak, and the leakage depends on size compared with wavelength; a mesh works as long as the openings are much smaller than the wavelength, which is why a microwave oven door, with holes of a millimetre or two, blocks 12 cm waves but you can see through it. A cage doesn't stop static magnetic fields, and low-frequency magnetic fields need thick or magnetic material. Anything penetrating the cage, like a wire, brings the outside in unless it's filtered. Examples are a shield can on a PCB, which with the ground plane forms a cage, a shielded room, a car or aircraft skin, and a coax cable's braid."

::: technical
**Electrostatics.** Inside a conductor in equilibrium $\mathbf E=0$ and the net charge sits on the surface. For a closed conducting shell enclosing a charge-free cavity, the cavity field satisfies $\nabla^2\phi=0$ with $\phi$ constant on the boundary, whose unique solution is $\phi$ = constant, $\mathbf E=0$ inside. So external static fields (and charges outside) are invisible to the inside (Faraday's ice-pail experiment). A charge *inside* induces equal and opposite charge on the inner surface, and the cage's outer surface shows it unless grounded: a grounded cage also screens the outside from the inside.

**Time-varying fields.** Surface current density $\mathbf K$ forms to cancel the tangential field; the response time of charges in copper ($\varepsilon/\sigma\sim10^{-19}$ s) is far shorter than any RF period. Penetration decays as $e^{-x/\delta}$ with $\delta=1/\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$; shielding effectiveness is reflection plus absorption ([[q0570]]).

**Apertures.** A mesh or slot of largest dimension $l$ transmits with $SE\approx20\log_{10}(\lambda/2l)$ dB for $l<\lambda/2$; hole depth gives waveguide-below-cutoff attenuation of about $27.3\,t/w$ dB (rectangular) or $32\,t/d$ dB (circular). Example: a single 2 mm hole at 2.45 GHz ($\lambda$ = 122 mm) gives about 29.7 dB by the slot formula, and an array of such holes in a conductive door with thickness gives more because of waveguide cutoff.

**What it does not stop.** DC and very low-frequency magnetic fields (a thin non-magnetic shell gives almost no attenuation at 50/60 Hz); fields carried in by wires, pipes and cables that penetrate; fields through large openings.

**Examples.** Shield cans plus ground planes and via fences; screen rooms and anechoic/EMC chambers; microwave oven doors; MRI scanner rooms; car and aircraft bodies (lightning current flows around the skin); coaxial cable shields; Faraday bags for phones.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Real-world effectiveness depends on seams, gaskets, doors and penetrations; the formulas are first-order estimates. For consumer "Faraday bags", effectiveness varies a lot by product and frequency; measure rather than assume.
:::

On a PCB, the cage is the shield can, the ground plane under it as the sixth wall, and the via fence. Other examples are an EMC test chamber, an MRI room, a car body, which is why lightning currents flow around the skin, and a coax cable's braid."

::: terms
- **Faraday cage / shield**: closed conductive enclosure that screens electric fields and waves.
- **Electrostatic shielding**: cancellation of external static fields by surface charge on a conductor.
- **Gauss's law**: electric flux through a closed surface equals the enclosed charge over $\varepsilon_0$.
- **Eddy current**: induced current in a conductor opposing a changing magnetic field.
- **Skin depth**: depth at which a field in a conductor falls to $1/e$.
- **Aperture**: hole or slot whose size relative to wavelength sets leakage.
- **Waveguide below cutoff**: a small tube that strongly attenuates waves below its cutoff frequency.
- **Screen room**: shielded room for EMC testing or secure work.

::: mistakes
- **"A Faraday cage blocks everything."** It doesn't block static magnetic fields and is limited at low frequency magnetic fields and by leaks.

::: deep
## The idea in electrostatics

Place a hollow metal shell in a uniform external electric field. Free charges in the metal respond until the net field inside the metal vanishes: positive charge accumulates on one side and negative on the other, producing a field that cancels the applied field *within the conductor*. Inside the cavity, with no charge, the electrostatic potential satisfies $\nabla^2\phi=0$ and is constant on the cavity wall (the whole shell is one equipotential). The unique solution of Laplace's equation with a constant boundary value is $\phi$ = constant everywhere inside, so $\mathbf E=-\nabla\phi=0$.

Figure: An external field (blue) pushes free charges to the surfaces (red). The induced charges produce a field inside the metal equal and opposite to the applied one, so the cavity sees none. Field lines end on the outer surface charge.

The reverse also holds: a charge *inside* an ungrounded cage induces opposite charge on the inner surface and equal charge on the outer surface, so outside you see its field. Connecting the cage to ground drains the outer charge and screens the outside from inside sources as well. That is why shield cans and screen rooms are grounded ([[q0571]]).

## Time-varying fields: waves

At RF the same idea holds dynamically. The charges in copper respond in about $\varepsilon/\sigma\approx10^{-19}$ s, effectively instantly. An incident wave drives a surface current that radiates a field that cancels the incident one inside. For a plane wave on a thick metal wall, what reaches the inside is the small amount that penetrates: reflected at the surface (impedance mismatch) and absorbed through a skin depth ([[q0570]]). A copper cage of 35 µm foil at 100 MHz has about 46 dB of absorption loss and 88 dB reflection loss before leakage.

## Holes: how the cage fails

If the cage is a mesh, or has slots and seams, the shield leaks. For an opening of largest dimension $l$,

$$SE\approx20\log_{10}\frac{\lambda}{2l}\ \ (l<\lambda/2).$$

Figure: Opening size sets the shielding. At 2.45 GHz a 1 mm opening gives about 35.7 dB, 5 mm about 21.8 dB and 20 mm about 9.8 dB. Doubling the opening costs 6 dB, and the shielding falls 6 dB for each doubling of frequency. The curves are capped by the formula's validity ($l<\lambda/2$); beyond that the opening transmits freely.

Example: a microwave oven runs at 2.45 GHz ($\lambda$ = 122 mm). Its door has holes about 1 to 2 mm wide: the slot formula gives about 30 to 36 dB for a single hole, and the perforated plate has thickness (waveguide below cutoff), a conductive seal and many holes, so total leakage is held well under regulatory limits. Visible light, at 400 to 700 nm, is thousands of times smaller than the hole, so it passes straight through: you can see the food, but the microwaves can't escape.

## What a Faraday cage doesn't do

- **Static magnetic fields (DC)**: eddy currents need change; a thin non-magnetic shell doesn't shield a permanent magnet. Mu-metal or thick steel is used for low-frequency magnetic shielding.
- **Low-frequency magnetic fields** (50/60 Hz): skin depth in copper is about 9 mm at 60 Hz; a thin shell absorbs little.
- **Anything entering through the wall**: wires, pipes, cables, waveguides without cutoff. A power cord brings in conducted noise; filters, feed-through capacitors and bonded shields are needed.
- **Fields through big openings and unsealed doors.**
- **Sound, and gases**: unrelated.

## Cage on a PCB

A board-level cage has five metal walls plus the board's ground plane as the sixth, and via fences through the board to close the sides between planes. The cage design rules: ground ring and contact spacing under $\lambda/20$, solid plane, filtered crossings, no antenna inside, and a cavity-resonance check ([[q0572]], [[q0569]]).

## Worked example: how thick must the wall be?

Absorption loss through a wall of thickness $t$ is $A=8.686\,t/\delta$ dB, with copper's skin depth $\delta\approx66.1/\sqrt{f}$ mm ($f$ in Hz). The thickness needed for 40 dB of *absorption alone* is $t=40\delta/8.686=4.6\delta$:

| Frequency | $\delta$ (Cu) | $t$ for 40 dB absorption | Absorption of 35 µm foil |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 Hz | 8.5 mm | 39 mm | ≈ 0 dB |
| 100 kHz | 0.21 mm | 0.96 mm | 1.5 dB |
| 10 MHz | 21 µm | 0.10 mm | 14.5 dB |
| 100 MHz | 6.6 µm | 0.03 mm | 46 dB |

This is why a thin foil cage works so well at VHF and above and so poorly at power-line frequencies. The 35 µm foil of a PCB plane is an excellent absorber at 100 MHz but is transparent to a 60 Hz magnetic field. At low frequency, electric fields are still stopped, because reflection loss from the impedance mismatch is large for a high-impedance field and does not need thickness; it is the magnetic field that needs absorption or a high-permeability path. Mu-metal, which diverts magnetic flux instead of relying on eddy currents, is the standard answer below a few kHz, and it is used in thin sheets with careful handling, since mechanical stress degrades its permeability.

## Openings as waveguides below cutoff

A hole through a thick wall is a short waveguide. A circular hole of diameter $d$ has a cutoff frequency for its lowest mode of $f_c=\frac{1.841\,c}{\pi d}$. For $d=3$ mm, $f_c=58.6$ GHz. Well below cutoff the field decays inside the hole exponentially with a loss of roughly $32\,t/d$ dB for wall thickness $t$ (rectangular openings: about $27.3\,t/w$ for the wide dimension $w$). A 3 mm hole in a 6 mm thick wall therefore contributes about $32\times6/3=64$ dB at 2.4 GHz, whereas the same 3 mm hole in a 0.2 mm foil, where $t/d$ is small, gives only about 2 dB of waveguide attenuation and the slot formula 26 dB determines the shielding. The practical lesson: thick walls with small round holes (honeycomb vents in EMC rooms and equipment racks) are very effective, while a thin sheet with the same holes relies only on each hole being small compared with the wavelength, and the leakage of many holes adds up.

## Limiting cases and when "E = 0 inside" fails

- **Not closed:** a cage with a gap is a slot antenna. As the gap length approaches half a wavelength the shielding collapses to zero, however thick the metal is.
- **Wires entering:** a cable passing through the wall carries the outside potential in. The shielded room works only with filtered power and signal lines.
- **Static case, charge inside:** a charge inside an ungrounded cage still produces an external field, as the induced outer-surface charge shows; grounding drains it.
- **Cage resonance:** a closed metal box has resonant modes ($f_{101}=\frac{c}{2}\sqrt{1/a^2+1/b^2}$ for a flat box). A box whose two largest dimensions are 1 m resonates near 212 MHz, and a 3 m × 6 m screen room near 56 MHz; and fields at that frequency can be larger inside than outside; EMC chambers add absorber to damp it.
- **Strong low-frequency magnetic fields:** the cage's own eddy currents reduce the field but cannot cancel it when $t<\delta$.

## Bench verification: measuring a cage

Measure the shielding effectiveness the way a lab does: a transmitting antenna on one side and a receiving antenna on the other, first with no shield to record the reference level, then through the shield. SE is the ratio in dB. For a board-level can, replace the antennas by a small near-field loop probe over the can lid and an identical probe measurement over the same part with the can removed. A real can reads 20 to 40 dB lower at the lid but shows hot spots at the seams and at each cable entry, which is exactly where the earlier equations predicted leaks. Check at several frequencies, because an opening that is fine at 500 MHz can be an effective slot antenna at 3 GHz. To test the grounding, measure the DC resistance from the can to the ground plane at several points: a soldered can should read in the milliohms (use a four-wire meter), so anything well above that at some corners indicates a poorly soldered seam, and RF contact will be worse than DC.

::: assumptions
- **Closed, continuous conductor** with thickness above a few skin depths for the frequency of interest.
- **Apertures are small relative to wavelength**; the slot formula is first-order.
- **Electrostatic/RF fields**: static and low-frequency magnetic fields are different (see "what it doesn't do").
- **Grounding** matters for sources inside the cage and for removing outer-surface charge.
- **Penetrations are filtered**; otherwise the cage is bypassed.

::: followups
### Why is the field zero inside a closed conducting shell?
Free charges in the metal move until the field in the metal is zero, so the shell is an equipotential. The potential inside a charge-free cavity satisfies Laplace's equation with a constant boundary value, whose unique solution is a constant. A constant potential means zero field. Any external field therefore ends on the outer surface charges.

### Why can you see through a microwave oven door but the microwaves don't escape?
The perforation holes are about 1 to 2 mm, far smaller than the 122 mm wavelength of 2.45 GHz microwaves, so each hole leaks little ($20\log(\lambda/2l)$ is about 30 dB or more for one hole, plus attenuation from the thickness acting as a waveguide below cutoff). Visible light has a wavelength of hundreds of nanometres, much smaller than the holes, so it passes freely.

### Is a car a Faraday cage in a lightning strike?
Approximately. A metal car body carries the lightning current around the outside and the interior field is small, so occupants are generally protected. It isn't a perfect cage: windows and seams leak at high frequencies, and the protection comes primarily from the current flowing in the shell rather than perfect field cancellation. Convertibles and fibreglass bodies don't provide it.

### Why must a shield be grounded if the cage argument says the interior is already field-free?
For external fields, grounding isn't required for static shielding of the inside. But for sources *inside* the cage, the outer surface carries induced charge unless grounded, so the outside sees the field. Practically, grounding gives the displacement current a path, keeps the cage at a defined potential, and avoids the cage re-radiating ([[q0571]]).

### Scenario: a screen room fails its shielding test at 1 GHz but passes at 100 MHz. What do you suspect?
Leakage through seams, door gaskets or penetrations. Shielding effectiveness through apertures decreases about 6 dB per doubling of frequency (and the absorption through walls grows), so a poor door seal or a poorly filtered cable shows up at the higher frequency. I'd locate the leak with a sniffer probe and a transmitter inside, and fix the gaskets, filters or bonding.
