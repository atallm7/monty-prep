::: testing
Whether you understand PoP as a specific stacking technique (a memory package soldered on top of a processor package, with solder balls between them) and can weigh it honestly. The interviewer wants the structure (bottom logic package with a ball footprint on its top side, top memory package), the reason it exists (board area and short, low-capacitance memory interconnect), and a balanced list of drawbacks (thermal coupling, height, warpage, test and yield compounding, rework, fixed memory choices, limited bandwidth and capacity scaling). Quantifying one benefit (flight time or area saved) shows engineering depth.
:::

::: why
Every smartphone-class design faces the same tension: a high-performance processor needs a fast, wide memory interface, but the board is tiny. PoP is one of the standard answers, so recognising it helps you read teardown photos, understand why phone SoCs and DRAM aren't sold as separate parts to hobbyists, and reason about thermal, signal-integrity and assembly consequences of stacking. It's also a gateway to the broader 2.5D/3D packaging ideas (chiplets, interposers, HBM) in the questions that follow.
:::

::: concepts
- **Package-on-package (PoP)**: two or more BGA-style packages stacked vertically, bottom one (logic) carries lands/balls on its top for the memory package. See [[q0644]].
- **LPDDR memory**: low-power DRAM used in mobile devices, with a wide, fast interface.
- **Interconnect length and parasitics**: shorter distance means less delay, inductance and capacitance. See [[q0039]].
- **Thermal coupling**: heat from the logic die must escape through or around the memory package. See [[q0648]].
- **Warpage and coplanarity**: stacked thin packages bend with temperature, causing opens or shorts at reflow.
- **Known-good-die/package testing**: stacking compounds yield loss. See [[q0650]].
- **Alternatives**: side-by-side placement, system-in-package (SiP), 2.5D/3D integration. See [[q0653]].
:::

::: answer
"A package-on-package chip is a stack of two packages. The bottom one is typically the processor, an application SoC, and its top surface has an array of solder-ball pads. The top package is the memory, usually LPDDR DRAM, and its balls land on those pads. The whole stack is soldered to the board as one unit with the bottom package's balls.

The main benefits are board area and electrical performance. The memory sits right above the SoC, so you save the footprint of a separate memory package, which in a phone is most of the area you'd spend on a memory chip plus its keep-out, and the interconnect between them is a millimetre or so of substrate instead of tens of millimetres of PCB trace. That means lower delay, lower capacitance and lower I/O power, and it's easier to meet timing on a high-speed LPDDR bus. It also lets a vendor mix and match: the same SoC with different memory sizes or suppliers.

The trade-offs are mostly thermal and manufacturing. The memory sits right on top of the hottest part, so the SoC's heat has to leave through the memory or down through the board, which makes the SoC run hotter and warms the DRAM. The stack is taller, and the packages warp differently during reflow, which causes opens and shorts at the interface. You also can't change the memory easily after assembly, rework is hard, and yield compounds: if either package is bad, you lose both unless you test before stacking. And you're limited by the ball count and area of the interface, so for very high bandwidth you'd go to HBM and an interposer instead."
:::

::: technical
**Structure.** Bottom package: processor die on a substrate (flip-chip or wire-bond), with peripheral lands or balls on the top surface at a fine pitch (about 0.4 to 0.5 mm is common) to receive the top package; bottom-side balls connect to the board (about 0.4 to 0.5 mm pitch in phones). Top package: one or more DRAM dies (often wire-bonded) on a small substrate with balls on its underside matching the bottom's top-side lands. The stack is assembled in one of two ways: the memory is placed and reflowed onto the logic package either at the package vendor or at the board assembler, either pre-stacked or placed during SMT.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Pitches, stack heights and ball counts vary by vendor and generation (JEDEC publishes outlines for stacked memory packages; I'm not citing specific document numbers). Treat pitches of 0.4 to 0.5 mm as typical, not as limits, and use vendor design guides.

**Electrical benefit: flight time and capacitance.** Signal delay on FR-4 microstrip is about $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/c \approx 6.2$ ps/mm (for $\varepsilon_{eff} \approx 3.5$). A 25 mm board route is about 156 ps; a 3 mm PoP interconnect about 19 ps. For LPDDR4X at 4266 MT/s the unit interval is 234 ps, so a 25 mm route consumes two thirds of a UI in flight time, which matters for skew budgets, while the PoP link is under 10% of it. With a rise time of about 0.3 UI = 70 ps, a line behaves as a transmission line when its delay exceeds about $t_r/6 \approx 12$ ps, i.e. longer than roughly 1.9 mm; the PoP link is near the edge of the lumped regime while a PCB trace is clearly a transmission line needing careful design.

**I/O energy.** The energy to charge a pin's capacitance each transition is of order $CV^2$. With $V = 0.6$ V (LPDDR4X VDDQ nominal) and a PCB route plus pads at 3.3 pF: 1.19 pJ per transition; with about 1 pF for the PoP interconnect: 0.36 pJ, about 3× lower. This ignores termination, and LPDDR's low-swing signalling changes absolute numbers, but the ratio follows capacitance.

**Bandwidth.** A 64-bit LPDDR4X-4266 interface (two 32-bit channels) gives $4266\times10^6\times64/8 = 34.1$ GB/s. A 64-bit LPDDR5X-8533 interface would give 68.3 GB/s (LPDDR4X itself tops out at 4266 MT/s). Higher bandwidth means more pins or higher speed; PoP interface pin counts and bump pitch limit how far it scales.

**Yield compounding.** If bottom and top packages have 98% yield each and stacking has 99% yield, the combined stack yield is $0.98\times0.98\times0.99 = 95.1\%$. If both packages are tested before stacking, only the assembly yield (99%) applies to the stack but you pay for testing. Use known-good packages.

**Trade-off table.**

| Aspect | PoP | Side-by-side | 2.5D/HBM |
|---|---|---|---|
| Board area | Smallest | Largest | Small (interposer package) |
| Memory bandwidth | High (LPDDR), limited by pins | High, but longer routes | Very high |
| Memory capacity flexibility | Fixed at assembly | Flexible choice, flexible count | Fixed in package |
| Thermal | Coupled, harder | Independent | Complex, coupled |
| Rework | Hard | Easier | Not practical |
| Cost | Moderate | Lower | High |
:::

::: short
"Package-on-package stacks a memory package, usually LPDDR, on top of a processor package, joined by solder balls. It saves board area and gives a very short, low-capacitance memory interface, so less delay and I/O power: 3 mm of stack versus about 25 mm of PCB trace is roughly 19 versus 156 ps. The downsides are thermal coupling since the DRAM sits on the hottest die, extra height, warpage at reflow, yield compounding when stacking, fixed memory after assembly and hard rework."
:::

::: long
"Package-on-package means two packages stacked and soldered together. The bottom package is the processor, and it has a second set of pads on its top surface. The top package is the memory, normally LPDDR DRAM, and its balls land on those pads. The stack goes onto the PCB like a single BGA.

The reasons are area and electrical performance. In a phone, putting the memory directly over the SoC saves the whole footprint of a separate memory package and keep-out. And the memory interconnect becomes a couple of millimetres through two substrates rather than tens of millimetres of PCB trace. Using about 6.2 picoseconds per millimetre, that's under 20 picoseconds of flight time versus about 150 for a 25 millimetre trace, against a unit interval of 234 picoseconds at LPDDR4X-4266, so timing margin is much easier. Capacitance is lower too, around 1 picofarad instead of 3 or so, so the CV-squared energy per transition is roughly three times smaller, which helps battery life.

The trade-offs are mostly thermal and mechanical. The memory sits right on top of the processor, so the SoC's heat has to leave through the memory package or down through the board, which raises the SoC temperature and warms the DRAM, and DRAM needs faster refresh when hot. The stack is taller. Two thin packages warp differently at reflow, so you can get opens at the corners or bridging. Memory type and size are fixed when you assemble, so you can't upgrade, and rework is very hard because you're reflowing a stack. Finally, yield compounds: 98% times 98% times 99% is only about 95%, so vendors test each package before stacking. If you need far more bandwidth than a PoP interface can carry, you go to HBM on an interposer instead."
:::

::: terms
- **PoP**: package-on-package, stacking packages vertically with solder balls between them.
- **LPDDR**: low-power DDR DRAM used in mobile devices.
- **Bottom/top package**: the logic (SoC) package and the memory package, respectively.
- **Interposer**: intermediate substrate (often silicon) connecting dies at fine pitch (2.5D integration).
- **Warpage**: bending of a package or board with temperature, due to CTE mismatch.
- **Known-good package (KGP)**: package verified before being stacked.
- **Unit interval (UI)**: duration of one bit period; 234 ps at 4266 MT/s.
- **VDDQ**: I/O supply of a DRAM.
- **SiP (system-in-package)**: several dies in one package, side by side or stacked.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it's memory stacked on top of a processor to save space".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Describes the **structure** (bottom package with top-side lands, top memory package) and who typically assembles it.
- Quantifies **benefits**: board area saved, flight time (about 6 ps/mm), capacitance and $CV^2$ energy.
- Gives **balanced drawbacks**: thermal coupling, height, warpage, test and **yield compounding** (with a number), rework, fixed memory.
- Places PoP **among alternatives** (side-by-side, SiP, 2.5D/HBM) and says when each wins.
- Mentions **LPDDR refresh vs temperature** as a thermal consequence ([[q0648]]).
- Flags **vendor-specific** pitches and heights honestly.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying the memory is on the same die.** PoP stacks packages, each with its own die; this is not an SoC with embedded DRAM.
- **Ignoring thermals.** The memory sits on the hottest part, which is the main reason PoP is limited in power.
- **Thinking PoP always improves bandwidth.** It shortens the link but the bandwidth is set by the interface width and speed.
- **Assuming PoP memory is upgradeable.** It's soldered and fixed.
- **Forgetting yield.** Stacking two good-but-untested packages multiplies failure rates.
- **Confusing PoP with HBM or chiplets.** HBM stacks dies with TSVs on an interposer; PoP stacks packages.
:::

Figure: The SoC die sits in the bottom package's cavity region, surrounded by a ring of interface balls (orange) that land the memory package above it. The memory's own dies are wire-bonded inside the top package. The SoC die is directly under the memory, which is the source of the thermal coupling.

Assembly flows differ. In the **pre-stacked** flow, a packaging house assembles and tests the PoP stack and ships a single part; the board assembler handles it as one BGA. In the **on-board stacking** flow, the board assembler places the bottom package, then the memory with flux or paste dip, then reflows the stack on the board; this allows different memory options but demands tight process control for warpage and alignment. In both cases the interface balls are smaller and finer-pitch than the bottom package's board balls.

## Why short interconnect matters

Consider a signal travelling on FR-4 microstrip: velocity $v = c/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$, delay per length $\tau = \sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/c = 6.2$ ps/mm for $\varepsilon_{eff} = 3.5$.

Figure: A 25 mm PCB route takes about 156 ps, two thirds of a bit time at 4266 MT/s, so skew and jitter budgets get tight; the 3 mm PoP link takes about 19 ps. Length mismatch between byte lanes is the real killer, so shorter nets mean smaller absolute mismatch for the same percent tolerance.

**Transmission-line behaviour.** A rule of thumb treats a net as a transmission line when its electrical length exceeds about one sixth of the rise time: $\tau\,\ell > t_r/6$. With $t_r = 0.3\,\text{UI} = 70$ ps, the threshold is 11.7 ps, so $\ell > 1.9$ mm. The PoP interface and the board-level route both exceed that, but with a 3 mm link the reflections are small and short-lived and the termination design is simpler; the 25 mm board route needs proper impedance control and, at higher speeds, on-die termination and training ([[q0623]]).

**Capacitance and energy.** Each pin charges its own parasitic capacitance on every transition, with energy about $CV^2$. At $V = 0.6$ V: 3.3 pF (PCB route and pad) gives 1.19 pJ, 1 pF (PoP) gives 0.36 pJ. LPDDR uses low-swing, terminated signalling so absolute values differ, but the ratio follows capacitance. Multiply by tens of data pins at gigahertz rates and the difference shows up in milliwatts, which matters in a phone.

## Costs of stacking

**Height.** The stack is the sum of two package heights plus the interface balls. It's usually around a millimetre or more taller than a single package; in thin phones, this is a hard design constraint, and it's why packages use thin substrates and mould caps that become more fragile and prone to warpage.

**Warpage.** During reflow, each package bends because its materials have different CTEs: silicon about 2.6 ppm/K, mould compound and substrate 10 to 20 ppm/K. The top package can warp "smile" or "cry" relative to the bottom, so some interface balls separate (open) while others squeeze together (bridge). The remedy is controlling warpage by materials and design (mould thickness, substrate stiffness), using solder paste or flux dip with compliant ball alloys, and matching warpage behaviour of the two packages through temperature.

**Test and yield.** Defect probabilities multiply: with $Y_{bot} = 98\%$, $Y_{top} = 98\%$, $Y_{stack} = 99\%$, the product is 95.1%. If the packages are tested individually before stacking, the stack only suffers the 99% assembly yield, but the extra test insertion costs money. Vendors do this for the top package (memory) and the bottom (SoC); the stack is then tested as a unit.

**Rework.** Removing a PoP means reflowing a two-package stack, which risks separating the memory from the SoC or damaging either; many manufacturers scrap and replace.

**Flexibility and supply.** The memory size and speed are fixed at assembly, and the processor and memory must share a ball map defined by a standard or by agreement between vendors. That's good for volume products and bad for configurability.

## Alternatives and when PoP wins

- **Side-by-side memory on the PCB**: more area and longer routes, but lowest cost, upgradeable options, easier thermals and rework. Dominates in laptops, embedded boards, and anything with space.
- **System-in-package (SiP) / multi-chip module**: dies side by side or stacked in one package; avoids the second ball interface; used for wearables and modules.
- **2.5D with an interposer (HBM)**: thousands of fine connections for extreme bandwidth; expensive; used in GPUs and accelerators ([[q0651]], [[q0653]]).
- **Memory-on-package variants**: the memory dies are placed beside the SoC on the same substrate with no second ball interface; this is used in recent premium laptops and phones to avoid the PoP thermal stack.

PoP wins when board area is the dominant constraint, the memory bandwidth requirement is in the LPDDR range, and thermals are manageable: i.e. smartphones, tablets, and compact consumer devices. It loses when power is high or flexibility is needed.

## Design implications for the board

Because the PoP stack is soldered to the PCB through the bottom package's balls only, the memory signals never touch the board; the board design concerns power, the processor's other interfaces and thermal management. Decoupling for the memory rails often sits beside the stack or on the underside, and the board layout must handle the stack's height and warpage with correct pad definitions and stencil volumes (as for any fine-pitch BGA, [[q0644]]).

::: applications
- **Smartphones and tablets**: application processor with LPDDR on top is the canonical PoP use.
- **Small single-board computers and early compact Linux boards**: some used PoP memory to save space ([[q0649]] lists examples).
- **Wearables and compact consumer devices**: tight area and low-power memory interface.
- **Automotive infotainment** modules where board area is tight.
- **Cameras and drones**: processors with stacked memory to save area and weight.

::: assumptions
The pitches (0.4 to 0.5 mm), flight-time constant (6.2 ps/mm), and capacitances are typical, not vendor data. $\varepsilon_{eff} = 3.5$ is an FR-4 microstrip approximation; stripline and substrate traces differ. LPDDR4X numbers (4266 MT/s, 0.6 V) are used as an example; newer standards (LPDDR5/5X) run faster and use different voltages. The $CV^2$ energy estimate is an order-of-magnitude comparison and ignores termination. The yield numbers are illustrative. Different companies split PoP assembly differently (vendor-stacked or board-stacked) and some have moved to alternatives such as memory-on-package or InFO-based stacks for thermal reasons. A reasonable alternative answer would emphasise that PoP is mostly a mobile technology and that desktops, servers and graphics products use other approaches.
:::

::: followups
### Why is PoP so common in phones?
Because phones are area- and height-constrained, run LPDDR at moderate power, and ship in huge volumes where a dedicated, vendor-validated stack is cheap per unit. Putting the memory on top of the processor saves the board area a separate package would occupy and gives short, low-capacitance interconnect, which cuts I/O power and eases timing. The thermal penalty is manageable since the processor's power is bounded by battery and skin temperature limits anyway.

### What are the main reliability risks?
Warpage-induced opens and bridges at the interface balls during reflow, thermal-cycling fatigue of the small interface balls due to CTE mismatch between two packages, and drop or shock failures at the fine-pitch joints. They're mitigated by controlling warpage, using underfill or edge bond on the stack, and extensive board-level reliability testing.

### How does PoP differ from HBM?
PoP stacks packages with solder balls, typically LPDDR, with a few hundred connections. HBM stacks bare DRAM dies with through-silicon vias and connects them to the processor through a silicon interposer with thousands of very fine connections, giving enormously higher bandwidth at higher cost and with its own thermal issues. PoP is a mobile-scale solution; HBM is for GPUs and accelerators.

### If the memory package and SoC are each 98% yield, what's the stacked yield?
If you stack them untested with a 99% assembly yield, the product is $0.98\times0.98\times0.99 = 95.1\%$. If each package is tested before stacking, you only lose the 99% assembly yield, but you pay for extra testing. So volume products test beforehand.

### Can I just upgrade the memory in a PoP device?
Not in practice. The memory is soldered over the SoC; replacing it means reflowing the stack and risking both packages. The option exists only at assembly: the vendor selects a memory part with a compatible ball map. That's why PoP devices ship with fixed memory.

### Scenario: your PoP-based product overheats under sustained load and the DRAM reports errors. What do you do?
Measure the temperatures: thermal camera and on-chip sensors for the SoC and the DRAM's temperature sensor. If the DRAM exceeds its range (refresh must double above 85 °C, for example), reduce the SoC power or throttle earlier, improve the heat path (graphite spreader, thermal pad to the case over the memory top, copper under the board), and review airflow. Longer term, consider a different package style (memory-on-package or side-by-side) or change the thermal design. [[q0648]] has the numbers.
:::

::: interpretations
"PoP chips" is sometimes used loosely for any stacked package; in this answer it means a memory package soldered on top of a logic package with a ball interface. The question asks for "tradeoffs", so the page gives a balanced list rather than just benefits. Vendors have variants (flip-chip bottom with moulded laser via, InFO-PoP) that change details but not the trade-offs. Thermals are covered in more depth in [[q0648]] and typical products in [[q0649]].
