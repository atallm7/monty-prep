::: testing
Whether you understand that $Z_0=\sqrt{(R'+j\omega L')/(G'+j\omega C')}$ is **frequency dependent only through the loss terms and material/field dispersion**. A strong answer gives three regimes: low frequency (RC regime, $|Z_0|$ large and falling as $1/\sqrt f$, phase −45°), the flat mid-band $\sqrt{L'/C'}$ where PCB design lives, and high frequency (skin effect, dielectric dispersion, small drifts and a small imaginary part). It also states what actually matters: it is *flat enough* for the band of interest, which is why one number (50 Ω) works.
:::

::: why
TDR plots, S-parameters and "why does my 50 Ω trace measure 55 Ω at 1 kHz?" questions all hinge on this. It separates people who treat $Z_0$ as a number from those who know it's a complex, weakly frequency-dependent quantity, and it explains why long, lossy lines distort edges. It is the PCB-specific version of [[q0724]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Complex $Z_0$**: $\sqrt{(R'+j\omega L')/(G'+j\omega C')}$. See [[q0721]].
- **Corner $\omega\approx R'/L'$**: below it the line is RC-dominated (diffusive). See [[q0722]].
- **Skin effect**: $R'\propto\sqrt f$ above the frequency where $\delta<t/2$. See [[q0725]].
- **Dielectric loss and dispersion**: $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$; $\varepsilon_r$ itself varies with $f$. See [[q0723]].
- **Geometric dispersion in microstrip**: $\varepsilon_{eff}$ rises slowly with frequency.
- **Band of interest**: the signal's spectrum up to $\approx0.35/t_r$.
:::

::: answer
"For the lossless ideal line the impedance is square root of L over C and doesn't depend on frequency at all. A real trace has resistance and a lossy dielectric, so the true expression is square root of R plus j omega L over G plus j omega C, and that does vary.

At very low frequency, below roughly a megahertz for a PCB trace, the resistance beats the inductive reactance and the line behaves like a distributed RC. The impedance is large, falls as one over square root of frequency, and has a phase of about minus 45 degrees. A 50 ohm trace could look like hundreds of ohms at 10 kilohertz. Once omega L exceeds R, the impedance settles to square root of L over C, and from there through the gigahertz range it's nearly flat, which is why we can just say it's 50 ohms.

At high frequency there are small drifts: skin effect raises R and slightly lowers L, dielectric loss adds G, and the dielectric constant itself changes with frequency, so the real part moves by a few percent and it picks up a small imaginary part. These show up mostly as loss and dispersion rather than as impedance steps.

So practically: design to a number, know it only holds above the corner frequency, and check the loss budget at the high end."
:::

::: technical
$$Z_0(\omega)=\sqrt{\frac{R'(\omega)+j\omega L'(\omega)}{G'(\omega)+j\omega C'}}$$
**Regimes** (for $G'\approx0$):
1. $\omega\ll R'/L'$: $Z_0\approx\sqrt{R'/(j\omega C')}$, $|Z_0|=\sqrt{R'/\omega C'}\propto f^{-1/2}$, phase $-45^\circ$. Signal diffuses; no wave behaviour.
2. $\omega\gg R'/L'$ (and $G'\ll\omega C'$): $Z_0\approx\sqrt{L'/C'}\left[1-j\left(\frac{R'}{2\omega L'}-\frac{G'}{2\omega C'}\right)\right]$, nearly real and flat.
3. High GHz: $R'\propto\sqrt f$ (skin), internal inductance drops (L′ decreases a few percent as current moves to the surface), $G'/\omega C'=\tan\delta$ constant, $\varepsilon_{eff}(f)$ changes.

**Corner.** For our reference trace ($w=0.36$ mm, 1 oz): $R'_{dc}\approx1.4\ \Omega$/m for the trace, doubled to about 2.7 Ω/m as a rough allowance for the return path (an assumption of this simplified model), $L'\approx295$ nH/m, $C'\approx118$ pF/m. $f_c=R'/(2\pi L')\approx2.7/(2\pi\cdot295\text{ n})\approx1.5$ MHz.

**Numbers (model).** With $R'(f)=2.7\sqrt{1+f/14\text{ MHz}}\ \Omega$/m: $|Z_0|\approx600\ \Omega$ at 10 kHz, 67 Ω at 1 MHz, 50.0 Ω at 100 MHz and stays within about 1% to 1 GHz in the model (a measured line may drift 1–3 Ω due to dispersion).

**Dispersion.** Microstrip $\varepsilon_{eff}$ rises with frequency (geometric dispersion) which lowers $Z_0$ a little; FR-4's $\varepsilon_r$ decreases slowly with frequency (relaxation), which raises it. The two partly cancel and the net sign is stack-dependent.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The net high-frequency drift of $Z_0$ for real FR-4 microstrip depends on the laminate's Dk(f), glass style, copper roughness and geometry; it is typically a few percent up to 10 GHz. The corner and low-frequency numbers above come from a simplified RLGC model.
:::

::: short

Above that it flattens to $\sqrt{L'/C'}$, about 50 Ω, and stays nearly flat through GHz, with small drifts from skin effect, dielectric loss and dielectric dispersion that mostly show up as loss rather than as an impedance change."
:::

::: long
"For a lossless line the characteristic impedance is square root of L prime over C prime, and there's no frequency in it. A real PCB trace has series resistance R prime and shunt conductance G prime, so the true expression is square root of R plus j omega L over G plus j omega C.

That gives three regions. At very low frequency, where R is bigger than omega L, which for a typical PCB trace is below roughly a megahertz, the line behaves like a distributed RC. The impedance is big, its magnitude goes as square root of R over omega C so it drops as one over the square root of frequency, and its phase is minus 45 degrees. For my reference trace that's something like 600 ohms at 10 kilohertz, 67 at one megahertz.

Above the corner, where omega L beats R, it settles to square root of L over C. That's the 50 ohms we design for, and from about 100 megahertz through several gigahertz it's close to flat. It does pick up a small imaginary part from the loss terms, R over two omega L minus G over two omega C.

At gigahertz frequencies other effects drift it by a few percent. Skin effect pushes R up like the square root of frequency and trims the inductance slightly. The dielectric loss tangent adds G. And the dielectric constant itself changes with frequency, FR-4's decreasing while microstrip's effective value rises through geometric dispersion, so they partly cancel. Practically, the line is well described by one real impedance across the signal band, and the more important frequency dependence is attenuation: conductor loss up like root f and dielectric loss up like f."
:::

::: terms
- **Complex characteristic impedance**: $Z_0$ including loss terms; magnitude and phase.
- **RC regime**: low-frequency region where $R'>\omega L'$ and the line diffuses.
- **Corner frequency ($R'/2\pi L'$)**: boundary between RC and LC behaviour.
- **Skin depth ($\delta$)**: $\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu_0)}$, depth of current penetration.
- **Loss tangent ($\tan\delta$)**: ratio of dielectric loss to energy storage.
- **Dispersion**: frequency dependence of velocity/permittivity, distorting wideband signals.
- **Effective permittivity ($\varepsilon_{eff}$)**: weighted dielectric constant seen by microstrip fields.
- **TDR**: time-domain reflectometry; measures impedance at the frequencies of its edge.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "it's constant." An exceptional answer:
- Writes the **complex $Z_0$ formula** and identifies the **three regimes** with the corner $R'/2\pi L'$ and a number (about 1–2 MHz here).
- States the **low-frequency behaviour**: $|Z_0|\propto f^{-1/2}$, −45°.
- Explains small high-frequency changes: **skin effect, $\tan\delta$, dispersion** of both geometry and material.
- Notes the **practical conclusion**: flat enough in band; frequency dependence is mainly **loss/dispersion**.
- Links to **measurement**: TDR shows impedance at the edge's bandwidth; VNA/ S-parameters show it vs frequency.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Impedance is constant at all frequencies."** Only in the lossless/high-frequency limit.
- **Confusing impedance with attenuation.** Loss grows steeply with $f$; $Z_0$ barely moves.
- **Using the DC resistance of the trace as the impedance.** A 50 Ω line has milliohm DC resistance; the DC behaviour is not $Z_0$.
- **Believing skin effect changes $Z_0$ a lot.** It changes R (loss) mainly; $L'$ drops a few percent.
- **Expecting a TDR to read 50 Ω at DC.** TDR reflects at the bandwidth of its edge, mostly the flat region.
- **Applying the RC-regime formula at GHz.**
:::

::: deep
## The formula and its limits

Using the reference trace ($L'=295$ nH/m, $C'=118$ pF/m, $R'(f)=2.7\sqrt{1+f/14\text{ MHz}}\ \Omega$/m including plane contribution, $G'=0$), the magnitude is $|Z_0|=\sqrt{|R'+j\omega L'|/(\omega C')}$:

Figure: Below the corner (~1.5 MHz) the magnitude rises as $f^{-1/2}$ (10× lower frequency gives √10 ≈ 3.2× higher impedance); above it the curve flattens onto √(L'/C') = 50 Ω. The flat region covers every frequency a digital edge cares about.

**Why the RC regime exists.** At low $f$ the voltage on the trace is set by series resistance charging the capacitance to the plane, a diffusion process with $\tau\sim R'C'\ell^2$, not a wave. The phase of $Z_0$ is $\tfrac12[\arctan(\omega L'/R')-90^\circ]$, from −45° (RC) toward 0° (LC).

**Imaginary part in the LC regime.** Expanding, $Z_0\approx\sqrt{L'/C'}\,[1+j(\tfrac{G'}{2\omega C'}-\tfrac{R'}{2\omega L'})]$. At 1 GHz the model gives $R'=23$ Ω/m against $\omega L'=1850$ Ω/m; with $\tan\delta=0.02$: $R'/2\omega L'=0.006$ and $G'/2\omega C'=0.01$, so the phase of $Z_0$ is only about +0.2°. Negligible for impedance matching, yet the same terms produce the attenuation.

## The three real high-frequency effects

1. **Skin effect.** Above $\delta<t/2$ (about 14 MHz for 35 µm copper) $R'$ rises as $\sqrt f$ and internal inductance falls. The real impedance moves by <1%; the attenuation $\alpha_c=R'/(2Z_0)$ rises.
2. **Dielectric loss.** $G'=\omega C'\tan\delta$ gives $\alpha_d\approx\pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}\tan\delta/c$; again mostly attenuation.
3. **Dispersion.** *Geometric*: in microstrip the field concentrates into the dielectric as $f$ rises, so $\varepsilon_{eff}$ increases (by roughly a few percent up to 10 GHz), lowering $Z_0$ slightly and slowing the wave. *Material*: FR-4's $\varepsilon_r$ falls with frequency (e.g. from about 4.5 at 1 MHz toward 4.1–4.3 at 10 GHz, laminate dependent), which raises $Z_0$. The sum is a drift of a few ohms at most.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The Dk(f) figures quoted are typical-range statements for common FR-4 laminates; actual curves come from the laminate datasheet or a measurement (e.g. split-post resonator data) and differ by supplier.

## What the measurements show

- **TDR**: a step with rise time $t_r$ probes frequencies up to $0.35/t_r$; the plateau it displays is the mid-band $Z_0$. The initial RC-regime effect appears as a slowly rising trace after a long line (the "DC" level shifts as resistive loss accumulates: each point along the line adds $R'\,dx/(2Z_0)$ of reflection, so the displayed impedance creeps upward by about $R'x$ at the time corresponding to position $x$, reaching roughly $Z_0+R'\ell$ at the far end).
- **VNA**: S-parameters vs frequency show attenuation growing, and $S_{11}$ for a matched line staying low across the band.
- **Impedance coupons** verify the plateau value, not the low-frequency one.

## Worked example: low-frequency impedance of a long line

A 0.3 m trace with $R'=2.7\ \Omega$/m has total series resistance 0.8 Ω. At 10 kHz the model gives $|Z_0|=\sqrt{R'/(\omega C')}=\sqrt{2.7/(6.28\times10^4\times1.18\times10^{-10})}=\sqrt{3.64\times10^{5}}=603\ \Omega$. The wave picture isn't relevant here: the 0.3 m line is $\ll\lambda$ (30 km) so it behaves like a lumped RC with 0.8 Ω and 35 pF, and the 603 Ω number merely tells you the line is a poor wave guide below 1 MHz. Compare with 100 MHz: $R'(f)=7.7\ \Omega$/m, $\omega L'=185$ Ω/m, $|Z_0|=50.0\ \Omega$.

## Why designers can ignore all this

The interesting band for edges is $f_{knee}=0.35/t_r$: 350 MHz for 1 ns and 3.5 GHz for 100 ps, so inside the flat region. What does vary is attenuation, which is why channel design focuses on insertion loss (equalisation) rather than on impedance drift. At 10 GHz the dielectric and conductor loss of a 5-inch FR-4 trace exceeds 5 dB.

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Copper roughness** raises $R'$ by up to a factor ~2 at high frequency and adds apparent inductance; its effect on $Z_0$ is small but on loss large.
- **Plane resistance and proximity effect** matter: return current in the plane is confined under the trace, adding to $R'$.
- **Temperature**: $\tan\delta$ and $\varepsilon_r$ drift, moving loss and slightly the impedance.

## Worked example: how trace width moves the corner and the loss

The corner frequency and the high-frequency loss both depend on $R'$, so the trace width, not only the stackup, sets them. Keep the 50 Ω target and the same $L'=295$ nH/m and $C'=118$ pF/m by scaling the dielectric height with the width (a narrower trace over a thinner dielectric), and use the same model $R'(f)=R'_0\sqrt{1+f/14\ \text{MHz}}$, where 14 MHz is where the skin depth equals half the 35 µm thickness, $f=\rho/(\pi\mu_0\delta^2)$ with $\delta=17.5\ \mu$m. The DC allowance is $R_0'=2\rho/(wt)$ (the factor 2 is the page's rough allowance for the return path):

| Width $w$ | $R'_0$ | Corner $R'_0/2\pi L'$ | $R'$ at 1 GHz | $\alpha_c=R'/2Z_0$ at 1 GHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.36 mm | 2.7 Ω/m | 1.5 MHz | 23 Ω/m | 0.05 dB/in |
| 0.15 mm | 6.6 Ω/m | 3.5 MHz | 56 Ω/m | 0.12 dB/in |
| 0.10 mm | 9.8 Ω/m | 5.3 MHz | 84 Ω/m | 0.18 dB/in |

Narrowing the line from 0.36 to 0.10 mm moves the RC-to-LC corner from 1.5 to 5.3 MHz and nearly quadruples conductor loss at 1 GHz. Both effects come from $R'\propto1/w$. Practical consequence: the tight 0.1 mm routes needed for BGA breakout and fine-pitch memory buses lose more per inch than the 0.36 mm main run, so use them only where required and keep them short. The impedance at frequencies above the corner is the same 50 Ω, which is why the impedance plot looks the same for all three while their loss differs by 4×.

## Worked example: how much timing does dispersion cost?

The impedance drifts only a few percent across the band, but the delay drifts too, and that matters for fast data. Suppose, as an illustrative assumption within the ranges quoted above, that the effective permittivity of the line rises by 5 % from low frequency to 10 GHz (geometric and material dispersion partly cancel, so this is a net figure that depends on the laminate). Delay scales with $\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}$, so it changes by about 2.5 %. Over a 150 mm trace with 0.885 ns of nominal delay:

$$\Delta t\approx0.025\times885\ \text{ps}=22\ \text{ps}$$

For a 10 Gb/s signal the unit interval is 100 ps, so the high-frequency components of an edge arrive about 0.22 UI after the low-frequency ones. The edge therefore smears, which contributes to inter-symbol interference alongside loss. For a 100 MHz bus (10 ns UI) the same 22 ps is invisible. The takeaway matches the page's conclusion: impedance drift is not the problem; frequency-dependent delay and loss are, and equalization is the tool for them.

## Worked example: measuring $Z_0(f)$ on a bench VNA

You can measure the characteristic impedance across frequency without a TDR by measuring the input impedance of the line open-circuited and short-circuited. For any line section, $Z_{in,oc}\,Z_{in,sc}=Z_0^2$, so

$$Z_0(f)=\sqrt{Z_{oc}(f)\,Z_{sc}(f)}$$

(a complex square root). Measure $S_{11}$ of a test trace once with the far end open and once with it shorted, convert each to impedance with $Z=50(1+S_{11})/(1-S_{11})$, multiply, and take the root. This works at all frequencies, including the low-frequency RC region where TDR is slow, and it reproduces the rising $|Z_0|$ below the corner. The identity holds for any length, but accuracy suffers wherever one of the two readings is extreme (very near a short or an open, which happens at multiples of a quarter wavelength and, for a short line, at low frequency), because the VNA resolves impedances far from 50 Ω poorly. Pick a length (several inches is typical) that keeps the band of interest away from those resonances, or measure two lengths, and de-embed or calibrate out the fixture's connector. At the lowest frequencies the line is electrically short, $Z_{oc}\approx1/(j\omega C'\ell)$ and $Z_{sc}\approx(R'+j\omega L')\ell$, and their product $(R'+j\omega L')/(j\omega C')$ gives the RC-regime $Z_0\approx\sqrt{R'/(j\omega C')}$, magnitude $\sqrt{R'/(\omega C')}$ at 45° below the axis.

## Limiting cases and when the model breaks

- **Very short lines.** If $\ell\ll\lambda$ and the delay is much smaller than the rise time, the line is a lumped $RLC$ network and $Z_0$ is not a meaningful quantity for it. The criterion is the same $t_r/6$ delay rule.
- **Very rough copper.** Above 5 to 10 GHz, roughness can double $R'$ and add apparent inductance, which pushes the corner up and the loss higher than the smooth-copper formulas, and can drift $Z_0$ by a few percent. Laminate suppliers offer low-profile foils for this reason.
- **Thick and thin dielectrics.** Very thin dielectrics (0.05 mm) give narrow traces and high $R'$; very thick ones (1 mm or more) need wide traces and become electrically large, so geometric dispersion appears at lower frequency.
- **Differential and coupled lines.** The even- and odd-mode impedances each drift with frequency, at slightly different rates (the modes have different field distributions), so the differential impedance can drift a little differently from the single-ended one.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **The numbers in a datasheet are one frequency.** A laminate's $\varepsilon_r$ and loss tangent are quoted at a specific frequency and measured with a specific method (for example split-post dielectric resonator at 10 GHz), and values at 1 GHz may differ. If a design depends on 2 or 3 % of impedance or delay, request the laminate's data at your frequency.
- **Moisture and temperature.** FR-4 absorbs water, which raises $\varepsilon_r$ and $\tan\delta$; a board measured fresh from the bake oven and a board stored in a humid environment differ in loss and slightly in delay.
- **Fixture effects.** At gigahertz frequencies a connector launch can dominate the measurement; compare a short and a long trace to subtract it, or use time-gating.
- **The plane's contribution.** The return-path resistance and its roughness change with the stackup (an inner plane with 0.5 oz copper has higher resistance than a 1 oz outer plane), so the same trace can have slightly different $R'$ on different layers.

## Bench verification

Verify the model's three regimes with three measurements: an LCR meter or DMM measures $R'\ell$ at DC (about 0.4 Ω for the trace alone over 0.3 m, from $1.4\ \Omega$/m; the model's 0.8 Ω includes the return-path allowance); the open-short VNA method above reveals the rising impedance below the corner; and a TDR with a fast edge gives the plateau $Z_0$ and the gentle upward creep from accumulating resistance. Finally an insertion-loss measurement of two trace lengths on the same board yields the loss per inch, which, plotted against frequency and fitted with the $a\sqrt f+bf$ form, separates the conductor loss $a$ from the dielectric loss $b$ and gives you the loss tangent of your laminate directly.

::: followups
### Does a 50 Ω trace measure 50 Ω with a multimeter?
No: a multimeter reads DC resistance (a fraction of an ohm for a short trace, or open circuit to the plane). $Z_0$ is a wave quantity defined where $\omega L'\gg R'$.

### Why does the TDR trace of a long lossy line slope upward?
The series resistance accumulates: each short section of resistance $R'dx$ reflects a fraction $R'dx/(2Z_0)$ of the step, so the displayed impedance at the time corresponding to position $x$ is about $Z_0+R'x$, reaching roughly $Z_0+R'\ell$ at the far end (e.g. 50 → 60 Ω for 10 Ω of total trace resistance). It's the DC-resistance signature, not a geometry change.

### How much does $Z_0$ really change at 10 GHz?
For well-behaved FR-4 microstrip, a few percent (1–3 Ω) from dispersion and loss, much smaller than the ±10% fabrication tolerance. Insertion loss, not impedance, is the issue at 10 GHz.

### What if the trace is very lossy (e.g. a thin trace on a poor laminate)?
$R'/\omega L'$ or $\tan\delta$ become large and $Z_0$ acquires a significant imaginary part; reflections off an ordinary 50 Ω termination increase slightly, and pulses spread (dispersion). Use a lower-loss laminate, wider trace or equalisation.

### Does skin effect make impedance frequency-dependent in coax too?
Same mechanism: $R'\propto\sqrt f$ and internal inductance falls, so $Z_0$ converges to $\sqrt{L'/C'}$ from above at high frequency. In coax the effect is tiny, since $\omega L'\gg R'$ almost everywhere.
:::

::: interpretations
The question is about the **characteristic impedance** of a trace vs frequency. A different reading is the **input impedance** of a finite stub or the **impedance of a trace as a lumped inductor** at low frequency (e.g. power traces), which is $R+j\omega L$; for that see the decoupling pages. Insertion loss vs frequency is [[q0724]].
