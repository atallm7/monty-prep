::: basics
### In plain words
Ohm's law says 12 V across 1 Ω gives 12 A. That number is real, but it is only where the current ends up, not where it starts. A coil also has inductance, so the current ramps up gradually. And if the coil belongs to a spinning motor, the motor's own back-EMF keeps the current well below 12 A.

### Picture it
Think of water in a long, heavy pipe when you open the tap. The water has to get moving first, so the flow builds up gradually. The pipe's friction sets the final flow, but the water's heaviness sets how fast it gets there. In a coil, resistance sets the final 12 A and inductance sets how fast the current climbs. The analogy breaks for a motor: a spinning motor lowers the limit itself, as if a pump at the far end pushed back.

### Words you need
- **Ohm's law**: $I = V/R$. The current through a resistor is voltage divided by resistance.
- **Inductance (L)**: how strongly a coil resists changes in its current, measured in henries (1 mH is a thousandth of a henry).
- **Time constant ($\tau = L/R$)**: how long the current takes to rise about 63% of the way to its final value.
- **Back-EMF**: the voltage a spinning motor makes against the supply. See [[q0341]].
- **Stall**: shaft held still, so there is no back-EMF.

### The simplest example
Put 12 V across a 1 Ω coil with 5 mH of inductance. The time constant is 5 mH / 1 Ω = 5 ms. After 5 ms the current is about 63% of 12 A, roughly 7.6 A. If this coil is a motor that spins up and builds 11.5 V of back-EMF, the current falls to (12 − 11.5) / 1 = 0.5 A.

That is the whole idea: 12 A is a limit, reached over time, and only for a solenoid or a stalled motor. The rest of this page covers the traps: AC, hot wire, wiring resistance, PWM and saturation.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can resist the Ohm's-law reflex ($12\ \text{V}/1\ \Omega = 12$ A) and say **when it is true and when it is not**. The question is a trap with three parts:

1. **Not instantly**: an inductor's current rises as $\frac{V}{R}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$; 12 A is only the *limit*.
2. **For a solenoid (DC), yes eventually**, once the transient dies out and assuming the coil's resistance is really 1 Ω and it survives 144 W.
3. **For a motor, only at stall.** A spinning motor generates back-EMF and draws far less; and in AC, hot coils, wiring resistance, PWM, and saturation all change the answer.
:::

::: why
This is the classic sanity check: do you know that "coil resistance" is not "load impedance"? Getting it wrong leads to undersized supplies (when you forget stall) or oversized ones (when you assume 12 A always), and to burnt coils when you leave a 1 Ω solenoid on at 12 V. It tests Ohm's law, RL transients, back-EMF, thermal sense and the idea of an idealized "infinite-current supply" in one question. The phrase "with infinite current output" tells you to treat the supply as an ideal voltage source with no internal resistance.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ohm's law and power**: steady DC current is $V/R_{total}$; heat is $V^2/R$. See [[q0001]].
- **RL step response**: $i(t) = \frac{V}{R}(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, $\tau = L/R$. See [[q0010]].
- **Back-EMF**: a spinning motor's current is $(V - K_e\omega)/R$. See [[q0341]].
- **Temperature coefficient of copper**: $+0.39\%$/K.
- **AC impedance**: $|Z| = \sqrt{R^2 + (\omega L)^2}$.
:::

::: answer
"Not immediately, and for a motor usually not at all. The coil resistance is 1 ohm, so 12 amps is the limit the current approaches, V over R, but a coil is an inductor, so the current starts at zero and rises with time constant L over R. With a few millihenries that's a few milliseconds, so I'd see roughly 63 percent of 12 amps after one time constant and over 99 percent after five.

When would I see 12 amps? For a solenoid on DC, once the transient is over, in steady state, because the inductor then drops no voltage and only the 1 ohm limits the current. That's 144 watts in the coil, so it won't survive long. For a motor, only when it's stalled, because as it spins it generates a back-EMF and the current is V minus K_e omega over R. At speed it might be half an amp.

When wouldn't I? At the instant of turn-on, since the inductor holds the current at zero. With a spinning motor, because of back-EMF. On AC, since the inductive reactance adds to the impedance. If the supply, wires, switch or MOSFET add resistance, the current is less than 12 amps. If the coil heats up, copper resistance rises, 0.4 percent per kelvin, so 12 amps becomes about 9. And with PWM, the average is duty times that."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** Ideal 12 V source, coil $R = 1\ \Omega$, inductance $L$ (say 5 mH, $\tau = 5$ ms), plus for a motor $E = K_e\omega$.

**DC solenoid / locked rotor.**

$$i(t) = \frac{V}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R} = 5\ \text{ms}$$

- $t = \tau$: $7.58$ A (63.2%); $t = 2.3\tau = 11.5$ ms: 10.8 A (90%); $t = 5\tau = 25$ ms: 11.92 A (99.3%).
- Steady state: $I = V/R = 12$ A, $P = V^2/R = 144$ W.

**Motor running.** $I = (12 - K_e\omega)/R$. With $K_e = 0.02$, at 350 rad/s: 5 A; at the no-load speed: about 0.5 A. 12 A only at $\omega = 0$.

**Resistance in the path.** Wire, connector, switch and MOSFET $R_{DS(on)}$ add in series: with 20 mΩ extra, $I = 12/1.02 = 11.76$ A. A real supply's output resistance also adds.

**Temperature.** Copper: $R(T) = R_0[1 + 0.00393(T - T_0)]$. At 100 °C (75 K above 25 °C): $R = 1.29\ \Omega$, $I = 9.27$ A, $P = 111$ W.

**AC.** At 12 V RMS, 60 Hz, $L = 5$ mH: $X_L = 1.885\ \Omega$, $|Z| = 2.13\ \Omega$, $I_{rms} = 5.6$ A, lagging by $62°$.

**PWM.** Average voltage $DV$: with a smoothing inductor the average current is $DV/R$ (6 A at $D = 0.5$).

**Saturation.** If the core saturates, $L$ drops and the current rises quickly (steeper $di/dt$), but the DC steady state is still $V/R$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The value of $L$ is not given in the question; 5 mH is assumed for illustration. The time to reach 12 A scales directly with $L/R$, so a larger-inductance solenoid takes proportionally longer (tens of ms to hundreds of ms for big coils).
:::

::: short
"Not instantly, and for a motor usually not at all. Twelve amps is V over R, the limit. The coil is an inductor, so the current rises as 12 times one minus e to the minus t over tau, with tau equal to L over R. A DC solenoid reaches 12 amps in steady state after about five time constants, and that's 144 watts, so it will burn out. A motor draws 12 amps only at stall: spinning, back-EMF cuts it to maybe half an amp. In AC, with a hot coil, or with extra wiring resistance, it would be lower."
:::

::: long
"The tempting answer is yes: 12 volts over 1 ohm is 12 amps. But the 1 ohm is only the DC resistance of the winding. A motor or solenoid is also an inductor, and a motor is also a generator.

Start with the inductor. The current can't jump, so at the moment I connect the supply it's zero, and then rises as V over R times one minus e to the minus t over tau, with tau equal to L over R. If the coil is 5 millihenries, tau is 5 milliseconds: 63 percent of 12 amps, 7.6 amps, after 5 ms, about 90 percent after 11.5, and effectively 12 amps after about 25 ms. So 12 amps is where it ends up, not where it starts.

When would I actually see 12 amps? For a solenoid on a DC supply, in steady state with the plunger seated. At that point the inductor drops no voltage and the current is just V over R. That's 144 watts in the coil, so it will cook unless it's pulsed, so a real 12 volt solenoid wouldn't have a 1 ohm coil unless it's a short-duty one. For a motor, only at stall, or very briefly at startup, because once it turns, the back-EMF subtracts from the 12 volts and the current is V minus K_e omega over R.

When wouldn't I? At the first instant. When the motor is spinning. On an AC supply where the reactance adds, 5.6 amps in my example. With any extra resistance: wires, a MOSFET, the supply itself. When the coil is hot, since copper goes up 0.4 percent per degree, so at 100 degrees it's about 9.3 amps. And with PWM, where the average is the duty cycle times 12."
:::

::: terms
- **Time constant $\tau = L/R$**: time for the RL current to reach 63.2% of its final value.
- **Steady-state current**: the DC limit $V/R$ after the transient.
- **Ideal voltage source**: source with zero output impedance, able to supply any current.
- **Locked-rotor (stall) current**: motor current with the shaft held, $V/R$.
- **Back-EMF**: speed-proportional voltage reducing motor current.
- **Temperature coefficient of resistance**: copper rises by about 0.39% per kelvin.
- **Inductive reactance**: $X_L = \omega L$, adds to impedance in AC.
- **Duty cycle $D$**: fraction of time the PWM switch is on.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "no, because of inductance and back-EMF".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Computes the **RL transient** with numbers (63% at one $\tau$, 99% at $5\tau$) and says the ideal supply sets only the *final* value.
- Separates the **solenoid** (reaches 12 A at DC steady state) from the **motor** (12 A only locked rotor) clearly.
- Notes **144 W** and that the coil would burn, so real 12 V coils don't have 1 Ω unless pulsed.
- Lists the **non-ideal effects** quantitatively: wiring/FET resistance, copper heating (9.3 A at 100 °C), AC impedance (5.6 A), PWM.
- Mentions **saturation** as the one effect that makes the transient faster than $L/R$ predicts but does not change the steady state.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Inductance limits the current to less than $V/R$ forever."** It only delays; steady-state DC current is $V/R$.
- **"A motor draws 12 A as long as it is on."** Only while stalled.
- **Forgetting the power.** 12 A at 12 V is 144 W; the coil dissipates it as heat and will fail if not rated for it.
- **Ignoring other resistances.** Wiring, connectors, FET, supply, and brush resistance reduce the current.
- **Treating $L$ as irrelevant.** It sets how long the current takes; relevant for PWM, fuses and protection timing.
- **Using the cold resistance for the hot coil.** Hot copper has up to 30% higher $R$.
:::

## Part 2: solenoid on DC: yes, in the end

Once the transient is over, $di/dt = 0$, so the inductor is a wire and $I = V/R = 12$ A, with $P = 144$ W continuously. A 24 V, 48 Ω solenoid draws 0.5 A and dissipates 12 W; a "12 V, 1 Ω" solenoid would be a very hot, high-current coil, found only in short-duty actuators (starter solenoids, for example, which are pulsed for a second or two). If left on, the winding heats at tens of kelvin per second ([[q0338]]) until the insulation fails, and the coil may open-circuit before ever reaching steady state.

## Part 4: the cases where you would *not* see 12 A

1. **t = 0**: inductor continuity, $i(0^+) = 0$.
2. **Spinning motor**: $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R < V/R$.
3. **AC supply**: at 12 V RMS, 60 Hz, $L = 5$ mH: $|Z| = \sqrt{1 + 1.885^2} = 2.13\ \Omega$, so $I_{rms} = 5.6$ A (lagging 62°).
4. **Extra resistance**: 20 mΩ of wiring and MOSFET gives 11.76 A; a real battery's internal resistance and sag reduce it further.
5. **Hot coil**: at +75 K, $R = 1.29\ \Omega$ and $I = 9.27$ A (power falls to 111 W).
6. **PWM**: with duty $D$ and smoothing $L$, $I_{avg} = DV/R$.
7. **Current limiting**: a driver with a current limit clamps the current to its set point.

## Part 5: what an "infinite current" supply means here

It means an ideal voltage source with zero output resistance, so the supply never limits the current: the coil and the wiring do. In practice, supplies have internal resistance (a battery of 50 mΩ drops 0.6 V at 12 A), current limits, and sag under load. This is the reason the exercise is a good interview question: it separates the device's behaviour from the source's.

## A short checklist for any "what current will it draw?" question

1. Is it DC or AC? PWM average?
2. Steady state or transient? ($\tau = L/R$)
3. Motor (back-EMF) or solenoid (no speed term)?
4. Hot or cold? (copper $+0.39\%$/K)
5. What else is in series? (wiring, switch, source)
6. What is the power and can the device handle it? ($V^2/R$)

## Worked example: sizing a driver for this coil

Suppose you must switch this coil on and off from a 12 V rail, and an interviewer asks what you would specify. Run the numbers in the order the circuit sees them.

**Turn-on.** The initial slope is $V/L = 12/0.005 = 2{,}400$ A/s. After 1 ms the current is $12(1 - e^{-0.2}) = 2.18$ A, so the transient is slow enough that a MOSFET or a relay contact sees no sharp inrush; the real stress arrives later in the ramp as the current climbs toward 12 A.

**Steady state.** The MOSFET carries 12 A continuously. With $R_{DS(on)} = 10$ mΩ the channel dissipates $12^2\times0.01 = 1.44$ W, which a small package can handle with copper area. The coil itself dissipates 144 W, which is the real problem: no ordinary 12 V coil is designed for that, so either the coil is a short-duty actuator or the design must reduce the average current.

**Peak and hold.** Drive full voltage for the pull-in time, then chop at 25% duty. The smoothed current is $0.25\times12/1 = 3$ A and the coil dissipates $3^2\times1 = 9$ W instead of 144 W, sixteen times less, because power goes as the square of current. The 20 kHz ripple is only $\Delta I = V D(1-D)/(Lf) = 12\times0.25\times0.75/(0.005\times20{,}000) = 22.5$ mA, so the average-current model is valid. Whether 3 A holds the load is a mechanical question about the plunger or the rotor, and the hold level needs margin for vibration and a hot coil.

**Turn-off.** The stored energy is $\tfrac12LI^2 = \tfrac12\times0.005\times144 = 0.36$ J if you interrupt 12 A, which a TVS or an unclamped MOSFET cannot absorb repeatedly; a freewheel diode lets the current decay with $\tau = L/R = 5$ ms instead, and a diode with a Zener in series makes the decay faster at the cost of a higher switch-node voltage ([[q0340]]).

## What a current-limited bench supply shows you

Connect a bench supply set to 12 V with an 8 A current limit. For the first few milliseconds the supply is in constant-voltage mode and the current ramps as before. When the current reaches 8 A the supply crosses into constant-current mode and the output voltage drops to whatever the load needs: $8\ \text{A}\times1\ \Omega = 8$ V. The coil now sees 8 V and 64 W, and the supply is dissipating or turning down 4 V across itself. This is the quickest way to find a coil's stall behaviour safely, because the coil never sees 144 W. It also explains confusing bench readings: "my 12 V supply shows 8 V at the terminals" is a limit, not a fault.

## Limiting cases

- **$L \to 0$.** The time constant vanishes and the current jumps to $V/R$. This is how a resistive heater behaves, and why the inductance matters only for switching.
- **$L \to \infty$ (a very large coil).** The current still reaches $V/R$ eventually, but the time scale may be longer than your pulse, so the *peak* current in a short pulse can be far less than 12 A.
- **$R \to 0$ (a shorted turn or a superconducting coil).** The current ramps linearly at $V/L$ without bound and the limit is the supply or the saturation of the core.
- **Saturation.** With an iron core, the ramp has a knee: the slope $di/dt = (V - iR)/L$ steepens as $L$ falls, producing a sudden current rise that nuisance-trips protective circuits.

## Verifying it on the bench

Measure $R$ with a four-wire (Kelvin) ohmmeter, not a two-wire one, because a 1 Ω coil is comparable to lead resistance. Then step the coil from a stiff supply through a MOSFET, with a 10 mΩ shunt or a current probe in series, and capture the current. Read off the 63% point to get $\tau$, then compute $L = \tau R$ and compare with an LCR meter. If the scope trace flattens below $V/R$, subtract: the difference is the series resistance in the loop (switch, wires, source). Repeat after the coil has warmed to see the final current shrink as copper resistance climbs.

::: followups
### How long until the current reaches 11.9 A?
About five time constants: $t = -\tau\ln(1 - 11.9/12) = 4.8\tau$. For $\tau = 5$ ms that's 24 ms. For a 100 mH solenoid coil, $\tau = 100$ ms, so about 0.5 s.

### What if the supply really has "infinite current" but the wire is 26 AWG, 2 m long?
The wire adds about 0.27 Ω (26 AWG is roughly 0.13 Ω/m), so $I = 12/1.27 = 9.4$ A and the wire dissipates $9.4^2\times0.27 = 24$ W, enough to heat or melt the insulation. The wire becomes the weak link. Choose the gauge for the stall/steady current.

### The solenoid is pulsed 10 ms on, 90 ms off. Is 12 A okay?
With $\tau = 5$ ms the current only reaches $12(1 - e^{-2}) = 10.4$ A (86%) by the end of the pulse, so the energy per pulse is about 0.55 J (numerical integral of $i^2R$), or about 5.5 W average at 10% duty, versus 14.4 W if you naively assumed a flat 12 A. Compare that with the coil's thermal rating; pulsed duty is often fine, but check the peak winding temperature as well.

### Would a bigger inductance reduce the final current?
No. Inductance only slows the rise; the steady DC current depends on $V$ and $R$. A bigger $L$ also stores more energy, $\tfrac12LI^2$, making turn-off more demanding ([[q0340]]).

### Scenario: you measure only 8 A in steady state on the bench with a 12 V supply and a 1 Ω coil. What do you check?
Measure the voltage at the coil terminals (the supply may be current limiting or sagging), measure the coil's hot resistance, and check wiring/contact resistance. Possible causes: the supply's CC limit at 8 A, a hot coil (R about 1.5 Ω), or 0.5 Ω in the wires and connectors. Use a 4-wire measurement to find the voltage drops.

### What changes if the same 1 Ω winding is wound on an iron core that saturates at 3 A?
When the current passes the saturation level $L$ collapses, so $di/dt = (V - iR)/L$ jumps and the current climbs much faster toward 12 A (see [[q0345]]). The final value is still $V/R$, but the rise has a knee, and the peak transient stress on the driver is higher.
:::
