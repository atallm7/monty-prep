::: basics
### In plain words
A motor's current is high whenever its speed is low compared to the supply voltage. That is the one rule behind every peak. Startup, stall, a jammed shaft and a heavy load all drag the speed down. Some less obvious cases, like reversing direction, can be even worse. Solenoids peak in simpler ways.

### Picture it
Picture pushing a shopping cart. A cart that is already rolling away from you needs only a light push. A cart that is standing still needs a big shove. A cart rolling toward you that you want to turn around needs the biggest effort of all, because you must stop it and push it back. The motor's current is that effort: low speed means a lot, and reversed speed means even more. The analogy breaks down because here the effort is electrical current, which heats the winding.

### Words you need
- **Back-EMF**: the voltage a spinning motor makes against the supply. See [[q0341]].
- **Stall current**: the current with the shaft held still, $V/R$.
- **Plugging**: reversing the supply polarity while the motor is spinning.
- **Shoot-through**: both transistors in one bridge leg on together, shorting the supply.
- **Current limit**: a driver feature that caps the peak current.

### The simplest example
A 12 V motor has a 1 Ω winding and reaches 11.5 V of back-EMF at full speed. At stall, the current is 12 V / 1 Ω = 12 A. Now reverse the supply at full speed. The back-EMF no longer opposes the supply but adds to it, so the voltage across the winding is 12 + 11.5 = 23.5 V, and the current is **23.5 A**, almost double stall.

That is the whole idea: low speed or reversed speed means big current. The rest of this page lists the other peaks, including braking, shoot-through and solenoid pull-in, and how to protect against each.
:::

This page lists the cases, ranks them, and says what to do about each. It is deliberately the "scenario catalogue" version of the same question as [[q0336]], which derives the circuit physics for the basic cases.

::: answer
"The rule for a motor is that current is highest when the back-EMF is lowest relative to the applied voltage, because the current is V minus K_e omega over R. So the biggest currents come from a few situations. First, startup and stall: speed zero, so about V over R, 12 amps for 12 volts and 1 ohm. Second, any heavy load or jam that holds the speed down. Third, and bigger than people expect, direction reversal, or plugging: if you flip the supply polarity while it's spinning at full speed, the back-EMF now adds to the supply instead of opposing it. At 575 rad/s that's 12 plus 11.5 volts across 1 ohm, so 23.5 amps, almost twice the stall current. Also braking by shorting the winding gives K_e omega over R, around 11.5 amps at full speed, and in a bridge, shoot-through can be hundreds of amps for microseconds.

For solenoids, the max is the steady state V over R once the coil's current has settled; some drivers deliberately overdrive during pull-in with a higher voltage for a stronger, faster pull. AC solenoids draw several times their holding current when the plunger is open.

And there are modifiers: a cold coil has lower resistance so more current, low supply or high source resistance lowers it but makes the rail sag, and PWM ripple adds a peak above the average. So I size the driver and supply for the worst case, plugging or shoot-through, and I use current limits, soft start and dead-time to keep it under control."
:::

::: technical
**Motor current law:** $I = \dfrac{V_{app} - K_e\omega}{R_{total}}$ where $V_{app}$ includes polarity.

| Situation | $V_{app}$ | $\omega$ | Current (12 V, 1 Ω, $K=0.02$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Free-running | +12 | 575 | 0.5 A |
| Loaded (0.1 N·m) | +12 | 350 | 5 A |
| Startup / stall / jam | +12 | 0 | 12 A (peak about 10.9 A, [[q0336]]) |
| Cold winding (-40 °C, $R = 0.75\ \Omega$) | +12 | 0 | about 16 A |
| **Plugging** (reverse at full speed) | -12 | 575 | $(-12 - 11.5)/1 = -23.5$ A |
| Reverse from loaded speed | -12 | 350 | $-19$ A |
| Dynamic braking (short the winding) | 0 | 575 | $-K_e\omega/R = -11.5$ A |
| Bridge shoot-through | 12 across two FETs | n/a | $12/(R_{ds1} + R_{ds2}) = 600$ A for 10 mΩ + 10 mΩ |

**Notes.**

- **Plugging** gives the highest *normal-operation* current: $I = (V + K_e\omega)/R \approx 2V/R$ at no-load speed. The braking torque $K_tI$ is large too, which is the point of plugging, but it stresses the driver, wiring and gears.
- **Cold copper:** $R(T) = R_0[1 + 0.00393(T - T_0)]$; at -40 °C, $R = 0.745\ R_{25}$, so stall current rises about 34%.
- **Supply and wiring:** reduce the peak ($I = V/(R + R_s)$) but sag the rail.
- **PWM:** current ripple adds to the average: peak $= I_{avg} + \Delta I/2$, with $\Delta I = VD(1-D)/(Lf)$.
- **Solenoid DC:** max $V/R$ at steady state; overdrive pull-in (for example 2x voltage for 20 ms) gives 2x current, 4x force for the pull-in time.
- **Solenoid AC:** inrush with the plunger open is several times the sealed current (design-dependent; check datasheet).
- **Faults:** shorted turns lower $R$ and raise current; a locked rotor in an AC induction motor draws 5 to 8 times rated current (typical range).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 5 to 8 times locked-rotor figure for AC induction motors and several-times AC solenoid inrush are typical ranges, not specifications. Brushed-DC peaks follow directly from $V/R$ in the model.
:::

::: long
"The way I think about it is the motor law: current equals the applied voltage minus the back-EMF, over the resistance. So the peak current happens when the back-EMF is smallest, or even negative, relative to what I'm applying.

Case one, startup and stall. Speed zero, no back-EMF, current is V over R. For 12 volts and 1 ohm, 12 amps. The same applies if the shaft jams or the load is so heavy it holds the speed low, because to make the torque at low speed you need current.

Case two, the one people forget, is reversal. If I flip the polarity while the motor is spinning at full speed, the back-EMF is still 11.5 volts in the old direction, and now the supply is also pushing in the other direction, so the voltage across the resistance is 23.5 volts and the current is 23.5 amps, almost double stall. It brakes hard, but it stresses the driver, the battery and the gearbox. Related: shorting the winding to brake gives K_e omega over R, about 11.5 amps at full speed, which decays as it slows.

Case three, hardware faults. In an H-bridge, if both transistors in a half-bridge turn on together, the only resistance is two milliohm-level Rds(on)s, so you can get hundreds of amps for microseconds. Dead-time prevents it.

For solenoids, the maximum is V over R in steady state, with a dip during the stroke. Some drivers deliberately overdrive during pull-in, double voltage for 20 milliseconds, for 4 times the force, then drop to a lower hold current. AC solenoids have several times their holding current when the plunger is open.

Modifiers: cold coils have lower resistance, so at minus 40 the stall current is about a third higher; PWM ripple adds on top of the average; and source resistance lowers the peak but sags the rail. So I size the supply, driver, wiring and fuse for plugging or the current limit, not the stall figure alone."
:::

::: terms
- **Stall current**: $V/R$ with the shaft held.
- **Plugging**: reversing the supply on a spinning motor to brake or reverse it quickly.
- **Dynamic braking**: shorting or loading the winding so back-EMF drives a braking current.
- **Shoot-through**: both transistors of a half-bridge conducting simultaneously.
- **Dead-time**: deliberate delay between turning one device off and the other on.
- **Overdrive (pull-in boost)**: temporarily higher voltage to a solenoid to close it faster.
- **Locked-rotor current**: current with the shaft held; for induction motors, several times rated.
- **Current ripple $\Delta I$**: peak-to-peak variation in PWM current.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Stopping at stall.** Reversal and braking exceed stall current; shoot-through exceeds both.
- **Using the running current for the fuse and wire.** They must handle the startup and stall duty or be protected by a limit.
- **Assuming a H-bridge driver is protected "because it has diodes".** The body diodes handle freewheeling, not shoot-through or plugging currents beyond their rating.
- **Forgetting that a cold motor draws more.** Winter stall current is higher.
- **Mixing up the three currents.** Peak sizes the driver/supply, RMS the heating, average the battery drain.
- **Treating solenoid overdrive as a fault.** Pull-in boost is a deliberate technique; the driver must limit its duration.
:::

## Catalogue of the peak cases

1. **Startup**: peak $\approx V/R$ after a few ms ([[q0336]]).
2. **Stall/jam**: sustained $V/R$; heating $V^2/R$ ([[q0338]]).
3. **Heavy load, low speed**: $I = \tau_L/K_t$; for 0.2 N·m, 10 A.
4. **Plugging**: about $(V + K_e\omega)/R$, up to roughly $2V/R$.
5. **Dynamic braking**: $K_e\omega/R$, decays with speed.
6. **Cold winding**: $R$ lower, e.g. -40 °C gives $R = 0.745\ \Omega$, $I_{stall} = 16.1$ A.
7. **PWM ripple**: add $\Delta I/2$ to the average.
8. **Shoot-through**: $V/(R_{ds,hi} + R_{ds,lo}) = 12/0.02 = 600$ A for 10 mΩ devices; limited in time by dead-time or desaturation protection.
9. **Supply transients and load dump** (automotive): higher $V$ gives proportionally more stall current.
10. **Shorted turns / winding faults**: lower $R$ and more current.

## The solenoid's peak cases

| Situation | Current | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| Steady on (DC) | $V/R$ | Maximum for a fixed voltage; $V^2/R$ continuous heat |
| Pull-in overdrive (2x V for 20 ms) | $2V/R$ | Force $\propto I^2$, so 4x at the instant; limit duration |
| During plunger travel | dip | Motional back-EMF $i\,dL/dx\,v$ |
| AC, plunger open | several times sealed current | Low impedance; burns out coils that jam open |
| Cold coil | higher | Copper $R$ lower at low temperature |

A jammed AC solenoid with the plunger stuck open keeps drawing the inrush current and burns out. That is a classic failure mode of AC solenoid valves and contactors.

## Worst-case sizing procedure

1. List all the cases that can occur in *your* system (can the motor be reversed on the fly? is there a brake? can the shaft jam?).
2. Compute each with worst-case $V$ (max supply), cold $R$, and tolerance.
3. Choose the **driver peak rating** for the largest *allowed* case, and add **current limiting** so that the larger cases cannot occur (for example limit plugging by ramping or by limiting the current).
4. Check the **supply**: peak and the sag it causes ([[q0343]]).
5. Check **thermal**: RMS current and stall duration; set a thermal cutoff.
6. Check the **fuse** and wire.
7. **Verify on the bench** with a current probe through a start, stall, reversal and brake.

## Example: sizing a robot motor driver

Motor: 12 V, 1 Ω, $K = 0.02$; battery 12 V with 0.1 Ω; driver is an H-bridge with 10 mΩ per device.

- Stall: $12/(1 + 0.1 + 0.02) = 10.7$ A.
- Plugging from full speed: $(12 + 11.5)/1.12 = 21$ A.
- Cold (-20 °C, $R_m = 0.82\ \Omega$): stall $12/0.94 = 12.7$ A.
- Decision: either use a driver rated at about 25 A (and the battery, wiring, fuse to match), or limit the current to about 10 A and ramp reversals; the second is cheaper and gentler on the gears.

## Worked example: what plugging costs in time, heat and energy

The 23.5 A figure is the instantaneous peak. To see what it means for the hardware, follow the whole reversal for the example motor ($J = 10^{-5}$ kg·m², $\tau_m = JR/(K_tK_e) = 25$ ms, friction ignored, inductance ignored because it is much faster). Command $-12$ V when the rotor is turning at $+575$ rad/s. The speed heads for $-V/K_e = -600$ rad/s as a first-order exponential, $\omega(t) = -600 + 1175\,e^{-t/\tau_m}$, so it crosses zero after $\tau_m\ln(1175/600) = 25\ \text{ms}\times0.672 = 16.8$ ms. Over that interval the current magnitude falls from 23.5 A to 12 A (in fact $i(t) = -23.5\,e^{-t/\tau_m}$ A), and from there it keeps shrinking toward zero as the rotor accelerates in the new direction.

The power that matters is the copper loss. At the start it is $23.5^2\times1 = 552$ W, about 3.8 times the 144 W of a stall, though it only lasts milliseconds. Integrating $i^2R$ from reversal to zero speed gives 5.1 J in the winding. That splits into 1.65 J of rotor kinetic energy ($\tfrac12J\omega^2 = \tfrac12\times10^{-5}\times575^2$) plus 3.45 J drawn from the supply. In other words plugging dissipates three times the energy the rotor started with, because the supply keeps pushing while the motor is being stopped. Dynamic braking by shorting the winding dissipates just the 1.65 J of kinetic energy, which is one reason it is the gentler way to stop.

Now compare a current limit at 10 A. The torque becomes $K_tI = 0.2$ N·m, so the deceleration is $0.2/10^{-5} = 20{,}000$ rad/s², and the rotor stops after $575/20{,}000 = 28.8$ ms. If the application can tolerate 12 ms of extra stopping time, the limit buys a driver that is less than half the size. That is the sort of trade an interviewer wants to hear: not "plugging is bad" but "here is what it costs and here is what I give up to avoid it."

## Where the peak catalogue goes wrong

- **Regeneration into the supply.** Full-voltage plugging does not regenerate: the supply delivers energy throughout (the 3.45 J above). Current-limited braking does. With a 10 A limit at 575 rad/s the back-EMF (11.5 V) exceeds the $IR$ drop (10 V), so the bridge only needs about +1.5 V average while the current is negative, and the motor, acting as a generator, pushes energy back through the switches and body diodes into the bus capacitor. The same happens when you decelerate by reducing duty. A battery absorbs it; a supply with a series diode or a big converter cannot, and the rail can rise past the driver's maximum rating ([[q0337]]). The peak voltage is an additional "case" to size for.
- **Mechanical elasticity.** Gearboxes have backlash and shafts twist. The braking torque spike is followed by a rebound that can briefly reverse the sign of the torque, and the real current transient rings at the mechanical resonance instead of decaying smoothly.
- **Friction and load torque.** The model omits them. A heavy friction load shortens the stop time and reduces the energy that has to be dissipated, while a regenerative load such as a falling mass adds energy.
- **Temperature and supply.** Cold copper, a fresh battery above nominal voltage (a 12 V lead-acid battery under charge sits near 14 V) and automotive transients all push the worst-case peak above the nominal 23.5 A. Evaluate the table at the extremes, not at nominal.
- **Sense limitations.** A shunt amplifier with a 10 A range will clip at 23.5 A and the controller will think the current is lower than it is; check the full-scale of the measurement chain against the peak you computed.

## Verifying peaks on the bench

Start with the supply set to a low voltage and the current limit enabled, then raise both in steps while capturing current with a probe and the bus voltage with a second channel. Trigger on the first edge of the direction signal. The captured peak should fall within a few percent of $(V + K_e\omega)/R_{total}$; a larger value means lower resistance than modeled (cold winding, short between turns), and a smaller value means the current limit or inductance is acting. Measure the bus voltage during the stop to see how much regenerated energy the supply absorbs. Only after these steps run the full-voltage case, with a fuse in series, and never reverse a loaded system on a bench supply that cannot sink current without watching its output.

::: followups
### Why is plugging worse than stall?
At stall the back-EMF is zero, so the voltage across the resistance is $V$. In plugging the back-EMF still equals $K_e\omega$ but now has the same sign as the new supply, so the resistance sees $V + K_e\omega \approx 2V$ at full speed. The current is nearly double and decreases as the motor slows.

### How do you prevent damaging currents during reversal?
Ramp the duty to zero, let the motor slow (or brake in a controlled way with a current limit), then reverse; or limit current in the driver (cycle-by-cycle) so the torque during plugging is capped. Direction changes in a closed-loop controller should pass through a current-limited braking phase.

### Which is bigger: stall current or dynamic-braking current?
Stall ($V/R$ = 12 A) is bigger than dynamic braking ($K_e\omega/R = 11.5$ A) at full speed in this example; for dynamic braking the maximum is $K_e\omega_{max}/R \approx V/R$ because $K_e\omega_{max} \approx V$. They are nearly equal for a motor spinning near its no-load speed. Plugging, however, is nearly double either.

### How would you measure these peaks?
A current probe or a low-value shunt and a scope triggered on the start, reversal and stall events; ensure the bandwidth and the shunt's inductance are adequate, and that the scope's trigger catches single events. For shoot-through, look at the bridge supply with a fast probe, and use a current-limited supply when first testing a new bridge.

### Scenario: field returns show H-bridge MOSFETs failing only in the winter. What do you suspect?
Cold: lower copper resistance gives higher stall and plugging currents (about a third more at -40 °C), more friction means more stalls, and batteries sag more. The current limit might also be miscalibrated at low temperature. Check peak currents against the MOSFET SOA at low temperature, add or lower the current limit, and ramp reversals.

### Does a larger inductance reduce these peaks?
It slows the rise ($di/dt = V/L$) but not the final peak in the long run, except for very short events like shoot-through, where the loop inductance limits the current rise over microseconds. Motor inductance of 1 mH gives only about 12 A/ms: the current limit has to act within a millisecond to cap the peak.
:::
