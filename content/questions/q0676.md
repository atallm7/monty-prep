::: testing
Whether you can state and **use** the rise-time/bandwidth rule: $f \approx 0.35/t_r$ for the bandwidth needed to pass an edge (with $t_r$ the 10-90% rise time), and the closely related knee frequency $\approx 0.5/t_r$ used for spectral content. The interviewer also wants to see that you know where $0.35$ comes from (a single-pole low-pass), when it is an approximation, and how to apply it to sizing a scope, a cable, an amplifier or deciding whether a trace is electrically long. Quoting the rule without being able to derive or use it is a weak answer.
:::

::: why
Every bandwidth decision in digital and mixed-signal hardware uses this rule: oscilloscope and probe selection, op-amp and ADC front-end bandwidth, filter design on a clock line, SI simulation range, EMC estimates, and the transmission-line threshold. It converts a time-domain number you can measure (rise time) into a frequency-domain number that sets cost and layout rules. It is probably the most reused single formula in signal integrity.
:::

::: concepts
- **10-90% rise time** $t_r$: time between 10% and 90% of the final value.
- **Single-pole step response**: $v(t) = V(1-e^{-t/RC})$; $t_r = RC\ln 9 = 2.2RC$ ([[q0010]]).
- **-3 dB bandwidth** of a single pole: $f_{3dB} = 1/(2\pi RC)$.
- **Spectral knee** of a trapezoid: where the envelope changes from $-20$ to $-40$ dB/decade ([[q0673]]).
- **Root-sum-square rise times**: cascaded stages combine as $t_{total}=\sqrt{\sum t_i^2}$.
- **Highest significant frequency** of a real square wave ([[q0675]]).
:::

::: answer
"The rule of thumb I use is bandwidth equals 0.35 divided by the 10 to 90 percent rise time. If a signal has a 1 ns rise time, I need about 350 MHz of bandwidth to pass it without slowing the edge noticeably.

It comes from a single-pole RC response. The step response has a 10 to 90 percent rise time of 2.2 RC, because it's the natural log of nine times RC, and the 3 dB bandwidth is one over 2 pi RC. Divide one by the other and you get about 0.35.

For the spectrum itself, a related number is the knee frequency, where the spectral envelope turns from 20 dB per decade to 40 dB per decade. Johnson uses about 0.5 over the rise time for that, and Ott's version, one over pi times a linear ramp time, comes out somewhat lower. They're all in the same ballpark and I'd treat them as 'the highest frequency that matters'.

I use it for sizing: for a 1 ns edge I'd want a scope with several times 350 MHz, so around 1.5 GHz, so the scope doesn't slow the edge by more than a few percent. And it tells me the clock frequency isn't what matters: a 10 MHz clock with a 1 ns edge still has content out to about 350 MHz."
:::

::: technical
**The rule.**
$$BW \approx \frac{0.35}{t_{r,10\text{-}90}}$$

**Derivation (single pole).** Step response $v(t)=V(1-e^{-t/\tau})$ with $\tau=RC$. Time to 10%: $t_{10}=-\tau\ln0.9$. Time to 90%: $t_{90}=-\tau\ln0.1$. So
$$t_r=\tau\ln 9 = 2.197\,\tau$$
and $f_{3dB} = 1/(2\pi\tau)$. Therefore
$$f_{3dB}\,t_r = \frac{\ln 9}{2\pi} = 0.3497 \approx 0.35$$
For other responses the constant differs slightly: a Gaussian response gives about 0.34 and a 2nd-order Butterworth about 0.34; higher-order Butterworth filters with sharper roll-off and overshoot give larger values (0.39 for 4th order, 0.43 for 6th, from a numerical step-response check). Many scope specifications quote 0.35 for $\geq 1$ GHz instruments and 0.4 for sharper-roll-off designs. Check the datasheet.

**Knee frequency for spectral content.** A trapezoid's envelope breaks at $1/(\pi t_{r,lin})$ with $t_{r,lin}$ the 0-100% ramp time. Converting with $t_{10\text{-}90}=0.8t_{lin}$ gives $0.8/(\pi t_{10\text{-}90}) = 0.255/t_{10\text{-}90}$. Johnson & Graham use the slightly larger $f_{knee}\approx 0.5/t_{r}$ to capture where the energy has fallen enough to ignore. So the useful range is $0.25/t_r$ (strict break point) to $0.5/t_r$ (conservative "highest frequency that matters").

**Combining rise times** (independent stages): $t_{total}=\sqrt{t_1^2+t_2^2+\dots}$. Scope measurement: $t_{meas}=\sqrt{t_{sig}^2+t_{scope}^2+t_{probe}^2}$ with $t_{scope}=0.35/BW_{scope}$.

**Sizing a scope for edge accuracy.** Error $\epsilon$ in measured rise time requires $t_{scope}/t_{sig}\le\sqrt{(1+\epsilon)^2-1}$. For $\epsilon=3\%$ that ratio is 0.247, so $BW_{scope}\ge 1.42/t_{sig}$, i.e. about $4\times$ the signal's $0.35/t_r$. For 2% error use about $5\times$.

**Examples.** $t_r=1$ ns: 350 MHz ($0.35/t_r$), 500 MHz ($0.5/t_r$). $t_r=100$ ps: 3.5 GHz and 5 GHz. $t_r=5$ ns (slow logic or heavily loaded output): 70 MHz and 100 MHz.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Constants differ between sources (0.35, 0.4, 0.5; 10-90 vs 20-80 vs 0-100 definitions). For 20-80% rise time of a single pole, the product is about 0.22/$t_{r,20\text{-}80}$. Always state which definition you use.
:::

::: long
"The rule of thumb is bandwidth equals 0.35 divided by the 10 to 90 percent rise time. So a 1 ns edge needs roughly 350 MHz, and a 100 ps edge needs roughly 3.5 GHz.

It's easy to derive, which is why I trust it. Take a single-pole RC low-pass. The step response is one minus e to the minus t over RC. The time to reach 10 percent and 90 percent differ by RC times the natural log of nine, which is 2.2 RC. The 3 dB bandwidth is one over 2 pi RC. Multiply the two and you get 0.35. Other filter shapes give 0.34 to 0.4, so it's a good approximation, not an exact law.

There's a related number for spectral content. A trapezoidal clock has a spectrum that falls at 20 dB per decade up to a knee, and 40 dB per decade after that. Johnson takes the knee as about 0.5 over the rise time, a bit conservative, and the strict break point of a linear ramp is nearer 0.25 over the 10 to 90 time. So I treat 0.35 to 0.5 over the rise time as the highest frequency that matters.

How I use it. For the scope: rise times add in quadrature, so a scope whose own rise time equals the signal's makes the edge look 41 percent slower. To keep the error to about three percent I want the scope's bandwidth about four times the signal's 0.35 over tr, so about 1.4 GHz for a 1 ns edge. For layout: the edge also tells me when a trace becomes a transmission line, because the edge's length in the board is rise time divided by delay per unit length.

The most important thing it says is that the clock frequency isn't what sets bandwidth. It's the edge."
:::

::: terms
- **Rise time $t_r$ (10-90%)**: time for a step to move from 10% to 90% of its final value.
- **Bandwidth ($f_{3dB}$)**: frequency where the gain is $1/\sqrt2$ (-3 dB) of the passband.
- **Single-pole response**: first-order low-pass; $t_r=2.2RC$.
- **Knee frequency**: where a trapezoid's spectral envelope steepens; about $0.25/t_r$ to $0.5/t_r$ depending on convention.
- **Root-sum-square (RSS)**: way to combine independent rise times, $\sqrt{t_1^2+t_2^2}$.
- **Electrical length**: physical length compared with the edge's spatial extent $t_r/t_d$.
- **Gaussian response**: bell-shaped step response with no overshoot; constant about 0.34.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "bandwidth is 0.35 over rise time" and stops.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives 0.35 from the RC step response ($\ln 9/2\pi$) in two lines, and states it is exact for one pole and approximate otherwise.
- Distinguishes the *system bandwidth to pass the edge* ($0.35/t_r$) from the *spectral knee of the signal* ($\approx0.5/t_r$ conservative, $1/(\pi t_{lin})$ strict) and says which you are quoting.
- Specifies the rise-time definition (10-90%) and combines rise times in quadrature.
- Applies it: scope selection with the 3% error calculation, trace length threshold, filter on a clock, EMC range.
- Mentions the practical conclusion: the edge, not the clock rate, sets the bandwidth.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using the clock frequency as the bandwidth.** The edge rate sets it; a 1 MHz clock with 1 ns edges needs hundreds of MHz.
- **Mixing rise-time definitions.** 20-80% rise times are about 0.6 of the 10-90% value for a single pole; 0-100% linear is 1.25 times the 10-90% value. The 0.35 constant is for 10-90%.
- **Adding rise times linearly.** They combine in quadrature (for independent, roughly Gaussian responses).
- **Choosing a scope with BW equal to $0.35/t_r$.** That already distorts the edge by about 40%.
- **Treating 0.35 as exact.** It is exact for one pole; a sharper filter gives 0.38 to 0.4 or more.
- **Quoting the rule for the fundamental.** It is a rule for the edge. For waveform fidelity you may also need the 5th harmonic of the fundamental, whichever is larger ([[q0675]]).
:::

::: deep
## Deriving 0.35

A single-pole low-pass has the step response
$$v(t) = V\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right).$$
Setting $v = 0.1V$ and $0.9V$:
$$t_{10}=-\tau\ln 0.9 = 0.105\tau,\qquad t_{90}=-\tau\ln 0.1 = 2.303\tau.$$
$$t_r = t_{90}-t_{10}=2.197\tau = \tau\ln 9.$$
The frequency response $H(f)=1/(1+jf/f_c)$ has its -3 dB point at $f_c = 1/(2\pi\tau)$. So
$$f_c\,t_r=\frac{2.197}{2\pi}=0.3497.$$
This is exact for a single pole and the origin of the constant in every scope datasheet.

## Why 0.35 is "close enough" for other responses

Other low-pass responses have the same qualitative property: bandwidth times rise time is roughly constant, because both measure the same thing (how fast the system can change). Typical values:

| Response | $BW\cdot t_{r,10\text{-}90}$ |
|---|---|
| Single pole (RC) | 0.35 |
| Gaussian | 0.34 |
| 2nd-order Butterworth | 0.34 |
| 3rd-order Butterworth | 0.36 |
| 4th-order Butterworth | 0.39 |
| 6th-order Butterworth (sharper roll-off, overshoot) | 0.43 |

Because the constant stays within about $\pm 25\%$ across these, the rule of thumb is reliable to within that factor. Sharper filters need a few percent to 20% more bandwidth than $0.35/t_r$ suggests, never less.

## From bandwidth to spectrum: the knee

Bandwidth describes a *system*. The knee describes a *signal*. For a trapezoid with 0-100% edge $t_{lin}$ the envelope breaks at $f=1/(\pi t_{lin})$ ([[q0673]]). With $t_{10\text{-}90}=0.8t_{lin}$ this is
$$f_{knee,strict}=\frac{0.8}{\pi t_{10\text{-}90}}=\frac{0.255}{t_{10\text{-}90}}.$$
Johnson & Graham deliberately choose a more conservative $f_{knee}=0.5/t_{r}$, to mark the frequency above which the spectrum is insignificant for most digital-design purposes. In practice use:

- **$0.35/t_r$** to size passive/active circuitry that must pass the edge (filters, scope, amplifier, probe).
- **$0.5/t_r$** for "up to what frequency do I model and test" (SI simulation range, emissions concern, connector rating).

## Worked scope-selection example

A signal has $t_r=1$ ns. What scope bandwidth do you need to measure the edge to within 3%?

1. $\epsilon=0.03$: $t_{scope}/t_{sig}\le\sqrt{1.03^2-1}=0.247$.
2. $t_{scope}\le0.247$ ns, so $BW\ge0.35/0.247\text{ ns}=1.42$ GHz.
3. Add the probe: a probe with 1 GHz bandwidth has $t=0.35$ ns, which by itself is 0.35 of the 1 ns signal. With probe $t_p=0.35$ ns and scope $t_s=0.247$ ns, system $t=\sqrt{0.35^2+0.247^2}=0.43$ ns, measured $\sqrt{1+0.43^2}=1.09$ ns, a 9% error. So a *system* (scope plus probe) of about 1.4 GHz, not just the scope.

## Electrical length: the edge on the board

An edge travelling on a PCB trace with delay about 170 ps/inch (FR4 stripline, typical) has a spatial extent
$$\ell_{edge}=\frac{t_r}{t_d}=\frac{1\text{ ns}}{170\text{ ps/in}}\approx5.9\text{ in}.$$
Common guidance: treat the trace as a transmission line (terminate, control impedance) when its length exceeds about $\ell_{edge}/6$ to $\ell_{edge}/10$; for a 1 ns edge that is about 0.6 to 1 inch. For a 100 ps edge it is about 0.06 to 0.1 inch, i.e. almost every trace. The same $0.35/t_r$ number gives the bandwidth: a 1-inch FR4 trace is about 170 ps long, and its first quarter-wave resonance is in the GHz range, so a 350 MHz-bandwidth edge sees it as electrically short only if it is much shorter than the edge.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 1/6 versus 1/10 criterion (and alternatives like "$t_r<2t_d$") are conventions with different acceptable reflection levels. State the criterion you use.

## Worked example: slowing an edge on purpose

A driver produces a 1 ns edge and you want 4 ns at the load to cut high-frequency emissions. Rise times add in quadrature, so the added RC stage needs
$$t_{RC}=\sqrt{4^2-1^2}=3.87\text{ ns}=2.2RC\;\Rightarrow\;RC=1.76\text{ ns}.$$
With 10 pF of load capacitance that is a total series resistance of 176 Ω (the driver's own output resistance counts, so a 25 Ω driver needs about 150 Ω externally). The corner of the filter is $1/(2\pi RC)=90$ MHz. Check the other side: a 4 ns edge has bandwidth $0.35/4\text{ ns}=88$ MHz, consistent. Whether this is acceptable depends on the timing budget: on a 10 MHz signal (100 ns period) it is trivial; on a 100 MHz signal (10 ns period) a 4 ns edge eats 40% of the period and you would not do it.

## What the rule does not tell you

- It does not tell you the *amplitude* of the content, only where it ends. Use the spectrum for that ([[q0673]]).
- It does not include overshoot and ringing, which add narrowband content at the ring frequency.
- It treats the edge as the only feature. A fast-switching but long-period signal has plenty of low-frequency content too; the bandwidth you need is the larger of the edge bandwidth and the *fundamental/5th harmonic* requirement.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> The strongest short answer: "0.35 over rise time. It's ln 9 over 2 pi for a single pole. I use 0.35 over tr for what a scope or filter must pass and about 0.5 over tr for how high I need to model the spectrum."

::: applications
- **Oscilloscope and probe selection**: required bandwidth for a given edge, using the 3% rule.
- **Op-amp or comparator choice** for fast edges: gain-bandwidth and slew rate must pass $0.35/t_r$ with gain.
- **RC filtering a clock** to slow edges: $t_r\approx2.2RC$ gives the resistor and capacitor.
- **SI simulation ranges**: choosing the highest frequency for S-parameter extraction (about $3\times$ to $5\times$ the knee).
- **Transmission-line decision**: the critical length $\ell_{edge}/6$ for terminated traces.
- **EMC test planning**: estimating which frequency range a given clock's edges will radiate into.
:::

::: assumptions
- **10-90% rise time definition**. For 20-80%, use about $0.22/t_{r,20\text{-}80}$ for a single pole.
- **Single-pole (or Gaussian-like) response**: monotonic step response, no overshoot.
- **Equal rise and fall** times. If different, use the faster edge for bandwidth requirements.
- **Linear, time-invariant** measurement chain.
- **Sufficient sample rate**: digital scopes need sample rate at least several times the bandwidth, otherwise samples are too sparse to represent the edge.

Valid alternatives: some texts quote $BW=0.45/t_r$ for sharper instrument responses and "0.35 to 0.5" for spectral content. In an interview, state your constant and its source.
:::

::: followups
### Why is the constant 0.35 for 10-90% rise time?
For a single-pole response, rise time is $\tau\ln9$ and the -3 dB bandwidth is $1/(2\pi\tau)$. Their product is $\ln 9/2\pi=0.3497$. It is the same number for any RC value, because it depends only on the shape of the exponential and the 10/90% definitions.

### What bandwidth do I need to pass a signal with a 2.5 ns rise time?
$0.35/2.5\text{ ns}=140$ MHz to pass the edge. For a scope to measure it within 3%, about 4 times that: roughly 570 MHz. For modelling the spectrum use $0.5/t_r=200$ MHz and simulate to about 600 MHz to 1 GHz.

### An amplifier has 20 MHz bandwidth and a scope has 100 MHz. What rise time will a very fast step show?
Amplifier $t=0.35/20\text{ MHz}=17.5$ ns, scope $t=3.5$ ns. Total $\sqrt{17.5^2+3.5^2}=17.8$ ns. The amplifier dominates; the scope barely matters. Use the quadrature rule to see which stage limits.

### Does the rule work for 20-80% rise times?
Not with 0.35. For a single pole, $t_{20\text{-}80}=\tau\ln4=1.386\tau$ so $BW\cdot t_{20\text{-}80}=0.22$. Many datasheets for high-speed digital parts quote 20-80%, so convert before applying the rule: $t_{10\text{-}90}\approx 1.59\,t_{20\text{-}80}$ for a single pole.

### What if the signal's edge has 20% overshoot and ringing at 1 GHz?
The rule gives only the edge bandwidth. Overshoot means the actual system is underdamped (the constant moves toward 0.4), and the ringing adds a narrowband component at 1 GHz which also needs to be passed. Size the bandwidth for the ringing frequency, and decide separately whether to damp the ring ([[q0696]]).
:::

::: interpretations
"The rule of thumb for the highest frequency content of a real square wave" can mean (a) $0.35/t_r$ (bandwidth) or (b) a knee frequency ($0.5/t_r$, or $1/(\pi t_r)$ for a linear 0-100% ramp), or (c) the "5th harmonic" rule for waveform fidelity. The answer gives all three and says when each applies. The sibling [[q0675]] covers the conceptual ideal-vs-real distinction.
