::: testing
Whether you can draw the circuits correctly **and** say why they work, with no memorization trap:

1. RC low-pass: series $R$, shunt $C$, output across $C$. RC high-pass: series $C$, shunt $R$, output across $R$.
2. The reasoning from impedances: a capacitor is open at DC and a short at high frequency; an inductor is the opposite.
3. The RL versions (and which one is which), the cutoff formulas $1/(2\pi RC)$ and $R/(2\pi L)$.
4. The practical point: **source and load interact with the filter**, and a buffer or an active stage fixes that.
:::

::: why
First-order filters are everywhere: anti-aliasing and noise filters in front of ADCs, AC coupling between amplifier stages, debouncing, supply-rail filtering, PWM smoothing, sensor signal conditioning. Drawing them correctly is table stakes; the more valuable signal is knowing how loading shifts the corner, why RC is almost always chosen over RL, and when to add an op-amp. Interviewers often ask for the circuit first and then probe with "what if the next stage has a 1 kΩ input impedance?"
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor impedance** $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)$: open at DC, short at high frequency. See [[q0024]].
- **Inductor impedance** $Z_L = j\omega L$: short at DC, open at high frequency. See [[q0040]].
- **Impedance divider**: $V_{out}/V_{in} = Z_2/(Z_1+Z_2)$. See [[q0008]].
- **Cutoff frequency**: RC: $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$; RL: $f_c = R/(2\pi L)$. See [[q0133]].
- **Loading**: the next stage's input impedance appears in parallel with the output element.
- **Active filter**: an op-amp adds gain and isolates source and load. See [[q0125]].
:::

::: answer
"For a first-order low-pass, I draw a resistor in series with the signal and a capacitor from the output node to ground, and I take the output across the capacitor. At DC the capacitor is open, so everything gets through. At high frequency the capacitor acts like a short and pulls the output to ground. The corner is $1/(2\pi RC)$.

For the high-pass I swap them: the capacitor goes in series and the resistor goes to ground, with the output across the resistor. Now DC is blocked by the capacitor, and high frequencies pass. Same corner formula.

You can build the same filters with an inductor. A low-pass is a series inductor with a resistor to ground, and a high-pass is a series resistor with an inductor to ground; the corner is $R/(2\pi L)$. But in practice I'd use RC, since inductors are bigger, costlier, lossy and pick up magnetic fields.

One caveat is loading. These formulas assume nothing is attached to the output. A load resistor in parallel with the capacitor changes both the DC gain and the corner. If that's a problem, I either use a much lower filter resistance than the load, or put an op-amp buffer after the filter, or build an active first-order filter around an op-amp, with a capacitor in parallel with the feedback resistor for a low-pass."
:::

::: technical
**RC low-pass (output across C):** $H(s)=\frac{1}{1+sRC}$, $f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi RC}$, DC gain 1, $Z_{out}=R\|\frac{1}{sC}$.

**RC high-pass (output across R):** $H(s)=\frac{sRC}{1+sRC}$, same $f_c$, gain → 1 at high frequency, blocks DC.

**RL low-pass (series L, output across R):** $H(s)=\frac{R}{R+sL}=\frac{1}{1+sL/R}$, $f_c=\frac{R}{2\pi L}$.

**RL high-pass (series R, output across L):** $H(s)=\frac{sL}{R+sL}$, same $f_c$.

**Active first-order low-pass (inverting):** input resistor $R_{in}$, feedback $R_f\|C_f$: $H(s)=-\frac{R_f}{R_{in}}\cdot\frac{1}{1+sR_fC_f}$, $f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi R_fC_f}$, DC gain $-R_f/R_{in}$, low output impedance.

**Active first-order high-pass (inverting):** $C_{in}$ in series with $R_{in}$, feedback $R_f$: $H(s)=-\frac{R_f}{R_{in}}\cdot\frac{sR_{in}C_{in}}{1+sR_{in}C_{in}}$, $f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi R_{in}C_{in}}$.

**Loading.** RC low-pass into load $R_L$: DC gain $\frac{R_L}{R+R_L}$ and corner $f_c'=\frac{1}{2\pi (R\|R_L)C}$. Rule of thumb: $R_L\ge 10R$ keeps the DC error within ~9% and the corner within ~10%.

**Source resistance.** A source resistance $R_s$ adds to $R$ in the low-pass: $f_c=1/(2\pi(R+R_s)C)$.
:::

::: short
"The RC low-pass is a series resistor with a capacitor to ground, output across the capacitor. The capacitor shorts out high frequencies. The high-pass swaps them: series capacitor, resistor to ground, output across the resistor, so the capacitor blocks DC. Both have a corner at $1/(2\pi RC)$. You can build the same filters with an inductor, series L plus a resistor to ground for a low-pass, but RC is usually preferred. And note that loading shifts the corner, so buffer it or use an active stage if needed."
:::

::: long
"Let me start with the low-pass. I draw a series resistor from the input to the output node, and a capacitor from the output node to ground. The output is the voltage across the capacitor. I can reason about it with limiting cases. At DC the capacitor is an open circuit, no current flows, there's no drop across the resistor, and the output equals the input. At very high frequency the capacitor is a short to ground, so the output goes to zero. In between, the transition is at the corner frequency, $1/(2\pi RC)$, where the capacitor's impedance equals the resistance and the output is down 3 dB.

The high-pass is the same two parts, swapped. The capacitor is in series with the signal and the resistor goes to ground. At DC the capacitor blocks everything, so the output is zero. At high frequency the capacitor is a short, so the signal passes. The corner is the same formula. This is exactly what an AC-coupling capacitor and the bias resistor after it do.

With an inductor I get duals. A series inductor with a resistor to ground is a low-pass, because the inductor's impedance rises with frequency, and a series resistor with an inductor to ground is a high-pass. The corner is $R/(2\pi L)$. I rarely choose these because inductors are bulky, expensive, have winding resistance, and radiate and pick up magnetic noise.

The main practical caveat is that all of this assumes an unloaded output and an ideal source. A load resistor appears in parallel with the capacitor, which lowers the passband gain and raises the corner. A source resistance adds to $R$. To avoid this, I keep the filter resistance a tenth or less of the load, or put a unity-gain op-amp buffer after the filter. An active version also works: an inverting amplifier with a capacitor in parallel with the feedback resistor gives a low-pass with gain, and the corner is $1/(2\pi R_fC_f)$."
:::

::: terms
- **Low-pass filter (LPF)**: passes low frequencies, attenuates high ones.
- **High-pass filter (HPF)**: passes high frequencies, blocks DC and low frequencies.
- **Corner / cutoff frequency**: where the gain is down 3 dB.
- **Time constant $\tau$**: $RC$ or $L/R$; $f_c = 1/(2\pi\tau)$.
- **Series element / shunt element**: the part in the signal path vs. the part to ground.
- **Loading**: the effect of the following stage's input impedance on the filter.
- **Buffer (follower)**: a unity-gain op-amp stage that isolates the filter from the load.
- **Active filter**: a filter that includes an amplifier (op-amp) in addition to passive components.
- **AC coupling**: a series capacitor used as a high-pass to block DC.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws the RC LPF and HPF and states $f_c$.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains each circuit with the **limiting cases** (open/short at DC and at high frequency) so the polarity cannot be mixed up.
- Gives the **RL duals** correctly and says why RC is preferred.
- Notes that the **output must be taken across the right element** and that the formula assumes **no load and zero source resistance**.
- Quantifies loading with an example (10 kΩ filter resistor into a 10 kΩ load halves the gain and doubles $f_c$) and offers the **buffer/active** fix.
- Shows a **design example**: choose $R$ from the loading and noise constraints, then calculate $C$ and round to a standard value.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Taking the output across the wrong part.** For the low-pass it's across $C$ (or $R$ in the RL case), not across the series resistor.
- **Mixing up RL and RC duality.** The RC low-pass has a *shunt capacitor*; the RL low-pass has a *series inductor*.
- **Forgetting the source resistance.** A 1 kΩ source driving a 10 kΩ RC filter moves the corner by 10%.
- **Ignoring load.** A load resistor of the same order as $R$ changes both gain and corner.
- **Using a polarized or high-leakage capacitor** (or a ceramic with a big DC-bias derating) and assuming the nominal value. The corner can shift by tens of percent.
- **Not providing a DC bias path** for the high-pass: a series capacitor followed by a high-impedance amplifier input needs the shunt resistor to define the DC level.
- **Cascading two RC stages and assuming the corner stays the same**; the second stage loads the first. See [[q0131]].
:::

Figure: Same two parts, swapped. In the low-pass, the capacitor to ground diverts high frequencies. In the high-pass, the series capacitor blocks low frequencies and the resistor to ground sets the DC level. Both have $f_c = 1/(2\pi\cdot10\text{ k}\Omega\cdot15.9\text{ nF}) \approx 1$ kHz.

**Reasoning by impedance.** In the low-pass, $V_{out}=V_{in}\frac{Z_C}{R+Z_C}$; as $\omega\to0$, $Z_C\to\infty$ so $V_{out}\to V_{in}$; as $\omega\to\infty$, $Z_C\to0$ so $V_{out}\to 0$. In the high-pass, $V_{out}=V_{in}\frac{R}{R+Z_C}$, the complementary behavior.

**Design recipe.**
1. Pick the corner $f_c$.
2. Choose $R$ for the loading/noise/source constraints (commonly 1 kΩ to 100 kΩ), then $C = 1/(2\pi R f_c)$.
3. Round to a standard value and recompute the corner.

Example: $f_c = 500$ Hz with $R=10$ kΩ needs $C = 31.8$ nF; the nearest standard value, 33 nF, gives 482 Hz (a 3.6% shift). With a 1% capacitor you'd still need to allow for tolerance and temperature coefficient; C0G/NP0 capacitors are stable, X7R and Y5V can drift or lose capacitance with DC bias.

Figure: The inductor duals. In the low-pass the series inductor's impedance $j\omega L$ rises with frequency and blocks the signal; the output is across $R$. In the high-pass the shunt inductor shorts low frequencies (including DC) to ground and the output is across $L$. $f_c = R/(2\pi L) = 1000/(2\pi\cdot0.159)\approx 1$ kHz.

RL filters appear where the inductor is already there for another reason (motor windings, power-line inductors), but are rare as signal filters: a 159 mH inductor for 1 kHz is physically large and has significant winding resistance and magnetic pickup, whereas a 15.9 nF capacitor is tiny. For power filtering at high currents, series inductance with a capacitor to ground (LC) is standard, a second-order filter.

## Loading, quantified

Take the RC low-pass above ($R = 10$ kΩ, $C = 15.9$ nF) and attach a 10 kΩ load. The load is in parallel with $C$ at DC, so the DC gain becomes $10/(10+10)=0.5$ (−6 dB) and the Thevenin resistance seen by $C$ is $R\|R_L = 5$ kΩ, so $f_c' = 1/(2\pi\cdot5\text{ k}\Omega\cdot15.9\text{ nF}) = 2.0$ kHz. The filter now has half the DC gain and twice the bandwidth.

## Which should I use?

| Need | Choose |
|---|---|
| Signal-level LPF/HPF, small and cheap | Passive RC |
| Filter driving a low-impedance or varying load | RC + op-amp buffer, or active filter |
| Gain as well as filtering | Active first-order filter |
| Power-line filtering at high current | LC (second order), or ferrite bead |
| AC-coupling a bias-sensitive input | Series C + bias resistor (HPF) |
| Inductor already present (motor, relay coil) | RL behavior is built in |

> [!TIP]
> To verify which output node is correct when you sketch, run the two limits in your head: at DC, should the output equal the input (low-pass) or be zero (high-pass)? The capacitor is open at DC, so the output across $C$ with a series $R$ equals the input: that's the low-pass.
:::

::: applications
- **ADC anti-aliasing**: a first-order RC (or RC plus a buffer) in front of a SAR ADC, with $R$ small enough for the ADC's sampling-capacitor settling.
- **AC coupling of audio and sensor signals**: series capacitor and bias resistor high-pass with corner of 1 to 20 Hz.
- **Button debounce**: RC low-pass with a corner of a few Hz to tens of Hz into a Schmitt-trigger input.
- **PWM-to-DC conversion**: RC low-pass averages a PWM signal into a slowly varying voltage.
- **Op-amp integrator/leaky integrator**: an op-amp with $R_f\|C_f$ is the active LPF above.
- **Supply-rail noise filtering**: RC or ferrite bead plus capacitor feeding a quiet analog block.
:::

::: assumptions
I assumed ideal components, an ideal voltage source driving the filter and no load unless stated. Real capacitors have ESR/ESL and DC-bias/temperature effects, real inductors have winding resistance and core saturation, and real resistors have parasitic capacitance. At high frequency the parasitics limit the stopband. I chose an inverting active filter for the op-amp examples; non-inverting versions exist (a unity-gain follower after a passive RC is the simplest, and avoids the sign inversion). For a **current-source** input (a photodiode), the active low-pass is a transimpedance amplifier with feedback capacitor. The corner formulas are for first-order filters only; cascaded stages interact unless buffered (see [[q0131]]).
:::

::: followups
### What happens to the RC low-pass corner if the source resistance is 1 kΩ and $R = 10$ kΩ?
The source resistance adds in series: $R_{total} = 11$ kΩ, so $f_c = 1/(2\pi\cdot11\text{ k}\Omega\cdot C)$, which is $1/1.1 = 0.91$ times the nominal, a 9% drop. If $R_s$ varies (a sensor or an unbuffered upstream stage), the corner varies with it.

### How would you design a first-order low-pass for a 12-bit ADC front end at 100 kHz sample rate with a 10 kHz signal bandwidth?
Pick $f_c$ a bit above the signal band, say 15 to 20 kHz, to avoid attenuating the signal at 10 kHz (at 10 kHz and $f_c=20$ kHz the gain is −0.97 dB). With $R=1$ kΩ, $C=1/(2\pi\cdot1\text{ k}\Omega\cdot20\text{ kHz})=7.96$ nF. But one pole only gives −20 dB/decade, so noise or tones near 50 kHz (the Nyquist frequency) are barely attenuated (about 8.6 dB). A second-order or higher filter is usually needed for strong anti-aliasing. And check that the ADC's sampling capacitor settles through $R$.

### Draw a high-pass filter that blocks 60 Hz mains but passes 1 kHz. What do you choose?
Corner somewhat above 60 Hz but well below 1 kHz, e.g. $f_c = 250$ Hz. Using $C = 100$ nF, $R = 1/(2\pi\cdot250\cdot100\text{ nF}) = 6.4$ kΩ (a standard 6.2 kΩ gives 257 Hz). The attenuation at 60 Hz is only 12.9 dB (a single-pole HPF), so for stronger rejection use a higher-order or notch filter. At 1 kHz the gain is −0.3 dB.

### How does an op-amp buffer after an RC low-pass change its behavior?
It removes the load dependence: the buffer's input impedance is huge, so the filter sees essentially no load and keeps its unloaded transfer function, while the low output impedance of the buffer drives the next stage. Cost: an op-amp, power, noise, and possible bandwidth or stability limits.

### Harder: you cascade two identical unbuffered RC low-pass sections. Is the corner still $1/(2\pi RC)$ and is the slope −40 dB/decade?
The slope is −40 dB/decade at high frequency, but the corner is lower. The second section loads the first, giving $H = 1/(1 + 3sRC + (sRC)^2)$. Its −3 dB frequency is $0.374/(2\pi RC)$, which is about 0.374 of the single-stage corner. With a buffer between the stages (or with the second stage scaled up in impedance), each pole stays at $1/(2\pi RC)$ and the combined −3 dB point is 0.644 of that.
:::
