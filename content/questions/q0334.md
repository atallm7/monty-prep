::: testing
Whether you can explain the **physical mechanism** rather than naming parts. The interviewer is listening for:

1. The two core laws: a current makes a magnetic field, and a current-carrying conductor in a field feels a force (Lorentz force). A moving conductor in a field also generates a voltage (back-EMF).
2. A clear picture of the solenoid (coil + plunger + spring, pulled to close the gap) and of the brushed motor (rotor coil in a field + commutator to keep torque one-directional).
3. The link to the electrical model: torque proportional to current, back-EMF proportional to speed.
:::

::: why
If you can explain how a motor works from first principles, then startup current, stall, back-EMF speed sensing, torque-current relationships, and driver design all follow. Interviewers use "describe how it works" to see whether you will be able to reason about problems you haven't seen, like why a motor draws less current when it speeds up or why a solenoid hums. For a hardware engineer the key outputs are two equations ($\tau = K_t I$ and $e = K_e\omega$) and the picture behind them.
:::

::: concepts
- **Magnetic field of a coil**: $B \propto NI$, concentrated by iron. See [[q0335]].
- **Lorentz force**: $F = BI\ell$ on a conductor carrying $I$ across flux density $B$.
- **Faraday/Lenz**: moving a conductor through a field induces a voltage that opposes the motion (back-EMF). See [[q0341]].
- **Reluctance force**: iron is pulled toward the stronger field to minimize magnetic energy (solenoid plunger).
- **Commutation**: reversing the current in a rotor coil at the right angle to keep torque in one direction.
:::

::: answer
"Both rely on the fact that current makes a magnetic field and a magnetic field pushes on current.

A solenoid is a coil with a movable iron plunger. When you drive current through the coil, it creates a magnetic field, and the iron gets pulled in toward where the field is strongest, which closes the air gap. A spring pulls the plunger back out when the current stops. The force is strongest when the gap is small, and it goes up with the square of the current times turns.

A brushed DC motor has a coil on the rotor sitting between magnets on the stator. Current in the coil in that magnetic field gives a force on each side of the loop, F equals B I L, in opposite directions, so you get a torque. After half a turn the forces would reverse, so the commutator and brushes flip the current direction in the coil, and the torque keeps pushing the same way. Torque is proportional to current: tau equals K_t times I.

The second half of the story is that a coil moving through a field generates a voltage, the back-EMF, proportional to speed, and it opposes the supply. So as the motor speeds up, the net voltage across the resistance falls and the current drops. That's why a motor draws a big current at start and a small one at full speed."
:::

::: technical
**Solenoid.**

- Magnetic circuit: $NI = \Phi\,\mathcal{R}$ where reluctance is dominated by the air gap $g$: $\mathcal{R}\approx 2g/(\mu_0A)$ for a two-gap path or $g/(\mu_0 A)$ for one gap.
- Stored energy $W = \tfrac12 L I^2$ with $L = N^2/\mathcal{R}$. Force $F = \tfrac12 I^2\,\partial L/\partial x$ (for a gap $g$ decreasing, $L$ rises).
- With a single gap and iron not saturated: $F \approx \dfrac{\mu_0 A (NI)^2}{2g^2}$.
- Example: $N = 500$, $I = 0.5$ A, $A = 1\ \text{cm}^2$, $g = 2$ mm gives $F = 0.98$ N; at $g = 1$ mm, $3.9$ N; at 4 mm, 0.25 N.
- Saturation limits the force at small gaps; the formula over-predicts there.

**Brushed DC motor.**

- Force on one conductor: $F = B I \ell$. For a rotor loop of area $A$ and $N$ turns whose plane is at angle $\theta$ to the field, torque $\tau = N B I A\cos\theta$ (maximum at $\theta = 0$, plane parallel to the field; zero in the neutral plane at $\theta = 90°$).
- Commutator: reverses current each half turn so that the torque keeps the same sign. With many coils and segments the torque ripple is small and the average torque is $\tau = K_t I$ with $K_t = N B A$ (effective).
- Back-EMF: rotating the loop at $\omega$ induces $e = N B A\,\omega\cos\theta$ (same angle dependence as the torque); averaged by commutation, $e = K_e\omega$, with $K_e = K_t$ in SI units (power balance: $e I = \tau\omega$).
- Circuit equation: $V = IR + L\,\dot I + K_e\omega$; mechanical: $J\dot\omega = K_t I - \tau_L - b\omega$.
- Example: 50 turns, $B = 0.3$ T, loop 3 cm by 2 cm ($A = 6\times10^{-4}$ m²): $K_t = 50\times0.3\times6\times10^{-4} = 0.009$ N·m/A; at 2 A, $\tau = 0.018$ N·m.

**Efficiency of conversion.** Electrical input $VI$ splits into $I^2R$ heat and $K_e\omega I$ mechanical power (before friction): at stall all of it is heat.
:::

::: short
"Both use the fact that current makes a magnetic field and a field pushes on current. A solenoid is a coil with an iron plunger that the field pulls in to close the air gap, with a spring to return it; force grows with turns times current and gets much stronger as the gap shrinks. A brushed motor has a rotor coil in a magnet's field, so F equals BIL on each side gives torque proportional to current, and the commutator flips the current so torque stays one way. The spinning coil also makes a back-EMF proportional to speed."
:::

::: long
"Let me start with the shared principle. A current in a wire makes a magnetic field, and a current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force, F equals B times I times L. Everything else is packaging.

A solenoid is a coil, usually many turns wound on a tube, with a movable iron plunger. When I put current through the coil, I get a field, and iron is pulled toward the region of strongest field, so the plunger is drawn in to close the air gap. A spring returns it when the current stops. The pull depends on the number of turns times current, squared, and on the gap: roughly mu0 A (NI) squared over 2 g squared. So it's weak when the plunger is far out and strong when it's closed, and that's why solenoids often use a strong pull-in pulse followed by a lower hold current.

A brushed DC motor does it with rotation. The rotor has coils sitting between permanent magnets. Current in a coil gives equal and opposite forces on its two sides, which makes a torque about the shaft. As the coil rotates through half a turn the torque would reverse, so the commutator, a split ring with brushes, swaps the current direction at the right moment. The result: torque proportional to current, tau equals K_t times I.

The other half: a conductor moving through a field generates a voltage, the back-EMF, e equals K_e times omega. It opposes the supply. So the current is I equals V minus K_e omega over R. At standstill there's no back-EMF so the current is huge, and as the motor speeds up the current falls until the torque just balances the load. That's the essential behaviour I'd carry into any drive design."
:::

::: terms
- **Lorentz force**: force $F = BI\ell$ on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field.
- **Magnetomotive force (MMF)**: $NI$, the "drive" for magnetic flux.
- **Reluctance**: opposition to magnetic flux; dominated by the air gap in a solenoid.
- **Torque constant $K_t$**: N·m per amp.
- **Back-EMF constant $K_e$**: volts per rad/s; equal to $K_t$ in SI units.
- **Commutator**: split-ring switch that reverses rotor current each half turn.
- **Armature**: the current-carrying rotor winding.
- **Pull-in / hold current**: higher current to start closing a solenoid, lower current to keep it closed.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "current makes a magnetic field, and the magnet attracts/repels so it moves".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Names the **two laws** (Lorentz force, Faraday/Lenz) and shows both appear in a motor ($\tau = K_t I$, $e = K_e\omega$).
- Explains **why commutation exists** (torque would reverse each half turn) and what replaces it in BLDC motors (electronics).
- Gives the **solenoid force law** with its $1/g^2$ and $(NI)^2$ dependence and what it implies (pull-in vs hold).
- Uses the **power balance** $eI = \tau\omega$ to show $K_e = K_t$.
- Connects to behaviour: startup current, falling current with speed, and heating at stall.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The coil becomes a magnet that is repelled by the stator magnet."** True qualitatively, but the quantitative picture is $F = BI\ell$ and commutation; without commutation the rotor would just oscillate to a stop.
- **"Motor torque depends on voltage."** Torque depends on *current*; voltage sets speed (through back-EMF) and thus how much current flows.
- **"The solenoid force is linear in current."** It scales with the square of current (for unsaturated iron).
- **"Back-EMF only exists when the motor is generating."** It exists whenever it spins, in motoring mode too; it is the voltage that limits the current.
- **Forgetting the return spring** and the gap dependence in the solenoid.
- **Mixing up $K_t$ and $K_e$ units.** In SI they are numerically equal (N·m/A = V·s/rad); in rpm units they are not.
:::

::: deep
## The solenoid: a coil that wants to close its gap

Wind $N$ turns on a bobbin. Pass current $I$, and the magnetic field $H$ in the iron and gap satisfies Ampère's law $\oint H\cdot dl = NI$. If the iron's permeability is much larger than air, nearly all the "magnetic voltage" drops across the air gap $g$:

$$B \approx \frac{\mu_0 N I}{g}$$

The pull on a pole face of area $A$ is the magnetic pressure $B^2/(2\mu_0)$ times area:

$$F = \frac{B^2 A}{2\mu_0} = \frac{\mu_0 A (NI)^2}{2 g^2}$$

The inductance rises as the plunger enters: $L \approx \mu_0 N^2 A/g$. So the electrical behaviour has a feature: when the plunger moves in, $L$ increases suddenly and, since the plunger motion generates a back-EMF-like term $I\,dL/dt$, the coil current *dips* during travel, then rises again to $V/R$ once it stops. That dip is the electrical signature of the plunger arriving ([[q0336]]).

## The brushed DC motor: force, torque, commutation

Take one rectangular loop of width $w$ and length $\ell$ in flux density $B$, carrying $I$. The two long sides carry opposite currents, so forces $F = BI\ell$ act in opposite directions at a lever arm $w/2$ each:

$$\tau = 2\cdot BI\ell\cdot\frac{w}{2}\cos\theta = BI A\cos\theta$$

At $\theta=0$ (loop parallel to the field) torque is maximum; at $\theta = 90°$ it is zero, and just beyond that it would reverse. The commutator reverses the current exactly there, so the torque stays positive. A real rotor has many coils at different angles and many commutator segments, which smooths the torque and gives $\tau \approx K_t I$ with $K_t = N B A$ (effective).

## Back-EMF: the same coil acting as a generator

Faraday's law says a loop turning at $\omega$ in a field generates a voltage proportional to the rate of flux change. After commutation the average is $e = K_e\omega$. Energy conservation ties the constants together: electrical power converted to mechanical is $eI = \tau\omega$, so

$$K_e\omega I = K_t I\,\omega \;\Rightarrow\; K_e = K_t \quad(\text{SI})$$

The full circuit is then $V = IR + L\dot I + K_e\omega$, so in steady state

$$I = \frac{V - K_e\omega}{R}$$

For the example 12 V, 1 Ω, $K=0.02$ motor: stall current 12 A; at 350 rad/s, $e = 7$ V and $I = 5$ A, giving $\tau = 0.1$ N·m and a mechanical power of $0.1\times350 = 35$ W out of 60 W in.

## Solenoid vs motor: summary

| Aspect | Solenoid | Brushed DC motor |
|---|---|---|
| Force/torque law | $F\propto (NI)^2/g^2$ | $\tau = K_t I$ |
| Moving element | Iron plunger | Rotor with current-carrying coils |
| Commutation needed | No | Yes (brushes or electronics) |
| Back-EMF | Weak, only while moving ($I\,dL/dx\cdot v$) | Strong, $K_e\omega$ |
| Steady current | $V/R$ | $(V - K_e\omega)/R$ |
:::

::: applications
- **Valves and locks**: solenoids with pull-in and hold currents (often PWM-reduced) to save power and heat.
- **Fans and pumps**: brushed or brushless motors where speed is set by voltage/PWM.
- **Robot drivetrains**: gearmotors with encoders, using $\tau = K_t I$ for torque control via current sensing.
- **Speed sensing without a tachometer**: reading back-EMF during PWM off-time ([[q0408]]).
- **Haptics**: vibration motors and linear resonant actuators in phones.
:::

::: assumptions
- The description is for a **permanent-magnet brushed DC motor**; wound-field, BLDC, stepper and induction motors differ in construction but share $F=BI\ell$ and Faraday's law.
- The solenoid formula assumes an unsaturated magnetic path with a single dominant air gap, and neglects fringing and leakage; real force is lower at large gaps and limited at small gaps by saturation.
- Constants ($N$, $B$, $A$) are illustrative.
- $K_e = K_t$ holds in SI units (V·s/rad and N·m/A) for an ideal machine; with rpm and oz-in units the numbers differ.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the commutator matter?
Without it the rotor torque reverses each half turn, so the rotor would just oscillate around the neutral position. The commutator reverses current in the winding at the neutral plane so the torque always pushes the same way. Brushless motors do the same job with electronic switches and position sensing.

### Why does a motor draw less current as it speeds up?
The rotating coil generates back-EMF $K_e\omega$, which subtracts from the supply voltage across the coil resistance: $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$. At stall there is no back-EMF so $I = V/R$; at no-load speed $I$ only supplies friction.

### How would you increase a solenoid's force by a factor of 4?
Double the current (if the coil can take $I^2R$ heating, probably only briefly), or double the turns at the same current (the coil resistance rises with turns, so the supply voltage must rise too), or halve the gap. Practically use a pull-in boost then drop to hold current.

### What does the solenoid current look like when the plunger moves?
Current rises with $\tau = L/R$ at first. When the plunger begins moving, inductance rises and the current dips (an $I\,dL/dt$ back-EMF term), then climbs to $V/R$ once the plunger stops. The dip can be used to detect plunger arrival.

### Scenario: a motor's no-load current is 0.5 A at 12 V and R = 1 Ω with K = 0.02. What is its no-load speed?
$e = V - IR = 12 - 0.5\times1 = 11.5$ V, so $\omega = e/K_e = 11.5/0.02 = 575$ rad/s, which is $575\times60/2\pi \approx 5490$ rpm.

### Why does the solenoid hum on AC?
The force goes with $I^2$ so it pulses at twice the line frequency. Shading rings (copper rings on the pole face that create a phase-shifted flux) or rectifying to DC keep the force from dropping to zero twice per cycle.
:::

"Describe how a motor/solenoid works" is answered for both: the solenoid as an electromagnet with a plunger, and the motor as a commutated coil in a field. For the motor the default is a brushed DC motor, as in the rest of this section. Brushless and stepper motors have their own questions later in the section; motor vs generator is [[q0346]].
