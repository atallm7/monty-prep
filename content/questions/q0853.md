::: testing
Whether you can run a **proper engineering comparison** instead of "photodiodes are better". The interviewer wants: the physics difference (bulk photoconductivity vs junction photocurrent), speed (milliseconds to hundreds of ms vs nanoseconds to microseconds), linearity (power law vs linear over many decades), signal level and the readout circuit (divider vs transimpedance amplifier), spectral response, temperature behaviour, cost and regulatory aspects (cadmium). Then the decision rule: where each wins. Giving a worked number for a divider output or a TIA gain is the clincher.
:::

::: why
This is a real design decision for any product with ambient light sensing, from a night-light to an outdoor lamp controller to a medical optical sensor. A photoresistor needs two parts and no analog design but is slow, drifty and nonlinear. A photodiode needs an op-amp but is fast, linear and precise. Knowing which to choose, and why, shows you can weigh cost, performance and design effort. It also checks that you can compute how each interfaces to an ADC, which is where many first designs fail.
:::

::: concepts
- **Photoconductivity**: absorbed photons raise carrier density in a semiconductor film (CdS), lowering resistance. Slow trap-dominated dynamics.
- **Photodiode photocurrent**: junction separates carriers, current proportional to power. See [[q0851]].
- **Voltage divider readout**: resistance to voltage. See [[q0008]].
- **Transimpedance amplifier**: current to voltage. See [[q0121]].
- **Response time and bandwidth**: time constant $\tau$ and $f_{-3dB}=1/(2\pi\tau)$.
- **Linearity vs power-law response**: $R\propto E^{-\gamma}$ for photoresistors.
- **Spectral response**: which wavelengths the device sees. See [[q0852]].
:::

::: answer
"The photoresistor, or LDR, is a piece of semiconductor, typically cadmium sulfide, whose resistance drops as light increases, from megaohms in the dark to maybe hundreds of ohms in sun. It is passive and trivial to use: a divider with a fixed resistor into an ADC. It is cheap, gives a large signal, and its roughly logarithmic response happens to cover a wide range of light levels with one ADC. The downsides are that it is slow, tens to hundreds of milliseconds and with a memory of its recent light history, it is nonlinear and drifts with temperature, it is not accurate from part to part, and CdS is a cadmium compound, which is restricted in many markets.

A photodiode generates a current proportional to light power, linear over many decades, with response times of nanoseconds to microseconds, and good stability and repeatability. The price is that the signal is small, nanoamps to microamps, so I need a transimpedance amplifier and some care with noise, dark current and layout.

So for something slow and non-critical, like turning on a night light at dusk with hysteresis, an LDR is fine and cheap. For anything that needs speed, such as an optical receiver, linearity, like a light meter, calibration, or regulatory compliance, I use a photodiode with a TIA, or an integrated ambient light sensor IC that already contains it."
:::

::: technical
**Physics.** *Photoresistor (LDR, CdS or CdSe)*: polycrystalline film; light generates carriers, resistance follows approximately $R=R_{10}\,(E/10\text{ lux})^{-\gamma}$ with $\gamma\approx0.5$ to 0.9 (typical, device-dependent). *Photodiode*: $I_{ph}=\mathcal R\,P$, linear to within a percent or so over 6 to 9 decades until the readout saturates.

**Comparison.**

| Property | Photoresistor (CdS) | Photodiode (Si) |
|---|---|---|
| Output | resistance, $\Omega$ to M$\Omega$ | current, pA to mA |
| Response | $R\propto E^{-\gamma}$ (power law) | $I\propto E$ (linear) |
| Speed | rise about 10 to 50 ms, decay tens to hundreds of ms | ns to µs (bandwidth to MHz) |
| Readout | divider + ADC | TIA + ADC |
| Spectral | peaks near the green (about 540 nm for CdS), like the eye | 400 to 1100 nm, peak near 900 nm |
| Linearity | poor, history-dependent | excellent |
| Temperature | resistance and sensitivity vary with T | dark current doubles every ~8 to 10 °C |
| Part-to-part spread | large (a factor of 2 or more) | small, repeatable |
| Cost / effort | very low, no op-amp | low, needs op-amp |
| Compliance | cadmium: restricted under RoHS in many applications | no cadmium |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> LDR speed, gamma, resistances and temperature coefficients depend on the specific device and light history; datasheets give typical, not guaranteed, values. RoHS exemptions for CdS cells are application and jurisdiction specific; check before using in a product.

**Divider readout example.** Assume an LDR with $R=10$ kΩ at 10 lux and $\gamma=0.7$, a 10 kΩ fixed resistor and 3.3 V. $V_{out}=3.3\cdot\frac{10\text{k}}{10\text{k}+R_{LDR}}$.

| Illuminance | $R_{LDR}$ | $V_{out}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.1 lux | 251 kΩ | 0.13 V |
| 1 lux | 50.1 kΩ | 0.55 V |
| 10 lux | 10 kΩ | 1.65 V |
| 100 lux | 2.0 kΩ | 2.75 V |
| 1000 lux | 398 Ω | 3.17 V |

Three decades of light span 0.55 V to 3.17 V, a signal an ADC reads directly. The compression is a feature for coarse sensing and a bug for measurement: at 1000 lux the slope is only 0.085 mV/lux against 58 mV/lux at 10 lux.

**Photodiode readout example.** About 6.7 nA/lux (5 mm² diode, white LED light, see [[q0851]]). With a 100 kΩ TIA: 0.67 mV/lux, perfectly linear: 0.67 mV at 1 lux (less than one 12-bit LSB of 0.81 mV), 0.67 V at 1000 lux, saturating near 4900 lux on a 3.3 V rail. Linear but narrow, so for 1 to 100,000 lux you add gain switching or an integrating converter.

**Bandwidth.** A photodiode into a 1 MΩ TIA with 10 pF input capacitance has an RC corner near 16 kHz (before loop gain extends it); an LDR with a 30 ms response is about 5 Hz. For flicker at 100/120 Hz the LDR averages it away, while a photodiode sees it.
:::

::: long
"Let me compare them on physics, readout, speed, linearity, spectrum and practicalities.

Physics: the photoresistor, usually cadmium sulfide, is a film where absorbed light creates carriers and lowers resistance. The resistance follows roughly a power law, R proportional to illuminance to the minus gamma with gamma around 0.7, so it spans megaohms in the dark to hundreds of ohms in bright light. A photodiode is a junction where light creates a photocurrent proportional to power, about 0.5 amps per watt.

Readout: the photoresistor goes into a divider with a fixed resistor, straight into an ADC. With a 10 kilohm fixed resistor and a 10 kilohm LDR at 10 lux, I get 1.65 volts at 10 lux, and three decades of light cover about half a volt to over 3 volts. The photodiode needs a transimpedance amplifier. About 6.7 nanoamps per lux for a 5 square millimetre diode and 100 kilohms gives 0.67 millivolts per lux, linear but narrow.

Speed: the LDR takes tens to hundreds of milliseconds and has memory of previous light. A photodiode is nanoseconds to microseconds, limited by the amplifier.

Linearity and accuracy: the diode is linear over many decades and repeatable between parts. The LDR is nonlinear, drifts with temperature and light history, and varies by a factor of two between parts.

Practical: the LDR is cents and needs no op-amp, but CdS is restricted in many markets. The photodiode costs a bit more in board area and design effort, and needs care with dark current, noise and layout, though integrated ambient light sensors package all that.

My rule: LDR for slow, coarse, low-cost sensing like a night light with hysteresis; photodiode for measurement, speed, calibration or compliance."
:::

::: terms
- **LDR / photoresistor**: light-dependent resistor, usually CdS.
- **Photoconductivity**: resistance decrease due to light-generated carriers.
- **Gamma ($\gamma$)**: exponent in $R\propto E^{-\gamma}$.
- **Light-history (memory) effect**: LDR resistance depends on prior illumination.
- **Photodiode responsivity**: photocurrent per watt, in A/W.
- **Dark current**: current in darkness (photodiode) and dark resistance (LDR).
- **Transimpedance amplifier**: op-amp current-to-voltage converter.
- **RoHS**: restriction of hazardous substances, including cadmium.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The LDR is more sensitive, so it is better for low light"**. It gives a big resistance change but is slow, noisy and imprecise. A photodiode with a high-value TIA resolves lower light levels in a measurable way.
- **Assuming linear ADC code vs lux for an LDR**. It is a power law; a linear lookup gives huge errors across decades.
- **Ignoring light history and temperature**. After exposure to bright light an LDR takes seconds to minutes to recover its dark resistance.
- **Connecting a photodiode to an ADC through a resistor**. The signal is nA to µA with high source impedance, so you need a TIA, or at least a buffer.
- **Forgetting cadmium regulations** for CdS cells in consumer products sold in regulated markets.
:::

::: deep
## Where each device comes from

**Photoresistor (photoconductor).** A thin polycrystalline film of CdS (or CdSe) between two electrodes. Light with $E>E_g$ (CdS: about 2.4 eV, wavelengths below about 515 to 550 nm; CdSe extends further red) generates electron-hole pairs, increasing conductivity $\sigma=q(\mu_nn+\mu_pp)$. In polycrystalline films, grain boundaries and trap states make carrier lifetimes long (milliseconds), which gives a very large photoconductive *gain* (one absorbed photon can pass many carriers through the device before recombining) but also the slow response and the memory effect. Resistance follows approximately

$$R(E)=R_{10}\left(\frac{E}{10\text{ lux}}\right)^{-\gamma},\qquad\gamma\approx0.5\text{ to }0.9.$$

**Photodiode.** A PN junction where the field sweeps photo-generated carriers out; no gain, but the lifetime issue disappears because collection happens within the transit time (ns). Photocurrent is $\mathcal R P$ over many decades, limited at the bottom by dark current and noise and at the top by series resistance or the readout.

The photodiode needs the transimpedance amplifier drawn in [[q0851]]: $V_{out}=I_{ph}R_f$, with $C_f$ for stability. The LDR circuit has no gain stage and no stability issue, which is the main reason it persists in cheap products.

## Speed: step response

An LDR responds to a light step with an exponential rise and a slower decay: rise time constants of roughly 10 to 50 ms and decay of tens to hundreds of ms for typical CdS cells, with a long tail (seconds to minutes) after strong illumination. The decay is slower in the dark and after bright exposure. A photodiode with a 1 MΩ TIA and 1 pF $C_f$ has bandwidth $1/(2\pi R_fC_f)=159$ kHz, and rise time $0.35/BW=2.2$ µs.

| Device | Time constant | $-3$ dB bandwidth |
|---|---|---|
| LDR (30 ms) | 30 ms | 5.3 Hz |
| Photodiode + 1 MΩ TIA, 1 pF | 1 µs | 159 kHz |
| Photodiode + 100 kΩ TIA, 1 pF | 0.1 µs | 1.6 MHz (op-amp GBW permitting) |

Consequences: an LDR cannot see 100 Hz mains-driven flicker, which can be good (it averages) or bad (you cannot detect it); it cannot decode a 38 kHz remote; and it lags by tenths of a second when sunlight appears, so a lamp switching on dusk does not respond instantly (which also avoids flicker from passing headlights).

## Dynamic range, noise and accuracy

**Photodiode**: the floor is dark current (nA, rising with temperature) and noise: $i_n=\sqrt{2q(I_{ph}+I_d)B+4kTB/R_f}$. With a large $R_f$ (100 MΩ) and low bandwidth (10 Hz), picoamp resolution (sub-lux) is achievable in a well-built circuit; the ceiling is amplifier saturation. Total linear range about 6 decades with gain switching.

**LDR**: noise includes thermal and 1/f (with generation-recombination) noise; the main errors are not noise but *drift and repeatability*: sensitivity changes with temperature, history, ageing, and part-to-part variation (typically a factor of 2 or more in resistance at a given lux, per datasheet tolerance bands). For an application that wants "dark/light" with a 10x margin, that is fine. For "is it 200 or 250 lux?" it is useless without per-unit calibration, which itself would drift.

## Spectral response

A CdS cell peaks around 540 nm, which happens to resemble the eye's response, and so reads "brightness" without an optical filter. A silicon photodiode peaks near 900 nm and extends past 1 µm, so it sees IR the eye cannot: sunlight, incandescent lamps and IR remote LEDs add error. Ambient light sensors fix this with filter coatings or dual-diode (visible minus IR) schemes. So the LDR's "free" eye-like response is a point in its favour that the diode must pay for with an optical filter.

## Environment and compliance

- **Temperature**: LDR dark resistance and sensitivity change a few tenths of a percent per °C, and more at the extremes (device-dependent); photodiode dark current doubles about every 8 to 10 °C.
- **Cadmium**: CdS cells contain cadmium, restricted by RoHS in many applications; check the exemptions and your market before designing around one. Lead-free CdS alternatives exist but are less common.
- **Ageing**: LDRs can drift after long illumination or humidity exposure; photodiodes are stable but filters (dye) can bleach.

## Design decision, worked example

*Product: outdoor garden lamp, turns on at dusk (about 10 lux), off at dawn (about 30 lux), battery powered, cost-critical.*
- LDR (10 kΩ at 10 lux) with a 10 kΩ divider and a MCU comparator/ADC: V_out = 1.65 V at 10 lux, about 2.26 V at 30 lux. Hysteresis gives clean switching, 100 ms slowness suppresses headlight flicker, cost a few cents.
- Photodiode alternative: need a TIA with 0.67 mV/lux, so at 10 lux 6.7 mV, 30 lux 20 mV; an ADC LSB of 0.8 mV gives about 1 lux/LSB, good, but the amplifier offset (hundreds of µV to mV) and temperature drift now matter, plus more parts and quiescent current.
- **Choice**: LDR, with the compliance check; or an integrated ambient light sensor IC (I2C, a few µA) if cadmium is a problem.

*Product: an optical smoke detector or a pulse-ox sensor.* Speed, linearity, ambient rejection and calibration repeatability matter: photodiode only.

## Real hardware behaviour

- **LDR hysteresis in the dark**: after daytime exposure, dark resistance rises slowly; the "dusk" threshold shifts depending on the day's brightness history unless the design allows for it.
- **Photodiode leakage on the PCB**: flux residue or humidity gives nA leakage on the high-impedance node; guard rings and clean assembly matter at pA levels.
- **Amplifier selection**: low input bias current op-amps (pA) are needed for photovoltaic-mode TIAs at high $R_f$.
- **Lens and cover**: both devices need a calibrated optical path; plastic windows have spectral transmission.
:::

::: applications
- **Dusk-to-dawn lamps and toys**: CdS photocells with a divider and a comparator.
- **Camera light meters and ambient light sensors**: photodiodes with a TIA or integrating readout.
- **Optical receivers and remote control decoders**: photodiodes (fast).
- **Smoke detectors and pulse oximeters**: photodiodes with modulation and lock-in.
- **Streetlight controllers**: photoresistors or photodiode IC sensors with time delay.
- **Solar trackers**: photodiode pairs and quadrant cells for direction.

::: followups
### Why is an LDR's response often described as logarithmic?
Its resistance follows a power law $R\propto E^{-\gamma}$ with $\gamma<1$, so over decades it behaves like a compression. In a divider, the output voltage changes by a roughly constant amount per decade over the middle of the range, which resembles a log. It is not truly logarithmic, and the slope flattens at the extremes.

### How would you choose R_F for an LDR divider?
Pick it near the LDR's resistance at the light level where you want maximum sensitivity (the divider's slope is steepest when $R_F\approx R_{LDR}$). For a dusk threshold at 10 lux with $R_{LDR}=10$ kΩ, $R_F=10$ kΩ. Then check the source impedance against the ADC's requirement and the current drain (3.3 V / 20 kΩ = 165 µA, so for a battery product use a higher value or power the divider only when sampling).

### When would a phototransistor be a good compromise?
When you want more signal than a photodiode without a TIA: a phototransistor multiplies photocurrent by β (about 100), so a few hundred µA of output can drive a resistor load. It is slower (µs to tens of µs), less linear and has a wider temperature coefficient. Typical in optical encoders, interrupters and IR receivers where speed is moderate.

### What if the photodiode TIA output is correct in the lab but noisy in the product?
High-impedance node: look at layout and shielding (guard ring, short trace), interference from switching regulators and flicker of the lamp, and the loop's stability with the cover and PCB capacitance (peaking). Add a feedback capacitor, a low-pass filter, a shield, and check ambient light flicker with a scope.

### Scenario: customers report the night light turns on late and off late after a sunny day. Why, and how do you fix it?
The LDR has a light-history effect: after strong illumination its resistance relaxes slowly, so at dusk it still reads "bright" for minutes. Fix with a design that tolerates it (lower threshold with hysteresis, time delay, comparison against a slowly adapted baseline), a different LDR with a faster recovery, or switch to a photodiode sensor.
