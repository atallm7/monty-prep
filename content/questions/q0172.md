::: basics
### In plain words
Once you know the order rails must start in, you need hardware to enforce it. The simplest method is to let one regulator's "I am ready" signal switch on the next regulator. If you need a pause between steps, you add a small resistor-capacitor delay. Bigger systems use a dedicated sequencer chip or a small controller.

### Picture it
Think of a relay race. The second runner may not start until the baton arrives, so no one has to guess a time. Adding a delay is like making the second runner count to five after receiving the baton. A weakness of the race: if the baton passes late or early, the whole chain shifts, and an RC delay can vary a lot with part tolerances.

### Words you need
- **Enable (EN) pin**: an input that turns a regulator on when its voltage rises above a threshold, often about 1 V.
- **Power-good (PG)**: an output that says the regulator's output is valid ([[q0171]]).
- **Open-drain**: an output that can only pull low, so it needs a pull-up resistor to go high ([[q0020]]).
- **RC delay**: a resistor feeding a capacitor, so the capacitor's voltage rises slowly instead of jumping.
- **Supervisor**: a chip that watches a voltage and releases a signal after a set delay.

### The simplest example
Rail A comes from regulator A. Its PG pin has a pull-up resistor to 3.3 V and also connects to the EN pin of regulator B. While A is not valid, PG pulls low and B stays off. When A is valid, PG is released and the pull-up lifts EN above its threshold, so B starts. To add delay, make the total resistance feeding EN 100 kΩ and put 100 nF from EN to ground. The time constant is $RC = 10$ ms, and EN crosses a 1.2 V threshold after about 4.5 ms. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the other tools, power-down order, and the weaknesses of each method.

::: why
Sequencing is easy to specify and surprisingly easy to get wrong in hardware: an RC delay that works at room temperature but not cold, a PG pull-up tied to a rail that is not up yet, an enable pin overdriven, or a power-down that leaves a rail floating. Engineers need a toolbox with cost, accuracy and complexity trade-offs, and knowing which to reach for (daisy chain for two or three rails, a sequencer or PMIC for many) is a practical hardware skill.
:::

::: concepts
- **Enable (EN) pin and threshold**: regulator turns on when EN exceeds a threshold, typically around 1 V, with hysteresis.
- **Power good (PG)**: usually open-drain output that is released (pulled high) when the output is valid ([[q0171]]).
- **RC delay**: $t=RC\ln\frac{V_{cc}}{V_{cc}-V_{th}}$ ([[q0004]]).
- **Soft-start and tracking pins**: set ramp rate or make one output follow another.
- **Supervisor / reset IC**: monitors a voltage and releases an output after a delay.
- **Load switch**: a pass FET with controlled slew rate that gates a rail to a load.
:::

::: answer
"There's a toolbox, from simple to flexible.

The simplest is chaining: take the power-good output of the first regulator and connect it to the enable pin of the next. PG is open-drain, so it needs a pull-up, and when rail A is valid, PG releases and enables rail B. That gives a threshold-based sequence with no timing guesses. If I need extra delay, I add an RC between PG and EN, and the delay is R times C times ln of Vcc over Vcc minus the enable threshold: 100 kilohms and 100 nanofarads gives about 4.5 milliseconds on a 3.3 volt pull-up with a 1.2 volt threshold, but the tolerance is large.

For a more controlled delay or to monitor a rail, I'd use a voltage supervisor with a programmable delay, which also drives reset. For more than three rails or complex rules, I'd use a dedicated sequencer or a PMIC, or a small CPLD or MCU driving the enables from the PG signals, with the caveat that the controller needs its own always-on supply.

Other tools: tracking pins on regulators so rails ramp together, soft-start capacitors to set the ramp time, and load switches with slew-rate control to gate a rail to a block.

For power-down I'd use the same signals in reverse and make sure the PG falls and rails discharge, perhaps with a diode across the RC to discharge fast. And I'd always verify on a scope across temperature."

::: technical
**Methods**

| Method | How it works | Good for | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PG-to-EN chain** | PG of rail $n$ (open-drain, pull-up) drives EN of rail $n+1$ | 2–4 rails, threshold-based | needs PG/EN pins; pull-up level vs EN rating; no inherent delay |
| **RC delay on EN** | $R$ from a supply or PG, $C$ to ground at EN | extra settle delay, low cost | wide tolerance; temperature dependent; slow, soft edge at EN |
| **Voltage supervisor / reset IC** | compares rail to a threshold, releases output after a fixed or capacitor-set delay | rail monitoring, reset hold | adds a part per rail; threshold choices |
| **Sequencer IC / PMIC** | multiple enables, programmable thresholds, delays, power-down order | many rails, FPGAs/SoCs | cost, configuration, a single point of failure |
| **MCU/CPLD/FPGA control** | GPIOs drive ENs after reading PG | flexible, field-programmable | needs its own always-on rail; firmware bugs; not ready at power-up |
| **Tracking (TRK/SS pins)** | slave output follows master (ratiometric or simultaneous) | core/I/O differential limits, DDR | need compatible parts; accuracy of dividers |
| **Soft-start capacitor** | $t_{ss}=C_{ss}V_{ref}/I_{ss}$ sets ramp time | controlling ramp rate and inrush | does not set order by itself |
| **Load switches** | enable pin + slew control gates a rail | gating subsystem rails, power-down | $R_{on}$ drop; extra part |

**RC delay equation.** An RC from $V_{cc}$ (through a pull-up or PG release) charging $C$ crosses the EN threshold $V_{th}$ at
$$t_d=RC\ln\frac{V_{cc}}{V_{cc}-V_{th}}.$$
With $R=100$ kΩ (pull-up plus series), $C=100$ nF, $V_{cc}=3.3$ V and $V_{th}=1.2$ V: $t_d=4.52$ ms. Spread: $V_{th}$ of 1.1–1.3 V gives 4.05–5.01 ms. With 20% capacitor tolerance and 1% resistors, the extremes are about 3.2 ms to 6.1 ms. Thus this is a *minimum* settling delay, not a precision timer.

**UVLO via EN divider.** To start a 12 V regulator at 9 V with a 1.2 V EN threshold: $R_1/R_2=9/1.2-1=6.5$, e.g. $R_2=10$ kΩ, $R_1=65$ kΩ (use a standard value such as 64.9 kΩ and check hysteresis).

**Tracking.** Ratiometric: slave $=(V_{S,nom}/V_{M,nom})\times V_M$ so both reach nominal together (e.g. 1.8/3.3 = 0.545). Simultaneous: slave $=V_M$ until it reaches its own nominal, then holds. Implemented by feeding a divided copy of the master into the slave's TRK pin (same divider ratio as the slave's FB divider for simultaneous).

**Power-down.** Reverse order using PG falling edges, plus discharge paths so the later rails do not linger; a fast-discharge diode across the RC resistor lets $C$ empty quickly when PG pulls low.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> EN thresholds (about 1.0–1.3 V), PG output behaviour (open-drain vs push-pull, voltage ratings, valid down to what supply) and tracking-pin behaviour differ between regulators. Always check the EN absolute maximum (it often must not exceed $V_{IN}$ or a rated value) and the PG pull-up supply.
:::

::: long
"I think of it as a toolbox, from the simplest to the most flexible.

The simplest is PG to EN chaining. Most regulators have a power-good pin, usually open-drain, and an enable pin. If I connect the PG of the first regulator, with a pull-up, to the EN of the second, the second starts only when the first is valid. It's threshold-based, so it adapts to load and temperature, there's no timing guess and almost no cost. I have to check that the pull-up voltage is within the EN pin's rating and that PG is valid down to low input voltage so the second rail stays off when the first isn't up.

If I want extra settling time, I add an RC between PG and EN. The delay is R C times ln of Vcc over Vcc minus Vth. With 100 kilohms, 100 nanofarads, 3.3 volts and a 1.2 volt threshold, that's about 4.5 milliseconds, but the threshold, capacitor and temperature move it by roughly plus or minus 30 percent, so I treat it as a minimum, not a precise timer.

For a defined delay or to monitor a rail, I'd use a voltage supervisor or reset IC, which compares the rail against a threshold and releases an output after a fixed or capacitor-programmed delay. That's also how I hold the processor in reset.

When there are many rails, like an FPGA with five or six, or complex rules, I'd use a sequencer IC, a PMIC, or a CPLD or small MCU that reads PG pins and drives enables. The catch is that the controller needs its own always-on supply and must be correct from reset.

Other building blocks: tracking pins, so slave rails ramp with a master; soft-start capacitors that set the ramp time and so limit inrush; and load switches with slew-rate control to gate a rail to a subsystem.

Finally, power-down. I reverse the order using PG falling edges, with discharge paths so rails don't float, perhaps a diode across the RC to discharge fast. And I verify it all on a scope across hot, cold and slow and fast input ramps."
:::

::: terms
- **EN (enable)**: pin that turns a regulator on/off, with a threshold and often hysteresis.
- **PG / PWRGD**: power-good flag, commonly open-drain.
- **Open-drain output**: pulls low actively and relies on a pull-up resistor to go high.
- **Supervisor**: IC that monitors a voltage and asserts reset or releases enable after a delay.
- **Sequencer**: IC (or logic) that controls multiple enables with programmable thresholds and delays.
- **Ratiometric / simultaneous tracking**: ramp proportionally / ramp at the same voltage.
- **Soft-start**: controlled ramp of the output.
- **Load switch**: integrated pass FET controlling a rail with slew-rate control.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Pull-up to a rail that isn't up yet.** PG never goes high, or it back-feeds; choose the pull-up supply deliberately.
- **Exceeding the EN pin rating.** Pulling EN to 5 V when its absolute max is $V_{IN}$ or 3.6 V can damage the regulator.
- **Trusting an RC as a precise delay.** Capacitor tolerance, temperature, threshold spread and leakage shift it.
- **A slow, soft edge at EN.** With little hysteresis it can chatter; check the EN pin's hysteresis and add a Schmitt/supervisor if needed.
- **Sequencing up but not down.** A rail that lingers after the order is violated can back-power a domain on power-off.
- **Putting the sequencer on a rail that comes up late.** The controller must be alive first, on an always-on supply.
:::

## Method 1: PG-to-EN daisy chain

Most DC-DC regulators and many LDOs have an EN pin and a PG pin. To chain:

1. Tie the first regulator's EN to $V_{in}$ (through a UVLO divider if needed).
2. Pull PG up through $R_{pu}$ (10–100 kΩ) to a rail that is *valid whenever PG must be valid*, often the first regulator's own input if EN-compatible, or an always-available logic rail. PG is typically only guaranteed valid down to a minimum input voltage, so check.
3. Connect PG to the next regulator's EN. Check that the pull-up voltage is below EN's absolute maximum and above its high threshold.
4. Repeat down the chain.

Advantages: no fixed delay guess, adapts to temperature and load, cost is only a resistor. Limitations: the sequence is serial (each stage adds its own soft-start plus PG delay), PG thresholds may be loose (e.g. 90% with tolerance), and PG may glitch during load transients unless it is filtered.

## Method 2: RC delay at EN

To add a settling delay, put a series $R_d$ and capacitor $C_d$ at EN, as in the figure. The EN voltage rises as
$$v_{EN}(t)=V_{cc}\left(1-e^{-t/\tau}\right),\qquad\tau=(R_{pu}+R_d)C_d,$$
crossing the threshold $V_{th}$ at $t_d=\tau\ln\frac{V_{cc}}{V_{cc}-V_{th}}$.

Figure: With $R=100$ kΩ and $C=100$ nF ($\tau=10$ ms) the 1.2 V threshold is crossed at 4.52 ms, only 0.45τ, so the rise is far from complete. Because the slope is fairly flat at the crossing, small changes in threshold or capacitance cause large time changes: a 1.1 V to 1.3 V threshold gives 4.05–5.01 ms, and adding 20% capacitor tolerance and temperature moves it from about 3.2 to 6.1 ms.

Design hints: choose $R$ high enough to keep PG and pull-up currents small but low enough that leakage (PCB, capacitor, EN input) does not matter; choose $C$ as C0G/X7R with known tolerance; add a **diode across $R_d$** (cathode toward PG) so that $C_d$ discharges quickly when PG pulls low (otherwise power-down or a short PG glitch will not reset the delay); and if EN has no hysteresis, add a Schmitt buffer.

**UVLO via EN divider.** A divider $R_1$ (top) and $R_2$ (bottom) at EN sets the input voltage at which a regulator starts: $V_{on}=V_{EN,th}(1+R_1/R_2)$. For 9 V start with $V_{EN,th}=1.2$ V: $R_1/R_2=6.5$, e.g. 64.9 kΩ and 10 kΩ. Different regulators in the system should be given UVLO thresholds that guarantee the intended order when the input ramps slowly (e.g., core regulator starts at a lower $V_{in}$ than the I/O regulator).

## Method 3: supervisors and reset ICs

A voltage supervisor compares a monitored voltage against a threshold and releases its output after a delay (fixed or set by a capacitor). Used for: (a) holding the processor in reset until all rails are valid (monitor the last rail, or combine PGs with an AND gate), (b) giving a delayed enable to a downstream regulator, (c) detecting brownout and asserting reset. Advantages: accurate threshold (about 1%) and defined delay. Costs: one part per monitored rail (multi-rail supervisors exist).

## Method 4: sequencer ICs, PMICs and controllers

For many rails, a sequencer IC has programmable thresholds, delays and enable outputs, and often a power-down order. A PMIC integrates regulators and sequencing and is configured by OTP or I²C. A CPLD/FPGA/MCU can implement arbitrary logic: read PG signals, apply delays, drive ENs, handle faults. Considerations: the controller needs its own always-on supply and must come up in a known state (its GPIOs should default to "enables off"); firmware bugs become hardware damage; and boot order must still be correct if the main processor *is* the sequencer (the chicken-and-egg problem: it needs rails to run).

Implementation: feed the slave's TRK pin from a divider off the master output (ratio equal to the slave's FB divider for simultaneous tracking) and choose the master's soft-start time $t_{ss}=C_{ss}V_{ref}/I_{ss}$ to meet the inrush and ramp-time window.

## Method 6: load switches and soft-start

If you cannot control a regulator's EN (or the rail feeds several blocks), a **load switch** gates the rail to a subsystem. Its EN comes from a PG or an MCU, and its slew-rate (set by a capacitor or fixed) limits inrush: $I=C_{load}\,dV/dt$, e.g. 100 µF in 2 ms at 3.3 V is 165 mA. Cost: the switch's $R_{on}$ drop and a part, but it also provides a clean power-down path, since the block can be fully disconnected.

## Power-down sequencing

- **Reverse order via PG:** when the first rail's PG falls, it disables the next, etc. A diode across the RC resistor lets $C_d$ discharge quickly.
- **Discharge paths:** active discharge pins or bleed resistors so rails do not float at partial voltages; important for back-powering and for restart after a quick power cycle.
- **Hold-up:** for a controlled shutdown after input loss, bulk capacitors store $E=\tfrac12C(V_1^2-V_2^2)$ so lower-priority rails can fall in order. The supervisor must trip *before* the rails sag below their regulation range.
- **Brownout:** a supervisor asserts reset when a rail drops below its threshold, forcing a controlled state.

## Choosing and verifying

| Situation | Choice |
|---|---|
| 2–3 rails, simple order | PG-to-EN chain (+ RC if a settle delay is needed) |
| Needs accurate delay or reset | voltage supervisor |
| 4+ rails or programmable rules | sequencer IC, PMIC, or CPLD/MCU |
| Rails must stay within a $\Delta V$ | tracking pins or simultaneous soft-start |
| Gating subsystems at runtime | load switches |

Verify: scope all EN, PG, rails and RESET together; trigger on the first enable; capture power-up and power-down; test with slow and fast input ramps, cold and hot, and a rapid power cycle; check each datasheet condition.

::: applications
- **FPGA boards:** a sequencer IC or PMIC with per-rail PG and power-down control.
- **Two-rail MCU + sensor boards:** PG-to-EN chaining with a small RC.
- **DDR memory subsystems:** tracking VDDQ and VTT, enabling VTT after VDDQ valid.
- **Mixed-signal boards:** analog rail enabled after its reference supply is valid via a load switch.
- **Power-sensitive IoT devices:** load switches gating sensors and radios, controlled by the MCU.
- **Rack and backplane cards:** hot-swap controllers combined with sequencing for ordered turn-on.

::: assumptions
- Regulators with EN and PG pins (or equivalent supervisors); for devices without them, a supervisor or load switch replaces the missing feature.
- RC-delay numbers use a first-order model ignoring EN pin input current and leakage; real circuits need tolerance analysis.
- EN threshold (1.2 V), pull-up (3.3 V) and delay values are illustrative.
- The "MCU as sequencer" option assumes the MCU is powered from an always-on rail and starts in a known-safe state.
- PG definitions differ: some parts assert PG only after a delay, some have a fixed deglitch; check the datasheet and add margin.

::: followups
### What if the regulator has no power-good pin?
Use a voltage supervisor to monitor the output (it provides the PG function with a defined threshold and delay), or use a comparator, or a resistor divider into the next regulator's EN (the output voltage itself crossing the EN threshold, which is crude and tracks the output with hysteresis), or time the sequence with an RC and accept the tolerance.

### How do you stop the second rail from starting while the first is only partly up (e.g., slow input ramp)?
Set the UVLO thresholds with EN dividers so the first regulator starts at a lower input voltage than the second, use a PG that is valid only when the output is in regulation, and add a supervisor on the first rail. Then the second rail stays off until the first is valid, regardless of how slowly the input rises.

### What's wrong with using an MCU GPIO to sequence all rails?
Not wrong, but: the MCU needs its own supply that is up first; GPIOs float at reset, so I'd add pull-downs on ENs so rails stay off until firmware drives them; firmware bugs can violate sequencing; and the order during brownout or watchdog reset must be safe. For safety-critical sequencing prefer hardware (PG chain or sequencer) with MCU supervision.

### How would you sequence the power down when the input is yanked?
Use hold-up capacitance on the input or intermediate bus so the supervisor's early warning (trip above the rails' minimum) has time to disable rails in reverse order; add discharge paths so no rail lingers; make sure back-powering paths (ESD diodes) are not forward-biased by still-active I/O. In the worst case, ensure the parts are specified to tolerate uncontrolled loss.

### Scenario: sequencing works at 25 °C but fails at −20 °C. What do you suspect?
The RC delay changes: capacitors lose capacitance in the cold (and X5R/X7R DC bias effects), the EN threshold shifts, soft-start currents change and rails take longer to ramp. A fixed delay is then too short. Switch to PG-based sequencing with a defined settling delay, or increase the margin, and re-check at temperature extremes.
:::
