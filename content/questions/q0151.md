::: testing
Whether you can connect *voltage excursions on a supply* to the *physics and function of the load*, not just say "it's bad". The interviewer wants two categories of harm (undershoot causes functional/timing failure, overshoot causes stress and reliability damage), a concrete idea of **who tolerates it** (loads with margin or their own regulation) and **who does not** (low-voltage digital cores, PLLs, ADC references, RF, memory), and a sense of the magnitudes (5% of 0.8 V is only 40 mV). Strong answers also mention causes (load steps, loop response, ESL, hot-plug) and mitigation.
:::

::: why
Supply tolerance is one of the first numbers on any processor or FPGA datasheet, and failing to meet it produces the worst kind of bug: intermittent, workload-dependent and temperature-dependent. As core voltages have fallen below 1 V, a few tens of millivolts decide whether a chip meets timing. At the other end, an overshoot that exceeds an absolute maximum rating can permanently damage a part. Understanding which loads care, and why, tells you how much decoupling, loop bandwidth and margin you need ([[q0142]], [[q0143]]) and what parameters to monitor ([[q0152]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Supply tolerance**: allowed deviation of $V_{DD}$ (e.g. ±5%) including DC error, ripple and transient.
- **Load transient response**: a current step $\Delta I$ causes a droop (undershoot) and rebound (overshoot) set by the output capacitance, ESR, ESL and loop bandwidth. See [[q0143]].
- **Timing margin**: CMOS gate delay rises as $V_{DD}$ falls, so undershoot can cause setup violations. See [[q0140]].
- **Absolute maximum rating**: a stress limit beyond which damage or reduced reliability may occur, distinct from the recommended operating range.
- **Power scaling**: dynamic power $\propto V_{DD}^2$, so overshoot increases power and heat.
- **Target impedance**: $Z_{target}=\Delta V/\Delta I$ ties tolerance to PDN design. See [[q0142]].
:::

::: answer
"Undershoot and overshoot are the supply dipping below or rising above its nominal value, usually during load transients or start-up. They're undesirable for different reasons.

Undershoot is a functional problem. When the supply droops, digital gates slow down. In a 0.9 volt core with a 0.35 volt threshold, a 5% droop makes the logic about 6% slower, and if the design had less timing margin than that, you get setup violations, corrupted data or a crash. Deeper droops trigger brown-out resets or corrupt memory writes, and in analogue circuits like ADCs and PLLs the supply variation turns into error and jitter.

Overshoot is a stress and reliability problem. Pushing a device above its maximum rating can break down the gate oxide or junctions. Even if it survives, it raises power: dynamic power scales with V squared, so 5% overshoot is about 10% more power. Repeated overstress shortens life.

When can it be tolerated? When the load has wide input range or large margin: motors, heaters, simple LEDs with current limiting, or anything with its own downstream regulator. It's also tolerable if brief and within the datasheet's transient allowance.

The sensitive loads are low-voltage high-performance digital, like CPUs, FPGAs and memory, where 5% of 0.8 volts is only 40 millivolts, plus PLLs, ADC and DAC references, and RF circuits, because supply noise appears directly in their output."
:::

::: technical
**Where it comes from.** A load current step $\Delta I$ is supplied first by the decoupling capacitors, then by the regulator after its loop responds. The excursion has three terms:
$$\Delta V\approx\Delta I\cdot ESR+L\frac{di}{dt}+\frac{\Delta I}{2\pi f_cC}$$
(ESR step, inductive spike, and charge depletion before the loop catches up). A load release causes the mirror-image overshoot as the inductor current in a buck regulator is still above the new load level. Other sources: start-up overshoot, hot-plug ringing, line transients, feedback-loop underdamping, and switching ripple.

**Why undershoot hurts.** Alpha-power law: delay $\propto V_{DD}/(V_{DD}-V_{th})^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha\approx1.3$ (assumed). For $V_{DD}=0.9$ V, $V_{th}=0.35$ V:

| Droop | Extra delay |
|---|---|
| 3% | +3.6% |
| 5% | +6.1% |
| 10% | +13.5% |

For $V_{DD}=0.8$ V, $V_{th}=0.3$ V a 5% droop gives +5.9%. If the timing slack is less, paths fail. Also: SRAM write/retention margin, flash program/erase voltage windows, PLL/oscillator frequency pushing, ADC reference error, brown-out detector trips, and analog bias shifts.

**Why overshoot hurts.** Voltage above the absolute maximum rating risks gate-oxide breakdown (time-dependent dielectric breakdown), hot-carrier and bias-temperature degradation, junction breakdown or latch-up. Dynamic power rises as $V^2$: +5% voltage is +10.25% power, +10% is +21%. Overshoot also can exceed capacitor voltage ratings and stress protection devices.

**Magnitudes matter.** The same 5% tolerance is $0.05\times3.3=165$ mV on a 3.3 V rail and only $0.05\times0.8=40$ mV on a 0.8 V rail, so low-voltage cores need far lower impedance.

**Tolerant loads.** Resistive heaters, lamps, many motors, bulk LED strings with current regulators, loads with wide-input regulators (e.g. 3-5.5 V), and brief excursions allowed by the datasheet (e.g. a stated duration of ±X% transient).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Voltage tolerance bands (±3%, ±5%, transient allowances), threshold voltages and $\alpha$ vary by process and product. Use the device datasheet and the vendor's PDN guidance for actual limits.
:::

::: long
"Undershoot and overshoot are the supply dropping below or rising above its target, mainly during load transients, start-up or hot-plug. The main cause on a processor rail is a current step: capacitors supply the first few nanoseconds, there's some inductive spike, and then a droop until the regulator's loop catches up. When the load releases, you get the opposite, an overshoot, because the inductor in a buck still carries more current than the load needs.

Undershoot is undesirable because logic gets slower as the supply falls. With the alpha-power model, a 0.9 volt core with a 0.35 volt threshold slows about 6% for a 5% droop, 13 or 14% for 10%. If timing slack is smaller than that, you get setup violations and silent data errors. Below the brown-out threshold you get resets, SRAM can lose retention or have write margin problems, flash programming fails, and for analog parts, ADC references and PLLs convert supply variation directly into error and jitter.

Overshoot matters because it stresses the silicon. Going above the absolute maximum rating risks oxide breakdown, hot-carrier damage or latch-up, and even within the rating, repeated higher voltage accelerates ageing. It also costs power since dynamic power goes as V squared: 5% overshoot is about 10% extra power.

It can be tolerated when the load has a wide operating range or its own regulation downstream, like a motor, a heater, an LED string with a current source, or an IC behind an LDO. It's also fine for short events inside the datasheet's transient allowance.

The sensitive loads are the high-performance digital cores, FPGAs and DDR memory, where tolerance is typically a few percent of a sub-1-volt rail, so tens of millivolts. Then PLLs, ADC and DAC references, and RF circuits. To control it, I'd design for a target impedance, ΔV over ΔI, with enough decoupling, a fast regulator loop with good phase margin, soft start and limiting of load step rate."
:::

::: terms
- **Undershoot / droop**: transient dip of the supply below nominal.
- **Overshoot**: transient rise of the supply above nominal.
- **Tolerance band**: allowed total deviation (DC accuracy plus ripple plus transient), e.g. ±5%.
- **Load transient**: sudden change in load current.
- **Absolute maximum rating**: stress limit beyond which damage may occur.
- **Timing margin / slack**: spare time in a path before it fails setup.
- **Brown-out detector**: circuit that resets the part when the supply falls below a threshold.
- **PSRR**: power-supply rejection ratio, how well an analog circuit ignores supply variation.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "undershoot can reset the chip and overshoot can damage it, so be careful."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the **mechanisms** in numbers: delay rises about 6% for a 5% droop (alpha-power law), power rises about 10% for a 5% overshoot ($V^2$).
- Separates **functional** failures (timing, retention, resets, analog error) from **reliability** damage (oxide stress, latch-up, ageing).
- Gives who is **sensitive** (sub-1 V cores, FPGA transceivers, DRAM, PLL/ADC/RF) with the key point that tolerance is a *percentage*, so lower voltage means smaller absolute margin (40 mV at 0.8 V).
- Gives who is **tolerant** (resistive/motor/heater loads, wide-range or post-regulated loads, short events within spec).
- Connects to design: target impedance, decoupling, regulator bandwidth and phase margin, soft start, load-step limiting and measuring at the load with a proper probe.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating tolerance as DC only.** The tolerance band includes ripple and transient excursions. A rail at exactly 3.3 V on a DMM can still dip 200 mV for 100 ns.
- **Thinking overshoot is harmless because it is "more voltage".** It breaks rated limits, and even small overshoot accelerates wear-out and raises power.
- **Measuring at the regulator and not at the load.** The droop at the package pins can be larger than at the regulator output because of inductance and resistance in between.
- **Using a long ground lead** when measuring: it adds an inductive artefact. Use a ground spring.
- **Assuming all loads care equally.** A heater doesn't; a PLL does. Prioritise.
- **Fixing it only with more bulk capacitance.** High-frequency excursions need low-inductance ceramics and short loops, not just microfarads.
:::

::: deep
## What causes the excursions

A power rail is a regulator, an output filter and a path to the load. When the load current steps, things happen in sequence, each dominating in a different time range:

1. **Nanoseconds**: the die's own capacitance and the nearest ceramics supply the current. The voltage dip is limited by the loop inductance $L\,di/dt$.
2. **Hundreds of nanoseconds to microseconds**: the bulk and mid-value capacitors supply the charge; the voltage falls as $\Delta Q/C$.
3. **Microseconds**: the regulator's control loop responds and the inductor current ramps up to the new load level. Undershoot ends when inductor current equals load current.
4. **After a load release**: the inductor holds more current than the load needs, so the excess charges the capacitor and the voltage overshoots until the loop reduces the current.

The first-order sum of the three terms is

$$\Delta V\approx\underbrace{\Delta I\cdot ESR}_{\text{resistive step}}+\underbrace{L_{loop}\frac{di}{dt}}_{\text{inductive}}+\underbrace{\frac{\Delta I}{2\pi f_cC}}_{\text{charge before loop reacts}}$$

Each term has its own cure: ESR (low-ESR capacitors), inductance (placement and via design, [[q0509]]), loop speed and $C$ (regulator design, [[q0143]]).

## Why undershoot hurts

**Digital timing.** CMOS gate delay follows roughly $t_d\propto V_{DD}/(V_{DD}-V_{th})^{\alpha}$ (alpha-power law, $\alpha\approx1.3$ for modern short-channel devices). Lowering $V_{DD}$ shrinks the overdrive $V_{DD}-V_{th}$ and delay rises faster than linearly.

Lower $V_{th}$ or a higher $V_{DD}$ flattens the curve; a low-voltage design near threshold is far more sensitive.

**Other undershoot failure modes.**
- **Brown-out reset**: the reset supervisor trips at its threshold; repeated chatter if the dip is brief but deep.
- **SRAM and registers**: write margin and retention degrade at low voltage.
- **Flash write/erase**: needs a stable window; a droop during programming can corrupt data.
- **Analog and mixed-signal**: ADC reference and bias shifts cause code errors; PLL/VCO supply sensitivity turns noise into jitter (supply pushing); amplifiers lose headroom.
- **Interfaces**: transceiver supplies affect eye height and jitter.

## Why overshoot hurts

- **Absolute maximum ratings.** Exceeding them risks permanent damage. Even below the maximum, voltage above the recommended range accelerates wear-out mechanisms such as time-dependent dielectric breakdown and hot-carrier or bias-temperature degradation. Datasheets typically state recommended operating voltages and limits; reliability margins are not disclosed in detail.
- **Power and heat.** $P_{dyn}\propto V^2$: 5% overshoot is +10.25% power; 10% is +21%. For a CPU at 50 W this adds 5 W while the excursion lasts.
- **Protection devices.** ESD and clamp structures may conduct, and a TVS or Zener may be stressed.
- **Component ratings.** Capacitors and other parts on the rail have voltage limits too; hot-plug ringing to 2× the input can destroy 16 V parts on a 12 V bus ([[q0143]]).
- **Start-up overshoot.** A regulator without soft start can overshoot as the output rises, stressing the load during power-up.

## Who is sensitive, who is tolerant

| Load | Sensitivity | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Advanced-node CPU/FPGA core (0.7-1.0 V) | Very high | Few-percent tolerance = tens of mV; timing and reliability |
| DDR / high-speed memory and transceivers | High | Timing and eye margin depend on supply noise |
| PLLs, VCOs, clock generators | High | Supply pushing turns into jitter |
| ADC/DAC references and precision analog | High | Supply noise appears as error (limited by PSRR) |
| RF amplifiers/transceivers | High | Noise sidebands and gain variation |
| Microcontroller on 3.3 V | Moderate | ±10% typical; brown-out thresholds |
| Logic with a wide range (e.g. 1.65-5.5 V) | Lower | Larger tolerance in absolute volts |
| Motors, heaters, lamps | Low | Average power matters, brief excursions are harmless |
| LED strings with current regulation | Low | Current is regulated, not voltage |
| Loads behind their own regulator (LDO/buck) | Low | Regulator rejects it, subject to PSRR |

> [!INTERVIEW]
> "When can it be tolerated?" Name two cases and say why: "A motor or heater cares about average power, not 100 mV spikes. And a load that has its own regulator with good PSRR just sees it filtered. What I won't tolerate is anything that pushes a part past its absolute maximum, or drops a timing-critical digital core below its minimum." That shows you think in terms of function, not just rules.

## Design measures

1. **Set a target impedance** $Z_{target}=\Delta V/\Delta I$ from the tolerance and the worst-case step and design the capacitor hierarchy to meet it ([[q0142]]).
2. **Fast, well-damped regulator**: crossover at a decent fraction of switching frequency with 45-60° or more phase margin; consider adaptive voltage positioning ("load line") so full-load sits at the low end and the release overshoot has headroom.
3. **Limit $di/dt$** in software or hardware: stagger wake-ups, ramp clocks and voltage.
4. **Soft start** and sequencing ([[q0171]]) to avoid start-up overshoot.
5. **Clamp** where necessary: TVS or Zener for hot-plug and line transients.
6. **Measure properly**: at the package/pins with a short-ground-spring probe and a bandwidth-limited scope; trigger in single-shot on the load step.
:::

::: applications
- **Processor VRMs and PMICs**: adaptive voltage positioning and fast loops to hold ±3-5% on sub-1 V rails during large current steps.
- **FPGA power design**: per-rail decoupling and tolerance checks using vendor power-estimation and PDN tools.
- **Flash/EEPROM programming**: a stable supply window is needed during write and erase.
- **Mixed-signal boards**: LDO plus filtering for ADC and PLL supplies to keep supply-induced error low.
- **Hot-plug and automotive systems**: surge/overshoot limiting with TVS, hot-swap controllers and damping.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Voltage-rail context.** The question sits in the power-supply section, so the main case is DC supply rails with load transients rather than signal overshoot (ringing on logic edges).
- **Alpha-power parameters** ($V_{th}$, $\alpha$) are illustrative; the exact delay sensitivity depends on process and operating point.
- **Tolerance depends on the datasheet.** Some parts specify separate DC and transient limits, or time-limited excursions.
- **Linear small-signal estimates** for load-step droop; large steps with current limiting are nonlinear.
- **Alternative valid answers.** In analog design, overshoot/undershoot may also refer to *signal* ringing in a step response; but for a power supply the answer is about rail excursions. Some also include the possibility that undershoot below ground at an I/O pin can inject current.
:::

::: followups
### Why is a few percent tolerance harder on a 0.8 V rail than on a 3.3 V rail?
Because tolerance is relative. 5% of 3.3 V is 165 mV, but 5% of 0.8 V is 40 mV, while the current steps are often larger on the low-voltage rail. $Z_{target}=\Delta V/\Delta I$ falls, so you need much lower PDN impedance and faster regulation.

### How much does the delay of a gate change if the supply drops 5%?
With the alpha-power law ($\alpha=1.3$, $V_{DD}=0.9$ V, $V_{th}=0.35$ V) about 6% slower; for 10% droop about 13.5%. That's why timing sign-off includes a supply-droop margin on top of PVT corners.

### Can overshoot be useful or harmless?
Yes: the load may tolerate it (the datasheet allows a few percent or brief excursions); for heaters, motors or current-regulated LEDs it's irrelevant. But when the rail feeds a part near its maximum rating, even a small overshoot matters, and the cost is higher power ($\propto V^2$) and accelerated ageing.

### What causes overshoot at start-up and how do you prevent it?
The output capacitor charges too fast or the feedback loop overshoots as it settles, especially without soft start, and sometimes from a pre-biased output. Use a soft-start ramp, check the loop's phase margin, and consider monotonic start-up with sequencing ([[q0171]]).

### How would you size output capacitance for a 1 A step with a 50 mV budget at a 100 kHz crossover?
$C\approx\Delta I/(2\pi f_c\Delta V)=1/(2\pi\cdot10^5\cdot0.05)=31.8$ µF effective. Add the ESR step $\Delta I\cdot ESR$ and derate for DC bias and tolerance, so I'd use around 50-100 µF of ceramic.

### Scenario: a board resets only when the GPU starts rendering. How do you find out if it's supply undershoot?
Probe the rail at the load (BGA via or the nearest test point) with a short ground spring, single-shot trigger on the falling edge, and look for a dip below the minimum or the brown-out threshold. If it is, check the target impedance, missing or derated capacitors, regulator transient response (loop), and whether the software can ramp up gradually.
:::

::: interpretations
"Overshoot/undershoot" in a power-supply context means excursions of the **supply voltage** from its nominal value, mainly under load transients, start-up and hot-plug. In signal-integrity contexts the same words refer to ringing on digital signals; this page is about power rails. "What type of loads are more sensitive" refers to the kind of load (digital core, analog, RF, motor, heater), not just a number.
:::
