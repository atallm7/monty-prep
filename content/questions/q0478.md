::: testing
Whether you know that a ground plane's job is to be **the return path directly under each signal**, so its layer position follows from where the signals are. The interviewer wants "layer 2, and the layer next to the bottom signal layer, adjacent to the routing layers," with the reasoning (return current hugs the trace, small loop, shielding, impedance control) and awareness of the compromises: in a 4-layer board the bottom layer may reference power, and a solid plane beats a sliced one.
:::

::: why
Most signal-integrity, EMI and crosstalk problems on a board are really return-path problems. Putting ground in the right layer is the cheapest EMI fix you'll ever make, because it costs nothing in parts. It is also a common interview filter: candidates who say "ground goes on the bottom" or "any layer works" reveal that they haven't thought about where current actually flows. Questions [[q0486]] and [[q0491]] expand on what a plane is and why it's on those layers.
:::

::: concepts
- **Return current**: at high frequency the return path under a signal is the path of lowest inductance, directly beneath the trace. See [[q0486]].
- **Loop area/inductance**: smaller loop, less radiation, less crosstalk, less ground bounce. See [[q0489]].
- **Reference plane**: the plane a signal's characteristic impedance is defined against. See [[q0479]].
- **Plane pairing**: ground close to power gives decoupling capacitance. See [[q0480]].
- **Shielding by planes**: a plane between two signal layers isolates them. See [[q0490]].
- **Continuity**: a plane with gaps or splits is no longer a good reference. See [[q0493]].
:::

::: answer
"Ground should go on the layers directly adjacent to the signal layers, so every signal has a solid return right underneath it.

On a four-layer board that means layer 2: signal on top, ground right below it, then power, then the bottom signal layer. I like having ground that close to the top because the dielectric between them is thin, which gives me tight 50 ohm traces and a small return loop. On a six-layer board I'd have ground on layer 2 and layer 5, so both outer layers have a ground plane next to them, and the inner signal layers sit between a ground and a power plane. On eight layers I'd add more ground planes so every signal layer is next to at least one.

The reasons are all about return current. At high frequency, the return current flows in the plane directly under the trace, because that's the lowest inductance path. A small loop radiates less, couples less to neighbours, and gives clean, predictable impedance. A ground plane between two signal layers also shields them from each other. The key requirement is that it's solid. If I cut it with slots or route across a gap, the return current detours and I lose the benefit."
:::

::: technical
**Where the ground goes**

| Layers | Ground planes | Logic |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Bottom (as a pour) | The only plane; keep it unbroken under critical traces |
| 4 (S/G/P/S) | L2 | Adjacent to the top signals; bottom signals reference power |
| 4 (S/G/G/S) | L2 and L3 | Both outer layers have a ground neighbour; power by pours |
| 6 (S/G/S/P/G/S) | L2 and L5 | Each outer layer has a ground neighbour; inner signal layer between G and P |
| 8 | L2, L4/L5, L7 (varies) | Every signal layer next to at least one plane |

**Why adjacent matters.** For a trace at height $h$ over a plane, high-frequency return current density spreads as $J(x)=\frac{I}{\pi h}\frac{1}{1+(x/h)^2}$. The fraction within $\pm k h$ is $\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan k$: 79.5% within $\pm3h$. A trace 0.2 mm from the plane has 80% of its return within about ±0.6 mm. The same trace over a plane 1.6 mm away spreads it over ±5 mm.

**Loop inductance.** A microstrip's inductance per length $L' = Z_0\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/c$. For a 0.3 mm trace, that is about 0.17 nH/mm at $h = 0.1$ mm, 0.32 nH/mm at 0.2 mm, and 0.73 nH/mm at 1.6 mm (IPC-2141 approximation, $\varepsilon_r=4.3$).

**Crosstalk proxy.** A simple coupling estimate $k\approx 1/(1+(s/h)^2)$ for spacing $s=0.3$ mm: $k=0.10$ at $h=0.1$ mm, 0.31 at 0.2 mm, 0.64 at 0.4 mm and 0.92 at 1 mm.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The IPC-2141 formulas are approximations (and strictly valid within limited $w/h$ ranges); the coupling proxy is a first-order engineering heuristic, not a field-solver result. The trends (smaller $h$ gives less inductance, less spreading and less crosstalk) are reliable. The exact values are not.
:::

::: short
"Ground goes directly next to the signal layers: layer 2 on a four-layer board, layers 2 and 5 on a six-layer. High-frequency return current flows in the plane right under the trace, so a close, solid ground gives a small loop, less radiation and crosstalk, and predictable impedance. A ground plane between two signal layers also shields them. The plane has to be continuous, so I don't cut it with slots or route across gaps."
:::

::: long
"The way I decide where ground goes is by asking where each signal's return current wants to flow, because ground has to be there.

At low frequency, return current takes the lowest resistance path. At high frequency, which in practice means edges faster than a few nanoseconds, it takes the lowest inductance path, and that's directly underneath the signal trace in the nearest plane. So I want a continuous ground plane immediately adjacent to every signal layer.

On a four-layer board that gives me signal, ground, power, signal, so ground is layer 2. The prepreg between layers 1 and 2 is thin, maybe 0.1 to 0.2 millimetres, so top traces are tightly coupled to ground, the impedance is easy to control, and the return loop is small. On a six-layer board, I'd put ground on layer 2 and layer 5, so each outer layer has a ground neighbour and the inner signal layer is between ground and power. At eight or more layers I'd keep adding ground planes so every signal layer is adjacent to one.

Secondary benefits: a solid plane between two signal layers shields them from each other, and a ground plane next to a power plane gives distributed capacitance.

The caveat is continuity. A ground plane with a slot or a gap under a fast signal forces the return current to detour around it, which makes a big loop, radiates, and couples into everything nearby. So I check every high-speed net for a solid reference, and when a signal changes layers I put a ground via nearby."
:::

::: terms
- **Return current**: the current flowing back to the source, in the plane closest to the signal at high frequency.
- **Reference plane**: the plane that defines a trace's impedance and return path.
- **Loop inductance**: inductance of the signal-plus-return loop; smaller when the plane is closer.
- **Plane split/slot**: a gap in the copper that breaks the return path.
- **Stitching via**: via that connects ground planes together, or provides a return path at a layer change.
- **Microstrip/stripline**: outer-layer trace over one plane / inner trace between two planes.
- **Plane pair**: power and ground planes close together, giving distributed capacitance.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer gives the *principle* (ground adjacent to signal layers because return current takes the least-inductance path) rather than just "layer 2". It also gives the exceptions and caveats: the S/G/P/S bottom layer references power; S/G/G/S for better SI; continuity matters more than layer number; when signals change layers the return needs a nearby stitch via; 2-layer boards use the bottom pour. A number helps: "79% of the return current is within ±3h."
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Ground goes on the bottom layer."** That's true for a 2-layer board only; on multilayer boards the best position is next to the signals.
- **Using the thick core between a signal layer and its plane** (e.g., signal on L1 and ground on L3 with the plane on L2 being power). The return gets far away.
- **Treating ground as "just a connection."** At high frequency it is a distributed conductor and a transmission-line half.
- **Cutting the plane with a trench of vias or slots** under fast signals. Anti-pad overlap from many vias can create a slot.
- **Believing one ground plane is enough on any board.** In 6+ layer boards each signal layer should have a ground neighbour.
- **Leaving ground only on outer-layer pours** and calling it a ground plane. A pour interrupted by traces is not a reference plane.
:::

::: deep
## Starting from the physics: where does the return current go?

Every signal current has to complete a loop. For a trace over a plane, the loop closes through the plane. At DC and very low frequency the return current spreads out across the plane to minimize resistance. But once frequency rises above roughly $f \gtrsim R/(2\pi L)$, inductance dominates, and the current follows the path that minimizes the *loop inductance*, which is the one directly under the trace (the current and its return form a tight pair of opposite currents whose magnetic fields cancel).

For a signal at height $h$ above an infinite plane, the surface current density is

$$J(x)=\frac{I}{\pi h}\cdot\frac{1}{1+(x/h)^2}$$

and the fraction within $\pm k h$ of the trace is $\frac{2}{\pi}\arctan k$: 50% in $\pm h$, 79.5% in $\pm3h$, 87% in $\pm5h$, 94% in $\pm10h$. See the plot in [[q0476]].

So the question "which layer should be ground" becomes: *which layer makes $h$ small for every signal layer?*

Even though inductance varies only logarithmically, crosstalk and radiation depend on the *spread* of return current and the area of the loop, and both get worse faster than $L'$ does.

## Layer by layer

**2-layer.** One side is signal, the other is a ground pour as complete as possible. Route critical nets on the signal side over the unbroken ground. The weakness: the board is 1.6 mm thick, so $h=1.6$ mm and traces are wide for 50 Ω (about 3 mm).

**4-layer S/G/P/S.** Ground is L2, so the top is nicely referenced. The bottom references the power plane L3 (which is an AC ground only because decoupling capacitors tie it to ground). The thick core separates the two planes. Keep fast signals on top, or use S/G/G/S if the bottom also has fast nets.

**6-layer S/G/S/P/G/S.** Ground on L2 and L5. The top references L2, the bottom references L5, and L3 sits between L2 (ground) and L4 (power). Power and ground at L4/L5 form a coupled pair.

**8-layer and up.** Add planes. A common pattern is one ground plane per signal layer, sometimes each stripline layer between two grounds.

## Continuity beats layer choice

A ground plane next to the trace is useless if the return current can't use it. Three common ways it is broken:

1. **Slots and splits** (e.g., power planes split into islands next to a ground-referenced signal).
2. **Via fields**: a row of through vias with overlapping anti-pads makes a slot.
3. **Layer changes** without a nearby return via.

If the return can't follow the trace, it detours; the loop area grows and the inductance and radiation grow with it. See [[q0493]] for the legitimate cases where ground is deliberately separated.

## Ground as a shield

A ground plane between two signal layers isolates them: the field from one terminates on the plane rather than the other layer. This is why, in a six-layer stack, an inner signal layer isn't placed directly beside another signal layer without a plane between them. See [[q0490]].

> [!INTERVIEW]
> The one-liner interviewers like: "Return current takes the path of least impedance, which at high frequency is the path of least inductance, directly under the trace. So I put solid ground right next to every signal layer."

## Worked example

A 4-layer board has 50 Ω USB traces on top. With 0.2 mm prepreg to L2 ground, a 0.335 mm trace is 50 Ω. If I had instead put power on L2 and ground on L3, the trace would still be referenced to the L2 power plane, so its return would use whatever AC-coupled path the power plane offers, probably through nearby decoupling capacitors. That works as long as the capacitors are close, but it's more fragile. So: ground on L2.

## Where the return current switches from resistive to inductive

The claim that "return current hugs the trace at high frequency" has a crossover, and it is worth computing so you know when it matters. A 0.335 mm by 35 µm copper trace has a resistance per length of

$$R' = \frac{\rho}{wt} = \frac{1.72\times10^{-8}}{(0.335\times10^{-3})(35\times10^{-6})} = 1.47\ \Omega/\text{m} = 1.47\ \text{m}\Omega/\text{mm}$$

and the loop inductance per length over a 0.2 mm dielectric is about 0.30 nH/mm. The loop's resistive and inductive impedances are equal at

$$f_x = \frac{R'}{2\pi L'} = \frac{1.47\times10^{-3}}{2\pi(0.30\times10^{-9})} \approx 0.78\ \text{MHz}$$

Below roughly a megahertz the return current is spread out by resistance (it takes the cheapest path, which may be far from the trace); above it, the loop inductance dominates and the current collapses under the trace. This is a rough estimate that counts only the signal trace's own resistance; the plane's resistance modifies the exact number, but the order of magnitude, around 1 MHz, is the useful part. Two practical consequences: audio and slow analog signals do not care whether the plane is directly beneath them (see [[q0493]] for when separation is deliberate), while any edge with content above a few megahertz does, which includes the fast edges on a "slow" digital clock.

## Worked example: the return current at a layer change

Consider a fast signal that starts on the top layer of an S/G/P/S board (referenced to ground, L2) and drops through a via to the bottom layer (referenced to power, L3). The return current has to hop from the ground plane to the power plane. It can do that in two ways: through a ground stitching via placed next to the signal via plus a decoupling capacitor between planes, or by displacement current through the plane capacitance, which in a 4-layer board with a 1.1 mm core is small (about 0.022 nF/in², from [[q0476]]). Say the signal via and a ground via 1 mm away carry the transition. The loop inductance of the via pair, with 0.15 mm via radius over the 1.6 mm board thickness, is approximately

$$L_{loop} \approx \frac{\mu_0 h}{\pi}\ln\frac{s}{r} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(1.6\times10^{-3})}{\pi}\ln\frac{1}{0.15} = 1.21\ \text{nH}$$

At a 400 MHz harmonic, that is $2\pi(4\times10^{8})(1.21\times10^{-9}) = 3.1\ \Omega$ of series impedance in the return. If the nearest ground via is 5 mm away instead, $\ln(5/0.15)$ gives 2.24 nH and 5.6 Ω. With a 100 mA transition over 1 ns, the loop-inductance voltage $L\,di/dt$ is 0.12 V versus 0.22 V. That voltage is not harmless: it appears as noise injected onto the reference planes, which the rest of the board and any attached cable then radiate.

A rule that falls out of this: place a ground via within about 1-2 mm of every high-speed signal via that changes reference planes, and prefer designs where the signal stays on the layer that sees ground, so no plane hop is needed.

## How far apart should stitching vias be?

Stitching vias between ground planes (and around a board edge) behave as a fence: the planes form a cavity, and a via spacing larger than a fraction of a wavelength lets energy leak out or resonate between them. The plane-cavity wavelength at 400 MHz in FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r\approx4.3$) is $\lambda = c/(f\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}) = 362$ mm, so a common rule of thumb of $\lambda/20$ gives about 18 mm. Highest frequency of concern sets the spacing: for 1 GHz content, $\lambda/20 \approx 7$ mm. This is a rule of thumb, not a standard; use tighter spacing near connectors and along edges where emissions escape.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **Ground planes are not equipotential.** A 35 µm copper plane has $R_\square = \rho/t = 0.49\ \text{m}\Omega/\square$, so a path 20 squares long (for example a narrow neck 5 mm wide and 100 mm long) is about 10 mΩ and drops 10 mV at 1 A. Holes, anti-pad fields and clearances around a connector can turn a plane into a few narrow squares, which matters for sensitive analog references.
- **Via anti-pad fields.** A dense row of through vias in a plane (for example under a BGA or a connector) can perforate it into a slot. Check the plane layer for this in the CAD tool's plane view before release.
- **Edge radiation.** At the board edge a plane no longer contains the field; fast signals near the edge radiate more. Keep fast traces a few trace-heights inside the plane's edge, and stitch the edge.

## Bench verification

Measure instead of trusting the layout. A near-field H-probe over the signal path shows where return current is concentrated: with a good plane the field is strong right over the trace and weak at its sides. Probing the voltage between two ground points with a short-spring ground clip (not the long clip lead) while the board runs reveals ground bounce at a layer change: a few tens of millivolts of spikes at the edge rate means a poor return path. A TDR on a test coupon run over a deliberate slot shows the impedance discontinuity directly, which is a convincing way to teach a team why plane gaps matter.

::: applications
- **USB/Ethernet microcontroller boards**: L2 ground directly under the top layer's differential pairs.
- **Six-layer processor boards**: G on L2 and L5; DDR on layers between planes.
- **Switching regulators**: continuous ground on L2 immediately below the power stage, to make the hot loop small.
- **RF boards**: ground directly under the RF layer (often the thin RF laminate), with via fences.
- **Two-layer sensor boards**: a bottom-layer ground pour with signals on top.
:::

::: assumptions
- Signals fast enough that return current is inductance-dominated (edges ≲ a few ns), which is almost always true for modern logic.
- A typical FR-4 multilayer where the outer prepreg is thinner than the core.
- Hybrid stacks, thick-core 4-layer boards and RF designs may vary. For example, RF boards put ground as close as the thin RF laminate allows, and some high-speed backplanes surround every stripline with grounds.
- Mixed-signal boards may split some regions, but within a solid plane. See [[q0493]].
- DC and low-frequency current (power return) spreads according to resistance, not inductance, so heavy DC returns need wide copper, not just a nearby plane.
:::

::: followups
### Why isn't ground on the bottom layer the best choice for a four-layer board?
Because the top signals would then be on L1 with ground on L4, separated by 1.6 mm of board; the return loop is large and traces are wide for 50 Ω. Placing ground on L2 gives a thin dielectric to the top layer and a compact loop. The bottom layer then references power on L3 instead, which is a compromise.

### What if I need both outer layers to have good ground references on four layers?
Use S/G/G/S, with two ground planes in the middle. Both outer signal layers have a ground neighbour, and the two grounds are joined with stitching vias. Power then has to be routed as wide traces or pours on the outer layers. It's cleaner for SI/EMI but harder to route power.

### Does adding ground pours on the outer layers replace a ground plane?
No. Pours on signal layers are interrupted by traces and parts; they add shielding and extra return paths, and with stitching vias they reduce impedance, but they don't give the uniform, continuous reference a plane does. I'd use them in addition to, not instead of, planes.

### What if a high-speed signal has to cross a plane gap?
Don't, if possible. If you must, add a stitching capacitor across the gap near the crossing or route the signal on a layer that references a different continuous plane, e.g. use another layer. A gap forces the return current to detour, and the extra loop radiates.

### Scenario: your board passes at 100 MHz, but you see a strong emission at 400 MHz near a connector. How would the ground layers be involved?
Possibly the signal is crossing a plane gap, or its return current has to detour at a layer change near the connector. I'd check the layout for slots under the signals leaving the connector, check there's a ground via beside every signal via, and see whether the cable shield bonds to ground near the entry point. A near-field probe over the board would show the hot area.
:::

"Which layer(s) should be the ground layer(s)?" has a short and a long reading. The short reading is "layer 2 and the layer next to the bottom," which applies to a typical 4–6 layer board. The long reading is about *principle*: ground is placed so that each signal layer has an adjacent, continuous plane. The sibling [[q0491]] ("Which layers typically have ground planes and why?") asks the same thing from a conventions angle; [[q0480]] asks the same for power planes.
