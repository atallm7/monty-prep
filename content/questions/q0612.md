::: testing
Whether you understand the bed-of-nails as a *mechanical and electrical interface*, not just "a lot of pins." The interviewer wants: spring-loaded probes (pogo pins) mounted in a custom drilled plate, the board pulled down by vacuum or pressed by a clamshell, probes landing on test pads, wired to the tester's switching matrix. Then what it captures (everything ICT and powered/functional tests can do through the pins), what it misses, and the practical constraints: probe pitch and pad size, force, alignment tolerance, probe wear and contact resistance, high-speed signal impact, and cost per fixture.
:::

::: why
The bed-of-nails is how most high-volume boards get tested in seconds. Design choices you make, such as test pad size and pitch, which side the pads are on, spacing from tall components, tooling holes, decide whether the fixture is cheap and reliable or expensive and flaky. Knowing the mechanics also explains common production problems: intermittent contact, false fails after thousands of cycles, warped boards, and damaged pads. See test points in [[q0619]] to [[q0625]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Spring probe (pogo pin)**: plunger, spring and barrel; tip styles for different pad types.
- **Probe plate and wiring**: drilled plate holds probes at board coordinates; wires or a translator board route to the tester.
- **Vacuum or pneumatic actuation**: pulls the board onto the probes, or presses it down.
- **Fixture types**: dedicated (one board), universal (grid) with translator, clamshell (both sides), in-line.
- **Registration**: tooling holes and fiducials align board and probes ([[q0596]]).
- **Contact resistance and wear**: tips age, oxidise and collect flux.
- **Structural and functional use**: same fixture can host ICT ([[q0609]]) and functional test ([[q0610]]).
:::

::: answer
"A bed-of-nails fixture is a custom test fixture that holds an array of spring-loaded probes, pogo pins, positioned to match the test pads on a specific board. The board sits over the probes, and a vacuum pulls it down, or a clamshell lid presses it, so every probe touches its pad at once. The probes are wired to the tester's switching matrix, which can then measure any net or component.

It's used for in-circuit test and often for functional test. What it captures is whatever the tester can measure through those contacts: shorts, opens, component values, orientation, and with power applied, powered tests of ICs and functional behaviour. It tests hundreds or thousands of nets in a few seconds, and it points to the faulty net or part.

The practical issues are mostly mechanical. Each probe presses with maybe a hundred grams, and with a thousand probes that's over a hundred kilograms of force, so the board must be supported. Probes need pads of around 0.9 mm or larger at a decent pitch, and the registration tolerance has to be within the pad radius. Tips wear out and get dirty, which raises contact resistance and causes false fails, so they're cleaned and replaced. And the fixture costs thousands and is tied to one layout, so it's for stable, higher-volume products."
:::

::: technical
**Construction.** A *probe plate* (drilled phenolic or fibre-glass composite) holds spring probes on a grid or at arbitrary positions, with receptacles beneath. Wires run from receptacles to an interface connected to the tester, or a *translator* board maps a universal grid to the board layout. A *top plate* with guide pins or tooling pins locates the board. Actuation: **vacuum** (a sealed cavity under the board is evacuated so atmospheric pressure presses the board onto the probes), **clamshell** (a lid with its own probes presses from above for two-sided access), or **pneumatic** press.

**Forces.** Each spring probe applies about 100-200 g (vendor dependent). 1,500 probes at 100 g give 150 kgf; at 200 g, 300 kgf (computed). A vacuum fixture over a 300 mm x 200 mm area at 80% of atmospheric pressure supplies $101\,325\times0.06\times0.8\approx4.9$ kN, roughly 500 kgf (computed). That comfortably exceeds the probe load, which is why vacuum fixtures work, provided the seal is good.

**Pad and pitch.** Test pads typically 0.9 mm (35 mil) or larger for standard probes; standard probe centres 2.54 mm (100 mil), then 1.91 mm (75 mil) and 1.27 mm (50 mil) with smaller, more fragile probes; finer probes exist at higher cost. A 100-mil grid over a 300 x 200 mm board has roughly 9,300 grid points (computed), so pad density is rarely the limit, but component keep-outs are.

**Alignment budget.** Worst-case stack-up: board tooling hole tolerance 0.05 mm, fixture drilling 0.05 mm, probe tip eccentricity or plunger play 0.05 mm gives ±0.15 mm, compared with a 0.9 mm pad (0.45 mm radius), so the tip still lands on the pad with 0.3 mm margin (computed). For fine-pitch pads the same stack-up becomes critical.

**What it captures.** Through the pins, the tester runs: opens/shorts, resistor, capacitor, inductor and diode measurements with guarding ([[q0609]]), powered analog and digital tests, vectorless tests for open IC pins, boundary scan via TAP pads, programming of on-board flash, and functional tests (power rails, I/O loopback). It does not capture function beyond the stimuli supplied, timing at speed (probe wiring adds inductance: a 15 mm probe of about 1 mm diameter is roughly 10 nH by the straight-wire estimate), or faults at nets without access.

**Wear.** Probes are rated for hundreds of thousands to a few million cycles (vendor and probe type dependent). At about 1,000 boards a day, 500,000 cycles is about 500 days (computed), but contamination from no-clean flux residue shortens useful life, so cleaning and contact-resistance monitoring are routine.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Probe force, pitch capabilities, cycle life and fixture costs vary with the probe family and fixture vendor. Treat all numbers here as typical, and check the vendor's DFT guidelines before layout.
:::

::: long
"A bed-of-nails fixture is a custom mechanical interface between the board and the tester. Imagine a plate drilled with a hole for every test pad on the board, and in each hole a spring-loaded probe, a pogo pin, wired back to the tester. The board drops onto guide pins over the probes, and a vacuum underneath, or a clamshell lid from above, pulls or pushes the board down so every probe contacts its pad at the same time.

Once the contacts are made, the tester's switching matrix can connect measurement instruments to any net. That's how ICT checks shorts, opens, resistor, capacitor and diode values and polarity. With power applied through some of the pins, it can do powered analog and digital tests, program flash, run boundary scan, and in a functional fixture, run the whole product test.

The capturing power is bounded by access: if a net has no pad, the fixture can't see it. And it doesn't test timing at speed, since the probes and wires add nanohenries of inductance and some stray capacitance.

The mechanical side is where fixtures get tricky. A thousand probes at about 100 grams each push with around 100 kilograms, so the board needs support pins to avoid bowing, and vacuum seals must be good. Alignment stacks tolerances: the board's tooling holes, the fixture's drilling and the probe's play. As long as the sum, say 0.15 mm, is much less than the pad radius, say 0.45 mm, contact is reliable. Probes wear out over hundreds of thousands of cycles and flux residue raises contact resistance, so you clean and replace them and monitor contact checks.

It costs thousands of dollars and is tied to a single layout, so it's for stable designs at volume."
:::

::: terms
- **Bed-of-nails**: fixture with an array of spring probes matching test pads.
- **Pogo pin / spring probe**: spring-loaded contact pin.
- **Probe plate**: drilled plate holding probes in position.
- **Translator**: board or plate converting a universal grid to board coordinates.
- **Vacuum fixture**: uses atmospheric pressure to press the board onto the probes.
- **Clamshell**: fixture with probes above and below the board.
- **Test pad / target**: probe landing area ([[q0619]]).
- **Contact resistance**: resistance of the probe-to-pad interface.
- **Support pin**: non-electrical pin limiting board deflection.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "a board with lots of pins that press on test points."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Describes construction (probe plate, probes, translator/wiring), actuation (vacuum, clamshell) and forces with numbers.
- Explains what it captures via the tester (ICT, powered, boundary scan, programming, functional) and what it misses (hidden nets, at-speed).
- Gives pad and pitch rules and an alignment tolerance budget.
- Discusses wear, contamination and contact-resistance monitoring.
- Covers economics and design implications: single-side access, keep-outs, stable layout, cost versus flying probe.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Assuming every net is testable.** Nets without a pad can't be probed.
- **Ignoring mechanical load.** Board bow from probe force can crack ceramic capacitors or lift BGAs; use support pins and keep-outs.
- **Putting test pads on both sides without need.** Two-sided access adds large cost and complexity.
- **Pads too small or too close to tall parts.** Probes need clearance for the plate and guides.
- **Ignoring contact resistance drift.** Dirty probes cause false fails that look like process problems.
- **Probing on component leads or via-in-pad without planning.** Leads move and can be damaged; dedicated pads are better.
:::

## Probe tips

| Tip style | Use |
|---|---|
| Crown (multi-point) | Pads and through-hole leads, good for flux-contaminated surfaces |
| Spear/chisel | Penetrates residue on solder |
| Flat | Plated pads and gold, low damage |
| Cup | Leads and pins that need centring |
| Concave | Solder balls/leads |

Pad finish and flux residue drive tip choice. No-clean flux residue can be an insulating film that the tip must penetrate with enough pressure; too sharp a tip damages soft pads.

## Contact and measurement quality

A spring probe adds contact resistance: tens of milliohms when clean, rising to ohms when contaminated. For low-ohm measurements (sense resistors, fuses) the tester uses *4-wire (Kelvin)* contacts with separate force and sense probes, so contact resistance drops out. Monitoring: periodically measure a known short or ground path through two probes and track the resistance; rising values trigger cleaning.

## High-speed and RF limits

A probe and its wire are an unshielded stub with inductance. A 15 mm probe is about 10 nH by the straight-wire formula (computed) and adds capacitance; at 100 MHz it has $2\pi f L = 6.3\ \Omega$ of reactance. This is why high-speed nets are tested via dedicated RF probes, test coupons, boundary scan or functional loopback, not with a standard bed-of-nails, and why designers sometimes avoid test pads on sensitive signals ([[q0623]]).

## Design rules (typical)

1. Test pad at least 0.9 mm diameter, bare copper or plated, no mask over it, at least 1.27 mm from other pads (2.54 mm for standard probes).
2. All pads on one side for a single-sided fixture.
3. Keep tall components away from pads by the plate and guide-post clearance (often 2-3 mm, vendor specific).
4. At least two tooling holes, non-plated, diameter and location per the vendor, plus fiducials ([[q0596]]).
5. Multiple pads for power and ground to reduce voltage drop and allow guarding.
6. Avoid pads on solder joints of components (they flex and have tall tops).
7. Plan board support points near large unsupported areas.

See [[q0621]] for layout guidelines in detail and [[q0622]] for when to use test points.

## Fixture economics and lifecycle

A dedicated fixture can cost several thousand USD for a mid-size board and weeks to build. A layout change that moves test pads can invalidate it. This is why the fixture is ordered once the layout is frozen, and why flying probe ([[q0611]]) is used for prototypes. With the cost model in [[q0605]], the example crossover is about 1,700 boards.

> Mention forces and tolerances with numbers: "A thousand probes at 100 g is 100 kg, so the board needs support, and my alignment stack is about 0.15 mm against a 0.45 mm pad radius." It sounds like experience and gives the interviewer something to probe.

::: followups
### Why does a bed-of-nails fixture need support pins?
Probe force on a large board deflects it, risking cracked ceramics or lifted BGA joints. Support pins under unsupported regions hold the board flat, and probe forces are kept low and balanced.

### Why can intermittent false fails appear after thousands of cycles?
Probe tips accumulate flux residue and oxidation, raising contact resistance, and spring wear lowers force. The fixture then fails good boards. Contact checks, cleaning and scheduled probe replacement fix it.

### What does a Kelvin contact add?
Separate force and sense probes at one point, so contact and wire resistance don't affect the measured voltage drop. It's needed for low-resistance measurements such as sense resistors.

### Where should test pads go on a dense double-sided board?
Put them on one side (usually the bottom), on the key nets, with one-sided fixture rules in mind. Use boundary scan and functional tests for the rest, and avoid forcing pads on sensitive signals. See [[q0624]] and [[q0625]].

### Can you test a 5 GHz signal path through a bed-of-nails?
Not through standard pogo pins, which add nanohenries of stub. You'd use dedicated RF test points with coaxial probes or connectors, loopback or built-in self-test, and rely on design verification for margins.
:::

::: interpretations
"Bed-of-nails" is the fixture; "ICT" is the test method that usually uses it. Interviewers often ask them together, so keep the distinction clear: the fixture provides access, the tester provides measurements. See [[q0609]] for the test and [[q0611]] for the fixtureless alternative.
:::
