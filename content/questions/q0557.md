::: testing
Whether you understand the *idea* behind four-wire sensing rather than the layout trick alone, and whether you can weigh it as an engineer would:

1. **What it is**: current is forced through one pair of terminals while voltage is sensed through a second, separate pair that carries almost no current.
2. **Why it works**: the unwanted resistance (solder joints, pads, copper) is in the force path, where its drop doesn't matter, not in the sense path.
3. **The trade-offs**: cost, footprint, routing effort, leftover errors in a "pseudo-Kelvin" two-terminal layout, thermal EMFs, and when it isn't worth it.
:::

::: why
The smaller the resistor, the more everything around it matters. Battery gauges, motor phases and server power rails use 0.5 to 5 mΩ shunts because they must not waste power, and at that size a solder joint or 1 mm of copper is a visible fraction of the resistor. Kelvin connections are how precision low-ohm measurements are done in labs (milliohm meters, four-point probes) and on boards. Interviewers use it to see if you can generalise: the same trick shows up in remote voltage sensing on power supplies, Kelvin source pins on power MOSFETs and four-point semiconductor probing.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ohm's law and series resistance**: unwanted series resistances add to what you measure when current flows through them. See [[q0001]].
- **Force vs sense paths**: two terminals deliver the current, two others measure the voltage. See [[q0554]].
- **High-impedance voltmeter**: the sense path carries nearly zero current, so the IR drop in the sense leads is nearly zero.
- **TCR (temperature coefficient)**: copper drifts about 0.39 %/°C; alloy shunts a lot less. See [[q0445]].
- **Thermoelectric (Seebeck) EMF**: junctions of dissimilar metals generate microvolts per degree of temperature difference.
- **Pad and solder resistance**: the contact between the resistor and the board adds a few tenths of a milliohm per end, depending on the part.
:::

::: answer
"A Kelvin connection is a four-wire connection. Two wires, the force wires, carry the current through the resistor. Two other wires, the sense wires, connect right at the resistive element and go to a high-impedance voltmeter or amplifier, so they carry almost no current. Because the sense wires carry no current, they have no voltage drop, so the voltage I read is exactly the voltage across the element, regardless of the resistance of the solder joints, pads and traces in the force path.

On a board it means either a four-terminal sense resistor, with two big current pads and two small sense pads, or, with a regular two-terminal part, tapping the sense traces at the inside edges of the pads.

The trade-offs: it's more accurate, especially for resistors below about 10 milliohms, where a solder joint or a millimetre of copper is a real fraction of the resistance, and it removes the copper's temperature coefficient from the measurement. The costs are a pricier four-terminal part or at least a larger footprint, two extra traces to route carefully as a pair, a bit more PCB area, and some leftover error in a two-terminal pseudo-Kelvin layout from current crowding in the pads. For big resistors, it's not worth it."
:::

::: technical
**Principle.** With a force current $I$ through terminals F+ and F−, and a voltmeter of input resistance $R_{in}$ at S+ and S−, the measured voltage is

$$V_{meas} = I R_s\cdot\frac{R_{in}}{R_{in}+R_{lead}} \approx I R_s\left(1 - \frac{R_{lead}}{R_{in}}\right).$$

With $R_{lead}$ = 100 mΩ and $R_{in}$ = 1 kΩ, the error is 0.01 %. Compare a 2-terminal measurement, where $V_{meas} = I(R_s + R_{contact1} + R_{contact2})$.

**Numbers.** If each solder-and-pad contact contributes about 0.2 mΩ (a rough order of magnitude; it varies with the part and the process), a 2 mΩ resistor has a 20 % error; 10 mΩ has 4 %; 100 mΩ has 0.4 %. Adding 5 mm of 1 mm wide 1 oz copper on each side adds 4.9 mΩ more. The error also drifts with temperature as copper does (0.39 %/°C).

**Implementation on PCB.**
- *4-terminal resistor*: separate sense pads connected to the element internally. The sense point is defined by the manufacturer, independent of solder.
- *2-terminal "pseudo-Kelvin"*: tap the sense traces at the inner pad edges, as in [[q0554]]. Residual error from current crowding in the pad and the exact tap geometry; it eliminates the trace and most of the pad.
- *Split-pad footprint*: each end has a force pad and a sense pad separated by a narrow gap.

**Trade-offs.**
- Accuracy and temperature stability improve most below about 10 mΩ.
- A 4-terminal part costs more and takes board space; the pads are less standardised (check the land pattern).
- Two more traces, matched, away from aggressors ([[q0555]]).
- The sense path is high-impedance, so noise pickup is the main issue.
- Thermoelectric EMF: at copper-alloy junctions, microvolts per °C of gradient (for example about 1 to 3 µV/°C for typical shunt alloys against copper; vendor-dependent), which matters at very low voltages.

**Where else:** remote sense on power supplies, MOSFET Kelvin-source pins, four-point probing, ohmmeters.
:::

::: long
"A Kelvin connection is the four-wire trick. I send the current through two terminals, the force terminals, and I measure voltage with two separate terminals, the sense terminals, connected right at the resistive element. The voltmeter or amplifier has a very high input impedance, so the sense wires carry basically no current, and with no current there's no voltage drop in them. Everything in the force path, the solder joints, the pads, the copper, drops voltage, but that voltage isn't in my measurement.

Why it matters: a good sense resistor might be 2 milliohms. A solder joint plus pad is maybe a few tenths of a milliohm at each end, and a millimetre of one ounce copper is half a milliohm. Without Kelvin I'm easily 20 to 100 percent off, and since copper has a temperature coefficient of 0.39 percent per degree against maybe 50 ppm for the shunt, the error drifts as the board heats.

On a board there are two ways. One is a true four-terminal resistor, with two big pads for the current and two small pads for sensing, so the sense point is fixed by the part. The other is a normal two-terminal resistor where I tap the sense traces off the inside edge of each pad. That's good but not perfect, because current crowds inside the pad and the exact tap position matters a little.

Trade-offs: better accuracy and temperature stability, mostly below ten milliohms. Costs: four-terminal parts are pricier, take more room and have less standard footprints; I have to route two extra traces as a matched pair away from noise; and sub-millivolt effects like thermoelectric voltages start to matter. Above, say, 50 milliohms, it usually isn't worth it."
:::

::: terms
- **Kelvin (four-wire) connection**: separate current-carrying (force) and voltage-sensing (sense) paths so that lead and contact resistance don't enter the measurement.
- **Force terminals / sense terminals**: F+/F− carry the current; S+/S− measure the voltage with negligible current.
- **Pseudo-Kelvin**: Kelvin taps made on a two-terminal part's pads; less ideal than a true four-terminal part.
- **Contact (solder joint) resistance**: the small extra resistance where the part meets the pad; a few tenths of a mΩ is common (varies).
- **TCR**: temperature coefficient of resistance; copper is much higher than shunt alloys.
- **Thermoelectric EMF (Seebeck)**: the voltage generated at junctions of dissimilar metals with a temperature difference.
- **Current crowding**: uneven current distribution inside a pad or terminal, which shifts the effective sense point.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "four-wire measurement that removes lead resistance".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws or describes force vs sense and says *why* the sense path has no drop (high-impedance input, so nearly zero current).
- Quantifies: solder-joint and trace resistances against a 1 to 5 mΩ resistor, with the percentage error, and the copper TCR problem.
- Distinguishes a true 4-terminal part from a pseudo-Kelvin two-terminal layout and names the leftover error (current crowding, tap position).
- Lists the trade-offs: cost, footprint, routing effort, noise susceptibility of the sense pair, thermoelectric EMF, and that above tens of mΩ it is overkill.
- Generalises: remote sense on power supplies, Kelvin source pin, four-point probe.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Thinking Kelvin removes the need for good routing of the sense lines.** They carry a tiny voltage and need a matched, tight pair ([[q0555]]).
- **Tapping the sense lines on the outside of the pads, or on the trace.** That puts copper back into the measurement.
- **Believing a 2-terminal resistor with Kelvin traces is as accurate as a 4-terminal part.** It's close, not equal, because of current crowding in the pad.
- **Using Kelvin on high-value resistors** (hundreds of mΩ) where it adds cost and effort for no measurable benefit.
- **Forgetting the amplifier input impedance.** A low-impedance or bias-current-hungry input makes the sense lines carry current and erodes the benefit.
- **Ignoring thermoelectric voltages** at microvolt level in very low-ohm precision measurements; use symmetric copper and avoid heat flow through the part.
:::

::: deep
## The idea in one picture

A resistor measurement is "measure V, divide by I". The trouble is that the voltage you measure includes whatever is between your voltmeter's probes. With a 2-wire measurement, that includes the leads and contacts. In a 4-wire measurement, the voltmeter connects to dedicated sense leads that carry no current. The voltage in a lead carrying no current is the same at both ends (Ohm's law: $V = IR$ with $I = 0$), so the voltmeter sees the voltage at the resistor element's own terminals.

## How much does it matter? It depends on the resistor value

Let $R_p$ be the total series contact and copper resistance in the sense path. The relative error of a 2-terminal measurement is

$$\varepsilon = \frac{R_p}{R_s}.$$

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Contact resistance of a soldered chip resistor and its pad is process- and part-dependent. Values of a few tenths of a milliohm per terminal are commonly quoted for low-ohm parts, but check the manufacturer's application notes for the specific resistor.

## Three ways to do it on a PCB

| Approach | How it works | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| **4-terminal resistor** | Two wide force pads and two small sense pads connected inside the part | Sense point defined by the manufacturer; independent of solder; best accuracy | More cost, bigger/less standard footprint, needs 4-pad land pattern |
| **2-terminal, Kelvin traces** | Normal part; sense traces tap the *inner edge* of each pad | Cheap, any footprint; removes trace and most pad resistance | Residual error from current crowding and tap position; depends on solder fillet |
| **2-terminal, tap on power trace** (not Kelvin) | Sense lines join the power trace | Easiest | Includes the trace; large, drifting error for low-ohm parts |

Why the 2-terminal tap is not perfect: the current spreads through the pad from where the trace enters to where the element starts. The potential on the pad's inner edge is the potential at the element's end *plus* whatever drop remains between that edge and the element body, from solder and the terminal metallisation. Manufacturers of low-ohm resistors specify their four-terminal parts so that the sense points are on the resistive element itself.

## Costs and side-effects

- **Routing.** Two extra traces per resistor, matched, short and away from noisy nodes. A bad Kelvin pair is no better than no Kelvin at all.
- **Noise.** The sense lines are high-impedance and hence susceptible. That's inherent to measuring millivolts.
- **Thermal EMF.** At the junctions of copper and the shunt alloy, a temperature difference generates a Seebeck voltage of a microvolt or so per degree. For a 1 mΩ shunt at 10 A (10 mV), a 10 °C difference at 2 µV/°C is 20 µV = 0.2 %, small but comparable to a precision target. Symmetric copper on both ends helps.
- **Heat.** A 4-terminal part has an extra path for heat from the element to the board, which is usually beneficial.
- **Amplifier input.** The sense path should not draw current. Instrumentation amplifier or CSA inputs (bias current of nA to µA) are fine; a low-impedance input is not.

## The same trick elsewhere

- **Power-supply remote sensing**: separate sense wires from the load back to the supply, so the supply regulates the voltage at the load, not at its output terminals.
- **Power MOSFET Kelvin source pin**: gate-drive return connects to a dedicated source pin so that $L\,di/dt$ in the power source lead isn't in the gate loop.
- **Four-point probes and milliohm meters**: measure sheet resistance and contact resistance.
- **Strain-gauge and RTD bridges**: 4-wire RTD connections cancel lead resistance.

## Worked example

A 3 mΩ shunt is used to measure 20 A. The ideal output is 60 mV. If the sense lines are connected through two solder joints and pads of 0.3 mΩ each, the amplifier sees $20\times(3+0.6) = 72$ mV: a 20 % error. A gain of 25 produces 1.8 V vs the intended 1.5 V. With Kelvin taps at the element, the reading is 60 mV and 1.5 V. The 0.6 mΩ of contacts still dissipates $I^2R = 400\times0.0006 = 0.24$ W, and the resistor itself 1.2 W, but the measurement ignores the contacts.
:::

::: applications
- **Battery fuel gauges and power banks**: 1 to 10 mΩ shunts with Kelvin traces (and often 4-terminal parts) to a gauge IC.
- **Motor phase and DC-link shunts**: low-ohm metal-strip resistors with sense pads.
- **Server power-rail monitors**: power-monitor ICs with 0.5 to 2 mΩ sense resistors.
- **Lab instruments**: bench DMMs' 4-wire resistance mode, milliohm meters and source-measure units use force/sense terminals.
- **Programmable power supplies**: remote-sense terminals that regulate at the load.
:::

::: assumptions
- **The sense input draws negligible current.** If it doesn't, the sense leads' resistance enters and Kelvin's advantage shrinks.
- **The parasitic resistance is *constant* during the measurement.** Kelvin removes its effect regardless of value, but not noise or thermal EMF.
- **The resistor is a low value (roughly under 10 mΩ) for the benefits to matter.** At hundreds of mΩ, a two-terminal measurement is acceptable.
- **Current is DC or low-frequency.** At high di/dt, the inductance of the resistor body and loop (and mutual inductance with the force path) also enters the sense voltage; Kelvin does not remove it. For fast current measurement, special low-inductance shunts are used.
- **Alternative approach**: for very high currents, a Hall or magnetic sensor ([[q0441]]) avoids the shunt altogether.
:::

::: followups
### Why doesn't the sense path have a voltage drop?
Because the sense path connects to a voltmeter or amplifier with very high input impedance, so the current is nA to µA. By Ohm's law the drop in a lead is $I R_{lead}$: with 1 µA and 1 Ω that is 1 µV. The potential at the amplifier pin is the potential at the element's terminal, to within a microvolt.

### Is a Kelvin connection on a 2-terminal chip resistor as good as a 4-terminal part?
Nearly, not quite. With two terminals the current has to spread across the pad and solder to the resistor body, so the potential at the pad's inner edge differs slightly from the potential at the element's end, and depends on solder fillet and tap position. A true four-terminal part has sense terminals connected to the element, so the manufacturer defines and tests the sense points.

### When would you not bother with Kelvin?
When the sense resistor is large (hundreds of mΩ or more), when the accuracy target is loose (a protection trip at ±10 % say), or when board space and cost matter more than accuracy. Even then I'd route the sense pair carefully for noise reasons.

### How big an error can thermoelectric EMF add?
For a copper-to-shunt-alloy junction the Seebeck coefficient is on the order of a microvolt per °C (vendor-dependent). On a 1 mΩ shunt carrying 10 A (10 mV signal), a 10 °C temperature difference between the two ends is about 10 to 30 µV, 0.1 to 0.3 %. It's usually negligible at high current and matters at low current: at 100 mA the signal is only 100 µV, so the same 20 µV is 20 %.

### Scenario: your gas-gauge IC reads 15 % high on a prototype but correctly on the evaluation board. What do you suspect?
The Kelvin routing. The prototype probably has the sense lines tapped off the power copper or the far side of the pads, so contact and trace resistance add to the shunt. I'd check where the sense traces connect, measure the voltage across the shunt's pads with a DMM or scope at a known current, compare it with the voltage at the gauge pins, and fix the layout so the taps are at the inner pad edges.
:::
