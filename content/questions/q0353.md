::: basics
### In plain words
For a DC motor, torque follows current, and speed follows voltage. More current through the winding gives more twisting force. More voltage lets the motor spin faster before its back-EMF catches up with the supply. This is the simplest way to think about what a motor controller is doing.

### Picture it
Think of a treadmill you are running on. You choose the belt speed with a dial: that is the voltage. How hard you have to push to keep up depends on the incline or load: that is the current and torque. A steeper hill means more effort at the same speed. The analogy has a gap: on a real motor, a heavier load also slows the speed a little.

### Words you need
- **Torque ($T$)**: the twisting force, $T = K_tI$.
- **Back-EMF ($E$)**: the voltage the spinning motor makes, $E = K_e\omega$. See [[q0341]].
- **Voltage equation**: $V = IR + K_e\omega$.
- **Stall**: shaft held still, so the current is maximum.
- **PWM duty**: the fraction of time the supply is on, which sets the average voltage.

### The simplest example
Use a 12 V motor with $R = 1$ Ω and $K_t = K_e = 0.02$. At 0.5 A, the torque is 0.02 × 0.5 = 0.01 N·m, and the speed is (12 − 0.5 × 1) / 0.02 = **575 rad/s**. Load it to 5 A, and the torque is 0.1 N·m, ten times more. The speed drops to (12 − 5) / 0.02 = **350 rad/s**.

That is the whole idea: current sets torque, and voltage (minus the $IR$ drop) sets speed. The rest of this page shows why $K_t$ and $K_e$ are the same number, and what that means for current limits and PWM.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can connect the electrical and mechanical sides of a motor with the two constants: torque comes from **current** ($T = K_tI$), speed comes from **voltage** (more precisely, from back-EMF, $\omega \approx (V - IR)/K_e$). The "why" is the real test: both come from the same $B\ell$ product (Lorentz force and Faraday's law), which is why $K_t = K_e$ in SI units and why power balance works. A strong answer also says where the simple mapping breaks (load changes speed through the $IR$ drop, friction offsets torque) and uses it to drive a motor: current limit for torque, PWM voltage for speed.
:::

::: why
This is the one-sentence model behind motor control. Torque control loops regulate current, speed control sets voltage (or a velocity loop commands current), driver sizing is about current, supply sizing about voltage, and datasheets quote $K_t$ and $K_v$ because they are the conversion factors between the electrical and mechanical worlds. Getting this wrong leads to undersized drivers (looking at speed rather than torque) and wrong expectations about what a given supply voltage will do.
:::

::: concepts
- **Lorentz force**: conductor in a field, $F = B\ell I$, so torque $\propto I$. See [[q0348]].
- **Faraday induction**: conductor moving in a field, $e = B\ell v$, so back-EMF $\propto \omega$. See [[q0341]].
- **Voltage equation**: $V = IR + K_e\omega$ (+ $L\,di/dt$ in transients). See [[q0001]] for Ohm's law.
- **Torque constant $K_t$ and voltage constant $K_e$ / $K_v$**: conversions; $K_t = K_e$ in SI.
- **Power balance**: $E I = T\omega$, mechanical power equals electrical power converted. See [[q0346]].
- **Curves**: [[q0352]] shows what these relations look like against speed and torque.
:::

::: answer
"High torque corresponds to high current, and high RPM corresponds to high voltage.

Why current: torque comes from the Lorentz force on the winding. Each conductor in the magnetic field feels a force $B\ell I$, so the total torque is proportional to current, $T = K_tI$. To get more torque I have to push more current through the winding. That's why a stalled motor, which is making maximum torque, draws maximum current, $V/R$.

Why voltage for speed: when the rotor spins, the same conductors cutting the field generate a back-EMF, $E = K_e\omega$, which opposes the supply. The motor settles at the speed where that back-EMF is nearly equal to the applied voltage. The voltage equation is $V = IR + K_e\omega$, so $\omega = (V - IR)/K_e$: speed is roughly proportional to voltage, minus a drop that grows with load current.

The two constants are actually the same number in SI units: $K_t$ in N·m per amp equals $K_e$ in volt-seconds per radian. You can prove it with energy conservation: the electrical power converted, $EI = K_e\omega I$, must equal the mechanical power $T\omega = K_tI\omega$.

In practice, this means current-limiting a driver limits torque, and PWM duty sets speed. And a heavier load pulls the speed down a bit because it needs more current, which means a bigger $IR$ drop."
:::

::: technical
**Torque ↔ current.** For $N$ conductors of active length $\ell$ at radius $r$ in flux density $B$: $T = N B\ell I\,r\cdot(\text{geometry}) \equiv K_tI$. Torque is linear in current (until saturation or demagnetisation). Including friction: $T_{shaft} = K_tI - T_f$.

**Speed ↔ voltage.** $E = K_e\omega$ (same $B\ell r$), and in steady state
$$\omega = \frac{V - IR}{K_e}.$$
At no load, $I = I_0$ is small, so $\omega_0 \approx V/K_e$.

**Equality of constants.** Power converted by the back-EMF: $P = EI = K_e\omega I$. Mechanical power: $T\omega = K_tI\omega$. Equating gives $K_e = K_t$ (in SI: V·s/rad = N·m/A).

**Vendor units.** $K_v$ (rpm/V) is the inverse of $K_e$: $K_e = \frac{60}{2\pi K_v}$ V·s/rad, so $K_t = \frac{9.549}{K_v}$ N·m/A ($K_t$ in mN·m/A equals 9549/$K_v$). Examples: 1000 rpm/V → 9.55 mN·m/A; 500 rpm/V → 19.1 mN·m/A; 2200 rpm/V → 4.34 mN·m/A. Our example motor, $K = 0.02$, is 477 rpm/V and 20 mN·m/A.

**Limits of the mapping.**
- Speed depends on load: $\Delta\omega = R\,\Delta T/(K_eK_t)$.
- Torque = $K_tI$ only for the *current in the winding*; friction and iron losses subtract.
- Dynamics: $L\,di/dt$ limits how fast current (hence torque) can change, and PWM supplies the *average* voltage ([[q0345]]).
- Gearing: output torque $= N\eta T_{motor}$ and output speed $= \omega/N$, so current $\propto T_{out}/N$ and voltage $\propto N\omega_{out}$.

**Design consequence — winding choice.** For a given motor body, doubling turns doubles $K_t$ and $K_e$ but quadruples $R$ (same copper volume). The *motor constant* $K_m = K_t/\sqrt R$ is unchanged. So you choose the winding by supply voltage: more turns for high-voltage low-current drive, fewer for low-voltage high-current.
:::

::: short
"Torque goes with current and speed goes with voltage. The Lorentz force on the winding gives $T = K_tI$, so more current means more torque; that's why stall, the highest torque, is also the highest current, $V/R$. Spinning generates a back-EMF $K_e\omega$ that opposes the supply, so $\omega \approx (V - IR)/K_e$: speed tracks voltage, minus a load-dependent drop. $K_t$ and $K_e$ are the same number in SI units, by energy conservation."
:::

::: long
"The short answer: torque is proportional to current, speed is proportional to voltage.

Torque first. The rotor torque comes from current-carrying conductors in the magnet's field. Each feels a force $B\ell I$, so the total torque is $T = K_tI$, with $K_t$ in newton-metres per amp set by the magnets, the number of turns and the geometry. So if I want more torque, I need more current. That's why a stalled motor, which is making its maximum torque, draws its maximum current, $V/R$, and why current limiting a driver is the same as torque limiting the motor.

Speed. The same conductors are cutting through the magnetic field as the rotor turns, so they generate a back-EMF, $E = K_e\omega$, opposing the applied voltage. The motor speeds up until the back-EMF plus the resistive drop equals the supply: $V = IR + K_e\omega$. Rearranged, $\omega = (V - IR)/K_e$. With a light load the $IR$ term is tiny, so speed is basically $V/K_e$; double the voltage, double the speed. That's why PWM duty sets speed. If the load increases, current rises, the $IR$ drop grows and speed sags a little.

And the two constants aren't independent. $K_t$ and $K_e$ are numerically equal in SI units, by energy conservation: the electrical power converted by the back-EMF, $EI$, is the mechanical power, $T\omega$. Vendors quote $K_v$ in rpm per volt: $K_t$ in newton-metres per amp is $9.55/K_v$. A 1000 rpm per volt motor gives about 9.5 millinewton-metres per amp.

In design terms: torque requirement sets the current rating of the driver and wiring; speed requirement sets the supply voltage; and the winding choice, more turns versus fewer, trades one for the other."
:::

::: terms
- **Torque constant $K_t$**: N·m/A, torque per amp of winding current.
- **Back-EMF constant $K_e$**: V·s/rad, voltage per unit angular speed. Equal to $K_t$ in SI.
- **Speed constant $K_v$**: rpm/V (no-load speed per volt), $K_v = 60/(2\pi K_e)$.
- **Back-EMF**: $E = K_e\omega$, the voltage generated by rotation. See [[q0341]].
- **Stall current**: $V/R$ at zero speed, maximum torque.
- **No-load speed**: $\approx V/K_e$.
- **Motor constant $K_m$**: $K_t/\sqrt R$ in N·m/√W; torque per root-watt of copper loss.
- **Current limit**: a driver feature that caps winding current, hence torque.
- **Speed regulation**: the drop in speed with load, $R/(K_eK_t)$.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "current for torque, voltage for speed" and stops. An exceptional answer:

- Gives the reason in physics: Lorentz force (torque $\propto I$) and Faraday induction (back-EMF $\propto\omega$), both from $B\ell$.
- States $K_t = K_e$ and proves it with power balance.
- Corrects the simple picture: speed depends on both $V$ and the load via $IR$; torque depends on current *minus* friction.
- Converts vendor units ($K_v$ in rpm/V) and does a quick number.
- Gives the design meaning: current limit = torque limit, PWM duty = speed, winding choice trades current vs voltage at constant $K_m$.
- Mentions gearing: reflected current and voltage with a gearbox ratio.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Saying high torque needs high voltage.** Voltage only matters because it drives current through $R$; at stall a given voltage gives $V/R$ and stall torque $K_tV/R$. Torque per se is current.
- **Saying speed is proportional to current.** At steady state speed follows voltage; current follows load. A motor at no load draws almost no current at any speed.
- **Mixing $K_v$ and $K_t$ units.** $K_v$ in rpm/V is the inverse of $K_e$; $K_t \approx 9.55/K_v$ N·m/A. Don't use rpm directly in the SI formulas.
- **Forgetting the $IR$ drop** and so predicting too high a speed under load.
- **Ignoring friction and efficiency.** $T_{shaft} = K_tI - T_f$; and with a gearbox the output torque is reduced by $\eta$.
- **Thinking a higher-voltage supply gives more torque from the same motor at the same current.** Torque depends on current; extra voltage lets it run faster or reach a given current at a higher speed (more back-EMF to overcome).
:::

::: deep
## Where the constants come from

Consider a rotor with total conductor length $\ell_{tot}$ in the active field (the "$B\ell$" of the whole winding) at radius $r$. Each conductor carrying current $I$ in a field $B$ feels a force $F = B\ell I$ (Lorentz). The torque is the force times radius, summed over all conductors:
$$T = (B\ell_{tot})\,r\,I \equiv K_tI.$$

The same conductors move at speed $v = r\omega$ through the same field, so by Faraday induction each develops an EMF $B\ell v$ and the whole winding develops
$$E = (B\ell_{tot})\,r\,\omega \equiv K_e\omega.$$

Same $B\ell_{tot}r$: so $K_t = K_e$ in SI units (N·m/A = V·s/rad). The energy argument says the same thing: the power taken from the supply by the induced EMF is $EI$; it can only go into mechanical power $T\omega$ (and no conductor losses, which are separate $I^2R$). So $K_e\omega I = K_tI\omega$.

## Why torque is current

Magnetic force acts on moving charge. Double the current in a conductor sitting in a fixed magnetic field and you double the force. The only way to raise torque at fixed geometry and magnets is to raise the current (or use more turns, which multiplies $K_t$ — at the price of resistance, see below). Voltage does not appear in $T = K_tI$.

So, at stall: voltage $V$ → current $V/R$ → torque $K_tV/R$ (maximum). At running speed, the same torque needs the same current, but the *voltage* required is higher because it must also overcome the back-EMF: $V = IR + K_e\omega$.

## Putting numbers to $K_v$

Vendors quote speed constants because they are easy to measure with a drill and a voltmeter.
$$K_e = \frac{60}{2\pi K_v},\qquad K_t = K_e = \frac{9.549}{K_v}\ \text{N·m/A}.$$

| $K_v$ (rpm/V) | $K_t$ (mN·m/A) | No-load speed at 12 V | Stall torque at 12 V with $R = 2\ \Omega$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 500 | 19.1 | 6000 rpm | 115 mN·m |
| 1000 | 9.55 | 12,000 rpm | 57 mN·m |
| 2200 | 4.34 | 26,400 rpm | 26 mN·m |

(Stall torque $= K_tV/R$.) Higher $K_v$ means a faster, lower-torque-per-amp motor; at the same resistance, it also stalls with the same current but gives less torque. This is why high-$K_v$ drone motors need a propeller load matched to their speed.

This is the practical meaning of "torque is current, speed is voltage": the motor designer trades them using the number of turns.

## Gears: reflecting current and voltage

A gearbox with ratio $N$ and efficiency $\eta$ turns motor torque $T_m$ and speed $\omega_m$ into $T_{out} = N\eta T_m$ and $\omega_{out} = \omega_m/N$. So *per amp* the output torque is $N\eta K_t$, and the voltage needed for a given *output* speed is $K_eN\omega_{out}$.

**Worked example.** A winch needs 2 N·m at 60 rpm out of a 50:1 box with $\eta = 0.7$, using our $K = 0.02$, $R = 2\ \Omega$, $T_f = 2$ mN·m motor. Motor torque needed: $2/(50\times0.7) = 57.1$ mN·m. Current: $(0.0571 + 0.002)/0.02 = 2.96$ A. Motor speed: $60\times50 = 3000$ rpm = 314 rad/s. Voltage: $IR + K_e\omega = 5.9 + 6.28 = 12.2$ V. So 12 V is *just* too little: the motor would run at about 2900 rpm. The torque target sets the current (driver and wire sizing); the speed target and that current set the voltage (supply). If 12.2 V isn't available, pick a lower ratio, a lower-$K_e$ winding or a 24 V supply.

## Where the mapping breaks (and how drivers deal with it)

- **Dynamics.** $I$ follows $V$ through $L$ (and the back-EMF). A torque change takes a few $L/R$ ([[q0345]]). Current-mode drivers regulate $I$ with a fast inner loop so torque tracks the command.
- **PWM.** Average voltage sets speed, but the current ripple means peak current exceeds average. At very low duty the ripple/dead zone makes the mapping nonlinear.
- **Saturation and demagnetisation.** At high current the core saturates and $K_t$ falls; extreme current can demagnetise magnets.
- **Temperature.** $R$ rises, magnets weaken: $K_t$ falls a bit (typically a few tenths of a percent per °C for neodymium), so more current per torque.
- **Friction and cogging.** $T_{shaft} = K_tI - T_f$, so there's a minimum current before the shaft moves (starting current).

## Closed-loop control built on this mapping

A typical cascaded servo has an inner **current loop** (commands voltage, regulates $I$, so effectively commands torque), a **velocity loop** (commands current to reach the target speed) and an outer **position loop** (commands velocity). The current loop works because torque ~ current; the velocity loop works because speed responds to torque through the inertia $J\,d\omega/dt = T - T_{load}$. Without feedback, "voltage = speed" is only approximate because of the load-dependent $IR$ drop.

::: applications
- **Torque-limited gripper or screwdriver clutch**: set the driver's current limit to the torque you want ($I = T/K_t$).
- **Drone ESC and motor choice**: pick $K_v$ to match prop and battery voltage so the motor runs at its efficient speed.
- **E-bike hub motors**: the controller's current limit sets the maximum torque; battery voltage sets top speed.
- **Torque sensing without a sensor**: estimate load torque from measured motor current ($T = K_tI - T_f$); used in stall detection, sensorless force control and clamp detection in power windows.
- **Speed measurement without a tach**: read back-EMF during a PWM off-period ($E = V - IR$), used in cheap speed control of brushed motors.
- **Choosing battery voltage**: higher voltage allows higher speed at the same current, so a 24 V system can deliver double the speed or half the current for the same power.
:::

::: assumptions
- **PM brushed DC motor in steady state**; the same relations hold for trapezoidal BLDC drive (phase current and $K_t$ per pair), and with field-weakening or saturation they change.
- **Constant $K_t = K_e$**: magnetically linear, temperature fixed. Real values are specified at 20–25 °C.
- **SI units**: $K_t$ in N·m/A and $K_e$ in V·s/rad. Mixed unit systems (oz-in/A, rpm/V, V/krpm) need conversion; the numerical equality is only in SI.
- **Negligible brush drop and friction** in the simple statement; add them for real predictions.
- **Ideal PWM**: average voltage is what sets speed.
- Alternative acceptable answers: "torque ↔ current and speed ↔ voltage" is usually enough; some interviewers also like "power ↔ current × voltage".
:::

::: followups
### If $K_t = K_e$, why do datasheets list both?
They are the same physical constant expressed in different unit systems or measured differently: $K_t$ in N·m/A (or oz-in/A) and $K_e$ in V/krpm (or V·s/rad). Listing both lets you work in whichever units you have, and discrepancies of a few percent point to measurement or temperature differences.

### A motor has $K_v = 1000$ rpm/V and $R = 0.5\ \Omega$. What is its stall torque on 12 V?
$K_t = 9.55$ mN·m/A. Stall current $= 12/0.5 = 24$ A, so $T_{stall} = 0.00955\times24 = 0.229$ N·m, with a stall dissipation of $24^2\times0.5 = 288$ W. That's why such a motor is never held at stall at full voltage.

### How can you control torque directly with a brushed motor?
Regulate current. Measure $I$ with a shunt, compare with a command and adjust PWM duty in a fast loop (a few kHz to tens of kHz). Then the torque is $K_tI$ regardless of speed or supply variations. Many motor drivers have this built in as "current mode" or current limit.

### Why does the motor draw less current at higher speed for the same voltage?
Because back-EMF rises, so $V - E$ falls, and $I = (V - E)/R$. At a fixed load torque, the motor automatically settles at the speed where $I = (T + T_f)/K_t$. If you reduce the load, current falls and speed rises until the back-EMF balances.

### What if you apply voltage to a motor with a locked rotor and measure the current?
$I = V/R$: this gives you the winding resistance (including brushes). Combined with a free-run test at known speed (measure $E$ and $\omega$ to get $K_e$), you can predict the whole curve. Keep the locked test short or at low voltage to avoid overheating.

### What happens to $K_t$ if you replace the motor's windings with thicker wire and fewer turns?
$K_t$ falls proportionally to the turns, $R$ falls with the square, so stall current rises and no-load speed rises at the same voltage; the torque per amp is smaller, but torque per watt of copper loss ($K_m$) is similar. You would use that winding for a low-voltage, high-current supply.
:::
