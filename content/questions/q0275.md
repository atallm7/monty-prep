::: testing
Whether you understand each component as a **function**, not a symbol, and can say what breaks when it is removed or mis-sized. A strong answer gives one clean job per part:

- **Inductor**: energy storage and current source (the thing that makes the voltage rise).
- **Diode**: one-way valve that routes inductor current to the output and stops the output capacitor from discharging into the switch.
- **Capacitor**: charge reservoir that supplies the load while the diode is off and smooths the pulsed current.

Then the interviewer probes for sizing: what sets $L$, what ratings the diode needs, what limits the capacitor (ESR, ripple current, DC-bias derating).
:::

::: why
Every component choice in a boost traces back to one of these three jobs. If you know the job, you know the spec: the inductor's $I_{sat}$ and DCR, the diode's reverse voltage and forward drop, the capacitor's ESR and ripple-current rating. On the bench, most boost failures are one of these three: saturated inductor, overheated or wrong-voltage diode, or capacitor that lost most of its capacitance under DC bias. The same questions appear for the buck in [[q0186]] and [[q0202]]; the boost answers differ in important ways because the output current is pulsed.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor current continuity**: the inductor resists current changes, so opening the switch forces its current somewhere else. See [[q0043]].
- **Energy in an inductor**: $\tfrac12LI^2$. Its stored energy sets how much can be delivered each cycle. See [[q0039]].
- **Diode as a switch**: conducts when forward biased, blocks when reverse biased, with forward drop $V_F$ and reverse-recovery or junction capacitance. See [[q0101]].
- **Capacitor charge balance**: average capacitor current is zero in steady state; $\Delta V=\Delta Q/C$. See [[q0025]].
- **Realistic capacitor**: ESR and ESL, DC-bias derating on ceramics. See [[q0030]].
- **Saturation**: inductance collapses above $I_{sat}$. See [[q0046]].
:::

::: answer
"Each one has one job. The inductor is the energy store. When the switch is on it charges from the input, and when the switch turns off its current has to keep flowing, so it pushes that current into the output at a voltage higher than the input. Without it there's no step-up. Its value sets the ripple current, and I need to check its saturation current against the peak current.

The diode is the one-way valve. When the switch is off it lets the inductor current flow to the output. When the switch turns on, SW goes to ground and the diode blocks, so the output capacitor can't discharge back through the switch. Without it the output would be shorted to ground every cycle. I'd pick a Schottky for low forward drop and make sure its reverse voltage and peak current ratings cover $V_{out}$ and the inductor peak.

The capacitor holds up the output. During the on-time nothing is coming from the inductor, so the capacitor alone supplies the load, and it absorbs the pulsed diode current during the off-time. It sets the output ripple, and what matters is capacitance, ESR and ripple current rating. And for ceramics, derating under DC bias."
:::

::: technical
**Inductor $L$.**
- Role: stores $\tfrac12LI^2$ during on-time; during off-time forces current into the output at $V_{out}>V_{in}$ since $v_L=L\,di/dt$ goes negative.
- Sizing: choose ripple $\Delta I_L=\dfrac{V_{in}D}{Lf_{sw}}$, typically 20 to 40% of $I_{in}=I_{out}/(1-D)$. $L=\dfrac{V_{in}D}{f_{sw}\Delta I_L}$.
- Ratings: $I_{sat}>I_{pk}=I_{in}+\Delta I_L/2$ (plus margin), thermal rating current $I_{rms}$, low DCR, and shielded construction for EMI.

**Diode $D$.**
- Role: unidirectional path from SW to $V_{out}$ in the off-time and blocking element in the on-time.
- Ratings: reverse voltage $V_R>V_{out}$ with margin (typ. 1.2 to 1.5x plus ringing), repetitive peak current $>I_{pk}$, average current $\ge I_{out}$, and low $V_F$ (loss $=V_FI_{out}$).
- Type: Schottky for low $V_F$ at low voltage; silicon-carbide Schottky or fast silicon for high-voltage (PFC) boosts.

**Capacitor $C_{out}$.**
- Role: supplies load for $DT$ and absorbs the pulsed diode current.
- Ripple: $\Delta V\approx\dfrac{I_{out}D}{C_{out}f_{sw}}+I_{pk}R_{ESR}$ and RMS current $I_{C,rms}\approx I_{out}\sqrt{D/(1-D)}$ (neglecting inductor ripple).
- Choice: low ESR, adequate voltage rating, ripple-current rating $>I_{C,rms}$, effective capacitance after DC-bias derating. Also affects the loop: $C_{out}$ and the load set the RHPZ and the output pole.

**Worked numbers (3.7 V to 5 V, 1 A, 500 kHz):** $D=0.26$, $I_{in}=1.35$ A. For $\Delta I_L=0.41$ A, $L=4.7\ \mu$H and $I_{pk}=1.56$ A (choose $I_{sat}\ge2$ A). For 20 mV capacitive ripple: $C\ge\dfrac{1\times0.26}{5\times10^5\times0.02}=26\ \mu$F effective. Diode loss $0.4$ W with $V_F=0.4$ V.
:::

::: short
"The inductor is the energy store: it charges while the switch is on and then forces its current into the output when the switch opens, which is what lifts the voltage. The diode is the one-way valve: it carries the inductor current to the output in the off-time and blocks in the on-time so the output capacitor isn't shorted to ground. The capacitor holds the output steady: it supplies the load during the on-time and smooths the pulsed diode current, and it sets the output ripple."
:::

::: long
"I'll take them one at a time, because each has one job and one failure mode.

The inductor is the energy store and it's the reason the voltage steps up. With the switch on, the input voltage ramps the inductor current up and stores $\tfrac12LI^2$. When the switch opens, the current can't stop, so the inductor voltage flips and pushes the current into the output even though the output is above the input. Its value sets the ripple current, $\Delta I=V_{in}D/(Lf)$, and I'd pick 20 to 40% of the average current. Then I check the saturation current against the peak, 1.56 A for a 3.7 to 5 V, 1 A example. If the inductor saturates, inductance collapses, current runs away and the switch dies.

The diode is the valve. In the off-time it connects the switch node to the output, which is the only route for the stored energy. In the on-time the switch node is at ground, and the diode blocks so the output cap isn't discharged through the switch. Remove it or put it in backwards and the output either never gets energy or is shorted every cycle. I'd use a Schottky because the forward drop matters: 0.4 V at 1 A is 0.4 W, 8% of a 5 W output. It needs a reverse voltage above $V_{out}$ and a peak rating above the inductor peak.

The capacitor holds the output rail. In the on-time it alone supplies the load, so ripple is roughly $I_{out}D/(Cf)$, 24 mV for 22 µF in this case, plus an ESR step. It also sees a pulsed current, about 0.6 A RMS here, so I check ripple-current rating and ESR, and for ceramics I check the DC-bias derating. I'd also remember it feeds the control loop: more capacitance lowers ripple but moves the RHP-zero-limited loop speed."
:::

::: terms
- **Energy storage element**: the inductor stores $\tfrac12LI^2$ in its magnetic field.
- **Saturation current ($I_{sat}$)**: current at which inductance drops (often quoted at a 20 to 30% drop, vendor-dependent).
- **DCR**: winding resistance, the source of inductor conduction loss.
- **Rectifier (catch) diode**: the output diode. Schottky for low $V_F$.
- **Reverse recovery / junction capacitance**: diode switching loss mechanisms.
- **ESR / ESL**: capacitor's equivalent series resistance and inductance, which set ripple steps and spikes.
- **Ripple current rating**: RMS current the capacitor can carry without overheating.
- **DC-bias derating**: ceramic capacitance falls as DC voltage rises.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** gives one-line roles: "inductor stores energy, diode blocks reverse flow, capacitor smooths".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Ties each role to the waveform: inductor ramps, diode carries a pulsed current (average $I_{out}$, peak $I_{pk}$), capacitor supplies the load during the on-time.
- Says what *happens if removed*: no inductor means no step-up (the switch shorts the source), no diode means the output is shorted to ground every on-time, no capacitor means the output pulses to zero between cycles.
- Gives sizing formulas with numbers and the ratings that matter ($I_{sat}$, $V_R$, ripple current).
- Notes the pulsed-current difference from a buck: the boost output capacitor takes the brunt of the ripple.
- Mentions DC-bias derating and diode $V_F$ loss without being asked.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"The capacitor boosts the voltage."** It does not. It filters. The step-up comes from inductor energy.
- **"The diode just stops the current from going backwards" and nothing else.** True, but the consequence is that it prevents $C_{out}$ from discharging through the on-switch and steers the stored energy to the output. Say both.
- **Sizing the inductor by value alone**, ignoring $I_{sat}$. A 4.7 µH part rated for 1 A saturates at 1.56 A.
- **Picking a diode rated only for the average current.** Check repetitive peak current, reverse voltage with ringing margin, and heat from $V_FI_{out}$.
- **Reading capacitance off the marking.** A 22 µF, 10 V ceramic can have 40% or less effective capacitance at 5 V bias (X5R, small case, vendor-dependent).
- **Forgetting the capacitor's ripple current rating.** In a boost the output capacitor carries the full pulsed diode current, unlike a buck.
:::

## The inductor: the energy source that lifts the voltage

Everything flows from $v_L=L\,di/dt$. In the on-time $v_L=V_{in}$ and current rises at $V_{in}/L$. When the switch opens, the inductor refuses to let its current change instantly, so $v_L$ becomes whatever is needed to keep $I_L$ flowing: $V_{in}-V_{out}-V_F$, which is negative. The inductor is therefore a **current source** for the output side during the off-time. See [[q0276]] for what happens if there is nowhere for that current to go.

**Choosing $L$.** Ripple ratio $r=\Delta I_L/I_{in}$. For $r=0.3$ at $I_{in}=1.35$ A the ripple is 0.405 A, so $L=\dfrac{V_{in}D}{f\Delta I_L}=\dfrac{3.7\times0.26}{5\times10^5\times0.405}=4.75\ \mu$H, so 4.7 µH. Trade-offs:

Inductor selection checklist: $I_{sat}$ above the peak with margin (say 1.2x to 1.3x the 1.56 A), a heating current rating above $I_{in,rms}$, low DCR (loss $=I_{in}^2R_{DCR}$, 55 mW for 30 mΩ), and shielded to limit radiated EMI ([[q0048]], [[q0259]]).

## The diode: valve and energy router

The diode has two jobs. **Off-time:** it provides the path from SW to the output so the inductor's energy can be delivered. **On-time:** with SW near 0 V and the output near 5 V, it blocks, so the output capacitor cannot discharge through the switch. If the diode is missing the output node has no path to the energy store, and if it is shorted $C_{out}$ would be shorted to ground whenever $Q$ turns on.

Key numbers for the example:
- Reverse voltage in the on-state: $V_{out}=5$ V (plus ringing). A 20 V Schottky gives 4x margin.
- Peak forward current: $I_{pk}=1.56$ A, average $I_{out}=1$ A.
- Forward drop: Schottky about 0.3 to 0.5 V at 1 A (varies with part and temperature). Loss $=V_FI_{out}=0.4$ W.
- Junction capacitance: Schottky $C_J$ of 100 to 300 pF charges and discharges every cycle, causing small spikes and ringing with the loop inductance.

Schottky diodes have leakage current that rises with temperature and voltage, so a hot boost running at its voltage limit may leak noticeably. Where $V_{out}$ goes above roughly 100 V (PFC at 400 V), silicon Schottkys are not available at that rating, and the choice is a fast silicon diode or a silicon-carbide Schottky, which has near-zero reverse recovery.

## The capacitor: reservoir and ripple filter

During the on-time the diode is off, so $C_{out}$ alone supplies $I_{out}$ for $DT=0.52\ \mu$s:
$$\Delta V_C=\frac{I_{out}DT}{C_{out}}=\frac{1\times0.52\ \mu}{22\ \mu}=23.6\text{ mV}$$
At turn-off the diode current jumps to $I_{pk}=1.56$ A and the capacitor current steps from $-1$ A to $+0.56$ A, so the ESR adds a step of $\Delta I\cdot R_{ESR}=1.56\times R_{ESR}$ (16 mV for 10 mΩ). The RMS ripple current is $I_{out}\sqrt{D/(1-D)}\approx0.59$ A, which must be below the capacitor's rating.

**DC-bias derating.** A ceramic capacitor's capacitance falls as the DC voltage across it rises (the dielectric's permittivity drops). For high-permittivity classes (X5R, X7R), a small-case part at a significant fraction of rated voltage can lose a large part of its nominal value. Say a 22 µF part retains 60% at 5 V: the real capacitance is 13 µF and ripple becomes $0.52\ \mu/13\ \mu\to 40$ mV, not 24 mV. Always read the maker's DC-bias curve.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The retained fraction (60% here) is illustrative. It varies strongly with case size, voltage rating, dielectric and vendor. Treat any assumed derating as a placeholder until you read the actual curve.

## What happens if you remove each

| Removed | Result |
|---|---|
| Inductor | Switch connects the source straight to ground when on (short), and the diode path conducts the source to the output when off. No step-up and likely destruction. |
| Diode | No path to deliver energy: SW flies up uncontrolled (avalanche of $Q$) as in [[q0276]], output falls to zero. Or, with an intrinsic body diode only, a lossy path. |
| Output capacitor | Output is a pulse train that drops to roughly zero in each on-time, and the SW ringing is not absorbed. Many controllers become unstable or trip. |
| Input capacitor | Source impedance and wiring inductance cause input ripple, noise and ringing, especially from a battery with long leads. |

::: applications
- **Power-bank boost stages**: low-DCR shielded inductors rated for the saturation current at full battery sag, a low-$V_F$ Schottky or synchronous FET, and several ceramic capacitors in parallel for low ESR.
- **LED drivers**: output capacitor sized for acceptable LED ripple current; the diode is a Schottky rated for the string voltage.
- **Offline PFC boost**: a large inductor, a SiC or ultrafast diode (soft recovery) and a bulk electrolytic capacitor sized for line-frequency ripple and hold-up time.
- **Display bias supplies**: tiny power inductors, small Schottky and a single ceramic capacitor, where ripple is critical for image quality.
- **Automotive**: automotive-qualified shielded inductors and AEC-Q grade ceramics and diodes for the same three jobs.
:::

::: assumptions
- Non-synchronous, CCM boost with a Schottky diode. In a synchronous design the diode's job is done by a MOSFET and the same sizing logic applies to its $R_{DS(on)}$ and body diode.
- The capacitive-ripple formula neglects inductor ripple current into the output. It is accurate when ESR is small and $C$ is moderate.
- "Remove the part" outcomes assume no other protection. Real ICs may have current limits that prevent destruction.
- Ripple targets, margins (20 to 40% ripple, $I_{sat}$ margin) are common practice and not universal rules.
- Another valid reading of the question is "why is each *there* (functionally)" without sizing. The first half of the answer covers that, the second half the engineering.
:::

::: followups
### Why does a boost converter need a bigger output capacitor than a buck of the same ripple spec?
In a buck the inductor feeds the output continuously, so the capacitor only absorbs the small inductor ripple. In a boost the output current is pulsed: for $DT$ the capacitor alone must supply $I_{out}$. Ripple is $I_{out}DT/C$, which grows with load and duty cycle, and the capacitor RMS current $I_{out}\sqrt{D/(1-D)}$ is much larger.

### What is the maximum voltage across the diode, and when?
During the on-time the diode is reverse biased with the full $V_{out}$ across it (anode at SW $\approx0$ V, cathode at $V_{out}$), plus ringing from the SW node's parasitic inductance and capacitance. A common rule is to choose $V_R$ at least 1.2 to 1.5x $V_{out}$, and more if the layout rings. For a 5 V output, a 20 V Schottky is typical.

### What happens if the inductor saturates?
Its inductance collapses, so $di/dt=V/L$ rises sharply, current climbs far above the design peak within a few cycles, and the switch's current limit (if any) trips or the switch overheats. Efficiency falls and the SW node shows a spike at the end of the on-time. Soft-saturation powder cores degrade gradually; ferrite cores saturate abruptly ([[q0046]]).

### Could you replace the diode with a resistor?
No. A resistor would conduct in the on-time too, discharging $C_{out}$ through the switch and wasting energy continuously. The converter needs a *unidirectional, voltage-controlled* element: a diode or a synchronous FET.

### Scenario: output ripple on your boost is 120 mV when the calculation says 25 mV. What do you check?
Measure properly first (tip-and-barrel at the capacitor, not a long ground lead, bandwidth limited). Then: effective capacitance (DC-bias derating can halve it), ESR step $I_{pk}R_{ESR}$ (several ceramics in parallel), the SW-node spikes coupling in from layout loop inductance (add a snubber or move the capacitor next to the diode), and whether the converter is in DCM or burst mode at that load where ripple is naturally higher.
:::

"The diode? The inductor? The capacitor?" is a three-part question. I answer it in the order inductor, diode, capacitor, because that is the order the energy moves. The conceptual definition of the boost is [[q0270]], the waveforms are [[q0271]], and the flyback voltage the inductor generates is [[q0276]]. For the buck counterpart see [[q0186]].
