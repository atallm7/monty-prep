::: testing
Whether you know the **voltage window** and *why* it exists, not just that "overcharge is bad". The hidden sub-skills:

1. You can state the safe range (about 3.0 to 4.2 V per cell) and what physically happens on each side: overcharge (lithium plating, oxidation, oxygen release, heating) versus overdischarge (SEI breakdown, copper dissolution, internal shorts on recharge).
2. You see that the two failures have **different timescales and signatures**: overcharge is an immediate safety event; overdischarge is often silent damage that bites on the *next* charge.
3. You can say how electronics prevent both, and spot the series-pack traps (a pack total can hide one bad cell).
:::

::: why
The voltage window is the first thing every lithium design is built around. Charger ICs, protection ICs, fuel gauges and low-battery firmware all exist to keep cells inside it. Real field failures come from leaving it: a failed charger, a device left in storage for months, a weak cell in a series pack, a "dead" pack revived with a bench supply. Knowing the mechanisms lets you choose thresholds, pick protection delays, and write storage and recovery rules that actually make sense.
:::

::: concepts
- **Operating window**: roughly 3.0 to 4.2 V per cell for cobalt/NMC LiPo, with narrower margins at the top. See [[q0305]].
- **Intercalation**: lithium ions sit in the graphite anode (charge) and the oxide cathode (discharge); the voltage tells you how full each electrode is.
- **Lithium plating**: when the anode cannot accept ions fast enough or is full, lithium deposits as metal.
- **SEI (solid-electrolyte interphase)**: protective film on the anode that decomposes when abused.
- **Protection thresholds**: OVP/UVP set by the protector or charger. See [[q0325]].
- **Series-pack imbalance**: pack voltage can look fine while one cell is out of range. See [[q0326]].
:::

::: answer
"Both hurt the cell, in different ways. If you overcharge a LiPo, above about 4.2 volts, you're pulling too much lithium out of the cathode, which destabilizes it, and the anode can't take more lithium so it plates as metal. The electrolyte also starts to oxidize and produce gas, so the pouch swells. All of that makes heat, and in the worst case you get thermal runaway and fire. So overcharge is the dangerous one.

If you overdischarge, below roughly 3.0 volts and certainly below 2.5, the cell isn't immediately a hazard but it's being damaged. The protective SEI layer on the anode breaks down, and at very low voltage the copper current collector can dissolve into the electrolyte. Capacity and power drop. The real danger comes if you then charge it: that copper can redeposit as dendrites and create an internal short, which can heat or ignite later. That's why chargers refuse to charge cells below a minimum and why protection circuits cut off at low voltage.

In a series pack it's trickier, because the pack voltage can look normal while one weak cell is over or under its limit. So you need per-cell monitoring, a good charger with CC/CV and termination, and a low-voltage cutoff in the load."
:::

::: technical
**Operating window (typical LCO/NMC pouch cell).** Full charge 4.20 V (some "high-voltage" cells 4.35 V); nominal 3.6 to 3.7 V; discharge cutoff 3.0 V (some protectors cut at 2.5 to 2.8 V). Storage near 3.8 V.

**Overcharge ($V > 4.2$ V).**

- Cathode: removing too much lithium from LiCoO₂ (delithiating beyond about half the lithium) destabilizes the layered structure, and at high delithiation the lattice can release oxygen.
- Anode: when the graphite is saturated, additional lithium ions cannot intercalate and deposit as metallic lithium (plating), which grows as dendrites and can bridge the separator.
- Electrolyte: oxidizes at the cathode at high potential (roughly above 4.5 V vs Li/Li⁺), generating gas (CO₂, CO and hydrocarbons) and heat, which causes swelling.
- Energy: the charger keeps pushing power into the cell; much of it ends as heat ($I^2R$ plus reaction heat), driving the cell towards thermal runaway ([[q0332]]).
- Consequences by severity: slight over-voltage (4.25 to 4.3 V) shortens life; larger overshoot gives gas, swelling, leaks; extreme overcharge can cause fire or explosion.

**Overdischarge ($V$ below about 3.0 V, especially < 2.0 V).**

- The anode potential rises as lithium is exhausted. At about 3 V or more versus Li/Li⁺ the copper current collector can begin to dissolve as Cu ions; the SEI also decomposes.
- On recharge, the dissolved copper deposits as dendrites, and the damaged SEI regrows consuming lithium. Result: lost capacity, increased resistance, and a risk of internal short.
- Below about 1 V per cell in a series pack, a weak cell can be driven into **reversal** (negative voltage) by its neighbours, producing gas and heat.
- Chargers typically refuse to charge below a floor (often around 2.0 to 2.5 V) or precharge gently; many guidelines recommend discarding cells that have been held under about 2 V.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The thresholds (3.0 V cutoff, 2.0 to 2.5 V "do not charge" floors, 4.5 V electrolyte oxidation onset) depend on chemistry, temperature and rate, and different manufacturers specify different limits. Use the cell datasheet.

**Prevention.** CC/CV charger with 4.2 V ±1% accuracy and a safety timer; protector IC with OVP (about 4.25 to 4.35 V) and UVP (about 2.5 to 3.0 V) per cell; low-voltage cutoff in the load or fuel-gauge firmware; balancing; storage at partial charge and disconnecting loads in storage.
:::

::: short
"Overcharging above about 4.2 volts pulls too much lithium out of the cathode, plates lithium on the anode and oxidizes the electrolyte, so you get gas, swelling, heat and potentially thermal runaway. Overdischarging below about 3 volts breaks down the anode's protective layer and can dissolve the copper collector, which then forms dendrites on recharge and risks an internal short. So overcharge is the immediate hazard and overdischarge is silent damage with a delayed one, and protection ICs, chargers and cutoffs exist to avoid both."
:::

::: long
"A LiPo is happy between about 3.0 and 4.2 volts per cell, and going outside that window damages it in two different ways.

Overcharge first. During charge, lithium ions leave the oxide cathode and go into the graphite anode. Past 4.2 volts you're removing too much lithium from the cathode, which destabilizes its crystal structure and at high delithiation it can release oxygen. At the same time the anode fills up, so extra lithium can't intercalate and deposits as metal, which grows dendrites. The electrolyte also starts oxidizing at the cathode surface and gives off gas, so the pouch swells. All of this makes heat, and the heat makes the reactions faster, so you can reach thermal runaway, venting and fire. It's an immediate safety problem.

Overdischarge is more sneaky. Below about 3 volts you're running out of lithium in the anode, its potential rises, the SEI layer starts to decompose, and at very low cell voltage the copper current collector can dissolve into the electrolyte. The cell may seem fine or just have lost capacity. The danger comes at the next charge, when that copper plates out as dendrites and can short the cell internally, giving a delayed failure. In a series pack, a weak cell can even be pushed negative by the others.

To prevent both: a charger with accurate CC/CV, termination and a timer; a protection IC that cuts off per cell at maybe 4.25 V and 2.5 to 3 V; a low-voltage cutoff in the product; balancing; and storing at partial charge with loads disconnected. Cells that have been deeply overdischarged should be retired, not revived."
:::

::: terms
- **Overcharge**: charging beyond the rated upper voltage (about 4.2 V per cell) or beyond capacity.
- **Overdischarge**: discharging below the minimum voltage (about 3.0 V, absolute floor around 2.0 to 2.5 V).
- **Lithium plating**: metallic lithium deposition on the anode surface instead of intercalation.
- **Dendrite**: needle-like metal growth that can pierce the separator.
- **Copper dissolution**: oxidation of the anode copper foil at high anode potential during deep discharge.
- **Cell reversal**: a cell in a series string driven to negative voltage by the others.
- **OVP / UVP**: over-voltage and under-voltage protection thresholds.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "overcharge can cause fire, overdischarge reduces capacity".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the **numbers**: 4.2 V upper limit, about 3.0 V lower limit, and why the thresholds differ by chemistry.
- Explains the **mechanism at both electrodes**: cathode instability and plating for overcharge; SEI breakdown and copper dissolution for overdischarge.
- Highlights the **asymmetry**: overcharge is immediate and dangerous; overdischarge is quiet and dangerous *on recharge*.
- Brings up **series packs** (a 12.0 V pack can hide a cell at 1.2 V) and cell reversal.
- Describes **practical prevention**: charger CC/CV and termination, protector thresholds with delays, load cutoff, storage voltage, retiring revived cells.
- Mentions standby loads as a cause of overdischarge in storage.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Overdischarge is harmless because the cell is just empty."** Deep discharge causes permanent damage and can make the cell unsafe to recharge.
- **"4.2 V is a soft limit."** It is a hard limit; a few tens of millivolts over measurably ages the cell, and significantly over is a safety event.
- **"The protector cut-off at 2.5 V means it's safe to sit at 2.5 V."** Time spent near the floor still damages; and self-discharge or a standby load keeps pulling down after cutoff.
- **"Reviving a dead LiPo with a bench supply is fine."** If it has been below about 2 V, it may have copper dendrites; many guidelines say discard.
- **Judging a series pack by total voltage.** One cell can be far outside the window while the sum looks normal ([[q0326]]).
- **"Overcharge only means too high a voltage."** Charging at too high a current or at low temperature also plates lithium even below 4.2 V.
:::

::: deep
## Where the voltage comes from

A lithium-ion cell's open-circuit voltage is the difference between the cathode and anode potentials (both versus Li/Li⁺). During charge, lithium ions leave the cathode and enter the graphite. The cell voltage rises because the cathode becomes delithiated (higher potential) and the anode becomes lithiated (potential approaches about 0.1 V and lower). The window of 3.0 to 4.2 V is where *both* electrodes are in their stable operating range.

## Overcharge in detail

1. **Cathode.** In LiCoO₂, charging removes lithium: $\text{LiCoO}_2 \rightarrow \text{Li}_{1-x}\text{CoO}_2 + x\,\text{Li}^+ + x\,e^-$. Practical cells use $x$ up to about 0.5 to 0.6 (that is what 4.2 V corresponds to). Taking $x$ higher destabilizes the layered structure and lowers the temperature at which it releases oxygen.
2. **Anode.** The graphite can hold only so much lithium. When it is full, or when the charging current is too high or the cell is cold, Li⁺ is reduced to *lithium metal* on the surface: plating. Plated lithium is highly reactive and forms dendrites.
3. **Electrolyte.** Carbonate solvents oxidize at the cathode surface at high potential, producing gas and heat.
4. **Heat.** $I^2R$ plus reaction heat pushes temperature up, which accelerates every reaction above. This is the positive feedback of thermal runaway.

A useful number: the energy in the cell is about 7.4 Wh for a 2 Ah, 3.7 V cell (26.6 kJ). An overcharge that pushes in even a few percent more lithium than the cathode can safely give up is *adding* energy into a more reactive state, and the electrolyte oxidation produces heat on top.

## Overdischarge in detail

1. **Potentials.** As the cell empties, the graphite anode runs out of lithium and its potential versus Li/Li⁺ climbs from about 0.1 V to above 1 V and then several volts, while the cathode potential falls. The *cell* voltage heads to zero.
2. **SEI decomposition.** The passivating film on the graphite breaks down at higher anode potentials.
3. **Copper dissolution.** The copper current collector begins to oxidize at an anode potential of roughly 3 V or more versus Li/Li⁺ (details vary), releasing Cu⁺/Cu²⁺ into the electrolyte. 
4. **On recharge.** Dissolved copper deposits as metallic dendrites. Together with repeated SEI regrowth that consumes lithium, the result is capacity loss, rising resistance and the possibility of an internal short.
5. **Cell reversal in series.** If a series string keeps being discharged after one cell is empty, the stronger cells push current through the empty one, driving its voltage *negative*. That is more damaging still: gas generation, polarity-reversal damage and heating.

> [!WARNING]
> A cell that has sat below about 2 V should be treated as damaged. Even if it "recovers" to a normal voltage on a bench supply, it may have a latent internal short.

## Series packs hide it

A pack protector or charger that only watches the sum cannot see this:

| Case | Cell voltages | Sum | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Overcharge hidden | 4.35, 4.20, 4.15, 4.10 V | 16.80 V | Cell 1 is 150 mV over the limit |
| Overdischarge hidden | 3.6, 3.6, 3.6, 1.2 V | 12.00 V | A 4S pack with a 3.0 V/cell pack cutoff reads "fine" while cell 4 is deeply overdischarged |

Hence per-cell monitoring, balancing, and cutoffs per cell ([[q0326]]).

## Where overdischarge actually comes from

Most real overdischarge is not heavy use; it is **storage with a standby load**. Suppose a device with a protector has a standby drain of 1 mA on a 2000 mAh pack. The last 10% of capacity is 200 mAh, which takes $200\ \text{mAh} / 1\ \text{mA} = 200$ h, about 8 days, to drain. After the protector disconnects, the cell still self-discharges slowly, and the protector's own quiescent current (microamps) can pull the cell lower over months. Design rules: low-power shutdown mode, a UVP that disconnects the load completely, and firmware that warns before cutoff.

## What to do with a cell that has been overdischarged

- **Slightly under (above about 2.5 V):** a charger with precharge can bring it back at a gentle current (about C/10); capacity may be reduced. Watch for heating or swelling.
- **Deep (below about 2.0 V) or held low for long:** treat as damaged. Recycle it ([[q0328]]).
- **Series pack with one bad cell:** replace the pack or the cell and rematch; do not simply balance-charge it.
:::

::: applications
- **Phone and laptop BMS**: per-cell OVP/UVP with deliberate delays, plus firmware low-battery warnings well above the hardware UVP.
- **RC/drone**: ESC low-voltage cutoff (often set per cell around 3.2 to 3.5 V under load) to avoid overdischarge in flight.
- **Storage**: shipping packs at partial charge (around 30 to 50%) so standby drain doesn't take them below the floor.
- **Charger design**: precharge currents for very low cells, minimum voltage thresholds, fault timers.
- **Production test**: checking OVP/UVP thresholds and timings on protector boards.
:::

::: assumptions
- Numbers are for **LiCoO₂/NMC-type LiPo** cells. LiFePO₄ has a window of about 2.5 to 3.65 V with different failure thresholds; LTO is different again.
- Thresholds are **typical**; use the datasheet for the actual cell.
- Mechanisms are summarized qualitatively; real degradation is a mix of temperature, rate, state of charge and time.
- "Overcharge" includes both too-high voltage and charging beyond capacity with the wrong control, and arguably too-high current or too-cold charging for plating purposes.
:::

::: followups
### Which is more dangerous: overcharge or overdischarge?
Overcharge is the immediate safety hazard (heat, gas, runaway). Overdischarge is mainly a damage mechanism, but it can produce a delayed hazard because copper dissolution leads to dendrites and internal shorts after recharge. Both are controlled by electronics, and a cell that has been seriously over-discharged should not be reused.

### Why do chargers refuse to charge a very low-voltage cell?
Below roughly 2.0 to 2.5 V the cell may have copper dissolution and SEI damage. Hammering it at full current can force dendrite growth and heating. The charger either refuses, or precharges at a small current (about C/10) until the voltage recovers, and uses a timer to flag a bad cell.

### In a 4S pack the total reads 12.0 V. Is it empty?
Not necessarily, and not necessarily safe. If every cell is at 3.0 V that is the cutoff. But 3.6, 3.6, 3.6, 1.2 V also adds to 12.0 V, and the 1.2 V cell is badly overdischarged. You need per-cell voltage monitoring.

### How does temperature interact with these limits?
Cold: charging below about 0 °C causes lithium plating even at normal voltage, and high internal resistance causes early cutoff under load. Hot: all side reactions accelerate; at high temperature a given overvoltage is much more dangerous, and the safe charge voltage is often derated. The charger's NTC input exists for this reason.

### How would you pick the UVP threshold for a protector IC?
Choose it above the point where damage starts and consider load transients: the cell's terminal voltage sags under load by $I R_{int}$, so a 3.0 V UVP on a pulsed load can trip early. Use a delay (hundreds of ms to seconds) to ignore transients, a threshold typically 2.5 to 3.0 V per cell, and a lower-voltage latch that disconnects the standby load.

### Scenario: a customer's device was stored for a year and now won't charge. What do you suspect and do?
Standby drain plus self-discharge probably took the cell under the charger's minimum voltage, so the charger's precharge or fault timer refuses. Measure the cell voltage. If it is above about 2.5 V, a controlled precharge may recover it; if it is far below 2.0 V, treat it as damaged and replace it. In the design, add a hard load disconnect and a storage-mode shipping state.
:::
