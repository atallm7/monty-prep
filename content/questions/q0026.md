::: testing
A quick yes/no question with a trap. The interviewer wants **negative**, but listens for:

1. The reason: $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C) = -j/(\omega C)$, so $X_C = -1/(\omega C)$.
2. The physical meaning of the sign: current **leads** voltage by 90° (capacitive), the opposite of an inductor.
3. Awareness that many textbooks quote "$X_C = 1/(2\pi fC)$" as a positive magnitude and put the minus sign in $Z = R - jX_C$. A strong candidate reconciles both without getting flustered.
4. Using the sign: series reactances add algebraically, so L and C cancel at resonance.
:::

::: why
The sign of reactance tells you whether a network looks inductive or capacitive, which drives real decisions: whether an antenna or matching network needs a series inductor or capacitor, whether a decoupling capacitor above its self-resonant frequency is still helping, whether a load needs power-factor correction, and whether a resonant tank is above or below resonance. Get the sign wrong and an impedance-matching calculation sends you to the wrong component. It's also the first step to reading a Smith chart, where capacitive impedances live in the lower half.
:::

::: concepts
- **Impedance** $Z = R + jX$: resistance is the real part, reactance the imaginary part. See [[q0024]].
- **Capacitor impedance**: $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)$, which is purely imaginary and negative.
- **Phase**: the angle of $Z$ is the angle by which voltage leads current.
- **Capacitor law** $i = C\,dv/dt$: current is the derivative of voltage, so it leads. See [[q0025]].
- **Inductor comparison**: $Z_L = j\omega L$, positive reactance ([[q0042]]).
- **Algebraic addition** of series reactances and resonance where they cancel.
:::

::: answer
"Negative. A capacitor's impedance is $1/(j\omega C)$, and since $1/j = -j$, that's $-j/(\omega C)$. So the reactance, the imaginary part, is $X_C = -1/(\omega C)$.

The sign carries real meaning. Negative reactance means the voltage lags the current by 90 degrees, or equivalently current leads voltage. That makes sense from $i = C\,dv/dt$: the current peaks when the voltage is changing fastest, which is a quarter cycle before the voltage peaks. An inductor is the opposite, $+j\omega L$, positive reactance, current lags.

One thing to watch: a lot of textbooks write $X_C = 1/(2\pi fC)$ as a positive number and then put the minus sign in the impedance, like $Z = R - jX_C$. That's the same physics, just a bookkeeping choice. What matters is that when you add a capacitor and inductor in series, their reactances subtract. For example, 10 mH and 1 µF at 1 kHz give about +63 Ω and −159 Ω, so the net is about −96 Ω, which means the circuit looks capacitive below its resonant frequency."
:::

::: technical
**Sign from the impedance.** With the engineering convention $v(t) = \text{Re}\{\mathbf V e^{j\omega t}\}$:
$$Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C} = -\frac{j}{\omega C} \quad\Rightarrow\quad R = 0,\;\; X_C = -\frac{1}{\omega C} < 0$$
Compare $Z_L = j\omega L$, $X_L = +\omega L > 0$.

**Phase meaning.** $\angle Z = \arctan(X/R)$ is the angle by which voltage leads current. $X < 0 \Rightarrow$ voltage lags current (capacitive). For a pure capacitor $\angle Z_C = -90^\circ$.

**Magnitude convention.** Many texts define $X_C \equiv 1/(\omega C) > 0$ and write $Z = R + j(X_L - X_C)$. Both conventions give identical results; the sign is "inside" the formula instead of "inside" $X_C$.

**Susceptance.** $Y_C = j\omega C$, so the capacitive susceptance $B_C = +\omega C$ is **positive** (while inductive susceptance $B_L = -1/(\omega L)$ is negative). Sign flips between the impedance and admittance pictures are a common source of confusion.

**Series RLC.** $X_{net} = \omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}$: negative below $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ (capacitive), zero at $f_0$, positive above (inductive). Example: 10 mH, 1 µF at 1 kHz: $X_L = +62.8$ Ω, $X_C = -159.2$ Ω, $X_{net} = -96.3$ Ω; $f_0 = 1.59$ kHz.

**Reactive power.** $Q = I_{rms}^2X$: negative for a capacitor. Capacitors are said to "supply" reactive power, which is why they correct lagging (inductive) power factor.

**Physics convention caveat.** With $e^{-i\omega t}$ time dependence (common in physics and some electromagnetics texts), $Z_C = i/(\omega C)$ and the sign of every reactance flips. The physics is unchanged.
:::

::: short
"Negative. $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C) = -j/(\omega C)$, so the reactance is $-1/(\omega C)$. That negative sign means current leads voltage by 90°, the opposite of an inductor's $+j\omega L$. Many books write $X_C$ as a positive magnitude and put the minus in $Z = R - jX_C$, which is equivalent. The practical point is that inductive and capacitive reactances cancel in series, which is what resonance is."
:::

::: long
"A capacitor has negative reactance. Its impedance is $1/(j\omega C)$, and because one over $j$ equals minus $j$, that's $-j/(\omega C)$. Reactance is the imaginary part of impedance, so $X_C = -1/(\omega C)$. An inductor, at $+j\omega L$, has positive reactance.

The sign isn't just algebra; it encodes the phase. The angle of an impedance is how far the voltage leads the current. For a capacitor it's −90°, so the voltage lags, or the current leads. I can see that from $i = C\,dv/dt$: if the voltage is a sine wave, the current is a cosine, which peaks a quarter cycle earlier, right when the voltage is crossing zero and changing fastest.

There's a notational thing worth mentioning so nobody gets confused. Lots of textbooks define $X_C = 1/(2\pi fC)$ as a positive number and write the impedance as $R - jX_C$, or $R + j(X_L - X_C)$. That's the same physics; they've just moved the minus sign. I'd make sure I'm consistent within a calculation. And if you flip to admittance, the signs flip too: a capacitor's susceptance $\omega C$ is positive.

Why it matters practically: reactances add algebraically. Put 10 mH and 1 µF in series at 1 kHz: the inductor is about +63 Ω, the capacitor about −159 Ω, so the net is −96 Ω, and the combination behaves like a capacitor. At about 1.6 kHz they cancel exactly, which is resonance. Above that it's inductive. This is exactly what happens with a real decoupling capacitor: below its self-resonant frequency it's capacitive, and above it the package inductance wins and the reactance goes positive.

It also shows up on a Smith chart, where capacitive loads sit in the lower half, and in power systems, where capacitors supply reactive power to cancel the lagging current of motors."
:::

::: terms
- **Reactance ($X$)**: the imaginary part of impedance, in ohms; stores and returns energy rather than dissipating it.
- **Capacitive reactance**: $X_C = -1/(\omega C)$ (signed), often quoted as the magnitude $1/(2\pi fC)$.
- **Inductive reactance**: $X_L = +\omega L$.
- **Leading / lagging**: in a capacitive circuit current leads voltage; in an inductive circuit current lags.
- **Susceptance ($B$)**: imaginary part of admittance; capacitive susceptance $+\omega C$ is positive.
- **Resonance**: the frequency where net reactance is zero, $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$.
- **Reactive power ($Q$)**: $I^2X$ in VAR; negative for capacitors in the usual convention.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "negative" or, worse, "positive, $1/(2\pi fC)$".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Says **negative** and shows why in one line: $1/(j\omega C) = -j/(\omega C)$.
- Explains the physical meaning: current leads voltage by 90°.
- Contrasts with the inductor's positive reactance.
- Reconciles the textbook "positive magnitude $X_C$, $Z = R - jX_C$" convention so there's no ambiguity.
- Uses the sign: series L and C reactances cancel at resonance; below SRF a real capacitor is capacitive, above it inductive.
- Optionally mentions susceptance has the opposite sign, and the physics $e^{-i\omega t}$ convention.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Positive, because $X_C = 1/(2\pi fC)$."** That's the magnitude. In the signed convention the reactance is negative; in the magnitude convention the minus sign lives in $Z = R - jX_C$.
- **Adding magnitudes of L and C reactances.** 63 Ω of inductive and 159 Ω of capacitive reactance in series is a net 96 Ω capacitive, not 222 Ω.
- **Mixing up lead/lag.** Remember "ELI the ICE man": in an inductor (L), E (voltage) leads I; in a capacitor (C), I leads E.
- **Confusing reactance and susceptance signs.** $Y_C = +j\omega C$: capacitive susceptance is positive even though capacitive reactance is negative.
- **Thinking negative reactance means negative resistance or energy generation.** A negative reactance still stores and returns energy; it dissipates nothing.
:::

::: deep
## Where the minus sign comes from

Start from the capacitor law $i = C\,dv/dt$ ([[q0025]]). With phasors, $d/dt \to j\omega$, so $\mathbf I = j\omega C\,\mathbf V$ and

$$Z_C = \frac{\mathbf V}{\mathbf I} = \frac{1}{j\omega C}$$

Now use $j\cdot j = -1$, so $\frac{1}{j} = \frac{j}{j^2} = -j$:

$$Z_C = -j\,\frac{1}{\omega C} = 0 + j\left(-\frac{1}{\omega C}\right)$$

Impedance is written $Z = R + jX$. Matching terms: $R = 0$ and

$$\boxed{X_C = -\frac{1}{\omega C}}$$

For an inductor, $v = L\,di/dt \Rightarrow Z_L = j\omega L$, so $X_L = +\omega L$.

## What the sign means physically

The angle of an impedance is the phase by which voltage leads current:

$$\angle Z = \arctan\frac{X}{R}$$

- $X > 0$ (inductive): voltage leads current.
- $X < 0$ (capacitive): voltage lags current, i.e. **current leads**.

For a capacitor the reason is visible in the time domain. If $v = V_m\sin\omega t$, then $i = C\,dv/dt = \omega CV_m\cos\omega t$. The cosine reaches its peak a quarter cycle before the sine. Current is largest when the voltage is crossing zero (where the slope is steepest) and zero when the voltage is at its peak (zero slope).

## Two conventions, one physics

You'll meet two ways of writing the same thing:

| Convention | Capacitive reactance | Series RLC impedance |
|---|---|---|
| Signed reactance (used on this page) | $X_C = -1/(\omega C)$ | $Z = R + j(X_L + X_C)$ |
| Magnitude reactance (many intro texts) | $X_C = +1/(\omega C)$ | $Z = R + j(X_L - X_C)$ |

Both give exactly the same $Z$. If an interviewer says "but $X_C = 1/(2\pi fC)$ is positive", the right response is: "Yes, as a magnitude, and then the impedance is $R - jX_C$. The reactive part of the impedance is negative."

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Physics and some electromagnetics texts use $e^{-i\omega t}$ time dependence instead of the engineering $e^{j\omega t}$. In that convention $Z_C = +i/(\omega C)$ and every reactance changes sign. Nothing physical changes. If you see an "inductive" impedance with a negative imaginary part, check which convention the source uses.

## Using the sign: reactances add algebraically

In series, impedances add, so reactances add **with their signs**. Take $L = 10$ mH and $C = 1$ µF in series with $R = 50$ Ω at 1 kHz:

- $X_L = +2\pi\cdot1000\cdot0.01 = +62.8$ Ω
- $X_C = -1/(2\pi\cdot1000\cdot10^{-6}) = -159.2$ Ω
- $X_{net} = -96.3$ Ω → the circuit is **capacitive** at 1 kHz.
- $|Z| = \sqrt{50^2 + 96.3^2} = 108.5$ Ω, $\angle Z = -62.6^\circ$ (current leads).

Resonance is where they cancel: $\omega_0 L = 1/(\omega_0C)$, so $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC}) = 1.59$ kHz. Above that, $X_{net} > 0$ and the same circuit is inductive.

## The same story inside every real capacitor

A real capacitor is an RLC in series: ESR, ESL and C ([[q0030]]). Its net reactance is $\omega\,\text{ESL} - 1/(\omega C)$:

- Below the self-resonant frequency: negative, the part acts as a capacitor.
- At SRF: zero, $|Z| = \text{ESR}$ (the minimum).
- Above SRF: positive, the part acts as an inductor ([[q0031]]).

So "does a capacitor have negative reactance?" has a refined answer for real parts: **yes, below its self-resonant frequency**. A 100 nF 0402 with about 1 nH of mounted ESL turns inductive around 16 MHz.

## Admittance flips the sign again

For parallel circuits it's easier to add admittances, $Y = G + jB$. For a capacitor:

$$Y_C = \frac{1}{Z_C} = j\omega C \Rightarrow B_C = +\omega C$$

So capacitive *susceptance* is positive while capacitive *reactance* is negative. Inductive susceptance is $-1/(\omega L)$. Smith chart users switching between impedance and admittance grids live with this flip constantly.

## Reactive power: capacitors "supply" VARs

Average power into an ideal reactance is zero, but there's a reactive power $Q = I_{rms}^2X = V_{rms}^2/X$. With $X_C < 0$, $Q_C < 0$: by convention a capacitor *supplies* reactive power while an inductor *absorbs* it. That's why a capacitor bank across an induction motor improves power factor: its leading current cancels the motor's lagging magnetizing current. Example: 100 µF on 120 V, 60 Hz: $X_C = -26.5$ Ω, $|Q| = 120^2/26.5 = 543$ VAR.
:::

::: applications
- **Impedance matching**: an antenna that measures $50 - j20$ Ω (capacitive) needs +20 Ω of series inductive reactance to cancel it.
- **Decoupling above SRF**: knowing a capacitor's reactance turns positive above SRF explains why multiple values and low-ESL packages are used ([[q0031]]).
- **Power-factor correction**: capacitor banks supply negative reactive power to cancel inductive motor loads.
- **LC resonant tanks and filters**: oscillators, RF filters and wireless-charging coils rely on L and C reactances cancelling at $f_0$.
- **Crystal oscillators**: load capacitors pull the crystal's frequency by adding negative reactance to its slightly inductive operating point.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Engineering phasor convention** ($e^{j\omega t}$). Physics' $e^{-i\omega t}$ flips all reactance signs.
- **Signed reactance convention**: $Z = R + jX$ with $X_C$ negative. With the magnitude convention, $X_C$ is positive and $Z = R - jX_C$; both are correct if used consistently.
- **Ideal capacitor**, or a real one below its SRF. Above SRF the net reactance of a real part is positive.
- **Sinusoidal steady state**: reactance is a frequency-domain concept.
:::

::: followups
### What is the net reactance of a 100 nF capacitor in series with 10 µH at 100 kHz? Is it capacitive or inductive?
$X_L = 2\pi\cdot10^5\cdot10^{-5} = +6.28$ Ω. $X_C = -1/(2\pi\cdot10^5\cdot10^{-7}) = -15.9$ Ω. Net = −9.6 Ω: capacitive. Resonance is at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{10^{-5}\cdot10^{-7}}) = 159$ kHz, so 100 kHz is below resonance, consistent.

### If an impedance analyser reads $Z = 2 + j5$ Ω for a "capacitor", what's going on?
The positive reactance means it's inductive at that frequency: you're above the part's self-resonant frequency, where ESL dominates. Lower the test frequency, or the part may be a large capacitor with a lot of lead or mounting inductance. Check against $f_{SRF} = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{L_{ESL}C})$.

### Does a capacitor in parallel with an inductor have positive or negative reactance?
It depends on frequency. Parallel LC: $Z = j\omega L/(1 - \omega^2LC)$. Below resonance the denominator is positive, so the reactance is positive (inductive: the inductor's lower impedance dominates). Above resonance it's negative (capacitive). At resonance the ideal tank's impedance goes to infinity. That's the opposite of the series case.

### How does the sign of reactance relate to which way a load's current is shifted on a scope?
With voltage on one channel and current (via a current probe or sense resistor) on the other, a capacitive load (negative X) shows the current waveform peaking *earlier* than the voltage. An inductive load shows the current peaking later. The time shift divided by the period, times 360°, is the phase angle.

### Why is capacitive susceptance positive if capacitive reactance is negative?
$Y = 1/Z$. For $Z_C = -j/(\omega C)$, $Y_C = 1/(-j/(\omega C)) = j\omega C$. Inverting a purely imaginary number flips its sign ($1/(-j) = j$). So susceptance and reactance always have opposite signs for a pure reactive element.
:::

::: interpretations
"Positive **of** negative" is a typo for "positive **or** negative". The question has a sibling in the inductor section, [[q0042]] ("Does an inductor have positive of negative reactance?"), whose answer is positive. Some interviewers use the magnitude convention ($X_C = 1/(2\pi fC)$, always positive), so a complete answer states "negative" and explains the $Z = R - jX_C$ form so both readings are covered.
