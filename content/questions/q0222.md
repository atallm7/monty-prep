::: testing
Whether you know what "dropout" actually means and can **map it onto the right circuit**. Dropout is an LDO concept: the minimum $V_{in}-V_{out}$ for the regulator to stay in regulation. A buck has its own version (the maximum duty cycle). The interviewer is checking if you:

1. Define dropout correctly and say where it comes from (pass-device on-resistance).
2. Connect headroom to efficiency, ripple/PSRR and heat for an LDO, and to duty-cycle limits for a buck.
3. Can reason about how $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$ and $I_{out}$ each move efficiency and ripple in a buck, with equations, not just "it gets worse".
:::

::: why
Choosing between an LDO and a buck, and checking that a rail still regulates when a battery sags, are daily decisions. A 3.3 V rail from a single Li-ion cell (3.0 to 4.2 V) lives right at the dropout edge. Dropout also explains why batteries seem to have "less capacity" than their rating: below the dropout point the regulator quits and the energy left in the cell is wasted. Related pages: [[q0211]] (why buck beats LDO), [[q0181]] (100% and 0% duty), and [[q0221]] (frequency).
:::

::: concepts
- **Dropout voltage ($V_{DO}$)**: the smallest $V_{in}-V_{out}$ at which an LDO still holds regulation, roughly $I_{out}R_{DS(on),pass}$.
- **Linear regulator efficiency**: $\eta\approx V_{out}/V_{in}$, with the excess dissipated as heat. See [[q0211]].
- **Duty cycle limits**: a buck regulates only if $D\le D_{max}$, set by minimum off-time or the bootstrap refresh. See [[q0181]].
- **Ripple relations**: $\Delta I_L=V_{out}(1-V_{out}/V_{in})/(Lf_{sw})$ is largest at maximum $V_{in}$. See [[q0195]].
- **PSRR**: an LDO's ability to reject input noise, which collapses as it approaches dropout.
- **Load-dependent loss**: fixed losses dominate at light load, $I^2R$ at heavy load. See [[q0217]].
:::

::: answer
"Dropout voltage is mostly an LDO term: it's how much headroom the regulator needs between input and output before it falls out of regulation. It's basically the output current times the on-resistance of the pass transistor, so it grows with load. For an LDO, efficiency is just $V_{out}/V_{in}$, so more headroom is more heat, and close to dropout the pass device is wide open so you lose gain and the power-supply rejection collapses, which means ripple and noise from the input leak to the output.

A buck has a similar limit but it shows up as maximum duty cycle. If $V_{in}$ drops toward $V_{out}$, the duty has to go toward 100 percent, and the controller runs out of room because it needs a minimum off-time. In that corner you get a dropout of roughly $I_{out}$ times the high-side resistance plus inductor DCR, and some parts just hold the switch on.

For a buck, higher $V_{in}$ means more ripple current and more switching loss, so efficiency drops and ripple grows. Higher $V_{out}$ at the same input usually improves efficiency because the same loss is spread over more power. And output current gives the familiar curve: bad at light load from fixed losses, best in the middle, and falling at the top from $I^2R$."
:::

::: technical
**LDO dropout.** For a PMOS or PNP-type pass device with on-resistance $R_{on}$, $V_{DO}\approx I_{out}R_{on}$ (so it scales with load and temperature). Efficiency is $\eta=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_q)}\approx\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}$. Pass-device dissipation is $(V_{in}-V_{out})I_{out}$. Near dropout the loop gain and PSRR fall because the pass FET leaves saturation and acts as a resistor, so input ripple passes through to the output.

**Buck dropout.** The maximum duty cycle $D_{max}$ is limited by minimum off-time ($D_{max}=1-t_{off,min}f_{sw}$) or, for NFET high sides, the bootstrap capacitor refresh. Regulation needs approximately
$$V_{in}\ge\frac{V_{out}+I_{out}(D_{max}R_{HS}+(1-D_{max})R_{LS}+R_{DCR})}{D_{max}}$$
In 100% duty mode, $V_{out}\approx V_{in}-I_{out}(R_{HS}+R_{DCR})$ and the ripple vanishes since nothing switches.

**Buck dependence on operating point.**
- $V_{in}$ up: $\Delta I_L=\frac{V_{out}(1-V_{out}/V_{in})}{Lf_{sw}}$ rises, overlap and $C_{oss}$ losses rise as $V_{in}$ and $V_{in}^2$, $D$ shrinks (minimum on-time risk).
- $V_{out}$ up: fixed losses are shared over more output power, so $\eta$ improves; ripple current peaks near $V_{out}=V_{in}/2$.
- $I_{out}$ up: conduction loss grows as $I^2$, ripple current is independent of $I_{out}$ (in CCM), and below $\Delta I/2$ the converter goes to DCM.
:::

::: short

For an LDO, efficiency is $V_{out}/V_{in}$ and PSRR collapses near dropout. A buck has the equivalent as a maximum duty cycle limit, where it needs about $V_{out}$ plus $I\cdot(R_{on}+DCR)$. In a buck, more $V_{in}$ means more ripple and more switching loss; more $V_{out}$ helps efficiency; and output current gives the curve that is poor at light load and slightly drooping at heavy load."
:::

::: long
"First, dropout is really an LDO concept, so let me start there. An LDO has a pass transistor, and it needs some voltage across it to stay in its gain region. Dropout is the minimum headroom, roughly the load current times the on-resistance, so something like 200 mV at 1 A. Efficiency of an LDO is basically $V_{out}$ over $V_{in}$, so 5 V to 3.3 V is 66 percent and the rest is heat: 1.7 W at 1 A. When $V_{in}$ falls close to dropout, the pass device is fully on and acts like a resistor. The loop loses gain, so the power supply rejection falls from maybe 60 dB to 20 or 30 dB, and ripple from the input shows up on the output.

A buck's equivalent is the duty-cycle limit. To keep regulating when $V_{in}$ falls toward $V_{out}$, the duty has to approach 100 percent. The minimum off-time, or the time the bootstrap needs to recharge, limits that, so you lose regulation somewhere around 90 to 99 percent duty. Past that, some parts go into 100 percent mode, hold the high-side on, and the output just follows the input minus $I(R_{on}+DCR)$ with no ripple.

Now the operating point. Higher input voltage on a buck raises the inductor ripple current, since there's more volt-seconds across the inductor in the on-state, and raises switching losses. So efficiency drops a bit, 97.5 percent at 5 V to maybe 96 percent at 24 V in my model. Higher output voltage at the same input improves efficiency since the fixed losses are amortised over more power. Output current: at light load fixed losses dominate and efficiency crashes, in the middle it peaks, and at heavy load the $I^2R$ terms pull it down. Ripple current is independent of load in CCM, but below half the ripple the inductor current goes discontinuous."
:::

::: terms
- **Dropout voltage ($V_{DO}$)**: minimum $V_{in}-V_{out}$ for an LDO to keep regulating at a given load current and temperature.
- **Headroom**: the actual $V_{in}-V_{out}$ available, compared with $V_{DO}$.
- **PSRR**: power-supply rejection ratio, $20\log(\Delta V_{in}/\Delta V_{out})$ in dB, usually falling with frequency and near dropout.
- **Maximum duty cycle ($D_{max}$)**: the largest on-fraction the buck controller can produce, limited by minimum off-time or bootstrap refresh.
- **100% duty / LDO mode**: the high-side FET stays on, so $V_{out}\approx V_{in}-I R$.
- **Quiescent current ($I_q$)**: the current the regulator itself draws, mattering at light load.
- **Volt-second balance**: the inductor's average voltage over a cycle must be zero in steady state, which sets $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating dropout as "the output voltage drop under load."** That is load regulation. Dropout is the minimum *input-output* difference for regulation.
- **Saying an LDO's efficiency is high near dropout and so LDOs are always efficient.** It is only efficient when $V_{out}\approx V_{in}$, and the quiescent current still matters at light load.
- **Believing buck ripple gets worse at heavy load.** In CCM the ripple current is set by $V$, $L$ and $f$ and does not depend on $I_{out}$ (the output *voltage* ripple is unchanged too).
- **Forgetting buck dropout.** A 3.3 V buck cannot regulate from a 3.2 V battery. It needs $V_{in}\gtrsim V_{out}+I(R_{on}+DCR)$ and in practice more if $D_{max}<100\%$.
- **Using maximum efficiency as the only metric.** At light load the number is dominated by quiescent and fixed losses.
:::

::: deep
## Where "dropout" comes from

An LDO is a pass transistor controlled by an error amplifier. To act as a controlled current source or variable resistor, the pass FET needs a minimum drain-source voltage. Below that, the FET is deep in triode, its resistance is as low as it can get ($R_{DS(on)}$), and the amplifier can't lower it further. The remaining relationship is simply Ohm's law:

$$V_{DO}\approx I_{out}\,R_{DS(on)}$$

A PMOS LDO with 200 mΩ pass device has about 200 mV of dropout at 1 A and 100 mV at 0.5 A. $R_{DS(on)}$ rises with temperature, so dropout is worse when hot. A PNP or Darlington (older "standard" LDOs) needs 0.5 to 2 V (check parts), and NMOS pass LDOs with a charge pump or auxiliary supply get very low dropout.

## What happens to efficiency and ripple in an LDO

Efficiency is the ratio of power out to power in:
$$\eta=\frac{V_{out}I_{out}}{V_{in}(I_{out}+I_q)}$$
For $V_{in}=5$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I_{out}=1$ A: $\eta=66\%$ and the pass device burns $(5-3.3)\cdot1=1.7$ W. Ripple at the output comes from input ripple times PSRR, and PSRR depends on loop gain. Far from dropout there might be 60 to 70 dB at low frequency; when headroom approaches $V_{DO}$ the loop gain falls and PSRR can drop by tens of dB.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact PSRR numbers vary a lot by part and frequency. Look at the datasheet's PSRR-versus-headroom graph. The qualitative drop near dropout is universal; the 60 dB to 20-30 dB figures here are illustrative.

## The buck version of dropout

A buck has no pass device in the linear sense, but the same question arises: how low can $V_{in}$ go before it cannot hold $V_{out}$? The answer is determined by how much of the cycle the high-side switch can be on.

Average inductor voltage must be zero in steady state, so $V_{out}\approx D V_{in}-I_{out}(\text{resistive drops})$. With $D\le D_{max}$:

$$V_{in,min}\approx\frac{V_{out}+I_{out}\left(D_{max}R_{HS}+(1-D_{max})R_{LS}+R_{DCR}\right)}{D_{max}}$$

For a 3.3 V, 2 A rail with $R_{HS}=15$ mΩ, $R_{LS}=8$ mΩ, DCR 10 mΩ:

| $D_{max}$ | $V_{in,min}$ |
|---|---|
| 100% | 3.35 V |
| 99% | 3.38 V |
| 95% | 3.53 V |
| 90% | 3.72 V |

Where does $D_{max}$ come from? Two common sources: a minimum off-time ($D_{max}=1-t_{off,min}f_{sw}$; 100 ns at 500 kHz gives 95%, at 2 MHz only 80%), and the bootstrap capacitor, which is recharged during the off-time via the low-side switch, so an NFET high-side cannot stay on forever without a refresh (parts often force a short off pulse every few tens of µs, or use a charge pump). That is why some buck datasheets say "100% duty cycle operation" and others cap at 90 to 95%.

In 100% mode, the output follows the input: $V_{out}=V_{in}-I\cdot(R_{HS}+R_{DCR})$. For 2 A and 25 mΩ total, the drop is only 50 mV, giving ~98.5% efficiency at $V_{in}=3.4$ V. This is a big advantage over an LDO for battery operation.

The buck still loses a little at high $V_{in}$ because overlap, $C_{oss}$ and ripple losses grow with $V_{in}$. Model numbers are optimistic; the shape is the point.

Reading numbers from the model:

| $V_{in}$ | Buck $\eta$ | LDO $\eta$ | Buck $\Delta I_L$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 V | 97.5% | 66% | 0.48 A |
| 12 V | 97.0% | 27.5% | 1.02 A |
| 24 V | 95.8% | 13.7% | 1.21 A |

**Effect of $V_{in}$ on ripple.** The ripple current $\Delta I_L=\frac{V_{out}(1-V_{out}/V_{in})}{Lf_{sw}}$ grows with $V_{in}$ and saturates at $V_{out}/(Lf_{sw})$: 1.4 A at infinite $V_{in}$ for this inductor. So the design must be checked at maximum $V_{in}$, which is why inductor selection is done at the worst-case corner.

**Effect of $V_{out}$.** At 12 V in and 2 A out the model gives 91.1% at 1 V, 94.8% at 1.8 V, 97.0% at 3.3 V, 98.0% at 5 V, 98.7% at 8 V. The loss in watts barely changes, so the same loss is a smaller fraction of a bigger output power. Also, a low $V_{out}$ means a small duty cycle and short on-time (minimum on-time trouble, [[q0221]]). The inductor ripple current for fixed $V_{in}$ peaks at $V_{out}=V_{in}/2$, because $V_{out}(V_{in}-V_{out})$ is maximised there.

**Effect of $I_{out}$.**

| $I_{out}$ | Efficiency (model) |
|---|---|
| 10 mA | 40.8% |
| 100 mA | 86.5% |
| 1 A | 96.9% |
| 2 A | 97.0% |
| 6 A | 95.3% |

At light load the fixed losses (gate charge, $C_{oss}$, controller bias) dominate. In the middle the curve flattens, and at high current the $I^2R$ terms pull it down. The inductor ripple current does not depend on $I_{out}$ in CCM. When $I_{out}<\Delta I_L/2$ (here 0.51 A) the converter enters DCM or PFM, which changes the picture entirely ([[q0229]], [[q0230]]).

::: applications
- **Li-ion powered 3.3 V rail (cell 3.0 to 4.2 V)**: a buck with 100% duty mode or a buck-boost, because an LDO wastes up to 0.9 V of headroom and quits near 3.5 V.
- **Post-regulation of a noisy buck with a low-dropout LDO** for a PLL or ADC reference: keep headroom larger than $V_{DO}$ with margin, so PSRR stays high at the switching frequency.
- **12 V automotive/industrial to 3.3 V logic**: a buck, since an LDO would dissipate 8.7 V × I.
- **Battery-powered sensor nodes**: LDO or buck chosen by load profile; sleeping at µA favours a nanoamp-$I_q$ LDO.
- **High step-down converters (24 V to 1 V)**: low duty cycle, so minimum on-time and high $V_{in}$ ripple determine the frequency and inductor.
:::

::: assumptions
- **"Dropout" interpretation.** I treat it as the LDO headroom definition, extended to the buck's duty-cycle limit. If the interviewer means a different thing (for example "$V_{in}-V_{out}$ in general"), the buck trends in the second half answer that.
- **CCM** for the ripple formulas, with a fixed inductor.
- **Loss model is illustrative**: real $R_{DS(on)}$, $Q_G$ and $C_{oss}$ differ and temperature changes $R_{DS(on)}$ by 30 to 60% across the range.
- **Fixed-frequency PWM.** COT and hysteretic converters change frequency with $V_{in}$ and $V_{out}$, so both the ripple and loss trends move somewhat.
- **Other topologies**: a buck-boost or 4-switch converter has no dropout, since it can step up as well as down.
:::

::: followups
### An LDO is rated for 300 mV dropout at 1 A. Can you run 3.3 V out from 3.5 V in at 1 A?
The headroom is 0.2 V, which is less than 0.3 V, so no: it will fall out of regulation, with $V_{out}$ somewhere near $3.5-0.3=3.2$ V (following the input minus the drop). It would work at 0.5 A (about 150 mV dropout, if dropout is linear in current), but PSRR will be low.

### How much heat does a 5 V to 3.3 V LDO produce at 800 mA?
$(5-3.3)\times0.8=1.36$ W. With a $\theta_{JA}$ of 60 °C/W that's an 82 °C rise, too hot for a small package. A buck at 90% efficiency would dissipate $3.3\cdot0.8\cdot(1/0.9-1)=0.29$ W.

### Why does buck inductor ripple peak at $V_{out}=V_{in}/2$?
Ripple is $\frac{V_{out}(V_{in}-V_{out})}{V_{in}Lf_{sw}}$. For fixed $V_{in}$ the numerator is a parabola maximised at $V_{out}=V_{in}/2$, where it equals $V_{in}/(4Lf_{sw})$. In practice we check the ripple at the worst-case combination of max $V_{in}$ and the target $V_{out}$.

### Your buck output sags and gets noisy when the battery drops to 3.5 V (3.3 V out). What's happening?
You are in the dropout region: duty cycle is hitting $D_{max}$, so the loop saturates and no longer regulates. Output follows the input minus resistive drops, and when the controller alternates between 100% and a refresh pulse (to recharge the bootstrap), a low-frequency ripple shows. Fixes: use a part with true 100% mode, a buck-boost, or lower the min. battery voltage spec.

### What if the interviewer says dropout reduces ripple?
In 100% duty mode, there's no switching so no switching ripple, true. But it also means no regulation, so any input noise passes straight to the output. It's "quiet" only because the converter has stopped working as a filter-regulator.
