::: testing
Whether you can define the two gains from the decomposition of two inputs into a **difference** and an **average**, state their ideal values, and connect them to a real number (CMRR) and a real mechanism (mismatch). The interviewer wants:

1. The definitions: $V_d=V_+-V_-$, $V_{cm}=(V_++V_-)/2$, and $V_{out}=A_dV_d+A_{cm}V_{cm}$.
2. Ideal values: **differential gain large (or the designed value), common-mode gain zero, so CMRR infinite**.
3. Why the real value isn't zero: resistor or transistor mismatch, finite tail-current-source resistance, and why it matters (rejecting noise, ground offsets and interference on cables and sensor bridges).
4. A number: CMRR in dB, and what it does to a real measurement.
:::

::: why
Real signals ride on junk: ground offsets between boards, 50/60 Hz pickup on a sensor cable, a bridge sensor sitting at 2.5 V with a 5 mV signal. Amplifiers that look at the *difference* and ignore what's common to both inputs are what make precision measurement, audio lines, current-sense amplifiers and differential serial links work. Being fluent in $A_d$, $A_{cm}$ and CMRR is essential for choosing instrumentation amplifiers, understanding why 1% resistors are not enough, and reading op-amp datasheets.
:::

::: concepts
- **Decomposition of two inputs**: any pair $(V_1,V_2)$ equals a differential part $V_d$ and a common-mode part $V_{cm}$. See [[q0683]].
- **Differential gain $A_d$**: the gain applied to the difference signal.
- **Common-mode gain $A_{cm}$**: the (unwanted) gain applied to the average.
- **CMRR**: $\text{CMRR}=\left|A_d/A_{cm}\right|$, in dB $20\log_{10}$.
- **Op-amp and feedback**: how a difference amplifier is built. See [[q0121]].
- **Resistor matching**: the ratio errors that create $A_{cm}$.
:::

::: answer
"Any pair of input voltages can be split into a differential part, which is the difference between them, and a common-mode part, which is their average. A good differential amplifier amplifies the difference by $A_d$ and ignores the common part, so $V_{out}=A_dV_d+A_{cm}V_{cm}$.

The ideal values are that $A_d$ is large for an open-loop op-amp, or whatever I design for in a closed-loop circuit, say 10, and $A_{cm}$ is exactly zero. So the ratio, the common-mode rejection ratio $A_d/A_{cm}$, is infinite.

In real hardware $A_{cm}$ isn't zero. In a difference amplifier built from four resistors, a mismatch makes the two input paths have slightly different gains, so part of the common-mode voltage leaks to the output. With 1% resistors and a gain of 10, the worst case CMRR is only about 275, or 49 dB. That means 1 V of common-mode noise produces about 37 mV at the output, bigger than the signal from a 1 mV differential input. Real op-amps are specified at 80 to 120 dB CMRR, but the surrounding resistors usually dominate, which is why instrumentation amps use laser-trimmed matched resistor networks."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.** For inputs $V_+$ and $V_-$ (or $V_1,V_2$):
$$V_d=V_+-V_-,\qquad V_{cm}=\frac{V_++V_-}{2},\qquad V_{out}=A_dV_d+A_{cm}V_{cm}$$
In terms of the single-ended gains $A_+$ and $A_-$: $A_d=\tfrac12(A_++|A_-|)$ and $A_{cm}=A_++A_-$ (with $A_-$ negative for an inverting input). Then
$$\text{CMRR}=\left|\frac{A_d}{A_{cm}}\right|,\quad \text{CMRR}_{dB}=20\log_{10}\left|\frac{A_d}{A_{cm}}\right|$$

**Ideal.** $A_{cm}=0$, so CMRR $=\infty$. For an ideal op-amp $A_d\to\infty$ open loop; with feedback the closed-loop $A_d$ is set by the resistors.

**Difference amplifier (four resistors).** $V_1$ through $R_1$ to the inverting input with feedback $R_2$; $V_2$ through $R_3$ to the non-inverting input with $R_4$ to ground:
$$V_{out}=V_2\frac{R_4}{R_3+R_4}\left(1+\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)-V_1\frac{R_2}{R_1}$$
Ideal match $R_4/R_3=R_2/R_1$ gives $V_{out}=\frac{R_2}{R_1}(V_2-V_1)$, $A_{cm}=0$. With resistor tolerance $t$ the worst case is approximately
$$\text{CMRR}\approx\frac{1+A_d}{4t}$$
**Example:** $R_1=R_3=10$ kΩ, $R_2=R_4=100$ kΩ, $A_d\approx10$, $t=1\%$: worst-case combination (two resistors high, two low) gives $A_{cm}=-0.037$, $A_d=10.18$, CMRR $=275$ ($48.8$ dB). With 0.1% resistors: CMRR $\approx2750$ (68.8 dB). A 1 V common-mode signal appears as $37$ mV at the output, versus a $10$ mV output for a 1 mV differential input: the noise is 3.7x the signal.

**Op-amp CMRR.** Datasheet CMRR is typically 70-120 dB at DC for general-purpose parts and falls with frequency (often by 20 dB/decade beyond a few hundred hertz to kHz for many parts). Input-referred, 100 dB CMRR means 1 V of common-mode looks like $1\text{ V}/10^5=10$ µV of differential input error.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> CMRR figures vary strongly by device, supply voltage, input common-mode range and frequency. The values quoted are typical ranges, not specs for any particular part.

**Physical origin in a differential pair.** A diff pair with a tail current source of finite output resistance $R_{tail}$ has $A_{cm}\approx-\dfrac{R_D}{2R_{tail}}$ and $A_d=\tfrac12g_mR_D$ (single-ended output, $g_m$ of each transistor), so CMRR $\approx g_mR_{tail}$. Better tail sources (cascoded, high $R_{tail}$) give better CMRR, and mismatches between $g_m$ or $R_D$ add a term.
:::

::: short
"Split two inputs into a difference and an average. Differential gain is the gain on the difference and common-mode gain is the gain on the average, so the output is $A_dV_d+A_{cm}V_{cm}$. Ideally $A_d$ is large (or the designed gain) and $A_{cm}$ is zero, so the common-mode rejection ratio $A_d/A_{cm}$ is infinite. In practice mismatch leaves a small $A_{cm}$: a four-resistor difference amp with 1% resistors and a gain of 10 has a worst-case CMRR of about 275, or 49 dB, which is why precision designs use matched networks or instrumentation amps."
:::

::: long
"For any two inputs I can write them as a differential part, $V_d=V_+-V_-$, and a common-mode part, the average $V_{cm}$. The amplifier output is $A_dV_d+A_{cm}V_{cm}$. $A_d$ is the differential gain, the one I want, and $A_{cm}$ is the common-mode gain, the one I don't.

The ideal values: differential gain is whatever the design calls for, very large open-loop for an op-amp or 10 for a gain-of-10 difference amp, and common-mode gain is exactly zero. That makes the common-mode rejection ratio, $A_d$ over $A_{cm}$, infinite. We usually quote it in dB.

Why does it matter? Think of a thermocouple or a bridge sensor at 2.5 V with a 5 mV signal, or a long cable picking up 60 Hz hum equally on both wires, or two boards with different grounds. All of those are common-mode. A good amplifier rejects them and amplifies only the difference.

In real circuits $A_{cm}$ isn't zero. In a difference amp built from four resistors, the output depends on the ratio $R_4/R_3$ matching $R_2/R_1$. If they differ, even by 1%, some common-mode leaks through. With 1% parts and a gain of 10 the worst-case CMRR is only $(1+A_d)/(4t)=275$, about 49 dB. For a 1 V common-mode input that's 37 mV at the output, which can swamp a 10 mV signal. So you get matched resistor networks or instrumentation amplifiers with 100 dB-plus CMRR. In an op-amp's input pair, CMRR comes from the tail current source's finite output resistance and from mismatch, and it falls with frequency, so a part with 100 dB at DC might have 60 dB at 10 kHz."
:::

::: terms
- **Differential-mode signal ($V_d$)**: the difference $V_+-V_-$.
- **Common-mode signal ($V_{cm}$)**: the average $(V_++V_-)/2$ that is the same on both inputs.
- **Differential gain ($A_d$)**: gain applied to $V_d$.
- **Common-mode gain ($A_{cm}$)**: gain applied to $V_{cm}$.
- **CMRR**: $|A_d/A_{cm}|$, often in dB.
- **Common-mode input range**: the span of $V_{cm}$ for which the amplifier works normally.
- **Instrumentation amplifier**: a differential amplifier with high input impedance and very high CMRR from matched internal resistors.
- **Tail current source**: the bias current source feeding a differential pair.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer defines the two gains and says "ideal $A_{cm}=0$". An exceptional answer:

- Uses the **decomposition** $V_d$, $V_{cm}$ and writes the output equation.
- Gives the ideal values and the **real mechanism**: resistor mismatch (with $(1+A_d)/(4t)$) or tail-source resistance in a pair.
- Shows a **numerical consequence**: 1% resistors at gain 10 gives 49 dB and a 37 mV error for 1 V of common-mode.
- Mentions **frequency dependence** and that CMRR is specified at a given $V_{cm}$ and frequency.
- Notes the difference between **closed-loop** $A_d$ (set by resistors) and **open-loop** $A_d$ (huge) and ties to instrumentation amps and differential signalling.
- Mentions input-referred interpretation: $V_{cm}/\text{CMRR}$ is an equivalent differential offset.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Confusing common-mode with "noise on one wire".** Common-mode means the *same* signal on both inputs; noise on only one input is differential.
- **Thinking ideal $A_{cm}$ is "1".** The ideal is zero, so rejection is infinite.
- **Treating CMRR as constant.** It falls with frequency, and depends on the common-mode level and the supplies.
- **Assuming matched-looking 1% resistors are enough.** Four 1% resistors can give only 48 dB.
- **Ignoring source impedance imbalance.** A mismatch of the source resistances at the two inputs converts common-mode into differential error, even with perfect resistors.
- **Mixing up dB definitions.** CMRR in dB is $20\log_{10}$ of a voltage-gain ratio.
:::

::: deep
## Decomposing two inputs

Any pair of voltages $V_1$ and $V_2$ can be rewritten as

$$V_1=V_{cm}-\frac{V_d}{2},\qquad V_2=V_{cm}+\frac{V_d}{2}$$

so $V_d=V_2-V_1$ and $V_{cm}=\tfrac12(V_1+V_2)$. This is a change of basis, nothing more. It is useful because an ideal differential amplifier is **orthogonal** in that basis: it gives gain $A_d$ to $V_d$ and zero to $V_{cm}$. The real amplifier has a little of each.

**Derivation.** Superposition. Set $V_2=0$: the circuit is an inverting amplifier, $V_{out1}=-\frac{R_2}{R_1}V_1$. Set $V_1=0$: the non-inverting input is $V_2\frac{R_4}{R_3+R_4}$, amplified by $1+\frac{R_2}{R_1}$. So

$$V_{out}=V_2\frac{R_4}{R_3+R_4}\left(1+\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)-V_1\frac{R_2}{R_1}.$$

Define $a_2=\frac{R_4}{R_3+R_4}\left(1+\frac{R_2}{R_1}\right)$ and $a_1=\frac{R_2}{R_1}$. Then $A_d=\tfrac12(a_1+a_2)$ and $A_{cm}=a_2-a_1$. With matched ratios $a_1=a_2=10$ so $A_{cm}=0$.

## Mismatch makes $A_{cm}$

Let each resistor be off by $\pm1\%$ in the worst-case pattern ($R_1$ low, $R_2$ high, $R_3$ high, $R_4$ low):

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| $a_1=R_2/R_1$ | 10.20 |
| $a_2$ | 10.17 |
| $A_d$ | 10.18 |
| $A_{cm}=a_2-a_1$ | $-0.037$ |
| CMRR $=A_d/|A_{cm}|$ | 275 (48.8 dB) |

The approximation $(1+A_d)/(4t)=11/0.04=275$ matches. A 1 V common-mode level appears as 37 mV at the output.

## Source-impedance imbalance

Even with perfect resistors, the *source* resistances add to $R_1$ and $R_3$. A 100 Ω difference between the two source impedances against a 10 kΩ $R_1$ is a 1% mismatch, the same CMRR hit as above. Instrumentation amps have very high input impedance so that source imbalance matters less (see [[q0451]] for a related current-sense context).

## Op-amp internal CMRR

In a differential pair with tail current source output resistance $R_{tail}$:

$$A_d=\tfrac12g_mR_D\ \text{(single-ended)},\qquad A_{cm}\approx-\frac{R_D}{2R_{tail}}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{CMRR}\approx g_mR_{tail}$$

With $g_m=40$ mS (each BJT biased at 1 mA, so a 2 mA tail) and $R_{tail}=100$ kΩ: CMRR $=4000$, 72 dB; a cascoded tail with $R_{tail}=10$ MΩ gives 100 dB-plus. Mismatch between the two halves adds a term, so op-amp CMRR is usually 80-120 dB. At high frequency the tail capacitance shunts $R_{tail}$ and CMRR falls, typically starting between a hundred hertz and kHz and dropping 20 dB/decade.

## Frequency dependence and common-mode range

CMRR is a function of frequency: stray capacitance in the tail and unequal input capacitances create phase differences between the paths. Datasheets give CMRR vs frequency curves. Also, CMRR is specified over a range of input common-mode voltage (ICMR). Outside it, the input stage loses bias and the amplifier fails completely, not just degraded CMRR. Always check that $V_{cm}$ plus signal stays within the range.

## Real hardware

- **Instrumentation amplifier** (three op-amp or integrated): input buffers give high impedance and a first stage with differential gain and unity common-mode gain, followed by a trimmed difference amplifier; CMRR is typically 90-130 dB at moderate gain.
- **Differential serial links** (LVDS, USB, Ethernet): receivers reject common-mode noise and ground shifts; CMRR and common-mode range are key.
- **Current-sense amplifiers** on a high-side shunt must reject a bus at 12-48 V while measuring millivolts ([[q0451]]).
- **Audio balanced lines**: a differential receiver cancels hum induced equally on both conductors.
:::

::: applications
- **Instrumentation amplifiers** for bridge sensors, ECG and strain gauges.
- **High-side current sensing** where the shunt sits at a high common-mode voltage.
- **Balanced audio and differential data links** that reject induced noise.
- **Op-amp selection**: choosing a CMRR high enough for the common-mode swing and frequency of the application.
- **ADC front ends** with differential inputs: rejecting reference and ground noise.
- **Measurement with a differential probe** across a floating node.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear small-signal model** with constant gains; CMRR varies with frequency and common-mode level in real amplifiers.
- **Four-resistor difference amplifier with an ideal op-amp** for the matching analysis; an op-amp's own finite CMRR adds to it, and source impedances add to $R_1$, $R_3$.
- **Worst-case resistor tolerances** are used for the number; a random draw gives a typical CMRR several times better than the worst case.
- **Open-loop vs closed-loop**: an op-amp's open-loop $A_d$ is about $10^5$ at DC and its datasheet CMRR is a ratio at that gain; in a closed-loop circuit the resistors set $A_d$ and mismatch usually dominates CMRR.
- Sometimes $A_{cm}$ is defined as the single-ended gain of the average of the two inputs, and some texts define $A_d$ differently by a factor of 2: state your definition.
:::

::: followups
### What is the CMRR of a difference amp with gain 1 and 1% resistors?
$(1+A_d)/(4t)=2/0.04=50$, only 34 dB. At gain 1, the leakage is relatively worse because the divider ratio has fewer decades to hide the error, which is why even unity-gain difference amplifiers use matched thin-film resistor networks.

### Why do instrumentation amps often have better CMRR at high gain?
The input stage gives differential gain $G$ and unity common-mode gain, so the overall CMRR of the first stage is multiplied by $G$ (the later difference stage's finite CMRR is divided by the first-stage gain when referred to the input). A gain-100 in-amp can have 120 dB CMRR even if its output stage only has 80 dB.

### How does CMRR translate into input-referred error?
$V_{error}=V_{cm}/\text{CMRR}$. With 100 dB (a ratio of $10^5$) and 5 V of common-mode, the input-referred error is 50 µV; if the signal is 5 mV full-scale that's 1%.

### Why does CMRR get worse with frequency?
Stray and input capacitances differ between the two input paths and the tail source's output capacitance shunts $R_{tail}$, so common-mode current changes with frequency and the paths no longer cancel. The result is a CMRR that is flat at low frequency and falls beyond a corner, typically tens of Hz to kHz.

### Harder: both inputs of a gain-of-10 difference amplifier are tied together and driven with 1 V; the output is 5 mV. What is the CMRR?
The input is purely common-mode, so $A_{cm}=5\text{ mV}/1\text{ V}=0.005$. With $A_d=10$, CMRR $=10/0.005=2000$, which is 66 dB. This is exactly how you measure CMRR on the bench: tie the inputs together, apply a common-mode signal, and refer the output error to the differential gain.

### Scenario: an ECG amplifier shows large 60 Hz hum even though the amplifier has 100 dB CMRR. What do you check?
Source-impedance imbalance (electrode contact resistance differs by kΩ), which turns common-mode into differential; long unshielded leads; a right-leg drive circuit missing or mis-wired; and input-stage common-mode range. Electrode impedance difference of 10 kΩ against the amp's 10 MΩ input is a 0.1% imbalance, which limits effective CMRR to around 60 dB regardless of the amplifier.
:::
