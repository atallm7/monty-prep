::: testing
Whether you can sketch a first-order frequency response **quickly and correctly** with the right numbers on it, and explain why it looks that way:

1. Axes: magnitude in **dB** versus **log frequency**, and phase in **degrees** versus log frequency.
2. Low-pass: flat at 0 dB, a corner at $f_c$, then a straight line falling **−20 dB/decade**; phase from 0° to −90°, passing **−45° at $f_c$**.
3. High-pass: mirror image: rising +20 dB/decade up to $f_c$, then flat; phase from +90° to 0°, +45° at $f_c$.
4. The key marks: −3 dB at $f_c$, ±5.7° and ±84.3° at a decade either side, and why the straight-line asymptote is an approximation.
:::

::: why
Bode plots are the common language for filters, amplifiers and control loops. You'll read them in datasheets (op-amp open-loop gain, filter responses), in loop-stability analysis, and in lab measurements with a network analyzer or a swept sine. A first-order filter is the building block: every real pole or zero in a bigger system looks like this, shifted along the frequency axis. Being able to sketch it by hand, with numbers, shows that you understand the transfer function rather than just remembering a picture.
:::

::: concepts
- **Transfer function**: $H(j\omega) = V_{out}/V_{in}$; for RC low-pass $H = \frac{1}{1+j\omega RC}$. See [[q0010]].
- **Corner (cutoff) frequency**: $f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi RC}$, where $|H| = 1/\sqrt2$. See [[q0133]].
- **Decibels**: $|H|_{dB} = 20\log_{10}|H|$.
- **Log frequency axis**: each decade (×10) takes the same horizontal distance, so power laws become straight lines.
- **Phase of a pole**: $-\arctan(f/f_c)$ for a low-pass.
- **Capacitor impedance**: $1/(j\omega C)$ falls with frequency. See [[q0024]].
:::

::: answer
"I'll take the RC low-pass as the example, with a corner frequency $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$, say 1 kHz. I draw two stacked plots with a log frequency axis.

On the magnitude plot, in dB, the response is flat at 0 dB well below the corner, because the capacitor is basically open. At the corner it's down 3 dB, that's 0.707 of the input. Above the corner it falls along a straight line at minus 20 dB per decade, so 20 dB down at 10 kHz, 40 dB down at 100 kHz. On the straight-line sketch I put the corner where the two asymptotes meet, and the true curve sits 3 dB under that point.

On the phase plot, it starts at 0 degrees at low frequency, passes through minus 45 degrees exactly at the corner, and goes toward minus 90 at high frequency. It's about minus 6 degrees one decade below the corner and minus 84 one decade above.

If it's a high-pass, I mirror it: the magnitude rises at plus 20 dB per decade up to the corner then is flat, and the phase goes from plus 90 down to zero, plus 45 at the corner. Same corner, same minus 3 dB point."
:::

::: technical
**Low-pass (RC, output across C).**
$$H(j\omega)=\frac{1}{1+j\omega RC} = \frac{1}{1+jf/f_c},\quad f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi RC}$$
$$|H| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}},\qquad \angle H = -\arctan\!\left(\frac{f}{f_c}\right)$$

**High-pass (RC, output across R).**
$$H(j\omega)=\frac{j\omega RC}{1+j\omega RC}=\frac{jf/f_c}{1+jf/f_c},\quad |H| = \frac{f/f_c}{\sqrt{1+(f/f_c)^2}},\quad \angle H = 90^\circ-\arctan\!\left(\frac{f}{f_c}\right)$$

**Key values (low-pass).**

| $f/f_c$ | $|H|$ | dB | phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.01 | 1.000 | 0.000 | −0.57° |
| 0.1 | 0.995 | −0.043 | −5.71° |
| 0.5 | 0.894 | −0.97 | −26.6° |
| 1 | 0.707 | −3.01 | −45° |
| 2 | 0.447 | −6.99 | −63.4° |
| 10 | 0.0995 | −20.04 | −84.3° |
| 100 | 0.0100 | −40.00 | −89.4° |

**Asymptotes.** Low-frequency asymptote: 0 dB. High-frequency asymptote: $-20\log_{10}(f/f_c)$, i.e. −20 dB/decade (−6 dB/octave), crossing 0 dB at $f_c$. Maximum error of the straight-line approximation: 3 dB, at $f_c$. A common phase approximation is a straight line from 0° at $0.1f_c$ to −90° at $10f_c$ through −45° at $f_c$, which is within about 6° of the true curve.
:::

::: short

Phase goes from 0 to −90 degrees, with exactly −45 at the corner. A high-pass is the mirror image: rising 20 dB per decade up to the corner then flat, phase from +90 to 0, +45 at the corner."
:::

::: long
"I'll draw the RC low-pass, a series resistor with the output across the capacitor. Its transfer function is $1/(1 + j\omega RC)$, so the corner frequency is $1/(2\pi RC)$. Let's say it's 1 kHz.

I draw two plots, one above the other, sharing a logarithmic frequency axis. The top one is magnitude in dB. At low frequency the capacitor is an open circuit, so there's no attenuation and the line is flat at 0 dB. At 1 kHz the magnitude is $1/\sqrt{2}$, which is minus 3 dB. Beyond that the capacitor increasingly shorts the output, and the response falls at 20 dB per decade, that's 20 dB down at 10 kHz, 40 dB down at 100 kHz. I sketch it as two straight lines meeting at the corner and then round it off, with the real curve 3 dB below the intersection.

The bottom plot is phase. At low frequency the output follows the input, so it's zero. At the corner the phase is exactly minus 45 degrees, and at high frequency it tends to minus 90, since the capacitor makes the circuit behave like an integrator. A decade below the corner it's about minus 6 degrees and a decade above about minus 84, so I draw an S-shaped curve, or a straight line from 0.1 to 10 times the corner.

For the high-pass I swap R and C. Same corner frequency, but now the magnitude rises at 20 dB per decade up to the corner and then flattens at 0 dB, and the phase leads: plus 90 at low frequency, plus 45 at the corner, zero at high frequency. The thing I'd label on every sketch is the corner frequency, the minus 3 dB point, the slope, and the phase at the corner."
:::

::: terms
- **Bode plot**: magnitude (dB) and phase (degrees) versus log frequency.
- **Corner / cutoff / −3 dB frequency ($f_c$)**: $1/(2\pi RC)$ for a first-order RC filter.
- **Pole**: a root of the denominator, here $s = -1/RC$; each pole contributes −20 dB/decade and up to −90° of phase.
- **Decade**: a factor of 10 in frequency.
- **Octave**: a factor of 2 in frequency; −20 dB/decade ≈ −6.02 dB/octave.
- **Asymptote**: the straight-line approximation of the curve far from the corner.
- **Phase lag / lead**: output lagging (negative phase, LPF) or leading (positive, HPF) the input.
- **Passband / stopband**: the frequencies passed (≈0 dB) and attenuated.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws a rolled-off curve and writes "−20 dB/dec".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Labels the axes (log frequency, dB, degrees) and **marks $f_c$ with a number** and the −3 dB point.
- Shows both the **asymptotes and the true curve**, noting the 3 dB gap at $f_c$.
- Gives the **phase anchors**: 0°, −45° at $f_c$, −90°, and the −5.7° / −84.3° values at ±1 decade.
- Draws the **high-pass mirror** and says LPF phase lags while HPF phase leads.
- Explains the physics: the capacitor goes from open to short with frequency, and at high frequency the RC low-pass acts as an integrator ($-90^\circ$, $-20$ dB/dec).
- States how to read it back: for $f \gg f_c$, $|H|\approx f_c/f$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using a linear frequency axis.** Then the response doesn't show straight-line asymptotes and you can't see multiple decades.
- **Putting −3 dB at the wrong place**, or drawing the corner where the *curve* is at −20 dB. The corner is where the asymptotes meet, and the curve is 3 dB down there.
- **Phase of −90° at the corner.** It's −45°. A first-order system only reaches −90° asymptotically.
- **Mixing up lag and lead.** LPF output lags (negative phase), HPF output leads (positive phase).
- **Saying "−20 dB per decade" without specifying it's per pole** and holds only well above the corner. Near the corner the slope is gentler.
- **Plotting amplitude on a linear scale** and calling it a Bode plot. The point of dB is that gains multiply as dB add.
- **Forgetting the 0 dB passband gain** is 1 (unity) for a passive RC; an active filter can have gain.
:::

::: deep
## Where the shape comes from

Start from the circuit: a series resistor, a capacitor to ground, output across the capacitor.

```circuit
 {"t":"wire","pts":[[6,3],[9,3]]},{"t":"dot","at":[8,3]},
 {"t":"C","a":[8,3],"b":[8,7],"label":"C","value":"159 nF"},
 {"t":"wire","pts":[[8,7],[8,8]]},{"t":"gnd","at":[8,8]},
 {"t":"wire","pts":[[8,3],[11,3]]},{"t":"term","at":[11,3],"label":"V_{out}","side":"right"}
]}
```
Figure: With no load, the circuit is a divider between $R$ and the capacitor's impedance $1/(j\omega C)$. Low frequency: $Z_C\to\infty$ so $V_{out}= V_{in}$. High frequency: $Z_C\to 0$ so $V_{out}\to 0$.

The divider gives
$$H(j\omega)=\frac{1/(j\omega C)}{R+1/(j\omega C)}=\frac{1}{1+j\omega RC}.$$
The magnitude is $|H|=1/\sqrt{1+(\omega RC)^2}$ and the phase $-\arctan(\omega RC)$. The corner is where $\omega RC=1$, i.e. $R = |Z_C|$, so the resistor and capacitor drop equal-magnitude voltages that are 90° apart, giving $1/\sqrt2$ and 45°.

## How to sketch it in 30 seconds

1. Compute $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$ and mark it on a log axis.
2. **Magnitude.** Draw a flat 0 dB line to the left of $f_c$, then a line of slope −20 dB/decade through $(f_c,\,0\text{ dB})$. Mark one point on it: $-20$ dB at $10f_c$. Round the corner so that the curve is at −3 dB at $f_c$.
3. **Phase.** Mark 0° far left, −90° far right and −45° at $f_c$. Draw an S-curve. Quick version: a straight line from 0° at $0.1f_c$ to −90° at $10f_c$.
4. Label the three numbers (corner, −3 dB, slope) and the phase at the corner.

The straight-line approximations are accurate to within 3 dB (magnitude, worst case at the corner) and about 6° (phase).

## What the asymptote really says

For $f\gg f_c$, $|H|\approx f_c/f$: the output amplitude is inversely proportional to frequency. That is the signature of an integrator. Doubling the frequency halves the output, which is −6 dB. A tenfold increase in frequency gives −20 dB. The phase tends to −90° for the same reason: integrating a sine gives a cosine delayed by a quarter cycle.

At very low frequencies the phase approaches 0° because the capacitor current is tiny and the resistor drops nothing.

## Time-domain connection

A phase of −45° at $f_c$ is a time delay of one eighth of a period ($45/360$). For a 1 kHz sine, the output lags by 125 µs, and has 70.7% of the amplitude. The low-pass also corresponds to a time constant $\tau = RC = 1/(2\pi f_c)$, which is 159 µs for a 1 kHz corner.

## Real-world notes

- **Source and load matter.** A real source resistance adds to $R$, and a load resistance forms a divider at DC and moves the corner (see [[q0130]]).
- **Parasitics limit the stopband.** At high frequency, stray capacitance and inductance create paths that bypass the filter, so the measured response flattens out instead of continuing down at −20 dB/decade (see [[q0131]]).
- **Measurement.** A network analyzer or a swept sine with an oscilloscope gives the same magnitude and phase. The phase is measured as the time difference between zero-crossings: $\phi = 360^\circ f\,\Delta t$.

## Why the corner is rounded: slopes you can actually derive

The "−20 dB/decade" slope is an asymptote. The true local slope of the magnitude curve follows from differentiating $|H|_{dB} = -10\log_{10}(1+x^2)$ with $x = f/f_c$ against $\log_{10}x$:

$$\frac{d|H|_{dB}}{d\log_{10}x} = -20\,\frac{x^2}{1+x^2}$$

At $x = 0.1$ the slope is −0.2 dB/decade (flat), at $x = 1$ it is exactly **−10 dB/decade** (half the asymptotic value), and at $x = 10$ it is −19.8 dB/decade. So the corner is not a kink; the curve eases from 0 to −20 over about two decades, centered on $f_c$. The phase behaves the same way: $d\varphi/d\ln x = -x/(1+x^2)$, which at the corner is −0.5 rad per e-fold, i.e. about **−66° per decade** (compute: $0.5 \times 57.3 \times 2.303 = 66°$). That steep middle section is why the straight-line phase approximation (−90° over two decades, i.e. −45° per decade) is only good to about 6°: the real curve is steeper than the line in the middle and flatter at the ends.

The two plots are not independent. For a minimum-phase system like this, the phase is determined by the slope of the magnitude: roughly −90° of phase for every −20 dB/decade of slope, smeared over neighboring frequencies. This is why a measured magnitude slope of −40 dB/decade should come with phase heading for −180°, and why a phase that disagrees with the slope tells you there is a pure delay or a right-half-plane zero hiding somewhere.

## Worked example: a 1 kHz anti-alias filter for a slow sensor

Say you digitize a 50 Hz-bandwidth sensor and want a first-order RC in front of the ADC with $R = 1\,\text{k}\Omega$ and $C = 159\,\text{nF}$, so $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC) = 1.0$ kHz. Check each thing you care about:

| Quantity | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Passband droop at 50 Hz | $-10\log_{10}(1+0.05^2)$ | −0.011 dB |
| Phase at 50 Hz | $-\arctan(0.05)$ | −2.9° |
| Group delay in the passband | $\tau = RC$ | 159 µs |
| Rejection of 50 kHz interference | $-10\log_{10}(1+50^2)$ | −34.0 dB (×0.02) |
| 10–90% step rise time | $2.2\,RC = 0.35/f_c$ | 350 µs |

The passband is essentially untouched, but the rejection at 50 kHz is only a factor of 50 in amplitude. If that noise must be pushed below one 12-bit LSB (about −72 dB relative to full scale), a single pole would need a corner far lower than the signal allows, and you would move to a second-order or higher filter. That is the practical limit of first-order filtering: you buy 20 dB per decade of frequency separation, nothing more.

The rise-time number is worth remembering because it lets you check the filter in the time domain with a square wave: a step through a first-order low-pass has $t_r \approx 0.35/f_{c}$, the same relationship scope vendors use to relate bandwidth and rise time.

## When the simple model breaks

- **Cascading two identical stages does not double the corner.** Two buffered RC stages, each with $f_c = 1$ kHz, have a combined −3 dB point at $f_c\sqrt{\sqrt2 - 1} = 644$ Hz, not 1 kHz, because the two −1.5 dB droops add. Without a buffer the second stage loads the first and the poles split to $(3\pm\sqrt5)/2 \cdot 1/RC$, putting the −3 dB point near 374 Hz.
- **Source resistance and load.** Any extra series resistance adds to $R$. A 100 kΩ-source in front of the 1 kΩ filter would move the corner to roughly 10 Hz. Always ask what drives the filter.
- **Scope probe loading.** A 1× probe is about 1 MΩ in parallel with roughly 100 pF (typical; check your probe). Put it on the 100 kΩ / 1 nF filter node and the DC gain drops by $1M/(1M+100k) = 0.909$ (−0.83 dB), and the capacitance rises 10%. In this particular case the corner lands back at 1.59 kHz only because the resistance ratio and the capacitance ratio happen to cancel; change the values and it shifts. A 10× probe (about 10 MΩ and a dozen pF) is the safer choice for this filter.
- **Capacitor tolerance and dielectric.** A Class II ceramic (X7R) can lose a large fraction of its capacitance under DC bias and drifts with temperature, so a "1 kHz" filter built with one may land well away from it. NP0/C0G or film capacitors keep $f_c$ within their tolerance. Resistor tolerance is usually the smaller term.
- **Stopband floor.** Capacitor ESL and resistor parasitic capacitance create a feedthrough path, so the measured response flattens out far above the corner rather than continuing at −20 dB/decade. See [[q0131]].

## Verifying it on the bench

1. **Swept sine.** Drive $V_{in}$ with a function generator at $0.1f_c$, $f_c$ and $10f_c$ while watching both nodes on the scope. Expect 1.0, 0.707 and 0.0995 amplitude ratios. If the generator is 50 Ω output-matched, include that 50 Ω in $R$ (a 50 Ω source adds 5% to a 1 kΩ resistor).
2. **Phase from time delay.** At $f_c = 1$ kHz, the output zero-crossing should lag by $\Delta t = (45/360)(1\text{ ms}) = 125$ µs. Use $\varphi = 360^\circ f\,\Delta t$.
3. **Find the corner without a sweep.** Raise the frequency until the output amplitude is 70.7% of input, or until the lag is one eighth of a period. Both should agree; if they don't, there is more than one pole.
4. **Step test.** A 1 kHz square wave in, measure the 10–90% rise: about 350 µs, equivalent to $RC = 159$ µs. A network analyzer or a scope with a built-in Bode function gives the whole curve in one sweep.
:::

::: applications
- **Anti-aliasing and ADC input filters**: an RC low-pass with its corner well below the Nyquist frequency.
- **AC coupling / DC blocking**: a series capacitor with a bias resistor forms a high-pass, typically with a corner of a few Hz to tens of Hz for audio.
- **Button debouncing and sensor smoothing**: RC low-pass with corner of a few Hz to hundreds of Hz.
- **Op-amp compensation**: an op-amp's dominant pole gives exactly this response with a very low corner (see [[q0126]]).
- **Control and PLL loop filters**: first-order sections are building blocks for loop filters.
- **Probe and instrument bandwidth**: an oscilloscope's front-end is roughly a first-order low-pass at the scope's bandwidth.
:::

::: assumptions
I used a first-order **passive RC** filter driven from an ideal voltage source and unloaded. A real source resistance adds to $R$ and shifts the corner down, and a load resistance both lowers the DC gain and raises the corner. The same shapes apply to an RL filter ($f_c = R/(2\pi L)$) and to active first-order filters (with an added passband gain and, for an inverting stage, an extra 180° of phase). The "−20 dB/decade" slope is the asymptotic value; within a decade of the corner the real slope is shallower. I plotted ideal components. Real capacitors have ESR and ESL, and resistors have parasitic capacitance, which affect the response many decades above the corner. For a **higher-order** filter the slope is −20n dB/decade with n poles, and the phase reaches −90n°.
:::

::: followups
### Where exactly is the −3 dB point on your sketch, and what is the error of the straight-line approximation there?
At $f_c$ exactly. The two asymptotes (0 dB and the −20 dB/dec line) intersect at 0 dB at $f_c$, but the true curve is 3.01 dB lower, which is the maximum error. One octave away (0.5 or 2 $f_c$) the error is about 1 dB; a decade away it's 0.04 dB.

### What is the phase at 0.1 and 10 times the corner, and why isn't it 0° and −90°?
$-\arctan(0.1) = -5.7°$ and $-\arctan(10) = -84.3°$. A single pole spreads its phase change over roughly two decades around the corner, so you only get within 1° of the limits at about 0.017 and 57 times the corner frequency.

### How does the plot change if I add a load resistor equal to $R$ across the capacitor?
The DC gain becomes $R_L/(R+R_L)=0.5$ (−6 dB), and the corner moves up: the capacitor now sees $R\|R_L = R/2$, so $f_c' = 1/(2\pi (R/2) C) = 2f_c$. The slope above the new corner is still −20 dB/decade. Interestingly the high-frequency asymptote is unchanged: $|H|\approx 0.5\cdot 2f_c/f = f_c/f$, the same as the unloaded filter. Loading just moves the corner out and drops the passband.

### What's the magnitude at 10 kHz for a 1 kHz low-pass? In dB and as a ratio.
$|H| = 1/\sqrt{1+100} = 0.0995$, which is $-20.04$ dB, or about −20 dB. A 1 V input gives 99.5 mV. At 100 kHz it's $-40$ dB (10 mV).

### Harder: you measure the phase of your supposedly first-order filter at 10 times the corner and get −130°. What does this suggest?
More than one pole. A first-order filter can't exceed −90°. The extra −40° suggests another pole or delay: a second filter stage or the source/load interacting, the op-amp's own bandwidth, cable delay or a scope-probe pole. A pure delay gives phase that keeps growing linearly with frequency. Check the circuit with a sweep and compare the magnitude slope: −40 dB/decade would indicate a second pole.
:::

::: interpretations
"A first-order filter" is ambiguous between low-pass and high-pass (and first-order all-pass), so I sketch the **low-pass** in detail and then the high-pass as its mirror. The question's wording "mag/phase response" means a **Bode plot**: magnitude in dB and phase in degrees versus log frequency. If the interviewer wants a single answer, start with the RC low-pass. The circuits are in [[q0130]] and the slope itself is discussed in [[q0131]]. The corner is discussed in [[q0133]].

::: rubric
- Draws magnitude (dB) and phase versus a **log** frequency axis :: Without the right axes, the straight-line behavior is lost.
- Marks the corner $f_c = 1/(2\pi RC)$ and the −3 dB point :: This is the single most important feature of the response.
- Draws the −20 dB/decade roll-off above the corner :: This is the defining slope of a first-order filter.
- Draws phase 0° → −45° at $f_c$ → −90° (LPF) :: Phase is half of the question, and −45° at the corner is a standard check.
