::: testing
Whether you think about **reliability and root cause**, not just the schematic. The interviewer wants you to name the realistic failure modes (open, drift, and the rare short), tie each to a cause (over-power, pulse/surge, over-voltage, thermal cycling and board flex, sulfurization, moisture, ESD on thin films, assembly defects), and say how you'd prevent or detect it (derating, package choice, pulse-rated parts, conformal coat, design margin). The mark of a good answer is "which way does it fail, and what does that do to the circuit?"
:::

::: why
Resistors are simple and very reliable, which is why their failures are surprising and costly: a pull-up that opens leaves a line floating; a feedback resistor that opens in a regulator can send the output to the input voltage; a current-sense resistor that drifts changes protection limits; a high-value divider resistor that leaks changes the ADC reading. Knowing the dominant failure *direction* (usually open) lets you do failure-mode analysis and design for graceful failure.
:::

::: concepts
- **Power dissipation and derating** ([[q0006]], [[q0018]]): thermal stress is the main killer.
- **Pulse and surge energy**: a short pulse can exceed the average-power rating by orders of magnitude, but has its own limit (energy, voltage).
- **Voltage rating**: working voltage and overload; arcing across the film or spiral trim.
- **Thermo-mechanical stress**: CTE mismatch, solder fatigue, board flex.
- **Environmental degradation**: humidity, sulfur, electromigration.
- **Failure direction**: open (most common), parametric drift, rarely short.
:::

::: answer
"The most common way a resistor fails is open circuit, usually from overheating. If you dissipate more than its rating for long enough, the film or wire overheats, the value drifts, and finally it burns open, sometimes with a brown or black mark on the body. A pulse or surge can do the same quickly: a high-energy spike can crack the film or burn through the trim spiral, even if the average power is small.

Second is drift: the value slowly changes because of heat, humidity, or aging, so a precision divider or sense resistor moves out of tolerance. Third are mechanical failures: a cracked body or solder joint from board flexing or thermal cycling, giving an intermittent or open connection. Chip resistors on a flexed board are a classic one. There's also sulfur in the air, which can corrode the silver terminations of thick-film parts and open them, and moisture and contamination on high-value resistors, which show up as leakage.

A short is rare for a resistor, but it can happen through arcing over a too-high voltage, or surface contamination and dendrites in high-impedance circuits.

To prevent it I derate power to about 50 percent, check the voltage and pulse ratings, choose the right type like anti-surge or anti-sulfur parts, mind the layout so the board doesn't flex, and use conformal coating in harsh conditions."
:::

::: technical
| Failure mode | Typical cause | Typical effect | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Open (thermal overstress) | $P>P_{rated}$ (derated), poor thermal path, hot ambient | Film burns open; discoloration | Derate ≥ 50 %, larger package, more copper |
| Open (surge/pulse) | Energy pulse (inrush, ESD, lightning, capacitor discharge) | Spiral trim fuses or film cracks | Pulse-/surge-rated (MELF, wirewound, anti-surge thick-film), series strings |
| Open (mechanical) | Board flex, thermal-cycle fatigue, drop | Cracked body or solder joint; intermittent | Pad layout orientation, keep parts away from board edges/depanelization, underfill for large parts |
| Open (sulfurization) | Sulfur-bearing atmosphere attacks silver end terminations | Terminations become Ag₂S, resistance rises or opens over months | Anti-sulfur series (gold/palladium or different terminations), conformal coat |
| Drift | Long-term heat, moisture, load-life aging, humidity | Value moves (0.1–several %), TCR | Thin-film/foil for precision, derate, seal |
| Short (rare) | Over-voltage arcing, dendritic growth in moisture, carbon track | Low resistance across element | Voltage rating, creepage, cleaning/coating |
| ESD damage | ESD to thin-film or small chips | Latent value shift or opens | ESD-robust types, input protection |
| Assembly defects | Tombstoning, solder voids, cold joint, wrong value | Open/intermittent or wrong value | DFM pad design, AOI, ICT |

**Derating:** most datasheets give full power up to about 70 °C ambient, falling linearly to zero at a maximum film temperature (commonly 125–155 °C): $P_{allowed}(T)=P_{rated}\,\dfrac{T_{max}-T}{T_{max}-70}$.
:::

::: short
"The dominant failure for a resistor is open circuit from overheating or a pulse. Others are drift from heat, humidity or aging, mechanical cracking from board flex or thermal cycling, sulfur corrosion of silver terminations, and rarely a short from arcing or contamination. I prevent them by derating power to about half, checking voltage and pulse ratings, picking anti-surge or anti-sulfur parts when needed, and keeping them away from flex areas."
:::

::: long
"Resistors fail in a few well-known ways, and the dominant direction is open circuit.

First, thermal overstress. If the average dissipation is above the rating, or the board is hot, the film temperature rises, the value drifts, and eventually it burns open. You often see a brown mark. The fix is derating: I run resistors at half their rated power or less, and I account for ambient temperature using the datasheet derating curve.

Second, pulses. A resistor can survive short pulses well above the average rating, but a high-energy pulse from an inrush, an ESD event, or discharging a capacitor can crack the film or fuse the trim cut. So for those places, a bleeder or inrush limiter, I choose pulse-rated parts, like MELF, wirewound, or anti-surge thick-film.

Third, mechanical. Chip resistors can crack from board flex during depanelization or screwing the board down, or the solder joint can fatigue through thermal cycling. That shows up as intermittent or open connections. I orient parts and keep them away from the board edges and mounting holes.

Fourth, environmental. In sulfur-rich atmospheres, silver in the end terminations turns into silver sulfide, which is an insulator, so the resistor goes open over months. Anti-sulfur parts or conformal coating fix that. Humidity and contamination affect high-value resistors by creating leakage paths.

Fifth, drift: the value slowly walks away because of heat or aging, which matters for precision dividers and shunts, and that's why you use thin-film or foil parts.

Shorts are rare, usually from arcing across a too-high voltage or dendrites in a damp, contaminated board."
:::

::: terms
- **Open-circuit failure**: the resistor loses continuity (infinite resistance).
- **Drift**: gradual change in resistance over time or temperature.
- **Derating**: using a part below its rated limit for reliability.
- **Pulse/surge rating**: energy or peak power a part can survive in a short event.
- **Sulfurization**: reaction of silver terminations with sulfur compounds producing insulating silver sulfide.
- **Trim spiral / laser trim**: the cut that sets value; a weak point for pulses.
- **Board flex cracking**: mechanical cracking of a ceramic chip from PCB bending.
- **Load-life stability**: ΔR/R after 1000 h at rated power and temperature.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "resistors burn out if you put too much power through them".

**An exceptional answer**:
- States that **open** is the dominant failure direction and explains what that does to circuits (floating pull-ups, runaway regulator feedback, loss of a current limit).
- Separates average power, pulse energy, and voltage stress as three distinct limits.
- Covers mechanical (flex, thermal cycling), environmental (sulfur, humidity), and assembly (tombstone, solder) causes.
- Gives a derating formula/rule and a worked example.
- Names mitigation by part type (anti-surge, anti-sulfur, MELF, wirewound, thin-film) and layout/process.
- Mentions detection and failure analysis: in-circuit measurements, visual inspection, X-ray, curve-trace.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Resistors can't fail in a dangerous way."** A shorted or open feedback resistor can send a regulator output to the input rail; a burning resistor is a fire hazard.
- **Using average power only.** A repetitive pulse can fail a part even if average power is below rating; and a single big pulse can too.
- **Forgetting temperature derating.** A 125 mW resistor in a 100 °C enclosure can only dissipate about 65 mW or less.
- **Ignoring voltage rating.** A 0603 across 200 V might arc even at tiny power.
- **Assuming a measured 'good' value in circuit means it's fine.** In-circuit, parallel paths mask opens/drift; lift one end to measure.
- **Neglecting mechanical stress**: placing chip resistors perpendicular to flex lines near board edges and V-groove.
:::

::: deep
## Thermal overstress: the most common cause

A resistor's film runs at $T_{film}=T_{amb}+P\theta$. At rated power and 70 °C, $T_{film}$ is at the maximum allowed (commonly around 125–155 °C; check the datasheet). Above that: the film oxidizes and the value drifts; metal and glass binders degrade; eventually, localized hot spots in the film (the trim spiral concentrates current) fuse open.

Figure: Above 70 °C the allowed power falls linearly to zero at the maximum film temperature (155 °C in this example; many parts use 125 °C). An enclosure at 100 °C allows only about 65 % of the rating before any additional derating.

Worked example: an 0603 (100 mW rated) dissipating 80 mW in a 90 °C environment. Allowed: $100\cdot(155-90)/85 = 76$ mW. So 80 mW exceeds the derated limit; and with a 50 % margin, 38 mW is the recommended ceiling. Change to 1206 or reduce the dissipation.

Also check power under **fault**: a resistor between 12 V and ground that is 100 Ω dissipates $144/100=1.44$ W; if it's a 1/4 W part and the fault persists, it will burn out. For safety-critical circuits, use a fusible or flameproof resistor that is designed to open safely.

## Pulses, surges and voltage

Short pulses are limited by **energy** (J) and **peak voltage**, not average power. When a pulse hits, the heat can't spread: the film's trim cut is a narrow current path that heats first. Example: a 100 nF capacitor charged to 400 V discharges 8 mJ into a series resistor: $E=\tfrac12CV^2=\tfrac12\cdot10^{-7}\cdot400^2=8$ mJ. A small general-purpose chip may not survive repeated events like that. Anti-surge thick-film, MELF, or wirewound types handle much more.

Over-voltage: each package has a maximum working voltage and an overload voltage. Exceeding it can arc across the trim cut or along the surface, particularly with contamination. High-value resistors in HV dividers use series strings so each element sees less than its rating, with adequate creepage.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Surge and pulse ratings differ greatly by manufacturer and series. Use the pulse-load curves in the specific datasheet; the numbers here are illustrative.

## Mechanical and thermal-cycling failures

Ceramic chip bodies are brittle, and a board flexed by depanelization, screw tightening or a connector insertion can crack them or the terminations. Thermal-cycling fatigue works the solder joints (CTE mismatch between ceramic and FR-4). Good practice:
- Orient chip parts parallel to the bend axis.
- Keep resistors away from board edges, V-scores and mounting holes.
- Use symmetrical pads to avoid tombstoning during reflow.
- For large parts (2512) in harsh environments, consider flexible terminations, underfill or staking.

## Corrosion, sulfur and moisture

Standard thick-film chips use silver-bearing inner terminations; sulfur-containing air (industrial areas, rubber, certain gasketing) forms silver sulfide, an insulator, and the part drifts up and finally opens, often months after assembly. **Anti-sulfur** series use different termination metallurgy. Moisture and contamination (flux residue, ionic contamination) give surface leakage that makes high-megohm resistors read low, and can promote dendritic growth that shorts between pads under bias.

## Drift and aging

Load-life specifications give ΔR/R after 1000 h at rated power and 70 °C (a general-purpose thick film may specify ±1 %, precision thin-film ±0.05 % or better). For precision circuits, drift is a design parameter: choose thin-film or foil, keep power low, and use ratio-matched networks so drift cancels.

## Failure analysis

1. **Visual:** look for discoloration, cracks, tombstones, solder fillets.
2. **Measure:** lift one lead, or compare with a known-good board, using four-wire for low values. In-circuit readings are corrupted by parallel paths.
3. **Thermal imaging:** a hot resistor under power points to overdissipation.
4. **Reproduce:** apply the suspected stress (flex, pulse, humidity) to a sample.
5. **Corrective action:** change the package, rating, or topology (for example add a series-string or a fusible part).

## Designing for graceful failure

Ask "which way will it fail, and what happens then?" An open pull-up leaves a floating input; an open feedback upper resistor can drive the output toward the input rail (OVP or a TVS clamp is the backstop); an open current-limit resistor lets full current through. In those cases, use redundant parts (two in series or parallel as appropriate), supervisor circuits, or fusible resistors, depending on the safe state.
:::

::: applications
- **Power supply bleeder and inrush resistors**: pulse-rated wirewound or MELF parts.
- **Current-sense shunts**: low-TCR, low-drift alloys, derated; failures change protection thresholds.
- **Automotive and industrial boards**: AEC-Q200 qualified, anti-sulfur parts for harsh atmospheres.
- **Feedback dividers** in regulators: use parts with good stability and consider the failure direction (OVP).
- **High-voltage dividers** in meters and power supplies: series strings with creepage/clearance.
- **Safety circuits**: fusible resistors that open safely, flameproof types.
:::

::: assumptions
- I treat "resistor" as the common **chip/film/wirewound** types; carbon composition and specialty parts have different failure statistics.
- The ranking (open > drift > short) reflects typical fixed resistors; potentiometers and variable resistors fail differently (wiper wear, noisy contact).
- Derating figures (70 °C knee, 125–155 °C maximum film temperature) are typical; use the datasheet.
- Environmental claims (sulfur, humidity) depend on termination metallurgy and coating.
- If asked for numbers like FIT rates, I would say they depend on the standard (MIL-HDBK-217, Telcordia) and part quality; I do not quote them.
:::

::: followups
### A pull-up resistor on an I²C line opens. What do you see?
The line floats high-Z when the bus is released: edges become slow or never rise, and noise couples in, so communication fails or becomes intermittent. If only one pull-up of several opens the effective pull-up increases (weaker, slower rise) and the bus may still work at low speed.

### A feedback resistor (upper) in a buck converter opens. What happens to the output?
The feedback node is pulled to ground by the lower resistor, so the controller sees too little output, and raises the duty cycle: output rises toward the input until OVP, a TVS or the load fails. That's why designers use OVP and why feedback resistor failure is a classic FMEA item.

### How do you tell a resistor drifted vs. a different board problem?
Measure it out of circuit (or one end lifted) and compare with its nominal value and tolerance. In circuit, measure node voltages and calculate the effective resistance from $V$ and $I$ as an indicator. Check the same position on a known-good board.

### What if a resistor has to survive a 1 kV, 10 µs surge?
Pick a part rated for pulse (anti-surge thick film, MELF or wirewound), check the datasheet pulse-load curve for the pulse energy and the voltage, use a series string so each element sees less voltage, and add a clamp (TVS/MOV) so the resistor sees a limited pulse. Verify by testing, not just by calculation.

### How does conformal coating help resistors?
It protects against humidity, contamination, and some corrosive gases (including sulfur in some coatings), reduces leakage across high-value resistors and dendritic growth. It doesn't fix over-power or mechanical flex, and it can add stress; so it's an addition, not a replacement for good part selection.
:::
