::: testing
Whether you can turn "Swiss cheese" into engineering. A power plane is a conductor; punching holes in it changes its resistance, inductance and current distribution. The interviewer looks for:

1. **Resistive**: narrow copper webs between antipads raise resistance and cause IR drop and hot spots.
2. **Current crowding**: current funnels through necks.
3. **Inductive and return-path**: the plane impedance rises, and signals referencing it lose their return path.
4. **Thermal**: the plane also spreads heat.
And how to design around it (stagger, shrink antipads, bridges, thicker copper).
:::

::: why
Power planes carry amps from regulator to load, and reference high-speed signals. A BGA's power balls feed a via field that can cut the plane under the chip into islands exactly where the current is highest. Debug stories where a processor browns out at load, or a plane gets warm, often trace to this.
:::

::: concepts
- **Sheet resistance**: 1 oz copper is about 0.49 mΩ/square. R = R_s · L/W.
- **Antipad and web**: clearance voids and the copper between them. See [[q0583]].
- **Current crowding**: current density rises through narrow sections.
- **Plane inductance and impedance**: perforation lengthens current paths.
- **Thermal relief**: spokes connecting a via to a plane, which also restrict current.
:::

::: answer
"A power plane is basically a sheet of copper, about half a milliohm per square for one ounce. Every through via that doesn't connect to it still cuts an antipad hole in it. If you pack too many, the holes get close together or merge, and the copper left between them becomes a narrow neck. Current has to squeeze through, so you get extra resistance, IR drop, current crowding and local heating. For scale, if 3 amps had to run 10 millimetres along a single 0.1 millimetre channel between merged rows of antipads, that's 100 squares, about 49 milliohms, so about 150 millivolts of drop, which is a lot on a 1 volt core rail. Real via fields have many channels in parallel, but the trend is the same.

The plane also gets higher inductance because current has to detour, which hurts transient response, and signals that reference that plane lose their return path.

To avoid it I stagger the vias, shrink antipads and pads as the fab allows, leave solid copper bridges in the current path, and use heavier copper or more planes where the current is large. Also, vias that do connect to the plane use thermal relief spokes, which limit current, so I use direct connection or many vias for power."
:::

::: technical
**Resistance of a neck.** $R=R_s\frac{L}{W}$, with $R_s=\rho/t=0.49$ mΩ/sq for 1 oz (35 µm). A neck $L=10$ mm, $W=1$ mm: 4.9 mΩ (15 mV at 3 A). $W=0.1$ mm: 49 mΩ (147 mV at 3 A). $W=5$ mm: 1.0 mΩ. (These treat all the current as forced along one channel, such as the copper left between two rows of nearly merged antipads; a real via field has many channels in parallel, so they are worst-case illustrations.) A single row of antipads crossed at right angles is much milder: each web is only about one antipad diameter long, so it adds roughly $p/(p-d_a)$ times the resistance of a strip $d_a$ long.

**Web.** For pitch $p$ and antipad $d_a$: $W=p-d_a$. Crowding factor $\approx p/(p-d_a)$: 3.3 for 1.0 mm pitch with 0.7 mm antipads, 10 for 0.9 mm.

**Power dissipation.** $P=I^2R$ concentrated in necks: 3 A through 49 mΩ is 0.44 W in a tiny area.

**Inductance and return.** Longer current paths raise plane spreading inductance and the impedance seen by decoupling caps; reference-plane slots enlarge signal loops.

**Via connection to the plane.** A via that *connects* to the plane through thermal-relief spokes adds some resistance (four 0.25 mm × 0.3 mm spokes in 1 oz copper are about 0.15 mΩ, small next to the roughly 1.3 mΩ barrel) but, more importantly, each spoke is a narrow 0.25 mm × 35 µm conductor that heats at high current and adds inductance. Vias are not soldered, so they rarely need reliefs at all: use direct (full) connection for power vias, and several vias.

**Mitigation.** Stagger rows, reduce antipad diameter, keep necks wide, add plane layers or 2 oz copper, add copper pours on adjacent layers in parallel, keep a clear lane for current.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Minimum web widths and antipad sizes depend on voltage rating, fab rules and stack-up; the numbers here are illustrations using 1 oz copper at room temperature.
:::

::: long
"A one-ounce plane is about half a milliohm per square, so it looks like a perfect conductor until you perforate it. Every via passing through the plane that isn't a power connection still takes an antipad, a clearance hole. Under a BGA with hundreds of vias, those holes can nearly touch. The copper web between two holes at one millimetre pitch with 0.9 millimetre antipads is only 0.1 millimetre wide. If the current is forced along a channel like that for ten millimetres, that's a hundred squares, about 49 milliohms, so 3 amps would lose around 150 millivolts and burn nearly half a watt in a tiny strip. That's a real problem on a one volt rail.

Beyond resistance, current has to detour so the plane's inductance rises and the decoupling network sees higher impedance; and any signal referenced to that plane loses its return path across the slot.

The fix is design discipline. Stagger via rows so there's always a continuous copper path, shrink pads and antipads to the fab minimum, leave deliberate copper bridges, use thermal reliefs only where soldering requires them, and use two ounce copper or additional plane layers or pours where current is big."
:::

::: terms
- **Sheet resistance**: ρ/t, in Ω per square.
- **Neck**: narrow copper section.
- **Current crowding**: higher current density at constrictions.
- **IR drop**: I·R voltage loss.
- **Thermal relief**: spoke connection of a via/pad to a plane.
- **Plane island**: copper isolated by merged antipads.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Assuming a plane is zero ohm.** It's about 0.5 mΩ/sq.
- **Ignoring antipads of non-power vias.**
- **Using thermal reliefs on power vias.** Spokes choke current.
- **Checking only DC.** Inductance matters for transients.
- **Aligned via rows.** They make slots.
:::

::: deep
## The plane as a resistor network

A 1 oz plane: $R_s=1.72\times10^{-8}/35\times10^{-6}=0.49$ mΩ/sq. A plain 50 × 50 mm plane fed along one full edge and drawn off the opposite edge is one square, about 0.5 mΩ; with small contacts (a regulator pad, a BGA corner) spreading resistance makes it a few times that. Perforation adds series necks.

## Other effects

**Heat.** In that worst case, 3 A × 147 mV = 0.44 W in a strip of about 1 mm², causing a hot spot.

**Inductance.** Slots lengthen current paths so plane spreading inductance and the impedance seen by the load rise, hurting transient response of the PDN (see [[q0534]]).

**Return paths.** Signals referenced to this plane see a slot; return currents detour, increasing crosstalk and EMI.

**Connecting vias.** A power via that connects through four thermal-relief spokes each maybe 0.25 mm wide, 0.3 mm long: a spoke is about 1.2 squares at 0.49 mΩ, about 0.6 mΩ each, four in parallel 0.15 mΩ. The resistance is modest; the limit is that each spoke is a narrow conductor that heats when it carries an amp or more. Vias aren't soldered, so use full connection for power vias.

## Design rules

1. Keep a continuous copper lane of at least a few times the required width along current paths.
2. Stagger via rows by half a pitch.
3. Use minimum pads and antipads.
4. Add parallel copper on adjacent layers.
5. Run DRC for neck width, or simulate IR drop in your tool.
:::

::: followups
### Why not just use thicker copper?
It lowers R_s proportionally (2 oz halves it), at higher cost and wider minimum etch features. It complements, not replaces, good via layout.

### How do you check a plane for IR drop?
Use the EDA tool's DC analysis, or hand-estimate with squares, and verify with 4-wire measurement on the board.

### Why do thermal reliefs matter for power vias?
Each spoke is a narrow strip of copper, so at high current the spokes heat and add resistance and inductance. Vias are not soldered, so they don't need the thermal isolation that reliefs give a soldered pin; for power vias use direct connection.

### Does it matter for ground planes too?
Yes: return current for both power and signals. A slotted ground plane worsens EMI and crosstalk.

### Scenario: core rail droops 120 mV at 3 A only on some boards. What would you check?
Plane necks under the BGA from via alignment or fab antipad variation; measure drop between regulator and BGA with a DMM, inspect the plane layer, and compare lots.
