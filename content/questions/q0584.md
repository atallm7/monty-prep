::: basics
### In plain words
A power plane is a sheet of copper that carries current to chips. Every via that passes through it without connecting still cuts a clearance hole in it. If the holes get close together, the copper between them becomes narrow necks. Current must squeeze through, which adds resistance, voltage drop and heat, and also raises inductance. You avoid it by staggering vias and shrinking the holes.

### Picture it
Picture a wide road with a row of concrete pillars across it. If the pillars are far apart, traffic flows around them with little trouble. If they are packed close together, all the cars must squeeze through the narrow gaps, and traffic backs up. The current in a plane is the traffic, and the gaps between holes are the narrow lanes.

### Words you need
- **Power plane**: a solid copper layer that delivers a supply voltage across the board.
- **Antipad**: the clearance hole in a plane around a via that does not connect to it.
- **Neck**: the narrow strip of copper left between two nearby holes.
- **Sheet resistance**: the resistance of one square of copper of any size. For standard 1 oz copper it is about 0.49 mΩ.
- **IR drop**: the voltage lost as current flows through resistance, $V = I \times R$.

### The simplest example
A neck that is 10 mm long and 0.1 mm wide is 100 squares long. Its resistance is 100 × 0.49 mΩ = 49 mΩ. Carrying 3 A, it drops 3 × 0.049 = 0.147 V, which is 147 mV. That is a big loss on a 1 V supply. A neck 1 mm wide is only 10 squares, 4.9 mΩ, or 15 mV. This is a worst case with all the current in one channel; real via fields have many channels in parallel.

That is the whole idea: narrower copper means more resistance and heat. The rest of this page covers inductance and connecting power vias.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can turn "Swiss cheese" into engineering. A power plane is a conductor; punching holes in it changes its resistance, inductance and current distribution. The interviewer looks for:

1. **Resistive**: narrow copper webs between antipads raise resistance and cause IR drop and hot spots.
2. **Current crowding**: current funnels through necks.
3. **Inductive and return-path**: the plane impedance rises, and signals referencing it lose their return path.
4. **Thermal**: the plane also spreads heat.
And how to design around it (stagger, shrink antipads, bridges, thicker copper).
:::

::: why
Power planes carry amps from regulator to load, and reference high-speed signals. A BGA's power balls feed a via field that can cut the plane under the chip into islands exactly where the current is highest. Debug stories where a processor browns out at load, or a plane gets warm, often trace to this.
:::

::: concepts
- **Sheet resistance**: 1 oz copper is about 0.49 mΩ/square. R = R_s · L/W.
- **Antipad and web**: clearance voids and the copper between them. See [[q0583]].
- **Current crowding**: current density rises through narrow sections.
- **Plane inductance and impedance**: perforation lengthens current paths.
- **Thermal relief**: spokes connecting a via to a plane, which also restrict current.
:::

::: answer
"A power plane is basically a sheet of copper, about half a milliohm per square for one ounce. Every through via that doesn't connect to it still cuts an antipad hole in it. If you pack too many, the holes get close together or merge, and the copper left between them becomes a narrow neck. Current has to squeeze through, so you get extra resistance, IR drop, current crowding and local heating. For scale, if 3 amps had to run 10 millimetres along a single 0.1 millimetre channel between merged rows of antipads, that's 100 squares, about 49 milliohms, so about 150 millivolts of drop, which is a lot on a 1 volt core rail. Real via fields have many channels in parallel, but the trend is the same.

The plane also gets higher inductance because current has to detour, which hurts transient response, and signals that reference that plane lose their return path.

To avoid it I stagger the vias, shrink antipads and pads as the fab allows, leave solid copper bridges in the current path, and use heavier copper or more planes where the current is large. Also, vias that do connect to the plane use thermal relief spokes, which limit current, so I use direct connection or many vias for power."
:::

::: technical
**Resistance of a neck.** $R=R_s\frac{L}{W}$, with $R_s=\rho/t=0.49$ mΩ/sq for 1 oz (35 µm). A neck $L=10$ mm, $W=1$ mm: 4.9 mΩ (15 mV at 3 A). $W=0.1$ mm: 49 mΩ (147 mV at 3 A). $W=5$ mm: 1.0 mΩ. (These treat all the current as forced along one channel, such as the copper left between two rows of nearly merged antipads; a real via field has many channels in parallel, so they are worst-case illustrations.) A single row of antipads crossed at right angles is much milder: each web is only about one antipad diameter long, so it adds roughly $p/(p-d_a)$ times the resistance of a strip $d_a$ long.

**Web.** For pitch $p$ and antipad $d_a$: $W=p-d_a$. Crowding factor $\approx p/(p-d_a)$: 3.3 for 1.0 mm pitch with 0.7 mm antipads, 10 for 0.9 mm.

**Power dissipation.** $P=I^2R$ concentrated in necks: 3 A through 49 mΩ is 0.44 W in a tiny area.

**Inductance and return.** Longer current paths raise plane spreading inductance and the impedance seen by decoupling caps; reference-plane slots enlarge signal loops.

**Via connection to the plane.** A via that *connects* to the plane through thermal-relief spokes adds some resistance (four 0.25 mm × 0.3 mm spokes in 1 oz copper are about 0.15 mΩ, small next to the roughly 1.3 mΩ barrel) but, more importantly, each spoke is a narrow 0.25 mm × 35 µm conductor that heats at high current and adds inductance. Vias are not soldered, so they rarely need reliefs at all: use direct (full) connection for power vias, and several vias.

**Mitigation.** Stagger rows, reduce antipad diameter, keep necks wide, add plane layers or 2 oz copper, add copper pours on adjacent layers in parallel, keep a clear lane for current.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Minimum web widths and antipad sizes depend on voltage rating, fab rules and stack-up; the numbers here are illustrations using 1 oz copper at room temperature.
:::

## The plane as a resistor network

A 1 oz plane: $R_s=1.72\times10^{-8}/35\times10^{-6}=0.49$ mΩ/sq. A plain 50 × 50 mm plane fed along one full edge and drawn off the opposite edge is one square, about 0.5 mΩ; with small contacts (a regulator pad, a BGA corner) spreading resistance makes it a few times that. Perforation adds series necks.

## Other effects

**Heat.** In that worst case, 3 A × 147 mV = 0.44 W in a strip of about 1 mm², causing a hot spot.

**Inductance.** Slots lengthen current paths so plane spreading inductance and the impedance seen by the load rise, hurting transient response of the PDN (see [[q0534]]).

**Return paths.** Signals referenced to this plane see a slot; return currents detour, increasing crosstalk and EMI.

**Connecting vias.** A power via that connects through four thermal-relief spokes each maybe 0.25 mm wide, 0.3 mm long: a spoke is about 1.2 squares at 0.49 mΩ, about 0.6 mΩ each, four in parallel 0.15 mΩ. The resistance is modest; the limit is that each spoke is a narrow conductor that heats when it carries an amp or more. Vias aren't soldered, so use full connection for power vias.

## Spreading resistance: the plane is a 2-D conductor, not a wire

The neck model treats current as flowing down a channel. The unperforated plane has its own resistance, because current fanning out from a small source (a regulator pad, a via cluster, a BGA corner) into a large sheet crowds near the source. For a thin sheet with sheet resistance $R_{sq}$, the resistance between a circular source contact of radius $r_1$ and a far boundary at radius $r_2$ is

$$R_{spread}=\frac{R_{sq}}{2\pi}\ln\frac{r_2}{r_1}$$

For 1 oz copper, a 2 mm radius feed and a 20 mm radius to the far side:

$$R_{spread}=\frac{0.49\ \text{m}\Omega}{2\pi}\ln(10)=0.18\ \text{m}\Omega$$

Because it is a logarithm, even a factor of ten in radius costs only $\ln(10)/2\pi\approx0.37$ of a square. Now perforate the region. With 23% of the area removed (the Maxwell estimate from [[q0583]], $\sigma_{eff}/\sigma=0.63$) the same fan-out becomes $0.18/0.63=0.29$ mΩ. Perforation at that level is not catastrophic in DC terms: about 0.3 mV at 1 A. The DC pain comes from the necks, and the necks come from tight pitch and merged antipads, not from the global hole fraction.

## Current density: why a neck heats

The same neck from the technical section carries 3 A through a cross-section of $0.1\ \text{mm}\times35\ \mu\text{m}=3.5\times10^{-3}\ \text{mm}^2$:

$$J=\frac{I}{Wt}=\frac{3}{0.1\times10^{-3}\times35\times10^{-6}}=8.6\times10^{8}\ \text{A/m}^2\ (857\ \text{A/mm}^2)$$

For comparison, the same 3 A in a 1 mm wide, 1 oz trace is 86 A/mm², and in a 0.5 mm trace it is 171 A/mm². The power density in the neck is $J^2\rho=1.26\times10^{10}\ \text{W/m}^3$; multiplying by the neck volume ($0.1\times10\times0.035\ \text{mm}^3$) gives back the 0.44 W from earlier. As a check on how fast that matters: copper's volumetric heat capacity is about $3.4\times10^{6}\ \text{J/(m}^3\text{K)}$, so with no heat flow at all the neck would warm at roughly $1.26\times10^{10}/3.4\times10^{6}\approx3.7\ \text{K/ms}$ (about 3,700 K/s). In practice heat leaks sideways into the surrounding copper and down into the laminate on that same millisecond timescale, so the neck settles at a steady-state rise set by the geometry rather than running away. Copper resistance also rises about 0.39% per °C, so a neck that warms by 40 K sees 16% more resistance (49 mΩ becomes about 57 mΩ), which makes it dissipate more. That positive feedback is mild for copper, but it is why a neck that is "fine" on the bench at 25 °C can droop more in a warm enclosure.

## The AC side matters more than the DC side

A designer who only counts milliohms will miss the larger effect. A plane pair carrying a transient current has a spreading inductance per square of about

$$L_{\square}=\mu_0 h$$

where $h$ is the dielectric thickness between the power and ground planes. For $h=100\ \mu\text{m}$:

$$L_\square=4\pi\times10^{-7}\times10^{-4}=126\ \text{pH per square}$$

Now compare with a power-integrity target. A 1.0 V core rail with a 5% ripple budget and a 5 A load step has

$$Z_{target}=\frac{0.05\ \text{V}}{5\ \text{A}}=10\ \text{m}\Omega$$

At 100 MHz one square of plane pair has an inductive reactance of $2\pi(10^8)(126\ \text{pH})=79\ \text{m}\Omega$, almost eight times the target, and three squares of path (a detour around a slot) is 237 mΩ. The DC resistance of the same path was around a milliohm. So the point is not that holes make IR drop (they do, a little); it is that they lengthen the path the *fast* current takes from the decoupling capacitors to the die, and at the frequencies where the PDN matters that path is dominated by inductance. This is the main electrical argument for keeping the plane under a BGA unbroken and for putting the capacitors on the surface and vias close to the power balls.

These are idealized parallel-plate numbers: they assume a uniform plane pair, no vias in the path, and an infinite plane. Real boards have via, capacitor-mounting and package inductance in series. Use the estimate for ranking changes (shorter detour, thinner dielectric), not for absolute prediction.

## Worked check: does a BGA via field starve a 1 V rail?

Imagine the core rail feeds a BGA through a square via field about 20 mm across, with 1 mm pitch and 0.7 mm antipads, on a 1 oz plane. The web is $p-d_a=0.3$ mm, so the crowding factor is $p/(p-d_a)=3.3$. Taking the 20 mm of field as 20 rows of necks each 0.7 mm deep: along a 20 mm wide path, the solid equivalent is $14/20=0.7$ squares (0.34 mΩ), and the perforated one is $0.7\times3.3=2.3$ squares (1.1 mΩ). At 3 A that is 3.4 mV, negligible against a 50 mV budget. Narrow the web to 0.1 mm (0.9 mm antipads, so each row of necks is now 0.9 mm deep) and the factor becomes 10: $(18/20)\times10=9$ squares, or 4.4 mΩ, which is 13 mV at 3 A. That is the pessimistic end, because the gap widens away from its narrowest point; integrating the real gap shape roughly halves it ([[q0583]]). The DC story only turns serious for sub-0.2 mm webs *and* amps of current, while the AC story (inductance) is a problem at any via density where the plane is cut into islands.

## Checking it on the bench

1. **DC drop**: apply a known load current and measure the voltage between the regulator's sense point and the BGA power ball with a good DMM or, better, a microvolt-resolution meter and 4-wire contact. Compare with the squares-based estimate. A result several times higher means a neck.
2. **IR camera**: at 3 A and a neck of tens of milliohms, a hot spot appears directly on the plane's surface.
3. **PDN impedance**: a 2-port shunt-through measurement on a VNA gives $Z=\frac{Z_0}{2}\cdot\frac{S_{21}}{1-S_{21}}\approx 25\,S_{21}$ Ω for a low-impedance rail on 50 Ω ports, which the fab-to-fab or lot-to-lot comparison can use to catch a plane cut.
4. **Load-step response**: a current step of a few amps with a fast probe on the rail at the BGA, compared with the simulated response.

## Design rules

1. Keep a continuous copper lane of at least a few times the required width along current paths.
2. Stagger via rows by half a pitch.
3. Use minimum pads and antipads.
4. Add parallel copper on adjacent layers.
5. Run DRC for neck width, or simulate IR drop in your tool.

::: followups
### Why not just use thicker copper?
It lowers R_s proportionally (2 oz halves it), at higher cost and wider minimum etch features. It complements, not replaces, good via layout.

### How do you check a plane for IR drop?
Use the EDA tool's DC analysis, or hand-estimate with squares, and verify with 4-wire measurement on the board.

### Why do thermal reliefs matter for power vias?
Each spoke is a narrow strip of copper, so at high current the spokes heat and add resistance and inductance. Vias are not soldered, so they don't need the thermal isolation that reliefs give a soldered pin; for power vias use direct connection.

### Does it matter for ground planes too?
Yes: return current for both power and signals. A slotted ground plane worsens EMI and crosstalk.

### Scenario: core rail droops 120 mV at 3 A only on some boards. What would you check?
Plane necks under the BGA from via alignment or fab antipad variation; measure drop between regulator and BGA with a DMM, inspect the plane layer, and compare lots.
