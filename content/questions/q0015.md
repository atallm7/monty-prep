::: testing
Whether you can go beyond "length and area" to a complete list of the variables that move a real resistor's value, and rank them by importance. The interviewer is checking that you:

1. Know the geometry and material formula $R=\rho L/A$ and where it comes from ([[q0014]]).
2. Know that $\rho$ itself depends on temperature, and can quote a number (copper about +0.39 %/K, metal-film resistors tens of ppm/K).
3. Can list the other real-world dependencies: frequency (skin effect), voltage, strain, light, aging, humidity, self-heating.
4. Connect these to design: tolerance, TCR, precision dividers, sensors.
:::

::: why
In real products, resistors drift. A divider that is accurate at 25 °C on the bench is off in a hot car; a 12 V trace that drops 1 % cold drops 1.3 % hot; a precision amplifier's gain drifts because of TCR mismatch; a 100 MΩ resistor's value depends on humidity. At the same time, designers *use* these dependencies deliberately: thermistors, strain gauges, photoresistors and RTDs are sensors because resistance depends on what you want to measure. Understanding the dependence list is how you decide between a 5 % carbon part and a 0.01 % thin-film network.
:::

::: concepts
- **Resistivity and geometry** ([[q0014]]): $R=\rho L/A$.
- **Temperature coefficient of resistance (TCR)**: $R(T)=R_0[1+\alpha(T-T_0)]$, in ppm/K for precision parts.
- **Skin and proximity effect** ([[q0056]]): AC current crowds into a thin surface layer, raising effective $R$.
- **Self-heating**: power $P=I^2R$ raises the part temperature and therefore its resistance.
- **Tolerance vs. drift**: initial tolerance is a manufacturing spread; drift is change over time, temperature and stress.
- **Sensors**: resistance can intentionally depend on temperature, strain, light or magnetic field.
:::

::: answer
"Resistance depends first on geometry and material: R equals rho L over A. Longer means more resistance, a bigger cross-section means less, and the resistivity rho is a property of the material, like copper at 1.7 times ten to the minus eight ohm-meters.

But rho isn't constant. The biggest practical dependence is temperature. For metals, resistance goes up with temperature: copper gains about 0.4 percent per kelvin, so a copper winding at 100 degrees C has about 30 percent more resistance than at 20. Semiconductors go the other way, which is how an NTC thermistor works. Precision resistors quote a temperature coefficient in ppm per degree, and a typical thin-film part might be 25 ppm, a thick-film chip maybe 100 to 200.

Then there's frequency, because at high frequency skin effect pushes current to the surface and increases effective resistance. And there are smaller effects: voltage coefficient, which matters for high-value resistors in dividers, mechanical strain, which is the basis of strain gauges, light for photoresistors, humidity and aging, and self-heating from the power the resistor dissipates.

Practically I'd choose the resistor technology according to which dependency matters: for a precision divider I'd use matched thin-film parts so they drift together."
:::

::: technical
**Basic:** $R = \rho\,L/A$.

**Temperature:** $R(T) = R_{ref}\,[1+\alpha\,(T-T_{ref})]$.
- Copper $\alpha\approx 3.9\times10^{-3}\ \text{K}^{-1}$ (about +0.39 %/K near 20 °C); aluminum about 0.39 %/K; platinum (RTD) about 0.385 %/K (Pt100); nichrome very small; manganin (shunt alloy) about 0 (a few ppm/K).
- Resistor parts: thick film 100–200 ppm/K typical (cheap parts up to several hundred); thin film 5–50 ppm/K (precision down to 1–2 ppm/K); wire-wound and foil have the lowest TCRs.
- NTC thermistor (B-parameter model): $R(T)=R_{25}\exp\!\left[B\left(\tfrac{1}{T}-\tfrac{1}{298.15}\right)\right]$, $T$ in kelvin.

**Frequency:** AC resistance rises as the skin depth $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f \mu)}$ falls below the conductor thickness; for copper $\delta\approx 66\ \mu$m at 1 MHz. Real resistors also develop reactance ([[q0016]]).

**Voltage:** voltage coefficient of resistance (VCR), ppm/V, significant in high-value thick-film resistors.

**Other:** strain (gauge factor $GF=(\Delta R/R)/\varepsilon\approx 2$ for metal foil gauges), light (CdS photoresistors), magnetic field (magnetoresistance), humidity, aging (drift of tens to hundreds of ppm per year in some film types), solder stress and board flex.

Self-heating: temperature rise $\Delta T = P\,\theta_{JA}$ so the resistance in a 1/10 W part dissipating 100 mW can shift by $\alpha\Delta T$, which may be several tenths of a percent.
:::

::: long
"The starting point is geometry and material. For a uniform conductor, R equals rho times length over cross-sectional area. So doubling the length doubles the resistance, and doubling the area halves it. Rho is the resistivity of the material. Copper is about 1.7 times ten to the minus eight ohm-meters, nichrome is about sixty times higher, which is why it's used in heaters.

The next most important thing is temperature. For metals, rho goes up linearly with temperature near room temperature, with a coefficient around 0.39 percent per kelvin for copper. If a motor winding goes from 20 to 100 degrees, its resistance rises by about 31 percent. For resistors we specify TCR in parts per million per degree: a thick-film chip is typically one to two hundred ppm, thin film can be 25 or better. Semiconductors go the other way, because heat creates more carriers, so an NTC thermistor drops from 10 kilohms at 25 degrees to about 3.6 kilohms at 50 with a B of 3950.

Then, frequency. At high frequency the current crowds near the surface, skin effect, so AC resistance exceeds DC resistance. That matters for inductor windings and traces.

There's also voltage coefficient, which matters for high-value resistors in dividers, mechanical strain, which is how strain gauges work, light for photoresistors, humidity, long-term aging, and self-heating.

The design takeaway is to decide which dependence matters. For a feedback divider I want matched temperature coefficients, so I'd use a matched thin-film network. For current sensing I want a low TCR alloy. For sensing temperature, I want a high TCR and use it deliberately."
:::

::: terms
- **Resistivity ($\rho$)**: intrinsic property in Ω·m.
- **TCR ($\alpha$)**: fractional change in resistance per kelvin, often in ppm/K.
- **NTC / PTC**: negative / positive temperature coefficient.
- **Skin effect**: AC current concentrates near the surface, raising resistance ([[q0056]]).
- **Voltage coefficient (VCR)**: change in resistance per volt applied, in ppm/V.
- **Gauge factor**: $(\Delta R/R)/\text{strain}$, about 2 for metal foil gauges.
- **Self-heating**: temperature rise due to the resistor's own dissipation.
- **Drift**: slow change of value with time/stress/humidity.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "length, area, material and temperature".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Writes $R=\rho L/A$ and then immediately says $\rho(T)$ and quotes the coefficient with a number.
- Contrasts metals (positive TCR) and semiconductors (negative TCR) and explains why.
- Brings up frequency (skin effect), voltage coefficient, strain and aging as real-resistor effects.
- Ties each to a design choice: thin-film vs thick-film, matched networks, shunt alloys, derating for self-heating.
- Mentions that sensors (RTD, NTC, strain gauge, LDR) exploit these dependencies.
- Gives a worked number (e.g. a copper coil's resistance rises 31 % from 20 to 100 °C).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Listing only length and area.** That leaves out temperature, which dominates drift in most real systems.
- **"Thicker wire has more resistance."** Area is in the denominator: thicker means less.
- **Assuming all materials increase resistance with temperature.** Semiconductors and carbon decrease.
- **Confusing tolerance with TCR.** Tolerance is the initial spread at 25 °C; TCR is the variation with temperature; drift is variation with time.
- **Ignoring self-heating.** A 0.1 W resistor at full power may run 50 °C above ambient, changing its value by several tenths of a percent for a 100 ppm/K part.
- **Forgetting AC resistance** in inductor windings and wide traces carrying MHz currents.
:::

::: deep
## Layer 1: geometry and material

$R=\rho L/A$ is the baseline ([[q0014]]). For PCB traces, use the **sheet resistance** $R_\square=\rho/t$ times the number of squares $L/w$. 1 oz copper (35 µm) has $R_\square=0.48\ \text{m}\Omega/\square$, so a 2 in, 5 mil trace (400 squares) is 0.19 Ω. Geometry in resistor manufacture is how values are set: laser trimming cuts a spiral or slot in the film to lengthen the current path, raising $R$ to its target value.

## Layer 2: temperature

For metals near room temperature
$$\rho(T)=\rho_{0}\,[1+\alpha(T-T_0)],$$
with $\alpha\approx0.0039\ \text{K}^{-1}$ for copper. So $R_{100^\circ C}/R_{20^\circ C}=1+0.00393\cdot80=1.314$. This is a handy trick: measure a motor winding's resistance cold and hot and infer the winding temperature without a thermocouple.

Resistor technologies, typical TCRs (vendor-dependent):

| Technology | Typical TCR | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon composition/film | −200 to −1000 ppm/K | cheap, noisy |
| Thick-film chip | ±100 to ±200 ppm/K | most common SMD |
| Thin-film chip | ±5 to ±50 ppm/K | precision, 0.01 %–0.1 % |
| Metal foil | ±0.2 to ±2 ppm/K | best-in-class precision |
| Wire-wound (manganin etc.) | ±5 to ±20 ppm/K | power, shunts |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> TCR ranges vary by vendor, series and resistance value; always read the specific datasheet. The numbers above are order-of-magnitude typical.

A 10 kΩ, ±100 ppm/K resistor over a 60 K swing changes by $10\,\text{k}\Omega\cdot10^{-4}\cdot60=60\ \Omega$ (0.6 %). In a divider with matched parts the ratio errors cancel to first order, which is why **matched resistor networks** quote ratio TCR (a few ppm/K) rather than absolute.

## Layer 3: self-heating

A resistor at power $P$ settles at $T=T_{amb}+P\,\theta$. For a 0603 with $\theta\approx 500$ K/W (an illustrative value), 50 mW causes 25 K of rise. At 100 ppm/K that is a 0.25 % change. In a precision circuit you derate the power by an order of magnitude or choose a larger package.

## Layer 4: frequency

At low frequency the current distributes uniformly. Above the frequency where the skin depth $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu_0)}$ equals the conductor half-thickness, $R_{ac}$ rises. For copper $\delta=66\ \mu$m at 1 MHz, 21 µm at 10 MHz. The effect is large for thick wire and inductor windings and small for thin-film resistors. Stray reactance matters earlier ([[q0016]]). ## Layer 5: other dependencies

- **Voltage coefficient**: high-value thick-film resistors (MΩ) can change value by tens of ppm per volt, a nuisance in high-voltage dividers.
- **Strain:** stretching changes $L$ and $A$ and the resistivity; foil gauges have gauge factor ≈ 2, so 1000 µε (0.1 %) changes $R$ by 0.2 %. Board flexing can shift a thin-film resistor by a measurable amount too.
- **Light:** CdS and similar photoresistors vary by orders of magnitude, slowly (ms).
- **Humidity and contamination:** surface leakage for high-impedance nodes (flux residue); moisture absorption in some films.
- **Aging:** film resistors drift a little over thousands of hours at high temperature (long-term stability specs are typically given as ΔR/R after 1000 h at rated power).
- **Soldering:** reflow can shift values by several tenths of a percent in some types.

## Worked example: an NTC divider, from sensitivity to self-heating error

Take a 10 kΩ NTC with $B = 3950$ K wired as the bottom leg of a divider with a 10 kΩ fixed resistor to 3.3 V, read by a 12-bit ADC. Using the B-parameter model from the technical section, the node voltage at four temperatures is:

| T (°C) | $R_{NTC}$ | $V_{node}$ | Slope $dV/dT$ | Fractional TCR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 33.6 kΩ | 2.543 V | −30.9 mV/K | −5.29 %/K |
| 25 | 10.0 kΩ | 1.650 V | −36.7 mV/K | −4.44 %/K |
| 50 | 3.59 kΩ | 0.871 V | −24.3 mV/K | −3.78 %/K |
| 85 | 1.09 kΩ | 0.324 V | −9.0 mV/K | −3.08 %/K |

The fractional TCR at any temperature is $\alpha = -B/T^2$ with $T$ in kelvin, which is where the −4.44 %/K at 25 °C comes from ($3950/298.15^2$). Notice how a thermistor is far more sensitive than a metal (−4.4 %/K against +0.39 %/K for copper), but the sensitivity is not constant. The divider response flattens at high temperature as the NTC falls well below the fixed resistor, so a 12-bit LSB (0.81 mV) corresponds to 0.022 K at 25 °C but about 0.09 K at 85 °C. For a battery pack monitor that is more than enough resolution. The trouble is elsewhere.

**Self-heating.** At 25 °C each resistor drops 1.65 V, so the NTC dissipates $1.65^2/10\text{k}\Omega = 0.27$ mW. A small bead or chip thermistor in still air has a dissipation constant on the order of a milliwatt per kelvin (this is vendor- and package-dependent, so read the datasheet; I use 1.5 mW/K as an illustration). Then the sensor sits $0.27/1.5 = 0.18$ K above the thing it is meant to measure, and the ADC reports a temperature 0.18 K too high, 8 times the ADC resolution. The fixes are exactly what the resistance-dependence list suggests: use a larger fixed resistor to lower the current, power the divider only during a measurement, or calibrate the offset out. A good test is to halve the excitation and see whether the reading moves.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Dissipation constants for thermistors depend on package, mounting and surrounding medium (air, potting, bonded to metal). The 1.5 mW/K figure above is illustrative, not a datasheet value.

## Error budget for a precision divider across temperature

Suppose a 2:1 reference divider uses two ±100 ppm/K thick-film resistors, and the board sees 25 °C to 85 °C (60 K). Each resistor can drift by $100\times10^{-6}\times60 = 0.6\%$. In the worst case they drift in opposite directions, and with a ratio of 0.5 the output error is $(1-0.5)(0.6\% + 0.6\%) = 0.6\%$. If both parts have the same sign and magnitude, the errors cancel almost completely, which is the whole argument for a matched network: a 5 ppm/K *ratio* TCR yields only $5\times10^{-6}\times60 = 0.03\%$, twenty times better, even though the absolute TCR of each resistor may be no better than the thick-film part. For a 12-bit system (1 LSB is 0.024 % of full scale), the thick-film pair already costs about 25 LSB over temperature.

Copper's dependence matters in the same way for layout. The 2-inch, 5-mil trace from the existing example measures 0.19 Ω at 25 °C and about 0.235 Ω at 85 °C. If this trace carries 1 A to a sense amplifier, the extra 45 mV is not a rounding error but a bias, and it is the reason current-sense layouts bring a separate Kelvin connection to the resistor pads instead of sharing the power trace.

## Limiting cases and when the simple model fails

- **Linear TCR over a wide range.** Using $R_0[1+\alpha\Delta T]$ for copper from −40 to 150 °C is acceptable (error well under 1 K when used for winding temperature estimates), but platinum RTDs use the quadratic Callendar–Van Dusen form, and an NTC across 0 to 100 °C needs at least Steinhart–Hart; the single-$B$ fit is accurate only near its reference points.
- **Very small or very large values.** At milliohm values, lead and solder resistance is no longer negligible against the film, so a two-wire measurement shows contact resistance plus TCR of the terminals. At gigaohms, surface leakage, humidity and the voltage coefficient dominate.
- **Fast dynamics.** Temperature-driven change is slow: a chip resistor has a thermal time constant of seconds, so it will follow a power step with a delay. This matters when a resistor sees a pulsed overload: the short-pulse rating is set by thermal mass, not by average power.

## Verifying on the bench

1. **Four-wire (Kelvin) measurement.** For anything below roughly 10 Ω, use a four-wire ohmmeter so test leads do not add into the result.
2. **Excitation sweep.** Measure at two excitation currents differing by 4×. The power differs 16×, so any difference in resistance is self-heating. Use the lowest excitation that leaves a readable signal.
3. **Temperature sweep.** Put the part in a thermal chamber or on a hot plate with a reference thermometer, let it soak until the reading stops moving, and record $R$ at three or more points. Plot $\ln R$ against $1/T$ for a thermistor (a straight line gives $B$), or $R$ against $T$ for a metal (a line gives $\alpha$).
4. **Look for thermal EMFs.** Switching the excitation polarity and averaging the two readings cancels the small thermoelectric voltages produced at dissimilar-metal junctions, which show up as offsets at milliohm and microvolt levels.

## Putting it to use

- **Precision divider** (e.g. ADC reference scaling): thin-film network with ratio tolerance 0.05 % and ratio TCR 5 ppm/K.
- **Current sensing:** low-TCR alloys (manganin, Evanohm-like families) so the sense voltage does not drift with self-heating; a 4-terminal (Kelvin) part avoids solder/lead resistance.
- **Heater:** high $\rho$ with stable TCR (nichrome).
- **Temperature sensor:** exploit TCR: Pt100 RTD (+0.385 %/K), NTC thermistor (−3 to −5 %/K around 25 °C).
:::

::: applications
- **RTD (Pt100/Pt1000)** thermometers in industrial control, with 0.385 %/K.
- **NTC thermistors** for battery pack temperature monitoring and inrush limiting in power supplies.
- **Strain gauges and load cells** in weighing scales (bridge of four gauges).
- **Shunt resistors** for current sensing in BMS and motor drives, made from manganin/low-TCR alloys.
- **Thin-film resistor networks** in precision ADC front ends, instrumentation amplifier gain-setting.
- **Copper-winding temperature estimation** in motors and transformers by resistance measurement.
:::

::: assumptions
- I treat temperature dependence as **linear**, valid for a modest range near room temperature; RTDs follow a slightly nonlinear (Callendar–Van Dusen) curve and NTCs follow the B-parameter or Steinhart–Hart model.
- Typical TCR, VCR and aging values are **order-of-magnitude** and technology-dependent.
- I take "resistance" to mean the resistance of an ordinary resistor or conductor; for semiconductors, resistance also depends on doping, field and light ([[q0055]]).
- At high frequency, effective resistance also includes dielectric and radiation effects (not discussed).
- If the interviewer asks only about *wires*, the answer is geometry, material, temperature and frequency.
:::

::: followups
### A copper motor winding measures 2.00 Ω at 20 °C and 2.52 Ω after running. What is its temperature?
$R/R_0=1.26=1+0.00393\,\Delta T$, so $\Delta T=66.2$ K and $T\approx 86$ °C. Check against the insulation class temperature limit.

### Which resistor type would you choose for a precision divider and why?
A matched thin-film network, because the two resistors are made on the same substrate and see the same temperature, so their ratio TCR (a few ppm/K) and ratio tolerance (0.01–0.05 %) are far better than two discrete thick-film parts with ±100 ppm/K each.

### Why does an NTC thermistor have a negative TCR?
It's a semiconducting ceramic (metal oxides). Heat promotes carriers across the energy gap or hopping barrier, increasing $n$ faster than scattering reduces mobility, so resistance falls exponentially with temperature, roughly $\exp(B/T)$.

### What if a resistor in your design has a power rating of 125 mW and you dissipate 100 mW in it?
It will run hot (the part is allowed a large temperature rise at its rated power, often tens of kelvin), shift in value by its TCR times the rise, age faster, and heat neighbors. Derate to 50 % or less, or use a bigger package. Resistor datasheets include a derating curve above 70 °C ambient.

### A 1 MΩ resistor in an input divider reads differently on humid days. Why?
Surface leakage across the board or resistor body (flux residue, moisture) creates a parallel path, and humidity makes it worse. At megaohm levels, a leakage path of 100 MΩ is a 1 % error. Fix with cleaning, conformal coating, guard rings and lower-value resistors where possible.
:::
