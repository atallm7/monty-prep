::: basics
### In plain words
A via is not a free wire. Avoid or limit vias where their cost outweighs the routing convenience. Fast edges and radio-frequency paths suffer because a via's inductance reflects part of the signal. Solder pads suffer from wicking, and a via is a poor sole path for high current. When you must use one, add a ground via beside it and keep layer changes few.

### Picture it
Think of a speed bump on a road. A slow cyclist hardly notices it. A speeding car gets thrown off, and the faster the car, the worse the jolt. A via is a speed bump for signals, and what counts is how fast the signal's edge is, not how often it repeats.

### Words you need
- **Rise time**: how quickly a signal edge goes from low to high. Shorter means faster.
- **Reflection**: part of a signal bounces back from a change in the path, such as a via.
- **Stub**: the unused part of a via barrel, which can resonate.
- **Return path**: the route the signal's current takes back to the source. A layer change can disrupt it.

### The simplest example
A typical via has about 1.3 nH of inductance, which is a billionth of a henry. On a 50 Ω line, the worst-case reflection is roughly $L / (2 Z_0 t_r)$. With a 1 ns edge (a billionth of a second) that is 1.3 nH / (2 × 50 Ω × 1 ns) = about 1.3% reflected. With a 100 ps edge the same via reflects about 13%. The via did not change, but the faster edge sees it as a big bump. This is an upper-bound estimate.

That is the whole idea: judge a via by the signal's edge speed and by what else it affects. The rest of this page covers other reasons and what to do instead.
:::

::: testing
Whether you treat a via as a **cost** and not a free wire. A weak answer says "avoid vias on high-speed signals" and stops. A strong answer separates the reasons, because each one has a different fix:

1. **Electrical**: impedance discontinuity, stubs, return-path breaks (fast and RF signals, differential pairs).
2. **Manufacturing/assembly**: vias in solder pads, close to pads, near board edges or on the wave-solder side.
3. **Reliability and mechanical**: high-aspect-ratio vias, stress locations, vias as the only path for high current.
4. **Layout cost**: via fields that perforate planes and eat routing channels.
And you should say what you do instead.
:::

::: why
Every via adds inductance, capacitance, a discontinuity in the return path, a place for the board to fail and a small tax on routing area and fab cost. For slow signals those costs vanish, which is why most nets are routed with vias freely. For a 10 Gb/s lane, a clock, an RF chain or a precision analog node, a careless layer change is a design bug. Knowing where the line is, and having numbers for it, is what the question is testing. It also sets up [[q0515]] (vias on high-speed traces), [[q0583]] and [[q0584]] (too many vias).
:::

::: concepts
- **Via parasitics**: roughly 1 nH and 0.5 pF for a 1.6 mm via. See [[q0574]].
- **Reflections from discontinuities**: a series inductance reflects an edge in proportion to $L/(2Z_0t_r)$.
- **Return path**: the signal's return current must follow it across layers. See [[q0575]].
- **Stub resonance**: unused barrel acts as a quarter-wave resonator. See [[q0576]].
- **Differential pair symmetry**: both legs should see the same discontinuities.
- **Manufacturability limits**: aspect ratio, annular ring, via-in-pad, edge clearance.
:::

::: answer
"I avoid vias wherever the via's parasitics or its manufacturing risk cost more than the routing convenience it gives me.

Electrically, that's fast edges and RF. A typical via is around a nanohenry, and the reflection from that goes up as the rise time shrinks: with a 1.3 nH via on a 50 ohm line, a 100 picosecond edge reflects around 13 percent in the worst case, while a one nanosecond edge reflects about one percent. So on multi-gigabit lanes, clocks, and RF paths I keep the signal on one layer if I can, or I limit it to as few layer changes as possible, add ground vias beside them, and use back-drilled or blind vias to kill the stub. In differential pairs I keep both vias symmetric so I don't add skew.

For manufacturing, I don't put an open via in an SMT pad, because solder wicks into it. I also keep vias away from board edges and mounting holes, where cracking and plating exposure are a risk, and I avoid tight aspect ratios.

And for power, I avoid having just one or two vias carry current, or covering a power plane with so many vias that it turns into Swiss cheese. In all these cases I either stay on one layer, move the via, add vias in parallel or choose a different type."
:::

::: technical
**Electrical reasons.**
1. **Reflection from the via's inductance.** For a linear ramp of 0–100 % rise time $t_r$ on a line $Z_0$, a series inductance $L$ reflects approximately $\rho\approx\frac{L}{2Z_0t_r}$ (upper bound; the via's capacitance partly compensates). For $L=1.3$ nH, $Z_0=50\ \Omega$:
 - $t_r=1$ ns: 1.3 %
 - $t_r=260$ ps: 5 %
 - $t_r=100$ ps: 13 %
 - $t_r=50$ ps: 26 %
 A 5 % reflection budget is met by single vias for edges slower than about 260 ps, but not for multi-gigabit signals.
2. **Stubs.** An unused barrel of length $\ell$ resonates at $f_0=c/(4\ell\sqrt{\varepsilon_r})$: 23 GHz for 1.6 mm and 12 GHz for 3 mm in FR-4. It degrades insertion loss well below $f_0$. See [[q0576]].
3. **Return path.** A layer change that crosses between different reference planes forces the return current through a nearby ground via or decoupling capacitor, enlarging the loop. This increases crosstalk and EMI.
4. **Differential-pair skew and mode conversion.** If one leg has a via and the other doesn't, or their vias see different ground proximity, you create skew and common-mode noise.
5. **RF and sensitive analog.** Vias change the effective ground reference and add inductance in ground paths, a particular problem for matching networks, LNAs and crystal oscillators.

**Manufacturing reasons.**
- Via in an SMT pad without fill and cap wicks solder (voids, opens). See [[q0578]], [[q0582]].
- Vias close to pads without mask dams cause solder bridging.
- Vias within a minimum distance of the board edge, V-groove or mounting hole risk breakout and plating damage in routing or depaneling.
- Very high aspect-ratio vias are costly and less reliable. See [[q0580]].

**Current and thermal reasons.** A single via is limited to roughly 0.8 A (conservative), so vias should not be the bottleneck for power.

**Layout reasons.** Each via has a pad on every layer and an antipad in every plane. Too many perforate planes ([[q0583]], [[q0584]], [[q0585]]).

**What to do instead.** Keep critical nets on one layer, choose layers that share a reference plane, shorten stubs (blind, micro, back-drill), add ground vias, route differential pairs symmetrically, move vias off pads or fill and cap them.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The reflection estimate $L/(2Z_0t_r)$ is a first-order upper bound. The via's capacitance often cancels part of it, and designers use field solvers for gigabit links. The 5 % and rise-time numbers are illustrations, not specifications.
:::

::: short
"I avoid vias where their cost outweighs their convenience. On fast edges, a 1.3 nH via on a 50 ohm line can reflect up to roughly 13 percent of a 100 picosecond edge, so for multi-gigabit lanes, clocks and RF I stay on one layer or minimise layer changes, add ground vias, and remove stubs. I also avoid open vias in solder pads, vias near board edges, single vias for power, and via fields that perforate planes."
:::

::: long
"My rule is that a via is free for slow signals and costly for fast ones, so I avoid vias wherever the cost shows up.

Electrically, the key number is that a typical via is about a nanohenry. On a 50 ohm line, the reflection from a series inductor scales as L over two Z-zero times the rise time. So for a one nanosecond edge that's about one percent, which I'd ignore. For a 100 picosecond edge it's up to about 13 percent, and for a 50 picosecond edge it's about 26. That's why on multi-gigabit lanes, clocks and RF I keep the signal on one layer where I can, and I count layer changes. If I must change layers, I pick layers that share a reference plane, add a ground via right next to the signal via so the return current has a path, and I remove the stub with a blind via or back-drilling. For differential pairs, I keep the vias symmetric to avoid skew.

For sensitive analog, crystals and matching networks, I avoid vias in the signal path and in the local ground return, because the added inductance and the broken return path matter more than the routing convenience.

On the manufacturing side, I avoid open vias in solder pads, because solder wicks into the barrel, and I keep vias away from board edges and mounting holes where plating can crack or get exposed.

And for power, I avoid relying on one or two vias, and I avoid so many vias in a plane that it fragments the copper. So: stay on one layer for the critical stuff, and use vias deliberately everywhere else."
:::

::: terms
- **Discontinuity**: any change in a transmission line's impedance that causes a reflection, such as a via.
- **Reflection coefficient ($\rho$)**: the ratio of reflected to incident voltage.
- **Stub**: the unused barrel beyond the layer where the signal exits.
- **Return path**: where the signal's return current flows, normally the adjacent plane.
- **Reference plane**: the plane a signal is coupled to.
- **Back-drilling**: removing a via stub by drilling from the back with a larger bit.
- **Mode conversion**: conversion of differential signal to common-mode energy due to asymmetry.
- **Via-in-pad**: a via placed inside a component's solder pad. See [[q0582]].
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "On high-speed traces, because they cause reflections."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Quantifies the penalty with the series-inductance reflection formula and gives a rise-time threshold (about 260 ps for 5 % with a 1.3 nH via).
- Lists distinct reasons (reflection, stub resonance, return path, differential skew) each with its own remedy.
- Includes the manufacturing reasons (via in pad, edge clearance, aspect ratio) and the power ones (single via bottleneck, plane perforation).
- Says what to do instead (stay on one layer, shared reference, ground via, back-drill, symmetric pairs).
- Understands the answer depends on edge rate, not bit rate or frequency alone.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Never use vias on high-speed signals."** Unrealistic. Control them: count, place ground vias, shorten stubs.
- **Judging by clock frequency, not edge rate.** A 10 MHz clock with a 1 ns edge behaves very differently from the same clock with a 100 ps edge.
- **Forgetting the return path.** The signal via may be perfect and the layout still fails if the return has no path.
- **Putting a via on one leg of a differential pair only.** Asymmetry creates skew and mode conversion.
- **Using vias as test points.** They are hard to probe and may be tented or plugged.
- **Treating "avoid" as absolute for manufacturing.** Via-in-pad is fine if filled and capped. The rule is "don't use an open one".
:::

The characteristic impedance $\sqrt{L/C}\approx53\ \Omega$ is close to 50 Ω, so the via is not a bad match by itself; the trouble comes from edges fast enough to see the lumped elements.

When the edge is slow, the line can't "see" the structure. When the edge is fast, it sees an inductor and a capacitor. The usual rule is that a discontinuity is electrically small if its delay is much smaller than the rise time. The via's own delay is $\sqrt{LC}\approx24$ ps, and the inductor-only reflection estimate gives

## Case by case: when to avoid

### 1. Multi-gigabit and clock nets

A 10 Gb/s NRZ signal has a unit interval of 100 ps and edges around 30–40 ps. A 1.3 nH via is a large step. Practical countermeasures:
- Minimise the number of layer changes (ideally zero, or two: one at each end where the transceiver and connector sit).
- Choose adjacent layers that share a reference plane.
- Put a ground via within 1–2 mm of each signal via (see [[q0575]]).
- Remove the stub (blind or microvia, or back-drill). See [[q0576]].
- Use anti-pad tuning (the size of the plane clearance) to lower the via capacitance and push its impedance toward 50 Ω; a field solver does this properly.

### 2. RF paths

At RF, vias in the ground return of matching networks, filters or LNAs shift the effective ground and add inductance in the wrong place. A 1.3 nH series inductance at 2.4 GHz is $2\pi\cdot2.4\times10^9\cdot1.3\times10^{-9}=19.6\ \Omega$. Place ground vias at the pad edge, use several in parallel (each parallel via cuts the inductance), and keep the RF trace on a single layer.

### 3. Differential pairs

Both legs should have the same number of vias, placed together, with ground vias that are symmetrical. A mismatch of one via adds a few ps of skew and converts differential energy to common-mode, which radiates. See [[q0518]].

### 4. Sensitive analog and crystals

A crystal's load capacitance is on the order of 10 pF; 0.5 pF of via adds a meaningful error (5 %), and the broken return path invites noise pickup. Keep the oscillator loop on one layer, tightly, over a solid plane. High-impedance nodes also dislike vias because they add leakage paths and pick up contamination.

### 5. SMT pads

An open via in a pad will wick solder and starve the joint. Either fill and cap it ([[q0578]]) or move it off the pad with a short trace and tent or plug it ([[q0579]]). For tiny passives (0402 and smaller), a via in only one of the two pads can pull solder asymmetrically and cause tombstoning, which is another reason to avoid it.

### 6. Edges, mounting holes and flex

Keep vias away from board edges, V-scores, and mounting holes, where routing or depaneling can crack plating or expose the barrel. Rules of thumb vary by fab (often a few tenths of a millimetre up to a millimetre or more). Keep vias out of bend regions on flex and rigid-flex boards.

### 7. Power

A single via is about 0.8 A. Never leave a power path with fewer vias than the current needs. Conversely, don't scatter dozens of vias through a plane so close together that the clearance holes merge (Swiss cheese; [[q0585]]).

### 8. Cost-sensitive designs

Each via is a drill hit. Boards with thousands of vias cost more in drilling time, and exotic types (blind, buried, micro) add lamination or laser steps. If a trace can stay on the layer, leave it there.

## A decision cheat sheet

| Situation | Avoid / limit | Do instead |
|---|---|---|
| Edge faster than about 260 ps on 50 Ω | layer changes | one layer, shared reference, ground via, short stub |
| Differential pair | asymmetric vias | symmetric vias, ground vias on both sides |
| RF matching network | ground via far from pad | ground vias at the pad, via arrays |
| Crystal/oscillator | vias in loop | single layer, tight loop over plane |
| SMT pad | open via in pad | fill and cap, or dogbone and tent |
| Board edge | vias within about 0.5-1 mm | keep clear of fab's rule |
| Power path | one or two vias | array sized by current |
| Plane | via rows with merged antipads | stagger, shrink antipads, add copper bridges |

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Close with: "I don't avoid vias, I budget them. For slow signals they're free. For the five or ten nets that matter, I decide before routing which layers they live on and I don't change layers unless I have to."
:::

::: applications
- **PCIe/USB/SerDes lanes**: routed on one or two layers, with ground vias at each transition and back-drilling on thick boards.
- **DDR memory interfaces**: via counts matched across byte lanes to preserve timing.
- **2.4 and 5 GHz radios**: RF trace kept on the top layer, with via fences and ground vias at component pads.
- **Precision analog front-ends**: guard rings and single-layer routing for high-impedance sensor inputs.
- **Crystal oscillator circuits**: loop kept on one layer on top of an unbroken ground plane.
- **Wave-soldered or selective-soldered boards**: vias kept out of exposed solder-side pads or plugged.
:::

::: assumptions
- 50 Ω single-ended line on FR-4, 1.6 mm board, 1.3 nH via (inductance only), rise time defined 0–100 %.
- The reflection estimate is an upper bound for a lumped series inductance; real vias are partially compensated by their capacitance.
- Edge rate, not clock frequency, sets the sensitivity. For a 10–90 % rise time, scale the 0–100 % numbers by roughly 1.25.
- The "within 1–2 mm" ground-via guideline is a rule of thumb, not a standard.
- For slow digital logic (below about 100 MHz with edges slower than a few ns), vias are effectively transparent.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the rise time matter more than the clock frequency?
Reflection from a small discontinuity depends on how fast the voltage changes across it. The inductive voltage is $L\,di/dt$, and $di/dt$ is set by the edge. A 10 MHz clock with a 100 ps edge has significant spectral content out to roughly the same frequency (about $0.35/t_r=3.5$ GHz) as a 1 GHz clock with the same edge, and each of its edges sees the via exactly the same way. So I look at $t_r$ and the knee, not just the clock rate.

### How would you reduce the harm if you absolutely must change layers on a fast signal?
Use adjacent layers that share a reference plane, add a ground via beside the signal via, pick the shortest via span (blind or microvia, or a transition near the surface), back-drill the stub, tune the antipad size, and simulate or measure with TDR. For a differential pair, do the same on both legs, symmetrically.

### Can you use a via-in-pad for a high-speed AC-coupling capacitor?
Yes if it is filled and capped, and the benefit is real: it eliminates the stub trace between pad and via, shortening the signal path. The cost is the fill process. A dogbone with a short stub works at lower speeds.

### Why not put vias right at the board edge to save space?
Routing and V-scoring can crack the plating or expose the barrel, causing opens or corrosion, and the copper near the edge is under mechanical stress. Fabs state a minimum copper-to-edge distance; I'd keep vias at least that far, often more near depanelling lines.

### Scenario: a 25 Gb/s link fails compliance. The only change from the working design was moving two connectors to opposite sides of the board. Why might vias be responsible?
Moving the connector to the other side added a layer change on each lane, plus longer stubs if the through vias weren't back-drilled. With a 1.6 mm board, a stub of about 1.2 mm resonates near 30 GHz and already adds loss well below it, and the extra vias add reflections. I'd check insertion loss and return loss with a VNA or TDR, add back-drilling or blind vias, and put ground vias beside each signal via.
:::
