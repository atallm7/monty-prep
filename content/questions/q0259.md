::: testing
Whether you understand an inductor as a **magnetic device with limits**, not an ideal $L$. The interviewer is checking: do you know *what* saturation is (flux density limit of the core, inductance collapses), *why* it is dangerous in a buck (current runs away because $di/dt = V/L$), *which current* to design against (the IC's current limit, not the load), and the difference between DC resistance and the frequency-dependent AC resistance (skin and proximity effect, plus core loss). Candidates who tie it to efficiency at light load and high $f_{SW}$ stand out.
:::

::: why
Inductors are the most commonly mis-specified part in a buck. A saturating inductor does not just "get a bit worse": the current spikes, the controller trips, FETs see peak currents far above design and the board might fail only on the cold-start, the hot day or the short-circuit test. And inductor loss is often the single biggest loss item at light load and megahertz frequencies, which is where DCR numbers alone mislead you.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor law**: $v = L\,di/dt$, so a smaller $L$ means a faster current rise. See [[q0211]].
- **Flux and saturation**: $B = \dfrac{L I}{N A_e}$; at $B_{sat}$ the core stops storing more flux and permeability drops.
- **Soft vs hard saturation**: powdered-iron/composite cores roll off gradually; ferrite stays flat then collapses.
- **$I_{sat}$ vs $I_{RMS}$ (heating current)**: two different datasheet ratings; use the lower limit for the situation.
- **DCR**: copper resistance of the winding, causes $I^2R$ loss with the DC+RMS current.
- **ACR**: effective resistance at the switching frequency; includes skin and proximity effects, and (loosely) core loss.
- **Current limit**: IC overcurrent threshold sets the real peak current ([[q0265]]).
:::

::: answer
"Saturation current matters because an inductor only stores energy while its core can take more flux. Once the current pushes the core to its saturation flux density, the inductance collapses. Since the inductor current slope is $V/L$, a collapsed L means the current shoots up with almost nothing to limit it except the resistance and the controller's reaction time. That can overshoot the current limit, stress the FETs, increase ripple and EMI, and in a short circuit ratchet the current until something breaks.

So I choose the inductor's saturation current above the IC's maximum current limit, with margin for temperature, since ferrite saturates at lower current when hot, and for the tolerance on the limit. The load current plus half ripple is not enough.

DCR versus ACR: DCR is the copper resistance, which sets $I^2R$ loss at full load. ACR is the effective resistance at the switching frequency, where skin and proximity effects and core loss add loss to the ripple component. At heavy load DCR dominates; at light load or high frequency with big ripple, ACR and core loss can dominate, so a low-DCR inductor can still be a hot, inefficient one."
:::

::: technical
**Saturation.** The core flux density is $B = \dfrac{L I}{N A_e}$. When $B$ approaches $B_{sat}$ (a few tenths of a tesla for ferrites, higher for powdered iron but with gradual roll-off, and falling with temperature), the incremental permeability falls and $L(I)$ drops. Datasheets define $I_{sat}$ as the DC current at which $L$ falls by a stated percentage (commonly 20 or 30%, vendor-dependent; check the definition). Consequences in a buck:

- The slope $di/dt = (V_{IN}-V_{OUT})/L_{sat}$ during on-time becomes much larger, so peak current overshoots the limit by $\Delta I = V\,t_d/L_{sat}$ over the comparator delay $t_d$.
- Ripple current rises, increasing RMS stress, EMI and output ripple.
- In the short-circuit case, current ratchets since off-time decay is slow and on-time ramp is fast ([[q0265]]).

**Design rule.** $I_{sat}(\text{at max temperature}) \geq I_{LIM(max)} + \text{margin}$, where $I_{LIM(max)}$ is the IC's maximum specified peak current limit (include tolerance and any DCM/transient values). Also check the heating current: $I_{RMS} \approx \sqrt{I_{OUT}^2+\Delta I^2/12}$ must be below $I_{rms}$ (temperature-rise rating, e.g. 40 °C rise; vendor-defined).

**Resistance.**
$$P_{L} \approx I_{RMS,DC}^2\,DCR + \frac{\Delta I^2}{12}\,ACR(f_{SW}) + P_{core}(\Delta B, f_{SW})$$
DCR $= \rho\,\ell/A$ (copper $\rho$ rises about 0.39%/°C). ACR rises with frequency through skin effect (skin depth in copper is $\delta \approx 66/\sqrt f$ mm, i.e. about 93 µm at 500 kHz) and proximity effect from adjacent turns/layers. Core loss depends on the ripple flux swing and frequency, not on the DC current. At heavy load in CCM with modest ripple, DCR dominates; at light load or when ripple is a large fraction of load, ACR/core loss dominate ([[q0217]]).
:::

So I pick $I_{sat}$ above the IC's maximum current limit, derated for temperature, not just load plus half ripple. DCR is the copper resistance that sets full-load conduction loss. ACR is the effective resistance at the switching frequency with skin and proximity effects, plus core loss, and it dominates at light load and high frequency."
:::

::: long
"Start with the physics: an inductor stores energy in the magnetic flux in its core, and flux density is proportional to L times current divided by turns and core area. The core can only carry so much flux. At saturation, effectively the core stops helping and the inductance drops, sometimes by a factor of five to ten in a ferrite, more gently in a powdered-iron or composite part.

Why that's bad in a buck: during on-time the current rises at $(V_{IN}-V_{OUT})/L$. If L collapses, that slope shoots up, so the current overshoots whatever limit the controller tries to enforce, because the current limit comparator and gate driver have a delay of tens to hundreds of nanoseconds. The FETs see far more than their design current, ripple and EMI spike, and in a hard short the current can ratchet up cycle after cycle.

So I design $I_{sat}$ against the *IC's maximum current limit*, at the hottest temperature, since ferrite saturates earlier when hot. Load current plus half ripple isn't enough, because the part has to survive startup, load steps and faults.

Then the resistance. DCR is the copper resistance; at full load the loss is roughly $I^2 \times DCR$. ACR is the effective resistance at the switching frequency, driven by skin and proximity effect, and it acts on the ripple current component. Core loss, which depends on ripple flux swing, is separate but gets lumped with it. At full load in CCM with 20 to 30% ripple, ripple loss is a rounding error and DCR wins. At light load, high frequency, or large ripple, ACR and core loss dominate, so I check the vendor's loss data or use a loss estimator."
:::

::: terms
- **Saturation current ($I_{sat}$)**: DC current at which inductance drops by a stated percentage (20 or 30% typical, vendor-specific).
- **Heating (RMS) current**: current causing a specified temperature rise (e.g. 40 °C).
- **DCR**: DC winding resistance.
- **ACR / Rac**: effective winding resistance at the switching frequency, including skin and proximity effects.
- **Skin effect**: current crowds near the conductor surface at high frequency, raising resistance.
- **Proximity effect**: adjacent conductors' fields redistribute current, adding loss.
- **Core loss**: hysteresis and eddy loss in the core, depends on flux swing and frequency.
- **Soft saturation**: gradual inductance roll-off (powdered/composite cores).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "if the inductor saturates it becomes a wire, so the current goes high" and "ACR is DCR at high frequency".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains saturation in terms of $B = LI/(NA_e)$ and shows why the current slope $V/L$ explodes.
- Quantifies a consequence: current overshoot $V\,t_d/L_{sat}$ over the current-limit delay.
- Says to design $I_{sat}$ against the **maximum current limit** over temperature, and separates $I_{sat}$ from $I_{rms}$.
- Distinguishes soft (powder/composite) from hard (ferrite) saturation and says why that matters for fault tolerance.
- Splits loss into $I^2 DCR$ + ripple $\times$ ACR + core loss and says when each dominates, with a number.
- Mentions temperature dependence of DCR (+0.39%/°C) and $I_{sat}$.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Choosing $I_{sat}$ = load current + ripple/2.** Peak current under faults and transients is set by the IC limit.
- **Mixing up $I_{sat}$ and $I_{rms}$.** They are two independent limits; use whichever is lower for the case that matters.
- **Reading DCR at 25 °C.** At 105 °C copper DCR is about 31% higher.
- **Assuming a low-DCR part is always efficient.** A big ferrite with low DCR can have high core loss at MHz; at light load, core and AC loss dominate.
- **Assuming the inductance is its nominal value at full load.** With $I_{sat}$ defined at 30% drop, ripple at peak current is notably larger.
- **Ignoring tolerance and temperature on $I_{sat}$.** Ferrite $B_{sat}$ falls when hot; vendor curves give $I_{sat}$ at 25 and 100 °C.
:::

::: deep
## What saturation is

An inductor's flux $\Phi = B A_e$ and linkage $N\Phi = L i$. For a core with fixed $N$ and $A_e$, $B = \dfrac{L i}{N A_e}$. As long as the core material has high permeability, $L$ stays constant and $B$ rises linearly with $i$. A magnetic material has only so many aligned domains; at $B_{sat}$ they are all aligned, and the material's incremental permeability drops towards that of air. The inductance falls toward the air-core value.

*Example geometry.* With $N = 10$, $A_e = 10$ mm² and $L = 6.8$ µH at $I = 5$ A, $B = (6.8\times10^{-6})(5)/(10\times10^{-5}) = 0.34$ T. That is at or beyond ferrite's saturation, so a designer would add an air gap (reducing permeability but raising $I_{sat}$) or use a powdered-iron core. This is why real inductors have gapped ferrite or distributed-gap powder cores.

**Overshoot at the limit.** If the IC's limit is 5 A and the delay from comparator trip to FET off is $t_d = 100$ ns, a healthy inductor overshoots by $V t_d/L = 8.7\times100\text{ns}/6.8\mu\text{H} = 0.13$ A. A saturated inductor with $L = 0.68$ µH overshoots by $1.28$ A, to over 6 A, which the FETs and the inductor itself must survive repeatedly in a hiccup mode.

## Soft vs hard saturation

Ferrite cores keep their inductance flat until near $B_{sat}$ and then fall abruptly: good efficiency, dangerous failure. Powdered-iron and composite (molded) cores have distributed air gaps and roll off gradually from the start, so their inductance at full load is already a bit lower than the nominal. This is gentler in a fault but gives more ripple at peak load and higher core loss. Vendor datasheets give $L$ vs DC bias curves; use them rather than a single $I_{sat}$ number.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Definitions of $I_{sat}$ (10, 20 or 30% drop) and of heating current (temperature rise of 20, 40 °C) differ between manufacturers, so two parts with the same printed numbers may not be equivalent. Always read the footnote defining the test condition.

## Choosing the saturation requirement

1. Find the IC's **maximum** peak current limit over temperature and tolerance (for a part rated 3 A, a typical limit might be 4.5 to 6 A; read the datasheet).
2. Add overshoot from comparator/gate delay ($Vt_d/L$), 10 to 20% temperature margin for ferrite.
3. Pick $I_{sat}$ (at 100 °C if given) above that. In the running 3 A example with a 5 A typical and 6 A maximum limit, I'd want about 6.5 to 7 A saturation current.
4. Check $I_{RMS} \approx 3.0$ A against $I_{rms}$ (say 4 A rating) and confirm temperature rise.

The cost of this margin is size; $I_{sat}$ in a given package rises with core volume and falls with L. If a part is too large, use a lower current-limit option on the IC, or accept foldback/hiccup behaviour.

## DCR and ACR

Conduction loss in the winding at full load:
$$P_{DCR} = (I_{OUT}^2 + \Delta I^2/12)\,DCR$$
For $I_{OUT} = 3$ A, $\Delta I = 0.79$ A and DCR = 15 mΩ, $P = 9.05\times0.015 = 0.136$ W. At 105 °C copper's resistance is $1+0.00393\times80 = 1.31$ times higher, so 0.178 W.

The **ripple** component also sees ACR. Skin depth in copper is $\delta = \sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu_0)}$: 93 µm at 500 kHz, 47 µm at 2 MHz and 30 µm at 5 MHz. Wire thicker than about $2\delta$ carries current mainly near its surface, and adjacent turns create proximity effect that can raise AC resistance several-fold over DC in multi-layer windings. Flat-wire and single-layer designs reduce this.

*How much does it matter?* In the 3 A example, ripple power is $\frac{\Delta I^2}{12}\times ACR = 0.052\times 4\,DCR \approx 0.003$ W if ACR is four times DCR (an illustrative factor): irrelevant. But for a small converter delivering 1 A with 1.5 A p-p ripple (r = 150%, typical at 3 MHz in a tiny part), the ripple's mean-square is $1.5^2/12 = 0.19$ A², so with ACR = 4×DCR the AC loss is 0.75 of the DC loss. The cheapest-DCR inductor may then not be the best. At light load in DCM/PFM, current is mostly ripple: *peak* currents are large and ACR plus core loss dominate.

## Core loss

Core loss in ferrite scales roughly as $f^{\alpha} B_{pk}^{\beta}$ with $\alpha\approx1.1$ to 1.6 and $\beta\approx2$ to 3 (Steinmetz fit; values are material-specific). $B_{pk}$ depends on the **ripple** flux swing, not the DC current. Halving the ripple (doubling L) cuts core loss by about 4 to 8×, while raising DCR for the extra turns, hence the optimum. This is why efficiency curves often show a lighter-load dip.

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Temperature.** Ferrite $B_{sat}$ falls with temperature, so an inductor that is just OK at 25 °C may saturate in a hot enclosure. DCR rises +0.39%/°C, increasing heating, a mild positive feedback.
- **Shielded vs unshielded.** Shielding with a magnetic material slightly reduces $I_{sat}$ at the same size but reduces EMI.
- **Audible noise.** Magnetostriction and inductor movement can cause audible tones in PFM/burst modes.
- **Self-resonance.** Winding capacitance gives a self-resonant frequency; above it the inductor looks capacitive and ringing on the switch node couples through ([[q0258]]).

::: applications
- **Any buck with an internal current limit**: pick $I_{sat}$ above the limit so short-circuit and start-up are safe.
- **Automotive DC-DC**: AEC-Q200 shielded inductors rated for 125 to 150 °C operation, with $I_{sat}$ checked at the hot end for high under-hood ambients.
- **MHz POL converters**: tiny low-profile inductors where ACR and core loss dominate, so vendors publish loss curves.
- **Battery devices with PFM**: low core loss material and small ripple to hold efficiency at mA loads ([[q0230]]).
- **High-current CPU rails**: coupled or low-profile powdered-iron inductors with soft saturation to tolerate large transients.
:::

::: assumptions
- Continuous conduction, fixed $f_{SW}$, and a peak current limit set by the IC. In DCM/PFM, peak currents are higher than the average, so the same $I_{sat}$ logic applies to the peak value.
- The values for ACR multiples and core-loss exponents are illustrative; use manufacturer data and loss calculators for real parts.
- The 100 ns delay and 5 A limit are example numbers. Actual current-limit delay and tolerance vary by IC.
- Valley-current-limit controllers see the peak = limit + ripple, which raises the $I_{sat}$ requirement relative to the limit nominal.
- Some inductors are specified with **both** saturation (L drop) and **heating** current; the real limit is the lower one for your condition.
:::

::: followups
### What happens in a buck if the inductor saturates during a load step?
During the step the controller increases duty, driving the inductor current up quickly. If the peak goes past $I_{sat}$, L drops, the slope increases, the current limit trips sooner than intended with larger overshoot, and the output may glitch because the controller then cycles in and out of current limit. The circuit usually recovers, but the FETs and the inductor see peak stress and EMI jumps. Using a part with $I_{sat}$ above the IC's limit prevents this.

### Why do powdered-iron inductors tolerate overcurrent better than ferrite ones?
Their distributed air gaps make the permeability roll off gradually as flux builds, so inductance falls 10 to 30% by $I_{sat}$ and keeps falling slowly beyond it rather than collapsing. Ferrite has high permeability until it reaches $B_{sat}$, then drops steeply. The trade-off is lower efficiency at normal current for powder (more core loss at high $f$, lower L at load).

### Two inductors have the same 6.8 µH and $I_{sat}$ but different DCR and size. How do you choose?
Compare loss at the operating point: $P \approx I_{RMS}^2 DCR$ + AC and core loss. At 3 A, a 10 mΩ vs 25 mΩ difference is $9\times0.015 = 0.135$ W, which is about 1.4% of 9.9 W output. If it's a light-load product, compare core and AC loss curves at the ripple and frequency in use. Also consider height, shielding/EMI, cost and thermal rise.

### Scenario: the board works at room temperature but trips current limit on a hot day. What do you suspect about the inductor?
Inductance dropping with temperature and bias: ferrite $B_{sat}$ falls at 100 °C, so effective $I_{sat}$ falls and ripple at peak rises, or DCR rise warms the part further. Check inductor temperature with a thermocouple/IR, view $i_L$ with a current probe (look for a knee in the ramp), and compare with the datasheet's $I_{sat}$ at 100 °C. Fix with a higher $I_{sat}$ part or a lower current-limit setting.

### Why can the ripple loss in the winding exceed the DC loss at high frequency?
Ripple current at $f_{SW}$ sees ACR, not DCR, which can be several times higher because of skin and proximity effects. If ripple is a large fraction of the load, as in MHz converters with tiny inductors ($r \gtrsim 1$), $\Delta I^2/12 \times ACR$ is comparable to $I_{OUT}^2\times DCR$. Plus core loss, which depends on ripple not load.
:::

::: interpretations
"ACR vs DCR" is read as winding resistance at AC vs DC. Some people use ACR to mean total AC loss including core loss (a so-called equivalent series resistance); I mention both. If the interviewer means ESR of the inductor, that's the same family of concept.
:::
