::: testing
Whether you understand that **a "ground" pin is not an ideal zero-volt node**. Wires and package leads have inductance, and when current changes quickly ($di/dt$) that inductance develops a voltage. The interviewer wants the mechanism ($V = L\,di/dt$ across the shared return path), the main variables (number of simultaneously switching outputs, edge rate, package inductance), the symptoms (quiet signals glitching, false triggers, resets) and the fixes (more ground pins, slower edges, decoupling, better package and layout). Bonus points for distinguishing ground bounce from supply droop.
:::

::: why
Ground bounce is a classic source of "it works until I switch all 16 outputs at once" bugs. It corrupts quiet lines, shifts input thresholds, causes spurious resets and clock glitches, and gets worse as edges speed up. It drives decisions about package choice, pin assignment, drive strength settings (slew control) and decoupling placement. It also underlies the questions about decoupling distance ([[q0504]], [[q0508]]) and parasitic inductance ([[q0509]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor law**: $v = L\,di/dt$; a fast current change through a small inductance produces a real voltage. See [[q0041]].
- **Return current path**: every signal current must return to its source, here through package ground pins and bond wires. See [[q0509]].
- **Parasitic inductance of leads, bond wires, vias**: roughly 1 nH per mm of wire; a few nH for a typical package ground path. See [[q0510]].
- **Simultaneous switching output (SSO / SSN)**: many outputs switching in the same direction add their $di/dt$ into one shared inductance.
- **Logic thresholds**: a bounce matters when it approaches $V_{IL}$ of a receiver or a quiet pin.
:::

::: answer
"Ground bounce is the voltage that appears on a chip's internal ground, relative to the board ground, when a lot of current changes quickly through the ground pin or bond wire. That path has inductance, maybe a couple of nanohenries, so the voltage is $V = L\,di/dt$. If eight outputs switch high-to-low at once, all their discharge current flows back through the same package ground inductance, the di/dt adds up, and the chip's ground node lifts by hundreds of millivolts for a nanosecond or so.

What you see is the chip's quiet outputs that are supposed to stay low jumping up with the bounce, which can false-trigger the next chip, and the chip's own inputs seeing their thresholds shifted because their reference moved. You can get double clocking, resets, or corrupted data that only happens when a wide bus changes.

To reduce it, I cut $L$ or $di/dt$: more ground pins in parallel, a package with shorter leads like a BGA, slower edges using slew-rate control or series resistors, fewer outputs switching at the same moment, and good decoupling and a solid ground plane right at the part."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism.** Output drivers discharge their load capacitance $C_L$ (the trace plus the receiver pin) to ground through the chip's ground pins. The return current $i_{gnd}(t)$ flows through the package's ground inductance $L_{gnd}$ (bond wire, lead frame or BGA ball path) before reaching board ground. The voltage across it is

$$V_{bounce} = N\,L_{gnd}\,\frac{di_1}{dt}$$

where $N$ outputs switch simultaneously and $di_1/dt$ is the current slope of each. With $L_{gnd}$ shared across $N_g$ ground pins in parallel, the effective $L$ falls to about $L/N_g$ (ignoring mutual inductance, which raises it).

**Numbers.** Take $N = 8$, $L_{gnd} = 2$ nH, each output sinking a smooth pulse of peak 16 mA lasting 1 ns, so $di_1/dt|_{peak} = 16\text{ mA}\cdot\pi/1\text{ ns} = 50$ mA/ns. Then $V_{bounce,pk} = 8\cdot 2\text{ nH}\cdot 50\text{ mA/ns} = 0.80$ V. Halve the edge time at the same current and the bounce doubles to 1.6 V; double $N$ and it also doubles. Edge rate is the lever: bounce scales roughly as $1/t_r$ at fixed current, and the current itself rises as edges speed up.

**Consequences.**
1. *Quiet-low output glitch*: a pin held low by its driver sits at die ground, so it rises with the bounce. A neighbouring receiver sees up to $V_{bounce}$ on a nominally low line; a bounce above $V_{IL}$ (for instance 0.8 V for LVTTL) can false-trigger it.
2. *Input threshold shift*: inputs are referenced to die ground. A board-level high of 3.3 V looks like $3.3 - V_{bounce}$ relative to the die, reducing high-level margin.
3. *Supply rail droop*: $V_{DD}$ and $V_{SS}$ bounce together; the on-die supply collapses by the sum of both inductive drops.

**Mitigation.** Lower $L$ (more ground pins, adjacent power/ground pairs, shorter package, direct low-inductance vias to the ground plane); lower $di/dt$ (slew-rate or drive-strength control, series resistors, lower $C_L$, lower swing, staggering output timing); local decoupling ([[q0508]]) supplies the burst of charge so the loop through the board is small; differential or balanced outputs cancel net $di/dt$.
:::

::: short
"Ground bounce is the voltage that appears on a chip's local ground when many outputs switch at once. The return current flows through package and bond-wire inductance, so $V = N\,L\,di/dt$ lifts the die ground relative to the board. Quiet outputs glitch and input thresholds shift, causing false triggers. I reduce it by lowering the inductance, with more ground pins and a better package, or by lowering di/dt with slower edges and fewer simultaneous switches."
:::

::: long
"Ground bounce is a noise voltage on a chip's internal ground caused by fast changing current through the inductance of the ground connection. A package's ground path, meaning the bond wire, lead or ball, plus the via to the plane, isn't zero ohms; it's maybe 1 to 5 nanohenries. When an output switches, it dumps its load capacitance's charge through that path, and the voltage across the inductance is $L$ times $di/dt$.

The problem is that it adds up. If eight outputs on a bus switch from high to low at the same instant, the current slopes add in the same shared inductance, so $V = N\,L\,di/dt$. With 2 nH, eight outputs and 50 milliamps per nanosecond each, that's about 0.8 V of bounce.

The effects are two-sided. A quiet output held low is tied to the die ground, so relative to the board ground it jumps up by the bounce, and the chip it drives might see that as a legitimate high and glitch. Also the chip's own inputs are referenced to that bouncing ground, so their effective thresholds move. You can get false clocks, double triggers, resets, or corrupted data on a bus, usually only when many pins toggle together, which makes it nasty to reproduce.

To fix it I attack $L$, $di/dt$, or $N$. For $L$: more ground pins in parallel, a package with shorter leads like a BGA, and a direct via to the ground plane. For $di/dt$: slower edges with slew control or series resistors, lower load capacitance, lower swing. For $N$: spread the switching in time, or move the sensitive signal to a pin far from the noisy outputs. And decoupling close to the package helps the supply side."
:::

::: terms
- **Ground bounce**: transient voltage between the die ground and the board ground caused by $L\,di/dt$ in the return path.
- **SSN / SSO**: simultaneous switching noise / outputs; the aggregate effect of many drivers toggling together.
- **Return path**: the route a signal's current takes back to its source; it carries the $di/dt$ that makes the voltage.
- **Package inductance**: bond wire, lead and via inductance in series with the die connection, in nH.
- **Slew-rate control**: output-driver setting that limits $dV/dt$ and thereby $di/dt$.
- **Quiet pin**: an output (or input) held at a static level that picks up the glitch.
- **Supply bounce ($V_{DD}$ droop)**: the matching disturbance on the supply side; together they reduce on-die supply.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "noise on ground when outputs switch". An exceptional one writes **$V = N\,L\,di/dt$**, explains that the voltage appears across the *package* inductance of the shared return, and walks the **two failure paths**: a quiet-low output rising (false trigger of the downstream chip) and the die's own input thresholds shifting. It quantifies a case (for example 8 outputs, 2 nH, 50 mA/ns gives 0.8 V), notes that parallel ground pins divide $L$ but mutual inductance limits the benefit, and ties the fix to **slew-rate control, pin assignment and decoupling placement**. It also notes the common field symptom: it only fails when the whole bus toggles with a specific data pattern.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Ground is 0 V everywhere."** Only at DC. At nanosecond edges every nanohenry matters; a 1 nH path with 1 A/ns is 1 V.
- **Blaming only the bypass capacitor.** A bypass cap supplies charge to the supply side, but ground bounce is voltage across the *return* inductance; the cap's own mounting inductance and the return path still matter.
- **Thinking more ground pins fully divide inductance.** Adjacent pins have mutual inductance, so $N$ pins give less than $L/N$ unless they are spread and carry opposing currents.
- **Confusing ground bounce with crosstalk.** Crosstalk is coupling between signal traces ([[q0694]]); ground bounce is a common-impedance (shared return) effect.
- **Slowing every edge blindly.** Slower edges cut bounce but eat setup margin and can hurt jitter ([[q0689]]); use slew control where needed.
- **Measuring bounce with a long ground clip.** The probe's own ground lead inductance creates a fake bounce. Measure on a quiet output or with a short spring.
:::

::: deep
## The physical picture

Take one output driver charging then discharging a load capacitor $C_L$. To discharge, the NMOS pulls the output to the **die's** ground pin. The charge must get back to the board's ground, and between the die and the board sits the package: bond wire, lead frame (or BGA trace), solder joint, via. A rule of thumb is about 1 nH per mm of straight wire, so a 4 mm bond wire plus lead is a few nH.

Voltage across an inductor depends on the *rate of change* of current, not the current itself. When the output starts to switch, $i_{gnd}$ ramps up (positive $di/dt$, die ground rises). When the current peaks, $di/dt = 0$ and the bounce crosses zero. As the current dies away, $di/dt$ is negative and the die ground dips *below* board ground. So bounce is a bipolar ringing pulse, and the L and the local capacitance can ring afterward ([[q0696]]).

## A worked number

Model each output's discharge current as $i_1(t) = I_p \sin^2(\pi t / T)$ for $0 < t < T$. Then

$$\frac{di_1}{dt} = I_p\,\frac{\pi}{T}\,\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi t}{T}\right),\qquad V_b(t) = N\,L_{gnd}\,\frac{di_1}{dt}$$

With $N = 8$, $L = 2$ nH, $I_p = 16$ mA, $T = 1$ ns:

$$V_{b,pk} = 8\cdot 2\times10^{-9}\cdot 0.016\cdot\pi/10^{-9} = 0.80\text{ V}$$

## Why a quiet output glitches

A quiet output is held low by its NMOS, which is connected to the die ground. If the die ground rises by $V_b$, that pin's voltage with respect to board ground rises by about $V_b$ (the NMOS has only a small on-resistance drop). Seen by the receiving chip, a "low" line pulses up by 0.8 V. If the receiver is a clock input, it may take that as a new rising edge (compare the monotonic requirement in [[q0690]]). The same bounce, referenced the other way, makes a board-level high input look smaller to the die: $3.3 - 0.8 = 2.5$ V, still above $V_{IH}$ = 2.0 V, but the margin is down from 1.3 V to 0.5 V.

## Do the mitigations, with arithmetic

| Fix | Effect on $V_b = N L\,di/dt$ |
|---|---|
| 2 ground pins instead of 1 | $L \to \approx L/2$ (a bit more, with mutual coupling) |
| Double the edge time | $di/dt \to$ half |
| Stagger 8 outputs into 2 groups | $N \to 4$ at any instant |
| 22 Ω series resistor on each output | limits peak current and $di/dt$ |
| Lower $C_L$ | less charge, less current |
| Flip-chip or BGA (about 0.5–1 nH) | $L$ down by 2–4x |

## Real hardware behaviour

- Datasheets for logic families commonly characterise this directly with a "quiet output" test (some list parameters such as $V_{OLP}$ and $V_{OLV}$, peak and valley of a quiet low output during simultaneous switching).
- FPGAs and large ASICs give guidelines on SSO limits per I/O bank and per ground pin, and recommend drive-strength and slew settings accordingly.
- On the PCB, ground bounce also exists *outside* the chip: a shared via or a thin ground return between a driver and the rest of the board. The same $L\,di/dt$ rules apply; this is why decoupling caps go right at the supply pins with short vias ([[q0504]], [[q0508]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Package ground inductance varies widely (sub-nH for flip-chip BGA up to 5–10 nH for long-lead DIPs and some QFPs). Use the manufacturer's package model or datasheet value, not the 2 nH used in the examples.
:::

::: applications
- **Wide parallel buses (DDR address/data, 16/32-bit MCU buses)**: SSO limits and drive-strength settings keep ground bounce inside margin.
- **FPGA I/O banks**: tools report SSO noise per bank; designers limit simultaneous fast outputs per power/ground pair.
- **LED or relay drivers on a microcontroller**: toggling many high-current outputs together can reset the MCU; fix with slower edges and local ground vias.
- **High-speed ADC/DAC boards**: digital switching noise bounces the shared ground and couples into the analog reference; split return paths and decoupling prevent it.
- **Logic family selection**: low-ground-bounce parts (lower slew, multiple ground pins) are chosen for wide-bus buffers.
- **Package selection**: BGAs and flip-chip packages have low inductance and are chosen for high-speed, high-pin-count devices.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Lumped inductance model**: the package return is a single inductor. At very high edge rates, the distributed nature of the plane and package must be modelled with full PDN analysis ([[q0509]]).
- **All outputs switch in the same direction at the same time**: the worst case. Real data patterns give a distribution; the worst case matters for guaranteed margin.
- **Constant $L$**: mutual inductance between adjacent bonds and nonlinear effects are ignored. Mutual inductance raises effective $L$ when currents flow the same way.
- **Ideal board ground**: board plane inductance is neglected; on a poor 2-layer board that is not valid.
- If the question is about the **supply** side, the same math applies to $V_{DD}$ with the opposite sign; decoupling capacitors and on-die capacitance share the burden.
:::

::: followups
### Ground bounce versus ground noise from a shared return trace, what's the difference?
The mechanism is the same, common-impedance coupling, but "ground bounce" usually refers to the chip package's return inductance, while board-level ground noise comes from sharing a return trace or via between circuits. The cure for the board version is a solid plane and separate return vias for high-di/dt currents.

### How many ground pins would I need to keep bounce below 100 mV in the 8-output example?
Need $L_{eff} = 0.1/(8\cdot 0.05) = 0.25$ nH. From 2 nH that's 8 parallel pins if perfectly uncoupled; mutual inductance makes it more, so I'd also slow the edges. In practice I'd combine two measures, such as 4 ground pins and a slew-limited drive, rather than rely only on pin count.

### Why does slowing the edge help, and what does it cost?
$V_b \propto di/dt$, so doubling the edge time halves the bounce. The cost is added propagation delay and lower setup margin, plus longer time in the receiver's threshold region ([[q0689]]). So slow only what you must.

### A signal fails only when a specific data pattern is sent. Could ground bounce explain it? How would you check?
Yes: a pattern that makes many outputs toggle together maximizes $N$. I'd scope a quiet output on the same chip with a short ground spring (it should stay flat) while sending that pattern, and look at its glitch size against $V_{IL}$. If it glitches, change drive strength or add ground/decoupling, then re-test.

### Could ground bounce be in the 'supply' as well? What does a bypass capacitor do?
Yes, the supply sees an equal and related bounce through $V_{DD}$ inductance. A nearby bypass capacitor supplies the transient current locally, so the current loop on the board is tiny and the inductance in it is small; but it can't fix the bond-wire inductance inside the package, which is why package choice matters.
:::
