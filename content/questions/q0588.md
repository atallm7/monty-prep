::: testing
Whether you know why testing is *hard*, not only what to test. The interviewer is probing for experience with the gap between the schematic and the real board:

1. **Access**: BGAs, inner layers, tiny pads, no test points.
2. **Measurement effects**: probes load and disturb the circuit; ground leads add ringing.
3. **Fault localisation**: shorts, intermittent faults, thermal-only failures.
4. **Ambiguity**: is it the design, the assembly, the firmware or the instrument?
5. **Scale and cost**: fixtures, time, coverage at volume.
:::

::: why
Most debug time is spent finding where the fault is, not fixing it. Engineers who plan for access (test points), account for instrument effects, and isolate variables finish bring-up in days instead of weeks. These are also the reasons design-for-test exists.
:::

::: concepts
- **Probe loading**: probe R and C change the circuit. See [[q0627]].
- **Ground loop and bandwidth**: see [[q0629]].
- **Test access / DFT**: see [[q0616]].
- **Intermittent faults**: temperature or mechanical dependence.
- **Test coverage**: fraction of faults detectable.
:::

::: answer
"The biggest challenge is access. Modern boards have BGAs, inner layers and tiny pads, so you can't just touch the node you care about. If the designer didn't add test points, you're stuck.

Second is that the measurement changes the circuit. A scope probe adds capacitance and resistance, a long ground lead rings and fakes noise, and a high-impedance node gets loaded by a multimeter. So you can break something by measuring it, or see a problem that isn't there.

Third is localising faults. A short between two planes, an intermittent crack that only appears when warm, or a marginal timing bug that only shows at temperature are hard to find without a methodical approach.

Fourth is ambiguity: when it doesn't work, is it the hardware, the assembly, the firmware or my test setup? I isolate by changing one thing at a time, comparing against a known-good board, and testing blocks separately.

And at scale there's cost and time: fixtures, programming, and getting good coverage without slowing the line."
:::

::: technical
**Challenges and mitigations.**

| Challenge | Why | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|
| Access | BGA/QFN, inner layers, fine pitch | test points, boundary scan, bed-of-nails planning, via access |
| Probe loading | 10x probe about 10 MΩ ‖ 12 pF; 1x about 1 MΩ ‖ 100 pF + cable | active probes, high-Z, probe the right node |
| Ground loop | lead inductance rings with probe C | spring ground, short return, differential probe |
| Bandwidth | scope/probe limits, rise time $t_r=0.35/BW$ | choose BW ≥ 3-5x signal knee |
| Shorts | resistance low, location unknown | current injection with voltage mapping, thermal camera |
| Intermittent faults | cracks, cold joints, thermal | thermal cycling, flexing, spray, logging |
| Ambiguity | multiple causes | compare boards, isolate blocks |
| Safety | high voltage, ESD | isolation, ESD protection |
| Scale | cost, time | AOI/ICT/FCT, DFT |

**Probe loading numbers.** At 100 MHz a 12 pF probe has $X_C=1/(2\pi\cdot10^8\cdot12\times10^{-12})\approx133\ \Omega$; a 100 pF 1x probe about 16 Ω. Hanging 133 Ω on a high-impedance node at 100 MHz changes it a lot. A 100 MHz scope has rise time about 3.5 ns.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Probe R/C values vary by model; use the probe's datasheet.
:::

::: short
"The main challenges are access, since BGAs, inner layers and tiny pads hide nodes; measurement effects, because probes load the circuit and long ground leads ring; localising shorts and intermittent faults; telling hardware, assembly, firmware and setup problems apart; and doing all this at scale with fixtures and coverage. I plan test points, use proper probing, and isolate variables one at a time."
:::

::: long
"There are five challenges I always plan for. The first is access. With BGAs, QFNs and inner layers you often can't reach the node you want, so if the designer didn't add test points or boundary scan, you're limited.

The second is that measuring disturbs the circuit. A typical ten-times scope probe is ten megohms with about twelve picofarads. At 100 megahertz that capacitance is about 130 ohms, which is a heavy load on a high-impedance node. And a long ground lead adds inductance that rings with the probe capacitance, so you see noise that isn't there or hide noise that is.

Third, finding the fault. A power short might be a few milliohms, and you have to locate it with current injection or a thermal camera. Intermittent faults, like a cracked via or a cold joint, only show with temperature or flexing.

Fourth, ambiguity. When a board doesn't work it could be design, assembly, firmware or my setup, so I change one thing at a time, compare against a known-good board, and test blocks separately.

And fifth, at volume, you need fixtures, test time and coverage that make economic sense, which is why DFT matters."
:::

::: terms
- **Test point**: pad for probing a node.
- **Probe loading**: circuit change from the probe's R and C.
- **Ground lead inductance**: probe return adds L.
- **Intermittent fault**: occurs only under some conditions.
- **Known-good board**: reference for comparison.
- **Coverage**: fraction of faults a test can detect.
- **Fixture**: test interface hardware.
:::

::: exceptional
**Average**: "Hard to probe small parts."

**Exceptional**:
- Quantifies probe loading and bandwidth.
- Distinguishes access, measurement, localisation, ambiguity, scale.
- Gives concrete mitigations for each.
- Mentions DFT as the root fix.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using the long alligator ground** on fast signals. Its inductance rings with the probe capacitance and invents noise. Use a spring tip.
- **Probing the wrong node**, for example on the far side of a series resistor. Probe at the pin that matters.
- **Blaming firmware first** without checking rails, resets and clocks. Prove the hardware basics first.
- **Changing several things at once.** You can't tell which change mattered. Change one variable at a time.
- **Ignoring ESD and safety.** A test can destroy a prototype or hurt you. Use a wrist strap, current limits and isolation where needed.
:::

::: deep
## Challenge 1: access

QFN pads are underneath; BGA balls are hidden; inner traces are buried. Remedies: test points on key nets ([[q0616]]), boundary scan, vias exposed for probing, and designing the board so each rail and clock can be reached.

## Challenge 3: locating faults

Short on a rail: inject current from a bench supply (e.g. 1 A at low voltage) and measure the rail-to-ground voltage at different points with a DMM on its millivolt range (it falls as you approach the short), or watch with a thermal camera. For intermittents: heat, cool with freeze spray, flex gently while monitoring.

## Challenge 4: ambiguity

Compare to a known-good board, swap the suspect part, split the circuit (cut jumpers or 0 Ω links) to isolate.

## Challenge 5: scale

Production test needs fixtures that touch every needed net quickly, so designing test points and test coverage is part of the design. See [[q0616]].

> A good story: "A noisy rail turned out to be my ground lead."

::: followups
### Why does a long ground lead create ringing?
Lead inductance forms a resonant circuit with probe capacitance; a 100 nH lead with 12 pF resonates near 145 MHz.

### How do you find a short between 3.3 V and ground?
Inject a limited current (say 1 A at a volt or less) and either find the hot part with a thermal camera or follow the falling millivolt readings along the rail toward the short. Remove parts to isolate.

### How would you test a signal under a BGA?
Use test points on fan-out vias, boundary scan, or reach it through connected devices' observation features.

### How can you tell if your scope bandwidth is enough?
Rise time $0.35/BW$ should be at least 3-5 times faster than the signal edge.

### Scenario: probing a 10 MΩ-source node with a 1x probe reads only about a tenth of the expected voltage. Why?
The 1x probe is about 1 MΩ, so it forms a divider with the 10 MΩ source: $V_{meas}=V\cdot\frac{1}{1+10}\approx0.09V$, a 91 % error. A 10x probe (10 MΩ) would still halve it, so for such nodes use an active/FET probe or a high-impedance meter and consider the node's real source impedance.
:::
