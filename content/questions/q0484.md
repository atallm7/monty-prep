::: basics
### In plain words
On a circuit board, ground is two things at once. It is the zero-volt reference that every voltage is measured against, and it is the route that every current takes to get back to its source. The schematic shows one ground symbol, but on the board it is real copper with real resistance and inductance. So current flowing in ground creates small voltages of its own, and those can disturb other circuits.

### Picture it
Imagine two apartments sharing one drain pipe. When one tenant empties a full bathtub, the other tenant sees the water level in their sink bob up and down. Two circuits sharing a ground conductor behave the same way: one circuit's current moves the other circuit's zero. The analogy is loose on one point: for fast changes the trouble comes from the pipe's "inertia" (inductance), not just its narrowness.

### Words you need
- **Return path**: the route current takes back to its source to complete the loop ([[q0004]]).
- **Reference**: the node called 0 V, against which other voltages are measured.
- **Inductance**: a conductor's resistance to sudden changes in current. A changing current through it creates a voltage, $L \times$ (rate of change).
- **Common-impedance coupling**: two circuits sharing a conductor, so one's current shows up as voltage for the other ([[q0497]]).
- **Ground plane**: a solid copper layer that gives a return path with very low resistance and inductance ([[q0486]]).

### The simplest example
A digital block's current jumps by 100 mA in 2 ns through a ground trace with 10 nH of inductance. The voltage across the trace is $L\,di/dt = 10\ \text{nH} \times 0.1\ \text{A} / 2\ \text{ns} = 0.5$ V. A sensor sharing that ground sees its zero jump by half a volt. Even steady current matters: a 1 mm wide, 50 mm long trace of standard copper is about 24 mΩ, so 1 A drops 24 mV. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers why a solid plane is the usual fix, ground loops and loop area, and the different kinds of ground.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand ground as a **conductor with impedance**, not as a magical zero-volt sink. The interviewer wants: ground is the reference node *and* the return path for every signal and supply current; because real copper has resistance and inductance, return currents create voltage drops that shift the "zero" for other circuits (common-impedance coupling), which is why grounding decides noise, accuracy and EMI. Good answers distinguish signal ground, power ground, chassis/earth, and say how a plane fixes most of the problem.
:::

::: why
Most "mystery" board problems (an ADC with extra noise, a microcontroller that resets when a motor starts, a board that fails radiated emissions, an op-amp that oscillates) are grounding problems in disguise. The schematic shows one ground symbol, but the board has many ground points at slightly different potentials. Understanding why is the foundation for all the other grounding questions ([[q0485]]–[[q0500]]) and is a fast interview filter for people who have actually debugged hardware.
:::

- **Return path**: current must complete a loop; every current out of a pin returns to its source through "ground". See [[q0004]].
- **Common-impedance coupling**: two circuits sharing a return conductor see each other's currents as voltage. See [[q0497]].
- **Conductor impedance**: $Z = R + j\omega L$; at high frequency the inductance dominates. See [[q0509]].
- **Ground plane**: a solid layer that gives low impedance and a defined return. See [[q0486]].
- **Earth/chassis vs. signal ground**: different purposes and often different nodes. See [[q0493]].

::: answer
"On a PCB, ground is two things at once. It's the reference node, the zero volts everything is measured against, and it's the return path, the route every bit of current takes to get back to its source. The schematic draws it as one symbol, but on the board it's real copper, and real copper has resistance and inductance.

That's why it matters. When current flows through a ground conductor, it creates a voltage drop, I times R at low frequency and L di/dt at high frequency. If two circuits share that conductor, one circuit's current moves the other circuit's zero. So a digital block switching a hundred milliamps in a couple of nanoseconds through ten nanohenries of ground trace lifts the local ground by half a volt, and a nearby sensor or ADC reads it as signal. The same thing at the level of loops: the return current's loop area sets how much it radiates and picks up.

The fix is mostly to give the return currents a low impedance, well-defined path: a solid ground plane directly under the signals, short connections to it with vias at every ground pin, and care about where large currents flow so they don't pass through sensitive references. That makes grounding the biggest factor in noise, signal integrity and EMI."
:::

::: technical
**Definition.** "Ground" on a PCB is a conductor network (traces, pours, planes, vias) that (1) defines 0 V for every circuit tied to it and (2) carries the return current for all signal and power currents.

**Non-ideal ground.** Any ground conductor has impedance $Z_g=R_g+j\omega L_g$. A current $I$ flowing in it creates $V_g = I Z_g$. For a trace 100 mm long, 0.25 mm wide, 35 µm thick:

- $R_g=\rho\ell/(wt)=1.68\times10^{-8}\times0.1/(0.25\times10^{-3}\times35\times10^{-6})=0.19$ Ω,
- $L_g\approx 141$ nH (about 1.4 nH/mm for an isolated trace),
- $|Z_g|=8.9$ Ω at 10 MHz and 89 Ω at 100 MHz.

**Common-impedance coupling.** Two loads A and B sharing $Z_g$ see $V_g = (I_A+I_B)Z_g$. A 10 nH shared segment with a 100 mA step in 2 ns gives $L\,di/dt=0.5$ V. A 16-bit ADC with a 3.3 V range has a 50 µV LSB, so 5 A through a shared 10 mΩ return (50 mV) is almost 1000 LSBs of error.

**Types of "ground".** Signal/analog ground (reference), digital ground, power ground (high-current returns), chassis/protective earth (safety, shielding, ESD) and isolated grounds (galvanically separated). They may be one node or tied at a deliberate point.

**Fix.** A solid plane: sheet resistance 0.48 mΩ/□ for 1 oz, and with a close reference the return loop inductance is about 0.3 nH/mm for a trace 0.2 mm above the plane, concentrated under the trace.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 1.4 nH/mm and 0.3 nH/mm figures are first-order estimates (Grover and IPC-2141-type approximations). Real inductance depends on geometry, nearby conductors and where the return actually flows.
:::

::: short
"Ground on a PCB is the reference node and the return path for every current. It's real copper with resistance and inductance, so return currents create voltage drops, I·R at low frequency and L·di/dt at high frequency. If circuits share a ground conductor they couple through it, which shows up as noise, offset errors, resets or emissions. A solid ground plane, short low-inductance connections, and keeping large currents away from sensitive references are how you control it."
:::

::: long
"Ground does two jobs. First it's the reference: every voltage in the circuit is measured against it. Second, and this is the one people forget, it's the return path. Every milliamp that leaves a supply has to come back, and it comes back through ground.

The idealization is that ground is a perfect zero-ohm node. In reality it's copper with resistance and inductance. A ground trace that's a hundred millimetres long and a quarter millimetre wide is about 0.2 ohms of resistance and something like 140 nanohenries of inductance. At 10 MHz that's about 9 ohms.

So when current flows through ground, there's a voltage drop. If two circuits share the same piece of ground, they share the drop. That's common-impedance coupling. If a digital block switches 100 milliamps in 2 nanoseconds through 10 nanohenries of shared ground, the local ground moves half a volt, and a neighbouring analog circuit sees that as signal. Likewise a motor drawing 5 amps through ten milliohms of shared return puts 50 millivolts on the reference of a 16-bit ADC, which is about a thousand LSBs.

There's also the loop area. At high frequency the return current flows right under the signal trace, so the loop area is set by how far the ground is from the trace, and that sets radiation and pickup.

So the practical approach is a solid ground plane next to the signal layers, vias at every ground pin, short returns for decoupling, and keeping high-current and fast-switching returns from running through sensitive circuits. Done well, grounding disappears; done badly, it's the cause of most of the noise on a board."
:::

::: terms
- **Ground (0 V reference)**: the node against which voltages are defined.
- **Return path**: the conductor path current takes back to its source.
- **Common-impedance coupling**: interference caused by two circuits sharing a conductor.
- **Ground bounce**: transient shift of local ground due to $L\,di/dt$.
- **Ground loop**: closed loop through grounds that picks up magnetic fields or carries noise currents.
- **Earth / chassis ground**: safety/shield connection to the enclosure or protective earth.
- **Ground plane**: continuous copper layer used as the return and reference.
- **Single-point (star) ground**: all returns meet at one node.
:::

::: exceptional
An exceptional answer states that ground is not a node but a *network of impedances*, and shows a number: "10 nH and 100 mA in 2 ns is half a volt." It distinguishes the low-frequency picture (resistance, where current takes the shortest-resistance path) from the high-frequency picture (inductance, where it follows the signal). It names the failure modes (common-impedance coupling, ground bounce, ground loops, large loop area) and the standard fixes. It also separates safety earth from signal ground.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Ground is 0 V everywhere."** On a real board different points differ by millivolts to volts under load or at speed.
- **"Any ground connection is a good ground."** A ground that goes through a long, thin trace is high impedance, especially at high frequency.
- **Thinking ground only matters for analog.** Digital ground bounce causes false triggers and EMI.
- **Treating the return current as taking the shortest route.** At high frequency it takes the lowest-inductance route, under the signal.
- **Mixing up earth, chassis and signal ground.** They have different roles and are connected deliberately, not automatically.
- **Using one thin trace to "ground everything".** Shared ground impedance couples every circuit to every other.
:::

## Ground is a loop, not a place

Take any circuit: a battery, a resistor load, a wire out and a wire back. The current in the "out" wire equals the current in the "back" wire. The schematic's ground symbol hides the "back" wire. On a PCB that back wire is copper, and its geometry matters.

## Low frequency: resistance

At DC and low frequency, current takes the minimum resistance path and spreads out. The sheet resistance of copper is $R_\square=\rho/t$: 0.48 mΩ/□ for 1 oz, 0.96 mΩ/□ for ½ oz and 0.24 mΩ/□ for 2 oz. A ground trace 1 mm wide and 50 mm long is 50 squares: 24 mΩ. Carrying 1 A, it drops 24 mV, enough to matter for a precision measurement. Rules: use wide copper or planes; route high-current returns separately from sensitive references; for a shunt measurement use a Kelvin (four-wire) connection so the sense lines don't carry the high current.

## High frequency: inductance

As frequency rises the inductive impedance $\omega L$ dominates. Above a few MHz, return current stops taking the minimum-resistance path and takes the minimum-*inductance* path, which is the one that encloses the smallest loop, directly under the forward trace. ## Ground bounce: the numbers

A digital output switching a load pulls current spikes through the ground connection. The voltage across the ground inductance is

$$V_{bounce}=L_g\frac{di}{dt}\times N$$

for $N$ outputs switching together. For $L_g=10$ nH, $\Delta I=100$ mA, $t_r=2$ ns: 0.5 V per output; eight outputs through the same pin could produce 4 V if no decoupling were present. Real bounce is limited by local decoupling and by the package's multiple ground pins, but the mechanism is clear.

Slow the edge to 3 ns or raise the number of ground pins to 4 and watch the bounce fall below the threshold; increase N or L and it grows.

## Ground loops and loop area

A **ground loop** forms where two ground connections create a closed conductor loop. A changing magnetic flux through the loop induces a voltage $V=-d\Phi/dt$ in it. For a 50 Hz magnetic field of 1 µT (an illustrative level near mains wiring) through a 100 cm² loop, the induced voltage is $\omega B A = 2\pi\times50\times10^{-6}\times10^{-2}=3.1$ µV. That's tiny for logic, but huge for a 20 µV sensor. At the board level the lesson is: avoid sending sensitive signals around big conductor loops, and keep the signal and its return close.

The loop area also determines emissions. For a small loop of area $A$ carrying current $I$ at frequency $f$, the far-field strength is proportional to $f^2 I A$. Halving the loop area halves the radiation; halving the frequency cuts it fourfold. See [[q0489]].

## Kinds of ground

| Name | Purpose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Signal / analog ground | Reference for small signals | Quietest; keep high currents out |
| Digital ground | Return for logic currents | Noisy; same plane usually fine |
| Power ground | High-current returns (motors, LEDs) | Route returns separately; join at a defined point |
| Chassis / earth | Safety, shielding, ESD discharge | Connected via a defined path, often through a capacitor or resistor/cap network; see [[q0493]] |
| Isolated ground | Galvanic isolation between domains | Isolators; no DC path |

## What a good grounding scheme achieves

1. **Low impedance** between any two ground points across the frequency range.
2. **Defined return paths** so the loop area is small.
3. **Separation of large and small currents** so return drops don't couple.
4. **Safety** and ESD discharge routes that don't go through sensitive circuits.

A multilayer board with a solid ground plane next to every signal layer gets items 1 and 2 nearly for free. Items 3 and 4 are decisions about placement and where connections are made. See [[q0485]] for layout techniques.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> The memorable sentence: "Ground isn't a node; it's a network of tiny impedances that every return current has to flow through." Follow it with one number (10 nH × 100 mA / 2 ns = 0.5 V) and you've shown you understand it quantitatively.
:::

::: applications
- **Mixed-signal data acquisition**: keeping digital return currents out of the ADC's reference region to preserve effective resolution.
- **Motor control boards**: separating the high-current power returns from the current-sense reference and the logic ground.
- **Switching regulators**: the input-capacitor to switch loop's ground connection decides conducted and radiated noise.
- **Microcontroller boards**: ground vias at every power pin so decoupling capacitors have a short return.
- **Audio and instrumentation**: avoiding ground loops that cause hum.
:::

::: assumptions
- A board with at least one ground plane (or a well-planned pour on a 2-layer board).
- The 141 nH, 0.3 nH/mm and sheet-resistance numbers are first-order estimates at room temperature with 1 oz copper.
- For very low-frequency or DC precision work the resistive picture dominates; for logic edges faster than a few ns the inductive picture does.
- "Ground" in a safety context (earth) has legal and standards requirements (for instance IEC 62368-1/UL rules) that this answer doesn't cover.
- Systems with isolated domains have multiple, deliberately separate grounds.
:::

::: followups
### Why is a ground plane better than a ground trace?
A plane has far lower resistance (0.48 mΩ/□) and, more importantly, far lower inductance for the return current, because the return can flow directly under each signal in a tight loop instead of sharing a long narrow conductor. It also avoids common-impedance sharing because current spreads over a large area. See [[q0488]].

### What is ground bounce?
A transient shift in a local ground voltage caused by $L\,di/dt$ when many outputs switch together through the same ground inductance (pin, bond wire, trace). A quiet input referenced to that ground sees it as a false signal. Mitigate with many ground pins, local decoupling, slower edges, and low-inductance connections.

### How do analog circuits get hurt by digital ground currents?
If digital return current flows under or through the analog region of the ground, it creates voltage differences across the plane that appear in series with analog signals and references. The fix is placement (keep digital return currents in their own area) and keeping a solid plane so currents follow their own signals' paths. See [[q0496]].

### What's the difference between ground and earth?
Ground is the circuit reference; earth is the connection to the physical earth/safety conductor. They may be connected, through a controlled path, but they aren't the same: the chassis may be tied to earth for safety while the signal ground floats or is tied at one point.

### Scenario: your microcontroller resets whenever a relay on the board turns on. How would you investigate grounding?
Check where the relay coil current returns: if it shares a ground segment with the MCU's reference or decoupling, the coil's switching (di/dt plus the flyback event) bounces the MCU's ground. Measure the MCU supply and ground with a short-lead probe at the pin during the event. Fix by giving the coil current its own return path to the supply, using a flyback diode close to the coil, adding local decoupling at the MCU, and keeping the MCU's returns separate from the relay's high-current path.
:::
