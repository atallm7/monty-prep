::: basics
### In plain words
How fast a PLL settles is set by its loop bandwidth. A wide bandwidth makes the PLL lock quickly and follow the reference closely. A narrow one makes it slow but calm. Both extremes cause problems, so the designer picks a compromise.

### Picture it
Think of steering a car. If you jerk the wheel at every bump, you correct instantly but also react to every little disturbance, and the car wobbles. If you steer very gently, the ride is smooth but you are slow to get back to the lane after a real change. A good driver is somewhere between. In a PLL the "bumps" are noise on the reference.

### Words you need
- **Loop bandwidth**: how fast the loop can respond to errors. See [[q0132]].
- **Lock time**: how long the loop takes to settle after start-up or a frequency change.
- **Jitter**: small random timing errors in the output clock.
- **Spur**: an unwanted tone in the output, often at the reference frequency.
- **Damping**: how quickly oscillation dies out. Too little means overshoot and ringing.

### The simplest example
Say the reference is 1 MHz. A rule of thumb keeps the loop bandwidth below about a tenth of that, so under 100 kHz. Settling takes roughly a few times $1/\text{bandwidth}$, so a wider loop settles in microseconds, while a loop ten times narrower takes about ten times longer. The wide loop passes more reference noise. The narrow loop lets more VCO drift and noise through. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds why each extreme hurts, and how to find the best middle.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can reason about a PLL's **loop bandwidth and damping as a design trade-off**, not as "faster is better". The interviewer wants to hear:

1. "Locks too fast" means a **wide loop bandwidth** (or low damping): more reference noise and spurs pass to the output, overshoot/ringing, and stability problems when the bandwidth approaches the PFD sampling rate.
2. "Locks too slow" means a **narrow bandwidth**: long lock time (startup, frequency changes), and poor suppression of VCO noise and drift, so more jitter and less ability to track input jitter.
3. The optimum: bandwidth where reference-related and VCO noise contributions cross; damping about 0.7 to 1.
4. What each does to **downstream circuits** that use the clock.
:::

::: why
Loop bandwidth is the most consequential PLL setting. Too wide and the output clock inherits reference jitter and spurs and may overshoot; too narrow and the system waits long to start, the clock wanders with temperature and supply noise, and a link may fail to track incoming jitter. In practice, clock-generator datasheets and SoC bring-up notes are full of loop-filter settings for exactly this reason. Being able to say which direction each symptom points (long wake-up time vs jittery output vs occasional loss of lock) is the debugging skill the question is probing.
:::

::: concepts
- **Loop bandwidth ($\omega_n$, crossover)**: how fast the loop corrects errors; sets lock time and the noise-filtering corner. See [[q0135]].
- **Damping ratio ($\zeta$)**: controls overshoot and ringing; $\zeta\approx0.7$ to 1 typical.
- **Lock time**: roughly $5/\omega_n$ for a $\zeta\approx0.7$ type-II loop (2% settling).
- **Noise transfer**: reference/PFD/CP noise is low-passed (with gain $N$); VCO noise is high-passed. See [[q0136]].
- **Sampled-loop stability**: the PFD samples at $f_{ref}$, so bandwidth must be $\ll f_{ref}$.
- **Jitter**: edge-timing variation; bandwidth sets which noise source dominates.
:::

::: answer
"It comes down to the loop bandwidth, and both extremes cause trouble.

If the PLL locks too fast, the loop bandwidth is wide. Then the loop tracks the reference very closely, which means it passes through the reference's noise and the phase detector and charge pump noise, multiplied by N, plus ripple at the reference frequency, which shows up as spurs. It can also overshoot and ring: when the frequency changes, the output may briefly go beyond the target. And if the bandwidth gets close to the reference frequency, the sampling delay of the phase detector eats the phase margin and the loop can become unstable, so you generally keep it below about a tenth of the reference.

If it locks too slow, the bandwidth is narrow. Lock time is long, so startup or frequency changes take longer, which matters for wake-up from sleep or dynamic frequency scaling. And the loop is slow to correct the VCO, so the VCO's own phase noise, drift from temperature and supply noise appear at the output, giving more jitter. It also can't follow input jitter or spread-spectrum modulation.

So the optimum is a compromise. I'd set the bandwidth around where the reference noise and the VCO noise contributions are equal, which minimizes total jitter, with a damping near 0.7, and keep it well under the reference frequency."
:::

::: technical
**Second-order model.** $\omega_n=\sqrt{\frac{I_{cp}K_{vco}}{2\pi NC_1}}$, $\zeta=\frac{RC_1\omega_n}{2}$; closed-loop −3 dB bandwidth $\approx2.05\omega_n$ for $\zeta=0.7$. Lock time (2% settling of a phase or frequency step) is about $4.9/\omega_n$ at $\zeta=0.7$, about $5.4/\omega_n$ at $\zeta=1$, and $13/\omega_n$ at $\zeta=0.3$.

**Overshoot.** Step response of the type-II loop (with the filter zero): 45% at $\zeta=0.3$, 30% at 0.5, 21% at 0.7, 13.5% at 1, 4.8% at 2.

**Too wide a loop bandwidth ($f_{BW}\to f_{ref}/10$ and beyond).**
- More **reference, PFD and charge-pump noise** reaches the output (in-band noise ∝ $N^2$).
- More **reference-spur** feedthrough at $\pm f_{ref}$.
- Less **phase margin**: the PFD/CP is a sampled system with an effective delay of about $T_{ref}/2$, giving extra phase lag of about $180°\cdot f_{BW}/f_{ref}$ at crossover, so a loop with 65° of margin on paper may have ~47° at $f_{BW}=f_{ref}/10$ and under 30° at $f_{ref}/5$ (approximation).
- **Overshoot** in the frequency response and possible cycle slips.

**Too narrow ($f_{BW}$ small).**
- **Lock time** scales as $1/f_{BW}$ (e.g. 155 µs at $f_n=5$ kHz vs. 15.5 µs at 50 kHz).
- **VCO noise** is not suppressed over a wider offset range; supply/temperature drift is corrected slowly.
- Poor tracking of **input jitter or modulation** (spread-spectrum, CDR).
- Larger loop-filter capacitors, more sensitivity to **leakage** (droop on the filter node).

**Noise optimum.** Integrated jitter² $\approx A\,f_{BW}+B/f_{BW}$ (reference-related ∝ bandwidth; VCO with $1/f^2$ phase noise ∝ $1/$bandwidth), minimal at $f_{BW}=\sqrt{B/A}$, where the two contributions are equal.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The $f_{ref}/10$ guideline and the $180°\,f_{BW}/f_{ref}$ delay approximation are rules of thumb; the exact stability limit depends on the loop filter, charge-pump architecture and extra poles. The noise-optimum model assumes white in-band noise and $1/f^2$ VCO noise.
:::

::: short
"It's the loop bandwidth trade-off. Too fast, meaning wide bandwidth, and the output inherits reference and charge-pump noise multiplied by N, reference spurs, and may overshoot or even go unstable as the bandwidth nears the reference frequency. Too slow and the lock time gets long, so startup and frequency changes lag, and the loop can't suppress the VCO's own noise and drift, so jitter rises, and it can't track input jitter. The sweet spot is where the two noise contributions are equal, with damping near 0.7, and bandwidth well under a tenth of the reference."
:::

::: long
"The question is really about loop bandwidth and damping, and the answer is that both extremes are bad for different reasons.

Start with too fast, a very wide loop bandwidth. The PLL tracks the reference tightly, which has three consequences. First, noise: in the band where the loop is working, the output follows the reference, so reference noise, plus phase-detector and charge-pump noise, passes straight through, multiplied by N. A wide loop opens that window wider. Second, the reference ripple, which sits at the reference frequency and is filtered by the loop filter, now leaks more strongly to the VCO control line, so you get reference spurs. And third, stability: the phase detector only updates once per reference cycle, which acts like a delay of about half a period. If the loop bandwidth gets within a factor of ten or so of the reference frequency, that delay eats the phase margin, the loop rings or overshoots, and eventually becomes unstable. So a fast loop might even overshoot its target frequency, which can be dangerous for logic that is running off that clock.

Now too slow, a narrow bandwidth. The loop takes a long time to lock; for a 5 kHz natural frequency, that's well over a hundred microseconds, versus about fifteen microseconds at 50 kHz. That matters at power-up, when waking from sleep, or when software changes the clock frequency. Beyond that, a narrow loop can't correct the VCO fast enough, so the VCO's own phase noise, temperature drift and supply-induced noise show up at the output, increasing jitter. And if the PLL is supposed to track something, like a spread-spectrum modulated reference or incoming data in a clock recovery circuit, a narrow loop will lag it.

So I'd pick the bandwidth near the crossing point of the reference-noise and VCO-noise contributions, which minimizes total jitter, choose damping around 0.7 to 1 to limit overshoot, and keep the bandwidth under about a tenth of the reference frequency. Then I'd check lock time against the system's requirements."
:::

::: terms
- **Loop bandwidth**: frequency range over which the loop corrects errors (≈ natural frequency × 2).
- **Natural frequency ($\omega_n$)**: second-order loop frequency parameter.
- **Damping ratio ($\zeta$)**: ratio setting overshoot; $\zeta<1$ underdamped.
- **Lock time**: time to settle within a tolerance (e.g. 2% or within a ppm band) after a change.
- **Overshoot / ringing**: output response exceeding the final value before settling.
- **Reference spur**: tone at offsets $\pm f_{ref}$ in the output spectrum.
- **Phase margin**: stability measure at the loop's crossover frequency.
- **Jitter transfer / tolerance**: how input jitter is passed or tracked by the loop.
- **Cycle slip**: temporary loss of lock where the phase error jumps by a full cycle.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "too fast: unstable or noisy; too slow: takes a long time to lock".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames both as **loop bandwidth/damping trade-offs** and gives **which noise goes where** (reference noise ×N through the low-pass; VCO noise through the high-pass).
- Gives **numbers**: lock time ≈ $5/\omega_n$ (15 µs at 50 kHz, 155 µs at 5 kHz), overshoot 21% at $\zeta=0.7$, bandwidth ≤ $f_{ref}/10$.
- Explains the **sampled-loop stability limit** (PFD delay of half a reference period).
- Finds the **optimum** where reference and VCO noise contributions are equal.
- Brings up **system consequences**: overshoot of frequency, startup/DVFS latency, failure to track spread-spectrum or CDR input jitter, and the reference spurs.
- Knows how to **measure** (scope $V_{ctrl}$ step response, phase-noise plot, lock-time capture).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Faster lock is always better."** Speed comes from wide bandwidth, which passes more reference noise and spurs and reduces stability margin.
- **Saying "slow lock = unstable".** A narrow loop is typically very stable; its issues are lock time and VCO noise.
- **Confusing lock time with acquisition range.** Capture range is how far off the VCO may start; lock time is how long convergence takes.
- **Ignoring damping.** A fast loop with $\zeta=0.3$ rings for many cycles even if it is "stable".
- **Forgetting the reference-frequency limit.** A loop bandwidth near $f_{ref}$ is not a continuous-time system any more; the PFD's sampling delay can destabilize it.
- **Treating jitter as only "VCO noise".** In-band reference/CP noise (×N) can dominate in a wide loop.
- **Using the clock before lock.** During acquisition the output frequency is wrong and may exceed the target.
:::

::: deep
## The one knob: bandwidth (plus damping)

A PLL's dynamics reduce to two numbers, the natural frequency $\omega_n$ and the damping $\zeta$ ([[q0135]]). The bandwidth roughly doubles $\omega_n$. Everything in this question follows from where you set them.

## Lock time

For a phase or frequency step, settling takes about $5/\omega_n$ at $\zeta=0.7$. With a 10 MHz reference:

| $f_n$ | $f_n/f_{ref}$ | 2% lock time | Comment |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 kHz | 1/2000 | ~155 µs | Very stable and clean, slow |
| 50 kHz | 1/200 | ~15.5 µs | Typical compromise |
| 500 kHz | 1/20 | ~1.55 µs | Closed-loop BW ≈ 1 MHz = $f_{ref}/10$: little margin left |

**Why overshoot matters.** For a frequency change from 800 MHz to 1000 MHz (a 200 MHz step), a $\zeta=0.7$ loop overshoots 21%, so the output frequency briefly rises to about 1042 MHz; a $\zeta=0.3$ loop reaches about 1090 MHz. During that time the clock is faster than the target: logic that was only timed for 1000 MHz can fail. Systems therefore either change frequency in steps, gate the clock, or hold the logic until the PLL reports lock (see [[q0139]]).

## What "too fast" costs

**1. Reference noise and spurs.** The closed-loop response from the reference to the output is low-pass with gain $N$ and corner at the loop bandwidth. A wider loop passes more of the reference/PFD/charge-pump noise, and charge-pump ripple at $f_{ref}$ is less attenuated relative to the loop bandwidth.

**2. Sampling delay and phase margin.** The PFD and charge pump deliver one correction per reference cycle, which behaves like a delay of $T_{ref}/2$. At a crossover frequency $f_c$ this adds phase lag of about $360°\cdot f_c\cdot T_{ref}/2=180°\,f_c/f_{ref}$.

## What "too slow" costs

**1. Lock time** (table above): a 155 µs lock is fine for a power-up clock but not for a processor that changes frequency every few tens of microseconds.

**2. VCO noise and drift.** Inside the loop bandwidth, feedback corrects the VCO. Outside, you get the free-running VCO's phase noise, a rising $1/f^2$ skirt toward the carrier. A narrow loop makes that skirt start closer to the carrier, so integrated jitter grows. Slow disturbances like temperature, supply and microphonic coupling are corrected slowly, so the output wanders.

**3. Tracking.** If the reference is modulated (spread-spectrum clocking, typically a modulation of tens of kHz) or the loop is a clock-data-recovery circuit tracking incoming jitter, a loop bandwidth below the modulation or jitter frequency can't follow, so the output either doesn't carry the modulation or the data are sampled off-center.

**4. Component and leakage sensitivity.** Narrow loops need large filter capacitors (area/pins) and are more sensitive to leakage current on the control node, which causes droop and spurs.

## The noise optimum

For white in-band noise and a $1/f^2$ VCO skirt, integrated jitter squared is roughly $A\,f_{BW}+B/f_{BW}$, minimized at $f_{BW}=\sqrt{B/A}$.

## Practical ways to get both

- **Switch loop bandwidth dynamically:** wide during acquisition (fast lock), narrow after lock detect (low jitter): many PLL chips do this by changing the charge-pump current or filter resistor.
- **Frequency pre-set / VCO band calibration:** coarse-tune the VCO digitally so the loop only has a small fine-tuning step.
- **Higher reference or PFD frequency:** lets the bandwidth go up without violating $f_{BW}<f_{ref}/10$.
- **Clean supply and layout** so a narrow loop doesn't pick up VCO disturbance.
:::

::: applications
- **Processor DVFS and sleep-wake**: lock time determines how quickly the clock can change, so many chips keep two PLLs or use a bypass mode.
- **Radio synthesizers**: wide loop for fast channel hopping (frequency agility) vs narrow loop for lowest phase noise; adaptive bandwidth switching is common.
- **Clock-data recovery**: loop bandwidth sets jitter tolerance and jitter transfer in serial links.
- **Jitter cleaners**: deliberately narrow-bandwidth PLLs that filter noisy reference jitter at the cost of long lock time.
- **Spread-spectrum clocks**: a PLL bandwidth wider than the modulation frequency passes the spreading.
- **Audio PLLs**: narrow loops to reject jitter on an incoming word clock.
:::

::: assumptions
I assumed a charge-pump PLL modelled as a continuous-time type-II second-order loop, valid when the bandwidth is much below the reference frequency. The overshoot numbers apply to a phase or frequency step in the reference with a proper loop filter (including the zero). Real PLLs have an additional pole ($C_2$), VCO gain that varies, and nonlinear acquisition behavior; the exact lock time also depends on how "locked" is defined (2%, ppm, phase error threshold). The **noise-optimum plot** is an illustrative model, not a measurement. **Jitter cleaners and CDRs** use the same trade-offs but with different aims (filtering input jitter vs. tracking it). **All-digital PLLs** have similar relationships with different noise sources (TDC quantization, DCO resolution).
:::

::: followups
### Your PLL takes 150 µs to lock, but the system needs 20 µs. What are your options?
Increase the loop bandwidth (e.g. 8× wider for 8× faster, subject to the $f_{ref}/10$ limit and noise), use dynamic bandwidth (wide until lock detect, narrow after), pre-load or calibrate the VCO band so the loop only has a small step to make, raise the PFD frequency, or use a second PLL already running at the next frequency and switch (clock mux). I'd check jitter and spurs after widening.

### How do you know the loop bandwidth is too wide in a measurement?
Look at the phase-noise or spectrum: raised close-in noise that matches the reference's noise ×N, a "bump" (peaking) near the loop bandwidth indicating low phase margin, and reference spurs at $\pm f_{ref}$. In the time domain, $V_{ctrl}$ rings after a step. A frequency counter or scope might also show the output overshooting during retunes.

### Why does a narrow loop not suppress VCO noise?
Inside the loop bandwidth the feedback measures phase error and corrects it, reducing the VCO's noise by the loop gain. Outside that band the loop gain is below 1 and cannot correct, so the VCO's own phase noise reaches the output. A narrow loop therefore leaves a wider band of unsuppressed VCO noise, and since the VCO skirt rises toward the carrier, that is the larger contribution.

### What's the difference between lock time and capture range?
Capture (pull-in) range is how far the starting frequency can be from the target and still acquire lock; lock time is how long convergence takes. A PFD-based loop with a wide-range VCO captures from anywhere in its tuning range, but may take a long time (cycle-slipping) if it starts far away, unless frequency pre-set or fast-acquisition modes are used.

### Harder: after a frequency switch your logic occasionally fails even though the PLL "locks" in 20 µs. Why might it fail only at that time?
The PLL's frequency overshoot or ringing during acquisition means the clock transiently runs faster than its final value, or that jitter is higher during the transient, so timing paths designed for the target frequency fail. Remedies: gate or hold the logic in reset until lock detect, change frequency in smaller steps, increase damping, or switch clocks through a glitch-free mux only after lock. See [[q0139]].
:::

::: interpretations
"Locks too fast or too slow" could mean (a) a loop with a wide or narrow *bandwidth* (what I answered), (b) a loop with insufficient *damping* (too fast, ringing) or too much damping (slow), or (c) literally the *lock time* during acquisition. They are the same knob. Unlike [[q0139]], which asks about squeezing the clock timing window and its effect on connected circuits, this question focuses on the PLL loop dynamics. Some interviewers might be asking about *clock-and-data recovery* loops: the same trade-offs apply, expressed as jitter tolerance and jitter transfer.
:::
