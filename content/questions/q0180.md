::: testing
Whether you can **calculate $D$ at the right level of accuracy for the situation**. The interviewer is looking for:

1. The ideal answer immediately: $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$ in CCM.
2. Awareness that real converters need $D$ a bit higher because of switch, inductor and diode drops, and that you can derive the corrected formula by averaging the switch node.
3. Other ways to get $D$: from a scope (on-time over period), from currents ($I_{in}/I_{out}$) or from ripple.
4. Knowing when the formula doesn't apply (DCM) and how to sanity-check the result against controller limits.
:::

::: why
Computing duty cycle is a daily task: sizing the inductor ripple, checking minimum on-time, interpreting a scope capture, and finding out why a converter runs hot. The ideal formula is one line, so interviewers push on the *corrections*. The ability to say "ideally $V_{out}/V_{in}$, but at 2 A with 25 mΩ of resistance it is about 0.28, not 0.275, and here's how I'd derive that" is a quick signal of real design experience. See [[q0182]] for the "given a ratio" version and [[q0183]] for the reverse direction.
:::

::: concepts
- **Ideal conversion ratio**: $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$ in CCM ([[q0178]]).
- **Average switch-node voltage**: the SW average minus the inductor DCR drop equals $V_{out}$.
- **Conduction drops**: $I R_{DS(on)}$ in each FET, $I\,R_{DCR}$ in the inductor, and $V_f$ for a diode.
- **Charge balance at the input**: $I_{in,avg}=D\,I_{out}$ in CCM, so $D=I_{in}/I_{out}$.
- **Ripple relation**: $\Delta I_L=V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf_{sw})$ lets you recover $D$ from a measured ripple.
- **Conduction mode**: in DCM the simple formulas fail ([[q0229]]).
:::

::: answer
"The quick answer is the ideal one: duty cycle equals output voltage divided by input voltage, so for 12 V to 3.3 V it's 3.3 over 12, which is 27.5 percent. That comes from the inductor having zero average voltage in steady state.

In a real converter I'd correct for the drops, because the controller has to make up the voltage lost in the switches and the inductor. If I average the switch node and subtract the inductor's DCR drop, then for a synchronous buck I get $D=\dfrac{V_{out}+I(R_{ls}+R_{DCR})}{V_{in}-I(R_{hs}-R_{ls})}$. With 20 mΩ high-side, 10 mΩ low-side, 15 mΩ DCR at 2 amps that's about 28 percent instead of 27.5. For a diode buck I'd put the diode drop in, $D=\dfrac{V_{out}+V_f+IR_{DCR}}{V_{in}-IR_{hs}+V_f}$, and with a 0.5 V Schottky that's about 30.7 percent, a much bigger correction.

If I have hardware, I can also get it by measurement: on the scope it's the on-time over the period at the switch node. Or from currents: the average input current is $D$ times the output current, so $D=I_{in}/I_{out}$. And I'd check the result against the controller's limits: at 1 MHz, 27.5 percent is a 275 ns on-time, which has to be above the minimum on-time. All of this assumes continuous conduction; at light load in DCM the formula changes."
:::

::: technical
**Ideal (CCM).** $D=\dfrac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}$, $t_{on}=D/f_{sw}$.

**Synchronous buck with drops.** Average switch-node voltage over a period, with $R_{hs}$, $R_{ls}$ the FET on-resistances and the inductor current $\approx I_{out}$:
$$\overline{v_{SW}}=D(V_{in}-IR_{hs})+(1-D)(-IR_{ls}),\qquad V_{out}=\overline{v_{SW}}-IR_{DCR}$$
$$\Rightarrow\ D=\frac{V_{out}+I\,(R_{ls}+R_{DCR})}{V_{in}-I\,(R_{hs}-R_{ls})}$$

**Asynchronous (diode) buck:**
$$D=\frac{V_{out}+V_f+I\,R_{DCR}}{V_{in}-I\,R_{hs}+V_f}$$

**From measurement.**
- Scope: $D=t_{on}/T$ at the SW node (50% crossings).
- Currents: $D=I_{in,avg}/I_{out}$ in CCM (includes any controller quiescent current as a small error).
- Ripple: $D=1-\dfrac{\Delta I_L\,L\,f_{sw}}{V_{out}}$.
- Analog modulator: $D=V_c/V_{ramp}$.

**Numbers** ($V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I=2$ A, $R_{hs}=20$ mΩ, $R_{ls}=10$ mΩ, $R_{DCR}=15$ mΩ, $V_f=0.5$ V):

| Case | $D$ |
|---|---|
| Ideal | 0.2750 |
| Synchronous with drops | 0.2796 |
| Diode (asynchronous) with drops | 0.3074 |

**DCM**: use the DCM relation, $D=M\sqrt{K/(1-M)}$, $M=V_{out}/V_{in}$, $K=2L/(RT)$ ([[q0182]]).
:::

I can also read it from the scope as on-time over period, or get it from $I_{in}/I_{out}$. All of that assumes continuous conduction."

::: long
"Let me start with the ideal case and then correct it. In continuous conduction the inductor's average voltage is zero, so the output equals the average of the switch node, $D$ times $V_{in}$. Therefore $D = V_{out}/V_{in}$. For 12 V to 3.3 V that's 0.275.

Real converters lose some voltage along the way, so the controller needs a slightly bigger $D$. I derive the correction by averaging the switch node. While the high-side is on, SW is $V_{in}$ minus the current times its on-resistance. While the low-side is on, SW is minus the current times its resistance, or minus the diode drop if it's an async buck. Then the output is that average minus the drop in the inductor's DC resistance. Solving for $D$ gives $D = \frac{V_{out}+I(R_{ls}+R_{DCR})}{V_{in}-I(R_{hs}-R_{ls})}$ for the synchronous case. With 20, 10 and 15 milliohms at 2 amps, I get 0.2796. For a diode with 0.5 volts forward drop, the numerator picks up the diode drop and I get 0.307, so 3 points higher than ideal. That's an easy way to see why the synchronous version helps.

Beyond calculation, I can also measure duty cycle. On a scope I read the on-time and period of the switch node. With a DMM I can filter SW and read $D\,V_{in}$. Or from the currents: the input current averages $D$ times the output current in CCM, so $D=I_{in}/I_{out}$. Ripple works too: $D=1-\Delta I_L L f_{sw}/V_{out}$.

Finally I sanity-check the result against the design range. If $V_{in}$ spans 9 to 15 V for a 3.3 V output, $D$ runs from 22 to 37 percent, and at 1 MHz that's 220 to 367 nanoseconds of on-time. I check it against the controller's minimum on-time and maximum duty. And all of this assumes CCM. At light load the converter goes into DCM or pulse skipping, where $D$ is much smaller than $V_{out}/V_{in}$."
:::

::: terms
- **Conversion ratio ($M$)**: $V_{out}/V_{in}$.
- **Ideal duty cycle**: $D=M$ in CCM with no losses.
- **Conduction drop**: voltage lost across $R_{DS(on)}$, DCR or a diode while current flows.
- **Average switch-node voltage ($\overline{v_{SW}}$)**: the DC component of SW; it feeds the LC filter.
- **DCR**: the DC resistance of the inductor winding.
- **Minimum on-time ($t_{on,min}$)**: the shortest high-side pulse the controller supports; sets $D_{min}=t_{on,min}f_{sw}$.
- **Maximum duty cycle ($D_{max}$)**: the longest allowed on-time fraction, limiting dropout operation.
- **Ripple ($\Delta I_L$)**: peak-to-peak inductor current variation.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the ideal formula *and its justification* (zero average inductor voltage), then immediately says real converters need more.
- Derives the loss-corrected formula by averaging SW, and quotes numbers: 0.275 ideal, 0.280 sync, 0.307 async at 2 A.
- Offers measurement methods (scope on-time/period, $I_{in}/I_{out}$, ripple) as cross-checks.
- Checks the answer against $t_{on,min}$, $D_{max}$ and the input range, not just the one nominal point.
- Notes DCM: $D$ is not $V_{out}/V_{in}$ at light load.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Inverting the ratio** and getting $V_{in}/V_{out}$ (that is the boost relation $1/(1-D)$, not buck).
- **Stopping at the ideal formula** when the question has load current, resistances or a diode in it.
- **Using the diode drop $V_f$ in the wrong place.** For the async buck, $V_f$ appears in the off-state: it adds to the numerator *and* to the denominator, as in the formula above.
- **Subtracting $V_f$ in the sync case** where it doesn't apply (the low-side FET is a resistance, except briefly in dead time).
- **Applying the CCM formula at light load.** At low current the converter is in DCM and $D$ will be much smaller than $V_{out}/V_{in}$ ([[q0229]]).
- **Mixing percent and fraction** in later formulas. Convert to a fraction (0.275) before using it in $t_{on}=D/f_{sw}$.
:::

::: deep
## Method 1: the ideal ratio

In CCM, inductor volt-second balance gives $V_{out}=D\,V_{in}$ ([[q0174]]). So

$$D=\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}}$$

For $V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V: $D=0.275$. Switching at 500 kHz: $t_{on}=0.275\times2\text{ µs}=550$ ns.

It is the right answer for a quick estimate, for sizing the ripple current and for sanity checks. It is the wrong answer when the conduction drops are comparable to $V_{out}$, which happens at low output voltage and high current.

## Method 2: including the drops

To get a more accurate $D$, average the switch-node voltage over one period.

**Synchronous buck.** High-side on for $DT$: SW $=V_{in}-IR_{hs}$. Low-side on for $(1-D)T$: SW $=-IR_{ls}$ (current flows from ground into SW). Ignoring dead time:

$$\overline{v_{SW}}=D(V_{in}-IR_{hs})-(1-D)IR_{ls}$$

The inductor has zero average voltage, so $V_{out}=\overline{v_{SW}}-IR_{DCR}$:

$$V_{out}=DV_{in}-I\left[D\,R_{hs}+(1-D)R_{ls}+R_{DCR}\right]$$

Solving for $D$:

$$\boxed{D=\frac{V_{out}+I\,(R_{ls}+R_{DCR})}{V_{in}-I\,(R_{hs}-R_{ls})}}$$

**Asynchronous buck (diode).** In the off-state SW sits at $-V_f$ (take $V_f$ as approximately constant):

$$\overline{v_{SW}}=D(V_{in}-IR_{hs})-(1-D)V_f,\qquad \boxed{D=\frac{V_{out}+V_f+I\,R_{DCR}}{V_{in}-I\,R_{hs}+V_f}}$$

### Worked example

$V_{in}=12$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $I=2$ A, $R_{hs}=20$ mΩ, $R_{ls}=10$ mΩ, $R_{DCR}=15$ mΩ, $V_f=0.5$ V.

- **Synchronous:** numerator $=3.3+2(0.010+0.015)=3.35$; denominator $=12-2(0.020-0.010)=11.98$; $D=0.2796$.
- **Asynchronous:** numerator $=3.3+0.5+2(0.015)=3.83$; denominator $=12-2(0.020)+0.5=12.46$; $D=0.3074$.

In terms of on-time at 500 kHz: 550 ns ideal, 559 ns synchronous, 615 ns asynchronous. The diode version asks for 12% more on-time than the ideal, an indicator of where the extra loss comes from: the diode burns $V_f\,I(1-D)=0.5\times2\times0.69=0.69$ W.

> These curves assume constant $R_{DS(on)}$ and a constant 0.5 V diode drop. Real FET resistance rises with temperature (by tens of percent between 25 °C and 100 °C), and diode $V_f$ varies with current and temperature. ## Method 3: measure it

**Scope on the switch node.** Probe SW with a short ground spring. Measure the positive pulse width at 50% of the swing (use the scope's "+Width" and "Period"). Example: $t_{on}=560$ ns at 500 kHz means $D=0.28$, which agrees with the synchronous calculation: healthy.

**From the currents.** In CCM, the input current is the inductor current while the high-side is on. Because the on-state ramp is centred on the average inductor current, $\overline{i_{in}}=D\,I_{out}$ regardless of losses. So

$$D=\frac{I_{in,avg}}{I_{out}}$$

For 2 A out and 0.559 A in, $D=0.28$. The controller's quiescent current adds to $I_{in}$ and makes $D$ look slightly high, so use this at moderate or high load.

**From the ripple.** Since $\Delta I_L=V_{out}(1-D)/(L f_{sw})$,
$$D=1-\frac{\Delta I_L\,L\,f_{sw}}{V_{out}}$$
A measured 1.02 A ripple with 4.7 µH at 500 kHz gives $D=1-1.02\times2.35/3.3=0.274$ (the inductance tolerance, often ±20%, limits the accuracy).

**From a DMM.** Low-pass SW with a simple RC filter and read $\overline{v_{SW}}=D\,V_{in}$.

**From the modulator.** Analog: $D=V_c/V_{ramp}$. Digital: $D=C/N$.

## Method 4: check against the design range

A single operating point is not enough. Plot $D$ over the whole input range:

Figure: $D$ is inversely proportional to $V_{in}$. Over a 9 to 15 V input the duty swings from 36.7% to 22%. Check the *smallest* $D$ (highest $V_{in}$) against minimum on-time: at 1 MHz it is 220 ns. Check the *largest* $D$ (lowest $V_{in}$) against the maximum duty and dropout.

At the extremes: if $t_{on,min}=100$ ns at 500 kHz then $D_{min}=5\%$ and the lowest reachable output at $V_{in}=24$ V is 1.2 V. If your design needs 1 V from 24 V, you need a lower frequency or a controller with shorter minimum on-time ([[q0181]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Minimum on-time figures depend on the controller and the layout; datasheet values (often tens to hundreds of ns) are vendor-specific, and some are specified only at a given $V_{in}$ and temperature.

## Method 5: DCM

If the load is light enough that $I_{out}<\Delta I_L/2$, the inductor current reaches zero each cycle and none of the CCM formulas hold. In DCM the output depends on the load as well, and the duty cycle needed for a given conversion ratio $M$ is

$$D=M\sqrt{\frac{K}{1-M}},\qquad K=\frac{2L}{R\,T}$$

(derived and checked in [[q0182]]). It's *smaller* than $M$ whenever $K<1-M$, which is the DCM condition. Practical converters in DCM, PFM or pulse-skipping modes don't hold a fixed $D$ at all.

::: applications
- **Estimating ripple and inductor size** at the extremes of the input range ([[q0212]]).
- **Checking controller limits**: minimum on-time at maximum $V_{in}$, maximum duty at minimum $V_{in}$.
- **Debugging a hot or low-voltage converter**: comparing the measured $D$ with the expected value reveals extra losses (higher $D$ than calculated) or dead-time problems.
- **Digital power control**: initializing the duty-cycle register with a feed-forward estimate $V_{out}/V_{in}$ so the loop only corrects the residual.
- **Soft-start design**: ramping $D$ from 0 to its final value over a defined time.
- **Efficiency estimates**: the difference between $D$ and $V_{out}/V_{in}$ tells you roughly how much voltage the conduction paths consume.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM**. The formulas are not valid in DCM or pulse-skipping modes.
- **Constant-resistance drops** ($R_{DS(on)}$, DCR) and an average inductor current equal to $I_{out}$. Real values vary with temperature and current ripple.
- **Dead time and edge times neglected.** They contribute about 1% (two 10 ns gaps at 500 kHz) and shift the numbers slightly.
- **Constant diode $V_f$**. In practice it varies with current.
- **Steady state**. During transients $D$ moves away from these values.
- **Buck topology**: boost and buck-boost use different relations ($1/(1-D)$ for boost).
:::

::: followups
### What duty cycle do you need for 5 V from 24 V (ideal)? And with a 0.5 V Schottky?
Ideal: $5/24=0.208$ (20.8%). With a 0.5 V diode and negligible other drops: $D=(5+0.5)/(24+0.5)=0.2245$ (22.5%). The diode adds 1.6 points. At a few amps the DCR and FET drops add a bit more.

### Your scope shows SW with $t_{on}=700$ ns at 500 kHz for a 12 V to 3.3 V converter. Is that normal?
$D=0.7/2=0.35$, versus about 0.28 expected. That's 25% more duty than needed, suggesting a lot of extra drops (heavy load, a saturating inductor, a high-resistance FET) or a loop that hasn't settled (startup, load step, or input drop). Check $V_{in}$ at the converter's pins, load current, and whether $V_{out}$ is actually 3.3 V.

### Why is the duty cycle with a diode higher than with a synchronous FET?
In the off-state the diode drops about 0.3 to 0.7 V while the FET drops $I\times$ a few tens of mΩ. The controller has to make up that voltage by making the on-state longer. At 3.3 V out, a 0.5 V diode is a 15% hit on the output voltage, which is why synchronous rectification dominates at low voltage ([[q0203]]).

### Can you get the duty cycle from the input and output current alone?
Yes, in CCM $D=I_{in}/I_{out}$ (the average input current is $D\,I_{out}$). That works with any losses but is distorted by quiescent current at light load. Combined with the power relation $\eta=V_{out}I_{out}/(V_{in}I_{in})$ you get $\eta=V_{out}/(V_{in}D)$, which is a neat way to estimate efficiency: for $D=0.28$, $\eta=3.3/(12\times0.28)=98\%$ (an upper bound, since it only includes conduction-type drops and neglects switching and gate-drive losses).

### Scenario: a buck is specced for $V_{in}$ = 4.5 to 36 V and $V_{out}$ = 3.3 V at 1 MHz. What do you check about the duty cycle?
$D$ ranges from $3.3/4.5=73\%$ down to $3.3/36=9.2\%$. At 1 MHz, the minimum on-time is 92 ns, so the controller's $t_{on,min}$ must be below that, or at 36 V the converter will skip pulses and the output ripple will go up. At the other end, 73% is fine if $D_{max}$ is above that. If not, reduce $f_{sw}$ (which lengthens $t_{on}$ for the same $D$) or choose a different controller.
:::

::: rubric
- Gives the ideal $D=V_{out}/V_{in}$ and a numeric example :: The baseline every interviewer expects.
- Justifies it through volt-second balance or the average switch node :: Shows understanding, not memorization.
- Corrects for real drops (FET resistance, DCR, diode $V_f$), ideally with the formula :: This is what the interview is probing at intermediate level.
- Shows the diode vs. synchronous difference in $D$ :: A concrete, memorable consequence.
- Offers measurement methods (scope $t_{on}/T$, $I_{in}/I_{out}$, ripple) :: Shows you can verify a calculation on the bench.
