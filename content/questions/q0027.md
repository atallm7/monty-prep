::: basics
### In plain words
The continuity condition says that an energy-storing part cannot change its state instantly. For an inductor the state is its current. For a capacitor it is its voltage. So an inductor resists sudden changes in current. If you try to force a sudden change, the inductor produces a large voltage to oppose it.

### Picture it
Think of a heavy freight train. Its speed is like the inductor's current. You cannot stop it instantly. If you somehow tried, the force would have to be enormous. In a circuit, the "force" is voltage, and so trying to stop the current suddenly makes a huge voltage spike. A capacitor works the same way, with voltage playing the role of speed. The analogy hides the energy: both store energy that must go somewhere.

### Words you need
- **Continuity**: a stored quantity, like inductor current, cannot jump.
- **Inductor**: a coil that stores energy in a magnetic field. $v = L\,di/dt$.
- **Inductive kick**: the voltage spike when current through an inductor is suddenly interrupted.
- **Flyback diode**: a diode across a coil that gives the current a safe path.
- **Lenz's law**: the induced voltage always opposes the change in current. See [[q0041]].

### The simplest example
A relay coil of 10 mH carries 120 mA, and a transistor turns off in 1 µs. The coil tries to keep its current going. The voltage is $v = L\,di/dt = 0.01 \times 0.12 / 10^{-6} = 1200$ V, about a kilovolt. That would destroy the transistor. A flyback diode across the coil lets the current circulate harmlessly. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers why current can't jump from both the equation and energy views, and the capacitor version of the same rule.
:::

::: testing
This question checks whether you understand **why** energy-storage elements behave the way they do during switching:

1. The continuity condition itself: **capacitor voltage** and **inductor current** cannot change instantaneously; $v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)$ and $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$.
2. The answer to the second part: inductors resist changes in **current** (not voltage).
3. The reasoning: $v = L\,di/dt$ would need infinite voltage for an instant current jump, and stored energy $\tfrac12Li^2$ can't change in zero time.
4. Practical consequence: when you interrupt an inductor's current, the voltage spikes (inductive kick), which is why flyback diodes exist. The question sits in the *capacitor* section, so a strong answer also gives the capacitor half.
:::

::: why
Continuity is the tool that turns switching-circuit problems from guesswork into a recipe: find the inductor currents and capacitor voltages just before the switch moves, carry them across the switching instant, and solve from there. In hardware it explains some of the most destructive events on a board: relay and solenoid coils killing the transistor that drives them, motor drivers avalanching their MOSFETs, and ringing on switch nodes. Every buck or boost converter relies on inductor current continuity to work at all. Interviewers ask it because it separates "memorized formulas" from real intuition about energy.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor law** $v = L\,di/dt$: voltage is proportional to the rate of change of current. See [[q0041]].
- **Capacitor law** $i = C\,dv/dt$: the dual relationship. See [[q0025]].
- **Stored energy**: $\tfrac12Li^2$ in an inductor's magnetic field, $\tfrac12Cv^2$ in a capacitor's electric field.
- **Finite power**: energy can't change in zero time without infinite power, so the state variables must be continuous.
- **Initial and final conditions** for first-order transients ([[q0028]], [[q0044]]).
- **Lenz's law**: an induced EMF opposes the change in current that produced it.

::: answer
"The continuity condition says the state variable of an energy-storage element can't change instantaneously. For an inductor that's the current: $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$. For a capacitor it's the voltage: $v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)$.

So, to the second part: inductors resist changes in current.

You can see it two ways. From the equation $v = L\,di/dt$: if the current jumped instantly, $di/dt$ would be infinite, which would need an infinite voltage. And from energy: an inductor stores $\tfrac12Li^2$ in its magnetic field, and you can't dump or create that energy in zero time without infinite power. Physically it's Lenz's law: when you try to change the current, the changing magnetic field induces a voltage that opposes the change.

This has a very practical consequence. If you have current flowing through a relay coil and you suddenly open the transistor driving it, the inductor tries to keep that current flowing, and the voltage shoots up until something conducts. With 10 mH carrying 120 mA and a switch opening in a microsecond, that's on the order of a kilovolt. That's why we put a flyback diode across the coil, so the current has somewhere to go. The capacitor version is the dual: capacitors resist changes in voltage, which is why they smooth supply rails."

::: technical
**Statement.** For finite voltages and currents (no impulses):
$$i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-),\qquad v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)$$
Other quantities (inductor voltage, capacitor current, resistor voltages/currents) **can** jump.

**Inductor: why current is continuous.**
- Integral form: $i_L(t) = i_L(0^-) + \frac1L\int_{0^-}^{t} v_L\,d\tau$. For bounded $v_L$, the integral over a vanishing interval is zero, so $i_L$ can't jump.
- Energy: $W_L = \tfrac12Li^2$; $p = dW/dt$ is finite, so $W$ and hence $i$ are continuous.
- Lenz: $v_L = L\,di/dt$ opposes the change.

**Inductors resist changes in current.** They impose $|v_L| = L|di/dt|$ in response to any attempt to change $i_L$. At DC steady state $di/dt = 0$ so $v_L = 0$ (short circuit). At the switching instant an inductor behaves like a **current source** equal to $i_L(0^-)$ (an open circuit if it was carrying zero current).

**Capacitor (dual).** $v_C = v_C(0^-) + \frac1C\int i\,d\tau$; capacitors resist changes in voltage and act as a voltage source $v_C(0^-)$ at the switching instant (a short if uncharged). See [[q0037]].

**Inductive kick example.** Coil: $L = 10$ mH, $R_{coil} = 100$ Ω on 12 V, so $I_0 = 120$ mA, $\tau = L/R = 100$ µs, $W = \tfrac12LI^2 = 72$ µJ. Interrupt in 1 µs: $v \approx L\,\Delta i/\Delta t = 10\text{m}\times0.12/1\,\mu = 1.2$ kV (in reality limited by switch breakdown/avalanche or parasitic capacitance). With a flyback diode, $i_L$ continues through the diode: coil terminal clamped near −0.7 V, current decays with $\tau \approx L/R_{coil}$.

**When continuity can "break".** Only with idealized impulses: an ideal voltage source switched directly across a capacitor, or an ideal current source forced through an inductor's open circuit, or two capacitors at different voltages connected directly (charge redistributes, energy is lost in the unavoidable resistance/radiation). Real circuits always have parasitic R and L that keep the physics finite.
:::

::: long
"The continuity condition is a rule about energy-storage elements: the variable that holds their energy can't change instantaneously. For an inductor that's current, for a capacitor it's voltage. Written out, $i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)$ and $v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)$, where $0^-$ is just before a switching event and $0^+$ is just after.

So inductors resist change in current. There are three ways I'd justify that. Mathematically, $v = L\,di/dt$; an instantaneous jump in current means infinite $di/dt$, which needs infinite voltage, and that doesn't exist. From energy, the inductor holds $\tfrac12Li^2$ in its magnetic field, and changing that energy in zero time would need infinite power. And physically it's Lenz's law: changing the current changes the magnetic flux, which induces a voltage opposing the change.

The way I use this is as a recipe for switching problems. I find the inductor currents and capacitor voltages just before the switch moves, usually from DC steady state where inductors are shorts and capacitors are opens. Then I carry those values across the switching instant. Right after switching, an inductor acts like a current source at its old current, and a capacitor acts like a voltage source at its old voltage. Everything else, like inductor voltage and capacitor current, is allowed to jump.

The practical example is inductive kick. Say a transistor drives a 10 mH relay coil at 120 mA. If the transistor turns off in about a microsecond, $L\,di/dt$ is around 1.2 kV. The coil will make whatever voltage it needs to keep the current flowing, so it'll avalanche the transistor or arc across a mechanical switch. That's why a flyback diode goes across the coil: the current keeps circulating through the diode and decays over roughly $L/R$, and the voltage is clamped to a diode drop.

The same principle is what makes a buck converter work. The inductor current can't stop when the high-side switch opens, so it forces the low-side diode or FET to conduct. And the capacitor version is why decoupling capacitors hold a rail steady during fast load steps."
:::

::: terms
- **Continuity condition**: inductor current and capacitor voltage are continuous in time for finite circuit quantities.
- **$0^-$ / $0^+$**: the instants immediately before and after a switching event.
- **State variable**: a quantity that captures an element's stored energy and "memory" ($i_L$, $v_C$).
- **Lenz's law**: an induced EMF opposes the change in magnetic flux that causes it.
- **Inductive kick (flyback)**: the large voltage spike produced when an inductor's current is interrupted.
- **Flyback / freewheeling diode**: a diode that gives inductor current a path when the driving switch opens.
- **Time constant ($\tau = L/R$)**: how fast an RL circuit's current approaches its final value.

::: mistakes
- **"Inductors resist current."** No: they resist *changes* in current. At DC steady state an ideal inductor is a short and passes current freely.
- **Saying inductor voltage is continuous.** It's the current that's continuous. Inductor voltage can jump instantly (that's the kick).
- **Swapping the elements.** Capacitor → voltage continuous; inductor → current continuous. Remember: each element's continuous quantity is the one in its energy formula.
- **Thinking opening a switch stops an inductor's current immediately.** The current finds a path: through an arc, parasitic capacitance, avalanche breakdown or a flyback diode.
- **Putting a flyback diode the wrong way round.** It must be reverse-biased during normal operation (cathode to the positive supply side), otherwise it shorts the supply through the switch.
:::

::: deep
## The core idea: energy can't teleport

Both reactive elements store energy:

$$W_L = \tfrac12Li_L^2,\qquad W_C = \tfrac12Cv_C^2$$

Power is the rate of change of energy, $p = dW/dt$. In any real circuit, voltages and currents are finite, so power is finite. A finite power can't change a stored energy by a finite amount in zero time. So $W_L$ and $W_C$ must be continuous functions of time, and since energy depends only on $i_L$ (or $v_C$), those quantities must be continuous too.

That's the continuity condition:

$$\boxed{i_L(0^+) = i_L(0^-)\qquad v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)}$$

## Inductors resist changes in current

### From the element equation

$$v_L = L\frac{di_L}{dt}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i_L(t) = i_L(0^-) + \frac1L\int_{0^-}^{t} v_L(\tau)\,d\tau$$

Over an infinitesimally short interval $0^-\to0^+$, the integral of a finite voltage is zero. So the current at $0^+$ equals the current at $0^-$. The only way to make $i_L$ jump is to apply an infinite voltage (an impulse), which real circuits can't supply.

### Physical picture: Lenz's law

Current through a coil creates magnetic flux $\Phi = Li$. Any change in that flux induces an EMF that opposes the change. Try to increase the current and the inductor develops a voltage that pushes back; try to decrease it and the inductor develops a voltage that tries to keep current flowing. The faster you try to change it, the bigger the opposing voltage. An inductor behaves like flywheel inertia for current: easy to keep spinning at a constant rate, hard to start or stop suddenly.

### What *can* change instantly

Only $i_L$ (and $v_C$) are protected. The inductor's *voltage* is free to jump: it will become whatever value is needed to keep its current continuous. Similarly, a capacitor's current can jump.

## The recipe for switching problems

1. **Before the switch ($t = 0^-$)**: assume DC steady state. Inductors are short circuits, capacitors are open circuits ([[q0028]], [[q0044]]). Solve for $i_L(0^-)$ and $v_C(0^-)$.
2. **At the switching instant ($t = 0^+$)**: replace each inductor with a current source of value $i_L(0^-)$ and each capacitor with a voltage source of value $v_C(0^-)$. Solve the resulting resistive circuit for everything else.
3. **Final state ($t\to\infty$)**: DC steady state again.
4. **In between**: first-order response $x(t) = x(\infty) + [x(0^+) - x(\infty)]e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = L/R_{th}$ or $R_{th}C$.

**Numbers.** $L = 10$ mH, $R_{coil} = 100$ Ω, $V_s = 12$ V:
- Steady-state current: $I_0 = 12/100 = 120$ mA; stored energy $\tfrac12(0.01)(0.12)^2 = 72$ µJ.
- **Without the diode**, opening the switch in ~1 µs demands $v_L \approx L\,\Delta i/\Delta t = 0.01\times0.12/10^{-6} = 1200$ V. > [!WARNING]
> A plain flyback diode makes relay release slower, because the current decays gently instead of being snapped off. When fast release matters, designers add a Zener or TVS in series with the diode, or an RC snubber, so the clamp voltage is higher (faster $di/dt = -V_{clamp}/L$) while staying within the switch's rating.

## The same principle runs every switching converter

In a buck converter the inductor current can't stop when the high-side switch turns off. Continuity forces the switch node to fly negative until the low-side diode or synchronous FET conducts, and the current keeps flowing to the load. Inductor current ramps up during the on-time ($di/dt = (V_{in} - V_{out})/L$) and down during the off-time ($-V_{out}/L$), but it's always continuous. That triangular ripple is continuity in action.

## The capacitor half, since this is the capacitor section

Everything above has a dual. A capacitor stores $\tfrac12Cv^2$ and obeys $i = C\,dv/dt$, so its **voltage** is continuous, and it **resists changes in voltage**. At the switching instant it acts like a voltage source equal to $v_C(0^-)$, a short if it was discharged. That's why an uncharged bulk capacitor draws a huge inrush when you plug in a board, and why decoupling capacitors hold rails steady during load steps. The capacitor version gets its own page in [[q0037]].

| | Inductor | Capacitor |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous quantity | Current $i_L$ | Voltage $v_C$ |
| Resists changes in | Current | Voltage |
| Can jump | Voltage $v_L$ | Current $i_C$ |
| At the switching instant acts like | Current source $i_L(0^-)$ | Voltage source $v_C(0^-)$ |
| At DC steady state | Short circuit | Open circuit |
| Danger when violated | Voltage spike (inductive kick) | Current spike (inrush) |

## When the ideal rule seems to fail

- **Two ideal capacitors at different voltages connected together**: the voltages must equalize instantly, which seems to violate continuity. In reality, wiring resistance and inductance limit the current and the "missing" energy is dissipated or radiated.
- **Opening a switch in series with an ideal inductor**: the ideal model gives infinite voltage. Real circuits have parasitic capacitance across the switch and coil, so the current briefly flows into that capacitance and rings. That's the ringing you see on relay drivers without proper clamping.
- **Saturating cores**: if the core saturates, $L$ drops sharply and current can rise much faster, but it is still continuous ([[q0046]]).
:::

::: applications
- **Relay, solenoid and motor drivers**: flyback diodes, TVS clamps or Zener+diode clamps give inductor current a path when the driver turns off.
- **Buck and boost converters**: inductor current continuity forces the freewheeling diode or synchronous FET to conduct; current-mode control senses that continuous current.
- **Ignition coils and boost converters**: deliberately exploit the kick, interrupting coil current to generate tens of kV for spark plugs or a stepped-up output voltage.
- **MOSFET avalanche ratings**: unclamped inductive switching (UIS) tests measure how much $\tfrac12LI^2$ energy a FET can absorb when it alone stops an inductor's current.
- **Transient circuit analysis**: computing initial conditions in RL/RC/RLC problems ([[q0009]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Finite voltages and currents**: continuity holds whenever no impulses are present, which is true in all physical circuits.
- **Linear, constant L**: with core saturation $L$ changes, but flux linkage (and current) are still continuous.
- **Lumped elements**: parasitic capacitance and resistance are ignored in the ideal statement but are what limit real spikes.
- **Interpretation**: the question's wording asks about inductors though it sits in the capacitor section; this page answers for inductors and gives the capacitor dual. The capacitor-focused version is [[q0037]], and the inductor-section duplicate is [[q0043]].

::: followups
### A 12 V source has charged a 1 mH inductor to 2 A through a resistor. A switch then disconnects the source and connects the inductor to a 10 Ω resistor. What is the voltage across the resistor at $t = 0^+$?
By continuity, $i_L(0^+) = 2$ A, now flowing through 10 Ω, so $v_R = 20$ V, even though the original source was only 12 V. The current then decays with $\tau = L/R = 100$ µs.

### Why does a flyback diode slow down relay release, and how do you fix it?
With just a diode, the coil voltage is clamped at about 0.7 V (plus its own IR drop), so $di/dt$ is small and the current (and magnetic hold force) decays over several $L/R$. Adding a Zener in series with the diode raises the clamp to, say, 24 V: $di/dt = -V_{clamp}/L$ is much larger, so the current dies quickly. The switch must withstand $V_s + V_{clamp}$.

### Which quantities can change instantaneously in an RLC circuit when a switch flips?
Inductor voltage, capacitor current, and every resistor voltage and current (unless pinned by a continuous quantity). Only $i_L$ and $v_C$ are guaranteed continuous. For example, in a series RL, the resistor current equals $i_L$, so it's continuous too, but in a parallel RL it can jump.

### What if you open the switch on an inductor with no flyback path at all? Where does the energy go?
The voltage rises until something conducts. If the switch is a MOSFET, it avalanches and absorbs $\tfrac12LI^2$ as heat; repeated or large events exceed its UIS rating. A mechanical contact arcs, eroding the contacts. With only parasitic capacitance $C_p$ present, the energy sloshes into it, with a peak voltage of roughly $I\sqrt{L/C_p}$, and rings down. For 10 mH, 120 mA and 100 pF that estimate is about 1.2 kV.

### How does inductor current continuity let a boost converter make a higher output voltage?
While the switch is on, the inductor current ramps up from $V_{in}$. When the switch opens, the current must keep flowing, so the switch node rises until the diode conducts into the output capacitor, at whatever voltage the output is sitting, even above $V_{in}$. In steady state the volt-seconds balance gives $V_{out} = V_{in}/(1-D)$.
