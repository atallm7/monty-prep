::: basics
### In plain words
You cannot buy a resistor of every possible value. Manufacturers make a limited set of standard values called the E-series. Each value is a fixed percentage bigger than the last (about 21% in the E12 series), so the steps grow as the values grow. The spacing is set by the part's tolerance, so any value you want is close to a standard one.

### Picture it
Think of shoe sizes. Shops do not stock every possible foot length. They stock sizes spaced so that each one fits a range of feet well enough. A finer series, like half sizes, fits more closely but means more sizes to stock. Resistor series work the same way. More values per decade means a closer fit and a tighter-tolerance part.

### Words you need
- **E-series**: the standard set of preferred values, such as E12, E24 and E96. The number is how many values per decade.
- **Decade**: a tenfold range, such as 10 to 100 Ω or 1 kΩ to 10 kΩ.
- **Tolerance**: how far a real resistor may be from its labeled value, such as 5% or 1%.
- **Preferred value**: a value in a standard series, which is cheap and easy to buy.
- **Voltage divider**: two resistors that set a ratio. See [[q0008]].

### The simplest example
E12 has 12 values per decade: 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82. Say you need 13 kΩ. E12 has no 13, but a 12 kΩ part at 10% may really be anywhere from 10.8 kΩ to 13.2 kΩ, so if your design can live with that, it will do. E24 has 13 exactly, with a 5% tolerance. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page lists the other series, the values engineers use most, and how to pick a series for a divider.
:::

::: testing
Practical familiarity: can you name the resistor values you reach for without thinking, and do you know *why* those values exist? Strong candidates don't just list "1k, 10k, 100k". They explain the **E-series** (logarithmic spacing so adjacent values overlap within tolerance), connect tolerance to series (E12 ≈ 10 %, E24 ≈ 5 %, E96 ≈ 1 %), know how to read markings, and say how that changes a design: dividers, LED current limits, pull-ups, feedback networks.
:::

::: why
You can't buy a 3.3 kΩ ± 0.01 % resistor from the bin on your bench; you pick from standard values. Designs have to be calculated with what exists: a divider that "should be" 31.25 kΩ is really 31.6 kΩ, and the output error from that is part of your error budget. Knowing common values also lets you read schematics at a glance, sanity-check a design ("why 4.7 k on I²C?") and keep the BOM small, which saves money in production.
:::

::: concepts
- **Preferred number series (E-series)**: geometric progressions defined in IEC 60063, with $E_N$ having $N$ values per decade, ratio $10^{1/N}$ ([[q0014]]).
- **Tolerance**: initial manufacturing spread, linked to series so neighboring values overlap.
- **Decades and multipliers**: the same pattern repeats ×10 (Ω, kΩ, MΩ).
- **Markings**: color bands for through-hole, 3/4-digit codes and EIA-96 codes for SMD.
- **Voltage divider** ([[q0008]]): the standard use where you pick two standard values to approximate a ratio.
- **Power and package**: the value is only half the spec; power rating and size matter too ([[q0018]]).
:::

::: technical
**E-series:** $R_k = 10^{k/N}$ rounded to 2 or 3 significant digits, $k=0..N-1$.

| Series | Values/decade | Ratio | Typical tolerance | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| E6 | 6 | 1.468 | 20 % | 10, 15, 22, 33, 47, 68 |
| E12 | 12 | 1.212 | 10 % | 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82 |
| E24 | 24 | 1.101 | 5 % (also 1 %) | adds 11, 13, 16, 20, 24, 30, 36, 43, 51, 62, 75, 91 |
| E48 | 48 | 1.050 | 2 % | |
| E96 | 96 | 1.0243 | 1 % | 100, 102, 105, 107, 110, 113, … |
| E192 | 192 | 1.0121 | 0.5 %, 0.25 %, 0.1 % | 100, 101, 102, 104, … |

The worst-case distance to the nearest value is about half the ratio step: ±10 % (E12), ±4.9 % (E24), ±1.2 % (E96). These are the ideal-grid figures; the hand-rounded E12/E24 lists have uneven steps (for example 13 to 15 in E24 is a 15 % jump, so a target between them can be 7 % from the nearest value), while E96 and finer follow the formula closely.

**Marking schemes**
- Color bands (axial): digits, multiplier, tolerance, e.g. yellow–violet–red–gold = 4.7 kΩ ±5 %.
- Three-digit SMD (5 %): "103" = 10 × 10³ = 10 kΩ; "4R7" = 4.7 Ω.
- Four-digit SMD (1 %): "1002" = 100 × 10² = 10 kΩ.
- EIA-96 (1 %, 0402/0603): two digits index the E96 value, a letter gives the multiplier, e.g. "01C" = 100 × 100 = 10 kΩ.

**Zero-ohm links:** marked "0" or "000"; rated by current (commonly 1–2 A for 0603) instead of resistance.
:::

::: long
"Standard resistor values come from the E-series, defined in IEC 60063. The idea is to cover each decade with a fixed number of logarithmically spaced values, so that each value is the same percentage above the last. E12 has twelve values per decade: 10, 12, 15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82. The ratio between neighbors is about 21 percent, which matches the 10 percent tolerance, so any target value is within about 10 percent of a standard one and the tolerance bands touch. E24 has 24 values and 5 percent tolerance, and E96 has 96 values with 1 percent parts and values like 100, 102, 105, 107.

In terms of what I use all the time: 10 ohms, 100 ohms, 1k, 10k, 100k, 1 meg; 4.7k as a pull-up on I2C and 10k on resets and straps; 22 or 33 ohms in series with clock lines; 330 or 470 for LED limiting; 49.9 ohms for 50-ohm terminations. And zero-ohm links.

How I use that in design: I choose the ratio first, then find the closest pair in the series that gives it, and check the worst-case error with tolerance. For example, to get 3.3 volts from 5 volts I'd want R2 over R1 plus R2 equals 0.66. 10k and 19.6k from E96 gives 3.31 volts, and with 1 percent parts it could be anywhere from about 3.29 to 3.33. If that's too loose I use 0.1 percent parts or a matched network.

Reading them: SMD parts use three or four digit codes, so 103 is 10k and 1002 is 10k at one percent, and the EIA-96 two-digit-and-letter code for small 1 percent chips."
:::

::: terms
- **E-series**: IEC 60063 preferred numbers (E6, E12, E24, E48, E96, E192).
- **Decade**: a factor of 10 range (100 Ω to 1 kΩ).
- **Tolerance**: allowed deviation from nominal value at 25 °C.
- **Color code**: bands encoding digits, multiplier, tolerance.
- **EIA-96**: SMD code with a 2-digit value index and a multiplier letter.
- **Zero-ohm link**: a jumper in resistor form.
- **Ratio tolerance / matching**: relative accuracy of two resistors in a network.
- **BOM consolidation**: using few distinct values to reduce purchasing/assembly cost.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Assuming any value exists.** 31.25 kΩ isn't standard; E96 gives 31.6 kΩ and the divider error from that shift must be calculated.
- **Using the nominal ratio and ignoring tolerance.** Two 1 % resistors can produce about ±2 % ratio error.
- **Mixing up marking codes.** "103" is 10 kΩ, not 103 Ω; "4R7" is 4.7 Ω.
- **Using E96 values on 5 % parts.** A 49.9 Ω resistor only means something with 1 % or better tolerance.
- **Forgetting that the series choice sets tolerance.** An E12 value on a 1 % part is possible, but the E12 grid is coarse.
- **Choosing odd values for no reason.** More distinct values means a bigger BOM and more placement errors.
:::

::: deep
## Why logarithmic spacing

Imagine you need to cover every resistance between 10 Ω and 100 Ω with parts of ±10 % tolerance. Linear spacing (10, 20, 30, …) would waste values at the high end (90 and 100 differ by 10 %) and leave huge gaps at the low end (10 to 20 is 100 %). The *relative* gap should be constant, so values form a geometric progression:

$$R_k = 10^{k/N},\quad k=0,1,\dots,N-1,$$

for $N$ values per decade. Neighboring values differ by a factor $10^{1/N}$: 1.468 for E6, 1.212 for E12, 1.101 for E24, 1.0243 for E96. Choose $N$ so that the ratio is close to $(1+t)/(1-t)$ for tolerance $t$: for $t=10\,\%$, $1.1/0.9=1.22$, matching E12.

The "rounded" values: the E12/E24 lists are not exactly $10^{k/N}$ rounded (E12 has 27, 33, 39, 47 where rounding would give 26, 32, 38, 46; E24 has 30 rather than 29). The tables in IEC 60063 are fixed lists, and they are what manufacturers use.

> [!NOTE]
> E48, E96 and E192 use three significant digits and round more consistently to $10^{k/N}$; E6–E24 use two digits with a few manual adjustments (for example 3.3 and 4.7 in E6, where exact rounding would give 3.2 and 4.6).

## Picking a pair from the series: a divider example

Goal: 3.3 V from 5 V, so $R_2/(R_1+R_2)=0.66$. With E24 values the best pair is 47 kΩ/91 kΩ (ratio 0.6594), but any 10 kΩ-ish combination works: 10 kΩ/20 kΩ gives 3.333 V (+1 % high); with E96, 10 kΩ/19.6 kΩ gives $5\cdot19.6/29.6=3.311$ V (+0.3 %).

Tolerance: with ±1 % parts, the worst case for the 10 kΩ/20 kΩ pair is
$$V_{out}=5\cdot\frac{20(0.99)}{10(1.01)+20(0.99)} = 3.311\ \text{V},\qquad 5\cdot\frac{20(1.01)}{10(0.99)+20(1.01)}=3.355\ \text{V}.$$
So the output lies between 3.311 and 3.355 V, about +0.3 % to +1.7 % above the 3.3 V target. The tolerance matters about as much as the grid.

## Making non-standard values

- **Series/parallel:** 47 kΩ + 4.7 kΩ = 51.7 kΩ; 10 kΩ ∥ 10 kΩ = 5 kΩ. This costs board area and a second part, but the tolerance does not double: independent errors partially average, so two 1 % parts combined are still about 1 % or a little better.
- **Trim pot in series:** a fixed resistor at 90 % of target plus a 10 % trim.
- **Tighter series:** 0.1 % E192 parts.
- **Digital pot or software calibration** if the system tolerates it.
- **Ratio-matched network:** when only the *ratio* matters, as in instrumentation amplifier gain setting.

## Reading what's on the part

Through-hole: four to five color bands (digits, multiplier, tolerance, sometimes TCR). SMD: a "103" means 10 × 10³ = 10 kΩ; "4R7" means 4.7 Ω; "1002" = 100 × 10² = 10 kΩ. EIA-96 "01C" = value index 01 (100) × multiplier C (100) = 10 kΩ. 0201 and smaller usually have no marking at all, so keep your reel labels.

## Worked example: hitting 3.3 V from a 0.8 V feedback pin

A buck regulator has $V_{FB} = 0.8$ V, and you need $V_{out} = 3.3$ V. The relation is $V_{out} = V_{FB}(1 + R_1/R_2)$, so the target ratio is $R_1/R_2 = 3.3/0.8 - 1 = 3.125$. The mistake beginners make is to fix $R_2$ at a round number like 10 kΩ and then take the nearest standard $R_1$. Look at what that gives:

| Choice | $R_1$ / $R_2$ | Resulting $V_{out}$ | Error |
|---|---|---|---|
| E24, $R_2$ = 10 kΩ | 30 kΩ / 10 kΩ | 3.200 V | −3.0 % |
| E96, $R_2$ = 10 kΩ | 31.6 kΩ / 10 kΩ | 3.328 V | +0.85 % |
| E96, $R_2$ = 10 kΩ | 30.9 kΩ / 10 kΩ | 3.272 V | −0.85 % |
| E24, free choice | 75 kΩ / 24 kΩ | 3.300 V | 0 % |

A search across all pairs finds that 75 kΩ and 24 kΩ, both ordinary E24 values, give a ratio of exactly 3.125. Because $75/24 = 3.125$, no E96 pair does better on the nominal value. The lesson is that the grid is two-dimensional: you have two resistors to choose, so the achievable ratios are far denser than the grid of a single resistor suggests. A spreadsheet or a ten-line script is the right tool, and I would do that rather than guess.

The nominal error is not the whole error, though. With 1 % resistors, $R_1$ high and $R_2$ low gives $0.8\,(1 + 75.75/23.76) = 3.351$ V, and the opposite corner gives 3.250 V, so the output spans −1.5 % to +1.5 % around the target. That is more than the 0.85 % grid error of the E96 pair, so spending effort on a perfect nominal value only pays once the tolerance is tighter. With 0.1 % parts the spread falls to about ±0.15 %, and then the regulator's own reference tolerance (often around 1 %, but vendor-dependent, so read the datasheet) dominates the budget.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Reference-voltage tolerance and feedback-pin bias current differ between regulators. The 0.8 V figure here is a generic design number, not a specific part.

## What the grid actually looks like

Earlier the page said neighboring values differ by $10^{1/N}$. That is the average step. The real E24 list is hand-rounded to two digits, so its steps are uneven: 15 to 16 is only 1.067, while 13 to 15 is 1.154. A target halfway (geometrically) between 13 and 15 is $\sqrt{1.154}-1 = 7.4\%$ from either, not the 4.9 % the ideal grid promises. Compare entries with the exact $10^{k/24}$ and you find 27, 30, 33 and 36 sit 3.4 % to 4.4 % above it. The smooth curve in the plot above is therefore the design intent; in E96 and finer, three-digit rounding follows the formula closely, so the plot is accurate there.

This also explains a practical habit. When a calculation lands in a wide gap of the E24 list, it is usually easier to move one resistor up a step and the other down a step than to reach for a tighter series, which is the pair search above done by hand.

## Real-hardware considerations

- **Package and value availability.** Not every E96 value is stocked in every package, and the cheap reels are E24 5 % or 1 %. A 24.9 kΩ 1 % part may cost the same as a 24 kΩ one, but check that it is on the approved parts list before designing it in.
- **Tolerance applies to the whole spec, not just the initial value.** A 1 % rating at 25 °C is the initial tolerance. Over temperature, with a 100 ppm/K film, add another 0.6 % from 25 to 85 °C, plus aging and board stress.
- **Two parts for one value.** Series or parallel combinations reach values off the grid, for example 22 kΩ in series with 4.7 kΩ gives 26.7 kΩ, a value no E24 part offers. This costs area and a second line on the BOM, and the parts should share a package and TCR class so they drift together.
- **Reading markings reliably.** A 1 % chip marked "1002" and a 5 % chip marked "103" look similar, and a 0201 has no marking at all. Check value with a meter in-circuit only after removing parallel paths, or by lifting one end.

## Verifying on the bench

1. Measure each resistor out of circuit with a 4-digit DMM to confirm that the stock is the value on the label, especially for loose assortment bins.
2. Power the regulator and measure $V_{out}$ at the load, not at the regulator pin, so that drops in the PCB trace are not mistaken for divider error.
3. Check that the measured value lies inside the worst-case window you calculated. If it does not, suspect the reference tolerance or a loading effect from the feedback pin's leakage, not just the divider.

## Design judgement

Don't stop at the arithmetic. A good designer also asks: What does the value *do* in the circuit (current, time constant, ratio)? What accuracy do I need? Is the power rating adequate? Do I have the part in stock, in the package I want? For prototypes, hobby kits (E12 5 % assortment) are fine; for production, use few values, mostly 1 % thick-film.

- **Pull-up/pull-down resistors** on logic and I²C lines: 4.7 kΩ and 10 kΩ ([[q0020]]).
- **LED current limiting**: $R=(V_{cc}-V_f)/I$ then pick the nearest standard value (3 V across 10 mA gives 300 Ω, so use 330 Ω from E12/E24).
- **Regulator feedback dividers**: E96 1 % or 0.1 % pairs to hit the target output.
- **Impedance matching**: 49.9 Ω terminators and 100 Ω differential terminations.
- **Filter RC networks**: with a standard capacitor value, pick R from E96 to hit the cutoff.
- **Rework/lab stock**: a decade box or E12 kit covers most prototypes.

- I assume the IEC 60063 E-series; some industries (military, aerospace, precision) use additional values or different series.
- The tolerance-to-series pairing is conventional, not mandatory: 1 % E24 parts exist, and 5 % E96 values are unusual.
- Quoted typical roles of values are conventions, not rules: 2.2 kΩ versus 4.7 kΩ on I²C depends on bus capacitance and speed.
- Current rating of 0 Ω links is vendor-dependent; check the datasheet.
- If the interviewer asks for "common values" only, a short list with the role of each value plus a mention of E-series is enough.

::: followups
### Why do E12 values include 22, 33, 47, 68 rather than evenly spaced numbers?
Because they follow $10^{k/12}$ approximately: 10, 12.1, 14.7, 17.8, 21.5, 26.1, 31.6, 38.3, 46.4, 56.2, 68.1, 82.5, rounded to two digits with some adjustments (15, 18, 22, 27, 33, 39, 47, 56, 68, 82). Each value is about 21 % above the last, so neighbors' ±10 % bands just touch.

### How would you get a 3.3 kΩ equivalent if you only have 10 kΩ and 4.7 kΩ resistors?
$10\ \text{k}\parallel 4.7\ \text{k}=3.197$ kΩ: -3 % from 3.3 kΩ. Or 3 × 10 kΩ in parallel = 3.33 kΩ (+1 %), which uses three parts. Good enough for many pull-ups, but not for a precision divider.

### What's the 'EIA-96 code 68B' resistance?
Code 68 is the E96 value 499 (the 68th value in the list, where 01 = 100); multiplier B = 10. So 499 × 10 = 4.99 kΩ.

### Why is 49.9 Ω used instead of 50 Ω?
49.9 Ω is the nearest E96 value to 50 Ω (within 0.2 %). 50 Ω itself is not in the E96 series, so 49.9 Ω 1 % is the standard termination part.

### What if two 1 % resistors in a divider give a 2 % ratio error and your ADC needs 0.1 %?
Use a matched network (ratio tolerance 0.05 % or better, ratio TCR of a few ppm/K), or calibrate the ratio in software with a measured reference. Also check that TCR and self-heating don't introduce drift of the same order.
