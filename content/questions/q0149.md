::: basics
### In plain words
A voltage divider works when whatever you connect to its output takes almost no current compared with the current already flowing through the two resistors. Then the output is just the resistor ratio times the input. That makes it good for sensing and setting thresholds, and bad for powering things.

### Picture it
Think of tasting a pot of soup with a tiny spoon. The spoonful is so small that it doesn't change the soup, so the taste tells you what the soup is like. If you tried to serve dinner with that spoon, you would drain the pot and change it. A divider feeding an input that draws nanoamps is the tiny spoon.

### Words you need
- **Divider ratio**: $R_2/(R_1+R_2)$, the fraction of the input that appears at the output.
- **Input impedance**: how much an input resists drawing current. High is good for a divider's load.
- **Thevenin resistance**: what the divider's output looks like from the load's view, $R_1 \parallel R_2$. See [[q0148]].
- **ADC**: a circuit that turns a voltage into a number.
- **Tolerance**: how far a real resistor can be from its stated value. See [[q0017]].

### The simplest example
Scale a 10 V signal down to 5 V for an ADC with two 10 kΩ resistors. The ADC input has 10 MΩ of resistance. That is in parallel with the bottom resistor, making it about 9.99 kΩ. The output is $10 \times 9.99/19.99 \approx 4.9975$ V, an error of only about 0.05%. The rule of thumb is that the load should be at least 100 times the divider's Thevenin resistance for about 1% error. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page adds more uses, such as regulator feedback and thresholds, and the trade-offs in picking resistor sizes.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can state the **rule** behind a divider ("the load must draw negligible current compared with the divider's own current") and then list the real places that rule holds: feedback sensing, ADC scaling with a buffer, comparator or enable thresholds, bias networks, level scaling of slow signals. The interviewer is checking that you can pick resistor values with reasons (loading, leakage, noise, tolerance, quiescent current), not just recite the formula, and that you can contrast this answer with [[q0148]].
:::

::: why
Dividers are everywhere in working boards: every adjustable regulator has one, every battery monitor has one, every enable pin with a UVLO threshold has one. Knowing exactly when a divider is the right tool, and how to size it, is basic hardware competence. It is also where small mistakes cause subtle errors: a 10 MΩ divider feeding an ADC with 1 µA of leakage, a feedback divider that is too noisy, or an enable threshold that drifts with a 1% resistor. This question is the complement of [[q0148]].
:::

::: concepts
- **Divider equation and Thevenin form**: $V_{out}=V_{in}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ with $R_{th}=R_1\parallel R_2$. See [[q0008]].
- **Loading rule**: error from a load $R_L$ is $R_{th}/(R_{th}+R_L)$; keep $R_L\ge100R_{th}$ for about 1%.
- **Quiescent current**: $V_{in}/(R_1+R_2)$ flows continuously; matters for battery products.
- **Input leakage and bias current**: $I_{leak}R_{th}$ appears as an offset.
- **Resistor tolerance and temperature coefficient**: set the ratio accuracy. See [[q0017]].
- **Feedback sensing**: regulator output set by $V_{out}=V_{FB}(1+R_1/R_2)$. See [[q0223]].
:::

::: answer
"A divider is applicable when whatever is connected to its output draws negligible current compared with the current flowing through the divider itself. Then the output is just the resistor ratio times the input, and the formula holds.

In practice that means signal-level and sensing jobs rather than power. The classic ones are a regulator's feedback network, where the divider feeds an error-amplifier input that draws nanoamps, and the divider sets the output voltage. Another is scaling a voltage down for an ADC, like a 12 volt rail or a battery, ideally into a buffer or an ADC with low enough source impedance. Another is setting thresholds: the enable pin of a regulator for an under-voltage lockout, or a comparator input. Also bias networks for a transistor or op-amp, and level-shifting slow signals.

For the design I'd pick the ratio first, then the magnitude. The rule of thumb is that the load should be at least 100 times the divider's Thevenin resistance for about one percent error. Lower values give a stiffer node but burn more current, so for a battery product I'd go up to hundreds of kilohms, but then I have to watch input leakage, noise and the sampling capacitor of an ADC, and sometimes add a small capacitor or a buffer. And I need to remember tolerance: with 1% resistors the ratio is off by about a percent and a half, so use 0.1% parts where accuracy matters."
:::

::: technical
**Condition for validity.** The load resistance (or equivalent) $R_L$ must satisfy $R_L\gg R_{th}=R_1\parallel R_2$. The fractional error is
$$\varepsilon=\frac{R_{th}}{R_{th}+R_L}$$
so $R_L=100R_{th}$ gives 0.99% and $R_L=1000R_{th}$ gives 0.1%.

**Applicable cases and sizing.**
1. *Regulator feedback.* $V_{out}=V_{FB}(1+R_1/R_2)$. Example: $V_{FB}=0.6$ V, $V_{out}=3.3$ V, $R_2=10$ kΩ gives $R_1=45$ kΩ; nearest E96 45.3 kΩ gives 3.318 V. With 1% resistors the output error is about ±1.6% (from the ratio) plus the reference tolerance. Datasheets often suggest a total divider resistance in the tens of kΩ up to a few hundred kΩ.
2. *UVLO / enable threshold.* The enable pin turns the regulator on at $V_{EN}=1.2$ V. To start at 9 V with $R_2=10$ kΩ: $R_1=R_2(9/1.2-1)=65$ kΩ; using 64.9 kΩ gives 8.99 V turn-on. Divider current at 9 V is 120 µA. A 100 nA pin leakage causes only 0.9 mV at the pin, or 6.5 mV at $V_{in}$.
3. *ADC scaling.* 4.2 V cell to a 3.3 V ADC: 100 kΩ / 100 kΩ gives 2.1 V and draws 21 µA. $R_{th}=50$ kΩ; a 50 nA leakage causes 2.5 mV error; a sampling capacitor needs a bypass capacitor (e.g. 100 nF) to supply its charge.
4. *Comparator and bias references,* where the input draws nA.
5. *Slow level shifting,* where the receiver has high input impedance but the RC from $R_{th}$ and pin capacitance limits speed.

**Error budget.** Ratio error from tolerance $\approx$ (1 - ratio)(ε₂ − ε₁); leakage error $I_{leak}R_{th}$; noise $\sqrt{4kTR_{th}\Delta f}$; temperature coefficient drift. Choose $R_{th}$ to balance these against standby current.

**When it fails.** If the load draws substantial or variable current, a divider is the wrong tool ([[q0148]]); buffer the divider output or use a regulator.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Recommended divider resistance ranges and maximum source impedance for ADC inputs differ by part and by sampling time. Use the device datasheet's "source impedance" and "feedback divider" guidance for any real design.
:::

::: long
"A voltage divider is applicable whenever the load on its output draws negligible current compared with the current through the divider. In Thevenin terms, the divider is a source of $V_{in}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ behind $R_1\parallel R_2$, so if the load resistance is much larger than that, the output is basically the ideal ratio. For about one percent error the load should be a hundred times the Thevenin resistance.

That puts it in the sensing and signal-level jobs. The big one is the feedback network of a regulator: the divider feeds the error-amplifier pin, which draws nanoamps, and sets $V_{out}=V_{FB}(1+R_1/R_2)$. For a 0.6 volt reference and a 3.3 volt output with a 10 kilohm bottom resistor, the top is 45 kilohms. Another is under-voltage lockout on an enable pin: if the enable threshold is 1.2 volts and I want it to turn on at 9 volts, a 65 kilohm over 10 kilohm divider does it, drawing about 120 microamps. Another is scaling a battery or rail down to an ADC range. A 4.2 volt cell into a 3.3 volt ADC through two 100 kilohm resistors gives 2.1 volts for 21 microamps, and a 100 nanofarad capacitor at the pin supplies the sampling charge. And bias networks, comparator thresholds and slow level shifting.

When choosing the actual resistor values, I'd trade off several things. Lower resistance is stiffer and quieter and less sensitive to input leakage, but burns more current, which matters on a battery. Higher resistance saves power but makes the node vulnerable to leakage, noise pickup and slow RC settling with the pin capacitance. And tolerance matters: two 1% resistors can put the ratio off by around 1.5 percent, so for precision I'd use 0.1% parts or a matched network.

What I would not do is use a divider to power anything. The load changes the output."
:::

::: terms
- **Thevenin resistance ($R_{th}$)**: $R_1\parallel R_2$, the divider's output resistance.
- **Loading error**: fractional change in output due to a load, $R_{th}/(R_{th}+R_L)$.
- **Feedback divider**: network that scales a regulator's output to its reference voltage ($V_{FB}$).
- **UVLO (under-voltage lockout)**: threshold below which a converter is disabled, often set via the EN pin divider.
- **Input leakage / bias current**: tiny current into or out of an IC pin that creates an error across $R_{th}$.
- **Source impedance (ADC)**: the resistance driving an ADC pin; limited so the sampling capacitor settles.
- **Ratio tolerance**: accuracy of $R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ resulting from resistor tolerances.
- **Quiescent (divider) current**: standing current $V_{in}/(R_1+R_2)$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "when you don't need to draw current from it, like for sensing."

**An exceptional answer**:
- States the rule quantitatively ($R_L\ge100R_{th}$ for 1%) and ties it to the Thevenin picture.
- Names several concrete uses, with a worked sizing for at least one (feedback divider: 0.6 V ref gives 45.3 kΩ / 10 kΩ; UVLO divider; battery to ADC).
- Covers the magnitude trade-off: quiescent current vs leakage, noise, settling with pin capacitance and the ADC sampling capacitor.
- Includes tolerance (a pair of 1% resistors gives about ±1.6% ratio error) and temperature coefficient, and says when to use 0.1% or a matched array.
- Contrasts with powering a load ([[q0148]]) and mentions a buffer as the fix when the downstream node is not high-impedance.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using a high-value divider on an ADC pin with no capacitor or buffer.** The sampling capacitor can't recharge through hundreds of kΩ in time, so readings are wrong.
- **Ignoring leakage and bias current.** 1 µA through 1 MΩ is a full volt of error.
- **Choosing resistors only for the ratio.** The magnitude controls power, noise, settling and leakage error.
- **Forgetting tolerance.** Two 1% resistors can give about 1.6-2% ratio error, which breaks a 1% regulator spec.
- **Placing the divider far from the sensed node** or letting a high-impedance node run long on the PCB where it picks up noise.
- **Using it where the downstream draw varies** (like a processor's supply), which is the case for [[q0148]].
:::

::: deep
## The rule that decides it

From [[q0008]], an unloaded divider gives $V_{out}=V_{in}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$. With a load $R_L$ the equivalent bottom resistor is $R_2\parallel R_L$ and the result is lower. In Thevenin form the divider has $V_{th}=V_{in}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ and $R_{th}=R_1\parallel R_2$, and the loaded output is $V_{th}R_L/(R_{th}+R_L)$. So the *design rule* is:

$$\text{loading error}=\frac{R_{th}}{R_{th}+R_L}\quad\Rightarrow\quad R_L\ge100\,R_{th}\ \text{for}\ \le1\%$$

A divider is the right tool whenever you can make this true at a reasonable standing current. That is the case for any node that feeds only the *input* of a high-impedance device: an amplifier or comparator input, a regulator's FB or EN pin, a MOSFET gate bias, an ADC behind a buffer.

## Use 1: setting a regulator's output voltage

An adjustable regulator compares the voltage at its feedback pin with an internal reference, $V_{FB}$. A divider from the output to the feedback pin sets the regulated output:

$$V_{out}=V_{FB}\left(1+\frac{R_1}{R_2}\right)$$

Example: $V_{FB}=0.6$ V, target 3.3 V, pick $R_2=10$ kΩ, then $R_1=10\text{ k}(3.3/0.6-1)=45$ kΩ. The nearest E96 value is 45.3 kΩ, giving $0.6(1+4.53)=3.318$ V (+0.5%). The feedback pin draws nanoamps, so the divider ratio is accurate. Choose the *total* resistance so the divider current (here $3.3/55.3\text{ k}=60$ µA) is much larger than the pin's bias current and small enough for light-load efficiency. See [[q0223]] and [[q0224]] for what happens when the total is too high or too low.

With 1% resistors, the worst-case ratio error is about ±1.6%, so a 1% reference plus 1% resistors gives about ±2.6% on the output in the worst case. Use 0.1% resistors if you need ±1% overall.

## Use 3: scaling a voltage to an ADC

A 4.2 V Li-ion cell can't connect directly to a 3.3 V ADC. Two 100 kΩ resistors give 2.1 V at 21 µA. The Thevenin resistance is 50 kΩ, which brings in three effects:

- **Leakage**: 50 nA of pin leakage causes $50\text{ nA}\times50\text{ kΩ}=2.5$ mV, about 0.12% of 2.1 V. Acceptable.
- **Sampling charge**: a SAR ADC's sample capacitor (picofarads) is switched onto the pin each conversion. $R_{th}$ forms an RC with that capacitor; if the settling time is longer than the acquisition time, you read the previous voltage. A 100 nF capacitor at the pin holds the node and supplies the sampling charge, while the RC with $R_{th}$ just filters (50 kΩ × 100 nF = 5 ms).
- **Noise**: 50 kΩ has thermal noise $\sqrt{4kTR\Delta f}\approx29$ µV rms over 1 MHz, usually negligible versus ADC LSBs, but notable on a 16-bit ADC with 1 MHz of bandwidth.

For fast signals or precision you would use a smaller $R_{th}$ or a unity-gain buffer (op-amp), which converts the divider into a "stiff" source.

## Use 4: thresholds and bias

A comparator threshold set by a divider off a reference, a pull-up/pull-down pair (a divider with a weak parallel path), a bias point for a BJT or op-amp stage (set high enough that base current doesn't disturb it, the classic rule: divider current about 10 times the base current), and potentiometers (an adjustable divider where the wiper feeds a high-impedance input).

## How to choose the magnitude

| If you pick | Benefit | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Small R (1-10 kΩ total) | Stiff, low noise, immune to leakage | High standing current ($V/R$), heat |
| Medium (10-200 kΩ) | Balanced; typical for feedback and UVLO | Watch ADC source impedance |
| Large (≥1 MΩ) | µA-level standby draw | Leakage, noise pickup, slow RC, humidity sensitivity |

Check each of: $I_{div}=V_{in}/(R_1+R_2)$ against the power budget; $I_{leak}R_{th}$ against accuracy; $R_{th}C_{pin}$ against bandwidth; tolerance against the ratio spec.

## The ratio error from tolerances

For $R_1,R_2$ with fractional tolerances $\pm t$, the worst-case ratio change is about $\pm t(1-\text{ratio})\cdot2$. For the 3.3 V feedback network above (ratio $R_2/(R_1+R_2)=0.181$), 1% parts give $-1.6\%/+1.6\%$ on $V_{out}$. Matched resistor networks track each other with temperature, so a 0.1% matched pair gives a better ratio than two independent 0.1% parts.
:::

::: applications
- **Adjustable buck/LDO feedback**: the divider from $V_{out}$ to FB sets the output (typical in nearly every regulator datasheet).
- **UVLO/OVLO and power-good thresholds** via EN/PG pins and window comparators.
- **Battery voltage monitoring** into an MCU ADC (two equal resistors plus a filter capacitor).
- **Bias for amplifier stages**: BJT base biasing and op-amp mid-supply reference with a buffered divider.
- **Potentiometer-based controls**: volume, tuning and threshold adjustments feeding high-impedance inputs.
:::

::: assumptions
- **The downstream device is high-impedance** (nanoamps or less) or buffered.
- **Ideal resistors**, except where tolerance and TCR are discussed.
- **DC or slow signals.** At high frequency, pin capacitance forms an RC with $R_{th}$; compensated dividers (like scope probes) match $R_1C_1=R_2C_2$.
- **Resistor values** such as 45.3 kΩ are from the E96 series with 1% tolerance. See [[q0017]].
- **Alternative valid answers.** A divider is also applicable for generating a *reference* from a stable supply, or as a pull-up/pull-down pair, or when the load is a known fixed resistor and the designer accepts the resulting ratio; all are consistent with the loading rule.
:::

::: followups
### How do you pick the divider resistances for an adjustable regulator?
Pick the bottom resistor near the datasheet's suggested value (often 10 kΩ-100 kΩ), compute the top from $R_1=R_2(V_{out}/V_{FB}-1)$, snap to the nearest 1% value, and recompute $V_{out}$. Check that the divider current is much larger than the FB pin bias current and not wasteful at light load. See [[q0224]].

### Why add a capacitor at the ADC pin of a divider?
It holds the node steady against the sampling capacitor's charge kick and filters noise. With 50 kΩ source impedance, a 100 nF capacitor gives a 5 ms time constant, so readings are accurate but slow; for fast signals you buffer instead.

### Design a divider to monitor a 12 V rail (up to 14.4 V) into a 3.3 V ADC.
Ratio at most $3.3/14.4=0.229$. Take $R_2=10$ kΩ and $R_1=36$ kΩ: ratio $10/46=0.217$, so 14.4 V gives 3.13 V (some headroom for resistor tolerance) and 12 V gives 2.61 V. Total 46 kΩ draws 0.26 mA at 12 V, and $R_{th}=7.8$ kΩ. Add a 100 nF capacitor at the pin and a clamp or series resistor for transients.

### What happens if the divider resistors are too high?
Leakage and bias currents create offset ($I\cdot R_{th}$), the node picks up noise, settling with pin capacitance slows, and humidity or flux residue on the board can add leakage paths. See [[q0224]].

### Can you use a divider for a level shifter?
For slow, one-direction signals into a high-impedance input, yes (e.g. 5 V to 3.3 V on an input). Edge rates are limited by $R_{th}$ times the input capacitance, and the high output is only the divided level. For fast or bidirectional lines use a proper level shifter. See [[q0147]].

### Scenario: your battery-voltage reading is 3% low only when the MCU is awake. What do you suspect?
The ADC's sampling or an I/O pin's pull-up is loading the divider only during activity, or the divider resistance is high enough that sampling charge or leakage affects it. Add a capacitor at the ADC pin or a buffer, or lower $R_{th}$.
:::

::: interpretations
The wording "to step down voltage for a power rail" in the sibling question [[q0148]] is about *powering* a rail. This question asks about the complementary case: when a divider *does* make sense. The answer is "when the load current is negligible", which in practice means sensing and signal applications, not supplying power.
