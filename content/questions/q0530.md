::: testing
Whether you can turn the termination *circuit* into a *layout*: the net should run **through** the termination (no stub), the resistor should be tiny and right at the pin, the termination's other end must reach its reference (ground plane, $V_{TT}$, or the partner trace) through the shortest possible path, and for differential pairs everything must be symmetric. The hidden sub-skill is that the parasitics of the *connection* (via inductance, stub length, pad capacitance) can destroy a perfectly valued resistor.
:::

::: why
Termination values are easy; termination layout is where boards fail. A 50 Ω resistor with a 5 mm stub and a long ground via is a worse match than no resistor. Reviewers check exactly these things: stub length, via placement, symmetry, plane references. It follows [[q0529]] (where) and precedes [[q0531]] (when).
:::

::: concepts
- **Net passes through the terminator**: the line ends at the resistor pad; no branch. See [[q0529]].
- **Stub length limit**: connection delay $\lesssim t_r/10$.
- **Parasitics of the return connection**: via L (0.5–1 nH) and pad C add to the termination. See [[q0515]].
- **Small packages**: 0402/0201 have the lowest ESL and pad capacitance.
- **Reference for the other end**: ground plane or a decoupled $V_{TT}$/rail. See [[q0503]].
- **Symmetry for pairs**: both halves identical. See [[q0519]].
:::

::: answer
"I treat the termination as part of the transmission line. For a series resistor, it's in-line: the trace from the driver goes straight into one pad of the resistor and out the other pad, as close to the driver pin as I can get it, a couple of millimetres, with the trace the same width on both sides, and no branch. So the signal flows through the resistor, rather than tapping off to it.

For a parallel termination at the far end, the line runs through the receiver's pad and then ends on the resistor pad, no T. The other end of the resistor gets its own via to the ground plane, placed right next to the pad, so the return path is a few tenths of a nanohenry. If it's a Thevenin pair or a termination to VTT, those rails get a decoupling cap right there, because the termination current flows through them.

For a differential pair, a single resistor across the pair right at the receiver, centered between the traces, symmetric, with the pair routing straight through its pads.

Other details: use 0402 or smaller parts to keep pad capacitance and inductance down, keep the reference plane unbroken underneath, and avoid resistor arrays on fast edges because of crosstalk and longer internal leads. And I check that the stub between the line and the resistor is under about a tenth of the rise time in delay."
:::

::: technical
**Series termination layout.** Driver pin → trace stub $\le$ a few mm → resistor pad 1 → pad 2 → continue with the controlled-impedance trace. In-line, same width, same layer; no vias between driver and resistor. Place within ~$t_r/(10\,t_{pd})$ of the pin (3.4 mm at 200 ps). Choose $R_s=Z_0-R_{out}$ from the IBIS output impedance.

**Parallel/end termination layout.** Line → receiver pad → resistor pad, with the resistor's tap short ($\lesssim t_r/10$). Other pad to the reference with a via adjacent to the pad (ideally via-in-pad or immediately beside it) to the solid plane. Thevenin: two resistors to $V_{CC}$ and ground, each with their own nearby via; add a decoupling cap on $V_{CC}$ close to the pair. $V_{TT}$ terminations (DDR): a local $V_{TT}$ island/plane with several 0.1 µF/10 µF capacitors, and the island fed from the regulator via a short path.

**Differential termination.** One resistor (100 Ω across a 100 Ω pair) between the two receiver pads, centered on the axis of the pair, placed symmetrically; the pair passes through the resistor pads without stubs. If AC-coupled, the capacitors come before the receiver/resistor.

**Parasitics.** A 0402 resistor has about 0.4–0.7 nH of series inductance and 0.1–0.2 pF of pad capacitance (vendor/footprint dependent). A ground via adds 0.5–1 nH. At $f_{knee}=0.35/t_r=1.75$ GHz (200 ps edge), 1 nH is 11 Ω: the termination $50+j11\ \Omega$ has $|\Gamma|=0.11$, an 11% reflection from layout parasitics alone.

**Do:** keep the plane solid under the part, use symmetric pads on pairs, keep clearance from other traces. **Don't:** branch the net to the resistor, use long thin necks, share ground vias between several resistors, or use resistor arrays on multi-hundred-MHz edges without checking crosstalk.
:::

::: long
"My rule is that the termination is part of the transmission line, so the net should run through it rather than tap off it.

For a series resistor, the trace from the driver goes into one pad of the resistor and out the other, in line, with the same trace width on both sides, and the resistor sits as close to the driver pin as the footprint allows, a couple of millimetres. There's no via between the pin and the resistor, no branch.

For a parallel termination at the far end, the trace runs through the receiver's pad and ends on one pad of the resistor. I keep the little connection between the line and the resistor to under about a tenth of the rise time in delay, so a few millimetres for a 200 picosecond edge. The other pad of the resistor goes to its reference through a via placed directly beside the pad, because the inductance of that via is in series with the termination. A nanohenry looks like 11 ohms at 1.75 gigahertz, which would make the 50 ohm termination 50 plus j11 and reflect 11 percent. If the reference is VTT or a Thevenin pair, those rails get a decoupling capacitor right at the resistors because the termination current is real and switches.

For a differential pair, one resistor across the pair at the receiver, centred between the two traces, with the pair passing straight through the pads so there's no stub, and it's symmetric. If the link is AC-coupled the resistor goes after the capacitors.

I also keep the plane unbroken beneath, use 0402 or smaller to minimise pad capacitance and inductance, avoid resistor arrays on fast edges because of crosstalk and longer internal leads, and check the result with a TDR or simulation."
:::

::: terms
- **In-line termination**: termination in the signal path with the net passing through it.
- **Stub**: branch from the line to the terminator.
- **Via-in-pad**: via placed inside the component pad for minimal loop inductance.
- **Thevenin termination**: resistors to supply and ground whose parallel value equals $Z_0$.
- **$V_{TT}$**: termination voltage rail.
- **Resistor array / network**: multiple resistors in one package.
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance of a component.
- **Plane cut-out (anti-pad)**: removal of plane copper under a pad to reduce capacitance.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "put them close." An exceptional answer:
- Distinguishes **in-line (series)** and **end-of-line (parallel)** layouts and says the net should **flow through** the termination.
- Quantifies the **stub rule** ($t_r/10$, mm) and the **via inductance penalty** (1 nH = 11 Ω at 1.75 GHz → $|\Gamma|=0.11$).
- Treats the **return connection and rails** (VTT, decoupling) as part of the design.
- Handles **differential symmetry** and AC-coupling order.
- Mentions **component choice** (0402/0201, no arrays) and **verification**.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Tapping off the net with a stub** to reach a terminator placed conveniently.
- **Long, thin ground connections**: a 5 mm thin trace to ground adds 3–5 nH.
- **Sharing one ground via among several resistors.** Shared inductance couples them.
- **Putting the termination before the last receiver.**
- **Using resistor arrays on fast clocks** without checking crosstalk and parasitics.
- **Placing differential termination off-centre or on one side**, causing asymmetry.
- **No decoupling on a VTT or Thevenin rail.** Termination current noise appears on the rail and modulates the signal.
:::

## Why the connection matters: a worked number

A parallel terminator is a 50 Ω resistor with a stub plus a via to ground. Use a 200 ps edge and evaluate at $f=0.35/t_r=0.35/200\text{ ps}=1.75$ GHz, which this page calls the knee frequency. (Johnson's knee, $0.5/t_r=2.5$ GHz, gives somewhat larger $|\Gamma|$, so these numbers are if anything optimistic.)

| Connection | Parasitic | Effect at 1.75 GHz | $|\Gamma|$ added |
|---|---|---|---|
| Via beside pad | 0.5 nH | $j5.5\ \Omega$ | 0.055 |
| Via 5 mm away on a thin trace | ≈ 4 nH | $j44\ \Omega$ | 0.40 |
| 0402 ESL | 0.5 nH | $j5.5\ \Omega$ | 0.055 |
| Both (1 nH) | 1 nH | $j11\ \Omega$ | 0.109 |

where $|\Gamma|=\dfrac{\omega L}{\sqrt{(2\cdot 50)^2+(\omega L)^2}}$ for the load $50+j\omega L$ on a 50 Ω line. A reflection above ~5–10% from the terminator itself is unacceptable. The remedy is geometry: via adjacent, short wide ground connection, small part.

## Return-path and reference

The termination current returns through the reference. For a ground-referenced parallel termination, that's the plane; the via should be adjacent to the pad (touching or via-in-pad with filled/capped via), and the signal's own return should be right below. For $V_{TT}$/Thevenin, the rail is a plane or island with decoupling capacitors (0.1 µF and a few µF) near the resistors; its source regulator must sink and source the termination current ([[q0503]]). If a termination current of 10–60 mA toggles with data, the rail has to hold. For differential terminations the resistor connects the two lines directly: there is no ground return, so symmetry is everything.

## Packages and arrays

0402 or 0201 resistors give the smallest pads (≈0.1–0.2 pF) and ESL (≈0.4–0.7 nH, footprint dependent). Resistor arrays save area for slow buses, but internal leads and shared common pads create crosstalk and 1–2 nH of lead inductance, so they're not recommended on fast edges.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Pad capacitance and ESL values vary with footprint, stackup and part; the quoted ranges are typical orders of magnitude. A 3D/field solver or the manufacturer's S-parameter model gives exact values.

## The terminator as a circuit: R in series with L

The table above uses a frequency-domain shortcut at the knee frequency. The time-domain picture is more instructive, and it gives you a number you can compare with a scope trace. A "50 Ω" terminator with parasitic inductance $L$ in its ground connection is $Z_L = Z_0 + sL$. Its reflection coefficient is

$$\Gamma(s)=\frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0}=\frac{sL}{sL+2Z_0}$$

For an ideal step this is $\Gamma(t)=e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = L/(2Z_0)$. The inductor starts out as an open circuit, so the first instant looks like a fully reflecting open, and then it relaxes toward a short as the current builds. With a real edge of rise time $t_r$ (a linear ramp), the reflected pulse peaks at

$$\Gamma_{pk}=\frac{\tau}{t_r}\left(1-e^{-t_r/\tau}\right)\approx\frac{L}{2Z_0t_r}\quad (t_r\gg\tau)$$

For a 200 ps edge:

| $L$ | $\tau$ | Peak reflection (time domain) | $\lvert\Gamma\rvert$ at 1.75 GHz (frequency domain) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 nH | 5 ps | 2.5% | 5.5% |
| 1 nH | 10 ps | 5.0% | 10.9% |
| 4 nH | 40 ps | 19.9% | 40% |

The two columns differ by roughly a factor of two for small $L$. That is not a contradiction; they answer different questions. The knee-frequency number is the reflection at one spectral component (and is the right one for a swept S11 measurement); the time-domain number is the peak voltage disturbance on a real ramp. Quote the second when someone asks "how many millivolts will I see on the scope". Both agree on the ranking, and both say the same thing: **inductance in the termination's return path is the thing to minimize**.

### Rough via inductance

A common closed-form estimate for a via of length $h$ and diameter $d$ (both in inches) is $L\approx 5.08\,h\,[\ln(4h/d)+1]$ nH. A 0.3 mm drill (0.0118 in) through a full 1.6 mm (0.063 in) board gives about 1.3 nH. That is the *worst* case: a via that goes the whole thickness of the board to a plane on the far side. If the ground plane is the adjacent layer, only a fraction of the barrel carries return current between the pad and the plane, and the loop is a few hundred picohenries. This is the quantitative reason to put the termination on the same side of the board as the plane it returns to (or, on a thick board, to use a buried or blind via), and why a via placed beside the pad beats one reached through a long neck.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The via inductance formula is an approximation for an isolated via; the return loop in a real design depends on where the nearest return vias and planes are. Treat it as an order-of-magnitude guide and extract the loop from a field solver for a marginal design.

## Power and package: the 0402 trap

The advice "use 0402 or smaller" collides with another physical fact. A 50 Ω resistor from a 3.3 V net to ground dissipates $V^2/R = 3.3^2/50 = 218$ mW when the net is held high, or about 109 mW at 50% duty. A typical 0402 chip resistor is rated on the order of 63 mW (check the datasheet; ratings differ with series and vendor), so a naive parallel termination of a 3.3 V CMOS line overheats a 0402 and drifts out of tolerance. Options:

- **Thevenin** (two 100 Ω resistors, 3.3 V to ground): the idle bias current costs $3.3^2/200=54$ mW, but when the line is held in one state the resistor to the *opposite* rail has the full 3.3 V across it, $3.3^2/100=109$ mW. Averaged over 50% duty that is about 54 mW per part, so the heat is shared rather than eliminated, and a held line still overloads a 0402 (a 0603 or larger is the safer choice).
- **AC termination** (50 Ω in series with a capacitor): zero DC power. Size $C$ so that $R_TC$ is several times the line's round-trip delay, so the capacitor looks like a short while each edge's reflection would arrive. The capacitor's charge then follows the data pattern, so this suits clocks and DC-balanced data better than long runs of identical bits.
- **A $V_{TT}$ rail** at half the signal swing: the current is about half (33 mA instead of 66 mA at 3.3 V into 50 Ω), because the voltage across the resistor is $V/2$ in either logic state (about 54 mW in the resistor), but the supply has to source and sink it ([[q0503]]).
- **A larger package** (0603 or 0805) for the DC-coupled case, accepting a slightly longer stub, and keeping the footprint tight.

This is a good example of an interviewer follow-up: "so what's the problem with parallel termination?" is answered by power, not by reflections.

## Debugging a termination that "doesn't work"

Layout problems have fingerprints on the scope, and an engineer should read them:

1. **Ringing at a frequency set by the stub**: a ring of period near $4t_s$ (the quarter-wave resonance of an open or lightly loaded stub), where $t_s$ is the stub's one-way delay, means a stub that is too long. Shorten or remove it.
2. **A spike at the leading edge of the receiver waveform that scales with edge rate**: the return-path inductance. Slow the driver by one drive-strength step; if the spike shrinks roughly in proportion, the cause is $L\,dI/dt$ and the fix is a better ground via.
3. **A flat-topped waveform that settles to the wrong level**: the termination resistor is too low, or the part is overheating. Measure the DC level at the receiver with the net held high; it should be $V_{OH}\cdot R_T/(R_T+R_{out})$.
4. **Differential skew or a common-mode bump**: asymmetric pad placement or one leg of the termination stubbed.

Confirm by probing at the pin of the receiver with the shortest ground spring you have, then repeating after reworking one variable (move the resistor, add a second ground via, change the value). A TDR on a test coupon laid out with the same footprint gives the termination's impedance profile directly, and is the cleanest way to settle a layout argument before spinning the board.

## Checklist

1. Net flows through the termination: no stub, no T.
2. Series resistor within ≈ $t_r/10$ delay of the driver pin.
3. End termination after the last receiver, tap $\lesssim t_r/10$.
4. Reference via adjacent to the pad; short, wide connection; one via per part if possible.
5. Decoupled VTT/Thevenin rails.
6. Differential: centred, symmetric, one resistor, after the AC caps.
7. Solid plane below; small packages; avoid arrays on fast edges.
8. Verify by simulation/TDR.
:::

::: applications
- **DDR address/command fly-by**: VTT termination resistors at the end of each net with an island and decoupling.
- **Clock buffers**: 22–33 Ω series resistors at the output pins.
- **LVDS/MIPI/HDMI receivers**: 100 Ω differential resistor at the pins (often integrated).
- **ECL/PECL (older) lines** terminated to $V_{CC}-2$ V with decoupled rails.
- **Backplane buses**: end-of-line terminators with decoupling.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes **controlled-impedance FR-4 microstrip**, resistors in 0402 or smaller, and edges of 100 ps to a few ns.
- The $t_r/10$ stub rule is a heuristic; follow interface-specific layout guides (e.g. DDR) when present.
- Parasitic values (0.5 nH vias, 0.4–0.7 nH ESL) are typical; use manufacturer models for tight designs.
- Receivers with **ODT** need no external parallel termination, only the series resistor if the standard calls for one.
- Slow edges ($t_d<t_r/6$) make layout of termination largely irrelevant.
:::

::: followups
### Can a termination resistor be via-in-pad?
Yes, with filled and capped vias (VIPPO), it minimises the loop inductance to the plane. It costs extra processing, and solder wicking into an unfilled via is a defect, so I use it only when the edge rate demands, otherwise a via directly beside the pad.

### How would you lay out a Thevenin termination pair?
Two resistors (e.g. 100 Ω each to $V_{CC}$ and ground for an effective 50 Ω) at the end of the line, adjacent to the receiver pad, each with its own via to its rail, and a decoupling capacitor on $V_{CC}$ next to them. They draw continuous current ($V_{CC}/200$ Ω = 16.5 mA at 3.3 V), so the rail must be solid.

### What if the receiver is a BGA and there's no room for a resistor near the pin?
Use ODT if the part has it; otherwise route the net through a via to the opposite side and place the resistor in-line directly under the BGA ball, via-in-pad if needed; or rely on a series resistor at the source. A far-away terminator on a stub does harm.

### How do you route the termination of a differential pair that passes through AC-coupling capacitors?
Order: driver → pair → AC caps → receiver pins → termination (often on-die). The caps are symmetric and close to each other; the termination resistor, if external, is after the caps, across the pair at the receiver.

### What changes for a 10 Gb/s edge (30 ps)?
Everything gets tighter: the stub limit becomes 0.5 mm, 0.5 nH is about 37 Ω at 11.7 GHz, and external terminators become impractical: use on-die termination and keep external parts only as AC caps and ESD with careful footprints.
:::
