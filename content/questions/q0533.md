::: testing
Whether you can compare the two fundamental schemes by **mechanism and consequence**: series absorbs the reflection at the *source* (half-step launched, full step only at the far end, no DC power, receiver must be at the end), parallel absorbs the wave at the *load* (full step all along the line, DC power, reduced levels, works for multi-drop). The best answers back it with a worked waveform, power and level numbers, and a recommendation by topology.
:::

::: why
This is the single most common termination decision on a real board, and the interview-standard comparison. The wrong choice either burns power for nothing (parallel on a point-to-point CMOS net) or gives glitches on a bus (series with mid-line receivers). The same question appears from the theory side in [[q0716]]; this page is the board-level, layout-aware version.
:::

::: concepts
- **Reflection coefficient and matching**: $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$. See [[q0711]].
- **Series termination**: makes $R_{out}+R_s=Z_0$ so $\Gamma_s=0$. See [[q0716]].
- **Parallel termination**: $R_T=Z_0$ at the end so $\Gamma_L=0$. See [[q0717]].
- **Incident-wave switching**: receivers must switch on the first incident wave. 
- **DC power and levels**: resistive load vs none. See [[q0532]].
- **Placement**: source vs end. See [[q0529]].
:::

::: answer
"Both stop reflections, but at different ends. Series termination is a resistor at the driver so that the driver plus the resistor equals the line impedance. The driver launches only half the step into the line, at the open far end the wave doubles to full amplitude, and the reflection returns to the source where it's absorbed. So there's no ringing, no DC power, just one resistor. But the line sees a half-height step for a round trip, so any receiver in the middle sees a staircase and a wrong or late switch. Only the receiver at the very end sees a clean edge.

Parallel termination is a resistor equal to Z0 at the end of the line. The wave arrives full amplitude and is absorbed, so every receiver along the line sees a clean full step on the first pass. The costs: the driver now sees a DC load, so power is real, 3.3 volts into 50 ohms is 66 milliamps, and the high level drops because the driver and the resistor form a divider; Thevenin, VTT and AC variants reduce that.

So: point-to-point, power-sensitive: series. Multi-drop buses or when the signal must be clean along the line, or high-speed differential links: parallel at the end. And I'll often use series for clocks because it also slows the edge a little and helps EMI."
:::

::: technical
**Series.** $R_s=Z_0-R_{out}$. Launch: $V_0=V\,Z_0/(R_{out}+R_s+Z_0)=V/2$. At an open load: $V_L=2V_0=V$ at $t_d$. Reflection returns at $2t_d$, absorbed since $\Gamma_s=0$. Source sees $V/2$ for $2t_d$ then $V$. Mid-line receiver at distance $x$: sees $V/2$ at $t_x$ then $V$ at $2t_d-t_x$; a mid-line threshold near $V/2$ risks a stall or double-switch. DC power ≈ 0. Receiver capacitance at the open end charges through the line's impedance, so the load edge is slowed by $\tau\approx Z_0C_{in}$ (e.g. 50 Ω × 5 pF = 250 ps); with a parallel terminator the same capacitance sees $Z_0/2$, so $\tau\approx Z_0C_{in}/2$.

**Parallel.** $R_T=Z_0$ at the end. Load sees $V_L=V\,R_T/(R_T+R_{out})$ after $t_d$, and no reflection ($\Gamma_L=0$); nothing returns to the source, so $R_{out}$ need not match. DC: $I=V/(R_T+R_{out})$: for 3.3 V, 20 Ω + 50 Ω, 47 mA, 2.36 V high level. Average power in the resistor with 50% duty ≈ 56 mW (3.3 V, 50 Ω gives 109 mW if driver ideal).

**Comparison** (3.3 V, $R_{out}=20$ Ω, $Z_0=50$ Ω):
| | Series ($R_s=30$ Ω) | Parallel to GND (50 Ω) |
|---|---|---|
| First wave at source | 1.65 V | 2.36 V |
| Load voltage at $t_d$ | 3.30 V | 2.36 V (no overshoot) |
| Overshoot / ringing | none | none |
| DC power | ≈ 0 | ≈ 56–110 mW |
| Mid-line receivers | see half step for $2t_d$ | see full step |
| Components | 1 R at source | 1 R at end (+ rail) |
| Noise margin | full swing | reduced $V_{OH}$ |
| Edge at a capacitive receiver | $\tau\approx Z_0C_{in}$ | $\tau\approx Z_0C_{in}/2$ |

**Without termination**: first wave $2.357$ V, load $4.71$ V ($+43\%$), ringing at $4t_d$ decaying by $0.43$ per round trip.
:::

::: short
"Series termination puts a resistor at the driver so driver plus resistor equals Z0: the line gets a half-amplitude step, it doubles at the far end, and the reflection is absorbed at the source. It costs no DC power, but only the end receiver sees a clean edge. Parallel termination puts Z0 at the end of the line: the full step arrives and is absorbed, so any receiver along the line sees it cleanly, but the driver feeds a DC load and the high level drops. Series for point-to-point and low power; parallel for multi-drop or when a clean wave along the line matters."
:::

::: long
"Both are matching schemes; they differ in where the reflection is absorbed.

With series termination I add a resistor at the driver so that its output impedance plus the resistor equals the line impedance. When the driver switches, the line sees a voltage divider between the source and Z0, so a half-amplitude step travels down the line. At the far end, which is an open or a high-impedance receiver, the voltage doubles to full amplitude. The reflected wave travels back and meets a matched source, where it's absorbed, so there's no ringing. There's no DC current, it's one resistor, and it slows the edge slightly because of the resistor with the input capacitance. The catch is that anywhere along the line before the end, the voltage sits at half amplitude for a round-trip time, so any receiver there could mis-switch. It only suits one receiver at the end.

With parallel termination I put a resistor of Z0 at the end of the line. The full-amplitude wave arrives and is absorbed, so every receiver along the line sees a clean full step on the first pass, which is what multi-drop buses need. The cost is the DC load: with a 20 ohm CMOS driver and 50 ohm termination to ground, I draw 47 milliamps and the high level falls from 3.3 volts to about 2.4. Variants like Thevenin, VTT and AC termination reduce that.

My choice follows the topology. Point-to-point CMOS: series, it's cheap and quiet, and it also reduces EMI. Multi-drop or fly-by buses: parallel at the end. High-speed differential: a 100 ohm differential termination, which is the parallel idea. And I verify with a scope at the receiver."
:::

::: terms
- **Series (source) termination**: resistor at the driver; absorbs the returning reflection.
- **Parallel (load) termination**: resistor at the end; absorbs the incident wave.
- **Incident-wave switching**: receiver switches on the first arriving wave; needed for multi-drop.
- **Reflected-wave switching**: receiver reaches its threshold only after the reflection arrives (series, end of line).
- **Launch amplitude**: voltage of the first wave on the line, $V Z_0/(R_s+Z_0)$.
- **$V_{OH}$ degradation**: lower high level due to the divider with a DC termination.
- **Round-trip time**: $2t_d$.
- **Thevenin/VTT/AC**: variants of parallel termination reducing DC power.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "series at source, parallel at load." An exceptional answer:
- Explains **mechanism**: half-step launch and doubling at the open end vs full-step absorption.
- Gives the **mid-line consequence** (half-amplitude plateau for $2t_d$) and why series fails on multi-drop.
- Gives **numbers**: $R_s=Z_0-R_{out}$, 66 mA / 2.36 V for parallel, ≈ 0 power for series, +43% overshoot unterminated.
- Notes **side effects**: edge slowing by the receiver capacitance ($\approx Z_0C_{in}$), EMI benefit of series, $V_{OH}$ loss, and the variants (Thevenin, VTT, AC).
- Matches choice to **topology** and verifies on a scope.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting series termination on a multi-drop bus.** Mid-line receivers see a half-step.
- **Forgetting the driver's own impedance** when sizing $R_s$ (use $Z_0-R_{out}$, not $Z_0$).
- **Using parallel to ground with a weak CMOS driver.** The high level collapses below $V_{IH}$.
- **Expecting series termination to give a clean waveform at the source.** The source waveform shows the half-step and the returning reflection; probe at the load.
- **Terminating at both ends with matched resistors "to be safe".** It halves the swing and wastes power.
- **Ignoring the receiver-capacitance delay** on a fast clock: at the open end $C_{in}$ charges through $Z_0$, so 50 Ω × 5 pF ≈ 250 ps.
:::

::: deep
## Waveforms at the receiver

Take a 3.3 V step, $R_{out}=20\ \Omega$, $Z_0=50\ \Omega$, $t_d=1$ ns, receiver at the end (open, ideal).

- **Unterminated:** launch $V_0=3.3\times50/70=2.357$ V; $\Gamma_s=-0.4286$, $\Gamma_L=+1$. Load voltage $=2V_0\sum\Gamma_s^k$ steps: 4.71 V at $t_d$, 2.69 V at $3t_d$, 3.56 V, 3.19 V... converging to 3.3 V.
- **Series ($R_s=30$ Ω):** $V_0=3.3\times50/100=1.65$ V; $\Gamma_s=0$; load jumps to 3.3 V at $t_d$ and stays.
- **Parallel (50 Ω to ground):** $V_0=2.357$ V; $\Gamma_L=0$; load sits at 2.357 V from $t_d$.

## Where each wave is, along the line

For series termination, the voltage along the line is $V/2$ in the region the first wave has covered, and the full $V$ only after the reflection returns. At a receiver at fraction $a$ of the line it is $V/2$ from $at_d$ to $(2-a)t_d$ and $V$ afterwards. At the middle ($a=0.5$) the half level lasts 1 ns of a 2 ns round trip.

## Numbers that decide the choice

| Metric | Series | Parallel (GND) | Parallel (Thevenin 100/100) |
|---|---|---|---|
| DC load, driver high | ≈ 0 | 47 mA, ≈ 111 mW in $R_T$ | ≈ 28 mA in $R_2$ (≈ 80 mW), ≈ 4.7 mA through $R_1$ |
| $V_{OH}$ | 3.30 V | 2.36 V | 2.83 V |
| $V_{OL}$ | 0 V | 0 V | 0.47 V |
| Mid-line receivers | half-step stall | clean | clean |
| Edge slowing by receiver $C_{in}$ | $\approx Z_0C_{in}$ | $\approx Z_0C_{in}/2$ | $\approx Z_0C_{in}/2$ |
| Parts | 1 | 1 | 2 |

Check of the parallel numbers: $I=3.3/(20+50)=47.1$ mA; power in $R_T$: $I^2R=111$ mW while high, about 56 mW averaged at 50% duty.

## EMI and robustness

Series resistors slow the edge a little (with load capacitance) and reduce the launched amplitude, cutting high-frequency content and radiated EMI; parallel terminations keep the full edge rate and add DC current loops. Therefore series termination on clocks is a common EMI measure, and parallel termination is preferred where the wave must be clean and fast on a long bus.

## Differential and multi-drop

A differential pair is conceptually parallel-terminated: a 100 Ω resistor across the receiver absorbs the incident wave, because a series scheme doesn't make sense for complementary drivers into high-impedance inputs (and the receivers need to switch on the first wave). A daisy-chained bus ends in a parallel terminator; DDR fly-by uses $V_{TT}$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Power and level numbers use ideal linear driver impedance (20 Ω); real CMOS outputs are nonlinear and depend on voltage, process and temperature, so use the IBIS model for sign-off.

## Real hardware behaviour

- **Driver impedance spread** (±20–30%) means a series resistor leaves a small residual reflection ($\Gamma_s=\Delta R/(2Z_0)$): ±5 Ω gives ±5%.
- **Receiver capacitance** at the end makes the end-of-line response an RC with the line, so the final value isn't exactly a doubling; the simulation with IBIS/package models accounts for it.
- **Probing at the driver** of a series-terminated line shows the staircase, which new engineers misread as a fault.

## Worked example: a 32-bit bus and a mid-line receiver

Suppose a board has a 32-bit unidirectional data bus, each bit a 3.3 V, 20 Ω driver into a 50 Ω line with $t_d=1$ ns, driving one receiver at the end with $C_{in}=4$ pF. Compare the two schemes on cost and behavior.

**Power.** Parallel-to-ground burns $I^2R_T=111$ mW per bit while high; with random data (50% high) that averages 55.5 mW, or $32\times0.0555=1.78$ W for the bus. Thevenin 100/100 draws about $3.3^2/200=54$ mW per bit even when the driver is idle, which is $\approx1.7$ W for the bus. Series termination draws essentially nothing at DC. On a battery-powered product the choice is made by that 1.7 W alone.

**Edge slowing from receiver capacitance.** At the end of a series-terminated line the receiver capacitance is charged through the line's $Z_0$ (the doubled wave behind $Z_0$), giving $\tau\approx Z_0C_{in}=50\times4\text{ pF}=200$ ps, a 10–90% rise time of about $2.2\tau=0.44$ ns on top of the driver edge. With parallel termination the capacitance sees $Z_0\parallel R_T=25$ Ω, so $\tau=100$ ps and $t_{10\text{-}90}\approx0.22$ ns. Both slow the signal, but the series scheme costs about twice as much edge degradation, which can limit the maximum data rate of a fast bus. At a 100 MHz bus with a 10 ns period, 0.44 ns is a trivial 4.4% of the bit time; at 1 GHz it eats 44% of the bit time and series termination is no longer a good idea.

**A receiver partway down the line.** Put a second receiver at $a=0.8$ of the line. For series termination it sees the half-level (1.65 V) from $0.8t_d=0.8$ ns until $(2-0.8)t_d=1.2$ ns: a 0.4 ns plateau right around the switching threshold of a 3.3 V CMOS input (about 1.65 V). A slow input or a clock pin will chatter or double-clock. With parallel termination the first wave already has the full amplitude at every tap and the line stays clean. This is the entire reason series termination is limited to point-to-point routes.

## Limiting cases

- **$R_{out}\to Z_0$:** the driver itself is a series termination, no added resistor, and both $\Gamma_s=0$ and the half-step launch happen automatically. Many modern drivers (DDR, LVCMOS with programmable drive strength) are designed this way.
- **$R_{out}\to0$ (very strong driver):** $\Gamma_s\to-1$, the line rings with large amplitude and the series resistor needs to be nearly all of $Z_0$ (and dissipates heat only on edges).
- **$t_d\ll t_r$:** neither is needed. The reflections arrive before the edge finishes and merge into it.
- **$R_T\to\infty$ or $R_s\to0$:** you are back to the unterminated line of the first waveform.

## Bench check: telling them apart on a scope

Probe both ends. Series-terminated line: the source end shows the half-step plateau for $2t_d$, then climbs to the full level; the far end shows one clean step. Parallel-terminated: both ends show the full step (the near end at the divided level), no plateau. If you see a plateau at the *far* end of a line meant to be terminated, the termination is mis-valued or in the wrong place. If the far-end level settles near 1.6 V rather than 3.3 V (the 53 Ω of driver plus series resistor dividing against 50 Ω), check that you did not leave a parallel-to-ground resistor on a line that was designed for series termination; a 50 Ω part left on the net is a classic assembly or schematic-reuse bug, and a multimeter measuring 50 Ω from the net to ground with power off finds it in seconds.
:::

::: applications
- **MCU/FPGA clock outputs**: 22–33 Ω series resistors at the pin.
- **SPI/parallel buses point-to-point**: series resistors on SCK and data.
- **DDR fly-by command/address buses**: parallel $V_{TT}$ terminations.
- **LVDS/PCIe/USB pairs**: differential parallel termination (on-die).
- **Legacy multi-drop backplanes (GTL, PECL)**: end-of-line parallel termination.
:::

::: assumptions
- Linear driver of impedance $R_{out}=20$ Ω, ideal line and open (high-Z, low-C) receiver.
- Single receiver at the end for series termination; multi-drop is discussed qualitatively.
- Numbers for power use 3.3 V and 50% duty; real data patterns differ.
- For lines short compared with the edge ($t_d<t_r/6$), neither termination is needed ([[q0531]]).
:::

::: followups
### Why does the series-terminated line launch half amplitude?
The driver (20 Ω) plus $R_s$ (30 Ω) is 50 Ω in series with the line's 50 Ω: a 1:1 divider, so the line sees $V/2$. The doubling at the open end brings it to $V$.

### Can I use series termination with two receivers, one at the end and one in the middle?
The middle receiver sees the half-step for a round-trip time, and in the region of its threshold the output may chatter or switch late. If the half-level is below $V_{IL}$ and the reflection arrives fast enough relative to its timing budget it can work, but that's fragile: use parallel termination or fly-by.

### How do I reduce the power of a parallel termination?
Use Thevenin to halve the DC load, terminate to mid-rail $V_{TT}$ (needs a regulator), or AC termination with a capacitor. Or lower the signal swing (e.g. LVDS, 350 mV).

### What if the driver impedance varies 15–30 Ω?
$R_s=30$ Ω then gives a total of 45–60 Ω, $\Gamma_s$ from −0.05 to +0.09: small residual reflection, so fine. For a precise match, use drivers with calibrated impedance (ZQ calibration) or on-die series termination.

### Why use series termination for clocks?
It prevents ringing with a single resistor, no DC power and slows the edge slightly (EMI benefit). The clock's only receiver is usually at the end; if the clock must go to several receivers, I use a clock buffer to give each its own point-to-point series-terminated line.
