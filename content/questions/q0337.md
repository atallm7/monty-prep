::: basics
### In plain words
A DC motor draws the least current when it spins freely at its top speed with nothing to push against. By then its own back-EMF almost cancels the supply voltage, so only a small current is left to overcome friction. A solenoid draws no current when it is off, and the smallest useful current is a low holding current once it has pulled in.

### Picture it
Imagine pedaling a bike on a smooth flat road at your top speed. You barely push, just enough to beat the friction and the wind. Add a hill or a headwind and you must push harder. A free-running motor is in that easy state. If something pushes the bike downhill faster than you can pedal, the bike pushes you, and the motor can even act as a generator.

### Words you need
- **No-load speed**: the speed a motor reaches when it has no mechanical load.
- **Back-EMF**: the voltage a spinning motor generates, close to the supply at no load ([[q0336]]).
- **Friction and windage**: the small drag from bearings and air that a free-running motor still overcomes.
- **Holding current**: the lower current that keeps a solenoid pulled in.
- **PWM**: switching the voltage on and off quickly so the average voltage and current are lower.

### The simplest example
With a 12 V supply and a 1 Ω motor, the current at stall is 12 A. At no load, the back-EMF is about 11.5 V, so the current is $(12 - 11.5) / 1 = 0.5$ A. A 24 V, 48 Ω solenoid draws $24 / 48 = 0.5$ A and 12 W while on. Once pulled in, switching it with 20% PWM cuts the average current to $0.2 \times 0.5 = 0.1$ A, and the power to about 0.48 W.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers the no-load speed, the instant of switch-on, regeneration and how a rising no-load current warns of trouble.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can read the same model in reverse: if peak current is at zero speed, where is the minimum? The hidden sub-skills:

1. You know the free-running (no-load) motor draws only the current needed for friction and windage, because back-EMF almost cancels the supply.
2. You can distinguish the **instant** minimum (current is zero at $t = 0^+$ because an inductor resists change) from the **steady-state** minimum (no-load current).
3. You can go beyond the obvious: the current can even go **negative** (regeneration), and a solenoid's minimum is zero when off, or the hold current when it is PWM-reduced.
:::

::: why
The minimum current sets the idle power of a drive, determines how much current the controller must resolve (sensing at both ends of the range), explains battery life at light load, and is a diagnostic: a motor with unusually high no-load current has a mechanical problem (friction, bad bearing, shorted turns). It is also the foundation for speed estimation from current. The question comes right after "most current" to test that you understand the full operating range.
:::

::: concepts
- **Motor equation**: $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$; current falls as speed rises. See [[q0336]].
- **Back-EMF**: $K_e\omega \to V$ at no-load speed. See [[q0341]].
- **Torque from current**: $\tau = K_tI$, so no-load current only supplies friction. See [[q0334]].
- **Inductor continuity**: current cannot jump, so it is zero at $t=0^+$. See [[q0010]].
- **Regeneration**: if driven faster than the supply speed, the motor generates and current reverses.
:::

::: answer
"For a motor there are really two answers. In steady state, the least current is at the highest speed with no mechanical load, the free-running or no-load condition. By then the back-EMF is almost equal to the supply voltage, so the voltage across the winding resistance is tiny and the current is just enough for the torque to overcome friction and windage. For my 12 V, 1 ohm example it's around half an amp, compared to 12 amps at stall.

The other answer is the instant you switch on: because the coil is an inductor, the current starts from zero and rises with time constant L over R. So at that first instant the current is as low as it can get, zero, even though it's about to climb to its peak.

And if you want to be pedantic, if something drives the shaft faster than the no-load speed, say a load pushing it downhill, the back-EMF exceeds the supply and the current reverses: the motor becomes a generator.

For a solenoid, the least current is zero when it's off, and when it's on, the lowest useful current is the hold current, which you can get by reducing the voltage with PWM once it has pulled in."
:::

::: technical
**Motor, steady state.** $I = \dfrac{V - K_e\omega}{R} = \dfrac{\tau_L + \tau_f}{K_t}$.

- Minimum with the shaft free: $\tau_L = 0$, so $I_{nl} = \tau_f/K_t$ (friction and windage) and $\omega_{nl} = (V - I_{nl}R)/K_e$.
- Example: $V = 12$ V, $R = 1\ \Omega$, $K = 0.02$, $\tau_f = 0.01$ N·m: $I_{nl} = 0.5$ A, $\omega_{nl} = (12 - 0.5)/0.02 = 575$ rad/s (5490 rpm). The ideal (frictionless) speed is $V/K_e = 600$ rad/s.
- Power at no load: input $VI = 6$ W; copper loss $I^2R = 0.25$ W; the other 5.75 W goes into friction (efficiency 0% at no load, since no useful output).

**Motor, transient.** $i(0^+) = 0$ since $L$ opposes instantaneous change. The current then rises with $\tau_e = L/R$.

**Regeneration.** If an external torque drives the shaft above $\omega = V/K_e$ (for example 650 rad/s), the back-EMF is $13$ V and $I = (12 - 13)/1 = -1$ A: current flows into the supply, and the motor brakes the load. A bench supply cannot absorb it; a battery can.

**Solenoid.** Off: $I = 0$. On: during the rise $i < V/R$; after pull-in the force needed to hold is small because the gap is small, so the current can be cut with PWM or an economizer. With PWM duty $D$ at high frequency (small ripple) the average current is $DV/R$ and the power $D^2V^2/R$. Example: 24 V, 48 Ω solenoid at $D = 0.2$: $I = 0.1$ A, $P = 0.48$ W instead of 12 W.

**Dependencies.** Higher supply voltage raises no-load speed but not no-load current much; higher friction (cold grease, wear, bad bearings) raises $I_{nl}$; shorted turns raise it too. A rising no-load current is a diagnostic.
:::

::: short
"For a motor, the steady-state minimum is at no load and top speed: the back-EMF almost equals the supply, so the current is just what's needed to overcome friction, about half an amp versus 12 amps at stall in my example. The absolute minimum is the instant of turn-on, when the inductor makes the current start at zero. If the shaft is driven faster than no-load speed, the current goes negative: regeneration. For a solenoid it's zero when off, or the small PWM hold current once it has pulled in."
:::

::: long
"Let me use the motor model: the current is the supply voltage minus the back-EMF, divided by the winding resistance. The back-EMF is K_e times speed, so the more the motor speeds up, the less voltage is left across the resistance, and the less current. The lowest steady-state current is therefore at the highest speed, which is the no-load condition: nothing on the shaft but friction and windage. There the back-EMF is almost equal to the supply, and the current is just the friction torque divided by K_t. For a 12 volt, 1 ohm motor with K of 0.02 and 10 milli-newton-metres of friction that's about 0.5 amps, at about 575 radians per second, 5500 rpm. Compare that to 12 amps at stall.

There's a second, transient answer. The winding is an inductor, so at the instant you switch on the current starts at zero and climbs with L over R. So technically the least current is the very first instant.

And there's a third case: if something drives the shaft above the ideal speed V over K_e, say a vehicle going downhill, the back-EMF exceeds the supply and the current flips negative. The motor is generating and pushing current back into the supply, braking the load. A battery can absorb that, a plain bench supply can't, so the bus voltage rises.

For a solenoid, the least current is zero when it's off. When it's on, the minimum you want is the hold current: once the plunger has closed, the air gap is small and a fraction of the pull-in current is enough, so you drop the average voltage with PWM and cut the power a lot."
:::

::: terms
- **No-load current $I_{nl}$**: current at free-running speed, supplying only friction and windage.
- **No-load speed**: highest steady speed with no load on the shaft.
- **Ideal speed $V/K_e$**: speed at which back-EMF equals the supply (frictionless).
- **Regeneration**: operation where the motor returns power to the source, current negative.
- **Windage**: air drag on the rotor.
- **Hold current**: reduced current that keeps a solenoid seated.
- **Economizer**: circuit that reduces coil current after pull-in.
- **Continuity of inductor current**: $i(0^+) = i(0^-)$.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "when it's running with no load".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives it from $I = (V - K_e\omega)/R$ and gives the **no-load current** as $\tau_f/K_t$ with a number.
- Separates the **steady-state** minimum from the **instantaneous** minimum at turn-on (zero by inductor continuity).
- Brings up **regeneration**: current can be negative when driven above $V/K_e$ and what the supply must be able to do.
- Uses it as a **diagnostic** (rising no-load current signals friction or shorted turns).
- Gives the solenoid case with **hold current and PWM economizing** and the $D^2V^2/R$ power.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Minimum current is zero at no load."** Friction and windage still need torque, so $I_{nl} > 0$; zero is the *ideal* frictionless limit.
- **"No load means no power."** The motor still draws $VI_{nl}$ watts, all of it ends up as friction heat and copper loss.
- **Confusing the low-speed state with low current.** Low speed means high current.
- **Ignoring regeneration.** The current can go negative; a supply that cannot sink current will see its rail rise.
- **"Solenoids draw less when the plunger is closed because the force is higher."** Current is $V/R$ regardless of position at DC steady state; you must *reduce the voltage* to reduce the current.
- **Assuming PWM hold power scales with duty.** With a smoothing inductor the power scales with $D^2$ (average current squared times R).
:::

## The current-speed line

Rearranging the steady-state equation gives a straight line from stall to the ideal speed:

$$I(\omega) = \frac{V - K_e\omega}{R}$$

The friction torque lets you calculate the real operating point: set the current to $\tau_f/K_t$ and solve for $\omega$.

## Where the power goes at no load

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Input power $VI$ | $12\times0.5 = 6$ W |
| Copper loss $I^2R$ | $0.25$ W |
| Back-EMF power $K_e\omega I$ | $11.5\times0.5 = 5.75$ W |
| Useful output | 0 (friction and windage convert all 5.75 W to heat) |

So an unloaded motor is not "free": it is a 6 W heater in this example. Overall efficiency is zero at no load, zero at stall (everything is copper loss), and peaks in between (the sim's curves view shows this).

## The instant of turn-on

The first instant is the minimum for a different reason: the winding inductance. For the same motor the current at $t = 0^+$ is zero and reaches 1 A after $-\tau_e\ln(1 - 1/12) \approx 0.087$ ms, with $\tau_e = L/R = 1$ ms. It is the least current *of the transient*, but it only lasts microseconds. So when someone asks for "least current" in a design discussion they usually mean the no-load steady state; mention the instant as a point about inductors.

## Regeneration

When the shaft is driven above the speed where $K_e\omega = V$, the motor is a generator. Example at 650 rad/s: $e = 13$ V, $I = -1$ A, so $P = 12\times(-1) = -12$ W: 12 W flows back into the source. The shaft has to supply the 13 W converted by the back-EMF ($eI$) plus friction, and 1 W of that is lost as copper heat. With a battery this charges it (regenerative braking). With a diode-fed supply, the current has nowhere to go and the bus voltage rises until something conducts or breaks.

## The solenoid's minimum

A solenoid has no speed-dependent term, so there are only three distinct current levels:

- **Off:** zero.
- **Pull-in:** the current needed to start motion, supplied by full voltage.
- **Hold:** much lower, since closing the gap multiplies the flux for the same $NI$.

An economizer or PWM drops the average voltage after pull-in. At $D = 0.2$ on a 24 V, 48 Ω coil the average current is 0.1 A and the power is $0.1^2\times48 = 0.48$ W, versus 12 W at DC: a 25-fold cut with 5 times lower current. The catch: the hold current must remain above the drop-out threshold including vibration and temperature margins (hot coil resistance rises but hold force depends on $NI$, so as the coil heats the current falls at fixed voltage; allow margin).

## Using the minimum as information

- **Motor health:** a rising no-load current indicates more friction (dry bearing, contamination) or shorted turns.
- **Speed estimation:** with known $K_e$ and $R$, $\omega \approx (V - IR)/K_e$ from measured current (a crude sensorless speed estimate).
- **Sensing range:** a current sensor must resolve 0.5 A at idle and survive 12 A at stall, a 24:1 dynamic range; resolution matters at the low end.

## Adding viscous friction: the honest no-load point

The page so far treats friction as a constant torque. Real bearings, brush contact and air drag add a term that grows with speed, $\tau_f = \tau_c + b\,\omega$. Substitute into the steady-state balance $K_t I = \tau_f$ together with $V = IR + K_e\omega$ and solve for the speed:

$$\omega_{nl} = \frac{V - R\tau_c/K_t}{K_e + Rb/K_t}, \qquad I_{nl} = \frac{\tau_c + b\,\omega_{nl}}{K_t}$$

Take the same motor with $\tau_c = 10$ mN·m and a modest $b = 10^{-5}$ N·m·s/rad. The denominator becomes $0.02 + 1\times10^{-5}/0.02 = 0.0205$, so $\omega_{nl} = 11.5/0.0205 \approx 561$ rad/s (5,357 rpm) and $I_{nl} = (0.01 + 10^{-5}\times561)/0.02 \approx 0.78$ A. The viscous term alone added 0.28 A, a 56% increase over the constant-friction estimate, even though at 561 rad/s it is only 5.6 mN·m. The lesson: a datasheet's no-load current is a property of the motor at its no-load speed on that supply voltage. Run it at 6 V and the speed halves, the drag drops, and $I_{nl}$ falls toward $\tau_c/K_t$. This is also why the "supply voltage barely changes no-load current" statement in the follow-ups is only approximately true.

## The efficiency sweet spot

Since efficiency is zero at both ends of the current range, there must be a maximum between them. With constant friction, mechanical output is $P_{out} = (K_tI - \tau_c)\,\omega$ and the input is $VI$. Using the linear current-speed line, differentiating with respect to $I$ gives the optimum at

$$I_{opt} = \sqrt{I_{nl}\,I_{stall}}, \qquad \eta_{max} = \left(1 - \sqrt{I_{nl}/I_{stall}}\right)^2$$

Numbers for the constant-friction motor: $I_{opt} = \sqrt{0.5\times12} = 2.45$ A, where $\omega = (12 - 2.45)/0.02 = 478$ rad/s and $P_{out} = (0.02\times2.45 - 0.01)\times478 = 18.6$ W from $29.4$ W in, so $\eta_{max} = 63.3\%$, matching $(1 - 0.204)^2$. At 1 A the efficiency is only 46% and at 5 A it is 53%, so the peak is broad but real. Notice what this says about design: a motor sized so its normal load sits at 2 to 3 A is operating where the minimum-current floor does least harm. A motor running at 0.8 A is mostly a friction heater.

## Where the simple picture breaks

- **Brush and contact drop.** A brushed motor has roughly a volt or so of brush drop that does not scale with current (vendor- and brush-material-dependent). It acts like a small constant subtraction from $V$ and makes very low currents less linear.
- **Temperature.** Copper resistance rises by about 0.393% per kelvin. At 80 °C (60 K above 20 °C) the winding is 1.236 Ω, so stall current falls from 12 A to 9.7 A. The no-load current barely moves because $IR$ is only 0.5 to 0.8 V of the 12 V. Bearing grease is the opposite: cold, it stiffens and $I_{nl}$ can rise noticeably on a cold start.
- **Magnet strength.** Neodymium magnets lose flux as they heat, which lowers $K_t$ and $K_e$ together. A lower $K_e$ pushes the no-load speed up while the current needed for the same friction torque goes up.
- **Commutation and PWM ripple.** A PWM-driven motor draws a pulsating current. The average still obeys the equations, but a meter reading RMS or an unfiltered sense amp will read high at light load, where ripple is a large fraction of 0.5 A.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The size of brush drop, the viscous coefficient $b$, and the temperature coefficient of magnet flux all depend on the specific motor and magnet grade. Treat the numbers above as representative of the method; for a real part, measure.

## Verifying it on the bench

1. Run the motor from a supply with a current display or a 0.1 Ω series shunt, shaft free, and wait for the current to settle (bearings warm up and the reading drifts for a minute or two). Record voltage, current and speed from a tach or an optical encoder.
2. Repeat at three or four voltages. Plot current against speed. A flat line at $\tau_c/K_t$ means friction is constant. A line with slope $b/K_t$ per rad/s means viscous drag; fit it and you have $\tau_c$ and $b$.
3. For the sense resistor, a 12-bit ADC over a 12 A full scale has a step of about 2.9 mA, which resolves 0.5 A to under 1%. A 0.1 Ω shunt drops only 50 mV at 0.5 A, so offset and amplifier drift matter more than the ADC.
4. Check the turn-on instant with a scope and current probe: the current should leave zero with slope $V/L$ and bend over at $L/R$ (1 ms here). If it does not start at zero, the probe is picking up capacitive spikes from the switch.
5. To see regeneration, spin the shaft with another motor or a drill above $V/K_e$ with a battery as the source and watch the current meter go negative. Do not try this with a bench supply that has no sink: its output will climb.

::: applications
- **Battery-powered devices**: no-load current determines idle runtime (a fan or pump that is "just spinning").
- **Motor diagnostics**: production test measuring no-load current and speed against limits to catch bad bearings or winding defects.
- **Regenerative braking**: e-bikes, EVs and elevators where negative current charges a battery or dissipates in a brake resistor.
- **Solenoid valves and locks**: economizer circuits that cut hold current to reduce heating.
- **Sensorless speed estimation**: using $V$, $I$, $R$ and $K_e$ to infer speed in cheap drives.
:::

::: assumptions
- The numbers use the same example motor as the rest of the section (12 V, 1 Ω, $K = 0.02$, 10 mN·m friction); real values vary.
- Friction is modelled as a constant (Coulomb) torque; viscous and windage losses grow with speed, so $I_{nl}$ rises a little with speed.
- PWM power formula assumes high enough frequency that current ripple is small.
- The supply can source current but not necessarily sink it; regeneration needs a battery or brake chopper.
- AC solenoids and induction motors have different no-load behaviour (magnetizing current).
:::

::: followups
### Why does a motor with no load still draw current?
Friction in the bearings and brushes, windage, and iron losses need torque even with nothing attached, so $I_{nl} = \tau_f/K_t$. In the example, 10 mN·m over 0.02 N·m/A is 0.5 A, around 6 W of input power.

### The no-load current of a new motor batch is double the old one. What are your suspects?
Higher friction (brush pressure, tight bearings, grease), misassembled shaft, or electrical issues: shorted turns lowering $K_t$ (more current for the same torque), or weak magnets. Measure speed too: lower no-load speed with higher current suggests mechanical drag; higher speed suggests weak magnets.

### What happens to the current if you increase the supply voltage with no load?
$I_{nl}$ stays about the same (set by friction), but the no-load speed rises roughly in proportion to $V$ (since $\omega \approx (V - I_{nl}R)/K_e$). Input power rises because friction losses grow with speed.

### How would you design a solenoid driver to minimize hold power?
Apply full voltage for the pull-in time (measured or from the current dip), then switch to PWM at a few kHz with enough duty to keep current above the guaranteed hold level plus margin. Use a flyback diode or active clamp for the off intervals; choose the frequency above audible range and with ripple small relative to hold current.

### Scenario: your battery-powered robot shows negative motor current when it rolls downhill. Is this a bug?
No: the motor is regenerating because the wheel is driven faster than the speed set by the drive voltage. Check the driver and battery can absorb it; if the battery is full or the BMS blocks charging, the bus voltage can rise and trip over-voltage protection. Add a brake resistor or limit regen.

### Why isn't no-load efficiency meaningful?
Efficiency is output over input. With no load there is no useful mechanical output, so the efficiency is 0% by definition even though the motor spends power; the useful metrics are no-load current and no-load speed.
:::

"Least amount of current" could mean the **steady-state** minimum (no load), the **instantaneous** minimum at turn-on (zero), or a **signed** minimum (negative current in regeneration). The page covers all three and notes which one is likely intended: the no-load free-running condition. For the opposite end see [[q0336]] and [[q0344]]; both extremes together are in [[q0338]].
