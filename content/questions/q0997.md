::: testing
Whether you can explain a 10x probe as a **frequency-compensated voltage divider**, not just "it divides by ten". The interviewer is checking that you know: the 9 MΩ + 1 MΩ divider, why that raises input resistance to 10 MΩ and cuts input capacitance, why there is a capacitor in parallel with the 9 MΩ (to keep the ratio flat versus frequency), the compensation adjustment with a square wave, the cost (less signal, so worse sensitivity and noise), and that you must tell the scope the probe is 10x.
:::

::: why
The probe is the first part of the measurement chain, and a bad probe setup gives a wrong reading before the scope's accuracy matters at all. A 1x probe presents about 100 pF to your circuit; a 10x presents about 10 pF and 10 MΩ. If you misunderstand the compensation you'll see overshoot or rounding on every square wave and blame the circuit. Calibrating the probe is also a classic "do you actually use a scope" check.
:::

::: concepts
- **Voltage divider**: $V_{out}=V_{in}R_2/(R_1+R_2)$. A 9 MΩ top resistor and the scope's 1 MΩ input make 1/10. See [[q0008]].
- **Capacitor impedance** ($1/(j\omega C)$): at high frequency the capacitances dominate the divider. See [[q0022]].
- **Compensation**: the condition $R_1C_1=R_2C_2$ makes the divide ratio frequency-independent.
- **Input loading**: lower capacitance and higher resistance mean less disturbance to the circuit. See [[q0999]].
- **Attenuation vs sensitivity**: dividing by 10 extends the range but lets scope noise dominate small signals.
:::

::: answer
"A 10x probe is a voltage divider built into the probe. It has a 9 megaohm resistor in the tip, in series with the scope's 1 megaohm input, so the scope sees one tenth of the signal. The scope is told it's a 10x probe and multiplies the display back up by ten, so I read the real voltage.

Why bother? Because it makes the probe load the circuit much less. Instead of 1 megaohm and maybe 100 picofarads, which is mostly the cable and scope input, the circuit sees about 10 megaohms and roughly 10 picofarads. That matters at high frequency, where the capacitance is what loads the circuit. It also extends the voltage range by ten.

The divider has capacitance too, which would make it frequency-dependent, so there is a small capacitor across the 9 megaohm resistor. When that capacitor is correct, $R_1C_1 = R_2C_2$, the division is 10 to 1 at every frequency. That's what the compensation screw does, and why I check it on the scope's square-wave calibration output. The cost is that the signal is ten times smaller, so noise from the scope is relatively bigger, and for tiny signals I might use 1x."
:::

::: technical
**Structure.** Tip resistor $R_1=9$ MΩ with a trimmer $C_1$ in parallel; scope input $R_2=1$ MΩ in parallel with $C_2=C_{cable}+C_{scope}$. $H(s)=\dfrac{R_2/(1+sR_2C_2)}{R_1/(1+sR_1C_1)+R_2/(1+sR_2C_2)}$.

**DC:** $H=R_2/(R_1+R_2)=1/10$.

**Compensation:** if $R_1C_1=R_2C_2$, the time constants cancel and $H(s)=R_2/(R_1+R_2)$ at all frequencies. With $R_1=9R_2$ this requires $C_1=C_2/9$. For example, $C_2=100$ pF gives $C_1\approx11$ pF.

**Input impedance.** Resistance $R_1+R_2=10$ MΩ. Capacitance is the series combination $C_1C_2/(C_1+C_2)$; with the example, 11.1 pF in series with 100 pF = 10 pF. A 1x probe would show about 100 pF plus its 1 MΩ.

**Under/over-compensated.** If $C_1$ is too small the high-frequency gain is lower than DC (rounded corners, slow edges: under-compensation). If too large, the high-frequency gain is higher (overshoot, ringing on the square top).

**Costs.** Signal is divided by 10: noise from the scope's front end is referred to the input ten times larger (e.g. 1 mV rms noise at the scope is 10 mV rms at the tip), so small signals need the 1x setting (if available) or an active probe. Divider ratio accuracy is limited by resistor matching (a few percent unless trimmed). Set the scope's probe attenuation to 10x so the vertical scale reads correctly.
:::

::: long
"A 10x probe is a voltage divider you carry in your hand. The tip has a 9 megaohm resistor. The scope input is 1 megaohm, so the pair divides the signal by ten. The scope is told it's a 10x probe and multiplies the displayed value by ten, so the number I read is the actual voltage at the tip.

What does it buy me? Two things. One is a higher input impedance: 10 megaohms instead of 1. The second matters more at speed: the capacitance the circuit sees. A 1x probe puts the cable and scope input capacitance, around 100 picofarads, on the node. A 10x probe puts those behind the divider, and the circuit sees roughly 10 picofarads. At 10 MHz, 100 picofarads is 160 ohms; 10 picofarads is about 1.6 kilohms. It also gives me ten times the voltage range at the input.

The subtlety is capacitance. The scope input and cable have capacitance C2 in parallel with the 1 megaohm. Without anything else, the divider would roll off with frequency. So there's a capacitor C1 across the 9 megaohm resistor. When R1 times C1 equals R2 times C2, the ratio is exactly 1 over 10 at all frequencies. That's the adjustment on the probe. I check it with a square wave from the scope's calibration terminal. If the top is rounded, it's under-compensated; if there's overshoot, it's over-compensated; I trim until the corners are square.

The downsides: the signal is ten times smaller at the scope input, so scope noise is relatively larger. For a millivolt-level signal I might use a 1x setting, or an active probe. And I need to make sure the scope's probe attenuation setting matches the switch, otherwise my readings are off by 10."
:::

::: terms
- **10x (10:1) probe**: a probe with a 10:1 passive divider.
- **Compensation capacitor ($C_1$)**: the trimmer across the tip resistor that balances the divider's time constants.
- **Compensation condition**: $R_1C_1=R_2C_2$ gives a frequency-independent ratio.
- **Probe attenuation setting**: the scope's 1x/10x/100x entry that rescales the display.
- **Input impedance**: $\approx 10$ MΩ in parallel with about 10-15 pF for a 10x probe.
- **Calibration (compensation) signal**: the scope's built-in square wave (often about 1 kHz) used for trimming.
- **Over/under-compensation**: too much or too little $C_1$, causing overshoot or rounding.
- **1x/10x switch**: a probe switch selecting a direct path or the divider; the 1x path has lower bandwidth.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it divides the signal by 10 and reduces loading."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws the divider with both $R$ and $C$ and states **$R_1C_1=R_2C_2$** as the flat-response condition.
- Computes example numbers: 9 MΩ/1 MΩ, $C_1=C_2/9$ (e.g. 11 pF for 100 pF), total about 10 pF.
- Explains why **low input capacitance** is the real benefit at high frequency (reactance numbers).
- Describes the square-wave compensation procedure and what the under/over-compensated shapes look like.
- Mentions the cost: SNR, because the scope's noise is not attenuated, and the need to set the scope's attenuation factor.
- Knows the 1x position has much lower bandwidth and more loading.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Forgetting to set the scope to 10x** (or leaving the switch on 1x while the scope is set to 10x). Readings are wrong by a factor of 10.
- **Thinking the capacitor is only there to "filter".** It is there to flatten the response. It is a compensation capacitor, not a filter.
- **Trimming the probe on a different scope input or channel and moving it.** Scope input capacitance varies from channel to channel and model to model, so recompensate when you change channels.
- **Believing the attenuation is exact.** The ratio depends on resistor tolerance and scope input resistance (1 MΩ ±1% or so) so the vertical accuracy is typically a few percent; for precision use a DMM.
- **Using 10x for tiny signals** and blaming noise on the circuit. The scope's own noise floor is ten times larger referred to the input.
- **Using the 1x position at high frequency**, where the cable and scope capacitance load the circuit and bandwidth falls to a few MHz.
:::

::: deep
## Why a divider at all?

A scope input is typically 1 MΩ in parallel with 10-20 pF, and a probe connects it through a coaxial cable of about 100 pF. Connect the tip directly (a 1x probe) and the circuit sees all of that: about 1 MΩ and 100-130 pF. At 10 MHz, 115 pF has reactance

$$X_C=\frac{1}{2\pi fC}=\frac{1}{2\pi\cdot10^7\cdot115\times10^{-12}}\approx138\ \Omega$$

A 138 Ω load at 10 MHz will pull down many nodes. Now put a 9 MΩ resistor in front. The circuit sees $9+1=10$ MΩ, and the capacitance is the series of a small tip capacitor $C_1$ with the cable and scope capacitance $C_2$: $C_1C_2/(C_1+C_2)$. With $C_2=100$ pF and $C_1=11.1$ pF this is 10 pF. At 10 MHz, 10 pF is about 1.6 kΩ, over ten times less loading.

## Compensation: the math

Write each block as an impedance: $Z_1=\dfrac{R_1}{1+sR_1C_1}$, $Z_2=\dfrac{R_2}{1+sR_2C_2}$. The divider's transfer function is

$$H(s)=\frac{Z_2}{Z_1+Z_2}$$

If $R_1C_1=R_2C_2=\tau$, then $Z_1=R_1/(1+s\tau)$ and $Z_2=R_2/(1+s\tau)$. The common factor cancels:

$$H=\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}=\frac{1}{10}$$

at all frequencies. If $C_1$ is not matched, there is a pole/zero pair. At low frequency $H=R_2/(R_1+R_2)$; at high frequency, only the capacitors matter and $H\to C_1/(C_1+C_2)$. So:

- $C_1/(C_1+C_2)<0.1$: high-frequency gain too low, edges look slow and rounded: **under-compensated**.
- $C_1/(C_1+C_2)>0.1$: high-frequency gain too high: **over-compensated**, with an overshoot on a square wave.

The uncompensated curve bends around $1/\big(2\pi (R_1\parallel R_2)(C_1+C_2)\big)$, with $R_1\parallel R_2=0.9$ MΩ and $C_1+C_2\approx111$ pF, so about 1.6 kHz. That is why a 1 kHz square wave on the scope's calibration terminal shows the error so clearly: the half-period (500 µs) is several time constants of 100 µs.

## What the square wave shows

Feed the probe a step of height $V$. At the instant of the step only the capacitors matter, so the scope sees $V\,C_1/(C_1+C_2)$. After the capacitors have settled (time constant $\tau=(R_1\parallel R_2)(C_1+C_2)\approx100$ µs) the resistors set the level, $V/10$. Therefore:

| $C_1$ | Initial step seen | Final level | Display |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 pF (under) | $0.048V$ | $0.100V$ | Edge rises to about half height, then creeps up to full: rounded corner |
| 11.1 pF (matched) | $0.100V$ | $0.100V$ | Square |
| 20 pF (over) | $0.167V$ | $0.100V$ | Spikes to 1.67x, then decays: overshoot with a sagging top |

The ratio of initial to final level, $10\,C_1/(C_1+C_2)$, is a number you can read off the screen and use to guess how far to turn the trimmer. A square wave exposes the error because it contains a step (all frequencies at once) and a flat top (the DC level to compare).

## The calibration procedure

1. Connect the probe to the scope's calibration output (typically a 1 kHz square wave of about 0.5 V to 5 V, check the label).
2. Set the scope's attenuation to 10x for that channel.
3. Display a few cycles and adjust the trimmer on the probe or its BNC compensation box with a small screwdriver.
4. A **flat top** means compensated; a **rounded leading edge** is under; a **spike and sag (overshoot)** means over.

The compensation depends on the scope channel's input capacitance, so recompensate after moving the probe to a different channel or scope.

## Noise, range and accuracy

Dividing by 10 helps the high side: a channel whose coarsest setting is, say, 10 V/div displays 100 V/div with a 10x probe, so ten times larger signals fit on screen (always check the probe's voltage rating). But it hurts the low side. If the scope's input noise is 1 mV rms (set by amplifier and quantization at a sensitive range), then at the probe tip it is equivalent to 10 mV rms. A 1x probe gives you 10x better sensitivity at the cost of about 1 MΩ and 100 pF. A good rule: measure signals above roughly 100 mV with 10x; below that consider 1x (at low frequency) or an active probe ([[q0996]]).

Accuracy: if the 9 MΩ and 1 MΩ were each 1% off in opposite directions, the ratio would be between 0.0982 and 0.1018 (±1.8%). Probes use better matched or trimmed parts and the scope can use the probe's identification to apply its scale, but a few percent is a normal limit for scope amplitude measurements.

## Bandwidth

The probe's own bandwidth is set by the tip capacitor, the cable (a lossy, sometimes compensated resistive-center-conductor cable) and the scope input. A 1x position has the full cable capacitance in parallel with the source and therefore a bandwidth of only several MHz, and with a 1 kΩ source it forms a pole at $1/(2\pi\cdot1000\cdot115\text{ pF})\approx1.4$ MHz. A 10x probe with 10 pF has a pole at 16 MHz for the same 1 kΩ source (and the probe's own bandwidth, often 100-500 MHz, applies above that).

::: applications
- **General bench use**: the default for debugging digital and analog signals up to 100-500 MHz.
- **Reading power rails and ripple** with low loading on the circuit.
- **Higher-voltage measurements** (the divider extends the range; a 100x probe goes further, check its rating).
- **Calibration check** before any measurement: the quick sanity test that the probe and channel are set up right.
- **Teaching labs and production test**, where compensation is routine.
:::

::: assumptions
- The scope input is 1 MΩ in parallel with some picofarads. For 50 Ω inputs, the story is different ([[q1000]]).
- The cable and scope capacitance are taken as lumped; at high frequency, cable effects (transmission line behavior, losses) add more.
- The numbers 9 MΩ, 1 MΩ, 10 pF are typical; probes vary (e.g. 10-15 pF).
- Not every probe is 10x: 1x, 10x switchable and 100x probes exist; the principle is the same.

If the interviewer asks "what does the 10x do" casually, the short answer is "divides by 10 and reduces loading." Show the compensation point to stand out.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the probe have an adjustment screw?
To set $C_1$ so that $R_1C_1=R_2C_2$ for the particular scope input and cable capacitance. Each scope channel has a slightly different $C_2$, so the probe has to be matched to it. Without it the division varies with frequency, which rounds or overshoots fast edges.

### If the scope input is 1 MΩ and 20 pF with a 100 pF cable, what should $C_1$ be for a 10x probe?
$C_2=120$ pF, so $C_1=C_2/9\approx13.3$ pF. The total input capacitance would be $13.3\cdot120/133.3=12$ pF. Probes usually have a small adjustable range around the nominal value.

### What happens if I use a 10x probe but leave the scope at 1x attenuation?
The scope displays a tenth of the real voltage. A 5 V signal looks like 0.5 V. Many modern scopes auto-detect probes via a sense ring, but older or passive ones do not.

### Why is bandwidth lower on the 1x setting?
In 1x there is no divider, so the full cable and scope capacitance (around 100 pF) load the circuit, and the probe forms a low-pass with the source resistance. Together with the cable's own loss this limits bandwidth to a few MHz to tens of MHz. The 10x position has a smaller effective capacitance.

### Would a 10x probe be good for measuring a 10 mV ripple on a 5 V rail?
It can work, but noise is a concern: the scope's noise floor is effectively 10x higher at the tip. Use AC coupling or an offset to put the ripple at high sensitivity, a short ground spring, bandwidth limit if appropriate, and consider a 1x probe only if bandwidth requirements are low. Alternatively use a dedicated power-rail probe.
:::

::: interpretations
"What does the 10x attenuation do" could also mean "what does the 10x do to my reading." The answer is: nothing to the reading if the scope setting is correct; it divides the signal by 10 at the scope input and the scope rescales it. The deeper answer, the compensated divider and loading, is what most interviewers are after. Input impedance of probes is covered in [[q0999]].
:::
