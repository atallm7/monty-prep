::: testing
Whether you can treat a battery as a real circuit element: an ideal source in series with a resistance, which is not constant. The interviewer wants the model (open-circuit voltage plus $R_{int}$), why it matters (voltage sag, heating, efficiency, maximum power, state-of-charge error, charge speed), and how it moves with **temperature** (strongly worse when cold), **aging** (grows), **state of charge**, **current** and **frequency** (DC pulse vs 1 kHz AC are different numbers because there are several time constants). The best answers know the equivalent-circuit picture (ohmic + RC pairs) and can say which physical process each part represents.
:::

::: why
Internal resistance is the number that decides whether your product works in the cold, under a radio burst, at the end of battery life or with a motor start. A device that works perfectly on a fresh bench cell can brown out at 0 °C or after a year because $IR_{int}$ pulls the terminal voltage below the regulator's minimum. $R_{int}$ also sets heat ($I^2R$), charging time, usable power, and is one of the best health indicators ([[q0312]]). Interviewers like this because it requires linking a circuit model to real electrochemistry.
:::

::: concepts
- **Thevenin model of a cell**: $V_{term}=V_{oc}-IR_{int}$ ([[q0001]]).
- **$I^2R$ heating and efficiency**: power lost inside the cell ([[q0292]]).
- **Ohmic vs polarisation resistance**: instantaneous drop vs slower build-up; RC pairs in the model ([[q0291]]).
- **Impedance and frequency**: a battery's impedance depends on frequency (EIS, 1 kHz meters).
- **Arrhenius temperature dependence**: ion transport and reaction rates slow when cold ([[q0306]]).
- **Aging**: SEI growth and contact loss increase resistance ([[q0301]]).
- **Maximum power transfer**: $P_{max}=V_{oc}^2/4R_{int}$.
:::

::: answer
"Internal resistance is the opposition inside the battery to current flow. The simplest model is an ideal voltage source, the open-circuit voltage, in series with a resistor, so the terminal voltage is the open-circuit voltage minus current times that resistance. It matters because it makes the voltage sag under load, it wastes power as heat, I squared R, it limits the maximum current and power the battery can deliver, and it makes state-of-charge estimates from voltage wrong under load. For a LiPo it's typically tens of milliohms for a phone cell.

It isn't a constant. With temperature it rises sharply in the cold, because the ions move more slowly and the reactions slow down: at zero degrees it might be two to three times the room-temperature value, and several times higher at minus twenty. With aging it increases as the SEI layer grows and contacts degrade, perhaps doubling by end of life. It also depends on state of charge, getting worse when nearly empty. And with frequency: at high frequency you only see the ohmic part, the electrolyte and contacts, so a 1 kilohertz meter reads lower than the DC resistance you'd get from a ten-second pulse, which includes charge transfer and diffusion. So you model it as a resistor plus a couple of parallel R-C pairs."
:::

::: technical
**Basic model.** $V_{term}=V_{oc}(SoC,T)-I\,R_{int}$ (discharge current positive). Power lost in the cell: $P_{loss}=I^2R_{int}$. Voltage efficiency: $\eta_V\approx 1-IR_{int}/V_{oc}$. Maximum matched-load power $P_{max}=V_{oc}^2/(4R_{int})$ at $I=V_{oc}/(2R_{int})$; short-circuit current is $V_{oc}/R_{int}$.

**Equivalent circuit (Thevenin / Randles-type).** $Z(s)=R_0+\sum_k\dfrac{R_k}{1+sR_kC_k}$.

- $R_0$ (ohmic): electrolyte, separator, current collectors, tabs and contacts. Responds instantly; sets the high-frequency intercept.
- $R_1C_1$ (charge-transfer + SEI): time constants of milliseconds to a second, hundreds of Hz to ~Hz.
- $R_2C_2$ (solid-state diffusion/concentration): seconds to minutes (mHz).
- At very high frequency (tens of kHz and above) inductance of the winding and leads appears.

**Dependence on conditions (typical, indicative).**

| Variable | Effect on $R$ | Physical reason |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature ↓ | strongly ↑ (about 2-3× at 0 °C, ~5× at -20 °C vs 25 °C) | electrolyte conductivity and reaction kinetics follow Arrhenius laws; charge transfer is the most temperature-sensitive part |
| Aging | ↑ (often 1.5-2× at 80% capacity, cell-dependent) | SEI growth, contact and particle loss, electrolyte consumption |
| State of charge | ↑ at low SoC (below about 20%); mild elsewhere | exchange current and diffusion slow at extremes of lithiation |
| Current magnitude | charge-transfer part ↓ at higher current | Butler-Volmer nonlinearity; pulse-dependent apparent $R$ |
| Charge vs discharge | slightly different | asymmetric kinetics |
| Frequency | ↓ with frequency until only $R_0$ remains | each RC pair is bypassed by its capacitor |
| Cell size/design | ∝ 1/(electrode area) | power cells use thin electrodes, big area |

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Temperature multipliers, aging growth and the exact RC time constants depend on chemistry, cell design and SoC. The numbers here (Arrhenius-like with $E_a\approx0.25$ eV) are illustrative, not datasheet values.

**Worked example (2 Ah cell, $V_{oc}=3.7$ V, $R_{25}=50$ mΩ).** At 10 A (5C), 25 °C: sag 0.5 V, heat 5 W. At 0 °C ($R\approx 122$ mΩ): sag 1.2 V, heat 12 W, and $P_{max}=28$ W vs 68 W at 25 °C. At -20 °C and aged ($R\approx 0.28\times2=0.56$ Ω): $P_{max}=6$ W, short-circuit current $3.7/0.56=6.6$ A; a 10 A load is impossible and the device browns out.
:::

::: long
"Internal resistance is the equivalent series resistance inside the cell. The simplest model is an ideal voltage source, the open-circuit voltage, in series with a resistor, so the terminal voltage under load is the open-circuit voltage minus I times R. For a phone-sized LiPo it's roughly tens of milliohms.

Why does it matter? First, voltage sag: if my load pulls two amps through fifty milliohms, I lose a tenth of a volt, and in the cold that's much more. A regulator that needs three volts in can drop out. Second, heat: the cell dissipates I squared R internally. Third, it limits the maximum current and power the battery can deliver; the matched-load limit is V squared over four R. Fourth, it makes voltage-based state-of-charge readings wrong under load, and it slows charging, because the charger hits the voltage limit earlier. And it's a health indicator, since it rises as the cell ages.

How does it vary? With temperature, strongly. Ion transport in the electrolyte and the electrode kinetics follow Arrhenius laws, so at zero degrees the resistance is two to three times the room-temperature value, and at minus twenty it can be five times or more. With aging, it grows as the SEI layer thickens and contacts degrade, perhaps doubling by end of life. With state of charge, it's higher near empty. And with frequency: the resistance has several parts with different time constants. The ohmic part from electrolyte, foils and contacts responds instantly; charge transfer takes milliseconds to a second; diffusion takes tens of seconds. At one kilohertz you mostly see the ohmic part, so an AC meter reads lower than a DC pulse measurement. That's why we model it as a series resistor plus one or two parallel RC pairs, and measure it with a pulse test or impedance spectroscopy."
:::

::: terms
- **Internal resistance ($R_{int}$)**: lumped series resistance causing voltage drop and heating.
- **Ohmic resistance ($R_0$)**: instantaneous part from electrolyte, foils, tabs and contacts.
- **Polarisation resistance**: the slower parts from charge transfer and diffusion.
- **DCIR / ACIR**: DC pulse resistance vs AC (e.g. 1 kHz) resistance.
- **EIS**: electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, impedance versus frequency.
- **Nyquist plot**: imaginary vs real impedance; intercept = $R_0$, semicircle = charge transfer.
- **Arrhenius law**: rate ∝ $\exp(-E_a/k_BT)$.
- **Voltage sag**: terminal voltage drop $IR$ under load.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "it's the resistance inside the battery that causes voltage drop and heat".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Gives the model *and* its consequences with equations: sag $IR$, heat $I^2R$, $P_{max}=V^2/4R$, short-circuit current.
- Treats $R$ as **a function**, not a constant: temperature (Arrhenius, 2-3× at 0 °C), aging (up to ~2× at EOL), SoC (rises when empty), current and frequency.
- Explains frequency dependence via the **equivalent circuit** (R0 + RC pairs) and relates each to a physical process (electrolyte/contacts, charge transfer + SEI, diffusion), and can say why 1 kHz ≠ DC pulse.
- Gives a numeric example in the cold/aged case that shows a design failing.
- Says how to measure it (pulse $\Delta V/\Delta I$ with timescale, EIS) and how to use it (health, thermal design, sizing).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating $R_{int}$ as a constant datasheet number.** It changes by a factor of several with temperature, SoC and age.
- **Quoting an AC 1 kHz value as "the" resistance.** It reflects only $R_0$; the DC value for a 10 s pulse can be 1.5-2× higher.
- **Ignoring wiring and contact resistance.** Connector, tab and protection MOSFET resistance (tens of mΩ) adds to the pack's effective $R$.
- **Using the matched-load $P_{max}$ as a rating.** At that point half the power is dissipated in the cell and it would overheat.
- **Thinking low $R$ is only about current.** It also gives efficiency, lower heat, fast charge and less SoC error.
- **Assuming capacity tells you health.** A cell can retain capacity while resistance doubles ([[q0301]]).
:::

::: deep
## Where the voltage goes

Connect a cell to a load and the terminal voltage drops by an amount that depends on *time* as well as current. The first part is immediate and the rest builds up over seconds. That is the signature of a circuit with a resistor and RC pairs, not a single resistor.

## Frequency dependence: impedance

If you excite the cell with a small sine of frequency $f$, the model's impedance is $Z(j\omega)=R_0+\sum_k R_k/(1+j\omega R_kC_k)$. At low frequency each capacitor is open and you see the DC total $R_0+R_1+R_2$; as frequency rises each RC branch is shorted by its capacitor and only $R_0$ remains.

In a Nyquist plot (imaginary vs real impedance) this appears as an intercept on the real axis at $R_0$, one or two semicircles for charge transfer/SEI and a sloping tail for diffusion.

## Temperature

Ion mobility in the electrolyte and the charge-transfer kinetics both follow Arrhenius behaviour, so resistance rises exponentially as $T$ falls: $R(T)=R_{25}\exp\!\left[\frac{E_a}{k_B}\left(\frac1T-\frac1{298.15}\right)\right]$. Charge transfer and SEI contributions have larger activation energies than the ohmic part, so the cold penalty is dominated by the polarisation resistance.

This is why a phone shuts down in the cold at a battery level that would be fine at room temperature: the cold, higher-R cell sags below the system cutoff under a burst load. Self-heating ($I^2R$) partly offsets it.

Consequences, with $V_{oc}=3.7$ V and an assumed 2 Ah cell:

| Condition | $R$ | Sag at 10 A | Heat at 10 A | $P_{max}=V^2/4R$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25 °C, new | 50 mΩ | 0.5 V | 5 W | 68 W |
| 0 °C, new | 122 mΩ | 1.2 V | 12 W | 28 W |

(Heat at −20 °C, new, is $10^2\times0.282=28$ W.) The last row means the cell cannot supply 10 A at all: terminal voltage would hit zero at 6.6 A. In practice the device resets or the protection trips long before.

## Aging

As the cell ages, the SEI thickens and the electrodes lose contact and active surface, so $R_1$ (charge transfer/SEI) grows the most and $R_0$ somewhat. A typical consumer cell may reach 1.5-2× its initial DC resistance by 80% capacity, with large cell-to-cell variation ([[q0301]]). Resistance growth is why power-hungry products fail before capacity does, and why fuel gauges track it ([[q0312]]).

## State of charge and current

Resistance is often flat through the middle of the SoC range and rises at low SoC (the last 10-20%) as lithium diffusion in the electrodes slows, partly explaining the sudden voltage collapse near empty. Because charge-transfer resistance is nonlinear (Butler-Volmer), the apparent resistance of a high-current pulse is smaller than that of a small-signal measurement; so "internal resistance" depends on how you measure it.

## Why it matters, in one list

1. **Sag**: $V_{term}$ can fall below the converter's minimum input (brown-out) ([[q0293]]).
2. **Heat**: $I^2R$ in the cell and in the series path; a 5 A load through 100 mΩ is 2.5 W.
3. **Power limit**: $P_{max}=V_{oc}^2/4R$; practical limit from heating is much lower.
4. **Charging**: the charger reaches its 4.2 V limit sooner when $R$ is high ($V=V_{oc}+IR$), so CC time shortens and the CV tail lengthens, with less charge in the fast phase ([[q0296]]).
5. **SoC estimation**: voltage-based estimates must subtract $IR$ ([[q0308]]).
6. **Pack matching**: cells in parallel share current inversely to resistance; in series, higher-R cells heat more.
7. **Health**: resistance trend is a robust SoH indicator ([[q0312]]).

::: applications
- **Phone shutdowns in cold weather**: high $R$ at low temperature plus a radio or CPU burst drops the terminal voltage below cutoff.
- **Drone and RC pack selection and retirement**: measure per-cell resistance with a charger's IR function; retire packs whose resistance doubled.
- **Power-tool and e-bike BMS**: derate current as temperature falls or resistance rises.
- **Fuel gauge ICs**: impedance-tracking algorithms update $R$ to compute remaining runtime and SoH.
- **Thermal design**: $I^2R_{int}$ sets the cell's heat dissipation, which sizes heat spreaders and fans ([[q0332]]).
:::

::: assumptions
The answer assumes a single Li-ion/LiPo cell described by a first- or second-order equivalent circuit and small-to-moderate currents. At very high C-rates the model is nonlinear and temperature feedback (self-heating) changes $R$ during a pulse. The numbers (Arrhenius with 0.25 eV, aging factors) are illustrative; real cells vary by chemistry and design (power cells have much lower $R$ than energy cells of the same capacity).

For packs, add interconnect, busbar, fuse and protection MOSFET resistance (often 20-100 mΩ in small packs) to the cell resistance; for parallel cells divide, for series cells add.
:::

::: followups
### Why is internal resistance higher at low temperature?
Both the electrolyte's ionic conductivity and the electrode reaction (charge-transfer) kinetics decrease exponentially as temperature falls (Arrhenius). The electrolyte viscosity rises, lithium diffuses more slowly in the solid particles, and the SEI conducts less well. The polarisation parts have the largest activation energy, so they dominate the increase: 2-3× at 0 °C and roughly 5× at -20 °C is typical.

### Why does a 1 kHz meter give a different value than a DC pulse test?
At 1 kHz the RC branches for charge transfer and diffusion are shorted by their capacitors, so the meter sees only the ohmic resistance $R_0$. A DC pulse lasting seconds charges those capacitors and includes $R_1+R_2$ as well. Neither is wrong; they measure different things. For predicting sag in a load pulse of a certain duration, use the DC pulse resistance at that duration.

### How does internal resistance affect charging?
During CC the terminal voltage is $V_{oc}+IR$, so higher R reaches the 4.2 V limit at a lower SoC, shortening the CC phase and lengthening the CV tail ([[q0296]]). It also dissipates $I^2R$ as heat during charge, limiting how fast you can charge.

### Two identical cells are wired in parallel but one has 2× the resistance. What happens?
They share current in inverse proportion to resistance: the low-R cell takes about two thirds of the current and the high-R cell about one third. The low-R cell runs hotter and ages faster, and the imbalance grows. Matching cells by resistance and capacity is standard for packs.

### What if the product browns out at 0 °C only when the battery shows 40% charge?
Likely $R$ is 2-3× its room-temperature value, so the burst current drops the terminal voltage below the system minimum even though energy remains. Options: limit peak current (spread out the load), add a bulk capacitor or supercapacitor to supply the burst, lower the cutoff threshold if the regulator allows, keep the battery warm, or use a lower-resistance cell. Confirm by measuring the voltage trace during the burst at that temperature.
:::

::: interpretations
"Internal resistance" can mean the DC resistance from a pulse or the AC (impedance) value, and datasheets often give the 1 kHz AC value only. I treat it as a family of related quantities and say which one when answering. "Frequency" is the AC impedance dependence; "etc." covers state of charge, current amplitude and cell-to-cell variation. See [[q0291]] for the measurement procedure and [[q0306]] for temperature effects on performance in general.
:::
