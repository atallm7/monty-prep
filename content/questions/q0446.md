::: testing
Whether you can run the **standard sizing calculation**: $R_s = V_{FS}/I_{max}$ with headroom, then sanity-check it against power, drop, standard values and low-current resolution, and whether you notice when the answer is physically silly (for example 27 Ω at 100 mA), which tells you an amplifier is needed.

The interviewer is watching for: unit care (mΩ, mV), headroom for overload, rounding to an available value, the $I^2R$ check, and ADC resolution at $I_{min}$. Bidirectional current (offset to mid-scale) is a common twist.
:::

::: why
This is the practical version of [[q0445]]: you're handed numbers (current range, ADC range) and must produce a resistor. On the job, you do this on every board with a current monitor; in interviews it's a fast way to check you can do engineering arithmetic with sensible judgment. The key lesson is that sizing the resistor to the ADC's *full-scale* directly is rarely feasible for amps-level currents, which motivates the amplifier question ([[q0453]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Ohm's law**: $V_s = IR_s$. See [[q0001]].
- **ADC full-scale and LSB**: $V_{LSB} = V_{FS}/2^N$. See [[q0812]] and [[q0819]].
- **Headroom (margin)**: designing full-scale above the largest expected current to avoid clipping on overloads.
- **Power and drop**: $P = I^2R_s$, $V_{drop} = IR_s$. See [[q0445]].
- **Preferred values**: resistors come in E-series (E24, E96); current-sense parts include 1, 2, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 50 mΩ and so on.
- **Dynamic range**: $I_{max}/I_{min}$ against ADC bits.
:::

::: answer
"I'd start from the ADC. If the input range is 0 to $V_{FS}$ and the maximum current is $I_{max}$, then the largest sense voltage I want is $V_{FS}$ minus some headroom for overload, say 10 to 20 percent. So $R_s = V_{FS}/(1.1\,I_{max})$, rounded to a standard value that keeps me under full-scale.

Then I sanity-check it three ways. Power: $I_{max}^2R_s$. Drop: $I_{max}R_s$ against what the load tolerates. Resolution: the current per ADC step is $V_{FS}/(2^N R_s)$, compared with the smallest current I need to see.

For a quick example, ADC 0 to 3.3 V and 100 mA max gives about 27 ohms, which drops nearly 3 volts and is useless in a supply path. That tells me I shouldn't connect the shunt directly to the ADC. I should pick a small shunt based on power, like 10 milliohms at 5 amps, and amplify the signal with a current sense amplifier so the full-scale current lands at the top of the ADC range. If current can reverse, the zero point goes to mid-scale and the usable swing is half."
:::

::: technical
**Direct-to-ADC sizing.** For unipolar current $0 \ldots I_{max}$, ADC range $0 \ldots V_{FS}$, headroom factor $k$ (for example 1.1 to 1.25):
$$R_s \le \frac{V_{FS}}{k\,I_{max}}$$
Choose the nearest standard value at or below this.

**Bipolar current** $\pm I_{max}$ with ADC range $0 \ldots V_{FS}$ and mid-scale offset $V_{FS}/2$: $R_s \le \dfrac{V_{FS}/2}{k\,I_{max}}$. For a differential ADC with range $\pm V_{in,max}$, $R_s \le \dfrac{V_{in,max}}{k\,I_{max}}$.

**Resolution.** $\Delta I_{LSB} = \dfrac{V_{FS}}{2^N R_s}$. Dynamic range needed for $\varepsilon$ accuracy at $I_{min}$: $2^N \ge \dfrac{I_{max}}{\varepsilon I_{min}}$ (rough rule), so for 500:1 range and 1 percent accuracy at minimum, $2^N \ge 5\times10^4$, about 15.6 bits.

**Checks.** $P = I_{max}^2R_s$ and $V_{drop} = I_{max}R_s$.

**Example A (direct, 3.3 V ADC).** $I_{max} = 100$ mA, $k = 1.1$: $R_s \le 3.3/(0.11) = 30$ Ω, so use 27 Ω. Drop 2.7 V, $P = 0.27$ W. Impractical: use an amplifier.

**Example B (PGA/differential ADC, ±250 mV).** $I_{max} = \pm10$ A, $k = 1.1$: $R_s \le 0.25/11 = 22.7$ mΩ, so 20 mΩ. $V_s = 200$ mV at 10 A; full-scale current 12.5 A; $P = 2$ W. With a 16-bit ADC over ±250 mV, LSB = 7.63 µV, which is 0.38 mA per count.

**Example C (low-side, amplified).** See [[q0453]]: pick $R_s$ from power and drop, then gain $G = V_{FS}/(kI_{max}R_s)$.
:::

::: short
"I take the ADC full-scale voltage, divide by the maximum current with about 10 percent headroom, and that gives the largest resistor: $R_s = V_{FS}/(1.1 I_{max})$. Then I check power $I^2R$, voltage drop, and resolution per LSB. If the result is silly, like 27 ohms at 100 milliamps, I use a small shunt and an amplifier instead. For bidirectional current I use half the range, since zero sits at mid-scale."
:::

::: long
"My approach is: compute the biggest resistor the ADC allows, then check whether the system can live with it.

Step one: the ADC accepts 0 to $V_{FS}$. I don't want the maximum current to hit exactly full-scale, because a transient or overload would clip, so I design for about 10 to 20 percent headroom. That gives $R_s = V_{FS}/(1.1 I_{max})$. If the current can go both ways, zero current sits at mid-scale, so I can only use half of the range per direction, and I replace $V_{FS}$ with $V_{FS}/2$.

Step two: round down to a standard value so full-scale is never exceeded, which for current-sense parts means 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 milliohms and so on.

Step three: check it. Power is $I_{max}^2R_s$, drop is $I_{max}R_s$, and resolution is $V_{FS}/(2^N R_s)$ amps per count. I compare those with my limits, and with the smallest current I care about. I also check dynamic range: if I need one percent at one-hundredth of full current, I need about 100 steps at the bottom, so the ADC needs about 100 times the full-scale count.

Example: a 3.3 volt ADC with 100 milliamps max. That's about 27 ohms, dropping 2.7 volts and burning 0.27 watts. Totally impractical for a supply path. So I choose a small shunt from power and drop, say 10 milliohms at 5 amps, which gives 50 millivolts, and add an amplifier of gain around 50 to bring it up to the ADC range. That's the standard chain, and the gain calculation is the next step."
:::

::: terms
- **Full-scale (FS) range**: the ADC input span that maps to codes 0 to $2^N-1$.
- **Headroom**: margin between maximum expected signal and full-scale.
- **LSB**: least significant bit, $V_{FS}/2^N$.
- **Preferred values (E-series)**: standard resistor values such as E24; current-sense resistors come in 1-2-2.5-5 sequences.
- **Bidirectional (bipolar) sensing**: current flows both ways; output biased at a reference such as mid-scale.
- **Dynamic range**: ratio of the largest to smallest measurable current.
- **PGA**: programmable-gain amplifier at an ADC input.
- **Burden**: voltage and power taken from the load by the sense element.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says $R = V/I$ and plugs in the ADC range.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Includes headroom, rounds down to a standard value, and does the unit conversions cleanly.
- Immediately checks power, drop and resolution, and says what the result implies.
- Recognizes when direct sensing is impossible (volts of drop) and moves to shunt-plus-amplifier, linking to the gain calculation.
- Handles bidirectional current by halving the usable range and stating the mid-scale offset.
- Checks dynamic range against ADC bits, and points out the offset floor ([[q0445]]).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Forgetting headroom.** Sizing exactly to full-scale clips on any overload or tolerance stack-up.
- **Rounding up to a standard value.** That can exceed full-scale; round down (or recompute).
- **Ignoring power and drop.** The ADC range is not the only constraint: $V_{FS}/I_{max}$ may put 3 V across the load path.
- **Treating bipolar like unipolar.** With mid-scale bias, only half the range is available per direction.
- **Mixing mΩ and Ω** or mA and A. Write units through every step.
- **Ignoring the low end.** A resistor that fills the ADC at $I_{max}$ may still leave $I_{min}$ at 1 to 2 LSBs.
:::

::: deep
## The core calculation

For a signal chain with only a resistor between the current and the ADC pin (low-side, ground-referenced, ADC range $0 \ldots V_{FS}$):

$$V_{ADC} = I R_s$$

To reach full-scale at $k I_{max}$:

$$R_s = \frac{V_{FS}}{k\,I_{max}}$$

Take the headroom $k = 1.1$ to $1.25$: a 10 to 25 percent overload margin plus resistor tolerance and ADC reference error.

### Example A: why direct sensing fails

3.3 V ADC, $I_{max} = 100$ mA. $R_s = 3.3/(1.1\times0.1) = 30$ Ω; E24 value 27 Ω gives 2.7 V at 100 mA.

- Drop: 2.7 V in the load path. A 3.3 V load now sees 0.6 V, if it works at all.
- Power: 0.27 W, in a part that must be at least a 0.5 W resistor.
- Low-side: the load's ground is lifted 2.7 V.

The calculation is correct and the design is nonsense. The lesson: **the ADC range is too big compared with a sensible burden voltage**. That is what a current sense amplifier fixes.

### Example B: ADC with a small differential input

Many metering and motor-control ADCs (delta-sigma modulators, PGA inputs) accept about ±250 mV differential. Then:

$I_{max} = 10$ A: $R_s = 0.25/(1.1\times10) = 22.7$ mΩ; round to **20 mΩ**.
- $V_s(10\text{ A}) = 200$ mV, so full-scale current $= 0.25/0.02 = 12.5$ A.
- $P = 10^2\times0.02 = 2$ W. A 3 W 2512 part, or 2 W with derating, would be marginal: you might drop to 10 mΩ and use 125 mV of the range, giving up resolution for power.
- 16-bit over ±250 mV: LSB $= 0.5/65536 = 7.63$ µV, so $0.38$ mA per count at 20 mΩ.

Here no extra amplifier is needed, because the ADC itself accepts small signals.

### Example C: range-of-currents version

$I_{min} = 10$ mA, $I_{max} = 5$ A, 12-bit 3.3 V ADC (LSB 0.806 mV).

- Dynamic range 500:1 needs, for 1 percent at $I_{min}$, about $5/(0.01\times0.01) = 5\times10^4$ counts, about 15.6 bits, more than 12.
- Direct: $R_s = 3.3/(1.1\times5) = 0.6$ Ω, giving 15 W at 5 A. Unusable.
- Shunt from power: 10 mΩ gives 0.25 W at 5 A. Add gain 50 (see [[q0453]]): LSB is $0.806\text{ mV}/(50\times0.01) = 1.6$ mA, so 10 mA reads as 6 counts (about 16 percent resolution, not 1 percent).

To reach better low-end resolution you need a higher-resolution ADC, more shunt, or two ranges. The sim below shows these numbers.

## Standard values

Current-sense resistors are sold in values like 0.5, 1, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 50, 100 mΩ (check your supplier). Round down to stay below full scale, or choose the next value up only if your headroom $k$ allows. Remember tolerance: a 1 percent resistor and a 1 percent reference mean up to 2 percent shift in full-scale current.

## Bipolar current

For ±$I_{max}$ with a single-supply ADC (0 to $V_{FS}$) and mid-scale bias $V_{FS}/2$:

$$R_s \le \frac{V_{FS}/2}{k\,I_{max}}$$

Zero current reads at $V_{FS}/2$ (code $2^{N-1}$), and negative currents move the reading down. The effective resolution is the same per amp but you've spent half the range on each direction.

## Putting it into a procedure

1. Write down $I_{max}$, $I_{min}$, ADC range, bits, and limits on drop and power.
2. Compute $R_{s,ADC} = V_{FS}/(kI_{max})$.
3. Compute $R_{s,max} = \min(V_{drop}/I_{max}, P/I_{max}^2)$.
4. If $R_{s,ADC} \le R_{s,max}$, use it (rounded down). Otherwise choose $R_s \le R_{s,max}$ and add gain $G = R_{s,ADC}/R_s$.
5. Check resolution at $I_{min}$ and offset floor $V_{os}/R_s$; adjust.

This is the same structure as [[q0445]], now with the ADC range driving the numbers.
:::

::: applications
- **Microcontroller current monitors**: 3.3 V ADC with amplified shunt for 0 to a few amps.
- **Metering ICs**: ±250 mV to ±500 mV delta-sigma inputs with 1 to 20 mΩ shunts.
- **Smart battery packs**: 12 to 16 bit ADC in a gauge IC across a 1 to 10 mΩ shunt.
- **Motor controllers**: shunt sized so stall current fills the ADC range via an amplifier.
- **Bench supplies**: switchable ranges (for example 100 mA / 1 A / 10 A) with different shunts.
- **Low-current (µA to mA) instruments**: bigger shunts (ohms to kilohms) because the burden at those currents is small.
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes a ground-referenced, low-side measurement so the ADC reads $IR_s$ directly. With a high-side shunt an amplifier or isolation is needed for common-mode.
- Assumes a resistive shunt with negligible offset; offset sets an additional lower bound on $R_s$ ([[q0445]]).
- Headroom of 10 to 25 percent is a typical choice, not a standard; if overloads are limited by a hardware current limit you may use less.
- If the interviewer gives an ADC range such as 0 to 3.3 V *and* implies a CSA, the "sense resistor" answer should come from drop/power and the CSA gain from the ADC range ([[q0453]]).
- For high-precision ADCs the reference tolerance also scales full-scale; include it in the budget.
:::

::: followups
### What if the current can flow in both directions?
Bias the signal to mid-scale with a reference ($V_{ref} = V_{FS}/2$ or a CSA's REF pin), and size so $\pm kI_{max}R_s$ fits in $\pm V_{FS}/2$. The resistor can be at most half as large as in the unipolar case for the same $I_{max}$ with a direct connection.

### The calculation gives 0.66 Ω for 5 A on a 3.3 V ADC. What do you do?
Reject it: 0.66 Ω at 5 A is 16.5 W and 3.3 V of drop. Choose a shunt from power and drop (for example 10 mΩ, 0.25 W, 50 mV) and add a CSA with gain about 50 to scale 50 mV up to about 2.5 V.

### How do you check whether the ADC resolution is enough?
Compute amps per count, $V_{FS}/(2^NGR_s)$, and compare with the smallest current and accuracy required. At 3.3 V, 12 bits, $G = 50$, 10 mΩ: 1.6 mA per count, so at 10 mA that's about 6 counts (±8 percent quantization). If you need 1 percent at 10 mA you need roughly 16-bit resolution or a different range scheme.

### Scenario: your ADC's reference is 2.5 V, not 3.3 V, and the board uses the same shunt. What changes?
Full-scale current drops to $2.5/3.3$ of the previous value for the same gain, so you must re-check clipping, which probably means reducing the gain by about 25 percent, or changing the shunt. LSB size shrinks proportionally, so amps per count also improves slightly.

### What if the current range is 1 mA to 10 A?
That's 10,000:1, so a single shunt and a 12-bit ADC won't give 1 percent at 1 mA (you'd need about 20 bits). Use two ranges (for example 100 mΩ for low and 5 mΩ for high), a 24-bit delta-sigma ADC with a high-gain zero-drift amplifier, or autoranging.
:::
