::: testing
Whether you understand **why** AC current crowds to the surface of a conductor (self-induced eddy currents, or equivalently the center of the wire having more inductance), and whether you can **quantify** it with the skin depth $\delta = \sqrt{\rho/(\pi f \mu)}$. Hidden sub-skills:

- Knowing the numbers: copper's skin depth is about 8.4 mm at 60 Hz, 65 µm at 1 MHz and 2 µm at 1 GHz.
- Knowing that resistance rises as $\sqrt f$ once $\delta$ is smaller than the conductor, and what that does to inductors, transformers and PCB traces.
- Knowing the fixes: litz wire, wide flat conductors, plating, smoother copper, and the related **proximity effect**.
:::

::: why
Skin effect is behind several real design problems. It is why a switching-converter inductor runs hotter than its DC resistance predicts (AC resistance, [[q0051]]), why high-frequency transformers use litz wire or foil, why PCB trace loss climbs with frequency and why high-speed boards specify smooth "low-profile" copper, and why RF parts are silver-plated. Interviewers like it because it tests whether you can connect electromagnetism (Faraday's and Lenz's laws) to a practical number on a datasheet or a loss budget.
:::

::: concepts
- **Resistance from geometry**: $R = \rho L/A$, so anything that shrinks the cross-section that carries current raises resistance. See [[q0001]].
- **Faraday's and Lenz's laws**: a changing magnetic flux induces an EMF that drives currents opposing the change.
- **Eddy currents**: circulating currents induced inside a conductor by a changing magnetic field.
- **Inductance and impedance**: paths that link more magnetic flux have more inductance, so at high frequency current takes the lower-inductance path.
- **Skin depth $\delta$**: the depth at which current density falls to $1/e$ (≈37%) of its surface value.
- **AC resistance / ACR**: the effective resistance at frequency, which can be many times the DC value. See [[q0051]].
:::

::: answer
"Skin effect is the tendency of AC current to flow near the surface of a conductor instead of uniformly through its cross-section, and it gets stronger with frequency.

The cause is the conductor's own magnetic field. The alternating current creates a changing magnetic field inside the wire, and by Faraday's law that induces eddy currents. Those eddy currents oppose the main current in the middle of the wire and add to it near the surface. Another way to see it: a current path through the center links more flux, so it has more inductance, and at high frequency the current prefers the lower-impedance outer paths.

The current density decays exponentially going into the conductor, with a characteristic skin depth $\delta = \sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu)}$. For copper that's about 8.4 mm at 60 Hz, 65 µm at 1 MHz and only about 2 µm at 1 GHz. Once $\delta$ is smaller than the conductor, only that thin outer layer carries current, so the effective area shrinks and resistance rises roughly with the square root of frequency.

Practically, it's why switching-converter inductors and transformers have higher AC resistance than DC resistance, why people use litz wire or copper foil, and why high-speed PCB traces get lossier with frequency and care about copper roughness."
:::

::: technical
**Mechanism.** For sinusoidal current in a good conductor, Maxwell's equations reduce to a diffusion equation for the current density. Its solution in a half-space is

$$J(d) = J_s\, e^{-d/\delta}\, e^{-j d/\delta},\qquad \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2\rho}{\omega\mu}} = \sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\pi f\mu_0\mu_r}}$$

The magnitude decays as $e^{-d/\delta}$ and the phase lags by $d/\delta$ radians. About 63% of the total current flows within one skin depth of the surface, and about 98% within 4δ.

**Copper** ($\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}$ Ω·m, $\mu_r = 1$): $\delta \approx 66\ \text{mm}/\sqrt{f/\text{Hz}}$, i.e. ≈ 65 µm/$\sqrt{f/\text{MHz}}$.

| f | 60 Hz | 10 kHz | 100 kHz | 1 MHz | 10 MHz | 100 MHz | 1 GHz | 10 GHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| δ (Cu) | 8.4 mm | 0.65 mm | 206 µm | 65 µm | 21 µm | 6.5 µm | 2.1 µm | 0.65 µm |

**AC resistance of a round wire** (radius $a$, $\delta \ll a$):

$$R_{ac} \approx \frac{\rho L}{2\pi a\,\delta}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{R_{ac}}{R_{dc}} \approx \frac{a}{2\delta} + \frac14$$

Skin effect matters once $\delta \lesssim a$; for a 1 mm-diameter wire that's above ≈17 kHz. At 10 MHz the same wire has $R_{ac}/R_{dc} \approx 12$. Since $\delta \propto 1/\sqrt f$, high-frequency $R_{ac} \propto \sqrt f$.

**Material dependence.** Higher resistivity gives deeper penetration (Al: 82 µm at 1 MHz). Magnetic materials have much smaller δ because of $\mu_r$: steel with $\mu_r \approx 200$ has δ ≈ 1.5 mm at 60 Hz, which is why induction heating works and why transformer cores are laminated.

**PCB traces.** 1 oz copper is ≈35 µm thick, equal to δ at ≈3.5 MHz. At GHz rates the current lives in a ~2 µm layer, mostly on the face toward the reference plane (proximity effect), so the copper's surface roughness (often 1–5 µm) lengthens the current path and can raise conductor loss substantially.

**Proximity effect.** Neighbouring conductors' fields redistribute current further. In multilayer windings it usually dominates over skin effect (Dowell's analysis).
:::

::: long
"Skin effect is the way alternating current crowds toward the outside of a conductor. At DC the current spreads uniformly over the whole cross-section. As frequency goes up, more and more of it flows in a thin layer near the surface.

The reason is the conductor's own magnetic field. Current in a wire creates a magnetic field circling it, including inside the wire. When the current alternates, that internal field changes, and Faraday's law says a changing flux induces an EMF, which drives eddy currents in the metal. By Lenz's law they oppose the change, and the geometry works out so they subtract from the current in the center and add near the surface. An equivalent way to say it: a path through the middle of the wire is surrounded by more flux than a path at the edge, so it has more inductance. At high frequency inductive reactance dominates, and current takes the low-impedance outer path.

The current density falls exponentially into the metal, with a skin depth $\delta = \sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu)}$. For copper that's about 8 mm at mains frequency, so it doesn't matter for normal wiring. But it's 65 µm at 1 MHz and about 2 µm at 1 GHz.

The consequence is resistance. Once the skin depth is smaller than the conductor, the effective area is roughly the perimeter times $\delta$, so resistance grows like the square root of frequency. A 1 mm copper wire at 10 MHz has about 12 times its DC resistance.

Where I'd see it: in a switching converter, the inductor's ripple current sees AC resistance, so the windings run hotter than the DCR suggests. High-frequency transformers use litz wire, many thin insulated strands woven together, or copper foil. On high-speed PCBs, current flows in a couple of microns on the side facing the ground plane, so copper roughness and trace width matter for loss. And there's a sibling effect, proximity effect, where nearby conductors push current around even more."
:::

::: terms
- **Skin effect**: the concentration of AC current near a conductor's surface, increasing with frequency.
- **Skin depth ($\delta$)**: depth at which current density drops to $1/e$ of the surface value; $\delta = \sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu)}$.
- **AC resistance ($R_{ac}$, ACR)**: effective resistance at a given frequency, including skin and proximity effects.
- **Eddy currents**: induced circulating currents that oppose changes in magnetic flux (Lenz's law).
- **Proximity effect**: current redistribution caused by the magnetic field of nearby conductors, e.g. adjacent winding layers or a trace over a plane.
- **Litz wire**: many individually insulated strands, each thinner than δ, twisted or woven so each strand spends equal time at every position.
- **Surface roughness**: microscopic copper profile that lengthens the current path when δ is comparable to the roughness height.
- **Internal inductance**: the part of a conductor's inductance from flux inside the conductor; it falls as current moves to the surface.
:::

:::

::: mistakes
- **"Current flows only on the surface."** Density decays exponentially; it doesn't stop at δ. About 63% of the current is within one δ and there's still some deeper.
- **Thinking skin effect matters at 50/60 Hz in normal wires.** Copper's δ is about 8.4 mm at 60 Hz, so it only matters for very thick bus bars and cables.
- **Assuming a thicker conductor always lowers AC resistance.** Once thickness exceeds a few δ, extra copper carries almost no current. Wider or more surface area helps; thicker doesn't.
- **Blaming everything on skin effect.** In multi-layer windings and closely spaced conductors, the proximity effect is often bigger. Litz wire only helps if strands are properly transposed and the frequency isn't so high that strand capacitance and proximity losses dominate.
- **Forgetting permeability.** δ scales as $1/\sqrt{\mu_r}$, so steel and nickel (including nickel underplating on some connectors and RF parts) have far smaller skin depths and higher loss.
- **Using $\delta = \sqrt{\rho/(\pi f \mu)}$ with mixed units.** Use ρ in Ω·m, μ in H/m ($\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$), f in Hz to get metres.
:::

::: deep
## Why current avoids the middle

Picture a long round copper wire carrying current $I(t)$. Ampère's law says there's a circular magnetic field around every bit of current, and **inside** the wire the field at radius $r$ comes from the current enclosed within $r$.

When $I(t)$ changes, that internal flux changes. Faraday's law says a changing flux induces an electric field around it, and in a conductor that drives an eddy current. Work out the direction with Lenz's law: the eddy current loops are oriented so they **oppose** the applied current near the axis and **add to** it near the surface. The net effect is that current is pushed outward.

The equivalent circuit view is often easier to say in an interview. Split the wire into concentric shells in parallel. A filament on the axis is encircled by the flux of all the outer shells; a filament on the surface isn't. So the central filaments have more **internal inductance**. At DC only resistance matters and all shells share current equally. At high frequency $\omega L$ dominates and current shifts to the outer, lower-inductance shells.

## The equation and where it comes from

Inside a good conductor, conduction current dwarfs displacement current, so combining $\nabla\times\mathbf H = \sigma\mathbf E$ with $\nabla\times\mathbf E = -j\omega\mu\mathbf H$ gives a diffusion equation:

$$\nabla^2 \mathbf J = j\omega\mu\sigma\,\mathbf J$$

For a flat surface (a good approximation whenever δ is much smaller than the radius of curvature), the solution decaying into the metal is

$$J(d) = J_s\,e^{-(1+j)d/\delta},\qquad \delta = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}} = \sqrt{\frac{\rho}{\pi f \mu_0\mu_r}}$$

Two things to notice: the magnitude falls by $1/e$ per δ, and the **phase lags** by one radian per δ, so deep current is out of phase with surface current. Integrating $|J|$ over depth, the total current equals what you'd get from the surface density flowing uniformly in a layer exactly δ thick. That's why the "current flows in a layer δ thick" shortcut gives the right resistance.

## From δ to AC resistance

If δ is much smaller than the conductor, the current effectively flows in a shell of thickness δ around the perimeter $P$:

$$R_{ac} \approx \frac{\rho L}{P\,\delta}$$

**Worked example: 1 mm diameter copper wire, 1 m long.**
- DC: $A = \pi(0.5\text{ mm})^2 = 0.785$ mm², $R_{dc} = 1.68\times10^{-8}\times1/0.785\times10^{-6} = 21.4$ mΩ.
- At 10 MHz: $\delta = 20.6$ µm, $P = \pi\times1$ mm, so $R_{ac} = \rho L/(\pi D\delta) \approx 259$ mΩ.
- Ratio ≈ 12. (The refined round-wire result $a/2\delta + 1/4$ gives 12.4.)

## Where it bites in real hardware

### Inductors and transformers in switching converters

A buck inductor carries a DC current plus a triangular ripple. The DC part sees DCR; the ripple's fundamental (at $f_{sw}$, often 100 kHz–3 MHz) and its harmonics see a higher AC resistance. At 500 kHz copper's δ is about 92 µm, so any wire thicker than ~0.2 mm is partly wasted for the ripple. In multilayer windings the **proximity effect** makes it worse: each layer sits in the field of the layers beyond it, which induces circulating currents (Dowell's method is the standard way to estimate it). Fixes:
- **Litz wire**: dozens to thousands of insulated strands, each thinner than δ, transposed so each strand spends equal time at the center and edge. Common in wireless-charging coils (~100–200 kHz) and resonant converter transformers.
- **Foil or planar windings**: thin, wide conductors, with thickness chosen near δ.
- **Interleaving** primary and secondary layers to reduce the field build-up that drives proximity loss.

This is the source of the "ACR" that inductor vendors increasingly specify ([[q0051]], [[q0050]]).

### PCB traces at multi-GHz

For a 0.2 mm wide, 35 µm thick, 10 cm long trace: $R_{dc} = 0.24$ Ω. At 1 GHz, δ = 2.06 µm. Over a ground plane, return-current coupling pulls most of the signal current to the trace face nearest the plane, so $R_{ac}$ is somewhere between $\rho L/(2w\delta) \approx 2.0$ Ω (both faces) and $\rho L/(w\delta) \approx 4.1$ Ω (one face): roughly 8–17× the DC value. Conductor loss therefore rises as $\sqrt f$ while dielectric loss rises as $f$ ([[q0058]]), and the two together set how far a 10+ Gb/s link can go.

**Surface roughness** matters because the treated copper surface (to make it stick to the laminate) has peaks of a few µm, comparable to δ. Current following the bumps travels further. Empirical roughness models (Hammerstad, Huray) predict conductor loss increases that saturate around 2× for very rough copper. This is why high-speed laminates offer "VLP"/"HVLP" (very low profile) copper.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Roughness-loss factors depend strongly on the copper foil type, the oxide/adhesion treatment and the model used. Treat "up to ~2×" as a rough guide and use the laminate vendor's data or a field solver for real designs.

### RF and microwave

Since all the current is in the top few µm, plating matters. Silver (lowest ρ) or gold plating on RF connectors and cavities lowers loss; a nickel barrier layer under gold can add loss because nickel is ferromagnetic (small δ, higher ρ). Hollow tubing works as well as solid rod for high-power RF coils and antennas.

### Power and magnetics at low frequency

At 60 Hz copper's δ is 8.4 mm and aluminium's about 10.6 mm, so skin effect only matters for large bus bars and transmission cables. Overhead lines often use aluminium strands around a steel core (ACSR): the outer aluminium carries most of the current, and the steel provides strength. In **magnetic** conductors δ collapses: steel with $\mu_r \approx 200$ has δ ≈ 1.5 mm at 60 Hz. That's why transformer cores are thin insulated laminations (to stop eddy currents) and why induction cooktops (~20–100 kHz) heat steel pans efficiently in a thin surface layer.

## Skin effect vs proximity effect

| | Skin effect | Proximity effect |
|---|---|---|
| Field responsible | conductor's own current | neighbouring conductors' currents |
| Current distribution | pushed to the whole perimeter | pushed to the sides facing (or away from) neighbours |
| Dominant in | isolated wires, single-layer coils | multilayer windings, traces over planes, bundled cables |
| Mitigation | litz, thin/wide conductors | interleaving, fewer layers, litz, spacing |
:::

::: applications
- **Switching-converter inductors**: AC resistance at the ripple frequency drives winding loss beyond DCR. Vendors publish ACR or loss calculators for this reason.
- **Wireless charging coils** (Qi, ~100–200 kHz): typically wound with litz wire to keep coil resistance and heating down.
- **High-speed PCB channels** (PCIe, Ethernet SerDes): conductor loss ∝ $\sqrt f$ plus roughness effects; low-profile copper is specified on premium laminates.
- **RF connectors and cavities**: silver or gold plating because only the top microns carry current.
- **Induction heating and cooktops**: deliberately exploit small δ in ferromagnetic steel to heat a thin surface layer.
- **Power transmission**: ACSR conductors and hollow or segmented bus bars for large AC currents.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Sinusoidal steady state.** For non-sinusoidal currents (square or triangular ripple), apply skin effect harmonic by harmonic; higher harmonics see much higher resistance.
- **Good conductor** (conduction current ≫ displacement current). True for metals up to far beyond microwave frequencies.
- **δ ≪ radius of curvature** for the simple exponential model. For a wire where δ is comparable to the radius, the exact solution uses Bessel functions; the annulus approximation is only rough near the knee.
- **Isolated conductor.** Nearby conductors and return planes add the proximity effect, which changes where current flows and often dominates.
- **Smooth surface and non-magnetic metal.** Rough copper and ferromagnetic platings (nickel) raise loss beyond the simple formula.
:::

::: followups
### What is copper's skin depth at 100 kHz, and does a 1 mm wire care?
$\delta = \sqrt{1.68\times10^{-8}/(\pi\times10^5\times4\pi\times10^{-7})} \approx 206$ µm. The wire's radius is 500 µm, about 2.4δ, so yes: the annulus estimate gives $R_{ac}/R_{dc} \approx 1.5$. For a 100 kHz inductor ripple you'd consider several thinner strands or litz wire.

### Why does resistance scale with √f at high frequency?
Once current lives in a layer of thickness δ around the perimeter, $R \approx \rho L/(P\delta)$. Since $\delta \propto 1/\sqrt f$, $R \propto \sqrt f$. On a log-log plot that's a slope of +10 dB per decade in resistance (a factor of 3.16 per decade).

### Why does litz wire help, and when does it stop helping?
Each strand is thinner than δ, so it carries current uniformly, and the strands are transposed so each spends equal time at every position in the bundle, forcing equal current sharing. That beats both skin and proximity effects. It stops helping when frequency gets high enough that δ approaches the strand diameter (strands would need to be impossibly thin), when the inter-strand capacitance and insulation reduce the copper fill factor too much, or when poor transposition lets proximity currents circulate. In practice litz is most effective up to roughly a few hundred kHz to low MHz.

### What happens to skin depth in a ferromagnetic conductor?
$\delta \propto 1/\sqrt{\mu_r}$. Steel with $\mu_r \approx 200$ and $\rho \approx 10^{-7}$ Ω·m has δ ≈ 1.5 mm at 60 Hz, versus 8.4 mm for copper. That's why solid steel cores would have huge eddy-current loss (hence laminations and ferrites) and why induction heating targets steel.

### What if you made a PCB trace twice as thick to reduce loss at 5 GHz?
It would barely help. At 5 GHz δ ≈ 0.9 µm, far less than 35 µm, so the extra copper carries almost no current. Making the trace wider (more surface area, then retuning the stack-up for impedance), using smoother copper, or a lower-loss dielectric is what actually helps.
