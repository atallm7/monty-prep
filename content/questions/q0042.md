::: testing
A quick check that you know the sign convention for reactance and **why** it is what it is:

1. Inductive reactance is **positive**: $X_L = +\omega L$, from $Z_L = j\omega L$. Capacitive reactance is negative, $X_C = -1/(\omega C)$.
2. What the sign means physically: voltage leads current by 90° (current lags), and the inductor "absorbs" reactive power (positive VAR).
3. You can use the sign: in a series LC, the net reactance tells you whether the combination looks inductive or capacitive, and it flips sign at resonance.
4. You know where the sign can flip in a real part (above the self-resonant frequency).
:::

::: why
The sign of reactance is how you tell, at a glance, whether something is behaving inductively or capacitively. That matters in impedance matching (you cancel +jX with −jX), in reading an impedance analyzer or Smith chart, in power systems (inductive motor loads draw lagging current and need power-factor correction), and in understanding why a decoupling capacitor above its self-resonance is really an inductor. Interviewers pair this with the capacitor version ([[q0026]]) to check the two are straight in your head, not memorized as a coin-flip.
:::

::: concepts
- **Impedance**: $Z = R + jX$; the real part is resistance, the imaginary part is reactance ([[q0040]]).
- **Inductor impedance**: $Z_L = j\omega L$, so $X_L = +\omega L$.
- **Capacitor impedance**: $Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C} = -\frac{j}{\omega C}$, so $X_C = -\frac{1}{\omega C}$ ([[q0026]]).
- **Phase**: positive reactance means voltage leads current; negative means voltage lags.
- **Reactive power**: $Q = I_{rms}^2X$; positive for inductors (they "consume" VAR), negative for capacitors.
- **Resonance**: series L and C cancel when $\omega L = 1/(\omega C)$ ([[q0009]]).
:::

::: answer
"Positive. An inductor's impedance is $j\omega L$, so its reactance is $+\omega L$. A capacitor's impedance is $1/(j\omega C)$, which is $-j/(\omega C)$, so its reactance is negative.

The sign comes straight from the math: the inductor's voltage is proportional to the derivative of current, and differentiating a sinusoid multiplies by $+j\omega$. Physically, positive reactance means the voltage leads the current by 90 degrees, or the current lags. The mnemonic I use is 'ELI the ICE man': in an inductor, L, the voltage E comes before the current I.

The sign is useful because it tells you which way a circuit leans. If I put an inductor and a capacitor in series, I just add the reactances. Below resonance the capacitor's negative reactance is bigger, so the combination looks capacitive. Above resonance the inductor wins and it looks inductive. At resonance they cancel to zero, which is the whole idea behind impedance matching and LC tanks.

One caveat on real parts: a real inductor only has positive reactance up to its self-resonant frequency. Above that, the winding capacitance dominates and the reactance goes negative, so it actually behaves like a capacitor."
:::

::: technical
**Sign.** With $Z = R + jX$:
$$Z_L = j\omega L \Rightarrow X_L = +\omega L > 0,\qquad Z_C = \frac{1}{j\omega C} = -\frac{j}{\omega C} \Rightarrow X_C = -\frac{1}{\omega C} < 0$$

Many textbooks write $X_C = 1/(\omega C)$ as a magnitude and $Z_C = -jX_C$; the signed convention above avoids ambiguity. Either way, the inductor's imaginary part is positive.

**Phase.** $\angle Z_L = +90^\circ$: $V = j\omega L\,I$ leads $I$. For a series RL, $\angle Z = \tan^{-1}(\omega L/R)$ lies between 0° and +90°.

**Admittance.** $Y_L = 1/(j\omega L) = -j/(\omega L)$: the **susceptance** of an inductor is negative, the opposite sign of its reactance. Don't mix the two when working with parallel circuits.

**Reactive power.** $S = P + jQ = I_{rms}^2 Z$. For an inductor $Q = I_{rms}^2\,\omega L > 0$: by convention it absorbs reactive power, and the current lags (lagging power factor). A capacitor supplies reactive power ($Q < 0$), which is why shunt capacitors correct the power factor of inductive loads.

**Combining.** Series reactances add with their signs. 10 µH + 100 nF in series: $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC}) = 159$ kHz. At 100 kHz, $X = 6.28 - 15.92 = -9.63$ Ω (capacitive); at 300 kHz, $X = 18.85 - 5.31 = +13.54$ Ω (inductive).

**Real inductor.** Model $(R + j\omega L)\parallel C_p$. Below the self-resonant frequency $f_{SRF} = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_p})$ the reactance is positive; at SRF it peaks and crosses through zero; above it the reactance is negative ([[q0047]]).

**Smith chart.** Positive reactance is the upper half (inductive), negative the lower half (capacitive).
:::

:::

::: long
"An inductor has positive reactance. Its impedance is $j\omega L$, so the imaginary part, the reactance, is plus omega L. Compare that to a capacitor, whose impedance is one over $j\omega C$. Since one over $j$ is minus $j$, the capacitor's reactance is negative, minus one over omega C.

The sign isn't arbitrary. It comes from the element equations. For an inductor, voltage is L times the derivative of current. Differentiating a sinusoid shifts it forward by 90 degrees, which in phasor form is multiplying by $+j\omega$. So the voltage leads the current. For a capacitor it's the current that's the derivative, so the current leads and the voltage lags. 'ELI the ICE man' captures it: in L, E leads I; in C, I leads E.

Why does the sign matter? Because it lets you combine reactive parts just by adding. Take 10 microhenries in series with 100 nanofarads. At 100 kilohertz, the inductor is about plus 6.3 ohms and the capacitor is about minus 15.9 ohms, so the net is minus 9.6 ohms: the combination looks capacitive. At 300 kilohertz, the inductor's plus 18.9 beats the capacitor's minus 5.3, so it's inductive. In between, around 159 kilohertz, they cancel: that's resonance. Impedance matching works the same way: if an antenna looks like 50 minus j20, you add plus j20 of inductive reactance to cancel it.

In power systems the sign shows up as reactive power. An inductive load like a motor absorbs positive VARs and draws current that lags the voltage, which is why utilities add capacitor banks to cancel it.

The caveat is real parts. A real inductor has capacitance between its turns, so above its self-resonant frequency the net reactance flips negative and the part behaves like a capacitor. So the honest answer is: positive, as long as you're below SRF."
:::

::: terms
- **Reactance (X)**: the imaginary part of impedance; stores rather than dissipates energy.
- **Inductive reactance**: $X_L = +\omega L$, positive and rising with frequency.
- **Capacitive reactance**: $X_C = -1/(\omega C)$, negative and shrinking in magnitude with frequency.
- **Susceptance (B)**: imaginary part of admittance; negative for inductors, positive for capacitors.
- **Lagging power factor**: current lags voltage, characteristic of inductive loads.
- **Reactive power (Q, VAR)**: $I_{rms}^2X$; positive for inductors (absorbed), negative for capacitors (supplied).
- **Resonance**: the frequency where net reactance of an L–C combination is zero.
- **ELI the ICE man**: mnemonic: in L, voltage (E) leads current (I); in C, current leads voltage.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "positive".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Says positive and shows why from $Z_L = j\omega L$, contrasting with $X_C = -1/(\omega C)$.
- Translates the sign into phase (voltage leads current) and gives the time-domain reason ($v \propto di/dt$).
- **Uses** the sign: adds reactances in a series LC with numbers, shows the flip from capacitive to inductive at resonance, or mentions matching networks cancelling reactance.
- Distinguishes reactance (positive) from susceptance (negative) for an inductor.
- Notes that a real inductor's reactance goes negative above SRF, and that some texts quote $X_C$ as a magnitude, so it's worth stating the convention.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Mixing up which one leads.** For an inductor, voltage leads current. If you get confused, re-derive: $v = L\,di/dt$, and the derivative of $\sin$ is $\cos$, which is 90° ahead.
- **Confusing reactance and susceptance signs.** An inductor's reactance is positive, its susceptance is negative. Parallel-circuit calculations use admittance, so be careful.
- **Adding magnitudes instead of signed reactances.** 6.3 Ω of $X_L$ and 15.9 Ω of $X_C$ in series is −9.6 Ω, not 22.2 Ω.
- **Saying "an inductor has negative resistance" or confusing reactance with resistance.** Reactance stores and returns energy; it doesn't dissipate.
- **Assuming a part is inductive at all frequencies.** Above SRF an inductor is capacitive; above its SRF a capacitor is inductive.
:::

::: deep
## Where the sign comes from

Take the element laws and drive them with a sinusoid. Using phasors, $\frac{d}{dt} \to j\omega$:

| | Element law | Phasor form | Impedance | Reactance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inductor | $v = L\,\frac{di}{dt}$ | $V = j\omega L\,I$ | $j\omega L$ | $+\omega L$ |
| Capacitor | $i = C\,\frac{dv}{dt}$ | $I = j\omega C\,V$ | $\frac{1}{j\omega C} = -\frac{j}{\omega C}$ | $-\frac{1}{\omega C}$ |

The asymmetry is just which variable gets differentiated. For the inductor, voltage is the derivative of current, so voltage gets the $+j$ (a 90° lead). For the capacitor it's the other way round, and dividing by $j$ gives $-j$.

In the time domain: if $i = I_p\sin\omega t$, then $v_L = \omega L I_p\cos\omega t = \omega L I_p\sin(\omega t + 90^\circ)$. Voltage peaks a quarter cycle *before* current.

## Using the sign: series LC and resonance

Because reactances in series simply add (with sign), an L and a C in series have net reactance

$$X(\omega) = \omega L - \frac{1}{\omega C}$$

which is negative at low frequency, zero at $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, and positive above. For $L = 10\ \mu$H, $C = 100$ nF, $f_0 = 159$ kHz:

This is exactly how impedance matching works. If an antenna presents $Z = 50 - j20$ Ω at 2.4 GHz, a series inductor with $X_L = +20$ Ω ($L = 20/(2\pi\cdot2.4\times10^9) \approx 1.33$ nH) cancels the capacitive part, leaving a clean 50 Ω.

## The sign in power: lagging current

For a load $Z = R + jX$ carrying RMS current $I$, complex power is $S = I^2 Z = I^2R + jI^2X$. With an inductor, $Q = I^2X_L > 0$: the load "absorbs" reactive power and the current **lags** the voltage. Induction motors, transformers and fluorescent ballasts all make lagging current. Utilities bill large customers for poor power factor, and the fix is a shunt capacitor whose negative reactive power cancels the inductive VARs. Same sign arithmetic, different scale.

## Reactance vs. susceptance: a sign trap

For parallel circuits we use admittance $Y = G + jB$. For an inductor,

$$Y_L = \frac{1}{j\omega L} = -\frac{j}{\omega L} \Rightarrow B_L = -\frac{1}{\omega L} < 0$$

So "inductive" means **positive reactance** but **negative susceptance**. Say which one you mean.

## When a real inductor's reactance goes negative

A real inductor has winding capacitance $C_p$ in parallel. Its impedance is $(R + j\omega L)\parallel 1/(j\omega C_p)$. Ignoring $R$:

$$X(\omega) = \frac{\omega L}{1 - \omega^2 LC_p}$$

Below $f_{SRF} = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC_p})$ the denominator is positive and the reactance is positive, rising faster than $\omega L$ as you approach SRF. Above SRF the denominator goes negative and so does the reactance: the part is capacitive. For 10 µH with 5 pF, SRF is about 22.5 MHz. See [[q0047]].

> [!TIP]
> On a Smith chart or VNA, "inductive" is the upper half and "capacitive" is the lower half. If your inductor shows up in the lower half at your operating frequency, you're above its SRF.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Answer the yes/no first ("positive"), then give the one-line reason ($j\omega L$), then show you can use it (series LC flips sign at resonance). Three sentences, and you've covered fundamentals, derivation and application.
:::

::: applications
- **Antenna and RF matching**: add series or shunt inductors to cancel capacitive reactance and land on 50 Ω.
- **Power-factor correction**: capacitor banks cancel the positive reactive power of inductive motor loads.
- **LC tank circuits and oscillators**: operate where the inductive and capacitive reactances cancel.
- **Crystal oscillators**: a crystal is operated in the narrow band between its series and parallel resonances where it looks inductive, resonating with load capacitors.
- **Decoupling analysis**: above its SRF a capacitor has positive reactance (it's an inductor), which sets how far up in frequency it helps.
- **Measurement**: impedance analyzers report the sign of X (or the phase), which is how you tell whether a part is behaving inductively at a given frequency.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Engineering sign convention** $e^{j\omega t}$ and $Z = R + jX$. Physics texts using $e^{-i\omega t}$ write $Z_L = -i\omega L$; the physical behaviour (voltage leads current) is unchanged.
- **Ideal inductor, or real inductor below its SRF.** Above SRF, the reactance of a real part is negative.
- Some textbooks quote $X_C = 1/(\omega C)$ as a positive magnitude and write $Z_C = -jX_C$. In that convention, both "reactances" are positive numbers and the sign lives in the $\pm j$. State which convention you're using.
- **Passive sign convention**: current enters the + terminal.
:::

::: followups
### What's the net reactance of 22 µH in series with 47 nF at 200 kHz? Is it inductive or capacitive?
$X_L = 2\pi(2\times10^5)(22\times10^{-6}) = 27.6$ Ω; $X_C = -1/(2\pi(2\times10^5)(47\times10^{-9})) = -16.9$ Ω. Net $X = +10.7$ Ω, inductive. Resonance is at $1/(2\pi\sqrt{22\,\mu\cdot47\,n}) \approx 156$ kHz, and 200 kHz is above it, consistent with inductive.

### Why does current lag voltage in an inductor, physically?
The inductor's voltage is set by how fast the current changes. To build up current you must first apply voltage; the current then accumulates over time (it's the integral of voltage divided by L). So the current always trails behind the voltage that's driving it, by exactly a quarter cycle for a sinusoid.

### What happens to the sign of the reactance of a parallel LC as frequency increases?
For an ideal parallel LC, $Z = j\omega L/(1-\omega^2LC)$. Below resonance the inductor's lower impedance dominates and the reactance is positive (inductive); it rises toward infinity at $\omega_0$; above resonance the capacitor dominates and it's negative. It's the opposite sequence from a series LC.

### An impedance analyzer shows your 1 µH inductor with a phase of −80° at 200 MHz. What's going on?
A phase near −90° means it's capacitive: you're above the self-resonant frequency. The winding capacitance now dominates. Choose a part with higher SRF (smaller L, fewer turns, a different construction such as air-core or thin-film) or operate at a lower frequency.

### What if you need to cancel an inductive reactance of +j30 Ω at 10 MHz? What would you add in series?
A capacitor with $X_C = -30$ Ω: $C = 1/(2\pi\cdot10^7\cdot30) \approx 530$ pF. At only that frequency the net series reactance becomes zero; at other frequencies it won't cancel, which is why matching networks are narrowband.
:::

::: interpretations
"Positive **of** negative" is a typo for "positive **or** negative". The question parallels [[q0026]] for capacitors. Some interviewers mean it as a trick about sign conventions, so it's worth mentioning that if $X_C$ is quoted as a magnitude, the "sign" lives in the $-j$; in the signed convention, inductors are positive and capacitors negative.
