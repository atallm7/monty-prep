::: testing
Whether you understand that "ground" on a real board is not one equipotential node and can explain the **consequences** in terms a hardware engineer cares about: logic levels, noise margin, analog accuracy, EMI and damage. The hidden sub-skills:

1. Knowing every logic level and analog measurement is *relative to a local ground*.
2. Turning a ground offset into a noise-margin or LSB budget with numbers.
3. Recognising symptoms (intermittent resets, ADC jumps with load, EMC failures).
4. Linking the effect to its cause (resistance, inductance, shared return) before you propose fixes.
:::

::: why
Ground offset is behind a huge share of "it works on the bench but not in the system" failures: UARTs that drop bytes when a motor starts, ADCs that drift with LED brightness, boards that fail radiated emissions because a cable is being driven by a ground voltage. Because the symptom is rarely labelled "ground", interviewers like this question to see whether you can reason from physics (V = IR + L di/dt) to a field failure. It also sets up the next three questions: the sources ([[q0498]]), the prevention ([[q0499]]) and the system-level resilience ([[q0500]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Ground has impedance**: $Z_g = R_g + j\omega L_g$, so current through it makes a voltage. See [[q0484]], [[q0001]].
- **Logic thresholds are measured against the receiver's local ground**: $V_{IH}$ and $V_{IL}$ are relative to the receiver's pin, not the driver's.
- **Noise margin**: the headroom between a driven level and its threshold, which a ground offset consumes ([[q0004]] for KVL).
- **Common-mode vs differential**: a ground offset is common-mode between two boards and is only rejected if the link is differential.
- **Ground bounce / SSN**: transient ground shifts from simultaneous switching, $V = L\,di/dt$.
- **Loop antennas and cable radiation**: a ground voltage driving an attached cable radiates.
:::

::: answer
"If the ground isn't at the same potential everywhere, then 'zero volts' means different things in different places, and everything on the board that's measured relative to ground gets an error. A digital receiver compares its input against its own local ground, so if the driver's ground is a few hundred millivolts away from the receiver's ground, I've eaten that much noise margin. A 3.3 V logic high might arrive looking like 3.0, and a low might arrive looking like 0.5 instead of 0, and if the offset is dynamic, I get glitches, false triggers or corrupted data.

For analog it's worse because the error goes straight into the measurement: an ADC reference or sensor ground that shifts by a millivolt is one or two LSBs. If the offset is large, I can exceed the absolute-maximum ratings of an input pin, forward bias an ESD diode, or even cause latch-up. And since a voltage between two grounds can drive any cable attached to the board, it also shows up as radiated or conducted EMI.

So I think of the ground as a resistor and inductor network carrying return currents. Anything that makes current flow where it shouldn't, or changes current fast, produces a ground difference, and I'd fix it by making the return impedance low, keeping the noisy returns away from the quiet ones, and using receivers that reject common-mode."
:::

::: technical
**Model.** Between two points A and B on the ground network, the voltage is

$$\Delta V_{AB}(t) = I_{ret}(t)\,R_{AB} + L_{AB}\frac{dI_{ret}}{dt}$$

where $R_{AB}$ and $L_{AB}$ are the path resistance and inductance and $I_{ret}$ includes all return current through that path.

**Digital effect.** A driver at ground A outputs $V_{OH}$ and $V_{OL}$ relative to A. The receiver at B sees $V_{OH} - \Delta V$ and $V_{OL} - \Delta V$, where $\Delta V = V_B - V_A$. Noise margins become $NM_H = (V_{OH}-\Delta V) - V_{IH}$ and $NM_L = V_{IL} - (V_{OL}-\Delta V)$. For 3.3 V LVCMOS with $V_{IH}\approx 2.0$ V and $V_{IL}\approx 0.8$ V, a 600 mV offset leaves either 0.7 V of high-level margin or 0.2 V of low-level margin, depending on its sign. A fast transient offset adds directly to other noise sources.

**Analog effect.** An ADC reading is $V_{in}-V_{AGND}$ relative to its reference. A 1 mV ground shift is 1.2 LSB for a 12-bit, 3.3 V converter (0.81 mV/LSB) and 20 LSB at 16 bit (50 µV/LSB). Shifts correlated with load activity look like gain or offset errors that vary with operating state, which are hard to calibrate away.

**Transient effect (ground bounce).** $N$ outputs switching through shared inductance $L$ with edge current $di/dt$ shift the local ground by $V = N\,L\,di/dt$. For 8 outputs, 5 nH and 20 mA in 1 ns each: $8\times 5\text{ nH}\times 20\text{ mA}/1\text{ ns} = 0.8$ V, enough to make a quiet input toggle.

**Other effects.**
- Exceeding input absolute maximum (typically supply + 0.3 V or ground − 0.3 V) forward-biases ESD structures and can inject current into the substrate, risking latch-up.
- Cables or enclosures referenced to a noisy ground become antennas; a few tens of mV at 100 MHz on a 1 m cable can fail radiated emissions.
- Differential and isolated links reject common-mode offset; single-ended links do not.
:::

::: short
"Every voltage on the board is measured against a local ground, so if grounds differ, I get errors everywhere. Logic levels arrive shifted, so noise margin shrinks and I get bit errors or false triggers. Analog measurements shift by the offset, which can be several LSBs. Large or fast offsets can exceed pin ratings, cause latch-up, or drive cables as antennas and fail EMC. It comes from current times ground impedance, so the fix is to lower that impedance and keep noisy returns apart."
:::

::: long
"The core idea is that a voltage only means something relative to a reference. On a board, 'ground' is really copper with resistance and inductance carrying return currents, so there's a voltage between any two points, V equals I times R plus L di/dt. If that's not negligible, a few things go wrong.

For digital, each receiver compares its input to its own local ground. If the driver and receiver are at different ground potentials, the difference adds to or subtracts from the signal. With 3.3 volt logic and a threshold around 2 volts for a high and 0.8 for a low, a half-volt offset in the wrong direction takes most of my margin, and then any other noise tips it over. Fast offsets, like ground bounce when eight outputs switch together through a few nanohenries, can be most of a volt for a nanosecond, enough for a quiet input to see a glitch, so you get double-clocked counters or corrupted bytes.

For analog it's even more direct. If an ADC's ground is a millivolt away from the sensor's ground, I read a millivolt of error. That's one to two LSBs for 12 bits, twenty for 16. Worst, it varies with what else is running, so it can't be calibrated out.

Beyond signal errors, a big offset can push an input beyond its absolute maximum rating, turning on ESD diodes and potentially latching up the part. And any cable connected to a ground that has noise on it will radiate, so it shows up in EMC testing.

Fixing it starts from the cause: lower impedance return paths with a solid plane, keep noisy returns away from quiet ones, decouple to shrink the di/dt loops, and use differential or isolated signaling where grounds can't be tied together."
:::

::: terms
- **Ground offset (ground shift)**: the voltage between two nominal ground points.
- **Noise margin**: the difference between a signal's valid level and the receiver's threshold.
- **Common-mode voltage**: a voltage that appears equally on both conductors or reference points; a ground offset is common-mode between boards.
- **Ground bounce / SSN**: transient ground shift from simultaneously switching outputs through shared inductance.
- **Return current**: current flowing back to the source through the ground network.
- **Absolute maximum rating**: the limit beyond which damage can occur; inputs are often limited to supply ±0.3 V.
- **Latch-up**: a parasitic thyristor in CMOS triggered by overvoltage or injected current, which can short supply to ground.
- **LSB**: the smallest step an ADC resolves; ground errors are often quoted in LSBs.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "noise" and "errors". An exceptional one:

- Starts from $\Delta V = IR + L\,di/dt$ and says that logic levels and ADC readings are both *relative to local ground*.
- Quantifies: a 600 mV offset leaves 0.2 V low-level margin on 3.3 V logic; 1 mV is 1.2 LSB at 12 bit and 20 LSB at 16 bit.
- Separates **static** (IR drop) from **dynamic** (L di/dt, ground bounce) offsets and gives a number for each.
- Notes the failure modes beyond logic errors: absolute-maximum violations, latch-up, and EMI from cables.
- Points to the right fixes for each, and that differential or isolated links reject offset.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Ground is 0 V, so it can't differ."** Ground is a reference node in the schematic; on the PCB it is copper carrying current.
- **Only thinking of DC.** The transient $L\,di/dt$ part is usually larger for fast digital.
- **Assuming the driver's output swing is what the receiver sees.** The receiver sees it relative to *its own* ground.
- **Blaming the signal trace** when the signal is clean at the driver but corrupt at the receiver: check the ground between them.
- **Probing with a long ground lead.** A scope ground clip adds its own inductance and can fake or mask a ground offset ([[q0492]]).
:::

::: deep
## First principles: a voltage needs a reference

Voltage is a difference. A logic receiver measures the difference between its input pin and its own ground pin. If you move that ground pin by 400 mV relative to the driver's ground, the receiver has no way to know. This is exactly like measuring the height of a table with a ruler resting on the floor: if the floor under the ruler is different from the floor under the table, you get the wrong height.

## Digital: noise margin eaten

Let the receiver ground be $\Delta$ above the driver ground. The receiver sees $V_{OH}-\Delta$ and $V_{OL}-\Delta$. For 3.3 V LVCMOS (using $V_{OH}\approx 3.3$ V, $V_{OL}\approx 0$, $V_{IH}=2.0$ V, $V_{IL}=0.8$ V, typical LVTTL-like thresholds):

$$NM_H = 3.3-\Delta-2.0 = 1.3-\Delta,\qquad NM_L = 0.8-(0-\Delta) = 0.8+\Delta$$

The signal is only safe while blue stays above 2.0 V and red below 0.8 V, which limits you to roughly −0.8 V < Δ < +1.3 V even with zero other noise, and far less once other noise is added.

Add the other noise sources (crosstalk, supply ripple, reflections) and the practical tolerance for ground offset is perhaps a few hundred millivolts. For 1.8 V or 1.2 V logic the budget is much smaller. Faster transients (ground bounce) are brief, but if they coincide with a clock edge they cause false triggers.

## Analog: the offset goes straight into the answer

An ADC measures $V_{in}-V_{GND}$ relative to a reference. Ground shifts therefore add directly:

| Resolution | LSB at 3.3 V | A 1 mV ground shift is |
|---|---|---|
| 10 bit | 3.2 mV | 0.31 LSB |
| 12 bit | 0.81 mV | 1.2 LSB |
| 16 bit | 50 µV | 20 LSB |
| 24 bit (sensor ±few mV range) | sub-µV | thousands |

A shift that depends on what else is running (an LED, a radio, a motor) shows up as a reading that changes with load. Because it correlates with activity, calibration at one operating point doesn't fix it.

## Beyond logic and ADCs

- **Overvoltage on pins.** Most CMOS inputs have ESD diodes to both rails. If the input voltage exceeds supply + 0.3 V or falls below ground − 0.3 V relative to *that chip's* pins, the diode conducts. The injected current can lift the substrate and in the worst case trigger latch-up.
- **Cables as antennas.** A cable's shield or return connects to the board ground. The ground noise voltage drives the cable against its distributed capacitance to the environment, and the cable radiates. This is a classic cause of failing radiated-emissions tests: even tens of millivolts at 100 MHz can matter.
- **Common-mode between boards.** Connecting two boards through a single-ended link also connects their ground offsets. Differential signalling (RS-485, LVDS, CAN) rejects common-mode within its range; isolators remove the dependency entirely ([[q0500]]).
- **Reference and bias shifts.** Op-amp and comparator thresholds, bandgap references and PLL/VCO supply nodes all reference ground. A shift appears as jitter, offset or phase noise.

## A quick diagnostic

1. Measure ground-to-ground with a short spring-tip ground on a scope across the two suspect points while the system runs. A flat line is good; a pulse train correlated with a load or clock is the offset.
2. Compare the received waveform with the driven waveform *both referenced to the receiver's ground*. If they differ, the ground path is guilty.
3. Look at the layout: where does the return of the noisy load go? Is it crossing under the sensitive part?

> [!INTERVIEW]
> Offer a number: "A 500 mV offset on 3.3 V logic leaves about 0.3 V of low-level margin, and a 1 mV shift is a full LSB on a 12-bit ADC." Numbers show you can budget, not just say "noise".

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Logic thresholds vary by family (LVCMOS thresholds are often $0.3V_{DD}$/$0.7V_{DD}$ rather than 0.8/2.0 V). The figures here use the common LVTTL-style values for illustration; check the datasheet for the parts in question.
:::

::: applications
- **UART or SPI between two boards** sharing a thin ground wire: byte errors when a motor starts.
- **Precision ADC board** where reading changes with an LED or a relay: ground shift in the analog return.
- **Microcontroller with many GPIOs switching**: ground bounce causes spurious resets or edges on quiet pins.
- **Automotive and industrial links (CAN, RS-485)**: ground offsets of several volts between nodes are expected; the standards specify common-mode ranges.
- **Failing EMC scans**: a cable radiating because a few tens of millivolts of ground noise drive its shield.
:::

::: assumptions
- Return impedance is dominated by the ground network between the two points (not by the signal trace).
- Logic thresholds used are 2.0 V (V_IH) and 0.8 V (V_IL) for 3.3 V logic; modern LVCMOS often uses 0.7 V_DD and 0.3 V_DD, which tighten the budget.
- A 12-bit, 3.3 V ADC is used for LSB numbers; the same arithmetic applies to any converter.
- If the link is differential or isolated the logic-level argument largely disappears, but analog reference and EMI effects remain.
- Another valid answer focuses on the high-speed view: ground offset between a driver and receiver is part of the return-path discontinuity and increases jitter and crosstalk.
:::

::: followups
### How much ground offset can 3.3 V logic tolerate?
With V_IH 2.0 V and V_IL 0.8 V and ideal drivers, about −0.8 V to +1.3 V, but that assumes no other noise. In practice you want the ground offset below roughly 100–200 mV at the receiver, because supply ripple, crosstalk and reflections consume the rest of the margin. For 1.8 V logic the tolerance is correspondingly smaller.

### How does ground offset appear on an ADC reading?
It adds directly as an error. At 12 bits and 3.3 V, 1 mV is about 1.2 LSB. If the offset correlates with load, such as an LED switching, the reading changes whenever the LED turns on. Averaging helps with random noise, not with a correlated offset.

### How would you measure the ground difference between two points on a board?
With a scope, a very short ground spring on one point and the probe tip on the other, while the system runs. A long ground lead adds its own inductance and fakes ringing ([[q0492]]). I'd look at the amplitude and what it's synchronous with: the clock, an LED, a switching regulator.

### What if the two boards connect only by a thin cable ground?
The cable ground has resistance and inductance, so return current produces a voltage between the boards. Add more ground conductors, use a differential link or isolation, and reduce the return current by adding local decoupling. If the cable shield is carrying return current, that radiates.

### Scenario: UART data corrupts only when a servo moves. What is your diagnosis?
The servo's current pulses flow through a ground segment shared with the UART return, creating a transient offset. I'd scope the receiver's ground against the transmitter's ground, move the servo return to its own path to the supply, add bulk and ceramic capacitance at the servo, and if the boards are separate, use a differential or isolated link.
:::

::: interpretations
"Ground plane potential differs throughout your board" most naturally means that different regions of the *same* board sit at different DC or transient voltages because of current flowing through the plane's impedance. A second reading is a board-to-board difference. The answer covers both; the sources are in [[q0498]], prevention is in [[q0499]], and system-level resilience in [[q0500]].
:::
