::: testing
Whether you can explain W/L as the **designer's knob** that sets how strong a transistor is, and also say what it costs. The interviewer wants:

1. The definition: $W$ = channel width (perpendicular to current), $L$ = channel length (source to drain), and $k=\mu C_{ox}W/L$.
2. The effects: drive current $\propto W/L$, on-resistance $\propto L/W$, transconductance $g_m\propto W/L$.
3. The cost: gate capacitance $\propto WL$, so a stronger transistor loads whatever drives it and burns more dynamic power; plus the roles of $L$ separately (speed, output resistance, matching) and PMOS sizing in CMOS.
:::

::: why
In IC design, W/L is the primary parameter you choose for every device. In board-level work it explains why a MOSFET with 5 mΩ $R_{DS(on)}$ is a physically huge die with gigantic gate charge, and why gate drivers must deliver big pulses. In digital design it is the basis of logical effort and buffer sizing ([[q0111]]). Interviewers ask it because the answer tests whether you can link layout geometry, the square-law equations, and system consequences (speed, power, area).
:::

::: concepts
- **Geometry**: $W$ is the width of the gate over active area; $L$ is the gate length between source and drain. Layout designers choose both.
- **Process transconductance** $k'=\mu C_{ox}$, set by the process; **device** $k=k'(W/L)$. See [[q0095]].
- **Saturation current / on-resistance**: $I_D=\tfrac12k V_{ov}^2$, $R_{on}\approx1/(kV_{ov})$. See [[q0099]].
- **Gate capacitance**: $C_g\approx C_{ox}WL$, a tax on every strong device.
- **CMOS sizing**: PMOS about 2-3 times wider to match NMOS. See [[q0097]].
- **Channel-length effects**: shorter $L$ is faster but lowers $r_o$ and worsens leakage and matching.
:::

::: answer
"W/L is the width-to-length ratio of the transistor's channel. The length is the distance between source and drain that the carriers travel, and the width is how wide the channel is, so you can think of the transistor as many parallel strips of channel. A wider channel carries more current, and a longer channel resists it more, so the drive strength scales with W/L. In the equations it's inside $k=\mu C_{ox}W/L$: the saturation current is $\tfrac12k(V_{GS}-V_t)^2$, the transconductance is $k\,V_{ov}$, and the on-resistance is about $1/(k\,V_{ov})$. Double W/L and you double the current and gm and halve $R_{on}$.

The catch is that gate capacitance goes up with the gate area, $W\times L$, so a bigger W costs more input capacitance, which slows whatever drives it and increases dynamic power. In a CMOS inverter that's the trade: wider devices switch the load faster but present more load to the previous stage. You also typically make the PMOS about 2 to 3 times wider than the NMOS to compensate for hole mobility.

Length is chosen separately. Minimum L gives the speed in digital logic. In analog, you use longer L for higher output resistance and better matching. And in power MOSFETs, a huge effective W/L, from millions of parallel cells, is how you get milliohms."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.** $W$: gate width (current flows across it); $L$: gate length (current flows along it). Square-law:

$$I_D=\tfrac12\mu C_{ox}\frac WL(V_{GS}-V_t)^2(1+\lambda V_{DS}),\qquad R_{on}\approx\frac{1}{\mu C_{ox}\frac WL V_{ov}},\qquad g_m=\mu C_{ox}\frac WLV_{ov}=\frac{2I_D}{V_{ov}}$$

**Scaling with W/L (fixed L, so $W$ scales):**

| Quantity | Dependence on $W$ at fixed $L$ |
|---|---|
| $I_D$, $g_m$ | $\propto W$ |
| $R_{on}$ | $\propto 1/W$ |
| Gate capacitance $C_g\approx C_{ox}WL$ | $\propto W$ |
| Drain/source junction cap $C_j$ | $\propto W$ |
| Intrinsic speed ($f_T\approx g_m/2\pi C_{gs}$) | independent of $W$ |
| Leakage (subthreshold, gate) | $\propto W$ |

**Intrinsic speed vs L.** With $C_{gs}\approx\tfrac23C_{ox}WL$: $f_T\approx\dfrac{3\mu V_{ov}}{4\pi L^2}$ (long-channel). For $\mu=0.04$ m²/V·s, $V_{ov}=0.3$ V, $L=1$ µm: about 2.9 GHz; it scales as $1/L^2$ in the long-channel model. Velocity saturation in short channels reduces this to roughly $1/L$, so the textbook formula overestimates short-channel speed.

**Output resistance.** $r_o\approx1/(\lambda I_D)$ with $\lambda\propto1/L$. Longer $L$ gives higher intrinsic gain $g_mr_o$; analog designers use $L$ several times the minimum.

**Examples.** (i) $k'=200$ µA/V², $V_{ov}=2.5$ V: W/L = 10 gives $R_{on}=1/(200\mu\cdot10\cdot2.5)=200$ Ω; W/L = 20 gives 100 Ω. (ii) To get $I_D=1$ mA at $V_{ov}=0.25$ V: $k=2I_D/V_{ov}^2=32$ mA/V², so $W/L=32\text{ m}/200\mu=160$; $g_m=8$ mS. (iii) A 5 mΩ power MOSFET at $V_{ov}=5$ V would correspond to an effective $W/L\approx1/(200\mu\cdot5\text{ m}\cdot5)=2\times10^5$ in this idealized model; real power devices have a different structure, but the idea of an enormous parallel width holds.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The $k'$ and mobility values above are illustrative for a generic process. Real power MOSFETs use vertical structures with different channel mobility and drift-region resistance, so $R_{on}$ is not just $1/(kV_{ov})$; treat example (iii) as a scaling illustration.
:::

::: short
"W/L is channel width over channel length, and it sets the transistor's strength. In the model $k=\mu C_{ox}W/L$, so drain current, transconductance and conductance all scale with W/L, and on-resistance scales with L/W. The cost is gate capacitance, which grows with W times L, so a stronger device loads its driver and uses more dynamic power. You use wider devices for speed and drive, longer L for output resistance and matching, and in CMOS you make the PMOS about 2 to 3 times wider to match the NMOS."
:::

::: long
"W/L is the aspect ratio of the channel: width divided by length. Picture the channel as a strip: carriers flow along the length from source to drain, and the width is how many parallel lanes there are. A wider channel is like more parallel resistors, so more current; a longer channel is like more series resistance, so less current. That's why everything lives inside $k=\mu C_{ox}W/L$. Saturation current is half k times overdrive squared, transconductance is $k$ times overdrive, and the on-resistance is about 1 over $k$ times overdrive. Double the W/L and you double the current and the gm, and halve the $R_{on}$.

What does it cost? The gate is a capacitor of area W times L, so gate capacitance rises with W. At fixed L, a transistor twice as wide is twice as strong but also presents twice the input capacitance and twice the junction capacitance, so the *intrinsic* speed, which goes like gm over C, doesn't change. What changes is that it can drive a *bigger external load* faster. That is the basis of buffer chains: each stage is a few times wider than the last. It also leaks more and burns more dynamic power, $C V^2 f$.

L plays a different role. Short L means higher speed and density, which is why digital logic uses the minimum. But longer L gives higher output resistance, since channel-length modulation shrinks, and better matching, so analog circuits like current mirrors use L several times the minimum.

In CMOS I size the PMOS about 2 to 3 times wider than the NMOS because hole mobility is lower, so rise and fall times match. And for discrete power MOSFETs, a 5 milliohm part is basically an enormous parallel W/L built from thousands of cells, which also explains its large gate charge."
:::

::: terms
- **W/L (aspect ratio)**: channel width divided by channel length.
- **Process transconductance ($k'=\mu C_{ox}$)**: the technology-dependent factor.
- **Device transconductance parameter ($k$)**: $k'W/L$ in A/V².
- **Transconductance ($g_m$)**: $dI_D/dV_{GS}=kV_{ov}$ in saturation.
- **On-resistance ($R_{on}$)**: $1/(kV_{ov})$ in deep triode.
- **Gate capacitance ($C_g$)**: about $C_{ox}WL$; the input load a stage presents.
- **Channel-length modulation ($\lambda$)**: shortens effective $L$ with $V_{DS}$; larger for short channels.
- **Fingers / multiplier**: layout technique to realize large W as parallel fingers.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer defines W/L and says "bigger means more current." An exceptional answer:

- Shows that **$I_D$, $g_m$ scale with W/L and $R_{on}$ with L/W** from the equations, with a number.
- Separates the roles of **W and L**: W buys drive at the cost of capacitance; L buys $r_o$, matching and low leakage at the cost of speed.
- States that gate capacitance scales as $WL$, hence **intrinsic speed doesn't improve with W**, only drive capability for a given external load.
- Ties to design practice: PMOS sizing, tapered buffers ([[q0111]]), analog long-L devices, power MOSFET cells.
- Mentions layout fingers, matching ($\sigma\propto1/\sqrt{WL}$) and leakage scaling with W.
- Flags where the square-law idealization fails (velocity saturation, short-channel effects).
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Wider is always better."** More W means more gate capacitance, junction capacitance, leakage and area, and slows the previous stage.
- **Believing W/L alone sets speed.** Intrinsic speed depends on $L$ (and overdrive); W sets the *drive strength* for a given load.
- **Mixing up W and L.** $I_D\propto W/L$: width is in the numerator.
- **Thinking longer L is useless.** Long channels give higher $r_o$ and gain, lower leakage and much better matching, which analog circuits need.
- **Forgetting the PMOS penalty.** Equal W/L gives the PMOS only 40% or so of the NMOS drive.
- **Using the square law for short-channel devices.** Velocity saturation makes $I_D$ closer to linear in $V_{ov}$ and the benefit of reducing $L$ smaller than $1/L^2$.
:::

::: deep
## What W and L physically are

A MOSFET's channel is a thin sheet of mobile charge under the gate. Current flows from source to drain along the **length** $L$. The **width** $W$ is the dimension perpendicular to the flow. In layout you see $W$ as the length of the gate stripe lying across the active region, and $L$ as the (much smaller) width of that polysilicon stripe.

## What changes with W/L

At fixed $V_{ov}$ with $k'=200$ µA/V², compute for a range of aspect ratios:

| W/L | $k$ (mA/V²) | $I_D$ at $V_{ov}=0.5$ V (µA) | $g_m$ (mS) | $R_{on}$ at $V_{ov}=2.5$ V (Ω) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0.4 | 50 | 0.2 | 1000 |
| 10 | 2 | 250 | 1.0 | 200 |
| 40 | 8 | 1000 | 4.0 | 50 |
| 160 | 32 | 4000 | 16 | 12.5 |

Everything scales linearly. If a 1 mA stage needs $g_m=8$ mS at $V_{ov}=0.25$ V, W/L must be 160. That is a design rule: first choose the bias current and overdrive, then W/L falls out as $W/L=2I_D/(k'V_{ov}^2)$.

## The cost: capacitance and the gate-drive tax

$C_g\approx C_{ox}WL$. For $C_{ox}=3.45$ fF/µm² (a 10 nm oxide), $W=10$ µm, $L=1$ µm: 34.5 fF. Make $W=40$ µm and it is 138 fF. The previous stage has to charge that capacitance every cycle, with energy $\tfrac12CV^2$ per edge and dynamic power $CV^2f$ ([[q0110]]).

At the same time the device's own drain junction capacitance also grows with $W$. So a bigger transistor is a better driver for a *fixed external load* but, driving itself, no faster:

$$\tau\approx R_{on}(C_{self}+C_{load})\propto\frac LW\left(WLC'+C_{load}\right)=L^2C'+\frac{L\,C_{load}}{W}$$

The first term doesn't depend on W (it's the intrinsic delay), the second falls as $1/W$. So increasing W helps until the load term becomes small relative to the self-loading term, after which extra W only wastes area, power and the previous stage's drive. That's the reasoning behind buffer chains with a stage ratio of about 3-4 ([[q0111]]).

## CMOS sizing

For a symmetrical inverter with equal rise and fall times, size so that $k_n=k_p$:

$$\frac{(W/L)_p}{(W/L)_n}=\frac{\mu_n}{\mu_p}\approx2\text{-}3$$

so the switching threshold is near $V_{DD}/2$ and $t_{pLH}\approx t_{pHL}$ ([[q0105]]). Making the PMOS smaller saves area and input capacitance but slows rising edges; some designs deliberately skew sizing for speed on a critical edge.

## Why L isn't always minimum

| Property | Short $L$ | Long $L$ |
|---|---|---|
| Speed ($f_T$) | high | low |
| Output resistance $r_o$ | low (large $\lambda$) | high |
| Intrinsic gain $g_mr_o$ | low | high |
| Matching ($\sigma_{V_t}\propto1/\sqrt{WL}$) | poor for small area | better |
| Leakage | worse (short-channel effects, DIBL) | better |
| Area | small | large |

Digital logic uses near-minimum $L$. Analog circuits (current mirrors, differential pairs, op-amp input stages) use longer $L$ ([[q0104]]). Matching improves with gate area $WL$ (Pelgrom's rule), which is why precision pairs are drawn large.

## Real hardware

- **Power MOSFETs** are built from huge numbers of small parallel cells; $R_{DS(on)}$ falls with total cell area, and so does the die's gate charge $Q_g$ and capacitance. Datasheet figures like $R_{DS(on)}\times Q_g$ are the figure of merit that captures this trade ([[q0102]]).
- **Short-channel effects**: velocity saturation, drain-induced barrier lowering and gate leakage mean that the textbook scaling only holds roughly.
- **Gate resistance**: very wide gates have noticeable distributed gate resistance, which is why large devices are folded into fingers.

## Worked example: sizing from $g_m/I_D$ rather than from a guess at W/L

The table above runs forward, from W/L to current. In practice you run it backward. A common analog sizing method starts from the efficiency metric $g_m/I_D$, which in square-law strong inversion equals $2/V_{ov}$ and in weak inversion approaches $1/(nV_T)\approx30$ V⁻¹ (taking $n\approx1.3$). Suppose a stage needs a bias current $I_D=100\ \mu$A at $g_m/I_D=10\ \text{V}^{-1}$ (a moderate-inversion compromise between gain per milliamp and speed). Then, with $k'=200\ \mu$A/V²:

1. $g_m=10\times100\ \mu\text{A}=1$ mS.
2. $V_{ov}=2/(g_m/I_D)=0.2$ V.
3. $W/L=\dfrac{2I_D}{k'V_{ov}^2}=\dfrac{2(100\ \mu\text{A})}{(200\ \mu\text{A/V}^2)(0.04\ \text{V}^2)}=25$.

Had you asked for $g_m/I_D=20$ (nearly weak inversion), $V_{ov}=0.1$ V and $W/L=100$: four times the width for twice the efficiency, and four times the gate capacitance. This illustrates the real trade-off of sizing: width buys transconductance per unit current at the cost of capacitance and speed. The $f_T=g_m/(2\pi C_g)$ of the device falls as you widen at fixed current, so low-power and high-speed designs sit at opposite ends of the $g_m/I_D$ axis.

## Worked example: how large must a matched pair be?

Mismatch between two nominally identical transistors is random and falls with gate area. Pelgrom's model gives the standard deviation of the threshold-voltage difference as
$$\sigma(\Delta V_t)=\frac{A_{VT}}{\sqrt{WL}}$$
with a process-dependent coefficient. A common rule of thumb is that $A_{VT}$ is of the order of 1 mV·µm for every nanometer of oxide thickness; for the 10 nm oxide used on this page that suggests about 10 mV·µm (illustrative, not data for any process). For a pair with $W=10\ \mu$m and $L=1\ \mu$m the area is 10 µm², so
$$\sigma(\Delta V_t)=\frac{10\ \text{mV}\cdot\mu\text{m}}{\sqrt{10\ \mu\text{m}^2}}=3.2\ \text{mV}$$
Translate that into a current mismatch for a mirror biased at $V_{ov}=0.2$ V. A threshold shift $\Delta V_t$ changes the current by $\Delta I/I=(g_m/I_D)\Delta V_t=(2/V_{ov})\Delta V_t=10\ \text{V}^{-1}\times3.2\ \text{mV}=3.2\ \%$. Adding a current-factor mismatch of, say, $1.5\ \%\cdot\mu\text{m}/\sqrt{10\ \mu\text{m}^2}=0.47\ \%$ (an illustrative coefficient) combines in quadrature to $\sqrt{3.2^2+0.47^2}=3.2\ \%$, dominated by $V_t$. To halve the mismatch you need four times the area: a 20 µm × 2 µm pair has $\sigma(\Delta V_t)=1.6$ mV and about 1.6 % current mismatch. Increasing the overdrive also helps, because $g_m/I_D$ falls: at $V_{ov}=0.4$ V the same 3.2 mV gives only 1.6 %. So precision mirrors use large area and high overdrive, whereas minimum-size logic transistors have mismatch of tens of millivolts and rely on digital noise margins instead.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The coefficients $A_{VT}$ and the current-factor term vary from process to process and with doping; foundry design kits give them. The "1 mV·µm per nm" rule is only a rough guide.

## Worked example: how many fingers, and what does each cost?

Take the 160 µm total width needed for the 8 mS stage above, built from $N=40$ fingers of $W_f=4\ \mu$m each. The reasons for fingers are electrical as well as geometric. A single 160 µm strip of polysilicon gate has a large series gate resistance; with a typical silicided poly sheet resistance of 8 Ω/□ (an assumed value), one finger of $W_f=2\ \mu$m and $L=0.18\ \mu$m has $R_{total}=8\times(2/0.18)=89\ \Omega$ along its length, and because the gate is charged along its length from one end the effective lumped resistance is about one third of that, 30 Ω, or one twelfth (7.4 Ω) if contacted at both ends. Add the capacitance and you have an RC delay in the gate itself that grows with $W^2$ for a single strip, but stays small when the transistor is subdivided, because the fingers are in parallel (their gate resistances divide by $N$) and each is short.

Fingers also share diffusion regions between neighbors: with $N$ fingers and shared source/drain strips, only about $N/2+1$ drain regions exist, so the drain junction capacitance per unit width is roughly halved compared with $N$ separate transistors. That is another reason the layout style improves speed.

## Layout effects on the effective W and L

The drawn W and L are not what the transistor sees. Lateral diffusion of source/drain under the gate shortens the electrical channel ($L_{eff}=L-2L_D$), and field-oxide encroachment narrows the effective width ($W_{eff}=W-\Delta W$). For a wide device these corrections are negligible: a 10 µm width with $\Delta W=0.05\ \mu$m is 0.5 % smaller. For a narrow device, they dominate: a drawn $W=0.5\ \mu$m with the same $\Delta W$ has an effective width 10 % smaller than drawn. In addition, stress from shallow-trench isolation shifts mobility and threshold depending on how far the gate sits from the trench edge, which causes systematic differences between devices of the same W/L. Analog layout therefore uses identical, dummy-flanked unit cells in common-centroid arrangements, so the effects cancel in a ratio.

## Verifying W/L behavior on the bench

- **Compare test structures.** If you have access to a test chip or matched discrete array (a quad MOSFET package, for instance), measure $I_D$ at the same $V_{GS}$ and $V_{DS}$ for devices of different W/L. The current ratio should equal the W/L ratio within a few percent for wide, long devices, and deviate for narrow or short ones.
- **Extract $k'$ and $V_t$.** From two $\sqrt{I_D}$ points, as in [[q0099]], then check $k=k'W/L$.
- **Measure matching.** Measure the same current in several nominally identical devices at a fixed $V_{GS}$ and compute the standard deviation of $I_D/\bar I_D$. Compare against $(g_m/I_D)\,A_{VT}/\sqrt{WL}$. If a pair mismatches more than predicted, look at layout orientation, thermal gradients and gate-oxide differences.
- **Capacitance and speed.** Measure the input capacitance of a large device with an LCR meter at the gate: it should scale with $WL$ and be about 3.45 fF/µm² for a 10 nm oxide. A ring oscillator of inverters at different W/L ratios shows the frequency change from the $1/W$ and $W$ terms of the delay equation.
:::

::: applications
- **Sizing a CMOS inverter or buffer**: choosing $(W/L)_p\approx2.5(W/L)_n$ and tapering stages to drive large loads.
- **Analog biasing**: picking W/L from the target $I_D$ and overdrive for gain stages and current mirrors ([[q0104]]).
- **Current-mirror ratios**: mirroring by setting W/L ratios (1:10 for a 10× copy).
- **Choosing power MOSFETs**: lower $R_{DS(on)}$ parts have larger die, higher $Q_g$, and need stronger gate drive.
- **Analog switches**: wide NMOS/PMOS pairs for low $R_{on}$ at the cost of charge injection and capacitance.
- **I/O drivers**: very wide output transistors set drive strength and slew rate.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Square-law, long-channel** model with $\lambda$ ignored; short-channel devices follow a velocity-saturation model with weaker dependence on $L$.
- **Uniform mobility** independent of W, L and orientation. Real devices have narrow-width and short-channel effects, and layout stress can change mobility.
- **Gate capacitance** approximated as $C_{ox}WL$ (the oxide capacitance), plus overlap and fringe terms ignored.
- **Process values** ($k'$, $C_{ox}$) are generic examples.
- For **power devices**, "W/L" is an effective parameter and drift-region resistance dominates at high voltage ratings.
:::

::: followups
### If I double W, what happens to current, gm, $R_{on}$, gate capacitance and intrinsic speed?
Current and $g_m$ double, $R_{on}$ halves, gate capacitance doubles, and the intrinsic speed ($g_m/C_{gs}$) is unchanged. The benefit is the ability to drive a larger external load.

### How do you size a MOSFET for 2 mA at 0.3 V overdrive with $k'=200$ µA/V²?
$W/L=2I_D/(k'V_{ov}^2)=4\text{ mA}/(200\mu\cdot0.09)=222$. That's a wide device, and $g_m=2I_D/V_{ov}=13.3$ mS. If the capacitance is too large, raise $V_{ov}$ and accept less $g_m$ per amp.

### Why is the PMOS typically drawn 2-3x wider in a CMOS gate?
Hole mobility is 2-3x lower, so the same W/L gives about 40% of the drive. Scaling W by the mobility ratio equalizes rise and fall times and puts the switching threshold near $V_{DD}/2$.

### Why would an analog designer pick $L$ much longer than the minimum?
Longer $L$ gives higher $r_o$ (less channel-length modulation), so higher $g_mr_o$ gain and better current-source accuracy, and much better matching because $\sigma_{V_t}\propto1/\sqrt{WL}$. Cost: area and lower speed.

### Harder: why doesn't making a transistor ever wider keep reducing the delay?
Once the load capacitance is small relative to the device's own parasitics, the delay saturates at the intrinsic value $\propto L^2$: the self-loading terms scale with W just as the drive does. Past that point extra width just costs area, power and the previous stage's speed. Optimal buffer chains use a stage ratio of about 3-4.

### Scenario: a power switch with a 5 mΩ MOSFET switches slowly on your 3.3 V gate driver, but a 20 mΩ part switches fast. Why?
The 5 mΩ part is effectively a much larger W/L, with correspondingly larger gate capacitance and gate charge $Q_g$. The same driver current takes longer to charge it: $t\approx Q_g/I_{drive}$. Check $Q_g$ and the driver's current capability, and choose based on the conduction vs switching loss trade-off at your frequency.
