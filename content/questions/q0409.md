::: basics
### In plain words
A motor, solenoid, or relay coil is an inductor, which resists any sudden change in its current. The simplest safe way to drive one is a MOSFET switch between the load and ground, controlled by a microcontroller pin. Always add a flyback diode across the load, so the current has somewhere to go when the switch opens. You vary speed or hold force with PWM. Size the switch for the starting surge, not the running current.

### Picture it
Picture a heavy truck rolling at speed. It cannot stop instantly, however hard the driver brakes. A road with a runaway-truck ramp gives it somewhere safe to slow down. The coil's current is the truck's momentum. The switch opening is the brake. The flyback diode is the escape ramp. Without a ramp, the truck plows into whatever is ahead, and in a circuit that is usually the MOSFET.

### Words you need
- **MOSFET (low-side)**: a transistor used as a fast electronic switch. "Low-side" means it sits between the load and ground.
- **Flyback (freewheel) diode**: a diode across the load that carries the coil's current after the switch opens. See [[q0340]].
- **Inrush / stall current**: the big current at start-up or when stuck, $V/R$, because a motor that is not turning makes no back-EMF (opposing voltage). See [[q0343]].
- **PWM duty cycle**: PWM switches on and off rapidly. The duty cycle, the fraction of each period the switch is on, sets the average voltage.
- **Time constant**: $L/R$, the time for the coil's current to fall to about 37% of its starting value.

### The simplest example
A 24 V solenoid coil of 24 Ω and 100 mH draws 1 A, and stores $\tfrac12 \times 0.1 \times 1^2 = 50$ mJ. When the MOSFET turns off, the diode catches that current. It decays with a time constant of $0.1/24 = 4.17$ ms, falling from 1 A to 0.1 A in about 9.6 ms. To hold the plunger cheaply, run PWM at 33% duty: the average is 8 V, so the hold current is about 0.33 A, instead of 1 A.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers sizing the MOSFET, diode, and gate resistors, a Zener diode for faster release, and PWM ripple.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can turn "a motor or solenoid is an inductor with a mechanical side" into a complete, safe, working drive circuit. The expected skeleton is a logic-level low-side MOSFET driven from a microcontroller, a freewheel (flyback) diode across the load, a gate resistor and pull-down, and PWM for speed or hold current. The hidden checks are: do you know why the diode is needed, do you size the FET for inrush and stall rather than running current, and do you know when to move up to a half-bridge, H-bridge or driver IC.
:::

::: why
Nearly every actuator on a board (fans, pumps, valves, relays, vibration motors, latches) is driven this way. A bare transistor switching a coil will usually work on the bench and fail in the field: the inductive spike kills the FET, or the GPIO is overloaded, or the FET runs half-on and cooks. This question is the entry point to the whole drive-circuit topic ([[q0410]] to [[q0419]]) and to the embedded-interface questions ([[q0429]] onward).
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor current continuity**: current in $L$ cannot change instantly, so turning the switch off forces the voltage to whatever it takes: $v = L\,\mathrm{d}i/\mathrm{d}t$. See [[q0340]].
- **Freewheel (flyback) diode**: gives the current somewhere to go when the switch opens.
- **Low-side switch**: an N-channel MOSFET between the load and ground; easy to drive from logic. See [[q0426]] for the high-side case.
- **PWM**: switching fast so the load's inductance averages the voltage; duty sets speed or hold current.
- **Inrush / stall current**: motors and solenoids draw $V/R$, far above the running current ([[q0343]]).
- **Gate drive**: gate charge, threshold, resistor and pull-down ([[q0430]], [[q0431]]).
:::

::: answer
"The simplest robust way is a low-side N-channel MOSFET. The load connects from the supply to the MOSFET's drain, the source goes to ground, and the microcontroller drives the gate, through a small series resistor, with a pull-down so the FET stays off while the MCU is booting. I'd pick a logic-level FET so a 3.3 V GPIO fully turns it on.

The critical part is the flyback diode across the load, cathode to the supply side. When the FET turns off, the inductor current can't stop instantly. Without a diode it forces the drain voltage up until something breaks down, usually the FET. With the diode, the current recirculates through the load and diode and decays harmlessly.

Then I size things. The FET's voltage rating has to be well above the supply, and its current rating has to cover stall or inrush, not just the running current, with a low enough Rds(on) that I2R heating is small. The diode needs to carry the full load current and be fast, and a Schottky is the common choice for low voltage.

For control I'd use PWM at 20 kHz or so, which is above hearing for a motor, so the average voltage sets speed, or for a solenoid I pull it in at full voltage and then PWM down to a hold current. If I need both directions, I'd go to an H-bridge, and if the supply is high or I want protection and diagnostics built in, a driver IC."
:::

::: technical
**Topology.** Load (motor or coil) between $V_S$ and the drain of an N-MOSFET $Q$; source to ground; freewheel diode $D$ with cathode at $V_S$ and anode at the drain node (i.e. across the load). A gate resistor $R_g$ (about 10 to 100 Ω for a GPIO drive) limits gate current and damps ringing; a pull-down $R_{pd}$ (10 to 100 kΩ) holds the gate low while the pin is high-Z.

**Why the diode.** At turn-off the load current $I_0$ continues. Without $D$ the drain voltage rises until the FET avalanches or the parasitic capacitance charges: with $C_{oss} + C_{wire}\approx 200$ pF and $I_0 = 1$ A the peak is huge, set by $\tfrac12 L I_0^2 = \tfrac12 C V^2$: for $L = 100$ mH, $I_0 = 1$ A, $E = 50$ mJ, which would need $V = \sqrt{2E/C} = 22$ kV if nothing broke down first. Real circuits clamp at the FET's breakdown voltage (avalanche), and the FET absorbs the energy: usually destroyed. With $D$, the drain is clamped at $V_S + V_f$.

**Current decay with a plain diode.** $i(t) = I_0\,e^{-t/\tau}$, $\tau = L/(R + R_D)$. For a 24 V, 24 Ω, 100 mH solenoid: $\tau = 4.17$ ms; the diode current falls to 10% in 9.6 ms. This is slow, which matters for release time ([[q0415]]).

**Sizing the switch.**
- $V_{DS(max)} \ge 1.5\text{ to }2\times V_S$ with margin for ringing and any clamp voltage.
- $I_D$ at the worst case: stall current $V_S/R$ (or the driver's current limit) and inrush. A 12 V, 1 Ω motor stalls at 12 A.
- Conduction loss $P = I_{rms}^2 R_{DS(on),hot}$; $R_{DS(on)}$ rises about 1.5 to 1.7x from 25 °C to 125 °C (vendor-dependent).
- Gate threshold below the logic high with margin: use a part whose $R_{DS(on)}$ is specified at $V_{GS} = 2.5$ V or 4.5 V for a 3.3 V GPIO.

**PWM.** Average voltage $= D V_S$. The load's $L/R$ smooths the current: ripple $\Delta I \approx V_S D(1-D)/(L f)$. Use $f$ above 20 kHz for audible silence, but low enough that switching loss and EMI are acceptable ([[q0418]]).

**Diode sizing.** Average diode current $= I_{load}(1 - D)$; peak equals the load current at turn-off. Reverse voltage rating $\ge V_S$. A Schottky has low $V_f$ (about 0.3 to 0.5 V) but higher leakage and a lower voltage rating; use an ultrafast silicon diode above about 60 to 100 V.
:::

::: long
"For a DC load like a motor, solenoid or relay coil, my default is a low-side N-channel MOSFET. The load goes between the supply and the drain, the source goes to ground, and the microcontroller drives the gate through a small resistor, with a pull-down to keep the FET off during reset. I'd choose a logic-level part so a 3.3 volt pin fully enhances it.

The thing that makes it work is the flyback diode across the load, cathode to the supply side. The inductor current can't stop instantly when the FET turns off, so without the diode the drain voltage shoots up until something avalanches, usually the FET. With the diode, the current recirculates through the load and decays, and the drain only goes a diode drop above the supply.

For sizing, I'd rate the FET at least twice the supply voltage and pick its current rating from the stall or inrush current, not the running current. A 12 volt motor with a 1 ohm winding can pull 12 amps at start. Then I'd check conduction loss, I squared times hot Rds(on), and the diode's current and voltage rating.

For control I'd use PWM, say 20 kHz so a motor is silent, and the inductance averages it so duty cycle sets speed. For a solenoid, I'd pull in at full voltage, then drop to a lower duty to hold, which saves heat. The cost of the diode alone is that it slows the current decay, so a solenoid releases slowly; if that matters I'd add a Zener or TVS in series.

If I need reversing, I'd use an H-bridge. If I need protection, current limit and fault flags, a driver IC. And if the load is mains or needs isolation, I'd look at a relay or SSR."
:::

::: terms
- **Low-side switch**: a switch between the load and ground; the load's other end is at the supply.
- **Flyback / freewheel diode**: diode across an inductive load that carries current after the switch opens ([[q0340]]).
- **Logic-level MOSFET**: a FET with $R_{DS(on)}$ specified at low gate voltage (2.5 to 4.5 V).
- **Gate resistor**: limits gate current and damps ringing ([[q0431]]).
- **Pull-down resistor**: holds the gate low when the driver is floating ([[q0430]]).
- **Duty cycle**: fraction of the period the switch is on.
- **Stall current**: $V/R$ with the shaft locked ([[q0343]]).
- **Hold current**: the lower current that keeps a solenoid closed.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "MOSFET and a diode". An exceptional answer:

- Explains **why** the diode is needed with the energy argument ($\tfrac12 L I^2$) and the consequence of omitting it.
- Sizes the FET from **stall/inrush current** and voltage margin, and checks $I^2R_{DS(on),hot}$.
- Mentions the **gate resistor, pull-down and logic-level threshold** by name with typical values.
- Notes the **side effect of a diode alone** (slow decay) and the Zener/TVS fix.
- Uses **PWM and hold current** for solenoids and knows when to move to an H-bridge or driver IC.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Leaving out the flyback diode** because "the MOSFET has a body diode". The body diode points the wrong way for a low-side switch: it conducts only if the drain goes *below* ground. It does not clamp a positive spike.
- **Wrong diode orientation.** Cathode to the positive supply side, so it is reverse-biased in normal operation. Backwards, it shorts the supply when the FET turns on.
- **Using a non-logic-level FET on a 3.3 V pin.** With a 4 V threshold the FET never turns fully on and overheats. Check $R_{DS(on)}$ at the actual gate voltage.
- **Sizing for running current.** The FET and the supply see stall and inrush; many drives die at start-up.
- **No gate pull-down.** During reset the GPIO floats, and the FET can turn on partially, powering the load or overheating.
- **Driving the FET from a GPIO that cannot supply the gate charge fast enough.** Slow edges increase switching loss. See [[q0431]].
:::

::: deep
## What makes an inductive load special

A resistor switched off just stops conducting. An inductor stores energy $\tfrac12 L I^2$ in its magnetic field and insists on keeping $I$ flowing. Switch off the path and the voltage across the coil rises, with whatever polarity keeps the current going, until some other path exists:

$$v_L = L\frac{\mathrm{d}i}{\mathrm{d}t}$$

If the interruption takes 10 ns for a 1 A, 100 mH load, that formula would give $0.1\times1/10^{-8} = 10$ MV, obviously impossible: something conducts first (a breakdown, an arc, the FET's avalanche). The energy is 50 mJ, which a small MOSFET's avalanche rating (single-pulse energy, often a few mJ to tens of mJ for small parts, vendor-dependent) may not survive.

## Turn-on and turn-off, step by step

**On.** $Q_1$ conducts; the load sees $V_S - I R_{DS(on)}$. Current rises as $i(t) = (V_S/R)(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$, with $\tau = L/R$. For a motor the rise is also shaped by back-EMF as the shaft accelerates.

**Off.** $Q_1$ opens. The drain voltage rises until $D_1$ conducts at $V_S + V_f$. The load current, now flowing through $D_1$, decays with $L/(R + R_D)$. Energy $\tfrac12 L I_0^2$ is dissipated mostly in the coil resistance and diode, not the FET.

## Worked example: 24 V solenoid, 1 A

Coil: $R = 24\ \Omega$ (so 1 A steady at 24 V), $L = 100$ mH (taking an average); the plunger pulls in once the current passes about 0.7 A and stays held down to about 0.3 A.

1. **Switch**: 60 V logic-level N-FET, $R_{DS(on)} \le 50$ mΩ at 4.5 V; hot value $\approx 80$ mΩ. Conduction loss at 1 A: $1^2\times0.08 = 0.08$ W: negligible, no heat-sink.
2. **Diode**: reverse rating $\ge 40$ V (Schottky ok), forward current rating $\ge$ the full coil current (1 A) as peak and $I(1-D)$ average. A 1 A to 3 A Schottky is fine.
3. **Gate network**: 47 Ω series (the peak gate current is a few tens of mA for a few tens of ns, limited by $R_g$ plus the pin's own output resistance) and a 100 kΩ pull-down. Gate charge of 10 nC at 20 kHz needs 0.2 mA average, well within a GPIO.
4. **PWM hold**: $D = 0.33$ gives an average of 8 V and a hold current of $8/24 = 0.33$ A; the coil dissipates $I^2R = 2.7$ W instead of 24 W, with 20 kHz ripple $\Delta I = 24\times0.33\times0.67/(0.1\times20000) = 2.7$ mA, negligible.
5. **Release**: with only the diode, the current falls from 1 A to 0.1 A in $\tau\ln10 = 9.6$ ms. If release time matters, add a Zener in series with the diode ([[q0415]]).

> [!TIP]
> If you only remember one rule: the flyback diode must carry the **full load current** at the moment of turn-off, and its voltage rating must exceed the supply.

## Motors: what changes

A brushed DC motor adds back-EMF and mechanical inertia. Starting from rest the winding is nearly a short, so inrush is $V/R$ (12 A for 12 V and 1 Ω) until back-EMF builds ([[q0343]]). So:

- Use **soft start** (ramp the PWM duty) or a **current limit** to avoid supply sag and thermal stress.
- Size the FET and diode for the limit, not the running current.
- Add a ceramic capacitor (say 100 nF) across the motor terminals and a bulk capacitor at the supply for brush noise and current spikes ([[q0355]]).

## PWM frequency and speed control

The load's inductance filters the PWM. For a motor with $L = 1$ mH and $R = 1\ \Omega$ at 12 V, 50% duty, 20 kHz: $\Delta I = V D(1-D)/(L f) = 12\times0.25/(0.001\times20000) = 0.15$ A peak-to-peak. At 1 kHz the same ripple would be 3 A, which makes noisy, inefficient operation. PWM above about 20 kHz is silent to most listeners; the trade-off against switching loss is in [[q0418]].

## When to leave the single FET

| Need | Move to |
|---|---|
| Both directions | H-bridge ([[q0411]]) |
| Braking | low-side pair or H-bridge ([[q0410]]) |
| Higher supply (above about 30 V) or high-side switching | gate-driver IC, bootstrap ([[q0426]], [[q0427]]) |
| Protection, current limit, diagnostics, small size | integrated driver IC ([[q0416]]) |
| Mains or isolation | relay or SSR ([[q0414]]) |
| Brushless motor | three half-bridges, ESC ([[q0369]]) |
:::

::: applications
- **Cooling fans and pumps**: low-side FET with PWM from an MCU, flyback diode across the motor.
- **Solenoid valves, door locks, vending machines**: pull-in/hold PWM with flyback diode or Zener clamp.
- **Relay coil drive**: transistor plus flyback diode (or an integrated driver array such as a Darlington sink array).
- **Vibration motors in phones and wearables**: tiny FET or driver IC with a diode.
- **Automotive actuators**: low-side drivers with integrated clamp and diagnostics for 12 V loads.
- **3D printer heaters (resistive) vs fans (inductive)**: the fan outputs need the diode; the heater does not.
:::

::: assumptions
- A **DC** inductive load (brushed motor, solenoid, relay coil) on a supply of up to a few tens of volts. AC loads and brushless motors have different driver topologies.
- **Unidirectional drive** with the load referenced to the supply (low-side switch). High-side, bidirectional and half-bridge drives follow in [[q0411]] and [[q0426]].
- MOSFET parameters ($R_{DS(on)}$ hot factor, avalanche energy, gate charge) are typical, vendor-dependent. Read the datasheet.
- The load inductance is treated as constant; a solenoid's inductance changes with plunger position, which changes the real waveforms ([[q0406]]).
- Layout (loop inductance, grounding) is a separate topic covered in the PCB section.
:::

::: followups
### Why isn't the MOSFET's body diode enough as a flyback diode?
For a low-side N-FET the body diode has its anode at the source (ground) and cathode at the drain. During turn-off the drain goes *above* the supply, which reverse-biases the body diode, so it does nothing. It only conducts if the drain goes below ground. (In an H-bridge or half-bridge, the opposite-side FET's body diode *is* the freewheel path, because it connects across the load to the supply.)

### The flyback diode makes my solenoid slow to release. What can I do?
The diode clamps the coil voltage to about $-0.7$ V, so the current decays slowly with $L/R$. Raise the clamp voltage: put a Zener or TVS in series with the diode, so the coil can see $-V_Z$ and the current falls linearly at $V_Z/L$. For $L = 100$ mH and $I_0 = 1$ A a 36 V Zener (plus the diode drop) takes the current from 1 A to 0.1 A in about $\tau\ln\frac{36.7+24}{36.7+2.4} = 1.8$ ms versus 9.6 ms for the plain diode. The FET then must withstand $V_S + V_Z$ ([[q0415]], [[q0413]]).

### How would you size the MOSFET for a 12 V, 1 Ω motor?
Rated voltage: at least 30 V (2x with margin; a 40 V part is common). Current: it must survive stall, 12 A, so pick a part with continuous rating at least 15 to 20 A and a low $R_{DS(on)}$ (say 10 mΩ at 25 °C, about 16 mΩ hot). Conduction loss at an RMS of 5 A is $25\times0.016 = 0.4$ W. Add a current limit in the drive so a long stall cannot overheat the motor either.

### What happens if the gate pull-down is missing?
While the MCU is in reset or booting, its pin is high-impedance; the gate floats and can drift or pick up noise, partially turning on the FET and energising the motor unexpectedly, or running it in the linear region where it overheats. A 10 to 100 kΩ pull-down guarantees off.

### Why PWM at 20 kHz and not 100 Hz?
At 100 Hz the current ripple is large (for $L = 1$ mH the ripple formula gives 30 A, more than the 12 A the motor could ever draw, which means the current swings between near zero and a large peak each cycle), the motor buzzes audibly and torque ripples. At 20 kHz the ripple is small (0.15 A) and above hearing. The cost is switching loss ∝ $f$ ([[q0418]]).
:::
