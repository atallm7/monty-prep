::: testing
Whether you can describe the switching-frequency trade-off **as a system, not a slogan** ("higher frequency means smaller parts but lower efficiency"). The interviewer is checking:

1. Which loss terms scale with frequency (overlap, $C_{oss}$, gate drive, dead time and recovery, core) and which do not (conduction, DCR, quiescent).
2. How ripple scales with frequency ($\Delta I_L\propto1/f$, capacitive ripple $\propto1/f^2$) and therefore how component size scales.
3. That the answer depends on what you hold constant: same inductor (efficiency has an optimum), or same ripple (efficiency falls monotonically as parts shrink).
4. Numbers, and awareness of the other forces that pick $f_{sw}$ (EMI bands, transient bandwidth, minimum on-time).
:::

::: why
Choosing $f_{sw}$ is the first and most consequential decision in a buck design: it sets the size and cost of the inductor and capacitors, the efficiency, the thermal budget, the EMI and the loop bandwidth. Questions 220 and 221 look at the extremes and the broader performance impact; this one is the core two-way trade for efficiency and ripple. Interviewers use it to see whether you can reason quantitatively about a trade rather than recite it.
:::

::: concepts
- **Frequency-proportional losses**: $\tfrac12V I(t_r+t_f)f$, $\tfrac12C_{oss}V^2f$, $Q_gVf$, $V_{SD}It_df$, $Q_{rr}Vf$ ([[q0213]]).
- **Conduction and DCR losses** depend on RMS current, not on $f$ directly ([[q0211]]).
- **Ripple scaling**: $\Delta I_L=V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf)$; $\Delta V\approx\Delta I/(8fC)+\Delta I\,ESR$ ([[q0212]]).
- **Core loss**: depends on flux swing and frequency (Steinmetz-type behavior).
- **Component size**: $L$ and $C$ for fixed ripple scale as about $1/f$ and $1/f$ (or $1/f^2$ for $C$ if $L$ is fixed).
:::

::: answer
"Frequency pulls efficiency and ripple in opposite directions. Higher frequency lowers ripple and lets you use smaller inductors and capacitors, because the inductor ripple current is $V_{out}(1-D)/(Lf)$ and the capacitor ripple goes as $1/f$ again on top of that. But every switching event costs energy, so overlap loss, $C_{oss}$ loss, gate-drive loss, dead-time and recovery loss all scale with frequency.

How that plays out depends on what I hold fixed. If I keep the same inductor, higher frequency reduces ripple current, which cuts RMS current and core loss, so efficiency actually improves a bit at first and then falls when switching loss takes over: in my 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A example it peaks near 300 kHz at about 94% and drops to 92% at 1 MHz and 80% at 5 MHz. If I instead shrink the inductor in proportion, to keep the same ripple current, then efficiency falls monotonically: 95.5% at 100 kHz, 93.5% at 500 kHz, 91% at 1 MHz, but the inductor goes from 23 µH to 2.4 µH.

So the choice is size and cost versus efficiency, and in practice it is bounded by EMI, minimum on-time and the control loop. Typical point-of-load converters sit at 300 kHz to 2 MHz."
:::

::: technical
**Frequency-proportional loss.** For the reference design (12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A, $D=0.275$): overlap $\tfrac12V_{in}I\,(t_r+t_f)f=1.2\times10^{-7}f$; $C_{oss}$: $1.44\times10^{-8}f$; gate drive $3\times10^{-8}f$; dead time $4.5\times10^{-8}f$; recovery $6\times10^{-8}f$. Sum: $2.69\times10^{-7}f$ W, i.e. 135 mW at 500 kHz, 27 W at 100 MHz (nonsense: edges would exceed the period).

**Ripple scaling.**
$$\Delta I_L=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{Lf},\quad I_{rms}^2=I_{out}^2+\frac{\Delta I_L^2}{12},\quad \Delta V\approx\frac{\Delta I_L}{8fC}+\Delta I_L\,ESR.$$

**Table** (model: $C=22$ µF, ESR 5 mΩ; fixed $L$ = 4.7 µH vs scaled $L=4.7\,\mu\text{H}\times500\text{ kHz}/f$):

| $f$ | Fixed $L$: $\eta$ | $\Delta I_L$ | $\Delta V$ | Scaled $L$: $\eta$ | $L$ | $\Delta V$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 kHz | 92.2% | 5.09 A | 315 mV | 95.5% | 23.5 µH | 63 mV |
| 200 kHz | 93.8% | 2.55 A | 85 mV | 95.0% | 11.8 µH | 34 mV |
| 300 kHz | 93.9% | 1.70 A | 41 mV | 94.5% | 7.8 µH | 24 mV |
| 500 kHz | 93.5% | 1.02 A | 16.7 mV | 93.5% | 4.7 µH | 16.7 mV |
| 1 MHz | 92.0% | 0.51 A | 5.4 mV | 90.9% | 2.35 µH | 10.9 mV |
| 2 MHz | 88.8% | 0.25 A | 2.0 mV | 85.7% | 1.18 µH | 8.0 mV |
| 5 MHz | 80.2% | 0.10 A | 0.6 mV | 71.5% | 0.47 µH | 6.2 mV |

**Required component values for a fixed spec** (30% ripple current at 2 A = 0.6 A; 20 mV with 3 mΩ ESR): $L=V_{out}(1-D)/(f\cdot0.6\,\text{A})$ and $C=0.6/[8f(0.02-0.6\times0.003)]$: 40 µH and 41 µF at 100 kHz; 8.0 µH and 8.2 µF at 500 kHz; 2.0 µH and 2.1 µF at 2 MHz. Both fall as $1/f$.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The efficiency numbers come from a simple loss model with assumed parameters; in the scaled-$L$ case the model keeps DCR and core loss scaling simple (optimistic for tiny inductors, which usually have higher DCR for the same current rating). Treat the shape (peak, slope) as the lesson, not the exact percentages.
:::

::: short
"Higher switching frequency lowers ripple and shrinks $L$ and $C$: inductor ripple current goes as $1/f$ and the capacitor ripple as $1/f^2$. But overlap, $C_{oss}$, gate-drive, dead-time and recovery losses all scale with $f$, so efficiency drops. With a fixed inductor, efficiency peaks at a moderate frequency (about 300 kHz in my example) because lower ripple current also cuts RMS and core loss. With the inductor scaled to hold ripple, efficiency falls steadily. So frequency trades size against efficiency, bounded by EMI and minimum on-time."
:::

::: long
"Switching frequency pulls efficiency and ripple in opposite directions, and the trade is mostly size against efficiency.

Take the ripple first. The inductor ripple current is $V_{out}(1-D)$ over $L\,f$, so it goes as $1/f$. The capacitor ripple is that current over $8\,f\,C$, so it goes as $1/f^2$ for a fixed inductor. Doubling frequency quarters the capacitive ripple. Alternatively, for a given ripple spec, both the inductor and capacitor you need shrink about in proportion to $1/f$. In my example, 30% ripple and 20 millivolts need a 40 microhenry inductor at 100 kilohertz and 2 microhenries at 2 megahertz.

The cost is switching loss. Every transition dissipates energy: overlap $\tfrac12VI\,t\,f$, the $C_{oss}$ energy, gate charge times drive voltage, dead-time diode conduction and reverse recovery. All of them scale linearly with $f$. For my 12 V to 3.3 V converter that is about 135 milliwatts at 500 kilohertz, 270 at 1 megahertz, 540 at 2 megahertz.

What happens to the efficiency depends on what you hold fixed. With a fixed 4.7 microhenry inductor, going down in frequency raises the ripple current, which raises the RMS current and the core loss. So efficiency has a broad peak, about 94% near 300 kilohertz, and falls to 92% at 1 megahertz and 80% at 5. If instead I scale the inductor to hold ripple constant, efficiency falls monotonically, 95.5% at 100 kilohertz to 91% at 1 megahertz, but the inductor shrinks from 23 to 2.4 microhenries.

So in practice I'd pick the lowest frequency that meets size, ripple and transient requirements, and avoid frequency bands that complicate EMI, such as the AM radio band in some products. Point-of-load converters sit at 300 kilohertz to 2 megahertz; GaN designs go higher."
:::

::: terms
- **Switching frequency ($f_{sw}$)**: repetition rate of the PWM cycle.
- **Frequency-proportional loss**: loss terms that scale linearly with $f_{sw}$.
- **Ripple current ratio**: $\Delta I_L/I_{out}$.
- **RMS current**: $\sqrt{I_{out}^2+\Delta I_L^2/12}$; sets conduction loss.
- **Core loss**: magnetic loss, depends on flux swing and frequency.
- **Constant-ripple scaling**: reducing $L$ in proportion to $f$ to keep $\Delta I_L$ fixed.
- **Power density**: output power per unit volume; rises with $f_{sw}$.
- **Minimum on-time**: shortest pulse the controller and FETs can produce.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: "Higher frequency means smaller components and lower ripple but more switching loss."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Lists *which* losses scale with $f$ and which do not, and quantifies at least one operating point.
- States the scalings: $\Delta I\propto1/(Lf)$, $\Delta V\propto1/(LCf^2)$, required $L,C\propto1/f$ for fixed ripple.
- Explains why efficiency can *improve* with $f$ at fixed $L$ (lower ripple current, RMS and core loss) and then fall, producing an optimum.
- Distinguishes fixed-$L$ from fixed-ripple (scaled-$L$) analyses, and notes real inductors don't scale perfectly.
- Names the other constraints: EMI bands, minimum on-time, loop bandwidth ($f_c<f_{sw}/5$), thermal limits, and relates to Q220/Q221.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Efficiency always drops with frequency."** At fixed $L$ it can rise at first, since ripple current and core loss fall.
- **"Ripple falls as 1/f."** The current ripple does; the capacitive voltage ripple falls as $1/f^2$ at fixed $L$ and $C$.
- **Forgetting that DCR and size scale with $L$.** In reality, smaller $L$ at the same current usually permits lower DCR, but going to tiny inductors saturates faster, so the model is only a guide.
- **Treating frequency as free.** Gate drive, $C_{oss}$ and dead-time losses are small each but add up and grow with $V_{in}$.
- **Ignoring non-efficiency limits** such as minimum on-time at high $V_{in}$ to low $V_{out}$, or EMI-band restrictions.
:::

::: deep
## Two kinds of terms

Total loss splits by how it scales:

$$P_{loss}=\underbrace{I_{rms}^2\,(DR_H+(1-D)R_L+DCR)}_{\text{conduction, depends on }I_{rms}}+\underbrace{k_{sw}\,f}_{\text{switching, gate, dead time, recovery}}+\underbrace{P_{core}(f,\Delta B)}_{\text{inductor}}+\underbrace{V_{in}I_q}_{\text{fixed}}$$

With $k_{sw}=2.69\times10^{-7}$ J for the reference design (the sum of the per-event energies). Conduction depends on frequency only through the ripple: $I_{rms}^2=I_{out}^2+\Delta I_L^2/12$.

## Case A: fixed inductor (4.7 µH)

As $f$ rises, $\Delta I_L$ falls as $1/f$, so the ripple's contribution to $I_{rms}^2$ ($\Delta I^2/12$: 2.2 A² at 100 kHz, 0.09 A² at 500 kHz, 0.02 at 1 MHz) disappears, and the core's flux swing falls. That is the left side of the efficiency curve. On the right side, $k_sw f$ grows linearly.

## Case B: ripple held constant

If the goal is the same ripple, then $L\propto1/f$, conduction loss is unchanged and the only change is the linear rise of switching terms. That means a clear price for each doubling of $f$: from 500 kHz to 1 MHz the loss rises by about 135 mW of extra switching plus a little extra core loss: about $-2.6$ points in the model. What you gain is a halving of $L$ (and of $C$ at fixed ripple, if ESR is not limiting).

## What you actually save: component size

For a fixed spec (30% ripple current, 20 mV, 3 mΩ ESR; 12 V to 3.3 V, 2 A):

| $f$ | $L$ required | $C$ required (effective) |
|---|---|---|
| 100 kHz | 39.9 µH | 41 µF |
| 200 kHz | 19.9 µH | 21 µF |
| 500 kHz | 8.0 µH | 8.2 µF |
| 1 MHz | 4.0 µH | 4.1 µF |
| 2 MHz | 2.0 µH | 2.1 µF |
| 5 MHz | 0.8 µH | 0.8 µF |

The inductor is usually the largest and most expensive passive, so frequency is the main lever for power density. But a 0.8 µH inductor rated for 2.3 A saturation current at 5 MHz has to have a low-loss core (ferrite, not powder iron) and its AC winding loss (skin and proximity effect) rises, so the DCR scaling assumed in the model is optimistic.

## Why efficiency drops: itemized

At 2 A in the reference design the frequency-proportional sum is 27 mW per 100 kHz. Going from 500 kHz to 2 MHz adds about 404 mW: overlap 180, recovery 90, dead time 68, gate drive 45 and $C_{oss}$ 22 mW. With a fixed inductor the lower ripple current gives back about 28 mW of conduction and core loss, so the total goes from 457 to 833 mW and efficiency from 93.5% to 88.8%. In the scaled-$L$ case the extra loss is larger (efficiency 85.7%) because the model also lets core loss grow as $f^{1.4}$ at constant flux swing, an assumption that is pessimistic if the smaller core is designed for lower flux.

## The other forces on $f_{sw}$ (brief; see [[q0220]])

- **Minimum on-time**: $D/f\ge t_{on,min}$ (about 30 to 100 ns). For 12 V to 1 V ($D=0.083$) with 50 ns: $f\le1.7$ MHz.
- **EMI**: avoid the AM broadcast band (about 0.53 to 1.7 MHz) in products that must meet radio-protection limits, and keep fundamental and harmonics out of sensitive bands.
- **Loop bandwidth**: crossover is typically below $f_{sw}/5$ to $f_{sw}/10$ ([[q0244]]); a higher $f_{sw}$ allows faster transient response.
- **Light-load efficiency**: fixed-frequency PWM at high $f$ has high fixed loss; PFM helps ([[q0214]]).

## Worked example: finding the efficiency optimum by hand

The curve with a fixed inductor has a maximum, and you can estimate where without a simulator. Group the loss into three pieces: a constant $a$, a ripple-dependent part that falls as $1/f^2$, and a switching part that rises as $f$:

$$P_{loss}(f)=a+\frac{b}{f^2}+k_{sw}f,\qquad \Delta I_L=\frac{509{,}043}{f}\ \text{A (for }L=4.7\ \mu\text{H, 12 V to 3.3 V)}.$$

The $1/f^2$ term comes from $\Delta I_L^2/12$ times the series resistance seen by the ripple current. Taking the FET and inductor resistance 65.5 mΩ from the model on this page and 5 mΩ of capacitor ESR, $b=\frac{509{,}043^2}{12}\times(0.0655+0.005)=1.52\times10^{9}\ \text{W}\cdot\text{Hz}^2$. Differentiating and setting to zero:

$$-\frac{2b}{f^3}+k_{sw}=0\;\Rightarrow\;f_{opt}=\left(\frac{2b}{k_{sw}}\right)^{1/3}=\left(\frac{3.04\times10^{9}}{2.69\times10^{-7}}\right)^{1/3}\approx224\ \text{kHz}.$$

At that frequency the ripple current is $509{,}043/224{,}000=2.27$ A peak-to-peak on a 2 A load, which is already a poor design: the valley current is 0.86 A, the peak 3.14 A, and the inductor must be rated for it. The full model, which also lets core loss fall as the flux swing shrinks, puts the optimum slightly higher, near 276 kHz. The useful number is how flat the top is: efficiency is 93.8% at 200 kHz, 93.9% at 276 kHz and 93.5% at 500 kHz, a spread of 0.4 points over a factor of 2.5 in frequency. That flatness is why $f_{sw}$ is chosen for size, EMI and bandwidth rather than to land on the efficiency peak.

## What the capacitor does to the frequency choice

The ripple figure on this page assumes the capacitor stays at its nominal value. A 22 µF multilayer ceramic rated near the output voltage can lose a large fraction of its capacitance under DC bias; the loss depends on the dielectric, case size and voltage rating, and tens of percent at 3.3 V is not unusual for small, high-capacitance parts. Suppose it is 12 µF effective. At the reference 500 kHz with the 1.02 A ripple current, the ripple becomes $\frac{1.018}{8\times500\ \text{kHz}\times12\ \mu\text{F}}+1.018\times0.005=21.2+5.1=26$ mV, against 16.7 mV for the nominal 22 µF. The 20 mV line is crossed. Raising $f_{sw}$ to 1 MHz with the same 4.7 µH inductor restores it: ripple current halves and the capacitive term falls by a factor of four, giving about $5.3+2.5=7.8$ mV even with the derated part. So the frequency lever is also a way to buy back capacitor derating and to keep MLCC capacitor counts down. Check the manufacturer's DC-bias curve for the part you actually use.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 12 µF effective capacitance is an assumed DC-bias derating for illustration. Real parts range from a few percent to well over half, so read the curve for the exact part, voltage rating and temperature.

## Frequency also changes how hot the parts get

Efficiency is not temperature. Every 100 kHz adds roughly 27 mW across the board in the reference design, but it lands mostly in the high-side FET and the driver, where the overlap and gate terms occur. If that FET is a small package with 80 °C/W of thermal resistance, 100 mW more switching loss is 8 °C more junction temperature, which you may need to budget at the highest ambient. By contrast, the extra loss in the inductor from lowering the frequency is spread across a larger body. When a thermal limit rather than an efficiency target drives the design, consider which part will run hottest at the chosen frequency.

## Verifying frequency effects on the bench

1. **Sweep $f_{sw}$ at fixed $L$.** Many controllers set frequency with a resistor or a SYNC pin. Step it through five or six values while holding $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$ and the load, and record input and output power at each. You should see a broad maximum rather than a monotonic trend if the ripple is large at the low end.
2. **Measure the ripple current and voltage.** Use a current probe on the inductor to confirm $\Delta I\propto1/f$, and the scope's tip-and-barrel technique across the output capacitor (no ground lead) to measure voltage ripple. Switching spikes are mostly probe artifacts if the ground loop is long.
3. **Thermal camera.** Scan the board at 300 kHz and at 1 MHz at full load. The high-side FET and controller should warm up with frequency, and the inductor should cool as ripple falls.
4. **Check the capacitor.** Sweep DC bias on the actual capacitor with an LCR meter that supports a bias source, or compare measured ripple with the nominal calculation. A mismatch like the 12 µF case above points to bias derating.

::: applications
- **Point-of-load bucks for FPGAs, MCUs, DDR**: 0.5 to 2 MHz to keep inductors small.
- **Battery-powered IoT devices**: 100 to 500 kHz or PFM to save switching loss.
- **Automotive**: about 400 kHz to 2.1 MHz with EMI-band (AM) avoidance and spread spectrum.
- **GaN-based converters and USB-PD chargers**: 1 to 5 MHz for power density.
- **High-power server/VRM stages**: 300 kHz to 1 MHz per phase balancing efficiency and transient response.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Reference converter** from [[q0213]], silicon FETs, CCM, constant frequency, forced PWM.
- **Loss parameters** (gate charge, $C_{oss}$, edges, dead time, $Q_{rr}$, core-loss scaling $f^{1.4}$, $\Delta B^2$) are representative assumptions.
- **DCR and capacitor ESR** held constant as $f$ changes; in reality they change with component choice and with AC losses at high $f$ (skin and proximity effects).
- **Capacitor values** nominal; derating makes the required $C$ larger.
- **Peak efficiency location** (about 275 kHz here) depends strongly on load current and $V_{in}$; at lower current the optimum moves down, at higher $V_{in}$ also lower.

::: followups
### Why can efficiency rise when I raise the frequency?
With a fixed inductor, ripple current $\propto1/f$ shrinks. $I_{rms}^2=I_{out}^2+\Delta I^2/12$ falls and flux swing (core loss) falls, so conduction and core loss drop faster than the switching loss rises at low frequency. In the example the optimum is near 275 kHz: 93.9% vs 92.2% at 100 kHz.

### Which losses scale with frequency?
Overlap switching loss, $C_{oss}$ loss, gate-drive loss, dead-time conduction, reverse recovery, and (for fixed flux swing) core loss. Not frequency-dependent: $I^2R$ in the FETs for a given RMS current, DCR, quiescent current. The sum of switching-related terms is about $2.7\times10^{-7}f$ W for the reference design.

### How does frequency affect the load transient?
A higher $f_{sw}$ allows a higher loop crossover (about $f_{sw}/10$ is typical), so faster response, and a smaller inductor slews its current faster. Both reduce the output capacitance needed for a given droop. At low $f_{sw}$ the loop bandwidth is limited and you need more capacitance ([[q0212]]).

### If I need to double the frequency but keep efficiency, what can I change?
Reduce the per-event energy: lower $Q_g$ and $C_{oss}$ devices (or GaN), shorter dead time, low-$Q_{rr}$ low side, faster and cleaner edges, lower $V_{in}$, and soft switching. And reduce the other losses with better magnetics so the total stays flat, which is why high-frequency designs lean on GaN and good layout.

### A 12 V to 1 V converter must run at 3 MHz. What would you check first?
Minimum on-time: $D=0.083$, so $t_{on}=28$ ns at 3 MHz. Most controllers can't produce that pulse (typical minimum 30 to 100 ns), so the controller would skip pulses or violate regulation. You would need a lower input voltage (two-stage conversion) or a lower frequency; also the switching loss at 12 V and 3 MHz is large.
:::

::: interpretations
"How does switching frequency impact efficiency/ripple?" asks for the two-way trade. The answer deliberately separates the fixed-$L$ and fixed-ripple views because the efficiency conclusion differs. Q220 asks about the upper and lower limits, and Q221 about performance more broadly (transient, EMI, size); this page keeps to efficiency and ripple.
