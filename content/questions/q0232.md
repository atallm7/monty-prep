::: basics
### In plain words
PWM and PFM are two ways to regulate. In PWM the switching frequency is fixed and the pulse width changes with load. In PFM every pulse is the same size and the controller changes how often it fires. PWM is quiet and fast, but wasteful at light load. PFM is efficient at light load, but noisier.

### Picture it
Think of two bus services. PWM runs buses on a fixed timetable, every few minutes, and resizes the bus to match the crowd. PFM uses one bus size and runs it more or less often depending on the crowd. A bus that starts its engine costs fuel each time. With a fixed timetable you pay the start-up cost constantly, even when almost nobody rides. The analogy hides the noise side: PFM's irregular timing makes the output noise harder to filter.

### Words you need
- **PWM**: a fixed frequency with a changing duty cycle ([[q0228]]).
- **PFM**: fixed-size pulses with a changing rate ([[q0231]]).
- **Light-load efficiency**: efficiency at small output currents, where fixed losses dominate.
- **EMI**: unwanted electrical noise; a spread-out spectrum is harder to filter.
- **Forced-PWM**: a mode pin that keeps the converter in PWM even at light load.

### The simplest example
Suppose each switching cycle costs 1 µJ of gate drive and switch losses. In PWM at 500 kHz that is $1\ \mu\text{J}\times500\text{k} = 0.5$ W, even if the load needs almost nothing. In PFM at a light load that needs only 2,000 pulses per second, it is $1\ \mu\text{J}\times2000 = 2$ mW. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers what you pay for that saving: bigger, lower-frequency ripple, a smeared spectrum and a slow first response to a load jump.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can contrast two **modulation strategies** on the dimensions that matter to a system designer, not just define them. The interviewer wants:

1. The one-line definitions: PWM varies pulse *width* at a fixed frequency; PFM varies the pulse *rate* with fixed-size pulses.
2. A balanced table: efficiency (light vs heavy load), ripple, noise/EMI spectrum, transient response, loop design, and audible noise.
3. A decision rule: which one for which application, and why most ICs automatically combine them.
:::

::: why
Choosing between PWM and PFM (or letting the IC decide) is a daily design decision. PWM gives a predictable, filterable spectrum and fast response; PFM gives dramatically better light-load efficiency at the price of ripple and noise. A phone camera supply, an RF PA rail and a coin-cell sensor all land on different sides. Interviewers use this question because it shows whether you think in terms of trade-offs and requirements rather than slogans.
:::

::: concepts
- **PWM**: fixed switching frequency; duty cycle $D$ set by the loop. See [[q0179]].
- **PFM**: fixed pulse size (peak current or on-time); pulse rate set by the load. See [[q0231]].
- **Conduction mode**: PWM can run in CCM or DCM; PFM is DCM by construction. See [[q0229]].
- **Switching losses**: energy per cycle times frequency. See [[q0217]].
- **Spectrum / EMI**: PWM energy sits at $f_{sw}$ and harmonics; PFM energy is spread at a varying, lower rate. See [[q0144]].
- **Light-load efficiency**: dominated by fixed per-cycle losses. See [[q0230]].
:::

::: answer
"The difference is what the controller changes to regulate. In PWM the switching frequency is fixed and the controller changes the duty cycle, so the pulse width tracks the load. In PFM the pulses are all the same size, a fixed peak current or a fixed on-time, and the controller regulates by changing how often it fires. At light load it fires rarely and sleeps in between.

That leads to the trade-offs. PWM has a clean, predictable spectrum at one frequency and its harmonics, so filtering and EMI are easy, the ripple is small and the transient response is fast because the loop is always running. But it burns gate drive and switch-node charging loss every cycle, so light-load efficiency is poor: tens of percent at milliamps.

PFM reverses that. Switching losses scale with the pulse rate, which scales with the load, so efficiency stays around 90 percent from a few hundred microamps up. But the ripple is larger and low frequency, it varies with load and can land in the audio band, the spectrum is smeared so EMI filtering is harder, and the converter needs time to wake up when the load jumps.

So I use PFM when battery life at idle dominates, and PWM when noise or transient response does, and most ICs switch between them by load, with a forced-PWM pin for noise-sensitive rails."
:::

::: technical
**Definitions.** PWM: $f_{sw}$ constant, $D=V_c/V_{ramp}$ (voltage mode) or set by the peak-current comparator. PFM: pulse of fixed $I_{pk}$ (or $t_{on}$), pulse rate $f_p=I_{out}/Q$, $Q=\frac{LI_{pk}^2}{2}\frac{V_{in}}{(V_{in}-V_{out})V_{out}}$.

**Numbers (12 V to 3.3 V, 4.7 µH, 22 µF, 500 kHz PWM vs $I_{pk}=0.6$ A PFM; $Q=0.354$ µC).**

| Quantity at 10 mA | PWM (fixed 500 kHz) | PFM |
|---|---|---|
| Switching rate | 500 kHz | 28 kHz |
| Efficiency (model) | 41% (forced CCM) | 91% |
| Charge per pulse | $I_{out}T=0.02$ µC (DCM) | 0.354 µC |
| Ripple from charge ($Q/C$) | 0.9 mV | 16 mV |
| Dominant spectrum | 500 kHz and harmonics | around 28 kHz, wandering |

**Heavy load.** PWM in CCM: lower RMS current, ripple from $\Delta I_L/(8Cf)$, efficiency about 97% in this (optimistic) model, typically around 90% in a real part of this rating. PFM can only supply up to about $I_{pk}/2$ (0.3 A here) and above that hands over to PWM.

**Dynamics.** PWM has a bandwidth of $\sim f_{sw}/10$ and an always-active loop; PFM has a wake-up delay (tens of µs) and a first pulse that can only supply about $Q$.

**Spectrum.** PWM puts its energy at $f_{sw}$ and harmonics. PFM's fundamental is the pulse rate $I_{out}/Q$ (28 kHz at 10 mA) and it wanders with the load, so a fixed notch filter works for one and not the other. The mode hand-over point is set by load current and has hysteresis.
:::

::: short
"PWM keeps the frequency fixed and varies the pulse width; PFM keeps the pulse size fixed and varies how often it fires. PWM gives a clean fixed-frequency spectrum, low ripple and fast response, but poor light-load efficiency because it switches constantly. PFM scales switching losses with load so it's efficient at light load, but has larger low-frequency ripple, smeared EMI and a slower response to load steps. ICs combine both automatically."
:::

::: long
"The core difference is what the controller varies. In PWM, the switching period is constant and the controller adjusts the on-time, so the duty cycle follows $V_{out}/V_{in}$ and the load. In PFM, each pulse is the same size, a fixed peak current or on-time, and the controller varies the gap between pulses.

At heavy load, PWM is better in almost every respect. The inductor current is continuous, so RMS current is low, ripple is small and at a known frequency, the spectrum is a line at $f_{sw}$ and its harmonics, which is easy to filter and to keep away from sensitive bands, and the loop is always active so transient response is fast. PFM can't even supply heavy loads, because the pulse rate saturates at about half the peak current.

At light load it flips. A PWM converter keeps switching at 500 kHz, paying gate charge and switch-node capacitance every cycle. In my 12 V to 3.3 V example that's about 41 mW of loss, so at 10 mA out, 33 mW, you get 41 percent efficiency. A PFM converter delivers 0.35 microcoulomb pulses, about 28 thousand a second for 10 mA, so the same losses fall by a factor of 17 and efficiency is around 91 percent.

The costs of PFM: each pulse raises the output by $Q/C$, about 16 mV with 22 µF, whereas the fixed-frequency converter's charge per cycle is only about 1 mV at 10 mA, so the ripple is bigger. It's at a lower and variable frequency, maybe in the audio band, and it's harder to filter. The output sits a bit above nominal, and a sudden load step has to wait for the converter to wake up.

So: PFM where idle efficiency matters, PWM where noise and transient response matter, and typically an IC does auto PFM/PWM with a forced-PWM option."
:::

::: terms
- **PWM**: pulse-width modulation, constant frequency and variable duty.
- **PFM**: pulse-frequency modulation, constant pulse size and variable rate.
- **Forced PWM (FCCM)**: constant-frequency operation at every load, including light load.
- **Auto PFM/PWM**: the IC picks the mode by load current (with hysteresis).
- **Spread spectrum**: deliberately varying $f_{sw}$ to lower EMI peaks. PFM spreads naturally.
- **Audible noise**: ripple or inductor/capacitor mechanical vibration at frequencies in the 20 Hz to 20 kHz band.
- **Load-step response**: how much the output deviates and how fast it recovers.
- **Mode transition**: the load at which the converter changes between PFM and PWM.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "PWM is fixed frequency and PFM is variable; PFM is more efficient at light load."

**An exceptional answer**:
- Frames them as different **control variables** (width vs rate) and ties both to the inductor-current waveform.
- Quantifies the efficiency gap at a specific load (41% vs 91% at 10 mA) and explains the loss mechanism.
- Compares **ripple** quantitatively (PFM ripple $\approx Q/C$ fixed per pulse vs PWM ripple shrinking with load).
- Covers **spectrum/EMI and audio**, **transient response** and **loop design**, not only efficiency.
- Gives a decision rule and mentions auto-mode, forced PWM and ultrasonic PFM.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"PFM is always better because it is more efficient."** Only at light load; at heavy load PWM has lower RMS current, lower ripple and better transient response.
- **"PWM can't be efficient at light load."** PWM with diode emulation and a low frequency can be decent; the fixed per-cycle loss just makes it worse than PFM. See [[q0230]].
- **Confusing PFM with spread-spectrum.** Spread spectrum dithers frequency around the nominal at fixed duty to cut EMI peaks; PFM changes the pulse rate by a large factor to match the load.
- **Assuming PFM ripple is "just a bit more".** It can be 5 to 20× the PWM-DCM ripple at the same load and capacitance, and low-frequency.
- **Ignoring the wake-up latency.** The first response to a load step is slower in PFM.
- **Forgetting the DC offset.** In PFM the average output is a little above nominal.
:::

::: deep
## What the controller changes

Both modes regulate the same quantity: the average energy delivered per second must equal the load power. PWM does it by adjusting the *size of a pulse* that repeats on a fixed clock. PFM does it by adjusting *how many pulses per second* of a fixed size.

PFM (green) keeps each pulse the same width and stretches the gaps as the load falls. (Schematic only; real timing has different scales.)

The same energy accounting applies. Per pulse the inductor delivers $E=\tfrac12LI_{pk}^2$ of stored energy plus what flows from the source during the on-time; for the standard example $Q=0.354$ µC per pulse at $I_{pk}=0.6$ A.

- PWM-DCM at 10 mA: $D=0.039$, $t_{on}=77$ ns, $I_{pk}=0.14$ A, charge per cycle $=I_{out}T=0.02$ µC, 500 thousand times a second.
- PFM at 10 mA: $I_{pk}=0.6$ A, $t_{on}=324$ ns, $Q=0.354$ µC, 28 thousand times a second.

The average current is the same (10 mA). PFM uses fewer, bigger pulses.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Property | PWM | PFM |
|---|---|---|
| Control variable | Pulse width (duty) | Pulse rate |
| Switching frequency | Constant | Proportional to load, from Hz-kHz to hundreds of kHz |
| Inductor mode | CCM or DCM | DCM (each pulse starts and ends at zero) |
| Light-load efficiency | Poor (fixed per-cycle losses) | Good (losses scale with load) |
| Heavy-load efficiency | Best | Not applicable (hands off to PWM) |
| Output ripple | Small, at $f_{sw}$ | Larger, low and variable frequency |
| Spectrum / EMI | Lines at $f_{sw}$ and harmonics, predictable | Smeared, lower, may hit audio band |
| Transient response | Fast, loop always active | Delayed at first (wake-up) |
| Loop compensation | Standard (CCM/DCM models) | Hysteretic comparator: no classic loop |
| Output DC accuracy | Tight | Offset above nominal by about half the ripple |
| Synchronisation to a clock | Possible | Not possible while in PFM |
| Typical $I_q$ | Hundreds of µA to mA | A few µA down to tens of nA |

## Ripple, EMI and audible noise

**Ripple.** In DCM-PWM at 10 mA each 2 µs cycle moves $I_{out}T=0.02$ µC, so the output wobbles by only $0.02\text{ µC}/22\text{ µF}\approx0.9$ mV at 500 kHz. In PFM every pulse is a full 0.354 µC, so the output steps 16 mV and droops between pulses: roughly 18× the PWM ripple at this load, and at a frequency of 28 kHz rather than 500 kHz. Full treatment in [[q0233]].

**EMI.** A PWM converter's conducted and radiated emissions are lines at $f_{sw}$ and harmonics, so a notch or ferrite filter is tuned to them, and the frequency can be chosen to miss sensitive bands (AM radio, an IF). PFM's frequency changes with load, so the noise moves; the energy per pulse is also higher (peak current 0.6 A vs 0.14 A), so each burst has stronger edges but a lower repetition rate. It is common for a product to pass EMI in PWM mode and fail in PFM at a specific load.

**Audible noise.** When the PFM rate falls into 100 Hz to 20 kHz, ceramic capacitors (piezoelectric effect) and inductors can sing. Ultrasonic modes enforce a minimum rate of about 20 to 25 kHz by adding extra pulses, trading some efficiency. For microphone, audio and RF paths, designers force PWM.

## Transient response and loop design

PWM keeps the loop running continuously: a load step is seen within a switching cycle or two, and the loop bandwidth is a sizeable fraction of $f_{sw}$. In PFM there's no continuous control signal. The comparator waits for the output to droop, and from deep sleep (a few µA bias) the controller needs tens of µs to wake. The first pulse supplies only 0.354 µC, equivalent to about 0.3 A for the time it lasts. For a 1 mA to 300 mA step with 22 µF the undershoot can be several tens of millivolts, more than PWM. The usual remedies: a larger $C_{out}$, a mode that switches to PWM on a large droop, or a faster wake-up comparator.

PFM also changes the loop design: it is essentially a hysteretic loop with a comparator, so no Type II/III compensation is needed for stability. (Stability is automatic, though ripple and accuracy are poorer.)

## Which to use

- **Battery-powered, long idle periods**: auto PFM/PWM, with the lowest $I_q$ possible.
- **RF, audio, precision ADC/PLL rails**: forced PWM (or a PWM buck followed by an LDO).
- **Fast-load processors**: PWM or COT; PFM only in idle states, with a wake-up signal from the processor.
- **EMI-critical products**: PWM at a chosen frequency, optionally with spread spectrum.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Thresholds for the PFM-to-PWM hand-over (as a fraction of full load) and quiescent currents are vendor and part dependent. The numbers above come from a simple loss model of one hypothetical converter, not from a datasheet, and its absolute efficiencies are optimistic (real parts usually land several points lower). The gap between the modes at light load is the robust conclusion.

::: applications
- **Smartphone/tablet rails (auto PFM/PWM)**: PFM during standby, PWM during active use.
- **Camera, RF PA and audio codec supplies**: forced PWM so switching noise stays at a known, filterable frequency.
- **Battery-powered medical and industrial sensors**: PFM-dominated converters targeting nA to µA standby current.
- **Wireless earbuds / hearing aids**: PFM with a minimum frequency above the audio band.
- **Server and PC VRMs**: PWM with phase shedding and diode emulation, so the efficiency curve stays smooth without a bursty mode on critical rails.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Peak-current PFM** with a fixed peak of 0.6 A and a PWM converter at 500 kHz; other PFM flavours (constant on-time, pulse skipping) have different numbers but the same trade-offs.
- **Single-phase buck**, 12 V to 3.3 V, with the loss model used elsewhere in this section (illustrative).
- **Hand-over thresholds** and sleep currents are device-specific.
- **"PFM" terminology** varies; some datasheets call fixed-frequency pulse skipping "PFM". The comparison here uses the variable-rate definition.
- **Ripple numbers** ignore ESR; including ESR increases both modes' ripple (by $I_{pk}\times ESR$ for PFM).

::: followups
### Why is PFM ripple larger than PWM ripple at the same load?
In PFM every pulse delivers a fixed, relatively large charge ($Q=0.354$ µC) and the output rises by $Q/C$ all at once, then droops until the next pulse. In PWM-DCM the charge per cycle is just $I_{out}T$, which is small at light load and shrinks as load falls. At 10 mA that is 0.02 µC vs 0.354 µC, about 18× less ripple.

### When would you force PWM even though it costs efficiency?
For rails that feed noise-sensitive circuits (RF, audio, high-resolution ADC/PLL reference), where a fixed, filterable switching frequency is more important than idle efficiency, or in systems where the load can step suddenly and wake-up latency would cause an unacceptable droop. Also when EMI compliance was only characterised in PWM.

### Does PFM mean the converter is unregulated between pulses?
No. The output capacitor feeds the load and the comparator watches the output continuously; the converter regulates by keeping the output between two thresholds. It is a hysteretic (bang-bang) regulator: the regulation band is the PFM ripple.

### Can a converter run PWM in DCM at light load and still be efficient?
Better than forced CCM, but not as good as PFM. In the model above, 500 kHz DCM still spends about 41 mW on gate and $C_{oss}$ losses. To fix this, lower the frequency (frequency foldback) or go to PFM. DCM removes the circulating-current term only.

### What is "ultrasonic PFM"?
PFM with a minimum pulse rate around 20 to 25 kHz. If the load is so light that the natural rate would be, say, 2 kHz, the controller inserts extra pulses to keep the rate above the audible range. The extra pulses cost efficiency but remove ceramic-capacitor and inductor audible noise.
