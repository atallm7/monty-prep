::: testing
Whether you can see a PCB as a circuit element and not as ideal wire. The interviewer wants the list (trace resistance, inductance, capacitance, mutual coupling, vias, pads, planes, dielectric loss, leakage) **with magnitudes**, and the understanding that **inductance is a loop property** that depends on the return path. The strongest answers connect each parasitic to a symptom: ringing, ground bounce, crosstalk, droop, slow edges, leakage errors, and say what layout practice fixes it.
:::

::: why
At low frequency a trace is a wire. At the edge rates in modern digital, switching power and RF designs it is a network of R, L and C, and that network decides whether the board works. A 20 nH power loop produces volts of spike when a switch turns off; a via adds an ohm of reactance at a few hundred MHz; a flux-contaminated pad can leak enough to ruin a pA-level measurement. Knowing which parasitic matters when is the difference between a layout that works first time and one that needs three spins ([[q0011]] introduced it for components; this is the board-level version).
:::

::: concepts
- **Resistance of a conductor**: $R=\rho l/(wt)$, plus skin effect at high frequency ([[q0056]]).
- **Inductance as a loop property**: $v=L\,di/dt$; it depends on loop area, so return path matters ([[q0039]], [[q0041]]).
- **Capacitance**: parallel-plate-like coupling between a trace and a plane, or between adjacent conductors ([[q0022]]).
- **Mutual coupling**: shared fields produce crosstalk (capacitive and inductive).
- **Dielectric loss and permittivity**: $\varepsilon_r$ and $\tan\delta$ of the laminate ([[q0058]]).
- **Parasitics of components**: ESR/ESL and package leads add to the board's parasitics ([[q0030]], [[q0016]]).
:::

::: answer
"A PCB isn't ideal wire, so every feature has parasitic R, L and C. The main ones are these.

Trace resistance: copper is a good conductor but not perfect; a 0.25 mm wide, 50 mm long trace in 1 oz copper is about 0.1 ohm, which matters for current sensing and power, and at high frequency skin effect raises it.

Trace inductance: roughly 0.3 nH per millimetre for a trace over a ground plane, and more if the return path is far away. It's really a loop property, so a big loop of current gives big inductance, and L di/dt gives voltage spikes and ground bounce.

Capacitance: to the plane below, about 0.1 pF per millimetre for a 50 ohm trace, and between neighbouring traces, which gives crosstalk. Planes also form a capacitor, which helps decoupling.

Vias add about a nanohenry and half a picofarad each. Pads, and component package parasitics like capacitor ESL, add more. There's also dielectric loss from the laminate, which attenuates high-frequency signals, and surface leakage resistance from flux or humidity, which matters on high-impedance nodes.

So practically: keep loops small, use a solid ground plane, keep traces short, and place decoupling close with short vias."
:::

::: technical
**Trace resistance.** $R=\rho l/(wt)$. For 1 oz copper (35 µm), 0.25 mm × 50 mm: $R=1.72\times10^{-8}\times0.05/(0.25\times10^{-3}\times35\times10^{-6})=0.098\ \Omega$. Skin depth $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu)}$ is 66 µm at 1 MHz, 6.6 µm at 100 MHz and 2.1 µm at 1 GHz, so AC resistance exceeds DC above roughly 10 MHz for 35 µm foil.

**Trace inductance and capacitance (microstrip, 50 Ω, $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3.0$).** $L'=Z_0\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/c=0.29$ nH/mm, $C'=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}/(Z_0c)=0.115$ pF/mm. For 50 mm: $L=14.4$ nH, $C=5.8$ pF, delay $\approx0.29$ ns. Over a poor return path $L'$ can be several times larger.

**Loop inductance.** A square 10 mm loop of 1 mm trace: $L\approx(2\mu_0a/\pi)[\ln(a/r)-0.774]\approx23$ nH. At $di/dt=1\ \text{A}/10\ \text{ns}$: $v=L\,di/dt=2.3$ V.

**Vias.** $L\approx5.08\,h\,[\ln(4h/d)+1]$ nH (inches), 1.6 mm board with 0.3 mm drill: about 1.3 nH ($X_L=8.2\ \Omega$ at 1 GHz). $C\approx1.41\,\varepsilon_r T D_1/(D_2-D_1)$ pF (inches): about 0.6 pF for 0.6 mm pad and 1.0 mm antipad.

**Planes.** $C/A=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r/h$: 0.2 mm FR-4 spacing gives about 19.5 pF/cm², so a 10×10 cm plane pair is about 1.9 nF.

**Dielectric loss.** $\tan\delta\approx0.02$ for FR-4 at 1 GHz; attenuation about 0.4 dB/inch at 5 GHz ([[q0469]]).
:::

::: long
"Every part of a PCB has parasitic resistance, inductance and capacitance. I'd group them like this.

Resistance first. A trace is $\rho l/(wt)$. A 0.25 millimetre wide, 50 millimetre long trace in 1 ounce copper is about 0.1 ohm. That's negligible for a signal but not for a milliohm shunt or a power rail carrying amps, and it increases at high frequency because of skin effect.

Inductance is usually the troublemaker. It's a loop property: it depends on the area enclosed by the signal and its return. Over a ground plane, a trace has about 0.3 nanohenries per millimetre, so 50 millimetres is about 14 nanohenries. A 10 millimetre square power loop is around 20 nanohenries, and a one amp, ten nanosecond edge across that is 2 volts. That's ground bounce and switch-node ringing.

Capacitance comes from the trace to the plane below, about 0.1 picofarad per millimetre for 50 ohm microstrip, and between adjacent traces, which gives crosstalk. Two planes spaced 0.2 millimetres apart give about 20 picofarads per square centimetre, which helps decoupling at high frequency.

Then vias, about 1 nanohenry and half a picofarad each, which is 8 ohms of reactance at a gigahertz; pads; component packages and ESL; dielectric loss, around 0.4 dB per inch at 5 gigahertz in FR-4; and surface leakage from flux and humidity that matters on megaohm nodes.

The practical fixes: keep loops small, use a solid reference plane, short traces, decoupling close to the pin, and spacing for crosstalk."
:::

::: terms
- **Parasitic**: an unintended R, L or C that comes from physical structure rather than a placed component.
- **Loop inductance**: inductance of the current path including its return; falls as the loop area shrinks.
- **Mutual inductance / capacitance**: coupling between adjacent conductors that causes crosstalk.
- **Skin effect**: current crowding to the surface at high frequency, raising AC resistance ([[q0056]]).
- **Via inductance**: series inductance of a plated barrel.
- **Ground bounce**: voltage disturbance on a ground connection caused by $L\,di/dt$.
- **Loss tangent ($\tan\delta$)**: dielectric loss factor of the laminate.
- **ESL**: equivalent series inductance of a component ([[q0030]]).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "resistance, inductance and capacitance of the traces".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Lists **trace R, L, C, mutual coupling, vias, pads, planes, dielectric loss and leakage** and gives **magnitudes** (0.1 Ω, 0.3 nH/mm, 0.1 pF/mm, 1 nH and 0.5 pF per via).
- Stresses that **inductance depends on the return path**, so layout, not just trace length, sets it.
- Connects each to a **symptom**: ground bounce, ringing, crosstalk, droop, loss, leakage error.
- Notes **which matters at which frequency**: R at DC and for sensing, L above about 1 MHz for 50 mm, C and loss at GHz.
- Names **mitigations**: planes, short loops, decoupling placement, spacing, guard traces and cleaning.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating a trace as inductance per length regardless of return path.** A trace far from its return can have 3 to 5 times the inductance of the same trace over a plane.
- **Ignoring vias.** At 1 GHz a 1.3 nH via is 8 Ω, which matters in a decoupling path and for series connections.
- **Forgetting that planes are capacitors.** A power/ground plane pair provides distributed capacitance, but it also supports resonances.
- **Assuming FR-4 permittivity is constant.** $\varepsilon_r$ varies with frequency, resin content and weave ([[q0469]]).
- **Neglecting surface leakage.** Flux residue and humidity can leak nanoamps into megaohm nodes, which dominates pA-level measurements.
- **Quoting trace inductance as 1 nH per mm for everything.** That rule suits free wires; microstrip over a plane is about 0.3 nH/mm.
:::

::: deep
## Why a trace is not a wire

A conductor carries current and is surrounded by electric and magnetic fields. The magnetic field stores energy proportional to $\tfrac12LI^2$, the electric field stores energy proportional to $\tfrac12CV^2$, and the conductor itself dissipates $I^2R$. These three are the parasitics. They are small, which is why a trace looks like a wire at low frequency, but the question is always: small compared with what? Compare each with the circuit's impedance at the frequency of interest.

## Resistance

$$R=\frac{\rho\,l}{w\,t}$$

For copper, $\rho\approx1.7\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\text{m}$. A 1 oz layer is 35 µm thick, so its sheet resistance is 0.49 mΩ/square. A 0.25 mm × 50 mm trace has 200 squares, so $R=0.098\ \Omega$. Two places it matters: current sensing and power (a 10 A path through 20 mΩ drops 0.2 V and dissipates 2 W) and the loop of a switching converter ([[q0467]] covers current capacity).

At high frequency, current crowds toward the surface to a depth $\delta=\sqrt{\rho/(\pi f\mu_0)}$:

| f | 100 kHz | 1 MHz | 14 MHz | 100 MHz | 1 GHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\delta$ (copper) | 206 µm | 65 µm | 17.5 µm | 6.6 µm | 2.1 µm |

Once $\delta$ is less than about half the foil thickness (above roughly 14 MHz for 35 µm), AC resistance rises as $\sqrt f$ ([[q0056]]). On microstrip the current also concentrates on the side facing the plane.

## Inductance: it is a loop property

A current needs a closed loop. The inductance is the flux linking the loop per amp, so it depends on loop area. A trace over a ground plane has a very small loop: the return current flows in the plane directly beneath the trace, close and mirrored. For a 50 Ω microstrip, the standard line relations give

$$L'=\frac{Z_0\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}}{c}\approx\frac{50\times1.73}{3\times10^{8}}=0.29\ \text{nH/mm},\qquad C'=\frac{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}}{Z_0\,c}\approx0.115\ \text{pF/mm}$$

so a 50 mm trace has about 14.4 nH, 5.8 pF and a delay of about 0.29 ns. Take the plane away (two traces out and back, 2 mm apart in free space) and the same length can have several times the inductance, because the loop is bigger.

For a rough free-loop estimate use the square-loop formula $L\approx(2\mu_0a/\pi)[\ln(a/r)-0.774]$: for a 10 mm side loop of 1 mm wide trace (effective radius about 0.25 mm), $L\approx23$ nH. If a switching converter forces 1 A to change in 10 ns through it, $v=L\,di/dt=2.3$ V appears across the loop. That is why power-stage layouts keep the input capacitor, high-side and low-side switches in the tightest possible loop ([[q0142]]).

## Capacitance and coupling

A trace over a plane has capacitance per length $C'$ (0.115 pF/mm for 50 Ω microstrip). It slows edges when driven from a high impedance, adds load to the driver and sets $Z_0$ with $L'$. Between adjacent traces there is a coupling capacitance $C_m$ and mutual inductance $M$: these cause **crosstalk** (capacitive coupling injects current proportional to $dV/dt$ into a victim; inductive coupling induces voltage proportional to $dI/dt$). Increasing spacing (3 times the trace width is a common rule of thumb for reducing crosstalk) and reducing the dielectric height to the plane both reduce coupling.

A power and ground plane pair is a parallel-plate capacitor, $C/A=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r/h$. For 0.2 mm FR-4 ($\varepsilon_r\approx4.4$) that is about 19.5 pF/cm², so a 10 × 10 cm pair has about 1.9 nF, which helps decoupling above about 100 MHz where discrete capacitors fail because of their inductance ([[q0031]], [[q0142]]). But it also forms resonant cavities.

## Vias, pads and connectors

A through-via is a short vertical wire with a distributed capacitor to the planes it passes. A common estimate (Howard Johnson's) is $L\approx5.08\,h\,[\ln(4h/d)+1]$ nH with $h$ and $d$ in inches: for a 1.6 mm board and 0.3 mm drill, $L\approx1.3$ nH, giving $X_L=2\pi f L=0.8\ \Omega$ at 100 MHz and 8.2 Ω at 1 GHz. Its capacitance, $C\approx1.41\,\varepsilon_r T\,D_1/(D_2-D_1)$ pF, is roughly 0.6 pF for a 0.6 mm pad in a 1.0 mm antipad. In a decoupling path, via inductance adds to the capacitor's own ESL ([[q0030]]), so use short, wide connections and two vias per pad for low impedance. A signal via can also leave an unused stub, which acts like a resonant open line at high speed.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The via L and C expressions are widely used approximations from signal-integrity literature; actual values depend on pad stack, antipad size, plane spacing and return vias. Treat them as estimates and use a field solver for critical designs.

Pads and package leads add typically a fraction of a pF to a few pF and a few nH, and connectors, cables and test points add more.

## Dielectric loss and surface effects

The laminate has a loss tangent $\tan\delta$ (about 0.02 for standard FR-4 at 1 GHz) which gives attenuation $\alpha_d\approx\pi f\sqrt{\varepsilon_{eff}}\tan\delta/c$; with $\varepsilon_{eff}\approx3.2$ that is about 0.08 dB/inch at 1 GHz and 0.4 dB/inch at 5 GHz ([[q0469]], [[q0058]]). Skin-effect loss in the copper adds to it.

Finally, the surface of the board is not a perfect insulator. Flux residue, moisture and contamination provide leakage between pads: 5 V across a 100 MΩ leakage path is 50 nA, which is a large error for a picoamp-level sensor input but harmless for logic. High-impedance nodes use guard rings, cleaning, conformal coating or slots.

## What to do about it

| Parasitic | Symptom | Layout practice |
|---|---|---|
| Resistance | Voltage drop, heat, sense error | Wider/thicker copper, shorter path, Kelvin sensing |
| Loop inductance | Ringing, ground bounce, EMI | Small loops, solid plane under signals, decoupling close |
| Capacitance | Slow edges, loading | Short traces, lower C drivers, careful plane cut-outs |
| Mutual coupling | Crosstalk | Spacing, ground guard, orthogonal routing on adjacent layers |
| Via | Impedance step, decoupling inductance | Fewer vias, via in pad, multiple vias |
| Dielectric loss | Attenuation at GHz | Low-loss laminate ([[q0469]]), shorter runs |
| Leakage | Errors at high impedance | Guard rings, cleaning, coating |
:::

::: applications
- **Switching converters**: minimizing the hot loop (input capacitor, high-side and low-side switches) to limit ringing and EMI ([[q0173]]).
- **High-speed digital buses (DDR, USB, PCIe)**: controlled-impedance traces, via transitions and length matching.
- **RF boards**: microstrip and via fences; parasitics are part of the circuit design.
- **Precision analogue front ends**: guard rings and leakage control around picoamp nodes.
- **Current-sense shunts**: Kelvin connections that avoid trace resistance ([[q0455]]).
- **Power distribution networks**: plane capacitance and decoupling placement ([[q0142]]).
:::

::: assumptions
I assumed a conventional FR-4 board with 1 oz copper and a 50 Ω microstrip over a continuous plane for the per-length numbers; stripline, wide traces, thicker dielectrics or no nearby plane change inductance and capacitance. The via formulas are approximations. The skin-effect transition frequency depends on foil thickness and roughness. For lower-frequency or DC circuits only the resistance and leakage matter; for RF and fast edges field solvers and measurement are used. A valid alternative answer organises parasitics by effect (delay, loss, coupling) rather than by element type.
:::

::: followups
### Why is inductance said to depend on the return path?
Because inductance is flux per unit current in the whole loop, not in the trace alone. A trace over a plane has its return current directly below, so the loop is only as big as the dielectric height (0.1 to 0.2 mm), and the fields cancel outside. If the plane has a slot the return current has to detour around it, the loop area grows and so does the inductance and EMI. That is why you should not route fast traces across gaps in a plane.

### How big is the parasitic of a decoupling capacitor's mounting?
The trace and via inductance from the capacitor to the chip's pins often exceeds the capacitor's own ESL (0.5 to 1 nH for 0402 or 0603): a 3 mm trace can add roughly 1 to 3 nH, and each via about 1 nH. At 100 MHz, 3 nH is 1.9 Ω, which can dominate a 0.1 Ω target impedance ([[q0142]]). Hence: capacitor on the same side as the chip, vias right at the pad, wide short traces.

### What if the trace is a high-impedance node (10 MΩ source)? Which parasitic dominates?
Capacitance to ground and leakage. 5 pF with 10 MΩ gives an RC bandwidth of $1/(2\pi\times10^{7}\times5\times10^{-12})=3.2$ kHz, and any surface leakage of 100 MΩ divides the signal by about 10%. Use shorter traces, guarding (driven shield at the same potential), cleaning and coating, and a buffer close to the source.

### How would you estimate when a trace stops behaving like a wire?
When the edge's electrical length is comparable to the trace length: if the one-way delay of the trace is more than about 1/6 to 1/10 of the signal rise time, treat it as a transmission line. With 5.8 ps/mm, a 50 mm trace is 0.29 ns; a 1 ns edge crosses the threshold (0.29 > 0.17), so termination is needed.

### Scenario: a buck converter's switch node rings at 200 MHz. Which parasitics do you look for?
The loop of the input capacitor, high-side FET and low-side FET: its inductance (tens of nH if poorly laid out) resonates with the switch's output capacitance $C_{oss}$ (hundreds of pF), $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$. For 20 nH and 30 pF that is about 205 MHz. Fix by tightening the loop, adding ceramic capacitors right at the FETs, and adding an RC snubber or slowing the gate drive.
:::

::: interpretations
"Parasitic elements present on a PCB" means unintended R, L and C of the board structure (traces, vias, planes, pads, laminate). I also included leakage and loss because they are board-level effects, and mentioned component parasitics only where they interact with the layout. Parasitics of the components themselves are in [[q0011]], [[q0030]] and [[q0016]].
:::
