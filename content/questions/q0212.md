::: testing
Whether you can run the **standard buck sizing procedure with real numbers** and know what each result is a lower bound for. The interviewer is checking:

1. The ripple-current equation $\Delta I_L=\dfrac{V_{out}(1-D)}{L f_{sw}}$ and the rule of thumb for picking $\Delta I_L$ (about 20 to 40% of $I_{out,max}$).
2. The output-ripple equation $\Delta V_{out}\approx\dfrac{\Delta I_L}{8f_{sw}C}+\Delta I_L\,ESR$, and which term dominates for ceramic vs electrolytic capacitors.
3. Practical corrections: DC-bias derating, inductor saturation current, current-limit margin.
4. That the ripple spec is often not the binding constraint: load-transient requirements can demand more capacitance.
:::

::: why
Sizing $L$ and $C$ is the first calculation in any buck design ([[q0263]]), and the one where candidates most often skip the "real part" corrections (a 22 µF ceramic at 3.3 V may give 10 µF; the inductor may saturate before the current limit). Ripple spec questions also come up from the load side: how much ripple can the FPGA, ADC or PLL tolerate ([[q0218]])? Doing the calculation in an interview, with the units right and the check at the end, is a strong signal.
:::

::: concepts
- **Inductor current ripple**: slope $(V_{in}-V_{out})/L$ for $DT$; $\Delta I_L=\frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})D}{Lf_{sw}}$ ([[q0195]]).
- **Capacitor voltage ripple**: the ripple current charges $C$; $\Delta V=\Delta Q/C$ with $\Delta Q=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\Delta I_L}{2}\cdot\tfrac{T}{2}$ ([[q0196]]).
- **ESR and ESL**: parasitics in series with $C$ that add ripple ([[q0194]]).
- **Saturation current**: the inductor's $L$ collapses above $I_{sat}$.
- **DC-bias derating**: class II ceramic capacitance falls with applied voltage.
- **Load transient**: the capacitor supplies/absorbs the step until the loop responds.
:::

::: answer
"I'd size the inductor first from the ripple current, then the capacitor from the voltage ripple, and then check the transient and the real-part corrections.

Say 12 V in, 3.3 V out, 3 A, 600 kHz, with a 20 mV peak-to-peak ripple spec. Duty is 0.275. I choose about 30% ripple current, 0.9 A, so $L=\dfrac{V_{out}(1-D)}{f\,\Delta I}=\dfrac{3.3\times0.725}{600\text{k}\times0.9}=4.4$ µH, and I'd take a standard 4.7 µH, which gives 0.85 A. Peak current is $3+0.42=3.42$ A, so I want a saturation rating comfortably above the converter's current limit, say 5 A.

For the capacitor, ripple is $\dfrac{\Delta I}{8fC}$ plus $\Delta I\cdot ESR$. With a 3 mΩ ceramic the ESR part is 2.5 mV, so I have 17.5 mV for the capacitive part, which needs $C\ge\dfrac{0.85}{8\times600\text{k}\times17.5\text{ mV}}=10$ µF effective. Ceramics lose maybe half their rated value at 3.3 V, so I'd put 22 µF nominal or more.

Last, I'd check the load transient: a 3 A step off with 100 mV overshoot needs about 60 µF by the energy method, so the transient can need more than the ripple does."
:::

::: technical
**Inductor.** $D=V_{out}/V_{in}=0.275$.
$$\Delta I_L=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{L\,f_{sw}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad L=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{f_{sw}\,\Delta I_L}.$$
Target $\Delta I_L=r\,I_{out}$ with $r\approx0.2$ to $0.4$ (a common starting point, e.g. in TI's SLVA477). For $r=0.3$: $L=3.3\times0.725/(6\times10^5\times0.9)=4.43$ µH. Use 4.7 µH: $\Delta I_L=0.848$ A (28%), $I_{pk}=3.42$ A, $I_{valley}=2.58$ A, $I_{rms}=3.01$ A. Specify $I_{sat}\ge I_{limit}$ plus margin and $I_{rms}$ rating above 3 A (heat rating).

**Output capacitor.** For a triangular capacitor current (CCM) with ripple $\Delta I_L$:
$$\Delta V_{out,pp}\approx\frac{\Delta I_L}{8\,f_{sw}\,C}+\Delta I_L\cdot ESR\ (+\ \text{ESL spike}).$$
Budget 20 mV, ESR 3 mΩ: $\Delta I\,ESR=2.5$ mV, leaving 17.5 mV: $C_{eff}\ge\dfrac{0.848}{8\times6\times10^5\times0.0175}=10.1$ µF. With 50% DC-bias derating the nominal value is about 20 to 22 µF; 22 µF nominal ($\approx11$ µF effective) gives $16.1+2.5\approx18.6$ mV.

**Why the formula (derivation).** Capacitor current $=i_L-I_{out}$, triangular, zero mean, peak $\Delta I/2$. The charge stored above zero is the triangle area $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{T}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\Delta I}{2}=\dfrac{\Delta I\,T}{8}$, so $\Delta V=\dfrac{\Delta I\,T}{8C}=\dfrac{\Delta I}{8fC}$.

**Transient check (load release).** Energy in $L$ is absorbed by $C$: $\tfrac12L\left(I_2^2-I_1^2\right)=\tfrac12C\left[(V_{out}+\Delta V)^2-V_{out}^2\right]$, so
$$C\ge\frac{L(I_2^2-I_1^2)}{(V_{out}+\Delta V)^2-V_{out}^2}.$$
For 3 A to 0, $\Delta V=0.1$ V: $C\ge4.7\mu\times9/0.67=63$ µF (effective). This ignores the control loop's response, so it is conservative; with a loop crossover $f_c$, $C\approx\Delta I_{step}/(2\pi f_c\Delta V)$ (for 1.5 A, 60 kHz, 50 mV: 80 µF).

**Input capacitor.** $C_{in}\ge\dfrac{I_{out}D(1-D)}{f_{sw}\,\Delta V_{in}}=\dfrac{3\times0.275\times0.725}{6\times10^5\times0.1}=10$ µF for 100 mV; RMS ripple current $I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}=1.34$ A.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 20 to 40% ripple-current ratio is a convention, not a law: lower ratios reduce core and capacitor stress, higher ratios give smaller inductors and faster response. Capacitor derating depends on the dielectric, package and voltage rating; use the manufacturer's DC-bias curve.
:::

::: long
"My procedure is: pick the ripple current, size the inductor, size the capacitor from the voltage ripple, then check saturation, derating and the transient.

Take 12 V in, 3.3 V out, 3 A, 600 kHz, and a 20 millivolt peak-to-peak ripple spec. Duty cycle is 3.3 over 12, 0.275.

For the inductor, the current ripple is $V_{out}(1-D)$ over $L\,f$. I usually target about 30% of full load, so 0.9 A. Solving for $L$: 3.3 times 0.725, over 600 k times 0.9, is 4.4 microhenries. I'd take the standard 4.7, which gives 0.85 amps of ripple, 28%. The peak is 3 plus half of that, 3.42 A, so I need a saturation current above the converter's current limit, say 5 amps, and an RMS rating above 3 amps.

For the capacitor, the ripple current is a triangle with zero mean, so the capacitive part of the voltage ripple is $\Delta I$ over $8\,f\,C$. The ESR part is $\Delta I$ times ESR: for a 3 milliohm ceramic that's 2.5 millivolts, so I have 17.5 millivolts left for the capacitive part. That gives $C$ of about 10 microfarads effective. Ceramics lose capacitance with DC bias, maybe half at 3.3 volts, so I'd use 22 microfarads nominal, and I'd check the DC-bias curve.

Then the transient. If the load drops 3 A to zero, the inductor energy has to go into the capacitor. Allowing 100 mV overshoot, the energy balance gives about 60 microfarads. That's more than the ripple needs, so the transient, not the ripple, may set the capacitor, unless the control loop reacts quickly.

Finally, trade-offs: a bigger inductor lowers ripple but is bigger, has higher DCR and slows the transient response; higher frequency lowers both $L$ and $C$ but increases switching loss. I'd show how doubling the frequency cuts the capacitor requirement by about four times for the same inductor."
:::

::: terms
- **Ripple current ratio ($r=\Delta I_L/I_{out}$)**: peak-to-peak inductor ripple divided by DC load current.
- **Peak-to-peak output ripple**: $\Delta V_{out,pp}$, from capacitor charging plus ESR.
- **ESR / ESL**: equivalent series resistance and inductance of a capacitor.
- **Saturation current ($I_{sat}$)**: current at which inductance drops (often 20 to 30%).
- **RMS (heating) current rating**: current that raises the part temperature by a set amount.
- **DC-bias derating**: loss of capacitance of class II ceramics with applied voltage.
- **Load release / step**: sudden drop in load current; inductor energy goes into $C_{out}$.
- **Crossover frequency ($f_c$)**: loop bandwidth, sets transient response.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer**: gives $\Delta I=(V_{in}-V_{out})D/(Lf)$ and $\Delta V=\Delta I/(8fC)$.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Runs the sequence with real numbers and units, and checks results (peak current, RMS, ESR vs capacitive share).
- Explains the 20 to 40% ripple ratio as a trade-off (inductor size, core/cap stress, transient) not a law.
- Shows the derivation of $1/(8fC)$ and the ESR crossover $f=1/(8\,ESR\,C)$ (4.2 MHz for 3 mΩ and 10 µF, so capacitive for ceramics; 12.5 kHz for 100 mΩ and 100 µF, so ESR-dominated for electrolytics).
- Applies derating, saturation margin and the transient energy check, and notes the transient may set $C$.
- Mentions scaling: $\Delta V\propto1/(L\,C\,f^2)$, input-cap RMS current and layout/ESL effects.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using nominal ceramic capacitance.** At 3.3 V a 22 µF, 6.3 V X5R can be 50 to 60% of nominal; use the DC-bias curve.
- **Choosing the inductor by $L$ alone.** $I_{sat}$ below the peak current means the inductor collapses at the worst moment ([[q0195]]).
- **Forgetting ESR.** For electrolytic or polymer output caps, ESR dominates; the $1/(8fC)$ term would give the wrong answer.
- **Using the full $\Delta I$ in $\Delta V=\Delta I/(8fC)$ for non-CCM.** In DCM the current shape differs.
- **Stopping at the ripple spec.** The load step may need 5 to 10 times more capacitance.
:::

::: deep
## Step 1: choose the ripple current and size $L$

Inductor current ripple, from $v_L=L\,di/dt$ during the off time (the simplest: $V_{out}$ across $L$ for $(1-D)T$):

$$\Delta I_L=\frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{L\,f_{sw}}.$$

Why a ratio $r=\Delta I_L/I_{out}$ of 0.2 to 0.4? Small ripple means larger $L$ (bigger, higher DCR, slower transient), large ripple means higher peak and RMS currents, higher core and capacitor losses and more ripple voltage. Around 0.3 is the knee.

| $L$ (µH) | $\Delta I_L$ (A) | Ratio | Peak (A) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2.2 | 1.81 | 60% | 3.91 |
| 3.3 | 1.21 | 40% | 3.60 |
| **4.7** | **0.85** | **28%** | **3.42** |
| 6.8 | 0.59 | 20% | 3.29 |
| 10 | 0.40 | 13% | 3.20 |

(12 V to 3.3 V, 3 A, 600 kHz.) Going from 4.7 to 10 µH only lowers the peak by 0.22 A but more than doubles the inductor volume; this is the diminishing-returns point.

Pick the inductor also on:
- **$I_{sat}$** at least the current limit plus margin (the current limit may be 4.5 to 5 A); saturation is a collapse of $L$, which makes ripple current explode.
- **$I_{rms}$ (heating)**: $\sqrt{I_{out}^2+\Delta I^2/12}=3.01$ A here.
- **DCR** for conduction loss ([[q0213]]) and **core loss**.

## Step 2: output ripple from the capacitor

Capacitor current is $i_C=i_L-I_{out}$: a triangle of peak-to-peak $\Delta I_L$, zero mean. Charge accumulates during the half period when $i_C>0$:

$$\Delta Q=\frac12\cdot\frac{T}{2}\cdot\frac{\Delta I_L}{2}=\frac{\Delta I_L\,T}{8}\ \Rightarrow\ \Delta V_C=\frac{\Delta I_L}{8\,f_{sw}\,C}.$$

The ESR drop is in phase with the current, so the peak-to-peak ESR ripple is $\Delta I_L\cdot ESR$. For ceramic capacitors the two components are 90° apart in time, so adding them linearly is conservative (the quadrature sum is smaller). The ESL contributes a spike at the edges of the capacitor current, but with a continuous inductor current the capacitor current has no sharp edges, so ESL matters little at the output (it matters at the input, and via the inductor's winding capacitance).

Solving for $C$:

$$C_{eff}\ge\frac{\Delta I_L}{8\,f_{sw}\left(\Delta V-\Delta I_L\,ESR\right)}=\frac{0.848}{8\times6\times10^5\times(0.02-0.00254)}=10.1\text{ µF}.$$

**Which term dominates?** The capacitive and ESR terms are equal when $\dfrac{1}{8fC}=ESR$, i.e. $f_x=\dfrac{1}{8\,ESR\,C}$:

| Capacitor | ESR | $C$ | $f_x$ | At 600 kHz |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ceramic | 3 mΩ | 10 µF | 4.2 MHz | capacitive dominates |
| Polymer | 20 mΩ | 100 µF | 62 kHz | ESR dominates |
| Electrolytic | 100 mΩ | 100 µF | 12.5 kHz | ESR dominates |

## Step 3: real-part corrections

- **DC-bias derating**: a class II ceramic (X5R/X7R) loses capacitance with DC voltage, strongly when the rated voltage is close to the bias. A 22 µF, 6.3 V part at 3.3 V might give 10 to 13 µF; larger case sizes and higher ratings derate less. Temperature and ageing add small further losses.
- **Parallel capacitors** reduce ESR and ESL in proportion; put a small (100 nF) ceramic next to the IC to handle the high-frequency edge noise.
- **Check the result**: 22 µF nominal $\to$ 11 µF effective: $\Delta V=16.1+2.5=18.6$ mV, meeting 20 mV with a small margin.

## Step 4: the transient often decides

Load release (3 A to 0): the inductor holds $\tfrac12L I^2=21$ µJ that the capacitor must absorb:

$$\tfrac12 L I^2=\tfrac12C\left[(V_{out}+\Delta V)^2-V_{out}^2\right]\ \Rightarrow\ C\ge\frac{4.7\,\mu\times9}{(3.4)^2-(3.3)^2}=\frac{42.3\,\mu}{0.67}=63\text{ µF}.$$

In reality the loop reduces the duty within a few switching periods, so the real requirement is lower. A loop-based estimate: $C\approx\dfrac{\Delta I_{step}}{2\pi f_c\,\Delta V}$. For a 1.5 A step, $f_c=60$ kHz, $\Delta V=50$ mV: 80 µF. So the transient spec can demand 5 to 8 times more capacitance than the ripple spec. A smaller $L$ also helps the transient (less stored energy and faster slew): $L$ trades off ripple and response.

## Step 5: input side

$\Delta V_{in}\approx\dfrac{I_{out}D(1-D)}{f\,C_{in}}$ (the pulsed input current drains the capacitor), and ripple current rating $I_{rms}=I_{out}\sqrt{D(1-D)}$: for 3 A, 10 µF for 100 mV and 1.34 A of RMS ripple ([[q0193]]).

| $D$ | 0.275 |
| $L$ | 4.7 µH, $I_{sat}\ge5$ A, $I_{rms}\ge3.1$ A |
| $\Delta I_L$ / $I_{pk}$ | 0.85 A / 3.42 A |
| $C_{out}$ | 22 µF nominal ceramic (about 11 µF effective), ESR about 3 mΩ |
| Predicted ripple | about 18.6 mV |
| Transient check | 63 µF (worst case, no loop) or 80 µF (loop-based) for the stated steps: may need more |

::: applications
- **Point-of-load converters for FPGAs and MCUs**: $L$ and $C$ sized from ripple and transient specs.
- **Ripple-sensitive rails (ADC, PLL references)**: smaller ripple ratios (10 to 20%) and extra filtering ([[q0215]]).
- **Battery-powered designs**: larger ripple ratios for small, light inductors at the expense of efficiency.
- **GaN and high-frequency converters**: megahertz operation lets $L$ and $C$ shrink dramatically.
- **Automotive**: capacitor DC-bias derating and temperature range (up to 125 to 150 °C) affect the margin.
:::

::: assumptions
- **CCM, constant frequency.** In DCM the ripple current and charge are different; the formulas change.
- **Ideal triangular current** with equal rise/fall slopes defined by $V_{in}$, $V_{out}$ and $L$.
- **ESR in phase with ripple current**; for ceramics the peak-to-peak addition is conservative.
- **Derating** of 50% for a small, low-voltage-rated X5R is typical but varies; use the actual curve.
- **Load-step formulas** are conservative simplifications (energy method) or approximations (loop-based), not a stability analysis ([[q0244]]).
- **Component tolerances** (inductance $\pm20\%$, capacitance $-20\%$ plus derating) mean you should design for the worst case.
:::

::: followups
### How does doubling the switching frequency change the required $C$ for the same ripple?
With the same $L$, $\Delta I_L$ halves and $1/(8fC)$ halves again, so the capacitive ripple falls by 4. For the same ripple you need one quarter of the capacitance (about 2.4 µF instead of 10 µF in the example), at the cost of higher switching loss.

### What if the output capacitor is an electrolytic with 100 mΩ ESR?
ESR dominates: $\Delta V\approx0.85\times0.1=85$ mV, far above 20 mV. Increasing $C$ does nothing. You would need to parallel many, use polymer or ceramic caps, add a small ceramic in parallel, or add a second LC stage ([[q0215]]).

### Your inductor is rated for 4.7 µH with 4 A saturation, but the converter's current limit is 5 A. Is this OK?
No: during a fault or load transient the current can reach the limit, saturating the inductor so $L$ drops, the ripple and peak current rise rapidly and the converter can fail or oscillate. Pick $I_{sat}$ above the current limit (plus tolerance) or lower the limit.

### Why is the ripple formula $1/(8fC)$ and not $1/(fC)$?
The capacitor current is a triangle with zero mean. Only half a period of it adds charge, and the triangle's area is half its base times its half-height: $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac T2\cdot\tfrac{\Delta I}{2}=\tfrac{\Delta I\,T}{8}$.

### How would you meet a 5 mV spec instead of 20 mV?
With only an output capacitor: about 4 times the capacitance, and lower ESR. More efficient: raise $f$ or $L$ (ripple scales as $1/(Lf^2C)$), use multiphase interleaving, or add an $LC$ or ferrite-bead stage or post-LDO ([[q0215]]).
:::
