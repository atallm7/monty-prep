::: testing
Whether you know the **full menu** of a multimeter and, more importantly, **how each function works and where it lies**. The easy answer is "voltage, current, resistance." A strong answer adds continuity, diode test, capacitance, frequency, temperature and duty cycle, then says for each what the meter actually does (forces a current, charges a capacitor, counts zero crossings) and the common trap (true-RMS vs average-responding AC, in-circuit resistance, burden voltage, bandwidth limits). The interviewer is checking measurement judgment, not a spec sheet.
:::

::: why
Day to day, a DMM is how you find the short, the open, the wrong resistor, the dead rail, the drifting battery. Each extra function (continuity, diode test, capacitance, frequency) saves a different instrument, but each also has a failure mode that gives a confident wrong number: an average-responding meter reads a square wave 11 % high; a diode-test voltage can forward-bias a junction you didn't expect; an in-circuit resistance reading includes every parallel path. Knowing what the meter does internally is what lets you trust the number or not. It follows from [[q0986]] (what a DMM is) and feeds into when to choose a scope instead ([[q0989]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Voltage, current, resistance**: the three core functions, each built on Ohm's law. See [[q0986]], [[q0001]].
- **True-RMS vs average-responding**: two ways to compute AC voltage that agree only for sine waves.
- **Crest factor**: peak ÷ RMS of a waveform; limits true-RMS accuracy.
- **Continuity and diode test**: low-power resistance and junction-drop checks.
- **Capacitance, frequency, temperature**: derived functions using charge time, zero-crossing counts and thermocouples.
- **AC bandwidth**: AC ranges are only specified up to a limited frequency.
:::

::: answer
"Beyond the basics, a typical DMM measures a surprising amount.

The core three: DC and AC voltage, DC and AC current, and resistance. For AC, I want a true-RMS meter, because an average-responding one is only right for sine waves. Then continuity, which is a low-resistance check with a beeper, and diode test, which pushes a small current through a junction and shows the forward drop, about 0.6 V for silicon. Many meters also do capacitance, by charging the capacitor with a known current and timing it, frequency and duty cycle by counting edges, and temperature with a thermocouple, with cold-junction compensation. Bench meters add four-wire resistance, dB, min/max and sometimes data logging.

Each has limits. Voltage loads a high-impedance node with its 10 megohms. Current ranges add burden voltage and have a fuse. Resistance should be measured out of circuit because parallel paths change the reading. AC specs only hold up to a limited frequency, often a few kilohertz to a hundred kilohertz, and for non-sine waveforms there's a crest-factor limit. And frequency or capacitance modes are convenient but not as accurate as a dedicated counter or LCR meter."
:::

::: technical
| Function | How it is measured | Typical limits and traps |
|---|---|---|
| DC voltage | Attenuator/gain → ADC | 10 MΩ input loads high-impedance sources; range 100 mV to 1000 V |
| AC voltage | RMS converter (true-RMS) or rectify-and-average scaled by 1.11 | Bandwidth ~ 1 kHz to 100s of kHz; crest-factor limit; DC offset handling (AC vs AC+DC) |
| DC / AC current | Shunt + voltage measurement | Burden voltage; fuse; mA to 10 A ranges; leads in the correct jacks |
| Resistance (2-wire) | Known current, measure voltage | Lead resistance; in-circuit parallel paths; test current heats small parts |
| Resistance (4-wire) | Force + sense leads | Needs 4 terminals; for low R |
| Continuity | Low-R threshold (tens of Ω) with beeper | Threshold varies; a beep doesn't mean low R (it could be 30 Ω) |
| Diode test | Constant current (about 1 mA), shows forward voltage | Open-circuit voltage 2–3 V or more; LED may light; value depends on test current |
| Capacitance | Charge with known current, measure time or voltage ramp | Short settle; ±1 to 5 % typical; discharge the capacitor first |
| Frequency / duty | Zero-crossing counter | Input sensitivity and bandwidth; slow on DMMs |
| Temperature | Thermocouple (usually type K) + cold-junction compensation | ±1 to 2 °C typical meter error plus probe error |

**True-RMS vs average-responding.** An average-responding meter rectifies, averages and multiplies by $\pi/(2\sqrt2)=1.1107$ so a sine reads correctly. For other shapes the factor is wrong: a ±1 V square wave has true RMS 1.000 V but reads 1.111 V (+11 %); a ±1 V triangle has true RMS 0.577 V but reads 0.555 V (−3.8 %). A true-RMS meter computes $\sqrt{\overline{v^2}}$ and is correct for any shape within its **crest-factor** and bandwidth limits.

**AC-coupled vs DC-coupled.** In AC mode most meters block DC, so a 0–5 V, 20 % duty-cycle PWM reads the *AC component only*: $\sqrt{V_{rms,total}^2-V_{dc}^2}=\sqrt{5-1}=2.00$ V. The DC mode reads the average, 1.00 V; AC+DC mode reads the total RMS, 2.24 V.

**Diode test.** About 1 mA flows through the device under test with an open-circuit voltage often 2–3 V (some meters up to 3.3 V or more), and the display shows the forward drop: ~0.5 to 0.7 V for silicon, 0.15 to 0.45 V for Schottky, 1.8 to 3.5 V for LEDs. A reversed or open diode reads "OL".
:::

::: long
"The basics are voltage, current and resistance. DC and AC voltage, DC and AC current, and resistance with either two wires or, on a better meter, four wires for low values.

Then the convenience functions. Continuity is a quick low-resistance check with a beeper. Diode test pushes about a milliamp through a junction and shows the forward drop, around 0.6 volts for silicon, so I can check a diode or the body diode of a MOSFET, or find a shorted junction. Many meters measure capacitance by charging the capacitor with a known current and timing the voltage ramp, frequency and duty cycle by counting edges, and temperature with a type K thermocouple with cold-junction compensation. Bench models add dB, min/max, relative mode, and logging.

For each one there's a thing to watch. Voltage loads the circuit with about 10 megohms. Current adds a burden voltage and has a fuse, and I have to put the leads in the right jacks. Resistance should be measured out of circuit with power off. For AC I want true-RMS: an average-responding meter reads a square wave about 11 percent high. And the AC bandwidth is limited, often to a few kilohertz, with a crest-factor limit for spiky signals. If I'm measuring a PWM, the meter in AC mode blocks the DC, so a 5 volt, 20 percent duty signal shows about 2 volts, in DC it shows 1 volt, and the total RMS is 2.24 volts. So I need to know which one I'm reading.

For capacitance, frequency and temperature, the DMM is convenient but less accurate than an LCR meter, a counter or a calibrated thermometer."
:::

::: terms
- **True-RMS**: computes the real root-mean-square of the input waveform.
- **Average-responding**: rectifies and averages, then scales assuming a sine wave.
- **Crest factor**: peak ÷ RMS; sine 1.41, triangle 1.73, narrow pulses much higher.
- **AC coupling**: blocking the DC component before measurement.
- **Continuity**: a low-resistance (beeper) test of a path.
- **Diode test**: measuring the forward voltage at a small constant current.
- **Cold-junction compensation**: correcting for the thermocouple reference junction's temperature.
- **Duty cycle**: percentage of a period a signal is high.
- **Relative (REL) mode**: subtracts a stored reading (e.g., lead resistance).
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists "voltage, current, resistance, continuity".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Goes beyond the list to **how each function works** (forced current, ramp time, zero-crossing count).
- Distinguishes **true-RMS from average-responding** and gives the numbers (square +11 %, triangle −3.8 %).
- Notes **AC bandwidth and crest-factor limits**, and AC vs DC vs AC+DC coupling with a PWM example.
- Mentions **loading and burden**, **in-circuit resistance**, and the **diode-test** forward drops by device type.
- Mentions **four-wire resistance** and where a dedicated instrument is better (LCR, counter, thermocouple meter).
- Gives a **debug example**: finding a short with continuity and diode test, checking a regulator's output, checking a battery's current.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Using an average-responding meter on non-sinusoidal signals** (inverter output, PWM, switching ripple) and trusting the number.
- **Measuring AC on a PWM or DC-offset signal** in AC mode and expecting the average. AC mode removes DC.
- **Measuring resistance in a powered circuit** (or in circuit at all) and misinterpreting the parallel paths or the charged capacitors.
- **Assuming a beeping continuity test means a good connection.** Many meters beep below 30 to 50 Ω; a poor joint with 20 Ω still beeps.
- **Using the 10 A current jack for small currents** (it has huge burden relative to its resolution) or measuring current across a voltage source.
- **Trusting the frequency range**: a DMM frequency counter is often limited to hundreds of kHz and needs a minimum amplitude.
:::

::: deep
## The functions, one by one

### DC voltage

The ADC measures a DC voltage after scaling. Typical handheld ranges run from a few hundred mV to 1000 V, and the input impedance is about 10 MΩ on most ranges. Check that your source resistance is far below 10 MΩ: error $=R_s/(R_s+10\ \text{M}\Omega)$. See [[q0986]] for the loading example and the DMM simulation.

### AC voltage: where the numbers go wrong

An AC reading is a mathematical choice, and DMMs differ in how they compute it:

- **Average-responding**: rectify, average, scale by 1.1107. Exactly correct only for a sine wave.
- **True-RMS**: compute the square root of the mean square, either with a thermal converter or a squaring circuit or by sampling and computing numerically. Correct for any waveshape inside its **crest-factor** and **bandwidth** specification.

Comparison for waveforms with ±1 V peak (symmetric, no DC):

| Waveform | True RMS | Average-responding reading | Error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sine | 0.707 V | 0.707 V | 0 % |
| Square | 1.000 V | 1.111 V | +11.1 % |
| Triangle | 0.577 V | 0.555 V | −3.8 % |

**Crest factor.** Crest factor is peak divided by RMS: 1.41 (sine), 1.73 (triangle), 1.0 (square), and for a 20 % duty 0–5 V pulse, $5/2.236=2.24$. True-RMS meters specify a maximum crest factor at full scale (commonly around 3 for handhelds, with more headroom at lower readings). Narrow current spikes, as in a rectifier input, can exceed it, so the meter under-reads.

**Coupling.** In AC mode, most meters block DC. A 0–5 V, 20 % duty PWM has $V_{dc}=1.00$ V and total RMS $=5\sqrt{0.2}=2.236$ V. AC mode shows the AC component only: $\sqrt{2.236^2-1.0^2}=2.00$ V. The DC mode shows 1.00 V. A meter with an "AC+DC" mode shows 2.24 V. Always know which you're reading.

**Bandwidth.** Handheld meters usually specify AC accuracy for sine waves up to about 1 kHz to a few kHz at full accuracy with extended ranges to 20 to 100 kHz at reduced accuracy. A 100 kHz switching ripple on a power rail is outside the specified range; use a scope.

### Current

Current ranges put a shunt in series. Two practical rules: put the leads in the current jack (and back in the voltage jack afterwards) and start on the highest range. The burden voltage means the circuit sees a slightly lower supply voltage than it would normally; see [[q0982]]. AC current has the same RMS and bandwidth issues as AC voltage.

### Resistance and continuity

The meter drives a known current or voltage through the unknown and measures the response. Open-circuit test voltages are a few volts, which can turn on semiconductor junctions in a circuit; some meters have a low-voltage ("low power ohms") range that doesn't. 2-wire resistance includes lead resistance (use REL mode to subtract it) and contact resistance. See [[q0985]] for 4-wire and for measuring L and C.

**Continuity** is a resistance check with a threshold (often tens of ohms) that triggers a beeper; some meters have a fast response so you can hear a short intermittent connection.

### Diode test

The meter supplies about 1 mA and shows the forward voltage. Typical readings:

| Device | Forward drop |
|---|---|
| Silicon junction / MOSFET body diode | 0.45–0.7 V (often 0.5–0.6 V in the meter) |
| Schottky diode | 0.15–0.45 V |
| Red LED | 1.6–2.0 V |
| White / blue LED | 2.7–3.5 V (may not light fully on 1 mA, some meters have 3 V+ open-circuit voltage) |

An open diode shows "OL", a shorted one near 0.000 V. Testing in both directions identifies orientation. In-circuit, parallel paths affect results.

### Capacitance

The meter charges the capacitor with a known current and measures the time or voltage change, or applies a small AC signal. Accuracy is typically a few percent, with ranges from nF to mF. Discharge the capacitor first (and never test charged capacitors). It's good for finding a dead or wildly off-value capacitor, not for characterizing ESR or tolerance. See [[q0985]] for the LCR approach.

### Frequency and duty cycle

DMMs count zero-crossings or edges over a gate time. Limits are the input sensitivity (a minimum voltage), bandwidth (often 100 kHz to a few MHz) and resolution. For precise timing use a frequency counter or scope.

### Temperature

A type K thermocouple connected to the meter produces a few tens of µV per °C. The meter measures the small voltage and adds the cold-junction temperature (from an internal sensor) to produce a reading. Total uncertainty includes the meter, the probe (roughly ±1 to 2.5 °C or 0.4 to 0.75 % of reading for type K, depending on its tolerance class) and the connection/thermal contact. Heat conduction along the probe and thermal mass of the junction also cause error.

## A short debugging recipe using only a DMM

1. **Power off, continuity** between supply and ground: a beep means a short; check which part is hot after applying current-limited power.
2. **Diode test** across the supply pins of a chip, red to ground: a normal chip shows a drop, a shorted one near 0.
3. **DC voltage** on each rail with power on, checking against expected values and sequence.
4. **Current** through the board's supply on a safe range: compare to the expected idle current.
5. **Resistance** (power off) of suspect parts, and capacitance of suspect capacitors.
6. For anything that changes faster than a few Hz, switch to a scope ([[q0989]]).

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Diode-test open-circuit voltage, continuity thresholds, AC bandwidth and crest-factor limits vary by model. The values given are typical; check the specification and the manual of the meter you use.
:::

::: applications
- **Finding shorts and opens** with continuity and diode test.
- **Verifying supply rails** and battery voltages on a board ([[q0294]]).
- **Checking components**: resistors, diodes, MOSFET body diodes, capacitors for gross faults.
- **Measuring average current** draw of a device or sleep current with a µA range.
- **Verifying a sensor** such as a thermocouple or thermistor with the temperature or resistance range.
- **Checking AC mains and appliances** with a properly rated true-RMS meter (appropriate CAT rating).
:::

::: assumptions
- Assumes a **mid-range handheld or bench DMM**. Entry-level meters may lack true-RMS, capacitance or temperature; high-end bench meters add 4-wire, higher bandwidth, logging and analysis.
- Numeric limits (bandwidth, crest factor, diode-test voltage, continuity threshold) are **typical** and vary by model.
- The AC examples assume a **symmetric** or specified waveform with no unknown DC; real signals need the coupling mode checked.
- Temperature accuracy depends on the **probe and thermal contact** as much as on the meter.
- For **power measurements** (VA, true power, power factor) a power meter or analyzer is more appropriate than a DMM.
:::

::: followups
### Why does my multimeter read 2 V for a 5 V PWM signal in AC mode?
In AC mode the meter blocks DC and shows the AC component's RMS. For 0–5 V with 20 % duty, the DC average is 1 V and the total RMS is 2.24 V, so the AC part is $\sqrt{2.24^2-1^2}=2.0$ V. In DC mode you'd see 1.0 V. Use AC+DC mode for total RMS, or a scope to see the waveform.

### How does a DMM measure capacitance?
It charges (or discharges) the capacitor with a known constant current and measures how long the voltage takes to change by a known amount, then uses $C=I\Delta t/\Delta V$. Others apply an AC test signal and measure the impedance. Accuracy is usually a few percent.

### What does a continuity beep tell you?
That the resistance is below the meter's threshold, often tens of ohms (for example 30 to 50 Ω), not that it is a good connection. A poor solder joint or a long thin trace can still beep. For an accurate resistance, use the ohms range, and for a milliohm path use four-wire measurement.

### Why shouldn't you measure resistance in a powered circuit?
The meter's test current adds to the circuit's own currents and the reading is meaningless; it can damage the meter and the circuit. Also, capacitors hold charge. Power off, discharge, and (if possible) measure one end lifted since parallel paths change the reading.

### Scenario: a square-wave generator output reads 5.55 V on one meter and 5.00 V on another. Which is right?
The square wave is ±5 V peak. A true-RMS meter reads 5.00 V (RMS of a symmetric square wave equals its peak). An average-responding meter calibrated for sine reads $1.1107\times5=5.55$ V (+11 %). So the true-RMS reading is right for RMS, and the second meter is the average-responding one.
:::

::: interpretations
"What can it measure?" asks for the menu, but the real evaluation is whether you know the caveats. Answer with the list in a sentence, then pick two or three functions to explain (AC true-RMS, current and burden, resistance in circuit). Follow-ups often move to "what *can't* it measure well?" which leads to the scope comparison ([[q0989]]) and the sample-rate question ([[q0988]]).
:::
