::: basics
### In plain words
The defining equation of a capacitor is $i = C\,dv/dt$. The current through it equals its capacitance times how fast the voltage across it is changing. If the voltage is steady, no current flows. If the voltage changes quickly, a lot of current flows. This one equation explains most of what capacitors do.

### Picture it
Think of pushing a heavy shopping cart. Your push is the current. The cart's speed is the voltage. A heavy cart, meaning a large capacitance, needs a bigger push to change its speed. Once it rolls steadily, you need no push at all. And you can never make it jump from 0 to 5 mph instantly, because that would take an infinite push. The analogy breaks down because a real cart has friction, and an ideal capacitor does not.

### Words you need
- **Rate of change ($dv/dt$)**: how many volts per second the voltage is rising or falling.
- **Capacitance (C)**: how much current you need per volt per second. See [[q0022]].
- **Time constant ($RC$)**: the natural time scale of a resistor charging a capacitor.
- **Open circuit**: no current flows. A charged capacitor at steady DC looks like this.
- **Continuity**: a capacitor's voltage cannot jump instantly.

### The simplest example
Push a constant 1 mA into a 1 µF capacitor. Then $dv/dt = i/C = 0.001/10^{-6} = 1000$ V/s, which is 1 V per millisecond. After 5 ms the voltage is 5 V. Stop the current and the voltage stays at 5 V, because no current means no change. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page gives the integral form and shows how adding a resistor produces the familiar exponential charging curve with time constant $RC$.
:::

::: testing
The interviewer wants $i = C\dfrac{dv}{dt}$, but they're really checking whether you **own** it:

1. You can derive it in one line from $Q = CV$ ([[q0022]]).
2. You know the equivalent **integral form**, $v(t) = v(t_0) + \frac1C\int_{t_0}^t i\,d\tau$, and what the initial condition means.
3. You can use it: constant current gives a ramp, a step in voltage needs an impulse of current, sinusoids give a 90° shift, and an RC circuit becomes a first-order ODE with an exponential solution.
4. You understand the consequences: capacitor voltage can't jump (continuity, [[q0037]]) and the capacitor is open at DC steady state ([[q0028]]).
:::

::: why
Every transient you'll analyse involving a capacitor comes from this equation: RC delays, power-up inrush, gate-drive current, slew-rate limits, ADC sample-and-hold settling, decoupling droop, op-amp integrators. When something on a board behaves "slowly" or draws a surprising current spike, $i = C\,dv/dt$ is usually the first equation you write. It's also the mirror image of the inductor's $v = L\,di/dt$ ([[q0041]]), and interviewers like to see that you recognise the duality.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitance definition** $Q = CV$: charge proportional to voltage. See [[q0022]].
- **Current as rate of charge flow**: $i = dq/dt$.
- **Passive sign convention**: current enters the terminal marked "+", so positive $i$ charges the capacitor.
- **Initial conditions**: the integral form needs $v(t_0)$; capacitors remember their past.
- **KCL/KVL** to combine the capacitor equation with the rest of the circuit. See [[q0004]].
- **First-order linear ODEs** and their exponential solutions.
:::

::: answer
"The defining equation for a capacitor is $i = C\,\dfrac{dv}{dt}$. The current through it is proportional to how fast the voltage across it is changing.

It falls right out of $Q = CV$: current is the rate of change of charge, so $i = dQ/dt$, and if $C$ is constant that's $C\,dv/dt$.

You can flip it into integral form too: $v(t) = v(0) + \frac{1}{C}\int_0^t i\,d\tau$. That makes it clear the capacitor integrates current, and it has memory through its initial voltage.

The equation tells you a lot of behaviour directly. If the voltage is constant, the current is zero, so at DC steady state a capacitor is an open circuit. If you push a constant current in, the voltage ramps linearly, like 1 mA into 1 µF giving 1 volt per millisecond. And because an instant jump in voltage would need infinite current, capacitor voltage is continuous. When you put it in a circuit with a resistor, KCL gives a first-order ODE, $RC\,dv/dt + v = V_s$, which is where the exponential charging curve with time constant $RC$ comes from."
:::

::: technical
**Derivation.** With passive sign convention and constant $C$:
$$q(t) = C\,v(t) \;\Rightarrow\; i(t) = \frac{dq}{dt} = C\frac{dv}{dt}$$
If $C$ varies with time (MEMS sensor, condenser microphone): $i = C\dfrac{dv}{dt} + v\dfrac{dC}{dt}$.

**Integral form.**
$$v(t) = v(t_0) + \frac1C\int_{t_0}^{t} i(\tau)\,d\tau$$

**Direct consequences.**
- DC steady state: $dv/dt = 0 \Rightarrow i = 0$ (open circuit).
- Constant current: $v$ ramps at $I/C$ (1 mA into 1 µF → 1 V/ms).
- Voltage cannot change instantaneously unless current is infinite (an impulse): $v(0^+) = v(0^-)$.
- Sinusoid $v = V_m\sin\omega t \Rightarrow i = \omega CV_m\cos\omega t$; hence $Z_C = 1/(j\omega C)$ ([[q0024]]).

**Energy.** $p = vi = Cv\,\dfrac{dv}{dt} = \dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12Cv^2\right)$, so stored energy $W = \tfrac12Cv^2$.

**Series RC driven by $V_s$** (KVL with $i = C\,dv_C/dt$):
$$RC\frac{dv_C}{dt} + v_C = V_s,\qquad v_C(t) = V_s + \left(v_C(0) - V_s\right)e^{-t/\tau},\ \ \tau = RC$$
After $1\tau$: 63.2%, $5\tau$: 99.3% of the way. Current: $i(t) = \dfrac{V_s - v_C(0)}{R}e^{-t/\tau}$.

**Laplace form.** $I(s) = sCV(s) - Cv(0^-)$.

**Op-amp integrator.** Inverting input at virtual ground forces $i = v_{in}/R$ into C: $v_{out} = -\dfrac{1}{RC}\int v_{in}\,dt + v_{out}(0)$.

**Worked numbers.** A 0 → 5 V edge in 10 µs across 1 nF needs $i = 1\,\text{nF}\times0.5\,\text{V/µs} = 0.5$ mA. $v = 5\sin(2\pi\cdot1\text{k}\,t)$ across 1 µF draws $i_{peak} = 31.4$ mA.
:::

::: long
"The differential equation for a capacitor is $i = C\,dv/dt$. Physically it says the current flowing into the capacitor is proportional to how fast its voltage is changing, with the capacitance as the constant.

Deriving it takes one step. A capacitor stores charge $Q = CV$. Current is the rate of flow of charge, $dQ/dt$. If the capacitance is constant, differentiating gives $i = C\,dv/dt$. If the capacitance itself changes, like in a MEMS accelerometer or a condenser microphone, there's an extra $v\,dC/dt$ term, and that's actually how those sensors produce a signal.

I can also write it as an integral: $v(t) = v(0) + \frac1C\int_0^t i\,d\tau$. That form highlights two things: the capacitor integrates current, and it has memory, because you need the initial voltage.

Then I'd pull out the consequences. If the voltage is constant, the derivative is zero, so no current flows: at DC steady state the capacitor is an open circuit. If I drive a constant current into it, the voltage ramps linearly at $I/C$. That's how ramp generators and soft-start circuits work. If I tried to change the voltage instantaneously, I'd need infinite current, so in any real circuit the capacitor voltage is continuous. That's the continuity condition.

Putting it into a circuit is where it gets useful. For a resistor in series with a capacitor driven by $V_s$, KVL gives $V_s = iR + v_C$, and substituting $i = C\,dv_C/dt$ gives $RC\,dv_C/dt + v_C = V_s$. That's a first-order linear ODE, and the solution is the familiar exponential, $v_C = V_s(1 - e^{-t/RC})$ from zero, reaching 63% after one time constant and about 99% after five.

The same equation also gives the AC behaviour: a sinusoidal voltage gives a cosine current, shifted 90 degrees, which is where the impedance $1/(j\omega C)$ comes from. And it's the dual of the inductor equation, $v = L\,di/dt$."
:::

::: terms
- **Capacitor I–V relation**: $i = C\,dv/dt$, the defining differential equation.
- **Integral form**: $v(t) = v(t_0) + \frac1C\int i\,dt$; the capacitor integrates current.
- **Initial condition**: the capacitor voltage at the start of the analysis, $v(0^-)$.
- **Time constant ($\tau = RC$)**: time for a first-order RC response to cover 63.2% of the remaining distance to its final value.
- **Continuity**: capacitor voltage cannot change instantaneously; $v(0^+) = v(0^-)$.
- **Passive sign convention**: current defined entering the "+" terminal, so $p = vi > 0$ means the capacitor absorbs energy.
- **Integrator**: a circuit (often an op-amp with a feedback capacitor) whose output is proportional to the integral of its input.
- **Duality**: the capacitor ($i = C\,dv/dt$) and inductor ($v = L\,di/dt$) equations swap voltage and current.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** writes $i = C\,dv/dt$.

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives it from $Q = CV$ and mentions the extra $v\,dC/dt$ term for time-varying capacitance.
- Gives the integral form with the initial condition, and explains "memory".
- Reads off the key behaviours with numbers: open at DC, ramp under constant current (1 mA/1 µF = 1 V/ms), voltage continuity.
- Builds the RC ODE from KVL and solves or states the exponential with $\tau = RC$.
- Connects it to impedance $1/(j\omega C)$ and to the inductor's dual equation.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Writing $v = C\,di/dt$**: that's mixing up the inductor's form. Check units: $C\,dv/dt$ has units F·V/s = C/s = A. ✓
- **Forgetting the initial condition** in the integral form. Two identical capacitors with different starting voltages respond differently to the same current.
- **Thinking current through a capacitor depends on voltage magnitude.** It depends on the *rate of change*. A capacitor at 1000 V DC carries zero steady current; one at 1 V changing at 1 V/ns carries a lot.
- **Sign errors**: with passive sign convention, positive current into the "+" terminal increases $v$. If you define current the other way, $i = -C\,dv/dt$.
- **Assuming the exponential applies to every circuit.** It's the solution for a capacitor with a Thevenin resistance driven by a constant source. Constant-current charging gives a ramp; RLC gives second-order behaviour.
:::

::: deep
## Deriving it, carefully

The capacitor's charge is proportional to its voltage, $q = Cv$ ([[q0022]]). Current is defined as the rate at which charge flows: $i = dq/dt$. Charge that flows into the capacitor's "+" terminal accumulates on that plate (and an equal charge leaves the other plate). So

$$i = \frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(Cv) = C\frac{dv}{dt} + v\frac{dC}{dt}$$

For a normal, fixed capacitor, $dC/dt = 0$:

$$\boxed{i = C\frac{dv}{dt}}$$

The second term isn't just a footnote. Condenser microphones and MEMS accelerometers keep $v$ roughly constant and let $C$ change mechanically; the resulting $v\,dC/dt$ current *is* the signal.

**Units check.** F × V/s = (C/V)(V/s) = C/s = A.

### The integral form and "memory"

Integrate both sides from a start time $t_0$:

$$v(t) = v(t_0) + \frac{1}{C}\int_{t_0}^{t} i(\tau)\,d\tau$$

The capacitor voltage at any moment depends on the **entire history** of current that has flowed into it, summarized by $v(t_0)$. This is why a capacitor is a state variable: you can't analyse an RC circuit without knowing its starting voltage.

## Reading the equation: four behaviours

### 1. Constant voltage → zero current (open at DC)

If $v$ isn't changing, $dv/dt = 0$, so $i = 0$. Once all transients have died out in a DC circuit, every capacitor carries no current and can be replaced by an open circuit for analysis ([[q0028]]).

### 2. Constant current → linear ramp

$$\frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{I}{C}$$

1 mA into 1 µF: 1 V/ms. 10 mA into 100 µF: 100 V/s, or 0.1 V/ms. This is the basis of ramp and sawtooth generators, slope compensation, soft-start (a current source charging a capacitor sets the regulator's reference ramp) and dual-slope ADCs.

### 3. Step in voltage → impulse of current

Read the equation the other way: if you try to force $v$ to jump instantly, $dv/dt$ is infinite and so is $i$. Real circuits always have some resistance and inductance, so instead you get a *large but finite* current spike. This is the inrush current when you hot-plug a board with big input capacitors, and the reason gate drivers need amps of peak current. It's also why capacitor voltage is treated as **continuous**: $v_C(0^+) = v_C(0^-)$ ([[q0037]]).

### 4. Sinusoid → 90° shifted sinusoid

$v = V_m\sin\omega t \Rightarrow i = \omega CV_m\cos\omega t$. The current leads by 90° and its amplitude grows with frequency; dividing gives the impedance $1/(j\omega C)$ ([[q0024]]). Example: 5 V peak at 1 kHz across 1 µF gives $i_{peak} = 2\pi\cdot1000\cdot10^{-6}\cdot5 = 31.4$ mA.

KVL around the loop for $t > 0$:

$$V_s = iR + v_C,\qquad i = C\frac{dv_C}{dt}$$

$$\Rightarrow\quad RC\frac{dv_C}{dt} + v_C = V_s$$

This is a first-order linear ODE. Its solution is the sum of the forced (final) response and a decaying natural response:

$$v_C(t) = V_s + \big(v_C(0) - V_s\big)e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau = RC$$

You can check it: at $t = 0$ it gives $v_C(0)$ (continuity ✓); as $t\to\infty$ it gives $V_s$ (open circuit at DC ✓); substituting into the ODE works ✓.

The current follows from $i = C\,dv_C/dt$:

$$i(t) = \frac{V_s - v_C(0)}{R}e^{-t/\tau}$$

At $t = 0^+$ the capacitor hasn't moved, so the whole difference $V_s - v_C(0)$ appears across $R$: the capacitor initially behaves like a voltage source equal to its initial voltage (a short if it started discharged).

| Time | Fraction of the way to final value |
|---|---|
| $1\tau$ | 63.2% |
| $2\tau$ | 86.5% |
| $3\tau$ | 95.0% |
| $5\tau$ | 99.3% |
| $\tau\ln 10 \approx 2.3\tau$ | 90% |

**Worked example.** 10 kΩ and 10 µF: $\tau = 0.1$ s. Charging from 0 V to 90% of a 3.3 V supply (2.97 V) takes $2.3\tau = 0.23$ s. This is the kind of calculation behind a simple power-on reset delay.

## The op-amp integrator: the equation as a circuit

Place a capacitor in the feedback path of an inverting op-amp. The inverting input sits at virtual ground, so the input current is $v_{in}/R$, and all of it must flow into the capacitor:

$$C\frac{d(0 - v_{out})}{dt} = \frac{v_{in}}{R} \;\Rightarrow\; v_{out}(t) = -\frac{1}{RC}\int_0^t v_{in}\,d\tau + v_{out}(0)$$

That's $i = C\,dv/dt$ implemented directly, used in PID controllers, waveform generators and the analog front end of dual-slope ADCs. In practice a large resistor across the capacitor limits DC gain so offsets don't integrate the output into a rail.

## Energy from the differential equation

Power into the capacitor is

$$p = vi = Cv\frac{dv}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac12Cv^2\right)$$

So the stored energy is $W = \tfrac12Cv^2$, and it depends only on the present voltage, not on the path taken to get there. That's a neat way to show an ideal capacitor is lossless.

## Real-world refinements

- **Nonlinear C**: Class II MLCCs and varactors have $C(v)$. Then $i = C(v)\,dv/dt$ with the *incremental* capacitance $C(v) = dq/dv$.
- **ESR in series**: the capacitor terminal voltage can jump by $i\cdot\text{ESR}$ when current steps, even though the ideal internal capacitor's voltage is continuous. That's the step you see in buck converter output ripple.
- **Leakage**: a parallel resistance adds $v/R_{leak}$ to the current, so a "held" voltage slowly decays (sample-and-hold droop).
- **Dielectric absorption**: extra slow RC branches make a discharged capacitor partially recover its voltage.
:::

::: applications
- **RC delays and power-on reset**: $\tau = RC$ sets how long a reset line stays low after power-up.
- **Gate drive sizing**: a MOSFET's gate charge must be delivered in the switching time, so $I \approx Q_g/t_{sw}$ (e.g. 20 nC in 20 ns = 1 A).
- **Inrush current limiting**: hot-swap controllers ramp $V_{out}$ at a controlled $dv/dt$ so that $I_{inrush} = C_{load}\,dv/dt$ stays within limits (e.g. 470 µF at 12 V/10 ms = 0.56 A).
- **Ramp and timer circuits**: the 555 timer, slope compensation, soft-start pins.
- **Op-amp integrators and PI compensators** in control loops.
- **Capacitive sensors**: MEMS accelerometers and microphones use the $v\,dC/dt$ term.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Constant, linear capacitance.** Otherwise include $v\,dC/dt$ or use incremental $C(v)$.
- **Passive sign convention**: current defined into the "+" terminal. Reversing the reference direction introduces a minus sign.
- **Lumped element**: the capacitor is small compared with the signal wavelength, so the voltage is the same across its terminals at any instant.
- **Ideal part**: no ESR, ESL, leakage or dielectric absorption. Include these when edges are fast or precision matters.

An interviewer may also accept the integral form as "the differential equation"; giving both is safest.
:::

::: followups
### A 100 µF capacitor is charged with a constant 10 mA. How long until it reaches 5 V from 0 V?
$dv/dt = I/C = 10\,\text{mA}/100\,\mu\text{F} = 100$ V/s, so 5 V takes 50 ms. Equivalently $t = CV/I = 100\,\mu\times5/10\,\text{m} = 0.05$ s.

### What's the current spike if you hot-plug a 470 µF capacitor onto a 12 V rail through 50 mΩ of total resistance?
Initially the capacitor is 0 V and acts like a short, so $I_{peak} \approx 12/0.05 = 240$ A (limited in reality by connector and trace inductance too). The time constant is $0.05\times470\,\mu = 23.5$ µs. That's why hot-swap controllers or NTC inrush limiters are used to control $dv/dt$.

### What is the differential equation for an inductor, and how does it compare?
$v = L\,di/dt$. It's the dual: swap voltage and current, and C with L. The inductor's current is continuous, it's a short at DC, and constant voltage gives a current ramp. See [[q0041]].

### What if the capacitance changes over time, like in a condenser microphone?
$i = d(Cv)/dt = C\,dv/dt + v\,dC/dt$. A condenser mic holds a bias voltage (via a large resistor, so $v$ is roughly constant on audio timescales), and sound moves the diaphragm, changing $C$. The resulting current $v\,dC/dt$, through the bias resistor, becomes the output signal.

### Solve for the capacitor voltage in an RC circuit discharging from 5 V through 1 kΩ, C = 1 µF. What is it after 2 ms?
$v(t) = 5e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = 1$ ms. At 2 ms: $5e^{-2} = 0.677$ V. The ODE is $RC\,dv/dt + v = 0$ with $v(0) = 5$ V.
