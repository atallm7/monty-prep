::: testing
Whether you've actually put resistors on a board. The interviewer wants:

1. The common SMD size codes (0402, 0603, 0805, 1206, 2512) and roughly what they mean physically, including that the code is imperial (inches × 100) and metric codes differ.
2. The link between **size and power rating** (and voltage rating), with the correct numbers.
3. The through-hole alternatives (axial 1/4 W, power types) and special formats: arrays, MELF, current-sense, thin-film.
4. Judgement: how you pick a package for power, assembly yield, density, and parasitics.
:::

::: why
Package choice is a trade between board area, power handling, voltage rating, parasitics, cost and manufacturability. Picking 0402 everywhere crams a board but can overheat a resistor that dissipates 150 mW, or fail the creepage for a 400 V node; picking 1206 everywhere wastes space. Hand-assembly and rework (prototypes) are far easier at 0603 and up than at 0201. Understanding sizes also lets you read a BOM and a footprint, and it is a standard "have you built a board" check.
:::

::: concepts
- **Power dissipation and derating** ([[q0006]]): $P = V^2/R=I^2R$ must stay below a fraction of the rated power at the board temperature.
- **Thermal path**: bigger body and bigger pads mean lower thermal resistance and a higher power rating.
- **Voltage rating**: set by body length and construction (maximum working voltage, and a maximum overload voltage).
- **Parasitics** ([[q0016]]): smaller packages have lower $L_s$ and $C_p$.
- **Assembly**: pick-and-place, solder paste volume and tombstoning risk grow harder as parts shrink.
- **Standard values and tolerances** ([[q0017]]): availability by package differs (E96 in 0402+, fewer options in 01005).
:::

::: answer
"For SMD, the sizes are named by length and width in hundredths of an inch. 0402 is 40 by 20 mils, about 1 by 0.5 millimeters. 0603 is 1.6 by 0.8 millimeters, 0805 is 2 by 1.25, 1206 is 3.2 by 1.6, and for power there's 2010 and 2512, which is 6.3 by 3.2. Smaller than that you've got 0201 and 01005, which show up in phones and wearables.

The rated power goes up with size, roughly 1/16 to 1/10 watt for 0402, 1/10 watt for 0603, 1/8 for 0805, 1/4 for 1206, and about 1 watt for 2512, though that's vendor dependent. Voltage ratings go up with length too, about 50 V for 0402 and 150 to 200 V for 0805 and 1206.

For through-hole, you've got axial 1/4 watt parts as the default, 1/2, 1 and 2 watt axials, and bigger wirewound or chassis-mount ones for tens of watts. There's also resistor arrays, MELF cylinders, and low-ohm current-sense parts.

For choosing, I'd compute the power, derate it by half, check the voltage rating, and consider the assembly. 0603 is the sweet spot for hand-solderable prototypes, 0402 for dense production boards."
:::

"For surface mount, the package code is the length and width in hundredths of an inch. 0402 is 40 by 20 mils, which is about 1 by 0.5 millimeters. 0603 is 1.6 by 0.8 millimeters, 0805 is 2 by 1.25, 1206 is 3.2 by 1.6, 2010 is 5 by 2.5, and 2512 is 6.3 by 3.2. Be careful because metric codes are different: a metric 0603 is an imperial 0201, so I always confirm which one a BOM is using. In phones you'll find 0201 and 01005.

The size sets the power. Typical general purpose parts: 0402 is about 1/16 to 1/10 watt, 0603 is 1/10 watt, 0805 is 1/8, 1206 is 1/4, 2512 is 1 watt, and some are rated for 2. The reason is that the heat has to leave through the terminations and the board, so a bigger body and bigger pads means a lower thermal resistance. The voltage rating also scales with length, and it's about 50 volts for 0402 up to 150 to 200 for 0805 and 1206. Those are typical, not universal.

For through-hole, 1/4 watt axial is the default, then 1/2, 1 and 2 watt, with wirewound for higher power, and chassis-mount or TO-220 packages if I need a heatsink.

There are also resistor arrays, which save placement and give matched values, MELF cylinders for stable, pulse-tolerant uses, and four-terminal current-sense parts.

Picking: I compute the actual dissipation, derate by 50 percent, check the voltage rating, think about assembly and rework, and parasitics if it's a fast circuit. 0603 for prototypes, 0402 for dense production, 1206 or 2512 where power matters."
:::

::: terms
- **Imperial / metric size code**: 0603 imperial = 1608 metric (1.6 × 0.8 mm); the metric 0603 is imperial 0201.
- **Power rating**: maximum continuous dissipation at a specified ambient (e.g. 70 °C), derated above.
- **Derating curve**: linear decrease of allowed power with temperature.
- **Working voltage / overload voltage**: maximum continuous and short-term voltage.
- **MELF**: Metal Electrode Leadless Face; cylindrical SMD resistor.
- **Resistor array**: several resistors in one package.
- **Kelvin (4-terminal) sense resistor**: separate current and sense terminals for accurate low-ohm measurement.
- **Tombstoning**: a small chip part standing up on one end during reflow due to unequal wetting.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** lists 0402, 0603, 0805 and says "bigger means more power".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Explains the naming (hundredths of an inch) and warns about the imperial/metric confusion.
- Gives approximate power and voltage ratings per size, and flags that they vary by vendor/series.
- Explains *why* size sets power (thermal path) and mentions derating vs temperature and board copper.
- Covers specialty types (array, MELF, Kelvin shunts, power thick-film, wirewound) and when each is chosen.
- States a selection procedure (compute power, derate, voltage, pulse, parasitics, assembly) with an example.
- Mentions assembly aspects: hand soldering limits, tombstoning, pick-and-place, inspection, rework.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Confusing imperial and metric codes.** A metric 0603 (0.6 × 0.3 mm) is not the common 0603 (1.6 × 0.8 mm); wrong footprints don't fit.
- **Using the full rated power.** Rated power is at 70 °C with a good thermal path; in a hot enclosure or with a small pad, derate heavily (50 % or more).
- **Ignoring voltage rating.** A single 0603 across 400 V exceeds its ~75 V rating, whatever its power; use a longer package or a series string.
- **Ignoring pulse power.** The average power can be fine while a short high-energy pulse fuses the film; use pulse-rated or MELF/wirewound parts.
- **Picking 01005/0201 for a prototype you will hand-solder.**
- **Thinking all parts of the same size have the same rating.** Thin-film, high-power and automotive series differ.
:::

::: deep
## Why size sets power

A resistor converts all its power into heat, which flows through the terminations into the PCB copper and out to air. The temperature rise is $\Delta T=P\,\theta$, where $\theta$ is the thermal resistance (K/W). A larger body with larger terminations and pads gives a lower $\theta$ and so allows more power for the same temperature rise. Manufacturers fix the maximum film temperature (typically 125–155 °C), and the rated power is the dissipation at 70 °C ambient that brings the part to it, with a linear derating curve above.

## Selecting a package: a worked example

Case: a 1 kΩ resistor across 12 V.
- $P=V^2/R = 144$ mW.
- With 50 % derating, I need a part rated for ≥ 288 mW: 1206 (250 mW) falls short, so use 1210 (500 mW) or 2010.
- Voltage: 12 V is well below any rating.
- Alternative: split the load. Two 500 Ω resistors in series (6 V across each) dissipate 72 mW each, and two 2 kΩ resistors in parallel (12 V across each) also dissipate 72 mW each, so two 1206 parts (about 29 % loaded) can replace one large part if you accept the extra placement; two 0805s (about 58 % loaded) would work only if you accept less than the 50 % margin.

Case: a 10 MΩ high-voltage divider on a 600 V bus. A single 0805 is rated ~150 V working. You need at least 4 resistors in series (150 V each) and, with margin, 6–8 parts of about 1.25–1.67 MΩ each so the voltage per part stays well below rating, and the creepage/clearance on the board must be respected.

Case: a 4.7 kΩ pull-up on a 3.3 V line: $P=V^2/R=2.3$ mW even when asserted, so 0402 is fine, and 0201 if density demands.

## Thermal and board effects

The rated power assumes standard pads. Copper area under the terminals acts as a heat spreader: wider pads and copper pours raise allowable power; a hot enclosure or neighboring heat sources lower it. In precision circuits, self-heating changes the resistance via TCR ([[q0015]]), so a 1 mW rise may matter for 0.01 % parts. Many precision parts therefore sit at 1/10 of rated power or less.

## Turning the derating curve into a thermal resistance

The UNCERTAIN note above gives a convenient model: rated power at 70 °C ambient, falling linearly to zero at about 155 °C. That is a straight line, so the allowed power at ambient $T_a$ is

$$P_{max}(T_a) = P_{rated}\,\frac{155 - T_a}{155 - 70} = P_{rated}\,\frac{155 - T_a}{85}.$$

The same two numbers hide a thermal resistance. At rated power the film sits at 155 °C with ambient at 70 °C, so $\theta = 85\ \text{K}/P_{rated}$:

| Package | Typical rating | Implied $\theta$ | Allowed at 85 °C ambient |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0603 | 100 mW | 850 K/W | 82 mW |
| 1206 | 250 mW | 340 K/W | 206 mW |
| 2010 | 500 mW | 170 K/W | 412 mW |
| 2512 | 1 W | 85 K/W | 824 mW |

These are curve-fit values for a standard land pattern, not measured numbers for any specific part. Even so they make the physics visible: the area ratio from 0603 to 2512 is about 16 and the implied $\theta$ ratio is 10, so thermal resistance falls a little slower than area, because heat leaves through the terminations and pads, not through the whole body. Now a design check. A 1206 dissipating 100 mW sits $100\text{ mW} \times 340\text{ K/W} = 34$ K above its pads, a 0603 at 50 mW sits 42.5 K above, and a 2 kΩ resistor across 5 V (12.5 mW) in a 0603 sits barely 11 K above.

## Worked example: a hot enclosure

Say a bleeder resistor dissipates 300 mW in a sealed box that reaches 85 °C. I want a 50 % margin on the allowed power at that temperature, so I need $P_{rated} \cdot 70/85 \cdot 0.5 \ge 300$ mW, which gives $P_{rated} \ge 300 \times 2 \times 85/70 = 729$ mW. A 2010 at 500 mW fails, a 2512 at 1 W passes with its allowed 824 mW at 85 °C and an actual load of 36 % of that. If space does not allow a 2512, use two 2010 parts in series, each at 150 mW against an allowed 412 mW (36 %). Series is better than parallel here because the dissipation splits evenly regardless of small value differences.

## Pulses: why average power is the wrong number

A chip resistor's film has little thermal mass, and short pulses are limited by energy, not average power. Imagine a 10 Ω resistor limiting the inrush into a 100 µF capacitor charging to 24 V. The capacitor stores $\tfrac12 CV^2 = 28.8$ mJ, and regardless of the resistance, the resistor dissipates that same 28.8 mJ in the charging event. The peak power is $24^2/10 = 57.6$ W with a 1 ms time constant. The average power, even at one event per second, is only 29 mW, so a naive check says that a 0603 is perfect. In reality, 57.6 W through a 0603 film for about a millisecond is far outside what a general-purpose part tolerates. Use the manufacturer's pulse-load curve (permitted power versus pulse duration) for an anti-surge or pulse-rated series, or a wirewound or MELF part, and repeat the check at the worst case, such as the supply hot-plugged with the capacitor fully discharged.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Pulse capability is series-specific and is shown on a log-log curve in each vendor's datasheet. Do not assume a number from the size code alone.

## Voltage rating and creepage

The 75 V figure for 0603 is a working voltage limit linked to the body length, because a long body keeps the film's electric field below the point where it degrades. For the 600 V, 10 MΩ divider in the example above, eight series resistors place 75 V across each, which only just reaches the typical 0603 working voltage with no margin, while six parts put 100 V across each, comfortably inside an 0805's ~150 V. Either way the board also needs surface spacing to avoid creepage tracks across contaminated FR-4, so the string is laid out in a line with the full body-to-body gaps kept clean. The dissipation is tiny in either case: $600^2/10\text{ M}\Omega = 36$ mW in total, about 4.5 mW per part with eight. Here the voltage rating, not the power, picks the package.

## Parasitics in numbers

An 0603 with its pads typically brings roughly half a nanohenry to a nanohenry of series inductance and a tenth of a picofarad or so across the body (order-of-magnitude values that depend on the land pattern and film layout; the vendor's S-parameter file is the real source). At 1 GHz, 0.7 nH is 4.4 Ω, which is already visible against a 50 Ω termination. This is why RF designers prefer 0402 and smaller for terminations and keep the pads short, and why placing a series resistor close to a driver matters more than which size it is.

## Verifying on the bench

1. **Temperature rise.** Run the circuit at its worst-case load and photograph the resistor with a thermal camera. Convert the reading to a rise over the board and compare with $P\theta$. Note that the emissivity of the white or black body is not 1 and reads low unless you correct for it; a thermocouple on the pad is a better cross-check.
2. **Pulse test.** For inrush resistors, capture the voltage across the part with a scope and a differential probe, integrate $v^2/R$ to get the energy, and compare it with the datasheet pulse curve. Repeat many times because film damage shows up as a drift in resistance, not as a failure on the first pulse.
3. **Resistance check after stress.** A 0.1 % shift after power cycling indicates the film is being driven too hard even if nothing has failed.

## Special formats

- **Resistor arrays:** 4 or 8 resistors in a single package (e.g. 0603 x4 convex terminals). Matched values and tracking tolerance; fewer placements.
- **MELF:** cylindrical metal-film parts with excellent stability and pulse handling and very low noise; larger and harder to place.
- **Thin-film chips:** ±0.01–0.1 % and TCR down to ±5 ppm/K at higher cost.
- **Current-sense resistors:** low value (0.5–100 mΩ), metal-strip/alloy; 2-terminal or Kelvin 4-terminal for accurate measurement.
- **Anti-surge/pulse-rated, high-voltage and anti-sulfur** series for harsh environments ([[q0019]]).
- **Zero-ohm links:** rated by current; usually 1–2 A for 0603/0805.

## Assembly and parasitics

Smaller parts have lower $L_s$ and $C_p$ ([[q0016]]), so they're preferred for RF and fast edges. Against that: 01005 and 0201 need very precise paste printing and placement and are hard to inspect or rework. Tombstoning risk rises for small parts with unequal pad heating, so keep pad geometry symmetric and avoid a via-in-pad on one side. For hand soldering, 0603 is about the smallest comfortable size; 0402 is doable with a steady hand and good tweezers.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> A good compact answer: "I'd pick the smallest package that meets power with 50 % derating, voltage rating, and any pulse requirement, and I'd stay with one or two sizes across the board to keep the BOM and assembly simple."
:::

::: applications
- **Dense consumer boards** (phones, wearables): 0201/01005 and 0402 for pull-ups, dividers, series damping.
- **General microcontroller boards**: 0402 or 0603 for most resistors; 0805/1206 for power-dissipating ones.
- **Power supplies**: 1206/2010/2512 for sense resistors, snubbers, bleeders; wirewound or chassis types for load dumps.
- **Precision instrumentation**: thin-film 0603/0805 networks.
- **Industrial/high-voltage**: long 1206 or MELF strings for dividers, plus HV-rated chip resistors.
- **Prototypes and hobby**: 0805/1206 and through-hole 1/4 W for hand assembly.
:::

::: assumptions
- Typical power and voltage ratings are for **general-purpose thick-film** chips at 70 °C; special series differ.
- The EIA code system is **imperial**; some regions and vendors list metric codes, and I confirm the system before sourcing.
- Power ratings assume a **standard land pattern** on FR-4; in a hot environment or with small pads, derate more.
- I treat "common" as meaning commonly used on general electronics boards; automotive and aerospace boards add qualification requirements (AEC-Q200, etc.) that may restrict choices.
- The best package is context-dependent: density, power, voltage, cost, assembly.
:::

::: followups
### What is the difference between 0603 imperial and 0603 metric?
Imperial 0603 is 0.06 × 0.03 in (1.6 × 0.8 mm); metric 0603 is 0.6 × 0.3 mm, which is imperial 0201. The names collide, so BOMs should state the system or give dimensions (1608M vs 0603I). Ordering the wrong one gives a part that can't be placed on your footprint.

### A resistor dissipates 200 mW in a 70 °C enclosure. Which package do you choose?
Derate by 50 %, so a 400 mW-class part: 1210 (500 mW typical) or 2010. Or split the dissipation between two 1 kΩ-class resistors in series, 100 mW each, using 1206 parts (250 mW rated). Check that the enclosure temperature doesn't push the film beyond the derating curve.

### Why would you use a resistor array?
Matched values and tracking for divider/ladder networks, lower assembly cost (one placement for 4–8 resistors), and smaller area for things like data-line series termination or pull-up banks. The trade-off is that you can't change one resistor independently and the power rating per element is lower.

### What if a thick-film resistor fails open after months in a humid industrial environment?
Silver in the terminations can react with sulfur-containing air to form silver sulfide, an insulator, eventually opening the connection. Use anti-sulfur (sulfur-resistant) series with different terminations, or conformal coat. See [[q0019]].

### How does pad size affect power rating?
The pad and attached copper is the heat sink. The datasheet's rating assumes a specific land pattern. A smaller pad or isolated copper raises the thermal resistance, so the part runs hotter at the same power. Adding copper pour or wider traces connected to the pads can raise the usable power by tens of percent.
:::
