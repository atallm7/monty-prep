::: basics
### In plain words
Where you put a decoupling capacitor and how you connect it matters as much as its value. The capacitor's current flows in a loop: out of the capacitor, into the chip's supply pin, through the chip, out the ground pin and back. Every layout rule is a way to shrink that loop. Place the capacitor next to the pin, connect it with short, wide paths or vias right at its pads, and use one capacitor per supply pin.

### Picture it
Picture a fire extinguisher. If it hangs right next to the stove, you grab it and act within a second. If it is down the hall, the fire has grown before you return. A decoupling capacitor is the extinguisher and the chip pin is the stove. The distance, and the route you must take, decide how fast help arrives. The analogy breaks in one place: here the "delay" is the inductance of the path, which resists fast current changes.

### Words you need
- **Loop inductance**: the inductance of the full current loop from capacitor to pin and back ([[q0501]]).
- **Via**: a plated hole joining layers. A shorter via has lower inductance ([[q0510]]).
- **Power and ground plane pair**: close planes that lower the loop's inductance ([[q0486]]).
- **Mounting inductance**: the pads, traces and vias that add to the capacitor's own ESL ([[q0030]]).
- **Value hierarchy**: smallest value nearest the pin, then larger ones further out ([[q0502]]).

### The simplest example
Take a 100 nF capacitor with 0.5 nH of total loop inductance. It resonates at about 22.5 MHz, and at 100 MHz its impedance is $2\pi f L = 2\pi \times 10^{8} \times 0.5\ \text{nH} \approx 0.31$ Ω. Mount the same part with 3 nH of trace and via, say 5 mm of fan-out, and the resonance drops to about 9.2 MHz. At 100 MHz the impedance becomes about 1.9 Ω. The capacitance never changed. Only the loop did. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers via inductance numbers, each rule in detail, the "through the cap" exception, and how to check the result.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can turn "decoupling needs low inductance" into **specific, ordered layout habits**. The interviewer is looking for:

1. Loop inductance as the organising idea: pin, capacitor, vias and plane form one current loop, and every rule is about making that loop small.
2. The concrete rules: closest possible, short and wide connections, vias at the pads, one capacitor per pin, smallest value nearest, same side as the IC.
3. Awareness of the trade-offs and exceptions (power through the cap vs via at the pad, BGA backside placement).
4. Some numbers for how much inductance each choice adds.
:::

::: why
Two boards with identical schematics and bills of materials can differ by 10× in supply noise because of how the decoupling capacitors were placed and routed. Layout is where decoupling is won or lost. It is also cheap to get right at the start and nearly impossible to fix after fabrication. Interviewers who ask this want to see that you think about the *current path*, not just parts on a schematic.
:::

::: concepts
- **Loop inductance**: the inductance of the full path from capacitor, through supply pin, die, ground pin, and back to the capacitor ([[q0501]]).
- **Via inductance**: each via adds inductance that scales with its length ([[q0510]]).
- **Plane proximity**: a power/ground plane pair close to the surface lowers the inductance of the return and spreading ([[q0486]]).
- **Capacitor ESL and mounting**: the part's own ESL plus pads and traces set the SRF ([[q0030]], [[q0509]]).
- **Value hierarchy**: smaller values closest to the pin ([[q0502]]).
- **Multiple capacitors**: layout of several values in a group ([[q0507]]).
:::

::: answer
"The one idea behind all the rules is to minimise the inductance of the loop that the capacitor's current travels around: from the capacitor, into the supply pin, through the chip, out the ground pin, and back to the capacitor. So the rules are all ways of making that loop small.

First, place the capacitor as close to the pin as possible, ideally touching it, on the same side as the chip, and put the smallest value closest. Second, connect it with short, wide traces, or better, connect straight to the planes with a via right at the capacitor's pad, and put the ground via right next to the power via so the loop area is tiny. If I have to choose, I'd rather have the via adjacent to the pad than a long trace to a via somewhere else. Third, one capacitor per supply pin or pin pair, not one shared between ten pins. Fourth, don't route other signals between the capacitor and the pin, and don't daisy-chain one capacitor's route through another. For BGAs, I'd put capacitors on the back directly beneath the balls with vias in the escape pattern.

And where a bigger bulk capacitor goes is less critical: near the regulator or the entry. I'd check the result by looking at supply noise with a short-lead probe."
:::

::: technical
**Governing relation.** The capacitor's effective impedance at frequency $f$ is $|Z|\approx\sqrt{ESR^2+(2\pi f L_{tot}-1/(2\pi f C))^2}$ with $L_{tot}=ESL + L_{pads} + L_{vias} + L_{plane} + L_{pkg}$. Above SRF $|Z|\approx 2\pi f L_{tot}$, so minimising $L_{tot}$ is the job. A 100 nF capacitor with 0.5 nH has SRF 22.5 MHz; with 3 nH of mounting it falls to 9.2 MHz, and its impedance at 100 MHz rises from 0.31 Ω to 1.9 Ω.

**Rules of thumb (priority order).**
1. *Distance*: as close to the pin as manufacturing allows. A few mm of trace is a few nH if unreferenced.
2. *Connection*: short, wide, direct; avoid necks. Prefer a via adjacent to each capacitor pad, with the ground via beside the power via to cancel field by mutual inductance.
3. *Vias*: shorter via = lower $L$. Put the power and ground planes close to the surface layer; $L_{via}\approx 5.08\,h\,[\ln(4h/d)+1]$ nH ($h$, $d$ in inches): 0.3 mm drill gives about 0.08 nH at 0.2 mm depth, 0.54 nH at 0.8 mm, 1.3 nH at 1.6 mm.
4. *Multiple vias per pad* for high-current or high-frequency (parallel vias divide $L$).
5. *One capacitor per power-pin pair.* Place each by its own pins, spread around the package.
6. *Order*: smallest value closest, then mid, then bulk further out.
7. *Same side as the IC*, or directly beneath it for BGAs.
8. *Orientation*: orient pads so power and ground connections face each other and the loop area is small.
9. *Don't share vias* between many capacitors, and don't thread a capacitor's connection through another.
10. *Keep other routing out* of the cap-to-pin area.

**Trade-off: "through the cap" vs "via at the pad".** For noise-sensitive or high-Z supplies (PLL, analog), some designers route the supply trace *through* the capacitor pad so noise on the rail passes the capacitor before the pin. For lowest inductance on a plane-fed digital supply, the via goes at the capacitor pad. Datasheet layout guidelines decide.

**Order of magnitude.** Good: 0402 capacitor, 0.3 mm vias at the pads to adjacent planes (0.2 mm dielectric): total mounting ≈ 0.3–0.7 nH. Poor: 5 mm trace each side to remote vias: 5–10 nH or more.
:::

::: short
"All the rules are about shrinking the loop inductance of capacitor, pin and planes. Put the capacitor as close to the supply pin as possible, same side as the chip, smallest value nearest. Connect with short, wide traces or vias right at the pads, power and ground vias next to each other, one capacitor per pin, no other routing in between. Keep power and ground planes near the surface to shorten the vias. For BGAs, mount capacitors on the back under the balls."
:::

::: long
"The way I think about it: a decoupling capacitor only works as well as the loop it sits in. The current goes from the capacitor to the supply pin, through the chip, out the ground pin and back into the capacitor. The inductance of that loop decides how fast the capacitor can respond, so every rule is about shrinking it.

First, distance: as close to the pin as I can get it, ideally the capacitor pad nearly touching the pin, on the same side as the chip. If there's more than one value, the smallest goes closest, because it's the one meant to handle the highest frequencies.

Second, the connection. I'd rather have a short, fat trace or, better, a via right at the capacitor pad that drops straight to the plane. And the power via and ground via should be right next to each other, since opposing currents reduce the inductance. Via length matters: with a 0.3 millimetre via it's about 0.08 nanohenries when the plane is 0.2 millimetres below, but 1.3 nanohenries if it has to go through a 1.6 millimetre board. So I like the power and ground planes near the top layer.

Third, one capacitor per pin or pin pair, placed by that pin, rather than one capacitor serving a whole row. Fourth, no other signal routes between the capacitor and its pin, and no daisy-chaining capacitor to capacitor through a long trace. And for BGAs I'd put them on the back under the ball field, using the via-in-the-escape pattern.

The one real trade-off is that for sensitive analog or PLL supplies I might route power through the capacitor pad so the rail is filtered before the pin; in that case I keep the trace short and wide.

Then I verify: probe the pin with a short ground spring and look at noise, or check the PDN impedance in simulation."
:::

::: terms
- **Loop inductance**: inductance of the current loop through capacitor, pins, die and planes.
- **Mounting inductance**: inductance added by pads, traces and vias between capacitor body and planes.
- **Via-in-pad / via adjacent to pad**: placing the via at the capacitor pad to minimise trace length.
- **Mutual inductance (field cancellation)**: opposite-direction nearby currents reduce net inductance.
- **Escape routing**: routing of a BGA's ball connections outward, using vias in the ball field.
- **Plane spreading inductance**: inductance of current spreading through the plane between vias.
- **Fan-out**: a trace from a pad to a via.
- **PDN**: power distribution network.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer lists "close, short, wide". An exceptional one:

- Anchors all rules in the **loop** (capacitor-pin-die-pin-capacitor) and says what each rule removes from it.
- Gives numbers: via inductance versus depth (0.08 nH at 0.2 mm, 1.3 nH at 1.6 mm), and what 3 nH does to SRF (22.5 → 9.2 MHz).
- Mentions **plane depth** (power/ground close to surface) and **via adjacency** as the biggest levers beyond distance.
- Knows the "through-the-cap" routing exception for sensitive supplies, and the BGA backside pattern.
- Closes with verification (short-lead probing, PDN simulation).
:::

::: mistakes
- **Placing capacitors "near" but with a long thin fan-out.** A 5 mm, 0.2 mm wide trace adds several nH, making the capacitor useless above a few MHz.
- **One capacitor shared by many pins.** The pin farthest from it sees the full path inductance.
- **Vias far from the pads** or one via shared by four capacitors.
- **Power and ground vias on opposite sides of the capacitor.** The loop area is large; place them side by side.
- **Placing bulk capacitors close but small values far away.** The hierarchy is the other way round.
:::

::: deep
## What you are really laying out

Draw the current's path. When the chip needs a burst of current, the closest source is the capacitor. The current flows:

1. out of the capacitor's power terminal,
2. along the trace or via to the IC's supply pin (or through the plane),
3. through the die,
4. out of the ground pin to the ground plane,
5. along the plane and via back to the capacitor's ground terminal.

That is one loop. Its inductance is roughly proportional to the loop area and (weakly) its aspect ratio. Every rule is a way to shrink that area:

## How much inductance does each choice add?

Use the standard partial-inductance estimate for a via of length $h$ and drill diameter $d$ (inches):

$$L_{via}\approx 5.08\,h\left[\ln\frac{4h}{d}+1\right]\ \text{nH}$$

| Via depth to plane | $L$ (0.3 mm drill) |
|---|---|
| 0.2 mm (power plane next to the surface) | ≈ 0.08 nH |
| 0.5 mm | ≈ 0.29 nH |
| 0.8 mm | ≈ 0.54 nH |
| 1.6 mm (plane on the opposite side of the board) | ≈ 1.3 nH |

A capacitor needs two vias (power and ground), so a 2-layer-deep stackup can add 2–3 nH to the loop before you count anything else. Placing the power plane directly beneath the top layer (layer 2 or 3 with thin prepreg) is one of the cheapest big wins. Traces add roughly 0.3–1 nH per mm depending on how close the return is, so 5 mm of fan-out can dwarf the capacitor's own 0.5 nH ESL.

## The rules in detail

**1. Distance and side.** Same side as the IC, adjacent to the supply pin. If a pin pair (VDD, GND) is on one edge, the capacitor spans exactly that pair. On BGAs, the back side under the ball is usually the best place because the vias go straight through the board under the ball.

**2. Connection geometry.** Use the shortest path from pin to pad. If you need a trace, make it as wide as the pad and shorter than the pad is long. Place the via *at the capacitor pad* or inside it where the fab allows via-in-pad with fill.

**3. Via pairs.** Put power and ground vias side by side. Currents flow in opposite directions, so their magnetic fields partially cancel, lowering the pair's inductance. Use 2 vias per pad when current or frequency demands.

**4. Planes.** Put the power and ground plane pair near the surface layer. Spacing between power and ground planes also sets plane-spreading inductance; a thin dielectric between them ($\le$ 0.1–0.2 mm) adds distributed capacitance and lowers inductance.

**5. Order.** Smallest value nearest the pin, then mid-value, then bulk. See [[q0507]] for a group of capacitors.

**6. One per pin.** If the IC has eight VDD pins, each gets its own capacitor nearby, or at least pair-wise sharing, with 100 nF each rather than one large shared capacitor.

**7. Keep the area clear.** No signal traces between the capacitor and pin, and nothing that forces the supply trace to snake.

## The "through the cap" exception

For sensitive supplies (PLL, VCO, analog reference rails), the supply may be routed from the rail, *through* the capacitor's pad, and then to the pin, so the capacitor filters noise from the rail before it reaches the pin. The connection from the capacitor to the pin must still be short and wide, and the capacitor's ground via must be adjacent. In high-current digital supplies, the plane-fed arrangement with vias at the pads is typically better. Follow the part's layout guidelines.

::: applications
- **Microcontrollers (QFP/QFN)**: one 100 nF per VDD pin on the same side, vias at the pads.
- **BGA processors and FPGAs**: capacitors on the back under the ball field, vias in the breakout pattern.
- **Switching regulators**: input capacitor adjacent to the VIN and GND pins, with a tight return.
- **RF and PLL ICs**: supply routed through small capacitors placed against the pins.
- **Board entry and regulator output**: bulk capacitors placed close to the connector or regulator.
:::

::: assumptions
- Four-layer or higher stackup with solid ground and power planes; on a 2-layer board the "plane" is a pour and inductances are higher.
- Capacitors are MLCCs; electrolytic and polymer parts have far higher ESL and are placed for bulk duty rather than high-frequency.
- Inductance numbers are order-of-magnitude. Real values depend on the exact geometry, pad shapes and the return current distribution.
- Where the datasheet gives a layout recommendation (e.g., power-through-cap, via placement), it takes priority.
- Another valid approach is to use specialized low-inductance capacitors (reverse-geometry, array) to reduce the ESL when board space is tight ([[q0511]]).
:::

::: followups
### Where do you put the vias relative to the capacitor pads?
Directly at the pads, as close as the via and pad design allow, with the power via and ground via next to each other so their opposing currents partially cancel. If space allows, two vias per pad. Avoid a via a few millimetres away connected by a trace.

### Which goes closest to the pin: 100 nF or 10 µF?
The 100 nF (or smaller). Its low ESL and high SRF let it supply the fastest current, and its connection inductance must be the smallest. The 10 µF is further out because it handles slower demands where a few nH doesn't matter.

### What if the IC has no space for a capacitor beside the pins?
Put the capacitor on the opposite side of the board, directly under the IC, with vias through the board to the pin pads, or choose a smaller 0201 capacitor, or use via-in-pad. The loop includes two vias of board thickness, so keep the stackup thin where possible.

### What's the effect of a 5 mm trace between the capacitor and the pin?
Roughly 1.5–5 nH depending on how close the return is. For a 100 nF capacitor (0.5 nH ESL) that drops SRF from 22 MHz to about 7–11 MHz and raises |Z| at 100 MHz from 0.3 Ω to about 1.3–3.5 Ω. For fast edges, it's equivalent to having no capacitor at all above that frequency.

### Scenario: layout review shows capacitors 8 mm from the FPGA with 0.15 mm traces. What do you do?
Reject it. Move them to the back of the board under the BGA, use short vias at the ball-field pitch, widen the connection or use vias directly in the pad, and re-run the PDN impedance against the vendor's target. If space prevents it, use more, smaller capacitors closer, accept lower-ESL packages, or reduce the dielectric thickness between planes.
:::
