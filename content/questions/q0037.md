::: testing
Whether you can state the continuity condition and, more importantly, **justify it** from $i=C\,dv/dt$ or from energy, then use it. The interviewer wants:

1. The statement: the voltage across a capacitor cannot change instantaneously (for finite current), $v_C(0^+)=v_C(0^-)$. A capacitor "resists changes in voltage".
2. The reason: an instantaneous jump needs infinite current ($i=C\,dv/dt$), or equivalently infinite power to change the stored energy $\tfrac12CV^2$ in zero time.
3. The usage: initial-condition shortcut for transients (an uncharged capacitor is a short at $t=0^+$, a charged one is a voltage source), and the dual for inductors.
4. Nuance: capacitor *current* can jump; and the "ideal" exceptions (two capacitors joined by an ideal wire) where charge, not voltage, is conserved.
:::

::: why
This is the basis of transient analysis: it gives you the initial condition for every RC, RLC and switching circuit without solving a differential equation. It explains inrush current into a bulk capacitor, why a decoupling capacitor can hold a rail steady for a few nanoseconds, why a probe's capacitance slows an edge, and why an RC filter's output takes time to move. It's also the capacitor half of a pair: capacitors resist changes in voltage; inductors resist changes in current ([[q0043]], [[q0027]]).
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor equation** ([[q0025]]): $i=C\,dv/dt$, so $dv/dt=i/C$ is finite when $i$ is finite.
- **Stored charge and energy**: $Q=CV$ and $E=\tfrac12CV^2$.
- **Initial and final states** ([[q0028]]): at $t=0^+$ a capacitor holds its previous voltage; at DC steady state it is an open circuit.
- **Time constant**: $\tau=RC$ sets how fast the voltage can change when current is limited by a resistance.
- **Dual** ([[q0043]]): inductor current is continuous, $v=L\,di/dt$.
- **Charge conservation**: for ideal topologies that force an instantaneous change, total charge on connected plates is conserved.
:::

::: answer
"The continuity condition says a capacitor's voltage can't change instantly: the voltage just after a switching event equals the voltage just before it. So a capacitor resists changes in voltage. Its current, on the other hand, can jump.

The reason is the capacitor equation, i equals C dv/dt. If the voltage jumped by a finite amount in zero time, dv/dt would be infinite and so would the current, and a real source can't supply that. Energy gives the same answer: the stored energy is half C V squared, and changing it instantaneously would need infinite power.

It's useful because it gives you the initial condition for any transient. If a capacitor starts uncharged, then right after the switch closes it still has zero volts across it, so it acts like a short circuit, and the current is the source voltage over the series resistance. As time passes it charges toward the source with time constant R C, the current decays, and at steady state it's an open circuit. If it started with, say, 2 volts on it, it behaves like a 2 volt source at the instant of switching.

The inductor is the dual: it's the current that is continuous, an inductor looks like an open circuit at the first instant if its initial current is zero, and its voltage can jump. And the caveat: if you connect two ideal capacitors at different voltages with an ideal wire, voltage does jump in theory, so you use conservation of charge, and in real life the wire resistance limits the current and restores continuity."
:::

::: technical
**Statement:** for finite capacitor current, $v_C(0^+)=v_C(0^-)$; equivalently $q(0^+)=q(0^-)$.

**Proof sketch:** $v_C(t)=v_C(t_0)+\dfrac1C\int_{t_0}^{t}i\,dt'$. If $i$ is bounded by $I_{max}$, then $|v_C(t)-v_C(t_0)|\le I_{max}(t-t_0)/C\to0$ as $t\to t_0$.

**Energy:** $P=\dfrac{dE}{dt}=v\,i$; a step in $E=\tfrac12Cv^2$ needs $P\to\infty$.

**Initial/final equivalents**

| Time | Uncharged C | C with $V_0$ |
|---|---|---|
| $t=0^+$ | short circuit | voltage source $V_0$ |
| $t\to\infty$ (DC) | open circuit | open circuit |

**RC step response** (source $V_s$ through $R$, $v_C(0)=0$): $v_C=V_s(1-e^{-t/\tau})$, $i=\dfrac{V_s}{R}e^{-t/\tau}$, $\tau=RC$. $i(0^+)=V_s/R$; $v_C(0^+)=0$.

**Capacitor current can jump:** $i(0^+)\ne i(0^-)$ in the example above.

**Exception (ideal):** two capacitors $C_1$ at $V_1$ and $C_2$ at $V_2$ joined at $t=0$: voltage jumps to $V_f=\dfrac{C_1V_1+C_2V_2}{C_1+C_2}$ (charge conserved); energy lost $=\tfrac12\dfrac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2}(V_1-V_2)^2$, dissipated in whatever resistance there actually is.
:::

::: long
"The continuity condition says that the voltage across a capacitor can't jump: the value just after a switching event equals the value just before. In other words, a capacitor resists changes in voltage, and the dual is that an inductor resists changes in current.

Why? Start from the capacitor equation, i equals C times dv/dt. If the voltage changed by a finite amount in zero time, dv/dt would be infinite, so the current would be infinite. No real source can do that, so for finite current the voltage has to ramp. Another way to see it: the energy is half C V squared. A sudden change in voltage would be a sudden change in energy, which needs infinite power.

What it gives me is the initial condition for any transient problem, without differential equations. Suppose an uncharged capacitor is connected through a resistor R to a source V at time zero. Just after, the capacitor still has zero volts, so it looks like a short: the current is V over R, its maximum. Then the capacitor charges, v rises as V times one minus e to the minus t over R C, the current decays, and at steady state it's an open circuit with the full V across it. If it started at V zero, it looks like a V zero source at that first instant. The current can jump, the voltage can't.

There's a subtlety: if I connect two capacitors at different voltages with an ideal wire, the voltage does jump, and I use conservation of charge. Ten microfarads at 5 volts joined to ten microfarads at zero settles at 2.5 volts, and half the energy disappears. That doesn't violate the rule: the ideal wire has zero resistance, so the current is an impulse. Real wires have resistance and inductance, so the current is finite and the voltage change takes a short time, and the lost energy goes into heat and radiation."
:::

::: terms
- **Continuity condition**: the state variable (capacitor voltage, inductor current) cannot jump for finite drive.
- **State variable**: the quantity tied to stored energy ($v_C$, $i_L$).
- **$t=0^-$ / $t=0^+$**: just before and just after the switching event.
- **Time constant $\tau=RC$**: the time scale of the exponential transition.
- **Inrush current**: initial surge into an uncharged capacitor, $V/R$.
- **Charge conservation**: total charge on isolated, connected plates stays constant.
- **Initial-condition equivalent**: short (uncharged C) or voltage source (charged C).
- **Impulse current**: idealized infinite, zero-width current spike.
:::

**An exceptional answer**:

:::

::: mistakes
- **"The capacitor current can't change instantly."** That's false; the *voltage* is continuous, the current can jump (it's $C\,dv/dt$).
- **Mixing up the dual.** Capacitor voltage and inductor current are continuous; capacitor current and inductor voltage are not.
- **Treating an uncharged capacitor as an open circuit at $t=0^+$.** It is a short at that instant; it's the open circuit at DC steady state.
- **Ignoring initial charge.** A precharged capacitor behaves like a voltage source at $t=0^+$, not a short.
- **Applying continuity to the terminal voltage of a real capacitor.** ESR makes $v_{terminal}=v_C+iR_{esr}$, which steps when $i$ steps.
- **Concluding that charge sharing between capacitors violates continuity** without noting the infinite-current idealization.
:::

::: deep
## Why the voltage cannot jump

Integrate the capacitor equation:
$$v_C(t)=v_C(t_0)+\frac{1}{C}\int_{t_0}^{t}i(\tau)\,d\tau.$$
If $|i|\le I_{max}$ then the change over a tiny interval $\Delta t$ is at most $I_{max}\Delta t/C$, which goes to zero with $\Delta t$. Voltage is the *integral* of current (divided by $C$), and an integral of a bounded function is continuous. The only way to make it jump is an impulse of current, with finite charge delivered in zero time. That's the loophole behind the charge-sharing exception below.

The energy statement is equivalent: $E=\tfrac12Cv^2$, $P=dE/dt$. A step in $v$ is a step in $E$, which needs infinite power.

Physically: the voltage across a capacitor is the charge on the plates divided by $C$, and charge has to physically arrive through the wires. A finite current moves a finite amount of charge per second, so the voltage builds at a finite rate.

## Initial and final equivalent circuits

Mentally replace the capacitor:

- **Just after switching ($t=0^+$)**: voltage source of value $v_C(0^-)$ (a short if that's 0 V).
- **Long after, DC steady state**: open circuit.

For the example: at $0^+$ the loop is $V_s$, $R$ and a 0 V source: $i=5/1\text{k}=5$ mA; at $\infty$, no current, $v_C=5$ V. The time constant then connects them. This "initial value, final value, time constant" three-step method solves any first-order circuit ([[q0028]]).

With initial voltage $V_0$: $v_C(t)=V_s+(V_0-V_s)e^{-t/\tau}$ and $i(0^+)=(V_s-V_0)/R$. For $V_0=2$ V: $i(0^+)=3$ mA.

## Real-world consequences

- **Inrush current:** a 1000 µF bulk capacitor behind a 0.1 Ω source (battery plus wiring) pulls up to $V/R$ = 120 A from a 12 V rail at connection; limit it with a resistor, NTC or soft-start.
- **Decoupling capacitors:** they hold the rail for the first nanoseconds of a load step because their voltage can't jump: $\Delta V=\Delta I\,\Delta t/C$ ([[q0032]]).
- **Probe loading:** a probe's 10 pF in parallel with the node slows an edge because charging it takes finite current.
- **ESR makes the terminal step:** a real capacitor's terminal voltage includes $i\cdot R_{esr}$, so when the current steps the terminal voltage steps by $\Delta I\,R_{esr}$ (the internal ideal-capacitor voltage is still continuous); ESL adds $L\,di/dt$ spikes ([[q0030]]).
- **Switched-capacitor circuits and charge pumps** deliberately connect capacitors at different voltages; the resistance of the switches limits the current, and the energy loss $\tfrac12C_{eq}\Delta V^2$ per transfer is the cost.

## The exception: charge sharing

Connect $C_1=10\ \mu$F at 5 V to an uncharged $C_2=10\ \mu$F through an ideal switch at $t=0$. Voltage continuity would require both capacitor voltages to hold, but they must be equal after joining. Charge conservation gives
$$V_f=\frac{C_1V_1+C_2V_2}{C_1+C_2}=\frac{50\ \mu\text{C}}{20\ \mu\text{F}}=2.5\ \text{V}.$$
Energy before: $\tfrac12(10\ \mu)(25)=125\ \mu$J. After: $\tfrac12(20\ \mu)(6.25)=62.5\ \mu$J. Half of the energy disappeared: $\tfrac12\cdot5\ \mu\text{F}\cdot25=62.5\ \mu$J. In the ideal circuit, the current was an impulse with infinite peak; in a real circuit, the series resistance $R$ makes the exchange take $\tau=R\,C_1C_2/(C_1+C_2)$ and dissipates the 62.5 µJ as heat in $R$ (independent of the value of $R$) or radiates it from the loop inductance. The exception is therefore a statement about the *idealization*, not about real capacitors.

## Worked example: taking the exception apart with a resistor

The charge-sharing exception is where the principle seems to break, so it is worth doing the case with a real resistance in the loop. Join $C_1 = 10\ \mu$F at 5 V to $C_2 = 10\ \mu$F at 0 V through a resistor $R$ at $t = 0$. The two capacitors are in series around the loop, so they behave like one capacitor $C_{eq} = C_1C_2/(C_1+C_2) = 5\ \mu$F discharging through $R$ from an initial difference $\Delta V = 5$ V:

$$i(t)=\frac{\Delta V}{R}e^{-t/\tau},\qquad \tau = R\,C_{eq}.$$

Now watch what happens as $R$ shrinks:

| $R$ | $\tau = RC_{eq}$ | Peak current $\Delta V/R$ | Charge moved $C_{eq}\Delta V$ | Energy lost in $R$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Ω | 5 µs | 5 A | 25 µC | 62.5 µJ |
| 0.01 Ω | 50 ns | 500 A | 25 µC | 62.5 µJ |
| 0 (ideal) | 0 | infinite | 25 µC | 62.5 µJ |

The voltage on each capacitor changes continuously for any $R>0$: it takes a few $\tau$ to settle at 2.5 V. As $R \to 0$ the peak current diverges and the duration shrinks, but the area under the current curve, the charge, stays fixed at 25 µC. In the limit the current becomes an impulse, which is the one thing the continuity argument forbids (it required bounded current). So continuity isn't violated by physics; the ideal circuit simply asks for an impossible current. The energy bookkeeping makes the same point: the lost 62.5 µJ is the same number for every $R$. A smaller resistor does not waste less, it wastes the same energy faster.

## What happens when the resistor is really an inductor

In a real circuit the loop has inductance as well, and when $R$ is tiny the inductance, not $R$, limits the current. With a 10 nH loop (a few centimeters of wiring, order of magnitude) and $C_{eq} = 5\ \mu$F, the circuit rings at $f = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC}) = 712$ kHz. The characteristic impedance is $\sqrt{L/C} = 45$ mΩ, so the peak current is about $\Delta V/Z_0 = 112$ A. The energy isn't dissipated cleanly: it sloshes between the capacitors and the inductor, and whatever small resistance is present damps it over many cycles, while some of it radiates. This is the physical explanation of the sparks, the contact welding, and the EMI burst that you see when two charged capacitor banks are connected through a hard switch.

## Worked example: a hot-plug that overshoots

The same mathematics gives the most common real failure related to this question. A 12 V supply connects through a cable with 1 µH of inductance to a board with a 10 µF ceramic capacitor, initially at 0 V. The capacitor voltage is continuous, so it cannot jump to 12 V; instead the inductor and capacitor form a series resonance:

- Ringing frequency: $f=1/(2\pi\sqrt{1\ \mu\text{H}\times10\ \mu\text{F}})=50.3$ kHz.
- Characteristic impedance: $\sqrt{L/C}=0.316\ \Omega$, so the peak charging current is on the order of $12/0.316\approx38$ A without damping.
- Damping ratio with 0.1 Ω of total series resistance: $\zeta=\tfrac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}=0.158$. The overshoot above the final value is $e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}=0.60$.
- Peak capacitor voltage: $12\times(1+0.60)=19.3$ V, well over a 16 V rating, or a 12 V-rated downstream regulator.

With an undamped LC the capacitor voltage reaches twice the source, 24 V. The fix is to add damping (a resistor of at least $2\sqrt{L/C}=0.63\ \Omega$ for critical damping, or an electrolytic with high ESR in parallel), or to control the ramp with a hot-swap controller, or to place a TVS. The principle is the same as the rest of the page: capacitor voltage can't jump, so the energy has to move through the inductance, and the result can overshoot.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 0.1 Ω and 1 µH figures are illustrative, not typical values for any specific cable or supply. Measure your own setup, since both depend on cable length, the source impedance and contact resistance.

## Limiting cases: when does a voltage step look instant?

A step is "instant" only compared with the circuit's time constant. A 10 pF scope-probe tip driven from a 50 Ω source has $\tau = 50\ \Omega\times10\ \text{pF} = 0.5$ ns; on a 100 ns time scale it looks like a step, though at the 0.5 ns scale the voltage is still rising smoothly. The same capacitor in series with a megaohm has a time constant of 10 µs. So whether a capacitor "holds" depends on the question you ask. For a capacitor connected to a low-impedance source, $\tau$ is small and the voltage follows the source closely; for a capacitor across a high-impedance node, it holds the voltage for many time constants. Sample-and-hold circuits and dynamic memory exploit exactly this: with a very small leakage the capacitor keeps its voltage for milliseconds.

Energy shows the same pattern. Charging $C$ through any resistor from a fixed source $V$ draws $CV^2$ from the source, stores $\tfrac12CV^2$ and dissipates $\tfrac12CV^2$ in the resistor, regardless of $R$. For 1 µF at 5 V that is 25 µJ drawn, 12.5 µJ stored and 12.5 µJ lost. That is why charging a capacitor from a fixed supply is never better than 50 % efficient, and why adiabatic charging with a ramp or an inductor is used when the energy matters.

## Verifying on the bench

1. **RC step.** Build the 1 kΩ and 1 µF circuit with a function generator in square-wave mode at 50 Hz (so each 10 ms half-period is 10$\tau$, long enough to settle). Probe the capacitor with a scope: the voltage should rise smoothly with $v_C = 3.16$ V at 1 ms for a 5 V step. Probe the resistor (or use a current probe) to see the current jump to 5 mA.
2. **Charge sharing.** Charge one 10 µF capacitor to 5 V with a bench supply, remove the supply, and connect it to a second capacitor with a 1 Ω resistor in between. Measure both capacitor voltages with a DMM after settling; you should find 2.5 V on each, give or take the meter's loading and the capacitors' tolerance. Repeat with a short wire instead of a resistor and note the click, the spark, or the ringing on a fast scope.
3. **Hot-plug.** Plug the same board into a supply through a long cable and capture the rail with a single-shot scope and a short ground spring. Compare the ringing frequency with $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, then solve for the cable's inductance. This is a reliable way to measure an inductance you cannot easily calculate.

## Dual: inductors

| | Capacitor | Inductor |
|---|---|---|
| Continuous | voltage $v_C$ (charge $q$) | current $i_L$ (flux $\lambda$) |
| Can jump | current | voltage |
| At $0^+$ (zero initial state) | short | open |
| DC steady state | open | short |
| Energy | $\tfrac12Cv^2$ | $\tfrac12Li^2$ |

See [[q0043]] and [[q0044]] for the inductor versions, and [[q0027]] for the question as it was phrased in the inductor context.

::: applications
- **Inrush-current limiting** for bulk capacitors (NTC thermistors, soft-start, pre-charge resistors in EV contactors).
- **Power-on reset circuits** (RC delay: a reset pin follows the slowly rising capacitor voltage).
- **Edge-rate and RC delay analysis** in digital I/O and switch debouncing.
- **Decoupling and holdup**: ride-through during a brief load step or input dropout.
- **Sample-and-hold circuits**: the hold capacitor retains the sampled voltage.
- **Charge pumps and switched-capacitor converters**: charge sharing and its loss.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Ideal capacitor with finite current**; in reality ESR, ESL and leakage modify the terminal behaviour.
- **Lumped-element, finite-source impedance** circuits; ideal sources and zero-resistance loops can give impulses.
- The initial state is known ($t=0^-$ voltage). If not, determine it from the previous steady state.
- Applies to the *ideal-capacitor voltage*; the measured terminal voltage can step through ESR.
- In an interview, you can state the rule for capacitors and the dual for inductors in one breath: "capacitor voltage and inductor current are continuous".
:::

::: followups
### A 100 µF capacitor, initially 0 V, is connected to a 12 V source through 10 Ω. What are the initial current, final voltage and time to reach 90 %?
$i(0^+)=12/10=1.2$ A; final $v_C=12$ V; $\tau=RC=1$ ms; 90 % at $t=\tau\ln10=2.3$ ms.

### What if the capacitor starts at 4 V in the same circuit?
$i(0^+)=(12-4)/10=0.8$ A; $v_C(t)=12-8e^{-t/\tau}$; 90 % of the *swing* (i.e. 11.2 V) happens at $t=\tau\ln(8/0.8)=2.3$ ms again, since the time constant is the same.

### Why does the capacitor current jump but not the voltage?
$i=C\,dv/dt$: $i$ is proportional to the *slope* of $v$. Slope can change instantly (the curve just has a corner), but $v$ itself is the integral of the slope, so it stays continuous. The inductor's current is the integral of its voltage in the same way.

### How would you limit the inrush current into a 2200 µF bulk capacitor on a 24 V input to 1 A?
$R\ge24/1=24\ \Omega$ initially. Use an NTC thermistor or a resistor shorted out by a relay/MOSFET after charge, or a soft-start (a MOSFET with a slow gate ramp: $I=C\,dV/dt$, so for 1 A, $dV/dt=1/2200\ \mu=454$ V/s, about 53 ms to reach 24 V).

### Can the voltage across a capacitor ever change instantly in a real circuit?
Not in a lumped real circuit with finite resistance and inductance. In very fast events you may see an apparent step from ESR/ESL (terminal voltage), or from a distributed effect: an extremely fast edge couples through the capacitor to the other node (a short circuit at high frequency), but the voltage *across* the ideal capacitor is still continuous.
