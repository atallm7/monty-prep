::: basics
### In plain words
Ringing is the wiggle you see on a signal after a fast edge. The voltage overshoots its final value, swings back past it, and bounces a few times before settling. It happens when a small inductance and a small capacitance in the path pass energy back and forth, and there is too little resistance to soak that energy up.

### Picture it
Picture a car with worn-out shock absorbers. Hit a bump and the car bounces up and down several times. The spring stores energy and the car's mass keeps it moving, much like a capacitor and an inductor trading energy. Good shock absorbers remove the energy, so the bounce stops quickly. In a circuit, a resistor is the shock absorber.

### Words you need
- **Overshoot**: the signal going past its final value on the first swing.
- **Inductance and capacitance**: inductance stores energy in flowing current (like the car's moving mass); capacitance stores it as voltage (like the spring). Every wire, pin and via has a little of each, a few nH and a few pF.
- **Damping**: resistance that soaks up the energy so the wiggle dies out fast.
- **Ring frequency**: how fast the wiggle repeats, about $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$.
- **Series resistor / termination**: a resistor added near the driver to provide damping ([[q0714]]).

### The simplest example
A driver pushes a sharp edge through 5 nH of inductance into 5 pF of pin capacitance. The ring frequency is about 1 GHz, so the wiggle repeats every nanosecond.

With only 3 Ω of resistance in the path, an ideal step overshoots by about 86%. Add resistance until the total is 20 Ω and the overshoot drops to about 35%. A bit more resistance and the ringing nearly disappears. Slowing the edge also helps, because a slow edge does not excite the resonance much.

That is the whole idea: inductance and capacitance ring unless resistance damps them. The rest of this page covers the damping math, ringing on long traces caused by reflections, and a checklist of fixes.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can explain ringing as an **underdamped resonance** (energy swapping between an inductance and a capacitance with too little resistance to dissipate it) and recognise the two places it comes from: *lumped* parasitics (trace stub, probe ground lead, gate loop, switch node) and *distributed* reflections on a transmission line. A strong answer gives the damping ratio $\zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$, the ring frequency $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$, overshoot versus $\zeta$, and the fixes (add damping, series or termination resistors, shorten loops) with a number or two.
:::

::: why
Ringing is the most visible symptom of poor signal integrity: overshoot that stresses clamp diodes, ringback that causes false triggers ([[q0691]]), and radiated emissions at the ring frequency. It appears on logic edges, MOSFET gates and switching nodes, and as an artifact of a bad scope probe ground. Being able to estimate where it comes from and how much resistance kills it is a practical skill that links circuit theory (RLC response) to PCB layout and measurement.
:::

::: concepts
- **Series RLC step response**: damping ratio $\zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$ and natural frequency $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$. See [[q0040]].
- **Parasitic inductance and capacitance**: about 1 nH per mm of wire, pF of pin and pad capacitance. See [[q0041]].
- **Reflections on a line**: $\Gamma = (Z_L - Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$; mismatch creates distributed ringing. See [[q0711]].
- **Overshoot and ringback**: how ringing interacts with thresholds. See [[q0690]].
- **Termination**: resistors that damp or absorb the reflections. See [[q0714]].
:::

::: answer
"Ringing is the oscillation you see on a signal after a fast edge, where the voltage overshoots its final value and then decays back and forth around it. It happens when there's inductance and capacitance in the path, a resonant circuit, and not enough resistance to damp it, so it's underdamped.

It comes from two places. One is lumped parasitics: a trace stub or via with a few nanohenries of inductance driving a pin capacitance of a few picofarads, a scope probe's ground lead, a MOSFET gate loop. The ring frequency is $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ and it's damped by the series resistance: the damping ratio is $\frac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$. The other is distributed: on a transmission line with a mismatch between source, line and load impedances, reflections bounce back and forth, and a low-impedance driver into an open line makes the receiver voltage overshoot and ring with a period of four times the line delay.

Why it matters: overshoot can exceed the clamp or absolute-maximum rating, ringback can cross the receiver threshold and cause false triggering, and the ring frequency radiates as EMI.

To fix it, add damping: a series resistor near the driver, proper termination, or a snubber, and reduce the inductance with shorter loops and a solid return path. Slowing the edge also helps because it stops exciting the resonance."
:::

::: technical
**Lumped model.** A step through resistance $R$ and inductance $L$ into a capacitance $C$ (series RLC, output across $C$) has

$$\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad \zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}},\qquad Q = \frac{1}{2\zeta}$$

and for $\zeta < 1$ the step response is $v(t) = V\left[1 - e^{-\zeta\omega_0 t}\left(\cos\omega_d t + \frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\sin\omega_d t\right)\right]$, $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$. Peak overshoot is $e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$; the first-ring peak occurs at $t = \pi/\omega_d$; the envelope decays with time constant $1/(\zeta\omega_0) = 2L/R$, so settling to 2% takes roughly $4\cdot 2L/R = 8L/R$. Critical damping: $R_{crit} = 2\sqrt{L/C}$.

**Example.** $L = 5$ nH (via/stub), $C = 5$ pF (pin), driver $R = 3\ \Omega$: $f_0 = 1.01$ GHz, $\zeta = 0.047$, overshoot 86% for an ideal step, $R_{crit} = 63$ Ω. With 20 Ω of series $R$: $\zeta = 0.32$, overshoot 35%; at 40 Ω: $\zeta = 0.63$, overshoot 7.7%.

**Edge-rate dependence.** If the edge rise time is much longer than the ring period $T_0 = 1/f_0$, the edge doesn't excite the resonance much. For $t_r \gtrsim T_0$ ringing is largely suppressed.

**Distributed (transmission-line) ringing.** A driver with source resistance $R_s < Z_0$ into an open line: reflection coefficient at the source $\Gamma_s = (R_s - Z_0)/(R_s + Z_0) < 0$, at the load $\Gamma_L = +1$. The receiver waveform steps every $2t_d$ with alternating sign; the fundamental ring period is $4t_d$. A 5-inch FR4 trace ($t_d \approx 850$ ps) rings at about 294 MHz. Series termination so that $R_{drv} + R_s = Z_0$ eliminates the source reflection and the ring.

**Fixes.** Damping resistance (series, snubber RC, ferrite bead), proper termination, lower $L$ (shorter, wider, closer return), lower $C$, slower edges. For a probe: short ground spring.
:::

::: long
"Ringing is the oscillation that follows a fast edge: the voltage overshoots the final value, undershoots, and decays after a few cycles. Physically it's an inductor and a capacitor trading energy back and forth, with too little resistance to burn it off.

The simplest model is a series RLC driven by a step, output across the capacitor. The ring frequency is $1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$. With 5 nanohenries of via or stub and 5 picofarads of pin capacitance that's about 1 gigahertz. The damping ratio is $\zeta = \frac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$; the critical resistance is $2\sqrt{L/C}$, about 63 ohms here. With a 3 ohm driver, $\zeta$ is 0.047, which gives 86 percent overshoot on an ideal step. Add a 40 ohm series resistor and the overshoot drops to under 8 percent.

The other cause is reflections on a transmission line. If a low impedance driver drives an open-ended line, the source reflection coefficient is negative and the load's is plus one, so the receiver voltage jumps above the final value, then gets pulled back, and rings with a period of four times the line delay. A 5 inch FR4 trace is about 850 picoseconds, so a ring near 300 megahertz.

The effects are overshoot that can hit clamp diodes or absolute-max limits, ringback that can cross the threshold and trigger a receiver twice, and EMI at the ring frequency.

The fixes are to add damping or terminate: a series resistor near the driver sized so the driver plus resistor equals the line impedance, a snubber on a switching node, a gate resistor on a MOSFET. And reduce the inductance: shorter loops, a solid ground plane, short probe ground. Slowing the edge so its rise time is longer than the ring period also stops it exciting the resonance."
:::

::: terms
- **Ringing**: damped oscillation after a fast edge.
- **Overshoot / undershoot**: peak excursion beyond the final value on the first swing.
- **Damping ratio ($\zeta$)**: $\frac{R}{2}\sqrt{C/L}$; below 1 means underdamped.
- **Quality factor ($Q$)**: $1/(2\zeta)$; higher means a longer, stronger ring.
- **Critical damping**: $R = 2\sqrt{L/C}$; fastest settling with no overshoot.
- **Snubber**: a series RC across a node to add damping at the ring frequency.
- **Series termination**: a resistor at the driver making $R_{drv} + R_s = Z_0$.
- **Ring frequency**: $f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{LC})$ for lumped; $\approx 1/(4t_d)$ for a mismatched line.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "oscillation due to inductance and capacitance". An exceptional answer gives the **damping ratio and ring frequency** with numbers, shows overshoot versus $\zeta$ (e.g. $\zeta = 0.4$ gives 25%, $\zeta = 0.7$ gives 4.6%), distinguishes **lumped** from **transmission-line** ringing (ring period $\approx 4t_d$ for a low-impedance driver into an open end), and connects the symptom to a **sizable fix**: "$R_{crit} = 2\sqrt{L/C}$", "make $R_{drv}+R_s = Z_0$". It also mentions that the ring can be **in the probe** (a long ground lead rings with the probe tip capacitance) and that slowing the edge below the ring period hides, but doesn't cure, the resonance.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Treating ringing as only "noise".** It is a deterministic resonance; its frequency and damping predict its behaviour and its cure.
- **Adding capacitance to fix it.** In a series RLC a bigger $C$ raises $\zeta$ only as $\sqrt{C}$ while lowering $f_0$ (into more of the edge's spectrum) and slowing the edge; a capacitor added at another node can form a *new* resonance with the loop inductance. The reliable cure is resistance.
- **Using the wrong resistor value.** Too small does nothing; too large slows the edge. Aim near $R_{crit}$ for lumped LC, and $Z_0 - R_{drv}$ for series termination.
- **Blaming the circuit for probe ringing.** A 10 cm ground lead ($\approx 100$ nH) with 10 pF resonates at about 159 MHz; use a ground spring.
- **Assuming a slower edge is always the fix.** It reduces excitation but costs timing margin; and it doesn't remove the resonance, so a later disturbance can still ring.
- **Ignoring the return path.** Most of the loop inductance is often in the return; a plane gap or long return path raises $L$.
:::

## Where the L and C come from

- **Probe ground lead**: 10 cm of wire is about 100 nH; with 10 pF probe-tip capacitance, $f_0 = 159$ MHz and $\sqrt{L/C} = 100\ \Omega$ (so $R_{crit} = 200\ \Omega$). A 1 ns edge has a lot of energy there, so the probe rings. A ground spring (1–2 nH) moves $f_0$ above 1 GHz.
- **Gate drive loop**: package + trace inductance of a few nH with $C_{iss}$ of hundreds of pF; the gate resistor sets $\zeta$ ([[q0688]] covers ground bounce in the same loops).
- **Buck switch node**: parasitic loop inductance (a few nH) with the MOSFET's $C_{oss}$; with $L = 5$ nH and $C_{oss} = 500$ pF, $f_0 = 101$ MHz and $\sqrt{L/C} = 3.2\ \Omega$, so an RC snubber with $R \approx 3.2\ \Omega$ and $C_{snub} \ge 3C_{oss}$ is a common starting point.
- **Stub or via on a signal line**: nH-class inductance with pin capacitance, as in the example above.

## Distributed ringing on a transmission line

When the trace is long compared with the edge, treat it as a line with delay $t_d$. The load (CMOS input) is almost open and the driver's impedance $R_s$ sets $\Gamma_s$. With a strong driver ($R_s < Z_0$) the result is the voltage staircase from [[q0690]]: 5.5 V, 1.83 V, 4.28 V, 2.65 V, ... for a 10 Ω driver into 50 Ω with 3.3 V. The signs alternate every $2t_d$ so the oscillation period is $4t_d$.

**Series termination design.** Choose $R_{series} = Z_0 - R_{drv}$: for $Z_0 = 50\ \Omega$ and $R_{drv} = 10\ \Omega$, $R_{series} = 40\ \Omega$. The wave launched into the line is $V/2$, doubles at the open end to $V$, and the returning reflection is absorbed at the source, so only one step arrives.

## Worked examples with numbers

**MOSFET gate loop.** Take $L = 10$ nH of gate-loop inductance (driver pin, trace, via, package lead) and $C_{iss} = 1$ nF. The ring frequency is $1/(2\pi\sqrt{10\text{ nH}\cdot1\text{ nF}}) = 50.3$ MHz, and critical damping needs a total series resistance of $2\sqrt{L/C} = 6.3\ \Omega$. A driver with 2 Ω output resistance plus a 4.7 Ω gate resistor gives $R = 6.7\ \Omega$, so $\zeta = 1.06$: no overshoot, and the gate edge is still fast. If you chose $R_g = 1\ \Omega$ instead, $R = 3\ \Omega$ gives $\zeta = 0.47$ and about 18% overshoot on the gate, enough to threaten the gate-oxide rating or cause a false turn-on of the other FET in a half-bridge.

**RC snubber on a switching node.** Starting values: $R_{snub} \approx \sqrt{L/C_{oss}} = 3.2\ \Omega$ and $C_{snub} \ge 3C_{oss} = 1.5$ nF. The snubber dissipates about $C_{snub}V^2f_{sw}$ per switch: with 12 V and 500 kHz that is $1.5\text{ nF}\cdot144\cdot0.5\text{ MHz} = 0.11$ W, which is why snubbers are tuned rather than made large.

**Probe artifact check.** A 100 nH, 10 pF probe loop has $\zeta = (R/2)\sqrt{C/L}$; with a 50 Ω source (a typical test source) $\zeta = 25\cdot0.01 = 0.25$, so the probe rings by about 44% on an ideal step at 159 MHz even if the circuit under test is perfectly damped.

## Ringing in the frequency domain

A ring at $f_0$ is a narrow peak in the edge's spectrum, which is why it appears on a spectrum analyser or in a radiated-emissions scan as a spike near $f_0$ and its harmonics. The $Q = 1/(2\zeta)$ of the resonance sets the peak height: a ring with $\zeta = 0.05$ ($Q = 10$) has a response peak 20 dB above its low-frequency value at $f_0$ (about 26 dB above a critically damped response, whose resonance gain is $-6$ dB). This is why fixing a ring often fixes an EMC failure at the same time.

## Fixing ringing: the checklist

1. **Find the loop**: where does the current flow and return? Reduce its area.
2. **Add the right resistance**: series R near the driver ($Z_0 - R_{drv}$ for lines; $\approx R_{crit}$ for lumped LC; snubber $\approx\sqrt{L/C}$ with $C_{snub} \gtrsim 3C$).
3. **Don't add capacitance blindly**: it lowers $f_0$, slows the edge and can create a new resonance elsewhere.
4. **Slow the edge**: only if timing allows ([[q0689]]).
5. **Re-measure with a short ground spring**: to avoid fixing a probe artifact.

> [!TIP]
> If ringing frequency doesn't change when you move the probe ground but its amplitude does, it's real. If frequency changes with the ground lead length, you're looking at a probe artifact.
:::

::: applications
- **Logic edges on unterminated traces**: series resistors at drivers on clock and strobe nets.
- **MOSFET gate drive**: the gate resistor sets $\zeta$ of the gate loop ring; too low gives ringing and false turn-on.
- **Switching converters**: RC snubbers across the switch node, or slower gate drive, reduce 100+ MHz ringing that causes EMI.
- **Scope probing**: using spring-tip ground and short loops to avoid measurement ringing.
- **Cables and connectors**: ferrites and series terminations damp ringing from cable inductance and receiver capacitance.
- **Crystal and RF input stubs**: unintended stubs resonate; layout avoids them.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Linear series RLC** with a step input; real drivers are nonlinear and edges have finite rise time, both of which reduce the ring below the ideal-step numbers.
- **Constant $L$ and $C$**; pin and junction capacitances vary with voltage, and clamp diodes cut overshoot beyond $V_{DD}$ + 0.7 V (making the observed overshoot lower but clamp currents high).
- **Lossless line** for the distributed case; real FR4 loss shortens the ring train at high frequency.
- **Single resonance**; real boards have several (multiple stubs, connectors, planes), and the dominant ring depends on the edge spectrum.
- **FR4 delay 170 ps/inch** is typical, not exact.
:::

::: followups
### How would you choose a series resistor to stop ringing on a 4-inch trace driven by a 15 Ω output?
Assuming $Z_0 = 50\ \Omega$, $R_{series} \approx 50 - 15 = 35\ \Omega$ (next standard 33 Ω), placed within a few millimetres of the driver. I'd check at the receiver that the edge is a single step and look at the drive impedance across process and temperature, since a driver can vary by ±30%.

### Does adding capacitance at the receiver cure ringing?
Not reliably. In the simple series RLC, $\zeta = \frac R2\sqrt{C/L}$ does grow with $C$, so a bigger load capacitance reduces the overshoot, but it also lowers $f_0$, slows the edge and stores more energy. A capacitor added elsewhere (say an extra decoupling cap) forms a new LC resonance with whatever inductance it sees. Capacitance doesn't dissipate energy; resistance does, so I'd add a resistor (series, termination or a snubber RC) and fix the loop inductance.

### What is the ring frequency of a 5 nH, 500 pF switch-node loop, and what snubber would you start with?
$f_0 = 1/(2\pi\sqrt{5\text{ nH}\cdot500\text{ pF}}) = 101$ MHz; $\sqrt{L/C} = 3.2\ \Omega$. I'd start with an RC snubber of 3.3 Ω and 1.5–2 nF (3–4x $C_{oss}$) across the lower switch, then adjust on the bench looking at power dissipation $C_{snub}V^2f$.

### Overshoot goes up when I reduce the series resistor, but timing improves. What's the trade-off?
The resistor trades edge rate for damping. Lower $R$ gives lower $\zeta$ and faster edges but more overshoot and ringback. I'd pick the highest $R$ whose edge still meets the timing budget with margin, which is usually the one giving $\zeta$ of about 0.6–0.8.

### How would you tell ringing from the DUT apart from ringing caused by the probe?
Change the ground lead length, or probe with a different method (ground spring, active probe). If the ring frequency shifts, or the ring disappears when the lead is shortened, it's the probe loop. If it persists, I compare with a non-invasive check such as a different probe point or a TDR on the net.
