::: basics
### In plain words
A decoupling capacitor is a small capacitor, often a 100 nF ceramic, connected between a chip's supply pin and ground, as close to the pin as possible. A chip draws current in sudden bursts lasting nanoseconds, one at every clock tick. The regulator that makes the supply voltage is too far away and too slow to answer. The capacitor sits right there and supplies each burst locally, so the voltage stays steady.

### Picture it
Imagine working at a desk with a glass of water beside you. When you are thirsty, you sip at once. If the only water were a tap down the hall, every sip would cost a trip. The capacitor is the glass, the regulator is the far-away tap, and the wiring between them is the hallway. The glass is refilled slowly between sips. The analogy breaks in one place: the "hallway" does not just take time, it resists sudden changes in current through its inductance, and that is the real problem.

### Words you need
- **Capacitor**: a part that stores charge and gives it back when the voltage starts to dip ([[q0022]]).
- **Inductance**: resistance to sudden changes in current. A supply trace has about 1 nH per millimeter without a nearby plane.
- **Loop inductance**: the inductance of the capacitor-to-pin-and-back loop. Short loops make decoupling work ([[q0503]]).
- **Bypass, decoupling and bulk**: names for the same kind of part doing different jobs ([[q0032]]).
- **Current step**: a sudden jump in the current a chip draws.

### The simplest example
A chip's current steps up by 100 mA in 2 ns, which is 50 mA per ns. The supply path has 20 nH of inductance. Without a nearby capacitor, the pin dips by $L\,di/dt = 20\ \text{nH} \times 0.05\ \text{A/ns} = 1.0$ V, a collapse on a 3.3 V supply. With a 100 nF capacitor at the pin, a 5 ns burst of 100 mA takes only $I\,t/C = 0.1 \times 5\ \text{ns}/100\ \text{nF} = 5$ mV out of it. That is the whole idea. The rest of this page covers why placement and loop inductance matter as much as the value, the capacitor's different roles, and which devices need one.
:::

::: testing
Whether you understand **why** a tiny capacitor sits at every supply pin, rather than just that "you always put 100 nF there". The interviewer is listening for:

1. The physical problem: digital and analog circuits draw current in fast bursts, but the supply is far away and has inductance and a slow control loop.
2. The role of the capacitor as a **local charge reservoir** and a **low-impedance path** at high frequency.
3. A numerical feel: how much droop a given capacitor allows.
4. A criterion for *when* it is needed, which is almost always, and an honest note about the exceptions ([[q0513]]).
:::

::: why
Decoupling is the most common single component group on any board, and the first thing a reviewer looks for. Missing or badly placed decoupling causes random resets, ADC noise, jitter and failed EMC tests that no firmware fix can cure. In an interview this is a gateway question: if you answer it with charge, inductance and numbers, the interviewer will move on to placement, values and parasitics ([[q0502]]-[[q0512]]). If you only say "it filters noise", they will dig until you show the physics.
:::

::: concepts
- **Capacitor as charge storage**: $Q = CV$, so a capacitor holds a voltage while supplying current for a short time ([[q0022]]).
- **Impedance of a capacitor**: $Z = 1/(j\omega C)$, which falls with frequency, giving a low-impedance path to ground for fast edges ([[q0024]]).
- **Inductance of the supply path**: any wire or trace has $L\approx$ 1 nH/mm without a plane, so $v = L\,di/dt$ limits what a distant source can deliver ([[q0030]]).
- **Real capacitor**: ESR and ESL limit the useful frequency range ([[q0031]]).
- **Bypass vs decoupling vs bulk**: the same part wears different names for different jobs ([[q0032]], [[q0143]]).
- **Transient current demand**: digital edges and clock-synchronous switching create current spikes every cycle ([[q0140]]).
:::

::: answer
"A decoupling capacitor is a small capacitor, usually a ceramic around 100 nanofarads, connected between a chip's supply pin and ground, as close to the pin as possible. It does two jobs: it's a local reservoir of charge, and it's a low-impedance path for high-frequency current.

The reason is that chips draw current in short bursts, every clock edge, in nanoseconds. The regulator is centimetres away, so between it and the chip there's trace and via inductance, and the regulator's control loop only responds in microseconds. Without a nearby capacitor, those fast current steps would have to come through that inductance, and v equals L di/dt would make the supply pin dip, ring, and inject noise back onto the shared rail. With the capacitor right at the pin, the burst current comes from it, the loop it flows through is tiny, and the rail only sees a smooth, slow draw.

When's it needed? Basically every IC with a supply pin: digital logic, MCUs, FPGAs, op-amps, ADCs, switching regulators on their inputs. The need rises with edge speed and current step size. The only real exceptions are cases where an extra capacitor would hurt, like a part that specifies a maximum load capacitance or stability needs a specific ESR, and I'd check the datasheet for those."
:::

::: technical
**Problem statement.** A CMOS load switches a current step $\Delta I$ in time $t_r$. The supply path between regulator and IC has inductance $L_{path}$ and resistance $R_{path}$. Without local capacitance the pin voltage dips by

$$\Delta V \approx L_{path}\frac{\Delta I}{t_r} + R_{path}\Delta I$$

For $L_{path}=20$ nH (about 2 cm of trace with no good return), $\Delta I = 100$ mA and $t_r = 2$ ns: $\Delta V = 20\text{ nH}\times 0.05\text{ A/ns} = 1.0$ V. Larger than most logic tolerances.

**Role of the capacitor.** A capacitor $C$ at the pin supplies the charge locally. For a burst of duration $t$ at current $I$ with no replenishment,

$$\Delta V \approx \frac{I\,t}{C}$$

100 mA for 5 ns from 100 nF: $\Delta V = 0.1\times 5\times10^{-9}/10^{-7} = 5$ mV. The capacitor-to-pin loop inductance $L_{loop}$ still matters; for 1 nH the same 100 mA step in 2 ns is 50 mV, which is why placement matters ([[q0503]]).

**Frequency view.** The supply-pin impedance should stay below a *target impedance* $Z_{target}=\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I$ from DC up to the frequency where on-die and package capacitance take over. The decoupling capacitors, with their ESL, set that impedance in the 100 kHz–100 MHz range.

**When needed.** Any device where $\Delta I/t_r$ times the path inductance would exceed the allowed supply ripple, which is every digital IC, most ADCs/DACs, op-amps (for stability and PSRR), clocks/PLLs, RF stages, and switching-regulator inputs. Rule of thumb: one 100 nF (ceramic, X7R, 0402/0603) per supply pin, plus a bulk capacitor per rail or per IC group.

**Typical hierarchy:** on-die (nF) → package → 10–100 nF ceramic at pin → 1–10 µF ceramic nearby → 47–470 µF bulk at regulator/entry.
:::

::: long
"A decoupling capacitor is a small capacitor, typically a hundred nanofarad ceramic, between a chip's supply pin and ground, mounted as close to the pin as I can get it.

The reason comes down to speed. A digital chip pulls current in bursts that last nanoseconds, every clock edge. The regulator that feeds it is centimetres away, so between them there's a trace, vias, and a return path, all with inductance, about a nanohenry per millimetre if there's no plane nearby. And the regulator's control loop reacts in microseconds, thousands of times too slow. If the chip asks for 100 milliamps in 2 nanoseconds through 20 nanohenries, V equals L di/dt is a full volt of dip. That's the supply collapsing under the chip, and the glitches on the rail are shared with every other chip on it.

The capacitor fixes that by being a local reservoir. The burst comes from the capacitor through a very short loop, so the inductance in series is a nanohenry or less. How much droop? Delta V equals I t over C, so 100 milliamps for 5 nanoseconds out of 100 nanofarads is 5 millivolts. The regulator then refills the capacitor slowly through the longer path, which is a smooth current, not a spike. The same capacitor also acts as a short to ground for high-frequency noise coming along the rail, which is why it's also called a bypass cap.

It's needed on basically every IC supply pin: digital logic, MCUs, FPGAs, op-amps and ADCs, and on the input of switching regulators. The faster the edges and the bigger the current step, the more it matters. Exceptions are rare and come from datasheet limits, like a part that needs a specific ESR or has a maximum load capacitance. And a single capacitor isn't the whole story: I'd add a bulk capacitor for slower load changes and mix values for broader frequency coverage."
:::

::: terms
- **Decoupling (bypass) capacitor**: a small capacitor between a supply pin and ground that supplies local fast current and shunts high-frequency noise.
- **Bulk capacitor**: a large capacitor (tens to hundreds of µF) for slower load changes ([[q0143]]).
- **Transient current**: the fast current step drawn when logic switches.
- **Loop inductance**: the inductance of the physical loop from capacitor through pin and ground return; sets how quickly the capacitor can respond.
- **Target impedance**: $Z_{target}=\Delta V_{allowed}/\Delta I$, the maximum impedance the supply network may show.
- **Droop**: the dip in supply voltage when load current steps.
- **ESR / ESL**: equivalent series resistance and inductance of a real capacitor.
- **PSRR**: power-supply rejection ratio, how well an analog part ignores supply noise.
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "it filters supply noise" and gives 100 nF. An exceptional one:

- Starts from the *current demand*: nanosecond bursts vs a regulator that responds in microseconds.
- Shows the failure *quantitatively*: $L\,di/dt$ gives 1 V on a 20 nH path, while $It/C$ gives 5 mV from 100 nF.
- Explains that the loop inductance between cap and pin, not just the capacitance, decides how well it works.
- Distinguishes the roles (local reservoir, bypass of noise, isolation between ICs on a shared rail).
- Gives the hierarchy (on-die, pin cap, mid-value, bulk) and acknowledges exceptions from datasheets.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"It filters noise" and nothing else.** The primary job is supplying current for fast transients; filtering is a side effect.
- **Choosing the value only by capacitance.** A 1 µF cap on a long trace is worse than 100 nF placed right at the pin.
- **Thinking one capacitor on the board is enough.** Each IC needs its own local capacitor; distance destroys the benefit ([[q0504]]).
- **Using the schematic as the layout.** The schematic shows a cap on the rail; the layout decides its inductance.
- **Forgetting DC-bias derating.** A 100 nF X5R in a small package can lose half its capacitance at rated voltage.
:::

::: deep
## The problem decoupling solves

Digital circuits do not draw steady current. Each clock edge, thousands of gates switch, and each switching event briefly connects the supply to ground through the charge and discharge of internal capacitances. At the chip level that is a current waveform made of spikes with nanosecond or sub-nanosecond edges, repeated every clock cycle.

The supply that feeds it has two limitations:

1. **Inductance.** A path of length $\ell$ has roughly $L \approx 1$ nH/mm if there is no nearby return plane, and perhaps 0.3 nH/mm when it runs over one. A 20 mm path is 6–20 nH.
2. **Bandwidth.** A switching regulator's control loop responds in 10–100 µs; an LDO in hundreds of ns to a few µs. Neither can follow a nanosecond event.

## Quantifying the problem

Without a capacitor, the chip sees the supply through $L_{path}$:

$$\Delta V = L_{path}\frac{di}{dt}$$

For $L_{path} = 20$ nH, a 100 mA step in 2 ns ($di/dt = 50$ mA/ns):

$$\Delta V = 20\times10^{-9}\times 5\times10^{7} = 1.0\ \text{V}$$

On a 3.3 V rail that is a 30 % collapse, enough to reset logic or miscompare an analog threshold.

With a capacitor at the pin, the load current is supplied from $C$, and the capacitor's voltage changes only as charge leaves it:

$$\Delta V_C = \frac{I\,t}{C}$$

A 100 nF capacitor covers a 5 ns burst with 5 mV droop but a 500 ns burst needs about 1 µF for 50 mV. Slower demand is the job of larger capacitors further away, and eventually the regulator.

This is the simplest justification for a hierarchy: different capacitances cover different time scales.

## It is not only the value: the loop matters

The capacitor supplies current through its own loop: out of the capacitor, through the supply pin, through the die, back out the ground pin and to the capacitor's ground terminal. That loop has inductance $L_{loop}$, and the burst current causes a drop $L_{loop}\,di/dt$ in series. The actual droop is roughly

$$\Delta V \approx \frac{I\,t}{C} + L_{loop}\frac{di}{dt}$$

With 1 nH of loop inductance and the same 50 mA/ns step, the second term is 50 mV, ten times the capacitive droop of 5 mV. For a 100 nF capacitor the inductive term usually dominates. That is why *placement and routing* matter more than the exact capacitor value ([[q0503]], [[q0504]]).

## Roles of the same part

| Role | What the capacitor does |
|---|---|
| Local reservoir | Supplies the burst so the rail does not droop |
| Bypass | Provides a low-impedance path for high-frequency noise on the rail to ground |
| Decoupling | Isolates one IC's switching current from others on the shared supply |
| Bulk | Larger capacitance for slower load steps, at the regulator or entry |

In practice the same physical capacitor does all of the first three. See [[q0032]] for a comparison with bulk capacitors.

## Which devices need it, and how much

- **Digital ICs (MCU, FPGA, memory, logic)**: always. One 100 nF per supply pin pair is the minimum; high-speed FPGAs/SoCs use a designed network of many values ([[q0506]]).
- **Op-amps and analog parts**: supply bypassing preserves stability and PSRR; typical 100 nF plus a few µF per supply. See [[q0505]].
- **ADC/DAC, references, PLLs**: sensitive to supply noise, often decouple separately and filter with a ferrite or RC.
- **Switching-regulator input**: a ceramic capacitor next to the input pins is mandatory to supply the pulsed input current.
- **Wireless modules and power amplifiers**: both RF-frequency bypass values (pF) and bulk for transmit bursts.

## What happens on a real board

On a scope, a missing capacitor shows as a supply rail with spikes at every clock edge, with ringing set by $L_{path}$ and the chip's own capacitance. Adding the capacitor reduces the spike, but if it is placed far away the loop inductance still limits it and the anti-resonance with the chip's capacitance sits lower in frequency. A common bench check is to probe the supply pin to ground with a spring-tip ground and compare ripple before and after.

> [!TIP]
> If asked for a number on the spot: "100 nF per power pin, 10 µF per IC or group, bulk at the regulator, with the smallest closest to the pin."

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The 1 nH/mm and 20 nH path inductance figures are estimates for an isolated trace; over a ground plane they are several times smaller. Capacitance values quoted are typical practice, not requirements: the datasheet for each device is the authority.
:::

::: applications
- **MCU/FPGA boards**: 100 nF at each VDD pin, plus bulk per rail.
- **Op-amp circuits**: 100 nF plus 1–10 µF at each supply pin to keep the amplifier stable and reject supply noise.
- **ADC and sensor front ends**: separate analog supply with local capacitors and, often, a ferrite bead.
- **Buck/boost regulators**: input ceramic capacitors at the input pins to supply the pulsed current.
- **Memory (DDR) and processors**: carefully designed arrays of values and sizes to meet a target impedance.
:::

::: assumptions
- A digital-style load that draws fast current steps is assumed. For purely linear, low-bandwidth loads, the capacitance requirement is lower, but oscillation and PSRR concerns still apply.
- The inductance numbers assume a trace with a modest return path; over a nearby plane they are lower.
- Capacitance quoted is nominal; DC bias and temperature derating can cut ceramic capacitance by half or more.
- Another valid answer defines decoupling by its function, isolating a circuit from the shared supply, and bypassing by its function, giving noise a path around a circuit; many engineers use the terms interchangeably.
:::

::: followups
### How much capacitance does a given chip need?
From the burst: $C \ge I\,t/\Delta V$ for the first ~5–50 ns, and then more mid-value and bulk capacitance for longer bursts, or from a target impedance $Z_{target}=\Delta V/\Delta I$ over frequency. In practice most MCUs are fine with 100 nF per pin, while FPGAs and processors need a calculated or vendor-recommended network.

### Why 100 nF specifically?
It is a convenient compromise: small enough in a 0402/0603 package to have a self-resonance around 15–25 MHz (ESL ~0.5-1 nH), large enough to supply the first tens of ns of a burst, and cheap. It's a convention, not a law: some parts want 10 nF or 1 µF at particular pins.

### What if the capacitor is 5 cm away?
The loop inductance rises to tens of nH, so the capacitor can't respond to nanosecond edges. The rail still sees the full $L\,di/dt$ spike at the pin, and the trace inductance forms an anti-resonance with the chip's own capacitance. It still helps slower loads. See [[q0504]] and [[q0508]].

### Why not rely on the regulator's output capacitor?
It is far from the chip, and the path inductance between them blocks fast transients. The regulator's capacitor handles load changes in the microsecond range; local decoupling handles the nanosecond range.

### Scenario: an MCU resets when its GPIOs toggle together. What do you check?
Supply and ground at the MCU pins with a short-lead probe: is there a dip when the GPIOs switch? I'd check whether the 100 nF caps are at the pins with short vias, whether there is a ground plane under them, and whether the GPIOs have a series resistor or slower drive setting. I'd also check brown-out settings. More capacitors help only if they are low inductance.
:::
