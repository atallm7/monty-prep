::: basics
### In plain words
Inside a chip, logic gates are built from transistors, which act as tiny switches controlled by a voltage. A CMOS gate uses two teams of switches. One team can connect the output to the supply voltage (a 1). The other can connect it to ground (a 0). For any input, exactly one team is on, so the gate wastes almost no power when nothing is changing.

### Picture it
Imagine a bucket that holds the output level. A valve on a pipe from a high tank can fill it (a 1), and a valve on a drain can empty it (a 0). The design rule is that the two are never both open and never both closed.

### Words you need
- **NMOS**: a transistor switch that closes when its input is 1. It connects the output to ground.
- **PMOS**: a transistor switch that closes when its input is 0. It connects the output to the supply.
- **Supply and ground**: the supply voltage is the "1" level, and ground (0 V) is the "0" level ([[q0105]]).
- **In series / in parallel**: switches in a line, where all must close to conduct, versus side by side, where any one is enough.

### The simplest example
Inverter: one PMOS to the supply and one NMOS to ground, sharing the input. Input 0: the PMOS closes, the NMOS opens, so the output is 1. Input 1 reverses it, and the output is 0.

NAND: two NMOS in series to ground, two PMOS in parallel to the supply. With A = B = 1, both NMOS close, so the output is 0. With A = 0 and B = 1, A's PMOS closes and the NMOS chain is broken, so the output is 1.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this page shows NOR, how AND is a NAND plus an inverter, the general series/parallel rule, and why designers prefer NAND over NOR.
:::

::: testing
Whether you can **go from a Boolean function to a transistor schematic**, not just recite "NMOS down, PMOS up". Specifically: the inverter as a complementary pair, the pull-up/pull-down duality (series NMOS pairs with parallel PMOS, and vice versa), why the natural CMOS gates are **inverting** (NOT, NAND, NOR), why an AND is a NAND plus an inverter, and the performance consequences (series stacks are slow, so NAND is preferred over NOR). Bonus points for sizing the transistors and for noting there is never a DC path from supply to ground.
:::

::: why
This is how every digital chip is actually built. Knowing the transistor structure of a gate explains delay (why a 4-input NOR is awful), power (why CMOS draws almost nothing when idle, and why $CV^2f$ dominates when switching), noise margin, and why standard-cell libraries are full of NAND/NOR/AOI cells and not ANDs and ORs. It also links the interview's "digital" and "analog" halves: the same MOSFET knowledge from the semiconductor section ([[q0105]], [[q0093]]) now builds logic. A candidate who can draw a NAND and justify the arrangement with a short argument shows real understanding of both worlds.
:::

::: concepts
- **NMOS and PMOS as switches**: an NMOS conducts when its gate is high (passes a good 0); a PMOS conducts when its gate is low (passes a good 1). See [[q0105]].
- **Complementary pull-up and pull-down networks**: PMOS network from $V_{DD}$ to output, NMOS network from output to ground; exactly one conducts for any input.
- **Duality**: where the NMOS network has transistors in series, the PMOS network has them in parallel, and vice versa.
- **Inverting logic**: because a pull-down turns on with a high input, the output goes *down* when inputs go *up*. Every single-stage CMOS gate is inverting ([[q0889]] for symbols).
- **Shoot-through and static power**: no DC path in steady state; a brief current spike flows when both devices are partially on during a transition ([[q0109]]).
- **Sizing and logical effort**: series transistors must be widened to keep drive strength; PMOS is widened ~2-3x for mobility.
:::

::: answer
"The building block is the CMOS inverter: one PMOS from VDD to the output and one NMOS from the output to ground, with both gates tied together as the input. Input low turns the PMOS on and the NMOS off, so the output is pulled up to VDD. Input high does the opposite and pulls the output to ground. One of them is always off, so in steady state there's no path from VDD to ground and basically no static current.

For a NAND, I extend that. Two NMOS in series for the pull-down, so the output only goes low when both A and B are high, and two PMOS in parallel for the pull-up, so the output goes high if either input is low. That's the NAND truth table. A NOR is the dual: NMOS in parallel, PMOS in series.

The general rule is that the pull-down network implements the *complement* of the function with AND as series and OR as parallel, and the pull-up is its dual. That's why CMOS gates are naturally inverting, and why an AND gate is really a NAND followed by an inverter, six transistors.

In practice I'd mention sizing: series transistors are weaker, so you make them wider, and the PMOS is wider than the NMOS because holes are slower. That's also why NAND is preferred over NOR in a library: a NOR has series PMOS, which is the slow type."
:::

::: technical
**Inverter.** PMOS source at $V_{DD}$, NMOS source at ground, drains tied (output), gates tied (input). $V_{in}=0$: PMOS on ($V_{SG}=V_{DD}$), NMOS off, $V_{out}=V_{DD}$. $V_{in}=V_{DD}$: reversed, $V_{out}=0$. Only the output node's capacitance charges/discharges; no DC current. Dynamic power $P=\alpha C V_{DD}^2 f$.

**General static-CMOS gate.** For output $Y=F(A,B,\ldots)$:
1. The **pull-down network** (NMOS, output to ground) conducts when $Y$ must be 0, so it implements $\bar F$: AND-ed terms are NMOS in **series**, OR-ed terms are NMOS in **parallel**.
2. The **pull-up network** (PMOS, $V_{DD}$ to output) is the **dual**: swap series and parallel.
3. For every input combination exactly one network conducts (no floating output, no DC short).

| Gate | Pull-down (NMOS) | Pull-up (PMOS) | Transistors |
|---|---|---|---|
| NOT | one NMOS | one PMOS | 2 |
| NAND2 | 2 in series | 2 in parallel | 4 |
| NOR2 | 2 in parallel | 2 in series | 4 |
| AND2 | NAND2 + NOT | | 6 |
| OR2 | NOR2 + NOT | | 6 |
| NANDn / NORn | $n$ series or parallel | dual | $2n$ |
| AOI21 $=\overline{AB+C}$ | $(A\text{ series }B)\parallel C$ | $(A\parallel B)$ series $C$ | 6 |

**Why non-inverting is two stages.** A single stage can only make the output fall when inputs rise (monotonic decreasing), since the pull-down needs high gate voltage. A non-inverting function needs two inversions.

**Sizing (rule of thumb).** If an inverter uses $W_n=1$, $W_p\approx2$ (mobility ratio $\mu_n/\mu_p\approx2$-3). For equal drive to that inverter: NAND2 uses $W_n=2$ (series) and $W_p=2$; NOR2 uses $W_n=1$ and $W_p=4$. Logical effort (input capacitance relative to an inverter, same drive): NAND2 $=4/3$, NOR2 $=5/3$; NAND3 $=5/3$, NOR3 $=7/3$. So NOR gates cost more area and delay for the same drive.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> Exact $W_p/W_n$ ratios and the transistor count of XOR/XNOR depend on process and topology: a static complementary XOR is usually quoted at roughly 10-12 transistors, while pass-gate versions are smaller (around 6-8) but give weaker levels. Treat these as typical, not universal.
:::

::: long
"I'll start with the CMOS inverter because every gate is built on the same idea. A PMOS sits between VDD and the output, an NMOS between the output and ground, and the gates are tied together as the input. If the input is low, the PMOS is on and the NMOS is off, so the output is connected to VDD. If the input is high, the NMOS is on and pulls the output to ground. Only one device conducts at a time, so there's no DC path from supply to ground, which is why CMOS idle power is so small. Power only shows up when the output switches, roughly alpha C V squared f.

For a two-input NAND, I want the output to go low only when both inputs are high. 'Both' means series, so two NMOS stacked between the output and ground. The pull-up must do the complement: pull the output high if either input is low, which is two PMOS in parallel. Four transistors total. For a NOR it's the dual: the NMOS are in parallel because either input high should pull the output low, and the PMOS are in series because the output only goes high when both inputs are low.

So the general recipe: write the complement of your function, make AND into series NMOS and OR into parallel NMOS for the pull-down, then take the dual for the PMOS pull-up. That's also why everything is inverting. A single stage can't be non-inverting, so AND is a NAND plus an inverter, six transistors.

Sizing matters in real designs. Series devices have higher resistance, so you widen them to match the drive of a single transistor. PMOS has lower mobility than NMOS so it's about twice as wide. A NOR puts the slow PMOS in series, so it needs a lot of area, which is why libraries lean on NAND gates and why wide NORs are avoided."
:::

::: terms
- **Pull-up network (PUN)**: PMOS devices between $V_{DD}$ and the output; conducts when the output should be 1.
- **Pull-down network (PDN)**: NMOS devices between the output and ground; conducts when the output should be 0.
- **Complementary (static) CMOS**: logic style where the PUN and PDN are duals and exactly one conducts in steady state.
- **Series / parallel duality**: AND of conditions becomes series devices, OR becomes parallel; the other network swaps them.
- **Inverting stage**: a single CMOS stage always produces an inverted function (NOT, NAND, NOR, AOI, OAI).
- **Logical effort**: relative input capacitance of a gate versus an inverter delivering the same drive; NAND2 $=4/3$, NOR2 $=5/3$.
- **Dynamic power**: $P=\alpha C V_{DD}^2 f$, from charging and discharging node capacitance.
- **Shoot-through (short-circuit) current**: brief current when both PMOS and NMOS conduct during an input transition.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** draws an inverter, maybe a NAND with the transistors in a plausible pattern, and says "NMOS on the bottom, PMOS on top".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Draws the PMOS network on top and NMOS network below, gates labeled A and B, with the **output node** clearly marked and **junction dots** where wires join.
- States the rule (series = AND in the pull-down, parallel = OR, PUN is the dual) and *verifies the NAND by walking through the four input cases*.
- Explains **why** CMOS is inverting and why AND/OR need an extra inverter (6 transistors instead of 4).
- Mentions **sizing** (series stacks widened, PMOS ~2-3x NMOS) and why NAND beats NOR for speed.
- Notes **no static current** and that dynamic power is $\alpha CV^2 f$, tying digital back to analog device behavior.
:::

::: mistakes
- **Putting NMOS in the pull-up.** An NMOS passes only a degraded 1 ($V_{DD}-V_{th}$), so the rule is PMOS to $V_{DD}$, NMOS to ground. Swap them and the gate has poor levels and static current.
- **Using the same topology (series) in both networks.** If both are series for a NAND, the output floats for some inputs and shorts for none. The networks must be **dual**.
- **Drawing the NAND with the PMOS in series.** That is a NOR. Check by input: for NAND, one input low must be enough to pull the output high.
- **Believing a gate "has no power".** There is no *static* current, but dynamic power ($\alpha CV^2f$) and brief shoot-through during transitions are real, and leakage matters in modern processes.
- **Claiming AND is a single 4-transistor stage.** A single stage inverts, so a 2-input AND needs the NAND plus an inverter (6 transistors).
- **Forgetting the body connections.** NMOS bodies go to ground and PMOS bodies to $V_{DD}$, usually implicit in the symbol (see [[q0091]]).
:::

::: deep
## Start from the switch picture

Treat each MOSFET as a voltage-controlled switch for digital purposes:

- An **NMOS** is closed when its gate is at logic 1, open at logic 0. It is good at connecting the output to **ground** (a strong 0).
- A **PMOS** is closed when its gate is at logic 0, open at logic 1. It is good at connecting the output to **$V_{DD}$** (a strong 1).

If you swapped roles (NMOS to $V_{DD}$), the output could only rise to $V_{DD}-V_{th}$ and the weak high would leave the next stage partially on. That asymmetry is why the pull-up is PMOS and the pull-down is NMOS. For the real device behavior (regions, threshold) see [[q0105]] and the CMOS inverter sim below.

Figure: The CMOS inverter. The PMOS (top) connects Y to V_DD when A is low; the NMOS (bottom) connects Y to ground when A is high. Both gates share the input A and both drains share the output Y.

Walk through the two states, because the interviewer will ask:

| A | PMOS | NMOS | Y |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | on | off | $V_{DD}$ (1) |
| 1 | off | on | 0 |

No DC path exists in either row, so quiescent current is (ideally) zero. During the transition there is a brief interval where both are on, giving the shoot-through current ([[q0109]]). The energy per cycle charging and discharging the load is $C V_{DD}^2$, so power is $\alpha C V_{DD}^2 f$ ([[q0110]]). Example: 10 fF at 1 V and 1 GHz, toggling every cycle ($\alpha=1$): $10\times10^{-15}\times1^2\times10^9=10\ \mu$W per node; with a realistic $\alpha\approx0.1$ it's 1 µW.

## NAND2

Requirement: $Y=\overline{AB}$. $Y=0$ only when $A=B=1$.

- Pull-down: "A **and** B" means both NMOS must conduct: **series**.
- Pull-up: the complement. $Y=1$ whenever $A=0$ **or** $B=0$: either PMOS conducting suffices, so **parallel**.

Check all four input cases:

| A | B | PMOS network | NMOS network | Y |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | both on (parallel) | open | 1 |
| 0 | 1 | $P_A$ on | open ($N_A$ off) | 1 |
| 1 | 0 | $P_B$ on | open ($N_B$ off) | 1 |
| 1 | 1 | both off | closed (series) | 0 |

Exactly one network conducts in each row. That is the verification worth saying aloud.

## NOR2

Requirement: $Y=\overline{A+B}$. $Y=1$ only when both are 0.

- Pull-down: "A **or** B pulls low": **parallel** NMOS.
- Pull-up: "A **and** B both low pulls high": **series** PMOS.

The same four-row check works: $A=B=0$ turns both PMOS on and both NMOS off, so $Y=1$. Any 1 on an input turns on a parallel NMOS and breaks the PMOS series chain, so $Y=0$.

## AND, OR and everything else

A single CMOS stage is **inverting**, because the pull-down turns on when an input rises. So non-inverting gates use two stages:

- **AND2** = NAND2 + inverter = $4+2=6$ transistors.
- **OR2** = NOR2 + inverter = 6 transistors.
- **Buffer** = two inverters = 4 transistors (and better drive).

More general gates follow from the same recipe. Example: $Y=\overline{AB+C}$ (an AND-OR-INVERT, AOI21). Pull-down: $AB$ is series, "+C" is parallel with it: $(N_A\text{ series }N_B)\parallel N_C$. Pull-up is the dual: $(P_A\parallel P_B)$ in series with $P_C$. Six transistors, one stage, and it implements AND + OR + NOT in a single gate, which is why standard-cell libraries are rich in AOI/OAI cells.

**XOR** has no single-stage series/parallel form, because it is not a unate function (the output rises on some input changes and falls on others). It is usually built as NAND/NOR/inverter combinations or with transmission gates.

## Sizing: why NAND beats NOR

Series transistors add resistance. To match the drive of a unit inverter ($W_n=1$, $W_p=2$ with a mobility ratio of about 2):

| Gate | NMOS width | PMOS width | Total input cap per pin (relative) | Logical effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inverter | 1 | 2 | 3 | 1 |
| NAND2 | 2 | 2 | 4 | 4/3 |
| NOR2 | 1 | 4 | 5 | 5/3 |

The NOR puts the **PMOS** (the weaker device) in series and must widen it 4x, so every NOR input is a bigger load on whatever drives it, and a NOR3 or NOR4 is worse still. So designers prefer NAND-based logic and limit fan-in (typically to 3 or 4 inputs) in a single stage, building wider functions as trees. See [[q0892]] for the universal-gate angle.

## Real hardware notes

- **Layout**: a standard cell stacks PMOS in an n-well on top and NMOS below, with polysilicon gate columns shared by a PMOS/NMOS pair, so the schematic's vertical alignment of A on P_A/N_A reflects the physical layout.
- **Bodies**: NMOS bodies tie to ground and PMOS bodies to $V_{DD}$ (not drawn in simple schematics), see [[q0091]].
- **Noise margin and levels**: the output swings full rail, with $V_{OH}\approx V_{DD}$ and $V_{OL}\approx0$, unlike TTL ([[q0108]]).
- **Input protection and ESD** diodes sit on pads, not inside internal gates.

> [!INTERVIEW]
> After drawing a NAND, say out loud: "A and B both high, series NMOS conduct, output low; either low, a parallel PMOS conducts, output high." The two-sentence check proves you did not just memorize the picture.
:::

::: applications
- **Standard-cell libraries** (ASIC/FPGA hard logic): INV, NAND2/3, NOR2/3, AOI21, OAI21 form the core cells. Everything else is composed of them.
- **74HC/74HCT-family logic ICs** (for example a 74HC00 quad NAND): each gate is the static CMOS structure shown above.
- **Processor and memory design**: SRAM bit cells use cross-coupled inverters ([[q0883]]); decoders and adders are built from NAND/NOR/AOI cells.
- **Low-power design**: understanding $\alpha CV^2f$ guides voltage scaling and clock gating.
- **Failure analysis**: a stuck-at-1 on a NAND output points to the pull-down stack or a short in the pull-up.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Static complementary CMOS** logic: the most common style. Dynamic logic, pass-transistor logic and domino logic have different structures.
- **Long-channel switch model**: transistors treated as ideal on/off switches. In reality they are in saturation/triode regions with finite resistance, and short-channel effects change the sizing ratios.
- **Mobility ratio** of 2 is a rule of thumb (2-3 in older processes; modern FinFET processes use discrete fins so ratios differ).
- The question says "NOT/AND/etc": I drew NOT, NAND and NOR from transistors and derived AND, OR and AOI from them. An interviewer who wants XOR will accept a gate-level composition or a transmission-gate design.
- Body effect, leakage and ESD structures are ignored in the schematic.
:::

::: followups
### Why can't you build an AND gate with a single stage of CMOS?
Each stage is inverting: the pull-down needs a high gate to conduct, so raising an input can only lower the output. A non-inverting function needs two inversions, so AND = NAND + inverter (6 transistors). A single stage can implement any *negative-unate* function.

### What happens if you build a NAND with the NMOS in parallel and the PMOS in series?
You get a NOR, since the wiring now implements $\overline{A+B}$. Mixed topology (series in both networks) fails differently: for some inputs neither network conducts (output floats), so the networks must be duals.

### How would you size a 3-input NOR to match an inverter's drive?
NMOS: all three in parallel at $W_n=1$. PMOS: three in series, each 3x the inverter's PMOS, so $W_p=6$ (inverter $W_p=2$). Logical effort $=(1+6)/3=7/3\approx2.33$. This is why 3- and 4-input NORs are avoided.

### Draw an XOR from NANDs.
Four NAND2 gates: $N_1=\overline{AB}$; $N_2=\overline{A\,N_1}$; $N_3=\overline{B\,N_1}$; $Y=\overline{N_2N_3}$. Check $A=1,B=0$: $N_1=1$, $N_2=0$, $N_3=1$, $Y=1$. $A=B=1$: $N_1=0$, $N_2=1$, $N_3=1$, $Y=0$. That's $4\times4=16$ transistors, which is why dedicated XOR cells exist.

### Why does a CMOS gate still draw current when it switches slowly?
Short-circuit current: with an input ramp slower than the output can follow, both PMOS and NMOS conduct simultaneously around $V_{DD}/2$. It scales with input transition time, which is why slow edges into CMOS inputs (like an RC-filtered button) should go through a Schmitt-trigger input. See [[q0109]].

### Harder: your NAND3 is much slower on one input than the others. Why?
The series NMOS stack is asymmetric. The input nearest the output sees only the stack's last device discharging the output node, while inputs lower in the stack have to discharge the internal nodes of the stack too. Place the latest-arriving signal on the input closest to the output to minimize delay. Internal-node capacitance also gives data-dependent delay.
:::
