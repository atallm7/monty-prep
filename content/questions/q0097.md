::: testing
Whether you understand that "N-channel vs P-channel" is a statement about **which carrier forms the channel**, and that everything else (polarity of the voltages, which rail it likes to sit on, why it is bigger) follows from that. The interviewer is listening for:

1. The doping story: NMOS = p-type body, n+ source/drain, electrons in the channel; PMOS = the mirror image with holes.
2. The sign conventions: NMOS turns on for $V_{GS} > V_t$ (positive), PMOS for $V_{SG} > |V_{tp}|$ (gate *below* source).
3. The practical consequence: holes are slower than electrons, so a PMOS must be 2-3x wider for the same current, which is why NMOS is the "default" switch and PMOS is used when you need a high-side switch with simple gate drive.
:::

::: why
Every digital chip is built from complementary pairs of these two devices, and every power stage has to choose between them for the high side. If you can explain the difference from the physics ("electrons vs holes, so mobility, so size, so cost, so which one I pick") you can answer a whole family of follow-ups on CMOS ([[q0105]]), high-side switching ([[q0107]]), and load switches without memorizing a table. Interviewers use this as a quick check that you know *why* the table looks the way it does.
:::

::: concepts
- **Majority carriers**: in n-type silicon the mobile carriers are electrons, in p-type they are holes. The channel of an NMOS is a thin *inverted* layer of electrons in p-type silicon; the PMOS channel is a layer of holes in n-type silicon. See [[q0096]].
- **Mobility**: electrons move about 2-3 times more easily than holes in silicon's inversion layers, so for the same geometry NMOS conducts more current than PMOS.
- **Threshold voltage sign**: $V_{tn} > 0$ and $V_{tp} < 0$ for enhancement devices. Everything for the PMOS is the NMOS equations with every voltage and current flipped. See [[q0098]].
- **Source is defined by polarity**: the NMOS source is the *lower* terminal; the PMOS source is the *higher* one. Source is whichever terminal sits at the carrier's origin.
- **Symbol and body connection**: arrow on the body/source pointing in for NMOS, out for PMOS. See [[q0089]], [[q0090]].
- **Body tie**: NMOS body goes to the lowest rail, PMOS body to the highest. See [[q0091]].
:::

::: answer
"The difference is the type of carrier that forms the channel. An N-channel MOSFET sits in a p-type body with n+ source and drain, and when I put a positive voltage on the gate it pulls electrons to the surface and inverts a thin layer into n-type. That is the channel. A P-channel device is the mirror image: n-type body, p+ source and drain, and a negative gate-to-source voltage pulls holes up to form the channel.

So the voltages flip. An NMOS turns on when the gate is higher than the source by more than $V_t$, around 1 V, and its source usually sits at ground. A PMOS turns on when the gate is lower than the source by more than $|V_t|$, and its source usually sits at the supply.

The other practical difference is mobility. Electrons are about two to three times more mobile than holes, so for the same W/L an NMOS has about 2.5 times the current and a lower on-resistance. To match them you make the PMOS wider, which costs area and gate capacitance. That's why you see NMOS used wherever you have a choice, and PMOS where it makes the gate drive simple, like a high-side load switch or the pull-up half of a CMOS inverter."
:::

::: technical
**Structure.**
- NMOS: p-type substrate/body, n+ source and drain, gate positive. Channel = inversion layer of **electrons**.
- PMOS: n-type body (an n-well in a standard CMOS process), p+ source and drain, gate negative. Channel = inversion layer of **holes**.

**On-condition and square-law currents** (strong inversion, long channel):

| | NMOS | PMOS |
|---|---|---|
| Turn-on | $V_{GS} > V_{tn} > 0$ | $V_{SG} > \lvert V_{tp}\rvert$, i.e. $V_{GS} < V_{tp} < 0$ |
| Triode | $V_{DS} < V_{GS}-V_{tn}$ | $V_{SD} < V_{SG}-\lvert V_{tp}\rvert$ |
| Saturation | $I_D = \tfrac12 k_n (V_{GS}-V_{tn})^2$ | $I_D = \tfrac12 k_p (V_{SG}-\lvert V_{tp}\rvert)^2$ |
| $k$ | $k_n = \mu_n C_{ox} W/L$ | $k_p = \mu_p C_{ox} W/L$ |
| Current direction | drain to source | source to drain |
| Source normally at | lowest potential (GND) | highest potential ($V_{DD}$) |
| Body tied to | lowest potential | highest potential |

**Mobility ratio.** $\mu_n/\mu_p \approx 2$-$3$ for typical processes (bulk silicon is about 1350 vs 480 cm²/V·s, about 2.8; the inversion-layer ratio is somewhat lower and process dependent). With the same $W/L$ and $V_{ov}=2$ V, taking $k_n' = 200$ µA/V² and $k_p' = 80$ µA/V²: $I_{Dn} = \tfrac12(200\,\mu)(10)(4) = 4$ mA but $I_{Dp} = 1.6$ mA, and the triode on-resistances $1/(k'(W/L)V_{ov})$ are 250 Ω vs 625 Ω. To equalize drive, $W_p/W_n \approx k_n'/k_p' = 2.5$.

**Consequences.** Equal-drive PMOS costs about 2.5x the area and gate capacitance; this is why NMOS-only logic (NMOS, then depletion-load NMOS) existed before CMOS, and why discrete power MOSFETs favour N-channel (lower $R_{DS(on)}$ per die area). PMOS survives where a high-side switch must be driven from logic referenced to the source rail.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The exact $\mu_n/\mu_p$ and $k'$ values depend on the process, gate-oxide thickness, strain engineering and channel length. In deep-submicron processes velocity saturation and strained silicon pull the ratio toward 1.5-2. Treat 2-3 as the textbook figure, not a datasheet value.
:::

::: short
"The difference is the carrier in the channel. An N-channel FET has an electron channel in a p-type body, turns on with the gate above the source by more than Vt, and its source usually sits at ground. A P-channel FET is the mirror image: hole channel, gate must go below the source, source usually at the supply. Because holes are about 2 to 3 times less mobile than electrons, a PMOS needs to be 2 to 3 times wider to match an NMOS, so NMOS is the default and PMOS is used where it simplifies high-side drive or for the pull-up in CMOS."
:::

::: long
"At the physics level, the difference is which carrier makes the channel. An NMOS is built in a p-type body with n+ source and drain regions. When the gate goes above the source by more than the threshold, the oxide field repels holes and attracts electrons, and the surface inverts into a thin n-type channel that connects source to drain. A PMOS is the exact complement: n-type body, p+ source and drain, and you need the gate below the source by more than the magnitude of the threshold to pull holes in.

That flips all the polarities. For the NMOS, current flows drain to source, I think of its source as the lower terminal and it normally sits at ground, with the body tied there too. For the PMOS, current flows source to drain, the source is the higher terminal and sits at the supply. The saturation equation is the same shape for both, one half k times the overdrive squared, just with $V_{SG}$ and $V_{SD}$ in place of $V_{GS}$ and $V_{DS}$.

The big practical difference is mobility. Electrons are roughly two to three times more mobile than holes, so with the same W/L an NMOS passes about 2.5 times the current and has about 2.5 times lower on-resistance. To match drive strength you widen the PMOS, which costs area and input capacitance. In a CMOS inverter that's why the PMOS is sized about 2 to 3 times the NMOS so the rise and fall times match.

So the rule of thumb: when I'm free to choose, NMOS, because it's smaller and cheaper for the same resistance. I pick a PMOS when I need a simple high-side switch, like a load switch on a battery rail where I can't easily get a gate voltage above the supply, or as the pull-up half of a complementary pair."
:::

::: terms
- **N-channel (NMOS)**: MOSFET whose channel is an inversion layer of electrons; turns on with $V_{GS}>V_{tn}>0$.
- **P-channel (PMOS)**: MOSFET whose channel is an inversion layer of holes; turns on with $V_{SG}>|V_{tp}|$.
- **Inversion layer**: a thin region at the silicon-oxide interface whose carrier type is opposite to the body's, created by the gate field.
- **Mobility ($\mu$)**: how fast carriers drift per unit electric field; sets $k' = \mu C_{ox}$.
- **Overdrive voltage ($V_{ov}$)**: $|V_{GS}|-|V_t|$, the voltage above threshold that sets channel charge and current.
- **n-well**: the n-type pocket in a p-substrate CMOS process in which PMOS devices are built.
- **Complementary (CMOS)**: using an NMOS and PMOS together so one is always off in steady state. See [[q0105]].
- **High-side / low-side switch**: switch between the load and the supply / between the load and ground. See [[q0107]].
:::

::: exceptional
An average answer says "N-channel uses electrons, P-channel uses holes, they are opposite polarity." An exceptional answer:

- Goes straight from carrier type to **doping of body/source/drain** and to the sign of the gate voltage that inverts the channel.
- States the on-condition for each in terms of source reference ($V_{GS}>V_t$ vs $V_{SG}>|V_t|$) and says *the source is the lower terminal for NMOS and the upper terminal for PMOS*.
- Quantifies the mobility penalty (about 2-3x) and turns it into a design consequence: PMOS width, area, gate capacitance, higher $R_{DS(on)}$ per die.
- Names a case where PMOS wins (simple high-side switch, reverse-battery protection, pull-up in CMOS) and a case where NMOS wins (power low-side, synchronous rectifier, anything cost- or efficiency-driven).
- Mentions body connections and the n-well so the answer ties to real silicon.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"NMOS is positive and PMOS is negative" with no reference.** The sign statements are about $V_{GS}$ relative to the *source*. A PMOS with its gate at 3 V is fully on if its source is at 5 V. State the voltage as $V_{SG}$.
- **Mixing up carrier flow and current direction.** In a PMOS the carriers are holes, and they move source to drain, which is the same direction as conventional current. In an NMOS the carriers are electrons moving source to drain, so conventional current flows drain to source. Always draw conventional current when writing equations.
- **Using a PMOS with a swapped source/drain.** In a switch that can see reverse voltage, the body diode ([[q0101]]) conducts the other way; always check which terminal is source.
- **Saying PMOS is "worse" without a reason.** It is worse in $R_{DS(on)}$ per area because of mobility, not because of a different operating principle. The equations are identical in form.
- **Forgetting the body connection.** NMOS body to the lowest rail, PMOS body to the highest. Floating or mis-tied bodies change $V_t$ (body effect) and can forward-bias junctions. See [[q0091]].
- **Believing the mobility ratio is exactly 2 or 3.** It is a process-dependent number; the platform answer is "roughly 2-3x, so size the PMOS accordingly and simulate".
:::

::: deep
## Start from the silicon

A MOSFET is a capacitor (gate / oxide / body) that can create its own conductor. Apply the right gate voltage and the silicon surface under the oxide flips its character. What flips depends on the body.

**NMOS.** The body is p-type, so holes are the majority carriers. A positive gate voltage pushes holes away from the surface, leaving a depletion region of fixed acceptor ions. Past the threshold voltage the field becomes strong enough to pull *minority* electrons (supplied by the n+ source) to the surface. That thin electron layer is the channel. It is n-type material in a p-type body, bounded by the n+ source and n+ drain.

**PMOS.** Flip every doping label. The body is n-type, the source and drain are p+, and a gate voltage lower than the source by $|V_{tp}|$ attracts holes to the surface.

## The equations are the same equations

In strong inversion, long-channel, with $\mu$ the inversion-layer mobility and $C_{ox}=\varepsilon_{ox}/t_{ox}$:

$$I_D=\tfrac12\,\mu C_{ox}\frac{W}{L}\,(V_{ov})^2\,(1+\lambda V_{DS})\ \text{(saturation)},\qquad R_{on}\approx\frac{1}{\mu C_{ox}\frac{W}{L}V_{ov}}\ \text{(deep triode)}$$

For the PMOS, replace $\mu_n$ with $\mu_p$ and write all voltages as source-referred magnitudes: $V_{ov}=V_{SG}-|V_{tp}|$, $V_{SD}$. That is the whole difference in the maths.

## Why the mobility gap matters in design

Mobility is a property of the carrier in the lattice: electrons in silicon have a lighter effective conduction mass and scatter less than holes in the valence band. With $R_{on}\propto 1/(\mu W/L)$, the penalty is about 2-3x in $R_{on}$ at equal area, or 2-3x in area at equal $R_{on}$.

- **Discrete power parts.** For a given die size and voltage rating, N-channel parts have much lower $R_{DS(on)}$ and lower gate charge, and cost less. PMOS parts exist, but you pay in resistance or in area. That's why high-current buck converters use NMOS on both sides, with a bootstrap/charge-pump to drive the high-side gate (see [[q0107]]).
- **CMOS inverters.** The pull-up PMOS is drawn about 2-3x wider than the pull-down NMOS so the rise and fall times (and the switching threshold at $V_{DD}/2$) match. Without that, tpLH and tpHL differ and the switching point is skewed.
- **Gate capacitance.** A 2.5x wider PMOS presents 2.5x more input capacitance. That is a cost paid by whatever drives the gate, in delay and dynamic power.

## Worked example: the same load switch two ways

Load: 1 A from a 5 V rail, gate driven by a 3.3 V logic pin. Assume $|V_t|=1$ V.

- **PMOS on the high side.** Source at 5 V, so the gate must be at least 1 V below that: $V_G\le 4$ V. Pull the gate to ground and $V_{SG}=5$ V, solidly on. To turn it off the gate must go to 5 V, but a 3.3 V logic pin can only reach 3.3 V, leaving $V_{SG}=1.7$ V, which is still *on*. So we need an NMOS pull-down plus a pull-up resistor to 5 V, or an open-drain driver. One extra transistor and a resistor, but no voltage above the rail is needed.
- **NMOS on the high side.** Source follows the load at about 5 V when on. To get $V_{GS}>V_t$ the gate must reach about 6-10 V, above the rail. This needs a charge pump or bootstrap, more parts, more design effort.
- **NMOS on the low side.** Source at ground, $V_G=3.3$ V gives $V_{GS}=3.3$ V, well on, and 0 V turns it off. Simplest, but the load's ground is now switched, which is not acceptable for many loads with shared grounds.

So the "why PMOS" answer is: convenience of the drive, not performance.

## Real hardware notes

- **Body connections.** In a standard CMOS process all NMOS share the p-substrate (tied to ground) while PMOS sit in n-wells (tied to $V_{DD}$ or their own source). Discrete power MOSFETs have the body tied to the source internally, giving the body diode ([[q0101]]).
- **Threshold magnitude is not always symmetric.** A process may give $V_{tn}=0.45$ V and $V_{tp}=-0.5$ V; both are set by implants and are tunable.
- **Leakage.** Subthreshold and gate leakage differ between N and P devices; high-$V_t$ and low-$V_t$ variants of each are offered in modern libraries.
- **Reverse-polarity protection.** A PMOS in the positive supply path, gate grounded, acts as a nearly lossless ideal diode: it turns on when the battery is the right way round and its body diode blocks otherwise (see [[q0079]]).
:::

::: applications
- **CMOS logic**: every gate pairs a PMOS pull-up network and an NMOS pull-down network ([[q0105]]).
- **High-side load switch on a battery rail**: a PMOS with a pull-up resistor and an NMOS (or open-drain GPIO) to pull its gate down, such as for power-gating a sensor.
- **Reverse-polarity protection** in the supply path: PMOS with gate to ground is an ideal-diode replacement with tens of mΩ instead of a 0.5 V diode drop.
- **Synchronous buck converter low side**: an NMOS replaces the freewheel diode; the high-side is NMOS with a bootstrap driver or PMOS for simplicity at low power ([[q0107]]).
- **Analog switches / transmission gates**: an NMOS and PMOS in parallel pass full-rail signals because each covers the range where the other loses overdrive.
- **Level shifters**: the single-NMOS bidirectional shifter is a classic example of a body-diode-assisted device (see [[q0104]]).
:::

::: assumptions
- **Enhancement-mode, strong inversion, long-channel** equations are used; short-channel velocity saturation reduces the dependence to roughly linear in $V_{ov}$ and shrinks the NMOS/PMOS gap. See [[q0098]] for depletion devices.
- **Mobility ratio of 2-3** is a textbook number. In scaled CMOS with strained silicon, hole mobility is boosted and the ratio can be about 1.5-2.
- **"Source" is defined by voltage polarity** in a symmetric device. In a discrete part, the body is tied to the source, so the labelled source/drain is fixed.
- The answer changes for **other technologies**: in GaN HEMTs and SiC MOSFETs, P-channel versions are not practical, so complementary gate drive is done with N-channel parts only.
:::

::: followups
### Which is faster in a CMOS inverter, the NMOS pull-down or the PMOS pull-up?
For equal W/L, the NMOS, because of the higher electron mobility: the falling edge is faster than the rising edge by about the mobility ratio. Sizing $W_p\approx 2$-$3\,W_n$ equalizes them and puts the switching threshold near $V_{DD}/2$. See [[q0105]].

### With $k_n'=200$ µA/V² and $k_p'=80$ µA/V², what W/L ratio makes a PMOS match an NMOS with W/L = 4?
Matching drive means $k_p'(W/L)_p = k_n'(W/L)_n$, so $(W/L)_p = 4\times 200/80 = 10$. The PMOS is 2.5x wider, which costs 2.5x the gate capacitance.

### Why is a PMOS often used for the high-side switch on a low-voltage rail?
Its source sits at the supply, so turning it on just means pulling the gate towards ground, which any logic output can do. An NMOS on the high side needs a gate voltage *above* the supply to stay on, so it needs a charge pump or bootstrap ([[q0107]]).

### What happens if the PMOS body is tied to ground instead of its source?
In a standard CMOS process the PMOS sits in an n-well, and the well must be at the highest potential. Tied to ground, the p+ source (at $V_{DD}$) to n-well (at 0 V) junction would be forward biased by the full supply and would conduct heavily. In a discrete part the equivalent mistake is wiring the device so the body diode conducts in the direction you wanted it to block.

### Harder: could you build a CMOS-like complementary pair with only N-channel devices, and why would you?
Yes, with two NMOS (high-side with a bootstrapped/charge-pumped gate drive and low-side), as in synchronous bucks and half bridges. You do it for lower $R_{DS(on)}$ and cost at high current, or because no good PMOS exists in the technology (GaN, SiC). The price is the floating gate drive and the shoot-through management that comes with it ([[q0109]]).

### Scenario: a PMOS high-side switch refuses to turn off with a 3.3 V GPIO driving its gate from a 12 V rail. What is wrong?
The GPIO only pulls the gate to 3.3 V, so $V_{SG}=12-3.3=8.7$ V, still far above $|V_t|$. The PMOS stays on. Fix it with a level-shifting NMOS: GPIO drives the NMOS gate, the NMOS pulls the PMOS gate to ground to turn on, and a pull-up resistor to 12 V turns the PMOS off (a Zener or resistor divider keeps $V_{GS}$ below its rating).
:::
