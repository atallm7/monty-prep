::: testing
Whether you can give a **loss-by-loss** answer rather than "yes it's more efficient" or "no it's less". A strong answer:

1. Splits losses into conduction ($I^2R$), switching, gate-drive/controller (fixed) and magnetics (DCR plus core/AC).
2. Says which scale with $1/N$, which are constant and which grow with $N$.
3. States what you hold fixed in the comparison: same per-phase parts or same total silicon.
4. Describes phase shedding and why efficiency vs load is a family of curves, not one number.
:::

::: why
Efficiency sets heat, battery life and cooling cost. At 1 V and 60 A the load is 60 W and each percent of efficiency is 0.6 W of heat to remove. Interviewers ask this to see whether you will just repeat the marketing claim. Real answers are nuanced: full-load efficiency can improve, light-load efficiency can get worse, and the cure (shedding) has its own consequences for ripple and transients.
:::

::: concepts
- **Conduction loss**: $I^2R_{eff}$ in FETs and inductor DCR ([[q0217]]).
- **Switching loss**: $\tfrac12 V_{in}I\,(t_r+t_f)\,f_{sw}$ per transition pair, proportional to the phase current.
- **Gate-drive loss**: $Q_gV_{dr}f_{sw}$ per phase, independent of load.
- **Inductor losses**: copper (DCR) plus core loss, which grows with ripple amplitude and frequency.
- **Fixed overhead**: controller and driver quiescent current.
- **Phase shedding**: turning phases off at light load to cut fixed losses ([[q0230]]).
:::

::: answer
"It depends which loss you look at, and what you compare against. Conduction loss is I squared R. If I add phases and keep each phase's FETs and inductor the same, the total conduction loss falls like one over N, because each phase carries 1/N of the current and there are N of them: N times (I/N) squared is I squared over N. So at high load efficiency goes up. But if I compare with a single phase built from the same total silicon, conduction loss is basically the same.

Switching loss in total is about the same, since each phase switches 1/N of the current at the same frequency. Gate-drive loss and driver and core loss go up with the number of active phases, because they don't depend on load. At light load that fixed overhead dominates, so a four-phase converter can be less efficient at 5 amps than a single phase. The fix is phase shedding: the controller turns off phases as load drops so the efficiency stays high across the range.

There are also second-order wins: lower ripple per phase can mean lower AC losses in the inductors, and you can choose smaller faster FETs because each carries less current.

So in short: better at heavy load, worse at light load unless you shed phases, with the exact answer depending on parts."
:::

::: technical
**Per-phase loss model** (phase current $I_{ph}=I_{out}/N$):

$$P_{ph}=I_{ph}^2R_{eff}+\tfrac12V_{in}I_{ph}(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}+Q_gV_{dr}f_{sw}+P_{core}+P_{ctrl}/N$$

with $R_{eff}=D R_{HS}+(1-D)R_{LS}+R_{DCR}$.

**Totals** for $N$ active phases:

- Conduction: $N I_{ph}^2R_{eff}=I_{out}^2R_{eff}/N$.
- Switching: $N\cdot\tfrac12V_{in}(I_{out}/N)(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}$, independent of $N$.
- Gate drive and core: $\propto N$.

**Worked example.** 12 V to 1 V, $D=1/12$, $R_{HS}=3$ mΩ, $R_{LS}=1$ mΩ, DCR 0.25 mΩ gives $R_{eff}=1.417$ mΩ per phase. Transition time $t_r+t_f=20$ ns, $f_{sw}=500$ kHz, gate-drive 50 mW per phase.

| Load, phases | Conduction | Switching | Gate | Total loss | Efficiency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60 A, 4 | 1.28 W | 3.60 W | 0.20 W | 5.08 W | 92.2% |
| 60 A, 1 (same parts) | 5.10 W | 3.60 W | 0.05 W | 8.75 W | 87.3% |
| 5 A, 4 | 0.01 W | 0.30 W | 0.20 W | 0.51 W | 90.8% |
| 5 A, 1 (shed) | 0.04 W | 0.30 W | 0.05 W | 0.39 W | 92.8% |

The single-phase 60 A row uses one phase's parts to carry the whole load and is not a realistic design. A fair "same silicon" comparison would give about 1.28 W of conduction loss for one phase too, and then the multiphase cost is the extra gate drive. The 4 vs 1 rows at 5 A are the real lesson: shedding recovers about 2 points.

> [!UNCERTAIN]
> The transition times, gate-drive power and resistances above are illustrative. Real values depend on the FETs, driver, layout and temperature; use datasheet numbers.
:::

::: short
"Multiphase efficiency depends on the loss. Conduction loss is $I^2R$: with the same per-phase parts it falls as 1 over N, but for the same total silicon it is unchanged. Switching loss in total is about the same. Gate drive, driver quiescent and core loss grow with the number of phases, so at light load a multiphase buck is less efficient unless it sheds phases. So it's better at high load and needs phase shedding to stay efficient at low load."
:::

::: long
"I'd go loss by loss.

Conduction loss first: I squared R in the FETs and in the inductor DCR. With N phases, each carries one N-th of the current, so each loses I over N squared times R, and the total is I squared R over N. That's if every phase uses the same FETs as the single-phase version. If I'm splitting the same total silicon among the phases, each phase's resistance is N times bigger and the total is the same as before. So the conduction advantage is partly just that you've added more silicon.

Switching loss: each phase is switching one N-th of the current, at the same per-phase frequency, so the total switching loss is the same as one phase carrying the full current. It scales with load current and frequency, not with N.

Then the fixed overhead: gate drive, driver quiescent current, controller, and inductor core loss. These don't scale with load and they scale with how many phases are on. So at light load, say 5 amps in a 60 amp design, four phases pay four times the gate-drive and core loss for almost no conduction benefit. Efficiency can be a couple of points worse than a single phase.

The standard answer is phase shedding: as load drops, the controller turns off phases, so only as many as are needed are switching. You get a family of efficiency curves, one per phase count, and the envelope is the efficiency you get with shedding. The caveats are that ripple and transient response change when you shed, so the controller has to bring phases back quickly on a load step, and the transitions have to be smooth.

Overall: multiphase can improve full-load efficiency, especially if you can choose smaller faster parts per phase, but its real efficiency story is about the whole load range."
:::

::: terms
- **Conduction loss**: $I^2R$ dissipated while a switch or winding carries current.
- **Switching loss**: energy lost in the voltage-current overlap at each transition.
- **Gate-drive loss**: $Q_gV_{dr}f_{sw}$, the energy to charge and discharge the gate each cycle.
- **Core loss**: magnetic hysteresis and eddy-current loss in the inductor core.
- **Fixed (quiescent) loss**: loss independent of the load.
- **Phase shedding**: disabling phases at light load.
- **Efficiency envelope**: the best efficiency across phase counts at each load.
- **Same-silicon comparison**: holding total FET area constant.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "multiphase is more efficient because current is split".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Derives total conduction loss $I^2R/N$ and notes the assumption (same per-phase parts).
- Notes that the same total silicon gives no conduction benefit.
- Says switching loss total is unchanged but gate drive and core loss scale with $N$.
- Describes the light-load penalty and the phase-shedding fix, with approximate numbers.
- Mentions the second-order effects: lower ripple per phase, ability to optimize FETs, thermal benefit (lower $R_{DS(on)}$ at lower temperature).
- Warns that shedding has transient and ripple consequences.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"More phases always means higher efficiency."** At light load fixed losses dominate and more phases hurt.
- **Forgetting what's held constant.** Equal parts vs equal silicon give opposite conclusions on conduction loss.
- **Believing switching loss divides by N.** Per phase it does, but the total does not.
- **Ignoring inductor core loss.** It grows with ripple and frequency and with number of inductors.
- **Treating one efficiency number as the answer.** Efficiency is a curve over load, input voltage and temperature.
- **Overlooking the thermal feedback.** Lower temperature reduces $R_{DS(on)}$ and DCR, improving conduction loss further.
:::

::: deep
## Loss by loss

### Conduction
For each phase $P_c=I_{ph}^2R_{eff}$. With $I_{ph}=I_{out}/N$:

$$P_{c,total}=N\left(\frac{I_{out}}{N}\right)^2R_{eff}=\frac{I_{out}^2R_{eff}}{N}$$

This assumes each phase has the same $R_{eff}$ as the single-phase version. Equivalent view: $N$ resistors in parallel have resistance $R/N$. If instead you split the same die area, each phase has $NR$ and the parallel combination is $R$ again, so no change.

Temperature helps too: lower loss per package means a lower junction temperature, and $R_{DS(on)}$ and copper resistance both have positive temperature coefficients, so the benefit is slightly larger than the ideal $1/N$ ([[q0242]]).

### Switching
$P_{sw}=\tfrac12V_{in}I\,(t_r+t_f)f_{sw}$ per transition pair. Each phase has $I_{ph}=I_{out}/N$ and there are $N$ of them, so the total is unchanged: $0.5\cdot12\cdot60\cdot20\text{ ns}\cdot500\text{ kHz}=3.6$ W for the example. This is often the largest term in a 12 V to 1 V converter, which is why switching frequency and edge speed matter so much. A single-phase converter at 2 MHz (to get the same ripple frequency) would have four times this loss; interleaving gets the 2 MHz ripple at 500 kHz switching loss.

### Gate drive, driver and controller
$P_g=Q_gV_{dr}f_{sw}$ per phase. For $Q_g=20$ nC, 5 V and 500 kHz this is 50 mW per phase. It is small at full load but at 5 A it is 40% of the whole loss budget with four phases.

### Magnetics
Copper loss is included in $R_{eff}$. Core loss depends on the flux swing, set by ripple current per phase, and on frequency. Lower ripple current per inductor, with the same $L$, means lower flux swing. With $N$ inductors you pay for $N$ cores.

## Phase shedding in practice

- The controller measures load current (or a proxy) and adds or drops phases with hysteresis to avoid chatter.
- On a load step it re-enables phases immediately, often all at once, so the transient is not delayed by the lower phase count.
- Ripple and loop gain change with $N$, so compensation must tolerate the range, or be scheduled.
- Below about one phase's lowest efficient point, converters move to DCM/pulse-skipping or PFM ([[q0230]]).

## Putting numbers on the trade

From the table in the technical section: 4 phases at 60 A gives 92.2% (5.08 W lost). At 5 A, 4 phases gives 90.8% and shedding to 1 phase gives 92.8%. Averaged over a real processor load profile that spends most of its time at light load, the shedding behavior can matter more to total energy than the full-load figure.
:::

::: applications
- **Server and desktop VRMs**: phase shedding profiles are tuned to the CPU's power states.
- **Laptop SoC rails**: dynamic phase count to extend battery life at idle.
- **GPU boards**: phases off in idle, all phases on for gaming loads.
- **Telecom point-of-load modules** designed for peak efficiency at a typical 40 to 60% load.
- **Digital controller telemetry**: efficiency vs phase count can be measured on the bench by forcing the number of phases.
:::

::: assumptions
- **Illustrative loss model**: simple switching-loss formula and constant $R_{eff}$. Real FET loss depends on body-diode conduction, dead time, $Q_{oss}$ and temperature.
- **CCM** at all loads; below some load the phase would enter DCM and the model changes.
- **Same $f_{sw}$ per phase** in every case.
- **The 1-phase 60 A row** is a hypothetical and not a good design; it shows what happens if you do not add silicon.
- Other answer: for a fixed total silicon budget, full-load efficiency may be nearly unchanged and the benefits come from thermal spreading and transient performance.
:::

::: followups
### Why does the total switching loss not change with N?
Each phase switches $I_{out}/N$ at the same frequency, so per-phase loss is $1/N$ of the single-phase value, and there are $N$ phases. The sum is the same. What you gain is the ability to get the effective ripple frequency of $Nf_{sw}$ without paying the switching loss of a converter running at $Nf_{sw}$.

### At what load should you shed from 4 phases to 2?
Where the saved fixed losses exceed the added conduction loss. In the model, 4 and 2 phases cross near 15 to 20 A. In practice the controller uses current thresholds with hysteresis and may switch earlier to protect transient performance, since the ripple is higher with fewer phases.

### Does phase shedding hurt transient response?
It can. With fewer phases the effective inductance is higher and the slew is lower, so a load step arriving in light-load mode sees a slower response. Controllers detect the step (output droop or a load-current signal) and re-enable phases within a few switching cycles. Some designs keep a minimum phase count if the application has hard transient needs.

### How would you measure efficiency of a multiphase buck fairly?
Four-wire measurement of input and output voltage and current, at several loads and input voltages, with the same temperature conditions and the output sensed at the load. Measure with phases forced at 1, 2 and 4 to build the curves. Account for the controller's bias supply if it is separate.

### Scenario: the efficiency at 30 A is lower than the simulation predicted by 2%. Where do you look?
Check switching loss first, since it is the largest term: actual edge times, dead time with body-diode conduction, and $Q_{oss}$ loss, and ringing. Then core loss (ripple and temperature), DCR at temperature, PCB resistance in the power path and sense-resistor loss. Compare the measured per-phase temperatures with the model to find which stage is hotter.
:::
