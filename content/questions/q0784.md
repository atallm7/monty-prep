::: testing
Whether you can name the two standard output types (**push-pull** and **open-drain**, with open-collector as the bipolar cousin) and then compare them on the axes that matter: speed, power, ability to share a line, voltage translation, and the extra parts needed. The best answers give a number (an RC rise time, a pull-up current) rather than adjectives.

Hidden sub-skills: wired-AND logic, pull-up sizing trade-off, contention safety, and recognising which one a given bus (I2C, interrupt lines, reset) uses.
:::

::: why
Picking the output type is a real design decision on every board: a lone LED enable wants push-pull; a shared interrupt line, an I2C bus or a reset line wants open-drain. Choosing wrong gives slow edges, wasted current, contention damage, or a bus that cannot have two masters. The question is a quick check that you know *why* standard buses are built the way they are.
:::

::: concepts
- **Push-pull**: PMOS and NMOS drive both levels actively. See [[q0783]].
- **Open-drain**: only an NMOS to ground; a resistor (pull-up) supplies the high level. See [[q0785]].
- **Wired-AND**: several open-drain outputs on one line: the line is low if any device pulls low.
- **Pull-up sizing**: the RC rise time against the sink current. See [[q0786]].
- **Contention**: two push-pull outputs fighting. See [[q0788]].
- **Level translation**: the pull-up can go to a different voltage than the chip's supply.
:::

::: answer
"The two common GPIO output types are push-pull and open-drain. Push-pull has a PMOS and an NMOS, so the pin actively drives both high and low. Open-drain has only the NMOS: it can pull the line low, or let go, and then an external pull-up resistor pulls the line high.

Push-pull wins on speed and power. Both edges are driven by a low-impedance transistor, so the edges are fast, a few nanoseconds into a typical load, and no current flows when the pin is high. It's the default for anything a single chip drives: LED enables, chip selects, SPI lines, UART TX.

Open-drain wins on sharing and flexibility. Several devices can connect to one wire without any danger of contention, because nobody ever drives high, so the line behaves as a wired-AND: any device can pull it low. That's why I2C, shared interrupt lines and reset lines use it. The pull-up can also go to a different voltage, so it doubles as a simple level translator.

The costs are speed and power: the rising edge is an RC curve through the resistor, for 4.7 kΩ and 100 pF that's about 400 nanoseconds, and every time the line is low it burns VDD over R, 0.7 mA at 3.3 V. And you need the external resistor."
:::

::: technical
**Structures.** Push-pull: PMOS + NMOS. Open-drain: NMOS only (open-collector uses an NPN). Many GPIOs can be configured either way by a register bit ([[q0787]]).

**Comparison.**

| Property | Push-pull | Open-drain |
|---|---|---|
| High level | actively driven by PMOS | resistor to $V_{pull}$ |
| Low level | NMOS | NMOS |
| Rising edge | fast: $\tau\approx R_{on,p}C\approx 25\ \Omega\times100\text{ pF}=2.5$ ns | slow: $\tau=R_{pu}C$; 30%-70% time $0.8473R_{pu}C$ |
| Falling edge | fast | fast (NMOS) |
| Static power when high | none | none |
| Static power when low | none | $V_{pull}^2/R_{pu}$ (e.g. 2.3 mW for 4.7 kΩ at 3.3 V) |
| Multiple drivers | contention if they disagree | safe; wired-AND |
| Extra parts | none | pull-up resistor |
| Level translation | fixed to $V_{DD}$ | $V_{pull}$ can differ (within pin tolerance) |
| Typical uses | SPI, UART TX, enables, LED drive | I2C, shared IRQ, reset, 1-wire, bus arbitration |

**Numbers (3.3 V, 100 pF load).**

| $R_{pu}$ | 30-70% rise | Current when low | Max toggle (approx $1/(2\times2.2RC)$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 kΩ | 85 ns | 3.3 mA | 2.3 MHz |
| 4.7 kΩ | 398 ns | 0.70 mA | 480 kHz |
| 10 kΩ | 847 ns | 0.33 mA | 230 kHz |

The pull-up has a lower bound too: $R_{pu,\min}=(V_{pull}-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$; 3 mA sink at $V_{OL}=0.4$ V gives about 970 Ω.

**Open-drain with a strong pull-up** speeds the rise edge at the cost of current; active pull-ups ("boosters") or push-pull with a direction pin are used when speed is needed.
:::

::: long
"The two common GPIO output types are push-pull and open-drain, and the trade-off is basically speed and simplicity versus sharing and flexibility.

Push-pull has a PMOS to VDD and an NMOS to ground. Whichever level I want, a transistor actively drives it with a low impedance, tens of ohms. So both edges are fast: with 25 ohms into 100 picofarads the time constant is about 2.5 nanoseconds. And there's no static current either way. It's the default for any signal one chip drives: SPI, UART TX, chip selects, enables. Its weakness is that if two push-pull outputs share a wire and disagree, you get a short through the two output stages, easily over 50 milliamps.

Open-drain has only the NMOS. The pin can pull the line low or release it, and an external pull-up resistor pulls it high when released. That gives the opposite trade-offs. Several devices can share one wire safely, because nobody ever drives high, so the line is low if any one device pulls low: a wired-AND. That's exactly what I2C, a shared interrupt line or a reset line needs, and it allows clock stretching and arbitration. The pull-up can also go to a different voltage than the chip supply, so it works as a simple level shifter.

The cost is the rising edge, which is an RC curve. With 4.7 kΩ and 100 pF, the 30 to 70 percent rise time is about 400 nanoseconds, which limits a bus to a few hundred kilohertz, and whenever the line is low it burns VDD over R, 0.7 milliamps here. Choose R too large and it's slow; too small and you exceed the sink current.

So I'd use push-pull for anything point-to-point or fast, and open-drain for shared or level-shifted lines."
:::

::: terms
- **Push-pull**: output with both pull-up and pull-down transistors actively driving.
- **Open-drain (open-collector)**: output with only a pull-down transistor.
- **Pull-up resistor**: external resistor that supplies the high level for an open-drain line.
- **Wired-AND**: shared line that is low if any device pulls low.
- **Rise time**: time for the RC edge, $\approx0.85R_{pu}C$ from 30% to 70%.
- **Sink current ($I_{OL}$)**: current the pin can absorb while staying under $V_{OL}$.
- **Contention**: two drivers forcing opposite levels onto one net.
- **Level translation**: converting a signal between supply domains.
:::

::: exceptional
**An average answer** says "push-pull drives both ways, open-drain needs a pull-up".

**An exceptional answer**:
- Compares on several axes: edge speed, static power, sharing, level translation, parts count.
- Quantifies the open-drain rise time with $R_{pu}C$ and the sink-current floor for $R_{pu}$.
- Explains **wired-AND** and links it to I2C, interrupts and reset lines, and clock stretching/arbitration.
- Mentions the contention risk for push-pull and why open-drain avoids it.
- Notes that many GPIOs can be configured either way, and which to choose for each signal.
:::

::: mistakes
- **"Open-drain is just a weaker push-pull."** It cannot drive high at all; the high comes from outside.
- **Forgetting the pull-up** on an open-drain line: it never goes high, or floats.
- **Choosing the pull-up only for speed.** The lower limit ($V_{pull}-V_{OL}$ over $I_{OL}$) and static current matter too.
- **Putting push-pull outputs on a shared wire** and hoping software prevents contention.
- **Thinking open-drain gives free level shifting at any voltage.** The pin's maximum voltage rating (5 V tolerance) still applies.
- **Assuming open-drain is always slow.** With a small pull-up and small $C$ it can reach MHz, at the price of current.
:::

## The same pin, two personalities

Most MCU pins let you choose the output type with one bit ([[q0787]]). In push-pull mode, both transistors of the output stage are available; in open-drain mode, the PMOS is simply never turned on. Everything about the trade-off follows from that one missing transistor.

## Edges: where the speed difference comes from

A push-pull edge is an RC charge through the transistor's on-resistance, tens of ohms. With $R_{on}=25\ \Omega$ and $C=100$ pF the time constant is 2.5 ns and the 10-90% time is $2.2\tau=5.5$ ns. At that point other limits dominate: the pad's slew-rate control and the trace's inductance.

An open-drain falling edge is the same NMOS discharge, equally fast. The rising edge is a charge through the resistor:

$$v(t)=V_{pull}\left(1-e^{-t/R_{pu}C}\right),\qquad t_{30\to70}=0.8473\,R_{pu}C,\qquad t_{10\to90}=2.2\,R_{pu}C$$

## Power

Push-pull dissipates only in transitions ($CV^2f$: 100 pF at 3.3 V and 1 MHz is about 1.1 mW, mostly in the transistors) plus any shoot-through. Open-drain additionally burns $V^2/R$ whenever the line is held low: 4.7 kΩ at 3.3 V is 2.3 mW. On a battery-powered design with a bus that sits low half the time, that matters: use a larger pull-up, or hold the line high when idle (which I2C does).

Figure: Two open-drain drivers on one line with one pull-up. The line is high only if both transistors are off; if either (or both) are on it is low: a wired-AND. If A and B both pull low, no harm is done; both transistors simply share the current.

This is what makes multi-master buses, shared interrupt requests and reset lines work. Several peripherals may request an interrupt on the same line; the MCU sees it low if any device asserts. For I2C, a slave can stretch the clock by holding SCL low and the master sees it. See [[q0753]] for the bus and [[q0761]] for advantages of open-drain drivers on that bus.

## Level translation

If the pin tolerates it, $V_{pull}$ can be different from the chip's $V_{DD}$: a 1.8 V device with an open-drain output and a pull-up to 3.3 V gives a 3.3 V logic high to the other chip, provided the pin is rated for 3.3 V when its own supply is 1.8 V. This is how many I2C level shifters (and the single-FET shifter) work. The speed limits above still apply.

## What each looks like on a scope

A push-pull signal looks like a clean square wave with sharp edges both ways, possibly with ringing on a long trace. An open-drain signal has the telltale asymmetry: a snappy falling edge and a lazy exponential rising edge that never quite looks like a square wave. If you see a shark-fin rising edge on a line you believed was push-pull, the pin was probably left in open-drain mode with a weak pull-up, a very common bring-up bug after copying a configuration from an I2C pin. The opposite symptom is a line that never rises: an open-drain output with the pull-up missing or the internal pull-up disabled sits near ground with only a leakage-driven slow drift.

Another quick test: measure the high level with the pin released. A push-pull pin sits at $V_{DD}$ regardless of load (within $I\,R_{on}$). An open-drain pin sits at whatever the pull-up and the loads make it. If the downstream input draws leakage current $I_{leak}$, the high level is $V_{pull}-I_{leak}R_{pu}$; with 1 uA through 100 kΩ that is a 0.1 V sag, while 10 uA of a leaky or contaminated node drops it by a full volt, which is how large pull-ups cause intermittent faults.

## Choosing

| Situation | Choice | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| SPI, UART TX, parallel data | push-pull | speed, one driver per wire |
| Enable or reset to one chip | push-pull (or open-drain with pull-up for safe default) | simple |
| Shared interrupt line | open-drain | any source can assert |
| I2C, 1-wire | open-drain | multi-device, arbitration |
| Output to a different voltage domain | open-drain with pull-up to the target rail | translation |
| Driving an LED or MOSFET gate | push-pull | needs both edges |
| Fast edges with a shared line | push-pull with tri-state direction control, or a buffer | pull-up too slow |

## A compromise: open-drain with an active pull-up

Faster buses (I2C Fast-mode Plus, some one-wire links) add current-source pull-ups or "rise-time accelerators" that help the rising edge, then turn off. They give push-pull-like edges with open-drain sharing. The simplest version is to size the passive resistor to the minimum allowed by the sink current.
:::

::: applications
- **I2C buses**: open-drain SDA/SCL with 2.2 to 10 kΩ pull-ups ([[q0753]]).
- **Shared interrupt lines**: several sensors wired-OR onto one MCU input.
- **Reset lines and supervisors**: open-drain reset outputs with a pull-up so several sources can hold the MCU in reset.
- **1-Wire sensors**: single bidirectional open-drain line.
- **LED, buzzer and SPI chip-select drive**: push-pull.
- **Level-translating a 1.8 V sensor output to a 3.3 V MCU**: open-drain with pull-up to 3.3 V.
:::

::: assumptions
- 3.3 V logic, a pin that supports both modes, 100 pF line capacitance unless stated, and ideal resistors.
- On-resistance 25 Ω is a representative value; real pins range from about 10 to 100 Ω depending on drive setting.
- The question says "two most common types"; I take them as push-pull and open-drain. See the interpretation note for another reading.
- 30-70% rise time is the I2C convention; other specs use 10-90%.

::: followups
### Why does I2C use open-drain instead of push-pull?
Because several devices share SDA and SCL and any of them can talk. With open-drain, no two devices ever fight, a slave can stretch the clock, and arbitration between masters works by "whoever sees a 0 they did not send backs off". See [[q0753]].

### How would you size an open-drain pull-up?
Between a minimum $R_{min}=(V_{pull}-V_{OL})/I_{OL}$ (about 970 Ω for 3.3 V, 0.4 V, 3 mA) and a maximum set by the allowed rise time, $R_{max}=t_r/(0.8473C)$ (about 11.8 kΩ for a 1 us spec and 100 pF). Pick a standard value between, e.g. 4.7 kΩ, trading current against speed. See [[q0786]].

### Can I connect two push-pull outputs together if they always send the same value?
Only if they truly never differ, including at start-up and during transitions. Skew between the two drivers causes brief shoot-through spikes, and a firmware bug or reset state could create a real fight. Use open-drain or a gate instead.

### What is the open-collector equivalent?
The same structure with an NPN (or a TTL output stage) instead of an NMOS: collector open, needs a pull-up. The saturation voltage $V_{CE(sat)}$ replaces $V_{OL}$.

### Harder: an open-drain line is too slow at 1 MHz. Options?
Reduce $C$ (shorter trace, fewer devices), reduce $R_{pu}$ within the sink-current limit, add a rise-time accelerator (active pull-up) or switch that signal to push-pull with explicit direction control. With 100 pF the maximum $R_{pu}$ for a 30-70% time of 300 ns is $300/(0.8473\times0.1)=3.5$ kΩ, which draws 0.93 mA when low.
:::
